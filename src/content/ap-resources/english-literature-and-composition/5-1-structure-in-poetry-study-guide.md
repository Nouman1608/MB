Poetry compresses meaning into a small space, so the arrangement of the words on the page carries a great deal of it. Where a line ends, where a stanza breaks, which phrase repeats and where a pattern changes: all of these are choices that relate one idea to another. This topic teaches you to describe a poem's structure and to explain what it does, using both **closed** and **open** forms.

## What structure is

**Structure** is the arrangement of the parts of a text, the relationship of the parts to each other, and the sequence in which the text reveals information. In a poem the parts are lines, stanzas, sentences, repeated phrases and patterns of sound. Structure is not decoration: the arrangement is how a poem develops its thought.

Two broad kinds of poetic form matter here.

- **Closed forms** follow predictable patterns in the structure of lines, stanzas, meter and rhyme. The pattern develops relationships among ideas: each repeated unit invites comparison with the last, and the pattern's completion or interruption carries meaning. The exam will not ask you to *label* a rhyme scheme, a metrical pattern or a named form. You are asked to explain what the arrangement does.
- **Open forms** do not follow expected or predictable patterns of lines or stanzas, but they still have structures that develop relationships between ideas: parallel phrases, stanza breaks that mark shifts, changes in line length, repetition.

A poem's structures often **combine** to emphasise certain ideas and concepts. A rhyme that ties two lines together, a stanza break between them and a change of tone after them may all point at the same turn in thought.

## The relationships structure can build

Ask what relationship an arrangement sets up between one part and another. The common ones are:

| Relationship | What it looks like | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| Parallel | Repeated phrasing across lines or stanzas | Invites comparison; builds emphasis |
| Build | Each unit adds weight or narrows focus | Creates momentum; intensifies feeling |
| Shift (volta) | A turn in thought or tone, often at a stanza break or a word like *but* | Changes the reader's understanding; carries the main idea |
| Contrast | Two parts placed side by side | Highlights a difference in traits or values |
| Interruption | A pattern is broken | Puts weight on the break |
| Resolution | A pattern completes | Gives closure, certainty or irony |

1. **Pattern.** What is repeated or regular: line length, stanza length, a repeated opening, a rhyme?
2. **Shift.** Where does it change? A new stanza, a change of subject, a turn from "I" to "you"?
3. **Relationship.** How do the parts before and after the shift relate: parallel, build, contrast, resolution?
4. **Effect.** What does that relationship make the reader see or feel about the poem's central idea?

## Worked example 1: a closed form that narrows

William Shakespeare's Sonnet 73 (1609) is a closed form of fourteen lines. The text below is in the modernised spelling of nineteenth-century anthologies.

> That time of year thou may'st in me behold
> When yellow leaves, or none, or few do hang
> Upon those boughs which shake against the cold,
> Bare ruin'd choirs, where late the sweet birds sang.
> In me thou seest the twilight of such day
> As after sunset fadeth in the west,
> Which by and by black night doth take away,
> Death's second self, that seals up all in rest.
> In me thou seest the glowing of such fire,
> That on the ashes of his youth doth lie
> As the deathbed whereon it must expire,
> Consumed with that which it was nourish'd by.
> This thou perceiv'st, which makes thy love more strong,
> To love that well which thou must leave ere long.

**Step 1, pattern.** Three four-line units each begin with a version of "In me thou seest" (or "That time of year thou may'st in me behold"), and each develops one image: autumn, twilight, a dying fire. A final two-line unit rhymes ("strong" / "long") and speaks directly of the listener's love.

**Step 2, shift.** The shift comes at the last two lines. The first twelve lines describe the speaker's ageing; the couplet turns to the listener: "This thou perceiv'st, which makes thy love more strong".

**Step 3, relationship.** The three images are parallel, but they also narrow. A year (autumn) shrinks to a day (twilight) and then to the last moment of a fire ("the ashes of his youth"). The time left grows shorter with each unit: a build toward ending. The couplet then relates the whole sequence to a human response: the loss of time makes love stronger.

**Step 4, effect.** The closed form delivers a controlled argument: three pictures of decline, then a conclusion drawn from them. Because the couplet is a short, rhymed unit, it feels like a settled judgement, so the poem ends not with despair but with an instruction to love "that well which thou must leave ere long". The pattern makes loss and love one thought, not two.

**A model sentence.** Shakespeare builds the sonnet from three parallel images that shrink from a year to a day to a dying fire, so that the rhymed couplet, which turns to the listener's love, lands as a conclusion the whole structure has prepared.

## Worked example 2: an open form that turns

Walt Whitman's "A Noiseless Patient Spider", published in the nineteenth century in *Leaves of Grass*, has two stanzas of five lines, with irregular long lines and no rhyme. The text below follows the nineteenth-century printing.

> A noiseless patient spider,
> I mark'd where on a little promontory it stood isolated,
> Mark'd how to explore the vacant vast surrounding,
> It launch'd forth filament, filament, filament out of itself,
> Ever unreeling them, ever tirelessly speeding them.
>
> And you O my soul where you stand,
> Surrounded, detached, in measureless oceans of space,
> Ceaselessly musing, venturing, throwing, seeking the spheres to connect them,
> Till the bridge you will need be form'd, till the ductile anchor hold,
> Till the gossamer thread you fling catch somewhere, O my soul.

**Step 1, pattern.** There is no rhyme or regular meter, so the pattern is built from repetition and parallel phrasing. Within the first stanza "filament, filament, filament" and "Ever unreeling... ever tirelessly speeding" repeat; in the second, "Till... till... till" and the participles "musing, venturing, throwing, seeking" repeat. The two stanzas are the same length and share vocabulary ("surrounding" and "Surrounded", "launch'd" and "fling").

**Step 2, shift.** The stanza break is the turn: the first stanza describes "it" (the spider), the second addresses "you O my soul".

**Step 3, relationship.** The stanzas are **parallel** (both describe a lonely figure launching threads into space) and the second is a **building application** of the first: what the spider does literally, the soul does figuratively. The repeated "till" gives the second stanza an anxious, open-ended momentum that the first does not have: the spider's work is observed; the soul's is still unfinished.

**Step 4, effect.** The open form has no rhyme to close it, and the poem ends on the unresolved word "somewhere", so the structure enacts what it describes: a thread flung out and not yet caught. The reader is invited to feel the soul's persistence and uncertainty, not to receive a neat conclusion.

**A model sentence.** Whitman matches two five-line stanzas, one about a spider and one about the soul, so that the repeated "till" of the second stanza turns observation into an unfinished effort that mirrors the spider's launching of "filament, filament, filament".

## Common misconceptions

- **"I must name the rhyme scheme."** You will not be asked to label schemes, meters or forms. Explain what the arrangement does.
- **"Free verse has no structure."** Open forms still use repetition, line breaks, stanza breaks and parallel phrasing to relate ideas.
- **"A pattern always means order and calm."** A regular pattern may be set up in order to be broken, or may sound controlled while the content is disturbing.
- **"Structure is the same as summary."** A summary says what happens; structure analysis says how the arrangement shapes the reader's understanding.
- **"Only the volta matters."** The pattern before a turn matters as much as the turn.
- **"A stanza break is just a pause."** A stanza break often marks a change of subject, speaker or tone and builds a relationship between the stanzas on each side.
