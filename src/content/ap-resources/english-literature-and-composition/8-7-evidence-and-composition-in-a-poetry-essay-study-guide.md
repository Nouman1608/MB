## Evidence and composition: the other half of the argument

In Topic 8.6 you learned to make a claim and explain it. This topic is about the material you explain and the writing you explain it in. Evidence is what the poem gives you; **composition** is how clearly you put it together. Under timed conditions, both decide whether a good interpretation reaches the reader.

Four ideas from the course framework carry this topic.

- **Writers use evidence strategically and purposefully** to illustrate, clarify, exemplify, associate, amplify or qualify a point. Each quotation has a job.
- **Evidence is effective** when your commentary explains the logical relationship between the evidence and the claim. A quotation with no explanation is only decoration.
- **Evidence is sufficient** when its quantity and quality give apt support to the line of reasoning: enough, from enough places, and good enough.
- **Developing an interpretation is recursive.** You may begin with a line of reasoning and look for evidence, or begin with striking evidence and discover the reasoning. In practice you move back and forth, and you should expect to revise your claim as you collect evidence.

The same framework treats **control of composition** (clear sentences, organised paragraphs, correct mechanics, honest attribution) as part of the skill, not an extra. In a timed essay you are not expected to use a formal citation style, but you must not blur whose words are whose.

## What evidence can do

| Job | What the quotation does | Example frame |
|---|---|---|
| Illustrate | Shows a general claim in a concrete case | "This is clear when the speaker says '...'" |
| Clarify | Makes a difficult claim easier to see | "In other words, '...' means..." |
| Exemplify | Offers a typical instance of a pattern | "The pattern appears in '...', and again in..." |
| Associate | Links to an idea, image or other part of the poem | "'...' echoes the earlier '...'" |
| Amplify | Pushes the claim further | "The last stanza intensifies this with '...'" |
| Qualify | Limits or complicates a claim | "Yet '...' complicates this, since..." |

Knowing the job helps you decide what to quote and what to say about it. An essay that only illustrates tends to repeat itself; one that also amplifies and qualifies builds a line of reasoning that moves.

## Choosing evidence

1. **Choose the smallest unit that carries the point.** A word or a short phrase is usually enough. "Marks of weakness, marks of woe" supports a claim about repetition; the whole stanza is not needed.
2. **Match it to the claim.** Before you quote, say what you are proving. Evidence that is vivid but irrelevant costs time and weakens the paragraph.
3. **Spread it.** An interpretation of the whole poem needs evidence from its beginning, middle and end. This does not mean quoting from every stanza; it means your argument should not rest on a single line.
4. **Prefer evidence you can analyse.** A phrase with a loaded word, a sound pattern, an image, a structural turn or a contrast gives you something to say. A plain statement of fact gives you little.
5. **Quote accurately.** Copy exactly, including punctuation. If you cannot copy a phrase reliably, describe it closely instead.

A quick method under time pressure is the **evidence ledger**: while you annotate the poem, jot four to six candidate phrases, and beside each write the claim it could support and its job (illustrate, amplify, qualify). When you draft, you are choosing from a list, not searching.

## Integrating quotations

A quotation should sit inside your sentence so that your grammar carries it.

- **Embed it.** "The speaker hears 'the mind-forged manacles' in every voice" is smoother than "The speaker hears something. 'The mind-forged manacles I hear.'"
- **Keep the poet's wording and punctuation.** Use brackets to change a word [like this] and an ellipsis (...) to omit words.
- **Show line breaks with a slash.** When you quote more than one line within a sentence, put a slash with spaces between lines: "Marks of weakness, marks of woe" is one line; "How the chimney-sweeper's cry / Every blackening church appals" is two.
- **Use the present tense** for what the poem does: "the speaker hears", not "the speaker heard".
- **Say "the speaker", not "the poet",** unless the question is about the poet's choices. The speaker is the voice in the poem; the poet shapes that voice.
- **Explain after you quote.** One or two sentences of commentary should follow every piece of evidence.

## Composition under time pressure

A suggested plan for a 40-minute essay:

| Minutes | Task |
|---|---|
| About 5 | Read the poem twice, paraphrase, annotate turns and devices |
| About 5 | Decide the interpretation, write the thesis, list claims and evidence |
| About 25 | Write the introduction (thesis), three body paragraphs and a brief conclusion |
| About 5 | Reread; fix errors that interrupt meaning; add one link or one qualification |

Clear composition means one idea per paragraph, topic sentences that move the argument, transitions that show logical relationships ("because", "so", "yet"), and mechanics that do not distract. Errors that interfere with communication limit the top score for evidence and commentary, so prefer simple, accurate sentences to ambitious, tangled ones.

## Worked example 1: auditing a weak paragraph

William Blake's "London" (1794) appears in part in Topic 8.3 and is the poem for this topic. Its first two stanzas read:

> I wander through each chartered street,  
> Near where the chartered Thames does flow,  
> A mark in every face I meet,  
> Marks of weakness, marks of woe.
>
> In every cry of every man,  
> In every infant's cry of fear,  
> In every voice, in every ban,  
> The mind-forged manacles I hear:

*Spelling and punctuation follow a nineteenth-century printing; some editions write "thro'" for "through".*

A student claims that Blake's speaker sees the city as a place of confinement. Here is the student's paragraph.

> Blake's speaker walks through London and says "I wander through each chartered street, / Near where the chartered Thames does flow, / A mark in every face I meet, / Marks of weakness, marks of woe. / In every cry of every man, / In every infant's cry of fear". This shows that London is a sad place. The poem is sad and the speaker feels sad.

**Audit.**

- *Quantity of quotation:* six lines pasted in. The claim is about confinement, but the quotation is not used.
- *Relevance:* only "chartered" and perhaps "manacles" bear on confinement; neither is analysed.
- *Commentary:* "This shows that London is a sad place" is a general feeling with no link to the claim.
- *Integration:* the quotation is dropped in with no explanation.
- *Claim and thesis:* the paragraph never states what it proves.

**Revision.**

> The speaker's repetition of "chartered" suggests that the city has turned even its landscape into property. By pairing "each chartered street" with "the chartered Thames", Blake gives a word of legal permission to places that should be open, so the river that "does flow" is fenced by the same logic as the pavement. This sets up the claim that the people the speaker meets are confined too: their faces carry "Marks of weakness, marks of woe", as if the city's restrictions had left a visible stamp on them. The evidence is brief, but each phrase does a job: "chartered" illustrates the idea of ownership, and the repeated "marks" amplifies it from the streets to the people.

**What changed.** The quotations are short, each is embedded, each has a stated job, and the commentary explains the link to the claim of confinement.

## Worked example 2: a model essay with annotations

**Prompt (written by Marlbridge).** Read William Blake's "London" (1794). Write an essay analysing how Blake presents the speaker's view of the city and its people.

Below is a model response written by Marlbridge. Labels in square brackets mark the moves; they are not part of the essay.

> [Thesis] Blake's speaker walks through London as a witness, not a tourist: he hears in every cry evidence of an order that confines the people it governs, and the poem suggests that the worst confinement is the kind people carry in their own minds.
>
> [Claim 1] The poem opens by turning the city's geography into ownership. [Evidence] The speaker wanders through "each chartered street" and beside "the chartered Thames", [Commentary] and the repetition of "chartered", a word for something licensed and bounded by law, suggests that even a flowing river has been fenced by commerce. Against these mapped places, the faces he meets bear "Marks of weakness, marks of woe". The repeated "marks" makes the people seem branded, as if the same authority that charters the streets has stamped them. [Link] The city is thus presented as a system that labels both place and person.
>
> [Claim 2] The speaker then moves from what he sees to what he hears, and the shift locates the oppression inside the people. [Evidence] "In every voice, in every ban, / The mind-forged manacles I hear" [Commentary] repeats "In every" until no voice escapes, and "manacles" names a physical restraint that is "mind-forged", made by the mind itself. The phrase implies that the people help to build their own captivity. [Link] The speaker's attention to sound makes him a listener who can detect what the eye cannot, and it widens the accusation beyond rulers to the habits of the ruled.
>
> [Claim 3] The third stanza assigns institutions their share of the guilt. [Evidence] The chimney-sweeper's cry makes "every blackening church" appal, and "the hapless soldier's sigh / Runs in blood down palace-walls". [Commentary] "Blackening" suggests both soot and moral stain, while the sigh that "runs in blood" gives a palace wall the look of a wound. The cries of the powerless become marks on the buildings of the powerful. [Link] The poem thus converts private suffering into public evidence.
>
> [Claim 4, qualifying and extending] The last stanza narrows the poem to its most intimate ground. [Evidence] "But most, through midnight streets I hear / How the youthful harlot's curse / Blasts the new-born infant's tear, / And blights with plagues the marriage hearse." [Commentary] "But most" tells us where the speaker's anger is strongest, and the harsh verbs "Blasts" and "blights" attack birth and marriage, the institutions of hope. The oxymoron "marriage hearse" joins a wedding carriage to a funeral one, so even love's ceremony carries death. The poem leaves open whether the curse is the woman's bitterness or a spreading disease, and the ambiguity lets it stand for both a social and a physical corruption.
>
> [Sophistication and conclusion] The poem therefore ends not with a remedy but with a fusion of joy and death, which is its judgment on the city: its institutions, and the people who accept them, pass suffering from one generation to the next. The speaker's mixture of pity and accusation shows that he does not merely condemn London; he mourns what it does to those who live there.

**Annotated self-score against the suggested Marlbridge rubric.**

| Row | Evidence from the model | Judgment |
|---|---|---|
| Thesis | A defensible interpretation (witness; confinement that includes the mind) that answers the prompt | Earns the point |
| Evidence and commentary | Specific, short quotations for each claim, each with explained effect and link; several techniques explained (repetition, diction, image, oxymoron) | Reaches the top band |
| Sophistication | Handles the tension between external and internal oppression, notices an ambiguity ("curse"), and sustains the idea of a system across the essay | Likely to earn the point |

This table is a Marlbridge teaching tool, not an official scoring guideline. A response in 40 minutes will be shorter than this model; what matters is that each paragraph does the same four moves.

## Common misconceptions

- **"More quotation means more evidence."** Quality and relevance matter; a long block is a sign of not analysing.
- **"I must quote every stanza."** Spread evidence, but a claim needs only the evidence that supports it.
- **"If I misremember a line I can paraphrase it as a quotation."** Never invent or alter a quotation; describe it instead.
- **"Composition is spelling and neatness."** It is also organisation, transitions and control of sentence structure.
- **"The thesis cannot change."** A recursive process lets evidence refine the claim; revise the thesis if the evidence points elsewhere.
- **"I need a citation style."** Not in timed essays; you must still make clear what is quoted.
