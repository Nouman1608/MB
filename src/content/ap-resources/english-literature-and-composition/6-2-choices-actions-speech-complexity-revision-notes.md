## Recap

- A character is **complex** when the text shows a **tension** inside them: they say one thing and do another, hold two values that cannot both be satisfied, or act against what they claim to believe.
- Three channels of evidence: **speech**, **action**, **private thought** (narrated, soliloquy, or inferred from details and stage directions).
- Inconsistencies between **private thoughts and behaviour** reveal tensions between **private and professed values**.
- **Competing, conflicting or inconsistent choices** add complexity to the whole text: they create suspense, irony or sympathy and invite interpretation.

## The six-step method

| Step | Ask | Write down |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Professed | What does the character say they value? | A quoted phrase |
| 2. Actual | What do they do? | The action or the avoided action |
| 3. Private | What do narration or small details suggest? | A detail or gesture |
| 4. Gap | Where do these differ? | The tension in one phrase |
| 5. Meaning | What does this say about values or fears? | A claim about the character |
| 6. Function | What does the tension do in the work? | Effect: suspense, irony, sympathy, plot turn |

## Key terms and examples

| Term | Meaning | Example (public domain) |
|---|---|---|
| Professed value | What a character claims to believe | Lady Macbeth's advice to appear an "innocent flower" |
| Private thought | What a character hides or feels privately | Macbeth's reasoning before the murder: "If it were done when 'tis done" |
| Competing choice | Two goals or values in conflict | Hamlet at prayer: "Now might I do it pat, now he is praying" |
| Cost | What a choice sacrifices; a sign of real conflict | Jane Eyre's declaration of freedom set beside a hard departure |

## Assumptions

- You are reading patterns across a work, not a single moment. Check whether the tension recurs.
- The writer has chosen each detail. Treat apparent inconsistency as a question to answer before treating it as an error.
- A gap between words and actions is not automatically hypocrisy. It can show fear, love, duty or self-deception.

## Mistakes to avoid

- Stopping at a label such as "hypocrite" or "conflicted".
- Describing both sides of the tension without saying what the tension suggests.
- Ignoring stage directions or small gestures that undercut a speech.
- Treating one dramatic act as the whole of a character.
- Missing function: say what the complexity does for the work.

## Quick self-check

**1.** A character announces that she does not care for gossip and then asks the maid three times who called while she was out. Name the tension.

**2.** In a play, a stage direction reads: *He says "I forgive you" and does not look up from the floor.* What does the direction add?

**3.** True or false: if a character acts differently in Chapter 2 and Chapter 20, the writer has made a mistake.

**4.** Which is better, "She is a hypocrite" or "Her professed indifference is undercut by her repeated questions, which suggests that she is more anxious about her reputation than she admits"? Why?

**Answers.**

1. Professed indifference against persistent curiosity: private concern shows through behaviour.
2. It undercuts the words. The forgiveness may be sincere but costly, or reluctant; the action invites the audience to doubt the speech.
3. False. It may be a change over time (Topic 7.1), a deliberate tension or a sign of an unreliable account. Investigate before concluding.
4. The second. It names the gap, explains what it suggests about the character's values and fears, and is based on evidence.
