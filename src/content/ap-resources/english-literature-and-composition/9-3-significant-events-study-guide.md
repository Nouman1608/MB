## When an event matters more than the others

A longer work contains hundreds of events. Most keep the story moving. A few change what the story is *about*. These are **significant events**: moments, or short runs of related moments, that alter a situation, force a choice, reveal what the central conflict is really over, or change how we must read everything before and after.

Treating an event as significant means explaining its **function**, not repeating its **content**. Content is what happens ("Magwitch tells Pip he is his benefactor"). Function is what the event does for the work ("it overturns Pip's assumptions about where his status comes from and tests the values he has built on them"). Weak commentary retells; strong commentary explains why this event, in this place, shapes the work's meaning.

Three ideas organise the topic.

- **Significant events often put competing value systems into collision.** A work's central conflict usually sets two ways of living or judging against one another, such as pride against humility, loyalty against ambition, or custom against honesty. The events where these values meet directly are where the work is most visible to its readers.
- **Events collide and accumulate.** Earlier events create expectations; later ones fulfil, complicate or overturn them. Sequence and placement create **anticipation and suspense**: the reader senses that something must break, and the event is the break.
- **A significant event can be a set.** A run of related scenes, such as a chain of betrayals or a series of refusals, may do the work of one event.

## A method for significant events

1. **Select.** Choose an event that changes something: what a character knows, what a relationship is, what options remain, or what the reader understands about the conflict.
2. **Say what changes.** State the difference between the situation before and after.
3. **Name the values in collision.** What two (or more) systems of value meet here, and who stands for each?
4. **Trace the build-up.** What earlier events led to this one, and what does it set up?
5. **Explain the function.** What does this event contribute to the work's interpretation: a reversal, a revelation, an escalation, a release?

## Worked example 1: a reversal that tests a value system (*Great Expectations*)

In Charles Dickens's *Great Expectations* (1861), Pip is a village boy who comes into "great expectations" from an unknown benefactor and moves to London to become a gentleman. He assumes the benefactor is the wealthy Miss Havisham. In Chapter 39 a visitor climbs the stairs of his lodging; he is the convict Pip helped in the opening chapter.

**Select and say what changes.** The visitor says: "Yes, Pip, dear boy, I've made a gentleman on you! It's me wot has done it!" The event changes what Pip knows (his benefactor is Magwitch, not Miss Havisham), what he can expect (his fortune is tied to a hunted criminal), and what the reader understands about his rise. Pip's reaction is physical: "All the truth of my position came flashing on me; and its disappointments, dangers, disgraces, consequences of all kinds, rushed in in such a multitude that I was borne down by them and had to struggle for every breath I drew."

**Values in collision.** Pip's value system has been built on gentility: that status comes from refinement, a good address and a right connection. Magwitch's is built on gratitude and labour: "I lived rough, that you should live smooth; I worked hard, that you should be above work." Pip's response, "The abhorrence in which I held the man, the dread I had of him, the repugnance with which I shrank from him, could not have been exceeded if he had been some terrible beast", shows the two systems colliding. The man who made him a gentleman is, by Pip's own standard, a repulsive one.

**Trace the build-up.** The convict was seen in Chapter 1; Pip's expectations arrived without explanation; the reader and Pip have both assumed a different source. Several earlier events (Miss Havisham's treatment of Estella, Pip's shame of Joe) have accumulated pressure on his snobbery. The revelation is the point at which they all become unavoidable.

**Function.** The event reverses the novel's central assumption and turns Pip's story from a rise to a moral test. It exposes that his gentility is built on the labour and gratitude of a man he despises, and it forces the long process (in the later chapters) by which Pip learns to see Magwitch's worth. Without this event, the novel's critique of class pretension would stay abstract.

*Model commentary:* "Magwitch's 'I've made a gentleman on you!' is the novel's hinge: by revealing that Pip's refinement is paid for by the labour of the man he finds repulsive, Dickens collides gentility with gratitude and begins the long humbling that gives the novel its moral shape."

## Worked example 2: a refusal that overturns expectations (*Pride and Prejudice*)

In Jane Austen's *Pride and Prejudice* (1813), Chapter 34 brings Darcy's first proposal to Elizabeth at the parsonage. The event is a set of related moments: the declaration, the manner of it, and her refusal.

**What changes.** Before, the reader has seen a series of rebuffs and misjudgments between the pair. Now Darcy declares himself: "In vain have I struggled. It will not do. My feelings will not be repressed. You must allow me to tell you how ardently I admire and love you." The narrator adds that he "was not more eloquent on the subject of tenderness than of pride", and that he dwelt on "his sense of her inferiority, of its being a degradation, of the family obstacles". Elizabeth's response is a refusal: "I have never desired your good opinion", and later that he is "the last man in the world whom I could ever be prevailed on to marry."

**Values in collision.** Darcy's declaration is bound to his sense of rank, which he cannot help expressing even as he proposes. Elizabeth's refusal rests on her sense of personal dignity, and on what she believes about him (including a false belief about Wickham). The event sets class pride against self-respect, and prejudice against pride, with each character's flaw visible to the reader at once.

**Build-up.** Through earlier chapters their misunderstandings have accumulated: his slight at the ball, her acceptance of Wickham's version, his intervention in Jane's affairs. The reader senses that a clash is coming.

**Function.** The refusal is a turning point because it does not end the relationship; it starts the change. Darcy's letter in the next chapter will force Elizabeth to reconsider, and both characters will have to revise their judgments. The event places the novel's two named failings, pride and prejudice, face to face, so that each partner's flaw is exposed by the other.

*Model commentary:* "Darcy's proposal fails because 'pride' and 'prejudice' collide in a single scene: his love is entangled in a speech about 'inferiority', and her refusal is shaped by a belief she has not tested, so the event makes both flaws visible and sets the revisions that the rest of the novel will require."

## Weak and strong versions side by side

| Weak | Why it falls short | Stronger |
|---|---|---|
| "In Chapter 39 Magwitch tells Pip that he is his benefactor." | Content only. | "Chapter 39 reverses Pip's assumption about his benefactor, forcing him to confront that his gentility depends on a man he despises." |
| "The proposal is a big scene." | Evaluates size, not function. | "The proposal places class pride and prejudice in one exchange, so both flaws are exposed." |
| "The event creates suspense." | Names an effect without evidence. | "The earlier misjudgments accumulate until Darcy's proposal arrives, so the refusal releases the tension and begins the revisions." |
| "The scene is important to the story." | Vague; the same sentence fits any event. | "The scene turns the novel from a story of misunderstanding to a story of change." |

## Common misconceptions

- **"The most dramatic event is the most significant."** A quiet event can be a hinge if it changes the situation.
- **"I can describe the event in detail and that counts as analysis."** Explain what the event does, not only what happens.
- **"Significant events only occur at the climax."** Turning points occur throughout; the climax is one of them.
- **"An event is significant because the author says so."** It is significant because of what changes and what values collide.
- **"I should discuss every event in order."** Choose the one or two that carry your argument.
- **"The build-up is background."** The accumulation of earlier events is part of why the later event matters.
