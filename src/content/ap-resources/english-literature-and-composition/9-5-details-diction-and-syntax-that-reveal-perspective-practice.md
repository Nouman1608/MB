These are **original Marlbridge practice questions**, not past exam questions. Passages are short extracts from public-domain works (author, title and date given) or an original prose passage written by Marlbridge, labelled as such. Mark points are a suggested Marlbridge rubric, not an official scoring guideline. Questions 1 to 6 are multiple choice; Questions 7 and 8 are written responses. Allow about 50 minutes. No calculator is needed.

## Question 1 (multiple choice · foundation)

Jane Austen's *Pride and Prejudice* (1813) opens: "It is a truth universally acknowledged, that a single man in possession of a good fortune must be in want of a wife." What does the phrase "universally acknowledged" most likely reveal about the narrator's perspective?

- (A) The narrator adopts the grand manner of a universal law with an ironic edge, preparing the reader to question how society speaks about marriage.
- (B) The narrator sincerely believes everyone agrees about marriage.
- (C) The narrator is a scientist.
- (D) The narrator dislikes bachelors.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (A).** "Universally acknowledged" is a sweeping, formal phrase for a social assumption. The irony becomes clear in the next sentence, where the "truth" is shown to serve the surrounding families.

- (B) takes the sentence at face value and ignores the irony.
- (C) misreads a formal register as a scientific one.
- (D) has no textual support.

</details>

## Question 2 (multiple choice · core)

In the same paragraph, the narrator says a newcomer "is considered as the rightful property of some one or other of their daughters". What does the diction "rightful property" suggest about the surrounding families' perspective?

- (A) They do not care about the newcomer.
- (B) They are poets.
- (C) They treat marriage as a matter of ownership and entitlement.
- (D) They are generous.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (C).** "Rightful property" is the language of ownership, so the families see the man as something to be claimed. The narrator's choice of the phrase exposes their view.

- (A) is contradicted by their evident interest in him.
- (B) and (D) are unsupported.

</details>

## Question 3 (multiple choice · core)

Huck begins his narrative in Mark Twain's *Adventures of Huckleberry Finn* (1884): "That book was made by Mr. Mark Twain, and he told the truth, mainly. There was things which he stretched, but mainly he told the truth." What do the diction and repetition suggest about Huck's perspective?

- (A) Huck admires Mr. Twain without reservation.
- (B) Huck is candid and unpretentious, speaking in a colloquial voice that hedges its own truth-telling ("mainly") and treats honesty as a matter of degree.
- (C) Huck is trying to deceive the reader.
- (D) Huck is an educated narrator who uses formal language.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (B).** The vernacular ("There was things") and the repeated hedge "mainly" present a plain-spoken narrator who qualifies his claims honestly.

- (A) is wrong: his praise is qualified.
- (C) is contradicted by his candour about stretching.
- (D) is contradicted by the non-standard grammar.

</details>

## Question 4 (multiple choice · core)

Read this passage, written by Marlbridge.

> I will say this for Mrs Pell: she kept the best-scrubbed step in the street. It was a pity, then, that nobody ever sat on it. She had, I noticed, a way of lowering her voice when she spoke about the Hendersons, not out of delicacy, I think, but the way one lowers a lantern over a well, to see what is down there. I took the room, paid in advance, and was careful to be seen reading.

Which statement best describes the narrator's perspective?

- (A) Admiring and uncritical of Mrs Pell.
- (B) Uninterested in the neighbours.
- (C) Angry and violent.
- (D) Wry and observant: the narrator notices Mrs Pell's nosiness, judges it with gentle irony, yet is aware of performing for her ("careful to be seen reading").

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (D).** The details (the scrubbed step nobody sits on; the lantern over a well) show an ironic observer. The last clause shows the narrator is not outside the game, which adds self-awareness.

- (A) is contradicted by the irony.
- (B) is contradicted by the narrator's close observation.
- (C) is not supported.

</details>

## Question 5 (multiple choice · core)

In Charles Dickens's *Great Expectations* (1861), the narrator writes: "I give Pirrip as my father's family name, on the authority of his tombstone and my sister,—Mrs. Joe Gargery, who married the blacksmith." What does this sentence reveal about the narrator's perspective?

- (A) A lawyer's careful legal precision.
- (B) A narrator who is angry with his sister.
- (C) An adult looking back with amused irony at a child's way of knowing things, since the "authority" cited is a tombstone and a sister.
- (D) A child's unreflective view, with no distance from events.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (C).** The word "authority" is grand for such sources, and the adult voice, which reports what the child believed, shows ironic distance. The paragraph continues with the child's fancies about his parents derived from the shapes of the letters.

- (A) misreads comic over-formality as legal precision.
- (B) has no textual basis in the sentence.
- (D) ignores the retrospective, ironic voice.

</details>

## Question 6 (multiple choice · stretch)

A student writes about the second sentence of *Pride and Prejudice*: "The long sentence delays its point until 'rightful property', so the irony arrives as a late jolt." Which statement best explains why this is a good comment on syntax?

- (A) It uses technical terms.
- (B) It ignores the text.
- (C) It names a sentence length and nothing else.
- (D) It describes the pattern in plain words and says what it does to the reader's experience and the narrator's irony.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (D).** It describes the sentence's pattern (long, delaying) and ties it to an effect (a late ironic jolt). That is what strong syntax commentary does.

- (A) is wrong: no technical terms are used or needed.
- (B) is wrong: it refers to a specific phrase.
- (C) would describe a weak comment such as "The sentence is long".

</details>

## Question 7 (written response · core)

Write a paragraph of about 150 words explaining how the diction and syntax of the opening paragraph of *Pride and Prejudice* reveal the narrator's perspective. Use at least two short quotations.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Sample response (written by Marlbridge).** Austen's narrator adopts a grand manner to expose a social habit. The first sentence announces "a truth universally acknowledged", the formal diction and balanced syntax making a claim about wealthy bachelors sound like a law of nature. The second sentence, long and heavily qualified, delays its point until the end, where the families treat the newcomer as "the rightful property of some one or other of their daughters". The shift from the elevated "truth" to the commercial "property" is a late jolt that reveals the narrator's irony: the so-called universal law serves the interests of people who want husbands for their daughters. The narrator's detached, ironic perspective is therefore established through the contrast between formal syntax and mercenary diction, and it prepares the reader to watch the novel's marriages with a critical eye.

**Suggested Marlbridge rubric (4 points)**

| Point | What earns it |
|---|---|
| 1 | Describes the perspective precisely (ironic, detached, shrewd) and not only "ironic". |
| 2 | Uses at least two accurate quotations. |
| 3 | Explains how diction and syntax create the perspective and its effect. |
| 4 | Notes the contrast with the community's view and says what it does for the reader. |

</details>

## Question 8 (written response · stretch)

Using the passage about Mrs Pell (Question 4), write a paragraph of about 150 words explaining how details, diction and syntax reveal the narrator's perspective and any complication in it.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Sample response (written by Marlbridge).** The narrator's perspective is wry, observant and quietly self-aware. The opening detail that Mrs Pell "kept the best-scrubbed step in the street", followed by the dry "It was a pity, then, that nobody ever sat on it", uses a short, flat sentence to turn praise into criticism. The comparison of her lowered voice to "a lantern" lowered "over a well" gives her gossip a faintly sinister curiosity, and "not out of delicacy, I think" shows a narrator who weighs motives. But the final clause complicates the stance: "careful to be seen reading" admits that the narrator is also performing for Mrs Pell. The irony therefore turns back on the narrator, so the perspective is not simply superior but implicated, which adds complexity to an otherwise light portrait.

**Suggested Marlbridge rubric (4 points)**

| Point | What earns it |
|---|---|
| 1 | Describes the perspective precisely (wry, observant, self-aware). |
| 2 | Uses at least two accurate details or quotations. |
| 3 | Explains how details, diction or syntax create it. |
| 4 | Notices and explains the complication (the narrator's own performance). |

</details>
