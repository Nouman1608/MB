## Understanding

- I can explain how a material object comes to represent an idea or concept and so becomes a symbol. *(Guide: "From object to symbol"; Practice Question 1)*
- I can explain that a symbol can represent different things depending on the reader's experience and the context. *(Guide: "From object to symbol"; Practice Question 3)*
- I can tell a common symbol from a contextual symbol. *(Guide: "From object to symbol"; Practice Question 3)*
- I can explain how a character can become symbolic by coming to represent an idea. *(Guide: "From object to symbol"; Practice Question 9)*

## Skills

- I can list the signals that an object carries symbolic weight: repetition, prominence, reaction, placement, change. *(Guide: "How to recognise a symbol"; Practice Questions 1 and 2)*
- I can use the four-step method: notice, associate, state, function. *(Guide: "A four-step method"; Practice Question 7)*
- I can trace a shift in a symbol's meaning and say what the shift shows. *(Guide: Worked example 2; Practice Questions 4 and 8)*
- I can write a claim about a symbol that includes evidence, meaning and function. *(Guide: Weak and strong claims table; Practice Questions 6 and 9)*

## Reasoning

- I can avoid treating every object as a symbol, and I can justify why a particular object is one. *(Guide: "Common misconceptions"; Practice Question 1)*
- I can explain why a symbol does not have a single fixed meaning and still insist on evidence. *(Guide: "Common misconceptions"; Practice Question 2)*
- I can use a public-domain example, such as blood in Macbeth, to explain how a symbol gains meaning through repetition. *(Guide: "Public-domain examples to learn from"; Practice Question 5)*
