## Structure is arrangement with a purpose

A poem is made of parts: **lines**, grouped into **stanzas**, forming **sentences** that may or may not match the lines, in a particular **order of ideas**. **Structure** is the arrangement of these parts and the relationship between them. A writer who puts an idea first, last, alone on a line or after a break has made a choice, and the choice affects how you read the idea.

Three principles guide analysis:

1. **Line and stanza breaks help to develop and relate ideas.** A break gives a moment of pause, separates one thought from the next or places two thoughts side by side.
2. **The arrangement of lines and stanzas contributes to the development of ideas.** An idea can be built up over several lines, then turned in the next stanza.
3. **Structure shapes expectation.** Because we read in order, what comes early prepares us for what follows, and what comes last has special force.

## What to look at

| Feature | What it is | What it can do |
|---|---|---|
| Line length | Short or long lines | Slow, quicken or isolate an idea |
| End-stopped line | The sense pauses at the line end | Weight, finality, calm |
| Enjambment | The sentence runs over the line end | Speed, urgency, a sense of flow |
| Stanza break | A white space between groups of lines | A turn, a pause, a change of focus |
| One-line stanza | A single line set apart | Emphasis, shock, a pause for thought |
| Sentence pattern | Short or long sentences against the line | Tension between the line and the sentence |
| Order of ideas | What comes first, middle, last | Build-up, reversal, conclusion |

When you comment on structure, link the feature to the *content*. "The line break after 'wrong' leaves the thought unfinished, which matches the speaker's unfinished fear" is structure analysis. "The poem has eight lines" is a description.

## Form as a frame

Some poems follow a **form**, a recognised arrangement. The sonnet is common in English: fourteen lines, often organised as three **quatrains** (groups of four lines) and a final **couplet** (two rhyming lines), or as an eight-line then six-line unit. A form gives the reader expectations (an argument that builds and then turns), and a writer can meet or surprise them. Treat the form as a frame, then ask what the poem does *inside* it.

## Worked example 1: the structure of a Shakespeare sonnet

Shakespeare's Sonnet 73 (published 1609) is a sonnet in three quatrains and a couplet. The text below is from the Project Gutenberg edition; spelling and punctuation vary between editions.

> That time of year thou mayst in me behold  
> When yellow leaves, or none, or few, do hang  
> Upon those boughs which shake against the cold,  
> Bare ruin'd choirs, where late the sweet birds sang.  
> In me thou see'st the twilight of such day  
> As after sunset fadeth in the west;  
> Which by and by black night doth take away,  
> Death's second self, that seals up all in rest.  
> In me thou see'st the glowing of such fire,  
> That on the ashes of his youth doth lie,  
> As the death-bed, whereon it must expire,  
> Consum'd with that which it was nourish'd by.  
> This thou perceiv'st, which makes thy love more strong,  
> To love that well, which thou must leave ere long.

**Step 1: Describe the arrangement.** Three quatrains each present a picture: the year (late autumn boughs), the day (twilight after sunset) and a fire (dying on its own ashes). A two-line couplet closes the poem.

**Step 2: Notice the order of ideas.** The time spans shrink: a season, then a day, then the last glow of a fire. The speaker presents his own old age three times, each closer to the end. That progression builds a sense of narrowing time.

**Step 3: Notice the repetition at the start of the second and third quatrains.** "In me thou see'st" begins both the fifth and ninth lines. The repeated phrase signals a series of equal pictures and keeps the addressee, "thou", in view as the person who is being asked to look.

**Step 4: Notice what the couplet does.** The couplet moves from picture to consequence. "This thou perceiv'st" turns from the images to the addressee's understanding, and the final line states what that understanding should produce: "To love that well, which thou must leave ere long." Because it is placed last and set apart in two lines, the conclusion gets the weight of an answer to the three pictures.

**Step 5: State what the structure does.** The three quatrains build an accumulation; the couplet resolves it by turning from the speaker's decline to the effect on the one who listens.

*Model sentence:* "The three quatrains present the speaker's age through shrinking images (a season, a day, a dying fire), and the final couplet turns from those pictures to the addressee ('This thou perceiv'st'), so the structure moves from description of decline to its consequence for love."

## Worked example 2: a poem whose structure follows its subject (original poem, written by Marlbridge)

**Low Water**

> The harbour empties twice a day, and boats  
> lean on their keels like tired animals,  
> and children walk out over what was sea  
> to pick up what the water was too careless to keep.
>
> Then the first cold ripple at the ankle.  
> Then the long shout up the beach.
>
> Everyone is back on the sand before the water  
> has finished arriving.

**Step 1: Describe the arrangement.** Three stanzas: four long lines, two short lines, two lines. The first stanza is one long sentence over four lines (the sentence is joined by "and"). The second stanza has two short sentence-fragments, each starting "Then". The third is a single sentence running over two lines.

**Step 2: What does the first stanza do?** Its long, flowing sentence and its enjambment (for example, "boats / lean", "over what was sea / to pick up") carry the reader along an unhurried scene. The pace imitates the slow, wide emptiness of low water.

**Step 3: What does the stanza break and the second stanza do?** The white space before "Then" cuts the calm. The two short lines are separate beats: a "ripple" (the first sign) and a "shout" (the alarm). The repeated "Then" makes the events feel like a countdown. The shorter lines give the sense of speed; the structure speeds up exactly as the tide does.

**Step 4: What does the last stanza do?** It returns to a longer sentence, but now the verb "arriving" comes last, after the enjambment "before the water / has finished arriving". The line break delays "has finished arriving", mimicking the water's final approach. The tone is calmer and slightly amused: everyone is safe, and the sea has taken its time.

**Step 5: State what the structure does.** The poem's structure is its meaning: the long calm sentence, the short urgent beats and the settling final sentence follow the rhythm of a tide coming in.

*Model paragraph opening:* "The structure of 'Low Water' enacts the movement of the tide. The first stanza's single, enjambed sentence spreads across four lines like the empty harbour; the second stanza's short, repeated 'Then' lines break the calm into beats of warning; and the final stanza lets the sentence run on until 'arriving', so the reader experiences the tide's return at the pace of the lines."

## Strong and weak statements about structure

| Weak | Why it is weak | Stronger |
|---|---|---|
| "The poem has three stanzas." | Names a feature; no effect | "The three stanzas move from calm to alarm to settling, matching the tide." |
| "There is enjambment, which makes it flow." | Generic effect; no link to content | "The enjambment in 'boats / lean on their keels' carries the eye across the empty harbour, as the sentence spreads like the water had." |
| "The couplet is at the end." | States position only | "The couplet turns from the pictures to the addressee, so the poem ends on the consequence." |

## Checking your inference

1. **Have you looked at the sentence as well as the line?** A sentence that ignores the line ending (enjambment) behaves differently from one that stops with it.
2. **Have you said what changes across a stanza break?** Time, focus, tone or speaker's attitude.
3. **Have you named the effect on the reader?** Expectation, surprise, relief or tension.
4. **Is your claim tied to a quoted line?** Structure claims need evidence like any other claim.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Structure means rhyme scheme."** Rhyme is one feature. Structure covers arrangement of lines, stanzas, sentences and ideas.
- **"Enjambment always means speed."** It often does, but the effect depends on the content. Say what it does *here*.
- **"A stanza break always shows a shift."** It often does, but sometimes it only gives a pause. Check the content on both sides.
- **"Every poem must fit its form neatly."** Writers often bend a form. Notice where the poem departs from the pattern and what that suggests.
- **"The last line is always the point."** The end carries weight, but the build-up matters. Judge the ending against what came before.
- **"Naming a technique is enough."** Analysis explains what the arrangement does to the ideas and to the reader.
