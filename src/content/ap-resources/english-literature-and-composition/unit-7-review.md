Read the big ideas and the method table, then try the six questions without notes. Each question combines two or more Unit 7 topics.

These are **original Marlbridge practice questions**, not past exam questions. All passages and the model essay were written by Marlbridge. The rubrics are a **suggested Marlbridge rubric**, not official scoring.

## Big ideas of the unit

- **Change comes from conflict.** A character changes, gradually or in an epiphany, or refuses to, and the group around them can act as a character ([7.1](/advanced-course-resources/english-literature-and-composition/7-1-character-change-complex-relationships-study-guide/)).
- **Setting does more than place a story.** It conveys values, marks shifts and reveals the characters who inhabit it ([7.2](/advanced-course-resources/english-literature-and-composition/7-2-setting-relationship-character-study-guide/)).
- **Time is managed.** Pace, order and withheld information shape emotion and meaning ([7.3](/advanced-course-resources/english-literature-and-composition/7-3-plot-order-sequence-study-guide/)).
- **Voice can be trusted or doubted.** Signs of unreliability and contradictory narrators make the reader an interpreter ([7.4](/advanced-course-resources/english-literature-and-composition/7-4-narrator-reliability-study-guide/)).
- **Images accumulate.** Motifs and symbolic settings emphasise ideas across a text ([7.5](/advanced-course-resources/english-literature-and-composition/7-5-symbol-imagery-fiction-study-guide/)); similes and personification choose traits to convey attitude ([7.6](/advanced-course-resources/english-literature-and-composition/7-6-simile-personification-fiction-study-guide/)).
- **An argument needs structure and proof.** Thesis, commentary and sophistication moves, supported by relevant and sufficient evidence ([7.7](/advanced-course-resources/english-literature-and-composition/7-7-thesis-commentary-study-guide/), [7.8](/advanced-course-resources/english-literature-and-composition/7-8-selecting-relevant-sufficient-evidence-study-guide/)).

## Key relationships and methods

| If the question asks you to... | Use... | Watch out for... |
|---|---|---|
| Explain change | Before, pressure, turn, after, function | Calling any realisation an epiphany |
| Analyse setting | Details, values, shifts, behaviour in place | Description without analysis |
| Analyse pacing | Expansion, compression, order of information | "Builds suspense" with no cause |
| Judge a narrator | Signs of unreliability; vantage and motive | Treating unreliable as lying |
| Explain imagery or symbol | Collect, group, state idea, track change, function | Fixed meanings with no evidence |
| Explain a simile | Parts, trait, connotations, attitude, function | "Paints a picture" |
| Write an argument | Defensible thesis, line of reasoning, audit, commentary | Quotation without commentary |

## Question 1 (multiple choice · 7.1 and 7.2)

**Passage written by Marlbridge.**

> At the Rectory, Nora had always spoken in the soft, quick voice of someone who expects to be interrupted. On the mountain, on the second day, she found she was giving instructions to the guide, and he was listening.

Which interpretation is best supported?

- (A) The mountain is warmer than the Rectory.
- (B) The guide is her servant.
- (C) Nora is rude to the guide.
- (D) The change of setting accompanies a change in Nora's behaviour: away from the Rectory's pressures she speaks with authority, and the contrast suggests that the setting has been constraining her.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (D).** The two settings are tied to two ways of speaking. The change in her voice marks a shift, and how she behaves in each place reveals the values each setting carries.

- (A) is not mentioned.
- (B) is unsupported.
- (C) goes beyond the evidence; giving instructions is not rudeness.

Topics: 7.1 (change), 7.2 (setting and character).
</details>

## Question 2 (multiple choice · 7.3 and 7.4)

**Passage written by Marlbridge.**

> I will give the morning in full, so that you may judge for yourselves. At six I rose. At seven I dressed. At eight I breakfasted on dry bread. At nine I was in the lane. At nine and one minute I saw him, and from that minute I will say only that I was entirely sober.

Which statement best describes how pace and reliability work together?

- (A) The passage proves that the narrator is sober.
- (B) The passage is a flashback with a reliable narrator.
- (C) The narrator slows the account to a crisp list of hours up to the crucial minute, then compresses the crucial event into a protest, so that the omission and the insistence on sobriety suggest he is hiding what happened.
- (D) The list of hours shows that the narrator is lazy.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (C).** The narrator gives dense detail for an unimportant stretch and almost nothing for the crucial moment. That distribution of pace, plus the over-insistent claim of sobriety, are signs of unreliability.

- (A) takes the narrator's claim at face value.
- (B) misidentifies the structure and ignores the signs of unreliability.
- (D) is irrelevant to the pace or the narrator's reliability.

Topics: 7.3 (pacing), 7.4 (reliability).
</details>

## Question 3 (multiple choice · 7.5 and 7.6)

**Passage written by Marlbridge.**

> Frost had come to the allotments in the night. It lay on the cabbages like a sheet over furniture in a house that would not be lived in again. Old Mr Venn walked between the rows with his hands behind him, and the frost, as he passed, took the shape of each footprint and kept it.

Which statement best explains the function of the imagery and the figurative language?

- (A) The footprints prove that Mr Venn is lost.
- (B) The frost is only a weather report.
- (C) The simile shows that the cabbages are valuable.
- (D) The simile compares the frost to a sheet over furniture, which presents the allotments as a closed-up house, and the frost's keeping of each footprint suggests a record of loss that fits the motif of absence.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (D).** The simile selects the traits of covering and abandonment, the personification-like keeping of footprints suggests a memory, and together they build a mood of absence.

- (A) goes beyond the evidence.
- (B) ignores the figurative language.
- (C) misreads the simile's traits.

Topics: 7.5 (imagery and motif), 7.6 (simile and personification).
</details>

## Question 4 (written response · 7.1, 7.2, 7.7 and 7.8)

**Passage written by Marlbridge.**

> On the final morning of the ferry's service, Dorrit Haig arrived at the slip an hour early, as she had on every morning of the thirty years she had kept the ticket hut. The river was the colour of a pewter plate. She swept the hut, which was swept; she wiped the counter, which was clean; she took the brass bell down from its hook and polished it with her sleeve until it gave back a small, bright, distorted Dorrit.
>
> At nine the first passengers came, and for once there were enough of them to make a crowd: men in their good coats, children let off school, a photographer from the county paper who asked her to stand by the bell. She stood by the bell. "Does it feel strange?" he asked. "It feels like Tuesday," said Dorrit, and rang it, once, as she always did, and the sound went out across the water like a person leaving a room.
>
> When the boat had gone she locked the hut, tested the lock, and walked up the hill without looking back, though at the top, where the road bends, she stopped for a long time as if she had been called.

Write an essay (or a full plan with a thesis and three claims) analysing how the narrator uses literary elements and techniques to portray Dorrit's response to the end of the ferry service. Allow about 40 minutes.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Sample high-quality response written by Marlbridge.**

Through ritual detail, a setting that records the end of a way of life, and one carefully placed simile, the narrator presents Dorrit meeting loss with a composure so complete that it becomes its own form of grief, and then allows the composure to give way in the passage's last image.

The narrator first shows composure as routine. Dorrit "swept the hut, which was swept" and "wiped the counter, which was clean". The repeated clause, in which the second half cancels the need for the first, turns her work into a ritual with no practical purpose. She is not cleaning; she is holding the day in the shape of other days. The polished bell "gave back a small, bright, distorted Dorrit", an image that suggests both pride in her work and a loss of clear self: in the one object that has marked her thirty years she sees only a shrunken, warped version.

The occasion then breaks into the routine. The crowd of "men in their good coats" and the photographer make the end public, but Dorrit answers the question "Does it feel strange?" with "It feels like Tuesday". The deflection refuses the occasion's drama, and the narrator follows it with the bell's single ring, as always, and a simile: the sound goes out "like a person leaving a room". The simile selects the trait of quiet departure, and by comparing sound to a person it makes the ending of the service feel like a leave-taking that Dorrit will not allow herself.

A reader might take her composure as a sign that she feels nothing. But the details argue the opposite. She "tested the lock", a check on something that no longer matters, which shows that the ritual continues because she cannot stop it. The final image tells the same story: she walks uphill "without looking back", yet stops at the bend "for a long time as if she had been called". The slowing of the sentence, after the brisk series of actions, gives her a moment of private response.

The passage therefore suggests that for Dorrit, and for a community that is losing a small service, the ending of a way of life is felt not in speeches but in the habits that cannot be given up.

**Suggested Marlbridge rubric (6 points, for self-assessment only).**

| Row | What to look for | Points |
|---|---|---|
| Thesis | A defensible interpretation that responds to the prompt and previews a line of reasoning | 0 to 1 |
| Evidence and commentary | Specific, relevant evidence with commentary that explains how it supports each claim, across the passage | 0 to 4 (1 for general references; 2 for some evidence with mostly summary; 3 for relevant evidence with some explanation; 4 for consistently specific evidence and explanation that supports a line of reasoning) |
| Sophistication and control | Integrates an alternative interpretation, broader significance or analogy, and writes with clear, controlled sentences | 0 to 1 |

**How the model meets the rubric.** The thesis claims a specific interpretation (composure as grief) and a direction (routine, deflection, release). The evidence moves through the passage in order and every detail is explained: the repeated clause, the bell, the answer "It feels like Tuesday", the simile of sound, the lock and the final stop. The paragraph beginning "A reader might take her composure" answers an alternative reading, and the last paragraph places the interpretation in a broader context.

**Note.** This sample is original and illustrative, not an official sample or scoring guideline.
</details>

## Question 5 (multiple choice · 7.7 and 7.8)

**Context written by Marlbridge.** A student's thesis about the Dorrit passage is "Dorrit hides her grief behind routine." For the first body paragraph she has only one piece of evidence: "She swept the hut, which was swept." Which revision would most improve the paragraph?

- (A) Add another adjective to the thesis.
- (B) Add evidence for the "grief" part of the claim, such as the final stop at the bend of the road, and explain how each piece supports the claim.
- (C) Replace the quotation with a plot summary.
- (D) Delete the claim about routine.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (B).** The claim has two parts, routine and grief. The existing evidence supports routine only. Adding evidence of grief and commentary for each piece makes the support sufficient.

- (A) does not add evidence.
- (C) replaces specific evidence with summary.
- (D) removes half of the claim.

Topics: 7.7 (thesis and commentary), 7.8 (relevant and sufficient evidence).
</details>

## Question 6 (written response · 7.4 and 7.8)

**Passages written by Marlbridge.** Two accounts of a village fair.

> *The innkeeper:* The fair was orderly as ever. I stood at the door all afternoon and saw no trouble.
>
> *The constable:* I made seven arrests before five o'clock, and every one of them had come from the inn.

Explain what the contradiction invites the reader to do, and say what two further pieces of evidence would help to decide between the accounts.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Model response.** The contradiction between "orderly as ever" and seven arrests invites the reader to weigh two perspectives. The innkeeper has a stake in the inn's reputation and may be minimising trouble, and he reports only what he saw from the door. The constable can report arrests but may be interested in showing his diligence. To decide, a reader would want evidence about what each could see (for example, whether the innkeeper's door faces the street where the arrests took place) and evidence from a neutral source, such as a third character's account or a record of the arrests. The contradiction may also be the point: it shows that the fair looked different from the inn and from the road.

**Suggested Marlbridge rubric (4 points).** One point for identifying the contradiction; one for considering each speaker's vantage point or motive; one for naming relevant further evidence (a neutral account, a record, a detail about position); one for explaining what the contradiction suggests or reveals.
</details>
