## Recap

- The **narrator** is a created voice that filters events for the reader.
- A narrator's **perspective** controls the details and emphases that affect how readers experience a text.
- Perspective shows in **diction**, **syntax**, **tone** and **what is included or omitted**.
- Readers infer **bias** from patterns in these choices. A reader who detects bias may find the narrator **less reliable**, which in turn changes how we read the characters the narrator describes.

## Evidence table

| Evidence | What to notice | Example of a comment |
|---|---|---|
| Diction | Loaded words, repeated terms, similes | "The simile 'like sheep' belittles the crew" |
| Syntax | Order and length of clauses; what is placed in subordinate clauses | "The act is tucked into a clause, softening it" |
| Tone | Defensive, mocking, admiring, calm | "'I will say only' sounds modest but introduces a catalogue of charges" |
| Omission | What the narrator does not explain | "We are not told what Garrick actually did" |
| Contradiction | Another voice, a document, an event | "The boy's 'Only the biscuit' undercuts the captain" |

## Reliability toolkit

1. What is the narrator's **stake**?
2. What is **left out**?
3. How does the narrator describe people they oppose?
4. Does anyone or anything **contradict** the account?
5. Does the **tone fit** the events?
6. What does the narrator **know**, and what can they not know?

## Assumptions

- Reliability is graded. A narrator may be reliable on events but biased on motives.
- You need evidence for a claim of bias: quote the word or point to the gap.
- Bias is useful to a reader: it reveals the narrator's values and position.

## Mistakes to avoid

- Writing "the narrator is unreliable" and stopping.
- Treating the narrator as the author.
- Believing that unreliable means lying.
- Ignoring what is omitted.
- Dismissing the narrator's whole account because of one slip.

## Quick self-check

**1.** A narrator describes a rival as "a clever, restless, thankless creature" and the narrator's own conduct as "a plain duty". What does the diction suggest?

**2.** A narrator's account says "everyone was in bed by ten". A neighbour later mentions seeing a lit window at midnight. What should you do with this?

**3.** True or false: a third-person narrator cannot be biased.

**4.** What is the difference between a narrator's tone and a narrator's reliability?

**Answers.**

1. The narrator judges the rival harshly and flatters themself; the loaded adjectives and the self-justifying "plain duty" suggest bias in the narrator's favour.
2. Treat it as a contradiction: it casts doubt on the account, and you should ask what else may have been left out and why.
3. False. A third-person narrator can still emphasise some details and omit others, or reveal partiality through word choice.
4. Tone is the attitude expressed (for example defensive or mocking); reliability is how far the reader can trust the account. A defensive tone can be a clue to reduced reliability.
