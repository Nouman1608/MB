When a poet wants you to understand something, the quickest route is often not an explanation but a sensation: the sound of gnats, the feel of a stone, the look of a sea from a great height. This topic teaches you to identify images, to say what they appeal to, and to explain how a collection of images, called **imagery**, emphasises ideas in a poem.

## What an image is

An **image** represents something through associations with the senses. The senses include sight, sound, touch, taste and smell, and also the feeling of movement or temperature. An image may be:

- **literal**: it simply presents a sensory detail ("the stubble-plains"), or
- **a form of comparison**: it presents the subject by likening it to something sensory (a "wrinkled sea", as if the sea had skin).

Descriptive words, such as adjectives and adverbs, contribute to sensory imagery. *Wrinkled* adds touch and age to the sea; *soft-dying* adds quietness and slow ending to the day. Verbs matter too: a sea that "crawls" has a different quality from one that "sparkles".

A single image focuses the reader on a moment. A **collection of images**, known as **imagery**, may emphasise ideas in parts of a text or throughout it. When several images share a sense or a quality (all cold, all high, all small), the pattern sends a message that no single image carries alone.

1. **Image.** Quote the exact words, not just "the imagery of nature".
2. **Sense.** Say which sense or senses it appeals to: sight, sound, touch, movement.
3. **Association.** Say what the image links the subject with: age, danger, tenderness, power.
4. **Effect.** Explain what that association does to the speaker's attitude or the idea of the poem.

When you have two or more images, add a fifth move: **pattern**. Ask what they share and what changes from the first to the last.

## Worked example 1: a small poem built from images

Alfred, Lord Tennyson's "The Eagle" (1851) has six lines:

> He clasps the crag with crooked hands;
> Close to the sun in lonely lands,
> Ring'd with the azure world, he stands.
>
> The wrinkled sea beneath him crawls;
> He watches from his mountain walls,
> And like a thunderbolt he falls.

**Step 1, images.** "clasps the crag with crooked hands"; "Close to the sun in lonely lands"; "Ring'd with the azure world"; "The wrinkled sea beneath him crawls"; "mountain walls"; "like a thunderbolt he falls".

**Step 2, senses.** The first stanza appeals to touch and shape (*clasps*, *crooked hands*), sight (*azure*, *sun*) and a sense of height and isolation (*lonely lands*). The second stanza adds the look of a distant sea (*wrinkled*), movement (*crawls*) and the force of a sudden fall (*thunderbolt*, a sight and a sound).

**Step 3, associations.** "Hands" for talons gives the bird human grip and will, and "crooked" suggests age and strength. "Ring'd with the azure world" places him at the centre of a vast circle of sky. The sea "wrinkled" and "crawling" looks slow, old and small from above, while "mountain walls" make the crag a fortress. "Thunderbolt" links his dive with lightning and speed.

**Step 4, effect and pattern.** The imagery moves from stillness to motion. The first stanza gathers images of height, grip and isolation, so the eagle looks lordly and watchful; the second makes the world below small and slow ("crawls") by contrast, until the last line unleashes speed ("thunderbolt") with a comparison. The pattern shifts from fixed to moving, from power held to power released, which is the poem's whole idea.

**A model sentence.** Tennyson fills the first stanza with images of grip and height ("clasps the crag", "Close to the sun") and the second with a slow, wrinkled sea, so that the "thunderbolt" simile releases the eagle's held power into sudden, absolute speed.

## Worked example 2: imagery of sound and light

John Keats's "To Autumn" (1820) ends with a stanza that answers its own question, "Where are the songs of Spring?" The speaker replies, "thou hast thy music too". The images are of dusk and of small sounds:

> While barred clouds bloom the soft-dying day
> And touch the stubble-plains with rosy hue;
> Then in a wailful choir the small gnats mourn
> Among the river sallows, borne aloft
> Or sinking as the light wind lives or dies;
> And full-grown lambs loud bleat from hilly bourn;
> Hedge-crickets sing, and now with treble soft
> The redbreast whistles from a garden croft;
> And gathering swallows twitter in the skies.

(Text as printed in nineteenth-century anthologies; punctuation varies between editions.)

**Step 1, images.** "barred clouds bloom the soft-dying day"; "stubble-plains with rosy hue"; "a wailful choir the small gnats mourn"; "borne aloft / Or sinking as the light wind lives or dies"; "full-grown lambs loud bleat"; "Hedge-crickets sing"; "treble soft"; "gathering swallows twitter".

**Step 2, senses.** The first lines are visual (*barred clouds*, *rosy hue*); the rest are mostly auditory (*wailful*, *bleat*, *sing*, *whistles*, *twitter*), together with movement (*borne aloft / Or sinking*).

**Step 3, associations.** "Soft-dying" joins gentleness and ending. *Wailful choir* and *mourn* give the gnats the sound of grief, yet *choir* and *music* make the sound sacred and chosen. The gnats "sinking as the light wind lives or dies" gives wind a life and death, so the soundscape becomes a small, living thing. The swallows "gathering" suggests preparation for departure.

**Step 4, effect and pattern.** The poem answers the loss of spring's songs with a quieter music of small sounds. The images shift from sight to sound and from large (clouds) to small (gnats, crickets, swallows), the volume falls to "treble soft", and the last image points toward leaving. The imagery builds a mood of gentle, accepting farewell and shows that the season's beauty lies in what is small and fading.

**A model sentence.** Keats builds the final stanza from soft, small sounds ("treble soft", "twitter") and fading light ("soft-dying day"), so that the imagery turns the end of the year into a quiet music the reader is asked to hear and accept.

## Literal and comparative images side by side

It helps to see the two kinds of image next to each other. The lines below were written by Marlbridge.

| Line | Kind of image | Sense | What it does |
|---|---|---|---|
| "Rain stitched the window shut." | Comparison (rain as sewing) | Sight, sound | Makes the rain a closing-in; the house feels sealed |
| "The kettle ticked as it cooled." | Literal | Sound | Gives a quiet, domestic stillness |
| "Her laugh was cold water down the hall." | Comparison (laugh as water) | Sound, touch | Suggests a sudden, startling relief |
| "The bread smelled of Sunday." | Comparison (smell as a day) | Smell | Links a sensation to memory and routine |

A literal image gives the sensation itself; a comparative image gives the sensation by likening it to something else, and so carries an extra association. Both can be powerful. What matters for analysis is saying which you are looking at and what it adds.

## Tracking imagery through a whole poem

When a poem has many images, make a quick table in your margin before you write. A three-column table is enough: the image, its sense, and what it suggests. Then look down the columns.

| Image (quoted) | Sense | Suggests |
|---|---|---|
| "Close to the sun in lonely lands" | Sight, space | Height, isolation, pride |
| "The wrinkled sea beneath him crawls" | Sight, movement | Age, slowness, smallness from above |
| "like a thunderbolt he falls" | Sight, sound, speed | Violent, sudden power |

Reading down the third column, the poem's main idea comes into view: power seen from a great height, then released. Notice that the pattern has a shape (stillness then speed), and an essay that describes the shape will say more than one that lists images. A useful habit is to write one sentence that begins "The images move from... to...". Good answers about imagery often rest on that sentence.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Imagery means description."** Images are chosen sensory appeals that carry associations; the question is what they suggest, not only what they show.
- **"Imagery is only visual."** Sound, touch, movement and temperature all count.
- **"Naming the sense is enough."** "This is visual imagery" earns little; link the image to an effect.
- **"Every image is a comparison."** Many images are literal. Say whether the image presents or likens.
- **"One image carries the poem."** Look for the collection and its pattern: what the images share and how they change.
- **"Adjectives are decoration."** Descriptive words build the sensory quality, as *wrinkled* and *soft-dying* show.
