These are **original Marlbridge practice questions**, not past exam questions. Passages are short extracts from public-domain works (author, title and date given) or an original prose passage written by Marlbridge, labelled as such. Mark points are a suggested Marlbridge rubric, not an official scoring guideline. Questions 1 to 6 are multiple choice; Questions 7 and 8 are written responses. Allow about 50 minutes. No calculator is needed.

## Question 1 (multiple choice · foundation)

In Chapter 31 of Mark Twain's *Adventures of Huckleberry Finn* (1884), Huck holds the letter that would betray Jim and says to himself: "All right, then, I'll go to hell", and tears it up. What does this choice most clearly reveal?

- (A) Huck wants to be punished.
- (B) Huck's loyalty to Jim outweighs the morality he has been taught, even though he believes the choice damns him.
- (C) Huck is careless and does not think about consequences.
- (D) Huck has lost the letter by accident.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (B).** The choice costs him what he believes is his soul, and the text shows him deliberating ("It was a close place"). His loyalty, built on shared experience, wins over his inherited beliefs.

- (A) is not supported; he does not seek punishment.
- (C) is contradicted by his "a-trembling" deliberation.
- (D) ignores that he tears it up deliberately.

</details>

## Question 2 (multiple choice · core)

In the same chapter Huck says, "You can't pray a lie—I found that out." Which statement best explains the effect of the gap between what Huck thinks he is doing and what the reader sees?

- (A) It shows that Huck is lying to the reader.
- (B) It shows that the narrator of the novel is the author.
- (C) It creates irony: the reader perceives the moral value of his act while Huck, limited by the teachings of his society, does not, and the irony criticises that society.
- (D) It makes Huck look stupid and the novel comic.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (C).** The reader understands more than the narrator: Huck calls his act wicked, but we see it as honourable. The irony points at a society whose moral teaching misleads him.

- (A) has no basis; Huck is sincere.
- (B) confuses author and narrator; Huck is a character whose limits the author uses.
- (D) is wrong; the novel treats Huck's struggle seriously.

</details>

## Question 3 (multiple choice · core)

In *Macbeth* (about 1606), Macbeth responds to the news of his wife's death with "She should have died hereafter. / There would have been a time for such a word." Why is this response complex?

- (A) It shows that he is pleased by the news.
- (B) It shows that he did not love her.
- (C) It is a plain, unambiguous statement of grief.
- (D) It is ambiguous (she ought to have died later, or she would have died someday anyway), withholds the grief we expect, and reveals the numbness that has replaced his earlier feeling for his "dearest partner of greatness".

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (D).** The line supports two readings, and in either case the absence of the expected grief reveals the character's emptied state. The "dearest partner" of his earlier letter makes the contrast sharper.

- (A) has no support.
- (B) overstates; the numbness is not the same as lack of love.
- (C) ignores the ambiguity and the withholding of grief.

</details>

## Question 4 (multiple choice · core)

Read this passage, written by Marlbridge.

> Nell told the neighbours she needed nobody. She said it at the gate and again at the baker's, and on the evening her brother's train was due she said it to the cat. But she ironed two cloths, though one would have done; she put the good cups out, and when the train was late she moved the kettle forward and back, forward and back, until she heard the latch.

Which statement best describes the complexity of Nell?

- (A) What she says (that she needs nobody) is contradicted by what she does (she prepares carefully for her brother), which shows she wants company she will not admit to.
- (B) She is truthful about her independence.
- (C) She dislikes her brother.
- (D) She is cruel to her cat.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (A).** The speech and the actions pull apart. The extra cloth, the good cups and the anxious kettle show a need her words deny.

- (B) takes her speech at face value and ignores her actions.
- (C) is contradicted by her preparation.
- (D) misreads "said it to the cat" as cruelty.

</details>

## Question 5 (multiple choice · core)

Which of the following is the strongest evidence that a character is **complex**, as opposed to simply inconsistent?

- (A) The character has an unusual name.
- (B) The character's choices show a pull between two values, and the work uses that pull to reveal something about the character or the society.
- (C) The character is mentioned in several chapters.
- (D) The character says different things on different days.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (B).** Complexity involves a meaningful tension between motives or values that the work puts to use.

- (A) is a surface detail.
- (C) concerns frequency, not complexity.
- (D) could describe a careless writer or a liar; it does not say what the variation means.

</details>

## Question 6 (multiple choice · stretch)

How does Huck's complexity (in Chapter 31) affect the reader's interpretation of him as a **narrator**?

- (A) It shows that he is limited as an interpreter of his own actions, so the reader must trust what he does more than what he says about himself.
- (B) It shows that he is an entirely reliable narrator.
- (C) It shows that he is not the narrator.
- (D) It shows that he narrates the novel in order to confess.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (A).** Huck narrates in good faith but misjudges the moral meaning of his own deed. That invites the reader to look through his explanations to his actions.

- (B) is wrong: his judgement of himself is unreliable even if his reporting is honest.
- (C) is contradicted by the first-person narration.
- (D) has no textual support.

</details>

## Question 7 (written response · core)

Write a paragraph of about 150 words explaining how Huck's choice in Chapter 31 reveals complexity and what that complexity does in the novel. Use at least two short quotations.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Sample response (written by Marlbridge).** Huck's choice reveals a conscience that works against the morality he was taught. He believes that helping Jim is a sin, and he tries to pray but cannot: "You can't pray a lie—I found that out." Holding the letter, he knows that it is "a close place" and that he must "decide, forever, betwixt two things". When he says, "All right, then, I'll go to hell", he treats the act as damnation, yet the reader sees it as the novel's moral high point. The gap between how Huck judges his act and how we judge it creates irony, and the irony criticises a society whose moral teaching would make a decent act feel wicked. It also teaches the reader to trust Huck's actions over his explanations, a pattern that shapes how we read the rest of the story.

**Suggested Marlbridge rubric (4 points)**

| Point | What earns it |
|---|---|
| 1 | Identifies the tension (taught belief versus loyalty). |
| 2 | Uses at least two accurate quotations from choice, action or speech. |
| 3 | Explains what the choice reveals and what it does (irony, criticism of society). |
| 4 | Connects outward (narrator's reliability, other episodes, the work's meaning). |

</details>

## Question 8 (written response · stretch)

Using the passage about Nell (Question 4), write a paragraph of about 150 words explaining how her speech and actions reveal complexity and what the contrast does in the passage.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Sample response (written by Marlbridge).** Nell's speech and actions pull in opposite directions, and the passage uses the contrast to show a loneliness she will not name. She tells everyone she "needed nobody", repeating it "at the gate", "at the baker's" and finally "to the cat", a progression in which the audience shrinks until the statement is addressed to no one who could contradict it. Yet she irons "two cloths, though one would have done" and puts out "the good cups", and when the train is late the repeated "forward and back, forward and back" shows her anxiety. The narrator reports these details without comment, so the reader must infer what they mean. The complexity is that her declaration of independence is itself a sign of need, and the passage makes the reader sympathetic to a pride that conceals longing.

**Suggested Marlbridge rubric (4 points)**

| Point | What earns it |
|---|---|
| 1 | Identifies the contrast between speech and action. |
| 2 | Uses at least two accurate details or quotations. |
| 3 | Explains what the contrast reveals about Nell's values or feelings. |
| 4 | Explains what the contrast does (sympathy, irony, the narrator's restraint). |

</details>
