## Recap

- A **thesis** expresses an **interpretation** of a literary text and needs a **defence**: textual evidence plus a **line of reasoning**.
- A thesis **may preview** the line of reasoning; it need not list points, devices or evidence.
- A **line of reasoning** is the logical sequence of claims that work together to defend the thesis.
- **Commentary** explains the relationship between evidence, claims and thesis. Without it, you have a list of quotations.

## Thesis checklist

| Question | If "no" |
|---|---|
| Is it an interpretation (about meaning or function), not a fact or a summary? | Add a claim about what the choice means |
| Could a thoughtful reader disagree? | It is probably not defensible; make it more specific |
| Is it specific to this work and this question? | Name the choice and its effect |
| Does it say why it matters? | Add the significance |
| Can I see the direction of the argument? | Preview the line of reasoning |

## Four-move commentary chain

1. **Interpret**: what does the evidence show?
2. **Explain how**: which words or choices produce that meaning?
3. **Connect to the claim**: how does this support the topic claim?
4. **Connect to the thesis**: how does it advance the whole argument?

## Weak to strong

| Weak | Strong |
|---|---|
| "Macbeth kills Duncan." (fact) | "Shakespeare makes Macbeth's conscience argue against his crime, so that the murder reads as betrayal, not ignorance." |
| "The author uses imagery." (device) | "The recurring blood imagery makes guilt a physical presence the characters cannot wash away." |
| "This shows that she is worried." (restates) | "The specific list, ending on the 'winter that was coming', shows that she knows precisely what the refusal costs." |

## Assumptions

- The thesis can develop as you gather evidence. Check at the end that it matches what you proved.
- You do not need a surprising thesis. You need a defensible and specific one.
- Every body claim should serve the thesis. If it does not, revise one or the other.

## Mistakes to avoid

- Stating a fact and calling it a thesis.
- Listing devices instead of making a claim.
- Presenting three examples as a line of reasoning.
- Quoting without explaining.
- Letting commentary stop at restatement.

## Quick self-check

**1.** Is "Hamlet is a play about revenge" a thesis? Why or why not?

**2.** Fix the sentence: "Dickens uses symbolism to show Miss Havisham's character."

**3.** A student has three body paragraphs on blood, a dagger and sleepwalking, in that order, but could swap them freely. What does this suggest?

**4.** Which commentary is stronger? (a) "This quotation shows guilt." (b) "The verb 'wash' turns guilt into a task that cannot be completed, so the stain becomes a measure of how far she has fallen from her earlier confidence."

**Answers.**

1. No. It is a topic or a general statement; it makes no defensible claim about meaning or function.
2. For example: "By having Miss Havisham stop the clocks and keep her wedding dress, Dickens presents her as a person who has made a past injury into a permanent condition, and thereby shows the cost of refusing to let time move."
3. The line of reasoning may be a list. The claims should build on each other so that the order matters.
4. (b). It interprets, explains how the word works and connects to a larger claim.
