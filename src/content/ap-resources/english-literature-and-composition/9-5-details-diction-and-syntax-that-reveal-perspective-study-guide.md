## Perspective: who is looking, and how can you tell?

Every story is told from somewhere. A **narrator** or a **character** sees events from a position: close or far, informed or ignorant, fond or hostile, honest or self-deceiving. That position is the **perspective**, and it controls what the reader is given. It decides which details are noticed, what is emphasised and what is left out, and how events are judged. Because a reader experiences the work only through that perspective, understanding it is understanding how the work wants to be read.

You cannot see perspective directly; you infer it from three kinds of evidence.

- **Details.** What the narrator chooses to mention, and what is passed over. A narrator who notices the state of a doorstep before the face of its owner has told you what matters to him.
- **Diction.** The words chosen, including their register (formal or colloquial) and connotation. Word choice carries judgement: calling someone "the rightful property" of another is not neutral.
- **Syntax.** The shape of sentences. A long, balanced, qualified sentence suggests control and irony; a run of short blunt ones suggests flatness, shock or impatience; an additive "and... and..." pattern suggests a speaker who thinks aloud and does not subordinate. You do not need technical labels; describe the pattern in plain words and say what it does.

The course also asks you to recognise that perspective is rarely simple.

- **Multiple, even contrasting, perspectives** can occur in one text, and the tension between them contributes to its complexity. A narrator's view may differ from a character's, or from the society's.
- **A narrator or speaker may change** over the course of a text, as a result of actions and interactions.
- **Changes and inconsistencies** in perspective may produce **irony** or complexity: a gap between what the narrator sees and what the reader sees.

## A method for perspective

1. **Identify the source.** Who is telling, or whose view are we given? First person, close third, omniscient?
2. **Collect the evidence.** List details noticed, key words (with their connotations) and the shape of the sentences.
3. **Describe the perspective.** Use a precise phrase: wry and detached; naive but candid; fondly amused; self-justifying. Mention limits or bias.
4. **Check for contrast or change.** Is there another perspective in the passage (a character's, a society's, the narrator's earlier self)? Does the perspective shift?
5. **Say what it does.** What does this perspective let the reader see, or hide? Does it create irony, sympathy or distance?

## Worked example 1: a narrator who sets a view against a society's (*Pride and Prejudice*)

Jane Austen's *Pride and Prejudice* (1813) begins:

> It is a truth universally acknowledged, that a single man in possession of a good fortune must be in want of a wife.
>
> However little known the feelings or views of such a man may be on his first entering a neighbourhood, this truth is so well fixed in the minds of the surrounding families, that he is considered as the rightful property of some one or other of their daughters.

**Source.** A third-person narrator who knows more than the characters and speaks with authority.

**Evidence.**

- *Syntax.* The first sentence is formal and balanced: it begins with a general statement ("It is a truth universally acknowledged") and delays its content to the end. The second is long and heavily qualified ("However little known... on his first entering a neighbourhood"), moving through concessions before it reaches its point.
- *Diction.* "Universally acknowledged" is grand and sweeping for a claim about marriage; "must be in want of" sounds like a law. In the second sentence, "rightful property" uses the language of ownership.
- *Details.* The narrator admits that the man's own "feelings or views" may be "little known", and turns at once to the minds of the "surrounding families". The man's wishes are not the issue; the families' are.

**Perspective.** The narrator is ironic and detached. She presents a social assumption as if it were a universal law, and then reveals whose interest it serves: the families with daughters. The perspective is that of a shrewd observer who sees the mismatch between how society speaks about marriage (as a rule about what men need) and how it behaves (as a pursuit of husbands).

**Contrast.** There are two perspectives in the passage: the community's, which treats a rich bachelor as a prize, and the narrator's, which stands slightly outside it. The tension between the two is the source of the irony: the "truth" is true only for people who think of marriage as a market.

**What it does.** It sets the novel's tone from the first sentence, establishes the narrator as a source of ironic commentary, and signals that the story will examine how money and marriage are linked.

*Model commentary:* "Austen's grand first sentence adopts the manner of a universal law, but the second sentence's 'rightful property' reveals whose desires it serves, so the narrator's ironic distance is established through the contrast between formal syntax and mercenary diction."

## Worked example 2: a narrator whose voice undercuts his claims (*Huckleberry Finn*)

Mark Twain's *Adventures of Huckleberry Finn* (1884) begins in Huck's own voice:

> You don't know about me without you have read a book by the name of The Adventures of Tom Sawyer; but that ain't no matter. That book was made by Mr. Mark Twain, and he told the truth, mainly. There was things which he stretched, but mainly he told the truth. That is nothing. I never seen anybody but lied one time or another, without it was Aunt Polly, or the widow, or maybe Mary.

**Source.** A first-person narrator, a boy, speaking in the vernacular.

**Evidence.**

- *Diction.* The language is colloquial and non-standard: "ain't no matter", "There was things", "without it was". It presents a speaker who does not pretend to be literary.
- *Syntax.* The sentences are short and loosely joined, as in speech: "That is nothing." The word "mainly" is repeated, a hedge that qualifies the judgment.
- *Details.* Huck's first comment on another writer is whether he told the truth; his second is that he has never seen anyone who did not lie, apart from three people, one of them hedged with "maybe".

**Perspective.** Huck is candid and unpretentious, sceptical of grand claims and of adults' honesty, and he speaks as a plain witness. The perspective is that of someone who does not mind admitting that truth is mostly a matter of degree.

**Contrast and change.** The reader has a second perspective, Twain's, implied by the joke that the narrator treats the author as another character, and we are invited to read Huck's candour as both sincere and comically limited. Later in the novel his perspective changes through his experience with Jim: in Chapter 31 he judges himself by a conscience that the reader sees past. The gap between what Huck sees and what the reader sees produces irony.

**What it does.** The opening teaches the reader to trust Huck's honesty about small things while watching for the limits of his understanding, and the colloquial voice makes the story's moral questions arrive as ordinary speech.

*Model commentary:* "Huck's repeated 'mainly' and his easy 'ain't no matter' establish a narrator who qualifies his own truth-telling without embarrassment, so the vernacular voice presents honesty as modest and human, and prepares the reader to see through his judgments later in the novel."

| Weak | Why it falls short | Stronger |

| "The narrator is ironic." | A label. | "The narrator's formal syntax and the phrase 'rightful property' give an ironic account of how a society treats a rich bachelor." |
| "Huck's language is informal." | Describes style, not perspective. | "Huck's colloquial voice and hedged 'mainly' present a candid, unpretentious witness who admits that truth is a matter of degree." |
| "The narrator has a view." | Empty. | "The narrator sees society's talk of 'a truth universally acknowledged' as a cover for its own interest." |
| "The sentences are long." | Describes form with no effect. | "The long, qualified second sentence delays its point until 'rightful property', so the irony arrives as a late jolt." |

## Common misconceptions

- **"The narrator is the author."** The narrator is a voice the author creates; attribute choices to the narrator.
- **"First-person narrators always tell the truth."** They may be mistaken, biased or self-deceiving, and the gap is a source of irony.
- **"A formal voice is neutral."** Formal diction and syntax carry judgement.
- **"I must use technical terms for syntax."** Plain description ("a long, delayed sentence") is enough if it explains the effect.
- **"Perspective is the same throughout."** It may change or be contrasted with another.
- **"Naming the perspective is the answer."** You must say what it does for the reader.
