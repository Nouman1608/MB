## Recap

- A **symbol** is an object that represents a meaning; it is **symbolic** or **representative** of that meaning.
- The move is from **literal** to **figurative**; the object stays itself in the story.
- A symbol can represent **different things** depending on the reader's experience and the context.
- **Common** symbols carry associations before a text starts; **contextual** symbols gain meaning only through their use in this text.
- A **character** can become symbolic when they come to represent an idea. A **setting** can too (Topic 7.5). You are not asked to label archetypes.

## Signals of symbolic weight

| Signal | Example of what to quote or point to |
|---|---|
| Repetition | "Spring after spring" |
| Prominence | A long description of something small |
| Character reaction | Avoiding it; guarding it; cannot part with it |
| Placement | A turning point or ending |
| Change | The object breaks, is lost, is given away |

## The four steps (plus one)

1. **Notice**: the object and the signals.
2. **Associate**: what the text connects it with.
3. **State**: the idea it represents, in a short phrase.
4. **Function**: what it does for the work (makes a feeling visible, marks change, links scenes, deepens conflict).
5. **Trace the shift** (if the meaning changes): what the change shows.

## Weak versus strong

| Weak | Strong |
|---|---|
| "The gate is a symbol." | "The gate represents Eleanor's sealed-off grief." |
| "The rose symbolizes love." (no evidence) | "The rose, given at the start and found withered after the quarrel, represents a love that cannot survive neglect." |
| "The storm is a symbol of the storm in her heart." | "By placing the storm at the moment of her decision, the narrator makes her inner turmoil visible and marks the turning point." |

## Assumptions

- Symbolic meaning must be built from evidence in the text. A common association is a start, not a finish.
- It is acceptable to offer more than one reading if the text supports both. Each reading needs evidence.
- A symbol belongs to the whole text. Check whether its meaning holds across scenes.

## Mistakes to avoid

- Treating every object as a symbol.
- Giving a symbol one fixed meaning without evidence.
- Writing "symbolizes" and stopping.
- Forgetting function.
- Ignoring how a symbol changes.

## Quick self-check

**1.** A character keeps a cracked teacup on the mantel, dusts it daily, and will not let anyone use it. Name two signals of symbolic weight.

**2.** What is the difference between a common symbol and a contextual symbol?

**3.** Improve this claim: "The bird symbolizes freedom."

**4.** A letter is at first a source of hope and later a cause of dread. What should you do in your analysis?

**Answers.**

1. Character reaction (dusts it daily, forbids use) and repetition or prominence (placed on the mantel and kept).
2. A common symbol carries associations that readers bring from before the text (a dove for peace); a contextual symbol gains its meaning from how this text uses it.
3. Add evidence and function: for example, "By having the caged bird released only after Maria decides to leave, the narrator makes the bird a figure for the freedom she lacks and then claims."
4. Trace the shift: describe the meaning at the start, the meaning at the end, and what the change shows about the characters or situation.
