## From claim to argument

Topic 6.6 set out the basics: a **thesis** that expresses an interpretation and requires a defence, a **line of reasoning** that links claims in a logical sequence, and **commentary** that explains how evidence, claims and thesis are related. In short fiction, those basics meet a particular challenge: a short story is compact, so a good argument must use its details precisely and show how the story's elements work together.

This topic revisits thesis and commentary for short-story analysis and adds the course's idea of **more sophisticated literary arguments**. These may:

1. **explain the significance or relevance of an interpretation within a broader context**;
2. **discuss alternative interpretations** of a text; or
3. **use relevant analogies** to help an audience better understand an interpretation.

These are not decorative extras. Each one deepens the argument by connecting it to something beyond a single sentence of evidence.

## The thesis for a short-fiction essay

A strong thesis about a short story:

- states a **specific interpretation** of what the story does or means;
- is **defensible**, that is, open to challenge and needing proof;
- often names a **choice** the writer makes and what it accomplishes;
- may **preview** the line of reasoning without listing points.

Revise it as you work. If the evidence in your notes does not support the claim, change the claim.

## The commentary chain

Remember the four moves: **interpret**, **explain how**, **connect to the claim**, **connect to the thesis**. Strong commentary in short fiction usually adds one more habit: **look for a pattern**. Because the text is short, the same element often appears more than once, and tracing it gives the commentary weight.

## Sophistication moves at a glance

| Move | What it does | Sentence starters | Risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Broader context** | Explains why the interpretation matters beyond the single text: for a society, an idea, human experience | "The story thereby extends beyond one marriage to ...", "This matters because ..." | Making a grand claim without evidence |
| **Alternative interpretation** | States a different reading and answers it | "A reader might see ... However, ...", "Although it is tempting to read ... as ..., the detail of ... suggests ..." | Listing readings without choosing between them |
| **Analogy** | Uses a parallel to clarify the interpretation | "Like a prisoner who mourns a kind jailer yet rejoices at an open gate, ..." | An analogy that distracts or doesn't fit |

## Worked example 1: building a thesis

Use Kate Chopin's The Story of an Hour (1894). Mrs Mallard, who has "a heart trouble", is told that her husband has died in a railway accident. She weeps, goes to her room, sits by an open window and experiences a rush of feeling that she names "free, free, free!" She goes downstairs; her husband walks in, alive; and the doctors say she died "of the joy that kills".

**Prompt (original, written by Marlbridge).** "Analyse how Chopin presents Mrs Mallard's response to the news of her husband's death, and explain what the presentation suggests about the story's wider concerns."

**Draft 1: "Mrs Mallard is sad and then happy."** A summary of her emotions; not an interpretation.

**Draft 2: "Chopin uses setting and symbolism to show her feelings."** It lists elements without a claim.

**Draft 3: "Chopin shows that Mrs Mallard feels free."** A claim, but one that most readers would accept, and it says nothing about why or what it suggests.

**Draft 4 (strong).** "By moving Mrs Mallard from a conventional outburst of grief to a private room where the open window brings spring and freedom, Chopin presents her joy as natural and long suppressed, so that the story's ironic ending condemns not a cruel husband but a marriage that leaves no space for the self."

**Why Draft 4 works.** It states a defensible interpretation (the freedom is natural and suppressed). It previews a line of reasoning (a move from conventional grief to the window, then the ending's irony). It also looks beyond the single moment, to what the ending condemns. It does not list evidence or devices.

**A line of reasoning for the essay.** (1) The conventional grief shows what Mrs Mallard is expected to feel. (2) The window and the language of spring present her new feeling as natural. (3) The marriage is shown as kind but constraining, so that her freedom is not an accusation of her husband. (4) The ending's irony shows that society leaves no space for such freedom.

## Worked example 2: commentary with an alternative interpretation and a broader context

**Claim for a body paragraph.** "The story presents the marriage as kind but constraining."

**Evidence.** Mrs Mallard knows she will weep when she sees "the face that had never looked save with love upon her". Yet she also recognises that "there would be no powerful will bending hers in that blind persistence with which men and women believe they have a right to impose a private will upon a fellow-creature."

**Commentary (with an alternative interpretation).**

1. *Interpret.* The two quotations hold together two facts: the husband was loving, and the marriage still imposed a will upon her.
2. *Alternative interpretation.* A reader might see her joy as callous, or as the effect of shock, and the husband as a tyrant whose death is welcome. However, the narrator's description of a face "that had never looked save with love" rules out the tyrant reading.
3. *Explain how.* The phrase "blind persistence" and the generalisation "men and women" shift the blame from one person to a habit of the whole society. Her husband is not singled out.
4. *Connect to the claim.* The marriage is therefore kind and constraining at once, and that combination makes her feeling harder to dismiss.
5. *Broader context.* The story's quarrel is thus with an institution, not with a person: it questions a social arrangement in which a "private will" is normally imposed in the name of love.
6. *Connect to the thesis.* This explains why the ending condemns marriage as a structure, not Brently Mallard as an individual.

**Model paragraph.** "The story presents the marriage as kind but constraining. Mrs Mallard knows that she will weep for 'the face that had never looked save with love upon her', and yet she also sees that her married life has meant a 'powerful will bending hers'. A reader might take her joy as callousness, or as the effect of shock; but the narrator's insistence on the husband's tenderness rules out the image of a tyrant. The phrase 'blind persistence' and the generalisation to 'men and women' move the blame from one man to a custom, so that the story's quarrel is with an institution in which a 'private will' is imposed in the name of love, and her freedom becomes a verdict on the arrangement, not on the man."

**An analogy (optional).** "Her situation resembles that of a prisoner who grieves for a kind jailer yet rejoices at an open gate." Use an analogy only if it clarifies the point, and keep it brief.

## A short model introduction

An introduction does not need a long preamble. It needs a clear route to the thesis. For the Chopin prompt, a compact version might read:

"Kate Chopin's The Story of an Hour compresses a marriage, a bereavement and a death into a few pages, and it is in that compression that its argument lies. By moving Mrs Mallard from a conventional outburst of grief to a private room where spring presses in, Chopin presents her joy as natural and long suppressed, so that the ending's irony condemns not a cruel husband but a marriage that leaves no space for the self."

Notice that the first sentence orients the reader without summarising the plot, and the second is the thesis. The rest of the essay then has a line of reasoning to follow.

## Self-assessment table (suggested Marlbridge rubric)

Rate a draft from 1 (not yet) to 3 (secure).

| Feature | 1 | 2 | 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Thesis | Summary or a list | General claim | Specific, defensible interpretation that previews a direction |
| Line of reasoning | A list of points | Some logic | Claims build, with each needing proof |
| Commentary | Restates evidence | Interprets | Interprets, explains how and connects to claim and thesis |
| Sophistication | None | A move that sits apart from the argument | A move that is integrated and supported by evidence |
| Control of the text | A few details | Several details | Details chosen precisely, with patterns traced |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Sophistication means big words."** It means developed, connected thinking: significance, alternatives or analogies, tied to evidence.
- **"An alternative reading weakens my thesis."** It strengthens it when you answer it with evidence.
- **"A broader context means a general statement about life."** It means a specific link, supported by the text, to an idea or social structure.
- **"An analogy always helps."** If it distracts or does not fit, leave it out.
- **"My thesis is fixed once written."** Revise it as the evidence demands.
