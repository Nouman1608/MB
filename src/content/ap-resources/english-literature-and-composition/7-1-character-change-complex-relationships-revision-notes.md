## Recap

- A character may **change**, gradually or suddenly, or may **remain unchanged**. Either has a function.
- Change **emerges from a conflict of values** or from **changes in circumstances**.
- An **epiphany** is a sudden moment of realisation that lets a character see something in a new light. It is often directly related to a central conflict and may affect the plot by prompting action.
- **Relationships** matter: a **group or force** can act as a character, and **inclusion or exclusion** reveals the group's collective attitude and possibly the character's attitude to the group.

## Five-step frame

| Step | Ask | Write down |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Before | What do they believe, want, see? | Early evidence |
| 2. Pressure | What conflict or change pushes them? | The event or choice |
| 3. Turn | Gradual or sudden? Any epiphany? | The moment and its signals |
| 4. After | What is different, or what has not moved? | Later evidence |
| 5. Function | What does this mean for the story? | Link to conflict and ending |

## Signs of an epiphany

- A shift in perception (a verb such as "saw", "knew", "realised") and often in tone or rhythm.
- Earlier details gain new meaning.
- The next action follows from the new view.
- The moment is tied to the central conflict.
- It may be **ironic**: the insight arrives too late or leads somewhere unexpected.

## Examples (public domain)

- **The Story of an Hour** (Kate Chopin, 1894): sudden realisation, "free, free, free!"; the ending "of the joy that kills" is ironic.
- **Young Goodman Brown** (Nathaniel Hawthorne, 1835): a night in the forest leaves him "a stern, a sad, a darkly meditative" man, changed in his attitude to his community.

## Assumptions

- A single change needs evidence from before and after.
- A group's behaviour can be analysed like a character's: what it does, what it values, what it suggests.
- An ending that reverses or complicates a change affects how you read the earlier change.

## Mistakes to avoid

- Assuming every character must change, or that change is always for the better.
- Calling any realisation an epiphany without linking it to the central conflict.
- Treating groups as background.
- Describing the change without naming the pressure that causes it.
- Missing the function: what does the change or lack of change mean?

## Quick self-check

**1.** A character who has always refused to forgive a brother quietly pays the brother's debt after a fire. Name what may have prompted the change and whether it is gradual or sudden.

**2.** True or false: if a character stays the same from beginning to end, the story is flawed.

**3.** In a story, the whole village falls silent when a stranger enters. What can you say about the village and the stranger?

**4.** What three things should you say about an epiphany?

**Answers.**

1. A change in circumstances (the fire) and perhaps a conflict between pride and family loyalty; it looks sudden, though the story may show earlier signs.
2. False. A refusal or inability to change can be the story's point; ask what pressure failed and what it shows.
3. The village acts as a collective character with an attitude of suspicion or exclusion; the stranger's response (defiance, ease, discomfort) shows how he or she relates to that attitude.
4. What the character realises, how it relates to the central conflict and what the character does as a result (or what the irony of the result is).
