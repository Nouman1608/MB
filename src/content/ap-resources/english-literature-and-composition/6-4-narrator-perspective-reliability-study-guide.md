## The narrator as a filter

Every story reaches the reader through a **narrator**, the voice that tells it. A narrator cannot report everything. The narrator selects which details to give, how much space to give them and how to describe them, and each of those choices carries a **perspective**.

You can picture the narrator as a filter between events and the reader.

The course puts it this way: a narrator's or speaker's **perspective controls the details and emphases that affect how readers experience and interpret a text**. That is why analysing narration is not a side issue. It is the way into almost every other element of a text.

## Four kinds of evidence for perspective

1. **Diction.** The words chosen carry attitudes. Compare "obstinate" with "principled" and "followed" with "stampeded after". Look for loaded or emotive words and for patterns.
2. **Syntax.** The arrangement of phrases and clauses can emphasise some details and tone down others. A long sentence that piles up charges and ends on a short one lands hard; a main idea buried in a subordinate clause is made to seem unimportant.
3. **Tone.** The narrator's attitude to events or characters (admiring, resentful, mocking, defensive) affects how readers interpret the ideas associated with them.
4. **Inclusion and omission.** What is included and what is not conveys perspective. The narrator may give a great deal of detail about one person's faults and none about their reasons.

## Reliability: how far should we trust the account?

A **reliable** narrator gives an account that the reader can accept as accurate and fair. A narrator's **reliability** is affected by the details they provide, the details they omit, their motives and what the rest of the text suggests.

Readers can **infer a narrator's biases by noting which details they choose to include and which they choose to omit**. Readers who detect bias may find the narrator **less reliable**, and the **reliability of a narrator may influence a reader's understanding of a character's motives**: if we distrust the narrator's account of a person, we look elsewhere for their reasons.

A useful toolkit for judging reliability:

| Question | What to look for |
|---|---|
| What is the narrator's stake? | Is the narrator an interested party, a defender, a rival, a witness? |
| What is left out? | Gaps, a sudden change of subject, a refusal to explain |
| How are others described? | Loaded words about people the narrator opposes |
| Does anyone contradict the narrator? | Other characters' remarks, documents, physical evidence |
| Does the tone fit the events? | Excessive calm, protest, over-explanation, self-justification |
| What does the narrator know? | Limits of age, position, access or understanding |

Remember that reliability is **a matter of degree**. A narrator may be right about events and biased about motives. Say what you trust and what you do not.

## Public-domain examples to learn from

**Wuthering Heights (Emily Brontë, 1847).** Much of the story is told by the housekeeper Nelly Dean to a visitor, Lockwood. Nelly grew up beside the families whose story she tells, and she takes actions that influence events. She is not a neutral observer, and a careful reader considers how her judgements of the other characters might be shaped by her own position and choices.

**Great Expectations (Charles Dickens, 1861).** The novel begins in the voice of an older Pip: "My father's family name being Pirrip, and my Christian name Philip, my infant tongue could make of both names nothing longer or more explicit than Pip. So, I called myself Pip, and came to be called Pip." The older narrator is amused at the child he was. That gentle tone gives the reader a perspective on the younger Pip's mistakes that Pip himself did not have at the time.

## Worked example 1: diction, syntax and tone reveal perspective

**Original passage written by Marlbridge for practice. It imitates the register of a nineteenth-century first-person narrative and is not taken from any published work.**

> I will say only that Mr Garrick was, throughout the voyage, an obstinate, a thankless, an ungovernable man; that the crew, who had been orderly under my command for twelve years, followed him like sheep; and that when at last I put him in irons, as any master would, it was done with the greatest regret.

**Task.** What does the narrator's language reveal about his perspective?

**Diction.** Three adjectives are stacked against Garrick: "obstinate", "thankless", "ungovernable". All three judge him from the point of view of someone who expects obedience and gratitude. The crew "followed him like sheep" is a simile that belittles both Garrick's followers and, by contrast, flatters the narrator, who sees himself as the proper leader of orderly men.

**Syntax.** The sentence builds through three clauses joined by semicolons. The accusation about Garrick comes first, so it frames everything that follows. The act of putting him in irons is tucked into a subordinate clause ("when at last I put him in irons") and then softened by "as any master would", which shifts the focus from the act to the narrator's justification.

**Tone.** "I will say only" sounds modest but introduces a catalogue of charges, so the tone is defensive. "With the greatest regret" claims reluctance in a way that suits his image.

**Inclusion and omission.** We hear what Garrick was like. We are not told what he did, what he asked for or what the crew's complaints were.

**Reliability.** The narrator is not shown to lie. But his language is the language of self-justification, and the missing details make the account partial. A reader should treat his judgement of Garrick as the opinion of an interested party.

**A model sentence.** "By stacking three adjectives against Garrick, comparing the crew to sheep and tucking the imprisonment into a clause beginning 'as any master would', the narrator reveals a perspective defined by his expectation of obedience, and his silence about Garrick's actual conduct invites the reader to doubt the account."

## Worked example 2: what is left out, and what contradicts

**Original passage written by Marlbridge for practice.** Earlier in the same narrative, the captain says: "Every man aboard was fed as well as the stores allowed." Later:

> "Mr Garrick only asked for the biscuit, sir," said the ship's boy. "Only the biscuit."
>
> The boy was a sullen creature, and I sent him below.

**Task.** How does this exchange bear on the narrator's reliability and on Garrick's motives?

**Contradiction by another voice.** The boy's remark suggests that the food was a point of conflict: Garrick "only asked for the biscuit". The repeated "Only the biscuit" hints that the request was small and reasonable. It sits uneasily with "fed as well as the stores allowed", where the qualification "as the stores allowed" already admits limits.

**How the narrator handles it.** He does not answer the boy. He labels him "a sullen creature" and "sent him below". The loaded phrase discredits the speaker, and removing him from the scene removes the question.

**Effect on the reader.** The reader now has a reason to doubt that the earlier account is complete. Information that the narrator has not given, what Garrick asked for and why, seems to be the very thing that explains the conflict.

**Motives.** If the narrator is less reliable, Garrick's motives look different. Instead of mere obstinacy, his behaviour may be a protest about rations. The narrator's reliability therefore affects the reader's understanding of the **character's** motives as well as the narrator's own.

**A model sentence.** "The boy's repeated 'Only the biscuit' and the captain's dismissal of him as a 'sullen creature' expose a gap in the captain's account, and that gap suggests that Garrick's defiance may have had a cause the narrator has chosen to omit."

## Common misconceptions

- **"The narrator is the author."** The narrator is a created voice. Analyse its choices.
- **"Unreliable means lying."** Bias, limited knowledge, emotion and self-interest all affect reliability.
- **"Only first-person narrators can be unreliable."** First-person narrators are the clearest cases, but a third-person narrator can also be partial in what is included, emphasised or omitted.
- **"One slip proves the narrator cannot be trusted."** Look for a pattern. Reliability is a matter of degree.
- **"If I find bias, I should ignore the narrator."** The narrator's bias is itself evidence. Use it to understand the narrator and to read around the account.
- **"Tone is the same as feeling."** Tone is the attitude conveyed by choices of words and sentences. Name the evidence for it.
