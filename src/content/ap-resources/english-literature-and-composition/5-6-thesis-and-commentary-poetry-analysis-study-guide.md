The poetry essay asks you to read a poem and write an argument about how the poet uses literary elements and techniques to convey a meaning. The earlier topics in this unit gave you the tools: structure, word choice, imagery, metaphor, personification, allusion. This topic shows how to turn those tools into an argument, with a **thesis** that makes a defensible claim, a **line of reasoning** that follows the poem's movement, and **commentary** that links each quotation to the claim and the thesis.

## What changes when the text is a poem

The core ideas from Topic 4.6 apply unchanged. A thesis expresses an interpretation that must be defended with textual evidence and a line of reasoning; commentary explains how the evidence supports the claims and the claims support the thesis. Poetry brings three differences.

1. **The evidence is compressed.** A single word, a line break or a stanza break may be the evidence. You often quote less and say more.
2. **Meaning moves.** Most poems turn: a speaker moves from observation to feeling, from one comparison to another, from certainty to doubt. Your line of reasoning should follow that movement, not the line numbers.
3. **Devices work together.** A shift in structure, an image and a word choice may all point at one idea. Your commentary should show how they combine.

## The thesis for a poem

A defensible thesis says **what the poem does and means, and how**, in a sentence a reader could argue with. Test it:

| Level | Example for Wordsworth's "Composed upon Westminster Bridge" | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Summary | "A man looks at London in the morning and thinks it is beautiful." | Retells; no claim |
| Device list | "Wordsworth uses personification, imagery and a simile." | Names devices; no idea |
| Developing | "Wordsworth uses personification to show that London is beautiful." | A claim, but the beauty is on the surface, and "how" is thin |
| Defensible | "Wordsworth presents London's beauty as a function of its stillness: only when the city's noise and smoke are absent can the speaker see it as a living 'heart' at rest, and the poem's turn from sight to feeling shows how deeply the sight moves him." | An arguable idea with a reason and a hint of the line of reasoning |

A strong poetry thesis often rests on a **tension, shift or ambiguity** in the poem, something a first reading passes over: a stillness that could be rest or death, a boast beside a ruin, a promise of permanence in a poem about loss.

## The line of reasoning for a poem

Plan two or three claims in the order of the poem's *movement*. For the thesis above, a line of reasoning could be:

1. The opening makes the man-made city seem natural, clothed in morning light.
2. The middle images of openness and clarity merge the city with sky and field, so beauty depends on the absence of smoke and noise.
3. The closing turn from sight to feeling, and the personification of the houses and the "heart", makes stillness the poem's sign of life.

Notice that these claims follow the poem from start to end but do not paraphrase it. Each claim is an idea, supported by evidence from a part of the poem.

1. **Evidence.** Quote the exact words.
2. **Device.** Say what kind of choice it is (personification, an image, a stanza break) and, if useful, what it appeals to.
3. **Effect.** Say what it makes the reader understand or feel.
4. **Link.** Connect it to your claim and to the thesis.

## Worked example 1: from a weak thesis to a defensible one

Wordsworth's "Composed upon Westminster Bridge, September 3, 1802" (published 1807) is a sonnet. Its last six lines read:

> Never did sun more beautifully steep
> In his first splendour valley, rock, or hill;
> Ne'er saw I, never felt, a calm so deep!
> The river glideth at his own sweet will:
> Dear God! the very houses seem asleep;
> And all that mighty heart is lying still!

(Text as printed in nineteenth-century anthologies.)

A student drafts: *"Wordsworth uses personification and imagery to show that London is beautiful."*

**Step 1, test it.** It is a claim, but the beauty is declared in the poem's first line ("Earth has not anything to show more fair"). The thesis names devices but does not say what they achieve, and it contains no tension.

**Step 2, find a tension.** The beauty the poem praises is a **stillness**: "silent, bare", "smokeless air", "a calm so deep", houses "asleep", the heart "lying still". Nothing is happening. A great city is admired at the moment it does not act. And "lying still" can mean rest or, more darkly, something lifeless.

**Step 3, make a claim.** "The poem presents London's beauty as a function of its stillness, and the last line's 'mighty heart... lying still' makes that stillness both peaceful and slightly uncanny."

**Step 4, a device-to-effect chain.** *Evidence:* "the very houses seem asleep". *Device:* personification: houses are given a sleeper's calm. *Effect:* the city becomes a living body at rest, so its quiet reads as peace, not emptiness. *Link:* it supports the claim that stillness is the sign of the city's life, and prepares the final image, "mighty heart".

**Result.** The thesis now states an interpretation, handles a tension, and can be supported with evidence from three stages of the poem.

**A model paragraph.** The closing lines complete a movement from sight to feeling by turning the city into a body at rest. The speaker's astonishment, "Ne'er saw I, never felt, a calm so deep!", shifts the verb from seeing to feeling, so that beauty becomes an experience as much as a view. The personification of "the very houses" as "asleep" and of the city as a "mighty heart" that is "lying still" makes silence the poem's sign of life: a heart at rest is still a heart. The faint chill in "still", which can also mean lifeless, adds a note of awe to the calm, which supports the thesis that the city's beauty lies in a stillness that is both peaceful and uncanny.

## Worked example 2: building a line of reasoning from a short poem

Alfred, Lord Tennyson's "The Eagle" (1851) has six lines, quoted in Topic 5.3. A student drafts: *"Tennyson uses imagery and a simile to describe an eagle."*

**Step 1, find the movement.** Stanza 1 gathers images of grip and height ("clasps the crag", "Close to the sun"). Stanza 2 describes the sea below as slow ("The wrinkled sea beneath him crawls") and ends with sudden speed ("like a thunderbolt he falls"). The poem moves from stillness to release.

**Step 2, make a thesis.** "Tennyson presents the eagle's power as something held in stillness until the final line, so that the poem's structure, not just its images, makes the reader feel the dive."

**Step 3, plan the reasoning.** (1) The first stanza accumulates fixed images of grip and height. (2) The second stanza makes the world below seem small and slow, so the eagle's stillness looks like patience. (3) The final simile releases speed, and its position as the last line turns the whole poem into preparation for it.

**Step 4, a commentary chain for claim 3.** *Evidence:* "like a thunderbolt he falls". *Device:* simile in the final line. *Effect:* it compares the dive to lightning, adding speed, force and a flash of light, and it arrives after the slowest line in the poem. *Link:* the contrast of stillness and speed is what the thesis claims the structure makes the reader feel.

## A self-assessment table

Use this suggested Marlbridge table to check a thesis and a paragraph on a poem. It echoes the three strands of the six-point exam essay (thesis, evidence with commentary, sophistication) but is Marlbridge's own and not official scoring.

| Strand | Not yet | Secure | Strong |
|---|---|---|---|
| Thesis | Summary or device list | A defensible claim about what the poem does | A claim with a reason, resting on a tension or shift in the poem |
| Evidence and commentary | Quotations named but not explained | Accurate quotations with device-to-effect commentary | Evidence from across the poem; commentary links to the thesis |
| Control and nuance | Line-by-line paraphrase | Paragraphs follow the poem's movement | A complication or ambiguity handled within the argument |

## Common misconceptions

- **"A thesis lists the poem's devices."** A thesis states an interpretation; devices appear in the reasoning as evidence of it.
- **"Follow the poem line by line."** Follow its movement; group the evidence by claim.
- **"Naming a device is analysis."** The effect and the link to the thesis are the analysis.
- **"Every part of the poem must be covered."** Choose the parts that best support the claims, and sample from across the poem.
- **"Ambiguity weakens the thesis."** Handled well, it strengthens it. Explain what the ambiguity adds.
- **"The speaker is the poet."** Refer to "the speaker" unless the poem's context says otherwise.
