Every story has a voice that tells it. The voice decides what the reader sees, in what words, and with what attitude. This topic teaches you to identify the narrator, describe the point of view and narrative distance, recognise stream of consciousness, and explain how details, diction and syntax reveal a perspective and tone.

## Who is telling, and from where

The **narrator** is the voice that tells the story. The **point of view** is the position from which the story is told. A few key ideas:

- A narrator may **stand outside** the story (a third-person narrator who refers to characters as *she*, *he*, *they*) or may be **a character in it** (a first-person narrator who says *I*). Narrators who are characters may speak directly to readers and either recall events or describe them as they occur.
- A narrator's perspective **controls the details and emphases** that affect how readers experience and interpret a text. Nothing is simply "there": everything that appears has been selected and described by someone.
- **Narrative distance** is how far the narrator stands from the events or characters: physically (close to the action or far from it), in time (telling it years later or as it happens), in relationship (friend, stranger, relative) and in emotional investment (involved or detached). A close narrator gives us thoughts and feelings; a distant one gives us surfaces and summaries.
- **Stream of consciousness** is a kind of narration in which a character's thoughts are related as a continuous flow, often with loose punctuation, sudden shifts and associative jumps.

The narrator is not the author. Always write "the narrator" when you analyse what the voice in the text says or feels, and keep claims about the author's choices separate.

## How a perspective shows itself: details, diction, syntax and tone

Three kinds of evidence reveal a narrator's perspective.

1. **Details.** What does the narrator notice and what is left out? A narrator who spends three sentences on the shabby carpet and one on the funeral shows what matters to them.
2. **Diction.** The choice of words. Descriptive words such as adjectives and adverbs do not only describe; they convey an attitude. *A lean dog* and *a starved dog* are the same animal and different perspectives.
3. **Syntax.** The way sentences are built. Long, flowing sentences may suggest reverie or calm; short, broken ones, anxiety or abruptness; a run of dashes and exclamations, agitation.

The attitude a narrator, character or speaker takes towards an idea, character or situation is the **tone**. Tone arises from perspective, so the narrator's background and position shape it. Name the tone precisely (*wry*, *defensive*, *tender*, *detached*) and show the words and sentence patterns that produce it.

1. **Voice.** Is the narrator inside or outside the story? First person or third?
2. **Distance.** Close or far? Present or remembered? Involved or detached?
3. **Evidence.** Pick two or three details, words or sentence patterns that show the perspective.
4. **Effect.** State the perspective and tone, and what they make the reader understand or feel.

## Worked example 1: a voice that argues with its reader

Edgar Allan Poe's "The Tell-Tale Heart" (1843) opens:

> True!—nervous—very, very dreadfully nervous I had been and am; but why will you say that I am mad? The disease had sharpened my senses—not destroyed—not dulled them.

**Step 1, voice.** A first-person narrator, a character in the story, who addresses "you" directly. He is not simply recalling events; he is arguing with someone who, he assumes, believes him mad.

**Step 2, distance.** He is looking back on events but speaks in the present ("I had been and am"). Emotionally he is extremely close: his anxiety is still active as he talks. We receive his thoughts only; we never see anything he does not report.

**Step 3, evidence.**

- *Details:* he lists his symptoms (nervousness, sharpened senses) and defends them as strengths.
- *Diction:* "dreadfully", "disease" and "sharpened" are words of illness; "healthily" and "calmly", a few lines later, are words he uses to claim the opposite.
- *Syntax:* the opening "True!" and the string of dashes break the sentence into jerks. An exclamation, then three interruptions inside one sentence: the syntax itself is nervous even as the narrator insists on calm.

**Step 4, effect.** The perspective is that of a man defending himself, and the tone is defensive and agitated, with a thin shell of confidence. Because the narrator controls every detail, the reader hears only his case; the gap between what he claims ("calmly") and how he speaks (in dashes and exclamations) lets us read beyond him.

**A model sentence.** Poe's narrator addresses "you" directly and punctuates his claim of calm with exclamations and dashes, so that the syntax of his defence reveals the anxiety he denies.

## Worked example 2: stream of consciousness

The following passage was written by Marlbridge for practice.

> Platform four, no, five, the board says five, and the bag, did I lock the door, I locked it, the key is in my hand, my hand is cold, Mum's coat was this colour, grey like the sky but not the sky, the train is late, is it late, three minutes isn't late, three minutes is nothing, she used to say three minutes is how long it takes to boil an egg properly.

**Step 1, voice.** The narration is a character's thoughts, set down as they occur, in the first person ("my hand") without quotation marks or a narrator's explanation.

**Step 2, distance.** The distance is minimal: the narrator is the character's mind. There is no summary, no outside description, and no time between the event and the telling.

**Step 3, evidence.**

- *Details:* the practical (the platform number, the bag, the key) lead into the personal (Mum's coat, a remembered saying about eggs). The cold hand ties body to feeling.
- *Diction:* "late" repeats three times, with the self-correction "is it late", and "nothing" tries to calm the same fear.
- *Syntax:* there is one long sentence with comma splices and no full stops: ideas follow one another by association (coat, colour, sky, grey) rather than logic. The lack of stops mimics a mind that cannot settle.

**Step 4, effect.** The tone is anxious and tender. The style shows a person using small practical worries to avoid a larger grief: the mother's coat and saying imply a loss. The stream of consciousness lets the reader sense the unspoken without being told.

**A model paragraph.** Marlbridge's passage places the reader inside a traveller's thoughts, so that her anxiety is felt, not described. The single unbroken sentence, with its comma splices and self-corrections ("is it late, three minutes isn't late"), imitates a mind that cannot rest, while the drift from the platform to "Mum's coat" shows that her real concern is not the train but a loss she cannot name.

## Narrative distance side by side

Distance is easiest to understand by changing one sentence. Each version below was written by Marlbridge about the same moment.

| Version | Distance | What the reader gets |
|---|---|---|
| "The old woman closed the gate and walked up the lane." | Far: an outside observer, only actions | A plain fact; feeling is left for the reader to infer |
| "Ada closed the gate, relieved that nobody had been there to see her go." | Medium: a narrator who can enter her feelings | An action plus a private reason |
| "Closed the gate. Nobody. Thank God nobody, not even Mrs Hale at her window, not even the dog." | Very close: her own thoughts, with loose syntax | The anxiety and relief as she experiences them |

Each version keeps the same event and changes how much of the character's mind the reader can reach. When you analyse a passage, ask where on this scale the narrator stands and whether the position moves: many stories begin at a distance and move closer at the moment of crisis, or the reverse. A shift in distance is itself a choice, and often the point at which a story's feeling concentrates. In Chopin's "The Story of an Hour" (1894), for example, the narrator begins with facts about the news and the family, then moves closer and closer into Louise's mind as she sits by the window, so that by the time she whispers "free, free, free!" the reader is almost inside her thoughts.

## Common misconceptions

- **"The narrator is the author."** The narrator is a voice within the text. Say "the narrator", not "the author", when you describe the voice.
- **"First person means unreliable."** First-person narration is limited, not necessarily false. Reliability is a separate judgment.
- **"Tone is a mood word with no evidence."** Tone needs support in diction and syntax. Name it precisely and quote the words.
- **"Narrative distance is only physical."** It may also be distance in time, relationship or emotion.
- **"Stream of consciousness is just messy writing."** The apparent mess is a controlled imitation of thought.
- **"Syntax is grammar."** For analysis, syntax means the effect of sentence shape: length, order, punctuation and repetition.
