## Recap

- A **foil** is a character whose **contrast** with another character illuminates the other's traits, attributes or values. Think of the metal sheet behind a gem.
- Foils work best when the two characters **share a situation** and **differ at one meaningful point**.
- Different characters have different **perspectives**: they notice different details, hold different values and reveal different information. So a longer work can give the reader a fuller picture than any one viewpoint.
- Always finish with **function**: why does the writer want the reader to see this contrast, and what question or conflict does it raise?

## Key relationships

| Idea | What to say | Useful evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Foil | Name the trait or value that each character shows by contrast | Paired speech, actions or reactions to the same event |
| Perspective | Say what the character notices, values and wants | Diction, what is mentioned or left out, tone |
| Function | Link the contrast to a larger conflict, question or interpretation | Where the contrast sits in the plot; repeated patterns |
| Nuance | Note where neither character is simply right or wrong | Qualities of the "worse" character that are admirable |

## The contrast frame in one line

**Same situation, different response, what it reveals, why the writer wants us to see it.**

## Assumptions to keep in mind

- You are analysing a **choice made by the writer**, not judging real people.
- Quote or point to specific details. "Edmund is cautious" needs a detail, such as "his eyes on the ledger", to carry the claim.
- A foil pair may be tested across the whole work. One scene gives a first reading; later scenes may confirm, complicate or reverse it.

## Mistakes to avoid

- Writing a list of differences with no conclusion. Add what the contrast shows.
- Equating "foil" with "villain" or "opposite". Foil is about illumination.
- Treating one character's description of another as fact. It is a perspective.
- Declaring that the writer supports one character without evidence. Look for nuance first.
- Stopping at traits. The strongest answers reach values and the central conflict.

## Quick self-check

**1.** Two sisters inherit a failing farm. One immediately sells the livestock to pay debts; the other refuses and works unpaid beside the hired hands. State a plausible shared ground and point of difference.

**2.** A housekeeper calls a new tutor "a cold fish who never smiles at the children". The master calls him "the only man in the house who tells me the truth". Name two things you learn about the tutor and one about each speaker.

**3.** True or false: if two characters are very different, one must be a foil to the other.

**4.** Complete this stem: "By contrasting X with Y in the same situation, the writer ... "

**Answers.**

1. Shared ground: the same debt and the same inheritance. Point of difference: financial pragmatism against loyalty to people and place, with the contrast showing which each sister values more.
2. About the tutor: he is reserved or unemotional with children, and honest with adults. About the housekeeper: she values warmth toward the children. About the master: he values candour.
3. False. A foil needs a contrast that reveals something about the other character, normally against shared ground. Difference alone is not enough.
4. A good completion names what each character's response shows and what larger question it raises, for example "... exposes a conflict between security and obligation that the rest of the novel must resolve."
