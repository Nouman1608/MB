## Understanding

- I can explain that a setting may become symbolic when it is associated with emotions, ideologies or beliefs. *(Guide: "Image, motif and symbolic setting"; Practice Question 3)*
- I can explain that some settings have developed near-universal associations, and why a text still needs to be read for its own use. *(Guide: "Image, motif and symbolic setting"; Practice Question 5)*
- I can define an image and say what imagery does. *(Guide: "What images do"; Practice Question 2)*
- I can define a motif as a pattern of recurring objects or images that emphasises a significant idea. *(Guide: "From image to motif"; Practice Question 1)*

## Skills

- I can use the five-step method to build a motif from scattered images. *(Guide: Worked example 1; Practice Questions 1 and 8)*
- I can identify signals that a setting is symbolic. *(Guide: Worked example 2; Practice Questions 3 and 4)*
- I can explain the function of sensory images (mood, making ideas concrete, characterising, linking). *(Guide: "What images do"; Practice Question 7)*
- I can trace how a motif changes across a story and say what the change shows. *(Guide: Worked example 1; Practice Question 8)*

## Reasoning

- I can strengthen a claim about a symbol by adding evidence from the text, not only a traditional association. *(Guide: "Common misconceptions"; Practice Question 5)*
- I can explain how a name and an image can work together as a symbol. *(Guide: Young Goodman Brown example; Practice Question 6)*
- I can use a public-domain story, such as The Fall of the House of Usher, to show how a setting becomes symbolic. *(Guide: "Public-domain examples to learn from"; Practice Questions 4 and 9)*
