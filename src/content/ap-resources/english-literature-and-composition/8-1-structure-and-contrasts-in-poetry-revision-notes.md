## Recap

Structure is the arrangement of a poem's parts and the order in which it reveals them. Lines, stanzas, sentences, punctuation, repetition and rhyme all create a pattern, and any interruption of a pattern puts emphasis on the interruption. Contrast (juxtaposition, irony, paradox) creates meaning from difference. In every case the analysis has three moves: **name** the feature, **quote** the words, **explain** what it does to the poem's meaning as a whole. The full explanation, with two worked examples, is in the [study guide](/advanced-course-resources/english-literature-and-composition/8-1-structure-and-contrasts-in-poetry-study-guide/).

## Key relationships

| Feature | What to look for | What it usually does |
|---|---|---|
| Line and stanza breaks | Where thoughts stop or run on (enjambment, end-stopping) | Creates momentum, strain or finality |
| Punctuation | Colon, dash, semicolon, question mark, full stop mid-line | Controls pace, certainty and balance |
| Volta | "But", "Yet", a new addressee or tone, often near line 9 or the couplet | Turns the thought; reframes what came before |
| Broken pattern | Rhyme, rhythm, repetition or stanza length that suddenly changes | Emphasises the break; signals loss, shock or release |
| Juxtaposition | Two things set side by side, possibly as antithesis | Makes a quality visible through difference |
| Situational irony | Events overturn what the situation led us to expect | Exposes pretension or fate |
| Verbal irony | Words mean the opposite of what they say | Signals criticism, bitterness or humour |
| Paradox | A statement that seems impossible but reveals a truth | Compresses an idea; invites rethinking |

## Conditions to remember

- A volta is found from the poem's thought, not from a line number. Sonnets often turn at line 9 or at the couplet, but not always.
- Contrast needs two terms. Name both sides of it and what separates them.
- A pattern needs to be established before its interruption can mean anything; describe the pattern first.
- Always tie the feature to the poem as a whole: what does this change in my reading of the beginning and the end?

## Mistakes to avoid

- Stopping at the label ("there is enjambment here").
- Calling any surprise "irony" or any odd statement "paradox".
- Describing the rhyme scheme and nothing else.
- Treating the volta as a separate poem: the turn should change how you read what came before it.
- Quoting a whole stanza when one phrase carries the point.

## Quick self-check

1. In Rossetti's "Remember", which word begins the volta, and what does the poem turn from and to?
2. In Shelley's "Ozymandias", why is the inscription's boast ironic?
3. Define paradox and give one example from this topic.
4. A poem has four regular rhyming quatrains and then a final stanza with no rhyme and shorter lines. Give one defensible reading of the change.
5. What is the difference between juxtaposition and irony?

**Answers.**
1. "Yet" (line 9). It turns from demanding to be remembered to permitting herself to be forgotten, for the listener's peace.
2. The king intends the inscription to inspire despair at his power; the surrounding ruin makes the reader despair at how power fades, so his own words work against him.
3. A paradox is a statement or situation that seems self-contradictory but reveals an idea; for instance Donne's "Death, thou shalt die", which means that death is only a sleep before eternal waking.
4. The loss of pattern can enact loss of order or control, or an emotional break; the shift emphasises what happens in the last stanza.
5. Juxtaposition places two things side by side so the difference matters; irony is a gap between expectation and outcome, or between statement and meaning. A juxtaposition may produce irony, but it does not have to.
