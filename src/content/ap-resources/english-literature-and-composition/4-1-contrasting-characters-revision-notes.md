## Recap

- **Contrasting characters** are set against each other so that a difference in what they value becomes visible.
- The **protagonist** is the main character. The **antagonist** is whatever opposes the protagonist: another character, a collective such as society, nature, or the protagonist's own internal conflict.
- A character's choices in speech, action and inaction reveal what that character values. When two value systems meet, conflict follows.
- The antagonist is not always a villain. Keep "who opposes?" separate from "who is good?".
- A contrast earns marks when you explain its **function**: what it creates, exposes or prepares in the story.
- Contrasts can build across scenes, can involve an absent character, and can shift or soften as the story goes on.

## Key relationships

| Move | What it looks like | Example wording |
|---|---|---|
| Detail | A specific line, action or description for each character | "stamps ledgers 'as if the stamp could keep the hour from moving'" |
| Difference | A precise statement of how they differ at that moment | "answers a rule with an urgent need" |
| Value | What each stands for | "order and predictability versus loyalty to a person" |
| Effect | What the contrast does for the story | "creates the conflict and prepares a change in the rule-keeper" |

| Kind of antagonist | Meaning | Where to look |
|---|---|---|
| Another character | A person who blocks or pressures the protagonist | Dialogue, refusals, demands |
| Collective | Society, family, a community's expectations | Narrator's generalisations, other people's reactions |
| Nature | Weather, distance, the physical world | Setting description, survival tension |
| Internal conflict | The protagonist's own doubt, fear or desire | Hesitation, contradictory thoughts |

## Assumptions

- You are writing about what the text shows, with short quotations, and not about the author's life.
- A contrast needs textual support on **both** sides. If you can only quote one character, you have a description, not a contrast.
- You can usually reach a defensible claim about function without deciding which character the reader should approve of.

## Mistakes to avoid

- Listing traits ("calm", "nervous") and stopping before values or effect.
- Assuming the antagonist must be a person, or must be evil.
- Writing two separate character sketches with no link between them.
- Quoting a whole sentence where three words would show the value.
- Treating a contrast as fixed when the text shows it changing.

## Quick self-check

1. A story's protagonist keeps trying to leave a village, and the village's silent disapproval keeps pulling her back. Who or what is the antagonist?
2. Character A will not break the rule; Character B pleads. Write the "value" step for each.
3. Why is "A is tall and B is short" not a contrast of the kind that matters here?
4. Name two things a contrast can do for a story.

**Answers.** (1) The collective: the village and its disapproval. (2) For example: A values order and fairness to everyone equally; B values the needs of an individual in trouble. (3) It names a physical difference only; it does not show different values or choices, so it cannot create conflict or theme unless the text makes height matter. (4) For example: create the central conflict; expose the cost of a value; prepare a character's change; sharpen a theme.
