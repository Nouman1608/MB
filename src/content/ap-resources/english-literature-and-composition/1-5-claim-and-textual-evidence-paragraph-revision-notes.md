## Recap

A literary-analysis paragraph begins with a **claim** (a statement about the text that needs defence) and **textual evidence** (specific details or short, exact quotations). A **link** sentence explains how the evidence supports the claim. Quotations are embedded in your own sentence and the present tense is used for what the text does.

## Key relationships

| Part | What it is | Test |
|---|---|---|
| Claim | A statement that needs defence with evidence | Could a reasonable reader ask "how do you know?" |
| Evidence | Specific details or short quotations | Is it exact, relevant and specific? |
| Link | A sentence explaining how the evidence supports the claim | Does it say *how*, not just place the quotation? |
| Fact | A statement that needs no defence | Not a claim |
| Summary | A retelling of events | Not a claim, not evidence |

## What you may assume

- The reader of your paragraph has read the text, so you do not need to retell it.
- You may paraphrase when exact words are not needed, but a claim should rest on details that the text contains.
- An interpretation can begin from a claim and then find evidence, or begin from details and then form a claim.

## Mistakes to avoid

- Opening with a fact or with a summary.
- Using a trait word ("nice", "bad") as the claim.
- Quoting a long block instead of the words you need.
- Changing a quotation, or quoting from memory.
- Mixing past and present tense when writing about the text.

## Key terms

- **Claim:** a statement about the text that requires defence with evidence.
- **Evidence:** specific details or short, exact quotations from the text.
- **Link:** a sentence explaining how the evidence supports the claim.
- **Embedded quotation:** a quotation built into the grammar of your own sentence.

## Sentence frames

- "[Character / narrator / speaker] [verb] … , which suggests that …."
- "The phrase '…' shows … because …."
- "Taken together, these details show …."

## Mini-example

Weak: "Ilse is sad." Better: "Ilse's habit of standing at the jetty 'for the full length of the old crossing' shows that she cannot accept that the ferry has ended."

## How questions ask about this

- *"Which of the following is a claim?"*: choose the statement that needs defence, not a fact or summary.
- *"Which evidence best supports the claim?"*: choose the detail that bears most directly on it.
- *"Which sentence best embeds the quotation?"*: look for exact words, grammar that fits and a link.
- Constructed response: open with a claim, add two accurate quotations, and write a link sentence.

## Quick self-check

1. Is "The story is set in a market" a claim? Why or why not?
2. Embed "the far end where the cobbles gave way to mud" in a sentence about Sandeep.
3. What does a link sentence do?

**Answers.**

1. No. It states a fact that needs no defence. A claim would say what the setting conveys.
2. For example: Sandeep is placed at "the far end where the cobbles gave way to mud", which marks him as an outsider.
3. It explains how the evidence supports the claim, so the reader sees the reasoning, not only the quotation.
