## Recap

Word choice (diction) matters because the poet could have used other words. To explain a word, say what it denotes, what it connotes, why it was preferred to a plainer alternative, and what that does to the speaker's attitude and the poem as a whole. **Ambiguity** means a word or phrase supports two or more defensible readings; the poem benefits from keeping both. Complex poems also coin words, join words and bend word order. When evidence is ambiguous, use it to **revise** your claim rather than merely noting that others might disagree. The full explanation, with two worked examples, is in the [study guide](/advanced-course-resources/english-literature-and-composition/8-2-word-choice-in-complex-poems-study-guide/).

## Key relationships

| Idea | In one line | Typical sentence frame |
|---|---|---|
| Denotation | The dictionary meaning | "The word literally refers to..." |
| Connotation | The associations the word carries | "The word suggests... which makes the speaker seem..." |
| Alternative | The plainer word the poet did not use | "Instead of 'X', the poet chooses 'Y', which adds..." |
| Ambiguity | Two or more supported readings | "On one reading..., on another..." |
| Tension | What is gained by keeping both | "Holding both suggests that..." |
| Revised claim | Your interpretation after the tension | "So the poem is less about... than about..." |
| Coinage or compound | A new or fused word | "The fused word makes the reader hear two ideas at once." |

## Conditions to remember

- A reading must be supported by the context; a dictionary meaning that does not fit is not a reading.
- Ambiguity is not vagueness, and it is not an excuse for having no claim.
- Two readings, well explained, are stronger than five listed.
- Connotations should be shared and context-backed, not personal feelings.

## Mistakes to avoid

- Writing "negative" or "positive" in place of describing the effect.
- Listing meanings of a word without choosing which matter.
- Acknowledging an alternative reading in one sentence and carrying on as before.
- Treating coinages as decoration rather than as arguments in miniature.
- Ignoring the speaker: the question is how the word shapes the speaker's attitude.

## Worked mini-example

Take "kindly" in Dickinson's "Because I could not stop for Death". The denotation is gently. The connotations are manners and courtship. *Reading A:* genuine gentleness. *Reading B:* a courtesy that leaves no choice, since the first line says she "could not stop" and he "kindly stopped for me". *Tension:* a calm that conceals a loss of control. *Revised claim:* the speaker's serenity is partly the politeness death imposes. One word, four moves, and a claim that has changed because of the evidence: that is the whole template.

## Quick self-check

1. What two meanings does Hopkins's "unleaving" carry?
2. Why do "kindly" and "civility" in Dickinson's poem create ambiguity?
3. What is the difference between acknowledging and revising?
4. Give the four steps of the ambiguity template.
5. What does "blight" connect in "Spring and Fall"?

**Answers.**
1. Leaves coming off (the reverse of leafing) and a leaving or departure.
2. They connote courtesy and courtship, yet the speaker had no choice about the journey, so the politeness seems both comforting and controlling.
3. Acknowledging notes that another reading exists; revising changes your own claim because of it.
4. Reading A, Reading B, tension, revised claim.
5. Plant disease and human mortality, so the fall of leaves and the human condition seem one thing.
