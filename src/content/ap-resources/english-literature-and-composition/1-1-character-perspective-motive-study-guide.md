## Characters are built from details, and readers infer the rest

A short story does not tell you what a character is like in the way a form tells you a person's age. It gives you **details** and leaves you to work out what they add up to. Three kinds of detail carry most of the weight:

- **Description:** what the narrator, another character or the character themselves says about appearance, habits, belongings or manner.
- **Dialogue:** what the character says, how they say it, what they avoid saying, and how others respond.
- **Behaviour:** what the character does or fails to do, especially under pressure or when they think nobody is watching.

Your task in this topic is to go from a detail to a claim about a character's **perspective** and **motive**. Those two words are the core vocabulary.

- **Perspective** is how a character understands their circumstances. It is shaped by background, personality, biases and relationships, and it decides what they notice, trust or fear.
- **Motive** is the reason behind a choice or action. Readers infer motives from what a character does or declines to do.

Notice what that definition does **not** say. Perspective is not the same as the author's opinion, and a character is not the same as the narrator. A character can be wrong about their own situation, and part of the pleasure of reading is seeing the gap between what a character believes and what the text lets you see.

Each link has a job. The detail must be **specific**: a quoted phrase, an action, a line of speech, not a general impression. The inference must be **earned**: say what the detail does, such as "this word choice treats the customer as an opponent". The claim at the end must be **limited by the evidence**: if you have two small details, do not announce a life philosophy.

A useful habit is to ask three questions of every detail:

1. **What is shown?** (the plain fact: he counts the till twice)
2. **What does it suggest?** (anxiety, caution, a need for control)
3. **Why might the writer include it?** (to make a small action stand for an attitude)

## Whose view is this description?

A key idea in this topic is that descriptions of a character can come from a speaker, a narrator, other characters, or the character. That matters because each source has a different relationship to the truth.

| Source of the description | What it can reveal | What to watch for |
|---|---|---|
| The narrator | Facts, selected emphasis, sometimes judgement | A narrator may be limited, sympathetic or hostile |
| Another character | That character's perspective as much as the described character's | Jealousy, affection, prejudice or ignorance |
| The character (words and actions) | Self-image, values, what they want others to believe | Self-deception, performance, evasion |

When you see a description, ask **who is saying this** before you decide what it proves.

## Worked example 1: a description from other people (public-domain text)

The opening sentence of Kate Chopin's short story "The Story of an Hour" (1894) reads: "Knowing that Mrs. Mallard was afflicted with a heart trouble, great care was taken to break to her as gently as possible the news of her husband's death."

**Step 1: What is shown?** A woman is about to hear news of a death. Other people have decided how to tell her, and they have done it carefully.

**Step 2: Whose view is it?** The phrase "great care was taken" is in the passive voice. It does not say who took the care, so the sentence presents a general, protective attitude. The description of Mrs. Mallard as "afflicted with a heart trouble" comes from this surrounding view, with the narrator reporting it, not from Mrs. Mallard herself.

**Step 3: What does it suggest?** The people around her treat her as fragile. They expect her to react to the news with collapse. The details "afflicted", "great care" and "as gently as possible" all point the same way.

**Step 4: Claim, kept within the evidence.** From this sentence alone you can say something about the perspective of the people around Mrs. Mallard: they see her as someone to be shielded. You cannot yet say what Mrs. Mallard herself believes or wants, because the text has not shown her thinking. A careful reader notes that this description sets up an **expectation** about her, which the rest of the story can meet or surprise.

*Model sentence:* "The passive phrase 'great care was taken' presents those around Mrs. Mallard as treating her as fragile, an expectation that the narrative has not yet tested against her own perspective."

Notice what the model sentence avoids: it does not call Mrs. Mallard weak. Doing so would confuse other people's view of her with the character she turns out to be.

## Worked example 2: an original passage (written by Marlbridge)

Read this short passage, which Marlbridge wrote for practice.

> Mr. Alder counted the till twice, which was one more time than the coins required. Behind him the clock above the pharmacy door ticked toward closing, and he did not look at it. When the girl from the bakery came in with the dented tin she wanted to return, he said the warranty had expired in March, though the receipt, which she held out to him like a small white flag, said April. "It's only a tin," she said. "Everything is only something," said Mr. Alder, and turned the sign on the door so that CLOSED faced the street, four minutes early. That evening he would tell his sister that the girl had been rude.

**Step 1: Collect the details.** List what is *shown*, not what you feel about it.

- He counts the till twice, "one more time than the coins required".
- He does not look at the clock.
- He says the warranty expired in March when the receipt says April.
- He answers "It's only a tin" with "Everything is only something".
- He turns the sign four minutes early.
- He will tell his sister the girl was rude.

**Step 2: Group them.** Three details (the extra count, the avoided clock, the early sign) concern **control**: he manages small rules and small amounts of time. Two details (the false date, the epigram) concern **evasion**: he refuses the customer's point instead of answering it. The last detail concerns **self-image**: he plans a version of events in which he is the wronged party.

**Step 3: Infer perspective.** Mr. Alder sees the shop, and probably much of life, as a place where small losses must be guarded against and where other people are a risk. The receipt "held out to him like a small white flag" is the narrator's image; it suggests the girl is offering peace, which Mr. Alder refuses to accept. The narrator's image therefore sets his hostility against her openness.

**Step 4: Infer motive.** His motive for the false date is not an easy question. The text allows two readings: he is protecting the shop's money, or he is protecting his own sense of being right. The final sentence favours the second, because a man defending money would not need to rehearse a story about rudeness for his sister.

**Step 5: Write the claim.**

*Model paragraph opening:* "Mr. Alder's small acts of control and evasion reveal a perspective in which customers are threats to be managed and not people to be served. He counts the till 'twice', ignores the clock, and denies a receipt that contradicts him, and the plan to describe the girl as 'rude' shows that his deeper motive is to keep his own record of the evening unblemished."

Check the model against the evidence: every phrase in the claim points to a quoted detail, and the one inference that goes furthest (the deeper motive) rests on the final sentence.

## Strong and weak versions side by side

| Weak | Why it is weak | Stronger |
|---|---|---|
| "Mr. Alder is a bad person." | A label with no evidence; "bad" is not a claim about the text | "Mr. Alder treats a small complaint as a threat, refusing a receipt that contradicts him." |
| "The girl is nice because she smiles." | Invents a detail the text does not give | "The girl offers the receipt 'like a small white flag', which presents her as seeking agreement." |
| "He counts the till twice, so he is careful." | Stops at the detail | "He counts the till twice, one more time than needed, which suggests that he checks things beyond what the situation requires." |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Perspective means a character's opinion."** It is wider: it covers what the character notices, assumes and fears. A character with a settled mistrust of customers has a perspective even if they never state an opinion.
- **"The narrator's description is always true."** A narrator may be partial. Treat a description as evidence of whose view it is as well as of the character described.
- **"A trait word is an analysis."** Words such as "arrogant" or "shy" are conclusions. Show the details that lead to them, and say what each detail does.
- **"The character is the author."** A character's view is not the author's view. Say "the narrator presents" or "the character believes", not "the author thinks".
- **"One detail proves a motive."** A single detail permits several readings. State the reading the *combination* of details supports and, where the text is open, say so.
- **"Motive and perspective are the same."** Perspective explains how the character sees; motive explains why they act. A claim that mentions both is usually richer, but each needs its own evidence.
