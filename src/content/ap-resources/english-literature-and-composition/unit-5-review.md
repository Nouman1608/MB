Read the big ideas and the method table, then try the five questions without notes. Each combines two or more Unit 5 topics. The last is a full poetry analysis essay.

These are **original Marlbridge practice questions**, not past exam questions. The public-domain poem is Percy Bysshe Shelley, "Ozymandias" (1818), in the nineteenth-century text printed below. The rubrics are a **suggested Marlbridge rubric**, not official scoring.

## Big ideas of the unit

- **Structure relates ideas.** Closed forms use predictable patterns and open forms use repetition, parallel and breaks; both develop relationships among ideas, and structures combine to emphasise them. (Topic 5.1)
- **Words carry more than meaning.** Literal and figurative meaning, connotation, descriptive words, hyperbole and understatement convey a perspective. (Topic 5.2)
- **Images and comparisons work through the senses and through traits.** Imagery emphasises ideas; metaphors transfer particular traits; personification characterises. (Topics 5.3 and 5.4)
- **Allusion imports associations** from outside the poem. (Topic 5.5)
- **A poetry essay needs a thesis, a line of reasoning that follows the poem's movement, and device-to-effect commentary.** Evidence must be relevant, sufficient, accurately quoted and integrated, and the essay coherent. (Topics 5.6 and 5.7)

## The method in one table

| Skill | Four steps | Key question |
|---|---|---|
| Structure | Pattern, shift, relationship, effect | What does the arrangement do to meaning? |
| Words and phrases | Literal, figurative, word, effect | What does this word suggest beyond what it says? |
| Imagery | Image, sense, association, effect (plus pattern) | What do the images share and how do they change? |
| Metaphor, personification | Sides, traits, left behind, effect | Which traits transfer, and which do not? |
| Allusion | Reference, associations, link, effect | What does the source bring? |
| Essay writing | Evidence, device, effect, link; claim, proof, purpose, flow | Does each paragraph support the thesis and flow from the last? |

## The poem

Use this poem for every question. Percy Bysshe Shelley, "Ozymandias" (1818), in the text of nineteenth-century anthologies.

> I met a traveller from an antique land
> Who said: Two vast and trunkless legs of stone
> Stand in the desert. Near them on the sand
> Half sunk, a shatter'd visage lies, whose frown
> And wrinkled lip and sneer of cold command
> Tell that its sculptor well those passions read
> Which yet survive, stamp'd on these lifeless things,
> The hand that mock'd them and the heart that fed;
> And on the pedestal these words appear:
> "My name is Ozymandias, king of kings:
> Look on my works, ye Mighty, and despair!"
> Nothing beside remains. Round the decay
> Of that colossal wreck, boundless and bare
> The lone and level sands stretch far away.

## Question 1 (multiple choice · 5.1 and 5.5)

Which statement best describes how the poem's structure and its allusion work together?

- (A) The traveller's account builds from the broken statue to the king's inscription and then to the empty desert, so the allusion to a mighty ruler is set between a ruin and a wilderness.
- (B) The poem has no structure.
- (C) The inscription is the last line.
- (D) The allusion is to the poet's friend.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (A).** Lines 2 to 8 describe the ruin, lines 9 to 11 give the king's boast, and lines 12 to 14 return to the sand. The boast is framed by decay on both sides, which creates the irony.

- (B) The poem is a closed form with a clear movement.
- (C) The inscription comes in the middle of the poem; the last line is about the sands.
- (D) The allusion is to a ruler, Ozymandias, not to a friend.

## Question 2 (multiple choice · 5.2 and 5.4)

The statue's face has a "sneer of cold command". Which statement best explains the effect of this phrase?

- (A) The words give the stone a human expression, so a lifeless object seems to preserve the king's arrogance and the sculptor's skill.
- (B) The words describe the weather.
- (C) The phrase means that the statue is cold to the touch only.
- (D) The phrase is an allusion to a painting.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (A).** "Sneer" and "command" are human attitudes carried by stone; the phrase suggests that the carved passions "survive" the king.

- (B) No weather is described.
- (C) The connotations are of arrogance, not temperature alone.
- (D) The phrase is not an allusion to art.

## Question 3 (short answer · 5.3 and 5.2)

Explain how the images in the last three lines ("Nothing beside remains. Round the decay / Of that colossal wreck, boundless and bare / The lone and level sands stretch far away") shape the poem's meaning. (4 points)

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Sample response (written by Marlbridge).** The final images are visual and spatial: "colossal wreck", "boundless and bare" and "lone and level sands". "Colossal" keeps the memory of size while "wreck" and "decay" show ruin, so the statue is both vast and destroyed. The adjectives "boundless", "bare", "lone" and "level" build an emptiness without limit, and the flat, repeated "l" sounds in "lone and level" slow the line to a stretch of sand. The imagery moves from the monument to the desert, so that the mighty king's works are swallowed by a larger, indifferent landscape.

**Suggested Marlbridge rubric (4 points).**

| Point | Awarded for |
|---|---|
| 1 | Identifies images with accurate quotations |
| 2 | Names the sense or the association (sight, space, emptiness) |
| 3 | Comments on at least one word choice (colossal/wreck, level) |
| 4 | Explains the effect on the poem's meaning (power swallowed by time) |

## Question 4 (short answer · 5.6 and 5.7)

Write a thesis, a three-step line of reasoning and, for each step, one short accurate quotation with its purpose (illustrate, qualify, amplify and so on). (6 points)

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Sample response (written by Marlbridge).** *Thesis:* Shelley uses a king's own boast, framed by ruin, to suggest that power survives only in what art records, and even that record turns against the ruler. *Step 1:* The opening presents a fragmentary monument whose carved expression outlives its maker. Evidence: "sneer of cold command", which illustrates the arrogance preserved in stone. *Step 2:* The inscription claims eternal power, and the poem places it between descriptions of ruin. Evidence: "Look on my works, ye Mighty, and despair!", which qualifies the king's claim by its context. *Step 3:* The close moves from monument to emptiness. Evidence: "The lone and level sands stretch far away", which amplifies the scale of loss.

**Suggested Marlbridge rubric (6 points).**

| Point | Awarded for |
|---|---|
| 1 | A defensible thesis |
| 2 to 4 | Each of three claims, logical and building |
| 5 | Accurate, short quotations for each step |
| 6 | The purpose of each quotation named correctly |

## Question 5 (essay · all topics)

Write a complete essay (about 40 minutes) analysing how the poet uses structure, imagery, figurative language and allusion to convey the speaker's view of power.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Sample high-quality response (written by Marlbridge).**

Shelley uses a ruler's own boast, framed by ruin, to suggest that power survives only in what art records, and that even that record turns against the ruler.

The poem's structure sets the boast between two descriptions of decay. The speaker passes on a traveller's account, so we meet the king at two removes, and the report moves from the "vast and trunkless legs of stone" to the inscription and then back to the desert. The inscription, "My name is Ozymandias, king of kings: / Look on my works, ye Mighty, and despair!", is the poem's centre, but it is surrounded by "shatter'd" fragments and "decay". The order matters: the reader reads the command to despair after seeing what the works have become, so the boast turns into irony.

The allusion to Ozymandias, the pharaoh, carries the associations of absolute power and monuments meant to last. The title "king of kings" claims rule over rulers, and "Mighty" addresses them as subjects. The allusion therefore sets up the largest possible claim for the poem's ruin to defeat. Because the name is given by the king himself on the pedestal, the poem lets him condemn himself: the surviving words are the only evidence of his pride.

The imagery and figurative language show where power truly lies. The face has a "sneer of cold command", a phrase that gives stone the king's arrogance. The sculptor "well those passions read", and the ambiguous line "The hand that mock'd them and the heart that fed" can mean that the sculptor imitated the passions or that he ridiculed them, while the king's heart fed them. Either way, the artist's reading of the king outlasts the king. The final adjectives, "boundless and bare", "lone and level", make the sand the only ruler left, so the power of the monarch yields to time.

The poem therefore moves from a monument, to a boast, to emptiness. The structure frames the boast, the allusion inflates it, and the imagery shows its end: what survives is not the king's power but an artist's record and the indifference of the desert.

**Suggested Marlbridge rubric (6 points, modelled on the three strands of the exam essay).**

| Strand | Points | Awarded for |
|---|---|---|
| Thesis | 1 | A defensible interpretation that answers the prompt |
| Evidence and commentary | 4 | Accurate, integrated evidence for each claim (1); device-to-effect commentary (1); a line of reasoning that follows the poem's movement and builds (1); links back to the thesis (1) |
| Sophistication | 1 | A complication or ambiguity handled within the argument (here, the double sense of "mock'd") |
