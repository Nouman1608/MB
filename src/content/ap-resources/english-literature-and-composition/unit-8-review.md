Use this review after the [Unit 8 diagnostic](/advanced-course-resources/english-literature-and-composition/unit-8-diagnostic/) and the topic practice sets. It shows how the skills of Unit 8 work together on one poem, and ends with a full model poetry-analysis essay and a suggested rubric.

These are **original Marlbridge practice questions**, not past exam questions. The poem and every question were written by Marlbridge. The rubrics are a suggested Marlbridge rubric, not an official scoring guideline. No calculator is needed. Allow about 75 minutes.

## The big ideas of Unit 8

- **Parts and whole.** Every device is explained by what it contributes to an interpretation of the entire poem.
- **Structure and contrast.** Patterns, interruptions, turns, juxtaposition, irony and paradox create meaning from difference.
- **Language that means more than one thing.** Connotation, ambiguity, symbol, extended metaphor and allusion all carry meaning beyond the literal.
- **Revision, not just acknowledgement.** When evidence is ambiguous, let it change your claim.
- **Argument.** A thesis is a defensible interpretation; commentary explains how evidence supports a line of reasoning; evidence is chosen to be relevant and sufficient.

## The poem

Read "The Net-Mender", written by Marlbridge. Every question below refers to it.

> Each morning, when the tide has drawn its line  
> along the slip, she sits to mend the nets:  
> the needle flicks; the knots go in like rhyme,  
> each one a small agreement with the sea.  
> Her father mended these. His father too.  
> The rope is nylon now, the mesh is wide,  
> but still she loops and pulls and trims and ties  
> the way they showed her, learning by the hand.
>
> The boats are fewer. In the harbour mouth  
> the quota-men have drawn their paper lines.  
> She hums a hymn of fishers by a lake,  
> called once to leave their nets and follow, and  
> she has not left. She mends. The gulls come in  
> and wait. The light slides grey across the water.  
> What lies between her thumb and thread is not  
> a net, exactly, but a way of holding on.

## Question 1 (multiple choice · structure and contrast)

The first line says "the tide has drawn its line"; the tenth says "the quota-men have drawn their paper lines". What is the most likely effect of this echo?

- (A) It shows that the poet ran out of words.
- (B) It sets a natural boundary beside a bureaucratic one, so the contrast makes the pressure on the woman's work felt without a direct complaint.
- (C) It marks the end of the poem.
- (D) It shows that the tide and the quota-men are the same.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (B).** Repeating "drawn" with "line(s)" invites comparison; the difference between a tide's line and "paper lines" is the contrast. The echo also supports the structural turn at "The boats are fewer".

- (A) is dismissive of a deliberate pattern.
- (C) is wrong: the echo falls at the start of the second stanza, not the end.
- (D) misreads a comparison as identity.

</details>

## Question 2 (multiple choice · symbol and metaphor)

"the knots go in like rhyme, / each one a small agreement with the sea." What do the simile and metaphor suggest, taken together?

- (A) That mending is a patterned craft, like verse, and each knot is a promise kept with the sea, so her work is both art and pact.
- (B) That nets are decorative.
- (C) That the sea is a lawyer.
- (D) That the woman writes poetry.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (A).** "Like rhyme" gives the work pattern and satisfaction; "agreement" gives each knot the force of a promise. The two comparisons combine to present the work as a relationship.

- (B) ignores the poem's emphasis on function and fidelity.
- (C) misreads "agreement" as legal.
- (D) takes a simile literally.

</details>

## Question 3 (multiple choice · allusion)

"She hums a hymn of fishers by a lake, / called once to leave their nets and follow, and / she has not left." The reference is to the gospel story of fishermen who leave their nets to follow Jesus. What is the best account of the allusion's function?

- (A) It shows that the poem is a hymn.
- (B) It shows that the woman is a priest.
- (C) It brings in associations of vocation and renunciation, and then complicates them, because the woman honours the image yet does not leave her nets, so her loyalty to her craft becomes a calling of its own.
- (D) It shows the speaker disapproves of fishing.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (C).** The allusion supplies the idea of a call to leave nets; the poem's "she has not left" departs from it, so the poem both uses and questions its source.

- (A) confuses a reference within the poem with the poem's form.
- (B) misreads humming a hymn as holding office.
- (D) has no textual support.

</details>

## Question 4 (multiple choice · commentary and evidence)

A student quotes "a way of holding on". Which commentary sentence is best?

- (A) "'A way of holding on' is the last line."
- (B) "The phrase shifts the net from an object to a practice, and its double sense (faithful continuing or stubborn clinging) keeps the speaker's attitude admiring but aware of the cost."
- (C) "The poet uses a metaphor here."
- (D) "This shows the woman is strong."

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (B).** It explains the shift in the symbol and develops the ambiguity of "holding on", linking both to the speaker's attitude.

- (A) states a fact of position without analysis.
- (C) names a device without effect.
- (D) is a general judgement.

</details>

## Question 5 (written response · word choice and ambiguity)

In about 70 to 90 words, explain how the ambiguity of "holding on" in the last line contributes to the poem's meaning.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Sample response (written by Marlbridge).** "Holding on" can mean continuing faithfully and clinging stubbornly. On the first reading, the woman's mending preserves a family lineage and a relationship with the sea. On the second, she holds on to something that official lines are narrowing, at some cost to herself. The poem does not choose, and the tension is the point: the speaker admires her loyalty while recognising that it may be a refusal to let go. The ambiguity therefore revises a simple praise of tradition into a more complex, elegiac view.

**Suggested Marlbridge rubric (3 points)**

| Point | What earns it |
|---|---|
| 1 | Sets out two defensible readings of "holding on". |
| 2 | Explains the tension between them. |
| 3 | Links the tension to the poem's overall meaning or the speaker's attitude. |

</details>

## Question 6 (written response · full essay)

**Prompt (written by Marlbridge).** Read "The Net-Mender". Write an essay analysing how the poet conveys the speaker's complex attitude toward the woman's work. Your essay should have a defensible thesis, a line of reasoning, and commentary that explains how specific evidence supports your argument. Allow about 40 minutes.

<details>
<summary>Model response, annotation and suggested rubric</summary>

**Model response (written by Marlbridge).** A model written in a calm 40 minutes would be shorter; this one is longer so that every move can be seen.

> The poem presents the net-mender's daily work as a quiet fidelity to inherited ways of life, and in its last line it redefines what she is preserving: not a tool but a continuity of kin and place.
>
> The first stanza makes the work a form of agreement. The speaker says that "the knots go in like rhyme, / each one a small agreement with the sea". The simile gives her craft the patterned satisfaction of verse, and the metaphor of "agreement" turns each knot into a promise kept between the woman and the water. The next line, "Her father mended these. His father too.", is a pair of short sentences that sounds like a lineage being recited, and the repeated "mended" makes the labour an inheritance. Even what has changed ("The rope is nylon now") does not alter the practice, since she still works "the way they showed her, learning by the hand". The stanza therefore establishes the work as both craft and bond.
>
> The second stanza turns, and the structure marks the turn. "The boats are fewer" is a flat, short statement after eight lines of settled rhythm, and the word "drawn" returns from the first line: where "the tide has drawn its line" described a natural boundary, "the quota-men have drawn their paper lines" describes a bureaucratic one. The echo juxtaposes two kinds of limit, and the difference lets the reader feel the pressure on her work without any complaint from the speaker.
>
> Against that pressure the poem sets an allusion. She "hums a hymn of fishers by a lake, / called once to leave their nets and follow", a reference to the gospel story of fishermen who left their nets to follow Jesus. The allusion carries associations of vocation and renunciation. But the next words complicate its source: "she has not left". She honours the image of fishers and nets while declining the call to abandon them, so her devotion is to the work itself, and the poem quietly suggests that fidelity to a craft can be a vocation too. The plain "She mends" carries the claim without emphasis.
>
> The last two lines redefine the central symbol. What she holds "between her thumb and thread is not / a net, exactly, but a way of holding on". The line break after "not" delays the correction, and the phrase moves the net from an object to a practice. It is also ambiguous: "holding on" can mean continuing faithfully or clinging stubbornly. The poem does not choose, and the tension keeps the speaker's attitude complex, admiring but aware that the loyalty has a cost.
>
> The poem therefore suggests that what the woman preserves is less a livelihood than a continuity: the net becomes a way of staying connected to her family, to the sea and to a life that official lines are narrowing. The speaker's attitude, tender, respectful and faintly elegiac, is built from the same details that make her work seem both ordinary and precious.

**Annotation: the line of reasoning.**

| Paragraph | Claim | Evidence | Unit 8 skills used |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | Work as agreement and inheritance | "knots go in like rhyme", "small agreement", "His father too" | Metaphor (8.4), diction (8.2), syntax |
| 3 | The turn shows pressure through contrast | "The boats are fewer", "drawn... line" and "drawn... lines" | Structure and contrast (8.1) |
| 4 | Fidelity as a vocation of its own | "hymn of fishers", "she has not left" | Allusion (8.5), diction |
| 5 | The symbol is redefined and made ambiguous | "not / a net, exactly, but a way of holding on" | Symbol (8.3), ambiguity (8.2) |
| 6 | Whole-poem meaning | Synthesis | Thesis and commentary (8.6) |

**Self-score against the suggested Marlbridge rubric.**

| Row | Evidence in the model | Judgment |
|---|---|---|
| Thesis | A defensible interpretation (fidelity redefined as continuity), responding to the prompt | Earns the point |
| Evidence and commentary | Short quotations embedded and explained for each claim, several techniques (simile, metaphor, echo, allusion, ambiguity), commentary linked to the thesis | Top band |
| Sophistication | The ambiguity of "holding on" is developed into a tension (faithful or stubborn) that is carried into the conclusion | Likely to earn the point |
