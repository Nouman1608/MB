## Recap

A **narrator** relates the events and sets up a relationship between text and reader; a narrator is **not** necessarily the author. **Point of view** is the position from which events are told (first person, third person). **Perspective** is how the narrator sees and judges the circumstances. Together they control the details and emphases the reader receives.

## Key relationships

| Idea | What it means | What to ask |
|---|---|---|
| First person | Narrator is involved in the events | What does this person know, miss, or want me to believe? |
| Third observational | Outside narrator reporting what can be seen | What is shown only through action and speech? |
| Third limited | Outside narrator tied to one mind | What does the character not know that I can see? |
| Third omniscient | Outside narrator with wide knowledge | What does the narrator comment on or judge? |
| Perspective | How the narrator sees circumstances | Which words carry judgement? |
| Reader's relationship | Trust, doubt, irony | Where do I see more than the narrator says? |

## What you may assume

- A narrator is a created voice, not the writer.
- A first-person narrator may be mistaken or selective.
- A third-person narrator is not automatically neutral.

## Mistakes to avoid

- Writing "the author thinks" instead of "the narrator suggests".
- Naming the point of view with no effect ("it is first person").
- Treating a narrator's claim as fact.
- Forgetting to say what the narrator cannot know.
- Confusing perspective with point of view.

## Key terms

- **Narrator:** the voice that relates the events; not necessarily the author.
- **Point of view:** the position from which events are told (first person, third person).
- **Perspective:** how the narrator sees and judges the circumstances.
- **Limited and omniscient:** a limited narrator is tied to one mind; an omniscient narrator knows several minds and events.

## Sentence frames

- "Because the narrator is [first person / third person limited], the reader receives … but must infer …."
- "The narrator's phrase '…' carries a judgement that …."
- "The gap between what the narrator says and what the details show suggests …."

## Mini-example

Narrator: a child recalling a summer. Says: "he did not like pears". Details show: the mother carries pears daily. Effect: the reader sees reconciliation that the narrator misses.

## How questions ask about this

- *"The narrator's point of view is best described as …"*: check pronouns and how close the narration stays to one mind.
- *"Which of the following can the narrator not provide?"*: think about what the position excludes (other minds, unseen events).
- *"The phrase … most directly reveals the narrator's attitude"*: find the words that carry judgement.
- Constructed response: name the point of view, what it lets the narrator provide, and what the reader must infer.

## Quick self-check

1. A narrator says "I decided he did not like pears." Which point of view is this, and what does the sentence show about the narrator?
2. Why might a writer choose a limited narrator?
3. In "a truth universally acknowledged", who is the narrator reporting for?

**Answers.**

1. First person; it shows a perspective that reaches a simple conclusion from partial information.
2. A limited narrator creates a gap: the reader can see more than the narrator, which creates irony, suspense or sympathy.
3. For a community, whose assumptions the narrator reports. The word "acknowledged" signals a shared belief that the narrator sets at a slight distance.
