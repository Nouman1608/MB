## Recap

- **Literal meaning** is what words state on the surface; **figurative meaning** is what they suggest beyond it.
- A quick test: if the literal reading is impossible or beside the point, the phrase is working figuratively.
- **Connotation** is the set of associations a word carries beyond its dictionary meaning (denotation).
- Words with **multiple meanings or connotations** add nuance and complexity that can contribute to interpretation.
- **Descriptive words** (adjectives and adverbs) qualify what they describe and affect how readers respond.
- **Hyperbole** exaggerates and **understatement** minimises; each focuses attention on a trait and conveys a perspective.
- Explain a word's function by **comparing it with an alternative** the poet could have used.

## Key relationships

| Idea | What to ask | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Literal | What do the words state? | the seas will dry |
| Figurative | What do they suggest? | a love that does not end |
| Connotation | What does the word carry? | *treads* is heavy and plain |
| Double meaning | Does the word support two senses? | *lease* as contract and as time |
| Hyperbole | What does exaggeration stress? | length and intensity of love |
| Understatement | What does minimising stress? | restraint, irony, plain honesty |

| Step | Four-step method |
|---|---|
| 1 | Literal: state the surface meaning |
| 2 | Figurative: say what it suggests |
| 3 | Word: pick the specific word or phrase |
| 4 | Effect: say what it does to attitude and meaning |

## Assumptions

- You begin from the literal sense before interpreting figuratively.
- Connotations are supported by **context**, not just personal impression.
- Hyperbole and understatement are judged by the gap between what is said and what is meant.

## Mistakes to avoid

- Skipping the literal meaning.
- Naming "hyperbole" without saying what it conveys.
- Treating figurative as meaning "untrue".
- Treating adjectives as mere decoration.
- Offering a connotation with no support from the poem.

## Quick self-check

1. Is "till a' the seas gang dry" literal or figurative? How do you know?
2. What is the connotation of "crept" compared with "walked"?
3. What does understatement stress about a speaker?
4. Why is comparing a word with an alternative useful?

**Answers.** (1) Figurative (hyperbole): the literal event is impossible, and the impossibility measures the love's length. (2) Fear, guilt or secrecy, as opposed to neutral movement. (3) Restraint, irony or a reluctance to show feeling, by treating something large as small. (4) It shows what the chosen word adds that the alternative lacks, so the function is explained, not merely asserted.

## Sentence frames to adapt

- "Literally, [words] describe [surface meaning]; figuratively, they suggest [idea]."
- "The verb '[word]' carries connotations of [x], where '[alternative]' would suggest [y]."
- "The exaggeration in '[phrase]' stresses [trait], conveying a [sincere / ironic] perspective."
- "By minimising [thing] as '[phrase]', the speaker shows [restraint / irony / denial]."

Fill the gaps with a quoted phrase and a precise word for the attitude or association; keep the alternative word in mind to show that the choice matters.
