## How to use this guide

This guide is for students who understand the course skills but want to turn them into three finished essays under time pressure. Read the sections in order, study the annotated models, then try the practice tasks near the end.

Every example here is **original Marlbridge practice**. The poem and the prose passage were written by Marlbridge for this guide. The literary argument model uses *Macbeth*, a public-domain play, and its quotations were checked against the Project Gutenberg text. None of this is past exam material. The rubric in this guide is a **suggested Marlbridge rubric**, written to show what a strong essay does. It is not an official scoring guideline, and the guide gives **no predicted score**.

## The essay section at a glance

The free-response section has three essays and is 55% of the exam. The section lasts 2 hours, and about 40 minutes per essay is recommended. Each essay is scored out of 6 points.

| Essay | What you are given | What you must do | Source of evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Poetry analysis | One poem of about 100 to 400 words | Analyse how the poet uses poetic elements and techniques to develop a complex idea or relationship named in the prompt | The poem in front of you |
| 2. Prose fiction analysis | One passage of about 600 to 800 words from a novel or story | Analyse how the writer uses literary elements and techniques to convey a complex idea, attitude or situation named in the prompt | The passage in front of you |
| 3. Literary argument | A prompt naming a concept, plus a list of about 40 suggested works | Choose a work of fiction you know well and analyse how the concept contributes to an interpretation of the work as a whole | Your memory of the work |

Every prompt ends with the same four instructions: respond with a defensible thesis, select and use evidence, explain how the evidence supports your line of reasoning, and use appropriate grammar and punctuation. Those four instructions are the scoring structure.

### The scoring dimensions

All three essays use the same three rows.

| Row | Points | What it rewards |
|---|---|---|
| A: Thesis | 0 or 1 | A defensible interpretation that responds to the prompt, not a restatement or a summary |
| B: Evidence and commentary | 0 to 4 | Specific evidence for each claim, and commentary that explains how it supports a line of reasoning |
| C: Sophistication | 0 or 1 | A sustained move that shows complex understanding, such as exploring a tension, setting the reading in a broader context, accounting for an alternative reading, or writing with a consistently vivid and persuasive style |

Row B has a note worth remembering: writing with grammar or mechanics errors that **interfere with communication** cannot earn the fourth point in that row. You do not need perfect prose, but a reader must be able to follow every sentence.

## Step 1: read, then find the tension

Essay quality is decided before you write a word. In the first minutes, do these things.

1. **Read the prompt first.** It tells you the idea the essay must be about: a complex relationship, attitude, situation or concept. Underline it.
2. **Read the text once straight through.** Do not annotate yet. You are listening for what it is about and where it turns.
3. **Read again with a pencil.** Mark shifts (a "But", a change of tense, a new image, a break), repeated words, and the strongest images.
4. **Name the tension.** A tension is two things pulling against each other: duty and desire, pride and loss, comfort and constraint. Strong theses nearly always come from a tension.
5. **Write the idea in one sentence** before you decide how to word the thesis: "The speaker sees X as a gift, but..." or "The narrator admires Y while quietly judging..."

For the literary argument essay, replace steps 2 to 4 with three questions: Which work do I know well enough to argue about? Which three or four moments in it speak to this prompt? What does the concept *do* for the work as a whole?

## Step 2: write a thesis that can be defended

The exam's wording is exact: a thesis must present a **defensible interpretation** that responds to the prompt. The scoring notes list what does not earn the point: restating the prompt, a generalised comment that does not respond to it, and describing the text instead of making a claim that needs a defence.

| Type | Example for the prose passage below | Earns the point? |
|---|---|---|
| Restates the prompt | "The writer uses literary techniques to portray Mrs. Anselm's complex response to leaving her job." | No |
| Describes the text | "Mrs. Anselm arrives early, wipes the counter and lines up the stamps." | No |
| Too general | "People find change difficult." | No |
| Defensible interpretation | "The narrator presents Mrs. Anselm's last morning as an act of concealed grief: she hides her pain in precise routines and a joke, and the gentle irony shows that her pride and her love for the work are the same feeling." | Yes |

A good thesis has three features.

- **It makes a claim someone could argue against.** If no reader could disagree, it is a description.
- **It answers the prompt.** Use the prompt's own focus (the relationship, the attitude, the concept), but not its sentence.
- **It can carry a line of reasoning.** A thesis with an "although" or "by... Shakespeare shows..." shape has room for three or four claims.

The thesis may be more than one sentence, and it may appear anywhere in the essay. Put it early. A thesis that arrives in the last paragraph forces the reader to reread the essay to see what it was building.

### From thesis to line of reasoning

A **line of reasoning** is the order of your claims, each of which supports the thesis. Plan it as a list of claims, not as a list of devices.

- Weak plan: "Paragraph 1: imagery. Paragraph 2: simile. Paragraph 3: structure."
- Stronger plan: "Claim 1: she hides her grief in routine. Claim 2: the narrator's irony treats this with affection, not mockery. Claim 3: the closing joke shows that her wit is her way of staying in control."

Each claim is a mini-argument that builds on the last, so the essay moves forward instead of circling.

## Step 3: evidence and commentary

Row B is worth four of the six points, so this is where most essays are won or lost. It has two parts that must work together.

**Evidence.** Choose short, specific details: a word, a phrase, a repeated image, a change in sentence length. "The poem uses a lot of imagery" is not evidence. "The 'white as weather' flour" is.

**Commentary.** Explain how the evidence supports the claim. A reliable chain has four links.

1. **Detail:** what exactly is in the text (quote briefly).
2. **Device:** the literary or poetic element at work (simile, diction, syntax, shift, structure, point of view).
3. **Effect:** what the device does to the reader's understanding, in specific words.
4. **Link:** how that effect supports your claim and your thesis.

The scoring notes distinguish levels clearly. Evidence that is mostly general scores low. Specific evidence for every claim, with consistent commentary, scores high. Commentary that only summarises the evidence ("The narrator says she wiped the counter. This shows she wiped the counter") scores low, because it does not explain *how the evidence supports* anything.

To reach the top of the row, the commentary must also explain how **multiple** literary elements or techniques contribute to the meaning. Observing the same technique twice is fine if each instance adds something new.

### Weak, better and strong commentary

| Level | Example (poem below) | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Summary | "The baker taps each loaf. This shows he checks the bread." | Repeats the detail |
| Device named | "The simile of the doctor shows the baker's care." | Names a device and a vague effect |
| Device and effect | "Comparing the baker to a doctor who 'listens at a back' makes bread-testing a form of diagnosis, so a routine job becomes attentive care." | Specific effect, but not yet linked |
| Linked to the line of reasoning | "Comparing the baker to a doctor who 'listens at a back' makes bread-testing a form of diagnosis. The tenderness it gives to a task that began as inheritance shows that the work has become chosen care, which is why the poem can end on the stranger without bitterness." | Detail, device, effect and link to the claim |

## Timing: about 40 minutes per essay

| Minutes | Activity | Aim |
|---|---|---|
| 0 to 6 | Read the prompt and text, mark shifts and images, find the tension | One-sentence idea |
| 6 to 9 | Draft the thesis; list three claims with one piece of evidence each | A visible line of reasoning |
| 9 to 36 | Write: thesis first, then one paragraph per claim | Each paragraph: claim, evidence, commentary, link |
| 36 to 40 | Reread: fix unclear sentences, add a complication if one is missing | Clarity |

Adjust for the literary argument essay: spend the first 5 minutes choosing the work and listing four moments, then write. A rule of thumb is that planning for 7 to 8 minutes saves more time in writing than it costs.

If you run short, finish the paragraph you are writing and add a one-sentence complication. A complete, organised essay of three paragraphs beats an unfinished essay of five.

## Essay 1: poetry analysis, annotated model

Here is an original poem, written by Marlbridge, and a prompt in the stable exam wording.

**Night Bakery** (written by Marlbridge)

> At three the ovens open like a held breath.  
> He tips the flour; it rises, white as weather,  
> and settles on his forearms like a mark  
> of some old trade nobody asked him to learn.
>
> His father stood here, and his mother kept  
> the accounts in a ledger with a burn  
> across its cover, from some forgotten fire.
>
> By five the loaves knock softly in the racks,  
> hollow and warm. He taps each one, the way  
> a doctor listens at a back for what is sound.
>
> Then light. The shutters lift. A stranger's hand  
> takes bread across the counter and is gone.

**Prompt.** In the poem "Night Bakery," the speaker describes a baker's night work and the people the bread is for. Read the poem carefully. Then, in a well-written essay, analyze how the poet uses poetic elements and techniques to develop the complex relationship between the baker and his work.

### Planning notes

- *Tension:* the work is inherited and unchosen ("nobody asked him"), yet he performs it with tenderness. It is also unseen: the stranger takes the bread "and is gone."
- *Shift:* stanza 4 begins "Then light," a change from dark, enclosed work to public daylight.
- *Devices to notice:* two similes in stanza 1 ("like a held breath," "like a mark"), the ledger's "burn," the doctor simile in stanza 3, the final short sentence.

### Model thesis and line of reasoning

*Thesis:* **"Night Bakery presents the baker's work as an inheritance he did not choose that becomes a quiet form of care: through similes that move from burden to attention, a family ledger that records loss, and an ending that withholds thanks, the poem shows devotion as something practised without recognition."**

*Line of reasoning:*

1. The opening frames the work as a mark he did not choose.
2. The family ledger makes the work a continuing inheritance that carries damage.
3. The doctor simile changes the work from duty into attention.
4. The ending complicates the care: it reaches a stranger who does not stay.

### Annotated body paragraph (claim 1)

> **[Claim]** The opening presents the baker's work as an inheritance that marks him before he can accept it. **[Evidence 1]** The flour "settles on his forearms like a mark," **[Commentary: device and effect]** and the simile makes a material of his trade into a brand: a mark is something put on a person, not something chosen. **[Evidence 2]** The following phrase, "of some old trade nobody asked him to learn," **[Commentary]** repeats the idea in the flatter language of an explanation, and the word "nobody" quietly removes any decision, so the line carries a little resentment. **[Link]** Yet the first line has already offered a more generous picture: the ovens "open like a held breath," as though the room itself is waiting. **[Complication, inside the claim]** The reader therefore meets the baker's burden and the dignity of his surroundings together, which is the first sign that his relationship to the work cannot be resolved into either complaint or pride.

What this paragraph does well: it makes a claim, gives two short pieces of evidence, explains the *effect* of the device, and links to the thesis by the idea of a relationship that is neither simply resentful nor simply proud.

### Annotated body paragraph (claim 4, the complication)

> **[Claim]** The poem's last two lines complicate the care it has built. **[Evidence]** After the doctor's attentive tapping, the shutters "lift," and "a stranger's hand / takes bread across the counter and is gone." **[Commentary: structure and syntax]** The sentence moves quickly and ends on the short verb "gone," and its pace after the slow tactile stanza makes the transaction feel like a loss. **[Commentary: diction]** The word "stranger" suggests that the bread's receiver has no relationship with the person who made it, and "hand" reduces the customer to a single part, as the baker's own arms were marked by flour. **[Link]** The poem therefore refuses a tidy conclusion that care is repaid by recognition. It shows care as something given to people who will not return it, and that is why the baker's work can be both an inheritance and, in the end, a choice to go on.

The final sentence ties the paragraph back to both the thesis and the tension identified in planning.

### Where the sophistication point comes from

In this model, the sophistication is not a separate flourish. It comes from the way the essay handles a **tension**: the work is unchosen *and* tender, and the poem's ending shows care continuing without reward. The essay would earn the point because that tension shapes the whole argument, in the thesis, in claim 1 and in claim 4.

## Essay 2: prose fiction analysis, annotated model

Here is an original passage written by Marlbridge. The real exam passage is longer, about 600 to 800 words, so this is an extract for practising one paragraph.

> On her last morning at the post office Mrs. Anselm arrived early, as she had for thirty-one years, and found that someone had already unlocked the door. She did not mind. She hung her coat on the hook that had never held anyone else's, wiped the counter although it was clean, and lined up the date stamps in the order of their age. Through the window the square was filling slowly with the people who would, at nine, come to say goodbye to her, and she resented them, a little, for coming. A goodbye, she thought, ought to be a thing one did quietly, like closing a drawer. When the first of them knocked she opened the shutter and said, "We are not open until nine," and watched the face on the other side try to decide whether she was joking.

**Prompt.** The following passage was written by Marlbridge for practice. In this passage, a character is about to leave a job she has held for most of her life. Read the passage carefully. Then, in a well-written essay, analyze how the writer uses literary elements and techniques to portray Mrs. Anselm's complex response to leaving.

### Planning notes

- *Tension:* routine and pride against feelings she will not show.
- *Narration:* third person, close to Mrs. Anselm, with a light ironic distance ("She did not mind," "a little").
- *Details to notice:* the hook "that had never held anyone else's," the clean counter wiped anyway, stamps lined up by age, the resentment of well-wishers, the closing-a-drawer simile, the closing joke.

### Model thesis

> **Through precise domestic detail, a narrator who keeps a gentle ironic distance, and a closing joke that hides emotion in wit, the passage presents Mrs. Anselm's last morning as concealed grief: her insistence on routine and her resentment of kindness are the forms her love for the work takes.**

### Annotated body paragraph

> **[Claim]** The narrator shows Mrs. Anselm's grief indirectly, by letting her routines do the speaking. **[Evidence 1]** She "wiped the counter although it was clean" and "lined up the date stamps in the order of their age." **[Commentary: detail and syntax]** The word "although" admits that the action is unnecessary, and the second sentence strings together three tidy actions in a calm, even rhythm, so the reader sees a woman using order to steady herself. **[Evidence 2]** The ordering of the stamps "in the order of their age" turns time into a thing she can arrange. **[Commentary: effect]** At the moment she is being asked to part with thirty-one years, she tidies them. **[Link]** Because the narrator never names her feeling, the reader must infer grief from behaviour, and that indirectness is itself the passage's way of respecting her reserve.

### Annotated body paragraph (the narrator's attitude)

> **[Claim]** The narrator's irony is affectionate, not mocking. **[Evidence]** Mrs. Anselm "resented them, a little, for coming," and she thinks that a goodbye "ought to be a thing one did quietly, like closing a drawer." **[Commentary: tone]** The qualifier "a little" and the neat drawer simile make her feelings sound reasonable and slightly comic at once. The narrator smiles at her fussiness, but the smile comes with understanding: closing a drawer is a small, private act, and the simile shows how she wishes a large, public loss could be. **[Link]** The same blend of humour and tenderness reaches its peak in the last sentence, where her joke ("We are not open until nine") makes her visitor "decide whether she was joking." The reader, who knows what the routine has cost her, can see that the joke is a defence.

### Sophistication here

The essay's complexity comes from holding two ideas together: her reserve is a form of pride, **and** the narrator treats that pride kindly. A weaker essay would say the narrator "mocks" her or that she is simply "sad." The sophisticated reading explains why the passage supports both humour and sympathy.

## Essay 3: literary argument, annotated model

**Prompt (written by Marlbridge).** Many works of literature feature a character who makes a choice that cannot be undone. Either from your own reading or from a list of suggestions, choose a work of fiction in which a character makes such a choice. Then, in a well-written essay, analyze how the choice contributes to an interpretation of the work as a whole. Do not merely summarize the plot.

### Plan from memory

Choose a work you know well. Here the choice is Shakespeare's *Macbeth* (about 1606).

| Moment | Reference | What it gives |
|---|---|---|
| The murder of Duncan and its aftermath | Act 2, Scene 2: "Macbeth does murder sleep" | The choice has an immediate inner cost |
| A decision to continue | Act 3, Scene 4: "I am in blood / Stepp'd in so far that, should I wade no more, / Returning were as tedious as go o'er" | The choice becomes a trap |
| Lady Macbeth's collapse | Act 5, Scene 1: "Out, damned spot!" | The cost falls on the people nearest him |
| The final emptiness | Act 5, Scene 5: "I have almost forgot the taste of fears" and "Tomorrow, and tomorrow, and tomorrow" | The choice has emptied his world of meaning |

### Model thesis and line of reasoning

*Thesis:* **"In *Macbeth*, Shakespeare makes the irreversible choice to kill Duncan the origin of a gradual emptying of meaning: the murder first costs Macbeth peace, then reduces his future to a single direction, and finally leaves him unable to feel anything, so that the play argues that ambition destroys the very capacity to value what it wins."**

Line of reasoning: (1) the choice destroys sleep and peace; (2) it turns the future into a narrowing path; (3) the cost spreads to Lady Macbeth; (4) it ends in numbness.

### Annotated body paragraph (claim 2)

> **[Claim]** After the murder, the choice becomes less a decision than a direction, and the play uses Macbeth's own image to say so. **[Evidence]** In Act 3, Scene 4 he says that he is "in blood / Stepp'd in so far that, should I wade no more, / Returning were as tedious as go o'er." **[Commentary: figurative language]** The extended river image makes crime a crossing: he is already in the middle, so going back is as tiring as going on. **[Commentary: effect]** The word "tedious" is unexpectedly mild for a man who has killed a king, and that understatement shows him weighing murder as an effort, not a horror. **[Link]** This is the point at which the irreversible choice begins to govern his later decisions, and it prepares the play's argument that ambition does not merely win a crown but removes the freedom to turn back.

### Annotated body paragraph (claim 4, with sophistication)

> **[Claim]** The final scenes show what the irreversible choice has done to Macbeth's ability to feel. **[Evidence 1]** He says that he has "almost forgot the taste of fears," and, told that his wife is dead, he answers that she "should have died hereafter" before reducing life to "Tomorrow, and tomorrow, and tomorrow." **[Commentary]** The repeated "tomorrow" makes time itself dull and mechanical, and the taste metaphor suggests that fear, once an inner sense, has become a lost appetite. **[Complication]** The speech is often read as pure despair, but its bareness is also a kind of precision: Macbeth sees his situation more clearly than at any earlier point, and he cannot use that clarity to change it. **[Link]** That combination of clear sight and helplessness is what turns a story of ambition into an argument about what ambition costs, and it is why the choice in Act 2 is the work's governing event and not merely its first crime.

What the paragraph shows about literary argument: the claim is about the whole play, the references are specific and brief, and the complication (clear sight without power) belongs to the argument, not to a decorative last line.

## A suggested Marlbridge rubric for self-marking

Use this to check a practice essay. It follows the three rows of the exam's scoring structure, but it is **Marlbridge's own and not official scoring**.

| Row | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A: Thesis (0 to 1) | No defensible thesis, or the thesis restates the prompt, summarises, or ignores the prompt | A claim that interprets the text and answers the prompt | n/a | n/a | n/a |
| B: Evidence and commentary (0 to 4) | Restates the thesis, repeats information, or gives irrelevant material | Mostly general evidence; commentary summarises and does not explain | Some specific evidence; some explanation, but no clear line of reasoning (or a faulty one) | Specific evidence for all claims; commentary explains how some evidence supports a line of reasoning; at least one literary element explained | Specific evidence for all claims; commentary is consistent; multiple elements or techniques explained and linked to the interpretation |
| C: Sophistication (0 to 1) | Does not meet the criteria | Complexity or context is part of the argument, not just a phrase | n/a | n/a | n/a |

### Checking your own sophistication

The point is awarded only for a **sustained** move that is part of the argument. These do **not** earn it:

- Sweeping generalisations such as "Throughout history, people have..."
- A sentence that only hints at another reading ("Although some may say...") and then drops it.
- One thematic comment that the essay does not keep up.
- Elaborate language that does not improve the argument.

These can earn it, if they appear throughout the essay's reasoning:

1. Identifying and exploring **complexities or tensions** within the text.
2. **Situating** the interpretation in a broader context, and showing why it matters.
3. Accounting for an **alternative interpretation** and answering it.
4. A style that is **consistently vivid and persuasive**.

## Common errors and how to fix them

| Problem | What it looks like | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Restating the prompt | "The author uses techniques to show complexity." | Replace "complexity" with the specific tension you found |
| Narrating the text | "First, then, next..." | Start each paragraph with a claim; use details as evidence |
| Device-spotting | A list of terms with no effect | Add the effect and the link for every device |
| Empty verbs | "shows," "uses," "conveys" with no object | Use precise verbs: "recasts," "withholds," "undercuts," "intensifies" |
| Long quotations | Three lines copied out | Quote the shortest accurate phrase and build the sentence around it |
| Final-sentence sophistication | A grand claim in the last line | Move the complication into the body, inside a claim |
| Introducing evidence you cannot explain | A striking quotation that does not fit the claim | Choose evidence that supports the claim, even if it is plainer |
| Literary argument from one scene | An essay entirely on the first chapter | Spread evidence across the work (beginning, middle, end) |

## Practise it

**Task 1 (poetry).** Using "Night Bakery," write the third body paragraph of the model (the paragraph that treats the doctor simile in stanza 3). Include one short quotation and use the four-link chain. Then mark your paragraph against Row B.

**Task 2 (prose).** Rewrite this weak paragraph so that it makes a claim, uses one short quotation and explains an effect: *"Mrs. Anselm is old-fashioned. She wipes the counter and lines up the stamps. This shows that she likes things neat."*

**Task 3 (literary argument).** Choose a novel or play you know well. For the prompt in Essay 3, write a thesis and a four-claim line of reasoning, and list one specific reference for each claim. If you cannot give an exact quotation, write an exact scene reference instead.

**Task 4 (timed).** Give yourself 40 minutes for a poem you have not seen from a public-domain collection, or from the poetry practice pages in this library. Follow the timing table. Afterwards, mark yourself on the rubric and write one sentence on the row you most need to improve.

## Self-check before you hand in

- Is there a thesis that makes a claim someone could disagree with?
- Does every body paragraph begin with a claim, not a summary?
- Is every quotation short, and is it followed by a sentence saying what the device does?
- Does at least one paragraph handle a tension, a context or an alternative reading, as part of the argument?
- Can a stranger follow every sentence?

## Common misconceptions

- **"A longer essay scores higher."** The rubric rewards support and explanation, not length. A focused three-paragraph essay with strong commentary beats a long list of devices.
- **"The more devices I name, the better."** Naming a device earns nothing unless you explain what it does to the meaning.
- **"A quotation is evidence by itself."** Evidence needs commentary. Without it, the essay summarises.
- **"The sophistication point belongs in the conclusion."** It must be sustained and part of the argument, so it belongs in the thesis and the body as well.
- **"There is one correct interpretation."** There is no single answer key. A defensible reading is one the text gives evidence for and the essay explains.
