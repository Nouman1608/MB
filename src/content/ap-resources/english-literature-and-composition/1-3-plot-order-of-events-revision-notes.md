## Recap

**Plot** is the sequence of events in a narrative, with each event connected to others, often as cause and effect. Plot and **exposition** focus the reader on what matters most. A writer may change the order of events to control what the reader knows. A strong analysis says what a sequence *does*, not just what happens.

## Key relationships

| Idea | What it means | Question to ask |
|---|---|---|
| Exposition | Introduces characters, setting, situation | What must I know before things change? |
| Rising action | Events that develop the conflict | What complicates things, and why? |
| Climax | The decisive turn | What cannot be undone after this? |
| Resolution | Consequences and any settlement | What has changed, what remains open? |
| Flashback | A return to earlier events | What does it explain or change? |
| Withholding | Delaying a key detail | What does the reader feel or guess meanwhile? |

## What you may assume

- A writer chooses the order of events, so the order can be questioned.
- Not every story has all four stages; describe what is there.
- A single event can serve more than one function (for example, both climax and revelation).

## Mistakes to avoid

- Summarising events instead of explaining their sequence.
- Writing "the flashback gives background" with no effect.
- Treating the climax as the noisiest moment.
- Forcing a story into stages it does not have.
- Forgetting to connect events to character or meaning.

## Key terms

- **Plot:** the sequence of connected events in a narrative.
- **Exposition:** information that introduces characters, setting and situation.
- **Flashback:** a return to earlier events inside the present of the narrative.
- **Withholding:** delaying a key detail so the reader's knowledge changes with the character's.

## Sentence frames

- "By opening with …, the writer makes the reader ask …, so that the later explanation …."
- "The event '…' causes …, which leads to …."
- "The sequence moves the reader's attention from … to …."

## Mini-example

Order: the empty slip first, the sale afterwards. Effect on knowledge: the reader first sees calm and then learns the cause. Result: calm becomes ambivalence.

## How questions ask about this

- *"The sequence of events in … mainly serves to …"*: say what the order makes the reader notice.
- *"The function of the flashback is to …"*: look for cause, background that changes meaning, or contrast with the present.
- *"Which pair of events are linked as cause and effect?"*: test whether the first leads to the second, not merely precedes it.
- Constructed response: describe the order briefly, then explain what it does to the reader's knowledge or expectation.

## Quick self-check

1. "The headmistress asks, so he opens the bell, so he finds a note." What kind of connection does "so" show?
2. Why might a writer open a story at the empty harbour slip and explain the sale afterwards?
3. What is wrong with "the climax is when the bell is opened because it is exciting"?

**Answers.**

1. Cause and effect: each event leads to the next.
2. Opening on the empty slip makes the reader ask why the character is calm, so the later explanation changes how they read his reaction.
3. It evaluates ("exciting") and does not say what the moment does. A better statement is that the discovery changes the meaning of the delay.
