These are **original Marlbridge practice questions**, not past exam questions. The poems were written by Marlbridge for this practice. Mark points are a **suggested Marlbridge rubric**, not an official scoring guideline. Multiple-choice questions have four options.

**Poem P (written by Marlbridge), "Reply to the Landlord".**

> Dear Sir, the roof is mended, as you asked,  
> or mended enough. I have stood on the stair  
> and counted the drips, and where there were nine  
> there are three. I do not complain of three.  
> You wrote that the tenants have been "difficult."  
> I am sorry to be a tenant. I have kept  
> the tin of paint you left, unopened, for the day  
> when I may have a wall I can call mine.

## Question 1 (multiple choice · core)

The opening "Dear Sir" most directly establishes that the speaker is

- (A) writing formally to someone with power over her
- (B) writing a casual note to a friend
- (C) addressing the reader of a newspaper
- (D) speaking to herself

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (A).** "Dear Sir" is a formal address that places the speaker as a tenant writing to a landlord.

- (B) is contradicted by the formality.
- (C) is not supported; the speaker later refers to what "you wrote".
- (D) is wrong because the poem is directed at an addressee.
</details>

## Question 2 (multiple choice · core)

"Where there were nine there are three. I do not complain of three." The speaker's decision not to complain most strongly suggests that she

- (A) is satisfied with the state of the roof
- (B) wants to be seen as reasonable while noting that the repair is incomplete
- (C) did not notice the three drips
- (D) has decided to leave the flat

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (B).** She reports the count and says she does not "complain", which makes the complaint indirectly while keeping the tone civil.

- (A) is contradicted by "or mended enough" and by the fact that she counts the drips.
- (C) is contradicted by the count.
- (D) is not stated; she keeps the paint "for the day when I may have a wall".
</details>

## Question 3 (multiple choice · core)

"You wrote that the tenants have been 'difficult.' / I am sorry to be a tenant." The second sentence most likely conveys

- (A) sincere regret about her behaviour
- (B) controlled irony: she turns the landlord's complaint back on his view of tenants
- (C) confusion about what the landlord meant
- (D) pride in being a tenant

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (B).** She does not apologise for any act but for the status "tenant", which implies that the landlord's word treats the condition of renting as a fault.

- (A) would require her to name a particular behaviour.
- (C) is contradicted by her precise reply to the word.
- (D) misreads the tone; the line is restrained and ironic, not proud.
</details>

## Question 4 (multiple choice · stretch)

The tin of paint, "unopened", most directly conveys the speaker's

- (A) wish to own a home in which she is free to make changes
- (B) intention to repaint the flat tomorrow
- (C) disrespect for the landlord's property
- (D) indifference to the appearance of the flat

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (A).** She is waiting for "a wall I can call mine"; the unopened tin signals restraint and aspiration.

- (B) contradicts "unopened, for the day when".
- (C) is contradicted by her careful keeping of the tin.
- (D) is contradicted by her wish for a wall of her own.
</details>

## Question 5 (multiple choice · core)

Which of the following best describes the speaker's tone across the poem?

- (A) Openly angry
- (B) Cheerful and carefree
- (C) Restrained and formal, with an undercurrent of resentment
- (D) Confused and uncertain

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (C).** The formal address, the counted drips and the apology that is not an apology combine politeness with pointed remarks.

- (A) overstates; the speaker never raises her voice.
- (B) contradicts the details of complaint.
- (D) is contradicted by the precision of her statements.
</details>

## Question 6 (multiple choice · stretch)

Which statement about the speaker is best supported by the poem?

- (A) She is the poet describing a real flat.
- (B) She is a voice created by the poet, and her perspective is shown by her word choice and details.
- (C) She is the landlord, writing to himself.
- (D) She has no perspective because she reports facts.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (B).** A speaker is a created voice, and what she chooses to report reveals perspective.

- (A) confuses speaker and poet; the poem does not tell us this.
- (C) contradicts "Dear Sir" and "You wrote".
- (D) is wrong because selection of facts ("counted the drips") is itself revealing.
</details>

## Question 7 (constructed response · core)

Write **two to three sentences** making a claim about the speaker's perspective on her situation, and support it with **two** short quotations from Poem P.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Sample response.** The speaker's perspective is that of a tenant who wants fair treatment but dares not demand it. She counts the drips, "where there were nine / there are three", and then says "I do not complain of three", which lets the evidence speak while avoiding open conflict.

**Suggested Marlbridge rubric (3 points).**

| Point | Awarded for |
|---|---|
| 1 | A claim about the speaker's perspective, not a summary |
| 2 | Two accurate quotations or close paraphrases |
| 3 | An explanation of how the lines support the claim |
</details>

## Question 8 (constructed response · stretch)

Explain how the speaker's word choices and the organisation of her thoughts in Poem P reveal her attitude to the landlord. Refer to at least **three** details.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Sample response.** The speaker's attitude is controlled resentment. The formal "Dear Sir" and the polite "mended enough" keep the surface civil. She builds her case with numbers ("nine … three") and then withdraws the complaint, which lets the figures do the criticising. Her reply to the word "difficult", "I am sorry to be a tenant", turns the landlord's insult into a statement about his view of tenants. The poem ends not with anger but with the unopened paint, which shows that her resentment is turned into a wish for a place of her own.

**Suggested Marlbridge rubric (4 points).**

| Point | Awarded for |
|---|---|
| 1 | Identifies an attitude (restrained resentment, irony, dignity) |
| 2 | Uses at least three accurate details |
| 3 | Explains at least one choice of word or order of thought (counting, withdrawal of complaint, reply to "difficult") |
| 4 | Links the details to the speaker's perspective as a whole |
</details>
