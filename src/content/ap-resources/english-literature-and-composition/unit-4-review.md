Read the big ideas and the method table, then try the five questions without notes. Each question combines two or more Unit 4 topics. The last is a full prose analysis essay.

These are **original Marlbridge practice questions**, not past exam questions. All unseen passages were written by Marlbridge for practice; the public-domain extract is from Kate Chopin, "The Story of an Hour" (1894). The rubrics are a **suggested Marlbridge rubric**, not official scoring.

## Big ideas of the unit

- **Characters reveal values through choices.** A contrast between characters is a contrast of values, and an antagonist is whatever opposes the protagonist: a person, a group, nature or an inner conflict. (Topics 4.1 and 4.2)
- **Relationships are layered.** Speech, action, avoidance, gesture and narration show what a relationship is really like; conflict often comes from different value systems. (Topic 4.2)
- **Setting works.** It creates mood and atmosphere and tells us about the people who inhabit it. (Topic 4.3)
- **Order is a choice.** The order in which a text reveals information shapes understanding, and contrasts within a text emphasise particular traits and conflicts of values. (Topic 4.4)
- **A narrator is a perspective.** Narrative distance, point of view and the details, diction and syntax a narrator uses reveal perspective and tone. (Topic 4.5)
- **An argument needs a thesis, a line of reasoning and commentary.** Evidence must be relevant and sufficient, and the essay must be coherent at sentence, paragraph and essay level. (Topics 4.6 and 4.7)

## The method in one table

| Skill | Four steps | Key question |
|---|---|---|
| Contrast of characters | Detail, difference, value, effect | What does the difference do for the story? |
| Relationships | Surface, undercurrent, evidence, meaning | What second feeling runs beneath the first? |
| Setting | Detail, mood, character, meaning | What does this place do, to the reader and the character? |
| Plot order and contrast | Sequence, shift, contrast, effect | What does the order of information do? |
| Narrator | Voice, distance, evidence, effect | What perspective and tone do the words show? |
| Essay writing | Question, claim, reasoning, commentary; then claim, proof, purpose, link | Is each claim defensible, supported and linked to the thesis? |

## Question 1 (multiple choice · 4.3 and 4.5)

Read the opening of this passage, written by Marlbridge. Use it for Questions 1, 2, 4 and 5.

> Mrs Ferreira's greenhouse stood at the end of the garden, and it was the only part of the property she had never let her husband paint. The glass was clouded with forty summers of lime and rain; inside, the tomatoes grew sideways toward whatever light found its way in. When Mr Ferreira, who had a theory about everything, proposed that they take the greenhouse down and put a proper patio in its place, she said, "Of course, dear," and went on watering.
>
> On Sunday he found her there at dusk, sitting on an upturned pot in the green gloom, her hands in her lap. He began to explain, again, how much brighter the garden would be. "It would," she said. "It would be very bright." He waited for the rest of the sentence, and, when it did not come, he sat down on the next pot, which was too small for him, and said nothing at all.

Which statement best combines the function of the setting with the narrator's attitude?

- (A) The clouded greenhouse is a gloomy, enclosed refuge, and the narrator treats Mr Ferreira with gentle irony ("a theory about everything").
- (B) The greenhouse is a dangerous place, and the narrator admires Mr Ferreira's theories.
- (C) The greenhouse is bright and open, and the narrator is angry with Mrs Ferreira.
- (D) The greenhouse is a symbol of money, and the narrator is indifferent.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (A).** "Clouded", "sideways" and "green gloom" create an enclosed, dim refuge. "Who had a theory about everything" is a mildly mocking aside, so the narrator stands slightly apart from him with wry amusement.

- (B) Nothing is dangerous; the narrator does not admire him.
- (C) The greenhouse is described as clouded, not bright, and the narrator is not angry.
- (D) Money is not mentioned.

## Question 2 (multiple choice · 4.1 and 4.2)

Which best describes the relationship between the Ferreiras?

- (A) Open hostility, shown by Mrs Ferreira's refusal to argue
- (B) Polite agreement that covers a quiet disagreement of values: light and tidiness against shelter and the unchanged
- (C) Complete harmony, since she says "Of course, dear"
- (D) Indifference, because he sits and says nothing

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (B).** "Of course, dear" and "It would be very bright" agree in words while she keeps watering, sits in the gloom and leaves the sentence unfinished. His changing in the end, sitting on the too-small pot, shows that he has begun to understand.

- (A) She never argues; hostility is not shown.
- (C) The actions contradict the words.
- (D) His silence is attentive, not indifferent: he "waited for the rest of the sentence".

</details>

## Question 3 (short answer · 4.4 and 4.5)

In Kate Chopin's "The Story of an Hour" (1894), the narrator begins with a general statement ("Knowing that Mrs. Mallard was afflicted with a heart trouble, great care was taken to break to her as gently as possible the news of her husband's death") and later reports what Louise "did not stop to ask". Explain how the order of events and the narrator's distance work together. (4 points)

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Sample response (written by Marlbridge).** Chopin begins with the news and the care taken to deliver it, so the opening is an outside view of a household managing a shock. As the story follows Louise to her room, the narrator draws closer, reporting that "She did not stop to ask if it were or were not a monstrous joy that held her", so the reader enters her mind just as she meets her own feeling. The order (news, solitude, discovery, return) therefore moves the reader from the public account of grief to a private one, which makes the final return of the husband more cruel because the reader has come to share her freedom.

**Suggested Marlbridge rubric (4 points).**

| Point | Awarded for |
|---|---|
| 1 | Describes the order of events (news, solitude, discovery, return) |
| 2 | Describes narrative distance changing from outside to close |
| 3 | An accurate quotation that shows closeness |
| 4 | Explains the combined effect (public to private; sharpened irony of the ending) |

## Question 4 (short answer · 4.6 and 4.7)

For the Ferreira passage, write a thesis, a two-step line of reasoning and, for each step, one short accurate quotation with its purpose (illustrate, qualify, amplify and so on). (5 points)

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Sample response (written by Marlbridge).** *Thesis:* The passage presents a marriage in which agreement is a form of quiet resistance, suggesting that what a person protects (here, a clouded greenhouse) can say more about their needs than what she says aloud. *Step 1:* Mrs Ferreira's compliance is only verbal. Evidence: she says "Of course, dear," and "went on watering", where the second clause qualifies the first. *Step 2:* Mr Ferreira's final silence shows he has begun to understand. Evidence: he sits "on the next pot, which was too small for him", an image that illustrates his willingness to share her place and to be uncomfortable in it.

**Suggested Marlbridge rubric (5 points).**

| Point | Awarded for |
|---|---|
| 1 | A defensible thesis |
| 2 | A first claim that connects to the thesis |
| 3 | A second claim that builds on the first |
| 4 | Accurate, short quotation for each step |
| 5 | The purpose of each quotation named correctly |

## Question 5 (essay · all topics)

Write a complete essay (about 40 minutes) analysing how the writer uses characterisation, setting and narrative perspective to present the relationship between Mr and Mrs Ferreira in the passage above.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Sample high-quality response (written by Marlbridge).**

The passage presents a marriage in which agreement works as quiet resistance, and it suggests that what a person protects can speak more truthfully than what she says.

The first paragraph establishes the two characters through their different relations to the greenhouse. Mrs Ferreira has "never let her husband paint" it, so her protection of the place is long-standing and firm, while Mr Ferreira, "who had a theory about everything", approaches it as a problem to be solved. The narrator's phrase about the theory carries mild irony: his confidence is comic, and the narrator stands a little apart from it. When he proposes a patio, her reply, "Of course, dear," is polite and then undercut by the narrator's next clause, "and went on watering". The gap between her words and her action shows that compliance is her way of refusing without conflict.

The setting reinforces her position. The glass is "clouded with forty summers of lime and rain", and the tomatoes grow "sideways toward whatever light found its way in". These details make the greenhouse a place that has survived on small amounts of light, which mirrors the way she endures on small amounts of attention. At dusk she sits in "the green gloom" with "her hands in her lap", and the stillness shows that the greenhouse is less a garden structure than a refuge. His plan to make the garden "brighter" is therefore not neutral: it would remove the condition in which she feels at home.

The final paragraph turns the contrast into change. Her answer, "It would be very bright", repeats his word and leaves out what she thinks of it, and he "waited for the rest of the sentence". The narrator lets his silence stand as a form of listening, and the closing image, in which he sits "on the next pot, which was too small for him", shows him accepting discomfort to share her place. The distance of the narrator shortens here: the earlier irony gives way to sympathy.

Thus the passage moves from a quarrel of values (brightness and change against shelter and continuity) to a quiet understanding. Characterisation gives the conflict, setting gives its meaning and narration shows the husband's change, so that what she does not say becomes the passage's central statement.

**Suggested Marlbridge rubric (6 points).**

| Strand | Points | Awarded for |
|---|---|---|
| Thesis | 1 | A defensible interpretation answering the prompt |
| Evidence and commentary | 4 | Accurate evidence from characterisation, setting and narration (1); commentary explaining how it supports each claim (1); a line of reasoning that builds (1); links back to the thesis (1) |
| Sophistication | 1 | Handles a tension or shift (the narrator's irony turning to sympathy) within the argument |
