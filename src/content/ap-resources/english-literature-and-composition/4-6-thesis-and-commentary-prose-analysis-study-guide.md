The prose fiction essay asks you to read a passage and write an argument about how the writer uses literary elements and techniques to create a meaning. Everything before this topic (character, setting, plot, narration) is the material you analyse; this topic is about the argument you build from it. A good essay depends on three connected things: a **thesis** that makes a claim you can defend, a **line of reasoning** that develops it, and **commentary** that explains how each piece of evidence supports that line.

## The practice passage

The passage below was written by Marlbridge for practice. It is used in this topic and in Topic 4.7.

> Bartholomew Lisle had kept the weighing-house at the end of Quay Street for forty years, and in that time he had never once been wrong by so much as an ounce. Merchants who distrusted one another trusted his scales. They came to him with sacks of grain and bales of wool, and he stood among them in his brown apron like a man standing between two quarrelling nations, saying nothing, turning the brass weight a quarter-turn until the beam sat level.
>
> When his granddaughter Nell came to tell him that the new harbour company would install a machine that weighed by itself, she found him polishing the pan of the great scale with the hem of his apron. "It will be quicker," she said. "It will be exact." "I am exact," he said, without looking up. "What the machine will not be," he added, after a moment in which the shop was silent except for the slow tick of the beam, "is sorry when it is wrong." He lifted the pan to the light, found nothing to correct, and set it down as gently as a sleeping child.

Imagine an essay prompt that asks: *How does the writer use character and setting details to present Bartholomew's relationship with his work?* The rest of this guide builds an answer.

## What a thesis is

A **thesis statement** expresses an **interpretation** of a literary text. It requires a defence through textual evidence and a line of reasoning, and both are explained in the essay through commentary. Three tests help:

1. **It makes a claim, not a summary.** "Bartholomew is a weigher whose granddaughter tells him about a machine" is a summary.
2. **It is arguable but defensible.** A reasonable reader could read the passage differently, yet you can support your reading from the text. "Bartholomew is a person" is not arguable.
3. **It answers the question with an idea.** It says what the passage means or does, in a sentence with a reason in it.

A thesis **may** preview the line of reasoning (the order of ideas the essay will follow), but it does not have to list the literary elements, the points or the quotations. A thesis is not a table of contents.

| Level | Example thesis (for the passage above) | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Summary | "The passage is about a weigher and his granddaughter." | Describes the content; no claim |
| Too obvious | "The writer uses description to show that Bartholomew is exact." | True of the surface, no interpretation, names a device only vaguely |
| Developing | "The writer uses a simile and dialogue to show that Bartholomew cares about his work." | A claim, but thin, and "cares about his work" is almost self-evident |
| Defensible | "By setting Bartholomew's slow, personal ritual against the machine's promise of exactness, the passage suggests that what a community trusts is not accuracy alone but a person who can be answerable for error." | A claim with a reason, an idea, an implied line of reasoning |

## The line of reasoning

A **line of reasoning** is the logical sequence of claims that work together to defend the thesis. Each body paragraph makes one claim, and the claims should build, not just repeat. For the defensible thesis above, a line of reasoning could be:

1. The opening establishes Bartholomew's trusted neutrality through ritual and a simile.
2. Nell's offer of exactness exposes a different definition of trust, based on being answerable.
3. His final gesture of gentle care suggests his work is a moral practice that a machine cannot perform.

Notice that the second claim depends on the first, and the third deepens the second. The line of reasoning is communicated through **commentary** that explains the logical link between the thesis and the claims and evidence in each paragraph.

## Commentary: what turns a quotation into an argument

Evidence alone does not prove anything. **Commentary** is your explanation of how the evidence supports the claim and the claim supports the thesis. Think of it as three small moves after the quotation:

1. **Explain** what the evidence shows (in your own words, not repeated).
2. **Connect** it to the claim of the paragraph (the "because" or "which suggests").
3. **Link** it to the thesis (the "so what": what does this add to your interpretation of the whole passage?).

Weak commentary repeats the quotation ("This shows that Bartholomew is exact") or praises it ("This is a powerful image"). Strong commentary says what the detail makes the reader understand and why that matters to your thesis.

1. **Question.** Underline what the prompt asks you to analyse.
2. **Claim.** Write the thesis in one or two sentences. Test it: could someone disagree? Does it say why?
3. **Reasoning.** Plan two or three claims in logical order.
4. **Commentary.** For each claim, choose evidence and write the explanation that links it to the claim and the thesis.

## Worked example 1: strengthening a thesis

A student drafts: *"The writer uses imagery and a simile to show that Bartholomew is a good weigher."*

**Step 1, test it.** Is it a claim? Barely: "good weigher" is stated in the passage's first sentence ("never once been wrong"). Is it arguable? Not really. Does it give a reason or an idea? No: it names devices ("imagery and a simile") but not what they do.

**Step 2, find a tension in the text.** The passage opens with Bartholomew "never once... wrong", but ends with his remark that what the machine "will not be... is sorry when it is wrong". He is exact, yet he talks about *being wrong* and being *sorry*. That tension suggests a more interesting idea: what he values is not perfection but responsibility.

**Step 3, turn the tension into a claim.** "Although Bartholomew is proud of his exactness, the passage suggests that what he really values, and what the community trusts, is a person's willingness to be answerable for mistakes."

**Step 4, check the line of reasoning.** (1) The simile and ritual in paragraph one present him as a neutral guarantor; (2) Nell's "exact" sets accuracy against his "sorry"; (3) the closing gesture links his care to moral responsibility. Each paragraph supports the thesis from a different angle.

**Result.** The revised thesis states an interpretation, can be disagreed with, and is supported by the passage. It also previews the line of reasoning without listing devices.

## Worked example 2: a body paragraph with commentary

Here is a paragraph for the first claim, with the moves marked.

> The opening presents Bartholomew as a neutral guarantor of fairness whose authority rests on personal restraint. He stands among distrustful merchants "like a man standing between two quarrelling nations, saying nothing, turning the brass weight a quarter-turn until the beam sat level". *(Explain)* The simile raises a shopkeeper's work to something like diplomacy: the "nations" quarrel, and he keeps the peace, not by speaking, but by a small repeated motion. *(Connect)* This implies that the trust placed in his scales is really trust in him, a person whose silence and patience make others willing to accept the result, which prepares the thesis's idea that what the community values is not accuracy alone but a trusted person. *(Link to thesis)*

**What the paragraph does well.** It opens with a claim about the passage's meaning (not "In the first paragraph, the writer says..."). It quotes a short, exact phrase rather than the whole sentence. The commentary is longer than the quotation and moves from what the simile does to what it implies to how it supports the thesis.

**What would make it weaker.** If the last sentence read "This shows Bartholomew is a trusted man", it would repeat the claim without explaining anything. If it said "The simile is a powerful image", it would praise without interpreting.

## A self-assessment table

Use this suggested Marlbridge table to check a thesis and one paragraph. It echoes the three strands of the six-point exam essay (thesis, evidence with commentary, sophistication), but it is Marlbridge's own and not official scoring.

| Strand | Not yet | Secure | Strong |
|---|---|---|---|
| Thesis | Summary or restates the prompt | A defensible claim | A defensible claim with a reason that previews the reasoning |
| Evidence and commentary | Quotation with little explanation | Accurate evidence and explanation linked to the claim | Evidence selected for a purpose; commentary links claim to thesis |
| Control and nuance | Disorganised or generic | Clear paragraphs and transitions | A complication or tension handled within the argument |

## Common misconceptions

- **"A thesis is a topic."** "This essay is about trust" is a topic. A thesis says what the text suggests about trust and why.
- **"I must list the devices in my thesis."** You may preview the line of reasoning, but you do not need a list of devices.
- **"More quotations mean more evidence."** Evidence is effective when commentary explains the logical link between it and the claim.
- **"Commentary is paraphrase."** Paraphrase repeats what is there; commentary explains what it implies.
- **"A thesis must be fixed before I read."** Interpretation is recursive. Evidence can lead to a claim, or a claim can lead you to evidence.
- **"A formulaic sentence such as 'The author uses X to show Y' guarantees a thesis."** It can work, but Y must be an idea worth arguing, not a restatement of the obvious.
