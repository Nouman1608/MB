## What evidence is for

In a literary argument, **evidence** is the detail from the text that supports a claim: a quotation, a described action, a pattern across scenes. Weak essays treat evidence as decoration. Strong essays use it with a purpose.

The course says writers use evidence **strategically and purposefully to illustrate, clarify, exemplify, associate, amplify or qualify** a point. Here is what each job looks like.

| Job | What the evidence does | Example (with a claim about Lydia, who hides distress) |
|---|---|---|
| **Illustrate** | Gives a concrete instance of the claim | She "poured the tea" as if nothing had happened |
| **Clarify** | Makes an abstract claim easier to see | "Composure" is made visible by the routine of folding and laying the letter down |
| **Exemplify** | Offers one typical case among many | Her reply, "a dull note from the agent", is one of several deflections |
| **Associate** | Links two details so each lights the other | The letter placed under the sugar bowl links domestic routine with hidden crisis |
| **Amplify** | Strengthens or extends the claim | Holding the letter to the lamp "as if the flame might alter what was written" shows how much she wishes it were otherwise |
| **Qualify** | Complicates or limits the claim | She puts it back "exactly as before", which suggests that her composure is partly real, not only a mask |

A paragraph that uses several of these jobs is doing more than listing proof. It is building a case.

## Relevant, effective and sufficient

- **Relevant**: the evidence bears directly on the claim. A vivid detail that does not support the point is a distraction.
- **Effective**: the writer uses **commentary to explain the logical relationship between the evidence and the claim**. Without commentary, the reader must guess why the quotation is there.
- **Sufficient**: its **quantity and quality** provide apt support for the line of reasoning. One quotation may be enough for a narrow point; a larger claim needs more than one, from different places.

### A recursive process

Developing an interpretation does not run in one direction. Sometimes you gather evidence and then form a line of reasoning from it. Sometimes you form the line of reasoning first and then look for evidence. Often you move back and forth. If the evidence **does not support the claim**, you revise the claim.

## Integrating quotations

Short, embedded quotations read more smoothly than long, dropped ones.

| Approach | Example | Comment |
|---|---|---|
| **Dropped** | Austen writes. "It is a truth universally acknowledged, that a single man in possession of a good fortune, must be in want of a wife." This shows irony. | The quotation is not connected to the sentence and the commentary is vague |
| **Embedded** | Austen's narrator announces as "a truth universally acknowledged" an idea that the opening chapters trace to one anxious mother, and the grand phrase becomes ironic. | Quotes only the words that carry the point and builds them into the sentence |

Quote what you need: a verb, a simile, a repeated phrase. Do not quote a whole passage when three words carry your point. Always follow a quotation with commentary.

## Composing with control

Clear writing makes reasoning visible. The course highlights three tools.

### Coordination and subordination

- **Coordination** joins ideas of equal weight with a conjunction: "Lydia appears calm, **and** her hands shake." It suggests **balance or equality** between ideas.
- **Subordination** makes one idea depend on another: "**Although** Lydia appears calm, her hands shake." It suggests **imbalance or inequality**, putting the emphasis on the main clause.

Choose the structure that matches your interpretation. If you believe the composure is only a surface, subordinate it.

### Words that communicate

Choose words that **enhance clear communication of an interpretation**. Prefer precise verbs to general ones ("betrays", "masks", "stages" instead of "shows" and "uses"). Avoid vague words such as "things" or "very".

### Punctuation that shows relationships

| Mark | Relationship it signals | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Comma + conjunction** | Two equal ideas | Lydia reads the letter twice, and she folds it along its old creases. |
| **Semicolon** | Two closely related independent clauses | She tells her sister it is a dull note; the lamp scene shows it is nothing of the kind. |
| **Colon** | What follows explains or illustrates what came before | The narrator reveals her distress through repetition: she reads the letter twice and returns it to the same place. |
| **Dash** | A sharp pause or an emphatic addition | The flame cannot change the words—and Lydia knows it. |
| **Comma after an introductory clause** | A dependent clause leading to the main idea | Although Lydia appears calm, her hands shake. |

## Worked example 1: choosing relevant and sufficient evidence

**Original passage written by Marlbridge for practice. It imitates the register of a nineteenth-century novel and is not taken from any published work.**

> Lydia Marsh read the letter once, standing, and then again, seated. She folded it along its old creases, laid it beneath the sugar bowl, and poured the tea. "A dull note from the agent," she told her sister. Later, when the house was quiet, she took it out and held it to the lamp, as if the flame might alter what was written; but the words stayed, and she put it back beneath the sugar bowl exactly as before.

**Claim.** The narrator shows Lydia concealing her distress behind routine.

**Task.** Choose evidence that is relevant and sufficient, and say what each piece does.

**Candidate details.** (a) "poured the tea"; (b) "A dull note from the agent"; (c) "held it to the lamp, as if the flame might alter what was written"; (d) "exactly as before"; (e) "folded it along its old creases".

**Assessment.**

| Detail | Relevant? | Job | Comment |
|---|---|---|---|
| (a) "poured the tea" | Yes | Illustrates | Shows routine continuing after the news |
| (b) "A dull note from the agent" | Yes | Exemplifies | Her spoken deflection hides what the letter means |
| (c) "held it to the lamp, as if the flame might alter what was written" | Yes | Amplifies | Private behaviour reveals the strength of her wish that it were untrue |
| (d) "exactly as before" | Yes | Qualifies | Complicates: the repeated placement may mean she wants the routine to hold |
| (e) "folded it along its old creases" | Weakly | Clarifies | "Old creases" suggests the letter has been handled before; interesting but not central to concealment |

**Sufficiency.** The claim has two parts, **concealment** and **distress**. (a) and (b) support concealment (public behaviour). (c) supports distress (private behaviour). A response that used only (a) would be insufficient because it would not show distress beneath the routine. The best choice is (b) and (c), with (a) as a supporting detail and (d) as a complication that makes the argument subtler.

**A model body sentence.** "In public she reduces the letter to 'a dull note from the agent', but in private she holds it to the lamp 'as if the flame might alter what was written', and the gap between her words and her gesture shows how much routine is asked to hide."

## Worked example 2: improving sentence control

**Draft paragraph.** "Lydia is calm. She is anxious. The narrator shows this. She read the letter twice and folded it and put it under the sugar bowl and the lamp scene shows it is not a dull note."

**Problems.** The sentences are short and flat, so no idea is made more important than another. The last sentence runs several actions together without punctuation and slips into the past tense, and "shows" is vague.

**Revision, step by step.**

1. **Combine the first two sentences with subordination** to match the interpretation that the calm is a surface: "Although Lydia appears calm, she is anxious."
2. **Use a colon** to introduce the evidence that supports the claim: "The narrator reveals her anxiety through repetition: she reads the letter twice, folds it along its old creases and returns it to the same place."
3. **Use a semicolon** to join two closely related ideas: "She tells her sister it is a dull note; the lamp scene shows it is nothing of the kind."
4. **Choose a precise verb**: replace "shows" with "betrays".

**Revised paragraph.** "Although Lydia appears calm, she is anxious. The narrator reveals her anxiety through repetition: she reads the letter twice, folds it along its old creases and returns it to the same place. She tells her sister it is a dull note; the lamp scene betrays that it is nothing of the kind."

**What changed.** The subordinate clause "Although Lydia appears calm" gives the calm less weight than the anxiety. The colon announces an explanation. The semicolon joins a statement and its contradiction. The verb "betrays" names the relationship between her words and her gesture.

## Common misconceptions

- **"More quotations means a stronger argument."** Quality and fit matter more than quantity.
- **"Long quotations impress."** Short, embedded quotations show that you know which words carry the meaning.
- **"Evidence speaks for itself."** It does not. Commentary explains what it shows and how.
- **"If the evidence does not fit, find another quotation."** You may also revise the claim. Interpretation is recursive.
- **"Coordination is always safe."** Joining everything with "and" suggests that everything is equally important. Choose structure to match the interpretation.
- **"Punctuation is decoration."** Punctuation shows how the parts of a sentence relate.
