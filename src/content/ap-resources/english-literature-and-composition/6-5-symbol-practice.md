These are **original Marlbridge practice questions**, not past exam questions. Passages marked "written by Marlbridge" are original; the lines from Shakespeare's Macbeth (about 1606) are in the public domain. The mark points are a **suggested Marlbridge rubric**, not an official scoring guideline. Allow about 45 minutes.

## Question 1 (multiple choice · foundation)

In a novel, which object is most likely to be symbolic?

- (A) A cup of tea mentioned once in a description of a breakfast table.
- (B) A coat described as "brown" on its first appearance.
- (C) A locked door that recurs at moments of conflict, which characters avoid and one character finally opens.
- (D) A carriage that carries a character from one town to another.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (C).** Repetition, character reaction, placement at moments of conflict and a final change in the object's condition are all signals of symbolic weight.

- (A) is a single mention with no emphasis.
- (B) is a descriptive detail, not marked by repetition or reaction.
- (D) is a functional object. It could become symbolic, but nothing in the description suggests it.
</details>

## Question 2 (multiple choice · core)

**Passage written by Marlbridge.**

> The red umbrella had hung in the hall since Father went to sea. Mother dusted it on Fridays but would not let anyone borrow it, even in the heaviest rain.

Which interpretation is best supported?

- (A) The umbrella represents the family's hope for Father's return and Mother's refusal to let go of the past.
- (B) The umbrella represents the family's wealth.
- (C) The umbrella shows that Mother is careless about the weather.
- (D) The umbrella is simply a convenient object for the plot.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (A).** The umbrella is tied to a date ("since Father went to sea"), preserved by ritual dusting and forbidden to use, which are signs that it stands for something other than shelter.

- (B) has no support: wealth is not mentioned.
- (C) contradicts the care that Mother takes of it.
- (D) ignores the signals of symbolic weight.
</details>

## Question 3 (multiple choice · core)

Which statement best describes the difference between a common symbol and a contextual symbol?

- (A) A common symbol is always correct; a contextual symbol is always a mistake.
- (B) A common symbol appears only in poetry; a contextual symbol only in novels.
- (C) A common symbol carries associations many readers already have; a contextual symbol gains its meaning from its use in a particular text.
- (D) A common symbol has no meaning; a contextual symbol has several.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (C).** Some symbols, such as a dove for peace, are so recurrent that readers bring associations to them; others only come to represent something through how the text uses them.

- (A) is nonsense: neither is a mistake.
- (B) confuses genre with the kind of symbol.
- (D) misdescribes both kinds.
</details>

## Question 4 (multiple choice · core)

**Passage written by Marlbridge.**

> In the first winter after the wreck, Ada had kept the lighthouse beam turned to her window and found it a comfort. By the third, she drew the curtain against it every evening.

The change in Ada's response to the lighthouse beam most likely serves to

- (A) show that the lighthouse has been moved.
- (B) suggest that the weather has become worse.
- (C) prove that she has stopped caring about sailors.
- (D) register a shift in her feelings from consolation to pain, as the beam comes to stand for the loss that it once softened.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (D).** The beam is a literal light, but the change in her behaviour, from keeping it in view to shutting it out, signals a change in what it means to her.

- (A) has no textual basis: the lighthouse is not said to move.
- (B) is not mentioned.
- (C) goes beyond the evidence; her avoidance suggests pain, not indifference.
</details>

## Question 5 (multiple choice · core)

After the murder of Duncan, Macbeth asks, "Will all great Neptune's ocean wash this blood / Clean from my hand?" The most plausible reading of "this blood" is that it

- (A) represents guilt that he cannot remove.
- (B) is only a description of a physical stain.
- (C) represents Duncan's royal status.
- (D) shows that Macbeth is a skilled doctor.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (A).** The question exaggerates the cleansing power needed ("all great Neptune's ocean"), which signals that the stain is moral as well as physical.

- (B) ignores the exaggeration that signals a figurative meaning.
- (C) has no basis in the lines quoted.
- (D) has no connection to the passage.
</details>

## Question 6 (multiple choice · stretch)

Which sentence best shows analysis of a symbol rather than simple identification?

- (A) The ring is a symbol.
- (B) The ring symbolizes marriage.
- (C) The ring is gold.
- (D) By returning to the ring at each argument and having Hana twist it but never remove it, the narrator makes it a sign of a commitment she resents but cannot abandon.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (D).** It gives evidence of repetition and behaviour, states the meaning (commitment resented but not abandoned) and implies function (it shows an internal conflict).

- (A) names the technique only.
- (B) gives a common meaning without evidence from the text.
- (C) is a literal description with no interpretation.
</details>

## Question 7 (written response · core)

**Passage written by Marlbridge.**

> The glasshouse stood at the end of the garden where Mrs Wray had kept her orchids until the frost of 'forty-one took every one. Since then nobody had cleared the broken panes, and the ivy had taken the door. Still, each Sunday, Mr Wray walked to the end of the path and stood a while with his hat in his hand, as though at a grave.

Write a paragraph of about 120 to 150 words explaining how the glasshouse functions as a symbol.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Model response.** The glasshouse is a literal structure, but the narrator surrounds it with signals of symbolic weight. It is tied to a specific loss, the frost that "took every one" of Mrs Wray's orchids, and it is left uncleaned, with "broken panes" and ivy over the door. Mr Wray's weekly ritual, standing "with his hat in his hand, as though at a grave", turns the neglected building into a memorial. The simile makes the meaning explicit: the glasshouse represents Mr Wray's unresolved mourning and his refusal to let the past be cleared away. The decay of the building, left to the ivy, mirrors the slow persistence of grief, and so makes visible a feeling that the character never states.

**Suggested Marlbridge rubric (6 points).**

| Point | What earns it |
|---|---|
| 1 | Identifies the glasshouse as both a literal structure and a symbol |
| 2 | Cites a signal of weight (link to a loss, neglect, ritual visits) |
| 3 | Uses the simile "as though at a grave" as evidence |
| 4 | States what it represents (unresolved mourning or refusal to let go) |
| 5 | Explains the function (makes an unspoken feeling visible; decay mirrors grief) |
| 6 | Uses precise quotation and clear analytic language |
</details>

## Question 8 (written response · core)

**Passages written by Marlbridge.**

> *Early:* She wore the red scarf up to her chin and said it was the warmest thing she owned.
>
> *Later:* The scarf lay on the hall table. Nobody touched it. Nobody moved it.

In three or four sentences, explain how the meaning of the scarf changes and what the change shows.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Model response.** At first the scarf is associated with warmth and affection: it is "the warmest thing she owned", and she wears it close. Later it lies untouched on the hall table, and the repetition of "Nobody touched it. Nobody moved it." makes it a marker of absence and of an unresolved situation that no one wants to address. The scarf has moved from a sign of comfort to a sign of loss, and the shift shows how a household has changed around someone who is no longer there.

**Suggested Marlbridge rubric (4 points).** One point for stating the early meaning with evidence; one for stating the later meaning with evidence; one for identifying the shift and the repetition as a signal; one for explaining what the change shows (absence, unspoken loss, change in the household). Other well-supported readings, for example a broken friendship, earn credit.
</details>

## Question 9 (written response · stretch)

Choose a longer work you know well, or use Macbeth or Jane Eyre. Identify one symbol and write 120 to 150 words that give evidence of symbolic weight, say what it represents and explain its function.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Model response (Jane Eyre).** In Charlotte Brontë's novel, the great chestnut tree in the orchard is where Rochester proposes, and that night lightning splits it. The tree is not simply scenery: it is a living thing tied to the moment of their promise, and its damage comes immediately after. It stands for the relationship that has just been formed, and the split announces that the union is threatened, which anticipates the revelation that prevents the marriage. The function is structural as well as thematic: the symbol links a scene of apparent happiness to the later crisis, and gives the reader a sign of danger before any character recognises it.

**Suggested Marlbridge rubric (4 points).** One point for a specific object with a signal of weight; one for stating what it represents; one for tying it to a later event or theme; one for explaining function. Any well-supported symbol from a longer work earns credit.
</details>
