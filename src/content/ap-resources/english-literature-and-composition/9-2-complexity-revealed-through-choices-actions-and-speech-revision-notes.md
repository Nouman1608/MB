## Recap

A **complex** character is pulled in more than one direction: they want incompatible things, believe one thing and do another, or surprise us. Find complexity in what the character **chooses, does and says**, not in what they or the narrator claim. Choices matter most when they cost something. Actions can contradict speech. A character's **response to the resolution** of the story reveals their values and may be inconsistent with earlier behaviour. Inconsistencies and unexpected developments are evidence: they shape how we read the character, other characters, the plot, the conflicts and the narrator's perspective. The full explanation, with worked examples from *Huckleberry Finn* and *Macbeth*, is in the [study guide](/advanced-course-resources/english-literature-and-composition/9-2-complexity-revealed-through-choices-actions-and-speech-study-guide/).

## Key relationships

| Evidence type | Ask | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Choice | What did it cost, and what were the alternatives? | Huck's decision to tear up the letter |
| Action | Does it match what the character says? | Nell irons two cloths but says she needs nobody |
| Speech | What is said, avoided or justified, and how? | Macbeth's "She should have died hereafter" |
| Response to the resolution | What does the reaction show about values? | Macbeth's turn to "Tomorrow, and tomorrow" |
| Inconsistency | What tension does it reveal? | A conscience against an inherited belief |

The method: **locate the tension, gather evidence from choice, action and speech, say what it reveals, say what it does, connect outward.**

## Conditions to remember

- Complexity is not likeability; villains can be complex.
- Inconsistency needs interpreting; do not treat it as a writer's mistake.
- Compare what is said with what is done and what the narrative shows.
- State the function of the complexity: irony, sympathy, moral questioning, or critique.

## Mistakes to avoid

- Calling a character "complex" without evidence.
- Taking self-description as truth.
- Describing the contradiction without saying what it reveals.
- Ignoring the narrator's limits (as with Huck).
- Treating one moment as the whole character.

## Worked mini-example

Take Huck in Chapter 31. *Tension:* the belief he was taught (helping Jim is a sin) against his loyalty. *Evidence:* "You can't pray a lie" and "All right, then, I'll go to hell". *Reveals:* experience of a friend outweighs inherited morality. *Does:* creates irony, since the reader sees a goodness Huck cannot. *Connect:* the reader learns to trust what Huck does more than what he says about himself.

## Quick self-check

1. Why do choices that cost something reveal more than free ones?
2. How can an action contradict speech, and what does that show?
3. Why is a response to the resolution revealing?
4. What irony does Huck's "I'll go to hell" create?
5. List the five steps of the method.

**Answers.**
1. A costly choice shows what the character values enough to sacrifice or refuse.
2. When a character acts against what they say, the action usually exposes what they truly feel or need.
3. Reaction to the outcome shows what the character most values, and may differ from earlier behaviour.
4. Huck thinks he is choosing damnation while the reader sees an honourable act, so the irony criticises the society that taught him.
5. Locate the tension; gather evidence from choice, action and speech; say what it reveals; say what it does; connect outward.
