## What the poetry essay asks of you

The poetry analysis essay gives you one unseen poem and about 40 minutes. You read it, decide what it means and how it works, and write an argument. It is scored on six points in all, in three rows: one point for a **thesis**, up to four for **evidence and commentary**, and one for **sophistication**. This guide is about the thesis and the commentary, the two skills that carry most of those points. Topic 8.7 deals with choosing evidence and controlling your writing.

Everything you learned in this unit (structure, contrast, word choice, symbol, metaphor, allusion) is material. The essay is where you decide which of it to use and how to organise it into an argument.

Four ideas explain the skill.

- A **thesis** states an interpretation of the poem, a claim someone could challenge, and it needs defence with evidence and reasoning.
- A **line of reasoning** is the logical sequence of claims that together defend the thesis. Each body paragraph advances one of those claims.
- **Evidence** is the quoted or closely described detail from the poem that supports a claim.
- **Commentary** is your explanation of how the evidence supports the claim, and how the claim supports the thesis. It is the part that earns most of the credit.

A thesis may preview the line of reasoning, but it does not have to list devices, techniques or evidence. A strong thesis commits to an interpretation; the essay then proves it.

## What a defensible thesis is, and is not

The prompt will ask you to analyse how a poet conveys something (an attitude, a complexity, a change). A thesis answers it with a claim about the poem.

| Kind of statement | Example (about Hardy's "The Man He Killed") | Does it work as a thesis? |
|---|---|---|
| Restates the prompt | "Hardy conveys the speaker's complicated attitude toward the man he killed." | No: it repeats the question. |
| Describes the poem | "The poem is about a soldier who killed a man in war and thinks about it." | No: it summarises; there is nothing to argue. |
| Talks about devices only | "Hardy uses repetition, dashes and dialect." | No: it names features but makes no claim about meaning. |
| Makes a claim | "The speaker is uneasy about the killing." | Partly: a claim, but thin and not yet an interpretation of how or why. |
| Defensible interpretation | "Hardy's speaker tries to justify the killing with the logic of war, but his faltering phrasing and his admission that the man might have been a friend reveal that the justification does not convince even him." | Yes: it can be argued, it responds to the prompt and it implies a line of reasoning. |

A good test is to ask: *could a reasonable reader disagree, and would I need evidence to answer?* If the answer to both is yes, you have a thesis.

## From thesis to line of reasoning to paragraphs

A line of reasoning is built by splitting the thesis into the claims needed to prove it, and arranging them in a logical order, often following the poem's movement but not always. For the Hardy thesis above, three claims might be:

1. The speaker's early imagined meeting at the inn shows that the dead man was an ordinary person he might have befriended.
2. In the third stanza the speaker's broken phrasing and repeated "because" show him straining to produce a reason.
3. The last stanza's understatement ("quaint and curious") shows that the speaker cannot make sense of war and has no better word.

Each claim becomes a body paragraph. The paragraphs are connected: the first establishes the loss, the second shows the speaker failing to explain it, and the third shows the effect of that failure on his view of war. That is a line of reasoning, not a list of three devices.

## The paragraph engine: claim, evidence, commentary, link

A body paragraph has four moves.

1. **Claim.** A sentence that states what you will prove, in your own words, and moves the line of reasoning forward.
2. **Evidence.** A short quotation or precise detail from the poem, embedded in your sentence.
3. **Commentary.** Two or three sentences that explain how the words work (the device or feature), what effect they produce, and why that supports your claim.
4. **Link.** A closing sentence that ties the paragraph back to the thesis or to the next claim.

The commentary is where most weak essays fail. Summary ("the speaker says he shot the man") restates the evidence. Device-spotting ("Hardy uses a dash") names it. Commentary says what the dash does and why that matters to the argument.

A reliable commentary chain is: **device, effect, meaning, thesis**. "The dash after 'because' (device) makes the speaker stop mid-sentence (effect), which shows him reaching for a reason he cannot supply (meaning), so his justification collapses at the moment he tries to give it (thesis)."

## Worked example 1: building a thesis from a poem

**Prompt (written by Marlbridge).** Read Thomas Hardy's poem "The Man He Killed" (written 1902; the full text is in practice question 7 of Topic 8.1). Write an essay analysing how Hardy presents the speaker's attitude toward the man he killed.

**Step 1, paraphrase.** The speaker, a former soldier, says that if he and the dead man had met in an inn they would have drunk together. In battle he shot the man because the man was his "foe", though he admits that the man may have enlisted for the same reason he did, being out of work. He concludes that war is "quaint and curious".

**Step 2, notice what the poem does.** The poem sets an imagined friendly meeting against a battlefield killing. In the third stanza the speaker stumbles ("because— / Because he was my foe"). The final judgement is an understatement.

**Step 3, decide what it means.** The speaker's reasons do not hold, and he knows it. The mild tone is the poem's way of making the reader feel the absurdity of war.

**Step 4, draft thesis versions.**

- *Weak:* "Hardy shows the speaker's attitude through dialect and repetition."
- *Better:* "The speaker feels unsure about the killing."
- *Strong:* "Hardy's speaker clings to the logic of war to explain the killing, but the hesitations in his speech and his admission that the dead man was much like himself reveal an uneasiness that his casual voice cannot suppress."

**Step 5, test it.** Is it defensible? Yes. Does it respond to the prompt? Yes, it states the attitude and implies how Hardy presents it. Does it imply a line of reasoning? Yes: the logic of war, the hesitation, and the admission.

## Worked example 2: building commentary from evidence

**Prompt (written by Marlbridge).** Analyse how Shakespeare's Sonnet 130 conveys the speaker's attitude toward his mistress.

The sonnet begins "My mistress' eyes are nothing like the sun", continues with comparisons such as "If hairs be wires, black wires grow on her head", and has a volta at the couplet: "And yet by heaven, I think my love as rare / As any she belied with false compare." (Punctuation varies between editions.)

**Thesis.** "By rejecting the exaggerated comparisons of conventional love poetry, Shakespeare's speaker redefines praise: he loves his mistress as she actually is, and the couplet shows that this honesty is a stronger compliment than flattery."

**A weak body paragraph.**

> The poet says that his mistress's eyes are not like the sun and her hair is like wires. The poem uses comparisons and then a couplet. This shows that he is saying she is not beautiful and that the poem is funny.

*What is wrong:* it summarises, it names "comparisons" with no effect, it draws a conclusion ("not beautiful") that contradicts the thesis, and it makes no link to the couplet.

**A strong body paragraph.**

> The speaker's first move is to reject the clichés of Elizabethan love poetry. "My mistress' eyes are nothing like the sun" borrows the language of praise and then flatly denies it, and the absurd comparison "If hairs be wires, black wires grow on her head" pushes the denial into comedy by treating the stock comparison of hair to golden wire with literal-minded exactness. The humour exposes the exaggeration in the tradition the speaker is parodying, so the reader is prepared to hear the opposite of flattery as sincerity. By the volta, "And yet by heaven, I think my love as rare / As any she belied with false compare", the poem has earned its claim: because the list of plain truths has removed every false comparison, the final declaration of love stands without decoration. The speaker's honesty becomes the highest praise he can offer.

*Why it works:* it begins with a claim; embeds short quotations; explains the technique (literal treatment of a conventional metaphor) and its effect (comedy that exposes exaggeration); connects to the couplet; and ends by linking back to the thesis.

## A self-assessment rubric (suggested Marlbridge rubric)

| Row (points) | What earns the credit |
|---|---|
| Thesis (1) | A defensible interpretation that responds to the prompt, instead of restating it, summarising the poem or only describing features. |
| Evidence and commentary (4) | **1:** mostly general evidence, with summary. **2:** some specific, relevant evidence and some explanation, but no clear line of reasoning. **3:** specific evidence for every claim, with explanation of how it supports a line of reasoning and how at least one technique contributes to meaning. **4:** specific evidence for every claim, consistently explained, with several techniques shown contributing to the interpretation. |
| Sophistication (1) | A sustained move beyond the obvious: explaining the significance of the interpretation, handling a tension or complexity, or placing it in a wider context. |

This table is a Marlbridge teaching tool, not an official scoring guideline.

## Common misconceptions

- **"My thesis must list three devices."** It need not name devices at all; it must make a claim about meaning.
- **"If I describe the poem accurately, I have a thesis."** Accuracy is not argument.
- **"Commentary means more summary."** Commentary explains how evidence supports the claim.
- **"Longer quotations are safer."** Short quotations let you analyse specific words.
- **"A thesis cannot change while I write."** Interpretation is recursive: evidence can refine your claim, and a revised thesis is better than a loyal wrong one.
- **"Sophistication is a fancy last line."** It must be developed, for example by handling a tension or complexity across the essay.
