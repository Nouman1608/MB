## Recap

- **Setting** is the time and place of a narrative and the details attached to it: weather, season, era, social world, objects in a room.
- A setting helps establish **mood and atmosphere**: the feeling the details create.
- The environment a character inhabits gives **information about that character**.
- A setting carries **values** associated with a place, and a move between settings can show a character's contrast between what they have and what they want.
- Setting can **match** or **contrast with** a character's state of mind. Both choices mean something.
- To explain function, go from detail to mood to character to meaning.

## Key relationships

| Idea | What it asks | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Detail | What concrete feature is there? | "the open window" |
| Mood | What feeling do the words create? | hopeful, expectant (*aquiver*, *delicious*) |
| Character link | Does the place match, contrast with or shape the character? | spring outside contradicts mourning, matches Louise's inner life |
| Meaning | What does this add to the story? | the gap between expected grief and real feeling |

| Setting type | Typical effect |
|---|---|
| Closed room, shut windows | Confinement, resignation, privacy |
| Open window, outdoor space | Possibility, release, exposure |
| Decay and dust | Neglect, passing time, endurance |
| Oppressive weather | Gloom, dread, pressure on a character |

## Assumptions

- You treat setting details as **chosen** by the writer, so each one needs a reason.
- You tie mood to specific words, not to general impressions.
- Weather or an object is treated as symbolic only if the text stresses or repeats it.

## Mistakes to avoid

- Saying "the setting is dark" without naming the details or the effect.
- Assuming the setting must match the character's feelings.
- Listing every detail instead of choosing the two or three that matter.
- Separating setting from character when the text links them.
- Naming a symbol with no textual support.

## Quick self-check

1. In Poe's "Fall of the House of Usher" (1839), which phrases create a heavy mood in the first sentence?
2. A character keeps all the windows closed and sets a saucer beneath each drip in the ceiling. What does the setting suggest about the character?
3. Why might a writer place a bright, springlike setting around a death?
4. Which is the better note: "The room is dim" or "The room's closed windows and dusty radiator create a stuffy mood that shows she endures instead of repairs"?

**Answers.** (1) "dull, dark, and soundless", "oppressively low". (2) She copes with problems instead of solving them, and has become resigned to decay. (3) To contrast the expected feeling with a character's real one, or to show the world's indifference or invitation. (4) The second: it names details, mood and a link to character.
