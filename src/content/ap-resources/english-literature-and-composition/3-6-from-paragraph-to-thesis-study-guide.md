## What a thesis is

A **thesis statement** expresses an **interpretation of a literary text** that **requires defence** with textual evidence and a line of reasoning. In Topic 1.5 you wrote claims for single paragraphs. A thesis is the claim that the whole essay defends, and the paragraph claims become steps that support it.

A thesis is not a summary of what happens and it is not a list of features the writer noticed. It is an answer to a question about what the text means or how it works, an answer that a reader could challenge and that you can defend.

## Four things a thesis must do

1. **Answer the prompt.** If the question asks how a conflict shapes a character, the thesis does that.
2. **Take an interpretive position.** It says something about meaning, not only about content.
3. **Be defensible.** The text can support it; a reasonable reader could disagree.
4. **Be specific.** It is about this text, not about literature in general.

A fifth feature is optional: it may **preview a line of reasoning**, that is, hint at the direction in which the argument will move.

## The thesis ladder

Most weak theses are an earlier step on a ladder. Use the ladder to climb.

| Rung | What it is | Example (on Chopin's "The Story of an Hour") | Is it a thesis? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Topic | A subject area | "Freedom in the story" | No |
| Fact | A statement that needs no defence | "Mrs. Mallard is told her husband has died" | No |
| Summary | A retelling | "Mrs. Mallard goes to her room, thinks about freedom and then her husband returns" | No |
| Device list | Names features | "Chopin uses setting, imagery and irony" | No |
| Observation | Notes a pattern | "The narrator describes the spring outside the window" | Not yet |
| Claim | An interpretive statement | "The spring scene prepares for Mrs. Mallard's feeling of freedom" | Close |
| Thesis | An interpretation that answers the question and carries a "so what" | "By setting a spring scene against the news of death, the narrator shows that Mrs. Mallard's real feeling is relief that she can live for herself, so that her grief, which the household expects, is shown to be only part of her response." | Yes |

The step from claim to thesis is the "so what": *what does this show about the whole text?*

## Previewing a line of reasoning

A **line of reasoning** is the logical sequence of claims that together defend the thesis. A thesis **may preview** it, for example by pointing to a movement from one idea to another ("from public duty to private desire") or by including a qualification ("although … , the narrator …"). It is **not** required to list the points, name literary elements or give evidence. A list such as "through imagery, symbolism and tone" says nothing about the interpretation and invites a mechanical essay.

Compare:

- *List:* "Chopin uses setting, irony and symbolism to show Mrs. Mallard's feelings."
- *Preview of reasoning:* "Chopin sets a lively spring against the news of a death to show that what Mrs. Mallard feels is not the grief others expect but a sense of freedom, and the story's final turn tests how far that private feeling can survive."

The second names a movement (from expected grief to private freedom, then to a test) without listing devices.

## Worked example 1: from paragraph claim to thesis (public-domain novel)

Prompt: *Explain how Jane Austen shows Elizabeth Bennet's change in understanding in* Pride and Prejudice.

In Chapter 36 Elizabeth reads Mr. Darcy's letter. The narrator reports: "She grew absolutely ashamed of herself. Of neither Darcy nor Wickham could she think, without feeling that she had been blind, partial, prejudiced, absurd." She exclaims: "I, who have prided myself on my discernment! … Till this moment, I never knew myself."

**Step 1: Observation.** Elizabeth calls herself "blind, partial, prejudiced, absurd" and says she "never knew" herself.

**Step 2: Paragraph claim.** Elizabeth now judges her earlier judgement harshly.

**Step 3: So what?** The novel's title words ("pride" and "prejudice") are turned on the character who, early on, was confident of her own discernment. The change is not in her opinion of Darcy only; it is a change in how she understands herself.

**Step 4: Draft thesis.** *Austen shows that Elizabeth's change is a change of self-knowledge: the heroine who "prided" herself on her "discernment" must recognise that her own prejudice has been the "folly", and so the novel treats insight into oneself as the condition for judging others fairly.*

**Step 5: Test it.**

| Test | Result |
|---|---|
| Answers the prompt (how Austen shows the change in understanding)? | Yes: it names the change as self-knowledge and the means (her own recognition) |
| Interpretive? | Yes: it makes a claim about what the novel treats as the condition for fair judgement |
| Defensible? | Yes: supported by the letter scene, and a reader could argue about emphasis |
| Specific? | Yes: names Elizabeth, "prided", "discernment", "folly" |
| Previews a line of reasoning? | Yes: it moves from her pride to her recognition to what that means for judging others |
| Lists devices? | No |

## Worked example 2: from observation to thesis (original passage, written by Marlbridge)

> By the third week, Odile had learned the house's rules without being told them. The good china was used only for guests who would not notice it, and the second-best for guests who would. Her aunt's praise was given as sparingly as sugar and was weighed in the same spoon. On Sundays the family walked to church two by two, with Odile at the back beside the dog, and nobody said that this was where she belonged; nobody needed to. It was the dog that looked round when she fell behind.

Prompt: *How does the narrator shape the reader's view of Odile's position in the household?*

**Step 1: Observations.** The rules are unspoken; the china is arranged by who will notice; praise is rationed like sugar; Odile walks at the back with the dog; "nobody said" where she belongs; the dog looks round.

**Step 2: Group them.** Some concern **unspoken rules** (china, "without being told", "nobody said"). Some concern **status** (praise rationed, the back of the line). One detail, the dog, concerns **who notices her**.

**Step 3: Draft claims.**

- *Weak:* "The narrator uses china, sugar and the dog." (list)
- *Better:* "The household's rules mark Odile as an outsider." (claim)

**Step 4: Add the so-what.** What does the arrangement show? That her position is communicated without being stated, and that the only kindness noticed comes from the least powerful member of the group.

**Step 5: Thesis.** *Through the household's unspoken rules of china, praise and seating, the narrator presents Odile's place as an outsider's, marked without ever being stated, and the dog's backward glance suggests that the only attention she receives comes from the one member of the group with no power to give or withhold it.*

**Step 6: Test it.** It answers the prompt (how the narrator shapes the view of Odile's position). It is interpretive (the unspoken marking of place, the dog as the only attentive one). It is defensible (each phrase can be supported with a detail). It is specific. It previews a line of reasoning (rules, then the dog) without listing devices.

## Strong and weak theses

| Weak | Why it is weak | Stronger |
|---|---|---|
| "Odile is an outsider in her aunt's house." | A claim with no "so what" and no sense of how it is shown | "The narrator presents Odile's outsider's place as unspoken but unmistakable, signalled by rationed praise and by a seat at the back with the dog." |
| "The narrator uses symbolism and imagery." | A list of devices | "The narrator makes domestic objects (china, sugar) do the work of rules." |
| "Odile feels sad." | A label with no evidence | "Odile's learning of the rules 'without being told' shows a child who adapts by watching, not by complaint." |
| "This passage is about family." | A topic | "The passage shows a family whose values are enforced by custom, not by statement." |

## A drafting routine

1. Read the prompt. Underline its verb ("explain", "show", "analyse") and its focus.
2. Make notes of patterns, not of every detail.
3. Write a sentence that answers the prompt in your own words.
4. Ask *So what?* and add the answer.
5. Test it: could someone disagree, can I defend it, does it answer the prompt?
6. Refine the wording until it is specific and not a list.

Interpretation is **recursive**: you may start from evidence and form a thesis, or start from a thesis and find the evidence, and you may revise the thesis as you read.

## Common misconceptions

- **"A thesis must list three points."** It may preview the reasoning, but a list is not required and often weakens it.
- **"A thesis is a fact about the text."** A fact needs no defence.
- **"A thesis should be as broad as possible."** Broad claims are hard to defend. Be specific.
- **"A thesis is final."** It may change as you plan the essay.
- **"A thesis must use literary terms."** Terms help only if they serve the interpretation.
- **"A thesis can ignore the prompt."** It must answer the question asked.
