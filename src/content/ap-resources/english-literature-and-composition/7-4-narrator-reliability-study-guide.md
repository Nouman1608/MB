## Why reliability matters

In short fiction the reader's whole view of events arrives through one voice, or a few. If that voice is trustworthy, we simply accept its picture. If it is not, we have to read between and against its words. The course's central statement is that **a narrator's reliability affects a narrative**: it shapes what we believe happened and how we judge the people involved.

Topic 6.4 gave you the tools for reading a narrator's perspective: diction, syntax, tone and omission. This topic concentrates on **reliability as a judgement**, and on the special cases of narrators who know what others cannot and of stories with more than one narrator.

## Sources of unreliability

A narrator may be unreliable for different reasons. Naming the reason helps you explain the effect.

| Source | What it looks like | Typical effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Self-interest** | The narrator defends or flatters themselves | The reader doubts the account of conflicts |
| **Bias** | Loaded descriptions of other people | The reader looks for the other side |
| **Limited knowledge** | A narrator who cannot see or understand all that happens | The reader infers more than the narrator knows |
| **Emotional or mental state** | Obsession, grief, fear, instability | The reader senses distortion even when the narrator is sincere |
| **Naivety or age** | A child or innocent who misreads events | Irony: the reader understands what the narrator does not |

## Signs to look for

1. **Self-contradiction.** The narrator says one thing and shows another.
2. **Over-insistence.** Repeated claims of calm, honesty or good motives ("I am perfectly sane") make the reader suspicious.
3. **Defensive address.** The narrator speaks as if to an accuser ("why will you say that I am mad?").
4. **Telling and showing do not match.** The reported actions do not fit the narrator's description of them.
5. **Contradiction by others.** Other characters, documents or events give a different account.
6. **Gaps.** What is missing: reasons, motives, key events.

## What narrators can and cannot know

The course notes that **some narrators or speakers may provide details and information that others do not or cannot provide**, and that **multiple narrators or speakers may provide contradictory information** in a text. Each narrator has a vantage point: a first-person narrator knows their own mind but not others'; a witness may know a scene but not its causes; a narrator telling a story decades later may have forgotten or reshaped what happened. When you read, ask what each narrator is positioned to know, and what they have reason to say.

## Public-domain examples to learn from

**The Tell-Tale Heart (Edgar Allan Poe, 1843).** The narrator addresses the reader with a challenge: "True!—nervous—very nervous and dreadfully so I had been and am; but why will you say that I am mad?" He insists on his calm, "Hearken! and observe how healthily—how calmly I can tell you the whole story", while describing a murder.

**The Cask of Amontillado (Edgar Allan Poe, 1846).** Montresor begins, "The thousand injuries of Fortunato I had borne as I best could, but when he ventured upon insult, I vowed revenge." The injuries are never described. He tells the story years later, and his closing report that "My heart grew sick; it was the dampness of the catacombs that made it so" offers an excuse for his own feeling.

## Worked example 1: a narrator who undermines himself

Use the opening of The Tell-Tale Heart, quoted above and below.

> "True!—nervous—very nervous and dreadfully so I had been and am; but why will you say that I am mad? The disease had sharpened my senses—not destroyed—not dulled them. ... Object there was none. Passion there was none. I loved the old man."

**Task.** What signs of unreliability does the narrator display?

**Defensive address.** The question "why will you say that I am mad?" shows that he expects an accusation. A narrator who is secure does not defend his sanity unprompted.

**Over-insistence and rhythm.** The dashes and repetition in "True!—nervous—very nervous" create a breathless tone that is at odds with the calm he claims. "Not destroyed—not dulled" repeats the denial of damage.

**Self-contradiction.** He says "Object there was none. Passion there was none. I loved the old man", yet the story is about murdering the old man. The staccato statements do not explain why he did it, and the claim to love sits uneasily beside the act.

**Telling and showing.** He asks us to "observe how healthily—how calmly I can tell you", but his language and behaviour reveal compulsion. What he thinks proves his sanity, the care of his plan, is what the reader sees as evidence of obsession.

**Function.** The effect is to place the reader in a position of knowing more than the narrator, which produces irony and unease. The reliability of the narrator is the story's main subject.

**A model sentence.** "By opening with a defensive question, a breathless string of dashes and a claim of calm that the story's events contradict, Poe shows a narrator who offers his meticulousness as proof of sanity and so reveals the reverse."

## Worked example 2: two narrators who contradict

**Original passages written by Marlbridge for practice.** Two siblings give accounts of the same night.

> *Lena:* I was in the kitchen all evening, and I heard the yard gate at nine, plain as day. Father went out with the lantern and did not return until after midnight. He was in a black temper and would not say where he had been.
>
> *Marcus:* I was in the barn from eight, and nobody crossed the yard. The gate was chained. Father sat with me until near eleven, mending harness, and was as merry as he ever is. Lena has a way of hearing things when she is angry at him.

**Task.** What does the contradiction invite the reader to do?

**What each can provide.** Lena, in the kitchen, can report sounds and the father's return. Marcus, in the barn, can report what he saw and did with his father. Each provides details the other cannot, but each is placed where the other's evidence is out of sight.

**Contradictions.** The accounts conflict on several points: whether Father left (Lena: yes; Marcus: no), whether the gate was open or "chained", and the father's mood ("black temper" or "merry"). Not all can be true.

**Perspective and motive.** Marcus offers a motive for Lena's account, "angry at him", and so tries to discredit it. That attempt is itself a sign of defensiveness, since Lena's anger is stated and not shown. Lena's detail "plain as day" could be insistence. Neither is neutral.

**What the reader gains.** The reader cannot simply adopt one account and must weigh sources: what each could see, what each wants and what each says about the other. The story may be using the contradiction to reveal tensions within the family, or to keep the central event unknowable.

**A model sentence.** "By giving Lena and Marcus incompatible accounts of the same night, each positioned to know what the other cannot and each tinted by feeling towards their father, the story makes the reader a judge of testimony and suggests that the family's truth lies in its divisions."

## Writing about reliability: a paragraph frame

A paragraph on reliability usually moves through five moves.

1. **Claim**: say how far the narrator can be trusted, and about what ("reliable on events, doubtful on motives").
2. **Evidence**: quote the sign (a contradiction, an over-insistent claim, a gap).
3. **Source**: name why the narrator may distort (self-interest, emotion, limited vantage).
4. **Effect**: say what the reader now does (reads around the account, doubts a judgement, notices irony).
5. **Significance**: say what this reveals about the narrator, another character or the story's concerns.

Notice that tone and reliability are related but different. Tone is the attitude conveyed; reliability is how far we trust the account. A defensive tone is a clue to reduced reliability, but it is not proof by itself. Always look for a second sign, such as an omission or a contradiction, before you conclude.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Unreliable means the narrator lies."** Bias, limited knowledge, emotion and age also produce unreliability.
- **"I must decide who is right."** Sometimes the contradiction is the point. Argue what it reveals.
- **"An unreliable narrator makes the story meaningless."** It makes the story more meaningful, because the reader discovers the gap between telling and showing.
- **"Only first-person narrators can be unreliable."** First-person narrators are the clearest cases, but any narrator can be partial.
- **"One lie ruins the narrator."** Look for a pattern. A narrator can be reliable about some things and unreliable about others.
