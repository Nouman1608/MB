## Recap

A character is revealed by **description**, **dialogue** and **behaviour**. A reader moves from a specific detail, through an inference, to a claim about the character's **perspective** (how they understand their situation) or **motive** (why they act). The claim must be limited by the evidence.

## Key relationships

| Idea | What it means | Evidence to look for |
|---|---|---|
| Description | What the narrator, another character or the character says about someone | Word choice, images, comparisons |
| Dialogue | What a character says and avoids saying | Tone, evasion, interruptions, responses |
| Behaviour | What a character does or fails to do | Choices under pressure, small habits |
| Perspective | How a character understands their circumstances | What they notice, assume, fear, trust |
| Motive | The reason for an action or choice | What the action gains or protects |
| Source of a description | Narrator, other character or self | Who is speaking, and what they want |

## What you may assume

- You may infer beyond what is stated, but only as far as the details allow.
- A character's view is not the author's view, and a narrator may be partial.
- If the text supports two readings, say so, and say which one the details favour.

## Mistakes to avoid

- Writing a trait word with no quoted detail.
- Treating a narrator's or another character's description as if it were the character's own view.
- Reaching a large conclusion from one small detail.
- Retelling what happens instead of saying what it suggests.
- Saying "the author thinks" when you mean "the character believes".

## Key terms

- **Detail:** a specific word, action, line of speech or object in the text that you can point to.
- **Inference:** a conclusion you draw from details; it must be limited by them.
- **Perspective:** how a character understands their circumstances, shaped by background, personality, bias and relationships.
- **Motive:** the reason behind a choice or action.

## Sentence frames

- "The detail '…' suggests that [character] sees … as …."
- "By [doing / saying] …, [character] shows a motive of …."
- "The narrator's description comes from [the room / another character], so it reveals their view as well as the character's."

## Mini-example

Detail: Mr. Alder turns the sign "four minutes early". Inference: he wants to end the encounter. Claim: he treats the complaint as something to be shut out, not answered.

## How questions ask about this

- *"The detail … most directly reveals that the character …"*: find the inference the detail supports and reject answers that go further than the text.
- *"Which claim about [character] is best supported?"*: choose the claim that accounts for several details, not just one.
- *"Whose perspective does this description reveal?"*: decide whether the narrator, another character or the character is speaking.
- Constructed response: make a claim, add two quotations, and explain how each supports it.

## Quick self-check

1. In one sentence, what is the difference between perspective and motive?
2. A character polishes a lens with the hem of his coat and does not turn round when his daughter enters. Name one inference this detail allows and one inference it does not.
3. A third character says "she never met a camera she didn't like". Whose perspective does this most directly reveal?

**Answers.**

1. Perspective is how a character understands their situation; motive is the reason behind an action.
2. Allowed: he is occupied with a routine task and avoids facing her, so he may be anxious or reserved. Not allowed: that he does not care about her, which the detail does not show.
3. It most directly reveals the speaker's perspective on the camera-loving character, and it may also reveal something about the described character, so it should be treated as evidence of both.
