These are **original Marlbridge practice questions**, not past exam questions. Every unseen passage was written by Marlbridge for practice. The public-domain extracts are from Kate Chopin, "The Story of an Hour" (1894), and Edgar Allan Poe, "The Fall of the House of Usher" (1839). The marks in the rubrics are a **suggested Marlbridge rubric**, not an official scoring guideline. Answer every question before opening its answer.

## Question 1 (multiple choice · foundation)

Read this passage written by Marlbridge.

> By noon the market square had stopped moving. Awnings hung limp over the stalls, the fruit sellers sat on upturned crates, and even the dogs lay flat in the strips of shade. Somewhere a shutter banged once and was still.

What mood does the setting mainly create?

- (A) Festive excitement
- (B) A heavy, drowsy stillness
- (C) Fear of an approaching crowd
- (D) Cheerful bustle

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (B).** "Stopped moving", "limp", "sat", "lay flat" and "was still" all build a motionless, heavy heat.

- (A) and (D) contradict the absence of movement and noise.
- (C) A crowd is not mentioned; the detail is of people and animals slowing.
</details>

## Question 2 (multiple choice · core)

In Poe's "The Fall of the House of Usher" (1839) the narrator rides through "a dull, dark, and soundless day in the autumn of the year, when the clouds hung oppressively low in the heavens". What is the main function of this detail?

- (A) It gives the date and season for factual accuracy.
- (B) It creates an oppressive, gloomy atmosphere before the characters appear.
- (C) It shows the narrator is poor.
- (D) It argues that autumn is the worst season.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (B).** *Dull*, *dark*, *soundless* and *oppressively low* all press down; the mood is established first.

- (A) The words are doing more than informing.
- (C) Money is not mentioned.
- (D) The sentence sets mood for this story; it does not argue a general claim.
</details>

## Question 3 (multiple choice · core)

In Chopin's "The Story of an Hour" (1894), Louise sits facing an open window, from which she sees "the tops of trees that were all aquiver with the new spring life". What is the most important effect of the setting?

- (A) It shows that the weather is warm.
- (B) It contrasts with the occasion of death and reflects Louise's awakening sense of life.
- (C) It shows the town is poor.
- (D) It suggests that Louise is afraid of the outdoors.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (B).** Spring, openness and growth contradict the mourning expected, and match the "free" she begins to feel.

- (A) is too literal and ignores the contrast with the occasion.
- (C) Nothing in the extract suggests poverty.
- (D) She is drawn to the window, not afraid of it.
</details>

## Question 4 (multiple choice · core)

Read this passage written by Marlbridge.

> Idris kept his desk bare: a lamp, a blotter, a ruler laid parallel to the edge. The cleaner who came on Fridays said that she had never once had to move a cup, because he never used one.

What does the setting most clearly suggest about Idris?

- (A) He is cheerful and sociable.
- (B) He is disciplined and sparing, living with little disorder or comfort.
- (C) He is poor and cannot afford furniture.
- (D) He enjoys cleaning.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (B).** The bare desk, the parallel ruler and the absence of a cup suggest control and restraint.

- (A) The details suggest nothing of sociability.
- (C) The desk is a choice (lamp, blotter, ruler), not a sign of poverty.
- (D) The cleaner's comment says she has nothing to move; it is not about his liking cleaning.
</details>

## Question 5 (multiple choice · stretch)

A character enters a bright, crowded festival after a long illness. The narrator says the music "seemed to come from the far side of a pane of glass". What is the best reading of the relationship between the character and the setting?

- (A) The character is thoroughly at home in the festival.
- (B) The setting is bright, but the character feels separated from it.
- (C) The festival is not meaningful to the story.
- (D) The character enjoys the music more than the crowd.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (B).** The "pane of glass" image places a barrier between the character and the lively setting: a mismatch between world and mood.

- (A) The pane of glass contradicts being at home.
- (C) The contrast is meaningful, since it exposes the character's distance.
- (D) The text compares music to a distant sound, not a preference.
</details>

## Question 6 (short answer · core)

Read this passage written by Marlbridge.

> Corin had moved into the cottage in March, and by October he still had not unpacked the books. They stood in their boxes along the hallway, spines out, like passengers waiting for a train. The fire in the front room he lit each evening and let go out by eight; the back room, with the window facing the hills, he never entered.

Explain how the setting reveals something about Corin. Use at least two details. (4 points)

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

A strong answer: The setting shows a man who has arrived but not settled. The books still in their boxes "like passengers waiting for a train" suggest temporary living; the fire lit and allowed to go out by eight suggests a half-hearted attempt at comfort. The unused back room with its view of the hills suggests something he avoids or cannot yet face. Together the details create a mood of suspended, unsettled waiting and show a character holding back from making a home.

**Suggested Marlbridge rubric (4 points).**

| Point | Awarded for |
|---|---|
| 1 | Identifies one relevant setting detail (boxed books, fire, back room) |
| 2 | Identifies a second detail |
| 3 | Links the details to a character trait or state (unsettled, avoiding, waiting) |
| 4 | Explains the mood or meaning (suspended, unresolved) created by the setting |
</details>

## Question 7 (short answer · core)

Chopin's "The Story of an Hour" (1894) places Louise in a room with an open window, "a comfortable, roomy armchair", and sounds from the street. Write two or three sentences explaining the **mood** of this setting and **one way** it relates to Louise's state. (4 points)

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Sample response (written by Marlbridge).** The window scene creates a mood of renewal and expectation: the "new spring life", the "delicious breath of rain" and the peddler's cry fill the room with life. It contrasts with the occasion of her husband's death but matches what Louise begins to feel, because the world outside enters her room as the thought of freedom enters her mind.

**Suggested Marlbridge rubric (4 points).**

| Point | Awarded for |
|---|---|
| 1 | Names an appropriate mood (renewal, hope, expectation) |
| 2 | Supports it with an accurate detail or phrase |
| 3 | Links the setting to Louise's state of mind |
| 4 | Notes the contrast with the expected mourning, or explains what that adds |
</details>

## Question 8 (short answer · stretch)

Write one paragraph (about 120 words) explaining how the setting in the following passage (written by Marlbridge) shows a relationship between the character and the place.

> The lighthouse at Skerry Point had not been lit in eleven years, but Maren still climbed its stairs each dusk. She dusted the great lens, wound the dead clock, and stood for a moment at the rail, facing the water. Below, the harbour lights came on one by one without her.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Sample response (written by Marlbridge).** The unlit lighthouse is a setting of purposeful obsolescence: the lens is dusted, the "dead clock" wound, yet the harbour lights "came on one by one without her". Maren's dusk ritual shows a character who keeps faith with a role that the world has replaced, so the place both honours and traps her. The cold stairs, the idle lens and the rail she stands at create a mood of dignified loneliness. The contrast between her faithful routine and the lights that no longer need her suggests how a person can remain bound to a purpose after its use has gone.

**Suggested Marlbridge rubric (4 points).**

| Point | Awarded for |
|---|---|
| 1 | A claim about the relationship between Maren and the lighthouse |
| 2 | Two accurate details from the passage |
| 3 | Mood named and tied to specific words |
| 4 | Commentary on what the setting adds to the interpretation (obsolete duty, loneliness, loyalty) |
</details>
