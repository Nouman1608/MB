## Recap

- Writers use evidence **strategically and purposefully** to illustrate, clarify, exemplify, associate, amplify or qualify.
- Evidence is **effective** when commentary explains its relationship to the claim, and **sufficient** when its **quantity and quality** give apt support.
- Interpretation is **recursive**: a claim can lead to evidence and evidence can reshape the claim.

## The five-question audit

| # | Question |
|---|---|
| 1 | Is it **relevant** to the claim, not merely the topic? |
| 2 | What **job** does it do? |
| 3 | **Where** is it (beginning, middle, ending)? |
| 4 | Does it **complicate** the claim? |
| 5 | Does the set **cover** every part of the claim? |

## Quality and quantity

- **Quality**: specific words, short embedded quotations, details that carry meaning.
- **Quantity**: enough to support the claim, including more than one location for a claim about a pattern.
- **Balance**: avoid one detail for a broad claim, or repeated details for a narrow one.

## Handling counter-evidence

1. Notice it.
2. Decide whether it defeats the claim or complicates it.
3. **Revise the claim** or explain the nuance.
4. Use it to make the argument more precise.

## Examples (public domain)

- **The Cask of Amontillado** (Edgar Allan Poe, 1846): for a claim about Montresor's reliability, useful details include the unnamed "thousand injuries", the smile "at the thought of his immolation" and the closing excuse of the "dampness of the catacombs". A detail about Fortunato's costume is interesting but not relevant to this claim.

## Mini-example

Claim: "She hides her fear behind politeness." Evidence set: a polite greeting (supports politeness), a trembling hand she tucks away (supports fear and hiding). Check: both parts of the claim are covered, from two different moments. If only the greeting were quoted, the claim would be insufficiently supported.

## Assumptions

- A claim often has several parts, and each needs support.
- Evidence that merely repeats adds length, not strength.
- The best check is to read the claim and ask, "What have I shown for each part?"

## Mistakes to avoid

- Using evidence that is about the topic but not the claim.
- Supporting only one part of a multi-part claim.
- Hiding counter-evidence.
- Relying on plot summary.
- Quoting at length when a phrase would do.

## Quick self-check

**1.** A claim has two parts: "She is generous and she resents it." The evidence shows only generous acts. What is missing?

**2.** A detail is vivid but unrelated to the claim. What should you do?

**3.** You find a quotation that contradicts your claim. List two options.

**4.** Why is evidence from the beginning, middle and end useful for a claim about a pattern?

**Answers.**

1. Evidence of resentment; the claim is not covered.
2. Leave it out, or save it for an argument where it is relevant.
3. Revise the claim to account for it, or explain how it complicates but does not defeat the claim.
4. It shows that the feature recurs and develops, which is what a pattern means.
