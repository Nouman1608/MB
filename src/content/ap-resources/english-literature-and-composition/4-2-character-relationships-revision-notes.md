## Recap

- A **nuanced** relationship holds more than one feeling at once: affection and resentment, duty and irritation, pride and silence.
- Evidence comes from what is **said** (and to whom), what is **done**, what is **avoided**, small **gestures and objects**, what the **narrator reports**, and **change** over time.
- The gap between speech and action often carries the complexity.
- Conflict between characters often arises from tension between their **different value systems**, even when they care about each other.
- Describe a relationship with a connecting word that keeps both layers: "and yet", "but", "while".
- Always tie a claim about the relationship to a specific, accurately quoted detail.

## Key relationships

| Step | Question to ask | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Surface | How do they appear to relate? | Polite exchange about soup |
| Undercurrent | What second feeling runs beneath it? | Reluctance; the soup is tipped away |
| Evidence | Which details show each layer? | The thank-you, then the sink |
| Meaning | What does the complexity add? | Shows courtesy as obligation, not warmth |

| Clue | What it can reveal |
|---|---|
| Praise to a third party only | Pride that cannot be said face to face |
| "Whatever you like", then fussing | Hidden tension behind a polite surface |
| "And yet she had loved him, sometimes" | Mixed feelings; love qualified by something else |
| A gesture that outlasts words | An attachment that persists beyond what is spoken |

## Assumptions

- You describe the relationship **as the text presents it**; you do not decide who is to blame.
- Both layers need evidence. A second feeling with no quotation behind it is a guess.
- A relationship can be nuanced in a story where no one raises a voice.

## Mistakes to avoid

- One-word labels ("loving", "hostile") with no second layer.
- Treating the narrator's summary as the only evidence.
- Equating conflict with dislike.
- Ignoring how a relationship changes within the extract.
- Quoting a long passage when two or three words show the tension.

## Quick self-check

1. A character says "I'm fine with whatever you decide" and then re-reads the menu four times. What does the gap suggest?
2. Why is "and yet" a useful phrase in analysis of relationships?
3. A son overhears his father praising him to a stranger but never to him. Describe the relationship in one sentence that holds two layers.
4. Why can a kind husband still be part of a complicated marriage?

**Answers.** (1) A hidden preference or anxiety beneath polite words. (2) It lets one sentence carry the surface and the undercurrent together. (3) For example: loyal and proud, yet unable to say so directly. (4) Because kindness does not remove the tension between one person's will and another's wish for self-possession; the complexity lies in the structure of the relationship, not just the husband's character.
