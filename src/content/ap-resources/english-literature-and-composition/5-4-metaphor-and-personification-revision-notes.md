## Recap

- A **metaphor** treats one thing as another; a simile does the same with *like* or *as*.
- Metaphorical comparisons focus on **particular traits, qualities or characteristics**, not on the whole objects.
- Comparisons communicate literal meaning and may also convey **figurative meaning** or transmit a **perspective**.
- An **extended metaphor** persists through parts of or a whole text, expanded by further details, similes and images; read it in context.
- **Personification** assigns a human trait or quality to a nonhuman object, entity or idea, and so **characterises** it.
- Use four steps: sides, traits, left behind, effect.

## Key relationships

| Step | Question | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Sides | What is compared with what? | hope and a little bird |
| Traits | Which qualities are transferred? | small, singing, persistent |
| Left behind | Which are not, and why does it matter? | the bird's hunger is denied: "never... asked a crumb" |
| Effect | What does it suggest about subject and speaker? | hope as a gift; grateful perspective |

| Device | What it does | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Metaphor | Transfers particular traits | silence as a locked drawer: closed, private |
| Simile | Same, with *like* or *as* | "like a thunderbolt he falls" |
| Extended metaphor | Develops a comparison with further details | hope, the storm, the chilly land |
| Personification | Gives a human trait to a nonhuman thing | autumn "sitting careless" |

## Assumptions

- A comparison is **partial**: only some traits transfer.
- Context decides which traits matter.
- Analysis says what the comparison does, not only what device it is.

## Mistakes to avoid

- Treating a metaphor as total identity.
- Naming "metaphor" or "personification" and stopping.
- Ignoring what the comparison leaves out.
- Treating an extended metaphor as a list of similes.
- Assuming a comparison means the same in every poem.

## Quick self-check

1. In "Hope is the thing with feathers", name two traits transferred and one left behind.
2. What does "sitting careless on a granary floor" do to the season of autumn?
3. How does changing "drawer" to "door" change a metaphor?
4. What makes a metaphor "extended"?

**Answers.** (1) Transferred: small and singing, persistent in storms; left behind: the need for a crumb of food. (2) It personifies autumn as a relaxed, unhurried figure, characterising the season as gentle. (3) It changes which traits transfer: a door suggests a barrier and what is kept out, a drawer what is kept inside. (4) The comparison continues through several lines or the whole poem and is expanded with additional details and images.

## Sentence frames to adapt

- "By comparing [subject] to [comparison], the poem transfers [trait] and [trait]."
- "The comparison does not carry [trait that is left behind], which makes [effect]."
- "The comparison extends through [stanzas or details], so the poem develops [larger idea]."
- "By giving [thing] the human trait of [trait], the poem characterises it as [character]."

Fill the first frame with traits that the poem's own details support. Naming what is left behind is often what lifts an answer above description.
