## Plot is a chain, not a list

**Plot** is the sequence of events in a narrative. The key word in that definition is *connected*: each event builds on the ones before it, often through cause and effect. Compare two statements.

- **A list:** "A caretaker repairs a bell. The bell is old. He finds a note."
- **A chain:** "The new headmistress asks the caretaker to repair a bell that has always run late, so he opens it, and what he finds there makes him refuse to do what she asked."

The second statement connects events with *so* and *and what he finds*. That is the beginning of plot analysis: look for the links, not just the items.

Plot also does something to the reader. By deciding what is shown, in what order and with how much space, a writer directs your attention to the parts of the story that matter most: characters, their relationships, the setting and the tension between them. This is the idea behind **exposition**, the information that introduces the situation and its people.

## The parts of a plot, as a vocabulary

Readers often describe plot in stages. Treat them as labels for what a stretch of text *does*, not as a form every story must fill.

| Stage | What it does | Question to ask |
|---|---|---|
| Exposition | Introduces characters, setting and the situation | What do I need to know before things change? |
| Rising action | Develops the conflict through connected events | What complicates the situation, and why? |
| Climax | The moment of greatest tension or decisive change | What choice or discovery cannot be undone? |
| Falling action and resolution | Shows consequences and settles, or leaves open, the conflict | What has changed, and what remains unsettled? |

Some stories begin near the climax, some end at the moment of change without a tidy resolution. A good analysis names what is in the text and does not force a template.

## The dramatic situation

The **dramatic situation** of a narrative is made of its setting, its action and the way the story places characters in conflict. Often it involves the rising or falling fortunes of a main character. When you describe a plot, ask: *Who wants something? What stands in the way? What changes?* That sets up the next topics in this unit and, later, the study of conflict in Unit 3.

## Order is a choice

A writer can tell events in the order they happen, or can rearrange them. Common choices include:

- **Chronological order.** Events follow the order in which they happen. The effect is clarity and momentum.
- **Opening in the middle or at the end.** The story starts with a striking moment, so the reader asks how it came about.
- **Flashback.** The narrative jumps to earlier events. This often supplies a cause or changes how we read the present.
- **Withholding.** Key information is delayed, so the reader experiences surprise or suspense at the same time as a character.

When you analyse a sequence, ask what the *order* does that another order could not do. Writing "the flashback gives background" is weak. Writing "the flashback follows the reader's puzzled reaction to Pell's calm, so the explanation turns calm into ambivalence" is analysis.

## Worked example 1: opening with a claim (public-domain text)

Edgar Allan Poe's "The Tell-Tale Heart" (1843) begins: "True!—nervous—very, very dreadfully nervous I had been and am; but why will you say that I am mad?"

**Step 1: Describe the order.** The story does not begin with events. It begins with the narrator's defence, spoken as though an accusation of madness has already been made. The events (the nights spent watching the old man, the killing, the hiding of the body, the police visit) are told afterwards, in order, as evidence for the defence.

**Step 2: What does the reader know at that point?** Very little about the facts, but a good deal about the speaker's state: he is agitated ("nervous"), he insists on his sanity, and he is addressing someone.

**Step 3: What does this sequence make the reader notice?** The narrator's *argument* comes before his *action*. So when the events follow, the reader is not just following a crime but testing a claim: does a man who plans so carefully sound mad? The ending, where the narrator is overwhelmed by the sound of the dead man's heart and confesses, answers the opening question in a way he did not intend.

**Step 4: Connect to meaning.** The structure places the narrator's insistence at the start and his collapse at the end. The order lets the reader see the distance between what he says about himself and what the events show.

*Model sentence:* "By opening with the narrator's defence of his sanity and only then recounting the killing, Poe makes the story a test of that claim, so that the final confession exposes the gap between what the narrator says and what he does."

## Worked example 2: a chain of events (original passage, written by Marlbridge)

> For three winters the school bell at Harrow Cross had rung eight minutes late, and nobody minded except the caretaker, Mr. Voss, who wound the mechanism each Sunday and reset it each Monday and found, each Monday, that it had drifted. In January the new headmistress, Ms. Achebe, asked him to repair it by the end of the month. He took the bell apart and discovered a folded note wedged inside the clapper, dated twenty years before, in the handwriting of the boy who had once been the caretaker's assistant: "Late so Dad has time to get here." Mr. Voss put the bell back together exactly as it was, wound it, and set it eight minutes slow.

**Step 1: Identify the stages.**

- **Exposition:** the first sentence. Three winters of a late bell, and Mr. Voss as the only person who minds.
- **Rising action:** the new headmistress asks him to repair it by the end of the month. This creates a deadline and a conflict between his duty to repair and the unstated reason for the lateness.
- **Climax:** the discovery of the note inside the clapper. It changes the meaning of the lateness.
- **Resolution:** he reassembles the bell, setting it "eight minutes slow".

**Step 2: Trace cause and effect.** The headmistress's request causes him to open the bell. Opening the bell causes him to find the note. The note causes his decision. Take out any link and the sequence fails.

**Step 3: Say what the sequence makes the reader notice.** The first sentence says that "nobody minded except" him, so the reader expects him to want the bell fixed. The sequence then reverses that expectation: the person who minded the lateness chooses to preserve it. The order lets the reader share his discovery at the same moment he makes it, because the note's wording is withheld until he opens the clapper.

**Step 4: Link to character and meaning.** The plot shows that the lateness, first presented as a fault, is a deliberate kindness: someone once built eight minutes of grace into the school day for a father who was late. Mr. Voss's final action preserves it. The structure turns a mechanical problem into a story about care and keeping a promise made by someone else.

*Model paragraph opening:* "The plot of 'The Bell' reverses the expectation set up in its first sentence. Mr. Voss, the only person who 'minded' the late bell, is the one who preserves it after the note in the clapper reveals that the delay was a kindness, so the sequence of request, discovery and decision turns a fault into an act of care."

## Strong and weak statements about plot

| Weak | Why it is weak | Stronger |
|---|---|---|
| "First he finds the note, then he fixes the bell." | Lists; wrongly states the outcome | "The discovery of the note reverses his purpose: he now restores the lateness." |
| "The flashback gives background." | Names a feature without an effect | "The flashback explains Pell's calm, turning it from indifference into ambivalence." |
| "The climax is exciting." | Evaluates; does not explain function | "The climax is the discovery of the note, because it changes what the delay means." |

## Checking your inference

1. **Can you point to the link?** Each cause-and-effect claim should be traceable to words in the text.
2. **Have you named what the order does?** "This detail comes first, so the reader…" is the pattern.
3. **Is the stage you named really there?** If a story has no clear resolution, say that and say what it leaves open.
4. **Is your claim about function, not summary?** If your sentence could be copied into a plot summary, add the *so what*.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Plot means what happens."** Plot is what happens, *how events are connected* and *how they are arranged*. Two stories with the same events can have different plots if the order differs.
- **"The climax is the loudest moment."** The climax is the decisive turn, which can be quiet, like opening a bell and reading a note.
- **"Every story has a neat resolution."** Many end with a change that is still unsettled. Say what is left open.
- **"A flashback just gives background."** A flashback does something in its position: it explains, complicates or changes the meaning of the present.
- **"Plot and structure are the same thing."** Plot is the sequence of events. Structure is the broader arrangement of the parts, including how the text reveals information.
- **"Skipping the order means I only need the plot summary."** On an exam, retelling events costs time and earns little. Select the events that matter and explain them.
