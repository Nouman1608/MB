## What the literary argument essay asks of you

The third essay on the exam, the literary argument, is different from the two analysis essays. You are not given a passage. You are given a **prompt** that names an idea or situation (a character's change, a conflict, a setting, a moral choice) and asked to **choose a work of fiction** you know well and argue how that element contributes to the **work as a whole**. Many prompts come with a list of suggested titles, but you may use any suitable work, and a work not on the list is fine if it fits the prompt and you know it deeply.

The essay is scored on the same six points as the other essays: one for the **thesis**, up to four for **evidence and commentary**, and one for **sophistication**. This guide focuses on the thesis and the commentary; [Topic 9.7](/advanced-course-resources/english-literature-and-composition/9-7-selecting-evidence-for-a-literary-argument-study-guide/) covers selecting evidence.

Three features set this essay apart.

- **The argument is about the whole work.** A single scene can be evidence, but the claim must be about what the element does for the entire work. An essay that treats one scene as the whole is limited.
- **You supply the evidence from memory.** You will not have the text. Accurate references to scenes, actions and details are acceptable evidence, and short quotations are valuable when you can recall them exactly. Never invent a quotation.
- **The choice of work matters.** A work that gives rich, varied evidence for the element in the prompt will let you build a line of reasoning; one that gives you only a single scene will not.

## A defensible thesis about a whole work

A thesis here is an interpretation of what the work does with the element named in the prompt, stated at the level of the whole text. It needs a **specific work**, a **specific claim**, and some indication of **what the element contributes to meaning**.

| Kind of statement | Example (prompt on a character's new self-understanding; work: *Pride and Prejudice*) | Does it work? |
|---|---|---|
| Restates the prompt | "In *Pride and Prejudice* a character comes to see herself differently, which contributes to the whole work." | No: it repeats the question. |
| Summarises the plot | "Elizabeth meets Darcy, refuses him, reads his letter, and later marries him." | No: nothing to argue. |
| Describes a character | "Elizabeth is witty and proud." | No: not about the element or the whole. |
| Makes a thin claim | "Elizabeth learns she was wrong about Darcy." | Partly: a claim, but it stays at plot level and says nothing about meaning. |
| Defensible interpretation | "Austen makes Elizabeth's recognition that she 'never knew' herself the hinge of the novel, because it turns a comedy of misjudgment into an argument that lasting marriage depends on self-knowledge, not on first impressions or fortune." | Yes: specific work, specific claim, a statement of what the recognition means for the whole. |

The same test applies as in poetry: *could a reasonable reader disagree, and would I need evidence to answer?*

## From thesis to line of reasoning

A line of reasoning splits the thesis into the claims you must prove, in an order that adds up. For a whole-work argument, three common patterns work well.

- **Development.** Follow the work's movement: how the element appears early, how it turns, and how it ends.
- **Layers.** Examine the element at different levels (in the character, in the relationships, in the society's values).
- **Cause and consequence.** Show what the element causes and what that does to the work's meaning.

For the *Pride and Prejudice* thesis a development pattern might be:

1. Elizabeth's early confidence in her own discernment sets up the failure of her judgement.
2. The recognition scene turns her pride into shame and rewrites her view of Darcy and Wickham.
3. Darcy's parallel change shows that self-knowledge is a shared requirement, not a lesson for one character.
4. The contrast with marriages made without it (Charlotte and Mr Collins; Lydia and Wickham) shows what the novel thinks self-knowledge is worth.

## Commentary on evidence from memory

Commentary has the same job as in the poetry essay: show how the evidence supports the claim and how the claim supports the thesis. A useful chain for fiction is **evidence, what it shows, how it supports the claim, how that supports the thesis**.

Because you may not recall exact words, you can use **precise description** as evidence ("when Elizabeth finishes the letter and cries that she has 'prided' herself on her discernment") and quote only what you are sure of.

## Sophistication: what it is and is not

The sophistication point rewards a **sustained** move beyond the obvious. The course framework names three routes (below), and the exam's sophistication row lists four ways to earn the point: exploring complexities or tensions within the work, situating the interpretation in a broader context, accounting for alternative interpretations, and a consistently vivid and persuasive style.

- **Explain the significance or relevance** of your interpretation within a broader context (the society the work portrays, the ideas it tests, the period it responds to).
- **Discuss alternative interpretations or tensions**, developing them rather than merely acknowledging them.
- **Use a relevant analogy** that helps the reader see your interpretation.

It is not a final sentence of grand claims, and it is not a list of "different readers think different things". It works when the essay's own reasoning handles complexity: for example, by showing that Elizabeth's recognition is real but partial, since the novel still lets her marry into wealth, and asking what that says about how far the novel's critique of money goes.

## Worked example 1: thesis from a prompt

**Prompt (written by Marlbridge).** Literature often shows characters who come to see themselves differently. Choose a novel or play in which a character's new self-understanding shapes the ending of the work. Write an essay analysing how this recognition contributes to the meaning of the work as a whole.

**Step 1, choose the work.** *Pride and Prejudice* (1813): Elizabeth's recognition is central, there is rich evidence across the novel, and I know it well.

**Step 2, decide what the recognition means.** It changes how Elizabeth judges, and it parallels Darcy's change. The novel's comedy turns on misjudgment, and the resolution requires its correction.

**Step 3, draft thesis versions.**

- *Weak:* "Elizabeth learns about herself in the novel."
- *Better:* "Elizabeth's self-knowledge leads to her marriage to Darcy."
- *Strong:* "Austen makes Elizabeth's recognition that she 'never knew' herself the hinge of the novel, because it turns a comedy of misjudgment into an argument that lasting marriage depends on self-knowledge, not on first impressions or fortune."

**Step 4, test it.** Specific work: yes. Specific claim: yes. Responds to the prompt: yes. Whole-work level: yes. Implies a line of reasoning: yes.

## Worked example 2: commentary and a model paragraph

**Claim.** The recognition scene converts Elizabeth's pride in her own discernment into shame, and so rewrites her view of the other characters.

**Evidence (quotations checked against a public-domain text).** After reading Darcy's letter she cries, "How despicably have I acted! [...] I, who have prided myself on my discernment!" She concludes: "Had I been in love, I could not have been more wretchedly blind. But vanity, not love, has been my folly." And: "Till this moment, I never knew myself."

**Commentary chain.**

- *What it shows:* her self-accusation uses the words of her own earlier confidence ("prided", "discernment") against herself.
- *How it supports the claim:* the exclamations and the balanced antitheses ("vanity, not love") show her re-sorting her whole history into a new pattern.
- *How it supports the thesis:* the line "I never knew myself" names self-knowledge as the lack that her earlier judgements depended on, so the novel's turning point is not a plot reversal but a change in how she knows.

**Model body paragraph (written by Marlbridge).**

> Elizabeth's recognition scene turns her pride in her discernment into shame, and the novel makes that reversal the hinge of its argument. When she finishes Darcy's letter she cries, "I, who have prided myself on my discernment!", using her own earlier boast as the measure of her failure. The balanced sentence that follows, "vanity, not love, has been my folly", sorts her whole history into a new pattern: the preference she felt for Wickham and the offence she took at Darcy are both traced to vanity, not to judgement. Austen does not let the revision stay private. By having Elizabeth conclude that "Till this moment, I never knew myself", she makes self-knowledge the condition that the earlier chapters lacked, so the misjudgments that drove the plot are re-read as failures of knowing, not of fact. The scene therefore supports the novel's larger claim that a marriage worth making begins in self-understanding.

*Why it works:* it begins with a claim; uses short quotations she can recall; explains specific features (the reuse of her own words, the balanced antithesis); and ends by linking to the thesis about the whole work.

## A self-assessment rubric (suggested Marlbridge rubric)

| Row (points) | What earns the credit |
|---|---|
| Thesis (1) | A defensible interpretation of the chosen work that responds to the prompt, rather than restating it or summarising the plot |
| Evidence and commentary (4) | **1:** general evidence with summary. **2:** some specific evidence and some explanation, but no clear line of reasoning. **3:** specific evidence for each claim with explanation of how it supports a line of reasoning. **4:** specific evidence for every claim, consistently explained, with several literary elements shown contributing to the interpretation of the whole work |
| Sophistication (1) | A sustained move beyond the obvious: significance in a broader context, a developed tension or alternative, or a helpful analogy |

This table is a Marlbridge teaching tool, not an official scoring guideline.

## Common misconceptions

- **"I should summarise the plot to show I know the book."** Knowledge shows through accurate, selective evidence; summary earns nothing on its own.
- **"The thesis must be about the whole plot."** It must be about what the element does for the whole work; the plot is only evidence.
- **"I need exact quotations."** Precise description of scenes is acceptable; never invent a quotation.
- **"Any famous book will do."** Choose a work that gives rich, varied evidence for the element in the prompt.
- **"The listed titles are required."** They are suggestions; a fitting work you know well is better.
- **"Sophistication is a clever final sentence."** It has to be sustained through the essay.
