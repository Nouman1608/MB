## Recap

A **symbol** is a concrete thing that, in the way a poem uses it, stands for something beyond itself. It is built by the poem (through repetition, position, surrounding words and the speaker's treatment of it), not looked up in a dictionary of symbols. Symbols often imply the speaker's **attitude or perspective**: how a speaker treats an object shows what the speaker feels about what the object represents. The full explanation, with worked examples from Blake, is in the [study guide](/advanced-course-resources/english-literature-and-composition/8-3-symbol-in-poetry-study-guide/).

## Key relationships

| Idea | In one line | How to use it |
|---|---|---|
| Image | Appeals to the senses; may be only descriptive | Do not call every image a symbol |
| Symbol | A concrete thing with weight beyond its literal role | Say what this poem makes it suggest |
| Metaphor | A comparison | Ask what is compared to what |
| One-to-one code | "Rose equals love" | Avoid; it ignores the poem |
| Attitude | What the speaker's treatment of the symbol implies | Link symbol to speaker |
| Whole | How the symbol changes the poem's meaning | End every explanation here |

The five questions: **spot** it, **track** it, say what it **suggests**, name the **attitude**, link to the **whole**.

## Conditions to remember

- A symbol needs evidence of weight: repetition, position, emotional charge, or an unusual treatment.
- State the meaning as a sentence about the poem, not as a single word.
- Two layers of meaning are fine if the context supports both; label which is primary.
- A symbol can interact with others in the poem; explain each first, then the interaction.

## Mistakes to avoid

- "The X symbolises Y" with nothing else.
- Treating all objects as symbols.
- Importing meanings from outside the poem (biography, a symbol dictionary).
- Forgetting the speaker: say what the treatment of the symbol implies about the speaker's perspective.
- Stopping at what the symbol means without saying what it does to the poem.

## Worked mini-example

Take the "invisible worm" in Blake's "The Sick Rose". *Spot:* the rose is addressed directly and the worm "flies". *Track:* the poem surrounds them with "night", "secret" and "dark". *Suggest:* a hidden, corrupting force that destroys a vulnerable joy. *Attitude:* horrified pity, in "O rose, thou art sick!". *Whole:* a joy can be destroyed from within by something unseen. The explanation is a sentence about this poem, not a dictionary entry.

## Quick self-check

1. What is the difference between an image and a symbol?
2. Why is "the rose means love" a weak explanation?
3. In "London", what two meanings does "blackening" support?
4. What does the speaker's attitude toward the "invisible worm" in "The Sick Rose" seem to be?
5. Name the five questions for explaining a symbol.

**Answers.**
1. An image appeals to the senses and may only describe; a symbol also stands for something beyond itself.
2. It is a fixed code that ignores how this poem uses the rose; a good explanation says what this poem makes it suggest.
3. Literal soot on the church and a figurative darkening of its moral standing.
4. Horrified pity: he sees what the rose cannot but can only describe the destruction.
5. Spot, track, suggest, attitude, whole.
