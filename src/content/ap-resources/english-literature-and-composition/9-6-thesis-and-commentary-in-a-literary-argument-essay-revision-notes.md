## Recap

The literary argument essay gives a prompt that names an idea or situation and asks you to **choose a work of fiction** and argue how that element contributes to the **work as a whole**. It is scored on six points: **thesis** (1), **evidence and commentary** (up to 4) and **sophistication** (1). A thesis is a defensible interpretation of the chosen work, specific to the prompt and the work, not a restatement or a plot summary. A **line of reasoning** splits it into claims (by development, by layers, or by cause and consequence). **Commentary** explains how evidence supports each claim and how each claim supports the thesis. **Sophistication** is a sustained move beyond the obvious: significance in a broader context, a developed tension or alternative reading, or a helpful analogy. The full explanation, with worked examples on *Pride and Prejudice*, is in the [study guide](/advanced-course-resources/english-literature-and-composition/9-6-thesis-and-commentary-in-a-literary-argument-essay-study-guide/).

## Key relationships

| Part | Test |
|---|---|
| Choice of work | Does it give rich, varied evidence for the element in the prompt, and do I know it well? |
| Thesis | Specific work, specific claim, what the element means for the whole; could a reasonable reader disagree? |
| Line of reasoning | Do the claims add up to the thesis, in a logical order? |
| Evidence | Specific scenes or details, accurate; quotations only when sure |
| Commentary | Evidence, what it shows, how it supports the claim, how that supports the thesis |
| Sophistication | Sustained: significance, tension or alternative, analogy |

| Pattern | Use when |
|---|---|
| Development | The element changes across the work |
| Layers | The element works at several levels (character, relationship, society) |
| Cause and consequence | The element produces effects that shape meaning |

## Conditions to remember

- The claim is about the whole work; a scene is evidence, not the subject.
- You may use description in place of quotation, but never invent a quotation.
- Listed works are suggestions; a fitting work you know well is better.
- Sophistication has to be developed within the essay, not added at the end.

## Mistakes to avoid

- Summarising the plot to show knowledge.
- Writing a thesis that restates the prompt.
- Choosing a work that offers only one scene of evidence.
- Quoting from memory inaccurately.
- Closing with a grand sentence that the essay has not earned.

## Quick self-check

1. How many points are available for thesis, evidence and commentary, and sophistication?
2. What three features set the literary argument essay apart?
3. Name three patterns for a line of reasoning.
4. What should you do if you cannot recall a quotation exactly?
5. Name the three routes to sophistication.

**Answers.**
1. 1, 4 and 1.
2. The argument concerns the whole work; evidence comes from memory; the choice of work matters.
3. Development, layers, cause and consequence.
4. Describe the scene precisely instead; never invent a quotation.
5. Explain significance in a broader context; develop an alternative interpretation or tension; use a relevant analogy.
