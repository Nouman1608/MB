## Recap

- A narrator's **reliability affects a narrative**: what we believe happened and how we judge the characters.
- Reasons for unreliability: **self-interest, bias, limited knowledge, emotional or mental state, naivety or age**.
- Some narrators provide **details that others do not or cannot**; **multiple narrators may contradict** one another.
- Reliability is a **matter of degree**. A narrator can be sound on one matter and doubtful on another.

## Signs of unreliability

| Sign | Example |
|---|---|
| Self-contradiction | "I never raise my voice", and the windows shake |
| Over-insistence | Repeated claims of calm or honesty |
| Defensive address | "Why will you say that I am mad?" |
| Telling and showing differ | Calls himself generous and counts every coin |
| Contradiction by others | Another voice gives a different account |
| Gaps | Missing reasons, motives or key events |

## Handling contradictory narrators

1. List the points of **conflict**.
2. For each narrator, ask what they can **see or know**.
3. Ask what each **wants** or fears.
4. Decide whether the text favours one, balances them or **leaves the question open**.
5. Say what the contradiction **reveals**.

## Examples (public domain)

- **The Tell-Tale Heart** (Edgar Allan Poe, 1843): "why will you say that I am mad?" and the insistence on calm.
- **The Cask of Amontillado** (Edgar Allan Poe, 1846): the "thousand injuries" are never described; the narrator's closing excuse for his sick heart is the dampness of the catacombs.

## Assumptions

- Evidence of unreliability must be specific: a phrase, a mismatch, a gap.
- A limited narrator may be honest; an unreliable one is distorted. The two can overlap.
- A contradiction between narrators is evidence, not an error.

## Tone is not reliability

Tone is the attitude a narrator conveys. Reliability is how far the reader can trust the account. A defensive tone is a clue, but you need a second sign, such as a contradiction or a gap, before you conclude that a narrator is unreliable.

## Mistakes to avoid

- Equating unreliable with lying.
- Choosing a "true" narrator without evidence.
- Using one detail to condemn the whole account.
- Forgetting that the narrator's unreliability affects our view of other characters.
- Naming the device without saying what it reveals.

## Quick self-check

**1.** A narrator says, "I was perfectly calm," three times in one paragraph. What may this suggest?

**2.** A child narrator reports accurately what the adults say but does not understand it. Is she unreliable, limited or both?

**3.** Two narrators disagree about whether a door was locked. List three things to check.

**4.** What does an unreliable narrator allow a writer to do?

**Answers.**

1. Over-insistence: the repetition may signal anxiety or a wish to persuade, which raises doubt.
2. Mainly limited: she is honest but does not understand. Her account may still be unreliable on interpretation, and the reader infers what she cannot.
3. What each can see; what each wants; what else in the text supports one account.
4. Create irony, reveal a character through self-betrayal and make the reader an active interpreter.
