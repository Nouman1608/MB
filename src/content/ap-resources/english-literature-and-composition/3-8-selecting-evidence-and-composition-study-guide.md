## Choosing evidence is part of the argument

In Topic 3.7 you learned that commentary connects evidence to claims. This topic is about the step before that: **choosing** the evidence, and the step after: **writing it up** so that the reader can follow you.

Writers use evidence **strategically and purposefully**. Two tests apply.

- **Relevant:** the detail bears directly on this claim, not only on the text in general.
- **Sufficient:** its quantity and quality together give apt support for the line of reasoning.

Evidence is **effective** when your commentary explains its logical relationship to the claim. A strong detail with no commentary is wasted; a weak detail with excellent commentary cannot carry the claim.

## Six purposes for evidence

A piece of evidence can be used to do different jobs. Naming the job helps you choose it and write about it.

| Purpose | What it does | Example of use |
|---|---|---|
| Illustrate | Shows the claim at work | A line that displays the character's attitude |
| Clarify | Makes a point precise | A line that defines what a word in your claim means in this text |
| Exemplify | Gives a representative example | One instance of a pattern |
| Associate | Links two ideas or images | A phrase that ties one idea to another |
| Amplify | Builds on or intensifies a point | A second detail that raises the stakes |
| Qualify | Limits or complicates a claim | A detail that shows the claim is not the whole truth |

A paragraph that uses **two or three different purposes** is usually stronger than one that repeats a single kind of detail.

## Worked example 1: selecting and using evidence (public-domain play)

Claim: *In Act 1, Scene 7 of* Macbeth, *Lady Macbeth redefines courage as consistency between desire and action.* (Project Gutenberg text.)

**Step 1: Gather candidates.**

| Candidate | Text |
|---|---|
| (a) | "Was the hope drunk / Wherein you dress'd yourself?" |
| (b) | "Art thou afeard / To be the same in thine own act and valour / As thou art in desire?" |
| (c) | "When you durst do it, then you were a man" |
| (d) | "Letting 'I dare not' wait upon 'I would,'" |
| (e) | "He has almost supp'd." (her first line in the scene) |
| (f) | Macbeth: "I dare do all that may become a man; / Who dares do more is none." |

**Step 2: Test relevance.** (e) is about the dinner and has no bearing on courage. (a) to (d) each relate to courage, and (f) is Macbeth's counter-definition, so it bears on the claim by contrast.

**Step 3: Assign purposes.**

- (b) **illustrates** the claim: it states the test directly ("To be the same in thine own act and valour / As thou art in desire").
- (c) **clarifies** what she means by "a man": the person who "durst do it".
- (d) **exemplifies** the failure she describes, using the image of "I dare not" waiting upon "I would".
- (a) **associates** his resolve with drunkenness and with clothing ("dress'd yourself"), so that his earlier hope seems a costume or a drunkenness he has slept off.
- (f) **qualifies** the claim: Macbeth has his own definition of manliness, so her definition is contested, not universal.

**Step 4: Select.** For one paragraph, choose (b), (c) and (f): an illustration, a clarification and a qualification. Add (d) only if the paragraph needs another example.

**Step 5: Write with commentary.**

> In Act 1, Scene 7, Lady Macbeth redefines courage as consistency between desire and action. She asks "Art thou afeard / To be the same in thine own act and valour / As thou art in desire?", which makes bravery a matter of matching what one wants with what one does. She sharpens the point by claiming that "When you durst do it, then you were a man", so that manliness is measured by daring and not by restraint. Macbeth resists this definition with "I dare do all that may become a man; / Who dares do more is none", and the contrast shows that her view of courage is a choice she imposes, not a settled truth, which makes the scene a contest over what courage means.

**Step 6: Check.** The paragraph uses three pieces of evidence for three purposes, each followed by commentary. The qualification strengthens the thesis by showing the scene as a contest of definitions.

## Composition: control of the sentence

**Grammar and mechanics that follow established conventions allow writers to communicate their interpretation clearly.** That means editing is part of analysis. Common sentence-level problems to check:

| Problem | Example | Edited |
|---|---|---|
| Comma splice | "Dolores opens the door, the heat meets her." | "Dolores opens the door, and the heat meets her." |
| Fragment | "Because the notebook is open." | "Because the notebook is open, the reader sees that his work was interrupted." |
| Tense shift | "The narrator describes the room and then showed her face." | "The narrator describes the room and then shows her face." |
| Subject-verb agreement | "The details of the room shows neglect." | "The details of the room show neglect." |
| Its / it's | "Its a small detail." | "It is a small detail." |
| Pronoun reference | "Dolores spoke to her mother after she left." | "After her mother left, Dolores spoke to her." |
| Quotation not fitting the sentence | "Dolores. 'Found the secateurs.'" | "Dolores 'found the secateurs, clean and sharp'." |
| Vague wording | "This shows a lot about her." | "This shows that she prefers action to speech." |

## Quoting accurately and smoothly

- Copy exactly, including capital letters and spelling.
- Cut to the words you will discuss. Use an ellipsis (…) when you leave out words in the middle.
- Fit the quotation to your grammar. Use square brackets for a necessary change that keeps the sense: the text says "I never knew myself", and your sentence may say that Elizabeth "never knew [her]self".
- Show a line break in verse or drama with a slash.
- Use the present tense for what the text does.

## Worked example 2: editing for clarity (original draft, written by Marlbridge)

Draft:

> In the greenhouse scene Dolores don't want to touch the notebook. She leaves it, where he left it, the page stop in the middle of a column which show his life was interrupted. Its a small detail but it matter.

**Step 1: Read for meaning.** The claim is that the notebook's unfinished column shows his life was interrupted, and that this small detail matters.

**Step 2: Mark the errors.**

| Error | Why it obscures meaning |
|---|---|
| "Dolores don't want" | Agreement ("doesn't" or "does not") |
| "She leaves it, where he left it, the page stop…" | Misplaced comma and a comma splice; "stop" does not agree |
| "which show" | Agreement; the subject of "show" is the page stopping |
| "Its a small detail but it matter" | "Its" for "It is"; missing comma before "but"; "matter" does not agree |
| "This is a small detail" is vague | The paragraph needs the claim in clearer words |

**Step 3: Edit.**

> In the greenhouse scene Dolores does not want to touch the notebook. She leaves it where he left it. The page stops in the middle of a column, which shows that his life was interrupted. It is a small detail, but it matters because it makes his absence visible without a word.

**Step 4: Check.** The meaning is the same, but the reader can now follow it without stopping to guess. The last clause, "because it makes his absence visible without a word", adds the so-what that the draft left out.

## Strong and weak use of evidence

| Weak | Why it is weak | Stronger |
|---|---|---|
| A long block quotation of the whole speech | Hides the words that matter | A short phrase embedded in a sentence, with commentary |
| Three details that all say the same thing | Repetition, not support | Two details of different kinds, and one that qualifies |
| A quotation chosen because it is striking | May not be relevant | A quotation chosen because it bears on the claim |
| Editing only at the end | Errors remain, and revision is rushed | A short edit of each paragraph as you finish it |

## Common misconceptions

- **"More quotations mean a better essay."** Relevance, variety and commentary matter more than number.
- **"Evidence that complicates my claim should be left out."** Relevant complications, handled well, strengthen an argument.
- **"Grammar is separate from analysis."** An unclear sentence can hide a good idea.
- **"A quotation is correct if the meaning is close."** Quote exactly or paraphrase openly.
- **"I must decide my thesis before I choose any evidence."** Interpretation is recursive. Evidence can lead to a line of reasoning, and a line of reasoning can send you back for more evidence.
- **"Editing means changing my ideas."** Editing for clarity leaves the ideas as they are and removes what blocks them.
