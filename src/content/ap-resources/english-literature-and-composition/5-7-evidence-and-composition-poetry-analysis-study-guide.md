In Topic 5.6 you built the argument for a poetry essay. This topic is about the materials and the finish: which words from the poem to use as evidence, how to quote them accurately and smoothly, how much is enough, and how to make the whole essay move from sentence to sentence and paragraph to paragraph so that a reader can follow your thinking. These are the skills of **selecting and using relevant and sufficient evidence** and **controlling the elements of composition**.

## Evidence from a poem

Poems are short and dense, so evidence is usually small: a word, a phrase, a line break, a stanza break. The rules you met in Topic 4.7 apply:

- **Relevant** evidence bears directly on the claim you are making.
- **Sufficient** evidence provides apt support through its quantity and quality: usually two or three well-chosen pieces per claim, and from more than one place in the poem if your claim is about the poem's movement.
- **Effective** evidence is explained: your commentary states the logical relationship between the quotation and the claim.
- Evidence is used **strategically and purposefully**: to illustrate, clarify, exemplify, associate, amplify or qualify. Ask of each quotation, *what is this doing for my claim?*

Developing an interpretation is **recursive**. A phrase may catch your eye and lead to a claim, or a claim may send you back to look for the best proof. Do not be afraid to change a claim when the evidence shows something more interesting.

## Quoting poetry

Three habits make your quotations accurate and readable.

1. **Quote the smallest accurate phrase.** The phrase "kindly stopped for me" shows Death's courtesy; you do not need the whole stanza.
2. **Integrate it into your own sentence.** Write: *Death is presented as a courteous host who "kindly stopped for me"*, not *The poem says. "He kindly stopped for me."*
3. **Mark line breaks with a slash.** When a quotation runs across lines, put a forward slash with a space on each side: *"The carriage held but just ourselves / And Immortality"*. Use a double slash ( // ) for a stanza break. Keep the poem's capitals and punctuation, and use an ellipsis if you omit words.

Quote the line break on purpose when it matters. A break can leave a word hanging at the end of a line ("Immortality") and change how you read it, and your commentary may need to point it out.

## Composition: coherence from sentence to essay

Coherence operates at three levels: within a sentence, within a paragraph, and across the whole essay. Writers achieve it when the arrangement of reasons, evidence and ideas is logical, and when they use **transitional elements** (words, phrases, clauses, sentences or even paragraphs) to show relationships between ideas.

| Tool | Level | What it does | Example for a poetry essay |
|---|---|---|---|
| Topic sentence | Paragraph | States the claim and links to the thesis | "The journey's second stage turns courtesy into routine." |
| Transitional word or phrase | Sentence | Shows contrast, cause, sequence or addition | "however", "as a result", "by contrast" |
| Repetition of a key term | Paragraph, essay | Keeps the argument audible | Repeating "courtesy" or "stillness" from the thesis |
| Synonyms | Sentence | Varies wording without changing the idea | "calm", "composure", "poise" |
| Clear pronoun reference | Sentence | Prevents confusion | "This repetition..." not "This shows..." |
| Parallel structure | Sentence | Matches grammar to matching ideas | "Where the first stanza hurries, the second lingers." |
| Linking sentence between paragraphs | Essay | Moves the argument forward | "If the first half of the poem makes death polite, the second half makes it permanent." |

1. **Claim.** Write the paragraph's claim in one sentence.
2. **Proof.** Choose one to three short phrases that support it, from more than one place if possible.
3. **Purpose.** Name what each does: illustrate, qualify, amplify.
4. **Flow.** Use a topic sentence, transitions and a closing link to the thesis.

## Worked example 1: choosing evidence

The poem is Emily Dickinson's "Because I could not stop for Death" (written about 1863; published 1890 under the title "The Chariot"). The text below is the five-stanza version printed in 1890.

> Because I could not stop for Death,
> He kindly stopped for me;
> The carriage held but just ourselves
> And Immortality.
>
> We slowly drove, he knew no haste,
> And I had put away
> My labor, and my leisure too,
> For his civility.
>
> We passed the school where children played,
> Their lessons scarcely done;
> We passed the fields of gazing grain,
> We passed the setting sun.
>
> We paused before a house that seemed
> A swelling of the ground;
> The roof was scarcely visible,
> The cornice but a mound.
>
> Since then 't is centuries; but each
> Feels shorter than the day
> I first surmised the horses' heads
> Were toward eternity.

Claim for one paragraph: *"The poem treats death as a courteous escort whose politeness makes the journey calm and even hospitable."*

| Candidate | Relevant? | Purpose | Decision |
|---|---|---|---|
| "He kindly stopped for me" | Yes: Death is the one who stops, and is kind | Illustrates the personified courtesy | Keep |
| "he knew no haste" | Yes: calm pace, no pressure | Amplifies the unhurried manner | Keep |
| "For his civility" | Yes: she gives up her work and leisure out of politeness | Clarifies why she yields | Keep a short phrase |
| "We passed the setting sun" | Not for this claim: it belongs to a claim about time and the passing of life | Exemplifies a stage of the journey | Save for the next paragraph |

**Sufficiency.** The claim is about manner, so three phrases from the first two stanzas give enough support. A fourth from the third stanza would shift the claim toward the journey, and a second claim should carry it.

**Integrated sentence.** Death is characterised as a courteous host who "kindly stopped for me" and who "knew no haste", so the speaker's surrender of "My labor, and my leisure too" appears as politeness in return, "for his civility", not as fear.

## Worked example 2: from list to flow

Here is a disjointed paragraph on the same poem (written by Marlbridge for this exercise).

> Death is polite. "He kindly stopped for me." Then they pass a school. There are children. The sun sets. This is the journey. The ending is about eternity.

**Diagnosis.** There is no topic sentence; the quotation is dropped in with no integration; "This is the journey" has no clear referent; the sentences are short and unlinked; the ending is announced with no evidence; nothing links to a thesis.

**Revision.**

> The speaker's journey with Death begins in courtesy and moves, stage by stage, toward permanence. Death "kindly stopped for me", and his unhurried manner ("he knew no haste") makes the speaker's yielding seem polite, not forced. As the carriage passes "the school where children played", "the fields of gazing grain" and finally "the setting sun", however, the repeated "We passed" turns each scene into a stage of a life left behind. Consequently the calm of the opening is not a denial of death but the means by which the poem lets the reader accept it.

**What changed.**

- A topic sentence states a claim ("begins in courtesy and moves... toward permanence").
- Quotations are short and built into sentences.
- Transitions ("however", "Consequently") mark contrast and consequence.
- The repetition "We passed" is named and explained, and the key terms *courtesy* and *toward permanence* link the paragraph to the thesis.
- The last sentence links back to the thesis and prepares the next paragraph.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Quote more lines to show I know the poem."** Evidence is sufficient when it supports the claim, not when it is long.
- **"Naming a device is evidence."** "There is personification" points at evidence; the words and the commentary are the evidence.
- **"Transitions are decoration."** Each one signals a relationship; choose it for its meaning.
- **"I can change a quotation slightly to fit my sentence."** Quote accurately; use an ellipsis or square brackets for changes.
- **"Every paragraph should cover the whole poem."** A paragraph should cover one claim, using the part of the poem that best supports it.
- **"Repetition is always poor style."** Deliberate repetition of key terms creates coherence.
