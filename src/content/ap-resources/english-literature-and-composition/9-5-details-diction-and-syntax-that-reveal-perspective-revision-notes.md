## Recap

**Perspective** is the position from which a story is told: what is noticed, what is emphasised, what is left out, and how it is judged. You infer it from **details** (what is mentioned), **diction** (word choice, register and connotation) and **syntax** (sentence shape, described in plain words). Texts can contain **multiple, even contrasting, perspectives**; a narrator or speaker may **change** through actions and interactions; and **changes or inconsistencies** in perspective may produce **irony** or complexity. Attribute the perspective to the narrator, not automatically to the author. The full explanation, with worked examples from *Pride and Prejudice* and *Huckleberry Finn*, is in the [study guide](/advanced-course-resources/english-literature-and-composition/9-5-details-diction-and-syntax-that-reveal-perspective-study-guide/).

## Key relationships

| Evidence | Ask | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Details | What is noticed or omitted? | The narrator turns at once to the "surrounding families" |
| Diction | What do the words connote? | "Rightful property" suggests ownership |
| Syntax | What pattern, and what does it do? | A long delayed sentence makes irony a late jolt |
| Contrast | Whose view is set against whose? | Society's market versus the narrator's irony |
| Change | Does the perspective shift? | Huck's self-judgement in Chapter 31 versus his opening candour |

| Weak description | Precise description |
|---|---|
| "ironic" | "ironic and detached, exposing a social habit" |
| "informal" | "candid and unpretentious, hedging its own truth-telling" |
| "sad" | "wry and self-aware, implicated in what it observes" |

## Conditions to remember

- The narrator is a voice, not simply the author.
- First-person narrators can be mistaken, biased or limited.
- Formal language is not neutral; it carries judgement.
- Always say what the perspective does for the reader: irony, sympathy, distance, trust.

## Mistakes to avoid

- Labelling the tone without evidence.
- Listing syntax features without an effect.
- Treating the narrator as the author.
- Missing a contrast between perspectives.
- Ignoring change over the course of the text.

## Worked mini-example

Take the opening of *Pride and Prejudice*. *Source:* an omniscient narrator. *Evidence:* the grand phrase "universally acknowledged", a long second sentence that delays its point, and "rightful property". *Perspective:* ironic and detached. *Contrast:* the families' view of a rich bachelor as a prize. *Does:* sets the novel's tone of social critique from the first sentence.

## Quick self-check

1. What three kinds of evidence reveal perspective?
2. What does "rightful property" suggest about the families' perspective?
3. How does Huck's voice present honesty?
4. Why might a narrator's changing perspective produce irony?
5. How can you write about syntax without technical terms?

**Answers.**
1. Details, diction and syntax.
2. That they treat marriage as ownership and the man as something to be claimed.
3. As modest and a matter of degree, through colloquial diction and the hedge "mainly".
4. A gap opens between what the narrator sees and what the reader sees, or between the narrator's earlier and later views.
5. Describe the sentence pattern in plain words (long and delayed, short and blunt, loose and additive) and say what it does.
