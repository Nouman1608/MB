## Recap

- An **allusion** is an indirect reference to something outside the text.
- Allusions can refer to **literary works** (including myths and sacred texts), **other works of art** (paintings, music), and **people, places or events**.
- An allusion brings the **associations** of its source into the poem without explaining them.
- An allusion is not a quotation: a quotation repeats words; an allusion refers indirectly.
- Explain an allusion in four steps: reference, associations, link, effect.
- An allusion may elevate, deepen, contrast or **ironise**; check how it sits against the poem's situation.

## Key relationships

| Step | Question | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Reference | What is alluded to? | the pharaoh Ozymandias |
| Associations | What does that bring? | absolute power, monumental pride |
| Link | How does it connect to the poem? | the boast sits beside a ruin |
| Effect | What does it do? | irony: power shown as temporary |

| Source type | Typical associations |
|---|---|
| Myth | Heroism, fate, endurance, divine power |
| Sacred text | Judgement, hope, sacrifice, creation |
| Art or music | Beauty, a mood or a period |
| Person | A quality or a role the person stands for |
| Place or event | Discovery, loss, triumph, catastrophe |

## Assumptions

- You read an allusion in the **context** of the poem's situation.
- If a source is unfamiliar, you work from signals in the text and from how the reference functions.
- Associations are supported by the poem, not just by general knowledge.

## Mistakes to avoid

- Naming a reference and stopping.
- Retelling the source in detail instead of using it.
- Assuming an allusion always praises.
- Confusing allusion with quotation.
- Ignoring the irony when a grand reference sits beside a small reality.

## Quick self-check

1. What kinds of source can an allusion refer to?
2. What do "Apollo" and "Homer" bring to Keats's sonnet?
3. How does Shelley's allusion to Ozymandias produce irony?
4. Why should you not simply retell a myth when explaining an allusion?

**Answers.** (1) Literary works (including myths and sacred texts), other works of art, and people, places or events. (2) The sacred, ancient height of poetry, a realm the speaker has travelled to. (3) The name and "king of kings" promise lasting power while the scene shows only a ruin. (4) The point is which associations matter and what they do to the poem; a retelling does not answer that.

## Sentence frames to adapt

- "The reference to [source] brings the associations of [x] and [y]."
- "By alluding to [source], the speaker places [situation] on the scale of [x]."
- "The allusion contrasts with [detail] to create irony, because [what each suggests]."
- "The allusion supports the speaker's attitude of [attitude] by [how]."

Name the source in a few words, give the associations that matter and tie them to a quoted detail. Do not retell the myth or the event.
