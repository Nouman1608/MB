## Structure and contrast: why the shape of a poem is part of its meaning

A poem is read in time. You meet the first line before the second, and what you learn early shapes how you take what comes later. **Structure** is the arrangement of a poem's parts and the order in which it reveals them: where lines and stanzas end, where sentences begin and stop, where a pattern is set up and where it is broken. Because a poet decides all of this, every structural choice is a candidate for evidence. The question to ask is never "what is the structure?" but "what does this arrangement make me notice, expect, or feel, and how does that help build the poem's meaning?"

Four ideas from this topic carry most of the weight.

- **Ideas and images can run past a line or stanza.** A sentence may begin in one line and finish three lines later, or cross from one stanza into the next (enjambment). When a thought refuses to stop where the line stops, the reader feels momentum, strain, or hurry. When a line ends exactly where the sentence ends (end-stopping), the reader feels closure, calm, or finality.
- **Punctuation is evidence.** A colon promises an explanation, a dash breaks the thought, a semicolon holds two ideas in balance, a question mark opens a doubt. Punctuation controls pace and signals what the speaker is sure or unsure of.
- **A pattern, once set up, makes any interruption stand out.** If a poem has run in regular rhyme, rhythm, repetition or stanza length and then departs from it, the departure becomes a point of emphasis. A turn in thought (a **volta**) is often marked this way, with a word such as "But" or "Yet", or with a change of subject.
- **Contrast creates meaning from difference.** Three kinds are named in the course framework: juxtaposition, irony and paradox.

## Three kinds of contrast

| Term | What it is | Test you can apply | What it often does |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Juxtaposition** | Two things placed side by side so their difference becomes meaningful. It can create or show an **antithesis** (a direct opposition). | Which two things are set against each other, and what changes because they sit together? | Makes a quality visible by contrast: grandeur beside ruin, youth beside age. |
| **Irony** | A gap between what is expected and what happens (**situational irony**) or between what is said and what is meant (**verbal irony**). The expectation may come from the reader's own assumptions or from expectations the poem has set up. | What did the poem lead me to expect, and what did I get instead? | Exposes pretension, self-deception or cruelty; often produces a critical or bitter tone. |
| **Paradox** | Seemingly contradictory elements set together, where the contradiction, resolved or not, reveals a hidden or unexpected idea. | What seems impossible here, and what truth does the impossibility point to? | Compresses a difficult idea into a surprising statement; gives the poem its intellectual spark. |

A short paradox from the seventeenth century shows how it works. Near the end of his sonnet "Death, be not proud", John Donne addresses death directly and ends with "One short sleep past, we wake eternally, / And death shall be no more; Death, thou shalt die." The claim that death will die is impossible if death is only the end of life. It is true if death is only a "short sleep" before eternal waking. The contradiction is the argument: it strips death of the power the opening lines say it has been wrongly given.

## A method for structure and contrast

1. **Paraphrase first.** Say in plain words what the poem says, stanza by stanza or in thought-units.
2. **Mark the turns.** Where does the subject, tone, addressee, tense or pattern change? Look for "but", "yet", "and yet", a new stanza, a change of punctuation, a broken rhyme.
3. **Name the contrast.** Is the poem setting two things side by side (juxtaposition), saying the opposite of what it means or letting events overturn expectation (irony), or stating something that seems impossible (paradox)?
4. **Explain the effect.** What does the break or contrast make the reader feel or understand that the poem could not achieve in a straight line?
5. **Bring it back to the whole.** How does this part change your interpretation of the entire poem, including its beginning and end?

Steps 4 and 5 are where the marks are. Naming a volta without explaining its effect is labelling, not analysis.

## Worked example 1: a turn that reverses a request

Christina Rossetti's sonnet "Remember" (1862) is spoken by someone who knows she will die. The whole poem is short enough to quote, so you can see every structural choice.

> Remember me when I am gone away,  
> Gone far away into the silent land;  
> When you can no more hold me by the hand,  
> Nor I half turn to go yet turning stay.  
> Remember me when no more day by day  
> You tell me of our future that you planned:  
> Only remember me; you understand  
> It will be late to counsel then or pray.  
> Yet if you should forget me for a while  
> And afterwards remember, do not grieve:  
> For if the darkness and corruption leave  
> A vestige of the thoughts that once I had,  
> Better by far you should forget and smile  
> Than that you should remember and be sad.

*The text follows the Project Gutenberg edition of Rossetti's Goblin Market, The Prince's Progress, and Other Poems (the poem was written in 1849 and first published in 1862). Punctuation varies slightly between editions.*

**Step 1, paraphrase.** The speaker asks to be remembered after her death, reminds the listener that later pleading will do no good, then says that if he forgets her it is better to forget and be happy than to remember and be sad.

**Step 2, mark the turns.** The first eight lines (the octave) follow one pattern: two quatrains, each opening with the imperative "Remember me", rhyming *abba abba*. Line 9 opens with "Yet", and the rhyme and thought both change. A volta has arrived at the traditional place for a sonnet's turn.

**Step 3, name the contrast.** The poem contrasts two requests that appear to contradict each other: *remember me* (lines 1 to 8) and *forget me, if that is happier for you* (lines 9 to 14). Together they form a paradox of love: the strongest demand the speaker can make is to release the listener from the demand.

**Step 4, explain the effect.** The repeated "Remember me" sets a firm, almost commanding pattern, and the line "Only remember me; you understand" tightens it with a semicolon and a clipped, insistent "Only". The "Yet" breaks that insistent rhythm, and the sentence that follows is long, qualified and conditional ("if... and afterwards... do not grieve"). The change from short imperatives to a hesitant, tender conditional enacts the speaker's shift from claiming the listener's memory to caring more about his happiness. The last line puts "remember" and "be sad" in the same breath, so the poem ends by linking memory to pain.

**Step 5, whole poem.** A reader might expect an elegy to end by intensifying its demand to be remembered. Instead the poem ends by giving permission to forget. This makes the poem's central statement not a plea for remembrance but a portrait of love that is willing to be forgotten for the beloved's sake.

*Model commentary sentence:* "The volta at 'Yet' converts a command into a concession: after eight lines of 'Remember me', the speaker's sudden preference for her listener's smile over his grief shows that the love she is asking him to remember is the kind that puts his happiness first."

## Worked example 2: irony built from juxtaposition

Percy Bysshe Shelley's sonnet "Ozymandias" (1818) reports what a traveller saw in a desert: a broken statue of a king. The traveller quotes its inscription:

> My name is Ozymandias, King of Kings;  
> Look on my works, ye Mighty, and despair!

and then the poem continues:

> Nothing beside remains. Round the decay  
> Of that colossal wreck, boundless and bare  
> The lone and level sands stretch far away.

*Capitalisation and punctuation vary between editions of this poem; the words quoted here are the ones in common modern texts.*

**Step 1, paraphrase.** A traveller describes two huge legs and a broken face of a statue in the desert. A boast carved on its base is still legible. Nothing else is left but sand.

**Step 2, mark the turns.** The inscription is the poem's loudest moment: two commanding lines in which the king speaks in his own voice. The next sentence, "Nothing beside remains", is short and flat, and its full stop falls in the middle of the line, so the poem pauses hard. The sudden drop in tone is the volta: it follows the boast immediately.

**Step 3, name the contrast.** This is **juxtaposition** (boast beside wreck) that produces **situational irony**. The inscription expects the "Mighty" to despair at the king's greatness; the reader, looking at the "colossal wreck", despairs of something different, the way greatness decays. The king's own words now prove the opposite of what he meant.

**Step 4, explain the effect.** "Colossal" and "King of Kings" belong to the register of grandeur, but "wreck", "decay" and "bare" take the same scale and apply it to ruin. The poem's last line lets the long sounds of "lone and level sands stretch far away" slow the reading and carry the eye outward into emptiness, so the reader experiences the boast being swallowed by space.

**Step 5, whole poem.** The poem passes judgement on power without ever stating a moral. By letting the king's own words become evidence against him, it argues that human ambition is brief and that the thing that outlasts him is a record of his arrogance, preserved by the sculptor who "read" and mocked his passions. The contrast is not decoration; it is the poem's argument.

## Weak and strong versions side by side

| Weak | Why it falls short | Stronger |
|---|---|---|
| "The poem has a volta at line 9." | Locates but does not explain. | "The volta at 'Yet' converts a command into a concession, showing that the speaker values her listener's peace above her own memory." |
| "Shelley uses irony to show the king is powerful." | Misreads the irony: it exposes the opposite of power. | "The inscription's boast is overturned by the surrounding wreck, so the king's own words prove how little his power lasted." |
| "The poem uses contrast." | Names no contrast and states no effect. | "Setting 'King of Kings' beside 'decay' and 'wreck' makes the scale of the ruin match the scale of the claim." |
| "Enjambment makes the poem flow." | Generalises; any poem could have this sentence. | "The long conditional that begins 'For if the darkness and corruption leave' does not reach its main clause until 'Better by far' two lines later, so the reader has to wait, as the speaker seems to hesitate before giving her permission." |

## Common misconceptions

- **"A volta is always at line 9 of a sonnet."** Often, not always. Find the turn from the poem's thought, then see where it falls. Some sonnets turn at the couplet, and some poems that are not sonnets have turns too.
- **"Irony means sarcasm."** Sarcasm is one form of verbal irony. Situational irony, where events overturn expectation, has no speaker being sarcastic at all.
- **"Paradox is just a contradiction."** A paradox looks contradictory but points to a truth. If the contradiction has no meaning, it is an inconsistency, not a paradox.
- **"Structure means the rhyme scheme."** Rhyme is one part. Sentence shape, punctuation, stanza breaks, repetition and order of ideas all count.
- **"If I name the device, I have analysed it."** The mark comes from explaining what the device does and tying it to the poem's overall meaning.
- **"A pattern must be perfect to be a pattern."** Even a loosely regular pattern makes departures noticeable. Describe what the reader would expect and how the poem departs.
