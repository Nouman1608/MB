## How to use this guide

This guide is for the first section of the exam. It explains how that section is built, gives a pacing plan, and teaches a repeatable way to read passages and answer each kind of question. Two practice sets follow, one prose and one poetry.

Every passage and question here is **original Marlbridge practice**, written for this guide. None of it is past exam material. The passages are shorter than real ones so you can practise the method in a few minutes, and the questions follow the style of the exam but are not taken from it. The guide gives **no predicted score**.

## The section at a glance

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| Number of questions | 55 |
| Time | 60 minutes |
| Weight | 45% of the exam |
| Format | Four options (A to D) per question |
| Structure | Five sets, each with about 8 to 13 questions after one passage |
| Passages | At least two prose fiction passages and at least two poems, of varying difficulty |
| Periods | A larger share of 20th-century and contemporary texts than earlier ones |

The section tests the same seven skill categories as the course. The approximate weighting is below.

| Skill category | Share of the section | What it looks like |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Character | 16 to 20% | What a detail, speech or action shows about a character or a relationship |
| 2. Setting | 3 to 6% | What a setting contributes to mood, values or meaning |
| 3. Plot and structure | 16 to 20% | How a shift, a contrast, an order of events or a stanza structure creates meaning |
| 4. Narrator or speaker | 21 to 26% | Perspective, tone, attitude, reliability, what a point of view reveals |
| 5. Word choice, imagery and symbols | 10 to 13% | What a word, a pattern of images or a symbol does |
| 6. Comparison | 10 to 13% | What a simile, metaphor or personification transfers |
| 7. Argument about interpretation | 10 to 13% | Which claim, evidence or reading a text supports |

Two things follow from these numbers. First, the narrator or speaker is the single biggest category, so always ask "who is speaking, and how do they feel about what they describe?" Second, nearly all questions are about **function**: what a choice does, not merely what it is.

## Pacing

Sixty minutes for 55 questions leaves about 65 seconds per question, but the passage reading comes out of the same time, so plan by **set**.

| Stage | Time per set | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Read the passage | 3 to 4 minutes | Read once actively, mark shifts, repeated words and the speaker's attitude |
| Answer the questions | 8 to 9 minutes | About 60 to 70 seconds each |
| Spare | 0 to 1 minute | Return to marked questions |

Over five sets that is about 60 minutes. If you can, aim to finish a set slightly under time so that you can use the surplus on the hard questions later.

Pacing rules:

1. **Do not stay on one question for more than about a minute and a half.** Choose the best option, mark the question and move on.
2. **Answer every question.** A blank can only score zero. If you must guess, eliminate what you can first.
3. **Check the clock at the end of each set,** not after every question.
4. **Do easier sets first only if you have chosen to.** The sets appear in a fixed order, and switching costs time. A safe default is to work in order.

## Reading a prose passage

Read once, actively. Use a four-step routine, marking in the margin as you go.

1. **Who tells it, and how do they feel?** Mark the narrator's distance (close or detached) and tone (admiring, ironic, tender, detached). Look for adverbs, qualifiers and small judgements: "a little," "plainly," "as if."
2. **What changes?** Mark each shift: a change of time, place, perspective, tone or length of sentence. Many structure questions ask about a shift.
3. **What is repeated or surprising?** Mark repeated words and images, and any detail that does not seem to fit. These are what questions about word choice and symbolism are built from.
4. **What does the last sentence do?** Endings reframe the passage. Note whether the final sentence confirms, undercuts or complicates what came before.

After reading, say the passage's point in one sentence: "The narrator shows a character who ____ but ____." That sentence will answer several whole-passage questions before you reach them.

## Reading a poem

Poems are shorter but denser, so the routine differs.

1. **Read the title and the last line first,** then the whole poem straight through. Titles often tell you the situation.
2. **Find the speaker and the addressee.** Who is talking, to whom, and when? A speaker looking back is different from one in the moment.
3. **Mark the structure:** stanza breaks, sentence breaks and where a sentence runs over a line. Ask what each section contributes and where the poem turns.
4. **Underline the figures:** simile, metaphor, personification, imagery, repetition. Next to each, jot the effect in two or three words.
5. **Paraphrase one stanza** in plain prose to check that you have understood the literal meaning before you interpret the figurative.

Sentences in poetry may run for several lines. Read to the punctuation, not to the line break, and then ask what the line break adds.

## The question types and how to answer them

| Type | Typical stem | How to answer |
|---|---|---|
| Function of a word or phrase | "The phrase '...' (line 4) primarily serves to..." | Reread the sentence; say what the phrase adds (attitude, contrast, irony, mood) before reading the options |
| Function of a sentence or paragraph | "In relation to the first paragraph, the second paragraph..." | Say what each part does (describes, explains, complicates, reverses), then name the relationship |
| Effect of a device | "The simile in lines 7 to 8 most nearly conveys..." | Name the two things compared and the shared quality; check context for which quality matters |
| Tone or attitude | "The narrator's attitude toward X can best be described as..." | Look at the adjectives, qualifiers and what is emphasised; avoid options that are too extreme |
| Reading in context | "In context, '...' most nearly means..." | Reread two lines before and after; test each option by replacing the word |
| Inference | "Which can be reasonably inferred...?" | The answer is supported by the text but not stated outright; reject options that need information the passage does not give |
| Structure and shift | "The shift in line 9 marks a movement from... to..." | Find the signal (a conjunction, a change of tense or image); name both sides |
| Whole passage | "The passage as a whole is best understood as..." | Use your one-sentence summary; reject options that cover only one part |
| Argument | "Which statement best expresses a defensible claim about...?" | Choose the claim that is specific, supported by details and about the text's meaning, not its subject |

### Say the answer first

A useful habit: after you read the question, cover the options and write a short answer of your own in the margin ("the bell is pitied, not hated"). Then look for the option that matches. This protects you from persuasive-sounding distractors.

## How wrong options go wrong

Most distractors fail in one of a small number of ways.

| Pattern | What it looks like | Example idea |
|---|---|---|
| Too extreme | Uses "always," "never," "completely," "only" | "The speaker rejects all work" when the speaker only misses one task |
| Too narrow | True of one detail but not of the whole question | Answers a whole-passage question with a single detail |
| Unsupported | Reasonable, but nothing in the text says it | Adds a motive the passage never gives |
| Opposite | Reverses a tone or relationship | "Hostile" when the text is affectionate |
| True but irrelevant | A correct statement that does not answer the question asked | Names a real device but not the one asked about |
| Literal for figurative | Takes a metaphor as fact | "The garden is a harbour" read as a real harbour |
| Echo of wording | Repeats a phrase from the passage but changes its meaning | Quoting a word from line 5 that does not answer the question |

When two options remain, compare each with the exact words of the question. The better option usually answers the question *as asked* (function, effect or attitude) and uses language that the text can bear.

## Prose practice set

Read the passage, then answer the questions. Each is followed by an answer and explanation. The passage is original, written by Marlbridge for practice.

> The bell at Harcourt School had not rung properly in eleven years. It gave, instead, a short dry cough, like a man clearing his throat before an apology, and the children had long since stopped looking up when it did. Mr. Penn, who had taught the third form since before the roof was mended, said it was a matter of discipline: a school ought to be able to start its day without being shouted at by metal.
>
> The new headmistress disagreed. On her second morning she carried a ladder across the yard, climbed to the belfry in a good grey skirt that she plainly regretted, and returned twenty minutes later with oil on her sleeve and a small brown bird's nest in her hand. "There," she said, setting it on Mr. Penn's desk as if it were evidence. He looked at it for some time. "We had always assumed," he said at last, "that it was the bell."
>
> The next morning the bell rang clear across the yard, and Mr. Penn, who had said nothing about it, was seen to stop at the gate and listen with his hat in his hand.

**Question 1.** The simile "like a man clearing his throat before an apology" (paragraph 1) most nearly suggests that the bell

- (A) is too loud for the school
- (B) sounds timid and faintly embarrassed
- (C) has been damaged by a person
- (D) is rung by someone who regrets it

<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (B).** Clearing the throat before an apology is a hesitant, unconfident sound, which matches "short dry cough." The simile makes the bell's failure sound shy and slightly sorry for itself.

- (A) is the opposite: the sound is short and dry, not loud.
- (C) is unsupported: the passage gives no human cause and the nest explains it later.
- (D) takes the "apology" literally and applies it to a person who rings the bell, not to the sound.
</details>

**Question 2.** Mr. Penn's remark that a school should start its day "without being shouted at by metal" most clearly serves to

- (A) show that he dislikes all loud noises
- (B) complain that the bell is rung too often
- (C) suggest that the pupils are badly behaved
- (D) present a defect as if it were a principle

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (D).** He calls the bell's weak cough "a matter of discipline" and gives a dignified reason for tolerating it. The grand phrase "shouted at by metal" turns an excuse into an opinion.

- (A) is too general and does not fit the passage: the bell is quiet, not loud.
- (B) is unsupported: the passage says the bell hardly rings properly, not that it rings too often.
- (C) is not addressed: discipline here concerns the bell, not the pupils.
</details>

**Question 3.** The phrase "as if it were evidence" suggests that the headmistress

- (A) presents the nest as proof that settles the matter
- (B) is hiding her intentions from Mr. Penn
- (C) expects Mr. Penn to be angry
- (D) wants to blame Mr. Penn for the bell's fault

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (A).** "Evidence" belongs to the language of argument: she sets the nest down like an exhibit in a case, quietly claiming the argument as won. The "as if" keeps a gentle amusement in the narrator's tone.

- (B) is the opposite: she is openly showing him.
- (C) is unsupported: no sign of expecting anger.
- (D) adds a motive of blame that the passage does not give.
</details>

**Question 4.** Mr. Penn's statement, "We had always assumed... that it was the bell," chiefly shows that he

- (A) has misunderstood how the bell works
- (B) is trying to hide his embarrassment by changing the subject
- (C) concedes, with some dignity, that a long-held belief was wrong
- (D) blames the previous headmaster for the problem

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (C).** The plural "we" shares the mistake, and "at last" shows the pause before he admits it. The statement is a concession made with restraint.

- (A) is too narrow: the issue is not how the bell works but what he believed.
- (B) is contradicted: he addresses the nest directly.
- (D) is unsupported: nobody is blamed.
</details>

**Question 5.** The final paragraph primarily serves to

- (A) summarise the headmistress's methods
- (B) introduce a new conflict between the headmistress and the pupils
- (C) explain why the nest was in the belfry
- (D) show Mr. Penn's changed feeling without stating it

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (D).** Mr. Penn "had said nothing," yet he stops and listens "with his hat in his hand," a gesture of respect. The narrator shows his reaction through behaviour.

- (A) restates what the previous paragraph already shows.
- (B) has no support: no conflict with pupils appears.
- (C) answers a question the passage never raises.
</details>

**Question 6.** The narrator's attitude toward Mr. Penn is best described as

- (A) scornful
- (B) fondly amused
- (C) unreservedly admiring
- (D) indifferent

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (B).** The narrator smiles at his stiff principle and his silent change of heart, but the picture of him with his hat in his hand is tender.

- (A) is too harsh: the passage never mocks him cruelly.
- (C) is too extreme: he is gently teased, not admired without reservation.
- (D) ignores the many small touches of fond humour.
</details>

## Poetry practice set

The poem below is original, written by Marlbridge for practice. Line numbers are shown as on the exam.

**Wash Day**

> 1 Monday, and the whole street breathes out sheets.  
> 2 My mother pegs them with her mouth full of pegs,  
> 3 talking round the wood as a swimmer talks  
> 4 through water: coal, the neighbours, someone's legs.
>
> 5 By noon each shirt stands upright in the wind  
> 6 like a man who has been told some news and cannot sit.  
> 7 The garden is a harbour; we are small boats.
>
> 8 By four it all comes down. She folds it flat  
> 9 in squares exact as the corners of a map,  
> 10 and I carry the pegs. Nothing in our house  
> 11 was ever said outright. It was ironed and put away.

**Question 7.** In line 1, the phrase "the whole street breathes out sheets" is an example of

- (A) a simile that compares the street to a house
- (B) an allusion to a literary work
- (C) personification that gives the street a shared, living energy
- (D) an understatement of the amount of washing

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (C).** A street cannot literally breathe. Giving it breath makes the neighbourhood one living thing and makes the washing feel like a communal exhale.

- (A) has no "like" or "as" and compares the street to nothing.
- (B) refers to no other work.
- (D) misreads the image: "breathes out" suggests plenty, not understatement.
</details>

**Question 8.** The comparison in lines 2 to 4, "talking round the wood as a swimmer talks / through water," most nearly suggests that the mother

- (A) speaks steadily and skilfully despite an obstacle
- (B) is out of breath from hard work
- (C) is afraid of the water
- (D) refuses to talk to her neighbours

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (A).** A swimmer who talks through water manages it with practice, and the mother manages to talk "round the wood" of the pegs. The simile conveys skill and ease.

- (B) is unsupported: the simile suggests competence, not exhaustion.
- (C) takes the water literally and misses the comparison.
- (D) is contradicted by the neighbours being among her topics.
</details>

**Question 9.** The comparison of the shirts to "a man who has been told some news and cannot sit" (lines 5 to 6) most strongly suggests

- (A) the mother's anger at her neighbours
- (B) a restless, slightly anxious energy beneath an ordinary scene
- (C) that the shirts have been damaged by the wind
- (D) a celebration that the whole street is attending

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (B).** A man who "cannot sit" after news is unsettled. The image lends the cheerful washing scene an undercurrent of unspoken tension, which the end of the poem develops.

- (A) imports a feeling the poem does not give to the mother.
- (C) uses the shirts' physical state, which the simile is not about.
- (D) reverses the tone: the man is disturbed, not festive.
</details>

**Question 10.** Between line 7 and line 8, the poem shifts primarily from

- (A) a warm memory to a bitter judgement
- (B) a description of a place to a description of a person
- (C) lively, open movement to ordered containment
- (D) the mother's view to the neighbours' view

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (C).** Before the break, shirts stand "in the wind" and the garden is a harbour. After it, the washing "comes down" and the mother "folds it flat / in squares." The poem moves from open movement to tidy enclosure.

- (A) overstates: the tone does not become bitter.
- (B) is wrong: the mother appears in both parts.
- (D) is unsupported: the neighbours are not a perspective in the poem.
</details>

**Question 11.** The final two lines ("Nothing in our house / was ever said outright. It was ironed and put away") most strongly suggest that the speaker sees the family's handling of feeling as

- (A) strictly fair to everyone
- (B) a cause of the mother's illness
- (C) deliberately cruel
- (D) similar to the handling of the laundry

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (D).** The pronoun "It" shifts from the washing to everything unsaid in the house, so feeling is folded and stored like cloth. The poem's domestic image becomes a comment on emotional reserve.

- (A) is not supported: there is no discussion of fairness.
- (B) adds an illness the poem never mentions.
- (C) is too extreme: the tone is quiet, not accusing.
</details>

## Using the practice sets

After each set, mark your answers, then write **why** you chose any wrong answer in one line. Look for patterns across sets.

| Pattern | What it means | What to change |
|---|---|---|
| You chose options that were true but did not answer the question | You are reading the options before you decide what is asked | Say the answer first, then match |
| You chose extreme options | You are reacting to emotive words | Look for qualifiers in the passage ("a little," "plainly") |
| You missed shift questions | You are not marking turns | Underline conjunctions and changes of tense or image as you read |
| You ran out of time on the last set | You spent too long early | Set a hard limit of about a minute and a half per question |

## Building a routine

- Do one timed set of 8 to 13 questions a few times a week, alternating prose and poetry.
- Keep an error log with three columns: the question type, why you were wrong, and the one-line rule you will use next time.
- Read poems aloud (or in your head with their sentences) so that you hear the structure.
- After each set, rewrite the passage's point in one sentence. That sentence is also the beginning of a thesis for the essay section, so multiple-choice practice and essay practice help each other.

## Common misconceptions

- **"The longest or most sophisticated option is the right one."** The right option is the one the text supports, in plain terms. Elaborate wording is often a decoy.
- **"If I know a device's name, I can answer."** Questions ask what a device does in context. Naming it is the first step, not the answer.
- **"I should skip hard passages."** Difficulty varies by passage, and each set carries similar weight. Do your best on every question rather than abandoning a set.
- **"Poems have a single hidden meaning."** The questions ask about meanings the text supports, such as a speaker's attitude or a shift. Reading closely beats guessing a secret message.
- **"I should leave doubtful questions blank."** A blank scores nothing. Eliminate what you can and choose.
