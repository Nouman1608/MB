## Recap

- **Setting** depicts a time and place and **conveys the values** associated with it.
- A **change of setting** may suggest other movements, changes or shifts in the narrative.
- **Contrasted settings** can establish a **conflict of values or ideas**.
- Characters' **interaction with their surroundings** reveals insights about the characters and about the settings.
- How a character **behaves in or describes** a setting reveals an **attitude** and contributes to development and interpretation.

## Grid

| Look at | Ask |
|---|---|
| Sensory details | What do they suggest or value? |
| Social details | What norms or pressures operate? |
| Temporal details | What mood or pressure of time? |
| Changes in details | What shifts in the narrative? |
| Character's behaviour | What attitude is shown to the place? |

## Examples (public domain)

- **The Open Boat** (Stephen Crane, 1897): "None of them knew the colour of the sky." The men look only at the waves; nature is indifferent.
- **To Build a Fire** (Jack London, 1908): the cold and grey that allow no error, and a man who misreads them.
- **The Garden Party** (Katherine Mansfield, 1922): "And after all the weather was ideal." A privileged garden against the cottages below sets up a conflict between comfort and compassion.

## Assumptions

- Evidence for setting includes details, repetition, contrast and the characters' reactions.
- A setting may convey different values to different characters.
- "Setting as symbol" is a further step that needs evidence (Topic 7.5).

## Mistakes to avoid

- Describing the setting and stopping.
- Ignoring what a character does within the setting.
- Missing a shift because the place has changed only briefly.
- Treating contrast as simple opposites without naming the values.
- Calling every setting symbolic.

## Sentence frame

"By [verb] [character] in [setting detail], the narrator [verb] a conflict between [value A] and [value B], and so [function]." Fill it with specific details and check that it says what the setting conveys, not only where it is.

## Quick self-check

**1.** A story's first scene is a cluttered kitchen full of voices; its last is an empty field at dawn. What shift might this mark?

**2.** A character in a rented room keeps the curtains drawn all day and talks of "the street" as "that noise". What does the behaviour reveal?

**3.** Name the first two steps of analysing a setting.

**4.** True or false: a setting that appears in only one paragraph cannot matter.

**Answers.**

1. A shift from social demands to solitude and reflection (or from obligation to freedom or exposure); the mood and the character's state change with the place.
2. An attitude of withdrawal or fear of the outside world; the room as refuge or confinement.
3. Notice the details (sensory, social, temporal) and ask what they suggest or value.
4. False. A brief setting can mark a turning point or contrast with a longer one.
