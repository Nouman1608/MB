## Recap

- **Plot** is the sequence of events as the text presents them, which may differ from the order in which they happen in the story world.
- The order in which a text reveals information is a **choice** that shapes what the reader knows, expects or feels at each point.
- Common ordering devices: chronological order, **flashback**, **delay** of a key fact, repetition, a time jump, an ending that returns to or reverses the opening.
- A **contrast** emphasises the particular traits or characteristics of the things compared.
- Contrasts often represent **conflicts in values** related to the perspectives of characters, narrators or speakers.
- You do not need to label archetypes or plot-diagram parts; describe order and effect.

## Key relationships

| Step | Question | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Sequence | In what order does the text give events? | missing key, flashback, discovery, kettle |
| Shift | Where does the order turn or information get held back? | the flashback delays who took the key |
| Contrast | What is set against what, and which trait or value does each carry? | shut door against filled kettle: guardedness against care |
| Effect | What does the order do for the reader? | suspicion gives way to tenderness |

| Pattern | Typical effect |
|---|---|
| Open with an outcome, then look back | Curiosity; explanation as release |
| Delay a fact | Suspense or irony |
| Repeat a phrase or event | Obsession, emphasis, rising pressure |
| Calm surface against inner panic | Self-deception, tension |

## Assumptions

- You read plot order as a **writer's choice** and ask what each choice does.
- A contrast is meaningful only when you can say which trait or value each side stands for.
- Evidence is quoted accurately and kept short.

## Mistakes to avoid

- Summarising events with no comment on order.
- Treating flashbacks as interruptions rather than choices.
- Naming a contrast without naming the values on each side.
- Labelling plot parts to look technical.
- Overlooking contrasts between what a character says and what the evidence shows.

## Quick self-check

1. A story opens with a smashed window, then goes back to the argument that caused it. What does this order do?
2. Poe's narrator says he was "never kinder to the old man" than in the week before he killed him. What contrast does this create?
3. Why is a time-jump not automatically a flashback?
4. Which is stronger: "There is a contrast between day and night" or "The contrast between the calm day and the frantic night shows how the narrator's control breaks down"?

**Answers.** (1) It begins with an effect and delays the cause, creating curiosity and making the explanation feel like a reveal. (2) Outward kindness against a hidden murderous plan; it emphasises deceit and self-deception. (3) A time-jump may skip forward in time; a flashback returns to an earlier time. (4) The second: it names the traits and the effect.
