These are **original Marlbridge practice questions**, not past exam questions. Passages are short extracts from public-domain works (author, title and date given) or an original prose passage written by Marlbridge, labelled as such. Mark points are a suggested Marlbridge rubric, not an official scoring guideline. Questions 1 to 6 are multiple choice; Questions 7 and 8 are written responses. Allow about 50 minutes. No calculator is needed.

## Question 1 (multiple choice · foundation)

Which of the following describes a **change in a character** rather than only an event in the plot?

- (A) A character is robbed on the road.
- (B) A character moves from one town to another.
- (C) A character who once distrusted all charity begins, after being helped by a stranger, to give her own money to others without being asked.
- (D) A character receives a letter.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (C).** A change is a difference in values or conduct that can be traced before and after a pressure: distrust gives way to generosity.

- (A), (B) and (D) are events or circumstances. They may cause change, but by themselves they show no difference in the character's values or conduct.

</details>

## Question 2 (multiple choice · core)

In Shakespeare's *Macbeth* (about 1606), Lady Macbeth says after the murder of Duncan, "A little water clears us of this deed", and in Act 5 she says in her sleep, "What, will these hands ne'er be clean?" What does the contrast reveal?

- (A) That she has become a better person since Act 2.
- (B) That she has forgotten what happened.
- (C) That Macbeth has persuaded her to confess.
- (D) That her earlier confidence that guilt could be washed away was mistaken, and her conscience has reasserted itself.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (D).** The early line treats guilt as a stain that "a little water" removes; the late line shows that the stain cannot be removed. The reversal reveals that her conscience, which she tried to suppress, is still active.

- (A) is wrong because her sleepwalking is torment, not moral improvement.
- (B) is contradicted by her re-living of the killings in sleep.
- (C) is unsupported; she does not confess to Macbeth in this scene.

</details>

## Question 3 (multiple choice · core)

In Jane Austen's *Pride and Prejudice* (1813), Mr Collins is described by the narrator as a man who "was not a sensible man", and his speech and behaviour remain consistent through the novel. Which statement best explains why he remains unchanged?

- (A) Austen forgot to give him a development.
- (B) As a minor character, he serves the plot and acts as a fixed point against which other characters' choices (Elizabeth's refusal, Charlotte's acceptance) are measured.
- (C) He has no values.
- (D) Characters in comedies never change.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (B).** Minor characters often stay unchanged because the narrative does not focus on them; they may advance the plot or highlight others. Collins's fixed pomposity makes both Elizabeth's refusal and Charlotte's acceptance meaningful.

- (A) assumes an accident where the evidence suggests a purpose.
- (C) is an overstatement; Collins has values (deference, rank), but they never alter.
- (D) is false; characters in comedy can change, and in this novel two of them do.

</details>

## Question 4 (multiple choice · core)

Read this passage, written by Marlbridge.

> Edmund Hale had kept the village school for thirty years, and in thirty years he had never once raised his voice. He believed that a quiet room taught itself. When the inspector came in March, Hale stood at the back with his hands folded and let the lesson run, though the girl at the front stumbled over her sums and the inspector's pencil scratched. "You might have helped her," said the inspector afterwards. "She will learn it," said Hale.
>
> In October the same girl, Bess, came to the schoolhouse door at dusk with her sums unfinished and her mother's rent unpaid. Hale put down his tea. He sat beside her on the step and worked each line aloud, correcting, explaining, until the lamp in the lane went on. "Again," he said, and heard in his own voice a sharpness he had never allowed it. He did not apologise for it. He had begun, he supposed, to be afraid of what a quiet room might cost.

Which statement best describes the change in Hale?

- (A) He moves from a belief that non-interference teaches best to a recognition that the cost of children's difficulties requires active help, even at the price of his own calm.
- (B) He becomes angry and cruel.
- (C) He does not change, because he continues to teach.
- (D) He loses his job.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (A).** The baseline is his creed ("a quiet room taught itself") and his refusal to help the girl. The pressure is Bess's hardship ("her mother's rent unpaid"). The response is active, sharp-voiced help, and the last sentence names the fear that shows his values have shifted.

- (B) misreads the "sharpness"; he is intent, not cruel.
- (C) ignores that his method and his belief have both changed.
- (D) has no evidence in the passage.

</details>

## Question 5 (multiple choice · core)

After reading Darcy's letter, Elizabeth says in *Pride and Prejudice*, "Till this moment, I never knew myself." What is the significance of the statement for her characterisation?

- (A) It shows that she has become a different person with no relation to her earlier self.
- (B) It is only a description of a headache.
- (C) It marks the recognition that her pride in her own discernment was itself a prejudice, a change in self-understanding that alters how she judges others.
- (D) It shows that she dislikes Darcy more.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (C).** "Never knew myself" is a self-recognition: the character who prided herself on seeing clearly sees that she misjudged. The sentence is the novel's clearest statement of her change.

- (A) overstates; she remains herself, with a new awareness.
- (B) is nonsense.
- (D) reverses the direction: the scene begins her revision of her opinion of Darcy.

</details>

## Question 6 (multiple choice · stretch)

Which statement best explains how Lady Macbeth's collapse and Macbeth's line "I have almost forgot the taste of fears" **together** affect the interpretation of *Macbeth*?

- (A) They show that the Macbeths are equally guilty and equally happy.
- (B) They show the pair exchanging positions: she, who began fearless, is overcome by conscience, while he, who began hesitant, hardens, so the play shows crime changing people in different and opposite ways.
- (C) They show that women are weaker than men.
- (D) They prove that Macbeth does not love his wife.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (B).** Reading the two changes together shows a pattern: the play sets two responses to guilt against each other, which supports a broader interpretation of crime's corrosive effect.

- (A) is contradicted by Lady Macbeth's torment.
- (C) imports a general claim the evidence does not support and ignores that Macbeth is also destroyed.
- (D) is not supported; his reaction to her death ("She should have died hereafter") is more complex.

</details>

## Question 7 (written response · core)

Write a paragraph of about 150 words explaining how Lady Macbeth's change (from "A little water clears us of this deed" to "What, will these hands ne'er be clean?") affects an interpretation of *Macbeth* as a whole. Include a baseline, a pressure, a response and a link to the work's meaning.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Sample response (written by Marlbridge).** Lady Macbeth's change turns the play from a story of ambition into an argument about conscience. Her baseline is the invocation "unsex me here", a wish to remove remorse, and her confidence after Duncan's murder: "A little water clears us of this deed". The pressure comes as the killings multiply and Macbeth withdraws from her; by Act 3 she admits "Naught's had, all's spent". In Act 5 her response is to wash invisible blood: "What, will these hands ne'er be clean?" The reversal shows that the guilt she treated as a stain that could be removed is permanent. If she had remained untouched, the play would imply that will can overcome conscience; because she breaks, the work argues that guilt survives even the strongest resolve, a conclusion strengthened by Macbeth's own hardening.

**Suggested Marlbridge rubric (4 points)**

| Point | What earns it |
|---|---|
| 1 | Identifies a baseline with specific evidence. |
| 2 | Identifies a pressure and a response, with a clear before and after. |
| 3 | Uses accurate quotation. |
| 4 | Links the change to the interpretation of the whole play (not only the character). |

</details>

## Question 8 (written response · stretch)

Using the passage about Edmund Hale (Question 4), write a paragraph of about 150 words explaining how details in the passage reveal Hale's change and what the change suggests about his values.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Sample response (written by Marlbridge).** The passage establishes Hale's baseline with details of stillness: he has "never once raised his voice", stands "with his hands folded" and trusts that "a quiet room taught itself". His reply to the inspector, "She will learn it", shows a value of non-interference. The second paragraph tests that creed with a hardship he cannot ignore, Bess at the door with "her mother's rent unpaid". His response is physical and immediate: he "put down his tea" and "sat beside her on the step", and the new sharpness of "Again" is a quality "he had never allowed". The final sentence names the cause, a fear of "what a quiet room might cost". Hale's calm was a belief that he could afford; the change shows that his values now include responsibility for what his restraint costs others.

**Suggested Marlbridge rubric (4 points)**

| Point | What earns it |
|---|---|
| 1 | A defensible claim about the change (from non-interference to responsibility). |
| 2 | Identifies baseline details and response details with accurate quotation. |
| 3 | Explains what the details reveal about Hale's values. |
| 4 | Explains the pressure and links the change to the passage's meaning. |

</details>
