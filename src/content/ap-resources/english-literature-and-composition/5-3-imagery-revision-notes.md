Short on time? This page is the recap. For explanations and worked examples, use the [full study guide](/advanced-course-resources/english-literature-and-composition/5-3-imagery-study-guide/).

## Recap

- An **image** represents something through associations with the senses: sight, sound, touch, taste, smell, and also movement and temperature.
- An image can be **literal** or it can be a **form of comparison**.
- **Descriptive words**, such as adjectives and adverbs, contribute to sensory imagery.
- A **collection of images**, known as **imagery**, may emphasise ideas in parts of a text or throughout it.
- Explain an image in four moves: image, sense, association, effect. Add a fifth, **pattern**, when you have several images.
- Describe the pattern: what the images share, and how they change from first to last.

## Key relationships

| Question | Why it matters | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Which sense? | Shows how the poem reaches the reader | *crawls* (movement), *azure* (sight) |
| Literal or comparison? | Decides whether to explain a likeness | "wrinkled sea" is a likeness to skin |
| What association? | Gives the image its meaning | *crooked hands* suggests age and grip |
| What effect? | Links the image to the poem's idea | power seen from above, then released |
| What pattern? | Moves from a list to an interpretation | stillness to sudden speed |

| Pattern | Typical reading |
|---|---|
| Images shrink in scale | Intimacy, fading, humility |
| Images shift from sight to sound | A turn from observing to listening |
| Images repeat a quality (cold) | A dominant mood or idea |
| Images move from fixed to moving | Held power released |

## Assumptions

- You **quote** the image and explain it; "nature imagery" is not an explanation.
- You name the **sense or senses** the image uses.
- You treat a collection of images as a pattern with a direction.

## Mistakes to avoid

- Saying "imagery" when you mean description.
- Naming the sense and stopping.
- Treating every image as a comparison.
- Quoting many images and explaining none.
- Forgetting that adjectives and verbs build the sensation.

## Quick self-check

1. Which senses does "a wailful choir the small gnats mourn" appeal to?
2. What does "wrinkled" add to "sea"?
3. How is a literal image different from a comparative one?
4. What do you gain by asking, "The images move from... to..."?

**Answers.** (1) Hearing, with an association of sorrow and music. (2) Touch and age: the sea looks like old skin, slow and small from above. (3) A literal image gives the sensation itself; a comparative image gives it by likening it to something else. (4) It turns a list into a pattern, which is the basis of an interpretation.

## Sentence frames to adapt

- "The image of '[quotation]' appeals to [sense] and associates [subject] with [quality]."
- "The adjective '[word]' adds [touch / sound / sight] and suggests [idea]."
- "The images move from [x] to [y], so the poem [shifts / builds] toward [idea]."
- "Because the images share [quality], the collection emphasises [idea]."

If you can fill the first frame for every image you mention, you will have covered image, sense, association and effect. Use the last two frames when you have several images.
