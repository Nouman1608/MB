## What this diagnostic is for

Use this page before or right after revising Unit 2 to find which topics to revisit. There is one question per topic, and two for the largest (cell structure and tonicity).

These are **original Marlbridge practice questions**, not past exam questions. They are not calibrated, so your number right does **not** predict an exam score.

**Calculator and data.** A calculator is allowed on both exam sections; a scientific one is enough. For solute potential use Ψs = −iCRT with R = 0.0831 L bar mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ and T in kelvin (°C + 273). All data, drugs and cells are fictional.

**How to work.** Answer everything first, writing a reason for each choice.

## Question 1 (multiple choice · 2.1)

The Golgi complex normally adds sugar chains to a membrane protein. In a mutant cell line it cannot. The protein is still made and folded, but most of it ends up in the wrong place. Which is the best explanation?

- (A) The sugar chains help label the protein so it is sorted to the right destination.
- (B) Without the sugar chains, ribosomes cannot make the protein.
- (C) The sugar chains supply the energy that moves the vesicle.
- (D) The rough ER now sends the protein straight to lysosomes.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (A).** The Golgi chemically modifies new products, for example by glycosylation, and these changes can decide where a protein goes.

- (B) is ruled out: the protein is still made.
- (C) confuses a label with a fuel.
- (D) invents a route that skips the Golgi.

**If you missed this:** read "Golgi complex: finishing and dispatch" in the [cell structure and function study guide](/advanced-course-resources/biology/2-1-cell-structure-function-study-guide/).
</details>

## Question 2 (multiple choice · 2.1)

Which statement about mitochondria and chloroplasts in a flowering plant is correct?

- (A) Plant cells have chloroplasts instead of mitochondria.
- (B) Leaf cells contain both; cells deep inside a root typically have mitochondria but no chloroplasts.
- (C) Both organelles have a single membrane.
- (D) Chloroplasts make all of a leaf cell's ATP, so leaf cells need no mitochondria.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (B).** Plant cells respire aerobically all the time, so they need mitochondria. Chloroplasts are found mainly in cells that receive light.

- (A) and (D) forget that making sugar does not remove the need to break it down for ATP.
- (C) is wrong: both have a **double** membrane.

**If you missed this:** read "Mitochondria and chloroplasts: two membranes, two energy roles" in the [cell structure and function study guide](/advanced-course-resources/biology/2-1-cell-structure-function-study-guide/).
</details>

## Question 3 (multiple choice · 2.2)

A cell is modelled as a cube with sides of 10 µm. Microvilli on its top face double the area of that face. Ignoring their small volume, what is the new surface area-to-volume ratio?

- (A) 0.60 µm⁻¹
- (B) 0.70 µm⁻¹
- (C) 0.80 µm⁻¹
- (D) 1.20 µm⁻¹

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (B).** SA = 6 × 10² = 600 µm²; V = 10³ = 1000 µm³. Doubling the 100 µm² top face adds 100 µm², so SA/V = 700 ÷ 1000 = 0.70 µm⁻¹.

- (A) is the ratio before the microvilli.
- (C) adds 200 µm², the face's whole new area, not the extra 100 µm².
- (D) doubles all six faces.

**If you missed this:** read "Ways to keep SA/V high" in the [cell size study guide](/advanced-course-resources/biology/2-2-cell-size-study-guide/).
</details>

## Question 4 (multiple choice · 2.3)

A transmembrane protein forms a water-filled pore that lets ions cross. Which description of its R groups is most likely?

- (A) Nonpolar R groups line the pore; charged R groups face the fatty acid tails.
- (B) Charged R groups line the pore and face the tails.
- (C) Nonpolar R groups line the pore and face the tails.
- (D) Polar and charged R groups line the pore; nonpolar R groups face the fatty acid tails.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (D).** Hydrophilic regions sit inside the protein (the pore) or in the fluid on each side; hydrophobic regions touch the tails.

- (A) swaps the regions.
- (B) puts charges among the nonpolar tails, which is unstable.
- (C) would anchor the protein but block hydrated ions.

**If you missed this:** read "Membrane proteins: R groups decide where each part sits" in the [plasma membrane study guide](/advanced-course-resources/biology/2-3-plasma-membrane-study-guide/).
</details>

## Question 5 (multiple choice · 2.4)

Vesicles of pure phospholipid are placed in a solution of ammonia (NH₃: small, polar, uncharged) and ammonium ions (NH₄⁺). Which prediction is best?

- (A) Both cross freely, because both are smaller than glucose.
- (B) Neither crosses, because both contain nitrogen.
- (C) NH₃ crosses in small amounts; NH₄⁺ hardly crosses.
- (D) NH₄⁺ crosses faster, because the phosphate heads attract it.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (C).** Small polar, uncharged molecules pass through a bilayer in small amounts. An ion, held in its shell of water, cannot enter the nonpolar layer of tails.

- (A) ignores charge; (B) invents a rule about elements.
- (D) forgets that crossing means getting through the tails.

**If you missed this:** read "Why the hydrophobic interior decides" in the [membrane permeability study guide](/advanced-course-resources/biology/2-4-membrane-permeability-study-guide/).
</details>

## Question 6 (multiple choice · 2.5)

A white blood cell takes in a whole bacterium. Which description is correct?

- (A) Exocytosis: vesicles fuse with the membrane, with no energy needed
- (B) Facilitated diffusion: it passes through a large channel protein
- (C) Passive transport: it moves down its own concentration gradient
- (D) Endocytosis: the membrane encloses it in a new vesicle, using energy

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (D).** A whole cell is far too large for any membrane protein, so the membrane encloses it in a vesicle, using energy.

- (A) names the reverse process, which moves material out.
- (B) and (C) describe real routes for small solutes, not for particles.

**If you missed this:** read "Bulk transport: endocytosis and exocytosis" in the [membrane transport study guide](/advanced-course-resources/biology/2-5-membrane-transport-study-guide/).
</details>

## Question 7 (multiple choice · 2.6)

Glucose enters a cell only through carrier proteins. Outside it is at 5 mmol L⁻¹, inside at 1 mmol L⁻¹. A poison suddenly stops ATP production. What happens to glucose entry in the next minute?

- (A) It stops, because carriers need ATP to change shape.
- (B) It reverses.
- (C) It continues, because glucose still moves down its concentration gradient.
- (D) It speeds up, because the carriers no longer spend ATP.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (C).** Facilitated diffusion is passive: the carrier provides a path, the gradient provides the drive.

- (A) and (D) confuse these carriers with ATP-using pumps.
- (B) would need the inside to be more concentrated.

**If you missed this:** read "Large polar molecules: carriers and saturation" in the [facilitated diffusion study guide](/advanced-course-resources/biology/2-6-facilitated-diffusion-study-guide/).
</details>

## Question 8 (multiple choice · 2.7)

All four solutions are at 25 °C in open beakers. Which has the **lowest** water potential?

- (A) 0.40 M sucrose
- (B) 0.25 M NaCl
- (C) 0.15 M CaCl₂
- (D) 0.30 M glucose

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (B).** R and T are the same, so compare i × C: sucrose 0.40, NaCl 2 × 0.25 = 0.50, CaCl₂ 3 × 0.15 = 0.45, glucose 0.30. NaCl gives the most particles: Ψ = −2 × 0.25 × 0.0831 × 298 = −12.4 bar.

- (A) has the highest molarity, but sucrose does not ionize (i = 1).
- (C) has the largest i, but 0.45 is less than 0.50.
- (D) has the **highest** water potential (−7.43 bar).

**If you missed this:** read "Calculating solute potential" in the [tonicity and osmoregulation study guide](/advanced-course-resources/biology/2-7-tonicity-osmoregulation-study-guide/).
</details>

## Question 9 (multiple choice · 2.8)

How does the sodium–potassium pump **directly** help make the inside of a resting cell negative?

- (A) Each cycle moves 3 Na⁺ out and 2 K⁺ in, so one more positive charge leaves than enters.
- (B) Each cycle moves 2 Na⁺ out and 3 K⁺ in.
- (C) It pumps Cl⁻ ions into the cell.
- (D) It has no direct effect, because it swaps ions one for one.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (A).** Per ATP, 3 Na⁺ leave and 2 K⁺ enter: a net loss of one positive charge. The pump also keeps up the gradients the membrane potential depends on.

- (B) reverses the numbers, which would make the inside more positive.
- (C) names the wrong ion.
- (D) forgets that the swap is unequal.

**If you missed this:** read "The sodium–potassium pump" in the [mechanisms of transport study guide](/advanced-course-resources/biology/2-8-mechanisms-transport-study-guide/).
</details>

## Question 10 (multiple choice · 2.9)

A eukaryotic cell builds compound F in one membrane-bound compartment and breaks F down in another. Which is the best explanation?

- (A) Mixing all the enzymes together makes F faster.
- (B) Separating the opposing reactions stops F being broken down as soon as it is made.
- (C) The cell needs two copies of one enzyme.
- (D) Every reaction needs a membrane to supply ATP.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (B).** Internal membranes reduce competing interactions; otherwise energy spent making F would be wasted.

- (A) is the opposite of what compartments do.
- (C) misreads the question: building and breaking need different enzymes.
- (D) overgeneralizes from the membranes that do make ATP.

**If you missed this:** read "Idea 1: compartments keep competing reactions apart" in the [cell compartmentalization study guide](/advanced-course-resources/biology/2-9-cell-compartmentalization-study-guide/).
</details>

## Question 11 (short answer · 2.10)

(a) A prokaryote has no membrane-bound organelles. Name **two** regions or structures in it that have specialized jobs, with each job.
(b) Give **two** observations that support the claim that mitochondria evolved from once free-living prokaryotes, and explain each.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**(a)** Any two: the **nucleoid**, holding the main DNA without a membrane; **ribosomes**, which make proteins; the **plasma membrane**, which carries the proteins of aerobic respiration; **thylakoid membranes** in cyanobacteria, where light is captured.

**(b)** Any two, each with its reason: **two membranes** (a prokaryote enclosed by a host membrane); **own circular DNA** (like a bacterial chromosome); **bacteria-like ribosomes** (leftover machinery of the ancestor); **division by splitting** (a cell cannot build one from scratch); **DNA sequences closest to one bacterial group** (shared ancestry).

**If you missed this:** read "Inside a prokaryotic cell" and "The evidence" in the [origins of cell compartmentalization study guide](/advanced-course-resources/biology/2-10-origins-cell-compartmentalization-study-guide/).
</details>

## Question 12 (short answer · 2.7)

A fictional animal cell at 37 °C has cytoplasm with the same solute potential as 0.15 M NaCl, and zero pressure potential. It is placed in solution X (0.30 M sucrose) or solution Y (0.10 M NaCl). Neither solute crosses the membrane.

(a) Calculate the water potential of the cell, X and Y.
(b) Describe X and Y as hypotonic, isotonic or hypertonic to the cell, and predict what happens in each.
(c) Explain why a plant cell placed in Y does not burst.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**(a)** T = 310 K. Cell: −2 × 0.15 × 0.0831 × 310 = **−7.73 bar**. X: −1 × 0.30 × 0.0831 × 310 = **−7.73 bar**. Y: −2 × 0.10 × 0.0831 × 310 = **−5.15 bar**.

**(b)** **X is isotonic** (i × C = 0.30 for both): no net water movement. **Y is hypotonic** (higher Ψ): water moves into the cell by osmosis, so it swells and may burst.

**(c)** The cell presses on its **wall**, which resists. Pressure potential rises until the cell's Ψ reaches −5.15 bar, and net entry stops: the cell is turgid.

**Watch for:** i = 1 for NaCl gives −3.86 bar for the cell and makes X look hypertonic.

**If you missed this:** read "Water potential: one number that predicts the direction" in the [tonicity and osmoregulation study guide](/advanced-course-resources/biology/2-7-tonicity-osmoregulation-study-guide/).
</details>
