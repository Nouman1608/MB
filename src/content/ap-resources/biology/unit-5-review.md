## Before you start

This review joins the five topics of Unit 5. Do the [unit diagnostic](/advanced-course-resources/biology/unit-5-diagnostic/) first, then try these without looking at the answers.

## Big ideas of the unit

- **One copy, two divisions.** DNA is copied once and the cell divides twice, giving four haploid cells. Meiosis I separates homologues; meiosis II separates sister chromatids ([Topic 5.1](/advanced-course-resources/biology/5-1-meiosis-study-guide/)).
- **Every gamete is a new mixture.** Independent assortment, crossing over and random fertilization make offspring unique. Nondisjunction gives gametes that are not haploid ([Topic 5.2](/advanced-course-resources/biology/5-2-meiosis-genetic-diversity-study-guide/)).
- **Mendel's laws are meiosis seen through alleles.** Homologues separating in anaphase I is segregation; pairs lining up independently is independent assortment ([Topic 5.3](/advanced-course-resources/biology/5-3-mendelian-genetics-study-guide/)).
- **Probability replaces big Punnett squares.** Split a cross into one-gene crosses, multiply for "and", add for "or".
- **Chi-square decides whether a difference is real,** not what caused it ([Topic 5.4](/advanced-course-resources/biology/5-4-non-mendelian-genetics-study-guide/)).
- **Where a gene sits changes the pattern.** Genes on one chromosome travel together unless a crossover separates them; genes on the X show different results in sons and daughters; genes in mitochondria and chloroplasts follow the mother ([Topic 5.4](/advanced-course-resources/biology/5-4-non-mendelian-genetics-study-guide/)).
- **How alleles act changes the ratio.** Incomplete dominance and codominance give the heterozygote its own phenotype, so 1 : 2 : 1 replaces 3 : 1. One gene with several effects (pleiotropy) passes those traits on together.
- **Phenotype = genotype acting in an environment.** Temperature, light, diet or a signal can change gene expression or how well a gene product works ([Topic 5.5](/advanced-course-resources/biology/5-5-environmental-effects-on-phenotype-study-guide/)). Hold genotype constant to see the environment's effect.

## Key relationships and methods

| Idea | Rule or method | Where it appears |
|---|---|---|
| Counting through meiosis | chromosomes = centromeres; DNA ∝ chromatids; G1 value × 2 after S, ÷ 2 after each division | 5.1 cell and DNA questions |
| Chromosome combinations | 2ⁿ per gamete (n = pairs); 2ⁿ × 2ⁿ per offspring of one couple | 5.2 |
| Nondisjunction | meiosis I: all four gametes abnormal; meiosis II: two of four | 5.2, aneuploid offspring |
| Probability | independent: P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B); mutually exclusive: P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) | 5.3 crosses and families |
| Chi-square | expected counts from the ratio; χ² = Σ (o − e)² ÷ e; df = categories − 1 | 5.3, 5.4 |
| Gene mapping | recombinants ÷ total × 100 = map units; at most about 50% | 5.4 linked genes |
| Sex linkage | sons get their X from the mother; fathers give X to every daughter | 5.4 crosses and pedigrees |
| Genotype vs environment | same genotype in two environments (clones, controlled cross) → any difference is environmental | 5.5 |

## Question 1 (multiple choice · mixed)

A plant is heterozygous (Aa) for one gene. There is no crossing over between gene A and the centromere. At which stage of meiosis are allele A and allele a first pulled to opposite poles of the cell?

- (A) Prophase I
- (B) Anaphase I
- (C) Anaphase II
- (D) S phase, before meiosis begins

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (B).** A and a sit on the two homologues of one pair. With no crossover near the gene, both chromatids of one homologue carry A and both of the other carry a. Homologues separate in anaphase I: that is Mendel's segregation.

- (A) is when homologues pair up and may cross over; they are still together.
- (C) separates sister chromatids, which carry the **same** allele here (unless a crossover had swapped them).
- (D) copies each allele; it does not separate A from a.
</details>

## Question 2 (multiple choice · mixed)

In a hypothetical flower, colour alleles Cᴿ (red) and Cᵂ (white) show incomplete dominance: CᴿCᵂ plants are pink. An unlinked gene gives tall (T), dominant to dwarf (t). What fraction of the offspring of CᴿCᵂTt × CᴿCᵂTt are pink and dwarf?

- (A) 1/16
- (B) 1/8
- (C) 3/16
- (D) 3/8

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (B).** Colour: CᴿCᵂ × CᴿCᵂ gives 1/4 red, 1/2 pink, 1/4 white. Height: Tt × Tt gives 1/4 dwarf. The genes are unlinked, so multiply: 1/2 × 1/4 = 1/8.

- (A) treats pink as 1/4, as if it were a homozygous class.
- (C) uses 3/4 for pink, as if the colour alleles showed complete dominance.
- (D) is pink and **tall** (1/2 × 3/4).
</details>

## Question 3 (multiple choice · mixed)

In a hypothetical mammal, XXY individuals develop as males. An X-linked recessive allele Xᵈ gives pale eyes. A dark-eyed carrier female (XᴰXᵈ) and a dark-eyed male (XᴰY) have a pale-eyed son with the chromosomes XᵈXᵈY. Assume no crossing over between the gene and the centromere. Which explanation fits?

- (A) Nondisjunction of the X and Y in the father at meiosis I
- (B) Nondisjunction of sister chromatids in the mother at meiosis II
- (C) Nondisjunction of the two X chromosomes in the mother at meiosis I
- (D) Crossing over between the X and the Y in the father

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (B).** The son has no Xᴰ, so the father gave only his Y. Both Xs came from the mother and both carry Xᵈ. Her two homologues differ, so the two Xᵈ copies must be sister chromatids that failed to separate in meiosis II.

- (A) would give a sperm with Xᴰ and Y, and the son would have dark eyes.
- (C) would put both her homologues, XᴰXᵈ, in the egg, so the son would have dark eyes.
- (D) does not change the number of chromosomes, and the father has no Xᵈ to pass on.
</details>

## Question 4 (constructed response · mixed)

A fictional insect has 2n = 4. Gene D is on chromosome pair 1 and gene E is on chromosome pair 2. One insect has genotype DdEe, and a G1 cell in its ovary contains 2.4 pg of DNA.

(a) Give the number of chromosomes and the DNA content per cell at metaphase I, at the end of meiosis I and at the end of meiosis II.
(b) List the gametes this insect can make, give the probability of each, and explain which event in meiosis makes all of them equally likely.
(c) Two DdEe insects are crossed. Calculate the probability of an offspring with the dominant D phenotype and the recessive e phenotype.
(d) In a related species, D and E are 8 map units apart on the same chromosome, and an insect inherited D with E from one parent and d with e from the other. Predict the percentage of each kind of gamete, and name the event that makes the rarer kinds.

<details>
<summary>Worked solution and suggested Marlbridge rubric</summary>

**(a)**

| Stage (per cell) | Chromosomes | Chromatids | DNA / pg |
|---|---|---|---|
| Metaphase I | 4 | 8 | 4.8 |
| End of meiosis I | 2 | 4 | 2.4 |
| End of meiosis II | 2 | 2 | 1.2 |

**(b)** **DE, De, dE and de**, each 1/4. At metaphase I each pair can face either way, independently of the other pair, and both orientations are equally likely.

**(c)** Dd × Dd: P(dominant D) = 3/4. Ee × Ee: P(ee) = 1/4. Product rule: 3/4 × 1/4 = **3/16**.

**(d)** Recombinants are 8%, shared equally: **De 4%**, **dE 4%**. Parental types are 92%: **DE 46%**, **de 46%**. Recombinants come from **crossing over** between non-sister chromatids in prophase I, between D and E.

| Point | What earns it |
|---|---|
| 1 | All three rows of (a) correct, with DNA halving after each division |
| 1 | Four gametes at 1/4 each, explained by independent orientation of the pairs at metaphase I |
| 1 | 3/16 by the product rule |
| 1 | 46, 46, 4, 4% with the parental types as the large classes |
| 1 | Crossing over between non-sister chromatids in prophase I named as the source of recombinants |

Total: 5 points.
</details>

## Question 5 (constructed response · mixed)

In a hypothetical beetle (XX females, XY males), black body (B) is dominant to tan (b) on an autosome. An eye-colour gene has a dominant allele for dark eyes and a recessive allele for pale eyes. A black, pale-eyed female is crossed with a black, dark-eyed male. Both are heterozygous for body colour. The 400 offspring are:

| Offspring | Black, dark-eyed females | Tan, dark-eyed females | Black, pale-eyed males | Tan, pale-eyed males |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Number | 156 | 44 | 147 | 53 |

(a) Explain how the data show that the eye-colour gene is X-linked, and give the parents' genotypes using Xᴰ and Xᵈ.
(b) Use your genotypes and the product rule to give the expected number in each class.
(c) Calculate χ², and state your conclusion.
(d) Predict the eye colour of the sons and daughters if a pale-eyed male is crossed with a dark-eyed female from a true-breeding line.

<details>
<summary>Worked solution and suggested Marlbridge rubric</summary>

**(a)** Every daughter is dark-eyed and every son pale-eyed; an autosomal gene would not differ by sex. Sons get their X from the mother, so she is **XᵈXᵈ**. Daughters get the father's X, so he is **XᴰY**. With body colour: **BbXᵈXᵈ** and **BbXᴰY**.

**(b)** P(female) = 1/2, all dark-eyed; P(male) = 1/2, all pale-eyed. P(black) = 3/4. So black dark females 1/2 × 3/4 = 3/8 → **150**; tan dark females 1/8 → **50**; black pale males 3/8 → **150**; tan pale males 1/8 → **50**.

**(c)** χ² = 36/150 + 36/50 + 9/150 + 9/50 = 0.24 + 0.72 + 0.06 + 0.18 = **1.20**. Degrees of freedom = 3; critical value 7.81. Since 1.20 < 7.81, **fail to reject** the null hypothesis. The data are consistent with an autosomal body-colour gene and an X-linked eye-colour gene assorting independently.

**(d)** XᴰXᴰ × XᵈY: **all offspring dark-eyed**; daughters are XᴰXᵈ carriers. Reciprocal crosses differ, as expected for a sex-linked gene.

| Point | What earns it |
|---|---|
| 1 | Eye colour linked to sex of offspring, so the gene is on the X |
| 1 | Correct genotypes BbXᵈXᵈ and BbXᴰY |
| 1 | Expected 150, 50, 150, 50 |
| 1 | χ² = 1.20, df 3, fail to reject, with the critical value compared |
| 1 | All dark-eyed in (d), with daughters as carriers |

Total: 5 points.
</details>

## Question 6 (constructed response · mixed)

In a hypothetical plant, purple flowers (P) are dominant to white (p). P codes for a pigment-pathway enzyme; p codes for an inactive version. A grower crosses two Pp plants and sows 400 seeds in a greenhouse at 20 °C and 400 at 32 °C:

| Greenhouse | Purple | White |
|---|---|---|
| 20 °C | 289 | 111 |
| 32 °C | 0 | 400 |

(a) Test the 20 °C results against a 3 : 1 ratio with chi-square.
(b) Give the expected numbers of PP, Pp and pp plants in the 32 °C greenhouse, and suggest an explanation for the result there.
(c) White plants from the 32 °C greenhouse are moved to 20 °C. Predict the fraction that will grow purple flowers, and explain.
(d) Describe a test cross to find whether a plant is PP or Pp, and explain why its offspring must be grown at 20 °C.

<details>
<summary>Worked solution and suggested Marlbridge rubric</summary>

**(a)** Expected: 400 × 3/4 = 300 purple, 100 white. χ² = (289 − 300)² ÷ 300 + (111 − 100)² ÷ 100 = 0.403 + 1.210 = **1.61**. Degrees of freedom = 1; critical value 3.84. Since 1.61 < 3.84, **fail to reject**: the data fit one gene with P dominant.

**(b)** Same cross, so the same genotypes: **100 PP, 200 Pp, 100 pp**. Yet every plant is white. A likely explanation: the P enzyme is inactive at 32 °C (for example, it denatures), so no pigment is made whatever the genotype. The environment changed the phenotype, not the alleles.

**(c)** **3/4**. Moving plants does not change genotypes. PP and Pp plants make working enzyme at 20 °C, so their new flowers are purple; pp plants stay white.

**(d)** Cross the plant with pp and grow the offspring at **20 °C**. All purple → **PP**; about half white → **Pp**. At 32 °C every offspring would be white, so the test would show nothing.

| Point | What earns it |
|---|---|
| 1 | Expected 300 and 100, χ² = 1.61 |
| 1 | df 1, critical value 3.84, fail to reject |
| 1 | Genotypes 100 : 200 : 100 at 32 °C, with the genotype unchanged |
| 1 | Enzyme activity (or expression) depends on temperature, so no pigment at 32 °C |
| 1 | 3/4 in (c), because PP and Pp make working enzyme at 20 °C |
| 1 | Test cross with pp, expected results for PP and Pp, offspring grown at 20 °C |

Total: 6 points.
</details>

## Question 7 (constructed response · mixed)

In a fictional mammal, a muscle weakness is caused by a mitochondrial DNA mutation. Cells can hold both normal and faulty mitochondria; animals with more than 50% faulty mitochondria in muscle show weakness.

- **Family 1:** an affected father and an unaffected mother have 6 offspring (3 daughters, 3 sons). None has any faulty mitochondria.
- **Family 2:** a mother with 40% faulty mitochondria and an unaffected father have 8 offspring. Percentage of faulty mitochondria in each offspring's muscle: 15, 30, 42, 55, 70, 38, 22, 48.

(a) Calculate the mean and range for Family 2, and the percentage of the offspring that show weakness.
(b) Explain why the offspring in Family 2 differ from each other.
(c) Explain the result in Family 1.
(d) A student claims the weakness is caused by a dominant allele on the X chromosome. Use Family 1 to evaluate the claim.
(e) Explain why a Punnett square cannot predict the offspring in Family 2.

<details>
<summary>Worked solution and suggested Marlbridge rubric</summary>

**(a)** Mean = (15 + 30 + 42 + 55 + 70 + 38 + 22 + 48) ÷ 8 = 320 ÷ 8 = **40%**. Range **15% to 70%**. Two offspring (55% and 70%) are above 50%: 2 ÷ 8 × 100 = **25%** show weakness.

**(b)** Mitochondria are shared out **at random** when cells divide, including the divisions that make eggs. Different eggs get different proportions of faulty mitochondria: the mean matches the mother's 40%, but individuals vary.

**(c)** The egg supplies the zygote's mitochondria; sperm usually contribute none. So an affected father passes on no faulty mitochondria.

**(d)** **Not supported.** A father gives his X to **every** daughter, so with an X-linked dominant allele all 3 daughters in Family 1 would be affected. None is.

**(e)** Mendelian ratios need two alleles per gene, one from each parent, that segregate in meiosis. Mitochondrial genes come from the mother only, in many copies, and are not sorted by segregation, so there is no fixed ratio.

| Point | What earns it |
|---|---|
| 1 | Mean 40%, range 15–70% and 25% affected |
| 1 | Random sharing of mitochondria into eggs explains the variation |
| 1 | Mitochondria come from the egg, not the sperm |
| 1 | X-linked dominant rejected because the daughters of an affected father are unaffected |
| 1 | Mendel's rules need paired nuclear alleles that segregate; organelle genes do not |

Total: 5 points.
</details>
