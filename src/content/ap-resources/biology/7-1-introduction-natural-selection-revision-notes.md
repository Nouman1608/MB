## Recap

- **Evolution:** a change in the heritable characteristics of a population over generations. Populations evolve; individuals do not.
- **Natural selection** is a major mechanism of evolution and the one that produces adaptations. Random processes (Topic 7.4) also cause evolution.
- **Darwin's argument** (Darwin and Wallace, 1858; *On the Origin of Species*, 1859): more offspring are produced than can survive; resources are limited, so individuals compete; individuals vary; much variation is inherited; so individuals with favourable phenotypes survive and reproduce more, and their traits become more common.
- **Fitness** is measured by reproductive success: offspring that themselves survive to breed.
- **Biotic** (living) and **abiotic** (non-living) conditions fluctuate, so different variants can be favoured in different generations. This changes the rate and direction of evolution.

## Key relationships

| Quantity | How to find it | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Mean offspring per individual | total offspring of a phenotype ÷ number of individuals | or: fraction surviving × offspring per survivor |
| Relative fitness, w | mean offspring of phenotype ÷ mean offspring of best phenotype | best phenotype has w = 1; no units |
| Fraction of a phenotype next generation | its offspring ÷ total offspring | assumes the trait is inherited exactly |
| Percentage survival | survivors ÷ starting number × 100 | survival alone is not fitness |

| Missing ingredient | Why selection fails |
|---|---|
| No variation | nothing to choose between |
| Variation not heritable | survivors' offspring do not share the trait |
| No difference in reproduction | every variant leaves the same share of offspring |

## Assumptions

- Projections to the next generation assume the trait is fully heritable and ignore mutation, migration and chance.
- Survival data alone do not show fitness; you also need offspring numbers.
- Fictional data in the guide and practice are for illustration only.

## Mistakes to avoid

1. Saying an individual "evolved" or "adapted" by changing its genes.
2. Saying a trait appeared "because the organism needed it".
3. Equating fitness with strength, size or lifespan.
4. Forgetting that the favoured trait depends on the current environment.
5. Dividing by the wrong phenotype: relative fitness divides by the **most successful** one.
6. Describing a graph without naming its axes, groups and units.

## Quick self-check

1. Phenotype X averages 1.5 offspring; phenotype Y, the most successful, averages 2.5. Relative fitness of X? *(1.5 ÷ 2.5 = 0.60)*
2. Name two biotic and two abiotic factors that could change selection on a lizard's body colour. *(Biotic: predators, prey, parasites, mates. Abiotic: rock colour, temperature, sunlight.)*
3. Why can a sterile animal that lives for 20 years have a fitness of zero? *(Fitness counts offspring, and it leaves none.)*
