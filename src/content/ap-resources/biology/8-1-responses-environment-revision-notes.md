## Recap

- Organisms detect changes **outside** (light, night length, temperature, predators) and **inside** (hunger, water loss, hormones) and respond.
- **Behavioural** responses are actions (moving, calling, hiding). **Physiological** responses are internal changes (heart rate, flowering, defensive chemicals).
- **Taxis**: movement directed toward (+) or away from (−) a stimulus. **Kinesis**: speed or turning rate changes with stimulus strength; no direction.
- **Innate** behaviour is inherited and correct first time. **Learned** behaviour changes with experience (imprinting, song learning, habituation). Selection favours both when they raise fitness.
- Organisms exchange information through **visual, audible, tactile, electrical and chemical** signals: to warn, to show dominance, to find food, to hold territory and to attract mates.
- **Cooperation** (pack hunting, herds and flocks, insect colonies, helping relatives) tends to raise individual fitness and population survival.
- Specific mechanisms of behaviour and communication are **not** examined. Link stimulus, response and fitness.

## Key relationships

| Idea | Link to fitness |
|---|---|
| response matches a reliable cue (night length, season) | reproduction timed for best conditions |
| warning colour, alarm call | fewer attacks; others escape |
| song, display, scent mark | more mates or a better territory → more offspring |
| cooperative hunting or defence | more food and safer young per individual |
| kin selection | shared alleles passed on through relatives |

| Statistic | Use |
|---|---|
| **χ² = Σ (o − e)² ÷ e** | o = observed count, e = expected count under "no preference" |
| **df = categories − 1** | χ² above the critical value → reject the null hypothesis |

## Assumptions behind experiments

- Animals act independently (each is one count) and each is counted once.
- The control differs from the experiment **only** in the factor tested (for example, both halves dark to check for side bias).
- One trial **supports** a conclusion for those conditions; it does not prove it.

## Mistakes to avoid

1. Calling an end-point count "taxis" without path data.
2. Saying animals behave "for the good of the species".
3. Using number of animals − 1 as degrees of freedom.
4. Choosing a control that changes two things at once.
5. Writing that rejecting the null hypothesis "proves" the hypothesis.
6. Describing a mechanism instead of the fitness consequence.

## Quick self-check

1. A fictional soil insect moves more slowly and turns more often in damp air than in dry air, with no consistent direction. Taxis or kinesis? *(Kinesis: the change is in speed and turning, not direction.)*
2. 50 insects in a two-sided chamber: 33 on one side, 17 on the other. Reject "no preference"? *(Expected 25 each; χ² = 64/25 + 64/25 = 5.12; df = 1; 5.12 > 3.84, so reject.)*
3. Why can a risky alarm call be favoured? *(If listeners are relatives, the caller's shared alleles survive in them: kin selection.)*
