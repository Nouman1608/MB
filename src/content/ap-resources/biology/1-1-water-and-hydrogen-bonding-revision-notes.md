## Recap

- Oxygen is more electronegative than hydrogen, so the O–H bonds are **polar covalent**. The bent shape makes the whole molecule **polar** (δ⁻ on O, δ⁺ on each H) but not charged.
- A **hydrogen bond** is a weak attraction between a δ⁺ H on one molecule and a δ⁻ O on another. Each water molecule can make up to four. They constantly break and re-form.
- Hydrogen bonds also form within and between large biological molecules, such as DNA and proteins.
- The network of hydrogen bonds causes cohesion, adhesion, surface tension, high specific heat, high heat of vaporization, low density of ice and strong solvent action.

## Key relationships

| Mechanism | Property | Biological outcome |
|---|---|---|
| H bonds between water molecules | cohesion, surface tension | water column pulled up xylem during transpiration |
| H bonds to polar surfaces | adhesion | water clings to xylem walls |
| energy breaks H bonds before raising temperature | high specific heat | stable body and lake temperatures |
| escaping molecule must break all its H bonds | high heat of vaporization | evaporative cooling (sweat, transpiration) |
| H bonds fix an open lattice | ice less dense than water | ice floats and insulates water below |
| δ⁻/δ⁺ ends surround ions and polar molecules | good solvent | transport of ions and sugars in cells and blood |

| Equation | Symbols and units |
|---|---|
| **q = m × c × ΔT** | q in J, m in g, c in J g⁻¹ °C⁻¹ (water: 4.18), ΔT in °C |
| **ΔT = q ÷ (m × c)** | rearranged to compare temperature changes |

## Assumptions behind the numbers

- q = mcΔT assumes **no energy is lost** to the surroundings and no change of state during warming.
- Values of c apply to the stated phase (liquid water, not ice or steam).
- Small experiments (few trials) **support** or **do not support** a hypothesis; they do not prove it.

## Mistakes to avoid

1. Calling the O–H bond inside water a hydrogen bond.
2. Saying high specific heat means water heats *quickly*.
3. Saying cohesion alone lifts water; evaporation provides the pull.
4. Explaining floating ice with trapped air.
5. Using the final temperature instead of ΔT in q = mcΔT.
6. Treating a correlation as proof of cause without ruling out confounding variables.

## Quick self-check

1. Why is a water molecule polar but not an ion? *(Unequal sharing in O–H bonds and a bent shape give δ⁻ and δ⁺ ends, but the charges sum to zero.)*
2. Energy to warm 100 g of water by 5.0 °C? *(100 × 4.18 × 5.0 = 2090 J, about 2.1 kJ)*
3. Why does sweating cool the body? *(The most energetic molecules break their hydrogen bonds and escape, lowering the average energy of what remains.)*
