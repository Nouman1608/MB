## How to use this guide

This guide is for any Biology student preparing for the free-response section. The same experiment and data skills turn up in every unit, so it covers the whole course. Read the explanations, study the worked examples, then try **Practise it** before opening the model answers.

Every example here is **original Marlbridge practice**, not past exam material, and all organisms and data are fictional. The marks shown are a **suggested Marlbridge rubric**, not official scoring.

## The free-response section at a glance

The free-response section has 6 questions in 90 minutes and is worth 50% of the exam. You read the questions on screen and write your answers, including graphs, by hand in a paper booklet. A four-function, scientific or graphing calculator is allowed, and a formula sheet is provided.

| Question | Type | Points | What the parts ask for |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Interpreting and Evaluating Experimental Results | 9 | Describe a concept; identify methods or describe data; analyse data or calculate; make and justify predictions |
| 2 | Interpreting and Evaluating Experimental Results with Graphing | 9 | Describe a concept; construct a graph (4 points); analyse, calculate or state a null hypothesis; make and justify predictions |
| 3 | Scientific Investigation | 4 | Describe a concept; identify procedures; state a null hypothesis or predict; justify |
| 4 | Conceptual Analysis | 4 | Describe and explain a concept; predict the effect of a disruption; justify |
| 5 | Analyze Model or Visual Representation | 4 | Describe and explain a model; represent a relationship; link to a wider principle |
| 6 | Analyze Data | 4 | Describe data; use data to evaluate a hypothesis; link results to biology |

A sensible Marlbridge time plan: about 22 minutes for each long question and 11 for each short one.

## Variables, controls and hypotheses

Label the set-up before you answer.

| Term | Meaning | Quick test |
|---|---|---|
| Independent variable (IV) | The factor deliberately changed | "What differs between groups?" |
| Dependent variable (DV) | What is measured | "What number goes in the table?" |
| Controlled variables | Factors kept the same | Anything else that could change the DV |
| Control group | A baseline for comparison | Usually the untreated condition |
| Negative control | No effect expected | Shows the effect needs the factor tested (e.g. boiled enzyme) |
| Positive control | An effect is known to happen | Shows the method can detect an effect |

**Be specific.** Name the quantity **and** how it is measured: "volume of oxygen collected in 2 minutes (mL)", not "how well the enzyme works".

**Null and alternative hypotheses.** H₀ says the independent variable has **no effect** on the dependent variable. The alternative says there is an effect, often with a direction. Name both variables:

- H₀: The concentration of compound K has no effect on the percentage of root-tip cells in mitosis.
- Alternative: Increasing the concentration of compound K decreases the percentage of root-tip cells in mitosis.

**Predicting and justifying.** A prediction gives a direction ("will be lower than the control"). A justification adds the mechanism linking cause to effect. One sentence of each is usually enough.

## Graphing data

Question 2 always asks for a graph. Points come from the elements below; a title earns nothing but still helps the reader.

| Element | What a strong graph does |
|---|---|
| Type | Line or scatter graph when both variables are continuous (time, concentration). Bar graph for categories (treatments, species). Histogram for the spread of one variable |
| Axes | IV on x, DV on y, both with units. A legend when there is more than one series |
| Scale | Even intervals using at least half the grid; include negative values if the data have them |
| Plotting | Accurate points; error bars whenever the table gives SE or ± 2 SE |
| Trend line | Join points or draw a best-fit line. Do not extend it past the data or to the origin; use a dashed line for any prediction |

**Reading error bars.** Bars of mean ± 2 SE are roughly a 95% interval. If two bars **do not overlap**, reject H₀; if they **overlap**, fail to reject H₀. This is an approximation, weakest with few repeats.

## The calculations you must be able to do

The formula sheet gives most equations. Always show the formula, the substituted numbers and the answer with units.

| Calculation | How | Common trap |
|---|---|---|
| Rate | change in quantity ÷ change in time | Wrong time interval; no units |
| Percent change | (new − original) ÷ original × 100 | Dividing by the new value; losing the minus sign |
| Mean | sum of values ÷ number of values | Including an outlier without comment |
| Standard deviation (s) | spread around the mean, using n − 1 | Using n instead of n − 1 |
| Standard error (SE) | s ÷ √n | Forgetting the square root; confusing SE with s |
| Chi-square (χ²) | Σ (o − e)² ÷ e with counts, df = categories − 1 | Using percentages instead of counts; df = sample size − 1 |
| Hardy–Weinberg | p + q = 1; p² + 2pq + q² = 1; q² = recessive phenotype frequency | Forgetting the square root for q |
| Water potential | Ψ = Ψp + Ψs and Ψs = −iCRT (i = 1 for sucrose, T in kelvin) | Using °C; forgetting the minus sign |

For chi-square, compare χ² with the critical value at p = 0.05 for your degrees of freedom. If χ² is **greater**, reject H₀: the difference is unlikely to be chance alone. If it is **smaller**, fail to reject H₀.

## Claim, evidence and reasoning

For "justify", "support a claim" or "evaluate", build three pieces:

1. **Claim:** a direct answer ("Above 35 °C the enzyme loses activity because it denatures").
2. **Evidence:** numbers from the data, with units ("Oxygen fell from 11.4 mL at 35 °C to 0.6 mL at 55 °C").
3. **Reasoning:** the principle linking evidence to claim ("Heat breaks hydrogen bonds holding the active site in shape, so substrate cannot bind").

Evidence alone is a description; reasoning alone is a textbook sentence. You need both.

## Worked example 1: Variables, controls and rates (Unit 3)

**Question.** An enzyme from a fictional soil fungus breaks hydrogen peroxide into water and oxygen. A student adds 1 mL of extract to 10 mL of hydrogen peroxide in pH 7 buffer and collects the oxygen released in 2 minutes at five temperatures. Mean results (three trials): 15 °C, 4.2 mL; 25 °C, 7.8 mL; 35 °C, 11.4 mL; 45 °C, 6.0 mL; 55 °C, 0.6 mL.
(a) Identify the independent and dependent variables. (b) Describe a suitable negative control. (c) Calculate the rate at 35 °C. (d) Predict the result if the 55 °C enzyme is cooled to 35 °C and retested, and justify your prediction.

**Weak answer.**

> (a) IV is temperature, DV is the enzyme. (b) Use no temperature. (c) 11.4. (d) It will work again because it is back at the best temperature.

**What it misses.** The DV must be measured, not "the enzyme". "No temperature" is impossible. The rate is not divided by time and has no units. The prediction ignores denaturation.

**Strong answer.**

> (a) IV: temperature of the reaction mixture (°C). DV: volume of oxygen collected in 2 minutes (mL).
> (b) Repeat each temperature with enzyme extract that has been boiled and cooled, keeping all volumes the same. No oxygen should be released, showing that oxygen comes from enzyme activity.
> (c) Rate = 11.4 mL ÷ 2 min = **5.7 mL min⁻¹**.
> (d) Oxygen release will stay low, near 0.6 mL in 2 minutes, not return to 11.4 mL. At 55 °C the hydrogen bonds holding the enzyme's tertiary structure break, so the active site changes shape. This denaturation is usually not reversed by cooling, so the substrate still cannot bind.

| Part | Suggested Marlbridge rubric (1 point each) |
|---|---|
| (a) | Both variables named with units or method of measurement |
| (b) | Boiled (or no) enzyme with everything else the same, and its purpose |
| (c) | 5.7 with units of mL min⁻¹ |
| (d) | Prediction of low activity **and** justification using denaturation of the active site |

## Worked example 2: Graphing and water potential (Unit 2)

**Question.** Cylinders cut from a fictional root vegetable were weighed, left for 24 hours in open beakers of sucrose solution at 22 °C, and reweighed. Mean percent change in mass: 0.0 M, +18.0%; 0.1 M, +9.6%; 0.2 M, +2.4%; 0.3 M, −4.8%; 0.4 M, −11.2%; 0.5 M, −16.0%.
(a) Describe the graph you would draw. (b) Estimate the sucrose concentration at which the tissue would show no change in mass. (c) Calculate the water potential of the root tissue.

**Weak answer.**

> (a) A bar graph of the results. (b) About 0.2 M because that is the smallest change. (c) Ψ = iCRT = 0.2 × 0.0831 × 22 = 0.37.

**What it misses.** Concentration is continuous, so a bar graph is wrong. The answer needs the point where the line crosses zero. The calculation uses °C, drops the minus sign and gives no units.

**Strong answer.**

> (a) A line graph: sucrose concentration (mol L⁻¹) on the x-axis, from 0 to 0.5 in even steps; mean percent change in mass (%) on the y-axis, running from −20 to +20 so that negative values show. Plot the six points and join them or draw a line of best fit.
> (b) The line crosses 0% between 0.2 M (+2.4%) and 0.3 M (−4.8%), at about **0.23 M**. At this concentration there is no net water movement.
> (c) The beakers are open, so Ψp of the solution is 0 and Ψ = Ψs. Ψs = −iCRT = −(1)(0.233 mol L⁻¹)(0.0831 L bar mol⁻¹ K⁻¹)(295 K) = **−5.7 bar**. With no net water movement, the tissue has the same water potential, about −5.7 bar.

| Part | Suggested Marlbridge rubric |
|---|---|
| (a) | 1 point: line graph with concentration on x and percent change on y, both with units; 1 point: even scales that include negative values |
| (b) | 1 point: 0.22–0.25 M read from where the line crosses zero |
| (c) | 1 point: correct substitution with T = 295 K and i = 1; 1 point: −5.7 bar (−5.4 to −6.0 accepted if consistent with (b)) with units |

## Worked example 3: Chi-square and the null hypothesis (Unit 5)

**Question.** In a fictional plant, flower colour shows incomplete dominance. Red × white gives pink offspring. Two pink plants are crossed and produce 200 offspring: 41 red, 114 pink and 45 white.
(a) State the null hypothesis. (b) Calculate χ². (c) Decide whether to reject the null hypothesis.

**Weak answer.**

> (a) The flowers are 1 : 2 : 1. (b) χ² = (20.5 − 25)² ÷ 25 + (57 − 50)² ÷ 50 + (22.5 − 25)² ÷ 25 = 2.04. (c) 2.04 is small so the hypothesis is proven correct.

**What it misses.** H₀ must say differences are due to chance. Percentages replace counts, which changes the value. "Proven" is never acceptable, and there is no df or critical value.

**Strong answer.**

> (a) H₀: The offspring occur in a 1 red : 2 pink : 1 white ratio, and any difference between observed and expected numbers is due to chance.
> (b) Expected: 200 × ¼ = 50 red, 200 × ½ = 100 pink, 50 white.
> χ² = (41 − 50)² ÷ 50 + (114 − 100)² ÷ 100 + (45 − 50)² ÷ 50 = 1.62 + 1.96 + 0.50 = **4.08**.
> (c) There are 3 categories, so df = 2 and the critical value at p = 0.05 is 5.99. As 4.08 < 5.99, **fail to reject** H₀. The data are consistent with incomplete dominance of a single gene with two alleles.

| Part | Suggested Marlbridge rubric (1 point each) |
|---|---|
| (a) | Null hypothesis naming the 1 : 2 : 1 ratio and chance |
| (b) | Expected counts and χ² = 4.08 |
| (c) | df = 2, critical value 5.99, and "fail to reject" with the comparison stated |

## Common mistakes

- Naming the dependent variable as an object ("the plant") instead of a measured quantity with units.
- Describing a control as "keeping everything the same"; that defines controlled variables, not a control group.
- Writing a null hypothesis that already predicts an effect.
- Drawing a bar graph for continuous data, leaving out axis units, or using uneven scales.
- Ignoring error bars, or saying overlapping ± 2 SE bars show a significant difference.
- Using percentages, not counts, in chi-square, or setting df to the sample size minus one.
- Taking q directly as the frequency of the recessive phenotype instead of its square root.
- Writing "the data prove", or quoting a trend with no number from the data.

## Practise it

**Task 1 (Unit 1).** A student measures how high water rises in glass tubes of internal diameter 0.5, 1.0, 1.5 and 2.0 mm, five tubes per size, and records mean height ± 2 SE. Describe the graph to draw, and give one controlled variable.

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

A scatter (or line) graph: tube diameter (mm) on x, mean water height (mm) on y, even scales. Plot each mean with vertical ± 2 SE error bars and a trend line that stops at the data, not at the origin. Controlled variable: water temperature (or type of glass). Height should fall as diameter rises: adhesion to the glass and cohesion through hydrogen bonds lift a narrow column higher.
</details>

**Task 2 (Unit 4).** Root tips are grown with or without a fictional compound K. The percentage of cells in mitosis in five root tips is: control 12, 14, 11, 13, 15; compound K 4, 9, 6, 8, 8. State the null hypothesis, calculate the mean and SE for compound K (control: mean 13, SE 0.71), and decide whether to reject the null hypothesis.

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

H₀: Compound K has no effect on the percentage of root-tip cells in mitosis.
Compound K: mean = 35 ÷ 5 = **7%**. Deviations are −3, 2, −1, 1, 1; squares sum to 16; s = √(16 ÷ 4) = 2.0; SE = 2.0 ÷ √5 = **0.89**.
Mean ± 2 SE: control 11.6–14.4%; compound K 5.2–8.8%. The intervals do not overlap, so **reject H₀**. Compound K appears to reduce cell division, although five root tips is a small sample.
</details>

**Task 3 (Unit 6).** Researchers think a fictional transcription factor, TF-K, switches on a heat-response gene, *hspB*, in yeast at 37 °C. Identify the independent variable, the dependent variable and a suitable control, and predict the result if the idea is correct.

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

IV: presence of a working TF-K gene (normal cells against cells with TF-K deleted). DV: amount of *hspB* mRNA after a set time at 37 °C. Control: normal yeast with working TF-K, same medium, temperature and time. Prediction: cells lacking TF-K will have much less *hspB* mRNA. Justification: a transcription factor binds DNA near the promoter and helps RNA polymerase start transcription, so without it, *hspB* transcription falls.
</details>

**Task 4 (Unit 7).** In a population of 500 fictional beetles, 80 show the recessive green colour. Assuming Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium, calculate p, q and the expected number of heterozygous beetles. State one assumption you made.

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

q² = 80 ÷ 500 = 0.16, so q = √0.16 = **0.4** and p = 1 − 0.4 = **0.6**. Heterozygote frequency 2pq = 2 × 0.6 × 0.4 = 0.48, so the expected number is 0.48 × 500 = **240 beetles**. Assumption: mating is random (or no migration, no selection, no mutation, a large population).
</details>

**Task 5 (Unit 8).** An island population of a fictional lizard has 400 individuals. In one year there are 120 births and 60 deaths. Calculate dN/dt and the percent change in population size. Predict how the growth rate will change as the population nears the island's carrying capacity, and justify your prediction.

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

dN/dt = B − D = 120 − 60 = **60 lizards per year**. Percent change = 60 ÷ 400 × 100 = **15%**, giving 460 lizards. As the population nears carrying capacity, the growth rate will slow towards zero. Justification: more lizards share the same food and space, so competition raises deaths and lowers births; in the logistic model (K − N) ÷ K approaches 0.
</details>
