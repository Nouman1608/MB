## Understanding

- I can name the four groups bonded to the central carbon of every amino acid. *(Guide: "Amino acids: one shared core, one variable R group")*
- I can sort R groups into nonpolar, polar and ionic, and say where each type tends to sit in a protein dissolved in water. *(Guide: R-group table; Practice Q3)*
- I can explain how a peptide bond forms between a carboxyl group and an amine group, and say what is released. *(Guide: Figure 1; Practice Q1)*
- I can state what holds each of the four levels of protein structure together, and say that secondary structure comes from the backbone, not the R groups. *(Guide: "Four levels of protein structure"; Practice Q1, Q3)*
- I can name the four kinds of R-group interaction in tertiary structure and say which one is covalent. *(Guide: "Tertiary structure"; Practice Q4)*
- I can explain why only proteins with two or more chains have quaternary structure. *(Guide: "Quaternary structure"; Practice Q6)*

## Calculation and skills

- I can count the peptide bonds and water molecules for one or more chains, using n − 1 per chain. *(Guide: Worked example 1; Practice Q2, Q7)*
- I can estimate the mass of a protein from the number of amino acids and their mean mass, and the mass change when it is hydrolysed. *(Guide: Worked example 1; Practice Q7)*
- I can calculate means and percentages from data on altered proteins. *(Guide: Worked example 2; Practice Q4)*
- I can identify the variables and a suitable control in an experiment on protein folding. *(Practice Q5)*

## Reasoning

- I can predict how replacing one amino acid changes a protein, giving the full chain: R group → interaction → shape → function. *(Guide: "Shape determines function", Worked example 2; Practice Q3, Q4, Q6)*
- I can explain why some single changes have a large effect and others almost none. *(Guide: "Shape determines function"; Practice Q4, Q6)*
- I can explain why a small data set supports, but does not prove, a claim about protein structure. *(Guide: Worked example 2)*
