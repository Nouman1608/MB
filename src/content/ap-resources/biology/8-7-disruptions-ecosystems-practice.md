These are **original Marlbridge practice questions**, not past exam questions. The mark points are a suggested Marlbridge rubric to help you check your work; they are not an official scoring guideline. Data for every question: χ² = Σ (o − e)² ÷ e; critical values at p = 0.05 are 3.84 (1 degree of freedom), 5.99 (2) and 7.81 (3). A calculator is assumed. Keep full calculator values until the last step. All organisms named "fictional" and all data sets are invented.

## Question 1 (multiple choice · foundation)

A farmer sprays a field with a new insecticide every season. Before the first spray, about 1 in 10 000 beetles in the field could survive it. Six generations later, 80% of the beetles survive the spray. Which statement best explains this change?

- (A) The insecticide caused mutations in beetles that needed resistance, and these beetles passed the new alleles to their offspring.
- (B) Beetles carrying resistance alleles were already present; the spray killed most susceptible beetles, so resistant beetles left more offspring and the allele became more common.
- (C) Each beetle gradually built up resistance during its life by being exposed to small doses, and passed this acquired resistance to its offspring.
- (D) The insecticide raised the mutation rate in all beetle genes, so resistance alleles became common within six generations.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (B).** A few beetles already survived before any spraying, so the resistance variation existed first. The insecticide acted as a selective pressure. Survivors reproduced, and the allele frequency rose over the generations.

- (A) states the misconception that the environment produces the mutations that are needed. Mutations are random with respect to need.
- (C) describes inheritance of acquired characteristics. Changes during an individual's life are not passed on through its genes.
- (D) Without selection, rare new alleles could not reach 80% in six generations; and resistance was present before spraying.
</details>

## Question 2 (multiple choice · core)

The table shows the relative fitness of three genotypes of a fictional snail in four different environments. In which environment does the snail show heterozygote advantage?

| Environment | AA | Aa | aa |
|---|---|---|---|
| W | 1.0 | 1.0 | 0.6 |
| X | 0.7 | 1.0 | 0.8 |
| Y | 1.0 | 0.85 | 0.7 |
| Z | 0.9 | 0.6 | 1.0 |

- (A) W
- (B) X
- (C) Y
- (D) Z

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (B).** In X the heterozygote (1.0) has a higher relative fitness than **both** homozygotes (0.7 and 0.8). That is the definition of heterozygote advantage. Both alleles will be kept in the population.

- (A) In W, Aa equals AA. That fits a dominant A allele with no extra benefit for the heterozygote.
- (C) In Y, fitness falls steadily from AA to aa. Selection favours A, and the heterozygote is intermediate.
- (D) In Z the heterozygote has the **lowest** fitness, the opposite of heterozygote advantage.
</details>

## Question 3 (multiple choice · core)

A fictional fish species is released into a lake. It eats the same insect larvae as a native fish. Its usual parasites and predators live only in its home river, thousands of kilometres away. Within ten years the newcomer is the most common fish in the lake and the native fish has declined by 70%. Which statement best explains these changes?

- (A) The newcomer occupied an empty niche, so it did not interact with the native fish.
- (B) Free of its natural enemies, the newcomer had a higher survival rate and outcompeted the native fish for the shared food.
- (C) The native fish declined because the newcomer is a predator of adult native fish.
- (D) Invasive species are not affected by density-dependent factors, so the newcomer's population can grow without limit.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (B).** Two pieces of the stem matter: the species share food, so they compete, and the newcomer has escaped its parasites and predators. Fewer deaths let its population grow fast, and the native fish lost the competition for larvae.

- (A) contradicts the stem: both species eat the same larvae, so the niches overlap.
- (C) The stem gives no evidence of predation on native fish. Competition explains the data without it.
- (D) Every population is eventually limited by density-dependent factors such as food. The newcomer's growth slows once it reaches the lake's carrying capacity for it.
</details>

## Question 4 (multiple choice · core)

A persistent, fat-soluble pollutant is found at 0.05 ppm in zooplankton. Its concentration increases 8 times at each step along this food chain:

zooplankton → small fish → large fish → seal

What is the expected concentration in the seal?

- (A) 1.2 ppm
- (B) 3.2 ppm
- (C) 25.6 ppm
- (D) 204.8 ppm

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (C).** There are three steps from zooplankton to seal. Concentration = 0.05 × 8 × 8 × 8 = 0.05 × 512 = 25.6 ppm.

- (A) adds the factors instead of multiplying: 0.05 × 8 × 3 = 1.2.
- (B) uses only two steps (0.05 × 8² = 3.2): this is the large fish.
- (D) multiplies by 8 four times (0.05 × 8⁴ = 204.8). It counts the four organisms as four steps, but there are only three steps between them.
</details>

## Question 5 (data analysis · core)

After heavy rain washes fertiliser from nearby farms into a fictional lake, students sample it every two weeks.

| Week | 0 | 2 | 4 | 6 | 8 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Phosphate / mg L⁻¹ | 0.02 | 0.15 | 0.10 | 0.05 | 0.03 |
| Algae (chlorophyll) / µg L⁻¹ | 4 | 30 | 55 | 20 | 8 |
| Dissolved oxygen at 4 m depth / mg L⁻¹ | 8.5 | 7.9 | 6.2 | 1.6 | 3.8 |
| Fish caught per net | 22 | 21 | 17 | 3 | 5 |

(a) Calculate the percentage increase in algae from week 0 to week 4.
(b) Dissolved oxygen is lowest at week 6, when algae are already declining. Explain why.
(c) Explain why the fish catch fell.
(d) Propose one action that would reduce the chance of this happening again, and explain how it works.

<details>
<summary>Model answer and suggested Marlbridge rubric</summary>

**(a)** (55 − 4) ÷ 4 × 100 = 51 ÷ 4 × 100 = **1275%** (the algae increased almost 14-fold).

**(b)** Phosphate had been limiting algal growth. The extra phosphate caused a bloom that peaked at week 4. As the algae died, they sank and were broken down by aerobic decomposers (bacteria). The decomposers multiplied, and their respiration used up the dissolved oxygen. So oxygen fell to 1.6 mg L⁻¹ at week 6, after the peak of the bloom, not at it.

**(c)** Fish need dissolved oxygen for aerobic respiration. At 1.6 mg L⁻¹ many fish suffocated or left the deeper water, so the catch fell from 22 to 3.

**(d)** Any one with a mechanism, for example: plant a buffer strip of vegetation between fields and the lake, so plant roots take up nitrate and phosphate before they reach the water; apply less fertiliser, or not before heavy rain; treat sewage to remove phosphate. Each reduces the nutrient input that triggers the bloom.

| Point | What earns it |
|---|---|
| 1 | 1275% (or about 1300%) with working |
| 1 | Bloom caused by phosphate, then death of algae |
| 1 | Aerobic decomposers' respiration uses oxygen, explaining the delay after the bloom |
| 1 | Fish decline linked to low oxygen **and** one valid preventive action with its mechanism |

Do not award the third point for "algae use up the oxygen": living algae produce oxygen by photosynthesis in the light.
</details>

## Question 6 (chi-square · core)

Surveyors map 200 patches of a fictional invasive climbing vine in a nature reserve. The reserve is 40% forest edge, 35% grassland and 25% forest interior by area. The patches are found in: **forest edge 116, grassland 62, forest interior 22**.

(a) State a null hypothesis for the distribution of the vine.
(b) Calculate χ² and state whether you reject or fail to reject the null hypothesis at p = 0.05.
(c) Suggest a biological explanation for the pattern.
(d) A manager says: "The vine can never spread into the forest interior." Evaluate this claim using the data.

<details>
<summary>Worked solution and suggested Marlbridge rubric</summary>

**(a)** "The vine patches are distributed in proportion to the area of each habitat" (habitat type has no effect on where the vine grows).

**(b)** Expected: edge 200 × 0.40 = 80; grassland 200 × 0.35 = 70; interior 200 × 0.25 = 50.

| Habitat | o | e | (o − e)² ÷ e |
|---|---|---|---|
| Forest edge | 116 | 80 | 36² ÷ 80 = 16.20 |
| Grassland | 62 | 70 | 8² ÷ 70 = 0.91 |
| Forest interior | 22 | 50 | 28² ÷ 50 = 15.68 |
| | | **Total** | **χ² = 32.79** |

Degrees of freedom = 3 − 1 = 2; critical value 5.99. Since 32.79 > 5.99, **reject the null hypothesis**. The vine is more common at forest edges and less common in the interior than area alone predicts.

**(c)** A climbing vine needs light and something to climb. Forest edges give both: trees for support and light from the open side. The canopy shades the interior, so the vine grows poorly there. (Accept other reasonable explanations, such as seeds spread by people along edge paths.)

**(d)** The claim is **not supported**. 22 patches were found in the forest interior, so the vine can grow there, though less often than expected. The data show only where the vine is now; it could spread further if the canopy is opened, for example by logging or storms.

| Point | What earns it |
|---|---|
| 1 | Null hypothesis linked to habitat area |
| 1 | Correct expected values |
| 1 | χ² ≈ 32.8 with df = 2 and the correct conclusion ("reject", not "prove") |
| 1 | Plausible biological explanation using light or support |
| 1 | Claim evaluated using the 22 interior patches |

Accept χ² carried forward from an error in the expected values.
</details>

## Question 7 (constructed response · stretch)

A fictional seabird nests on an island in the eastern tropical Pacific. It feeds its chicks on small fish that feed on plankton. Researchers record the sea-surface temperature anomaly (the difference from the long-term average) and the number of chicks fledged per 100 nests.

| Year | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Temperature anomaly / °C | +0.1 | −0.3 | +2.1 | +0.4 | −0.2 |
| Chicks fledged per 100 nests | 62 | 68 | 9 | 55 | 64 |

Year 3 was a strong El Niño year.

(a) Calculate the mean number of chicks fledged per 100 nests in the four other years, and the percentage decrease in year 3 compared with this mean.
(b) Explain the low breeding success in year 3, starting from the change in ocean conditions.
(c) Some climate models predict that strong warm-water events may become more frequent. Predict the effect on the seabird population, and explain why a population with low genetic diversity would be at greater risk.
(d) Explain why breeding success recovered in year 4, and why this recovery does not mean the event had no lasting effect.

<details>
<summary>Model answer and suggested Marlbridge rubric</summary>

**(a)** Mean = (62 + 68 + 55 + 64) ÷ 4 = 249 ÷ 4 = **62.25** chicks per 100 nests. Decrease = (62.25 − 9) ÷ 62.25 × 100 = **85.5%** (about 86%).

**(b)** In an El Niño year, the surface water warms and upwelling of cold, nutrient-rich water weakens. With fewer nutrients, there is less phytoplankton growth, so less food for zooplankton and for the small fish that eat them. Parent birds cannot find enough fish, so fewer chicks are fed well enough to fledge. The disruption passes up the food chain.

**(c)** More frequent warm years would mean more years of very low breeding success. The population would shrink, because too few young replace adults that die. A population with low genetic diversity is less likely to contain individuals with variations that help them cope, for example by foraging on different prey or nesting at a different time. So selection has less to work with, and the risk of local extinction is higher.

**(d)** In year 4 the anomaly was small (+0.4 °C), so upwelling and food supply returned to near normal. But year 3's missing chicks are a lost age group: fewer birds will reach breeding age later.

| Point | What earns it |
|---|---|
| 1 | Mean 62.25 and decrease about 85–86% |
| 1 | Reduced upwelling → fewer nutrients → less plankton |
| 1 | Chain continued to fewer fish → fewer chicks fed |
| 1 | Prediction of population decline with a reason |
| 1 | Low genetic diversity linked to fewer useful variants for selection |
| 1 | Recovery explained, plus a lasting effect (lost age group) |
</details>
