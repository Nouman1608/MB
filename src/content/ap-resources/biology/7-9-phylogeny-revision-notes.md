## Recap

- A tree is a **testable hypothesis** about evolutionary relationships. It is revised when new evidence disagrees (for example, DNA placed whales next to hippos).
- **Root** = ancestor of all lineages shown. **Node** = most recent common ancestor of the lineages beyond it, and a speciation event. **Clade** = an ancestor plus all its descendants.
- Branches can **rotate** at a node without changing any relationship. Living tips are cousins, never ancestors of each other.
- Trees can be built from morphology of living or fossil species and from DNA or protein sequences. **Molecular data** are usually more reliable: many more characters, and less often misled by **convergent evolution**.

## Key relationships

| Term | Meaning | Use on a tree |
|---|---|---|
| Ancestral character | present in the outgroup and the common ancestor | cannot separate groups |
| Derived character | new state, gained **or lost** | placed on the branch where it arose |
| Shared derived character | derived state in two or more lineages | marks a clade |
| Outgroup | lineage less closely related to the rest | shows which state is ancestral |
| Cladogram | branching order only | branch length means nothing |
| Phylogenetic tree | branching order plus time or change | read dates from the axis |

| Molecular clock | Working |
|---|---|
| **rate = differences ÷ time since a dated split** | calibrate with a fossil-dated split |
| **time = differences ÷ rate** | estimate other splits |

## Assumptions behind the numbers

- A molecular clock assumes mutations build up at a **steady rate** in that gene on every branch. Real rates vary, so dates are estimates.
- Fossils give a **minimum** age for a lineage: it existed at least that long ago.
- A derived character shared by several lineages is assumed to have arisen **once**, in their common ancestor (the simplest explanation).

## Mistakes to avoid

1. Judging relatedness by which tips are printed side by side.
2. Calling one living species the ancestor of another, or calling the outgroup the ancestor.
3. Using a character present in the outgroup to define a clade.
4. Ignoring losses (eyes, legs, pigment) as derived characters.
5. Reading time from the branch lengths of a cladogram.
6. Saying a lineage with more derived characters is "more evolved".

## Quick self-check

1. What does a node represent? *(The most recent common ancestor of the lineages that branch from it; a speciation event.)*
2. A clock runs at 4 differences per million years. Two species differ by 40. When did they split? *(40 ÷ 4 = 10 million years ago.)*
3. Two unrelated desert plants both store water in thick stems. Why is this weak evidence of close relationship? *(Similar conditions can select for similar traits separately: convergent evolution.)*
