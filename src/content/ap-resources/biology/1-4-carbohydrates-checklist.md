## Understanding

- I can explain that monosaccharides are the monomers of complex carbohydrates, and name glucose, fructose, galactose, ribose and deoxyribose as examples. *(Guide: "Monosaccharides: the monomers")*
- I can describe how two monosaccharides join by a covalent bond with the loss of one water molecule, and how hydrolysis reverses this. *(Guide: Figure 1; Practice Q4)*
- I can name the monomers of maltose, sucrose and lactose. *(Guide: "Joining sugars")*
- I can tell a linear polysaccharide from a branched one in a diagram. *(Guide: Figure 2; Practice Q4)*
- I can state where starch, glycogen and cellulose are found, whether each is linear or branched, and what each does. *(Guide: "Linear or branched"; Practice Q1)*
- I can explain why storing glucose as an insoluble polymer is better than storing free glucose. *(Guide: "Storage polysaccharides")*

## Calculation and skills

- I can count the monomers, links and water molecules in a carbohydrate chain, using n − 1 links for n monomers. *(Guide: Worked example 1; Practice Q2, Q4)*
- I can write the formula and calculate the molar mass of a short chain of glucose units. *(Guide: Worked example 1; Practice Q4)*
- I can use a simple model to calculate how the number of chain ends changes the rate of glucose release. *(Guide: Worked example 2; Practice Q6)*
- I can identify the variables and the purpose of each control in an enzyme experiment on starch and cellulose. *(Practice Q5)*

## Reasoning

- I can explain why branching lets glycogen release glucose quickly. *(Guide: Worked example 2; Practice Q3)*
- I can explain how straight cellulose chains and hydrogen bonds between them make strong fibres. *(Guide: "Structural polysaccharides"; Practice Q7)*
- I can predict and justify the effect of a change in branching on glucose release. *(Practice Q6)*
- I can explain why humans cannot digest cellulose even though it is made of glucose. *(Guide: "Background"; Practice Q5, Q7)*
