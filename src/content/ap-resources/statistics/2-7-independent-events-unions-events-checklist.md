## Understanding

- I can explain in context what it means for two events to be independent. *(Guide: "What does 'independent' mean?")*
- I can state the three equivalent conditions for independence and explain why checking one is enough. *(Guide: "Three ways to check for independence")*
- I can explain why P(A ∩ B) is subtracted in the general addition rule, using a Venn diagram. *(Guide: Figure 1; Practice Q1)*
- I can explain why mutually exclusive events with non-zero probabilities cannot be independent. *(Guide: Worked example 3; Practice Q7(c))*
- I can explain why past results of independent trials do not change the next result. *(Practice Q7(b))*

## Calculation

- I can check independence from a two-way table by comparing P(A | B) with P(A), showing both values. *(Guide: Worked example 1; Practice Q4)*
- I can check independence from given probabilities, including finding P(A ∩ B) from P(A ∪ B). *(Practice Q2, Q6)*
- I can use P(A ∩ B) = P(A) · P(B) for independent events, including more than two events. *(Guide: Worked example 2; Practice Q5, Q7(a))*
- I can calculate P(A ∪ B) with the general addition rule and check it with 1 − P(neither). *(Guide: Worked examples 1 and 2; Practice Q4, Q6)*
- I can find the probability of at least one success in several independent trials with 1 − P(none). *(Guide: Worked example 2; Practice Q3)*

## Reasoning

- I can judge whether an independence assumption is reasonable in context, and say what changes if it is not. *(Guide: Worked example 2; Practice Q5(c))*
- I can show every probability calculation as rule, substituted values and answer, and interpret the result in context. *(All worked examples)*
