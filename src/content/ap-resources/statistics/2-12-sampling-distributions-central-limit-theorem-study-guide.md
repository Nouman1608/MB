## Statistics vary from sample to sample

A **parameter** describes a whole population, such as the population mean μ or the population proportion p. A **statistic** is calculated from a sample, such as the sample mean x̄ or the sample proportion p̂.

Take a different random sample and you will usually get a different value of the statistic. This is **sampling variability**. It is not a mistake: it is what random sampling does. To use a statistic well, you need to know **how much** it tends to vary, and what values are typical. That is what a sampling distribution tells you.

The **sampling distribution of a statistic** is the distribution of the values of that statistic for **all possible samples of a given size** from a given population.

## Three different distributions

Students often mix these up. Keep them apart by asking, "What is one dot on the graph?"

| Distribution | One value is… | Example (call durations) |
|---|---|---|
| Population distribution | one individual in the population | the length of one call, out of all 2,000 calls |
| Distribution of one sample | one individual in your sample | the length of one of the 40 calls you sampled |
| Sampling distribution | the statistic from one whole sample | the mean length x̄ of one sample of 40 calls |

The first two show how **individuals** vary. The sampling distribution shows how a **statistic** varies from sample to sample.

## Worked example 1: an exact sampling distribution

**Question.** A school keeps exactly five beehives (fictional). Last summer their honey yields were 2, 3, 5, 8 and 12 kg, so the population mean is μ = 30 ÷ 5 = 6 kg. (a) List every possible random sample of 2 hives (without replacement) and find the sampling distribution of x̄. (b) Describe it, and compare it with samples of 3 hives.

**(a)** There are 10 possible samples of 2 hives. Each is equally likely in a simple random sample.

| Sample (kg) | 2, 3 | 2, 5 | 2, 8 | 2, 12 | 3, 5 | 3, 8 | 3, 12 | 5, 8 | 5, 12 | 8, 12 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| x̄ (kg) | 2.5 | 3.5 | 5.0 | 7.0 | 4.0 | 5.5 | 7.5 | 6.5 | 8.5 | 10.0 |

So the sampling distribution of x̄ for n = 2 has these 10 values, each with probability 1/10 = 0.1.

**(b)**

1. **Centre.** The mean of the 10 sample means is 60 ÷ 10 = 6.0 kg, exactly μ.
2. **Variability.** The sample means run from 2.5 kg to 10.0 kg (range 7.5 kg). Their standard deviation, treating the 10 values as the whole distribution, is about 2.22 kg. The population's standard deviation is about 3.63 kg, so the sample means vary less than individual hives.
3. **A probability.** P(5 ≤ x̄ ≤ 7) = 4/10 = 0.4. So a sample of 2 hives gives a mean within 1 kg of μ only 40% of the time.
4. **Samples of 3 hives.** There are again 10 possible samples. Their means run from 3.33 kg to 8.33 kg (range 5.0 kg), with mean 6.0 kg and standard deviation about 1.48 kg. Now P(5 ≤ x̄ ≤ 7) = 5/10 = 0.5.

**Interpretation.** Both sampling distributions are centred on the population mean of 6 kg. With 3 hives per sample, the sample means are less spread out, so a single sample mean is more likely to be close to μ.

**Check.** The 10 sample means for n = 2 add to 60, and 60 ÷ 10 = 6 = μ. (Topic 3.1 explains why a statistic whose sampling distribution is centred on the parameter is called unbiased.)

## Simulating a sampling distribution

Real populations are too large to list every sample. Instead, **simulate**:

1. **Assume** a value for the parameter, for example p = 0.75 or a model for the population.
2. **Generate** a random sample of size n from that population with technology.
3. **Calculate and record** the statistic for that sample.
4. **Repeat** steps 2 and 3 many times (hundreds or thousands).
5. **Graph** the recorded values. This simulated distribution **approximates** the sampling distribution.

Describe the result as you would any distribution: **shape, centre and variability, in context**. Then you can ask whether an observed statistic is typical or unusual **if the assumed parameter value is true**.

## Randomization distributions for experiments

In an **experiment**, the randomness comes from **random assignment** of subjects to treatments, not from random sampling. A **randomization distribution** shows how much a statistic, such as a difference in means, varies just because of which subjects happened to land in which group.

1. Write each subject's **response value** on a card.
2. **Shuffle** the cards and deal them at random into groups of the original sizes. This is one **reallocation**.
3. **Calculate and record** the statistic (for example, mean of group A − mean of group B).
4. **Repeat** many times and graph the results.

This keeps every response exactly as observed and only changes the group labels. So it shows the values the statistic would take if the treatment made **no difference** to anyone's response. If the observed statistic sits far out in the tail, chance assignment alone is an unlikely explanation.

## Worked example 3: a memory-app experiment

## The central limit theorem

The **central limit theorem (CLT)** says: the sampling distribution of the **mean of a random sample** can be approximated by a **normal distribution**, and the **larger the sample**, the better the approximation. This holds even when the population itself is not normal.

Figure 3 shows the CLT at work. A fictional helpline has a population of 2,000 call durations. The population is strongly **skewed to the right**: most calls are short, a few are very long. Its mean is 3.83 minutes, its median 2.60 minutes and its standard deviation 3.87 minutes. For each sample size, technology took 1,000 random samples and recorded each sample mean.

## Worked example 4: describing the CLT simulation

**Question.** Use Figure 3 to describe how the sampling distribution of x̄ changes as n increases from 2 to 40.

| n | Mean of the 1,000 x̄ values | Standard deviation of the x̄ values | Shape |
|---|---|---|---|
| population | 3.83 (μ) | 3.87 (σ) | strongly right-skewed |
| 2 | 3.81 | 2.66 | right-skewed |
| 10 | 3.81 | 1.26 | slightly right-skewed |
| 40 | 3.82 | 0.60 | roughly symmetric, bell-shaped |

1. **Shape.** As n increases, the skew fades. For n = 40 the histogram is close to a normal curve. This is the CLT.
2. **Centre.** For every n, the sample means are centred close to the population mean of 3.83 minutes.
3. **Variability.** The sample means vary less as n grows: their standard deviation falls from 2.66 minutes (n = 2) to 0.60 minutes (n = 40). Larger samples give sample means that are closer to μ more of the time.

**Interpretation.** For random samples of 40 calls, the mean call duration is approximately normally distributed around 3.83 minutes, and most sample means lie between about 2.6 and 5.0 minutes, even though individual calls are very skewed and run from under 1 minute to over 30.

**Check.** The individual calls inside one sample of 40 are still right-skewed, like the population. Only the distribution of the **sample means** becomes approximately normal. Topic 4.1 gives a formula for the standard deviation of x̄ and a rule of thumb for how large n must be.

## Common misconceptions

- **"The sampling distribution is the distribution of my sample."** Your sample's data show individuals. The sampling distribution shows the statistic over all possible samples.
- **"The CLT makes the population, or my sample, normal."** It does not. Only the distribution of sample means becomes approximately normal.
- **"More simulated samples means a bigger sample size."** The number of repetitions (200, 1,000) improves the picture of the sampling distribution. The sample size n is the number of individuals in each sample, and only n changes the spread of the statistic.
- **"A larger sample size changes the centre."** The centre stays near the parameter; it is the variability that shrinks.
- **"An unusual result proves the claim is false."** It gives evidence against the claim. Unusual results do occasionally happen when a claim is true.
- **Reallocating with different group sizes.** A randomization must keep the original group sizes and the observed responses; only the labels move.
- **Describing a simulated distribution with no context.** Name the statistic, the sample size and the assumed parameter value, and describe shape, centre and variability.

Try the [practice questions](/advanced-course-resources/statistics/2-12-sampling-distributions-central-limit-theorem-practice/) now, then use the [revision notes](/advanced-course-resources/statistics/2-12-sampling-distributions-central-limit-theorem-revision-notes/) and the [checklist](/advanced-course-resources/statistics/2-12-sampling-distributions-central-limit-theorem-checklist/) to consolidate. For the normal model itself, look back at the [Topic 2.11 study guide](/advanced-course-resources/statistics/2-11-normal-distribution-study-guide/).
