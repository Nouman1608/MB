## Recap

- Statistics helps you learn from data when the answer **varies** from one individual to another.
- A **statistical study** uses data from a sample so that it can answer an investigative question about the whole population.
- We sample because the population is too large, or too hard, costly or destructive to measure in full.
- Each part of a study, and each number from it, matches something real. Saying what it matches is writing **in context**.

## Key relationships

| Term | Meaning | Example (fictional Sandmere College bus survey) |
|---|---|---|
| Investigative question | The question the study answers | What proportion of the college's students would use a Friday late-night bus? |
| Population | All items or individuals of interest | All Sandmere College students |
| N | Population size | 2,400 |
| Sample | Subset of the population that data come from | The 150 students asked |
| n | Sample size (n < N) | 150 |
| Datum | One piece of information about one individual | One student's "Yes" |
| Data set | The whole collection of data | All 150 answers |

## Assumptions and conventions

- Capital **N** is always the population size; lower-case **n** is always the sample size.
- A population can be people, objects, animals, days or anything else the question is about.
- A sample result describes the sample. Another sample would probably give a slightly different value.
- A valid investigative question is decided **before** the data are analysed and is not changed afterwards.

## Mistakes to avoid

1. **Swapping N and n**, or calling the sample "the population".
2. **Treating a look-up fact as statistical**: "What time does the bus leave?" needs no data that vary.
3. **Vague questions**: words like "good" or "like" need a defined, measurable meaning.
4. **Changing the question after seeing results.** This makes the evidence look stronger than it is.
5. **No context**: "58%" alone earns little. Say 58% *of whom*, *doing what*.
6. **Claiming the sample value is exactly the population value.**

## Quick self-check

1. A club asks 120 of its 900 members whether they want a summer trip. Give N and n. *(N = 900 members, n = 120 members asked)*
2. In question 1, 42 of the members asked say yes. State the result in context. *(42 of the 120 club members asked (35%) said they want a summer trip.)*
3. Is "How old is the club's founder?" a statistical investigative question? *(No. It has one fixed answer, so no data that vary are needed.)*
