## Recap

- A **bar chart** shows one categorical variable with one bar per category. The bar's height (or length) is the **frequency** or the **relative frequency**.
- A **pie chart** shows the same distribution as slices of a circle. Each slice's share of the whole circle is its **relative frequency**, and all the slices together make up the whole.
- A frequency bar chart and a relative frequency bar chart of one data set have the same shape; only the axis scale differs.
- Graphs are evidence: a claim must quote values read from the graph and be stated in context.
- Tables, bar charts and pie charts can all **compare two or more groups** on the same categorical variable.

## Key relationships

| You know | You want | Use |
|---|---|---|
| count, n | relative frequency | count ÷ n |
| relative frequency | slice angle | relative frequency × 360° |
| slice angle, n | count | (angle ÷ 360°) × n |
| relative frequency, n | count | relative frequency × n |
| all slice angles | a check | they add to 360° |
| two groups with different n | a fair comparison | compare relative frequencies |

## Assumptions and conventions

- For a pie chart, every individual is in **exactly one** category and no category is left out.
- Bars are separate, of equal width, and the frequency axis starts at 0.
- Order the bars by size, or keep a natural order (for example, "Never, Sometimes, Often") if there is one.
- Rounded percentages may total 99.9% or 100.1%. Say the difference is due to rounding.

## Mistakes to avoid

1. **Comparing counts** from groups of different sizes. Use proportions.
2. **"Most common" read as "most".** A majority needs more than 0.50.
3. **Using a percentage as an angle.** 30% is 108°, not 30°.
4. **A pie chart for "tick all that apply" data.** The parts add to more than the whole. Use a bar chart.
5. **An axis that does not start at 0**, which exaggerates differences.
6. **Calling a bar chart "skewed".** Categories have no number-line order, so describe the most and least common categories instead.
7. **Reading a count from a proportion without n.** You cannot.

## Quick self-check

1. A category has relative frequency 0.15. What is its slice angle? *(0.15 × 360° = 54°)*
2. In a pie chart of 64 people, a slice has angle 90°. How many people is that? *(90 ÷ 360 × 64 = 16 people)*
3. Group A (n = 500) has 150 people in a category; group B (n = 200) has 70. Which group has the larger proportion in it? *(A: 0.30; B: 0.35, so B, even though its count is smaller)*
