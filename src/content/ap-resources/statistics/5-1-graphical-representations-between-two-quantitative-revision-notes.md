## Recap

- **Bivariate quantitative data** are ordered pairs (x, y) of two quantitative variables measured on the **same individuals**.
- A **scatterplot** shows one dot per individual. Do not join the dots.
- The **explanatory variable** (used to explain or predict) goes on the **x-axis**. The **response variable** (being predicted) goes on the **y-axis**.
- Label both axes with the variable name and units. Use equal steps; an axis need not start at zero.

## Key relationships

| Feature | What it describes | Words to use |
|---|---|---|
| Form | the shape of the pattern | linear; non-linear (curved) |
| Direction | what y tends to do as x increases | positive (tends to increase); negative (tends to decrease) |
| Strength | how closely the points follow the pattern | strong; moderate; weak |
| Unusual features | anything that breaks the pattern | unusual points; clusters; gaps |

A model sentence: "There is a **strong, negative, non-linear** association between **age** and **price** of these cars: **older cars tend to cost less**, and there are **no unusual points**."

## Assumptions and conventions

- Both variables are quantitative and paired by individual.
- "Explanatory" means "used to predict", not "causes".
- If neither variable naturally explains the other, either axis choice is fine.
- A scatterplot can support or challenge a claim. An association alone does not show cause and effect.

## Mistakes to avoid

1. **Swapping the axes**: the variable you want to predict goes on the y-axis.
2. **"Linear because I can draw a line through it"**: look for a constant rate of change.
3. **"Steep, so strong"**: steepness and strength are different ideas.
4. **"Always" instead of "tends to"**: associations are tendencies.
5. **No context**: name both variables and say what the direction means.
6. **Missing unusual features**: mention any outlier or cluster, with a possible reason.
7. **Joining the dots** or unequal scale steps.

## Quick self-check

1. A café owner wants to predict daily soup sales from the outside temperature. Which variable goes on the y-axis? *(Daily soup sales: it is the response)*
2. Points lie very close to a straight line that falls gently. Is the association weak because the slope is small? *(No. Close points mean strong; it is a strong, negative, linear association)*
3. Response values run from 12 to 57. Is a y-axis from 0 to 60 in steps of 10 acceptable? *(Yes: equal steps covering all values)*
4. A scatterplot shows two separate groups of points with a gap between them. What is this feature called? *(Clusters)*
