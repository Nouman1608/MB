## What this diagnostic is for

Use this short check to find which topics in Unit 9 (Thermodynamics) you should revisit. It has eight multiple-choice and three short-answer questions. These are **original Marlbridge practice questions**, not past exam questions. They are not calibrated against real exam results, and your total does not predict an exam score. What matters is **which** questions you miss.

How to take it:

- Work without notes, in about 30 minutes. Write a reason for each answer.
- A scientific calculator is allowed. Data: k_B = 1.38 × 10⁻²³ J/K; R = 8.31 J/(mol·K); c for water = 4180 J/(kg·K). Every gas is ideal and monatomic, so U = (3/2)nRT = (3/2)PV. W is the work done **on** the gas, so ΔU = Q + W. Add 273 to convert °C to K.
- Open each answer only after you have committed to your own.

## Question 1 (multiple choice · 9.1)

A sealed, rigid container of gas is heated. Which statement best explains, in terms of atoms, why the pressure of the gas increases?

- (A) The atoms move faster on average, so each collision with a wall gives a larger impulse and collisions happen more often.
- (B) The atoms expand when heated, so they take up more of the container and hit the walls more often.
- (C) Heating adds more atoms to the gas, so more atoms hit each square metre of wall.
- (D) Each collision gives a larger impulse, but the number of collisions per second stays the same.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (A).** A higher temperature means a higher average kinetic energy, so the atoms move faster. A faster atom changes its momentum by more in each bounce, and it reaches the walls more often. Both effects raise the force per unit area.

- (B) In the ideal gas model the atoms' own volume is negligible and does not change.
- (C) cannot happen: the container is sealed, so N is fixed.
- (D) has half the reasoning. Faster atoms also reach the walls more often.

**If you missed this:** read "Pressure from collisions" in the [Topic 9.1 study guide](/advanced-course-resources/physics-2/9-1-kinetic-theory-temperature-pressure-study-guide/).
</details>

## Question 2 (multiple choice · 9.2)

A 5.0 L container holds 1.2 × 10²³ gas molecules at 7 °C. What is the pressure of the gas?

- (A) 9.27 × 10⁴ Pa
- (B) 2.32 × 10³ Pa
- (C) 92.7 Pa
- (D) 5.58 × 10²⁸ Pa

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (A).** Convert first: T = 7 + 273 = 280 K and V = 5.0 × 10⁻³ m³. You are given a number of molecules, so use PV = Nk_BT: P = (1.2 × 10²³)(1.38 × 10⁻²³ J/K)(280 K) ÷ (5.0 × 10⁻³ m³) = 9.27 × 10⁴ Pa.

- (B) uses 7 °C instead of 280 K.
- (C) leaves the volume in litres.
- (D) uses R with a number of molecules. R goes with moles; k_B goes with molecules.

**If you missed this:** read "The ideal gas law" in the [Topic 9.2 study guide](/advanced-course-resources/physics-2/9-2-ideal-gas-law-study-guide/).
</details>

## Question 3 (multiple choice · 9.3)

Object A is placed in thermal contact with object C and left until there is no net energy transfer between them. Separately, object B is placed in thermal contact with C and left in the same way. A and B are then placed in contact with each other. Which statement is correct?

- (A) There is no net energy transfer between A and B, because they are at the same temperature.
- (B) There is no net energy transfer between A and B only if they have the same internal energy.
- (C) Net energy flows from the larger object to the smaller one until their internal energies are equal.
- (D) A and B stop exchanging any energy at all as soon as they touch.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (A).** Each object reached thermal equilibrium with C, so each is at C's temperature. Systems at the same temperature have no net energy transfer. This is how a thermometer (C) works.

- (B) and (C) use internal energy to decide equilibrium. Temperature decides it.
- (D) confuses "no net transfer" with "no transfer". Atoms at the boundary still collide and pass energy both ways; the flows just balance.

**If you missed this:** read "Thermal equilibrium" in the [Topic 9.3 study guide](/advanced-course-resources/physics-2/9-3-thermal-energy-transfer-equilibrium-study-guide/).
</details>

## Question 4 (multiple choice · 9.4)

A gas in a cylinder is compressed slowly by a piston while the cylinder sits in a large water bath, so the temperature of the gas stays constant. Which row is correct for the gas?

- (A) W > 0, ΔU = 0, Q < 0
- (B) W > 0, ΔU > 0, Q = 0
- (C) W < 0, ΔU = 0, Q > 0
- (D) W = 0, ΔU = 0, Q = 0

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (A).** The piston pushes in, so work is done on the gas: W > 0. T is constant, so U = (3/2)nRT is constant: ΔU = 0. Then Q = ΔU − W = −W < 0. Energy leaves the gas into the bath.

- (B) describes an adiabatic compression. The bath keeps T constant, so energy can leave by heating.
- (C) uses the "work done by the gas" sign. It describes an isothermal **expansion**.
- (D) ignores the work done by the piston.

**If you missed this:** read "Four special processes" in the [Topic 9.4 study guide](/advanced-course-resources/physics-2/9-4-first-law-of-thermodynamics-study-guide/).
</details>

## Question 5 (multiple choice · 9.4)

A gas is in a rigid container of volume 2.5 × 10⁻³ m³. It is cooled, and its pressure falls from 4.0 × 10⁵ Pa to 1.6 × 10⁵ Pa. What is the energy transferred to the gas by heating?

- (A) −900 J
- (B) −600 J
- (C) +900 J
- (D) −1500 J

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (A).** Rigid means ΔV = 0, so W = 0 and Q = ΔU. With U = (3/2)PV: ΔU = 1.5 × (1.6 × 10⁵ − 4.0 × 10⁵) Pa × 2.5 × 10⁻³ m³ = −900 J. Energy leaves the gas.

- (B) is Δ(PV) without the factor 3/2.
- (C) has the right size but the wrong sign. The gas cools, so Q < 0.
- (D) uses (5/2)Δ(PV), the constant-pressure result.

**If you missed this:** read "Energy inside a gas" and the special-process table in the [Topic 9.4 study guide](/advanced-course-resources/physics-2/9-4-first-law-of-thermodynamics-study-guide/).
</details>

## Question 6 (multiple choice · 9.5)

Blocks X and Y have the same mass and are made of different materials. Each receives the same energy by heating. The temperature of X rises three times as much as the temperature of Y. How do their specific heats compare?

- (A) c_X = c_Y ÷ 3
- (B) c_X = 3c_Y
- (C) c_X = c_Y ÷ 9
- (D) c_X = c_Y, because the masses and energies are equal

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (A).** From Q = mcΔT, with Q and m the same, c ∝ 1/ΔT. X has three times the temperature rise, so it has one-third of the specific heat.

- (B) treats a large temperature rise as a sign of a large specific heat. A high c means a **small** temperature change.
- (C) squares the factor. The relationship is inverse, not inverse square.
- (D) forgets that c belongs to the material.

**If you missed this:** read "Energy to change temperature" in the [Topic 9.5 study guide](/advanced-course-resources/physics-2/9-5-specific-heat-thermal-conductivity-study-guide/).
</details>

## Question 7 (multiple choice · 9.5)

The foam wall of a cool box is 3.0 cm thick. The temperature difference across it is 15 K. On a hotter day the difference rises to 25 K. What foam thickness, with the same area and material, would keep the rate of energy conduction the same as before?

- (A) 5.0 cm
- (B) 1.8 cm
- (C) 3.0 cm
- (D) 8.3 cm

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (A).** Q/Δt = kAΔT/L. With k and A fixed, the rate stays the same if ΔT/L stays the same. So L must rise by the same factor as ΔT: 3.0 cm × 25/15 = 5.0 cm.

- (B) multiplies by 15/25, as if a thicker wall conducted faster.
- (C) assumes the thickness does not matter.
- (D) squares the factor. The rate is proportional to 1/L, not 1/L².

**If you missed this:** read "Conduction through a slab" in the [Topic 9.5 study guide](/advanced-course-resources/physics-2/9-5-specific-heat-thermal-conductivity-study-guide/).
</details>

## Question 8 (multiple choice · 9.6)

In which process does the entropy of the named system **decrease**?

- (A) A sealed jar of hot jam (the system) cools on a shelf in a room.
- (B) An ice cube (the system) melts in a warm room.
- (C) A gas (the system) expands freely into an empty chamber inside an insulated box.
- (D) A sliding book (the system) comes to rest on a table because of friction and warms slightly.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (A).** The jam is a closed system. Energy leaves it by cooling, so its entropy can fall. The room gains more entropy than the jam loses, so the total still increases.

- (B) Energy enters the ice by heating, so its entropy rises.
- (C) No energy enters, but the gas spreads into more space, so its entropy rises.
- (D) The book's kinetic energy spreads into random motion of its atoms, so its entropy rises.

**If you missed this:** read "Isolated systems and closed systems" in the [Topic 9.6 study guide](/advanced-course-resources/physics-2/9-6-entropy-second-law-thermodynamics-study-guide/).
</details>

## Question 9 (short answer · 9.1)

A gas is made of molecules of mass 5.0 × 10⁻²⁶ kg at 320 K.

(a) Calculate the average kinetic energy of a molecule.
(b) Calculate the rms speed of the molecules.
(c) At what temperature would the rms speed be twice as large?

<details>
<summary>Worked answer</summary>

1. (a) K_avg = (3/2)k_BT = 1.5 × (1.38 × 10⁻²³ J/K)(320 K) = **6.62 × 10⁻²¹ J**.
2. (b) ½m v_rms² = K_avg, so v_rms = √(2K_avg/m) = √(2 × 6.624 × 10⁻²¹ ÷ 5.0 × 10⁻²⁶) = **515 m/s**.
3. (c) v_rms ∝ √T, so doubling v_rms needs 4 times the kelvin temperature: **1280 K**.

The most common slip is 640 K in (c).

**If you missed this:** read "Temperature and the motion of atoms" in the [Topic 9.1 study guide](/advanced-course-resources/physics-2/9-1-kinetic-theory-temperature-pressure-study-guide/).
</details>

## Question 10 (short answer · 9.4)

0.050 mol of gas at 450 K is in a cylinder with insulated walls. It expands and does 120 J of work on a piston.

(a) State Q, W and ΔU for the gas.
(b) Calculate the final temperature of the gas.

<details>
<summary>Worked answer</summary>

1. (a) Insulated walls, so **Q = 0**. The gas does 120 J of work, so the work done on it is **W = −120 J**. Then **ΔU = Q + W = −120 J**.
2. (b) ΔU = (3/2)nRΔT, so ΔT = −120 J ÷ [1.5 × 0.050 mol × 8.31 J/(mol·K)] = −193 K. Final temperature = 450 K − 193 K = **257 K**.

An adiabatic expansion cools the gas.

**If you missed this:** read "Two ways to change internal energy" in the [Topic 9.4 study guide](/advanced-course-resources/physics-2/9-4-first-law-of-thermodynamics-study-guide/).
</details>

## Question 11 (short answer · 9.6)

A gas in a cylinder is compressed slowly while it sits in cold water, so its temperature stays the same. A student says: "The entropy of the gas goes down, so this process breaks the second law." Evaluate the claim.

<details>
<summary>Worked answer</summary>

The first part is right: energy leaves the gas by heating (Q < 0), so its entropy decreases. The conclusion is wrong. The gas is a closed system, not an isolated one. The second law says only that the **total** entropy of an isolated system never decreases. The water gains energy, and its entropy rises by at least as much as the gas's falls.

**If you missed this:** read "The second law" and "Isolated systems and closed systems" in the [Topic 9.6 study guide](/advanced-course-resources/physics-2/9-6-entropy-second-law-thermodynamics-study-guide/).
</details>
