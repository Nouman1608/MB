## What a shock is

In Topic 3.5 the economy sat in equilibrium where AD crossed SRAS. Now something changes. A **shock** is an event that **shifts** the AD curve or the SRAS curve.

- A **positive shock** shifts a curve to the **right** (an increase in AD or in SRAS).
- A **negative shock** shifts a curve to the **left** (a decrease in AD or in SRAS).

This topic is about the **short run**: nominal wages and some other input prices are still sticky, and the LRAS curve does not move. You compare the old equilibrium (E₁) with the new one (E₂) and say what happened to three things: the **price level**, **real output** and **employment** (and so unemployment).

One rule makes every question easier. **Identify which curve shifts and in which direction before you draw anything.** A change in spending by households, firms, the government or foreigners shifts AD (Topic 3.1). A change in production costs shifts SRAS (Topic 3.3).

## Demand shocks: shifts of AD

When AD increases, buyers want more real output at every price level. At the old price level there is now a **shortage**: inventories run down, so firms raise prices. As the price level rises, sticky wages make it profitable to produce more, so firms move **up** the SRAS curve, hire more workers and raise output.

**A positive AD shock raises the price level, real output and employment in the short run. Unemployment falls.**

A negative AD shock works the other way. At the old price level there is a surplus, so the price level falls; firms move down SRAS, produce less and lay off workers.

**A negative AD shock lowers the price level, real output and employment in the short run. Unemployment rises.**

Inflation caused by an increase in AD is called **demand-pull inflation**: spending "pulls" the price level up.

## Our fictional economy: Valdoria

**Valdoria** is the fictional country used across the Marlbridge macroeconomics pages; its currency is the **valda (VD)**. The starting point is the long-run equilibrium from Topic 3.5: **price level 104**, **real GDP VD 840 billion = Yf**, unemployment **5.0%** (the natural rate) with a labour force of 25.0 million. All data are fictional.

| Price level (index) | 96 | 100 | 104 | 108 | 112 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| AD₁ (VD billion) | 880 | 860 | 840 | 820 | 800 |
| SRAS₁ (VD billion) | 760 | 800 | 840 | 880 | 920 |

As before, assume every VD 40 billion of real output needs 0.25 million workers, so each VD 40 billion change in output moves the unemployment rate by 1.0 percentage point.

## Worked example 1: a positive demand shock

**Question.** Valdorian firms become more optimistic and raise planned investment by **VD 15 billion** at every price level. The marginal propensity to consume (MPC) is **0.75**. (a) By how much does AD shift? (b) Find the new short-run equilibrium. (c) Calculate the inflation rate and the changes in real output, employment and the unemployment rate. (d) Why does real output rise by less than the shift in AD? (e) Name the type of inflation and the output gap.

**(a)** Spending multiplier = 1 ÷ (1 − MPC) = 1 ÷ 0.25 = **4**. AD shifts right by 4 × 15 = **VD 60 billion** at every price level (Topic 3.2).

| Price level | 96 | 100 | 104 | 108 | 112 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| AD₂ (VD billion) | 940 | 920 | 900 | 880 | 860 |
| SRAS₁ (VD billion) | 760 | 800 | 840 | 880 | 920 |

**(b)** AD₂ = SRAS₁ = VD 880 billion at a price level of **108**. E₂ is at PL₂ = 108, Y₂ = **VD 880 billion**.

**(c)** Inflation rate = (108 − 104) ÷ 104 × 100 = **3.85%**. Real output rises by 880 − 840 = **VD 40 billion** (40 ÷ 840 × 100 = **4.76%**). Employment rises by **0.25 million**, from 23.75 to 24.00 million, so the unemployment rate falls from **5.0% to 4.0%**.

**(d)** At the old price level (104), buyers now want VD 900 billion, a shortage of 900 − 840 = VD 60 billion. But the shortage pushes the price level up, and a higher price level reduces the quantity of real output demanded along AD₂ (through the wealth, interest-rate and exchange-rate effects from Topic 3.1). From 104 to 108, the quantity demanded on AD₂ falls from VD 900 billion to VD 880 billion. So output rises by VD 40 billion, not the full VD 60 billion. The rest of the extra spending is absorbed by higher prices.

**(e)** **Demand-pull inflation.** Output (VD 880 billion) is now above Yf (VD 840 billion): a **positive (inflationary) output gap** of VD 40 billion.

**Check.** The price level, output and employment all rose together. That is the signature of an AD shock.

## Supply shocks: shifts of SRAS

A supply shock changes **production costs** across the economy, so it shifts SRAS (Topic 3.3).

A **negative supply shock** (higher energy or raw-material prices, a drought, a rise in nominal wages, higher expected inflation) makes each level of output more expensive to produce. SRAS shifts **left**. At the old price level firms now supply less than buyers want, so the price level rises; buyers move up along AD and buy less. Firms produce less and need fewer workers.

**A negative SRAS shock raises the price level but lowers real output and employment in the short run. Unemployment rises.**

This combination, a rising price level with falling output and rising unemployment, is called **stagflation**. Inflation caused by a decrease in SRAS is **cost-push inflation**: higher costs "push" the price level up.

A **positive supply shock** (cheaper energy, a bumper harvest, higher productivity) shifts SRAS **right**.

**A positive SRAS shock lowers the price level but raises real output and employment in the short run. Unemployment falls.**

So for SRAS shocks, the price level and output move in **opposite** directions. For AD shocks they move in the **same** direction.

## Worked example 2: a negative supply shock

**Question.** World energy prices rise sharply. Valdorian firms' costs rise so that they supply **VD 60 billion less** at every price level. AD does not change. (a) Write the new SRAS schedule and find the new short-run equilibrium. (b) Calculate the inflation rate and the changes in real output, employment and unemployment. (c) Name the type of inflation and the output gap. (d) Compare the result with Worked example 1.

**(a)** Subtract 60 from each SRAS₁ value.

| Price level | 96 | 100 | 104 | 108 | 112 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| AD₁ (VD billion) | 880 | 860 | 840 | 820 | 800 |
| SRAS₂ (VD billion) | 700 | 740 | 780 | 820 | 860 |

AD₁ = SRAS₂ = VD 820 billion at a price level of **108**. E₂ is at PL₂ = 108, Y₂ = **VD 820 billion**.

**(b)** Inflation rate = (108 − 104) ÷ 104 × 100 = **3.85%**. Real output **falls** by 840 − 820 = **VD 20 billion** (−2.38%). VD 20 billion of output is 0.125 million jobs, so employment falls from 23.75 to 23.625 million. Unemployed = 25.0 − 23.625 = 1.375 million, so the unemployment rate rises from **5.0% to 5.50%**.

**(c)** **Cost-push inflation**, with **stagflation**: the price level rose while output fell and unemployment rose. Output is VD 20 billion below Yf: a **negative (recessionary) output gap**.

**(d)** Both shocks raised the price level from 104 to 108, so the inflation rate alone cannot tell you which shock happened. Output tells you: it **rose** to VD 880 billion after the AD shock but **fell** to VD 820 billion after the SRAS shock. Unemployment fell to 4.0% in one case and rose to 5.50% in the other.

**Check.** At the old price level (104), SRAS₂ gives only VD 780 billion while AD₁ is still VD 840 billion: a shortage of VD 60 billion, which is why the price level has to rise.

## All four shocks from the same starting point

Starting from Valdoria's long-run equilibrium (PL 104, VD 840 billion, 5.0% unemployment), here is each shock with a shift of VD 60 billion.

| Shock | Example cause | New PL | New real GDP (VD billion) | Unemployment | Output gap |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| AD increases | More business optimism, higher government spending | 108 ↑ | 880 ↑ | 4.0% ↓ | Positive |
| AD decreases | Falling consumer confidence, a recession abroad cutting exports | 100 ↓ | 800 ↓ | 6.0% ↑ | Negative |
| SRAS decreases | Higher energy prices, higher expected inflation | 108 ↑ | 820 ↓ | 5.50% ↑ | Negative |
| SRAS increases | Cheaper raw materials, higher productivity | 100 ↓ | 860 ↑ | 4.50% ↓ | Positive |

The pattern to remember:

- **Same direction** for the price level and output → an **AD** shock.
- **Opposite directions** → an **SRAS** shock.
- **Demand-pull** inflation comes from AD shifting right; **cost-push** inflation comes from SRAS shifting left.

## Common misconceptions

- **"A rise in the price level shifts SRAS right" (or "shifts AD left").** A price-level change is a movement along the curves. Only the determinants shift them.
- **"Higher energy prices shift AD left, because people spend less."** Energy is an input for almost every firm, so the main effect is on costs: SRAS shifts **left**.
- **"All inflation is demand-pull."** Inflation can also come from rising costs (cost-push). Check whether output rose or fell.
- **"After an AD shock, output rises by the full shift."** With an upward-sloping SRAS, part of the shift shows up as a higher price level, so output rises by less than the horizontal shift.
- **"A negative supply shock lowers the price level, because output falls."** It **raises** the price level while output falls: stagflation.
- **"Unemployment and the price level always move in opposite directions."** True for AD shocks; for a negative SRAS shock both rise together.
- **Shifting LRAS for a temporary cost shock.** A short-lived rise in input prices shifts SRAS only.
- **Showing only the final position.** Graphs need both curves (old and new), both equilibria and the new price level and output labelled.
