## What GDP is good for

In Topic 2.1 you learned that **GDP is the market value of all final goods and services produced within a country in a given period**. It is the most widely used indicator of a country's economic performance because:

- it measures the **total output** of an economy in a single number;
- it is collected regularly, so you can see whether output is **growing or shrinking** (the business cycle, Topic 2.7);
- it uses the same rules everywhere, so countries can be **compared**;
- governments and central banks use it to judge whether the economy needs help.

But GDP was designed to measure **market production**. It was never meant to measure everything that makes life good. When you use it as a measure of well-being or living standards, it has important limitations.

## Exclusions by design are not limitations

Be careful to separate two ideas.

| Left out on purpose (correct accounting) | Missed because it cannot easily be measured (a limitation) |
|---|---|
| Intermediate goods (to avoid double counting) | Unpaid work at home and volunteering (nonmarket activity) |
| Used goods (not new production) | Unreported and illegal market activity (the informal economy) |
| Shares, bonds and other financial transactions (no production) | Leisure time |
| Transfer payments (nothing produced in return) | Damage to the environment and use of natural resources |

The left column is how GDP *should* work. The right column is where GDP falls short.

## The main limitations

### 1. Nonmarket transactions

GDP counts only goods and services that are **bought and sold in markets**. A great deal of production never passes through a market:

- cooking, cleaning and caring for children or elderly relatives at home;
- growing vegetables in your own garden or repairing your own house;
- volunteering for a charity or community group.

This output is real, but no price is recorded, so GDP leaves it out. Two problems follow.

- GDP **understates** total production, more so in countries where more work is done at home.
- When work moves **from the home into the market**, measured GDP rises even if total production hardly changes. For example, if a family starts paying a cleaner to do work it used to do itself, the cleaner's pay is added to GDP, but the cleaning was being done before.

### 2. The informal (underground) economy

Some market transactions are deliberately **hidden** from the government: cash payments not declared to avoid tax, unregistered businesses, and illegal goods and services. Because they are not reported, they are mostly missing from official GDP. The informal economy is larger in some countries than in others, which makes comparisons between countries less reliable.

### 3. Leisure

GDP does not value **free time**. If people work longer hours, GDP rises, even though they have less time for rest, family and friends. If a country chooses a shorter working week, GDP may fall even though many people feel better off.

### 4. The environment and natural resources

GDP counts the value of output but does not subtract the **damage** caused by producing it, such as air and water pollution, or the **using up of natural resources**, such as forests and fish stocks. Worse, spending to **repair damage** is counted as new output. Clean-up after a chemical spill, or rebuilding after a flood, adds to GDP even though people are no better off than before the disaster.

### 5. What is produced, and its quality

GDP adds up the money value of output. It does not say whether the output makes life better. VD 1 billion spent on prisons and security counts the same as VD 1 billion spent on schools. GDP also captures **quality improvements** and **new products** poorly: a phone that costs the same as last year's model but does far more adds little to GDP.

### 6. How income is shared

GDP is a **total**. It says nothing about the **distribution of income**. GDP can rise while most of the gain goes to a small group, so a typical household may not be better off.

### 7. Population and prices

- **Population.** A large country produces more in total even if each person has little. Divide by population to get **GDP per person (GDP per capita)**:

**GDP per person = GDP ÷ population**

- **Prices.** Nominal GDP rises when prices rise even if output does not. Adjusting for prices gives real GDP, covered in Topic 2.6.

### Measures that look beyond GDP (background)

Because of these limits, economists also use other indicators. For example, the United Nations Development Programme publishes the **Human Development Index**, which combines income per person with measures of health (life expectancy) and education. You will not be asked to calculate it.

## Worked example 1: total GDP or GDP per person?

**Question.** Valdoria and Kestria are fictional countries. Kestria's figures have been converted into valdas (VD).

| Country | GDP | Population |
|---|---|---|
| Valdoria | VD 1,000 billion | 40 million |
| Kestria | VD 1,800 billion | 120 million |

(a) Which economy produces more? (b) Calculate GDP per person in each country. In which country is average income higher? (c) Next year Valdoria's GDP grows by 3% and its population by 4%. What happens to GDP per person?

**(a)** Kestria. Its GDP is 1,800 ÷ 1,000 = **1.8 times** Valdoria's.

**(b)** Change both to the same units first: 1 billion = 1,000 million.

- Valdoria: 1,000,000 million ÷ 40 million = **VD 25,000 per person**
- Kestria: 1,800,000 million ÷ 120 million = **VD 15,000 per person**

Average income is **higher in Valdoria**, about 1.67 times Kestria's, even though Kestria's total GDP is larger.

**(c)** New GDP = 1,000 × 1.03 = VD 1,030 billion. New population = 40 × 1.04 = 41.6 million. GDP per person = 1,030,000 ÷ 41.6 = **VD 24,759.62**. That is a **fall of 0.96%**, even though GDP grew.

**Check.** A quick estimate is 3% − 4% = −1%, close to the exact −0.96%. Output grew more slowly than the number of people sharing it.

**Interpretation.** GDP per person is a better guide to the *average* standard of living than total GDP. It is still an average: it does not show how income is shared, how long people work, or the value of home production in either country.

## Worked example 2: does measured GDP track well-being?

**Question.** Three events happen in Valdoria in 2026. For each, state the effect on measured GDP and explain whether GDP gives a fair picture of what happened to well-being.

(a) A storm destroys a road bridge. The government spends VD 300 million rebuilding it.
(b) 10,000 volunteers each give 100 hours to teach adults to read. Similar paid tutoring costs VD 12 an hour.
(c) A new law shortens the working week. Output falls by VD 5 billion, but each worker gains several hours of free time a week.

**(a)** Measured GDP **rises by VD 300 million** (government purchases). But the rebuilding only restores a bridge that already existed. Valdorians are not better off than before the storm, and they lost the use of the bridge while it was rebuilt. GDP **overstates** the gain in well-being.

**(b)** The volunteering is **not counted** in GDP because no payment is made. Its value at market prices is 10,000 × 100 × VD 12 = **VD 12 million**. GDP **understates** the economy's true output by this amount, and the newly literate adults gain far more over their lives.

**(c)** Measured GDP **falls by VD 5 billion**. GDP counts the lost output but gives no value to the extra leisure. Whether people are better or worse off depends on how much they value the free time. GDP alone cannot tell you.

**Takeaway.** A rise in GDP is not always good news, and a fall is not always bad news. Always ask *why* GDP changed.

## Common misconceptions

- **"GDP measures happiness or well-being."** GDP measures market output. Well-being also depends on leisure, health, the environment and how income is shared.
- **"Leaving out transfer payments is a limitation."** It is correct accounting; nothing is produced. The same is true of intermediate goods, used goods and financial transactions.
- **"Unpaid work is not production."** Cooking, caring and volunteering produce real services. GDP misses them because they have no market price.
- **"Higher GDP means people are better off."** Not if population grew faster, if the extra spending repaired damage, or if most of the gain went to a few people.
- **"The bigger economy has the higher living standard."** Compare GDP per person, not total GDP.
- **"Spending on cleaning up pollution is subtracted from GDP."** It is added to GDP. The pollution damage itself is not subtracted.
- **"The informal economy means illegal goods only."** It also includes legal work that is hidden from the tax authorities, such as undeclared cash jobs.
- **Dividing billions by millions without converting.** VD 480 billion ÷ 12 million is VD 40,000 per person, not VD 40.
