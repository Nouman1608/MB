## What demand means

In everyday speech, "demand" just means wanting something. In economics it means more than that. **Demand** is the quantity of a good or service that buyers are **willing and able** to buy at each possible price, in a given period, with everything else held constant.

Three parts of this definition matter:

- **Willing and able.** Wanting a car is not demand if you cannot pay for it. Demand needs both the wish and the money.
- **At each possible price.** Demand is a whole list of price–quantity pairs, not one number.
- **Other things held constant.** Economists say *ceteris paribus* (Latin for "other things equal"). We change the price and keep income, tastes and all other influences fixed.

Two terms you must keep apart:

- **Quantity demanded:** the amount buyers want at **one particular price**.
- **Demand:** the **whole relationship** between price and quantity demanded, shown as a schedule or a curve.

## The law of demand

The **law of demand** says that, other things equal, when the price of a good rises, the quantity demanded falls, and when the price falls, the quantity demanded rises. Price and quantity demanded have an **inverse** (negative) relationship.

Why does this happen? You only need the direction for this course, but these reasons help you explain it:

- **Substitution:** when a good becomes more expensive, buyers switch to other goods that now look cheaper by comparison.
- **Purchasing power:** a higher price means a buyer's income buys less, so they tend to buy less of the good.
- **Extra satisfaction falls:** each extra unit usually adds less satisfaction than the one before, so buyers will buy more units only if the price is lower.

### Demand schedule and demand curve

A **demand schedule** is a table of prices and quantities demanded. A **demand curve** plots the same data on a graph:

- **Price** goes on the **vertical axis**.
- **Quantity** goes on the **horizontal axis**.
- The curve is labelled **D**.

Because price and quantity demanded move in opposite directions, the demand curve **slopes downward** from left to right. Economists still call it a "curve" even when it is drawn as a straight line.

## Our fictional market: mango juice in Valdoria

**Valdoria** is a fictional country. Its currency is the **valda (VD)**. We look at the weekly market for bottles of mango juice in one Valdorian town. The town has two neighbourhoods, North and South. All numbers are fictional.

| Price per bottle | North: quantity demanded | South: quantity demanded |
|---|---|---|
| VD 2 | 600 | 400 |
| VD 3 | 480 | 320 |
| VD 4 | 360 | 240 |
| VD 5 | 240 | 160 |
| VD 6 | 120 | 80 |

## Worked example 1: market demand and a movement along the curve

**Question.** (a) Build the town's market demand schedule. (b) Show that it obeys the law of demand. (c) The price rises from VD 3 to VD 5. What happens, and how is it shown on the graph?

**Step 1: add across at each price.** Market demand is the sum of the quantities that all buyers demand **at the same price**. You add the quantities, not the prices.

| Price | North | South | Market quantity demanded |
|---|---|---|---|
| VD 2 | 600 | 400 | 600 + 400 = **1,000** |
| VD 3 | 480 | 320 | 480 + 320 = **800** |
| VD 4 | 360 | 240 | 360 + 240 = **600** |
| VD 5 | 240 | 160 | 240 + 160 = **400** |
| VD 6 | 120 | 80 | 120 + 80 = **200** |

On a graph this means adding each neighbourhood's curve **sideways** (horizontally), at each price.

**Step 2: check the law of demand.** Each time the price rises by VD 1, the market quantity demanded falls by 200 bottles. Price and quantity demanded move in opposite directions, so the curve slopes downward.

**Step 3: the price change.** When the price rises from VD 3 to VD 5, the quantity demanded falls from **800** to **400** bottles a week, a fall of 400 bottles.

**Interpretation.** Only the price of mango juice changed. Buyers' incomes, tastes and the other influences did not. So the demand curve does **not** move. Buyers move **along** the curve from point K to point M in Figure 1. This is a **decrease in quantity demanded**, not a decrease in demand.

## Change in quantity demanded v. change in demand

This is the distinction that costs most marks in this unit.

| | Change in **quantity demanded** | Change in **demand** |
|---|---|---|
| Caused by | A change in the good's **own price** | A change in a **determinant** (anything except the good's own price) |
| On the graph | **Movement along** the existing curve | The **whole curve shifts** |
| Words to use | "Quantity demanded rises/falls" | "Demand increases/decreases" |

An **increase in demand** means buyers want **more at every price**: the curve shifts **right**. A **decrease in demand** means they want **less at every price**: the curve shifts **left**.

## The determinants of demand

Each determinant below shifts the market demand curve. The direction depends on what changes.

- **Consumer income.**
  - For a **normal good**, higher income increases demand (shift right). Most goods are normal.
  - For an **inferior good**, higher income decreases demand (shift left), because buyers switch to goods they prefer. Examples might be the cheapest instant noodles or long-distance bus travel for people who can now afford to fly.
  - Whether a good is normal or inferior depends on how buyers behave, not on its quality.
- **Prices of related goods.**
  - **Substitutes** are used in place of each other (mango juice and orange juice). If the price of a substitute **rises**, demand for this good **increases**.
  - **Complements** are used together (printers and ink cartridges). If the price of a complement **rises**, demand for this good **decreases**.
- **Tastes and preferences.** Advertising, fashion or new health information that makes a good more popular increases demand.
- **Expectations.** If buyers expect the price to **rise** soon, or (for a normal good) expect their income to rise, demand **today** increases. If they expect the price to fall soon, they wait, and demand today decreases.
- **Number of buyers.** More buyers in the market (from population growth or new customers) increase market demand. Fewer buyers decrease it.

| Event | Demand for good X | Curve |
|---|---|---|
| Income rises, X is normal | Increases | Right |
| Income rises, X is inferior | Decreases | Left |
| Price of a substitute for X rises | Increases | Right |
| Price of a complement of X rises | Decreases | Left |
| X becomes more fashionable | Increases | Right |
| Buyers expect X's price to rise next month | Increases now | Right |
| Number of buyers falls | Decreases | Left |
| Price of X itself rises | No change in demand; quantity demanded falls | Movement along |

## Worked example 2: shifting the demand curve

**Question.** Start from the town's market demand in Worked example 1 (call it D₁). Mango juice is a normal good.

(a) Average incomes in the town rise. At every price, buyers now want 200 more bottles a week. Find the new schedule, D₂, and the change at a price of VD 4.
(b) Separately, the price of orange juice, a substitute, falls. At every price, buyers now want 200 fewer bottles of mango juice. Find the new schedule, D₃.
(c) On D₂, at what price would buyers want only 600 bottles?

**Step 1: the new schedules.**

| Price | D₁ (original) | D₂ (incomes rise) | D₃ (orange juice cheaper) |
|---|---|---|---|
| VD 2 | 1,000 | 1,200 | 800 |
| VD 3 | 800 | 1,000 | 600 |
| VD 4 | 600 | 800 | 400 |
| VD 5 | 400 | 600 | 200 |
| VD 6 | 200 | 400 | 0 |

**(a)** At VD 4, the quantity demanded rises from **600** to **800** bottles. The price did not change, so this is an **increase in demand**: D₁ shifts **right** to D₂. Mango juice is normal, so higher income raises demand.

**(b)** Orange juice is now relatively cheaper, so some buyers switch to it. Demand for mango juice **decreases**: D₁ shifts **left** to D₃. At VD 4, quantity demanded falls from 600 to **400**.

**(c)** On D₂, buyers want 600 bottles at **VD 5**. Before the income rise they wanted 600 bottles at VD 4. So after an increase in demand, buyers are willing to pay a **higher price for the same quantity**. This is another way to read a rightward shift: the curve also lies **higher** at every quantity.

**Check.** Each new schedule still obeys the law of demand. Within D₂ or D₃, a higher price still means a lower quantity demanded. Only the position of the curve has changed.

## Common misconceptions

- **"The price fell, so demand increased."** No. A fall in the good's own price increases the **quantity demanded**. It is a movement along the curve.
- **"Demand increased, so the curve moves up the line."** An increase in demand moves the **whole curve right**. It is not a movement along it.
- **Adding prices when finding market demand.** Add the **quantities** at the same price. Prices stay as they are.
- **"A rise in the price of a substitute decreases demand."** It **increases** demand for this good, because buyers switch towards it.
- **Mixing up substitutes and complements.** Ask: are the goods used **instead of** each other (substitutes) or **together** (complements)?
- **"Higher income always increases demand."** Only for **normal** goods. For inferior goods, demand decreases.
- **"Expecting a higher price later means people buy less now."** They usually buy **more** now, before the price rises.
- **"Demand shifted right, so the price rose."** In this topic you only know that buyers want more at every price. What happens to the market price needs supply as well (Topics 1.5 and 1.6).
- **Unlabelled graphs.** Always label the axes (Price, Quantity), the curves (D₁, D₂) and use an arrow to show the direction of a shift.
