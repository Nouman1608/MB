## Recap

- **Absolute advantage:** a producer makes **more** of a good than another producer with the same resources.
- **Comparative advantage:** a producer makes a good at a **lower opportunity cost** than another producer.
- A producer can have an absolute advantage in both goods, but **not** a comparative advantage in both (two goods, two producers).
- Each side should **specialise** in its comparative-advantage good and **trade** for the other.
- With mutually beneficial **terms of trade**, both sides **consume outside their own PPC**. The PPCs themselves do not move.

## Key relationships

| Data type | Absolute advantage | Opportunity cost of 1 unit of X |
|---|---|---|
| Output (units made with all resources, or PPC intercepts) | **larger** number | max Y ÷ max X (other good on top) |
| Input (hours or resources per unit) | **smaller** number | hours per X ÷ hours per Y (same good on top) |

| Idea | Rule |
|---|---|
| Comparative advantage | lower opportunity cost |
| Mutually beneficial terms for 1 X | more than the seller's opportunity cost, less than the buyer's |
| Gain per unit traded | buyer: own cost − price paid; seller: price received − own cost |
| Straight-line PPC | constant opportunity cost = size of the slope |

## Assumptions behind the numbers

- Two producers, two goods, straight-line PPCs (constant opportunity cost).
- Both producers have the same quantity of resources (or the same time) in output tables.
- No transport costs or trade barriers.
- Equal opportunity costs mean no comparative advantage and no gains from trade.

## Mistakes to avoid

1. **Deciding comparative advantage by who makes more.** That is absolute advantage.
2. **Using the output rule on input data**, or the reverse.
3. **Writing the opportunity cost upside down.** Say: "1 X costs ___ Y."
4. **Choosing terms outside the range**, or exactly equal to one opportunity cost (no gain for that side).
5. **Saying trade shifts the PPC.** Trade changes consumption, not production possibilities.
6. **Claiming one producer has a comparative advantage in both goods.**

## Quick self-check

1. Country A can make 50 shirts or 25 bags; country B can make 30 shirts or 10 bags. What is each country's opportunity cost of 1 bag? *(A: 2 shirts; B: 3 shirts)*
2. Who has the comparative advantage in bags, and in shirts? *(A in bags; B in shirts)*
3. State the range of mutually beneficial terms for 1 bag. *(more than 2 but fewer than 3 shirts)*
4. A potter needs 2 hours per pot and 4 hours per vase. What is her opportunity cost of 1 vase? *(2 pots)*
