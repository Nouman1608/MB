## Recap

- A **financial asset** is a claim to future payments. The three classes in this topic are **money**, **bonds** and **stocks**.
- The most liquid forms of money are **cash** and **demand deposits** (checking accounts).
- A **bond** is an interest-bearing loan to a government or firm. A **stock** is a share of ownership (**equity**).
- **Liquidity:** how easily an asset can be spent or turned into money without losing value. **Rate of return:** the gain as a percentage of the cost. **Risk:** the chance the return differs from what you expected, including losses.
- More liquid assets pay less. Higher expected returns usually come with more risk.
- The interest on an existing bond is fixed, so its **price** adjusts when market rates change.
- The **opportunity cost of holding money** is the interest forgone on other assets, such as bonds.

## Key relationships

| Relationship | Statement or formula | Use it to |
|---|---|---|
| Liquidity ranking | cash, demand deposits (highest) → bonds, stocks | compare assets |
| Return and risk | higher expected return ↔ higher risk | explain why people hold several assets |
| Bond price and interest rate | inverse: market rate ↑ ⇒ price of existing bonds ↓ | predict capital gains and losses |
| One-year bond price | payment at maturity ÷ (1 + market rate) | calculate a bond price |
| Opportunity cost of money | amount held as money × interest rate on bonds | find the cost of holding money |
| Interest rate ↑ | holding money costs more ⇒ people hold less money | link to money demand (Topic 4.5) |

## Assumptions behind the numbers

- Cash and demand deposits pay no interest unless the question says otherwise. If they pay some, the opportunity cost is the **difference** in interest.
- The bond issuer is expected to pay in full; the price changes come from market interest rates.
- Bond calculations use one year to maturity so that one division gives the price.

## Mistakes to avoid

1. Saying bond prices and interest rates move **together**. They move in opposite directions.
2. Thinking the **interest payment** on an existing bond changes. The price changes.
3. Calling a stock a **loan**. It is ownership.
4. Giving **inflation** as the opportunity cost of holding money. The answer is interest forgone.
5. Mixing up **liquidity** with return or safety.
6. Forgetting that a bondholder who sells after rates rise takes a **capital loss**.

## Quick self-check

VD is the valda, the currency of the fictional country Valdoria used in the study guide.

1. A bond will pay VD 840 in one year. The market interest rate is 5%. What is its price today? *(VD 800)*
2. Market interest rates fall. What happens to the price of a previously issued bond? *(It rises.)*
3. You keep VD 12,000 as cash when bonds pay 3%. What is the yearly opportunity cost? *(VD 360)*
4. Which is more liquid: a demand deposit or a share of stock? *(The demand deposit.)*
