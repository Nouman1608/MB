## Inflation as a monetary event

In Topic 2.4 you learned to measure inflation. In this topic you learn what causes inflation that **lasts**.

A single shock can push the price level up once. A bad harvest or a jump in oil prices raises costs, and prices rise. But a shock like this does not keep prices rising year after year. For the price level to keep rising, something must keep raising aggregate demand. In the long run, the only thing that can do that without limit is **money**.

The main idea of this topic:

- **Inflation** results when the money supply grows **too fast** for a **long time**. "Too fast" means faster than the economy's ability to produce more goods and services.
- **Deflation** results when the money supply **falls** (or grows too slowly) for a long time.

This is why economists call inflation a **monetary phenomenon**. Too much money chasing the same amount of goods bids up their prices.

## The equation of exchange

Every purchase of a final good or service uses money. Count the spending in two ways.

- **Money side:** the money supply (**M**) multiplied by the number of times each unit of money is spent in a year. That number is the **velocity of money (V)**.
- **Goods side:** the price level (**P**) multiplied by the quantity of real output (**Y**), real GDP. P × Y is **nominal GDP**.

Both sides measure the same total spending, so:

**M × V = P × Y**

This is the **equation of exchange**. Some books write Q instead of Y for real output; the meaning is the same.

What each letter means:

| Symbol | Name | What it measures | Example unit |
|---|---|---|---|
| M | Money supply | The stock of money in the economy | VD billion |
| V | Velocity of money | Times an average valda is spent on final output in a year | times per year (no unit) |
| P | Price level | A price index ÷ 100, for example the GDP deflator | 1.20 means an index of 120 |
| Y | Real output | Real GDP | VD billion at base-year prices |

Rearrange the equation to find any one letter:

- **V = (P × Y) ÷ M** (velocity = nominal GDP ÷ money supply)
- **P = (M × V) ÷ Y**
- **Y = (M × V) ÷ P**
- **M = (P × Y) ÷ V**

The equation is always true, because velocity is **defined** as nominal GDP ÷ M. On its own, it says nothing about cause and effect. For that we need the quantity theory.

## The quantity theory of money

The **quantity theory of money** adds two assumptions to the equation of exchange:

1. **Velocity is stable.** People's habits of paying and holding money change slowly, so V is roughly constant (or changes in a predictable way).
2. **Real output is set by real resources.** In the long run, Y is at full employment. It depends on labour, capital, natural resources and technology, not on the amount of money. This is the vertical LRAS curve from Topic 3.4.

If V and Y do not change, the equation leaves only one way for M × V = P × Y to stay true when M changes: **P must change by the same percentage as M.**

**Quantity theory: over many years, the speed at which money grows sets the inflation rate.**

When the economy is at full employment, a change in the money supply has **no effect on real output in the long run**. Economists call this the **long-run neutrality of money**. Money changes nominal values (prices, nominal wages, nominal GDP), not real ones.

## Showing it on an AD–AS graph

The quantity theory describes the long run. The AD–AS model shows how the economy gets there.

Valdoria is the fictional country used across the Marlbridge macroeconomics pages. Its currency is the valda (VD). As in Topic 3.4, its full-employment output is **Yf = VD 840 billion**.

Start at point A: long-run equilibrium at Yf, with a price level of 120. Valdoria's central bank then raises the money supply by 12.5%.

1. **Short run (A to B).** More money lowers interest rates and raises spending, so **AD shifts right** (AD₁ to AD₂). Nominal wages are still fixed, so firms produce more. Real GDP rises above Yf and the price level rises.
2. **Adjustment (B to C).** Output is above full employment, so labour is scarce. Workers see that prices have risen and demand higher nominal wages. As input prices rise, **SRAS shifts left** (SRAS₁ to SRAS₂).
3. **Long run (C).** The economy returns to **Yf = VD 840 billion**, at a price level of **135**. Real output is unchanged. The price level is 12.5% higher, the same percentage as the rise in M.

If the central bank raises the money supply **again and again**, AD keeps shifting right and SRAS keeps shifting left behind it. Output stays at Yf in the long run, but the price level climbs every year. That is ongoing inflation. A **sustained fall** in the money supply works the other way: AD keeps shifting left, nominal wages eventually fall, and the price level falls. That is deflation.

## Worked example 1: using MV = PY

**Question.** In 2026 Valdoria's money supply is VD 240 billion and its nominal GDP is VD 1,008 billion. Real GDP is at full employment, VD 840 billion.

(a) Calculate the velocity of money.
(b) Calculate the price level.
(c) The central bank raises the money supply to VD 270 billion. Velocity does not change and, in the long run, real GDP stays at full employment. Calculate the new price level and the percentage changes in M and P.

**(a)** V = nominal GDP ÷ M = 1,008 ÷ 240 = **4.2**. Each valda is spent on final output 4.2 times a year.

**(b)** P = (M × V) ÷ Y = (240 × 4.2) ÷ 840 = 1,008 ÷ 840 = **1.20**. As an index, the price level is **120**.

**(c)** New P = (270 × 4.2) ÷ 840 = 1,134 ÷ 840 = **1.35** (index **135**).

- % change in M = (270 − 240) ÷ 240 × 100 = **12.5%**
- % change in P = (1.35 − 1.20) ÷ 1.20 × 100 = **12.5%**
- Nominal GDP rises from VD 1,008 billion to VD 1,134 billion. Real GDP stays at VD 840 billion.

**Interpretation.** With V and Y fixed, the price level rises by exactly the same percentage as the money supply. These are points A and C in Figure 1.

**Check.** The same idea in a larger case: if M doubled from 240 to 480, M × V would double from 1,008 to 2,016, and P would double from 1.20 to 2.40. Real output would still be 840. Doubling the money supply only doubles the prices.

## The quantity theory in growth rates

Exam questions often give growth rates instead of levels. Taking the percentage change of each side of MV = PY gives a useful approximation:

**% change in M + % change in V ≈ % change in P + % change in Y**

The % change in P is the **inflation rate**. Rearranged:

**Inflation rate ≈ % change in M + % change in V − % change in Y**

If velocity is constant (0% change):

**Inflation rate ≈ money growth − real GDP growth**

So money can grow at the same rate as real output without causing inflation. The extra money is needed to buy the extra goods. Inflation comes from money growth **beyond** output growth.

This rule is an approximation. It is very close when the rates are small. For an exact answer, work out the levels as in Worked example 2.

## Worked example 2: money growth with a growing economy

**Question.** Start from Valdoria in Worked example 1 (M = 240, V = 4.2, Y = 840, P = 1.20). Over the next year the money supply grows by 9%. Velocity stays at 4.2. Investment in new capital means full-employment real GDP grows by 3%.

(a) Use the growth-rate rule to estimate the inflation rate.
(b) Calculate the exact new price level and inflation rate.
(c) Roughly what rate of money growth would give 2% inflation?

**(a)** Inflation ≈ 9% + 0% − 3% = **6%**.

**(b)** New M = 240 × 1.09 = VD 261.6 billion. New Y = 840 × 1.03 = VD 865.2 billion.

New P = (261.6 × 4.2) ÷ 865.2 = 1,098.72 ÷ 865.2 = **1.2699** (index about 127.0).

Inflation = (1.2699 − 1.20) ÷ 1.20 × 100 = **5.83%**.

The estimate of 6% is close to the exact 5.83%. The small gap is because growth rates multiply rather than add: 1.09 ÷ 1.03 = 1.0583.

**(c)** Inflation ≈ money growth − 3%, so 2% inflation needs money growth of about 2% + 3% = **5%**. (The exact figure is 1.02 × 1.03 − 1 = 5.06%.)

**Interpretation.** In the long run, the central bank's choice of money growth sets the inflation rate. Real growth depends on real resources, so the bank cannot raise long-run output by printing faster. Faster money growth only means faster inflation.

## When velocity changes

The quantity theory says velocity is **stable**, not that it can never change. If people start paying faster (for example, they hold less cash because they expect prices to rise), V rises. With M and Y unchanged, P must then rise. If V falls, the same money supports less spending and prices rise more slowly or fall.

In a calculation, never assume V is constant if the question gives you a change in V. Use the full growth-rate rule, or work with levels.

## Common misconceptions

- **"M × V = P × Y proves that money causes inflation."** The equation is always true by definition. The causal claim comes from the quantity theory's assumptions: stable V and Y fixed at full employment.
- **"More money makes a country richer in the long run."** At full employment, extra money raises prices, not real output. Real GDP depends on resources and technology.
- **"Any rise in the price level is inflation caused by money."** A one-time supply shock can raise prices once. Inflation that **continues** needs continuing money growth.
- **"Money growth of 5% always means 5% inflation."** Only if V and real output are constant. With real growth of 3% and constant V, inflation is about 2%.
- **"Money has no effect on real output at all."** In the **short run** it can raise output (A to B in Figure 1). Neutrality is a **long-run** result.
- **"Velocity is the amount of money spent."** Velocity is a number of times per year (nominal GDP ÷ M), not an amount of money.
- **"P in the equation is the inflation rate."** P is the price **level**. The inflation rate is the percentage change in P.
- **Forgetting nominal GDP is P × Y.** If a question gives nominal GDP, you already have the right-hand side of the equation.
