## Understanding

- I can explain what rotational inertia measures and why it depends on both mass and the distribution of mass about the axis. *(Guide: "Resistance to changes in rotation")*
- I can explain why a hoop has twice the rotational inertia of a disk with the same mass and radius. *(Guide: "Why a hoop beats a disk"; Practice Q3)*
- I can explain why I is smallest about an axis through the centre of mass. *(Guide: "The parallel axis theorem"; Worked example 1)*

## Calculation and derivation

- I can calculate I = Σmr² for small objects, using the perpendicular distance to the axis. *(Guide: Worked example 1; Practice Q1)*
- I can derive I for a uniform rod about any perpendicular axis by integration, including ML²/12 and ML²/3. *(Guide: "Deriving the standard results"; Practice Q4)*
- I can derive I for a rod with a nonuniform λ(x), and find its centre of mass. *(Guide: Worked example 2; Practice Q7)*
- I can derive I for a thin cylindrical shell, a disk and an annular ring by adding coaxial rings, and check the limiting cases. *(Guide: "Deriving the standard results"; Practice Q5)*
- I can use I = I_cm + Md², always starting from the centre-of-mass axis. *(Guide: Worked example 2; Practice Q2, Q7)*

## Graphs and reasoning

- I can compare rotational inertias of objects with the same mass but different mass distributions without calculating. *(Guide: Figure 2, Worked example 3; Practice Q3)*
- I can linearise data with a graph of I against r² and read the mass and the fixed part of I from it. *(Guide: "Linking to experiments"; Practice Q6)*
- I can explain why a mistaken use of the parallel axis theorem between two off-centre axes gives the wrong answer. *(Practice Q7(d))*
