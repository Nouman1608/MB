These are **original Marlbridge practice questions**, not past exam questions. The mark points are a suggested Marlbridge rubric to help you check your work; they are not an official scoring guideline. Data: C 12.01, O 16.00 g mol⁻¹.

## Question 1 (multiple choice · foundation)

Which of the following is a chemical change?

- (A) Dry ice (solid carbon dioxide) turns directly into a gas
- (B) Sugar dissolves in a cup of water
- (C) A slice of bread turns brown and crisp in a toaster
- (D) Salt is recovered by evaporating sea water

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (C).** Toasting forms new brown compounds with a different taste and smell from the starting bread. Cooling the toast does not turn it back into bread.

- (A) is sublimation, a change of phase. The gas is still CO₂.
- (B) makes a mixture. Evaporating the water returns the sugar.
- (D) separates a mixture. The salt was in the sea water all along.
</details>

## Question 2 (multiple choice · core)

A student mixes two colourless solutions in a beaker. Which observation is the strongest evidence that a chemical change has occurred?

- (A) The mixture is colourless
- (B) A few small bubbles appear on the walls of the beaker as it stands in a warm room
- (C) A yellow solid forms and settles to the bottom
- (D) The temperature of the mixture rises by 0.2 °C

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (C).** A solid appearing when two solutions are mixed is a precipitate: an insoluble substance that was not present in either solution. It is also a new colour.

- (A) is no evidence either way. Many reactions, and many mixings with no reaction, give a colourless mixture.
- (B) describes dissolved air coming out of solution as the liquid warms, a physical change.
- (D) is a very small temperature change. Mixing or diluting solutions can cause a change this size with no reaction, so it is much weaker evidence than a new solid.
</details>

## Question 3 (multiple choice · core)

Four particle diagrams are described below. A and B are different elements. Which one shows a chemical change?

- (A) Before: 6 AB molecules packed closely. After: the same 6 AB molecules spread far apart.
- (B) Before: 4 A₂ molecules in the left half of a box and 4 B₂ molecules in the right half. After: the same 8 molecules evenly mixed.
- (C) Before: 4 A₂ molecules and 2 B₂ molecules. After: 4 A₂B molecules.
- (D) Before: 6 A atoms in a regular pattern. After: 6 A atoms in an irregular arrangement, still touching.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (C).** The A–A and B–B bonds break and new A–B bonds form, making a new substance, A₂B. Atoms are conserved: 8 A and 4 B before; 4 × 2 = 8 A and 4 × 1 = 4 B after.

- (A) is a liquid boiling (or a gas expanding). The molecules are unchanged.
- (B) is mixing two gases. No new molecules form.
- (D) is a solid melting. The atoms are the same; only their arrangement changes.
</details>

## Question 4 (constructed response · foundation)

Classify each change as physical or chemical. Give one piece of evidence and link it to whether a new substance forms.

(a) A puddle of water disappears on a sunny day.
(b) An antacid tablet dropped into water fizzes; the gas turns limewater cloudy.
(c) Green nickel(II) chloride solution is mixed with colourless sodium hydroxide solution and a pale green solid forms.
(d) Ethanol and water are mixed; the mixture warms slightly and stays clear and colourless.

<details>
<summary>Worked solution and suggested Marlbridge rubric</summary>

**(a) Physical.** Evaporation is a change of phase; the water vapour is still H₂O.
**(b) Chemical.** The gas is carbon dioxide (limewater test), which was not present as a substance in the tablet or the water, so a new substance has formed.
**(c) Chemical.** A precipitate forms: a solid that was not present in either solution (nickel(II) hydroxide). The colour is similar, so the solid, not the colour, is the evidence.
**(d) Physical.** Mixing two liquids makes a solution. The small temperature rise is not enough on its own: no new substance is detected, and the ethanol can be separated by distillation.

| Point | What earns it |
|---|---|
| 1 | (a) physical, with a phase-change reason |
| 1 | (b) chemical, linking the identified gas to a new substance |
| 1 | (c) chemical, using the precipitate as the evidence |
| 1 | (d) physical, explaining why the temperature change does not decide it |

No point for a classification without a reason.
</details>

## Question 5 (constructed response · core)

A student mixes 25.0 mL of 1.0 mol L⁻¹ hydrochloric acid with 25.0 mL of 1.0 mol L⁻¹ sodium hydroxide solution. Both solutions are colourless and start at 21.2 °C. The products are sodium chloride and water.

(a) Predict what the student will see by eye when the solutions are mixed.
(b) The mixture reaches 28.0 °C. Calculate the temperature change.
(c) The student writes: "I saw nothing happen, so there was no reaction." Evaluate this claim.
(d) Suggest one further test that would show a new substance has formed.

<details>
<summary>Worked solution and suggested Marlbridge rubric</summary>

**(a)** Nothing visible: the mixture stays clear and colourless, with no bubbles and no solid.
**(b)** ΔT = 28.0 − 21.2 = **+6.8 °C** (a rise).
**(c)** The claim is **not supported**. A reaction does not have to be visible. The 6.8 °C rise is evidence that energy was released when the solutions were mixed, which suggests a new substance formed.
**(d)** Any one: test the pH of the mixture and show it is close to neutral, unlike either starting solution; or evaporate the water and show that the white solid left is sodium chloride, a substance that was not in either starting solution.

| Point | What earns it |
|---|---|
| 1 | Predicts no visible change |
| 1 | +6.8 °C with the sign or the word "rise" |
| 1 | Rejects the claim, using the temperature data as evidence |
| 1 | A valid test that targets a new substance (pH or recovered NaCl) |

For (c), accept a cautious answer that says the temperature rise strongly suggests a reaction and (d) would confirm it.
</details>

## Question 6 (constructed response · core)

A student adds a spoonful of grey zinc powder to a blue solution of copper(II) sulfate and stirs. Zinc sulfate solution is colourless. The reaction is Zn(s) + CuSO₄(aq) → ZnSO₄(aq) + Cu(s).

(a) Predict **three** observations the student could make.
(b) Explain how each observation supports the claim that a chemical change has occurred.

<details>
<summary>Worked solution and suggested Marlbridge rubric</summary>

**(a)** Any three:
- the blue colour of the solution fades and the solution can become colourless;
- a red-brown (pinkish) solid, copper, appears;
- the grey zinc powder gets less as it is used up;
- the mixture gets warm.

**(b)** The fading blue shows that copper(II) ions are being used up and replaced by colourless zinc ions. The red-brown solid is a new substance (copper metal) that was not present at the start. Zinc being used up shows a starting substance has changed into something else. Warming shows energy is released as new substances form, though on its own this would be weak evidence.

| Point | What earns it |
|---|---|
| 1 | Colour of solution fades |
| 1 | Red-brown or pink-brown solid forms |
| 1 | A third valid observation (zinc used up, or temperature rise) |
| 1 | Links at least two observations to a new substance forming |

Do not award "the solution turns a different colour" without saying it fades or loses its blue.
</details>

## Question 7 (constructed response · stretch)

Two identical flasks each hold excess dilute hydrochloric acid and some marble chips (calcium carbonate). The marble chips react, giving carbon dioxide gas. Flask A is left open. Flask B has a balloon fixed over its neck. Each flask is weighed before and after the reaction; ignore any buoyancy effect of the balloon.

| | Before (g) | After (g) |
|---|---|---|
| Flask A (open) | 85.20 | 84.76 |
| Flask B (balloon) | 112.58 | 112.58 |

(a) Calculate the change in mass of Flask A.
(b) Calculate the amount, in moles, of carbon dioxide that left Flask A.
(c) A student says: "Flask A shows that atoms are destroyed in some reactions." Evaluate this claim using both sets of data.

<details>
<summary>Worked solution and suggested Marlbridge rubric</summary>

**(a)** 84.76 − 85.20 = **−0.44 g** (a loss of 0.44 g).
**(b)** M(CO₂) = 12.01 + 2(16.00) = 44.01 g mol⁻¹; n = 0.44 g ÷ 44.01 g mol⁻¹ = **0.010 mol** (2 significant figures, because 0.44 g has 2).
**(c)** The claim is **wrong**. Flask B, where the gas cannot escape, shows no change in mass, so the total mass of atoms is the same after the reaction. Flask A loses mass only because the carbon dioxide formed by the reaction escapes into the air. The atoms are rearranged into new substances, not destroyed.

| Point | What earns it |
|---|---|
| 1 | −0.44 g (or "loss of 0.44 g") |
| 1 | 0.010 mol with molar mass shown |
| 1 | Uses Flask B (no change) to show mass is conserved when nothing escapes |
| 1 | Explains Flask A's loss as gas escaping, not atoms being destroyed |

Accept carried-forward errors from (a) in (b).
</details>
