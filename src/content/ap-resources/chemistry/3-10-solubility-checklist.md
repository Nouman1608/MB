Work through the list without notes. If you cannot do a statement, follow its link, then come back. Guide = [study guide](/advanced-course-resources/chemistry/3-10-solubility-study-guide/); Practice = [practice questions](/advanced-course-resources/chemistry/3-10-solubility-practice/).

## Understanding

- I can explain the difference between soluble, miscible and immiscible. *(Guide: "Soluble, insoluble and miscible"; Practice Q1)*
- I can describe dissolving as overcoming solute–solute and solvent–solvent attractions and forming solute–solvent attractions. *(Guide: "Dissolving at the particle level")*
- I can explain why substances with similar intermolecular interactions tend to mix. *(Guide; Practice Q1, Q4)*
- I can explain why nonpolar substances dissolve poorly in water without saying that water repels them. *(Practice Q7)*

## Skills

- I can predict whether a substance dissolves better in water or in hexane, naming the interactions in the solute, the solvent and between them. *(Guide: Worked example 1; Practice Q2, Q4, Q5)*
- I can draw a particulate model of an ionic compound dissolved in water, with the ions separate, in the right ratio, and the water molecules oriented correctly. *(Guide: Figure 1; Practice Q3, Q5)*
- I can read solubility data and describe the trend for a series of molecules with a growing hydrocarbon chain. *(Guide: Figure 2, Worked example 2; Practice Q6)*
- I can use the pattern in a data series to estimate a missing value. *(Practice Q6)*

## Reasoning

- I can explain why a longer hydrocarbon chain lowers solubility in water and raises solubility in nonpolar solvents. *(Guide: Worked example 2; Practice Q6)*
- I can explain why some ionic compounds, such as calcium carbonate, barely dissolve in water, and recall which salts are always soluble. *(Guide: "Not every ionic compound dissolves")*
- I can explain the degree to which the like-dissolves-like model describes real solubility, and what it cannot predict. *(Guide: Worked example 3; Practice Q7)*
