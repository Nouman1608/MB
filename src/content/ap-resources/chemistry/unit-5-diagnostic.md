## What this diagnostic is for

Use this page to find **which Unit 5 topics to revisit**: one question per topic, two for Topic 5.3. Answer every question before opening any answer, then use the table at the end.

These are **original Marlbridge practice questions**, not past exam questions. Species written with letters are fictional and all data are invented. The questions are not calibrated, so your total gives no predicted score: treat each wrong answer as a pointer to a topic.

**Calculator and data.** A scientific calculator with ln is allowed. [A]ₜ − [A]₀ = −kt; ln[A]ₜ − ln[A]₀ = −kt; 1/[A]ₜ − 1/[A]₀ = kt; t½ = 0.693/k. Energies are in kJ mol⁻¹.

## Question 1 (multiple choice · 5.1)

Hydrogen sulfide burns: 2 H₂S(g) + 3 O₂(g) → 2 SO₂(g) + 2 H₂O(g). At one moment, SO₂ is forming at 0.18 M s⁻¹. At what rate is O₂ being used up?

- (A) 0.09 M s⁻¹
- (B) 0.12 M s⁻¹
- (C) 0.18 M s⁻¹
- (D) 0.27 M s⁻¹

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (D).** 3 mol O₂ are used per 2 mol SO₂ formed: 0.18 × 3/2 = 0.27 M s⁻¹.

- (A) is the rate of reaction (0.18 ÷ 2), not the rate for O₂.
- (B) uses the ratio upside down (× 2/3).
- (C) ignores the coefficients.

**If you missed this:** [Topic 5.1 study guide](/advanced-course-resources/chemistry/5-1-reaction-rates-study-guide/): "Rates of different species: use the coefficients".
</details>

## Question 2 (multiple choice · 5.2)

Initial rates for P + Q → R at one temperature:

| Experiment | [P] (M) | [Q] (M) | Initial rate (M s⁻¹) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0.010 | 0.020 | 1.5 × 10⁻⁵ |
| 2 | 0.030 | 0.020 | 1.35 × 10⁻⁴ |
| 3 | 0.030 | 0.040 | 1.35 × 10⁻⁴ |

Which gives the rate law and the rate constant?

- (A) rate = k[P]², k = 0.15 M⁻¹ s⁻¹
- (B) rate = k[P]²[Q], k = 7.5 M⁻² s⁻¹
- (C) rate = k[P], k = 1.5 × 10⁻³ s⁻¹
- (D) rate = k[P]², k = 0.15 s⁻¹

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (A).** Experiments 1 → 2: [P] × 3, rate × 9, so second order in P. Experiments 2 → 3: [Q] × 2, rate unchanged, so zero order in Q. k = 1.5 × 10⁻⁵ ÷ (0.010)² = 0.15 M⁻¹ s⁻¹.

- (B) includes Q, but doubling [Q] had no effect.
- (C) treats a ninefold rise as first order.
- (D) has the wrong units for a second-order k.

**If you missed this:** [Topic 5.2 study guide](/advanced-course-resources/chemistry/5-2-introduction-rate-law-study-guide/): "The method of initial rates" and "The rate constant, k".
</details>

## Question 3 (multiple choice · 5.3)

A reaction is first order in A. [A] falls from 0.600 M to 0.150 M in 25.0 min. What is the rate constant?

- (A) 0.0555 min⁻¹
- (B) 0.0277 min⁻¹
- (C) 0.0180 M min⁻¹
- (D) 0.200 M⁻¹ min⁻¹

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (A).** k = ln(0.600 ÷ 0.150) ÷ 25.0 = 0.0555 min⁻¹. Or: 0.600 → 0.300 → 0.150 M is two half-lives, so t½ = 12.5 min and k = 0.693 ÷ 12.5 = 0.0554 min⁻¹ (the same to rounding).

- (B) treats 25.0 min as one half-life.
- (C) is the zero-order slope, (0.600 − 0.150) ÷ 25.0.
- (D) uses the second-order law.

**If you missed this:** [Topic 5.3 study guide](/advanced-course-resources/chemistry/5-3-concentration-changes-over-time-study-guide/): "Half-life".
</details>

## Question 4 (short answer · 5.3)

Compound A decomposes at constant temperature. The times are **not** equally spaced.

| t (min) | 0 | 10 | 30 | 60 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| [A] (M) | 0.500 | 0.4094 | 0.2744 | 0.1506 |

(a) Show, with calculated values, which plot is linear, and state the order in A.
(b) Find k and the half-life.
(c) How long after the start does [A] reach 0.0500 M?

<details>
<summary>Worked answer</summary>

**(a)** The time steps are unequal, so compare **slopes**.

| Interval (min) | Slope of [A] (M min⁻¹) | Slope of ln[A] (min⁻¹) | Slope of 1/[A] (M⁻¹ min⁻¹) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0–10 | −0.00906 | −0.0200 | 0.0443 |
| 10–30 | −0.00675 | −0.0200 | 0.0601 |
| 30–60 | −0.00413 | −0.0200 | 0.0999 |

Only ln[A] has a constant slope: **first order in A**.

**(b)** Slope = −k, so **k = 0.0200 min⁻¹**. t½ = 0.693 ÷ 0.0200 = **34.7 min**.

**(c)** t = ln(0.500 ÷ 0.0500) ÷ 0.0200 = 2.303 ÷ 0.0200 = **115 min**.

**If you missed this:** [Topic 5.3 study guide](/advanced-course-resources/chemistry/5-3-concentration-changes-over-time-study-guide/): "Testing the data: which plot is straight?" and Worked example 1.
</details>

## Question 5 (multiple choice · 5.4)

An elementary step has a rate constant with units M⁻² s⁻¹ (rate in M s⁻¹). Which could the step be?

- (A) X → products
- (B) X + Y → products
- (C) 2X → products
- (D) 2X + Y → products

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (D).** M⁻² s⁻¹ means third order overall (k × M³ = M s⁻¹). For an elementary step the orders are the coefficients: 2X + Y gives rate = k[X]²[Y].

- (A) is unimolecular: k in s⁻¹.
- (B) and (C) are bimolecular (M⁻¹ s⁻¹).

**If you missed this:** [Topic 5.4 study guide](/advanced-course-resources/chemistry/5-4-elementary-reactions-study-guide/): "Molecularity" and "Where the units of k come from".
</details>

## Question 6 (multiple choice · 5.5)

A gas mixture that reacts in one elementary step is compressed to half its volume at constant temperature. Which is correct?

- (A) Collisions happen more often, and the fraction of collisions with at least Eₐ is unchanged.
- (B) Collisions happen more often, and a larger fraction have at least Eₐ, because particles hit harder.
- (C) The collision frequency is unchanged, but a larger fraction of collisions have at least Eₐ.
- (D) Collisions happen more often, and Eₐ falls.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (A).** Halving the volume doubles every concentration, so particles meet more often. The temperature, and so the fraction beyond Eₐ, is unchanged.

- (B) links concentration to collision energy.
- (C) reverses the two effects.
- (D) Compression does not change Eₐ.

**If you missed this:** [Topic 5.5 study guide](/advanced-course-resources/chemistry/5-5-collision-model-study-guide/): "Collision frequency and concentration".
</details>

## Question 7 (multiple choice · 5.6)

Two elementary steps have similar Arrhenius factors A. Step X has Eₐ = 45 kJ mol⁻¹ and ΔH = −90 kJ mol⁻¹. Step Y has Eₐ = 70 kJ mol⁻¹ and ΔH = −20 kJ mol⁻¹. Which statement is correct?

- (A) X has the larger k because its Eₐ is smaller; a 10 K rise increases Y's k by the larger factor.
- (B) X has the larger k because its ΔH is more negative; a 10 K rise increases both by the same factor.
- (C) X has the larger k because its Eₐ is smaller; a 10 K rise increases X's k by the larger factor.
- (D) Y has the larger k, because heating lowers its larger Eₐ the most.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (A).** A lower barrier means more collisions reach the transition state, so X is faster. Y's barrier lies farther out in the tail of the energy distribution, where warming multiplies the fraction by more.

- (B) ΔH says nothing about speed.
- (C) has the temperature effect backwards: a larger Eₐ means a larger factor.
- (D) Temperature does not change Eₐ.

**If you missed this:** [Topic 5.6 study guide](/advanced-course-resources/chemistry/5-6-reaction-energy-profile-study-guide/): "Temperature and the Arrhenius equation".
</details>

## Question 8 (multiple choice · 5.7)

A₂ + 2B → 2AB is proposed to go by four steps:

Step 1: A₂ + Q → A₂Q
Step 2: A₂Q → A + AQ
Step 3: AQ + B → AB + Q
Step 4: A + B → AB

Which list gives **all** the intermediates?

- (A) A₂Q, AQ and A
- (B) A₂Q, AQ, A and Q
- (C) A₂Q and AQ only
- (D) Q only

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (A).** Each is made, then used: A₂Q (made in 1, used in 2), AQ (2, then 3), A (2, then 4).

- (B) includes Q, which is **used first** (step 1) and **given back** (step 3): a catalyst.
- (C) misses A, made in step 2 and used in step 4.
- (D) names the catalyst, not an intermediate.

**If you missed this:** [Topic 5.7 study guide](/advanced-course-resources/chemistry/5-7-introduction-reaction-mechanisms-study-guide/): "The parts of a mechanism".
</details>

## Question 9 (multiple choice · 5.8)

For X₂ + 2Y → 2XY, a proposed mechanism is Step 1 (slow): X₂ + Y → X₂Y; Step 2 (fast): X₂Y + Y → 2XY. Doubling [X₂] doubles the rate; doubling [Y] doubles the rate. A student says: "This proves the mechanism." Which is the best evaluation?

- (A) Consistent but not proven: another mechanism could predict the same rate law.
- (B) Proven, because the predicted and experimental rate laws match.
- (C) Rejected, because the overall equation has 2Y, so the reaction must be second order in Y.
- (D) Rejected, because the intermediate X₂Y is not in the rate law.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (A).** The steps add up, and the slow first step predicts rate = k[X₂][Y], which matches. A match shows the mechanism is possible, not unique.

- (B) overstates the evidence.
- (C) uses the overall coefficients as orders. The second Y reacts after the slow step.
- (D) An intermediate should **not** appear in a testable rate law.

**If you missed this:** [Topic 5.8 study guide](/advanced-course-resources/chemistry/5-8-reaction-mechanism-rate-law-study-guide/): "Testing a mechanism against experiment".
</details>

## Question 10 (short answer · 5.9)

A + 2B → P is proposed to go by:

Step 1 (fast, reversible): A + B ⇌ C
Step 2 (fast, reversible): C + B ⇌ D
Step 3 (slow): D → P

(a) Identify the intermediates.
(b) Derive the rate law in terms of [A] and [B].
(c) Predict the effect on the rate of tripling [B].

<details>
<summary>Worked answer</summary>

**(a)** **C** (made in step 1, used in step 2) and **D** (made in step 2, used in step 3).

**(b)**

1. Slow step: rate = k₃[D], but D is an intermediate.
2. Step 2 at equilibrium: [D] = (k₂/k₋₂)[C][B]. Step 1 at equilibrium: [C] = (k₁/k₋₁)[A][B].
3. Substitute: rate = k₃(k₂/k₋₂)(k₁/k₋₁)[A][B]², so **rate = k[A][B]²**.

**(c)** Second order in B, so the rate rises by 3² = **9 times**.

**If you missed this:** [Topic 5.9 study guide](/advanced-course-resources/chemistry/5-9-pre-equilibrium-approximation-study-guide/): "The pre-equilibrium recipe" and Worked example 1.
</details>

## Question 11 (multiple choice · 5.10)

A two-step reaction has step 1: Eₐ = 40, ΔH = +15; step 2: Eₐ = 60, ΔH = −70. With the reactants at 0, which describes its profile?

- (A) Intermediate at +15, TS 2 at +75, products at −55; step 2 is rate-limiting.
- (B) Intermediate at +15, TS 2 at +60, products at −55; step 2 is rate-limiting.
- (C) Intermediate at +15, TS 2 at +75, products at −55; step 1 is rate-limiting, because it is uphill.
- (D) Intermediate at +15, TS 2 at +75, products at −70; step 2 is rate-limiting.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (A).** TS 1 = 40; intermediate = 0 + 15 = 15; TS 2 = 15 + 60 = 75; products = 15 − 70 = −55. Step 2 has the larger barrier (60 > 40), and TS 2 is the highest point.

- (B) measures step 2's barrier from the reactants instead of from the intermediate.
- (C) An uphill step is not necessarily slow; barriers decide.
- (D) uses step 2's ΔH as the overall ΔH.

**If you missed this:** [Topic 5.10 study guide](/advanced-course-resources/chemistry/5-10-multistep-reaction-energy-profile-study-guide/): "Reading the numbers" and Worked example 2.
</details>

## Question 12 (multiple choice · 5.11)

Heating and adding a catalyst both increase the fraction of collisions that can react. How does each do this?

- (A) Heating changes the particles' energy distribution; the catalyst provides a new path with a lower activation energy.
- (B) Heating lowers the activation energy; the catalyst changes the energy distribution.
- (C) Both lower the activation energy of the original path.
- (D) Both shift the energy distribution to higher energies.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (A).** Heating flattens the Maxwell–Boltzmann curve and moves it right, so more area lies beyond the same Eₐ. A catalyst leaves the curve alone but changes the mechanism, lowering the threshold.

- (B) swaps the two effects.
- (C) Neither changes the original path's barrier; the catalyst gives a different path.
- (D) A catalyst gives the particles no extra energy.

**If you missed this:** [Topic 5.11 study guide](/advanced-course-resources/chemistry/5-11-catalysis-study-guide/): "Why a lower barrier means a faster reaction".
</details>
