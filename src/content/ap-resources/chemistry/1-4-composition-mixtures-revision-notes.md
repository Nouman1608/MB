## Recap

- A **pure substance** contains one type of particle: atoms, molecules or formula units. A compound with two elements is still pure if every particle is the same.
- A **mixture** contains two or more types of particle. The proportions can vary, so a mixture has no formula.
- A pure compound always has the **same mass percent** of each element (law of definite proportions).
- A mixture's mass percent of an element depends on how much of each component it holds. For two components it lies **between** the two pure values.
- **Elemental analysis** measures the mass of an element in a sample. Use it to find relative numbers of atoms (via moles) and to judge purity.

## Key relationships

| Relationship | Use it to |
|---|---|
| mass fraction of X = (atoms of X × atomic mass of X) ÷ M | find the fixed composition of a pure compound |
| mass of compound = mass of X ÷ mass fraction of X | find how much compound supplied the element |
| purity (%) = mass of compound ÷ mass of sample × 100 | find the percent of the sample that is the compound |
| a + b = moles of X; M₁a + M₂b = total mass | solve a two-component mixture when both parts contain X |

## Assumptions

- In the one-step purity method, the **impurity contains none** of the analysed element.
- The analysis recovers **all** of the element; losses make the purity look too low.
- Molar masses assume the natural mix of isotopes.

## Mistakes to avoid

1. **Element percent ≠ compound percent.** 40.8% Cl in a sample does not mean 40.8% KCl.
2. **Simple averages.** A mixture's mass percent is a weighted average, not the midpoint.
3. **"Two elements = mixture."** NaCl is pure; salt plus sand is a mixture.
4. **Ignoring a second source of the element.** If both components contain it, you need two equations.
5. **Purity over 100%.** This signals the impurity contains the element, or a data error.
6. **Rounding mass fractions early.** Keep them unrounded until the last step.

## Quick self-check

1. What is the mass percent of oxygen in pure MgO? *(39.69%)*
2. A 25.0 g sample of impure MgO contains 12.0 g of Mg. The impurity has no Mg. What is the purity? *(79.6% MgO)*
3. Could a mixture of NaCl and KCl be 65% chlorine by mass? *(No: the limits are 47.55% and 60.66%)*
4. Three samples from one bottle give 31%, 36% and 28% Ca. Pure or mixture? *(Mixture: the values vary)*
