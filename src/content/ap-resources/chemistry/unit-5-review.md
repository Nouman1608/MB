## How to use this review

Use this page after the [Unit 5 diagnostic](/advanced-course-resources/chemistry/unit-5-diagnostic/) and the topics it flagged. Answer all seven questions on paper before opening any answer. Each combines two or more topics.

These are **original Marlbridge practice questions**, not past exam questions. All species written with letters are fictional and all data are invented. Each rubric is a **suggested Marlbridge rubric**, not official scoring, and your total gives no predicted score.

## Big ideas of the unit

- **Rates of different species are linked by coefficients**, and are always positive ([Topic 5.1](/advanced-course-resources/chemistry/5-1-reaction-rates-study-guide/)).
- **An overall rate law is measured, not read from the equation.** Initial rates give the orders and k ([Topic 5.2](/advanced-course-resources/chemistry/5-2-introduction-rate-law-study-guide/)).
- **One long experiment also gives the order.** Whichever of [A], ln[A] or 1/[A] against t is straight names the order; its slope gives k ([Topic 5.3](/advanced-course-resources/chemistry/5-3-concentration-changes-over-time-study-guide/)).
- **For one elementary step, the coefficients are the orders.** Three-particle collisions are rare ([Topic 5.4](/advanced-course-resources/chemistry/5-4-elementary-reactions-study-guide/)).
- **Rate depends on collision frequency, energy and orientation.** Concentration changes the first; temperature mainly the second ([Topic 5.5](/advanced-course-resources/chemistry/5-5-collision-model-study-guide/)).
- **Eₐ is the climb to the transition state.** A higher barrier gives a slower, more temperature-sensitive step ([Topic 5.6](/advanced-course-resources/chemistry/5-6-reaction-energy-profile-study-guide/)).
- **A mechanism must add up, use reasonable steps and match the rate law.** Intermediates are made then used; catalysts used then given back ([Topics 5.7](/advanced-course-resources/chemistry/5-7-introduction-reaction-mechanisms-study-guide/) and [5.8](/advanced-course-resources/chemistry/5-8-reaction-mechanism-rate-law-study-guide/)).
- **If a fast reversible step comes first, substitute out the intermediate** using forward rate = reverse rate ([Topic 5.9](/advanced-course-resources/chemistry/5-9-pre-equilibrium-approximation-study-guide/)).
- **A multistep profile has one hump per step.** Measure each barrier from where its step starts; the largest marks the slow step ([Topic 5.10](/advanced-course-resources/chemistry/5-10-multistep-reaction-energy-profile-study-guide/)).
- **A catalyst changes the mechanism, not ΔH.** It lowers the highest barrier both ways and can appear in the rate law ([Topic 5.11](/advanced-course-resources/chemistry/5-11-catalysis-study-guide/)).

## Key relationships and methods

| Idea | Relationship or method | Topic |
|---|---|---|
| Rates of species | rate of reaction = −(1/a) Δ[A]/Δt = (1/c) Δ[C]/Δt | 5.1 |
| Rate law | rate = k[A]ᵐ[B]ⁿ; rate ratio = (concentration ratio)^order; k in M¹⁻ⁿ s⁻¹ | 5.2 |
| Integrated laws | zero: [A] vs t slope −k; first: ln[A] vs t slope −k; second: 1/[A] vs t slope +k | 5.3 |
| Half-life | first order only: t½ = 0.693/k, independent of [A]₀ | 5.3 |
| Elementary step | orders = coefficients; molecularity = particles colliding | 5.4 |
| Temperature | k = A e^(−Eₐ/RT): used to explain, never to calculate | 5.5, 5.6 |
| Profile | Eₐ(reverse) = Eₐ(forward) − ΔH; overall ΔH = sum of step ΔH | 5.6, 5.10 |
| Mechanism rate law | slow first step: its own rate law; fast reversible step first: k₁[reactants] = k₋₁[intermediate], then substitute | 5.8, 5.9 |
| Catalyst | used then regenerated; in the rate law if it reacts at or before the slow step | 5.11 |

## Question 1 (multiple choice · mixed)

The reaction 2A → B is first order in A, with −Δ[A]/Δt = k[A] and a half-life of 150 s. At what rate is B forming at the moment when [A] = 0.40 M?

- (A) 9.2 × 10⁻⁴ M s⁻¹
- (B) 1.8 × 10⁻³ M s⁻¹
- (C) 2.7 × 10⁻³ M s⁻¹
- (D) 3.7 × 10⁻³ M s⁻¹

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (A).** k = 0.693 ÷ 150 s = 4.62 × 10⁻³ s⁻¹ (Topic 5.3). −Δ[A]/Δt = 4.62 × 10⁻³ × 0.40 = 1.85 × 10⁻³ M s⁻¹ (Topic 5.2). B forms half as fast: 9.2 × 10⁻⁴ M s⁻¹ (Topic 5.1).

- (B) is the rate for A, not for B.
- (C) divides the concentration by the half-life (0.40 ÷ 150).
- (D) doubles the rate for A instead of halving it.
</details>

## Question 2 (multiple choice · mixed)

A + B + C → D has this mechanism: Step 1 (slow): A + B → I; Step 2 (fast): I + C → D. At constant temperature, [C] is doubled. Which statement is correct?

- (A) I–C collisions become more frequent, but the number of A–B collisions per second is unchanged, so the rate is unchanged.
- (B) C collides twice as often, so the rate doubles.
- (C) The rate is unchanged, because doubling [C] lowers the activation energy of step 2.
- (D) The rate doubles, because all three reactants are in the overall equation.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (A).** The slow first step sets rate = k[A][B] (Topics 5.4 and 5.8). More C makes step 2 collisions more frequent (Topic 5.5), but step 2 was already fast. The bottleneck, A–B collisions, is unchanged: zero order in C.

- (B) applies the collision argument after the bottleneck.
- (C) right result, wrong reason: concentration never changes Eₐ.
- (D) reads orders from the overall equation.
</details>

## Question 3 (multiple choice · mixed)

For 2A + B → A₂B, experiments give rate = k[A]²[B]. Which mechanism adds up to the overall equation, predicts this rate law **and** uses only unimolecular or bimolecular steps?

- (A) Step 1 (fast, reversible): A + A ⇌ A₂. Step 2 (slow): A₂ + B → A₂B.
- (B) One step: 2A + B → A₂B.
- (C) Step 1 (slow): A + B → AB. Step 2 (fast): AB + A → A₂B.
- (D) Step 1 (fast, reversible): A + A ⇌ A₂. Step 2 (slow): A₂ + B → AB + A.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (A).** The steps add to 2A + B → A₂B (Topic 5.7). The slow step gives rate = k₂[A₂][B]; the pre-equilibrium gives [A₂] = (k₁/k₋₁)[A]², so rate = k[A]²[B] (Topic 5.9). Both steps are bimolecular.

- (B) fits, but it is termolecular, which is rare (Topic 5.4).
- (C) adds up but predicts rate = k[A][B].
- (D) has the right rate law, but its steps add to A + B → AB.
</details>

## Question 4 (constructed response · mixed)

Initial rates for M₂ + 2N → 2MN at one temperature:

| Experiment | [M₂] (M) | [N] (M) | Initial rate (M s⁻¹) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0.040 | 0.050 | 3.0 × 10⁻⁵ |
| 2 | 0.160 | 0.050 | 6.0 × 10⁻⁵ |
| 3 | 0.160 | 0.150 | 1.8 × 10⁻⁴ |

(a) Determine the order with respect to M₂ and to N.
(b) Write the rate law and calculate k with units.
(c) Explain why this reaction cannot be a single elementary step.
(d) Mechanism I: M₂ + N → MN + M (slow); M + N → MN (fast). Mechanism II: M₂ ⇌ 2M (fast, reversible); M + N → MN (slow, happens twice). Which is consistent with the data? Show your reasoning.
(e) Predict the initial rate when [M₂] = 0.090 M and [N] = 0.20 M.

<details>
<summary>Worked solution and suggested Marlbridge rubric</summary>

**(a)** Experiments 1 → 2: [M₂] × 4, rate × 2. 4ᵐ = 2, so **m = ½**. Experiments 2 → 3: [N] × 3, rate × 3, so **first order in N**.

**(b)** rate = k[M₂]^½[N]. k = 3.0 × 10⁻⁵ ÷ (0.040^½ × 0.050) = 3.0 × 10⁻⁵ ÷ (0.20 × 0.050) = **3.0 × 10⁻³ M^(−½) s⁻¹** (overall order 1½, so M^(1 − 1.5) s⁻¹). All three experiments agree.

**(c)** An elementary step's orders are its coefficients, which are whole numbers, so an order of ½ cannot come from one collision. (M₂ + 2N as one step would also be termolecular, predicting k[M₂][N]².)

**(d)** Both mechanisms add up to M₂ + 2N → 2MN.
- Mechanism I: slow first step, so rate = k[M₂][N]. First order in M₂: **rejected**.
- Mechanism II: slow step rate = k₂[M][N]; M is an intermediate. k₁[M₂] = k₋₁[M]², so [M] = (K₁[M₂])^½ and rate = k₂K₁^½[M₂]^½[N]. **Consistent** (not proven).

**(e)** rate = 3.0 × 10⁻³ × (0.090)^½ × 0.20 = 3.0 × 10⁻³ × 0.30 × 0.20 = **1.8 × 10⁻⁴ M s⁻¹**.

| Point | What earns it |
|---|---|
| 1 | (a) Half order in M₂ from experiments 1 and 2 |
| 1 | (a) First order in N from experiments 2 and 3 |
| 1 | (b) Rate law and k = 3.0 × 10⁻³ |
| 1 | (b) Units M^(−½) s⁻¹ |
| 1 | (c) Elementary orders are whole-number coefficients |
| 1 | (d) Mechanism I rejected because it predicts first order in M₂ |
| 1 | (d) Mechanism II: [M] substituted to give [M₂]^½[N] |
| 1 | (e) 1.8 × 10⁻⁴ M s⁻¹ (carry forward from (b)) |

**Total: 8 points.**
</details>

## Question 5 (constructed response · mixed)

Compound A decomposes: A → 2B. At 300 K a student records:

| t (s) | 0 | 50 | 100 | 150 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| [A] (M) | 0.0800 | 0.0566 | 0.0400 | 0.0283 |

At 320 K, with the same starting concentration, the half-life is 25 s.

(a) Show that the reaction is first order in A, using calculated values.
(b) Find k at 300 K, with units.
(c) Calculate the rate of formation of B at t = 100 s at 300 K.
(d) Find k at 320 K, and the time at 320 K for [A] to fall from 0.0800 M to 0.0100 M.
(e) Use a sketch of Maxwell–Boltzmann curves to explain why k is four times larger at 320 K. Evaluate a student's claim that Eₐ is lower at 320 K.

<details>
<summary>Worked solution and suggested Marlbridge rubric</summary>

**(a)** ln[A]: −2.526, −2.872, −3.219, −3.565, changing by −0.346, −0.347, −0.346 per 50 s: constant, so ln[A] against t is straight. [A] falls by 0.0234, 0.0166, 0.0117 M and 1/[A] rises by 5.2, 7.3, 10.3 M⁻¹: neither constant. **First order.** (Also: [A] halves every 100 s.)

**(b)** Slope = −0.00693 s⁻¹, so **k = 6.93 × 10⁻³ s⁻¹** (or 0.693 ÷ 100 s).

**(c)** −Δ[A]/Δt = k[A] = 6.93 × 10⁻³ × 0.0400 = 2.77 × 10⁻⁴ M s⁻¹. B forms twice as fast: **5.54 × 10⁻⁴ M s⁻¹**.

**(d)** k = 0.693 ÷ 25 s = **0.0277 s⁻¹**, four times the 300 K value. 0.0800 → 0.0100 M is three half-lives: **75 s**. (Check: ln 8 ÷ 0.0277 = 75 s.)

**(e)** Sketch: number of particles against kinetic energy. The 320 K curve has a lower peak, shifted to higher energy, with a thicker tail and the same area. With Eₐ marked in the tail, a larger area lies beyond it at 320 K, so a larger fraction of collisions reach the transition state. Collisions are only slightly more frequent, so this is the main cause. The student is **wrong**: Eₐ, the gap from reactants to transition state, does not change with temperature.

| Point | What earns it |
|---|---|
| 1 | (a) ln[A] values with constant differences (or constant half-life) |
| 1 | (a) At least one other plot shown not to be linear |
| 1 | (b) k = 6.93 × 10⁻³ s⁻¹ with units |
| 1 | (c) 5.54 × 10⁻⁴ M s⁻¹, doubling the rate for A |
| 1 | (d) 0.0277 s⁻¹ and 75 s |
| 1 | (e) Sketch: lower, right-shifted peak at 320 K |
| 1 | (e) Larger fraction beyond Eₐ named as the main cause |
| 1 | (e) Rejects the claim: Eₐ does not change with temperature |

**Total: 8 points.** Do not award the sketch point if the 320 K peak is drawn taller.
</details>

## Question 6 (constructed response · mixed)

Without a catalyst, S + T → ST happens in one step with Eₐ = 115 kJ mol⁻¹ and ΔH = −35 kJ mol⁻¹. Adding a little Q makes it much faster, by this proposed mechanism:

Step 1 (slow): S + Q → SQ
Step 2 (fast): SQ + T → ST + Q

On the catalysed profile, relative to the reactants at 0 kJ mol⁻¹: TS 1 is at 68, SQ at 22, TS 2 at 51 and the products at −35.

(a) Show that the steps add up, and identify the catalyst and the intermediate with reasons.
(b) Write the rate law for the catalysed reaction. With k = 0.80 M⁻¹ s⁻¹, calculate the rate when [S] = 0.050 M and [Q] = 0.0020 M, and say what happens if [Q] is doubled.
(c) Calculate Eₐ for each catalysed step and show that the profile agrees with step 1 being slow.
(d) Sketch both profiles on one set of axes and state two features that stay the same.
(e) Calculate the reverse activation energy with and without Q, and comment.

<details>
<summary>Worked solution and suggested Marlbridge rubric</summary>

**(a)** Cancel SQ and Q: **S + T → ST**. **Q** is the catalyst: used in step 1, given back in step 2. **SQ** is the intermediate: made in step 1, used in step 2.

**(b)** Slow first step: **rate = k[S][Q]** = 0.80 × 0.050 × 0.0020 = **8.0 × 10⁻⁵ M s⁻¹**. Doubling [Q] doubles the rate (1.6 × 10⁻⁴ M s⁻¹), although Q is not in the overall equation.

**(c)** Step 1: Eₐ = 68 − 0 = **68 kJ mol⁻¹**. Step 2: Eₐ = 51 − 22 = **29 kJ mol⁻¹**, from SQ. Step 1 has the larger barrier (and TS 1 is the highest point), so it is slow, as proposed.

**(d)** Uncatalysed: one hump to 115. Catalysed: two humps (68 and 51) with a valley at 22 for SQ. Both start at 0 and end at −35, so the **reactant and product energies** and **ΔH = −35 kJ mol⁻¹** are the same. (Check: +22 + (−57) = −35.)

**(e)** From the products (−35) to the highest point: without Q, 115 + 35 = **150 kJ mol⁻¹**; with Q, 68 + 35 = **103 kJ mol⁻¹**. Both directions are lowered by 47 kJ mol⁻¹, so Q speeds up the reverse reaction too.

| Point | What earns it |
|---|---|
| 1 | (a) Correct sum with cancellations |
| 1 | (a) Q catalyst and SQ intermediate, with reasons |
| 1 | (b) rate = k[S][Q] from the slow first step |
| 1 | (b) 8.0 × 10⁻⁵ M s⁻¹ and the rate doubles |
| 1 | (c) 68 and 29 kJ mol⁻¹ (step 2 from SQ); step 1 slow |
| 1 | (d) Sketch: one hump at 115; two lower humps with a dip; same ends |
| 1 | (e) 150 and 103 kJ mol⁻¹; both directions lowered equally |

**Total: 7 points.**
</details>

## Question 7 (constructed response · mixed)

Gas A dimerises: 2A → A₂. The rate law is written −Δ[A]/Δt = k[A]².

| t (s) | 0 | 100 | 200 | 400 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| [A] (M) | 0.0200 | 0.0100 | 0.00667 | 0.00400 |

(a) Show that the data fit a second-order rate law, and find k. Note that the times are not equally spaced.
(b) Calculate the rate of formation of A₂ at t = 200 s.
(c) Predict the time at which [A] reaches 0.00200 M.
(d) A student says: "The first half-life was 100 s, so [A] will be 0.00500 M at 200 s." Evaluate this.
(e) If the reaction is one elementary step, explain at the particle level why rate ∝ [A]². What can the data show about this proposal?

<details>
<summary>Worked solution and suggested Marlbridge rubric</summary>

**(a)** 1/[A]: 50, 100, 150, 250 M⁻¹. Slopes: 50 ÷ 100, 50 ÷ 100, 100 ÷ 200 = **0.500 M⁻¹ s⁻¹** each, so 1/[A] against t is straight. ln[A] slopes are −0.00693, −0.00405, −0.00255 s⁻¹, not constant. Second order; slope = +k, so **k = 0.500 M⁻¹ s⁻¹**.

**(b)** −Δ[A]/Δt = 0.500 × (0.00667)² = 2.22 × 10⁻⁵ M s⁻¹. One A₂ forms for every two A used: **1.11 × 10⁻⁵ M s⁻¹**.

**(c)** t = (1/0.00200 − 1/0.0200) ÷ 0.500 = (500 − 50) ÷ 0.500 = **900 s**.

**(d)** **Wrong.** Only a first-order half-life is constant. The second half-life (0.0100 → 0.00500 M) is (200 − 100) ÷ 0.500 = 200 s, so [A] reaches 0.00500 M at 300 s; the data show 0.00667 M at 200 s. Half-lives lengthen because the rate falls with [A]².

**(e)** Two A particles must collide. Doubling [A] doubles the A particles **and** the partners each can hit, so A–A collisions per second rise fourfold; at constant temperature the successful fraction is unchanged, so rate ∝ [A]². The data are **consistent** with a bimolecular step but do not prove it: a multistep mechanism could give the same rate law.

| Point | What earns it |
|---|---|
| 1 | (a) Constant 1/[A] slope shown, and ln[A] slope not constant |
| 1 | (a) k = 0.500 M⁻¹ s⁻¹ |
| 1 | (b) 1.11 × 10⁻⁵ M s⁻¹, half the rate for A |
| 1 | (c) 900 s |
| 1 | (d) Rejects the claim: second-order half-lives get longer |
| 1 | (e) Collision-counting reason for [A]² |
| 1 | (e) Consistent with one step, but not proof |

**Total: 7 points.**
</details>
