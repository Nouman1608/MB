These are **original Marlbridge practice questions**, not past exam questions. The mark points are a suggested Marlbridge rubric to help you check your work; they are not an official scoring guideline. Data: ΔG°f at 298 K: CaCO₃(s) −1129.1, CaO(s) −603.3, CO₂(g) −394.39 kJ mol⁻¹. T(K) = T(°C) + 273.15. Reactions X, Z and the dimerisation in Question 7 use invented data, made up for practice.

## Question 1 (multiple choice · foundation)

A reaction is endothermic, and the number of moles of gas decreases from reactants to products. Under standard conditions, at which temperatures is the reaction thermodynamically favoured?

- (A) At all temperatures
- (B) Only at high temperatures
- (C) Only at low temperatures
- (D) At no temperature

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (D).** Endothermic means ΔH° > 0. Fewer moles of gas means matter is less dispersed, so ΔS° < 0 and −TΔS° > 0. Both terms in ΔG° = ΔH° − TΔS° are positive, so ΔG° > 0 at every temperature.

- (A) is the case ΔH° < 0, ΔS° > 0.
- (B) is the case where both are positive; here ΔS° is negative.
- (C) is the case where both are negative; here ΔH° is positive.
</details>

## Question 2 (multiple choice · core)

For a reaction, ΔH° = −120 kJ mol⁻¹ and ΔS° = −150 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹. What is ΔG° at 400 K?

- (A) −180 kJ mol⁻¹
- (B) −60.0 kJ mol⁻¹
- (C) +60.0 kJ mol⁻¹
- (D) +59 880 kJ mol⁻¹

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (B).** Convert ΔS° to −0.150 kJ K⁻¹ mol⁻¹. Then ΔG° = −120 − (400)(−0.150) = −120 + 60.0 = −60.0 kJ mol⁻¹. The reaction is favoured at 400 K, although the entropy term has cancelled half of ΔH°.

- (A) adds TΔS° instead of subtracting it: −120 + (400)(−0.150).
- (C) calculates TΔS° − ΔH°, the right numbers in the wrong order.
- (D) leaves ΔS° in J: −120 − (400)(−150). Mixing kJ and J gives a huge, meaningless number.
</details>

## Question 3 (multiple choice · core)

Quicklime absorbs carbon dioxide: CaO(s) + CO₂(g) → CaCO₃(s). Using the data at the top of the page, what is ΔG° at 298 K?

- (A) +131.4 kJ mol⁻¹
- (B) −131.4 kJ mol⁻¹
- (C) −1129.1 kJ mol⁻¹
- (D) −2126.8 kJ mol⁻¹

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (B).** ΔG° = ΔG°f(CaCO₃) − [ΔG°f(CaO) + ΔG°f(CO₂)] = −1129.1 − (−603.3 − 394.39) = −1129.1 + 997.69 = −131.4 kJ mol⁻¹. ΔG° < 0, so the reaction is favoured at 298 K.

- (A) is reactants minus products, which reverses the sign.
- (C) uses only the product value and ignores the reactants.
- (D) adds all three values instead of subtracting the reactants.
</details>

## Question 4 (multiple choice · core)

Solid sodium nitrate, NaNO₃, dissolves readily in water at 25 °C, and the temperature of the solution falls. Which statement best explains why the dissolving is thermodynamically favoured?

- (A) The process is exothermic, so ΔH° < 0 makes ΔG° negative.
- (B) The process is endothermic, but ΔS° > 0 as ions spread through the solution, and TΔS° is larger than ΔH°.
- (C) The process absorbs energy from the surroundings, and any process that absorbs energy is favoured.
- (D) ΔG° is positive, but the solid dissolves anyway because dissolving is fast.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (B).** The falling temperature shows the process takes in energy, so ΔH° > 0. Ions leave fixed lattice positions and spread out among the water molecules, so ΔS° > 0. Since the solid does dissolve, ΔG° < 0, which means TΔS° > ΔH° at this temperature.

- (A) misreads the temperature drop: an exothermic process would warm the solution.
- (C) is false in general: many endothermic processes (such as melting ice at −10 °C) are not favoured.
- (D) confuses speed with favourability. Rate decides how quickly a process happens, not which direction is favoured; a fast rate cannot make an unfavoured process go.
</details>

## Question 5 (calculation · core)

Reaction X has ΔH° = +64.8 kJ mol⁻¹ and ΔS° = +172 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹. Assume both are constant with temperature.

(a) Calculate ΔG° at 298 K and state whether X is favoured.
(b) Calculate the temperature above which X is favoured.
(c) Calculate ΔG° at 450 K.

<details>
<summary>Worked solution</summary>

**(a)** ΔS° = 0.172 kJ K⁻¹ mol⁻¹. TΔS° = 298 × 0.172 = 51.26 kJ mol⁻¹. ΔG° = 64.8 − 51.26 = **+13.5 kJ mol⁻¹**. Not favoured at 298 K.

**(b)** ΔG° = 0 when T = ΔH° ÷ ΔS° = 64 800 ÷ 172 = **377 K** (about 104 °C). Above this, −TΔS° outweighs ΔH°.

**(c)** ΔG° = 64.8 − 450 × 0.172 = 64.8 − 77.4 = **−12.6 kJ mol⁻¹**. Favoured, as (b) predicts.

Suggested mark points (4): 1 for converting ΔS° to kJ (or ΔH° to J); 1 for +13.5 kJ mol⁻¹ with "not favoured"; 1 for 377 K; 1 for −12.6 kJ mol⁻¹. Accept 376.7 K.
</details>

## Question 6 (constructed response · core)

Salt Z dissolves in water: Z(s) → Z(aq). For this process, ΔH° = +26.0 kJ mol⁻¹ and ΔS° = +105 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹.

(a) Explain, in terms of particles, why ΔS° is positive.
(b) Calculate ΔG° at 298 K. Is the dissolving favoured?
(c) A student cools the water to 285 K. Calculate ΔG° and state whether the conclusion in (b) changes.
(d) Identify whether enthalpy or entropy drives the dissolving, and justify your answer.

<details>
<summary>Worked solution and suggested Marlbridge rubric</summary>

**(a)** In the solid the ions are held in fixed positions in a lattice. In solution they move freely and are spread through a much larger volume, so matter is more dispersed and ΔS° > 0.

**(b)** ΔG° = 26.0 − 298(0.105) = 26.0 − 31.29 = **−5.3 kJ mol⁻¹**. ΔG° < 0, so dissolving is favoured.

**(c)** ΔG° = 26.0 − 285(0.105) = 26.0 − 29.93 = **−3.9 kJ mol⁻¹**. Still negative, so still favoured, but less strongly, because the entropy term is smaller at lower T.

**(d)** **Entropy.** ΔH° > 0 works against dissolving; only the −TΔS° term is negative, and it is larger in size than ΔH°.

| Point | What earns it |
|---|---|
| 1 | Ions go from fixed lattice positions to moving freely through the solution (more dispersed) |
| 1 | −5.3 kJ mol⁻¹ with ΔS° converted to kJ, and "favoured" |
| 1 | −3.9 kJ mol⁻¹ and "still favoured" |
| 1 | Entropy, because ΔH° is positive (unfavourable) and −TΔS° outweighs it |

Accept answers to (c) carried forward from a unit error in (b) only if the same method is used consistently.
</details>

## Question 7 (constructed response · stretch)

A gas A dimerises: 2A(g) → A₂(g). For this reaction, ΔH° = −58.0 kJ mol⁻¹ and ΔS° = −176 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹.

(a) Justify the sign of ΔS° at the particle level.
(b) Determine the range of temperatures over which the dimerisation is thermodynamically favoured.
(c) A student claims: "Because ΔH° is negative, the dimerisation is favoured at every temperature." Evaluate the claim, using a calculation at 400 K.

<details>
<summary>Worked solution and suggested Marlbridge rubric</summary>

**(a)** Two moles of gas particles become one mole. Fewer independent particles moving through the container means matter is less dispersed, so ΔS° < 0.

**(b)** Both ΔH° and ΔS° are negative, so the reaction is favoured only at low T. The changeover is T = ΔH° ÷ ΔS° = (−58 000) ÷ (−176) = 329.5 K. The dimerisation is favoured **below about 330 K** (about 56 °C).

**(c)** At 400 K: ΔG° = −58.0 − 400(−0.176) = −58.0 + 70.4 = **+12.4 kJ mol⁻¹**. ΔG° > 0, so the dimerisation is not favoured at 400 K, and the claim is **wrong**. A negative ΔH° favours the reaction, but the −TΔS° term is positive and grows with temperature; above 330 K it is larger than the size of ΔH°.

| Point | What earns it |
|---|---|
| 1 | Fewer moles of gas, so less dispersal of matter |
| 1 | Favoured only below about 330 K, with T = ΔH° ÷ ΔS° shown |
| 1 | +12.4 kJ mol⁻¹ at 400 K |
| 1 | Claim rejected, with the reason that −TΔS° is positive and outweighs ΔH° at high T |

Do not award the last point for "the claim is wrong" without a reason linked to the entropy term.
</details>
