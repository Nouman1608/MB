Short on time? This page is the recap. For explanations, diagrams and worked examples, use the [full study guide](/advanced-course-resources/chemistry/2-3-structure-ionic-solids-study-guide/).

## Recap

- An **ionic solid** is a **repeating three-dimensional array** of cations and anions that extends to the surface of the crystal.
- Each ion is surrounded by ions of the **opposite** charge. This gives the most attraction and keeps like charges apart.
- There are **no molecules**. The formula (NaCl, CaF₂) gives only the **ratio** of ions; one set of ions in that ratio is a formula unit.
- **Specific crystal structures are not assessed.** You need the general model, not names of structures.

## Key relationships

| Idea | What to use | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Strength of attraction | ∝ \|q₊ × q₋\| / d, with d ≈ r₊ + r₋ | Larger charges or shorter d give stronger attraction |
| Charge effect | 2+/2− vs 1+/1− | Product of charges is 4 times larger: the biggest effect |
| Size effect (equal charges) | Ion radius down a group increases | Smaller ions: shorter d, stronger attraction |
| Isoelectronic ions | Same electrons, more protons | Smaller ion (Mg²⁺ < Na⁺; F⁻ < O²⁻) |

## Rules for a particulate drawing

1. Alternate + and −; like charges never touch.
2. Cations usually smaller than anions (for example K⁺ 138 pm, Cl⁻ 181 pm).
3. Match the formula ratio; the total charge of the drawing is zero.
4. Label charges with signs or symbols, not colour alone.

## Assumptions

- Ions are treated as hard spheres that touch their opposite-charge neighbours.
- |q₊q₋| / d is for **ranking** similar solids; it does not give a melting point.
- Radii from different tables differ slightly; trends do not.

## Mistakes to avoid

1. Drawing ion pairs or "NaCl molecules".
2. Saying bigger ions attract more strongly.
3. Ranking a 1+/1− solid above a 2+/2− solid because of a small size difference.
4. Claiming the solid conducts because it "has ions". The ions must be mobile (molten or dissolved).
5. Forgetting the ratio: CaF₂ needs two F⁻ for each Ca²⁺.

## Quick self-check

1. Which has the shorter distance between ion centres, LiF or LiI? *(LiF: 76 + 133 = 209 pm, against 296 pm for LiI)*
2. In a drawing of CaF₂ with 5 Ca²⁺ ions, how many F⁻ ions should there be? *(10)*
3. Why does an ionic crystal shatter when a layer slides? *(Like charges line up and repel.)*
