## What changes and what stays the same

Unit 4 is about chemical reactions. Before you write equations for them, you need to recognise when a reaction has happened at all. Every change in matter falls into one of two groups.

- A **physical change** alters the properties of a substance, such as its state, shape or how it is mixed with other things, but **not its composition**. Ice melting is still H₂O. Sugar dissolved in tea is still sugar.
- A **chemical change** (a chemical reaction) turns the starting substances into **new substances**, usually with a different composition. When iron rusts, iron and oxygen become an iron oxide, a new compound with new properties.

The key word is *composition*: which atoms are present and how they are joined. In a physical change, every particle you started with is still there, in the same form. In a chemical change, the atoms are all still there too, but they are joined in **new combinations**.

## Physical changes

Two families of physical change come up again and again.

**Changes of phase.** Melting, freezing, boiling, condensing, sublimation (solid straight to gas) and deposition (gas straight to solid). The particles move closer together or further apart, but each particle is unchanged. Iodine crystals turning into a purple vapour are still I₂ molecules.

**Making or separating mixtures.** Dissolving sugar in water, mixing two liquids, or stirring sand into water makes a mixture. Filtering, evaporating, distilling and chromatography separate one. None of these forms a new substance, so the components can be recovered with their original properties.

## Evidence of a chemical change

You cannot see atoms rearranging. What you can see are the effects of a new substance forming. Four observations are common evidence.

| Evidence | What you see | Example of a reaction |
|---|---|---|
| Heat or light produced | The mixture warms up, glows or burns | Magnesium ribbon burns with a bright white light |
| A gas forms | Bubbles or fizzing in a liquid, or a smell | Chalk (calcium carbonate) fizzes in dilute acid as carbon dioxide forms |
| A precipitate forms | Two clear solutions mix and a solid appears | Barium chloride and sodium sulfate solutions give a white solid, barium sulfate |
| Colour change | A new colour that is not just dilution | A copper wire in silver nitrate solution: the solution turns blue and silvery crystals grow |

Each of these tells you that *something* happened. A new substance usually has different properties from the old ones: a different colour, a different solubility, a different state at room temperature. That is why these observations point towards a reaction.

## Evidence is a clue, not proof

Every sign in the table can also happen **without** a reaction. That is the main thing examiners test in this topic.

| Observation | Physical change that shows it |
|---|---|
| Temperature change | Dissolving solid sodium hydroxide in water makes the water hot; dissolving ammonium nitrate makes it cold |
| Bubbles | Boiling water; opening a bottle of fizzy water (dissolved carbon dioxide leaves the solution) |
| Solid appears | Cooling a saturated solution until crystals form; freezing |
| Colour change | Diluting a coloured solution makes it paler; heating some solids changes their colour reversibly |

The reverse is also true: some reactions give **no** obvious sign. When dilute hydrochloric acid is added to sodium hydroxide solution, both solutions and the product are colourless. Only a thermometer shows that a reaction has happened.

So use the observation as a starting point, then ask the deciding question in Figure 2: **has a new substance with a different composition formed?**

Good tests for a new substance include:

- **Identify the product.** Carbon dioxide turns limewater cloudy. Hydrogen burns with a squeaky pop. A precipitate can be filtered off and shown to be a different compound from either starting solid.
- **Try to get the starting material back by a physical method.** If evaporating the water returns the original solid, with the same mass and melting point, no new substance was formed.
- **Compare properties.** A product with a different melting point, solubility or colour from every starting substance is a new substance.

## Writing an evidence-based answer

Questions on this topic often ask you to "justify" or "support a claim". A strong answer has three parts, and each one earns credit on its own.

1. **Claim.** State the classification plainly: "This is a chemical change."
2. **Evidence.** Quote the specific observation or data: "A white solid formed when the two colourless solutions were mixed."
3. **Reasoning.** Link the evidence to composition: "The solid is a substance that was not present in either solution, so atoms or ions have combined in a new way."

The third part is the one students leave out. "Bubbles formed, so it is chemical" is a claim plus evidence, but no reasoning. Compare it with: "Bubbles formed and the gas turned limewater cloudy, so the gas is carbon dioxide; neither starting substance was carbon dioxide, so a new substance formed."

When you are asked to **predict** the result of an experiment, work the other way round. Start from what you know will form (a gas, an insoluble solid, a coloured ion, energy released), then say what each product will look like in the apparatus described: bubbles in a liquid, cloudiness in a clear solution, a temperature rise on a thermometer, or a falling reading on a balance if a gas can escape. Be specific to the set-up: "the solution turns cloudy" is better than "a reaction happens".

When the evidence is weak, say so. "The temperature rose, which suggests a reaction, but dissolving can also release heat, so a further test is needed" is a better answer than a confident claim built on one observation.

## Worked example 1: classifying observations

**Question.** A student records five changes. Classify each as physical or chemical and give the evidence.

| Change | Observation |
|---|---|
| (a) | Wax melts in a warm room |
| (b) | A piece of zinc is added to dilute sulfuric acid; bubbles form and the zinc gets smaller |
| (c) | A blue copper(II) sulfate solution is diluted with water; it becomes pale blue |
| (d) | Pale green iron(II) hydroxide left in air slowly turns orange-brown |
| (e) | Salty water is distilled; pure water collects and a white solid is left behind |

**Answer.**

1. **(a) Physical.** Melting is a change of phase. The liquid wax has the same composition as the solid and sets again on cooling.
2. **(b) Chemical.** The zinc is used up and a gas (hydrogen) forms. Neither zinc nor the acid is a gas, so the gas is a new substance.
3. **(c) Physical.** The colour fades because the same blue ions are spread through more water. No new colour appears; this is a change in the mixture, not a reaction.
4. **(d) Chemical.** A new colour appears that is not just dilution. The iron(II) compound reacts with oxygen to form a new iron(III) compound.
5. **(e) Physical.** Distillation separates a mixture. The water and the salt both have their original composition.

**Check.** In (b) and (d) a starting substance disappears or a different substance appears. In (a), (c) and (e) every substance is still present.

## Worked example 2: predicting the results of an experiment

**Question.** An open conical flask holds excess dilute hydrochloric acid. The flask and a 0.243 g piece of magnesium ribbon, resting on the pan beside it, sit on a balance that reads 152.367 g in total. The student drops the ribbon into the acid. The reaction is Mg(s) + 2HCl(aq) → MgCl₂(aq) + H₂(g). Predict what the student will observe, and the final balance reading.

1. **Gas.** One product is hydrogen gas, so bubbles form on the magnesium.
2. **Solid disappears.** Magnesium is used up, because the acid is in excess. The ribbon gets smaller and vanishes.
3. **Temperature.** The flask gets warm. This reaction releases energy as heat (you will meet this in Unit 6).
4. **Mass.** The open flask lets the hydrogen escape, so the reading falls by the mass of hydrogen produced.
   - n(Mg) = 0.243 g ÷ 24.31 g mol⁻¹ = 0.0100 mol.
   - The equation gives 1 mol H₂ for every 1 mol Mg, so n(H₂) = 0.0100 mol.
   - m(H₂) = 0.0100 mol × 2.016 g mol⁻¹ = 0.020 g.
   - Final reading ≈ 152.367 − 0.020 = **152.347 g**.

**Interpretation.** The mass seems to "disappear", but atoms are not destroyed. If the student fits a balloon over the neck of the flask to trap the hydrogen, the reading stays the same (apart from a small buoyancy effect of the inflated balloon). A loss of mass from an open container is itself evidence that a gas has formed.

## Worked example 3: designing a test

**Question.** A student dissolves 5.00 g of an unknown white solid X in 50.0 mL of water. The temperature falls from 22.0 °C to 16.4 °C. The student claims that "a chemical reaction happened, because the temperature changed". Plan a test of this claim and say what result would show the change was physical.

1. **Weigh the evidence.** A temperature change of −5.6 °C is a sign of a possible reaction, but dissolving alone can absorb or release energy. The observation does not decide the question.
2. **Plan the test.** Ask whether X is still present. Evaporate the water slowly at room temperature, then dry and weigh the solid that is left. Measure its melting point and compare it with a sample of the original X.
3. **Physical result.** The student recovers 4.97 g of white solid (99.4% of the original mass; a little is lost on the glassware), and its melting point matches that of X. The same substance came back, so dissolving X was a physical change.
4. **Chemical result.** If the recovered solid had a different mass that cannot be explained by losses, a different melting point, or if a gas had been given off, a new substance would have formed.

**Why this works.** The test targets composition, which is what separates the two kinds of change. The temperature reading on its own could not.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Any temperature change means a reaction."** Dissolving and changes of state also take in or give out energy.
- **"Bubbles mean a gas has been made."** Boiling and dissolved gas escaping give bubbles too. Ask whether the gas is a new substance.
- **"A colour change is always chemical."** Dilution changes how strong a colour looks. A *new* colour is better evidence than a *paler* one.
- **"No visible change means no reaction."** Many reactions, such as strong acid with strong base, look like nothing is happening. Measure the temperature.
- **"In a chemical change, atoms are created or destroyed."** Atoms are only rearranged. Mass is conserved in a closed container; an open one loses mass only because a gas escapes.
- **"Physical changes are always easy to reverse."** Many are (melting, dissolving), but breaking glass is physical and you cannot easily undo it. Composition, not reversibility, is the test.
