## Recap

- A **catalyst** increases the rate of a reaction and is not used up overall.
- It works by increasing the number of **effective collisions**: it opens a **new path with a lower activation energy**, and/or it helps reactants meet with a **better orientation**.
- The catalysed reaction has a **different mechanism**: new elementary steps and new intermediates.
- In the mechanism the catalyst is **used up in one step and made again later**. It is often used up in the **rate-determining step**, so it can appear in the rate law.
- Three types: **binding catalysts** such as enzymes (catalyst–reactant complex), **acid–base catalysis** (a proton is gained or lost), **surface catalysis** (reactants bond to a solid surface).

## Key relationships

| Idea | What it means | Use it to |
|---|---|---|
| Catalyst vs intermediate | Catalyst: reactant first, product later. Intermediate: product first, reactant later | label species in a mechanism |
| Rate law from the slow step | If the catalyst is in the slow (first) step, rate ∝ [catalyst] | predict the effect of adding more catalyst |
| Highest point of the path | Overall barrier = highest transition state − starting energy | compare catalysed and uncatalysed barriers, both directions |
| ΔH = E(products) − E(reactants) | Same for both paths | show the catalyst does not change ΔH |

## Assumptions behind the reasoning

- The temperature is the **same** with and without the catalyst, so the spread of collision energies is the same.
- The proposed mechanism is consistent with the experimental rate law; a mechanism can be supported by data but never proved.
- For a surface catalyst, only atoms on the **surface** can take part.

## Mistakes to avoid

1. **"Not used in the reaction."** It is used, then regenerated.
2. **"Gives particles more energy."** It lowers the threshold; the energy spread is unchanged.
3. **"Changes ΔH."** Reactants and products keep the same energies.
4. **"Forward only."** The reverse barrier drops by the same amount.
5. **Swapping catalyst and intermediate.** Check which appears first as a reactant.
6. **Drawing one lower hump.** A catalysed path usually has extra humps and a dip for the new intermediate.

## Quick self-check

1. In the steps Q + R → QR, then QR + T → V + R, which species is the catalyst? *(R: used in step 1, made again in step 2. QR is the intermediate.)*
2. A reaction has Ea = 90 kJ mol⁻¹ and ΔH = −25 kJ mol⁻¹. A catalyst lowers the highest barrier to 55 kJ mol⁻¹. What is the reverse barrier with the catalyst? *(55 + 25 = 80 kJ mol⁻¹, down from 115 kJ mol⁻¹.)*
3. A catalyst is consumed in the slow first step. What happens to the rate if you triple its concentration? *(The rate triples, if the step is first order in the catalyst.)*
4. Why is powdered nickel a better hydrogenation catalyst than a nickel bar of the same mass? *(More surface atoms, so more sites where H₂ and the alkene can bind.)*
