These are **original Marlbridge practice questions**, not past exam questions. The mark points are a suggested Marlbridge rubric to help you check your work; they are not an official scoring guideline. All K_sp values are at 25 °C. Salts named with capital letters (E, M, L, G, and A to E in Question 3) are fictional; the ion charges are given or follow from the formula.

## Question 1 (multiple choice · foundation)

The fictional salt E₂(SO₄)₃ contains E³⁺ and SO₄²⁻ ions. Which expression is K_sp for E₂(SO₄)₃?

- (A) [E³⁺]²[SO₄²⁻]³
- (B) [E³⁺][SO₄²⁻]
- (C) (2[E³⁺])(3[SO₄²⁻])
- (D) [E³⁺]²[SO₄²⁻]³ / [E₂(SO₄)₃]

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (A).** The dissolving equation is E₂(SO₄)₃(s) ⇌ 2 E³⁺(aq) + 3 SO₄²⁻(aq). Each ion concentration is raised to the power of its coefficient, and the solid is left out.

- (B) ignores the coefficients.
- (C) multiplies by the coefficients instead of using them as powers.
- (D) puts the solid in a denominator. A pure solid never appears in an equilibrium expression.
</details>

## Question 2 (multiple choice · core)

A fictional salt M₂X₃ dissolves as M₂X₃(s) ⇌ 2 M³⁺(aq) + 3 X²⁻(aq). If its molar solubility is s, which expression equals K_sp?

- (A) s⁵
- (B) 6s⁵
- (C) 72s⁵
- (D) 108s⁵

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (D).** [M³⁺] = 2s and [X²⁻] = 3s. K_sp = (2s)²(3s)³ = 4s² × 27s³ = 108s⁵.

- (A) forgets that each formula unit releases 2 M³⁺ and 3 X²⁻ ions.
- (B) multiplies the coefficients (2 × 3) but does not raise them to powers.
- (C) swaps the powers: 2³ × 3² = 72. The coefficient 2 belongs with the power 2, and 3 with the power 3.
</details>

## Question 3 (multiple choice · core)

Four fictional salts have these K_sp values. Which salt has the **greatest** molar solubility in water?

| Salt | Ion ratio | K_sp |
|---|---|---|
| AB | 1:1 | 1.0 × 10⁻⁸ |
| AC₂ | 1:2 | 4.0 × 10⁻⁹ |
| A₂D | 2:1 | 3.2 × 10⁻¹¹ |
| AE₃ | 1:3 | 2.7 × 10⁻¹⁵ |

- (A) AB
- (B) AC₂
- (C) A₂D
- (D) AE₃

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (B).** Calculate s for each salt.

| Salt | K_sp in terms of s | s (mol L⁻¹) |
|---|---|---|
| AB | s² | √(1.0 × 10⁻⁸) = 1.0 × 10⁻⁴ |
| AC₂ | 4s³ | ∛(1.0 × 10⁻⁹) = 1.0 × 10⁻³ |
| A₂D | 4s³ | ∛(8.0 × 10⁻¹²) = 2.0 × 10⁻⁴ |
| AE₃ | 27s⁴ | ⁴√(1.0 × 10⁻¹⁶) = 1.0 × 10⁻⁴ |

- (A) has the largest K_sp, which is the trap: K_sp values cannot be compared directly when the ion ratios differ.
- (C) is more soluble than AB but five times less soluble than AC₂.
- (D) has by far the smallest K_sp but the same solubility as AB, because its expression has the power 4.
</details>

## Question 4 (multiple choice · foundation)

Which of these salts would you expect to have K_sp greater than 1 at 25 °C?

- (A) AgBr
- (B) BaSO₄
- (C) CaCO₃
- (D) NH₄NO₃

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (D).** All ammonium salts and all nitrate salts are soluble. A soluble salt gives large ion concentrations in a saturated solution, so its K_sp is greater than 1.

- (A), (B) and (C) contain none of the always-soluble ions (Na⁺, K⁺, NH₄⁺, NO₃⁻). Each is a familiar precipitate, so its K_sp is much less than 1. You do not need to memorise extra solubility rules: the ammonium and nitrate rule is enough to pick (D).
</details>

## Question 5 (calculation · core)

The fictional salt LD₂ (molar mass 210.0 g mol⁻¹) dissolves as LD₂(s) ⇌ L²⁺(aq) + 2 D⁻(aq). Its K_sp is 5.0 × 10⁻⁷.

(a) Calculate the molar solubility of LD₂.
(b) State the concentration of D⁻ in the saturated solution.
(c) Calculate the maximum mass of LD₂ that can dissolve in 500.0 mL of water.

<details>
<summary>Worked solution and suggested Marlbridge rubric</summary>

**(a)** K_sp = [L²⁺][D⁻]² = (s)(2s)² = 4s³. So s³ = 5.0 × 10⁻⁷ ÷ 4 = 1.25 × 10⁻⁷ and s = ∛(1.25 × 10⁻⁷) = **5.0 × 10⁻³ mol L⁻¹**.

**(b)** [D⁻] = 2s = **1.0 × 10⁻² mol L⁻¹**.

**(c)** Moles in 0.5000 L = 5.0 × 10⁻³ × 0.5000 = 2.5 × 10⁻³ mol. Mass = 2.5 × 10⁻³ mol × 210.0 g mol⁻¹ = **0.53 g** (0.525 g before rounding to 2 significant figures).

| Point | What earns it |
|---|---|
| 1 | K_sp = 4s³ (or s(2s)²) set up correctly |
| 1 | s = 5.0 × 10⁻³ mol L⁻¹ |
| 1 | [D⁻] = 1.0 × 10⁻² mol L⁻¹ (carry forward 2 × their s) |
| 1 | Mass 0.53 g, using the volume in litres and the molar mass |

Common errors: s = √K_sp = 7.1 × 10⁻⁴ M (treats the salt as 1:1); s = ∛K_sp = 7.9 × 10⁻³ M (forgets the 4).
</details>

## Question 6 (calculation · core)

A saturated solution of the fictional salt M₂X is made by stirring excess solid with water. The salt dissolves as M₂X(s) ⇌ 2 M⁺(aq) + X²⁻(aq). Analysis shows [M⁺] = 2.6 × 10⁻⁴ M in the saturated solution.

(a) Calculate the molar solubility of M₂X and [X²⁻].
(b) Calculate K_sp for M₂X.
(c) A student mixes 25.0 mL of 1.0 × 10⁻³ M MNO₃ with 25.0 mL of 1.0 × 10⁻³ M Na₂X. Predict, with a calculation, whether M₂X precipitates.

<details>
<summary>Worked solution and suggested Marlbridge rubric</summary>

**(a)** Each formula unit releases 2 M⁺, so s = [M⁺] ÷ 2 = **1.3 × 10⁻⁴ mol L⁻¹**, and [X²⁻] = s = **1.3 × 10⁻⁴ M**.

**(b)** K_sp = [M⁺]²[X²⁻] = (2.6 × 10⁻⁴)²(1.3 × 10⁻⁴) = **8.8 × 10⁻¹²**.

**(c)** Mixing doubles the volume, so each concentration halves: [M⁺] = 5.0 × 10⁻⁴ M and [X²⁻] = 5.0 × 10⁻⁴ M.
Q = [M⁺]²[X²⁻] = (5.0 × 10⁻⁴)²(5.0 × 10⁻⁴) = 1.25 × 10⁻¹⁰.
Q > K_sp (about 14 times larger), so the solution holds more ions than equilibrium allows: **M₂X precipitates** until Q falls to K_sp.

| Point | What earns it |
|---|---|
| 1 | s = [M⁺] ÷ 2 = 1.3 × 10⁻⁴ M, with [X²⁻] equal to s |
| 1 | K_sp = 8.8 × 10⁻¹² from [M⁺]²[X²⁻] |
| 1 | Concentrations halved on mixing and Q = 1.25 × 10⁻¹⁰ (or 1.3 × 10⁻¹⁰) |
| 1 | Compares Q with K_sp and concludes a precipitate forms |

Common errors: using 2.6 × 10⁻⁴ M for both ions gives K_sp = 1.8 × 10⁻¹¹ (the X²⁻ value is double what it should be). Forgetting dilution gives Q = 1.0 × 10⁻⁹; the conclusion is the same here, but the method point is lost.
</details>

## Question 7 (constructed response · stretch)

Two fictional salts have the same K_sp at 25 °C, 1.0 × 10⁻¹². Salt GH dissolves as GH(s) ⇌ G⁺(aq) + H⁻(aq); salt GJ₂ dissolves as GJ₂(s) ⇌ G²⁺(aq) + 2 J⁻(aq).

(a) A student claims: "The two salts have the same K_sp, so they are equally soluble." Use calculations to evaluate this claim.
(b) A second student adds another 1 g of solid GH to a beaker of saturated GH solution (with solid already present) and stirs at 25 °C. She predicts that [G⁺] will increase. Is she right? Explain.

<details>
<summary>Worked solution and suggested Marlbridge rubric</summary>

**(a)** For GH: K_sp = s², so s = √(1.0 × 10⁻¹²) = **1.0 × 10⁻⁶ mol L⁻¹**.
For GJ₂: K_sp = 4s³, so s = ∛(2.5 × 10⁻¹³) = **6.3 × 10⁻⁵ mol L⁻¹**.
The claim is **incorrect**: GJ₂ is about 63 times more soluble. The K_sp expressions have different forms (s² against 4s³), so equal K_sp values do not mean equal solubilities. K_sp values compare directly only for salts with the same ion ratio.

**(b)** She is **not** right. The solution is already saturated, so dissolving and precipitating are happening at equal rates. K_sp = [G⁺][H⁻] does not include the solid, so adding more solid does not change Q or K_sp; nothing shifts. [G⁺] stays at 1.0 × 10⁻⁶ M, and the extra solid simply stays undissolved. Only a change in temperature would change K_sp and the ion concentrations.

| Point | What earns it |
|---|---|
| 1 | Both molar solubilities calculated correctly |
| 1 | Claim rejected with the reason that the expressions differ (different ion ratios or powers) |
| 1 | States [G⁺] does not change |
| 1 | Explains that the solid is not in the K_sp expression (or that the solution is already at equilibrium), so Q still equals K_sp |
</details>
