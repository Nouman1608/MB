## Question 1 (multiple choice · 9.1)

For which process is ΔS of the system expected to be **positive**?

- (A) 2CO(g) + O₂(g) → 2CO₂(g)
- (B) PCl₅(g) → PCl₃(g) + Cl₂(g)
- (C) 4NH₃(g) + 5O₂(g) → 4NO(g) + 6H₂O(l)
- (D) Ag⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq) → AgCl(s)

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (B).** One mole of gas becomes two, so matter is more dispersed.

- (A) goes from 3 mol of gas to 2.
- (C) The water is liquid, so gas falls from 9 mol to 4.
- (D) Dispersed ions become fixed in a solid: matter is less dispersed.

**If you missed this:** read "Matter dispersal in reactions" in the [Topic 9.1 study guide](/advanced-course-resources/chemistry/9-1-introduction-entropy-study-guide/).
</details>

## Question 2 (short answer · 9.2)

Methanol burns in a hot flame, where the water forms as a gas:

2CH₃OH(l) + 3O₂(g) → 2CO₂(g) + 4H₂O(g)

S° at 298 K (J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹): CH₃OH(l) 126.8; O₂(g) 205.2; CO₂(g) 213.8; H₂O(g) 188.8.

(a) Predict the sign of ΔS° and give a reason.
(b) Calculate ΔS°.
(c) Calculate the entropy change when 8.01 g of methanol burns.

<details>
<summary>Worked answer</summary>

**(a)** **Positive.** Moles of gas rise from 3 to 6 (liquid methanol does not count).

**(b)** Products: 2(213.8) + 4(188.8) = 1182.8. Reactants: 2(126.8) + 3(205.2) = 869.2.
ΔS° = 1182.8 − 869.2 = **+313.6 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹**, which agrees with (a).

**(c)** n = 8.01 ÷ 32.042 = 0.2500 mol of methanol = 0.1250 mol of reaction (2 methanol per reaction). ΔS = 0.1250 × 313.6 = **+39.2 J K⁻¹**.

**If you missed this:** read Worked example 1 in the [Topic 9.2 study guide](/advanced-course-resources/chemistry/9-2-absolute-entropy-entropy-change-study-guide/).
</details>

## Question 3 (multiple choice · 9.3)

An invented gas-phase reaction has ΔH° = −84.0 kJ mol⁻¹ and ΔS° = −210 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹. Over which range is it thermodynamically favoured?

- (A) Below about 127 °C
- (B) Above about 127 °C
- (C) Below about 400 °C
- (D) At all temperatures, because ΔH° is negative

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (A).** Both signs are negative, so it is favoured only at low T. ΔG° = 0 at T = 84 000 ÷ 210 = 400 K, about 127 °C.

- (B) (−, −) is favoured at **low** T.
- (C) reads 400 K as 400 °C.
- (D) ignores the −TΔS° term, which grows with T.

**If you missed this:** read "The four sign combinations" in the [Topic 9.3 study guide](/advanced-course-resources/chemistry/9-3-gibbs-free-energy-thermodynamic-favorability-study-guide/).
</details>

## Question 4 (multiple choice · 9.3)

ΔG°f at 298 K (kJ mol⁻¹): NH₃(g) −16.4; NO(g) +86.60; H₂O(g) −228.58. What is ΔG° for 4NH₃(g) + 5O₂(g) → 4NO(g) + 6H₂O(g)?

- (A) −959.5 kJ mol⁻¹
- (B) +959.5 kJ mol⁻¹
- (C) −125.6 kJ mol⁻¹
- (D) −1090.7 kJ mol⁻¹

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (A).** Products: 4(+86.60) + 6(−228.58) = −1025.08. Reactants: 4(−16.4) + 5(0) = −65.6. ΔG° = −1025.08 − (−65.6) = **−959.5 kJ mol⁻¹**. ΔG°f of O₂, an element, is zero.

- (B) is reactants minus products.
- (C) ignores the coefficients.
- (D) adds the reactant term instead of subtracting it.

**If you missed this:** read Method 1 in the [Topic 9.3 study guide](/advanced-course-resources/chemistry/9-3-gibbs-free-energy-thermodynamic-favorability-study-guide/).
</details>

## Question 5 (multiple choice · 9.4)

Paper sits in air for centuries without burning, yet the combustion of cellulose, its main substance, has a large negative ΔG°. Which conclusion is best supported?

- (A) It is under kinetic control: the activation energy is so high that the rate at room temperature is too small to measure.
- (B) Paper and oxygen are at equilibrium, so Q = K.
- (C) K for the combustion must be less than 1 at room temperature.
- (D) ΔG° becomes positive at room temperature.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (A).** A favoured process with no measurable rate is under kinetic control. A flame gets it over the barrier.

- (B) No change is not equilibrium: at equilibrium almost no paper would remain.
- (C) ΔG° < 0 means K > 1.
- (D) Nothing here changes the sign of ΔG°.

**If you missed this:** read "Kinetic control is not equilibrium" in the [Topic 9.4 study guide](/advanced-course-resources/chemistry/9-4-thermodynamic-kinetic-control-study-guide/).
</details>

## Question 6 (multiple choice · 9.5)

A reaction has K = 1.0 at 350 K, and ΔH° is positive. Assume ΔH° and ΔS° are constant. Which is true at 298 K?

- (A) ΔG° > 0 and K < 1
- (B) ΔG° < 0 and K > 1
- (C) ΔG° = 0 and K = 1
- (D) ΔG° > 0 and K < 0

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (A).** K = 1 means ΔG° = 0 at 350 K, so ΔS° = ΔH° ÷ 350 K > 0. With both positive, the reaction is favoured only above 350 K. At 298 K, ΔG° > 0 and K < 1.

- (B) describes temperatures above 350 K.
- (C) K changes with T, because ΔG° does.
- (D) K = e^(−ΔG°/RT) is never negative.

**If you missed this:** read "Where temperature comes in" in the [Topic 9.5 study guide](/advanced-course-resources/chemistry/9-5-free-energy-equilibrium-study-guide/).
</details>

## Question 7 (multiple choice · 9.6)

Dissolving which salt gives the most negative entropy change from **reorganising the water** around the ions?

- (A) CsI
- (B) KBr
- (C) NaCl
- (D) MgF₂

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (D).** Small, highly charged ions such as Mg²⁺ (and small F⁻) hold water molecules in tight, ordered shells, so the water loses the most entropy.

- (A) Cs⁺ and I⁻ are large and singly charged: they order the least water.
- (B) and (C) have singly charged ions, which order far less water than Mg²⁺.

**If you missed this:** read "Three factors at the particle level" in the [Topic 9.6 study guide](/advanced-course-resources/chemistry/9-6-free-energy-dissolution-study-guide/).
</details>

## Question 8 (multiple choice · 9.7)

Two invented reactions are coupled: X → Y, ΔG° = +18.0 kJ mol⁻¹, and Y + Z → W, ΔG° = −25.0 kJ mol⁻¹. Which row is correct for the overall reaction X + Z → W?

- (A) ΔG° = −7.0 kJ mol⁻¹; Y is the common intermediate
- (B) ΔG° = +43.0 kJ mol⁻¹; Y is the common intermediate
- (C) ΔG° = −7.0 kJ mol⁻¹; Z is the common intermediate
- (D) ΔG° = −43.0 kJ mol⁻¹; W is the common intermediate

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (A).** Y, made in the first reaction and used in the second, cancels. ΔG° values add: +18.0 + (−25.0) = −7.0 kJ mol⁻¹, favoured.

- (B) ignores the sign of the second value.
- (C) Z is a reactant overall, not an intermediate.
- (D) W is the product.

**If you missed this:** read about the common intermediate in the [Topic 9.7 study guide](/advanced-course-resources/chemistry/9-7-coupled-reactions-study-guide/).
</details>

## Question 9 (multiple choice · 9.8)

To purify copper, an impure copper bar and a pure copper sheet dip into CuSO₄(aq) and are connected to a power supply. The pure sheet grows. Which statement is correct?

- (A) The impure bar is the anode: Cu(s) → Cu²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ happens there, and it loses mass.
- (B) The impure bar is the cathode, because reduction happens at the electrode that loses mass.
- (C) Electrons travel through the solution from the impure bar to the pure sheet.
- (D) The pure sheet is the anode, because Cu²⁺ ions are made there.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (A).** Cu²⁺ is reduced at the growing sheet (the cathode), so oxidation happens at the impure bar (the anode), which dissolves.

- (B) The electrode losing mass is oxidised: the anode.
- (C) Electrons stay in the wire; ions carry charge in solution.
- (D) Cu²⁺ is used up at the sheet.

**If you missed this:** read "Electrolytic cells" in the [Topic 9.8 study guide](/advanced-course-resources/chemistry/9-8-galvanic-voltaic-electrolytic-cells-study-guide/).
</details>

## Question 10 (multiple choice · 9.9)

What is ΔG° for Ni(s) + 2Ag⁺(aq) → Ni²⁺(aq) + 2Ag(s)?

- (A) −203 kJ mol⁻¹
- (B) −101 kJ mol⁻¹
- (C) −357 kJ mol⁻¹
- (D) +203 kJ mol⁻¹

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (A).** Ag⁺ is reduced: E° = 0.80 − (−0.25) = +1.05 V. With n = 2, ΔG° = −nFE° = −(2)(96,485)(1.05) = −202,600 J = **−203 kJ mol⁻¹**.

- (B) uses n = 1, counting the electrons for one Ag⁺ only.
- (C) doubles the silver potential. E° is never multiplied by coefficients.
- (D) A positive E° always gives a negative ΔG°.

**If you missed this:** read Worked example 1 in the [Topic 9.9 study guide](/advanced-course-resources/chemistry/9-9-cell-potential-free-energy-study-guide/).
</details>

## Question 11 (multiple choice · 9.9)

Under standard conditions, which metal reduces Sn²⁺(aq) to tin but does **not** reduce Fe²⁺(aq) to iron?

- (A) Nickel
- (B) Zinc
- (C) Copper
- (D) Magnesium

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (A).** With Sn²⁺: E° = −0.14 − (−0.25) = +0.11 V, favoured. With Fe²⁺: E° = −0.44 − (−0.25) = −0.19 V, not favoured.

- (B) Zinc reduces both (+0.62 V and +0.32 V).
- (C) Copper reduces neither: −0.48 V and −0.78 V.
- (D) Magnesium reduces both.

**If you missed this:** read about the sign of E°cell in the [Topic 9.9 study guide](/advanced-course-resources/chemistry/9-9-cell-potential-free-energy-study-guide/).
</details>

## Question 12 (multiple choice · 9.10)

A cell runs Ni(s) + 2Ag⁺(aq) → Ni²⁺(aq) + 2Ag(s). Which starting concentrations give the **largest** cell potential?

- (A) [Ni²⁺] = 0.010 M, [Ag⁺] = 1.0 M
- (B) [Ni²⁺] = 1.0 M, [Ag⁺] = 1.0 M
- (C) [Ni²⁺] = 1.0 M, [Ag⁺] = 0.10 M
- (D) [Ni²⁺] = 0.10 M, [Ag⁺] = 0.10 M

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (A).** Q = [Ni²⁺] ÷ [Ag⁺]² is 0.010 in (A), 1 in (B), 100 in (C) and 10 in (D). The smallest Q puts the cell **furthest from equilibrium**, so E is largest (about 1.11 V; E° = 1.05 V).

- (B) is standard: E = E°.
- (C) Q > 1, closer to equilibrium: E < E°.
- (D) Equal dilution still changes E, because [Ag⁺] is squared: Q = 10, E ≈ 1.02 V.

**If you missed this:** read "Comparing Q with 1 and with K" in the [Topic 9.10 study guide](/advanced-course-resources/chemistry/9-10-cell-potential-under-nonstandard-conditions-study-guide/).
</details>

## Question 13 (short answer · 9.11)

A cell making aluminium runs at a steady 1.50 × 10⁵ A. Al³⁺ ions are reduced at the cathode.

(a) Calculate the mass of aluminium made in 1.00 hour.
(b) The same charge passes through molten NaCl. How do the moles of sodium made compare with the moles of aluminium? Explain.

<details>
<summary>Worked answer</summary>

**(a)**

1. q = I × t = 1.50 × 10⁵ × 3600 s = 5.40 × 10⁸ C, so n(e⁻) = 5.40 × 10⁸ ÷ 96,485 = 5597 mol.
2. Al³⁺ + 3e⁻ → Al: n(Al) = 5597 ÷ 3 = 1866 mol; m = 1866 × 26.98 g = **50.3 kg**.

**(b)** **Three times as many.** Na⁺ + e⁻ → Na needs one electron per atom; Al needs three. So 5597 mol of electrons give 5597 mol of Na.

**If you missed this:** read Worked example 1 in the [Topic 9.11 study guide](/advanced-course-resources/chemistry/9-11-electrolysis-faradays-law-study-guide/).
</details>
