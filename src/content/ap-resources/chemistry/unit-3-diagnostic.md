## What this diagnostic is for

Use this page to find **which Unit 3 topics to revisit**. There is one question per topic, and two for Topic 3.1, which the rest of the unit builds on. Answer every question before opening any answer.

These are **original Marlbridge practice questions**, not past exam questions. They are not calibrated and give no predicted score: a wrong answer points to a topic, not a grade.

**Calculator and data.** A scientific calculator is allowed. R = 0.08206 L atm mol⁻¹ K⁻¹; K = °C + 273.15; h = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J s; c = 2.998 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹; N_A = 6.022 × 10²³ mol⁻¹. Molar masses: H 1.008, He 4.003, C 12.01 g mol⁻¹. Treat gases as ideal.

## Question 1 (multiple choice · 3.1)

Between the molecules of which substance do **only** London dispersion forces act?

- (A) Dichloromethane, CH₂Cl₂
- (B) Carbon dioxide, CO₂
- (C) Hydrogen sulfide, H₂S
- (D) Methylamine, CH₃NH₂

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (B).** CO₂ is linear, so its two C=O bond dipoles cancel: nonpolar, dispersion only.

- (A) In CH₂Cl₂ the C–Cl and C–H dipoles do not cancel.
- (C) H₂S is bent, so polar.
- (D) H on N: hydrogen bonding.

**If you missed this:** read "Forces that need a permanent dipole" in the [Topic 3.1 study guide](/advanced-course-resources/chemistry/3-1-intermolecular-interparticle-forces-study-guide/).
</details>

## Question 2 (multiple choice · 3.1)

Which hydrogen halide has the highest boiling point?

- (A) HI
- (B) HBr
- (C) HCl
- (D) HF

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (D).** Only HF molecules hydrogen bond (H on F). Boiling points: HF about 20 °C, HCl −85 °C, HBr −67 °C, HI −35 °C.

- (A) is the "most electrons" trap. Dispersion explains HCl < HBr < HI, but hydrogen bonding in HF outweighs it.
- (B) and (C) have fewer electrons than HI and no hydrogen bonding.

**If you missed this:** read "Hydrogen bonding" in the [Topic 3.1 study guide](/advanced-course-resources/chemistry/3-1-intermolecular-interparticle-forces-study-guide/).
</details>

## Question 3 (multiple choice · 3.2)

Why does a crystal of an ionic solid shatter when struck with a hammer?

- (A) Only intermolecular forces hold neighbouring layers together.
- (B) Ionic bonds are weak, so a small force breaks them.
- (C) A shift of one layer lines up ions of the same charge, and they repel.
- (D) The blow releases mobile electrons, which push the layers apart.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (C).** Each ion is surrounded by opposite charges. A small shift brings like charges face to face, and they repel.

- (A) An ionic solid has no molecules; it is held by ion–ion attractions.
- (B) Ionic attractions are strong, which is why melting points are high.
- (D) Ionic solids have no mobile electrons, which is why they do not conduct.

**If you missed this:** read "Ionic solids" in the [Topic 3.2 study guide](/advanced-course-resources/chemistry/3-2-properties-solids-study-guide/).
</details>

## Question 4 (multiple choice · 3.3)

A gas can be squeezed into a much smaller volume, a liquid hardly at all. Why?

- (A) Gas particles are far apart with empty space between them; liquid particles already touch.
- (B) Gas particles are smaller than liquid particles of the same substance.
- (C) Liquid particles are joined by covalent bonds; gas particles are not.
- (D) Gas particles move more slowly, so they are easier to push together.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (A).** Compressing a gas removes empty space; a liquid has almost none.

- (B) The molecules are the same in both phases.
- (C) Liquid molecules are held by intermolecular forces, not bonds between molecules.
- (D) Speed does not set compressibility.

**If you missed this:** read "Why solids and liquids have similar molar volumes" in the [Topic 3.3 study guide](/advanced-course-resources/chemistry/3-3-solids-liquids-gases-study-guide/).
</details>

## Question 5 (short answer · 3.4)

A rigid 1.50 L flask at 27.0 °C contains 0.0600 mol of N₂ and some helium. The total pressure is 1.64 atm.

(a) Calculate the partial pressure of N₂.
(b) Calculate the amount of helium, in moles.
(c) Calculate the mole fraction of helium.

<details>
<summary>Worked answer</summary>

**(a)** T = 300.15 K. P(N₂) = nRT/V = 0.0600 × 0.08206 × 300.15 ÷ 1.50 = **0.985 atm**.

**(b)** P(He) = 1.64 − 0.985 = 0.655 atm, so n(He) = PV/RT = 0.655 × 1.50 ÷ (0.08206 × 300.15) = **0.0399 mol**.

**(c)** X(He) = 0.0399 ÷ 0.0999 = **0.399**. Check: 0.655 ÷ 1.64 = 0.399.

Common slip: using 27.0 instead of 300.15 K.

**If you missed this:** read "Mixtures of gases: partial pressures" in the [Topic 3.4 study guide](/advanced-course-resources/chemistry/3-4-ideal-gas-law-study-guide/).
</details>

## Question 6 (multiple choice · 3.5)

Helium is at 200 K. At about what temperature would methane, CH₄, molecules have the same average speed as the helium atoms?

- (A) 50 K
- (B) 200 K
- (C) 800 K
- (D) 3200 K

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (C).** Average kinetic energy, ½mv², is proportional to Kelvin temperature. For equal speeds, T must scale with mass: 16.04 ÷ 4.003 = 4.01, so T ≈ 800 K.

- (A) divides by the mass ratio; a heavier molecule needs a higher temperature.
- (B) gives equal kinetic energy, not equal speed.
- (D) squares the mass ratio.

**If you missed this:** read "Comparing speeds of different gases" in the [Topic 3.5 study guide](/advanced-course-resources/chemistry/3-5-kinetic-molecular-theory-study-guide/).
</details>

## Question 7 (multiple choice · 3.6)

At very high pressure, a gas occupies a larger volume than the ideal gas law predicts. Which assumption of the kinetic molecular theory has failed?

- (A) Collisions between particles are elastic.
- (B) The particles take up a negligible share of the container's volume.
- (C) Average kinetic energy is proportional to Kelvin temperature.
- (D) The particles move randomly in straight lines between collisions.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (B).** Closely packed particles fill a real share of the space, so the sample needs more than the ideal volume.

- (A), (C) and (D) do not explain an oversized volume.

**If you missed this:** read "Effect 2: particle volume raises the volume (or pressure)" in the [Topic 3.6 study guide](/advanced-course-resources/chemistry/3-6-deviation-ideal-gas-law-study-guide/).
</details>

## Question 8 (multiple choice · 3.7)

What volume of 0.250 M sodium phosphate, Na₃PO₄, contains 0.0450 mol of Na⁺?

- (A) 11.3 mL
- (B) 180 mL
- (C) 540 mL
- (D) 60.0 mL

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (D).** Each Na₃PO₄ gives 3 Na⁺: n(Na₃PO₄) = 0.0150 mol, and V = 0.0150 ÷ 0.250 = 0.0600 L.

- (A) multiplies moles by molarity.
- (B) ignores the 3 Na⁺ per formula unit.
- (C) multiplies by 3 instead of dividing.

**If you missed this:** read "Ionic solutes: each ion has its own concentration" in the [Topic 3.7 study guide](/advanced-course-resources/chemistry/3-7-solutions-mixtures-study-guide/).
</details>

## Question 9 (multiple choice · 3.8)

A particle diagram represents 0.20 L of Al₂(SO₄)₃(aq) and shows 4 Al³⁺ ions. Each ion drawn stands for 0.010 mol. How many sulfate ions should it show, and what is [SO₄²⁻]?

- (A) 6; 0.060 M
- (B) 6; 0.30 M
- (C) 4; 0.20 M
- (D) 12; 0.60 M

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (B).** Al³⁺ : SO₄²⁻ is 2 : 3, so 6 sulfate ions (charge check: 4(+3) + 6(−2) = 0). 0.060 mol ÷ 0.20 L = 0.30 M.

- (A) gives moles, not moles per litre.
- (C) draws equal numbers, as if the charges matched.
- (D) uses 3 sulfate per Al³⁺, leaving a net charge of −12.

**If you missed this:** read "Reading concentration from a diagram" in the [Topic 3.8 study guide](/advanced-course-resources/chemistry/3-8-representations-solutions-study-guide/).
</details>

## Question 10 (multiple choice · 3.9)

Propan-1-ol, CH₃CH₂CH₂OH, and its isomer methoxyethane, CH₃OCH₂CH₃, are mixed and distilled. Which statement about the first distillate is correct?

- (A) It is rich in methoxyethane: its molecules cannot hydrogen bond to each other, so its vapor pressure is higher.
- (B) It is rich in propan-1-ol, because its O–H bonds break easily when heated.
- (C) It matches the mixture, because the isomers have the same molar mass.
- (D) It is rich in methoxyethane, because its molecules have fewer electrons.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (A).** Propan-1-ol hydrogen bonds (O–H); methoxyethane has no H on O. Its weaker attractions let more molecules escape, so the vapor is rich in it.

- (B) Distillation separates whole molecules; no covalent bonds break.
- (C) and (D) Both have 34 electrons and similar dispersion forces; hydrogen bonding makes the difference.

**If you missed this:** read "Distillation: using vapor pressure" in the [Topic 3.9 study guide](/advanced-course-resources/chemistry/3-9-separation-solutions-mixtures-study-guide/).
</details>

## Question 11 (short answer · 3.10)

Ammonia, NH₃, and methane, CH₄, both have 10 electrons. Ammonia is very soluble in water; methane is only slightly soluble.

(a) Name the strongest attraction between water and (i) NH₃, (ii) CH₄.
(b) Explain the difference in solubility using the attractions broken and formed.

<details>
<summary>Worked answer</summary>

**(a)** (i) **Hydrogen bonding**: NH₃ donates (N–H) and accepts (lone pair on N). (ii) **Dipole–induced dipole**, since CH₄ is nonpolar.

**(b)** Dissolving breaks some water–water hydrogen bonds. NH₃ replaces them with similar hydrogen bonds to water; CH₄ offers only much weaker attractions, so little dissolves.

**If you missed this:** read "Dissolving at the particle level" in the [Topic 3.10 study guide](/advanced-course-resources/chemistry/3-10-solubility-study-guide/).
</details>

## Question 12 (multiple choice · 3.11)

For one molecule, which order of energy gaps runs from **smallest** to **largest**?

- (A) rotational < electronic < vibrational
- (B) vibrational < rotational < electronic
- (C) electronic < vibrational < rotational
- (D) rotational < vibrational < electronic

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (D).** Rotational gaps match microwave photons, vibrational gaps infrared, and electronic gaps visible and ultraviolet.

- (A) UV and visible photons carry more energy than infrared ones.
- (B) Microwaves have longer wavelengths than infrared, so lower photon energy.
- (C) reverses the order: the "longer wavelength, more energy" error.

**If you missed this:** read "Matching the region to the transition" in the [Topic 3.11 study guide](/advanced-course-resources/chemistry/3-11-spectroscopy-electromagnetic-spectrum-study-guide/).
</details>

## Question 13 (short answer · 3.12)

An excited atom drops to a lower level and emits light of wavelength 512 nm.

(a) Calculate the frequency.
(b) Calculate the energy of one photon, and of one mole of photons in kJ mol⁻¹.
(c) What does the photon energy tell you about the atom?

<details>
<summary>Worked answer</summary>

**(a)** ν = c/λ = 2.998 × 10⁸ ÷ 5.12 × 10⁻⁷ = **5.86 × 10¹⁴ s⁻¹**.

**(b)** E = hν = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ × 5.856 × 10¹⁴ = **3.88 × 10⁻¹⁹ J**. × 6.022 × 10²³ = **234 kJ mol⁻¹**.

**(c)** It **equals the gap** between the two energy levels.

**If you missed this:** read "The two equations" in the [Topic 3.12 study guide](/advanced-course-resources/chemistry/3-12-properties-photons-study-guide/).
</details>

## Question 14 (multiple choice · 3.13)

A student makes calibration standards from a dye stock solution that had partly evaporated, so each standard is more concentrated than its label says. The concentration she then finds for an unknown will be

- (A) correct, because the calibration curve corrects for errors in the standards.
- (B) too high, because the standards absorb more light.
- (C) too low, because the standards read too high for their labels, so the line is too steep.
- (D) too high, because evaporation increases the molar absorptivity.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (C).** Each standard is plotted at its label but absorbs for its true, higher concentration, so the slope (εb) is too large and c = A ÷ slope too low.

- (A) A calibration curve is only as good as its standards.
- (B) gets the direction wrong.
- (D) ε belongs to the dye at that wavelength; it does not change with concentration.

**If you missed this:** read "Calibration curves" and Worked example 3 in the [Topic 3.13 study guide](/advanced-course-resources/chemistry/3-13-beer-lambert-law-study-guide/).
</details>
