## Recap

- **Soluble**: a noticeable amount dissolves. **Miscible**: two liquids mix in all proportions. **Immiscible**: they form two layers.
- Dissolving overcomes **solute–solute** and **solvent–solvent** attractions and forms **solute–solvent** attractions.
- It happens readily when the new attractions are comparable to those lost, which is when solute and solvent have **similar intermolecular interactions**: "like dissolves like".
- Nonpolar substances stay out of water because water molecules hold **each other** tightly by hydrogen bonds, not because water repels them.

## Key relationships

| Solute type | Dissolves well in | Main solute–solvent interaction |
|---|---|---|
| Ionic (e.g. NaCl) | Water (often) | Ion–dipole |
| Molecules with O–H or N–H (e.g. ethanol, glucose) | Water | Hydrogen bonding |
| Nonpolar (e.g. I₂, alkanes, wax) | Hexane and other nonpolar solvents | London dispersion |
| Polar head + long nonpolar chain (e.g. 1-hexanol) | Less in water, more in hexane as the chain grows | Both, in competition |

**Water around ions:** δ− oxygen ends face **cations**; δ+ hydrogen ends face **anions**.

**Always soluble in water (needed in Unit 4):** all sodium, potassium, ammonium and nitrate salts.

## Assumptions and limits of the model

- "Like dissolves like" compares **types** of interaction. It ignores their **strengths**, so it can fail: CaCO₃ and AgCl are ionic but barely dissolve.
- It is qualitative: it predicts which solvent is better, not how much dissolves.
- Mixing also spreads particles out, which favours dissolving; that idea is developed in a later unit.

## Mistakes to avoid

1. **"Water repels hexane."** It attracts hexane weakly; it attracts itself far more strongly.
2. **"Ionic = soluble."** Check the strength of the ion–ion attractions, and learn the always-soluble list.
3. **Drawing water backwards** around an ion. Opposite partial and full charges face each other.
4. **"Dissolving breaks covalent bonds."** Molecular solutes such as glucose stay intact.
5. **Answering "like dissolves like" with nothing else.** Name the forces in the solute, in the solvent and between them.
6. **Confusing density with mixing.** Density decides which layer is on top, not whether liquids mix.

## Quick self-check

1. Which way do water molecules face a Mg²⁺ ion? *(Oxygen ends, δ−, toward the ion.)*
2. Is I₂ more soluble in water or in hexane? Why? *(Hexane: both nonpolar, so dispersion forces replace dispersion forces.)*
3. Put in order of increasing solubility in water: 1-hexanol, ethanol, 1-butanol. *(1-hexanol < 1-butanol < ethanol)*
4. Why do ethanol and water mix in all proportions? *(Ethanol–water hydrogen bonds replace the ethanol–ethanol and water–water hydrogen bonds that are lost.)*
