These are **original Marlbridge practice questions**, not past exam questions. All samples and data are invented for practice. The mark points are a suggested Marlbridge rubric to help you check your work; they are not an official scoring guideline. Data for every question: C 12.01, O 16.00, Na 22.99, Mg 24.31, S 32.06, Cl 35.45, K 39.10, Ca 40.08, Fe 55.85, Cu 63.55 g mol⁻¹.

## Question 1 (multiple choice · foundation)

Which of the following samples is a mixture?

- (A) Solid carbon dioxide, made only of CO₂ molecules
- (B) A gas made of N₂ molecules and O₂ molecules
- (C) Crystals of sodium chloride, made only of NaCl formula units
- (D) Liquid bromine, made only of Br₂ molecules

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (B).** A mixture contains two or more types of particle. Option (B) has two: N₂ molecules and O₂ molecules. Their proportions could change and it would still be "a mixture of nitrogen and oxygen".

- (A) contains two elements, but every particle is an identical CO₂ molecule, so it is a pure compound.
- (C) is a pure ionic compound: one type of formula unit, even though it is made of two kinds of ion.
- (D) is a pure element. Bromine exists as Br₂ molecules, but they are all the same.
</details>

## Question 2 (multiple choice · core)

A white powder is a mixture of magnesium oxide, MgO, and calcium oxide, CaO, only. Which of the following could be the mass percent of oxygen in the powder?

- (A) 25.0%
- (B) 34.0%
- (C) 42.0%
- (D) 68.2%

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (B).** Find the limits set by the two pure compounds.

- Pure MgO: 16.00 ÷ 40.31 × 100 = 39.69% O.
- Pure CaO: 16.00 ÷ 56.08 × 100 = 28.53% O.

Any mixture of only these two must lie between 28.53% and 39.69%. Only 34.0% is in that range.

- (A) is below the CaO value. A powder with this much less oxygen would need a third component with little or no oxygen.
- (C) is above the MgO value, which no mix of the two can reach.
- (D) adds the two percentages (39.69 + 28.53). Mass percents of a mixture are averaged (weighted by mass), never added.
</details>

## Question 3 (multiple choice · core)

A 5.00 g sample of impure calcium carbonate, CaCO₃, is analysed and found to contain 1.80 g of calcium. The impurity contains no calcium. What is the percent by mass of CaCO₃ in the sample?

- (A) 36.0%
- (B) 40.0%
- (C) 89.9%
- (D) 111%

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (C).**

1. Mass fraction of Ca in CaCO₃ = 40.08 ÷ 100.09 = 0.40044.
2. Mass of CaCO₃ = 1.80 g ÷ 0.40044 = 4.495 g.
3. Purity = 4.495 g ÷ 5.00 g × 100 = 89.9%.

- (A) is 1.80 ÷ 5.00 × 100: the mass percent of **calcium** in the sample, not of calcium carbonate.
- (B) is the mass percent of calcium in **pure** CaCO₃ (40.04%), which ignores the measured data.
- (D) multiplies by the mass fraction instead of dividing (5.00 × 0.40044 ÷ 1.80). A purity above 100% is impossible, which is a warning sign.
</details>

## Question 4 (calculation · core)

A 3.000 g sample is a mixture of sodium sulfate, Na₂SO₄, and sodium chloride, NaCl. Elemental analysis shows that it contains 0.5000 g of sulfur.

(a) Calculate the percent by mass of Na₂SO₄ in the mixture.
(b) Calculate the total mass of sodium in the sample.

<details>
<summary>Worked solution and suggested Marlbridge rubric</summary>

**(a)** Only Na₂SO₄ contains sulfur, so all 0.5000 g of S came from it.

1. M(Na₂SO₄) = 2(22.99) + 32.06 + 4(16.00) = 142.04 g mol⁻¹.
2. Mass fraction of S = 32.06 ÷ 142.04 = 0.22571.
3. Mass of Na₂SO₄ = 0.5000 g ÷ 0.22571 = 2.2152 g.
4. Percent by mass = 2.2152 ÷ 3.000 × 100 = **73.84%**.

*Mole route:* n(S) = 0.5000 ÷ 32.06 = 0.015596 mol = n(Na₂SO₄); m = 0.015596 × 142.04 = 2.2152 g.

**(b)** Sodium comes from **both** salts.

1. Mass of NaCl = 3.000 − 2.2152 = 0.7848 g.
2. Na in Na₂SO₄ = 2.2152 g × (45.98 ÷ 142.04) = 0.7171 g.
3. Na in NaCl = 0.7848 g × (22.99 ÷ 58.44) = 0.3087 g.
4. Total Na = 0.7171 + 0.3087 = **1.026 g**.

| Point | What earns it |
|---|---|
| 1 | Uses the sulfur mass to find the mass (or moles) of Na₂SO₄, with all S assigned to Na₂SO₄ |
| 1 | 73.84% Na₂SO₄ (accept 73.8%) |
| 1 | Finds the mass of NaCl by difference and recognises that both salts contain sodium |
| 1 | 1.026 g of sodium (accept 1.03 g), carrying forward the answer to (a) |

Common error: reporting 0.5000 ÷ 3.000 × 100 = 16.67% as the percent of Na₂SO₄. That is the percent of sulfur.
</details>

## Question 5 (calculation · stretch)

A sample of black iron-oxide powder is a mixture of iron(II) oxide, FeO, and iron(III) oxide, Fe₂O₃, only. Analysis shows the powder is 74.00% iron by mass.

(a) Show that the powder cannot be pure FeO or pure Fe₂O₃.
(b) Calculate the percent by mass of FeO in the powder.

<details>
<summary>Worked solution and suggested Marlbridge rubric</summary>

**(a)** M(FeO) = 55.85 + 16.00 = 71.85 g mol⁻¹, so FeO is 55.85 ÷ 71.85 × 100 = **77.73% Fe**.
M(Fe₂O₃) = 2(55.85) + 3(16.00) = 159.70 g mol⁻¹, so Fe₂O₃ is 111.70 ÷ 159.70 × 100 = **69.94% Fe**.
74.00% matches neither fixed value, so the powder is not either pure compound. It lies between them, so a mixture of the two is possible.

**(b)** Let x be the mass fraction of FeO. Then (1 − x) is the mass fraction of Fe₂O₃.

0.77731x + 0.69944(1 − x) = 0.7400
0.07788x = 0.04056
x = 0.5209

The powder is **52.1% FeO by mass** (and 47.9% Fe₂O₃).

*Alternative:* take a 100.0 g sample containing 74.00 g Fe and set up a two-equation system in moles, n(FeO) + 2n(Fe₂O₃) = 74.00 ÷ 55.85 and 71.85n(FeO) + 159.70n(Fe₂O₃) = 100.0. This gives the same 52.1 g of FeO.

| Point | What earns it |
|---|---|
| 1 | Both mass percents of iron calculated correctly |
| 1 | States that 74.00% matches neither pure value |
| 1 | Correct equation (mass or mole route) that accounts for iron from both oxides |
| 1 | 52.1% FeO (accept 52.0–52.2%) |

Check: 52.1% is just over half, and 74.00% is just over halfway from 69.94% to 77.73%, so the answer is sensible.
</details>

## Question 6 (constructed response · core)

A technician has two bottles, P and Q, both labelled "copper(II) oxide, CuO". She takes three samples from each bottle and measures the percent of copper by mass.

| Sample | Bottle P: % Cu | Bottle Q: % Cu |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 79.9 | 75.2 |
| 2 | 79.8 | 77.0 |
| 3 | 79.9 | 73.9 |

(a) Calculate the percent by mass of copper in pure CuO.
(b) Identify which bottle contains a mixture. Justify your answer with **two** pieces of evidence from the table.
(c) Assume the impurity in bottle Q contains no copper. Calculate the percent of CuO in sample 1 from bottle Q.

<details>
<summary>Worked solution and suggested Marlbridge rubric</summary>

**(a)** M(CuO) = 63.55 + 16.00 = 79.55 g mol⁻¹. Percent Cu = 63.55 ÷ 79.55 × 100 = **79.89%**.

**(b)** **Bottle Q** contains a mixture.

- Evidence 1: every Q value is **below** 79.89%, the fixed value for pure CuO. Something with less copper (or none) is present.
- Evidence 2: the Q values **vary** from sample to sample (73.9% to 77.0%). A pure compound has a fixed composition, so its samples agree, as bottle P's do within 0.1%. Varying composition is a property of mixtures.

**(c)** Mass of CuO per 100 g of sample = 75.2 g ÷ 0.79887 = 94.13 g, so the sample is **94.1% CuO**.

| Point | What earns it |
|---|---|
| 1 | 79.89% Cu in CuO (accept 79.9%) |
| 1 | Identifies Q and uses the fact that its values are lower than the pure value |
| 1 | Uses the variation between Q samples, linked to the idea that a pure compound has a fixed composition |
| 1 | 94.1% CuO |

Do not award the points in (b) for "Q is a mixture" with no evidence from the data. The variation in bottle Q also suggests the impurity is not spread evenly through the bottle.
</details>

## Question 7 (explanation · stretch)

A sample sold as "potassium chloride, KCl" is analysed. The results give a mole ratio of potassium atoms to chlorine atoms of 1 : 1.12.

(a) Explain how this result shows that the sample is not pure KCl.
(b) A student suggests the impurity is sand, which contains neither potassium nor chlorine. Evaluate this suggestion.
(c) Describe what kind of substance the impurity could be.

<details>
<summary>Model answer and suggested Marlbridge rubric</summary>

**(a)** In pure KCl every formula unit contains one K and one Cl, so the ratio must be exactly 1 : 1 in any pure sample. A ratio of 1 : 1.12 means there is extra chlorine that did not come from KCl, so a second substance is present.

**(b)** The suggestion is **not consistent** with the data. Sand adds mass but contains neither K nor Cl, so it would leave the K : Cl ratio at 1 : 1. (Sand could lower the mass percent of each element, but it cannot change their mole ratio.) The sample may also contain sand, but sand alone cannot explain the result.

**(c)** The impurity must contain chlorine but less than one potassium per chlorine (usually no potassium at all), for example a chloride of another metal such as MgCl₂ or CaCl₂. Elemental analysis of K and Cl alone cannot say which one; analysing for other elements, such as Mg or Ca, would identify it.

| Point | What earns it |
|---|---|
| 1 | States that pure KCl has a fixed 1 : 1 ratio, so 1 : 1.12 needs a second chlorine-containing substance |
| 1 | Explains that sand contains neither element and so cannot change the K : Cl ratio |
| 1 | Identifies the impurity as a chlorine-containing substance without matching potassium, with a valid example or a valid follow-up analysis |
</details>
