## Understanding

- I can define the standard enthalpy of formation, including "1 mol" and "elements in their standard states". *(Guide: "What a standard enthalpy of formation is")*
- I can write the formation equation for a compound, using fractions where needed. *(Practice Q2)*
- I can explain why an element in its standard state has ΔH°f = 0, and name forms of elements that are not zero. *(Guide; Practice Q1, Q6)*
- I can explain why the formula works, using a detour through the elements. *(Guide: Figure 1)*

## Calculation

- I can calculate ΔH° as ΣnΔH°f(products) − ΣnΔH°f(reactants), with every coefficient applied. *(Guide: Worked examples 1 and 2; Practice Q3, Q4)*
- I can choose the right value for the state shown, such as H₂O(l) or H₂O(g). *(Guide: Worked example 2; Practice Q5)*
- I can scale ΔH° to a given mass or amount of one substance. *(Practice Q5)*
- I can calculate ΔH° for a physical change, such as vaporising water. *(Guide: "Physical processes too")*
- I can work backwards from a known ΔH° to find one unknown ΔH°f. *(Guide: Worked example 3; Practice Q6)*

## Reasoning

- I can use the sign of ΔH° to say whether a process is exothermic or endothermic. *(Guide: Worked example 1; Practice Q4)*
- I can describe an energy diagram with the elements at 0 and explain why a reaction between substances with positive ΔH°f can still be exothermic. *(Practice Q7)*
- I can explain why formation enthalpies give more accurate reaction enthalpies than bond enthalpies. *(Guide: "Why we need a table of formation enthalpies")*
