## Acids and bases as proton donors and acceptors

In Topic 4.7 you met acid-base reactions as reactions that move a proton from one species to another. The **Brønsted-Lowry model** gives the two roles names:

- A **Brønsted-Lowry acid** is a **proton donor**. It gives away an H⁺.
- A **Brønsted-Lowry base** is a **proton acceptor**. It takes an H⁺, using a lone pair of electrons to form a new bond to it.

A hydrogen atom that has lost its electron is just a proton, so "H⁺" and "proton" mean the same thing here. An acid must therefore contain a hydrogen atom that it can give away. A base needs a lone pair to hold the incoming proton. Notice that a base does not have to contain OH⁻. Ammonia, NH₃, and the carbonate ion, CO₃²⁻, are both bases because they can accept a proton.

The model describes a *reaction*. A species is an acid or a base because of what it does in that reaction. You will see below that the same species can be an acid in one reaction and a base in another.

The course focuses on acid-base reactions **in aqueous solution**. Another model, the Lewis model, describes acids and bases in terms of electron pairs; it is not assessed in this course.

## Conjugate acid-base pairs

When an acid gives away its proton, what is left can take a proton back. So the leftover species is a base, called the acid's **conjugate base**. In the same way, when a base accepts a proton it becomes the **conjugate acid** of that base.

A **conjugate acid-base pair** is two species that differ by **exactly one H⁺**:

- Conjugate base = acid **minus one H⁺**. The charge goes **down by 1**.
- Conjugate acid = base **plus one H⁺**. The charge goes **up by 1**.

For example, the conjugate base of NH₄⁺ is NH₃, and the conjugate acid of OH⁻ is H₂O.

Every proton-transfer reaction has **two** conjugate pairs. A good habit is to label all four species, as in Figure 1. The reverse reaction is also a proton transfer: H₃O⁺ can give a proton back to NO₂⁻. That is why H₃O⁺ is called an acid and NO₂⁻ a base.

## Water: an acid or a base

Water plays a special role in aqueous solutions because its structure lets it do both jobs:

- The O atom in H₂O has **two lone pairs**. One can bond to an incoming H⁺, so water can **accept a proton** and become H₃O⁺. It acts as a base.
- The O–H bonds are **polar**, and an H can leave as H⁺ when a base is present to take it. Water then **donates a proton** and becomes OH⁻. It acts as an acid.

Which role water plays depends on its partner. With an acid such as HNO₂, water accepts the proton (Figure 1). With a base such as methylamine, water donates one:

CH₃NH₂(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇌ CH₃NH₃⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq)

Water molecules can even transfer a proton to each other: H₂O + H₂O ⇌ H₃O⁺ + OH⁻. This happens only to a tiny extent, but it means pure water always contains a few H₃O⁺ and OH⁻ ions. Unit 8 makes this quantitative.

A species that can act as either a proton donor or a proton acceptor is called **amphiprotic**. Water is the most important one. Ions such as HSO₃⁻ and HCO₃⁻ are also amphiprotic: they have an H to give away and a lone pair to accept one.

## Worked example 1: label the acid, base and conjugate pairs

**Question.** For each reaction, identify the Brønsted-Lowry acid, the base, the conjugate acid and the conjugate base. State the role water plays.

(a) HNO₂(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇌ H₃O⁺(aq) + NO₂⁻(aq)

(b) CH₃NH₂(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇌ CH₃NH₃⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq)

**(a)** Find the species that loses an H⁺: HNO₂ becomes NO₂⁻. So HNO₂ is the **acid** and NO₂⁻ is its **conjugate base**. H₂O gains the H⁺ and becomes H₃O⁺, so H₂O is the **base** and H₃O⁺ is its **conjugate acid**. Water acts as a base.

**(b)** H₂O loses an H⁺ and becomes OH⁻, so H₂O is the **acid** and OH⁻ is its **conjugate base**. CH₃NH₂ gains the H⁺ and becomes CH₃NH₃⁺, so CH₃NH₂ is the **base** and CH₃NH₃⁺ is its **conjugate acid**. Water acts as an acid.

**Check.** In each pair the formulas differ by one H and the charges differ by 1: HNO₂ (0) and NO₂⁻ (−1); CH₃NH₃⁺ (+1) and CH₃NH₂ (0). Water appears as a base in (a) and as an acid in (b).

## Worked example 2: an amphiprotic ion

**Question.** The hydrogen sulfite ion, HSO₃⁻, is amphiprotic.

(a) Write its conjugate base and its conjugate acid.
(b) Write an equation in which HSO₃⁻ acts as an acid with hydroxide ions, and one in which it acts as a base with hydronium ions.

**(a)** Conjugate base: remove one H⁺ and lower the charge by 1, giving **SO₃²⁻**. Conjugate acid: add one H⁺ and raise the charge by 1, giving **H₂SO₃**.

**(b)** As an acid (donating H⁺ to OH⁻):

HSO₃⁻(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → SO₃²⁻(aq) + H₂O(l)

As a base (accepting H⁺ from H₃O⁺):

HSO₃⁻(aq) + H₃O⁺(aq) → H₂SO₃(aq) + H₂O(l)

**Check.** Count charge on each side. First equation: −1 + (−1) = −2 on the left; −2 + 0 = −2 on the right. Second: −1 + 1 = 0; 0 + 0 = 0. Both balance. A common slip is to write the conjugate base as "SO₃⁻", removing the H but forgetting to change the charge.

## Strong and weak acids, and their conjugate bases

When an acid dissolves in water, it donates protons to water molecules. Acids differ in how completely this happens:

- A **strong acid** ionises essentially **completely**: almost every acid molecule gives its proton to water. Examples are HCl, HBr, HI, HNO₃, HClO₄ and H₂SO₄ (for its first proton).
- A **weak acid** ionises only **partly**: at any moment most of the acid molecules are still intact. Examples are HNO₂, HF and ethanoic acid.

Now think about the conjugate bases. The extent of ionisation is a competition for the proton. If an acid gives up its proton completely, its conjugate base must have almost no pull on a proton. So:

> **The stronger the acid, the weaker its conjugate base.**

Cl⁻, the conjugate base of the strong acid HCl, has a negligible tendency to accept a proton in water. NO₂⁻, the conjugate base of the weak acid HNO₂, accepts protons noticeably. That is why so much HNO₂ stays as molecules: the NO₂⁻ ions keep taking protons back.

The same idea works the other way round for bases: the stronger a base, the weaker its conjugate acid.

**(a)** Work out how many acid molecules there were at the start. Each ionised molecule gives one H₃O⁺ and one anion, so count either.

- HX: 5 X⁻ ions and 0 HX molecules, so 5 of 5 ionised: **100%**.
- HY: 1 Y⁻ ion and 4 HY molecules, so 1 of 5 ionised: **20%**.

**(b)** HX ionises completely, so **HX is the stronger acid** (it behaves as a strong acid). HY is a weak acid.

**(c)** In the HY solution, most protons stay attached to Y. So Y⁻ holds on to protons more strongly than X⁻ does. **Y⁻ is the stronger base.** This matches the rule: the weaker acid has the stronger conjugate base.

**(d)** The HX box has 5 H₃O⁺ ions and the HY box has 1, in the same volume. The H₃O⁺ concentration in the HX solution is **5 times** that in the HY solution, even though the two acids were added in equal amounts.

**Interpretation.** Equal amounts of acid do not give equal H₃O⁺ concentrations. Strength describes how fully an acid ionises, not how much acid you added.

## Common misconceptions

- **"A base must contain OH⁻."** In the Brønsted-Lowry model a base is any proton acceptor: NH₃, CH₃NH₂, CO₃²⁻ and H₂O can all be bases.
- **"Strong means concentrated."** Strength is the *fraction* that ionises. A dilute solution of a strong acid is still fully ionised; a concentrated weak acid is still mostly molecules.
- **"The conjugate base of a strong acid is a strong base."** The opposite is true. Cl⁻ is an extremely weak base because HCl gives up its proton so readily.
- **Changing the formula but not the charge.** The conjugate base of HSO₃⁻ is SO₃²⁻, not SO₃⁻.
- **Pairing species that differ by two protons.** H₂SO₃ and SO₃²⁻ are not a conjugate pair; each pair differs by exactly one H⁺.
- **"Water is just the solvent."** In many aqueous acid-base reactions water is a reactant, acting as the acid or the base.
- **"H⁺ floats around on its own in water."** Protons are always attached to water molecules. H₃O⁺(aq) and H⁺(aq) are two ways of writing the same thing.
