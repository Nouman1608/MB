## Recap

- Vaporizing overcomes interparticle forces **completely**, so vapor pressure and boiling point track force strength directly.
- Melting only **rearranges** particles. Melting points usually rise with force strength, but packing in the crystal also matters.
- **Ionic solids:** strong attractions between ions; low vapor pressure, high melting and boiling points; brittle (like charges meet when layers slide); conduct only when molten or dissolved.
- **Covalent network solids:** atoms of nonmetals or metalloids bonded covalently throughout (diamond, graphite, SiO₂, SiC). Very high melting points. 3D networks are hard and rigid because bond angles are fixed; graphite's layers slide, so it is soft.
- **Molecular solids:** separate molecules held by weak intermolecular forces. Low melting points; no conductivity, since electrons stay in bonds and lone pairs. Can be very large molecules or polymers.
- **Metallic solids:** cations in a sea of mobile electrons. Conduct heat and electricity; malleable and ductile. Interstitial alloys are more rigid but still conduct.
- In large biomolecules and polymers, noncovalent interactions set the shape, and the shape sets the function.

## Key relationships

| Observation | Explanation to give |
|---|---|
| High vapor pressure, low boiling point | weak forces between particles; many escape at a given temperature |
| Conducts as a solid | mobile electrons (metal; graphite is the network exception) |
| Conducts only when molten or dissolved | ions that are fixed in the solid become free to move |
| Never conducts, very high melting point | covalent network |
| Never conducts, low melting point | molecular solid |
| Shatters when struck | ionic: like charges forced together |
| Bends or flattens | metallic: cations rearrange in the electron sea |

## Assumptions behind the comparisons

- Boiling points are compared at the **same pressure**; vapor pressures at the **same temperature**.
- The solid is **pure**; impurities change melting points.
- The classification uses several properties together, not one alone.

## Mistakes to avoid

1. Saying covalent bonds break when a molecular solid melts or boils.
2. Classifying a solid as ionic just because it melts at a high temperature.
3. Saying ionic solids conduct as solids.
4. Explaining diamond's hardness with intermolecular forces.
5. Using melting points alone to rank intermolecular forces.
6. Naming the property and the force but never describing the particles.

## Quick self-check

1. A solid melts at 2700 °C and never conducts. What kind of solid is it? *(Covalent network)*
2. Why does solid CO₂ vaporize far more easily than SiO₂ melts? *(CO₂: weak forces between molecules; SiO₂: covalent bonds must break)*
3. Why is graphite soft? *(Layers held by weak dispersion forces slide past each other)*
4. Which has the higher vapor pressure at 20 °C, ethanol or diethyl ether? *(Diethyl ether: no hydrogen bonding)*
