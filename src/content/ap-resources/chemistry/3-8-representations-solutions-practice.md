These are **original Marlbridge practice questions**, not past exam questions. The mark points are a suggested Marlbridge rubric to help you check your work; they are not an official scoring guideline. The diagrams are described in words; where a question asks you to draw, a description of what you would draw with the same features earns the same credit. Water is polar: O is δ−, each H is δ+. Assume ionic solutes dissolve completely.

## Question 1 (multiple choice · foundation)

Which description matches a correct particle diagram of magnesium chloride solution, MgCl₂(aq)?

- (A) Several MgCl₂ units, each a Mg atom joined to two Cl atoms, spread among water molecules
- (B) Separate Mg²⁺ and Cl⁻ ions, twice as many Cl⁻ as Mg²⁺, each ion surrounded by water molecules
- (C) Separate Mg²⁺ and Cl⁻ ions in equal numbers, each surrounded by water molecules
- (D) Mg atoms and Cl₂ molecules spread among water molecules

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (B).** MgCl₂ is ionic, so its ions separate when it dissolves. The formula gives 2 Cl⁻ for every Mg²⁺, which also balances the charges: +2 and 2 × (−1).

- (A) shows the compound as intact units. Dissolved ionic compounds do not stay as pairs or groups.
- (C) has the wrong ratio, so the charges in the box would not balance.
- (D) shows neutral metal atoms and chlorine molecules: dissolving does not turn ions back into elements.
</details>

## Question 2 (multiple choice · foundation)

In a solution of potassium bromide, how are the water molecules nearest to a K⁺ ion arranged?

- (A) Each points a hydrogen atom towards the K⁺ ion.
- (B) Each points its oxygen atom towards the K⁺ ion.
- (C) They point in random directions, because K⁺ does not attract water.
- (D) They are joined to the K⁺ ion by covalent bonds.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (B).** K⁺ is positive and attracts the partially negative oxygen end of each water molecule (an ion–dipole attraction).

- (A) is the arrangement around an anion such as Br⁻. Next to K⁺, the δ+ hydrogens would be repelled.
- (C) ignores the ion–dipole attraction. K⁺ does attract water; this attraction is what holds water molecules in an ordered shell around the ion.
- (D) The attraction is an intermolecular (ion–dipole) force, not a shared electron pair.
</details>

## Question 3 (multiple choice · core)

Diagram P represents 0.10 L of a solution and shows 8 solute particles. Diagram Q represents 0.20 L of a solution of the same solute and shows 12 solute particles. Which statement is correct?

- (A) Q is more concentrated, because it contains more solute particles.
- (B) P is more concentrated, because it has more solute particles per unit volume.
- (C) P and Q have the same concentration, because both contain the same solute.
- (D) The concentrations cannot be compared without the molar mass of the solute.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (B).** P has 8 ÷ 0.10 L = 80 particles per litre; Q has 12 ÷ 0.20 L = 60 particles per litre. P is more concentrated.

- (A) compares particle counts without dividing by volume.
- (C) The identity of the solute does not fix its concentration.
- (D) Concentration in a diagram is particles per volume; molar mass is not needed to compare.
</details>

## Question 4 (calculation · core)

A particle diagram represents 0.200 L of calcium nitrate solution, Ca(NO₃)₂(aq). It shows 4 Ca²⁺ ions and 8 NO₃⁻ ions. Each ion drawn represents 0.00500 mol of that ion.

(a) Show that the diagram is consistent with the formula.
(b) Calculate [Ca²⁺] and [NO₃⁻].
(c) Water is added until the solution represented has a volume of 0.400 L. State how many of each ion the new diagram should show, and give the new concentrations.

<details>
<summary>Worked solution</summary>

**(a)** Ca(NO₃)₂ gives 1 Ca²⁺ : 2 NO₃⁻. The diagram has 4 : 8 = 1 : 2. Charge: 4(+2) + 8(−1) = 0. Consistent.

**(b)** n(Ca²⁺) = 4 × 0.00500 = 0.0200 mol, so [Ca²⁺] = 0.0200 ÷ 0.200 = **0.100 M**.
n(NO₃⁻) = 8 × 0.00500 = 0.0400 mol, so [NO₃⁻] = 0.0400 ÷ 0.200 = **0.200 M**.

**(c)** Dilution does not add or remove ions: the diagram should still show **4 Ca²⁺ and 8 NO₃⁻**, now spread through a box representing 0.400 L. [Ca²⁺] = 0.0200 ÷ 0.400 = **0.0500 M**; [NO₃⁻] = 0.0400 ÷ 0.400 = **0.100 M**.

Suggested mark points (4): 1 for the ratio or charge argument in (a); 1 for both concentrations in (b); 1 for the unchanged particle counts in (c); 1 for both halved concentrations. Accept "each concentration halves because the volume doubles" for the last point if the values are given.

Common error: drawing 2 Ca²⁺ and 4 NO₃⁻ after dilution. That would represent removing solute, not adding water.
</details>

## Question 5 (constructed response · core)

Methanol, CH₃OH, mixes with water in all proportions.

(a) Describe a particle diagram of a dilute solution of methanol in water, showing the interactions between methanol and water.
(b) A student draws methanol in water as CH₃⁺ and OH⁻ ions. Explain why this is wrong.
(c) Explain how your diagram for (a) would differ from one for sodium chloride solution.

<details>
<summary>Model answer and suggested Marlbridge rubric</summary>

**(a)** A few whole CH₃OH molecules spread evenly among many water molecules. Dashed lines show hydrogen bonds: from the H on methanol's O–H to the O of a nearby water molecule, and from an H of a water molecule to methanol's O atom. The CH₃ group does not form hydrogen bonds.

**(b)** Methanol is a molecular compound held together by covalent bonds. It dissolves as intact molecules; its C–O bond does not break, so no CH₃⁺ or OH⁻ ions form.

**(c)** For NaCl(aq) the solute is drawn as separate Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions in a 1 : 1 ratio. The interactions are ion–dipole attractions, so water's O faces each Na⁺ and an H faces each Cl⁻. In methanol solution there are no ions; the interactions are hydrogen bonds between neutral molecules.

| Point | What earns it |
|---|---|
| 1 | Methanol shown as whole molecules spread among water molecules |
| 1 | Hydrogen bond correctly placed between the O–H of one molecule and an O atom of the other |
| 1 | (b) Methanol is molecular / covalently bonded, so it does not form ions on dissolving |
| 1 | (c) NaCl drawn as separate ions with ion–dipole orientation, contrasted with hydrogen bonding between molecules |

Do not award the second point for a hydrogen bond drawn to the H atoms of the CH₃ group.
</details>

## Question 6 (constructed response · core)

A student is asked to draw a particle diagram of sodium sulfate solution, Na₂SO₄(aq). The student's diagram shows:

- 3 Na⁺ ions;
- 3 separate S atoms and 12 separate O atoms;
- water molecules around each Na⁺ with their H atoms pointing towards the Na⁺.

(a) Identify **three** errors in the diagram.
(b) Describe a corrected diagram that uses 3 sulfate units.

<details>
<summary>Model answer and suggested Marlbridge rubric</summary>

**(a)**
1. **Sulfate broken apart.** SO₄²⁻ is a polyatomic ion held together by covalent bonds; it stays as one unit with a 2− charge.
2. **Wrong ratio / charges do not balance.** Na₂SO₄ needs 2 Na⁺ per SO₄²⁻. With 3 sulfate units there must be 6 Na⁺. As drawn, 3 Na⁺ against 3 sulfate units would leave a total charge of 3 − 6 = −3.
3. **Water reversed around Na⁺.** The δ− oxygen of each water molecule should face the positive Na⁺ ion.

**(b)** 6 Na⁺ and 3 SO₄²⁻ ions, all separate. Each SO₄²⁻ is drawn as one unit (an S joined to four O, with an overall 2− label). Water around each Na⁺ has O facing inwards; water around each SO₄²⁻ points an H towards the ion. Charge: 6(+1) + 3(−2) = 0.

| Point | What earns it |
|---|---|
| 1 | Sulfate must stay as a whole SO₄²⁻ ion |
| 1 | Ratio must be 2 Na⁺ : 1 SO₄²⁻ (or charge does not balance) |
| 1 | Water's O, not H, should face Na⁺ |
| 1 | Corrected diagram: 6 Na⁺ and 3 SO₄²⁻ with correct orientation around both ions |
</details>

## Question 7 (calculation and explanation · stretch)

Diagram A represents 0.10 L of calcium chloride solution and shows 3 Ca²⁺ and 6 Cl⁻ ions. Diagram B represents 0.10 L of sodium chloride solution and shows 4 Na⁺ and 4 Cl⁻ ions. Each ion drawn represents 0.010 mol. The two solutions are mixed, and no reaction occurs.

(a) State the number of each ion in a diagram of the whole mixture, and the volume it represents.
(b) Calculate [Ca²⁺], [Na⁺] and [Cl⁻] in the mixture.
(c) Compare [Cl⁻] in the mixture with [Cl⁻] in A and in B, and explain the result.

<details>
<summary>Worked solution and suggested Marlbridge rubric</summary>

**(a)** 3 Ca²⁺, 4 Na⁺ and 6 + 4 = 10 Cl⁻ ions, in a box representing 0.10 + 0.10 = **0.20 L**.

**(b)**
- [Ca²⁺] = (3 × 0.010) ÷ 0.20 = **0.15 M**
- [Na⁺] = (4 × 0.010) ÷ 0.20 = **0.20 M**
- [Cl⁻] = (10 × 0.010) ÷ 0.20 = **0.50 M**

Charge check: 2(0.15) + 0.20 = 0.50 M positive; 0.50 M negative.

**(c)** Before mixing, [Cl⁻] was 0.60 M in A (6 × 0.010 ÷ 0.10) and 0.40 M in B (4 × 0.010 ÷ 0.10). The mixture's 0.50 M lies between them. Because equal volumes were mixed, the result is the simple average here; the chloride ions from both solutions are now spread through twice the volume. Each of Ca²⁺ and Na⁺, which came from only one solution, has its concentration halved.

| Point | What earns it |
|---|---|
| 1 | Correct particle counts and 0.20 L |
| 1 | All three concentrations correct |
| 1 | Correct [Cl⁻] for A and B before mixing |
| 1 | Explains that Cl⁻ from both solutions is spread through the combined volume (and why the value lies between) |

Common error: keeping the volume at 0.10 L gives [Cl⁻] = 1.0 M, which is higher than either starting solution and so cannot be right.
</details>
