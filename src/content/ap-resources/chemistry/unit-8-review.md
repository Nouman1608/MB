## How to use this review

Use this page after the [Unit 8 diagnostic](/advanced-course-resources/chemistry/unit-8-diagnostic/), once you have revisited the topics it flagged. Answer all seven on paper before opening any answer. Each combines topics.

These are **original Marlbridge practice questions**, not past exam questions. Substances named with letters are fictional, with invented data. Each rubric is a **suggested Marlbridge rubric**, not official scoring, and your total gives no predicted score. Data are at 25 °C (K_w = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴) unless a question says otherwise.

## Big ideas of the unit

- **Water sets the scale.** [H₃O⁺][OH⁻] = K_w in every aqueous solution; neutral means equal, which is pH 7 only at 25 °C ([Topic 8.1](/advanced-course-resources/chemistry/8-1-introduction-acids-bases-study-guide/)).
- **Strong means complete.** A strong acid or base gives its full amount of H₃O⁺ or OH⁻ (two OH⁻ for a group 2 hydroxide) ([Topic 8.2](/advanced-course-resources/chemistry/8-2-ph-poh-strong-acids-bases-study-guide/)).
- **Weak means mostly not ionized.** K_a or K_b connects the starting concentration to pH, and K_a × K_b = K_w links a conjugate pair ([Topic 8.3](/advanced-course-resources/chemistry/8-3-weak-acid-base-equilibria-study-guide/)).
- **React first, then think about equilibrium.** A strong acid or base reacts completely; what is left decides the method ([Topic 8.4](/advanced-course-resources/chemistry/8-4-acid-base-reactions-buffers-study-guide/)).
- **A titration curve is all of this on one graph.** Equivalence gives moles, half-equivalence gives pK_a, and the species left at equivalence set its pH ([Topic 8.5](/advanced-course-resources/chemistry/8-5-acid-base-titrations-study-guide/)).
- **Structure explains strength.** Anything that stabilizes the conjugate base (electronegativity, inductive effects, resonance) makes a stronger acid ([Topic 8.6](/advanced-course-resources/chemistry/8-6-molecular-structure-acids-bases-study-guide/)).
- **pH against pK_a decides the form.** Below pK_a the protonated form wins; above it, the deprotonated form. This also chooses indicators ([Topic 8.7](/advanced-course-resources/chemistry/8-7-ph-pka-study-guide/)).
- **Buffers work by reaction.** The base member removes added acid, the acid member removes added base ([Topic 8.8](/advanced-course-resources/chemistry/8-8-properties-buffers-study-guide/)); the ratio sets the pH ([Topic 8.9](/advanced-course-resources/chemistry/8-9-henderson-hasselbalch-equation-study-guide/)); the amounts set the capacity ([Topic 8.10](/advanced-course-resources/chemistry/8-10-buffer-capacity-study-guide/)).
- **pH can move a solubility equilibrium.** If one ion of a salt is a weak base, a weak acid or OH⁻, changing the pH removes it and more solid dissolves ([Topic 8.11](/advanced-course-resources/chemistry/8-11-ph-solubility-study-guide/)).

## Key relationships and methods

| Idea | Relationship or method | Topic |
|---|---|---|
| Water | K_w = [H₃O⁺][OH⁻]; pH + pOH = pK_w; neutral pH = pK_w ÷ 2 | 8.1 |
| Strong acid or base | [H₃O⁺] = c; [OH⁻] = c or 2c (group 2) | 8.2 |
| Weak acid or base | x ≈ √(K × c), check x ÷ c < 5%; K_a × K_b = K_w | 8.3 |
| Mixing | moles first; excess strong reagent → excess ÷ total volume; partial → buffer; equal → conjugate sets pH | 8.4 |
| Titration | n(analyte) = n(titrant) at equivalence; pH = pK_a at half-equivalence; one steep rise per acidic proton | 8.5 |
| Structure | more stable conjugate base → stronger acid | 8.6 |
| Predominant form | [A⁻]/[HA] = 10^(pH − pK_a); indicator pK_a near equivalence pH | 8.7 |
| Buffer pH | pH = pK_a + log([A⁻]/[HA]), with moles or concentrations | 8.8, 8.9 |
| Capacity | acid capacity = moles of A⁻; base capacity = moles of HA | 8.10 |
| Solubility | removing a basic anion (or OH⁻) lowers Q below K_sp, so more dissolves | 8.11 |

## Question 1 (multiple choice · mixed)

25.00 mL of 0.0600 M of the weak base W (pK_b = 4.90) is titrated with 0.0600 M HCl. Which indicator is the best choice for the end point?

- (A) Indicator P, pK_a = 3.6
- (B) Indicator Q, pK_a = 5.3
- (C) Indicator R, pK_a = 7.0
- (D) Indicator S, pK_a = 9.1

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (B).** Equivalence is at 25.00 mL: 1.500 mmol HW⁺ in 50.00 mL, 0.0300 M (Topic 8.5). K_a(HW⁺) = 10^−(14.00 − 4.90) = 7.9 × 10⁻¹⁰ (Topic 8.3), so [H₃O⁺] = √(7.9 × 10⁻¹⁰ × 0.0300) = 4.9 × 10⁻⁶ M and pH = 5.31. Pick the closest indicator pK_a (Topic 8.7).

- (A) changes colour only in excess acid.
- (C) assumes "neutralized" means neutral; HW⁺ is a weak acid.
- (D) 9.10 is the pK_a of HW⁺: the pH at the half-equivalence point, not the end point.
</details>

## Question 2 (multiple choice · mixed)

0.0300 mol of NaOH is added to 0.0500 mol of the weak acid HR (pK_a = 4.50) in 0.500 L of water. Which describes the final solution?

- (A) pH 4.68; it resists added acid better than added base.
- (B) pH 4.32; it resists added acid better than added base.
- (C) pH 4.68; it resists added base better than added acid.
- (D) pH 12.78; it is not a buffer.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (A).** React first (Topic 8.4): OH⁻ is limiting, leaving 0.0200 mol HR and 0.0300 mol R⁻, a buffer. pH = 4.50 + log(0.0300 ÷ 0.0200) = 4.68 (Topic 8.9). There is more R⁻, which removes added acid, so the capacity for acid (0.0300 mol) is larger than for base (0.0200 mol) (Topic 8.10).

- (B) inverts the ratio.
- (C) gets the capacity backwards: HR is the smaller amount.
- (D) treats the OH⁻ as unreacted.
</details>

## Question 3 (multiple choice · mixed)

Hypochlorous acid, HOCl, and hypobromous acid, HOBr, are both weak acids. Which statement compares 0.10 M solutions of each and their conjugate bases?

- (A) The HOBr solution has the higher pH, and OBr⁻ is the stronger base.
- (B) The HOCl solution has the higher pH, and OCl⁻ is the stronger base.
- (C) The HOBr solution has the higher pH, and OCl⁻ is the stronger base.
- (D) Both have the same pH, because both acids are weak and equally concentrated.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (A).** Chlorine is more electronegative than bromine, so it stabilizes OCl⁻ more: HOCl is the stronger acid (Topic 8.6). The weaker acid, HOBr, gives less H₃O⁺ at equal concentration, so a higher pH (Topic 8.3). Since K_a × K_b = K_w, its conjugate base, OBr⁻, is the stronger base.

- (B) reverses the argument.
- (C) mixes the two: a weaker acid always has the stronger conjugate base.
- (D) "Weak" covers a huge range of K_a values.
</details>

## Question 4 (constructed response · mixed)

A student titrates 25.00 mL of the weak acid HN with 0.150 M NaOH. The starting pH is 2.85 and the equivalence point is at 15.00 mL.

(a) Calculate the concentration of HN.
(b) Use the starting pH to calculate K_a and pK_a of HN.
(c) Predict the pH after 7.50 mL and after 10.00 mL of NaOH. Explain why the mixture at these volumes is a buffer.
(d) Calculate the pH at the equivalence point.
(e) Her partner says: "At the equivalence point we have added exactly enough base to neutralize the acid, so the pH should be 7." Evaluate the claim.

<details>
<summary>Worked solution and suggested Marlbridge rubric</summary>

**(a)** n(OH⁻) at equivalence = 15.00 mL × 0.150 M = 2.250 mmol = n(HN). c = 2.250 ÷ 25.00 = **0.0900 M**.

**(b)** [H₃O⁺] = [N⁻] = 10⁻²·⁸⁵ = 1.413 × 10⁻³ M; [HN] = 0.0900 − 0.0014 = 0.0886 M.
K_a = (1.413 × 10⁻³)² ÷ 0.0886 = **2.25 × 10⁻⁵**; **pK_a = 4.65**.

**(c)** NaOH reacts completely (HN + OH⁻ → N⁻ + H₂O), leaving large amounts of both HN and N⁻: a buffer.

- 7.50 mL is half the equivalence volume: 1.125 mmol each of HN and N⁻, so **pH = pK_a = 4.65**.
- 10.00 mL: 1.500 mmol OH⁻ leaves 0.750 mmol HN and 1.500 mmol N⁻. pH = 4.65 + log(1.500 ÷ 0.750) = **4.95**.

**(d)** All 2.250 mmol is now N⁻ in 40.00 mL: [N⁻] = 0.05625 M. K_b = K_w ÷ K_a = 4.4 × 10⁻¹⁰.
[OH⁻] = √(4.4 × 10⁻¹⁰ × 0.05625) = 5.0 × 10⁻⁶ M; pOH = 5.30; **pH = 8.70**.

**(e)** **Wrong.** Equal moles have reacted, but N⁻, the conjugate base of a weak acid, takes protons from water (N⁻ + H₂O ⇌ HN + OH⁻), so the pH is 8.70. pH 7 at equivalence holds only for strong acid with strong base.

| Point | What earns it |
|---|---|
| 1 | (a) 0.0900 M from moles at equivalence |
| 1 | (b) K_a ≈ 2.2 × 10⁻⁵ to 2.3 × 10⁻⁵ and pK_a ≈ 4.65 |
| 1 | (c) Buffer: both HN and N⁻ present after complete reaction with OH⁻; pH 4.65 at 7.50 mL |
| 1 | (c) pH 4.95 at 10.00 mL from the mole ratio |
| 1 | (d) [N⁻] = 0.05625 M (total volume 40.00 mL) and K_b from K_w ÷ K_a |
| 1 | (d) pH 8.70 |
| 1 | (e) Rejects the claim: N⁻ is a weak base that makes OH⁻ |

**Total: 7 points.** Accept carried-forward values from (b).
</details>

## Question 5 (constructed response · mixed)

A technician makes a buffer from 0.250 L of 0.200 M of the weak base Z (pK_b = 5.40) by adding 1.00 M HCl.

(a) Calculate the volume of HCl needed to give pH 8.90.
(b) Write net ionic equations for the reactions that protect the buffer when a small amount of strong acid, and then of strong base, is added.
(c) Calculate the largest amount of HCl and of NaOH the buffer can neutralize. In which direction is it stronger?
(d) A trainee adds 50.0 mL of the 1.00 M HCl instead. Calculate the pH and explain why the result is not a buffer.

<details>
<summary>Worked solution and suggested Marlbridge rubric</summary>

**(a)** HZ⁺ is the acid member: pK_a = 14.00 − 5.40 = 8.60.
[Z]/[HZ⁺] = 10^(8.90 − 8.60) = 10⁰·³⁰ = 2.00.
Total Z = 0.250 L × 0.200 M = 0.0500 mol. Each mole of HCl turns one Z into HZ⁺, so n(HZ⁺) = 0.0500 ÷ 3.00 = 0.0167 mol (leaving 0.0333 mol Z): **16.7 mL** of 1.00 M HCl.

**(b)** Added acid: **Z(aq) + H₃O⁺(aq) → HZ⁺(aq) + H₂O(l)**
Added base: **HZ⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → Z(aq) + H₂O(l)**

**(c)** Acid capacity = moles of Z = **0.0333 mol HCl**. Base capacity = moles of HZ⁺ = **0.0167 mol NaOH**. It is **stronger against added acid**, because it holds twice as much Z as HZ⁺ (pH above pK_a).

**(d)** 50.0 mL × 1.00 M = 0.0500 mol HCl, exactly equal to Z. Only HZ⁺ is left: 0.0500 mol in 0.300 L = 0.167 M.
K_a = 10⁻⁸·⁶⁰ = 2.5 × 10⁻⁹; [H₃O⁺] = √(2.5 × 10⁻⁹ × 0.167) = 2.0 × 10⁻⁵ M; **pH = 4.69**.
Not a buffer: the base member Z has been used up, so nothing is left to remove added acid.

| Point | What earns it |
|---|---|
| 1 | (a) pK_a of HZ⁺ = 8.60 and ratio [Z]/[HZ⁺] = 2.0 |
| 1 | (a) 16.7 mL of HCl |
| 1 | (b) Both net ionic equations correct |
| 1 | (c) 0.0333 mol HCl and 0.0167 mol NaOH |
| 1 | (c) Stronger against acid, because more Z than HZ⁺ |
| 1 | (d) pH 4.69 from K_a of HZ⁺ at 0.167 M |
| 1 | (d) Not a buffer: only one member of the pair remains |

**Total: 7 points.**
</details>

## Question 6 (constructed response · mixed)

Three solutions are each 0.0500 M at 25 °C: perchloric acid, HClO₄; a carboxylic acid, R–COOH (K_a = 1.5 × 10⁻⁵); and its sodium salt, R–COONa.

(a) Calculate the pH of each solution.
(b) Rank the three solutions by [OH⁻], highest first.
(c) ClO₄⁻ and R–COO⁻ are both conjugate bases of acids. Explain, using their structures, why R–COO⁻ is a weak base but ClO₄⁻ has almost no tendency to accept a proton.
(d) The HClO₄ solution is heated to a temperature where K_w = 3.8 × 10⁻¹⁴. Calculate [OH⁻] and the pH of neutral water at this temperature. Is the HClO₄ solution still acidic?

<details>
<summary>Worked solution and suggested Marlbridge rubric</summary>

**(a)**

- HClO₄ is a strong acid: [H₃O⁺] = 0.0500 M, **pH = 1.30**.
- R–COOH: x = √(1.5 × 10⁻⁵ × 0.0500) = 8.7 × 10⁻⁴ M (1.7% ionized, under 5%), **pH = 3.06**.
- R–COO⁻: K_b = K_w ÷ K_a = 6.7 × 10⁻¹⁰. [OH⁻] = √(6.7 × 10⁻¹⁰ × 0.0500) = 5.8 × 10⁻⁶ M; pOH = 5.24; **pH = 8.76**.

**(b)** **R–COONa > R–COOH > HClO₄**: [OH⁻] = 5.8 × 10⁻⁶, 1.2 × 10⁻¹¹ and 2.0 × 10⁻¹³ M, from [OH⁻] = K_w ÷ [H₃O⁺].

**(c)** In R–COO⁻, resonance shares the negative charge over **two** oxygens. That stabilizes the ion, but not enough to stop it accepting protons weakly. In ClO₄⁻ the charge is spread over **four** oxygens, and the electronegative oxygens on the chlorine pull electron density away (inductive effect). The ion is so stable that it barely accepts a proton, which is why HClO₄ ionizes completely.

**(d)** [OH⁻] = 3.8 × 10⁻¹⁴ ÷ 0.0500 = **7.6 × 10⁻¹³ M**. pK_w = 13.42, so neutral pH = **6.71**. [H₃O⁺] is still 0.0500 M (pH 1.30), far larger than [OH⁻], so the solution is **still acidic**.

| Point | What earns it |
|---|---|
| 1 | (a) pH 1.30 and 3.06 |
| 1 | (a) pH 8.76, using K_b = K_w ÷ K_a |
| 1 | (b) Correct order, linked to pH or K_w |
| 1 | (c) R–COO⁻: charge spread over two oxygens, still able to accept a proton |
| 1 | (c) ClO₄⁻: charge spread over four oxygens plus inductive pull, so very stable and a negligible base |
| 1 | (d) [OH⁻] = 7.6 × 10⁻¹³ M and neutral pH 6.71 |
| 1 | (d) Still acidic, because [H₃O⁺] > [OH⁻] |

**Total: 7 points.**
</details>

## Question 7 (constructed response · mixed)

20.00 mL of 0.0600 M of the acid H₂Q is titrated with 0.100 M NaOH. The curve shows two steep rises, at 12.00 mL and 24.00 mL.

| Volume of NaOH (mL) | 0.00 | 6.00 | 12.00 | 18.00 | 24.00 | 28.00 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| pH | 2.14 | 3.03 | 5.46 | 7.90 | 10.17 | 11.92 |

(a) Use the volumes to show that H₂Q gives up two protons.
(b) Estimate pK_a1 and pK_a2. Justify your choice of readings.
(c) Identify the major Q-containing species at 12.00 mL and at 24.00 mL, and explain why the pH at 24.00 mL is above 7.
(d) The calcium salt CaQ is slightly soluble. Predict how its solubility in a buffer at pH 5.5 compares with that in a buffer at pH 10.0, and whether it changes much between pH 11 and pH 12. Explain.

<details>
<summary>Worked solution and suggested Marlbridge rubric</summary>

**(a)** n(H₂Q) = 20.00 mL × 0.0600 M = 1.200 mmol. First rise: 12.00 mL × 0.100 M = 1.200 mmol OH⁻ (one per H₂Q). Second rise: 2.400 mmol in total, **two OH⁻ per H₂Q**, so two acidic protons.

**(b)** Use the half-equivalence points. 6.00 mL is halfway to the first: [H₂Q] = [HQ⁻], so **pK_a1 ≈ 3.0**. 18.00 mL is halfway between the two: [HQ⁻] = [Q²⁻], so **pK_a2 ≈ 7.9**.

**(c)** At 12.00 mL each H₂Q has lost one proton: **HQ⁻** dominates. At 24.00 mL **Q²⁻** dominates. Q²⁻, the conjugate base of the weak acid HQ⁻, takes protons from water (Q²⁻ + H₂O ⇌ HQ⁻ + OH⁻): pH above 7.

**(d)** CaQ(s) ⇌ Ca²⁺(aq) + Q²⁻(aq).

- **pH 5.5:** below pK_a2, so H₃O⁺ converts Q²⁻ to HQ⁻. [Q²⁻] falls, Q < K_sp and more solid dissolves: **more soluble than at pH 10.0**, where Q²⁻ predominates.
- **pH 11 and 12:** both well above pK_a2, so Q²⁻ is hardly protonated at either: **little change**.

| Point | What earns it |
|---|---|
| 1 | (a) 1.200 mmol acid and 2.400 mmol OH⁻ at the second rise: two protons |
| 1 | (b) pK_a1 ≈ 3.0 and pK_a2 ≈ 7.9 from the half-equivalence volumes |
| 1 | (c) HQ⁻ at 12.00 mL and Q²⁻ at 24.00 mL |
| 1 | (c) Q²⁻ is a weak base that makes OH⁻ |
| 1 | (d) More soluble at pH 5.5: Q²⁻ is protonated, Q < K_sp, shift to dissolve |
| 1 | (d) Little change from pH 11 to 12, because both are well above pK_a2 |

**Total: 6 points.** Species concentrations on a diprotic curve are not calculated; qualitative reasoning is enough.
</details>
