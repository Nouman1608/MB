## Recap

- A **weak acid** HA reacts with water to make H₃O⁺, but only a small percentage of molecules ionize. [H₃O⁺] is much less than the starting concentration c.
- In a weak acid solution: [HA] ≫ [H₃O⁺] ≈ [A⁻] ≫ [OH⁻].
- A **weak base** B reacts with water to make OH⁻ and HB⁺, again only slightly. [OH⁻] is much less than c.
- K_a and K_b are equilibrium constants. They change only with temperature, not with dilution.
- **Percent ionization rises on dilution**, even though [H₃O⁺] (or [OH⁻]) falls.

## Key relationships

| Relationship | Use it to |
|---|---|
| K_a = [H₃O⁺][A⁻] / [HA]; pK_a = −log K_a | describe a weak acid; smaller K_a (larger pK_a) = weaker acid |
| K_b = [OH⁻][HB⁺] / [B]; pK_b = −log K_b | describe a weak base |
| x ≈ √(K × c) | find [H₃O⁺] (acid) or [OH⁻] (base) when x < 5% of c |
| % ionization = (x ÷ c) × 100% | from K and c, or from c and a measured pH |
| K_a = x² / (c − x) with x = 10^(−pH) | find K_a from a measured pH |
| K_a × K_b = K_w; pK_a + pK_b = 14.00 (25 °C) | switch between a conjugate acid and base |

## Assumptions

- The temperature is 25 °C, so K_w = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴.
- The acid is **monoprotic**: one H₃O⁺ per molecule.
- Water's own H₃O⁺ (or OH⁻) is negligible next to what the acid (or base) makes.
- c − x ≈ c only when x is under about 5% of c. Otherwise solve the quadratic.

## Mistakes to avoid

1. **Treating a weak acid as strong.** pH ≠ −log c.
2. **Forgetting to convert pOH.** A weak base calculation gives [OH⁻] first.
3. **Mixing up pK_a direction.** Larger pK_a = weaker acid.
4. **Skipping the 5% check.**
5. **Using K_a in a K_b problem.** Convert with K_b = K_w ÷ K_a.
6. **Thinking dilution lowers percent ionization.** It raises it.

## Quick self-check

1. Find the pH of 0.20 M of an acid with K_a = 5.0 × 10⁻⁶. *(x = 1.0 × 10⁻³ M; pH 3.00; 0.50% ionized)*
2. An acid has pK_a 4.00. What are pK_b and K_b of its conjugate base? *(pK_b 10.00; K_b = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁰)*
3. Find the pH of 0.10 M of a base with K_b = 1.0 × 10⁻⁵. *([OH⁻] = 1.0 × 10⁻³ M; pOH 3.00; pH 11.00)*
