## What a particle diagram of a solution must show

In Topic 3.7 you described solutions with numbers: 0.25 M CaCl₂, for example. A **particle diagram** describes the same solution as a picture of a tiny volume of it. Exam questions ask you both to draw these diagrams and to read information from them. A good diagram communicates two things.

1. **Concentrations.** The *relative numbers* of each kind of particle must be right. In CaCl₂(aq) there must be twice as many Cl⁻ ions as Ca²⁺ ions. If you compare two diagrams, the number of particles per unit volume shows which solution is more concentrated.
2. **Interactions.** The way particles are arranged must reflect the forces between them, which you met in Topic 3.1. Solvent molecules near an ion point their oppositely charged end towards it. A molecular solute that can hydrogen bond is drawn with hydrogen bonds to the solvent.

Use a key, labels or distinct shapes for each particle type. Colour alone is not enough, because diagrams are often printed in black and white.

## Drawing ionic solutes

When an ionic compound dissolves in water, its ions separate and spread out among the water molecules. Follow four rules:

- **Separate the ions.** NaCl(aq) is drawn as free Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions, not as Na–Cl pairs and not as a lump of solid.
- **Use the formula ratio.** For Na₂CO₃(aq) draw two Na⁺ for every CO₃²⁻. A diagram with 6 Na⁺ and 3 CO₃²⁻ is correct; 4 Na⁺ and 4 CO₃²⁻ is not.
- **Keep polyatomic ions whole.** SO₄²⁻, NO₃⁻, CO₃²⁻ and NH₄⁺ are held together by covalent bonds, which do not break on dissolving. Draw each one as a single unit with its charge.
- **Check the charges.** Total positive charge must equal total negative charge in the box. For 2 Ca²⁺ and 4 Cl⁻: +4 and −4.

### How water surrounds ions

A water molecule is polar. The oxygen carries a partial negative charge (δ−) and each hydrogen a partial positive charge (δ+). Each dissolved ion is surrounded by several water molecules, held by **ion–dipole** attractions:

- Around a **cation** (such as Na⁺), the water molecules point their **oxygen** atoms inwards.
- Around an **anion** (such as Cl⁻), they point a **hydrogen** atom inwards.

The reason is simple electrostatics: opposite charges attract. The positive Na⁺ attracts the δ− end of each water molecule; the negative Cl⁻ attracts a δ+ end. If a diagram shows hydrogen atoms pointing at Na⁺, it shows a repulsion, not an attraction, and it is wrong.

## Drawing molecular solutes

A molecular solute does not break into ions when it dissolves. Glucose, sucrose, ethanol and methanol stay as **whole molecules** among the water molecules. What you show instead is how they interact with water:

- **Methanol in water (CH₃OH).** Methanol has an O–H group. Draw hydrogen bonds (dashed lines) both ways: from the H of methanol's O–H to the O of a water molecule, and from the H of a water molecule to methanol's O. The CH₃ end does not hydrogen bond.
- **Iodine in hexane (I₂ in C₆H₁₄).** Both are nonpolar, so the only attractions are London dispersion forces. There is no particular orientation to show; the I₂ molecules are simply spread evenly among the hexane molecules.

The same counting rules apply. If a diagram represents 0.20 M glucose and 0.10 M methanol in the same volume, it should show twice as many glucose molecules as methanol molecules.

## Reading concentration from a diagram

A box in a diagram stands for a definite volume of solution. The concentration it shows is:

> **concentration ∝ number of solute particles ÷ volume represented**

Two consequences follow.

- If two boxes represent **equal volumes**, the box with more solute particles is more concentrated.
- If the volumes are **different**, you must divide by the volume before you compare. A bigger box with more particles can still be the more dilute solution.

**Dilution** is easy to draw: keep the same number of solute particles and make the box represent a larger volume. Doubling the volume halves the concentration.

## A four-point check for any solution diagram

Before you hand in a drawing, or when you judge someone else's, run through these questions in order:

1. **Are the solute particles the right kind?** Ions for ionic compounds (with polyatomic ions whole), whole molecules for molecular compounds.
2. **Is the ratio right?** Count each kind of particle and compare with the formula. Then add up the charges: they must total zero.
3. **Are the interactions right?** Water's O towards cations, H towards anions; hydrogen bonds drawn between an H on O (or N) and a lone pair on O (or N) of the other molecule.
4. **Does the amount match the concentration?** If the question gives a concentration or a comparison, check that the number of particles per volume shows it, including after any dilution or mixing.

A diagram can be neat and still lose credit on any one of these. Most errors in exam answers are in steps 2 and 3.

## Worked example 1: from a diagram to molarity

**Question.** A particle diagram represents 0.250 L of potassium sulfate solution, K₂SO₄(aq). It shows 6 K⁺ ions and 3 SO₄²⁻ ions, each drawn as a single unit, with water molecules oriented around them. Each ion drawn stands for 0.0100 mol of that ion. (a) Is the ratio of ions correct? (b) Find [K⁺], [SO₄²⁻] and the molarity of K₂SO₄.

1. **Ratio.** K₂SO₄ gives 2 K⁺ per SO₄²⁻. 6 : 3 = 2 : 1, so the ratio is correct. Charge check: 6(+1) + 3(−2) = 0.
2. **Moles.** n(K⁺) = 6 × 0.0100 = 0.0600 mol; n(SO₄²⁻) = 3 × 0.0100 = 0.0300 mol.
3. **Concentrations.** [K⁺] = 0.0600 mol ÷ 0.250 L = 0.240 M; [SO₄²⁻] = 0.0300 mol ÷ 0.250 L = 0.120 M.
4. **Salt molarity.** One SO₄²⁻ per formula unit, so [K₂SO₄] = [SO₄²⁻] = 0.120 M.

**Answer.** (a) Yes, 2 : 1 with zero total charge. (b) [K⁺] = **0.240 M**, [SO₄²⁻] = **0.120 M**, K₂SO₄ is **0.120 M**.

**What else the diagram should show.** The water molecules nearest each K⁺ should have O facing the ion; those nearest each SO₄²⁻ should have H facing one of the sulfate's oxygen atoms.

## Worked example 2: finding the errors in a student's diagram

**Question.** A student is asked to draw CaCl₂(aq). The student's box shows 3 units, each a Ca atom joined to two Cl atoms, with water molecules around them. Around every unit, the water molecules point their H atoms inwards. Identify the errors and describe a correct diagram.

1. **Ions not separated.** CaCl₂ is ionic. In solution there are no Ca–Cl units: the Ca²⁺ and Cl⁻ ions move apart, each surrounded by its own water molecules.
2. **Charges missing.** The particles should be labelled Ca²⁺ and Cl⁻, not drawn as neutral atoms.
3. **Wrong orientation near the cation.** Water near Ca²⁺ should point its O atom (δ−) towards the ion. H atoms facing Ca²⁺ would mean like charges facing each other.

**A correct diagram** shows, for example, 3 Ca²⁺ and 6 Cl⁻ ions spread out separately. Water molecules around each Ca²⁺ have O inwards; those around each Cl⁻ have one H inwards. Charge: 3(+2) + 6(−1) = 0.

**Why the ratio matters.** Keeping 3 calcium and 6 chlorine was the one thing the student got right. A diagram with equal numbers of Ca²⁺ and Cl⁻ would be wrong even with perfect orientation.

## Worked example 3: comparing two diagrams

**Question.** Box A represents 0.50 L of a sugar solution and contains 6 sugar molecules. Box B represents 1.00 L of the same kind of sugar solution and contains 8 sugar molecules. Which solution is more concentrated, and to what volume would solution A have to be diluted to match B?

1. Particles per litre: A = 6 ÷ 0.50 L = 12 per L; B = 8 ÷ 1.00 L = 8 per L.
2. A is more concentrated, by a factor of 12 ÷ 8 = 1.5, even though B shows more molecules.
3. To match B, the 6 molecules of A must be spread through 6 ÷ 8 per L = 0.75 L.

**Answer.** **A** is more concentrated; diluting A to **0.75 L** would make the two match.

## Common misconceptions

- **Drawing ionic compounds as pairs or lattices in solution.** Dissolved ions are separate, each with its own shell of water molecules.
- **Breaking up polyatomic ions.** NO₃⁻ stays as one NO₃⁻ unit; the N–O bonds are covalent and do not break on dissolving.
- **Splitting molecular solutes into ions.** Glucose and ethanol dissolve as whole molecules.
- **Water the wrong way round.** O faces cations; H faces anions. Ask yourself, "do these ends attract?"
- **Wrong ion ratio.** Count from the formula and check that the charges sum to zero.
- **"More particles drawn means more concentrated."** Only if the boxes represent the same volume.
- **"Dilution removes solute particles."** A diluted diagram has the same number of solute particles in a larger volume.
- **Beyond the scope.** Colligative properties and calculations of molality, percent by mass or percent by volume are not assessed in this course. Molarity is the concentration you will calculate.
