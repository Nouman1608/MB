## How to use this review

Use this page after the [Unit 2 diagnostic](/advanced-course-resources/chemistry/unit-2-diagnostic/) and any topics it flagged. Answer all seven questions on paper before opening any answer. Each combines two or more topics.

These are **original Marlbridge practice questions**, not past exam questions. The alloy composition in Question 5 is invented for practice. Each rubric is a **suggested Marlbridge rubric**, not official scoring, and your total gives no predicted score.

**Data.** Pauling electronegativities (no unit): Li 0.98, Ca 1.00, Be 1.57, Cr 1.66, Fe 1.83, Si 1.90, C 2.55, S 2.58, N 3.04, Cl 3.16, O 3.44, F 3.98. Radii and molar masses are in the calculator note above.

## Big ideas of the unit

- **Electronegativity decides how a pair is shared.** Equal pull gives a nonpolar bond, unequal pull a polar bond, and a large pull by a nonmetal on a metal gives ions. Bonding is a continuum ([Topic 2.1](/advanced-course-resources/chemistry/2-1-types-chemical-bonds-study-guide/)).
- **A bond is a well in a potential energy curve.** Its position is the bond length and its depth is the bond energy. Smaller atoms and higher bond order give shorter, stronger bonds ([Topic 2.2](/advanced-course-resources/chemistry/2-2-intramolecular-force-potential-energy-study-guide/)).
- **An ionic solid is an extended array.** Coulomb's law, |q₊q₋| / d, ranks the attraction; charge usually beats size ([Topic 2.3](/advanced-course-resources/chemistry/2-3-structure-ionic-solids-study-guide/)).
- **A metal is cores in a sea of delocalized electrons.** Added atoms of similar size substitute; much smaller atoms go into gaps ([Topic 2.4](/advanced-course-resources/chemistry/2-4-structure-metals-alloys-study-guide/)).
- **Properties are evidence.** Mobile ions, mobile electrons or neither explain conductivity; like-charge repulsion explains brittleness (2.1, 2.3, 2.4).
- **A Lewis diagram accounts for every valence electron.** Count first, adjust for charge, then check octets. Period 2 atoms never exceed 8 ([Topic 2.5](/advanced-course-resources/chemistry/2-5-lewis-diagrams-study-guide/)).
- **More than one diagram? Use resonance and formal charge.** Equivalent structures average the bond order; formal charge ranks structures that are not equivalent ([Topic 2.6](/advanced-course-resources/chemistry/2-6-resonance-formal-charge-study-guide/)).
- **Domains decide shape.** Count domains, place lone pairs, name the geometry and hybridization, then decide whether bond dipoles cancel ([Topic 2.7](/advanced-course-resources/chemistry/2-7-vsepr-hybridization-study-guide/)).

## Key relationships and methods

| Idea | Relationship or method | Topic |
|---|---|---|
| Bond polarity | larger electronegativity difference, larger bond dipole; δ− on the more electronegative atom | 2.1 |
| Potential energy curve | bond length = distance at the minimum; bond energy = depth of the well (positive) | 2.2 |
| Ionic attraction | ∝ \|q₊q₋\| / d, with d = r₊ + r₋ for touching ions | 2.2, 2.3 |
| Particulate models | ionic: alternate charges, formula ratio, sensible sizes; metal: identical cores, shared electrons, total charge zero | 2.3, 2.4 |
| Alloy type | percent difference = \|r(host) − r(added)\| ÷ r(host) × 100; similar size substitutes, much smaller fits gaps | 2.4 |
| Lewis count | valence electrons, + 1 per negative charge, − 1 per positive charge; bonds = (needed − available) ÷ 2 | 2.5 |
| Formal charge | FC = valence − nonbonding − ½ bonding; sum = overall charge | 2.6 |
| Average bond order | bonds to equivalent atoms ÷ number of equivalent positions | 2.6 |
| VSEPR | domains = bonded atoms + lone pairs; 2, 3, 4 domains → sp, sp², sp³ | 2.7 |
| Dipole moment | polar bonds **and** a shape in which they do not cancel | 2.1, 2.7 |

## Question 1 (multiple choice · mixed)

Which species has a trigonal planar molecular geometry **and** all its bonds to the central atom identical in length?

- (A) The carbonate ion, CO₃²⁻
- (B) The nitrite ion, NO₂⁻
- (C) Phosgene, COCl₂
- (D) Phosphorus trifluoride, PF₃

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (A).** CO₃²⁻ has 4 + 3(6) + 2 = 24 electrons (Topic 2.5). Each octet structure has one C=O and two C–O bonds, and the double bond can be to any of the three oxygens, so the three bonds are identical, with bond order 4 ÷ 3 = 1.33 (Topic 2.6). Carbon has 3 domains and no lone pair: trigonal planar (Topic 2.7).

- (B) Resonance does make the two N–O bonds equal (order 1.5), but N has a lone pair: 3 domains, **bent**.
- (C) Phosgene is trigonal planar, but C=O and C–Cl bonds are not the same length.
- (D) The three P–F bonds are identical, but P has one lone pair (26 electrons): 4 domains, **trigonal pyramidal**.
</details>

## Question 2 (multiple choice · mixed)

Which molecule has a dipole moment?

- (A) SiCl₄
- (B) SCl₂
- (C) BeCl₂ (gas phase)
- (D) CS₂

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (B).** S–Cl is polar (difference 3.16 − 2.58 = 0.58, Cl is δ−; Topic 2.1). SCl₂ has 6 + 2(7) = 20 electrons, which leaves two lone pairs on S: 4 domains, **bent** (Topic 2.7). The two bond dipoles point partly the same way and add.

- (A) Si–Cl bonds are very polar (1.26), but four identical bonds in a tetrahedron cancel.
- (C) Be–Cl bonds are polar (1.59), but Be has only two domains (an incomplete octet with 4 electrons), so the molecule is linear and the dipoles cancel.
- (D) CS₂ is linear, and its C–S bonds are almost nonpolar (difference 0.03).
</details>

## Question 3 (multiple choice · mixed)

Potential energy curves are drawn for the nitrogen–oxygen bond in four species: NO⁺, NO₂⁻, NO₃⁻ and hydroxylamine, H₂N–OH. Which species has the N–O curve with its minimum at the **shortest** distance and the **deepest** well?

- (A) NO⁺
- (B) NO₂⁻
- (C) NO₃⁻
- (D) H₂N–OH

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (A).** NO⁺ has 5 + 6 − 1 = 10 electrons. Needed for two octets: 16, so 6 shared electrons: an N≡O triple bond with one lone pair on each atom (Topic 2.5). Bond orders: NO⁺ 3, NO₂⁻ 1.5, NO₃⁻ 1.33, H₂N–OH 1 (Topic 2.6). The highest bond order gives the shortest, strongest bond, so its well is furthest left and deepest (Topic 2.2).

- (B) Resonance spreads 3 bonds over 2 positions: order 1.5.
- (C) Nitrate has the most bonds in total, but they are spread over three positions: order 1.33.
- (D) Hydroxylamine (14 electrons) has only an N–O single bond. Its curve is the longest and shallowest of the four.
</details>

## Question 4 (constructed response · mixed)

Calcium sulfide, CaS, and lithium fluoride, LiF, are both white solids with high melting points.

(a) Classify the bonding in each solid. Use the electronegativity data and the types of element.
(b) Calculate the distance between ion centres, d, for each solid, then |q₊q₋| / d for each and the ratio of the CaS value to the LiF value.
(c) A student claims: "LiF must have the stronger attraction between its ions, because its ions are smaller." Evaluate the claim with numbers from (b), and predict which solid has the higher melting point.
(d) Describe a two-dimensional particulate model of CaS that uses 8 ions.

<details>
<summary>Worked solution and suggested Marlbridge rubric</summary>

**(a)** Both are **ionic**: each is a metal with a nonmetal. Differences: LiF 3.00; CaS 1.58. CaS has the smaller difference, but a group 2 metal and a group 16 nonmetal form Ca²⁺ and S²⁻ ions; the difference alone does not decide.

**(b)** d(CaS) = 100 + 184 = **284 pm**; d(LiF) = 76 + 133 = **209 pm**.
CaS: |(+2)(−2)| / 284 = 4 ÷ 284 = **0.01408 pm⁻¹**. LiF: 1 ÷ 209 = **0.00478 pm⁻¹**. Ratio CaS : LiF = **2.94**.

**(c)** The claim is **not supported**. LiF's ions are closer, but only by a factor of 284 ÷ 209 = 1.36. CaS's charge product is 4 times larger. Charge wins: 4 ÷ 1.36 = 2.94, so CaS has the stronger attraction, and it is predicted to have the **higher melting point**.

**(d)** **4 Ca²⁺ and 4 S²⁻** (1 : 1; charge 4(+2) + 4(−2) = 0) in one continuous array, alternating so that each ion touches only opposite charges. S²⁻ is drawn larger than Ca²⁺ (184 against 100 pm). Charges are labelled.

| Point | What earns it |
|---|---|
| 1 | (a) Both ionic, with metal + nonmetal as the reason (not the difference alone) |
| 1 | (b) Both distances correct |
| 1 | (b) Both values of \|q₊q₋\| / d and the ratio 2.9 |
| 1 | (c) Rejects the claim: compares the distance factor (about 1.4) with the charge factor (4) |
| 1 | (c) CaS predicted to have the higher melting point, linked to stronger attraction |
| 1 | (d) 4 : 4 ratio, alternating charges, S²⁻ larger than Ca²⁺ |

**Total: 6 points.** A student who says "CaS, because its charges are larger" without comparing the distances earns the prediction point but not the evaluation point.
</details>

## Question 5 (constructed response · mixed)

A stainless steel contains 81.6% iron, 18.0% chromium and 0.40% carbon by mass.

(a) Use the radii to classify how chromium atoms and carbon atoms fit into the iron lattice. Show your reasoning.
(b) Calculate the number of Fe, Cr and C atoms in every 100 atoms of the alloy. Explain why the atom percent of carbon is much larger than its mass percent.
(c) State how many atoms of each element a 50-atom particulate model should show, and where the carbon atoms go.
(d) A student claims: "Chromium transfers electrons to iron, so the bonding in this alloy is ionic." Evaluate the claim using electronegativity and one property of the alloy.

<details>
<summary>Worked solution and suggested Marlbridge rubric</summary>

**(a)** Chromium: |126 − 128| ÷ 126 × 100 = **1.6%**, a comparable size, so chromium atoms replace iron atoms at lattice positions: **substitutional**. Carbon: |126 − 75| ÷ 126 × 100 = **40.5%** (carbon's radius is 0.60 of iron's), much smaller, so carbon atoms fit in the gaps: **interstitial**. This alloy is both.

**(b)** Take 100 g.
- n(Fe) = 81.6 ÷ 55.85 = 1.4611 mol
- n(Cr) = 18.0 ÷ 52.00 = 0.3462 mol
- n(C) = 0.40 ÷ 12.01 = 0.0333 mol
- Total = 1.8405 mol

Per 100 atoms: **79.4 Fe, 18.8 Cr, 1.8 C**. A carbon atom is much lighter than an iron atom (12.01 against 55.85), so a small mass of carbon still contains many atoms: 0.40% by mass becomes 1.8% of the atoms.

**(c)** 50 × 0.794 = 39.7, 50 × 0.188 = 9.4, 50 × 0.018 = 0.9: about **40 Fe and 9 Cr** at lattice positions, and **1 C** in a gap. That is roughly one carbon atom for every 44 iron atoms, not a carbon atom in every gap.

**(d)** The claim is **incorrect**. Chromium (1.66) and iron (1.83) are both low-electronegativity metals; the difference is only 0.17. Neither can take electrons from the other. Both release valence electrons into a delocalized sea: **metallic bonding**. Evidence: the alloy conducts electricity as a solid, which an ionic solid cannot do.

| Point | What earns it |
|---|---|
| 1 | (a) Cr substitutional, with the small size difference |
| 1 | (a) C interstitial, with the large size difference |
| 1 | (b) Moles of all three elements from 100 g |
| 1 | (b) 79.4 : 18.8 : 1.8 per 100 atoms, with the light carbon atom as the reason for the larger atom percent |
| 1 | (c) About 40 Fe, 9 Cr and 1 C, with C in a gap |
| 1 | (d) Rejects the claim: both are low-electronegativity metals, electrons delocalized, supported by a metallic property |

**Total: 6 points.** Accept carried-forward values in (c).
</details>

## Question 6 (constructed response · mixed)

The cyanate ion, OCN⁻, has carbon in the centre. The fulminate ion, CNO⁻, contains the same three atoms but has nitrogen in the centre. Both ions are linear.

(a) Calculate the number of valence electrons in each ion and the number of bonds an octet diagram needs.
(b) Draw (or describe) the three octet resonance structures of OCN⁻, give the formal charge on each atom, and identify the structure that contributes most.
(c) The best structure of CNO⁻ is [C≡N–O]⁻. Give its formal charges, and use formal charge to explain which arrangement of the atoms, cyanate or fulminate, is predicted to be more stable.
(d) Give the number of electron domains on the central atom and its hybridization in each ion.
(e) Predict whether the C–O bond in cyanate is longer or shorter than a C=O double bond. Justify your prediction.

<details>
<summary>Worked solution and suggested Marlbridge rubric</summary>

**(a)** 6 + 4 + 5 + 1 = **16 electrons** for each. Needed for three octets: 24, so 24 − 16 = 8 shared electrons = **4 bonds**.

**(b)**

| Structure | FC on O | FC on C | FC on N | Sum |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| [O=C=N]⁻ | 6 − 4 − 2 = 0 | 4 − 0 − 4 = 0 | 5 − 4 − 2 = −1 | −1 ✓ |
| [O≡C–N]⁻ | 6 − 2 − 3 = +1 | 0 | 5 − 6 − 1 = −2 | −1 ✓ |
| [O–C≡N]⁻ | 6 − 6 − 1 = −1 | 0 | 5 − 2 − 3 = 0 | −1 ✓ |

The third structure, **[O–C≡N]⁻**, contributes most. It and [O=C=N]⁻ have the smallest charges (one −1 each), but it puts the −1 on oxygen, the most electronegative atom. [O≡C–N]⁻ has a −2 charge and +1 on oxygen.

**(c)** [C≡N–O]⁻: C = 4 − 2 − 3 = **−1**, N = 5 − 0 − 4 = **+1**, O = 6 − 6 − 1 = **−1** (sum −1). Even fulminate's best structure has three nonzero formal charges, one of them −1 on carbon, the least electronegative atom. Cyanate's best structure has a single −1, on oxygen. Formal charge predicts that the **cyanate arrangement** is more stable.

**(d)** In both ions the central atom has 2 bonded atoms and no lone pair: **2 domains, sp**, linear (180°).

**(e)** **Longer.** The main structure has a C–O single bond, and only the minor structure [O=C=N]⁻ has C=O. The average C–O bond order is between 1 and 2, closer to 1, so the bond is longer and weaker than a C=O double bond.

| Point | What earns it |
|---|---|
| 1 | (a) 16 electrons and 4 bonds |
| 1 | (b) Three valid structures with correct formal charges |
| 1 | (b) [O–C≡N]⁻ chosen, with −1 on the most electronegative atom as the reason |
| 1 | (c) Formal charges −1, +1, −1, and cyanate preferred because its charges are fewer and better placed |
| 1 | (d) 2 domains and sp for both central atoms |
| 1 | (e) Longer, justified by an average bond order between 1 and 2 |

**Total: 6 points.**
</details>

## Question 7 (constructed response · mixed)

Boron trifluoride, BF₃, and ammonia, NH₃, combine to form a single molecule, F₃B–NH₃, in which a new bond joins boron to nitrogen.

(a) Describe the Lewis diagrams of BF₃ and NH₃. State how many electrons surround B and N.
(b) For BF₃ and NH₃ before they combine, give the shape and approximate bond angle around B and N, and the hybridization of each.
(c) State which of BF₃ and NH₃ has a dipole moment, with a reason for each.
(d) In F₃B–NH₃, the B–N bond is made from the lone pair on nitrogen. Calculate the formal charges on B and N in F₃B–NH₃.
(e) Predict the shape around boron, the F–B–F angle and the hybridization of boron in F₃B–NH₃.
(f) Use your answers to (a) to explain why boron, and not nitrogen, accepts the electron pair.

<details>
<summary>Worked solution and suggested Marlbridge rubric</summary>

**(a)** BF₃ (24 electrons): three B–F single bonds, three lone pairs on each F, nothing on B. **B has 6 electrons** (an incomplete octet; all formal charges are zero). NH₃ (8 electrons): three N–H bonds and one lone pair on N. **N has 8 electrons**.

**(b)** BF₃: 3 domains, **trigonal planar**, **120°**, **sp²**. NH₃: 4 domains with one lone pair, **trigonal pyramidal**, a little **under 109.5°**, **sp³**.

**(c)** **NH₃ has a dipole moment**: the N–H bonds are polar (N 3.04, H 2.20) and the pyramid is unsymmetrical, so the bond dipoles add. **BF₃ has none**: the B–F bonds are very polar, but three identical dipoles 120° apart cancel.

**(d)** In the combined molecule boron has 4 bonds and no lone pair; nitrogen has 4 bonds and no lone pair.
B: 3 − 0 − ½(8) = **−1**. N: 5 − 0 − ½(8) = **+1**. Every F and H is 0, so the sum is 0, as it must be for a neutral molecule.

**(e)** Boron now has 4 bonded atoms and no lone pair: 4 domains, **tetrahedral** around B, F–B–F **about 109.5°** (down from 120°), **sp³**. The BF₃ part becomes a pyramid.

**(f)** Boron has only 6 electrons, so it has room for one more pair; accepting nitrogen's lone pair gives it an octet. Nitrogen already has an octet and, as a period 2 atom, cannot hold more than 8.

| Point | What earns it |
|---|---|
| 1 | (a) Both diagrams, with 6 electrons on B and 8 on N |
| 1 | (b) BF₃ trigonal planar, 120°, sp² |
| 1 | (b) NH₃ trigonal pyramidal, under 109.5°, sp³ |
| 1 | (c) NH₃ polar and BF₃ not, each with a shape-based reason |
| 1 | (d) B −1 and N +1, with the sum checked |
| 1 | (e) Tetrahedral around B, about 109.5°, sp³ |
| 1 | (f) B has an incomplete octet and room for a pair; N is full and cannot exceed 8 |

**Total: 7 points.**
</details>
