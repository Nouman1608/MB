## Recap

- Writers make **comparisons** (similes, metaphors, analogies and anecdotes) to **relate** an argument to an audience.
- An effective comparison must be **shared and understood** by the audience; otherwise it does not advance the writer's purpose.
- A comparison has a **subject** and a **familiar side**. It **transfers** a feeling, structure or value from the second to the first, and it **hides** differences.
- On the exam, name both sides, say what is transferred and explain the effect on this audience.

## Key relationships

| Form | Marker | Typical effect |
|---|---|---|
| Simile | *like* or *as* | Keeps the comparison visible; vivid |
| Metaphor | One thing spoken of as another | Compresses an attitude into a phrase |
| Analogy | Extended point-by-point match | Argues: if the match holds, so does the conclusion |
| Anecdote | A short story as parallel or illustration | Makes the issue concrete and human |

## Testing a comparison

1. **Who is the audience** and what do they know well?
2. **What is the familiar side?** Do they share it?
3. **What is transferred?** (feeling, structure, value)
4. **What is hidden?** (differences the comparison skips)
5. **What does the audience now believe or feel** about the subject?

## Public-domain anchors

- **Booker T. Washington (1895)**: "as separate as the fingers, yet one as the hand in all things essential to mutual progress". Everyone knows the hand; the image transfers unity with distinctness, and it appeals to an audience who valued economic cooperation.
- **Washington, "Cast down your bucket where you are"**: an anecdote of a ship at sea, used as an analogy for finding resources close at hand.
- **Abraham Lincoln (1858)**: "A house divided against itself cannot stand" draws on a familiar domestic image, so an audience can grasp the danger of division at once.

## Worked mini-example

**Marlbridge sentence:** "Letting the river bank erode is like ignoring a leaking roof: each wet season costs more than the last."

- Subject: neglect of the river bank. Familiar side: an unrepaired roof.
- Audience: homeowners, who know that small leaks become expensive damage.
- Transferred: delay makes the cost grow.
- Hidden: a river is far larger and less controllable than a roof.
- **Analysis sentence:** "By comparing erosion to a leaking roof, the writer lets homeowners apply their own experience of repair costs to the river bank, which makes early action seem sensible."

## Mistakes to avoid

1. **Naming the device without saying what it does.**
2. **Ignoring the audience**: a comparison is only effective for people who understand it.
3. **Forgetting what is hidden**, which weakens evaluation.
4. **Mixing metaphors** in your own writing ("the tip of a sinking ship's iceberg").
5. **Clichés** that no longer make the audience see anything new.
6. **Treating an anecdote as proof** rather than as illustration or comparison.

## Quick self-check

1. Is "The company was a sinking ship" a simile or a metaphor? *(A metaphor: it speaks of one thing as another without "like" or "as".)*
2. What must be true of the familiar side of a comparison for it to work? *(The audience must share and understand it.)*
3. Why might a comparison to sailing be a poor choice for an audience of inland city teenagers? *(They may lack the experience that gives the image its meaning, so nothing is transferred.)*
