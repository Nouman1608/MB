## Recap

- **Coordination** joins ideas of equal rank with *for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so*, correlative pairs (*both... and*), or a semicolon.
- **Subordination** makes one idea depend on another through words such as *although, because, when, if, unless, while, who, which*.
- Writers frequently use **coordination to show balance or equality** and **subordination to show imbalance or inequality**.
- Changing the conjunction changes the relationship, and often the writer's stance.

## Key relationships

| Structure | What it signals |
|---|---|
| A, and B | Addition, equal weight |
| A, but B | Contrast between equals; B often last and stressed |
| A; B | Two related ideas set side by side |
| A, so B | Cause and effect between equals |
| Although A, B | A conceded, B is the main point |
| A because B | B is the reason for A |
| If / unless A, B | B depends on a condition |
| B, which / who A | A is extra information about B |

## Contrast sets to remember

- "The scheme cut waiting lists, **but** it cost a great deal." (balanced contrast)
- "**Although** the scheme cut waiting lists, it cost a great deal." (cost is the main point)
- "The scheme cut waiting lists, **although** it cost a great deal." (benefit is the main point)

## Public-domain anchors

- **Lincoln (1865)**: "Both read the same Bible and pray to the same God; and each invokes His aid against the other." Coordination gives the two sides equal standing and sets up the irony of the final clause.
- **Dickens (1859)**: "It was the best of times, it was the worst of times..." Independent clauses of equal form place opposites side by side so that they coexist.

## Worked mini-example

**Three versions of one fact set (Marlbridge):**

1. "The park is beautiful, and the park is dangerous at night." Equal weight; both ideas matter.
2. "Although the park is beautiful, it is dangerous at night." The beauty is conceded; the danger is the main point. The writer is probably arguing for better lighting.
3. "The park is beautiful, although it is dangerous at night." The danger is conceded; the beauty is the main point. The writer is probably defending the park.

**Analysis sentence:** "By subordinating the park's beauty to its danger, the writer concedes a point to appear fair and then directs the reader to the main concern, safety."

## Mistakes to avoid

1. **Reading "subordinate" as "unimportant".**
2. **Describing the structure without its effect**: always say what the arrangement shows about the relationship.
3. **Treating "but" and "although" as identical.**
4. **A comma splice** in your own writing: two independent clauses joined only by a comma.
5. **A string of "and"s** that gives every idea the same weight when you mean to rank them.
6. **Using subordination so heavily** that the main claim is lost.

## Quick self-check

1. Identify the subordinate clause: "The mayor resigned because the audit found errors." *(because the audit found errors)*
2. Which sentence stresses that the product failed: "Although it was cheap, it broke" or "It was cheap, although it broke"? *(The first: "it broke" is the main clause and sits in the end position. The second makes the low price the main point.)*
3. What does a semicolon between two independent clauses suggest? *(Two related ideas of equal rank, with the link left for the reader to draw.)*
