## Why feelings belong in an argument

Many students are taught that a good argument is "logical" and that emotion is a weakness. That is too simple. People are moved to act by what they feel as well as by what they know. A speaker who wants a neighbourhood to protect a park must make the neighbours care about the park. The course framework says that, to achieve a purpose, **writers make choices in an attempt to relate to an intended audience's emotions and values**. Emotion is therefore a tool of persuasion, not a mistake in it. The skill here is to **identify those choices and explain how they work**, and, as a writer, to use them responsibly.

A helpful way to think: **evidence tells an audience that a claim is true; emotion and values tell them that it matters to them.** An argument that does only the first can be correct and still be ignored. An argument that does only the second can move people and still be unsound. Strong arguments connect the two.

## Where emotion comes from in a text

Writers cannot simply announce an emotion. They build it from language and content. Look for these sources:

| Source | How it works | Example (written by Marlbridge) |
|---|---|---|
| **Connotation** | Words carry associations beyond their dictionary meaning. | "Squatting" a building feels different from "occupying" or "sheltering in" it. |
| **Imagery** | Sensory detail lets readers see, hear or feel a scene. | "The swing still turns in the wind, but no child has touched it since March." |
| **Narrative and anecdote** | A story about a person invites sympathy. | "Mrs Okafor has queued at the clinic door at five each morning since the bus was cut." |
| **Shared experience** | A reference to a common memory builds belonging. | "Every one of us remembers the smell of the school library on a wet Monday." |
| **Values language** | Words such as *fairness*, *duty*, *freedom*, *neighbour* trigger what the audience holds dear. | "It is simply not fair that the children who most need the bus are the ones who lose it." |
| **Rhythm and repetition** | Patterned sentences raise the intensity of a feeling. | "We have waited. We have written. We have signed. We will not wait again." |
| **Direct address** | "You" and "we" involve the audience personally. | "You know this road." |

An emotion in a text rarely comes from one device alone. Several often work together.

## Relating to values

A **value** is something an audience treats as important or right: fairness, loyalty, freedom, family, safety, tradition, progress, thrift. Writers relate to values in three common ways.

1. **Naming the value** and claiming it for their side. "This is a question of fairness."
2. **Framing** a proposal in terms of a value the audience holds. A plan to charge for parking can be framed as *fairness* (those who use the road should pay for it) to one audience and as *thrift* to another.
3. **Linking a feeling to a value.** A story about a pensioner left in the cold touches both pity and the value of care for the elderly.

The same facts can be framed in very different ways, and the choice of frame tells you which audience the writer has in mind.

## When emotion is persuasive and when it is not

Emotion can be a strong ally, but it can also weaken an argument if misused. Test it.

- **Relevant?** Does the emotion arise from something that actually bears on the claim? A tearful story about an unrelated topic is a distraction.
- **Proportionate?** Does the feeling fit the situation? Extreme language about a minor matter looks manipulative.
- **Supported?** Is the emotional appeal backed by evidence? A moving story plus a verifiable fact is stronger than either alone.
- **Respectful?** Does it treat the audience as capable of judgement? Appeals that play on fear without evidence, or mock opponents, can alienate thoughtful readers.

On the exam you are not asked to judge the writer's morals, but you should be able to explain what an appeal does and whether it suits the audience and purpose.

## A public-domain example: Chief Joseph's surrender speech (1877)

On 5 October 1877, at Bear Paw in Montana, the Nez Perce leader known as Chief Joseph announced the end of his people's long retreat from the United States Army. The words below were recorded and translated by a listener at the time: "Hear me, my chiefs! I am tired; my heart is sick and sad. From where the sun now stands I will fight no more forever."

The audience included his own people and the army officers, and the account was soon read by a wider American public. Notice how the passage relates to emotion and values:

- **Direct address and imperative** ("Hear me, my chiefs!") establishes authority and asks for attention.
- **Plain statements of feeling** ("I am tired; my heart is sick and sad") are unadorned. The simplicity makes them credible and moving.
- **Imagery of the sun** ("From where the sun now stands") sets the moment of surrender in a natural, timeless frame.
- **The finality of "no more forever"** closes a long struggle with a sense of dignity, not of triumph or defeat alone.

The emotional effect is restrained and the speaker's authority never slips. That combination of restraint and feeling is often more powerful than extravagant language.

## Worked example 1: explaining an emotional appeal

**Task.** Read this passage, written by Marlbridge: "Every morning, Mrs Okafor, who is seventy-eight, walks the two miles to the clinic because the bus no longer runs. She stops twice to rest on a wall that was never meant for resting. The council calls this a saving. She calls it Tuesday." Explain how the passage relates to the audience's emotions and values.

**Step 1: Identify the appeals.**

- **Narrative detail** (one named, elderly person, her daily routine) invites **sympathy**.
- **Imagery** ("a wall that was never meant for resting") makes the effort visible.
- **Contrast of voices** ("The council calls this a saving. She calls it Tuesday.") sets an official euphemism against an ordinary person's reality.

**Step 2: Link to the audience.**

The audience is likely residents who value **care for the elderly** and **fairness in public services**. The passage shows an unfair cost falling on a vulnerable person, so it connects the feeling (sympathy, indignation) to a value (fairness).

**Step 3: State the effect.** The appeal makes an abstract budget decision personal, moving readers to see the bus cut as a human cost, not a line in a report.

**Check.** The answer names devices, ties each to a feeling and a value, and says what the appeal does to the argument. It does not say simply "this is emotional".

## Worked example 2: adding emotion and values to a flat paragraph

**Task.** Improve this flat draft, written by Marlbridge, so that it relates better to an audience of local residents: "The playing field is being sold to developers. This will reduce green space. People will have fewer places to exercise."

**Step 1: Diagnose.** The draft states facts and a consequence but has no image, no person and no value. It is **accurate but inert**.

**Step 2: Choose a value.** Residents value **community** and **children's wellbeing**.

**Step 3: Add imagery and a human case.** Replace "green space" with something concrete: the field where the Saturday football teams play, the lane where children learn to ride bikes.

**Step 4: Connect feeling to value, keeping evidence.**

> The Marsh Lane field, where Saturday football has been played for sixty years, is being sold to developers. If it goes, the nearest open space for the 300 children at Marsh Lane Primary will be a car-park-sized lawn two miles away. A community that cares for its children does not trade their playing field for a row of houses.

**Check.** The revision keeps the facts (the sale, sixty years, 300 children) and adds a concrete place, a human group, and an appeal to a shared value ("a community that cares for its children"). The emotion is **earned by detail**, not asserted by adjectives such as "terrible" or "shocking".

## How the exam asks about this

- **Reading:** you may be asked what a word's connotation contributes, how an anecdote relates to the audience's emotions, or which sentence appeals most to a shared value.
- **Writing:** you may be asked which revision adds an appropriate emotional appeal for a stated audience, or which detail best suits a purpose such as honouring or warning.
- **Rhetorical analysis essay:** discuss appeals as part of how choices serve a purpose for an audience. Link each device to an effect and to the audience.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Emotional appeals are always illogical."** Emotion can be relevant and well supported. It is a problem only when it replaces evidence or is out of proportion.
- **"Strong emotion needs strong adjectives."** Often plain language and concrete detail carry feeling better than loaded words.
- **"Values are the same for every audience."** Different audiences rank values differently; a frame that moves one may leave another cold.
- **"An anecdote is always emotional."** It may also serve as evidence or as an example. Ask what it does in that text.
- **"If a text moves me, it is a good argument."** Feeling is one test of persuasiveness; reasoning and evidence must also be sound.
- **"I should name the emotion I want."** Telling readers "feel sad" rarely works. Show the situation that makes them feel it.
