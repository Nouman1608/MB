## Two questions to ask of every source

When you read a source, two questions help you place it.

1. **What does it argue?** This is its **position**: where it stands on the issue.
2. **Where is it arguing from?** This is its **perspective**: the vantage point shaped by the writer's background, interests and expertise.

The course framework says that a position and a perspective are different, and that sources may have the **same position** on a subject yet each come from a **different perspective** based on background, interests and expertise.

A map metaphor helps. Two people may both point to the same hill and say, "That is the best viewpoint." Their *position* is the same. But one is a farmer who sees the hill as grazing land, and one is a photographer who sees it as a skyline. Their *perspectives* differ, and so do the reasons they give.

| | Position | Perspective |
|---|---|---|
| Question | What do you argue? | From where do you argue? |
| Example | "Phones should be banned in class." | "As a teacher who has watched attention drift for twenty years..." |
| Can change between sources? | Yes (for, against) | Yes (teacher, parent, student, doctor) |
| Two sources may share one while differing in the other | Yes | Yes |

## Clues to perspective

Perspective is often signalled, though not always stated. Look for:

- **Role or background:** "As a parent...", "In my thirty years as a nurse...".
- **Interests:** what the writer stands to gain or lose.
- **Expertise:** what kind of knowledge the writer relies on (data, personal experience, professional training).
- **Choice of evidence:** a teacher cites lesson time; a parent cites safety; a student cites accessibility.
- **Vocabulary and tone:** the words a writer reaches for show their concerns.
- **What is left out:** a source may not mention concerns that matter to others.

A perspective is not an error. Everyone writes from somewhere. But noticing a perspective explains why the source chooses the evidence it does, and lets you judge how far that evidence travels.

## Same position, different perspectives

Consider a debate about whether schools should ban phones during lessons. Here are three fictional sources.

> **Source D** (a secondary school teacher, fictional): "I support a ban. In my classes, a phone on the desk costs ten minutes of attention before the lesson has begun. Students concentrate better when the temptation is out of sight."
>
> **Source E** (a parent, fictional): "I also support a ban, but for a different reason. Phones follow children into school with whatever group chats and arguments they had last night. A school day without them is a break from that."
>
> **Source F** (a sixth-form student, fictional): "I oppose a blanket ban. I use my phone to translate words in lessons, and I know a student who needs hers to monitor a medical condition. Ban the games, not the tool."

| Source | Position | Perspective | Evidence it emphasises |
|---|---|---|---|
| D | For a ban | Teacher (classroom management) | Lost attention and lesson time |
| E | For a ban | Parent (wellbeing and social pressure) | Group chats, arguments |
| F | Against a blanket ban | Student (practical needs, accessibility) | Translation, medical monitoring |

Sources D and E share a **position** but differ in **perspective**, so they give different reasons. Source F has a different position and a different perspective.

A writer who understands this can do more than say "two sources agree". The writer can say: "Teachers and parents support the ban for different reasons: teachers see lost lesson time, and parents see unwanted social pressure. That two groups with different concerns reach the same conclusion strengthens the case, although neither speaks for students whose phones are tools."

## Perspective in a published text

Susan B. Anthony, in 1873, argued that women already held the right to vote under the Constitution:

> It was we, the people; not we, the white male citizens; nor yet we, the male citizens; but we, the whole people, who formed the Union.

(Susan B. Anthony, "On Women's Right to Vote", 1873)

Her **position** is that the Constitution's "people" includes women. Her **perspective** is that of a woman indicted for voting: she argues from personal exclusion and from the principle of citizenship. The repeated "not we..." clauses show the perspective at work: she gives the audience the categories used to exclude her and rejects them one by one. A male lawyer who agreed with her position might argue from a perspective of constitutional law, and a different set of evidence and tone would follow.

## Using position and perspective in writing

When synthesising, ask of each source: *position, perspective, and what it adds*.

1. **Group by position.** Which sources agree and which disagree?
2. **Within a group, note perspectives.** Do they give different reasons?
3. **Use the difference.** Show that the same conclusion is reached from different routes, or that an opposing source rests on a perspective others lack.
4. **Name the perspective** when it explains the evidence. "From a classroom teacher's viewpoint..." is an economical way to show why the evidence is shaped as it is.

## Worked example 1: classifying and combining

**Task.** Write a sentence that combines Sources D, E and F in your argument that schools should replace a blanket phone ban with a rule about when phones may be used.

**Step 1: Classify.** D (for ban, teacher), E (for ban, parent), F (against ban, student).

**Step 2: Find the common ground.** All three are concerned about the effects of phones. D and E worry about attention and pressure; F worries about losing useful functions.

**Step 3: Choose the claim.** A rule about *when and how* phones may be used meets D's concern about attention and E's about social pressure without removing the practical uses F mentions.

**Step 4: Draft.**

> A teacher worried about lost lesson time and a parent worried about group-chat pressure both argue for a ban, from different concerns, and a student who relies on her phone for translation and for a classmate's medical monitoring argues against; a rule that limits when phones may be used, instead of whether they may be brought, would answer the first two without sacrificing the uses the third describes.

**Step 5: Check.** The sentence names each perspective, shows how the positions relate and uses them to support the writer's own claim.

## Worked example 2: how perspective shapes evidence

**Task.** Explain how Source D's perspective shapes the evidence it offers.

**Step 1: Identify the perspective.** A teacher who sees the effect of phones on classroom attention.

**Step 2: List the evidence.** "A phone on the desk costs ten minutes of attention before the lesson has begun." "Students concentrate better when the temptation is out of sight."

**Step 3: Link evidence to perspective.** Both pieces of evidence concern the classroom: lesson time and concentration. They are the observations a teacher is best placed to make.

**Step 4: Note what the perspective leaves out.** The teacher does not mention students' practical needs (translation, medical monitoring) or home life, which Sources F and E raise.

**Step 5: Write the analysis sentence.** *Source D's evidence is drawn from the classroom, which makes it persuasive about attention and lesson time but silent about the practical and social uses of phones that other perspectives raise.*

## Common misconceptions

- **"Position and perspective mean the same thing."** Position is what a source argues; perspective is where it argues from.
- **"Sources that agree say the same thing."** They may share a position but reach it from different perspectives and with different evidence.
- **"Having a perspective makes a source unreliable."** Everyone has one. Reliability depends on how the source handles evidence and other views.
- **"Perspective is always stated."** Often you infer it from role, interests, evidence and tone.
- **"Using several perspectives weakens an argument."** Using them well shows that the writer has considered the issue from several sides.
- **"A source's perspective explains everything it says."** It explains tendencies, not every claim; evaluate each claim on its evidence.

## Try it yourself

Two fictional sources discuss whether a school canteen should stop selling fizzy drinks. **Source 1** (a dentist): "Fizzy drinks coat teeth in sugar and acid; I see the damage every week." **Source 2** (a canteen manager): "Fizzy drinks are our best-selling item, and losing them would cost the canteen money." State each source's position and perspective.

<details>
<summary>Suggested answer</summary>

Source 1 takes the position that the canteen should stop selling fizzy drinks and argues from a **dental-health** perspective, using professional experience of tooth damage as evidence. Source 2 takes the position that the canteen should keep selling them (implied by the loss it describes) and argues from a **business** perspective, using sales and cost as evidence. Neither mentions the other's concern, which a writer using both sources could point out.
</details>
