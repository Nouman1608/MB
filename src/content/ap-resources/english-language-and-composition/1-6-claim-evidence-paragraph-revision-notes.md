## Recap

A body paragraph develops **one claim** with **evidence** and **commentary**. Writers **embed** quoted, paraphrased or summarised source material in their own sentences so that it reads as part of their argument. A dropped quotation (sitting alone) is a weakness; a quotation that is part of your grammar and followed by commentary is a strength.

## The paragraph pattern

1. **Claim** in your own words.
2. **Lead-in** naming the source or context.
3. **Evidence** (quotation, paraphrase or summary), embedded.
4. **Commentary** explaining how the evidence supports the claim.
5. **Link** to the larger argument.

In a timed essay, spend most of your space on step 4.

## Three methods

| Method | What it is | Use when |
|---|---|---|
| Quotation | Exact words in quotation marks | The wording itself matters |
| Paraphrase | Same idea, new words and new structure, about the same length | The idea matters more than the wording |
| Summary | A short statement of the main point of a longer passage | You need the gist |

All three need credit to the source and a link to your claim.

## Embedding techniques

- Use a **phrase** as part of your own grammar: "his call for 'malice toward none'".
- Use a **signal phrase** with a comma or colon: "As Lincoln urges, 'let us strive on...'".
- Make the quotation the **object** of a verb: "He asks the nation to act 'with firmness in the right'".
- Use brackets or ellipses to adapt grammar or omit words, but never change the meaning.

## Worked mini-example

Claim: Lincoln's Second Inaugural (4 March 1865) turns the audience from war to rebuilding. Embedded evidence: By asking the nation to proceed "with malice toward none, with charity for all," Lincoln removes revenge from the agenda. Commentary: the verb "strive" and the noun "work" make peace a demanding task.

Weak version: "Lincoln wants to heal the nation. 'With malice toward none, with charity for all.' This shows his tone." The quotation is dropped in and the comment is too thin.

## Signal verbs

| Verb | Suggests |
|---|---|
| states, notes | neutral report |
| urges, appeals | the writer wants action |
| warns | risk or threat |
| concedes | a point granted to an opponent |
| insists | a strongly held claim |

Choose a verb that matches what the writer does. Do not say "proves" when the source only suggests.

## Checks before you move on

- Can I cover the quotations and still read a paragraph of my own?
- Is each quotation short and chosen for its wording?
- Does my commentary say how the evidence supports the claim, rather than repeat it?
- Have I credited the source for quotations, paraphrases and summaries?

## Mistakes to avoid

- Dropping a quotation in as its own sentence.
- Using a long quotation when a phrase will do.
- Paraphrasing by swapping a few words.
- Summarising with your own opinion added.
- Leaving a source uncredited when paraphrasing.
- Writing commentary that only repeats the evidence ("This shows that Lincoln wants peace").

## Quick self-check

1. What is a dropped quotation? **A quotation placed as its own sentence with nothing joining it to the writer's argument.**
2. When is a quotation better than a paraphrase? **When the wording itself matters.**
3. What must a paraphrase change? **The words and the sentence structure, not just a few words.**
4. Which verb best suggests the writer wants action: "notes" or "urges"? **"Urges".**
5. Name the part of a paragraph that explains how the evidence supports the claim. **The commentary.**
