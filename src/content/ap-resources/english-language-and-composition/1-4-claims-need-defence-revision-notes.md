## Recap

A **claim** is a position the writer asks the audience to accept, and it must be one that **requires a defence**: a reasonable person could disagree, so the writer must give evidence or reasoning. A fact, a topic and a preference are not claims. A strong claim is arguable, specific, supportable and significant. Claims may be explicit or implicit, and a long text has a main claim supported by sub-claims.

## Key relationships

| Statement type | Test | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Fact | Can be checked, not in dispute | "The library opens 40 hours a week." |
| Topic | Names a subject, asserts nothing | "Library hours" |
| Preference | Personal taste, cannot be disproved | "I like quiet places." |
| Claim | A reasonable person could dispute it | "The council should restore the library's hours." |

## Kinds of claim

- **Fact or existence:** something is, was or will be so, but is disputed.
- **Definition:** what something counts as.
- **Cause:** one thing produces another.
- **Value:** something is good, bad, right or wrong.
- **Policy:** something should be done.

Each kind tells you what must be defended: a causal claim needs a causal link, a policy claim needs a problem and a workable remedy.

## Upgrading a weak claim

1. Ask "Could a reasonable person disagree?" If no, the sentence is a fact or a topic.
2. Take a side: add a verb of judgement or recommendation (should, harms, improves).
3. Narrow it: name the group, place or situation.
4. Check support: could you defend it with evidence or reasoning?

Weak: "Social media is a big part of life." Stronger: "Social media has made teenagers better informed about world events but worse at sustaining attention on any one of them."

## Worked mini-examples

- Lincoln, 1858: "I believe this government cannot endure, permanently half slave and half free." A claim about the future, disputed in its day, so it needs a defence.
- Thoreau, 1849: "I heartily accept the motto, 'That government is best which governs least'..." A claim of value and policy: those who think government should do more would dispute it.

## Reading for claims on the exam

- Ask of each sentence: would a reader reply "Why?" or "I'm not sure"? If so, it is a claim.
- State the main claim in one sentence, then list the sub-claims by paragraph.
- If no sentence states the main claim, summarise what the evidence adds up to.
- Do not quote a fact as if it were the claim.

## Mistakes to avoid

- Treating a fact, topic or preference as a claim.
- Writing a claim so broad that nobody could dispute it.
- Assuming a claim must be false or controversial worldwide.
- Expecting the main claim always to appear in the first paragraph.
- Writing a claim that cannot be supported by evidence (a pure taste).

## Quick self-check

1. Is "The school day ends at 3.30 p.m." a claim? **No, it is a fact.**
2. What question tests whether a sentence is a claim? **Could a reasonable person disagree, so that I would need to defend it?**
3. Why is "Homework" not a claim? **It is a topic: it asserts nothing.**
4. Improve "Technology changes learning." **For example: "Tablets in classrooms widen pupils' access to information but weaken their ability to read at length."**
5. Name two kinds of claim. **For example, policy and cause (also fact, definition, value).**
