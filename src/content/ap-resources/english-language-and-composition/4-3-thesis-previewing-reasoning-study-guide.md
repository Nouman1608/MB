## What a thesis already tells the reader

A **thesis statement** is the central claim of an argument, the sentence a reader could agree or disagree with. In Unit 2 you wrote theses that required defence. This topic adds a refinement: a thesis **may preview the line of reasoning**. That means it can give the reader a map of how the argument will go.

The course framework puts it carefully. A thesis may preview the line of reasoning. This does *not* mean the thesis must list the points of the argument, the aspects to be analysed or the specific evidence to be used. The preview is an **indication of structure**, not a table of contents.

Think of a thesis in three levels of detail:

| Level | What it does | Example (Marlbridge, on free public transport) |
|---|---|---|
| Claim only | States a position | "Cities should make public transport free." |
| Claim with a governing reason | States a position and the idea that holds the argument together | "Cities should make public transport free because fares work against the purpose of the service." |
| Claim with a preview of reasoning | States a position and signals the reasons or shape of the argument | "Cities should make public transport free, because fares exclude the riders who need the service most, consume much of what they raise, and keep drivers in their cars." |

All three are acceptable. The third gives a reader the clearest expectations, and in a timed essay it tells the reader quickly that the writer has a plan. The choice depends on the length and complexity of the argument. A short essay may do well with a claim and one governing reason. A long argument with several moves benefits from a preview.

## Preview, announcement and list

It is easy to confuse a preview with two things that look similar but fail.

- **An announcement** states what the essay will do: "In this essay I will discuss the cost of fares, the cost of collecting them and congestion." It names topics, makes no claim and can be neither true nor false.
- **A bare list** names parts without a claim or a link: "Free public transport affects cost, collection and congestion." It does not say *what* the effect is.
- **A preview** makes a claim and signals the reasoning that supports it, in sentences that can be argued with.

Compare the three side by side.

| Version | Text | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| Announcement | "This essay will look at fares, collection costs and cars." | Weak: it describes a task, takes no position |
| Bare list | "Fares, collection and congestion are all relevant to free transport." | Weak: no claim, no relation between the parts |
| Preview | "Free transport would repair three failures: fares shut out riders who need the service, collecting them eats revenue, and the price of a ticket sends drivers back to their cars." | Strong: a claim with reasons the reader can test |

## How a preview signals structure

A thesis can signal structure in several ways:

- **Parallel reasons:** "because A, B and C" suggests an order of reasons.
- **A progression:** "moves from... to..." tells the reader the argument develops in stages.
- **A contrast or concession:** "although X, Y" signals that the writer will acknowledge another view before developing the claim.
- **A sequence of effects:** "first... and finally..." in an analysis of a speech shows the order of the writer's choices.

For a **rhetorical analysis** essay, a thesis may preview the strategies and their effect: "By beginning with a shared memory and then moving to a statistic, the speaker first earns the audience's trust and then makes the demand for change difficult to refuse." This signals two stages in the speech and the writer's reading of their purpose.

## Reading a preview

In reading tasks you may be asked to **identify the thesis** and describe **what it indicates about the argument's structure**. A reliable method:

1. **Find the claim.** Which sentence takes a position?
2. **Look for signals.** Are there reasons, a progression, a contrast or a concession?
3. **Check against the body.** Do the topic sentences follow what the thesis signalled?
4. **Describe it in terms of function.** For example: "The thesis tells the reader that the argument will first show a cost, then a benefit."

## Worked example 1: writing a previewing thesis from a plan

**Task.** Argue whether cities should make public transport free. (The claims below are for practice in structure; they are not presented as research findings.)

**Step 1: Make a line-of-reasoning plan.** The writer decides:

- Reason 1: fares are a barrier for the lowest-income riders, who rely on transport most.
- Reason 2: collecting fares is itself costly, so part of the money raised never reaches the service.
- Reason 3: if travel is free, more people use buses and fewer drive, reducing congestion.

**Step 2: Draft a claim.** "Cities should make public transport free." A defensible claim, but it gives the reader nothing about why.

**Step 3: Add the previewing reasons using a *because* clause.** Draft: "Cities should make public transport free, because fares exclude riders who need the service, cost money to collect and keep drivers in their cars."

**Step 4: Check that it is a preview and not a list.** The sentence takes a position, and each reason is a claim someone could dispute (that fares exclude, that they are costly to collect, that they keep drivers in cars). The reasons are in the order the body will follow.

**Step 5: Polish for style and precision.** Revised: "Cities should make public transport free, because a fare excludes the riders who most need the service, absorbs money in its own collection and keeps drivers behind the wheel."

**Step 6: Check against the body.** Body paragraph 1 will argue exclusion; paragraph 2, collection cost; paragraph 3, drivers. If the writer later finds the best evidence is about drivers, the thesis can be reordered; the point is that thesis and body agree.

## Worked example 2: reading a thesis for its signals

Read this introduction (Marlbridge passage).

> Every spring the town fills its flowerbeds with annuals that die by October. A better use of the same money lies in the soil beneath them: native perennials that return each year. The switch is worth making because perennials cost less over five years, support local insects that annuals ignore, and ask the town for less water once they are established.

The three body paragraphs begin:

> *Paragraph 2:* "The first argument is financial: an annual is bought again every year..."
> *Paragraph 3:* "Perennials also feed and shelter insects that the town's annual beds cannot."
> *Paragraph 4:* "Finally, a perennial that is established needs less watering..."

**Step 1: Find the thesis.** The final sentence of the introduction: "The switch is worth making because perennials cost less over five years, support local insects that annuals ignore, and ask the town for less water once they are established." The claim is that the switch is worth making; the *because* clause gives the reasons.

**Step 2: Identify the signal about structure.** The thesis names three reasons in a sequence: cost, insects, water. A reader expects three developments in that order.

**Step 3: Check against the body.** The topic sentences follow the order (financial, insects, watering), with "first" and "finally" marking the sequence. The preview matches the structure.

**Step 4: Describe the preview.** *The thesis previews the line of reasoning by pairing its claim with three reasons in a fixed order, so the reader can follow the argument and measure each paragraph against a promise.*

**Step 5: Note what the preview does not do.** It does not list evidence: it does not say what the cost figures are or which insects. A preview need not.

## Weak, better and strong theses for an analysis

Suppose the task is to analyse a short speech in which a captain addresses a rowing team before a race (Marlbridge example).

| Version | Thesis | Comment |
|---|---|---|
| Weak | "The captain uses ethos, pathos and logos." | A list of labels; no claim about effect or purpose |
| Better | "The captain motivates the team by appealing to their emotions." | A claim, but it does not show how or why |
| Strong | "By starting from what the team has already endured and then turning to what is still to be earned, the captain converts weariness into pride and makes the final push feel like a debt to themselves." | A claim with a preview of the speech's movement and its effect |

## Common misconceptions

- **"A preview means listing every point."** The framework says a thesis need not list the points, aspects or evidence.
- **"A preview is the same as announcing what you will discuss."** An announcement takes no position.
- **"Naming three reasons always makes a good thesis."** Three weak or unrelated reasons make a weak thesis. The reasons must support the claim and be developed.
- **"The thesis cannot change once I start writing."** If the body reveals a better order or a better claim, revise the thesis. Agreement between the thesis and the body is what matters.
- **"A thesis that previews is always one long sentence."** It can be two sentences. Clarity is the test.
- **"A thesis preview is only for argument essays."** It is as useful in a rhetorical analysis, where it can signal the stages of the speaker's strategy and effect.
