## Two jobs for descriptive words

An **adjective** describes a noun; an **adverb** describes a verb, an adjective or another adverb. Together we can call them **descriptive words** or modifiers. The course framework observes that these words do two things at once:

1. They **qualify or modify** the things they describe (the *tall* building, *slowly* walked).
2. They **convey a perspective** toward those things.

Take the phrase "a *so-called* solution". The adjective "so-called" modifies "solution" and, at the same time, tells you the writer doubts that it is one. "A *carefully* worded statement" and "a *cleverly* worded statement" describe the same statement but suggest different attitudes: one respectful, the other suspicious.

| Phrase | What it modifies | What it conveys |
|---|---|---|
| "a *modest* proposal" | The proposal | Restraint, reasonableness (or irony, in context) |
| "a *sweeping* reform" | The reform | Ambition, perhaps recklessness |
| "*barely* adequate" | The adjective | Disapproval |
| "*merely* a suggestion" | The noun | Dismissal |
| "*fortunately*, the vote passed" | The whole sentence | Approval of the result |
| "the *regrettably* necessary cuts" | The adjective | Reluctance and justification |

A reader should therefore **read modifiers for attitude**, and a writer should **choose them for attitude**.

## Precision

**Precise word choice** means using the word that says exactly what you mean and rules out other things. The framework notes that precise word choice **reduces confusion** and may help the audience perceive the writer's perspective.

Vague words make readers guess. Compare:

| Vague | Precise |
|---|---|
| "The meeting was bad." | "The meeting ran two hours and ended without a decision." |
| "The shop sells many things." | "The shop sells bicycles, spare parts and secondhand maps." |
| "She went quickly to the door." | "She sprinted to the door." |
| "The result was very good." | "The result exceeded the target by a fifth." |
| "A thing happened." | "A pipe burst." |

Precision usually comes from three habits:

- **Replace vague words with exact ones.** *Thing, stuff, good, bad, nice, very, really, a lot* are signals to stop and find something specific.
- **Prefer strong verbs to verb-plus-adverb.** "Whispered" beats "said quietly"; "dashed" beats "ran quickly".
- **Use concrete nouns and numbers** when you can.

Precise language does **not** mean fancy language. The aim is the exact word, and often it is a plain one.

### Precision and perspective

Precision also affects how the reader sees the writer's stance. A writer who says "the policy failed" has given a verdict; one who says "the policy reduced waiting times by a minute but raised costs by a tenth" has given information and trusts the reader to judge. In argument, each can be right in its place, but readers should know which they are getting.

## Modifiers in published prose

Abraham Lincoln's Second Inaugural Address (1865) contains this sentence:

> Fondly do we hope—fervently do we pray—that this mighty scourge of war may speedily pass away.

The adverbs "fondly" and "fervently" modify "hope" and "pray" and convey the intensity and affection of the wish; "speedily" says how soon. The adjective "mighty" and the noun "scourge" (a whip, a cause of suffering) together present the war as an immense force of punishment. The inverted word order ("Fondly do we hope") puts the modifiers first, so the reader meets the feeling before the thing hoped for.

In Thomas Paine's *The American Crisis* (1776) the phrase "the summer soldier and the sunshine patriot" uses nouns as modifiers: "summer" and "sunshine" describe a soldier and a patriot who serve only in fair weather. The two short modifiers carry an entire judgement, and a whole argument about loyalty, in one image.

## Worked example 1: making a draft precise

**Draft (Marlbridge example).** From an argument that a town should repair its public pool:

> The pool is in a bad state. The changing rooms are not nice, and a lot of things are broken. Many people have had problems there, and it is very unpleasant.

**Step 1: Mark the vague words.** "bad state", "not nice", "a lot of things", "broken", "problems", "very unpleasant".

**Step 2: Ask what the writer actually knows.** Suppose the writer can name the facts: the changing-room floor is cracked, two of six showers do not work, a ceiling tile has fallen, and a swimmer slipped on the wet tiles last month.

**Step 3: Replace vague with precise.**

> The pool is falling apart. In the changing rooms, the floor tiles are cracked and two of the six showers do not work. Last month a ceiling tile fell, and a swimmer slipped on the wet floor.

**Step 4: Add a modifier that conveys perspective, where it helps.**

> Last month a ceiling tile fell, and a swimmer slipped on the **dangerously** wet floor.

"Dangerously" is an adverb that does two jobs: it qualifies the floor and conveys the writer's concern.

**Step 5: Check for support.** "Falling apart" is a judgement, so the precise details that follow must earn it, and they do.

## Worked example 2: analysing modifiers and precise words

Read this passage (Marlbridge passage).

> The council's **sleek** new brochure promises a **bold** vision for the harbour. It **blandly** announces 40 new jobs without saying where they will come from. **Quietly**, on page nine, it admits that the fishermen's slipway will close.

**Step 1: Identify modifiers.** *sleek*, *bold*, *blandly*, *Quietly*.

**Step 2: Name what each conveys.**

- *Sleek* (new brochure) suggests glossy, polished surface, with a hint of style over substance.
- *Bold* (vision) is the brochure's own claim, reported with slight irony.
- *Blandly* (announces) suggests an unspecific, uninformative manner.
- *Quietly* (admits) suggests concealment: bad news tucked away.

**Step 3: Look at the precise details.** "40 new jobs", "page nine" and "the fishermen's slipway" are exact. They let the reader check the writer's claims, and they give the criticism more force than if the writer had said "The council is misleading."

**Step 4: Link to purpose.** The writer's perspective is sceptical. The adverbs and adjectives tell the reader how to take the brochure, and the precise details give that scepticism a basis.

**Step 5: Write the analysis sentence.** *The writer pairs sceptical modifiers such as "sleek", "blandly" and "Quietly" with exact details like "40 new jobs" and "page nine", so that the reader both senses the writer's distrust and is given the facts to share it.*

## Common misconceptions

- **"Descriptive words are decoration."** They qualify and convey perspective, so they can carry argument.
- **"More modifiers make better description."** Stacked or redundant modifiers weaken writing. Choose exact nouns and verbs first.
- **"Precise means long or technical."** Precise means exact. A plain word can be the most precise.
- **"Vague words are safe."** They make the reader guess and give nothing to argue with.
- **"A strong adverb always strengthens a verb."** Sometimes a stronger verb is better: "shouted" instead of "said loudly".
- **"The writer's perspective is only in the thesis."** It runs through word choice, including modifiers.

## Self-assessment: rate your own revision for precision

| Criterion | Not yet | Getting there | Secure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Vague words | Thing, stuff, good, very remain | Some replaced, some remain | Every vague word replaced by a specific one |
| Verbs | Weak verb plus adverb throughout | A few strong verbs | Strong verbs where a verb-and-adverb pair would be used |
| Modifiers | Stacked or decorative | Some carry meaning | Each modifier adds meaning or perspective that the noun lacks |
| Support | Judgements with no detail | Some detail | Judgements backed by exact details the reader can check |

## Try it yourself

Revise this sentence for precision, keeping the claim: "The park was in a very bad state and a lot of the equipment was broken."

<details>
<summary>Suggested answer</summary>

> The park was littered with glass, two of its four swings had no seats and the roundabout no longer turned.

The vague "very bad state" and "a lot of the equipment was broken" became specific details (glass, swings without seats, a seized roundabout). The reader can now picture the park and judge for themselves. If the writer wants to convey perspective as well, a modifier such as "dangerously" before "littered" would add a judgement the details support.

## Checklist for reading precision

- Which words in this sentence are exact, and which are general?
- What would the reader have to guess if the details were removed?
- Which modifiers show the writer's attitude?
- Do the exact details support the attitude the modifiers convey?
