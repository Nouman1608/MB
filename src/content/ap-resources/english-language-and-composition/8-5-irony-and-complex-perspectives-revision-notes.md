## Recap

- Writers may signal a **complex or ironic perspective** through **stylistic choices**.
- **Irony may emerge from the differences between an argument and readers' expectations or values.**
- The reader reads in three layers: **surface meaning**, **signals**, then **intended meaning and purpose**.
- Irony depends on **shared knowledge**: if the audience misses the signals, the writer may be misread.

## Key relationships

| Kind | What happens |
|---|---|
| Verbal irony | The writer says the opposite of what is meant |
| Understatement | Says less than the truth warrants |
| Overstatement | Says more than the truth warrants |
| Situational irony | The outcome contradicts what was expected |
| Satire | A sustained ironic text that exposes a flaw |
| Sarcasm | A sharp, mocking form of verbal irony |

| Signal | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Incongruity | Calm voice, outrageous content |
| Exaggeration or understatement | Claims pushed far beyond or below belief |
| Clash with values | A proposal no decent reader accepts |
| Voice against writer | A smug or pompous speaker the writer does not share |
| Mock-formal diction | Elaborate language for trivial or shocking points |

## How to analyse irony

1. **State the surface meaning.**
2. **List the signals** with quotations.
3. **State the intended meaning** that makes sense of the whole.
4. **Explain the purpose** and the effect on the audience (who shares the values the irony relies on?).

## Public-domain anchors

- **Jonathan Swift (1729)**, *A Modest Proposal*: a calm, statistical voice proposes something horrifying; the incongruity exposes attitudes toward the poor.
- **Samuel Johnson (1755)**, *Dictionary*, "oats": a deadpan definition that delivers a joke through the contrast of "horses" and "the people".
- **Jane Austen (1813)**: "It is a truth universally acknowledged..." applies elevated diction to a social assumption, which signals irony.

## Worked mini-example

**Marlbridge passage:** "Our new bus timetable is a triumph of optimism. Buses will arrive when they arrive, giving passengers a thrilling sense of adventure."

- **Surface:** praises the timetable.
- **Signals:** "triumph of optimism", "thrilling sense of adventure" (exaggeration); the content describes unreliable service.
- **Intended meaning:** criticism of unreliable buses.
- **Analysis sentence:** "By praising unreliability in enthusiastic terms, the writer exposes the timetable's failure with humour, trusting readers to see the gap between the cheerful voice and the delays."

## Mistakes to avoid

1. **Reading ironic text literally** and attributing its surface view to the writer.
2. **Equating irony with sarcasm.**
3. **Calling a text ironic without quoting signals.**
4. **Ignoring the audience's role**: irony depends on shared values.
5. **Forgetting the purpose**: irony is chosen to do something, such as expose, mock or persuade.
6. **Using irony in your own timed essay** without making the signals unmistakable.

## Quick self-check

1. What are the three layers of reading an ironic text? *(Surface meaning, signals, intended meaning and purpose.)*
2. Name two signals of irony. *(Any two: incongruity, exaggeration or understatement, clash with values, mock-formal diction, a gap between voice and writer.)*
3. Why can irony be risky for a writer? *(An audience that does not share the values or miss the signals may read it literally.)*
