## Recap

Writers use evidence **strategically and purposefully** to **illustrate, clarify, set a mood, exemplify, associate or amplify** a point. Strategic evidence strengthens validity and reasoning, relates to the audience's emotions and values, and builds the writer's credibility. When reading, explain what a piece of evidence is doing; when writing, choose evidence by the job you need done.

## Six jobs

| Job | Meaning | Short example |
|---|---|---|
| Illustrate | Make a general point visible | The bus shelter full by nine |
| Clarify | Make an unfamiliar idea understandable | Compound interest as a rolling snowball |
| Set a mood | Create an atmosphere | "The hush of a waiting room" |
| Exemplify | A representative instance | The Corner Café's closure |
| Associate | Link to something respected or feared | Comparing a library closure with a school closure |
| Amplify | Raise the scale or force | Not one shop but twelve |

## Four things to weigh when choosing

1. The **claim**: does the evidence bear directly on it?
2. The **audience**: will it convince these readers and connect with their values?
3. The writer's **credibility**: is it reliable, relevant and fair?
4. The **purpose**: inform, persuade, honour, warn or move to act?

## Source credibility

Ask whether the source is **qualified**, **independent** and **current**. A named study by a relevant department carries more weight than "I read somewhere".

## Strategic selection versus cherry-picking

Selecting is normal. Hiding what cuts against you can mislead. A fair writer acknowledges contrary evidence and explains why it does not change the conclusion.

## Worked mini-example

Lincoln, 16 June 1858: "A house divided against itself cannot stand." Clarifies (turns a political problem into a familiar picture), associates (the phrase comes from the Gospels, which his audience knew), and amplifies ("cannot stand" implies collapse). It prepares the claim that follows: "I believe this government cannot endure, permanently half slave and half free."

For a governors' audience asked to keep a minibus: choose the cost comparison with taxis (validity) and the usage table (exemplifies need) before a parent's story (illustration) or a cheerful photograph (mood only).

## How to write about it

1. Quote the evidence briefly.
2. Name the job (illustrate, clarify, associate...).
3. Say how it fits the audience and purpose.
4. Link to the claim.

## Mistakes to avoid

- Calling evidence "an example" and stopping.
- Choosing the most dramatic evidence regardless of relevance.
- Assuming true evidence is automatically well chosen.
- Ignoring contrary evidence.
- Treating a famous source as automatically credible.
- Saying an analogy proves a claim.

## Quick self-check

1. Name three jobs of evidence. **For example illustrate, clarify, amplify.**
2. Why associate a point with something the audience respects? **It transfers respect or feeling and makes the point seem familiar.**
3. What question tests whether evidence is relevant? **Does it bear directly on what the claim says?**
4. What is cherry-picking? **Choosing only the evidence that suits and hiding what does not.**
5. What job does Lincoln's "house divided" image do first? **It clarifies by turning an abstract problem into a familiar picture.**
