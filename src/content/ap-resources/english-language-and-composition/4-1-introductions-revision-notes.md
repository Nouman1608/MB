Short on time? This page is the recap. For explanations and worked examples, use the [full study guide](/advanced-course-resources/english-language-and-composition/4-1-introductions-study-guide/).

## Recap

- The **introduction** introduces the subject and/or the writer to the audience.
- It may **present the thesis**, but it need not.
- It may **orient, engage and focus** the audience.
- Common strategies: a quotation, an intriguing statement, an anecdote, a question, a statistic or data, contextualised information, a scenario.
- A strategy is judged by its **effect on this audience for this purpose**, not by its type.
- In reading questions, name the strategy and then its effect. In writing questions, choose the opening that best prepares the reader for the claim.

## Key relationships

| Job | What the reader should know afterwards | Typical tools |
|---|---|---|
| Orient | Subject, speaker, situation, why now | Context, a scenario, the writer's own position or role |
| Engage | A reason to keep reading | Anecdote, surprising fact, question, striking image |
| Focus | What kind of claim is coming | A thesis, or a sentence that points straight at it |

| If the audience is... | Consider opening with... |
|---|---|
| A decision-making body that values evidence | Data or contextual information, then the claim |
| A general audience that needs to care | Anecdote or scenario |
| An expert or busy reader | The thesis first |
| Sceptical or hostile | A shared value, a concession, or a question that does not threaten them |

## Assumptions and conventions

- In a timed essay an introduction is usually three to five sentences.
- A short quotation is fine if it is **accurate**, **relevant** and **discussed**.
- Where the exam gives sources, refer to them by author or title; do not invent facts or numbers to make an opening more vivid.
- Opening with the thesis is acceptable; so is leading to it.

## Mistakes to avoid

1. **Starting with "Since the beginning of time..."** or any other empty generalisation.
2. **Announcing the plan** ("In this essay I will discuss...") instead of making a claim.
3. **A decorative quotation** that is never connected to the argument.
4. **A long anecdote** that delays the claim until the reader has lost the thread.
5. **A dictionary definition** that does not help the argument.
6. **Writing an opener unconnected to the claim** so that the two feel like different essays.
7. **Naming a strategy without its effect** when analysing a text ("the writer uses a question" and stopping there).

## Quick self-check

1. Name the three jobs of an introduction. *(Orient, engage, focus.)*
2. A writer opens a speech to a town council with the cost of a proposal and the number of people affected. Which strategies are these, and why might they suit that audience? *(Data and contextual information; councillors decide on costs and effects.)*
3. Why is "In this essay I will discuss both sides of the issue" a weak opening? *(It describes the task, takes no position and gives the reader no stake.)*
