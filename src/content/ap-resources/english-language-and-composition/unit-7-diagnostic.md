Use this diagnostic to find **which Unit 7 topics to revisit**: one short question per topic, with extra questions on the claim and sentence topics.

These are **original Marlbridge practice questions**, not past exam questions. All passages are written by Marlbridge, and all figures are invented for practice. The diagnostic is not calibrated and gives **no predicted score**.

**How to take it.** Allow 30 minutes and answer everything before opening any answer. No calculator is needed. The three written questions need only a few sentences each.

## Question 1 (multiple choice · 7.1)

A writer opens an essay on school meals: "At 12.10, forty students stand in a queue that will not reach the till before the bell." Which move does the opening mainly make?

- (A) A quotation from an expert
- (B) A scenario that engages the reader with a concrete problem
- (C) A summary of the argument
- (D) A call to act

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (B).** It places the reader in a specific situation, which engages them and sets up a problem.

- (A) has no quoted voice.
- (C) Nothing has been argued yet to summarise.
- (D) Nothing asks the reader to act.

**If you missed this:** [Topic 7.1 study guide](/advanced-course-resources/english-language-and-composition/7-1-introductions-and-conclusions-study-guide/).
</details>

## Question 2 (multiple choice · 7.2)

Which claim is most likely to be criticised as an oversimplification?

- (A) "Some students find music helps them concentrate."
- (B) "Homework never helps anyone."
- (C) "In the trial school, test scores rose slightly after the change."
- (D) "The evidence suggests longer breaks may help younger pupils."

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (B).** "Never" and "anyone" are absolutes that a single counterexample would refute.

- (A) limits itself with "some".
- (C) names its scope and its size ("slightly").
- (D) uses "suggests" and "may".

**If you missed this:** [Topic 7.2 study guide](/advanced-course-resources/english-language-and-composition/7-2-complexity-and-oversimplification-study-guide/).
</details>

## Question 3 (multiple choice · 7.3)

In "Where bus services are frequent, fares can fall without harming service," which part limits the claim by **condition or place**?

- (A) "fares"
- (B) "Where bus services are frequent"
- (C) "fall"
- (D) "harming service"

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (B).** The "Where..." clause sets the circumstances in which the claim holds.

- (A) and (C) are the subject and main verb.
- (D) is the thing the claim says will not happen; it is not a limit on the claim's scope.

**If you missed this:** [Topic 7.3 study guide](/advanced-course-resources/english-language-and-composition/7-3-qualifying-claims-with-modifiers-study-guide/).
</details>

## Question 4 (multiple choice · 7.4)

"When every plan had failed, when every appeal had been refused, the town finally built its own bridge." This sentence is best described as

- (A) loose, because the main clause comes first.
- (B) periodic, because the main clause is held back until the end.
- (C) simple, because it has one verb.
- (D) balanced, because it has two equal independent clauses.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (B).** Two dependent "when" clauses build up before the main clause arrives.

- (A) reverses the order.
- (C) The sentence has three verbs and three clauses.
- (D) The first two clauses are dependent, not independent.

**If you missed this:** [Topic 7.4 study guide](/advanced-course-resources/english-language-and-composition/7-4-clauses-and-sentence-arrangement-study-guide/).
</details>

## Question 5 (multiple choice · 7.5)

"Although the museum was crowded, the visitors were polite." Compared with "The museum was crowded, and the visitors were polite," the first sentence

- (A) gives the two ideas equal weight.
- (B) subordinates the crowding and treats the politeness as the main point.
- (C) subordinates the politeness.
- (D) has the same structure.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (B).** "Although..." makes the crowding a conceded, subordinate idea, and the main clause carries the politeness.

- (A) describes the "and" version.
- (C) reverses the roles.
- (D) is false: one is complex, the other compound.

**If you missed this:** [Topic 7.5 study guide](/advanced-course-resources/english-language-and-composition/7-5-coordination-and-subordination-study-guide/).
</details>

## Question 6 (multiple choice · 7.6)

Which sentence is punctuated correctly?

- (A) "The results were clear; the school changed its policy."
- (B) "The results were clear, the school changed its policy."
- (C) "The results were: clear the school changed its policy."
- (D) "The results were clear the school, changed its policy."

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (A).** A semicolon correctly joins two related independent clauses.

- (B) is a comma splice.
- (C) misplaces the colon and omits a break.
- (D) misplaces the comma and creates a run-on.

**If you missed this:** [Topic 7.6 study guide](/advanced-course-resources/english-language-and-composition/7-6-grammar-mechanics-and-punctuation-study-guide/).
</details>

## Question 7 (multiple choice · 7.7)

A notice prints "Parking permits must be renewed **by 31 May**." The boldface on "by 31 May" mainly

- (A) turns the phrase into a title.
- (B) makes the deadline prominent so that readers notice it.
- (C) shows that the phrase is a quotation.
- (D) signals the end of a paragraph.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (B).** Bold creates emphasis through contrast with the surrounding plain text.

- (A) Titles are usually italicised or set larger.
- (C) Quotation marks would mark a quotation.
- (D) Bold does not mark paragraph ends.

**If you missed this:** [Topic 7.7 study guide](/advanced-course-resources/english-language-and-composition/7-7-design-features-for-emphasis-study-guide/).
</details>

## Question 8 (short answer · 7.2 and 7.3)

Revise this claim so that it keeps a position but avoids oversimplification: "Social media destroys friendships." Use at least two different kinds of modifier and name them.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Sample.** "Heavy social media use **can** weaken some friendships **when** it replaces time spent together, although it also helps many people stay in touch across distance." Modifiers: "can" (certainty), "some" (amount), "when it replaces time spent together" (condition), "although it also helps many people..." (concession).

**Marking guide.** Full credit for a revision that (1) keeps a position, (2) uses at least two different kinds of modifier correctly named and (3) is still arguable.

**If you missed this:** Topics [7.2](/advanced-course-resources/english-language-and-composition/7-2-complexity-and-oversimplification-study-guide/) and [7.3](/advanced-course-resources/english-language-and-composition/7-3-qualifying-claims-with-modifiers-study-guide/).
</details>

## Question 9 (short answer · 7.4 and 7.5)

Rewrite "The shop raised its prices, but sales went up" so that the **sales rise** is the main point and the price rise is a concession. Explain what your structure does.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Sample.** "Although the shop raised its prices, sales went up." The price rise is in a subordinate "Although" clause (a concession), and the main clause, which is also in the end position, states the sales rise.

**Marking guide.** Full credit for a subordinated concession, the sales rise in the main clause and a sentence of explanation that mentions subordination and the end position.

**If you missed this:** Topics [7.4](/advanced-course-resources/english-language-and-composition/7-4-clauses-and-sentence-arrangement-study-guide/) and [7.5](/advanced-course-resources/english-language-and-composition/7-5-coordination-and-subordination-study-guide/).
</details>

## Question 10 (short answer · 7.1 and 7.6)

Write a two-sentence conclusion to an essay arguing that schools should teach first aid. Use one colon or dash purposefully and name the conclusion move you use.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Sample.** "A first-aid lesson costs a morning; the skill might one day save a life. Schools should teach it, because the one moment a student needs it will not wait for a better time." The move is **explaining implications** with a purposeful semicolon joining two related clauses (a colon or a dash would also be acceptable if used purposefully).

**Marking guide.** Full credit for two sentences that add significance or implication (not just repetition), a correctly used mark that has a purpose and a named move.

**If you missed this:** Topics [7.1](/advanced-course-resources/english-language-and-composition/7-1-introductions-and-conclusions-study-guide/) and [7.6](/advanced-course-resources/english-language-and-composition/7-6-grammar-mechanics-and-punctuation-study-guide/).
</details>
