## Two layers of meaning

Every word has at least two layers.

- **Denotation** is the **literal meaning**: what the word refers to, the sense you would find in a dictionary. The denotation of *home* is a place where a person lives.
- **Connotation** is the **set of feelings, associations and judgements** the word carries beyond that literal sense. *Home* connotes warmth, safety, belonging. *House* has the same basic denotation but little of that feeling.

The course framework says simply that words have both connotative and denotative meanings. This matters because a writer's choice between words with the same denotation is a choice between attitudes.

## Same meaning, different attitude

Consider words that all describe a person who spends little money.

| Word | Denotation | Connotation |
|---|---|---|
| thrifty | careful with money | Admiring: wise, responsible |
| frugal | careful with money | Neutral to admiring: disciplined |
| economical | careful with money | Neutral: practical |
| cheap | careful with money | Critical: unwilling to pay fairly |
| stingy | careful with money | Strongly critical: mean |

The denotation is almost identical, and the effect is not. A writer who calls a neighbour "thrifty" invites the reader to admire; one who calls the same neighbour "stingy" invites the reader to disapprove.

More examples:

| Neutral | Positive | Negative |
|---|---|---|
| walk | stroll | trudge |
| determined | resolute | stubborn |
| unusual | distinctive | peculiar |
| crowd | gathering | mob |
| old | seasoned | outdated |

Connotation depends on **context**. "Ambitious" can be a compliment in a job reference and a criticism in a description of a friend who steps on others. It also depends on **audience and culture**: a word that sounds warm in one community may sound dismissive in another. A writer should consider who is reading.

## How connotation builds tone and style

**Tone** is the writer's attitude toward the subject (Topic 6.5). Readers infer tone largely from word choice, and especially from the **positive, negative or neutral connotations** of the words. A paragraph that describes a protest as "a gathering of residents" has a different tone from one that describes "a mob of agitators", although both may refer to the same event.

**Style** is the overall pattern of a writer's choices. A writer who consistently chooses formal, elevated words (*endeavour, commence*) has a different style from one who chooses plain words (*try, start*), though the denotations match.

When you analyse a text, move through four steps:

1. **Pick out** significant words and phrases.
2. **Name the connotation** (positive, negative, neutral, and what kind of association).
3. **Compare** with what the writer might have said.
4. **Link** the choice to tone and to purpose.

## Connotation in a published text

In his Rochester address (1852), Frederick Douglass turns the language of national celebration against itself:

> To him, your celebration is a sham; your boasted liberty, an unholy license; your national greatness, swelling vanity.

(Frederick Douglass, "What to the Slave Is the Fourth of July?", 1852)

Each pair sets a familiar, flattering phrase against a harsh one. "Liberty" and "greatness" have strong positive connotations for his audience, but Douglass attaches them to words with negative connotations: **boasted** suggests empty bragging, and **swelling** suggests inflation and rot, not strength. "License" in the sense of *permission* is neutral; with "unholy" it connotes **excess and abuse of freedom**. "Sham" means a pretence. Douglass thus uses the connotations of every key word to say that the nation's celebration is false from the point of view of the enslaved.

## Choosing words strategically

When writing, decide on the attitude you want the reader to take and then select words that carry it. Consider three questions:

1. **What do I want the reader to feel about this subject?** (admire, doubt, sympathise, worry)
2. **What do these words connote to this audience?**
3. **Do the connotations match what the evidence can support?** If you call a plan "reckless" you owe the reader an argument that it is, and if the word is not earned the reader may stop trusting you.

Be especially careful not to **signal what you did not mean**. Calling a candidate's speech "a rant" tells the reader you dislike it; if you wanted a neutral account you should have said "speech".

## Worked example 1: choosing connotation for an audience

**Task.** A student writes a letter to a school newspaper arguing that students should be allowed to leave the grounds at lunchtime. The draft sentence is: "Students want to get out of school at lunch."

**Step 1: Identify the attitude to be created.** The audience includes parents and teachers who may fear misbehaviour. The writer wants them to see students as responsible.

**Step 2: Examine the draft's connotations.** "Get out of" suggests escape from something unpleasant; "want" suggests mere desire. The sentence implies that students see school as a prison, which will alarm the audience.

**Step 3: Choose words with the right associations.**

> Students ask for the responsibility of leaving the grounds at lunchtime.

"Ask for" is polite and mature; "responsibility" casts the privilege as an obligation; "leaving the grounds" is neutral and exact.

**Step 4: Check against the evidence.** If the writer can show that older students already manage their time well, "responsibility" is earned. If not, the word is a claim that needs support.

**Step 5: Check for unintended signals.** "Ask" suggests asking permission, which fits the respectful tone.

## Worked example 2: analysing connotation in a passage

Read this passage (Marlbridge passage).

> The developers *swarmed* over the old orchard with their *machines*, *tearing out* trees that had *stood* for a century. By evening, the *quiet* hillside was a *scar* of mud.

**Step 1: Pick out the key words.** *swarmed*, *machines*, *tearing out*, *stood*, *quiet*, *scar*.

**Step 2: Name their connotations.**

- *Swarmed* suggests insects or an unwelcome, numerous invasion.
- *Machines* is cold and impersonal compared with "workers" or "crews".
- *Tearing out* connotes violence compared with "removing".
- *Stood* personifies the trees as steady and dignified.
- *Quiet* suggests peace, contrasting with the noise of the machines.
- *Scar* connotes a wound.

**Step 3: Compare with alternatives.** A neutral version: "The developers arrived at the old orchard with equipment, removing trees that had grown for a century. By evening, the hillside was cleared."

**Step 4: Say what the choices do.** The words make the developers seem aggressive and the orchard seem a victim, so the writer's attitude is critical of the development.

**Step 5: Write the analysis sentence.** *By choosing words such as "swarmed", "tearing out" and "scar", the writer gives the development the connotations of invasion and injury, which leads the reader to sympathise with the orchard.*

## Common misconceptions

- **"A word means what the dictionary says, nothing more."** The dictionary gives the denotation; connotation is part of the real meaning for readers.
- **"Synonyms are interchangeable."** They share a denotation but often differ in connotation.
- **"Strong connotations are always manipulative."** They are a legitimate tool, but a reader should ask whether the evidence supports the attitude.
- **"A neutral word has no effect."** A neutral choice can be a strategy, for example to appear objective.
- **"Connotation is the same for every reader."** It varies by culture, context and audience.
- **"To analyse word choice, list the words."** The analysis must say what each word connotes and what that does for tone and purpose.

## Try it yourself

Rank these words for a person who does not give up, from most admiring to most critical, and explain the connotation of the two at the ends: *determined, obstinate, persistent, stubborn, resolute.*

<details>
<summary>Suggested answer</summary>

One defensible ranking is **resolute, determined, persistent, stubborn, obstinate**. "Resolute" connotes firm, admirable purpose, often in the face of difficulty. "Obstinate" connotes unreasonable refusal to change, even when reasons are given. All five share the denotation of continuing despite difficulty; the difference is the writer's judgement of whether the continuing is wise. Slightly different rankings are acceptable if the connotations are explained.
</details>

## Checklist for reading a word's connotation

- Does the word carry praise, blame or neither?
- Would a neighbouring word have said the same thing more neutrally?
- Does the word fit the writer's evidence, or does it overreach?
- Does the audience share this association?
