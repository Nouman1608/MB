## Recap

- To **rebut** is to offer a **contrasting perspective** on an argument and its evidence, or **alternative evidence**, to **propose** that all or a portion of a competing claim is invalid.
- To **refute** is to **demonstrate, using evidence**, that all or a portion of a competing claim is invalid.
- A rebuttal reframes or offers competing evidence; a refutation shows with evidence that the claim fails.
- Writers enhance credibility when they **refute, rebut or concede** opposing arguments and contradictory evidence.

## Key relationships

| | Rebut | Refute |
|---|---|---|
| Method | Contrasting perspective or alternative evidence | Evidence demonstrating invalidity |
| Force | Proposes | Demonstrates |
| Use when | Evidence is mixed or the dispute is about interpretation or values | Strong evidence shows the claim is false or flawed |

| Tool | Example |
|---|---|
| Counterexample | A case where the claim fails |
| Challenge an assumption | "The argument assumes that..." |
| Challenge the evidence | Sample too small, source biased, comparison unfair |
| Alternative explanation | Another cause for the same facts |
| Unintended consequences | The proposal produces the opposite of its aim |
| Higher priority or value | Fairness before cost |

## A fair response in four steps

1. **State** the opposing claim as its holder would.
2. **Say** what part you rebut or refute.
3. **Give** the view or the evidence.
4. **Explain** what follows for your thesis.

## Public-domain anchor

**John Stuart Mill, *On Liberty* (1859)**: the belief that truth always prevails over persecution is "one of those pleasant falsehoods which men repeat after one another till they pass into commonplaces, but which all experience refutes." Mill names the claim, calls it an unexamined commonplace and appeals to evidence from experience. His phrase "pleasant falsehoods" concedes the belief's appeal while rejecting it.

## Worked mini-example

**Opposing claim (Marlbridge):** "Homework is pointless because pupils copy it."

- **Rebuttal:** "Copying shows a problem with the tasks, not with homework itself; tasks that need personal reflection are harder to copy."
- **Refutation:** "In a survey of 300 pupils, 82 per cent said they completed most homework themselves, and those who did scored higher." *(Invented for practice.)*

**Analysis sentence:** "The writer rebuts by shifting attention to task design and refutes by offering survey evidence, so the claim that homework is pointless is answered both by perspective and by data."

## Mistakes to avoid

1. **Knocking down a strawman.**
2. **Calling a rebuttal a refutation** (or the reverse) without checking whether evidence demonstrates the point.
3. **Attacking the person** instead of the claim.
4. **Refuting a part and claiming the whole.**
5. **Offering no evidence** in a refutation.
6. **Ignoring the strongest objection.**

## Quick self-check

1. Which uses evidence to demonstrate invalidity: rebut or refute? *(Refute.)*
2. Name two tools for answering a claim. *(Any two: counterexample, challenge an assumption, challenge the evidence, alternative explanation, unintended consequences, higher priority.)*
3. Why is a strawman damaging? *(It misrepresents the opposing view, so informed readers distrust the writer.)*
