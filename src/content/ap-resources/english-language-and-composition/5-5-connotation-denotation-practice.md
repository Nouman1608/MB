## Question 1 (multiple choice · foundation)

A writer wants to describe a child who asks many questions in a way that shows approval. Which word best does this?

- (A) nosy
- (B) prying
- (C) curious
- (D) meddlesome

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (C).** All four words refer to a person who asks questions, but "curious" has a favourable connotation of eagerness to learn.

- (A) suggests unwelcome interest in others' business.
- (B) suggests intrusion.
- (D) suggests interfering in matters that are not one's concern.
</details>

## Question 2 (multiple choice · core)

Read this passage (Marlbridge passage).

> The protesters trickled in, a handful of tired neighbours holding hand-lettered signs.

Which statement best describes the effect of the word choice?

- (A) It alarms the reader by suggesting the protesters are a threat.
- (B) It makes the protesters seem small, modest and sympathetic, so the reader is likely to view them kindly.
- (C) It shows that the writer despises the protesters.
- (D) It presents the protesters in a purely neutral way.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (B).** "Trickled", "handful", "tired" and "hand-lettered" suggest small numbers, weariness and homemade effort, which invites sympathy.

- (A) A threatening crowd would be "swarmed", "stormed" or similar.
- (C) The words are gentle, not scornful.
- (D) The language is not neutral: a neutral version might read "Some protesters arrived with signs."
</details>

## Question 3 (multiple choice · core)

In his Rochester address (1852), Frederick Douglass says of the enslaved person's view of the nation: "your national greatness, swelling vanity". What does the word "swelling" contribute?

- (A) It suggests healthy growth.
- (B) It suggests inflation or disease, so that the nation's "greatness" is shown as puffed up and unhealthy.
- (C) It shows that Douglass agrees with the audience's pride.
- (D) It introduces a new topic.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (B).** Applied to "vanity", "swelling" connotes something inflated and unwholesome, which undermines the positive word "greatness".

- (A) The word is attached to vanity, so it does not signal healthy growth.
- (C) The passage criticises the pride.
- (D) The phrase continues a list of contrasts rather than introducing a new topic.
</details>

## Question 4 (multiple choice · core)

A student is writing a formal letter to a mayor and wants to report that the council refused a proposal. Which word is best suited to the audience and register?

- (A) trashed
- (B) shot down
- (C) declined
- (D) nixed

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (C).** "Declined" is formal and neutral in tone, which suits a letter to a public official.

- (A) is informal and carries a dismissive connotation.
- (B) is a colloquial, violent metaphor.
- (D) is slang and informal.
</details>

## Question 5 (multiple choice · foundation)

Which statement about connotation and denotation is most accurate?

- (A) A word's denotation is its emotional associations, and its connotation is its dictionary meaning.
- (B) Words with the same denotation can carry different connotations, so a writer's choice among them shapes the reader's attitude.
- (C) Connotation is the same for every reader.
- (D) Connotation matters only in poetry.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (B).** Denotation is the literal meaning; connotation is the associations, and writers choose among synonyms for their connotations.

- (A) reverses the definitions.
- (C) Connotation varies with context, culture and audience.
- (D) Connotation matters in every kind of writing, including arguments.
</details>

## Question 6 (constructed response · core)

Here is a plain statement (Marlbridge passage): "Mr Okoye has kept the same car for twenty years and repairs it himself." Write two versions of the sentence, using the same facts: one that makes Mr Okoye seem admirable and one that makes him seem foolish. Underline or list the words that create each effect.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Sample response (Marlbridge).**

*Admiring:* Mr Okoye is a **loyal, resourceful** owner who has **cared for** the same car for twenty years and **keeps it running** with his own skilled hands.

*Critical:* Mr Okoye **clings to** the same **clapped-out** car after twenty years and **tinkers with** it himself instead of **admitting** it should be replaced.

*Words and effects:* "loyal", "resourceful", "cared for" and "skilled" carry admiration; "clings to", "clapped-out", "tinkers" and "admitting" suggest stubbornness and neglect.

**Suggested Marlbridge rubric (4 points).**

| Point | Award it when the response... |
|---|---|
| 1 | Keeps the facts the same in both versions (twenty years, same car, self-repair) |
| 1 | Uses clearly positive connotations in the first version |
| 1 | Uses clearly negative connotations in the second version |
| 1 | Identifies the words that create each effect |
</details>

## Question 7 (constructed response · core)

Read this passage (Marlbridge passage).

> The headmaster *shuffled* to the podium, *clutching* his notes, and *droned* through the results. Nobody *stirred*. When he finally sat down, the applause was *polite*.

Explain how the writer's word choice conveys an attitude toward the headmaster and the occasion.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Sample response.** The verbs "shuffled" and "clutching" suggest an uncertain, reluctant figure, not a confident speaker. "Droned" connotes a monotonous, tiresome delivery, and "Nobody stirred" implies that the audience is bored or numb. "Polite" applause is courteous but unenthusiastic, so the occasion ends without warmth. Together the words convey a gently critical, mildly satirical attitude: the writer sees the ceremony as dull and the headmaster as ineffectual, without being cruel.

**Suggested Marlbridge rubric (4 points).**

| Point | Award it when the response... |
|---|---|
| 1 | Quotes at least two specific words |
| 1 | Names the connotation of each word accurately |
| 1 | Identifies the attitude toward the headmaster and the occasion |
| 1 | Explains how the choices create that attitude (not only lists them) |
</details>

## Question 8 (constructed response · stretch)

Write a paragraph of four to six sentences for a sceptical town council arguing that the town should build a skate park. Choose words whose connotations will reassure the audience. Then explain two of your word choices and say how you avoided an overstated tone.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Sample response (Marlbridge).**

> A skate park would give the town's young people a **supervised, purpose-built** place to practise a skill they already pursue on the high street. Rather than **banning** skaters from steps and car parks, the council can **channel** their energy into a **safe, well-lit** facility that residents can **monitor**. Neighbouring towns that have built similar parks report fewer complaints about skating in public spaces, which suggests the project is a **sensible** response to an existing problem, not a risk the town is **inviting**.

*Word choices:* "supervised, purpose-built" and "safe, well-lit" connote order and safety for a worried audience; "channel" suggests guiding energy, not suppressing it. I avoided an overstated tone by using "suggests" and "sensible", so the claim does not rely on loaded words alone.

**Suggested Marlbridge rubric (5 points).**

| Point | Award it when the response... |
|---|---|
| 1 | Uses connotations that suit the audience (reassuring, orderly, practical) |
| 1 | Avoids words that would alarm this audience |
| 1 | Supports the attitude with a reason or evidence |
| 1 | Explains at least two word choices accurately |
| 1 | Shows awareness of the limit of loaded words (a measured tone) |
</details>
