## Recap

- A **modifier** is a word, phrase or clause that qualifies or limits the scope of something. In a claim, it tells the audience how far the claim reaches.
- Writers use modifiers **strategically**: to fit the evidence and the audience, not to dodge commitment.
- Modifiers come as **words** (often, largely, may), **phrases** (in most urban districts, with supervision) and **clauses** (although the evidence is limited, unless funding is cut).
- A qualified claim keeps a **position** and becomes **more defensible**.

## Key relationships

| Dimension | Question | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Frequency | How often? | often, rarely, usually |
| Amount | How much? | some, many, most, largely, partly |
| Group | For whom? | among older pupils, in rural schools |
| Place or time | Where or when? | in coastal towns, in the short term |
| Condition | Under what circumstances? | if, unless, provided that |
| Certainty | How sure? | may, can, tends to, suggests |
| Concession | What is granted? | although, despite |

**Strength scale (weak to strong):** might, may, can, tends to, usually, almost always, always. Match the point on the scale to the evidence.

## Rules of thumb

- **One study, one case** supports "may" or "suggests".
- **Many consistent studies** support "usually" or "generally".
- **A definition or law** needs no modifier.
- **Position, scope, condition**: give each claim all three where the evidence needs them.

## Worked mini-example

**Overstated:** "Remote work makes everyone more productive."

**Qualified:** "Remote work **can** raise productivity **for roles that need focused, independent effort**, **particularly where** employers provide the right equipment, although collaborative work **may** suffer."

Modifiers: "can" (certainty), "for roles that need focused, independent effort" (group), "particularly where..." (condition), "although collaborative work may suffer" (concession plus certainty). The position survives, and a reader can now agree or disagree with a precise claim.

**In an analysis:** "By limiting the claim to roles that need focused effort, the writer avoids an easy counterexample and appears more credible."

## Mistakes to avoid

1. **Hiding the claim** under so many modifiers that no position remains.
2. **Using a weak modifier for strong evidence**, which sounds timid.
3. **Using a strong modifier for weak evidence**, which overreaches.
4. **Naming a modifier without its effect** in an analysis.
5. **Putting a modifier far from what it modifies**, which makes the sentence ambiguous (see Topic 8.6).
6. **Treating all hedges as the same**: "may" and "usually" assert different things.

## Quick self-check

1. Underline the modifiers: "In most cities, buses can reduce congestion unless fares rise sharply." *(In most cities; can; unless fares rise sharply.)*
2. Which modifier is stronger, "may" or "usually"? *(Usually: it claims a pattern, not a mere possibility.)*
3. Write one sentence limiting "Exams should be abolished" by group and condition. *(For example: "For subjects assessed by coursework, exams should be abolished, provided that moderation keeps marking fair.")*
