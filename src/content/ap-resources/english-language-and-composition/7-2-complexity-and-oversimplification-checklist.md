Guide = [study guide](/advanced-course-resources/english-language-and-composition/7-2-complexity-and-oversimplification-study-guide/); Practice = [practice questions](/advanced-course-resources/english-language-and-composition/7-2-complexity-and-oversimplification-practice/).

## Understanding

- I can explain how a lack of understanding of a subject leads to oversimplification and generalisation. *(Guide: "Why simple claims break"; Practice question 1)*
- I can explain why effective arguments avoid absolute terms when they join an ongoing conversation. *(Guide: "Why simple claims break"; Practice question 4)*
- I can explain why a qualified claim can be stronger than an absolute one. *(Guide: "Writing the revised claim without going vague")*

## Skills

- I can spot absolute words, hasty generalisation, single cause and false dichotomy in a claim. *(Guide: "How oversimplification looks on the page"; Practice questions 1 and 6)*
- I can sort a new piece of evidence into supports, refutes or complicates. *(Guide: "Evidence has three possible relationships to a claim"; Practice questions 3 and 7)*
- I can revise an oversimplified claim into one that has position, scope and condition. *(Guide: Worked example 1; Practice questions 2 and 5)*
- I can avoid over-hedging while still qualifying a claim. *(Guide: "Writing the revised claim without going vague"; Practice question 2)*

## Reasoning

- I can explain how a writer's choices show an understanding of complexity, and how that affects credibility. *(Guide: Worked example 2; Practice question 8)*
- I can decide whether to change a claim's scope or abandon its position when evidence complicates it. *(Guide: Worked example 1; Practice question 7)*
- I can use a public-domain example, such as Mill's sentence on knowing only one's own side, to show precision about limits. *(Guide: "A public-domain example of respect for complexity")*
