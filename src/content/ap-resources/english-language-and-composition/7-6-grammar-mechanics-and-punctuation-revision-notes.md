## Recap

- Grammar and mechanics that follow **established conventions** enable **clear communication**.
- On the exam, small errors typical of unrevised timed writing do not hurt; errors so frequent or serious that they **interfere with communication** do.
- Writers also use **punctuation strategically** to show relationships among ideas.
- Punctuation advances a purpose by **clarifying, organising, emphasising, indicating purpose, supplementing information** or **contributing to tone**.

## Key relationships

| Mark | Job | Quick example |
|---|---|---|
| Comma | Organises parts of a sentence; cannot join two independent clauses alone | "When the vote closed, the council met." |
| Colon | Points forward to an explanation, list or quotation after a complete clause | "Three groups suffered: staff, volunteers and students." |
| Semicolon | Joins related independent clauses; separates items in a complex list | "The notice was small; nobody saw it." |
| Dash | Emphatic break, interruption or addition | "Not for a week—for a year." |
| Hyphen | Joins words into a modifier; prevents ambiguity | "a well-known report" |
| Parentheses | Quiet supplementary information | "The fee (which is optional) rose." |
| Quotation marks | Words quoted; a word used in a special or ironic sense | "the so-called experts" |
| End marks | Close a sentence and set its tone | "?" engages; "!" sparingly |

## Errors that block meaning

1. **Comma splice**: two independent clauses with only a comma.
2. **Run-on**: two independent clauses with no punctuation.
3. **Fragment**: a dependent clause alone.
4. **Unclear pronoun reference.**
5. **Misused apostrophe** (its and it's; plural and possessive).
6. **Misplaced modifier** (see Topic 8.6).

## Public-domain anchors

- **Lincoln (1863)**: "we can not dedicate—we can not consecrate—we can not hallow—this ground." The dashes delay each verb and the object, making the climb audible.
- **Lincoln (1865)**: "Fondly do we hope—fervently do we pray—that this mighty scourge of war may speedily pass away." The dashes give a prayer-like rhythm.

## Worked mini-example

**Sentence (Marlbridge):** "The test had one rule: no talking. Break it—and you were out."

- The **colon** points forward, so the rule arrives as an announcement.
- The **full stop** gives the rule a moment of silence.
- The **dash** makes the reader pause before the consequence, so "and you were out" lands with force.

**Analysis sentence:** "The writer uses a dash to delay the consequence, so the reader experiences the same pause as a student about to be told off, which makes the rule feel strict."

**Editing check:** "The test had one rule, no talking, break it and you were out." contains two comma splices; the original punctuation fixes them and adds purpose.

## Mistakes to avoid

1. **Naming the mark without the effect** in an analysis.
2. **Using a colon after an incomplete clause** ("The groups were: ...").
3. **Treating a semicolon as a comma.**
4. **Overusing dashes or exclamation marks**, so that they stop standing out.
5. **Proofreading the source** when asked to explain it.
6. **Ignoring ambiguity**: a missing hyphen or comma can change the meaning.

## Quick self-check

1. Correct: "The vote was close, the council delayed." *(Use a semicolon, a full stop or "so": "The vote was close, so the council delayed.")*
2. Which mark points forward to an explanation? *(The colon.)*
3. What difference in effect is there between a dash and parentheses around an aside? *(A dash draws attention to the aside; parentheses play it down.)*
