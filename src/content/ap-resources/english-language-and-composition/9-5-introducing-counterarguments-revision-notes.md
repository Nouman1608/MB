## Recap

- **Transitions may be used to introduce counterarguments.** They tell the reader that an opposing view is coming and what the writer will do with it.
- A common sequence is **introduce, concede, pivot, respond, return** to the thesis.
- A counterargument can come in the **introduction, early, in the middle, late, within a single paragraph**, or in the **conclusion**; choose by what the reader is likely to be thinking.
- **Not all arguments explicitly address a counterargument.** In analysis, describe what is there and do not invent an opposing view.

## Key relationships

| Purpose | Transitions |
|---|---|
| Introduce an opposing view | Some argue that, critics claim, it might be objected that |
| Concede | Admittedly, granted, it is true that, of course |
| Pivot | However, yet, still, even so, nevertheless, on the other hand |
| Contrast with evidence | In fact, by contrast |
| Return to thesis | Therefore, for this reason, thus |

| Placement | Effect |
|---|---|
| Introduction | Shows awareness of the debate |
| Early body | Clears a strong objection before building the case |
| Middle | Follows a related point |
| Late | Builds the case, then answers the main objection |
| Within a paragraph | Concede, pivot, return briefly (timed essays) |

## Deciding what to include

1. **What objection would a careful, informed reader raise first?**
2. **Is it strong enough to need an explicit answer?**
3. **How much space can I afford?** (Timed essay: a paragraph or a few sentences.)
4. **Where does it fit best for this audience?**

## Public-domain anchors

- **Thomas Paine (1776)**: "Tyranny, like hell, is not easily conquered; yet we have this consolation with us, that the harder the conflict, the more glorious the triumph." The transition "yet" turns from a concession of difficulty to a claim that answers discouragement.
- **Abraham Lincoln, Gettysburg Address (1863)**: no explicit counterargument; the speech manages doubt implicitly through framing and structure. A good example that not all arguments address opposition explicitly.

## Worked mini-example

**Thesis (Marlbridge):** "Schools should teach touch typing."

"Some argue that handwriting is more important. **Admittedly**, handwriting helps young children to learn letter shapes. **However**, by secondary school, most pupils write essays on a keyboard, and slow typing costs them time in every exam. **Therefore**, typing deserves a place in the timetable alongside handwriting."

Sequence: introduce, concede, pivot, respond, return.

## Mistakes to avoid

1. **Inventing a strawman counterargument.**
2. **Using "however" for a contrast that is not an opposing view.**
3. **Spending too long on a minor objection** in a timed essay.
4. **Claiming a text refutes an opposing view when it does not mention one.**
5. **Placing the counterargument where it does not meet the reader's likely objection.**
6. **Using a hostile transition** ("some fools think") that loses the audience.

## Quick self-check

1. Name two transitions that introduce a counterargument. *(For example: "Some argue that", "It might be objected that", "Critics claim".)*
2. Where might a counterargument go for a hostile audience? *(Early, so that their concern is acknowledged before the case is built.)*
3. Can an effective argument avoid an explicit counterargument? *(Yes; not all arguments address one explicitly, and some manage opposition implicitly.)*
