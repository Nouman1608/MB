## Arguments arrive in a conversation that has already started

Imagine walking into a room where several people have been talking for an hour. If you start announcing your view straight away, you risk repeating what has been said, contradicting someone who has already answered you, or missing what the argument is actually about. A sensible newcomer listens first, works out who holds which position and why, and then speaks.

Written arguments work the same way. Almost every subject worth writing about is already being discussed in articles, speeches, reports and other texts. The final unit of this course builds on that idea. The course describes it plainly: **effectively entering into an ongoing conversation about a subject means engaging the positions that have already been considered and argued about.**

That sentence asks two things of you.

1. You must **know** the positions already on the table (listening).
2. You must **engage** them: show how your claim agrees with, disagrees with, qualifies or goes beyond them (speaking).

On the exam's synthesis question, the sources are the conversation. In a rhetorical analysis you also read how the writer places the text in a conversation. In the argument essay, you enter a conversation about a question that the prompt sets, and the more you show you understand the debate, the more convincing your own claim becomes.

## Step 1: map the conversation

Before drafting a claim, make a quick map. For each source or position ask:

- **What is the position?** (one sentence in your own words)
- **What is the reasoning and evidence?**
- **Who does it disagree with, and where exactly does the disagreement lie?** (values, facts, definitions, priorities)
- **What does it assume?**

A map often shows that the disagreement is narrower than it looks. Two writers may agree that a problem exists and differ only on whether the remedy should be a rule or a choice.

## Step 2: represent other positions fairly

If you distort another position, readers who know it will stop trusting you. To represent a view fairly:

- **State it so that its holder would recognise it.** Avoid a caricature that is easy to knock down.
- **Attribute it.** Name the source or the group: "Critics of the scheme argue that..." not "Some people say".
- **Use a signal verb that fits.** A source that *argues* is different from one that *assumes*, *admits*, *illustrates* or *overlooks*.
- **Quote or paraphrase accurately**, and keep the context.

Common signal verbs and what they tell the reader:

| Verb | Suggests that the source... |
|---|---|
| argues, claims, maintains | puts forward a position |
| illustrates, demonstrates, shows | gives evidence for a point |
| concedes, admits, acknowledges | grants something against its own side |
| assumes, takes for granted | relies on an idea without proving it |
| overlooks, ignores | omits something relevant (use only if true) |
| qualifies, complicates | limits or adds conditions to a claim |

## Step 3: place your own claim

Once you have the map, say how your claim relates. There are four main relationships.

| Relationship | Typical wording | Example (topic: school phones) |
|---|---|---|
| Agree and extend | "X is right that..., and the same logic shows..." | "Critics are right that phones distract; the same logic suggests limiting them in lessons." |
| Disagree | "X argues..., but this overlooks..." | "A total ban overlooks that many students use phones to access learning tools." |
| Qualify | "X is partly right: ... only when..." | "Allowing phones for learning helps, but only when teachers set clear limits." |
| Add something new | "The debate has focused on..., but has not considered..." | "Both sides discuss rules; few ask how pupils will learn to self-regulate." |

Your thesis should make clear which relationship you have, because that is what makes it a response and not just a statement.

## A public-domain example of entering a conversation

In 1848, Elizabeth Cady Stanton and others wrote the Declaration of Sentiments at Seneca Falls. It deliberately echoes the Declaration of Independence of 1776, which declares that "all men are created equal", by writing: "We hold these truths to be self-evident: that all men and women are created equal."

The sentence enters a conversation that was already known to every reader. It **takes up the founding text's words** and then **adds two words** ("and women") that expose what the original left out. By repeating the familiar form, the writers show that they accept the principle the audience already honours, and by changing it they claim that the principle has not been applied. The reader cannot easily reject the argument without rejecting the founding text, so the engagement is also a strategy of persuasion.

## Worked example 1: mapping and placing a claim

**Task.** Here are three source excerpts (written by Marlbridge) about whether towns should restrict holiday lets in order to keep homes available for residents.

- Source 1 (a residents' group): "Every holiday let removes a home from the market, pushing up rents for local people."
- Source 2 (a tourism board): "Holiday lets bring visitors who spend money in shops and cafés, supporting local jobs."
- Source 3 (a housing researcher): "Evidence from a few towns suggests that restrictions help most where tourism is a small part of the economy and homes are scarce."

Plan a thesis that enters the conversation.

**Step 1: map.**
- Source 1: position (restrict), reasoning (supply and price of homes), assumption (every holiday let removes a home that residents would otherwise rent).
- Source 2: position (don't restrict), reasoning (visitor spending), assumption (the spending benefits local people).
- Source 3: position (it depends), reasoning (local conditions), evidence (a few towns), a qualifying voice.

**Step 2: find the real disagreement.** Sources 1 and 2 disagree about **priority** (housing versus tourist income). Source 3 suggests the answer depends on **context**.

**Step 3: place the claim.** Choose "qualify and extend": *Towns should restrict holiday lets where housing is scarce and tourism is a small part of the economy, as the researcher suggests, but should avoid blanket bans that would remove the visitor spending on which other towns depend.*

**Step 4: check fairness.** Each source is represented accurately, and the thesis uses Source 3's evidence as a condition while crediting the concerns of Sources 1 and 2. The claim is arguable and addresses the actual disagreement.

## Worked example 2: how a writer enters a conversation

**Task.** Explain how the paragraph below, written by Marlbridge, enters a conversation.

> "Advocates of the four-day school week say it saves money and improves morale. They are right that districts with small budgets have saved on transport and heating. But the evidence they cite comes largely from rural districts, where journeys are long. In towns where most pupils walk, the savings are slight, and the cost, one fewer day of free school meals for families who depend on them, is real. The debate should therefore begin not with 'Does the four-day week work?' but with 'For whom?'"

**Step 1: identify the existing position.** Advocates say the four-day week saves money and improves morale.

**Step 2: how it is represented.** Fairly: it names the group ("Advocates"), states their two claims and **concedes** what is accurate ("They are right that...").

**Step 3: how the writer's claim relates.** It qualifies and redirects: the evidence comes mainly from rural districts, and in other settings the cost may outweigh the benefit. The writer adds something new (free school meals) that the advocates' evidence does not consider.

**Step 4: the final move.** The closing sentence reframes the debate by changing the question to "For whom?", which positions the writer as someone moving the conversation forward, not merely taking a side.

**Model sentence.** "The writer enters the conversation by fairly stating and partly conceding the advocates' claim, then redirects the debate to a question their evidence ignores."

## Common misconceptions

- **"My essay should start from scratch."** Starting from scratch ignores what has been argued and often repeats it.
- **"Summarising every source is the same as entering the conversation."** You must also respond to them.
- **"Only opposing views count."** Supporting and complementary positions matter too, and a conversation includes allies.
- **"I should use 'some people say' to refer to opponents."** Name the position and attribute it so that your engagement is specific.
- **"A fair summary weakens my argument."** A fair summary strengthens it, because readers trust a writer who understands the other side.
- **"There are only two sides."** Many conversations contain several positions, and your claim may sit between or beyond them.
