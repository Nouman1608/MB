## Recap

A **thesis** is the main, overarching claim a writer defends with reasoning and evidence. It is **not necessarily one sentence** and **not necessarily stated outright**. It may come first, last, or only by implication, and a thorough reading may be required. Part of the reading skill is describing **any indication the thesis gives of the argument's structure**.

## Key relationships

| Pattern | Where the thesis is | Clue |
|---|---|---|
| Thesis first | Early, then supported | "The council should..." |
| Thesis last | After evidence builds to it | Final sentence of the last paragraph |
| Implied | Never stated | Irony, satire, tone, the pattern of evidence |
| Across sentences | Claim plus qualification | Two or three linked sentences |

## What is not a thesis

| Look-alike | Why |
|---|---|
| Topic | Names a subject, takes no position |
| Fact or summary | States what happened |
| Sub-claim | Supports one paragraph only |
| Announcement | Says what the text will do |
| Counterargument | The view the writer will answer |
| Question | Raises the issue without answering |

## Finding the thesis

1. Read the introduction and conclusion.
2. Look for judgement words: should, must, cannot, the real problem is.
3. Summarise each paragraph; find the claim all summaries support.
4. Watch for the turn ("but", "yet", "however").
5. Check the title and last line.
6. For ironic texts, read twice and reverse the stated view.
7. Write the thesis in your own words and test it against every paragraph.

## Worked mini-example

Stanton, Declaration of Sentiments (1848): "We hold these truths to be self-evident: that all men and women are created equal." The addition "and women" to the opening of the Declaration of Independence is the argument in miniature, and the grievances and resolutions follow from it.

Swift, "A Modest Proposal" (1729): the narrator's literal proposal is not the thesis. The implied thesis, supported by the narrator's rejection of real reforms near the end, is that policies and attitudes already treat Ireland's poor as a resource, and genuine reform is needed.

Marlbridge Orchard Road passage: thesis last. "Orchard Road does not need fewer trees; it needs a council that counts what the trees give as carefully as it counts what they cost."

## Mistakes to avoid

- Assuming the thesis is always in the first paragraph.
- Choosing a fact, topic or sub-claim as the thesis.
- Taking an ironic narrator's words literally.
- Choosing the counterargument because it is stated most clearly.
- Expecting a single sentence.
- Not testing the thesis against every paragraph.

## Quick self-check

1. Can a thesis be implied? **Yes: you may need a thorough reading to infer it.**
2. Is "The council removed fifteen trees" a thesis? **No, it is a fact.**
3. What test shows that a sentence is only a sub-claim? **It supports one paragraph, not the whole argument.**
4. Why read an ironic text twice? **The narrator's stated view may be the opposite of the writer's thesis.**
5. Where might a thesis appear besides the introduction? **At the end, after reasoning builds to it, or across several sentences.**
