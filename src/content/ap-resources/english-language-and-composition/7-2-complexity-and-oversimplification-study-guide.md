## Why simple claims break

A claim such as "Social media is making teenagers lonely" is easy to say and easy to remember. It is also easy to knock down. Which teenagers? On which platforms? Compared with what? Is it the platform or the hours spent? Is it loneliness, or is it a different feeling that survey questions label as loneliness?

A writer who cannot answer these questions has not yet understood the subject well enough. That is the first idea of this topic: **a lack of understanding of the complexities of a subject leads to oversimplification and generalisation.** The writer sees one cause, one effect and one group, and reports them as if there were nothing else.

There is a second idea. **Arguments rarely arrive in an empty room.** Almost every subject worth arguing about already has a conversation around it: earlier writers, rival positions, evidence that points in different directions. An argument that ignores that conversation will be corrected by it. So effective arguments **avoid expressing claims, reasoning and evidence in absolute terms**. They stay open to the facts that do not fit.

None of this means you cannot take a firm position. It means that firmness should come from precision, not from volume.

## How oversimplification looks on the page

Learn to spot these signals in other writers' arguments and in your own drafts.

| Signal | Example | What goes wrong |
|---|---|---|
| Absolute words | "Everyone knows...", "never", "always", "all", "none", "impossible" | One counterexample refutes the claim |
| Hasty generalisation | "My cousin quit the team, so team sports burn kids out" | A small sample is stretched to a whole population |
| Single cause | "Crime rose because of video games" | Other possible causes are ignored |
| False dichotomy | "Either we ban phones or we accept failing grades" | Middle positions are hidden |
| Loaded labels | "Those people" | A varied group is flattened into one |
| Unqualified comparison | "Public transport is better than cars" | Better for whom, on what measure, where? |

These are not just faults of logic; they are faults of **credibility**. An audience that spots a sweeping claim starts to distrust the writer's other claims as well.

## What complexity looks like

A complex claim is one that has understood its own limits. It usually does one or more of these things:

- It **names its scope**: "in cities with frequent services", "among adults under thirty".
- It **names its conditions**: "when the budget is not cut", "if schools provide supervised study time".
- It **separates different cases** that the simple claim lumps together.
- It **admits the strongest competing evidence** and explains how the claim survives or changes.
- It **uses degree rather than all-or-nothing**: "often", "usually", "in many cases", "increasingly".

Compare the two versions of one claim.

| Simple | Complex |
|---|---|
| "Homework is useless." | "For primary-age pupils, homework appears to add little to achievement, though regular practice may help older students, particularly when it is brief and well-targeted." |

The complex version is longer, but it is also **more defensible and more informative**. It tells the reader where the writer thinks the evidence points and for whom.

## Evidence has three possible relationships to a claim

By the end of Unit 7 you should be able to sort a new piece of evidence into one of three groups, quickly.

1. **It supports the claim.** It fits the claim as it stands. Use it.
2. **It refutes the claim.** It shows that the claim, as worded, is wrong. Change or drop the claim.
3. **It complicates (qualifies) the claim.** It does not destroy the claim, but it shows the claim is true only in some conditions or for some cases. Narrow, condition or restate the claim.

Many students treat the third kind as if it were the second and throw away a good claim, or treat it as the first and ignore the complication. The skill is in the third box.

## A public-domain example of respect for complexity

John Stuart Mill, in *On Liberty* (1859), wrote: "He who knows only his own side of the case, knows little of that." The sentence is an argument against oversimplification itself. Mill's claim is that you cannot even understand your own position fully until you understand the opposing one. Notice how the sentence is built: a short, balanced statement with the word "little" instead of "nothing". Mill does not exaggerate. He says the person knows *little*, which is exact, defensible and still sharp.

## Worked example 1: revising a claim in the light of new evidence

**Task.** A student wrote this thesis in a draft: "Cities that ban cars from their centres always get busier shops." Three pieces of evidence appear in the sources, written by Marlbridge for this example:

- Source A: In one mid-sized city, retail takings rose by 9 per cent in the year after the centre was closed to cars.
- Source B: In a second city, shops on the pedestrianised street reported a fall in takings in the first six months, followed by recovery in the second year.
- Source C: A survey of shops in a third city found that those selling bulky goods lost customers, while cafés and clothing shops gained.

**Step 1: sort the evidence.**
Source A **supports** the claim. Source B **complicates** it: the effect is not immediate. Source C **complicates** it: the effect varies by type of shop. None of them proves "always".

**Step 2: find the weak point in the wording.** The claim contains the absolute "always" and the vague "busier shops". Two of three sources show exceptions.

**Step 3: revise.** The revised claim should keep the position (pedestrianisation can benefit centres) and add scope and conditions: "Banning cars from a city centre can raise trade for shops that depend on foot traffic, although the benefit may take more than a year to appear and shops selling bulky goods may lose out."

**Step 4: check.** The new claim is still arguable (a reader could disagree), is supported by all three sources, and does not depend on an absolute. It is longer but also more informative.

## Worked example 2: how a writer avoids oversimplification

**Task.** Read this paragraph, written by Marlbridge, and explain how the writer shows an understanding of complexity.

> "It is tempting to say that electric cars solve the problem of transport emissions. The tailpipe figure supports the claim: an electric car emits no exhaust. But the claim shrinks the problem. The emissions of an electric car depend on how its electricity is generated, and the manufacture of its battery is carbon-intensive. For a driver on a grid dominated by coal, the gains may be modest. For one on a grid that is largely hydro or wind, they are substantial. The more accurate claim is that electric cars can cut emissions, and that how much they cut depends on where the electricity comes from."

**Step 1: find the simple claim being rejected.** "Electric cars solve the problem of transport emissions." The word "solve" is an absolute.

**Step 2: find the evidence and how it is used.** The writer concedes the evidence that supports the simple claim (the tailpipe) before showing why it is incomplete. That is fairness, and it builds credibility.

**Step 3: find the qualifying moves.** The writer uses conditions ("depend on how its electricity is generated"), cases ("a grid dominated by coal... a grid that is largely hydro or wind") and verbs of degree ("can cut", "modest", "substantial").

**Step 4: state the effect.** The paragraph lands on a claim that is more precise than the one it began with. The audience is more likely to trust a writer who has weighed the objection than one who has ignored it.

## Writing the revised claim without going vague

A common failure is to over-correct. "Cars may sometimes have some effect on emissions in certain situations" is not complexity; it is mush. Three tests keep a revised claim useful:

1. **Can someone disagree with it?** If not, it says too little.
2. **Does it say how much, for whom or under what conditions?** If not, add one of these.
3. **Does it still answer the question the task asks?** If not, you have strayed.

A useful pattern is: *position + scope + condition*. "Four-day school weeks can improve staff retention (position) in rural districts with long travel distances (scope), provided pupils' instructional hours are protected (condition)."

## Common misconceptions

- **"Complex means long."** Complexity is accuracy about limits, not word count. A short sentence can be complex ("He knows little of that").
- **"Qualifying a claim weakens it."** A qualified claim is harder to refute, so it is stronger.
- **"Any counterexample destroys an argument."** Only against an absolute. Against a qualified claim, a counterexample may simply fall outside the stated scope.
- **"If the evidence complicates my claim, I should change my position."** Often you should change the scope. Keep the position if the evidence still supports its core.
- **"Hedge words are the only way to be complex."** Scope, conditions and separating cases do more work than "perhaps".
- **"The exam wants balance, so I should be neutral."** The exam wants a defensible position that engages other views, not neutrality.
