## Recap

- **Diction** is the choice of words; **syntax** is the arrangement of words into phrases, clauses and sentences.
- A writer's choices of syntax and diction **influence how the writer is perceived** and may influence **how far the audience accepts the argument**.
- **Word choice may reflect a writer's biases** and can affect **credibility** with a particular audience.
- Credibility is relative: the same word may persuade one audience and alienate another.

## Key relationships

| Choice | What to analyse | Possible effect on trust |
|---|---|---|
| Connotation | Attitude carried by a word ("thrifty" vs "stingy") | Reveals approval or disapproval; can look fair or biased |
| Register | Formal, informal, technical, slang | Signals respect, expertise or familiarity |
| Precision | Exact figures and terms vs vague inflation | Exact words build trust |
| Loaded terms | Judgement smuggled into description | Win allies, lose the undecided |
| Pronouns | "we", "you", "they" | Include or divide the audience |
| Sentence length | Long (reasoning) vs short (force) | Measured or urgent |
| Parallelism and balance | Repeated shapes | Rhythm, fairness or contrast |

## Reading bias in word choice

1. **Find the loaded words** and swap in neutral ones.
2. **Ask what the original adds**: a judgement, an emotion, an insult?
3. **Decide who would accept it**: allies, the undecided or opponents?
4. **State the effect** on credibility for that audience.

## Public-domain anchors

- **Frederick Douglass (1852)**: "This Fourth [of] July is yours, not mine. You may rejoice, I must mourn." Short balanced sentences, a pronoun divide and a contrast between "may" and "must".
- **Abraham Lincoln (1863)**: "Four score and seven years ago" uses an elevated, scriptural register to give the occasion solemnity.

## Worked mini-example

**Marlbridge sentences:** (1) "The council has delayed the repair for two years." (2) "The lazy council has dithered for two long, wasted years."

(1) is neutral and precise. (2) uses loaded words ("lazy", "dithered", "wasted"). For a neutral reader, (2) may sound like an attack and lower trust; for people angry at the council, it may sound like a shared feeling. **Analysis sentence:** "By choosing the loaded verb 'dithered', the writer reveals a hostile attitude, which risks alienating readers who have not yet judged the council."

## Mistakes to avoid

1. **Describing diction as "strong" or "powerful"** without saying what it connotes.
2. **Ignoring syntax** and discussing only vocabulary.
3. **Treating bias as always bad**: link it to the audience.
4. **Assuming formal means credible.**
5. **Leaving out the audience** when evaluating credibility.
6. **Quoting a phrase without analysing it.**

## Quick self-check

1. What is the difference between denotation and connotation? *(Denotation is the literal meaning; connotation is the attitude or feeling the word carries.)*
2. Why might the same loaded word help credibility with one audience and harm it with another? *(Credibility depends on whether the audience shares the attitude the word reveals.)*
3. What impression does a series of short, plain sentences tend to create? *(Directness, force or finality; in the right setting, honesty and authority.)*
