## Recap

- A **clause** has a subject and a predicate. An **independent clause** can stand alone; a **dependent clause** cannot. Every sentence needs at least one independent clause.
- A **phrase** has no subject-and-verb pair of its own.
- **Arrangement** of clauses, phrases and words emphasises ideas. The end of a sentence is usually the most emphatic position; the beginning is next.
- Sentences are also arranged in paragraphs: a short sentence after a long one can spotlight an idea.
- The course asks you to **read and analyse** these structures. You are not required to write complex sentences in timed essays, but you must write clearly.

## Key relationships

| Term | Description | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Simple | One independent clause | Direct, plain |
| Compound | Two or more independent clauses | Equal weight, or a sequence |
| Complex | Independent plus dependent clause(s) | Shows reasons, conditions, contrasts |
| Compound-complex | Two or more independent plus dependent | Dense, can hold nuance |
| Loose (cumulative) | Main clause first, then detail | Natural, conversational |
| Periodic | Main clause held back to the end | Suspense, weight on the last idea |
| Balanced | Two matching parts | Equality or contrast |

## How to analyse a sentence

1. **Find the verbs** and their subjects to mark the clauses.
2. **Spot the main clause**: where is it, first or last?
3. **Identify the idea in the end position.**
4. **Link to purpose**: why does the writer want that idea emphasised?
5. **Write one sentence** naming the structure, the emphasis and the effect.

## Public-domain anchors

- The **Declaration of Independence** (1776) opens with a periodic sentence: its main clause ("a decent respect to the opinions of mankind requires...") comes last.
- **Lincoln's Gettysburg Address** (1863) ends a sentence on "what they did here", placing the soldiers' actions in the strongest position.

## Worked mini-example

**Sentence (Marlbridge):** "Although the report was clear, although the warnings were repeated, the board did nothing."

1. **Clauses:** two dependent "although" clauses and one independent clause ("the board did nothing").
2. **Type:** complex.
3. **Arrangement:** periodic; the main clause is held back to the end.
4. **Emphasis:** the board's inaction, in the strongest position, after two conceded facts that make it harder to excuse.
5. **Model analysis sentence:** "By delaying the main clause until after two concessions, the writer builds expectation and then ends on the board's inaction, which makes the failure seem all the more inexcusable."

## Mistakes to avoid

1. **Labelling without explaining**: "This is a complex sentence" is not analysis.
2. **Equating length with sophistication.**
3. **Forgetting the end position** when asked about emphasis.
4. **Mistaking a phrase for a clause**: "after the vote" has no subject and verb.
5. **A comma splice**: two independent clauses joined with only a comma.
6. **Rearranging without a purpose**: always say which idea you want to stress.

## Quick self-check

1. Is "Because it rained, the match was cancelled" simple, compound, complex or compound-complex? *(Complex: one dependent clause and one independent clause.)*
2. Which idea does the sentence "The vote was close, but the council had never been so united" put in the strongest position? *(That the council was united.)*
3. What is the main effect of a periodic sentence? *(It delays the main idea to the end, building suspense and weight.)*
