## Conventions exist so that readers can follow you

Grammar is the system of rules for how words combine into sentences. **Mechanics** covers spelling, capitalisation and punctuation. When a writer follows the **established conventions** of both, the reader's attention goes to the ideas. When the conventions break down, the reader's attention goes to decoding, and the argument loses force.

The course is clear about proportion. It is not a grammar course; students are expected to be able to write complete sentences before they begin. Small slips of the kind that appear in an unrevised timed essay do not lower a score. What hurts is a pattern of errors so serious that the meaning is no longer clear. So your aims are:

1. **Write clearly and conventionally**, so that nothing gets in the way.
2. **Use punctuation purposefully**, so that it helps show how your ideas relate.
3. **Read other writers' punctuation as a choice** and be able to say what it does.

## Errors that interfere with meaning

These are the errors most likely to make a reader stumble.

| Error | Example | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Comma splice | "The vote was close, the council delayed the decision." | Use a semicolon, a full stop, or a conjunction: "The vote was close, so the council delayed the decision." |
| Run-on (fused sentence) | "The vote was close the council delayed the decision." | Add punctuation or a conjunction |
| Fragment | "Because the vote was close." | Attach it to a main clause |
| Misused apostrophe | "The councils decision", "the vote's were close" | "The council's decision"; "the votes were close" |
| Unclear pronoun | "The mayor told the clerk she was late." | Name the person: "The mayor told the clerk that the clerk was late." |
| Misplaced modifier | "Covered in dust, the clerk found the file." | "The clerk found the file, covered in dust." (see Topic 8.6) |

Most of these produce **ambiguity** or **confusion**, which is why they matter more than a stray spelling slip.

## What punctuation can do

Punctuation is not decoration. According to the course, punctuation advances a writer's purpose by **clarifying, organising, emphasising, indicating purpose, supplementing information** or **contributing to tone**. Different marks do different jobs.

| Mark | Common uses | A purposeful effect |
|---|---|---|
| Comma | Separates items, introductory phrases, non-essential elements; joins clauses with a conjunction | Organises a sentence so the reader can see its parts; slows the pace |
| Colon | Introduces a list, explanation, example or quotation | Points forward: "here is what I mean" |
| Semicolon | Joins related independent clauses; separates items in complex lists | Sets two ideas side by side as equals and invites the reader to link them |
| Dash | Marks a break, an interruption or an emphatic addition | Emphasis, surprise or a change of direction |
| Hyphen | Joins words into one modifier or compound ("well-known report") | Clarifies meaning (a man-eating shark and a man eating shark are different) |
| Parentheses | Add supplementary information | Quiet aside; lowers the volume of the added material |
| Quotation marks | Mark someone's words, or a word used in a special sense | Attributes; can signal irony or distance ("so-called experts") |
| End marks (. ? !) | Close sentences | Full stop: calm, finality; question: engages; exclamation: strong feeling (use sparingly) |

## Public-domain examples

**Dashes for emphasis: Lincoln, Gettysburg Address (1863).** "But, in a larger sense, we can not dedicate—we can not consecrate—we can not hallow—this ground."

Each dash interrupts the sentence, so that the reader meets three verbs (*dedicate, consecrate, hallow*) one at a time, with a pause after each. The verbs climb in solemnity, and the dashes make that climb audible. The last dash delays the object, "this ground", so the whole sentence arrives at the place the audience is standing on.

**Dashes for a plea: Lincoln, Second Inaugural Address (1865).** "Fondly do we hope—fervently do we pray—that this mighty scourge of war may speedily pass away."

The two dashes set apart two parallel clauses ("fondly do we hope", "fervently do we pray") from the main idea. The pauses give the sentence the rhythm of a prayer and make the hope sound sincere before the sentence completes itself. A pair of commas would have made the interruption sound like a routine aside.

## Worked example 1: explaining a punctuation choice

**Task.** Explain how the punctuation in the sentence below, written by Marlbridge, contributes to its effect.

> "The committee made its decision: the bridge would close. Not for a week—for a year."

**Step 1: identify the marks.** A colon after "decision"; a full stop; a dash in the final sentence; and a short fragment ("Not for a week") before it.

**Step 2: say what each mark does.**
- The **colon** points forward and promises information. The reader expects the decision, so the announcement "the bridge would close" lands with weight.
- The full stop after "close" **separates** the announcement from the correction that follows, giving the bad news a moment of silence.
- The **dash** produces a sharp turn: "Not for a week—for a year." The pause after "week" creates a false expectation of something modest, and what follows overturns it.

**Step 3: link to purpose.** The writer wants the audience to feel the shock of the longer closure. The punctuation controls timing, so that the reader experiences the shock in the order the writer intends.

## Worked example 2: editing for clarity

**Task.** Edit this paragraph, written by Marlbridge, so that errors that interfere with meaning are removed. Explain each change.

> "The librarys new opening hours began on Monday, many students did not know. A notice went up outside the door it was small and easy to miss. Three groups were affected: the early shift staff, the evening volunteers and the students who needed a quiet place to study, they were worst hit."

**Step 1: find the errors.**
1. "librarys" needs an apostrophe: **library's**.
2. "Monday, many students did not know" is a **comma splice**.
3. "outside the door it was small" is a **run-on**.
4. "the early shift staff, the evening volunteers and the students who needed a quiet place to study, they were worst hit" attaches a new independent clause with a comma, another **comma splice**; also "early shift staff" is a stacked modifier that reads better as "early-shift staff".

**Step 2: correct.**

"The library's new opening hours began on Monday, but many students did not know. A notice went up outside the door; it was small and easy to miss. Three groups were affected: the early-shift staff, the evening volunteers and the students who needed a quiet place to study. The students were worst hit."

**Step 3: explain.**
- A conjunction ("but") makes the contrast explicit instead of leaving the reader to guess.
- The semicolon joins the two related clauses about the notice.
- The colon before the list is correct because it follows a complete clause ("Three groups were affected").
- Ending the list with a full stop and starting a new sentence removes the comma splice and gives "worst hit" a sentence of its own, which is a stronger position.

**Check.** The meaning is unchanged, but the reader no longer has to work out where one idea ends and the next begins.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Punctuation is just rules."** Rules enable clarity, but choices among marks also shape pace, emphasis and tone.
- **"A comma is a safe all-purpose mark."** A comma cannot join two independent clauses by itself.
- **"A semicolon is a fancy comma."** It joins two independent clauses or separates items in a complex list, and it links ideas as equals.
- **"A colon can follow any word."** A colon normally follows a complete clause ("Three groups were affected: ..."). Writing "The groups were: the staff..." is a common error.
- **"Dashes and parentheses are interchangeable."** A dash raises the volume of the added material; parentheses lower it.
- **"Exclamation marks show enthusiasm, so more is better."** Overuse makes writing sound breathless; one in an essay is usually enough.
- **"On the exam I must correct every error in a source."** The tasks ask you to explain what punctuation does, not to proofread.
