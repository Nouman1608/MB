Read the big ideas and the method table, then try the six questions without notes. Each question combines two or more Unit 8 topics.

These are **original Marlbridge practice questions**, not past exam questions. All passages are written by Marlbridge, and the people, places and details are invented. The rubrics are a **suggested Marlbridge rubric**, not official scoring.

## Big ideas of the unit

- **Comparisons relate an argument to an audience** only when the audience shares and understands the familiar side; they transfer a feeling, structure or value and hide differences ([8.1](/advanced-course-resources/english-language-and-composition/8-1-comparisons-for-an-audience-study-guide/)).
- **Diction and syntax shape credibility.** Word choice can reveal bias, and its effect depends on the audience ([8.2](/advanced-course-resources/english-language-and-composition/8-2-diction-syntax-and-credibility-study-guide/)).
- **Audiences are unique and dynamic.** Writers fit evidence, organisation and language to perspective, context and needs ([8.3](/advanced-course-resources/english-language-and-composition/8-3-choices-for-a-particular-audience-study-guide/)).
- **Style is the mix of word choice, syntax and conventions**, and it produces tone ([8.4](/advanced-course-resources/english-language-and-composition/8-4-style-study-guide/)).
- **Irony emerges from a gap** between an argument and readers' expectations or values, signalled by style ([8.5](/advanced-course-resources/english-language-and-composition/8-5-irony-and-complex-perspectives-study-guide/)).
- **Modifiers and parenthetical elements** qualify, clarify and add information; place modifiers close to their targets and use asides for a purpose ([8.6](/advanced-course-resources/english-language-and-composition/8-6-modifiers-and-parenthetical-elements-study-guide/)).

## Key relationships and methods

| If the task asks you to... | Do this | Watch out for... |
|---|---|---|
| Analyse a comparison | Name both sides, what is transferred, what is hidden, why this audience understands it | Naming the device only |
| Judge diction and syntax | Pick out connotation, register, sentence structure; say what impression and trust result | Calling words "powerful" |
| Explain choices for an audience | Link evidence, organisation and language to perspective, context and needs | Treating audience as tone only |
| Describe style | Label + evidence + effect | Confusing style with tone |
| Read irony | Surface meaning, signals, intended meaning, purpose | Taking the surface meaning as the writer's view |
| Repair or analyse modifiers | Place beside the target; check commas for essential and non-essential; say what an aside adds | Dangling modifiers; unpunctuated asides |

## Question 1 (multiple choice · 8.5, 8.2)

Use this letter (written by Marlbridge) for Questions 1 to 4.

> "Dear Editor,
>
> I write, as a grateful resident, to thank the Parks Department for its bold new policy of cutting the grass at six every morning. Nothing says 'community' like a mower under the window while the rest of us are still asleep (and, frankly, who needs sleep?). The department tells us that the early start 'minimises disruption to daytime users of the park'—by which it means, I suppose, that disruption has been moved to the people who live beside it. Like a baker who sells all the bread before the shop opens, the department has found an ingenious way to serve everyone except those who ask. I look forward to the autumn, when the leaf blowers will, no doubt, arrive at five."

What is the writer's intended meaning?

- (A) Sincere thanks for the early mowing
- (B) A criticism of the early mowing, signalled by mock-gratitude and exaggeration
- (C) A neutral report of the department's schedule
- (D) A request for a new bakery

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (B).** Signals include "grateful" and "bold new policy" applied to something annoying, "Nothing says 'community' like a mower under the window", and the ironic hope of leaf blowers "at five". The surface praise clashes with readers' values (a quiet morning).

- (A) takes the surface meaning literally.
- (C) ignores the tone and signals.
- (D) The baker is only a comparison.

Topics: 8.5 (irony), 8.2 (word choice).
</details>

## Question 2 (multiple choice · 8.1)

What does the comparison "Like a baker who sells all the bread before the shop opens" transfer to the department's behaviour?

- (A) The idea that the department is very successful
- (B) The idea that the department serves people who are not the ones who are meant to benefit, so it seems absurd
- (C) The idea that bread is cheaper than grass
- (D) The idea that bakers work early

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (B).** The baker's habit would leave real customers with nothing, and the comparison transfers that absurdity to a department whose early start inconveniences residents.

- (A) misreads the ironic framing.
- (C) is irrelevant to the argument.
- (D) is a detail, not the point of the comparison.

Topics: 8.1 (what a comparison transfers).
</details>

## Question 3 (multiple choice · 8.6, 8.5)

What does the parenthetical "(and, frankly, who needs sleep?)" add?

- (A) Essential information needed to identify the mower
- (B) A quiet, sarcastic aside that adds personal voice and strengthens the irony
- (C) A statistic
- (D) A definition

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (B).** The parentheses lower the volume while the rhetorical question exaggerates the point, so the aside adds voice and irony without being essential.

- (A) The sentence would make sense without it.
- (C) There is no figure.
- (D) It defines nothing.

Topics: 8.6 (parenthetical elements), 8.5 (irony).
</details>

## Question 4 (multiple choice · 8.4, 8.2)

Which label best describes the style of the letter?

- (A) Plain and literal, with short sentences
- (B) Polite and formal in diction, with an ironic, mock-grateful tone
- (C) Ornate and mournful
- (D) Technical and abstract

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (B).** The register ("I write, as a grateful resident, to thank...") is courteous and formal, but the content is critical, so the style produces ironic, mock-grateful tone.

- (A) The sentences are not short or plain in this way.
- (C) The tone is not mournful.
- (D) There is no technical vocabulary.

Topics: 8.4 (style and tone), 8.2 (register).
</details>

## Question 5 (constructed response · 8.3, 8.5)

Explain how the writer's choices in the letter suit the intended audience of local newspaper readers, and identify one risk of using irony with this audience. (6 marks)

<details>
<summary>Sample answer and suggested rubric</summary>

**Sample.** Local readers are likely to share the experience of early-morning noise and the value of quiet, so the mock-gratitude invites them to recognise the complaint without being told. The evidence is concrete and local (a mower under the window at six, leaf blowers at five), and the department's own phrase "minimises disruption" is quoted and then undercut, which uses the department's words against it. The baker comparison is simple and familiar to all readers. The tone is courteous, so the letter does not seem abusive, which keeps wavering readers on side. The risk is that readers who miss the signals, or who are not sympathetic, might take the thanks literally, or the Parks Department might dismiss the letter as sarcasm rather than answer the substance.

| Mark | What earns it |
|---|---|
| 1 | Identifies a shared experience or value of the audience |
| 1 | Explains how irony relies on it |
| 1 | Identifies an evidence or language choice suited to local readers |
| 1 | Explains its effect |
| 1 | Identifies a risk of irony (literal misreading or dismissal) |
| 1 | Bases the answer on the text and the audience |
</details>

## Question 6 (constructed response · 8.3, 8.4, 8.6)

Rewrite the argument for **the director of the Parks Department**, who is currently neutral and open to evidence, in a direct, respectful style (four to five sentences). Include one correctly placed modifier and one parenthetical element, and explain **two** differences from the original letter. (8 marks)

<details>
<summary>Sample answer and suggested rubric</summary>

**Sample.** "Dear Director, I live beside the park, and since the new schedule began, the mower has started at six every morning. Residents on Orchard Lane (about sixty homes) report being woken before their alarms, and three have contacted the council. I understand that the earlier start is meant to reduce disruption to park users, but the noise now falls on those nearest the park. Would the department consider starting at eight on weekdays, as it did last year, and publish the results of a trial?"

**Differences.** (1) The audience is an open-minded official, so the new version states the evidence plainly and proposes a solution instead of using irony, which this audience might not read as a serious argument. (2) The tone is respectful and the register is formal, with a question that invites cooperation; the original used mock-praise, which suits newspaper readers but could provoke defensiveness.

| Mark | What earns it |
|---|---|
| 1 | Keeps the same underlying complaint |
| 1 | Uses direct, respectful diction suitable for the director |
| 1 | Includes concrete, checkable evidence |
| 1 | Offers a proposal or question that invites cooperation |
| 1 | A modifier is placed next to what it modifies |
| 1 | A parenthetical element is correctly punctuated and adds information |
| 1 | Explains one difference tied to the audience |
| 1 | Explains a second difference |
</details>
