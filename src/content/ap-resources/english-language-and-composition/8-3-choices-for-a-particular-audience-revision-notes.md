## Recap

- Audiences are **unique** (different groups respond differently) and **dynamic** (they change over time and within a text).
- Writers must consider the audience's **perspectives, contexts and needs** when choosing **evidence, organisation and language**.
- The same claim may need different evidence, a different order and a different register for different audiences.
- Understanding an audience means anticipating concerns and meeting them honestly, not flattering them.

## Key relationships

| Audience stance | Strategy |
|---|---|
| Hostile | Start with common ground; show you understand their concern; use evidence they trust; concede early; avoid insult |
| Neutral or undecided | Give context and clear reasons; show fairness; deal with other views |
| Supportive | Reinforce shared values; move them to action; give practical next steps |

| Choice | Adjust it by asking |
|---|---|
| Evidence | Which kind (data, expert, personal story, shared text) does this audience trust? |
| Organisation | Where should the claim, concession and strongest point go for these readers? |
| Language | What register, vocabulary and comparisons will they understand and respect? |

## Audience checklist

1. **Perspective**: beliefs, values, stance toward the claim.
2. **Context**: when, where and in what medium they meet the text; recent events.
3. **Needs**: what they must know, decide or do; what worries they bring.
4. **Choices**: evidence, organisation, language.

## Public-domain anchor

**Susan B. Anthony (1873)**: "It was we, the people; not we, the white male citizens; nor yet we, the male citizens; but we, the whole people, who formed the Union." She uses a text her audience knows (the Constitution's opening words) and a step-by-step syntax of negatives to widen the meaning of "the people", which suits an audience that included potential jurors.

## Worked mini-example

**Marlbridge claim:** "Pupils should have a say in the timetable."

- To **teachers** (worried about workload): evidence from schools that ran a pilot, a plan limited to one decision, and calm language.
- To **pupils** (supportive): a simple way to vote, and an energetic, direct tone.

**Analysis sentence:** "By proposing a limited pilot and citing other schools, the writer answers teachers' likely worry about workload, which shows an awareness of their needs."

## Reading the audience from the text

In an exam passage you cannot ask the audience who they are, so read the clues.

- **Address and setting**: a title, salutation or occasion ("Fellow citizens", "To the board") shows who is being addressed.
- **Assumed knowledge**: what the writer explains and what the writer leaves unexplained shows what the audience is expected to know.
- **Values appealed to**: safety, fairness, thrift or tradition tell you what the writer thinks the audience cares about.
- **Anticipated objections**: a concession or an answered doubt shows where the audience is expected to resist.

State only what the clues support, and quote the words that support it.

## Mistakes to avoid

1. **Saying "the writer knows the audience" without a specific choice.**
2. **Treating audience as tone only.**
3. **Inventing facts about the audience** that the text does not support.
4. **Assuming all readers are alike.**
5. **Flattering instead of persuading.**
6. **Forgetting that audiences change** as an argument unfolds.

## Quick self-check

1. Name the three audience features to consider. *(Perspectives, contexts and needs.)*
2. Which strategy suits a hostile audience first? *(Common ground, then concession, then strong evidence they trust.)*
3. Why might the same evidence suit one audience and not another? *(Each audience trusts different kinds of evidence and has different needs.)*
