## Recap

**Evidence** is what a writer offers to support a claim. The CED lists facts, statistics, examples, details, anecdotes, personal observations and experiences, testimonies, expert opinions, experiments, analogies and illustrations. Evidence is not a claim: you can check it or it illustrates, while a claim argues a position. Useful evidence is relevant, credible, representative and suited to the audience. Evidence supplies the material; reasoning and commentary explain why it supports the claim.

## Key relationships

| Type | Signal | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fact | Verifiable statement | Hard to dispute | May be too general |
| Statistic | Number summarising many cases | Shows scale | Misleading if source or comparison is unclear |
| Example / detail | One specific case | Concrete | May not be typical |
| Anecdote | Short story | Human | Personal and unrepresentative |
| Testimony | A witness's statement | First-hand | Possible bias or memory error |
| Expert opinion | A qualified authority | Authority on technical points | Only within the expert's field |
| Experiment / study | Test or research | Controlled | Depends on method |
| Analogy | A comparison | Clarifies the unfamiliar | Never exact |

## Four questions to test evidence

1. Is it **relevant** to exactly what the claim says?
2. Is the source **credible** and in a position to know?
3. Is it **representative**, not a rare case?
4. Does it **suit the audience** and purpose?

## Worked mini-example

Claim: "A four-day school week would not reduce learning." Best evidence: a study of several schools that measured test scores for two years and found them unchanged, because it measures the outcome named in the claim. Weaker: "many people like long weekends" (not about learning), a single pupil's opinion (personal, unrepresentative), and "four-day weeks are used in rural areas" (shows practice, not results).

Stanton, Declaration of Sentiments (1848): a catalogue of specific restrictions ("He has never permitted her to exercise her inalienable right to the elective franchise"). The evidence is a list of particular facts whose weight comes from accumulation and from imitating the form of the Declaration of Independence.

## Reading tips

- Identify the claim first; then ask which sentences the claim depends on.
- Decide whether each supporting sentence is checkable (fact, statistic) or illustrative (anecdote, analogy).
- When asked what evidence "adds", say what it does for the claim: shows scale, makes it concrete, adds authority, clarifies by comparison.

## Mistakes to avoid

- Calling a statement of position evidence.
- Calling any quotation evidence without checking relevance.
- Assuming statistics are always stronger than stories.
- Using evidence that proves something other than the claim.
- Leaving evidence without commentary.

## Quick self-check

1. Is "The ferry is failing the island" evidence or a claim? **A claim.**
2. What type of evidence is "Late sailings rose from 12 to 68 percent"? **A statistic.**
3. What is a weakness of an anecdote? **It is personal and may not be representative.**
4. Which test question checks whether evidence matches a claim? **Is it relevant to exactly what the claim says?**
5. What is an analogy for? **To clarify an unfamiliar idea by comparing it with a familiar one.**
