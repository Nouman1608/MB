## Three relationships between evidence and a thesis

Every piece of evidence you meet while building an argument has a relationship with your thesis. The course states this directly: **evidence and sources will either support, complement or contradict a writer's thesis.** Knowing which relationship you are dealing with tells you what to do with it.

| Relationship | What it does | How a writer typically uses it |
|---|---|---|
| **Supports** | Bears directly on the claim and makes it more likely to be true | Offered as proof, with commentary that links it to the thesis |
| **Complements** | Adds context, a related example or another dimension that extends or deepens the claim without proving it directly | Used to widen the argument, to show significance or to add a new angle |
| **Contradicts** | Cuts against the claim, or part of it | Acknowledged and answered by qualifying the claim, conceding a point, rebutting or refuting |

You will meet this classification in three places: when you choose and arrange your own evidence, when you analyse how a writer uses sources, and when you read a set of sources for the synthesis question.

## Seeing the relationships at work

Take the thesis: **"Free public libraries improve a town's educational outcomes."** (Evidence below was written by Marlbridge and is invented for practice.)

| Evidence | Relationship | Why |
|---|---|---|
| "Pupils in towns with a library within a mile scored on average five points higher on reading tests than comparable pupils elsewhere." | Supports | Directly links libraries to educational outcomes |
| "Libraries also run adult literacy classes and job-search sessions, with over 2,000 attendances last year." | Complements | Widens the argument to adult education; does not prove the effect on school pupils |
| "A review found that, once family income was taken into account, the effect of library access on pupils' scores was small." | Contradicts (in part) | Qualifies the strength of the claim: the effect may be mostly due to family income |

All three belong in a careful argument. The first provides the central proof, the second broadens the thesis's significance, and the third calls for a more precise claim, such as: "Free libraries improve educational outcomes, though the effect is modest once family income is considered."

## Using each type well

**Supporting evidence.** Do not drop it into a paragraph and move on. Use the **evidence-commentary chain**: introduce, quote or paraphrase, then explain how it proves the claim. Choose evidence that is **relevant** (it bears on this claim), **sufficient** (more than one case, if the claim is general) and **credible** (a source the audience will trust).

**Complementary evidence.** Say what the evidence adds: "This matters beyond the classroom because..." Complements widen an argument and show significance. They are not a substitute for support, so make sure the central claim has direct evidence too.

**Contradictory evidence.** Do not hide it. If it is strong, or if your reader is likely to think of it, deal with it. There are four standard responses, which Topics 9.3 and 9.4 explore in detail:

| Response | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Qualify** | Narrow your claim so that the evidence no longer contradicts it |
| **Concede** | Accept the evidence or part of it as correct, and say what that changes |
| **Rebut** | Offer a contrasting view or alternative evidence to suggest the contradicting claim is invalid |
| **Refute** | Use evidence to demonstrate that the contradicting claim is invalid |

A writer who acknowledges contradictory evidence and responds to it earns credibility. A writer who ignores it may seem uninformed or dishonest.

## Choosing and judging sources

When you decide how to use a source, ask:

1. **What exactly does it claim**, and how strong is its evidence?
2. **Who produced it and for what purpose?** (A trade body may be reliable on facts and still select them to suit its case.)
3. **Does it bear on this thesis** directly, partly or only loosely?
4. **Where does it fit**: support, complement or contradict?

Keep the **source's claim** and **your commentary** distinct. A synthesis essay is not a list of what each source says; it is your argument, with sources used in the roles you give them.

## A public-domain example

Henry David Thoreau, in *Walden* (1854), gives his own experiment as evidence for his claim that people can live simply and meaningfully: "I went to the woods because I wished to live deliberately, to front only the essential facts of life, and see if I could not learn what it had to teach, and not, when I came to die, discover that I had not lived."

The sentence presents the experience at Walden Pond as a kind of test. As **supporting** evidence for his thesis about simple living, it is first-hand and specific. A critic could say that it is also only one person's experience, which may be insufficient for a general claim, so a careful reader would weigh it as evidence of what is possible rather than of what is typical. The example shows that the **kind** of evidence matters as well as its direction: personal experience can support a thesis powerfully and still be limited in scope.

## Worked example 1: classifying evidence

**Task.** The thesis is: **"Cities should plant more street trees."** Classify each source (written by Marlbridge; the figures are invented) and say how you would use it.

- Source A: "Streets with mature trees were on average 3 degrees cooler on summer afternoons than streets without."
- Source B: "Property values on tree-lined streets were 5 per cent higher."
- Source C: "Tree roots caused damage to pavements and drains on 12 per cent of planted streets within ten years."

**Step 1: classify.**
- A **supports** the thesis directly (trees cool streets, a benefit).
- B **complements**: it adds an economic benefit, a new dimension that extends the case but does not prove the climate argument.
- C **contradicts** in part: it shows a cost that qualifies the claim.

**Step 2: decide how to use each.**
- A: lead with it, then comment: "A three-degree difference can mean the difference between a safe and an unsafe afternoon for older residents."
- B: use it to broaden the thesis: "Beyond cooling, trees can add economic value, which helps to offset planting costs."
- C: acknowledge and respond: "Root damage is a real cost, affecting about one planted street in eight, but it can be reduced by choosing suitable species and planting distances."

**Step 3: check.** The three relationships are each used for a different purpose, and the contradiction is answered rather than hidden. The resulting claim might read: "Cities should plant more street trees, choosing species that limit root damage."

## Worked example 2: analysing how a writer uses sources

**Task.** Explain how the writer uses the two sources in the paragraph below (written by Marlbridge).

> "A survey of 1,200 commuters found that those who cycled to work took, on average, two fewer sick days a year (Source 1). Cycling also reduces pressure on the road network: one study estimated that each additional hundred daily cyclists removes about eighty cars from a major route (Source 2). Critics point out that the survey did not control for fitness, since healthier people may be more likely to cycle (Source 1, discussion). That is fair, and it means the health case rests on a probable, not a proven, link. Yet even if fitter people cycle more, the traffic benefit does not depend on who they are."

**Step 1: classify.**
- Source 1 (sick days): **supports** the claim that cycling to work is beneficial.
- Source 2 (road network): **complements**; it adds a different benefit (traffic) rather than proving the health claim.
- The criticism of Source 1 (fitness): **contradicts in part** by questioning causation.

**Step 2: how the writer handles each.** The writer uses Source 1 as the lead evidence and Source 2 to broaden the argument. The writer then **concedes** the criticism ("That is fair"), qualifies the health claim ("probable, not proven"), and shows that the traffic benefit stands whatever the fitness link.

**Step 3: say why this is effective.** Each source has a role, the contradiction is acknowledged openly, and the final claim is narrower and more defensible than it would have been without the objection.

## Common misconceptions

- **"All evidence should support my thesis."** Some evidence complements and some contradicts, and a strong argument uses all three honestly.
- **"Complementary means weak."** It extends the argument in ways direct support cannot.
- **"Contradictory evidence means my thesis is wrong."** It may mean the thesis needs qualifying, or that the contradicting claim can be rebutted.
- **"Quoting a source is the same as using it."** The commentary is what makes the source do work.
- **"I should hide contradictions in a timed essay."** Acknowledging them briefly is a sign of control.
- **"All sources are equally reliable."** Consider the source's purpose, expertise and evidence.
