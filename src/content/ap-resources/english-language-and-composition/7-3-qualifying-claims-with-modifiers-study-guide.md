## What a modifier does to a claim

A **modifier** is a word, phrase or clause that adds to, narrows or adjusts the meaning of something else in a sentence. In a claim, modifiers are the tools that set the **scope**: they tell the audience how far the claim reaches.

Take the bare claim "Cities should ban cars." It is a statement without limits. Now add modifiers:

- "Cities **with good public transport** should **gradually** restrict cars **in their historic centres**."

Three modifiers have done three jobs: **which cities** (a phrase), **how fast** (an adverb) and **where** (a phrase). The claim is still a claim. It is now precise enough that someone can examine it, agree with it, or disagree with a particular part.

Unit 7's key idea is that writers **strategically** use words, phrases and clauses as modifiers to **qualify or limit the scope** of an argument. "Strategically" means that the writer chooses the modifier to fit the evidence and the audience, not to avoid commitment.

## The dimensions of scope

Ask what the claim could be limited by.

| Dimension | Question it answers | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Frequency | How often? | often, rarely, usually, in most cases, increasingly |
| Amount | How much or how many? | some, many, most, few, largely, partly, almost |
| Group | For whom? | among adults over fifty, for first-time buyers, in rural schools |
| Place or time | Where or when? | in coastal towns, since 2000, in the short term |
| Condition | Under what circumstances? | if funding continues, unless staff are trained, provided that... |
| Degree of certainty | How sure is the writer? | may, might, can, appears to, suggests, tends to, is likely to |
| Concession | What does the writer grant? | although costs are high, despite its flaws |

Most useful modifiers belong to one of these dimensions. If you cannot say which dimension a modifier belongs to, it may be decoration.

## Words, phrases and clauses as modifiers

Modifiers come in three sizes.

**Words.** Single adverbs and adjectives: "*often*", "*largely*", "*modest* gains", "*almost* certainly". Modal verbs are modifiers of the main verb's certainty: "may", "can", "might".

**Phrases.** Groups of words without a subject and verb of their own: "*in most urban districts*", "*for the first few years*", "*according to the latest survey*", "*with proper supervision*".

**Clauses.** Subject and verb together, usually dependent: "*although the evidence is limited*", "*unless the law changes*", "*provided that pupils have access to a quiet room*", "*which has not been tested outside cities*".

A clause modifier is often the most powerful because it can state a full condition or concession. It is also easy to bury. Check that the reader can still see the main claim after the modifier.

If a study of one school shows an effect, "may" is honest. If dozens of studies agree, "usually" or "generally" is honest, and "may" is timid. If the claim is a definition, no modifier is needed.

## Keeping the position visible

A common error is to hide the claim under modifiers. Compare:

- "It could perhaps be argued by some that there might be a case in certain circumstances for a degree of reform." (Nothing is claimed.)
- "The voting age should be lowered to sixteen for local elections, where the issues are close to teenagers' daily lives." (A position, limited in a precise way.)

A modifier should **narrow** a claim, not dissolve it. After you have added modifiers, read the sentence once more and ask: "What exactly am I committed to now?"

## A public-domain example

In his "House Divided" speech (1858), Abraham Lincoln wrote: "I do not expect the Union to be dissolved—I do not expect the house to fall—but I do expect it will cease to be divided." (He then states that it will become "all one thing, or all the other.")

Notice the choice of verb. Lincoln does not say "the Union will not be dissolved" (a prediction stated as certainty); he says "I do not expect". The verb "expect" marks a considered judgement rather than a certainty, and he is exact about what he expects and what he does not. The structure also separates two outcomes ("not dissolved", "will cease to be divided"), which is a way of limiting the claim to the one he is arguing for.

## Worked example 1: qualifying an overstated claim (writing)

**Task.** Revise "Schools should ban homework" three ways, so that each version suits a different amount of evidence. (Written by Marlbridge; the evidence is invented for practice.)

**Evidence set 1.** A single study of 200 primary pupils found no gain in achievement from homework.
**Evidence set 2.** Several studies across different countries find little benefit for primary pupils and mixed benefit for secondary pupils.
**Evidence set 3.** Reviews of dozens of studies find a small but consistent benefit for older pupils when homework is short.

**Step 1: choose scope dimensions.** The evidence concerns **age group** and **length of homework**, so these two dimensions matter most.

**Step 2: draft each version.**

- Set 1 (one study, one age group, strength: possibility): "Homework **may** add little for **primary-age pupils**, which suggests that schools **could** reconsider setting it."
- Set 2 (several studies, mixed result, strength: likelihood): "Schools **should consider** reducing homework for **primary-age pupils**, where the benefit **appears** small, **while keeping it under review** for older pupils."
- Set 3 (reviews, small but consistent effect, strength: stronger for the older group): "For **older pupils**, **short, focused** homework **usually** brings a **modest** gain; for **primary pupils** the case for it is weak."

**Step 3: check.** Each version keeps a position, names its group and uses a modifier whose strength reflects the evidence. None says "ban" or "always" and none dissolves into "might perhaps". The point is to see that the same subject yields different claims as the evidence changes.

## Worked example 2: analysing modifiers in a passage (reading)

**Task.** Explain how the writer uses modifiers in this passage, written by Marlbridge, and what effect they have.

> "Tourism has transformed many small coastal towns, and not always for the better. Where visitors stay for a weekend, the income is welcome, and local shops largely thrive. But in towns where holiday lets have replaced homes, the schools can lose pupils and the winter streets can feel empty. The evidence does not show that tourism harms every community; it suggests that the harm falls on those where visitors outnumber residents for most of the year."

**Step 1: find the modifiers and sort them.**
- Group/place: "many small coastal towns"; "Where visitors stay for a weekend"; "in towns where holiday lets have replaced homes".
- Frequency/amount: "not always"; "largely"; "most of the year".
- Certainty: "can lose", "can feel", "suggests".
- Concession/limit: "the income is welcome"; "does not show that tourism harms every community".

**Step 2: say what each set limits.** The writer begins with a general claim and immediately limits it ("many", "not always"). The two cases separate communities where tourism helps from those where it harms, by condition ("where"). The last sentence **refuses the absolute** ("every community") and states the more exact claim.

**Step 3: state the effect.** The modifiers make the argument fair and defensible: the writer concedes the benefits and then locates the harm precisely. The audience is likelier to trust the claim because it does not exaggerate.

**Model sentence for an exam answer.** "By using limiting phrases such as 'not always' and the condition 'where visitors outnumber residents', the writer narrows a broad accusation to a specific group of towns, which makes the claim harder to refute and more credible."

## Common misconceptions

- **"Modifiers are just filler."** In a claim they set the scope. Deleting them changes what the writer is committed to.
- **"More modifiers means a better claim."** A claim buried in modifiers says nothing. Use the ones the evidence requires.
- **"Only adverbs qualify a claim."** Phrases and clauses often do it better, especially to set conditions.
- **"'May' always shows that the writer is weak."** It shows the evidence supports possibility. It is a flaw only if the evidence is stronger.
- **"A qualified claim is a concession of defeat."** A qualified claim has fewer points of attack.
- **"I should name the modifier but need not say its effect."** In analysis, saying "the writer uses a modifier" earns little. Say what it limits and why.
