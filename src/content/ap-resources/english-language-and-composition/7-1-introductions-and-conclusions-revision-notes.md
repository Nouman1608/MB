## Recap

- An **introduction** introduces the subject and/or the writer, and it **orients**, **engages** and **focuses** the audience. It may present the thesis.
- A **conclusion** brings the argument to a **unified end**. It may present the thesis, but it should do more than repeat.
- Both are **choices made for a rhetorical situation**. A hostile audience, a friendly one and an examiner each need a different opening.
- When you analyse them on the exam, **name the move** and **explain its effect** on this audience and purpose.

## Key moves

| Part | Moves to recognise and use |
|---|---|
| Introduction | quotation · intriguing statement · anecdote · question · statistic or data · contextualised information · scenario |
| Conclusion | significance in a broader context · connections · call to act · change in behaviour or attitude · solution · compelling image · implications · summary · link to the introduction · thesis placed last |
| Test for either | Does it orient, engage and focus (introduction)? Does it unify and add something (conclusion)? |

## Choosing a move

- **Who is reading?** A board that must decide wants context and a thesis early; a general reader may need an anecdote first.
- **What is my purpose?** To persuade a sceptic, lead with common ground or a concrete scene; to inform, lead with the context.
- **What is the setting?** In a timed essay, put the position within the first few sentences.

## Model moves to remember

- **Anthony (1873, public domain)** opens with a scenario of her own indictment: it orients the crowd, engages them with personal stakes and focuses the argument through the phrase "alleged crime".
- **Lincoln (1865, public domain)** closes with a call to act wrapped in a change of attitude ("with malice toward none, with charity for all"): the ending turns the speech's meaning forward rather than repeating it.
- **A Marlbridge model**: an essay that opens on a student waiting for a bus in the dark can close by returning to the same bus stop, now as a choice the board must make. The echo gives the sense of a unified end.

## Mistakes to avoid

1. **Announcing instead of arguing**: "In this essay I will discuss..." has no content.
2. **Vague openers**: "Since the dawn of time..." or a dictionary definition that nobody disputes.
3. **A hook that goes nowhere**: an anecdote or question with no link to the claim.
4. **A conclusion that only repeats** the introduction in different words.
5. **New evidence in the conclusion** that deserved a body paragraph.
6. **Naming a device without an effect**: "The writer uses an anecdote" is not analysis. Add what it does for this audience.

## Quick self-check

1. Name three moves an introduction can make. *(Any three of: quotation, intriguing statement, anecdote, question, statistic or data, contextualised information, scenario.)*
2. A conclusion returns to the story that opened the essay. Which move is that, and what effect can it have? *(Connecting to the introduction; it gives a sense of completeness and shows how the argument changed the meaning of the opening.)*
3. Why is "In conclusion, I have shown that..." followed only by a repeat of the thesis a weak ending? *(It adds no significance, implication or action, so the audience gains nothing from the last words.)*
