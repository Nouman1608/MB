## Recap

- **Oversimplification** usually comes from not understanding the complexities of a subject. It produces generalisations: one cause, one group, one effect.
- **Arguments enter an ongoing conversation.** Effective ones avoid expressing claims, reasoning and evidence in **absolute terms**, because rival positions and counterexamples already exist.
- New evidence **supports**, **refutes** or **complicates** a claim. The third is the hardest and the most useful: narrow the scope or add a condition rather than abandon the claim.
- A good revision is **more precise**, not just more cautious.

## Key relationships

| Signal in a claim | Problem | Typical fix |
|---|---|---|
| "always", "never", "everyone", "no one" | One counterexample refutes it | Replace with a degree word ("often", "most") or name the cases |
| "X causes Y" (single cause) | Other causes ignored | "X contributes to Y, alongside..." |
| "Either A or B" | False dichotomy | Show the range between A and B |
| Small sample generalised | Hasty generalisation | Narrow to the sample, or add evidence |
| Vague comparison ("better") | Unstated criteria | Say better for whom and by what measure |
| Over-hedged ("may sometimes perhaps") | Says nothing | Restore a position with scope and condition |

**Pattern for a complex claim:** position + scope + condition.

## Sorting new evidence

1. Does the evidence fit the claim as worded? It **supports** the claim: use it.
2. Does it make the claim, as worded, false? It **refutes** the claim: rework it.
3. Does it show the claim is true only in some cases or conditions? It **complicates** the claim: narrow, condition or split it.

## Worked mini-example

**Claim:** "Zoos are cruel." **Evidence:** Source A shows poor conditions in one small zoo; Source B shows that accredited zoos run breeding programmes for endangered species; Source C shows that welfare varies widely between zoos.

- A **supports** the claim for one zoo only.
- B **complicates** it by showing a benefit.
- C **complicates** it by showing variation.

**Revision:** "Zoos with poor standards are cruel, and even well-run zoos must justify keeping animals captive, but accredited zoos that run breeding programmes can serve conservation." The position survives, and it now has scope and conditions.

## Mistakes to avoid

1. **Treating a complication as a refutation** and dropping a good claim.
2. **Treating a refutation as a complication** and adding a "sometimes" that fits nothing.
3. **Over-hedging**: a claim nobody could disagree with is not an argument.
4. **Ignoring the strongest opposing evidence** instead of addressing it.
5. **Confusing length with complexity.** Be exact, not wordy.
6. **Using a hedge word with no content**: "may" is not a substitute for naming the conditions.

## Quick self-check

1. Rewrite "Everyone benefits from longer school days" so it avoids an absolute. *(For example: "Many students benefit from longer school days when the extra time is used for supervised study or clubs.")*
2. A source shows that a policy worked in cities but not in rural areas. Does it support, refute or complicate the claim "The policy works"? *(Complicate: the claim holds in some conditions only.)*
3. Why can a qualified claim be stronger than an absolute one? *(It is harder to refute, so it keeps its credibility when counterexamples appear.)*
