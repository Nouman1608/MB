## Two methods, two promises

Topic 3.6 introduced the typical methods of development. Here we look closely at two of them, **narration** and **cause-effect**, because they are among the most common in arguments and because the framework describes exactly what each does.

- **Narration.** "When developing ideas through narration, writers offer **details about real-life experiences** and offer **reflections and insights on the significance of those experiences**."
- **Cause-effect.** "When developing ideas through cause-effect, writers **present a cause, assert effects or consequences of that cause, or present a series of causes and the subsequent effect(s)**."

Each description holds two parts. Narration needs **story plus significance**. Cause-effect needs **links plus reasoning**. A passage that offers only half of either is incomplete.

## Narration in an argument

### What it is

A narrative in an argument is a short account of events, usually real, that supports an idea. It differs from a story told for its own sake: its job is to **develop the writer's point**. The framework stresses both halves:

1. **Details about a real-life experience:** who, where, when and what happened, with concrete specifics.
2. **Reflection and insight:** what the experience means. This is the step that turns a story into an argument.

Without reflection, an anecdote is an illustration the reader must interpret alone. Without details, reflection has nothing to stand on.

### Features of effective narration

| Feature | What it does | Example (written by Marlbridge) |
|---|---|---|
| **Specific detail** | Makes the event real and credible. | "On 14 January, a Tuesday, I waited forty minutes at the Harbour Road stop with a pushchair." |
| **Selection** | Includes only details that serve the point. | The weather is mentioned because it affects the wait; the colour of the pushchair is not. |
| **Sequence** | Orders events so the reader follows. | "First... then... by the time the bus came..." |
| **Perspective** | Tells who is narrating and what they knew then and know now. | "I did not know then that..." |
| **Reflection** | States the significance. | "What I learned was that a timetable is a promise, and ours is broken." |

### Where narration works best

- To **open** an argument and make an abstract issue human.
- To **illustrate** a claim midway.
- To **close** by returning to the opening story with new understanding.

Narrative gives pathos and ethos (the writer was there), but must not replace evidence for a general claim. A sceptical audience usually wants a story **and** data.

## Cause-effect in an argument

### What it is

Cause-effect reasoning explains how one thing leads to another. The framework names three shapes:

| Shape | Description | Example (invented for practice) |
|---|---|---|
| **A cause with asserted effects** | One cause, one or more effects. | "The closure of the bridge forced longer bus routes and reduced weekday visits to the clinic." |
| **A cause with consequences** (a chain) | Cause leads to effect, which becomes a cause of a further effect. | "Later closing times reduced homework-club attendance; with less support, coursework marks fell; fewer pupils then qualified for the next course." |
| **A series of causes with a subsequent effect** | Several causes combine to produce an outcome. | "Rising rents, falling footfall and the loss of the market together emptied the high street." |

### Making cause-effect convincing

Cause-effect is only as strong as its **links**. Ask of each link:

1. **Is there evidence of the connection?** A study, a pattern across cases, a mechanism that explains how A leads to B.
2. **Could something else explain it?** Consider rival causes (see Topic 3.5, false cause).
3. **Is the claimed effect proportionate?** A small cause rarely explains a very large effect.
4. **Is the writer clear about strength?** Words such as "caused", "contributed to", "may have led to" signal different degrees of certainty. Match your words to your evidence.

### Signals of cause and effect

*because, since, as a result, therefore, consequently, so, thus, led to, resulted in, gave rise to, contributed to, owing to, if... then.*

## Narration and cause-effect together

The two methods combine well. A story can show a causal chain at work: "I missed the interview because the bus was late; because I missed the interview, the job went to someone else; and that is how a timetable decided my year." The reflection then states the general claim the story supports: "Unreliable buses do not only waste time; they change the course of people's lives."

## A public-domain example of narration with reflection: Thoreau (1854)

In *Walden*, Henry David Thoreau explains his two years living beside a pond with a statement of purpose: "I went to the woods because I wished to live deliberately, to front only the essential facts of life, and see if I could not learn what it had to teach, and not, when I came to die, discover that I had not lived."

The sentence is a compact narrative of an experience (he went to the woods) joined to **reflection on its significance**: it was a test of whether he could live fully. The detail of the experience (the woods, the pond) supports the larger argument, developed across the book, that people live "lives of quiet desperation" because they accept inherited routines. The story is not told for its own sake. Its significance is the argument.

## A public-domain example of cause-effect: Wollstonecraft (1792)

Mary Wollstonecraft, in *A Vindication of the Rights of Woman*, writes: "Strengthen the female mind by enlarging it, and there will be an end to blind obedience."

This is a clear **cause-effect** sentence: the cause is the strengthening and enlarging of women's minds (education), and the effect is the end of "blind obedience". The structure is a condition and consequence ("and there will be"). She then adds a reason for resistance to the change, that "blind obedience is ever sought for by power", which gives a second causal claim: those with power oppose education because it would undermine obedience. The method lets her present reform not as a favour but as the natural consequence of a cause readers can bring about.

## Worked example 1: building a narrative paragraph with reflection

**Task.** Write a paragraph, using narration, that supports the claim "Unreliable bus services change the course of people's lives." Use the following details (invented for practice): the writer missed a job interview on 9 March because the 8.15 bus did not come; she waited 50 minutes; the interview was at 9.00; the job went to someone else.

**Step 1: Select details that serve the point.** The date, the time, the length of the wait and the missed interview all matter. Skip details that do not.

**Step 2: Order the events.** Wait, realise, miss, learn the outcome.

**Step 3: Add reflection.** State the significance: a small failure of service had large consequences.

**Step 4: Draft.**

> On 9 March I stood at the Harbour Road stop at 8.05, an hour before an interview for a job I badly wanted. The 8.15 never came. I waited fifty minutes, watched the 8.40 pass full, and walked into the building at 9.20 to be told the panel had finished. The job went to someone else. That morning taught me something that no timetable shows: that an unreliable bus does not simply waste time; it hands a person's chances to whoever happened to live closer to a reliable route.

**Step 5: Check.**

- *Details:* date, stop, times, outcome.
- *Sequence:* clear and ordered.
- *Reflection:* the last sentence states what the experience means and ties it to the claim.
- *Selection:* nothing irrelevant.

**Result.** The story supports the general claim by showing one case and then reflecting on what it reveals.

## Worked example 2: analysing a cause-effect chain

**Task.** Analyse the reasoning in this paragraph, written by Marlbridge with invented data, and judge whether the cause-effect links are convincing.

> When the council cut the evening bus in March, attendance at the homework club fell from 48 pupils to 19, because many could no longer get home safely afterwards. With less support, coursework marks in Year 11 fell by an average of seven marks, and fewer pupils qualified for the sixth-form course in September. The saving of £16,000 on the bus has therefore cost the town far more in lost qualifications.

**Step 1: Identify the chain.**

1. Cut evening bus (cause) → pupils cannot get home safely (effect/cause).
2. → attendance at homework club falls (effect/cause).
3. → less support → coursework marks fall (effect/cause).
4. → fewer qualify for the sixth-form course (effect).
5. → cost to the town (conclusion).

**Step 2: Test each link.**

- *Link 1–2:* the writer gives a reason ("could no longer get home safely") and data (48 to 19). Reasonable.
- *Link 2–3:* "with less support" is an assumption that the club improves marks. Evidence would help (marks of members compared with non-members).
- *Link 3–4:* the fall in marks leading to fewer qualifying is plausible but is not quantified.
- *Link 4–5:* "far more" is not supported by any figure: no cost of lost qualifications is given. This is an **overstated conclusion**.

**Step 3: Judge.** The chain is plausible and the first link is well supported, but the later links rely on assumptions, and the conclusion outruns the evidence. The writer should either add evidence for the later links or soften the language ("may well cost").

**Check.** The analysis names each link, evaluates the support for it, and links the weakness to the sufficiency of the evidence.

## How the exam asks about narration and cause-effect

- **Reading:** how a narrative contributes to an argument; what a writer's reflection adds to an anecdote; how a cause-effect chain supports a claim; where the chain is weak.
- **Writing:** which sentence best adds reflection to a narrative; which revision makes a causal claim clearer; which transition shows a cause-effect relationship.
- **Essays:** narration works well in argument and as part of an analysis of a text that uses it. Cause-effect appears in all three essays. Always link the method to a purpose and audience.

## Common misconceptions

- **"A narrative is an anecdote with no function beyond decoration."** In argument, narration must develop an idea, and reflection makes this clear.
- **"The story proves the claim."** One story illustrates; it rarely proves a general claim.
- **"Narration must be about me."** It may be about others or historical events, as long as it offers real details and reflection.
- **"Cause-effect means 'because'."** The word helps, but the link needs evidence or reasoning.
- **"Any consequence is a result of the cause."** A later event may have other causes (see Topic 3.5).
- **"A longer chain is more convincing."** Each added link adds risk. A short, well-supported chain beats a long, assumed one.
