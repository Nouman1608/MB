## Recap

To achieve a purpose, writers choose language and content that relate to an audience's **emotions and values**. Emotion is built from **connotation, imagery, narrative, shared experience, values language, rhythm and direct address**. Emotion tells an audience that a claim **matters**; evidence tells them it is **true**. Strong arguments connect both. Appeals should be relevant, proportionate, supported and respectful.

## Key relationships

| Source of emotion | What it does | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Connotation | Adds associations to a word | "occupying" vs "squatting" |
| Imagery | Makes a scene felt | "a swing no child has touched since March" |
| Narrative | Invites sympathy for a person | Mrs Okafor's walk to the clinic |
| Shared experience | Builds belonging | "Every one of us remembers..." |
| Values language | Triggers what the audience holds dear | fairness, duty, neighbour |
| Rhythm | Intensifies a feeling | "We have waited. We have written. We have signed." |

## Ways of relating to values

1. **Name** the value and claim it ("This is a question of fairness").
2. **Frame** a proposal through a value (a parking charge as fairness or thrift).
3. **Link** a feeling to a value (pity for a pensioner plus care for the elderly).

## Tests for an emotional appeal

- **Relevant** to the claim?
- **Proportionate** to the matter?
- **Supported** by evidence?
- **Respectful** of the audience's judgement?

## Worked mini-example

Chief Joseph, 5 October 1877 (as recorded and translated at the time): "Hear me, my chiefs! I am tired; my heart is sick and sad. From where the sun now stands I will fight no more forever." Devices: direct address and imperative (authority, attention); plain statements of feeling (credible); image of the sun (a timeless frame); "no more forever" (finality with dignity). Effect: restrained feeling that is more affecting than extravagant language.

Flat draft: "The playing field is being sold. This will reduce green space." Improved: name the place (the Marsh Lane field, sixty years of Saturday football), name the people (300 children), appeal to a shared value (a community that cares for its children), and keep the facts.

## Writing and reading tips

- Show the situation; do not tell readers what to feel.
- Prefer concrete detail to emotive adjectives.
- Pair emotion with evidence.
- When analysing, name the device, the feeling or value, and the effect on the audience.

## Mistakes to avoid

- Saying a text is "emotional" without naming a device or an effect.
- Treating all emotional appeals as illogical.
- Using extreme language for a minor issue.
- Relying on emotion with no evidence.
- Assuming every audience shares the same values.

## Quick self-check

1. Name three sources of emotion in a text. **For example connotation, imagery, narrative.**
2. What is the difference between naming a value and framing through a value? **Naming states the value outright; framing presents a proposal in terms of a value.**
3. Why is "no more forever" effective? **It gives finality and dignity to a surrender.**
4. Which test asks whether an emotional appeal fits the size of the issue? **Proportionate.**
5. Why might a values frame work for one audience and not another? **Audiences rank values differently.**
