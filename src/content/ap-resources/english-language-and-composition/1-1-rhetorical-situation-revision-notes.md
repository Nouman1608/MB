## Recap

A **rhetorical situation** is the set of circumstances around a text: **exigence** (the prompt), **writer**, **audience**, **purpose**, **context** and **message**. Reading rhetorically means asking not only what a text says but why it says it this way, to these people, at this moment. Every choice of evidence, structure and style is a response to the situation.

## Key relationships

| Component | Ask | Typical wrong answer |
|---|---|---|
| Exigence | What triggered this text? | Writing the purpose instead. |
| Writer | Who speaks, with what credibility? | Only the name, with no role or experience. |
| Audience | Who is addressed (primary and secondary)? | "Everyone" or "people". |
| Purpose | What should change because of this text? | The topic ("to talk about libraries"). |
| Context | When, where, on what occasion? | Only a date. |
| Message | What is the main position communicated? | A list of details instead of a position. |

## How to describe a situation in two minutes

1. Underline the sentence that tells you what prompted the text. That is the exigence.
2. Mark every clue about the speaker: job, experience, relationship to readers.
3. Note who is addressed directly ("Councillors...") and who else would read it.
4. State the purpose as a verb: persuade, honour, warn, explain, mobilise.
5. Add time, place and occasion for the context.
6. Write the message in one sentence that contains a position.

## Worked mini-example

Lincoln, Gettysburg, 1863. Exigence: the dedication of a cemetery after the July battle. Writer: the President, speaking briefly. Audience: the bereaved crowd and the wider Union public through newspapers. Purpose: to honour the dead and give the war a purpose the living can take up. Context: autumn 1863, war undecided, on the battlefield. Message: the nation, founded on equality, must ensure that government of the people, by the people, for the people shall not perish. Note how the short length fits the solemn occasion.

## Where this appears on the exam

- **Multiple choice (reading):** which sentence shows the writer's awareness of the audience, which description of the situation best fits the passage.
- **Multiple choice (writing):** which revision best suits the stated audience or occasion.
- **Rhetorical analysis essay:** the prompt states the situation; your thesis and body paragraphs should show how choices respond to it.
- **Synthesis and argument essays:** you create your own situation, so decide your audience and purpose before you write.

## Mistakes to avoid

- Confusing exigence (cause) with purpose (goal).
- Describing the situation with labels so vague they fit any text.
- Naming one audience when the text has a primary and a secondary one.
- Listing the situation and then never using it to explain a choice.
- Treating the modern reader as the audience of an older text.

## Quick self-check

1. A speech is delivered at a cemetery dedication. Is "the dedication" the exigence or the purpose? **Exigence.** The purpose is what the speaker wants to achieve, such as giving the war meaning.
2. Which component is "a local newspaper, one week before a vote"? **Context.**
3. A letter is addressed to councillors but published in a newspaper. Name the two audiences. **Primary: the councillors. Secondary: newspaper readers.**
4. Is "libraries are part of what makes a town" the message or the purpose? **The message.**
5. Why is "to talk about the war" a weak statement of purpose? **It names a topic, not an effect on the audience.**
