## The path from first sentence to last

A good argument is not a heap of points. It is a **path**: each step leads to the next and the whole route ends at the thesis. That path is the **line of reasoning**. The course framework treats it as a central idea: writers **guide the reader's understanding of a text's lines of reasoning and claims through the text's organisation and integration of evidence**, and **the sequence of paragraphs in a text reveals the argument's line of reasoning**.

When you read, you describe the line of reasoning and decide **whether it supports the thesis**. When you write, you **develop a line of reasoning and use commentary to explain it throughout the argument**. This topic concerns both.

## What a line of reasoning is

A **line of reasoning** is the logical sequence of claims, evidence and commentary by which a writer moves a reader from a starting point to a conclusion. It answers "How do you get from here to there?" Think of stepping stones: if one is missing or out of order, the reader falls in.

A line of reasoning has these parts:

- **A thesis** (the destination).
- **A series of supporting claims** (the stones), usually one per paragraph or section.
- **Evidence for each claim** and **commentary** that explains why it supports that claim.
- **Connections** between the claims: reasons for the order and links ("because", "therefore", "yet", "even so").

## Two common patterns

The framework names two ways to arrange reasoning relative to the thesis.

| Pattern | What happens | When it helps |
|---|---|---|
| **Claim, then reasoning.** | The writer states the thesis early and then develops reasons and evidence for it. | The audience is receptive, or needs to know the position at once. |
| **Reasoning, then claim.** | The writer leads the reader through steps and arrives at the thesis at the end. | The audience may resist; building the case first makes the conclusion feel earned. |

Neither is better in general. The right pattern depends on the audience and context. A writer addressing a hostile audience may delay the claim, while a writer addressing a council that needs a decision may state it at once.

## Why the order of paragraphs matters

Readers infer the writer's reasoning from the order of the paragraphs. Common ordering principles:

- **Chronological:** past, present, future.
- **Cause to effect** (or effect to cause).
- **Least to most important** (building to a climax) or **most to least** (leading with the strongest point).
- **Concession then refutation:** granting the opposing view, then answering it.
- **Problem, then solution.**
- **Broad to narrow** (or narrow to broad).

A reader should be able to ask of any paragraph, "Why is this here, and why now?" and find an answer in the argument. If paragraphs could be shuffled without changing the sense, the reasoning is probably weak.

## Commentary builds the line, not just the paragraph

In Topic 3.1 commentary linked evidence to a claim. Here it also links **claim to claim**. Between paragraphs, commentary (often in the topic sentence or final sentence) says how the previous point leads to the next: "Having shown that the cost is manageable, we must ask whether the benefit is real." Without such links, the reader sees a list of separate claims, not a line.

Useful connecting ideas are:

- **Addition and extension:** "Moreover", "In addition".
- **Consequence:** "Therefore", "As a result".
- **Contrast or concession:** "Yet", "However", "Even so".
- **Example:** "For instance".
- **Sequence:** "First", "Next", "Finally".

Use them for meaning, not decoration. "However" must introduce a genuine contrast.

## Checking whether a line of reasoning supports the thesis

When you read, test each step.

1. **State the thesis in your own words.**
2. **Summarise each paragraph's claim in a phrase.**
3. **Ask of each:** "Does this lead the reader toward the thesis?"
4. **Look for gaps:** is a step missing between claim and conclusion?
5. **Look for detours:** does a paragraph wander from the thesis?
6. **Decide:** does the line of reasoning, taken as a whole, support the thesis?

## A public-domain example: Gettysburg's three-part path (1863)

Lincoln's address is a masterclass in line of reasoning because it is so short that every step is visible. Its three paragraphs form a sequence in time.

1. **Past:** "Four score and seven years ago our fathers brought forth on this continent, a new nation, conceived in Liberty, and dedicated to the proposition that all men are created equal." The founding ideal is set out.
2. **Present:** "Now we are engaged in a great civil war, testing whether that nation, or any nation so conceived and so dedicated, can long endure." The war is presented as a test of that ideal, and the ceremony is explained.
3. **Future:** "It is for us the living, rather, to be dedicated here to the unfinished work..." The living are charged with the task.

The path is **past (what was promised), present (what is at stake), future (what must be done)**. The order is cause for the conclusion: because the nation was founded on equality, and because the war tests it, the living must finish the work. The thesis, that the nation must complete its founding promise so that "government of the people, by the people, for the people, shall not perish from the earth", comes at the end of a reasoning-then-claim sequence.

## Worked example 1: mapping a line of reasoning

**Task.** Map the line of reasoning of the following three-paragraph argument, written by Marlbridge, and state whether it supports the thesis.

> **Paragraph 1.** Last year the library's opening hours were cut by a third, and the young people who rely on it most noticed first. (Evidence: a count showing evening visits by under-18s fell from 310 a week to 140; invented figures.)
>
> **Paragraph 2.** Opening hours matter more for teenagers than for any other group, because many have no quiet space at home and no money for alternatives. (Evidence: a survey of 200 teenagers, 118 of whom said the library was the only quiet place to work; invented.)
>
> **Paragraph 3.** Therefore the council should restore evening hours, because the saving of £16,000 is small beside the cost to young people's education.

**Step 1: State the thesis.** The council should restore the library's evening hours.

**Step 2: Summarise each paragraph.**

1. Evening cuts have hit young people (the effect).
2. Why the effect is serious (the explanation).
3. What should therefore be done (the recommendation).

**Step 3: Check the links.** Paragraph 1 shows *what* happened, paragraph 2 explains *why it matters*, and paragraph 3 draws the conclusion with "therefore". The order is **effect, significance, remedy**: a problem-then-solution path.

**Step 4: Test for gaps and detours.** No gaps: each step leads to the next. No detours: every paragraph concerns evening hours and young people. One weakness: paragraph 3 asserts that the £16,000 is "small", but gives no comparison, so the final step is less supported.

**Result.** The line of reasoning supports the thesis well, with a small gap at the end: a comparison would complete it.

## Worked example 2: building a line of reasoning from scrambled claims

**Task.** An argument (written by Marlbridge) claims that schools should teach personal finance. Here are five claims in the wrong order. Put them in an order that forms a line of reasoning, and explain your choice.

- (a) Teaching personal finance would help close the gap.
- (b) Many school leavers cannot read a payslip or compare loan costs.
- (c) Critics say the curriculum is already too full.
- (d) The gap leaves young adults vulnerable to expensive borrowing.
- (e) Schools should therefore make personal finance a required subject.

**Step 1: Find the thesis.** (e), a recommendation.

**Step 2: Build a problem-then-solution sequence.** Start with the problem and its consequence: (b) establishes the gap; (d) explains why it matters.

**Step 3: Introduce the remedy:** (a) says teaching would close the gap.

**Step 4: Address the counterargument:** (c) raises the objection that the curriculum is full. It belongs after the remedy and before the conclusion, so the writer can answer it.

**Step 5: Conclude:** (e).

**Result.** (b), (d), (a), (c), (e).

**Check.** Each step prepares the next: gap, consequence, remedy, objection, recommendation. If (c) came first, the reader would hear an objection before any case had been made. If (e) came first, the argument would be a claim-then-reasoning pattern, which is also possible; it would then lead (e), (b), (d), (a), (c). Either is coherent, but the order must have a reason.

## How the exam asks about the line of reasoning

- **Reading:** which description best fits the sequence of the argument; whether a paragraph supports the thesis; what a paragraph's position contributes.
- **Writing:** which sentence best connects two paragraphs; where a new paragraph should be placed; which sentence best supports the writer's line of reasoning.
- **Essays:** a coherent line of reasoning is a core part of what earns top marks. In the rhetorical analysis essay, describe how the writer's sequence leads the audience to the thesis.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Any order of paragraphs will do if each is good."** Order is part of the argument. A shuffled argument loses force.
- **"The thesis must come first."** Either pattern can work; choose by audience.
- **"Transitions create a line of reasoning."** Words such as "however" help only if the ideas really are connected.
- **"A line of reasoning is the same as an outline."** An outline lists topics; a line of reasoning states how each step leads to the next.
- **"Commentary belongs only inside paragraphs."** Commentary also links paragraphs and shows how the argument progresses.
- **"If every paragraph is relevant, the reasoning is sound."** Relevance is necessary but not enough: the steps must also lead the reader toward the thesis without gaps.
