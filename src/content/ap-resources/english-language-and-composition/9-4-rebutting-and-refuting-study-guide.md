## Two ways to answer an opposing claim

Conceding (Topic 9.3) accepts something of the other side. Rebutting and refuting go the other way: they argue that an opposing claim, or part of it, is **invalid**. The course separates them carefully.

- **To rebut** is to offer a **contrasting perspective** on an argument and its evidence, or to provide **alternative evidence**, to **propose** that all or a portion of a competing position or claim is invalid.
- **To refute** is to **demonstrate, using evidence**, that all or a portion of a competing position or claim is invalid.

The difference is one of **strength and method**. A rebuttal says, "Look at it this way, or look at this other evidence; the claim may not hold." A refutation says, "Here is the evidence that shows it does not hold." A refutation closes the question; a rebuttal reopens it on the writer's terms.

| | Rebut | Refute |
|---|---|---|
| What the writer does | Offers a contrasting perspective or alternative evidence | Demonstrates with evidence that the claim is invalid |
| Force | Proposes or suggests | Shows |
| Typical wording | "That view overlooks...", "Another reading is...", "Other evidence points the other way" | "The figures show that this is not so", "This cannot be true because...", "The record contradicts it" |
| Best when | Evidence is mixed or the dispute turns on interpretation or values | Strong evidence shows the claim to be false or the reasoning flawed |

Both, when fair, **enhance credibility**; so does conceding. A writer who can concede, rebut and refute in the right places is seen as someone who has weighed the evidence.

## Tools for answering a claim

| Tool | What it does | Example (written by Marlbridge) |
|---|---|---|
| **Counterexample** | Shows a case where the opposing claim fails | "The claim that all online courses have low completion rates is contradicted by the open university, where completion is 80 per cent." |
| **Challenge an assumption** | Exposes an unstated premise and questions it | "The argument assumes that pupils who miss the bus cannot reach school; yet a quarter of them cycle." |
| **Challenge the evidence** | Shows the sample, source or method is inadequate | "The survey asked only 40 people, all of them members of the club." |
| **Offer an alternative explanation** | Gives another cause for the same facts | "Test scores rose, but the test itself changed that year." |
| **Show unintended consequences** | Argues that the proposal causes the opposite of its aim | "A ban would push the activity into unsupervised places." |
| **Appeal to a more important value or priority** | Reframes the dispute | "Even if the cost is higher, fairness for pupils without a computer must come first." |

The first four tend to support **refutation** because they rely on checkable evidence; the last two tend to support **rebuttal** because they offer a contrasting perspective.

## Fairness: avoid the strawman

Before you rebut or refute, **state the opposing claim accurately**. A strawman is a distorted version that is easy to knock down: "Opponents want children to be left unsupervised." Refuting that proves nothing about what opponents actually say. Informed readers will see it, and your credibility will suffer.

A fair response has four parts:

1. **State** the opposing claim as its holder would.
2. **Say** what part you are rebutting or refuting (all of it, or only a part).
3. **Give** the contrasting view or the evidence.
4. **Explain** what follows for your thesis.

## A public-domain example

In *On Liberty* (1859), John Stuart Mill takes on a common belief, that truth always overcomes persecution. He calls it "one of those pleasant falsehoods which men repeat after one another till they pass into commonplaces, but which all experience refutes."

Mill names the opposing claim, labels it as a commonplace repeated without examination, and says that experience refutes it. He then goes on, in the pages that follow, to **offer historical evidence** that truths have been suppressed by persecution. The sentence is a **refutation in outline**: it states the claim, says it is false, and promises evidence. Notice also the diction: "pleasant falsehoods" concedes that the belief is attractive, which makes his rejection feel less like an attack on his readers and more like a correction of a comfortable habit.

## Worked example 1: one claim, one rebuttal and one refutation

**Task.** The opposing claim (written by Marlbridge) is: **"Pupils who use laptops in lessons learn less, so laptops should be banned."** Write a rebuttal and a refutation, and say what each does.

**Step 1: state the claim fairly.** The opponents argue that laptop use reduces learning and therefore want a ban.

**Step 2: write a rebuttal (contrasting perspective).**
"The concern about distraction is real, but it treats the laptop as the problem and not the lesson. Where teachers set tasks that need the laptop, such as drafting and editing, pupils have a reason to look at the screen, and the question becomes how lessons are designed, not whether laptops are allowed."

*What it does:* offers a **contrasting perspective** (the problem lies in lesson design) that suggests the ban is the wrong remedy. It does not claim to have proved the opponents wrong.

**Step 3: write a refutation (evidence).**
"The claim that laptops cause lower learning is not supported by the study its supporters cite. That study compared pupils in two different schools, one with laptops and one without, and the school with laptops also had larger classes. Another study, of 800 pupils in the same schools before and after laptops were introduced, found no change in test scores."

*What it does:* uses **evidence** that challenges the comparison (a confounding difference in class size) and **offers alternative evidence** (before-and-after results) to show that the claim does not hold. The evidence is invented for practice.

**Step 4: compare.** The rebuttal reframes; the refutation demonstrates. A strong paragraph might use the refutation first, then the rebuttal to deal with the part of the claim that evidence cannot settle (what laptops are for).

## Worked example 2: analysing a refutation

**Task.** Explain how the writer refutes the claim in the paragraph below (written by Marlbridge).

> "Some say that the town's cycle lanes are empty, and therefore a waste of money. But the claim rests on a single count taken on a wet Monday morning in February. Counters installed on the same lanes recorded an average of 640 trips a day across the year, rising to over 1,100 in June. A lane is not wasted because it is quiet on the worst morning of the year."

**Step 1: the opposing claim.** The lanes are empty, so they are a waste of money.

**Step 2: how it is stated.** Fairly and briefly: "Some say that the town's cycle lanes are empty."

**Step 3: how the writer answers.**
- **Challenges the evidence:** the claim rests on "a single count taken on a wet Monday morning in February" (a sample too small and unrepresentative).
- **Gives alternative evidence:** counters record 640 trips a day on average, and over 1,100 in June, across the year.
- **Challenges the reasoning:** "A lane is not wasted because it is quiet on the worst morning of the year."

**Step 4: classify.** This is a **refutation**: it uses evidence to demonstrate that the claim is invalid, and it is aimed at the whole claim.

**Step 5: effect.** The paragraph is short, accurate and fair. Its authority comes from the numbers, so even a reader who supports the opponents must reckon with them.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Rebutting and refuting are the same."** A rebuttal offers a contrasting view or evidence to propose that the claim is invalid; a refutation demonstrates with evidence that it is.
- **"A refutation must destroy the whole opposing position."** It can address only a portion.
- **"The louder the denial, the stronger the refutation."** A refutation's strength is in its evidence.
- **"I can win by attacking the opponent's character."** That is not refutation; it changes the subject.
- **"Strawman arguments are shrewd."** They are easy to spot and weaken credibility.
- **"If I cannot refute a claim, I have lost."** You can concede, rebut or qualify instead.
