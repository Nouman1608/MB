## Recap

Arguments persuade or motivate action through **appeals**, the modes of persuasion. **Logos** appeals to reason (evidence, logic, structure). **Ethos** appeals to credibility and character (knowledge, fairness, shared values, good will). **Pathos** appeals to emotion and values. Most sentences do more than one job, so name the **primary** appeal and say what it accomplishes for this audience and purpose.

## Key relationships

| Appeal | Built from | Signal words and moves |
|---|---|---|
| Logos | Facts, statistics, reasons, cause and effect, structure | "because", "therefore", figures, comparisons |
| Ethos | Experience, fairness, shared values, reliable sources, careful language | "I have worked...", concessions, accurate citation |
| Pathos | Connotation, imagery, narrative, values language | Vivid scenes, personal stories, "fairness", "neighbour" |
| Kairos (optional) | The right moment | "Now is the time", "before the vote" |

## Worked mini-example

Anthony, 1873: "It was we, the people; not we, the white male citizens; nor yet we, the male citizens; but we, the whole people, who formed the Union." Logos: argues from the founding text by narrowing and rejecting restrictions on "the people". Pathos: the rising rhythm of "not... nor yet... but" and the widening scope. Ethos: places the speaker inside the founding words her audience reveres.

Marlbridge paramedic passage: "I have worked as a paramedic for twenty-two years" (ethos); "fourteen of seventeen cardiac arrests happened more than five minutes from an ambulance" (logos, invented figure); "I have knelt beside a father on his own front path" (pathos and ethos).

## Common confusions

- A statistic is not always logos: it can arouse fear or lean on a credible source.
- Citing an expert for authority is usually ethos.
- Pathos covers any feeling, not only sadness.
- Ethos is not only a job title; tone, fairness and accuracy build it.
- Appeals are tools, not good or bad in themselves.

## How to write about appeals

1. Quote or point to the choice.
2. Name the **primary** appeal.
3. Explain the effect on **this audience**.
4. Link it to the **purpose**.

Example: "By citing twenty-two years as a paramedic, the speaker establishes expertise, so the audience of councillors can trust her figures."

## Where this appears on the exam

- **Multiple choice (reading):** which sentence is primarily an appeal to ethos, logos or pathos; what an appeal adds to the argument.
- **Multiple choice (writing):** which added sentence would best build credibility or support a claim with reasoning.
- **Rhetorical analysis essay:** appeals are part of explaining how choices serve a purpose for an audience.

## Mistakes to avoid

- Listing the three appeals as a checklist without explanation.
- Forcing each sentence into only one box.
- Treating all emotion as manipulation.
- Naming an appeal and stopping.
- Treating logos as numbers only.

## Quick self-check

1. Which appeal does "I have taught for thirty years" most directly serve? **Ethos.**
2. Which appeal does a vivid story of a struggling pensioner mainly serve? **Pathos.**
3. Which appeal is cause-and-effect reasoning? **Logos.**
4. Why name a primary appeal? **Because sentences often do more than one job, and naming the main one shows control.**
5. What is kairos? **The right moment for an argument or action.**
