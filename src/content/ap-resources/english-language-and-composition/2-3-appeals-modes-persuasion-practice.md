These are **original Marlbridge practice questions**, not past exam questions. Any passage not credited to a named public-domain author was written by Marlbridge for practice; quotations from named authors are public-domain texts quoted briefly. The rubrics are a suggested Marlbridge rubric to help you check your own work; they are not an official scoring guideline. There is no calculator. Try each question before opening its answer.

## Question 1 (multiple choice · foundation)

"As a doctor who has treated asthma for twenty years, I can tell you that this pollution is harming children." The **primary** appeal is to

- (A) logos, because it contains a statistic
- (B) ethos, because it establishes the speaker's expertise
- (C) pathos, because it describes children
- (D) kairos, because it names a year

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (B).** The first clause presents the speaker's qualifications and experience to make the audience trust her judgement.

- (A) is wrong: no statistic is given.
- (C) is wrong: children are mentioned but the sentence does not evoke feeling through imagery or story; the main work is done by the credentials.
- (D) is wrong: no moment or deadline is invoked.

</details>

## Question 2 (multiple choice · foundation)

"Because the crossing has no light, children must judge the gaps in traffic themselves, so they will eventually misjudge one." This sentence is primarily an appeal to

- (A) ethos
- (B) pathos
- (C) none of these
- (D) logos

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (D).** It lays out a chain of cause and effect, a piece of reasoning.

- (A) is wrong: it does not point to the writer's credibility.
- (B) is wrong: it does not evoke a feeling through imagery or story, though the prospect may be sobering.
- (C) is wrong: it clearly uses a recognised appeal.

</details>

## Question 3 (multiple choice · core)

A speaker says: "I know that many of you distrust councils, and you have good reason. I have served on this one for six years and have voted against its worst ideas." Which appeal is **most prominent**?

- (A) Ethos: it acknowledges the audience's distrust and builds trust through fairness and experience
- (B) Logos: it presents a statistic
- (C) Pathos: it describes a family's suffering
- (D) Kairos: it names a deadline

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (A).** The speaker concedes the audience's distrust (fairness) and cites experience, both of which build credibility.

- (B) is wrong: no statistic is used.
- (C) is wrong: no family or suffering is described.
- (D) is wrong: no deadline appears.

</details>

## Question 4 (multiple choice · core)

A writer quotes a named climate scientist to support a claim about warming. Which statement is the **most accurate** description?

- (A) It is always pathos.
- (B) It is never an appeal.
- (C) It is usually an appeal to ethos, since the authority of the source lends credibility, and it may also support logos if the scientist's reasoning is explained.
- (D) It is an appeal to kairos.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (C).** Citing an expert primarily builds credibility; if the expert's reasoning is presented, it also supports logos.

- (A) is wrong: nothing in the description suggests emotion.
- (B) is wrong: citation of authority is one of the ways of persuading.
- (D) is wrong: no timing is involved.

</details>

## Question 5 (multiple choice · core)

Susan B. Anthony (1873): "It was we, the people; not we, the white male citizens; nor yet we, the male citizens; but we, the whole people, who formed the Union." Which statement best explains the sentence's **logos**?

- (A) It states a statistic about the number of voters.
- (B) It tells a story about an individual woman.
- (C) It argues from the founding wording by narrowing the phrase "the people" and rejecting each restriction in turn.
- (D) It names her profession.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (C).** The logical force comes from testing the phrase "the people" and showing that each narrowing is not what it says.

- (A) is wrong: no statistic appears.
- (B) is wrong: no story is told.
- (D) is wrong: her profession is not named in the sentence.

</details>

## Question 6 (constructed response · core)

Read this passage, written by Marlbridge with invented figures.

> I have coached the under-elevens at Dale Park for fourteen years. Last season, 31 of our 40 players said they would stop playing if the pitch fees rose again. Last Saturday, I watched a boy borrow boots from another team because his family could not afford a pair. Sport should not be something only the lucky afford.

Identify the primary appeal of each of the first three sentences and explain.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Model answer.** Sentence 1 is ethos: fourteen years of coaching shows experience and credibility on the subject. Sentence 2 is logos: the statistic that 31 of 40 players would stop supports the claim that fees threaten participation. Sentence 3 is pathos (with some ethos): the image of a boy borrowing boots evokes sympathy and makes the cost of fees human, and the first-hand observation shows the writer sees the problem in person.

**Suggested Marlbridge rubric (5 points)**

| Points | What earns it |
|---|---|
| 1 | Sentence 1 identified as ethos with a reason. |
| 1 | Sentence 2 identified as logos with a reason. |
| 1 | Sentence 3 identified as pathos with a reason. |
| 1 | Notes a secondary appeal in at least one sentence (for example ethos in sentence 3). |
| 1 | Explanations refer to words or details in the passage. |

</details>

## Question 7 (constructed response · core)

The following paragraph has a claim and plenty of feeling but no credibility and little reasoning: "Our parks are being destroyed and it is a total disgrace. Everyone is furious. The council must stop." Revise it into a paragraph of three to four sentences that adds an ethos appeal and a logos appeal, and name where each appears.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Model answer.** "I have walked in Cutler Park every morning for eleven years, and I was among the volunteers who cleared the pond in 2021. Since March the council has removed three of its eight benches and cut the grass-cutting from fortnightly to monthly, and in a petition last week 410 of 520 residents who passed the gate signed asking for the cuts to be reversed. A park is cheap to keep and costly to lose; the council should restore the maintenance before the summer." Ethos: the eleven years and volunteering. Logos: the removal of three of eight benches, the cut in grass-cutting, the petition figures, and the cost reasoning.

**Suggested Marlbridge rubric (5 points)**

| Points | What earns it |
|---|---|
| 1 | Adds a credibility appeal (experience, fairness or service) that is specific. |
| 1 | Adds a logos appeal with at least one fact, statistic or step of reasoning. |
| 1 | Keeps the original claim. |
| 1 | Removes or tones down the unsupported emotional assertions (for example 'total disgrace'). |
| 1 | Correctly names where each appeal appears. |

Figures in the model answer are invented for practice; accept any plausible, specific details.

</details>

## Question 8 (constructed response · stretch)

Explain how one sentence from Susan B. Anthony's 1873 address, "It was we, the people; not we, the white male citizens; nor yet we, the male citizens; but we, the whole people, who formed the Union," works through **at least two** appeals. Name each appeal and explain how it works.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Model answer.** Logos: the sentence argues from the Constitution's opening phrase by testing it against two narrower readings and rejecting both, which leaves only the inclusive reading. Pathos: the repeated structure ("not... nor yet... but") builds a rhythm that rises to "the whole people", so that the conclusion feels like a widening of justice. Ethos: she speaks from within the founding text that her audience honours, which presents her as a defender of the nation's principles and not an outsider.

**Suggested Marlbridge rubric (5 points)**

| Points | What earns it |
|---|---|
| 1 | Names one appeal accurately. |
| 1 | Explains how it works with reference to specific wording (for example the repeated narrowing). |
| 1 | Names a second appeal accurately. |
| 1 | Explains how it works with reference to specific wording or rhythm. |
| 1 | Links at least one appeal to the audience or to her purpose. |

</details>
