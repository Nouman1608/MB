## What makes a thesis defensible

Topic 2.6 asked you to find a thesis in someone else's writing. This topic asks you to **write** one. The writing skill is to write a thesis statement that **requires a defence**, and, where appropriate, one that **previews the structure** of the argument. (Topic 4.3 treats the previewing thesis in detail.)

A **defensible** thesis is one that a writer can reasonably defend, and that *needs* defending. Those two ideas pull in opposite directions, so a good thesis lives between them.

- **Needs defending:** it is not obvious and not a bare fact. A reasonable reader could disagree.
- **Can be defended:** it is specific and supportable with evidence and reasoning within the space available. It is not so vast or vague that no essay could prove it.

## Qualities of a strong thesis

| Quality | What it means | Weak | Strong |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Arguable** | Takes a position a reasonable person might dispute. | "Social media exists." | "Social media has made teenagers better informed but less able to concentrate." |
| **Specific** | Names what, who and in what way. | "Technology is important." | "Tablets in primary classrooms should replace printed reading books only for older pupils." |
| **Focused** | Fits the length and time of the essay. | "War is wrong." | "The 1863 Gettysburg Address redefines the war's purpose for a divided audience." |
| **Reasoned** | Gives or implies a reason (often with *because*). | "Zoos are bad." | "Zoos should be phased out because they cannot meet the needs of large, wide-ranging animals." |
| **Responsive** | Answers the question or task actually set. | Restates the prompt without a position. | Takes a clear stance on the issue in the prompt. |

## Shapes of thesis to avoid

1. **The announcement:** "In this essay I will discuss pollution." It states a topic and a plan, not a position.
2. **The fact:** "Pollution is a problem in many cities." Nobody disputes it.
3. **The restatement of the prompt:** if the prompt asks "To what extent should cars be banned from city centres?" then "Cars should or should not be banned" is not a thesis.
4. **The vague evaluation:** "Pollution is bad" or "this speech is very effective". Say how and why.
5. **The list of unconnected points:** "The writer uses imagery, repetition and rhetorical questions." This names devices without saying what they accomplish.
6. **The overreach:** "This policy will solve all of society's problems." It cannot be defended.

## Thesis formulas that work (and the danger of formulas)

Several patterns help you draft. Use them as scaffolds, not scripts.

- **Claim + reason:** "Cities should ban cars from their centres because the health benefits of cleaner air outweigh the inconvenience to drivers."
- **Concession + claim:** "Although cars bring convenience, city centres should be closed to them because..."
- **Claim + qualification:** "City centres should be closed to private cars, with exceptions for residents and deliveries, because..."
- **Rhetorical analysis pattern:** "[Writer] uses [strategies] to [accomplish purpose] for [audience]."

For rhetorical analysis, the key word is **to**. A defensible analytical thesis says *what the choices accomplish*, not only which choices exist: "By ... Lincoln ... in order to ...".

## Thesis for the three exam essays

| Essay | What your thesis must do | Example direction (original) |
|---|---|---|
| **Synthesis** | Take a defensible position on the issue and draw on sources to defend it. | "Schools should shorten the summer break because evidence of learning loss outweighs the cost of the change." |
| **Rhetorical analysis** | Make a claim about how the writer's choices achieve a purpose for an audience. | "By... the speaker..., which..." |
| **Argument** | Take a position on a general question and defend it with your own reading, experience and knowledge. | "Public libraries are worth funding because they provide something the market does not: a free place to be." |

For each, the thesis should be **a claim, not a plan**, and should address what the prompt asks.

## Does the thesis need to preview the structure?

Not always. A thesis may preview the main points ("because it reduces pollution, improves safety and revives local trade"), and that can help the reader follow the line of reasoning. But it can also be a single strong claim. Previews are optional. Avoid a rigid "three-point" list that forces a paragraph structure that does not fit your evidence.

## Revising a weak thesis: four questions

1. **Could a reasonable person disagree?** If not, make it more arguable.
2. **Is it specific?** If it names no group, place or action, narrow it.
3. **Does it say why?** Add the reason or consequence.
4. **Can I defend it in the time and space I have?** If not, reduce its scope.

## Worked example 1: from topic to thesis

**Task.** The argument prompt reads (written by Marlbridge): "Some people believe that city centres should be closed to private cars. Write an essay that argues your position on this issue." A student's first draft is "City centres and cars are a big problem today." Revise it into a defensible thesis.

**Step 1: Diagnose.** "A big problem" is vague, and the sentence takes no position: it does not say whether cars should be banned.

**Step 2: Take a position.** Decide: yes, ban private cars from the centre (with exceptions).

**Step 3: Add a reason.** Why? Cleaner air, safer streets, more space for shops and walking.

**Step 4: Add a qualification.** Residents and deliveries must still be able to enter.

**Step 5: Draft.** "City centres should be closed to private cars, with exceptions for residents and deliveries, because the gains in air quality and pedestrian safety outweigh the inconvenience to drivers."

**Step 6: Test.**

- *Arguable?* Yes. Drivers and shopkeepers might dispute it.
- *Specific?* It names the area, the exceptions and the reason.
- *Focused?* It can be defended in an essay of a few paragraphs.
- *Responsive?* It takes a position on the issue in the prompt.

**Check.** The revised thesis answers "so what do you think, and why?" in a single sentence. If you can write that sentence, the rest of the essay has a direction.

## Worked example 2: a rhetorical analysis thesis

**Task.** Write a thesis for a rhetorical analysis of this passage from Abraham Lincoln's Gettysburg Address (1863): "...we can not dedicate—we can not consecrate—we can not hallow—this ground. The brave men, living and dead, who struggled here, have consecrated it, far above our poor power to add or detract." Later he says: "It is for us the living, rather, to be dedicated here to the unfinished work..."

**Step 1: Identify the situation.** A cemetery dedication in 1863, in the midst of war, addressing a crowd and the wider nation. Purpose: honour the dead and call the living to continue the work.

**Step 2: Identify what the choices do.**

- Repetition of "we can not" in a series builds a rhythm that sounds like humility.
- The insistence that the dead have already consecrated the ground shifts the focus from the ceremony to the soldiers.
- "It is for us the living, rather" turns the audience from spectators into participants.

**Step 3: Draft a thesis that says what the choices accomplish.**

> By stressing that the living cannot add to what the dead have already consecrated, Lincoln uses humility, repetition and a turn to "us the living" to transform a memorial into a call to continue "the unfinished work".

**Step 4: Test.** *Arguable?* Another reader might stress different devices. *Specific?* It names the strategies and the effect. *Reasoned?* It links choices to a purpose. *Responsive?* It analyses how the choices serve the purpose for the audience.

**Check.** Contrast the weak thesis "Lincoln uses repetition, imagery and a serious tone." That names devices but does not say what they accomplish and could apply to any speech.

## How the exam asks about the thesis

- **Writing multiple choice:** which sentence would make the best thesis for a given draft; which revision makes a thesis more specific or arguable.
- **Essays:** the thesis point in each essay's scoring is earned by a **defensible position**, not by a restatement of the prompt. A thesis that is only a plan, a fact or a summary does not earn credit.

## Common misconceptions

- **"A thesis must be one sentence."** It can be two or three, as long as the main claim is clear.
- **"A thesis should be as long and detailed as possible."** It should be clear and focused. A long list can hide the claim.
- **"A thesis can be a question."** A question raises the issue; a thesis answers it.
- **"A thesis that takes a middle position is not arguable."** A nuanced position that still takes a stand ("X is justified in cases A and B but not C") can be strong.
- **"I should write the thesis last."** You can draft a working thesis early and revise it as the argument develops.
- **"Restating the prompt in my own words is a thesis."** Only if it also takes a position.
