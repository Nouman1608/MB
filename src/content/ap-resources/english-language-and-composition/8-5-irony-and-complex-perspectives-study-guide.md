## When words mean more than they say

A reader who takes every sentence literally will sometimes get a writer exactly wrong. Writers sometimes say one thing and intend another, or present a view they do not hold in order to expose it. This is **irony**.

The course puts the point briefly: writers may signal a **complex or ironic perspective** through stylistic choices, and irony may emerge from the **differences between an argument and the readers' expectations or values**. Two parts of that sentence are worth unpacking.

1. **Irony is signalled through style.** There is no label on the page saying "this is ironic". The reader detects it through word choice, tone, syntax and the way the ideas fit together.
2. **Irony depends on readers' expectations and values.** The effect comes from a gap: the writer says something that clashes with what the audience expects or believes, and the clash makes the reader look for a second meaning.

## Kinds of irony you will meet

| Kind | What happens | Example (written by Marlbridge) |
|---|---|---|
| Verbal irony | The writer says the opposite of what is meant | "What a relief to learn the council has solved traffic by moving the road signs." |
| Understatement | The writer deliberately says less than the truth warrants | "The meeting was a little lively," said of a shouting match |
| Overstatement (exaggeration) | The writer deliberately says more than the truth warrants | "Waiting ten minutes at the counter was the greatest trial of my life." |
| Situational irony | What happens is the opposite of what was expected | A fire station that burns down |
| Satire | A sustained ironic text that exposes a flaw in people or institutions | A mock proposal that takes a bad attitude to its logical end |

The first three are matters of style. The last two concern events or the shape of a whole argument.

## Signals of irony

Look for the following. One signal alone may mislead; several together are strong.

- **Incongruity.** The tone does not match the content: a calm, reasonable voice describing something monstrous.
- **Exaggeration or understatement.** The claim is pushed beyond belief, or reduced far below what is true.
- **A clash with the audience's values.** The proposal contradicts what any decent reader would accept.
- **A gap between the speaker and the writer.** The text sounds like a voice that the writer does not share, for example a smug official.
- **Loaded or mock-formal diction.** Elaborate language used for a trivial or shocking point.
- **Context.** What the audience knows of the writer or the occasion suggests that the surface meaning cannot be the whole.

## Public-domain examples

**Satire with a calm voice: Jonathan Swift, *A Modest Proposal* (1729).** The full title is *A Modest Proposal for Preventing the Children of Poor People in Ireland from Being a Burden to Their Parents or Country*. The title already carries two signals: "modest" promises something mild, and the word "burden" repeats how the powerful spoke about the poor. In the essay Swift's calm, statistical speaker proposes that poor children be sold as food, writing of a child as "a most delicious nourishing and wholesome food". The proposal is horrifying, but the voice is reasonable and businesslike, and that **incongruity** is the main signal. Swift does not want readers to adopt the proposal; he wants them to see what attitudes toward the poor lead to if followed to a logical end.

**Understatement and a gap between voice and writer: Samuel Johnson, *A Dictionary of the English Language* (1755).** Johnson defined *oats* as "A grain, which in England is generally given to horses, but in Scotland supports the people." The definition looks neutral. The contrast between "horses" and "the people", and the comparison of England and Scotland, delivers a joke at Scotland's expense, in the dry manner of a reference book. The deadpan **tone against the content** is the signal.

**Irony in an opening line: Jane Austen, *Pride and Prejudice* (1813).** "It is a truth universally acknowledged, that a single man in possession of a good fortune, must be in want of a wife." The pompous phrase "a truth universally acknowledged" announces an unquestionable fact, but the claim itself is only a social assumption, and the novel goes on to show that it is the families with unmarried daughters, rather than the single men, who are anxious about marriage. The elevated diction applied to a trivial opinion is the signal.

## Worked example 1: reading irony

**Task.** Explain the irony in the passage below, written by Marlbridge.

> "At last, a council that understands the real problem facing our town: too many people can read. Since the library opened, citizens have been seen reading, thinking and even talking about what they have read. Ask yourself whether this is safe. Fortunately, our leaders are acting. From Monday the library will close at noon, which should reduce the problem by at least half."

**Step 1: surface meaning.** The writer seems to praise the council for restricting library hours because reading is a problem.

**Step 2: signals.**
- **Clash with values:** hardly any reader thinks that reading is a danger or that thinking is unsafe.
- **Exaggeration:** "too many people can read" is absurd.
- **Mock-formal tone:** "Ask yourself whether this is safe" imitates an official warning.
- **Deadpan tone:** the cheerful "At last" and "Fortunately" sit against an outrageous idea.
- **Precise absurdity:** "reduce the problem by at least half" applies a calculation to a nonsense claim.

**Step 3: intended meaning.** The writer is **criticising** the council's decision to cut library hours, and suggests that it harms the town by restricting access to reading.

**Step 4: purpose and audience.** The audience is local citizens who value libraries. Irony invites them to see the decision's absurdity for themselves, which can be more persuasive than a direct complaint, and it creates a bond of shared knowledge between writer and reader.

**Step 5: risk.** A reader who does not notice the signals might think the writer supports the closure. Irony needs an audience that shares the writer's values.

## Worked example 2: a complex perspective

**Task.** Explain how the writer of the passage below, written by Marlbridge, signals a complex perspective.

> "I am, I admit, a lover of the old high street. I grew up on its crooked pavements and, I am told, it has not been mended since. I go there to be reminded of what a town looks like when no one has had the money to improve it. If anything, the empty shops have a kind of dignity. And yet I notice that I buy my groceries online."

**Step 1: find the apparent position.** The speaker declares love and nostalgia for the high street.

**Step 2: find the tension.** Small phrases undermine the praise: "I am told" (distance), "no one has had the money" (a criticism hidden as a compliment), "a kind of dignity" (damning with faint praise), and the final sentence which reveals that the speaker has abandoned it.

**Step 3: state the perspective.** The writer is both fond of the high street and complicit in its decline, and presents that contradiction with a self-aware, gently ironic tone.

**Step 4: purpose.** The complex voice makes the argument more honest than a straight lament: the reader is invited to recognise the same contradiction in themselves.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Irony just means saying the opposite."** Often it is subtler: understatement, exaggeration, a clash of tone or a gap between a voice and its writer.
- **"Sarcasm and irony are the same."** Sarcasm is one sharp form of verbal irony.
- **"If a text is ironic, I cannot say what the writer thinks."** You can: state the surface meaning, the signals and the intended meaning.
- **"An ironic writer has no position."** Irony usually carries a strong position; it just does not state it directly.
- **"Irony is always funny."** It can be bitter, sad or angry; Swift's is not comic in effect.
- **"One signal is enough."** A calm voice alone does not prove irony; several signals together do.
