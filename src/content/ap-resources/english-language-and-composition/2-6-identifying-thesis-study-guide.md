## What a thesis is

Earlier topics dealt with single claims. A whole essay, speech or article, however, usually has one **overarching claim** that everything else serves. That claim is the **thesis**. The course framework defines it as **the main, overarching claim a writer is seeking to defend or prove by using reasoning supported by evidence**.

Think of a text as a tree. The thesis is the trunk. Each paragraph's claim is a branch, and the evidence is the leaves. If you can find the trunk, you can see how the whole tree is held together.

Identifying the thesis is a **reading** skill with a second half: you should also describe **any indication the thesis gives of the argument's structure**. Some theses preview the order of the argument ("because of X, Y and Z"). Others do not, and you must work out the structure from the text itself.

## A thesis is not always one sentence, and not always stated

The framework makes two important points that many students overlook.

1. A thesis is **not necessarily a single sentence**. It may take two or three sentences, or the idea may be built up across a paragraph.
2. A thesis is **not necessarily an explicit statement**. Some writers never state it outright. You may need a **thorough reading** of the whole text to identify it.

Where a thesis *is* directly expressed, it often appears in an introduction or near the beginning, but it can also come at the end (a writer may lead readers through a line of reasoning and *then* arrive at it), or even in the middle after a setup. Never assume that it must be in the first paragraph.

| Pattern | Where the thesis is | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Thesis first** | Early, then supported. | A policy proposal that begins "The council should close the road to cars." |
| **Thesis last** | After evidence and reasoning build to it. | An essay that ends "Orchard Road does not need fewer trees." |
| **Thesis implied** | Never stated; deduced from the whole text. | A satirical essay whose real position is the opposite of the narrator's. |
| **Thesis across sentences** | Two or three sentences work together. | A claim followed by a qualification. |

## Thesis versus things that are not the thesis

Test each candidate sentence against these look-alikes.

| Look-alike | Why it is not the thesis | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Topic** | Names a subject; asserts nothing. | "Street trees." |
| **Fact or summary** | States what happened; no position. | "The council removed fifteen trees between 2019 and now." |
| **A sub-claim** | A supporting point for one paragraph. | "The repairs cost less than the newsletter." |
| **Announcement** | Says what the text will do, not what it claims. | "This essay will discuss street trees." |
| **Counterargument** | The view the writer is going to answer. | "The council says trees cost too much." |
| **Question** | Raises the issue but does not answer. | "Are trees worth keeping?" |

A useful test: **a thesis is the sentence that every paragraph, taken together, is trying to prove.** If a sentence only explains one paragraph, it is a sub-claim. If it could be removed without changing the rest of the argument, it is probably not the thesis.

## Methods for finding the thesis

1. **Read the introduction and conclusion first.** Writers often state or restate the thesis there.
2. **Look for words of judgement and recommendation:** *should, must, cannot, is not merely, the real problem is.*
3. **Summarise each paragraph in a phrase.** Then ask what claim all the summaries support.
4. **Watch for the turn.** A word such as *but*, *yet* or *however* often moves from the counterargument to the writer's own position.
5. **Check the title and the last line.** These often point to the thesis.
6. **If the text is ironic, read it twice.** The narrator's stated view may be the opposite of the writer's.
7. **Write the thesis in your own words, in one or two sentences,** and test it against every paragraph.

## A public-domain example of a stated thesis: the Declaration of Sentiments (1848)

The Declaration of Sentiments, written for the Seneca Falls convention and drafted by Elizabeth Cady Stanton, states its central claim directly: "We hold these truths to be self-evident: that all men and women are created equal." Everything that follows, including the list of grievances and the resolutions, is organised to support this claim and to demand its consequences in law. Note the way the thesis adapts the opening of the Declaration of Independence by adding "and women". That small change is the whole argument in miniature, and a reader who finds it has found the document's thesis.

## A public-domain example of an implied thesis: Swift's "A Modest Proposal" (1729)

Jonathan Swift's essay is a famous case of a thesis that is **never stated**. The narrator calmly proposes that the poor of Ireland should sell their children as food. Read literally, that is the narrator's claim. But a thorough reading, and the context of Irish poverty under English policy and landlord indifference, shows that Swift is satirising proposals made by officials who treat the poor as an economic resource. Near the end the narrator rejects genuine remedies, such as taxing absentee landlords, which signals what Swift believes should happen. The **implied thesis** is something like: *the economic and political arrangements that impoverish Ireland already treat its poor as a commodity, and real reform, not callous projects, is required.* The writer's thesis is the opposite of the narrator's proposal.

## Worked example 1: a delayed thesis (written by Marlbridge)

> Each November the council sweeps the leaves from Orchard Road, and every spring it quietly sweeps away something else: the blossom trees themselves. In 2019 there were forty-one; today there are twenty-six. A retired gardener told me she once knew every tree by the colour of its flowers.
>
> The council says the trees lift paving and block drains, and it is right that they cost money. But the repairs come to about £3,000 a year, less than the council spent on its own newsletter. Meanwhile the trees cool the road on hot days and give the street a name people can find on a map.
>
> Orchard Road does not need fewer trees; it needs a council that counts what the trees give as carefully as it counts what they cost.

(The figures are invented for practice.)

**Task.** Identify the thesis and any indication of structure.

**Step 1: Summarise each paragraph.**

1. The council has been removing trees; residents notice.
2. The council's objection (cost) is real but outweighed by the benefits.
3. The street needs a fairer accounting of trees' value.

**Step 2: Look for the sentence all three support.** The last sentence, "Orchard Road does not need fewer trees; it needs a council that counts what the trees give as carefully as it counts what they cost," states a position and asks for a change in the council's way of deciding.

**Step 3: Check against the look-alikes.**

- "In 2019 there were forty-one; today there are twenty-six" is a **fact**.
- "The council says the trees lift paving and block drains" is the **counterargument**.
- "The repairs come to about £3,000 a year, less than the council spent on its newsletter" is a **sub-claim** supported by a figure.

**Step 4: Indication of structure.** The thesis does not preview the order, but the passage leads readers through a line of reasoning (the loss, the objection, the answer) and then arrives at the thesis: a **thesis-last** structure.

**Result.** Thesis: the council should value trees by their benefits as well as their costs, rather than removing them. The delay lets the writer build the evidence and concede the opponent's point before stating the position.

## Worked example 2: identifying an implied thesis

**Task.** A short passage, written by Marlbridge, reads in part: "I am delighted by the new rule that every pupil must hand in their phone at 8.30. Think of the saving! No more cracked screens, no more arguments, no more pupils who can find their friends in a corridor. And think of the community spirit of nearly two hundred children all unable to prove the time is 'nearly nine'." The passage continues in this mock-delighted tone for two more paragraphs and ends: "Truly, we have discovered how to make the most of our young people's time: remove everything they use it for."

State the implied thesis and explain how you found it.

**Step 1: Notice the tone.** The writer says "delighted" but lists effects that are plainly negative ("no more pupils who can find their friends"). The enthusiasm is ironic.

**Step 2: Notice the last sentence.** "Remove everything they use it for" is a critical summary, not a compliment.

**Step 3: State the thesis.** The new phone rule is a heavy-handed and counterproductive way of managing pupils' time, because it removes tools pupils use to organise and connect without addressing the problem it was meant to solve.

**Step 4: Check.** Every paragraph's mock praise, once reversed, supports this claim. The thesis is implied, so a literal reading would get it wrong. The skill is to read through the irony to what the writer actually wants the audience to think.

## How the exam asks about the thesis

- **Reading:** which sentence best states the passage's thesis; which description of the argument's structure fits; whether a sentence is a thesis or a sub-claim.
- **Writing:** which sentence would be the best thesis for a draft; where the thesis belongs in the introduction.
- **Essays:** your own thesis is an assessed element (see Topic 2.7). In the rhetorical analysis essay you also need to identify the writer's own thesis in the passage.

## Common misconceptions

- **"The thesis is always the last sentence of the first paragraph."** It may be anywhere, or implied.
- **"A thesis must be one sentence."** It can take two or more sentences.
- **"The thesis is the topic."** A topic names the subject; a thesis takes a position on it.
- **"The thesis is whatever the speaker says most loudly."** In irony or satire the stated view may be the opposite of the thesis.
- **"If the text never states a thesis, it has none."** It has one, but you must infer it from the whole text.
- **"The thesis and the conclusion are the same."** A conclusion may restate the thesis, but it also looks ahead or reflects (see Topic 4.2).
