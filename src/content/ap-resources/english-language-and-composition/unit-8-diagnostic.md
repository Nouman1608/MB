Use this diagnostic to find **which Unit 8 topics to revisit**: at least one short question per topic.

These are **original Marlbridge practice questions**, not past exam questions. All passages are written by Marlbridge, and all details are invented for practice. The diagnostic is not calibrated and gives **no predicted score**.

**How to take it.** Allow 30 minutes and answer everything before opening any answer. No calculator is needed. The three written questions need only a few sentences each.

## Question 1 (multiple choice · 8.1)

A writer tells a group of gardeners, "Starting a new habit is like planting seeds: you will not see growth for weeks, but the roots are forming." What must be true for the comparison to work?

- (A) The audience must understand how seeds grow.
- (B) The audience must dislike gardening.
- (C) The comparison must be at least three sentences.
- (D) The writer must avoid the topic of habits.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (A).** An effective comparison must be shared and understood by the audience.

- (B) would make the comparison negative or unclear.
- (C) Length is not required.
- (D) The comparison is meant to illuminate the topic, not avoid it.

**If you missed this:** [Topic 8.1 study guide](/advanced-course-resources/english-language-and-composition/8-1-comparisons-for-an-audience-study-guide/).
</details>

## Question 2 (multiple choice · 8.1)

Which of the following is an **analogy**?

- (A) "The test was a nightmare."
- (B) "A library is like a lighthouse: it stands in the same place every night, and people who have lost their way know where to look."
- (C) "I once met a man who never read."
- (D) "He ran as fast as a hare."

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (B).** It extends a comparison across several matching points.

- (A) is a metaphor.
- (C) is an anecdote.
- (D) is a simile.

**If you missed this:** [Topic 8.1 study guide](/advanced-course-resources/english-language-and-composition/8-1-comparisons-for-an-audience-study-guide/).
</details>

## Question 3 (multiple choice · 8.2)

A letter to a neutral newspaper calls the opposing side "a gang of selfish liars". The likely effect on undecided readers is

- (A) increased trust, because the writer is passionate.
- (B) reduced trust, because the loaded diction reveals bias and offers no evidence.
- (C) no effect.
- (D) agreement, because insults are always persuasive.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (B).** Undecided readers usually look for fairness, so insults tend to damage credibility.

- (A) and (D) overgeneralise from audiences who already agree.
- (C) Readers notice loaded diction.

**If you missed this:** [Topic 8.2 study guide](/advanced-course-resources/english-language-and-composition/8-2-diction-syntax-and-credibility-study-guide/).
</details>

## Question 4 (multiple choice · 8.3)

A writer persuades **hospital managers** with cost data and **patients** with personal stories. This shows that the writer is

- (A) changing the claim completely.
- (B) choosing evidence suited to each audience's perspective and needs.
- (C) being inconsistent.
- (D) avoiding evidence.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (B).** Different audiences trust and need different kinds of evidence.

- (A) The claim can stay the same.
- (C) Adapting evidence is deliberate, not inconsistent.
- (D) Both audiences receive evidence.

**If you missed this:** [Topic 8.3 study guide](/advanced-course-resources/english-language-and-composition/8-3-choices-for-a-particular-audience-study-guide/).
</details>

## Question 5 (multiple choice · 8.4)

"I'm telling you, it's not hard. Pick one thing. Do it. Then do the next." The style is best described as

- (A) ornate and formal.
- (B) conversational and brisk, with contractions, direct address and short imperatives.
- (C) technical and abstract.
- (D) aphoristic and balanced.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (B).** The contractions, "I'm telling you" and short commands give a conversational, brisk feel.

- (A) has no elaborate vocabulary or long sentences.
- (C) has no technical terms.
- (D) is a maxim-like style, which is not present.

**If you missed this:** [Topic 8.4 study guide](/advanced-course-resources/english-language-and-composition/8-4-style-study-guide/).
</details>

## Question 6 (multiple choice · 8.5)

"I admire the council's tireless commitment to making sure nothing changes." The most likely intended meaning is

- (A) sincere praise.
- (B) criticism, signalled by the mock-praise and the clash with the value that councils should act.
- (C) a statement of fact.
- (D) a neutral description.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (B).** "Tireless commitment" applied to inaction signals irony.

- (A) takes the surface meaning literally.
- (C) and (D) ignore the signals.

**If you missed this:** [Topic 8.5 study guide](/advanced-course-resources/english-language-and-composition/8-5-irony-and-complex-perspectives-study-guide/).
</details>

## Question 7 (multiple choice · 8.6)

Which sentence has a **misplaced modifier**?

- (A) "She wore a scarf to the meeting that her aunt had knitted."
- (B) "She wore the scarf that her aunt had knitted to the meeting."
- (C) "Her aunt had knitted the scarf she wore to the meeting."
- (D) "To the meeting she wore a scarf her aunt had knitted."

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (A).** "That her aunt had knitted" is next to "the meeting", so it seems that the aunt knitted the meeting.

- (B), (C) and (D) place the clause beside "scarf".

**If you missed this:** [Topic 8.6 study guide](/advanced-course-resources/english-language-and-composition/8-6-modifiers-and-parenthetical-elements-study-guide/).
</details>

## Question 8 (short answer · 8.1 and 8.3)

Write a one-sentence comparison to persuade **a group of cyclists** to wear helmets. Name the form and say why it suits the audience.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Sample.** "A helmet is like a good lock: you hope you never need it, and the one day you do, you are glad of the three seconds it took." Form: **simile (or analogy)**. Cyclists understand locks and the habit of taking a small precaution, so the idea transfers.

**Marking guide.** Full credit for a comparison from the cyclists' experience, a correct form and a reason that refers to the audience.

**If you missed this:** Topics [8.1](/advanced-course-resources/english-language-and-composition/8-1-comparisons-for-an-audience-study-guide/) and [8.3](/advanced-course-resources/english-language-and-composition/8-3-choices-for-a-particular-audience-study-guide/).
</details>

## Question 9 (short answer · 8.2 and 8.4)

Describe the style of "Three reports. Three delays. No answers." and explain what it suggests about the writer's attitude.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Sample.** The style is **terse and accusatory**. The three short fragments repeat the same pattern ("Three..."), which builds a sense of accumulating failure, and the absence of verbs leaves no room for excuses. The tone suggests frustration and controlled anger.

**Marking guide.** Full credit for a precise label, evidence from the syntax and an inference about attitude.

**If you missed this:** Topics [8.2](/advanced-course-resources/english-language-and-composition/8-2-diction-syntax-and-credibility-study-guide/) and [8.4](/advanced-course-resources/english-language-and-composition/8-4-style-study-guide/).
</details>

## Question 10 (short answer · 8.5 and 8.6)

Add a parenthetical element to "The treasurer presented the budget" so that it adds an ironic edge, and explain how it works.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Sample.** "The treasurer presented the budget—with the confidence of a man who had never checked it." The dash emphasises the aside, and the mock-admiring phrase implies that the treasurer's confidence is unearned, which signals irony through understatement and a clash with the expectation that budgets are checked.

**Marking guide.** Full credit for a correctly punctuated parenthetical element, an ironic meaning and an explanation of the signal.

**If you missed this:** Topics [8.5](/advanced-course-resources/english-language-and-composition/8-5-irony-and-complex-perspectives-study-guide/) and [8.6](/advanced-course-resources/english-language-and-composition/8-6-modifiers-and-parenthetical-elements-study-guide/).
</details>
