## Question 1 (multiple choice · foundation)

Which statement best describes how irony can arise?

- (A) From a writer repeating the same word
- (B) From a gap between what an argument says and what readers expect or value
- (C) From a writer using long sentences
- (D) From a writer avoiding all opinions

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (B).** Irony emerges when the words clash with the readers' expectations or values, which prompts them to look for a second meaning.

- (A) Repetition can be a device but does not itself create irony.
- (C) Sentence length is a style feature, not the source of irony.
- (D) Irony usually carries a strong view, indirectly.
</details>

## Question 2 (multiple choice · core)

A reviewer writes that a three-hour film, in which almost nothing happens, "held my attention for the first twenty minutes." This is best described as

- (A) overstatement.
- (B) understatement that implies strong criticism.
- (C) a literal compliment.
- (D) situational irony.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (B).** By crediting the film with only twenty minutes of attention out of three hours, the reviewer says far less than the truth warrants and implies a harsh verdict.

- (A) The statement is smaller, not larger, than the real reaction.
- (C) A literal reading misses the implied criticism.
- (D) Nothing happens contrary to expectation; it is a verbal effect.
</details>

## Question 3 (multiple choice · core)

Which feature would **most** strongly signal that a writer is being ironic?

- (A) A calm, reasonable voice proposing something that no decent reader would accept
- (B) A list of statistics
- (C) A formal title
- (D) A direct statement of the writer's opinion

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (A).** The clash between a calm voice and an unacceptable proposal is the classic signal of satire.

- (B) Statistics alone do not signal irony.
- (C) A formal title may or may not be ironic.
- (D) A direct statement is the opposite of ironic indirectness.
</details>

## Question 4 (multiple choice · core)

Why can irony be a risk for a writer?

- (A) It cannot be misread.
- (B) An audience that does not share the writer's values or misses the signals may take the surface meaning literally.
- (C) It makes the text too short.
- (D) It always makes the audience laugh.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (B).** The effect depends on shared knowledge and values; without them, the surface meaning may be read as the writer's real view.

- (A) The risk of misreading is exactly the problem.
- (C) Irony has nothing to do with length.
- (D) Irony can be angry or sad as well as comic.
</details>

## Question 5 (multiple choice · stretch)

"The new parking charge will, of course, be a wonderful opportunity for drivers to practise generosity." Which is the best statement of the **intended meaning**?

- (A) The writer sincerely welcomes the charge.
- (B) The writer is criticising the charge as an unwelcome cost, by pretending to praise it.
- (C) The writer is describing a charity.
- (D) The writer is neutral about the charge.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (B).** The phrases "of course" and "wonderful opportunity... generosity" are mock-positive framings of a payment drivers would resent.

- (A) takes the surface meaning literally.
- (C) There is no mention of charity beyond the ironic word.
- (D) The mock praise reveals a critical stance.
</details>

## Question 6 (constructed response · core)

Read the passage below, written by Marlbridge.

> "I should say at once that I admire the committee's courage. It takes real bravery to hold a consultation, ignore every reply and announce, with no trace of embarrassment, that the public has spoken. We are fortunate to have such leaders. I only wish I could understand which public it was."

Explain the irony, quoting **at least two** signals, and explain what it achieves. (6 marks)

<details>
<summary>Sample answer and suggested rubric</summary>

**Sample.** The surface meaning is praise for the committee's courage. The signals show the opposite: "real bravery" is applied to ignoring the public, which clashes with the value that consultation should be honest; the phrase "with no trace of embarrassment" implies that embarrassment would have been appropriate; "We are fortunate to have such leaders" is a mock-positive claim; and the final sentence, "I only wish I could understand which public it was", reveals the writer's real view that the committee ignored the actual respondents. The intended meaning is criticism of a consultation that was not genuine. The irony lets the writer attack the committee while keeping the voice polite, and it invites readers who value honest consultation to share the judgement, which builds solidarity.

| Mark | What earns it |
|---|---|
| 1 | States the surface meaning (praise) |
| 1 | States the intended meaning (criticism) |
| 1 | Quotes and explains a first signal |
| 1 | Quotes and explains a second signal |
| 1 | Explains what irony achieves (criticism with a polite voice, or shared judgement) |
| 1 | Links to the audience's values |
</details>

## Question 7 (constructed response · core)

Write a short ironic passage (three to four sentences) about **a very long queue at a school canteen**. Make the signals clear, and then explain two of them in a sentence each. (6 marks)

<details>
<summary>Sample answer and suggested rubric</summary>

**Sample.** "Nothing prepares a student for the thrill of the canteen queue. Forty minutes of standing, in the company of friends one cannot reach, are a perfect way to practise patience. By the time the doors close, the lucky ones will have a whole sandwich. It is hard to imagine a better use of a lunch hour."

**Signals.** "Thrill" and "perfect way to practise patience" exaggerate in praise a plainly unpleasant experience, so the surface meaning clashes with what readers value (a lunch hour to eat and rest). "It is hard to imagine a better use of a lunch hour" is a mock-conclusion that makes the criticism obvious.

| Mark | What earns it |
|---|---|
| 1 | The passage has a surface meaning of praise |
| 1 | The intended meaning (criticism of the queue) is clear |
| 1 | Uses exaggeration or mock-formal diction as a signal |
| 1 | Keeps a deadpan or cheerful tone against unpleasant content |
| 1 | Explains the first signal |
| 1 | Explains the second signal |
</details>

## Question 8 (constructed response · stretch)

Samuel Johnson defined "oats" in his *Dictionary* (1755, public domain) as: "A grain, which in England is generally given to horses, but in Scotland supports the people."

Explain how the definition is ironic and how its style contributes to the effect. (5 marks)

<details>
<summary>Sample answer and suggested rubric</summary>

**Sample.** The surface meaning is a neutral dictionary definition of a grain. The irony lies in the contrast between "horses" and "the people": by saying that Scotland uses as food for people what England gives to animals, Johnson implies a joke about the Scots, using an apparently factual form. The style supports this: the definition is brief, dry and balanced, with "in England... but in Scotland" setting up the contrast, and the deadpan, reference-book tone makes the sting more pointed because it does not announce itself as a joke. The reader's expectation of a plain definition clashes with the barbed content, which is where the irony emerges.

| Mark | What earns it |
|---|---|
| 1 | States the surface meaning (neutral definition) |
| 1 | Identifies the contrast ("horses" and "the people") |
| 1 | Explains the implied meaning |
| 1 | Comments on the deadpan style or balanced structure |
| 1 | Links the effect to the reader's expectation of a neutral definition |
</details>
