## What style is

When you recognise a writer from a single paragraph, without a name on the page, you are recognising **style**. The course defines it simply: a writer's **style is made up of the mix of word choice, syntax and conventions** employed by that writer.

That definition has three parts, and they tell you where to look.

| Part | What to look at | Example features |
|---|---|---|
| **Word choice (diction)** | Kind of words | Plain or elaborate; concrete or abstract; short Anglo-Saxon words or longer Latinate ones; technical, slang or formal; positive or negative connotation |
| **Syntax** | How sentences are built | Short or long; simple or complex; loose, periodic or balanced; parallelism; fragments; questions |
| **Conventions** | How the writer handles grammar and mechanics | Punctuation habits (dashes, semicolons, colons); first or third person; contractions; paragraph length; use of italics |

A **style** is therefore not an extra layer of decoration added to meaning. It is the way the meaning is carried.

## Style, tone and purpose

**Tone** is the attitude the writer shows toward the subject or the audience: respectful, urgent, ironic, affectionate, indignant, matter-of-fact. Style is the means; tone is the result. Change the means and you change the tone.

Compare two sentences about the same event (written by Marlbridge).

- "The river flooded the lower town on Monday night. About forty homes were affected."
- "On Monday night, without warning and without mercy, the river rose, swallowing the lower town and forty homes with it."

The first is **plain and restrained**: short sentences, specific facts, no figures of speech. The tone is calm and informative. The second is **dramatic**: a long, flowing sentence, repeated "without", the metaphor "swallowing". The tone is emotional and urgent. Neither is "better". One suits a news bulletin, the other a campaign to raise money for flood relief.

## Describing style precisely

Weak descriptions say "the writer's style is powerful" or "interesting". Good descriptions follow a pattern:

> **Label + evidence + effect.**

- **Label:** a precise term for the style or tone (plain, aphoristic, formal, conversational, urgent, restrained).
- **Evidence:** the specific words, sentence structures or punctuation that justify the label.
- **Effect:** what the style does for the purpose and audience.

Examples of useful labels, with features that justify them:

| Label | Typical features |
|---|---|
| Plain | Short common words, direct statements, little figurative language |
| Formal | Longer, abstract vocabulary; complex sentences; no contractions; third person |
| Conversational | Contractions, direct address ("you"), questions, informal words |
| Aphoristic | Compressed, balanced statements that sound like maxims |
| Ornate | Elaborate vocabulary, long sentences, many modifiers and images |
| Restrained | Understatement, controlled emotion, careful qualification |
| Urgent | Short sentences, imperatives, repetition, exclamations |

## Public-domain examples

**Aphoristic style: Francis Bacon, "Of Studies" (1625).** "Reading maketh a full man; conference a ready man; and writing an exact man."

The sentence has three balanced parts, each with the pattern *activity, result, man*. The second and third parts leave out the verb ("maketh"), which the reader supplies, so the sentence is compressed. The semicolons separate three equal claims, and the repeated structure makes the sentence sound like a maxim. The style is **aphoristic and balanced**, and it suits a writer giving counsel in a short, memorable form.

**Witty, compressed style: Ralph Waldo Emerson, "Self-Reliance" (1841).** "A foolish consistency is the hobgoblin of little minds, adored by little statesmen and philosophers and divines."

The sentence has a witty metaphor ("hobgoblin"), the repeated adjective "little" and a list of professions that gives the line a mocking ring. The tone is **ironic and confident**: Emerson makes the reader feel that valuing consistency for its own sake is petty. The syntax packs a judgement into a single, rolling sentence.

## Worked example 1: describing a style

**Task.** Describe the style of the passage below, written by Marlbridge, and explain its effect.

> "Look, I'm not going to pretend this is easy. You've got exams in six weeks, a part-time job and a family that wants to see you now and then. But here's the thing: you don't need a perfect plan. You need a start. Ten minutes tonight. Ten more tomorrow. That's how it begins."

**Step 1: word choice.** Everyday, informal words ("Look", "here's the thing", "a start"); contractions ("I'm", "you've", "here's", "you don't", "That's"); the second person ("you") throughout.

**Step 2: syntax.** Short sentences and fragments ("Ten minutes tonight. Ten more tomorrow."), simple structures, and a repeated pattern in the last four sentences. A single complex sentence at the start ("I'm not going to pretend this is easy") is followed by quick declaratives.

**Step 3: conventions.** Direct address, frequent contractions, and full stops that give each short unit its own beat.

**Step 4: label.** **Conversational and encouraging**, with a **plain, urgent** rhythm in its last lines.

**Step 5: effect.** The style creates the impression of a friendly adviser speaking to the reader, which lowers the barrier of a daunting task. The short repeated sentences make the first step feel small and doable. The tone is warm and reassuring, which suits an audience of anxious students.

## Worked example 2: changing style to suit a purpose

**Task.** The same content is to be written for two purposes: (a) a **formal report** to a school board and (b) a **text message** to a friend. The content: "The Year 10 trip is cancelled because too few pupils signed up." (Written by Marlbridge.)

**Step 1: identify the audience and purpose.** (a) The board needs a clear, factual record of the decision. (b) The friend wants to know quickly, and in an informal tone.

**Step 2: write (a).** "The proposed Year 10 trip to the science museum has been cancelled. Fewer than the required minimum of twenty pupils registered by the closing date, and the school is therefore unable to meet the operator's booking conditions."

**Step 3: write (b).** "Trip's off. Not enough people signed up. Gutted."

**Step 4: analyse.**
- (a) uses formal diction ("proposed", "registered", "booking conditions"), a longer complex sentence, third person and no contractions. The tone is **objective and authoritative**.
- (b) uses contractions, fragments and a one-word emotional comment. The tone is **casual and sympathetic**.

**Step 5: conclude.** The facts are the same, but the style changes the relationship with the reader. A mismatch (the text message sent to the board, or the report sent to a friend) would feel wrong, which shows that style is a choice for an audience.

## Learning styles by imitation

One of the oldest ways to widen your own range is to **imitate a model sentence**, keeping its structure and changing its content. Take Bacon's sentence: "Reading maketh a full man; conference a ready man; and writing an exact man." A student might imitate the pattern with a modern subject: "Practice makes a steady player; competition, a sharp one; and reflection, a wise one." The new sentence keeps the three balanced parts, the semicolons and the omitted verb in the second and third parts, so it borrows the aphoristic rhythm, and it applies it to a different idea.

The exercise teaches two things. It shows that a style is made of **choices you can learn**, and it shows how much of a sentence's effect comes from structure rather than from content. You are not expected to write like Bacon in an exam essay. The point is to know the options so that you can choose clear, controlled prose suited to your purpose, and to recognise what other writers are doing when you analyse them.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Style is how fancy the writing is."** Style includes plain and restrained writing, and plain writing can be very effective.
- **"Style and tone are the same."** Style is the manner (the means); tone is the attitude it produces.
- **"I should just name an adjective for the style."** Name it, then show the features and explain the effect.
- **"A writer has only one style."** Writers adjust their style for different audiences and purposes.
- **"Contractions and short sentences are always informal."** They can also be forceful in a formal speech; check the whole mix.
- **"The content of a text and its style are separate."** The style carries and shapes the meaning, as the two versions of the flood sentence show.
