## Recap

- To **concede** is to **accept all or a portion of a competing position or claim as correct**, to **agree it is correct under a different set of circumstances**, or to **acknowledge the limitations of your own argument**.
- Writers **enhance their credibility** when they concede, rebut or refute opposing arguments and contradictory evidence.
- A concession is a **strategy, not a surrender**: concede, pivot, then reassert the thesis.
- Concede what is **true and relevant**; do not give away the heart of the thesis.

## Key relationships

| Form of concession | Example (Marlbridge) |
|---|---|
| Accept a portion as correct | "Critics are right that the scheme cost more than planned." |
| Correct in other circumstances | "A ban would work where buses are reliable." |
| Acknowledge own limits | "My evidence comes from only three schools." |

| Signal words | Use |
|---|---|
| although, while, granted, admittedly | open a concession |
| but, yet, however, even so, still | pivot |
| therefore, so, thus | reassert |

## The pattern

1. **Concede**: state the opposing point accurately.
2. **Pivot**: turn back with a contrast word.
3. **Reassert**: return to the thesis, now narrower or better supported.

## Public-domain anchor

**Frederick Douglass (1852)**: "The signers of the Declaration of Independence were brave men. They were great men too." Douglass concedes the founders' greatness, a view his audience held strongly, before criticising the nation for failing their ideals. The concession wins a hearing, and the later criticism is framed as holding the nation to its own principles.

## Worked mini-example

**Thesis (Marlbridge):** "Pupils should choose their own revision methods."

- **Concede:** "Teachers are right that some pupils choose methods that do not work."
- **Pivot:** "but the answer is guidance, not compulsion."
- **Reassert:** "Pupils should choose their own methods, with teachers helping them test which ones work."

**Analysis sentence:** "By conceding that some choices are poor, the writer meets the teachers' likely objection and narrows the thesis into a safer position."

## Choosing what to concede

Ask three questions before you concede.

1. **Is it true?** Concede only what the evidence supports.
2. **Is it relevant?** Concede a point your reader is actually likely to raise, not a trivial one.
3. **Can I still hold my thesis?** If the concession removes the reason for your claim, narrow the claim first or concede something else.

In a timed essay, one clear concession of one or two sentences, followed by a pivot, is usually enough. It shows that you have weighed the other side without using up the time you need for your own evidence.

## Mistakes to avoid

1. **Conceding the heart of the thesis.**
2. **No pivot** after the concession.
3. **Conceding something untrue** to seem polite.
4. **Conceding every point.**
5. **Treating every "although" as a concession.**
6. **Placing the concession where it does not meet the reader's likely objection.**

## Quick self-check

1. What are the three forms of concession? *(Accept a portion as correct; agree it is correct under different circumstances; acknowledge limits of your own argument.)*
2. Why does conceding improve credibility? *(It shows you have understood the other side and are fair-minded.)*
3. What follows a concession in a well-built paragraph? *(A pivot and a reassertion of the writer's claim.)*
