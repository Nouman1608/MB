These are **original Marlbridge practice questions**, not past exam questions. The mark points are a suggested Marlbridge rubric to help you check your work; they are not an official scoring guideline. All countries and data are fictional. Every country here is small: its trade does not change the world price. A four-function calculator is enough.

Questions 1, 4, 5 and 6 use the towel market of **Quellmoor**, a fictional small country:

- domestic demand: Qd = 200 − 20P
- domestic supply: Qs = 20P − 40

P is the price in dollars per towel and Q is in thousand towels per month, so every surplus is in **thousands of dollars**. The world price of towels is **$4**.

## Question 1 (multiple choice · foundation)

Quellmoor opens its towel market to trade at the world price of $4. How many towels does it import each month?

- (A) 40 thousand
- (B) 80 thousand
- (C) 120 thousand
- (D) 160 thousand

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (B).** At $4, Qd = 200 − 80 = 120 and Qs = 80 − 40 = 40. Imports fill the gap: 120 − 40 = 80 thousand towels.

- (A) is domestic quantity supplied. That is what Quellmoor makes itself, not what it buys abroad.
- (C) is domestic quantity demanded. Part of it is met by Quellmoor's own producers, so it overstates imports.
- (D) adds Qd and Qs. Imports are the difference, not the sum.
</details>

## Question 2 (multiple choice · core)

Under autarky, the price of copper wire in a small country is below the world price. The country then opens to free trade. Which row is correct?

- (A) Domestic price falls; consumer surplus rises; producer surplus falls; total surplus rises.
- (B) Domestic price rises; consumer surplus falls; producer surplus rises; total surplus falls.
- (C) Domestic price rises; consumer surplus falls; producer surplus rises; total surplus rises.
- (D) Domestic price rises; consumer surplus rises; producer surplus rises; total surplus rises.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (C).** The world price is higher, so the domestic price rises to it and the country exports. Buyers pay more and buy less, so consumer surplus falls. Sellers get more and sell more, so producer surplus rises. Producers gain more than consumers lose, so total surplus rises.

- (A) describes an importing country, where the world price is below the autarky price.
- (B) gets the winners and losers right but assumes the consumers' loss outweighs the producers' gain. The gain triangle means total surplus rises.
- (D) forgets that consumers pay a higher price. Trade does not make every group better off.
</details>

## Question 3 (multiple choice · core)

A small country imports bicycles. Its government puts a per-unit tariff on imported bicycles. Which statement is correct?

- (A) The world price of bicycles rises by the amount of the tariff, and imports rise.
- (B) The domestic supply curve shifts to the left, so domestic production falls.
- (C) Domestic production rises, imports fall, and total economic surplus falls.
- (D) Consumer surplus does not change, because foreign sellers pay the tariff.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (C).** The domestic price rises to Pw + t. Domestic producers move up their supply curve and make more; buyers move up their demand curve and buy less, so imports fall. The two deadweight-loss triangles mean total surplus falls.

- (A) mixes up the world price and the domestic price. A small country cannot change the world price, and imports fall, not rise.
- (B) treats the tariff as a cost to domestic producers. It is charged only on imports, so the domestic supply curve stays put and domestic output rises along it.
- (D) assumes foreign sellers bear the tariff. In this model the domestic price rises by the full tariff, so consumer surplus falls.
</details>

## Question 4 (calculation · core)

Use the Quellmoor towel market.

(a) Find the autarky price and quantity.
(b) Calculate consumer surplus, producer surplus and total surplus under autarky.
(c) Calculate the same three values under free trade at $4.
(d) State which group gains and which loses, and find the net change in total surplus.

<details>
<summary>Worked solution</summary>

**(a)** 200 − 20P = 20P − 40, so 40P = 240, P = **$6** and Q = 200 − 120 = **80** thousand towels.

Demand meets the price axis at $10 (Qd = 0). Supply meets it at $2 (Qs = 0).

**(b)** CS = ½ × 80 × (10 − 6) = **160**. PS = ½ × 80 × (6 − 2) = **160**. Total = **320** ($ thousand).

**(c)** At $4, Qd = 120 and Qs = 40. CS = ½ × 120 × (10 − 4) = **360**. PS = ½ × 40 × (4 − 2) = **40**. Total = **400**.

**(d)** Consumers gain 360 − 160 = 200; producers lose 160 − 40 = 120. Total surplus rises by **80**. Check: the gain triangle has base 80 (imports) and height 6 − 4 = 2, so ½ × 80 × 2 = 80.

Suggested mark points (4): 1 for $6 and 80; 1 for autarky CS and PS; 1 for free-trade CS and PS; 1 for consumers gain, producers lose, net +80.

Common error: using $0 instead of $2 as the bottom of the producer surplus triangle. Supply starts at $2 here.
</details>

## Question 5 (graph · core)

Quellmoor puts a tariff of **$1 per towel** on imports. The world price stays at $4.

(a) Draw a correctly labelled graph of the Quellmoor towel market showing the world price, the price with the tariff, the quantities supplied and demanded at each price, and imports with the tariff. Shade the tariff revenue and the deadweight loss.
(b) Calculate the tariff revenue and the deadweight loss.
(c) Calculate the change in consumer surplus and in producer surplus.
(d) What is the smallest tariff that would stop all imports?

<details>
<summary>Model answer and suggested Marlbridge rubric</summary>

**(a)** Vertical axis "Price ($ per towel)"; horizontal axis "Towels (thousands per month)". Downward-sloping domestic demand D and upward-sloping domestic supply S crossing at $6 and 80. A horizontal line at Pw = $4 and a second, higher line at Pw + t = $5. At $4 mark Qs = 40 and Qd = 120; at $5 mark Qs = 60 and Qd = 100. Imports with the tariff = 100 − 60 = **40**, shown as the gap at $5. Shade the rectangle between $4 and $5 from 60 to 100 as revenue, and the two triangles beside it (from 40 to 60 and from 100 to 120, between $4 and $5) as deadweight loss.

**(b)** Revenue = 1 × 40 = **$40 thousand**. DWL = ½ × 1 × (60 − 40) + ½ × 1 × (120 − 100) = 10 + 10 = **$20 thousand**.

**(c)** CS at $5 = ½ × 100 × (10 − 5) = 250, down from 360: change **−110**. PS at $5 = ½ × 60 × (5 − 2) = 90, up from 40: change **+50**. Check: 110 = 50 + 40 + 20.

**(d)** Imports stop when the domestic price reaches the autarky price of $6. Smallest prohibitive tariff = 6 − 4 = **$2**.

| Point | What earns it |
|---|---|
| 1 | Axes labelled; D and S drawn; Pw and Pw + t shown as horizontal lines with Pw + t above Pw |
| 1 | Qs rises and Qd falls at Pw + t, with imports shown as the smaller gap |
| 1 | Revenue rectangle and both deadweight-loss triangles shaded in the correct places |
| 1 | Revenue 40 and DWL 20 |
| 1 | CS −110 and PS +50, and the $2 prohibitive tariff |

Do not award the first point if the domestic supply curve is shifted instead of drawing a new price line.
</details>

## Question 6 (constructed response · core)

Instead of a tariff, Quellmoor sets an import quota of **60 thousand towels** per month. The world price stays at $4. You do not need to draw a graph.

(a) Explain why the domestic price rises, and find the new domestic price.
(b) What happens to the quantity of towels produced in Quellmoor? Explain.
(c) Explain how consumer surplus and total surplus change compared with free trade.
(d) A tariff of $0.50 per towel would give the same domestic price. Identify one difference between that tariff and the quota.
(e) Would a quota of 90 thousand towels change anything? Explain.

<details>
<summary>Model answer and suggested Marlbridge rubric</summary>

**(a)** At $4, buyers want 80 thousand more towels than domestic firms supply, but only 60 thousand may be imported. The shortage pushes the price up until Qd − Qs = 60. (200 − 20P) − (20P − 40) = 60 gives 40P = 180, so P = **$4.50**. Check: Qd = 110, Qs = 50, gap = 60.

**(b)** Domestic production **rises** from 40 to **50** thousand towels. The higher price makes domestic producers move up their supply curve.

**(c)** Buyers pay more ($4.50 rather than $4) and buy less (110 rather than 120), so **consumer surplus falls** (by 57.5 if calculated). **Total surplus falls**: towels are now made at home at a cost above the world price, and some buyers who valued towels above $4 no longer buy them. These are deadweight losses.

**(d)** The tariff earns the government 0.50 × 60 = **$30 thousand** a month; the quota earns the government **nothing**. (Under the quota, that gap between the domestic and world price on imported towels goes to whoever holds the right to import.)

**(e)** **No.** Free-trade imports are only 80 thousand. A limit of 90 thousand does not bind, so the price stays at $4.

| Point | What earns it |
|---|---|
| 1 | Shortage at $4 explained **and** price $4.50 found |
| 1 | Domestic output rises to 50, linked to the higher price |
| 1 | CS falls and total surplus falls, with a reason |
| 1 | Tariff raises revenue, the quota does not |
| 1 | 90 thousand quota does not bind because free-trade imports are 80 |
</details>

## Question 7 (explanation · stretch)

Quellmoor's apple market: Qd = 90 − 10P and Qs = 10P − 10, with P in dollars per box and Q in thousand boxes per month. The world price is **$7 per box**.

(a) Find the autarky price and show that Quellmoor will export apples.
(b) Calculate exports at the world price.
(c) Calculate the changes in consumer, producer and total surplus.
(d) A newspaper writes: "Opening the apple market harms Quellmoor, because families now pay more for apples." Evaluate this claim.

<details>
<summary>Model answer and suggested Marlbridge rubric</summary>

**(a)** 90 − 10P = 10P − 10, so P = **$5** and Q = 40. The world price of $7 is above $5, so domestic sellers can earn more abroad: Quellmoor exports.

**(b)** At $7: Qd = 90 − 70 = 20; Qs = 70 − 10 = 60. Exports = 60 − 20 = **40** thousand boxes.

**(c)** Demand meets the price axis at $9; supply at $1.

- CS: ½ × 40 × (9 − 5) = 80 → ½ × 20 × (9 − 7) = 20: change **−60**.
- PS: ½ × 40 × (5 − 1) = 80 → ½ × 60 × (7 − 1) = 180: change **+100**.
- Total surplus: 160 → 200: change **+40** ($ thousand). Check: ½ × 40 × (7 − 5) = 40.

**(d)** The claim is **partly right**. Families do pay more and their surplus falls by 60. But apple growers gain 100, and 60 of that is simply surplus moved from buyers to sellers inside Quellmoor. The extra 40 is a real gain: Quellmoor now sells abroad, at $7 a box, apples that cost its growers less than $7 to produce, and the boxes its own families stop buying were valued by them at less than $7. So the country as a whole is better off; the trade creates winners and losers rather than harming the country. A fair conclusion names both groups and the net gain.

| Point | What earns it |
|---|---|
| 1 | Autarky price $5 compared with $7 to show exports |
| 1 | Exports = 40 |
| 1 | CS −60 and PS +100 |
| 1 | Total surplus +40 |
| 1 | Evaluation accepts the consumer loss **and** explains the larger producer gain, concluding total surplus rises |
</details>
