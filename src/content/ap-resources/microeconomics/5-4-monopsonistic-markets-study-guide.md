## What a monopsony is

A **monopsony** is a market with **one buyer**. In a labour market, it is a firm that is the **only employer** (or by far the largest employer) of a type of worker. Think of a single mine in an isolated town, or a hospital that employs almost every nurse in a remote region.

Its key features are:

1. **One employer** of this type of labour in the market.
2. **Workers have few alternatives.** Moving to another town or retraining for another job is slow or costly.
3. **The firm is a wage setter, not a wage taker.** Because it is the whole market, the supply curve it faces is the **market supply curve of labour**, which slopes upward.

Compare this with Topic 5.3. A wage taker faces a horizontal supply line: it can hire more workers at the same wage. A monopsony faces the upward-sloping market supply. To attract more workers, it must **raise the wage**. In this topic we assume the firm pays **the same wage to every worker** it hires.

## Why MRC is above the wage

The **marginal resource cost (MRC)**, also called marginal factor cost, is the extra cost of hiring one more worker: change in total labour cost ÷ change in labour.

Suppose 3 workers will work for $70 a day each, but a 4th worker will only come for $80. Because everyone gets the same wage, the firm must also raise the 3 existing workers from $70 to $80. Hiring the 4th worker adds:

- the new worker's wage: **$80**;
- plus the raise for the existing workers: 3 × $10 = **$30**.

So MRC = $80 + $30 = **$110**, even though the wage is only $80.

This is true for every worker after the first, so **MRC > wage** (the supply price of labour). On a graph, the **MRC curve lies above the supply curve** and rises more steeply. For a straight-line supply curve W = a + bL, the MRC line is **MRC = a + 2bL**: the same vertical intercept and **twice the slope**.

## The hiring rule: MRC = MRP, then read the wage from supply

A monopsony still maximises profit by comparing the extra revenue and the extra cost of each worker:

- **MRP > MRC:** hiring the worker raises profit. **Hire.**
- **MRP < MRC:** hiring the worker lowers profit. **Do not hire.**

So it hires up to the quantity where **MRC = MRP**. This is the same rule as in Topic 5.3. The difference is that MRC is no longer equal to the wage.

The **wage** is then read from the **supply curve** at that quantity. The supply curve shows the lowest wage at which that many people will work, so there is no reason to pay more.

Two steps, in order:

1. **Quantity:** go to where **MRC = MRP**, then down to the horizontal axis (Lm).
2. **Wage:** from Lm, go up to the **supply curve**, then across to the vertical axis (Wm).

A common error is to read the wage at the MRC = MRP point. That point is **above** the wage.

To draw the graph:

- Vertical axis: wage (and MRC, MRP) in dollars per period. Horizontal axis: quantity of labour.
- **S**: upward-sloping market supply of labour. This is also the firm's average cost of labour per worker.
- **MRC**: above S and steeper, starting from the same point on the vertical axis.
- **MRP = D**: downward-sloping; it is the firm's demand for labour.
- Mark **Lm** below the MRC = MRP point, and **Wm** on the S curve at Lm.

## Monopsony compared with a competitive labour market

If the same labour market were perfectly competitive, the wage and quantity would be set where **market supply meets labour demand**, S = MRP (point Lc, Wc in Figure 1).

| Outcome | Monopsony | Perfectly competitive labour market |
|---|---|---|
| Hiring rule | MRC = MRP | MRC = W, so S = MRP for the market |
| Supply curve facing the firm | upward-sloping market supply | horizontal at the market wage |
| MRC compared with wage | MRC > W | MRC = W |
| Employment | lower (Lm) | higher (Lc) |
| Wage | lower (Wm) | higher (Wc) |
| Wage compared with MRP of last worker | W < MRP | W = MRP |

Why the monopsony stops early: workers between Lm and Lc have an MRP **above** the wage they would accept, so hiring them would create value. But to hire them, the monopsony would have to raise the wage for **all** its workers. That extra cost makes the hire unprofitable for the firm. The value those jobs would have created is lost: a **deadweight loss**, shown by the triangle between MRP and S from Lm to Lc. The monopsony's outcome is therefore **inefficient** (you will study market inefficiency in Unit 6).

## Worked example 1: Brackwater Mill hires weavers

**Question.** Brackwater Mill is the only employer of weavers in a remote valley. It pays all weavers the same daily wage. The table shows the wage needed to attract each number of weavers and each weaver's MRP.

| Weavers | Wage needed ($ per day) | MRP ($ per day) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 50 | 125 |
| 2 | 60 | 120 |
| 3 | 70 | 115 |
| 4 | 80 | 110 |
| 5 | 90 | 105 |
| 6 | 100 | 100 |
| 7 | 110 | 95 |

(a) Calculate total labour cost (TLC) and MRC for each number of weavers.
(b) How many weavers does the mill hire, and what wage does it pay?
(c) What would the outcome be in a competitive labour market?
(d) At the monopsony quantity, compare the last weaver's MRP with the wage.

**(a)** TLC = wage × number of weavers. MRC = change in TLC.

| Weavers | Wage ($) | TLC ($) | MRC ($) | MRP ($) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 50 | 50 | 50 | 125 |
| 2 | 60 | 120 | 70 | 120 |
| 3 | 70 | 210 | 90 | 115 |
| 4 | 80 | 320 | 110 | 110 |
| 5 | 90 | 450 | 130 | 105 |
| 6 | 100 | 600 | 150 | 100 |
| 7 | 110 | 770 | 170 | 95 |

Check the 4th weaver: new wage $80 plus a $10 raise for 3 existing weavers = 80 + 30 = $110. ✓ This matches 320 − 210.

**(b)** Compare MRP with MRC. For weavers 1 to 3, MRP is above MRC. For the 4th, MRP = MRC = $110. For the 5th, MRP ($105) is below MRC ($130). The mill hires **4 weavers**. From the supply schedule, 4 weavers will work for **$80 per day**, so that is the wage.

**(c)** In a competitive market, employment rises until the wage equals MRP. At 6 weavers, MRP = $100 = the wage needed. The 7th weaver's MRP ($95) is below the $110 wage needed. So **6 weavers at $100 per day**: more workers and a higher wage.

**(d)** At 4 weavers, MRP = $110 but the wage is $80. Each weaver is paid **$30 less** than the revenue the last one adds. The 5th and 6th weavers have MRPs ($105 and $100) above the wages they would accept ($90 and $100), but the mill does not hire them because their MRC ($130 and $150) is higher still.

**Interpretation.** The mill's labour cost at 4 weavers is $320 against $470 of revenue from their MRPs (125 + 120 + 115 + 110), leaving $150 a day. At 6 weavers it would be $600 against $675, leaving only $75. Hiring fewer workers at a lower wage is more profitable for the mill.

## Worked example 2: Dunmere Quarry with equations

**Question.** Dunmere Quarry is the only employer in an isolated town. Labour supply is W = 4 + 0.5L and the MRP of labour is MRP = 22 − 0.5L, where W and MRP are in dollars per hour and L is the number of workers.

(a) Write the MRC equation.
(b) Find the monopsony's employment and wage.
(c) Find the competitive employment and wage.
(d) Calculate the deadweight loss.

**(a)** The supply curve has intercept 4 and slope 0.5. MRC has the same intercept and twice the slope: **MRC = 4 + L**.

Check with total labour cost, TLC = W × L. At 12 workers, TLC = 12 × (4 + 6) = $120 per hour. At 13 workers, TLC = 13 × (4 + 6.5) = $136.50. The 13th worker adds $16.50, which is what MRC = 4 + L gives halfway between 12 and 13 (4 + 12.5 = 16.5). ✓

**(b)** Set MRC = MRP: 4 + L = 22 − 0.5L, so 1.5L = 18 and **Lm = 12 workers**. The wage comes from the supply curve: W = 4 + 0.5 × 12 = **$10 per hour**. At L = 12, MRC = MRP = $16, which is $6 above the wage.

**(c)** Set S = MRP: 4 + 0.5L = 22 − 0.5L, so L = 18 and W = 4 + 9 = **$13 per hour**. The competitive market would employ **18 workers at $13**.

**(d)** The deadweight loss is the triangle between MRP and S from L = 12 to L = 18. Its height at L = 12 is 16 − 10 = $6; its base is 18 − 12 = 6 workers. DWL = ½ × 6 × 6 = **$18 per hour**.

**Check.** The monopsony outcome has a lower wage ($10 < $13) and fewer workers (12 < 18) than the competitive outcome, as it should. Figure 1 shows these numbers.

## Background: a minimum wage in a monopsony

This section goes slightly beyond the topic's core statements, but it uses the same tools and is a common extension.

Suppose Dunmere's town sets a minimum wage of **$13 per hour**. Up to 18 workers are willing to work at $13, so the quarry can now hire any of them at $13: its MRC is a flat $13 up to 18 workers. It hires where MRP = $13, which is at **L = 18**. Both the wage (from $10 to $13) and employment (from 12 to 18) **rise**.

This only works within a range. A minimum wage of $12 also raises employment, to 16 (the number willing to work at $12). A minimum wage above $16 (the MRP at Lm) leads the quarry to hire **fewer** than 12. For example, at $18 the quarry hires only 8, where MRP = $18.

## Common misconceptions

- **"The monopsony pays the wage where MRC = MRP."** That point gives the **quantity**. The wage is read from the **supply curve** at that quantity, and it is lower.
- **"MRC equals the wage, as in Topic 5.3."** Only for a wage taker. A monopsony must raise everyone's wage to hire one more worker, so MRC > W.
- **"Monopsony means one seller."** That is monopoly. Monopsony means one **buyer**.
- **"A monopsony hires more workers to get them cheaply."** It hires **fewer** workers than a competitive market, which is how it keeps the wage low.
- **"MRC and supply start at different points."** For a straight-line supply curve, they share the vertical intercept; MRC is twice as steep.
- **"The monopsony's demand curve is the MRC curve."** Its demand for labour is its **MRP** curve. MRC is a cost curve.
- **"In a monopsony, workers are paid their MRP."** They are paid less than the MRP of the last worker hired.
