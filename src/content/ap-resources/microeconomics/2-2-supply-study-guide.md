## From buyers to sellers

In Topic 2.1 you studied buyers. Now look at the other side of the market: the **sellers**, or producers. Like buyers, sellers respond to **incentives**. A higher price rewards producing more. A tax per unit sold is a penalty. A subsidy per unit is a reward. Sellers also face constraints, above all the **cost** of the resources they use and the **technology** they have.

Throughout this guide we use **Fenmoor Valley**, a fictional farming area. Three farms grow strawberries and sell them by the crate:

- **Hillside**, a family farm on well-drained slopes;
- **Riverside**, a newer farm on heavier soil;
- **Orchard End**, a large farm with good, sheltered fields.

## What supply means

**Supply** is the quantity of a good that sellers are **willing and able** to offer for sale at each possible price, during a given period, with all other influences held constant.

- **Willing and able:** a farm must want to sell at that price **and** be able to produce the crop.
- **At each possible price:** supply is a list of price-quantity pairs (a **supply schedule**) or a whole curve.
- **Other influences held constant:** we change only the good's own price.

**Quantity supplied** is one number: the amount offered at one particular price.

## The law of supply

The **law of supply** says that, other things equal, when the price of a good rises, the quantity supplied rises; when the price falls, the quantity supplied falls. Price and quantity supplied move in the **same** direction.

On a graph with price on the vertical axis and quantity on the horizontal axis, the supply curve **slopes upward**. A change in the good's own price moves sellers **along** the curve.

## Why the supply curve slopes upward

**Marginal cost (MC)** is the extra cost of producing one more unit. For most producers, MC **rises** as output rises. You met the reason in Topic 1.3: resources are not equally suited to every task. Hillside first picks the easy, sunny rows with its own family workers. To grow more, it must use shadier rows, pay overtime and hire pickers who are less experienced. Each extra crate costs more than the one before.

Hillside grows strawberries in blocks of 20 crates. The marginal cost per crate for each block is:

| Block of 20 crates | 1st | 2nd | 3rd | 4th | 5th | 6th |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Hillside's MC ($ per crate) | 8 | 13 | 18 | 23 | 28 | 33 |

A seller produces an extra block when the price **covers its marginal cost** (price ≥ MC), because then the extra block adds at least as much to revenue as to cost. So:

- at $10 per crate, Hillside produces only the 1st block (MC $8): **20 crates**;
- at $15, it adds the 2nd block (MC $13): **40 crates**;
- at $20, it adds the 3rd block (MC $18): **60 crates**.

A higher price makes higher-cost output worth producing. That is the **profit incentive** behind the law of supply, and it is why the supply curve slopes upward. You can read a supply curve the other way too: its height at a quantity is the **lowest price** sellers would accept for that last unit, which is its marginal cost. You will study this link in detail in Unit 3.

Doing the same for every farm gives the **individual supply schedules** (crates per week):

| Price per crate | Hillside | Riverside | Orchard End |
|---|---|---|---|
| $10 | 20 | 0 | 40 |
| $15 | 40 | 20 | 60 |
| $20 | 60 | 40 | 80 |
| $25 | 80 | 60 | 100 |
| $30 | 100 | 80 | 120 |

Riverside supplies nothing at $10, because its lowest marginal cost is $12 per crate. A low price keeps high-cost sellers out of the market.

## Worked example 1: market supply and a movement along the curve

**Question.** Use the three supply schedules.
(a) Build the market supply schedule for Fenmoor Valley strawberries.
(b) The price rises from $15 to $25 per crate. Find the change in quantity supplied and show it on a graph.
(c) Explain Hillside's response using marginal cost.

**(a)** Market supply is the **sum** of the individual quantities supplied **at each price**:

| Price per crate | Hillside + Riverside + Orchard End | Market quantity supplied (crates per week) |
|---|---|---|
| $10 | 20 + 0 + 40 | **60** |
| $15 | 40 + 20 + 60 | **120** |
| $20 | 60 + 40 + 80 | **180** |
| $25 | 80 + 60 + 100 | **240** |
| $30 | 100 + 80 + 120 | **300** |

As with demand, this is **horizontal summation**: add quantities at the same price.

**(b)** At $15, quantity supplied is 120 crates. At $25, it is 240 crates. Change = 240 − 120 = **+120 crates per week**.

Only the own price changed, so the curve does **not** move. Sellers move **along** S from point a to point b. This is an **increase in quantity supplied**, not an increase in supply.

**(c)** At $15, Hillside produces the blocks whose MC is at most $15: the 1st ($8) and 2nd ($13), so 40 crates. At $25, the 3rd ($18) and 4th ($23) blocks are now worth producing, so it supplies 80 crates. The 5th block (MC $28) still costs more than the price, so Hillside stops there.

**Check.** The market schedule rises by 60 crates for every $5, so it is a straight line: quantity supplied = 12 × price − 60. At $25: 300 − 60 = 240. It reaches zero at $5, below every farm's lowest marginal cost, so no strawberries are offered at or below that price.

## Change in quantity supplied versus change in supply

| | Change in **quantity supplied** | Change in **supply** |
|---|---|---|
| Caused by | the good's **own price** | any **determinant** of supply (not own price) |
| On the graph | **movement along** the existing curve | the **whole curve shifts** |
| Words to use | "quantity supplied rises / falls" | "supply increases / decreases" |

An **increase in supply** shifts the curve **to the right**: more is offered at every price, or, reading up, each quantity is offered at a lower price. A **decrease in supply** shifts it **to the left** (up).

## The determinants of supply

These are the influences, other than own price, that shift supply. Most of them work by changing **costs** or the **incentive** to sell.

- **Prices of inputs (resources).** Higher wages, rent, fuel or raw-material prices raise costs and **decrease** supply. Cheaper inputs **increase** it.
- **Technology and productivity.** Better methods produce more from the same resources, lowering cost per unit, so supply **increases**.
- **Prices of other goods the seller could produce.** If the price of an alternative product rises, sellers move resources towards it, so supply of this good **decreases**. (If a Fenmoor farm can grow raspberries in the same fields, a rise in the raspberry price reduces strawberry supply.)
- **Taxes and subsidies.** A tax per unit sold acts like an extra cost and **decreases** supply. A subsidy per unit lowers the effective cost and **increases** supply. Costly regulations also decrease supply.
- **Expectations.** If sellers of a good that can be stored expect its price to rise soon, they may hold some back now, so current supply **decreases**.
- **Number of sellers.** More firms in the market **increase** market supply.
- **Natural conditions.** For farm goods especially, good weather increases supply; floods, drought or disease decrease it.

## Worked example 2: shifting the supply curve

**Question.** Start from the market schedule in Worked example 1 (call it S₁). Treat each event separately.
(a) A new picking machine lowers every farm's marginal cost of every block by $5 per crate. Find the new schedule S₂.
(b) Pickers' wages rise, raising every farm's marginal cost of every block by $5 per crate. Find the new schedule S₃.
(c) Compare quantity supplied at $20 on S₁, S₂ and S₃, and the price sellers need for 180 crates on each curve.

**(a)** With MC $5 lower, each farm now supplies at any price what it used to supply at a price $5 **higher**. For example, Hillside's blocks now cost $3, $8, $13, $18…, so at $20 it produces 4 blocks (80 crates) instead of 3. Adding the farms:

| Price | S₁ | S₂ (new machine) | S₃ (higher wages) |
|---|---|---|---|
| $10 | 60 | **120** | **0** |
| $15 | 120 | **180** | **60** |
| $20 | 180 | **240** | **120** |
| $25 | 240 | **300** | **180** |
| $30 | 300 | **360** | **240** |

**(b)** With MC $5 higher, each farm supplies at any price what it used to supply at a price $5 **lower**. At $10, every farm's lowest marginal cost is now above $10 (Hillside $13, Riverside $17, Orchard End $11), so nothing is offered. The S₃ column is S₁ moved one row down.

**(c)** At $20: S₁ gives 180 crates, S₂ gives 240 (60 more) and S₃ gives 120 (60 fewer). Reading up instead, 180 crates need a price of $20 on S₁, only $15 on S₂ and $25 on S₃. Technology **increased** supply (shift right, or down by $5). Higher wages **decreased** supply (shift left, or up by $5).

## Common misconceptions

- **"A higher price increases supply."** A higher own price increases **quantity supplied**: a movement along the curve. Supply itself does not change.
- **"A decrease in supply shifts the curve down."** It shifts it **left**, which is also **up**: sellers need a higher price for each quantity.
- **"Firms supply more at a higher price because buyers want more."** The reason is cost: a higher price covers the rising marginal cost of extra output. Buyers want **less** at a higher price.
- **"A tax on sellers shifts demand."** A per-unit tax on sellers raises their costs, so it **decreases supply**.
- **"Higher demand increases supply."** A change in demand shifts the demand curve only. Sellers may then sell more, but that is a movement along the supply curve (Topic 2.7).
- **"Market supply is the average of the firms' supply."** It is the **sum** of quantities supplied at each price.
- **"Better technology for another product increases supply of this one."** If the other product becomes more profitable, resources move towards it, so supply of this good may **decrease**.
