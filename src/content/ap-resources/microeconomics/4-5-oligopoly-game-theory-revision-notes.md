## Recap

- An **oligopoly** has a **few large firms**, **high barriers to entry** and **interdependence**: each firm's best choice depends on its rivals' choices. A two-firm oligopoly is a **duopoly**.
- Products can be identical (cement) or differentiated (cars).
- Oligopoly is **inefficient**: price is usually above marginal cost, so there is a deadweight loss.
- Firms have an incentive to **collude** and form a **cartel** that acts like one monopoly. But each member gains by **cheating**, cheating is hard to detect, and collusion is illegal in many places, so cartels tend to break down.
- Result: oligopoly prices are usually **higher** and quantities **lower** than in perfect competition, but rarely as extreme as monopoly.

## Key relationships

| Term | Meaning | How to spot it in a matrix |
|---|---|---|
| Payoff matrix (normal form) | Rows = one player's actions, columns = the other's; each cell shows both payoffs | First number = row player, second = column player |
| Dominant strategy | One action is better whatever the other player does | The same action wins in **both** columns (row player) or **both** rows (column player) |
| Nash equilibrium | No player can raise its payoff by changing its own action alone | Both payoffs in the cell are best responses |
| Prisoner's dilemma | Dominant strategies lead to an outcome worse for both than cooperation | Equilibrium cell is beaten by another cell for **both** players |
| Incentive to change a dominant strategy | A payment to one action, or a penalty on the other, that reverses every comparison | Must **exceed the largest gap** across the other player's actions |

## Assumptions and scope

- Two players, two actions each, one play, simultaneous choices.
- Games with more players or actions, game trees and mixed strategies are outside the course.

## Mistakes to avoid

1. Picking the cell with the **highest total** as the Nash equilibrium.
2. Comparing one player's payoff with the **other player's** payoff.
3. Comparing across the diagonal instead of **within** one column or row.
4. Saying there is no equilibrium because one player lacks a dominant strategy.
5. Closing only the **smaller** payoff gap when finding the incentive needed.
6. Saying oligopolies always reach the monopoly price.

## Quick self-check

Payoffs (Row, Column): (Up, Up) = (5, 5); (Up, Down) = (1, 7); (Down, Up) = (7, 1); (Down, Down) = (3, 3).

1. What is Row's dominant strategy? *(Down: 7 > 5 and 3 > 1.)*
2. What is the Nash equilibrium? *(Down, Down) with (3, 3); Column's dominant strategy is also Down.*
3. Is this a prisoner's dilemma? *(Yes: both would prefer (Up, Up) with 5 each.)*
4. How much would a reward for choosing Up need to exceed to make Up dominant for Row? *(2, the larger of the gaps 7 − 5 = 2 and 3 − 1 = 2; here they are equal.)*
