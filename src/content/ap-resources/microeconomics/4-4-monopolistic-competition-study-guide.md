## A market between two extremes

Think of the cafés, hairdressers, restaurants or clothing shops in a large town. There are many of them, and opening a new one is not hard. But each one is a little different: its location, its menu, its style, its service. That combination is called **monopolistic competition**. It has features of both perfect competition and monopoly.

| Feature | Monopolistic competition |
|---|---|
| Number of firms | many, each small relative to the market |
| Product | **differentiated**: similar, but not identical (close substitutes) |
| Entry and exit | **easy**: low barriers to entry in the long run |
| Each firm's demand curve | **downward-sloping**, but quite elastic, because rivals sell close substitutes |
| Price-setting | each firm has a little market power over its own product |
| Non-price competition | common: advertising, branding, quality, location, service |

Because the product is differentiated, a café that raises its price a little loses some customers but not all of them. Its demand curve slopes down, so like a monopolist it must lower its price to sell more, and its **MR curve lies below its demand curve**. But because there are many close substitutes, that demand is fairly **elastic**.

## Differentiation and advertising

Firms try to make their product stand out. They change the product itself (recipes, design, quality), where and when they sell it (location, opening hours), the service around it, and how buyers see it (brand name, packaging, **advertising**).

Advertising has two aims:

1. **Raise demand** for this firm's product (shift its demand curve to the right).
2. **Make demand less elastic** by persuading buyers that rivals' products are poorer substitutes, so the firm can charge more without losing many customers.

Advertising also has a cost. It is usually a fixed cost (it does not depend on how many units are sold), so it raises **ATC** but does not change **MC**. An advertising campaign pays only if the extra revenue is larger than its cost. And in the long run, if advertising makes profits, rivals copy it and new firms enter, so the profit does not last.

## The short run: profit, loss or zero

In the short run the number of firms is fixed. Each firm uses the same rule as any other firm:

1. Produce where **MR = MC**.
2. Read the price **up to the demand curve** at that quantity.
3. Compare price with **ATC** at that quantity:
   - P > ATC: **economic profit** = (P − ATC) × Q;
   - P < ATC: **economic loss** = (ATC − P) × Q;
   - P = ATC: **zero economic profit** (a normal profit).

A firm making a loss keeps producing in the short run if price covers average variable cost (P ≥ AVC), because it then loses no more than its fixed cost (which it would lose anyway by shutting down). If P < AVC at every output, it shuts down in the short run.

The short-run graph looks just like the monopoly graph from Topic 4.2. The difference shows up in the long run.

## Worked example 1: a café from short run to long run

**Question.** Kettle & Fern is one of many cafés in the fictional town of Brackwater. Its coffee is a little different from its rivals'. Its costs are TC = 200 + Q + 0.005Q² (Q = cups per day, in dollars), so MC = 1 + 0.01Q. Its demand is P = 7.4 − 0.015Q, so MR = 7.4 − 0.03Q.

(a) Find its profit-maximising output and price, and its economic profit or loss.
(b) Explain what happens in the long run.
(c) In the long run its demand becomes P = 5 − 0.015Q. Find the new output, price and profit, and show that the new demand curve just touches ATC.

**(a)**

1. Set MR = MC: 7.4 − 0.03Q = 1 + 0.01Q, so 6.4 = 0.04Q and **Q = 160 cups per day**.
2. Price from demand: P = 7.4 − 0.015 × 160 = 7.4 − 2.4 = **$5.00**.
3. ATC at 160 = 200 ÷ 160 + 1 + 0.005 × 160 = 1.25 + 1 + 0.80 = **$3.05**.
4. Economic profit = (5.00 − 3.05) × 160 = **$312 per day**.

**(b)** Cafés in Brackwater are earning economic profits, and entry is easy. New cafés open. Each new café takes some of Kettle & Fern's customers, and gives the remaining ones more close substitutes. So Kettle & Fern's demand curve **shifts left** and becomes **more elastic**. Entry continues until there is no economic profit left to attract new firms.

**(c)**

1. New MR = 5 − 0.03Q. Set MR = MC: 5 − 0.03Q = 1 + 0.01Q, so **Q = 100 cups per day**.
2. P = 5 − 0.015 × 100 = **$3.50**.
3. ATC at 100 = 200 ÷ 100 + 1 + 0.005 × 100 = 2 + 1 + 0.5 = **$3.50**.
4. Economic profit = (3.50 − 3.50) × 100 = **$0**.

**Check.** At Q = 100 the demand curve and the ATC curve meet at $3.50, and both have the same slope there (each falls by $0.015 per extra cup), so demand **touches** ATC without crossing it. At every other quantity ATC is above demand, so no output can earn a profit. This tangency is the long-run equilibrium.

## The long run: zero economic profit

Free entry and exit drive every firm to zero economic profit:

- **Profits** → firms enter → each firm's demand shifts left (and becomes more elastic) → profit falls until P = ATC.
- **Losses** → some firms leave → the customers of those firms go to the remaining firms → each remaining firm's demand shifts right (and becomes less elastic) → the loss shrinks until P = ATC.

In long-run equilibrium, at the quantity where MR = MC, the **demand curve is tangent to the ATC curve**, so P = ATC.

## Why the long run is inefficient

The tangency must happen on the **downward-sloping part of ATC**, because the demand curve slopes down. That has two results.

**1. Excess capacity (not productively efficient).** The firm produces **less than the quantity that minimises ATC**. Kettle & Fern makes 100 cups, but its ATC would be lowest ($3.00) at 200 cups. The gap of 100 cups is its **excess capacity**. Each cup costs more to make than it would at minimum ATC ($3.50 rather than $3.00).

**2. Allocative inefficiency.** **Price is greater than marginal cost**: $3.50 > $2.00. Some buyers value an extra cup at more than the $2.00 it costs to make, but they do not get it. Kettle & Fern's demand curve is above its MC curve from 100 cups until they meet at 160 cups ($2.60). The **deadweight loss** is the triangle between them:

DWL = ½ × (160 − 100) × (3.50 − 2.00) = ½ × 60 × 1.50 = **$45 per day**.

You can find the other areas in the same way. **Consumer surplus** is the triangle above the price and below demand: ½ × 100 × (5.00 − 3.50) = **$75 per day**. **Producer surplus** is revenue minus variable cost: 3.50 × 100 − (1 × 100 + 0.005 × 100²) = 350 − 150 = **$200 per day**. That is exactly the $200 fixed cost, which is another way to see that economic profit is zero.

There is a balancing point. Excess capacity and a price above minimum ATC can be seen as the **cost of variety**. Buyers get many slightly different cafés, menus and locations to choose from, which they may value.

| Long run | Perfect competition | Monopolistic competition | Monopoly |
|---|---|---|---|
| Economic profit | zero | **zero** | can be positive (barriers to entry) |
| Price and MC | P = MC | **P > MC** | P > MC |
| Price and ATC | P = minimum ATC | **P = ATC, above minimum** | P can exceed ATC |
| Productively efficient? | yes | **no** (excess capacity) | usually no |
| Allocatively efficient? | yes | **no** (deadweight loss) | no |

## Worked example 2: a barber's loss, from a table

**Question.** Tidewell Barbers is one of many barbers in a fictional city. Its fixed costs are $450 per day. The table shows its demand and total cost.

| Haircuts per day (Q) | 10 | 20 | 30 | 40 | 50 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Price ($) | 27 | 24 | 21 | 18 | 15 |
| Total cost ($) | 520 | 610 | 720 | 850 | 1,000 |

(a) Find the profit-maximising output and the profit or loss.
(b) Should Tidewell stay open in the short run?
(c) Explain what happens in the long run.

**(a)** Work out total revenue (TR = P × Q) and profit (TR − TC).

| Q | 10 | 20 | 30 | 40 | 50 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| TR ($) | 270 | 480 | 630 | 720 | 750 |
| Profit ($) | −250 | −130 | **−90** | −130 | −250 |

The best Tidewell can do is **30 haircuts at $21**, with a **loss of $90 per day**. Check with marginal values: from 20 to 30 haircuts, MR = (630 − 480) ÷ 10 = $15 per haircut and MC = (720 − 610) ÷ 10 = $11, so expanding pays. From 30 to 40, MR = $9 and MC = $13, so it does not.

**(b)** At 30 haircuts, variable cost = 720 − 450 = $270, so AVC = 270 ÷ 30 = **$9**. Price ($21) is above AVC, so Tidewell covers its variable costs and has $360 left towards its $450 of fixed costs. Staying open loses $90; shutting down would lose all $450 of fixed costs. **It should stay open in the short run.**

**(c)** Barbers in the city are making losses, so in the long run some of them **leave** the market. Their customers move to the barbers that remain. Tidewell's demand shifts **right** and becomes less elastic, until price equals ATC at the profit-maximising output. For example, if Tidewell's demand became P = 36 − 0.4Q, it would cut 30 haircuts at **$24**, and its ATC at 30 haircuts is 720 ÷ 30 = **$24**, so economic profit would be zero. Its output of 30 would still be below the output that minimises ATC (about 67 haircuts per day), so excess capacity remains.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Monopolistically competitive firms make long-run profits because they have market power."** Their market power is small and entry is free. New firms compete away any economic profit.
- **"In the long run, the firm produces at minimum ATC, like a perfectly competitive firm."** The demand curve slopes down, so it touches ATC to the **left** of the minimum. That is excess capacity.
- **"The long-run tangency is where MC crosses ATC."** MC crosses ATC at the minimum of ATC. The tangency is between **demand** and ATC, at a smaller quantity.
- **"Zero economic profit means the owners earn nothing."** It means a normal profit: revenue covers every cost, including the owners' opportunity cost.
- **"Entry shifts the market supply curve, so the firm's demand stays the same."** In this model, entry moves each firm's own **demand** curve to the left, because customers are shared among more firms.
- **"Advertising shifts the MC curve up."** Advertising is usually a fixed cost, so it raises ATC, not MC.
- **"Reading the price from MR."** As in monopoly, the price comes from the demand curve above the MR = MC quantity.
