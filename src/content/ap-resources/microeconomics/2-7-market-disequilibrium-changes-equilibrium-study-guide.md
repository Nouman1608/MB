## From a resting point to a moving one

In Topic 2.6 you found the **equilibrium**: the price at which the quantity buyers want to buy equals the quantity sellers want to sell. This topic asks two questions:

1. What happens when the price is **not** at equilibrium?
2. What happens when something changes demand or supply, so that the equilibrium itself **moves**?

Throughout this guide we use the fish market in **Port Ellery**, a fictional harbour town. Each day, local boats sell mackerel to restaurants and shops, by the crate. The daily market is:

- Demand: **Qd = 200 − 5P**
- Supply: **Qs = 10P − 100**

where P is the price in dollars per crate and Q is crates per day.

| Price ($ per crate) | 10 | 15 | 20 | 25 | 30 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Quantity demanded | 150 | 125 | **100** | 75 | 50 |
| Quantity supplied | 0 | 50 | **100** | 150 | 200 |

At $20, both quantities are 100 crates. So the equilibrium price is **$20** and the equilibrium quantity is **100 crates per day**. Setting 200 − 5P = 10P − 100 gives 15P = 300, so P = 20, which confirms the table.

At equilibrium, consumer surplus is ½ × 100 × (40 − 20) = **$1,000** per day. Producer surplus is ½ × 100 × (20 − 10) = **$500**. Total surplus is **$1,500**. (The demand curve meets the price axis at $40 and the supply curve at $10.)

## Shortages and surpluses

When the price is not at equilibrium, the two quantities differ.

- A **shortage** (excess demand) exists when **quantity demanded is greater than quantity supplied**. This happens at any price **below** equilibrium. Size of shortage = Qd − Qs.
- A **surplus** (excess supply) exists when **quantity supplied is greater than quantity demanded**. This happens at any price **above** equilibrium. Size of surplus = Qs − Qd.

In Port Ellery:

- At **$30**, sellers bring 200 crates but buyers want only 50. There is a **surplus of 150 crates**.
- At **$15**, buyers want 125 crates but sellers bring only 50. There is a **shortage of 75 crates**.

Notice the size of a shortage or surplus is a horizontal distance on the graph. It is measured in **units of the good**, not in dollars.

## How market forces restore equilibrium

A competitive market corrects itself because the shortage or surplus changes what buyers and sellers do.

**Surplus (price too high).** At $30, boats return with unsold fish. Sellers who want to sell their catch cut their prices. As the price falls, two things happen at once: quantity demanded **rises** (a movement down along the demand curve) and quantity supplied **falls** (a movement down along the supply curve). The surplus shrinks until it disappears at $20.

**Shortage (price too low).** At $15, restaurants find there is not enough fish. Some offer more than $15 to make sure they get a crate. As the price rises, quantity supplied **rises** and quantity demanded **falls**. The shortage shrinks until it disappears at $20.

Neither curve shifts during this adjustment. Only the **price** changes, and both quantities move **along** their curves. This is why an equilibrium is stable: any move away from it creates pressure to move back.

### Surplus lost while the market is out of equilibrium

At a disequilibrium price, the quantity actually **traded** is the **smaller** of Qd and Qs. Buyers cannot be made to buy more than they want, and sellers cannot be made to sell more than they want.

At $30 only 50 crates are traded. Assume the buyers who value fish most get it.

- Consumer surplus = ½ × 50 × (40 − 30) = **$250**.
- Producer surplus = area between $30 and the supply curve up to 50 crates. The supply curve gives $15 at 50 crates, so PS = 50 × (30 − 15) + ½ × 50 × (15 − 10) = 750 + 125 = **$875**.
- Total surplus = **$1,125**, which is $1,500 − $1,125 = **$375 less** than at equilibrium.

The $375 is lost because 50 crates that buyers valued more than they cost to catch are not traded. You can check it directly: ½ × (100 − 50) × (30 − 15) = $375. You will call this kind of loss **deadweight loss** in Topic 2.8.

Notice that sellers who **do** sell at $30 gain: PS rises from $500 to $875. Buyers lose more than sellers gain, so the market as a whole is worse off.

## Changes in equilibrium: four single shifts

When a **determinant** of demand or supply changes (Topics 2.1 and 2.2), a curve shifts. At the old price there is now a shortage or surplus, and the market adjusts to a new equilibrium.

| Event | Shift | At the old price | Equilibrium price | Equilibrium quantity |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Demand increases | D right | shortage | **rises** | **rises** |
| Demand decreases | D left | surplus | **falls** | **falls** |
| Supply increases | S right | surplus | **falls** | **rises** |
| Supply decreases | S left | shortage | **rises** | **falls** |

A memory aid: a **demand** shift moves price and quantity in the **same** direction. A **supply** shift moves them in **opposite** directions.

Always state the full chain: event → which curve shifts and which way → shortage or surplus at the old price → price changes → movement along the **other** curve → new equilibrium.

## Worked example 1: a surplus at the start of the day

**Question.** One morning, Port Ellery's fish sellers open with a price of $25 per crate.
(a) Is there a shortage or a surplus, and how large is it?
(b) Describe what happens to price, quantity demanded and quantity supplied as the market adjusts.
(c) How many crates are traded at $25, and how much consumer surplus do buyers get?

**(a)** At $25:

1. Quantity demanded = 200 − 5 × 25 = 75 crates.
2. Quantity supplied = 10 × 25 − 100 = 150 crates.
3. Qs > Qd, so there is a **surplus** of 150 − 75 = **75 crates**.

**(b)** Sellers with unsold fish cut prices. As the price falls towards $20, quantity demanded **rises** from 75 to 100 (movement along D), and quantity supplied **falls** from 150 to 100 (movement along S). The surplus disappears at **$20 and 100 crates**.

**(c)** Only **75 crates** are traded, the smaller of 75 and 150. Consumer surplus = ½ × 75 × (40 − 25) = **$562.50**, compared with $1,000 at equilibrium.

**Check.** The price $25 is above $20, so a surplus is the right answer. A price above equilibrium always gives a surplus; a price below always gives a shortage.

## Elasticity decides the size of the change

The direction of each change comes from the table above. The **size** depends on the price elasticities of demand and supply.

Suppose bad weather cuts the catch, so supply falls by 30 crates at every price: Qs = 10P − 130. Compare Port Ellery's demand with a flatter demand curve, Qd = 300 − 10P, that passes through the same starting point ($20, 100 crates) but is more elastic there.

| Demand curve | Elasticity at $20 | New price | New quantity | Price change | Quantity change |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Qd = 200 − 5P | 1.0 | $22 | 90 | +$2.00 | −10 crates |
| Qd = 300 − 10P | 2.0 | $21.50 | 85 | +$1.50 | −15 crates |

When demand is **more elastic**, buyers respond strongly to a higher price. A supply decrease then causes a **smaller price rise** and a **larger fall in quantity**. When demand is **more inelastic**, most of the effect shows up as a higher price.

The same logic works for supply. If supply is very elastic, a demand increase mostly raises quantity. If supply is very inelastic (for example, fish already landed today), a demand increase mostly raises price.

| Shift | More elastic other curve | More inelastic other curve |
|---|---|---|
| Demand shift | bigger change in Q, smaller change in P (elastic supply) | bigger change in P, smaller change in Q (inelastic supply) |
| Supply shift | bigger change in Q, smaller change in P (elastic demand) | bigger change in P, smaller change in Q (inelastic demand) |

## When both curves shift

Sometimes two events happen together. Each shift on its own gives a clear answer, but together one variable is **indeterminate**: it depends on which shift is larger.

| Demand | Supply | Price | Quantity |
|---|---|---|---|
| increases | increases | **indeterminate** | rises |
| decreases | decreases | **indeterminate** | falls |
| increases | decreases | rises | **indeterminate** |
| decreases | increases | falls | **indeterminate** |

Port Ellery shows why. Start at $20 and 100 crates and let demand rise by 30 crates at every price.

- If supply also rises by 30, the new equilibrium is $20 and 130 crates: price **unchanged**.
- If supply rises by 60, it is $18 and 140 crates: price **falls**.
- If demand rises by 60 and supply by 30, it is $22 and 150 crates: price **rises**.

Quantity rose every time, but price went three different ways. On an exam, state what you can predict, name the variable that is indeterminate, and say that it depends on the relative size of the shifts.

## Common misconceptions

- **"A shortage means the good is scarce."** Scarcity is permanent. A shortage happens only when the price is below equilibrium, and it ends when the price rises.
- **"A surplus is good because there is plenty."** A surplus means unsold output at the current price. It pushes the price down, and while it lasts, total surplus is lower than at equilibrium.
- **"When price rises, supply increases."** A higher price increases **quantity supplied**, a movement along the curve. Supply shifts only when a determinant changes.
- **"An increase in demand raises the price, which then reduces demand."** The higher price reduces **quantity demanded**, a movement along the new demand curve. Demand does not shift back.
- **"Shortage size is measured in dollars."** It is a quantity: Qd − Qs at the given price.
- **"If both curves shift right, price must rise."** Price is indeterminate; only quantity is certain to rise.
- **"Higher price always means lower consumer surplus."** Not when demand itself has increased. Compare the areas under the relevant demand curve.
- **"At a disequilibrium price, the quantity traded is quantity demanded."** It is the **smaller** of Qd and Qs.
