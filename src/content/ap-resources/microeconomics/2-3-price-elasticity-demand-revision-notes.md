## Recap

- **Price elasticity of demand (PED)** measures how strongly quantity demanded responds to a change in the good's own price, in percentage terms.
- PED is negative (law of demand); quote and compare its **magnitude**, |PED|.
- **Elastic** |PED| > 1; **inelastic** |PED| < 1; **unit elastic** |PED| = 1. Each label applies to a **price range**.
- **Perfectly inelastic** (|PED| = 0): vertical demand curve. **Perfectly elastic** (|PED| infinite): horizontal demand curve.
- **Slope is not elasticity.** A straight-line demand curve is elastic above its midpoint, unit elastic at it and inelastic below it.

## Key relationships

| Relationship | How to use it |
|---|---|
| **PED = % change in Q ÷ % change in P** | Percentages, never raw changes |
| % change (midpoint) = change ÷ average of the two values × 100 | Same answer whether price rises or falls |
| % change in Q = \|PED\| × % change in P | Predict a response from a known elasticity |
| TR = P × Q | Also buyers' total expenditure |

| Demand | Price rises | Price falls |
|---|---|---|
| Elastic | TR falls | TR rises |
| Inelastic | TR rises | TR falls |
| Unit elastic | TR unchanged | TR unchanged |

## What makes demand more elastic

- Many close **substitutes** (the main factor).
- A **luxury** rather than a necessity.
- A **large share** of the buyer's income.
- **More time** for buyers to adjust.
- A **narrowly defined** market (one brand, not the whole product).

## Assumptions behind the model

- Only the good's own price changes; income, tastes and other prices stay the same, so we move **along** one demand curve.
- The elasticity found is for the range measured, not for the whole curve.

## Mistakes to avoid

1. Dividing the change in quantity by the change in price instead of the percentage changes.
2. Calling a steep curve "inelastic" without checking where on the curve you are.
3. Saying inelastic means quantity does not change at all (that is perfectly inelastic).
4. Getting the total revenue test backwards: elastic means price and TR move in **opposite** directions.
5. Comparing signed values: −2 is **more** elastic than −0.5.
6. Mixing up the formula and calculating % change in price ÷ % change in quantity.

## Quick self-check

1. Price falls from $9 to $7 and quantity rises from 30 to 50. Find |PED| with the midpoint method. *(Q: 20 ÷ 40 = 50%; P: 2 ÷ 8 = 25%; |PED| = 2, elastic)*
2. A café raises its prices and its total revenue rises. Was demand elastic or inelastic? *(Inelastic: price and TR moved in the same direction.)*
3. Why can a straight-line demand curve be both elastic and inelastic? *(The same raw change is a different percentage at different prices and quantities.)*
