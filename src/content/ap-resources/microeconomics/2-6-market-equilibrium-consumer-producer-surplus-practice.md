These are **original Marlbridge practice questions**, not past exam questions. The mark points are a suggested Marlbridge rubric to help you check your work; they are not an official scoring guideline. All places, markets and data are fictional. Only addition, subtraction, multiplication and division are needed, so a four-function calculator is enough.

## Question 1 (multiple choice · foundation)

The weekly market for kites in a fictional town is:

| Price ($) | 10 | 12 | 14 | 16 | 18 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Quantity demanded | 90 | 80 | 70 | 60 | 50 |
| Quantity supplied | 30 | 50 | 70 | 90 | 110 |

What are the equilibrium price and quantity?

- (A) $14 and 70 kites
- (B) $12 and 80 kites
- (C) $16 and 90 kites
- (D) $14 and 140 kites

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (A).** At $14, quantity demanded and quantity supplied are both 70. No kites are left unsold and no buyer who will pay $14 goes without.

- (B) reads quantity demanded at $12, but sellers offer only 50 there: a shortage of 30.
- (C) reads quantity supplied at $16, but buyers want only 60 there: a surplus of 30.
- (D) adds quantity demanded and quantity supplied. At equilibrium they are equal, and 70 kites change hands.
</details>

## Question 2 (multiple choice · core)

In a market with straight-line demand and supply curves, the demand curve meets the price axis at $50 and the supply curve meets it at $10. The equilibrium price is $30 and the equilibrium quantity is 400 units. What is consumer surplus?

- (A) $4,000
- (B) $8,000
- (C) $6,000
- (D) $12,000

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (A).** Consumer surplus is the triangle below demand and above the price: base 400, height 50 − 30 = 20. CS = ½ × 400 × 20 = **$4,000**.

- (B) forgets the ½: it gives the rectangle, not the triangle.
- (C) measures the height from zero to the price ($30). That area is below the price line, not above it.
- (D) is total spending, 30 × 400. Spending is what buyers pay, not the extra benefit they receive.
</details>

## Question 3 (multiple choice · core)

A perfectly competitive market with no market failures is in equilibrium. Which statement is correct?

- (A) Total surplus is as large as possible.
- (B) Consumer surplus equals producer surplus.
- (C) Consumer surplus is as large as possible.
- (D) Every buyer who wants the good can buy it, whatever the most they would pay.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: (A).** At equilibrium, every unit whose marginal benefit is at least its marginal cost is traded, and no unit with a higher cost than benefit is. So total surplus is maximised: the market is efficient.

- (B) assumes the two triangles match. Their sizes depend on the shapes of the curves.
- (C) confuses total surplus with one part of it. Consumer surplus alone is not what equilibrium makes as large as possible.
- (D) ignores the price. Only buyers whose willingness to pay is at least the equilibrium price buy; the rest choose not to.
</details>

## Question 4 (calculation · core)

At a fictional college book swap, six students want to buy a used economics textbook and six students have one to sell. Each wants to trade one book.

| | 1st | 2nd | 3rd | 4th | 5th | 6th |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Buyers' willingness to pay ($) | 48 | 42 | 36 | 30 | 24 | 18 |
| Sellers' minimum acceptable price ($) | 12 | 18 | 26 | 32 | 38 | 44 |

(a) Show that $31 is an equilibrium price, and state the equilibrium quantity.
(b) At $31, calculate consumer surplus, producer surplus and total surplus.
(c) The organiser pairs the 4th buyer with the 4th seller as well. What happens to total surplus? Explain.

<details>
<summary>Worked solution</summary>

**(a)** At $31, buyers with willingness to pay of at least $31 are the first three (48, 42, 36): quantity demanded = 3. Sellers with a minimum of at most $31 are the first three (12, 18, 26): quantity supplied = 3. Quantity demanded equals quantity supplied, so $31 is an equilibrium price and **3 books** are traded. (Any price above $30 and below $32 works. At $29, four want to buy but only three will sell; at $34, four will sell but only three want to buy.)

**(b)**

- CS = (48 − 31) + (42 − 31) + (36 − 31) = 17 + 11 + 5 = **$33**.
- PS = (31 − 12) + (31 − 18) + (31 − 26) = 19 + 13 + 5 = **$37**.
- Total surplus = 33 + 37 = **$70**. Check: (48 − 12) + (42 − 18) + (36 − 26) = 36 + 24 + 10 = $70.

**(c)** The 4th buyer values a book at $30 but the 4th seller needs $32. The trade adds 30 − 32 = −$2, so total surplus **falls to $68**. The book costs the seller more to give up than it is worth to the buyer.

Suggested mark points (3): 1 for showing quantity demanded and quantity supplied are both 3 at $31; 1 for CS $33, PS $37 and total $70; 1 for $68 with the reason (value below minimum price).
</details>

## Question 5 (graph · core)

In the fictional port of Merrow, the daily market for fresh fish has a straight-line demand curve that meets the price axis at $20 per kg and the quantity axis at 1,000 kg. The straight-line supply curve starts at $6 per kg at zero quantity and passes through $15 per kg at 600 kg. The equilibrium price is $12 per kg.

(a) Draw a correctly labelled supply and demand graph for this market. Show the equilibrium price and quantity.
(b) Shade and label consumer surplus and producer surplus.
(c) Calculate consumer surplus, producer surplus and total surplus.

<details>
<summary>Model answer and suggested Marlbridge rubric</summary>

**(a)** Vertical axis: "Price ($ per kg)". Horizontal axis: "Quantity of fish (kg per day)". Demand D falls from $20 to 1,000 kg. Supply S rises from $6 through $15 at 600 kg. The demand curve gives $12 at 400 kg (20 − 12 = 8, and 8 is 40% of 20, so 40% of 1,000 kg). The supply curve also gives $12 at 400 kg (it rises $9 over 600 kg, so $6 over 400 kg). Equilibrium: **$12 per kg and 400 kg per day**, with dashed lines to both axes.

**(b)** Consumer surplus: the triangle between $12 and $20 on the price axis, below D, up to 400 kg. Producer surplus: the triangle between $6 and $12, above S, up to 400 kg. Use different shading (for example hatching and dots) and label each.

**(c)**

- CS = ½ × 400 × (20 − 12) = ½ × 400 × 8 = **$1,600 per day**.
- PS = ½ × 400 × (12 − 6) = ½ × 400 × 6 = **$1,200 per day**.
- Total surplus = **$2,800 per day**.

| Point | What earns it |
|---|---|
| 1 | Both axes labelled, D downward-sloping and S upward-sloping, with the curves labelled |
| 1 | Equilibrium shown at $12 and 400 kg |
| 1 | CS and PS correctly placed and labelled |
| 1 | CS $1,600 and PS $1,200 |
| 1 | Total surplus $2,800 |

Do not award the third point if either triangle extends past 400 kg.
</details>

## Question 6 (constructed response · core)

On the fictional island of Tolvane, the equilibrium price of lobster is $40 per kg and the equilibrium price of crab is $9 per kg. Both markets are perfectly competitive.

(a) Define market equilibrium.
(b) Suppose lobster were priced at $50 per kg. Explain what would happen in the lobster market and how the price would change.
(c) Explain how the much higher price of lobster guides the decisions of (i) buyers and (ii) people who fish.
(d) Use the supply and demand model to give one reason why lobster might cost more than crab.

<details>
<summary>Model answer and suggested Marlbridge rubric</summary>

**(a)** Market equilibrium is the price and quantity at which the quantity buyers want to buy equals the quantity sellers want to sell, so the market clears with no shortage or surplus.

**(b)** At $50, above equilibrium, quantity supplied would exceed quantity demanded: there would be a **surplus** of lobster. Sellers with unsold lobster would cut their prices. As the price falls, quantity demanded rises and quantity supplied falls, until the price returns to $40.

**(c)** (i) The high price tells buyers lobster is scarce compared with how much people want it. Buyers who value it less eat crab or other food instead, so lobster goes to those who value it most. (ii) The high price tells people who fish that lobster is highly valued. It gives them an incentive to spend more of their time, boats and traps catching lobster, so resources move towards the good people value more.

**(d)** Any one of: lobster may be harder or more costly to catch, so supply is smaller at each price; demand for lobster may be higher at each price, for example because more buyers see it as a treat. The equilibrium price is higher when supply is lower or demand is higher.

| Point | What earns it |
|---|---|
| 1 | Definition with quantity demanded = quantity supplied |
| 1 | Surplus at $50, with sellers cutting the price back towards equilibrium |
| 1 | Price rations lobster among buyers |
| 1 | Price gives an incentive to move resources into lobster fishing |
| 1 | A reason based on lower supply or higher demand |
</details>

## Question 7 (explanation · stretch)

Use the Merrow fish market from Question 5.

(a) Find the marginal benefit and marginal cost of the 300th kg and of the 500th kg of fish.
(b) Calculate total surplus if only 300 kg were traded, and if 500 kg were traded.
(c) Use your answers to explain why the equilibrium quantity is efficient.
(d) State one condition under which the market equilibrium would **not** maximise total surplus.

<details>
<summary>Model answer and suggested Marlbridge rubric</summary>

**(a)** Demand falls $2 per 100 kg, from $20; supply rises $1.50 per 100 kg, from $6.

- 300th kg: MB = 20 − 6 = **$14**; MC = 6 + 4.50 = **$10.50**.
- 500th kg: MB = 20 − 10 = **$10**; MC = 6 + 7.50 = **$13.50**.

**(b)** Total surplus is the area between demand and supply up to the quantity traded.

- **300 kg:** a trapezoid with parallel sides 20 − 6 = 14 (at zero) and 14 − 10.50 = 3.50 (at 300 kg). Area = ½ × (14 + 3.50) × 300 = **$2,625**. That is $175 less than at equilibrium.
- **500 kg:** the full equilibrium triangle ($2,800) minus the loss on kilograms 400 to 500, where cost exceeds benefit: ½ × 100 × (13.50 − 10) = $175. Total = **$2,625**.

**(c)** At 300 kg, the next kilogram is worth $14 to a buyer but costs only $10.50 to supply, so trading more would add surplus. At 500 kg, the last kilograms cost more than they are worth, so they subtract surplus. Only at 400 kg, where MB = MC = $12, is there nothing left to gain. Total surplus is largest ($2,800) there, so the equilibrium quantity is efficient.

**(d)** Any one: there is a **market failure**, for example fishing harms others (such as overfishing that hurts future catches) and that cost is not in the supply curve; or buyers' benefits are not all shown in demand; or the market is not perfectly competitive.

| Point | What earns it |
|---|---|
| 1 | All four MB and MC values |
| 1 | Total surplus of $2,625 at both quantities, with working |
| 1 | Efficiency explained by comparing MB and MC either side of 400 kg |
| 1 | A valid condition, such as a market failure, with a brief reason |

Accept a loss of $175 at each quantity instead of the totals if the method is clear.
</details>
