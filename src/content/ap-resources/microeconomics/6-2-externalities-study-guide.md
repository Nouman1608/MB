## What an externality is

In Topic 6.1 you saw that a competitive market is efficient only when buyers and sellers bear **all** the benefits and costs of their choices. An **externality** is what happens when they do not.

**An externality is a cost or benefit of producing or consuming a good that falls on a third party: someone who is not the buyer or the seller and who is not paid or charged for it through the market.**

- A **negative externality** imposes a cost on third parties. Its size per unit is the **marginal external cost (MEC)**.
- A **positive externality** gives a benefit to third parties. Its size per unit is the **marginal external benefit (MEB)**.

Social values add the external part to the private part:

- **MSC = MPC + MEC** (marginal social cost = marginal private cost + marginal external cost)
- **MSB = MPB + MEB** (marginal social benefit = marginal private benefit + marginal external benefit)

The efficient quantity is still where **MSB = MSC**, because that is where total economic surplus, including third parties, is greatest. Externalities can arise from **production** or from **consumption**:

| Type | Example | Which curve differs | Market result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Negative, in production | a factory's smoke damages nearby crops | MSC is **above** MPC | too much |
| Negative, in consumption | loud music at a late party disturbs neighbours | MSB is **below** MPB | too much |
| Positive, in production | a beekeeper's bees pollinate a neighbour's orchard | MSC is **below** MPC | too little |
| Positive, in consumption | a vaccinated person makes others less likely to catch a disease | MSB is **above** MPB | too little |

A useful rule: for a production externality the **cost** curves split; for a consumption externality the **benefit** curves split.

## Why markets ignore external effects

**Rational agents respond to private costs and benefits, not external ones.** A paper mill compares the price of paper with its own marginal cost. The damage its waste does to fishing downstream is not on its bill, so it does not affect its output decision. A person deciding whether to get a vaccination weighs their own protection against the price, not the protection it gives to others.

Why are these effects left out? There are two main reasons:

1. **Property rights are not well defined.** Nobody owns the river, the air or the quiet of a street. If no one owns them, no one can charge for their use, so damaging them is free to the person doing it.
2. **Transaction costs are high.** Even when rights exist, the people affected may be too many, or too hard to identify, to bargain with. Two neighbours can talk; ten thousand people downwind of a factory cannot easily organise.

Positive externalities have an extra problem: **free riding**. If a benefit spills over to people who cannot be excluded from it (it is **non-excludable**), each of them has an incentive to let someone else pay. Suppose one farm in a valley restores a wetland that holds back floodwater for every farm. The other farms gain whether or not they contribute, so it is rational for each to pay nothing. Too little of the good is provided. You will see this again with public goods in Topic 6.3.

What the graph shows:

- The market finds where MPB = MPC (point M). It ignores the $20 external cost.
- The socially optimal point is where MSB = MSC (point O).
- **The market produces too much, at too low a price.** The price does not include the damage to fishing.
- For each unit between Qs and Qm, MSC is above MSB. Making those units loses surplus. The **deadweight loss** is the triangle between MSC and MSB, from Qs to Qm, pointing to O.

Be careful: the whole band between MPC and MSC up to Qm is the **total external cost**, not the DWL. Most of that cost comes with units that are still worth producing. Only the triangle is lost surplus.

## Positive externalities on a graph

For a positive consumption externality, MSB lies **above** MPB (demand). Buyers ignore the benefit to others, so the market stops at Qm, where MPB = MPC. The socially optimal quantity Qs, where MSB = MSC, is larger. **The market produces too little.** The DWL is the triangle between MSB and MSC from Qm to Qs, again pointing to where MSB = MSC. Figure 2, in Worked example 2, shows this case with numbers.

## Policies that correct externalities

| Policy | How it works | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Per-unit tax** (on a negative externality) | set the tax equal to the MEC **at the optimal quantity**; MPC + tax = MSC there, so the market moves to Qs | raises revenue; the price buyers pay rises |
| **Per-unit subsidy** (on a positive externality) | set the subsidy equal to the MEB **at the optimal quantity**; the market expands to Qs | costs the government money |
| **Environmental regulation** | limits on output or emissions, required equipment, bans on harmful methods | can reach the target directly, but raises no revenue and needs the regulator to know the right limit |
| **Public provision** | the government supplies the good itself, often free or cheap (e.g. vaccinations, parks) | used when the benefit spills over widely |
| **Assignment of property rights** | give someone a legal right (e.g. to clean water), so damage must be paid for or bargained over | needs rights that can be enforced |
| **Reassignment of rights through private transactions** | rights are bought and sold: one side pays the other to stop, or firms trade pollution permits | works best when transaction costs are low |

When the MEC or MEB is the same for every unit, the right tax or subsidy is just that amount. If the MEC rises with output, use its value **at the optimal quantity**, not at the market quantity.

## Worked example 1: a tax on paper

**Question.** Use the Rennick River paper market above.
(a) Find the market quantity and price.
(b) Find the socially optimal quantity and price.
(c) Calculate the DWL.
(d) The government sets a per-unit tax of $20 on paper. Find the new quantity, the price buyers pay, the price sellers keep and the tax revenue.
(e) Compare the total external cost before and after the tax.

**(a)** MPB = MPC: 100 − Q = 20 + Q, so 2Q = 80, **Qm = 40 thousand tonnes** and **P = $60 per tonne**.

**(b)** MSB = MSC: 100 − Q = 40 + Q, so 2Q = 60, **Qs = 30 thousand tonnes** and **P = $70 per tonne**.

**(c)** At Qm = 40: MSC = 40 + 40 = $80 and MSB = 100 − 40 = $60. DWL = ½ × (40 − 30) × (80 − 60) = ½ × 10 × 20 = **$100 thousand per year**.

**(d)** The tax adds $20 to every unit, so sellers now face 20 + Q + 20 = 40 + Q, which is the MSC line.

1. 100 − Q = 40 + Q gives **Q = 30 thousand tonnes**: the optimal quantity.
2. Buyers pay **$70**; sellers keep 70 − 20 = **$50** per tonne.
3. Tax revenue = 20 × 30 = **$600 thousand per year**.

**(e)** Before: 20 × 40 = $800 thousand. After: 20 × 30 = $600 thousand. The tax cuts the damage, but not to zero. Each of the remaining 30 thousand tonnes is worth at least its full social cost to buyers, so producing them is efficient.

**Check.** The tax works because it makes sellers pay the external cost: it **internalises** the externality.

## Worked example 3: bargaining over property rights

**Question.** Brackenhill Quarry (fictional) wants to start blasting at night. This would add **$30 thousand** a year to its profit. The noise would cost the only nearby hotel **$50 thousand** a year in lost bookings. Assume the two can bargain at no cost.
(a) What is the efficient outcome?
(b) What happens if the hotel has the legal right to quiet nights?
(c) What happens if the quarry has the legal right to blast?
(d) Why might bargaining fail if 2,000 households were affected instead of one hotel?

**(a)** The blasting gains $30 thousand but costs $50 thousand. Society is $20 thousand worse off with it, so the efficient outcome is **no night blasting**.

**(b)** The quarry would need to pay the hotel at least $50 thousand for permission. Blasting is worth only $30 thousand to the quarry, so it does not buy the right. **No blasting.**

**(c)** The hotel can offer the quarry a payment between $30 thousand and $50 thousand to stop. Any amount in that range leaves both better off. **No blasting.**

**(d)** Each household would have to be found and included, and the cost of organising them would be large: high **transaction costs**. Each household would also hope that others pay, because it gains from quiet nights either way: **free riding**. The bargain probably never happens, so a government policy (a ban on night blasting, or a charge for it) may be needed.

**Interpretation.** With well-defined rights and low transaction costs, private bargaining reaches the efficient outcome **whichever side holds the right**. The right only decides who pays whom. Economists call this result the Coase theorem.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Any cost of making a good is an externality."** Only costs that fall on third parties count. Wages and materials are private costs.
- **"With a negative externality, the market price is too high."** It is too **low**: it leaves out the external cost, so too much is bought.
- **"The socially optimal level of pollution is zero."** It is where MSB = MSC, which usually involves some pollution.
- **"The DWL of a negative externality is the area between MPC and MSC."** That band is the total external cost. The DWL is only the triangle beyond Qs.
- **"The tax should equal the MEC at the market quantity."** It should equal the MEC at the **optimal** quantity. With a constant MEC the two are the same; with a rising MEC they are not.
- **"Free riders are behaving irrationally."** Free riding is a rational response to a benefit you cannot be excluded from.
- **"A positive externality shifts the demand curve."** Demand stays as the MPB curve; the MSB curve is a separate line. A subsidy is what changes the market outcome.
