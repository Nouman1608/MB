## One question for every policy

In Topic 2.8 you analysed taxes, subsidies and price controls in a perfectly competitive market. This topic uses the same tools in **every** market structure. Every firm, whatever the market, produces where marginal revenue equals marginal cost (or, in a labour market, where the marginal revenue product equals the marginal resource cost). So for any policy, ask:

**Does the policy change marginal cost, or the price the firm can charge? Or does it change only fixed cost?**

- If it changes **MC** or the firm's **MR**, the profit-maximising quantity changes, so price and output change.
- If it changes only **fixed cost**, the profit-maximising quantity stays the same. Only profit changes.

## Per-unit taxes and subsidies

A **per-unit tax** of $t adds $t to the cost of every unit, so it shifts MC (and supply) **up by $t**. A **per-unit subsidy** of $s shifts MC **down by $s**.

**In perfect competition**, as in Topic 2.8, a per-unit tax raises the price consumers pay, lowers the net price firms keep, cuts the equilibrium quantity, reduces consumer and producer surplus, raises government revenue (tax × quantity) and creates deadweight loss. A subsidy does the opposite, but costs the government subsidy × quantity and also creates deadweight loss (the extra units cost more than they are worth). **Elasticity decides who bears the tax**: the side of the market that is **less elastic** bears more of it. The more elastic demand and supply are, the more quantity falls, and the bigger the deadweight loss.

**In monopoly**, the tax shifts MC up, so the new MR = MC point is at a **lower quantity**. The firm sets a higher price from the demand curve. A monopoly already produces too little, so the tax pushes output **further** below the efficient quantity and deadweight loss grows. With a straight-line demand curve and constant MC, the price rises by **half** the tax; with rising MC it rises by less than half. A per-unit subsidy does the reverse: MC shifts down and output rises towards the efficient level. In principle, a large enough subsidy could bring a monopoly to the efficient quantity, but it is expensive.

## Lump-sum taxes and subsidies

A **lump-sum tax** is a fixed amount the firm pays whatever its output (for example, a $108 thousand licence fee per month). A **lump-sum subsidy** is a fixed payment to the firm. Neither changes marginal cost or marginal benefit; **only fixed cost changes**, and so does ATC.

- In the short run, every firm, in any market structure, keeps the same quantity and price. Profit falls by the tax (or rises by the subsidy).
- A monopoly that is earning economic profit can pay a lump-sum tax without changing its output, so the tax creates **no extra deadweight loss**.
- If a lump-sum tax turns profit into a long-run loss, firms will eventually leave the market. Only then do quantity and price change.

## Price controls in different market structures

A binding **price ceiling** (a maximum price below the current price) and a binding **price floor** (a minimum price above it) have different effects in different market structures.

**Perfect competition.** A binding ceiling cuts the quantity supplied, causing a shortage; a binding floor cuts the quantity demanded, causing a surplus. Either way, the quantity traded falls and deadweight loss appears. The more elastic supply and demand are, the larger the shortage or surplus.

**Monopoly.** A monopoly normally holds back output because selling one more unit forces it to cut the price on all units, so MR is below price. A ceiling changes that. Up to the quantity where the ceiling meets demand, each extra unit sells at the ceiling, so **MR equals the ceiling price** (the MR curve becomes flat there). The firm now produces where the ceiling meets MC, or where it meets demand, whichever comes first.

- A ceiling where **demand meets MC** gives the **allocatively efficient** quantity: output rises, price falls, deadweight loss disappears.
- A ceiling between the monopoly's MC (at its unregulated output) and its unregulated price still **raises** output, but if it is below the price where demand meets MC it also causes a shortage.
- A ceiling **below MC at the unregulated output** makes the firm produce **less** than before.

**Monopolistic competition.** Each firm also faces a downward-sloping demand curve, so the same logic applies: a ceiling a little below its price can raise its output in the short run.

**Monopsony.** A price floor in a labour market is a **minimum wage**. A monopsony hires where MRC = MRP and pays the lower wage from the supply curve. A minimum wage set above that wage makes MRC equal the minimum wage over the range where workers are willing to work. Up to the wage where supply meets MRP, a minimum wage raises **both** the wage and employment. In a perfectly competitive labour market, a binding minimum wage always **reduces** employment.

## Regulating a natural monopoly

A natural monopoly has ATC falling across the whole of market demand, so MC is below ATC (Topic 4.2). A regulator can choose between three outcomes:

| Option | Rule | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Unregulated | MR = MC, price from demand | low output, high price, economic profit, large deadweight loss |
| **Fair-return** price | P = ATC | zero economic profit, more output, smaller deadweight loss |
| **Socially optimal** price | P = MC | allocatively efficient output, no deadweight loss, but P < ATC, so the firm makes a **loss** |

At P = MC the firm would shut down in the long run, so the government must pay it a **lump-sum subsidy** equal to the loss. It must be lump-sum: a per-unit subsidy would shift MC and change the output the regulator wanted.

## Antitrust policy

**Antitrust policy** (also called competition policy) tries to make markets more competitive. Governments can block mergers that would create a dominant firm, break up existing monopolies, and outlaw collusion such as cartel price-fixing. More competition pushes price towards MC and raises output. Antitrust suits markets where many firms could survive; for a natural monopoly, splitting the firm would raise ATC, so regulation is usually better. You will not be asked to draw a graph of collusion and the policy against it.

## When intervention increases efficiency

Government intervention in an imperfect market increases efficiency **only if it corrects the incentive that caused the inefficiency**. A monopoly's problem is that MR is below price, so it restricts output. A ceiling where demand meets MC fixes that incentive. A per-unit tax on the monopoly makes it worse, and a lump-sum tax leaves it unchanged.

## Worked example 1: regulating Marrick Water

**Question.** Marrick Water (fictional) is a natural monopoly. Demand is P = 60 − 0.5Q, so MR = 60 − Q (Q in thousand households, P in $ per month). MC is constant at $10 and fixed cost is $800 thousand per month, so ATC = 10 + 800 ÷ Q. Find the outcome (a) unregulated, (b) with a socially optimal price, (c) with a fair-return price. Compare the deadweight loss.

**(a)** MR = MC: 60 − Q = 10, so Q = **50**. P = 60 − 0.5 × 50 = **$35**. ATC = 10 + 800 ÷ 50 = $26. Profit = (35 − 26) × 50 = **$450 thousand**.

**(b)** P = MC: 60 − 0.5Q = 10, so Q = **100**, P = **$10**. ATC = 10 + 800 ÷ 100 = $18. Loss = (18 − 10) × 100 = **$800 thousand**, equal to the fixed cost. A **lump-sum subsidy of $800 thousand** per month keeps the firm open without changing its MC.

**(c)** P = ATC: 60 − 0.5Q = 10 + 800 ÷ Q. Q = 80 works: P = 60 − 40 = $20 and ATC = 10 + 10 = $20. So Q = **80**, P = **$20**, economic profit **$0**. (Q = 20 also solves the equation, but the regulator chooses the larger output.)

**Deadweight loss** (triangle between demand and MC, from the quantity produced to 100):

- Unregulated: ½ × (100 − 50) × (35 − 10) = **$625 thousand**.
- Fair return: ½ × (100 − 80) × (20 − 10) = **$100 thousand**.
- Socially optimal: **$0**.

**Interpretation.** Moving from U to F to S raises output and consumer surplus ($625 thousand, then $1,600 thousand, then $2,500 thousand) and shrinks the deadweight loss. The cost of full efficiency is a subsidy paid by taxpayers.

## Worked example 2: taxing Ashgrove Biotech

**Question.** Ashgrove Biotech (fictional) holds a patent on an inhaler. Demand is P = 60 − Q, so MR = 60 − 2Q. MC = Q, and fixed cost is $100 thousand per month (Q in thousand inhalers per month, P in $). Compare (a) no tax, (b) a $6 per-unit tax and (c) a lump-sum tax that raises the same revenue.

**(a)** MR = MC: 60 − 2Q = Q, so Q = **20**, P = **$40**. The efficient quantity is where P = MC: 60 − Q = Q, so Q = 30. Deadweight loss = ½ × (30 − 20) × (40 − 20) = **$100 thousand**.

**(b)** MC becomes Q + 6. MR = MC: 60 − 2Q = Q + 6, so Q = **18**, P = 60 − 18 = **$42**.

1. Consumers pay $2 more; the firm keeps $42 − $6 = $36 per unit.
2. Tax revenue = 6 × 18 = **$108 thousand**.
3. Deadweight loss = ½ × (30 − 18) × (42 − 18) = **$144 thousand**, up from $100 thousand.

**(c)** A lump-sum tax of $108 thousand does not change MC, so Q stays **20** and P stays **$40**. Profit falls by $108 thousand (from $500 thousand to $392 thousand). Deadweight loss stays **$100 thousand**.

**Interpretation.** Both taxes raise $108 thousand, but the per-unit tax adds $44 thousand of deadweight loss and raises the price; the lump-sum tax adds none.

## Worked example 3: a price ceiling on Ashgrove

**Question.** Return to untaxed Ashgrove. Find output and deadweight loss with a ceiling of (a) $30, (b) $24, (c) $16.

The firm produces where MC (= Q) equals the ceiling, as long as buyers want that many.

**(a)** $30: Q = 30, and buyers want 60 − 30 = 30, so there is **no shortage**. Deadweight loss = **$0** (Figure 1).

**(b)** $24: Q = 24, but buyers want 36: a **shortage of 12 thousand**. Output still rises from 20 to 24. Deadweight loss = ½ × (30 − 24) × (36 − 24) = **$36 thousand**, less than $100 thousand.

**(c)** $16: Q = 16, **below** the unregulated 20, with a shortage of 44 − 16 = 28 thousand. Deadweight loss = ½ × (30 − 16) × (44 − 16) = **$196 thousand**: worse than no ceiling.

**Check.** A ceiling between $20 (MC at the unregulated output) and $40 (the unregulated price) raises output; $30 is best.

## Common misconceptions

- **"A lump-sum tax raises a monopoly's price."** It changes only fixed cost. MR = MC at the same quantity, so price and output stay the same.
- **"A price ceiling always reduces output."** That is true in perfect competition, not for a firm with market power.
- **"Regulating a natural monopoly at P = MC lets the firm break even."** P = MC is below ATC, so the firm makes a loss and needs a lump-sum subsidy. P = ATC gives zero economic profit.
- **"A per-unit subsidy is fine for the natural monopoly loss."** It would shift MC and change output. Covering the loss needs a lump-sum payment.
- **"Any government intervention fixes a market failure."** Only policies that correct the underlying incentive raise efficiency. A per-unit tax on a monopoly increases deadweight loss.
- **"A minimum wage always reduces employment."** In a monopsony, a minimum wage up to the level where supply meets MRP raises employment.
- **"Breaking up any monopoly helps."** For a natural monopoly, two firms would duplicate fixed costs and raise ATC.
