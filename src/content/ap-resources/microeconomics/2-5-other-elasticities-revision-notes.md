## Recap

- Elasticity can be measured for **any** determinant of demand or supply: % change in quantity ÷ % change in the cause.
- **Income elasticity of demand (YED)** shows how quantity demanded responds to a change in income. Its sign shows whether a good is **normal** or **inferior**.
- **Cross-price elasticity of demand (XED)** shows how quantity demanded of one good responds to a change in the price of **another** good. Its sign shows whether the goods are **substitutes**, **complements** or **not related**.
- The size shows how strong the response is: a large positive XED means close substitutes; a large negative XED means strong complements.
- Changes in income or in a related good's price **shift** the demand curve. They are not movements along it.

## Key relationships

| Relationship | How to use it |
|---|---|
| **YED = % change in Qd ÷ % change in income** | keep the sign |
| **XED = % change in Qd of X ÷ % change in price of Y** | name X and Y; keep the sign |
| % change = change ÷ average of the two values × 100 | midpoint method, for two values of each |
| % change in Qd = elasticity × % change in the cause | for predictions |

| Result | Meaning | When the cause rises, demand curve… |
|---|---|---|
| YED > 0 | normal good | shifts right |
| YED < 0 | inferior good | shifts left |
| XED > 0 | substitutes | shifts right |
| XED < 0 | complements | shifts left |
| YED or XED = 0 | unrelated | does not move |

Background only: YED above 1 is often called income elastic (many luxuries); YED between 0 and 1 is income inelastic (many necessities).

## Assumptions

- Each elasticity changes **one** cause and holds everything else constant, including the good's own price.
- The result applies to the range of values you measured.

## Mistakes to avoid

1. Dropping the minus sign, which turns an inferior good into a normal one, or complements into substitutes.
2. Dividing raw changes instead of percentage changes.
3. Putting the cause on top: quantity always goes in the numerator.
4. Calling complements "positive" because they are used together.
5. Drawing a movement along the curve for a change in income or in another good's price.
6. Using the total revenue test with YED or XED.

## Quick self-check

1. Income rises 10% and the quantity demanded of a good falls 5%. What is YED, and what type of good is it? *(−5 ÷ 10 = −0.5: inferior.)*
2. The price of good Y rises 20% and the quantity demanded of good X rises 30%. What is XED, and how are the goods related? *(30 ÷ 20 = +1.5: substitutes.)*
3. Two goods are strong complements. The price of one falls. What happens to demand for the other? *(It increases: the curve shifts right.)*
