## Why specialise and trade?

In Topic 1.3 you saw that scarcity forces every economy to choose a point on or inside its PPC. On its own, an economy can never consume more than its PPC allows. This topic shows a way round that limit. If producers **specialise** in what they give up least to make, and then **trade**, everyone can end up with more than they could make alone.

The idea works for any producer: a person, a business or a country. Throughout this guide we use two fictional countries, **Corvania** and **Delmora**. Each has the **same quantity of resources** (the same number of workers, machines and land), and each can make two goods:

| Country | Rice (thousand sacks per month) | Shoes (thousand pairs per month) |
|---|---|---|
| Corvania | 120 | 40 |
| Delmora | 60 | 30 |

Each number is the most the country can make if it puts **all** its resources into that one good. Both PPCs are straight lines, so opportunity costs are constant.

## Absolute advantage

A producer has an **absolute advantage** in a good if it can make **more** of that good than another producer using the same quantity of resources. Put another way, it can make the same amount using **fewer** resources.

Corvania makes 120 thousand sacks of rice, Delmora only 60. Corvania makes 40 thousand pairs of shoes, Delmora only 30. So **Corvania has an absolute advantage in both goods**.

Absolute advantage is easy to spot, but it does **not** decide who should make what. For that you need opportunity cost.

## Comparative advantage

A producer has a **comparative advantage** in a good if it can make that good at a **lower opportunity cost** than another producer. Recall from Topic 1.3:

**Opportunity cost of 1 unit of X = units of Y given up ÷ units of X gained**

For a straight-line PPC, use the two maximums. If Corvania moves all its resources from shoes to rice, it gives up 40 thousand pairs of shoes and gains 120 thousand sacks of rice.

| Country | Opportunity cost of 1 pair of shoes | Opportunity cost of 1 sack of rice |
|---|---|---|
| Corvania | 120 ÷ 40 = **3 sacks of rice** | 40 ÷ 120 = **1/3 pair of shoes** |
| Delmora | 60 ÷ 30 = **2 sacks of rice** | 30 ÷ 60 = **1/2 pair of shoes** |

- Shoes: Delmora gives up 2 sacks, Corvania 3. **Delmora has the comparative advantage in shoes.**
- Rice: Corvania gives up 1/3 of a pair, Delmora 1/2. **Corvania has the comparative advantage in rice.**

Notice two things. First, Delmora has **no** absolute advantage at all, but it still has a comparative advantage. Second, each country's two opportunity costs are reciprocals (3 and 1/3; 2 and 1/2). So with two goods and two producers, one producer has the comparative advantage in one good and the other producer has it in the other good. The only exception is when the opportunity costs are equal; then neither has a comparative advantage.

- **Intercepts** show absolute advantage: the higher intercept on an axis means more of that good from the same resources.
- **Slopes** show comparative advantage. With shoes on the horizontal axis, the **flatter** line gives up less rice per pair, so that producer has the comparative advantage in shoes. The **steeper** line has the comparative advantage in the good on the vertical axis.

## Output data and input data

Questions give data in one of two forms. Read the column headings carefully before you divide.

| Form | What the numbers mean | Who has the absolute advantage | Opportunity cost of 1 X |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Output** | units made in a given time or with given resources | the **larger** number | (output of Y) ÷ (output of X) |
| **Input** | hours (or workers, or acres) needed to make one unit | the **smaller** number | (input per X) ÷ (input per Y) |

If the input rule is hard to remember, convert input data into output data first: divide the time available by the hours per unit. Then use the output rule. Worked example 2 does both and shows they agree.

## Worked example 1: absolute and comparative advantage from output data

**Question.** Use the Corvania and Delmora table.
(a) Who has the absolute advantage in each good?
(b) Who has the comparative advantage in each good?
(c) Which good should each country specialise in?

**(a)** Compare outputs from the same resources. Rice: 120 > 60. Shoes: 40 > 30. **Corvania has the absolute advantage in both.**

**(b)**

1. Corvania: 1 pair of shoes costs 120 ÷ 40 = 3 sacks of rice; 1 sack of rice costs 40 ÷ 120 = 1/3 pair.
2. Delmora: 1 pair of shoes costs 60 ÷ 30 = 2 sacks of rice; 1 sack of rice costs 30 ÷ 60 = 1/2 pair.
3. Shoes: 2 < 3, so **Delmora** has the comparative advantage.
4. Rice: 1/3 < 1/2, so **Corvania** has the comparative advantage.

**(c)** **Corvania should specialise in rice and Delmora in shoes.** Specialisation follows comparative advantage. If Corvania made everything because it is "better at both", it would waste its resources on shoes, which cost it 3 sacks of rice each, when Delmora can make them for 2.

**Check.** The two comparative advantages are in different goods, as they must be when costs differ.

## Worked example 2: input data

**Question.** Ana and Ben are fictional furniture makers. The table shows the **hours of work** each needs to make one unit.

| Maker | Hours per table | Hours per chair |
|---|---|---|
| Ana | 3 | 1 |
| Ben | 6 | 1.5 |

(a) Who has the absolute advantage in each good?
(b) Find each maker's opportunity cost of a table and of a chair. Who has the comparative advantage in each?
(c) Check your answer to (b) by converting to output in a 12-hour day.

**(a)** Fewer hours per unit means more output from the same time. Tables: 3 < 6. Chairs: 1 < 1.5. **Ana has the absolute advantage in both.**

**(b)** For input data, opportunity cost of 1 X = (hours per X) ÷ (hours per Y).

1. Ana: the 3 hours she spends on a table could make 3 ÷ 1 = **3 chairs**. A chair costs 1 ÷ 3 = **1/3 table**.
2. Ben: the 6 hours he spends on a table could make 6 ÷ 1.5 = **4 chairs**. A chair costs 1.5 ÷ 6 = **1/4 table**.
3. Tables: 3 < 4, so **Ana** has the comparative advantage in tables.
4. Chairs: 1/4 < 1/3, so **Ben** has the comparative advantage in chairs.

**(c)** In 12 hours, Ana can make 12 ÷ 3 = 4 tables **or** 12 ÷ 1 = 12 chairs. Ben can make 12 ÷ 6 = 2 tables **or** 12 ÷ 1.5 = 8 chairs. Using the output rule: Ana's table costs 12 ÷ 4 = 3 chairs and Ben's costs 8 ÷ 2 = 4 chairs. The answers match.

**Common slip.** With input data, dividing "the other good over this good" (the output rule) gives the reciprocal: 1 ÷ 3 instead of 3. Always ask: "In the time it takes to make one table, how many chairs could I have made?"

## Specialisation and gains from trade

Go back to Corvania and Delmora. Without trade, each country must consume what it produces. Suppose that before trade they choose these points on their PPCs:

| | Shoes (thousand pairs) | Rice (thousand sacks) |
|---|---|---|
| Corvania, no trade | 15 | 120 − 3 × 15 = 75 |
| Delmora, no trade | 10 | 60 − 2 × 10 = 40 |
| **Total output, no trade** | **25** | **115** |
| Corvania specialises in rice | 0 | 120 |
| Delmora specialises in shoes | 30 | 0 |
| **Total output, specialised** | **30** | **120** |

Specialising according to comparative advantage raises **total** output of both goods: 5 thousand more pairs of shoes and 5 thousand more sacks of rice, with exactly the same resources. Each good is now made by the producer who gives up least to make it. Those extra goods are the **gains from specialisation**. Trade is how the two countries share them.

## Terms of trade

The **terms of trade** are the rate at which one good is exchanged for the other, such as "1 pair of shoes for 2.5 sacks of rice".

Delmora exports shoes. Making a pair costs it 2 sacks of rice, so it will only sell a pair for **more than 2 sacks**. Corvania imports shoes. Making a pair itself would cost 3 sacks of rice, so it will only pay **less than 3 sacks**. So:

**Mutually beneficial terms of trade: 1 pair of shoes for more than 2 but fewer than 3 sacks of rice.**

The rule in general: **the terms of trade must lie between the two producers' opportunity costs.**

- At 2.5 sacks per pair, Delmora gains 2.5 − 2 = 0.5 sack on each pair it sells, and Corvania saves 3 − 2.5 = 0.5 sack on each pair it buys. Both gain.
- At 3.5 sacks per pair, Corvania refuses: it can make shoes itself for 3 sacks.
- At 1.5 sacks per pair, Delmora refuses: it would get less than its own cost of 2 sacks.
- At exactly 2 or exactly 3, one side gains nothing, so there is no reason for it to trade.

You can state the same range the other way round. Inverting it, 1 sack of rice must trade for between 1/3 and 1/2 of a pair of shoes: again, between the two opportunity costs of rice.

## Worked example 3: consuming beyond the PPC

**Question.** Corvania and Delmora specialise fully and trade at **1 pair of shoes for 2.5 sacks of rice**. Delmora exports 18 thousand pairs of shoes to Corvania.
(a) How much rice does Delmora receive?
(b) What does each country consume after trade? Compare with its no-trade point.
(c) Show that each country consumes a combination outside its own PPC.

**(a)** 18 × 2.5 = **45 thousand sacks of rice**.

**(b)**

1. Delmora keeps 30 − 18 = 12 thousand pairs of shoes and has 45 thousand sacks of rice. Before trade it had 10 and 40. **Gain: 2 thousand pairs and 5 thousand sacks.**
2. Corvania keeps 120 − 45 = 75 thousand sacks of rice and has 18 thousand pairs of shoes. Before trade it had 75 and 15. **Gain: 3 thousand pairs, with no loss of rice.**
3. Check: totals are 12 + 18 = 30 pairs and 45 + 75 = 120 sacks, exactly what was produced.

**(c)** On its own PPC, with 12 thousand pairs Delmora could have at most 60 − 2 × 12 = 36 thousand sacks of rice. It now has 45, so the point (12, 45) is **outside** its PPC. For Corvania, with 18 thousand pairs the most rice on its PPC is 120 − 3 × 18 = 66 thousand sacks. It has 75, so (18, 75) is also **outside** its PPC.

## Common misconceptions

- **"The producer with the absolute advantage should make everything."** Specialisation follows comparative advantage. Corvania is better at both goods but should leave shoes to Delmora.
- **"A producer with no absolute advantage has nothing to gain from trade."** Delmora is worse at both goods but still has a comparative advantage in shoes, and it gains from trade.
- **"One producer can have the comparative advantage in both goods."** Not with two goods and two producers. Lower cost in one good means higher cost in the other, because the two costs are reciprocals.
- **"Trade shifts the PPC outward."** Trade changes **consumption**, not production possibilities. The PPC moves only when resources or technology change.
- **"Any rate of exchange makes both sides better off."** Only terms strictly between the two opportunity costs do. Outside that range one side refuses; at either end one side gains nothing.
- **"For input data, use the same division as for output data."** Input numbers are costs per unit, so the rule flips. Convert to output if unsure.
- **"Comparative advantage is about money costs or wages."** In this model it is about opportunity cost: how much of the other good is given up.
