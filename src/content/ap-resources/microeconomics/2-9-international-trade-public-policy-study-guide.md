## From a closed market to an open one

So far in this unit, every market has been **closed**: only domestic buyers and domestic sellers. A country that does not trade with others is in **autarky**. Its price and quantity are set where domestic demand meets domestic supply.

When a country opens a market to trade, its buyers can also buy from foreign sellers, and its sellers can also sell to foreign buyers. In this topic we use a **small economy**: one whose buying and selling are too small to change the **world price** (Pw). It can buy or sell as much as it wants at Pw, so the world price acts like a horizontal line on the domestic graph.

At the world price, two things can happen:

- **Pw is below the autarky price.** Domestic buyers want more than domestic sellers will supply at Pw. The gap is filled by **imports**: imports = Qd − Qs at Pw.
- **Pw is above the autarky price.** Domestic sellers supply more than domestic buyers want at Pw. The extra is sold abroad as **exports**: exports = Qs − Qd at Pw.

Either way, the domestic price moves to the world price, and the gap between domestic demand and domestic supply is filled by trade. This guide builds on the surplus and deadweight-loss ideas from [Topic 2.8](/advanced-course-resources/microeconomics/2-8-effects-government-intervention-markets-study-guide/).

Throughout, we use **Tarvos**, a fictional small country. Its domestic rice market is:

- demand: Qd = 120 − 10P
- supply: Qs = 10P

where P is the price in dollars per kg and Q is in thousand tonnes per year. Because 1 thousand tonnes is 1 million kg, an area of "$1 per kg × 1 thousand tonnes" is **$1 million**. Every surplus below is in millions of dollars.

Read the surplus areas from Figure 1:

| | Autarky (P = $6) | Free trade (P = $3) |
|---|---|---|
| Consumer surplus | A | A + B + C + E |
| Producer surplus | B + C + F | F |
| Total surplus | A + B + C + F | A + B + C + E + F |

Three things to notice:

1. **B + C** used to be producer surplus. After trade it belongs to consumers. It is a **transfer**, not a gain for the country.
2. **E** is new surplus that did not exist before. It is the **gain from trade**: total surplus rises by E.
3. **Producers lose** (they sell less at a lower price) and **consumers gain** (they buy more at a lower price). The gain to consumers is larger than the loss to producers.

## The exporting case

If the world price is **above** the autarky price, everything flips. Domestic buyers face a higher price and buy less; domestic sellers get a higher price and sell more, shipping the extra abroad. **Producers gain, consumers lose**, and total surplus still rises. Worked example 3 shows this with numbers and Figure 3.

| | Pw below autarky price | Pw above autarky price |
|---|---|---|
| Domestic price | falls to Pw | rises to Pw |
| Domestic quantity supplied | falls | rises |
| Domestic quantity demanded | rises | falls |
| Trade | imports = Qd − Qs | exports = Qs − Qd |
| Consumer surplus | rises | falls |
| Producer surplus | falls | rises |
| Total surplus | rises | rises |

## Tariffs and quotas: why and how

Governments sometimes restrict imports, often to protect domestic producers and their workers, or to raise revenue. The two tools in this topic are:

- A **tariff**: a tax on each unit of an imported good. With a per-unit tariff t, imports cost Pw + t, so the domestic price rises to **Pw + t**.
- An **import quota**: a legal limit on the **quantity** of a good that may be imported in a period.

Both raise the domestic price above the world price, so both help domestic producers and hurt domestic consumers. Both reduce total surplus compared with free trade.

## Worked example 1: surplus before and after trade

**Question.** Use the Tarvos rice market and Figure 1, with Pw = $3.
(a) Find domestic quantity demanded, quantity supplied and imports at the world price.
(b) Calculate consumer, producer and total surplus under autarky and under free trade.
(c) How much do consumers gain, how much do producers lose, and what is the net gain?

**(a)** At P = 3: Qd = 120 − 30 = **90**; Qs = 10 × 3 = **30**; imports = 90 − 30 = **60** thousand tonnes.

**(b)** Demand meets the price axis at $12 (where Qd = 0). Supply starts at the origin.

*Autarky (P = 6, Q = 60):*

1. CS = ½ × 60 × (12 − 6) = **180**.
2. PS = ½ × 60 × (6 − 0) = **180**.
3. Total surplus = **360** ($ million).

*Free trade (P = 3, Qd = 90, Qs = 30):*

1. CS = ½ × 90 × (12 − 3) = **405**.
2. PS = ½ × 30 × (3 − 0) = **45**.
3. Total surplus = **450** ($ million).

**(c)** Consumers gain 405 − 180 = **225**. Producers lose 180 − 45 = **135**. Net gain = 225 − 135 = **90** ($ million).

**Check with the areas.** B = 30 × 3 = 90 and C = ½ × 30 × 3 = 45, so B + C = 135: the producers' loss. E is a triangle with base 90 − 30 = 60 (the imports) and height 6 − 3 = 3, so E = ½ × 60 × 3 = **90**: the net gain. Consumers gain B + C + E = 225. Everything agrees.

**(a)** Domestic price = Pw + t = 3 + 1 = **$4**. Qs = 10 × 4 = **40**. Qd = 120 − 40 = **80**. Imports = 80 − 40 = **40** thousand tonnes, down from 60.

**(b)** The tariff is paid on each imported unit: revenue = t × imports = 1 × 40 = **$40 million** (area K).

**(c)**

1. New CS = ½ × 80 × (12 − 4) = 320. Change = 320 − 405 = **−85**.
2. New PS = ½ × 40 × 4 = 80. Change = 80 − 45 = **+35** (area H).
3. Deadweight loss: J = ½ × (40 − 30) × 1 = 5 and L = ½ × (90 − 80) × 1 = 5, so **DWL = 10**.
4. Check: total surplus = CS + PS + revenue = 320 + 80 + 40 = 440, compared with 450 under free trade. It fell by **10**, the deadweight loss.

**Why two triangles?** J is lost because Tarvos now grows 10 thousand tonnes at a cost above the world price, when it could have imported them for $3. L is lost because buyers who valued rice above $3 (but below $4) no longer buy those 10 thousand tonnes.

**(d)** Imports stop when the domestic price reaches the autarky price, $6. So any tariff of **$3 or more** per kg is **prohibitive**: imports fall to zero, revenue is zero, and the market returns to autarky.

**Interpretation.** The tariff moves the market partway back towards autarky. Producers gain and the government collects revenue, but consumers lose more than both together.

## Quotas: effects without a graph

You will not be asked to draw a quota, but you must explain its effects. Suppose Tarvos allows at most **40 thousand tonnes** of rice imports a year, instead of a tariff.

At the world price of $3, buyers want 60 thousand tonnes more than farmers supply, but only 40 may come in. There is a shortage, so the domestic price rises. It keeps rising until the gap between Qd and Qs equals the quota:

| Price ($ per kg) | Qd | Qs | Qd − Qs |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3.00 | 90 | 30 | 60 |
| 3.50 | 85 | 35 | 50 |
| **4.00** | **80** | **40** | **40** |
| 4.50 | 75 | 45 | 30 |

So the price settles at **$4**, the same as the $1 tariff. The effects:

- **Domestic production rises** from 30 to 40 thousand tonnes. Domestic farmers fill part of the gap the quota leaves.
- **Price rises and consumption falls** (90 to 80), so **consumer surplus falls**.
- **Total surplus falls**: the same deadweight loss appears, because too much is grown at home and too little is consumed.
- **No tariff revenue.** The government collects nothing. The gap between the domestic price and the world price on each imported unit goes to whoever holds the right to import (background: this is often called quota rent).

A quota set at or above free-trade imports (60 here) has no effect: it does not bind.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Imports equal the quantity demanded at the world price."** Imports are the **gap**: Qd − Qs. Tarvos buys 90 but grows 30 itself, so it imports 60.
- **"Free trade makes everyone in the country better off."** Total surplus rises, but one group loses: producers when the country imports, consumers when it exports.
- **"Producers' lost surplus is a loss to the country."** Most of it (B + C) is a transfer to domestic consumers. The country's net change is the gain triangle.
- **"A tariff shifts the domestic supply curve."** The domestic supply curve does not move. The price at which imports are available rises from Pw to Pw + t, and producers move **along** their supply curve.
- **"Tariff revenue is part of the deadweight loss."** Revenue is a transfer from consumers to the government. It stays in the country's total surplus. Only J and L are lost.
- **"A small country's tariff lowers the world price, so foreign sellers pay it."** In this model the world price does not change; the domestic price rises by the full tariff.
- **"A quota raises revenue like a tariff."** The government earns nothing from a quota (unless it sells import licences, which is beyond this topic).
- **"Exporting raises the price, so total surplus falls."** Consumers lose, but producers gain more; total surplus rises.
