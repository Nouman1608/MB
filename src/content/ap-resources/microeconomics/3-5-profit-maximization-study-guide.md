## The question every firm must answer

A firm can produce many different quantities. Which one should it choose? In this course we assume that firms want to make as much **economic profit** as possible.

**Economic profit = total revenue (TR) − total cost (TC)**

Total cost here includes both explicit and implicit costs, as you saw in Topic 3.4. So "maximize profit" means "make the gap between TR and TC as large as possible".

There are two ways to find that output:

1. **The total approach.** Work out TR − TC at every output and pick the largest.
2. **The marginal approach.** Compare what the **next unit** adds to revenue with what it adds to cost.

Both give the same answer. The marginal approach is the one the course tests most, because it explains **why** the answer is right, and it works on a graph.

## Marginal revenue and marginal cost

**Marginal revenue (MR)** is the extra revenue from selling one more unit:

**MR = change in TR ÷ change in quantity**

**Marginal cost (MC)** is the extra cost of producing one more unit (Topic 3.2):

**MC = change in TC ÷ change in quantity**

The change in profit from one more unit is simply **MR − MC**.

- If MR > MC, the unit adds more to revenue than to cost. Profit **rises**. Produce it.
- If MR < MC, the unit adds more to cost than to revenue. Profit **falls**. Do not produce it.

So the firm keeps adding units while MR is above MC and stops where the two meet. This gives the **profit-maximizing rule**:

**Produce the quantity where MR = MC (where MC is rising).**

This is marginal analysis again, the same idea as in Topic 1.6: keep doing an activity while its marginal benefit is at least its marginal cost. For a firm, the marginal benefit of a unit is its MR.

### Using the rule with whole units

Tables usually give MR and MC for whole units. Then MR may never exactly equal MC. Use this version of the rule:

- Produce **every unit whose MR is greater than or equal to its MC**.
- Stop **before the first unit whose MC is greater than its MR** (once MC is rising).

If one unit has MR exactly equal to MC, producing it leaves profit unchanged. The usual convention is to produce that unit, so the answer is the quantity where MR = MC.

### What MR looks like for different firms

| Type of firm | What happens to price as it sells more | MR |
|---|---|---|
| A firm that **takes the market price** (it is too small to affect it) | price stays the same | **MR = price** for every unit |
| A firm that **must lower its price** to sell more | price falls | **MR < price**, and MR falls as output rises |

Why is MR below price in the second case? To sell one more unit, the firm must cut the price on **every** unit it sells, not just the extra one. The revenue lost on the earlier units pulls MR below the price. You will meet these firms in detail in Unit 4. The rule MR = MC is the same for both.

## Worked example 1: a firm that takes the market price

**Question.** Hollin Candle Co., a fictional workshop, sells boxes of candles at the market price of $15 per box. It cannot change this price. Its fixed cost is $30 per day.

| Boxes per day (Q) | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 | 8 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Total cost (TC, $) | 30 | 40 | 47 | 53 | 60 | 69 | 81 | 97 | 116 |

(a) Find MR and MC for each box.
(b) Use the marginal approach to find the profit-maximizing output.
(c) Check your answer with the total approach, and state the profit.
(d) The market price falls to $10. Find the new best output and the result.

**(a)** MR is $15 for every box, because each extra box adds $15 to revenue. MC is the change in TC from one row to the next:

| Box | 1st | 2nd | 3rd | 4th | 5th | 6th | 7th | 8th |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| MR ($) | 15 | 15 | 15 | 15 | 15 | 15 | 15 | 15 |
| MC ($) | 10 | 7 | 6 | 7 | 9 | 12 | 16 | 19 |

**(b)**

1. Boxes 1 to 6 each have MR ($15) greater than MC. Each adds to profit.
2. The 7th box has MC = $16 > MR = $15. It would reduce profit by $1.
3. So the profit-maximizing output is **6 boxes per day**.

**(c)** TR = $15 × Q.

| Q | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 | 8 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| TR ($) | 0 | 15 | 30 | 45 | 60 | 75 | 90 | 105 | 120 |
| TC ($) | 30 | 40 | 47 | 53 | 60 | 69 | 81 | 97 | 116 |
| Profit ($) | −30 | −25 | −17 | −8 | 0 | 6 | **9** | 8 | 4 |

Profit is largest at **6 boxes: $90 − $81 = $9 per day**. The two approaches agree.

**(d)** Now MR = $10 for every box.

1. The 1st box has MC = $10 = MR, so it neither adds nor removes profit. MC is still **falling** here, so do not stop: the next boxes cost less than $10 each.
2. Boxes 2 to 5 have MC of $7, $6, $7 and $9, all below $10. Produce them.
3. The 6th box has MC = $12 > $10, and MC is rising from here. Stop at **5 boxes**.
4. TR = $10 × 5 = $50; TC = $69. Result = **−$19 per day**, a loss.

Check: at 4 boxes the result is $40 − $60 = −$20; at 6 boxes it is $60 − $81 = −$21. So 5 boxes gives the **smallest loss**. Producing nothing would give a loss of $30 (the fixed cost). Topic 3.6 explains how a firm decides between producing at a loss and shutting down.

**Interpretation.** The rule MR = MC finds the **best** output, but "best" can mean "smallest loss". Notice also part (d) step 1: MR equals MC at the first box, but MC is falling there. That crossing is not the profit-maximizing point.

On a graph, find the profit-maximizing output in three steps:

1. Draw MR and MC with dollars per unit on the vertical axis and quantity on the horizontal axis.
2. Find where MC **rises through** MR.
3. Drop a line down to the quantity axis. That quantity is the profit-maximizing output.

To the left of that point, MR is above MC, so each extra unit adds profit. To the right, MC is above MR, so each extra unit cuts profit. Profit is highest exactly at the crossing.

Notice what the graph does **not** show directly: the size of the profit. To find it you need average total cost or TR and TC. You will do this on a full cost-curve graph in Topics 3.6 and 3.7.

## Worked example 2: a firm that must lower its price to sell more

**Question.** Larchmont Glass Studio, a fictional workshop, sells hand-blown glass bowls. To sell more bowls per day it must lower its price. Its fixed cost is $20 per day.

| Bowls per day (Q) | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Price ($) | 30 | 28 | 26 | 24 | 22 | 20 | 18 |
| MC of that bowl ($) | 8 | 10 | 12 | 14 | 16 | 18 | 20 |

(a) Calculate TR and MR.
(b) Find the profit-maximizing output and price.
(c) Calculate the maximum profit.
(d) The owner says, "Sell 7 bowls. More sales means more revenue." Evaluate this.

**(a)** TR = price × quantity. MR = change in TR.

| Q | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| TR ($) | 30 | 56 | 78 | 96 | 110 | 120 | 126 |
| MR ($) | 30 | 26 | 22 | 18 | 14 | 10 | 6 |

MR is below the price from the 2nd bowl on. For example, the 4th bowl sells for $24, but to sell it the studio cuts the price of the first 3 bowls by $2 each. So MR = $24 − $6 = $18.

**(b)**

1. Bowls 1 to 4: MR ($30, $26, $22, $18) is above MC ($8, $10, $12, $14). Each adds profit.
2. The 5th bowl: MR = $14 < MC = $16. It would reduce profit by $2.
3. So the profit-maximizing output is **4 bowls per day**.
4. The price comes from the price row at that quantity: **$24 per bowl**. Do not use MR as the price.

**(c)** TC = fixed cost + the sum of the MCs: $20 + $8 + $10 + $12 + $14 = $64. TR = $24 × 4 = $96. Profit = $96 − $64 = **$32 per day**.

Check with the totals: profit is $28 at 3 bowls and $30 at 5 bowls, both less than $32.

**(d)** The owner is right that revenue rises: TR is $126 at 7 bowls, more than $96. But the 5th, 6th and 7th bowls each cost more to make than they add to revenue (MR of $14, $10 and $6 against MC of $16, $18 and $20). TC rises to $20 + $98 = $118, so profit falls to $126 − $118 = **$8**. Maximizing revenue is not the same as maximizing profit.

## Why fixed costs do not change the output

Suppose Hollin Candle Co.'s rent rises, so its fixed cost goes up from $30 to $50 per day. Fixed costs do not change with output, so **MC does not change**. The 7th box still costs $16 more than the 6th. The firm still makes **6 boxes**, but its profit falls by $20, from $9 to −$11.

A change that **does** affect MC changes the output. If the cost of wax rises, every box's MC rises. MC now crosses MR at a lower quantity, so the firm makes fewer boxes. In the same way, a higher market price raises MR for a price-taking firm, so it makes more.

| Change | Effect on MR or MC | Profit-maximizing output |
|---|---|---|
| Fixed cost rises (rent, a yearly licence) | neither changes | **no change**; profit falls |
| Cost of a variable input rises (materials, hourly wages) | MC rises | **falls** |
| Market price rises (price-taking firm) | MR rises | **rises** |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Firms maximize profit where profit per unit is highest."** Profit per unit (price − ATC) is not the target. A firm may gain total profit by selling more units at a lower margin, as long as MR ≥ MC.
- **"Firms maximize revenue."** Revenue keeps rising after profit has peaked (Worked example 2(d)). The goal is the biggest gap between TR and TC.
- **"Produce where price equals MC."** That is the rule only when price = MR, for a price-taking firm. For a firm that must cut its price to sell more, use MR, not price.
- **"The price is where MR and MC cross."** The crossing gives the **quantity**. A price-setting firm reads its price from the demand curve at that quantity.
- **"MR = MC means the firm makes a profit."** It gives the largest profit **or the smallest loss** (Worked example 1(d)).
- **"Any point where MR = MC will do."** If MC crosses MR while MC is falling, producing more still raises profit. Use the crossing where MC is rising.
- **"Higher fixed costs make a firm cut output."** Fixed costs do not change MC, so the best output is unchanged in the short run.
