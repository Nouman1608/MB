## Markets need property rights, incentives and constraints

Unit 1 asked how an economy decides what to produce. In a market economy, much of the answer comes from **buyers and sellers trading**. This topic looks at the buyers' side.

Trade only works well when **property rights** are clear. A property right is a legally protected right to own, use and sell something. When you pay for a phone, you expect it to be yours, and you expect the law to back you if someone takes it. If ownership were unclear, or a seller could take the good back, few people would pay much for anything. A well-defined system of property rights is what makes buying worthwhile.

Buyers also **respond to incentives**. An incentive is anything that rewards or penalises a choice. Price is the most important one: a lower price rewards buying more. But buyers also face **constraints**, limits on what they can do:

- **Income:** you cannot spend more than you have (or can borrow).
- **Time:** a cinema trip takes three hours; a busy student may not have them.
- **Laws and regulations:** an age limit, a licence requirement or a ban can stop some people buying at any price.

Demand describes how buyers behave once all of these are taken into account.

## What demand means

**Demand** is the quantity of a good that buyers are **willing and able** to buy at each possible price, during a given period, with all other influences held constant.

Three phrases in that definition matter:

- **Willing and able.** Wanting a sports car is not demand unless you can pay for it.
- **At each possible price.** Demand is a whole list of price-quantity pairs (a **demand schedule**), or a whole curve, not one number.
- **Other influences held constant.** Economists call this *ceteris paribus* ("other things equal"). We change only the good's own price and watch what happens.

**Quantity demanded** is one number: the amount buyers want at one particular price.

## The law of demand

The **law of demand** says that, other things equal, when the price of a good rises, the quantity demanded falls; when the price falls, the quantity demanded rises. Price and quantity demanded move in **opposite** directions.

On a graph, price goes on the vertical axis and quantity on the horizontal axis. Because of the law of demand, the demand curve **slopes downward**. A change in the good's own price moves buyers **along** the curve. The curve itself stays where it is.

## Why the demand curve slopes downward

Three ideas explain the law of demand. Suppose the price of a cup of chai at a college café falls from $4 to $2.

1. **Substitution effect.** Chai is now cheaper compared with coffee, juice and other drinks. Some buyers switch from those drinks to chai.
2. **Income effect.** A lower price raises your **purchasing power** (your real income). With a weekly drinks budget of $20, you could afford 5 cups at $4 but 10 cups at $2. Feeling richer, you can buy more chai. (This is the normal case. You will meet goods where extra income reduces purchases below.)
3. **Diminishing marginal utility.** From Topic 1.6, each extra cup gives less extra satisfaction than the one before. You will only buy an extra cup, which you value less, if the price falls.

The third idea leads to a powerful way of reading a demand curve. A buyer keeps buying while the **marginal benefit (MB)** of the next unit is at least the price. So the height of the demand curve at any quantity shows the marginal benefit of the last unit bought. **The demand curve is a marginal benefit curve.**

## From marginal benefit to individual demand

Throughout this guide we use the café at **Larkfield College**, a fictional college. Three students buy chai there. Their marginal benefit from each cup in a week, valued in dollars, is:

| Cup | 1st | 2nd | 3rd | 4th | 5th | 6th | 7th | 8th |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Dina's MB ($) | 7 | 6 | 5 | 4 | 3 | 2 | 1 | – |
| Felix's MB ($) | 6 | 5 | 4 | 3 | 2 | 1 | – | – |
| Hana's MB ($) | 8 | 7 | 6 | 5 | 4 | 3 | 2 | 1 |

Each student buys every cup whose MB is **at least** the price. At $4, Dina buys her 1st to 4th cups (MB $7, $6, $5, $4) but not the 5th (MB $3). So her quantity demanded at $4 is 4 cups. Repeating this at each price gives each student's **individual demand schedule**:

| Price per cup | Dina | Felix | Hana |
|---|---|---|---|
| $1 | 7 | 6 | 8 |
| $2 | 6 | 5 | 7 |
| $3 | 5 | 4 | 6 |
| $4 | 4 | 3 | 5 |
| $5 | 3 | 2 | 4 |

Each schedule obeys the law of demand: a higher price, a smaller quantity.

## Worked example 1: market demand and a movement along the curve

**Question.** Use the three schedules above.
(a) Build the market demand schedule for chai at the Larkfield café.
(b) The café raises its price from $2 to $4 per cup. Find the change in market quantity demanded and describe it on a graph.
(c) Use marginal benefit to explain why Dina buys fewer cups.

**(a)** Market demand is the **sum** of the individual quantities demanded **at each price**. Add across each row:

| Price per cup | Dina + Felix + Hana | Market quantity demanded (cups per week) |
|---|---|---|
| $1 | 7 + 6 + 8 | **21** |
| $2 | 6 + 5 + 7 | **18** |
| $3 | 5 + 4 + 6 | **15** |
| $4 | 4 + 3 + 5 | **12** |
| $5 | 3 + 2 + 4 | **9** |

Because we add quantities at the same price, this is called **horizontal summation**: on a graph, you add the curves sideways, not up. A real market has thousands of buyers, but the method is the same.

**(b)** At $2, quantity demanded is 18 cups. At $4, it is 12 cups. Change = 12 − 18 = **−6 cups per week**.

**(c)** At $2, Dina buys every cup with MB of at least $2: her 1st to 6th cups. At $4, the 5th cup (MB $3) and 6th cup (MB $2) are no longer worth the price, so she drops them. She now buys 4 cups.

**Check.** Over the prices in the table, the market schedule falls by 3 cups for every $1 rise, so the points lie on a straight line, quantity demanded = 24 − 3 × price. At $4 this gives 24 − 12 = 12. The line is only a summary of the steps: at $8, Hana still buys her 1st cup (MB $8), and only above $8, the highest MB of any buyer, does nobody buy.

## Change in quantity demanded versus change in demand

This distinction appears in almost every exam question on markets.

| | Change in **quantity demanded** | Change in **demand** |
|---|---|---|
| Caused by | the good's **own price** | any **determinant** of demand (not own price) |
| On the graph | **movement along** the existing curve | the **whole curve shifts** |
| Words to use | "quantity demanded rises / falls" | "demand increases / decreases" |

An **increase in demand** shifts the curve **to the right**: buyers want more at every price, and each unit's marginal benefit is higher. A **decrease in demand** shifts it **to the left**.

## The determinants of demand

These are the influences, other than own price, that shift demand:

- **Income.** For a **normal good**, higher income increases demand. For an **inferior good**, higher income decreases demand, because buyers switch to something they prefer (for example, from bus travel to a car, or from the cheapest rice to a better brand).
- **Prices of related goods.**
  - **Substitutes** can be used in place of each other (chai and coffee). If the price of a substitute **falls**, demand for this good **decreases**.
  - **Complements** are used together (chai and biscuits). If the price of a complement **falls**, demand for this good **increases**.
- **Tastes and preferences.** Fashion, advertising, health news or habit can raise or lower demand.
- **Expectations.** If buyers expect the price to rise next month, demand **now** increases. Expected higher future income can also raise demand now.
- **Number of buyers.** More buyers (a bigger population or new customers arriving) increases market demand.

Constraints from laws and regulations work through these determinants. For example, a new minimum age for buying a good reduces the **number of buyers** who can legally purchase it, so market demand decreases.

## Worked example 2: shifting the demand curve

**Question.** Return to the Larkfield café. Treat each event separately, starting from the market schedule in Worked example 1 (call it D₁).
(a) The college raises its student grant. Chai is a normal good, and each student now buys **one more cup at every price**. Find the new schedule D₂.
(b) A rival café cuts the price of coffee. Each student's MB from every cup of chai falls by $1. Find the new schedule D₃.
(c) Compare quantity demanded at $3 on D₁, D₂ and D₃.

**(a)** Three students each add one cup, so the market adds 3 cups at every price:

| Price | D₁ | D₂ (grant rises) | D₃ (coffee cheaper) |
|---|---|---|---|
| $1 | 21 | **24** | **18** |
| $2 | 18 | **21** | **15** |
| $3 | 15 | **18** | **12** |
| $4 | 12 | **15** | **9** |
| $5 | 9 | **12** | **6** |

**(b)** If every MB value falls by $1, each student now buys one fewer cup at each price. For example, Dina's MB is now $6, $5, $4, $3… so at $4 she buys 3 cups instead of 4. Adding the three students gives the D₃ column: 3 fewer cups at every price. Coffee is a **substitute** for chai, so a cheaper substitute **decreases** demand for chai.

**(c)** At $3: D₁ gives 15 cups, D₂ gives 18 (3 more) and D₃ gives 12 (3 fewer). The price is the same in each case, so these are **shifts**, not movements along a curve.

## Common misconceptions

- **"A fall in price increases demand."** A fall in the good's own price increases **quantity demanded**. Demand (the whole curve) does not change.
- **"Demand rises, so the price rises, so demand falls again."** This mixes a shift with a movement. Treat the shift first; the effect on price is the subject of Topic 2.7.
- **"Higher income always increases demand."** Only for normal goods. For inferior goods, higher income decreases demand.
- **"If the price of a substitute falls, demand for this good rises."** It falls: buyers switch to the cheaper substitute.
- **"Market demand is the average of individual demands."** It is the **sum** of quantities demanded at each price.
- **"Add demand curves vertically."** Add quantities at the same price: horizontally.
- **"Wanting something is demand."** Demand needs willingness **and** ability to pay.
- **"An increase in demand moves the curve up and to the left."** An increase moves it **to the right** (or, equally, up): more is wanted at every price.
