## Understanding

- I can define elastic, inelastic and perfectly inelastic collisions using the total kinetic energy of the system. *(Guide: "Two conservation questions for every collision"; Practice Q1)*
- I can explain that momentum is conserved in every collision of an isolated system, whatever happens to kinetic energy. *(Guide: Common misconceptions; Practice Q1(D), Q3(D))*
- I can explain that individual kinetic energies may change in an elastic collision while the system total stays the same. *(Guide: Worked example 2; Practice Q1)*
- I can explain where the kinetic energy goes in an inelastic collision, and why total energy is still conserved. *(Guide: "Inelastic collisions"; Figure 2)*

## Skills

- I can find the common velocity after a perfectly inelastic collision, with signed velocities. *(Guide: Worked example 1; Practice Q2)*
- I can classify a collision from masses and velocities by comparing kinetic energy totals. *(Guide: Worked examples 2 and 3; Practice Q4)*
- I can derive the fraction of kinetic energy transformed when a moving object sticks to one at rest, and use it to predict changes. *(Guide: Worked example 1(c), "Comparing scenarios with the expressions"; Practice Q3)*
- I can read collision data from a velocity–time graph and find a force and the centre-of-mass velocity. *(Guide: Figure 1; Practice Q5)*
- I can plot K_after against K_before and use its slope to test a prediction. *(Guide: "Testing whether a collision is elastic"; Practice Q6)*

## Reasoning

- I can explain why kinetic-energy conservation must not be used across a sticky collision, and solve a two-stage problem with momentum then energy. *(Practice Q7)*
- I can argue that objects bouncing apart is not enough to show a collision is elastic. *(Guide: Worked example 3)*
- I can explain why an explosion increases a system's kinetic energy while conserving its momentum. *(Guide: "Explosions: the reverse of sticking together")*
