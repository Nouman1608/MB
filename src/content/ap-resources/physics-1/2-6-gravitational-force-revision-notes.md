Short on time? This page is the recap. For diagrams, graphs and worked examples, use the [full study guide](/advanced-course-resources/physics-1/2-6-gravitational-force-study-guide/). This is the algebra-based course: no calculus is needed.

## Recap

- Any two masses **attract**. The force acts along the line joining their centres of mass and can be drawn from each centre of mass.
- The two forces are a third-law pair: equal and opposite, whatever the masses.
- A **field** gives the force per kilogram at each point. Gravitational field strength g is in N/kg, which equals m/s².
- **Weight** is the gravitational force from a planet or moon on a nearby small object: mg.
- g is effectively **constant** when the object’s distance from the centre changes by only a tiny fraction (anything near the ground).
- **Apparent weight** is the normal force. It differs from mg whenever the system accelerates. A system **appears weightless** when gravity is the only force on it (free fall), or when no forces act.
- **Equivalence principle:** in an accelerating frame, you cannot tell apparent weight from real gravity.
- **Inertial mass** (resists changes in motion) and **gravitational mass** (feels gravity) are equal by experiment, so all objects fall with the same acceleration.

## Key relationships

| Relationship | Symbols and units | Use it to |
|---|---|---|
| F_g = G m₁m₂ / r² | G = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N·m²/kg², r in m | force between two masses |
| g = F_g / m = GM / r² | N/kg = m/s² | field strength at distance r from mass M |
| weight = mg | g = 9.8 N/kg at Earth’s surface | gravitational force near a planet |
| F_N = m(g + a_y), +y up | a_y signed | apparent weight in a lift |
| F_g ∝ 1/r² | | factor-of-change: 2r → ¼ force |

## Assumptions behind the numbers

- Planets and spheres act as if all their mass is at their centre (for points outside them).
- Near Earth’s surface, g = 9.8 N/kg (10 N/kg where stated).
- The ground is an inertial frame; a lift that accelerates is not.

## Mistakes to avoid

1. **Using surface-to-surface distance.** Use centre to centre, so add the planet’s radius to a height.
2. **Forgetting to square r.** Halving r gives four times the force.
3. **Saying the larger body pulls harder.** The forces are equal; the smaller body accelerates more.
4. **Linking scale readings to velocity.** Only the acceleration matters.
5. **"No gravity in orbit."** g is still large; there is just no normal force.
6. **Mixing mass and weight.** kg is not N.

## Quick self-check

1. The distance between two objects is tripled. By what factor does the gravitational force change? *(It becomes one ninth.)*
2. A 70 kg astronaut stands on the Moon, where g = 1.6 N/kg. What is her weight there? *(about 110 N)*
3. Take +y up. A 50 kg person stands on a scale in a lift accelerating downward at 2.0 m/s². What does the scale read? *(390 N)*
