## How to use this guide

This guide is for students in the **algebra-based Physics 1 course** who know the physics but lose marks on diagrams, derivations, explanations and lab questions. It covers the whole course.

Every example and task here is **original Marlbridge practice**, not past exam material. The marks shown are a **suggested Marlbridge rubric**, not official scoring.

## The free-response section at a glance

Section II has **4 questions in 95 minutes** and is worth **50%** of the exam. You read the questions in Bluebook and handwrite answers in a paper booklet. Any four-function, scientific or graphing calculator is allowed. The order is fixed:

| Question | Type | Points | Suggested time | Main demands |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Mathematical Routines (MR) | 10 | 20–25 min | Representation, derivation, calculation, justified claim |
| 2 | Translation Between Representations (TBR) | 12 | 25–30 min | Diagram, derived equation, graph, then consistency or prediction |
| 3 | Experimental Design and Analysis (LAB) | 10 | 25–30 min | Procedure, then plotting and analysing given data |
| 4 | Qualitative/Quantitative Translation (QQT) | 8 | 15–20 min | Reasoned claim, derivation, link between them |

Practice 1 (creating representations) is tested **only** in this section, never in multiple choice.

## Representations that earn marks

A representation scores when it is **complete** and **agrees** with the situation.

| Representation | Rules that earn credit | Common way to lose it |
|---|---|---|
| Free-body diagram | A dot for the system; one straight arrow per force, starting on the dot; each labelled with its type and what exerts it; longer arrows for bigger forces | Adding "ma", a net force or velocity; drawing components; missing a force |
| Position–time graph | Gradient = velocity. Curving upwards = acceleration in the + direction. | Sharp corners where velocity changes smoothly |
| Velocity–time graph | Gradient = acceleration; area = displacement. A straight line means constant acceleration. | Treating the line's height as position |
| Energy bar chart | Name the system and the zero of U_g; one bar per energy store at each moment; a zero value is a line on the zero level; total constant if no outside work | No ΔE_thermal bar when friction acts inside the system |

## Deriving symbolically, then checking

"Derive" means start from a law or an equation-sheet relationship and show each algebraic step. Four steps:

1. **Write the starting principle in symbols**, such as ΣF_x = ma_x or K_i + U_i = K_f + U_f + ΔE_thermal.
2. **Substitute from your diagram.** Each arrow on your free-body diagram becomes a term with a sign.
3. **Solve for the target** using only the symbols allowed. No numbers yet.
4. **Check the result** before moving on.

| Check | How to do it | What a pass looks like |
|---|---|---|
| Units | Replace each symbol with its SI unit and simplify | Both sides have the same unit |
| Limiting case | Set one quantity to an extreme (0, 90°, very large) | The result matches what you know for that simple case |
| Direction of change | Increase one variable in your head | The answer changes the way physics says it should |

## Translating between representations

When a question shows one situation in several forms, use a shared feature as a bridge:

| From | To | Bridge to look for |
|---|---|---|
| Free-body diagram | Velocity–time graph | Net force direction gives the sign of the gradient |
| Velocity–time graph | Position–time graph | Height of the v–t line gives the gradient of the x–t curve |
| Energy bar chart | Equation | Each bar becomes one term; bars before = bars after |
| Equation | Graph | Which variable is on each axis, and whether the relationship is linear, squared or inverse |

To justify agreement, name the **shared feature** and show it matches in both. "They agree" alone earns nothing.

## The paragraph argument

The QQT question, and parts of the MR and TBR questions, ask for a claim backed by reasoning **beyond** an equation. Use this structure:

1. **Claim.** One clear sentence: greater than, less than, equal, increases, decreases.
2. **Governing law.** Name it and say why it applies ("it floats, so it is in equilibrium").
3. **Chain.** Two or three linked steps, each saying *what* changes and *why*.
4. **Link back.** Show that any equation you derived predicts the same.

## Designing and analysing an experiment

A sound procedure:

- changes **one** independent variable over at least five values and measures **one** dependent variable;
- keeps everything else fixed, and says so;
- names realistic lab equipment (metre stick, stopwatch, motion or force sensor, balance);
- repeats and averages, or uses a sensor;
- says how the data will be analysed, usually with a straight-line graph.

**Linearising** turns a curved relationship into a straight line, so the gradient gives a constant:

| Relationship | Plot vertical vs horizontal | Gradient equals |
|---|---|---|
| d = ½at² | d vs t² | a ÷ 2 |
| v² = 2gh | v² vs h | 2g |
| T = 2π√(m ÷ k) | T² vs m | 4π² ÷ k |
| F = mv² ÷ r (fixed m, r) | F vs v² | m ÷ r |

Take the gradient from **two far-apart points on your best-fit line** and give its units.

## Worked example 1: Deriving a pulling force (Unit 2)

**Question.** A student drags a 15 kg box across a carpet at constant speed, pulling a strap at angle θ above the horizontal. The coefficient of kinetic friction is μ_k. (a) Draw a free-body diagram. (b) Derive the tension T in terms of m, g, θ and μ_k. (c) Evaluate it for θ = 30° and μ_k = 0.40, and check your expression.

**Weak answer.** "Forces: weight, normal, friction, tension. Friction = μmg, so T cos θ = μmg and T = μmg ÷ cos θ = 68 N."

**What it misses.** No labelled diagram. The strap pulls partly upward, so F_N is **less** than mg, and friction is overestimated. No check.

**Strong answer.**

(a) Four arrows from a dot: F_g (Earth on box) down; F_N (carpet on box) up, shorter than F_g; F_f (carpet on box) backwards; F_T (strap on box) at θ above the horizontal, forwards.

(b) Vertical: F_N + T sin θ − mg = 0, so F_N = mg − T sin θ. Horizontal, constant velocity: T cos θ − μ_k(mg − T sin θ) = 0. So **T = μ_k mg ÷ (cos θ + μ_k sin θ)**.

(c) T = 0.40 × 147 ÷ (0.866 + 0.200) = **55 N** (55.2 N), with F_N = 119 N.

Checks: units are N, since μ_k and trig terms have none. θ = 0 gives T = μ_k mg = 59 N, the horizontal-pull result. μ_k = 0 gives T = 0: no friction, no force needed at constant speed.

| Point | What earns it (suggested Marlbridge rubric) |
|---|---|
| 1 | Four labelled forces, F_N shorter than F_g, no extra arrows |
| 1 | F_N = mg − T sin θ from the vertical equation |
| 1 | Correct expression for T |
| 1 | 55 N |
| 1 | A valid limiting-case or units check, stated |

## Worked example 2: Energy bar charts and a derivation (Unit 3)

**Question.** A 2.0 kg block is released from rest at height H = 1.5 m on a ramp inclined at 35°, with μ_k = 0.20. (a) Draw energy bar charts for the top (A) and bottom (B). (b) Derive the speed at the bottom in terms of g, H, θ and μ_k. (c) Show that your chart and equation agree.

**Weak answer.** "At A it has potential energy, at B kinetic. mgH = ½mv², so v = √(2gH) = 5.4 m/s." Bar charts show one U_g bar at A and an equal K bar at B.

**What it misses.** No system named. Friction turns some energy into thermal energy, so the K bar must be **shorter** than the U_g bar, and the speed is too large.

**Strong answer.** (a) System: block + Earth + ramp, with U_g = 0 at the bottom.

(b) mgH = ½mv² + ΔE_th. Friction is μ_k mg cos θ over a ramp length H ÷ sin θ, so ΔE_th = μ_k mgH ÷ tan θ. The mass cancels: **v = √[2gH(1 − μ_k ÷ tan θ)]** = √[2(9.8)(1.5)(1 − 0.286)] = **4.6 m/s** (4.58 m/s).

(c) The bracket, 0.714, is the fraction of the 29.4 J that becomes kinetic: 0.714 × 29.4 J = 21.0 J, the K bar. Checks: μ_k = 0 gives √(2gH); μ_k = tan θ gives v = 0, as friction then balances gravity along the slope.

## Worked example 3: The paragraph argument (Unit 8)

**Question.** A garden sprayer is a wide, closed tank of water. A pump raises the air above the water to a gauge pressure P_g. Water leaves through a nozzle at the same height as the water surface. (a) A student says that doubling P_g doubles the speed of the jet. Is the student right? Justify your claim without equations. (b) Derive the jet speed v in terms of P_g and the water density ρ, and evaluate it for P_g = 2.0 × 10⁵ Pa. (c) Explain whether (a) and (b) agree.

**Weak answer.** "More pressure pushes the water harder, so doubling the pressure doubles the speed. P = ½ρv², so v = 20 m/s."

**What it misses.** The claim is wrong, and "pushes harder" is not a reason: no law, no chain, and no part (c). Its own equation contradicts its claim.

**Strong answer.**

(a) **No: the speed rises by less than double.** Energy is conserved as the water flows (Bernoulli's equation). The pressure does work on each cubic metre of water pushed out, and that work becomes kinetic energy. Doubling P_g doubles the kinetic energy of each cubic metre. Kinetic energy depends on the square of the speed, so the speed grows by a factor less than 2.

(b) Bernoulli from the surface (pressure P_atm + P_g, speed close to 0 in a wide tank) to the nozzle (pressure P_atm, same height): P_g = ½ρv², so **v = √(2P_g ÷ ρ)** = √(2 × 2.0 × 10⁵ ÷ 1000) = **20 m/s**.

(c) They agree: P_g sits under a square root, so doubling it multiplies v by √2 ≈ 1.4, giving 28 m/s, not 40 m/s. Limiting case: P_g = 0 gives v = 0.

| Point | What earns it (suggested Marlbridge rubric) |
|---|---|
| 1 | Correct claim: the speed less than doubles |
| 1 | Energy conservation (Bernoulli) named as the governing law |
| 1 | Chain: double pressure, double kinetic energy per volume, speed up by a factor under 2 |
| 1 | Correct derivation and 20 m/s |
| 1 | Agreement explained using the square root (factor √2) |

## Worked example 4: Design and linearise (Unit 1)

**Question.** (a) Describe a procedure to find the acceleration of a cart released from rest on a fixed incline. (b) A group records the data below. Choose what to plot so the graph is a straight line, and find the acceleration.

| d (m) | 0.20 | 0.40 | 0.60 | 0.80 | 1.00 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| t (s) | 0.70 | 0.97 | 1.21 | 1.37 | 1.55 |

**Weak answer.** "Roll the cart down and time it. Plot d against t. Using the last row, a = 2d ÷ t² = 0.83 m/s²."

**What it misses.** One trial, no variable changed. d against t is a curve, and one data point ignores the rest.

**Strong answer.**

(a) Keep the angle and cart fixed. Release from rest at marked distances d = 0.20 m to 1.00 m (metre stick) from a fixed finish line. Time each run with a stopwatch, three trials averaged per distance, or use a motion sensor.

(b) From rest, d = ½at², so plot **d (vertical) against t² (horizontal)**. The t² values are 0.49, 0.94, 1.46, 1.88 and 2.40 s², close to a straight line through the origin. Two points on the best-fit line are (0.48 s², 0.20 m) and (2.15 s², 0.90 m). Gradient = 0.70 ÷ 1.67 = 0.42 m/s². Since the gradient = a ÷ 2, **a = 0.84 m/s²**.

| Point | What earns it (suggested Marlbridge rubric) |
|---|---|
| 1 | One independent variable (d), one measured (t), the rest fixed |
| 1 | Realistic equipment, with repeats or a sensor |
| 1 | d against t² (or equivalent linear pair) |
| 1 | Gradient from the best-fit line, with units |
| 1 | a = 2 × gradient = 0.84 m/s² |

## Common mistakes

- Drawing components, "ma" or a "net force" arrow on a free-body diagram.
- Assuming F_N = mg when another force has a vertical component or the object accelerates vertically.
- Putting numbers in at the start of a "derive" part, so the expression cannot be checked.
- Energy bar charts with no system named, or with no thermal bar when friction acts.
- Saying two representations "agree" without naming the feature that matches.
- Designing an experiment that changes two variables at once, or uses one trial.
- Taking the gradient from two data points instead of the best-fit line, or forgetting its units.

## Practise it

**Task 1 (Unit 1).** A cart's velocity rises steadily from 0 to 3.0 m/s in 2.0 s, then stays at 3.0 m/s for 3.0 s. Describe the position–time graph and find the total displacement.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

From 0 to 2.0 s the x–t graph curves upward from a zero gradient. From 2.0 s to 5.0 s it is a straight line of gradient 3.0 m/s, joining smoothly with no corner. Displacement = area under v–t = ½(2.0)(3.0) + (3.0)(3.0) = **12 m**.
</details>

**Task 2 (Unit 4).** A 0.40 kg ball at rest is struck. The force rises linearly from 0 to 600 N in 2.0 ms, then falls linearly to 0 at 4.0 ms. Find the impulse, the ball's final speed and the average force.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

Impulse = area of the triangle = ½(600)(0.0040) = **1.2 N·s**. Δp = J, so v = 1.2 ÷ 0.40 = **3.0 m/s**. Average force = 1.2 ÷ 0.0040 = **300 N**, half the peak, as a triangle should give.
</details>

**Task 3 (Unit 5).** A uniform chest lid of mass M and width L is hinged along its back edge. You hold it open at angle θ above the horizontal by lifting straight up on its front edge. Derive the force you need and check it.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

Torques about the hinge. Lever arms are horizontal distances: L cos θ for your lift, (L ÷ 2) cos θ for the weight. So F L cos θ − Mg(L ÷ 2) cos θ = 0, and **F = Mg ÷ 2**, whatever the angle. Units: N. Check: θ = 0 gives a horizontal lid held at one end, which needs half its weight at each end.
</details>

**Task 4 (Unit 6).** A flywheel (I = 0.50 kg·m²) spins at 40 rad/s and drives a small generator that lights a 5.0 W lamp. Assume all of the flywheel's kinetic energy reaches the lamp at that steady rate. Claim how the lighting time changes if the flywheel starts at 80 rad/s instead, justify, then derive the time and check your claim.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

The time becomes **four times as long**. The lamp uses the stored kinetic energy at the same rate either way. Rotational kinetic energy depends on the square of ω, so doubling ω stores four times the energy. Derivation: t = K ÷ P = Iω₀² ÷ (2P) = 0.50 × 40² ÷ (2 × 5.0) = **80 s**. At 80 rad/s, t = 320 s, agreeing with the claim. Units: kg·m²/s² ÷ W = J ÷ (J/s) = s.
</details>
