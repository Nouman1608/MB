Short on time? This page is the recap. For explanations, diagrams and worked examples, use the [full study guide](/advanced-course-resources/physics-1/2-1-systems-center-mass-study-guide/). This is the algebra-based course: no calculus is needed.

## Recap

- A **system** is what you choose to study; the rest is the **environment**. Show the choice with a dashed boundary.
- **Internal** interactions are between parts inside the boundary. **External** interactions cross it.
- A system's properties come from its parts and their interactions. Its internal structure affects how you analyse it.
- If the parts do not matter for the question, treat the system as **one object** at its center of mass, with the total mass.
- Parts can move differently from each other and from the system as a whole. Changing outside conditions can change the structure (a stack that starts to slide apart).
- Energy or mass can cross the boundary (an **open** system). Moving the boundary can often make the system closed.

## Key relationships

| Relationship | Symbols and units | Use it to |
|---|---|---|
| x_cm = Σmᵢxᵢ ÷ Σmᵢ | m in kg, x in m | locate the center of mass along x |
| y_cm = Σmᵢyᵢ ÷ Σmᵢ | m in kg, y in m | locate it along y (separate calculation) |
| symmetry rule | — | place it on every line of symmetry without arithmetic |
| uniform symmetric part → one particle at its centre | — | handle a plank, rod or plate in a calculation |
| distance ratio = inverse mass ratio (two particles) | — | check a two-particle answer |

## Assumptions behind the numbers

- Small objects are **particles**: all their mass at one point.
- "Uniform" means mass is spread evenly, so the part's center of mass is at its geometric centre.
- Light frames, rods or strings have **negligible mass** unless a mass is given.
- The course calculates centers of mass for **up to five particles** in two dimensions, or for highly symmetric systems.

## Mistakes to avoid

1. **No origin stated.** The value of x_cm means nothing without one.
2. **Midpoint used for unequal masses.** The center of mass is nearer the heavier mass.
3. **Dividing by the number of particles** instead of the total mass.
4. **Forgetting a symmetric part's own mass**, such as the plank under the objects on it.
5. **Assuming the center of mass is inside the material.** A ring's is in its empty centre.
6. **Treating a system as one object when its parts move relative to each other** in a way that matters.
7. **Calling an interaction internal without drawing the boundary.** It depends on the system you chose.

## Quick self-check

1. With +x to the right and the origin at a 4.0 kg mass, a 1.0 kg mass sits at x = 1.0 m. Where is the center of mass? *(x_cm = 0.20 m)*
2. Where is the center of mass of a uniform square tile? *(Where its diagonals cross: on all four lines of symmetry.)*
3. Three equal masses sit at x = 0, 0.30 m and 0.90 m. Where is their center of mass? *(0.40 m: with equal masses, the plain mean of the positions.)*
4. A student pushes a box that has a smaller box resting on it. For the system "both boxes", is the friction between the boxes internal or external? *(Internal: both boxes are inside the boundary.)*
