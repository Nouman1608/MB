This set practises **3.3.4.3 Addition polymers** from section 3.3.4 Alkenes of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Chemistry (9620) specification, Version 5.3. It is International AS content, assessed in Unit 2 and in the synoptic Unit 5. Revise first with the [addition polymers study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-3-4-3/) or the [addition polymers revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-3-4-3-revision-notes/). Take Ar values as H = 1.0, C = 12.0, F = 19.0 and Cl = 35.5. Write repeating units as structural formulae in brackets, for example –[CH₂–CHCl]–, with n understood.

## Section A: short questions

**1.** Which is the repeating unit of poly(but-1-ene)?

A: –[CH₂–CH₂–CH₂–CH₂]–

B: –[CH₂–CH(CH₂CH₃)]–

C: –[CH(CH₃)–CH(CH₃)]–

D: –[CH₂=CH(CH₂CH₃)]– **[1]**

**2.** Give the IUPAC name of the polymer made from CH₂=CHCl. **[1]**

**3.** Write the repeating unit of the polymer formed from methyl propenoate, CH₂=CHCOOCH₃. **[1]**

**4.** A section of polymer chain is –CF₂–CCl₂–CF₂–CCl₂–CF₂–CCl₂–.

**(a)** Give the repeating unit. **[1]**

**(b)** Give the structural formula of the monomer. **[1]**

**5.** Explain why addition polymers such as poly(propene) are unreactive. **[2]**

**6.** Write the repeating unit of the polymer formed from 2-methylbut-2-ene, (CH₃)₂C=CHCH₃, and name the polymer. **[2]**

## Section B: structured questions

**7.** Poly(ethene) is a solid at room temperature even though its chains are non-polar.

**(a)** Name the type of intermolecular force between poly(ethene) chains. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain why the total attraction between chains is large. **[2]**

**(c)** Name the additional type of intermolecular force between chains of poly(chloroethene), and explain why it is present. **[2]**

**8.** Low-density and high-density poly(ethene) are made from the same monomer by different processes.

**(a)** Describe the difference in chain structure between the two forms. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain why high-density poly(ethene) is stiffer and has a higher melting point. **[2]**

**9.** PVC is used both as rigid window frames and as flexible cable insulation.

**(a)** Explain how a plasticiser changes the properties of PVC. **[3]**

**(b)** Suggest why the plasticiser does not change the chemical formula of the repeating unit. **[1]**

**10.** A sample of poly(tetrafluoroethene) has an average Mr of 4.00 × 10⁵.

**(a)** Calculate the Mr of the monomer. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the average number of monomer units in a chain. **[1]**

**(c)** State the mass of tetrafluoroethene that forms 5.00 tonnes of polymer, assuming complete conversion, and explain your answer. **[2]**

**11.** A student draws the repeating unit of poly(propene) as –[CH₂–CH₂–CH₂]–. Explain the error and give the correct repeating unit. **[2]**

**12.** A section of polymer chain is –CH₂–CH(C₆H₅)–CH₂–CH(C₆H₅)–CH₂–CH(C₆H₅)–.

**(a)** How many repeating units are shown? **[1]**

**(b)** Name the monomer and the polymer. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain why this polymer has no C=C bonds even though the monomer has one. **[1]**

**13.** Poly(ethene) was first made in a high-pressure process. Later, new catalysts allowed it to be made at lower pressure with different properties.

**(a)** Explain how this illustrates that knowledge and understanding of polymer production and properties has developed over time. **[2]**

**(b)** Suggest why a manufacturer of plastic bags would choose the high-pressure form, and a manufacturer of water pipes the low-pressure form. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** **B** [1]

**2.** **Poly(chloroethene)** [1]

**3.** **–[CH₂–CH(COOCH₃)]–** [1]

**4. (a)** **–[CF₂–CCl₂]–** [1]

**(b)** **CF₂=CCl₂** [1]

**5.** The polymer chain is saturated / has no C=C bonds for electrophiles to attack [1]; its C–C and C–H bonds are strong and almost non-polar [1]

**6.** **–[C(CH₃)₂–CH(CH₃)]–** [1]; **poly(2-methylbut-2-ene)** [1]
*Examiner insight:* The two methyl groups on one carbon and the single methyl on the other must stay where they were in the monomer.

**7. (a)** Van der Waals forces (induced dipole–dipole forces) [1]

**(b)** Each chain contains a very large number of electrons, giving large induced dipoles [1]; the long chains are in contact with their neighbours along a great length, so many attractions add together [1]

**(c)** Permanent dipole–dipole forces [1]; the C–Cl bonds are polar because chlorine is more electronegative than carbon [1]

**8. (a)** Low-density poly(ethene) has branched chains [1]; high-density poly(ethene) has few branches (mainly unbranched chains) [1]

**(b)** Unbranched chains can pack more closely together [1]; so the van der Waals forces between chains are stronger, needing more energy to separate them [1]

**9. (a)** Plasticiser molecules fit between the polymer chains [1]; they push the chains further apart, so the forces between chains are weaker [1]; the chains can slide past each other more easily, so the PVC becomes flexible [1]

**(b)** The plasticiser only mixes physically with the chains; it does not react with them or form covalent bonds [1]

**10. (a)** Mr(C₂F₄) = 2(12.0) + 4(19.0) = **100.0** [1]

**(b)** 4.00 × 10⁵ ÷ 100.0 = **4000** [1]

**(c)** **5.00 tonnes** [1]; addition polymerisation forms no other product, so every atom of monomer ends up in the polymer (100 % atom economy) [1]

**11.** The methyl group has been placed in the backbone; the backbone should contain only the two carbons of the C=C [1]; correct repeating unit **–[CH₂–CH(CH₃)]–** [1]

**12. (a)** **Three** [1]

**(b)** Monomer: **phenylethene**, C₆H₅CH=CH₂ [1]; polymer: **poly(phenylethene)** [1]

**(c)** During polymerisation each C=C opens and its electrons form single bonds to neighbouring monomer units [1]

**13. (a)** The new catalysts gave a polymer with a different chain structure (few branches instead of many) from the same monomer [1]; understanding that chain structure controls how closely chains pack, and so the forces between them and the properties, allowed chemists to make the form they wanted on purpose [1]

**(b)** Bags: the high-pressure (low-density) form has branched chains that pack loosely, so it is soft and flexible, suitable for thin films [1]; pipes: the low-pressure (high-density) form has closely packed chains with stronger forces between them, so it is stiffer and stronger [1]
*Examiner insight:* Link each property to chain packing and the strength of the forces between chains, not to the strength of covalent bonds.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Side groups drawn into the backbone.
- A C=C left in the repeating unit, or extension bonds missing.
- Unreactivity explained by intermolecular forces.
- Plasticisers said to "weaken the bonds".
- Polymer names without brackets.
