This guide covers sub-topic **2.3 Books of prime entry**. The syllabus names seven books: "cash book, petty cash book, sales journal, purchases journal, sales returns journal, purchases returns journal and the general journal". It also asks for posting from them, trade and cash discount, "the dual function of the cash book", recording receipts and payments "made by cash, cheques, debit and credit cards, online and bank transfers", the imprest system, and the benefits and limitations of keeping cash on the business property and of manual and digital methods of original entry.

The examples follow Amberley Bikes, owned by Nadia Rahim. Amounts are in dollars. Use this guide with the [2.3 revision notes](/resources/cambridge-igcse-accounting-0452-books-of-prime-entry-revision-notes/) and [2.3 practice questions](/resources/cambridge-igcse-accounting-0452-books-of-prime-entry-practice/). ## 1. Why books of prime entry are used

Transactions are first listed in a book of prime entry, in date order, before being posted to the ledger. This groups similar transactions so that a single total can be posted instead of dozens of separate entries, keeps the ledger uncluttered, and gives a record that can be checked against the source documents.

| Book | Records | Source document | Posting |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sales journal | Credit sales of goods | Copies of invoices sent | Each customer debited; total credited to sales |
| Purchases journal | Credit purchases of goods for resale | Invoices received | Each supplier credited; total debited to purchases |
| Sales returns journal | Goods returned by customers | Copies of credit notes sent | Each customer credited; total debited to sales returns |
| Purchases returns journal | Goods returned to suppliers | Credit notes received | Each supplier debited; total credited to purchases returns |
| Cash book | All cash and bank receipts and payments | Receipts, counterfoils, paying-in slips, bank statements | The other half of each entry posted to the ledger |
| Petty cash book | Small cash payments | Petty cash vouchers | Analysis column totals debited to expense accounts |
| General journal | Anything not in another book | Letters, memos, asset invoices | Each line posted as shown |

**Worked example 1: choosing the book**

| Transaction at Amberley Bikes | Book |
|---|---|
| Sold three bikes on credit to a cycling club | Sales journal |
| Bought helmets for resale on credit | Purchases journal |
| Bought a repair stand on credit from an equipment dealer | General journal (it is a non-current asset, not goods for resale) |
| A customer returned a faulty lock | Sales returns journal |
| Paid 6.50 for postage from the petty cash tin | Petty cash book |
| A customer paid by debit card | Cash book, bank column |
| A credit customer is declared bankrupt and the debt is written off | General journal |

## 2. Three journals and their postings

On 3 August Amberley sells on credit to Downs Cycling Club 2,400 and to M Achebe 650. The sales journal totals 3,050. Posting: debit Downs Cycling Club 2,400 and M Achebe 650 in the sales ledger; credit sales 3,050 in the nominal ledger. Only the **total** reaches the sales account, which is why journals save time. Purchases and both returns journals work the same way, with the sides reversed as shown in the table.

The **general journal** records each entry with the debit first, the credit indented beneath, and a short narrative:

```
                                                   Debit $   Credit $
5 Aug   Equipment                                   1,850
          Shore Workshop Supplies                              1,850
        Purchase of repair stand on credit
```

## 3. The cash book and its dual function

The cash book is a **book of prime entry**, because receipts and payments are first recorded in it, and it is also the **ledger account** for cash and for bank, because its balances are those accounts' balances. No separate cash or bank account is kept in the nominal ledger.

**Methods of payment decide the column.** Notes and coins go in the cash column. Cheques, debit card and credit card receipts, online payments and bank transfers go in the bank column, because the money passes through the bank account.

### Worked example 2: a three-column cash book for August

Opening balances on 1 August: cash 410, bank 3,480.

- 2 Aug: cash sales 640.
- 4 Aug: P Lowe, who owed 800, paid by bank transfer, taking 2.5% cash discount.
- 7 Aug: paid Velo Parts the 1,200 owed by online transfer, taking 2% cash discount.
- 9 Aug: sales paid by debit and credit cards, 1,150.
- 12 Aug: wages paid in cash, 420.
- 15 Aug: 300 cash paid into the bank.
- 20 Aug: rent paid by bank transfer, 950.
- 26 Aug: Nadia withdrew 200 cash for private use.

| Date | Details | Disc. allowed | Cash | Bank | Date | Details | Disc. received | Cash | Bank |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 Aug | Balances b/d | | 410 | 3,480 | 7 Aug | Velo Parts | 24 | | 1,176 |
| 2 Aug | Sales | | 640 | | 12 Aug | Wages | | 420 | |
| 4 Aug | P Lowe | 20 | | 780 | 15 Aug | Bank (contra) | | 300 | |
| 9 Aug | Sales | | | 1,150 | 20 Aug | Rent | | | 950 |
| 15 Aug | Cash (contra) | | | 300 | 26 Aug | Drawings | | 200 | |
| | | | | | 31 Aug | Balances c/d | | 130 | 3,584 |
| | | **20** | **1,050** | **5,710** | | | **24** | **1,050** | **5,710** |

Cash: 1,050 - 920 = 130. Bank: 5,710 - 2,126 = 3,584. Both are brought down as debit balances on 1 September.

**Posting.** P Lowe is credited with 780 *and* 20, clearing the 800. Velo Parts is debited with 1,176 and 24. The discount columns are not balanced: their **totals** are posted, debiting discount allowed (an expense) with 20 and crediting discount received (income) with 24. The contra needs no posting. Trade discount never appears in the cash book: only cash discount does.

## 4. The petty cash book and the imprest system

Under the **imprest system** the petty cashier starts each period with a fixed float. At the end of the period the main cashier pays in exactly the amount spent, which restores the float.

### Worked example 3: a 150 float

| Voucher | Details | Total | Postage | Cleaning | Travel | Stationery |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Stamps | 18.40 | 18.40 | | | |
| 2 | Window cleaner | 25.00 | | 25.00 | | |
| 3 | Bus fares | 32.60 | | | 32.60 | |
| 4 | Printer paper | 14.80 | | | | 14.80 |
| 5 | Parcel post | 9.20 | 9.20 | | | |
| 6 | Taxi | 11.50 | | | 11.50 | |
| | **Totals** | **111.50** | **27.60** | **25.00** | **44.10** | **14.80** |

Cash left in the tin is 150.00 - 111.50 = 38.50. The main cashier pays in 111.50 (credit bank in the cash book, debit petty cash), returning the float to 150. Each analysis total is debited to its expense account.

**The purpose of the imprest system** is control. At any moment, cash plus vouchers should equal the float, so a shortage is found quickly. Small payments are handled by one trusted person, the main cashier is not interrupted for each one, and the analysis columns mean only a few totals are posted.

## 5. Keeping cash at the business property

| Benefits | Limitations |
|---|---|
| Small expenses can be paid immediately | Risk of theft, by staff or outsiders |
| Change is available for cash customers | Notes can be lost or miscounted |
| No bank fees or delay for small payments | Time is needed to count and bank takings |
| Customers who prefer cash can still buy | Cash may not be insured above a set limit |

Businesses limit the risk by banking takings daily, keeping a small float, using a safe and checking the cash against the records.

## 6. Manual and digital original entry

| | Manual books | Digital methods |
|---|---|---|
| Benefits | Cheap; no equipment or power needed; easy to learn; no risk of online hacking | Fast; totals and postings are automatic; fewer arithmetic errors; reports on demand; bank feeds and card terminals record many transactions automatically |
| Limitations | Slow; arithmetic errors; each total posted by hand; hard to copy and search; lost in fire or flood | Cost of software, hardware and training; needs power and internet; risk of hacking or data loss; an input error is repeated in every report |

## Common mistakes

- Recording a non-current asset bought on credit in the purchases journal.
- Putting card receipts or bank transfers in the cash column.
- Balancing the discount columns instead of totalling them.
- Posting the discount allowed total to the credit side of discount allowed.
- Restoring the petty cash float with the cash left in the tin instead of the amount spent.

## Exam technique

- When asked to **prepare** a cash book, rule both sides with matching columns, bring down both balances, and label contra entries.
- In a "which book" question, look first at **how** payment was made (credit, cash, bank) and **what** was bought (goods for resale or an asset).
- **Explain** a benefit or limitation by saying what it leads to: "cash kept overnight may be stolen, so the business loses money and its records do not agree with the cash held".
- In **discuss** questions on manual and digital entry, give points on both sides and end with a judgement for the business described.
