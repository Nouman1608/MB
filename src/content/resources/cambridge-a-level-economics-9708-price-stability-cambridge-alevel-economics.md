## Inflation, deflation and disinflation

- **Inflation:** a sustained rise in the general price level. The **rate of inflation** is the percentage rise over a year.
- **Deflation:** a sustained fall in the general price level, so the inflation rate is negative.
- **Disinflation:** a fall in the rate of inflation while it remains positive. Prices still rise, but more slowly.

### Reading a data series

| Year | Inflation rate | What is happening to prices |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 4.0% | Rising (inflation) |
| 2 | 2.5% | Still rising, more slowly (disinflation) |
| 3 | 0.6% | Still rising, more slowly again (disinflation) |
| 4 | -0.8% | Falling (deflation) |

A common error is to say prices fell in Year 2 because the line on a graph of inflation went down. The line shows the **rate** of change; prices only fall when the rate is below zero. Also distinguish **percentage points** from per cent: a fall in inflation from 4.0% to 2.5% is a fall of 1.5 percentage points.

## Measuring changes in the price level: the CPI

The **consumer price index** measures the average change in the prices of a representative basket of goods and services bought by households.

1. A **household expenditure survey** finds what typical households buy.
2. Items are grouped into categories, and each category gets a **weight** equal to its share of total spending.
3. Prices are collected regularly for each item, and a price index is calculated for each category.
4. The CPI is the **weighted average** of the category indices. The base year is set at 100.

### Worked example 1: weights from spending shares

An invented household survey shows average monthly spending of 1,000 cu: food 400, rent 350 and transport 250. Over the year, food prices rise 6%, rent rises 3% and transport prices fall 2%.

```
Weights: food 400/1,000 = 0.40, rent 0.35, transport 0.25

Weighted price change = 0.40 x 6 + 0.35 x 3 + 0.25 x (-2)
                      = 2.40 + 1.05 - 0.50 = 2.95%

CPI rises from 100 to 102.95: inflation is about 3.0%
```

The fall in transport prices pulls the average down, but by only 0.5 points because transport has the smallest weight. A large price change in a lightly weighted category moves the CPI less than a small change in a heavily weighted one.

### Difficulties in measurement

- **The representative household does not exist.** A pensioner who spends more on heating, or a young renter, can face very different inflation from the average.
- **Weights go out of date** between surveys as spending patterns change.
- **Substitution:** when one item becomes dearer, people switch to cheaper alternatives, so a basket with fixed weights can overstate the rise in the cost of living.
- **Quality changes:** a phone that costs 10% more but does far more is partly a better product, not just a higher price.
- **New products** take time to enter the basket.
- **Data collection** may miss informal markets and price differences between regions.

## Money (nominal) values and real data

**Nominal** values are measured in the prices of the time. **Real** values remove the effect of price changes, so they measure quantities or purchasing power.

```
Real value = nominal value / price index x 100
Approximate real growth = nominal growth - inflation
```

### Worked example 2: real GDP and a real interest rate

An invented economy's nominal GDP rises from 500 billion cu to 540 billion cu (8%), while the price index rises from 100 to 105.

```
Real GDP in year 2 (year 1 prices) = 540 / 105 x 100 = 514.3
Real growth = (514.3 - 500) / 500 x 100 = 2.9%
```

Most of the 8% rise was higher prices, not more output. A saver earning 4% interest when inflation is 6% has a **real interest rate** of about 4 - 6 = -2%: the money in the account buys less at the end of the year than at the start.

## Causes of inflation

| | Demand-pull | Cost-push |
|---|---|---|
| Origin | Aggregate demand grows faster than the economy's capacity to supply | Costs of production rise, so firms raise prices to protect profit margins |
| AD/AS | AD shifts right | Short-run AS shifts left |
| Output and employment | Rise (until capacity is reached) | Fall |
| Typical triggers | Tax cuts, higher government spending, rising consumer confidence, an export boom, cheap credit | Wage rises above productivity growth, higher energy or raw material prices, a fall in the exchange rate that raises import prices, higher indirect taxes |

### Telling them apart from evidence

The key is what happens to **output** alongside the price level. *Original extract:* "Retail sales grew strongly, unemployment fell to a ten-year low and prices rose 5%." Rising output and falling unemployment with rising prices point to **demand-pull**. *Contrast:* "World oil prices doubled; manufacturing output fell and prices rose 5%." Falling output with rising prices points to **cost-push**. Both can operate at once, and a rise in prices can lead workers to press for higher wages, which raises costs further.

## Consequences of inflation

| Who | Likely effect |
|---|---|
| People on fixed money incomes and savers whose interest is below inflation | Lose purchasing power |
| Borrowers with fixed repayments | Gain, because the real value of their debt falls |
| Lenders | Lose, unless interest rates rise with inflation |
| Firms | Costs of changing prices (menu costs); uncertainty about future costs and prices can delay investment |
| Households | Time and effort spent managing money to avoid losses (shoe-leather costs) |
| Exporters | Lose price competitiveness if domestic inflation is higher than in trading partners, unless the exchange rate falls |

### Evaluation

The consequences depend on whether inflation is **anticipated** and on its **rate and stability**. If wages, pensions and interest rates are adjusted in line with expected inflation, little is redistributed. Unanticipated or volatile inflation does the most harm, because contracts and plans are based on the wrong expectations. Low and stable inflation is generally easier to live with than high or erratic inflation, which is why governments make **price stability**, rather than zero inflation, their objective.

## Annotated model answer

**Question:** Explain the difference between deflation and disinflation. **[4]**

> **Deflation** is a **sustained fall in the general price level** *(1: definition)*, so the inflation rate is **negative** *(1: sign)*. **Disinflation** is a **fall in the rate of inflation** *(1: definition)*, but the rate stays **positive**, so the price level is still rising, only more slowly *(1: contrast)*.

## Common mistakes

- Saying prices fall when inflation falls from 4% to 2%.
- Confusing a price index level (for example 108) with an inflation rate.
- Dividing by the new index value when calculating inflation.
- Comparing nominal values across years as if they measured quantities.
- Drawing cost-push inflation as a shift in AD.
- Listing consequences without saying who gains and who loses.

## Exam technique

- **Calculate** inflation as (new index - old index) / old index x 100, and real values as nominal / index x 100.
- **Define** with "sustained" and "general price level".
- **Explain** a cause with the chain: trigger, curve that shifts, effect on the price level and on output.
- In data responses, use output and employment evidence to identify the cause.
- In Section C essays, judge consequences by whether inflation is anticipated and how high and stable it is.
