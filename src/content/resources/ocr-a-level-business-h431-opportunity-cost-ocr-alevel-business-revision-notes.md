## The three specification points

1. Explain what is meant by opportunity cost.
2. Identify the opportunity cost of a business decision.
3. Analyse the impact of opportunity cost on business decision making.

## Core recall

### Meaning

- Opportunity cost: the benefit of the next best alternative given up when a choice is made.
- Exists because resources are scarce and each can be used only once at a time.
- Next best only: never the total of all rejected options.
- A benefit forgone, not money spent: idle cash has an opportunity cost.

### Identify it in four steps

1. List the realistic alternatives for the same resource.
2. Value each (money, or a stated benefit).
3. Rank them; note which was chosen.
4. The opportunity cost is the highest-valued alternative not chosen.

### Scarce resources and their opportunity costs

| Resource | Opportunity cost is... |
|---|---|
| Money | Return from the next best use: interest saved, interest earned, profit from another project |
| Time | What the owner or managers could have achieved instead |
| Capacity | Contribution from the work the machine, space or staff could have done |
| People | Output and income the person would have produced elsewhere |

### Capacity rule

- Spare capacity: opportunity cost of using it is low, close to zero.
- Full capacity: an extra order displaces existing work; opportunity cost = contribution lost on that work.
- Decision: accept if the order's contribution exceeds the contribution displaced (plus any extra costs).

### Owner's view

- Accounting profit ignores the owner's forgone salary and the return on the owner's capital.
- Profit after opportunity costs = accounting profit − salary forgone − return forgone on capital.
- A negative figure means the owner would be better off financially elsewhere, before non-financial benefits.

### Sunk cost versus opportunity cost

- Sunk cost: already spent and unrecoverable; has no alternative use now; ignore it.
- Opportunity cost: a future benefit given up; include it.

### Impact on decision making

- Forces comparison between options, not judgement of one in isolation.
- Reveals hidden costs of idle cash, space and time.
- The same decision can be right or wrong depending on capacity and circumstances.
- Links to risk: the next best option may be safer.
- Links to strategy: committing resources can close off future options.

### Limitations

- The next best alternative and its value are often estimates.
- Non-financial benefits are hard to compare.
- Many alternatives make the next best unclear.
- Managers underestimate the value of their own time.

## Quick worked reminder

Thornfield Joinery (invented), at full capacity: a 120-door order gives £19,200 contribution but uses 480 hours, displacing 8 staircases worth £2,700 each, £21,600. Accepting reduces profit by £2,400. With spare hours, the order would add £19,200.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Opportunity cost | The benefit of the next best alternative given up |
| Scarcity | Resources are limited relative to the uses for them |
| Contribution | Selling price minus variable cost, per unit or in total |
| Capacity | The maximum output a business can produce with its resources |
| Sunk cost | A cost already incurred that cannot be recovered |
| Trade-off | Giving up some of one thing to gain more of another |

## Exam traps

- Do not add the rejected options together.
- Opportunity cost is not the price paid.
- Ignore sunk costs; include only future costs and benefits.
- Check whether capacity is spare before calling an extra order profitable.
- Name the alternative and give its value whenever figures exist.
- Remember time and management attention, not only money.

## Self-test

1. Define opportunity cost.
2. A firm can spend £30,000 on A (benefit £5,000 a year), B (£4,200) or C (£3,100). It chooses A. What is the opportunity cost?
3. Why is the answer to question 2 not £7,300?
4. Give an example of the opportunity cost of an owner's time.
5. Why can idle cash have an opportunity cost?
6. A machine has spare hours. What is the opportunity cost of using them, roughly, and why?
7. An order earns £8,000 contribution but displaces work earning £9,500. Should it be accepted on these figures? By how much does profit change?
8. Profit £52,000; owner's forgone salary £40,000; owner's £150,000 capital could earn 5%. Calculate profit after opportunity costs.
9. Distinguish a sunk cost from an opportunity cost.
10. Give one way opportunity cost links to risk.
11. State two limitations of using opportunity cost in decisions.
12. Why might a strategy have an opportunity cost that is hard to measure?

### Answers

1. The benefit of the next best alternative given up when a choice is made.
2. Option B, **£4,200 a year**.
3. The money can be spent only once, so only the next best alternative is given up, not both B and C.
4. Two days a week spent on bookkeeping could have been spent winning new customers.
5. It could earn interest or repay a loan, so leaving it idle gives up that benefit.
6. Close to zero, because the hours would otherwise not be used for anything.
7. No: profit falls by **£1,500**.
8. Return forgone 0.05 × 150,000 = £7,500; 52,000 − 40,000 − 7,500 = **£4,500**.
9. A sunk cost has already been spent and cannot be recovered, so it should be ignored; an opportunity cost is a future benefit given up by choosing, so it should be included.
10. The next best option may be safer, so choosing a risky project gives up security as well as a return.
11. Any two: the alternative's value is an estimate; non-financial benefits are hard to compare; the next best option may be unclear; managers undervalue their own time.
12. Committing resources to one strategy can close off future options, such as responding to a rival, which cannot easily be valued in advance.
