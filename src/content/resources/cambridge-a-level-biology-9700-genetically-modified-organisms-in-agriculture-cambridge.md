## Why genetic engineering in agriculture?

The world population and its demand for food keep rising, while the area of land suitable for farming is limited. Food production can rise by increasing:

- **Productivity**: more product per unit of land, feed, water or time.
- **Quality**: better nutritional value, appearance or storage properties.

Selective breeding (17.2) achieves this slowly, over many generations, and only with alleles already in the species. Genetic engineering can add a single, specific gene, even from an unrelated species, in one generation.

## GM salmon

**The modification.** Atlantic salmon have been given a **growth hormone gene from Chinook salmon** together with a **promoter from another fish, the ocean pout**. The promoter keeps the growth hormone gene switched on all year round. Normal Atlantic salmon produce growth hormone mainly in warmer months, so they grow seasonally.

**How it raises productivity**

1. Growth hormone is secreted throughout the year.
2. The fish grow continuously and reach market size in a shorter time.
3. Less time in tanks means less feed, energy and labour per fish, so more fish can be produced from the same facilities.

**Containment measures**

- The fish are **females** and **sterile** (they have three sets of chromosomes, so they cannot produce viable gametes).
- They are farmed in **land-based tanks**, not sea cages.

These measures reduce the risk that GM salmon escape and interbreed with wild salmon or compete with them for food.

**Worked example (invented figures).** *A farm raises 50 000 salmon per production cycle. Unmodified fish take 30 months to reach market size; GM fish take 18 months. How many fish could the farm produce in 90 months with each type, if each tank is restocked immediately?*

- Unmodified: 90 ÷ 30 = 3 cycles → 3 × 50 000 = **150 000 fish**.
- GM: 90 ÷ 18 = 5 cycles → 5 × 50 000 = **250 000 fish**.
- Same tanks, about 1.7 times as many fish: higher productivity.

## Herbicide resistance in soybean

**The problem.** Weeds compete with crop plants for light, water and mineral ions, reducing yield. A broad-spectrum herbicide kills weeds, but it would also kill the crop.

**The modification.** The herbicide glyphosate kills plants by inhibiting an enzyme they need to make certain amino acids. GM soybean carries a gene from a bacterium (*Agrobacterium*) coding for a **version of that enzyme that glyphosate does not inhibit**. The GM plant keeps making the amino acids when sprayed.

**How it raises productivity**

1. The farmer sprays the whole field with glyphosate while the crop is growing.
2. Weeds are killed; the soybean plants survive.
3. Less competition for light, water and ions → higher yield per hectare.
4. Fewer, better-timed herbicide applications and less ploughing to control weeds, saving fuel and reducing soil erosion.

**A problem driven by natural selection.** Repeated spraying with one herbicide is a strong selection pressure. Any weed with a mutation giving resistance survives, reproduces and passes on the allele, so resistant weed populations increase. This is the same process as antibiotic resistance in bacteria.

## Insect resistance in cotton

**The problem.** Caterpillars of moths such as bollworms feed on cotton plants and their bolls (the seed capsules that produce the fibre), reducing yield. Farmers spray insecticides repeatedly, which is costly, harms non-target insects and can harm farm workers.

**The modification.** Cotton plants carry a gene from the soil bacterium ***Bacillus thuringiensis*** (**Bt**) that codes for a **Bt toxin**, a protein that is harmless until eaten. In the alkaline gut of certain insect larvae it is activated, binds to specific receptors on cells lining the gut and damages the gut wall, so the larvae stop feeding and die.

**How it raises productivity**

1. Every cell of the plant makes Bt toxin.
2. Only insects that **eat the crop** are affected; insects that do not feed on it, including many predators of pests, are spared.
3. Less damage to bolls → higher yield of cotton fibre.
4. Far less insecticide spraying → lower costs and less harm to wildlife and workers.

**Managing resistance.** Pests can evolve resistance to Bt toxin by natural selection. Farmers plant **refuges**: areas of non-Bt cotton nearby. Susceptible insects survive in the refuge and mate with any resistant insects, so the resistance allele stays rare (it is diluted in the population).

## Comparing the three examples

| | GM salmon | Herbicide-resistant soybean | Insect-resistant cotton |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gene source | Chinook salmon (gene), ocean pout (promoter) | Bacterium (*Agrobacterium*) | Bacterium (*Bacillus thuringiensis*) |
| Protein made | Growth hormone, all year | Enzyme not inhibited by glyphosate | Bt toxin |
| Improves | Productivity (growth rate) | Productivity (less weed competition) | Productivity (less insect damage) |
| Main risk | Escape and interbreeding with wild fish | Resistant weeds; gene flow to wild relatives | Resistant pests; effects on non-target species |

## Ethical and social implications (19.3.2)

Organise a "discuss" answer under headings, with points on both sides.

**Food security and economics**

- For: higher yields; more food from the same land; lower production costs; less land cleared for farming.
- Against: GM seed is often patented and expensive, and farmers may need to buy new seed each season, which is difficult for poorer farmers; a few large companies control much of the seed supply.

**Environment**

- For: less insecticide spraying with Bt crops; less ploughing with herbicide-resistant crops; contained GM fish farms take pressure off wild stocks.
- Against: herbicide-resistant weeds and Bt-resistant pests evolve; genes may transfer to wild relatives by cross-pollination; heavy herbicide use reduces wild plant diversity on and around farms; escaped fish could harm wild populations.

**Human health and consumer choice**

- For: GM foods are tested before approval; less pesticide residue on food.
- Against: some consumers fear unknown long-term effects or new allergens; many want **clear labelling** so they can choose.

**Animals and values**

- For: faster growth means less time in captivity per fish.
- Against: some people object in principle to altering animals' genomes or moving genes between species; concerns about the welfare of fast-growing animals.

## Common mistakes

- Saying Bt toxin "kills all insects". It affects insects that eat the crop.
- Saying herbicide-resistant crops "do not need herbicide". The point is that herbicide can be sprayed on them.
- Claiming the herbicide causes mutations in weeds. Resistance alleles arise by chance and are selected.
- Forgetting why GM salmon are sterile and kept in tanks.
- Describing only benefits, or only concerns, in a "discuss" answer.
- Mixing up the gene source (Chinook salmon) and the promoter source (ocean pout).

## Exam technique

- **Explain** how a GMO helps food supply: gene inserted → protein made → effect on the organism → effect on yield or quality.
- **Discuss**: at least two benefits and two concerns, ideally named to a specific example.
- Link resistance answers to **natural selection**: variation, selection pressure, survival, reproduction, increased allele frequency.
