## Questions

**1.** Define productive efficiency and allocative efficiency, and state one condition for each. **[4]**

**2.** A fictional quarry produces 50 tonnes. Total private cost is 640 CU and total external cost is 210 CU.

**(a)** Calculate the total social cost. **[1]**
**(b)** The marginal private cost of the 51st tonne is 15 CU and its marginal external cost is 6 CU. Calculate the marginal social cost. **[1]**
**(c)** In a separate market, private benefit is 520 CU and external benefit is 140 CU. Calculate the social benefit. **[1]**
**(d)** Explain what MSB = MSC shows. **[1]**

**3.** The table shows utility for two people under four allocations.

| Allocation | Person A | Person B |
|---|---|---|
| J | 4 | 7 |
| K | 6 | 7 |
| L | 6 | 4 |
| M | 3 | 9 |

**(a)** Explain why K is a Pareto improvement on J. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why a move from K to M is not a Pareto improvement. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain one limitation of Pareto optimality as a guide to policy. **[2]**

**4.** A fictional firm has total cost TC = 1,600 + 4Q², where Q is output in units.

**(a)** Find the output at which the firm is productively efficient, and the average cost there. **[3]**
**(b)** The firm produces 16 units. Calculate how much more it costs per unit, and in total, than at the efficient output. **[3]**

**5.** Explain two reasons for market failure other than externalities. **[6]**

**6.** A fictional dye factory has MPB = 120 − 4Q and MPC = 30 + 2Q, where Q is in thousand litres and values are CU per litre. The marginal external cost is a constant 18 CU per litre.

**(a)** Calculate the free-market output and price. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the socially optimal output. **[3]**
**(c)** Calculate the deadweight welfare loss. **[3]**
**(d)** Explain why the free market produces too much. **[2]**

**7.** A fictional orchard grower has MPB = 64 − 2Q and MPC = 8 + 2Q, with Q in thousand crates. Nearby beekeepers gain a marginal external benefit of 12 CU per crate.

**(a)** Calculate the market output and price. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the socially optimal output. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the welfare loss. **[3]**

**8.** A fictional insurer covers 2,000 households against a loss of 8,000 CU. It expects a 3% chance of a claim and sets the premium at the expected loss. After cover, the chance rises to 5%.

**(a)** Calculate the expected claim per household before and after cover. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the insurer's total loss. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why this is moral hazard and not simply asymmetric information. **[2]**

**9.** A fictional firm sells 500 units a year and invests in new methods. Its unit cost over five years is 90, 84, 78, 72 and 66 CU. A rival that does not invest has a flat unit cost of 80 CU.

**(a)** Calculate each firm's total cost over the five years. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain what dynamic efficiency means and why the investing firm may be called dynamically efficient. **[3]**

**10.** A fictional council considers a flood barrier. Fees paid by users are 6 CU million. Damage avoided is 40 and recreation gains are 8, both external. Construction costs 36 and maintenance 9. Lost habitat is a cost of 7.

**(a)** Calculate the social benefit, the social cost and the net social benefit. **[3]**
**(b)** Recalculate the net social benefit if damage avoided is 10% lower. **[2]**
**(c)** Assess whether the council should proceed. **[3]**

**11.** Evaluate whether a negative externality always justifies government intervention. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** Productive efficiency is producing at the lowest possible cost per unit [1], where MC = AC at minimum AC [1]. Allocative efficiency is producing the goods consumers value most [1], where P = MC (or MSB = MSC) [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Pair each definition with its own condition. Answers that mix up the two conditions are a common weakness.

**2. (a)** 640 + 210 = **850 CU** [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** 15 + 6 = **21 CU** [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** 520 + 140 = **660 CU** [1]. **[1]**
**(d)** It shows the output at which the last unit costs society exactly what it is worth, so allocative efficiency is achieved [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the addition. Make clear you are adding external to private values, not social to private.

**3. (a)** A is better off (4 to 6) [1] and B is unchanged at 7, so nobody loses [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** B gains (7 to 9) [1] but A loses (6 to 3), so someone is worse off [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Many allocations are Pareto optimal, including very unequal ones [1], so it says nothing about fairness or which optimum to choose [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Quote the numbers from the table for each person. Saying only that "someone loses" gains less than naming who.

**4. (a)** AC = 1,600/Q + 4Q and MC = 8Q [1]. Setting 1,600/Q + 4Q = 8Q gives Q² = 400, so **Q = 20** [1]. AC = 80 + 80 = **160 CU** [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** At Q = 16, AC = 100 + 64 = 164 [1]. Extra per unit = **4 CU** [1]. Total extra = 16 × 4 = **64 CU** [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Derive MC from TC before using it. The condition is MC = AC, not a guess at the minimum.

**5.** Any two developed reasons. Public goods are non-excludable and non-rival [1], so firms cannot charge and supply none [1], and provision needs to come from tax [1]. Market power lets firms restrict output [1], so price is above marginal cost [1] and output is below the allocatively efficient level [1]. Other valid reasons: imperfect information, immobile factors, unequal incomes. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Name the reason, then say how it leads to the wrong allocation. Lists without the consequence tend to read as shorter.

**6. (a)** 120 − 4Q = 30 + 2Q, so 90 = 6Q [1]. **Q = 15 thousand litres, P = 60 CU** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** MSC = 30 + 2Q + 18 = 48 + 2Q [1]. 120 − 4Q = 48 + 2Q, so 72 = 6Q [1]. **Q = 12 thousand litres** [1]. **[3]**
**(c)** At Q = 15, MSC = 78 and MPB = 60, a gap of 18 [1]. The quantity difference is 3 [1]. Loss = ½ × 18 × 3 = **27 thousand CU** [1]. **[3]**
**(d)** Producers ignore the external cost of 18 CU [1], so they supply units where MSC exceeds MSB [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Add the external cost to the supply side only. Keep the units consistent with the thousands in the question.

**7. (a)** 64 − 2Q = 8 + 2Q, so 56 = 4Q [1]. **Q = 14, P = 36** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** MSC = 8 + 2Q − 12 = 2Q − 4 [1]. 64 − 2Q = 2Q − 4 gives **Q = 17** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** At Q = 14, MPB = 36 and MSC = 24, a gap of 12 [1]. The quantity difference is 3 [1]. Loss = ½ × 12 × 3 = **18 thousand CU** [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* An external benefit of production lowers MSC. A common slip is to add it, which moves the optimum the wrong way.

**8. (a)** Before: 0.03 × 8,000 = **240 CU** [1]. After: 0.05 × 8,000 = **400 CU** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Premiums 240 × 2,000 = 480,000 [1]. Claims 400 × 2,000 = 800,000, so the loss is **320,000 CU** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** The risk rose after cover was bought because households took less care [1], whereas asymmetric information concerns who knows more before the deal [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Point to the change in behaviour after the contract. That timing is what separates the two ideas.

**9. (a)** Investing firm: (90 + 84 + 78 + 72 + 66) × 500 = 390 × 500 = **195,000 CU** [1]. Rival: 80 × 5 × 500 = **200,000 CU** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Dynamic efficiency is efficiency improving over time [1]. The investing firm starts with higher costs but its unit cost falls each year, to below the rival's from year 3 [1], saving 5,000 CU over five years [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Refer to the time dimension. Comparing a single year misses the point of dynamic efficiency.

**10. (a)** SB = 6 + 40 + 8 = **54** [1]. SC = 36 + 9 + 7 = **52** [1]. Net = **+2 CU million** [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** Damage avoided = 36, so SB = 6 + 36 + 8 = 50 [1]. Net = 50 − 52 = **−2 CU million** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** On the central figures it is marginally positive [1], but a 10% fall in one value reverses it [1], and habitat loss and damage avoided are hard to value, so proceed with caution or gather better estimates [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Reach a judgement and support it with the sensitivity result. A bare "yes" or "no" is weaker.

**11.** Credit for analysis [1 each]: a negative externality means MSC exceeds MPC [1]; the market over-produces [1]; there is a welfare loss [1]; intervention such as a tax or regulation could correct it [1]. Credit for evaluation [1 each]: the externality may be small, so the cost of acting outweighs the gain [1]; the externality is hard to measure, so a tax could be set wrongly [1]; government failure, such as poor information or lobbying, may make things worse [1]; alternatives such as bargaining or property rights may work when parties are few [1]. A supported conclusion [2]: for example, intervention is justified when the loss is large and can be measured, but not automatically. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* Use a diagram if you can, and finish with a judgement tied to the size of the externality. Descriptions without a reasoned conclusion tend to stay at analysis.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Adding the external cost or benefit to the wrong curve.
- Using the wrong direction: negative externalities give over-production, positive ones under-production.
- Shading too much area as welfare loss, rather than the triangle between MSB and MSC.
- Confusing productive and allocative efficiency, or their conditions.
- Calling a move a Pareto improvement when someone loses.
- Forgetting units, or the thousands in a quantity.
- Evaluating in a single sentence, without a reason or a conclusion.
- Mixing up asymmetric information with moral hazard.
