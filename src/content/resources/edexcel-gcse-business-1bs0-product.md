## What 2.2.1 contains

| Specification heading | Bullet points |
|---|---|
| The design mix | function, aesthetics, cost |
| The product life cycle | the phases of the product life cycle; extension strategies |
| Differentiation | the importance to a business of differentiating a product/service |

The specification does not list the life cycle phases. The standard four, used here, are introduction, growth, maturity and decline. Many textbooks add a development phase before launch, when the product earns no revenue at all; it is worth knowing because it explains why a new product starts with heavy costs.

## The design mix: which element leads?

Every product balances **function** (does it work, is it reliable and easy to use?), **aesthetics** (how it looks, feels, sounds, smells or tastes) and **cost** (how much it costs to make, which affects price and profit). The skill examiners test is recognising which element matters most for a particular product and target market.

| Product type | Leading element | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Power drill for builders | Function | Buyers need power and reliability every day |
| Designer handbag | Aesthetics | Customers pay for appearance and image |
| Supermarket own-label washing-up liquid | Cost | Buyers compare prices and expect it simply to work |
| Smartphone | All three | Must work well, look desirable and hit a price point |

Changing one element nearly always affects the others. Better materials improve function and appearance but raise unit cost. Cutting cost may make the product look or perform worse. A good answer shows this knock-on effect for the product in the question.

### Worked example 1: a redesign at Copperleaf Kettles

Copperleaf sells a standard kettle at £25. Each costs £11 to make and it sells 4,000 a year. A redesign with a quieter boil and a matt finish would add £3.50 to unit cost; the new price would be £32 and sales are forecast at 3,600. The design work costs £8,000 once.

| | Current kettle | Redesigned kettle |
|---|---|---|
| Contribution per kettle | £25 − £11 = £14 | £32 − £14.50 = £17.50 |
| Total contribution | £14 × 4,000 = £56,000 | £17.50 × 3,600 = £63,000 |
| After one-off design cost (year 1) | £56,000 | £63,000 − £8,000 = £55,000 |

In year 1 the redesign earns £1,000 less; from year 2 it earns £7,000 a year more, if the forecast holds. The redesign improves function and aesthetics at the expense of cost, and the trade-off pays off only if customers value the quieter, better-looking kettle enough to accept the higher price.

## The product life cycle in decision-making

The life cycle tracks sales of a product over time. It is useful because each phase suggests different decisions about the rest of the marketing mix.

| Phase | Sales and profit | Typical decisions |
|---|---|---|
| Introduction | Sales low; costs of launch and promotion high, so profit is usually negative | Heavy promotion to build awareness; limited distribution; price set to launch strategy |
| Growth | Sales rise quickly; profit becomes positive as fixed costs are spread | Widen distribution; build brand loyalty before rivals copy the product |
| Maturity | Sales level off; profit often at its highest; competition is strongest | Defend market share; consider extension strategies; promotion to remind customers |
| Decline | Sales and profit fall | Extend, reduce support, or withdraw the product |

Two points earn credit in analysis answers. First, profit lags behind sales: a product can be selling well in introduction and still be making a loss. Second, the cycle is not fixed. Its length varies greatly: some fashion and toy products pass through all four phases within a year, while some food brands stay in maturity for decades because of repeated extension strategies.

## Extension strategies

An extension strategy aims to lengthen the life of a product, usually when it is in late maturity or early decline. Common methods:

- **Change the product:** new flavours, colours, sizes or features.
- **New markets:** sell to a different segment (for example, a children's product aimed at adults) or in a new country.
- **New uses:** promote another use for the same product.
- **Change the packaging or the promotion:** a refreshed look or a new advertising message.
- **Change the price or place:** a price cut, or selling through new outlets such as online.

Timing matters. If the business waits until sales have collapsed, retailers may already have cleared shelf space for rivals. If it acts too early, it spends money on a product that would have sold well anyway.

### Worked example 2: is a new flavour worth it?

Brackenfield Granola's original recipe is in decline. Without action, sales next year are forecast to fall to 22,000 packs. A new flavour, costing £12,000 to develop and launch, is forecast to hold sales at 30,000 packs. Each pack earns £1.40 contribution.

1. Extra packs = 30,000 − 22,000 = 8,000
2. Extra contribution = 8,000 × £1.40 = £11,200
3. Gain in year 1 = £11,200 − £12,000 = **−£800**

On year 1 alone the extension loses money. If the new flavour keeps sales 8,000 packs higher for two years, the gain is £22,400 − £12,000 = **£10,400**. The decision therefore depends on how long the effect lasts, which is a forecast, not a fact.

## Why differentiation matters

Differentiation is making a product or service distinct from rivals' in a way customers value. Methods include design, features, quality, branding, packaging, customer service and convenience. A distinctive feature that customers value is often called a unique selling point (USP), a standard term.

The specification asks for the **importance to a business**. Build your answer around these effects:

- **Reason to choose:** in a crowded market, a differentiated product gives customers a reason to buy it rather than a near-identical rival.
- **Pricing power:** customers will often pay more for something they see as different or better, so the business does not have to compete only on price.
- **Loyalty:** customers attached to a distinctive product are less likely to switch.
- **Protection in maturity:** differentiation helps defend market share when competition is strongest.

Its limits: it costs money, rivals may copy it, and a difference customers do not value adds cost without sales. Differentiation is most important when products in the market are otherwise similar, and least important when customers buy almost entirely on price.

## Common mistakes

- Listing function, aesthetics and cost with no link to the product in the question.
- Saying profit is highest in the growth phase in every case. It is usually highest in maturity, once launch costs are behind the product.
- Treating every fall in sales as decline. A seasonal dip is not a life cycle phase.
- Recommending an extension strategy without checking whether the extra contribution covers its cost.
- Writing "differentiation lets the business charge more" without saying why customers would accept the higher price.

## Exam technique

- **Identify:** read a life cycle phase from a sales graph or table; quote the figures that show it.
- **Calculate:** contribution, the cost of a redesign or an extension strategy, or a percentage change in sales.
- **Outline:** two linked points in context, for example how the aesthetics of Copperleaf's kettle affect its price.
- **Explain:** a statement with two expansions, without context.
- **Analyse:** take one product decision and follow its effect on sales, costs and profit for the business.
- **Justify:** choose between two product options, such as two extension strategies, using the figures, and give a reason the other option is weaker.
- **Evaluate:** weigh the design trade-offs or the value of differentiation, then say what the conclusion depends on.
