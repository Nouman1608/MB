**This guide covers the first period of the thematic study Medicine and change, c800-present day.** The specification names "Medieval Islamic Medicine: preserving, translating, and passing on ancient and new knowledge from Europe and Asia, the 'Translation Movement'; the development of universities and centres of learning; Islamic physicians - ethics and knowledge, including Al-Razi and Ibn-Sina; hospitals - bimaristans; surgery, tools, techniques, including the work of Al-Zahrawi; developments in public health, hygiene, water supply, and sanitation; innovations in pharmacology, including the work of Al-Baytar, and Al-Kindi; contribution to medicine in Europe and Asia." The [Paper 2 Section B study guide](/resources/igcse-oxfordaqa-world-history-thematic-studies-study-guide/) summarises this period in one paragraph; this page explains each named element and why the period mattered for the rest of the thematic study.

## Setting the scene

From the eighth century the **Abbasid caliphate**, with its capital at Baghdad (founded in 762), ruled a vast area from Central Asia to North Africa. Other great cities of the Islamic world included Damascus, Cairo and Córdoba in al-Andalus (Muslim Spain). Trade routes linked these cities with Byzantium, Persia, India and China, so goods, people and ideas moved across them. The making of **paper**, learned from China in the eighth century, made books far cheaper than parchment and helped knowledge spread.

## The Translation Movement

Between the eighth and tenth centuries scholars in Baghdad translated large numbers of works into Arabic:

- **Greek** medicine and science, including the works of Hippocrates and Galen, and Dioscorides on medicinal plants;
- **Persian** medical learning, associated with the academy at Gundishapur;
- **Indian** medical texts, including Ayurvedic works.

The **House of Wisdom** in Baghdad, associated especially with the Caliph al-Ma'mun (ruled 813-833), became a centre of translation and research. **Hunayn ibn Ishaq** (died 873), a Christian physician, translated many of Galen's works, comparing manuscripts to produce accurate versions. Translators often worked for patrons who paid well for books.

The movement **preserved** ancient knowledge that might otherwise have been lost and **combined** traditions from three continents. Physicians did not simply copy: they tested, criticised and added to what they read.

## Universities and centres of learning

Learning was supported by mosques, libraries, hospitals and wealthy patrons. Among the oldest continuously operating places of learning are **al-Qarawiyyin** in Fez, founded in 859 by Fatima al-Fihri, and **al-Azhar** in Cairo, founded in the 970s. Medicine was often taught through apprenticeship and in hospitals, where students watched experienced physicians examine patients. Large libraries in Baghdad, Cairo and Córdoba held medical texts for study.

## Physicians: ethics and knowledge

**Ethics.** Physicians were expected to be learned, honest and compassionate, and to treat rich and poor alike. A ninth-century book, *Practical Ethics of the Physician* by al-Ruhawi, set out the duties of the doctor. In 931 the caliph in Baghdad ordered that physicians should be examined before being allowed to practise, an early form of licensing.

**Al-Razi** (c854-c925), known in Europe as Rhazes, directed hospitals in Rayy and Baghdad. He stressed careful **observation** of patients and recording of cases. His treatise on **smallpox and measles** was the first to distinguish clearly between the two diseases. He wrote *Doubts about Galen*, showing a willingness to question the greatest ancient authority when observation disagreed with it, and his huge medical notebook was compiled after his death as *The Comprehensive Book*. He also wrote a guide for people who could not reach a doctor.

**Ibn Sina** (980-1037), known in Europe as Avicenna, was a physician and philosopher from Bukhara. His *Canon of Medicine*, completed around 1025, organised the medical knowledge of Greek, Islamic and other traditions into five books covering the body, diseases, treatments and drugs. It discussed the spread of disease by contagion and set out rules for testing whether a drug worked. The *Canon* became a standard textbook in the Islamic world and, after its Latin translation, in European universities for centuries.

## Hospitals: bimaristans

The word **bimaristan** comes from Persian words meaning "place of the sick". Large hospitals were founded in cities including Baghdad (the Adudi hospital, 982), Damascus (the hospital of Nur al-Din, 1154) and Cairo (the Mansuri hospital, 1284). Their features included:

- **free treatment** for patients regardless of wealth or religion, paid for by charitable endowments;
- **separate wards** for men and women and for different conditions, such as fevers, eye diseases, surgery and mental illness;
- **pharmacies**, libraries and kitchens;
- **teaching** of medical students;
- **records** of patients and treatments.

Bimaristans were centres of both care and learning, very different from most religious hospitals in Europe at the time, which offered rest and prayer rather than medical treatment.

## Surgery: Al-Zahrawi

**Al-Zahrawi** (c936-1013), known in Europe as Albucasis, worked in Córdoba. His encyclopaedia, *Kitab al-Tasrif* (*The Method of Medicine*), ended with a volume on surgery that described operations and included drawings of around two hundred surgical instruments, many of his own design. He wrote on cauterisation to stop bleeding, tying blood vessels, stitching wounds (including the use of catgut), setting bones, removing bladder stones, dentistry and childbirth. Its Latin translation influenced European surgeons for centuries.

## Public health, hygiene, water and sanitation

- **Washing.** Ritual washing before prayer encouraged regular cleanliness, and **public baths** (*hammams*) were common in cities.
- **Water supply.** Aqueducts, underground channels (*qanats*), wells and public fountains supplied towns.
- **Sanitation.** Some cities had drains and systems for removing waste.
- **Regulation.** Market inspectors (*muhtasib*) checked the cleanliness of markets, the quality of food and the honesty of traders.

## Pharmacology

Pharmacy became a separate profession, and pharmacists were inspected.

**Al-Kindi** (c801-873), a philosopher in Baghdad, applied **mathematics** to medicine, attempting to calculate the strength of compound drugs from the strengths of their ingredients; he also compiled a formulary of medicines.

**Ibn al-Baytar** (died 1248), born near Málaga in al-Andalus, travelled across North Africa and the Middle East studying plants and worked in Cairo and Damascus. His *Compendium on Simple Medicaments and Foods* described about 1,400 substances, including many not recorded before, and their uses.

## Contribution to medicine in Europe and Asia

**Europe.** Arabic texts reached Europe through Spain, Sicily and southern Italy. In the eleventh century **Constantine the African** translated Arabic medical works into Latin in Italy, and in the twelfth century **Gerard of Cremona** in Toledo translated the *Canon* and Al-Zahrawi's surgery. Medical schools such as **Salerno** and **Montpellier** used these texts, and the *Canon* remained part of university teaching into the seventeenth century.

**Asia.** The tradition spread east with scholars and rulers to Persia, Central Asia and India, where it developed into **Unani** medicine ("Greek" medicine), still practised in South Asia today.

## Worked example: a 6-mark answer

*Explain one way in which the Translation Movement affected medicine.* **[6]**

> One way was that it **preserved and combined medical knowledge from different civilisations**. In Baghdad, scholars at the House of Wisdom, such as Hunayn ibn Ishaq, translated the works of Galen and Hippocrates into Arabic, alongside Persian and Indian texts. This meant that physicians had access to a wider range of knowledge than any single tradition offered, so they could compare and test ideas, as Al-Razi did when he wrote *Doubts about Galen*. As a result, the knowledge was not only kept alive but improved, and it later passed to Europe when Arabic works such as Ibn Sina's *Canon* were translated into Latin.

## Common mistakes

- Describing Islamic physicians as only copying the Greeks: they observed, questioned and added.
- Confusing Al-Razi (smallpox and measles) with Ibn Sina (the *Canon*).
- Leaving out pharmacology or public health, both named in the specification.
- Treating Islamic medicine as separate from later European medicine, instead of explaining how it passed on.
- Using vague statements ("they had good hospitals") instead of precise features.
