A **consumer** is a person who buys goods or services for their own use. Consumers are often at a disadvantage when they deal with businesses. They may not know how a product is made, they cannot test it before they buy online, and a faulty product can cause harm or waste money. **Consumer protection** is the set of laws, rules and actions that protect buyers from unfair or unsafe treatment. Sub-topic 6.3 of Cambridge O Level Commerce (7100) has two content points: the **reasons for consumer protection** and the **actions taken by buyers and sellers to resolve consumer protection complaints**.

## Reasons for consumer protection

| Reason | Explanation | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Safety** | Goods and services must not harm users | A toy with small parts that a child can swallow |
| **Quality and fitness for purpose** | Goods should work as described and last a reasonable time | A kettle that stops working after two days |
| **Accurate information** | Buyers need true descriptions, labels and prices to make good choices | A food label that hides an allergy risk |
| **Fair prices and contracts** | Protects buyers from hidden charges, unfair terms and price-fixing | A contract with charges only mentioned in small print |
| **Unequal knowledge and power** | The seller knows more than the buyer, and large businesses are stronger than individuals | A buyer cannot judge the quality of a second-hand car |
| **Protecting vulnerable groups** | Children, older people and people with little education can be easily misled | Pushy selling of expensive plans to elderly people |
| **Honest advertising** | Prevents false claims and misleading offers | A claim of "50% off" when the price was raised first |
| **Building trust** | If customers trust the market, more trade takes place, which helps honest businesses | Shoppers feel safe buying online |
| **A way to get problems put right** | Buyers need a way to obtain a repair, a replacement or a refund | A route for complaints when a product is faulty |

Honest businesses also benefit from consumer protection, because dishonest rivals cannot win customers by cheating.

### Typical consumer problems

- Goods that are **faulty**, **unsafe** or **not as described**.
- Goods that **do not arrive**, or arrive late or damaged.
- **Hidden charges** or unfair contract terms.
- **Misleading advertising** (this links to ethics in 6.2).
- **Poor service**, such as repairs done badly.
- **Data misuse** by a business.

## Actions by buyers to resolve complaints

A buyer with a complaint should act in steps, moving up only if the earlier step fails.

1. **Keep proof of purchase.** A receipt, invoice, order email or bank statement shows what was bought, when and from whom. Photographs of the fault help.
2. **Contact the seller.** Explain the problem politely and say what outcome you want: a refund, a repair or a replacement. Do this quickly.
3. **Make a formal complaint in writing.** Include dates, order number, the problem and the remedy requested. Keep a copy.
4. **Contact the manufacturer or use the guarantee (warranty).** A guarantee is a promise by the maker or seller to repair or replace the product within a set time.
5. **Contact a consumer organisation or official body.** These give advice and may take up the complaint.
6. **Ask the card provider or bank for a refund** when the buyer paid by credit or debit card and the seller does not respond.
7. **Take legal action** through a court or small claims procedure. This is a last resort because it takes time and money.
8. **Warn others.** Writing an honest review or telling friends may put pressure on the seller, but the buyer must stick to the facts.

## Actions by sellers to resolve complaints

Sellers want to keep their customers and their reputation, so they should deal with complaints fairly and quickly.

1. **Listen and apologise.** Treat the customer politely and show that the complaint is being taken seriously.
2. **Investigate.** Check what was sold, whether the product is faulty and whether the customer is right.
3. **Offer a remedy.** The seller can **refund** the money, **repair** the product, **replace** it, or give a **credit note** (a document that can be used to buy other goods).
4. **Compensate** the customer if there was extra loss or inconvenience, for example by paying for return postage.
5. **Provide clear returns and warranty policies** so customers know what to expect.
6. **Keep records of complaints** and find the causes, so the problem does not happen again.
7. **Train staff** in customer service and quality checks.
8. **Recall unsafe products.** If a product is found to be dangerous, the seller removes it from sale and asks buyers to return it.

### Which remedy?

| Situation | Suitable remedy |
|---|---|
| Faulty item within the first weeks | Refund or replacement |
| Minor fault, buyer wants to keep it | Repair, or partial refund |
| Item out of stock | Credit note or alternative product |
| Item arrives damaged | Replacement and refund of postage |
| Unsafe product | Recall and full refund |

### Worked example: a refund

A customer buys a headset online for $60. Postage was $5. The headset is faulty. The customer returns it and pays $4 for return postage.

1. Refund of price and original postage = 60 + 5 = **$65**.
2. A fair seller also repays the return postage of $4, so the customer is refunded 65 + 4 = **$69**.

If the seller offers a replacement instead, the customer pays nothing more. If the seller gives a 20% partial refund because the customer wants to keep the headset, the refund is 20% × 60 = $12.

### Worked example: the cost of complaints

A shop sells 3,000 kettles in a year and receives 45 complaints about a faulty switch.

1. Complaint rate = 45 ÷ 3,000 × 100 = **1.5%**.
2. Each complaint costs $20 to replace and handle: 45 × 20 = **$900**.

The shop can use this information to ask the supplier to fix the switch.

### Worked example: a product recall

A company finds that a baby toy may break dangerously. It has sold 5,000 toys at $35. Offering a full refund to every buyer would cost up to 5,000 × 35 = **$175,000**. This is expensive, but ignoring the danger could harm children, lead to legal action and destroy the company's reputation.

## Case: a faulty blender

*Invented example.* Tariq buys a blender for $48 from an online shop. After ten days it stops working.

1. **Proof:** Tariq finds his order email and photographs the broken part.
2. **Contact:** he writes to the shop with the order number and asks for a replacement.
3. **Seller's response:** the shop's customer service team apologises, checks the order and the photograph, and sends a replacement with a prepaid return label. It records the complaint in a log.
4. **Learning:** the log shows that 9 of the shop's 300 blenders sold this month have the same fault (9 ÷ 300 = 3%). The shop contacts the supplier and asks for the batch to be checked.
5. **If the shop had refused:** Tariq could make a formal written complaint, use the maker's guarantee, ask his card provider for a refund and contact a consumer organisation. Court would be a last resort for such a small sum.

The case shows how buyer and seller actions fit together. A good seller solves the problem quickly and uses the complaint to improve. A well-informed buyer follows the steps in order and keeps records.

## Annotated model answer

**Question:** A customer bought a mobile phone that stopped working after three weeks. Explain **two** actions the customer could take to resolve the complaint. [4]

> The customer could contact the seller with the receipt as proof of purchase [1] and ask for a refund, repair or replacement because the phone is faulty [1]. If the seller does not respond, the customer could make a formal written complaint or contact a consumer organisation for help [1], which puts pressure on the seller to put things right [1].

**Why it scores 4:** two actions are named and each is explained with the reason it helps.

## Common mistakes

- Giving the reasons for consumer protection as "to protect businesses". The aim is to protect buyers, although honest sellers benefit.
- Listing actions with no order or reason.
- Forgetting proof of purchase.
- Saying the buyer should go straight to court.
- Describing only what the buyer can do when the question asks about the seller as well.
- Mixing up a refund, a replacement and a credit note.

## Exam technique

- **Reasons**: give a reason, then an example that shows the harm to the buyer.
- **Actions**: name the action, say who does it, and explain how it helps.
- **Calculate** refunds carefully: include postage only if the seller should repay it.
