## Why analyse operational performance

Operations managers need numbers to judge whether the business is meeting its operational objectives (see the [operational objectives guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-setting-operational-objectives/)). Data lets a business compare performance over time, compare it with rivals (benchmarking), spot problems early, plan staffing and equipment, and check whether a change has worked.

## The four calculations

### Labour productivity

**Labour productivity** measures the output each worker produces in a period.

> labour productivity = total output ÷ number of employees

It may also be measured per hour worked:

> labour productivity per hour = total output ÷ total hours worked

Productivity depends on skills, motivation, equipment, working methods and management. Rising productivity means a worker produces more with the same input, which tends to lower costs per unit. Always state the unit, for example "800 units per employee per month".

### Unit cost (average cost)

**Unit cost** is the average cost of making one unit.

> unit cost = total cost ÷ total output
> total cost = fixed costs + variable costs, where variable costs = variable cost per unit × output

Fixed costs (such as rent) do not change with output in the short run; variable costs (such as materials) do. Because fixed costs are spread over more units when output rises, the unit cost usually falls as output increases, until the business reaches its capacity limits.

### Capacity

**Capacity** is the maximum output a business can produce in a given period with its existing resources (space, machinery, staff and time). It is not fixed for ever: it can be raised by buying equipment, adding shifts or using more space, and lowered by closing down a line.

### Capacity utilisation

**Capacity utilisation** shows how much of the available capacity is being used.

> capacity utilisation (%) = actual output ÷ maximum possible output × 100

## Worked example: Marsden Ceramics

Marsden Ceramics makes plates. Data for one month:

| Item | Figure |
|---|---|
| Output | 48,000 plates |
| Maximum capacity | 60,000 plates |
| Employees | 60 |
| Hours worked per employee | 160 |
| Fixed costs | £90,000 |
| Variable cost per plate | £3.00 |

**Step 1: labour productivity.**

- Per employee: 48,000 ÷ 60 = **800 plates per employee**.
- Per hour: total hours = 60 × 160 = 9,600; 48,000 ÷ 9,600 = **5 plates per hour**.

**Step 2: unit cost.**

- Variable costs = 48,000 × £3.00 = £144,000.
- Total cost = £90,000 + £144,000 = £234,000.
- Unit cost = £234,000 ÷ 48,000 = **£4.875**, about £4.88.

**Step 3: capacity utilisation.** 48,000 ÷ 60,000 × 100 = **80%**.

**Step 4: what if output rises?** Marsden wins a contract and raises output to 54,000 with the same 60 staff.

- Labour productivity = 54,000 ÷ 60 = **900 plates per employee**, an increase of 12.5%.
- Variable costs = 54,000 × £3.00 = £162,000; total cost = £252,000.
- Unit cost = £252,000 ÷ 54,000 = **£4.67** (to two decimal places), a fall of about 4.3%.
- Capacity utilisation = 54,000 ÷ 60,000 × 100 = **90%**.

**Step 5: at full capacity.** Output of 60,000 would give variable costs of £180,000, total cost of £270,000 and unit cost of **£4.50**.

**Interpretation.** More output spreads the fixed costs of £90,000 over more plates, so unit cost falls. The workers have not changed in number, so productivity is higher. But Marsden is now close to capacity: if demand rose further it would need extra shifts, overtime (which may raise variable costs per unit) or investment in new machinery. The data supports the decision to take the contract, provided that the contract price is above the unit cost and quality can be maintained.

## Interpreting capacity utilisation

| Utilisation | Likely position | Consequences |
|---|---|---|
| Low (for example under 60%) | **Under-utilised**: spare capacity | High unit costs because fixed costs are spread over few units; wasted resources; scope to take new orders, so cut prices or seek contracts |
| Moderate to high (about 80 to 90%) | Efficient use with some flexibility | Lower unit costs, with room to cope with some extra demand |
| 100% or more | **Full capacity** | Lowest unit cost at first, but no flexibility; risk of turning away customers, late deliveries, staff stress, machine breakdown; higher costs from overtime or outsourcing |

There is no single ideal figure. A hospital operating at 100% has no room for an emergency; a bakery with seasonal demand may accept low utilisation off-season to cope with the peak.

## Interpreting labour productivity and unit costs

Compare figures:

- **over time**: is productivity rising or falling?
- **against competitors** (benchmarking): is the business more or less efficient than rivals?
- **against targets**: have the operational objectives been met?

Possible explanations for a fall in productivity include poor morale, high absence, weaker training, inadequate equipment or new, inexperienced workers. A rise in unit cost might come from higher material prices, lower output (because fixed costs are spread more thinly), wage increases or waste. A good answer looks for the cause in the case and recommends a response that fits.

Productivity and unit costs also interact: if wages per worker rise by 5% but productivity rises by 10%, labour cost per unit falls.

## Using operations data in decision making and planning

- **Capacity decisions**: persistently high utilisation suggests investing in extra capacity; persistently low utilisation suggests closing a line, finding new customers or sharing resources.
- **Staffing**: productivity data shows how many workers are needed for a given output.
- **Pricing and orders**: unit cost sets a floor for pricing, so a business can judge whether an order is worth accepting.
- **Improvement**: trends and benchmarks identify where to focus effort.
- **Planning**: forecasting demand against capacity indicates when to hire, extend or outsource.

## Limits of operations data

- Figures are **averages** and can hide variation between shifts or products.
- Productivity counts quantity, not quality; a team can raise output while making more errors.
- Unit cost depends on how fixed costs are shared in a multi-product firm, which is a judgement.
- Data is from the **past**, while decisions affect the future.
- Short periods may be unrepresentative (for example a month with a holiday).
- Technology (such as automation) may raise productivity per worker while increasing capital costs, so labour productivity alone may flatter performance.

## Technique for exam questions

- Write the formula, show the substitution and give the unit (units per worker, £ per unit, %).
- Interpret: say what the figure means for the business and compare it with a target, last year or a rival.
- For "assess" or "evaluate" questions, discuss causes and limits of the data, and make a judgement about what the business should do.

## Common mistakes

- Using total cost instead of unit cost when asked for cost per unit.
- Forgetting to include fixed costs in total cost.
- Dividing output by capacity without multiplying by 100.
- Treating high capacity utilisation as always good.
- Reporting productivity per worker when the question gives hours (or the other way round).
- Calculating correctly but giving no interpretation.
