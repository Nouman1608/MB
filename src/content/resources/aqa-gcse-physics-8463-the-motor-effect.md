## 4.7.2.1 Electromagnetism (both tiers)

When a current flows through a conducting wire, a **magnetic field is produced around the wire**. Field lines are
circles centred on the wire. The field is **stronger for a larger current** and **weaker further from the wire**.
Reversing the current reverses the direction of the field.

### Why a solenoid is stronger

A **solenoid** is a wire wound into a long coil. Each turn of wire produces its own field. Inside the coil, the
fields from all the turns point the same way and **add together**, so the field inside is **strong and uniform**.
Outside, the field has the same shape as the field of a bar magnet, with an N pole at one end and an S pole at the
other.

The magnetic effect of a solenoid can be increased by:

- **adding an iron core**: the iron becomes an induced magnet and adds its field to the coil's field
- increasing the **current**
- adding **more turns** in the same length of coil.

A solenoid with an iron core is an **electromagnet**. Its big advantage over a permanent magnet is control: switch
the current off and the soft iron loses its magnetism almost at once.

### Interpreting an electromagnetic device: the electric bell

The specification asks you to explain unfamiliar devices from a diagram. Use the same chain every time. For an
electric bell:

1. Switch closed: current flows through the coil, so the iron core becomes an electromagnet.
2. The electromagnet attracts a soft iron **armature**, and the hammer on the armature strikes the gong.
3. Moving the armature **breaks a contact** in the circuit, so the current stops.
4. The core loses its magnetism; a spring pulls the armature back.
5. The contact closes again, current flows again, and the cycle repeats rapidly while the switch is pressed.

The same chain (current → electromagnet → attracts iron → movement → effect) explains door locks, scrap-metal
cranes and relays.

## 4.7.2.2 The motor effect and Fleming's left-hand rule (Higher tier only)

When a **conductor carrying a current is placed in a magnetic field**, the magnet producing the field and the
conductor **exert a force on each other**. This is the **motor effect**. The force arises because the wire's own
circular field interacts with the field of the magnet.

**Fleming's left-hand rule** shows the relative directions. Hold your left thumb, first finger and second finger at
right angles to each other:

| Finger | Shows | Direction convention |
|---|---|---|
| First finger | Field | North to south |
| Second finger | Current | Conventional current, positive to negative |
| Thumb | Force (motion) | The direction the wire is pushed |

Using the rule correctly:

- Reverse the current **or** the field: the force reverses.
- Reverse **both**: the force is unchanged.
- Wire **parallel** to the field: **no force**. The rule applies to a wire at right angles to the field, where
  the force is greatest.

### F = BIl

For a conductor at right angles to a magnetic field:

**force = magnetic flux density × current × length**, F = B I l

- F in newtons (N); B in tesla (T); I in amperes (A); l in metres (m).
- The force increases with a **stronger field**, a **larger current** and a **longer length of wire in the field**.
- F is directly proportional to each of B, I and l: double the current and the force doubles.

**Worked example 1.** A wire carrying 4.0 A lies at right angles to a field of flux density 0.25 T. A length of
8.0 cm is inside the field. Calculate the force on the wire.

```
l = 8.0 cm = 0.080 m
F = B I l = 0.25 × 4.0 × 0.080 = 0.080 N
```

**Worked example 2.** A force of 0.054 N acts on 12 cm of wire carrying 3.0 A at right angles to a field.
Calculate the magnetic flux density.

```
l = 0.12 m
B = F / (I l) = 0.054 / (3.0 × 0.12) = 0.054 / 0.36 = 0.15 T
```

**Worked example 3 (proportional reasoning).** In worked example 1, the current is halved and the length in the
field is tripled. Find the new force without using the equation from scratch.

```
F ∝ I × l   →   new F = 0.080 × ½ × 3 = 0.12 N
```

## 4.7.2.3 Electric motors (Higher tier only)

A **coil of wire carrying a current in a magnetic field tends to rotate**. Explain it in steps:

1. The current flows **one way along one side** of the coil and **the opposite way along the other side**.
2. Both sides are at right angles to the field, so a force acts on each (motor effect).
3. By Fleming's left-hand rule, the currents are opposite, so the forces are in **opposite directions**: one side is
   pushed up and the other down.
4. The two forces make the coil **turn about its axis**.

To make a motor turn faster, increase the current or use a stronger magnetic field (or more turns on the coil). To
reverse the direction of rotation, reverse the current or the field. In a practical d.c. motor, a split-ring
commutator reverses the current in the coil every half turn, so the forces keep turning it the same way.

## 4.7.2.4 Loudspeakers and headphones (Higher tier only)

A moving-coil loudspeaker uses the motor effect to turn **variations in current** in an electrical circuit into
**pressure variations in the air** (sound waves).

- The coil sits in the field of a permanent magnet and is fixed to a cone.
- An alternating current in the coil produces a force on it; when the current reverses, the force reverses.
- The coil and cone vibrate back and forth at the **frequency of the current**.
- A **larger current** gives a larger force, a larger vibration and so a **louder** sound (bigger amplitude).
- The vibrating cone produces compressions and rarefactions in the air: a sound wave.

Headphones work the same way on a smaller scale.

## Common mistakes

- Drawing the field around a wire as straight lines. It is concentric circles.
- Saying an iron core "makes the current bigger". It adds its own induced field.
- Using the right hand for the motor effect, or swapping which finger is field and which is current.
- Leaving length in centimetres in F = BIl.
- Applying F = BIl to a wire parallel to the field, where the force is zero.
- In motor explanations, forgetting to say the currents in the two sides are in opposite directions.
- Saying a loudspeaker works with a steady d.c.; a constant current gives a constant force and no vibration.

## Exam technique

For a **calculate** question, write the equation, convert units, substitute, and give the unit; a correct answer
with no working may still score, but working protects you if you slip. **Explain how a device works** questions are
marked on a sequence, so number your steps in your head and link each to the next with "so". **Suggest** questions
about increasing a force want named factors (current, field strength, length). If asked to **determine** a
direction, state the rule you used and what each finger represents; a bare "upwards" is risky if the diagram is
misread.
