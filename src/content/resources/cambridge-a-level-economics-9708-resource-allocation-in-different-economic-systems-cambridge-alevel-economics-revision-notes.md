The Topic 1 introduction asks you to assess the systems' strengths and weaknesses.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Economic system | The institutions and rules through which a society allocates resources |
| Market economy | Resources privately owned; decisions decentralised and co-ordinated by prices |
| Planned economy | Resources state-owned; decisions made centrally by planners |
| Mixed economy | Private and public sectors both allocate resources |
| Decentralised decisions | Many separate agents each decide for themselves |
| Centralised decisions | A central authority decides for the whole economy |
| Price mechanism | The interaction of demand and supply that sets prices and moves resources |
| Output target | A quantity a state enterprise is instructed to produce |
| Rationing | Distributing goods by rules (queues, coupons, quotas) rather than price |
| Private sector | Firms and individuals that own and run resources for themselves |
| Public sector | Government-owned organisations and services |

## The three systems compared

| | Market | Planned | Mixed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ownership | Private | State | Both |
| Who decides | Consumers and firms | Planners | Both, government for some services |
| Co-ordination | Prices and profit | The plan and targets | Prices plus taxes, subsidies, regulation, state provision |
| What | What consumers will pay for | What planners choose | Mostly market; state chooses some |
| How | Cheapest effective method | Methods set to meet targets | Firms choose within rules |
| For whom | Those with income | By allocation and rationing | Market incomes adjusted by taxes and benefits |

## How a change in wants is transmitted

- **Market:** wants rise → price rises → profit rises → firms expand and bid for resources → resources move. No central decision needed.
- **Planned:** wants rise → shortage and queues → planners must notice → plan revised next period → resources reallocated. Slow, and depends on information reaching planners.

**Quick example.** Planners allocate 300 units of fertiliser to wheat farms and 200 to rice farms. Farmers would actually use 250 on wheat and 250 on rice. Result: 50 units unused on wheat farms and a shortage of 50 on rice farms. In a market, the higher price rice farmers would pay draws the 50 units across.

## Strengths and weaknesses

| Market strengths | Market weaknesses |
|---|---|
| Efficient use of resources | Inequality: allocation follows ability to pay |
| Fast response to consumer wants | Public goods not provided |
| Innovation rewarded by profit | Merit goods under-consumed, demerit goods over-consumed |
| Wide consumer choice | Booms and slumps in output and employment |

| Planned strengths | Planned weaknesses |
|---|---|
| Can share basic goods more equally | Information problem: shortages and surpluses |
| Provides public and merit goods | Weak incentives: low quality, little innovation |
| Can direct resources to national priorities quickly | Limited consumer choice |
| Can keep employment high | Rationing, queues and waste |

**Mixed economies** aim to combine price co-ordination with government action where markets fail, but government decisions can themselves be poor.

## Assessment checklist

- Use more than one criterion: efficiency, responsiveness, innovation, equity, provision of public and merit goods, stability.
- Recognise that every real economy is mixed; discuss the **degree** of intervention.
- Link judgements to values: weighting efficiency against fairness is a normative choice.
- Apply to the market in the question: health care, housing and energy raise different issues.

## Exam traps

- Defining a system only by ownership, not decision-making.
- Saying planned economies have no prices (they do, but planners set them).
- Treating mixed economies as all alike.
- Listing strengths without a criterion or judgement.
- Assuming government intervention always improves the outcome.

## Self-test

1. Define a market economy.
2. Who decides what to produce in a planned economy?
3. What co-ordinates decisions in a market economy?
4. Give one reason a planned economy may produce shortages.
5. Give one reason a market economy may produce inequality.
6. Why does a market economy fail to provide public goods?
7. Explain how the "for whom" question is answered in a mixed economy.
8. Planners allocate 120 units of steel to bridges and 80 to housing. Builders would use 100 on each. What happens in each sector?
9. Give two strengths of a planned economy.
10. Give two strengths of a market economy.
11. Why are almost all real economies mixed?
12. What makes the balance between market and state a normative question?

### Answers

1. An economy in which resources are privately owned and decisions are made by individuals and firms, co-ordinated by prices.
2. The government planning authority.
3. Prices, through the interaction of demand and supply.
4. Planners lack full information about consumers' wants and producers' costs, so output targets do not match demand.
5. Goods go to those with the income to pay, so those with low incomes may go without.
6. Non-payers cannot be excluded, so people free-ride and firms cannot earn revenue from them.
7. Mainly by market incomes, adjusted by taxes, benefits and services provided free by the government.
8. Bridges: 20 units more than needed, a surplus. Housing: 20 units short, a shortage.
9. Any two of: more equal sharing of basic goods; provision of public and merit goods; can direct resources to priorities; can keep employment high.
10. Any two of: efficient use of resources; responds quickly to wants; rewards innovation; wide choice.
11. Pure markets and pure planning each have serious weaknesses, so societies combine price co-ordination with government action.
12. It depends on how much weight is placed on efficiency compared with fairness, which is a value judgement.
