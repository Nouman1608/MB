| Impacts of technology on production | balancing cost, productivity, quality and flexibility |

The topic overview says Topic 2.3 is about meeting customer needs through design, supply, quality and sales decisions. Operations is where those needs are met in practice.

## The purpose of operations

Operations is the part of a business that turns inputs (materials, labour, equipment, energy) into outputs that customers want. The specification names two purposes:

- **To produce goods:** physical products such as furniture, food or clothing.
- **To provide services:** intangible activities such as haircuts, repairs, tutoring or deliveries.

Services cannot usually be stored, and the customer is often present while the service is provided. That changes how operations are run: a hairdresser cannot make haircuts in advance for a busy Saturday, so it must plan staff hours around demand instead of building stock.

## The three production processes

| | Job | Batch | Flow |
|---|---|---|---|
| What it means | One product made at a time, often to a customer's own specification | A group of identical products made together, then the next group of a different type | Continuous production of identical products along a production line |
| Typical examples | Wedding dresses, bespoke kitchens, portrait paintings | Bread loaves of different types, sizes of school uniform, flavours of ice cream | Soft drinks, cars, tins of beans |
| Workforce | Skilled, flexible staff | Semi-skilled; some switching between tasks | Workers or machines doing one repeated task |
| Flexibility | Very high | Medium | Low |
| Cost per unit | High | Medium | Low |

The choice depends mainly on demand. A business with small orders for unique products uses job production. One with steady demand for a range of standard products uses batch production. One with very high, steady demand for an identical product can justify the investment in a flow line.

## Impact on productivity, costs and prices

Productivity measures how efficiently inputs are turned into output. A standard measure is **labour productivity = output ÷ number of workers** for a given period. (This is the standard form; it is not one of the Appendix 3 formulae.)

The specification links processes to three outcomes:

- **Keeping productivity up:** batch and flow production let workers repeat tasks and use specialised equipment, so each worker produces more.
- **Keeping costs down:** higher productivity spreads labour and fixed costs across more units, lowering cost per unit.
- **Allowing for competitive prices:** lower unit costs let the business charge less than rivals, or match their prices with a higher profit.

### Worked example 1: Calloway Garden Furniture moves from job to batch

Calloway makes wooden benches. Four craftspeople each earn £2,400 a month. Making each bench to order (job production), they complete 32 benches a month. Switching to batch production of three standard designs, with parts cut in groups, they complete 56 a month.

| | Job production | Batch production |
|---|---|---|
| Labour productivity | 32 ÷ 4 = 8 benches per worker | 56 ÷ 4 = 14 benches per worker |
| Labour cost per bench | £9,600 ÷ 32 = £300 | £9,600 ÷ 56 = **£171.43** |

The labour cost of each bench falls by £128.57, which could let Calloway cut its price to compete with garden centres. The trade-off is flexibility: customers can no longer choose any size or wood, and Calloway must hold stock of finished benches until they sell. If its customers valued made-to-order benches, the lower price might not make up for lost sales.

## Technology in production

Technology in operations includes automated machinery, robots, computer-aided design and manufacture, and software that schedules production or tracks orders. The specification asks you to **balance** four factors:

| Factor | Possible gain | Possible cost |
|---|---|---|
| Cost | Lower labour cost per unit; less waste | High purchase or lease cost; maintenance; training |
| Productivity | More output per worker and per hour; machines can run longer | Breakdowns stop production |
| Quality | Consistent output, fewer human errors | A machine fault repeats on every unit until spotted |
| Flexibility | Programmable machines can switch products quickly | Specialised machines may only do one job |

"Balancing" means a business rarely gains on all four. A small business with varied orders may find that a machine raises fixed costs and reduces flexibility; a large producer with steady demand gains far more.

### Worked example 2: Lindsey Print buys a digital press

Lindsey Print employs four print workers at £26,000 a year each and produces 40,000 print jobs a year. A digital press would cost £36,000 a year to lease and maintain, needs only two workers, and would raise output to 48,000 jobs.

| | Before | After |
|---|---|---|
| Annual cost of labour and press | 4 × £26,000 = £104,000 | (2 × £26,000) + £36,000 = £88,000 |
| Output | 40,000 | 48,000 |
| Cost per job | £104,000 ÷ 40,000 = £2.60 | £88,000 ÷ 48,000 = **£1.83** |
| Labour productivity | 10,000 jobs per worker | 24,000 jobs per worker |

Cost and productivity both improve. Quality may also improve, with sharper, consistent prints. The questions to raise are flexibility (can the press handle unusual papers and sizes that the old process could?), the risk of breakdowns with fewer staff to cover, and the effect on the two workers whose jobs go, including redundancy costs and staff morale.

## Operations in services

The same ideas apply to services. A dental practice can raise productivity with online booking software that cuts empty appointment slots, and a car wash can move from hand washing (closer to job production) to an automated tunnel (closer to flow). The balance is the same: lower cost per customer and faster service against higher investment and a less personal service.

## Common mistakes

- Describing the processes without linking them to productivity, cost per unit and price, which is what the specification asks.
- Confusing productivity (output per worker) with production (total output).
- Saying technology always improves quality and flexibility; it can reduce flexibility.
- Assuming flow is always best; it needs very high, steady demand to justify the investment.
- Forgetting that services are part of operations.

## Exam technique

- **Calculate:** labour productivity, cost per unit before and after a change, or a percentage change in output.
- **Outline:** two linked points in context, such as how batch production affects Calloway's prices.
- **Explain:** a general statement with two expansions, for example why flow production lowers unit costs.
- **Analyse:** follow a change of process or technology to productivity, costs, prices and customers.
- **Justify:** choose between two processes or two machines using the figures, including the flexibility trade-off.
- **Evaluate:** weigh cost, productivity, quality and flexibility for the business in the case and conclude on what the decision depends.
