These notes condense key question 4.2 of Cambridge O Level Sociology (2251), *What is the role of the
family for the individual and society?* Three perspectives each answer the question "who benefits
from the family?" differently: society as a whole (functionalism), capitalism and the ruling class
(Marxism), or men (feminism). Learn one named study and one mechanism for every concept listed.

## Syllabus coverage

- **4.2.1 Functionalist:** benefits to members and society; functions; loss of functions debate;
  changing functions over time; "warm bath" theory; importance of the nuclear family.
- **4.2.2 Marxist:** benefits to capitalism; exploitation; reproduction of class inequalities; unit of
  consumption; ideological control.
- **4.2.3 Feminist:** benefits to patriarchy; domestic division of labour; domestic violence and
  abuse; joint and segregated conjugal roles; dual burden / triple shift; decision-making; gendered
  socialisation.
- **4.2.4** Strengths and limitations of all three views.
- **4.2.5** Whether family life is positive or negative for its members.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Function | The contribution an institution makes to keeping society running |
| Primary socialisation | Learning basic norms and values in early childhood, mainly in the family |
| Warm bath theory | Parsons: the family relieves adult stress so that workers return refreshed |
| Loss of functions | The view that specialised institutions took over family tasks after industrialisation |
| Functional fit | Parsons: the nuclear family suits the mobility needs of industrial society |
| Unit of consumption | The family as a buyer of goods and services that generate profit |
| Ideological control | Teaching ideas that make inequality seem normal or fair |
| Patriarchy | A system in which men hold power over women |
| Domestic division of labour | How housework and childcare are divided between partners |
| Segregated conjugal roles | Partners have separate tasks and leisure |
| Joint conjugal roles | Partners share tasks, decisions and leisure |
| Dual burden | Paid work plus most of the domestic work |
| Triple shift | Paid work, domestic work and emotion work |
| Emotion work | Managing family members' feelings and relationships |
| Gendered socialisation | Learning behaviour seen as appropriate for one's gender |

## Functionalism in five lines

- **Murdock (1949):** four functions: sexual, reproductive, economic, educational.
- **Parsons (1955):** two irreducible functions: primary socialisation and stabilisation of adult
  personalities (the warm bath).
- **Loss of functions:** Parsons says production, education and health care moved to factories,
  schools and the state, leaving the family more specialised.
- **Against:** Fletcher (1966) says family functions have grown in importance, with the state
  supporting rather than replacing the family.
- **Nuclear family:** Parsons's functional fit: small and mobile, suited to industrial work.

## Marxism in five lines

- **Inheritance:** Engels (1884): monogamy secures legitimate heirs for private property, so wealth
  and class stay in families.
- **Exploitation:** women's unpaid domestic labour maintains today's workers and raises tomorrow's
  (Benston, 1969).
- **Consumption:** families spend wages on goods; advertising and peer pressure target parents and
  children.
- **Illusion of private life:** Zaretsky (1976): the family offers escape from work but props up the
  economy.
- **Ideology:** children learn obedience to authority, preparing them to accept hierarchy at work.

## Feminism in six lines

- **Housework:** Oakley (1974): women carry most housework and childcare.
- **Conjugal roles:** Bott (1957) distinguished joint from segregated roles; feminists say "joint" often
  hides unequal responsibility.
- **Dual burden and triple shift:** paid work, domestic work and emotion work (Duncombe and Marsden).
- **Decisions:** Edgell (1980): men take the important decisions, women the routine ones.
- **Violence:** Dobash and Dobash (1979): violence often follows a perceived challenge to the
  husband's authority.
- **Socialisation:** Oakley's manipulation and canalisation steer children into gender roles.

## Strengths and limitations (4.2.4) in one sentence each

- **Functionalism:** explains why families exist almost everywhere, but paints a harmonious, middle-class
  picture that ignores abuse and inequality.
- **Marxism:** explains inheritance and consumer pressure, but ignores the real support families give
  and the gender inequality within classes.
- **Feminism:** exposes unequal work and power, but may understate change and treat all women's
  experiences as the same.

## Positive or negative? (4.2.5)

- **Positive:** love and support; care for the sick and elderly; socialisation; shared income.
- **Negative:** domestic violence and child abuse; unequal work; control of women and young people;
  Leach (1967) on the isolated family as a source of discontent.
- **Best judgement:** it depends which member you ask; gender, age and class shape experience.

## Exam traps

- The warm bath stabilises **adults**, not children.
- The loss of functions debate needs **both** Parsons and Fletcher.
- Do not use "feminists think men are bad": explain the mechanism, such as unequal decision-making.
- Marxist answers must say *how* capitalism benefits: consumption, unpaid labour, inheritance or
  ideology.
- Dual burden is two loads; triple shift is three. Keep them separate.
- In an evaluation, criticise a view with another view **and** with evidence of change.

## Self-test

1. Name Murdock's four functions of the family.
2. What are Parsons's two irreducible functions?
3. What is the warm bath theory?
4. Who argued that family functions have grown, not shrunk?
5. How, according to Engels, does the family reproduce class inequality?
6. Explain the family as a unit of consumption.
7. How does women's unpaid domestic labour benefit capitalism?
8. Distinguish joint from segregated conjugal roles.
9. What is the triple shift?
10. What did Edgell find about decision-making?
11. What triggered violence in many cases studied by Dobash and Dobash?
12. Give one limitation of the feminist view.

### Answers

1. Sexual, reproductive, economic and educational.
2. Primary socialisation of children and stabilisation of adult personalities.
3. The family relieves the stress of adult life, especially work, so that adults return refreshed and
   society stays stable.
4. Ronald Fletcher (1966).
5. Monogamy ensures legitimate heirs, so private property is inherited and wealth stays in the same
   families across generations.
6. Families spend wages on goods and services, generating profit for capitalists, encouraged by
   advertising and peer pressure.
7. It maintains current workers and raises the next generation of workers at no cost to employers.
8. Segregated roles mean separate tasks and leisure; joint roles mean shared tasks, decisions and
   leisure.
9. Paid work, domestic work and emotion work carried by women.
10. Men made the decisions seen as most important, such as moving house and major finances; women
    made routine decisions.
11. Husbands' sense that their authority was being challenged.
12. Any one: it may understate change towards more equal roles; it may treat all women as sharing the
    same experience regardless of class or ethnicity; it may overlook families women find supportive.
