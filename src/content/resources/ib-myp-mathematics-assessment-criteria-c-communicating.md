This guide covers Criterion C, communicating, from the IB *Mathematics guide for the MYP* (for use from September 2020 and January 2021, first assessment 2022). Criterion C is about how clearly your mathematics reaches a reader: the notation you use, the representations you choose, how you link them, and how you set out your reasoning. The year-by-year wording of the strands is compared in [Criteria C and D by year](/resources/ib-myp-mathematics-criteria-c-and-d-by-year-communicating-and-applying/); this page shows how to meet each strand in your own writing. Revise with the [Criterion C revision notes](/resources/ib-myp-mathematics-assessment-criteria-c-communicating-revision-notes/) and practise with the [Criterion C practice questions](/resources/ib-myp-mathematics-assessment-criteria-c-communicating-practice/).

## What Criterion C assesses

Criterion C is marked out of 8 and has five strands. In Year 5 you should be able to:

1. use appropriate mathematical language (notation, symbols and terminology) in both oral and written explanations;
2. use appropriate forms of mathematical representation to present information;
3. move between different forms of mathematical representation;
4. communicate complete, coherent and concise mathematical lines of reasoning;
5. organize information using a logical structure.

Strand iii is not assessed in Year 1. Criterion C is rarely assessed on its own: it is usually judged alongside another criterion, often in an investigation (Criterion B) or a real-life task (Criterion D), so the work you write up for that task is the evidence.

## Strand i: notation, symbols and terminology

Small habits decide this strand. Each row shows a common weak form and a correct one.

| Weak | Correct | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 3.6E5 | 3.6 × 10⁵ | Calculator notation is not mathematical notation |
| x^2, 2^n | x², 2ⁿ | Write powers as superscripts |
| 5 x 3 with the letter x | 5 × 3 | The letter x is a variable |
| 4 + 6 = 10 × 3 = 30 | 4 + 6 = 10, then 10 × 3 = 30 | An equals sign must join equal things |
| 1/2x | (1/2)x or 1/(2x) | Make clear what is in the denominator |
| x = 2.3 (after rounding) | x = 2.33 (3 s.f.) or x ≈ 2.33 | Show that a value has been rounded |
| "the slope thing" | gradient | Use the correct term |
| answer 45 | 45 cm² | Give units |
| angle ABC = 40 | ∠ABC = 40° | Use angle notation and the degree sign |

Terminology matters as much as symbols: say *gradient*, *intercept*, *hypotenuse*, *median*, *frequency*, *expression*, *equation* and *identity* correctly. An expression has no equals sign; an equation does; an identity (≡) holds for every value.

## Strand ii: choosing a representation

| Information | Usually best shown as |
|---|---|
| A few exact values | A table |
| A relationship between two variables | An equation or formula |
| A trend, rate of change or maximum | A graph |
| Shapes, lengths and angles | A labelled diagram |
| Categories of data | A bar chart or pie chart |
| Spread of numerical data | A box-and-whisker diagram or histogram |
| A conclusion | A sentence in words |

A representation is only appropriate if a reader can use it without help. For a graph that means a title, labelled axes with units, an even scale, plotted points that are visible, and a key if there is more than one line. For a table it means headings with units and values in a sensible order.

## Strand iii: moving between forms (worked example 1)

A farmer has 40 m of fencing to make a rectangular pen against a long wall. The wall forms one side. What dimensions give the largest area?

1. **Words to diagram.** Draw the rectangle with the wall along one side. Label the two sides at right angles to the wall x m, so the side parallel to the wall is (40 − 2x) m.
2. **Diagram to expression.** Area A = x(40 − 2x) = 40x − 2x².
3. **Expression to table.**

| x (m) | 0 | 5 | 10 | 15 | 20 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A (m²) | 0 | 150 | 200 | 150 | 0 |

4. **Table to graph.** Plot A against x. The points form a parabola, symmetrical about x = 10.
5. **Graph back to words.** The largest area is at the line of symmetry: x = 10, so the pen is **10 m by 20 m**, with area **200 m²**. The model only makes sense for 0 < x < 20.

Each step carries the same information in a new form, and the last step returns to the context. Saying *why* x = 10 (the symmetry of the table and graph) is what shows you understand the link, not just that you can draw each form.

## Strand iv: lines of reasoning (worked example 2)

Reasoning is **complete** when no needed step is missing, **coherent** when each step follows from the previous one, and **concise** when nothing unnecessary is included. Geometric reasoning tests this well, because each step needs a reason.

Two parallel lines are crossed by a transversal. One angle is 3y + 10 and the angle co-interior to it is 2y − 5, both in degrees. Find y.

Weak version: "3y + 10 + 2y − 5 = 180, y = 35."

Complete, coherent and concise version:

```
Co-interior angles between parallel lines add up to 180°.
So (3y + 10) + (2y − 5) = 180
5y + 5 = 180
y = 35
Check: 3(35) + 10 = 115° and 2(35) − 5 = 65°, and 115° + 65° = 180°.
```

The weak version reaches the right value, but the reader cannot see why the angles add to 180°. One line naming the fact turns it into reasoning. Connecting words such as *so*, *because*, *therefore* and *which means* show how each step follows.

## Strand v: logical structure

A logically structured piece of work lets a reader find anything quickly. For a written task, use this order:

1. **Aim**: the question in your own words.
2. **Information and assumptions**: what is given, what you assume, and the variables you define.
3. **Method**: numbered steps, each with its working beside it.
4. **Results**: a table or graph that collects them.
5. **Conclusion**: a sentence that answers the aim, with units and a sensible degree of accuracy.

Headings, numbered steps and labelled diagrams referred to in the text ("see Table 1") all help. Long blocks of calculation with no words between them do not.

## Before and after: an annotated rewrite

**Before.** "20% of 45 = 9 = 45 − 9 = 36 so its 36 and then 36 × 1.2 = 43.2 so not same"

**After.**

```
Price after a 20% discount: 45 × 0.8 = 36
Price after a 20% increase on 36: 36 × 1.2 = 43.20
43.20 ≠ 45, so the final price is not the original price.
The increase is 20% of a smaller amount (36), so it adds back less than was removed.
```

What changed: no chained equals signs (strand i); one step per line (strand v); each line follows from the one before (strand iv); a final sentence answers the question and gives the reason (strand iv, complete).

## Common mistakes

- Chaining equals signs between quantities that are not equal.
- Using calculator notation or the letter x for multiplication.
- Drawing a graph with no axis labels, units or title.
- Changing variable letters part-way through, or never defining them.
- Giving a correct answer with no sentence linking it to the question.
- Including every rough calculation, so the main line of reasoning is hard to follow.
- Presenting the same information in two forms without saying how they connect.

## Exam technique

Several command terms point straight at Criterion C. *Show that* needs every step, so a reader can follow from the given information to the result. *Explain* needs reasons, so add a *because*. *Justify* needs valid reasons or evidence. *Represent* or *draw* means a complete representation with labels and units. When typing mathematics in an on-screen task, use the notation tools for powers, fractions and roots rather than keyboard shortcuts such as ^ and /.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is Criterion C assessed on its own?**
Usually not. It is normally judged alongside another criterion, often using the way you write up an investigation or a real-life solution.

**What is the difference between coherent and concise reasoning?**
Coherent reasoning means each step follows from the one before. Concise reasoning means nothing unnecessary is included. Year 5 asks for both, as well as complete reasoning.

**Why is chaining equals signs a problem?**
An equals sign states that two things are equal. Writing 4 + 6 = 10 × 3 = 30 claims that 10 equals 30, so the notation is wrong even though the final value is right.
