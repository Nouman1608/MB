**This guide covers the whole of the Section B option The origins of the First World War, c1890-1915 in OxfordAQA International GCSE History (9245).** The specification divides it into two parts: "Two armed camps and increasing tension between the powers, up to 1913" and "The outbreak of the First World War". Section B is assessed through four questions: a 3-mark knowledge question, a 6-mark question explaining one cause or consequence, a 9-mark question explaining two, and a 12-mark question that ends in a judgement. The [Paper 1 Section B study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-world-history-9245-paper-1-section-b/) explains these question types for all four options. Follow up with the [origins of the First World War revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-world-history-9245-origins-of-the-first-world-war-revision-notes/) and the [origins of the First World War practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-world-history-9245-origins-of-the-first-world-war-practice/).

## What the specification says

The option "focuses on the causes of the First World War and seeks to show how and why conflict occurred and why it proved difficult to prevent it and preserve peace". It includes "the impact of the Alliance system, the arms race, imperial rivalries, nationalism and why a regional conflict escalated into a World War".

|---|---|

## Two armed camps

### The alliance system

By the 1890s Europe was dividing into two groups. The **Triple Alliance** (1882) joined Germany, Austria-Hungary and Italy; Italy was a weak member and later stayed out of the war. After Wilhelm II dismissed Bismarck in 1890 and let the treaty with Russia lapse, Russia turned to France, and the **Franco-Russian Alliance** of 1894 meant that Germany might have to fight on two fronts. Britain, long in "Splendid Isolation", began to seek friends. The **Entente Cordiale** (1904) settled colonial disputes with France, and the **Anglo-Russian Agreement** (1907) did the same with Russia. Britain, France and Russia are often called the Triple Entente. The Entente was not a binding military alliance, but it drew Britain towards France.

The effect was **fear of encirclement** in Germany, and a belief in the other camp that Germany was aggressive. A quarrel between two states could draw in the others.

### Kaiser Wilhelm II and Weltpolitik

Wilhelm II wanted Germany to be a world power. His policy of **Weltpolitik** meant building an overseas empire and a great navy, a "place in the sun". German and British colonial rivalries in Africa and the Pacific increased tension. The Kaiser's attitude towards Britain alternated between friendliness and jealousy, and his speeches and telegrams caused alarm.

### The arms race and the Anglo-German Naval Race

Every power expanded its armed forces, because each feared the others and because **militarism** gave the army and navy great influence. The most important rivalry was naval. Admiral Tirpitz's Navy Laws (from 1898) created a German fleet that threatened British dominance of the seas. Britain launched HMS Dreadnought in 1906, a battleship so powerful that it made older ships obsolete, and both countries began to build more. Britain, which depended on its navy to protect its empire and its trade, saw the German fleet as a threat. The naval race strengthened British friendship with France and Russia. Germany and France also expanded their armies; Russia's army grew rapidly and was the largest in Europe.

### Rivalries in North Africa

In the **First Moroccan Crisis** (1905) Wilhelm visited Tangier and spoke in favour of Moroccan independence. His aim was to test the new Entente Cordiale and to weaken French influence. The conference at Algeciras (1906) supported France, and Germany was isolated, so the Entente grew stronger. In the **Second Moroccan Crisis** (1911) Germany sent the gunboat *Panther* to Agadir after France sent troops to Morocco. Britain backed France strongly. Germany accepted part of the French Congo as compensation. The effect was again to bring Britain and France closer, with military talks between their armies, and to increase German bitterness.

### Rivalries in the Balkans

The Ottoman Empire was in decline and its Balkan lands attracted rivals. Austria-Hungary, a multi-national empire, feared **Slav nationalism**, which might encourage its own Slav peoples to break away. Serbia wanted to unite south Slavs, and Russia presented itself as protector of the Slavs.

In the **Bosnian Crisis** (1908-09) Austria-Hungary annexed Bosnia and Herzegovina, which it had governed since 1878 and which contained many Serbs. Serbia was furious; Russia, which was weak after defeat by Japan in 1905, was forced to back down when Germany supported Austria-Hungary. Russia vowed not to be humiliated again.

In the **Balkan Wars** (1912-13) the Balkan states first defeated the Ottoman Empire, then fought among themselves. Serbia gained territory and confidence. Austria-Hungary insisted on creating Albania to keep Serbia from the sea. Austria-Hungary now regarded Serbia as a serious threat, and Serbia looked to Russia for support.

## The outbreak of war

### Short-term causes

**Austria-Hungary and Serbia.** Austria-Hungary wanted to weaken Serbia. Serbia wanted to unite south Slavs, including those in Bosnia. Some Serbian nationalists, including a secret society called the **Black Hand**, were prepared to use violence.

**Sarajevo.** On 28 June 1914 Archduke Franz Ferdinand, heir to the Austro-Hungarian throne, visited Sarajevo, the capital of Bosnia. A first bomb attack failed, but later in the day a young Bosnian Serb, **Gavrilo Princip**, who was supported by the Black Hand, shot the Archduke and his wife.

**The July crisis.** Austria-Hungary wanted to punish Serbia, but needed to be sure of German backing. In early July Germany gave unconditional support, the so-called **blank cheque**. On 23 July Austria-Hungary sent an **ultimatum** with ten demands. Serbia accepted most of them but rejected the demand that Austrian officials take part in the investigation inside Serbia. On 28 July Austria-Hungary declared war on Serbia.

### The widening of the conflict

The alliances now worked like a chain. Russia mobilised to defend Serbia. Germany saw Russian mobilisation as a threat and declared war on Russia on 1 August, and on France on 3 August. German military planning left little room for delay. The **Schlieffen Plan** aimed to defeat France in about six weeks by attacking through neutral Belgium before turning on Russia, which was expected to mobilise slowly. This made the invasion of Belgium on 4 August part of the plan, not a choice at the last moment.

**Britain's decision.** Britain had not promised to fight alongside France, but it had a moral commitment through the Entente and a legal duty to defend Belgium under the 1839 Treaty of London. When Germany invaded Belgium, Britain declared war on 4 August.

**Responsibility.** Historians give different weights to different countries. Germany gave Austria-Hungary the blank cheque and had the Schlieffen Plan. Austria-Hungary issued an ultimatum it knew Serbia would struggle to meet. Serbia's nationalists helped to plan the assassination. Russia's mobilisation made a regional war more likely. Britain's lack of a clear warning to Germany and the French desire for revenge also played a part. **Diplomacy failed** because the leaders were committed to the alliances and to military timetables, and because proposals for a conference were not accepted.

**Escalation to 1915.** The war spread beyond Europe. Japan joined the Allies in August 1914. The Ottoman Empire joined the Central Powers late in 1914. Italy, in spite of the Triple Alliance, joined the Allies in 1915, in return for promises of territory. The European empires brought in their colonies, with fighting in Africa and the Pacific, which made the conflict global.

## Writing the answers

### 3 marks

Give three precise points. Example: *Give three ways in which Britain and Germany were rivals before 1914.* The naval race and the Dreadnought; colonial rivalries; the Kaiser's Weltpolitik; the German fear of encirclement; British support for France in the Moroccan Crises.

### Worked example: a 9-mark answer

*Explain two reasons why a regional conflict in the Balkans became a world war in 1914.* **[9]**

> **First, the alliances turned a quarrel into a chain reaction.** Austria-Hungary's war on Serbia drew in Russia, which saw itself as the protector of the Slavs and mobilised. Germany, bound to Austria-Hungary, declared war on Russia and then on France, Russia's ally. So a conflict between two states became a war between the two armed camps.
>
> **Second, the Schlieffen Plan spread the war to Belgium and Britain.** Germany's plan to defeat France quickly meant invading neutral Belgium. Britain had guaranteed Belgian neutrality in 1839, and on 4 August it declared war. The plan therefore added Britain and its empire to the war, and made it impossible to confine the fighting to eastern Europe.

### The 12-mark judgement

*"Germany was mainly responsible for the outbreak of the First World War." How far do you agree?* Cover the given factor in depth: the blank cheque, Weltpolitik, the naval race and the Schlieffen Plan. Then at least one other factor: Serbian nationalism and the Black Hand, Austria-Hungary's ultimatum, Russia's mobilisation, the alliance system. Compare the factors, for example by arguing that Germany made the war wider but that Austria-Hungary and Serbia began it, and give a judgement supported by evidence.

## Common mistakes

- Saying the assassination was the only cause. It was the trigger; the alliances, the arms race and rivalries were the underlying causes.
- Confusing the Entente (1904 and 1907) with the Triple Alliance (1882).
- Writing that the Moroccan Crises weakened the Entente. They strengthened it.
- Saying Britain went to war only because of the Entente. Belgium mattered.
- Treating the Schlieffen Plan as a reaction to events in July. It was drawn up years earlier.
- Writing a list of causes in a 12-mark answer without comparing them.
