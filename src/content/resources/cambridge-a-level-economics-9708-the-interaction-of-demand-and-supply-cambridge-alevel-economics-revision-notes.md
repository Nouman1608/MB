## Equilibrium (2.4.1)

- **Equilibrium:** quantity demanded = quantity supplied; no tendency for price to change.
- **Disequilibrium:** quantity demanded and supplied differ at the current price.
- **Price below equilibrium:** excess demand (shortage) -> price is bid up -> quantity demanded contracts, quantity supplied extends.
- **Price above equilibrium:** excess supply (surplus) -> price is cut -> quantity demanded extends, quantity supplied contracts.
- During adjustment, the curves **do not shift**; only movements along them occur.

**Solving equations:** set Qd = Qs, solve for P, substitute back for Q. Excess demand or supply at another price = the gap between Qd and Qs at that price.

## Shifts (2.4.2)

| Change | Price | Quantity |
|---|---|---|
| D right | up | up |
| D left | down | down |
| S right | down | up |
| S left | up | down |

**Combined shifts:** one variable is certain, the other depends on the relative size of the shifts.

| Combined | Certain | Uncertain |
|---|---|---|
| D right + S right | Quantity up | Price |
| D left + S left | Quantity down | Price |
| D right + S left | Price up | Quantity |
| D left + S right | Price down | Quantity |

**Quick diagnosis from data:** price and quantity moving the same way points to a demand shift; moving in opposite directions points to a supply shift.

## Related markets (2.4.3)

| Link | Definition | Effect to remember |
|---|---|---|
| Joint demand | Complements used together | Price of A falls -> demand for B shifts right |
| Alternative demand | Substitutes meeting the same want | Price of A rises -> demand for B shifts right |
| Derived demand | Demand for an input arises from demand for the product it makes | Demand for product rises -> demand for input shifts right |
| Joint supply | One process produces two or more goods together | Output of A rises -> supply of B shifts right |

Evaluate links by their **strength** (close or weak substitutes), the **share of cost** an input makes up, and **time** to adjust.

## Functions of price (2.4.4)

- **Signalling (transmission of preferences):** a price change carries information to producers and consumers about what is wanted and what is scarce.
- **Incentivising:** higher prices raise expected profit, encouraging firms to supply more and resources to move in; lower prices push resources out and encourage buyers to buy more.
- **Rationing:** when a good is scarce, a higher price limits quantity demanded to the amount available, allocating it to those willing and able to pay.

Limits: allocation by ability to pay, not need; slow response when supply is inelastic; signals depend on good information.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Market equilibrium | Price at which quantity demanded equals quantity supplied |
| Excess demand | Quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied at the current price |
| Excess supply | Quantity supplied exceeds quantity demanded at the current price |
| Complement | Good consumed together with another |
| Substitute | Good that can replace another |
| Derived demand | Demand for a factor or input that depends on demand for the final product |
| Joint supply | Two or more products produced together by one process |
| Price mechanism | The system in which prices allocate resources through rationing, signalling and incentives |

## Exam traps

- Shifting a curve to remove a shortage. Shortages close through movements along the curves.
- Stating definite changes in both price and quantity after two shifts.
- Confusing joint supply (production link) with joint demand (consumption link).
- Calling derived demand "demand that comes from another good being cheaper".
- Listing the three functions of price without applying them to the market in the question.
- Leaving out a time period or the conditions behind "equilibrium".

## Self-test

1. Define market equilibrium.
2. Qd = 120 - 4P and Qs = 6P - 30. Find equilibrium price and quantity.
3. In Question 2, what is the excess demand at a price of 12?
4. What happens to equilibrium price and quantity when supply decreases?
5. Demand increases and supply increases. Which variable is certain to change, and how?
6. Give an example of joint demand.
7. Explain why the demand for bricklayers is a derived demand.
8. A rise in the price of beef leads to more cattle being raised. What happens to the supply of leather, and why?
9. Name the three functions of price in the syllabus.
10. What does "transmission of preferences" mean?
11. Give one limitation of rationing by price.
12. Price rises and quantity falls in a market. Which curve has most likely shifted, and which way?

**Answers**

1. The price at which quantity demanded equals quantity supplied, so there is no tendency for price to change.
2. 120 - 4P = 6P - 30, so 150 = 10P, P = 15 and Q = 120 - 60 = 60.
3. Qd = 120 - 48 = 72; Qs = 72 - 30 = 42; excess demand = 30.
4. Price rises and quantity falls.
5. Quantity rises; the effect on price depends on the relative sizes of the shifts.
6. Printers and ink cartridges, cars and petrol, or games consoles and games.
7. Bricklayers are wanted because buildings are wanted; demand for their labour depends on demand for construction.
8. Supply of leather shifts right, because leather is produced jointly with beef from the same cattle.
9. Rationing, signalling (transmission of preferences) and incentivising.
10. Price changes pass information about what consumers want to producers, who can respond by changing output.
11. Goods go to those able to pay, not necessarily to those in greatest need.
12. Supply has most likely shifted left.
