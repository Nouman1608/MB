These notes condense section **3.3.13.4 DNA** of the AQA AS and A-level Chemistry specification (7404/7405), for teaching from September 2015. It is A-level only content, examined on Paper 2 and Paper 3. Explanations and worked examples are in the [DNA study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-dna/), and exam-style questions are in the [DNA practice questions](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-dna-practice/). ## What you must be able to do

- Describe a **nucleotide**: phosphate + 2-deoxyribose + one of four bases.
- Describe a **single strand**: a polymer of nucleotides, with the **sugar–phosphate** backbone.
- Describe the **double helix** of two complementary strands.
- **Explain** how hydrogen bonding between base pairs leads to the two complementary strands.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Nucleotide | Phosphate + 2-deoxyribose + base |
| 2-deoxyribose | A pentose (5-carbon) sugar with no –OH on carbon 2 |
| Base | Adenine (A), cytosine (C), guanine (G) or thymine (T) |
| Backbone | Alternating sugar and phosphate groups joined by covalent bonds |
| Complementary | Each base has a specific partner on the other strand |
| Double helix | Two strands twisted around each other |
| Base pair | Two bases joined by hydrogen bonds |

## The nucleotide

```
phosphate – 2-deoxyribose – base
```

- The **base is attached to the sugar**, not the phosphate.
- The structures of phosphate, 2-deoxyribose and the four bases are in the Data Booklet.

## The strand

- The **phosphate** of one nucleotide bonds covalently to the **2-deoxyribose** of the next.
- This gives a **sugar–phosphate–sugar–phosphate** chain.
- The **bases hang off** the sugars.
- The **order of bases** carries the information.

## The double helix

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| Strands | Two, complementary |
| Backbones | On the outside |
| Bases | Pointing inwards |
| Pairing | A–T and C–G |
| Held by | Hydrogen bonds between bases |

## Base pairs

| Pair | Hydrogen bonds |
|---|---|
| A–T | 2 |
| C–G | 3 |

- Hydrogen bonds form between **N–H** (δ+ H) and a lone pair on **O or N**.
- Each pair has the same overall width, so the double helix has a regular diameter.
- A pairs only with T, and C pairs only with G.

## Why hydrogen bonds?

| Point | Explanation |
|---|---|
| Each is weak | The strands can separate for copying |
| Many in total | The helix is stable overall |
| Backbone covalent | The chain is not broken when strands separate |

## Model explanation

"Each base has a specific arrangement of N–H and C=O/N groups. Adenine can form two hydrogen bonds with thymine, and guanine can form three with cytosine. Because the bases only fit with these partners, the sequence on one strand determines the sequence on the other, so the two strands are complementary. The hydrogen bonds are weak, so the strands can be separated, but the covalent backbone stays intact."

## Calculations

- Complementary sequence: A↔T, C↔G.
- Hydrogen bonds = 2 × (number of A–T pairs) + 3 × (number of C–G pairs).
- In double-stranded DNA, %A = %T and %C = %G.
- If %A = x then %C = (100 − 2x) ÷ 2.

Example: a double helix of 500 base pairs is 60 % C–G pairs. C–G pairs = 300 (900 hydrogen bonds); A–T pairs = 200 (400 hydrogen bonds); total 1300 hydrogen bonds.

## Worked examples to copy

**Example 1.** Strand 1 is G–G–A–T–C. State strand 2 and the number of hydrogen bonds.

1. Complement each base: G→C, G→C, A→T, T→A, C→G.
2. Strand 2: C–C–T–A–G.
3. Pairs: G–C (3), G–C (3), A–T (2), T–A (2), C–G (3).
4. Total = 3 + 3 + 2 + 2 + 3 = 13 hydrogen bonds.

**Example 2.** Explain why DNA with more C–G pairs needs a higher temperature to separate.

1. Each C–G pair has three hydrogen bonds; each A–T pair has two.
2. More C–G pairs means more hydrogen bonds per base pair.
3. More energy is needed to break them, so a higher temperature is needed.

## DNA and proteins side by side

| | Protein | DNA |
|---|---|---|
| Monomer | Amino acid | Nucleotide |
| Link | Peptide, –CO–NH– | Phosphate–sugar covalent bond |
| Information in | Order of amino acids | Order of bases |

## Strand and helix compared

| Feature | In a strand | Between strands |
|---|---|---|
| Bond type | Covalent | Hydrogen |
| Joins | Phosphate to sugar | Base to base |
| Strength | Strong | Weak, but many |

## Exam traps

- A–C and G–T pairs.
- Base attached to the phosphate.
- Backbone described as hydrogen-bonded.
- 3 hydrogen bonds for A–T.
- "Ribose" instead of "2-deoxyribose".
- Strands described as identical.
- Hydrogen bonds said to be broken when the DNA is strong.
- Missing the idea that pairs fit specific partners.

## Self-test

1. Name the three components of a nucleotide.
2. Which part is the base attached to?
3. Name the four bases.
4. What type of bond joins nucleotides in a strand?
5. Which groups are linked by this bond?
6. What is the sequence of the complementary strand to A–A–G–C–T?
7. How many hydrogen bonds are between A and T?
8. How many hydrogen bonds are between C and G?
9. Why are the strands described as complementary?
10. Why is it useful that the bonds between strands are hydrogen bonds?
11. In a sample of DNA, 22 % of bases are guanine. What percentage is adenine?
12. How many hydrogen bonds are there in the sequence G–C–A–T?

### Answers

1. Phosphate, 2-deoxyribose and a base.
2. The sugar (2-deoxyribose).
3. Adenine, cytosine, guanine, thymine.
4. A covalent bond.
5. The phosphate of one nucleotide and the 2-deoxyribose of another.
6. T–T–C–G–A.
7. Two.
8. Three.
9. Each base pairs only with a specific partner, so one strand determines the other.
10. They are weak enough for the strands to separate but strong enough in total to hold the helix together.
11. C = 22 %, so A + T = 56 %, so A = 28 %.
12. The four pairs are G–C (3), C–G (3), A–T (2) and T–A (2), so 3 + 3 + 2 + 2 = 10.
