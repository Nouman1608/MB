## Section A

**1.** State **two** physical properties, named in the syllabus, that vary with temperature and can be used to measure it. **[2]**

**2.** Convert:

**(a)** 77 K to degrees Celsius

**(b)** –40 °C to kelvin

**(c)** a fall in temperature of 30 K to a fall in degrees Celsius. **[3]**

**3.** State the value of absolute zero in degrees Celsius, and explain why a temperature of –300 °C cannot exist. **[2]**

**4.** State what is meant by the statement that the thermodynamic temperature scale does not depend on the property of any particular substance. **[1]**

**5.** A thermocouple has one junction in a flame and the other in melting ice. Explain why the second junction is kept at a fixed, known temperature. **[2]**

**6.** A student writes: "A change of 1 K is the same as a change of 1 °C, so 50 °C is the same as 50 K." Correct the student's statement. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** A fixed mass of gas is kept at constant pressure. Its volume is 40.0 cm³ at 0.0 °C and 54.6 cm³ at 100.0 °C. Assume the volume varies linearly with temperature.

**(a)** Use these data to estimate the value of absolute zero in degrees Celsius. **[3]**

**(b)** Predict the volume of the gas at 50.0 °C. **[1]**

**(c)** Explain why absolute zero cannot be found by cooling the gas until its volume is zero. **[1]**

**8.** A platinum resistance thermometer and a liquid-in-glass thermometer are both calibrated at 0 °C and 100 °C. They are then placed in the same beaker of warm water.

| | At 0 °C | At 100 °C | In the beaker |
|---|---|---|---|
| Resistance / Ω | 25.0 | 34.8 | 29.6 |
| Length of liquid column / cm | 3.0 | 21.0 | 11.7 |

**(a)** Calculate the temperature of the water on the resistance scale. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the temperature of the water on the liquid-in-glass scale. **[2]**

**(c)** Both thermometers are in thermal equilibrium with the water. Explain why their readings differ. **[2]**

**9.** A gas is at 27 °C.

**(a)** Convert this temperature to kelvin. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the temperature, in °C, to which the gas must be heated for its thermodynamic temperature to triple. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain why tripling the Celsius temperature (to 81 °C) does not triple the thermodynamic temperature. **[1]**

**10.** The density of water is greatest at about 4 °C.

**(a)** Explain why the density of water is not a suitable thermometric property between 0 °C and 8 °C. **[2]**

**(b)** State **one** other requirement of a good thermometric property. **[1]**

**11.** A physical property P of a material is measured at three temperatures: P = 2.0 units at 20 °C, 3.5 units at 50 °C and 2.0 units at 80 °C. Explain why P cannot be used to measure temperatures over the range 20 °C to 80 °C. **[2]**

**12.** Explain the difference between an empirical temperature scale and the thermodynamic temperature scale, and state the zero of the thermodynamic scale. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** Any two [1 each]: density of a liquid; volume of a gas at constant pressure; resistance of a metal; e.m.f. of a thermocouple.

**2. (a)** 77 – 273.15 = –196 °C [1].

**(b)** –40 + 273.15 = 233 K [1].

**(c)** 30 °C (temperature differences are the same on both scales) [1].

**3.** –273.15 °C [1]; this is the lowest possible temperature, and –300 °C is below it (it would be a negative thermodynamic temperature) [1].

**4.** The scale is defined without reference to how any one material's property changes, so it gives the same temperature whatever thermometer substance is used [1].

**5.** The e.m.f. depends on the temperature difference between the two junctions [1]; keeping one junction at a known temperature means the e.m.f. depends only on the temperature of the other, so it can be calibrated [1].

**6.** The size of one degree is the same on both scales, but the zeros differ [1]; 50 °C = 50 + 273.15 = 323 K [1].

**7. (a)** Gradient = (54.6 – 40.0) ÷ 100.0 = 0.146 cm³ per °C [1]; V = 0 when θ = 0.0 – 40.0 ÷ 0.146 [1] = –274 °C [1].

**(b)** 40.0 + 50.0 × 0.146 = 47.3 cm³ [1].

**(c)** A real gas liquefies (and solidifies) long before reaching absolute zero, so the zero-volume point must be found by extrapolation [1].

**8. (a)** θ = (29.6 – 25.0) ÷ (34.8 – 25.0) × 100 [1] = 46.9 °C [1].

**(b)** θ = (11.7 – 3.0) ÷ (21.0 – 3.0) × 100 [1] = 48.3 °C [1].

**(c)** Each scale assumes its property varies linearly with temperature between the fixed points [1]; the resistance of platinum and the expansion of the liquid do not vary with temperature in the same way, so the scales agree only at the fixed points [1].

**9. (a)** 27 + 273.15 = 300 K [1].

**(b)** 3 × 300.15 = 900 K [1]; 900.45 – 273.15 = 627 °C [1].

**(c)** The zero of the Celsius scale is not absolute zero, so Celsius temperatures are not proportional to thermodynamic temperatures (81 °C is only 354 K) [1].

**10. (a)** Because the density has a maximum near 4 °C, the same density occurs at two different temperatures [1], so a reading could not be matched to a single temperature [1].

**(b)** It must change measurably for small changes in temperature; or it must be reproducible [1].

**11.** P has the same value (2.0 units) at two different temperatures [1], so a reading of 2.0 units would not identify a single temperature; P does not vary in one direction over the range [1].

**12.** An empirical scale is based on a particular property of a particular substance, calibrated at fixed points, and different empirical scales can disagree between those points [1]. The thermodynamic scale does not depend on the property of any substance [1]. Its zero is absolute zero, 0 K (–273.15 °C) [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Naming devices when properties are asked for.
- Adding 273.15 to a temperature change.
- Writing –273.15 K for absolute zero, or °K as a unit.
- Treating Celsius temperatures as if they could be multiplied or divided meaningfully.
- Saying two calibrated thermometers "should" agree everywhere, or that one of them must be faulty.
- Forgetting that extrapolation is needed to reach absolute zero from gas data.
