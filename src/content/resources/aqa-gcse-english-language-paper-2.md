This guide covers **Paper 2: Writers' Viewpoints and Perspectives**,
the second of two examined components in AQA GCSE English Language
(8700), for first teaching 2015. It complements the site's existing
[Paper 1 study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-english-language-paper-1/),
[Paper 1 revision notes](/resources/gcse-english-language-paper-1-revision-notes/),
[Paper 1 practice questions](/resources/gcse-english-language-paper-1-practice/)
and [Paper 2 practice questions](/resources/gcse-english-language-paper-2-practice/)
by covering the paper those resources do not: where Paper 1 (Explorations
in Creative Reading and Writing) works with fiction and narrative
description, Paper 2 works entirely with non-fiction, comparing how
different writers present their own viewpoint on a shared topic.

## Syllabus coverage

**AQA GCSE ENGLISH LANGUAGE (8700) -- PAPER 2 WRITERS' VIEWPOINTS AND PERSPECTIVES**

- **Section A: Reading** -- two unseen non-fiction or literary
  non-fiction extracts on a related theme, drawn from the 19th
  century plus either the 20th or 21st century, depending on the exam
  series. Genres include high-quality journalism, articles, reports,
  essays, travel writing, accounts, sketches, letters, diaries,
  autobiography and biographical writing. Questions require students
  to identify and interpret information and ideas, explain writers'
  use of language and structure to achieve effects, and compare the
  writers' different perspectives across the two texts. Section A is
  worth 40 of the paper's 80 marks.
- **Section B: Writing** -- a single writing task related to the theme
  of the Section A sources, requiring students to produce a piece of
  writing (for example, an article, a letter, or a speech) that
  presents their own viewpoint or perspective on the topic, using
  appropriate form, tone and rhetorical technique. Section B is also
  worth 40 marks.

The paper lasts 1 hour 45 minutes and, together with Paper 1, forms
the full external assessment for GCSE English Language; a separate,
non-exam-assessed Spoken Language endorsement is reported but carries
0% weighting toward the overall grade.

## Why two texts, and why one from the 19th century

Unlike Paper 1's single fictional extract, Paper 2 is built entirely
around comparison. The specification requires all texts to be
"high-quality, challenging" and to represent "a substantial piece of
writing," making genuine demands in content, structure and language --
transient material such as instant news feeds is explicitly excluded.
Pairing a 19th-century source with a modern one is a deliberate design
choice: it forces students to read across historical distance, noticing
how tone, assumed audience and rhetorical convention differ between,
say, a Victorian travel account and a 21st-century opinion piece on
the same broad theme, rather than simply comparing two contemporary
writers whose styles are more likely to overlap.

## What "comparing perspectives" actually means

The specification's own aims are precise: students should "read and
evaluate texts critically and make comparisons between texts," and
"summarise and synthesise information or ideas from texts." In
practice, this means a strong Section A answer does not describe each
text's viewpoint separately and then note a difference at the end -- it
identifies specific, comparable points (attitude to the same event,
choice of emotive language, use of a particular structural device) and
discusses how each writer's choices serve their distinct purpose or
audience, throughout the answer rather than as an afterthought.

## Section B: writing to a specified viewpoint

Section B does not ask students to write neutrally -- it requires
presenting *a* viewpoint, clearly and persuasively, on the theme set by
Section A. This means the writing skills tested here (rhetorical
questions, direct address, structured argument, a deliberately chosen
tone) are different in kind from Paper 1's narrative and descriptive
skills, even though both are assessed against similar technical
accuracy criteria (Standard English, accurate grammar, punctuation and
spelling, and a wide vocabulary). Students who default to a Paper
1-style narrative opening in a Paper 2 writing task -- telling a story
rather than making and developing an argument -- consistently
underperform relative to their technical accuracy, because the task
demands a different genre of writing altogether.

## Common mistakes

- **Summarising each Section A text separately instead of comparing
  them directly**, especially failing to address *how* the two writers'
  methods differ, not just *what* each one says.
- **Ignoring the historical gap between the two texts.** A 19th-century
  extract will use period-appropriate vocabulary, sentence structure
  and assumptions that a modern reader may misread as simply "old
  fashioned" rather than as a deliberate stylistic and historical
  feature worth analysing.
- **Writing a narrative in Section B** when the task calls for
  viewpoint-driven, persuasive or discursive writing -- the two require
  genuinely different structures and techniques.
- **Treating Section A and Section B as unconnected halves of the
  paper**, when the specification deliberately links them: Section B's
  writing task shares its theme with Section A's reading sources, and
  strong candidates let ideas encountered while reading inform the
  content of their own writing.
- **Under-using structural analysis in Section A**, focusing only on
  individual word choices (language) while neglecting how a writer
  orders and structures information across the whole extract to build
  their argument.

## How to approach it

Practise reading two thematically linked non-fiction extracts side by
side and drafting comparison points before writing a full answer,
since the comparative skill -- not comprehension of either text alone
-- is what Section A is built to test. Read genuinely across the
19th-century/modern divide during revision, using real extracts from
each period, so that period-specific vocabulary and tone become
familiar rather than a source of anxiety in the exam itself. For
Section B, practise writing to a stated viewpoint under timed
conditions, deliberately avoiding narrative structure, and check draft
responses against the technical-accuracy criteria (Standard English,
grammar, punctuation, vocabulary) that apply equally to both papers.
Finally, since Section A and B share a theme, use planning time to let
ideas and vocabulary from the reading sources inform the writing task,
rather than treating the two sections as entirely separate 45-minute
exercises.
