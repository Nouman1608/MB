## 6.2.5(a): Making and purifying an organic solid

This outcome links to PAG6, Synthesis of an organic solid, in the specification.

| Technique | Purpose | Detail that earns credit |
|---|---|---|
| Quickfit apparatus | Leak-free assembly | Ground-glass joints; clamp flask and condenser |
| Heating under reflux | Heat a slow reaction for a long time | Vertical condenser, open top; water in at the bottom |
| Distillation | Remove a volatile liquid | Thermometer bulb level with the side arm |
| Filtration under reduced pressure | Collect crystals quickly and fairly dry | Büchner funnel, side-arm flask, vacuum source |
| Recrystallisation | Remove impurities from a solid | Minimum hot solvent; cool; filter; wash cold; dry |
| Melting point | Check purity and identity | Capillary tube; heat slowly near the expected value |

### Method in steps: recrystallisation

1. Pick a solvent: product very soluble hot, slightly soluble cold.
2. Add hot solvent until the crude solid just dissolves (the **minimum** volume).
3. Filter while hot if insoluble solid remains.
4. Cool slowly, then in ice.
5. Filter under reduced pressure.
6. Wash with a little **ice-cold** solvent.
7. Dry, then weigh and take the melting point.

Why it works: the product saturates the cold solution and crystallises. Soluble impurities are too dilute to saturate it, so they stay dissolved.

### Melting point rules

- Pure: sharp range (about 1 °C) at the known value.
- Impure: lower **and** wider range.
- Damp: melts low as well, so dry first.

### Worked reminder: mass recovered

3.40 g of crude solid gives 2.55 g after recrystallisation. Recovery = 2.55 ÷ 3.40 × 100 = **75.0%**. Some product always stays in the cold solvent, so recovery is never 100%.

### Must-know distinctions

- **Reflux vs distillation.** Reflux returns vapour to the flask; distillation sends it to a receiver.
- **Reduced pressure vs gravity filtration.** Suction is faster and leaves a drier solid.
- **Hot solvent vs cold wash.** Use the minimum of hot solvent to dissolve, and only a little ice-cold solvent to rinse.
- **PAG5 vs PAG6.** PAG5 is the organic liquid (separating funnel, drying, redistillation); PAG6 is the organic solid.

## 6.2.5(b): Several functional groups in one molecule

First name every group, then go through likely reagents one at a time.

| Reagent | Group that responds | What you see or get |
|---|---|---|
| Na₂CO₃(aq) | Carboxylic acid only | Effervescence (CO₂) |
| NaOH(aq) | Carboxylic acid and phenol | Salt forms; no gas |
| Br₂(aq) | Alkene (addition), phenol (substitution) | Orange colour removed; phenol also gives a white precipitate |
| 2,4-DNP | Aldehyde or ketone | Orange precipitate |
| Tollens' reagent, warm | Aldehyde only | Silver mirror |
| K₂Cr₂O₇/H₂SO₄ | Primary or secondary alcohol, aldehyde | Orange to green |
| NaBH₄ | Aldehyde or ketone | Alcohol; C=C untouched |
| Dilute HCl | Amine | Ammonium salt |
| Hot aqueous acid or alkali | Ester, amide, nitrile (acid hydrolysis) | Hydrolysis products |

O–H and N–H groups hydrogen bond. They raise melting and boiling points and increase solubility in water.

### Worked reminder: a hydroxy ester

Compound H is CH₃CH(OH)CH₂COOCH₃, methyl 3-hydroxybutanoate.

1. **Groups.** A secondary alcohol (OH on carbon 3) and a methyl ester.
2. **Acidified dichromate, heat under reflux.** Oxidation turns the secondary alcohol into a ketone, CH₃COCH₂COOCH₃. The colour changes from orange to green.
3. **Hot aqueous NaOH.** The ester is hydrolysed to sodium 3-hydroxybutanoate and methanol.
4. **Sodium carbonate.** No effervescence: there is no COOH group.
5. **Chirality.** Carbon 3 carries H, OH, CH₃ and CH₂COOCH₃, so it is a chiral centre.

## 6.2.5(c): Multi-stage routes

### Route map by goal

| Goal | Steps |
|---|---|
| Add one carbon to a chain | Haloalkane + CN⁻ in ethanol, or aldehyde/ketone + NaCN(aq)/H⁺(aq) |
| Add a carbon chain to a ring | Friedel–Crafts: haloalkane or acyl chloride + halogen carrier |
| Amine, same carbon count | Haloalkane + excess ethanolic NH₃ |
| Amine, one more carbon | Haloalkane → nitrile (CN⁻) → amine (H₂/Ni) |
| Aromatic amine | Arene → nitroarene (conc. HNO₃/H₂SO₄) → amine (Sn/conc. HCl) |
| Carboxylic acid | Oxidise primary alcohol or aldehyde; hydrolyse nitrile or ester |
| Ester of an alcohol | Acid + alcohol (conc. H₂SO₄); or acid anhydride or acyl chloride + alcohol |
| Ester of a phenol | Acid → acyl chloride (SOCl₂) → + phenol |
| Primary or secondary amide | Acyl chloride + NH₃, or + primary amine |
| Alcohol from carbonyl | NaBH₄ |

### Method in steps

1. List the groups in the start and target, and count carbons.
2. Spot the carbon-adding step, if any.
3. Work backwards one step at a time.
4. On a ring, order substitutions by directing effects: OH and NH₂ direct to 2 and 4; NO₂ directs to 3.
5. Make reactive groups such as NH₂ late in the route.
6. Give reagents, conditions and every intermediate.
7. Use any extra reaction the paper supplies exactly as given.

### More must-know distinctions

- **Ethanolic NH₃ vs CN⁻ then H₂/Ni.** Both give a primary amine from a haloalkane. Only the nitrile route adds a carbon.
- **NaBH₄ vs H₂/Ni.** NaBH₄ reduces only C=O in aldehydes and ketones. H₂/Ni reduces C=C and C≡N.
- **Carboxylic acid vs acyl chloride.** Both make esters of alcohols. Only the acyl chloride readily esterifies a phenol, and it reacts without an acid catalyst.
- **Benzene vs phenol nitration.** Benzene needs concentrated HNO₃ and concentrated H₂SO₄. Phenol reacts with dilute HNO₃ and gives a mixture of 2- and 4-nitrophenol that must be separated.

### Small worked reminders

- **Propanal to 2-hydroxybutanoic acid.** NaCN(aq)/H⁺(aq) gives 2-hydroxybutanenitrile; heating under reflux with dilute acid hydrolyses CN to COOH.
- **Benzene to 1-phenylbutan-1-ol.** Butanoyl chloride with AlCl₃ (Friedel–Crafts acylation) gives the ketone C₆H₅COCH₂CH₂CH₃; NaBH₄ then reduces it to the secondary alcohol C₆H₅CH(OH)CH₂CH₂CH₃, which has a chiral centre.
- **Two-stage yield.** Stages of 80% and 65% give 0.80 × 0.65 × 100 = **52.0%** overall.

## Quick self-test

1. Why is the minimum volume of hot solvent used in recrystallisation?
2. Why are the collected crystals washed with ice-cold solvent?
3. A sample melts from 131 °C to 135 °C. Take the pure compound's melting point as 137 °C. Is the sample pure?
4. Crude product 3.40 g; recrystallised product 2.55 g. What percentage was recovered?
5. Which reagent shows that a molecule with a phenol group also has a COOH group?
6. Which test tells an aldehyde from a ketone?
7. Give the two stages to turn 1-chlorobutane into pentanoic acid.
8. Phenyl butanoate is needed from phenol. What should react with the phenol, and why not butanoic acid?
9. Name the reaction and reagents that put an ethyl group onto a benzene ring.
10. Which reagent reduces a C=O in a molecule but leaves its C=C unchanged?
11. A route has two stages with yields of 80% and 65%. What is the overall yield?

### Answers

1. So the solution is saturated when cold and most product crystallises; extra solvent keeps product dissolved.
2. To rinse off solution holding impurities without dissolving much product.
3. No. It melts below 137 °C and over a 4 °C range.
4. 2.55 ÷ 3.40 × 100 = **75.0%**.
5. Sodium carbonate solution: effervescence of CO₂. Phenol alone gives none.
6. Tollens' reagent. Aldehydes give a silver mirror; ketones leave it unchanged.
7. KCN in ethanol, heat under reflux, gives pentanenitrile; then dilute acid, heat under reflux, gives pentanoic acid.
8. Butanoyl chloride; phenol is not readily esterified by a carboxylic acid.
9. Friedel–Crafts alkylation: chloroethane with a halogen carrier such as AlCl₃.
10. NaBH₄ (H₂/Ni would also reduce the C=C).
11. **52.0%**.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "dissolve in hot solvent" without "minimum volume".
- Washing crystals with room-temperature solvent.
- Saying an impure sample has a higher melting point, or omitting the wider range.
- Describing gravity filtration when filtration under reduced pressure is asked for.
- Giving aqueous KCN for the nitrile step; the specification pairs CN⁻ with ethanol.
- Using ammonia when the target amine has one more carbon than the haloalkane.
- Esterifying a phenol with a carboxylic acid.
- Ordering ring substitutions against the directing effects, or making NH₂ before an acyl chloride step.
- Using NaBH₄ to reduce a carboxylic acid or a C=C bond.
- Missing an intermediate's structure in a multi-stage route.
