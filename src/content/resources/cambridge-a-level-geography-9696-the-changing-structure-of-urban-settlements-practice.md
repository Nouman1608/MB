## Section A

**1.** Define the term "functional zonation". **[2]**

**2.** Explain why retailing is usually able to pay the highest rent at the centre of a city. **[3]**

**3.** In the fictional city of Corran, the rent each land use can pay (in illustrative units) at a distance *d* km from the centre is: retail 900 − 300*d*; offices 600 − 150*d*; housing 300 − 50*d*.

**(a)** Calculate the distance from the centre at which offices replace retail as the highest bidder. Show your working. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the distance at which housing replaces offices, and the distance at which housing's bid falls to zero. **[2]**

**(c)** Suggest **two** reasons why land use in the real city of Corran may not follow this pattern. **[2]**

**4.** The table shows the distribution of two groups across three districts of the fictional city of Fenwick.

| District | Group X | Group Y |
|---|---|---|
| North | 600 | 100 |
| Central | 300 | 150 |
| South | 100 | 250 |
| Total | 1 000 | 500 |

**(a)** Calculate the index of dissimilarity for groups X and Y, using D = ½ × Σ |(x ÷ X) − (y ÷ Y)| × 100. Show your working. **[3]**

**(b)** Explain **two** processes that could have produced this pattern. **[4]**

**5.** Explain why manufacturing has moved from inner-city sites to the edges of many cities in high income countries. **[4]**

## Section B

**6.** Describe and explain the changes that have taken place in the CBDs of many cities in high income countries. **[6]**

**7.** Explain how planning can influence the location of retailing within an urban area. **[4]**

**8.** Explain why residential segregation by ethnicity occurs. **[5]**

**9.** Compare the location of offices in cities in the 1960s with their location today. **[3]**

**10.** "Accessibility is the most important factor affecting the location of activities within urban areas." With reference to examples, how far do you agree? **[15]**

## Answers

**1.** The grouping of similar land uses or functions into distinct areas of a city [1], such as a retail core, industrial zones and residential areas, produced by competition for space [1]. **[2]**

**2.** The centre is the most accessible point in the city, reachable from all directions by public transport [1]. Shops need the largest possible number of customers passing by [1]. High turnover per square metre means retailers can afford the highest rents, so they outbid other uses [1]. **[3]**

**3. (a)** 900 − 300*d* = 600 − 150*d*, so 300 = 150*d* [1]; *d* = 2 km [1].

**(b)** 600 − 150*d* = 300 − 50*d*, so 300 = 100*d* and *d* = 3 km [1]. Housing's bid reaches zero at 300 ÷ 50 = 6 km [1].

**(c)** Planning controls such as zoning or protected historic buildings [1]; other accessible points, such as a ring road junction or railway station, creating secondary peaks of land value [1]. **[6]**

*Examiner insight:* also credit physical barriers such as a river or steep slope, or inertia of older land uses, in (c).

**4. (a)** Proportions of X: 0.6, 0.3, 0.1; of Y: 0.2, 0.3, 0.5 [1]. Differences: 0.4, 0.0, 0.4, total 0.8 [1]. D = ½ × 0.8 × 100 = 40 [1].

**(b)** The housing market: if group Y has lower incomes, it can afford only cheaper housing, perhaps concentrated in the South [1], so income differences sort the groups into different districts [1]. The influence of family and friends: newcomers from group Y settle near relatives who help them find housing and work [1], so the group becomes concentrated in particular districts over time [1]. **[7]**

*Examiner insight:* also credit culture (places of worship, shops) or planning (allocation of public housing) as processes in (b).

**5.** Old inner sites are small, multi-storey and congested, with no room to expand [1]. Edge-of-city land is cheaper and allows large single-storey buildings with parking [1]. Firms now depend on road transport, so sites near motorway junctions are more accessible than inner sites near railways or canals [1]. Planning policies created industrial estates and science parks on the edge, while many inner factories closed during deindustrialisation [1]. **[4]**

**6.** Retail floor space has fallen as shoppers use out-of-town centres [1] and online shopping [1]. Vacant units have become cafés, restaurants and leisure uses [1]. Some offices have moved to business parks, and others have been converted to apartments [1], so the CBD's residential population has grown through re-urbanisation [1]. Pedestrianisation and public transport schemes have been introduced to reduce congestion [1]. **[6]**

**7.** Zoning decides which land uses are allowed in each area [1]. A "town centre first" policy can refuse permission for out-of-town shopping centres [1]. Planners can create pedestrian areas and car parks that make centres more attractive to shops [1]. Incentives or regeneration funds can attract retailers to declining areas [1]. **[4]**

**8.** New migrants settle near family and friends who help with housing and jobs [1]. Groups may choose to live near places of worship, specialist shops and community organisations [1]. Lower average incomes in some ethnic groups restrict them to cheaper housing areas [1]. Discrimination by landlords, lenders or estate agents limits choice [1]. Wealthier groups may move away from areas as minorities arrive, increasing separation [1]. **[5]**

**9.** In the 1960s most offices were concentrated in the CBD for accessibility to workers and clients [1]. Today many are in business parks on the urban edge near motorways and airports [1], although central offices remain for high-status firms that need face-to-face contact [1]. **[3]**

**10.** The real exam marks extended answers with levels of response, not point by point. The points below are indicative creditworthy content.

*Introduction:* defines accessibility as ease of reaching a place [1] and identifies other factors: economic, social, environmental and political [1].

*Agree:* bid rent theory shows that accessibility to customers explains the concentration of retail and offices in the CBD [1]. Decentralisation followed changes in accessibility: car ownership and ring roads made edge sites accessible [1]. Manufacturing moved to motorway junctions, and business parks to airports [1].

*Disagree:* planning can override accessibility, for example by restricting out-of-town retail [1]. Land price and the need for space explain manufacturing and warehousing locations [1]. Environmental factors such as flood risk and attractive settings affect housing and offices [1]. Social factors, including income and ethnicity, explain residential patterns [1]. Online shopping has reduced the importance of physical accessibility for retail [1].

*Variation:* accessibility matters more for retail than for housing [1]; in LIC and MIC cities, informal settlements locate where land is available, not where it is accessible [1].

*Use of examples:* named urban areas or developments with specific detail [1].

*Judgement:* a reasoned view on "most important" [1] that varies by type of activity [1]. **[15]**

*Examiner insight:* the strongest answers show that accessibility itself changes over time with transport and technology.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Algebra errors in bid rent questions; set the two expressions equal and show each step.
- Forgetting the ½ in the index of dissimilarity.
- Describing CBD change without causes.
- Explaining segregation only as discrimination.
- Comparisons written as two separate descriptions.
