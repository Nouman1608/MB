## Section A

**1.** Explain what is meant by a radiation surplus at a given latitude. **[2]**

**2.** State **two** reasons why high latitudes receive less solar radiation per unit area than low latitudes. **[2]**

**3.** Name the surface winds that blow from the subtropical high-pressure belt towards the ITCZ in the northern hemisphere, and state the direction they blow from. **[2]**

**4.** Explain why land heats up and cools down faster than the sea. **[3]**

**5.** Explain how a cold ocean current affects the climate of the coast beside it. **[3]**

**6.** Explain why the position of the ITCZ changes between seasons. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** The table shows illustrative annual net radiation values for the southern hemisphere.

| Latitude (°S) | 10 | 25 | 35 | 45 | 55 | 65 | 75 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Net radiation (W/m²) | +55 | +30 | +5 | -20 | -45 | -65 | -85 |

**(a)** Identify the two latitudes in the table between which net radiation changes from surplus to deficit. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the difference in net radiation between 10°S and 75°S. **[1]**

**(c)** Explain the pattern shown in the table. **[4]**

**8.** Two fictional stations lie at 55°N. Station E is on the west coast of a continent beside a warm ocean current. Station F is on the east coast of the same continent beside a cold current, with prevailing winds blowing from the interior.

| | Mean January (°C) | Mean July (°C) |
|---|---|---|
| Station E | 4 | 14 |
| Station F | -12 | 16 |

**(a)** Calculate the annual temperature range at each station. **[2]**

**(b)** Suggest reasons for the differences between the two stations. **[4]**

**9.** Explain how the atmosphere transfers energy from low to high latitudes. **[6]**

**10.** Describe and explain the seasonal movement of the pressure and wind belts. **[6]**

**11.** Explain how the distribution of land and sea contributes to the South Asian monsoon. **[4]**

**12.** "Ocean currents are more important than winds in transferring energy around the globe." Discuss this view. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** Incoming solar radiation absorbed at that latitude is greater than the outgoing longwave radiation lost [1], so over a year the latitude gains more energy than it loses [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Two from: the sun's rays strike at a low angle and are spread over a larger area [1]; radiation passes through a longer path in the atmosphere and more is absorbed or scattered [1]. Accept high albedo of ice and snow, or no daylight in winter, in place of either. **[2]**

**3.** The north-east trade winds [1], blowing from the north-east [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Water has a higher specific heat capacity, so more energy is needed to raise its temperature [1]. Radiation penetrates water to some depth, whereas on land it heats only a thin surface layer [1]. Water mixes, carrying heat downwards, so the surface warms and cools more slowly [1]. **[3]**

**5.** The cold current cools the air above it, lowering coastal temperatures, especially in summer [1]. Cooled air is dense and stable, so it does not rise and little rain forms [1]. Coastal fog may form where moist air passes over the cold water, but the coast is often very dry, as in coastal deserts [1]. **[3]**

**6.** The overhead sun migrates between the tropics during the year [1], and the zone of strongest heating and rising air, the ITCZ, follows it after a short lag [1]. **[2]**

**7. (a)** Between 35°S and 45°S [1].

**(b)** 55 - (-85) = 140 W/m² [1].

**(c)** At low latitudes the high sun concentrates radiation on a small area, giving a surplus [1]. Towards the pole, lower sun angles spread radiation over larger areas and through more atmosphere [1], and ice and snow reflect much of it [1]. Outgoing longwave radiation falls much less with latitude, so it exceeds absorbed radiation polewards of about 40°S [1]. **[6]**

**8. (a)** Station E: 14 - 4 = 10°C [1]; station F: 16 - (-12) = 28°C [1].

**(b)** At E, the warm current releases heat into the air in winter, keeping January mild [1], and westerly winds from the ocean carry this maritime air onshore [1]. At F, prevailing winds blow from the cold continental interior in winter, bringing very low temperatures [1]. The interior heats strongly in summer, so these winds raise F's July temperature above E's despite the cold current [1]. **[6]**

**9.** In the Hadley cell, air heated at the equator rises and flows polewards at high level, carrying heat [1]. Evaporation in the tropics stores energy as latent heat in water vapour [1], which is released when the vapour condenses at higher latitudes [1]. In mid-latitudes, depressions and anticyclones move warm air polewards and cold air equatorwards [1]. The westerlies and jet streams steer these systems [1]. Air sinking at the poles flows outwards as polar easterlies, completing the exchange of air between latitudes [1]. **[6]**

**10.** The overhead sun moves north to the Tropic of Cancer in June and south to the Tropic of Capricorn in December [1]. The ITCZ follows the zone of maximum heating, so it lies north of the equator in the northern summer and south of it in the southern summer [1]. The subtropical highs and subpolar lows shift several degrees polewards in summer [1]. As a result, the trade winds and westerlies also shift, so some places come under different wind belts in different seasons [1]. Movement is greater over land, which heats quickly, than over the oceans [1]. In winter, strong cooling creates large high-pressure areas over continents, such as over Siberia [1]. **[6]**

**11.** In summer, the Asian land mass heats much faster than the Indian Ocean [1], so a large low-pressure area forms over the land [1]. Moist air is drawn in from the ocean, bringing heavy rain [1]. In winter, the land cools quickly, high pressure forms over the continent, and dry winds blow outwards towards the sea [1]. **[4]**

**12.** For the view: water has a high heat capacity, so currents carry and store very large amounts of heat [1]. Warm currents such as the North Atlantic Drift strongly raise winter temperatures on the coasts they reach [1]. Oceans release heat slowly, so they influence climate over seasons and years [1]. In the deep tropics ocean transport is particularly important [1].

Against the view: the atmosphere carries the larger share of poleward transfer overall [1]. It moves both sensible heat and latent heat in water vapour, quickly, over continents as well as oceans [1]. Winds drive many surface ocean currents, so the two systems are linked [1].

Judgement: winds carry more energy overall and act faster, but ocean currents dominate regional climates near coasts and store heat for longer, so the importance of each depends on scale and location [1]. **[8]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Explaining the radiation pattern with "distance from the sun".
- Giving a range from negative values incorrectly, for example 16 - 12 instead of 16 - (-12).
- Naming a wind without its direction, or with the wrong direction for the hemisphere.
- Describing seasonal shifts without linking them to the overhead sun.
- Ignoring the context in a stem, such as the direction of prevailing winds.
