Revision notes for the thematic study **Popular movements (from 1750 CE onwards)** in IB DP History (first assessment 2028), for SL and HL. Four inquiry questions, sixteen lines of inquiry, no prescribed examples. See the [study guide](/resources/ib-dp-history-2028-thematic-popular-movements/) and the [practice questions](/resources/ib-dp-history-2028-thematic-popular-movements-practice/).

## Paper 2 in one box

| Part | Task | Marks |
|---|---|---|
| Section A | Analyse a concept, illustrated by an example from the thematic study | 6 |
| Section B (a) | Short response on one example | 4 |
| Section B (b) | Essay using examples from at least two regions | 15 |

Total 25 marks, 1 hour 45 minutes.

## Definition and types

Popular movements: the collective efforts of a group of people to bring about a change. Types in the guide: movements for political freedoms and equality (civil rights, women, youth, labour); movements that advocate an idea or issue (peace, environment); Indigenous rights; independence and anti-colonial movements.

## The four inquiry questions and lines of inquiry

| Inquiry question | Lines of inquiry |
|---|---|
| Why did popular movements emerge? | Political factors; economic factors; role of ideas; social factors |
| How did popular movements create change? | Political participation; non-violent methods; cultural influence; violent methods |
| How were popular movements challenged? | Political opposition; divisions within the movement; violent opposition; resilience of traditional ideas |
| What was the impact of popular movements? | Political change; social change; experiences of women; experiences of marginalised groups |

## Quick evidence by line of inquiry

**Emergence**

- Political: exclusion from power; the Rowlatt Acts; denial of the vote in the American South.
- Economic: food price rises in Poland in 1980; low wages under apartheid.
- Ideas: satyagraha; Christian belief; equality and democracy.
- Social: segregation, discrimination and the exclusion of women.

**Methods**

- Participation: petitions, lobbying, the 1994 election in South Africa.
- Non-violent: Salt March; Montgomery boycott; March on Washington; Defiance Campaign.
- Cultural: freedom songs, newspapers, film.
- Violent: suffragette arson; Umkhonto we Sizwe sabotage (historians debate the effects).

**Challenge**

- Political opposition: martial law in Poland; the Cat and Mouse Act.
- Divisions: suffragists and suffragettes; Congress and the Muslim League; Black Power.
- Violent opposition: Selma, Sharpeville and Soweto.
- Traditional ideas: gender roles; racial hierarchy; opposition to land rights.

**Impact**

- Political: Civil Rights Act 1964; Voting Rights Act 1965; the acts of 1918 and 1928; independence in 1947.
- Social: end of legal segregation; the limits of the 1994 settlement.
- Women: Montgomery and the Women's Political Council; women in the Salt March and suffrage campaign.
- Marginalised groups: 1967 referendum (about 90 per cent in favour); Mabo (1992).

## Section A and B(a)

**Section A.** One-line definition of the concept; one specific example; analysis; judgement. For significance, give criteria; for continuity and change, give both.

**Section B (a).** One example: identify, describe, then explain how it relates to the question.

## Section B (b): the essay

1. Argument in the opening.
2. Examples from at least two regions.
3. Organise by argument, not by movement.
4. Compare explicitly: similarities, differences, patterns, exceptions.
5. Reasoned judgement.

## Worked example: a short plan

Question: "To what extent were non-violent methods more effective than violent methods in popular movements?"

- Thesis: non-violent methods were usually more effective in winning wide support and legitimacy, but they depended on pressure and context, and violence sometimes added to that pressure.
- Paragraph 1 (Asia): the Salt March and non-cooperation in India: mass participation, international attention, arrests.
- Paragraph 2 (Americas): the civil rights movement: boycotts and marches, and violent opposition that gained sympathy.
- Paragraph 3 (Africa): the ANC's turn to sabotage after Sharpeville, alongside mass protest and international pressure.
- Paragraph 4 (Europe): suffragette militancy and its effect: increased attention but also division.
- Conclusion: non-violence was more effective as a rule, but results depended on the opponent and on international context.

## Common mistakes

- Narrating one movement.
- Treating a movement as united.
- Forgetting women and marginalised groups.
- Using two examples from one region.

## Self-test

1. Define popular movements as the guide does.
2. Name two types of popular movement listed in the guide.
3. When was the Salt March?
4. What was Sharpeville (1960)?
5. What did the 1967 Australian referendum allow?
6. Name two lines of inquiry under "How were popular movements challenged?"

**Answers.**

1. The collective efforts of a group of people to bring about a change.
2. Any two of: movements for political freedoms and equality; movements that advocate an idea or issue; Indigenous rights movements; independence and anti-colonial movements.
3. March to April 1930.
4. The shooting of protesters by police in South Africa, followed by banning of the ANC and PAC.
5. The federal government to make laws for Aboriginal people, and the counting of Aboriginal people in the census.
6. Any two of: political opposition; divisions within the movement; violent opposition; resilience of traditional ideas.
