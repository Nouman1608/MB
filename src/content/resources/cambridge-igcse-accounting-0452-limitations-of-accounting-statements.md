## Why accurate statements can still mislead

A set of financial statements can be arithmetically correct, follow every rule and still give an incomplete picture. The statements record past transactions in money, using methods the business has chosen. Users then make decisions about the future. Section 6.5 asks you to explain the gap between what the statements show and what a user needs to know.

## 1. Historic cost

Under the historic cost concept, assets are recorded at what they cost when bought. This is objective, because the cost can be checked against an invoice, but it creates three limitations.

**Asset values are out of date.** Ashdown Joinery bought a cutting machine four years ago for 48,000 and depreciates it at 10% of cost a year. Its net book value is 48,000 − (4 × 4,800) = 28,800. A new machine of the same kind now costs 66,000. The statement of financial position shows 28,800, which tells a user neither what the machine could be sold for nor what it would cost to replace.

**Depreciation is based on the old cost.** The annual charge of 4,800 is a share of a price paid four years ago. When the machine has to be replaced, the business will need far more than the depreciation charged so far.

**Money in different years is not comparable.** Ashdown's revenue rose from 400,000 to 432,000, which looks like growth of 8%. If its selling prices rose by 6% over the year, then 432,000 ÷ 1.06 = about 407,547 of sales at last year's prices, so the real increase in what was sold is only about 1.9%. The statements do not adjust for changing prices, so a user who reads only the figures overestimates growth.

**Effect on ratios.** Old assets at low book values make capital employed small, so ROCE can look high for a business with ageing equipment.

## 2. Application of accounting policies

Many figures depend on choices and estimates made by the business:

- the depreciation method (straight line or reducing balance), the rate and the expected useful life and residual value
- the valuation of inventory at the lower of cost and net realisable value, which needs an estimate of net realisable value
- the size of the allowance for irrecoverable debts
- the limit used under the materiality concept to decide what is capitalised
- judgements about whether spending is capital or revenue expenditure

### Worked example: one business, two sets of choices

Pemberton Supplies has revenue of 300,000 and profit of 60,000 before three year-end items. It has new equipment costing 50,000 and trade receivables of 40,000, and it is creating an allowance for irrecoverable debts for the first time.

| Item | Policy set A | Policy set B |
|---|---|---|
| Depreciation on equipment | Straight line, 10% of cost: 5,000 | Reducing balance, 30%: 15,000 |
| Allowance for irrecoverable debts | 2% of 40,000: 800 | 5% of 40,000: 2,000 |
| Inventory written down to net realisable value | Judged unnecessary: 0 | Damaged items written down: 1,500 |
| Profit for the year | 60,000 − 5,800 = **54,200** | 60,000 − 18,500 = **41,500** |
| Profit margin | 54,200 ÷ 300,000 × 100 = 18.07% | 41,500 ÷ 300,000 × 100 = 13.83% |

The same transactions give profits 12,700 apart. Both sets of choices can be acceptable if applied consistently. The limitation is that a user cannot see the effect of the choices from the profit figure alone, which matters when comparing with another business or after a change of policy. The consistency concept reduces, but does not remove, the problem: it keeps one business comparable with itself, not with others.

## 3. Non-financial aspects

The money measurement concept means only items that can be reliably measured in money are recorded. Some of the most important influences on a business are therefore missing. The syllabus gives three examples.

| Aspect | Why it matters | Why it is not shown |
|---|---|---|
| Skill of the workforce | Skilled staff improve quality, speed and customer service, and so future profit | Staff are not owned and their skill cannot be reliably valued; only wages appear, as an expense |
| Location of the business | A site near customers or transport links can raise sales and lower costs for years | The statements show what the premises cost or the rent paid, not the advantage of the site |
| Economic climate | A recession, rising interest rates or higher prices affect sales and costs | It is outside the business and is not a transaction |

Other aspects examiners accept in the same way include customer loyalty and reputation, the quality of management, staff morale, the strength of competitors and the business's effect on the environment.

### Worked example: identical figures, different prospects

Two bakeries, Corran Loaf and Fenwick Bread, each report revenue of 180,000 and profit of 27,000. Corran's head baker has thirty years' experience and the shop sits beside a busy railway station. Fenwick has just lost its only skilled baker, and a new supermarket with an in-store bakery is opening next door. The statements show two equal businesses; a lender or buyer who knew the non-financial facts would judge Corran far more likely to keep its profit.

## Annotated model answer

*Question: Hamid plans to buy a shop. He says its statement of financial position shows exactly what the business is worth. Evaluate his view.* [6 marks]

> The statement shows the assets less liabilities, which gives some guide to value **(for)**. However, non-current assets are shown at historic cost less depreciation, so the premises may be worth much more or less than their book value **(historic cost, applied)**. Figures such as depreciation and the allowance for irrecoverable debts depend on the policies chosen, so another accountant might show different totals **(policies, applied)**. The shop's reputation, location and the skill of its staff are not recorded because they cannot be measured in money, yet they affect what a buyer would pay **(non-financial, applied)**. The economic climate could also change future sales **(further point)**. Hamid's view is therefore wrong: the statement is a starting point, but he needs current valuations and information about the shop's location, staff and market before deciding on a price **(judgement)**.

## Common mistakes

- Saying historic cost is "wrong". It is a recognised concept; the limitation is that cost may differ from current value.
- Writing "different policies" without naming one, such as the depreciation method.
- Listing non-financial aspects without explaining how each affects the business.
- Confusing a limitation of the statements with an error in them.
- Missing the economic climate, which is outside the business entirely.

## Exam technique

- **State** a limitation: one of the three factors, named.
- **Explain**: name the factor, show its effect on a figure, and say why that misleads the user.
- **Discuss** or **Evaluate** the usefulness of the statements: give what they do show, then their limitations, then a judgement about how far the user can rely on them.
- **Suggest** what extra information a user needs: current valuations, details of policies, information about staff, location and markets.
