This guide covers topic 1.4 Population density and distribution in Theme 1 (Population and settlement) of the Cambridge IGCSE Geography (0460) syllabus for examination in 2025 and 2026. The syllabus asks you to "describe the factors influencing the density and distribution of population", covering physical, economic, social and political factors, and requires **two** case studies: a densely populated country or area and a sparsely populated country or area, at any scale from local to regional. The [Population Density and Distribution revision notes](/resources/igcse-geography-0460-population-density-distribution-revision-notes/) summarise the factors and describe Egypt and Brazil; this guide adds density calculations, a sorting framework for factors and a matched pair of dense and sparse case studies. ## Density and distribution are different ideas

- **Population density** is the average number of people per unit of area: **density = population ÷ area**, usually in people per km².
- **Population distribution** is the pattern of where people live: even or uneven, clustered, linear (along a river, coast or road) or scattered.

Two countries can have the same density but very different distributions. Egypt's average density hides the fact that nearly everyone lives along the Nile.

**Worked calculation (fictional region, invented data).** Region T has 2,400,000 people and an area of 16,000 km².

Density = 2,400,000 ÷ 16,000 = **150 people per km²**.

If 1,800,000 of those people live in a river valley of 3,000 km², the valley's density is 1,800,000 ÷ 3,000 = **600 people per km²**, while the remaining 600,000 people on 13,000 km² give about **46 people per km²**. The average of 150 describes neither area well, which is why case studies must explain the distribution as well as the density.

## A framework for factors

The syllabus groups factors into four types. Learn at least two from each and say whether they **attract** people (positive) or **discourage** them (negative).

| Type | Attracts people | Discourages people |
|---|---|---|
| **Physical** | Flat or gently sloping land; fertile soils (river silt, volcanic soils); reliable water; moderate climate; minerals and energy resources; natural harbours | Steep, high mountains; extreme cold or heat; very low or extremely high rainfall; dense forest; poor soils; frequent hazards |
| **Economic** | Jobs in industry and services; ports and trade routes; good transport; investment | Few jobs; remote from markets; high costs of building and supplying settlements |
| **Social** | Services such as schools and hospitals; family and community ties; long history of settlement | Lack of services; isolation; cultural or historical avoidance of an area |
| **Political** | New capitals and planned towns; resettlement schemes; stable government; open borders | Conflict; restricted border zones; government limits on settlement |

Physical factors often explain the **original** pattern; economic, social and political factors explain why it has **changed** or been **reinforced**. Many dense areas began on fertile farmland and grew further as cities, ports and industries developed there.

## Case study: a densely populated area, Java (Indonesia)

Java is one of the most densely populated large islands in the world. More than half of Indonesia's people live on it, although it makes up only a small fraction of the country's land area.

- **Physical**: a chain of volcanoes has produced deep, fertile volcanic soils. A hot, wet tropical climate allows rice to be grown in terraced and irrigated fields, often with more than one harvest a year, so the land can feed very large numbers of people.
- **Historical and economic**: Java was the centre of Dutch colonial administration and plantations, and the capital, Jakarta, grew on its north coast as the main port and centre of government. Most of Indonesia's industry, services and transport links developed on Java, attracting migrants from other islands.
- **Social**: dense networks of villages, towns and services reinforce the pattern, because people stay near family and services.
- **Political responses**: for decades the government ran a transmigration programme that resettled families from Java to less crowded islands such as Sumatra and Kalimantan. In 2019 the government announced that the capital would move to a new city, Nusantara, in East Kalimantan on Borneo, partly to reduce pressure on Jakarta and Java. Both show governments trying to change a distribution, with only partial success so far.

## Case study: a sparsely populated area, Nunavut (Canada)

Nunavut, in Canada's far north, became a separate territory in 1999. It covers about 2 million km², roughly a fifth of Canada, but the 2021 census counted only about 37,000 people. Its density is about 0.02 people per km². Most residents are Inuit. People live in about 25 small communities, mostly on the coast; the capital, Iqaluit, is on Baffin Island.

- **Physical**: an Arctic climate with long, very cold, dark winters; permafrost makes building and water supply difficult; the ground is tundra or ice, so crops cannot be grown. Coastal sites give access to the sea for hunting and for summer supply ships.
- **Economic**: few jobs outside government services and mining, such as the Mary River iron mine on Baffin Island. Food, fuel and building materials must be flown or shipped in, so living costs are very high.
- **Social and accessibility**: no roads connect the communities with the rest of Canada, so travel is by air, or by sea in summer. Specialist healthcare and higher education often mean travelling south.
- **Political**: the creation of the territory gave Inuit greater self-government and created administrative jobs in Iqaluit, which has grown faster than other communities.

Comparing the two case studies makes a strong answer: Java's fertile volcanic soils and long history of farming support millions of people, while Nunavut's permafrost and isolation support only a few thousand people in each community.

## Describing a distribution from a map

1. **Overall pattern**: even or uneven; where people are concentrated.
2. **Dense areas**: name them, with direction and features ("along the south coast", "in the river valley").
3. **Sparse areas**: name them in the same way.
4. **Evidence**: quote densities or values from the key.
5. **Anomalies**: any dense pocket in a sparse region, such as a mining town or oasis.

Keep description and explanation separate: "describe" questions want the pattern, not the reasons.

## Model answer: explain why an area is sparsely populated

*Question: Explain why some areas have a low population density. Use examples.*

> Areas with extreme climates are sparsely populated (knowledge). In Nunavut, long winters and permafrost prevent farming and make building expensive (application), so food has to be brought in by air or sea, raising the cost of living and discouraging settlement (analysis). Remoteness adds to this: without road links there are few jobs except in government and mining, so few people move there (analysis). Physical factors are the main reason, but the lack of economic opportunities keeps the population low even where modern technology could overcome the cold (evaluation).

## Common errors

- Using "density" when describing a pattern, or "distribution" when giving a number.
- Listing factors ("climate, relief, soil") without explaining how each affects the number of people.
- Giving only physical factors when the syllabus names four types.
- Choosing a dense area and a sparse area but giving no place-specific details.
- Calculating density as area ÷ population.

## Exam technique

- **Calculate**: write the formula, substitute and give units (people per km²).
- **Describe**: pattern, named places, figures, anomalies.
- **Explain**: factor → effect on living conditions or jobs → effect on population.
- **Compare**: use both case studies in the same sentence with "whereas".
