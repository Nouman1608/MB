## Where aquatic food comes from

Aquatic food is produced by **capture fisheries** (wild harvest from seas, lakes and rivers) and **aquaculture** (farming of fish, crustaceans, molluscs and aquatic plants). Demand has risen with population, income and the spread of refrigerated transport. Capture production has levelled off since the 1990s as many stocks reached or passed their limits, so nearly all growth in supply since then has come from aquaculture.

Wild production is limited by **primary productivity**, which varies greatly across the oceans:

| Zone | Productivity | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Continental shelves | High | Shallow, sunlit water; nutrients from rivers and mixing from the seabed |
| Upwelling zones | Very high | Deep nutrients brought to the surface |
| Estuaries, coral reefs, kelp forests | Very high per unit area | Nutrient input, and many producers |
| Open ocean gyres | Low | Strong stratification keeps nutrients in deep water |

Most of the world's catch comes from the small share of the ocean over continental shelves and in upwelling zones. Food chains in the open ocean are also long (from tiny phytoplankton through several consumer levels), so little of the producers' energy reaches the large fish people eat.

## The surplus-yield idea behind MSY

The Topic 4 guide states that MSY is taken at about half the carrying capacity. The reason comes from the logistic growth model. A stock's annual growth (its **surplus yield**, the amount that can be removed without shrinking the stock) depends on how big the stock is:

```
surplus yield = r × N × (1 − N ÷ K)
r = maximum growth rate per year, N = stock size, K = carrying capacity
```

At small N there are few fish to breed; near K, competition slows growth. The surplus is largest between the two.

### Worked example: surplus yield at different stock sizes

A fictional stock has K = 100 000 t and r = 0.4 per year.

```
N = 20 000 t:  0.4 × 20 000 × (1 − 0.2) = 6 400 t per year
N = 50 000 t:  0.4 × 50 000 × (1 − 0.5) = 10 000 t per year  (maximum: MSY)
N = 80 000 t:  0.4 × 80 000 × (1 − 0.8) = 6 400 t per year
```

**Interpretation.** A catch of 10 000 t a year can be sustained only if the stock is held at 50 000 t. A catch of 6 400 t can be sustained at either 20 000 t or 80 000 t, but the larger stock is far safer: it is further from collapse, more resilient to a bad recruitment year and gives a higher catch per unit effort. If the stock is at 20 000 t and fleets take 10 000 t, more than the surplus is removed and the stock shrinks further each year.

Limitations of MSY in practice:

- K and r are estimates, often uncertain, and they change with climate and food supply.
- Stocks fluctuate naturally; a fixed MSY catch in a poor year can trigger collapse.
- MSY treats one species in isolation and ignores its role in the food web, including as prey for seabirds and marine mammals.
- Many managers now aim for catches below MSY to leave a safety margin (a precautionary approach).

## How fishing changes food webs

- **Fishing down the food web.** Daniel Pauly and colleagues showed in the late 1990s that, as large predatory fish were depleted, global catches shifted towards species from lower trophic levels. The average trophic level of landings fell.
- **Trophic cascades.** Removing predators can allow their prey to increase, with knock-on effects lower down, for example more sea urchins grazing kelp forests.
- **Shifting baselines.** Pauly also described the "shifting baseline syndrome": each generation of scientists or fishers judges the health of a stock against the state they first knew, so long-term decline goes unnoticed.
- **Size selection.** Taking the largest fish removes the most productive spawners and can favour fish that mature at smaller sizes.

## Case study: the collapse of Grand Banks cod

Atlantic cod off Newfoundland, Canada, supported a fishery for centuries. After the Second World War, large factory trawlers with sonar dramatically raised catches, which peaked in the late 1960s and then fell. Canada extended its fishing limit to 200 nautical miles in 1977, but domestic fleets then expanded and quotas were set using optimistic stock estimates. Catch rates were kept up by technology that found the remaining fish, hiding the decline. In 1992 the stock had fallen so far that Canada declared a moratorium on commercial fishing for northern cod, and tens of thousands of people in Newfoundland lost their jobs. Recovery has been very slow, despite the moratorium.

Lessons for an exam answer:

- Effort and technology can keep catches high while the stock collapses (link to catch per unit effort).
- Scientific uncertainty was resolved in favour of short-term catches.
- Removing a dominant predator changed the ecosystem, which may itself slow recovery.
- Social and economic costs of collapse were far greater than the cost of earlier, smaller cuts.

## Rights-based and ecosystem-based management

Standard rules (quotas, mesh sizes, closed seasons, protected areas) are covered in the Topic 4 guide. Two further approaches:

- **Individual transferable quotas** (ITQs, or catch shares): the total allowable catch is divided into shares owned by fishers, who can trade them. Because a share is worth more when the stock is healthy, owners have an incentive to support conservation, and the race to fish ends. Critics point out that shares tend to concentrate in large companies, pushing out small-scale fishers.
- **Ecosystem-based fisheries management** sets catches for a species with its predators, prey and habitat in mind, rather than one species at a time.
- **Certification** schemes such as the Marine Stewardship Council (MSC) label wild fisheries that meet sustainability standards, letting consumers and retailers reward good practice.

## Lower-impact aquaculture

Aquaculture can be made more sustainable by changing *what* is farmed and *how*:

- **Lower trophic levels**: farming seaweed, mussels and oysters needs no feed, and filter feeders can remove excess nutrients from the water.
- **Integrated multi-trophic aquaculture** (IMTA): fish are farmed alongside seaweed and shellfish, which take up the fish's dissolved and particulate waste. One species' output becomes another's input, as in a natural ecosystem.
- **Recirculating aquaculture systems**: fish are raised in tanks on land where water is filtered and reused, preventing escapes and pollution, at a high energy cost.
- **Feed changes**: replacing fishmeal with plant proteins, algae or insect meal reduces pressure on wild forage fish.

## Model answer: explain

**Question.** Explain why a fishery can collapse even though annual catches remained steady for several years before the collapse. **[4]**

**Answer.** As the stock declined, fleets increased their effort, using more boats, more time at sea or better technology such as sonar [1]. This kept total catches steady even though each unit of effort caught fewer fish, so catch per unit effort was falling [1]. Managers who relied on catch figures did not see the decline, so quotas stayed too high [1]. Catches above the surplus yield reduced the stock further each year until too few adults remained to breed, and the stock collapsed [1].

## Common mistakes

- Stating that MSY is "the most fish you can catch". It is the largest catch that can be sustained indefinitely, at a specific stock size.
- Assuming that a stock below half of K can sustain an MSY catch.
- Writing that aquaculture always reduces pressure on wild fish; feed for carnivorous species may come from wild catches.
- Describing the cod collapse without the role of effort, technology and uncertain estimates.
- Recommending ITQs without considering who ends up owning the shares.

## Exam technique

- **Calculate**: substitute into the surplus-yield expression step by step and state the units (t per year).
- **Explain**: link each human action to its effect on stock size, surplus or food web.
- **Evaluate** a management method: effectiveness, enforcement, social effects, judgement.
- **To what extent**: in essays on aquaculture as a solution, weigh feed, pollution, habitat and disease against reduced wild harvest and food security, and conclude.
