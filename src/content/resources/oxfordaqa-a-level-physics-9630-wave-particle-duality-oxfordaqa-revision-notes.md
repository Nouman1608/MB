These notes condense section **3.5.11 Wave particle duality** of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Physics (9630) specification: electron diffraction as evidence that "particles possess wave properties", the photoelectric effect as evidence that "electromagnetic waves have a particulate nature", the de Broglie equation λ = h/mv, how and why diffraction changes with momentum, and how understanding of matter "changes over time". Details of particular particle diffraction methods are not expected.

For explanations and worked examples, read the [wave-particle duality study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-physics-9630-wave-particle-duality-oxfordaqa/). Test yourself with the [wave-particle duality practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-physics-9630-wave-particle-duality-oxfordaqa-practice/).

Data: h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J s; e = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C; electron mass 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ kg; proton and neutron mass 1.67 × 10⁻²⁷ kg.

## Core recall

- Electron diffraction shows that particles have wave properties.
- The photoelectric effect shows that electromagnetic radiation has a particle nature.
- Both light and matter show wave or particle behaviour depending on the experiment.
- de Broglie: λ = h/mv = h/p.
- Diffraction is noticeable when λ is comparable to the gap or the atomic spacing.
- Greater momentum: shorter λ, less diffraction, smaller angles and rings.
- Everyday objects have wavelengths far too small for any diffraction to be seen.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Wave-particle duality | The idea that both matter and electromagnetic radiation show wave and particle properties |
| de Broglie wavelength | The wavelength associated with a moving particle, λ = h/p |
| Momentum p | Mass × velocity, in kg m s⁻¹ |
| Electron diffraction | Spreading of an electron beam by the regular atomic layers in a thin crystalline sample, giving maxima and minima |
| Photon | A quantum of electromagnetic energy, showing the particle nature of radiation |
| Model | A scientific description that explains evidence and makes testable predictions |

## Equations

| Equation | Use |
|---|---|
| λ = h/mv | Wavelength from mass and speed |
| Ek = eV | Energy of an electron accelerated from rest through p.d. V |
| p = √(2mEk) | Momentum from kinetic energy |
| λ = h/√(2meV) | Wavelength of an accelerated electron |

Scaling rules: λ ∝ 1/v at fixed mass; λ ∝ 1/√V for accelerated electrons; at equal kinetic energy λ ∝ 1/√m.

## Method: wavelength of an accelerated electron

1. Ek = eV (convert to joules).
2. p = √(2mEk).
3. λ = h/p.
4. Compare λ with the atomic spacing (about 10⁻¹⁰ m) to judge whether diffraction will be noticeable.

## Momentum and diffraction: the chain

Momentum up → λ = h/p down → λ smaller compared with the spacing → less diffraction → smaller angles, smaller rings.

## Changing ideas: the outline

| Period | Idea | Evidence or reason |
|---|---|---|
| 17th century | Newton: particles of light; Huygens: waves | Competing explanations of reflection and refraction |
| Early 1800s | Wave model of light | Young's double-slit interference |
| 1860s | Light is an electromagnetic wave | Maxwell's theory |
| 1900 and 1905 | Quanta of energy; photons | Planck; Einstein's explanation of the photoelectric effect |
| 1924 | Particles have a wavelength | de Broglie's hypothesis |
| 1927 | Electron diffraction observed | Davisson and Germer; G. P. Thomson |

Models are kept while they explain the evidence and revised when new evidence contradicts them; new ideas are accepted after other scientists repeat and confirm the results.

## Quick reminders with numbers

- Electron through 150 V: λ = 1.00 × 10⁻¹⁰ m, similar to atomic spacing.
- Electron through 600 V: λ = 5.0 × 10⁻¹¹ m (half, since V is four times larger).
- Proton with the same kinetic energy as an electron: λ is 42.8 times smaller.
- Neutron at 2200 m s⁻¹: λ = 1.8 × 10⁻¹⁰ m.

## Exam traps

- The photoelectric effect is evidence for photons, not for electron waves.
- Use momentum, not kinetic energy, in λ = h/p.
- The p.d. is not the momentum: go through Ek = eV first.
- More momentum means less diffraction, not more.
- Do not describe a particular diffraction apparatus in detail; it is not expected.
- A heavier particle does not always have a shorter wavelength: compare momenta.

## Self-test

1. What does electron diffraction suggest about particles?
2. What does the photoelectric effect suggest about electromagnetic waves?
3. Find the de Broglie wavelength of an electron moving at 2.2 × 10⁷ m s⁻¹.
4. An electron's speed is halved. What happens to its wavelength?
5. An electron is accelerated through 50 V. Find its wavelength.
6. The accelerating voltage is increased by a factor of 9. By what factor does λ change?
7. Why does a beam of electrons through a crystal produce a clear pattern, while a beam of tennis balls through a window does not?
8. Explain what happens to an electron diffraction pattern when the electrons' momentum is increased.
9. An electron and a proton have the same speed. Which has the longer wavelength, and by roughly what factor?
10. Who proposed that particles have a wavelength, and when?
11. Give one reason why scientific models of light changed over time.
12. A neutron and an electron have the same de Broglie wavelength. What else must they have in common?

### Answers

1. Particles have **wave properties**.
2. They have a **particulate nature** (photons).
3. λ = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴/(9.11 × 10⁻³¹ × 2.2 × 10⁷) = **3.3 × 10⁻¹¹ m**.
4. It **doubles**.
5. p = √(2 × 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 50) = 3.82 × 10⁻²⁴ kg m s⁻¹, so λ = **1.7 × 10⁻¹⁰ m**.
6. λ falls by a factor of **3** (√9).
7. The electrons' wavelength is comparable to the **atomic spacing**; the balls' wavelength is far smaller than any gap.
8. λ decreases, so there is **less diffraction**: the rings or maxima move closer to the centre.
9. The **electron**, by about **1800** times (the mass ratio).
10. **de Broglie, in 1924**.
11. **New experimental evidence**, for example interference supporting waves, or the photoelectric effect requiring photons.
12. The same **momentum**.
