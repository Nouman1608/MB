## 3.3.1 Production processes

| Method | Definition | Use when | Watch for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Job | One item made to order | Unique or made-to-measure goods | High cost per item, skilled labour |
| Batch | Identical items made in groups, one group after another | Several variants in set quantities | Idle time between batches, stock build-up |
| Flow | Continuous production of a standard product | Very high demand for identical goods | Expensive set-up, inflexible |
| Cell | Small teams each making a whole part or product | Medium volume, need for flexibility and teamwork | Needs multi-skilled staff |

Method to choose a method: ask how many items, how similar they are, how much customers want to specify, and how big the budget for equipment is.

### Efficiency

- Efficiency: more output from the resources used, or equal output from fewer resources, so unit cost falls.
- JIT: little or no stock, materials arrive when needed.
- Kaizen: continuous improvement, meaning many small changes put forward by employees at every level.

## 3.3.2 The role of procurement

### JIT versus buffer stock

| | Benefits | Costs and risks |
|---|---|---|
| JIT | Lower storage and holding costs, less cash tied up, less waste and damage | More frequent deliveries, lost bulk-buying discounts, production stops if a delivery is late |
| Buffer stock | Spare stock to meet unexpected demand or cover late deliveries | Storage, insurance, risk of damage or going out of date, cash tied up |

Evaluate JIT for a given business: weigh the savings against extra delivery cost and lost economies of scale, then check reliability of suppliers and how predictable demand is.

### Choosing a supplier

| Factor | Question to ask |
|---|---|
| Price | What is the total cost once delivery, discounts and credit terms are included? |
| Quality | Are materials good enough to avoid faults and returns? |
| Reliability | Does it deliver the right goods on time, every time? |

Formula reminder (standard form): cost per usable unit = price / (1 - proportion unusable).

Worked reminder. Price 3.60 with 10% unusable: 3.60 / 0.90 = 4.00 per usable unit. A rival at 3.90 with 2% unusable: 3.90 / 0.98 = 3.98. The dearer-looking price is cheaper per usable unit, by about 0.02.

## 3.3.3 The concept of quality

- Quality means meeting customer expectations, which differ for goods (durability, safety, performance) and services (accuracy, speed, courtesy, consistency).
- Identify problems by inspection, complaints, returns, reviews, surveys and staff reports.
- Measure by percentage defective, return rate, complaints, repeat purchase and satisfaction scores.
- Consequences of poor quality: refunds and replacements, lost sales, damaged reputation, lost loyalty, low staff morale, possible legal problems.

### TQM

Total quality management: every employee in every department is responsible for quality, and faults are prevented at each stage instead of only being found at the end.

| Advantages to a business | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Fewer defects and less waste | Training and time to introduce |
| Lower cost of rework and returns | Needs commitment from managers |
| Greater customer loyalty and reputation | Change of habits can meet resistance |
| Staff feel involved | Benefits can take time to appear |

### Costs and benefits of quality

| Costs | Benefits |
|---|---|
| Better materials, training, equipment, checking | Fewer returns, stronger reputation, loyalty, possibly higher prices |

### Quality as the business grows

- Managers are further from daily work, so standards can slip.
- Outsourcing: less direct control over the other firm's standards; the business still gets the blame.
- Franchising: one weak outlet can damage the brand; the franchisor needs standards, training and inspections.

Worked reminder. A quality scheme costs 2,000 and cuts returns from 500 to 150 items, each return costing 9. Saving = 350 x 9 = 3,150. Net gain = 3,150 - 2,000 = 1,150.

## 3.3.4 Good customer services

| Method | Example |
|---|---|
| Product knowledge | Staff who can advise which model suits a customer's needs |
| Customer engagement | Friendly greeting, listening, quick problem handling |
| Post-sales services | User training, help lines, servicing |

| Benefits of good service | Dangers of poor service |
|---|---|
| Customer satisfaction and loyalty, repeat custom, good word of mouth | Dissatisfied customers, poor reputation, lost sales |

Good service has costs too: training, staff time and systems.

### ICT and customer service

| Tool | Role |
|---|---|
| Websites | Information, FAQs, order tracking, live chat at any hour |
| E-commerce | Buying online at any time; wider market; needs delivery and returns to work |
| Social media | Fast two-way contact and feedback; replies are public |

## Applying the topic to a case study

Short answers earn credit for knowing a term. Longer answers need the term applied to the business in the stem, with a consequence and, for evaluation, a judgement.

### Chain for analysis

Name the point, say what it does in this business, then say what that means for cost, sales, reputation or customers. For example: batch production lets a bakery change recipe between groups, so it can offer variety, but oven time is lost between batches, which raises cost per loaf.

### Chain for evaluation

State the case for, state the case against, use a figure or fact from the stem, then decide and give the condition on which your decision depends. Useful conditions in this topic include how reliable suppliers are, how predictable demand is, how much customers pay for quality, and how large the business is.

### Linking the four parts

- Production method affects quality: flow production gives consistency, job production allows personal finish but varies more.
- JIT needs reliable suppliers and high quality inputs, because there is no spare stock to cover faults.
- Good customer service can offset a small quality problem, but cannot replace quality in the long run.
- Growth through outsourcing or franchising makes TQM and clear standards more important.
- Websites and social media create more chances to show good service and also more chances for complaints to be seen.

## Must-know distinctions

- Job versus batch: job is one item to a customer's order, batch is a group of identical items.
- Flow versus cell: flow is a continuous line for one standard product, cell is a team making a whole piece of work.
- JIT versus buffer stock: JIT holds as little as possible, buffer stock is spare on purpose.
- TQM versus inspection: TQM prevents faults at every stage, inspection finds them after.
- Outsourcing versus franchising: outsourcing gives work to another firm, franchising lets others run outlets under your brand.

## Quick self-test

1. Name the production method suited to making a one-off wedding dress for a customer.
2. Give one cost of using JIT.
3. Define buffer stock.
4. Price 5.00 with 20% unusable. Calculate the cost per usable unit.
5. State two factors, other than price, in choosing a supplier.
6. What does TQM stand for?
7. Give two ways a business might measure quality.
8. Why does franchising put quality at risk?
9. Give one post-sales service.
10. State one way social media helps customer service.
11. What does Kaizen mean in practice?
12. Which production method is best for a very high volume of one standard product?

### Answers

1. Job production.
2. More frequent deliveries cost more, or bulk-buying discounts are lost (also: production stops if a delivery is late).
3. Spare stock kept to meet unexpected demand or cover late delivery.
4. 5.00 / 0.80 = **6.25** per usable unit.
5. Quality and reliability.
6. Total quality management.
7. Any two from: percentage defective, return rate, number of complaints, repeat purchases, satisfaction scores.
8. Independent owners run outlets under the brand, so a weak outlet may damage the brand and the franchisor has less direct control.
9. Any one of: user training, help line, servicing.
10. It gives fast two-way contact so queries can be answered and feedback gathered.
11. Everyone looks for small, regular improvements.
12. Flow production.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Defining a production method without linking it to the business in the stem.
- Giving only the benefits of JIT and forgetting the delivery cost and lost economies of scale.
- Picking a supplier on price alone.
- Confusing buffer stock with JIT.
- Writing that TQM is a final check, not a whole-business approach.
- Listing customer service methods without a consequence such as loyalty or reputation.
- Quoting figures without a unit or without a judgement at the end of an evaluation.
- Ignoring the stem: naming outsourcing or franchising risks when the business uses neither.
