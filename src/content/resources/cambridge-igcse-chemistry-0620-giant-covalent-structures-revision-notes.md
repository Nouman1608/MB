Condensed recall for **2.6 Giant covalent structures**, Cambridge IGCSE Chemistry 0620. For
full explanations and model answers, use the
[Giant Covalent Structures study guide](/resources/cambridge-igcse-chemistry-0620-giant-covalent-structures/).

> **Tier note:** silicon(IV) oxide and its comparison with diamond are *(Supplement)*
> content for Extended candidates only.

## The big idea

Giant covalent = **many atoms joined by strong covalent bonds in a continuous network**.
No separate molecules → no intermolecular forces to overcome → melting means **breaking
covalent bonds** → **very high melting point**.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Giant covalent structure | Huge network of atoms joined by covalent bonds throughout the solid |
| Tetrahedral | Four bonds pointing to the corners of a tetrahedron around an atom |
| Layer | Flat sheet of hexagonal rings of carbon atoms in graphite |
| Delocalised electron | Electron not fixed to one atom or bond; free to move through the structure |
| Weak forces between layers | Attractions holding graphite's layers together, much weaker than covalent bonds |
| Inert electrode | Electrode that conducts but does not react during electrolysis (graphite or platinum) |

## Diamond in one chain

4 covalent bonds per C, tetrahedral, rigid 3-D network
→ **very hard**, very high melting point
→ all outer electrons in bonds → **no delocalised electrons** → does not conduct
→ use: **cutting tools** (because hard)

## Graphite in one chain

3 covalent bonds per C, hexagonal **layers**
→ **weak forces between layers** → layers slide → soft, slippery → use: **lubricant**
→ 4th electron **delocalised** → conducts → use: **electrode** (also very high melting
point and unreactive)
→ strong covalent bonds within layers → very high melting point

## Silicon(IV) oxide (Supplement)

- Each **Si bonded to 4 O**; each **O bonded to 2 Si**; tetrahedral; giant 3-D network.
- Ratio Si : O = 1 : 2 → SiO₂ (a ratio, not a molecule).
- **Like diamond:** very hard, very high melting and boiling points, does not conduct,
  insoluble in water — because both are 3-D giant covalent networks with no delocalised
  electrons.
- **Unlike CO₂:** CO₂ is simple molecular (weak intermolecular forces, gas at room
  temperature).

## Property grid

| | Diamond | Graphite | SiO₂ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hard? | Very | No — soft | Very |
| Melting point | Very high | Very high | Very high |
| Conducts electricity? | No | Yes | No |
| Reason for conductivity result | No free electrons | Delocalised electrons | No free electrons or ions |

## Three-link answers for the syllabus uses

Each use answer needs **structure → property → use**:

| Use | Structural feature | Property |
|---|---|---|
| Diamond in cutting tools | Each C bonded to four others in a rigid 3-D network of strong covalent bonds | Very hard |
| Graphite as a lubricant | Layers held by weak forces | Layers slide; soft and slippery |
| Graphite as an electrode | One delocalised electron per C | Conducts electricity (plus very high melting point, unreactive) |

## Identifying a giant covalent substance from data

| Observation | Points to |
|---|---|
| Very high melting point, does not conduct solid or molten, insoluble | Giant covalent (diamond-type or SiO₂) |
| Very high melting point, conducts as a solid, soft, non-metal | Graphite |
| High melting point, conducts only when molten or aqueous | Ionic |
| Low melting point, does not conduct | Simple molecular |

## Exam traps

- Graphite's layers slide because of **weak forces between layers**, not weak covalent
  bonds.
- Graphite conducts by **delocalised electrons**; never write "ions" or "free protons".
- Diamond's cutting use is due to **hardness**; do not write "because it has a high melting
  point" as the main reason.
- Graphite's electrode use needs **conductivity** first; high melting point and low
  reactivity are supporting points.
- A soft substance can still have a very high melting point: graphite.
- Silicon and carbon are in the same group, but SiO₂ and CO₂ have completely different
  structures — do not transfer CO₂'s properties.
- Questions saying "relate to structure" want the structural feature named, not just the
  property.

## Self-test

1. How many covalent bonds does each carbon atom form in diamond?
2. How many covalent bonds does each carbon atom form in graphite?
3. Why does graphite conduct electricity?
4. Why does diamond not conduct electricity?
5. Explain why graphite is slippery.
6. Give the syllabus use of diamond and the property it depends on.
7. Give two reasons graphite is suitable for electrodes.
8. Why do both diamond and graphite have very high melting points?
9. *(Supplement)* Describe the bonding around a silicon atom in SiO₂.
10. *(Supplement)* How many silicon atoms is each oxygen atom bonded to in SiO₂?
11. *(Supplement)* Give two properties shared by diamond and silicon(IV) oxide.
12. *(Supplement)* Why is SiO₂ a solid with a very high melting point while CO₂ is a gas?

**Answers**

1. Four.
2. Three.
3. Each carbon has one delocalised electron that can move through the structure, along the
   layers.
4. All four outer electrons of each carbon are in covalent bonds; no delocalised electrons.
5. Its layers are held by weak forces, so they slide over each other.
6. Cutting tools; diamond is very hard.
7. It conducts electricity; it has a very high melting point and is unreactive (inert).
8. Many strong covalent bonds must be broken in a giant structure.
9. It forms four covalent bonds to four oxygen atoms, arranged tetrahedrally.
10. Two.
11. Any two: very hard; very high melting point; do not conduct electricity; insoluble in
    water.
12. SiO₂ is a giant covalent network, so melting breaks strong covalent bonds; CO₂ is
    made of simple molecules with weak intermolecular forces that need little energy to
    overcome.
