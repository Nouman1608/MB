**HL only.** These questions practise the Paper 3 regional study on independence movements in the Americas, 1763 to 1860. Choose one country and answer from its evidence. Revise first with the [study guide](/resources/ib-dp-history-2028-independence-movements/) and the [revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-history-2028-independence-movements-revision-notes/).

## Questions

**1.** Explain two actions of a colonial power that led to the emergence of an independence movement in one country. **[4]**

**2.** Read the extract (constructed for practice).

> "We are neither Europeans nor Indians, but a species midway between the legitimate owners of the country and the Spanish usurpers. We must dispute with the natives for titles of possession, and at the same time maintain ourselves against the invaders." (Constructed for practice: a paraphrase in the spirit of a creole leader's reflections in 1815.)

**(a)** State two difficulties the author identifies for the people he describes. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain how this extract could be used to understand why creole elites supported independence, and one limitation of using it alone. **[4]**

**3.** Explain how the French alliance (1778) helped the American cause. **[3]**

**4.** Analyse the contribution of one military leader to the success of an independence movement. **[6]**

**5.** Explain the reasons for the failure of the first Venezuelan republic (1811 to 1812) and the success of the later campaigns. **[6]**

**6.** Examine the problems faced by one new nation state in the 20 years after independence. **[8]**

**7.** Discuss the experiences of marginalised groups after independence in one country. **[8]**

**8.** To what extent did the Monroe Doctrine (1823) change relations between the United States and independent states in Latin America? **[8]**

**9.** Paper 3 style essay question: "Enlightenment ideas were the main cause of the emergence of independence movements in the Americas." To what extent do you agree, with reference to one country? **[15]**

## Answers

**1.** Two developed points, 2 marks each (1 for the action, 1 for how it led to a movement). United States: Parliament passed the Stamp Act (1765) and later taxes to pay for the Seven Years' War [1], which colonists opposed as taxation without representation, leading to protests and boycotts [1]; the Coercive Acts (1774) closed Boston harbour and reduced self-government in Massachusetts [1], which united other colonies in the First Continental Congress [1]. Spanish America: the Bourbon reforms raised taxes and favoured Spanish-born officials [1], which angered criollo elites [1]. Haiti: French trade restrictions and racial laws [1] bred resentment among planters, free people of colour and enslaved people [1]. **[4]**

**2. (a)** Any two: they are neither Europeans nor Indigenous, so their status is unclear [1]; they must claim rights against both the Indigenous population and the Spanish [1]; they are fighting a European power while seeking recognition as native-born [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Use: it shows that creoles felt excluded from power by Spanish officials though they were born in the Americas [1], and supports the view that status and political exclusion were reasons for supporting independence [1]. Limitation: it speaks only for the creole elite and says nothing of Indigenous people, enslaved people or the poor [1], and it is a reflection written to persuade a foreign audience, so it may present the case in a favourable way [1]. **[4]**

**3.** France provided loans, supplies and weapons [1], troops and a naval force [1] that were decisive at Yorktown in 1781, where the French fleet blocked British escape and French soldiers joined the siege [1]. **[3]**

**4.** Choose any leader, for example Washington. Identification of the leader's contribution [1]. Analysis: kept the army together through hardship such as Valley Forge [1]; avoided a decisive defeat and won at crucial points [1]; accepted civilian control, which kept the army aligned with Congress [1]; his reputation held the states together [1]. Judgement on how far the success depended on him rather than other factors such as French support [1]. Similar marks for Bolívar (Boyacá and Carabobo, crossing the Andes, llanero alliance, but dependent on Sucre and British volunteers) or Toussaint. **[6]**

**5.** Failure: the republic was divided between supporters and opponents among elites and the poor [1]; royalist forces led by Monteverde took advantage [1]; the 1812 earthquake was blamed by the clergy on the rebels [1]. Success: Bolívar built a stronger coalition including the llaneros and promised freedom to enslaved soldiers [1]; the Andean campaign to Boyacá (1819) surprised Spanish forces [1]; British volunteers and Spanish weakness at home helped [1]. **[6]**

**6.** Choose one state, for example Gran Colombia or the United States. Economic: war debt and damage [2]. Political: disputes about the shape of the state, such as federalism versus centralism, and leadership rivalries [2]. External: threats from former rulers or neighbours, for example Britain in the War of 1812 [2]. Judgement on which problem was most serious, with a reason [2]. **[8]**

**7.** Choose a country. Possible content for the United States: slavery continued and expanded in the South [2]; Indigenous nations lost land through treaties and removal, as in the 1830 Indian Removal Act [2]; free Black people faced legal restrictions [1]. Possible content for Haiti or Latin America: abolition occurred in some states, but forced labour, debt or indigenous tribute persisted [2]. Comparison of experiences and a judgement about continuity and change [1]. **[8]**

**8.** The doctrine warned Europe not to colonise or intervene [1]. Change: it set a principle that the Americas were not open to European colonisation, and later served as a basis for US claims [2]. Limits: the US lacked naval power in 1823, and the British navy enforced the idea in practice [2]; European interventions continued, for example in the Falkland Islands in 1833 [1]. Latin American reaction: some welcomed it, others were wary [1]. Judgement [1]. **[8]**

**9.** Use the Paper 3 markbands. A top answer (13 to 15 marks) shows in-depth understanding of the argument, evaluates a range of perspectives and reaches a substantiated judgement. Content for the United States (about 4 marks): Locke, natural rights, Paine, the Declaration. Competing causes (about 4 marks): taxation and the economic burden after 1763, colonial self-government, and trade disputes; the role of religion and local networks. Perspectives (about 3 marks): the ideological reading associated with Bailyn, the economic reading associated with Beard and others, and a view that events and decisions mattered more than any underlying cause. Judgement (about 4 marks): for example, that ideas provided the language and goals but the immediate causes were British policies, so ideas were necessary but not sufficient. **[15]**
