**These notes cover the poetry question on Paper 4 Unseen**, which the syllabus says "is based on a
poem or extract from a poem" and, like the prose question, requires "a critical commentary … and to
demonstrate an appreciation of the text". They condense the
[unseen poetry commentary study guide](/resources/cambridge-igcse-english-literature-0475-unseen-poetry-commentary/).
For the paper's format, route and timing, see the
[Paper 4 Unseen revision notes](/resources/igcse-english-literature-paper-4-unseen-revision-notes/).

## The task in brief

- One question from a choice of two: a poem (or poem extract) or a prose passage.
- A critical commentary on the printed text, guided by the question's focus.
- Around 20 minutes reading and planning, as the syllabus advises.
- 25 marks; AO1 to AO4 equally weighted.

## The four-pass routine

| Pass | Purpose | Output |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Literal sense | Speaker, addressee, situation, title, any printed note | Two sentences of plain understanding |
| 2. Question focus | Underline the key words; mark relevant lines | Lines starred for relevance |
| 3. Methods | Imagery, sound, structure, form | Margin notes on effects |
| 4. Shape and plan | How the poem moves; four or five points | A one-sentence answer and a plan |

## Key terms and their effects

| Term | Meaning | Typical effect to explain |
|---|---|---|
| Speaker / persona | The voice of the poem, not assumed to be the poet | Frames whose feelings we share |
| Volta (turn) | A shift in tone, time or argument | Marks the poem's central change |
| Enjambment | A sentence running over a line break | Suspense, momentum, a double meaning at the break |
| End-stopping | A line ending with punctuation | Finality, control, a pause |
| Caesura | A pause within a line | Hesitation, emphasis, a break in feeling |
| Minor sentence | A sentence without a main verb | Bluntness, listing, fragmented thought |
| Juxtaposition | Placing contrasting ideas side by side | Sharpens the difference |
| Semantic field | A group of related words | Builds atmosphere or a hidden theme |
| Present participle | A verb ending in "-ing" | Ongoing action, a sense of continuation |
| Couplet | A pair of lines, often closing a poem | Summary, twist or emphasis |
| Free verse | No regular rhyme or metre | Speech-like, flexible; still analyse line breaks |
| Rhyme / half-rhyme | Matching or nearly matching end sounds | Harmony, or unease when the match is imperfect |

## Tentative interpretation

- Use "suggests", "perhaps", "could imply" where the poem is open.
- Offer a second reading only when the words support it.
- Do not invent biography or events the poem does not give.
- Commit to a clear overall answer to the question.

## Structure of the commentary

1. **Introduction:** situation, one-sentence answer, the poem's movement.
2. **Body:** four or five paragraphs following the poem's stages.
3. **Form and structure:** woven in, or one paragraph on line, stanza and ending.
4. **Conclusion:** judgement on the question's key word.

## Analysing a line break

Ask three questions:

1. What word sits at the end of the line, and what does it mean on its own?
2. What does the next line add, confirm or overturn?
3. What does the reader feel during the pause?

Example (original): "I list the house as if a list / could keep it." On its own, "as if a list"
holds out a hope that the next line shows is impossible.

## Extracts from longer poems

Treat the extract's first and last lines as its opening and ending. Do not guess what comes before or
after.

## Exam traps

- **Paraphrasing each line.**
- **Feature-spotting** without effect.
- **Inventing a story** behind the poem.
- **Skipping the title and ending.**
- **One-word over-reading** that ignores the rest of the poem.
- **No plan**, leading to a shapeless commentary.
- **Losing the key word** ("movingly", "vividly") partway through.

## Self-test

1. What does a critical commentary on an unseen poem require, in the syllabus's words?
2. How long does the syllabus advise for reading and planning?
3. What should Pass 1 of the reading routine establish?
4. Why refer to "the speaker" rather than "the poet"?
5. Define a volta.
6. What is the difference between enjambment and end-stopping?
7. Name two effects of a caesura.
8. Why is tentative language useful in an unseen commentary, and what should it not become?
9. How should you treat an extract from a longer poem?
10. What is usually the clearest way to organise a poetry commentary?
11. In the original line "The gate swung shut. And then, again, it opened.", what do the caesura and
    the final word suggest?
12. Rewrite "There is alliteration in 'cold cracked stone'" as analysis.

**Answers:** 1. "a critical commentary" on the printed text and "to demonstrate an appreciation of
the text". 2. Around 20 minutes. 3. The speaker, addressee, situation, title and any printed note: the
literal sense. 4. The voice may be a persona, and the poem may not be autobiographical. 5. A turn or
shift in tone, time or argument. 6. Enjambment runs a sentence over a line break; end-stopping closes
the line with punctuation. 7. Any two: hesitation; emphasis on the words around it; a break in
feeling or thought; a change of direction. 8. It allows readings grounded in the words when the poem
is open; it should not become vague hedging that never commits to an answer. 9. Treat its first and
last lines as its opening and ending, without guessing at the rest. 10. Following the poem's
movement, stage by stage. 11. The pauses slow the moment and suggest hesitation; "opened" reverses the
closure, suggesting hope or an unexpected return. 12. For example: "The hard 'c' sounds of 'cold
cracked stone' make the line itself sound brittle, so the reader feels how harsh and unwelcoming the
place is."
