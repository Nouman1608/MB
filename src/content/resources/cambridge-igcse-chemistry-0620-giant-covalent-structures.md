This guide covers subtopic **2.6 Giant covalent structures** in Topic 2, Atoms, elements and
compounds, for Cambridge IGCSE Chemistry 0620 (2026–2028 series). The short overview in
[Ionic, Covalent and Metallic Bonding](/resources/ionic-covalent-and-metallic-bonding/)
introduces diamond and graphite; this page builds the full structure-to-property-to-use
chain that six-mark comparison questions demand.

## What the syllabus asks for

**Core**

- Describe the giant covalent structures of **graphite** and **diamond**.
- Relate their structures and bonding to their uses, **limited to**: graphite as a
  **lubricant** and as an **electrode**; diamond in **cutting tools**.

**Supplement** (Extended candidates only)

- Describe the giant covalent structure of **silicon(IV) oxide, SiO₂**.
- Describe the **similarity in properties** between diamond and silicon(IV) oxide, related
  to their structures.

The uses list is deliberately short. Questions will centre on these three uses, so learn
the specific structural reason behind each one.

## What "giant covalent" means

In a **giant covalent structure** (sometimes called a macromolecule or network), a very
large number of atoms are joined by **covalent bonds** in a continuous network that extends
throughout the solid. There are no separate molecules, so there are no weak intermolecular
forces to overcome when the solid melts. To melt it, **strong covalent bonds must be
broken**, and there are a great many of them. This is why giant covalent substances have
**very high melting and boiling points** — the opposite of the simple molecules in subtopic
2.5, even though both involve covalent bonds.

## Diamond

**Structure.** Diamond is pure carbon. Each carbon atom is covalently bonded to **four**
other carbon atoms, arranged **tetrahedrally** around it. This pattern repeats in three
dimensions to form a rigid giant network.

**Properties that follow:**

- **Very hard** — every atom is locked in place by four strong covalent bonds in all
  directions; scratching or breaking diamond means breaking many of them.
- **Very high melting point** — many strong covalent bonds must be broken.
- **Does not conduct electricity** — all four outer electrons of every carbon atom are used
  in bonds, so there are **no delocalised (free) electrons** and no ions.

**Use: cutting tools.** Diamond's **hardness** lets it cut, drill and grind other hard
materials, such as rock and glass, without being worn away. Tips of drill bits and saw
blades for stone are coated with diamond.

## Graphite

**Structure.** Graphite is also pure carbon. Each carbon atom is covalently bonded to
**three** others, forming flat **layers of hexagonal rings**. The layers are stacked on top
of one another and held together only by **weak forces**. Because each carbon uses only
three of its four outer electrons in bonding, the fourth electron becomes **delocalised**:
free to move along the layers.

**Properties that follow:**

- **Soft and slippery** — the weak forces between layers allow the **layers to slide over
  one another**.
- **Conducts electricity** — the delocalised electrons move through the structure and carry
  charge.
- **Very high melting point** — melting requires breaking the strong covalent bonds within
  the layers, not just separating the layers.

**Use 1: lubricant.** Because layers slide easily over each other, powdered graphite
reduces friction between moving parts. Its very high melting point means it still works in
hot conditions, such as some engine and lock parts, where an oil might break down.

**Use 2: electrode.** Graphite **conducts electricity** through its delocalised electrons.
It also has a **very high melting point** and is **unreactive** in most electrolysis cells,
which is why the syllabus describes carbon/graphite as an **inert electrode** in
electrolysis.

## Comparing the two forms of carbon

| Feature | Diamond | Graphite |
|---|---|---|
| Bonds per carbon atom | 4 | 3 |
| Arrangement | Tetrahedral, 3-D network | Flat hexagonal layers |
| Forces between layers | Not applicable | Weak |
| Delocalised electrons | None | One per carbon atom |
| Hardness | Very hard | Soft, slippery |
| Electrical conductivity | Does not conduct | Conducts |
| Melting point | Very high | Very high |
| Syllabus use | Cutting tools | Lubricant; electrode |

Both are the same element. Every difference in the table comes from the difference in
structure and bonding — the point an examiner wants you to make explicitly.

## Silicon(IV) oxide, SiO₂ (Supplement)

**Structure.** Silicon(IV) oxide, found as sand and quartz, is a giant covalent compound.
Each **silicon atom is covalently bonded to four oxygen atoms**, arranged tetrahedrally,
and each **oxygen atom is bonded to two silicon atoms**. The network extends in three
dimensions. Since every oxygen is shared between two silicon atoms, the overall ratio of
Si : O is **1 : 2**, which gives the formula SiO₂. As with ionic formulae, SiO₂ is a ratio,
not a molecule.

**Similarity to diamond.** Both have a **three-dimensional giant covalent network** in which
each silicon (or carbon) atom forms four bonds arranged tetrahedrally. So both:

- are **very hard**;
- have **very high melting and boiling points**;
- **do not conduct electricity**, because no electrons are delocalised and there are no
  ions;
- are **insoluble in water**.

**Contrast with carbon dioxide.** Carbon and silicon are both in Group IV, but CO₂ is a
**simple molecular** gas while SiO₂ is a **giant covalent** solid. Melting SiO₂ breaks
strong covalent bonds; turning solid CO₂ into a gas only overcomes weak intermolecular
forces.

## Worked model answers

**Question (Core):** *Explain why graphite is used as a lubricant.* **[2]**

> Graphite has **layers** of carbon atoms held together by **weak forces** ✓. The layers
> can **slide over each other** ✓.

**Question (Supplement):** *Describe the similarity in properties between diamond and
silicon(IV) oxide and relate it to their structures.* **[3]**

> Both are **very hard / have very high melting points** ✓. Both are **giant covalent**
> structures with each atom joined by **strong covalent bonds** in a 3-D network ✓, so a
> **large amount of energy** is needed to break the many bonds ✓.

## Common mistakes

- **"Graphite's layers are held by covalent bonds."** The bonds *within* layers are
  covalent; *between* layers the forces are weak.
- **"Graphite conducts because of free ions."** It has delocalised electrons, not ions.
- **"Diamond conducts heat so it conducts electricity."** Learn the electrical property: no
  delocalised electrons, no conduction.
- **Saying graphite has a low melting point** because it is soft. Softness comes from
  sliding layers; melting needs covalent bonds broken.
- **Writing SiO₂ as simple molecules** or giving it CO₂'s properties.
- **Using a use not on the list** without explaining it, such as pencils; stick to
  lubricant, electrode and cutting tools.

## Exam technique: command words

- **Describe the structure** — number of bonds per atom, arrangement (tetrahedral or
  layers), giant network, and for graphite the delocalised electrons.
- **Relate structure to use** — a three-link chain: structural feature → property → use.
- **Explain** a property — name the bond or force, its strength, and the energy or particle
  movement that follows.
- **Compare** — make each point for both substances, ideally in the same sentence.

## Quick checklist

- Diamond: 4 bonds, tetrahedral, hard, no conduction, cutting tools
- Graphite: 3 bonds, layers, weak forces between layers, delocalised electrons, lubricant
  and electrode
- *(Supplement)* SiO₂: Si bonded to 4 O, O bonded to 2 Si, giant 3-D network
- *(Supplement)* diamond and SiO₂ share hardness, high melting point, no conduction
