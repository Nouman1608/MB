## Section A: comparison skills

Read Poem 1 and Poem 2, then answer Questions 1 to 8.

> **Poem 1: Ferry Crossing**
>
> She stands at the rail the whole way over,
> not looking back, not looking ahead,
> watching the water close behind the boat
> as if the sea were tidying up after her.
> On the far side a cousin waves a scarf.
> She lifts one hand, and keeps the other
> on the bag that holds her mother's plates.

> **Poem 2: Address**
>
> I learned this street the way you learn a song:
> the bakery first, the barber's pole, the bend
> where Mr Haddad's roses grew too long
> and caught your sleeve, as if to make a friend.
>
> They've changed the name. The roses are a wall.
> The barber's is a shop that sells new phones.
> I walk it anyway, and know it all,
> the way a body still remembers bones.

**1.** Identify **one** idea that both poems share. **[1]**

**2.** In Poem 1, explain the effect of the simile "as if the sea were tidying up after her". **[3]**

**3.** In Poem 1, what does the final image of the bag suggest about the woman's feelings? **[3]**

**4.** In Poem 2, how does the simile in the first line present the speaker's relationship with the
street? **[3]**

**5.** Poem 1 is written in free verse; Poem 2 uses rhymed quatrains. Explain how each form suits its
poem. **[4]**

**6.** Compare how the endings of the two poems present the relationship between the past and the
present. **[4]**

**7.** Write **one** comparative topic sentence about the two poems' attitudes to change, using a
comparative connective. **[2]**

**8.** A student writes: *"Poem 1 uses enjambment and Poem 2 uses rhyme."* Explain why this would
gain little credit, and rewrite it so that it would gain more. **[3]**

## Section B: exam-style questions

Answer each with two poems you have studied from *People and Places*. Allow about 45 minutes per
question.

**9.** Compare how poets present the influence of a place on a person's sense of identity in **two**
poems from *People and Places*. **[30]**

**10.** Compare how poets present loss or absence in **two** poems from *People and Places*. **[30]**

**11.** Compare how poets use the voice of a speaker to present their ideas in **two** poems from
*People and Places*. **[30]**

## Answers

### Section A

**1.** One mark for a valid shared idea: leaving or losing a place and what a person keeps from
it [1]. Also credit: memory; change; belonging. **[1]**

**2.** The simile personifies the sea as someone clearing up after a guest [1], suggesting that the
place she leaves erases her presence quickly and calmly [1]; this may make her departure feel final,
or suggest that home carries on without her, which could explain why she is "not looking
back" [1]. **[3]**

**3.** She holds the bag protectively, showing that her mother's plates matter more than greeting
the cousin [1]. The plates suggest family, home and everyday meals carried into the new place [1].
Lifting only "one hand" suggests her feelings are divided between welcome and loss [1]. **[3]**

**4.** Learning a street "the way you learn a song" suggests it became familiar through repetition
[1] and affection, as if it were something the speaker enjoyed [1]; songs are remembered without
effort, foreshadowing the ending where the speaker still "know[s] it all" [1]. **[3]**

**5.** Poem 1: free verse and flowing, enjambed lines suit a calm, observed journey, and the lack
of rhyme matches the woman's silence and uncertainty [1] [1]. Poem 2: regular rhymed quatrains give a
sense of order and memory, like the "song" of the first line [1], so the steady pattern continues
even when the street changes, mirroring how the speaker's memory stays fixed [1]. **[4]**

**6.** Poem 1 ends on the bag of plates, an object carried from the past into the present, so the
past is held onto physically and protected [1] [1]. Poem 2 ends with the simile "the way a body still
remembers bones", so the past survives inside the speaker even though the street has changed [1]; whereas
the woman in Poem 1 carries the past with her, the speaker in Poem 2 has absorbed it so deeply that
it cannot be removed [1]. **[4]**

**7.** One mark for a valid comparison and one for a correct connective [1] [1]. Example: "Whereas
the woman in Poem 1 meets change in silence, the speaker of Poem 2 resists it by walking the old
street 'anyway'." **[2]**

**8.** It only names features, without saying what they do [1] or comparing their effects [1].
Improved: "Poem 1's enjambment lets the journey flow on without pause, suggesting the woman cannot
stop the move, whereas Poem 2's rhyme creates a steady order that reflects the speaker's fixed memory
of the street." [1] **[3]**

### Section B

These questions are marked holistically against level descriptors rather than by adding up points.
The guidance below shows what a high-level answer does; marks depend on the poems chosen, and other
valid approaches should be credited.

**9. Place and identity.**

- **AO1:** a clear argument about *how* each place shapes identity: for example, one speaker defined
  by belonging, another by distance or exile. Context, such as migration or a rural upbringing, used
  only where it explains the presentation.
- **AO2:** analysis of methods in both poems, such as pronouns ("I", "we") showing whether identity
  is personal or shared; imagery that ties the body to the land; structural shifts showing identity
  changing.
- **AO3:** an integrated comparison with comparative connectives, accurate terminology and a
  conclusion that answers the question. **[30]**

**10. Loss or absence.**

- **AO1:** what is lost (a person, a home, a way of life) and each poem's attitude to it: grief,
  acceptance, anger or nostalgia.
- **AO2:** how absence is shown indirectly, for example through objects, empty spaces, silence or a
  tense change from past to present; how endings resolve or leave open the loss.
- **AO3:** an argued comparison: not "both are sad" but how differently each poet makes the reader
  feel the absence. **[30]**

**11. Voice of a speaker.**

- **AO1:** what each speaker believes or feels about people or places, and how far the reader trusts
  them.
- **AO2:** first, second or third person; dramatic monologue or lyric voice; direct address;
  questions and imperatives; tone shifts; whether the speaker reveals more than they intend.
- **AO3:** comparison of the *effect* of each voice on the reader, with precise terms such as persona
  and tone, and a clear overall judgement. **[30]**

**What separates the top answers:** a conceptual argument stated at the start, comparison inside
every paragraph, short embedded quotations, and form and structure analysed as well as language.

## How to use this page

- Do Section A in about 25 minutes, checking each answer against the points above.
- For Section B, write one full answer under timed conditions, then highlight AO1 points in one
  colour and AO2 points in another to check the balance.
- Use your theme grid to choose poems for each Section B question before writing.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why are the Section A poems not from the anthology?**
To practise comparison skills without reproducing copyright texts. Apply the same skills to your
studied poems in Section B.

**How long should a 30-mark answer be?**
Quality matters more than length; in about 35 minutes of writing, three or four developed
comparative paragraphs with an introduction and conclusion is typical.
