## Origins: causes to rank

- **Agricultural change:** higher yields, enclosure, labour released for industry. Treat as gradual.
- **Population growth:** labour supply and demand for cheap goods.
- **Resources:** coal for steam power; iron for machines, rails and ships.
- **Capital:** merchant profits, banks, later joint-stock companies; in late industrializers, state budgets and foreign loans.
- **Technology:** textile machinery and steam engines first, then steel, chemicals and electricity.
- **Markets:** domestic demand, colonies and overseas trade.
- **Government:** from enabling (property rights, patents, tariffs, infrastructure) to directing (Meiji Japan, Witte's Russia, Stalin's Five-Year Plans).

**Ranking tip:** decide whether a factor was *necessary* (industrialization could not happen without it) or *decisive* (it explains why it happened then and there).

## Development: stages and technologies

| Stage | Features | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Early (c.1750-1830s) | Textiles, water then steam power, canals | Arkwright's Cromford Mill (1771); Watt's engine (patented 1769) |
| Railway age (1830s-1870s) | Railways and iron, steam at sea, the telegraph | Liverpool and Manchester Railway (1830) |
| "Second" industrial revolution (c.1870-1914) | Steel, chemicals, electricity, oil; large firms | Germany and the USA overtaking Britain in steel output by about 1900 |
| Twentieth century | Mass production, state planning, later services and new technology | Ford's moving assembly line (1913); Soviet Five-Year Plans from 1928 |

## Comparative case summary

| | Britain | Germany | Meiji Japan | Russia / USSR |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Start | From the 1750s | Accelerated after 1871 | After 1868 | 1890s; again from 1928 |
| Driver | Private enterprise | Banks, cartels, state support | State, then *zaibatsu* | State and foreign capital; then the party-state |
| Leading sectors | Cotton, coal, iron | Steel, chemicals, electricals | Silk, cotton, then heavy industry | Railways, coal, steel |
| Political response | Reform Acts, Factory Acts | Social insurance (1883-89) to weaken the SPD | Oligarchic control; Meiji Constitution (1889) | Repression; 1905 Revolution |

## Impact: key evidence

- **Urbanization:** rapid growth of industrial towns; overcrowding, cholera, poor sanitation.
- **Public health:** Chadwick's report (1842) and the Public Health Act (1848) in Britain.
- **Work:** factory discipline, long hours, child labour; Factory Act 1833 (no children under nine in textile mills; inspectors); Ten Hours Act 1847.
- **Labour organizations:** Combination Acts repealed (1824); Tolpuddle Martyrs transported (1834); growth of skilled unions, then mass unions.
- **Political change:** Chartism (1838-48); new socialist and labour parties; state welfare.
- **Women:** paid factory work (especially textiles), but lower pay and legal limits on hours.
- **Environment:** Alkali Act 1863; London smog (1952) and the Clean Air Act (1956).

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Proto-industrialization | Rural, household-based production for distant markets before factories |
| Factory system | Production concentrated in one building, with powered machines and timed labour |
| Urbanization | Growth in the share of people living in towns |
| Laissez-faire | The idea that the state should not interfere in the economy |
| *Zaibatsu* | Large Japanese family-controlled business groups such as Mitsui and Mitsubishi |
| Cartel | Firms agreeing to fix prices or output; common in German heavy industry |
| Standard of living debate | Argument over whether early industrialization made workers better or worse off |
| Economic backwardness thesis | Gerschenkron's view that late industrializers relied more on banks and the state |
| Second industrial revolution | The phase after about 1870 based on steel, chemicals, electricity and oil |

## Historians' perspectives to use accurately

- **Robert Allen:** high wages and cheap coal made mechanization profitable in Britain.
- **Kenneth Pomeranz:** coal and the resources of the Americas explain why Britain diverged from advanced regions of China.
- **E. P. Thompson:** a "pessimist" on working-class experience in early industrial England.
- **Alexander Gerschenkron:** the state and banks substitute for missing prerequisites in late industrializers.

## Exam traps

- **Answering on "impact" with only social effects.** Political and environmental effects are part of the topic; one paragraph on each strengthens range.
- **Assuming a revolution happened overnight.** Show change over decades.
- **Same-region pairs.** Britain and Germany are both in Europe; check whether the question requires different regions.
- **Forgetting the end date.** The topic runs to 2005, so later cases such as state-led growth in the twentieth century are valid if studied.
- **Unsupported judgement.** Each paragraph needs a mini-judgement that links back to the question.

## Self-test

1. Name four causes of industrialization. *Any four from agricultural change, population, resources, capital, technology, labour, markets, government.*
2. When was the Zollverein formed? *1834.*
3. Which invention is linked to Watt and what did it improve? *The separate condenser, which made steam engines far more fuel-efficient.*
4. Who led Russian industrialization in the 1890s? *Sergei Witte, as finance minister.*
5. What were *zaibatsu*? *Large family-controlled Japanese business groups.*
6. What did the Ten Hours Act of 1847 do? *Limited the working hours of women and young people in textile mills.*
7. What were Bismarck's three social insurance laws and dates? *Sickness (1883), accident (1884), old age and disability (1889).*
8. Which report led to the Public Health Act of 1848? *Chadwick's 1842 report on the sanitary condition of the labouring population.*
9. What is the difference between "necessary" and "decisive" causes? *Necessary causes had to be present; decisive causes explain why industrialization happened when and where it did.*
10. What was the argument of Pomeranz's *The Great Divergence*? *That coal and the resources of the Americas, not a unique European culture, explain Britain's breakthrough.*
11. Give one environmental law and its trigger. *The Clean Air Act (1956), after the London smog of 1952.*
12. Why is Britain and Japan a strong comparative pair? *They are in different regions and show contrasting roles of resources and the state.*
