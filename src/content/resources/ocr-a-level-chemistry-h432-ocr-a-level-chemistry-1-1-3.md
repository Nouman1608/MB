This guide goes deeper into **1.1.3 Analysis** in OCR A Level Chemistry A (H432), Version 3.1 (May 2026), for first assessment in 2017. Analysis skills are assessed in the written papers, and they are where much of the mathematics in a practical question sits.

The [practical skills in the written examination guide](/resources/ocr-a-level-chemistry-practical-skills-written-examination/) covers 1.1.1 to 1.1.4 together, including an Arrhenius gradient and intercept. This page covers analysis alone, with different examples: choosing which data to process, reaching a conclusion the data can support, applying the significant figure rules from M1.1, setting up graph axes, drawing tangents, reading half-lives and using a calibration curve. Condense it with the [1.1.3 revision notes](/resources/ocr-a-level-chemistry-h432-ocr-a-level-chemistry-1-1-3-revision-notes/) and test yourself with the [1.1.3 practice questions](/resources/ocr-a-level-chemistry-h432-ocr-a-level-chemistry-1-1-3-practice/).

## 1.1.3(a): processing and interpreting

### Choosing the data to process

Processing starts with deciding what to include. For titrations, M1.2 says you should be able to "select appropriate titration data (i.e. identification of outliers) in order to calculate mean titres". Leave out the rough titre and any titre that does not agree with the others; average only the concordant ones.

Other processing turns raw readings into the quantity a relationship needs: 1/time as a measure of rate, ln k against 1/T for the Arrhenius equation, or a percentage from two masses.

### Valid conclusions

A conclusion is valid when it answers the question asked and the data support it.

- **Stay within the data.** A rate measured between 20 °C and 50 °C says nothing certain about 90 °C.
- **Correlation is not causation.** If two variables rise together, one does not necessarily cause the other: another variable may change with both.
- **Qualitative results limit what you can say.** A test that shows "no halide present" does not identify which anion is present.
- **Quote evidence.** "The rate doubles when [A] doubles" is supported by numbers; "the rate goes up" is not specific enough to show the order.

**Worked example 1: what can be concluded?** A student adds bromine water to three liquids: X decolourises it, Y and Z do not. Y gives a silver mirror with Tollens' reagent; Z does not.

- X is likely to contain a C=C bond, since alkenes decolourise bromine water. Phenol also decolourises it, so the test alone does not prove an alkene.
- Y is an aldehyde: a silver mirror with Tollens' reagent is the test for an aldehyde group.
- Z is neither an alkene nor an aldehyde. That is all these tests show; it could be a ketone, an alcohol or something else.

## 1.1.3(b): mathematical skills

Section 5 of the specification lists the mathematical skills. In practical analysis the most common are: ratios and percentages, means, standard form, logarithms (for pH and ln k), changing the subject of an equation, and gradients and intercepts. Show each step, so method marks are available even if a later value slips.

## 1.1.3(c): significant figures

M1.1 says you should "report calculations to an appropriate number of significant figures given raw data quoted to varying numbers of significant figures" and "understand that calculated results can only be reported to the limits of the least accurate measurement".

**The rules.**

1. Count significant figures in each measurement used in the calculation.
2. Give the answer to the **smallest** number among them.
3. Keep full calculator values for intermediate steps; round only at the end.
4. Use standard form to show significant figures clearly: 2400 to 2 s.f. is 2.4 × 10³.

| Value | Significant figures |
|---|---|
| 0.0250 | 3 (leading zeros do not count; the trailing zero does) |
| 22.45 | 4 |
| 1.0 × 10⁻³ | 2 |
| 50.0 | 3 |

**Worked example 2.** A 25.0 cm³ sample of sodium hydroxide needs a mean titre of 22.45 cm³ of 0.100 mol dm⁻³ hydrochloric acid. Find the concentration of the sodium hydroxide.

```
n(HCl) = 0.100 × 22.45/1000 = 2.245 × 10⁻³ mol
n(NaOH) = 2.245 × 10⁻³ mol (1 : 1)
c(NaOH) = 2.245 × 10⁻³ ÷ 0.0250 = 0.0898 mol dm⁻³
```

The data have 3, 4 and 3 significant figures, so the answer is given to 3: **0.0898 mol dm⁻³**.

## 1.1.3(d)(i): axes and scales

- The **independent variable** goes on the x-axis.
- Label each axis with **quantity / unit**, such as "[I₂] / mol dm⁻³".
- Choose a scale so the points cover **more than half the grid** in each direction.
- Use easy steps: 1, 2 or 5 units per major division (or 10, 20, 50 …). Avoid 3 or 7, which make plotting and reading slow and error-prone.
- An axis need not start at zero unless you must read an intercept at zero or extrapolate to it.
- Plot points as small crosses, and draw a single best-fit line or smooth curve, ignoring anomalies.

## 1.1.3(d)(ii): gradients, tangents and intercepts

### Gradient of a straight line

Use a triangle drawn on the line that covers at least half the line's length. Read the two points from the line, not from data points.

### Tangent to a curve (M3.5)

On a concentration–time curve, the rate at a given time is the gradient of the tangent there.

**Worked example 3.** A tangent drawn to a curve of [I₂] against time at t = 0 passes through (0 s, 0.0080 mol dm⁻³) and (160 s, 0.0000 mol dm⁻³).

```
gradient = (0.0000 − 0.0080) ÷ (160 − 0) = −5.0 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹
initial rate = 5.0 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹
```

The gradient is negative because iodine is used up; the rate is quoted as a positive value. Draw the tangent long, so the two points you read are far apart.

### Half-life from a graph

On a concentration–time graph, read the time for the concentration to halve, then halve again.

**Worked example 4.** [A] falls from 0.080 to 0.040 mol dm⁻³ between 0 s and 45 s, and from 0.040 to 0.020 mol dm⁻³ between 45 s and 90 s. Both half-lives are 45 s. A constant half-life shows the reaction is first order with respect to A.

### Intercepts

If the x-axis starts at zero, read the y-intercept directly. If it does not, calculate it: find the gradient m, take a point (x, y) on the line, and use c = y − mx.

### Calibration curves (M3.2)

A calibration curve is plotted from standards of known concentration and used to find an unknown.

**Worked example 5.** Standard copper(II) solutions give these absorbances in a colorimeter:

| [Cu²⁺] / mol dm⁻³ | 0.010 | 0.020 | 0.030 | 0.040 | 0.050 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Absorbance | 0.12 | 0.24 | 0.36 | 0.48 | 0.60 |

The points lie on a straight line through the origin with gradient 0.60 ÷ 0.050 = 12 dm³ mol⁻¹. An unknown solution gives an absorbance of 0.42, so its concentration is 0.42 ÷ 12 = **0.035 mol dm⁻³**. Read the value from your drawn line, and use the curve only within the range of the standards.

## Common mistakes

- Including the rough titre, or a titre that is not concordant, in the mean.
- Concluding beyond the range of the data, or claiming cause from a correlation.
- Rounding intermediate values, then quoting too many figures at the end.
- Using awkward scales such as 3 units per division, or plotting on less than half the grid.
- Taking a gradient from two data points that are not on the line.
- Quoting a rate from a tangent as negative.
- Reading an intercept from an axis that does not start at zero.

## Exam technique

- **"Calculate"**: show each step, keep unrounded values, and give the final answer to the significant figures the data justify.
- **"Determine"** from a graph: draw the triangle or tangent on the graph and write the coordinates you used.
- **"Plot"**: label axes with quantity and unit, choose a sensible scale, and draw one best-fit line.
- **"What can you conclude"**: state the conclusion and quote the evidence; say what the data cannot show.
