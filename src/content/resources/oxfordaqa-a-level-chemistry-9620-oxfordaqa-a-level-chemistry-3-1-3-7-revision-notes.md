These notes condense **3.1.3.7 Forces between molecules** from the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Chemistry (9620) specification, Version 5.3. It is International AS content, examined in Unit 1. For explanations and worked examples, read the [forces between molecules study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-1-3-7/); for exam-style questions, use the [forces between molecules practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-1-3-7-practice/).

## Core ideas

- Three forces: **induced dipole–dipole (van der Waals)**, **permanent dipole–dipole**, **hydrogen bonding**.
- Melting and boiling of molecular substances overcome these forces; **covalent bonds inside molecules do not break**.
- Stronger forces → more energy needed → higher melting and boiling points.
- Hydrogen bonding explains the **low density of ice** and the **anomalous boiling points** of NH₃, H₂O and HF.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Temporary dipole | An uneven electron distribution at one instant, caused by electron movement |
| Induced dipole | A dipole created in a molecule by a neighbouring dipole |
| Van der Waals forces | Attractions between temporary and induced dipoles; act between all molecules |
| Permanent dipole–dipole force | Attraction between the δ+ end of one polar molecule and the δ− end of another |
| Hydrogen bond | Attraction between a δ+ H bonded to N, O or F and a lone pair on N, O or F of another molecule |
| Anomalous boiling point | A boiling point far above the trend for the group, caused by hydrogen bonding |

## The three forces compared

| | Van der Waals | Permanent dipole–dipole | Hydrogen bonding |
|---|---|---|---|
| Found between | All molecules | Polar molecules only | Molecules with H on N, O or F |
| Cause | Moving electrons create temporary dipoles that induce dipoles | Permanent bond dipoles that do not cancel | Very polar N–H, O–H or F–H, plus a lone pair on N, O or F |
| Increases with | Number of electrons; surface contact | Size of the permanent dipole | Number of hydrogen bonds per molecule |
| Relative strength | Weakest per interaction (but can be large for big molecules) | Intermediate | Strongest of the three |

All three are much weaker than covalent bonds.

## Useful data patterns

- Noble gases: boiling point rises He → Xe (more electrons, stronger van der Waals).
- C₅H₁₂ isomers: pentane 36 °C > 2-methylbutane 28 °C > 2,2-dimethylpropane 10 °C (branching reduces surface contact).
- Same electrons, different forces: ethane −89 °C < fluoromethane −78 °C < methanol 65 °C.
- Group 15 hydrides: NH₃ −33 °C is anomalously high; PH₃ −88 °C, AsH₃ −62 °C, SbH₃ −17 °C follow the van der Waals trend.
- Group 14 hydrides show no anomaly: C–H cannot hydrogen bond.

## Hydrogen bond checklist (for drawings)

- δ+ on the H; δ− on the N, O or F.
- A lone pair on the N, O or F of the second molecule.
- A dashed line from H to that lone pair.
- X–H···Y close to 180°.

## Ice

- Each H₂O forms **four** hydrogen bonds in ice (two via its H atoms, two via its lone pairs).
- This holds molecules in an **open** tetrahedral lattice with much empty space.
- On melting, some hydrogen bonds break and molecules pack closer, so **water is denser than ice**.

## Answer frame for boiling-point comparisons

1. List every force present in each substance.
2. Name the difference (electrons, shape, permanent dipole, hydrogen bonds).
3. State which is stronger and that more energy is needed to overcome it.

## Exam traps

- "Bonds break on boiling": say **intermolecular forces** are overcome.
- Forgetting that polar molecules also have van der Waals forces.
- Claiming hydrogen bonds in CH₃F, CH₃CHO or CH₃OCH₃: their H atoms are bonded to carbon.
- A hydrogen bond drawn to an atom rather than a lone pair.
- Explaining ice density without the **open lattice**.
- Comparing molecules with very different numbers of electrons and using only polarity.

## Self-test

1. Explain how an induced dipole–dipole force arises.
2. Between which molecules do van der Waals forces act?
3. Why does xenon boil at a higher temperature than argon?
4. Why does 2,2-dimethylpropane boil lower than pentane?
5. State the two conditions needed for a hydrogen bond.
6. Can CH₃OCH₃ molecules hydrogen bond to each other? Explain.
7. Propanone and butane have the same Mr. Why does propanone boil higher?
8. Why is the boiling point of NH₃ higher than that of PH₃?
9. How many hydrogen bonds does each water molecule form in ice?
10. Why is ice less dense than liquid water?
11. Which is the strongest of the three forces between molecules?
12. What is overcome when liquid methanol boils?

### Answers

1. Electron movement gives one molecule a temporary dipole, which induces a dipole in a neighbouring molecule; the two dipoles attract.
2. All molecules (and noble gas atoms).
3. Xenon atoms have more electrons, so stronger van der Waals forces, needing more energy to overcome.
4. Its compact, branched molecules have less surface contact, so weaker van der Waals forces.
5. H bonded to N, O or F; a lone pair on N, O or F of another molecule.
6. **No**: all its H atoms are bonded to carbon, so none is δ+ enough. (It can accept a hydrogen bond from water through its oxygen lone pairs.)
7. Propanone is polar (C=O), so it has permanent dipole–dipole forces as well as similar van der Waals forces.
8. NH₃ molecules form hydrogen bonds; PH₃ molecules do not.
9. Four.
10. Hydrogen bonds hold the molecules in an open lattice; on melting the lattice collapses and the molecules pack closer.
11. Hydrogen bonding.
12. Hydrogen bonds, permanent dipole–dipole forces and van der Waals forces between methanol molecules (not the covalent bonds).
