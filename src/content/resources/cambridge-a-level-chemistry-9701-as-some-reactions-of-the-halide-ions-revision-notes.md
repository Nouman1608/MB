These notes condense subtopic **11.3, Some reactions of the halide ions** (Cambridge International AS & A Level Chemistry 9701, 2025–2027, AS Level). Full explanations and the equation-building method are in the [11.3 study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-chemistry-9701-as-some-reactions-of-the-halide-ions/). The [Group 17 revision notes](/resources/as-chem-group17-revision-notes/) summarise the whole topic.

## The outcomes

- Relative reactivity of halide ions as **reducing agents**.
- Reactions with **Ag⁺(aq) then NH₃(aq)** (complex formula not required).
- Reactions with **concentrated H₂SO₄**, with **balanced equations**.

## Reducing power

```
2X-  ->  X2 + 2e-          halide ion is oxidised; it is the reducing agent
I-  >  Br-  >  Cl-         reducing power increases down the group
```

Why: larger ion → outer electrons further from the nucleus and more shielded → lost more easily.

## Silver nitrate then ammonia

Method: dissolve → add **dilute HNO₃** (removes carbonate; adds no halide) → add **AgNO₃(aq)** → add **NH₃(aq)**.

| Ion | Precipitate | Dilute NH₃ | Concentrated NH₃ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cl⁻ | **White** AgCl | Dissolves | Dissolves |
| Br⁻ | **Cream** AgBr | Does not dissolve | Dissolves |
| I⁻ | **Yellow** AgI | Does not dissolve | Does not dissolve |

```
Ag+(aq) + Cl-(aq) -> AgCl(s)
Ag+(aq) + Br-(aq) -> AgBr(s)
Ag+(aq) + I-(aq)  -> AgI(s)
```

**Why the ammonia results differ:** solubility falls AgCl > AgBr > AgI. Ammonia forms a soluble complex with Ag⁺, removing Ag⁺ from AgX(s) ⇌ Ag⁺(aq) + X⁻(aq). The less soluble the solid, the more concentrated the ammonia must be; AgI never dissolves.

## Concentrated sulfuric acid

**Step 1 (all halides, acid–base, not redox):** NaX + H₂SO₄ → NaHSO₄ + HX (steamy fumes)

**Step 2 (Br⁻ and I⁻ only, redox):** sulfur (+6) is reduced.

| S product | Oxidation number | Half-equation |
|---|---|---|
| SO₂ | +4 | H₂SO₄ + 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → SO₂ + 2H₂O |
| S | 0 | H₂SO₄ + 6H⁺ + 6e⁻ → S + 4H₂O |
| H₂S | −2 | H₂SO₄ + 8H⁺ + 8e⁻ → H₂S + 4H₂O |

Balanced equations to know:

```
2HBr + H2SO4  ->  Br2 + SO2 + 2H2O
2NaBr + 3H2SO4 -> 2NaHSO4 + Br2 + SO2 + 2H2O
2HI + H2SO4   ->  I2 + SO2 + 2H2O
6HI + H2SO4   ->  3I2 + S + 4H2O
8HI + H2SO4   ->  4I2 + H2S + 4H2O
```

## Observations

| Halide | Observations |
|---|---|
| Cl⁻ | Steamy fumes (HCl) only |
| Br⁻ | Steamy fumes; orange-brown vapour (Br₂); colourless choking gas (SO₂) |
| I⁻ | Some steamy fumes; purple vapour/black solid (I₂); yellow solid (S); bad-egg smell (H₂S) |

How far sulfur is reduced (Cl⁻ none, Br⁻ to +4, I⁻ to −2) is the evidence for the reducing power order.

## Building any sulfuric acid equation in three steps

1. Choose the sulfur product and write its half-equation (2, 6 or 8 electrons for SO₂, S or H₂S).
2. Write enough 2HX → X₂ + 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ to supply the same number of electrons (one, three or four times).
3. Add, cancel H⁺ and electrons, then check every element.

Starting from a solid salt instead of HX, add the acid–base step (NaX + H₂SO₄ → NaHSO₄ + HX) once for each HX used, as in 2NaBr + 3H₂SO₄ → 2NaHSO₄ + Br₂ + SO₂ + 2H₂O.

## Mixtures of halides

If a solution contains both Cl⁻ and I⁻, silver nitrate gives a pale yellow mixture of precipitates. Dilute ammonia dissolves the AgCl and leaves the yellow AgI, so the two can be told apart.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Reducing agent | A species that loses electrons and is oxidised, reducing something else |
| Precipitate | An insoluble solid formed when two solutions are mixed |
| Soluble complex | Here, a soluble species formed between Ag⁺ and ammonia (formula not required) |
| Acid–base reaction | Proton transfer, with no change in oxidation numbers |
| Steamy fumes | Droplets formed when a hydrogen halide meets moist air |
| Half-equation | An equation showing the electrons lost or gained by one species |

## Exam traps

- Reducing power of halide ions given as decreasing down the group.
- Acidifying with HCl or H₂SO₄ instead of HNO₃.
- AgBr described as white or yellow; it is **cream**.
- Calling step 1 a redox reaction.
- Chloride shown reducing H₂SO₄.
- H₂S given as the only iodide product.
- Omitting "concentrated" or "dilute" when describing the ammonia test.
- Forgetting state symbols in the precipitate ionic equations when asked.

## Self-test

1. Write the half-equation for iodide ions acting as a reducing agent.
2. Why is I⁻ a stronger reducing agent than Cl⁻?
3. Why is dilute nitric acid added before silver nitrate?
4. Give the colour of the precipitate formed by bromide ions with silver nitrate.
5. A precipitate dissolves in concentrated ammonia but not dilute ammonia. Which halide was present?
6. Explain why AgI does not dissolve in concentrated ammonia.
7. Write the equation for step 1 when sodium chloride reacts with concentrated sulfuric acid.
8. Why is step 1 not a redox reaction?
9. What is the oxidation number of sulfur in SO₂, S and H₂S?
10. Write a balanced equation for HBr reducing H₂SO₄.
11. Name the gas with a bad-egg smell formed from sodium iodide and concentrated sulfuric acid, and give the oxidation number of sulfur in it.
12. Why is concentrated sulfuric acid unsuitable for preparing hydrogen iodide?

**Answers:**

1. 2I⁻ → I₂ + 2e⁻
2. I⁻ is larger, so its outer electrons are further from the nucleus and more shielded, and are lost more easily.
3. To remove carbonate (and similar) ions that would also give a precipitate; nitric acid adds no halide ions.
4. Cream.
5. Bromide.
6. AgI is so insoluble that ammonia cannot lower the Ag⁺ concentration enough for the solid to dissolve.
7. NaCl + H₂SO₄ → NaHSO₄ + HCl
8. It is proton transfer; no oxidation numbers change.
9. +4, 0 and −2.
10. 2HBr + H₂SO₄ → Br₂ + SO₂ + 2H₂O
11. Hydrogen sulfide, H₂S; −2.
12. The HI formed is oxidised by the sulfuric acid to iodine, so little HI is obtained.
