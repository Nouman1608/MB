## 1. Fiscal deficits and surpluses (3a)

The fiscal balance is government revenue minus government spending in one period, usually a year. A negative balance is a **fiscal deficit**: spending exceeds revenue and the gap is borrowed. A positive balance is a **fiscal surplus**: revenue exceeds spending and the surplus can repay debt or be saved.

Economists also split out interest. The **primary balance** is revenue minus spending excluding interest payments. It shows whether the government's own programmes are paying for themselves, before the cost of past borrowing.

**Worked example (Country K, illustrative year).** Revenue is 612. Spending other than interest is 640. The debt at the start of the year is 1,300 and the average interest rate is 3%. GDP is 2,400.

```
Interest          = 0.03 x 1,300 = 39
Total spending    = 640 + 39 = 679
Fiscal balance    = 612 - 679 = -67   (a deficit of 67)
Deficit / GDP     = 67 / 2,400 = 2.79%
Primary balance   = 612 - 640 = -28   (a primary deficit)
```

Deficits are compared across time and countries as a percentage of GDP, because 67 means little without the size of the economy.

## 2. Automatic stabilisers and discretionary policy (3a)

**Automatic stabilisers** are rules already in the tax and benefit system that move the balance whenever output moves, without any new decision. Tax receipts rise in a boom, especially under progressive income tax, and unemployment benefits fall. In a slump the reverse happens. They cushion swings in aggregate demand.

**Discretionary fiscal policy** is a deliberate change in tax rates, spending programmes or benefit rules by the government.

| | Automatic stabilisers | Discretionary policy |
|---|---|---|
| Decision needed | None, built into the system | Yes, a policy choice |
| Timing | Immediate | Lags in recognising, deciding and acting |
| Size of response | Fixed by existing rules | Chosen to fit the problem |
| Example | Benefit payments rise as firms lay off workers | A one-year cut in a sales tax rate |

**Chain of reasoning.** Output falls by 100. Income tax at an average 30% of that income means tax receipts fall by 30. Benefits rise by 5. The balance worsens by 35 and household disposable income falls by 65, not 100. Spending falls by less than output, so the downturn is cushioned. The cost is a bigger deficit.

## 3. The fiscal deficit and the national debt (3a)

A deficit is a **flow**: it is measured over a period. The **national debt** is a **stock**: the total the government owes at a point in time, the sum of past borrowing less repayments. A deficit adds to the debt and a surplus reduces it.

Continuing Country K: the debt rises from 1,300 to 1,300 + 67 = 1,367. If nominal GDP grows 3.5% to 2,484, the debt ratio moves from 1,300 / 2,400 = 54.2% to 1,367 / 2,484 = 55.0%. The debt ratio rose even though the economy grew, because borrowing (5.2% of the debt) outpaced growth (3.5%).

A country can run a surplus and still have a high debt, and it can run a small deficit while the debt ratio falls, if GDP grows faster than the debt. Never treat the two as interchangeable.

## 4. Structural and cyclical deficits (3a)

A **cyclical deficit** arises because the economy is below its potential output, so revenue is lower and benefit spending higher than at normal activity. It disappears when output returns to potential. A **structural deficit** remains even when output is at potential: spending is higher than revenue at full capacity. Only policy change, or lasting growth in potential output, removes it.

**Worked example (Country K).** Potential output is 2,500 and actual output is 2,400.

```
Output gap          = 2,400 - 2,500 = -100  (-4.0% of potential)
Automatic response  = 0.30 tax + 0.05 benefits = 0.35 per unit of gap
Cyclical deficit    = 0.35 x 100 = 35
Structural deficit  = 67 - 35 = 32  (1.28% of potential output)
```

If output recovers to potential with no policy change, the deficit falls from 67 to 32, not to zero. A government that sees only the headline 67 may think the recovery will fix it. The cyclically adjusted figure shows that 32 needs policy action. The split rests on estimating potential output, which is uncertain, so treat it as an estimate.

## 5. Factors influencing deficits and debts (3b)

| Factor | Effect on deficit or debt |
|---|---|
| Phase of the economic cycle | Slump widens the deficit through automatic stabilisers; boom narrows it |
| Growth of potential output | Faster trend growth raises revenue and shrinks the debt ratio |
| Interest rates | Higher rates raise interest payments on new and refinanced debt |
| Demographics | An older population raises pension, health and care spending |
| Tax and spending decisions | Tax cuts, new programmes or capital projects change the balance |
| Shocks and crises | Rescues of banks, war, disasters and pandemics add one-off borrowing |
| Tax base and compliance | A large informal economy or evasion lowers revenue |
| Dependence on a volatile revenue source | A fall in a commodity price cuts revenue at once |
| Inflation | Raises nominal GDP, so the ratio falls, but pushes up interest rates and index-linked costs |
| Starting debt | A large stock means large interest payments for any given rate |

**Debt dynamics.** The debt ratio stays stable when the primary surplus (as a share of GDP) equals d(r - g)/(1 + g), where d is the debt ratio, r the average interest rate and g nominal GDP growth. Country L has d = 0.90, r = 5% and g = 2%: it needs a primary surplus of 0.90 x 0.03 / 1.02 = 2.65% of GDP. Country M has d = 0.80, r = 2% and g = 4%: it can run a primary deficit of 0.80 x 0.02 / 1.04 = 1.54% of GDP with the ratio unchanged. The gap between interest rates and growth matters as much as the deficit.

## 6. Significance of deficits and debts (3c)

### Impact on interest rates

Government borrowing adds to the demand for loanable funds. If savings are limited, interest rates rise, which can crowd out private investment and interest-sensitive consumption. If lenders doubt that the government can repay, they demand a risk premium, so yields rise further. Effects are smaller when there is spare saving, when the central bank holds bonds, or when borrowing is in a slump with weak private demand.

### Debt servicing

**Debt servicing** is the interest paid on debt. For Country K, 39 / 612 = 6.4% of revenue goes on interest. Suppose 40% of the debt (520) is refinanced at 6% while the rest stays at 3%.

```
New interest  = 0.06 x 520 + 0.03 x 780 = 31.2 + 23.4 = 54.6
Rise          = 54.6 - 39 = 15.6
Share of revenue = 54.6 / 612 = 8.9%
```

The extra 15.6 is an opportunity cost: it cannot fund schools or hospitals, and it widens the deficit unless taxes rise. Debt in foreign currency or held abroad adds exchange-rate risk and sends interest payments out of the economy.

### Intergenerational equity

Borrowing moves the cost of today's spending to future taxpayers. Whether that is fair depends on what the money buys. Suppose 300 is borrowed at 3% for a road that gives benefits worth 14 a year. Interest is 9 a year, so future users gain 14 - 9 = 5 a year. If the same 300 funds current benefits, future taxpayers pay 9 a year and receive no asset. Borrowing for investment can be fair because those who pay also benefit. Borrowing for current spending shifts costs to people who did not receive the benefit. Some borrowing smooths a recession that would otherwise hurt the same generation.

### Judging significance

Assess any claim that debt is "too high" using these tests:

- the size relative to GDP, and the trend
- the gap between the interest rate and nominal growth
- who holds the debt (domestic or foreign) and in which currency
- the maturity: short-dated debt is refinanced soon
- the purpose: investment or current spending
- whether the deficit is cyclical or structural
- the state of the economy: borrowing in a slump differs from borrowing in a boom

## Common errors

- Calling the debt "the deficit", or adding the deficit to GDP.
- Saying all deficits are bad. A cyclical deficit in a slump is partly the stabilisers working.
- Describing automatic stabilisers as policy changes.
- Using a debt-to-GDP ratio without saying whether it is rising or falling.
- Listing "crowding out" with no chain from borrowing to interest rates to investment.
- Giving intergenerational equity as "future generations pay" without asking what they receive.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the primary balance?**
The primary balance is government revenue minus government spending excluding interest payments on debt.

**Why are deficits compared as a percentage of GDP?**
A deficit in cash terms means little without the size of the economy, so deficits are compared across time and countries as a share of GDP.

**Can a country run a budget surplus and still have a high national debt?**
Yes. The deficit or surplus is a flow measured over a year, while the national debt is the stock of past borrowing, so a surplus can leave a high debt in place.

**Which Edexcel International A Level Economics section covers fiscal deficits and national debt?**
Section 4.3.5 in Unit 4 of the Pearson Edexcel International A Level Economics specification covers deficits and surpluses, automatic stabilisers, structural and cyclical deficits, national debt and their significance.
