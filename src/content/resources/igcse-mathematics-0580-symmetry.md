## Line symmetry

A **line of symmetry** (mirror line) splits a shape into two halves that are mirror images: fold along it and one half fits exactly on the other. To test a line, go from each vertex straight across the line at 90° and the same distance beyond it. If there is a matching vertex there every time, it is a line of symmetry.

A diagonal is *not* automatically a line of symmetry. A rectangle's diagonal cuts it into two congruent triangles, but folding along it does not make them fit on top of each other.

## Rotational symmetry

A shape has **rotational symmetry** if it looks exactly the same after a turn of less than 360° about a fixed point (the centre of rotation). The **order of rotational symmetry** is the number of positions in one full turn in which the shape looks the same.

- Every shape fits onto itself after a full 360° turn, so the smallest order is 1. Order 1 means **no rotational symmetry**.
- If the order is n, the smallest turn that maps the shape onto itself is 360° ÷ n.
- With tracing paper: trace the shape, turn the tracing once round about the centre, and count how many times it fits.

### A useful link between the two

If a flat shape has **at least one** line of symmetry, its order of rotational symmetry equals its number of lines. A shape with **no** lines can still have rotational symmetry: a parallelogram has order 2.

## Symmetry of triangles and quadrilaterals

The syllabus notes include the properties of triangles, quadrilaterals and polygons that follow directly from their symmetries. Learn the counts and *why* the properties follow.

| Shape | Lines of symmetry | Order of rotational symmetry |
|---|---|---|
| Equilateral triangle | 3 | 3 |
| Isosceles triangle (not equilateral) | 1 | 1 |
| Scalene triangle | 0 | 1 |
| Square | 4 | 4 |
| Rectangle (not a square) | 2 | 2 |
| Rhombus (not a square) | 2 | 2 |
| Parallelogram (not a rectangle or rhombus) | 0 | 2 |
| Kite (not a rhombus) | 1 | 1 |
| Isosceles trapezium | 1 | 1 |
| Trapezium (not isosceles) | 0 | 1 |

Properties that come from these symmetries:

- **Isosceles triangle:** the line of symmetry joins the apex to the midpoint of the base. It bisects the apex angle, meets the base at 90° and reflects one base angle onto the other, so the base angles are equal.
- **Rectangle:** the two lines of symmetry join the midpoints of opposite sides. They are not the diagonals.
- **Rhombus:** the two lines of symmetry **are** the diagonals. So each diagonal bisects the angles it passes through, and the diagonals cross at 90°.
- **Square:** it has the lines of a rectangle and the lines of a rhombus, four in all.
- **Parallelogram:** a half-turn about the point where the diagonals cross maps it onto itself. So opposite sides are equal, opposite angles are equal and the diagonals bisect each other.
- **Isosceles trapezium:** its line of symmetry joins the midpoints of the parallel sides, so the base angles are equal in pairs and the diagonals are equal.

### Worked example 1 (non-calculator): a rhombus

PQRS is a rhombus. Angle PQR = 116°. Find angle QPR.

```
The diagonals of a rhombus are its lines of symmetry.
QS is a line of symmetry, so it bisects angle PQR:
  angle PQS = 116° ÷ 2 = 58°
Let the diagonals meet at M. The diagonals of a rhombus cross at 90°,
so angle PMQ = 90°.
In triangle PMQ:  angle QPM = 180° − 90° − 58° = 32°
M lies on PR, so angle QPR = 32°.
```

Check: PR bisects angle QPS, so angle QPS = 64°, and 116° + 64° = 180° (co-interior angles).

### Worked example 2 (non-calculator): an isosceles trapezium

ABCD is an isosceles trapezium with AB parallel to DC and AD = BC. Angle DAB = 68°. Find angles ABC, ADC and BCD.

```
The line of symmetry joins the midpoints of AB and DC.
It reflects A onto B and D onto C.
  angle ABC = angle DAB = 68°
AB is parallel to DC, so co-interior angles add to 180°:
  angle ADC = 180° − 68° = 112°
  angle BCD = angle ADC = 112°   (by the symmetry)
Check: 68 + 68 + 112 + 112 = 360°
```

## Symmetry of regular polygons

A regular polygon with n sides has **n lines of symmetry** and **rotational symmetry of order n**.

- If n is **odd**, every line of symmetry goes through a vertex and the midpoint of the opposite side (a regular pentagon has 5 such lines).
- If n is **even**, half the lines go through pairs of opposite vertices and half go through the midpoints of pairs of opposite sides (a regular octagon has 4 of each).

The smallest turn that maps a regular polygon onto itself is 360° ÷ n. That is also the size of one exterior angle, which links this subtopic to the polygon angle facts in C4.6.

### Worked example 3 (non-calculator): working back from a turn

A regular polygon fits onto itself after a turn of 24°, and 24° is the smallest turn that does this. Find the number of sides, the number of lines of symmetry and the size of one interior angle.

```
Order of rotational symmetry = 360° ÷ 24° = 15
A regular polygon has order n, so n = 15 sides.
Lines of symmetry = 15
Exterior angle = 360° ÷ 15 = 24°
Interior angle = 180° − 24° = 156°
```

## Symmetry on a grid

You may be asked to draw a line of symmetry on a grid, write its equation, or add points so a shape has a given symmetry.

### Worked example 4: the equation of a line of symmetry

Triangle PQR has vertices P(1, 4), Q(4, 1) and R(5, 5). Show that the triangle is isosceles and find the equation of its line of symmetry.

```
PR² = (5 − 1)² + (5 − 4)² = 16 + 1 = 17
QR² = (5 − 4)² + (5 − 1)² = 1 + 16 = 17
PQ² = (4 − 1)² + (1 − 4)² = 9 + 9 = 18
PR = QR = √17, so the triangle is isosceles with apex R.
Swapping the x- and y-coordinates maps P(1, 4) to Q(4, 1)
and maps R(5, 5) to itself.
Reflection in y = x swaps x and y, so the line of symmetry is y = x.
```

It has 1 line of symmetry and order 1. Equations of lines are covered in [coordinate geometry](/resources/igcse-mathematics-coordinate-geometry/).

### Worked example 5: completing a shape with rotational symmetry

Points A(1, 1), B(4, 1) and C(5, 3) are three vertices of a hexagon. The hexagon has rotational symmetry of order 2 about the point (3, 3). Find the other three vertices.

```
Order 2 means a half-turn (180°) about (3, 3) maps the shape onto itself.
A half-turn about (3, 3) sends (x, y) to (6 − x, 6 − y),
because (3, 3) is the midpoint of each point and its image.
  A(1, 1) → (5, 5)
  B(4, 1) → (2, 5)
  C(5, 3) → (1, 3)
The hexagon is A(1, 1), B(4, 1), C(5, 3), (5, 5), (2, 5), (1, 3).
```

This hexagon has order 2 but **no** lines of symmetry, like a parallelogram. Rotations are described fully in the [transformations and vectors study guide](/resources/igcse-mathematics-transformations-and-vectors/).

## Symmetry of solids (Extended only)

The syllabus example for E4.5 part 2 is to identify **planes** and **axes** of symmetry.

- A **plane of symmetry** cuts a solid into two halves that are mirror images. It is the 3D version of a line of symmetry.
- An **axis of symmetry** is a line through the solid about which it can be turned and look the same in more than one position in a full turn. The order counts those positions.

### Right prisms

For a right prism, look at the cross-section first.

- Each line of symmetry of the cross-section gives a plane of symmetry that runs along the length of the prism.
- There is one more plane: the plane halfway along the length, parallel to the two ends.
- The axis along the length, through the centre of the ends, has the same order as the cross-section's rotational symmetry.

| Solid | Planes of symmetry | Rotational symmetry about the axis through the ends |
|---|---|---|
| Cuboid, all edges different (e.g. 2 × 3 × 5) | 3 | order 2 |
| Cuboid with two square faces (e.g. 3 × 3 × 7) | 5 | order 4 |
| Cube | 9 | order 4 |
| Triangular prism, equilateral cross-section | 4 | order 3 |
| Triangular prism, isosceles cross-section | 2 | none (order 1) |
| Regular hexagonal prism | 7 | order 6 |
| Cylinder | infinitely many | any angle of turn |

A cuboid with all edges different has three axes of symmetry, each of order 2, through the centres of pairs of opposite faces. A cylinder's planes are every plane containing its central axis, plus the plane halfway up.

### Right pyramids and cones

In a **right pyramid** the apex is directly above the centre of the base. Every plane of symmetry contains the vertical axis through the apex. So:

- planes of symmetry = lines of symmetry of the base
- order about the vertical axis = order of rotational symmetry of the base
- there is **no** horizontal plane of symmetry.

| Solid | Planes of symmetry | Order about the vertical axis |
|---|---|---|
| Square-based right pyramid | 4 | 4 |
| Rectangle-based right pyramid (base not square) | 2 | 2 |
| Regular hexagonal-based right pyramid | 6 | 6 |
| Right circular cone | infinitely many | any angle of turn |

### Worked example 6: naming a plane of symmetry

ABCDEFGH is a cuboid. The base ABCD is a 4 cm by 4 cm square and the edges AE, BF, CG and DH are vertical and 9 cm long. Is the plane ACGE a plane of symmetry?

```
Plane ACGE contains the vertical edges AE and CG and the base diagonal AC.
A diagonal of a square is a line of symmetry of the square.
Reflecting in plane ACGE maps B to D, F to H and the cuboid onto itself.
Plane ACGE is a plane of symmetry.
```

If the base were a 4 cm by 6 cm rectangle, AC would not be a line of symmetry of the base, so ACGE would **not** be a plane of symmetry.

## Common errors

- Counting the diagonals of a rectangle or parallelogram as lines of symmetry.
- Giving the order of rotational symmetry as 0. The smallest order is 1, which means no rotational symmetry.
- Saying a parallelogram has 2 lines of symmetry because it has order 2.
- Counting each line twice on an even-sided regular polygon: a regular hexagon has 6 lines, not 12.
- Adding a horizontal plane of symmetry to a pyramid or cone.
- For a prism, forgetting the plane halfway along the length, or counting it twice.
