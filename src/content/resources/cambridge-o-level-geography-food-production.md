## What 3.2 covers

- The main features of an **agricultural system**: inputs, processes
  and outputs.
- How **physical inputs** (relief, climate, soil) and **human inputs**
  (economic and social) influence the type of farming.
- Classifying farming: commercial or subsistence; arable, pastoral or
  mixed; intensive or extensive.
- The **causes of food shortages**, both natural and economic or
  political, their **effects**, and **possible solutions**.
- **Two case studies**: a farm or agricultural system, and a country or
  region suffering from food shortages.

## The farm as a system

| Part | Examples |
|---|---|
| Physical inputs | Relief (gradient, altitude), climate (temperature, rainfall, length of growing season), soil (fertility, depth, drainage) |
| Human inputs | Labour, capital, machinery, seeds, fertiliser, pesticides, irrigation, access to markets and transport, government policy, land ownership and tradition |
| Processes | Ploughing, sowing, transplanting, weeding, spraying, irrigating, harvesting, milking, rearing animals |
| Outputs | Crops, meat, milk, wool, eggs; income or food for the family; by-products such as straw and manure |
| Feedback | Profit reinvested as next year's inputs, or manure returned to the fields |

Physical inputs set the **limits** of what can be farmed; human
inputs decide **how intensively** and **for whom**. Steep slopes and
thin soils favour pastoral farming; flat land with fertile soil and a
warm growing season favours arable. A farmer with capital can
overcome some limits, for example by irrigating dry land or building
glasshouses.

## Classifying farming

- **Commercial**: produce sold for profit. **Subsistence**: produce
  feeds the farmer's family, with little surplus.
- **Arable**: crops. **Pastoral**: animals. **Mixed**: both.
- **Intensive**: high inputs of labour or capital per hectare and
  high output per hectare, on small areas. **Extensive**: low inputs
  and output per hectare, over large areas.

Combine all three when classifying. Examples:

| Farm | Classification |
|---|---|
| Wet rice on small family plots in a river delta | Intensive, subsistence, arable |
| Large cattle station in a dry grassland | Extensive, commercial, pastoral |
| Glasshouse tomatoes and flowers near a large city | Intensive, commercial, arable (horticulture) |
| Nomadic herding of cattle and goats in a semi-arid region | Extensive, subsistence, pastoral |

## Case study 1: wet rice in the Ganges–Brahmaputra delta, Bangladesh

**Physical inputs.** The delta is flat and low-lying, built of fertile
alluvium deposited by the rivers and renewed by floods. Temperatures
are high all year. The summer monsoon (roughly June to September)
brings heavy rain that floods the paddy fields to the depth rice
needs; clay in the soil holds the water.

**Human inputs.** Farms are small and worked mainly by family labour,
with draught animals or small power tillers. Seed, fertiliser and,
where available, irrigation water from wells or canals are the main
purchased inputs.

**Processes.** Seedlings are raised in a nursery bed, then
transplanted by hand into flooded fields surrounded by low earth
banks (bunds). Fields are weeded and the water level managed; the crop
is harvested and threshed largely by hand.

**Outputs.** Rice, mostly eaten by the family with any surplus sold
locally; straw for fuel, animal feed or roofing. Where irrigation
allows, a second crop is grown in the dry season, so the same land
can produce more than one harvest a year.

**Why it is intensive**: large labour input on small plots gives high
yields per hectare, which is necessary to feed a dense rural
population.

## Food shortages: causes

**Natural**

- **Drought**: crops fail and animals die for lack of water and
  pasture.
- **Floods and tropical storms**: crops destroyed, soil washed away,
  stores and roads damaged.
- **Pests and diseases**: for example, desert locust swarms that strip
  crops.

**Economic and political**

- **Low capital investment**: farmers cannot afford fertiliser,
  improved seed, irrigation or storage.
- **Poor transport and distribution**: food cannot reach areas of
  need, or surplus cannot reach markets.
- **Conflict**: farmers flee, fields are abandoned, markets close and
  aid is blocked.
- **Other human factors**: rapid population growth, land degradation
  from overcultivation or overgrazing, and land used for export cash
  crops rather than food.

## Food shortages: effects

Effects run from individuals to whole countries: **undernutrition**
and **malnutrition** (including stunted growth in children); lower
resistance to disease; less energy to work, so output falls further;
children leaving school; migration to towns or camps; dependence on
food aid; and in extreme cases **famine** with widespread deaths.

## Case study 2: South Sudan

South Sudan became independent in 2011. Civil war broke out in late
2013, and in February 2017 famine was declared in parts of Unity
State. Use it to show how causes combine:

- **Conflict** drove farmers from their land, disrupted planting and
  harvests, closed markets and made it dangerous to deliver aid.
- **Economic collapse** and rising food prices left families unable to
  buy food.
- **Poor infrastructure**: few all-weather roads, so many areas are cut
  off in the rainy season.
- **Climatic shocks** such as drought and floods add to the pressure.

Effects included severe malnutrition, very large numbers of people
displaced within the country and into neighbouring countries, and
heavy reliance on international food aid.

## Solutions

| Short term (relief) | Long term (increasing output) |
|---|---|
| Emergency food aid | Irrigation schemes and small dams |
| Therapeutic feeding for malnourished children | High-yielding and drought-resistant seed varieties (the Green Revolution approach) with fertiliser |
| Cash transfers so families can buy food | Appropriate technology: hand pumps, terracing, improved grain stores |
| Early-warning systems for drought | Better roads and markets; pest control; peace and stable government |

Evaluate solutions: food aid saves lives but does not raise output and
can undercut local farmers; Green Revolution seeds raise yields but
need costly fertiliser and water; appropriate technology is cheap and
locally maintained but works on a small scale.

## Common mistakes

- Classifying a farm on only one scale ("it is arable").
- Listing inputs without saying whether they are physical or human.
- Writing "lack of food" as a *cause* of food shortage.
- Effects that stop at "people go hungry"; develop them.
- Case studies without a named place.

## Exam technique

- **Describe** a system: inputs, processes, outputs in order, with
  specific examples from your farm.
- **Explain** how a physical factor influences farming: factor → what
  it allows or prevents → resulting farming type.
- **Suggest** solutions for an unfamiliar area: match each solution to
  a cause given in the resource.
- In 7-mark case study answers, use **place-specific detail** and
  develop each point.
