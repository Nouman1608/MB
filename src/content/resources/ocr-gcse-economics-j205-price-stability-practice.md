These questions cover section 3.4 of the OCR GCSE (9-1) Economics specification (J205, Version 2.0, June 2026), Price stability. All countries and figures are invented. Revise first with the [price stability study guide](/resources/ocr-gcse-economics-j205-price-stability/) and the [price stability revision notes](/resources/ocr-gcse-economics-j205-price-stability-revision-notes/).

## Section A: short-answer questions

**1.** State what is meant by price stability. **[1]**

**2.** Explain the difference between a nominal value and a real value. **[2]**

**3.** A loaf of bread cost £1.40 in the base year and costs £1.61 now. Calculate its price index. **[1]**

**4.** The CPI rises from 104.2 to 108.9 over a year. Calculate the rate of inflation to one decimal place. **[2]**

**5.** An item costs £240. Inflation is 3%, 5% and 2% in the next three years. Calculate its price after three years, to the nearest penny. **[2]**

**6.** Inflation falls from 6% to 3%. Which one of the following is true? **[1]**

- A. Prices are falling.
- B. Prices are rising, but more slowly.
- C. The economy is in deflation.
- D. The purchasing power of money is rising.

**7.** Explain one cause of cost-push inflation. **[2]**

## Section B: structured and extended questions

**8.** The table shows the CPI basket for Country J and the price change in each category over one year.

| Category | Weight | Price change |
|---|---|---|
| Housing | 0.25 | +4% |
| Energy | 0.06 | +35% |
| Food | 0.14 | +9% |
| Transport | 0.15 | +3% |
| Other | 0.40 | +1% |

**(a)** Calculate the rate of CPI inflation. **[3]**
**(b)** Identify the category that contributed most to inflation and explain why it did so despite its small weight. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why this inflation is likely to be cost-push rather than demand-pull. **[2]**

**9.** Country K's inflation rate over six years is shown. Its target is 2%.

| Year | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Inflation (%) | 1.8 | 2.2 | 2.0 | 5.6 | 8.4 | 4.1 |

**(a)** Describe the pattern of inflation compared with the target. **[2]**
**(b)** An item cost £60 at the start of Year 4. Calculate its price at the end of Year 6, to the nearest penny. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain whether Country K had price stability in Years 1 to 3. **[1]**

**10.** Sam saves £6,000 for one year at 2.5% interest. Inflation over the year is 6%.

**(a)** Calculate Sam's balance at the end of the year. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the balance in start-of-year purchasing power, to the nearest pound. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain what has happened to Sam's purchasing power. **[1]**

**11.** Evaluate the consequences of high inflation for producers. **[6]**

**12.** Evaluate whether demand-pull or cost-push factors are more likely to cause inflation that lasts. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** Low and predictable inflation, such as the UK's 2% target [1]. **[1]**

**2.** A nominal value is measured in money of the day, with no adjustment for price changes [1]. A real value is adjusted for inflation, so it shows what the money can actually buy [1]. **[2]**

**3.** 1.61 ÷ 1.40 × 100 = **115** [1]. **[1]**

**4.** (108.9 − 104.2) ÷ 104.2 × 100 [1] = **4.5%** [1]. **[2]**

**5.** 240 × 1.03 × 1.05 × 1.02 [1] = **£264.75** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Adding the rates (10%) gives £264.00, which loses the accuracy mark.

**6.** **B** [1]. Positive inflation means the price level is still rising. **[1]**

**7.** A rise in the price of energy or raw materials raises firms' costs of production [1]. Firms pass the higher costs on to customers in higher prices, so the general price level rises [1]. (Credit also: higher wages; higher indirect taxes.) **[2]**

**8. (a)** Contributions: housing 0.25 × 4 = 1.0; energy 0.06 × 35 = 2.1; food 0.14 × 9 = 1.26 [1]. Transport 0.15 × 3 = 0.45; other 0.40 × 1 = 0.40 [1]. Total = **5.21%**, about 5.2% [1].
**(b)** Energy, contributing 2.1 percentage points [1]. Its weight is small but its price rose by 35%, far more than any other category, so its weight × change was the largest [1].
**(c)** The biggest rises are in energy and food, which are costs for firms and households, rather than a broad rise across all items [1]. Higher energy costs raise the costs of producing almost everything, so firms pass them on in prices, which is cost-push [1]. **[7]**

**9. (a)** Inflation was close to the 2% target in Years 1 to 3, between 1.8% and 2.2% [1]. It then rose well above target, peaking at 8.4% in Year 5, before falling to 4.1% in Year 6, still double the target [1].
**(b)** 60 × 1.056 × 1.084 × 1.041 [1] = **£71.50** [1].
**(c)** Yes: inflation was low and stable, within 0.2 percentage points of the target [1]. **[5]**

**10. (a)** 6,000 × 1.025 = **£6,150** [1].
**(b)** 6,150 ÷ 1.06 = **£5,802** [1].
**(c)** Sam's balance rose in money terms, but prices rose faster, so the savings buy about 3.3% less than at the start [1]. **[3]**

**11.** Costs: firms' costs of materials, energy and wages rise [1], and frequent price changes create menu costs and uncertainty, which may lead firms to delay investment [1]. If domestic inflation is higher than in other countries, exporters' prices become less competitive and they lose sales abroad [1]. Benefits: firms that can raise prices quickly, perhaps because demand is price inelastic, may protect or increase their profits [1], and the real value of their fixed-rate loans falls [1]. Judgement: high inflation is usually harmful for producers overall because uncertainty discourages investment, but the effect is smallest for firms with strong brands that can pass costs on and largest for exporters facing foreign competition [1]. **[6]**

**12.** Demand-pull: when total spending grows faster than the economy can produce, firms raise prices [1]. If spending is fuelled by cheap borrowing or rising confidence, it can continue for a long time [1], and low unemployment lets workers win pay rises that add cost pressures [1]. Cost-push: a rise in energy or raw material prices raises costs and prices [1], but if the shock is one-off, inflation should fall once prices stop rising [1]. However, if workers demand higher wages to keep up, expectations can turn a cost shock into a wage-price spiral that lasts [1]. Evaluation: the persistence of either type depends on expectations and on how quickly the central bank responds with higher interest rates [1]. Judgement: demand-pull is more likely to last without policy action because its causes continue, whereas cost-push often fades unless it feeds into expectations and wages [1]. **[8]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Saying falling inflation means falling prices.
- Adding inflation rates across years.
- Leaving out a category or its weight in a CPI calculation.
- Converting a balance to real terms by subtracting percentages instead of dividing.
- Writing about consumers when the question asks about producers.
