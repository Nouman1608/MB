## Questions

### Section A: short-answer questions

**1.** Distinguish between a population and a sample. **[2]**

**2.** Identify the research method used in each study.

> A: A researcher changes the lighting in half the classrooms of a school and compares pupils' test scores with those in unchanged classrooms.
> B: A researcher compares the anxiety of residents before and after an earthquake struck their town.
> C: A researcher measures hours of sleep and exam marks for 60 students to see whether they are related.

**[3]**

**3.** Explain **one** ethical issue raised by covert observation and how a researcher could deal with it. **[3]**

**4.** Nine participants' scores on a concentration test were:

> 12, 15, 15, 17, 18, 18, 18, 19, 20

**(a)** Calculate the mean (to one decimal place), median, mode and range. **[4]**
**(b)** Use the mean and median to identify the type of distribution and explain your answer. **[2]**

**5.** In a study, 36 of 48 participants conformed at least once. Express this as a fraction in its simplest form and as a percentage. **[2]**

**6.** A researcher wants to know whether older and younger adults differ in how many faces they recognise. Write a non-directional hypothesis for this study. **[2]**

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**7.** A researcher investigates whether drinking a caffeinated drink affects concentration in 16- to 18-year-olds.

**(a)** Identify and operationalise the independent and dependent variables. **[2]**
**(b)** Identify **two** extraneous variables and explain how each could be controlled. **[4]**
**(c)** Explain **one** advantage of using a matched pairs design for this study. **[2]**

**8.** A researcher plans a naturalistic observation of helping behaviour in a primary school playground.

**(a)** Suggest **three** behavioural categories for helping behaviour. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain **one** advantage of the researcher being a non-participant observer. **[2]**

**9.** A questionnaire investigates students' attitudes to homework. Write **one** closed question and **one** open question for it, and explain **one** advantage of each type. **[4]**

**10.** Explain what is meant by demand characteristics, and suggest how they could affect the caffeine study in question 7. **[3]**

**11.** Design a field experiment to investigate whether people are more likely to pick up litter when a sign asks them to. Refer to the hypothesis, variables, sampling and ethics. **[8]**

**12.** Discuss the strengths and limitations of self-report techniques in psychological research. **[8]**

## Answers

Questions 11 and 12 are extended responses. The real exam marks these with levels of response, not point by point; the [1] points show the kind of content that earns credit.

**1.** The population is the whole group the researcher is interested in and wants findings to apply to [1]. The sample is the smaller group of people from that population who actually take part in the study [1]. **[2]**

**2.** A: field experiment [1]. B: natural experiment [1]. C: correlation [1]. **[3]**

**3.** Participants cannot give informed consent because they do not know they are being observed [1]. This also risks invading privacy [1]. The researcher could observe only in public places where people would expect to be seen, and debrief participants afterwards with the option to withdraw their data [1]. **[3]**

**4. (a)** Mean = 152 / 9 = 16.9 [1]. Median = 18 (the 5th value) [1]. Mode = 18 [1]. Range = 20 - 12 = 8 [1].
**(b)** Negatively skewed [1], because the mean (16.9) is below the median (18), pulled down by a few low scores such as 12 [1]. **[6]**

**5.** 36 / 48 = 3/4 [1]; 3/4 x 100 = 75% [1]. **[2]**

**6.** There will be a difference in the number of faces correctly recognised [1] between adults aged over 60 and adults aged 18 to 30 [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* both groups and the measured outcome must be operationalised; "age affects face memory" earns at most one mark.

**7. (a)** IV: whether participants drink a 250 ml caffeinated drink or the same drink without caffeine [1]. DV: the number of target letters correctly crossed out on a page in three minutes [1].
**(b)** Time of day, because alertness varies [1]: test all participants at the same time, such as 10 a.m. [1]. Previous caffeine intake [1]: ask participants to avoid caffeine for 12 hours before the study [1].
**(c)** Participants could be matched on usual daily caffeine intake [1], so differences in tolerance do not explain differences in concentration, without the order effects of repeated measures [1]. **[8]**

**8. (a)** For example: picking up something another child has dropped [1]; helping a child who has fallen to stand up [1]; showing another child how to play a game [1].
**(b)** The researcher does not interact with the children [1], so they are less likely to influence the behaviour being recorded and can remain more objective [1]. **[5]**

**9.** Closed: "How many hours of homework do you do each week? 0-2 / 3-5 / 6 or more" [1]. Advantage: answers are quantitative and easy to compare and analyse [1]. Open: "How does homework affect your free time?" [1]. Advantage: participants can explain their views in depth, giving richer qualitative data [1]. **[4]**

**10.** Demand characteristics are cues that allow participants to guess the aim of the study [1]. Participants who realise they have had caffeine may expect to concentrate better [1] and try harder, so the result reflects expectations rather than the caffeine itself [1]. **[3]**

**11.** *Indicative content (levels marking applies).* Directional hypothesis: more people will pick up litter when a sign asks them to than when there is no sign [1]. IV: sign present or absent [1]. DV operationalised: number of people out of every 50 passers-by who pick up a planted piece of litter [1]. Setting: the same park path at the same times on matched days, controlling extraneous variables [1]. Design: independent groups, as different passers-by experience each condition [1]. Sampling: opportunity sample of passers-by, which may limit generalisation [1]. Ethics: participants cannot consent, so behaviour must be public and no personal data recorded [1]. No harm or deception beyond everyday experience, and no identifying information kept [1]. **[8]**

**12.** *Indicative content (levels marking applies).* Questionnaires can collect data from large samples quickly and cheaply [1]. Closed questions give quantitative data that are easy to analyse [1]. Interviews give access to thoughts and feelings that cannot be observed [1]. Unstructured interviews give rich, detailed qualitative data [1]. Limitation: social desirability bias, as people present themselves favourably [1]. Limitation: leading questions or interviewer effects may distort answers [1]. Limitation: people may lack insight into their own behaviour [1]. Judgement: self-report is valuable for studying attitudes, but findings are stronger when checked against observation [1]. **[8]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Identifying a method without using the clue in the scenario.
- Forgetting to order scores before finding the median.
- Hypotheses with unoperationalised variables.
- Extraneous variables named with no control described.
- Design answers that skip ethics or sampling.
