This guide covers subtopic **2.4 Ions and ionic bonds** in Topic 2, Atoms, elements and
compounds, for Cambridge IGCSE Chemistry 0620 (2026–2028 series). It goes deeper than the
bonding overview in
[Ionic, Covalent and Metallic Bonding](/resources/ionic-covalent-and-metallic-bonding/),
with more compounds, a step-by-step method for diagrams and the full property explanations.

## What the syllabus asks for

**Core**

- Describe the formation of positive ions (**cations**) and negative ions (**anions**).
- State that an ionic bond is a **strong electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged
  ions**.
- Describe the formation of ionic bonds between elements from **Group I and Group VII**,
  using dot-and-cross diagrams.
- Describe the properties of ionic compounds: high melting and boiling points; good
  electrical conductivity when aqueous or molten, poor when solid.

**Supplement** (Extended candidates only)

- Describe the **giant lattice structure** of ionic compounds as a regular arrangement of
  alternating positive and negative ions.
- Describe ionic bond formation between ions of **any metallic and non-metallic elements**,
  using dot-and-cross diagrams.
- **Explain**, in terms of structure and bonding, the two properties listed in the Core.

The difference between the tiers is the command word: Core candidates *describe* the
properties; Extended candidates must *explain* them.

## How ions form

An atom has equal numbers of protons and electrons, so it is neutral. An **ion** is formed
when an atom loses or gains electrons, so the numbers no longer match.

- **Metal atoms lose** their outer-shell electrons. They are left with more protons than
  electrons, so they become **positive ions (cations)**.
- **Non-metal atoms gain** electrons until their outer shell is full. They then have more
  electrons than protons, so they become **negative ions (anions)**.

In both cases the ion ends up with the same electronic configuration as a noble gas. The
charge on the ion follows from the group number:

| Group | Electrons lost or gained | Ion charge | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| I | lose 1 | 1+ | Li⁺, Na⁺, K⁺ |
| II | lose 2 | 2+ | Mg²⁺, Ca²⁺ |
| III | lose 3 | 3+ | Al³⁺ |
| V | gain 3 | 3− | N³⁻ |
| VI | gain 2 | 2− | O²⁻, S²⁻ |
| VII | gain 1 | 1− | F⁻, Cl⁻, Br⁻, I⁻ |

Anions formed from single non-metal atoms take an "-ide" name: chlor**ide**, ox**ide**,
sulf**ide**, nitr**ide**. Cations keep the metal's name: sodium ion, calcium ion.

**Writing the electron change.** Examiners often ask you to show the electron transfer as
a configuration change. For sodium: Na (2,8,1) → Na⁺ (2,8), losing one electron. For
chlorine: Cl (2,8,7) → Cl⁻ (2,8,8), gaining one electron. Na⁺ now has the configuration of
neon and Cl⁻ that of argon.

## The ionic bond

Once the ions exist, the **ionic bond** is the **strong electrostatic attraction between
oppositely charged ions**. Use this exact wording. The bond is not the transfer of the
electron itself: the transfer *makes* the ions, and the attraction between them *is* the
bond.

## Dot-and-cross diagrams: a reliable method

Dot-and-cross diagrams show outer-shell electrons only, with one atom's electrons as dots
and the other's as crosses. Follow these steps for any ionic compound.

1. Write the electronic configuration of each atom.
2. Decide how many electrons each metal atom loses and each non-metal atom gains.
3. Find the ratio of atoms so that **electrons lost = electrons gained**.
4. Draw each ion separately in square brackets with its charge at the top right.
5. Show the metal ion with no outer electrons (or with its full inner shell, now the outer
   one) and the non-metal ion with eight outer electrons: its own as crosses, the
   transferred ones as dots.

### Worked example 1 (Core): lithium fluoride

- Li is 2,1 and loses one electron. F is 2,7 and gains one electron.
- One electron lost and one gained, so the ratio is 1 : 1 and the formula is LiF.
- Diagram: **[Li]⁺** with no electrons drawn in its outer shell, next to **[F]⁻** showing
  seven crosses and one dot (eight in total).
- Li⁺ (2) has the configuration of helium; F⁻ (2,8) that of neon.

### Worked example 2 (Supplement): calcium chloride

- Ca is 2,8,8,2 and loses **two** electrons. Each Cl is 2,8,7 and gains only **one**.
- Two electrons must go somewhere, so **two chlorine atoms** are needed: the formula is
  CaCl₂.
- Diagram: one **[Ca]²⁺** and **two separate [Cl]⁻ ions**, each with seven crosses and one
  dot. You may draw the second chloride ion or write "2" in front of one bracketed ion;
  both show the ratio clearly.

The same logic gives sodium oxide: O (2,6) needs two electrons, each Na (2,8,1) gives one,
so two sodium ions are needed for each oxide ion, Na₂O. For aluminium oxide the numbers
are larger: each Al (2,8,3) loses three and each O gains two, so the lowest common
multiple of 6 electrons gives Al₂O₃.

## The giant ionic lattice (Supplement)

Ionic compounds do not form molecules. In solid sodium chloride, each Na⁺ is surrounded by
Cl⁻ ions and each Cl⁻ by Na⁺ ions, repeating in every direction. The syllabus wording is a
**regular arrangement of alternating positive and negative ions**, and the structure is
called a **giant lattice** because it extends throughout the crystal. A formula such as
NaCl or CaCl₂ gives the **ratio** of ions in the lattice, not the size of a molecule.

## Properties of ionic compounds

**High melting and boiling points.** Core: describe — ionic compounds are solids at room
temperature with high melting and boiling points; sodium chloride melts at 801 °C.
Extended: explain — the electrostatic attractions between oppositely charged ions are
**strong** and act **in all directions throughout the giant lattice**, so **a large amount
of energy** is needed to overcome them.

**Electrical conductivity.** Core: describe — poor when solid; good when molten or dissolved
in water. Extended: explain — a current is a flow of charged particles. In the solid, the
ions are **held in fixed positions** in the lattice and cannot move. When the compound is
melted or dissolved, the lattice breaks down and the ions are **free to move** and carry
the charge.

Note that it is the **ions** that move, not electrons. Writing "free electrons" for an
ionic compound is a serious error: that is the explanation for metals and graphite.

## Common mistakes

- **Calling the electron transfer the bond.** The bond is the attraction between the ions.
- **Charges in the wrong place.** Write Ca²⁺, not Ca⁺² or Ca2+ inside the formula. In a
  formula the charges are not shown at all: CaCl₂, not Ca²⁺Cl₂⁻.
- **One chloride ion for calcium.** The ratio must balance electrons lost and gained.
- **Drawing a shared pair** between the ions in an ionic diagram. That is a covalent
  diagram.
- **"Molecules of sodium chloride."** Ionic compounds are lattices of ions.
- **Mixing up the conductivity explanation** with metals: ionic compounds conduct because
  ions move.

## Exam technique: command words

- **State** — a short fact, such as the definition of an ionic bond. No explanation is
  needed.
- **Describe** — say what happens, step by step: "the sodium atom loses one electron to
  form Na⁺; the chlorine atom gains this electron to form Cl⁻".
- **Explain** — give the reason using structure and bonding. A full high-melting-point
  answer names the **lattice**, the **strong attraction between oppositely charged ions**
  and the **large amount of energy** needed.
- **Complete the dot-and-cross diagram** — check the total number of electrons in each
  bracket and the charge on each ion before moving on.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do Core candidates need to draw calcium chloride or magnesium oxide?**
The Core outcome is limited to Group I with Group VII compounds, such as sodium chloride and
lithium fluoride. Diagrams for other metal–non-metal compounds are Supplement content.

**Why does a solid ionic compound not conduct, even though it contains charged particles?**
The ions are held in fixed positions in the lattice, so they cannot move to carry charge.
Melting or dissolving frees them.

**Is an ionic formula the formula of one molecule?**
No. It shows the simplest ratio of ions in the giant lattice.
