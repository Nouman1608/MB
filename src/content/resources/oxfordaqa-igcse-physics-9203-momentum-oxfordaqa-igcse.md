This guide covers **3.1.4 Momentum** (statements a to c) of the OxfordAQA International GCSE Physics (9203) specification, for exams May/June 2018 onwards. The whole section is marked P, so it is assessed in the full Physics award. The [momentum, collisions and road safety guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-physics-momentum-collisions-and-safety-in-road-transport/) sets out the basic method for collisions and explosions; this page goes further into the situations where marks are usually lost: head-on collisions with signs, rebounds where the velocity reverses, recoil, and full qualitative explanations of safety features. Quick recall is in the [Momentum revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-physics-9203-momentum-oxfordaqa-igcse-revision-notes/) and exam-style questions are in the [Momentum practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-physics-9203-momentum-oxfordaqa-igcse-practice/).

## What the specification asks for

| Statement | Content |
|---|---|
| a | All moving objects have momentum: p = m × v |
| b | In a closed system, total momentum before an interaction equals total momentum after (conservation of momentum); calculations involve two objects, in collisions and explosions |
| c | F = Δp ÷ t: force equals the rate of change of momentum; explain qualitatively air bags, seat belts, side impact bars, crumple zones, gymnasium crash mats, cushioned playground surfaces and cycle helmets |

## Momentum is a vector, so signs matter

```
p = m x v     p in kg m/s, m in kg, v in m/s
```

Momentum has the same direction as the velocity. In one-dimensional problems, choose one direction as positive before you write any numbers, and keep that choice for the whole question. A velocity in the other direction is negative, and so is its momentum. Two objects with equal and opposite momenta have a **total** momentum of zero, even though each one is moving.

## What makes a system closed

A closed system is one in which no external force acts on the objects, so the only forces are those the objects exert on each other. Those internal forces come in equal and opposite pairs (Newton's Third Law), so any momentum one object gains, the other loses.

In real situations, friction from the ground or drag from the air is an external force. Questions usually tell you to ignore it, or the collision lasts so short a time that its effect is negligible. If a question asks why measured momenta do not quite match, friction acting during the measurement is the expected reason.

## Head-on collisions

**Worked example 1: two cars that lock together.** Car A, mass 1200 kg, travels right at 15 m/s. Car B, mass 900 kg, travels left at 10 m/s. They collide head-on and move off together. Take right as positive.

```
before: (1200 x 15) + (900 x -10) = 18 000 - 9000 = 9000 kg m/s
after:  (1200 + 900) x v = 2100 v
2100 v = 9000, so v = 4.3 m/s (4.29 m/s) to the right
```

The wreckage moves right because car A had more momentum. Leaving out the minus sign for car B gives 13 m/s, a common wrong answer.

## Rebounds: the change in momentum

When an object bounces back, its velocity reverses, so the change in momentum is **larger** than its momentum before the bounce.

```
change in momentum = final momentum - initial momentum
```

**Worked example 2: a tennis ball hitting a wall.** A ball of mass 0.058 kg hits a wall at 20 m/s and rebounds at 15 m/s. It is in contact with the wall for 0.0040 s. Take the direction towards the wall as positive.

```
initial momentum = 0.058 x 20 = 1.16 kg m/s
final momentum   = 0.058 x (-15) = -0.87 kg m/s
change = -0.87 - 1.16 = -2.03 kg m/s
size of force on the ball = 2.03 / 0.0040 = 507.5 N, about 510 N, away from the wall
```

By Newton's Third Law the ball pushes on the wall with the same size of force, towards the wall. Subtracting 15 from 20 to get 0.29 kg m/s is the classic error: it treats the rebound as if the ball had only slowed down.

## Recoil and explosions

When two objects start at rest and push apart, the total momentum before is zero, so afterwards the two momenta are equal in size and opposite in direction. The lighter object moves faster.

**Worked example 3: skaters pushing apart.** Two ice skaters, of masses 60 kg and 45 kg, stand at rest facing each other and push apart. The 45 kg skater moves off at 2.0 m/s.

```
total before = 0
after: 45 x 2.0 + 60 x v = 0
90 + 60 v = 0, so v = -1.5 m/s
```

The 60 kg skater moves at 1.5 m/s in the opposite direction. Each skater's momentum is 90 kg m/s in size. The same reasoning explains the backward recoil of a cannon or the movement of a small boat when a person jumps off its bow.

## Force as the rate of change of momentum

```
F = change in momentum / time    F = Δp / t
```

F is the average force during the change, in newtons, and t is the time over which the change happens, in seconds. Two ways to change an object's momentum by the same amount are a large force for a short time or a small force for a long time. The safety features in statement c all use the second option.

### Building a full qualitative explanation

Statement c asks you to explain safety features **qualitatively**. A strong answer has four links in order:

1. In the collision or fall, the person's momentum must change to zero (or by a fixed amount).
2. The feature **increases the time** over which this change happens.
3. So the **rate of change of momentum** is smaller.
4. So the **force** on the person is smaller, reducing injury.

Some features add a second effect: an air bag spreads the force over a larger area of the body, and a seat belt holds the person in the seat. These are extra points, not replacements for the four links.

| Feature | How it works |
|---|---|
| Air bag | The bag deflates as the head and chest move into it, lengthening the stopping time |
| Seat belt | The belt stretches a little as the body moves forward, lengthening the stopping time |
| Crumple zone | The front or rear of the car crushes in a controlled way, so the passenger compartment stops over a longer time |
| Side impact bar | A strong bar in the door spreads the force of a side impact through the car body and slows the intrusion of the door, so the occupant's momentum changes more gradually |
| Crash mat | The mat compresses as the gymnast lands |
| Playground surface | Rubber or bark compresses as a child lands |
| Cycle helmet | The foam liner crushes as the head stops |

**Worked example 4: a gymnast landing.** A gymnast of mass 50 kg lands at 6.0 m/s. On a hard floor she stops in 0.050 s; on a crash mat she stops in 0.40 s.

```
change in momentum = 50 x 6.0 = 300 kg m/s
hard floor: F = 300 / 0.050 = 6000 N
crash mat:  F = 300 / 0.40  = 750 N
```

The stopping time is eight times longer on the mat, so the force is eight times smaller.

## Common mistakes

- Dropping the negative sign for an object moving the opposite way.
- Treating a rebound as a slowing down when calculating the change in momentum.
- Saying a safety feature "absorbs the momentum". Momentum is not absorbed; its rate of change is reduced.
- Saying an air bag "reduces the momentum". The change in momentum is the same; the time is longer.
- Forgetting that explosions start with zero total momentum.
- Using a time in milliseconds without converting to seconds.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Calculate** in collisions: state the positive direction, write "momentum before = momentum after", substitute with signs, then solve.
- **Explain** a safety feature: use the four-link chain above with the words "time", "rate of change of momentum" and "force".
- **Describe** an interaction: say what each object does, with directions.
- **Show that**: give every step; the final value is already given, so marks are for the working.
