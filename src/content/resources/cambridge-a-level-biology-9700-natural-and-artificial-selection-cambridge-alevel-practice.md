These questions cover section 17.2 (Natural and artificial selection) of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Biology 9700 syllabus for examination in 2025, 2026 and 2027. This is A Level content, examined in Paper 4. All data are invented for the exercise. The Hardy–Weinberg equations are p + q = 1 and p² + 2pq + q² = 1. Revise first with the [17.2 study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-biology-9700-natural-and-artificial-selection-cambridge-alevel/) and the [17.2 revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-biology-9700-natural-and-artificial-selection-cambridge-alevel-revision-notes/).

## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Which force of natural selection favours individuals at both extremes of a range of phenotypes?

A: Stabilising

B: Directional

C: Disruptive

D: Artificial **[1]**

**2.** State two conditions that must apply for the Hardy–Weinberg principle to be used. **[2]**

**3.** A recessive condition affects 1 in 400 people. Calculate the frequency of the recessive allele and the frequency of carriers. **[2]**

**4.** What is the most likely long-term result of a population bottleneck?

A: Increased mutation rate

B: Reduced genetic diversity

C: More heterozygotes than before

D: Formation of new alleles **[1]**

**5.** A student wrote: "The antibiotic made the bacteria become resistant." Explain why this statement is incorrect. **[2]**

**6.** Explain why F1 hybrid maize plants are vigorous and uniform. **[2]**

### Section B: longer questions

**7.** A population of insects feeds on nectar and shows variation in the length of its mouthparts. After several dry years, only plants with long, deep flowers survive in the area.

**(a)** Explain how natural selection could lead to an increase in the mean length of the insects' mouthparts. **[5]**

**(b)** Name the type of selection shown. **[1]**

**8.** The table shows the percentage survival of babies of different birth masses in a past human population.

| Birth mass / kg | 1.5 | 2.5 | 3.5 | 4.5 | 5.5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Survival / % | 60 | 90 | 98 | 92 | 70 |

**(a)** Identify the type of selection shown by these data. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain the effect of this selection on the range and mean of birth mass over many generations. **[2]**

**9.** A gene has two codominant alleles, A and B. In a sample of 1000 individuals there were 420 AA, 460 AB and 120 BB.

**(a)** Calculate the frequencies of alleles A and B. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the numbers of each genotype expected if the population is in Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium. **[2]**

**(c)** Comment on your results. **[1]**

**10.** A small island population, founded by a few settlers several hundred years ago, has a much higher frequency of an allele for a rare recessive condition than the mainland population it came from. The allele gives no advantage. Explain how this difference could have arisen. **[3]**

**11.**

**(a)** Outline how a variety of wheat that is resistant to a fungal disease and has a high yield could be produced. **[3]**

**(b)** Explain how the milk yield of dairy cattle has been increased by selective breeding. **[3]**

**12.**

**(a)** Explain why maize plants from inbred lines have low yields. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain why farmers growing hybrid maize buy new seed each year instead of saving their own. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** C [1]

**2.** Any two: large population [1]; random mating [1]; no mutation, no migration, no selection (accept any).

**3.** q² = 1 ÷ 400 = 0.0025, so q = **0.05** [1]. p = 0.95, so 2pq = 2 × 0.95 × 0.05 = **0.095** [1].

**4.** B [1]

**5.** The resistance allele arose by a random mutation before or independently of the antibiotic [1]. The antibiotic acts as a selective agent, killing non-resistant bacteria so that resistant ones survive and reproduce [1].

**6.** They are produced by crossing two different inbred lines, so every F1 plant has the same genotype and is uniform [1]. They are heterozygous at many loci, so harmful recessive alleles are masked and the plants are vigorous (hybrid vigour) [1].

**7.**

**(a)** The insects vary in mouthpart length, partly because of different alleles [1]. With only deep flowers available, there is competition for nectar, and insects with longer mouthparts can feed more successfully [1]. They are more likely to survive and reproduce [1]. They pass on the alleles for long mouthparts to their offspring [1]. Over many generations, the frequency of these alleles increases and the mean mouthpart length rises [1].

**(b)** Directional selection [1]

**8.**

**(a)** Stabilising selection [1]

**(b)** Babies of intermediate mass are most likely to survive and reproduce, while those at both extremes are less likely, so the range narrows [1]. The mean stays about the same, near 3.5 kg [1].

**9.**

**(a)** A = ((2 × 420) + 460) ÷ 2000 = 1300 ÷ 2000 = **0.65** [1]; B = 1 − 0.65 = **0.35** [1].

**(b)** AA = 0.65² × 1000 = 422.5 (about 423) and AB = 2 × 0.65 × 0.35 × 1000 = 455 [1]; BB = 0.35² × 1000 = 122.5 (about 123) [1].

**(c)** The observed numbers (420, 460, 120) are very close to the expected numbers, so the population appears to be in Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium for this gene [1].

**10.** The few founders happened, by chance, to carry the allele at a higher frequency than the mainland population: the founder effect [1]. The island population remained small and isolated, so genetic drift could change allele frequencies randomly [1]. Since the allele gives no advantage, its high frequency is due to chance, not natural selection [1].

**11.**

**(a)** Cross a high-yielding variety with a variety (or wild relative) that has alleles for resistance [1]. Expose the offspring to the fungus and select those that are both resistant and high-yielding [1]. Cross these back with the high-yielding variety and repeat the selection over several generations [1].

**(b)** A bull's value cannot be measured directly, so bulls are progeny-tested by recording the milk yields of their daughters [1]. Bulls with high-yielding daughters are used to fertilise high-yielding cows, often by artificial insemination [1]. The best offspring are selected and the process is repeated over many generations, increasing the frequency of alleles for high milk yield [1].

**12.**

**(a)** Inbreeding makes plants homozygous at most loci [1]. Harmful recessive alleles become homozygous and are expressed, so the plants are less vigorous (inbreeding depression) [1].

**(b)** Seed from F1 hybrids produces an F2 generation in which alleles segregate [1]. The F2 plants are genetically variable, so they are not uniform and many lose hybrid vigour [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing that individuals adapt or change during their lifetime.
- Leaving out reproduction and passing on alleles in natural selection answers.
- Saying the antibiotic causes resistance mutations.
- Using p² instead of 2pq for carriers.
- Confusing genetic drift with selection.
- Saying hybrids are homozygous.
