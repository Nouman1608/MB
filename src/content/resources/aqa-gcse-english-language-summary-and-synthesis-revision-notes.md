These notes condense the **summary and synthesis** content of 3.1.1 Critical reading and comprehension in AQA GCSE
English Language (8700): "identifying the main theme or themes; summarising ideas and information from a single
text; synthesising from more than one text". It is assessed by **AO1** (identify and interpret explicit and implicit
information and ideas; select and synthesise evidence from different texts), most directly in the **8-mark summary
question** of Paper 2 Section A.

## The answer in one paragraph

A summary picks out the main ideas of a text in your own words. Synthesis joins ideas from two texts into connected
points. In the Paper 2 summary question, write about the focus named in the question only, make inferences from
short quotations in **both** sources, and link them with comparative connectives. Do not analyse language.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Main theme | What a text is mainly about |
| Summary | A concise account of the key ideas, in your own words |
| Synthesis | Combining information from more than one text into connected points |
| Explicit information | Stated directly in the text |
| Implicit information | Suggested but not stated; found by inference |
| Inference | A reasonable conclusion drawn from evidence |
| Focus | The specific aspect the question asks you to summarise |

## Where it is tested

| Paper and question | Marks | Skill |
|---|---|---|
| Paper 1, short-form question | 4 | Identify information from one source |
| Paper 2, short-form question | 4 | Identify true statements about a source |
| Paper 2, longer-form question | 8 | Summarise differences or similarities across two sources |

Paper 2 sources: one 19th-century text and one 20th- or 21st-century text, non-fiction and literary non-fiction, on a
linked theme.

## Method in steps: the 8-mark summary

```
1. Underline the focus and whether it asks for differences or similarities.
2. Highlight 3-4 relevant details in each source.
3. Pair the details into points of comparison.
4. Write 2-3 paragraphs: point -> Source A evidence + inference -> connective -> Source B evidence + inference.
5. Check: both sources used in every paragraph? Inferences made? No language analysis?
```

## Connectives

| Differences | Similarities |
|---|---|
| whereas, while, in contrast, however, on the other hand, unlike | similarly, likewise, both, in the same way, also |

## Worked reminder: from retelling to inference

- **Retelling (low level):** "Source A says the house was small and Source B says the flat was big."
- **Inference (high level):** "The cramped house in Source A, where 'eight of us shared two rooms', suggests a family
  struggling to survive, whereas the 'spacious' flat in Source B implies comfort and choice."

## Summarising a single text in four steps

1. One-sentence main theme.
2. One key idea per paragraph.
3. Cut examples and repetition.
4. Rewrite in your own words, in order.

## Planning grid (use it in the exam margin)

| Focus point | Source A detail | What it suggests | Source B detail | What it suggests |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Who controls the day | "up at five", "may not sit" | Hours set by employer; low status | "I choose them" | Freedom and choice |
| What work gives back | "what Cook leaves" | Survival only | "pays for my driving lessons" | Independence and a future |

Two or three rows like this are enough for a strong answer. Each row becomes one synthesised paragraph.

## What examiners reward at the top level

Read AQA's published mark schemes for the exact wording; in general, the strongest answers:

- show a **perceptive** understanding of both sources, not just a clear one;
- make **perceptive inferences** from both, linking them in each point;
- use **judicious** (well-chosen, precise) references;
- show **clear connections** between the texts throughout.

Answers that only paraphrase, or treat one source much more fully than the other, stay in the lower levels.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Summary vs synthesis:** one text's main ideas vs connected points from two texts.
- **Explicit vs implicit:** stated vs implied.
- **Summary question vs comparison question:** what the sources show (AO1) vs how writers convey their perspectives
  (AO3).

## Quick self-test

1. Quote the specification's definition of summary and synthesis.
2. Which assessment objective assesses synthesis?
3. How many marks is the Paper 2 summary question worth?
4. Name three connectives for differences.
5. Why should you avoid language analysis in the summary question?
6. Turn this into an inference: "In Source B the children 'laughed and ran to the gate'."

### Answers

1. "Identifying the main theme or themes; summarising ideas and information from a single text; synthesising from
   more than one text."
2. AO1 (select and synthesise evidence from different texts).
3. 8 marks.
4. Any three: whereas, while, in contrast, however, on the other hand, unlike.
5. It is assessed on AO1 (understanding information), so technique analysis earns no credit there and wastes time.
6. For example: the children seem eager and happy to be at school, suggesting school is a welcoming place for them.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Two separate summaries with no links.
- Paraphrasing without inference.
- Using only one source.
- Writing about techniques instead of content.
- Losing the question's focus.
- Long quotations with no comment.

## Frequently asked questions

**Should I analyse language in the 8-mark summary?**
No. The summary question rewards AO1 only: selecting evidence and making inferences about the focus
in the question. Analysis of language belongs to the next question on the paper.

**How many points do I need?**
There is no fixed number. Examiners reward clear, perceptive inferences supported by apt references
to both sources, so two or three well-developed comparative points beat a long list of facts.

**Do I need to compare the two sources?**
Yes. The question asks about similarities or differences, so link the sources with comparative
connectives such as whereas, similarly and in contrast.
