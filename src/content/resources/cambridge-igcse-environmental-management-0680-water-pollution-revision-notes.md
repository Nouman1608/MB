Condensed notes for sub-topic 3.2 of Cambridge IGCSE Environmental Management (0680). For full explanations and worked examples, use the [water pollution study guide](/resources/cambridge-igcse-environmental-management-0680-water-pollution/).

## The five sources (3.2.1) and what each adds

| Source | Main pollutants | Typical route into water |
|---|---|---|
| Domestic waste | Detergents with phosphates, food waste, oils, household chemicals, litter | Drains, dumping near rivers |
| Sewage | Faecal bacteria, organic matter, nutrients | Missing or leaking sewers, overflows in heavy rain |
| Plastic waste | Bottles, bags, packaging, fragments | Litter, landfill, drains |
| Industrial processes | Heavy metals, solvents, acids, toxic chemicals, warm water | Pipes discharging wastewater, mine runoff |
| Agricultural practices | Nitrates and phosphates, manure, slurry, pesticides | Surface runoff and leaching through soil |

Recall tip: sort the sources by what they cause. Pathogens come mainly from sewage; nutrients from farms, sewage and detergents; persistent toxins from industry and pesticides; physical litter from plastics.

## The six impacts (3.2.2) in one line each

- **Cholera**: bacteria in water contaminated by faeces cause severe diarrhoea and dehydration.
- **Accumulation of industrial toxins**: substances that are not broken down build up in water and sediment over the years.
- **Bioaccumulation**: a toxin builds up in a single organism over its lifetime because it is absorbed faster than it is lost.
- **Biomagnification**: the concentration rises at each trophic level as consumers eat many contaminated organisms.
- **Acid rain**: lower pH in rivers and lakes kills fish, eggs and invertebrates; aluminium released from soil harms gills.
- **Eutrophication**: nutrient enrichment from organic content and inorganic ions leads to oxygen loss and death of aquatic animals.

## Eutrophication chain to memorise

Nutrients leach or run off → algal bloom → light blocked, plants below die → algae die → bacteria decompose dead material and multiply → bacterial respiration uses dissolved oxygen → fish and invertebrates die.

With raw sewage, bacteria feed on the organic matter directly, so oxygen drops even without a bloom.

## The three strategies (3.2.3)

| Strategy | What it involves | Tackles | One limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Improved sanitation | Toilets, sewers, sealed septic tanks, latrines sited away from wells | Cholera, organic pollution | Costly network; poorly sited latrines leak into groundwater |
| Treatment of sewage | Screening, settling of solids, bacteria breaking down organic matter with air, optional nutrient removal | Pathogens, oxygen loss, eutrophication | Expensive to run; overflows in storms |
| Pollution control and legislation | Discharge limits, permits, monitoring, fines, pesticide bans, fertiliser rules, buffer strips | Industrial toxins, farm runoff | Depends on enforcement and inspection |

## Key terms

| Term | Definition to learn |
|---|---|
| Pollutant | A substance or energy added to the environment that causes harm |
| Leaching | Soluble ions washed down through the soil by water |
| Runoff | Water flowing over the land surface into rivers, carrying pollutants |
| Nutrient enrichment | Raised levels of nitrates and phosphates in water |
| Algal bloom | Rapid growth of algae covering the water surface |
| Eutrophication | The sequence from nutrient enrichment to oxygen depletion and death of aquatic life |
| Bioaccumulation | Build-up of a toxic substance in one organism over time |
| Biomagnification | Increase in concentration of a toxic substance at each step up a food chain |
| Persistent substance | One that is not broken down by organisms or natural processes |
| Sanitation | Safe, hygienic disposal of human waste |

## Numbers and data skills

- Concentration factor = concentration in organism / concentration in water. Example with invented figures: 12 ppm in a heron and 0.0004 ppm in water gives 12 / 0.0004 = 30,000 times.
- Dissolved oxygen falls downstream of a sewage outfall, then recovers further downstream as the organic matter is used up and water mixes with air. Expect a graph with a dip and a recovery; describe the dip with figures, then explain it with bacterial respiration.
- When a graph shows algae rising before oxygen falls, the time lag is the period when algae grow, die and are decomposed.

## Exam traps

- **Living algae are not the cause of the oxygen crash**. Name bacterial decomposition and respiration.
- **Bioaccumulation is not biomagnification**. Write "one organism, over time" or "food chain, each level" so the examiner sees you know which is which.
- **Leaching is not runoff**. Leaching goes down through soil; runoff goes over the surface. Both carry fertiliser.
- **Persistent toxins harm apex predators most**, not the organisms at the bottom of the chain that first absorb them.
- **Match strategy to source**. Sewage treatment does little for mercury from a factory; legislation and on-site treatment do.
- **Cholera is bacterial**. Do not describe a vector; that belongs to malaria in sub-topic 3.3.

## Self-test

1. Name the five sources of water pollution in the syllabus.
2. State two pollutants released by agricultural practices.
3. What is the difference between leaching and runoff?
4. Define bioaccumulation.
5. Define biomagnification.
6. Why do apex predators carry the highest concentrations of persistent toxins?
7. Which organisms use up the oxygen during eutrophication, and by which process?
8. Why does raw sewage reduce oxygen in a river faster than fertiliser does?
9. How does acid rain affect organisms in lakes?
10. State the three strategies for improving water quality.
11. Give one limitation of pollution legislation.
12. A fish contains 6 ppm of mercury; the water contains 0.002 ppm. How many times more concentrated is the mercury in the fish?

**Answers**

1. Domestic waste, sewage, plastic waste, industrial processes, agricultural practices.
2. Any two: nitrate or phosphate fertiliser, manure or slurry, pesticides.
3. Leaching carries dissolved ions down through the soil; runoff carries water and pollutants over the surface.
4. The build-up of a toxic substance in a single organism over time.
5. The increase in concentration of a toxic substance as it is passed up a food chain.
6. Each consumer eats many contaminated prey and keeps the toxin, so concentration increases at every level and is highest at the top.
7. Bacteria (decomposers), by aerobic respiration as they break down dead algae and plants.
8. Bacteria feed on the organic matter in sewage straight away; fertiliser must first cause algal growth and death.
9. Water becomes more acidic, so fish, eggs and invertebrates die, populations fall and food webs are disrupted; aluminium released from soils damages fish gills.
10. Improved sanitation, treatment of sewage, pollution control and legislation.
11. It needs monitoring and enforcement; where inspectors are few or fines are low, polluters may ignore it.
12. 6 / 0.002 = 3,000 times.
