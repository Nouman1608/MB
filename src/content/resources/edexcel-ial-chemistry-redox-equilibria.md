## 16.1 Oxidation and reduction

Oxidation is loss of electrons, or an increase in oxidation number. Reduction is the reverse. Both definitions work in every block.

### Worked example 1: three blocks in one reaction

In 2Al + 3Cu²⁺ → 2Al³⁺ + 3Cu, aluminium (p-block) goes from 0 to +3: it is oxidised and is the reducing agent. Copper (d-block) goes from +2 to 0: it is reduced. In Ca + Cl₂ → CaCl₂, calcium (s-block) goes from 0 to +2.

## 16.2–16.4 Standard electrode potentials and the hydrogen electrode

The **standard electrode potential**, E⦵, is the voltage a half-cell produces when connected to a standard hydrogen electrode, with both under standard conditions. The conditions are:

- temperature 298 K
- pressure of any gases 100 kPa
- concentration of ions 1.00 mol dm⁻³

In the **standard hydrogen electrode**, platinum coated with platinum black sits in 1.00 mol dm⁻³ H⁺ ions (hydrochloric acid, say) while H₂ at 100 kPa is bubbled past it, all at 298 K. By definition, E⦵ = 0.00 V. The platinum is inert and conducts; the porous coating speeds up the H₂/H⁺ equilibrium.

A reference is needed because a single electrode potential cannot be measured: a voltmeter needs two connections, so it reads a difference. Fixing one half-cell at zero puts every other half-cell on a common scale.

## 16.5 and Core Practical 12: measuring and investigating cells

**(i) Metal or non-metal with its ions.** For a metal, dip a clean strip of the metal into a 1.00 mol dm⁻³ solution of its ions. For a non-metal such as chlorine, bubble the gas at 100 kPa over a platinum electrode in a 1.00 mol dm⁻³ chloride solution.

**(ii) Two ions of one element.** Put a platinum electrode into a solution containing both ions, each at 1.00 mol dm⁻³, for example Fe²⁺ and Fe³⁺. Platinum is needed because neither ion is a solid.

Join the half-cell to the hydrogen electrode (or another half-cell of known E⦵) with a **salt bridge** and a **high-resistance voltmeter**. A salt bridge of filter paper soaked in potassium nitrate lets ions move without the solutions mixing; nitrate ions do not precipitate metal ions, whereas a chloride bridge would precipitate silver chloride in a silver half-cell. The voltmeter draws almost no current, so it reads the emf.

In Core Practical 12 you build several cells and compare readings with values calculated from E⦵ data. Clean metals with emery paper first. Gaps between measured and calculated values usually come from non-standard conditions or an oxide layer.

## 16.7 and 16.8 Cell emf and cell diagrams

**E⦵cell = E⦵(right) − E⦵(left)**, where the right-hand half-cell is the positive electrode (more positive E⦵) and the left-hand half-cell is the negative electrode.

In a cell diagram, a single line is a phase boundary, a comma separates species in one phase, and a double line is the salt bridge. Electrodes go at the outer ends; the left half-cell is written as an oxidation, the right as a reduction.

### Worked example 2: a nickel–bromine cell

Use these values: Ni²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ ⇌ Ni(s), E⦵ = −0.25 V; Br₂(aq) + 2e⁻ ⇌ 2Br⁻(aq), E⦵ = +1.07 V.

1. Bromine has the more positive E⦵, so it is the positive (right) electrode and needs platinum.
2. Cell diagram: **Ni(s) | Ni²⁺(aq) || Br₂(aq), Br⁻(aq) | Pt(s)**
3. E⦵cell = +1.07 − (−0.25) = **+1.32 V**
4. Overall: Ni + Br₂ → Ni²⁺ + 2Br⁻.

## 16.9 Why conditions matter

A half-cell is an equilibrium, such as Ag⁺(aq) + e⁻ ⇌ Ag(s). Changing the concentration of an ion shifts that equilibrium and changes E. Raising [Ag⁺] shifts it to the right, so E becomes more positive; lowering [Ag⁺] does the opposite. Temperature and gas pressure also matter, so a measured E matches E⦵ only under standard conditions.

## 16.10 and 16.11 Feasibility, entropy and K

A reaction is **thermodynamically feasible** if its E⦵cell is positive: E⦵(reduction) − E⦵(oxidation).

### Worked example 3: air and iron(II) solutions

Use these values: O₂ + 4H⁺ + 4e⁻ ⇌ 2H₂O, E⦵ = +1.23 V; Fe³⁺ + e⁻ ⇌ Fe²⁺, E⦵ = +0.77 V.

For oxygen oxidising Fe²⁺ in acid: E⦵cell = +1.23 − (+0.77) = **+0.46 V**. This is positive, so O₂ + 4H⁺ + 4Fe²⁺ → 2H₂O + 4Fe³⁺ is feasible. Iron(II) solutions for titration are therefore made up fresh.

The specification states that E⦵cell is directly proportional to the total entropy change and to ln K. So:

- E⦵cell positive means ΔStotal positive, and K greater than 1.
- E⦵cell negative means ΔStotal negative, and K less than 1.
- A larger positive E⦵cell means a larger K, so the reaction goes further towards completion.

### Worked example 4: comparing two reactions

Reaction P has E⦵cell = +0.20 V and reaction R +0.60 V, with the same number of electrons transferred. As ln K is proportional to E⦵cell, ln K for R is 0.60/0.20 = 3 times ln K for P, so K for R equals K for P cubed.

## 16.12 Limits of the predictions

E⦵ values say whether a reaction can happen, not how fast. A feasible reaction with a high activation energy may not be seen: the system is **kinetically stable**. E⦵ values also assume standard conditions. Concentrated solutions, heat or other gas pressures can change E enough to reverse a prediction, especially when E⦵cell is close to zero.

## 16.13 and 16.14 The electrochemical series and disproportionation

Standard electrode potentials are also called **standard reduction potentials**, as each half-equation is written as a reduction. In order, they form the **electrochemical series**: the strongest oxidising agents are on the left of the most positive half-equations, and the strongest reducing agents on the right of the most negative.

Disproportionation is one species being oxidised and reduced at once. Find the half-equation where the species is reduced and the one where it is the product (reverse this for its oxidation). E⦵cell = E⦵(species reduced) − E⦵(species as product).

### Worked example 5: hydrogen peroxide

Use these values: H₂O₂ + 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ ⇌ 2H₂O, E⦵ = +1.77 V; O₂ + 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ ⇌ H₂O₂, E⦵ = +0.68 V.

1. E⦵cell = +1.77 − (+0.68) = **+1.09 V**, so 2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂ is feasible.
2. Yet the solution keeps for months: without a catalyst the reaction is slow (16.12).

## 16.15–16.17 Redox titrations (Core Practicals 13a and 13b)

**Iron(II) with manganate(VII):** 5Fe²⁺ + MnO₄⁻ + 8H⁺ → 5Fe³⁺ + Mn²⁺ + 4H₂O. MnO₄⁻ : Fe²⁺ is 1 : 5. Acidify with dilute sulfuric acid, not hydrochloric acid, whose chloride ions manganate(VII) would oxidise. It is self-indicating: once all the Fe²⁺ has reacted, one extra drop of manganate(VII) leaves a pale pink colour that persists.

**Iodine with thiosulfate:** 2S₂O₃²⁻ + I₂ → S₄O₆²⁻ + 2I⁻. I₂ : S₂O₃²⁻ is 1 : 2. An oxidising agent releases iodine from excess potassium iodide. Titrate until the brown turns pale yellow, add starch (blue-black), then continue until the blue-black just disappears. Starch added early binds a lot of iodine, which is released slowly and blurs the end point.

### Worked example 6 (structured): iron in a moss killer

5.00 g of moss killer was dissolved in dilute sulfuric acid, giving 250.0 cm³ of solution. Titrating 25.0 cm³ portions needed a mean of 18.45 cm³ of 0.0200 mol dm⁻³ KMnO₄. Find the percentage by mass of iron(II).

1. Moles MnO₄⁻ = 0.0200 × 18.45/1000 = 3.69 × 10⁻⁴ mol
2. Moles Fe²⁺ in 25.0 cm³ = 5 × 3.69 × 10⁻⁴ = 1.845 × 10⁻³ mol
3. Moles Fe²⁺ in 250.0 cm³ = 1.845 × 10⁻² mol
4. Mass Fe = 1.845 × 10⁻² × 55.8 = 1.03 g
5. Percentage = 1.03/5.00 × 100 = **20.6%**

### Worked example 7 (unstructured): copper in brass

1.20 g of brass was dissolved in acid, neutralised and diluted to 250.0 cm³. Excess potassium iodide was added to 25.0 cm³ of this: 2Cu²⁺ + 4I⁻ → 2CuI + I₂. The iodine needed 12.60 cm³ of 0.100 mol dm⁻³ sodium thiosulfate. Find the percentage of copper.

Plan the ratio first: 2Cu²⁺ gives 1 I₂, which needs 2S₂O₃²⁻, so Cu²⁺ : S₂O₃²⁻ = 1 : 1.

- Moles S₂O₃²⁻ = 0.100 × 12.60/1000 = 1.26 × 10⁻³ mol = moles Cu²⁺ in 25.0 cm³
- Moles Cu in 250.0 cm³ = 1.26 × 10⁻² mol; mass = 1.26 × 10⁻² × 63.5 = 0.800 g
- Percentage = 0.800/1.20 × 100 = **66.7%**

### Worked example 8: uncertainty and validity (16.16)

For worked example 6, take each burette reading as ±0.05 cm³, pipette ±0.06 cm³, flask ±0.30 cm³, and each of two balance readings ±0.005 g.

| Measurement | Uncertainty | Percentage |
|---|---|---|
| Titre 18.45 cm³ | ±0.10 cm³ (two readings) | 0.54% |
| Pipette 25.0 cm³ | ±0.06 cm³ | 0.24% |
| Flask 250.0 cm³ | ±0.30 cm³ | 0.12% |
| Mass 5.00 g | ±0.01 g | 0.20% |
| **Total** | | **1.10%** |

1.10% of 20.6% is about ±0.23 percentage points. A label claim of 22.0% differs by 1.4 percentage points, far more than this. The apparatus cannot explain it, so look for a systematic cause, such as air oxidising some iron(II) first. The titre contributes most, so a larger titre (more dilute KMnO₄) would cut the uncertainty most.

## 16.18 and 16.19 Fuel cells

A fuel cell uses the energy released when a fuel reacts with oxygen to generate a voltage. Fuel and oxygen are fed in continuously at separate electrodes. Hydrogen, methanol and other hydrogen-rich fuels are used.

| Electrolyte | Negative electrode | Positive electrode |
|---|---|---|
| Acidic | H₂ → 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ | O₂ + 4H⁺ + 4e⁻ → 2H₂O |
| Alkaline | H₂ + 2OH⁻ → 2H₂O + 2e⁻ | O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻ → 4OH⁻ |

Both give 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O overall.

## Common errors

- Subtracting E⦵ values the wrong way round, giving a negative emf for a cell that works.
- Multiplying E⦵ when a half-equation is scaled up. E⦵ never changes.
- Leaving platinum out of an ion/ion half-cell.
- Forgetting the ×5 for Fe²⁺, the ×2 for thiosulfate, or the scale-up to the full flask.
- Adding starch too early.
