## Key definitions

- **Balanced equation**: formulas of all reactants and products, with coefficients that make the number of atoms of each element equal on both sides. The coefficients give the **mole ratio**.
- **State symbols**: (s) solid, (l) liquid, (g) gas, (aq) aqueous solution. The guide expects them in your equations.
- **Limiting reactant**: the reactant that is completely used up first. It fixes the maximum amount of product.
- **Excess reactant**: a reactant present in more than the amount needed; some remains at the end.
- **Theoretical yield**: the amount of product calculated from the limiting reactant, assuming complete conversion and no losses.
- **Experimental yield**: the amount of product actually obtained.
- **Percentage yield**: experimental yield as a percentage of theoretical yield.
- **Atom economy**: the molar mass of the desired product as a percentage of the total molar mass of all reactants in the balanced equation. It measures efficiency in green chemistry.

## Formulas

| Quantity | Relationship | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Amount from mass | n = m/M | m in g, M in g mol⁻¹ |
| Amount in solution | n = cV | c in mol dm⁻³, V in dm³ (cm³ ÷ 1000) |
| Amount of gas | n = V/Vm | Vm = 22.7 dm³ mol⁻¹ at STP (given as data on this page) |
| Gas to gas | volume ratio = mole ratio | only for gases at the same temperature and pressure |
| Percentage yield | (experimental ÷ theoretical) × 100 | same units top and bottom |
| Atom economy | (M of desired product ÷ total M of reactants) × 100 | multiply each M by its coefficient |

The guide notes that the atom economy equation is given in the data booklet, and that relative atomic masses to two decimal places from the data booklet should be used in calculations.

## Method in steps

**Balancing (Reactivity 2.1.1)**

1. Correct formulas first; never alter a subscript.
2. Balance elements found in one substance per side, then H, then O (or a free element) last.
3. Keep unchanged polyatomic ions together.
4. Lowest whole numbers; add state symbols.

**Reacting quantities (Reactivity 2.1.2)**

1. Known quantity → moles.
2. Moles of wanted = moles of known × (wanted coefficient ÷ known coefficient).
3. Moles of wanted → mass, volume or concentration.

**Limiting reactant (Reactivity 2.1.3)**

1. Moles of each reactant.
2. Divide each by its coefficient. The smallest value is the limiting reactant.
3. Base every product calculation on the limiting reactant.
4. Excess left = moles available − moles used.

**Percentage yield (Reactivity 2.1.4)**

1. Identify the limiting reactant.
2. Theoretical yield from its moles and the ratio.
3. Divide the experimental yield by the theoretical yield, then multiply by 100.

**Atom economy (Reactivity 2.1.5)**

1. Write the balanced equation for the chosen route.
2. Total the molar masses of all reactants, each times its coefficient.
3. Divide the molar mass of the wanted product (times its coefficient) by that total.

## Worked reminders

**Quick limiting check.** 2Na(s) + Cl₂(g) → 2NaCl(s) with 0.30 mol Na and 0.20 mol Cl₂. Na: 0.30 ÷ 2 = 0.15. Cl₂: 0.20 ÷ 1 = 0.20. Sodium gives the smaller value, so it is limiting: 0.30 mol NaCl forms and 0.20 − 0.15 = 0.05 mol Cl₂ is left.

**Gas volumes without Vm.** N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) → 2NH₃(g) at constant temperature and pressure: 15 cm³ of nitrogen reacts with 45 cm³ of hydrogen to give at most 30 cm³ of ammonia. You only need the coefficients.

**Atom economy in one line.** For any reaction where the only product is the one you want, atom economy is 100 %. Each by-product lowers it.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Mole ratio vs mass ratio.** The equation gives a mole ratio. 1 g of one reactant never reacts with "2 g" of another just because the coefficient is 2.
- **Limiting vs excess.** The limiting reactant is not always the one with the smaller mass, or even the smaller number of moles; divide by coefficients first.
- **Theoretical vs experimental yield.** Theoretical comes from calculation; experimental comes from the balance.
- **Below 100 % vs above 100 %.** Losses, side reactions and incomplete reaction push the yield down. A wet or impure product can push the recorded yield above 100 %.
- **Percentage yield vs atom economy.** Yield depends on how well the reaction was carried out. Atom economy depends only on the equation and cannot be improved by better technique.
- **Atom economy vs waste.** They are inversely related: the lower the atom economy, the larger the share of reactant mass that ends up as by-products.
- **(aq) vs (l).** A solution in water is (aq); a pure liquid such as water itself or ethanol is (l).

## Quick self-test

Use these Ar values: H 1.01, O 16.00, S 32.07, K 39.10, Cu 63.55, Zn 65.38. Vm = 22.7 dm³ mol⁻¹ at STP.

1. Write a balanced equation, with state symbols, for the complete combustion of liquid pentane, C₅H₁₂, with products at room temperature.
2. Aqueous silver nitrate is mixed with aqueous potassium bromide. A precipitate of silver bromide forms. Write the equation with state symbols.
3. Copper(II) oxide is heated in hydrogen: CuO(s) + H₂(g) → Cu(s) + H₂O(g). Calculate the mass of copper made from 7.95 g of CuO.
4. 0.654 g of zinc reacts with excess hydrochloric acid: Zn(s) + 2HCl(aq) → ZnCl₂(aq) + H₂(g). Calculate the volume of hydrogen at STP, in cm³.
5. N₂(g) + O₂(g) → 2NO(g). What volume of NO forms from 40 cm³ of N₂ with excess O₂, all at the same conditions?
6. 22.50 cm³ of KOH(aq), concentration 0.120 mol dm⁻³, neutralises 15.00 cm³ of sulfuric acid: 2KOH(aq) + H₂SO₄(aq) → K₂SO₄(aq) + 2H₂O(l). Find [H₂SO₄].
7. For 2SO₂(g) + O₂(g) → 2SO₃(g), 0.50 mol SO₂ is mixed with 0.20 mol O₂. Identify the limiting reactant and the maximum amount of SO₃.
8. State the difference between theoretical yield and experimental yield.
9. A preparation has a theoretical yield of 12.4 g. The student collects 9.30 g. Calculate the percentage yield.
10. Hydrogen can be made by electrolysis of water: 2H₂O(l) → 2H₂(g) + O₂(g). Calculate the atom economy for hydrogen.
11. Explain why the addition of HBr to propene has an atom economy of 100 %.

### Answers

1. C₅H₁₂(l) + 8O₂(g) → 5CO₂(g) + 6H₂O(l)
2. AgNO₃(aq) + KBr(aq) → AgBr(s) + KNO₃(aq)
3. M(CuO) = 79.55 g mol⁻¹; n = 7.95 ÷ 79.55 = 0.09994 mol; 1 : 1 ratio; m(Cu) = 0.09994 × 63.55 = **6.35 g**
4. n(Zn) = 0.654 ÷ 65.38 = 0.01000 mol = n(H₂); V = 0.01000 × 22.7 = 0.227 dm³ = **227 cm³**
5. Ratio N₂ : NO = 1 : 2, so **80 cm³**
6. n(KOH) = 0.120 × 0.02250 = 0.002700 mol; n(H₂SO₄) = 0.001350 mol; [H₂SO₄] = 0.001350 ÷ 0.01500 = **0.0900 mol dm⁻³**
7. SO₂: 0.50 ÷ 2 = 0.25; O₂: 0.20 ÷ 1 = 0.20. **O₂ is limiting**; n(SO₃) = 2 × 0.20 = **0.40 mol** (0.10 mol SO₂ left over)
8. Theoretical yield is calculated from the limiting reactant assuming complete reaction and no loss; experimental yield is the amount actually obtained.
9. 9.30 ÷ 12.4 × 100 = **75.0 %**
10. Desired: 2 × 2.02 = 4.04; reactants: 2 × 18.02 = 36.04; atom economy = 4.04 ÷ 36.04 × 100 = **11.2 %**
11. There is only one product, so every atom in the reactants ends up in it and no mass is lost as by-product.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Changing a formula, such as writing KOH₂, to make an equation balance.
- Missing or wrong state symbols, for example (aq) for a precipitate or (g) for water at room temperature.
- Using the ratio upside down: the wanted coefficient goes on top.
- Plugging cm³ straight into n = cV.
- Using the molar volume for a gas-to-gas comparison where the volume ratio alone was enough, then rounding twice.
- Picking the limiting reactant by comparing masses rather than moles divided by coefficients.
- Basing a yield on the excess reactant.
- Mixing grams and moles in the percentage yield fraction.
- Forgetting coefficients when totalling reactant molar masses for atom economy.
- Saying a by-product that is sold raises the atom economy; the number comes only from the equation.
