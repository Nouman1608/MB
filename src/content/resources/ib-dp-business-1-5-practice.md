## Section A: Short-answer questions

**1.** Define the term *economies of scale*. **[2]**

**2.** Explain one advantage of a strategic alliance compared with a joint venture. **[2]**

**3.** Explain one external economy of scale that a software firm might gain from being located in a city with many other software firms. **[2]**

**4.** Garrow Paints makes tins of paint. At 10,000 tins a month its fixed costs are 30,000 and its variable cost is 4.00 per tin. At 25,000 tins a month, fixed costs are 45,000 and a bulk discount cuts variable cost to 3.60 per tin.

**(a)** Calculate the unit cost at each output. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the percentage fall in unit cost. **[1]**

**(c)** Identify the two economies of scale shown, and the saving per tin from each. **[2]**

**5.** State two internal diseconomies of scale. **[2]**

**6.** Outline one reason why a business might choose internal growth rather than external growth. **[2]**

## Section B: Structured questions

**7.** Morvane Insurance has grown from one office to six, and its management layers have increased from three to six. It employs 900 staff. Annual staff turnover has risen from 8% to 19%. Replacing one employee costs 6,000.

**(a)** Calculate the extra annual cost caused by the rise in staff turnover. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain two diseconomies of scale that Morvane Insurance may be experiencing. **[4]**

**8.** Elspeth Clocks is a three-person business that repairs antique clocks for private collectors. A department store has offered a contract for 2,000 new clocks a year. Accepting would need a workshop four times larger and ten new staff. Discuss whether Elspeth Clocks should accept the contract and grow. **[6]**

**9.** Dunmore Cycles makes bicycles and wants to launch electric bicycles, but it has no battery expertise. It is considering three options: acquiring the battery maker BattTec for 20m; a 50:50 joint venture with the battery firm Voltra, to which Dunmore would contribute 6m; or a strategic alliance in which Voltra supplies batteries and the two firms market e-bikes together.

**(a)** The joint venture is expected to earn 2.4m a year. Calculate how many years it would take Dunmore to recover its contribution from its share of the profit. **[2]**

**(b)** Recommend which option Dunmore Cycles should choose. **[8]**

**10.** Tomas can buy a franchise of a well-known bakery brand or open an independent bakery.

| | Franchise | Independent |
|---|---|---|
| Up-front fee | 40,000 | none |
| Royalty | 8% of sales | none |
| Expected annual sales | 450,000 | 330,000 |
| Gross margin | 50% | 50% |
| Other annual costs | 150,000 | 150,000 |

**(a)** Calculate the profit of each option in the first year. **[4]**

**(b)** Calculate the profit of each option in the second year. **[1]**

**(c)** Explain two non-financial factors Tomas should consider. **[4]**

**11.** Explain two reasons why a hostile takeover may cost the buyer more than a friendly merger. **[4]**

**12.** Explain why a franchisor's brand is at risk when it grows by franchising, and outline one way to reduce this risk. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** Falls in average (unit) cost [1] that result from an increase in the scale of output [1]. **[2]**

**2.** A strategic alliance does not create a new legal entity [1], so it is quicker and cheaper to set up and easier to end if it does not work, whereas a joint venture must be formed and later unwound [1]. **[2]**

**3.** A pool of skilled software workers develops in the city [1], so the firm can recruit trained staff more quickly and with lower training costs [1]. (Specialist suppliers or shared infrastructure also earn credit.) **[2]**

**4. (a)** 10,000 tins: 4.00 + 30,000/10,000 = **7.00** [1]. 25,000 tins: 3.60 + 45,000/25,000 = 3.60 + 1.80 = **5.40** [1]

**(b)** (7.00 − 5.40)/7.00 × 100 = **22.9%** [1]

**(c)** Purchasing economy: 4.00 to 3.60, saving **0.40** per tin [1]. Technical (spreading fixed costs) economy: 3.00 to 1.80, saving **1.20** per tin [1]

*Examiner insight:* fixed costs rose in total, but fell per tin. Spotting that difference is what the calculation is testing.

**5.** Any two: poor communication through more layers [1]; weak coordination between sites or departments; falling motivation and higher staff turnover [1]. **[2]**

**6.** Internal growth keeps the existing culture and control [1], avoiding the clashes and integration costs of combining with another business. (Lower risk of overpaying, or pacing growth to retained profit, also earns credit.) [1] **[2]**

**7. (a)** Extra leavers = (19% − 8%) × 900 = 99 [1]. Extra cost = 99 × 6,000 = **594,000** a year [1]

**(b)** Communication: doubling the management layers from three to six means messages pass through more people [1], so decisions are slower and errors are found later, raising costs [1]. Motivation: staff in a six-office firm may feel remote from decision-makers [1], which the rise in turnover to 19% suggests, adding 594,000 a year in replacement costs [1]. **[4]**

**8.** Award up to 6 marks for balanced discussion linked to the case, with a judgement. For example:

- The contract gives a large, steady order, raising revenue and spreading fixed costs [1].
- But mass-producing new clocks is a different activity from expert antique repair, and the firm's reputation rests on personal service to collectors [1].
- A workshop four times larger and ten new staff mean heavy fixed costs and possibly debt [1], so losing the single department store customer would leave the firm exposed [1].
- The owners may value control and craft over size, a common reason to stay small [1].
- Judgement: Elspeth Clocks should probably decline, or negotiate a smaller trial order, unless it can keep its repair niche separate and secure a long contract [1]. **[6]**

**9. (a)** Dunmore's share = 50% × 2.4m = 1.2m [1]. 6m / 1.2m = **5 years** [1]

**(b)** Award up to 8 marks for a reasoned recommendation using the case. Credit points such as:

- Acquisition gives Dunmore full control of battery know-how and supply [1], but 20m is over three times the joint venture outlay, and the risk is carried alone [1].
- An acquisition may bring a culture clash between a bicycle maker and a battery maker [1].
- The joint venture brings Voltra's expertise and shares risk, with a 5-year recovery [1], but control and profit are shared and disputes may arise [1].
- The alliance needs little capital and is quick and easy to leave [1], but Dunmore gains little expertise and depends on Voltra for a key component [1].
- Judgement: the joint venture balances expertise, cost and risk for a firm entering an unfamiliar product; the acquisition would suit only if Dunmore sees batteries as core to its future and can raise 20m [1]. **[8]**

*Examiner insight:* a recommendation earns the top marks when it compares all the options on criteria from the case (expertise, cost, control, risk) and states what would change the choice.

**10. (a)** Franchise: gross profit 0.5 × 450,000 = 225,000; royalty 0.08 × 450,000 = 36,000 [1]; profit before the fee = 225,000 − 36,000 − 150,000 = 39,000, and after the 40,000 fee, **−1,000** (a loss) [1]. Independent: gross profit 0.5 × 330,000 = 165,000 [1]; profit = 165,000 − 150,000 = **15,000** [1]

**(b)** Franchise **39,000**; independent **15,000** [1]

**(c)** Independence: as a franchisee Tomas must follow the brand's menu, prices and shop design [1], so he loses the freedom to adapt to local tastes [1]. Brand reputation: the franchise brings a recognised name that attracts customers [1], but a scandal elsewhere in the chain could damage his sales through no fault of his own [1]. **[4]**

**11.** The target's board resists, so the buyer usually has to offer shareholders a higher price per share (a premium) to persuade them to sell [1], raising the cost of gaining control [1]. Managers and staff of the target may be hostile after the deal [1], so morale falls and key staff leave, increasing integration costs [1]. **[4]**

**12.** Each franchisee runs its own outlet, so poor service or quality at one outlet can harm the reputation of the whole brand [1][1]. The franchisor can reduce the risk by setting strict standards in the contract and inspecting outlets regularly [1]. **[3]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calculating unit cost but not linking the fall to a named economy of scale.
- Assuming higher total fixed costs mean higher unit cost.
- Discussing growth without the owners' objectives or the firm's niche.
- Comparing growth methods on cost alone, ignoring control, expertise and risk.
- Forgetting the up-front fee in a franchisee's first-year profit.
- Ending a "recommend" or "discuss" answer without a clear judgement.
