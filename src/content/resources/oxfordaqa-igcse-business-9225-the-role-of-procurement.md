This guide covers section **3.3.2 The role of procurement** of OxfordAQA International GCSE Business (9225), part of Topic 3, Business operations. The specification asks you to evaluate the use of **just in time (JIT)** stock management for a given business, recognising that the benefit of reduced costs must be balanced against **the cost of more frequent deliveries and lost purchasing economies of scale**; to understand the benefits of holding **spare (buffer) stock** to meet demand against the cost of holding it; and to analyse the **factors that affect the choice of supplier**, including **price, quality and reliability**. You will not be asked to draw or interpret stock control charts. All businesses and figures in this guide are invented.

For the whole topic, see the [Business Operations study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-business-business-operations/). Condensed notes are in the [revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-business-9225-the-role-of-procurement-revision-notes/) and questions are in the [practice set](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-business-9225-the-role-of-procurement-practice/).

## What is procurement?

**Procurement** is the process of finding and buying the materials, components and services a business needs. It includes choosing suppliers, negotiating prices, ordering and arranging delivery. Good procurement keeps costs down, makes sure production never stops for lack of supplies and protects the quality of the final product.

Two decisions are central: how much stock to hold and who to buy from.

## Managing stock

**Stock** (inventory) includes raw materials, part-finished goods and finished goods waiting to be sold. Holding stock has benefits and costs.

| Benefits of holding stock | Costs of holding stock |
|---|---|
| Production or sales are not interrupted by a late delivery | Storage space, rent, heating and security |
| Can meet a sudden rise in demand | Money is tied up in stock rather than used elsewhere |
| Bulk buying may earn discounts | Risk of damage, theft, waste and goods becoming out of date |
| Protection against price rises | Insurance costs |

A business has to find a balance between holding too much stock and too little.

### Just in time (JIT)

In **JIT**, materials are delivered only when they are needed in production, and finished products are made only when ordered. The business holds very little stock.

| Benefits of JIT | Drawbacks of JIT |
|---|---|
| Lower storage and handling costs | More frequent deliveries, so higher delivery costs |
| Less money tied up in stock | Lost bulk-buying discounts (purchasing economies of scale) |
| Less waste, damage or out-of-date stock | A late delivery can stop production |
| Frees up space for production | Cannot meet a sudden rise in demand quickly |
| Fresh materials, so better quality in some products | Needs reliable suppliers and good communication |

The specification stresses balance: a saving in holding costs must be set against the extra costs of frequent deliveries and the loss of bulk discounts.

**Worked example 1: is JIT worthwhile?** Kestrel Kettles needs 1,200 heating elements a month.

- **Bulk buying:** one delivery a month. Price $5.00 per element and a delivery charge of $100. Average stock held is 600 elements, costing $0.50 per element per month to hold.
- **JIT:** four deliveries a month. Price $5.40 per element (smaller orders get no discount) and a delivery charge of $100 each. Average stock is 150 elements.

```
Bulk: purchase 1,200 x $5.00 = $6,000; delivery $100;       total = $6,100
JIT:  purchase 1,200 x $5.40 = $6,480; deliveries 4 x $100 = $400; total = $6,880
Extra cost of JIT = $6,880 - $6,100 = $780

Holding cost, bulk = 600 x $0.50 = $300
Holding cost, JIT  = 150 x $0.50 = $75
Saving on holding  = $300 - $75 = $225

Net extra cost of JIT = $780 - $225 = $555 a month
```

For Kestrel, JIT costs $555 a month more. It would make sense only if other benefits, such as released storage space that could be rented out or less waste, are worth more than $555 a month.

JIT is more suitable for businesses with reliable local suppliers, valuable or perishable stock, and steady demand, such as a restaurant that buys fresh ingredients daily. It is less suitable where suppliers are unreliable, are far away or where demand is unpredictable.

### Buffer stock

**Buffer stock** is a minimum quantity of stock kept as a safety margin. If a delivery is late or demand is higher than expected, the buffer stock lets production or sales continue.

**Worked example 2: how much buffer?** A factory uses 100 elements a day. Deliveries usually take 4 days after ordering. If a supplier is occasionally 3 days late, the factory could use 100 x 3 = 300 elements while waiting. A buffer stock of 300 elements costs 300 x $0.40 = $120 a month to hold if each element costs $0.40 a month. If one day's lost production costs $1,500, then one stoppage costs as much as 12.5 months of holding the buffer ($1,500 / $120 = 12.5). Here the buffer is cheap insurance.

| Benefits of buffer stock | Costs of buffer stock |
|---|---|
| Avoids stopping production if a delivery is late | Storage and insurance costs |
| Meets sudden rises in demand | Money tied up in stock |
| Customers are not kept waiting, which protects the reputation | Items may go out of date or be damaged |
| Allows time to solve problems with suppliers | May hide inefficiency by making problems less visible |

A buffer stock is a compromise: businesses using JIT often keep a small buffer, and businesses holding large stocks may reduce them to cut costs.

## Choosing a supplier

Businesses buy from suppliers who meet their needs at an acceptable cost. The specification names three main factors.

| Factor | What to consider |
|---|---|
| Price | The cost per unit, delivery charges, discounts for large orders and credit terms. The cheapest supplier is not always the best |
| Quality | Whether materials meet the required standard and have few defects. Poor materials lead to poor products, returns and loss of reputation |
| Reliability | Whether the supplier delivers on time and in the right amounts. A late delivery can stop production |

Other factors can include the supplier's location, how quickly they respond to problems, their environmental and ethical standards, and whether they offer credit.

**Worked example 3: comparing suppliers.** Kestrel needs 10,000 heating elements a year, delivered in 20 batches. Supplier A charges $4.00 per element, and is expected to deliver 2 of the 20 batches late. Supplier B charges $4.40, and is expected to deliver 0.2 batches late on average (a 1% late rate). A late batch stops production and costs $4,000.

```
Supplier A: 10,000 x $4.00 = $40,000 + 2 late batches x $4,000 = $8,000  -> $48,000
Supplier B: 10,000 x $4.40 = $44,000 + 0.2 late batches x $4,000 = $800  -> $44,800
```

Supplier B is dearer per element ($0.40 more) but cheaper overall because it is more reliable: $44,800 against $48,000. This shows why price should not be the only factor. The late-delivery figures are expected averages, not guaranteed results.

If quality also differs, add the cost of defective items. If A's elements are 2% defective and B's 0.5%, then A gives 9,800 usable elements and B gives 9,950, which raises A's cost per usable element to $48,000 / 9,800 = $4.90 and B's to $44,800 / 9,950 = $4.50.

## Putting it together in a recommendation

When the question asks you to evaluate a stock or supplier decision for a business:

1. Identify the type of business: perishable or durable stock, steady or uncertain demand, local or distant suppliers.
2. Compare the costs: holding, delivery, lost discounts, cost of a stoppage.
3. Consider the risks: late delivery, shortage, waste.
4. Decide and justify, saying what would change your mind.

## Worked exam-style answer

**Question (6 marks):** Explain why a business may choose a dearer supplier.

**Answer:** The cheapest supplier may deliver late or send faulty parts. If Supplier A charges $4.00 but is expected to be late twice a year, and each late delivery stops production at a cost of $4,000, its expected cost for 10,000 units is $40,000 plus $8,000, or $48,000. Supplier B charges $4.40 and is expected to be late only 0.2 times a year, so its expected cost is $44,000 plus $800, or $44,800. B is cheaper overall, because it is more reliable. B's higher quality may also reduce waste and returns. Therefore, price should be weighed against reliability and quality.

## Common mistakes

- Treating JIT as an automatic advantage. It saves holding costs but adds delivery costs and risk.
- Forgetting lost purchasing economies of scale when smaller orders are placed.
- Confusing buffer stock with stock in general. Buffer stock is the safety margin.
- Choosing the cheapest supplier without checking quality and reliability.
- Writing "the stock might run out" without saying what happens to production, customers or profit.
- Drawing stock control charts. The specification says they will not be asked for.

## Exam technique for this section

For **state** questions, name the stock method or supplier factor. For **explain** questions, link the method to a cost or benefit: "JIT reduces storage costs because the business holds very little stock". For **calculate** questions, show each cost separately before totalling. For **evaluate** questions, say which type of business JIT or a buffer suits, balance cost against risk, and give a judgement based on the facts given.
