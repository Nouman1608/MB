Section 9.2 of Cambridge International A Level Sociology (9699), *Theories of the media and
influences on media content*, is the most theory-heavy part of Paper 4's Media section. The
syllabus lists seven points: pluralist theories; Marxist and neo-Marxist theories; factors that
influence the selection and presentation of news; the concepts of mass manipulation and hegemony as
different ways of understanding the production of media content; the postmodernist contribution;
censorship as a factor influencing content; and how the media may influence the political
process, including agenda setting, opinion polls and news reporting.

## Pluralist theories in more depth

Pluralism treats the media as a market place of ideas. Its argument rests on four claims:

1. **Consumer sovereignty.** Audiences choose; outlets that ignore their wishes lose readers,
   viewers and advertisers.
2. **Diversity of outlets.** Many competing media represent many groups and opinions.
3. **Professional journalism.** Journalists value accuracy and independence, and editors resist
   interference.
4. **Regulation and public service.** Laws and public-service broadcasting protect balance. The
   BBC's founding aim under John Reith, to "inform, educate and entertain", is the classic
   statement of this ideal.

Pluralists therefore see the media as a **mirror**: content reflects what audiences want and what
is happening in society.

**Evaluation.** Critics reply that ownership is concentrated, that audiences can only choose from
what is offered, and that commercial pressure favours cheap, popular content over diversity.
Pluralism is stronger as a description of public-service broadcasting than of commercial markets.

## Marxist and neo-Marxist theories

### The manipulative (instrumental) approach

Owners deliberately use the media to spread ideas that serve their class interests. Supporters
cite proprietors who appoint sympathetic editors or intervene in editorial lines. Harold Evans,
editor of *The Times*, resigned in 1982 after disputes with its owner Rupert Murdoch, and later
described the conflict in *Good Times, Bad Times* (1983).

### The political economy approach

Peter Golding and Graham Murdock argued that economic structure, not owners' personal views,
shapes content. Media firms must make profits, so they depend on advertising, cut costs and avoid
content that might alienate advertisers or large audiences. The result is a narrow range of views
without anyone giving orders. This approach sits between the manipulative and hegemonic models.

### The hegemonic (neo-Marxist) approach

Following Antonio Gramsci, hegemony is the dominance of ruling-class ideas through consent rather
than force. Journalists, often from similar social backgrounds, share assumptions that make
certain views seem like common sense and others seem extreme. The Glasgow University Media Group's
*Bad News* (1976), *More Bad News* (1980) and *Really Bad News* (1982) found that television
news on industrial disputes tended to give more weight to management's account.

## Mass manipulation versus hegemony

| | Mass manipulation | Hegemony |
|---|---|---|
| **Source of bias** | Owners' deliberate intervention | Shared assumptions of journalists and editors |
| **Mechanism** | Direct control: hiring, firing, editorial instructions | Routine news values, sources and "common sense" |
| **Audience** | Passive, accepts the message | Consents, but can resist; hegemony must be constantly maintained |
| **Key thinkers** | Miliband; Herman and Chomsky's propaganda model | Gramsci; Glasgow University Media Group; Stuart Hall |
| **Weakness** | Little direct evidence of owners' orders in most newsrooms | Hard to prove what journalists take for granted |

The syllabus asks you to see these as *different ways of understanding the production of content*:
one locates power in individuals, the other in culture and routine.

## What influences the selection and presentation of news?

- **News values.** Galtung and Ruge's list (1965) was updated by Tony Harcup and Deirdre O'Neill
  (2001, revised 2017), who added values such as celebrity, entertainment and, for online news,
  shareability.
- **Gatekeeping.** David Manning White's "Mr Gates" study (1950) showed a wire editor rejecting
  stories partly on personal judgement.
- **Primary definers.** Stuart Hall and colleagues (*Policing the Crisis*, 1978) argued that
  journalists rely on powerful, accredited sources such as police and politicians, whose framing
  of an issue becomes the starting point for coverage.
- **Churnalism.** Nick Davies' *Flat Earth News* (2008) argued that under time and budget pressure
  journalists recycle press releases and agency copy rather than investigate.
- **Framing.** Robert Entman (1993) described framing as selecting some aspects of reality and
  making them more salient, which suggests a particular definition of the problem, cause and remedy.
- **Commercial pressures, owners and practical constraints** (deadlines, budgets, location) also
  shape content.

## The postmodernist contribution

Postmodernists argue that in media-saturated societies the distinction between reality and its
representation breaks down. Jean Baudrillard's *The Gulf War Did Not Take Place* (1991)
argued that for Western audiences the 1991 Gulf War existed mainly as a televised spectacle of
images and commentary. Audiences are fragmented, meanings multiply, and no single class or owner
can impose a dominant ideology.

**Contribution:** explains fragmentation, the role of images and the active construction of
meaning. **Limitation:** underplays the concentrated economic power of media corporations, which
Marxists and political economists emphasise.

## Censorship as an influence on content

| Type | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **State censorship** | Government prevents publication | Blocking of foreign websites by China's internet controls |
| **Legal limits** | Laws restrict what can be published | Defamation, privacy and official secrets laws |
| **Voluntary systems** | Advisory agreements with the state | The UK's DSMA-Notice system on national security |
| **Regulation** | Standards enforced by regulators | Broadcast impartiality rules |
| **Commercial censorship** | Avoiding content that might offend advertisers | Dropping stories that criticise major advertisers |
| **Self-censorship** | Journalists anticipate owners', advertisers' or states' reactions | Avoiding topics that risk legal action |

Censorship supports both Marxist views (protecting powerful interests) and pluralist ones (laws
made by elected governments to protect individuals).

## How the media may influence the political process

- **Agenda setting** — telling people what to think about, by choosing which issues to cover.
- **Framing** — telling people how to think about an issue by emphasising certain causes and
  solutions.
- **Opinion polls** — published polls can create a **bandwagon** effect (support for the party
  shown winning) or an **underdog** effect; they also shape coverage as a "horse race".
- **News reporting** — partisan newspapers may support parties openly. After the UK general
  election of 1992, *The Sun*'s headline "It's The Sun Wot Won It" claimed credit for the result,
  though researchers dispute how much the press actually changes votes.
- **Social media** — targeted advertising and the spread of misleading content.

**Evaluation:** media influence may be greater on undecided voters and on which issues are
discussed than on settled loyalties; people also choose media that already match their views.

## Common mistakes

- Treating all Marxists as instrumentalists; distinguish manipulation, political economy and
  hegemony.
- Listing news values without saying how they shape *which* stories run and *how*.
- Confusing agenda setting (what to think about) with framing (how to think about it).
- Writing about postmodernism without saying what it *contributes*, which is the syllabus wording.
- Treating censorship as only state censorship.

## Exam technique

- For **evaluate** questions on theory, set up a contrast (pluralism versus Marxism, or
  manipulation versus hegemony) and use evidence on news production to judge between them.
- For the political process bullet, separate the three named mechanisms: agenda setting, opinion
  polls and news reporting.
- Link back to 9.1: ownership trends are evidence for Marxist theories; audience choice and
  public-service broadcasting are evidence for pluralism.
