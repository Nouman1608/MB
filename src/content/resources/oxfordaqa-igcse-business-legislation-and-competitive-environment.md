| Section | Content | What you must be able to do |
|---|---|---|

The specification does not name particular Acts or regulators. Keep your answers general ("employment law requires...") rather than quoting laws from one country. Students are expected to be "aware of" some points and to "assess" or "analyse" others, so prepare to write chains of reasoning, not just lists.

## 3.2.5 Legislation

### Three areas of law

- **Employment law** covers how a business treats the people who work for it. Examples of the topics it deals with include pay, hours, contracts and fair treatment.
- **Health and safety law** requires a business to protect employees, and often customers and visitors, from harm.
- **Consumer law** protects buyers. It covers goods and services that must be as described, safe and of acceptable quality.

Legislation is a legal requirement. That separates it from ethics (section 3.2.2), which covers choices a business makes that go further than the law demands.

### Assessing the impact: a four-part checklist

The specification lists four effects to assess. Use them as a checklist, then add the context of the business in the question.

1. **Cost.** Wages, equipment, record-keeping, insurance, refunds.
2. **Training needs.** Managers and staff must learn what the law requires and how to apply it.
3. **Recruitment.** Rules on pay, contracts and safety shape what a business can offer applicants and how it hires.
4. **Consequences of failure.** Fines, legal costs, compensation, forced changes, lost reputation and lost sales.

### Worked example: a national minimum wage rise

Orsino Furniture Works employs 24 people. 14 of them are paid the national minimum wage. The minimum rises from 6.50 to 7.20 per hour. Each of the 14 works 40 hours a week for 50 weeks. Annual sales revenue is 1,120,000.

```
Hours per worker per year      = 40 x 50                = 2,000
Rise per hour                  = 7.20 - 6.50            = 0.70
Extra cost per worker          = 2,000 x 0.70           = 1,400
Extra cost for 14 workers      = 14 x 1,400             = 19,600
As % of sales revenue          = 19,600 / 1,120,000     = 1.75%
```

The analysis does not stop at the number. Orsino could raise prices by about 1.75% to cover the cost, but that risks losing sales to rivals. Or it could accept lower profit.

Now consider the benefit side. If the higher pay cut the number of leavers from 6 to 3 a year, and each replacement costs 900 to recruit and train, the saving is 3 x 900 = 2,700. The net cost falls to 16,900. This is the logic behind the **benefits and drawbacks of a national minimum wage**:

| Benefits to a business | Drawbacks to a business |
|---|---|
| Staff may feel more motivated and remain longer, so fewer replacements need hiring and training | Higher wage bill, especially for labour-intensive businesses |
| Workers have more spending power, which can raise demand for products | Pressure to raise pay for staff just above the minimum, to keep differences |
| All rivals in the country face the same floor, so none can win customers simply by underpaying | Higher prices or lower profit; small firms with thin margins may struggle |
| Clear rule that helps recruitment planning | Businesses may cut hours or hire fewer people |

### Worked example: a safe working environment

Orsino must fit guards to its saws. The guards cost 6,300 once. Training 24 staff for 4 hours each, valuing time at 8.00 an hour, costs 24 x 4 x 8.00 = 768. A course fee adds 600, so training is 1,368 and compliance is 7,668 in total.

Suppose one injury happened because the guards were missing. The business faces a compensation claim of 15,000, a fine of 5,000 and 3,200 of lost output. That is 23,200, roughly three times the compliance cost.

```
Compliance      6,300 + 1,368          =  7,668
Non-compliance  15,000 + 5,000 + 3,200 = 23,200
```

**Benefits of safe-workplace laws to a business:** fewer accidents and less absence, better morale, a good reputation with recruits and customers, and no price advantage for rivals who cut corners. **Drawbacks:** equipment and training costs, time spent on risk checks and records, and disruption while changes are made. Smaller businesses feel the fixed cost more heavily because they spread it over fewer sales.

Note the recruitment link: a business with a poor safety record finds it harder to attract applicants, and managers need training to run inspections.

### Worked example: consumer protection

Vantrey Sports Gear sells online and ships 1,500 orders a month at an average of 60 per order. About 4% come back because the item was faulty or not as described. A refund plus return postage costs 65 per return.

```
Returns per month      = 1,500 x 0.04            = 60
Cost per month         = 60 x 65                 = 3,900
Cost per year          = 3,900 x 12              = 46,800
```

Vantrey adds a quality check costing 0.40 per order and cuts returns to 2%.

```
Returns after check    = 1,500 x 0.02            = 30
Return cost            = 30 x 65                 = 1,950
Check cost             = 1,500 x 0.40            = 600
New monthly total      = 2,550   (saving 1,350 a month, 16,200 a year)
```

Consumer protection laws therefore **impact on businesses** in two ways. They add cost (checks, refunds, clear product descriptions, staff training on complaints). They also build trust, which can raise repeat purchases, and they stop rivals from winning sales with misleading claims. Failure to follow them can mean compensation, fines, legal fees, damaged reputation and lost customers.

### Common errors on legislation

- Saying a law "affects" a business without naming the route: which cost, which training need, which hiring change.
- Treating all legislation as pure cost. Always weigh the benefits.
- Mixing up ethics and legislation.
- Writing about one country's named Acts. The specification does not ask for them.

## 3.2.6 Competitive environment

### Markets and competition

A **market** is where buyers and sellers come together to trade a good or service. It can be a physical place, a website or a whole country's trade in one product. **Competition** exists when two or more businesses try to win the same customers. (These are the standard forms; the specification asks you to understand the terms.)

### Worked example: analysing the impact of competition

Stellmark Cycles sells 600 bikes a year at 400 each. Each bike costs 300, so profit is 100 a bike, or 60,000. A rival opens and cuts its price by 10% to 360.

If Stellmark matches the price, its margin falls to 60. To earn the same 60,000 it would need 60,000 / 60 = 1,000 bikes, an increase of about 67%.

```
Rival price cut           = (400 - 360) / 400      = 10%
Margin if matched         = 360 - 300              = 60
Bikes for same profit     = 60,000 / 60            = 1,000
Extra bikes needed        = 1,000 - 600            = 400  (66.7% more)
```

Potential impacts of competition on a business:

- **Lower prices or margins**, as in the example.
- **Lost sales and market share** if customers switch.
- **Higher marketing and service costs** to keep customers.
- **Pressure to improve** quality, design or customer service.
- **Benefits for customers**, such as choice, which can raise the market's size.

Which effect matters most depends on the business: how loyal its customers are, how different its product is, and how big its cost cushion is.

### Minimal or no competition

You should be able to identify when a business faces little or none:

- It is the only seller in an area, for example the only shop in a remote village.
- It holds a patent, licence or exclusive right.
- Start-up costs are very high, so few can enter.
- Its product is highly specialised.
- A government controls the supply.

The business can then charge higher prices and keep more profit. But it may have less pressure to improve service, risks attracting new entrants, and can face public criticism or regulation if it charges too much.

### Risk and uncertainty

**Risk** is the chance that something harms the business. **Uncertainty** exists because nobody can know the future. All businesses face uncertainty because customers' tastes change, rivals act, costs and exchange rates move, the economy shifts and technology develops. Examples of risks include falling sales, new competitors, unpaid bills, theft, accidents, poor cash flow and shortages of supplies. A new business has the added risk that it has no track record.

**Why do entrepreneurs take these risks?** Common reasons are the chance of profit and financial independence, being their own boss, turning an idea or skill into a business, meeting a need they have spotted, and the satisfaction of building something. The reward must look worth the risk of losing the money invested.

**Activities that minimise risk** include:

- Market research before launch, to test demand.
- A business plan with cash-flow forecasts.
- Insurance against fire, theft or injury.
- Offering several products or serving several markets (spreading risk).
- Keeping cash reserves and controlling costs.
- Building customer loyalty and supplier relationships.
- Training staff and following the law, so that fines and accidents are avoided.

No activity removes risk. Each has a cost: market research and insurance cost money.

### Developing countries competing with developed countries

Businesses in developing countries can compete through **lower cost advantage**, because labour and some other costs are often lower. They may also offer local knowledge, local materials or a niche product.

Nuroo Textiles makes shirts in a developing country and sells to a developed one. Its unit costs are labour 1.90, materials 3.10, shipping 0.70. A developed-country rival has labour 6.80, materials 3.10 and delivery 0.20.

```
Nuroo cost per shirt      = 1.90 + 3.10 + 0.70     = 5.70
Rival cost per shirt      = 6.80 + 3.10 + 0.20     = 10.10
Cost advantage            = 10.10 - 5.70           = 4.40  (43.6% of rival cost)
```

That 4.40 lets Nuroo undercut on price or earn a higher profit. But the advantage is not guaranteed. Longer delivery times, weaker transport links, variable quality, exchange-rate movements and customers' trust in the rival can all cut into it. Developed-country rivals compete with branding, quality and service.

### Common errors on competition

- Defining competition as only price. Quality, service and branding matter too.
- Assuming a business with no competition faces no risk.
- Giving a list of risks without linking one to the business in the question.
- Saying developing-country firms always win on price.
