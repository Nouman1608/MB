## Section A

**1.** Define "hormone". **[2]**

**2.** State two ways in which a hormone differs from a neurotransmitter. **[2]**

**3.** Name the gland that releases cortisol and the pathway that ends in its release. **[2]**

**4.** Distinguish between a releaser pheromone and a primer pheromone. **[2]**

**5.** State why Newcomer et al. (1999) used a placebo group and a double-blind procedure. **[2]**

**6.** Outline the finding of Wedekind et al. (1995). **[3]**

## Section B

**7.** A researcher gives one group of volunteers a nasal spray containing oxytocin and another group a spray containing salt water. Both groups then decide how much money to lend to a stranger.
(a) Identify the independent and dependent variables.
(b) Explain one reason why the researcher should also include a condition in which participants lend money to a computer that returns money at random. **[4]**

**8.** Explain why Newcomer et al. (1999) may have limited ecological validity as evidence about stress and memory. **[3]**

**9.** A magazine headline says: "Scientists discover the trust hormone." Using research, explain why this headline oversimplifies the evidence. **[4]**

**10.** Outline two reasons why the existence of human pheromones is disputed. **[4]**

**11.** Explain one study investigating the effect of one hormone on human behaviour. **[9]**

**12.** Evaluate research into the effect of pheromones on human behaviour. **[22]**

## Answers

**1.** A chemical messenger released by an endocrine gland into the bloodstream **[1]** that acts on target cells with matching receptors elsewhere in the body **[1]**.

**2.** Hormones travel in the blood rather than across a synapse **[1]**; they act more slowly and their effects last longer **[1]**. Also creditable: they act on more widespread targets.

**3.** The adrenal cortex (adrenal glands) **[1]**; the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis **[1]**.

**4.** A releaser pheromone triggers an immediate behavioural response, such as approach or mating **[1]**; a primer pheromone produces a slower physiological change, such as altering the timing of a reproductive cycle **[1]**.

**5.** The placebo group shows what happens without cortisol, so any difference can be attributed to the hormone **[1]**; double-blind procedures stop participants' and researchers' expectations from affecting memory performance or scoring **[1]**.

**6.** Men wore the same T-shirt for two nights and women rated the odours **[1]**. Women tended to prefer the odour of men whose MHC (immune-system) genes were dissimilar to their own **[1]**. The preference was reversed in women taking oral contraceptives **[1]**.

**7.** (a) IV: whether the spray contains oxytocin or salt water **[1]**; DV: the amount of money lent to the stranger **[1]**.
(b) Oxytocin might simply make people more willing to take financial risks **[1]**; if lending to a random computer is unaffected while lending to a person increases, the effect can be attributed to trust in another person rather than to risk-taking **[1]**.

**8.** Participants took cortisol tablets for four days rather than experiencing a stressful event **[1]**. Real stress involves other hormones, such as adrenaline, and the person's own appraisal of the situation **[1]**. The memory test was recall of a prose passage in a controlled setting, which may not reflect memory in everyday stressful situations **[1]**.

**9.** Kosfeld et al. (2005) did find that oxytocin increased the amount investors entrusted to a human trustee **[1]**, but the sample was all male and the dose was artificial **[1]**. Nave, Camerer and McCullough (2015) reported that replications have been inconsistent **[1]**, and De Dreu et al. (2011) found oxytocin increased in-group favouritism, so it does not increase trust in everyone **[1]**.

**10.** Two reasons, two marks each:
- No human pheromone has been chemically identified; Wyatt (2015) argued that candidate molecules were chosen without first isolating an active chemical from body odour **[2]**.
- Key findings have not held up: McClintock's (1971) menstrual synchrony was not found in later studies such as Yang and Schank (2006), and Hare et al. (2017) found that androstadienone and estratetraenol did not affect judgements of faces **[2]**.
- Also creditable for two marks: human behaviour is strongly influenced by learning, culture and expectation, so it is hard to show that a chemical alone caused an effect.

**11.** Award up to nine marks across knowledge and understanding and use of research:
- names the hormone and behaviour, for example cortisol and verbal memory **[1]**;
- explains the mechanism: released by the adrenal cortex at the end of the HPA axis, acting on receptors in the hippocampus **[2]**;
- states the aim of Newcomer et al. (1999): whether stress-level cortisol impairs verbal declarative memory **[1]**;
- describes the procedure: double-blind random allocation to high dose, low dose or placebo for four days; prose recall tested **[2]**;
- gives the findings: impaired recall in the high-dose group only, recovering after treatment **[2]**;
- links the study back to the question: a hormone released in stress changes a cognitive behaviour **[1]**.
Kosfeld et al. (2005) on oxytocin and trust is equally creditable. Evaluation is not required.

**12.** Marked with the Paper 1 essay criteria:
- **A Focus (2):** states that the issue is whether chemical signals between people affect behaviour, and that the evidence is contested **[2]**.
- **B Knowledge and understanding (6):** defines pheromone; distinguishes releaser and primer; explains why animal evidence is strong and why human evidence is harder to obtain **[6]**.
- **C Use of research (6):** two or three studies explained and linked, for example Stern and McClintock (1998), Wedekind et al. (1995) and Hare et al. (2017) **[6]**.
- **D Critical thinking (6):** Stern and McClintock used odourless compounds, reducing conscious expectation, but did not identify the active chemical; Wedekind's odour preference could be learned and is not clearly a pheromone; synchrony failed to replicate; Hare et al. challenge the best-known candidates; Wyatt's methodological critique; a conclusion that the case is unproven **[6]**.
- **E Clarity and organisation (2):** a clear argument from introduction to conclusion **[2]**.

## Where marks are usually lost

- **Question 7:** naming "oxytocin" as the IV without the comparison condition.
- **Question 9:** criticising the headline without any research.
- **Question 11:** spending half the answer on evaluation, which earns nothing in a short answer.
- **Question 12:** describing moth or mouse studies at length; they show what a pheromone is, not that humans have one.
