This guide covers subtopic **7.2 Oxides** in Topic 7, Acids, bases and salts, of Cambridge IGCSE Chemistry 0620, 2026–2028 series. The [acids, bases and salts study guide](/resources/acids-bases-and-salts/) lists the oxide types and the named examples. This page shows the chemistry behind the classification: balanced equations for each type, how to decide the type of an oxide from experimental results, why the metal/non-metal link works, and where oxides appear elsewhere in the syllabus. Then use the [oxides revision notes](/resources/cambridge-igcse-chemistry-0620-oxides-revision-notes/) and the [oxides practice questions](/resources/cambridge-igcse-chemistry-0620-oxides-practice/).

## What the syllabus asks for

**Core** (all candidates):

- Classify oxides as acidic, including SO₂ and CO₂, or basic, including CuO and CaO, related to metallic and non-metallic character.

**Supplement** (Extended candidates only):

- Describe amphoteric oxides as oxides that react with acids and with bases to produce a salt and water.
- Classify Al₂O₃ and ZnO as amphoteric oxides.

The subtopic is short, but questions on it often sit inside longer questions about acid rain, salt preparation or the Periodic Table, so the equations matter.

## The link to metallic and non-metallic character

The type of oxide follows from the type of element:

| Element | Oxide is usually | Behaves like | Reacts with |
|---|---|---|---|
| Metal | Basic | A base | Acids |
| Non-metal | Acidic | An acid | Bases / alkalis |

Across a period the elements change from metallic to non-metallic, so their oxides change from basic to acidic. In Period 3, sodium oxide and magnesium oxide are basic, aluminium oxide is amphoteric (Extended), and sulfur dioxide is acidic. Knowing where an element sits in the Periodic Table therefore lets you predict how its oxide behaves.

## Acidic oxides

Non-metal oxides such as carbon dioxide and sulfur dioxide are acidic. They react with bases and alkalis to form a salt and water:

```
CO2(g) + 2NaOH(aq) → Na2CO3(aq) + H2O(l)
SO2(g) + 2NaOH(aq) → Na2SO3(aq) + H2O(l)
CO2(g) + Ca(OH)2(aq) → CaCO3(s) + H2O(l)
```

The last equation is the limewater test for carbon dioxide: the insoluble calcium carbonate makes the limewater milky.

Many acidic oxides also dissolve in water to give acidic solutions:

```
CO2(g) + H2O(l) ⇌ H2CO3(aq)        carbonic acid (weakly acidic)
SO2(g) + H2O(l) → H2SO3(aq)        sulfurous acid
```

This is why sulfur dioxide from burning fossil fuels that contain sulfur compounds causes **acid rain** (Topic 10). An acidic oxide does **not** react with acids; it would be like mixing two acids.

## Basic oxides

Metal oxides such as copper(II) oxide and calcium oxide are basic. They react with acids to form a salt and water:

```
CaO(s) + 2HCl(aq) → CaCl2(aq) + H2O(l)
CuO(s) + 2HNO3(aq) → Cu(NO3)2(aq) + H2O(l)
```

Some basic oxides dissolve in water and form alkalis:

```
CaO(s) + H2O(l) → Ca(OH)2(aq)
Na2O(s) + H2O(l) → 2NaOH(aq)
```

Copper(II) oxide is insoluble, so it is a base but not an alkali. You cannot test its pH in water; you show it is basic by its reaction with an acid (the black solid dissolves to give a blue solution).

**Acidic oxide meets basic oxide.** Calcium oxide removes sulfur dioxide from waste gases in flue gas desulfurisation:

```
CaO(s) + SO2(g) → CaSO3(s)
```

A basic oxide neutralises an acidic oxide, forming the salt calcium sulfite.

## Amphoteric oxides (Supplement)

An amphoteric oxide reacts with **both** acids and bases, producing a salt and water each time. The syllabus names two: **aluminium oxide** and **zinc oxide**.

With acids they behave like basic oxides:

```
ZnO(s) + 2HCl(aq) → ZnCl2(aq) + H2O(l)
Al2O3(s) + 6HCl(aq) → 2AlCl3(aq) + 3H2O(l)
```

With aqueous sodium hydroxide they behave like acidic oxides:

```
ZnO(s) + 2NaOH(aq) → Na2ZnO2(aq) + H2O(l)       sodium zincate
Al2O3(s) + 2NaOH(aq) → 2NaAlO2(aq) + H2O(l)      sodium aluminate
```

Zinc and aluminium are metals, so you might expect basic oxides; their amphoteric behaviour is the exception you must learn. In an exam it is usually enough to state the products as "a salt and water". The word equations above show what those salts are called.

## Balancing oxide equations

Every oxide ion, O²⁻, combines with two H⁺ ions to make one water molecule. Count the oxide ions in the formula first, and the rest follows.

- **Na₂O + nitric acid.** One oxide ion needs 2H⁺, so 2HNO₃: Na₂O + 2HNO₃ → 2NaNO₃ + H₂O.
- **MgO + hydrochloric acid.** One oxide ion needs 2H⁺, so 2HCl: MgO + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂O.
- **Al₂O₃ + sulfuric acid (Extended).** Three oxide ions need 6H⁺, which is 3H₂SO₄, giving 3H₂O: Al₂O₃ + 3H₂SO₄ → Al₂(SO₄)₃ + 3H₂O.

Then check the metal: the salt formula must match the metal ion's charge (Al³⁺ with SO₄²⁻ gives Al₂(SO₄)₃).

## Deciding the type of an unknown oxide

Add a sample of the oxide to dilute hydrochloric acid in one test tube and to aqueous sodium hydroxide in another, warming gently. A reaction is shown by the solid dissolving or the mixture warming.

| Reacts with acid? | Reacts with alkali? | Type |
|---|---|---|
| Yes | No | Basic |
| No | Yes | Acidic |
| Yes | Yes | Amphoteric (Extended) |

### Worked example

A student tested three white solid oxides, J, K and L.

| Oxide | Dilute HCl | Aqueous NaOH | Added to water, universal indicator |
|---|---|---|---|
| J | Dissolves | Dissolves | Insoluble |
| K | Dissolves | No change | Dissolves; purple |
| L | No change | No change | Insoluble |

- **J** reacts with both: **amphoteric**. It could be zinc oxide or aluminium oxide.
- **K** reacts with acid only and dissolves to give a strongly alkaline solution: **basic**, and its element is a metal. It could be calcium oxide.
- **L** shows no reaction in this test. The data do not show it to be acidic, basic or amphoteric, so the correct response is that it cannot be classified from these results. Do not force it into one of the categories without evidence.

## Common mistakes

- **Saying acidic oxides react with acids.** They react with bases and alkalis.
- **Classifying every metal oxide as basic** (Extended): ZnO and Al₂O₃ are amphoteric.
- **Using "amphoteric" to mean "neutral".** Amphoteric oxides react with both acids and bases; they are not unreactive.
- **Forgetting that "salt and water" is the product both ways** for an amphoteric oxide.
- **Calling copper(II) oxide an alkali.** It is insoluble.
- **Writing CaO + H₂O → CaOH.** Calcium forms Ca²⁺, so the hydroxide is Ca(OH)₂.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Classify**: give the type for each oxide named; one word each is enough unless a reason is requested.
- **Explain** a classification: link to the element (metal or non-metal), or to the reactions observed.
- **Describe** (Extended, amphoteric): "reacts with acids and with bases to produce a salt and water". Both halves are needed.
- **Suggest** the identity of an unknown oxide: use the evidence given and choose a named example from the syllabus that fits it.
- When writing equations, balance the water: one oxide ion reacts with two H⁺ ions to make one water molecule.
