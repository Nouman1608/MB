This guide goes deeper on **2.2 Motivation**, part of AS Level topic 2 (Human resource management) in Cambridge International AS & A Level Business 9609, for examination in 2026, 2027 and 2028. The syllabus content is:

- **2.2.1 Motivation as a tool of management and leadership**: the need to motivate employees to achieve the objectives of a business.
- **2.2.2 Human needs**: a simple explanation of human need, and how human needs may or may not be satisfied at work.
- **2.2.3 Motivation theories**: the main content theories (Taylor, Mayo, Maslow, Herzberg and McClelland) and the process theory (Vroom).
- **2.2.4 Motivation methods in practice**: the theories in practical situations; payment methods (time based, salary, piece rates, commission, bonuses, profit sharing, performance-related pay, fringe benefits); non-financial motivators (training, opportunities for promotion, development, status, job re-design, team working, empowerment, participation, job enrichment); and ways employees can participate in the management and control of business activity.

## Why motivation is a management tool

Managers achieve objectives through other people. A motivated workforce affects business performance through several measurable routes:

- **Productivity**: more output per worker lowers labour cost per unit.
- **Quality**: careful work means fewer faults, returns and complaints.
- **Labour turnover**: staff who stay save recruitment and training costs.
- **Absence**: fewer days lost means less overtime or temporary cover.
- **Ideas**: engaged staff suggest improvements.

In a case study, these give you evidence. Rising absence, high turnover or falling quality are *symptoms* that suggest a motivation problem; the theories help explain the cause.

## How the theories relate to each other

Examiners reward answers that use theories as tools rather than reciting them. It helps to know where they agree and disagree.

**Two views of people at work.** Taylor treated workers as mainly economic: they respond to pay linked to output. Mayo's human relations view stressed social needs: attention, belonging and being part of a group. Most later theories sit closer to Mayo, but Taylor's ideas still shape piece rates and output targets.

**Maslow and Herzberg overlap.** Herzberg's hygiene factors (pay, conditions, security, supervision, policy) roughly match Maslow's lower levels (physiological, safety and some social needs). Herzberg's motivators (achievement, recognition, responsibility, the work itself, advancement) roughly match Maslow's esteem and self-actualisation. The difference is in what happens when needs are met. For Maslow, a satisfied need stops motivating and the next level takes over. For Herzberg, hygiene factors never truly motivate; they only prevent dissatisfaction.

**McClelland explains why people differ.** Maslow implies everyone climbs the same ladder. McClelland says individuals have different strengths of need for achievement, affiliation and power, shaped by experience. This explains why the same reward motivates one employee and not another.

**Vroom asks a different question.** The content theories ask *what* motivates. Vroom's expectancy theory asks *how* a person decides how much effort to give: will effort lead to performance, will performance lead to a reward, and is the reward valued? This makes Vroom useful for evaluating any scheme, because it shows where a scheme can break down even when the reward is attractive.

## Criticisms you can use in evaluation

| Theory | Strength in practice | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Taylor | Clear link between effort and pay; suits measurable, repetitive work | Ignores social needs; dull, closely controlled work can lower morale; quality may suffer |
| Mayo | Shows the value of attention, teamwork and consultation | Based on studies in one workplace; output may have risen simply because workers knew they were being observed |
| Maslow | Simple and intuitive; reminds managers that basic needs come first | People do not always move up in order; the levels are hard to measure; needs may vary between cultures |
| Herzberg | Explains why a pay rise can have a short-lived effect | Developed from interviews with professional staff; pay may motivate low-paid workers more than the theory suggests |
| McClelland | Explains individual differences; useful in matching people to roles | Needs are hard to measure reliably |
| Vroom | Shows exactly where a scheme fails (effort, performance or reward link) | Assumes people calculate rationally; values cannot be measured precisely |

Use one limitation in an evaluation and tie it to the case. For example: "Herzberg suggests pay will not motivate the cleaners for long, but they are on the minimum wage, so a pay rise may meet basic needs that are currently unmet."

## Human needs that work may fail to meet

2.2.2 asks how needs may *not* be satisfied. Common examples:

- Zero-hours or short contracts: no security.
- Remote or isolated work: weak sense of belonging.
- Narrow, repetitive tasks: no achievement or growth.
- Rigid supervision: no responsibility or recognition.
- Long or irregular hours: health and family life suffer.

## Diagnosing a motivation problem: worked example

**Calloway Contact Centre** employs 120 staff. Last year 42 left, and absence averaged 11 days per employee. Staff are paid an hourly rate above the local average. Calls are timed and scripted; staff cannot resolve problems without a supervisor.

1. **Symptoms**: 42 ÷ 120 = 35% of staff left in a year; absence is high.
2. **Diagnosis using theory**: pay is above average, so the hygiene factor of pay is unlikely to be the cause. The job lacks Herzberg's motivators: no responsibility, no achievement, no variety. Close control reflects Taylor's approach.
3. **Possible solutions**:
   - **Empowerment**: let staff resolve common complaints up to a set refund value.
   - **Job enrichment**: add responsibility for following up a customer's problem to the end.
   - **Team working**: small teams with shared targets, meeting social needs.
   - **Training**: build skills to handle complex calls, with a route to promotion.
4. **Check with Vroom**: staff must believe the new responsibilities are achievable (expectancy) and that success is recognised (instrumentality), or the changes will not raise effort.
5. **Evaluate cost**: if replacing a leaver costs $3 000 in recruitment and training, cutting departures from 42 to 30 saves 12 × 3 000 = $36 000 a year, which can be compared with the cost of training.

## Choosing between financial methods

Instead of describing each payment method, match it to the job:

| Job feature | Suitable methods | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Output easy to count, quality easy to check | Piece rate | Pay rises with effort |
| Sales role, results traceable to one person | Commission, bonus | Links pay to sales |
| Output depends on teamwork | Team bonus, profit sharing | Avoids rewarding one person for shared work |
| Professional work, hard to measure in units | Salary, performance-related pay | Stability plus a link to appraised performance |
| Skills in short supply | Fringe benefits, salary | Helps retain staff |

The trap to avoid is recommending piece rates where quality or safety matters, or commission where customers need impartial advice.

## Non-financial methods and participation

- **Job re-design** is the general term for changing the tasks in a job. It includes rotating tasks to reduce boredom and widening the range of tasks.
- **Job enrichment** is a specific form of re-design that adds *responsibility* and decision-making, not just more tasks.
- **Empowerment** gives staff authority over their own work; it needs training and trust to succeed.
- **Participation** ranges from being consulted (suggestion schemes, team briefings) to sharing decisions (works councils, worker directors) to sharing ownership (employee share schemes). The deeper the participation, the greater the potential commitment, but the slower decisions may become.

## Annotated model answer

*Question: Evaluate whether financial rewards are the most effective way to motivate nurses in a private hospital.*

> Financial rewards meet basic needs and, as a hygiene factor, prevent dissatisfaction; if the hospital pays below rivals, nurses may leave, so competitive pay is necessary. *(Application and analysis.)* However, nursing is complex and team-based, so piece rates or individual bonuses could encourage rushing or damage cooperation. *(Analysis of a limitation.)* Many nurses may be motivated by the work itself and by recognition, which fits Herzberg's motivators, so training, promotion routes and involvement in decisions may raise effort more for each dollar spent. *(Counter-argument linked to theory.)* Overall, pay is necessary but not sufficient; the most effective approach depends on whether current pay is competitive, because if it is, non-financial motivators are likely to give the larger gain. *(Judgement with a condition.)*

## Common mistakes

- Reciting a theory with no link to the business in the question.
- Treating Maslow and Herzberg as if they say the same thing.
- Describing Vroom's terms without explaining how a weak link lowers effort.
- Recommending one method for every type of worker.
- Forgetting the cost of a motivator when evaluating it.

## Exam technique: command words

- **Identify** or **Define**: name a motivator, payment method or need precisely.
- **Explain**: show how the method or theory affects effort in the stated job.
- **Analyse**: link symptom → cause (via a theory) → effect on the business.
- **Evaluate** or **Assess**: weigh at least two methods, consider cost and the workforce, and decide.
- **Advise** or **Justify**: recommend a specific motivator for the business in the case and support it with evidence from it.
