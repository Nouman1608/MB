The specification asks you to explain the balance of payments; the components of the current account (trade in goods, trade in services, primary income and secondary income); the policy objective of a sustainable balance of payments position; and imbalances. You must also calculate the balances on the different components and evaluate the causes and consequences of imbalances. Every country and figure below is invented.

## 1. The balance of payments as a record

The **balance of payments** records the value of all transactions between the residents of one country (households, firms and the government) and the rest of the world over a period. The test is residence, not where the product goes: a home hotel selling a room to a visiting tourist is an export, though nothing crosses the border.

Each transaction is entered as a **credit** (money flowing in) or a **debit** (money flowing out). A **balance** is always credits minus debits, so a negative number is a deficit and a positive one a surplus.

The record has three main parts:

- the **current account**, covering trade and income flows;
- the **capital account**, a small account for capital transfers such as debt forgiveness;
- the **financial account**, covering purchases of assets and borrowing across borders, such as foreign direct investment, buying shares and bonds, and bank loans.

Because every purchase must be paid for, the accounts together balance once an errors and omissions item is added. A current account deficit is therefore matched by a net inflow on the financial and capital accounts: the country sells assets or borrows to pay for spending more abroad than it earns. "A balance of payments deficit" in the news almost always means the current account.

## 2. The four components of the current account

| Component | What it records | Credit example | Debit example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Trade in goods | Tangible products | Cars shipped to a foreign dealer | Imported fruit |
| Trade in services | Intangible products | A foreign student's tuition fees at a home university | A resident's ski holiday abroad |
| Primary income | Income earned by factors of production across borders: compensation of employees and investment income (interest, profits, dividends) | Dividends paid to a home pension fund by a foreign company | Profits sent home by a foreign-owned factory |
| Secondary income | Transfers with nothing received in return | Money sent home by family members who live and work abroad | Government aid to another country; remittances sent abroad by migrant workers |

To classify a transaction, ask first whether something is produced and sold: if so it is trade, a good if tangible, a service if not (shipping, insurance, banking, tourism and education are services). Otherwise ask whether it rewards a factor of production (primary income) or is a one-way gift (secondary income).

A classic trap: a foreign firm *building* a factory at home is a financial account inflow, not a current account item. Only the profits it later sends abroad appear, as a primary income debit.

## 3. Calculating balances

Each component has its own balance, and they build up in a fixed order.

- **Balance of trade in goods** = goods exports − goods imports
- **Balance of trade in services** = service exports − service imports
- **Balance of trade** (goods and services) = the two added together
- **Current account balance** = balance of trade + primary income balance + secondary income balance

**Worked example 1: Country Rello (values in billion currency units, bn).** GDP is 1,160 bn.

| Component | Credits | Debits | Balance |
|---|---|---|---|
| Trade in goods | 310 | 352 | −42 |
| Trade in services | 145 | 98 | +47 |
| Primary income | 62 | 81 | −19 |
| Secondary income | 9 | 24 | −15 |

- Balance of trade: −42 + 47 = **+5 bn**, a small surplus.
- Current account: 5 − 19 − 15 = **−29 bn**, a deficit.
- As a share of GDP: 29 ÷ 1,160 × 100 = **2.5%**.

Rello's goods deficit is more than offset by its services surplus. The current account deficit comes from income flows: foreigners earn more on assets they own in Rello than Rello's residents earn abroad, and transfers flow out. "Rello imports too many goods" misreads the data.

**Working backwards.** Examiners often give the current account balance and ask for a missing figure. Suppose next year Rello's current account deficit is 35 bn, its goods balance is −50 bn, its services balance is +49 bn and its secondary income balance is −15 bn. Then −50 + 49 + primary income − 15 = −35, so the primary income balance is **−19 bn**, unchanged.

## 4. Sustainability and imbalances

An **imbalance** is a current account deficit or surplus that is large or persistent. The policy objective is not a zero balance but a **sustainable** position: one the country can keep without being forced into a sudden, painful correction, such as a sharp fall in the exchange rate or deep cuts in spending.

Four questions decide whether a position is sustainable.

| Question | More sustainable | Less sustainable |
|---|---|---|
| How large is it relative to GDP? | A deficit of 1 to 2% of GDP | A deficit of 7% of GDP |
| How long has it lasted? | One or two years in a boom | A decade or more |
| How is it financed? | Long-term direct investment in productive firms | Short-term borrowing or flows of money that can leave quickly |
| What is the money spent on? | Imported machinery that raises future output and exports | Imported consumer goods in a credit-fuelled boom |

**Worked example 2: two invented countries.** Arland has a current account deficit of 18 bn on GDP of 300 bn: 6.0% of GDP. Bexin has a deficit of 40 bn on GDP of 2,000 bn: 2.0% of GDP. Bexin's deficit is bigger in money terms but far smaller relative to the size of its economy, so on this test Arland's position is the more worrying. Then ask the other three questions before reaching a verdict.

Surpluses can be imbalances too. A persistent surplus means a country spends less than it produces and lends the difference abroad; it can reflect weak domestic demand, and one country's surplus is another's deficit, which causes friction.

## 5. Evaluating the causes of imbalances

Group causes into short-term (cyclical) and long-term (structural). Then judge which dominates.

- **Faster growth at home than abroad.** Rising incomes raise import spending while exports depend on foreign incomes; this cyclical cause reverses when growth slows.
- **Relative inflation.** If domestic prices rise faster than partners' prices and the exchange rate does not change, exports become dearer and imports relatively cheaper.
- **The exchange rate.** A strong currency makes exports dearer in foreign currency and imports cheaper at home.
- **Productivity and costs.** Higher labour costs per unit of output weaken price competitiveness.
- **Non-price factors.** Quality, design, reliability and after-sales service affect demand for exports at any price.
- **Income flows.** Heavy foreign ownership of domestic assets produces primary income debits; migrant workers' remittances raise secondary income debits.

**Worked example 3: relative inflation.** Over a year Country Corvan's prices rise 6% while its trading partners' prices rise 2%, and the exchange rate is unchanged. Corvan's goods become dearer relative to foreign goods by 1.06 ÷ 1.02 − 1 = **3.9%**. How far its trade balance worsens depends on how price elastic demand for its exports and imports is: if buyers can easily switch, the effect is large; if Corvan sells specialised goods with few substitutes, it is small.

**Evaluation lines.** Is the cause temporary (a boom) or lasting (falling competitiveness)? Do the data place the problem in goods, services or income? Is one cause, such as a currency movement, a symptom of another, such as high inflation?

## 6. Evaluating the consequences of imbalances

| Area | Persistent deficit | Persistent surplus |
|---|---|---|
| Aggregate demand | Net exports (X − M) reduce AD, lowering output and employment, other things equal | Net exports add to AD, supporting growth but risking inflation near full capacity |
| Exchange rate | Pressure to depreciate, which raises import prices | Pressure to appreciate, which hurts exporters |
| Future income | Assets sold or debt built up abroad lead to larger primary income debits later | Assets acquired abroad bring primary income credits later |

The consequences are not automatically bad: a deficit can mean residents enjoy imported goods and foreign firms see the country as a good place to invest. The judgement turns on the four sustainability questions in section 4 and on the state of the economy: a deficit matters less when unemployment is low and growth strong.

## Common mistakes

- Calculating a balance as debits minus credits.
- Leaving primary and secondary income out of the current account balance.
- Recording foreign direct investment itself in the current account.
- Treating spending by foreign tourists at home as an import.
- Judging a deficit by its money size without relating it to GDP.
- Writing that a deficit is always harmful, without asking how it is financed and why it arose.

## Exam technique

- **Calculate:** show the formula, substitution and answer with its unit (bn, or % of GDP). A missing minus sign on a deficit usually costs the mark.
- **Explain:** give the full chain, for example higher relative inflation, dearer exports, lower export volumes, a larger deficit.
- **Evaluate:** weigh at least two causes or consequences using the data, and conclude on which matters most and what the answer depends on, such as financing or whether the cause is cyclical.
