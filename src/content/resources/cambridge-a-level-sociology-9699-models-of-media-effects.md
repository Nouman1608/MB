Section 10.2 of Cambridge International A Level Sociology (9699), *Different models of media
effects*, sits at the centre of heading 10 in Paper 4. The syllabus lists four points: direct
effects models of media influence, including the hypodermic syringe model; indirect effects models,
including the uses and gratification model, the two-step flow model, the normative model and the
cultural effects model; debates about the strengths and limitations of the different models; and
arguments and evidence about the extent to which human behaviour is influenced by the media.

The [media representation and media effects guide](/resources/a-level-cambridge-sociology-media-representation-and-effects/)
summarises each model in a table. This page explains where the models came from, what research
each depends on and how to compare them. Use it with the
[models of media effects revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-sociology-9699-models-of-media-effects-revision-notes/)
and the [models of media effects practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-sociology-9699-models-of-media-effects-practice/).

## A way to organise the models

Every model answers two questions: **how active is the audience?** and **how direct and fast is
the effect?** Placing each model on these two dimensions gives an essay structure.

| Model | Audience | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Hypodermic syringe | Passive, isolated individuals | Direct, immediate, uniform |
| Two-step flow | Embedded in social groups | Indirect, filtered through opinion leaders |
| Uses and gratifications | Active, choosing media to meet needs | Audience shapes the effect |
| Normative | Shaped by group norms | Depends on how far media messages fit or fill gaps in those norms |
| Cultural effects | Partly active, but exposed over long periods | Slow, cumulative, shaping "common sense" |

## Direct effects: the hypodermic syringe model

The model holds that media messages are "injected" into a mass audience of isolated individuals
and produce immediate, similar effects. It grew out of concern in the 1920s and 1930s about wartime
propaganda and the rise of mass media in industrial societies, where traditional community ties
were thought to have weakened.

**Evidence used in support:** laboratory studies of imitation, such as Bandura's Bobo doll
experiments; fears about propaganda; copycat incidents.

**The War of the Worlds case.** Orson Welles's radio drama of 1938, presented as news of a Martian
invasion, is often cited as proof of direct effects. Yet Hadley Cantril's study *The Invasion from
Mars* (1940) found that listeners' responses varied: those with more education and "critical
ability", or who checked other sources, were much less likely to be frightened. The classic example
therefore supports a more complex model.

**Limitations:** ignores audience interpretation, social context and differences between people;
effects measured in laboratories may not last or transfer to real life.

## Indirect effects models

### Two-step flow

Paul Lazarsfeld, Bernard Berelson and Hazel Gaudet's *The People's Choice* (1944), a study of
voters in the 1940 US presidential election, found that personal contact influenced voting more
than the media directly. Ideas flowed from the media to **opinion leaders**, who were more
attentive to the media, and from them to others. Katz and Lazarsfeld's *Personal Influence* (1955)
developed the model.

- **Strength:** recognises that audiences are members of social groups.
- **Limitation:** opinion leaders are also influenced by others, so a **multi-step flow** is more
  realistic; online, "influencers" are both media producers and opinion leaders, blurring the
  model's two steps.

### Uses and gratifications

This model reverses the question: not what media do to people, but what people do with media.
Denis McQuail, Jay Blumler and J.R. Brown (1972) identified four main uses:

1. **Diversion** — escape from routine and problems.
2. **Personal relationships** — companionship and conversation about media.
3. **Personal identity** — comparing oneself with characters or ideas.
4. **Surveillance** — finding out about the world.

- **Strength:** treats audiences as active and explains why people respond differently to the same
  content.
- **Limitation:** assumes people know their needs; media may create the needs they satisfy; it
  can underplay the power of media owners.

### Normative model

The normative model holds that the media's influence depends on the **norms** of the groups people
belong to. Messages that fit an audience's existing norms are likely to be accepted and reinforced;
those that conflict with them are often rejected. Where other agencies of socialisation, such as
family or community, provide weak guidance, media representations may help set norms of what is
acceptable. This links the normative model to explanations of imitation in 10.3: the same content
may have little effect on people with strong social bonds and more effect on those without them.

- **Strength:** explains why effects vary between groups.
- **Limitation:** media and other influences are hard to separate, so the role of the media is
  difficult to measure.

Joseph Klapper's *The Effects of Mass Communication* (1960) supports a similar conclusion: through
**selective exposure**, **selective perception** and **selective retention**, people tend to choose,
interpret and remember media content in line with what they already believe, so the media mainly
**reinforce** existing attitudes.

### Cultural effects model

The cultural effects model argues that repeated media messages, received over many years, slowly
shape how people understand the world. The effect is not immediate copying but a gradual change in
what seems normal or "common sense".

- **George Gerbner's cultivation research** found that heavy television viewers in the USA were
  more likely than light viewers to see the world as dangerous, which he called **mean world
  syndrome**. Gerbner also described **mainstreaming** (heavy viewers from different groups
  sharing similar outlooks) and **resonance** (stronger effects when media images match a viewer's
  own experience).
- **Greg Philo's *Seeing and Believing* (1990)**, from the Glasgow University Media Group, asked
  groups to write their own news reports of the 1984–85 miners' strike using photographs. Many
  reproduced the violent picture shown on television news, and some believed picketing had been
  mostly violent even though they had no direct experience, although people with personal
  experience of the strike often rejected the media version.

- **Strength:** explains long-term influence on attitudes and links media to ideology.
- **Limitation:** long-term effects are hard to isolate from other influences; correlation in
  cultivation studies does not prove causation.

## Reception analysis: the active audience

Stuart Hall's encoding/decoding model (1973) and David Morley's *The Nationwide Audience* (1980)
are often used alongside uses and gratifications. They show that audiences may take a **dominant**,
**negotiated** or **oppositional** reading of the same text, depending on their social position.
This is useful evidence against direct effects, while still recognising that texts are encoded
with a preferred meaning.

## How much is behaviour influenced by the media?

| Arguments that influence is strong | Arguments that influence is limited |
|---|---|
| Long-term cultivation of attitudes (Gerbner) | Selective exposure and reinforcement (Klapper, 1960) |
| News shapes what people believe happened (Philo, 1990) | Personal influence matters more (Lazarsfeld et al., 1944) |
| Imitation in experiments (Bandura) | Active, varied readings (Hall; Morley) |
| Media saturation in modern life | Even the War of the Worlds panic varied with critical ability (Cantril, 1940) |

**Methodological point.** Each model rests on a method: laboratory experiments for direct effects,
surveys and panel studies for two-step flow, interviews for uses and gratifications, content
analysis and long-term surveys for cultivation, and qualitative audience research for reception
analysis. Evaluating a model therefore means evaluating its method.

## Annotated model paragraph

*Question focus: evaluate the view that audiences are passive receivers of media messages.*

> The hypodermic syringe model supports the view, and the 1938 *War of the Worlds* broadcast is
> often cited as evidence of mass panic. **[AO1]** However, Cantril (1940) found that responses
> varied with education and whether listeners checked other sources, which undermines the
> assumption of a uniform, passive audience. **[AO3: evidence against]** Morley's *Nationwide*
> study (1980) shows groups decoding the same programme in different ways according to their
> social position. **[AO2 and AO3]** Audiences are therefore active, though the cultural effects
> model warns that activity does not protect them from long-term influence. **[AO3: judgement]**

## Common mistakes

- Describing models one by one without comparing them.
- Listing the four uses and gratifications without showing how they make the audience active.
- Using Bandura as if it proved real-world violence; evaluate the laboratory setting.
- Forgetting the normative and cultural effects models, which the syllabus names.
- Confusing the cultural effects model (slow, cumulative) with direct effects.

## Exam technique

- For **explain** questions, name the model, state its view of the audience and give one piece of
  evidence.
- For **evaluate** questions, use the passive/active and fast/slow dimensions to compare models,
  then judge which explains more, using methodological points.
- Link to 10.3: the violence debate is a test case for these models.
