## Questions

### Section A: short-answer questions

**1.** Explain what is meant by the payback period. **[2]**

**2.** A project costs £50,000 and returns £15,000 a year for five years. Calculate the payback period. **[2]**

**3.** Explain why a future cash inflow is worth less than the same sum received today. **[2]**

**4.** Which **one** of the following is a weakness of the average rate of return (ARR)?

A: It cannot be shown as a percentage.

B: It ignores when the cash inflows arrive.

C: It ignores the initial cost.

D: It cannot be used for projects over two years. **[1]**

**5.** Explain what a positive net present value shows. **[2]**

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**6.** Ormside Bakery is considering Project C, a new oven costing £80,000. Net cash inflows are expected to be £20,000, £30,000, £35,000 and £25,000 in years 1 to 4.

Discount factors at 10%: year 1 0.909; year 2 0.826; year 3 0.751; year 4 0.683.

**(a)** Calculate the payback period. **[3]**

**(b)** Calculate the average rate of return. **[3]**

**(c)** Calculate the net present value at 10%. **[4]**

**(d)** At 15% the discount factors are 0.870, 0.756, 0.658 and 0.572. Calculate the NPV at 15% and explain what the change shows. **[5]**

**7.** Ormside also considers Project D, a delivery van costing £80,000, with inflows of £10,000, £20,000, £30,000 and £50,000 in years 1 to 4. The NPV of Project D at 10% is +£2,290, its ARR is 9.4% and its payback is 3.4 years.

**(a)** Compare Projects C and D using payback, ARR and NPV at 10%. **[4]**

**(b)** Recommend and justify which project Ormside should choose. **[8]**

**8.** Explain **two** qualitative factors that Ormside should consider before buying the new oven. **[4]**

**9.** Evaluate the usefulness of payback as a method of investment appraisal for a small business with limited cash. **[6]**

**10.** A hotel group is considering a £2 million refurbishment. The NPV at its 8% discount rate is +£150,000, but the refurbishment will close the hotel for four months and the forecasts depend on a rise in tourism. Evaluate whether the hotel group should go ahead. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** The payback period is the length of time it takes for the cash inflows from an investment [1] to equal (recover) the initial cost [1]. **[2]**

**2.** £50,000 ÷ £15,000 [1] = **3.33 years** (about 3 years and 4 months) [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Money received today can be invested or used to earn a return [1], and future cash is less certain, so a future sum is worth less in present terms [1]. **[2]**

**4.** **B** [1]. **[1]**

**5.** A positive NPV shows that the present value of the cash inflows is greater than the initial cost [1], so the project earns more than the required rate of return and adds value to the business [1]. **[2]**

**6. (a)** Cumulative inflows: year 1 £20,000; year 2 £50,000; year 3 £85,000 [1]. After 2 years £30,000 is still to recover; year 3 inflow is £35,000, so 30,000 ÷ 35,000 = 0.857 [1]. Payback = **2.86 years** (about 2 years and 10 months) [1]. **[3]**

**(b)** Total inflows = £110,000; net return = £110,000 − £80,000 = £30,000 [1]. Average annual return = £30,000 ÷ 4 = £7,500 [1]. ARR = £7,500 ÷ £80,000 × 100 = **9.4%** (9.375%) [1]. **[3]**

**(c)** Present values: £20,000 × 0.909 = £18,180; £30,000 × 0.826 = £24,780; £35,000 × 0.751 = £26,285; £25,000 × 0.683 = £17,075 [1] [1]. Total = £86,320 [1]. NPV = £86,320 − £80,000 = **+£6,320** [1]. **[4]**

**(d)** Present values at 15%: £17,400; £22,680; £23,030; £14,300 [1] [1]. Total = £77,410 [1]. NPV = £77,410 − £80,000 = **−£2,590** [1]. The project is worthwhile at 10% but not at 15%, so the decision depends on the discount rate: if Ormside's cost of finance or required return is 15% or more, the project does not meet it [1]. **[5]**

**7. (a)** Payback: C is shorter (2.86 years against 3.4) [1]. ARR: both are 9.4%, because total inflows are the same (£110,000) [1]. NPV at 10%: C is +£6,320 and D +£2,290, so C is higher by £4,030 [1] [1]. **[4]**

**(b)** Recommend Project C [1]. It has the faster payback, so cash is recovered a little over half a year sooner [1], and the higher NPV, because its cash arrives earlier [1]. The ARR does not separate the projects, since it ignores timing [1].
Risks: both rely on forecast inflows, and C's NPV turns negative at 15%, so it is sensitive to the discount rate (the van's NPV would be lower still) [1].
Qualitative: a new oven may raise quality and capacity, and fit a baking business, whereas a van supports delivery and may open a new market but involves driver costs [1].
Judgement: C is the better choice on all the numerical measures, but Ormside should check the demand forecasts and whether it can afford the cash, since the van's longer payback leaves it more exposed if sales are weak [1]. This would change if a delivery service were a key part of Ormside's strategy [1]. **[8]**

**8.** Staff: bakers may need training to use the new oven, which has a cost and time, but may raise morale and skills [1] [1]. Brand and quality: a better oven may improve the product, which could raise customer satisfaction and reputation [1] [1]. (Also accept environmental impact, risk, strategic fit, or disruption during installation, each with a developed reason.) **[4]**

**9.** For: payback is simple to calculate and understand [1]; it shows how soon cash is recovered, which matters when the business has little cash and cannot wait [1]; a short payback reduces risk, because forecasts are less reliable the further ahead they look [1].
Against: it ignores all cash after the payback date, so a project with large later returns may be rejected [1]; it ignores the time value of money [1].
Judgement: payback is useful for a business with limited cash as a first screen, but it should be used with NPV or ARR before a final decision [1]. **[6]**

**10.** For: the NPV is positive at +£150,000, so the project is expected to return more than 8% and add value [1]; a refurbished hotel may attract more guests and higher prices, which supports long-term revenue [1]. The NPV already allows for the time value of money, so the benefit is not overstated by distant cash [1].
Against: £150,000 is small compared with a £2 million outlay (7.5% of the cost), so a small fall in the forecasts could make the NPV negative [1]. The forecasts depend on tourism, which is uncertain [1]. The four-month closure means lost income and possible job losses, which are costs not always included in the NPV [1]. Staff and regular guests may be lost [1].
Judgement: the numbers favour going ahead, but the margin is thin and the risk is high, so the group should test the sensitivity of the NPV to lower tourism and consider refurbishing in phases to keep part of the hotel open [1]. (Maximum 8 marks.) **[8]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Leaving out the fraction of a year in payback.
- Forgetting to subtract the initial cost to find NPV.
- Using total return instead of average annual return for ARR.
- Giving figures without comment on the decision.
- Ignoring qualitative factors in a recommendation.
