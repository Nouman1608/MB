Subtopic 13.1 of Cambridge International AS & A Level Physics 9702 (2025–2027) covers the gravitational field as a field of force, the definition of field strength as force per unit mass, and field lines. These notes are condensed for recall. For full explanations and worked examples, read the [Gravitational field study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-physics-9702-a-gravitational-field/); to apply the ideas, try the [Gravitational field practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-physics-9702-a-gravitational-field-practice/).

## Core definitions

- **Field of force**: a region in which an object with a particular property experiences a force. For gravity, the property is mass.
- **Gravitational field**: a region in which a mass experiences a force.
- **Gravitational field strength g**: the **force per unit mass** on a (small test) mass placed at that point.

```
g = F / m          W = mg
unit: N kg⁻¹  (= m s⁻²)
```

## Facts to hold on to

- g is a **vector**, directed towards the mass causing the field.
- The field exists whether or not a test mass is present; it is a property of the source.
- The test mass should be **small** so it does not disturb the field it measures.
- Gravity is **always attractive**, so gravitational field lines always point towards masses.
- Field strength equals the acceleration of free fall **only** when gravity is the only force acting.
- Fields from several masses add as **vectors**.

## Field-line rules

| Rule | What it shows |
|---|---|
| Arrow points towards the mass | Direction of the force on a small mass |
| Closer lines | Stronger field |
| Lines never cross | Only one resultant direction at each point |
| Lines end on masses, never start on them | Gravity only attracts |
| Lines meet a sphere's surface at 90° | The field of a uniform sphere is radial |

## The two patterns

| Pattern | Where | Appearance | Field |
|---|---|---|---|
| Radial | Around an isolated sphere or point mass | Straight lines to the centre, spreading out with distance | Weakens with distance |
| Uniform | Small region near a planet's surface | Parallel, equally spaced, perpendicular to surface | Same magnitude and direction everywhere |

The uniform field is a close-up of the radial field: over a small height the lines are nearly parallel.

**Two masses**: lines curve into both bodies; there is a point on the line between them, **closer to the smaller mass**, where the resultant field is zero.

## Comparing fields of force

| Field | Acts on | Field strength | Can repel? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gravitational | Mass | Force per unit mass (N kg⁻¹) | No |
| Electric | Charge | Force per unit positive charge (N C⁻¹) | Yes |

## Quick calculations

- 0.500 kg hanging at rest with a reading of 1.86 N: g = 3.72 N kg⁻¹.
- Two fields at right angles, 3.0 and 4.0 N kg⁻¹: resultant 5.0 N kg⁻¹ at 53° to the 3.0 N kg⁻¹ field.
- Two fields along the same line in opposite directions, 6.0 and 2.5 N kg⁻¹: resultant 3.5 N kg⁻¹ towards the stronger source.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Field of force | Region in which an object experiences a non-contact force |
| Test mass | A small mass used to detect and measure a field without disturbing it |
| Field line | Line showing the direction of the force on a small mass at each point |
| Radial field | Field whose lines meet at the centre of a sphere |
| Uniform field | Field with the same strength and direction at all points |
| Weight | The gravitational force on a mass, W = mg |

## Exam traps

- Writing "the force on a mass" without "per unit mass".
- Drawing arrows outwards, or lines that start on a mass.
- Lines that cross, or radial lines that are unevenly spaced or bent near the surface.
- Adding perpendicular field strengths arithmetically.
- Saying a resting object "has acceleration g" because it is in a field of strength g.
- Forgetting the unit, or giving N kg (no minus sign) instead of N kg⁻¹.

## Self-test

1. Define gravitational field strength.
2. What property must an object have to experience a force in a gravitational field?
3. Show that N kg⁻¹ is equivalent to m s⁻².
4. Why should a test mass be small?
5. Why do gravitational field lines never cross?
6. State one difference between gravitational and electric fields.
7. A 3.0 kg mass at rest on a spring balance on a moon reads 4.9 N. Calculate g there.
8. Describe the field lines near the surface of the Earth over a region of a few metres.
9. Two fields of 0.80 N kg⁻¹ and 0.60 N kg⁻¹ act at right angles at a point. Find the resultant field strength.
10. Where, between a planet and its moon, is the resultant field zero: nearer the planet or nearer the moon?
11. A book rests on a table where g = 9.81 N kg⁻¹. Why is its acceleration zero?
12. What does wider spacing between field lines tell you?

**Answers:** 1. The force per unit mass on a small mass placed at that point. 2. Mass. 3. 1 N = 1 kg m s⁻², so N kg⁻¹ = kg m s⁻² ÷ kg = m s⁻². 4. So that its own field does not alter the field being measured. 5. At each point the force on a mass has one direction only. 6. Gravitational forces are always attractive (electric forces can repel); or gravity acts on mass, electric fields act on charge. 7. 4.9 ÷ 3.0 = 1.6 N kg⁻¹. 8. Parallel, equally spaced lines, perpendicular to the surface, pointing downwards. 9. √(0.80² + 0.60²) = 1.0 N kg⁻¹. 10. Nearer the moon (the smaller mass). 11. The normal contact force from the table balances its weight, so the resultant force is zero. 12. A weaker field.
