## Quality: fit for purpose

A product has quality when it is **fit for purpose**: it does what the customer expects, reliably, for a price the customer thinks fair. Quality is judged by the customer, not by the business. A cheap umbrella that lasts a season may be fit for purpose for one buyer; the same umbrella is poor quality for a customer who wants one for ten years.

**Internal quality standards** are the targets and methods a business sets for itself, as opposed to external standards such as British or International Standards set by outside bodies.

## Quality control and quality assurance

**Quality control (QC)** checks products or services after, or at set points during, production to find faults. Examples are an inspector testing a sample of bottles or a manager checking a finished repair. The aim is to **detect** defects and stop faulty items from reaching the customer.

**Quality assurance (QA)** is a system built into the whole process to **prevent** defects. It covers the checking of materials from suppliers, clear working instructions, staff training, regular maintenance of machines and records that trace any fault to its cause. Responsibility is spread across all staff.

| | Quality control | Quality assurance |
|---|---|---|
| Aim | Find faults | Prevent faults |
| When | After or during production | Throughout, from design to delivery |
| Who | Inspectors or supervisors | All staff, with the system designed by management |
| Cost pattern | Inspection cost; faulty goods are made, then scrapped or reworked | Training and system cost first; fewer faults later |
| Weakness | Waste has already happened; sampling may miss faults | Needs commitment and time to set up; can become paperwork |

Most businesses use both.

## Why businesses set quality standards

- To meet customer expectations and win repeat orders.
- To protect reputation and brand image.
- To reduce waste, rework and returns.
- To reduce costs of complaints, refunds and replacements.
- To meet legal duties, for example for food safety and consumer protection.
- To support higher prices where customers see better quality.
- To keep staff and suppliers working to the same targets.

## Measuring wastage

**Wastage** is the materials or output lost during production because they are spoiled, faulty or scrapped. A common measure is the wastage rate.

Wastage rate (%) = (units wasted ÷ units started or input) × 100

The specification asks you to calculate and interpret wastage but gives no formula, so state yours. Some questions use materials (for example kilograms of flour) instead of units.

### Worked example 1: calculating and interpreting wastage

Lowther Bakery (invented) starts 50,000 loaves in a year and scraps 1,750 because they are misshapen or burnt. Each scrapped loaf costs £9 in ingredients, labour and energy.

Wastage rate = 1,750 ÷ 50,000 × 100 = **3.5%**. Cost of waste = 1,750 × £9 = **£15,750**.

The next year the bakery starts 52,000 loaves and scraps 1,196.

Wastage rate = 1,196 ÷ 52,000 × 100 = **2.3%**. Cost = 1,196 × £9 = £10,764.

Interpretation:

- The rate fell by 1.2 percentage points. That is the more useful comparison because output grew; the number of loaves scrapped fell by 554, which understates the improvement in a larger run.
- The saving on the cost of waste is £15,750 − £10,764 = **£4,986**.
- Had the rate stayed at 3.5%, scrapped loaves would be 3.5% × 52,000 = 1,820, so 624 fewer loaves were wasted than expected (1,820 − 1,196).
- A rate is only meaningful against a target or a benchmark. If good bakeries achieve 2%, Lowther still has room to improve.
- The cost per scrapped item matters: scrapping at the end of production wastes more than scrapping at the start, because more labour has been spent.

## Total quality management (TQM)

**Total quality management** is an approach in which quality is the responsibility of every employee and every department, not just inspectors, and in which the aim is to meet or exceed customer expectations through continuous improvement.

Its main features are:

- **Customer focus:** quality is defined by customer needs, and "customers" includes the next department in the process.
- **Everyone is responsible:** each worker checks their own work and passes on only good work.
- **Prevention, not detection:** the target is zero defects, with problems solved at their cause.
- **Continuous improvement:** small steps, often linked to kaizen.
- **Teamwork and empowerment:** quality circles or teams may solve problems, and workers can stop the line when they find a fault.
- **Training:** staff need the skills and information to do this.
- **Supplier involvement:** suppliers are expected to meet agreed standards.

**How and why TQM is used.** It is used in manufacturing and services, such as hospitals, hotels and banks, to cut the cost of poor quality, improve customer satisfaction and involve staff. Benefits are fewer defects, lower waste, improved morale through involvement and a strong reputation. Limitations: it takes a long time to change a culture, it needs commitment from senior managers, training is costly, and staff may suffer "improvement fatigue" if targets keep rising. If it is not supported by managers it becomes a slogan.

## Worked example 2: the value of training

Lowther Bakery spends £12,000 on a quality-training programme for its 20 production staff. It teaches temperature control, shaping and basic problem-solving. After it, the wastage rate falls from 3.5% to 2.3% on 50,000 loaves, and customer returns fall, saving £3,000 a year.

Reduction in scrapped loaves = (3.5% − 2.3%) × 50,000 = 600 loaves. Saving on waste = 600 × £9 = **£5,400**.

Total annual saving = £5,400 + £3,000 = **£8,400**.

Payback = £12,000 ÷ £8,400 = **1.4 years**.

**Evaluation.**

- The payback is short, so the programme looks worthwhile if the improvement lasts. It may not: staff who leave take their skills away, and refresher training is a continuing cost.
- Some of the improvement may be due to other changes, such as new ovens, so the full saving may not come from training.
- Non-financial benefits: staff feel valued and have more responsibility, which may reduce turnover; customers trust the product more; the bakery can win contracts with supermarkets that insist on standards.
- Costs not in the figures: the lost production while staff are in training.

**Judgement.** Training is a high-value way to improve quality because it tackles the cause of faults. It works best alongside clear procedures, good materials and managers who act on problems that staff raise.

## Training and the stakeholders

| Stakeholder | Possible benefit | Possible cost |
|---|---|---|
| Owners | Lower waste; stronger reputation | Cost of training; lost output during training |
| Employees | New skills; more responsibility; career prospects | Pressure to meet targets; time spent away from work |
| Customers | Fewer faulty products; more reliable service | Higher prices if costs are passed on |
| Suppliers | Clear specifications; long-term contracts | Higher standards to meet |

## Common mistakes

- Treating quality control and quality assurance as the same thing.
- Writing that quality control "prevents" defects. It finds them; assurance prevents them.
- Using the number of faulty units instead of a rate when comparing years of different size.
- Dividing waste by output instead of by units started or input.
- Describing TQM as inspection. It is a culture in which everyone takes responsibility.
- Claiming that training always improves quality without comparing cost and benefit.

## Exam technique

When asked to distinguish, give the difference in a single sentence using words such as "whereas". When asked to calculate wastage, write the formula, show the working and add a unit. When asked to evaluate training, include a cost and benefit, a time frame and a conclusion that depends on something specific in the case.
