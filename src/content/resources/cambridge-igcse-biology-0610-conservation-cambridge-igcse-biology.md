This guide goes deeper into one sub-topic of the Cambridge IGCSE Biology 0610 syllabus for examination in 2026, 2027 and 2028: **20.4 Conservation**, the last part of topic 20, Human influences on ecosystems. It has nine learning outcomes. Outcomes 1 to 4 are Core. Outcomes 5 to 9, on conserving forests and fish stocks, reasons for conservation, AI and IVF, and the risks of low genetic variation, are Supplement content, so they are **Extended only** (Papers 2 and 4).

The [human influences on ecosystems study guide](/resources/igcse-biology-0610-human-influences-on-ecosystems/) lists each method. This page explains why each method works and builds full answers for the "explain" outcomes. Afterwards, use the [conservation revision notes](/resources/cambridge-igcse-biology-0610-conservation-cambridge-igcse-biology-revision-notes/) and the [conservation practice questions](/resources/cambridge-igcse-biology-0610-conservation-cambridge-igcse-biology-practice/).

## 20.4.1 and 20.4.2 Sustainable resources

> A sustainable resource is one which is **produced as rapidly as it is removed** from the environment **so that it does not run out**.

Both halves earn marks. The syllabus limits the examples of resources that can be managed sustainably to **forests** and **fish stocks**. Fossil fuels are not sustainable, because they formed over millions of years and are used far faster.

### Worked example 1: is the catch sustainable?

A fish stock contains 400 000 tonnes of fish. Each year, reproduction and growth add about 60 000 tonnes (illustrative figures).

- If boats catch **50 000 tonnes** a year, less is removed than is produced: the stock is **sustainable** and can grow slightly.
- If boats catch **90 000 tonnes** a year, 30 000 tonnes more is removed than is produced each year. The stock falls; after one year it is 400 000 − 30 000 = **370 000 tonnes**, and it keeps shrinking. The catch is **not sustainable**.

## 20.4.3 Why organisms become endangered or extinct

An **endangered** species has so few individuals that it is at risk of extinction. Each cause works by raising deaths or reducing births.

| Cause | How it reduces the population |
|---|---|
| Climate change | habitat becomes too hot, dry or wet; food plants or prey change; species cannot move or adapt fast enough |
| Habitat destruction | food, shelter and breeding sites are removed |
| Hunting | adults are killed faster than they are replaced; often for meat, skins or horns |
| Overharvesting | a species used as a resource, such as a fish or a timber tree, is removed faster than it reproduces |
| Pollution | toxic substances kill organisms or reduce breeding; low oxygen in polluted water kills fish |
| Introduced species | a new predator eats native species, or a competitor takes their food, or it brings a disease |

In an answer, always add the consequence: "so the death rate becomes higher than the birth rate".

## 20.4.4 Conserving endangered species (Core)

- **Monitoring and protecting species and habitats:** counting populations shows whether numbers are falling; nature reserves protect the habitat; laws ban hunting or trade.
- **Education:** people learn why the species matters and how to avoid harming it, for example by not buying products made from it.
- **Captive breeding programmes:** animals are bred in zoos or breeding centres, where they are safe from predators and hunting, and offspring are released into protected habitats.
- **Seed banks:** seeds are dried and stored at low temperature, so they stay alive for many years; they can be grown to replace lost wild populations. Seeds take up little space, so many species can be stored.

## 20.4.5 Conserving forests (Extended only)

| Method | Why it conserves the forest |
|---|---|
| Education | people understand the value of forests and buy timber from sustainable sources |
| Protected areas | logging and clearing are banned or limited, so habitats and species survive |
| Quotas | a limit on the number of trees felled keeps removal below the rate of regrowth |
| Replanting | new trees replace felled ones, so the forest is produced as rapidly as it is removed |

## 20.4.6 Conserving fish stocks (Extended only)

| Method | Why it conserves the stock |
|---|---|
| Education | consumers choose sustainably caught fish; fishers understand why limits matter |
| Closed seasons | no fishing during the breeding season, so adults can reproduce |
| Protected areas | no fishing in breeding or nursery grounds, so young fish survive |
| Controlled net types and mesh size | a larger mesh lets young fish escape so they survive to breed; banning certain nets reduces capture of other species |
| Quotas | a limit on the mass or number caught keeps removal below the rate of reproduction |
| Monitoring | counting the stock shows whether it is falling, so quotas can be adjusted |

### Worked example 2: annotated model answer

**Question:** Explain how closed seasons and mesh size help to conserve a fish stock. [4]

**Answer:** During a closed season no fishing is allowed in the breeding season (1), so adult fish can reproduce and replace those caught (1). A larger mesh size allows young, small fish to escape through the net (1), so they survive to become adults and breed (1).

## 20.4.7 Reasons for conservation programmes (Extended only)

The syllabus limits these to four:

1. **Maintaining or increasing biodiversity.**
2. **Reducing extinction.**
3. **Protecting vulnerable ecosystems,** such as coral reefs or rainforests that are easily damaged.
4. **Maintaining ecosystem functions:** **nutrient cycling** (decomposers and plants recycle carbon and nitrogen) and **resource provision**, including **food**, **drugs** (many medicines were first found in plants), **fuel** (wood) and **genes** (wild relatives of crops may carry alleles for disease resistance useful in breeding).

## 20.4.8 AI and IVF in captive breeding (Extended only)

- **Artificial insemination (AI):** sperm is collected from a male and inserted into a female's reproductive tract. Sperm can be frozen and transported between zoos or countries, so the animals themselves need not be moved, and a male in one country can father offspring in another.
- **In vitro fertilisation (IVF):** eggs are collected from a female and fertilised by sperm in a dish in a laboratory. The resulting embryos are placed in the uterus of a female to develop; this can be a different female from the one that produced the eggs.

Both methods let breeders **choose unrelated parents**, which helps keep genetic variation in the captive population.

## 20.4.9 Risks when a population becomes small (Extended only)

When a population falls to a few individuals, **genetic variation is reduced**: many alleles are lost and the survivors are often closely related. The risks:

- **Less ability to survive change.** If a new disease or a change in the environment occurs, it is less likely that any individuals carry alleles that help them survive, so the whole population may die.
- **More inbreeding.** Related individuals breed together, so offspring are more likely to inherit two copies of a harmful recessive allele and show the harmful condition.
- **Higher risk of extinction** as a result of both.

### Worked example 3: applying the idea

A wild cat species falls to 40 individuals, all descended from a few survivors. A virus appears that kills most of the cats it infects. Explain why the species is at risk. [3]

**Answer:** The small population has low genetic variation (1). It is unlikely that any cats have alleles giving resistance to the virus (1), so most or all cats could die and the species could become extinct (1).

## Common mistakes

- Defining a sustainable resource as one that "will not run out" without "produced as rapidly as it is removed".
- Listing causes of endangerment without saying how each reduces the population.
- (Extended) Saying a larger mesh "catches fewer fish" without explaining that young fish escape to breed.
- (Extended) Mixing up AI (sperm placed in a female) and IVF (fertilisation outside the body).
- (Extended) Writing that a small population "cannot reproduce". It can, but its lack of variation makes it vulnerable.

## Exam technique for 20.4 command words

- **Describe** a sustainable resource: both parts of the syllabus wording.
- **Explain** endangerment: cause → effect on births or deaths.
- **Explain** forest and fish methods (Extended): method → how removal stays below production.
- **Explain** risks of small populations (Extended): low variation → fewer helpful alleles → extinction risk.

## Frequently asked questions

**Are AI and IVF Core or Extended in Cambridge IGCSE Biology 0610?**
Describing the use of AI and IVF in captive breeding programmes is Supplement content, so it is Extended only. Captive breeding programmes themselves are Core.

**Why is low genetic variation a risk to an endangered species?**
With fewer alleles in the population, it is less likely that any individuals can survive a new disease or a change in the environment, so the species is more likely to become extinct.
