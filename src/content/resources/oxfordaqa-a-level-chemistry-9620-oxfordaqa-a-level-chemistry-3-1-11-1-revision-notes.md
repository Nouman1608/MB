These notes condense **3.1.11.1 Rate equations** from the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Chemistry (9620) specification: the rate equation Rate = k[A]^m[B]^n with orders of 0, 1 or 2, definitions of order and rate constant, rate calculations, the effect of temperature on k, and the Arrhenius equation in both forms. The specification says the equations and R will be given when required. This is International A2 content (**International A-level only**), assessed in Unit 4.

For full worked examples see the [rate equations study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-1-11-1/), and test yourself with the [rate equations practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-1-11-1-practice/). The whole of section 3.1.11 is summarised in the [section 3.1.11 revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-rate-equations-revision-notes/).

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Rate of reaction | Change in concentration of a reactant or product per unit time (mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹) |
| Order with respect to a reactant | The power to which its concentration is raised in the rate equation |
| Overall order | Sum of the individual orders |
| Rate constant, k | Constant of proportionality in the rate equation; fixed for a reaction at a given temperature |
| Activation energy, Ea | Minimum energy needed for a collision to lead to reaction (J mol⁻¹ in the Arrhenius equation) |
| Arrhenius constant, A | The constant in k = Ae^(−Ea/RT); same units as k |

## Core facts

- Rate = k[A]^m[B]^n, with m and n each 0, 1 or 2 in this specification.
- Orders come from experiment, not from the balanced equation.
- A zero-order reactant does not change the rate when its concentration changes, and [A]⁰ = 1.
- **Only temperature changes k** (a catalyst changes it too, by lowering Ea). Concentration, pressure and volume change the rate, not k.
- Raising T increases k because a larger proportion of molecules have energy ≥ Ea.

## Scaling factors

Rate factor = (concentration factor)^order, multiplied across all reactants.

| Change | Rate = k[A] | Rate = k[A]² | Rate = k[A][B] | Rate = k[A]²[B] |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| [A] × 2 | × 2 | × 4 | × 2 | × 4 |
| [A] × 2 and [B] × 2 | × 2 | × 4 | × 4 | × 8 |
| Volume doubled (all concentrations halved) | × 1/2 | × 1/4 | × 1/4 | × 1/8 |

## Units of k

Overall order 0: mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹. Order 1: s⁻¹. Order 2: mol⁻¹ dm³ s⁻¹. Order 3: mol⁻² dm⁶ s⁻¹. Order 4: mol⁻³ dm⁹ s⁻¹.

Method: k = rate ÷ (concentration terms), then cancel units.

## Arrhenius toolkit

| Task | Equation to use |
|---|---|
| k at one temperature | k = Ae^(−Ea/RT) |
| Ea or A from a graph | ln k = −Ea/RT + ln A; plot ln k against 1/T; gradient = −Ea/R; intercept = ln A |
| Compare k at two temperatures | ln(k₂/k₁) = (Ea/R)(1/T₁ − 1/T₂), from subtracting the log form at each temperature |
| A from one data point | A = k × e^(Ea/RT) |

Units check: Ea in J mol⁻¹, T in K, R = 8.31 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹.

**Typical size of the effect:** for Ea = 50 kJ mol⁻¹, a 10 K rise near room temperature roughly doubles k. The larger Ea, the bigger the factor.

## Exam traps

- Using kJ mol⁻¹ in the exponent: the answer is out by a factor that is impossible to spot later.
- Celsius temperatures in 1/T.
- Saying "a higher concentration increases the rate constant".
- Writing the order definition as "the number of moles in the equation".
- Forgetting negative powers in units of k (mol⁻¹ dm³ s⁻¹, not mol dm³ s⁻¹).
- Reading the Arrhenius gradient as +Ea/R and giving a negative Ea.
- Using the two end points of the data rather than a line of best fit for the gradient.

## Quick self-test

1. Define the order of reaction with respect to a reactant.
2. Define the rate constant.
3. Deduce the units of k for Rate = k[A]²[B].
4. Rate = k[P][Q]. [P] is doubled and [Q] tripled. By what factor does the rate change?
5. Rate = k[R]². The rate is 4.8 × 10⁻⁴ mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹ when [R] = 0.020 mol dm⁻³. Calculate k with units.
6. Explain why k increases when the temperature is raised.
7. An Arrhenius plot has gradient −9.20 × 10³ K. Calculate Ea in kJ mol⁻¹.
8. A first-order reaction has A = 2.0 × 10¹¹ s⁻¹ and Ea = 75.0 kJ mol⁻¹. Calculate k at 350 K.
9. State what is plotted on each axis to obtain a straight line from the Arrhenius equation, and what the intercept represents.
10. A reactant is zero order. Its concentration is doubled. What happens to the rate?
11. Rate = k[A][B]. The reaction mixture is diluted to three times its volume. By what factor does the rate change?
12. State the units of A for a second-order reaction.

### Answers

1. The power to which the concentration of that reactant is raised in the rate equation.
2. The constant of proportionality linking the rate to the concentration terms in the rate equation; fixed at a given temperature.
3. k = rate / [A]²[B] = mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹ / (mol dm⁻³)³ = **mol⁻² dm⁶ s⁻¹**.
4. 2 × 3 = **× 6**.
5. k = 4.8 × 10⁻⁴ / (0.020)² = **1.2 mol⁻¹ dm³ s⁻¹**.
6. At a higher temperature a larger proportion of molecules have energy equal to or greater than Ea, so a larger proportion of collisions are successful.
7. Ea = 9.20 × 10³ × 8.31 = 7.65 × 10⁴ J mol⁻¹ = **76.5 kJ mol⁻¹**.
8. Ea/RT = 75 000 / (8.31 × 350) = 25.79; k = 2.0 × 10¹¹ × e^(−25.79) = **1.3 s⁻¹**.
9. ln k on the y-axis, 1/T on the x-axis; intercept = **ln A**.
10. The rate is **unchanged**.
11. Each concentration is divided by 3: rate × (1/3)(1/3) = **× 1/9**.
12. **mol⁻¹ dm³ s⁻¹** (the same as k).
