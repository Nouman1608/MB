This guide covers section 2.2, Demand, of the OCR GCSE (9-1) Economics specification (J205), Version 2.0, for first assessment in 2019, in component J205/01. The specification is not tiered. All products, firms and figures are invented.

The [markets, specialisation, demand and elasticity guide](/resources/ocr-gcse-economics-markets-sectors-specialisation-and-demand-with-price/) introduces demand alongside section 2.1. This page concentrates on 2.2 alone: building a market demand curve from data, a full list of shift factors, consequences in figures, and price elasticity of demand (PED) linked to revenue. Condense it with the [2.2 revision notes](/resources/ocr-gcse-economics-j205-demand-revision-notes/), then use the [2.2 practice questions](/resources/ocr-gcse-economics-j205-demand-practice/).

## What the specification asks

Learners should be able to:

- explain what is meant by demand;
- draw and explain a demand curve using data, including individual and market demand;
- draw shifts of, and movements along, the demand curve;
- analyse the causes and consequences for consumers and producers of shifts of, and movements along, the demand curve;
- explain price elasticity of demand;
- draw demand curves of different elasticity;
- evaluate the importance of price elasticity of demand for consumers and producers.

## What demand means

**Demand** is the quantity of a good or service that consumers are willing **and able** to buy at each price over a period of time. Wanting something is not enough; a consumer must be able to pay. The time period matters too: "300 a week" and "300 a year" describe very different markets.

Demand curves normally slope downwards from left to right: as price falls, the quantity demanded rises. A lower price makes the good more affordable, and it becomes better value compared with alternatives.

## Drawing a demand curve from data

**Worked example: cinema tickets in Northgate (invented).** Three consumers' monthly demand:

| Price (£) | Consumer A | Consumer B | Consumer C | Market demand |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 4 | 6 | 4 | 8 | 18 |
| 6 | 5 | 3 | 6 | 14 |
| 8 | 3 | 2 | 4 | 9 |
| 10 | 2 | 1 | 2 | 5 |

Market demand is the **sum of individual demands at each price**: at £6, 5 + 3 + 6 = 14.

Steps for drawing it:

1. Put **price** on the vertical axis and **quantity demanded** on the horizontal axis, with units ("Price (£)", "Tickets per month").
2. Choose even scales that fit the data, starting both axes at zero.
3. Plot each pair, such as (14, £6).
4. Join the points with a smooth line or ruled line and label it **D**.

The market curve lies further to the right than any individual curve because it adds everyone's demand together.

## Movements along versus shifts

- A change in the good's **own price** causes a **movement along** the curve: an extension (price falls, more demanded) or a contraction (price rises, less demanded).
- A change in **anything else** that affects demand causes the whole curve to **shift**: right for an increase, left for a decrease.

| Cause of a shift | Shift right when... | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Income | incomes rise (for most goods) | More restaurant meals as wages grow |
| Price of a substitute | a substitute becomes dearer | Train fares rise, so coach demand rises |
| Price of a complement | a complement becomes cheaper | Cheaper games consoles raise demand for games |
| Tastes and fashion | the product becomes popular | A celebrity wears a brand of trainers |
| Advertising | a successful campaign runs | A cereal advert raises sales |
| Population | more potential buyers | A new housing estate near a supermarket |
| Seasons and weather | conditions favour the product | Sun cream in summer |

## Consequences in figures

**Worked example: umbrellas in a wet week (invented).** A market stall area's weekly demand and supply:

| Price (£) | Normal demand | Wet-week demand | Supply |
|---|---|---|---|
| 8 | 50 | 70 | 10 |
| 10 | 40 | 60 | 20 |
| 12 | 30 | 50 | 30 |
| 14 | 20 | 40 | 40 |
| 16 | 10 | 30 | 50 |

- Normal week: equilibrium £12 and 30 umbrellas; spending = 12 × 30 = £360.
- Wet week: demand shifts right by 20 at every price. At £12 there is now a shortage of 50 − 30 = 20.
- Sellers raise the price; the market clears at £14 and 40 umbrellas; spending = 14 × 40 = £560.

**For producers:** price rises by 2 ÷ 12 × 100 = 16.7% and sales rise by 10 ÷ 30 × 100 = 33.3%, so revenue rises from £360 to £560.
**For consumers:** more umbrellas are bought, but each costs more; some buyers who would have paid £12 now go without.

The analysis chain examiners reward: **cause → which curve shifts and which way → shortage or surplus at the old price → new price and quantity → effect on consumers and on producers.**

## Price elasticity of demand

**PED** measures how responsive the quantity demanded is to a change in price.

```
PED = % change in quantity demanded ÷ % change in price
```

The answer is normally negative, because price and quantity demanded move in opposite directions. Judge elasticity by its size, ignoring the minus sign:

| Value (ignoring sign) | Description | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | Perfectly inelastic | Quantity does not change at all |
| Between 0 and 1 | Inelastic | Quantity changes by a smaller % than price |
| 1 | Unitary | Same % change in quantity and price |
| Greater than 1 | Elastic | Quantity changes by a larger % than price |

**Worked example 1: a bus fare (invented).** The fare rises from £2.00 to £2.40; journeys fall from 50,000 to 46,000 a week.

```
% change in price    = (2.40 − 2.00) ÷ 2.00 × 100 = +20%
% change in quantity = (46,000 − 50,000) ÷ 50,000 × 100 = −8%
PED = −8 ÷ 20 = −0.4   (inelastic)
Revenue before = 2.00 × 50,000 = £100,000
Revenue after  = 2.40 × 46,000 = £110,400
```

**Worked example 2: a branded cereal (invented).** The price is cut from £3.00 to £2.70; sales rise from 2,000 to 2,500 boxes a week.

```
% change in price    = −10%
% change in quantity = +25%
PED = 25 ÷ −10 = −2.5   (elastic)
Revenue before = 3.00 × 2,000 = £6,000
Revenue after  = 2.70 × 2,500 = £6,750
```

The rule that follows: when demand is **inelastic**, a price **rise** increases total revenue; when demand is **elastic**, a price **cut** increases total revenue.

### What makes demand elastic or inelastic

- **Substitutes:** many close substitutes make demand more elastic. One brand of cereal has many; bus travel in a town without trains has few.
- **Necessity or luxury:** necessities tend to be inelastic.
- **Share of income:** an item that takes a large share of income, such as a holiday, tends to be more elastic than a cheap item, such as salt.
- **Habit:** habit-forming products tend to be inelastic.
- **Time:** demand becomes more elastic over time as people find alternatives.

### Drawing curves of different elasticity

On the same axes and scale:

- **Perfectly inelastic:** a vertical line. Quantity is fixed whatever the price.
- **Perfectly elastic:** a horizontal line. Any price rise loses all sales.
- **Relatively inelastic:** a steep curve. A large price change gives a small quantity change.
- **Relatively elastic:** a shallow curve. A small price change gives a large quantity change.

Label each curve, and show a price change with dotted lines to both axes so the different quantity responses are visible.

## Why PED matters

**For producers:** PED tells a firm whether a price change will raise or lower revenue. A firm with inelastic demand can raise its price with a small loss of sales; a firm facing elastic demand risks losing many customers to rivals. Firms try to make demand less elastic through branding and loyalty schemes.

**For consumers:** when demand is inelastic, consumers bear price rises because they have few alternatives; this hits people on low incomes hardest when the good is a necessity, such as heating fuel. When demand is elastic, consumers can switch, so they are protected by competition.

**Limits:** PED is an estimate from past data; it changes over time, differs between groups of consumers, and other factors (such as income) may change at the same time as price. A strong evaluation says how reliable the estimate is.

## Common mistakes

- Shifting the curve when only the good's own price changed.
- Adding prices instead of quantities when finding market demand.
- Dividing % change in price by % change in quantity (the formula is the other way round).
- Calling a PED of −0.4 "elastic" because it is negative.
- Saying a price rise always raises revenue.
- Drawing curves of different elasticity on different scales.

## Exam technique

- **Draw:** labelled axes, curves labelled D1 and D2, original and new prices and quantities marked.
- **Calculate PED:** show both percentage changes, then divide; state whether elastic or inelastic.
- **Analyse:** use the cause-to-consequence chain above.
- **Evaluate PED:** consider producers and consumers, and question the reliability of the estimate.
