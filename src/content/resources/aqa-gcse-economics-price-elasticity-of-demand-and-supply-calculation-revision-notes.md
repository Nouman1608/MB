## Key definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Price elasticity of demand (PED) | The size of the response of quantity demanded to a price change |
| Price elasticity of supply (PES) | The size of the response of quantity supplied to a price change |
| Price elastic | Quantity changes by a bigger percentage than price (size above 1) |
| Price inelastic | Quantity changes by a smaller percentage than price (size below 1) |
| Unit elastic | The two percentage changes are equal (size exactly 1) |

## The two formulas

| | Formula | Usual sign |
|---|---|---|
| PED | %ΔQd ÷ %ΔP (percentage change in quantity demanded over percentage change in price) | Negative |
| PES | %ΔQs ÷ %ΔP (percentage change in quantity supplied over percentage change in price) | Positive |

Percentage change = change ÷ original value × 100.

Remember that quantity always goes on the top line. Price goes underneath.

## Method in steps

1. Work out the change in price and the change in quantity (new minus old).
2. Turn each change into a percentage of its starting value.
3. Put the quantity percentage over the price percentage.
4. Compare the size with 1 and state elastic or inelastic.
5. If asked, say what it means for revenue, for the firm or for consumers.

## Worked reminder: PED

A shop (illustrative) raises a price from £50 to £55. Sales fall from 400 to 320.

```
% change in price    = 5 / 50 × 100   = +10%
% change in quantity = -80 / 400 × 100 = -20%
PED = -20 / +10 = -2.0
```

The size is 2.0, which is above 1, so demand is **price elastic**. Revenue falls from £20,000 to £17,600 (55 × 320).

## Worked reminder: PES

A workshop (illustrative) sees price rise from £4 to £5. Output rises from 800 to 1,000.

```
% change in price    = 1 / 4 × 100     = +25%
% change in quantity = 200 / 800 × 100 = +25%
PES = +25 / +25 = +1.0
```

PES is exactly 1, so supply is **unit elastic**.

## Rearranging the formula

| You know | You want | Method |
|---|---|---|
| PED and % change in price | % change in quantity | PED × % change in price |
| PED and % change in quantity | % change in price | % change in quantity ÷ PED |
| PES and % change in price | % change in quantity supplied | PES × % change in price |

Example: PES is 0.5 and quantity supplied is to rise 6%. Price must rise 6 ÷ 0.5 = 12%.

## Factors affecting PED

| Factor | More elastic demand | More inelastic demand |
|---|---|---|
| Substitutes | Plenty of close alternatives | Hardly any |
| Type of good | Luxury | Necessity |
| Share of income | Large share | Small share |
| Habit | Not habit forming | Habit forming or addictive |
| Time | Long run, when buyers can adjust | Short run |
| How the good is defined | A single brand | A broad category |

## Factors affecting PES

| Factor | More elastic supply | More inelastic supply |
|---|---|---|
| Spare capacity | Idle machines and workers | Running at full capacity |
| Stocks | Finished goods in store | No stocks, or perishable goods |
| Time | Long run | Short run |
| Production period | Quick to make | Slow to make, such as crops or buildings |
| Switching resources | Easy to move to another product | Specialist machines and skills |

## Implications at a glance

| If | Producers | Consumers |
|---|---|---|
| Demand is inelastic | Price rise raises revenue; pricing power | Pay more, can do little about it; taxes on the good hit them hard |
| Demand is elastic | Price cut raises revenue; competing on price; rises risk losing sales | More choice and bargaining power |
| Supply is elastic | Can meet rising demand quickly | Extra demand met with more output and smaller price rises |
| Supply is inelastic | Cannot respond quickly; may miss sales or be left with unsold stock | Prices jump when demand rises; shortages |

## Must-know distinctions

- **Steepness is only a guide.** Calculate the number rather than relying on how a curve looks.
- **Negative sign versus size.** A PED of -3 is more elastic than -0.5. Compare the sizes.
- **Short run versus long run.** Both PED and PES usually rise with time.
- **Revenue versus profit.** Revenue is price times quantity. Profit also depends on costs.
- **Demand versus quantity demanded.** A price change is a movement along the curve and elasticity measures its size. Do not describe it as a shift.

## Worked reminder: a price cut and a forecast

A cafe (illustrative) cuts a price from £4.00 to £3.20. Sales rise from 250 to 300 a day.

```
% change in price    = -0.80 / 4.00 × 100 = -20%
% change in quantity = +50 / 250 × 100    = +20%
PED = +20 / -20 = -1.0
```

The size is exactly 1, so demand is unit elastic. Revenue is £1,000 before (4.00 × 250) and £960 after (3.20 × 300). Revenue is almost unchanged, but it dips slightly because the percentage changes are measured from different starting points.

Forecast: a firm sells 800 units and PED is -0.5. A 10% price rise gives -0.5 × 10% = -5%, so sales fall to 800 × 0.95 = 760.

## Revenue reminders

| Situation | Price rise | Price cut |
|---|---|---|
| Elastic (size above 1) | Revenue falls | Revenue rises |
| Inelastic (size below 1) | Revenue rises | Revenue falls |
| Unit elastic (size 1) | Revenue roughly unchanged | Revenue roughly unchanged |

Revenue = price × quantity. For a quick check, multiply the price index by the quantity index. Price at 110 and quantity at 90 gives 110 × 90 ÷ 100 = 99, a fall of 1%.

## Command words

- **Calculate**: show both percentage changes, then the division, and give the answer with its sign.
- **State**: a short answer, no explanation needed.
- **Explain**: give the reason and the consequence, for example "few substitutes, so buyers keep buying, so demand is inelastic".
- **Evaluate**: weigh both sides, include short run against long run, and finish with a judgement.

## Short evaluation chain

"A firm with inelastic demand should raise price."

- Revenue rises in the short term because quantity falls by a smaller percentage than price rises.
- But rivals may enter or respond with lower prices.
- Customers may find substitutes over time, so the PED estimate may not hold.
- The estimate may come from a single past change with other factors moving too.
- Higher prices may harm goodwill, particularly for a necessity.
- A recommendation depends on how long the firm plans to stay, on costs and on how easily rivals can respond.

## Quick self-test

1. State the formula for PED.
2. A price rises from £8 to £10 and quantity demanded falls from 1,200 to 1,080. Calculate PED and say whether demand is elastic or inelastic.
3. A 5% rise in price leads to a 15% rise in quantity supplied. Calculate PES.
4. PED is -0.8 and the firm raises price 10%. What is the percentage change in quantity demanded? If sales were 500, what are they now?
5. Give two factors that make demand for a good more elastic.
6. Name two things that raise the responsiveness of supply.
7. Price falls 20% and quantity demanded rises 10%. Calculate PED. What happens to revenue?
8. A firm has price elastic demand and wants to raise revenue. Should it raise or cut price? Explain.
9. Why is the supply of fresh strawberries inelastic in the short run?
10. PES is 0.5 and quantity supplied needs to rise 6%. What price rise is needed?

### Answers

1. PED = %ΔQd ÷ %ΔP, the percentage change in quantity demanded over the percentage change in price.
2. Price +25%, quantity -10%. PED = -10 ÷ 25 = **-0.4**. The size is below 1, so demand is **inelastic**.
3. PES = 15 ÷ 5 = **3.0** (elastic).
4. -0.8 × 10% = **-8%**. New sales = 500 × 0.92 = **460**.
5. Any two from: many close substitutes, a luxury, a large share of income, a long time to adjust, a narrowly defined good.
6. Any two from: spare capacity, stocks of finished goods, a long time to adjust, a short production period, resources easily switched.
7. PED = 10 ÷ -20 = **-0.5**. Demand is inelastic, so revenue **falls**. As an index, 80% of the quantity at 110% of the price gives 88% of the revenue.
8. **Cut** price. Quantity demanded rises by a bigger percentage than price falls, so revenue rises.
9. Strawberries take weeks to grow and cannot be stored for long, so output cannot respond quickly to a price rise.
10. **12%** (6 ÷ 0.5).

## Where marks are usually lost

- Taking a percentage change of the new value, not the starting one.
- Putting price on the top of the fraction.
- Writing a bare number and not stating whether demand or supply is elastic or inelastic.
- Reading a negative PED as "inelastic". Use the size.
- Writing "demand does not change" when it means "demand changes by a smaller percentage".
- Writing "substitutes" with no link to buyers switching away when price rises.
- Using revenue and profit as if they were the same thing.
- Giving only one side of a pricing decision when the question asks you to evaluate or advise.
- Leaving out the short-run and long-run difference when a question mentions time.
- Using a PED calculated for one product to talk about a different product.
