These questions cover section **3.3.3.1 Nucleophilic substitution** of the AQA AS and A-level Chemistry specification (7404/7405), for teaching from September 2015. It is AS content, examined on Paper 2. Learn the ideas first in the [Nucleophilic substitution study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-nucleophilic-substitution/) and the [Nucleophilic substitution revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-nucleophilic-substitution-revision-notes/). Use Aᵣ values H = 1.0, C = 12.0, N = 14.0, Cl = 35.5, Br = 79.9, Ag = 107.9.

## Questions

**1.** State what is meant by the term nucleophile. **[1]**

**2.** Explain why the carbon atom in the C–Br bond of 1-bromopropane is attacked by nucleophiles. **[2]**

**3.** 1-chloropropane is converted into propan-1-ol.

**(a)** Give the reagent and conditions. **[2]**

**(b)** Write an equation for the reaction. **[1]**

**(c)** Name the type of mechanism. **[1]**

**4.** Outline the mechanism for the reaction of 1-bromobutane with cyanide ions. Include curly arrows, the dipole and the lone pair, and state the name of the organic product. **[4]**

**5.** Bromoethane reacts with a large excess of ammonia in a sealed tube.

**(a)** Write an equation for the overall reaction. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain why ammonia is used in large excess. **[1]**

**(c)** Explain why a sealed tube is used. **[1]**

**6.** Chloroethane, bromoethane and iodoethane are each heated with aqueous sodium hydroxide.

**(a)** Put the three compounds in order of increasing rate of reaction. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain this order. **[2]**

**(c)** A student says chloroethane should react fastest because the C–Cl bond is the most polar. Explain why this is not correct. **[2]**

**7.** A student compares the rates of hydrolysis of 1-chlorobutane, 1-bromobutane and 1-iodobutane using aqueous silver nitrate.

**(a)** Write the ionic equation for the reaction that produces the precipitate. **[1]**

**(b)** State the colour of each precipitate. **[2]**

**(c)** State two variables that must be controlled. **[2]**

**(d)** Explain why ethanol is added to each tube. **[1]**

**8.** Hydrolysis of a sample of 1-chlorobutane (Mᵣ = 92.5) is carried out and the chloride ions are precipitated as silver chloride. The mass of dried silver chloride is 2.868 g (Mᵣ = 143.4).

**(a)** Calculate the amount, in mol, of silver chloride formed. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the mass of 1-chlorobutane that was hydrolysed. **[2]**

**9.** 10.89 g of bromoethane (Mᵣ = 108.9) is heated under reflux with excess potassium cyanide in aqueous ethanol. 3.85 g of propanenitrile (Mᵣ = 55.0) is obtained. Calculate the percentage yield. **[3]**

**10.** Design a route from 1-bromopropane to butylamine, CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂NH₂. Give the reagents and conditions for each step and the intermediate compound. Explain why the route cannot be completed in one step using ammonia. **[5]**

## Answers

**1.** A species that donates a lone pair of electrons to form a covalent bond [1]. **[1]**

**2.** Bromine is more electronegative than carbon, so the C–Br bond is polar [1]; the carbon has a partial positive charge (δ+), which attracts a nucleophile [1]. **[2]**

**3. (a)** Aqueous sodium (or potassium) hydroxide [1]; warm or heat under reflux [1]

**(b)** CH₃CH₂CH₂Cl + OH⁻ → CH₃CH₂CH₂OH + Cl⁻ [1]

**(c)** Nucleophilic substitution [1]. **[4]**

**4.** Dipole shown, δ+ on carbon and δ− on bromine [1]; arrow from the lone pair on the carbon of CN⁻ to the δ+ carbon [1]; arrow from the C–Br bond to the bromine atom [1]; product pentanenitrile, with Br⁻ shown [1]. **[4]**

*Examiner insight:* The attacking lone pair in cyanide is on carbon. An arrow from nitrogen loses the mark.

**5. (a)** CH₃CH₂Br + 2NH₃ → CH₃CH₂NH₂ + NH₄Br [1]

**(b)** So that the amine formed does not react with more bromoethane, giving secondary and tertiary amines [1]

**(c)** Ammonia is a gas that would escape if heated in an open vessel (or: the reaction is carried out under pressure) [1]. **[3]**

**6. (a)** Chloroethane, bromoethane, iodoethane (increasing rate) [1]

**(b)** The C–X bond must be broken in the reaction [1]; the C–I bond is the weakest so needs the least energy to break (lowest activation energy), and the C–Cl bond is the strongest [1]

**(c)** The rate depends on how easily the bond breaks, not on how polar it is [1]; C–Cl is more polar, but its bond enthalpy is higher than that of C–Br or C–I, so it breaks the least easily [1]. **[5]**

**7. (a)** Ag⁺(aq) + X⁻(aq) → AgX(s) [1]

**(b)** AgCl white [1]; AgBr cream and AgI yellow [1]

**(c)** Any two from: same volume and concentration of halogenoalkane, same volume and concentration of silver nitrate, same temperature, same carbon skeleton (the halogen is the only change) [2]

**(d)** So that the halogenoalkane and the aqueous silver nitrate dissolve in a common solvent and mix [1]. **[6]**

**8. (a)** n(AgCl) = 2.868 ÷ 143.4 = **0.02000 mol** [1]

**(b)** n(C₄H₉Cl) = n(AgCl) = 0.02000 mol [1]; mass = 0.02000 × 92.5 = **1.850 g** [1]. **[3]**

**9.** n(C₂H₅Br) = 10.89 ÷ 108.9 = 0.1000 mol [1]; theoretical mass of C₂H₅CN = 0.1000 × 55.0 = 5.50 g [1]; percentage yield = 3.85 ÷ 5.50 × 100 = **70.0 %** [1]. **[3]**

**10.** Step 1: heat under reflux with potassium cyanide in aqueous ethanol [1]; to give butanenitrile, CH₃CH₂CH₂CN, with Br⁻ [1]. Step 2: reduce the nitrile, for example with hydrogen and a nickel catalyst (or LiAlH₄ in dry ether) [1]; to give butylamine [1]. Ammonia alone would replace Br with NH₂ and give propylamine, which has only three carbons, so the chain must be lengthened by the cyanide step [1]. **[5]**

*Examiner insight:* The cyanide step is the only substitution in this specification that lengthens the carbon chain.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Starting the curly arrow on the hydrogen of OH⁻ or the nitrogen of CN⁻.
- Explaining the rate trend by polarity or electronegativity.
- Omitting the ethanol solvent and the controlled variables in rate experiments.
- Giving ethanolic instead of aqueous hydroxide for substitution.
- Forgetting the halide charge in a mechanism.
