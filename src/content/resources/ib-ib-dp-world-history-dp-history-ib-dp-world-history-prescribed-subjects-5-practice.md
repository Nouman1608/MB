> below are invented for this exercise** -- none is a real historical document,

Related: [Conflict and intervention study guide](/resources/ib-ib-dp-world-history-dp-history-ib-dp-world-history-prescribed-subjects-5/) and [Conflict and intervention revision notes](/resources/ib-ib-dp-world-history-dp-history-ib-dp-world-history-prescribed-subjects-5-revision-notes/).

Section A concentrates on case study 1, Rwanda. Section B is a one-hour source paper on case study 2, Kosovo, with the Paper 1 tariffs of 3, 2, 4, 6 and 9 marks.

**Source A** -- *Invented for practice:* extract from a statement by a NATO spokesperson at a press conference, April 1999.

> We did not choose this conflict. For a year the Belgrade government ignored every demand of the Security Council, every ceasefire and every agreement. After Račak, after the talks failed, we could not stand by while a European government drove a whole people from their homes. Our targets are military. Our aim is simple: the forces of repression out, the refugees home, and an international presence to keep the peace.

**Source B** -- *Invented for practice:* description of a cartoon published in a newspaper in a non-NATO country, May 1999. A giant boot labelled "NATO" is about to stamp on a small map labelled "Yugoslavia". Under the boot, a tiny figure labelled "UN Charter" is being crushed. A man in a suit standing beside the boot holds a sign reading "Humanitarian". The caption reads: "Helping."

**Source C** -- *Invented for practice:* extract from the testimony of a Kosovo Albanian refugee to an aid worker in a camp across the border, April 1999.

> The police came at dawn with masked men. They said we had one hour to leave and that NATO could protect us now. They took my husband's identity papers and tore them up. We walked for two days to the border. In every village on the road the houses were burning. We do not know where my brother is.

**Source D** -- *Invented for practice:* extract from a modern historian's assessment of the Kosovo intervention, published in 2010.

> NATO went to war to prevent a humanitarian catastrophe and, in its first weeks, presided over one: the expulsions accelerated once the bombing began. Yet by June the Yugoslav forces had gone and most refugees were soon able to return. The intervention bypassed the Security Council and set a precedent its critics feared, but it also ended a decade of repression. Few interventions have been so plainly illegal in form or so defensible in outcome.

## Section A

**1.** Identify **two** ways in which Belgian colonial rule contributed to divisions between Hutu and Tutsi. **[2]**

**2.** Explain why the RPF invasion of October 1990 increased the danger to Tutsi inside Rwanda. **[3]**

**3.** Outline the role of RTLM radio in the genocide. **[2]**

**4.** Explain **two** reasons why the UN Security Council reduced UNAMIR in April 1994. **[4]**

**5.** Evaluate Operation Turquoise as an intervention. **[4]**

**6.** Explain the significance of the Akayesu judgment of 1998. **[3]**

**7.** Explain how the refugee crisis after 1994 contributed to conflict in Zaire. **[3]**

## Section B

**8.** What, according to Source A, were the reasons for and aims of NATO's intervention? **[3]**

**9.** What is the message conveyed by Source B? **[2]**

**10.** With reference to its origin, purpose and content, analyse the value and limitations of Source C for a historian studying the treatment of civilians in Kosovo in 1999. **[4]**

**11.** Compare and contrast what Sources A and D reveal about the NATO intervention. **[6]**

**12.** Using the sources and your own knowledge, evaluate the success of international intervention in Kosovo between 1998 and 2002. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** Any two, 1 mark each: treating Hutu and Tutsi as fixed racial categories (1); recording ethnicity on identity cards from the 1930s (1); favouring Tutsi for education, chiefships and office (1); switching support to the Hutu majority at the end of colonial rule (1). **[2]**

**2.** The government and extremists portrayed all Tutsi inside Rwanda as accomplices of the invaders (1). Thousands of Tutsi were arrested and local massacres took place in the early 1990s (1). The war gave Hutu Power groups a reason to organise militias and spread propaganda presenting Tutsi as a threat to Hutu survival (1). **[3]**

**3.** It broadcast propaganda dehumanising Tutsi, for example calling them "cockroaches" (1). During the genocide it encouraged killing and broadcast names and locations of people to be targeted (1). **[2]**

**4.** 2 marks for each developed reason: after ten Belgian peacekeepers were killed, Belgium withdrew and urged the full withdrawal of the mission (2); the US, after the deaths of its soldiers in Somalia in 1993, opposed new risks for UN forces and resisted a stronger mandate (2); the Security Council treated the crisis as a breakdown of the ceasefire in a civil war rather than as genocide, so protecting the peacekeepers seemed the priority (2). **[4]**

**5.** Up to 4 marks for a balanced evaluation. Strengths: it was authorised by the Security Council (1); it created a safe zone in the south-west and saved some lives (1). Limitations: it arrived in late June, after most killing had already taken place (1); France had been close to the Habyarimana regime, and critics argue the zone allowed génocidaires and former government forces to escape to Zaire (1). **[4]**

**6.** It was the first conviction for genocide by an international court (1). It recognised rape and sexual violence as acts of genocide when committed with intent to destroy a group (1). It established that the 1948 Genocide Convention could be enforced against individuals, strengthening international criminal justice (1). **[3]**

**7.** Around two million Hutu fled, many to camps in eastern Zaire (1). Former soldiers and militias controlled the camps and launched attacks into Rwanda (1). Rwanda backed a rebellion that overthrew Mobutu in the First Congo War (1996-1997), spreading the conflict across the region (1). **[3]**

**8.** 1 mark per point, up to 3: Belgrade had ignored Security Council demands and agreements (1); Račak and the failure of the talks (1); to stop the expulsion of a people (1); aims of removing Yugoslav forces, returning refugees and installing an international peace force (1). **[3]**

**9.** 1 mark per point, up to 2: NATO's intervention was an act of overwhelming force against a small state (1); it violated the UN Charter and international law (1); the humanitarian justification is presented as hypocritical (1). **[2]**

**10.** Markbands (summarised from the guide's Question 2 descriptors): **3-4 marks**, explicit discussion of both value and limitations, linked to origin, purpose and content; **1-2 marks**, limited, one-sided, or not linked to the topic.
- *Value:* origin, an eyewitness and victim speaking within weeks of the events; purpose, to record her experience for an aid worker; content shows organised expulsion, destruction of identity papers, burning villages and separation of families.
- *Limitations:* one person's experience on one route, which cannot show the overall scale; trauma and the setting of a refugee camp may affect what is recalled; it gives no information about violence against Serbs or KLA actions. **[4]**

**11.** Markbands: **5-6 marks**, detailed comparison and contrast with explicit links throughout; **3-4 marks**, some similarities and differences, possibly unbalanced; **1-2 marks**, superficial or sources described separately.
- *Comparisons:* both present the intervention as a response to Yugoslav repression; both identify the return of refugees and removal of Yugoslav forces as its aims or outcome.
- *Contrasts:* A presents NATO as forced to act after the Security Council was ignored, D says NATO "bypassed" the Council and calls the action illegal in form; A claims NATO acted to stop expulsions, D notes that expulsions accelerated once the bombing began; A is written during the war to justify it, D judges it in hindsight as defensible in outcome. **[6]**

**12.** Markbands: **7-9 marks**, focused response using the sources and accurate own knowledge, with effective synthesis; **4-6 marks**, mostly focused, uses sources and some own knowledge; **1-3 marks**, limited focus, sources described or own knowledge only.
- *From the sources:* A (aims of intervention), B (illegality and hypocrisy), C (expulsions during the bombing), D (short-term catastrophe, long-term success).
- *Own knowledge, before the bombing:* Security Council demands in 1998; the October 1998 agreement and the OSCE verifiers; Račak; Rambouillet.
- *Own knowledge, the intervention:* Operation Allied Force (24 March-10 June 1999); Russian and Chinese opposition; civilian casualties, including the bombing of the Chinese embassy in Belgrade; Yugoslav withdrawal and Resolution 1244.
- *Own knowledge, after:* UNMIK and KFOR; return of most Albanian refugees; reprisals against Serbs and Roma; the ICTY indictment of Milošević, his fall (2000), transfer (2001) and trial (2002).
- *Judgement:* for example, the intervention succeeded in its immediate aims but failed to protect civilians during the bombing and left a disputed precedent and an unresolved status for Kosovo. **[9]**

## Frequently asked questions

**Why does Question 12 start in 1998 rather than 1999?**
International intervention began before the bombing, with Security Council resolutions and the OSCE mission, so it counts as part of the record.

**Can I use the Rwanda case in a Kosovo answer?**
A brief comparison can sharpen a judgement, but Paper 1 is marked on the case study set, so keep the focus on Kosovo.
