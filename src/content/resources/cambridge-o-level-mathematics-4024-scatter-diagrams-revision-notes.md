These notes condense section 9.5, Scatter diagrams, of the Cambridge O Level Mathematics (Syllabus D) 4024 syllabus for 2025, 2026 and 2027. A full worked example is in the [scatter diagrams study guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-mathematics-4024-scatter-diagrams/), and exam-style questions are in the [scatter diagrams practice questions](/resources/cambridge-o-level-mathematics-4024-scatter-diagrams-practice/).

## The three outcomes

1. Draw and interpret scatter diagrams, with points clearly marked (for example as small crosses).
2. Understand positive, negative and zero correlation.
3. Draw by eye, interpret and use a straight line of best fit.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Scatter diagram | a graph of paired data, one point per item |
| Correlation | how strongly two variables follow a straight-line pattern |
| Positive correlation | as one increases, the other tends to increase |
| Negative correlation | as one increases, the other tends to decrease |
| Zero correlation | no linear relationship |
| Strong / weak | points close to / widely spread around a line |
| Line of best fit | a single ruled straight line drawn by eye through the pattern |
| Outlier | a point that does not fit the pattern |
| Interpolation | estimating within the range of the data |
| Extrapolation | estimating outside the range of the data (unreliable) |

## Line of best fit rules (from the syllabus notes)

- A **single ruled line** drawn **by inspection**.
- It **extends across the full data set**.
- It need not pass through any point, but there should be a **roughly even distribution of points either side** along its whole length.

Also: ignore clear outliers, do not force the line through the origin, and do not draw a line when there is zero correlation.

## Plotting checklist

- Labels and units on both axes.
- Even scales; points spread over most of the grid.
- Small crosses, plotted exactly.
- Points not joined.

## Using the line

- **Estimate y from x:** up from x to the line, across to the y-axis.
- **Estimate x from y:** across from y to the line, down to the x-axis.
- **Gradient:** change in y ÷ change in x between two points on the line; interpret as "for each extra unit of x, y changes by about ...".
- **Reliability:** good for interpolation with strong correlation; poor for extrapolation or weak correlation.

### Example

Hot drinks sold, d, at a café against the temperature, T °C. The correlation is negative. A line of best fit passes through (5, 120) and (25, 40).

```
gradient = (40 − 120) ÷ (25 − 5) = −4
at T = 15:   d = 120 − 4 × 10 = 80 drinks
for d = 100: 100 = 120 − 4(T − 5), so T = 10 °C
```

Interpretation: each 1 °C rise in temperature is associated with about 4 fewer hot drinks sold. At 40 °C the line gives 120 − 4 × 35 = −20 drinks, which is impossible: extrapolation fails.

## Describing correlation: model sentences

- "Strong negative correlation: on warmer days the café tended to sell fewer hot drinks."
- "Weak positive correlation: taller people tended to have slightly larger hand spans, but with many exceptions."
- "Zero correlation: there is no relationship between shoe size and test score."

## Choosing axes and scales

- If one variable may help explain the other (temperature and drinks sold), put it on the horizontal axis.
- Choose scales so the points fill most of the grid; an axis may start above zero if that is clear.
- Use equal steps on each axis, such as 2, 5 or 10 units per large square, so points can be plotted exactly.

## Strength of correlation

| Description | What the points look like | Reliability of estimates |
|---|---|---|
| Strong | a narrow band close to a straight line | good inside the data |
| Weak | a wide band with a slight trend | rough at best |
| Zero | no trend at all | no line should be drawn |

## Exam traps

- Dot-to-dot joining of points.
- A line that does not cover the full data set.
- A line through the origin or the end points by habit.
- An outlier included when placing the line.
- A correlation type with no context sentence.
- Saying one variable causes the other.
- Using the line far outside the data without saying it is unreliable.

## Self-test

1. What type of correlation would you expect between the age of a car and its value?
2. What type between shoe size and maths test score?
3. Give two rules from the syllabus for a line of best fit.
4. What is an outlier, and what should you do with it when drawing a line of best fit?
5. A line passes through (5, 120) and (25, 40). Find its gradient.
6. Use the line in question 5 to estimate d when T = 15.
7. Use it to find T when d = 100.
8. What does the line predict at T = 40, and why is this unreliable?
9. Explain the difference between interpolation and extrapolation.
10. Why should you not draw a line of best fit for data with zero correlation?
11. Ice cream sales and swimming pool visits show positive correlation. Does buying ice cream cause pool visits?
12. How should points be marked on a scatter diagram?

### Answers

1. **Negative** (older cars tend to be worth less).
2. **Zero**.
3. Any two of: a **single ruled line** by inspection; **extends across the full data set**; **roughly even distribution of points either side** along its length.
4. A point that **does not fit the pattern**; **ignore it** when placing the line.
5. −80 ÷ 20 = **−4**.
6. **80**.
7. **10 °C**.
8. **−20 drinks**; T = 40 is **outside the data** and the result is impossible.
9. Interpolation estimates **within** the data range; extrapolation goes **outside** it and is less reliable.
10. There is **no linear relationship** to describe; a line would suggest one.
11. **No**: correlation does not show cause; both rise in **hot weather**.
12. As **small crosses (×)**, not joined.
