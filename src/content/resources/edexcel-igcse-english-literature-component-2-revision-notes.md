**What is 4ET1 Component 2?** Component 2 (paper 4ET1/02) is a 1 hour 30 minute open-book exam worth
60 marks and 40% of the Pearson Edexcel International GCSE in English Literature. You write two
30-mark essays: one on a modern drama text (Section A, AO1 and AO2) and one on a literary heritage
text (Section B, AO1, AO2 and AO4).

Condensed for the final weeks. For the full explanation, use the
[Modern Drama and Literary Heritage Texts study guide](/resources/igcse-edexcel-english-literature-component-2/).

## Which texts are set?

| Section A: Modern Drama (choose one) | Section B: Literary Heritage (choose one) |
|---|---|
| *A View from the Bridge* (Arthur Miller) | *Romeo and Juliet* (Shakespeare) |
| *An Inspector Calls* (J B Priestley) | *Macbeth* (Shakespeare) |
| *The Curious Incident of the Dog in the Night-time* (Mark Haddon, adapted by Simon Stephens) | *The Merchant of Venice* (Shakespeare) |
| *Kindertransport* (Diane Samuels) | *Pride and Prejudice* (Jane Austen) |
| *Death and the King's Horseman* (Wole Soyinka) | *Great Expectations* (Charles Dickens) |
| | *The Scarlet Letter* (Nathaniel Hawthorne) |

The exam copy must be the prescribed edition and completely unmarked.

## How are the marks split?

| Section | AO1 | AO2 | AO4 | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A: Modern Drama | 15 | 15 | – | 30 |
| B: Literary Heritage | 10 | 10 | 10 | 30 |

So a third of every Section B mark is for context (AO4): it is not an optional extra.

## The open-book exam changes what to revise

Because an unmarked copy of each set text is permitted in the exam room,
**memorising quotations word-for-word is not the priority** it is on a
closed-book paper. What matters instead is speed of navigation: knowing
roughly where key scenes, speeches or passages sit in the text, so you
can locate and analyse them quickly under exam pressure.

Practise this directly: time yourself finding a specific scene or
passage in your set text from a cold start, using only chapter/act
divisions and your own annotations, not a search function. Annotate a
personal copy in advance (your unmarked exam copy must stay clean) with
brief marginal flags at points you expect to return to.

## Section A: Modern Drama (AO1 + AO2)

Two marks-earning habits matter most:

- **Personal response with evidence** (AO1) — a defensible interpretation
supported by specific reference to the text, not just plot summary.
- **Analysis of craft** (AO2) — how the playwright's use of language,
stage directions, structure or dramatic form creates meaning for an
audience, not just a reader.

Drama-specific point often missed: a play is written to be **performed**,
so stage directions, staging choices and what an audience sees and hears
in the moment (not just what characters say) are legitimate, markable
analytical territory.

## Section B: Literary Heritage Texts (AO1 + AO2 + AO4)

This is the section that most often loses easy marks, because AO4
(contextual understanding) is additional to AO1/AO2 and is easy to
forget under time pressure. The fix is structural: **build one
contextual point into every paragraph**, not as a bolted-on final
sentence but woven into the analysis itself — connect a textual choice
directly to the historical, social or cultural conditions the text was
written in and responds to.

**Weak vs strong, side by side:**

| Weak (AO1/AO2 only) | Strong (AO1/AO2 + AO4) |
|---|---|
| States what a character does or says | Explains what that choice reveals about the period's values |
| Notes a theme is present | Links the theme to a real historical or social condition of the time |
| Treats the text as timeless | Treats the text as a product of, and response to, its era |

## Choosing between essay options

Each section offers a choice of two essay questions. **Spend one minute
weighing both before committing** — the question that sounds more
straightforward on first read is not always the one your prepared
material actually answers best. A rushed choice, followed by
mid-answer realisation that the other option suited you better, is a
common and avoidable loss of marks.

## Timing across 1 hour 30 minutes

With two 30-mark essays in 90 minutes, allow roughly 45 minutes each,
including the one-minute option check. The most frequent timing failure
is letting the (often more comfortable) drama essay in Section A run
over, leaving Section B's contextual analysis rushed or omitted
entirely — which is costly precisely because AO4 marks are only
available there.

## Building the historical/social context bank

Rather than researching a set text's context broadly and hoping it
proves useful, prepare **three or four specific, citable facts** about
the period (a relevant social attitude, a historical event, a
prevailing belief about class, gender or authority) that connect
directly to the text's central concerns. A fact that cannot be
connected to a specific moment or theme in the text is not revision-
ready, however interesting it is on its own.

This also protects against a common failure mode: writing generically
about "the context of the time" without naming what that context
actually was. A strong AO4 sentence names the specific historical or
social condition, not just gestures at "the period."

## Exam traps

- Memorising quotations instead of practising fast navigation of an
unmarked text.
- Writing a strong Section A response but forgetting AO4 entirely in
Section B.
- Treating stage directions as scenery rather than analysable authorial
choices.
- Committing to an essay option without briefly weighing the alternative.
- Letting Section A overrun and rushing Section B's contextual analysis.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can I annotate the copy I take into the exam?**
No. The specification says prescribed editions are allowed but must be completely unmarked. Annotate
a separate study copy instead.

**Is Component 2 the same as Component 3?**
They cover the same set texts and skills. Component 2 is the examined route; Component 3 is the
non-examined coursework alternative with two 30-mark assignments. You take one or the other.

**What counts as context for AO4?**
The specification lists the writer's situation where relevant, the historical setting, social and
cultural attitudes, the literary context, and how the text is received by different audiences over time.


## Self-test

1. Why does the open-book format reduce the value of memorising exact
quotations?
2. Which assessment objective is unique to Section B, and what must a
strong paragraph do to earn it?
3. Give one example of dramatic-craft evidence beyond spoken dialogue.
4. Why should both essay options be briefly considered before choosing?
5. What is the most common timing failure across the two sections, and
why is it costly?

**Answers:** 1. Because the unmarked text is available in the exam,
success depends on locating and analysing relevant passages quickly,
not on word-perfect recall. 2. AO4 (contextual understanding); a strong
paragraph connects a specific textual choice directly to the
historical, social or cultural conditions the text was written in,
rather than adding context as an afterthought. 3. Stage directions,
staging choices, or what an audience would see and hear performed, as
distinct from what characters literally say. 4. Because the
question that looks easiest on first read is not always the one your
prepared material answers best; a quick check avoids a mid-answer
change of direction. 5. Letting the Section A drama essay run over
time, which leaves Section B's contextual analysis rushed — costly
because AO4 marks are only available in Section B.
