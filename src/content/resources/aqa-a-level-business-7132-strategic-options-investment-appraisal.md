This guide goes deeper into section **3.7.8 Analysing strategic options: investment appraisal** of the AQA AS and A-level Business specification (7132, for first teaching from September 2023). Section 3.7 is for the A-level only. The specification's content is:

- **financial methods of assessing an investment**, including the calculation and interpretation of **payback, average rate of return and net present value**
- **factors influencing investment decisions**, including **investment criteria, non-financial factors, risk and uncertainty**.

The specification's Annex also lists "calculate investment appraisal outcomes and interpret results" as a quantitative skill. All businesses and figures are invented.

## Why appraise investments?

An investment in machinery, a new site, a product launch or a takeover commits money now in the hope of returns later. Many strategic options are costly and hard to reverse. Investment appraisal turns forecasts into numbers so that options can be compared against each other and against a target. It does not remove uncertainty, because every figure is a forecast.

## The three methods

### Payback

**Payback** is the time taken to recover the initial cost from the cash inflows.

*Method:* add up the net cash flows year by year (cumulative cash flow) until the total reaches the cost. If it is reached part-way through a year, calculate the fraction:

**Payback fraction = remaining amount to recover ÷ cash flow in that year**

*Strengths:* simple; focuses on cash recovery and risk; useful when cash is tight or the future is uncertain.

*Weaknesses:* ignores cash flows after payback, so a project that earns a fortune later may look poor; ignores the timing within the period and the time value of money; the acceptable payback period is arbitrary.

### Average rate of return (ARR)

**ARR = average annual profit ÷ initial investment × 100**

where **average annual profit = (total net cash inflows − initial cost) ÷ number of years**.

*Strengths:* uses all the years' data; gives a percentage that can be compared with a target return or the cost of borrowing.

*Weaknesses:* ignores the timing of returns (£50,000 in year 5 is treated the same as in year 1); ignores the time value of money; uses averages that hide the pattern.

### Net present value (NPV)

Money received later is worth less than money received now, because it could be invested in the meantime and because inflation and risk reduce its value. NPV **discounts** future cash flows to their present value, using a discount rate that reflects the cost of finance or the required return.

**Present value = cash flow × discount factor**, where the discount factor for year *n* at rate *r* is 1 ÷ (1 + r)ⁿ. In examinations a table of discount factors is normally given.

**NPV = total present value of cash inflows − initial cost**

*Decision rule:* accept a project with a positive NPV (it earns more than the discount rate); reject if negative. When comparing projects, the higher NPV is better.

*Strengths:* takes account of timing and the time value of money; uses all cash flows; gives an absolute measure of value created.

*Weaknesses:* more complex; the result depends heavily on the chosen discount rate; the forecasts of far-future cash flows are least reliable; does not show the size of the investment relative to the gain.

## Worked example: Cadogan Bakery's new oven line

The project costs **£220,000**. Forecast net cash inflows are:

| Year | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Net cash flow (£) | 50,000 | 70,000 | 80,000 | 80,000 | 60,000 |

### Payback

| Year | Net cash flow | Cumulative |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | −220,000 | −220,000 |
| 1 | 50,000 | −170,000 |
| 2 | 70,000 | −100,000 |
| 3 | 80,000 | −20,000 |
| 4 | 80,000 | +60,000 |

Payback is during year 4. Remaining at the start of year 4: £20,000. Fraction: 20,000 ÷ 80,000 = 0.25 of a year, which is 3 months. **Payback = 3 years 3 months.**

### ARR

- Total inflows: 50,000 + 70,000 + 80,000 + 80,000 + 60,000 = **£340,000**
- Total profit: 340,000 − 220,000 = **£120,000**
- Average annual profit: 120,000 ÷ 5 = **£24,000**
- ARR = 24,000 ÷ 220,000 × 100 = **10.9%**

### NPV at 8%

| Year | Cash flow | Discount factor (8%) | Present value |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 50,000 | 0.926 | 46,300 |
| 2 | 70,000 | 0.857 | 59,990 |
| 3 | 80,000 | 0.794 | 63,520 |
| 4 | 80,000 | 0.735 | 58,800 |
| 5 | 60,000 | 0.681 | 40,860 |
| | | Total | 269,470 |

NPV = 269,470 − 220,000 = **+£49,470**.

At 12% the discount factors are 0.893, 0.797, 0.712, 0.636 and 0.567, giving a total present value of £242,300 and an NPV of **+£22,300**. The NPV falls as the discount rate rises, but remains positive.

### Interpreting the results

- Payback of 3 years 3 months is within a typical target of, say, 4 years, but only just beyond the 3-year point.
- An ARR of 10.9% is above a borrowing cost of 7%, for example, but whether it is good depends on the required return.
- A positive NPV at both rates suggests the project adds value, and the margin is reasonable.

**Judgement.** On the figures the project is acceptable. But the whole result rests on forecasts. If cash inflows turned out 10% lower in every year, present value at 8% would be £242,523 and NPV would fall to **£22,523**, still positive but much smaller. The bakery should check the sales forecasts (are the five-year figures realistic?), whether the oven's life is five years, and how the extra production fits with its wider strategy.

## Factors influencing investment decisions

### Investment criteria

Firms set targets such as "payback within three years", "ARR above 12%" or "NPV greater than zero". Targets depend on the cost of finance, the firm's attitude to risk, and the return available from alternative uses of funds. A firm with limited funds may use **capital rationing**: ranking projects and funding the best until money runs out.

### Non-financial factors

Not all considerations are in the numbers:

- **Strategic fit:** does the investment support the mission and objectives?
- **Impact on employees:** automation may cause redundancies and low morale, or create new skilled roles.
- **Environmental and ethical impact:** emissions, waste, reputation, community opinion.
- **Impact on brand and customers:** better quality, or the risk of disruption.
- **Competitors' reactions:** a rival may copy the investment or cut prices.
- **Flexibility:** can the asset be used for other products, or sold?
- **Legal and regulatory aspects.**

A project with a modest financial return may still be right if it protects the brand or opens a strategic route; one with a high return may be wrong if it conflicts with the firm's values.

### Risk and uncertainty

**Risk** can be estimated (a probability can be given); **uncertainty** is when probabilities cannot be known. Both affect forecasts of sales, costs, interest rates and economic conditions.

Sources: changes in demand, technology, competitors, costs and regulation; the longer the project, the less reliable the forecasts. Ways to deal with them:

- **sensitivity analysis:** vary key assumptions (as with the 10% fall above) to see how robust the decision is
- **scenario planning:** best, expected and worst cases
- **a higher discount rate** for risky projects
- **a shorter payback requirement**
- **staged investment** or pilots to test results before committing fully
- **gathering better data**, such as market research.

## Choosing between methods

No method is complete. A strong appraisal uses several:

| Method | Best at | Weak at |
|---|---|---|
| Payback | Speed of recovery; risk | Later returns; time value |
| ARR | Overall profitability as a percentage | Timing; time value |
| NPV | Time value; value created | Complexity; depends on the discount rate |

Disagreement between methods is common. A project may have a long payback but a high NPV, so the decision rests on the firm's priorities: a firm short of cash might prefer a short payback, while one with plenty of funds and a long-term view may prefer NPV.

## Exam technique

- **Calculate:** show each step, give units and round as asked. State "years and months" for payback if requested.
- **Interpret:** compare with the criterion given, say whether to accept, and explain what the result means.
- **Evaluate:** discuss reliability of the forecasts, the role of non-financial factors and how the decision would change if assumptions changed.

## Common mistakes

- Forgetting to subtract the initial cost when calculating NPV.
- Treating ARR as total profit divided by cost, forgetting to divide by the number of years.
- Reading payback in years as a decimal and not converting 0.25 of a year into months.
- Applying the discount factor to the wrong year.
- Not interpreting the answer in relation to a target or the case.
- Ignoring non-financial factors.
