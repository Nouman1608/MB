*Extract 1 (written for practice; not by a real historian)*

> The wartime alliance concealed, but never removed, a gulf of purpose. Stalin regarded the capitalist powers as temporary partners whose hostility would return once Germany was beaten, and he acted on that belief long before Western leaders abandoned their hopes. The pledges given at Yalta about free elections were, for him, words to be interpreted as Soviet security required. In Poland the London government was pushed aside; in Romania and Bulgaria coalition governments became fronts for communist control. Western statesmen were slow, even reluctant, to draw conclusions: Roosevelt hoped personal trust would bridge the divide, and even Truman's sharper tone in 1945 produced no change of policy for many months. It was only when Soviet pressure reached Iran, Turkey and Greece that Washington accepted that a line had to be held. The Truman Doctrine and the Marshall Plan were improvised answers to a crisis that Moscow had created. The blockade of Berlin in 1948 confirmed what the coup in Prague had already shown -- that the division of Europe was being imposed from the East.

*Extract 2 (written for practice; not by a real historian)*

> American planners did not wait for Soviet provocation to define their aims. *Extract 3 (written for practice; not by a real historian)*

> For forty years historians argued about Soviet intentions without being able to read a single Soviet document. Now that the archives of Moscow and its allies have been partly opened, some questions look different. The papers show a leader who believed in the inevitability of conflict between capitalism and socialism, who saw security in the control of territory and people, and who could not tolerate any partner he did not dominate. They also show advisers who reported what Stalin wished to hear, as in the ambassador's telegram from Washington in 1946 that portrayed American policy as a bid for world supremacy. Western leaders made mistakes, and American power was real, but democracies argued in public and changed course; the Soviet system answered to one man. Ideology was not a veneer over Russian national interest: it shaped how that interest was understood. While Stalin lived, a cold war was the most likely outcome.

## Answers

**1.** Interpretation is what can be inferred from the extract about the historian's claims and conclusions (1); approach is what the historian brings to the topic -- their interests, the questions they ask and how these reflect their ideology and beliefs (1). **[2]**

**2.** Any three, 1 mark each: Allied intervention against the Bolsheviks in the Russian Civil War, 1918-20 (1); US refusal to recognise the USSR until 1933 (1); Comintern's commitment to world revolution (1); Western appeasement and the Munich agreement, seen by Stalin as encouraging Hitler eastwards (1); the Nazi-Soviet Pact of 1939 (1). **[3]**

**3.** Two ways, 2 marks each: revisionists contrast Truman's blunt, inexperienced style -- for example his sharp meeting with Molotov in April 1945 -- with Roosevelt's diplomacy, arguing that he hardened US policy (2); traditionalists present Truman's firmness as a realistic response to Soviet behaviour, showing that personality mattered less than Stalin's actions (2); post-revisionists argue that personalities interacted with domestic pressures, so Truman responded to Congress and public opinion as much as to his own instincts (2). **[4]**

**4.** 2 marks for each historian with argument. Traditionalist: Herbert Feis, or Arthur Schlesinger Jr -- Soviet ideology and expansion caused the Cold War and the West reacted defensively (2). Revisionist: William Appleman Williams -- US Open Door economic expansion; or Gar Alperovitz -- atomic diplomacy to pressure the USSR; or Gabriel Kolko -- the USA sought to reshape the post-war world in its own interests (2). **[4]**

**5.** The Riga axioms saw the USSR as an ideologically driven revolutionary state that could not be trusted (1); the Yalta axioms saw it as a traditional great power with which bargains on spheres of interest were possible (1). **[2]**

**6.** Two reasons, 2 marks each: the Vietnam War led many historians to question the claim that US foreign policy was always defensive (2); the New Left's critique of American capitalism encouraged study of economic motives (2); the opening of more US government records allowed historians to examine American decision-making in detail (2). **[4]**

**7.** Any three, 1 mark each: Soviet archival documents, including Politburo and foreign ministry papers (1); East European party archives (1); Chinese documents (1); the Novikov telegram (1946) (1); the Venona decrypts of Soviet espionage messages, released in 1995 (1); publications of the Cold War International History Project (1). **[3]**

**8.** Soviet-era historians blamed Western imperialism and capitalist encirclement (1), pointing to events such as the Fulton speech and US atomic diplomacy (1), and presented Soviet policy as defending peace and socialism -- the reverse of traditionalists, who blamed Soviet ideology and expansion (1). **[3]**

**9.** It was based on newly opened Soviet and East European archives (1); it returned much of the responsibility to Stalin's personality and ideology, echoing traditionalism (1); but it came after, and reacted against, post-revisionist arguments of shared responsibility, so it represents a new stage rather than a simple return (1). **[3]**

**10.** Indicative content. **Interpretation (AO4):** the USSR was primarily responsible -- Stalin's ideological hostility and expansion in Eastern Europe created the division of Europe, while the West responded slowly and reluctantly. **Approach:** focuses on Soviet actions and Stalin's beliefs; treats Western policy as reactive and improvised ("improvised answers"); moral and political rather than economic; consistent with traditionalist writing. **Supporting knowledge (AO1):** the Lublin Poles favoured over the London government; communist-dominated coalitions in Romania and Bulgaria; the Declaration on Liberated Europe at Yalta; Soviet pressure on Iran (1946) and on Turkey over the Straits; the Czech coup (February 1948); the Berlin Blockade. **Testing knowledge:** revisionists point to the end of Lend-Lease, atomic diplomacy and the Marshall Plan's conditions; post-revisionists to mutual misperception; the Novikov telegram shows Soviet fears; the "percentages" agreement (1944) suggests Western acceptance of spheres. **Level 6:** explains the whole interpretation and how its approach -- stressing intention on one side and reaction on the other -- produces it. AO4 20 + AO1 20 = **[40]**

**11.** Indicative content. **Interpretation (AO4):** the USA was primarily responsible -- its economic ambition to create an open world economy under American leadership, backed by atomic power, drove a weak, insecure USSR into defensive control of Eastern Europe. **Approach:** focuses on American economic planning and motives; reads Soviet policy through Soviet security needs ("seen from the Kremlin"); stresses economic power and structure; characteristic of revisionism and the Open Door and atomic-diplomacy arguments. **Supporting knowledge (AO1):** Bretton Woods (1944) and the USSR's non-membership of the IMF and World Bank; the abrupt end of Lend-Lease (1945); Truman's knowledge of the bomb at Potsdam; the Marshall Plan's conditions and Molotov's walk-out (July 1947); Soviet wartime losses. **Testing knowledge:** traditionalists cite Soviet actions in Poland and Romania before the Marshall Plan; post-1991 evidence of Stalin's ideology; the Czech coup. **Level 6:** explains how the economic and Kremlin-centred approach produces the claim that the Cold War grew from American determination. AO4 20 + AO1 20 = **[40]**

**12.** Indicative content. **Interpretation (AO4):** Stalin's ideology and personality made a cold war the most likely outcome while he lived; Western errors and American power were real but secondary. **Approach:** archive-based ("now that the archives... have been partly opened"); interested in Soviet decision-making and ideology; contrasts democratic and dictatorial systems; argues against the view that ideology was a veneer -- characteristic of post-post-revisionism and the New Cold War history. **Supporting knowledge (AO1):** the Novikov telegram (September 1946); Stalin's February 1946 election speech on the incompatibility of capitalism and communism; the creation of Cominform (1947) and Zhdanov's "two camps" doctrine; control of Eastern Europe through security services. **Testing knowledge:** revisionist emphasis on US economic power; Roberts's argument that Stalin hoped for continued co-operation; Leffler's case that US national security policy intensified conflict; the extract's concession that "Western leaders made mistakes". **Level 6:** explains the interpretation as a whole and links it to the historian's use of new sources and interest in ideology. AO4 20 + AO1 20 = **[40]**
