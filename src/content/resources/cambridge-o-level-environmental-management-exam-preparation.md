Cambridge O Level Environmental Management 5014 is assessed by two equally weighted, externally
assessed papers, each lasting 1 hour 45 minutes and carrying 80 marks and 50% of the qualification.
Paper 1 Theory is divided into Section A (short and structured questions, 20 marks) and Section B
(short-answer and extended-response questions based on source material, 60 marks). Paper 2 Management
in Context consists of short and extended-response questions based on source material throughout. The
syllabus is not tiered and grades run A*-E. These notes build a preparation plan alongside the site's
guides to
[Rocks and Minerals and Their Exploitation](/resources/o-level-cambridge-environmental-management-rocks-and-minerals/)
and [Energy and the Environment](/resources/o-level-cambridge-environmental-management-energy-and-the-environment/).

## Both papers are source-based -- this is not a recall qualification

Three-quarters of Paper 1's marks and the whole of Paper 2 are built on source material supplied in
the paper: data tables, graphs, maps, diagrams and short texts. **Exam-preparation priority**: the
central examinable skill is reading unfamiliar environmental data accurately and using it to support
a point, and it improves with practice on material you have not seen before. Revising content notes
without practising on unseen figures prepares you for only a quarter of the assessment.

## Paper 1's two sections need different pacing

Section A carries 20 marks and Section B carries 60, within a single 105-minute paper. Proportionally
that is roughly 26 minutes for Section A and 79 for Section B. **Exam-preparation priority**: rehearse
that split explicitly. Section A's short and structured questions are the part of the qualification
where over-writing is most tempting and least rewarded -- every extra minute spent developing a
short-answer response is a minute unavailable for the extended responses where the marks actually
concentrate.

## Describing data and explaining data are separate marks

A question asking you to describe a trend wants what the figures show -- direction, magnitude, any
anomaly, quoted with values. A question asking you to explain it wants the environmental process
responsible. Answers routinely conflate the two, giving the cause when asked for the pattern or
asserting a pattern when asked for the cause. **Exam-preparation priority**: on every practice
data question, identify which of the two is being asked before writing a word, and quote actual
figures whenever you are describing.

## Extended responses need a structure, not just more content

Section B and Paper 2 both carry extended-response questions, which typically require weighing more
than one consideration -- environmental, economic and social factors rarely point the same way in this
subject. **Exam-preparation priority**: practise a fixed structure: the effect, the evidence from the
source, the counter-consideration, then a judgement. An extended answer that lists environmental
impacts without acknowledging the economic pressure that produced them is incomplete regardless of
how much accurate content it contains.

## Sustainable management questions expect specific, workable measures

"Management in context" is the whole premise of Paper 2, and management questions reward named,
practical strategies applied to the situation described -- not general appeals to protect the
environment. **Exam-preparation priority**: for each topic, memorise two or three specific management
strategies together with a stated limitation of each, since a strategy offered without its cost or
constraint reads as an assertion rather than an evaluation.

## Worked routine: answering a data-response question

The routine below is an original model written for this resource, not a reproduction of any official
past paper, insert or mark scheme.

```
Step 1 - read the axes, units and scale before the data:
A misread unit (thousands vs millions, per year vs cumulative)
invalidates everything that follows.

Step 2 - identify what the command word wants:
DESCRIBE -> the pattern, with quoted figures.
EXPLAIN  -> the process causing it.
SUGGEST  -> a plausible reason, applied to this context.

Step 3 - if describing, quote and compare:
Give the overall direction, one specific value with its unit, and
any point where the pattern breaks.

Step 4 - if explaining, name the process, then link it:
Name the environmental process, then state the mechanism connecting
it to the specific change shown in the data -- not to the topic
in general.
```

Step 3 is where most description marks are won or lost: "the figure increases" is one observation,
while "it rises from 12 to 34 units between 1990 and 2010, with a brief fall around 2001" is three.

## Before/during exam checklist

- **Before the exams**: practise on unseen data, graphs and maps rather than only on content notes;
  learn two or three specific management strategies per topic, each with a stated limitation;
  rehearse the describe-versus-explain distinction until it is automatic.
- **During Paper 1**: hold Section A to roughly a quarter of the time, matching its share of the
  marks, and move on rather than polishing short answers.
- **During either paper**: read axes, units and scales before reading the data itself.
- **On extended responses**: use effect, evidence, counter-consideration, judgement -- and take the
  evidence from the source material provided rather than from memory alone.

## Self-test

1. What proportion of the qualification is assessed on source material rather than on recall alone?
2. Roughly how should Paper 1's 105 minutes be split between Sections A and B, and why?
3. What three things should a good "describe the trend" answer contain?
4. Why should a management strategy be offered together with a limitation?

**Answers:** 1. All of Paper 2 and Section B of Paper 1 -- that is 60 of Paper 1's 80 marks plus all
80 of Paper 2's, so roughly 87% of the total. 2. Around 26 minutes for Section A and 79 for Section B,
matching their 20/60 mark split -- Section A is where over-writing is most tempting and least
rewarded. 3. The overall direction of the change, at least one specific quoted value with its unit,
and any point at which the pattern breaks. 4. Because an unqualified strategy is an assertion; naming
its cost or constraint is what turns it into the evaluation that extended-response questions reward.
