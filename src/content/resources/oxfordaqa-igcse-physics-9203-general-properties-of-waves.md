This guide covers **3.3.1 General properties of waves** (statements a to h) of the OxfordAQA International GCSE Physics (9203) specification, for exams May/June 2018 onwards. The [wave properties, sound and ultrasound guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-physics-wave-properties-sound-and-ultrasound/) introduces each statement; this page goes deeper into the evidence that waves do not carry matter, reading wave graphs, unit handling in v = f λ, what happens at boundaries, and the three diffraction applications the specification names. Quick recall is in the [General properties of waves revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-physics-9203-general-properties-of-waves-revision-notes/) and exam-style questions are in the [General properties of waves practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-physics-9203-general-properties-of-waves-practice/).

## What the specification asks for

| Statement | Content |
|---|---|
| a | A wave is a disturbance from an oscillating source that transfers energy and information in the direction of travel, without transferring matter |
| b | Transverse waves: oscillations perpendicular to the direction of energy transfer |
| c | Longitudinal waves: oscillations parallel to the direction of energy transfer, with compressions and rarefactions |
| d | Electromagnetic and water waves are transverse; sound is longitudinal; mechanical waves can be either |
| e | At a boundary between materials, waves can be reflected, transmitted or absorbed, or a combination |
| f | Refraction happens because of a change in velocity; diffraction happens through a narrow gap or at an edge, and is appreciable when the wavelength is comparable to the gap or obstacle; applied to optical instruments, medical ultrasound and radio reception |
| g | Explain frequency, wavelength, period, amplitude and wavefront |
| h | v = f λ |

## Evidence that waves do not carry matter

Statement a is often tested by asking for an observation that shows it.

- **A floating object on water waves** bobs up and down as waves pass but does not travel along with them. The water near it oscillates in place; the energy moves on.
- **A ribbon tied to a stretched spring (slinky)** moves back and forth (longitudinal) or side to side (transverse) about one position as the wave passes along the spring. The ribbon does not move to the far end.

In each case describe what the marker does, then state the conclusion: the medium oscillates about a fixed position while energy travels through it.

Waves also carry **information**. Radio and light signals carry broadcasts and data; the specification links this to communication in section 3.3.2.

## Transverse and longitudinal waves compared

| Feature | Transverse | Longitudinal |
|---|---|---|
| Direction of oscillation | Perpendicular to energy transfer | Parallel to energy transfer |
| Features along the wave | Peaks (crests) and troughs | Compressions and rarefactions |
| Examples in the specification | Electromagnetic waves, water waves | Sound waves |
| Shown on a slinky by | Moving one end side to side | Pushing and pulling one end along its length |

A **compression** is a region where the particles are pushed closer together; a **rarefaction** is a region where they are further apart. The wavelength of a longitudinal wave is the distance from the centre of one compression to the centre of the next.

**Mechanical waves** travel through a material (a medium). They can be either type: a wave on a rope is transverse; a sound wave in air is longitudinal.

## The wave terms (statement g)

| Term | Meaning | Unit |
|---|---|---|
| Amplitude | The maximum displacement of a point from its rest position | m |
| Wavelength, λ | The distance from one point on a wave to the same point on the next wave, for example crest to crest | m |
| Frequency, f | The number of waves passing a point (or produced by the source) each second | Hz |
| Period, T | The time for one complete wave to pass a point | s |
| Wavefront | A line joining points on a wave that are at the same stage of oscillation, such as all the crests | — |

Period and frequency are linked: T = 1 ÷ f. A wave with frequency 50 Hz has a period of 0.020 s.

Amplitude is measured from the **rest position** to a crest, not from crest to trough. Measuring crest to trough gives twice the amplitude.

### Reading a displacement-distance graph

**Worked example 1.** A graph of displacement against distance for a wave on a rope shows five complete waves across 2.0 m. The highest point is 0.030 m above the rest line. The wave has a frequency of 25 Hz.

```
wavelength = 2.0 / 5 = 0.40 m
amplitude = 0.030 m
wave speed v = f x λ = 25 x 0.40 = 10 m/s
period T = 1 / 25 = 0.040 s
```

Count complete waves (crest to crest or one full up-down cycle), not crests alone: five crests can span only four wavelengths if the graph starts on a crest.

## The wave equation and units

```
v = f x λ     v in m/s, f in Hz, λ in m
```

Prefixes appear constantly in wave questions, so convert first:

| Prefix | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| k (kilo) | × 10³ | 200 kHz = 2.0 × 10⁵ Hz |
| M (mega) | × 10⁶ | 100 MHz = 1.0 × 10⁸ Hz |
| G (giga) | × 10⁹ | 2.4 GHz = 2.4 × 10⁹ Hz |
| m (milli) | × 10⁻³ | 5.0 mm = 5.0 × 10⁻³ m |

**Worked example 2: two radio stations.** Radio waves travel at 3.0 × 10⁸ m/s.

```
long-wave station, f = 200 kHz: λ = 3.0 x 10^8 / 2.0 x 10^5 = 1500 m
FM station, f = 100 MHz:        λ = 3.0 x 10^8 / 1.0 x 10^8 = 3.0 m
```

These wavelengths matter for diffraction, below.

## Waves at a boundary

When a wave reaches a boundary between two materials, three things can happen, often together:

- **Reflection:** the wave bounces back into the first material.
- **Transmission:** the wave passes into the second material (and may refract).
- **Absorption:** the wave's energy is transferred to the second material, often heating it.

Light hitting a window is partly reflected (you see a faint reflection), mostly transmitted, and slightly absorbed. Answers should say which processes happen and, if asked, which is the main one.

## Refraction: a change in velocity

A wave refracts when its velocity changes as it crosses into a different material or, for water waves, into water of a different depth. The **frequency stays the same**, because it is set by the source. Since v = f λ, a lower speed means a shorter wavelength. If the wave meets the boundary at an angle, the change in speed makes it change direction.

## Diffraction and its applications

Diffraction is the spreading of a wave as it passes through a gap or around an edge. It is **appreciable** (large enough to notice) when the wavelength is comparable to the size of the gap or obstacle. When the gap is many wavelengths wide, the wave passes through with very little spreading.

The specification names three applications.

**Radio reception.** Long-wave radio, with wavelengths of hundreds or thousands of metres, diffracts around hills and large buildings, so it can be received in valleys behind them. FM and television signals have wavelengths of about a metre or less, so they diffract very little around hills and need a more direct path from the transmitter.

**Optical instruments.** Light passing through the circular opening (aperture) of a telescope or camera lens diffracts slightly, which blurs fine detail. Making the aperture much larger than the wavelength of light **reduces** diffraction, giving sharper images. This is one reason large telescopes have wide lenses or mirrors.

**Ultrasound in medicine.** Ultrasound used in scanning has a very high frequency, so its wavelength in the body is short (around a millimetre or less). Short waves diffract less around small structures, so the beam stays narrow and the image can show fine detail.

The common thread is the comparison of wavelength with size: say which is larger and what that means for the amount of spreading.

## Common mistakes

- Measuring amplitude from crest to trough.
- Counting crests instead of complete wavelengths on a graph.
- Saying that a wave carries the water (or air) along with it.
- Stating that frequency changes during refraction.
- Saying diffraction is greatest when the gap is "small", without comparing it with the wavelength.
- Forgetting to convert kHz or MHz before using v = f λ.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Describe** an observation: say what the marker or wave does, then what that shows.
- **Explain** diffraction effects: compare wavelength with gap or obstacle size, then state the effect.
- **Calculate**: convert prefixes first, then rearrange v = f λ.
- **Compare** transverse and longitudinal waves: one comparative statement per feature.
