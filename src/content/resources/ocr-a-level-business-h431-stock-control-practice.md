## Questions

### Section A: short-answer questions

**1.** Explain what is meant by buffer stock. **[2]**

**2.** Explain what is meant by lead time. **[2]**

**3.** A business uses 60 units of a part a day. The lead time is 3 days and the buffer stock is 120 units. Calculate the reorder level. **[2]**

**4.** Explain **one** disadvantage to a business of holding too much stock. **[2]**

**5.** Which **one** of the following best describes kanban?

A: Ordering a fixed quantity every Friday.

B: A card signal that tells the previous stage to supply more material.

C: Buying the quantity that minimises ordering plus holding cost.

D: Keeping a large buffer of finished goods.

**[1]**

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**6.** Clayburn Tools buys steel blanks. It uses 25 blanks a day. The lead time is 6 days, the buffer stock is 100 blanks and the reorder quantity is 450 blanks.

**(a)** Calculate the reorder level and the maximum stock level. **[3]**

**(b)** Calculate the number of days between orders. **[2]**

**(c)** The supplier's lead time rises to 10 days. Calculate the new reorder level and explain what would happen if Clayburn kept the old reorder level. **[4]**

**7.** Clayburn's annual demand is 5,000 blanks. Each order costs £45 to place and holding one blank costs £3.60 a year.

**(a)** Calculate the economic order quantity (EOQ), to the nearest whole blank. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the total annual ordering and holding cost for an order size of 450 and for the EOQ from part (a). Use average stock equal to half the order size. **[4]**

**(c)** Evaluate whether Clayburn should adopt the EOQ. **[6]**

**8.** Pelham Components makes engine parts for a car manufacturer on a continuous flow line. A director proposes cutting buffer stock to a minimum and moving to just in time (JIT).

Evaluate the relationship between this method of production and the proposal to adopt JIT. **[9]**

**9.** Explain **two** ways in which electronic methods of stock control might help a supermarket. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** Buffer stock is the minimum level of stock that a business aims to hold at all times [1], to protect it against late deliveries or sudden rises in demand [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Lead time is the time between placing an order [1] and receiving the delivery [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Reorder level = buffer stock + (daily usage × lead time) = 120 + (60 × 3) [1] = **300 units** [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Holding stock ties up cash that could be used elsewhere [1], and there are extra costs for storage, insurance and security, and a risk of waste or damage [1]. Accept stock going out of date. **[2]**

**5.** **B** [1]. **[1]**

**6. (a)** Reorder level = 100 + (25 × 6) = 100 + 150 = **250 blanks** [1] [1]. Maximum stock = 250 + 450 − 150 = **550 blanks** [1]. **[3]**

**(b)** Days between orders = reorder quantity ÷ daily usage = 450 ÷ 25 [1] = **18 days** [1]. **[2]**

**(c)** New reorder level = 100 + (25 × 10) = 100 + 250 = **350 blanks** [1] [1]. If Clayburn kept 250, stock would fall by 25 × 10 = 250 during the lead time, from 250 to 0 [1]. The buffer stock would be used up and any further delay or rise in usage would cause a stock-out and halt production [1]. **[4]**

**7. (a)** EOQ = √(2 × 5,000 × 45 ÷ 3.60) [1] = √125,000 = 353.6, so **354 blanks** [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** At 450: orders = 5,000 ÷ 450 = 11.11, so ordering cost = 11.11 × £45 = £500; holding cost = 225 × £3.60 = £810; total = **£1,310** [1] [1]. At 354: orders = 5,000 ÷ 354 = 14.12, so ordering cost = £635.59; holding cost = 177 × £3.60 = £637.20; total = **£1,272.79** [1] [1]. **[4]**

**(c)** The EOQ saves £1,310 − £1,272.79 = £37.21 a year [1], which is small, about 2.8% of the cost at the current order size [1]. The model assumes steady demand and constant costs [1], and ignores discounts for bigger orders, storage space and the risk of steel price changes [1]. Smaller, more frequent orders mean more deliveries and administration, which the order cost already includes [1].
Judgement: the saving is too small to justify a major change, but it costs nothing to move towards the EOQ at the next order if the supplier is willing, so Clayburn could adopt it gradually while keeping the buffer for supply risk [1]. **[6]**

**8.** Flow production runs continuously, so every part must be available at every moment [1]. A late delivery would stop the whole line and also stop the customer's assembly line [1], which would probably bring penalty payments and lost trade [1].
JIT would release cash and warehouse space, and reduce waste [1], and the flow line's steady, predictable demand suits regular deliveries and kanban signals [1].
Against this, the cost of a stoppage in flow production is high, so removing buffer stock removes the protection against supplier failure, traffic or breakdown [1]. The proposal depends on how reliable the suppliers are, how close they are and how good the information links are [1].
Stakeholders: the car manufacturer wants guaranteed supply; employees face stoppages and lay-offs if parts run short; suppliers must deliver to a tight schedule [1].
Judgement: Pelham should reduce buffer stock gradually, and keep some safety stock for critical parts until suppliers have proved reliable, because a short stoppage would cost more than the holding costs saved [1]. **[9]**

**9.** Award up to 2 marks for each of two ways. Barcode scanning at the checkout records each sale [1], so the system knows the stock level in real time and can place an automatic order at the reorder level [1]. Electronic records reduce counting errors and theft that goes unnoticed [1], so the supermarket can reduce the stock lost or wasted [1]. **[4]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Forgetting to multiply daily usage by lead time.
- Subtracting the wrong quantity when finding maximum stock.
- Describing JIT as "no stock" rather than as stock arriving when needed.
- Treating EOQ as a guaranteed saving without stating its assumptions.
- Writing about stock control in general terms instead of the production method in the case.
