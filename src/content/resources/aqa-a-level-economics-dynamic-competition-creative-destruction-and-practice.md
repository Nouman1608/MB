## Questions

**1.** Define non-price competition and give two examples. **[3]**

**2.** Firm Delta sells lamps for 27 each. Variable cost per lamp is 18. A marketing campaign costs 31,500.

**(a)** Calculate the number of extra lamps Delta must sell to break even on the campaign. **[2]**
**(b)** The campaign adds 4,200 lamps. Calculate the net gain. **[2]**

**3.** Explain one short-run benefit and one long-run benefit of competition. **[4]**

**4.** Kettle K costs 45 and lasts 5 years. Kettle L costs 28 and lasts 2.5 years. Calculate the cost per year of each and explain why the maker of K might stress durability rather than cut its price. **[4]**

**5.** In an invented market for meal kits in Country B, one firm (Tamsin) sells 30,000 boxes a month at 25 with an average cost of 17. A new firm (Pellet) enters with a cheaper delivery system. The market price falls to 21. Tamsin sells 26,000 boxes and Pellet 14,000, and Tamsin's average cost is unchanged.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage change in Tamsin's profit. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain how the data illustrate creative destruction. **[3]**

**6.** An entrant's total entry cost is 180,000, of which 25% can be recovered on leaving. Calculate the sunk cost and explain why it affects how contestable the market is. **[4]**

**7.** In the same market, the entrant expects to sell 3,000 units a month at a price of 8.40 with a unit cost of 7.20, for nine months before the incumbent responds. The sunk cost is 135,000.

**(a)** Calculate the net gain or loss from a nine-month hit-and-run entry. **[4]**
**(b)** The sunk cost falls to 10% of 180,000. Calculate the new net gain or loss. **[2]**

**8.** State two features of a perfectly contestable market and explain why a market with one seller might still keep price close to average cost. **[4]**

**9.** Evaluate whether profits earned by a firm with monopoly power will always be competed away over time. **[9]**

**10.** In Country C an incumbent can sell 6,000 units a month at 22 with an average cost of 13, or 9,000 units at 16 with an average cost of 12.50. A would-be entrant has an average cost of 17.

**(a)** Calculate the incumbent's monthly profit at each price and the percentage change in profit if it moves to the lower price. **[4]**
**(b)** Explain why the incumbent might choose the lower price. **[3]**
**(c)** If the incumbent keeps its price at 22, an entrant could sell 3,000 units a month at 19 with a unit cost of 14 for six months, with an entry cost of 200,000. Evaluate whether entry is likely if 30% of the entry cost can be recovered, and if 85% can be recovered. **[5]**

**11.** Explain two ways in which technological change can lead to creative destruction. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** Non-price competition is rivalry between firms based on something other than a lower price [1], such as product quality or design [1], branding, after-sales service, delivery speed or cost reduction [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* A definition that says only "not about price" tends to earn less than one that names what firms compete on; give two distinct examples, not two wordings of one.

**2.** (a) Margin per lamp = 27 - 18 = 9 [1]. Break-even = 31,500 / 9 = **3,500 lamps** [1]. **(b)** Extra margin = 4,200 x 9 = 37,800 [1]. Net gain = 37,800 - 31,500 = **6,300** [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the margin before dividing; working that is set out step by step lets a reader see your method even if one figure slips. Give units or a currency symbol when the question does.

**3.** Short run: competing firms undercut each other, so price falls for consumers [1], because a firm charging more than rivals loses sales [1]. Long run: firms have time to invest in research and new processes [1], which lowers unit costs and improves products over time, benefits that last [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Questions that separate short and long run reward an answer that places each benefit in the right time frame and explains the mechanism, not a list of benefits.

**4.** K: 45 / 5 = 9 a year [1]. L: 28 / 2.5 = 11.20 a year [1]. K is cheaper per year of use by 2.20 [1]. The maker of K can compete on quality and durability, keeping a higher sticker price and avoiding a price war that rivals would copy quickly [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Answers that compare the two per-year figures and then link to the firm's choice gain more than answers that stop at the calculation.

**5.** (a) Profit before = (25 - 17) x 30,000 = 240,000 [1]. Profit after = (21 - 17) x 26,000 = 104,000 [1]. Change = (104,000 - 240,000) / 240,000 = **-56.7%** [1]. **(b)** A new entrant with a cheaper delivery system entered the market [1]. The incumbent's profit and market share fell and price fell for consumers [1]. Consumer spending rose from 750,000 to 840,000 (a rise of 12%) as more boxes were sold: the new firm created new sales as the old firm's position was destroyed [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* In a percentage-change calculation, divide by the original figure. In the explanation, tie each point to a number from the data rather than defining the term in general.

**6.** Recoverable = 180,000 x 0.25 = 45,000 [1]. Sunk cost = 180,000 - 45,000 = **135,000** [1]. Money that cannot be got back on leaving makes entry a bigger gamble [1]. The higher the sunk costs, the less contestable the market, as entrants fear a loss if they fail [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Be clear which part is sunk. A common slip is to treat the recoverable share as the sunk cost.

**7.** (a) Monthly profit = 3,000 x (8.40 - 7.20) = 3,600 [1]. Nine months = 32,400 [1]. Net = 32,400 - 135,000 = **-102,600** [1]. A loss, so the hit-and-run entry does not pay [1]. **(b)** New sunk cost = 180,000 x 0.10 = 18,000 [1]. Net = 32,400 - 18,000 = **+14,400** [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* State the decision as well as the figure; the conclusion shows you understand what the number means for the entrant.

**8.** Any two of: free entry [1], free exit or no sunk costs [1]. Even one seller faces the threat that a rival will enter and take any supernormal profit [1], so it keeps price close to average cost and earns about normal profit [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* The link between the threat of entry and the incumbent's pricing is what shows understanding; features listed with no link usually earn only part of the credit.

**9.** Chain: monopoly power gives large profit [1], which gives an incentive for new firms to enter and innovate [1], overcoming barriers, so the incumbent loses sales and the profit falls: creative destruction [1]. Application: the process is stronger where barriers are low and sunk costs small, or where entrants have access to finance [1], and it may be weaker if the incumbent holds patents or raises sunk costs through advertising or contracts [1]. Evaluation: it can take years, during which consumers pay high prices [1]; the incumbent may buy or copy the rival [1]; the entrant may itself become dominant, so profits move rather than disappear [1]. Judgement: profits are not always competed away, and the speed depends on the height of barriers and the pace of technological change [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* The strongest answers argue a line from start to end and reach a reasoned judgement, rather than listing points for and against; say what the outcome depends on.

**10.** (a) Profit at 22 = (22 - 13) x 6,000 = 54,000 [1]. Profit at 16 = (16 - 12.50) x 9,000 = 31,500 [1]. Change = (31,500 - 54,000) / 54,000 = **-41.7%** [1]. The answer is a fall in profit [1]. **(b)** The entrant's cost is 17, so at 16 it cannot compete profitably [1]. At 22 it could undercut and profit, so the incumbent risks losing sales [1]. A lower price secures lasting but smaller profit and deters entry [1]. **(c)** Entrant's monthly profit = 3,000 x (19 - 14) = 15,000 [1]. Six months = 90,000 [1]. If 30% recoverable, sunk = 140,000, net = -50,000, so entry is unlikely [1]. If 85% recoverable, sunk = 30,000, net = +60,000, so hit-and-run entry is likely [1]. Judgement: high sunk costs protect the incumbent, so contestability depends on them [1]. **[12]**
*Examiner insight:* In a longer data question, set out each calculation separately and then use the results in the evaluation; conclusions that cite the figures are easier to credit than general statements.

**11.** First, a new technology can make an existing product obsolete [1], so sales and jobs in the old industry fall while the new product creates a new market [1]. Second, a technology can lower costs or open a new way to reach customers [1], helping an entrant overcome the barriers that protected the incumbent and take its market share [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Each way needs a developed link from technology to the market outcome; naming a technology without the effect on incumbent and entrant tends to earn little.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using the new figure as the base in a percentage change.
- Treating the recoverable share of an entry cost as the sunk cost.
- Writing that creative destruction only closes firms, without the new products and entry.
- Listing features of a contestable market without linking them to the incumbent's behaviour.
- Giving benefits of competition without saying whether they are short run or long run.
- Giving a calculation with no working, units or conclusion.
- Describing non-price competition with no effect on revenue, cost or market share.
- Ending an evaluation without a judgement or without saying what the answer depends on.
