## What you must be able to do

- 1.4 (a) **Describe** the principles of professional ethics.
- 1.4 (b) **Apply** them to accounting roles and functions.
- 1.4 (c) **Explain** the public interest and how it is applied to accounting roles and functions.

The specification does not name the principles. Use the five fundamental principles of the international code for professional accountants.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning to learn |
|---|---|
| Professional ethics | Standards of behaviour expected of accountants because others trust their work |
| Integrity | Honest and straightforward; never linked with false or misleading information |
| Objectivity | Judgement not overridden by bias, conflict of interest or pressure |
| Professional competence and due care | Knowledge and skill kept up to date; work done carefully and to standard |
| Confidentiality | Work information not disclosed without authority or used for personal gain |
| Professional behaviour | Obeying laws and rules; avoiding conduct that discredits the profession |
| Conflict of interest | The accountant, or someone close, gains from the decision being made |
| Public interest | The wellbeing of everyone who relies on accounting information, not only the employer |
| Safeguard | An action that reduces a risk to a principle, such as disclosure or independent review |

## Each principle: trigger and response

| Principle | Typical trigger in a question | Expected response |
|---|---|---|
| Integrity | Asked to invent, hide, backdate or alter an entry | Refuse; record the true figure; explain why |
| Objectivity | Gift, family link, bonus based on profit, pressure from a manager | Disclose the interest; refuse the gift; let someone independent decide |
| Competence and due care | Unfamiliar task, deadline pressure, skipped checks | Admit the gap; seek training, help or more time; check the work |
| Confidentiality | Friend, relative or rival asks for work information; former employer's data | Do not disclose; disclose only with permission or where the law requires |
| Professional behaviour | Online comments about clients, exaggerated qualifications, breaking rules | Act lawfully and courteously; do not discredit the profession |

## The four-step "apply" answer

1. Fact from the scenario that creates the risk.
2. Name of the principle at risk.
3. Why it is at risk, and who could be misled or harmed.
4. What the accountant should do.

Model in one paragraph: "A supplier has offered the clerk a 200 voucher (fact). This threatens objectivity (principle) because the clerk may favour that supplier when deciding which invoices to pay first, so other suppliers are treated unfairly (why). The clerk should decline the voucher and tell the manager (action)."

## Public interest in roles and functions

- **Recording:** complete, accurate records, because statements built from them are relied on.
- **Payroll:** correct deductions passed to the authorities.
- **Reporting:** statements must not mislead lenders, suppliers, investors or employees.
- **Auditing:** an honest report even if the client dislikes it.
- **Advising:** no help to hide income or evade tax.

Key idea: when the employer's wishes would mislead outsiders, the public interest comes first.

## Must-know distinctions

| Pair | Difference |
|---|---|
| Integrity and objectivity | Integrity: is the record true? Objectivity: is the judgement free from bias? |
| Confidentiality and public interest | Confidentiality limits disclosure; it does not require hiding wrongdoing that the law requires to be reported |
| Competence and due care | Competence is knowing how; due care is doing it carefully and on time |
| Employer's interest and public interest | The employer pays the accountant; the public relies on the information |

## Exam traps

- Naming every principle in the hope one is right. Pick the one the facts point to.
- Describing a principle with one word ("honesty") when "describe" needs a full sentence.
- Leaving out the action at the end of an "apply" answer.
- Calling confidentiality absolute, so the accountant protects a fraud.
- Writing "public interest means what the public wants".
- Using a generic example instead of the scenario's role, such as payroll or purchase ledger.
- Confusing a breach of competence with a breach of integrity when no one intended to mislead.

## Quick self-test

1. Name the five fundamental principles.
2. Which point of section 1.4 uses the command word "apply"?
3. A bookkeeper is told to record a sale dated 2 January as a December sale. Which principle is mainly at risk?
4. A credit controller's best friend owes the business 900 and asks for more time. Which principle is at risk, and what should the controller do?
5. Describe professional competence and due care.
6. An accounts assistant tells a neighbour the owner's annual drawings. Which principle is breached?
7. Define the public interest in accounting.
8. Give two groups outside the business who rely on its financial statements.
9. Is confidentiality ever overridden? Give one case.
10. An accountant posts a joke about a client's mistakes on social media. Which principle is breached?
11. What is a safeguard? Give one example.
12. Why is a profit-related bonus a threat to objectivity?

### Answers

1. Integrity, objectivity, professional competence and due care, confidentiality, professional behaviour.
2. 1.4 (b): apply the principles to accounting roles and functions.
3. Integrity: the record would be false. (Due care is also relevant, as income belongs to the period it is earned.)
4. Objectivity, because a personal relationship could influence the decision. The controller should disclose the friendship and let another person decide.
5. Having and keeping up to date the knowledge and skill needed for the work, and doing it carefully, thoroughly and in line with standards.
6. Confidentiality.
7. The wellbeing of everyone who relies on accounting information, such as lenders, investors, suppliers, employees and tax authorities, not only the employer or client.
8. Any two from: lenders, suppliers, investors, employees, tax authorities, customers.
9. Yes: where the law requires disclosure, or where the client or employer gives permission.
10. Professional behaviour (and confidentiality if the client can be identified).
11. An action that reduces a threat to a principle, such as disclosing a family link and passing the decision to a colleague.
12. The accountant gains personally from a higher reported profit, so judgements about estimates may be biased upwards.

## Frequently asked questions

**How many principles of professional ethics should I learn for 4AC1?**
Learn the five fundamental principles of the international code: integrity, objectivity, professional competence and due care, confidentiality and professional behaviour. The specification itself does not list them.

**What should every "apply" answer on ethics include?**
A fact from the scenario, the principle at risk, why it is at risk and what the accountant should do. Leaving out the action is the most common way to lose credit.

**Does the public interest mean the accountant ignores the employer?**
No. The accountant still works for the employer, but must not follow instructions that would mislead people who rely on the accounts.
