radiation** of the AQA GCSE Physics (8463) specification. The section is marked **physics only**, so it
appears in GCSE Physics but not in Combined Science. It is assessed on Paper 1 at Foundation and Higher
Tier, and none of it is marked (HT only). It has three parts: background radiation (4.4.3.1), different
half-lives (4.4.3.2) and uses of nuclear radiation in medicine (4.4.3.3).

## What the specification asks

| Spec point | What you must be able to do |
|---|---|
| 4.4.3.1 Background radiation | Name natural and man-made sources; explain how occupation and location change the level and dose; use doses in Sv and mSv |
| 4.4.3.2 Different half-lives | Explain why the hazard of a radioactive material depends on its half-life; use data in standard form |
| 4.4.3.3 Uses of nuclear radiation | Describe and evaluate the use of radiation to explore internal organs and to control or destroy unwanted tissue; evaluate perceived risks using given data and consequences |

You do not need to recall the sievert as the unit of dose, but you must be able to use it when a question
gives it.

## 4.4.3.1 Background radiation

Background radiation is the radiation around us all the time. The specification groups its sources in two
sets.

- **Natural sources**: rocks (some rocks contain radioactive isotopes, and some release the radioactive gas
  radon) and **cosmic rays** from space.
- **Man-made sources**: **fallout from nuclear weapons testing** and from **nuclear accidents**, such as the
  Chernobyl accident in 1986.

The level of background radiation, and the dose a person receives, can depend on:

- **location**: areas built on granite release more radon, so homes there can have higher levels; at high
  altitude there is less atmosphere above you to absorb cosmic rays.
- **occupation**: airline crew spend many hours at altitude, so they receive more cosmic radiation;
  hospital radiography staff and nuclear industry workers work near sources; miners work underground near
  rock.

### Radiation dose

**Radiation dose** is a measure of the risk of harm to the body from exposure to radiation. It is measured
in **sieverts (Sv)**. Typical doses are small, so millisieverts are used:

```
1000 mSv = 1 Sv
to change mSv into Sv, divide by 1000:   3.6 mSv = 3.6 ÷ 1000 = 0.0036 Sv = 3.6 × 10⁻³ Sv
to change Sv into mSv, multiply by 1000: 0.025 Sv = 25 mSv
```

### Background in measurements

A detector always picks up background radiation, even with no source present. In any experiment you
measure the background count-rate first and **subtract it** from every reading.

**Worked example 1.** With no source present, a Geiger-Muller tube records 42 counts in 60 s. With a
source in place it records 1530 counts in 60 s. Find the count-rate due to the source alone.

```
background count-rate = 42 ÷ 60   = 0.70 counts/s
measured count-rate   = 1530 ÷ 60 = 25.5 counts/s
corrected count-rate  = 25.5 − 0.70 = 24.8 counts/s
```

If you skip the correction, a half-life read from the data comes out too long, because the count-rate
never falls below the background level.

## 4.4.3.2 Different half-lives

Radioactive isotopes have a **very wide range of half-lives**, from fractions of a second to billions of
years. Uranium-238, for example, has a half-life of about 4.5 × 10⁹ years; technetium-99m, used in medical
imaging, has a half-life of about 6 hours. Questions often give half-lives in standard form, so convert
them to a common unit before you compare them.

The hazard from a radioactive material depends on its half-life in two ways.

| Short half-life | Long half-life |
|---|---|
| Nuclei decay quickly, so the activity is high at first | Nuclei decay slowly, so the activity is lower |
| Intense hazard soon after the material is made or released | Lower hazard at any moment |
| Activity falls quickly, so the hazard is soon small | Material stays radioactive for a very long time, so it must be stored safely for centuries or longer |

**Worked example 2.** Two materials each start with a count-rate of 800 counts/min. Material X has a
half-life of 2.0 × 10¹ minutes. Material Y has a half-life of 3.0 × 10⁴ years. Compare them after 2 hours.

```
X: half-life = 20 min;  2 hours = 120 min = 120 ÷ 20 = 6 half-lives
   800 → 400 → 200 → 100 → 50 → 25 → 12.5 counts/min
Y: 2 hours is a tiny fraction of 30 000 years, so the count-rate is still about 800 counts/min
```

Both start with the same count-rate, so the hazard is similar at first. Within two hours X has fallen
from 800 to about 12 counts/min, so it is soon safe to handle; Y hardly changes for thousands of years.
Spent nuclear fuel contains long half-life isotopes, which is why it must be stored securely for a very long
time.

## 4.4.3.3 Uses of nuclear radiation in medicine

The specification names two medical uses.

### Exploring internal organs (tracers)

A patient swallows, inhales or is injected with a small amount of a radioactive isotope (a **tracer**). It
collects in the organ being studied, and a detector outside the body records where the radiation comes from.
The tracer should:

- emit **gamma** radiation, so the radiation passes out of the body to the detector and causes little
  ionisation on the way;
- have a half-life of **a few hours**: long enough to finish the test, short enough that the activity in the
  body soon falls to a low level.

Technetium-99m (gamma emitter, half-life about 6 hours) is widely used in this way.

### Controlling or destroying unwanted tissue

High doses of ionising radiation kill cells. That is a hazard in everyday life but a treatment in medicine.

- **External beams**: gamma rays are aimed at a tumour from several directions. The tumour, where the beams
  cross, receives a large dose; the healthy tissue each beam passes through receives a smaller one.
- **Sources placed inside the body**: a radioactive isotope is put in or next to the unwanted tissue. For
  example, the thyroid gland absorbs iodine, so iodine-131 is used to treat some thyroid conditions; its beta
  radiation destroys thyroid tissue from the inside and travels only a short distance through tissue.

### Evaluating benefits and risks

"Evaluate" means weigh the evidence on both sides and reach a judgement. In this section that means comparing
the benefit to the patient with the risk of harm, using the data you are given.

**Perceived risk** is how dangerous people believe something is. It can be much larger or smaller than the
measured risk. Patients often fear a scan more than the background radiation they receive every year, even
when the doses are similar. Data let you test the perception.

**Worked example 3 (invented data).** A patient's yearly background dose is 2.4 mSv. A tracer scan of the
kidneys gives a dose of 1.2 mSv. Evaluate whether the scan is worth the risk.

```
1.2 ÷ 2.4 = 0.5
the scan adds a dose equal to half a year's background radiation
```

A strong conclusion: the scan adds about six months' worth of background dose, so the extra risk of harm is
small. The benefit is a diagnosis that could not be made from outside the body. The tracer has a short
half-life, so the dose is delivered over hours, not years. The benefit outweighs the risk, but the scan
should only be done when there is a medical reason.

### Annotated model answer

**Question (6 marks).** A tumour can be treated with a beam of gamma rays from outside the body. Evaluate
this treatment.

> Gamma rays are ionising, so a large enough dose kills the cells of the tumour **[benefit linked to
> property]**. Gamma rays penetrate tissue well, so the beam can reach a tumour deep in the body **[why gamma
> is chosen]**. However, the beam also passes through healthy tissue on the way in and out and can damage
> it, increasing the risk of side effects or of new cancers **[risk linked to property]**. The risk is
> reduced by aiming beams from several directions so that only the tumour receives the full dose
> **[how the risk is limited]**. Staff leave the room during treatment so that they are not exposed
> **[precaution]**. Overall the benefit of destroying a life-threatening tumour is greater than the risk of
> damage to healthy tissue, so the treatment is justified **[judgement]**.

Each sentence pairs a fact with a reason, and the answer ends with a judgement. A list of advantages with no
conclusion stays in the lower mark levels.

## Common mistakes

- Naming "nuclear power stations" as the man-made source. The specification's man-made sources are
  **fallout from nuclear weapons testing** and **nuclear accidents**.
- Calling radon gas or X-rays from a hospital scan "fallout". Fallout comes from weapons testing and
  accidents.
- Dividing by 100 instead of 1000 when converting mSv into Sv.
- Saying a long half-life material is "more radioactive". Its activity is lower; it is hazardous for
  **longer**.
- Choosing an alpha emitter as a tracer. Alpha cannot leave the body, so it cannot be detected outside and
  it strongly ionises the tissue around it.
- Writing "radiation is dangerous" in an evaluation without saying what harm, how likely it is, and how it
  compares with the benefit.
- Forgetting to subtract background count before working out a half-life.

## Exam technique

- **Describe**: state the facts in order, for example how a tracer moves to an organ and is detected.
- **Explain**: give a reason, linked with "because" or "so". "Gamma is used because it passes out of the
  body to the detector."
- **Evaluate**: both sides, then a judgement that refers to the data in the question.
- **Calculate**: show the conversion step when units are mixed; examiners give method marks for it.

With a table of isotopes, use every column and reject each unsuitable option with a reason.
