Condensed recall for **7.4 Sustainable management of tropical environments**, the case study section of Topic 7, Paper 3 Advanced Physical Geography Options. The syllabus asks for threats (exploitation) to, and problems of sustainably managing, areas within **either** the rainforest **or** the savanna ecosystem, and an evaluation of attempted solutions. For full explanations, read the [7.4 study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-sustainable-management-of-tropical-environments/), then try the [7.4 practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-sustainable-management-of-tropical-environments-practice/).

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Exploitation | Use of an ecosystem's resources, such as timber, land, minerals or wildlife |
| Sustainable management | Using an area to meet present needs without reducing its ability to meet future needs, environmentally, economically and socially |
| Deforestation | Permanent removal of forest cover |
| Degradation | Loss of quality, such as soil fertility or biodiversity, without full clearance |
| Bush encroachment | Spread of woody shrubs into savanna grassland, often after fire suppression or heavy grazing |
| Overgrazing | Grazing beyond what vegetation can recover from |
| Payments for ecosystem services | Paying landowners to keep services such as carbon storage, water and biodiversity |
| Community-based natural resource management | Local communities given rights and income from managing resources |
| Conservancy | Land pooled by owners and managed for wildlife and tourism |
| Certification | Labelling of products, such as timber or palm oil, produced to agreed standards |
| Agroforestry | Growing trees with crops or livestock on the same land |
| Ecotourism | Tourism based on nature that aims to benefit conservation and local people |
| Human-wildlife conflict | Damage by wild animals to crops, livestock or people |

## Threats

| Rainforest | Savanna |
|---|---|
| Cattle ranching and soy farming | Overgrazing by livestock |
| Logging, legal and illegal | Conversion to crops |
| Mining and oil | Fuelwood and charcoal collection |
| Roads opening the forest to settlers | Poaching of wildlife |
| Dams flooding forest | Changed fire regimes: too frequent, or suppressed |
| Plantations such as oil palm | Fencing that blocks wildlife migration |

## Why management is difficult

**Physical**

- Rainforest nutrients are in the biomass, so cleared soils lose fertility within a few years.
- Bare soils under heavy rain suffer leaching, erosion and laterite crusts.
- Secondary forest takes decades to regain structure.
- Savanna rainfall varies, so safe stocking levels change from year to year.
- Savanna balance depends on fire and grazing; too much or too little changes it.

**Human**

- Poverty makes short-term use rational.
- Insecure land rights discourage long-term care.
- Global demand rewards conversion.
- Remote areas are hard to police.
- Interests conflict: governments, companies, farmers, indigenous peoples, conservationists.
- Wildlife damages crops and livestock.

## Case study summaries

**Costa Rica (rainforest)**

- Forest cut for cattle pasture after 1950; around a fifth of the country forested by the 1980s.
- 1996 forestry law banned conversion of forest; payments for environmental services began in 1997 through a national fund, FONAFIFO.
- Payments per hectare for protection, tree planting and agroforestry; four services recognised (carbon, water, biodiversity, scenic beauty).
- Funded partly by a share of the fuel tax.
- Forest cover recovered to over half the country; ecotourism grew.
- Limits: recovery also reflects declining ranching and rural out-migration; some payments for land not at risk; harder for poor smallholders to join.

**CAMPFIRE, Zimbabwe (savanna)**

- Began 1989 in communal lands.
- Rural district councils sold hunting and tourism rights; revenue to be shared with communities.
- Early gains: income for schools, clinics, wells; wildlife valued.
- Limits: councils kept much revenue; small benefits per household; reliance on trophy hunting; 2014 United States suspension of elephant trophy imports cut income; 2000s crisis weakened it.

**Maasai Mara conservancies, Kenya (savanna)**

- Since the mid-2000s, landowners pool plots and lease them to safari operators.
- Regular lease payments; limits on tourist beds; managed grazing.
- Keeps land open for wildlife.
- Limits: depends on international tourism (collapse during COVID-19); non-members do not benefit.

## Evaluation checklist

For each solution ask:

1. Did it meet its aim? What is the evidence?
2. Who gained and who lost?
3. Is it funded for the long term?
4. Did it cause leakage (clearance moving elsewhere)?
5. Could it be copied elsewhere?
6. Was the result caused by the scheme, or by other changes at the same time?

## Exam traps

- Study **one** ecosystem in depth; the syllabus says either rainforest or savanna.
- Link threats to physical fragility from 7.3.
- Sustainability has social and economic parts, not only environmental.
- Always give a date, a figure or a named scheme.
- Do not credit a scheme with all improvement if other factors changed too.
- End with a judgement that says for whom and why a scheme worked.

## Self-test

1. Define sustainable management.
2. Give two threats to rainforest and two to savanna.
3. Why do cleared rainforest soils lose fertility quickly?
4. What is bush encroachment and what can cause it?
5. When did Costa Rica's payments for environmental services begin?
6. Name the four services recognised by Costa Rica's scheme.
7. Give one reason why Costa Rica's forest recovery cannot be credited only to payments.
8. What does the acronym CAMPFIRE stand for, and when did it start?
9. Give two weaknesses of CAMPFIRE.
10. How do Maasai Mara conservancies give landowners an income?
11. What is leakage in forest conservation?
12. Give one strength and one weakness of certification schemes.

### Answers

1. Using an area to meet present needs without reducing its ability to meet future needs, environmentally, economically and socially.
2. Rainforest: ranching, logging (also mining, roads). Savanna: overgrazing, poaching (also fuelwood, crop conversion).
3. Most nutrients were in the biomass, which is removed, and heavy rain leaches the soil.
4. The spread of woody shrubs into grassland; fire suppression or heavy grazing.
5. 1997, following the 1996 forestry law.
6. Carbon storage, watershed protection, biodiversity and scenic beauty.
7. Cattle ranching became less profitable and rural people moved to towns.
8. Communal Areas Management Programme for Indigenous Resources; 1989.
9. Councils kept much of the revenue; dependence on trophy hunting and outside decisions.
10. Safari operators pay regular lease fees for using pooled land.
11. Clearance displaced from a protected area to another area.
12. Strength: uses consumer pressure. Weakness: covers only part of the market, and monitoring is costly.
