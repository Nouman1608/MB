This guide covers section **3.1.5 Business location** of OxfordAQA International GCSE Business (9225), part of Topic 1, Business in the real world. The specification asks you to understand the factors that influence where a business is located: **proximity to the market, availability of raw materials, labour, competition, costs and government influence**. Questions usually give a business and a choice of site, and ask you to weigh the factors against each other and recommend one. All businesses and figures in this guide are invented.

For the whole topic, see the [Business in the Real World study guide](/resources/igcse-oxfordaqa-business-business-in-the-real-world/). Condensed notes are in the [revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-business-9225-business-location-revision-notes/) and questions are in the [practice set](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-business-9225-business-location-practice/).

## Why location matters

The location of a business affects its costs, its sales and how easily it can find staff. A poor choice is expensive to correct, because moving a shop or factory is costly and disruptive. That is why a business weighs several factors before it decides. The right location depends on the type of business: a factory, a restaurant and an online retailer each care about different things.

## The six factors in the specification

### 1. Proximity to the market

The **market** is where the customers are. Being close to customers helps in two ways. A shop or restaurant needs to be where there are many customers, such as a busy high street, a shopping centre or a transport hub. A business that sells heavy or fragile goods, or goods that must be fresh, saves on transport and delivery times by being close to its customers, for example a bakery or a concrete mixing plant.

For businesses that sell online, proximity matters less. Customers can order from anywhere and the goods are posted. These businesses may care more about the cost of the site and access to delivery companies.

### 2. Availability of raw materials

Some businesses are tied to the place where their raw materials are found. A quarry, a fishing business and a sawmill must be near stone, fish and trees. Locating a business near its raw materials reduces transport costs, especially when the raw materials are bulky, heavy or lose weight during processing, or when they spoil quickly. A fruit canning factory is best close to the farms that supply the fruit.

### 3. Labour

A business needs a supply of workers with the right skills at a price it can afford. Some places have a large pool of skilled workers because of local colleges or because similar firms already exist there. Wage rates vary between areas, and a business that needs many workers, such as a clothing factory or call centre, may choose an area where wages are lower, so long as workers have the right skills. If a location has a shortage of workers, the business may have to pay more or train people itself.

### 4. Competition

Competition can attract or deter a business.

- **Locating near competitors** (clustering) can help. A new restaurant on a street of restaurants benefits from customers who come to the area to eat. Car showrooms often group together for the same reason.
- **Locating away from competitors** can help a business that wants to be the only supplier in an area, such as a petrol station in a small town.

The right choice depends on whether customers compare before buying or buy from the nearest convenient place.

### 5. Costs

Costs include **rent or purchase price**, rates and taxes, wages and transport. City centres are expensive; edge-of-town sites and industrial areas are cheaper. A new business with little money may choose a cheaper site even if fewer customers pass by. Costs should be compared as a total, not one by one.

**Worked example 1: comparing two sites.** A printing firm chooses between Site A in the city and Site B on an industrial estate. Monthly costs are:

| Cost | Site A (city) | Site B (estate) |
|---|---|---|
| Rent | $3,500 | $2,000 |
| Wages | $9,000 | $7,500 |
| Transport | $1,200 | $3,000 |
| **Total** | **$13,700** | **$12,500** |

```
Site A total = 3,500 + 9,000 + 1,200 = $13,700
Site B total = 2,000 + 7,500 + 3,000 = $12,500
Difference   = $13,700 - $12,500 = $1,200 a month = $14,400 a year
```

Site B is $14,400 a year cheaper, even though it has higher transport costs. However, if customers prefer to visit the city shop, Site A may bring in more sales. Cost is one factor, not the whole answer.

### 6. Government influence

Governments influence location through:

- **Grants and cheaper loans** for businesses that locate in areas with high unemployment.
- **Lower taxes or rent** in special development zones.
- **Planning rules**, which control where shops, factories and homes can be built.
- **Infrastructure**, such as roads, railways, ports and broadband, which make a location more attractive.
- **Restrictions**, such as keeping noisy or polluting factories away from housing.

A grant may make an otherwise expensive location the cheapest choice.

## Putting the factors together

The factors can point in different directions. The usual skill is to decide which matters most for the business and say why.

| Type of business | Most important factors | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Clothes shop | Proximity to the market, competition | Needs passing customers and may gain from other shops nearby |
| Sawmill | Raw materials, costs | Timber is bulky and expensive to move |
| Call centre | Labour, costs | Needs many trained staff at an affordable wage; customers do not visit |
| Online retailer | Costs, transport links, labour | Customers do not visit; warehouse near delivery routes |
| Fresh food producer | Market, raw materials | Products spoil quickly |

**Worked example 2: footfall and sales.** A cafe in a busy street has 1,200 people passing each day and 5% of them come in. On a quiet street, only 300 people pass and 2% come in.

```
Busy street = 5% x 1,200 = 60 customers a day
Quiet street = 2% x 300 = 6 customers a day
```

If the average customer spends $6 and the cafe is open 360 days, the busy street brings 60 x $6 x 360 = $129,600 a year and the quiet street 6 x $6 x 360 = $12,960. The busy street's higher rent is only worthwhile if it is less than the extra $116,640 of sales that it makes possible, after allowing for the cost of the extra food and staff.

**Worked example 3: transport cost per unit.** A cement firm sells 6,000 tonnes a month. At its present site, transport costs are $18,000 a month; at a site closer to its customers, the cost is $4,500.

```
Present transport cost per tonne = 18,000 / 6,000 = $3.00
New transport cost per tonne     = 4,500 / 6,000 = $0.75
Saving per tonne                 = $2.25
```

For heavy products, a lower transport cost per unit can be a major advantage.

## Choosing a location in the exam

Follow these steps for a recommendation question.

1. Identify what kind of business it is and what it needs most.
2. Go through the factors, using details from the case.
3. Compare the sites on the factors that matter most.
4. Recommend one, acknowledging the strongest argument for the other.

## Worked exam-style answer

**Question (6 marks):** Analyse why a bakery might choose a busy high street for its location.

**Answer:** A bakery sells fresh products that customers usually buy on impulse or on the way to work, so being close to the market matters most. On a busy high street 5% of passers-by might come in, which for 1,200 people a day is 60 customers, ten times more than on a quiet street. This gives the bakery higher sales and a better chance of covering its costs. However, rent on a high street is higher, so the extra sales must exceed the extra rent. Being near other food shops may also attract more shoppers, although it brings competition. The bakery should choose the high street if the additional revenue clearly outweighs the additional rent.

Notice the structure: factor, figure from the case, effect on sales and cost, then a limit.

## Common mistakes

- Listing all six factors for every business. Pick the ones that are most relevant.
- Saying that "cheap rent is best" without checking wages, transport and sales.
- Assuming that being near competitors is always bad.
- Forgetting that online businesses may not need to be near their customers.
- Giving a recommendation without a reason from the case study.
- Confusing government influence with government ownership.

## Exam technique for this section

For **identify** and **state** questions, name the factor. For **explain** questions, link the factor to the business: "being near the fish market reduces transport costs for the fish processing firm because fresh fish spoils quickly". For **analyse** questions, follow the chain from the location factor to the cost or sales effect. For **evaluate** questions, weigh up the sites, decide which factors are most important for this business, and justify the final judgement.
