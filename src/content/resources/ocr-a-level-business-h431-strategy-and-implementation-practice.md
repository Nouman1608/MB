## Questions

### Section A: short-answer questions

**1.** Explain the relationship between a business's objectives, its strategy and its tactics. **[3]**

**2.** Kelstone Bakery is a chain of eight bakeries. Classify each of the following as an objective, a strategy or a tactic: (i) increase sales revenue by 12% within two years; (ii) move into supplying sandwiches to offices across the city; (iii) a "buy two, get one free" offer on rolls this Friday. **[3]**

**3.** Explain **one** reason why a business needs to plan the implementation of its strategy. **[3]**

**4.** State **two** contents of an implementation plan. **[2]**

**5.** Explain what is meant by a functional strategy. **[2]**

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**6.** Larkfield Pet Supplies has two shops. Its objective is to double sales revenue in four years. Its proposed strategy is to open three new shops at a cost of £250,000 each, funded by a bank loan. Each new shop would have fixed costs of £9,000 a month. Customers spend an average of £18 a visit, and the goods sold cost Larkfield an average of £10.80 per visit. The owner forecasts 1,600 customer visits a month at each new shop.

**(a)** Calculate the break-even number of customer visits a month for one new shop. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the margin of safety for one new shop, in visits and as a percentage of forecast visits. **[2]**

**(c)** Calculate the forecast monthly profit from the three new shops together. **[2]**

**(d)** Evaluate Larkfield's proposed strategy. **[9]**

**7.** Using your answers to Question 6, recommend and justify **one** way Larkfield could alter its strategy. **[6]**

**8.** Vantor Engineering has made low-cost metal parts for other manufacturers for 20 years. Overseas rivals now undercut its prices, and its profit has fallen for three years. The directors propose a new strategy: to make specialist precision parts for medical-equipment makers. This would need £2 million of new machinery, retraining for most of the 140 staff and the loss of about 30 jobs in the low-cost section. Evaluate the impact and importance of this change of strategy to Vantor Engineering and its stakeholders. **[12]**

**9.** Marisco Hotels, which owns five hotels, has chosen a strategy of winning more repeat guests. It is introducing a loyalty scheme and a new booking system at all five hotels in the same month. Staff were told about the changes one week before launch. Analyse **two** reasons why this strategy might fail at the implementation stage. **[6]**

**10.** Fernhill Cider sells cider to pubs and shops in England. Its objective is to raise annual profit by £20,000 within four years. It has £150,000 available and two options.

| | Option 1: export to the Netherlands | Option 2: alcohol-free cider for existing UK customers |
|---|---|---|
| Cost | £120,000 | £80,000 |
| Forecast extra profit a year | £30,000 | £24,000 |
| Knowledge of the market | None | Strong |

**(a)** Calculate the payback period for each option. **[2]**

**(b)** Recommend and justify which strategy Fernhill Cider should choose. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** Objectives are the targets the business wants to reach [1]; the strategy is the long-term plan for reaching them [1]; tactics are the short-term actions that carry out the strategy, so each level serves the one above [1]. **[3]**

**2.** (i) Objective [1]. (ii) Strategy [1]. (iii) Tactic [1]. **[3]**

**3.** Resources such as cash and staff time must be available at the right moment [1]; for example, a new site cannot open until fitting-out is paid for and staff are trained [1]; without a plan, steps happen in the wrong order and the strategy is delayed or costs more than expected [1]. Also accept responsibility, communication, milestones or risk, with development. **[3]**

**4.** Any two from: actions and their sequence; who is responsible; timeline and milestones; resources and budget; measures of success; communication plan; risks and fallback [1] [1]. **[2]**

**5.** A plan for one department or function, such as marketing or human resources [1], that sets out its part in delivering the corporate strategy [1]. **[2]**

**6. (a)** Contribution per visit = 18 − 10.80 = £7.20 [1]; 9,000 ÷ 7.20 = **1,250 visits** a month [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** 1,600 − 1,250 = **350 visits** [1]; 350 ÷ 1,600 × 100 = **21.9%** [1]. **[2]**

**(c)** Per shop: (1,600 × 7.20) − 9,000 = 11,520 − 9,000 = £2,520 [1]; three shops: **£7,560** a month [1]. **[2]**

**(d)** Suitability: opening shops is a sensible route to doubling revenue, since three new shops would more than double the number of outlets [1].
Feasibility: the £750,000 loan is very large compared with forecast profit of £7,560 a month, or £90,720 a year, so payback on the shops would take about 8.3 years [1], well beyond the four-year objective [1].
Acceptability: a margin of safety of 21.9% gives some protection [1], but loan interest has not been included and would reduce profit further, so the bank may doubt repayment [1]; the owner's personal risk is high if the business has only two existing shops [1].
Other points: the forecast of 1,600 visits is the owner's own and may be optimistic [1].
Judgement: the strategy is suitable for the objective but not feasible in its current form, because the borrowing is too large for the profit it generates [1]; its success depends on the accuracy of the visit forecast and on the cost of the loan [1]. **[9]**

**7.** Recommendation: open one shop first, funded by a smaller loan [1]. This reduces borrowing from £750,000 to £250,000 [1], so the loan is easier to repay from profit and the bank is more likely to agree [1]. It tests whether the forecast of 1,600 visits is accurate before more is committed [1]. What is kept: the growth-by-new-shops strategy, which suits the objective [1]. Trade-off: doubling revenue in four years becomes less likely, so the objective may need extending [1]. Also accept alternatives such as smaller premises or an online channel, with justification. **[6]**

**8.** Impact on the business: the change moves Vantor away from a market where it cannot match overseas prices [1] into a niche where quality and precision matter more than price, so margins may be higher [1]. But £2 million of machinery is a large commitment that is hard to reverse [1], and Vantor has no experience of medical-equipment customers, who may demand strict quality standards [1].
Stakeholders: about 30 employees lose their jobs, and the local community loses income [1]; the remaining 110 staff gain more skilled, possibly more secure work after retraining, though some may fear the change [1]; shareholders face short-term cost and risk, but could gain if profit recovers [1]; lenders will want evidence that the new market can repay any borrowing [1].
Importance: profit has fallen for three years, so continuing with the old strategy may threaten survival [1], which makes a change of strategy important rather than optional [1].
Judgement: the change is probably necessary, but its success depends on winning medical customers quickly [1]; Vantor could reduce the risk by securing contracts or trial orders before buying all the machinery, and by retraining staff in stages [1]. **[12]**

**9.** Poor communication: staff heard about the changes only a week before launch [1], so they may not understand the loyalty scheme well enough to explain it to guests, and may resist a change they had no say in [1]; guests then receive a poor experience, which works against the aim of repeat visits [1].
Poor sequencing and overload: launching both changes at all five hotels in the same month means any fault in the booking system affects every hotel at once [1]; staff must learn two new systems together, so mistakes are likely [1]; a phased roll-out, starting with one hotel, would have allowed problems to be fixed first [1]. **[6]**

**10. (a)** Option 1: 120,000 ÷ 30,000 = **4 years** [1]. Option 2: 80,000 ÷ 24,000 = **3.33 years** (about 3 years 4 months) [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** Recommendation: Option 2 [1].
Both options meet the £20,000 profit objective on forecast figures [1], so the choice turns on cost, speed of return and risk [1].
Option 2 pays back faster, in about 3.3 years compared with 4 [1], and costs £40,000 less, leaving £70,000 of the £150,000 available as a cushion [1].
Fernhill has strong knowledge of its UK customers, so the forecast is more reliable and the risk lower [1]; Option 1 involves a market Fernhill knows nothing about, with risks from distribution, regulation and exchange rates [1].
Option 1 offers higher profit, £6,000 a year more, so if the export forecast proved accurate it would better meet the objective [1].
Judgement: Option 2 is the better strategy because the forecast is more reliable and the cost lower; the condition is that demand for alcohol-free cider among existing customers is confirmed by research, and Fernhill could consider exporting later once Option 2 is established [1]. **[9]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calling a promotion or a price cut a strategy.
- Evaluating a strategy only on fit with the objective, without testing whether it can be afforded.
- Leaving out the trade-off when recommending an alteration.
- Treating every stakeholder as affected in the same way by a change of strategy.
- Ignoring the case figures when recommending between options.
