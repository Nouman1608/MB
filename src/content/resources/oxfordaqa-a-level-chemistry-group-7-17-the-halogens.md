## 3.2.3.1 Electronegativity

The specification defines electronegativity as an atom's power to attract the electron pair in a
covalent bond. Down Group 7 it **falls**. On the Pauling scale the values are roughly F 4.0, Cl 3.2,
Br 3.0 and I 2.7.

The reason: each element down the group has an extra occupied shell, so the atom is larger and there is
more shielding. The shared pair sits further from a more shielded nucleus, so it is pulled less strongly,
even though the proton number rises.

### Worked example 1: fluorine against bromine

*Explain why fluorine is more electronegative than bromine.*

- A fluorine atom has two shells and bromine four, so in a bond to fluorine the shared pair is closer to
  the nucleus.
- It is shielded only by 1s electrons, so fluorine's nucleus attracts it more strongly.

## 3.2.3.1 Boiling points

| Element | Formula | Look at room temperature | Electrons per molecule | Boiling point / K |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| fluorine | F₂ | pale yellow gas | 18 | 85 |
| chlorine | Cl₂ | pale green gas | 34 | 239 |
| bromine | Br₂ | red-brown liquid | 70 | 332 |
| iodine | I₂ | grey-black solid | 106 | 457 |

All four are simple molecular, made of diatomic molecules. Boiling overcomes only the van der Waals
(induced dipole–dipole) forces between molecules, which get stronger as the number of electrons per
molecule rises. The X–X covalent bond stays intact.

### Worked example 2: a gas and a liquid

*Bromine boils at 332 K and fluorine at 85 K. Explain the difference.*

```
Structure:  both simple molecular, diatomic
Electrons:  F₂ has 18, Br₂ has 70, so Br₂ has stronger van der Waals forces
Energy:     more energy needed to separate Br₂ molecules: higher boiling point
```

## 3.2.3.1 Oxidising ability and displacement

A halogen is an oxidising agent because it takes electrons: X₂ + 2e⁻ → 2X⁻. Its oxidising ability
**decreases** down the group. A larger atom must pull the incoming electron into an outer shell that is
both more distant and more shielded, so the pull on it is weaker.

So a halogen oxidises the halide ions of any halogen **below** it, displacing that halogen from aqueous
solution.

Colours in water can be faint, so shake the mixture with a non-polar solvent such as cyclohexane. The
halogen moves into the upper organic layer: iodine is violet there (brown in water), and bromine gives an
orange layer.

### Worked example 3: naming an unknown halide

*A colourless solution contains one sodium halide, Y. A student adds chlorine water to one portion, then
cyclohexane, and shakes: the organic layer turns orange. With bromine water and cyclohexane,
the organic layer just shows the colour of the bromine added, and no new halogen appears. Identify Y and
write the ionic equation.*

- Chlorine made a new halogen with a bromine-type colour, so Y's ion was oxidised to bromine.
- Bromine water made no new halogen; an iodide would have turned the layer violet.
- **Y is sodium bromide.**

```
Cl₂ + 2Br⁻ → 2Cl⁻ + Br₂
reduction:  Cl₂ + 2e⁻ → 2Cl⁻
oxidation:  2Br⁻ → Br₂ + 2e⁻
```

## 3.2.3.1 Reducing ability of halide ions

A halide ion is a reducing agent because it gives electrons away: 2X⁻ → X₂ + 2e⁻. This power
**increases** from F⁻ to I⁻ (F⁻ < Cl⁻ < Br⁻ < I⁻). In a bigger ion the outer electrons are further out and
more shielded, so one is lost more easily.

Concentrated sulfuric acid with the solid sodium halides shows this. For all four the first step is acid–base,
and the hydrogen halide escapes as steamy fumes:

```
NaX + H₂SO₄ → NaHSO₄ + HX
```

What happens next depends on how strong a reducing agent X⁻ is:

| Solid | Redox step? | What you see | Sulfur product | S oxidation state |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| NaF or NaCl | no | steamy fumes only | none | stays +6 |
| NaBr | yes | steamy fumes; orange-brown vapour (Br₂); a choking gas (SO₂) | SO₂ | +4 |
| NaI | yes, further | steamy fumes; black solid and purple vapour (I₂); yellow solid (S); bad-egg smell (H₂S) | SO₂, S and H₂S | +4, 0 and −2 |

The further sulfur falls from +6, the stronger the reducing agent.

### Worked example 4: one overall equation from the solid

*Write a single equation for solid sodium iodide reacting with concentrated sulfuric acid to give sodium
hydrogensulfate, iodine, hydrogen sulfide and water.*

```
Electron count:  S goes +6 → −2, gaining 8e⁻; each I⁻ → ½I₂ loses 1e⁻, so 8 I⁻ are needed
Redox part:      8I⁻ give 4I₂; 1 H₂SO₄ gives H₂S
Sodium:          8 Na⁺ end up in 8 NaHSO₄, which needs 8 more H₂SO₄
Water:           balance O and H with 4H₂O
Overall:         8NaI + 9H₂SO₄ → 8NaHSO₄ + 4I₂ + H₂S + 4H₂O
```

Check: S 9 = 8 + 1; O 36 = 32 + 4; H 18 = 8 + 2 + 8.

## 3.2.3.1 Identifying halides with silver nitrate

Acidify the sample solution with dilute nitric acid and add aqueous silver nitrate. Chloride, bromide and iodide give precipitates of
different colours; fluoride gives none, because silver fluoride is soluble.

```
Ag⁺(aq) + X⁻(aq) → AgX(s)
```

| Ion | Precipitate | In dilute NH₃(aq) | In concentrated NH₃(aq) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cl⁻ | white AgCl | dissolves | dissolves |
| Br⁻ | cream AgBr | stays | dissolves |
| I⁻ | yellow AgI | stays | stays |

The solubility of the silver halides in ammonia **falls** from AgCl to AgI. The specification asks you to
explain three choices:

- **Silver nitrate** is used because it gives a precipitate with Cl⁻, Br⁻ and I⁻, and the colours differ.
- **It is acidified** (with nitric acid) to remove ions such as carbonate or hydroxide, which would
  otherwise give their own precipitates with silver ions. Hydrochloric acid would add chloride, so it is
  never used for this.
- **Ammonia is added** because white, cream and yellow are hard to tell apart. Solubility in ammonia
  settles it.

### Worked example 5: purity of a bromide

*A 0.600 g sample of impure calcium bromide, CaBr₂, is dissolved. Excess acidified silver nitrate gives
1.034 g of dry AgBr. The impurity contains no halide. Calculate the percentage purity. (Ar: Ag = 107.9,
Br = 79.9, Ca = 40.1)*

```
Mr(AgBr)  = 107.9 + 79.9 = 187.8
n(AgBr)   = 1.034 ÷ 187.8 = 0.005506 mol = n(Br⁻)
n(CaBr₂)  = 0.005506 ÷ 2 = 0.002753 mol      (each CaBr₂ gives two Br⁻)
Mr(CaBr₂) = 40.1 + 2(79.9) = 199.9
mass      = 0.002753 × 199.9 = 0.5503 g
purity    = 0.5503 ÷ 0.600 × 100 = 91.7 %
```

## 3.2.3.2 Chlorine and water

Chlorine reacts reversibly with water, forming chloride ions and chlorate(I) ions:

```
Cl₂ + H₂O ⇌ HClO + HCl      or      Cl₂ + H₂O ⇌ 2H⁺ + ClO⁻ + Cl⁻
```

Chlorine starts at 0 and ends at −1 (Cl⁻) and +1 (ClO⁻). One element both oxidised and reduced is a
**disproportionation**.

In sunlight, chlorine water gives off oxygen and leaves chloride ions:

```
2Cl₂ + 2H₂O → 4H⁺ + 4Cl⁻ + O₂
```

This is not disproportionation: chlorine only goes down (0 to −1), and oxygen goes up (−2 to 0).

### Worked example 6: oxygen from chlorine water

*A flask of chlorine water left in sunlight releases 0.144 g of oxygen. Calculate the mass of chlorine
that reacted. (Mr: O₂ = 32.0, Cl₂ = 71.0)*

```
n(O₂)  = 0.144 ÷ 32.0 = 0.00450 mol
n(Cl₂) = 2 × 0.00450 = 0.00900 mol      (2Cl₂ : 1O₂)
mass   = 0.00900 × 71.0 = 0.639 g
```

### Chlorine in water treatment

Water suppliers dose drinking water with chlorine to kill bacteria and prevent water-borne disease.
Chlorine is toxic, and organic matter in the water can turn some of it into chlorinated organic compounds
such as chloroform. You should appreciate, as the specification puts it, that the health gain outweighs
the toxic effects.

Communities judge the pros and cons of any chemical put into water. Fluoride is one case: added in some
places against tooth decay, but too much causes fluorosis, so it is debated.

## 3.2.3.2 Chlorine and cold, dilute sodium hydroxide

Chlorine bubbled into cold, dilute alkali gives:

```
ionic:  Cl₂ + 2OH⁻ → Cl⁻ + ClO⁻ + H₂O
full:   Cl₂ + 2NaOH → NaClO + NaCl + H₂O
```

Again chlorine disproportionates, from 0 to −1 and +1. The solution of sodium chlorate(I), NaClO, is used as
**bleach** and as a disinfectant for cleaning.

### Worked example 7: making a bleach solution

*How much chlorine is needed to make 500 cm³ of a solution that is 0.640 mol dm⁻³ in NaClO, using excess
cold, dilute NaOH? (Mr: Cl₂ = 71.0)*

```
n(NaClO) = 0.640 × (500 ÷ 1000) = 0.320 mol
n(Cl₂)   = 0.320 mol            (1 Cl₂ : 1 NaClO)
mass     = 0.320 × 71.0 = 22.7 g
```

## Required practical 3: test-tube tests for ions

| Ion | What to do | Positive result |
|---|---|---|
| Group 2 cations | add NaOH(aq); separately add dilute H₂SO₄ | Mg²⁺ gives a white hydroxide precipitate but no sulfate one; Ba²⁺ gives a white sulfate precipitate, with little or no hydroxide one |
| NH₄⁺ | warm with NaOH(aq); test the gas with damp red litmus | litmus turns blue (ammonia) |
| halide ions | dilute HNO₃, then AgNO₃(aq), then NH₃(aq) | as in the silver halide table |
| OH⁻ | red litmus or universal indicator | litmus blue; indicator shows alkali |
| CO₃²⁻ | dilute acid; pass the gas into limewater | fizzing; limewater turns milky |
| SO₄²⁻ | acidified BaCl₂(aq) | white precipitate of BaSO₄ |

## Common errors

- Saying the Br–Br or I–I bond breaks when the element boils.
- Treating the NaCl reaction with sulfuric acid as redox; it is acid–base only.
- Giving H₂S as a product of sodium bromide.
- Forgetting the factor of two when a metal halide holds two halide ions.
- Calling the sunlight reaction disproportionation.
