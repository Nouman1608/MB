## 1. Analysing the product portfolio: the Boston Matrix

A **product portfolio** is the full range of products a business sells. The **Boston Matrix** places each product on two axes: **market growth** (how fast the whole market is expanding) and **market share** (usually measured relative to the largest rival, so a figure above 1 means the product leads its market).

| | High market share | Low market share |
|---|---|---|
| **High market growth** | **Star**: earns well but needs heavy spending to keep up with the market | **Question mark**: uncertain future; needs cash to build share or should be dropped |
| **Low market growth** | **Cash cow**: strong, steady earnings with little investment needed | **Dog**: weak earnings in a static market |

**Significance for marketing planning.** The matrix helps managers decide where marketing money goes:

- **Build** a promising question mark with promotion or price cuts, funded by cash cows.
- **Hold** stars so they become the next cash cows when their market slows.
- **Harvest** cash cows: keep spending low and take the cash.
- **Divest** dogs that drain management time, unless they support other products.

A **balanced portfolio** has enough cash cows to fund the stars and question marks that will earn the future profits.

**Worked example: Wyndle Snacks**

| Product | Market growth (% a year) | Relative market share | Category |
|---|---|---|---|
| Salted Twists | 1.5 | 2.2 | Cash cow |
| Lentil Puffs | 11 | 1.6 | Star |
| Seaweed Crisps | 14 | 0.5 | Question mark |
| Crunch Rods | 2.0 | 0.4 | Dog |

Using 5% growth and a relative share of 1 as the dividing lines, Wyndle has one product in each box. A sensible plan: use the cash from Salted Twists to fund promotion of Seaweed Crisps, keep investing in Lentil Puffs, and review whether Crunch Rods still earns a positive contribution.

**Limitations.** Defining "the market" changes the result; high share does not guarantee high profit; the matrix is a snapshot; and a dog may be worth keeping if customers buy it alongside other lines.

## 2. Assessing performance from the accounts

### Reading and amending the statements

An **income statement** shows performance over a period. A **statement of financial position** shows what the business owns and owes at one date.

**Worked example: Selsworth Kitchenware plc (£000)**

| Income statement | Year 1 | Year 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue | 6,000 | 7,200 |
| Cost of sales | (3,600) | (4,680) |
| **Gross profit** | 2,400 | 2,520 |
| Operating expenses | (1,500) | (1,656) |
| **Operating profit** | 900 | 864 |
| Interest | (100) | (180) |
| Taxes | (160) | (124) |
| **Net profit** | 640 | 560 |

| Statement of financial position (year end) | Year 1 | Year 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Non-current assets | 4,200 | 5,340 |
| Inventory | 500 | 800 |
| Trade receivables | 540 | 846 |
| Cash | 360 | 154 |
| **Current assets** | 1,400 | 1,800 |
| Trade payables | (396) | (702) |
| Short-term borrowing | (304) | (298) |
| **Current liabilities** | (700) | (1,000) |
| Non-current liabilities | (1,000) | (2,000) |
| **Net assets** | 3,900 | 4,140 |
| Share capital | 1,000 | 1,000 |
| Retained earnings | 2,900 | 3,140 |
| **Total equity** | 3,900 | 4,140 |

Net assets always equal total equity. Retained earnings rose by 240: net profit of 560 less dividends of 320. Selsworth has 4 million shares; the opening inventory for Year 1 was 400.

**Amending a statement.** Suppose an audit finds £120,000 of Year 2 inventory is obsolete and must be written off (ignore tax). Inventory falls to 680 and current assets to 1,680. The loss is charged to cost of sales, so gross profit falls to 2,400 and operating profit to 744. Retained earnings and total equity fall by 120 to 4,020, so capital employed falls to 6,020. Recalculate every ratio that uses these lines.

### Ratio analysis

Capital employed = non-current liabilities + total equity: 4,900 in Year 1 and 6,140 in Year 2.

| Ratio | Formula | Year 1 | Year 2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gross profit margin | 100 × gross profit ÷ revenue | 40% | 35% |
| Operating profit margin | 100 × operating profit ÷ revenue | 15% | 12% |
| Net profit margin | 100 × net profit ÷ revenue | 10.67% | 7.78% |
| ROCE | operating profit ÷ capital employed × 100 | 18.37% | 14.07% |
| Current ratio | current assets ÷ current liabilities | 2.0 | 1.8 |
| Acid test ratio | (current assets − inventory) ÷ current liabilities | 1.29 | 1.0 |
| Gearing | non-current liabilities ÷ capital employed × 100 | 20.41% | 32.57% |
| Receivables days | trade receivables ÷ revenue × 365 | 32.9 | 42.9 |
| Payables days | trade payables ÷ cost of sales × 365 | 40.2 | 54.8 |
| Inventory turnover | cost of sales ÷ average inventory | 8.0 times | 7.2 times |
| Dividend per share | total dividends ÷ number of shares | 7.5p | 8p |
| Dividend yield | dividend per share ÷ share price × 100 | 3% (price £2.50) | 5% (price £1.60) |

Year 2 ROCE in full: `864 ÷ (2,000 + 4,140) × 100 = 864 ÷ 6,140 × 100 = 14.07%`. Average inventory for Year 2 is (500 + 800) ÷ 2 = 650, so inventory turnover is 4,680 ÷ 650 = 7.2 times.

**Interpretation.** Revenue grew 20% but cost of sales grew 30%, so every margin fell. ROCE fell because capital employed rose by a quarter, mostly through new long-term borrowing, while operating profit did not rise. Gearing rose from about a fifth to about a third. Liquidity is weaker but still adequate. Customers now take ten days longer to pay; Selsworth is taking longer to pay suppliers, which supports cash but may strain relationships. The higher yield partly reflects a falling share price, which suggests investors are less confident.

After the inventory write-off, ROCE falls further to 744 ÷ 6,020 × 100 = **12.36%** and the current ratio to **1.68**, but the acid test ratio stays at **1.0** because inventory is excluded from it.

### The value of ratio analysis

Ratios turn raw figures into comparable measures, show trends and help owners, lenders and investors judge performance and risk. But a ratio describes rather than explains, and one-off events can distort it.

### Trends, benchmarking and context

- **Trends:** one year's ratio means little; three to five years show direction.
- **Benchmarking:** compare with competitors or the industry average. A current ratio below 1 can be acceptable for a retailer that is paid in cash at once but buys from suppliers on credit.
- **Business environment:** a recession, rising interest rates or a new competitor can explain a fall.
- **Objectives and strategy:** Selsworth's lower ROCE may be the expected first stage of an expansion strategy, with a new factory not yet at full output. A business chasing growth will accept lower margins for a while; one seeking returns for shareholders will not.

### Profit centres

A **profit centre** is a part of a business (a branch, product line or division) for which both revenue and costs are measured, so its profit can be calculated.

Selsworth's Year 2 revenue of 7,200 came from three profit centres:

| £000 | Shops | Online | Trade |
|---|---|---|---|
| Revenue | 3,100 | 2,600 | 1,500 |
| Direct costs | 2,010 | 1,586 | 1,300 |
| Contribution to overheads | 1,090 | 1,014 | 200 |
| Central overheads (20% of revenue) | 620 | 520 | 300 |
| Profit | 470 | 494 | (100) |

**Purpose and value:** shows which parts perform well; motivates managers judged on their own results; guides decisions on where to invest or close.

**Problems:** overheads must be shared out and the method is arbitrary. The trade division "loses" 100 only after overheads; it still contributes 200, so closing it would cut Selsworth's total profit unless those overheads also disappear. Centres may compete rather than cooperate.

### Limitations of financial reporting

- **Historic:** the accounts show what has happened, not what will happen.
- **No non-financial factors:** they miss staff morale, brand reputation and environmental impact, all of which affect future performance.
- A statement of financial position shows one date and may not be typical of the year.

## 3. Analysing business culture

**Business culture** is the shared values, attitudes and ways of behaving in an organisation: "the way we do things here".

**Why it matters:** culture shapes how staff treat customers, how quickly decisions are made, whether people take risks and whether they stay. It can help or block any strategy.

**Factors influencing culture:**

- **leadership and employee values**: what founders and staff believe matters
- **leadership style**: autocratic leaders encourage obedience; democratic leaders encourage involvement
- **mission**: a clear purpose that managers follow
- **reward systems**: bonuses for individual sales encourage competition; team rewards encourage cooperation
- **organisational structure**: tall structures tend to be cautious; flat ones more flexible
- **customer expectations**: customers who expect speed or safety push the culture that way
- **business size**: small firms are often informal; large firms more rule-based

**Positive versus negative culture.** A positive culture (trust, openness, customer focus) raises engagement, productivity and retention. A negative culture (blame, fear, cutting corners) raises labour turnover and the risk of unethical behaviour.

**Strong versus weak culture.** In a **strong** culture most staff share the same values, so behaviour is consistent and little supervision is needed; but a strong culture can resist necessary change. In a **weak** culture values differ across teams, giving inconsistent service but more openness to change.

**Example.** Corrisand Software grew from 12 to 300 staff. Its informal, risk-taking culture helped it launch products fast, but large clients now expect tight security and testing, a reason to change.

**Reasons for changing culture:** a new leader or owner, a merger, poor performance, new customer expectations, a scandal, or a new strategy that the old culture blocks.

**Problems of changing culture:** it takes years; staff resist, especially in strong cultures; key people may leave; new values must be backed by changed rewards, structure and leaders' behaviour or nothing changes.

## Common errors

- Using total assets instead of capital employed in ROCE.
- Leaving inventory in the acid test.
- Using closing inventory when the question gives opening and closing figures.
- Judging a ratio without a trend, benchmark or link to strategy.
- Recommending closure of a profit centre that still makes a positive contribution.
