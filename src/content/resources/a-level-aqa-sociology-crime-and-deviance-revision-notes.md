## Four content areas (3.3.1)

1. **Crime, deviance, social order and social control** — why most people conform most of the time; how deviance is defined and regulated.
2. **Social distribution of crime by ethnicity, gender, social class**, plus recent patterns/trends — who commits, and who is *recorded* as committing, crime.
3. **Globalisation and crime; media and crime; green crime; human rights and state crimes** — crime examined at a global/institutional scale.
4. **Crime control, surveillance, prevention, punishment, victims, the criminal justice system** — how society responds once crime is defined/identified.

## The two core themes — connect every explanation to one

- **Socialisation, culture and identity** — how group norms are learned/transmitted (e.g. subcultural explanations).
- **Social differentiation, power and stratification** — whose behaviour gets defined and processed as criminal in the first place (e.g. class-based explanations).

Stating a crime theory without linking it to one of these themes misses what the specification tests.

## Theory-to-explanation map

| Perspective | Explanation of crime |
|---|---|
| **Consensus-based** | Crime as failure of shared norms, or as a functional safety valve |
| **Conflict-based** | Crime as a product of unequal power; law written/applied to serve some groups over others |
| **Interactionist/labelling** | Crime and deviance socially constructed through labelling, not an inherent property of an act |

Apply this map directly to social-distribution content: e.g. why working-class or ethnic-minority offending might be *recorded* at different rates than it actually occurs — a labelling or conflict lens, not an assumption that official statistics simply describe reality.

## The four content areas are one connected picture, not four topics

Globalisation/crime, green crime and state crime all extend "social order/control" and "social distribution" to a global/institutional scale — the same debate about how crime is defined and by whom applies to a corporation's environmental harm (green crime) or a state's own human rights violations (state crime), not only to an individual offender. Media's role in constructing public understanding of crime connects directly to how crime statistics/social distribution are popularly (mis)understood.

## Linking to Theory and Methods (3.3.2)

Because Crime and Deviance is examined *with* Theory and Methods on the same paper, practise linking an explanation to a methodological point — e.g. how a positivist reliance on official crime statistics might itself produce a distorted picture of social distribution. Always name a **specific data source** (official statistics vs. self-report/victim studies) and state what it can/cannot reliably show, rather than citing a bare statistic as uncontested fact.


## Worked example: applying the theory map to a scenario

A newspaper reports that a particular ethnic-minority group is "over-represented" in official arrest statistics for a certain offence. A weak answer simply repeats the statistic as fact. A strong answer applies the theory map: a **labelling/interactionist** explanation would question whether policing practices (such as disproportionate stop-and-search rates) mean this group is disproportionately *processed* through the criminal justice system relative to actual offending, rather than the statistic reflecting a true difference in underlying behaviour; a **conflict-based** explanation would connect this to unequal power in how laws are enforced across social groups; a **consensus-based** explanation would be the least well-suited lens here, since it tends to explain why *most* people conform, rather than accounting for differential treatment of specific groups within the justice system. Naming which lens fits, and explaining why the others fit less well, is exactly the kind of theoretical evaluation Paper 3 essay questions reward over simply describing the statistic.

## Victims and the criminal justice system — a frequently under-revised content area

The fourth content area (crime control, surveillance, prevention, punishment, victims and the criminal justice system) is sometimes under-revised relative to the more theory-heavy first two areas, but it connects to the same core themes just as directly: victimology's finding that risk of victimisation is unevenly distributed across social groups links to social differentiation and power, while public perceptions of "typical" victims and offenders (often shaped by media coverage) link to socialisation and culture. Treating this fourth area as a genuine content area requiring the same theory-to-theme linking as the first three, rather than a lighter add-on, closes a common gap in otherwise strong revision.

## Exam traps

- Describing a crime theory without connecting it to either core theme (socialisation/culture/identity, or differentiation/power/stratification).
- Treating official crime statistics as simply "the truth" rather than a data source shaped by policing/recording practices.
- Revising green crime/state crime as unrelated add-ons instead of the same theoretical debate applied at institutional/global scale.
- Citing a statistic on social distribution without naming which data source it comes from.
- Forgetting Paper 3 combines Crime and Deviance with Theory and Methods — an answer that never draws on 3.3.2 material misses marks available for synopticity.

## Self-test

1. Name the two core themes running through the crime and deviance content.
2. Give one consensus-based, one conflict-based and one labelling-based explanation of class differences in offending.
3. Why do official statistics and self-report/victim studies sometimes give different pictures of the social distribution of crime?
4. How does green crime extend the same theoretical question used for individual crime?
5. Why should a crime-and-deviance answer also draw on Theory and Methods content?

**Answers:** 1. Socialisation, culture and identity; social differentiation, power and stratification. 2. Consensus: crime as a failure of shared norms or a functional safety valve. Conflict: crime and its enforcement reflecting unequal power. Labelling: apparent class differences partly reflecting differential policing/labelling rather than differences in underlying behaviour. 3. Official statistics only capture crime that is reported and recorded (subject to policing practices/bias); self-report and victim studies capture experiences of crime that may never appear in official records. 4. It applies the same question — how is harmful behaviour defined, and by whom — to environmental damage caused by powerful corporate actors, showing the debate operates at an institutional/global scale too. 5. Because Paper 3 examines Crime and Deviance together with Theory and Methods in one paper, and the specification explicitly expects students to draw together knowledge and skills across the course, not treat the two halves as separate.
