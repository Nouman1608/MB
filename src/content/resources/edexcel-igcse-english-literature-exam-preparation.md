Pearson Edexcel International GCSE English Literature 4ET1 has one compulsory component and a choice
for the second. Every candidate takes **Component 1: Poetry and Modern Prose** -- 2 hours, 90 marks,
60% of the qualification, sat **closed book**, covering the poetry collection and a modern prose text.
For the remaining 40% a centre enters candidates for **either Component 2** (Modern Drama and Literary
Heritage Texts: a 1 hour 30 minute externally assessed **open book** exam, 60 marks -- Section A modern
drama, 30 marks; Section B literary heritage text, 30 marks) **or Component 3**, two coursework
assignments of 30 marks each, internally set and assessed and externally moderated by Pearson. These
notes complement the site's guides to
[Poetry and Modern Prose](/resources/igcse-edexcel-english-literature-poetry-and-modern-prose/) and
[Modern Drama and Literary Heritage Texts](/resources/igcse-edexcel-english-literature-component-2/).

## Closed book on Component 1 changes what revision actually is

Component 1 is sat without the texts. That makes memorised quotation a hard requirement rather than a
refinement, and it makes Component 1 the component where revision method matters most. **Exam-
preparation priority**: build a memorised quotation bank early -- for each poem and for each phase of
the prose text, a small number of short quotations chosen because they can be analysed in several
directions, not because they are the most famous lines.

## Component 2 is open book -- which is not the same as needing less preparation

Having the text available removes the memory burden and raises the expectation of precision: an open
book answer that quotes loosely or ranges vaguely across the play compares badly with one that goes
straight to the right moment. **Exam-preparation priority**: practise *navigating* the text at speed.
Know where the turning points are, and where the two or three passages you would want for any likely
question sit. Time spent hunting through a text in the exam is time not spent writing.

## Short, flexible quotations beat long, impressive ones

A four-word quotation containing an interesting verb can support analysis of imagery, tone and
character; a three-line quotation usually cannot be analysed at all in the time available. **Exam-
preparation priority**: when building the quotation bank, choose short phrases with a single striking
word in them, and rehearse two different points from each. Under a closed book condition this also
halves the memorisation load.

## Poetry answers need comparison technique, not just two separate readings

Where a question invites comparison, the marks come from the comparison itself -- similarities and
differences in method and effect -- rather than from a paragraph on one text followed by a paragraph on
the other. **Exam-preparation priority**: practise integrated comparison, in which each paragraph
addresses a point of comparison and draws on both texts, rather than a two-halves structure.

## On the coursework route, two assignments means two chances to be precise

Component 3 is two assignments of 30 marks each. **Exam-preparation priority**: use the redrafting
time on the argument rather than only on expression -- specifically, on making sure each paragraph
advances a single point about the writer's method and its effect, and that the assignment's actual
question is being answered throughout rather than a general appreciation of the text.

## Worked routine: choosing and using a quotation

The routine below is an original model written for this resource, not a reproduction of any official
past paper, set text or mark scheme.

```
Step 1 - identify the point BEFORE the quotation:
Decide what you are arguing, then find evidence for it. Choosing a
quotation first and reasoning backwards produces vague paragraphs.

Step 2 - keep the quotation short enough to embed:
Fold it into your own sentence rather than dropping it in as a
standalone line.

Step 3 - analyse a specific word, not the quotation as a whole:
Name the word. Say what it connotes. Say why that connotation
matters for the point in step 1.

Step 4 - name the method:
Metaphor, caesura, dramatic irony, structural placement -- the
technical term earns credit when it is tied to the effect.

Step 5 - connect to the whole text or to the question's theme:
One sentence linking this moment to the wider work, so the
paragraph contributes to an argument rather than sitting alone.
```

Step 3 is where the difference between grades usually lies: analysis of a specific word is
demonstrable, while comment on a quotation as a whole tends to restate it.

## Before/during exam checklist

- **Before the exams**: build a memorised bank of short, flexible quotations for Component 1's poetry
  and prose; if sitting Component 2, practise navigating the open-book texts at speed and know where
  the key passages are; practise integrated comparison rather than a two-halves structure.
- **On the coursework route**: redraft for argument and for the actual question set, not only for
  expression.
- **During Component 1**: rely on short embedded quotations and analyse individual words.
- **During Component 2**: go straight to the passage you planned to use rather than searching, and
  do not let having the text tempt you into long quotation.
- **In every paragraph**: point first, then evidence, then a named method, then the effect, then the
  link to the whole text.

## Self-test

1. Which component is closed book, and what does that require of revision?
2. Why does open book not mean less preparation?
3. Why are short quotations more useful than long ones?
4. What makes a comparison answer score well?

**Answers:** 1. Component 1 (Poetry and Modern Prose) -- it requires a memorised bank of quotations
built early, since the texts are not available in the exam. 2. Because having the text raises the
expectation of precision and adds a navigation cost: an answer that hunts for a passage loses writing
time, so knowing where the key moments sit is itself preparation. 3. Because a short phrase with one
striking word can be embedded in a sentence and analysed in several directions, while a long quotation
consumes time and usually cannot be analysed within it. 4. Integrated comparison -- each paragraph
addressing a point of similarity or difference in method and effect and drawing on both texts --
rather than a paragraph on one followed by a paragraph on the other.
