These notes condense section 3.2.3.2 Break-even analysis of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Business (9725) specification, Version 1.1. It is International AS content, examined in Unit 2. The specification wording to keep in mind: "interpret and manipulate break-even charts" and "calculate and interpret break-even output and profit/loss at different output levels". The [break-even study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-business-9625-9725-break-even-analysis-9725/) has the full teaching and longer examples; the [break-even practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-business-9625-9725-break-even-analysis-9725-practice/) test it. Every business named below is invented.

## The nine figures the specification lists

| Term | Meaning | How to calculate |
|---|---|---|
| Fixed costs | Costs that do not change with output in the short run | Given: rent, salaries, insurance, loan interest |
| Variable costs | Costs that change directly with output | Variable cost per unit × units |
| Total costs | All costs at a given output | Fixed costs + variable costs |
| Revenue (turnover) | Money from sales | Price per unit × units sold |
| Profit | What is left after all costs | Revenue − total costs |
| Contribution per unit | What each unit adds towards fixed costs and then profit | Price − variable cost per unit |
| Total contribution | Contribution from all units sold | Contribution per unit × units sold |
| Break-even output | Output where revenue = total costs (no profit, no loss) | Fixed costs ÷ contribution per unit |
| Margin of safety | How far sales can fall before a loss | Actual output − break-even output |

## Three shortcuts that save time

1. **Profit = total contribution − fixed costs.** Same answer as revenue − total costs, fewer steps.
2. **Profit = margin of safety × contribution per unit.** Works because every unit above break-even adds its full contribution to profit.
3. **Output for a target profit = (fixed costs + target profit) ÷ contribution per unit.**

### Quick worked reminder: Penhallow Phone Cases

Price £12, variable cost £4.50 a case, fixed costs £6,000 a month, sales 1,100 cases.

```
Contribution     = 12 − 4.50 = £7.50
Break-even       = 6,000 ÷ 7.50 = 800 cases
Margin of safety = 1,100 − 800 = 300 cases
Profit           = 300 × 7.50 = £2,250
Revenue at break-even = 800 × 12 = £9,600
At 650 cases: loss = (800 − 650) × 7.50 = £1,125
```

## Reading a break-even chart

| You need | Where to look |
|---|---|
| Fixed costs | Where the total-cost line meets the money axis |
| Variable cost per unit | Rise in total costs ÷ rise in output between two points |
| Price | Revenue at any output ÷ that output |
| Break-even output | Where revenue and total-cost lines cross, read down |
| Revenue at break-even | Same crossing point, read across |
| Profit or loss at an output | Vertical gap between the two lines at that output |
| Margin of safety | Horizontal gap from break-even to actual output |

The revenue line always starts at the origin; the total-cost line starts at the level of fixed costs.

## What-if changes (one at a time)

| Change | Line that moves | Break-even | Margin of safety (sales unchanged) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Price up | Revenue steeper | Down | Up |
| Price down | Revenue flatter | Up | Down |
| Variable cost per unit up | Total costs steeper | Up | Down |
| Variable cost per unit down | Total costs flatter | Down | Up |
| Fixed costs up | Fixed and total costs shift up in parallel | Up | Down |
| Fixed costs down | Fixed and total costs shift down in parallel | Down | Up |
| Sales volume up | None | No change | Up |

**Method for any change:** new contribution → new break-even → new margin of safety → new profit → compare with the old figures → say what it means for the business.

## Interpreting the results

- **High fixed costs** mean a high break-even: the business is vulnerable to falling sales.
- **Low contribution per unit** means many sales are needed for each pound of fixed cost.
- **A small margin of safety** means a small drop in sales, such as from a new rival or a recession, would cause losses.
- **A price rise** lowers break-even but only helps if sales do not fall too far. Find the sales level at which the new price gives the same profit as the old one, then judge whether a fall that large is likely.
- **Break-even helps** start-ups plan and support loan applications, and lets managers test decisions before making them.

## Limitations to choose from

- One selling price assumed; discounts and promotions ignored.
- Constant variable cost per unit assumed; bulk-buying discounts ignored.
- All output assumed sold.
- Fixed costs step up when capacity is expanded.
- Hard to use with many products sharing fixed costs.
- Only as accurate as the estimates.

Choose the one that fits the case; a list without context earns little in an evaluation.

## Exam traps

- Dividing fixed costs by price instead of contribution.
- Rounding break-even down. 911.3 units means 912.
- Answering in units when the question asks for revenue at break-even (or the reverse).
- Claiming higher sales lower break-even output.
- Treating a fall in price as always bad: volume may rise enough to raise profit.
- Mixing monthly and annual figures.
- Leaving out "£" or "units" in a final answer.

## Self-test

1. Define contribution per unit.
2. Price £20, variable cost £12 a unit, fixed costs £16,000. Calculate break-even output.
3. The same business sells 2,500 units. Calculate the margin of safety.
4. Use your answer to question 3 to calculate profit without working out revenue.
5. A total-cost line starts at £9,000 and reaches £15,000 at 1,500 units. Calculate variable cost per unit.
6. On a break-even chart, what shows profit at a given output?
7. What happens to the total-cost line when rent increases?
8. What happens to break-even output when sales volume rises?
9. Fixed costs £7,300, contribution per unit £8. Calculate break-even output.
10. Fixed costs £4,800, contribution £6 a unit, target profit £1,800. How many units are needed?
11. Give one reason break-even is less useful for a restaurant with a large menu.
12. A business has a margin of safety of −120 units. What does this mean?

### Answers

1. Selling price per unit minus variable cost per unit: the amount each unit contributes towards fixed costs and then profit.
2. Contribution = 20 − 12 = £8. Break-even = 16,000 ÷ 8 = **2,000 units**.
3. 2,500 − 2,000 = **500 units**.
4. 500 × £8 = **£4,000**.
5. (15,000 − 9,000) ÷ 1,500 = **£4 per unit**.
6. The vertical gap between the revenue line and the total-cost line, where revenue is above total costs.
7. It shifts upwards in parallel (same slope, higher starting point), so break-even rises.
8. **No change**; profit and margin of safety rise instead.
9. 7,300 ÷ 8 = 912.5, so **913 units** (round up).
10. (4,800 + 1,800) ÷ 6 = **1,100 units**.
11. Dishes have different prices and variable costs but share fixed costs, so there is no single contribution per unit to divide by.
12. Output is 120 units **below** break-even, so the business is making a loss of 120 × contribution per unit.
