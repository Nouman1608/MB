These questions cover section 3.13.3, Solar energy, of OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Physics (9630), which is International A-level only content. Learn the content first with the [solar energy study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-physics-9630-solar-energy/) and the [solar energy revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-physics-9630-solar-energy-revision-notes/). Take the power output of the Sun as 3.85 × 10²⁶ W where needed.

## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** State the inverse square law for the intensity of radiation from a point source, and one condition for it to apply. **[2]**

**2.** Explain why the intensity of solar radiation at the Earth's surface is less than just above the atmosphere. **[2]**

**3.** Venus is 1.08 × 10¹¹ m from the Sun. Calculate the intensity of solar radiation there. **[2]**

**4.** A solar cell's maximum power point is at 0.48 V and 2.9 A. Calculate the maximum power and the load resistance needed to draw it. **[2]**

**5.** State the effect on the voltage and on the current of connecting identical cells in series, and of connecting identical strings in parallel. **[2]**

**6.** Explain why shading a single cell in a series string greatly reduces the output of the whole string. **[2]**

### Section B: structured questions

**7.** Near the Earth, 1.50 × 10¹¹ m from the Sun, the solar intensity is 1.36 × 10³ W m⁻². A spacecraft carries solar panels of total area 12 m² and efficiency 25%, always facing the Sun.

**(a)** Calculate the intensity at Mars, 2.28 × 10¹¹ m from the Sun. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the electrical power of the panels at Mars. **[1]**

**(c)** Calculate the distance from the Sun at which the panels deliver 500 W. **[2]**

**8.** A solar cell of area 0.010 m² is lit by an intensity of 800 W m⁻². These readings are taken.

| V / V | 0 | 0.20 | 0.40 | 0.45 | 0.50 | 0.55 | 0.60 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| I / A | 2.40 | 2.38 | 2.30 | 2.20 | 1.95 | 1.40 | 0 |

**(a)** Calculate the power for each reading. **[2]**

**(b)** State the maximum power and calculate the load resistance at which it occurs. **[2]**

**(c)** Calculate the efficiency of the cell at maximum power. **[2]**

**9.** The cell in Question 8 is moved into light of half the intensity.

**(a)** Predict the new short-circuit current. **[1]**

**(b)** Describe how the shape of the V-I characteristic changes. **[2]**

**(c)** Estimate the new maximum power. **[1]**

**10.** Each cell in an array gives 0.50 V and 3.0 A at maximum power. The array must supply at least 36 V and 12 A.

**(a)** Calculate the number of cells needed in each series string. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the number of strings needed in parallel. **[1]**

**(c)** Calculate the total number of cells and the array's maximum power. **[2]**

**(d)** State the effect on the array of one cell becoming shaded. **[1]**

**11.** In Required practical 10 a student measures the resistance R of an LDR at distance d from the filament of a small lamp.

| d / m | 0.25 | 0.35 | 0.45 | 0.55 | 0.65 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| R / kΩ | 1.14 | 2.18 | 3.68 | 5.40 | 7.65 |

**(a)** Show that R is proportional to d². **[2]**

**(b)** Explain what this result shows about light intensity, stating the assumption you make. **[2]**

**(c)** Describe two precautions that improve the accuracy of this investigation. **[2]**

**12.** A household roof panel of area 1.6 m² has an efficiency of 20%. Over the 8 hours of daylight on a typical day, the mean intensity on the panel is 500 W m⁻².

**(a)** Calculate the electrical energy produced by one panel in a day, in J and in kWh. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain why the mean intensity over the day is much lower than the peak intensity. **[2]**

**(c)** The household uses 10 kWh per day. Calculate the number of panels needed on such a day. **[1]**

## Answers

**1.** Intensity is inversely proportional to the square of the distance from the source, I ∝ 1/r² [1]; any one condition: point source radiating equally in all directions, or no absorption between source and detector [1] **[2]**

**2.** The atmosphere absorbs and scatters part of the radiation [1]; when the Sun is not overhead the path through the atmosphere is longer and the beam is spread over a larger area of ground (or cloud blocks it) [1] **[2]**

**3.** I = P/4πr² = 3.85 × 10²⁶/(4π × (1.08 × 10¹¹)²) [1] = 2.6 × 10³ W m⁻² [1] **[2]**

**4.** P = 0.48 × 2.9 = 1.4 W [1]; R = 0.48/2.9 = 0.17 Ω [1] **[2]**

**5.** Series: voltages add, current is that of one cell [1]; parallel strings: currents add, voltage is that of one string [1] **[2]**

**6.** The same current must pass through every cell in series [1]; the shaded cell can produce only a small current, which limits the current, and so the power, of the whole string [1] **[2]**

**7. (a)** I = 1.36 × 10³ × (1.50/2.28)² [1] = 589 W m⁻² [1]

**(b)** 589 × 12 × 0.25 = 1.8 × 10³ W [1]

**(c)** Intensity needed = 500/(12 × 0.25) = 167 W m⁻² [1]; r = 1.50 × 10¹¹ × √(1360/167) = 4.3 × 10¹¹ m [1] **[5]**

**8. (a)** 0, 0.48, 0.92, 0.99 W [1]; 0.98, 0.77, 0 W [1]

**(b)** Maximum power 0.99 W at 0.45 V and 2.20 A [1]; R = 0.45/2.20 = 0.20 Ω [1]

**(c)** Input = 800 × 0.010 = 8.0 W [1]; efficiency = 0.99/8.0 = 12% [1] **[6]**

*Examiner insight:* With data at only a few voltages, the true maximum may lie between readings; quote the largest calculated value.

**9. (a)** 1.20 A, half the original, because the short-circuit current is proportional to intensity [1]

**(b)** The flat part of the curve drops to about half its height [1]; the open-circuit voltage stays almost the same, falling only slightly [1]

**(c)** About half: roughly 0.5 W [1] **[4]**

**10. (a)** 36/0.50 = 72 cells [1]

**(b)** 12/3.0 = 4 strings [1]

**(c)** 72 × 4 = 288 cells [1]; power = 36 × 12 = 432 W [1]

**(d)** The current, and so the output, of the string containing it falls sharply; the other three strings are unaffected [1] **[5]**

**11. (a)** R/d² = 18.2, 17.8, 18.2, 17.9, 18.1 kΩ m⁻² [1]; these are constant to within about 2%, so R ∝ d² [1]

**(b)** Conductance 1/R ∝ 1/d² [1]; assuming the LDR's conductance is proportional to the light intensity, the intensity is proportional to 1/d², the inverse square law [1]

**(c)** Any two: measure distance to the filament, not the bulb casing; darken the room and subtract the background reading with the lamp off; keep the LDR facing the lamp each time; avoid reflections from nearby surfaces; take repeat readings [1] [1] **[6]**

**12. (a)** Energy = 1.6 × 0.20 × 500 × 8 × 3600 = 4.6 × 10⁶ J [1]; = 1.3 kWh [1]

**(b)** Near sunrise and sunset the Sun is low, so its light travels through more atmosphere and is spread over a larger area of panel, giving lower intensity [1]; cloud reduces the intensity at times [1]

**(c)** 10/1.28 = 7.8, so 8 panels [1] **[5]**

*Examiner insight:* Round a number of panels up; 7 panels would not meet the demand.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Not squaring the ratio of distances.
- Reading maximum power at the ends of the V-I curve.
- Swapping the series and parallel rules.
- Concluding the inverse square law from LDR data without stating the assumption.
- Rounding a number of panels or cells down.
