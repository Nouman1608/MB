These questions practise the "Evidence" section of Component 4, the Cambridge Research Report, in Cambridge International AS & A Level Global Perspectives & Research (9239), 2026-2028, where "the research report must be evidence-based" and candidates "should be aware of and explore the strengths and potential weaknesses of all types of evidence". For the component as a whole, see the [Cambridge Research Report study guide](/resources/a-level-global-perspectives-9239-component-4-cambridge-research-report/).

> **These are original questions written for Marlbridge.** The Research Report is coursework, so these questions practise the evidence decisions behind it; they are not past-paper questions. All sources, organisations and data are fictional. Marks are for practice only.

Revise first with the [evidence study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-global-perspectives-9239-evidence/).

## Section A

**1.** Define primary evidence and secondary evidence. **[2]**

**2.** Classify each item as primary or secondary evidence for a project on the question "Did the 1970s oil crisis change household energy use?" **[4]**

- **(i)** A diary kept by a family in 1974 describing how they reduced heating.
- **(ii)** A university textbook chapter summarising the economic effects of the crisis.
- **(iii)** A government table of household fuel consumption for 1972 to 1976.
- **(iv)** A candidate's own interviews with three people who remember the period.

**3.** Name **two** types of primary evidence the syllabus gives as examples. **[2]**

## Section B

**4.** A newspaper article from 1990 about the growth of shopping centres could be primary or secondary evidence. Explain how this depends on the candidate's research question. **[3]**

**5.** Read this description of a source.

> A report by a drinks company reports that 85% of people who tried its new energy drink "felt more alert". The report is based on 120 volunteers recruited in the company's shops and was published by the company's marketing team.

Identify **three** features that a candidate should assess before using this report to argue that energy drinks improve concentration. **[3]**

**6.** A candidate writes: "This source is reliable because it is on a government website." Explain why this is a weak assessment and rewrite it as a stronger one. **[3]**

**7.** Two sources give different figures for the number of trees planted in a country last year: Source X says 12 million and Source Y says 8 million. Explain **three** questions the candidate should ask to decide why they differ. **[3]**

**8.** A candidate's project asks "Should university education be free?" Her evidence so far comes only from articles written by economists who support free education. Explain **two** problems and suggest **two** ways she could improve her evidence. **[4]**

**9.** Using the pattern Claim, Evidence, Assessment, Link, write a short paragraph for a report on "Is nuclear power a good solution to climate change?" using this fictional evidence: "A national statistics office reports that nuclear plants in Country A produced 40% of its electricity in 2024 with low carbon emissions, but the plants took 12 years to build." **[4]**

**10.** A candidate quotes a statistic in the report but lists no reference in the bibliography. Explain **three** consequences for the report. **[3]**

**11.** A candidate conducted a survey of 25 students in her own school about phone use. She writes: "This proves that most teenagers worldwide spend more than four hours a day on their phones." Explain **three** problems with this claim and rewrite it so that it matches her evidence. **[5]**

**12.** "Primary evidence is always better than secondary evidence." Evaluate this claim. **[8]**

---

## Answers

**1.** Primary evidence is first-hand information, such as documents, texts, artefacts, data from observation, interviews and surveys [1]. Secondary evidence is the arguments and opinions of specialists which reflect on or synthesise primary evidence [1]. **[2]**

**2.** (i) primary [1]; (ii) secondary [1]; (iii) primary (data gathered at the time) [1]; (iv) primary (first-hand interviews) [1]. Accept (iii) as primary or as a secondary compilation if the candidate explains how it is used. **[4]**

**3.** Any two: historical documents [1]; literary texts [1]; artefacts [1]; data from scientific observation [1]; interviews [1]; surveys [1]. **[2]**

**4.** If the question is about what happened in shopping centre growth, the article is secondary evidence, because it reports and interprets events [1]. If the question is about how newspapers in 1990 reported or represented shopping centres, the same article is primary evidence, because it is the thing being studied [1]. So the candidate must say how the source is being used [1]. **[3]**

**5.** Any three [3]: the source is produced by a company with an interest in selling the drink [1]; the sample is small (120) and recruited in the company's shops, so it may not represent the population [1]; "felt more alert" is a self-reported feeling, not a measure of concentration [1]; there is no comparison group or control [1]; the purpose is marketing, not research [1]. **[3]**

**6.** It is weak because it states a feature of the website, not an assessment of the source [1]; a government source may still be selective, out of date or produced for a policy purpose [1]. Stronger: "This data comes from the national statistics office and uses a published method, which supports its reliability, but it was last updated in 2021 and so may not show recent changes." [1] **[3]**

**7.** Any three [3]: Did they count the same thing, for example trees planted or trees surviving [1]? Did they use the same period and boundaries [1]? Who produced each figure, and for what purpose [1]? How was the number collected [1]? Is one an estimate and the other an official count [1]? **[3]**

**8.** Problems, one mark each [2]: all the sources share the same view, so the evidence is one-sided and cannot support a balanced judgement [1]; they come from one type of expert, so other kinds of evidence, such as student experience, are missing [1]. Improvements, one mark each [2]: add sources by economists or policymakers who oppose free university education [1]; add other evidence types, such as data on graduate earnings or a survey of students [1]. **[4]**

**9.** For example: "Supporters argue that nuclear power cuts emissions [Claim, 1]. A national statistics office reports that nuclear plants in Country A produced 40% of its electricity in 2024 with low carbon emissions [Evidence, 1]. This is official data from one country, so it shows what is possible but not that it applies everywhere [Assessment, 1]. However, the same source notes that the plants took 12 years to build, which suggests nuclear power may be too slow to meet urgent targets [Link, 1]." **[4]**

**10.** Any three [3]: the report loses credit for complete and appropriate citation and referencing, which is part of the AO3 criterion [1]; the reader cannot check the evidence, so the claim is harder to trust [1]; it could be seen as presenting someone else's information without acknowledging it, which raises concerns about academic honesty [1]. **[3]**

**11.** Problems, any three [3]: the sample is only 25 students, so it is too small [1]; the students are from one school, so they are not representative of teenagers worldwide [1]; "proves" is too strong for survey evidence [1]; "more than four hours" may not match what she asked, and reported time can differ from actual use [1]. Rewrite, for example: "In my survey of 25 students at one school, most reported using their phones for more than four hours a day. This is a small sample, so it suggests but does not prove a wider pattern." [2] **[5]**

**12.** Indicative content, up to 8 marks: up to 2 for the case for the claim, up to 4 for the case against, and 2 for a justified judgement.

For: primary evidence is first-hand and close to the original situation [1]; the candidate can design what to collect and test claims directly [1].

Against: primary evidence can have small samples or a narrow scope [1]; a candidate's own interviews or survey may be biased by how they are done [1]; secondary evidence can offer expert synthesis across many studies, which a candidate cannot collect alone [1]; some questions, such as long-term trends or past events, can only be examined through secondary sources [1].

Judgement, for example: neither is always better; the best evidence depends on the question and on how well each source is assessed, and a strong report uses both and explains their strengths and limits [2]. **[8]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- **Question 2**: classifying by the type of source rather than by how it is used.
- **Question 5**: saying "the company is biased" without naming the feature that shows it.
- **Question 6**: treating an official website as automatically reliable.
- **Question 11**: not matching the strength of the claim to the size of the sample.
- **Question 12**: asserting "primary is better" without a reason or a counter-case.
