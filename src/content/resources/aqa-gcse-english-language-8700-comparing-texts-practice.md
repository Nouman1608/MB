## Section A — Short questions

**1.** Explain the difference between a writer's **viewpoint** and a writer's **perspective**, with an example. **[3]**

**2.** A student's comparison paragraph begins: *"Source A talks about the city. Source B also talks about the city."* Explain why this is a weak opening and rewrite it as a comparative judgement. **[3]**

**3.** Read the two original sources.

> **Source A (letter in a 19th-century style):** *"The new railway is a marvel. In four hours I was carried a distance
> my grandfather took three days to travel, and I arrived fresh enough to dine with my hosts."*
>
> **Source B (modern blog):** *"My train was cancelled, the replacement bus was full, and I spent the evening on a
> platform bench eating crisps for dinner."*

State **one** difference in the writers' perspectives on rail travel and support it with a quotation from each. **[3]**

**4.** Using the sources in Question 3, compare **one** method each writer uses. **[4]**

**5.** Give **two** ways in which the date of a 19th-century source might explain a difference in perspective from a modern source. **[2]**

**6.** Sort these connectives into those that show **difference** and those that show **similarity**: *whereas, likewise, in contrast, equally, unlike, both*. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** A viewpoint is the specific position a writer takes on an issue [1]; a perspective is the wider way of seeing that produces it, shaped by experience, time and values [1]. Example: two writers may both hold the viewpoint that a factory should close, one from the perspective of a nearby resident worried about health and the other from that of a former worker who feels betrayed [1].

**2.** It describes the shared topic without making any comparison [1], and "also" adds the second source rather than weighing it against the first [1]. A rewrite, for example: "While Source A presents the city as a place of opportunity, Source B sees it as lonely and hostile" [1].

**3.** A clear difference in perspective, for example that Source A is impressed by rail travel while Source B is frustrated by it [1]; a supporting quotation from Source A, such as "a marvel" or "arrived fresh" [1]; a supporting quotation from Source B, such as "cancelled" or "eating crisps for dinner" [1].

**4.** Source A's method with evidence, for example the comparison with "my grandfather took three days", which measures progress across generations [1], and its effect: the reader is made to feel the scale of change [1]. Source B's method with evidence, for example a list of three mishaps ("cancelled... full... crisps") [1], and its effect: a comic, deflating build-up of frustration, linked by a comparative connective to Source A [1].

**5.** Any two of the following, one mark each (2 × [1]): the railway or other technology was new in that period, so wonder at it was natural; the writer may assume a different audience, such as one correspondent rather than a public readership; social assumptions, for example about class, work or duty, may go unquestioned in the older text.

**6.** Difference: whereas, in contrast, unlike. Similarity: likewise, equally, both. One mark for every two connectives placed correctly, so all six correct earns 3 marks (3 × [1]).

**7.** Two marks for each of four aspects (one for apt notes on each source), up to 8. For example:
- overall perspective: A accepts pollution as the unavoidable price of work, B rejects it as a choice [2];
- tone: A resigned but quietly suffering, B urgent and confident [2];
- key method: A uses personal sensory detail ("thick and yellow", "the baby coughs"), B uses direct address and a rhetorical challenge ("Ask any parent") [2];
- ending: A ends on private suffering, B on a short, absolute claim ("It is a right") [2].
Other valid aspects, such as use of evidence or voice, are credited.

**8.** Award up to 10 marks:
- a comparative judgement of perspectives opening the answer, for example A's resigned acceptance against B's demand for change [2];
- Source A method with apt quotation and effect, for example the repeated "price of" showing pollution is accepted as a trade-off [2];
- Source B method with apt quotation and effect, for example the choice framed in two balanced options ("We can... or we can..."), making the answer seem obvious [2];
- second paragraph comparing personal and emotive evidence: A's private details ("my chest aches") against B's appeal to parents and inhalers [2];
- comparative connectives used throughout and a brief, relevant link to context, for example A writing when industry was seen as the source of livelihood [2].

**9.** A similarity: both writers show pollution harming children's health [1]. Source A presents it through a private, understated detail, "the baby coughs all night", conveying helpless suffering [1]; Source B presents it as public evidence in an argument, "any parent whose child uses an inhaler" [1], using it to pressure readers into agreeing [1].

**10.** Source A ends with "Still, my chest aches, and the baby coughs all night" [1], where "Still" quietly questions the mother's acceptance, suggesting private doubt [1]. Source B ends with the short declaratives "Clean air is not a luxury. It is a right" [1], revealing a confident, public perspective that sees clean air as an entitlement worth demanding [1].

**11.** The skill assessed is comparison, so separate paragraphs mostly describe rather than compare [1]; the final sentence is a bare assertion with no comparison of perspectives or methods [1]. Fix: plan aspects and write integrated paragraphs that discuss both sources on each aspect, using comparative connectives [1].

**12.** A diary is private, so the writer of Source A records feelings without trying to persuade anyone [1], and doubts emerge indirectly, through a detail such as "Still" [1]. An opinion article is public and aims to change readers' minds [1], so the writer of Source B uses direct address, a clear structure and a forceful ending to persuade [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Comparing only what the sources say, not how they say it.
- Analysing methods with no link to the writers' perspectives.
- Writing two separate commentaries.
- Ignoring the endings and structure of the sources.
- Using "also" and "and" in place of comparative connectives.
