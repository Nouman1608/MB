## Questions

### Section A

**1.** A fall in the price of tea, other things equal, causes: **[1]**

A: A rightward shift of the demand curve for tea.

B: An extension of demand for tea.

C: A leftward shift of the supply curve for tea.

D: A contraction of demand for tea.

**2.** Which of the following would shift the supply curve of bread to the left? **[1]**

A: A fall in the price of bread.

B: A rise in consumers' incomes.

C: A rise in the price of wheat.

D: A new, faster baking machine.

**3.** Printers and ink cartridges are complements. A rise in the price of printers will most likely: **[1]**

A: Increase demand for ink cartridges.

B: Decrease demand for ink cartridges.

C: Increase the supply of ink cartridges.

D: Have no effect on the market for ink cartridges.

**4.** Which pair of goods is in joint supply? **[1]**

A: Tea and coffee.

B: Cars and petrol.

C: Lamb and wool.

D: Wheat and barley.

**5.** In a market, Qd = 240 - 8P and Qs = 12P - 60. Calculate the equilibrium price and quantity. **[2]**

**6.** Using your answer to question 5, calculate consumer surplus and producer surplus. **[2]**

### Section B

**7.** The market for coffee beans in Country R is Qd = 1,000 - 50P and Qs = 150P - 200, where P is the price in pounds per kilogram and Q is in thousand kilograms.

**(a)** Calculate the equilibrium price and quantity. **[2]**

**(b)** A frost reduces supply, so the supply equation becomes Qs = 150P - 500. Calculate the shortage at the original price and the new equilibrium price and quantity. **[3]**

**(c)** Calculate the change in consumer surplus caused by the frost. **[2]**

**8.** Using the coffee example in question 7, explain the likely effects of the rise in the price of coffee on the market for tea and on the market for coffee machines. **[4]**

**9.** Battery costs fall at the same time as petrol prices rise. Explain, with the aid of a diagram, the effects on the market for electric cars. **[6]**

**10.** Explain what is meant by derived demand, and how a fall in demand for steel would affect the market for iron ore. **[4]**

**11.** Explain how a surplus in the market for wheat would be removed. **[3]**

**12.** Evaluate the usefulness of the supply and demand model for predicting changes in the prices of farm products. **[10]**

**13.** The table shows the weekly demand for loaves of bread from three households in a small invented village, and the market supply.

| Price (£) | Household 1 | Household 2 | Household 3 | Market supply |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 5 | 4 | 6 | 6 |
| 2 | 3 | 3 | 4 | 10 |
| 3 | 2 | 1 | 3 | 14 |

**(a)** Calculate market demand at each price. **[1]**

**(b)** Identify the equilibrium price and explain why the market would not stay at a price of £1. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain why the ceteris paribus assumption is needed when the market demand curve is drawn from this table. **[2]**

**14.** A cut in the number of trains reduces the supply of rail travel, and the price of commuter tickets rises. Evaluate the impact of the price rise on consumer and producer surplus. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** **B** [1]. A change in the good's own price causes a movement along the curve. **[1]**

**2.** **C** [1]. Wheat is an input, so its price raises costs. **[1]**

**3.** **B** [1]. Fewer printers are bought, so less ink is needed. **[1]**

**4.** **C** [1]. Raising sheep produces both. **[1]**

**5.** 240 - 8P = 12P - 60, so 300 = 20P and **P = 15** [1]. Q = 240 - 120 = **120** [1]. **[2]**

**6.** Demand meets the price axis at 30, so consumer surplus = 0.5 x (30 - 15) x 120 = **900** [1]. Supply meets it at 5, so producer surplus = 0.5 x (15 - 5) x 120 = **600** [1]. **[2]**

**7. (a)** 1,000 - 50P = 150P - 200, so P = **£6** [1] and Q = **700 thousand kg** [1].

**(b)** At £6, Qs = 900 - 500 = 400, so the shortage is 700 - 400 = **300 thousand kg** [1]. New equilibrium: 1,000 - 50P = 150P - 500, so P = **£7.50** [1] and Q = **625 thousand kg** [1].

**(c)** Demand meets the price axis at £20. Before: 0.5 x 14 x 700 = £4,900 thousand; after: 0.5 x 12.50 x 625 = £3,906.25 thousand [1]. Consumer surplus falls by **£993.75 thousand** [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* Find the demand intercept once and reuse it; it does not change when supply shifts.

**8.** Tea is a substitute for coffee (competitive demand) [1], so the higher coffee price shifts demand for tea to the right, raising the price and quantity of tea [1]. Coffee machines are complements to coffee (joint demand) [1], so demand for them shifts left, lowering their price and quantity sold [1]. **[4]**

**9.** Lower battery costs reduce the cost of producing electric cars, so supply shifts right [1]. Higher petrol prices make petrol cars, a substitute, more costly to run, so demand for electric cars shifts right [1]. Both shifts raise the quantity of electric cars bought and sold [1]. The supply shift lowers price while the demand shift raises it, so the change in price depends on which shift is larger [1]. Diagram: axes labelled price and quantity; supply shifting from S1 to S2 and demand from D1 to D2 [1]; new equilibrium at a clearly higher quantity, with price shown as higher, lower or unchanged depending on the shifts drawn and that uncertainty stated [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* With two shifts, say which variable is certain and which is not; that is the analysis the question rewards.

**10.** Derived demand is demand for an input that comes from demand for the final good it helps produce [1]. Iron ore is used to make steel, so a fall in demand for steel reduces the quantity of iron ore steelmakers want [1]. Demand for iron ore shifts left [1], so its price and the quantity traded fall, and demand for mine workers may fall too [1]. **[4]**

**11.** At a price above equilibrium, farmers cannot sell all their wheat [1]. They cut prices to clear stocks [1], which extends demand and contracts supply until quantity demanded equals quantity supplied at a lower price [1]. **[3]**

**12.** Useful: the model predicts the direction of price changes after a shock, such as a poor harvest shifting supply left and raising price [1]; with estimates of the curves, it can show the size of changes, as in question 7 [1]; it explains why farm prices are volatile, because supply shifts with weather while demand for food is fairly stable [1]; and it traces effects on related markets such as substitutes and inputs [1]. Limits: it assumes other things are equal, but many factors change at once [1]; the curves are hard to estimate accurately [1]; farm supply responds with long lags because crops take a season to grow, so the market may not reach equilibrium quickly [1]; government schemes such as price support or buffer stocks override market prices [1]. Judgement: the model is very good at predicting the direction of change and explaining volatility [1], but less reliable for exact prices, so it works best combined with data and knowledge of the specific market [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* Weigh what the model predicts well (direction) against what it predicts poorly (exact size and timing).

**13. (a)** Adding the quantities at each price: **15** at £1, **10** at £2 and **6** at £3 [1].

**(b)** Equilibrium is **£2**, where market demand and market supply are both 10 [1]. At £1 there is a shortage of 15 − 6 = 9 loaves, so buyers bid the price up, supply extends and demand contracts until the market reaches £2 [1].

**(c)** The table holds incomes, the prices of other goods, tastes and the number of households constant, so it shows the effect of price alone [1]. If any of these changed, the quantities in the table would change and the market demand curve would shift rather than stay as drawn [1]. **[5]**

**14.** Award up to 6 marks.
- Commuters pay more on every ticket they still buy, so part of consumer surplus is transferred to the rail operator [1].
- Fewer journeys are made, so the surplus on the lost journeys disappears for both sides, and consumer surplus clearly falls [1].
- Producer surplus may rise or fall: the operator gains from the higher price but loses on the journeys no longer sold [1].
- Commuter demand is price inelastic in the short run, as there are few substitutes for getting to work, so few journeys are lost and most of the effect is a transfer from commuters to the operator [1].
- Surplus is a limited measure: it does not show that the loss falls hardest on low-income commuters, and it leaves out external effects such as more traffic if some commuters switch to driving [1].
- Judgement: in the short run commuters bear most of the loss, but demand becomes more elastic over time as people move home, change jobs or work from home, so the transfer to the operator shrinks in the long run [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Separate the transfer on units still bought from the surplus lost on units no longer bought, then use elasticity to say which is larger.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Shifting the curve for a change in the good's own price.
- Forgetting to find the price-axis intercept for surplus.
- Not stating which variable is uncertain when both curves shift.
- Mixing up complements and substitutes.
- Unlabelled diagrams.
- Ending the essay without a judgement.
