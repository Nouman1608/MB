## Section A: comparison skills (original poems)

Read both poems, then answer questions 1 to 6.

> **My Father's Coat**
>
> It still hangs by the door, a shape of him  
> the hook has kept. I lift the sleeve  
> and smell the bus, the rain, the cigarettes  
> he swore he'd stopped. I put it back. I leave.

> **Lesson**
>
> My mother taught me how to ride by letting go.  
> She ran beside me, one hand on the seat,  
> then none. I didn't know. I didn't know  
> until I turned and saw her, small, complete,  
> applauding in the road, and wobbled, and stayed up.
>
> *(Both poems are original, written for this practice page.)*

**1.** State one similarity in subject between the two poems. **[1]**

**2.** Identify one difference between the speakers' feelings about their parent. **[2]**

**3.** Compare how the two poets use the final line of each poem. **[4]**

**4.** Rewrite the following as a single comparative sentence that names a method and an effect in
each poem: "'My Father's Coat' uses smell. 'Lesson' uses repetition." **[3]**

**5.** Compare how the two poets use rhyme. **[4]**

**6.** Write a two-sentence comparative overview for the question "Compare how the poets present a
parent in 'My Father's Coat' and 'Lesson'." **[3]**

## Section B: anthology essay questions

Answer each in about 40 minutes, using your copy of the anthology.

**7.** Compare how the poets present childhood experience in *Half-past Two* and *Hide and Seek*. **[30]**

**8.** Compare how the poets present a relationship between a parent and child in *Do not go gentle
into that good night* and **one** other poem from the anthology. **[30]**

**9.** Compare how the poets present power in a relationship in *My Last Duchess* and *La Belle Dame
sans Merci*. **[30]**

## Answers

### Section A

**1.** Any one: both are about a parent; both are memories; both centre on a physical object or
action (a coat, a bicycle) (1).

**2.** "My Father's Coat" suggests grief, or unease, at a parent who is absent (1); "Lesson" is
proud and joyful about a parent's support and the speaker's independence (1).

**3.** Up to 2 marks for each poem, credited for comparison. "My Father's Coat" ends with two short,
end-stopped sentences ("I put it back. I leave."), an abrupt ending that suggests the speaker
cannot bear to stay with the memory (2). In contrast, "Lesson" ends on a longer, overflowing line,
the only one to break the rhyme pattern, whose list of verbs ("wobbled, and stayed up") enacts
balance being found, a hopeful ending (2).

**4.** For example: "Whereas the list of smells in 'My Father's Coat' brings the father's habits
back with painful vividness, the repetition of 'I didn't know' in 'Lesson' recreates the child's
innocent confidence, so one poem dwells on loss and the other on growth." 1 mark for each method
with its effect; 1 for a valid comparative connective linking them.

**5.** "My Father's Coat" rhymes only "sleeve" and "leave" (1), so the rhyme links touching the coat
with walking away, stressing the speaker's withdrawal (1). "Lesson" uses alternating rhyme
("go/know", "seat/complete") (1), which gives a steady rhythm like a balanced ride until the
unrhymed last line breaks free, as the child does (1).

**6.** For example: "Both poets present a parent through a vivid, physical memory, but the father in
'My Father's Coat' is present only as an absence, while the mother in 'Lesson' is present in her
act of letting go. As a result, the first poem feels like mourning and the second like
celebration." 1 for a shared idea, 1 for a difference, 1 for a clear statement of effect.

### Section B: how to mark questions 7 to 9

Each essay is marked holistically across AO2 and AO3. The bands below are Marlbridge's illustrative
guidance, not Pearson's published mark scheme.

| Marks | Typical features |
|---|---|
| 25-30 | Perceptive, sustained comparison throughout; precise analysis of language, form and structure in both poems; judicious short quotations |
| 19-24 | Thorough comparison of ideas and methods; clear analysis of effects in both poems |
| 13-18 | Clear comparison, sometimes of content more than methods; explanation of effects |
| 7-12 | Some comparison, often at the end; some comment on methods |
| 1-6 | Largely separate description of each poem; little comparison |

**7. Indicative content:**

- Both poems present a child's limited understanding through an adult poem, but *Half-past Two*
  uses a third-person narrator looking back, while *Hide and Seek* uses a second-person "you" in the
  present tense, placing the reader inside the game.
- Fanthorpe presents time from the child's point of view: the child knows times only by events, and
  invented compound words for them show his logic. Scannell presents time through sensory detail
  (cold, the smell of the shed) that makes the waiting feel long.
- Both feature adults or others who forget the child: the teacher in *Half-past Two*, the seekers who
  give up in *Hide and Seek*. The comparison lies in tone: Fanthorpe's is gently comic and ends with a
  sense of timeless escape; Scannell's moves from excitement to isolation and unease.
- Structure: both poems turn on the moment the child realises something; candidates may compare how
  each ending leaves the child, and the reader, suspended.

**8. Indicative content (any valid second poem; *Piano* and *Poem at Thirty-Nine* are shown):**

- *Do not go gentle into that good night*: a son pleads with a dying father to resist death. The
  villanelle's two refrains repeat insistently, so form mirrors the speaker's urgency and inability to
  accept loss; the examples of different kinds of men build an argument before the final, personal
  address to the father.
- With *Piano*: Lawrence's adult speaker is overwhelmed by memories of his mother as music returns him
  to childhood. Where Thomas's speaker fights against the present loss, Lawrence's gives in to the
  past; the regular rhyming couplets of *Piano* contrast with the villanelle's tighter pattern, and
  both create a sense of feeling barely held in check.
- With *Poem at Thirty-Nine*: Walker's speaker admires and misses her father, recognising herself in
  him, in short free-verse lines; the tone of reflective acceptance contrasts with Thomas's anger.
- Strong answers compare the speaker's role (pleading, remembering, admiring) and how form and
  repetition express each one.

**9. Indicative content:**

- *My Last Duchess* is a dramatic monologue in which the Duke controls the conversation, the portrait
  and, by implication, his late wife's fate. His enjambed rhyming couplets give a controlled surface
  while the run-on lines suggest his self-revealing flow of speech; the reader judges him by what he
  lets slip.
- *La Belle Dame sans Merci* is a ballad in which power seems to lie with the mysterious lady: the
  knight is left alone, pale and drained. The bleak natural imagery and the short final line of each
  stanza create an atmosphere of loss; the poem's circular structure returns to the opening setting,
  showing he cannot escape.
- Comparison: in one poem a man exerts power over a woman, in the other a man appears to fall under a
  woman's power, though both poems are told from a male viewpoint and the women never speak for
  themselves. Credit answers that explore this shared silence and differing voices.
- Form: the monologue's single voice versus the ballad's question and answer; both use regular form
  in tension with disturbing content.
