## Questions

**1.** Describe how London forces arise between two molecules of nitrogen, N₂. **[3]**

**2.** State whether each substance forms hydrogen bonds between its own molecules. Give a reason each time.

**(a)** Methylamine, CH₃NH₂ **[1]**
**(b)** Trimethylamine, (CH₃)₃N **[1]**
**(c)** Ethanoic acid, CH₃COOH **[1]**
**(d)** Chloromethane, CH₃Cl **[1]**

**3.** This question is about liquid hydrogen fluoride.

**(a)** Describe, or sketch, a hydrogen bond between two HF molecules. Show partial charges and the lone pair
involved. **[2]**
**(b)** State the F-H···F bond angle and explain why it takes this value. **[2]**

**4.** Boiling temperatures: H₂O 373 K, HF 293 K, NH₃ 240 K. Fluorine is the most electronegative of the three
elements. Explain why water, not hydrogen fluoride, has the highest boiling temperature. **[4]**

**5.** Three isomers of C₅H₁₂ have these boiling temperatures: pentane 309 K, 2-methylbutane 301 K,
2,2-dimethylpropane 283 K.

**(a)** How many electrons does each isomer molecule contain? **[1]**
**(b)** Explain the order of boiling temperatures. **[3]**

**6.** Butan-1-ol, CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂OH, boils at 391 K. Pentane boils at 309 K. Explain why butan-1-ol is much less
volatile than pentane. **[4]**

**7.** Use these boiling temperatures: HCl 188 K, HBr 206 K, HI 238 K.

**(a)** Explain the trend from HCl to HI. **[3]**
**(b)** Hydrogen fluoride boils at 293 K. Explain why this is much higher than the value predicted from the HCl to
HI trend. **[2]**

**8.** A block of ice of volume 2.40 m³ floats in a freshwater lake. Densities: ice 0.917 g cm⁻³, liquid water
1.00 g cm⁻³. The fraction of a floating object below the surface equals its density divided by the density of
the liquid.

**(a)** Calculate the volume of the block above the water surface. **[2]**
**(b)** Use ideas about hydrogen bonding to account for the block floating. **[3]**

**9.** A student shakes three liquids separately with water.

**(a)** 1-chlorobutane forms a separate layer, even though its molecules are polar. Explain this. **[3]**
**(b)** Propan-1-ol mixes completely with water. Explain this. **[2]**
**(c)** Suggest a better solvent than water for iodine, I₂, and explain your choice. **[2]**

**10.** Boiling temperatures of the Group 15 hydrides: NH₃ 240 K, PH₃ 185 K, AsH₃ 211 K, SbH₃ 256 K.

**(a)** Explain the trend from PH₃ to SbH₃. **[2]**
**(b)** From PH₃ to SbH₃ the boiling temperature rises by about 2 K for each extra electron. Use this to estimate
the boiling temperature NH₃ would have without hydrogen bonding. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why the actual boiling temperature of NH₃ is higher than your estimate, and why each NH₃ molecule
forms fewer hydrogen bonds than each H₂O molecule. **[3]**

**11.** A 60.0 W heating coil keeps water boiling in an open vacuum flask that stands on a balance. Over 900 s
the balance reading drops by 22.6 g.

**(a)** Calculate the enthalpy change of vaporisation of water, in kJ mol⁻¹. **[4]**
**(b)** Use this value for the true enthalpy change of vaporisation: +40.7 kJ mol⁻¹. Suggest why the
experimental value is higher. **[1]**
**(c)** State which forces are overcome when water boils, and which bonds are not broken. **[2]**

**12.** A student stirs 4.00 g of ammonium nitrate, NH₄NO₃, into 40.0 g of water in a polystyrene cup. The
thermometer reading drops by 7.3 K. Use c = 4.18 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹ and treat the 40.0 g of water
as the mass heated.

**(a)** Calculate the enthalpy change of solution of ammonium nitrate. Give the sign. **[4]**
**(b)** Describe how water molecules interact with the ions as the solid dissolves. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why ammonium nitrate does not dissolve in hexane. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** Electrons move randomly, so at one instant the electron cloud of one N₂ molecule is uneven, creating an
**instantaneous dipole** [1]. This dipole distorts the electron cloud of a neighbouring molecule, creating an
**induced dipole** [1]. The δ+ end of one dipole attracts the δ− end of the other [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Use both words, "instantaneous" and "induced"; "temporary charges" alone is too vague.

**2. (a)** **Yes**: H is bonded directly to N, and N has a lone pair [1].
**(b)** **No**: N has a lone pair, but every H is bonded to carbon [1].
**(c)** **Yes**: the O-H group provides δ+ H, and O atoms have lone pairs [1].
**(d)** **No**: polar, but no H bonded to N, O or F [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* "No" or "yes" alone is not enough when a reason is asked for; point to the specific bond.

**3. (a)** F-H···F-H, with δ+ on H and δ− on F in each molecule [1]; the hydrogen bond drawn from the H of one
molecule to a **lone pair** on F of the other [1].
**(b)** **About 180°** [1]. The two electron-rich F atoms repel, so they sit as far apart as possible, in line
with H [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Draw the hydrogen bond as a dashed or dotted line ending at a lone pair, not at the F nucleus
in general.

**4.** All three form hydrogen bonds [1]. Each H₂O molecule has two δ+ H atoms and two lone pairs, so on average it
forms **twice as many hydrogen bonds per molecule** as HF [1]. HF has three lone pairs but only one H,
so it forms fewer hydrogen bonds, even though each one is stronger [1]. More hydrogen bonds per molecule means
more energy is needed to separate water molecules [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* The key idea is the number of hydrogen bonds; electronegativity alone predicts the wrong order.

**5. (a)** **42** electrons in each (5 × 6 + 12 × 1) [1].
**(b)** Only London forces act between alkane molecules [1]. The more branched the molecule, the more compact and
spherical it is, so the **surface contact** between neighbouring molecules is smaller [1]. Weaker London forces
need less energy to overcome, so boiling temperature falls: pentane > 2-methylbutane > 2,2-dimethylpropane [1].
**[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Because the electron count is the same, any answer based on "fewer electrons" or "smaller
mass" earns nothing here.

**6.** Both molecules have **42 electrons** [1], so their London forces are similar [1]. Butan-1-ol also has an O-H
group, so it forms **hydrogen bonds** between molecules [1]. Extra energy is needed to overcome these, so
butan-1-ol has a higher boiling temperature and lower volatility [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* State the equal electron count explicitly; it is what makes the comparison fair.

**7. (a)** The number of electrons increases (18, 36, 54) [1], so London forces get stronger [1]. The bonds become
less polar, so permanent dipole interactions weaken, but the increase in London forces is larger, so boiling
temperature rises [1].
**(b)** HF forms **hydrogen bonds** (H bonded to F, which has lone pairs) [1]; breaking these
takes far more energy than overcoming the London and dipole attractions in HCl [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Mention that dipoles weaken down the group, then say London forces outweigh this.

**8. (a)** Fraction below = 0.917 / 1.00 = 0.917, so fraction above = 0.083 [1]. Volume above = 2.40 × 0.083
= **0.199 m³** [1].
**(b)** In ice each molecule forms **four hydrogen bonds, arranged tetrahedrally** [1]. This holds the molecules
in an **open structure** with empty space between them [1]. On melting, some hydrogen bonds break and the
structure partly collapses, so molecules pack closer together in the liquid [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Give the unit, and describe the open arrangement; "more hydrogen bonds" alone explains nothing.

**9. (a)** 1-chlorobutane cannot form hydrogen bonds with water (no H on N, O or F) [1]. To mix, water molecules
would have to break hydrogen bonds with each other [1], and only weaker permanent dipole and London attractions
would form with 1-chlorobutane, so too little energy is released and the liquids separate [1].
**(b)** The O-H group of propan-1-ol forms **hydrogen bonds with water molecules** [1]; these replace the hydrogen
bonds broken in each pure liquid [1].
**(c)** **Hexane** (or cyclohexane) [1]: iodine and hexane both have only London forces, so similar attractions
form between solute and solvent [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* In (a), "polar" is a trap; explain the failure to hydrogen bond, not "non-polar".

**10. (a)** Electron number increases from PH₃ to SbH₃ [1], so London forces strengthen and boiling demands
more energy [1].
**(b)** NH₃ has 10 electrons, 8 fewer than PH₃ [1]. Estimate = 185 − 8 × 2 ≈ **169 K** (about 170 K) [1].
**(c)** NH₃ forms **hydrogen bonds** (H on N, lone pair on N), which need extra energy to overcome, so it boils
about 70 K above the estimate [1]. Each NH₃ has three H atoms but **only one lone pair** [1], so it can accept
only one hydrogen bond, while H₂O, with two H atoms and two lone pairs, can form more [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the working behind an estimate; a bare number is hard to credit.

**11. (a)** Energy = 60.0 × 900 = 54 000 J = 54.0 kJ [1]. Moles = 22.6 / 18.0 = 1.256 mol [1].
ΔvapH = 54.0 / 1.256 [1] = **+43.0 kJ mol⁻¹** [1].
**(b)** Some energy is lost to the surroundings and does not evaporate water, so the energy per mole is
overestimated [1].
**(c)** **Hydrogen bonds** (and London forces) between water molecules are overcome [1]; the covalent O-H bonds
within molecules are not broken [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* Vaporisation is endothermic, so give the positive sign; convert J to kJ before dividing.

**12. (a)** Moles NH₄NO₃ = 4.00 / 80.0 = 0.0500 mol [1]. q = 40.0 × 4.18 × 7.3 = 1221 J [1].
ΔH = 1.221 / 0.0500 = 24.4 kJ mol⁻¹ [1]. The temperature fell, so the process is endothermic: **ΔH = +24.4 kJ
mol⁻¹** [1].
**(b)** The δ− oxygen atoms of water molecules are attracted to NH₄⁺ ions, and the δ+ hydrogen atoms to NO₃⁻ ions
[1]. Forming these ion-dipole attractions (**hydration**) releases energy that compensates for breaking the lattice
[1].
**(c)** Hexane is non-polar and has only London forces [1], so it cannot hydrate the ions; too little energy would
be released to overcome the strong ionic attractions in the lattice [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the mass of water, not the mass of solid, in q = mcΔT, and decide the sign from the
temperature change.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Saying a compound hydrogen bonds because it contains hydrogen, without checking H is on N, O or F.
- Arguing about boiling temperatures without first comparing electron numbers.
- Using "fewer electrons" to explain branching, when isomers have the same number.
- Explaining the HCl to HI trend with dipoles, which weaken down the group.
- Describing ice's lower density without the tetrahedral, open structure.
- Saying halogenoalkanes are non-polar to explain their poor solubility in water.
