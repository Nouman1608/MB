These notes cover section 3.2.3.1, Water and energy, of the OxfordAQA International GCSE Geography (9230) specification, Version 4.3. Students study either Water and energy resources or Population and communication in Section C. Key ideas: demand for water and for energy is rising globally but supply can be insecure, which may lead to conflict; different strategies can be used to increase supply. For full explanations, read the [water and energy study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-geography-9230-water-and-energy/).

## Specification checklist

- **Surplus and deficit** (security and insecurity) of water and energy; global inequality and patterns.
- **Reasons** for rising consumption; **factors** affecting supply; **impacts** of insecurity.
- **Strategies** to increase supply.
- **Four examples:** large-scale water transfer; local water scheme (LIC or NEE); fossil fuel extraction; local renewable energy scheme (LIC or NEE).
- **Sustainable futures:** conservation, groundwater management, recycling, carbon footprints.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Water security | Reliable access to enough safe water |
| Physical scarcity | Not enough water exists naturally |
| Economic scarcity | Water exists but people cannot reach or afford it |
| Abstraction | Taking water from rivers, lakes or aquifers |
| Aquifer | Underground rock storing water |
| Desalination | Removing salt from seawater |
| Grey water | Used household water that can be reused |
| Energy security | Reliable, affordable energy |
| Renewable / non-renewable | Replaced naturally / will run out |
| Carbon footprint | Carbon dioxide released by an activity or person |

## Water: the essentials

| Topic | Key points |
|---|---|
| Surplus | Parts of Canada, Brazil, Russia, South-East Asia |
| Deficit | North Africa, Middle East, parts of South Asia and northern China |
| Inequality | HIC households use hundreds of litres a day; many LIC households only tens; over two billion lack safely managed water |
| Rising use | Economic development (industry, irrigation, appliances), rising population |
| Availability factors | Climate; geology (aquifers); pollution; over-abstraction; limited infrastructure; poverty |
| Impacts | Waterborne disease; lower food output; reduced industrial output; conflict where demand exceeds supply |

## Water strategies

| Strategy | Advantage | Disadvantage |
|---|---|---|
| Dams and reservoirs | Reliable store; power; flood control | Costly; flood land; displace people |
| Water transfers | Bring water to dry areas | Very expensive; harm the source area |
| Desalination | Reliable coastal supply | High energy and cost; brine waste |
| Conservation | Cheap; cuts demand | Needs behaviour change |
| Groundwater management | Prevents aquifer depletion | Needs monitoring and rules |
| Recycling and grey water | Reuses water | Needs treatment and plumbing |

### Example: South-North Water Transfer, China (check your own example)

- Moves water from the Yangtze basin in the wet south to dry, crowded northern China, including Beijing and Tianjin.
- Eastern and middle routes in operation; the middle route takes water from the Danjiangkou reservoir.
- **Advantages:** water for tens of millions of people; supports industry and farming; eases over-use of groundwater.
- **Disadvantages:** tens of billions of dollars; hundreds of thousands relocated; less water in the source river; pollution risk along the route.

### Example: sand dams in Kitui, Kenya (local scheme)

- Small concrete walls across seasonal rivers; sand builds up behind and stores water, protected from evaporation.
- **Advantages:** cheap; built by the community; water closer to home; sand filters the water; helps small-scale farming.
- **Disadvantages:** needs suitable river beds; slow to fill; limited yields.

## Energy: the essentials

| Topic | Key points |
|---|---|
| Consumption | Highest per person in HICs; rising fast in NEEs such as China and India |
| Supply | Fossil fuels concentrated in a few regions, so many countries must import |
| Rising use | Economic development, rising population, technology |
| Supply factors | Physical, cost of exploitation, technology, political |
| Impacts of insecurity | Drilling in difficult, sensitive areas; economic and environmental costs; lower food and industrial output; conflict |

## Energy strategies

| Type | Sources | Advantage | Disadvantage |
|---|---|---|---|
| Non-renewable | Coal, oil, gas, nuclear | Reliable, large scale | Will run out; fossil fuels add carbon dioxide; nuclear waste |
| Renewable | Biomass, wind, hydro, tidal, geothermal, wave, solar | Do not run out; low carbon in use | Intermittent; high set-up costs; landscape and wildlife effects |

### Example: oil in the Niger Delta, Nigeria (fossil fuel extraction)

- **Advantages:** most of Nigeria's export earnings and much government revenue; jobs; funds development.
- **Disadvantages:** oil spills and gas flaring polluting water, soil and air; harm to farming and fishing; local communities gain little; unrest and corruption.

### Example: micro-hydro in Nepal (local renewable scheme)

- Villages divert part of a stream through a pipe to a turbine.
- **Advantages:** electricity for lights, phones and mills; cheap to run; no fuel; less kerosene and wood used.
- **Disadvantages:** output falls in the dry season; floods and landslides can damage equipment; high build cost; limited power.

## Sustainable future: energy

- **Individuals:** use less electricity, travel less by car, eat less meat to cut carbon footprints.
- **Conservation:** insulation, efficient buildings and public transport, demand reduction, more efficient use of fossil fuels.

## Worked exam answer

**Question:** Explain the advantages and disadvantages of a large-scale water transfer scheme. [6 marks]

**Model answer:** "The South-North Water Transfer in China moves water from the wet Yangtze basin to dry cities in the north, such as Beijing. Its advantages are that it supplies water to tens of millions of people, supports industry and farming, and reduces the need to pump groundwater that is running low. However, it cost a very large amount of money, hundreds of thousands of people had to move to make way for the reservoir and canals, and the Han River loses water in its source area. There is also a risk of pollution on the way. Overall, the scheme tackles a serious shortage, but its costs are heavy and fall on different groups."

How it scores: named scheme with location, two developed advantages, two developed disadvantages, and a judgement.

## Exam traps

- Water scarcity may be **economic** as well as physical.
- Give **both** advantages and disadvantages for each example.
- Nuclear power is **non-renewable**.
- Renewable energy has disadvantages (cost, intermittency, impacts).
- Conservation reduces **demand**; dams and transfers increase **supply**.
- Name the place and give two or three facts for each example.
- Impacts of insecurity should cover disease, food, industry **and** conflict.

## Self-test

1. Define water security.
2. Name **two** factors that reduce water availability.
3. Give **two** reasons why water consumption is rising.
4. What is desalination and what is its main disadvantage?
5. Give **one** advantage and **one** disadvantage of a water transfer scheme.
6. Why are sand dams suitable for a dry area in an LIC?
7. Give **two** reasons why energy demand is rising.
8. Name **two** renewable and **two** non-renewable energy sources.
9. Give **one** advantage and **one** disadvantage of extracting a fossil fuel in your example.
10. Suggest **two** ways in which an individual can reduce their carbon footprint.

**Answers**

1. Having reliable access to enough safe water.
2. Any two: low rainfall; pollution; over-abstraction; limited infrastructure; poverty.
3. Any two: economic development; rising population; changing diets.
4. Removing salt from seawater; it uses a lot of energy and is costly.
5. For example: brings water to dry areas; very expensive, or harms the source area.
6. They are cheap, built by local people with simple tools, and store water protected from evaporation.
7. Any two: economic development; rising population; technology.
8. Renewable: wind and solar (or hydro, tidal, geothermal, wave, biomass). Non-renewable: coal and oil (or gas, nuclear).
9. For example (Niger Delta): export earnings and jobs; oil spills and flaring.
10. Any two: use less electricity; use public transport or walk; eat less meat; insulate the home.
