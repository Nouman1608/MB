This guide goes deeper into section **4.6.4 Classification of living organisms** of the AQA GCSE Biology (8461)
specification, for first teaching from 2016. It is a short section, but its questions nearly always give you
information, such as a classification table or an evolutionary tree, and ask you to use it. Inheritance,
variation and evolution is assessed on Paper 2. Nothing in 4.6.4 is marked biology only or Higher tier only.

## What the specification asks you to do

- Know that living things have traditionally been classified by **structure and characteristics** in a system
  developed by **Carl Linnaeus**.
- Recall the levels: **kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus, species**.
- Know that organisms are named by the **binomial system** of genus and species.
- **Use information given** to show understanding of the Linnaean system.
- **Describe the impact of developments in biology** on classification systems.
- Know the **three-domain system** developed by **Carl Woese**: archaea, bacteria and eukaryota.
- Interpret **evolutionary trees**, which use current classification data for living organisms and fossil data
  for extinct organisms.

## The Linnaean hierarchy

Each level is a group inside the one above it. A kingdom contains many phyla, a phylum many classes, and so on,
down to the species. The further down you go, the fewer organisms the group contains and the more features they
share.

A memory sentence for the order: **K**ing **P**hilip **C**ame **O**ver **F**or **G**ood **S**oup.

**Worked example 1: using a classification table**

| Level | Lion | Tiger | Domestic cat | Grey wolf |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Kingdom | Animalia | Animalia | Animalia | Animalia |
| Phylum | Chordata | Chordata | Chordata | Chordata |
| Class | Mammalia | Mammalia | Mammalia | Mammalia |
| Order | Carnivora | Carnivora | Carnivora | Carnivora |
| Family | Felidae | Felidae | Felidae | Canidae |
| Genus | *Panthera* | *Panthera* | *Felis* | *Canis* |
| Species | *leo* | *tigris* | *catus* | *lupus* |

*Which two of these animals are most closely related? Explain your answer.*

> The lion and the tiger [1]. They share every level down to the genus, *Panthera*, which is more levels than
> any other pair [1].

*Which animal is least closely related to the others?*

> The grey wolf. It shares levels only down to order; it is in a different family, Canidae.

The rule: **the lower the level two organisms share, the more closely related they are.** Count down from the
top until the names differ.

## Binomial names

The binomial system gives every species a two-part name: the **genus** followed by the **species**. The lion is
*Panthera leo*. The conventions are:

- the genus starts with a capital letter, the species with a lower-case letter;
- the name is printed in italics, or underlined when handwritten;
- after the first use the genus can be shortened to its initial, as in *P. leo*.

Why binomial names matter: common names vary between languages and regions, and one common name can refer to
different species. A scientific name refers to exactly one species wherever it is used. Two species sharing a
genus name, such as *Panthera leo* and *Panthera tigris*, are known at once to be closely related.

A common trap: "*Panthera*" alone is not the name of a species. It is the genus, which contains several species.

## How developments in biology changed classification

Linnaeus worked in the 18th century and could only compare features he could see, such as body shape, number of
legs or the structure of flowers. The specification names two developments that led to **new models of
classification**:

1. **Improvements in microscopes** revealed internal structures, such as whether a cell has a nucleus and which
   organelles it contains.
2. **Better understanding of biochemical processes** allowed scientists to compare the chemicals inside cells.

Classification is a **model**. When evidence improves, scientists revise the model. This is the same pattern you
meet in 4.6.3, where ideas about evolution and genetics changed as evidence built up.

## Woese's three-domain system

Using evidence from **chemical analysis**, Carl Woese proposed the **three-domain system**. A domain is a level
above kingdom. Organisms are divided into:

| Domain | What it contains | Key point |
|---|---|---|
| Archaea | Primitive bacteria usually living in extreme environments | Look like bacteria, but chemical analysis shows they are a separate group |
| Bacteria | True bacteria | Prokaryotic cells |
| Eukaryota | Protists, fungi, plants and animals | Cells with a nucleus |

The key idea for exams: under the microscope archaea and bacteria look alike, since neither has a nucleus, so
older systems grouped them together. Chemical analysis showed they are very different from each other, so they
were placed in separate domains. That is an example of a "development in biology" changing classification.

Archaea living in extreme environments link to **extremophiles** in Ecology (4.7.1.4), such as bacteria in
deep-sea vents.

## Evolutionary trees

An **evolutionary tree** shows how scientists believe organisms are related. It is built from **current
classification data for living organisms** and **fossil data for extinct organisms**. Time usually runs from the
bottom (or left) of the tree to the top (or right).

**Worked example 2: reading a tree.** Below, each branch point (marked +) is a common ancestor. Species V is
extinct and known only from fossils.

```
                 +------------- species P
          +------+
          |      +------------- species Q
   -------+
          |      +------------- species R
          +------+
                 |    +-------- species S
                 +----+
                      +--- V (extinct)
```

*Which species is most closely related to S?*

> V. S and V share the most recent common ancestor (the last branch point before they split).

*Is species R more closely related to Q or to S?*

> S. Trace back from R: it joins the S and V branch before it joins the P and Q branch.

*A student says "species S evolved from species R". Explain why this is wrong.*

> Both are tips of the tree, so both are present-day (or end-point) species. The tree shows that they share a
> common ancestor, not that one evolved from the other.

The method:

1. Put your finger on each organism at the tip of its branch.
2. Trace each branch back until they meet. That point is their most recent common ancestor.
3. The more recently two branches meet, the more closely related the organisms are.

## Common mistakes

- Writing the levels in the wrong order, especially swapping order and family or class and phylum.
- Starting the species name with a capital letter, or writing the species name without the genus.
- Saying archaea are "a kind of eukaryote". They have no nucleus; they are their own domain.
- Saying Woese used microscopes. The specification says his system came from chemical analysis.
- Reading an evolutionary tree by counting how close two names are on the page rather than tracing back to the
  branch point.
- Claiming one living species on a tree evolved from another living species.

## Exam technique for AQA command words

- **Use the information** or **use the table**: quote the shared levels or names from the data you are given.
- **Explain** why two organisms are closely related: name the lowest level they share.
- **Describe** the impact of developments: name the development (microscopes, biochemistry), what it revealed,
  and the change it caused (new groups, three domains).
- **Suggest** where an extinct organism fits: use fossil evidence, such as shared features with a living group.
