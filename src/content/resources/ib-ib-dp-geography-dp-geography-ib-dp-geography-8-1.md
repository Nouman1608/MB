This guide goes deeper into sub-topic 8.1 of Unit 1, Changing population, in IB Diploma Programme Geography (first assessments 2019): population and economic development patterns. It is part of the core studied by every SL and HL student and examined in Paper 2. The [8.1 revision notes](/resources/ib-ib-dp-geography-population-and-economic-development-patterns-revision-notes/) already cover physical and human factors, the global pattern, World Bank income groups, Friedmann's core-periphery model, cumulative causation, megacity growth and the demographic transition model. This page adds what those notes leave short: how to measure how concentrated a population is, how to test the link between population and development statistically, how different measures of development change the picture, and how to measure regional inequality within a country. Test yourself with the [8.1 practice questions](/resources/ib-ib-dp-geography-dp-geography-ib-dp-geography-8-1-practice/); the [Population distribution study guide](/resources/ib-dp-geography-population-distribution/) gives the wider picture of the unit.

## What this sub-topic asks you to do

- Explain the physical and human factors behind population distribution at global and national scales.
- Explain how population distribution relates to economic development, including core-periphery patterns.
- Explain the growth of megacities through natural increase and migration.
- Use data to describe, compare and explain these patterns.

## Measuring how concentrated a population is

Saying a population is "unevenly distributed" is description. Measuring it is analysis. A **Lorenz curve** shows how concentrated a population is across the regions of a country.

### Worked example 1: building a Lorenz curve

An invented country has six regions. Rank them from the highest to the lowest density, then add up the percentages.

| Region | Share of population (%) | Share of land area (%) | Cumulative area (%) | Cumulative population (%) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A (delta) | 40 | 10 | 10 | 40 |
| B (coastal plain) | 25 | 10 | 20 | 65 |
| C (river valley) | 15 | 15 | 35 | 80 |
| D (plateau) | 10 | 20 | 55 | 90 |
| E (hills) | 6 | 20 | 75 | 96 |
| F (desert) | 4 | 25 | 100 | 100 |

Plot cumulative population against cumulative area. If people were spread evenly, the points would fall on a straight diagonal line (20% of the land holding 20% of the people). Here, 65% of the people live on 20% of the land, so the curve bows far above the diagonal. The further it bows, the more concentrated the population. The same method lets you compare two countries, or one country at two dates, and show whether population is concentrating (for example through migration to the core) or spreading out.

A limit: the result depends on how many regions are used and where their boundaries fall. Fewer, larger regions hide concentration within them.

## Testing the link between population and development

A common claim is that fertility falls as income rises. Spearman's rank correlation tests how strong and consistent such a relationship is.

### Worked example 2: Spearman's rank

Invented data for eight countries. Rank each variable from highest (1) to lowest (8), then find the difference (d) between ranks.

| Country | GNI per capita (thousand dollars) | Rank | Total fertility rate | Rank | d | d squared |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| P | 1.2 | 8 | 5.8 | 1 | 7 | 49 |
| Q | 2.5 | 7 | 4.1 | 3 | 4 | 16 |
| R | 4.0 | 6 | 4.6 | 2 | 4 | 16 |
| S | 6.5 | 5 | 2.9 | 4 | 1 | 1 |
| T | 9.0 | 4 | 2.2 | 5 | -1 | 1 |
| U | 14.0 | 3 | 1.9 | 6 | -3 | 9 |
| V | 28.0 | 2 | 1.6 | 8 | -6 | 36 |
| W | 45.0 | 1 | 1.7 | 7 | -6 | 36 |

Sum of d squared = 164; n = 8.

**rs = 1 - (6 x sum of d squared) / (n x (n squared - 1)) = 1 - (6 x 164) / (8 x 63) = 1 - 984 / 504 = 1 - 1.952 = -0.95**

The result, -0.95, shows a very strong negative relationship: higher income is consistently associated with lower fertility. Compare it with a table of critical values for n = 8; a value this strong exceeds the critical value even at the stricter significance level in standard tables, so it is very unlikely to have arisen by chance.

Three cautions belong in any answer:

1. **Correlation is not causation.** Income may not lower fertility directly; female education, urbanisation and lower infant mortality, which rise with income, are the likely mechanisms.
2. **Outliers matter.** Country R has higher fertility than poorer Country Q, which might reflect culture, religion or policy.
3. **Small samples** give unreliable results; eight countries cannot represent the world.

## Measuring development: GNI versus HDI

**Gross national income (GNI) per capita** measures average income. The **Human Development Index (HDI)**, published by the United Nations Development Programme, combines three dimensions: a long and healthy life (life expectancy at birth), knowledge (years of schooling) and a decent standard of living (GNI per capita).

Countries can rank quite differently on the two:

- A country with high income from oil or minerals but weaker health and education can rank lower on HDI than on GNI.
- A country that has invested heavily in health and education can rank higher on HDI than its income alone predicts.

Both are national averages. Neither shows inequality within a country, so a high average can hide large poor regions. That is why core-periphery analysis matters.

## Measuring regional inequality within a country

Compare each region's share of national output with its share of population.

### Worked example 3: core and periphery

Invented data for one country:

| Region | Share of population (%) | Share of GDP (%) | GDP per person relative to national average |
|---|---|---|---|
| Capital region (core) | 20 | 38 | 38 / 20 = 1.9 |
| Industrial north | 30 | 32 | 32 / 30 = 1.07 |
| Agricultural south | 35 | 22 | 22 / 35 = 0.63 |
| Mountain interior | 15 | 8 | 8 / 15 = 0.53 |

Output per person in the core is 1.9 / 0.53 = about **3.6 times** that in the mountain interior. This gap is what drives internal migration from periphery to core, which in turn feeds megacity growth in the capital, an illustration of cumulative causation that links population distribution directly to economic development.

## How megacity growth links the two

Megacities sit at the centre of this sub-topic because they combine both themes. Their growth comes from natural increase among young residents and from migration driven by the income gap between core and periphery. In return, megacities concentrate investment, infrastructure and jobs, which strengthens the core further. A strong answer explains this two-way link with a named megacity and its national context.

## Annotated model answer

*Examine the relationship between population distribution and economic development within one country.* **(sample paragraph)**

"In many countries, economic activity concentrates in a core region with the best infrastructure and connections, and this attracts migrants from poorer peripheral regions **[mechanism]**. Output per person in the core can be several times that in the periphery, and this gap drives the migration that makes the core's population grow faster than the national average **[measured link]**. Physical geography sets limits, since deserts or mountains may stay sparsely populated however policy changes **[physical factor]**. However, governments can alter the pattern through regional policy, as when Brazil moved its capital to Brasilia in 1960 to develop the interior **[human intervention]**. The relationship is therefore two-way: development shapes distribution, and concentration of people reinforces development in the core **[judgement]**."

## Common mistakes

- Describing distribution as "uneven" without measuring how uneven.
- Treating a strong correlation as proof of cause.
- Using GNI alone as if it captured development.
- Discussing national averages without considering regional inequality.
- Separating megacity growth from the national core-periphery pattern.

## Exam technique

- **Describe** distribution data with values, regions and anomalies.
- **Explain** patterns with named physical and human factors for named places.
- **Analyse** relationships with a statistic or a clear comparison, and state its limits.
- **Examine** and **discuss** questions need both the link between population and development and the factors that complicate it.
