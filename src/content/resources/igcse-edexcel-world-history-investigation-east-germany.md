Historical Investigation A5, "East Germany, 1958–90", is one of five investigation options in Section
A of Pearson Edexcel International GCSE History (4HI1) Paper 2. It examines why the German Democratic
Republic (GDR) built the Berlin Wall, how the ruling Socialist Unity Party (SED) stabilised the state
through economic planning and the Stasi, what daily life was like, how relations with West Germany
changed under Ostpolitik, and how the Peaceful Revolution of 1989 led to reunification in October
1990. The exam is source-based.

## Syllabus coverage

The 4HI1 specification (Issue 5, February 2026) sets five key topics for A5:

1. **The Berlin Wall crisis, 1958–63** — the refugee problem and its impact, the influence of Ulbricht
   and Khrushchev, and the Wall's impact on East Berliners, security and escapes.
2. **Stabilisation and control, 1962–87** — economic stabilisation, the New Economic System and the
   Economic System of Socialism (1962–71), Comecon, economic problems of the 1980s; the Stasi, control of
   the young, religion and the Church, propaganda and censorship, support and opposition, relations with
   the Eastern bloc.
3. **Life in the GDR, 1962–87** — employment, housing, education and welfare, the changing role of women,
   Honecker and a GDR identity, mass media and sport.
4. **Ostpolitik, 1969–87** — relations with West Germany, the agreements of 1970–72, travel and
   communication, the impact on the GDR's economy, and international recognition and state visits.
5. **The Peaceful Revolution, 1987–90** — Gorbachev's influence, the border openings in Hungary and Austria,
   Gorbachev's visit in October 1989, the fall of Honecker, Krenz and the opening of the Wall, the collapse
   of the SED government, the elections of March 1990 and reunification.

## Why was the Berlin Wall built?

By 1961 about 2.7 million people had left the GDR since 1949, most through the open border in Berlin.
Many were young and skilled — doctors, engineers, teachers — so the loss threatened the economy and the
regime's legitimacy. In November 1958 **Khrushchev** demanded that the Western powers leave West Berlin,
but they refused. GDR leader **Walter Ulbricht** pressed Moscow to let him close the border. After the
Vienna summit with Kennedy (June 1961) failed and the outflow accelerated, Khrushchev agreed. On the night
of **12–13 August 1961** the border was sealed with barbed wire, soon replaced by concrete walls, a "death
strip", watchtowers and armed guards.

The Wall split families and cut East Berliners off from jobs and relatives in the West. The GDR called it
an "anti-fascist protection rampart". People still tried to **escape** — through tunnels, by jumping from
windows on the border, or hidden in vehicles — and many were shot; the death of Peter Fechter, left to
bleed to death at the Wall in 1962, caused outrage.

## How did the SED stabilise and control the GDR?

With emigration stopped, the economy could be planned. Ulbricht introduced the **New Economic System**
(from 1963), which gave factory managers more freedom and used profit and bonuses as incentives, followed
by the **Economic System of Socialism** (from 1968). Growth improved, and the GDR became one of the most
industrialised members of **Comecon**, trading heavily with the USSR. After **Erich Honecker** replaced
Ulbricht in 1971, policy shifted to consumer goods, housing and subsidies. By the 1980s this caused
serious **economic problems**: welfare spending exceeded what the economy could afford, the state borrowed
from the West, and consumer goods were scarce and of **poor quality** — buyers waited years for a Trabant
car.

**State control** rested on the **Stasi**, the Ministry for State Security, which by the 1980s had about
90,000 full-time staff and a large network of informers, opening mail and tapping phones. Young people
were organised in the Young Pioneers and the **Free German Youth** (FDJ). The Protestant **Church** was
pressured, but in 1978 an agreement gave it limited independence; in the 1980s churches sheltered peace
and environmental groups. **Propaganda and censorship** controlled newspapers, radio and television,
though most East Germans could watch West German TV. Support came from those who valued security and
welfare; opposition included dissidents such as the singer Wolf Biermann, expelled in 1976. Relations with
the **Soviet Union** remained the foundation of the regime; Soviet troops were stationed in the GDR.

## What was daily life like in the GDR?

The state guaranteed **employment**, cheap rents and subsidised food, transport and childcare. A huge
**housing** programme built prefabricated flats, though many older buildings decayed. **Education** was free
and organised in ten-year polytechnic schools mixing academic and practical work, with political loyalty
expected. **Welfare** included free healthcare. **Women** were encouraged to work — most did — supported by
crèches and maternity benefits, and abortion was legalised in 1972; but women still did most housework and
few reached top posts.

Under Honecker the regime promoted a separate **GDR identity**; the 1974 constitution removed references to
a single German nation. **Mass media** spread the party line. **Sport** was a source of national pride: the
GDR finished second in the medal table at the 1976 Montreal Olympics, although its success was later
shown to rest partly on state-organised doping.

## How did Ostpolitik change relations with West Germany?

West German Chancellor **Willy Brandt** (from 1969) pursued **Ostpolitik**, seeking "change through
rapprochement". Treaties with the USSR and Poland (1970), the **Four Power Agreement** on Berlin (1971) and the
**Basic Treaty** (1972), in which the two German states recognised each other's borders, followed. Both joined
the United Nations in 1973, and the GDR gained wide **international recognition**. **Travel restrictions** were
relaxed for West Germans visiting the East, phone links improved, and some East German pensioners could visit
the West. West German credits and payments helped the GDR's economy and **standard of living**, but also
increased its dependence on the West. **State visits** culminated in Honecker's visit to West Germany in 1987.

## How did the Peaceful Revolution end the GDR, 1987–90?

**Gorbachev**'s reforms (glasnost and perestroika) encouraged East German protesters, while Honecker
refused to follow them. In 1989 **Hungary** dismantled its border fence with **Austria** and in September
opened the border completely; thousands of East Germans fled west, while others occupied West German
embassies in Prague and Warsaw. At the GDR's 40th anniversary in October 1989 **Gorbachev**'s visit
signalled that Soviet troops would not intervene. Mass demonstrations — especially the Monday
demonstrations in **Leipzig** ("We are the people") — grew. **Honecker** was forced out on 18 October and
replaced by **Egon Krenz**. On **9 November 1989**, after a confused announcement of new travel rules, crowds
forced the border crossings open and the **Berlin Wall** fell. The SED's power collapsed; in the **March 1990
elections** the CDU-led Alliance for Germany won, and after monetary union (July) and the Two Plus Four
Treaty, Germany was **reunified on 3 October 1990**.

## Frequently asked questions

**Was the Wall a success for the GDR?** It stopped emigration and allowed economic stabilisation, but it
became a symbol of a regime that had to imprison its people.

**How far did East Germans support the regime?** Many valued security and welfare; fear of the Stasi and
lack of choice limited open opposition until 1989.

**What was the immediate cause of the GDR's end?** The opening of the Hungarian border, mass protest, the
fall of the Wall and the SED's collapse, with Gorbachev refusing to intervene.
