## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Exports | Goods and services produced in a country and sold abroad |
| Imports | Goods and services that residents and firms buy from producers abroad |
| Global interdependence | Countries relying on one another for markets, goods and materials |
| Free trade | Trade with no tariffs, quotas or other barriers |
| Free-trade agreement (FTA) | A deal between countries to remove barriers on trade among themselves |
| Exchange rate | The amount of one currency needed to buy a unit of another |
| Appreciation | A rise in a currency's value against another |
| Depreciation | A fall in a currency's value against another |
| Tariff | A tax on imports |

## Benefits of trade (3.2.4.1)

| Benefit | The reasoning |
|---|---|
| Specialisation | Each country makes what it gives up least to produce, so total output rises |
| Lower prices, more choice | Imports add variety and competition |
| Economies of scale | A bigger market spreads fixed costs, so average cost falls |
| Access to resources | Buy what cannot be produced at home |
| Efficiency | Foreign rivals push local firms to cut costs |

**Method for average cost with exports:** average cost = variable cost per unit + fixed cost / output.

**Reminder.** A workshop has fixed costs of 30,000 and variable costs of 2 a unit.
At 5,000 units: 2 + 30,000/5,000 = **8**. At 15,000 units (after exporting): 2 + 30,000/15,000 = **4**.

**Reminder: trade as a share of GDP.** Trade share = (exports + imports) / GDP x 100. Exports share = exports / GDP x 100. A higher share means the economy is more open and more exposed to events abroad.

## Global interdependence

| Gain | Risk |
|---|---|
| Supplies and markets from many countries | Recession in a main partner cuts exports |
| Lower costs | A shortage abroad can stop local production |
| Technology shared | Overreliance on one export or one supplier |

**Method for an export shock:** fall in total exports (%) = share of exports going to the partner x percentage fall in sales to that partner.
Example: 50% of exports go to Country B and sales there fall 12%. Fall = 0.50 x 12% = **6%** of total exports.

## Free trade

**For:**
- Lower prices and wider choice
- Competition raises efficiency
- Specialisation and scale
- Less risk of retaliation

**Against (arguments for protection):**
- Infant industries need time to grow
- Job losses in uncompetitive industries
- Dumping below cost
- Strategic industries such as food and energy
- Overdependence on imports or a few exports
- Weaker standards abroad can undercut local firms

**Evaluation checklist.** For each argument ask about the size of the effect, its duration, the winners and losers, and whether there is a better remedy than restricting trade (retraining, for instance).

## Free-trade agreements (FTAs)

| Benefits | Costs |
|---|---|
| Cheaper imports | Protected industries lose protection |
| Larger market for exporters | Lost tariff revenue |
| More competition | Trade diversion from cheaper outsiders |
| More foreign investment | Less freedom over rules |
| Lower admin costs | Gains and losses unevenly spread |

**Tariff reminder.** A 12% tariff on a good worth 250 adds 250 x 0.12 = 30, so the price is 280. Removing the tariff under an FTA saves 30 per unit for the buyer and costs the government 30 per unit imported.

## Exchange rate determination (3.2.4.2)

The rate is set where the demand for a currency equals its supply.

| Demand for the arlo comes from | Supply of the arlo comes from |
|---|---|
| Foreign buyers of A's exports | A's residents buying imports |
| Tourists visiting A | A's residents travelling abroad |
| Investment into A | Investment out of A |
| Savers attracted by high interest rates | Savers leaving when rates are low |
| Speculators expecting a rise | Speculators expecting a fall |

**Diagram in words.** Vertical axis: exchange rate (bexo per arlo). Horizontal axis: quantity of arlo. Demand slopes down and supply slopes up; the equilibrium rate is where they cross. A rightward shift in demand moves the rate up (appreciation). A rightward shift in supply moves it down (depreciation).

**Method for finding a new equilibrium from a table:** (1) add or subtract the shift at every rate on the shifted curve; (2) find the rate where demand and supply are equal; (3) work out the percentage change = (new rate - old rate) / old rate x 100.

## Effects of exchange rate changes

| If the arlo depreciates | Consumers | Producers |
|---|---|---|
| Imports | Dearer | Importers pay more; firms using imported parts face higher costs |
| Exports | Cheaper for foreigners | Exporters more price competitive |
| Holidays abroad | Dearer | Home tourism more attractive |
| Competition | Local goods relatively cheaper | Local firms face less import competition |

An **appreciation** reverses every row.

**Converting prices.** If 1 arlo = 2.5 bexo, a 500-bexo import costs 500/2.5 = 200 arlo. If the rate falls to 2.0, it costs 500/2.0 = 250 arlo, which is 50 more.

**Percentage changes differ.** Moving from 2.0 to 2.5 bexo per arlo is a 25% rise for the arlo, but arlo per bexo goes from 0.50 to 0.40, a 20% fall for the bexo.

**Holiday reminder.** A hotel bill of 3,000 bexo costs 1,500 arlo at 2.0 and 1,200 arlo at 2.5, so a stronger arlo saves 300.

**Evaluation points.** Depreciation helps exporters more if demand for exports is elastic. It raises costs for firms with imported inputs. Effects may take time because of contracts and fixed prices.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Exports** earn foreign money and give a currency demand; **imports** use up local money and add currency supply.
- **Depreciation** lowers the value of a currency and changes prices of both imports and exports; a **tariff** is a tax that raises only the price of imports.
- **Free trade** means no barriers; an **FTA** removes barriers only between members.
- **Higher exchange rate** helps buyers of imports; **lower exchange rate** helps exporters, subject to elasticity.

## Quick self-test

1. Define imports.
2. Country X has GDP 80, exports 24 and imports 20 (billion arlo). Calculate exports as a percentage of GDP and trade as a percentage of GDP.
3. At 2.0 bexo per arlo a good costs 300 arlo. State its price in bexo, and its price after an appreciation to 2.5.
4. Country P sends 50% of its exports to Country Q. Sales to Q fall by 12%. Calculate the fall in P's total exports.
5. A 15% tariff is added to an import priced at 40. Calculate the new price and the tariff revenue on 200 units.
6. State two arguments against free trade.
7. Give two reasons why the demand for a currency may rise.
8. The rate falls from 2.0 to 1.5 bexo per arlo. Calculate the percentage change, and the arlo cost of a 90-bexo part before and after.
9. Using the study guide table (equilibrium 2.0, 70 million), supply falls by 20 million at every rate. Find the new equilibrium rate and percentage change.
10. Explain one benefit and one cost of a depreciation for a producer.

### Answers

1. Goods and services bought from other countries.
2. Exports 24/80 x 100 = **30%**. Trade (24 + 20)/80 x 100 = **55%**.
3. 300 x 2.0 = **600 bexo**; 300 x 2.5 = **750 bexo** (up 25%).
4. 0.50 x 12% = **6%**.
5. Tariff 40 x 0.15 = 6, new price **46**. Revenue 200 x 6 = **1,200**.
6. Any two of: infant industries, job losses, dumping, strategic industries, overdependence, weaker standards.
7. Foreigners buy more exports; higher interest rates attract savers; more foreign investment; speculation of a rise.
8. (1.5 - 2.0)/2.0 x 100 = **-25%**. Part cost 90/2.0 = **45 arlo**, then 90/1.5 = **60 arlo**, 15 more.
9. New supply is 70, 60, 50, 40, 30 against demand 50, 60, 70, 80, 90. They match at **2.2** (60 = 60), a **10% appreciation**.
10. Benefit: exports become cheaper abroad, so sales may rise. Cost: imported components cost more in local currency, so unit costs rise.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "the exchange rate falls" with no currency named, so the direction cannot be judged.
- Saying depreciation makes imports cheaper.
- Converting in the wrong direction: multiplying or dividing by the rate without checking which currency it is quoted in.
- Dividing a change by the new figure rather than the starting one.
- Describing a benefit of trade with no chain (for example, "more competition" with no link to lower prices).
- Listing arguments against free trade with no explanation of why they matter, or no judgement.
- Treating an FTA as the same thing as free trade with the whole world.
- Forgetting that an exchange rate change helps one group and harms another.

## Frequently asked questions

**What are the arguments against free trade?**
Arguments for some protection include protecting infant industries, avoiding job losses in uncompetitive industries, preventing dumping below cost, protecting strategic industries such as food and energy, avoiding overdependence, and stopping imports made under weaker standards from undercutting local firms.

**How do you calculate the effect of a fall in exports to one trading partner?**
Multiply the share of total exports going to that partner by the percentage fall in sales there. For example, if 50% of exports go to one country and sales there fall 12%, total exports fall by 6%.

**How is an exchange rate determined in a free market?**
The exchange rate is set where the demand for a currency equals its supply. Demand comes from foreigners who need the currency to buy the country's exports, pay for tourism, invest or save there; supply comes from residents changing it into foreign currency, for example to buy imports.

**When does a depreciation help exporters most?**
A depreciation helps exporters more if demand for their exports is price elastic, because the lower foreign price then raises sales by a larger proportion. It also raises costs for firms with imported inputs, and effects may take time because of contracts and fixed prices.
