This guide covers **4.4.1 Reactivity of metals** of the AQA GCSE Chemistry (8462) specification, for teaching
from September 2016 onwards (version 1.2). It includes 4.4.1.1 Metal oxides, 4.4.1.2 The reactivity series,
4.4.1.3 Extraction of metals and reduction, and 4.4.1.4 Oxidation and reduction in terms of electrons. The
first three are for both tiers. **4.4.1.4 is marked (HT only)** and is Higher Tier only. The topic is assessed
on Paper 1. There is no required practical named in this subsection, although reactions of metals with water and acids are
basic to practical work.

For quick recall use the [reactivity of metals revision notes](/resources/aqa-gcse-chemistry-8462-reactivity-of-metals-revision-notes/),
and test yourself with the [practice questions](/resources/aqa-gcse-chemistry-8462-reactivity-of-metals-practice/).

## Metal oxides: oxidation and reduction (4.4.1.1)

Metals react with oxygen to form **metal oxides**. For example, 2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO and 2Cu + O₂ → 2CuO. These are
**oxidation** reactions because the metals **gain oxygen**. **Reduction** is the loss of oxygen.

| Term | Definition (oxygen) | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Oxidation | Gain of oxygen | 2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO: magnesium is oxidised |
| Reduction | Loss of oxygen | CuO + C → Cu + CO: copper oxide is reduced |

In a single reaction, one substance is oxidised while another is reduced.

### Worked example 1: oxidised and reduced

*Identify the substance oxidised and the substance reduced: 2CuO + C → 2Cu + CO₂*

```
CuO -> Cu : copper oxide loses oxygen -> reduced
C -> CO2  : carbon gains oxygen        -> oxidised
```

## The reactivity series (4.4.1.2)

When metals react with other substances, the metal atoms form **positive ions**. The **reactivity** of a metal is
related to its **tendency to form positive ions**: the more easily a metal loses electrons to form an ion, the
more reactive it is.

The metals in the specification, from most to least reactive, are: **potassium, sodium, lithium, calcium,
magnesium, zinc, iron, copper**. The non-metals carbon and hydrogen are often placed in the series too. In the commonly used series the full order
is potassium, sodium, lithium, calcium, magnesium, aluminium, carbon, zinc, iron, hydrogen, copper.

| Metal | Reaction with cold water | Reaction with dilute acid |
|---|---|---|
| Potassium | Violent; hydrogen burns with a lilac flame | Too dangerous to try |
| Sodium | Vigorous; melts into a ball and fizzes | Too dangerous to try |
| Lithium | Fizzes steadily | Too dangerous to try |
| Calcium | Reacts steadily, fizzing, forming a cloudy solution | Reacts vigorously |
| Magnesium | Very slow reaction with cold water | Reacts quickly, fizzing |
| Zinc | No visible reaction | Reacts at a moderate rate |
| Iron | No visible reaction | Reacts slowly |
| Copper | No reaction | No reaction |

Observation notes: with water, the Group 1 metals and calcium make a metal hydroxide solution and hydrogen. With
acids, the metals make a salt and hydrogen.

```
2Na + 2H2O -> 2NaOH + H2        Ca + 2H2O -> Ca(OH)2 + H2
Mg + 2HCl -> MgCl2 + H2         Zn + 2HCl -> ZnCl2 + H2         Fe + 2HCl -> FeCl2 + H2
```

The reactions with water and acids are limited to room temperature and do not include reactions with steam.

### Displacement reactions

A **more reactive metal can displace a less reactive metal from a compound**. If you add magnesium to copper(II)
sulfate solution, magnesium takes the place of copper: Mg + CuSO₄ → MgSO₄ + Cu. The blue colour fades and brown
copper forms. If you add copper to magnesium sulfate, nothing happens, because copper is less reactive.

### Worked example 2: deducing an order

*A student adds metals A, B, C and D to solutions of salts of the other metals. A tick means a reaction occurred.*

| Metal | Salt of A | Salt of B | Salt of C | Salt of D |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | – | ✗ | ✗ | ✗ |
| B | ✓ | – | ✗ | ✓ |
| C | ✓ | ✓ | – | ✓ |
| D | ✓ | ✗ | ✗ | – |

```
C displaces A, B and D          -> C is the most reactive
B displaces A and D but not C   -> B is second
D displaces A only              -> D is third
A displaces nothing             -> A is the least reactive
Order: C > B > D > A
```

### Worked example 3: why a displacement reaction happens

*Explain why zinc displaces copper from copper(II) sulfate solution: Zn + CuSO₄ → ZnSO₄ + Cu*

Zinc is more reactive than copper, so zinc has a greater tendency to form positive ions. Zinc atoms form zinc
ions, and copper ions are changed to copper atoms, which appear as a brown solid.

## Extraction of metals (4.4.1.3)

Unreactive metals such as **gold** are found in the Earth as the metal itself. Most metals are found as
compounds, usually oxides, and need chemical reactions to extract them.

- Metals **less reactive than carbon** can be extracted from their oxides by **reduction with carbon**.
- Metals **more reactive than carbon**, such as potassium and aluminium, cannot be extracted this way.

```
2Fe2O3 + 3C -> 4Fe + 3CO2       2CuO + C -> 2Cu + CO2       ZnO + C -> Zn + CO
```

In each equation, the metal oxide loses oxygen (reduced) and the carbon gains oxygen (oxidised). Knowledge of the
details of the industrial processes is not required; you may be given information and asked to interpret or
evaluate a process.

### Worked example 4: choosing an extraction method

*Lead and potassium are both found as compounds. Which can be extracted from its oxide by heating with carbon?*

Lead is less reactive than carbon, so heating its oxide with carbon removes the oxygen and gives the metal.
Potassium is more reactive than carbon, so carbon cannot remove the oxygen from its oxide.

## Oxidation and reduction in terms of electrons (4.4.1.4, HT only)

**Oxidation is loss of electrons and reduction is gain of electrons.** The reactivity of a metal is its tendency
to lose electrons, so metal atoms are oxidised when they form positive ions.

### Half equations and ionic equations (HT only)

```
Zn -> Zn2+ + 2e-        zinc loses electrons: oxidised
Cu2+ + 2e- -> Cu        copper ions gain electrons: reduced
Ionic equation: Zn + Cu2+ -> Zn2+ + Cu
```

The sulfate ions are spectator ions: they are unchanged and do not appear in the ionic equation.

### Worked example 5: displacement as redox (HT only)

*Iron is added to copper(II) sulfate solution. Write the ionic equation and identify what is oxidised and
reduced.*

```
Fe + Cu2+ -> Fe2+ + Cu
Fe -> Fe2+ + 2e- : iron is oxidised (loses electrons)
Cu2+ + 2e- -> Cu : copper ions are reduced (gain electrons)
```

### Worked example 6: magnesium and oxygen (HT only)

*In 2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO, identify the oxidation and the reduction in terms of electrons.*

```
Mg -> Mg2+ + 2e-  : magnesium loses electrons: oxidised
O2 + 4e- -> 2O2-  : oxygen gains electrons: reduced
```

## Common mistakes

- Writing that oxidation is "gain of electrons". Oxidation is loss of electrons (HT only).
- Placing hydrogen or carbon in the wrong position in the series.
- Saying that a less reactive metal can displace a more reactive one.
- Writing "iron oxide is oxidised" when it loses oxygen (it is reduced).
- Forgetting charges in ionic equations (HT only).
- Including spectator ions in an ionic equation (HT only).

## Exam technique

- **Deduce an order:** compare each pair of metals, then list the order and say how you decided.
- **Identify oxidation and reduction:** state the gain or loss of oxygen or electrons (HT only for electrons).
- **Explain displacement:** the more reactive metal has a greater tendency to form positive ions.
- **Ionic equations (HT only):** write the half equations first and check that charge balances.
