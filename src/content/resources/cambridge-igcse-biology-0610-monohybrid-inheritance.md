This guide covers sub-topic **17.4 Monohybrid inheritance** of the Cambridge IGCSE Biology 0610 syllabus for examination in 2026, 2027 and 2028. Outcomes 1 to 12 are Core. Outcomes 13 to 18, on test crosses, codominance, ABO blood groups and sex linkage, are Supplement content and are marked **Extended only**.

The [inheritance study guide](/resources/igcse-biology-0610-inheritance/) sets out the terms and one worked example of each type of cross. This page builds the problem-solving skills examiners test: working backwards from offspring to parents, reading ratios from real counts, deducing genotypes in a pedigree, and handling codominant and sex-linked crosses that differ from the textbook cases. Afterwards, use the [monohybrid inheritance revision notes](/resources/cambridge-igcse-biology-0610-monohybrid-inheritance-revision-notes/) and the [monohybrid inheritance practice questions](/resources/cambridge-igcse-biology-0610-monohybrid-inheritance-practice/).

## The terms in one example

In pea plants, the allele for round seeds (**R**) is dominant to the allele for wrinkled seeds (**r**).

| Genotype | Homozygous or heterozygous? | Phenotype | Pure-breeding? |
|---|---|---|---|
| RR | homozygous | round | yes, with another RR plant |
| Rr | heterozygous | round | no |
| rr | homozygous | wrinkled | yes, with another rr plant |

- **Genotype** is the genetic make-up, written as the alleles present. **Phenotype** is the observable feature.
- A **dominant** allele is expressed if it is present; a **recessive** allele is expressed only when no dominant allele is present.
- Two identical homozygous individuals that breed together are **pure-breeding**: all their offspring are the same as them. A heterozygous individual is not pure-breeding, because it can pass on either allele.

**Choosing symbols.** Use one letter for a gene, a capital for the dominant allele and the lower-case version for the recessive. Pick a letter whose capital and lower case look different when handwritten (R/r rather than S/s), and give a key if the question does not.

## Working forwards: predicting offspring (Core)

Lay out every cross in the same order: parental phenotypes, parental genotypes, gametes, Punnett square, offspring genotypes, offspring phenotypes and ratio. Each line can earn a mark even if a later line goes wrong.

The syllabus limits Core crosses to **1 : 1** and **3 : 1** phenotype ratios. Two crosses give them:

| Cross | Offspring genotypes | Phenotype ratio |
|---|---|---|
| Rr × Rr | 1 RR : 2 Rr : 1 rr | 3 round : 1 wrinkled |
| Rr × rr | 1 Rr : 1 rr | 1 round : 1 wrinkled |

Outcome 12 asks you to use a Punnett square when a cross gives more than one genotype. In Rr × Rr, the square shows three genotypes but only two phenotypes, which is why the genotype ratio (1 : 2 : 1) and the phenotype ratio (3 : 1) are different. Always check which one the question asks for.

## Working backwards: from offspring to parents (Core)

Many questions give you the offspring and ask for the parents' genotypes.

| Offspring phenotypes | Parents' genotypes |
|---|---|
| about 3 dominant : 1 recessive | both heterozygous (Rr × Rr) |
| about 1 dominant : 1 recessive | heterozygous × homozygous recessive (Rr × rr) |
| all recessive | both homozygous recessive (rr × rr) |
| any recessive offspring at all | **both** parents carry at least one recessive allele |

### Worked example 1: turning counts into a ratio

*Two round-seeded pea plants were crossed. The offspring were 296 round and 104 wrinkled. Deduce the parents' genotypes.*

```
Ratio = 296 ÷ 104 : 104 ÷ 104
      = 2.85 : 1
      ≈ 3 : 1
```

A 3 : 1 ratio means both parents are **Rr**. Real counts are close to, but rarely exactly, the expected ratio, because which gametes fuse is a matter of chance. Divide both numbers by the smaller one to find the ratio.

## Interpreting pedigree diagrams (Core)

A pedigree diagram shows a family over several generations. Read the key first: usually squares are males, circles are females and shaded symbols show the characteristic.

### Worked example 2: deducing genotypes in a family

The family is described in words here, as a question would show it in a diagram. A, dominant, gives normal skin colour; a, recessive, gives albinism.

- Person 1 (male) and person 2 (female) are both unaffected. They have two children: 3, an affected daughter, and 4, an unaffected son.
- Person 4 has children with person 5, an affected woman. Their children are 6, unaffected, and 7, affected.

**Step 1: dominant or recessive?** Persons 1 and 2 are unaffected but have an affected child. The allele must be **recessive**, and both parents must carry it.

**Step 2: genotypes.**

| Person | Phenotype | Genotype | Reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1, 2 | unaffected | **Aa** | each passed an a to child 3 |
| 3 | affected | **aa** | recessive phenotype |
| 5 | affected | **aa** | recessive phenotype |
| 4 | unaffected | **Aa** | his child 7 is aa, so he must have passed on an a |
| 6 | unaffected | **Aa** | received a from mother 5 and A from father 4 |
| 7 | affected | **aa** | recessive phenotype |

Notice that person 4's genotype could not be decided from his parents alone (he could be AA or Aa). It was his **child** that settled it. Always look both up and down the pedigree.

## Test crosses (Extended only)

An individual showing the dominant phenotype may be homozygous or heterozygous. Cross it with a **homozygous recessive** individual:

- All offspring dominant → the unknown parent is probably **homozygous dominant**.
- Any recessive offspring → the unknown parent must be **heterozygous**; with large numbers, expect about 1 : 1.

**Why homozygous recessive?** Its gametes all carry the recessive allele, so each offspring's phenotype shows directly which allele came from the unknown parent.

*Example:* a round-seeded plant crossed with a wrinkled plant gives 52 round and 49 wrinkled seeds. The ratio is about 1 : 1, so the round parent is **Rr**.

## Codominance and ABO blood groups (Extended only)

**Codominance**: both alleles in a heterozygous organism contribute to the phenotype. Neither is recessive to the other.

The ABO blood group gene has three alleles: **Iᴬ** and **Iᴮ** are codominant, and both are dominant to **Iᵒ**.

| Phenotype | Possible genotypes |
|---|---|
| A | IᴬIᴬ, IᴬIᵒ |
| B | IᴮIᴮ, IᴮIᵒ |
| AB | IᴬIᴮ |
| O | IᵒIᵒ |

### Worked example 3: an AB × O cross

```
Parents' phenotypes:   group AB        group O
Parents' genotypes:      IᴬIᴮ            IᵒIᵒ
Gametes:               Iᴬ   Iᴮ          Iᵒ   Iᵒ

            Iᴬ        Iᴮ
   Iᵒ     IᴬIᵒ      IᴮIᵒ
   Iᵒ     IᴬIᵒ      IᴮIᵒ

Phenotypes: group A : group B = 1 : 1
```

None of the children can be group AB or group O, even though those are the parents' own groups. A child must receive one allele from each parent, and the group O parent can pass on only Iᵒ.

## Sex linkage (Extended only)

A **sex-linked characteristic** is a feature whose gene is on a **sex chromosome**, which makes it **more common in one sex** than the other. **Red-green colour blindness** is the syllabus example: the gene is on the **X chromosome**, and the Y chromosome carries no allele of it.

### Worked example 4: a colour-blind mother

*A colour-blind woman has children with a man with normal colour vision. Xᴮ is normal vision; Xᵇ is colour blindness.*

```
Parents' genotypes:     XᵇXᵇ              XᴮY
Gametes:               Xᵇ   Xᵇ           Xᴮ   Y

             Xᵇ         Xᵇ
   Xᴮ      XᴮXᵇ       XᴮXᵇ
   Y       XᵇY        XᵇY

Daughters: all XᴮXᵇ, normal vision (carriers)
Sons:      all XᵇY, colour-blind
```

Every son receives his only X chromosome from his mother, so all sons are colour-blind. Every daughter receives the father's Xᴮ, so none is colour-blind. This is why a son's colour vision depends on his mother's alleles, and why males are affected more often: a male needs only one recessive allele.

## Common mistakes

- Writing gametes with two alleles, such as "Rr". A gamete carries **one** allele of each gene.
- Giving the genotype ratio when the phenotype ratio is asked for.
- Expecting real counts to match the ratio exactly.
- Deciding a pedigree genotype from parents only, when a child gives the answer.
- (Extended) Using a heterozygous individual for a test cross. It must be **homozygous recessive**.
- (Extended) Writing blood group alleles as A, B and O, or IᵒIᴬ in a muddled order; give genotypes clearly with the letter I and superscripts.
- (Extended) Leaving out the Y chromosome in sex-linked crosses, or putting an allele on it.

## Exam technique

- **Describe** a term: use the syllabus wording, such as "having two identical alleles of a particular gene".
- **Use a genetic diagram**: include every line, label phenotypes, and state the ratio or probability.
- **Interpret** a pedigree: identify dominant or recessive first, with evidence, then deduce genotypes person by person.
- **Explain** a test cross (Extended): name the cross, give both possible outcomes and what each shows.
- **Calculate** a ratio from counts: divide by the smaller number and round to the nearest expected ratio.
