This guide goes deeper on section 6.4 (Business and ethical issues) of the Cambridge IGCSE Business 0264 syllabus for examination in 2027, 2028 and 2029. Its content statement, 6.4.1 Ethical issues, lists: "ethical issues which may affect businesses, e.g. child labour, paying fair wages to employees, paying fair prices to suppliers, using suppliers who do not damage the environment"; "how businesses may respond to ethical issues"; and "advantages and disadvantages of a business being ethical". The broader [ethical issues and pressure groups guide](/resources/igcse-business-0264-business-and-ethical-issues-and-pressure-groups/) costs a fair wage and a fair supplier price. This page concentrates on the difference between ethics and law, on ethical problems that arise in a business's supply chain, on conflicts between stakeholders, and on reaching a balanced judgement. Revise with the [business and ethical issues revision notes](/resources/cambridge-igcse-business-0264-business-and-ethical-issues-revision-notes/) and practise with the [business and ethical issues practice questions](/resources/cambridge-igcse-business-0264-business-and-ethical-issues-practice/).

## What the syllabus asks for

| Point in 6.4.1 | What you must be able to do |
|---|---|
| Ethical issues, e.g. the four named examples | Recognise an ethical issue in a case and say who is affected |
| How businesses may respond | Describe realistic responses and their effects |
| Advantages and disadvantages of being ethical | Weigh gains against costs for the business in the question |

The four issues are given as examples ("e.g."), so a case study may present a different one, such as honest advertising or the safety of a product. The method for answering stays the same.

## Ethics and the law

**Ethics** are moral principles about what is right and wrong. An **ethical decision** is one a business makes because it is the right thing to do, even if a cheaper or more profitable choice is available.

| | Legal requirement | Ethical choice |
|---|---|---|
| Who sets it | Government, through laws | The business itself |
| What happens if ignored | Fines or legal action | Possible damage to reputation, but no legal penalty |
| Example | Paying at least the legal minimum wage | Paying more than the minimum so workers can live decently |

The line between them moves. Some countries ban practices that others allow, and laws change over time. A business that operates in, or buys from, several countries may find that something legal in one place would be seen as unethical by its customers in another. This is one reason supply chains create ethical problems.

## Ethical issues in the supply chain

A business is not only judged on what it does in its own premises. Customers, the media and pressure groups also judge it on the suppliers it chooses. Two of the syllabus examples, child labour and suppliers who damage the environment, usually arise here.

### Child labour

Child labour means employing children in work that is harmful to them or that stops them going to school. A retailer may never employ a child itself, yet still sell products made by children if a supplier, or a supplier's supplier, does.

### Suppliers who damage the environment

A business may choose a supplier because it is cheap, without asking how its low costs are achieved. If the supplier pollutes rivers or clears forests, the buyer shares responsibility in the eyes of many customers.

### Worked example: Larkspur Footwear

Larkspur Footwear buys 60,000 pairs of shoes a year from Supplier M at $11.40 a pair. An inspection finds that Supplier M employs children in one of its workshops. Supplier N, which is regularly inspected and does not use child labour, offers the same shoes at $12.90 a pair. Larkspur's profit is $420,000 a year.

```
Extra cost per pair = 12.90 - 11.40 = $1.50
Extra cost per year = 1.50 x 60,000 = $90,000
Profit if cost is absorbed = 420,000 - 90,000 = $330,000
Fall in profit = 90,000 / 420,000 x 100 = 21.4%
```

Larkspur has three realistic responses.

| Response | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|
| Switch to Supplier N | Ends the link with child labour quickly; can be publicised | Profit falls by $90,000 unless prices rise; a new supplier relationship carries risk |
| Stay with M, but require it to stop using child labour, and inspect regularly | Keeps the cheaper price; may improve conditions for workers at M | Inspections cost money; M may not change, or may hide the practice |
| Do nothing | No extra cost in the short term | If customers or campaigners find out, sales and reputation may suffer badly |

A subtle point earns credit: simply dropping Supplier M does not necessarily help the children, who may move to worse work elsewhere. Some businesses therefore choose to stay and require change, with a deadline. Either response can be justified if it is explained.

## Conflicts between stakeholders

Ethical decisions usually help some stakeholders and cost others.

| Decision | Stakeholders who gain | Stakeholders who may lose |
|---|---|---|
| Pay employees more than the legal minimum | Employees; possibly customers, through better service | Owners, through lower profit; customers, if prices rise |
| Pay a higher price to a small farm supplier | Supplier and its workers | Owners; customers if prices rise |
| Use a supplier with cleaner production | Local community near the supplier; future generations | Owners; price-sensitive customers |

Good answers name a specific stakeholder, say how they are affected and why. "Shareholders may receive lower dividends because profit falls by $90,000" is far stronger than "it is bad for shareholders".

## How businesses may respond

Responses range from doing the minimum to making ethics part of the brand.

1. **Obey the law and go no further.** Cheapest, but leaves the business open to criticism.
2. **Write an ethical policy** covering wages, suppliers and the environment, so managers have clear rules.
3. **Check suppliers** through inspections, and set deadlines for improvement.
4. **Change suppliers or materials** to ones that meet the policy.
5. **Tell customers** through labels and promotion, but only what is true.
6. **Respond to concerns** raised by employees, customers or campaigners.

## Advantages and disadvantages of being ethical

The advantages and disadvantages depend on the business. Use the case to decide which matter most.

- **Who are the customers?** If they compare prices closely, higher costs and prices may lose sales. If they care about how products are made, being ethical may win sales.
- **How visible is the brand?** A well-known brand has more to lose from a scandal.
- **How large is the extra cost?** A $90,000 rise against a $420,000 profit is significant; a small rise may be easily absorbed.
- **Is the change permanent?** Higher supplier prices continue each year, while benefits such as loyalty may build slowly.

## Annotated model answer

Question: *Consider whether Larkspur Footwear should switch to Supplier N. Justify your answer.*

Model answer: Switching would end Larkspur's link with child labour, which protects its reputation. If the media reported that its shoes were made by children, it could lose far more than $90,000 in sales. It could also promote its shoes as made without child labour, which may attract customers who care about how products are made. However, switching would cut profit by 21.4%, from $420,000 to $330,000, unless Larkspur raised its prices, and some customers may buy cheaper shoes from rivals. On balance Larkspur should switch, because the risk of a scandal is greater than the certain cost, and raising the price by $1.50 a pair on shoes that sell well may recover most of the cost.

Why it earns credit: both sides use the case figures, the judgement follows from the comparison, and a way to reduce the drawback is suggested.

## Exam technique

The command words in the 0264 syllabus include **calculate**, **define**, **identify**, **state**, **outline**, **explain**, **consider** and **justify**. In "consider" questions, weigh at least one advantage and one disadvantage *for the business in the case*, then decide.

## Common errors

- Saying a business must act ethically "because it is the law". Ethics goes beyond the law.
- Ignoring the supply chain: a business can be linked to child labour without employing children itself.
- Writing "good for reputation" without saying how reputation turns into sales or loyalty.
- Treating the extra cost as a one-off when it continues every year.
