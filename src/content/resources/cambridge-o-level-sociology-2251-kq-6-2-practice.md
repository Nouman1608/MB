These questions practise key question 6.2 of Cambridge O Level Sociology (2251), *What are the
patterns of and explanations for crime and deviance?* Set A follows the six-part (a)–(f) structure the
syllabus describes for each Paper 2 question; Set B gives extra practice on individual explanations
and theories.

## Set A — Patterns and explanations

**(a)** Define the terms:
- (i) *status frustration* **[2]**
- (ii) *social exclusion* **[2]**

**(b)** Give **two** of Merton's adaptations to strain. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain **three** reasons why young people may be over-represented in crime statistics. **[6]**

**(d)** Explain **three** ways Marxists explain crime. **[6]**

**(e)** Discuss feminist explanations of gender differences in crime. **[8]**

**(f)** Evaluate the view that crime is mainly committed by the working class. **[14]**

## Set B — Extra practice

**(g)** Explain how a moral panic may lead to deviancy amplification. **[4]**

**(h)** Explain **two** of Cloward and Ohlin's types of subculture. **[4]**

**(i)** Explain **two** criticisms of the chivalry thesis. **[4]**

**(j)** Explain **two** ways the postmodernist view differs from other explanations of crime. **[4]**

## Answers — Set A

**(a)(i) [2]** Frustration felt by people, often working-class boys, who cannot achieve status by
legitimate means such as school success (1), which may lead them to form a subculture that gives
status for deviance (1) (Albert Cohen, 1955).

**(a)(ii) [2]** Being cut off from mainstream society (1), for example through unemployment, poor
education or lack of access to services, which weakens a person's stake in conforming (1).

**(b) [2]** Any two, 1 mark each: conformity; innovation; ritualism; retreatism; rebellion.

**(c) [6]** Three reasons, 2 marks each (point plus development):
- **Police targeting / stop and search** in public spaces where young people gather means more of
  their offences are detected.
- **Stereotyping** of youth as troublemakers leads to suspicion and arrest.
- **Self-fulfilling prophecy**: young people labelled as trouble may accept the label and offend
  further.
- **Status frustration**: school failure leads to subcultures that reward deviance.
- **Lack of opportunity**: youth unemployment blocks legitimate routes to income.
- **Gang culture** offers status, belonging and protection.
- **Weaker social control** as young people move away from family supervision before taking on adult
  responsibilities.

**(d) [6]** Three ways, 2 marks each:
- **Capitalism is criminogenic**: it encourages greed and competition, so crime occurs in all classes.
- **Laws protect the ruling class**: most laws protect private property.
- **Selective law enforcement**: working-class crime is policed heavily; crimes of the powerful are
  rarely prosecuted (Box, 1983).
- **Ideological role**: moral panics divert attention from economic problems (Hall et al., 1978).
- **Deprivation**: inequality pushes poorer people towards property crime.

**(e) [8]** Levels: 7–8 clear, developed, both sides with evaluation; 4–6 some development; 1–3
basic.
- **Control:** Heidensohn (1985): women controlled at home, in public and at work, so they have fewer
  opportunities to offend.
- **Class and gender deals:** Carlen (1988): women offend when the rewards of conformity are not
  available.
- **Male violence:** patriarchy and misogyny explain violence against women.
- **Evaluation:** explains the gender gap well; but may treat all women as the same; female crime has
  risen in some societies; masculinity explanations (Messerschmidt, 1993) and the chivalry thesis
  offer alternatives.

**(f) [14]** Levels: 11–14 balanced, developed, evaluated with a conclusion; 7–10 developed but
less balanced; 4–6 limited; 1–3 basic.

*For the view:*
- Official statistics show working-class people over-represented in convictions.
- Merton (1938): innovation when legitimate means are blocked.
- Status frustration (A. Cohen, 1955) and subcultures (Cloward and Ohlin, 1960).
- Deprivation and social exclusion.
- New Right: inadequate socialisation in an underclass.

*Against the view:*
- Box (1983): crimes of the powerful are widespread and more harmful but rarely punished.
- White-collar and corporate crime (Sutherland) is under-recorded.
- Police targeting of poorer areas inflates working-class figures.
- Self-report studies suggest offending is more widespread across classes.
- Labelling: stereotypes of the "typical criminal" shape arrests.

*Conclusion:* working-class people are more visible in the statistics, but crime is committed by all
classes. Credit a reasoned judgement.

## Answers — Set B

**(g) [4]** Media exaggerate a group's deviance, presenting them as folk devils (1), which creates
public concern and pressure for action (1). Police and courts respond with tougher control, which
leads to more arrests and may provoke further deviance by the group (1). The cycle continues, as in
Stanley Cohen's study of mods and rockers (1972) (1).

**(h) [4]** Any two, 2 marks each: **criminal** subcultures, where adult crime networks give young
people illegitimate opportunities to earn; **conflict** subcultures, where no such networks exist,
so gangs use violence to gain status; **retreatist** subcultures, for those who fail in both
legitimate and illegitimate routes, often involving drug use.

**(i) [4]** Two criticisms, 2 marks each: women who break gender norms may be treated more harshly
rather than more leniently; men's and women's offences differ in seriousness, so different sentences
may be justified; self-report studies suggest the gender gap is real, not just a product of lenient
treatment; it is based on older evidence.

**(j) [4]** Two ways, 2 marks each: rejects a single cause of crime; stresses pleasure, thrill and
identity rather than structure (Katz, 1988; Lyng, 1990); focuses on new forms of crime such as cyber
crime; sees crime as an individual choice in a consumer society rather than the result of class or
strain.

**(k) [4]** Two patterns, 2 marks each: young men are frequent victims of street violence; poorer
neighbourhoods suffer more burglary and property crime; women are the main victims of domestic and
sexual violence; minority groups are targets of hate crime; repeat victimisation concentrates risk on
some people and places.

**(l) [8]** Levels as for A(e).
- *Explanation:* an underclass with weak families, absent fathers and welfare dependency fails to
  socialise children (Murray); lack of male role models; crime as a result of inadequate
  socialisation.
- *Evaluation:* links family and crime; but blames the poor rather than structural causes such as
  unemployment; ignores crimes of the powerful; lone-parent families vary widely; Marxists and
  feminists reject the view.

## Frequently asked questions

**How do I avoid mixing up the two Cohens?** Albert Cohen wrote on status frustration and
subcultures (1955); Stanley Cohen wrote on moral panics and folk devils (1972).

**Should I always question the statistics?** Yes, for any pattern question. Show that statistics may
reflect policing as well as offending.

**Which theory is best for corporate crime?** Marxism, with Box's work on crimes of the powerful.
