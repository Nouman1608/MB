## Definitions

| Term | Meaning for Paper 1 |
|---|---|
| Text type | The kind of text: letter, blog post, report, speech, article and so on |
| Conventions | The features a reader expects of that text type: layout, opening, closing, register |
| Register | How formal or informal the language is |
| Context | Where and why the text exists (a magazine, a council, a club newsletter) |
| Audience | Who reads it, and your relationship to them |
| Purpose | What the text must do: inform, persuade, request, complain, advise, reflect |
| Meaning | The main message the reader should take away |
| Variation | How language changes by region, group and situation |

Context, audience, purpose, meaning and variation are the five conceptual understandings the brief
names for the course.

## Register markers at a glance

| Marker | Informal | Formal |
|---|---|---|
| Form of address | Familiar "you" (*tu*, *du*, *tú*) | Polite "you" (*vous*, *Sie*, *usted*) |
| Greeting | First name, "Hi" | Title and surname, formal formula |
| Vocabulary | Everyday, slang, emotive | Precise, impersonal |
| Sentences | Fragments, questions, exclamations | Complete, linked, measured |
| Requests | "Let me know!" | "I would be grateful if you could…" |
| Closing | Friendly sign-off | Fixed formal formula |

Neutral register sits between the two: clear, standard language for a wide public.

## Text-type families

This page groups text types by audience. Learn the list your teacher gives you.

| Family | Reader | Examples | Must-show conventions |
|---|---|---|---|
| Personal | Yourself or people you know | Diary, personal email or letter, personal blog post | Date or greeting; first person; feelings; informal to neutral |
| Professional | An organisation, or you in a working role | Formal letter, formal email, report, proposal | Purpose stated early; one point per paragraph; formal formulas; headings in reports |
| Mass media | A wide public | Article, opinion piece, speech, leaflet, review | Headline or greeting to audience; neutral register; devices that engage many readers |

## Method in steps: decoding a task

1. Read all three tasks to the end.
2. For each, underline: **role**, **reader**, **purpose**, **text type**, **content points**.
3. Ask: do I control this text type? Do I have the topic vocabulary?
4. Choose the task where both answers are yes, and commit.

## Method in steps: planning and checking

1. Write the one-sentence **meaning** you want the reader to take away.
2. Set the **register** from the reader, and fix your form of address.
3. Plan one paragraph per point, in an order that suits the purpose.
4. Draft the **opening** and **closing** first: they carry most conventions.
5. Write, then check: every content point covered; register held to the end; conventions visible
   throughout, not only at the start.
6. HL: check that you weigh, evaluate or answer an objection, not only describe.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Audience vs purpose.** Audience is who reads; purpose is what the text must do to them. A
  letter to a manager (audience) can complain, request or apply (purpose).
- **Formal letter vs formal email.** Both use a formal salutation and close. The email has a subject
  line, is shorter, and usually drops postal addresses.
- **Article vs blog post.** An article is usually third person and neutral, with a headline and
  quotations. A blog post is first person, more personal, and invites comments.
- **Report vs proposal.** A report sets out findings and recommendations about what happened. A
  proposal argues for something that should happen, with a plan and benefits.
- **Speech vs essay.** A speech greets an audience, signposts for listeners, uses spoken devices and
  ends with thanks or an appeal. An essay does none of these.
- **Review vs article.** A review judges something (a film, a restaurant, an app) and gives a
  verdict. A feature article explores a subject without needing a verdict.
- **Neutral vs formal.** Neutral is for the wide public: clear and polite. Formal is for a reader in
  authority: fixed formulas, polite address, impersonal phrasing.

## Worked reminders

**One set of facts, two text types.** A school ran a week without single-use plastic (Sharing the
planet).

- **Blog post:** "Day three and I cracked: I bought a bottle of water. Has anyone else found this
  harder than they expected?" First person, informal, a question to readers.
- **Report:** "Aim: to measure the effect of the plastic-free week. Findings: most classes reduced
  their waste. Recommendation: repeat the week each term." Headings, impersonal, recommendations.

**One slip, whole text.** "Dear Mr Hale, I am writing to apply for the summer post… Anyway, hope
to hear from you soon!" The last line is informal. Replace with: "I look forward to hearing from
you."

**From describing to weighing (HL).** Describing: "The new timetable starts at eight. Lessons are
longer." Weighing: "Although an earlier start may tire some students, longer lessons would give us
time for the practical work we currently rush." The concession ("although…") and the conditional
("would give") show the complex structures and higher-order thinking HL tasks demand.

## Target-language checklist

Before the exam, write these down in your target language and learn them:

- A formal opening and closing formula for letters and emails.
- The polite and familiar forms of "you", and the verb forms and possessives that go with each.
- Three ways to state purpose: "I am writing to…", "The aim of this report is…", "Today I want to
  talk about…".
- Three polite request structures, one using a conditional.
- Five connectives for a formal text (however, furthermore, therefore, on the other hand, finally).
- Two structures of concession ("although…", "even if…") for HL-style argument.

## Quick self-test

1. How many writing tasks do you choose from in Paper 1, and how many do you complete?
2. What is Paper 1 worth, and how many marks is it out of?
3. Name the five conceptual understandings the brief lists.
4. What two things does the brief say HL Paper 1 tasks demand, beyond a higher word range?
5. A task asks you to write to a local council about bus services. Which register, and why?
6. Give two conventions of a formal letter's opening paragraph.
7. What is the main difference in purpose between a report and a proposal?
8. Name two conventions that show a text is a speech.
9. A blog post opens "The aim of this text is to inform readers about…". What is wrong?
10. Which part of a text carries most of its conventions?
11. In a formal letter in French, which form of "you" do you use?

### Answers

1. A choice of three; you complete one.
2. 25% of the final grade; 30 marks.
3. Context, audience, purpose, meaning, variation.
4. More complex language and structures, and higher-order thinking skills.
5. Formal: the reader is an official body you do not know, and the purpose (a complaint or request)
   needs a polite, measured tone.
6. Any two: formal salutation; purpose stated in the first sentence; polite form of address; context
   for writing (where you saw the notice, why you are writing now).
7. A report sets out findings about what happened, with recommendations; a proposal argues for
   something that should happen, with a plan.
8. Any two: greeting to the audience; signposting for listeners; rhetorical questions; inclusive
   "we"; thanks or an appeal at the end.
9. The register and opening belong to a report. A blog post needs an engaging, personal first line.
10. The opening and the closing.
11. *Vous*.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing the right content in the wrong text type: an essay when a speech or leaflet was asked for.
- Setting the register at the start and losing it by the last paragraph, especially in the closing
  line of a formal letter.
- Mixing polite and familiar forms of "you" in the same text, including in verb endings.
- Showing conventions only in the heading, then writing plain paragraphs with no sign of the type.
- Covering one content point and forgetting the second one in the task.
- Choosing a task for its topic without the vocabulary to write about it.
- Translating English formulas word for word instead of using the target-language ones.
- At HL, describing or reporting without weighing, evaluating or answering an objection.
