This guide goes deeper on **5.2 Sources of finance**, part of AS Level topic 5 (Finance and accounting) in Cambridge International AS & A Level Business 9609, for examination in 2026, 2027 and 2028. The syllabus content is:

- **5.2.1 Business ownership and sources of finance**: the relationship between the form of business ownership and the availability of sources of finance.
- **5.2.2 Internal and external sources of finance**: internal sources (owners' investment, retained earnings, sale of unwanted assets, sale and leaseback of non-current assets, working capital) and external sources (share capital, debentures, new partners, venture capital, bank overdrafts, leasing, hire purchase, bank loans, mortgages, debt factoring, trade credit, micro-finance, crowd funding and government grants).
- **5.2.3 Factors affecting the sources of finance**: cost, flexibility, need to retain control, the use to which it is put, level of existing debt.
- **5.2.4 Selecting the source of finance**: the appropriateness of each possible source in a given situation.

## Ownership decides which doors are open

| Form of ownership | Sources typically available | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Sole trader | Owner's savings, retained earnings, overdraft, small bank loans, trade credit, leasing, hire purchase, micro-finance, crowd funding, grants | No shares to sell; unlimited liability and dependence on one person make lenders cautious |
| Partnership | As above, plus new partners | A new partner brings capital but shares profit and control |
| Private limited company | As above, plus shares sold privately (to family, friends, employees) and venture capital | Limited liability reassures investors; shares cannot be offered to the general public |
| Public limited company | As above, plus shares sold to the public and debentures | Stock-market listing and larger size give access to large sums |

Two points add depth. First, **limited liability** makes investors more willing to provide capital, because they can lose only what they put in. Second, **size and track record** matter as much as legal form: a long-established sole trader with reliable profits may borrow more easily than a new limited company with no trading history.

## Comparing sources that look alike

Many exam marks come from choosing between two similar options. Learn the distinctions.

**Overdraft or bank loan?**
- Overdraft: flexible, interest only on the amount used, but usually a higher rate and can be withdrawn at short notice. Suits fluctuating short-term needs.
- Loan: fixed sum and repayment schedule, usually a lower rate, but interest is paid on the whole sum whether or not it is needed. Suits a known, longer-term need.

**Leasing or hire purchase?**
- Leasing: the business never owns the asset; maintenance is often included; easy to upgrade. Suits equipment that quickly becomes outdated.
- Hire purchase: the business owns the asset after the final payment; total cost includes interest. Suits assets with a long useful life.

**Worked comparison.** Arden Labs needs a $30 000 analyser for three years.

| | Lease | Hire purchase |
|---|---|---|
| Payments | 36 × $850 | $6 000 deposit + 36 × $780 |
| Total paid | $30 600 | $34 080 |
| Owned at end? | No | Yes |
| Maintenance | Included | Arden pays |

Leasing costs $3 480 less over three years and includes maintenance, but Arden owns nothing at the end. If the analyser will still be useful after three years, hire purchase may be better value; if technology changes fast, leasing avoids being stuck with an outdated machine.

**Share capital or debentures?**
- Shares: no interest and no repayment, but new owners share control and future profits (dividends).
- Debentures: long-term loans with fixed interest and a repayment date; no loss of control, but interest must be paid even in bad years.

**Venture capital or crowd funding?**
- Venture capital: large sums and business expertise from specialist investors, but they take a significant share and influence over decisions.
- Crowd funding: many small investors or backers; can also test demand and build publicity. Success is uncertain, and the idea is exposed publicly.

**Micro-finance or bank loan?**
- Micro-finance provides small loans to people who cannot get ordinary bank loans, often because they lack security or a credit record. It suits very small enterprises, especially in developing economies.

**Government grant**
- No repayment, but conditions apply (for example, creating jobs in a region or investing in clean technology), the amount is limited, and applications take time.

## Internal sources: hidden costs

Internal finance is often described as "free", but each source has a cost.

- **Retained earnings**: owners give up dividends now; the opportunity cost is what the money could earn elsewhere.
- **Sale of unwanted assets**: one-off; assets sold quickly may fetch less than their value.
- **Sale and leaseback**: brings a large sum, but the business pays rent for years and loses any rise in the property's value.
- **Working capital**: cutting inventory or chasing customers releases cash, but too little inventory risks lost sales, and pressure on customers may lose goodwill.
- **Owners' investment**: puts personal savings at risk, and the amount is limited by what the owner has.

## The five choice factors, applied

5.2.3 lists five factors. Treat them as a checklist when recommending a source.

1. **Cost**: interest, fees, dividends, or the discount given to a factor.
2. **Flexibility**: can the amount or the repayments change?
3. **Need to retain control**: will owners accept new shareholders or partners?
4. **Use to which it is put**: long-term assets need long-term finance; short-term gaps need short-term finance.
5. **Level of existing debt**: heavily indebted firms find borrowing expensive or impossible and may need equity or internal sources.

### Applying the checklist: four scenarios

| Situation | Suitable source | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| A café owner needs $4 000 for a month while waiting for a catering client to pay | Overdraft | Short, uncertain need; pay interest only on the amount used |
| A family-owned private limited company needs $2 million for a new factory but wants to keep control | Bank loan or mortgage | Long-term use; no new shareholders; property can secure the loan |
| A plc already heavily in debt needs $50 million to expand abroad | Share issue | Adding debt would raise interest costs and risk; shares need no repayment |
| A new app developer with no profits or assets needs $500 000 | Venture capital | Banks unlikely to lend without security or a track record; investors accept risk for a share of future value |

## Combining sources

Large projects are often financed from more than one source. A hotel extension might be funded by a mortgage on the building, hire purchase for kitchen equipment and retained earnings for furnishings. Recommending a combination, matched to each element's use, is a strong evaluative point.

## Annotated model answer

*Question: Advise Tilly, a sole trader florist, on the best source of finance for a $12 000 delivery van.*

> A van is used for several years, so Tilly needs medium- to long-term finance rather than an overdraft. *(Use to which it is put.)* As a sole trader she cannot sell shares, so her main options are her savings, a bank loan, leasing or hire purchase. *(Ownership limits the choice.)* Hire purchase spreads the cost and gives her ownership after the last payment, and the van itself secures the agreement, which suits a small business with limited assets. *(Justified choice.)* However, it adds interest to the total cost; if Tilly has savings she could use them and avoid interest, though this leaves her with less cash for emergencies. *(Counter-point.)* I would advise hire purchase unless her savings are large enough to cover the van and still leave a cash buffer. *(Recommendation with condition.)*

## Common mistakes

- Recommending a share issue to a sole trader or partnership.
- Choosing an overdraft for a long-term asset.
- Calling retained earnings "free".
- Confusing leasing (never owned) with hire purchase (owned at the end).
- Listing sources without using the case details to choose between them.

## Exam technique: command words

- **Identify**: name an internal or external source.
- **Explain**: give a feature of the source and how it affects the business.
- **Analyse**: link the source to cost, control or risk for this business.
- **Evaluate** or **Assess**: compare at least two suitable sources against the five factors and decide.
- **Advise** or **Justify**: recommend one source (or a combination) for the business in the case, with reasons drawn from it.
