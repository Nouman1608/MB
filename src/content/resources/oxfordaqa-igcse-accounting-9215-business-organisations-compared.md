Section 3.4 of the OxfordAQA International GCSE Accounting (9215) specification asks for "the advantages and disadvantages of different organisations", listing sole traders, partnerships and limited companies. The same section later asks you to "distinguish between share capital and loan capital" for limited companies, which is often what decides a comparison question. The [sole traders' financial statements and comparison guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-accounting-sole-traders-financial-statements-and-comparison-with-o/) gives a short summary table. This guide goes further: the features behind each advantage, how ownership and profit look different in each set of accounts, and how to build an answer to a scenario. Figures are invented and in dollars.

Recap with the [business organisations revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-accounting-9215-business-organisations-compared-revision-notes/) and test yourself with the [business organisations practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-accounting-9215-business-organisations-compared-practice/).

## Six features that drive every comparison

Most advantages and disadvantages come from six features. Learn the features and you can work out the points for any scenario.

| Feature | Sole trader | Partnership | Limited company |
|---|---|---|---|
| Owners | One person | Two or more partners | Shareholders |
| Liability for debts | Unlimited | Unlimited for ordinary partners | Limited to the amount invested in shares |
| Main sources of long-term finance | Owner's savings, bank loans | Partners' capital, bank loans | Issuing shares, loan capital such as debentures, bank loans |
| Control and decisions | Owner alone | Shared between partners | Directors manage for the shareholders |
| What happens to profit | All belongs to the owner | Shared according to the partnership agreement | Kept as retained earnings or paid as dividends |
| Continuity | Depends on the owner | Affected when a partner leaves | Continues when shareholders change |

The business entity concept applies to all three in the accounts: the business is recorded separately from its owners. Only the limited company is also legally separate from its owners, which is why the shareholders' liability can be limited.

## Unlimited and limited liability

Unlimited liability means that if the business cannot pay its debts, the owner's personal possessions, such as a house or car, can be used to pay them. For a sole trader or ordinary partner, the risk of losing more than they put in is the main disadvantage.

Limited liability means a shareholder can lose only the amount paid, or agreed to be paid, for their shares. This makes investors more willing to provide capital. The flip side for lenders and suppliers is that they cannot claim from the shareholders personally, so they may ask for security before lending to a small company.

## Share capital and loan capital

| | Share capital | Loan capital (for example, debentures) |
|---|---|---|
| Provided by | Shareholders, who become owners | Lenders, who are not owners |
| Return | Dividends, paid only if the directors decide to and there are profits | Interest, which must be paid whether or not there is a profit |
| Repayment | Not normally repaid while the company continues | Repaid on an agreed date |
| Control | Ordinary shareholders vote | No vote |
| In the statement of financial position | Equity | Non-current liability (current liability once repayable within a year) |
| In the income statement | Dividends are not an expense | Interest is an expense |

## How ownership looks in each set of accounts

The clearest way to see the differences is to compare the bottom part of each statement of financial position. These three businesses are invented.

**Sole trader: Rina Park, florist**

| Capital | |
|---|---|
| Opening capital | 40,000 |
| Add profit for the year | 18,000 |
| Less drawings | (11,000) |
| Closing capital | 47,000 |

**Partnership: Mensah and Lowe, opticians**

| | Mensah | Lowe | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| Capital accounts | 30,000 | 20,000 | 50,000 |
| Current accounts | 3,500 | 1,500 | 5,000 |
| Total | | | 55,000 |

Each partner's capital account usually stays fixed. Shares of profit, interest on capital and partnership salaries are credited to the current account, and drawings and interest on drawings are debited to it.

**Limited company: Northgate Tiles Ltd**

| Equity | |
|---|---|
| Ordinary share capital (100,000 shares of 1 each) | 100,000 |
| Retained earnings | 26,000 |
| Total equity | 126,000 |
| Non-current liabilities: 8% debentures | 40,000 |

The debenture holders receive interest of 8% × 40,000 = 3,200 a year, an expense in the income statement. Dividends to shareholders come out of retained earnings and are not an expense.

## Advantages and disadvantages in context

**Sole trader.** Advantages: keeps all the profit, makes quick decisions, needs few formalities to start, and its affairs are private. Disadvantages: unlimited liability, finance limited to personal savings and what a bank will lend, long hours with no one to share the work, and the business may end if the owner is ill or dies.

**Partnership.** Advantages: more capital from several partners, a wider range of skills, and work and losses are shared. Disadvantages: unlimited liability for ordinary partners, and each partner may be bound by the decisions of the others. Profit must be shared, and disagreements can damage the business. A written agreement, which sets out profit sharing, interest on capital and salaries, reduces disputes.

**Limited company.** Advantages: limited liability, the ability to raise large sums from many shareholders and from loan capital, and continuity. Disadvantages: more legal rules and paperwork, which cost time and money; original owners may lose control as more shares are issued; and profit is shared among more people.

## Worked example: an "assess" answer

*Kofi runs a successful bicycle repair shop as a sole trader. He wants 60,000 to open a second shop. His friend Adaeze has offered to invest 30,000 and work in the business. Assess whether Kofi should form a partnership with Adaeze or a limited company.*

A strong answer applies features to Kofi, weighs both sides and reaches a judgement.

1. **Partnership, for**: Adaeze's 30,000 covers half the cost, and she adds labour for the second shop. Setting up is simple and cheap.
2. **Partnership, against**: Kofi would still have unlimited liability, now for debts that Adaeze's decisions might create. He must share profit.
3. **Company, for**: both would have limited liability, which matters more now the business is borrowing to expand. Shares could be issued to Adaeze in return for her 30,000, and the remaining 30,000 might come from a loan or more shares.
4. **Company, against**: more rules and costs, and if shares are sold to outsiders Kofi could lose control.
5. **Judgement**: if the second shop is risky and needs borrowing, a limited company protects both owners' personal assets and is the better choice. If the expansion is small and they trust each other, a partnership with a written agreement is cheaper and simpler.

Notice that every point names Kofi, Adaeze or the 60,000. Generic lists without application gain few marks in an assess question.

## Common mistakes

- Saying a sole trader "has no capital". The owner's capital is shown in the statement of financial position.
- Describing dividends as an expense.
- Treating loan capital as part of equity.
- Claiming partners have limited liability without saying the partnership type allows it. In an ordinary partnership liability is unlimited.
- Listing advantages without linking them to the business in the question.

## Exam technique

- **State** or **identify**: one feature or one advantage, briefly.
- **Explain**: give the point and its consequence, for example "limited liability, so shareholders only risk the amount paid for their shares, making it easier to attract investors".
- **Assess** or **evaluate**: points on both sides applied to the case, followed by a supported judgement.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why can a limited company raise more finance than a sole trader?**
It can sell shares to many investors, who are attracted by limited liability, and it can issue loan capital such as debentures. A sole trader relies mainly on personal savings and bank borrowing.

**Is a dividend the same as drawings?**
Both take value out of the business for the owners, but drawings are taken by a sole trader or partner, while dividends are paid to shareholders after the directors decide on them. Neither is an expense.
