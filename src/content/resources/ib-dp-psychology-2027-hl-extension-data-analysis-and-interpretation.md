Data analysis and interpretation is listed with the HL extensions in the IB Psychology guide for first assessment 2027, but there is a difference. The guide says it "should be studied by SL and HL students but it is **not** assessed at SL". For **HL students** it is assessed in **Paper 3** (four source-based questions, 30 marks, 1 hour 45 minutes) and it supports the internal assessment. This page is therefore HL-focused, though SL students will find it useful for Paper 2 and the research proposal. It is the skills strand that sits behind the culture, motivation and technology extensions.

Related: [revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-psychology-2027-hl-extension-data-analysis-and-interpretation-revision-notes/) and [practice set](/resources/ib-dp-psychology-2027-hl-extension-data-analysis-and-interpretation-practice/).

## What the guide requires

The guide's Table of research methodology terms gives the vocabulary and the learning objectives. For data analysis and interpretation the terms are:

- **Representations:** bar graph, box and whisker plot, frequency table, histogram, line graph, scatterplot.
- **Distributions:** for example normal distribution and skewness; outliers.
- **Statistics:** descriptive statistics, inferential statistics, correlation coefficient.
- **Qualitative analysis:** thematic analysis.

The learning objectives are to:

1. understand that data are represented and analysed in different forms depending on the design of the study and the nature of the data;
2. analyse and interpret different types of data tables, graphs and results;
3. analyse and interpret descriptive and inferential statistics;
4. understand the stages of thematic analysis, in which patterns in textual data are found and grouped into themes relevant to the research aim.

The guide also says the related terms in the research methodology section (research methods, sampling, reliability and validity, generalisability, reflexivity, transferability and credibility) should be familiar to HL students, because they are used to argue about research in Paper 3 and the internal assessment. You are asked to **interpret** statistics, not to calculate complex tests from raw data.

## Choosing and reading displays

| Display | Used for | What to look for |
|---|---|---|
| Frequency table | Counts of each value or category | The most frequent value, gaps, spread |
| Bar graph | Comparing categories or group means | Axis start (a truncated axis exaggerates differences), error bars if shown |
| Histogram | Distribution of one continuous variable in bins | Shape, centre, spread, outliers |
| Line graph | Change over time or across ordered conditions | Trend, turning points, whether lines cross |
| Scatterplot | Relationship between two variables | Direction, strength, outliers, curved pattern |
| Box and whisker plot | Median, quartiles and range | Median line, box (middle half), whiskers, outliers |

Always check the axes, units, sample size, the scale of the y-axis and what the error bars mean before interpreting.

## Descriptive statistics

Descriptive statistics summarise the data you have.

- **Mean:** the sum divided by the number of values. Uses all the data but is pulled by outliers.
- **Median:** the middle value when ordered. Not affected by outliers.
- **Mode:** the most frequent value.
- **Range:** highest minus lowest. Simple but depends on two values.
- **Standard deviation (SD):** the typical distance of values from the mean. A larger SD means more spread.

### Worked example: calculating and comparing

A constructed experiment gives memory scores (out of 30) for two groups of 10 participants.

- Group A: 14, 15, 16, 16, 17, 18, 18, 19, 20, 26
- Group B: 10, 12, 13, 14, 14, 15, 16, 16, 17, 18

**Group A.** The sum is 179, so the mean is 179 divided by 10, which is **17.9**. The 5th and 6th values are 17 and 18, so the median is **17.5**. The mode is 16 and 18 (each appears twice, so the data are bimodal). The range is 26 minus 14, which is **12**. The sample standard deviation is about **3.4**.

**Group B.** The sum is 145, so the mean is **14.5**. The median is the average of 14 and 15, which is **14.5**. The values 14 and 16 each appear twice. The range is 18 minus 10, which is **8**. The sample standard deviation is about **2.4**.

**Interpretation.** Group A scored higher on average (17.9 against 14.5). Group A also has a larger spread. The score of 26 is much higher than the rest of Group A. Without it, the mean of Group A would fall to 17.0 and the range to 6, but the median would hardly move (17). This shows why the median is more resistant to an outlier than the mean.

### Box plot from the same data

Using the median of each half of the data:

| | Lowest | Lower quartile | Median | Upper quartile | Highest |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Group A | 14 | 16 | 17.5 | 19 | 26 |
| Group B | 10 | 13 | 14.5 | 16 | 18 |

The middle half of Group A (16 to 19) sits higher than the middle half of Group B (13 to 16). The interquartile range is 3 in both groups. A common rule marks as an outlier any value more than 1.5 times the interquartile range above the upper quartile; here 19 plus 4.5 is 23.5, so 26 is flagged as an outlier.

## Distributions

- A **normal distribution** is symmetrical and bell-shaped, with mean, median and mode close together.
- **Positive skew:** a long tail to the right; the mean is pulled above the median. For Group A the mean (17.9) is above the median (17.5), a small positive skew caused by the outlier.
- **Negative skew:** a long tail to the left; the mean is below the median.
- Skewed data suggest that the median is a better measure of the centre than the mean.
- **Outliers** should be checked rather than automatically deleted. They may be data entry errors, a different kind of participant or genuine variation.

## Correlation

A **correlation coefficient** (r) measures the strength and direction of a linear relationship between two variables, from -1 to +1.

- The sign shows the direction: positive means both rise together; negative means one rises as the other falls.
- The size shows strength: values near 0 are weak, values near ±1 are strong. A rough guide is below 0.3 weak, 0.3 to 0.6 moderate, above 0.6 strong.
- Squaring r gives the proportion of variation shared. If r = 0.30 the shared variation is 0.09, which is 9 per cent.

### Worked example

Eight students reported their hours of daily social media use (1, 2, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7) and their well-being score (78, 75, 72, 70, 65, 60, 58, 52). The mean hours are 3.75 and the mean score is 66.25. The correlation coefficient is **r = -0.99**, a very strong negative relationship. Squared, this is 0.99, so the two variables share nearly all their variation in this small sample.

But interpretation matters more than the number. The sample is only eight students. The relationship says nothing about **cause**: low well-being may lead to more use, or a third variable may affect both. A scatterplot should be checked, because one outlier can create or hide a correlation and a curved relationship gives a low r even when the variables are related.

## Inferential statistics

Inferential statistics use sample data to judge whether a result could be due to chance, and so whether it can be generalised.

- A **null hypothesis** says there is no difference or relationship in the population. The result is tested against it.
- The **p-value** is the probability of getting a result at least this extreme if the null hypothesis were true. By convention, p below 0.05 is called statistically significant.
- A **type I error** is rejecting a true null hypothesis (a false positive). A **type II error** is failing to reject a false null hypothesis (a false negative).
- **Significance is not importance.** A big sample can make a tiny difference significant. Report the size of the effect as well.
- **Confidence** also depends on design: a significant result from a poorly controlled study still cannot show a cause.

### Worked example

For Groups A and B above, an independent-samples t-test gives t(18) = 2.59. The critical value of t for 18 degrees of freedom at the 0.05 level (two-tailed) is 2.10, and at the 0.01 level it is 2.88. As 2.59 is greater than 2.10 but less than 2.88, the result is significant at the 0.05 level but not at 0.01. So p is below 0.05 (and above 0.01). The researcher may reject the null hypothesis of no difference between groups, accepting a risk of under 5 per cent of a type I error. The groups are small (10 each), and there is an outlier in Group A, so the conclusion should be careful.

You will not be asked to run this test in Paper 3. You may be given a statistic and asked to say what it means and what it does not mean.

## Thematic analysis

Thematic analysis finds patterns of meaning in qualitative data such as interview transcripts. The guide asks you to understand the stages that move from text to themes. A common sequence has six steps.

1. **Familiarisation:** read and reread the data; note first impressions.
2. **Coding:** label parts of the text that relate to the research aim (for example "avoiding conflict", "fear of judgment").
3. **Searching for themes:** group related codes into candidate themes.
4. **Reviewing themes:** check the themes against the coded extracts and the whole data set; merge, split or drop themes.
5. **Defining and naming themes:** state what each theme is about and why it matters for the aim.
6. **Reporting:** write the analysis with extracts as evidence.

**Credibility** in thematic analysis comes from reflexivity (the researcher examining their own influence), triangulation, a second coder (inter-rater reliability), checking interpretations with participants and keeping an audit trail. **Transferability** depends on a detailed description of the context.

## Interpreting data in Paper 3

The four questions test interpretation of graphs, data analysis, research considerations and synthesis. Use a routine.

1. **Read the source:** title, variables, units, sample, method.
2. **Describe:** state the main values or pattern with numbers.
3. **Compare:** use differences and spread, not only the means.
4. **Conclude:** state a conclusion that goes no further than the design allows.
5. **Evaluate:** name a limitation tied to the source (sampling, measure, design).
6. **Link:** connect to the extension (culture, motivation or technology) and to the concepts.

## Common mistakes

- Reading a bar graph without checking the axis.
- Comparing means without looking at the SD or the sample sizes.
- Treating a correlation as proof of cause.
- Saying that a result is "significant" when it means large or important.
- Describing thematic analysis as counting words.
- Concluding about a whole population from a small, unrepresentative sample.
