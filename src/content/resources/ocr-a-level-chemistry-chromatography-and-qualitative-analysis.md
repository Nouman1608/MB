## How chromatography separates a mixture

Every chromatography method has two phases:

- a **stationary phase**, which stays put;
- a **mobile phase**, which moves through or over the stationary phase and carries the mixture with it.

Components held more strongly by the stationary phase move more slowly, so the mixture spreads out.

## 6.3.1(a): One-way thin-layer chromatography

In TLC the stationary phase is a thin layer of a solid, usually silica gel or alumina, on a glass, metal or plastic sheet. The mobile phase is a solvent. Components are held on the surface of the solid by **adsorption**.

"One-way" means the plate is developed once, in one direction, with one solvent.

**Running a plate (PAG6)**

1. Mark a baseline in pencil roughly 1 cm above the lower edge (ink would run).
2. Spot the sample and pure references on the line with a fine capillary.
3. Stand the plate in a covered tank, solvent below the baseline.
4. Remove the plate before the solvent reaches the top and mark the solvent front at once.
5. Locate colourless spots under an ultraviolet lamp or with iodine vapour, and ring them in pencil.

**Rf value**

Rf = distance moved by the component ÷ distance moved by the solvent front

Measure both from the baseline; for the spot, use its centre. Rf is a ratio, so it has no units, and it can never exceed 1.

On a polar silica plate, a more polar component is adsorbed more strongly and has a **lower** Rf. Rf depends on the plate, solvent and temperature, so run references on the same plate.

### Worked example 1: following a reduction by TLC

A student reduces a ketone with NaBH₄ (6.1.2(b)). She spots pure ketone, pure alcohol product and the reaction mixture after 30 minutes. The solvent front is 6.40 cm above the baseline.

- Ketone reference: spot centre at 4.16 cm
- Alcohol reference: spot centre at 1.28 cm
- Reaction mixture: spots at 1.28 cm and 4.16 cm

**Step 1. Calculate the Rf values.**
Ketone: 4.16 ÷ 6.40 = **0.65**. Alcohol: 1.28 ÷ 6.40 = **0.20**.

**Step 2. Match the spots.** The mixture lane has spots with Rf 0.20 and 0.65, the same as the two references run on the same plate.

**Step 3. Conclude.** Product and unreacted ketone are both present, so the reaction is not finished.

**Step 4. Explain.** The alcohol's O–H group hydrogen bonds to the silica, so it is adsorbed more strongly and has the lower Rf.

**Limitation.** Two compounds can share an Rf value, so a match supports an identification but does not prove it.

## 6.3.1(b): Gas chromatography

In GC the mobile phase is an unreactive carrier gas, such as helium or nitrogen. The stationary phase is a high-boiling liquid on a solid support inside a long, thin column in an oven. The sample is injected, vaporised and carried through the column to a detector. The chromatogram shows detector response against time.

### (b)(i) Retention time

The **retention time** is how long a component takes, from the moment of injection, to reach the detector. Read it at the top of the peak. A component that is more soluble in the liquid stationary phase, or less volatile, spends longer in the column and has a longer retention time.

Retention time also depends on the column, oven temperature and carrier gas flow rate. To identify a peak, compare it with a pure standard run under the same conditions. Two compounds can share a retention time.

### (b)(ii) Amounts and proportions

The **area** under each peak is proportional to the amount of that component. The instrument integrates the peaks and gives you the areas.

Percentage of a component = (its peak area ÷ total of all peak areas) × 100

This simple method assumes the detector responds equally to each component. For an exact concentration you need a calibration curve.

### Worked example 2: proportions in a fruit flavouring

A flavouring gives three peaks. Standards run under the same conditions identify them as ethyl ethanoate, ethyl butanoate and pentyl ethanoate. The integration values are 1840, 3220 and 1140.

**Step 1. Total the areas.** 1840 + 3220 + 1140 = 6200

**Step 2. Find each percentage.**
- Ethyl ethanoate: 1840 ÷ 6200 × 100 = **29.7%**
- Ethyl butanoate: 3220 ÷ 6200 × 100 = **51.9%**
- Pentyl ethanoate: 1140 ÷ 6200 × 100 = **18.4%**

### External calibration curves

An **external** calibration uses standards prepared and run separately from the sample.

1. Make up solutions of the pure compound at several known concentrations that span the expected value.
2. Run each standard under identical conditions and record its peak area.
3. Plot peak area (y-axis) against concentration (x-axis) and draw the line of best fit.
4. Inject the sample in the same way and find its concentration from the line.
5. If the sample was diluted before injection, multiply by the dilution factor.

### Worked example 3: limonene in a citrus extract

| Limonene standard / g dm⁻³ | 0.50 | 1.00 | 1.50 | 2.00 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Peak area | 1210 | 2400 | 3630 | 4810 |

A citrus extract was diluted by a factor of 5 and injected. Its limonene peak area was 2950.

**Step 1. Plot and fit.** The points fall close to a straight line through the origin; the best-fit equation is area = 2406 × concentration + 5.

**Step 2. Read off.** Concentration = (2950 − 5) ÷ 2406 = **1.22 g dm⁻³** in the diluted sample. On a graph, read across from 2950 to the line and down to the x-axis.

**Step 3. Allow for dilution.** 1.22 × 5 = **6.12 g dm⁻³** in the original extract.

The unknown lies inside the range of the standards; reading beyond them would mean extrapolating, which is less reliable.

## 6.3.1(c): Tests for organic functional groups

These tests use a test-tube scale: a few drops of liquid or a little solid, with a few cm³ of reagent. Key techniques for PAG7:

- Take a new sample of the unknown for every test.
- Many organic liquids are flammable. Warm them in a hot water bath or with an electric heater, never over a naked flame.
- Record observations precisely ("orange solution turns green"), then state the inference.

| Spec | Group | Reagent and conditions | Positive result |
|---|---|---|---|
| (c)(i) | Alkene C=C | Bromine water; shake, no heating needed | Orange solution decolourises |
| (c)(ii) | Haloalkane | Aqueous silver nitrate in ethanol, warm in a water bath | Precipitate: white (Cl), cream (Br), yellow (I) |
| (c)(iii) | Phenol | NaOH(aq), then separately Na₂CO₃(aq) | Reacts with NaOH; no effervescence with carbonate |
| (c)(iv) | Carbonyl (aldehyde or ketone) | 2,4-DNP | Yellow-orange precipitate |
| (c)(v) | Aldehyde | Tollens' reagent; warm (water bath) | Silver mirror |
| (c)(vi) | Primary or secondary alcohol, aldehyde | Acidified dichromate, warm in a water bath | Orange solution turns green |
| (c)(vii) | Carboxylic acid | Na₂CO₃(aq) | Effervescence; gas turns limewater milky |

**(i) Alkenes.** Bromine adds across the C=C bond, for example CH₂=CHCH₃ + Br₂ → CH₂BrCHBrCH₃. Phenol also decolourises bromine water, but it gives a white precipitate of 2,4,6-tribromophenol as well (6.1.1(i)).

**(ii) Haloalkanes.** Water in the reagent hydrolyses the C–X bond, releasing halide ions: R–X + H₂O → R–OH + H⁺ + X⁻. The silver ions then precipitate the halide: Ag⁺(aq) + X⁻(aq) → AgX(s). Ethanol is the common solvent, because haloalkanes do not mix with water. Iodoalkanes give a precipitate fastest, because the C–I bond is the weakest.

**(iii) Phenols.** Phenol is a weak acid, neutralised by NaOH: C₆H₅OH + NaOH → C₆H₅ONa + H₂O. It is too weak to react with carbonate, so there is no fizzing. This separates a phenol from a carboxylic acid.

**(iv) Carbonyl compounds.** 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine gives a yellow-orange precipitate with aldehydes and ketones. The equation and the derivative's structure are not required. To identify the compound, filter off the precipitate, recrystallise it, dry it and measure its melting point. A sharp melting point matching data for one derivative identifies the compound.

**(v) Aldehydes.** Tollens' reagent is ammoniacal silver nitrate, made fresh. The aldehyde becomes a carboxylic acid (RCHO + [O] → RCOOH). Silver ions are reduced to silver, Ag⁺ + e⁻ → Ag, which coats the tube as a mirror. Ketones are not oxidised, so there is no change.

**(vi) Primary and secondary alcohols and aldehydes.** Warm with K₂Cr₂O₇/H₂SO₄. Orange Cr₂O₇²⁻ is reduced to green Cr³⁺. Tertiary alcohols and ketones resist oxidation, so the solution stays orange. Primary and secondary alcohols both turn it green; to tell them apart, distil off the product and test it with Tollens' (an aldehyde means primary).

**(vii) Carboxylic acids.** For example, 2C₄H₉COOH + Na₂CO₃ → 2C₄H₉COONa + CO₂ + H₂O. Confirm the gas is carbon dioxide with limewater.

### Worked example 4: sorting four colourless liquids

Bottles A to D hold butanal, butanone, butan-2-ol and 2-methylpropan-2-ol, in some order.

1. **2,4-DNP.** A and C give yellow-orange precipitates, so they are the carbonyl compounds. B and D give no precipitate.
2. **Tollens' on A and C.** C gives a silver mirror; A does not. **C is butanal** and **A is butanone**.
3. **Acidified dichromate on B and D.** B turns green; D stays orange. **B is butan-2-ol** (secondary) and **D is 2-methylpropan-2-ol** (tertiary).

## Common errors

- Measuring to the top of a spot, or from the bottom of the plate, instead of from baseline to spot centre.
- Using peak height instead of peak area for amounts.
- Forgetting the dilution factor after reading a calibration graph.
- Saying 2,4-DNP shows an aldehyde. It shows a C=O in an aldehyde or ketone; Tollens' then decides.
- Expecting dichromate to separate primary from secondary alcohols.
- Writing "turns clear" for bromine water instead of "orange to colourless".
- Warming flammable organic liquids over a Bunsen flame.
