Sub-topic 3.3 of the Cambridge O Level Environmental Management (5014) syllabus for 2027 to 2029 focuses on two diseases linked to water in different ways. **Malaria** depends on water because mosquitoes breed in it; **cholera** depends on water because the bacteria travel in it. The four statements ask you to describe how malaria spreads (3.3.1), describe strategies to control malaria (3.3.2) and cholera (3.3.3), and discuss the benefits and limitations of those strategies (3.3.4). Keeping the two routes of spread separate is the key to almost every question.

## How mosquitoes spread malaria

Malaria is caused by a parasite called **Plasmodium**. It is not caught by drinking water. Instead it is carried by a **vector**, an organism that transmits a disease-causing organism from one host to another. For malaria the vector is the **female Anopheles mosquito**.

The cycle has three linked steps, and the syllabus expects all three:

1. An **infected** female Anopheles mosquito bites a person and the Plasmodium parasite passes into the person's blood.
2. The person becomes infected and develops malaria.
3. A **non-infected** mosquito bites the infected person, takes in the parasite with the blood, and can then infect the next person it bites.

Only female mosquitoes bite, because they need a blood meal to produce eggs. The link to water is the mosquito's life cycle: eggs are laid on still water, and the larvae and pupae live in that water before the adults emerge. Puddles, blocked drains, water tanks, discarded tyres, irrigation channels and the edges of ponds can all become breeding sites. This is why malaria can increase after heavy rain or when new reservoirs and irrigation schemes are built.

## Strategies to control malaria

The syllabus divides malaria control into two groups. It helps to ask of each strategy: does it stop the bite, or does it reduce the number of mosquitoes?

### Personal protection (stops infection of the individual)

- **Nets**: sleeping under a mosquito net, especially one treated with insecticide, prevents bites at night, when Anopheles mosquitoes mainly feed. A treated net also kills mosquitoes that land on it.
- **Insect repellent**: applied to skin or clothing, it discourages mosquitoes from biting in the evening and outdoors.
- **Vaccination**: vaccines against malaria are now given to young children in some countries. They reduce the number of severe cases but do not give complete protection.
- **Antimalarial drugs**: taken to prevent infection, for example by travellers, or to treat people who are infected. Treating the sick quickly also reduces the number of people from whom mosquitoes can pick up the parasite.

### Vector control (reduces the mosquito population)

- **Cover or drain breeding areas**: covering water tanks, draining puddles and swamps and clearing containers removes the still water where larvae develop.
- **Spraying large areas with insecticides**: kills adult mosquitoes, or larvae in water, over a whole district.
- **Sterilising male mosquitoes**: large numbers of males are made sterile and released. Females that mate with them lay eggs that do not hatch, so the next generation is smaller.
- **Biological control**: using a natural enemy, such as fish that eat mosquito larvae in ponds, or bacteria that are toxic to larvae.

## Strategies to control cholera

Cholera is a bacterial disease spread when water or food is contaminated with faeces from an infected person. It causes severe diarrhoea and dehydration. Control aims to break the route from faeces to mouth.

- **Handwashing** with soap after using the toilet and before handling food removes bacteria from hands.
- **Adequate sanitation and sewage treatment** keep human waste away from rivers, wells and food.
- **Potable water supply**: **boiling** water kills the bacteria; **chlorination** of public supplies or household containers does the same on a larger scale.
- **Vaccination**: oral cholera vaccines protect individuals and are used in outbreaks and in high-risk areas.

## Discussing benefits and limitations

Statement 3.3.4 asks you to weigh strategies, so learn at least one benefit and one limitation for each.

| Strategy | Benefit | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Nets | Cheap; protects during sleep; treated nets also kill mosquitoes | Must be used every night and replaced when torn; no protection outdoors in the evening |
| Repellent | Protects outdoors | Must be reapplied; regular cost |
| Malaria vaccine | Reduces severe illness in children | Several doses needed; protection is partial |
| Antimalarial drugs | Prevent or cure infection | Cost; side effects; the parasite can become resistant |
| Draining breeding sites | Removes larvae with no chemicals | Labour-intensive; impossible for every puddle in the rainy season; can damage wetland habitats |
| Insecticide spraying | Kills many mosquitoes quickly | Harms other insects and food chains; mosquitoes can become resistant; repeated cost |
| Sterile males | Targets only mosquitoes | Needs large numbers bred and released repeatedly; high cost and expertise |
| Biological control | No chemicals; can be self-sustaining | Introduced predators may harm native species |
| Handwashing | Very cheap and effective | Depends on soap, water and changing habits |
| Sanitation and sewage treatment | Removes the source of contamination for everyone | High construction cost |
| Boiling and chlorination | Makes water safe immediately | Boiling needs fuel; chlorine supplies must be maintained |
| Cholera vaccine | Rapid protection in an outbreak | Protection fades over time; does not remove the contamination |

A general judgement that works for both diseases is that combined strategies are most effective, and that long-term solutions (sanitation, removing breeding sites) are cheaper over time than repeated short-term ones (drugs, spraying), even though they cost more at the start.

## Worked example: evaluating a net programme

*Figures invented for practice.* A district of 40,000 people recorded 240 malaria cases per 1,000 people in the year before a net programme and 150 cases per 1,000 in the year after.

1. Fall in rate = 240 − 150 = 90 cases per 1,000 people.
2. Percentage fall = 90 ÷ 240 × 100 = 37.5%.
3. Cases before = 240 × 40 = 9,600; cases after = 150 × 40 = 6,000; so 3,600 fewer cases.

Interpreting the result: nets clearly helped, but 6,000 cases remain. Suggestions with reasons would include adding vector control to reduce mosquito numbers, because nets protect only people who are asleep under them, and supplying antimalarial drugs so infected people recover quickly and stop passing the parasite to mosquitoes.

## Annotated model answer

*Question: A refugee camp has had a cholera outbreak. Describe three strategies to control cholera in the camp.* **[3]**

> Provide handwashing stations with soap next to every latrine and food area, so bacteria are removed from hands. ✔
> Treat drinking water by chlorinating storage tanks, or give families fuel to boil water, so that the bacteria are killed. ✔
> Vaccinate people in the camp with an oral cholera vaccine to protect them while sanitation is improved. ✔

Each strategy is named, applied to the camp and given a short reason. "Clean water" alone would not earn a mark, because it does not say how the water is made safe.

## Common mistakes

- Writing that malaria is spread by drinking dirty water. It is spread by mosquito bites.
- Saying the mosquito causes malaria. The mosquito is the vector; Plasmodium is the parasite.
- Leaving out the third step: a non-infected mosquito picks up the parasite from an infected person.
- Giving nets as a cholera strategy, or boiling water as a malaria strategy.
- Discussing strategies without any limitation.

## Exam technique

For **describe** questions on transmission, use the three numbered steps and name the vector and the parasite. For **describe** strategies, say what the strategy is and how it works. For **discuss**, put each strategy against at least one limitation and end with a judgement, such as which combination suits the place in the question. Mark totals are a guide: a 3-mark "describe" usually needs three distinct strategies.
