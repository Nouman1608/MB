Quick recall for section 3.2 **Households** of the Cambridge O Level Economics 2281 syllabus for examination in 2026: "the influences on spending, saving and borrowing", including "income, the rate of interest and confidence – between different households and over time". Full explanations are in the [households study guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-economics-2281-households/); test yourself with the [households practice questions](/resources/cambridge-o-level-economics-2281-households-practice/). All figures are invented.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Disposable income | Income after direct taxes, plus state benefits; what the household can spend or save |
| Spending (consumption) | Using income to buy goods and services |
| Saving | Disposable income not spent |
| Borrowing | Taking a loan to spend now and repay, with interest, later |
| Dissaving | Spending more than current income by drawing on past savings |
| Rate of interest | The reward for saving and the cost of borrowing |
| Confidence | How secure households feel about future jobs, income and prices |
| Precautionary saving | Saving to protect against possible future problems, such as job loss |
| Wealth | The stock of assets a household owns at a point in time |
| Variable-rate loan | A loan whose interest rate changes when market interest rates change |

## Income

- Higher income: more spending and more saving in total.
- The share saved usually rises with income; poor households spend almost all their income.
- Example: incomes of $800, $2,000 and $5,000 a month with saving of $8, $200 and $1,250 give saving shares of 1%, 10% and 25%.
- Wealth matters too: rising house or share prices can raise spending without any change in income.

## Rate of interest

| A rise in interest rates | Effect |
|---|---|
| Savers | Higher reward, so more saving; higher interest income may raise their spending |
| New borrowers | Loans cost more, so less borrowing |
| Existing variable-rate borrowers | Repayments rise, cutting disposable income and spending |
| Goods bought on credit (cars, houses, furniture) | Demand falls most |

A cut in interest rates has the opposite effects. The rate change **transfers income between borrowers and savers**.

Example: a $60,000 variable-rate debt costs $200 a month at 4% and $300 a month at 6%. A $20,000 deposit earns $800 a year at 4% and $1,200 at 6%.

## Confidence

- High: more spending and borrowing, less saving.
- Low: more precautionary saving, purchases of durable goods delayed, less borrowing.
- Can change quickly, ahead of any change in income.
- Also affects banks' willingness to lend.

## Other influences

Availability of credit; expected price changes; household size and age; taxes and benefits; attitudes to saving and debt.

## Between households

| Comparison | Typical difference |
|---|---|
| Rich and poor | Rich save a larger share; poor more likely to borrow for emergencies |
| Young and old | Young borrow; old run down savings |
| Borrowers and savers | Interest rate rises hurt borrowers, help savers |
| Secure and insecure jobs | Insecure workers save more as a precaution |

## Over time

- **Life cycle**: borrow when young, save in middle age, dissave in retirement.
- **Economic cycle**: in a boom, spending and borrowing rise; in a recession, precautionary saving rises, which can deepen the downturn.

## Chains to learn

- **Income falls**: less disposable income → spending on non-essentials cut first → saving falls → some households borrow to cover essentials.
- **Interest rates cut**: borrowing cheaper → more loans for cars and homes → variable-rate borrowers have more to spend → savers earn less, so saving becomes less attractive → spending rises, if confidence allows.
- **Confidence falls**: fear of job loss → precautionary saving rises → big purchases delayed → firms sell less, which can itself lead to job losses.
- **Credit easier to get**: more households qualify for loans → borrowing and spending rise, even at the same interest rate.

## Exam traps

- Saying a rate rise cuts everyone's spending. Savers' income rises.
- Confusing income with wealth.
- Treating confidence as the same as income.
- Comparing "households" without naming the two types.
- Answering "over time" without life stages or changing conditions.
- Listing influences without a chain to spending, saving or borrowing.

## Quick self-test

1. Define disposable income.
2. Why do low-income households usually save a smaller share of their income?
3. A household earns $4,000 a month and saves $600. Calculate the share saved.
4. Give two effects of a rise in interest rates on households.
5. Why might a retired household gain from a rise in interest rates?
6. A household owes $30,000 on a variable-rate loan. The rate rises from 5% to 7%. Calculate the rise in annual interest.
7. What is precautionary saving?
8. Why might spending fall before anyone loses their job?
9. Describe how a person's borrowing and saving typically change through life.
10. State one influence on spending other than income, interest and confidence.
11. Why might a cut in interest rates fail to raise household spending?

### Answers

1. Income after direct taxes, plus state benefits, available to spend or save.
2. Most of their income goes on essentials such as food and housing, leaving little to save.
3. 600 ÷ 4,000 × 100 = 15%.
4. Any two: saving becomes more attractive; borrowing becomes more expensive; variable-rate borrowers have less to spend; demand for goods bought on credit falls.
5. Retired households are more likely to hold savings, so their interest income rises.
6. 30,000 × 0.07 − 30,000 × 0.05 = 2,100 − 1,500 = $600 a year.
7. Saving to protect against possible future problems, such as losing a job.
8. Confidence falls on news of possible job losses, so households save more and delay purchases.
9. Borrow when young (study, car, home), save more and repay debts in middle age, then spend savings in retirement.
10. Any one: availability of credit; expected price changes; household size; taxes and benefits; attitudes to saving.
11. Confidence may be low, households may already have heavy debts, or banks may be unwilling to lend.
