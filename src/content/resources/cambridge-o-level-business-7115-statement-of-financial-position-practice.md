These questions cover section 5.4 Statement of financial position of the Cambridge O Level Business Studies 7115 syllabus for examination in 2026. Constructing a statement is not assessed, so the questions ask you to classify, read and interpret. All businesses and figures are invented, and money is in dollars. Revise first with the [statement of financial position study guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-business-7115-statement-of-financial-position/) and the [statement of financial position revision notes](/resources/cambridge-o-level-business-7115-statement-of-financial-position-revision-notes/).

## Section A: Short-answer questions

**1.** Define the term "current liability". **[2]**

**2.** Identify the classification of each item on a statement of financial position: inventories; a mortgage; payables; machinery. **[4]**

**3.** State the difference between a non-current asset and a current asset. **[2]**

**4.** A business has non-current assets of $180,000, current assets of $60,000, current liabilities of $35,000 and non-current liabilities of $75,000. Calculate its equity. **[2]**

**5.** Explain why a bank overdraft is classed as a current liability even when a business has used one for several years. **[3]**

**6.** Explain why a large figure for retained profit does not mean a business has a large amount of cash. **[3]**

## Section B: Structured questions

**7.** Ottery Hotel, statement of financial position ($):

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Premises | 900,000 |
| Furniture and fittings | 120,000 |
| Inventories (food and drink) | 8,000 |
| Receivables | 4,000 |
| Cash | 18,000 |
| Payables | 22,000 |
| Mortgage (non-current) | 500,000 |
| Share capital | 300,000 |
| Retained profit | 228,000 |

**(a)** Calculate the total non-current assets and the total current assets. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain why most of a hotel's assets are non-current. **[2]**

**(c)** Calculate the percentage of the hotel's long-term finance (equity plus non-current liabilities) that comes from the mortgage. Give your answer to one decimal place. **[2]**

**(d)** Explain **one** risk to the hotel of financing itself in this way. **[3]**

**8.** Pinecrest Wholesale, statements of financial position ($):

| | Year 1 | Year 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Non-current assets | 150,000 | 150,000 |
| Inventories | 40,000 | 75,000 |
| Receivables | 30,000 | 52,000 |
| Cash | 25,000 | 2,000 |
| Payables | 35,000 | 48,000 |
| Bank overdraft | 0 | 21,000 |
| Bank loan (non-current) | 50,000 | 50,000 |
| Equity | 160,000 | 160,000 |

**(a)** Calculate working capital in each year. **[2]**

**(b)** The owner says: "Working capital is the same in both years, so our short-term position has not changed." Explain why this conclusion is wrong. **[4]**

**(c)** Explain **two** actions Pinecrest could take to improve its cash position. **[4]**

**9.** Tolland Sports has inventories of $60,000, cash of $2,000 and a bank overdraft of $18,000. It sells inventory that cost $24,000 for $20,000 in cash and uses the cash to clear the overdraft.

**(a)** Calculate Tolland's cash balance after the sale and the repayment. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the loss on the sale and identify the part of equity that it reduces. **[2]**

**(c)** Consider whether Tolland Sports should raise finance in this way. Justify your answer. **[5]**

**10.** State the effect of each transaction on a business's statement of financial position.

**(a)** Buying a delivery van for $15,000 in cash. **[2]**

**(b)** Taking out a five-year bank loan of $40,000. **[2]**

**11.** A bank manager studies a company's statement of financial position before deciding whether to lend it money. Explain **two** deductions the bank manager could make from the statement. **[4]**

**12.** Consider whether a statement of financial position on its own shows whether a business is successful. Justify your answer. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** An amount the business owes [1] that must be paid within one year, such as payables or an overdraft [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Inventories: current asset [1]. Mortgage: non-current liability [1]. Payables: current liability [1]. Machinery: non-current asset [1]. **[4]**

**3.** A non-current asset is kept and used for more than a year, such as a building [1], whereas a current asset is expected to be turned into cash or used up within a year, such as inventory [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Net assets = 180,000 + 60,000 − 35,000 − 75,000 [1] = **$130,000**, which equals equity [1]. **[2]**

**5.** An overdraft is borrowing that the bank can ask to be repaid at short notice [1]. So it must be treated as due within a year [1], however long the business has actually used it, because the business cannot rely on it continuing [1]. **[3]**

**6.** Retained profit is part of the owners' stake in the business, not a bank balance [1]. The profit may already have been spent on non-current assets or inventory [1], so a business can show high retained profit and still have very little cash [1]. **[3]**

**7. (a)** Non-current assets = 900,000 + 120,000 = **$1,020,000** [1]. Current assets = 8,000 + 4,000 + 18,000 = **$30,000** [1].

**(b)** A hotel earns its revenue from rooms and facilities that it keeps for many years [1], while it holds little stock and most guests pay at or before their stay, so current assets are small [1].

**(c)** Equity = 300,000 + 228,000 = $528,000 [1]. 500,000 ÷ (528,000 + 500,000) × 100 = **48.6%** [1].

**(d)** Nearly half its long-term finance is borrowed, so the hotel must make mortgage repayments and interest payments [1] whether or not it has many guests [1]. If bookings fall, for example in a quiet season, it may struggle to pay and could risk losing the premises, which secure the mortgage [1]. **[9]**

**8. (a)** Year 1: (40,000 + 30,000 + 25,000) − 35,000 = **$60,000** [1]. Year 2: (75,000 + 52,000 + 2,000) − (48,000 + 21,000) = **$60,000** [1].

**(b)** The make-up of current assets has changed [1]. In Year 1, cash and receivables of $55,000 more than covered current liabilities of $35,000 [1]. In Year 2, cash and receivables of $54,000 are less than current liabilities of $69,000, and cash has fallen to $2,000 [1], so Pinecrest now depends on selling its much larger inventory and on its overdraft to pay its debts [1].

**(c)** Action 1: sell surplus inventory, which has almost doubled [1], turning it into cash to reduce the overdraft, though discounts may reduce profit [1]. Action 2: chase customers who owe money or shorten credit terms [1], as receivables have risen by $22,000; collecting faster brings in cash without borrowing, although some customers may move to suppliers offering longer credit [1]. **[10]**

*Examiner insight:* Equal working capital totals can hide a big change in liquidity. Look inside the totals.

**9. (a)** 2,000 + 20,000 − 18,000 [1] = **$4,000** [1].

**(b)** Loss = 24,000 − 20,000 = **$4,000** [1], which reduces retained profit [1].

**(c)** For: the cash is raised quickly and clears the overdraft, so Tolland stops paying overdraft interest [1]; there is no new debt and no loss of ownership [1]. Against: selling below cost causes a $4,000 loss [1], and with inventory down to $36,000 Tolland may not have the sizes or products customers want, losing sales [1]. Judgement: it is sensible if the stock sold was slow-moving or out of season, but not if it was popular stock that would have sold at full price soon [1]. **[9]**

**10. (a)** Non-current assets rise by $15,000 [1] and cash (a current asset) falls by $15,000, so total assets are unchanged [1].

**(b)** Cash rises by $40,000 [1] and non-current liabilities rise by $40,000, so equity is unchanged [1]. **[4]**

**11.** Deduction 1: how the business is financed [1]. If loans are already large compared with equity, the bank may judge that more borrowing is risky because repayments are already high [1]. Deduction 2: which assets the company owns [1]. Non-current assets such as premises could be used as security for the loan, making the bank more willing to lend (also credit: whether current assets cover current liabilities) [1]. **[4]**

**12.** For: the statement shows whether the owners' stake is growing, for example through rising retained profit [1], and whether the business owns valuable assets and can cover its short-term debts [1]. Against: it is a snapshot of one date, so it does not show the profit or revenue earned during the year [1]; it says nothing about customer satisfaction, market share or staff [1]; and asset values may not reflect what they would sell for [1]. Judgement: it gives part of the picture, but success also needs evidence from the income statement and non-financial information, so on its own it is not enough [1]. **[6]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calling an overdraft a non-current liability.
- Treating retained profit or inventories as cash.
- Stopping at "working capital is the same" without looking at what current assets are made of.
- Forgetting that a loss on selling inventory reduces retained profit.
- Describing lines of the statement rather than making a deduction.
- Ignoring the type of business when commenting on its assets.
