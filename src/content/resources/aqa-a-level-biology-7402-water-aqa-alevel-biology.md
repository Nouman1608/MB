This guide goes deeper into **3.1.7 Water** from the AQA AS and A-level Biology specification (7401/7402), first teaching 2015, examined at AS and A-level. The specification states that water is a major component of cells and names five properties that matter in biology.

The [water and inorganic ions study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-biology-water-and-inorganic-ions/) already explains each property and works calculations on heat capacity, latent heat and a potometer. This page takes a different route. It traces every property back to one cause, the hydrogen bond, so that you can explain any property from first principles; it maps where water appears as a reactant or product across the whole course; and it practises the data-interpretation questions that ask you to apply water's properties to an organism or habitat. Condense it with the [3.1.7 revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-biology-7402-water-aqa-alevel-biology-revision-notes/) and practise with the [3.1.7 practice questions](/resources/aqa-a-level-biology-7402-water-aqa-alevel-biology-practice/).

## What the specification says

Water is a major component of cells. In particular, water:

- is a metabolite in many metabolic reactions, including condensation and hydrolysis reactions
- is an important solvent in which metabolic reactions occur
- has a relatively high heat capacity, buffering changes in temperature
- has a relatively large latent heat of vaporisation, providing a cooling effect with little loss of water through evaporation
- has strong cohesion between water molecules; this supports columns of water in the tube-like transport cells of plants and produces surface tension where water meets air.

## One cause: polarity and hydrogen bonds

A water molecule is **polar**. The oxygen atom attracts the shared electrons more strongly than the hydrogen atoms do, so the oxygen end carries a slight negative charge (δ−) and each hydrogen a slight positive charge (δ+). The δ+ hydrogen of one molecule is attracted to the δ− oxygen of a neighbour. This attraction is a **hydrogen bond**.

Each hydrogen bond is weak, but in liquid water every molecule forms hydrogen bonds with several neighbours, and the bonds are constantly breaking and re-forming. Most of water's biologically useful properties come from this.

| Property | How hydrogen bonding or polarity causes it |
|---|---|
| Solvent | Polar water molecules surround charged ions and polar molecules, separating them |
| High heat capacity | Much energy is used breaking hydrogen bonds before molecules move faster, so temperature rises slowly |
| Large latent heat of vaporisation | Many hydrogen bonds must be broken for a molecule to escape as vapour |
| Strong cohesion | Hydrogen bonds attract water molecules to each other |
| Surface tension | At an air surface, molecules are pulled inward and sideways by hydrogen bonds, forming a "skin" |

Using this table, you can explain a property in an unfamiliar context by naming the hydrogen bonds and saying what they do.

## Water as a metabolite across the course

A **metabolite** is a substance that takes part in metabolic reactions. The specification names condensation and hydrolysis, and you will meet water in other reactions later.

| Reaction | Water used or produced? | Where in the course |
|---|---|---|
| Condensation to build polysaccharides, polypeptides, polynucleotides, triglycerides | Produced | 3.1.1 to 3.1.5 |
| Hydrolysis of polymers and of triglycerides | Used | 3.1.1 to 3.1.3 |
| Hydrolysis of ATP | Used | 3.1.6 |
| Resynthesis of ATP from ADP and Pi | Produced | 3.1.6 |
| Digestion in the gut | Used | 3.3.3 |
| Photolysis in the light-dependent reaction | Used (split) | 3.5.1 (A-level) |
| Final step of aerobic respiration | Produced | 3.5.2 (A-level) |

If an exam question asks for the role of water in a reaction, check which direction the reaction runs. Saying "water is released" for hydrolysis is a common slip.

## Water as a solvent

Metabolic reactions occur in solution, in the cytoplasm and in body fluids. Because water is polar:

- **Ions** such as sodium, chloride and phosphate dissolve, each surrounded by water molecules oriented with the oppositely charged end towards it.
- **Polar molecules** such as glucose and amino acids dissolve because they form hydrogen bonds with water.
- **Non-polar molecules** such as triglycerides do not dissolve. They clump together, which is why lipids form droplets and why the phospholipid bilayer (3.1.3) can act as a barrier.

Being a solvent also makes water a transport medium: blood plasma carries dissolved glucose, ions and urea; xylem carries dissolved mineral ions; phloem carries dissolved sucrose.

## Heat capacity: buffering temperature

Heat capacity is the energy needed to raise the temperature of a given mass by a given amount. Water's relatively high value means:

- Organisms, which are mostly water, change temperature slowly, so enzymes work in a more stable environment.
- Large bodies of water, such as lakes and seas, change temperature far less than the air above them, making them stable habitats.

### Worked example 1: interpreting temperature data

Readings were taken every four hours over one day at a pond. The data are invented for practice.

| Time | 00:00 | 04:00 | 08:00 | 12:00 | 16:00 | 20:00 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Air (°C) | 6 | 3 | 11 | 22 | 25 | 14 |
| Pond water at 0.5 m (°C) | 13 | 12 | 13 | 15 | 17 | 15 |

1. Range of air temperature = 25 − 3 = **22 °C**.
2. Range of pond temperature = 17 − 12 = **5 °C**.
3. The air range is 22 ÷ 5 = **4.4 times** the pond range.

Explanation: water has a relatively high heat capacity because energy is used to break hydrogen bonds, so the pond absorbs heat during the day and loses it at night with only a small change in temperature. Organisms in the pond experience a more stable temperature than those on land, so their enzyme activity is more constant.

Description, then calculation, then explanation linked to hydrogen bonds is the order mark schemes reward.

## Latent heat of vaporisation: cooling with little water loss

Evaporation removes a large amount of energy for each gram of water, because many hydrogen bonds must break. So:

- **Sweating** in mammals and **transpiration** from leaves cool the surface.
- Because each gram removes so much energy, a large cooling effect needs only a small mass of water, which is the "little loss of water" in the specification.

### Worked example 2: comparing cooling

Take the latent heat of vaporisation of water as 2.4 kJ g⁻¹ (data supplied). A runner's body must lose 1,500 kJ by evaporation of sweat.

Mass of water evaporated = 1,500 ÷ 2.4 = **625 g**.

If a liquid had a latent heat of only 0.8 kJ g⁻¹, the mass needed would be 1,500 ÷ 0.8 = 1,875 g, three times as much. A high latent heat means cooling without dehydration.

## Cohesion and surface tension

**Cohesion** is the attraction of water molecules to each other by hydrogen bonds. In the xylem, the tube-like transport cells of plants, water forms continuous columns. As water evaporates from leaves, cohesion lets the column be pulled up as a whole, which you will meet as the cohesion-tension theory in 3.3.4.2, covered in the [exchange with the environment study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-biology-exchange-with-environment/).

**Surface tension** is produced where water meets air. Molecules at the surface are pulled inward and sideways by hydrogen bonds, so the surface behaves like a thin, elastic film. Small invertebrates such as pond skaters can stand on it, making the surface a habitat.

## Exam technique

- **Explain** a property by naming hydrogen bonding, saying what it does, then linking to the organism.
- **Describe** data with figures: give ranges or differences, not "the pond is more stable" alone.
- **Suggest** in unfamiliar contexts by choosing the right property: temperature questions need heat capacity; cooling questions need latent heat; transport questions need cohesion or solvent.

## Common mistakes

- Saying water has a high heat capacity "so it heats up quickly". It heats up slowly.
- Mixing up heat capacity (temperature change) and latent heat (evaporation).
- Calling hydrogen bonds strong. Each is weak; there are many.
- Writing that triglycerides dissolve in water.
- Forgetting the "little loss of water" part of the latent heat statement.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why is water a good solvent?**
Water is polar, so its molecules surround charged ions and polar molecules and separate them. Non-polar molecules, such as lipids, do not dissolve.

**What is the difference between heat capacity and latent heat of vaporisation?**
Heat capacity is the energy needed to change the temperature of water; latent heat of vaporisation is the energy needed to turn liquid water into vapour.

**How does cohesion help plants?**
Hydrogen bonds hold water molecules together, so continuous columns of water can be pulled up the xylem as water evaporates from leaves.

**What does "water is a metabolite" mean?**
Water takes part in metabolic reactions, as a reactant in hydrolysis and a product of condensation.
