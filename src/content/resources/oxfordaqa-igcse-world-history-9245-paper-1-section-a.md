**Section A of Paper 1** in OxfordAQA International GCSE History (9245) is a source-based depth
study. You study one of two options, **Peacemaking and the League of Nations, c1919-1939** or
**Crises of the Cold War, 1960-1975**, and every question tests source skills (AO3). The
[International Relations Depth Studies overview](/resources/igcse-oxfordaqa-world-history-international-relations-depth-studies/)
explains the paper as a whole; this guide covers the Section A questions and the knowledge you need
to use sources well in either option. 9245 is taught from September 2026 and first examined in
May/June 2028.

## How Section A works

- **Five sources**: a booklet of **three** is released in advance (1 February for the May/June
  series, 1 July for October/November); **two** are unseen and appear only in the exam.
- **No notes or annotations** may be taken in. A clean copy of all five sources is provided.
- The sources may be visual or written, primary or secondary.

The specification describes four questions:

| Question | Marks | Task |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 4 | Use an unseen source's content and/or provenance, and contextual knowledge, to **support a given claim** |
| 2 | 6 | Explain a **difference** between an unseen source and a pre-released one, using the content of each and your own knowledge |
| 3 | 8 | Evaluate how an unseen source is **useful** to a historian studying a given issue, using content, provenance and contextual knowledge |
| 4 | 12 | Analyse and evaluate a given issue or development using **a range of the sources** and your own knowledge |

Questions 1-4 each target Part 1 or Part 2 of the option's content. For Question 3 the
specification states that evaluating a source's limitations, how it is *not* useful, is not
required: concentrate on what the source can show.

## Using the pre-release well

You cannot take notes in, so preparation means **understanding**, not memorising annotations. For
each pre-released source, work out:

1. **Provenance**: who produced it, when, for whom, and why.
2. **Content**: what it says or shows, and the message of a cartoon or poster.
3. **Context**: which events in the specification it relates to, and what was happening at that
   date.
4. **Links**: which other topics it could be used for, since the question may target either part of
   the option.

## Method for each question

**Question 1 (support a claim).** Quote or describe a detail from the source that supports the
claim, then add one precise piece of contextual knowledge that also supports it. Provenance can
help: a source produced by someone in a position to know is strong support.

**Question 2 (explain a difference).** State the difference clearly in one sentence, show it with
content from *both* sources, then explain *why* they differ, using your own knowledge: different
dates, authors, audiences or purposes.

**Question 3 (usefulness).** Explain what the content tells a historian about the named issue, with
knowledge confirming it; then explain how the provenance makes the source useful. A government
poster is useful as evidence of what that government wanted people to believe; a private letter is
useful for one person's reaction.

**Question 4 (range of sources and knowledge).** Plan for both sides of the issue. Use and *link*
several sources, both unseen and pre-released, add your own knowledge, and finish with a supported
judgement.

## Option 1: Peacemaking and the League of Nations, c1919-1939

**Part 1: peacemaking, 1919-23.** The Big Three had different aims: Clemenceau wanted security
and to weaken Germany; Lloyd George wanted a peace that punished Germany but allowed trade to
recover; Wilson's Fourteen Points included self-determination and a League of Nations. Germany
called the Treaty of Versailles a *Diktat* because it was not allowed to negotiate. Beyond the
terms you should know (war guilt, reparations, military limits, territorial losses), learn the
wider settlement: Saint-Germain with Austria (1919), which forbade union with Germany; Neuilly
with Bulgaria (1919); Trianon with Hungary (1920); and Sèvres with the Ottoman Empire (1920),
which Turkish nationalists led by Mustafa Kemal rejected, leading to its replacement by the
**Treaty of Lausanne (1923)**.

**Part 2: the League.** The Covenant set out the League's purpose; it worked through the Assembly,
the Council, the Permanent Court of International Justice and a Secretariat, with agencies such as
the International Labour Organization. Its powers ran from moral condemnation to economic sanctions
and, in theory, military action by members. Membership changed: the USA never joined; Germany
joined in 1926 and left in 1933, as did Japan in 1933; the USSR joined in 1934; Italy left in
1937.

In the 1920s it settled the **Aaland Islands** (1921, awarded to Finland) and **Upper Silesia**
(1921, divided after a plebiscite). It failed over **Vilna** (1920), which Poland kept. Over
**Corfu** (1923), Mussolini occupied the island after an Italian general was murdered; the
Conference of Ambassadors made Greece pay compensation, a sign that great powers could bypass the
League. In **Bulgaria** (1925) the League made Greece withdraw its invading troops. Outside the
League, the **Locarno treaties** (1925) confirmed Germany's western borders, and the
**Kellogg-Briand Pact** (1928) renounced war as an instrument of policy, but had no means of
enforcement. The **Geneva Protocol** of 1924, which would have strengthened the League's dispute
procedures, never came into force.

The 1930s: the **Depression** weakened cooperation; Japan invaded **Manchuria** (1931), the Lytton
Report condemned Japan and Japan left the League (1933); Italy invaded **Abyssinia** (1935), and
sanctions did not include oil while Britain and France negotiated the secret **Hoare-Laval Pact**;
Britain and France followed **non-intervention** in the Spanish Civil War; the **World Disarmament
Conference** (1932-34) collapsed after Germany walked out in 1933.

## Option 2: Crises of the Cold War, 1960-1975

**Part 1: threats to peace in the 1960s.** In May 1960 an American **U2** spy plane was shot down
over the USSR and its pilot, Gary Powers, captured; the Paris Summit collapsed when Eisenhower
would not apologise. In August 1961 East Germany began building the **Berlin Wall** to stop
emigration to the West; Kennedy protested but accepted it, later visiting West Berlin in 1963. The
**arms race** produced intercontinental ballistic missiles and submarine-launched Polaris missiles;
in the **space race**, Yuri Gagarin became the first person in space in April 1961, and the Apollo
programme landed Americans on the Moon in 1969.

**Cuba**: Castro took power in 1959; the CIA-backed **Bay of Pigs** invasion failed in April 1961;
in October 1962 U2 photographs revealed Soviet missile sites. Kennedy announced a naval
"quarantine"; after thirteen days Khrushchev agreed to remove the missiles in return for a US
pledge not to invade Cuba, and the USA secretly agreed to remove its missiles from Turkey. A
hotline followed in 1963, and the Partial Test Ban Treaty that year.

**Czechoslovakia, 1968**: Alexander Dubček's reforms in the **Prague Spring** relaxed censorship;
fearing the spread of reform, Warsaw Pact forces invaded in August 1968. The **Brezhnev Doctrine**
stated that the USSR would intervene where communism was threatened in the Eastern bloc.

**Part 2: Vietnam.** The **Domino Theory** held that if one state fell to communism, its neighbours
would follow. Kennedy increased the number of US advisers and backed the **Strategic Hamlets**
programme. After the **Gulf of Tonkin incident** (1964), Congress gave Johnson wide powers;
bombing (Operation Rolling Thunder) and ground troops followed from 1965. The Viet Cong used
guerrilla tactics; the US used **search and destroy**, napalm and the defoliant Agent Orange. The
**Tet Offensive** (1968) and news of the **My Lai** massacre (1968) turned more Americans against
the war. Nixon's **Vietnamisation** handed fighting to South Vietnamese forces while widening the
war into Cambodia and Laos; four students were killed at **Kent State University** in 1970. Nixon
visited China in 1972; the **Paris Peace Accords** of 1973 ended direct US involvement; Saigon fell
in April 1975.

## Worked example: Question 3

**Source (invented for this guide, not a real document):** a report written in September 1923 by a
fictional British journalist in Geneva: "The League's members spoke bravely of justice, but when
Italy refused to accept its judgement, the matter was handed to the Conference of Ambassadors, and
Greece was told to pay."

**How useful is this source to a historian studying the weaknesses of the League in the 1920s?**

> The source is useful because its content shows a key weakness: when a great power refused to
> cooperate, the dispute was passed to the Conference of Ambassadors instead of being settled by
> the League. My own knowledge supports this, since after Mussolini occupied Corfu, Greece was made
> to pay compensation even though the Greek government was not proven responsible for the general's
> murder. The provenance adds to its usefulness: a journalist writing in Geneva in September 1923
> was reporting at the time and close to the League's meetings, so the source shows how the crisis
> was seen by observers as it happened and that the League's authority was already being
> questioned in 1923, not only after its failures in the 1930s.

The answer uses content, knowledge and provenance, and stays on what the source *can* show.

## Common mistakes

- Describing a source without linking it to the question's issue.
- In Question 2, describing the two sources separately without stating the difference.
- In Question 3, judging usefulness by source type alone ("it is a cartoon, so it is biased").
- In Question 4, using only one or two sources, or using sources with no own knowledge.
- Bringing annotated pre-release material into the exam: it is not permitted.

## Exam technique

Paper 1 lasts 1 hour 45 minutes for 60 marks, so Section A's 30 marks deserve about 50 minutes.
Spend roughly a minute a mark, and leave Question 4 a full fifteen to eighteen minutes.
