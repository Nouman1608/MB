These notes condense **3.1.3.2 Nature of covalent and dative covalent bonds** from the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Chemistry (9620) specification, Version 5.3. It is International AS content, examined in Unit 1, drawn on by Unit 2 and able to reappear in Unit 5. Fuller explanations and worked examples are in the [covalent and dative covalent bonds study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-1-3-2/); exam-style questions are in the [covalent and dative covalent bonds practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-1-3-2-practice/).

## The subtopic in four lines

1. A single covalent bond is a shared pair of electrons.
2. Double and triple bonds contain two and three shared pairs.
3. A co-ordinate (dative covalent) bond is a shared pair with both electrons supplied by one atom.
4. Draw a covalent bond as a line and a co-ordinate bond as an arrow from donor to acceptor.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Covalent bond | Shared pair of electrons between two atoms, attracted to both nuclei |
| Bonding pair | Pair of electrons shared between two atoms |
| Lone pair | Pair of outer electrons on one atom, not used in bonding |
| Double bond | Two shared pairs between the same two atoms |
| Triple bond | Three shared pairs between the same two atoms |
| Co-ordinate (dative covalent) bond | Shared pair in which both electrons come from one atom |
| Donor | Atom that supplies the lone pair |
| Acceptor | Atom or ion with an empty orbital that receives the pair |

## Why a covalent bond holds atoms together

The two nuclei are both attracted to the shared pair of electrons between them. Say "electrostatic attraction between the nuclei and the shared pair" when asked what holds the atoms together.

## Molecules to be able to draw

| Molecule | Bonds | Lone pairs |
|---|---|---|
| H₂ | H–H | none |
| Cl₂ | Cl–Cl | 3 on each Cl |
| HCl | H–Cl | 3 on Cl |
| H₂O | 2 × O–H | 2 on O |
| NH₃ | 3 × N–H | 1 on N |
| CH₄ | 4 × C–H | none |
| O₂ | O=O | 2 on each O |
| CO₂ | 2 × C=O | 2 on each O, none on C |
| C₂H₄ | C=C and 4 × C–H | none |
| N₂ | N≡N | 1 on each N |

Count check: in every diagram, the total number of electrons drawn must equal the total number of outer electrons of all the atoms (adjusted for any charge).

## Co-ordinate bonds

**Conditions:** a lone pair on the donor, and an empty orbital on the acceptor.

| Species | Co-ordinate bond | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| NH₄⁺ | H₃N→H⁺ | All four N–H bonds then identical |
| H₃O⁺ | H₂O→H⁺ | Oxygen keeps one lone pair |
| H₃N→BF₃ | N→B | B has only six outer electrons in BF₃ |
| Al₂Cl₆ | 2 × Cl→Al | Two AlCl₃ units joined; each Al then has four bonds |
| CO | O→C | Plus two ordinary shared pairs: a triple bond overall |

**Drawing:** in a dot-and-cross diagram both electrons of a co-ordinate bond carry the donor's symbol. In a displayed formula, the arrow points from donor to acceptor. Put square brackets and the overall charge around a whole ion.

## Spotting a co-ordinate bond in an unfamiliar species

1. Look for an H⁺ joining a molecule with a lone pair (such as NH₃, H₂O or an amine): the new bond is co-ordinate.
2. Look for a central atom with fewer than eight outer electrons (such as B in BF₃ or Al in AlCl₃) next to a molecule or atom with a lone pair.
3. Check that the electron count works: the donor loses a lone pair, and the acceptor gains a full outer shell.
4. Draw the arrow from the atom that had the lone pair.

## Exam traps

- "Sharing electrons" is not enough: say a shared **pair**.
- A co-ordinate bond, once formed, is the same as any other covalent bond between those atoms.
- The arrow points towards the acceptor.
- Hydrogen never has a lone pair.
- Each line is one pair; a double bond needs two lines.
- The charge on NH₄⁺ belongs to the whole ion.
- BF₃ and AlCl₃ have fewer than eight outer electrons on the central atom; that is why they act as acceptors.
- A donor must have a lone pair: CH₄ cannot donate.

## Self-test

1. Define a single covalent bond.
2. What holds two covalently bonded atoms together?
3. How many shared pairs are in a C=C bond?
4. How many lone pairs are on the nitrogen atom in NH₃?
5. Draw (in words) the bonds in N₂, including lone pairs.
6. Define a co-ordinate bond.
7. State the two conditions needed for a co-ordinate bond to form.
8. Write an equation for the formation of the oxonium ion.
9. Why can BF₃ accept a lone pair?
10. In which direction does the arrow point in H₃N→BF₃?
11. Explain why all four bonds in NH₄⁺ are identical.
12. How many co-ordinate bonds are in one Al₂Cl₆ molecule?

### Answers

1. A shared pair of electrons between two atoms.
2. Electrostatic attraction between both nuclei and the shared pair of electrons.
3. Two.
4. One.
5. A triple bond (three shared pairs) between the nitrogen atoms, with one lone pair on each nitrogen.
6. A shared pair of electrons in which both electrons are supplied by one atom.
7. The donor has a lone pair; the acceptor has an empty orbital in its outer shell.
8. H₂O + H⁺ → H₃O⁺
9. Boron has only six electrons in its outer shell, so it has an empty orbital.
10. From nitrogen (donor) to boron (acceptor).
11. Each is a shared pair of electrons between N and H; where the electrons came from makes no difference once the bond has formed.
12. Two.
