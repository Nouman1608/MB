These practice questions cover **Criterion C: Speaking** for IB MYP Language Acquisition. They are aligned to the International Baccalaureate Organization, Middle Years Programme Subject Brief -- *Language acquisition*, from 2020 (first eAssessment May 2023/November 2023), and apply to the course examined from the May 2023 and November 2023 eAssessment sessions onwards. MYP has no SL/HL split; the set suits MYP years 4 and 5. MYP has no prescribed content list -- schools design their own units -- so this page covers a skill the brief's criteria name.

Every question trains Criterion C. Real MYP work is judged against criterion level descriptors (levels 1-8 in four bands), so the `[1]` points below are a revision aid, not IB marks. Your teacher will share the task-specific clarifications. Questions are in English; answer the speaking ones aloud in your target language too. All places and data are invented.

Revise first from the [study guide](/resources/ib-myp-language-acquisition-criterion-c-speaking/) or [revision notes](/resources/ib-myp-language-acquisition-criterion-c-speaking-revision-notes/). ## Questions

**1.** (Criterion C) State three facts the MYP Language Acquisition subject brief gives about how the speaking examination is assessed. **[3]**

**2.** (Criterion C) Explain what makes a text "multimodal", and give one example of a multimodal text you could be asked to speak about. **[2]**

**3.** (Criterion C) Name the interaction strategy each student is using.

- (i) "Sorry, could you say that again?"
- (ii) "It's the machine that washes plates -- I can't remember the word."
- (iii) "Last year we buy -- sorry, we bought -- a new car."
- (iv) "Hmm, let me think about that for a second…"
- (v) "I think it's a good idea. Do you agree?"

**[5]**

**4.** (Criterion C) Read Source A.

> **Source A -- original text written for Marlbridge.** An infographic on a fictional school's website. Small figures walk, cycle, ride a bus or sit in a car on the way to a school gate. Headline: "Half of us already travel green -- what about the rest?" Data from a survey of 240 students: walk 84, bus 66, car 54, cycle 36.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage of students who walk or cycle, and say whether the headline is accurate. **[1]**
**(b)** Using the see-read-think-relate method, give one spoken sentence for each step. **[4]**
**(c)** Explain how the headline and the data work together to persuade. **[2]**

**5.** (Criterion C) A teacher asks, "Do you like living in your town?" The student says, "No." Rewrite the answer so it extends the exchange, and label each part of the chain you use. **[4]**

**6.** (Criterion C) Read this transcript extract. For each numbered problem, say whether it is mainly a fluency, accuracy or pronunciation issue, and suggest one fix.

> "Yesterday I go **(1)** to the market with my… my… we… we were **(2)** looking for fish. The man said it came off the *sheep* **(3)** this morning, um… **(4)**" -- then a home-language word.
>
> (At point 3 the student meant "ship"; the long vowel changed the word.)

**[4]**

**7.** (Criterion C) After a poster about a beach clean-up, the teacher asks: "Should governments ban single-use plastic?" The student prepared only personal points. Give a model answer for each move of the three-move recovery. **[3]**

**8.** (Criterion C, self-review, calculation) A student records two 3-minute practice responses on the same stimulus. Their notes:

| | Words spoken | Pauses over 3 seconds | Errors noted |
|---|---|---|---|
| Recording 1 | 210 | 9 | 14 |
| Recording 2 | 264 | 4 | 11 |

**(a)** Calculate the speaking rate, in words per minute, for each recording. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the errors per 100 words for each recording, to 1 decimal place. **[2]**
**(c)** The student says, "Recording 2 is better because I spoke faster." Evaluate this claim. **[2]**

**9.** (Criterion C) The brief calls the speaking examination "an interactive summative assessment task". Explain how this should shape your preparation, and describe two suitable activities. **[4]**

**10.** (Criterion C, extended) Read Source B.

> **Source B -- original text written for Marlbridge.** A fictional town's events web page. The photo shows families at dusk releasing paper lanterns onto a river. Headline: "One night, one river, a thousand wishes." Details: "Riverside Lantern Night -- Saturday, from 19:00. Make your lantern from recycled paper at the library workshop."

**(a)** Give an opening spoken response that covers all four steps of see-read-think-relate. **[4]**
**(b)** Predict two follow-up questions the teacher could ask, each linked to a different MYP global context, and outline an answer to each. **[4]**
**(c)** Identify one risk to your accuracy and one risk to your fluency in this task, and say how you would manage each. **[2]**

**11.** (Criterion C, extended) A classmate's plan for the last month:

> "I'll write a full answer on each of ten topics and learn them by heart. In the exam I'll speak as fast as I can so I don't forget anything."

Evaluate the plan and propose an improved four-week plan. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** Any three: it is **internally assessed**, not part of the on-screen examination [1]; it is **marked by your classroom teacher** against the published speaking examination marking criteria for MYP year 5 [1]; the **IB moderates a sample** from each school each session and adjusts grades as necessary [1]. Also accept: interactive; worth 24 marks. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* "State" needs three separate facts; "the teacher marks it" and "it is marked in school" are one fact said twice.

**2.** A multimodal text combines **more than one mode of meaning**, such as words with images, layout or sound [1]. Example: **a poster with a photo and a slogan** (or an advert, infographic, web page) [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A words-only example, such as a letter, cannot earn the example point.

**3.** (i) **Asking for repetition** [1]. (ii) **Paraphrase / circumlocution** [1]. (iii) **Self-correction** [1]. (iv) **Holding the turn / buying time** [1]. (v) **Giving a view and inviting one** [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Name the strategy, not the language point -- for (iii), "past tense" describes the grammar, not what the speaker is doing.

**4. (a)** (84 + 36) ÷ 240 × 100 = **50%**, so the headline's "half" is **accurate** [1].
**(b)** See: "Students are walking, cycling or riding to school." [1] Read: "The headline says half of us travel green; walking is the biggest group." [1] Think: "The question targets the other half -- car and bus users." [1] Relate: "At my school many parents drive, but not everyone lives close enough to walk." [1]
**(c)** The data gives **evidence** that makes the headline believable [1]; the headline then **turns the numbers into a challenge** to the reader, using "us" to include them [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* Reading out the numbers stays at "read"; (c) credits what the words do with the data.

**5.** Model: "Not really **(answer)** [1], because there's little for teenagers to do in the evenings **(reason)** [1]. Last Friday we had nowhere to go but a café that closed at seven **(example)** [1]. If the town opened a youth centre, I'd like it more **(consequence)** [1]." **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* The four parts must link; four unrelated sentences show range but not a coherent extended answer.

**6.** (1) **Accuracy** -- wrong tense after "yesterday"; fix: link time words to past forms in practice [1]. (2) **Fluency** -- repeated restarts; fix: plan the chunk "we were looking for" before speaking, or use a filler [1]. (3) **Pronunciation** -- vowel changed the word's meaning; fix: drill the minimal pair with shadowing [1]. (4) **Fluency (strategy)** -- "um" and a switch to another language; fix: a target-language filler or paraphrase [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A fix must match its label -- "learn more vocabulary" for a pronunciation error earns nothing.

**7.** **Buy time:** "That's a big question -- let me think." [1] **Narrow:** "I can only really speak about my own town." [1] **Link back:** "The poster showed bottles all over the beach, so banning some items would help, even if it's hard for shops." [1] **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* A link back must reuse something from the stimulus.

**8. (a)** Recording 1: 210 ÷ 3 = **70 words per minute** [1]. Recording 2: 264 ÷ 3 = **88 words per minute** [1].
**(b)** Recording 1: 14 ÷ 210 × 100 = **6.7** errors per 100 words [1]. Recording 2: 11 ÷ 264 × 100 = **4.2** errors per 100 words [1].
**(c)** Speed alone is not fluency; faster speech can still be hard to follow [1]. Recording 2 is better because **long pauses fell from 9 to 4** and **the error rate fell** [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Compare error rates, not raw error counts -- 11 errors in more words is a bigger improvement than 14 → 11 suggests.

**9.** "Interactive" means a **two-way conversation**, so you must react to unseen questions [1]; prepare **adaptable frames and strategies, not fixed answers** [1]. Activity 1: **partner practice** -- one describes a new stimulus, the other asks three unseen questions [1]. Activity 2: **record and review**, noting three fixes each time [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Two activities with no link to "interactive" earn only half.

**10. (a)** See: "Families by a river at dusk are letting paper lanterns float away." [1] Read: "The headline makes it sound magical, and lanterns are made from recycled paper." [1] Think: "It wants families to come together, and shows the organisers care about waste." [1] Relate: "My town has a light festival too, but I wonder where the lanterns end up." [1]
**(b)** Personal and cultural expression: "Why do people keep traditions like this?" [1] Outline: shared memories, a place's identity, a tradition from your culture [1]. Globalization and sustainability: "Is it right to put lanterns in a river?" [1] Outline: harm to wildlife versus paper that breaks down; collect lanterns downstream [1].
**(c)** Accuracy risk: mixing tenses when comparing past festivals with this coming one -- keep one time frame per sentence [1]. Fluency risk: a missing word such as "lantern" -- paraphrase ("the paper light that floats") [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* In (b), a question with a named context but no outlined answer earns one point of two.

**11.** Evaluation: memorised answers **break down when a question is unexpected** [1]; ten topics cannot cover every stimulus, so answers may **not respond to the text** [1]; racing **raises errors and hurts pronunciation** -- speed is not fluency [1]; there is **no partner practice** and no review [1]. Improved plan: Week 1 -- confirm your level and its descriptors; build frames for see-read-think-relate [1]; Week 2 -- rebuild the phrase bank; practise the extending chain on new stimuli [1]; Week 3 -- partner practice with unseen questions, recorded and reviewed [1]; Week 4 -- timed rehearsals on fresh stimuli, targeting your three most frequent errors [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* "Evaluate" needs a judgement of the old plan as well as a better one; a new plan alone earns half.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing a stimulus's image and never interpreting its purpose or audience.
- One-line answers that close the exchange instead of extending it.
- Switching to a memorised paragraph when a follow-up question is unexpected.
- Comparing raw error counts instead of error rates when reviewing recordings.
- Using home-language words or "um" to fill pauses.
- Not asking anything back, so the task is not interactive.
