**This practice set covers the cognitive approach in Cambridge International AS & A Level Psychology 9990 (syllabus for 2024 to 2026).** Section A has short-answer questions on the assumptions and the three core studies. Section B has longer evaluation, comparison and issues-and-debates questions. Full mark-scheme answers follow.

## Section A: short-answer questions

**1.** Outline what is meant by the *input – process – output* assumption of the cognitive approach, using the study by Pozzulo et al. as an example. **[3]**

**2. (a)** Identify the independent variable in the study by Andrade. **[1]**

**2. (b)** Identify **one** dependent variable in the study by Andrade. **[1]**

**3.** Explain why Andrade used a surprise memory test. **[2]**

**4.** Andrade used an independent measures design. Outline **one** weakness of using this design in her study. **[2]**

**5.** Explain **two** reasons why Baron-Cohen et al. revised the original Eyes test. **[4]**

**6.** In the study by Baron-Cohen et al., describe the correlation between Eyes test scores and Autism Spectrum Quotient scores, and state what it suggests. **[2]**

**7.** Explain the difference between a *false positive response* and a *correct rejection* in a target-absent line-up. **[2]**

**8.** Suggest **one** reason why Pozzulo et al. used cartoon faces as well as human faces. **[2]**

## Section B: longer questions

**9.** Evaluate the study by Andrade in terms of validity and ethics. **[10]**

**10.** Compare the samples used by Baron-Cohen et al. and Pozzulo et al. **[4]**

**11.** Explain how the study by Pozzulo et al. can be applied to everyday life. **[3]**

**12.** Explain **one** strength and **one** weakness of using photographs of the eye region to measure theory of mind. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** Up to 3 marks. Input: information enters the system, such as the target face shown to participants (1). Process: the information is encoded, stored and later compared with the faces in the line-up (1). Output: the response, such as choosing a face or saying the target is not present (1).

**2. (a)** 1 mark: whether or not the participant doodled (shaded shapes) while listening to the message.

**2. (b)** 1 mark for either: the number of names correctly monitored (written down) during the message; or the number of names and places recalled in the surprise test.

**3.** Up to 2 marks. If participants had known about the memory test, they might have deliberately rehearsed the names and places (1), so the test would measure effort to memorise rather than how much attention was paid during the boring message (1).

**4.** Up to 2 marks. Different people took part in each condition, so participant variables such as natural memory ability or tendency to daydream could differ between the groups (1); a difference in recall might then be caused by these differences rather than by doodling (1).

**5.** 2 marks for each reason explained. Any two of: the original had only two answer choices, often opposites, so guessing gave a high chance of a correct answer (1) and the test could not discriminate well (1); many adults scored at or near the maximum (a ceiling effect) (1), so it could not show individual differences in the general population (1); there were too few items (1), which reduced the reliability of scores (1).

**6.** Up to 2 marks. A negative correlation: the higher the AQ score, the lower the Eyes test score (1). This suggests that people with more autistic traits find it harder to infer mental states from the eyes, supporting the test as a measure of social sensitivity (1).

**7.** Up to 2 marks. A false positive is when the witness picks someone even though the culprit is not in the line-up, so an innocent person is identified (1). A correct rejection is when the witness correctly says the culprit is not there (1).

**8.** Up to 2 marks. For example: cartoon characters may be more familiar or interesting to young children (1), so using them as well as human faces lets the researchers see whether the type of face affects identification and keeps children engaged (1).

**9.** Level-based: 9–10 marks for a balanced evaluation that covers both validity and ethics, with points applied to the study and a conclusion. Indicative content:

- *Validity, strength*: a standard recording and printed shapes controlled what all participants heard and did, so differences in recall are more likely to be due to the doodling IV.
- *Validity, strength*: the surprise test stopped deliberate rehearsal, so recall reflects attention during the message.
- *Validity, weakness*: shading printed shapes is not spontaneous doodling, and a recorded message in a laboratory is unlike a real call or lecture, so ecological validity is low.
- *Validity, weakness*: daydreaming was inferred, not measured, so the explanation for the effect is uncertain; participants had just finished another study, which might affect boredom levels differently.
- *Ethics, strength*: the task was harmless and participants were from a research panel used to taking part, so risk of distress was minimal.
- *Ethics, weakness*: participants were not told about the memory test, a mild deception; this was justified because knowing would have altered the dependent variable, and a debrief could explain the real aim.
- *Conclusion*: a well-controlled and ethical study whose main limitation is the artificial task.

**10.** Up to 4 marks: 2 marks per comparison, each linked to both studies. Similarity: both compared groups that differed in a characteristic that could not be manipulated (1), people with and without Asperger syndrome or high-functioning autism in Baron-Cohen et al., and children versus adults in Pozzulo et al. (1). Difference: all of Baron-Cohen et al.'s participants were adults (1), whereas Pozzulo et al. included young children, who needed parental consent (1).

**11.** Up to 3 marks. Young children made more false positive responses when the culprit was absent (1), so police should be cautious about relying on a child's identification from a line-up (1), for example by using procedures that make it easy for the child to say "not there" and by seeking other evidence (1).

**12.** 2 marks for each point explained. Strength: every participant sees exactly the same standardised photographs (1), so scores can be compared fairly between groups and the test can be repeated (1). Weakness: in real life people judge mental states from the whole face, voice and context (1), so a static photograph of the eyes may not reflect everyday theory of mind, lowering ecological validity (1).

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need to name the Autism Spectrum Quotient?**
Naming it shows you know the correlational part of the study, which the syllabus mentions. "A questionnaire measuring autistic traits" also earns credit.

**How precise should the Andrade result be?**
"About 29% more information recalled by the doodling group" is precise and easy to learn.
