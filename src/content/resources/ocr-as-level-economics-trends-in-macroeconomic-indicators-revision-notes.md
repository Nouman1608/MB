## Key definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Trend | The general direction of a series over several years |
| Level | Where a series stands at one date |
| Rate of change | Percentage change from one period to the next |
| Percentage point | The unit for the change in a rate (unemployment 6.0% to 4.5% is a fall of 1.5 percentage points) |
| Economic cycle | Swings of actual output around the long-run path |
| Peak and trough | Turning points: the top and bottom of an upswing and downswing in output |
| Recession | A fall in real output; commonly two quarters in a row of falling real GDP |
| Disinflation | Inflation is still positive but lower than before; the price level keeps rising more slowly |
| Deflation | A fall in the general price level |
| Developed, emerging, developing | Groups by income level and speed of change: high income, fast-industrialising, and low income |
| GDP per head | GDP divided by population |

## Formulas and methods

| Measure | Formula |
|---|---|
| Growth rate (%) | (new - old) / old x 100 |
| Compound average growth (%) | ((final / first) to the power of 1/n - 1) x 100, with n yearly steps |
| Peak-to-trough change (%) | (trough - peak) / peak x 100 |
| Unemployment rate (%) | unemployed / labour force x 100 |
| Real wage change | roughly nominal wage growth minus inflation (exactly (1 + w) / (1 + p) - 1) |
| Current account as % of GDP | balance / GDP x 100 |
| Real GDP per head growth | roughly real GDP growth minus population growth |

### Method: describe a trend in four steps

1. State the direction and the size: "real GDP rose from 200 to 214, up 7.0%".
2. Name the turning points: peak, trough, and when the level recovered.
3. Compare periods: average growth before and after a shock.
4. Give a likely cause, linked to AD or AS, using "could" if the data does not name it.

### Worked reminder: Country Z

Real GDP in Country Z is 200 in year 1, 214 in year 2 and 207 in year 3.

- Year 2 growth = (214 - 200) / 200 = **7.0%**.
- Year 3 growth = (207 - 214) / 214 = **-3.27%**.
- Over both years real GDP is 3.5% higher than at the start, even though the last year was a contraction.

### Worked reminder: inflation and real wages

The price index rises from 160 to 168, so inflation = 8 / 160 = **5.0%**. Nominal wages rise by 2.0%. Real wages change by 1.02 / 1.05 - 1 = **-2.86%**, a clear fall in real purchasing power.

### Worked reminder: percentage points

Unemployment in Country Z is 1.2 million of a labour force of 24.0 million: **5.0%**. After a recession it is 1.68 million of 24.0 million: **7.0%**. The rise is **2.0 percentage points**, not 2%. In percentage terms the number unemployed has risen by 40%.

### Worked reminder: compound average growth

Real GDP index rises from 100 to 121.6 over 8 years. Average annual growth = (1.216 to the power of 1/8) - 1 = **2.47%** a year.

Country Z has GDP of 540 billion currency units and 12.0 million people, so GDP per head is **45,000**. If real GDP rises by 3.0% and population by 1.0%, real GDP per head rises by about 1.98%, close to the 2.0% rule of thumb.

## The UK over about the last 20 years: recall table

This is a summary for organising your answer. It is not a data source; use the figures you are given in a question, and check anything else against the Office for National Statistics.

| Phase | Pattern to recall | Cause to link |
|---|---|---|
| Mid-2000s | Steady growth, low unemployment, inflation near the 2% target, a current account deficit | Strong consumer spending, easy credit, cheap imports |
| 2008 to 2009 | Deep recession, rising unemployment (less than the output fall implied), Bank Rate cut to 0.5% in March 2009 | Financial crisis, loss of confidence, credit shortage |
| 2010s | Slow recovery, weak productivity growth, falling unemployment, budget deficit reduced | Low investment, tight fiscal policy, low interest rates |
| 2020 to 2021 | Sharp fall then rebound in output, unemployment up by less than feared, very large deficit | Public health restrictions, income support, Bank Rate cut to 0.1% in March 2020 |
| 2021 onwards | Inflation surge (CPI peaked at 11.1% in October 2022), Bank Rate raised to 5.25% by August 2023, weak growth | Energy and import prices, supply bottlenecks, tighter policy |

Across the period: a persistent **current account deficit**, slower **productivity growth** after 2008 to 2009, and policy that shifted between loose and tight.

## Comparing the UK with other economies: checklist

| Group | Typical features | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| Developed | High income per head, services-led, slower growth | Similar structure, so differences often come from policy or shocks |
| Emerging | Fast growth, industrialising, rising incomes | Low starting base; may face high inflation or volatile capital flows |
| Developing | Low income per head, often dependent on primary products | Fast population growth, volatility, limited data quality |

Questions to ask whenever you compare:

1. Is it the same year, the same definition and the same unit?
2. Is the comparison in totals or per head?
3. How far does the starting level differ?
4. Is the data one year or an average?
5. What does the indicator leave out, such as distribution, quality of jobs or the environment?

## Must-know distinctions

- **Level versus rate.** A falling unemployment rate can still mean unemployment is above its old level.
- **Growth versus recovery.** Positive growth after a recession does not mean lost output is back.
- **Disinflation versus deflation.** Falling inflation is not falling prices.
- **Totals versus per head.** Population growth can make strong growth look weak in living standards.
- **Percentage versus percentage point.** Use the right one for rates.
- **Simple versus compound average.** Compound is correct for growth over several years.

## Quick self-test

1. Real GDP index rises from 80 to 88. Calculate the percentage growth.
2. Unemployment falls from 6.0% to 4.5%. State the change in percentage points and in percentage terms.
3. Real GDP rises from 300 to 309 while population grows by 1.0%. Calculate the growth in real GDP per head.
4. Explain why growth of 2.0% after a recession does not mean the economy has recovered.
5. State the difference between disinflation and deflation.
6. Name two features that distinguish emerging from developed economies.
7. Give one reason why comparing growth rates of an emerging economy and the UK may mislead.
8. Give two long-run trends in UK macroeconomic performance since the mid-2000s.
9. A country has a current account deficit of 36 on GDP of 1,200. Express it as a percentage of GDP.
10. Say why unemployment may fall more slowly than output recovers.

### Answers

1. (88 - 80) / 80 = **10.0%**.
2. **1.5 percentage points**; in percentage terms (4.5 - 6.0) / 6.0 = **-25.0%**.
3. 309 / 300 = 1.03, and 1.03 / 1.01 - 1 = **1.98%**.
4. Output may still be below its previous peak, and spare capacity and unemployment may remain, so the level has not recovered.
5. Disinflation means inflation slows but stays positive; deflation means the general price level actually falls.
6. Any two of: faster growth, lower income per head, industrialisation, a larger primary or manufacturing sector, a younger or faster-growing population.
7. The emerging economy starts from a much lower base, so a high percentage growth can be a small absolute rise.
8. Any two of: a persistent current account deficit, weaker productivity growth after 2008 to 2009, an inflation surge after 2021, large budget deficits after 2008 to 2009 and 2020.
9. 36 / 1,200 x 100 = **3.0%** of GDP.
10. Firms rehire cautiously, some job losses are structural, and workers may need retraining or to move.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing a series without quoting numbers from the data given.
- Mixing up percentage points and per cent for changes in rates.
- Saying "the economy recovered" because growth is positive.
- Using a simple average of growth rates where the question needs a compound figure.
- Comparing countries on different years or measures.
- Giving causes without linking them to AD or AS.
- Quoting remembered UK statistics from outside the question without a year.
- Listing differences between economy groups without a judgement about UK performance.

## Frequently asked questions

**How do you calculate real wage growth?**
Divide one plus the growth in nominal wages by one plus inflation and subtract 1. If nominal wages rise by less than prices, real wages fall.

**What trends has the UK shown over the last 20 years?**
The OCR AS Level Economics (H060) notes highlight a persistent current account deficit, slower productivity growth after 2008 to 2009, and policy that shifted between loose and tight.

**Why use GDP per head rather than total GDP?**
Population growth can make strong total growth look weak in terms of living standards. Real GDP per head grows by roughly real GDP growth minus population growth.

**What is the difference between a level and a rate?**
A level is where a series stands, such as the number unemployed, while a rate is how fast it is changing. A falling unemployment rate can still leave unemployment above its old level.
