## 3.1.1 Purpose and nature of businesses

How to use this page: read a section, cover it, then write the key terms from memory. Finish with the self-test and mark it honestly. Anything you miss goes on a short list to re-read in the study guide the next day.

### Why businesses start

The specification lists five reasons. Learn them as a set.

| Reason | Cue in a case study |
|---|---|
| Producing goods | Makes or manufactures items |
| Supplying services | Does something for customers |
| Distributing products | Moves or sells on goods made by others |
| Fulfilling a business opportunity | Spots something missing locally |
| Providing a good or service to benefit others | A cause or community aim |

### Key definitions

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Goods | Physical products |
| Services | Activities done for customers |
| Needs | What people must have to live |
| Wants | What people would like but could live without |
| Opportunity cost | The next best alternative given up when a choice is made |
| Enterprise | Spotting an opportunity, taking a risk and organising resources |
| Entrepreneur | The person who does this |

### Factors of production

Land (natural resources and sites), labour (people), capital (man-made resources such as machines and vehicles), enterprise (organising the rest and taking the risk).

Memory aid: a printing firm's plot of land is land, its press operators are labour, its printing press is capital and its owner's decision to set up is enterprise.

### Sectors

| Sector | Activity | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Primary | Extracting or growing natural resources | Farming, fishing, mining |
| Secondary | Manufacturing and building | Furniture making, construction |
| Tertiary | Services | Retail, banking, hairdressing |

### Opportunity cost method

1. List the options and the value of each.
2. Pick the best (the one chosen).
3. Opportunity cost is the value of the second best, not the sum of all the rest.

Worked reminder: Zephyrwick Loft Conversions can take one job this month: a loft conversion earning £4,800 profit, or a garage conversion earning £3,900 profit, or two small repair jobs earning £2,100 between them. It takes the loft conversion. The opportunity cost is £3,900.

### Entrepreneurs

- Characteristics: hard working, innovative, organised, willing to take a risk.
- Objectives: be their own boss, flexible working hours, pursue an interest, earn more money, identify a gap in the market, dissatisfaction with a current job.
- Tip: in an exam, quote the evidence. A case study that says an owner "left her job after a manager ignored her ideas" shows dissatisfaction with a current job.

### A changing business environment

The four named causes are technology, economic situation, legislation and environmental expectations. For each, ask what changes and which function it affects.

| Cause | Possible effect |
|---|---|
| Technology | New ways to sell or produce, so operations and marketing change |
| Economic situation | Less spending power lowers demand, affecting finance and marketing |
| Legislation | New rules that can add costs or alter how people are employed |
| Environmental expectations | Pressure to cut waste, changing operations and marketing |

The specification also asks you to be aware of the effect on the four functional areas: business operations, human resources, marketing and finance.

## 3.1.2 Business ownership

### Liability

Unlimited liability: the owner is personally responsible for all business debts, so personal assets such as a house can be used. Limited liability: owners can lose only what they invested.

| Structure | Liability |
|---|---|
| Sole trader | Unlimited |
| Partnership | Unlimited |
| Private limited company (ltd) | Limited |
| Public limited company (plc) | Limited |

Not-for-profit organisations: do not assume. State the position only if the question gives it.

Worked reminder: Cordell & Finch Joinery owes £41,000 and its assets sell for £17,500. The shortfall is £41,000 − £17,500 = £23,500. As a partnership, the partners are personally liable for it. As a ltd, a shareholder who invested £8,000 loses that £8,000.

### Comparison table

| Structure | Control | Finance | Profits | Main benefit | Main drawback |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sole trader | One owner decides | Owner's funds, loans | Owner keeps all | Control and simplicity | Unlimited liability, limited capital |
| Partnership | Shared between partners | More capital than one person | Shared as agreed | Shared skills and workload | Disagreements, unlimited liability |
| Private limited (ltd) | Shareholders elect directors | Shares sold privately, loans | Dividends per share | Limited liability | More paperwork, shares not freely sold |
| Public limited (plc) | Directors run it for many shareholders | Shares sold to the public | Dividends to many | Large capital | Takeover risk, high costs, public scrutiny |
| Not-for-profit | Trustees or volunteers, guided by a cause | Donations, grants, trading income | Surplus reinvested | Focus on a cause | Uncertain income |

### Ltd versus plc

- Both have limited liability.
- A ltd cannot offer shares to the public; a plc can.
- Plc is private sector. "Public" means the public may buy shares.
- Plc owners can lose control to a takeover; a ltd owner can usually decide who buys shares.

### Method in steps: which structure?

1. Underline what the business needs (capital, control, risk protection).
2. Match each need to a structure's benefit or drawback.
3. Weigh both sides, make a judgement and justify it.
4. State what would change your decision.

Examples of judgement language: "A sole trader is suitable for a low-risk start-up, as long as the owner is willing to accept unlimited liability", or "A plc suits a firm that needs very large capital, though the owners must accept the risk of takeover".

### Calculation reminders

Profit shares in a partnership: multiply total profit by each partner's agreed fraction.
Dividend per share: total dividend ÷ number of shares. Shareholder's dividend: dividend per share × shares held.
Debt shortfall: debts − asset sale proceeds.

Check: a ratio of 3:1 on £36,000 profit gives £27,000 and £9,000. Dividends of £12,000 on 40,000 shares give 30p per share.

## Must-know distinctions

- Goods versus services: goods can be touched; services are carried out.
- Needs versus wants: needs are essential.
- Capital (equipment) versus finance (money).
- Enterprise (the activity) versus entrepreneur (the person).
- Limited liability versus limited company: liability is the feature, company is the structure.
- Ltd versus plc: who may buy shares.
- Profit (kept by owners) versus surplus (normally reinvested in a not-for-profit organisation).

## Quick self-test

1. State the three sectors and give one example for each.
2. Define opportunity cost.
3. A baker can make 100 loaves (profit £70) or 60 cakes (profit £84) or 30 pies (profit £51) with the same oven time. She chooses cakes. State the opportunity cost.
4. Name the four factors of production.
5. Outline two characteristics of an entrepreneur.
6. State two named causes of a changing business environment.
7. Which two structures have unlimited liability?
8. Explain one difference between a ltd and a plc.
9. Two partners share profit 5:3. Profit is £40,000. Calculate each share.
10. A company declares dividends of £30,000 on 50,000 shares. Calculate the dividend for someone holding 2,500 shares.
11. Give one benefit of being a sole trader and one drawback.
12. State what happens to surplus in a not-for-profit organisation.

### Answers

1. Primary (farming), secondary (manufacturing furniture), tertiary (retail). Other examples are acceptable.
2. The next best alternative given up when a choice is made.
3. The next best is the loaves at £70, so the opportunity cost is £70.
4. Land, labour, capital and enterprise.
5. Any two of hard working, innovative, organised, willing to take a risk, each with a short explanation.
6. Any two of technology, economic situation, legislation, environmental expectations.
7. Sole traders and partnerships.
8. A plc may sell shares to the public; a ltd may not.
9. 5/8 × £40,000 = £25,000 and 3/8 × £40,000 = £15,000.
10. £30,000 ÷ 50,000 = £0.60 per share; 2,500 × £0.60 = £1,500.
11. Benefit: full control and all the profit. Drawback: unlimited liability.
12. It is normally reinvested into the organisation's purpose, not shared out among owners.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Naming the opportunity cost as the money spent, not the next best alternative.
- Stating that limited liability means the business cannot lose money.
- Giving a generic list of benefits for each structure without linking to the business in the scenario.
- Saying a plc is run by the government.
- Giving only one side in an evaluation, or no judgement.
- Describing entrepreneurial characteristics without showing how they help the business.
- Classifying a business into the wrong sector because of the product it sells rather than the activity it carries out.
- Forgetting to show a calculation step, such as the shortfall before deciding who is liable.
