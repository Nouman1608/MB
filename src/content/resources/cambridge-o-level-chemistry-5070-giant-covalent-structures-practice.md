These questions cover section 2.6, Giant covalent structures, of the Cambridge O Level Chemistry 5070 syllabus (2026–2028). Revise first with the [giant covalent structures study guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-chemistry-5070-giant-covalent-structures/) and the [giant covalent structures revision notes](/resources/cambridge-o-level-chemistry-5070-giant-covalent-structures-revision-notes/).

## Section A

**1.** State the number of covalent bonds formed by each carbon atom in diamond and in graphite. **[2]**

**2.** State the type of particle in graphite that allows it to conduct electricity. **[1]**

**3.** In silicon(IV) oxide, state the number of covalent bonds formed by:

**(a)** each silicon atom **[1]**

**(b)** each oxygen atom **[1]**

**4.** Give **one** use of diamond and **one** use of graphite named in the syllabus. **[2]**

**5.** State **two** physical properties shared by diamond and silicon(IV) oxide. **[2]**

**6.** A student writes: "Graphite has a low melting point because it is soft." State whether the student is correct and give a reason. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** Describe the structure of graphite. **[4]**

**8.** Explain why graphite is used as a lubricant. **[2]**

**9.** Explain, in terms of structure and bonding, why diamond is used in drill tips for cutting through rock. **[3]**

**10.** The table compares three substances.

| Substance | Melting point | Electrical conductivity of solid | Hardness |
|---|---|---|---|
| S | very high | conducts | soft |
| T | very high | does not conduct | very hard |
| V | low | does not conduct | soft |

**(a)** Identify which substance could be graphite and which could be silicon(IV) oxide. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain why substance T does not conduct electricity. **[1]**

**11.** Carbon dioxide and silicon(IV) oxide are both oxides of Group IV elements. Carbon dioxide is a gas at room temperature, but silicon(IV) oxide melts at over 1500 °C. Explain this difference in terms of structure and bonding. **[4]**

**12.** Diamond and graphite are both made only of carbon atoms. Explain why graphite conducts electricity but diamond does not. **[3]**

**13.** Describe the similarity in properties between diamond and silicon(IV) oxide, and explain it in terms of their structures. **[4]**

**14.** An engineer is choosing materials for three jobs: an electrode for electrolysing a molten salt, a dry lubricant for a hot oven hinge, and the cutting edge of a saw for stone.

**(a)** A colleague suggests using diamond for the electrode because, like graphite, it is pure carbon with a very high melting point. Explain why diamond is unsuitable. **[2]**

**(b)** Suggest why powdered silicon(IV) oxide would not work as a lubricant, although powdered graphite does. **[2]**

**(c)** Choose the best material of the three for the saw's cutting edge, and give the structural reason. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** Diamond: **4** [1]. Graphite: **3** [1]. **[2]**

**2.** **Delocalised electrons** [1]. **[1]**

**3. (a)** 4 [1]

**(b)** 2 [1]

**[2]**

**4.** Diamond: **cutting tools** (drill tips, saw edges) [1]. Graphite: **lubricant** or **electrode** [1]. **[2]**

**5.** Any two [1] [1]: very hard; very high melting point; do not conduct electricity; insoluble in water. **[2]**

**6.** **Not correct**: graphite has a **very high** melting point [1]; melting requires breaking the **strong covalent bonds within the layers**, while its softness is due only to weak forces between layers [1]. **[2]**

**7.** Each carbon atom forms **three covalent bonds** to other carbon atoms [1], in flat **layers of hexagonal rings** [1]; the fourth outer electron of each atom is **delocalised** [1]; the layers are held together by **weak forces** [1]. **[4]**

**8.** The forces **between the layers are weak** [1], so the layers can **slide over one another** easily, reducing friction [1]. **[2]**

**9.** Each carbon atom forms **four strong covalent bonds** [1] in a rigid **three-dimensional (tetrahedral) network** [1], so diamond is **extremely hard** and can cut rock without being worn away [1]. **[3]**

**10. (a)** Graphite: **S** [1]; silicon(IV) oxide: **T** [1]

**(b)** It has **no delocalised electrons** (and no ions) to carry charge [1]

**[3]**

**11.** Carbon dioxide is **simple molecular**: small separate CO₂ molecules [1] held by **weak intermolecular forces**, which need little energy to overcome [1]. Silicon(IV) oxide is a **giant covalent structure** [1] in which melting requires breaking **many strong covalent bonds**, needing a large amount of energy [1]. **[4]**

**12.** In graphite each carbon forms **three** bonds, leaving **one delocalised electron** per atom [1] that is **free to move** along the layers and carry charge [1]. In diamond all **four** outer electrons are used in **covalent bonds**, so there are no delocalised electrons [1]. **[3]**

**13.** Both are **very hard** and have **very high melting points** (and neither conducts) [1]. Both have a **giant covalent structure** [1] in which every atom is held in a rigid **three-dimensional tetrahedral network** [1] of **strong covalent bonds**, which need a lot of energy to break [1]. **[4]**

*Examiner insight:* the question asks for the similarity and the explanation; answers that compare diamond with graphite instead miss the point.

**14. (a)** Diamond **does not conduct electricity** [1], because all four outer electrons of each carbon are in covalent bonds, so there are **no delocalised electrons** [1]

**(b)** Silicon(IV) oxide has a rigid **3D network of strong covalent bonds** with **no layers** [1], so there is nothing that can slide; graphite's **layers** are held by **weak forces** and slide over one another [1]

**(c)** **Diamond** [1]: each carbon is held by four strong covalent bonds in a rigid 3D network, so it is the **hardest** of the three [1]

**[6]**

*Examiner insight:* in (a), "diamond is an insulator" earns the first mark only; the second mark needs the reason in terms of electrons.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Saying graphite conducts by moving ions.
- Writing that graphite's covalent bonds are weak.
- Giving SiO₂ a simple molecular structure like CO₂.
- Explaining hardness with "strong bonds" but not the rigid 3D network.
- Linking the electrode use to slipperiness instead of conductivity.
