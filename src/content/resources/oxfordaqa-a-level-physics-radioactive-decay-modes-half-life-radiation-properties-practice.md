## Questions

**1.** (a) Radium-224 has nucleon number 224 and proton number 88. It decays by alpha emission to a nuclide X. Find the nucleon number and proton number of X, and name X given that proton number 86 is radon. **[3]**
**(b)** Caesium-137 (A = 137, Z = 55) decays by beta-minus emission to a barium nuclide. Write the complete decay equation using A and Z values. **[3]**

**2.** (a) Fluorine-18 (A = 18, Z = 9) decays by beta-plus emission. Write the decay equation and state what changes inside the nucleus. **[3]**
**(b)** A free neutron decays. Write the equation and state why an antineutrino appears in it. **[2]**

**3.** Alpha particles from a source of one nuclide all have the same kinetic energy. Beta particles from a source of another nuclide have kinetic energies spread continuously from near zero up to a maximum. Explain how this difference led to the hypothesis that the neutrino exists. **[4]**

**4.** A detector measured the count rate near a source. With the source removed the background rate was 30 counts per minute.

| t / min | 0 | 5 | 10 | 15 | 20 | 30 | 40 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Measured rate / counts per min | 1230 | 846 | 586 | 408 | 287 | 149 | 85 |

(a) Describe how you would use these data to find the half-life of the source. **[3]**
**(b)** Use your method to show the half-life is about 9 minutes, and find the corrected rate at t = 36 min. **[3]**

**5.** A sample of sodium-24 has an activity of 2.4 × 10⁶ Bq. Its half-life is 15 h. Find the activity after 60 h, and the least time, in whole half-lives, after which the activity is below one thousandth of its starting value. **[4]**

**6.** (a) Technetium-99m (A = 99, Z = 43) emits a gamma ray and becomes technetium-99. Write the equation. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain two reasons why technetium-99m is suitable for medical diagnosis. **[2]**
**(c)** A dose has an activity of 360 MBq at 07:00. Take the half-life as 6.0 h. Find its activity at 19:00. **[2]**

**7.** A student identifies the radiation from a source using a Geiger-Müller tube at a fixed distance. The background rate is 18 counts per minute.

| Absorber | None | Paper | 1 mm aluminium | 3 mm aluminium | 6 mm aluminium | 10 mm aluminium | 10 mm aluminium + 25 mm lead |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Measured rate / counts per min | 1450 | 1442 | 1019 | 396 | 236 | 229 | 92 |

(a) Find the corrected rates and decide which of alpha, beta and gamma are emitted. **[5]**
**(b)** Give two precautions the student should take when carrying out the experiment. **[2]**

**8.** (a) Explain why an alpha emitter is considered more hazardous inside the body than outside it. **[3]**
**(b)** Aluminium foil is rolled to a required thickness. Suggest a suitable type of radiation for a thickness gauge, and justify the choice of both the radiation and the half-life of the source. **[4]**

**9.** The count rate from a small gamma source was measured at several distances. A background of 0.6 counts per second was present in each measured value.

| r / m | 0.20 | 0.30 | 0.40 | 0.50 | 0.80 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Measured rate / counts per s | 90.6 | 40.6 | 23.1 | 15.0 | 6.2 |

(a) Show that the data are consistent with I = I₀/r². **[4]**
**(b)** Give one source of error in this experiment and one safety precaution. **[2]**
**(c)** Predict the corrected rate at 1.2 m. **[1]**

**10.** A hand-held detector counts for 1 minute with no source three times, giving 54, 61 and 57 counts. With a gamma source at 0.30 m it gives 1337 counts in 1 minute.
**(a)** Find the corrected count rate at 0.30 m. **[2]**
**(b)** Find the count rate that the detector would record at 0.90 m, including background. **[3]**
**(c)** State one reason the measured value could be higher than predicted. **[1]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** Alpha emission removes 4 from A and 2 from Z [1]. A = 220 and Z = 86 [1]. Proton number 86 is **radon-220** [1].
**(b)** Beta-minus: Z rises by 1 so the barium nuclide has Z = 56 [1]. A stays 137 [1]. Cs-137 (137, 55) → Ba-137 (137, 56) + e⁻ (0, −1) + antineutrino (0, 0) [1].
*Examiner insight:* In a decay equation, each particle and each number needs to be right, so leaving out the antineutrino or writing the electron's A as 1 loses credit even when the daughter is right.

**2. (a)** F-18 (18, 9) → O-18 (18, 8) + e⁺ (0, +1) + neutrino (0, 0) [2]. A proton changes into a neutron [1].
**(b)** n (1, 0) → p (1, 1) + e⁻ (0, −1) + antineutrino [1]. An antineutrino accompanies a beta-minus electron, as in beta-minus decay [1].
*Examiner insight:* Beta-plus takes the neutrino and beta-minus the antineutrino. Linking the particle to the correct decay is a typical point to state clearly.

**3.** Alpha decay shares a fixed energy release between two bodies, so each alpha has one energy [1]. If beta decay also had two bodies, beta particles would all have one energy [1]. A continuous range and missing energy and momentum means a third particle shares the release [1]. This particle must be neutral and very weakly interacting, which was called the neutrino [1].
*Examiner insight:* A strong answer states the comparison with alpha decay and refers to both energy and momentum, as the specification does; "something else is emitted" alone is too vague.

**4. (a)** Subtract the 30 counts per minute background from every reading [1]. Plot corrected rate against time and draw a smooth curve [1]. Read the time for the corrected rate to halve, repeating from more than one starting value, and compare them [1].
**(b)** Corrected values are 1200, 816, 556, 378, 257, 119 and 55 [1]. Rate falls from 1200 to 600 at about 9 min, and from 600 to 300 at about 18 min, so each halving takes about **9 min** [1]. 36 min is 4 half-lives, so 1200 ÷ 16 = **75 counts per minute** corrected (105 measured) [1].
*Examiner insight:* Working with the uncorrected readings makes the half-life come out too long, so show the subtraction explicitly and say which rate you quote.

**5.** 60 ÷ 15 = 4 half-lives [1]. Activity = 2.4 × 10⁶ ÷ 16 = **1.5 × 10⁵ Bq** [1]. (½)⁹ = 1/512 is not below 1/1000, but (½)¹⁰ = 1/1024 is [1]. Time = 10 × 15 = **150 h** [1].
*Examiner insight:* Show the number of half-lives and the fraction. A bare final time with no reason for taking 10 rather than 9 is hard to credit.

**6. (a)** Tc-99m (99, 43) → Tc-99 (99, 43) + γ [1], with A and Z unchanged and no other particle [1].
**(b)** It emits gamma, which leaves the body to be detected [1], and no alpha or beta, so the dose is lower; its half-life of about 6 h is long enough for a scan but short enough to limit exposure [1].
**(c)** 07:00 to 19:00 is 12 h = 2 half-lives [1]. 360 ÷ 4 = **90 MBq** [1].
*Examiner insight:* "Suitable" needs a property linked to a consequence. Saying only "short half-life" without the effect on the patient does not give the reason.

**7. (a)** Corrected rates: 1432, 1424, 1001, 378, 218, 211, 74 counts per minute [1]. Paper changes the rate by less than 1%, so alpha is not emitted [1]. Aluminium cuts 1424 to about 215, so beta is emitted [1]. The rate hardly changes between 6 mm and 10 mm of aluminium, so the remaining radiation is not beta [1]. Lead then reduces it from 211 to 74, so gamma is emitted [1]. The source emits **beta and gamma**.
**(b)** Measure the background first and subtract it [1]. Keep the distance and counting time fixed, and handle the source with tongs [1].
*Examiner insight:* Quote corrected values and say what each change shows. Alpha only travels a few centimetres in air, so a source far from the tube shows no paper effect because the alpha never arrives; tie the conclusion to the distance used.

**8. (a)** Outside the body, the outer layer of dead skin absorbs alpha [1]. Inside the body, alpha is strongly ionising [1] and deposits all its energy in a very small volume of living tissue [1].
**(b)** Beta [1], because a few millimetres of aluminium absorbs beta, so the count rate changes with thickness [1]. Alpha would be stopped entirely and gamma would pass through almost unchanged [1]. A long half-life keeps the source activity steady, so a change in count rate is due to thickness [1].
*Examiner insight:* A choice of radiation alone is not enough; give a reason for each choice and compare it with the other two.

**9. (a)** Subtract 0.6: corrected 90.0, 40.0, 22.5, 14.4, 5.6 [1]. Find I × r²: 3.6, 3.6, 3.6, 3.6, 3.58 [2]. These are constant within rounding, so I ∝ 1/r², consistent with I = I₀/r² [1].
**(b)** Error: the effective positions of source and tube are uncertain, which matters most at small r [1]. Safety: use tongs, store the source in a shielded box, keep exposure short [1].
**(c)** I = 3.6 ÷ 1.2² = **2.5 counts per second** [1].
*Examiner insight:* A consistency check needs calculated values and a conclusion, not only "rate falls as distance rises".

**10. (a)** Mean background = (54 + 61 + 57) ÷ 3 = 57.3 per minute [1]. Corrected = 1337 − 57.3 = **1280 per minute** [1].
**(b)** 0.90 m is 3 times 0.30 m, so the corrected rate falls by 9 [1]. 1280 ÷ 9 = 142 per minute [1]. Add background: 142 + 57.3 = 199.5, so about **200 per minute** [1].
**(c)** Gamma scattered from nearby walls or objects, or a higher background during the count, adds counts [1].
*Examiner insight:* The inverse-square law applies to the corrected rate, so subtract before dividing and add the background back only if the question asks for the recorded value.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Leaving out the neutrino or antineutrino in a beta equation, or choosing the wrong one.
- Writing an electron or positron with a nucleon number other than 0.
- Not subtracting the background before finding a half-life or applying the inverse-square law.
- Taking half-life from one starting point and not checking a second.
- Ranking the hazards without stating where the source is: in the body, or beyond the skin.
- Choosing a radiation for gauging without explaining why the others do not work.
- Dividing the activity by 2 once for each hour rather than once for each half-life.
- Using 1/r instead of 1/r² when checking the law.
