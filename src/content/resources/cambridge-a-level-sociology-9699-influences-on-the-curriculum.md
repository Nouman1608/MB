Section 5.3 of Cambridge International A Level Sociology (9699) asks why schools teach what they teach. The syllabus lists four strands: the social construction of knowledge; factors influencing the content of the curriculum, including power, status, culture, economic demands and gender; education and cultural reproduction, including the ethnocentric curriculum, the gendered curriculum and the hidden curriculum; and the curriculum and the concept of cultural capital. It is examined on Paper 3 – Education and connects to the key concept of Power, control and resistance.

The [education, society and the curriculum overview](/resources/a-level-sociology-9699-education-society-and-the-curriculum/) introduces Apple, Coard, Spender, Jackson, Illich and Bourdieu's forms of capital. This page adds the classic analyses of how knowledge is organised and ranked, more ways to apply each factor, and a debate about what a fair curriculum would look like.

## What "curriculum" includes

Sociologists distinguish three layers:

- the **formal curriculum**: subjects, syllabuses and exam content that are written down;
- the **hidden curriculum**: lessons learned from the way school is organised;
- the **null curriculum**: what is left out altogether, which teaches pupils that some knowledge does not count.

Answers that consider all three show stronger understanding than answers that treat the curriculum as a list of subjects.

## The social construction of knowledge

To say school knowledge is socially constructed is to say that *which* knowledge is selected, how it is divided into subjects and how it is ranked all reflect human decisions and power, rather than the nature of knowledge itself.

**Michael F. D. Young** (1971) argued that knowledge treated as high status in schools tends to be **literate** (written rather than oral), **individualistic** (assessed by individual work), **abstract** and **unrelated to everyday life**. These features match the culture of dominant groups. Practical, oral and collective knowledge is ranked lower.

**Basil Bernstein**'s "On the Classification and Framing of Educational Knowledge" (1971) offered tools to compare curricula:

- **Classification** is the strength of the boundaries between subjects. Strong classification keeps subjects separate.
- **Framing** is how much control teachers and pupils have over what is taught and how. Strong framing means little choice.

Bernstein called strongly bounded, separate-subject curricula a **collection code** and more blended curricula an **integrated code**. The concepts let you analyse any system: an exam-driven national curriculum is usually strongly classified and strongly framed.

**Nell Keddie**'s "Classroom Knowledge" (1971) showed construction happening inside one school. On a humanities course meant for all abilities, teachers gave higher streams more abstract, theoretical knowledge and lower streams more everyday, commonsense content. The same course became different knowledge for different pupils, according to teachers' views of their ability.

## Factors influencing the content of the curriculum

| Factor | How it shapes content | Applied example (general) |
|---|---|---|
| **Power** | Governments, exam bodies and elites decide what is compulsory and examined | A national curriculum set by a ministry; contested history syllabuses after political change |
| **Status** | Academic subjects carry more prestige than vocational or practical ones | University entry rewarding "traditional" subjects |
| **Culture** | Dominant language, religion and history are built into content | Instruction in a former colonial language rather than local languages |
| **Economic demands** | Governments push subjects linked to growth and employment | Expansion of computing, science and vocational qualifications |
| **Gender** | Subjects and content become associated with one sex; women's contributions may be omitted | Science examples centred on men; craft subjects divided by sex in the past |

**Power links the other factors.** Those with power decide which culture, which kinds of status and which economic needs count. Gramsci's idea of **hegemony** — rule through consent as well as force — explains why curricula that favour dominant groups are widely accepted as simply "common sense".

**Paulo Freire**, in *Pedagogy of the Oppressed* (Portuguese 1968; English 1970), criticised the **banking concept** of education, in which teachers deposit approved knowledge in passive pupils. He argued that education can either adapt people to the existing order or help them question it, which makes the curriculum political whichever way it is designed.

## Cultural reproduction through the curriculum

**Ethnocentric curriculum.** Content that treats one ethnic group's history, language and values as the norm. In many former colonies, curricula were long organised around the colonising country's language and history. Ngũgĩ wa Thiong'o's *Decolonising the Mind* (1986) argued that teaching through a colonial language shapes how children see their own culture. Apply this beyond Britain: any multi-ethnic society faces choices about whose language and history are central.

**Gendered curriculum.** This includes subject choices that follow stereotypes, content that leaves out women's contributions, and classroom practices that give boys more space. Radical feminists see this as part of patriarchy; liberal feminists point to reforms such as compulsory science for all and more diverse examples as evidence of progress.

**Hidden curriculum.** Ranking, timetabling, uniform and teacher authority teach lessons not written in any syllabus. Functionalists read these as preparation for adult roles; Marxists as preparation for obedient work; feminists as reinforcement of gender roles, for example through dress codes or who is asked to do which classroom jobs.

## The curriculum and cultural capital

Bourdieu's argument is that a curriculum based on dominant-class culture is not neutral: pupils who have met its books, art and ways of speaking at home find school knowledge familiar and appear naturally able. The curriculum therefore converts embodied cultural capital into institutionalised capital — qualifications.

**A debate about the solution.** Young later changed position. In *Bringing Knowledge Back In* (2008), he argued that all pupils have a right to **powerful knowledge**: specialised, subject-based knowledge that lets people think beyond their own experience. On this view, giving working-class pupils only "relevant" everyday content, as Keddie observed, denies them the knowledge elites take for granted. Critics reply that deciding what counts as powerful knowledge is itself an exercise of power.

This debate gives you a strong evaluation point: is a fair curriculum one that reflects every group's culture, or one that gives every group access to the same high-status knowledge?

## Worked example: Question 3 style

*Quotation:* "The school curriculum is chosen because it contains the most useful knowledge." Give two arguments against this view.

> **Argument 1.** Young (1971) argues that high-status school knowledge is literate, abstract, individualistic and unrelated to daily life. Many practical skills are arguably more useful but are ranked lower. This suggests content is chosen for its fit with dominant-class culture rather than its usefulness. However, Young's later argument for powerful knowledge suggests abstract knowledge can be genuinely useful.
>
> **Argument 2.** The curriculum reflects power, not neutral judgement. Governments and exam bodies decide content, and an ethnocentric curriculum gives priority to the dominant group's language and history. When a minority's history is left out — the null curriculum — this reflects whose voice counts, not whether that knowledge is useful.

## Worked essay plan

*"The content of the curriculum reflects the interests of powerful groups." Evaluate this view.*

1. Define curriculum (formal, hidden, null) and social construction of knowledge.
2. Support: Young on high-status knowledge; Apple; Gramsci's hegemony; ethnocentric and gendered content; Bourdieu on cultural capital.
3. Mechanisms: Bernstein's classification and framing; Keddie on knowledge differentiated by stream.
4. Against: functionalists — a shared curriculum builds solidarity; economic demands may serve everyone's employability; reforms have diversified content.
5. Evaluation: Young's powerful knowledge argument complicates the simple "elite knowledge" critique.
6. Judgement: power shapes selection, but the knowledge selected may still benefit those given access to it.

## Common mistakes

- Treating the hidden curriculum as part of the formal curriculum.
- Using "ethnocentric" to mean "racist teachers"; it describes curriculum content.
- Assuming economic demands are neutral; who defines the economy's needs is itself a question of power.
- Discussing the gendered curriculum only as subject choice, ignoring content and classroom practice.

## Exam technique

- In Question 2, choose two clearly different factors (for example status and gender) rather than two versions of one.
- Apply concepts to a society you know: language of instruction and contested history content work in almost any country.
- For "Evaluate", reach a judgement on whether power is *the* main influence or one factor among several.
