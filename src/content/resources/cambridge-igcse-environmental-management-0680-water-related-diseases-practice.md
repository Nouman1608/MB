## Section A

**1.** Define the term *vector*. **[1]**

**2.** Name the organism that causes malaria and the organism that carries it. **[2]**

**3.** State **two** strategies to control cholera. **[2]**

**4.** State what is meant by *potable water*. **[1]**

**5.** Give **two** places near homes where mosquitoes may breed. **[2]**

## Section B

**6.** Describe how malaria is passed from one person to another. **[4]**

**7.** Explain why draining ponds and covering water containers reduces malaria in a village. **[3]**

**8.** A health team plans to release sterilised male mosquitoes in a town.
(a) Explain how this reduces the mosquito population.
(b) Suggest **one** limitation of this strategy. **[4]**

**9.** The table shows malaria cases in a district before and after a programme to distribute insecticide-treated bed nets.

| Year | Cases per 1,000 people |
|---|---|
| Before programme | 240 |
| Two years after | 150 |
| Five years after | 190 |

(a) Calculate the percentage fall in cases between before the programme and two years after.
(b) Suggest **two** reasons why cases rose again between two and five years after. **[4]**

**10.** After a flood, cholera breaks out in a camp for displaced people. Describe **three** strategies that would control the outbreak and explain how each one works. **[6]**

**11.** Explain why bed nets and insect repellent would not help to control cholera. **[2]**

**12.** A government has money for **one** of two malaria programmes: spraying insecticides over wetlands, or supplying antimalarial drugs and vaccination for children. Discuss the benefits and limitations of each programme. **[6]**

**13.** Discuss the benefits and limitations of boiling and chlorination as ways to provide potable water in a rural area. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** An organism that carries a disease-causing organism from one host to another [1].

**2.** Cause: the *Plasmodium* parasite [1]. Carrier (vector): the female *Anopheles* mosquito [1].

**3.** Any two, 1 mark each: handwashing [1]; boiling or chlorination of drinking water [1]. Also accept adequate sanitation and sewage treatment, or vaccination.

**4.** Water that is safe to drink [1].

**5.** Any two, 1 mark each: ponds or puddles [1]; uncovered water containers or tanks [1]. Also accept blocked drains, irrigation channels, or any still or slow-moving water.

**6.** A female *Anopheles* mosquito feeds on the blood of an infected person [1] and takes in the *Plasmodium* parasite [1]; the parasite develops inside the mosquito [1]; when the mosquito bites another person, it passes the parasite into their blood [1].

**7.** Female mosquitoes lay eggs on still water [1]; removing or covering the water leaves nowhere for eggs to be laid and larvae to develop [1]; so fewer adult mosquitoes are produced and fewer people are bitten and infected [1].

**8. (a)** Large numbers of sterilised males are released and mate with wild females [1]; these matings produce no offspring [1]; so the next generation is smaller and the population falls over time [1].
**(b)** Any one: expensive to rear and sterilise millions of mosquitoes, or it takes several generations to have an effect [1]. Also accept: mosquitoes from untreated areas can fly in.

**9. (a)** Fall = 240 − 150 = 90 [1]; 90 / 240 × 100 = 37.5% [1].
**(b)** Any two, 1 mark each: nets became torn or the insecticide wore off and they were not replaced [1]; mosquitoes became resistant to the insecticide on the nets [1]. Also accept: people stopped using the nets regularly, or a wetter period increased breeding sites.

**10.** Three strategies, 2 marks each. Supply potable water by chlorinating or boiling it [1], which kills the cholera bacteria before people drink it [1]. Provide adequate sanitation such as latrines away from water sources [1], so faeces containing bacteria do not contaminate water [1]. Promote handwashing with soap and supply soap and clean water [1], which removes bacteria from hands before eating or preparing food [1]. Also accept vaccination, giving protection to people who are exposed to the bacteria.

**11.** Cholera is spread by bacteria in water or food contaminated with faeces [1]; it has no insect vector, so stopping mosquito bites does not stop infection [1].

**12.** Spraying: benefit, kills large numbers of mosquitoes and larvae across the breeding area, protecting everyone [1]; limitation, harms non-target species in the wetland and mosquitoes may become resistant, so spraying must be repeated [1]. Drugs and vaccination: benefit, treat infected children and reduce severe illness, saving lives directly [1]; benefit, fewer infected people means fewer mosquitoes pick up the parasite [1]; limitation, the vaccine gives only partial protection and the parasite can become resistant to drugs [1]. Judgement, linked to the scenario, for example: drugs and vaccination protect the most vulnerable group without harming the wetland, but neither programme alone removes the vector, so a combination is best [1].

**13.** Boiling: benefit, simple and kills bacteria, and can be done in any home [1]; limitation, needs fuel, which costs money and may lead to cutting trees for firewood [1]. Chlorination: benefit, treats large volumes and keeps water safe in storage or pipes [1]; limitation, needs a regular supply of chemicals and correct dosing, which is hard to organise in remote areas [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "mosquitoes cause malaria" instead of naming the parasite and calling the mosquito the vector.
- Describing transmission in only one direction, from mosquito to person.
- Suggesting a malaria strategy for a cholera question, or the reverse.
- Calculating a percentage change from the new value instead of the original value.
- Giving a list of strategies in a "discuss" question with no limitations and no judgement.
