## Section A

**1.** Which of these materials are attracted by a magnet: aluminium, cobalt, copper, nickel, steel? **[2]**

**2.** A student has three identical-looking metal bars, X, Y and Z. End 1 of X repels end 1 of Y. End 2 of Z is attracted to both ends of X. State which bars must be magnets and explain how you know. **[3]**

**3.** State two rules that must be followed when drawing magnetic field lines. **[2]**

**4.** A plotting compass is placed at a point near a bar magnet. State what the needle shows about the magnetic field at that point. **[1]**

**5.** Explain why an unmagnetised iron key is attracted to the N pole of a magnet. **[3]**

## Section B

**6.** A recycling plant uses a large electromagnet to lift iron and steel scrap from a mixture of waste and drop it into a separate skip.

**(a)** State why aluminium cans are not lifted by the electromagnet. **[1]**

**(b)** The core of the electromagnet is made of iron. Explain why a steel core would be unsuitable. **[2]**

**(c)** Some steel scrap still clings together in a lump after the current is switched off. Explain why. **[2]**

**7.** A student investigates the magnetic field around a single bar magnet using a plotting compass.

**(a)** Describe the method the student should use to draw one field line. **[4]**

**(b)** The student then repeats the experiment using iron filings. Give one advantage and one disadvantage of iron filings compared with a plotting compass. **[2]**

**(c)** Suggest why the student should carry out the experiment away from steel objects. **[1]**

**8.** Two bar magnets are placed in a line on a bench with their N poles facing each other, 8 cm apart.

**(a)** Describe the shape of the field pattern between the two N poles. **[2]**

**(b)** A plotting compass is placed exactly halfway between the poles. Explain why the needle does not point along the line joining the magnets. **[2]**

**(c)** One magnet is turned round so that an S pole now faces the other magnet's N pole. State two ways in which the field pattern between them changes. **[2]**

**9.** A teacher needs a region of uniform magnetic field for a demonstration.

**(a)** State what is meant by a uniform magnetic field. **[1]**

**(b)** Describe how two permanent magnets should be arranged to produce a uniform field. **[3]**

**(c)** Describe how the uniform field would be shown on a diagram. **[2]**

**(d)** Explain why the field is not uniform near the edges of the gap. **[1]**

**10.** A compass needle is a thin magnetised strip balanced on a pivot.

**(a)** Explain whether the needle should be made from a magnetically hard or a magnetically soft material. **[2]**

**(b)** A small piece of soft iron is held close to the compass. The needle swings towards the iron, whichever end of the needle is nearer. Explain this observation. **[2]**

**11.** A student says: "Field lines show where the magnetic field is, and the field is zero between the lines." Evaluate this statement. **[3]**

**12.** Describe how a student could find out whether a steel paper clip has become magnetised after being stroked with a magnet. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** Cobalt, nickel and steel [2: all three for 2 marks; two correct with no wrong answer for 1 mark; any wrong answer included loses 1 mark].

**2.** X and Y must be magnets [1], because repulsion only happens between two magnets [1]. Z cannot be shown to be a magnet: being attracted to both ends of X is what an unmagnetised magnetic material does [1].

**3.** Any two from: lines go from N to S outside the magnet (with arrows) [1]; lines never cross [1]; lines are closer together where the field is stronger [1]; maximum 2.

**4.** The direction of the field at that point: the N end of the needle points along the field line in the direction of the field [1].

**5.** The magnet's field induces magnetism in the key [1]. The end nearest the N pole becomes an S pole [1]. Unlike poles attract, so the key is pulled towards the magnet [1].

**6. (a)** Aluminium is not a magnetic material [1].
**(b)** Steel is magnetically hard [1], so it would stay magnetised when the current is switched off and would not release the scrap [1].
**(c)** The steel pieces were magnetised by induction in the electromagnet's field [1]; steel is magnetically hard, so it keeps some magnetism and the pieces still attract each other [1].

**7. (a)** Place the magnet on paper and draw round it [1]; put the compass near one pole and mark a dot where the needle's N end points [1]; move the compass so its other end is at the dot and mark again, repeating until the other pole is reached [1]; join the dots with a smooth curve and add an arrow from N to S [1].
**(b)** Advantage: shows the whole pattern quickly [1]. Disadvantage: does not show the direction of the field [1].
**(c)** Steel is magnetic (it becomes magnetised in the field), so it would distort the field pattern being measured [1].

**8. (a)** Field lines from each N pole curve away from the other magnet [1]; no lines cross the gap between the poles [1].
**(b)** At the midpoint the two fields are equal in strength and opposite in direction, so they cancel [1]; this is a neutral point with no resultant field, so the needle has no preferred direction [1].
**(c)** Field lines now pass across the gap from the N pole to the S pole [1]; there is no neutral point between them (and the magnets now attract) [1].

**9. (a)** A field with the same strength and direction at every point in the region [1].
**(b)** Use two flat magnets [1], placed parallel to each other [1], with unlike poles (N and S) facing across the gap [1].
**(c)** Straight, parallel field lines [1], equally spaced, with arrows from N to S [1].
**(d)** The field lines spread out (bulge outwards) at the edges, so the spacing and direction change there [1].

**10. (a)** Magnetically hard [1], because the needle must keep its magnetism permanently to point along the field [1].
**(b)** The compass needle induces magnetism in the soft iron [1]; the induced pole nearest the needle is always the unlike pole, so the needle is attracted to the iron whichever end is closer [1].

**11.** The statement is incorrect [1]. Field lines are a model: the field exists everywhere around the magnet, including between the lines drawn [1]. The lines show the direction of the field, and their spacing shows its strength, not where it is present [1].

**12.** Bring each end of the paper clip close to each pole of a known magnet in turn [1]. If one end is repelled by a pole, the clip is magnetised [1]. Attraction alone does not show magnetisation, because an unmagnetised clip is attracted to both poles [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Answering "it is attracted" as the test for a magnet.
- Saying "steel is hard" without the magnetic meaning: hard to magnetise and keeps its magnetism.
- Describing the compass method without saying the dot is placed at the N end of the needle, or without joining dots into a curve.
- Missing "unlike poles facing" and "parallel" in the uniform-field arrangement.
- Forgetting that a neutral point exists only between like poles.
