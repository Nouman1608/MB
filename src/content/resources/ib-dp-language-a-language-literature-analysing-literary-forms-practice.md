## Questions

### Passage A -- prose

> I was not upset when Mother left the house to Clara. I want that understood. I had my own flat by
> then, three rooms above the chemist's, and a view of the canal if you leaned out far enough. Clara
> had always loved the house more than I had; everyone said so. Even at nine she would stand in the
> hall and name the rooms aloud, like a woman showing guests around. I never did that. I was the one
> who found the loose board under the stairs, the one who kept a tin of buttons hidden there for
> eleven years. But that was a child's game. When the solicitor read the will, I nodded, and I
> believe I smiled. Afterwards, on the bus, I found my hand closed so tightly around my gloves that
> the seams had left marks on my palm.

### Passage B -- poetry: "The Key"

> My father wound the kitchen clock each week
> on Sunday, with a key he kept from me;
> he counted out the turns and would not speak
> of who had owned it first. I let it be.
> The winter that he died, the clock ran down.
> I found the key beneath his folded shirts,
> a woman's name cut small, a far-off town,
> a date, and nothing else. The nothing hurts.
> I could have left it there. I could have thrown
> the key into the river by the mill.
> Instead I wound the clock, alone, alone,
> and heard it keeping time, as he kept still.
> Some Sundays now I count the turns out loud
> and say her name, which he was not allowed.

### Passage C -- drama

> *(A kitchen, late evening. A biscuit tin sits on the top shelf of a dresser. RUTH, in her sixties,
> is sorting papers at the table. Her nephew DANIEL enters, still in his coat.)*
> DANIEL: You said there was nothing left to sign.
> RUTH: There isn't. *(She slides a large envelope under the pile.)* Tea?
> DANIEL: I can't stay. The agent wants the keys by Friday.
> RUTH: Then he'll have them by Friday.
> *(DANIEL crosses to the dresser and touches the tin. RUTH stops sorting.)*
> DANIEL: Gran kept buttons in this.
> RUTH: *(Too quickly.)* Leave that. It's only rubbish now.
> *(He lets go. She does not start sorting again until he has turned away.)*
> DANIEL: Friday, then.
> RUTH: Friday.
> *(He goes. RUTH takes the tin down, opens it, and sits looking at what is inside. The audience
> does not see it. Lights fade.)*

**1.** Passage A. Identify the narrative voice and support your answer with a quotation. **[2]**

**2.** Passage A. Explain how "I want that understood" and one other detail suggest the narrator may
be unreliable. **[4]**

**3.** Passage A. Explain the effect of the move back in time to when Clara was nine. **[3]**

**4.** Passage A. How does the final sentence show the narrator's feelings rather than state them? **[3]**

**5.** Passage B. Identify the form of the poem and its rhyme scheme, with evidence. **[3]**

**6.** Passage B. Scan the first line and name its metre. **[2]**

**7.** Passage B. Explain the effect of the enjambment from line 3 to line 4 and the caesura in line 4. **[4]**

**8.** Passage B. How does the final couplet change the meaning of the poem? **[4]**

**9.** Passage C. Explain what one stage direction reveals that the dialogue hides. **[3]**

**10.** Passage C. Explain how dramatic irony works in this scene, including the final stage
direction. **[4]**

**11.** Passage B. Guiding question: *How does the poet use the sonnet form to present what is
inherited and what is left unsaid?* Write a thesis and a three-paragraph plan with evidence. **[8]**

**12.** Passages A and C. Plan a comparative response: *Compare how the writers present something
hidden, with reference to the conventions of prose fiction and drama.* Give a thesis and three
woven comparative points. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** First person [1]: the narrator is a character who speaks as "I", e.g. "I was not upset" [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Naming the voice without a quotation earns only half; the evidence is what makes it
an answer.

**2.** "I want that understood" insists too firmly, which invites doubt [1]; it is addressed to a
listener the narrator seems to need to convince [1]. The final detail of the gloves contradicts the
claim "I was not upset" [1]. "I believe I smiled" shows the narrator is unsure of their own behaviour,
so the account cannot be fully trusted [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Strong answers show the text contradicting the narrator, not just call the
narrator "biased".

**3.** The flashback contrasts Clara, who names "the rooms aloud" in the open [1], with the narrator's
secret attachment: the "loose board" and hidden "tin of buttons" [1]. It suggests the narrator loved
the house just as much but in hiding, so the inheritance takes away something only the narrator knew about [1].
**[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Say what the flashback lets the reader infer about the present, not just that the
time changes.

**4.** The narrator reports only a physical detail: a hand "closed so tightly" [1]. The seams leaving
"marks on my palm" imply anger or grief that the narrator has denied [1]. Because the reader draws
the conclusion, it feels truer than the narrator's statements and completes the case against the
denial [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* "Show, don't tell" is a label; credit comes for explaining what the reader infers.

**5.** A sonnet: fourteen lines [1]. Shakespearean: three quatrains and a closing couplet [1]. Rhyme
scheme ABAB CDCD EFEF GG, e.g. week/speak, me/be, and the couplet loud/allowed [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Check the rhyme scheme before naming the sonnet type; the line count alone is not
enough.

**6.** my **FA** | ther **WOUND** | the **KIT** | chen **CLOCK** | each **WEEK**: ten syllables, five
feet, each unstressed then stressed [1]. **Iambic pentameter** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the stresses; a metre named with no scansion cannot be checked.

**7.** Line 3 ends on "would not speak", so for a moment the silence seems total [1]. Running on to
"of who had owned it first" reveals that the silence has a subject, a hidden history [1]. The full
stop after "first" is a caesura that stops the question dead [1]; "I let it be" is short and flat,
showing the child accepting the silence [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Tie each line-break effect to the words at the break; general claims about "flow"
earn little.

**8.** The couplet moves into the present tense with "now", marking the turn [1]. "count the turns out
loud" reverses the father's silent counting in line 3 [1]. Speaking "her name" breaks the silence the
quatrains built up [1]. "which he was not allowed" reveals the father was forbidden, not unwilling,
changing him from secretive to constrained, and turning the poem from grief into a kind of release [1].
**[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Top answers show how the couplet echoes earlier lines; the turn is an argument, not
just a change of mood.

**9.** "*She slides a large envelope under the pile*" [1] comes straight after "There isn't", so the
action contradicts her words [1]. The audience sees she is concealing something from Daniel about the
house, which creates suspicion [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Treat stage directions as evidence equal to dialogue; quote them like any other
line.

**10.** The audience sees Ruth hide the envelope; Daniel does not [1]. "*Too quickly*" and her pause in
sorting show the audience her anxiety about the tin, which Daniel misses [1]. The final direction
reverses this: "*The audience does not see it*", so now Ruth knows more than we do [1]. The scene ends
on unanswered suspense, making the audience feel what Daniel felt: shut out [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Explain who knows what at each moment; "there is dramatic irony" alone is
identification.

**11.** Model plan (original writing for Marlbridge). The real task is marked holistically against
the IB criteria your teacher will share; these points are indicative.

- *Thesis:* the sonnet's strict pattern mirrors the father's rituals of silence, and the final
  couplet breaks that silence by speaking the name [1].
- *Point 1, quatrain 1:* the ritual "each week" and counted "turns" match the regular metre; the
  enjambment "would not speak / of who" places the secret at the line break [1]; evidence quoted and
  embedded [1].
- *Point 2, quatrain 2:* the list "a woman's name ... a far-off town, / a date" gives facts without
  story [1]; caesura and repetition in "The nothing hurts" make absence the subject [1].
- *Point 3, quatrain 3 and couplet:* anaphora "I could have ... I could have" weighs rejected choices
  [1]; "kept still" plays on keeping time and keeping silent [1]; the couplet's "out loud" and "not
  allowed" turn inheritance from an object into a voice [1]. **[8]**

*Examiner insight:* Plans that follow the guiding question in every point beat plans that walk through
the poem line by line.

**12.** Model plan (original writing for Marlbridge). Indicative points only; the IB marks the real
task holistically.

| Point | Passage A (prose) | Passage C (drama) |
|---|---|---|
| Who hides | The narrator hides grief and anger behind denial [1] | Ruth hides objects from Daniel, and finally from us [1] |
| How form reveals | First-person denial undercut by a physical detail [1] | Stage directions contradict dialogue [1] |
| Hidden object | Tin under the stairs, told in a flashback [1] | Tin on the dresser, present and visible on stage [1] |

- *Thesis:* both writers present concealment through a gap between words and actions, but prose
  exposes it through a narrator who gives the game away, while drama makes the audience watch and then
  withholds [1].
- Woven comparison inside each point, not block by block [1]; embedded quotations from both passages
  [1].
- A closing judgement on effect: A leaves us knowing more than the narrator admits; C leaves us
  knowing less than Ruth [1]. **[10]**

*Examiner insight:* The strongest plans compare the effect of each form's conventions; weaker ones list
features of A, then features of C.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Naming a technique (enjambment, dramatic irony, unreliable narrator) without its effect in this
  passage.
- Treating the first-person narrator's statements in Passage A as reliable facts.
- Naming a metre without marking the stresses, or from a single line with no check.
- Calling a poem a sonnet from its length alone, without the rhyme scheme.
- Ignoring stage directions in drama, or quoting only dialogue.
- Quoting whole lines instead of the two or three words that do the work.
- Writing a comparative plan as two separate blocks with a "both" sentence at the end.
