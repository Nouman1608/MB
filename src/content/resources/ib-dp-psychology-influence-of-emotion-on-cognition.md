This guide covers **the influence of emotion on cognitive processes**, mainly memory and attention, for IB DP Psychology (first assessment 2027). The 2027 guide does not list emotion as a topic of its own. It does ask, in the learning and cognition context, for the role of biological factors and the influence of environmental factors on cognitive processes (attention, memory, perception or language), and the research here gives examples of both: arousal hormones and the amygdala as biological factors, and an emotionally charged event as an environmental influence. The [cognitive approach study guide](/resources/ib-dp-psychology-cognitive-approach/) introduces reconstructive memory and schema; this page deals with a different question: what happens to attention and memory when an event is emotionally charged? The usual example is the **flashbulb memory**, supported by research on attention and on the biology of emotional memory. The 2019 course listed the influence of emotion on cognitive processes as a content point; its last examination session was November 2026.

## What the topic asks

In the 2027 guide the cognitive processes for study are attention, memory, perception and language, and its glossary also counts learning, thinking and decision-making as cognitive processes. To use this material you need to show that emotion changes at least one of them, explain how, and evaluate the evidence. Most students choose memory, because the research is well developed and allows a clear debate: does emotion make memories more accurate, or only more vivid and more confidently held?

Keep the approach in view. The topic belongs to the cognitive approach, so the explanation should be about mental processes such as attention, rehearsal and reconstruction. Biological research on the amygdala can support the argument, but it should not take over an answer to a cognitive question.

## Flashbulb memory theory

**Brown and Kulik (1977)** introduced the term **flashbulb memory**: a vivid, detailed and long-lasting memory of the circumstances in which a person learned of a surprising and important event. They asked American participants whether they remembered where they were, what they were doing and who told them when they heard of events such as the assassination of President Kennedy. Many reported detailed memories of the circumstances, years later. Black participants reported flashbulb memories more often than white participants for the assassinations of civil rights leaders such as Martin Luther King Jr, which suggests that personal importance, not just surprise, matters.

Brown and Kulik proposed a special mechanism, which they called "Now Print!". An event that is surprising and consequential triggers a neural process that records the scene almost like a photograph, which is then strengthened by rehearsal.

Typical features of a flashbulb memory, in the categories Brown and Kulik used, include the place, the ongoing activity, the informant, the person's own feelings, the feelings of others and the aftermath.

**Evaluation.** The study relied on retrospective self-report, with no record of what participants actually experienced at the time, so it measured vividness and confidence, not accuracy. Its strength is that it identified a real phenomenon, the feeling of a vivid memory, and two factors that seem to produce it.

## Testing accuracy: Neisser and Harsch (1992)

To test accuracy, a researcher needs a record made at the time. **Neisser and Harsch (1992)** gave American university students a questionnaire the morning after the explosion of the space shuttle *Challenger* in 1986, asking how they had heard the news. About two and a half years later, students answered the same questions and rated their confidence.

Many later accounts differed substantially from the originals, sometimes in every major detail, yet many students were highly confident in them, and some insisted their later version was correct when shown their own earlier handwriting. Neisser argued that flashbulb memories are reconstructed like other memories: they feel special because the event is rehearsed and retold, and each retelling can change the story.

**Evaluation.** The test-retest design gives strong evidence about accuracy, because the first record was made within a day. However, the sample was small and drawn from one university, and the *Challenger* explosion may have been less personally important to some students than the events in Brown and Kulik's study.

## Comparing with everyday memories: Talarico and Rubin (2003)

**Talarico and Rubin (2003)** asked students at an American university, on 12 September 2001, to record how they had learned of the attacks of the previous day and to record an ordinary memory from the same period. Students were retested after one, six or thirty-two weeks.

Consistency declined at a similar rate for both kinds of memory. What differed was how the memories felt: ratings of vividness and belief in accuracy stayed high for the 11 September memories but fell for the everyday memories.

This study adds a control condition that Neisser and Harsch lacked. It suggests that emotion does not preserve accuracy; it preserves confidence. That is a precise conclusion you can use in an essay: emotion influences the **subjective quality** of memory more than its accuracy.

## Emotion and attention: weapon focus

Emotion can also narrow attention. **Loftus, Loftus and Messo (1987)** showed participants slides of a customer in a restaurant who held either a gun or a cheque. Eye-movement recording showed more fixations on the gun, and participants who saw the gun were less able to identify the customer afterwards. This **weapon focus** effect suggests that an emotionally arousing object captures attention, so less of the surrounding scene is encoded. It also offers a different way of using emotion and cognition in an essay: emotion influences what is attended to in the first place, which then affects what can be remembered.

## Biological support: the amygdala

Two studies show why emotional events may be remembered differently:

- **Cahill, Prins, Weber and McGaugh (1994)** gave participants either propranolol, a drug that blocks the action of adrenaline and noradrenaline at beta-adrenergic receptors, or a placebo, before they viewed a series of slides with either an emotional or a neutral story. A week later, the placebo group remembered the emotional story better than the neutral one; in the propranolol group this advantage disappeared. Arousal hormones seem to be needed for the memory boost.
- **Sharot et al. (2007)** asked people in New York, about three years after 11 September 2001, to recall the day while their brains were scanned with fMRI. Those who had been in downtown Manhattan, close to the World Trade Center, showed greater amygdala activation when recalling the attacks than when recalling ordinary summer events; those who had been further away in midtown did not show this difference so clearly.

Use these as supporting evidence: they suggest a mechanism (arousal hormones and the amygdala) that explains why emotional memories feel strong, without showing that they are accurate.

## Worked example: planning a 15-mark Section C response

Paper 1 Section C offers two concept-based extended-response questions, each from a different context. The question below is written in that style.

**Question:** "Evaluate one theory of how emotion may affect one cognitive process, with reference to the concept of measurement." **[15]**

1. **Focus:** flashbulb memory theory (Brown and Kulik, 1977) and its claim that emotional events are recorded by a special mechanism, so memory is the cognitive process.
2. **Knowledge:** define flashbulb memory, the role of surprise and consequentiality, and "Now Print!".
3. **Supporting research:** Brown and Kulik's findings on personal importance; Sharot et al. (2007) as a possible neural basis.
4. **Challenging research:** Neisser and Harsch (1992) on inconsistency with high confidence; Talarico and Rubin (2003), where flashbulb and everyday memories declined at a similar rate.
5. **Critical thinking and concept link:** measurement: how accuracy, vividness and confidence are each measured; retrospective self-report versus test-retest designs; samples of students from single universities; the role of rehearsal and the media as an alternative explanation; whether confidence and accuracy should be separated.
6. **Conclusion:** emotion clearly changes how vivid and certain a memory feels, but the evidence does not support the claim that flashbulb memories are more accurate than everyday memories.

## Common mistakes

- Treating "flashbulb memory" as proof that emotional memories are accurate.
- Describing Neisser and Harsch without explaining why the first questionnaire, completed the next morning, matters.
- Turning a cognitive-approach essay into a biology essay about the amygdala.
- Forgetting that the topic is about **cognitive processes** in general: attention, as in weapon focus, is also relevant.
- Writing "emotion improves memory" without saying which aspect: detail, accuracy, vividness or confidence.

## Exam technique

A Section B question (6 marks) gives an unseen situation in a context, for example a witness recalling an emergency in the learning and cognition context, and asks you to apply your knowledge: here, the roles of arousal, attention and memory. Paper 1 does not assess the details of research studies, so use a study to support a point, then state what it shows about emotion and memory. A Section C response (15 marks) needs evaluation, links to the concept in the question and a judgement. **Evaluate** asks for strengths and limitations of the theory or research, **discuss** for a balanced review, and **to what extent** for a conclusion about degree. Talarico and Rubin's distinction between consistency and confidence is the most useful single point for a top-band conclusion.
