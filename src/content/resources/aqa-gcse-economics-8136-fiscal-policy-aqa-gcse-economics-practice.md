These questions cover **section 3.2.3.1 Fiscal policy** in the AQA GCSE Economics (8136) specification for first teaching from September 2017: how fiscal policy affects income and expenditure, how it is used to achieve objectives, and the government budget. There are no tiers. All countries and figures are invented. For explanations, see the [fiscal policy study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-economics-8136-fiscal-policy-aqa-gcse-economics/) and the [fiscal policy revision notes](/resources/aqa-gcse-economics-8136-fiscal-policy-aqa-gcse-economics-revision-notes/).

## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Which **one** of the following is an example of contractionary fiscal policy? **[1]**

A: A cut in the Bank Rate

B: A rise in income tax

C: More government spending on schools

D: A cut in VAT

**2.** Which **one** of the following is a transfer payment? **[1]**

A: The salary of a police officer

B: The State Pension

C: The cost of building a hospital

D: Spending on army equipment

**3.** State what is meant by a budget surplus. **[1]**

**4.** Country W's government receives 860 billion units in revenue and spends 905 billion units. Its GDP is 1,800 billion units.

**(a)** Calculate the budget balance and state whether it is a deficit or a surplus. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the budget balance as a percentage of GDP. **[1]**

**5.** A government's debt is 1,200 billion units at the start of Year 1. It runs a deficit of 45 billion in Year 1, a deficit of 30 billion in Year 2 and a surplus of 15 billion in Year 3, which it uses to repay debt.

**(a)** Calculate the debt at the end of Year 3. **[2]**

**(b)** State why the debt rose in Year 2 even though the deficit was smaller than in Year 1. **[1]**

**6.** Classify each item as a direct tax, an indirect tax, current spending or capital spending. **[4]**

- VAT
- Corporation tax
- Paying nurses' wages
- Building a new bridge

**7.** Explain **one** reason why a cut in income tax may not increase consumer spending by much. **[2]**

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**8.** Explain how an increase in government spending on hospitals could affect levels of income and expenditure in the economy. **[4]**

**9.** Explain why a government's budget deficit may rise during a recession even if it does not change its policies. **[3]**

**10.** Explain **two** consequences of a government running a budget surplus. **[4]**

**11.** Country Z's inflation rate is 6%, well above its target. Unemployment is low and its current account deficit is growing. The government plans to raise income tax.

**(a)** Explain how a rise in income tax could reduce inflation. **[3]**

**(b)** Explain how the policy could affect Country Z's current account deficit. **[2]**

**12.** Country Y is in a recession and unemployment is rising. Its national debt is already high. Evaluate whether its government should increase spending on infrastructure, financed by borrowing. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** **B** [1]. Higher income tax reduces disposable income and demand; a Bank Rate cut is monetary policy. **[1]**

**2.** **B** [1]. The pension transfers income without buying any output. **[1]**

**3.** When government revenue is greater than government spending [1]. **[1]**

**4. (a)** 860 − 905 = **−45** [1], a **deficit** of 45 billion [1].

**(b)** 45 ÷ 1,800 × 100 = **2.5%** of GDP [1]. **[3]**

**5. (a)** 1,200 + 45 + 30 = 1,275 at the end of Year 2 [1]; 1,275 − 15 = **1,260 billion** [1].

**(b)** There was still a deficit, and any deficit is borrowed and adds to the debt [1]. **[3]**

**6.** VAT: indirect tax [1]. Corporation tax: direct tax [1]. Nurses' wages: current spending [1]. New bridge: capital spending [1]. **[4]**

**7.** Any one developed: households may save the extra income [1] because they are worried about their jobs or want to repay debts [1]; or they may spend it on imports [1], so it does not raise demand for domestic firms [1]. **[2]**

**8.** Spending on hospitals adds directly to demand: construction firms and suppliers receive orders [1], and the government employs more staff [1]. Those workers earn wages and spend them in other businesses [1], so those businesses' sales and incomes rise too, and total income and expenditure rise by more than the first spending [1]. **[4]**

**9.** In a recession unemployment rises, so spending on benefits increases [1]. Incomes, profits and spending fall, so receipts from income tax, corporation tax and VAT fall [1]. Spending rises while revenue falls, so the gap between them widens [1]. **[3]**

**10.** Any two developed: the government can repay debt [1], which reduces future interest payments and frees money for services [1]; it takes more out in taxes than it spends [1], so total demand is lower and growth may slow [1]; it may have been reached by cutting services or investment [1], harming future productive capacity [1]. **[4]**

**11. (a)** Higher income tax reduces households' disposable income [1], so they spend less on goods and services [1]. Lower demand reduces the pressure on firms to raise prices, so demand-pull inflation falls [1].

**(b)** Households cut spending on imports as well as domestic goods [1], so debits fall and the current account deficit narrows [1]. **[5]**

**12.** *For more borrowing to fund infrastructure:*

- In a recession, spending by households and firms is weak, so government spending supports demand and jobs [1].
- Construction workers spend their wages, so other firms gain too [1].
- New infrastructure raises productive capacity and future growth, which increases future tax revenue [1].

*Against:*

- Debt is already high, so borrowing more raises interest payments [1], leaving less for public services or needing higher taxes later [1].
- Infrastructure projects take time to plan, so jobs may come after the recession ends [1].
- Some spending may go on imported materials, weakening the effect on the domestic economy [1].

*Judgement:* the government should borrow for infrastructure if the projects can start quickly and will raise future output, because the recession makes extra demand valuable [1]; if debt and interest costs are already very high, it should choose a smaller, targeted programme [1]. **[9]**

*Examiner insight:* Use both features of the case, the recession and the high debt, in your judgement.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calling an interest-rate change fiscal policy.
- Leaving out the sign or the words "deficit" and "surplus".
- Thinking a smaller deficit reduces the debt.
- Explaining only the first effect of extra spending, not the knock-on effect on incomes.
- Judging borrowing without reference to the state of the economy.
