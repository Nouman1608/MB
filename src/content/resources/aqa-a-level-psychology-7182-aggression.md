## How Paper 3 is set out

Section 2.2 of the specification prints the structure. Paper 3 is a written exam of 2 hours, 96 marks in total, worth 33.3% of the A-level. It assesses compulsory content 8 (Issues and debates in Psychology) and "one from option 1, 9–11, one from option 2, 12–14, one from option 3, 15–17".

- **Section A:** Issues and debates, compulsory. Multiple choice, short answer and extended writing, 24 marks.
- **Section D:** "one topic from option 3, 15–17 above, multiple choice, short answer and extended writing, 24 marks". Option 3 is Aggression (15), Forensic Psychology (16) and Addiction (17).

Sections B and C cover one topic each from Option 1 and Option 2. Research methods and maths skills are also assessed in Paper 3. Your teacher will confirm your school's option choices.

## Neural and hormonal mechanisms

**The limbic system** is a set of structures under the cortex that process emotion. The **amygdala** judges threat and triggers fast emotional responses. The **hypothalamus** helps turn those responses into bodily arousal. The **orbitofrontal cortex** (OFC) normally damps limbic activity, so poor OFC control can allow reactive aggression.

- **Klüver and Bucy (1939)** removed parts of the temporal lobes, including the amygdala, in rhesus monkeys. The monkeys became much tamer.
- **Gospic et al. (2011)** scanned 35 people playing the ultimatum game, a money-splitting task. The amygdala responded quickly when unfair offers were rejected. A benzodiazepine (an anti-anxiety drug) lowered amygdala activity and made people less likely to reject unfair offers.

**Serotonin** is a neurotransmitter with an inhibitory role. It helps the OFC hold back impulses. Low serotonin is linked to impulsive aggression.

- **Raleigh et al. (1991)** found that, in vervet monkeys, treatments that raised serotonin activity were followed by less aggression, and treatments that lowered it by more.
- **Virkkunen et al. (1994)** found lower levels of a serotonin by-product in the spinal fluid of impulsive violent offenders than in non-impulsive offenders.

**Testosterone** is an androgen. It is thought to act on brain areas such as the amygdala and OFC.

- **Dabbs et al. (1987)** measured saliva testosterone in 89 male prisoners. Those with the highest levels had mostly committed violent crimes. Those with the lowest had mostly committed non-violent crimes.

**Evaluation.** Drug studies such as Gospic et al. show cause and effect, not just correlation. However, the amygdala does not act alone: the OFC and serotonin interact with it. Animal findings may not generalise, because human aggression is shaped by thought and culture. Hormone research is often correlational: violence and status contests may themselves raise testosterone. The **dual-hormone hypothesis (Mehta and Josephs, 2010)** proposes that high testosterone predicts dominance mainly when cortisol is low. A single hormone is too reductionist.

**Worked paragraph (testosterone).** *Testosterone may raise the likelihood of aggression in men. Dabbs et al. found that prisoners with the highest saliva testosterone had mostly committed violent crimes. This fits the idea that the hormone sensitises brain areas involved in threat. But the data are correlational: prison conflict could raise testosterone, so the direction of cause is unclear. The dual-hormone hypothesis suggests testosterone matters most when cortisol is low, so a single-hormone account is too simple.*

## Genetic factors and the MAOA gene

**Twin studies** compare identical (MZ) and non-identical (DZ) twins. **Coccaro et al. (1997)** studied adult male twins and estimated that genes explained about half the variance in direct physical aggression. **Rhee and Waldman (2002)** pooled twin and adoption studies and estimated that about 41% of the variance in antisocial behaviour was genetic.

**MAOA** is a gene that codes for the enzyme monoamine oxidase A. This enzyme breaks down serotonin and other neurotransmitters. A low-activity variant (MAOA-L) is linked to aggression.

- **Brunner et al. (1993)** studied 28 men in a Dutch family with a history of impulsive violence. They had a fault in the MAOA gene and low MAOA activity.
- **Caspi et al. (2002)** followed boys in Dunedin, New Zealand. Low MAOA activity predicted antisocial behaviour mainly in those who had been maltreated as children. That group was about 12% of the male cohort but accounted for 44% of its violent convictions. This is a **gene-environment interaction**.

**Evaluation.** Twin designs assume MZ and DZ twins share equally similar environments, which may not hold. Brunner's family was one unusual case. Caspi et al. show genes are not destiny: the low-activity variant mattered most with maltreatment. That supports a diathesis-stress view and avoids biological determinism.

**Worked paragraph.** *The MAOA gene helps explain why some people are more aggressive. The low-activity variant means less of the enzyme that breaks down serotonin. Caspi et al. found this variant predicted violence mostly in men maltreated as children. So the gene seems to raise vulnerability rather than cause aggression directly. This interactionist finding is a strength because it fits evidence that environment matters.*

## The ethological explanation

Ethology studies animals in their natural setting. **Lorenz** argued that aggression is **adaptive**: it spreads animals across territory and lets the strongest breed. Within a species, aggression is often **ritualistic**, ending with an appeasement display such as a wolf exposing its neck.

- An **innate releasing mechanism (IRM)** is an inborn neural process that is triggered by a specific stimulus in the environment.
- A **fixed action pattern (FAP)** is the sequence of behaviour the IRM releases. It is **universal** in the species, **ballistic** (once started, it runs to the end), **single-purpose** and triggered by a **specific stimulus**.
- **Tinbergen (1951)** showed male three-spined sticklebacks attacking crude models with a red underside but largely ignoring realistic models without red. The red patch acted as the sign stimulus.

**Evaluation.** Tinbergen's work gives controlled, repeatable evidence. But many "fixed" patterns vary with experience and context, so "action patterns" may be a better term. Ritualised aggression is not universal: Jane Goodall observed chimpanzees at Gombe killing members of a former group in the 1970s. Extending animal findings to humans is weak, because human aggression is heavily shaped by culture and learning.

**Worked paragraph.** *Aggression may be released by specific triggers. Tinbergen found sticklebacks attacked a red-bellied model even when it looked nothing like a fish. The red underside was the stimulus that set off the IRM, and the attack ran as a fixed sequence. This supports a biological, innate account. However, the Gombe chimpanzee killings show that animal aggression is not always ritualised and harmless.*

## Evolutionary explanations of human aggression

Evolutionary psychologists argue that aggression helped ancestors survive and reproduce. Males face **paternity uncertainty**: they cannot be sure a child is theirs. **Sexual jealousy** may have evolved to prevent **cuckoldry** (raising another man's child).

- **Mate retention strategies** include **direct guarding** (watching a partner's behaviour) and **negative inducements** (threats or violence to stop a partner leaving).
- **Shackelford et al. (2005)** surveyed men and women in heterosexual relationships. Men's use of mate retention tactics predicted their violence towards partners.
- **Daly and Wilson (1988)** argued that sexual jealousy is a leading motive in partner violence and homicide.

**Evaluation.** The theory explains a sex difference: men show more physical aggression than women. But it cannot easily explain cultural variation in levels of aggression, which suggests learning matters. Many claims cannot be tested directly, because the ancestral environment cannot be observed. It also risks being used to excuse violence, which raises social sensitivity.

**Worked paragraph.** *Sexual jealousy may be an evolved response to paternity uncertainty. Shackelford et al. found that men who used more mate retention tactics were more violent towards partners. This supports the claim that some aggression aims to keep a mate. But the evidence is correlational and self-reported, and the explanation is hard to falsify because ancestral behaviour cannot be observed.*

## Social psychological explanations

**Frustration-aggression hypothesis.** **Dollard et al. (1939)** proposed that frustration always leads to aggression, and aggression is always caused by frustration. Releasing aggression is **cathartic**. If the cause is unavailable or too powerful, aggression is **displaced** onto another target. **Berkowitz (1989)** revised the idea: frustration creates negative feelings, which make aggression more likely but not certain. **Berkowitz and LePage (1967)** found that angered participants gave more electric shocks when guns, rather than badminton racquets, were nearby (the "weapons effect"). **Geen (1968)** found that frustrated participants gave stronger shocks than controls. However, **Bushman (2002)** found that venting anger on a punchbag increased later aggression, which contradicts catharsis.

**Social learning theory applied to aggression.** Children learn aggression by **observing** models, especially those they **identify** with. **Vicarious reinforcement** shows them aggression pays. The mediational processes (attention, retention, reproduction, motivation) operate, and **self-efficacy** for aggression grows when it works. In **Bandura et al. (1961)**, children who watched an adult attack a Bobo doll imitated much of that aggression. In **Bandura (1965)**, children who saw a model punished imitated less, but all groups imitated when offered a reward, showing learning differs from performance. Evaluation: it explains cultural variation; but Bobo dolls are designed to be hit, so the studies may lack validity for aggression towards people.

**De-individuation.** In a crowd or under anonymity, people lose personal identity and self-awareness, so social norms against aggression weaken. **Zimbardo (1969)** found that female students in hoods and lab coats gave longer shocks than named participants. **Diener et al. (1976)** found that anonymous children in groups were more likely to take extra Halloween sweets. But **Postmes and Spears (1998)** reviewed 60 studies and found weak overall support. Anonymity seems to increase following of local group norms, which may be peaceful as well as aggressive.

**Worked paragraph (de-individuation).** *De-individuation may explain violence by masked crowds. Anonymity reduces self-awareness, so people stop checking behaviour against their own standards. Zimbardo found hooded participants gave longer shocks. But Postmes and Spears found anonymity leads people to follow the group's norms, so a masked crowd will only become violent if aggression is the norm.*

## Institutional aggression in prisons

**Dispositional explanation: the importation model (Irwin and Cressey, 1962).** Prisoners bring their characteristics, values and norms into prison. Violent histories, gang ties and an "inmate code" learned outside predict violence inside.

**Situational explanation: the deprivation model.** Features of the prison cause aggression. **Sykes (1958)** described the **pains of imprisonment**: loss of liberty, goods and services, heterosexual relationships, autonomy and security. Aggression is a response to these frustrations. Overcrowding, heat, noise and poor staff management may add to it.

**Evaluation.** In a study by **Camp and Gaes**, prisoners with the same risk score were randomly assigned to lower- or higher-security prisons, and misconduct rates were similar. This supports importation over situation. But the deprivation model has practical value: if conditions cause violence, better management, more activity and less crowding should reduce it. An **interactionist** view (individual factors plus prison conditions) fits the evidence best.

**Worked paragraph.** *The importation model claims prisoners bring aggression in with them. Camp and Gaes randomly assigned similar-risk prisoners to lower- or higher-security prisons, and misconduct rates were similar. This suggests the prisoner matters more than the prison regime. However, Sykes' deprivations, such as loss of autonomy, may still add frustration, so an interactionist account is stronger.*

## Media influences, including computer games

Laboratory and correlational research suggests violent media may raise short-term aggression. **Anderson and Dill (2000)** found that students who played a violent game gave longer noise blasts to an opponent than those who played a non-violent game. **Ferguson (2015)** argued that effects are small and partly due to publication bias.

The specification names three explanations:

- **Desensitisation.** Repeated exposure reduces the physiological and emotional response to violence. **Carnagey et al. (2007)** found that participants who played a violent game for 20 minutes had lower heart rate and skin response to real violence on film.
- **Disinhibition.** Media make violence seem normal and acceptable, especially when unpunished or rewarded. Usual social restraints weaken.
- **Cognitive priming.** Violent content activates aggressive thoughts and scripts. These are then more easily triggered by a later cue. **Josephson (1987)** found that boys who watched a violent film with walkie-talkies in it were more aggressive in a hockey game when the referee carried a walkie-talkie.

**Evaluation.** Noise blasts are not real-world aggression. Correlational studies cannot rule out that aggressive people choose violent games.

**Worked paragraph (desensitisation).** *Desensitisation suggests violent games blunt reactions to real violence. Carnagey et al. found lower heart rate and skin response to real violence after 20 minutes of violent play. A blunted response may make aggression feel less unpleasant. But this was a short lab effect, and lower arousal is not the same as behaving aggressively.*

## Common errors

- Calling a fixed action pattern "learned": it is innate, triggered by an IRM.
- Saying MAOA "causes" violence. Caspi et al. show an interaction with maltreatment.
- Confusing importation (dispositional) with deprivation (situational).
- Writing about Bobo dolls without linking them to human aggression.
- Listing desensitisation, disinhibition and priming without explaining how each leads to behaviour.
- Treating correlational hormone data as proof of cause.
- Drifting into offending behaviour ([Forensic Psychology](/resources/aqa-a-level-psychology-7182-forensic-psychology/) content).
