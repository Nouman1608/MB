These questions cover sub-topic 2(c) Biological molecules, points 2.7–2.14B, of Pearson Edexcel International GCSE Biology (4BI1), Issue 3. Question 10 is on point 2.14B, which is examined on Paper 2 only.

## Section A

**1.** Molecule X contains carbon, hydrogen and oxygen only. Molecule Y contains carbon, hydrogen, oxygen and nitrogen. State which molecule could be a protein and give a reason. **[2]**

**2.** Name the smaller basic units that make up (i) glycogen, (ii) protein, (iii) lipid. **[3]**

**3.** State the condition needed for the Benedict's test and the colour seen if a large amount of glucose is present. **[2]**

**4.** Explain why an enzyme catalyses only one type of reaction. **[2]**

**5.** In two tubes, the end point of an enzyme reaction is reached after 40 s and after 64 s. Calculate the rate in each tube, using rate = 1 ÷ time, and state which tube has the faster reaction. **[3]**

**6.** Explain why enzymes are described as biological catalysts. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** A student tests four drinks for glucose. She mixes 2 cm³ of each drink with 2 cm³ of Benedict's solution and heats them in a water bath at 80 °C for 5 minutes.

| Drink | P | Q | R | S |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Colour | Orange | Blue | Green | Brick-red |

**(a)** Rank the drinks from highest to lowest glucose concentration. **[2]**
**(b)** Give **two** variables the student controlled to make the comparison fair. **[2]**
**(c)** Suggest how the method could be changed to give a numerical measure of glucose concentration. **[1]**

**8.** Lipase digests fat in milk into fatty acids and glycerol. The fatty acids lower the pH. Describe how a student could use milk, lipase, sodium carbonate solution and phenolphthalein indicator (pink in alkali, colourless below about pH 8) to investigate the effect of temperature on lipase activity. **[6]**

**9.** A student investigates the effect of temperature on a protease.

| Temperature / °C | 15 | 25 | 35 | 45 | 55 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Time to end point / s | 480 | 240 | 120 | 150 | 720 |

**(a)** Calculate the rate at 35 °C and at 55 °C, using rate = 1000 ÷ time. **[2]**
**(b)** Describe and explain the effect of temperature on the rate of reaction. **[4]**
**(c)** Suggest how the student could find the optimum temperature more precisely. **[1]**

**10.** (Paper 2 only) A student investigates the effect of pH on the activity of amylase at pH 4, 5, 6, 7, 8 and 9.

**(a)** State the independent variable and how it would be set. **[2]**
**(b)** Give **two** variables that should be controlled. **[2]**
**(c)** The rate is very low at pH 4. Explain why. **[2]**

**11.** Plants store carbohydrate as starch rather than glucose.

**(a)** Explain why starch is better than glucose as a storage molecule. **[2]**
**(b)** Describe how a protein is built from smaller molecules. **[2]**

**12.** A sports drink contains glucose and protein, but no starch or fat. Describe the tests a student could do to confirm this, giving the result expected for each of the four tests. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** Y [1], because proteins contain nitrogen, and carbohydrates and lipids do not [1]. **[2]**

**2.** (i) Glucose (simple sugars) [1]. (ii) Amino acids [1]. (iii) Fatty acids and glycerol [1]. **[3]**

**3.** Heat in a water bath (about 80 °C) [1]; brick-red [1]. **[2]**

**4.** The active site has a specific shape [1], and only a substrate with a complementary shape fits into it [1]. **[2]**

**5.** 1 ÷ 40 = **0.025 s⁻¹** [1]; 1 ÷ 64 = **0.016 s⁻¹** [1]; the 40 s tube is faster [1]. **[3]**

**6.** They speed up metabolic reactions [1] without being used up or changed themselves, and they are made by living organisms [1]. **[2]**

**7. (a)** S, P, R, Q [2] (one mark if one pair is reversed).
**(b)** Any two: volume of drink; volume of Benedict's solution; temperature of the water bath; heating time [1] [1].
**(c)** Use a colorimeter to measure the colour (or filter and weigh the precipitate) [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Blue is a negative result, so drink Q has no detectable glucose.

**8.** Mix milk with sodium carbonate solution and a few drops of phenolphthalein so it is pink [1]. Place the milk mixture and the lipase in separate tubes in a water bath at the first temperature until both reach it [1]. Add a fixed volume of lipase to the milk and start a stopwatch [1]. Record the time for the pink colour to disappear, as fatty acids lower the pH [1]. Repeat at a range of temperatures, for example 20, 30, 40, 50 and 60 °C, three times each, calculating rate = 1 ÷ mean time [1]. Keep the volumes and concentrations of milk, lipase and sodium carbonate the same [1]. **[6]**

**9. (a)** 35 °C: 1000 ÷ 120 = **8.3** [1]; 55 °C: 1000 ÷ 720 = **1.4** [1].
**(b)** The rate rises from 15 °C to a maximum at 35 °C (from 2.1 to 8.3) [1], because molecules gain kinetic energy and collide more often, forming more enzyme–substrate complexes [1]. Above 35 °C the rate falls to 1.4 at 55 °C [1], because the active site changes shape so the substrate no longer fits and the enzyme is denatured [1].
**(c)** Repeat with temperatures 1–2 °C apart between 25 °C and 45 °C [1]. **[7]**

**10. (a)** pH [1], set using buffer solutions [1].
**(b)** Any two: temperature (water bath); volume or concentration of amylase; volume or concentration of starch [1] [1].
**(c)** pH 4 is far from the optimum, so the active site changes shape [1]; starch fits less well, so fewer enzyme–substrate complexes form [1]. **[6]**

**11. (a)** Starch is insoluble [1], so it does not draw water into the cells by osmosis, and it is too large to leave the cells [1].
**(b)** Many amino acids [1] are joined together in a chain, in a reaction catalysed by enzymes [1]. **[4]**

**12.** Benedict's solution, heated: colour changes from blue to green, yellow, orange or brick-red, so glucose is present [1]. Biuret: purple, so protein is present [1]. Iodine: stays orange-brown, so no starch [1]. Ethanol then water: stays clear with no white emulsion, so no fat [1]. **[4]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Saying enzymes are killed instead of denatured.
- Describing a trend without quoting data.
- Treating a longer time as a faster rate.
- Forgetting to heat in the Benedict's test, or heating ethanol near a flame.
- Giving the reagent's own colour (blue for biuret) as a positive result.
- Leaving out the buffer when describing the pH practical.
