## 4.2.1 Principles of organisation

Large organisms are built in levels. Each level is made from the one below it.

1. **Cells** are the basic building blocks of all living organisms.
2. A **tissue** is a group of cells with a similar structure and function.
3. An **organ** is an aggregation of tissues performing specific functions.
4. Organs are organised into **organ systems**, which work together to form **organisms**.

Cardiac muscle cells form muscle tissue.
The heart is an organ: it contains muscle tissue, but also other tissues, such as the tissue that lines its
chambers. The heart, the blood vessels and the blood make up the circulatory system, which is an organ system.
The circulatory system works with the respiratory system (the lungs) and the digestive system to keep the whole
organism supplied.

Notice the key word in the organ definition: *tissues*, plural. A single tissue is not an organ. An exam answer
that calls "muscle tissue" an organ, or calls "the heart" a tissue, loses the mark.

### Size and scale (MS 1c)

The specification expects you to understand how big each level is. Cells are measured in micrometres (µm),
organs in centimetres, and whole organisms in metres. Remember the conversions:

- 1 mm = 1000 µm
- 1 cm = 10 mm = 10 000 µm
- 1 m = 1000 mm = 1 000 000 µm

**Worked example 1.** A heart cell is about 10 µm wide. Suppose a heart is 12 cm long. How many times longer is
the heart than the cell, and how many orders of magnitude is this?

```
Convert to the same unit:   12 cm = 12 x 10 000 um = 120 000 um
Ratio:                       120 000 / 10 = 12 000
12 000 is about 10^4, so the heart is about 4 orders of magnitude larger.
```

## 4.2.2.2 The heart

The heart is an organ that pumps blood around the body. Its walls are mostly muscle. It has four chambers:

- two **atria** at the top, which receive blood coming back to the heart;
- two **ventricles** at the bottom, which pump blood out of the heart.

The right side and the left side are separated by a wall of muscle, so oxygenated and deoxygenated blood do not
mix. Valves inside the heart stop blood flowing backwards, so blood moves one way only: atria, then ventricles,
then out into the arteries. You need to know what valves do, but not their names.

### The route of blood through the heart

```
Body -> vena cava -> right atrium -> right ventricle -> pulmonary artery -> LUNGS
Lungs -> pulmonary vein -> left atrium -> left ventricle -> aorta -> BODY
```

| Vessel | Carries blood | Oxygenated? |
|---|---|---|
| Vena cava | From the body into the right atrium | Deoxygenated |
| Pulmonary artery | From the right ventricle to the lungs | Deoxygenated |
| Pulmonary vein | From the lungs into the left atrium | Oxygenated |
| Aorta | From the left ventricle to the rest of the body | Oxygenated |
| Coronary arteries | To the heart muscle itself | Oxygenated |

Two vessels break the "arteries carry oxygenated blood" pattern. The pulmonary artery is an artery (it carries
blood away from the heart) but its blood is deoxygenated. The pulmonary vein is a vein (it carries blood towards
the heart) but its blood is oxygenated. Define arteries and veins by **direction**, not by oxygen.

The **coronary arteries** branch from the aorta and run over the surface of the heart. Heart muscle cells need
their own supply of oxygen and glucose for respiration. They cannot take it from the blood inside the chambers,
so the coronary arteries deliver it.

### The double circulatory system

The heart pumps blood in a **double circulatory system**. In one complete circuit of the body, blood passes
through the heart twice.

- The **right ventricle** pumps blood to the **lungs**, where gas exchange takes place.
- The **left ventricle** pumps blood around the **rest of the body**.

Blood returning from the lungs comes back to the heart and is pumped again, so it leaves for the body under
higher pressure. This is why the left ventricle has a thicker, more muscular wall than the right ventricle: it
pumps blood all the way around the body, while the right ventricle pumps blood only to the lungs, which are close by.

### Controlling the heart rate

The natural resting heart rate is controlled by a **group of cells located in the right atrium** that act as a
**pacemaker**. These cells set the rhythm at which the heart muscle contracts.

If the natural pacemaker does not work properly, the heart rate can become irregular. **Artificial pacemakers**
are electrical devices used to correct irregularities in the heart rate. A small device is placed under the skin,
with a wire to the heart, and it sends electrical impulses to keep the heart beating at a regular rate.

## 4.2.2.2 The lungs and gas exchange

You need four structures only:

| Structure | Role |
|---|---|
| **Trachea** | Carries air from the mouth and nose down into the chest |
| **Bronchi** | Two tubes that branch from the trachea, one into each lung |
| **Alveoli** | Tiny air sacs where gas exchange takes place |
| **Capillary network** | Surrounds the alveoli; blood picks up oxygen and gives up carbon dioxide |

In the alveoli, oxygen diffuses from the air into the blood. Carbon dioxide diffuses from the blood into the air
and is breathed out. Deoxygenated blood arrives from the pulmonary artery, and oxygenated blood leaves in the
pulmonary vein.

### How the lungs are adapted for gaseous exchange

| Feature | Why it helps diffusion |
|---|---|
| Millions of alveoli | A very large surface area for gases to cross |
| Alveolus walls are very thin | A short diffusion distance between air and blood |
| A dense capillary network | Blood constantly carries oxygen away and brings carbon dioxide, keeping steep concentration gradients |
| Breathing (ventilation) | Brings fresh air in and removes stale air, so the gradients stay steep |

When you explain an adaptation, always finish the sentence with the effect on diffusion: "large surface area,
**so more oxygen can diffuse into the blood at once**".

## 4.2.2.2 Arteries, veins and capillaries

The body contains three different types of blood vessel. The specification asks you to explain how the structure
of each relates to its function.

| | Artery | Vein | Capillary |
|---|---|---|---|
| Direction | Away from the heart | Towards the heart | Links arteries to veins, through tissues |
| Pressure | High | Low | Low |
| Wall | Thick, with layers of muscle and elastic fibres | Thinner, less muscle and elastic tissue | Only one cell thick |
| Lumen (space inside) | Narrow compared with wall thickness | Large | Very narrow, just wide enough for red blood cells |
| Valves | None along their length | Yes, to prevent backflow | None |

**Arteries.** Blood leaves the ventricles at high pressure. The thick muscular wall withstands that pressure.
Elastic fibres let the wall stretch as each surge of blood arrives and then recoil.

**Veins.** By the time blood reaches the veins the pressure is low. The large lumen lets blood flow back easily,
and **valves** stop it flowing backwards.

**Capillaries.** These are where substances are exchanged with body cells. Their walls are only one cell
thick, so the diffusion distance is short. Oxygen and glucose diffuse out to the cells, and carbon dioxide and other
wastes diffuse in. Huge numbers of capillaries mean every cell is close to one.

## Rate calculations for blood flow (MS 1a, 1c)

A rate is an amount divided by time. Write the formula, substitute, and give the unit.

```
rate = amount / time
```

**Worked example 2: heart rate from a pulse count.** A student counts 21 pulse beats in 15 seconds. Calculate the
heart rate in beats per minute.

```
Beats per second = 21 / 15
Beats per minute = 21 / 15 x 60 = 84 beats per minute
```

**Worked example 3: volume of blood pumped per minute.** A ventricle pumps 70 cm³ of blood per beat. The heart
rate is 72 beats per minute. Calculate the volume pumped per minute in dm³.

```
Volume per minute = volume per beat x beats per minute
                  = 70 x 72 = 5040 cm3 per minute
1 dm3 = 1000 cm3, so 5040 cm3 = 5.04 dm3 per minute
```

**Worked example 4: rate of blood flow through a vessel.** 300 cm³ of blood flows through a vessel in 25 seconds.
Calculate the rate of flow in cm³ per second and in cm³ per minute.

```
Rate = 300 / 25 = 12 cm3/s
Per minute: 12 x 60 = 720 cm3/min
```

**Worked example 5: speed of blood in a capillary.** Under a microscope, a red blood cell moves 0.6 mm along a
capillary in 2 seconds. Calculate the speed.

```
Speed = distance / time = 0.6 / 2 = 0.3 mm/s
```

Blood moves slowly through capillaries. This gives more time for substances to diffuse between the blood and
the cells.

## Common errors

- Calling the heart a tissue, or muscle tissue an organ. An organ contains several tissues.
- Saying the left ventricle pumps blood to the lungs. It is the **right** ventricle.
- Writing that all arteries carry oxygenated blood. The pulmonary artery carries deoxygenated blood.
- Saying the pacemaker cells are in the left atrium, or in a ventricle. They are in the **right atrium**.
- Explaining the thicker left ventricle wall without saying it pumps blood **around the whole body**.
- Saying capillaries have "thin walls" without saying **one cell thick** and **short diffusion distance**.
- Giving veins thick walls, or arteries valves along their length.
- Dividing time by amount in a rate calculation, or leaving out the unit.
