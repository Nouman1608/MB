## Section A: short answers

**1.** Explain one way in which the discovery of gold encouraged segregation. **[3]**

**2.** Distinguish between petty apartheid and grand apartheid, with one example of each. **[4]**

**3.** State the immediate cause of the 1976 Soweto uprising and one way it spread. **[2]**

## Section B: source and argument

*Constructed for practice, not a real source:* "The regime could survive the protesters, the guerrillas and the speeches. What it could not survive was the day that the banks stopped lending and the factories could not find the foreign currency to buy parts." (a historian's view, paraphrased)

**4.** Explain the argument made in this extract about the end of apartheid. **[3]**

**5.** Explain one way in which a historian could challenge this view. **[3]**

**6.** Examine the importance of Steve Biko and Black Consciousness for the development of resistance to apartheid. **[6]**

## Section C: essay questions

**7.** To what extent was the South African War caused by the desire to control the gold of the Transvaal? **[15]**

**8.** Evaluate the view that the Truth and Reconciliation Commission was a success. **[15]**

## Answers

**1.** One mark for a way (labour demand, pass laws, compounds, migrant labour, colour bar, or land legislation); one for explanation (mine owners wanted cheap, controlled African labour while protecting white skilled jobs); one for example (compound system on the Witwatersrand, job reservation under the Mines and Works Act, or the Natives Land Act of 1913 pushing Africans into wage labour). **[3]**

**2.** Petty apartheid: one mark for definition (everyday racial separation in public places and personal relations) and one for an example (Separate Amenities Act 1953; Prohibition of Mixed Marriages Act 1949). Grand apartheid: one mark for definition (large structural policies of separate political and territorial development) and one for an example (Group Areas Act 1950; Bantu Education Act 1953; homelands from 1959). **[4]**

**3.** One mark for the immediate cause: student protest against Afrikaans being used as a language of instruction. One mark for spread or response: protests spread beyond Soweto to other townships; police fired on children (Hector Pieterson became a symbol) and a national uprising followed. **[2]**

**4.** One mark for the claim that economic pressure ended apartheid; one for the contrast with protests and armed struggle which the regime could survive; one for the mechanism (loss of credit and foreign exchange and the crisis of confidence in 1985). **[3]**

**5.** One mark for a challenge, up to two for development. For example: the economic crisis was partly caused by township revolts and strikes, so internal resistance was the base of the pressure; sanctions were only partly effective and some countries traded on; business leaders met the ANC in the 1980s so economic pressure also worked through negotiation; the end of the Cold War and de Klerk's own decisions were also important. **[3]**

**6.** Up to 6 marks. Credit importance: Biko's argument for psychological liberation; the founding of SASO (1968) and the Black People's Convention; links to the 1976 student generation; his death in custody in 1977 which drew global attention. Credit limits: organisations were banned in 1977; the ANC's long organisation and Mandela's symbolism were more important in the end; the Black Consciousness emphasis on racial identity differed from the ANC's non-racial approach. Level guide: 1 to 2 for description; 3 to 4 for partial examination; 5 to 6 for a balanced examination with a judgement. **[6]**

**7.** Marking points, up to 15 in total:
- up to 3 for understanding the claim and the factors to compare;
- up to 4 for evidence in favour (gold of the Witwatersrand, British capital, Milner, Rhodes and the Jameson Raid, Uitlander grievances as a cover);
- up to 4 for other causes and perspectives (British imperial supremacy, Boer nationalism and Kruger's policy, fear of Transvaal power, debates between economic and political historians);
- up to 4 for evaluation and judgement, for example that gold made the Transvaal vital but the war was about who would be dominant in southern Africa. **[15]**

**8.** Marking points, up to 15 in total:
- up to 3 for understanding the criteria for success (truth, justice, reconciliation, avoiding violence, victim support);
- up to 4 for evidence of success (public hearings, the testimony of victims, the amnesty process for those who told the truth, Tutu's role, the peaceful transition);
- up to 4 for criticisms (limited prosecutions, perpetrators who did not apply, reparations that were small, wider society's beneficiaries not examined);
- up to 4 for a judgement that is consistent with the evaluation of perspectives from victims, perpetrators and historians. **[15]**

## Model paragraph and feedback

Here is a model paragraph for Question 8, followed by points on how to improve a draft.

*Model paragraph:* "The Truth and Reconciliation Commission, chaired by Archbishop Desmond Tutu from 1995, can be judged by what it promised and by what it delivered. It promised amnesty to those who made a full disclosure of politically motivated crimes, and it gave victims a public platform. By these measures it achieved a great deal: thousands of people testified and the country avoided the cycle of revenge that many had feared. However, critics point out that few perpetrators applied, that prosecutions were rare and that reparations were small. This essay argues that the Commission was a success in truth-telling and in supporting a peaceful transition, but only a partial success in justice."

*Feedback points:*

- The paragraph sets criteria first, then applies them. This is the key habit in "evaluate" questions.
- Give at least one named event or date per paragraph, such as the Act of 1995 or the final report in 1998.
- Show more than one perspective: victims' families, former officials and historians may disagree.
- Make the conclusion match the evaluation. If you called the Commission a partial success, say which criteria it met.
