| Specification content | Recall |

| Calculation and use of cash flows | Future, incremental cash only; add back depreciation; ignore sunk and unchanged costs |
| Payback and NPV | Terms: payback period, net present value, discount factor, cost of capital |
| Benefits and limitations | Payback is simple but short-sighted; NPV is thorough but depends on the cost of capital |
| Evaluation of projects | Financial and non-financial factors, then a clear decision |

## Key definitions

- **Capital investment appraisal**: using accounting techniques to decide whether a capital project
  is worthwhile, or which of several projects to choose.
- **Net cash flow**: cash received minus cash paid because of the project, for one year.
- **Payback period**: the time the project's net cash inflows take to repay the initial outlay.
- **Cost of capital**: the rate of return the business must pay to providers of finance; the minimum
  return a project must earn, used as the discount rate.
- **Discount factor**: the multiplier that turns a future cash flow into its present value.
- **Present value**: what a future cash flow is worth in today's money.
- **Net present value (NPV)**: the total of the present values of all a project's cash flows, with the
  initial outlay as a negative.
- **Residual value**: cash received from selling or scrapping the asset at the end of the project.
- **Sunk cost**: money already spent that the decision cannot change.

## Formulas

| What | Formula |
|---|---|
| Payback (equal inflows) | initial outlay ÷ annual net cash inflow |
| Months into the payback year | (amount still to recover ÷ that year's net cash inflow) × 12 |
| Discount factor, year n, rate r | 1 ÷ (1 + r)ⁿ |
| Present value | cash flow × discount factor |
| NPV | total present value of inflows − initial outlay |
| Cash flow from forecast profit | profit + depreciation (+ any unchanged apportioned costs charged) |
| Straight-line depreciation | (cost − residual value) ÷ useful life |

If discount factors are given in a question, use them as given. Otherwise calculate them; these notes
round factors to three decimal places.

## Relevant cash flows: in or out?

| Item | Include? | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Purchase and installation cost | Yes, year 0 | Cash paid because of the project |
| Extra sales receipts or cost savings | Yes | Incremental inflows |
| Extra running costs, wages, maintenance | Yes | Incremental outflows |
| Residual value | Yes, final year | Cash received on disposal |
| Depreciation | No | Non-cash; cost already in the outlay |
| Market research or feasibility study already paid | No | Sunk cost |
| Share of existing rent or head-office costs | No | Paid whether or not the project goes ahead |

## Method in steps

**Cash flows**

1. Start from cash, or from profit with depreciation added back.
2. Remove sunk costs and unchanged apportioned overheads.
3. Put the outlay in year 0 and the residual value in the final year.

**Payback**

1. Write the cash flows in a table with a cumulative column, starting at the negative outlay.
2. Find the year in which the cumulative figure turns positive.
3. Months = (amount outstanding at the start of that year ÷ that year's inflow) × 12.
4. State the answer in years and months, rounded as the question asks.

**NPV**

1. List each year's net cash flow, outlay in year 0.
2. Multiply each by its discount factor (year 0 factor = 1.000).
3. Add the present values; the outlay is negative.
4. State the result with its sign, then the decision: positive accept, negative reject.

## Worked reminders

**Equal inflows.** A van costs £60 000 and saves £16 000 cash a year.
Payback = 60 000 ÷ 16 000 = 3.75 years. 0.75 × 12 = 9 months, so **3 years 9 months**.

**Small NPV.** Outlay £50 000; net inflows £22 000 a year for 3 years; cost of capital 12%; factors
0.893, 0.797, 0.712.

| Year | Cash flow (£) | Factor | Present value (£) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | (50 000) | 1.000 | (50 000) |
| 1 | 22 000 | 0.893 | 19 646 |
| 2 | 22 000 | 0.797 | 17 534 |
| 3 | 22 000 | 0.712 | 15 664 |
| | | NPV | **2 844** |

Positive, so accept on financial grounds, though the margin is small. With equal inflows you can also
add the factors (0.893 + 0.797 + 0.712 = 2.402) and multiply: 22 000 × 2.402 = 52 844.

## Benefits and limitations

**Payback**

- Benefits: quick and easy to understand; favours early cash returns, helping liquidity; relies on the
  nearer, more reliable forecasts; suits industries with fast-changing technology.
- Limitations: ignores cash flows after payback; ignores the time value of money; does not measure
  overall value added; the cut-off period is a judgement.

**NPV**

- Benefits: allows for the time value of money; uses all the cash flows; gives a clear decision rule
  tied to the cost of capital; shows the value a project adds in £.
- Limitations: harder to calculate and explain; the cost of capital is hard to estimate and changes the
  answer; assumes year-end cash flows; does not show return per £ invested when outlays differ.

**Both**: depend entirely on forecasts, which are least reliable in later years.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Cash flow vs profit**: appraisal uses cash. Profit includes depreciation and accruals.
- **Payback vs NPV**: payback measures speed of return (liquidity and risk); NPV measures value added.
  They can rank two projects differently.
- **Timing**: payback assumes cash arrives evenly through the year (so months make sense); NPV assumes
  each year's cash arrives at the year end.
- **Higher cost of capital, lower NPV**: discount factors get smaller as the rate rises, so present
  values fall.
- **Financial vs non-financial factors**: figures first, then staff, customers, quality, suppliers,
  environment, legal and strategic fit.

## Quick self-test

1. A machine costs £45 000 and generates £12 000 net cash a year. Calculate payback in years and months.
2. Calculate the discount factor for year 2 at a cost of capital of 6%, to three decimal places.
3. A cash inflow of £25 000 arises at the end of year 3. The discount factor is 0.751. Calculate its
   present value.
4. Forecast profit is £14 000 after charging depreciation of £9 000. What is the net cash flow?
5. A business has spent £2 500 on a survey for a proposed project. Is this a relevant cash flow?
6. The present values of a project's inflows total £63 400 and the outlay is £65 000. State the NPV and
   the decision.
7. Outlay £70 000; inflows £30 000, £25 000, £20 000 in years 1 to 3. Calculate payback.
8. Explain why a project's NPV falls when the cost of capital rises.
9. State two non-financial factors a business might consider before buying new machinery.
10. Which method ignores cash flows that arise after the outlay has been recovered?
11. Project P has a shorter payback than project Q, but Q has the higher positive NPV. Which does each
    method favour, and give one reason a business might still choose P.

### Answers

1. 45 000 ÷ 12 000 = 3.75 years = **3 years 9 months**.
2. 1 ÷ 1.06² = **0.890**.
3. 25 000 × 0.751 = **£18 775**.
4. 14 000 + 9 000 = **£23 000**; depreciation is non-cash, so add it back.
5. **No.** It is a sunk cost; it has been paid whatever is decided.
6. 63 400 − 65 000 = **−£1 600**. Negative, so reject on financial grounds.
7. Cumulative: (40 000), (15 000), +5 000 in year 3. 2 years + (15 000 ÷ 20 000) × 12 = **2 years 9
   months**.
8. A higher rate gives smaller discount factors, so every future inflow has a lower present value
   while the year-0 outlay is unchanged.
9. Any two: effect on staff morale or jobs; training needed; product quality; supplier reliability and
   servicing; environmental impact; health and safety.
10. **Payback.**
11. Payback favours **P**; NPV favours **Q**, as it adds more value. A business might still choose P if
    it is short of cash, or if Q's later forecasts look unreliable, because P recovers the outlay sooner.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Treating depreciation as a cash outflow, or not adding it back to a profit forecast.
- Including sunk costs such as a survey already paid for.
- Including apportioned fixed overheads that do not change.
- Forgetting the residual value, or putting it in year 1 instead of the final year.
- Using the wrong year's inflow in the months calculation.
- Discounting the year-0 outlay, or forgetting to subtract it.
- Quoting an NPV without its sign, or with no accept or reject decision.
- Listing benefits and limitations without explaining why each matters.
- Ending an evaluation with no clear recommendation.
