These notes condense **4.2.3.2 Graphite** of the AQA GCSE Chemistry (8462) specification (for teaching from
September 2016, version 1.2). It is Paper 1 content for both tiers, with no Higher-only statements and no
required practical. For full explanations, see the [graphite study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-chemistry-8462-graphite/);
then practise with the [graphite practice questions](/resources/aqa-gcse-chemistry-8462-graphite-practice/).

## Core facts

- In graphite, **each carbon atom forms three covalent bonds** with three other carbon atoms, forming **layers of
  hexagonal rings**.
- There are **no covalent bonds between the layers**.
- **One electron from each carbon atom is delocalised.**
- You must be able to **explain the properties of graphite** in terms of its structure and bonding.
- **Graphite is similar to metals** in that it has **delocalised electrons**.

## Structure summary

| Feature | Graphite |
|---|---|
| Element | Carbon |
| Bonds per carbon atom | 3 covalent bonds |
| Shape of the layers | Flat sheets of hexagonal rings |
| Between the layers | No covalent bonds; weak forces |
| Electrons | 1 delocalised electron per carbon atom |
| Overall structure | Giant covalent |

## Property explanations

| Property | Explanation |
|---|---|
| Conducts electricity | Delocalised electrons are free to move and carry charge |
| Soft, slippery | Layers slide because there are no covalent bonds between them |
| Very high melting point | Many strong covalent bonds within the layers must be broken |

## Graphite versus diamond

| | Diamond | Graphite |
|---|---|---|
| Bonds per atom | 4 | 3 |
| Structure | 3D network | Layers |
| Delocalised electrons | None | One per atom |
| Hard or soft | Very hard | Soft |
| Conducts | No | Yes |

## Uses

| Use | Reason |
|---|---|
| Pencils | Soft; layers rub off |
| Lubricant | Layers slide |
| Electrodes | Conducts, high melting point |

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Graphite | A giant covalent form of carbon made of layers of hexagonal rings |
| Hexagonal ring | A ring of six carbon atoms |
| Layer | A flat sheet of bonded carbon atoms |
| Delocalised electron | An electron free to move through the structure |
| Lubricant | A substance that reduces friction between surfaces |
| Allotrope | A different structural form of the same element |

## Model answers

**Describe the bonding in graphite.** Each carbon atom forms three covalent bonds with three others, forming
layers of hexagonal rings. There are no covalent bonds between the layers. One electron per atom is delocalised.

**Why is graphite soft?** The layers can slide over one another because there are no covalent bonds between them.

**Why does graphite conduct?** One electron per carbon atom is delocalised and free to move, so it carries charge.

**Why does graphite have a high melting point?** The strong covalent bonds in the layers must be broken, and there
are very many of them.

## Atoms per hexagon

Each hexagon has six corner atoms, and each atom is shared between three hexagons. So each hexagon contains
6 ÷ 3 = 2 carbon atoms. In 50 hexagons there are 100 carbon atoms.

## Representing graphite

| Check in a diagram | Expected |
|---|---|
| Bonds on each carbon atom inside a layer | 3 |
| Shape of rings | Hexagons |
| Lines between layers | None, because there are no covalent bonds between layers |

A 2D diagram shows a layer clearly but not how the layers stack.

## Six-mark answer plan

1. Each carbon bonded to three others.
2. Layers of hexagonal rings.
3. No covalent bonds between layers, so layers slide: soft.
4. One delocalised electron per atom, so it conducts.
5. Strong covalent bonds within layers, so a high melting point.
6. Compare with diamond (four bonds, no free electrons).

## Graphite and metals

| | Graphite | Metal |
|---|---|---|
| Delocalised electrons | One per carbon atom | From outer shells of the atoms |
| Structure | Giant covalent layers | Positive ions in a sea of electrons |
| Conducts | Yes | Yes |
| Can be bent and shaped | No, but layers slide | Yes, layers of ions slide |

## Exam traps

- **"Weak covalent bonds between layers."** There are no covalent bonds between layers.
- **"Soft because the covalent bonds are weak."** The covalent bonds in each layer are strong.
- **Forgetting the delocalised electron.** It is why graphite conducts.
- **Mixing up bonds per atom.** Graphite three, diamond four.
- **"Graphite has molecules."** It is a giant covalent structure.
- **"Graphite is a metal."** It is a non-metal that conducts like a metal.

## Quick self-test

1. How many covalent bonds does each carbon atom form in graphite?
2. What shape are the rings in each layer?
3. Are there covalent bonds between the layers?
4. What happens to the fourth outer electron of each carbon atom?
5. Why does graphite conduct electricity?
6. Why is graphite soft?
7. Give one use of graphite that depends on it being slippery.
8. Why does graphite have a very high melting point?
9. In what way is graphite similar to a metal?
10. How many carbon atoms belong to each hexagon in a layer, if each atom is shared by three hexagons?

### Answers

1. Three.
2. Hexagonal (six carbon atoms).
3. No.
4. It is delocalised.
5. The delocalised electrons are free to move and carry charge.
6. The layers can slide over each other because there are no covalent bonds between them.
7. A lubricant, or pencils.
8. Many strong covalent bonds within the layers must be broken.
9. It has delocalised electrons that conduct electricity.
10. Two.
