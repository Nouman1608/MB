## What "living standards" means

Living standards describe how well people live: the goods and services they can consume, and also their health, education, working hours, safety and environment. No single number captures all of this, so economists use **indicators**: measurable figures that point towards the wider idea. An answer gains credit when it treats an indicator as evidence about living standards, not as living standards itself.

## 5.1.1 Real GDP per head: three adjustments

GDP is the total value of output produced in a country in a year. On its own it is a poor guide to how well people live, so it is adjusted in three steps.

| Step | Adjustment | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Start with nominal GDP (current prices) | This is the raw money value of output |
| 2 | Divide by a price index and multiply by 100 to get **real** GDP | Removes the effect of inflation, so a rise shows more output, not just higher prices |
| 3 | Divide real GDP by the population to get real GDP **per head** | Allows for the number of people sharing the output |

**Worked example: three growth figures from one set of data.** Country P (invented):

| | Year 1 | Year 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Nominal GDP | 600 billion | 726 billion |
| Price index (Year 1 = 100) | 100 | 110 |
| Population | 40 million | 42 million |

1. Nominal GDP growth = (726 − 600) ÷ 600 × 100 = **21%**.
2. Real GDP in Year 2 = 726 ÷ 110 × 100 = 660 billion. Real growth = (660 − 600) ÷ 600 × 100 = **10%**.
3. Real GDP per head: Year 1 = 600 billion ÷ 40 million = 15,000. Year 2 = 660 billion ÷ 42 million = 15,714 (to the nearest whole number). Growth = (15,714 − 15,000) ÷ 15,000 × 100 = **4.8%**.

A government might quote 21%. Only 4.8% reflects the change in output available per person. When a question gives you nominal figures, say which adjustment is missing before you draw a conclusion.

### Advantages of real GDP per head

- Data are collected regularly in most countries, so trends can be followed year by year.
- It adjusts for inflation and population, so it allows fair comparison over time and between countries of different sizes.
- Higher real income per head usually means people can buy more food, housing and services, so it is linked to material living standards.

### Disadvantages of real GDP per head

- **Distribution:** it is an average and says nothing about who receives the income.
- **Non-market output:** subsistence farming, unpaid care and housework are left out, so it understates output where these are common.
- **Hidden economy:** unrecorded, untaxed activity is missing.
- **Quality of life:** working hours, leisure, health, education and pollution are not included.
- **Comparison between countries:** figures must be converted into one currency, and exchange rates do not reflect what money buys in each country.

## 5.1.1 The Human Development Index

The HDI is published by the United Nations Development Programme. It gives each country a value between 0 and 1, and combines three components.

| Component | Measured by |
|---|---|
| A long and healthy life | Life expectancy at birth |
| Education | Mean years of schooling of adults, and expected years of schooling for children starting school |
| A decent standard of living | Gross national income per head, adjusted for differences in prices between countries |

Because income is one of three components, two countries with similar income can have very different HDI values if one has better health or schooling.

**Advantages:** it measures more than income; health and education are things people value for their own sake; it shows when a country's income is not reaching people as longer lives and more schooling; a single value makes ranking easy.

**Disadvantages:** it is still an average, so inequality is hidden; it leaves out freedom, security, the environment and the quality (not just quantity) of schooling and healthcare; deciding how to combine three different things is a judgement; and data on schooling can be out of date in some countries.

### Why the two indicators can disagree

Country Q (invented) earns most of its income from mineral exports controlled by a few firms. Country R (invented) has lower income but spends heavily on clinics and schools.

| | Country Q | Country R | Country S |
|---|---|---|---|
| Real GDP per head (common currency) | 32,000 | 12,000 | 4,000 |
| HDI (invented values) | 0.71 | 0.78 | 0.52 |
| Rank on GDP per head | 1 | 2 | 3 |
| Rank on HDI | 2 | 1 | 3 |

Q ranks first on income but second on the HDI. A likely explanation is that Q's high average income is concentrated among a few people and has not been turned into longer lives and more schooling for most. S ranks last on both: when income is very low, health and education usually suffer too. Use the data to say what each indicator shows, then explain the gap.

## 5.1.2 Differences within a country

An average for the whole country can describe almost nobody.

**Worked example: a national average that hides two regions.** In Country T (invented), 40% of people live in cities with average income of 24,000 and 60% live in rural areas with average income of 6,000.

National average = (0.4 × 24,000) + (0.6 × 6,000) = 9,600 + 3,600 = **13,200**.

City dwellers earn nearly twice the average; rural households earn less than half of it. Differences in living standards within a country come from:

- **Wealth ownership:** owners of land, property and shares receive rent, interest and profit as well as wages.
- **Skills and education:** jobs needing scarce skills pay more.
- **Region:** cities often have more firms, better-paid jobs and better services than rural areas.
- **Access to services:** two households with the same income live differently if only one has clean water, a clinic and a school nearby.
- **Discrimination:** some groups may be paid less or excluded from certain jobs.
- **Government policy:** taxes, benefits and public services can narrow or widen gaps.

## 5.1.2 Differences between countries

Living standards differ between countries because of differences in:

- **Productivity:** capital, technology and skills raise output per worker and so income.
- **Natural resources:** they can raise income, although earnings may reach few people.
- **Health and education:** both raise productivity and are part of living standards in themselves.
- **Population growth:** if population grows as fast as output, income per head does not rise.
- **Political stability and institutions:** conflict and corruption discourage investment.
- **Trade:** countries exporting a narrow range of low-value products earn less and face price swings.

## Annotated model answer

**Question (original):** Discuss whether the HDI is a better indicator of living standards than real GDP per head. **[8]**

> The HDI includes life expectancy and years of schooling as well as income per head. These matter because a person who lives longer and is better educated has more choices, even at the same income. *(Explains why the extra components matter.)*
>
> Real GDP per head measures only output per person. A country can have a high figure from mineral exports while most people have short lives and little schooling, so it can overstate how well most people live. *(Uses a context to show the weakness.)*
>
> However, the HDI is also an average, so like GDP per head it hides how unequally income, health and education are shared. Neither measure includes pollution, working hours or political freedom. *(Balances: a shared limitation.)*
>
> Real GDP per head is published more often and is useful for tracking short-term changes in income, while schooling data change slowly. *(A genuine advantage of the other side.)*
>
> Overall, the HDI is the better indicator of overall well-being because it covers health and education. Real GDP per head is more useful for measuring changes in material income over a year. The best judgement uses both alongside data on how income is distributed. *(Conclusion with a condition.)*

## Exam technique

- **Define:** give the precise meaning. "Real GDP per head is the total output of a country, adjusted for inflation, divided by its population."
- **Explain:** link a cause to an effect. For a disadvantage, say what is missed and why that misleads.
- **Analyse:** build a chain, such as higher income per head → more spending on food and healthcare → longer life expectancy → higher HDI.
- **Discuss:** give both sides and finish with a judgement that names what it depends on.
- **Calculate:** show each step and state the unit; round only at the end.

## Common mistakes

- Using nominal GDP to compare living standards over time.
- Comparing total GDP between countries with very different populations.
- Saying the HDI "measures happiness". It measures health, education and income.
- Listing disadvantages without explaining how they mislead.
- Concluding that a rise in real GDP per head means everyone is better off.
- Forgetting that the HDI is also an average and hides inequality.
