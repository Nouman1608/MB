This guide goes deeper on one section of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Biology (9610) specification, Version 5.1: **3.1.2 Cells and cell structure**, in Unit 1, The diversity of living organisms. Unit 1 is part of the International AS and counts towards the full International A-level.

The section has two parts. The first, eukaryotic cells, combines tools (microscopes and cell fractionation) with a list of organelles to know by appearance, ultrastructure and function. The second, prokaryotic cells, is a short list of differences you must be able to state precisely. You must be able to use magnification = size of image / size of object, and identify organelles from optical and electron micrographs.

## Magnification and resolution

**Magnification** is how many times larger the image is than the object. **Resolution** is the smallest distance between two points that can still be seen as separate. A microscope can have high magnification and poor resolution, which gives a larger but blurry image. Resolution, not magnification, is what limits the detail you can see.

### Calculating magnification

The formula is magnification = size of image / size of object. Rearranged, size of object = size of image / magnification.

The usual difficulty is units. Convert the image and object to the same unit before you divide. There are 1,000 micrometres (µm) in 1 millimetre (mm).

**Worked example 1.** A cell is shown in a photograph at a magnification of x1,500. The cell measures 45 mm across in the photograph. Calculate its real width in micrometres.

1. Object size = image size / magnification = 45 mm / 1,500.
2. 45 / 1,500 = 0.03 mm.
3. 0.03 mm x 1,000 = **30 µm**.

**Worked example 2.** A scale bar on an electron micrograph is 20 mm long and is labelled 2 µm. Calculate the magnification.

1. Convert to the same unit: 2 µm = 0.002 mm.
2. Magnification = 20 mm / 0.002 mm = **x10,000**.

### The three types of microscope

| | Optical | Transmission electron (TEM) | Scanning electron (SEM) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Principle | Light passes through the specimen | A beam of electrons passes through a very thin specimen | A beam of electrons scans the surface and reflected electrons are detected |
| Resolution | Low, about 0.2 µm | Very high, to the order of 0.1 nm | High, but lower than TEM |
| Image | Two-dimensional, in colour | Two-dimensional, in black and white | Three-dimensional appearance, in black and white |
| Living specimens | Yes | No | No |
| Limitations | Cannot resolve small organelles such as ribosomes | The specimen is in a vacuum, so it must be dead; must be very thin; preparation may create artefacts | Cannot show internal structure; lower resolution than TEM; specimen in a vacuum |

Resolution depends on wavelength. Electrons have a much shorter wavelength than visible light, so electron microscopes resolve much smaller structures. An **artefact** is something visible on the image that was not in the living cell, caused by preparing the specimen.

## Cell fractionation and ultracentrifugation

Cell fractionation separates organelles so that each can be studied. There are two stages.

1. **Homogenisation.** The tissue is broken up in a blender or homogeniser, in a solution that is **cold**, **isotonic** and **buffered**.
   - Cold: enzymes work slowly, so enzymes released from lysosomes do not digest the organelles.
   - Isotonic: the solution has the same water potential as the cells, so organelles do not burst or shrink by osmosis.
   - Buffered: the pH stays constant, so enzymes and proteins are not denatured.
2. **Ultracentrifugation.** The filtered homogenate is spun at increasing speeds. At each speed the densest organelles form a pellet at the bottom and the supernatant is poured off and spun faster.

The usual order of separation, from first to last, is nuclei, then chloroplasts or mitochondria, then lysosomes, then endoplasmic reticulum fragments and ribosomes. The principle is that the heaviest, densest structures settle at the lowest speed.

## The eukaryotic structures

| Structure | Appearance and ultrastructure | Function |
|---|---|---|
| Plasma membrane (including cell-surface membrane) | Phospholipid bilayer with proteins; a thin line on electron micrographs | Controls what enters and leaves; holds the cell contents; separates the cell from its surroundings |
| Cell wall | Outside the membrane in plants and many other cells; in plants made of cellulose fibres | Gives strength and support; stops the cell bursting in dilute solutions |
| Nucleus | Surrounded by a double membrane (nuclear envelope) with pores; contains chromatin and a nucleolus | Holds the DNA; pores let mRNA leave; controls the activity of the cell; nucleolus makes ribosomes |
| Mitochondria | Double membrane; the inner membrane is folded into cristae; the matrix inside | Site of aerobic respiration; makes ATP |
| Chloroplasts | Double membrane; stacks of thylakoids (grana) in the fluid stroma | Site of photosynthesis |
| Golgi apparatus | Stack of flattened, curved sacs with vesicles at the edges | Modifies and packages proteins and lipids; makes lysosomes and secretory vesicles |
| Lysosomes | Small spherical sacs with a single membrane | Contain hydrolytic enzymes that digest worn-out organelles and engulfed material |
| Ribosomes | Very small; not surrounded by a membrane | Site of protein synthesis (translation) |
| Rough endoplasmic reticulum | Folded membranes covered with ribosomes | Folds and transports proteins made by its ribosomes |
| Smooth endoplasmic reticulum | Folded membranes with no ribosomes | Makes and transports lipids |
| Cell vacuole | Single membrane (tonoplast) enclosing cell sap | Stores water, ions and sugars; helps keep the plant cell turgid |

To identify a structure on a micrograph, look for the membrane count first. A double membrane with folds inside gives a mitochondrion. A double membrane with stacked discs gives a chloroplast. Dots on folded membranes give rough endoplasmic reticulum. A sac with a single membrane and dense contents may be a lysosome.

### Connecting the structure to the job

Examination answers should link structure to function. A mitochondrion's cristae increase the surface area of the inner membrane, so more enzymes for ATP production can be held there. Rough endoplasmic reticulum is abundant in cells that secrete protein, such as enzyme-producing cells, because the ribosomes make the protein and the cisternae carry it. A cell that makes steroid hormones is likely to have a lot of smooth endoplasmic reticulum.

## From cells to systems

In a complex multicellular organism, eukaryotic cells have specific functions. Similar cells are organised into **tissues**, tissues into **organs**, and organs into **systems**. A leaf is an organ containing several tissues; a gas exchange system is made of several organs.

## Prokaryotic cells

Prokaryotic cells are smaller than eukaryotic cells and differ in having:

- cytoplasm with no membrane-bounded organelles;
- small ribosomes;
- no nucleus, with DNA that forms a circular molecule, not associated with proteins, free in the cytoplasm;
- a cell wall containing the glycoprotein murein.

Many also have one or more **plasmids**, a **capsule** around the cell and one or more **flagella**.

When you compare the two cell types, give each point as a pair: for example, "prokaryotic DNA is circular and free, whereas eukaryotic DNA is linear and inside a nucleus". A statement without the comparison is worth less.

## Worked example 3: an extended comparison

**Question:** Compare the structure of a prokaryotic cell and a plant cell. [4 marks]

**Answer:** Both have a cell wall, but the prokaryote's wall contains murein, whereas the plant cell wall is made of cellulose (1). The prokaryote's DNA is circular and free in the cytoplasm, whereas plant DNA is linear and inside a nucleus (1). The prokaryote has small ribosomes and no membrane-bounded organelles, whereas the plant cell has mitochondria, chloroplasts and other organelles (1). The prokaryote is smaller (1).

## Common mistakes

- Using "magnification" when the question is about seeing more detail. That is resolution.
- Forgetting to convert millimetres to micrometres before using the formula.
- Saying that electron microscopes can show living cells.
- Saying the nucleus is "the control centre" with no detail. State that it contains DNA, and name the pores or nucleolus.
- Calling the prokaryotic wall "cellulose".
- Describing the cell fractionation solution as "isotonic" without saying why.
- Saying that prokaryotes have "no DNA". They have DNA, but not in a nucleus.

## Exam technique

- **Show each step of a magnification calculation.** A correct unit conversion usually earns a mark of its own.
- **State the reason for each condition in fractionation.** Cold, isotonic and buffered each need a different explanation.
- **Use the evidence on a micrograph.** Name the feature you can see (cristae, grana, pores) and say what it tells you.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need to know the exact resolution of each microscope?**
The specification asks for principles and limitations, not exact values. Know the order: electron microscopes resolve far more detail than optical microscopes, and the TEM resolves more than the SEM.

**Why are some cells packed with mitochondria?**
Cells with a high energy demand, such as muscle cells, need a lot of ATP, so they have many mitochondria.
