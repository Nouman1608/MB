Section 3.2.2.5 of the AQA GCSE Economics (8136) specification has three content points: "Balance of trade and balance of payments", "Balance of payments surpluses and deficits on the current account" and "Government policies to influence the balance of payments". The additional guidance asks you to perform "simple calculations using current account balance of payments figures" and to understand "the meaning and significance" of a deficit or surplus and "the reasons" for one. This guide concentrates on the skills that cost most marks: deciding which way a flow goes, handling signs in calculations, and judging whether a deficit matters.

The section sits in 3.2 How the economy works, assessed on Paper 2, with no tiers. Figures are invented unless a real UK fact is stated. ## The rule that decides every flow

Ask one question: **does money come into the country or go out of it?**

- Money coming in is a **credit** (+). It happens when foreigners pay UK residents.
- Money going out is a **debit** (−). It happens when UK residents pay foreigners.

The direction of the money is often the opposite of the direction of the people or goods, which is where students go wrong.

| Transaction | Part of the current account | Credit or debit for the UK? |
|---|---|---|
| A German firm buys cars made in the UK | Trade in goods | Credit (export) |
| A UK family holidays in Spain | Trade in services | Debit (import): the family pays Spanish hotels |
| An American student pays fees to a UK university | Trade in services | Credit (export): the money comes to the UK |
| A UK insurer covers a ship owned by a Greek company | Trade in services | Credit (export) |
| A UK company receives profits from its factory in India | Primary income | Credit |
| A Japanese owner of a UK factory takes its profits home | Primary income | Debit |
| UK government aid to another country | Secondary income | Debit |
| A worker in the UK sends money to family abroad | Secondary income | Debit |

Tourism is the classic trap: a British tourist abroad is an **import** of services, and a foreign tourist in London is an **export**.

## Building the balances

- **Balance of trade in goods** = exports of goods − imports of goods.
- **Balance of trade in services** = exports of services − imports of services.
- **Balance of trade** = the two added together.
- **Current account balance** = balance of trade + net primary income + net secondary income.

### Worked example: Country M (invented, billion units)

| Item | Credits | Debits | Balance |
|---|---|---|---|
| Goods | 210 | 265 | −55 |
| Services | 140 | 96 | +44 |
| **Balance of trade** | | | **−11** |
| Primary income | 18 | 27 | −9 |
| Secondary income | 4 | 11 | −7 |
| **Current account** | | | **−27** |

Country M has a current account **deficit of 27 billion**. With GDP of 1,600 billion, that is 27 ÷ 1,600 × 100 = **1.7%** of GDP (1 d.p.). Expressing the balance as a share of GDP shows how large it is relative to the size of the economy.

### Worked example: finding a missing figure

An invented country's current account is −12. Its goods balance is −40, its services balance +31 and net primary income −5. What is net secondary income?

−40 + 31 − 5 = −14 so far. To reach −12, secondary income must be **+2**. Set up the sum, then solve for the gap.

## Why deficits and surpluses arise

Think in terms of **competitiveness** and **demand**.

- **Price competitiveness.** If UK inflation is higher than that of trading partners, or the pound is strong, UK goods become dearer compared with foreign goods. Exports fall and imports rise.
- **Non-price competitiveness.** Quality, design, reliability and brand reputation matter. A country can run a surplus with high prices if its goods are seen as the best.
- **Income at home.** When UK incomes rise quickly, households buy more of everything, including imports. A boom tends to widen a deficit.
- **Income abroad.** When trading partners are in recession, they buy fewer UK exports.
- **The structure of the economy.** A country with a small manufacturing sector imports many goods; one with a strong financial sector may earn a surplus on services.

## Does a deficit matter? Four tests

| Test | Less worrying | More worrying |
|---|---|---|
| Size | Small share of GDP | Large share of GDP |
| Duration | Short-lived, during a boom | Persistent for many years |
| Cause | Importing machinery that will raise future output | Importing consumer goods because domestic firms are uncompetitive |
| Financing | Foreign firms investing in new factories | Borrowing from abroad that adds to debt |

A deficit has to be paid for by money flowing in on the other parts of the balance of payments, through foreign investment, borrowing from abroad or selling assets to overseas buyers. A surplus is not automatically good either: it can mean households at home are consuming less than they could, and one country's surplus is another's deficit.

## Policies to influence the balance of payments

Policies to reduce a current account deficit work in one of two ways.

**Reduce total spending at home.** Higher income tax, lower government spending or a higher Bank Rate cut households' spending, and some of that spending would have gone on imports. The conflict: lower spending at home also cuts demand for UK goods, slowing growth and raising unemployment.

**Switch spending towards UK goods.** A lower exchange rate makes exports cheaper abroad and imports dearer at home. Tariffs and quotas make imports dearer or limit them. The conflicts: dearer imports raise inflation, and tariffs may bring retaliation and breach trade agreements.

**Improve competitiveness for the long term.** Supply-side policies such as training, investment incentives and lower taxes on business profits raise productivity and quality, so UK firms win more sales at home and abroad. This avoids the conflicts above, but it takes years.

## Annotated model answer

*Question (original):* Explain **one** reason why a rise in UK incomes may increase the current account deficit. **[3]**

*Model answer:* When incomes rise, households have more to spend on goods and services [the starting point]. Part of the extra spending goes on imports, such as foreign cars, electronics and holidays abroad [links income to imports]. Imports are debits, so if exports do not rise by as much, the deficit on the current account widens [completes the chain in balance of payments terms].

## Common mistakes

- Treating a holiday abroad as an export.
- Treating the current account deficit as the government's budget deficit.
- Dropping minus signs, or writing a number with no "deficit" or "surplus".
- Saying a deficit "means the country is losing money". It means more money flows out on the current account than in, and it is financed by flows elsewhere.
- Recommending tariffs with no mention of retaliation or trade agreements.

## Exam technique

- **Calculate** questions: lay out each balance on its own line with signs, then state deficit or surplus.
- **Explain** questions on reasons: start with competitiveness or income, then show whether exports or imports change.
- **Evaluate** questions on deficits: apply the four tests (size, duration, cause, financing) to the data in the case.
- **Evaluate** questions on policies: name the conflict with another objective, such as growth or inflation.
