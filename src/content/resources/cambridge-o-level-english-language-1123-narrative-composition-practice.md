> **These are original questions written for Marlbridge**, for practice on the narrative option in 1123
> Paper 2 Section B, where "a narrative piece of writing tells a story containing a sequence of
> connected events which may be real or imaginary". Question 10 is a full composition at the syllabus
> tariff of 25 marks; the other questions build its separate skills, with practice marks.

Related: [Narrative composition study guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-english-language-1123-narrative-composition/),
[revision notes](/resources/cambridge-o-level-english-language-1123-narrative-composition-revision-notes/)
and the [Paper 2 Writing study guide](/resources/o-level-english-language-paper-2-writing/).

## Section A

**1.** Rewrite as two or three sentences in which the events are connected, not just listed: "I went to
the station. I bought a ticket. I sat on the train. I got off." **[2]**

**2.** Which plan is better sized for 350–450 words, and why? Plan A: *a family moves country, the
narrator changes school three times, makes and loses friends, and finally settles over five years.*
Plan B: *on her first day at a new school, the narrator cannot find her classroom and is helped by a
boy who turns out to be in trouble himself.* **[2]**

**3.** Rewrite this opening to create interest from the first sentence: "I woke up at seven o'clock and
got ready for school. It was a normal day." **[2]**

**4.** Rewrite each "telling" sentence so that it shows the feeling: (a) "My father was proud of me."
(b) "The room was tense." **[2]**

**5.** Correct the punctuation and layout of this dialogue:

> where are you going asked Sam. nowhere I said, just out.

**[3]**

**6.** A story ends: "Then I woke up and realised it had all been a dream." The story is about a girl
who finds a lost wallet and has to decide whether to return it. Suggest a stronger ending and explain
why it is stronger. **[2]**

**7.** Slow down this key moment into about 50 words: "I opened the envelope and read my results."
**[3]**

## Section B

**8.** Plan the five stages (opening, complication, rising tension, turning point, resolution) of a
story for the title *Write a story about a time when someone kept a promise*. **[5]**

**9.** Write the opening paragraph (about 80 words) of your story for Question 8. **[4]**

**10.** Write a story of about 350–450 words for **one** of these titles:

(a) *Write a story in which a stranger asks for help.*

(b) *Write a story called "The Wrong Bus".* **[25]**

## Answers

**1.** For example [2]: "I reached the station with two minutes to spare, but the ticket machine
swallowed my last note. By the time the guard had found me a ticket, the train I needed was pulling
away." One mark for causal connection between events, one for a sense of consequence or tension.

**2.** Plan B [1]: it has one character, one day and one problem, so there is room to show feelings and
build tension; Plan A covers five years and many events, which would become a summary [1].

**3.** For example [2]: "The first thing I noticed that morning was that my sister's bed had not been
slept in." One mark for an engaging, specific opening; one for a hint of what is at stake.

**4.** One mark each [2], for example: (a) "My father read the letter twice, then folded it carefully
and put it in his shirt pocket, close to his heart." (b) "Nobody looked up when the door opened; even
the clock seemed to tick more quietly."

**5.** Corrected [3]:

> "Where are you going?" asked Sam.
>
> "Nowhere," I said. "Just out."

One mark for quotation marks around the spoken words; one for capital letters and the question mark
inside the speech; one for a new line for the new speaker.

**6.** For example [2]: the girl stands at the door of the wallet's owner, an elderly man, who opens
it with tears in his eyes because the wallet held his late wife's photograph; she walks home lighter
[1]. It is stronger because it comes from the story's own problem and shows the result of her
decision, instead of cancelling everything that happened [1].

**7.** Model [3]:

> The envelope was thinner than I had expected. I slid my finger under the flap, slowly, as if speed
> might change what was inside. The paper crackled. For a second the letters swam, and I could only see
> my own name, printed far too neatly. Then my eyes found the first grade.

Marking: the moment is extended with physical detail [1]; thoughts or feelings are shown [1]; sentence
lengths are varied to build tension [1].

**8.** One mark per stage [5], for example:
1. Opening: Bilal promises his younger brother he will be at his football final.
2. Complication: on the day, Bilal's bus breaks down halfway across the city.
3. Rising tension: no taxis; his phone battery is dying; the match is starting.
4. Turning point: he borrows a bicycle from a stranger who asks only that he return it.
5. Resolution: he arrives for the last ten minutes, sees his brother look up and wave; he cycles back
   to return the bicycle, keeping a second promise.

**9.** Model [4]:

> "You'll be there, won't you?" Saad asked for the third time that week, standing in my doorway in his
> new boots. I told him I would, the way I always did. It was only on Saturday morning, watching the
> bus wheeze to a stop halfway across the city with smoke curling from its engine, that I realised how
> many times I had said those words and how often I had not meant them.

Marking: engaging opening line using dialogue [1]; character and stakes established [1]; complication
introduced [1]; accurate punctuation and varied sentences [1].

**10.** Marked out of 25, all writing (W1, W2, W3, W5) [25]. High-level responses:

- tell a story with connected events and a clear shape, controlled pace and a satisfying ending that
  grows from the story (W2);
- create convincing characters whose thoughts and feelings are shown (W1);
- use precise vocabulary and a range of sentence structures for effect (W3);
- are accurate, including dialogue punctuation and consistent tense (W5).

For (a), strong stories make the stranger's request the central problem and the narrator's choice the
turning point. For (b), strong stories use the mistake as the complication that leads somewhere
meaningful, rather than a chain of unconnected adventures. Lower-level responses list events, end with
a dream or a sudden rescue, or lose control of tense and punctuation.

## Where marks are usually lost

- **Question 1**: adding adjectives instead of connections.
- **Question 5**: putting the question mark outside the quotation marks.
- **Question 10**: spending 300 words on the build-up and rushing the turning point in two lines.
- **Question 10**: a moral stated at the end ("I learned to always help people") instead of an ending
  that shows it.
