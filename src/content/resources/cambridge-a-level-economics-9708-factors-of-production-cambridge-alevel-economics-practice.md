All businesses and data are invented. Revise first with the [factors of production study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-economics-9708-factors-of-production-cambridge-alevel-economics/) and the [factors of production revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-economics-9708-factors-of-production-cambridge-alevel-economics-revision-notes/).

## Section A: Short questions

**1.** Which **one** of the following is an example of capital as a factor of production? **[1]**

A: Shares held by a pension fund

B: A forklift truck used in a warehouse

C: A lake used to supply water to a factory

D: Savings held in a bank account

**2.** Which **one** of the following correctly pairs a factor of production with its reward? **[1]**

A: Capital and profit

B: Enterprise and interest

C: Land and rent

D: Labour and interest

**3.** Which **one** of the following is an investment in human capital? **[1]**

A: A firm buys a new delivery van

B: A government builds a new motorway

C: A worker completes a course in accounting software

D: A farmer buys more land

**4.** Distinguish between human capital and physical capital, giving an example of each. **[4]**

**5.** Explain why profit is described as a residual reward. **[2]**

**6.** Explain why a salaried manager of a large company might not be regarded as an entrepreneur. **[2]**

## Section B: Structured questions

**7.** A small printing business has annual revenue of 180,000 cu. It pays wages of 85,000 cu, rent of 18,000 cu, interest of 7,000 cu on a loan for its printing press, and 40,000 cu for materials from other firms.

**(a)** Calculate the reward to enterprise. **[2]**

**(b)** Revenue falls by 20% while all costs stay the same. Calculate the new reward to enterprise. **[2]**

**(c)** Use your answers to explain why the entrepreneur is described as a risk-bearer. **[2]**

**8.** A bicycle workshop employs 5 workers who each build complete bicycles. Together they build 20 bicycles a week. The owner divides the work into frame assembly, wheel building, fitting and inspection, and output rises to 30 bicycles a week. The weekly wage bill is 2,400 cu and does not change.

**(a)** Calculate output per worker before and after the change. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the labour cost per bicycle before and after the change. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain **two** reasons why dividing the work raised output. **[4]**

**9.** Explain **two** disadvantages of the division of labour for workers. **[4]**

**10.** A hospital buys a new scanner but has too few staff trained to operate it. Explain why physical and human capital are described as complements, using this example. **[3]**

**11.** Explain the role of the entrepreneur in organising the factors of production. **[4]**

**12.** Assess whether specialisation always benefits an economy. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** B [1]. A forklift is a man-made good used in production. A and D are financial assets; C is land. **[1]**

**2.** C [1]. Capital earns interest, enterprise earns profit and labour earns wages. **[1]**

**3.** C [1]. It raises the worker's skills. A and B are physical capital; D is land. **[1]**

**4.** Human capital is the skills, knowledge, experience and health embodied in workers [1], for example the training of a qualified electrician [1]. Physical capital is man-made equipment and structures used in production [1], for example a factory's machinery [1]. **[4]**

**5.** Profit is what remains after all the other factors (land, labour and capital) have been paid their rent, wages and interest [1]. Because those payments are usually fixed in advance, profit is uncertain and can be negative [1]. **[2]**

**6.** The manager organises the factors of production but receives a contracted salary, which is a wage [1]. The risk of loss falls on the shareholders who own the company, so the manager does not bear the risk that defines the entrepreneur [1]. **[2]**

**7. (a)** Profit = 180,000 − 85,000 − 18,000 − 7,000 − 40,000 [1] = **30,000 cu** [1].

**(b)** New revenue = 0.8 × 180,000 = 144,000 cu [1]. Profit = 144,000 − 150,000 = **−6,000 cu**, a loss [1].

**(c)** The wages, rent and interest must be paid whatever happens to revenue [1], so the fall in revenue is borne entirely by the entrepreneur, whose reward swings from a profit of 30,000 cu to a loss of 6,000 cu [1]. **[6]**

**8. (a)** Before: 20 ÷ 5 = **4 bicycles per worker** [1]. After: 30 ÷ 5 = **6 bicycles per worker** [1].

**(b)** Before: 2,400 ÷ 20 = **120 cu** per bicycle [1]. After: 2,400 ÷ 30 = **80 cu** per bicycle [1].

**(c)** Reason 1: each worker repeats one task, becoming faster and more skilled at it [1], so each task takes less time [1]. Reason 2: no time is lost moving between tasks and changing tools [1], so more of each working day is spent producing [1]. Other valid reasons: workers can be matched to the tasks they are best at; specialised tools become worthwhile. **[8]**

**9.** Disadvantage 1: repeating one narrow task is monotonous [1], which can lower motivation and job satisfaction and increase absence [1]. Disadvantage 2: workers develop narrow skills [1], so if demand for that task falls, for example because it is automated, they find it harder to move to other jobs [1]. **[4]**

**10.** The scanner (physical capital) produces little unless trained staff (human capital) operate it [1], and trained radiographers are less productive without the equipment [1]. The two are used together, so the benefit of investing in one depends on having enough of the other, and the hospital gains little from the scanner until it trains or hires more staff [1]. **[3]**

**11.** The entrepreneur decides what to produce, identifying a want that customers will pay to satisfy [1]. They decide how to produce it, choosing the combination of land, labour and capital [1]. They hire or buy those factors, for example renting premises, employing staff and buying equipment [1], and co-ordinate them so that output is produced at the lowest cost for the quality required [1]. **[4]**

**12.** Award up to 8 marks across knowledge, analysis and evaluation.
- Definition: concentrating on a narrow range of tasks or products [1].
- Benefit: output per worker rises through repetition and the use of specialised equipment [1].
- Benefit: lower unit costs allow lower prices or higher output, easing scarcity [1].
- Benefit: regions or countries can concentrate on what they produce at relatively low cost and exchange for other goods [1].
- Cost: boredom and lower motivation can reduce quality [1].
- Cost: narrow skills and dependence on a few industries create unemployment if demand changes [1].
- Cost: dependence on exchange exposes the economy to disruption of supplies [1].
- Judgement: specialisation usually raises total output, but its benefits depend on flexible labour markets and reliable exchange, so it does not always benefit an economy, with a reasoned conclusion [1]. **[8]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calling money, shares or savings capital.
- Mixing up the rewards to capital and enterprise.
- Forgetting that profit can be negative.
- Giving productivity advantages without any disadvantages.
- Defining the entrepreneur without both risk and organisation.
- Ending an assess question without a judgement.
