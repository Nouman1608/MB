These notes condense section **3.1.8.2 Gibbs free-energy change, ΔG, and entropy change, ΔS** of the AQA A-level Chemistry specification (7405), for teaching from September 2015. It is A-level only content, examined on Paper 1 (and Paper 3). The [Gibbs free-energy and entropy study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-gibbs-free-energy-change-and-entropy-change/) explains each calculation with worked examples, and the [practice questions](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-gibbs-free-energy-change-and-entropy-change-practice/) give exam-style practice.

## What the specification asks

- ΔH alone is **not sufficient** to explain feasible change.
- **Entropy change, ΔS**: increasing disorder, in physical and chemical changes.
- **ΔG = ΔH − TΔS**; the reaction is feasible if **ΔG ≤ 0**.
- **Calculate ΔS** from absolute entropy values.
- Use the equation to find how ΔG varies with **temperature**, and the temperature at which a reaction becomes feasible.
- Find ΔS and ΔH from a graph of ΔG against T.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Entropy, S | A measure of disorder; units J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ |
| ΔS | Entropy change of the reaction |
| ΔG | Gibbs free-energy change, kJ mol⁻¹ |
| Feasible | Can happen (ΔG ≤ 0); says nothing about rate |
| Absolute entropy | Entropy value of a substance (always positive) |

## Predicting the sign of ΔS

| Change | ΔS |
|---|---|
| Solid to liquid to gas | Increases |
| More moles of gas in the products | Positive |
| Fewer moles of gas | Negative |
| Solid dissolving | Usually positive |
| Increase in temperature | S rises |

Gas ≫ liquid > solid. The entropy of a gas is much greater because the particles are free and far apart.

## Formulae

```
ΔS = ΣS(products) − ΣS(reactants)       J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹, multiply by coefficients
ΔG = ΔH − TΔS                            kJ mol⁻¹; T in K; ΔS ÷ 1000
T = ΔH ÷ ΔS                              the temperature where ΔG = 0
```

## The four cases

| ΔH | ΔS | Result |
|---|---|---|
| − | + | Always feasible |
| + | − | Never feasible |
| − | − | Feasible at low T only |
| + | + | Feasible at high T only |

## Worked reminders

**ΔS.** CaCO₃(s) → CaO(s) + CO₂(g): S = 92.9, 39.7, 213.6.
ΔS = 39.7 + 213.6 − 92.9 = **+160.4 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹**.

**ΔG.** N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃: ΔH −92 kJ mol⁻¹, ΔS −198.8 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹, T = 298 K.
ΔG = −92 − 298 × (−0.1988) = **−32.8 kJ mol⁻¹**. Feasible.

**Minimum temperature.** CaCO₃ decomposition: ΔH +178, ΔS +0.1604 kJ K⁻¹ mol⁻¹.
T = 178 ÷ 0.1604 = **1110 K**. Above this ΔG is negative.

**Maximum temperature.** Ammonia: T = −92 ÷ −0.1988 = **463 K**. Above this the reaction is not feasible.

**Melting ice.** ΔH +6.01 kJ, ΔS +22.0 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹: T = 6.01 ÷ 0.0220 = **273 K**.

## Graph of ΔG against T

ΔG = −ΔS × T + ΔH, a straight line y = mx + c:

| Feature | Value |
|---|---|
| Gradient | −ΔS |
| Intercept on ΔG axis (T = 0) | ΔH |
| Intercept on T axis | ΔH ÷ ΔS, the temperature at which the reaction becomes feasible or ceases to be |

Example: ΔG = +25, 0, −25, −50 kJ mol⁻¹ at 300, 400, 500, 600 K. Gradient = −75 ÷ 300 = −0.25 kJ K⁻¹ mol⁻¹, so ΔS = +250 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹. At 300 K: 25 = ΔH − 75, so ΔH = +100 kJ mol⁻¹.

## Why endothermic reactions can happen

If ΔS is positive and TΔS is greater than ΔH, then ΔG is negative. For example, ammonium nitrate dissolves in water although the process is endothermic: the ions are free to move and the disorder increases.

## Feasible versus fast

- ΔG ≤ 0 means a reaction is **possible**.
- It does not tell you whether it will happen at a measurable rate.
- Many feasible reactions are very slow because of a high activation energy (diamond turning to graphite).

## Common mistakes

- Using J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ with kJ mol⁻¹ without converting.
- Using °C for T.
- Not multiplying S by coefficients.
- Doing reactants minus products for ΔS.
- Thinking ΔG gives the rate.
- Saying a reaction is feasible if ΔG > 0.
- Forgetting the sign of the gradient is −ΔS.
- Forgetting units in the final answer.

## Self-test

1. Define entropy.
2. State the units of entropy.
3. State the sign of ΔS for 2NO₂(g) → N₂O₄(g), with a reason.
4. Calculate ΔS for 2H₂O₂(l) → 2H₂O(l) + O₂(g), given S: H₂O₂(l) 109.6, H₂O(l) 69.9, O₂(g) 205.0 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹.
5. State the condition for a reaction to be feasible.
6. ΔH = +50 kJ mol⁻¹ and ΔS = +120 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹. Calculate ΔG at 300 K.
7. Calculate the minimum temperature at which the reaction in question 6 is feasible.
8. ΔH = −200 kJ mol⁻¹ and ΔS = −100 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹. At what temperature does the reaction stop being feasible?
9. In which of the four sign combinations is a reaction feasible at all temperatures?
10. What do the gradient and the intercept of a ΔG against T graph give?
11. Why is it wrong to say that a feasible reaction will definitely occur?
12. Explain why the dissolving of ammonium nitrate is spontaneous although endothermic.

### Answers

1. A measure of the disorder of a system (the number of ways of arranging the particles and energy).
2. J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹.
3. Negative: two moles of gas become one mole, so the disorder decreases.
4. ΔS = 2(69.9) + 205.0 − 2(109.6) = 344.8 − 219.2 = **+125.6 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹**.
5. ΔG ≤ 0 (zero or negative).
6. ΔG = 50 − 300 × 0.120 = **+14 kJ mol⁻¹**, not feasible.
7. T = 50 ÷ 0.120 = **417 K**.
8. T = −200 ÷ −0.100 = **2000 K**; above this the reaction is not feasible.
9. ΔH negative and ΔS positive.
10. Gradient = −ΔS; intercept on the ΔG axis = ΔH.
11. Feasibility says nothing about the rate; the activation energy may be so high that the reaction is very slow.
12. ΔS is positive (ions free to move in solution) and TΔS is greater than ΔH, so ΔG is negative.
