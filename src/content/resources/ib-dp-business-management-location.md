AO2 is application and analysis. ## Reasons for a specific location of production

The guide asks for "the reasons" but does not list them, so you need a working set. The groups below are a standard set, not the guide's own list. Group them so you can recall them under pressure.

**Cost factors**
- Land, rent and building costs.
- Labor costs and the supply of workers with the right skills.
- Cost of raw materials and components, and the cost of moving them.
- Cost of delivering finished goods to customers.
- Tax rates, grants and other government incentives.

**Market and supply factors**
- Closeness to customers, which shortens delivery times and lets the business respond to changes in demand.
- Closeness to suppliers, which cuts transport costs and delays.
- Closeness to other firms in the same industry, where a supply of skilled workers and specialist services may already exist.

**Operating environment**
- Quality of roads, ports, power, water and communications.
- Political stability and the legal framework.
- Trade barriers such as tariffs and quotas, and exchange rate movements.
- Environmental rules and the business's own sustainability aims.

**Other reasons**
- The brand image linked to a place of origin.
- Room to expand later.
- The personal preference or history of the owners.

### Worked example: choosing between three sites

Glasswick Cookware will make 120,000 pans a year at one new site. It has costs for three possible sites.

| Site | Fixed costs per year | Variable cost per pan | Delivery to market per pan |
|---|---|---|---|
| A (beside the main market) | 1,440,000 | 14.50 | 1.20 |
| B (mid-distance) | 960,000 | 13.00 | 3.10 |
| C (distant, low wages) | 1,080,000 | 9.80 | 4.40 |

All figures are in dollars.

Step 1: total cost = fixed costs + output x (variable cost + delivery cost).
- A: 1,440,000 + 120,000 x 15.70 = 3,324,000.
- B: 960,000 + 120,000 x 16.10 = 2,892,000.
- C: 1,080,000 + 120,000 x 14.20 = 2,784,000.

Step 2: cost per pan = total cost / 120,000. A is 27.70, B is 24.10 and C is 23.20.

Step 3: judge. Site C is cheapest by 108,000 a year over B, a gap of about 3.7% of B's cost. That margin is small. A long delivery distance means longer lead times, more stock in transit and less ability to react to a sudden change in demand. Site A costs 540,000 a year more than C but gives fast delivery. The numbers support C, but the decision depends on how much the business values speed and flexibility.

Notice the pattern. Quantitative data lets you rank the sites. Qualitative factors then test whether the ranking is safe. A strong answer uses both.

## Ways of reorganizing production

Two questions separate the four terms. Who does the work, the business itself or an outside firm? Where is it done, in the home country or abroad?

| | Done by an outside firm | Done by the business itself |
|---|---|---|
| Home country | Outsourcing/subcontracting (national) | Insourcing |
| Another country | Outsourcing/subcontracting (international), often called offshore outsourcing | Offshoring to the business's own overseas operation |

Reshoring moves work from another country back to the home country. The work may then be done by the business itself or by a home-based contractor. The guide says all four ways are covered "both nationally and internationally", so be ready to describe each in a national and an international form.

### Outsourcing/subcontracting

The business pays another firm to carry out a part of production, or all of it, instead of doing it internally. Subcontracting usually describes handing a specific job or component to another firm.

Possible advantages: the business pays only for what it uses, avoids buying equipment, gains access to specialist skill and can focus on what it does best.

Possible disadvantages: less control over quality and delivery dates, dependence on the contractor, risk that confidential designs leak, and a contract price that may rise.

Worked example. Nyrith Audio makes speakers. Its own assembly line has fixed costs of 480,000 a year and variable costs of 7.10 per unit. A contractor will assemble for 9.40 per unit with no fixed cost.
- At 150,000 units: in-house costs 480,000 + 150,000 x 7.10 = 1,545,000. Outsourcing costs 150,000 x 9.40 = 1,410,000. Outsourcing is 135,000 cheaper.
- At 250,000 units: in-house costs 2,255,000. Outsourcing costs 2,350,000. In-house is 95,000 cheaper.
- The two options cost the same where 480,000 = (9.40 - 7.10) x output. That gives 208,696 units (to the nearest unit).

Below about 208,700 units a year, outsourcing is cheaper. Above it, in-house production is cheaper. If Nyrith expects demand to grow past that figure, outsourcing is a short-run saving and a long-run cost. The decision should also weigh quality control and how well the contractor can cope with surges in orders.

### Offshoring

Offshoring means moving production, or part of it, to another country. The business may open its own plant there or use a local contractor. The term describes where the work happens. The term outsourcing describes who does it. A business can offshore without outsourcing, and outsource without offshoring.

Typical drivers: lower wages, cheaper land, access to new markets, nearby raw materials and favorable government incentives. Typical problems: transport costs and delays, import duties, exchange rate risk, differences in culture and language, harder quality checks, and public criticism if working conditions are poor.

Worked example. Zerrin Footwear makes 80,000 pairs a year at home at a cost of 34.00 per pair. An overseas factory would produce at 21.00 per pair. Shipping adds 4.50 per pair and import duty is 8% of the production cost.
- Duty = 0.08 x 21.00 = 1.68.
- Landed cost = 21.00 + 4.50 + 1.68 = 27.18 per pair.
- Saving = 34.00 - 27.18 = 6.82 per pair, so 80,000 x 6.82 = 545,600 a year.
- Moving costs 360,000 once. The saving covers this in 360,000 / 545,600 = 0.66 years, about 8 months.

Now test the result. If shipping rose to 7.20, landed cost would be 21.00 + 7.20 + 1.68 = 29.88. The saving falls to 4.12 per pair, or 329,600 a year, and recovery of the moving cost takes about 1.1 years. The offshoring case is still positive, but it is sensitive to shipping, duty and exchange rates. Say so in your answer.

### Insourcing

Insourcing means bringing in-house an activity that was done by an outside firm. A business might do this to regain control of quality, protect designs, build its own skills or save money once volumes are high.

Worked example. Pennaby Foods pays a packing firm 2.60 per unit for 300,000 units a year, which is 780,000. Packing in-house would cost 270,000 fixed plus 1.35 per unit, which is 270,000 + 300,000 x 1.35 = 675,000. Insourcing saves 105,000 a year. The cost is equal at 270,000 / (2.60 - 1.35) = 216,000 units. If sales fell below 216,000, Pennaby would have been better off outsourcing. Insourcing converts a variable cost into a fixed one, which raises risk when demand is uncertain.

Other points: the business needs space, machinery and trained staff, so it must recruit and train. Staff at the former contractor may lose their jobs, which affects that firm as a stakeholder.

### Reshoring

Reshoring means moving production that was offshored back to the home country. Reasons include rising overseas wages, long lead times, quality problems, shipping costs or disruption, a wish to use a "made at home" selling point, or pressure from customers and governments.

Worked example. Rudhall Bikes makes 25,000 frames a year overseas at a landed cost of 118 per frame. Home production would cost 131. Reshoring adds 13 x 25,000 = 325,000 a year to production cost, plus a one-off 450,000 for the new line. On cost alone, reshoring looks poor. Rudhall's case must rest on other benefits: faster delivery, less stock held, fewer defects, a stronger brand and less exposure to shipping disruption. If those are worth more than 325,000 a year, reshoring is justified.

## Evaluating a location change

For AO3 questions, build the judgement in four steps.
1. State the change and its main purpose.
2. Give benefits and costs with figures where you have them.
3. Consider stakeholders: owners, employees at both sites, customers, suppliers, communities and governments.
4. Reach a conclusion that names what the decision depends on, such as volume, time horizon or the business's aims.

Remember the Unit 5 conceptual understandings in the guide: operational changes can affect other business activities, and they may affect a business's ethical stance. Offshoring to cut labor costs can raise questions about working conditions. Reshoring can protect home jobs but raise prices. Sustainability also matters: long shipping routes add emissions, though a more efficient overseas plant may offset some of this.

## Common errors

- Treating outsourcing and offshoring as the same thing.
- Calling any move abroad "offshoring" even when the business has simply bought a product from a foreign supplier.
- Listing location factors without linking them to the business in the case study.
- Comparing production cost only and leaving out shipping, duty and one-off costs.
- Stating a conclusion without naming the condition it depends on.
- Forgetting the national forms: subcontracting to a firm in the same country is outsourcing too.
