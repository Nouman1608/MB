This guide covers **remedies** in section 3.4 Law of contract of AQA A-level Law (7162). The
specification names "Compensatory damages (including categories of recoverable loss, causation,
remoteness and mitigation)", "Equitable remedies of specific performance and rescission" and
"Termination of contract for breach". The theory section also asks about "the nature and effectiveness
of remedies including specifically consumer remedies". For the contract option in outline, start with
the [Law of contract study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-law-law-of-contract-study-guide/).

## Compensatory damages

Damages for breach of contract are **compensatory**, not punitive. They are available **as of right**
once breach and loss are shown. The aim is to put the claimant, so far as money can, in the position they
would have been in **if the contract had been performed** (Robinson v Harman, 1848).

### Categories of recoverable loss

| Category | Meaning | Authority |
|---|---|---|
| Expectation loss | The value of the performance promised, such as the difference between contract price and market price, or lost profit | Robinson v Harman (1848) |
| Reliance loss | Wasted expenditure incurred in reliance on the contract, claimed where profit is too speculative | **Anglia Television v Reed (1972)**: an actor's withdrawal; the company recovered its wasted pre-contract and post-contract preparation costs |
| Loss of a chance | Loss of a real chance of a benefit | **Chaplin v Hicks (1911)**: a contestant wrongly denied the chance to attend the final interview of a beauty contest recovered for the lost chance |
| Cost of cure or loss of amenity | Where cost of cure is out of all proportion to the benefit, damages may reflect lost amenity instead | **Ruxley Electronics v Forsyth (1996)**: a swimming pool built shallower than agreed but still safe; rebuilding was unreasonable, and £2,500 for loss of amenity was awarded |
| Non-pecuniary loss | Generally not recoverable for distress (**Addis v Gramophone, 1909**), but recoverable where a major object of the contract is pleasure, relaxation or peace of mind | **Jarvis v Swans Tours (1973)**: disappointing holiday; **Farley v Skinner (2001)**: surveyor failed to report aircraft noise as specifically asked |

**Nominal damages** are awarded where there is a breach but no provable loss.

### Causation

The breach must be an **effective cause** of the loss. A claimant's own unreasonable act, or that of a
third party, may break the chain. In **Quinn v Burch Brothers (Builders) (1966)** the defendants failed
to supply a stepladder, and the claimant used an unfooted trestle instead and fell. The breach gave the
occasion for the injury but did not cause it, so the claim for the injury failed.

### Remoteness

Even if caused by the breach, a loss is recoverable only if it is not too remote. **Hadley v Baxendale
(1854)** set two limbs:

1. losses arising **naturally**, in the usual course of things, from the breach; and
2. losses that both parties **reasonably contemplated** when the contract was made, as the probable
   result of breach, because of special knowledge.

In Hadley, a carrier delayed returning a broken crankshaft; the mill's lost profits were not recoverable
because the carrier did not know the mill had no spare.

- **Victoria Laundry v Newman Industries (1949)**: delivery of a boiler was late. Ordinary lost profits
  were recoverable, but the especially lucrative dyeing contracts, which the seller did not know about,
  were too remote.
- **The Heron II (Koufos v C Czarnikow, 1969)**: a loss is within the first limb if it is "not unlikely"
  to result; a fall in the sugar market during a delayed voyage was recoverable.
- **Parsons v Uttley Ingham (1978)**: if the **type** of loss was within contemplation, the full extent
  is recoverable, even if more serious than expected. Pig nuts went mouldy in a badly ventilated hopper
  and the pigs died; illness was within contemplation.
- **Transfield Shipping v Mercator Shipping (The Achilleas, 2008)**: the House of Lords also asked
  whether the defendant had **assumed responsibility** for the type of loss; a charterer returning a ship
  late was liable only for the market rate for the overrun, not the loss of a lucrative follow-on
  charter.

### Mitigation

The claimant must take **reasonable steps** to reduce the loss and cannot recover loss that could
reasonably have been avoided (**British Westinghouse v Underground Electric Railways, 1912**).

- **Payzu v Saunders (1919)**: a buyer refused the seller's offer to continue supplying for cash, and
  could not recover losses that accepting the offer would have avoided.
- **Brace v Calder (1895)**: a dismissed employee who refused re-employment on the same terms recovered
  only nominal damages.

The duty is to act reasonably, not perfectly. Reasonable costs of mitigation can be recovered.

## Equitable remedies

Equitable remedies are **discretionary**: the court may refuse them even where a breach is proved.

### Specific performance

Specific performance orders the defendant to carry out the contract. It is granted only where **damages
are inadequate**.

- **Land** is treated as unique, so specific performance is commonly ordered for contracts to buy land.
- **Unique goods**: in **Falcke v Gray (1859)** the court accepted that specific performance can be
  ordered for rare items such as unusual china vases, though it was refused on other grounds.
- **Beswick v Beswick (1968)**: specific performance was ordered so that a widow, as administratrix,
  could enforce a promise to pay her an annuity, because damages to the estate would be nominal.

It will normally be refused for **personal service** contracts, contracts that would need **constant
supervision** (**Co-operative Insurance v Argyll Stores, 1997**: an order to keep a supermarket open
was refused), where it would cause **severe hardship** (**Patel v Ali, 1984**), and where the claimant
has acted unfairly or delayed.

### Rescission

Rescission sets the contract aside and restores the parties to their pre-contract positions. It is the
main remedy for **misrepresentation** and **economic duress**, which make a contract voidable. It is
lost by affirmation, lapse of time, intervening third-party rights or where restitution is impossible.
Rescission undoes the contract from the start; termination for breach, below, ends it for the future.

## Termination of contract for breach

Termination is a **self-help remedy**: the innocent party can treat itself as discharged from future
performance without going to court. It is available for:

- breach of a **condition** (Poussard v Spiers, 1876);
- a serious breach of an **innominate term**, judged by whether it deprives the innocent party of
  substantially the whole benefit of the contract (**Hong Kong Fir Shipping v Kawasaki, 1962**; in
  **Cehave v Bremer (The Hansa Nord, 1976)** a minor breach of an innominate term did not justify
  termination); and
- **renunciation**, including anticipatory breach.

The innocent party must **elect**. Terminating ends both parties' future obligations and allows damages
for loss of the bargain; affirming keeps the contract alive. Termination for a breach that is not
repudiatory is itself a repudiation.

For **consumers**, the Consumer Rights Act 2015 gives a statutory version: the short-term and final
rights to reject goods end the contract with a refund.

## Worked example

*Facts: Leah, a caterer, agrees to buy a commercial oven from Ovenco for £10,000, telling Ovenco she
needs it for a wedding contract starting on 1 May worth £6,000 profit. On 5 April Ovenco tells her
delivery will be late; it arrives on 20 May. A similar oven was available elsewhere for £11,000 from
10 April, but Leah did not buy one.
She also lost an unusually profitable banquet worth £9,000 that she never mentioned.*

```
Breach:        Late delivery, announced in advance. If time was a condition,
               Leah could terminate; she accepted the oven, so she claims
               damages.
Loss:          Expectation loss: the profit she would have made.
Remoteness:    Wedding profit – within the second limb of Hadley, as Ovenco
               was told (Victoria Laundry). Banquet profit – not mentioned
               and unusually lucrative, so too remote.
Mitigation:    She could have bought an oven for £11,000 on 10 April, an
               extra £1,000, and kept the wedding contract. A court may say
               she should have done so (Payzu v Saunders), limiting damages
               to the £1,000 extra cost rather than £6,000 lost profit.
Equitable:     Specific performance is irrelevant after delivery, and an
               oven is not unique.
```

## Common mistakes

- Describing damages as a punishment for breach.
- Applying the remoteness test at the time of breach instead of the time of contracting.
- Forgetting that the claimant cannot recover loss that reasonable mitigation would have avoided.
- Claiming specific performance as of right; it is discretionary and needs damages to be inadequate.
- Confusing rescission (setting aside from the start) with termination (ending future obligations).

## Exam technique

- **Explain** questions: state each rule with its leading case, as in the tables above.
- **Suggest** and **Advise** questions: identify the breach, the loss, then work through causation,
  remoteness and mitigation, calculating any figures, before considering equitable remedies.
- **Consider** questions: weigh borderline points, such as whether a loss was within the parties'
  contemplation.
- **Discuss** questions: evaluate whether remedies achieve full compensation, whether limits such as
  Addis are justified, and how effective consumer remedies are.
