Historical Investigation A2, "Russia and the Soviet Union, 1905–24", is one of five investigation
options in Section A of Pearson Edexcel International GCSE History (4HI1) Paper 2. It follows Russia
from the 1905 Revolution, through the collapse of tsarism in February 1917 and the Bolshevik seizure
of power in October, to Bolshevik victory in the Civil War, War Communism, the New Economic Policy
and Lenin's death in 1924. The exam tests source skills: describing features, cross-referencing
sources and evaluating an interpretation.

## Syllabus coverage

The 4HI1 specification (Issue 5, February 2026) sets five key topics for A2:

1. **Tsarist rule in Russia, 1905–14** — tsarist rule and discontent in 1905, the Potemkin mutiny and
   the soviets, the 1905 Revolution, the October Manifesto and why Nicholas survived, Nicholas and the
   first four Dumas, opposition groups, Stolypin's repression and land reform, the Lena Goldfields
   strike.
2. **Opposition to tsarist rule, 1914–17** — the war's economic, social and political effects,
   Rasputin, the causes of the February Revolution in Petrograd, the army mutiny, the abdication and
   the Provisional Government.
3. **The Provisional Government and the Bolshevik Revolution** — its weaknesses, the Petrograd Soviet,
   Lenin and the Bolsheviks, the April Theses, the July Days, the Kornilov Revolt, the takeover, and the
   roles of Lenin and Trotsky.
4. **Bolshevik consolidation and the Civil War** — the 1917 decrees, the closure of the Constituent
   Assembly, Brest-Litovsk, the two sides and the reasons for Bolshevik victory.
5. **War Communism and the NEP** — causes, nature and effects of War Communism, the Kronstadt Mutiny, the
   NEP and opposition to it, and Lenin's achievements to 1924.

## Why was there a revolution in 1905, and why did Nicholas survive?

Nicholas II ruled as an **autocrat**, backed by the army, the Orthodox Church and the secret police.
Peasants wanted land, workers in fast-growing cities faced long hours and slums, national minorities
resented Russification, and liberals wanted a parliament. Defeat in the **Russo-Japanese War**
(1904–05) exposed government incompetence. On **Bloody Sunday** (January 1905), troops fired on a
peaceful march led by Father Gapon. Strikes, peasant risings and the **Potemkin mutiny** (June 1905)
followed, and workers set up **soviets** (councils), notably in St Petersburg.

The **October Manifesto** promised civil rights and an elected **Duma**. Nicholas survived because the
Manifesto split the opposition (liberals accepted it), peace with Japan freed loyal troops, a large
French loan restored finances, and the army crushed the St Petersburg Soviet and the Moscow rising in
December 1905. The Fundamental Laws (1906) then reasserted his supreme power.

## How did Nicholas govern between 1906 and 1914?

Nicholas dissolved the **first and second Dumas** quickly when they demanded reform. **Stolypin**
(Prime Minister 1906–11) changed the electoral law to produce a more conservative **third Duma**, which
ran its full term, as did the **fourth** (from 1912). Stolypin combined **repression** — field courts
martial and so many hangings that the noose became known as "Stolypin's necktie" — with **land reform**
that let peasants leave the commune and buy land, aiming to create a loyal class of prosperous
farmers. He was assassinated in 1911. Opposition groups grew: the Socialist Revolutionaries, the
Social Democrats (split in 1903 into Bolsheviks and Mensheviks), and liberal Kadets and Octobrists.
At the **Lena Goldfields** (1912) troops shot striking miners, sparking a new wave of strikes.

## Why did tsarism collapse in February 1917?

The **First World War** brought heavy defeats (Tannenberg, 1914) and millions of casualties. Railways
could not supply both the army and the cities, so food and fuel ran short and prices soared. In 1915
Nicholas took personal command, making himself responsible for defeats and leaving the government to
**Alexandra**, who was influenced by **Rasputin**. Rasputin's power over appointments discredited the
monarchy; he was murdered by aristocrats in December 1916.

In **February 1917** strikes and bread queues in **Petrograd** turned into mass demonstrations. When
soldiers of the garrison **mutinied** and joined the crowds, the government had no force left. Duma
leaders formed a **Provisional Government**, while workers and soldiers formed the **Petrograd Soviet**.
Generals advised Nicholas to go, and he **abdicated** on 2 March 1917.

## Why did the Bolsheviks seize power in October 1917?

The **Provisional Government** was unelected and shared power with the Soviet, whose **Order Number 1**
said soldiers need obey only orders the Soviet approved. It kept fighting the war (the June offensive
failed), delayed land reform and postponed elections to a Constituent Assembly. **Lenin** returned in
April and issued the **April Theses**: no support for the Provisional Government, "Peace, Bread and
Land" and "All power to the Soviets". After the **July Days**, Bolsheviks were blamed and Lenin fled
to Finland. But in the **Kornilov Revolt** (August) Kerensky armed the Red Guards to defend Petrograd,
restoring the Bolsheviks' standing. By September they had majorities in the Petrograd and Moscow
soviets. **Trotsky**, chairman of the Petrograd Soviet, organised the **Military Revolutionary
Committee**, which seized key points on 24–25 October; the Winter Palace fell with little resistance.
Lenin's determination and Trotsky's organisation, the government's weakness and popular slogans
explain the success.

## How did the Bolsheviks keep power, 1917–21?

The **Decree on Peace** and **Decree on Land** won support, and the Cheka (secret police) was set up in
December 1917. The **Constituent Assembly**, in which the Socialist Revolutionaries had won the most
seats, was closed after one day in January 1918. The **Treaty of Brest-Litovsk** (March 1918) ended the
war with Germany at the cost of huge territories, including Ukraine, Poland and the Baltic lands.

In the **Civil War** (1918–21) the **Reds** faced the **Whites** — monarchists, liberals and generals such
as Kolchak, Denikin and Yudenich — supported by foreign intervention. The Reds won because they held
the central industrial area and railways, Trotsky built a disciplined **Red Army**, War Communism and
the Cheka kept supplies and order, and propaganda presented them as defenders of Russia. The Whites
were divided, geographically scattered and feared by peasants who thought they would take back land.

## What were War Communism and the NEP?

**War Communism** (1918–21) nationalised industry, requisitioned grain, banned private trade and
imposed labour discipline. It supplied the Red Army but caused peasant revolts and contributed to
the famine of 1921. When sailors at **Kronstadt**, once Bolshevik heroes, mutinied in March 1921
demanding free soviets, the Red Army crushed them. Lenin then introduced the **New Economic Policy**
(1921): a tax in kind replaced requisitioning, peasants could sell surpluses, and small businesses
reopened, while the state kept the "commanding heights" of heavy industry and banking. Production
recovered, but many communists saw the NEP as a retreat to capitalism. By Lenin's death in January
1924 the Bolsheviks had won the Civil War, created the USSR (1922) and established a one-party state.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why did the 1905 revolution fail but February 1917 succeed?** In 1905 the army stayed loyal and the
opposition split; in 1917 the Petrograd garrison mutinied and the elite abandoned Nicholas.

**Was the October Revolution a popular rising or a coup?** A classic interpretation debate: weigh
mass support for Bolshevik slogans against the small-scale seizure organised by the Military
Revolutionary Committee.

**Why did Lenin introduce the NEP?** Famine, peasant revolts and the Kronstadt Mutiny showed War
Communism could not continue.
