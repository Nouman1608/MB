Section 3.1 of the Cambridge IGCSE Commerce (0715) syllabus takes the idea of exports and imports from Topic 1 and asks what international trade does for a country, what makes it hard for traders, how it is measured, and how governments encourage or restrict it. It has four parts: **benefits to a country** (3.1.1), **challenges to exporters and importers** (3.1.2), the **Balance of Trade and Balance of Payments** (3.1.3), and **free trade, trading blocs, trade restrictions and support for exporters** (3.1.4). This page goes deeper than the international trade section of the [Globalisation of Trade study guide](/resources/igcse-cambridge-commerce-globalisation-of-trade/), with more worked figures and applied examples.

## What are the benefits of international trade to a country? (3.1.1)

The syllabus names five. Each needs a reason, not just a label.

| Benefit | Why it happens |
|---|---|
| Wider choice of goods | Consumers can buy products their own country cannot make, such as tropical fruit in a cold country, or more brands and models |
| Higher standard of living | More choice and lower prices let people buy more with their income; exports also earn income for workers |
| Lower cost of imports | Another country may produce a good more cheaply because of its climate, resources, skills or scale |
| More investment | Foreign firms set up factories, offices and warehouses to trade with the country, bringing capital and technology |
| Creation of jobs | Export industries, ports, transport firms and shops selling imports all employ people |

## What challenges do exporters and importers face? (3.1.2)

| Challenge | What it means for traders |
|---|---|
| Distance | Longer delivery times, higher transport costs, greater risk of damage |
| Language | Misunderstandings in orders, contracts and labels; translation costs |
| Methods of payment | Buyers and sellers may use different systems; arranging secure international payment |
| Documentation | Extra documents such as the certificate of origin and bill of lading |
| Transport | Choosing between sea, air, road and rail; booking space; delays at ports |
| Customs duties | Taxes on imports raise the cost and the selling price |
| Non-payment | Hard to check a distant buyer's reliability or to recover money owed abroad |
| Currencies | The buyer and seller use different currencies, so one must be exchanged |
| Foreign exchange rates | Rates change, so the value of a sale or the cost of a purchase can change before payment |
| Competition | Exporters compete with local firms and other exporters in the foreign market |
| Different legal systems | Rules on product safety, labelling and contracts differ, and disputes are harder to settle |

### How does a change in exchange rate affect an exporter? (invented figures)

A manufacturer in Country A prices a lamp at **A$100**.

- When A$1 = 0.90 euros, a European buyer pays 100 x 0.90 = **90 euros**.
- If Country A's currency rises to A$1 = 1.00 euro, the buyer pays **100 euros**, so the lamp is dearer abroad and exports may fall.
- If it falls to A$1 = 0.80 euros, the buyer pays **80 euros**, so exports become more competitive.

For an **importer** in Country A, the effect is reversed: a stronger currency makes imports cheaper.

## How do the Balance of Trade and Balance of Payments differ? (3.1.3)

- **Balance of Trade**: the difference between the value of a country's **exports of goods** (visible exports) and its **imports of goods** (visible imports) over a period, usually a year.
- **Balance of Payments**: the record of **all** a country's transactions with the rest of the world: trade in goods, trade in services (invisible trade, such as tourism, banking, insurance and shipping), and financial flows such as investment.

**Surplus**: exports greater than imports. **Deficit**: imports greater than exports.

### Worked example: calculation and interpretation (invented figures, $ million)

| Item | Exports | Imports |
|---|---|---|
| Goods | 540 | 610 |
| Services | 230 | 150 |
| Net investment flow into the country | +25 | |

- Balance of Trade = 540 - 610 = **-70**, a deficit of $70 million on goods.
- Balance on services = 230 - 150 = **+80**, a surplus.
- Balance on goods and services = -70 + 80 = **+10**.
- Including the net investment inflow: 10 + 25 = **+35**, so the Balance of Payments figure from these data is a surplus of $35 million.

**Interpretation**: the country buys more goods than it sells, but earns more than enough from services, such as tourism or financial services, to cover the gap, and it is attracting investment. A question may ask what this suggests: for example, that the country's strengths are in services rather than manufacturing.

### How do commercial activities affect them?

- A hotel welcoming foreign tourists earns **service exports**, improving the Balance of Payments.
- A supermarket importing more foreign food increases **visible imports**, worsening the Balance of Trade.
- A local insurance company covering ships for overseas clients earns **invisible exports**.
- A foreign MNC building a factory brings an **investment inflow**; when it later sends profits home, money flows out.
- A manufacturer that starts exporting through ecommerce raises **visible exports**.

## Free trade, trading blocs and trade restrictions (3.1.4)

**Free trade** is international trade without barriers such as tariffs, quotas or embargoes.

A **trading bloc** is a group of countries that agree to reduce or remove trade barriers between themselves. Features: free or easier trade between members; often a **common external tariff** charged on imports from non-members; agreed rules on standards; in some blocs, free movement of people or capital, or a shared currency.

| Advantages of joining | Disadvantages of joining |
|---|---|
| Larger market for exporters, with no tariffs inside the bloc | More competition for local firms from other members |
| Cheaper imports of goods and raw materials from members | Must follow the bloc's rules and may lose control of trade policy |
| Attracts investment from firms wanting access to the whole bloc | Imports from non-members may become dearer if a common external tariff applies |
| Political cooperation between members | Membership fees or contributions in some blocs |

### The three trade restrictions

| Restriction | Meaning | Effect on commercial activities |
|---|---|---|
| Tariff | A tax on imports | Raises the price of imports; importers sell less or earn less; local producers gain protection |
| Quota | A limit on the quantity of a good that may be imported | Shortages of the imported good; prices rise; importers must find other sources |
| Embargo | A ban on trade in a good or with a country | Trade stops completely; firms lose suppliers or markets and must reorganise supply chains |

**Tariff calculation** (invented figures): a shoe imported at $50 with a 20% tariff costs 50 + (0.20 x 50) = **$60** before transport and profit are added.

### Sources of support for exporters

- **International trade fairs**: exhibitions where exporters meet foreign buyers, show products and learn about markets.
- **Cheaper bank loans**: finance at lower interest to help exporters fund production and wait for payment.
- **Insurance guarantees**: cover against the risk of a foreign buyer not paying, so exporters can sell to new markets with confidence.

## What are the common mistakes in 3.1?

- Saying the Balance of Trade includes services; it covers goods only.
- Writing "deficit" for a positive figure, or giving the answer without a sign or the words surplus or deficit.
- Mixing up currencies (a challenge of different money) and exchange rates (a challenge of changing values).
- Describing a quota as a tax, or an embargo as a limit.
- Listing benefits of a trading bloc without the drawbacks.

## Exam technique

- **Calculate**: write the formula, substitute, give the sign and say surplus or deficit.
- **Interpret**: say what the figure shows about the country's trade.
- **Explain** a challenge: say how it affects cost, risk or sales for the trader named.
- **Discuss** joining a trading bloc: both sides, applied to the country or firm, then a conclusion.
