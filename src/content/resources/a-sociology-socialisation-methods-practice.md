## Section B

**4.** Sociologists disagree about whether society shapes individuals or individuals create society.

**(a)** Name two structuralist perspectives and one social action perspective. **[3]**

**(b)** Explain the functionalist view of the role of socialisation. **[3]**

**(c)** Explain how Giddens's concept of structuration attempts to resolve the debate. **[4]**

**5.** A researcher wants to study bullying in a secondary school.

**(a)** Explain **two** practical problems with using participant observation here. **[4]**

**(b)** Explain **two** ethical problems. **[4]**

**(c)** Explain why an interpretivist would prefer unstructured interviews to a questionnaire for this topic. **[4]**

**6.** Evidence from feral children is used in the nature–nurture debate.

**(a)** Explain what such cases suggest. **[2]**

## Section C

**7.** Distinguish between a subculture and a counter-culture, giving an example of each. **[4]**

**8.** Explain the postmodernist view of identity, and how it differs from the structuralist view. **[4]**

**(a)** Explain what a sampling frame is, and why its absence is a problem here. **[3]**

## Answers

**1. Primary** — occurs in **early childhood**, teaching language and basic norms [1]; agency: the **family** [1].
**Secondary** — occurs **later**, teaching wider social norms [1]; agency: **school**, peer group or media [1].

**2. Ascribed** status is **fixed at birth** and cannot be changed, e.g. sex or caste [1]; **achieved** status is **earned through effort**, e.g. an occupation [1].

**3. Reliability** — repeating the study with the same method produces the **same results** [1]. **Validity** — the research gives a **true and accurate picture** of what it claims to measure [1].

**4. (a)** Structuralist: **functionalism** and **Marxism** (or feminism) [1] [1]. Social action: **interactionism** [1].

**(b)** Socialisation transmits shared **norms and values** [1], creating **value consensus** [1], which binds society together and maintains social order and stability [1].

**(c)** Structuration holds that **structure and action shape each other continuously** [1]. People act **within structures** that constrain them [1], but their actions **reproduce or change those structures** over time [1]. This avoids treating individuals either as puppets of society or as wholly free agents [1].

**5. (a)** Any two, each explained: **gaining access** requires permission from gatekeepers such as the head teacher, who may refuse [1] [1]; the researcher is an **adult and cannot pass as a pupil**, so covert observation is impossible [1] [1].
*(Also: bullying happens unpredictably and often out of sight, so it may not be observed at all.)*

**(b)** Any two: **informed consent** — pupils are minors, so parental consent is needed, which is difficult to obtain for a whole school [1] [1]; **protection from harm** — witnessing bullying without intervening is ethically indefensible, but intervening destroys the research [1] [1].

**(c)** Interpretivists seek ***verstehen*** — understanding the **meanings** participants attach to their experience [1]. Unstructured interviews allow **follow-up questions** and let respondents describe experiences **in their own words** [1], giving high **validity** [1]. A questionnaire imposes the **researcher's own categories** and cannot explore a sensitive topic in depth [1].

**6. (a)** They suggest that behaviour assumed to be natural — such as language and social interaction — is in fact **learnt through socialisation** [1], since individuals raised without human contact fail to develop it [1].

**(b)** **Cross-cultural variation** [1] — norms and gender roles differ markedly between societies, which biology alone cannot explain [1].

**(c)** Such cases are **extremely rare and unrepresentative** [1], and the children often suffered **severe abuse or had pre-existing conditions**, so the effects cannot be attributed to isolation alone [1].

**7.** A **subculture** is a group within society with distinct norms and values, e.g. a youth subculture with its own dress and music [1] [1]. A **counter-culture** is a subculture actively **opposed** to the mainstream, e.g. a group rejecting mainstream values entirely rather than simply differing from them [1] [1].

**8.** Postmodernists argue that **grand narratives no longer explain society**, and identity is **fluid, chosen and consumed** rather than fixed [1] [1]. This differs from the structuralist view, where identity and behaviour are **shaped by social structures** such as class or the family, largely outside the individual's control [1] [1].

**9. (a)** A **sampling frame** is the list from which a sample is drawn [1]. Without one, the researcher cannot guarantee every student has a known and equal chance of selection, so the sample may not be representative [1] [1].

**(b)** Any reasonable non-random method with explanation, e.g. **opportunity sampling** — selecting whichever students are available and willing — is quick and requires no frame, though it risks being unrepresentative [2] [1]; or **snowball sampling**, useful where students can be reached through existing contacts [2] [1].

**10.** Triangulation **combines two or more methods** so that the strengths of one offset the weaknesses of another [1] [1], increasing confidence in the findings — for example, combining a questionnaire's reliability with an interview's validity [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Confusing reliability with validity.
- Describing a perspective without applying it to the question.
- Evaluating a method in the abstract instead of for **this** research topic.
- Giving only one side in an evaluation.
- Confusing ascribed with achieved status.
