## Section B

Answer **one** question from this section.

**4.** Evaluate the view that structuralist perspectives provide a better explanation of human behaviour and society than social action perspectives. **[26]**

**5.** Evaluate the practical and ethical problems a researcher would face in using participant observation to study bullying in a secondary school, and explain why an interpretivist would prefer this method to a questionnaire for this topic. **[26]**

## Answers

**1.** Primary socialisation occurs in **early childhood**, teaching language and basic norms and values through the **family** [2]. Secondary socialisation occurs **later**, teaching the wider norms of society, through agencies such as **school**, the peer group or the media [2]. The two are linked: secondary socialisation builds on the foundation primary socialisation lays down, rather than replacing it [2].

**2. (a)** **Ascribed** status is **fixed at birth** and cannot be changed, e.g. sex or caste [2]; **achieved** status is **earned through a person's own effort**, e.g. an occupation or qualification [2].

**(b)** Cases of children raised with little or no human contact suggest that behaviour long assumed to be natural — language, social interaction, even walking upright — is in fact **learnt through socialisation**, since these children fail to develop it without human contact [3]. However, such cases are **extremely rare and methodologically weak as evidence**: the children involved are usually few in number, unrepresentative, and often had **pre-existing conditions or suffered severe abuse and neglect**, so it is difficult to attribute their lack of development to social isolation alone rather than to these other factors [3]. A stronger version of the same argument draws on **cross-cultural variation** — norms, gender roles and behaviours assumed to be universal differ markedly between societies, which a purely biological explanation cannot account for [2].

**3. (a)** **Reliability** means that repeating the research using the same method would produce the **same results** [3]. **Validity** means the method gives a **true and accurate picture** of what it claims to measure [3].

**(b)** **Triangulation** combines two or more research methods so that the strengths of one method can offset the weaknesses of another, increasing overall confidence in the findings [4]. For example, a researcher might combine a large-scale **questionnaire**, which is reliable and can be repeated but may lack depth, with a small number of **unstructured interviews**, which are high in validity but harder to replicate — the questionnaire's breadth and the interviews' depth compensate for each other's limitations [4]. Triangulation is particularly valued by researchers who reject a rigid positivist/interpretivist divide, since it treats quantitative and qualitative data as complementary rather than as rival, incompatible approaches [2].

## Model answer guidance for Section B

**Essay 4** should open by defining both traditions — **structuralist** perspectives (functionalism, Marxism, feminism) see individual behaviour as shaped by the structure of society, while the **social action** perspective (interactionism) sees society as built up from individuals' own meanings and interactions. The essay should then set out the structuralist case in detail: functionalism's account of value consensus and social order, and Marxism's account of how class structure shapes behaviour and consciousness, each with a named theorist or concept. It should set out the social action case with equal weight: interactionism's focus on labelling, the negotiated nature of everyday interaction, and its criticism that structuralist accounts are over-deterministic and neglect individual agency. The strongest answers use **Giddens's concept of structuration** — the idea that structure and action shape each other continuously, so that people act within structures that constrain them while their actions simultaneously reproduce or change those structures — to argue that neither tradition alone is adequate, and reach a reasoned judgement rather than simply describing both sides in turn.

**Essay 5** should identify and explain at least two **practical problems** (for example, gaining access requires permission from gatekeepers such as a head teacher who may refuse it; an adult researcher cannot pass as a pupil, so covert observation is impossible; and bullying often happens unpredictably and out of sight, so it may go unobserved even with access granted) and at least two **ethical problems** (informed consent is difficult to obtain from minors and their parents across a whole school; and witnessing bullying without intervening is ethically indefensible, while intervening would compromise the research). It should then explain why an interpretivist would prefer unstructured interviews to a questionnaire for this specific topic: interpretivists seek **verstehen** — an empathetic understanding of the meanings participants attach to their own experience — and unstructured interviews allow follow-up questions and let respondents describe experiences in their own words, giving higher validity than a questionnaire's fixed, researcher-imposed categories, which cannot explore a sensitive topic like bullying in depth. The strongest answers weigh this preference against unstructured interviews' own weaknesses (small, unrepresentative samples; low reliability) before reaching a conclusion.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Confusing reliability with validity.
- Describing a perspective without applying it to the question.
- Evaluating a method in the abstract instead of for **this** research topic.
- Giving only one side of an essay question rather than weighing both traditions or both problems against each other.
- Confusing ascribed with achieved status.
