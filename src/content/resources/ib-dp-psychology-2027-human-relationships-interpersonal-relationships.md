Interpersonal relationships is the second aspect of the **human relationships** context in IB Psychology (first assessment 2027). The guide says a lot depends on how relationships develop and what influences them. It lists purely cognitive models, genetic factors, chemical messengers and communication or language, and it adds that understanding these makes it possible to design strategies for improving relationships. The guide asks students to understand **one or more of social relationships or interpersonal conflict and aggression**. This page uses social relationships (friendship and romantic partnership) as the main thread and shows how each idea also applies to conflict and aggression, so you can adapt it to whichever choice your class makes.

## The four areas of study

The guide gives four areas. Each has a learning objective that starts with a verb you must meet.

| Area | Learning objective |
|---|---|
| Chemical messengers | The role of one or more chemical messengers in interpersonal relationships |
| Cognitive explanations | One or more cognitive explanations for interpersonal relationships |
| Communication and language | The role of communication in interpersonal relationships |
| Strategies for improving relationships | One or more strategies for improving interpersonal relationships |

## 1. Chemical messengers

A **chemical messenger** is a substance that carries a signal in the body. **Neurotransmitters** work across synapses. **Hormones** travel in the blood. **Pheromones** are chemicals released into the environment that may affect other individuals of the same species.

### Oxytocin and trust

Oxytocin is a hormone and neuromodulator linked to bonding. In an experiment by Kosfeld and colleagues (2005), participants sprayed oxytocin or a placebo into the nose and then played a trust game. A larger share of the oxytocin group made the maximum investment with an unknown partner. The authors interpreted this as oxytocin increasing willingness to trust. This supports a causal role for oxytocin in social behaviour, because participants were randomly allocated and the manipulation was controlled.

### Dopamine and serotonin in romantic love

Aron and colleagues (2005) used fMRI to scan people who reported being intensely in love while they looked at photographs of their partner. Activity was found in regions rich in dopamine, such as the ventral tegmental area and the caudate nucleus, which are involved in reward and motivation. This suggests that early romantic love resembles a reward-driven state. The scans are correlational: they show activity while participants viewed a photograph, not that dopamine causes love.

### Testosterone and aggression

Dabbs and colleagues (1987) measured testosterone in saliva among male prisoners and reported higher levels in those who had committed violent crimes. This is a correlation, and it cannot show direction: aggression and dominant behaviour can also raise testosterone. Reviews suggest the link is modest and depends on situation, so most psychologists treat testosterone as one influence among many, interacting with social context.

### Pheromones

In a well-known study, Wedekind and colleagues (1995) asked women to smell T-shirts worn by men and rate pleasantness. Women tended to prefer the smell of men whose immune-system genes (MHC) differed from their own. The result has been debated and replication has been mixed, and human pheromones have not been conclusively identified.

### Evaluating the chemical messenger explanation

- **Strengths:** experiments with placebo and random allocation (oxytocin) support causal claims; brain imaging allows non-invasive measurement.
- **Limitations:** biological reductionism (love is far more than chemistry); many studies use small samples of mostly male or student participants; intranasal oxytocin effects may be small and context dependent; results may differ for people in long-term relationships.
- **Concepts:** causality, measurement, perspective (reductionist versus holistic).

## 2. Cognitive explanations

A cognitive explanation says that how we **think** about other people shapes relationships. The guide's overview mentions "purely cognitive models". Choose one or more.

### Similarity and attraction

Byrne's research and later work suggest that we like people whose attitudes are similar to ours, since similarity is rewarding and validates our views. Proximity (propinquity) and familiarity also matter in friendship formation.

### Social exchange and investment

Thibaut and Kelley's social exchange theory says we weigh the rewards and costs of a relationship and compare the outcome with our **comparison level** (what we expect) and the **comparison level for alternatives**. Rusbult's **investment model** adds that commitment rises with satisfaction and with investment (time, shared friends, emotional effort) and falls if alternatives look attractive. Equity theory says that partners are happiest when ratio of inputs to outputs seems fair.

**Worked example.** Two friends have grown apart. Ayesha puts in most of the effort in keeping contact and gets little back. By social exchange, rewards are falling and costs rising. By the investment model, Ayesha will stay if she has invested a lot and has few attractive alternatives. A strong answer uses the model to predict whether the friendship continues.

### Attributions and expectations

Attribution theory looks at how partners explain each other's behaviour. In happy relationships, partners tend to excuse negative behaviour ("he was tired") and credit positive behaviour to character. In distressed relationships the pattern reverses. Fincham and Bradbury (1993) followed married couples over time and found that distress-maintaining attributions were linked to later marital satisfaction. Positive illusions (seeing a partner more favourably than is objectively true) may help relationships last, which is a cognitive bias with a benefit.

### Cognition and aggression

For interpersonal conflict and aggression, **hostile attribution bias** is a cognitive explanation: people who interpret ambiguous actions as deliberately hostile respond aggressively. Dodge's research with children found that aggressive children were more likely to read ambiguous situations as hostile. A schema of other people as threatening can drive this bias.

### Evaluating cognitive explanations

- **Strengths:** testable, practical (the thinking can be changed), and they link to the cognitive approach (schemas, biases).
- **Limitations:** correlational designs (do negative thoughts cause unhappiness, or does unhappiness cause negative thoughts?), self-report measures and Western samples. They underplay emotion and biology.
- **Concepts:** causality (bidirectional ambiguity), bias, perspective and measurement.

## 3. Communication and language

Communication is how partners exchange meaning. The guide asks for the **role of communication** in relationships.

- **Self-disclosure.** Altman and Taylor's social penetration theory proposes that relationships deepen as partners share more personal information, gradually and reciprocally. Aron and colleagues (1997) paired strangers and had them answer questions that escalated in personal depth; these pairs reported greater closeness than pairs who made small talk. That suggests structured self-disclosure can build closeness quickly.
- **Patterns of conflict.** Gottman's observational research on couples describes four damaging patterns: criticism, contempt, defensiveness and stonewalling. He argues that stable couples keep a high ratio of positive to negative interactions. The accuracy of his predictions about divorce is disputed, and his samples were mostly American couples.
- **Language style and accommodation.** People often adjust speech to be more like their partner's. Convergence signals liking and belonging; divergence signals distance.
- **Digital communication.** Texts and social media remove tone and body language, which can create misunderstanding, but they also allow people to stay in touch and to disclose more easily. (This is the area where the HL technology extension adds examples.)

**Evaluation.** Observational research has high ecological validity, but being observed may change behaviour (participant reactivity), and coding the behaviour is subjective, so inter-rater reliability matters. Culture affects what counts as polite or direct communication.

**Concepts:** measurement (coding behaviour), culture and perspective, change.

## 4. Strategies for improving relationships

Strategies follow from the explanations above. A good answer names the strategy, says which explanation it rests on and evaluates the evidence.

| Strategy | Based on | How it works | Evaluation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Communication training for couples | Gottman-type observation | Practise active listening, soften complaints, take breaks when overwhelmed | Practical and observable, but long-term outcomes rely on self-reports |
| Attribution retraining | Cognitive explanations | Learn to question negative explanations of a partner's behaviour | Costs little; needs motivation and may not suit every couple |
| Structured self-disclosure | Social penetration theory | Escalating, reciprocal sharing | Short-term closeness found in lab studies; unclear for lasting change |
| Shared goals and contact between groups | Social identity and contact ideas | Cooperative tasks reduce conflict | Evidence from schools and camps; effects depend on equal status |
| Anger management and social skills training | Cognitive and social learning accounts of aggression | Spot triggers, reframe hostile interpretations, practise calm responses | Supports for children and adolescents; transfer outside the programme varies |

**Worked example.** A pair of flatmates quarrel over chores. The strategy is to schedule a weekly check-in with rules: each person states a need without blaming, the other repeats it back, and then they agree one change. The rule of repeating back reduces defensiveness (communication) and encourages charitable attributions (cognitive). A good evaluation notes that the flatmates must both want to change.

## The class practical: a survey or questionnaire

The practical for this context is a survey or questionnaire. The guide suggests the love quiz of Hazan and Shaver (1987) and the cross-cultural study of attraction by Buss (1989). These examples are suggestions only.

- **Hazan and Shaver (1987)** published a questionnaire in a newspaper and asked readers to choose the description that fitted their feelings about romantic relationships. The responses were grouped into secure, avoidant and anxious types, in proportions similar to those found in infants. Evaluate: self-selected sample, retrospective self-report, a forced-choice measure.
- **Buss (1989)** surveyed people in many countries about what they wanted in a partner. Across cultures, men tended to rate physical attractiveness and youth more highly, and women tended to rate financial prospects and ambition more highly. Evaluate: self-reported preferences are not actual choices; samples were often not representative of whole populations.

For Paper 2, focus on the method: sampling, question design, closed versus open items, pilot testing, anonymity and the biases that threaten self-report (social desirability and response bias).

## Linking the concepts

- **Causality:** experiments (oxytocin) versus correlations (brain scans, attributions).
- **Measurement:** coding conflict, questionnaires and operationalising "closeness".
- **Perspective:** biological, cognitive and communication-based accounts, plus culture.
- **Bias:** positive illusions, hostile attribution bias, sampling bias in surveys.
- **Change:** which strategies change relationships and whether the change lasts.
- **Responsibility:** consent in studies about relationships, privacy, and advice to couples that assumes a particular model of a good relationship.

## Common mistakes

- Reducing love to a hormone and ignoring evaluation of reductionism.
- Describing a study without linking it to chemical messengers, cognition or communication.
- Writing a general list of "ways to improve relationships" without a link to an explanation.
- Overclaiming from correlational brain imaging.
- Ignoring culture when describing "good communication".
