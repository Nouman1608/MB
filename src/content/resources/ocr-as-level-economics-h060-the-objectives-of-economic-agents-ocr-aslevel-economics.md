## What maximisation means

To **maximise** is to choose the option that gives the greatest possible value of an objective, subject to a constraint. A consumer's constraint is income; a firm's is its costs and the demand for its product; a government's is its budget. Maximising usually means thinking at the margin: carrying on with an activity while the extra benefit is at least the extra cost.

## Consumers: maximising utility

Economists assume consumers aim to maximise **utility**, the satisfaction they get from consumption, given their income and prices.

**Worked example (invented).** Maya values visits to a climbing wall. The most she would pay for each extra visit in a month is £20 for the first, £14 for the second, £10 for the third, £7 for the fourth and £4 for the fifth. A visit costs £8.

- The first three visits are each worth more to her than £8, so she buys them.
- The fourth is worth only £7, less than its price, so she stops at three.
- Her net gain is (20 - 8) + (14 - 8) + (10 - 8) = £20, which is her consumer surplus.

Each extra visit adds less satisfaction than the one before, so the value of extra units falls. That is why demand curves slope downward: at a lower price, more units are worth buying.

## Workers: income and leisure

Workers are assumed to maximise their utility from a combination of income and leisure. A higher wage raises the reward for each hour worked, which usually encourages more work. But workers also value job security, conditions, location and the satisfaction of the work itself, so they may accept lower pay for a more rewarding job.

## Firms: maximising profit, and alternatives

The standard assumption is that firms aim to **maximise profit**, the difference between total revenue and total cost. They may pursue other objectives too.

**Firm Kappa (invented).** Price falls as output rises. Output is in thousand units and money in £ thousand.

| Output | Price | Total revenue | Total cost | Profit |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 46 | 46 | 31 | 15 |
| 2 | 42 | 84 | 44 | 40 |
| 3 | 38 | 114 | 59 | 55 |
| 4 | 34 | 136 | 76 | 60 |
| 5 | 30 | 150 | 95 | 55 |
| 6 | 26 | 156 | 116 | 40 |
| 7 | 22 | 154 | 139 | 15 |
| 8 | 18 | 144 | 164 | -20 |

| Objective | Output chosen | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Profit maximisation | 4 | Highest profit, £60 thousand |
| Revenue maximisation | 6 | Highest revenue, £156 thousand |
| Sales maximisation subject to covering costs | 7 | Largest output with profit at least zero |
| Satisficing: largest revenue with profit of at least £50 thousand | 5 | Profit of 55 meets the target; revenue 150 is the highest of outputs 3 to 5 |

The table shows that the objective chosen changes output and price. A revenue maximiser produces more and charges less than a profit maximiser, so consumers may gain while owners earn less.

### Why firms might not maximise profit

- **Separation of ownership and control:** in large companies, shareholders own the firm but managers run it. Managers may prefer higher sales, growth or status, because their pay and prestige depend on the size of the firm.
- **Satisficing:** with many stakeholders (owners, workers, customers, local communities), managers may aim for satisfactory results across several goals rather than the maximum of one.
- **Survival:** a new firm or one in a downturn may simply aim to cover its costs.
- **Lack of information:** firms rarely know their revenue and costs at every output, so they cannot locate the profit-maximising point precisely.
- **Corporate social responsibility:** firms may accept lower profit to treat workers, suppliers or the environment well, which can also protect their reputation and long-run profit.
- **Social enterprises and not-for-profit organisations** pursue social aims and use any surplus for them.

## Government: maximising the welfare of society

Government is assumed to aim to maximise social welfare: the well-being of society as a whole. In practice this means balancing objectives such as growth, low unemployment, stable prices, a fair distribution of income and protection of the environment. These often conflict, for example when a policy that raises growth increases pollution.

Two complications matter for evaluation. Welfare is hard to measure, because it includes things without a price, such as health and clean air. And governments are made up of people with their own objectives: politicians may favour policies that win votes in the short run over those that raise welfare in the long run.

## Evaluating the assumed objectives

| Assumption | Why it is useful | Why it may not hold |
|---|---|---|
| Consumers maximise utility | Predicts that demand falls as price rises | Habit, poor information, impulse buying |
| Workers balance income and leisure | Explains responses to wage changes | Non-pay factors, limited choice of hours |
| Firms maximise profit | Gives clear predictions about output and price | Managerial objectives, satisficing, lack of information |
| Government maximises welfare | Sets a benchmark for judging policy | Conflicting goals, political incentives, measurement problems |

A balanced judgement: the maximising assumptions are simplifications that make predictions possible, and they often predict well, but real agents frequently pursue several goals at once.

## Annotated model answer

*Question: Using the data for Firm Kappa, explain why a revenue-maximising firm would charge a lower price than a profit-maximising firm. [4]*

> A profit maximiser chooses output of 4 thousand units, where profit is highest at £60 thousand, and charges £34. A revenue maximiser expands to 6 thousand units, where total revenue peaks at £156 thousand. To sell the extra units it must cut the price to £26. Beyond 4 thousand units, each extra unit adds more to cost than to revenue, so profit falls to £40 thousand, but revenue is still rising until 6 thousand units.

*Why it works:* it uses figures from the table for both objectives and explains the link between output, price and profit.

## Common mistakes

- Treating revenue and profit as the same thing.
- Saying firms "always" maximise profit, or that they never do.
- Defining satisficing as "not trying"; it means aiming for satisfactory results across goals.
- Listing objectives without explaining why an agent might choose them.
- Ignoring the constraint: maximising is always subject to income, costs or a budget.

## Exam technique

- **Section A:** multiple-choice items may give a table and ask which output a firm with a stated objective would choose. Read the objective carefully: revenue, profit, or sales subject to a profit constraint.
- **Section B:** use the data to show how the objective changes output, price or profit.
- **Section C:** an essay on whether firms maximise profit should consider who controls the firm, competition and information, ending with a judgement about which firms are most likely to maximise profit.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is utility measurable?**
Not directly. Economists use willingness to pay as a practical way of showing how much extra satisfaction a unit gives.

**Why might competition push firms toward profit maximisation?**
In very competitive markets, firms that do not keep costs low and profits up may make losses and be forced out, so survival requires behaving close to profit maximisation.
