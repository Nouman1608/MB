## 4.2.1 Identify and classify costs

**Fixed costs** do not change when output changes, within the range the business normally works in. Rent, insurance and managers' salaries are typical. **Variable costs** change directly with output: raw materials, packaging and the wages of workers paid per item. **Total cost** is fixed costs plus variable costs. **Average cost** (also called cost per unit) is total cost divided by the number of units produced. The syllabus gives these four as examples, so learn a definition and a formula for each.

| Cost | How to find it |
|---|---|
| Total variable cost | variable cost per unit x output |
| Total cost | fixed costs + total variable cost |
| Average cost | total cost / output |

### Worked example: classifying and calculating

Holmcastle Candles pays rent of 6 000, insurance of 1 000 and salaries of 5 000 each month. These are fixed, so fixed costs are 12 000. Each candle needs wax (1.50), a jar (0.90) and labour (1.60). Variable cost per candle is 1.50 + 0.90 + 1.60 = 4.00.

| Output | Total variable cost | Total cost | Average cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3 000 | 12 000 | 24 000 | 8.00 |
| 6 000 | 24 000 | 36 000 | 6.00 |

Total cost rose by half, but output doubled. Average cost fell because the same 12 000 of fixed costs is shared over more candles (4.00 per candle at 3 000 units, 2.00 at 6 000). This is why higher output often lowers average cost.

### Cost-based decisions: stop or continue?

A business may ask whether a product that shows a loss should be dropped. The key question is which costs go away if production stops. Variable costs stop. Fixed costs often do not, at least in the short run. So compare the revenue from the product with its variable costs. If revenue is higher, the product makes a **contribution** (revenue minus variable costs) towards fixed costs, and stopping makes the business worse off. This is the standard way to approach the decision; the syllabus only asks for simple cost-based decisions.

### Worked example: the scarf line

Bexwyn Fabrics sells 2 000 scarves a month at 9 each. Variable cost is 6.50 per scarf. Fixed costs of 7 000 a month are charged to the scarf line, and the firm pays them whether or not scarves are made.

- Revenue = 2 000 x 9 = 18 000
- Variable costs = 2 000 x 6.50 = 13 000
- Contribution = 18 000 - 13 000 = 5 000
- Loss if it continues = 5 000 - 7 000 = a loss of 2 000
- Loss if it stops = 0 - 7 000 = a loss of 7 000

Continuing loses 2 000; stopping loses 7 000. The scarf line should continue for now. A good answer adds that this changes if the fixed costs could be avoided, if the money could earn more elsewhere, or if the contribution will turn negative.

## 4.2.2 Economies and diseconomies of scale

**Economies of scale** are the cost advantages that let average cost fall as a business increases its scale of production. They are not simply "lower costs": the saving is in cost per unit.

| Type | How average cost falls | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Purchasing | Bulk buying earns discounts from suppliers | A juice maker buys fruit by the lorry load at a lower price per kilo |
| Marketing | The cost of advertising is spread over more units | One TV advert supports a very large sales volume |
| Financial | Larger firms are seen as lower risk, so banks may charge lower interest | A bigger firm borrows at a cheaper rate |
| Managerial | Large firms can afford specialist managers who work more efficiently | A full-time finance director replaces the owner doing the accounts |
| Technical | Large machines, or a production line, lower cost per unit but only pay off at high output | A high-speed bottling line |

**Diseconomies of scale** are the rise in average cost that can happen when a business becomes too large.

- **Poor communication.** Messages pass through many layers, arrive late or change on the way, so mistakes and delays cost money.
- **Lack of commitment from employees.** Workers in a very large firm may feel unimportant, so absence and labour turnover rise and productivity falls.
- **Weak coordination.** Many departments or sites pull in different directions, which causes duplication and delays.

### Worked example: a cost curve

Pellbright Juice records these results (all amounts in dollars).

| Output (litres) | Total cost | Average cost |
|---|---|---|
| 10 000 | 12 000 | 1.20 |
| 20 000 | 19 000 | 0.95 |
| 40 000 | 32 000 | 0.80 |
| 80 000 | 68 000 | 0.85 |
| 120 000 | 116 400 | 0.97 |

Average cost falls from 1.20 to 0.80 as output rises to 40 000 litres: economies of scale, such as bulk fruit discounts and a faster filling machine. After 40 000 it rises, to 0.97 at 120 000 litres. This points to diseconomies, perhaps because supervisors cannot coordinate three shifts. Draw average cost against output and you get a U shape: it falls, bottoms out, then rises.

## 4.2.3 Break-even analysis

**Break-even** is the point where a business sells enough for total revenue to equal total cost, so it makes neither profit nor loss. Below it there is a loss; above it there is a profit.

Two standard forms are used. The syllabus does not print formulas for them, so these are the standard forms:

- Contribution per unit = selling price per unit - variable cost per unit
- Break-even output = fixed costs / contribution per unit

### Worked example: break-even output

Ostenfield Kites sells kites at 18 each. Variable cost is 7 per kite and fixed costs are 33 000 a month.

- Contribution per unit = 18 - 7 = 11
- Break-even output = 33 000 / 11 = **3 000 kites**

Check: revenue 3 000 x 18 = 54 000; total cost 33 000 + 3 000 x 7 = 54 000. They match.

### Constructing, completing or amending a chart

A simple break-even chart puts output along the horizontal axis and money (for both costs and revenue) up the vertical axis. Three lines are drawn.

1. **Fixed costs**: a horizontal line at 33 000.
2. **Total costs**: starts at 33 000 on the vertical axis (zero output) and rises. Plot a second point, for example at 6 000 kites: 33 000 + 6 000 x 7 = 75 000.
3. **Total revenue**: starts at the origin and rises more steeply. At 6 000 kites it is 6 000 x 18 = 108 000.

Where total revenue crosses total costs is break-even, at 3 000 kites. Two points per line are enough, and a ruler makes the lines straight.

```
Money
  ^                       / Revenue
  |                     /   . Total costs
  |                   /  .
  |                 X
  |             . /    <- X = break-even
  |  FC ----.------------------- fixed costs
  |    .  /
  +---------------------------> Output
```

To **amend** a chart when something changes, move the right line. Higher rent lifts the fixed cost line and the total cost line by the same amount, so break-even moves right. A higher price makes the revenue line steeper, so break-even moves left. Lower variable cost per unit makes the total cost line less steep, so break-even also moves left.

### Margin of safety

The **margin of safety** is how far sales (or output) are above the break-even level. It shows how far sales can fall before the business makes a loss.

- Margin of safety (units) = actual or expected output - break-even output
- As a percentage = margin of safety / actual or expected output x 100 (standard form)

If Ostenfield expects to sell 3 800 kites: 3 800 - 3 000 = **800 kites**, or 800 / 3 800 x 100 = 21.1% of expected sales. On the chart it is the gap between break-even and the actual output, measured along the output axis. A large margin means the business has more protection if demand falls.

### Using break-even to help decisions

Suppose Ostenfield raises the price to 20, but expects sales to fall to 3 300 kites.

- New contribution = 20 - 7 = 13
- New break-even = 33 000 / 13 = 2 538.46, so about **2 539 kites**
- Profit at 3 300 kites = 13 x 3 300 - 33 000 = **9 900**
- Profit at the old price and 3 800 kites = 11 x 3 800 - 33 000 = **8 800**

The higher price lowers break-even and raises profit, even with fewer sales. A justified answer also notes that the result depends on the forecast fall in demand. If sales dropped much further, the decision would look different.

### Limitations of break-even analysis

- It assumes all output is sold. Unsold stock still costs money.
- It assumes the selling price stays the same at every output, so discounts for bulk orders are ignored.
- It assumes variable cost per unit is constant. Bulk buying (a purchasing economy) would change it.
- Fixed costs are treated as fixed, but at high output a business may need a new factory.
- It is usually drawn for one product, but many firms sell several.
- It depends on forecasts, which can be wrong, and it is a snapshot that ignores competitors' reactions.

## Common errors

- Dividing fixed costs by the selling price instead of contribution per unit.
- Writing "costs fall" instead of "average cost falls" for economies of scale.
- Putting the fixed cost line through the origin on a chart. It starts above zero on the vertical axis.
- Calling a benefit a type of economy without linking it to cost per unit.
- Stopping a product because it shows a loss without checking the contribution.
- Giving margin of safety in money when the question gave units, or leaving out units.
