## Questions

**1.** Distinguish between a direct tax and an indirect tax, giving one example of each. **[4]**

**2.** In Country C, the marginal propensity to save is 0.10, to pay tax 0.30 and to import 0.20.

**(a)** Calculate the multiplier and the change in national income if government spending rises by 9bn. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain one reason why the actual change in real output might be smaller. **[2]**

**3.** In Country C, income tax has a tax-free allowance of 15,000, a rate of 12% on income from 15,000 to 35,000 and 32% on income above 35,000.

**(a)** Find the tax bill and the average rate for incomes of 30,000 and 80,000. **[3]**
**(b)** State whether the tax is progressive, proportional or regressive, with a reason. **[2]**

**4.** A government charges every household a fixed licence fee of 150. Two households have incomes of 15,000 and 60,000. Calculate the fee as a percentage of each income and classify the charge. **[3]**

**5.** Country D has GDP of 1,200bn. Its public spending (billions) is: pensions and benefits 105, health 69, education 45, defence 21, new road and rail construction 30, public order and administration 30.

**(a)** Calculate transfer payments as a percentage of total spending and of GDP, and total spending as a percentage of GDP. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain one reason why a government might undertake the capital spending shown. **[3]**

**6.** Explain how fiscal policy can be used to influence aggregate supply. **[6]**

**7.** Explain the difference between vertical equity and horizontal equity as principles of taxation. **[4]**

**8.** Analyse how government spending and taxation can affect the pattern of economic activity. **[8]**

**9.** Evaluate the case for raising more tax revenue through a broad tax on spending rather than through income tax. **[9]**

**10.** Country E has a marginal propensity to withdraw of 0.4. It considers a rise in government spending of 8bn, or a tax cut of 8bn of which 50% is spent on domestic goods.

**(a)** Calculate the change in national income under each policy. **[4]**
**(b)** Evaluate which policy would be better for the economy. **[6]**

**11.** Country A raises 300bn in tax, 200bn direct and 100bn indirect, and plans to raise 150bn from each. Assume every household's direct tax falls by 25% and its indirect tax rises by 50%. Household L has income of 20,000, spends 90% of it and pays direct tax of 5% of income. Household H has income of 100,000, spends 60% of it and pays direct tax of 25% of income. Indirect tax is an effective 10% of spending.

**(a)** Calculate the total tax paid by each household as a percentage of income before and after the change. **[4]**
**(b)** Assess the economic significance of the change in the distribution of taxation. **[8]**

**12.** Explain one macroeconomic and one microeconomic function of fiscal policy, and show how a single measure can serve both. **[5]**

## Answers

**1.** A direct tax is levied on income, profit or wealth [1] and paid by the person or firm taxed, such as income tax [1]. An indirect tax is levied on spending [1] and collected by the seller who passes it on in the price, such as a sales tax or an excise duty [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A definition plus an example for each type covers a "distinguish" command. Two examples with no contrast leave the difference unstated.

**2. (a)** MPW = 0.10 + 0.30 + 0.20 = 0.60 [1]. Multiplier = 1 / 0.60 = 1.67 [1]. Change in income = 9 × 1.67 = **15bn** [1].
**(b)** If the economy is near full capacity [1], extra demand raises prices rather than output, so real output rises by less than 15bn [1]. (Alternatives: time lags, or crowding out of private spending.) **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the three withdrawals being added before the division. Link any reason to the size of the output change.

**3. (a)** At 30,000: (30,000 − 15,000) × 0.12 = 1,800 [1]. At 80,000: 20,000 × 0.12 + 45,000 × 0.32 = 2,400 + 14,400 = 16,800 [1]. Average rates: 6% and 21% [1].
**(b)** The tax is **progressive** [1] because the average rate rises as income rises [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Working band by band shows where each rate applies. Comparing average rates, not amounts paid, supports the classification.

**4.** 150 / 15,000 = 1.0% [1]. 150 / 60,000 = 0.25% [1]. The share of income falls as income rises, so the charge is **regressive** [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Classification depends on the percentage of income, so give both percentages before naming the type.

**5. (a)** Total spending = 105 + 69 + 45 + 21 + 30 + 30 = 300bn. Transfers: 105 / 300 = **35%** of total spending [1]; 105 / 1,200 = **8.75%** of GDP [1]; total spending 300 / 1,200 = **25%** of GDP [1].
**(b)** Roads and rail raise the economy's productive capacity [1]. Private firms may underprovide them because the benefits are spread widely and hard to charge for [1]. Lower transport costs raise productivity and potential output, and construction adds to demand in the short run [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Pick the right denominator for each percentage, as the same 105 gives two figures. For the reason, link the spending to a market failure or growth effect.

**6.** Spending on infrastructure lowers firms' costs and raises productivity [1], shifting long-run aggregate supply right [1]. Spending on education and training improves workers' skills [1], raising potential output [1]. Tax incentives, such as lower tax on extra earnings or relief on investment, raise labour supply or investment [1], though the response is uncertain and slow [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Write each route as a short chain ending in a shift of aggregate supply.

**7.** Vertical equity means that people with a greater ability to pay should pay more tax [1], for example higher earners paying a higher share of income [1]. Horizontal equity means that people in similar circumstances should pay the same tax [1], for example two employees on equal pay paying equal tax regardless of how they spend [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Give each term its own definition and example, so the two ideas stay apart.

**8.** A fuel tax raises the cost of road travel [1], shifting demand to rail and efficient vehicles [1]. Spending on regional transport raises firms' profitability in that region [1], attracting investment and jobs, so activity shifts between areas [1]. Subsidies for one industry lower its costs [1] and draw labour and capital from others [1]. Higher public spending raises the public sector's share of output [1]; if borrowing raises interest rates, private investment may be crowded out [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* "Pattern of activity" needs a before and after, such as from one sector, region or mode to another. Write examples as chains of cause and effect.

**9.** For a spending tax: a broad base makes it hard to avoid and gives stable revenue [1], and it leaves the return from extra work untaxed, so work incentives are less affected [1]. It can also target harmful goods to correct externalities [1]. Against: it is often regressive, because low-income households spend a larger share of income [1], so a household spending 95% of income pays a larger share than one spending 55% [1]. Income tax can be progressive, supporting vertical equity [1] and protecting low earners with an allowance [1], but high rates may weaken incentives and encourage avoidance [1]. Judgement: the better choice depends on whether the government values equity or incentives more [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* A one-sided list of merits stays descriptive. Tie each tax to a principle, and close on a judgement and say what it hinges on.

**10. (a)** k = 1 / 0.4 = 2.5 [1]. Spending: 8 × 2.5 = **20bn** [1]. Tax cut: first-round injection = 8 × 0.5 = 4bn [1]; 4 × 2.5 = **10bn** [1].
**(b)** Spending gives a larger boost because the whole sum is injected at once [1]. A tax cut leaves the choice with households and can raise work incentives, but some is saved, more if confidence is low [1]. With spare capacity either policy raises output; near full capacity both raise prices [1]. Capital projects can also raise aggregate supply, while wasteful spending raises demand only [1]. Projects take time to start while a tax cut acts quickly [1]. Judgement: spending is stronger in a deep recession, but a tax cut may suit if efficiency and choice matter more [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* Carry the calculated figures into the evaluation: 20bn against 10bn beats a general claim.

**11. (a)** Household L before: direct 1,000 plus indirect 18,000 × 0.10 = 1,800, total 2,800, so **14%** [1]. After: direct 750 plus indirect 2,700 = 3,450, so **17.25%** [1]. Household H before: direct 25,000 plus indirect 6,000 = 31,000, so **31%** [1]. After: 18,750 plus 9,000 = 27,750, so **27.75%** [1].
**(b)** The poorer household pays a larger share and the richer one a smaller share, so the tax system is less progressive [1]. Post-tax incomes are 17,200 and 69,000 before (a ratio of 4.01) and 16,550 and 72,250 after (4.37) [1], so inequality in disposable income widens [1]. Lower direct tax may improve incentives to work and invest [1], supporting aggregate supply [1]. Indirect taxes are harder to avoid [1]. Household L spends more of its income, so lower disposable income cuts consumption more [1]. The result rests on the assumption of equal proportional changes, and on the government's priorities [1]. **[12]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the calculated percentages as evidence, and name both a gain (incentives) and a loss (equity) before judging.

**12.** A macroeconomic function is influencing aggregate demand, such as cutting income tax to raise consumption in a downturn [1], which raises output and employment [1]. A microeconomic function is changing outcomes in a market, such as a tax on a good that causes pollution [1], which raises its price and reduces output towards the socially efficient level [1]. A fuel tax does both: it corrects an externality in one market and also raises revenue and reduces demand across the economy [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Label which example is macro and which micro, using a different measure for each before showing the overlap.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calling a tax progressive because the amount paid rises, without comparing average rates.
- Applying the top rate to the whole income instead of the top slice only.
- Counting the whole of a tax cut as the injection when part is saved or spent on imports.
- Using the wrong denominator when expressing spending as a percentage.
- Describing a tax without judging it against a principle.
- Ending an evaluation without a judgement tied to a condition.
