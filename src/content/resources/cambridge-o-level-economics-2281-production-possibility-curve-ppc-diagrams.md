## 1.4.1 Definition and drawing

A **production possibility curve** shows the maximum combinations of two goods (or services) that an economy can produce when all its resources are fully and efficiently used, with its current technology.

It is the economic problem in a diagram: resources are finite, so there is a limit to output (the curve), and producing more of one good means producing less of the other (the slope).

### Worked example 1: drawing from data

Talvia (invented) produces two types of good: machines (capital goods) and consumer goods.

| Point | Machines (thousands) | Consumer goods (thousands) |
|---|---|---|
| A | 0 | 200 |
| B | 10 | 192 |
| C | 20 | 176 |
| D | 30 | 150 |
| E | 40 | 110 |
| F | 50 | 0 |

How to draw it:

1. Put one good on each axis and label both with the good and its units: machines (thousands) along the horizontal axis, consumer goods (thousands) up the vertical axis.
2. Use an even scale on each axis, starting at zero.
3. Plot A to F accurately.
4. Join them with a smooth curve, not straight segments, and label it PPC.

A rough sketch of the shape:

```
Consumer goods (thousands)
200 | A
    |    B
    |       C
150 |          D
    |
    |              E
    |
    |
  0 +-----------------F--- Machines (thousands)
    0    10   20   30   40   50
```

### Why the curve bows outwards

The curve is concave to the origin (it bends outwards) because resources are not equally good at producing both goods. When Talvia makes only a few machines, it uses the resources best suited to machines, giving up few consumer goods. As it makes more machines, it has to switch resources that are much better at making consumer goods, so each extra 10 thousand machines costs more consumer goods. This is **increasing opportunity cost**.

If resources were equally suited to both goods, the opportunity cost would be constant and the PPC would be a **straight line**.

## 1.4.2 Points under, on and beyond the curve

| Location | Example (machines, consumer goods) | Significance |
|---|---|---|
| On the curve | D (30, 150) | All resources fully and efficiently used; productively efficient |
| Under (inside) the curve | G (20, 140) | Some resources unemployed or used inefficiently; more of both goods is possible |
| Beyond (outside) the curve | H (30, 170) | Not attainable with current resources and technology |

At G, Talvia could move to C and gain 36 thousand consumer goods (176 − 140) without giving up any machines. A point inside the curve is often caused by unemployment, for example in a recession, or by resources being used in the wrong way.

Point H could become attainable only if the PPC shifts outwards (section 1.4.4).

## 1.4.3 Movements along the curve and opportunity cost

A movement along the curve means reallocating fully employed resources from one good to the other. The amount of one good given up is the opportunity cost of the extra units of the other.

**Worked example 2.** Talvia moves from C to D.

- Machines rise by 30 − 20 = 10 thousand.
- Consumer goods fall by 176 − 150 = 26 thousand.
- Opportunity cost of the extra 10 thousand machines = **26 thousand consumer goods**.
- Opportunity cost of 1 machine = 26 ÷ 10 = **2.6 consumer goods**.

**Worked example 3: increasing opportunity cost in the table.**

| Move | Extra machines (thousands) | Consumer goods given up (thousands) | Opportunity cost per machine |
|---|---|---|---|
| A to B | 10 | 8 | 0.8 |
| B to C | 10 | 16 | 1.6 |
| C to D | 10 | 26 | 2.6 |
| D to E | 10 | 40 | 4.0 |
| E to F | 10 | 110 | 11.0 |

The opportunity cost rises with every move, which is why the curve gets steeper towards F.

A movement from inside the curve to a point on it (G to C) is not a movement along the curve and has no opportunity cost in this sense: unused resources are brought into production.

## 1.4.4 Shifts of the curve: causes and consequences

### Outward shift

An outward shift of the whole PPC means the economy can produce more of both goods. Its **productive capacity** has increased. This represents economic growth in the sense of a rise in potential output.

Causes are increases in the quantity or quality of the factors of production (section 1.2), or better technology:

- More labour, for example from population growth or net immigration.
- Better-quality labour from education, training or healthcare.
- More capital from investment, or better capital from new technology.
- Discovery of new natural resources.
- Improvements in enterprise or in the way factors are organised.

### Inward shift

An inward shift means productive capacity has fallen. Causes include a natural disaster or war that destroys capital and land, large-scale emigration of workers, or the depletion of a natural resource.

### Shifts that affect one good only

If new technology raises productivity only in making machines, the curve pivots outwards along the machine axis. The maximum output of consumer goods (where machines are zero) does not change, but at every other point more is possible.

### Consequences: the capital goods choice

Where an economy chooses to produce on its curve affects its future growth.

- At B, Talvia makes 10 thousand machines and 192 thousand consumer goods. Living standards today are high, but little new capital is added.
- At D, it makes 30 thousand machines and 150 thousand consumer goods. People consume 42 thousand fewer consumer goods now, but the extra machines raise productive capacity, so the PPC shifts out further in later years.

The opportunity cost of faster future growth is consumption today. Consequences of an outward shift include the potential for higher output, incomes and living standards; whether they actually rise depends on resources being fully used, so the economy produces on the new curve rather than inside it.

## Common errors

- Unlabelled axes, or labelling them "goods" instead of naming the two goods.
- Drawing the PPC as straight lines between points when the data show increasing opportunity cost.
- Calling a move from inside the curve to the curve an outward shift.
- Saying a point beyond the curve is "inefficient". It is unattainable.
- Giving opportunity cost as a money value instead of an amount of the other good.
- Stating a cause of a shift without linking it to the factors of production.

## Exam technique

- **Define**: "the maximum combinations of two goods an economy can produce with all resources fully and efficiently used".
- **Draw**: label axes and the curve; plot accurately; mark and label any points the question names.
- **Calculate**: show the subtraction for each good, then divide.
- **Explain** a shift: cause → change in the quantity or quality of resources → change in productive capacity → curve shifts.
- **Analyse** a choice between points: what is gained now, what is given up, and the effect on future growth.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why is a PPC usually curved?**
Resources are not equally suited to producing both goods, so as more of one good is produced, increasingly unsuitable resources are switched to it and the opportunity cost rises.

**What is the difference between a movement along a PPC and a shift of a PPC?**
A movement along the curve reallocates fully used resources, with an opportunity cost. A shift changes the economy's productive capacity because the quantity or quality of resources, or technology, has changed.
