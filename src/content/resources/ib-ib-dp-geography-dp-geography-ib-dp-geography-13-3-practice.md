## Section A

**1.** Define resilience. **[1]**

**2.** Distinguish between absorptive and transformative capacity. **[2]**

**3.** Define the insurance protection gap. **[1]**

**4.** Outline one advantage and one disadvantage of index insurance. **[2]**

**5.** State two reasons why people may ignore a warning to evacuate. **[2]**

## Section B

**6.** A city compares two measures to reduce storm damage over 20 years.

| | Cost (million dollars) | Damage avoided per year (million dollars) |
|---|---|---|
| Measure X: sea wall | 60 | 4 |
| Measure Y: mangrove restoration | 10 | 1.5 |

**(a)** Calculate the total benefit of each measure over 20 years. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the benefit-cost ratio of each measure. **[2]**

**(c)** Suggest one benefit of Measure Y that the ratio does not capture. **[1]**

**7.** Explain why resilience is unevenly available within a country. **[3]**

**8.** Explain how national and global action can strengthen local resilience. **[3]**

**9.** Explain one limitation of relying on mobile phone alerts as an early-warning system. **[2]**

## Section C

**10.** Evaluate the view that local communities are the most important source of resilience to global risks. **[10]**

**11.** To what extent can technology make places resilient to global risks? **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** The capacity of a person, community or system to anticipate, absorb, adapt to and recover from shocks [1]. **[1]**

**2.** Absorptive capacity is the ability to cope with a shock using existing resources, such as savings or stores, so basic functions continue [1]; transformative capacity is the ability to change the underlying system so that the risk itself is reduced, such as relocating a settlement or reforming land rights [1]. **[2]**

**3.** The share of economic losses from disasters that is not covered by insurance [1]. **[1]**

**4.** Advantage: it pays out quickly and cheaply when a measurable trigger, such as low rainfall, is reached [1]. Disadvantage: a farmer may suffer losses but receive no payout if conditions on their land differ from the index [1]. **[2]**

**5.** They underestimate the danger because they came through earlier events unharmed [1]; they fear that their property or livestock will be stolen or lost if they leave [1]. (Also credit lack of trust in authorities or lack of transport.) **[2]**

**6. (a)** X: 4 x 20 = **80 million dollars** [1]. Y: 1.5 x 20 = **30 million dollars** [1].

**(b)** X: 80 / 60 = **1.33** [1]. Y: 30 / 10 = **3.0** [1].

**(c)** For example, mangroves also store carbon, support fisheries or provide habitat for wildlife [1]. **[5]**

**7.** Wealthier households can afford insurance, savings and stronger housing, so they recover faster [1]. Location matters: informal settlements are often on the most hazardous land [1], and groups such as older people, people with disabilities, informal workers and undocumented migrants may be excluded from warnings, transport or social protection [1]. **[3]**

**8.** National governments can fund infrastructure, set building standards and provide social protection that local communities cannot afford alone [1]. Global frameworks and finance, such as disaster risk reduction agreements and climate funds, channel money and expertise to vulnerable places [1], so local knowledge and organisation are backed by resources from higher scales [1]. **[3]**

**9.** Alerts reach only people with a phone, network signal and power [1], so the poorest or most remote people, who are often most vulnerable, may not receive them [1]. **[2]**

**10.** Indicative content; award one mark per valid, developed point up to 10:

- Defines resilience and identifies local, national and global scales [1].
- For: local knowledge identifies vulnerable people and safe places quickly [1].
- For: social networks and community organisations support absorptive capacity, as volunteer warning systems show [1].
- For: community-based measures fit local needs and are trusted [1].
- Against: local communities often lack funds for large infrastructure or insurance [1].
- Against: national governments set building codes, social protection and emergency services [1].
- Against: global finance, insurance pools and frameworks supply resources and coordination [1].
- Scales interact: national and global support makes local action effective [1].
- Uses accurate located evidence, such as Cuba's civil defence system [1].
- Conclusion: a supported judgement, for example that local action is essential for saving lives but depends on higher scales for long-term resilience [1]. **[10]**

**11.** Indicative content; award one mark per valid, developed point up to 10:

- Defines resilience and the types of technology involved [1].
- For: satellite monitoring tracks storms, droughts and crop conditions [1].
- For: early-warning systems and mobile alerts give people time to act [1].
- For: engineering, such as sea walls and earthquake-resistant buildings, absorbs shocks [1].
- For: data on exposure helps target investment [1].
- Against: technology depends on access, maintenance and funding [1].
- Against: warnings fail without trust, understanding and means to respond [1].
- Against: structures designed for expected events can be overwhelmed and may encourage risky development [1].
- Uses accurate located evidence [1].
- Conclusion: a supported judgement, for example that technology greatly strengthens resilience only where governance and community action are also strong [1]. **[10]**

*Examiner insight:* "To what extent" answers on technology are strongest when they explain the conditions that make technology work, not only its benefits.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Comparing total benefits instead of benefit-cost ratios, or the reverse, without saying which matters.
- Treating resilience as only physical infrastructure.
- Describing one scale only.
- Ignoring who is excluded from a resilience measure.
- Using resilience, adaptation and mitigation as if they meant the same thing, when each describes a different kind of response.
