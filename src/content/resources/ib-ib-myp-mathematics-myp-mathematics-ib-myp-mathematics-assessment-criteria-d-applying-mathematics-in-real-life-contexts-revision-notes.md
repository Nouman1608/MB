These notes condense Criterion D (applying mathematics in real-life contexts) from the IB *Mathematics guide for the MYP* (first assessment 2022). Full worked tasks are in the [Criterion D study guide](/resources/ib-ib-myp-mathematics-myp-mathematics-ib-myp-mathematics-assessment-criteria-d-applying-mathematics-in-real-life-contexts/); practise with the [Criterion D practice questions](/resources/ib-ib-myp-mathematics-myp-mathematics-ib-myp-mathematics-assessment-criteria-d-applying-mathematics-in-real-life-contexts-practice/). ## The criterion in one table

| Strand | Year 5 wording, in short | What to write |
|---|---|---|
| i | Identify relevant elements of authentic real-life situations | What matters, what does not, what you assume |
| ii | Select appropriate mathematical strategies | The model and why it fits |
| iii | Apply the strategies successfully to reach a solution | Correct working with units |
| iv | Justify the degree of accuracy | How accurate, and the evidence for it |
| v | Justify whether the solution makes sense | A comparison with reality and a limitation |

Maximum 8. All five strands apply in Years 1, 3 and 5. The verbs in strands iv and v rise from describe or explain (Years 1 and 3) to justify (Year 5).

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Authentic situation | One that could genuinely happen, with real decisions to make |
| Relevant element | Information that affects the answer |
| Assumption | Something taken as true to make the problem solvable |
| Model | The mathematics chosen to represent the situation |
| Break-even point | The value at which two options cost the same |
| Degree of accuracy | How precisely an answer is given and can be trusted |
| Bounds | The smallest and largest values a measurement could have |
| Limitation | A way the model differs from reality |
| Domain of a model | The range of inputs for which it is realistic |

## Matching situations to strategies

| Situation | Strategy |
|---|---|
| Fixed charge plus a rate | Linear model; compare totals; find the break-even point |
| Repeated percentage growth or decay | Exponential model with a multiplier |
| Covering, filling, wrapping | Area, volume, surface area; then units and packs |
| Heights and distances you cannot measure directly | Trigonometry, Pythagoras' theorem |
| Best buy | Unit price on the same basis |
| Speed, rates, time | distance = speed × time, with matching units |
| Variation in data | Mean, median, range, graphs |

## Accuracy checklist

- Round in the way the context needs: money to the cent, counts to whole numbers.
- Round up for things you must have enough of (buses, tins, packs).
- Round down for complete things you can make or finish (full days, whole items).
- Never give more significant figures than the least accurate data.
- To *justify* accuracy, use the bounds of the measurements and show the range of answers.

Worked reminder: an angle of 38° to the nearest degree lies between 37.5° and 38.5°. From 15 m away with eye height 1.6 m, the height lies between 13.1 m and 13.5 m, so 13 m (2 s.f.) is justified.

Worked reminder: a length of 4.2 m to the nearest 0.1 m lies between 4.15 m and 4.25 m.

## Making sense: three checks

1. **Size**: is the value believable for the situation?
2. **Form**: whole number, positive, sensible units?
3. **Model limits**: which assumptions might fail, and how would that change the answer?

Worked reminder: a seedling 8 cm tall grows 2.5 cm a week. The linear model predicts 138 cm after a year, but growth rarely stays constant, so the model should be limited to the period over which steady growth was measured.

## Exam traps

- Using every number given, including irrelevant ones.
- Comparing options for different amounts or periods.
- Writing "3.37 buses".
- Dividing area by tile area when cut tiles cannot be reused.
- Giving 13.32 m when the angle was only measured to the nearest degree.
- "It makes sense because it is a reasonable answer", which repeats the claim without evidence.
- Forgetting standing or fixed charges when comparing tariffs.
- Mixing units, such as minutes and hours in a speed.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Explain vs justify**: explain gives reasons; justify gives reasons backed by evidence, such as calculated bounds.
- **Accurate vs precise-looking**: many decimal places do not make an answer more accurate than its data.
- **Assumption vs limitation**: an assumption is what you take as true; a limitation is the effect if it is not.
- **Strategy vs calculation**: strand ii is the choice of model; strand iii is carrying it out.

## Quick self-test

1. List the five strands of Criterion D.
2. Which strands change from "explain" in Year 3 to "justify" in Year 5?
3. 230 people need minibuses that seat 16. How many minibuses?
4. Plan P costs 12 plus 0.08 per minute; Plan Q costs 20 plus 0.04 per minute. Find the break-even number of minutes.
5. Which plan in question 4 is cheaper for 150 minutes?
6. A 500 g pack costs 3.75 and a 750 g pack costs 5.40. Which is better value?
7. A length is 6.4 m to the nearest 0.1 m. State its bounds.
8. 1500 grows at 4% compound per year. Write the amount after n years.
9. State one assumption in question 8.
10. A runner covers 400 m in 64 s. Why is predicting a 10 km time from this unreliable?
11. Give one size check you could use on the cost of painting a room.
12. What does a break-even point tell you when making a recommendation?

### Answers

1. Identify relevant elements; select strategies; apply them; justify accuracy; justify whether the solution makes sense.
2. Strands **iv and v**.
3. 230 ÷ 16 = 14.375, so **15 minibuses**.
4. 12 + 0.08m = 20 + 0.04m gives 0.04m = 8, so **m = 200 minutes**.
5. P: 12 + 12 = 24; Q: 20 + 6 = 26, so **Plan P**.
6. 0.75 per 100 g and 0.72 per 100 g, so the **750 g pack**.
7. **6.35 m ≤ length < 6.45 m**.
8. **1500 × 1.04ⁿ**.
9. For example, the rate stays at 4% and no money is withdrawn.
10. A runner cannot hold a 400 m pace over 10 km, so the model of constant speed fails.
11. For example, compare with the cost of a few tins of paint; hundreds of tins would signal an error.
12. The range of values over which each option is better, so the recommendation can state when it would change.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I have to state assumptions in Criterion D?**
Stating them is the clearest evidence for strand i, and they let you judge the solution in strand v. A solution with no assumptions is hard to evaluate.

**Is one limitation enough when judging a solution?**
One well-explained limitation, linked to how it affects the answer, is stronger than a list of vague ones. In Year 5, support it with evidence where you can.
