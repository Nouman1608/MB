Condensed recall for **7.1 Tropical climates**, the first section of Topic 7, Tropical environments, in Paper 3 Advanced Physical Geography Options. For full explanations and a worked climate-data example, read the [7.1 study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-tropical-climates/); then test yourself with the [7.1 practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-tropical-climates-practice/).

The syllabus asks for the distribution and characteristics of humid tropical and seasonally humid tropical climates, the roles of the ITCZ, subtropical anticyclones and monsoons, and the annual and diurnal variations of temperature and rainfall.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Humid tropical climate | Hot all year with rain in every month; supports rainforest |
| Seasonally humid tropical climate | Hot all year with a wet season and a dry season; supports savanna |
| Hadley cell | Tropical circulation: rising air near the equator, poleward flow aloft, sinking near 30°, trade winds returning at the surface |
| ITCZ | Intertropical convergence zone: low-pressure belt where the trade winds meet and air rises |
| Doldrums | Old name for the calm, variable winds of the ITCZ |
| Trade winds | Surface winds blowing towards the ITCZ, from the north-east (northern hemisphere) and south-east (southern hemisphere) |
| Coriolis effect | Deflection of moving air by the Earth's rotation: to the right in the northern hemisphere, left in the southern |
| Subtropical anticyclone | High-pressure cell near 30°N/S formed by sinking, warming, drying air |
| Temperature inversion | Layer where temperature rises with height, capping convection |
| Monsoon | Seasonal reversal of wind direction |
| Orographic rain | Rain caused by air forced to rise over high ground |
| Harmattan | Dry, dusty north-easterly wind from the Sahara over West Africa in the dry season |
| Annual range | Warmest monthly mean minus coolest monthly mean |
| Diurnal range | Daily maximum minus daily minimum temperature |
| Af / Am / Aw | Köppen classes: rainforest, monsoon and savanna climates |

## The circulation in four steps

1. Intense heating near the equator makes air rise, cool adiabatically and condense: heavy convective rain, low pressure.
2. Air spreads polewards near the top of the troposphere.
3. Air sinks near 30°N and 30°S, warms adiabatically and dries: subtropical highs, clear skies.
4. Trade winds return towards the equator and converge at the ITCZ, deflected by the Coriolis effect.

## ITCZ movement: what to remember

- Follows the overhead sun between the tropics, but with a lag of about one to two months.
- Moves much further over land than over the ocean, because land heats and cools faster.
- Lies slightly north of the equator on average, because the northern hemisphere has more land.
- Near the equator: under the ITCZ most of the year, rain in every month, often two peaks near the equinoxes.
- Towards the tropics: one wet season in the high-sun months, a long dry season under the subtropical high. The wet season is shorter and less reliable with distance from the equator.

## The South Asian monsoon in brief

| | Summer (wet) | Winter (dry) |
|---|---|---|
| Land temperature | Much hotter than the ocean; Tibetan Plateau heats the air above it | Cooler than the ocean |
| Pressure over land | Low | High over central Asia |
| ITCZ | Pulled far north | Well south |
| Winds | South-westerly, onshore and moist | North-easterly, offshore and dry |
| Jet stream | Subtropical westerly jet moves north of the Himalayas | Jet lies south of the Himalayas |
| Rain | Heavy, especially orographic rain on the Western Ghats and Himalayan foothills | Little over most of India |

Onset in Kerala, south-west India, is normally around 1 June. Monsoons also affect West Africa and northern Australia.

## Annual and diurnal patterns

**Humid tropical**

- Annual range very small, often only 1-3 °C.
- Diurnal range larger than the annual range.
- Daily cycle: clear morning, heating, cumulus by late morning, afternoon thunderstorms, clearer evening.
- Cloud and humidity limit night-time cooling.

**Seasonally humid tropical**

- Larger annual range.
- Hottest month just before the rains: high sun, clear skies, dry ground.
- Diurnal range greatest in the dry season (clear skies) and smallest in the wet season (cloud).
- Rainfall varies more from year to year: droughts and late onsets.

## Reading a climate table: checklist

| Check | What it tells you |
|---|---|
| Coolest month at least 18 °C? | It is a tropical (A) climate |
| Every month 60 mm or more? | Af, no dry season |
| One wet season, several near-dry months? | Aw, savanna |
| Two rainfall peaks? | ITCZ passes overhead twice; usually near the equator |
| Hottest month before the wettest? | Seasonally humid; cloud and evaporation cool the wet season |
| Annual range under 3 °C? | Humid tropical |

## Exam traps

- The ITCZ does not track the sun exactly; give the lag and the land-ocean difference.
- Savannas do not have a cold winter. Their seasons are wet and dry.
- Trade winds blow *towards* the ITCZ, not away from it.
- Sinking air in a subtropical high warms and dries; it does not cool.
- A monsoon is a wind reversal, not simply "heavy rain".
- When describing data, quote ranges and totals with units and name the wettest and driest months.
- Do not use the diurnal range to describe seasons, or the annual range to describe a day.

## Self-test

1. What is the minimum mean temperature of the coolest month in a Köppen tropical climate?
2. Why does air sink near 30°N and 30°S?
3. From which direction do the trade winds blow in the southern hemisphere?
4. Why does the ITCZ move further north over South Asia than over the eastern Pacific?
5. Why do some equatorial stations have two rainfall peaks?
6. What caps convection in a subtropical anticyclone?
7. Name the dry wind that blows from the Sahara over West Africa in the dry season.
8. Give two reasons why the summer monsoon brings heavy rain to the Western Ghats.
9. Why is the diurnal range larger than the annual range in humid tropical areas?
10. Why is the hottest month in many savanna areas before the wet season?
11. A station has a warmest month of 31.5 °C and a coolest month of 23.0 °C. What is its annual range?
12. Why is rainfall less reliable towards the poleward edge of the savanna?

### Answers

1. 18 °C.
2. Air flowing polewards aloft cools by radiating to space and converges, so it sinks; it then warms adiabatically.
3. From the south-east.
4. Land heats much faster than the ocean, so the zone of greatest heating, and the low pressure, moves further over the continent.
5. The ITCZ passes overhead twice a year, near the March and September equinoxes.
6. A temperature inversion formed by warming, sinking air.
7. The harmattan.
8. Moist south-westerly winds blow onshore from the ocean, and the mountains force the air to rise, giving orographic rain.
9. Day length and sun angle change little through the year, but every day has strong heating and night-time cooling.
10. The sun is high and skies are clear before the ITCZ arrives; once rains begin, cloud and evaporation lower temperatures.
11. 31.5 − 23.0 = 8.5 °C.
12. The ITCZ reaches these places only briefly and not every year by the same amount, so the length and amount of the wet season vary.
