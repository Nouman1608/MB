This guide covers content item 1.5.1, Business stakeholders, in Topic 1.5 (Understanding external influences on business) of the Pearson Edexcel Level 1/Level 2 GCSE (9-1) in Business (1BS0) specification, Issue 2, July 2022. It belongs to Theme 1, examined in Paper 1. The broader [guide to stakeholders and technology](/resources/edexcel-gcse-business-stakeholders-and-technology/) sets out each group's objectives in a table. This page goes further into the skill examiners test most: tracing one business decision through to every group it touches, and judging how a business should handle the conflicts that follow. All businesses and figures are invented.

## What 1.5.1 contains

| Heading in the specification | Points listed |
|---|---|
| Who business stakeholders are and their different objectives | Shareholders (owners), employees, customers, managers, suppliers, local community, pressure groups, the government |
| Stakeholders and businesses | How stakeholders are affected by business activity; how stakeholders impact business activity; possible conflicts between stakeholder groups |

A stakeholder is any individual or group with an interest in, or affected by, the activities of a business. The specification lists eight groups; learn all eight by name, because a multiple-choice or Give question may ask you to pick them out.

## Stakeholder or shareholder?

These words look alike but mean different things.

- A **shareholder** owns part of a company. Shareholders are one stakeholder group.
- A **stakeholder** is anyone with an interest in the business, including shareholders.

So every shareholder is a stakeholder, but most stakeholders are not shareholders. In a sole trader business the "shareholder (owner)" group is simply the owner.

## Inside and outside the business

A useful way to organise the eight groups (standard textbook practice, not wording from the specification) is by where they sit.

| Internal (within the business) | External (outside the business) |
|---|---|
| Shareholders (owners), managers, employees | Customers, suppliers, local community, pressure groups, the government |

Internal groups usually have more direct influence over day-to-day decisions. External groups influence the business through what they buy, sell, allow or campaign for.

## Objectives in more depth: why groups want different things

The specification asks for the groups' "different objectives". The reason they differ is that each group gains from the business in a different way.

- **Shareholders** gain from profit and from a rise in the value of the business. Anything that raises costs without raising revenue works against them in the short run.
- **Employees** gain from wages, security and conditions, which are costs to the business.
- **Customers** gain from low prices and high quality, which squeeze profit if costs do not fall.
- **Managers** gain from pay, status and success, and may favour growth because a bigger business brings bigger roles.
- **Suppliers** gain from large, regular orders at good prices, paid on time.
- **The local community** gains from jobs and local spending, but bears noise, traffic and pollution.
- **Pressure groups** gain when the business changes its behaviour on the issue they campaign about.
- **The government** gains from taxes, employment and businesses that follow the law.

Because many of these gains come from the same pool of revenue, conflict is built in.

## Tracing one decision: affected by, and impact on

The specification separates two directions:

- **Affected by business activity:** what the decision does *to* each group.
- **Impact on business activity:** what each group can do *back* to the business.

### Worked example: Harlow Cross Garden Centre

Harlow Cross Garden Centre plans to open a café and stay open until 9pm on weekdays. It expects extra revenue of £120,000 a year, will hire 6 staff, and expects extra costs of £96,000.

1. Extra profit = £120,000 − £96,000 = £24,000 a year
2. If the business currently makes £80,000, profit rises by £24,000 ÷ £80,000 × 100 = 30%

| Group | How it is affected | How it could impact the plan |
|---|---|---|
| Shareholders | Profit forecast to rise by 30% | Approve the investment or refuse to fund it |
| Employees | More shifts available; later finishing times | Agree to evening shifts, or refuse and leave |
| Customers | Somewhere to eat; can shop after work | Use the café, or not, which decides whether revenue reaches £120,000 |
| Managers | More staff to manage; a bigger role | Plan the rotas and opening carefully, or poorly |
| Suppliers | New orders for food and drink | Offer good prices, or deliver late |
| Local community | 6 new jobs; more evening traffic | Complain to the council about traffic and lights |
| Pressure groups | A local environmental group may object to food waste | Run a campaign that harms the centre's image |
| Government | More tax from profits and wages | The local council could refuse permission or set conditions on opening hours |

The table shows why a stakeholder answer is rarely one-sided: the same decision helps some groups and harms others.

## Conflicts between stakeholder groups

A conflict occurs when meeting one group's objective makes it harder to meet another's. Common pairs:

- **Shareholders v employees:** higher wages reduce profit.
- **Customers v shareholders:** lower prices reduce profit per sale.
- **Local community v managers:** expansion brings growth but also disturbance.
- **Suppliers v the business:** higher supplier prices raise costs.

### How businesses deal with conflict

A business cannot satisfy every group fully, so it must decide. Typical approaches:

- **Prioritise** the group with most influence or most at stake in that decision. A small business close to failure may put survival, and so shareholders and customers, first.
- **Compromise:** for Harlow Cross, closing at 8pm rather than 9pm might satisfy neighbours while keeping most of the extra trade.
- **Communicate:** explaining a decision early, for example meeting residents, can reduce opposition.
- **Share the gain:** part of the extra profit could fund a pay rise for evening staff, aligning employees with shareholders.

Which approach is best depends on how powerful each group is and how much the decision matters to them. Saying so is the route to the top level in Evaluate answers.

## Annotated model answer

**Question (Explain style):** Explain **one** possible conflict between shareholders and customers. [3]

> Shareholders want high profits *(statement of fact)*. One way to raise profit is to increase prices *(expansion 1)*, but customers want low prices, so a price rise that benefits shareholders means customers pay more and may feel the product is poorer value *(expansion 2)*.

Explain questions have no context, so a general example is fine. The answer gives a statement and two linked expansions.

## Common mistakes

- Confusing stakeholders with shareholders.
- Listing groups affected without saying *how* they are affected.
- Writing about only one direction when the question asks how stakeholders impact the business.
- Saying a business should "keep everyone happy". Conflicts mean it usually cannot.
- Forgetting pressure groups and the government, which are easy marks in Give questions.

## Exam technique

- **Give:** name groups or objectives; no explanation is needed.
- **Explain:** one statement plus two expansions; no context.
- **Analyse:** take the decision in the source and trace its effect on named groups.
- **Evaluate:** decide which group's interests should come first, or whether a decision is worth the conflict it causes, and support the conclusion.
