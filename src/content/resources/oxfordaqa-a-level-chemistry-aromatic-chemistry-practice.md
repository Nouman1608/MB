## Questions

**1.** Which conditions convert methylbenzene into (chloromethyl)benzene?

- A chlorine with aluminium chloride, in the dark
- B chlorine with ultraviolet light
- C hydrogen chloride at room temperature
- D aqueous chlorine with sodium hydroxide

**[1]**

**2.** A C–C single bond is about 154 pm long and a C=C double bond about 134 pm. All six carbon–carbon bonds in benzene are about 139 pm. Explain how these data support a delocalised model of benzene rather than cyclohexa-1,3,5-triene. **[3]**

**3.** One textbook gives these enthalpies of hydrogenation, each forming cyclohexane: cyclohexa-1,3-diene (two C=C bonds), −230 kJ mol⁻¹; benzene, −206 kJ mol⁻¹.

**(a)** Use the diene value to estimate ΔH for hydrogenating one C=C bond. **[1]**
**(b)** Predict ΔH for hydrogenating cyclohexa-1,3,5-triene. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate how much more stable benzene is than the triene, using these data. **[2]**
**(d)** Calculate the heat released when 1.95 g of benzene is fully hydrogenated. **[2]**

**4.** In the nitration of benzene, the positive intermediate loses H⁺. It could instead, in principle, gain a hydrogensulfate ion and form an addition product. Explain why the H⁺ is lost instead. **[3]**

**5.** Methylbenzene is nitrated with a mixture of concentrated nitric and sulfuric acids.

**(a)** Give an equation showing how the electrophile forms. **[1]**
**(b)** Draw an outline mechanism to show how 4-nitromethylbenzene forms. **[3]**
**(c)** Name the other main organic product. **[1]**
**(d)** 4.60 g of methylbenzene gives 5.48 g of a mixture of mononitro isomers, C₇H₇NO₂. Calculate the percentage yield of mononitro product. **[3]**

**6.** Benzene is warmed with fuming sulfuric acid.

**(a)** Identify the electrophile and explain why it acts as an electrophile. **[2]**
**(b)** Using curly arrows, outline how benzene and this electrophile react to give benzenesulfonic acid. **[3]**
**(c)** Name the main product when methylbenzene is sulfonated. **[1]**

**7.** Give one reason why nitration and one reason why sulfonation are important steps in synthesis. **[2]**

**8.** Benzene is heated with 2-chloro-2-methylpropane, (CH₃)₃CCl, and aluminium chloride.

**(a)** Give an equation showing how the carbocation forms. **[1]**
**(b)** Name the organic product. **[1]**
**(c)** Show, with an equation, that aluminium chloride is re-formed. **[1]**
**(d)** Calculate the mass of product made from 5.46 g of benzene if the yield is 85.0%. **[3]**
**(e)** Suggest why benzene is used in excess. **[1]**

**9.** A chemist wants to make 2-methyl-1-phenylpropan-1-one, C₆H₅COCH(CH₃)₂, in one step from benzene.

**(a)** Name the organic reagent needed. **[1]**
**(b)** Give the formula of the electrophile. **[1]**
**(c)** Suggest why the aluminium chloride must be kept dry. **[1]**
**(d)** State the functional group in the product. **[1]**

**10.** Chlorine reacts with methylbenzene by a free-radical mechanism.

**(a)** State the condition needed. **[1]**
**(b)** Write the equation for the initiation step. **[1]**
**(c)** Write the two propagation steps that form (chloromethyl)benzene. **[2]**
**(d)** A termination step forms a hydrocarbon, C₁₄H₁₄. Write its structural formula. **[1]**
**(e)** With more chlorine, a product with Mr = 161.0 forms. Identify it. **[1]**

**11.** Benzene reacts with chlorine in ultraviolet light.

**(a)** Give the balanced equation. **[1]**
**(b)** Name the type of reaction. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the percentage by mass of chlorine in the organic product. **[2]**

**12.** Samples of chlorobenzene and (chloromethyl)benzene are each warmed with aqueous sodium hydroxide. Each mixture is then made acidic with dilute HNO₃ before aqueous AgNO₃ is added.

**(a)** State what is seen for each compound. **[2]**
**(b)** Write an equation for the reaction of (chloromethyl)benzene with hydroxide ions. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain the difference in reactivity of the chlorine in the two compounds. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** **B** [1] **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Option A puts chlorine on the ring because a catalyst in the dark gives electrophilic attack, so read the conditions, not just the reagent.

**2.** The triene would have three shorter C=C and three longer C–C bonds, so two different lengths [1]. All benzene's bonds are equal, so every bond shares the same delocalised p electrons [1]. Their length lies between single and double, so each bond has more electron density than a single bond but less than a double bond [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Each point should use the data: an answer that only says "benzene is delocalised" without comparing the lengths does not explain anything.

**3. (a)** −230 / 2 = **−115 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]
**(b)** 3 × (−115) = **−345 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]
**(c)** −206 − (−345) = +139, so benzene releases 139 kJ mol⁻¹ less energy [1]; benzene is **139 kJ mol⁻¹ more stable** [1].
**(d)** n = 1.95 / 78.0 = 0.0250 mol [1]; 0.0250 × 206 = **5.15 kJ** [1] **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* In (c), the final mark needs the direction stated: say benzene is the more stable compound, not just "a difference of 139".

**4.** Losing H⁺ restores the full delocalised ring of p electrons [1]. An addition product would not have a complete delocalised ring [1]. So it would lose the extra stability from delocalisation, making substitution energetically favoured [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Link each step: delocalisation, then stability, then the choice of substitution. "Benzene doesn't do addition" restates the question.

**5. (a)** **HNO₃ + 2H₂SO₄ → NO₂⁺ + H₃O⁺ + 2HSO₄⁻** [1]
**(b)** Arrow from the ring to the N of NO₂⁺, attacking the carbon opposite CH₃ [1]. Intermediate: that carbon carries H and NO₂, with a partial ring (horseshoe) across the remaining five carbons and + in its centre [1]. The C–H bond electrons move back into the ring (arrow), releasing H⁺ and forming 4-nitromethylbenzene [1].
**(c)** **2-nitromethylbenzene** (1-methyl-2-nitrobenzene) [1]
**(d)** n(C₇H₈) = 4.60 / 92.0 = 0.0500 mol [1]. Mr(C₇H₇NO₂) = 137.0; theoretical mass = 0.0500 × 137.0 = 6.85 g [1]. Yield = 5.48 / 6.85 × 100 = **80.0%** [1] **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* In (b) the NO₂ must be drawn opposite the methyl group; a correct mechanism at the wrong position does not give the named product.

**6. (a)** **Sulfur trioxide, SO₃** [1]. The three oxygen atoms withdraw electron density, so the sulfur atom is strongly δ+ and accepts an electron pair [1].
**(b)** Arrow from the ring to the S of SO₃ [1]. Intermediate: attacked carbon carries H and SO₃⁻, horseshoe with + inside [1]. Arrow from the C–H bond into the ring; H⁺ is lost and the SO₃⁻ group is protonated to give C₆H₅SO₃H [1].
**(c)** **4-methylbenzenesulfonic acid** [1] **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* SO₃ is neutral; giving it a positive charge, or pointing the arrow at an oxygen, costs the relevant mark.

**7.** Nitration: manufacture of explosives, or forming amines by reducing the nitro group [1]. Sulfonation: manufacture of surfactants (detergents) or of sulfonamides [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Each mark is for a use matched to the right reaction; listing uses with no link to a reaction gives no credit.

**8. (a)** **(CH₃)₃CCl + AlCl₃ → (CH₃)₃C⁺ + AlCl₄⁻** [1]
**(b)** **(1,1-dimethylethyl)benzene**, C₆H₅C(CH₃)₃ [1]
**(c)** **H⁺ + AlCl₄⁻ → AlCl₃ + HCl** [1]
**(d)** n(benzene) = 5.46 / 78.0 = 0.0700 mol [1]. Mr(C₁₀H₁₄) = 134.0; theoretical mass = 9.38 g [1]. At 85.0%: 9.38 × 0.850 = **7.97 g** [1].
**(e)** The alkyl group makes the product more reactive than benzene, so excess benzene makes further substitution less likely [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* An electrophile equation must balance for charge; the + on the carbocation and the − on AlCl₄⁻ must both appear.

**9. (a)** **2-methylpropanoyl chloride**, (CH₃)₂CHCOCl [1]
**(b)** **(CH₃)₂CHCO⁺** [1]
**(c)** Water would react with (hydrolyse) AlCl₃ and remove the catalyst [1].
**(d)** **Ketone** (carbonyl group between the ring and the alkyl group) [1] **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* For (a), work backwards: the C=O carbon joined to the ring comes from the acyl chloride, so all four carbons of the side chain must be in the reagent.

**10. (a)** **Ultraviolet light** [1]
**(b)** **Cl₂ → 2Cl•** [1]
**(c)** C₆H₅CH₃ + Cl• → C₆H₅CH₂• + HCl [1]; C₆H₅CH₂• + Cl₂ → C₆H₅CH₂Cl + Cl• [1]
**(d)** **C₆H₅CH₂CH₂C₆H₅** [1]
**(e)** **(Dichloromethyl)benzene**, C₆H₅CHCl₂ [1] **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Propagation steps lose credit if the radical dot is on the wrong atom or if H• appears instead of HCl.

**11. (a)** **C₆H₆ + 3Cl₂ → C₆H₆Cl₆** [1]
**(b)** **Free-radical addition** [1]
**(c)** Mr(C₆H₆Cl₆) = 291.0 [1]; 213.0 / 291.0 × 100 = **73.2%** [1] **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Writing C₆H₅Cl here shows a mix-up with ring substitution; the specification describes ring addition for benzene.

**12. (a)** (Chloromethyl)benzene: white precipitate [1]. Chlorobenzene: no precipitate [1].
**(b)** **C₆H₅CH₂Cl + OH⁻ → C₆H₅CH₂OH + Cl⁻** [1]
**(c)** In (chloromethyl)benzene the Cl is on an sp³ side-chain carbon that is δ+, so the hydroxide nucleophile substitutes it and releases Cl⁻ [1]. In chlorobenzene one chlorine lone pair becomes partly delocalised into the ring's p-electron system [1]. So the C–Cl bond is strengthened (partial π character) and harder to break [1]. The delocalised electron density also repels the approaching nucleophile [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* Each explanation point should be tied to the compound it describes; one line on each compound earns less than a full comparison.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Quoting a stability figure without saying benzene is the more stable compound.
- Starting the first arrow on a ring carbon instead of the delocalised ring.
- Drawing the intermediate without the + or with a full circle.
- Giving 3-nitromethylbenzene as a main product from methylbenzene.
- Treating SO₃ as a positive ion, or arrowing to its oxygen.
- Mixing up conditions: AlCl₃ for ring reactions, UV light for radical reactions.
- Writing chlorobenzene for the product of benzene with chlorine in UV light.
- Leaving HCl out of Friedel–Crafts equations.
- Explaining chlorobenzene's low reactivity without the lone pair overlapping with the ring.
