This guide covers **3.2.2 Section B: Resource security** of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Geography (9635) specification, Version 3.2. It is assessed in Section B of the Unit 2 exam (40 marks, all questions compulsory). The specification divides it into 3.2.2.1 resource development, 3.2.2.2 natural resource issues, 3.2.2.3 water security, 3.2.2.4 energy security, 3.2.2.5 resource futures and 3.2.2.6 case studies. You must engage with both water and energy, though you may concentrate on one of them in your case studies.

The [resource security revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-geography-9635-resource-security-revision-notes/) give the definitions and the main examples. This guide develops the reasoning behind them -- how reserves are classified and why they change, what an environmental impact assessment involves, how physical geography controls supply and cost, and how to write evaluative answers on conflict and futures. Practise with the [resource security practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-geography-9635-resource-security-practice/).

## What makes something a resource

A resource is any part of the environment that people value and can use. The economist Erich Zimmermann summed this up as "resources are not, they become": oil was not a resource until technology and demand gave it value. This matters because it explains why reserves grow, why resource frontiers move, and why a resource can lose value when a substitute appears.

The specification's main classification is **stock** (finite on human timescales, such as fossil fuels, uranium and fossil groundwater) against **flow** (renewed continuously, such as sunlight, wind and river flow). Some flow resources become stock-like when overused: an aquifer pumped faster than it recharges behaves as a finite store.

## Evaluating stock resources

The specification lists four categories: **measured reserves, indicated reserves, inferred resources and possible resources**. These are often shown on a **McKelvey box**, which has two axes:

- **Geological certainty** -- how confidently the quantity is known, from measured (high) through indicated and inferred to possible (low).
- **Economic feasibility** -- whether extraction is profitable at current prices and technology.

Only material that is both well known and economic counts as a reserve. When prices rise or technology improves, material moves across the economic boundary and becomes a reserve without any new discovery.

**Worked example (invented figures):** a country has proven oil reserves of 2,400 million barrels and produces 120 million barrels a year.

> Reserves-to-production ratio = 2,400 / 120 = **20 years**.

This does *not* mean the oil runs out in 20 years. Reserves are revised as exploration continues and prices change, and production rates change too. The ratio is a snapshot of the present balance between known supply and output.

**Development over time** follows exploration, exploitation and development. The **resource frontier** moves into harder places -- deep-water fields off Brazil, the Arctic, Canada's oil sands -- as technology and prices allow. **Resource peak** is the point at which output reaches a maximum before declining; the date depends as much on economics and demand as on geology.

## Environmental impact assessment

The specification links **sustainable resource development** with **environmental impact assessment (EIA)**. A typical EIA works in stages:

1. **Screening** -- deciding whether the project needs a full assessment.
2. **Scoping** -- identifying the impacts that matter most, such as on water, ecosystems or communities.
3. **Baseline study** -- recording conditions before the project.
4. **Prediction** -- estimating the size and duration of impacts.
5. **Mitigation** -- proposing ways to avoid or reduce harm.
6. **Consultation and decision** -- involving the public and authorities before approval.
7. **Monitoring** -- checking impacts once the project runs.

Evaluate EIA as a tool: it improves decisions only if it is independent, if consultation genuinely includes affected groups and if monitoring is enforced.

## Physical geography and supply

For both water and energy, the specification asks how **climate, geology and drainage** affect supply in volume and quality.

| Control | Water supply | Energy supply |
|---|---|---|
| **Climate** | Rainfall amount, reliability and evaporation set surface supply | Sunshine, wind and river regimes set renewable potential; temperature shapes demand |
| **Geology** | Permeable rocks form aquifers; impermeable rocks favour reservoirs; some rocks affect water quality | Sedimentary basins hold oil, gas and coal; geothermal energy needs heat close to the surface |
| **Drainage** | River basins collect water and define where transfers are needed | Steep, high-discharge rivers give hydro-electric potential |

Iceland shows how one physical setting can lower energy costs: active volcanism gives abundant geothermal energy, and glacial rivers give hydro-electric power, so almost all its electricity comes from renewable sources.

## Major schemes and their impacts

**Water -- the Aswan High Dam, Egypt (completed 1970):** it created Lake Nasser, stores water across years to irrigate farmland and generates hydro-electricity. But it traps the Nile's sediment, so the delta has lost its natural supply and parts of the coast have eroded; farmers rely more on artificial fertiliser; and Nubian communities were displaced when the reservoir filled.

**Energy -- the Alberta oil sands, Canada:** extraction by open-pit mining and in-situ steam methods has cleared boreal forest, created large tailings ponds and uses large volumes of water from the Athabasca River. Pipelines carrying the oil to markets have been contested on environmental and Indigenous rights grounds; the Keystone XL extension was cancelled in 2021.

## Energy in a globalising world

The specification asks about **competing national interests** and **transnational corporations** in production, processing and distribution. State-owned companies such as Saudi Aramco and private corporations such as Shell and ExxonMobil control exploration, refining and shipping across many countries. Supply passes through **chokepoints** such as the Strait of Hormuz, so disruption in one place affects prices everywhere. Water geopolitics differs because rivers cross borders: the **Indus Waters Treaty** of 1960, brokered by the World Bank, divided the Indus rivers between India and Pakistan, while the Nile has no comparable agreement covering all riparian states.

## Sustainability issues

- **Acid rain** comes from sulphur dioxide and nitrogen oxides released by burning fossil fuels.
- The **enhanced greenhouse effect** links energy use to climate change.
- **Nuclear waste** needs long-term isolation; Finland is building a deep geological repository at Onkalo.
- **Energy conservation** and water strategies such as **virtual water trade, recycling and grey water** reduce demand rather than expanding supply.

## Resource futures and case studies

Futures depend on **technological, economic, environmental and political** developments. The specification singles out **desalination** as the link between water and energy: it needs large amounts of energy, so its price and carbon footprint depend on how that energy is produced.

The **first case study** examines water *or* energy issues in a global or regional setting, including the relationship between resource security and **human welfare**. The **second** examines a specified place where the **physical environment affects the availability and cost** of water or energy and how it is used -- for example a desert city relying on desalination, or a mountain region using hydro-electric power.

## Worked example: an annotated answer

*Question: Assess the view that water scarcity inevitably leads to conflict.* **[9]**

> Shared rivers can create tension where upstream states control supply, as with the Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam on the Blue Nile, which has caused long-running disputes with Egypt and Sudan over how fast its reservoir is filled **(evidence for)**. However, scarcity also drives cooperation: the Indus Waters Treaty has survived since 1960 despite wider hostility between India and Pakistan **(counter-evidence)**. Within countries, conflict is often local, between farmers and growing cities, and is usually resolved through pricing or allocation rules rather than violence **(scale)**. Technology such as desalination and virtual water trade can reduce pressure **(alternative outcome)**. Scarcity therefore raises the risk of conflict, but institutions and technology decide whether that risk becomes conflict **(judgement)**.

## Exam technique

- **Explain:** give a mechanism, for example how rising prices move resources into reserves.
- **Assess / evaluate:** weigh named evidence on both sides, consider scale and timescale, and reach a judgement.
- **Resource items:** quote data from maps and tables before interpreting them.

## Common mistakes

- Treating reserves or reserves-to-production ratios as fixed countdowns.
- Describing EIA as a single report rather than a process.
- Writing generally about "water" or "energy" with no named scheme or place.
- Assuming scarcity always leads to conflict.
- Ignoring the energy cost of desalination when discussing water futures.
