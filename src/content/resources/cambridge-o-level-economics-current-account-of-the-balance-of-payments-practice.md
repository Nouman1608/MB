## Questions

**1.** State which part of the current account records each of these for Country D: **(a)** a foreign tourist pays for a hotel stay; **(b)** a firm pays interest to a foreign lender; **(c)** the government sends emergency aid to another country; **(d)** timber is sold abroad. **[4]**

**2.** Country D's balances for one year, in millions of units, are: goods -73, services +41, primary income -19, secondary income +22.

**(a)** Find the current account balance and say if it is in surplus or in deficit. **[2]**
**(b)** Country D's GDP is 1,450 million units. Express the balance as a percentage of GDP. **[2]**

**3.** Country W, one year, millions of units. Goods: exports 880, imports 790. Services: exports 205, imports 260. Primary income: received 112, paid 140. Secondary income: received 64, sent 31. GDP is 1,600.

**(a)** Work out the balance for goods, services, primary income and secondary income. **[4]**
**(b)** Find the current account balance and say whether it is in surplus or in deficit. **[2]**
**(c)** Express the balance as a percentage of GDP. **[1]**

**4.** A country has a current account deficit of 12. Its goods balance is -35, services +20 and primary income -9. Calculate the secondary income balance. Show your working. **[3]**

**5.** Explain two possible causes of a current account deficit. **[4]**

**6.** Country H earns most of its export income from one crop. Its export volume stays the same, but the world price of the crop falls by 15%. Export earnings were 600 million units and imports are 700 million units.

**(a)** Calculate the new export earnings and the fall in earnings. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the goods balance before and after the price fall. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why depending on one export makes the current account unstable. **[2]**
**(d)** Explain how one supply-side policy could reduce this dependence. **[2]**

**7.** Country J's currency, the rin, falls from 1 rin = 0.80 dru to 1 rin = 0.64 dru (the dru is a foreign currency). A machine exported by J costs 250 rin. A foreign good costs 160 dru.

**(a)** Calculate the machine's price in dru before and after the fall. **[2]**
**(b)** Demand for J's exports has a PED of 1.8. Calculate the percentage change in quantity demanded. **[2]**
**(c)** Demand for imports has a PED of 0.3. Calculate the foreign good's price in rin before and after, and explain what happens to J's spending on it. **[3]**

**8.** Country P has run a large current account deficit for several years.

**(a)** Explain how the deficit could affect GDP and employment. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain how, under a floating exchange rate, the deficit could lead to higher inflation. **[3]**

**9.** Country L keeps its exchange rate fixed. It has a deficit of 24 million units a year and foreign reserves of 99 million units.

**(a)** Calculate how many years the reserves could cover the deficit, to 1 decimal place. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain what the government might be forced to do as the reserves run low. **[3]**

**10.** Country N imports 2,000 bicycles a year at 150 units each. It adds a 20% tariff. PED for the bicycles is 0.5.

**(a)** Find the price each buyer now pays. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the new quantity imported. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the payment to foreign sellers before and after the tariff, and the saving. **[2]**
**(d)** Explain one reason why the tariff may not remove a large deficit. **[2]**

**11.** A government considers cutting spending and raising taxes to reduce a current account deficit. Evaluate whether this is the best policy. **[8]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** Services trade [1]. **(b)** Primary income, as an outflow [1]. **(c)** Secondary income [1]. **(d)** Goods trade [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Name the component, not just "income" or "trade". Answers that say "income" alone leave the examiner unable to tell primary from secondary.

**2. (a)** -73 + 41 - 19 + 22 = **-29** [1]. This is a **deficit** [1].
**(b)** 29 ÷ 1,450 × 100 [1] = **2.0%** of GDP (a deficit) [1].
*Examiner insight:* Keep the minus sign and say "deficit". A bare "29" leaves the direction unclear.

**3. (a)** Goods 880 - 790 = **+90** [1]. Services 205 - 260 = **-55** [1]. Primary income 112 - 140 = **-28** [1]. Secondary income 64 - 31 = **+33** [1].
**(b)** 90 - 55 - 28 + 33 = **+40** [1], a **surplus** [1].
**(c)** 40 ÷ 1,600 × 100 = **2.5%** [1].
*Examiner insight:* Lay out one balance per line, then add. Neat working lets you pick up credit even if one balance is wrong.

**4.** Known balances: -35 + 20 - 9 = -24 [1]. Secondary income = total minus known = -12 - (-24) [1] = **+12** [1].
*Examiner insight:* Subtracting a negative number is where most slips happen. Check by adding all four balances back to -12.

**5.** Two causes, each with a reason and a developed link. For example: prices rise faster than rivals' [1], so exports fall and imports rise [1]; incomes rise strongly [1], so households buy more imports [1]. Other creditworthy causes include profits paid to foreign owners (a primary income outflow) and a recession abroad that cuts demand for exports. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A list of causes with no link to the account earns less than two causes explained step by step.

**6. (a)** 600 × 0.85 = **510** [1], a fall of **90 million** [1].
**(b)** Before: 600 - 700 = **-100** [1]. After: 510 - 700 = **-190** [1].
**(c)** Export earnings depend on one crop's world price [1], so a price fall cuts earnings sharply while import spending stays high [1].
**(d)** Example: training and investment in manufacturing [1] create other exports, so earnings are less tied to one price [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* In part (c) say why the account is unstable, not only that one product is risky. Link the price to earnings and to the balance.

**7. (a)** Before: 250 × 0.80 = **200 dru** [1]. After: 250 × 0.64 = **160 dru** [1].
**(b)** Price change: 160 ÷ 200 = -20% [1]. Quantity change: 1.8 × 20 = **+36%** [1].
**(c)** Before: 160 ÷ 0.80 = **200 rin**; after: 160 ÷ 0.64 = **250 rin**, a 25% rise [1]. Quantity falls 0.3 × 25 = 7.5% [1]. Spending: 1.25 × 0.925 = 1.156, so spending **rises about 15.6%** because demand is inelastic [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* State the direction of each price change before using PED. Applying PED to the wrong price is a common reason for losing credit here.

**8. (a)** Imports exceed exports, so net exports fall [1]. Net exports are part of total demand, so demand for home output and GDP are lower [1]. Firms cut output and jobs [1].
**(b)** More of the currency is supplied than demanded [1], so its value falls [1]. Imported goods and materials cost more, raising firms' costs and prices [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Build each answer as a chain with "so" or "because". Jumping from "deficit" straight to "inflation" leaves out the steps that earn credit.

**9. (a)** 99 ÷ 24 [1] = **4.1 years** [1].
**(b)** To hold a fixed rate the central bank sells reserves [1]. When reserves are nearly gone it cannot support the rate [1]. It might raise interest rates, borrow abroad, or devalue the currency [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Give the unit "years" and round last of all. Name a concrete action in part (b), not "take action".

**10. (a)** 150 × 1.20 = **180 units** [1].
**(b)** Price rise 20%; quantity falls 0.5 × 20 = 10% [1]. 2,000 × 0.90 = **1,800 bicycles** [1].
**(c)** Before: 2,000 × 150 = 300,000. After: 1,800 × 150 = 270,000 [1]. Saving **30,000** [1].
**(d)** Example: demand is inelastic, so quantity falls less than the price rises [1], so imports fall only modestly; or trading partners may retaliate [1], cutting exports. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* Pay foreign sellers 150 per bicycle, not 180. The tariff goes to the government, so do not count it as an import payment.

**11.** How it works: higher taxes and lower spending cut incomes [1], so spending on imports falls [1]. Strength: it can act fairly fast and also lowers inflation, which may help competitiveness [1]. Weakness: it lowers output and raises unemployment [1], conflicting with the aims of growth and full employment [1]. It may not address the cause: if the deficit comes from low quality or poor productivity, only supply-side policy fixes it [1]. Judgement: it is a short-run measure, effective for a small deficit caused by excess demand [1], but a poor choice alone if the deficit is structural, where supply-side policy is better despite being slow [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Evaluation gains credit when it reaches a judgement tied to a condition, such as the cause or size of the deficit. A balanced list with no conclusion usually stays at description.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Dropping the sign on a deficit or forgetting to state "deficit" or "surplus".
- Subtracting a negative balance incorrectly when finding a missing component.
- Confusing secondary income (gifts, aid, remittances) with trade in services.
- Applying PED to the wrong price or quantity.
- Counting a tariff as a payment to the foreign seller.
- Stopping a chain of reasoning after one step.
- Treating every deficit as harmful without discussing cause, size or time.
- Listing policies without judging how effective they are.
