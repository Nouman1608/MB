This guide goes deeper into one part of Paper 1: **3.1.1 Prose fiction**, assessed in Section A of
OxfordAQA International GCSE English Literature (9275). Paper 1 is taken on both Route A and Route B,
so every candidate needs this section. For the whole paper, including the drama section and the
structure of the exam, start with the
[Prose and Drama study guide](/resources/igcse-oxfordaqa-english-literature-prose-and-drama/).

## What 3.1.1 covers

You study one prose fiction text from the specification's prescribed list, which includes novels
and an OxfordAQA short stories anthology. This guide mostly says "novel", but the same methods apply
to short stories. Set texts change from time to
time: OxfordAQA has published a revised specification for first teaching from September 2026 with a
refreshed text list, so always check which list applies to your exam year.

In the exam, Section A carries 30 marks, half of a 60-mark, 1 hour 30 minute paper. Paper 1 is
**closed book**, so you will not have your text in front of you except for any extract printed on the
paper. You answer one question on your set text, choosing between an essay-style question and a
passage-based question.

Answers are marked against three assessment objectives:

| AO | What it means for a novel |
|---|---|
| AO1 | Understanding of, and engagement with, the text's themes, ideas and contexts |
| AO2 | Analysis of how the writer creates meanings and effects (language, structure, form) |
| AO3 | An informed personal response, using appropriate terminology in coherent, accurate writing |

AO2 is where many candidates lose ground. Retelling what happens shows that you know the text, but
the marks rise when you explain **how** the writer has shaped the reader's response.

## The writer's methods: a toolkit for prose

Prose works through different methods from poetry or drama, and naming the right one makes your
analysis precise.

### Narrative voice and viewpoint

- **First-person narration** places the reader inside one character's mind. Ask how much this
  narrator understands, and whether an older narrator is looking back on a younger self.
- **Third-person narration** can be close to one character (limited) or move freely between many
  (omniscient). Notice when the narrator comments, judges or withholds.
- An **unreliable narrator** reports events in a way the reader learns to question. The gap between
  what the narrator says and what the reader sees is itself a method.

### Structure

Structure is the order and shape of the story: where it starts, how time moves (chronologically, in
flashback, through a frame narrative), where the turning points sit, how chapters open and close,
and how the ending answers or refuses to answer the opening. **Foreshadowing** and **parallel
scenes** are especially useful, because they let you connect two points in the text in a single
paragraph.

### Characterisation

Writers build characters directly (the narrator tells us what someone is like) and indirectly
(through speech, actions, thoughts and how other characters react). A strong answer tracks how a
character **changes**, or why they fail to change, and asks what purpose that serves.

### Setting, symbol and motif

Places and objects often carry meaning. A setting can reflect a character's state of mind, contrast
with it, or press on it. A **motif** is a repeated image or idea; following one through a novel gives
you a ready-made line of argument across the whole text.

### Context

Context earns credit when it explains something in the text, not when it is attached as a separate
history lesson. Ask: what did the writer's society believe about class, gender, family or justice,
and how does the novel question or reflect that?

## Choosing your question

**Passage-based questions** print an extract. Read the question closely: if it asks you to write
about the extract, keep your analysis close to the printed words; if it also asks you to refer to the
rest of the novel, you must do both. The extract gives you exact evidence, so your AO2 analysis can
be detailed and precise.

**Essay-style questions** ask about a character, relationship or theme across the whole text. Here
your quotation bank does the work. Plan at least three points from different stages of the novel so
your answer shows development.

Before the exam, decide which type you usually perform better on, but stay flexible: on the day,
choose the question you can answer with the strongest evidence.

## Worked example 1: analysing a short extract

The passage below is invented for practice and is not from any set text.

> The house had been sold before Lena was told. She learned it from the sign at the gate, tilted
> slightly, as though it too were embarrassed. Inside, her father was wrapping cups in newspaper with
> great care, each one turned three times before it went into the box. "We'll talk later," he said,
> not looking up. Later, she understood, was a place he had been keeping for years.

A paragraph that reaches the higher levels might read:

> The writer presents Lena's exclusion from her family's decisions through what she is *not* told.
> The short opening sentence states the sale bluntly, and the passive "had been sold" hides who made
> the decision, mirroring how power has been kept from her. The personified sign, "embarrassed",
> displaces onto an object the shame the father will not show, while his careful, repeated wrapping
> of the cups suggests a man who can give attention to things more easily than to his daughter.
> The final sentence turns "later" into "a place", a metaphor that makes delay sound permanent: the
> reader realises this avoidance is a habit, not a single moment.

Notice the pattern: a clear claim about the question, short embedded evidence, a named method, and an
explanation of its effect on the reader. The final sentence also widens from the moment to the
character's whole pattern of behaviour, which is what an essay on the full novel would develop.

## Worked example 2: planning an essay question

Suppose the question is: *How does the writer present the theme of ambition in your studied novel?*
A five-minute plan should give you an argument, not a list of scenes.

1. **Line of argument:** decide what the novel suggests about ambition overall (for example, that it
   promises freedom but costs a character their relationships).
2. **Early point:** how ambition is introduced, with one short quotation and its method.
3. **Middle point:** a turning point where ambition is tested or rewarded.
4. **Late point:** how the ending judges ambition, and how the structure supports that judgement.
5. **Context:** one precise link, placed where it explains a choice the writer has made.

Each paragraph should move the argument forward. If you can swap two paragraphs without anyone
noticing, the essay is a list rather than an argument.

## Common mistakes

- **Plot summary.** If a paragraph would make sense to someone who has only seen a film version, it
  probably lacks analysis of the writer's methods.
- **Treating characters as real people.** Write "the writer presents Lena as..." rather than "Lena
  is...", so that you keep the writer's choices in view.
- **Feature spotting.** Naming a technique ("this is a metaphor") without explaining its effect earns
  little.
- **Overlong quotations.** Two to six words, embedded in your sentence, usually work best. Long
  quotations waste time in a closed-book exam and are harder to recall accurately.
- **Ignoring the extract.** On a passage-based question, answers that drift straight into the rest of
  the novel miss the close analysis the extract invites.
- **Bolted-on context.** A paragraph of history with no link back to the text does not earn AO1
  credit for context.

## Exam technique and command words

Paper 1 gives you 90 minutes for two 30-mark answers, so allow about 45 minutes for Section A,
including around five minutes to plan. Questions are usually phrased as **"How does the writer
present..."**, or use command words such as **explore** or **discuss**. All of these ask for analysis
and an argued response, not description.

- **How does the writer present...?** Focus on methods: every paragraph should say what the writer
  does and why.
- **Explore** invites you to look at more than one possibility, such as two ways a character could be
  read.
- **Discuss** asks you to build a reasoned argument that weighs evidence from across the text.

Begin with a short introduction that answers the question directly in one or two sentences, then
write three or four developed paragraphs, and finish with a brief conclusion that returns to your
line of argument.

## Revising a closed-book prose text

Because Paper 1 is closed book, revision should build recall you can rely on:

- Learn eight to ten short, versatile quotations, each tagged with the method it shows and the
  themes or characters it could support.
- Make a one-page timeline of the novel's key turning points so that you can place evidence at the
  beginning, middle and end.
- Practise writing a single analytical paragraph in eight to ten minutes from memory.
