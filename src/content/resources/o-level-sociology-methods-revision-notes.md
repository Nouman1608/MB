## Research methods

**Primary** data is collected by the researcher; **secondary** already exists. **Quantitative** is numerical; **qualitative** is descriptive.

| Concept | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Reliability** | Repeating the study gives the same result |
| **Validity** | It gives a true picture |
| **Representativeness** | The sample reflects the wider population |

**Quantitative methods are strong on reliability and weak on validity; qualitative methods are the reverse.** That single trade-off answers most methods questions.

| Method | Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|---|
| **Questionnaire** | Cheap, large samples, reliable, easily analysed | Low response rate, low validity, no chance to explain |
| **Structured interview** | Reliable, comparable, better response rate | Interviewer bias, limited depth |
| **Unstructured interview** | Valid, detailed, flexible | Time-consuming, unreliable, small sample |
| **Participant observation** | Very valid, reveals real behaviour | Hawthorne effect, going native, ethically difficult |
| **Official statistics** | Cheap, large-scale, comparable | Socially constructed by the state |

**Sampling:** random, systematic, stratified, quota, snowball (for hidden groups), opportunity. The **sampling frame** is the list drawn from; its absence often forces a non-random method.

**Pilot study** — a small-scale trial run to find problems with wording, timing or procedure before the main study.

**Ethics:** informed consent, confidentiality, protection from harm, no deception, right to withdraw.

## Culture, socialisation and identity

- **Culture** — the whole way of life of a group: norms, values, roles, beliefs.
- **Norms** are expected behaviours; **values** are beliefs about what matters.
- **Primary socialisation** — family, early childhood. **Secondary socialisation** — school, peer group, media, religion, workplace.
- **Sanctions** — positive or negative, formal or informal.
- **Ascribed status** is fixed at birth; **achieved status** is earned.

**Nature versus nurture:** the sociological evidence is cross-cultural variation in norms and gender roles, and feral children cases where individuals raised without human contact fail to develop language or normal social behaviour.

**Subculture** — a group with distinct norms within a wider society. **Counter-culture** — one actively opposed to the mainstream.

## Inequality

**Social class:**

- **Life chances** — opportunities for health, education, housing and income. Class predicts them more strongly than any other single factor.
- **Social mobility** — **intergenerational** compares a person with their parents; **intragenerational** compares stages of one person's own career.
- **Open** societies allow movement by merit; **closed** societies, such as caste systems, fix status at birth.
- **Absolute poverty** is lacking the necessities for survival; **relative poverty** is falling below the standard considered acceptable in that society — which is why relative poverty exists in wealthy countries.
- **Cycle of deprivation** — poor housing and diet → poor health and education → low-paid work → poverty in the next generation.

**Gender:** the **glass ceiling** blocks women's promotion; horizontal segregation concentrates them in particular occupations and vertical segregation at lower levels. Causes: gender socialisation, domestic and caring responsibilities, discrimination, interrupted careers. Changes: equal-pay laws, girls' educational success, changing attitudes.

**Ethnicity:** discrimination in employment and housing, institutional racism, negative media representation. **Ethnic disadvantage overlaps heavily with class disadvantage**, and much of the apparent ethnic effect narrows once class is accounted for — a qualification worth making.

**Age:** the young and the old both face distinct disadvantages — youth unemployment and low pay at one end, ageism and pension poverty at the other.

## Exam traps

- Confusing reliability with validity.
- Confusing absolute with relative poverty.
- Confusing ascribed with achieved status.
- Confusing intergenerational with intragenerational mobility.
- Evaluating a method in the abstract rather than for the specific topic.
- Giving only one side when evaluation requires both plus a judgement.

## Self-test

1. Distinguish reliability from validity, and say which methods favour each.
2. What is a sampling frame, and why does its absence matter?
3. Distinguish absolute from relative poverty.
4. Explain the cycle of deprivation.
5. Why is it difficult to separate ethnic from class disadvantage?

**Answers:** 1. Reliability is getting the same result on repetition — favoured by quantitative methods; validity is giving a true picture — favoured by qualitative methods. 2. The list of the population from which a sample is drawn; without one, random or systematic sampling is impossible, so a non-random method such as snowball or opportunity sampling must be used. 3. Absolute poverty is lacking the basic necessities for survival; relative poverty is falling below the standard of living considered acceptable in that particular society. 4. Poor housing and diet cause poor health and educational underachievement, leading to low-paid insecure work, which reproduces the same conditions for the next generation. 5. Ethnic minority groups are disproportionately represented in lower socio-economic positions, so much of the apparent ethnic effect narrows once social class is controlled for.
