## The demand for labour (1.2.5 a)

Firms hire labour to produce goods and services, so labour demand is a **derived demand**: it follows from the demand for what the labour makes. If more people want bread, bakeries need more bakers.

Three factors shift demand for labour:

- **Demand for the final product.** Chain: product demand rises, output rises, the firm hires more workers, so labour demand shifts right.
- **Availability of substitutes, including machines.** A cheaper or better machine lets firms replace workers, so demand for that labour shifts left. If machines become dearer, demand for workers rises.
- **Productivity.** Workers who produce more per hour bring in more revenue for each wage paid, so firms want more of them.

Quantity versus demand: a new wage only slides you along the demand curve. The three factors above shift the whole curve.

## The supply of labour (1.2.5 b)

Supply of labour is the number of people willing and able to work at each wage.

| Factor | Effect on supply in a market | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Population size | Larger, shifts right | More people of working age |
| Migration | Inward migration shifts right; emigration shifts left | Changes the number of workers available |
| Age distribution | More people aged 20 to 60 shifts right; an ageing population shifts left | Fewer people of working age |
| Retirement age | Raised, shifts right | People stay in the labour force longer |
| School-leaving age | Raised, shifts left (in the short run) | Young people stay in education instead of working |
| Female participation | Higher, shifts right | More women in paid work, helped by childcare and equal opportunity |
| Skills and qualifications | More people qualified for a job, shifts right for that job | The pool of suitable workers grows |
| Ability to move location or type of work | More mobility, shifts right for the jobs where workers are needed | Occupational and geographical mobility fill gaps |

## Quantity and quality of labour, and human capital (1.2.5 c, d)

- **Quantity** is how many workers, or hours, a firm can hire. Too few workers and orders go unfilled.
- **Quality** is how skilled, healthy and motivated the workers are, which determines productivity.

**Human capital** is the skills, knowledge and health of the workforce. Education and training raise it.

Chain for a firm: training builds skills, output per worker rises, cost per unit falls and quality improves.

**Worked example (illustrative).** A firm pays 12 an hour. Untrained staff make 20 units an hour; after training they make 26.
- Labour cost per unit before: 12 ÷ 20 = 0.60.
- Labour cost per unit after: 12 ÷ 26 = 0.46 (to 2 decimal places).
- Fall: (0.60 − 0.46) ÷ 0.60 ≈ 23%, so the same wage buys more output.

**Quantity against quality.** Firm A has 40 workers making 20 units an hour each (800 units). Firm B has 30 workers making 28 each (840 units). Quality can beat quantity.

Costs of training: fees, lost output while staff train, and the risk that trained staff leave for rivals. For the economy, a better educated workforce raises productivity and incomes. See [factors of production and productivity](/resources/edexcel-igcse-economics-factors-of-production-sectors-productivity-and/).

## Labour market diagrams (1.2.5 e)

Axes: wage (vertical), quantity of labour or employment (horizontal). The demand curve for labour slopes down (firms hire fewer workers at a higher wage). The supply curve slopes up (more people offer to work at a higher wage). They cross at the equilibrium wage and equilibrium employment.

**Worked example.** In the market for cleaning staff in Town T (illustrative), wage is per hour.

| Wage | Workers demanded | Workers supplied |
|---|---|---|
| 6 | 700 | 300 |
| 8 | 600 | 400 |
| 10 | 500 | 500 |
| 12 | 400 | 600 |
| 14 | 300 | 700 |

Equilibrium wage = 10; equilibrium employment = 500.

**Shift in demand.** More offices open, so demand rises by 100 workers at every wage. The new equilibrium is a wage of 11 and employment of 550. Wage up 10%, employment up 10%.

**Shift in supply.** Suppose supply falls by 100 at every wage (for example, many retire). The new equilibrium is a wage of 11 and employment of 450. Wage up 10%, employment down 10%.

| Shift | Effect on wage | Effect on employment |
|---|---|---|
| Demand right | Rises | Rises |
| Demand left | Falls | Falls |
| Supply right | Falls | Rises |
| Supply left | Rises | Falls |

When you draw, label both axes, the curves D and S (and D1 or S1 for a shifted curve) and the old and new equilibrium points.

## Trade unions (1.2.5 f)

A trade union is a body of workers who bargain as one group. By collective bargaining it can seek higher wages, better working conditions, shorter hours and job security. A union's power comes from the threat of industrial action such as a strike.

Using the Town T table: a union bargains a wage of 12. Employment falls to 400 (demand at 12), so 500 − 400 = 100 jobs are lost, while the 400 who keep jobs gain 2 an hour each, 800 an hour in total. The 100 who lose jobs lose out.

| Possible benefits | Possible costs |
|---|---|
| Higher pay and safer conditions for members | Fewer jobs if wage is above equilibrium |
| Voice for workers, fewer disputes | Higher costs for firms, so higher prices or lower profit |
| Better pay can raise motivation and productivity | Strikes disrupt output |

## Government policies for externalities (1.2.6 a, b)

An externality is a spillover cost or benefit felt by third parties. A factory's pollution is a negative externality, so the market produces too much. Each policy aims to cut output or pollution to the socially best level.

**Taxation (illustrative).** Chemical market: demand P = 100 − 2Q, supply (private cost) P = 20 + 2Q. Equilibrium Q = 20, P = 60. Pollution costs society 16 per unit, so the true cost is 36 + 2Q and the best output is 16 (price 68). A tax of 16 per unit moves supply up by 16, and the market settles at output 16 and price 68. Revenue = 16 × 16 = 256. Consumers pay 8 more and producers keep 8 less, so each bears 128 of the tax. External cost falls from 20 × 16 = 320 to 256.
- Advantages: raises revenue, makes polluters pay.
- Disadvantages: hard to set at the right level; demand may be inelastic so pollution barely falls; may hurt poor consumers.

**Subsidies.** For a positive externality the market produces too little, so a subsidy encourages activities with external benefits or cleaner production. Solar-panel market: demand P = 90 − Q, supply P = 30 + Q, equilibrium 30 units at 60. A subsidy of 10 per unit moves supply to 20 + Q. Output rises to 35, buyers pay 55, producers receive 65. Cost to the government = 10 × 35 = 350.
- Advantages: lowers prices, boosts green output.
- Disadvantages: costs taxpayers, firms may depend on it.

**Fines.** A penalty for breaking a pollution rule. A firm saves 24,000 by dumping waste and has a 30% chance of being caught. A fine of 60,000 has an expected cost of 0.3 × 60,000 = 18,000, which is less than the saving, so it still dumps. A fine of 100,000 gives 30,000, which deters it.
- Advantages: punishes, easy to understand.
- Disadvantages: needs monitoring, only works if caught, harm already done.

**Regulation.** Laws that limit or ban, such as emission limits or banning a chemical.
- Advantages: certain result, quick for dangerous harms.
- Disadvantages: compliance and enforcement costs; no incentive to cut below the limit; can raise costs.

**Pollution permits.** The government sets a cap and issues permits that firms can trade. Firm X must cut 20 tonnes and can do so at 40 per tonne, costing 800. Firm Y can cut at 15 per tonne. If permits trade at 25, X buys 20 permits for 500 (saving 300) and Y cuts an extra 20 tonnes at a cost of 300, earning 500 (net gain 200). Total pollution is unchanged but the cost is lower.
- Advantages: certain cap, flexible, rewards cleaner firms.
- Disadvantages: need to set the cap and monitor firms, big firms may buy up permits, may not reduce pollution much if the cap is high.

## Regulating competition (1.2.6 c)

Governments use competition rules to:
- **Promote competition**: remove entry barriers, support small firms, and investigate unfair practices.
- **Limit monopoly power**: ban cartels and price fixing, fine abuse of dominance, cap prices of natural monopolies or break up large firms.
- **Protect consumer interests**: laws on safe products, truthful advertising and labelling, fair contracts, and ways to claim refunds.
- **Control mergers and takeovers**: a regulator investigates mergers that would create a large market share and can block them or set conditions.

**Worked example.** Four firms have market shares of 35%, 25%, 20% and 20%. The first two propose to merge: 35 + 25 = 60% of the market. The regulator is likely to be concerned, because the larger firm could raise price. If price rises from 8 to 10, that is a 25% rise and, on 50,000 units, consumers pay an extra 2 × 50,000 = 100,000.

Evaluation: rules cost money to enforce, and a merger may bring economies of scale and lower costs.

## Minimum wage (1.2.6 d)

A minimum wage is a legal wage floor.

**Reasons:** reduce poverty and inequality, stop exploitation of low-paid workers, and make work pay more than benefits.

**Worked example (Town T).** A minimum of 12 is above the equilibrium of 10, so it is binding. Demand = 400, supply = 600, so there is a surplus of labour of 200 (unemployment). Employment falls by 100 (20%). The wage bill changes from 10 × 500 = 5,000 to 12 × 400 = 4,800. If it is raised to 13, demand is 350, supply is 650 and the surplus is 300. A minimum of 9 would have no effect (below equilibrium).

Introducing: draw a horizontal line above equilibrium, find demand and supply at that wage, and mark the gap. Increasing: move the line up and the gap widens.

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Higher pay for those in work | Unemployment if set above equilibrium |
| Reduces poverty and inequality | Higher costs for firms, so higher prices or job cuts |
| Boosts motivation and spending | Informal work, hard to enforce |
| Reduces reliance on benefits | May not help poor households with no earner |

**Small business example.** A bakery has 6 staff working 35 hours for 50 weeks, which is 10,500 hours. A rise from 9.00 to 10.50 is 16.7%, adding 1.50 × 10,500 = 15,750 to the wage bill (from 94,500 to 110,250).

## Common errors

- Shifting the demand curve when only the wage changes.
- Saying a minimum wage always causes unemployment: it depends where it is set.
- Forgetting that fines and regulation need enforcement.
- Drawing diagrams without labelled axes.

## Frequently asked questions

**What does a trade union do?**
A trade union is a body of workers who bargain as one group. Through collective bargaining it seeks higher wages, better working conditions, shorter hours and job security for its members.

**How can a trade union reduce employment?**
If the union pushes the wage above the equilibrium, firms demand fewer workers, so some jobs are lost while the members who keep their jobs earn more.

**Why do governments set a minimum wage?**
A minimum wage is a legal wage floor intended to reduce poverty and inequality, stop exploitation of low-paid workers and make work pay more than benefits.

**How do governments regulate competition?**
They promote competition by removing entry barriers, limit monopoly power by banning cartels and price fixing, protect consumers with laws on safety and advertising, and investigate mergers that would create a large market share.

**What policies can a government use to reduce pollution?**
The Edexcel specification covers taxation, subsidies, fines, regulation and pollution permits. Each has advantages and disadvantages, such as the difficulty of setting a tax at the right level or the need to monitor firms.
