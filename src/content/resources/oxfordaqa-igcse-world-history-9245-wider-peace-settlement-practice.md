Content tested: "the peacemakers' treatment of Austria, Hungary and the Ottoman Empire; strengths of the treaties and the difficulties they created for different states; the Treaty of Lausanne."

## Section A: knowledge checks

**1.** Give three terms of the Treaty of Saint-Germain (1919). **[3]**

**2.** Give three ways in which Hungary was affected by the Treaty of Trianon (1920). **[3]**

**3.** Give three ways in which the Treaty of Lausanne (1923) differed from the Treaty of Sèvres (1920). **[3]**

**4.** Give three difficulties that the wider settlement created for the new states of Central and Eastern Europe. **[3]**

## Section B: source-based questions

**Source A** *(pre-release, invented)* -- From an editorial imagined as published in a Prague newspaper, 1920.
"For three hundred years we were ruled from Vienna. Now Czechs and Slovaks govern themselves, in a republic with a parliament elected by all. President Wilson's promise has been kept."

**Source B** *(pre-release, invented)* -- A description of a cartoon imagined as published in a Vienna newspaper, 1919. A huge head labelled "Vienna" sits on a tiny, thin body labelled "Austria". Customs officers from neighbouring states block every road to markets. The caption reads: "How shall the head be fed?"

**Source C** *(pre-release, invented)* -- From a declaration imagined as issued by the Turkish nationalist assembly in Ankara, 1920.
"The treaty signed by the Sultan's ministers gives our coasts to Greece, our Straits to foreigners and our finances to the Allies. The Turkish nation did not sign it and will not accept it."

**Source D** *(unseen, invented)* -- From a report imagined as written by an official of the League of Nations minorities section, 1925.
"Petitions continue to arrive from Hungarian communities in Transylvania complaining of the closure of Hungarian schools and the seizure of land. The Romanian government replies that these are internal matters."

**Source E** *(unseen, invented)* -- From a letter imagined as written by a British diplomat at Lausanne to his wife, July 1923.
"We have signed today, and it is a very different treaty from Sèvres. The Turks have won on the battlefield what they could never have won in Paris, and none of the Allies had the stomach to fight them for it. At least this peace has been agreed, not dictated."

**5.** Use Source D and your contextual knowledge to support the claim that the wider settlement created problems for minorities. **[4]**

**6.** Sources A and D give different impressions of self-determination in the new states. Explain one difference between them, using the content of each source and your own knowledge. **[6]**

**7.** How useful is Source E to a historian studying the Treaty of Lausanne? Use the content and provenance of Source E and your contextual knowledge. **[8]**

**8.** Sources C and E give different views of the peace with Turkey. Explain one difference between them, using the content of each source and your own knowledge. **[6]**

**9.** "The wider peace settlement of 1919-23 created more problems than it solved." Using Sources A-E and your own knowledge, how far do you agree? **[12]**

**10.** "Self-determination was applied fairly in the wider peace settlement." Using Sources A-E and your own knowledge, how far do you agree? **[12]**

## Answers

**How these guides work.** 3-mark checks: [1] per relevant point. Support a claim (4): source detail [1], explained as support [1], contextual knowledge [1], linked to the claim [1]. Difference (6): difference stated [2], support from both sources [2], explanation using knowledge [2]. Usefulness (8): content useful [2], knowledge supporting content [2], provenance useful [2], knowledge on provenance [2]; limitations are not required. Range of sources (12): sources used and linked [4], provenance used to weigh sources [2], own knowledge [4], judgement [2].

**1.** Any three, [1] each: Austria recognised the independence of Czechoslovakia, Poland, Hungary and Yugoslavia; South Tyrol and Trieste went to Italy; German-speaking Bohemia and Moravia went to Czechoslovakia; union with Germany was forbidden; the army was limited to 30,000; reparations.

**2.** Any three, [1] each: it lost about two-thirds of its territory; Transylvania went to Romania; Slovakia and Ruthenia went to Czechoslovakia; over three million Hungarians lived outside the new borders; it became landlocked; its army was limited to 35,000; it faced reparations; resentment dominated its politics.

**3.** Any three, [1] each: Lausanne was negotiated, Sèvres imposed; Turkey kept Smyrna, which Sèvres had placed under Greek administration; Turkey kept eastern Thrace; no reparations at Lausanne; the plans for an independent Armenia were dropped; the Straits were demilitarised but no longer under the international control Sèvres had planned; foreign financial control and privileges were ended.

**4.** Any three, [1] each: large minorities, such as German speakers in Czechoslovakia; tensions between nationalities within states such as Yugoslavia; loss of the old imperial trading area and new tariffs; small size and military weakness; hostility from revisionist neighbours such as Hungary; refugees.

**5.** Source detail [1]: Hungarian communities in Transylvania complain of school closures and land seizures. Explained as support [1]: a minority placed inside a new state was losing its schools and land, so the settlement had created a grievance. Contextual knowledge [1]: under the Treaty of Trianon Transylvania was transferred from Hungary to Romania, leaving many Hungarians under Romanian rule. Linked to the claim [1]: the Romanian reply that these were "internal matters" shows that the minority treaties gave the League little power to protect them.

**6.** Difference stated [2]: Source A presents self-determination as fulfilled, with Czechs and Slovaks governing themselves democratically, while Source D shows a minority denied fair treatment in a new state. Support from both [2]: A says "Wilson's promise has been kept"; D describes Hungarian petitions about schools and land. Explanation using knowledge [2]: A reflects the view of a nationality that gained its own state in 1918, while D concerns a people, the Hungarians, who were left as minorities when borders were drawn to reward Romania and other allies; the new Czechoslovak state itself contained about three million German speakers, so self-determination was applied to some peoples and not others.

**7.** Content useful [2]: it shows Lausanne was very different from Sèvres, that Turkey gained through military victory, and that the Allies were unwilling to fight to enforce Sèvres. Knowledge supporting content [2]: Kemal's nationalists defeated the Greek army and retook Smyrna in September 1922; at Chanak Britain's allies would not support military action; Lausanne let Turkey keep Anatolia, Smyrna and eastern Thrace without reparations. Provenance useful [2]: a British diplomat present at Lausanne, writing privately to his wife on the day of signing, gives a frank insider's view of how the Allies saw the treaty. Knowledge on provenance [2]: Britain had led the push for Sèvres and nearly fought at Chanak, so a British diplomat's admission that the Turks had "won" is strong evidence of how far the Allies had retreated.

**8.** Difference stated [2]: Source C rejects the peace terms as a betrayal forced on Turkey, while Source E describes a later peace accepted by Turkey because it was negotiated. Support from both [2]: C says "the Turkish nation did not sign it and will not accept it"; E says "this peace has been agreed, not dictated". Explanation using knowledge [2]: they are about different treaties and dates: C responds to Sèvres in 1920, which gave Smyrna to Greek administration and placed the Straits and finances under foreign control; E describes Lausanne in 1923, after the nationalists' victories had forced the Allies to negotiate.

**9.** Sources used and linked [4]: B and D show economic and minority problems, and D, written by a League official with no reason to exaggerate, confirms the grievances of minorities; C and E show the Ottoman settlement had to be rewritten after a war; A shows the settlement's success for nations such as the Czechs and Slovaks. Provenance [2]: A is a Czech nationalist editorial, likely to praise the settlement; B is Austrian, likely to stress weakness. Own knowledge [4]: Austria needed a League loan by 1922; Hungary lost about two-thirds of its land; Italy's "mutilated victory" and the seizure of Fiume; Arab hopes were replaced by mandates after Sykes-Picot; but Lausanne produced a lasting border, and new states such as Poland and Czechoslovakia gained independence. Judgement [2]: for example, the settlement solved the question of who would rule the collapsed empires, but the way borders were drawn created minority, economic and revisionist problems that later helped dictators such as Hitler and Mussolini.

**10.** Sources used and linked [4]: A supports the view for Czechs and Slovaks; D challenges it for Hungarians in Romania; B shows that German-speaking Austrians were not allowed to choose union with Germany; C shows Turks rejecting a settlement imposed by foreign powers, and E shows that they were eventually able to determine their own borders. Provenance [2]: A is an interested party celebrating its own independence; D is an official report of complaints, strong on the existence of grievances. Own knowledge [4]: union between Austria and Germany was forbidden; about three million German speakers were placed in Czechoslovakia; over three million Hungarians were left outside Hungary; the Arab lands became British and French mandates rather than independent states; Italy received German-speaking South Tyrol; but Poles, Czechs, Slovaks and South Slavs gained states. Judgement [2]: for example, self-determination was applied to the allies and new nations of 1918 but not to the defeated peoples, so it was not applied fairly.
