Formulas follow the specification where it gives them; click-through rate, conversion rate, sales growth, index numbers and return on marketing spend use standard forms. ## Questions

**1.** Identify the type of market (B2B, B2C or C2C) in each case: (a) a firm supplying uniforms to hotels; (b) individuals selling used bicycles to each other through an app; (c) a bakery selling bread to walk-in shoppers. **[3]**

**2.** Lathrigg Bakes sold £25.2m in a regional market worth £210m. A year later the market was worth £231m and Lathrigg sold £29.4m.

**(a)** Calculate the growth of the market. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate Lathrigg's market share in the later year, to 1 decimal place. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate Lathrigg's sales growth, to 1 decimal place, and comment on what it shows about its share. **[3]**

**3.** Rilbury Rugs raised the price of a rug from £80 to £88. Monthly sales fell from 2,000 rugs to 1,700 rugs.

**(a)** Calculate PED for the rug. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the change in revenue, as a percentage. **[3]**

**4.** When average incomes rose by 6%, demand for Orbellow Gadgets' smart speaker rose by 9%, while demand for its basic radio fell by 3%.

**(a)** Calculate the income elasticity of demand for each product. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain what each value shows about the type of good. **[2]**

**5.** Harlsey Gym measures yearly membership revenue at three sites against a base year.

| Site | Base year | Latest year |
|---|---|---|
| East | £84,000 | £96,600 |
| West | £60,000 | £58,200 |
| Central | £72,000 | £79,200 |

**(a)** Calculate an index number for each site, with the base year as 100. **[3]**
**(b)** A recruitment campaign cost £15,000 and the profit from marketing activities was £9,000. Calculate the return on marketing spend. **[2]**

**6.** Hobsley Foods surveys 300 customers. 55% say they would buy a new soup. The researcher reports a 95% confidence level and an interval of 49% to 61%. The launch needs at least 50% of customers to buy. Explain what the interval means for the decision and how it could be narrowed. **[4]**

**7.** Ashkelder Outdoors sells walking boots to keen hill walkers who value sustainable brands and who buy twice a year.

**(a)** Identify **two** bases of segmentation shown. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain **one** disadvantage of operating in a niche market. **[2]**

**8.** Hartsyke Foods sells four products. Product P has a high share of a slow-growing market. Product Q has a low share of a fast-growing market. Product R has a low share of a shrinking market. Product S has a high share of a fast-growing market.

**(a)** Identify the Boston Matrix category of each product. **[4]**
**(b)** Product P is in the maturity stage of its life cycle. Recommend **one** extension strategy and justify it. **[2]**

**9.** Penmarrow Lamps sells 1,000 energy-saving lamps a month at the going rate of £30. The unit cost is £14. It plans a penetration price of £24 and estimates price elasticity of demand at −1.8. Analyse the effect of the plan on revenue and on profit margin. **[6]**

**10.** Ostlethwaite Optics sells spectacles only through independent high-street retailers and is considering its own website. Analyse **two** advantages of direct distribution through e-commerce and **one** disadvantage of using intermediaries. **[6]**

**11.** Selwyck Books earns 60% of its yearly sales in the last three months. Analyse how it could adapt its marketing mix to this seasonal demand. **[4]**

**12.** Yoxmere Stationery spent £6,000 on a pay per click campaign. It produced 200,000 advert views, 5,000 clicks and 400 purchases, and the profit from marketing activities was £3,000. The owner is considering continuing the campaign and using artificial intelligence to personalise offers from customer data.

**(a)** Calculate the click-through rate, the conversion rate (purchases as a percentage of clicks) and the return on marketing spend. **[3]**
**(b)** Evaluate whether Yoxmere should continue the campaign and add personalised offers. **[7]**

## Answers

**1.** (a) B2B [1]; (b) C2C [1]; (c) B2C [1]
*Examiner insight:* Match each label to who is buying from whom; the buyer's identity decides the market type.

**2. (a)** (231 − 210) ÷ 210 × 100 [1] = **10%** [1]
**(b)** 29.4 ÷ 231 × 100 [1] = **12.7%** [1]
**(c)** (29.4 − 25.2) ÷ 25.2 × 100 [1] = **16.7%** [1]; sales grew faster than the market (10%), so share rose from 12.0% to 12.7% [1]
*Examiner insight:* Show the formula and use the right year in the denominator each time; the comment should compare two figures rather than repeat one.

**3. (a)** % change in quantity = −300 ÷ 2,000 × 100 = −15% and % change in price = 8 ÷ 80 × 100 = +10% [1]; PED = −15 ÷ 10 = **−1.5** [1]
**(b)** Old revenue = 80 × 2,000 = £160,000 [1]; new revenue = 88 × 1,700 = £149,600 [1]; change = −10,400 ÷ 160,000 × 100 = **−6.5%** [1]
*Examiner insight:* Keep the sign and the working visible; revenue is price times quantity at each stage, so compute both before finding the change.

**4. (a)** Speaker: 9 ÷ 6 = **+1.5** [1]; radio: −3 ÷ 6 = **−0.5** [1]
**(b)** The speaker is a normal good, income elastic, because demand grows faster than income [1]; the radio is inferior, since its demand falls when incomes grow [1]
*Examiner insight:* A value needs a reading: say what the sign and the size tell the business.

**5. (a)** East: 96,600 ÷ 84,000 × 100 = **115** [1]; West: 58,200 ÷ 60,000 × 100 = **97** [1]; Central: 79,200 ÷ 72,000 × 100 = **110** [1]
**(b)** 9,000 ÷ 15,000 × 100 [1] = **60%** [1]
*Examiner insight:* An index is a value divided by the base value, times 100, not the difference; label the answers with the site names.

**6.** The true proportion of all customers probably lies between 49% and 61%, with 95% confidence [1]; the estimate of 55% is only a central figure [1]; the lower end is below the 50% needed, so the launch could fall short and carries some risk [1]; a larger or better-chosen sample would narrow the interval and make the decision safer [1]
*Examiner insight:* Apply the interval to the decision in the stem rather than only defining it.

**7. (a)** Psychographic (values or lifestyle) [1]; behavioural (buying frequency) [1]
**(b)** Customers are few, so sales and growth are limited [1], and the business is exposed if the segment shrinks or a rival enters [1]
*Examiner insight:* Take each basis from a detail in the stem; a disadvantage needs its consequence, not just a label.

**8. (a)** P: cash cow [1]; Q: question mark [1]; R: dog [1]; S: star [1]
**(b)** For example, rebranding or new packaging to refresh appeal [1], because the market is slow-growing and P's strong share means a small boost protects a major source of cash [1]
*Examiner insight:* Identification earns the first marks; a recommendation needs a reason connected to the product's position.

**9.** A price cut of £6 on £30 is −20% [1]; quantity rises by 1.8 × 20 = 36%, to 1,360 lamps [1]; revenue rises from £30,000 to 24 × 1,360 = £32,640, up 8.8% [1]; margin per lamp falls from £16 (53.3% of price) to £10 (41.7%) [1]; total margin falls from £16,000 to £13,600 [1]; so revenue rises but profit falls, and the plan only pays if extra share brings later gains, or repeat buyers accept a higher price [1]
*Examiner insight:* Separate revenue from profit; an analysis that stops at revenue misses the margin effect the stem invites.

**10.** Advantage one: the firm keeps the full margin instead of sharing it with retailers [1], improving profit per pair [1]. Advantage two: it collects data on customers' purchases [1], enabling targeted offers and better product decisions [1]. Disadvantage of intermediaries: retailers take a margin and control how the brand is displayed [1], so the firm has less control over price and image [1]
*Examiner insight:* Each point needs a development after the identification; saying "cheaper" or "wider reach" alone is a statement, not an analysis.

**11.** Promotion: advertise and offer gift sets before the peak [1], so awareness peaks when demand rises [1]. Price and place: use discounts in quiet months to spread sales [1] and extend online availability or temporary stock to meet the peak [1]
*Examiner insight:* Link each mix element to the pattern of demand; generic mix descriptions do not use the seasonal detail.

**12. (a)** Click-through rate = 5,000 ÷ 200,000 × 100 = **2.5%** [1]; conversion rate = 400 ÷ 5,000 × 100 = **8%** [1]; return on marketing spend = 3,000 ÷ 6,000 × 100 = **50%** [1]
**(b)** A 50% return suggests the campaign pays its way [1]. An 8% conversion rate suggests the website turns visitors into buyers [1]. Personalisation using data analytics could raise conversion and customer retention [1]. However, it needs systems and staff time, and its cost may reduce the return [1]. Use of website cookies and personal data raises privacy concerns that could harm reputation [1]. The data come from one campaign, and the profit figure may not capture later repeat sales [1]. Judgement: continue the campaign, since it is profitable, and trial personalisation on a small scale first, measuring whether conversion rises by more than the added cost [1]
*Examiner insight:* An evaluation needs a conclusion that says what the decision depends on; calculating the measures is only worth marks if the answer then uses them.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using the wrong year as the base for percentage change, share or index.
- Giving elasticity without reading it as elastic, inelastic, normal or inferior.
- Reporting revenue without checking the effect on profit margin.
- Naming a Boston Matrix category with no decision.
- Defining a confidence interval without applying it.
- Evaluating with a list and no judgement.
