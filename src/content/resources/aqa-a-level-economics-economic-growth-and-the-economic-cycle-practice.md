## Questions

**1.** Explain what is meant by long-run economic growth. **[2]**

**2.** Country B is an illustrative economy.

**(a)** Its real GDP rises from 640 to 652.8. Calculate the growth rate. **[2]**
**(b)** Its nominal GDP rises by 9.2% while its price level rises by 4.0%. Calculate the growth in real GDP. **[3]**

**3.** Country C's potential output is 720 and its actual real GDP is 698.4. Calculate the output gap and state what it shows. **[4]**

**4.** Using a production possibility curve, explain the difference between short-run and long-run economic growth. **[4]**

**5.** The table shows invented data for Country D.

| Year | Real GDP growth | Inflation | Unemployment | Investment growth |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | +4.2% | 4.8% | 4.1% | +9% |
| 2 | +0.8% | 4.0% | 4.3% | -3% |
| 3 | -1.9% | 2.1% | 6.0% | -12% |
| 4 | +3.1% | 1.4% | 5.4% | +6% |

Identify the likely phase of the cycle in each year and justify each using the data. **[4]**

**6.** Explain two supply-side determinants of the long-run trend rate of growth. **[4]**

**7.** Country E imports most of its energy. World energy prices rise sharply. Using an AD/AS diagram described in words, analyse the likely effect on Country E's economy. **[5]**

**8.** In Country A an index of house prices rises from 100 to 150 and then falls to 105.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage fall from the peak. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain how an asset price bubble can contribute to a recession. **[4]**

**9.** Households in Country G owe debt equal to 110% of annual income.

**(a)** Calculate the interest payments as a percentage of income when the interest rate is 3% and when it is 6%. **[3]**
**(b)** Assess whether a rise in interest rates would cause a downturn. **[5]**

**10.** Between two years, Country H's real GDP index rises from 100 to 124 and its emissions index rises from 100 to 111.

**(a)** Calculate the change in emissions per unit of output. **[3]**
**(b)** Evaluate whether this growth is sustainable. **[6]**

**11.** Country J raises its trend rate of growth from 2% to 3% a year through higher investment. Evaluate the costs and benefits for individuals, the economy and the environment. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** An increase in the productive capacity of the economy [1], shown as an outward shift of the PPC or LRAS and measured by the trend rate of growth of real output over time [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Answers that mention capacity and the time dimension give a complete definition; a bare "rise in GDP" does not separate long-run from short-run growth.

**2. (a)** (652.8 − 640)/640 [1] × 100 = **2.0%** [1]
**(b)** Real growth = 1.092/1.04 − 1 [1] = 1.05 − 1 [1] = **5.0%** [1]. A subtraction (9.2 − 4.0) gives 5.2%, which is not accurate.
*Examiner insight:* On real growth, show the deflating step; subtracting inflation from nominal growth is an approximation and the working shows you know the difference.

**3.** Gap = (698.4 − 720)/720 [1] × 100 [1] = **−3.0%** [1]. Real GDP is 3.0% below productive potential, a negative output gap with spare capacity [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Dividing by actual output instead of potential is a common slip; also state what the sign means, because the figure alone does not interpret the result.

**4.** Short-run growth: the economy moves from a point inside the PPC to a point nearer the curve as unemployed resources are used, so actual output rises [1] without any change in capacity [1]. Long-run growth: the whole PPC shifts outward because the quantity or quality of resources, or technology, improves [1], so a higher level of output becomes attainable [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Name the axes and say whether a point moves or the curve shifts; a movement along the curve is opportunity cost, not growth.

**5.** Year 1: boom [1], as growth is strong, inflation is high, unemployment is low and investment is rising. Year 2: slowdown [1], as growth falls to +0.8% and investment is falling, although output is still rising. Year 3: recession [1], as real GDP falls, investment drops sharply and unemployment rises. Year 4: recovery [1], as growth returns and investment rises, while unemployment is still high. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Use more than one indicator per phase and note any lagging one, such as unemployment; a verdict based on a single column is weak.

**6.** Capital stock: higher investment in machinery and infrastructure raises the capacity of firms to produce [1], so LRAS shifts right and the trend rate rises [1]. Labour quality: better education and training raise productivity per worker [1], which increases potential output over time [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Each determinant needs a link to capacity; naming a factor without saying how it raises potential output leaves the reasoning incomplete.

**7.** World energy prices raise firms' costs [1]. SRAS shifts left [1]. In the diagram, the new SRAS crosses AD at a higher price level [1] and lower real GDP [1]. Inflation rises while output falls, and a negative output gap may open and unemployment may rise, so the shock is stagflationary [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* State the direction of each variable on the diagram; naming the curve that shifts is worth little unless both the price level and real GDP are described.

**8. (a)** (105 − 150)/150 × 100 [1] = **−30.0%** [1]
**(b)** Rising prices raise wealth and the value of collateral, so households borrow and spend more [1], which lifts AD in the boom [1]. When prices fall, owners with large loans may be in negative equity [1], so they cut spending and banks cut lending, shifting AD left and reducing output [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Link each step to AD or spending; stopping at "house prices fall" without the transmission to output gives only part of the chain.

**9. (a)** Interest burden = debt/income × rate. At 3%: 1.10 × 3% [1] = **3.3% of income**. At 6%: 1.10 × 6% [1] = **6.6% of income** [1]. 
**(b)** Doubling the rate doubles the burden [1], leaving less income for spending, so consumption and AD may fall [1]. However, the effect depends on how many households have variable-rate debt [1], whether incomes are growing [1], and whether savers, who gain, offset some of the loss. The overall judgement is that a downturn is likely if debt is widespread and rates rise quickly, but not certain [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* An assessment needs a judgement with a reason, such as the debt distribution; a one-sided list of effects stays at description.

**10. (a)** Emissions per unit of output = 111/124 [1] = 0.895 [1], so a fall of **10.5%** [1].
**(b)** Emissions per unit of output fall, so growth is cleaner [1]. However, total emissions are 11% higher [1], so total pressure on the environment has increased [1]. Sustainability depends on whether technology continues to improve faster than output grows [1], whether policy such as carbon pricing is in place [1], and on the time period, since the damage may fall on later generations. Overall it is not yet sustainable, though the trend is in the right direction [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* Compare the intensity figure with the absolute total, and finish with a judgement; the numbers support both views.

**11.** Benefits: individuals gain higher incomes and more jobs [1], and a 3% trend roughly doubles output in about 23 years compared with 35 years at 2%, using 70 divided by the rate [1]. The economy gains tax revenue for public services [1] and a larger capital stock [1]. Costs: higher investment means less consumption now [1], inequality may widen if gains go to owners of capital [1], and imports and resource use may rise, so emissions and depletion increase [1]. Evaluation: the net effect depends on the type of investment, such as green technology [1], on whether growth per head rises as population changes [1], and on the time period, since costs come first and benefits later [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* Cover all three groups the question names and make a judgement; listing benefits and costs but never weighing them leaves the answer unfinished.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Treating any rise in output as long-run growth.
- Subtracting inflation from nominal growth instead of deflating.
- Dividing an output gap by actual GDP and dropping the sign.
- Describing a shock without saying which curve shifts and which way.
- Reading the phase of the cycle from one indicator.
- Describing a PPC shift for a recovery from recession.
- Stopping the bubble chain before it reaches spending and output.
- Giving a list of costs and benefits with no judgement about size or timing.
