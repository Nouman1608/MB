## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Biological sex | Physical differences: chromosomes, hormones, reproductive anatomy |
| Gender | The psychological and social experience of being a man, woman or another identity |
| SRY gene | Gene on the Y chromosome that triggers testes development |
| Testosterone | Androgen made mainly by the testes; drives male development before birth and at puberty |
| Oestrogen | Hormone made mainly by the ovaries; female secondary sex characteristics and menstrual cycle |
| Oxytocin | Hormone released in labour and breastfeeding; linked to social bonding |
| Androgen insensitivity syndrome | XY; cells do not respond (fully or partly) to androgens |
| Klinefelter's syndrome | XXY; male; lower testosterone, usually infertile |
| Turner syndrome | One X chromosome (XO); female; short stature, ovaries develop atypically |
| Binary identity | Identifying as either a man or a woman |
| Non-binary | Identity not exclusively man or woman |
| Gender fluid | Identity that changes over time |
| Androgyny | On the BSRI, scoring high on both masculine and feminine traits |
| Gender identity (Kohlberg) | Can label own gender; thinks it could change |
| Gender stability | Knows gender is stable over time, not across situations |
| Gender constancy | Knows gender is stable over time and situations |
| In-group schema | Detailed knowledge about one's own gender |
| Out-group schema | Knowledge about the other gender, largely ignored by young children |
| Gender incongruence | Marked, persistent mismatch between experienced gender and sex assigned at birth (ICD-11 term) |

## Theories and studies at a glance

| Who | What | Finding | One evaluation point |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bem (1974) | BSRI: 60 traits, 7-point self-rating | Masculine and feminine scored separately; androgynous = high on both | Traits from 1970s US students; may lack temporal and cultural validity |
| Taylor et al. (2000) | "Tend and befriend" | Women's stress response linked partly to oxytocin | Theoretical model; does not show gender differences are only hormonal |
| Berenbaum and Hines (1992) | Girls with CAH (high prenatal androgens) | Preferred toys typically chosen by boys more than female relatives did | Parents' treatment may have played a part |
| Van Goozen et al. (1995) | Trans people receiving hormone treatment | Testosterone raised aggression; anti-androgens lowered it | Small sample; hormone effects only |
| Diamond and Sigmundson (1997) | Follow-up of David Reimer | Raised as a girl after an injury; rejected that identity | A single unique case |
| Imperato-McGinley et al. (1979) | Dominican Republic community, XY children appearing female at birth | 17 of 18 raised as girls changed to a male identity during or after puberty | Community attitudes may also explain the change |
| Kohlberg (1966) | Stages: identity, stability, constancy | Gender learning driven by cognitive maturity | Gender-typed preferences appear before constancy (Martin and Little, 1990) |
| Slaby and Frey (1975) | Children watched a film of male and female adults | Higher-constancy children attended more to the same-sex model (significant for boys only) | Supports Kohlberg, but weaker for girls |
| Martin and Halverson (1981) | Gender schema theory | Gender identity alone is enough to start schemas | Does not explain where schema content comes from |
| Martin and Halverson (1983) | Memory for gender-inconsistent pictures | Children distorted them to fit stereotypes | Supports schema-driven memory |
| Bradbard et al. (1986) | Unfamiliar objects labelled for boys or girls | Children explored and recalled more about own-gender objects | Lab task; artificial |
| Fagot (1978) | Parents observed with toddlers | Parents reacted more positively to gender-typical play | Supports direct reinforcement |
| Smith and Lloyd (1978) | Mothers played with a six-month-old infant dressed and named as a boy or girl | Toy choice and play style varied with the perceived sex | Shows reinforcement starts early |
| Mead (1935) | Three societies in Papua New Guinea | Different gender roles, e.g. Tchambuli women dominant | Criticised as subjective; later fieldwork questioned her account |
| Williams and Best (1990) | Cross-national study of stereotypes | Broad agreement on stereotypes across many countries | Suggests some universality |
| Furnham and Farragher (2000) | Adverts in Britain and New Zealand | Men in autonomous roles, women in familial roles | Correlational; effect on viewers not measured |
| Williams (1986) | "Notel", a Canadian town before and after TV | Children's gender stereotyping increased | Natural experiment; other changes possible |
| Zhou et al. (1995) | Post-mortem BSTc in trans women | BSTc size female-typical | Chung et al. (2002): difference emerges only in adulthood |

## Must-know distinctions

- **Sex vs gender.** Sex is biological; gender is psychological and social.
- **Klinefelter's vs Turner.** Klinefelter's is XXY and male. Turner syndrome is one X and female.
- **Non-binary vs gender fluid.** Non-binary is not exclusively man or woman. Gender fluid changes over time.
- **Stability vs constancy.** Stability is across time. Constancy is across time and situations.
- **Kohlberg vs gender schema theory.** Kohlberg says active gender learning starts at constancy (about 6). Martin and Halverson say it starts with basic identity (about 2 to 3).
- **Direct vs vicarious reinforcement.** Direct: the child is rewarded. Vicarious: the child sees a model rewarded.
- **BSRI vs identity.** The BSRI measures self-rated traits, not gender identity.
- **Gender incongruence vs gender dysphoria.** Incongruence is the ICD-11 term for the mismatch itself. Dysphoria (DSM-5) refers to the distress it may cause.

## Issues and debates hooks

Gender essays reward links to wider debates. Use them briefly and tie each to a named theory. The debates themselves are covered in the [Issues and debates revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-psychology-7182-issues-and-debates-revision-notes/).

- **Nature-nurture:** biological explanations stress nature; SLT stresses nurture; schema theory sits between, with maturing cognition shaped by the information a culture supplies.
- **Determinism:** hormonal explanations are close to biological determinism; cognitive theories allow the child an active role.
- **Reductionism:** explaining gender through testosterone alone reduces a complex identity to one level of explanation.
- **Cultural bias:** much of the research, including the BSRI, comes from Western samples.
- **Social sensitivity:** research on gender incongruence can affect how a group is treated, so wording and interpretation matter.

## Mini essay plan

**Question type:** "Compare Kohlberg's theory with gender schema theory as explanations of gender development." **[16]**

1. **AO1 Kohlberg:** three stages, approximate ages, constancy triggers imitation of same-gender models.
2. **AO1 gender schema theory:** identity starts schemas; in-group vs out-group; schemas filter memory and attention.
3. **AO3 support:** Slaby and Frey (1975) for Kohlberg; Martin and Halverson (1983) and Bradbard et al. (1986) for schemas.
4. **AO3 challenge:** gender-typed preferences before constancy (Martin and Little, 1990) challenge Kohlberg but fit schema theory.
5. **AO3 issues and debates:** both see the child as active, a strength over SLT. Both are nurture-light: they do not explain schema content without culture. Cultural bias: research is mostly Western.
6. **Judgement:** schema theory explains early gender behaviour better, but a full account combines cognition with social learning and biology.

Keep the description tight. Spend most of your time on linked evaluation; your teacher will confirm how the marks split.

## Quick self-test

1. Which hormone is linked to labour, breastfeeding and bonding?
2. What chromosome pattern is found in Klinefelter's syndrome?
3. What happens in complete androgen insensitivity syndrome?
4. Define a gender fluid identity.
5. How many traits does the BSRI contain, and how are they split?
6. What BSRI category describes someone low on both scales?
7. Name Kohlberg's three stages in order.
8. What is an out-group schema?
9. Name one SLT process apart from reinforcement.
10. Which study looked at a town before and after television arrived?
11. What did Chung et al. (2002) find about the BSTc?
12. Where is gender incongruence classified in ICD-11?

### Answers

1. Oxytocin.
2. XXY.
3. An XY person's cells do not respond to androgens, so external genitals are female and testes are internal.
4. A gender identity that changes over time.
5. 60: 20 masculine, 20 feminine, 20 neutral fillers.
6. Undifferentiated.
7. Gender identity, gender stability, gender constancy.
8. Knowledge about the other gender, which young children tend to ignore.
9. Identification, modelling, or a mediational process (attention, retention, motor reproduction, motivation).
10. Williams (1986), the "Notel" study.
11. The sex difference in BSTc size does not appear until adulthood.
12. Under conditions related to sexual health, not as a mental disorder.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Swapping the chromosome patterns for Klinefelter's and Turner syndrome.
- Describing oxytocin only as "the love hormone" with no link to biological sex or bonding.
- Saying the BSRI measures gender identity or that it has three categories. It gives four classifications.
- Defining gender stability and constancy as the same thing.
- Giving SLT evaluation that could apply to any behaviour, such as aggression, with no gender example.
- Using David Reimer as proof without noting it is one unique case.
- Writing about gender incongruence in stigmatising language, or giving your own verdict instead of weighing evidence.
- Spending most of a 16-mark answer on description, leaving little room for evaluation.
