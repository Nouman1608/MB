Condensed recall for **4.2 Geophysical hazard risks** (IB DP Geography, Option D: Geophysical hazards). The [Geophysical hazards study guide](/resources/ib-ib-dp-geography-geophysical-hazards-study-guide/) has the longer account.

## Risk

**Risk** is the probability that a hazard will cause harm. It depends on the hazard's nature and on the **exposure** of people and assets.

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Hazard | A natural event that threatens people and property, such as an earthquake or eruption |
| Exposure | The people and assets located where the hazard can reach them |
| Vulnerability | How susceptible those exposed are to harm |
| Risk | The probability of harm, combining hazard, exposure and vulnerability |

A remote volcano that erupts often may carry little risk because few people are exposed; a less active volcano beside a large city may carry far more.

A **hazard profile** records: magnitude, frequency, duration, speed of onset, spatial extent, predictability. Frequency and magnitude are typically inversely related: large events are rarer.

Conceptual formula: **risk = (hazard x vulnerability) / capacity to cope.** Worked check with illustrative scores: (6 x 8) / 4 = 12 versus (6 x 3) / 9 = 2. Risk is not normally calculated this way in practice.

### Comparing hazard profiles

| Feature | Earthquake | Volcanic eruption | Landslide |
|---|---|---|---|
| Speed of onset | Sudden, seconds | Often weeks to months of build-up, then rapid | Sudden, often after heavy rain |
| Duration | Seconds to minutes, plus aftershocks | Days to years | Minutes |
| Spatial extent | Wide area of shaking | Mostly local to regional; ash can travel far | Local |
| Predictability | Very low for timing | Moderate, through precursors | Low to moderate, linked to rainfall thresholds |

## Monitoring and warning

| Hazard | What can be done |
|---|---|
| Volcanoes | Precursors (seismic swarms, ground deformation, gas changes) allow warnings, though timing and scale remain uncertain |
| Earthquakes | Precise prediction is not currently possible; focus on long-term probability forecasts, early warning giving seconds of notice, and building design |

Distinguish the terms:

- **Prediction:** when, where and how big.
- **Forecasting:** long-term probability.
- **Warning:** alert after an event has begun or signs appear.

### Volcano monitoring tools

- **Seismometers** record small earthquakes as magma moves.
- **Tiltmeters, GPS and satellite radar** measure swelling of the ground as magma accumulates.
- **Gas sensors** detect rising sulfur dioxide and carbon dioxide.
- **Thermal imaging** picks up heating at the surface.

Scientists combine these into **alert levels** that tell authorities when to evacuate.

### How earthquake early warning works

Sensors near the fault detect the fast but weak **P waves** and send an automatic alert before the slower, more damaging S and surface waves arrive. Japan's national system can stop high-speed trains, halt lifts and warn the public, but people very close to the epicentre may get little or no warning.

Forecasting uses the record of past earthquakes. The **seismic gap** idea suggests that a section of an active fault that has not ruptured for a long time has built up strain and is more likely to move, though timing remains uncertain.

## Case studies

### Successful warning: Mount Pinatubo, Philippines, 1991

After months of increasing activity, Philippine volcanologists working with the United States Geological Survey monitored earthquakes, ground swelling and gas output, produced hazard maps and raised alert levels in stages. Tens of thousands of people were evacuated, including from a large US air base, before one of the largest eruptions of the twentieth century. Many thousands of lives are thought to have been saved, although lahars continued to cause damage for years afterwards.

### Failure of communication: Nevado del Ruiz, Colombia, 1985

A relatively small eruption melted part of the summit ice cap, generating lahars that travelled down river valleys and buried the town of Armero, killing more than 20,000 people. A hazard map had shown the danger, but warnings were not acted on in time. The lesson is that monitoring is useless unless warnings are communicated clearly and trusted.

### Communicating uncertainty: L'Aquila, Italy, 2009

Before a deadly earthquake, a run of small tremors had worried residents, and an official meeting gave the public an impression of reassurance. Several scientists and an official were later convicted of manslaughter over how risk was communicated; the scientists' convictions were overturned on appeal. The case shows how difficult it is to communicate an uncertain forecast.

## Hazard mapping

Seismic hazard maps and volcanic hazard zones show spatial patterns of risk and guide planning.

Maps feed into **land-use zoning**, such as keeping housing out of lahar paths or requiring stronger buildings in high-shaking zones, and into insurance pricing. Their weakness is that they depend on the historical record, which may be short.

## Why people live near hazards

Fertile volcanic soils, trade routes, geothermal energy, or simply lack of choice.

Further reasons: tourism income, minerals, family and cultural ties to land, and **hazard perception**. People who have not experienced a major event may underestimate the risk, some accept risk as part of life (fatalism), and others trust that technology and government will protect them. Perception varies with experience, education, wealth and culture.

## Model paragraph

*Examine why volcanic eruptions are generally easier to warn for than earthquakes.* "Volcanoes usually give measurable precursors as magma rises, such as earthquake swarms, ground swelling and changes in gas output, so monitoring can justify staged alerts, as at Pinatubo in 1991. Earthquakes, in contrast, release strain suddenly with no reliable short-term sign, so warning is limited to the seconds provided by early warning systems after rupture begins. However, even volcanic warnings are uncertain in timing and size, and the Nevado del Ruiz disaster shows that warnings only work if they are communicated and acted upon."

## Exam traps

- Saying earthquakes can be predicted.
- Confusing hazard (the event) with risk (the probability of harm).
- Ignoring exposure and vulnerability when explaining risk.
- Treating the risk formula as a measured quantity.
- Assuming good monitoring guarantees safety: communication and trust matter.
- Explaining hazard perception as ignorance alone, ignoring economic necessity.
