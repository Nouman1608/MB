## The ozone layer as a system

Treat stratospheric ozone the way Topic 1 treats any system: a storage with inputs and outputs.

- **Storage:** ozone molecules spread through the stratosphere, most concentrated roughly 15–35 km up. Even there ozone is only a few parts per million of the air.
- **Input (formation):** UV radiation splits O₂; each free oxygen atom joins another O₂ to make O₃.
- **Output (natural breakdown):** UV splits O₃ back into O₂ and O, and oxygen atoms recombine with ozone to form O₂.

Formation and breakdown balance, so the storage is in **dynamic equilibrium**. The UV energy absorbed in each breakdown is turned into heat, which is why the stratosphere warms with height. Human-made chlorine and bromine add a new output, faster destruction, without adding any new input. The equilibrium therefore shifts to a lower storage: a thinner layer.

### What "thin" means in numbers

Total column ozone is measured in **Dobson units (DU)**. One DU is the amount of ozone that would form a layer 0.01 mm thick if all the ozone above a point were brought down to standard temperature and pressure. A typical column of 300 DU would make a layer just 3 mm thick. An ozone hole is an area where the column falls below 220 DU.

### Which UV the layer removes

| Band | Wavelength (nm) | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| UV-A | 315–400 | Mostly reaches the surface |
| UV-B | 280–315 | Mostly absorbed by ozone; the share that gets through rises as ozone thins |
| UV-C | 100–280 | Completely absorbed by oxygen and ozone |

Depletion matters because of UV-B. UV-C stays blocked even under a severe ozone hole, and UV-A is barely affected by ozone at all.

## Why some substances do more harm than others

An ozone-depleting substance (ODS) must do three things: survive in the troposphere for years, rise into the stratosphere, and there release chlorine or bromine when UV breaks it apart. CFCs are ideal at the first step because they are unreactive, non-toxic and insoluble, the same properties that made them attractive as refrigerants and propellants. Rain does not wash them out, so they spread through the whole atmosphere.

Two properties set how damaging an ODS is.

1. **Atmospheric lifetime.** CFC-11 lasts about 50 years and CFC-12 about 100 years. HCFCs contain hydrogen, so more of them break down in the troposphere before reaching the ozone layer.
2. **The halogen it carries.** Bromine destroys ozone far more efficiently per atom than chlorine, which is why halons, used in fire extinguishers, are so damaging even in small quantities.

These are combined in the **ozone depletion potential (ODP)**: the damage done by 1 tonne of a substance compared with 1 tonne of CFC-11, which is given an ODP of 1. Approximate values: CFC-11 = 1, halon-1301 = 10, HCFC-22 = 0.055. Multiplying mass by ODP gives **ODP tonnes**, the unit the Montreal Protocol uses to set limits. It works just like CO₂-equivalent in climate questions.

### Worked example 1: ODP tonnes

A fictional country uses 120 t of CFC-11, 8 t of halon-1301 and 900 t of HCFC-22 in one year.

```
CFC-11:     120 × 1     = 120 ODP t
Halon-1301:   8 × 10    =  80 ODP t
HCFC-22:    900 × 0.055 =  49.5 ODP t
Total                   = 249.5 ODP t

Halon share of damage   = 80 ÷ 249.5 × 100  = 32.1%
HCFC-22 share by mass   = 900 ÷ 1028 × 100  = 87.5%
HCFC-22 share of damage = 49.5 ÷ 249.5 × 100 = 19.8%
```

The halon is under 1% of the mass but about a third of the damage. The lesson for policy is to phase out the high-ODP substances first, which is the order the Montreal Protocol followed.

## Why the hole forms over Antarctica in spring

The basic chlorine cycle runs everywhere in the stratosphere, but most chlorine is normally held in **reservoir compounds** such as hydrogen chloride and chlorine nitrate, which do not attack ozone. The Antarctic winter changes that.

1. A strong ring of winds, the **polar vortex**, isolates the air over Antarctica.
2. Temperatures fall low enough for **polar stratospheric clouds** to form.
3. Reactions on the cloud particles turn the reservoir compounds into chlorine molecules, which build up in the dark.
4. When sunlight returns in September and October, UV splits these molecules into chlorine atoms, and rapid catalytic destruction begins.
5. As the vortex breaks down in late spring, ozone-rich air mixes back in and the hole closes until the next year.

The Arctic sees smaller and more variable losses because its stratosphere is usually warmer and its vortex weaker, so fewer clouds form. Be ready to explain this contrast; it is a favourite "explain" question.

## Evidence and the timeline of discovery

- **1974:** Mario Molina and F. Sherwood Rowland proposed that chlorine from CFCs could destroy stratospheric ozone. With Paul Crutzen, they shared the 1995 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for work on ozone chemistry.
- **1985:** scientists of the British Antarctic Survey (Farman, Gardiner and Shanklin) published ground-based measurements showing large springtime ozone losses over Antarctica.
- **1985:** the Vienna Convention set up international co-operation, before any controls were agreed.
- **1987:** the Montreal Protocol set binding cuts in ODS production and use.

The order matters for evaluation. Countries agreed to act while important parts of the science were still being confirmed, which is often given as an example of the **precautionary principle**.

## Managing ozone depletion with the pollution management model

| Level | Ozone examples | Comment |
|---|---|---|
| Alter the human activity | Replace CFCs with alternatives such as hydrocarbons in fridges, pump sprays in place of aerosols, non-halon fire suppression | Tackles the cause; depended on substitutes being available |
| Control the release | Recover refrigerant from old fridges and air-conditioners; capture and destroy foam-blowing agents; licensing and trade controls | Needed because ODS already in equipment, called banks, keep leaking for decades |
| Clean up and restore | No practical way to remove ODS from the stratosphere or add ozone back | Recovery relies on natural removal over decades |

The lack of any clean-up option is the reason prevention was the only real strategy.

### Why recovery is slow

Even after production stops, ODS keep reaching the stratosphere: old banks leak and long-lived gases break down only slowly. Suppose a gas has a half-life of 35 years. After 35 years half the original amount remains; after 70 years a quarter remains. This is why the Antarctic layer is projected to recover only around the 2060s, decades after the main phase-outs.

## Strengths and weaknesses of the ozone regime

**Strengths to cite**
- Every UN member state ratified the Montreal Protocol.
- Phase-out schedules were tightened through later amendments as the science grew clearer.
- The Multilateral Fund, set up under the 1990 London Amendment, paid developing countries' costs of switching, which brought them in.
- Trade restrictions with non-parties gave countries a reason to join.

**Weaknesses and complications**
- Substitutes created new problems: HCFCs still deplete ozone, and HFCs are strong greenhouse gases, which led to the Kigali Amendment (2016).
- Enforcement depends on monitoring. In 2018 scientists reported that the fall in atmospheric CFC-11 had slowed, pointing to new illegal production; emissions fell again after the finding was acted on.
- Nitrous oxide also depletes ozone but is not controlled by the Montreal Protocol.

## Common mistakes

- Writing that the ozone hole lets in more heat. It lets in more UV-B.
- Saying UV-C reaches the ground under the hole. It stays blocked.
- Treating ODP and GWP as the same measure. ODP compares ozone damage with CFC-11; GWP compares warming with CO₂.
- Explaining the Antarctic hole with "it is cold" alone. The chain needs the vortex, the clouds, the release of chlorine and the return of sunlight.
- Claiming the problem is solved. The layer is recovering, not recovered.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Outline** the formation of ozone: two equations, or two linked sentences, with UV named.
- **Explain** a pattern, such as the spring timing: give the mechanism step by step, with "so" or "because" linking each step.
- **Calculate** from data: show the formula, keep units (DU, ODP t, million km²) and use the starting value as the base for percentage change.
- **Evaluate** or **to what extent** questions on the Montreal Protocol: give evidence of success, at least two reasons, at least two limitations, then a judgement that says how far the success can be copied for climate change.
