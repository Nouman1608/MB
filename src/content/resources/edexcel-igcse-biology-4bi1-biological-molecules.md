## 1. Elements and building blocks (2.7–2.8)

| Molecule | Elements | Built from | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Carbohydrate | Carbon, hydrogen, oxygen | Simple sugars | Starch (plants) and glycogen (animals), from glucose |
| Protein | Carbon, hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen (some also sulfur) | Amino acids | Enzymes, haemoglobin, muscle protein |
| Lipid (fat or oil) | Carbon, hydrogen, oxygen | Fatty acids and glycerol | Fats in adipose tissue, oils in seeds |

Two points examiners reward. First, **nitrogen** is the element that distinguishes proteins: an unknown molecule containing nitrogen is a protein, not a carbohydrate or lipid. Second, starch and glycogen are **both** made of many glucose units; they differ in where they are found and how the chains are arranged, not in their building block. Lipids contain proportionally much less oxygen than carbohydrates.

Large molecules are useful for **storage** because they are insoluble or less soluble, so they do not affect the water balance of cells by osmosis. Digestion breaks them back into their small units so they can be absorbed.

## 2. Food tests done well (2.9)

| Test for | Method | Positive result | Negative result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Glucose | Add Benedict's solution, heat in a water bath at about 80 °C for a few minutes | Green, yellow, orange or brick-red | Stays blue |
| Starch | Add a few drops of iodine solution | Blue-black | Stays orange-brown |
| Protein | Add biuret solution (or sodium hydroxide then dilute copper sulfate) | Purple (lilac) | Stays blue |
| Fat | Shake the sample with ethanol, then pour the liquid into water | Cloudy white emulsion | Stays clear |

**Semi-quantitative Benedict's.** The colour after heating shows roughly how much glucose was present. Comparing equal volumes of sample and reagent, heated for the same time: blue (none) → green (trace) → yellow → orange → brick-red (most).

**Worked example: three fruit juices.** A student tests 2 cm³ of each juice with 2 cm³ of Benedict's solution in a water bath for 5 minutes.

| Juice | Colour after heating |
|---|---|
| A | Yellow |
| B | Brick-red |
| C | Green |

Order of glucose concentration, highest first: **B, A, C**. To make the comparison fair, the student kept the volumes, the heating temperature and the heating time the same. A tube of 2 cm³ of water with Benedict's, heated alongside, acts as a control, showing that the reagent alone stays blue. Because the result is judged by eye, a colorimeter would make it quantitative.

**Safety.** Ethanol is flammable, so the fat test is done away from flames, and Benedict's tubes are heated in a water bath, not a Bunsen flame. Wear eye protection with biuret solution, which contains an alkali.

## 3. Enzymes as biological catalysts (2.10)

A **catalyst** speeds up a chemical reaction without being used up. **Enzymes** are proteins that catalyse **metabolic reactions**, the reactions in living organisms. They work both in reactions that break molecules down, such as digestion and respiration, and in reactions that build them up, such as joining glucose into starch or amino acids into proteins.

Each enzyme has an **active site** with a specific shape. Only a substrate with a **complementary** shape fits, forming an enzyme–substrate complex; the reaction happens and the products leave, so the enzyme can be used again. This is why each enzyme catalyses only one reaction or type of reaction.

## 4. Temperature (2.11–2.12)

- **Below the optimum:** raising the temperature gives enzyme and substrate molecules more kinetic energy, so they collide more often and more enzyme–substrate complexes form each second. The rate rises.
- **At the optimum:** the rate is highest.
- **Above the optimum:** the enzyme's shape, including its active site, changes. The substrate no longer fits, so fewer complexes form and the rate falls. When the change is permanent, the enzyme is **denatured**.

**Worked example: trypsin and milk.** Trypsin is a protease that digests the protein in milk, so a cloudy milk suspension turns clear. A student mixes 5 cm³ of milk suspension with 1 cm³ of trypsin solution, each first left in a water bath at the test temperature, and times how long the mixture takes to go clear.

| Temperature / °C | 20 | 30 | 40 | 50 | 60 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Time to clear / s | 300 | 160 | 95 | 140 | 600 |
| Rate (1000 ÷ time) / s⁻¹ | 3.3 | 6.3 | 10.5 | 7.1 | 1.7 |

**Rate = 1 ÷ time**, multiplied by 1000 here to give convenient numbers. A shorter time means a faster rate.

*Question: Describe and explain the effect of temperature on the rate of this reaction. [6]*

**Model answer.** From 20 °C to 40 °C the rate increases, from 3.3 to 10.5 [1]. This is because molecules have more kinetic energy, so trypsin and protein collide more often and more enzyme–substrate complexes form [1]. The highest rate in these results is at 40 °C, so the optimum is around 40 °C [1]. Above 40 °C the rate falls, to 1.7 at 60 °C [1], because the shape of the active site changes [1], so the protein no longer fits and the enzyme is denatured [1].

The answer quotes data with units, uses "active site", and separates the rising and falling parts of the curve. Because readings are only every 10 °C, the true optimum could lie anywhere between 30 °C and 50 °C; testing every 2 °C in that range would locate it more precisely.

**Variables in the temperature practical.** Independent: temperature. Dependent: time to clear. Controlled: volume and concentration of milk and of trypsin, pH (using a buffer), and the same judgement of "clear", for example when a cross drawn under the tube becomes visible. Pre-warming both solutions matters, because otherwise the reaction starts below the set temperature.

## 5. pH (2.13 and 2.14B)

Each enzyme has an **optimum pH**. Away from it, the pH alters the shape of the active site, so the substrate fits less well and the rate falls; at extreme pH the enzyme is denatured. Enzymes suit where they work: protein-digesting enzymes in the stomach work best in acid, while trypsin, working in the small intestine, works best in slightly alkaline conditions.

**Paper 2 practical example (2.14B).** The trypsin method is repeated at 40 °C using **buffer solutions** to set the pH:

| pH | 5 | 6 | 7 | 8 | 9 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Time to clear / s | 420 | 250 | 140 | 95 | 130 |

The fastest rate is at pH 8 in these results, so the optimum is close to pH 8. Temperature is now the controlled variable, kept at 40 °C with a water bath; volumes and concentrations stay the same.

## Common mistakes

- Saying enzymes are "killed" at high temperature: they are proteins, not living things, so they are **denatured**.
- Saying the substrate is denatured, or that the active site is "destroyed" without saying its **shape** changes.
- Describing a graph without quoting figures from it.
- Reading a longer time as a faster rate: rate = 1 ÷ time.
- Heating ethanol or Benedict's tubes over a flame.
- Giving starch and glycogen different building blocks: both are made of glucose.
- Forgetting nitrogen in proteins.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Describe** a trend: state the pattern and quote figures with units at the turning points.
- **Explain**: give the reason, using kinetic energy and collisions below the optimum and active site shape above it.
- **Identify** a molecule from its elements: nitrogen means protein.
- **Suggest** an improvement to a practical: smaller temperature steps near the optimum, repeats, a colorimeter in place of judging by eye.
- **Evaluate** results: comment on the number of readings and repeats, controls, and how confident the conclusion can be.
