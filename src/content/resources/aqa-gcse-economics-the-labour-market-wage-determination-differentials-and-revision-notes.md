## Key definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Labour market | Where workers sell their labour and employers buy it |
| Wage | The price of labour |
| Derived demand | Firms' need for workers, which exists only because buyers want what the workers help produce |
| Equilibrium wage | The wage where workers wanted equal workers willing |
| Wage differential | A difference in pay between occupations or within one |
| Gross pay | Total pay before deductions (basic, overtime, bonus, commission) |
| Net pay | Take-home pay: gross pay minus deductions |
| Deductions | Items such as income tax, National Insurance and pension contributions |

## Role of the labour market

- It brings workers and employers together, so jobs are filled.
- The wage acts as a signal. A high wage tells workers a job is scarce and attracts them; a low wage tells them it is not.
- It sets incomes for most households, which they then spend in other markets.
- It works through demand and supply, but wages can be slow to adjust, and workers cannot always move between areas or jobs.

## Demand and supply of labour

| | Demand for labour | Supply of labour |
|---|---|---|
| Comes from | Firms | Workers |
| Slope | Down: higher wage, fewer workers hired | Up: higher wage, more willing |
| Shifts right if | Product demand rises, productivity rises, machines become dearer | More people trained, migration in, other jobs pay less, better conditions |
| Shifts left if | Product demand falls, productivity falls, machines become cheaper | Fewer trained, longer training, other jobs pay more, worse conditions |

Remember the exam rule: a change in the **wage** moves you along a curve; a change in anything else shifts the curve.

## Method: finding and changing a wage

1. Find equilibrium: set Qd equal to Qs and solve for W, then put W back into one equation for Q.
2. For a shift, change the equation by the amount given at every wage, then solve again.
3. For a shortage or surplus at a wage, work out Qd and Qs at that wage and subtract.
4. State what the wage does: shortage pushes it up, surplus pushes it down.

**Worked reminder.** Town B (illustrative): Qd = 180 − 6W, Qs = 4W − 20, W in pounds an hour.

- Equilibrium: 180 − 6W = 4W − 20, so 200 = 10W. W = **£20**, Q = 180 − 120 = **60**.
- At £16: Qd = 180 − 96 = 84; Qs = 64 − 20 = 44. Shortage of **40**.
- Demand falls by 20 at every wage: Qd = 160 − 6W. Then 180 = 10W, W = **£18**, Q = 52. Wage and employment fall.
- Supply rises by 30 at every wage: Qs = 4W + 10. Then 170 = 10W, W = **£17**, Q = 78. Wage falls, employment rises.

Diagram in words: the up-and-down axis shows the hourly wage and the left-to-right axis shows the number of workers. The demand line falls and the supply line climbs; where they meet is the equilibrium. A shift right of either curve moves the crossing point to the right.

Summary of effects:

| Change | Wage | Employment |
|---|---|---|
| Demand curve moves right | Higher wage | More workers hired |
| Demand curve moves left | Lower wage | Fewer workers hired |
| Supply curve moves right | Lower wage | More workers hired |
| Supply curve moves left | Higher wage | Fewer workers hired |

## Wage differentials

| Between occupations | Within an occupation |
|---|---|
| Skills and qualifications | Experience and seniority |
| Training time and cost | Performance, bonus, commission |
| Risk, stress, unsocial hours | Extra qualifications, responsibility |
| Revenue each worker brings in | Location |

**Chain of reasoning:** a scarce skill means low supply, strong demand means high demand, so the wage is high. Always end with the wage.

**Written answer pattern (explain a differential):** name the cause, say whether it affects supply or demand, say which way the curve shifts, then state the effect on the wage. For example, a long training period means fewer qualified people, so supply is lower, and the wage is higher.

**Evaluation:** differentials persist if training is long, people cannot easily move, or jobs are chosen for non-pay reasons.

**Percentage gap method.** Gap = higher − lower. Percentage = gap ÷ the pay you are comparing against × 100.

Example: Job P pays £15 an hour and Job R pays £24.

- R pays (24 − 15) ÷ 15 × 100 = **60%** more than P.
- P pays (24 − 15) ÷ 24 × 100 = **37.5%** less than R.

The gap is the same £9 each time; the base changes.

## Gross and net pay

| Item | Rule |
|---|---|
| Gross pay | Basic pay + overtime + bonus + commission |
| Overtime pay | Hours × normal rate × the multiplier (time and a half is 1.5) |
| Deduction | Tax, National Insurance, pension (use the rates the question gives) |
| Net pay | Gross pay − all deductions |

**Method in steps**

1. Work out each part of gross pay and add.
2. Apply each rate. If an allowance is given, apply the rate only to pay above it.
3. Add the deductions and subtract from gross.
4. Check: net must be smaller than gross.

**Worked reminder: weekly pay.** A worker is paid £14 an hour for 30 hours, plus a £60 bonus. Tax is 20% and National Insurance is 8%, each on gross pay above £100 a week.

- Gross: 14 × 30 = 420; 420 + 60 = **£480**.
- Pay above £100: 380. Tax: 0.20 × 380 = £76. National Insurance: 0.08 × 380 = £30.40.
- Net: 480 − 76 − 30.40 = **£373.60**.

**Worked reminder: annual pay.** A salary of £24,000. Tax is 20% above a £9,000 allowance, National Insurance is 6% of gross, and the pension is 2.5% of gross.

- Tax: 0.20 × 15,000 = £3,000. National Insurance: £1,440. Pension: £600.
- Deductions: £5,040. Net: 24,000 − 5,040 = **£18,960** a year, so 18,960 ÷ 12 = **£1,580** a month.
- Deductions are 5,040 ÷ 24,000 = 21% of gross.

**Total income** means adding every source, using net amounts for work income: net pay + second job (net) + interest + other payments received.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Shortage versus surplus.** Below equilibrium: shortage. Above: surplus.
- **Shift versus movement.** Only a wage change is a movement.
- **Between versus within** an occupation. Name which one a question asks for.
- **Gross versus net.** Gross is before deductions; net is after.
- **Gross rise versus net rise.** A pay rise in gross terms gives a smaller percentage rise in net when an allowance is fixed.

## Quick self-test

1. Explain in one sentence why demand for labour is described as derived.
2. State two factors that shift the supply of labour to the left.
3. Using Qd = 180 − 6W and Qs = 4W − 20, state the shortage at a wage of £14.
4. A shop expands and raises demand for staff at every wage. State the effect on the wage and employment.
5. Give two reasons why an experienced worker may be paid more than a trainee in the same job.
6. Job A pays £10 an hour and Job B pays £12.50. Calculate how much more B pays, as a percentage of A.
7. Define net pay.
8. Name two deductions from gross pay.
9. Pay is £9 an hour for a 40-hour week. Four extra hours are worked at time and a half. Calculate gross pay for the week.
10. Gross pay is £2,000 a month and deductions are £430. Calculate net pay and deductions as a percentage of gross.

### Answers

1. Firms hire only because customers want what the workers help to make.
2. Any two: more training needed, rival jobs pay more, fewer qualified people, worse conditions, fewer migrants.
3. Qd = 180 − 84 = 96; Qs = 56 − 20 = 36; shortage = **60**.
4. Demand shifts right: wage **rises** and employment **rises**.
5. Any two: more productive, greater skill, seniority or responsibility, performance pay.
6. (12.50 − 10) ÷ 10 × 100 = **25%**.
7. Pay left after deductions such as tax, National Insurance and pension contributions; take-home pay.
8. Any two of income tax, National Insurance, pension contributions.
9. 9 × 40 = 360; overtime 9 × 1.5 = 13.50 × 4 = 54; gross = **£414**.
10. Net = 2,000 − 430 = **£1,570**; 430 ÷ 2,000 = **21.5%**.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Drawing or describing a wage change as a shift in the curve when the wage itself changed.
- Shifting supply when the cause is a change in demand for the product.
- Stating a differential without linking it to supply or demand.
- Using the wrong base in a percentage gap.
- Applying a tax rate to the whole of gross pay when an allowance is given.
- Treating net pay as larger than gross pay.
- Forgetting the overtime multiplier.
- Leaving out units: pounds, hours or workers.
- Finishing an evaluation answer without a judgement.
