These notes condense section 3.2.4 (HIV as an example of a human disease caused by a virus) of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Biology (9610) specification, Version 5.1, in Unit 2. For explanations, worked drug examples and treatment data, use the [HIV study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-biology-9610-hiv-as-an-example-of-a-human-disease-caused-by-a-virus-oxfordaqa-alevel-biology/); then try the [HIV practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-biology-9610-hiv-as-an-example-of-a-human-disease-caused-by-a-virus-oxfordaqa-alevel-biology-practice/).

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| HIV | Human immunodeficiency virus |
| Acellular | Not made of cells: no cytoplasm, ribosomes or metabolism of its own |
| Capsid | Protein coat enclosing the RNA and the viral enzymes |
| Envelope | Outer layer of the particle, taken from the host cell plasma membrane, containing glycoproteins |
| Glycoprotein | Protein with carbohydrate attached; on HIV, binds to CD4 receptors |
| CD4 helper T-cell | The host cell HIV infects; these cells initiate the body's response to infections |
| Reverse transcriptase | Enzyme that makes DNA from an RNA template |
| Integrase | Enzyme that inserts viral DNA into a host chromosome |
| Protease | Enzyme that cuts long viral protein chains into working proteins |
| AIDS | Acquired immune deficiency syndrome: the stage when helper T-cell numbers are so low that other infections take hold |

## Structure from outside in (3.2.4.1)

1. Envelope (from host plasma membrane) with glycoproteins.
2. Protein capsid.
3. Inside the capsid: RNA, reverse transcriptase, integrase and protease.

## Replication cycle (3.2.4.2)

| Step | What happens | Enzyme or part |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Binding | Glycoproteins bind to CD4 receptors on a helper T-cell | Glycoproteins |
| 2. Entry | Envelope fuses with cell membrane; RNA and enzymes released into the cell | Envelope |
| 3. Reverse transcription | Viral RNA used as a template to make double-strand DNA | Reverse transcriptase |
| 4. Integration | Viral DNA inserted into a host chromosome | Integrase |
| 5. Transcription | Viral DNA may be transcribed to give viral RNA | Host enzymes |
| 6. Translation | Viral proteins made on host ribosomes; long chains cut to size | Host ribosomes; protease |
| 7. Assembly and release | RNA, enzymes and proteins packaged; particles leave, taking host membrane as envelope | Capsid proteins; host membrane |

**Why the order matters:** only DNA can be integrated and transcribed by the host, so RNA must first be reverse-transcribed. Integrated DNA is copied with the host's chromosomes and can stay unexpressed ("may be transcribed").

## From HIV to AIDS

- Infected helper T-cells are damaged or destroyed; numbers fall.
- Fewer helper T-cells, so a weaker immune response.
- Other pathogens cause infections a healthy person would resist: AIDS.

## Drug reasoning method

1. Find the step the drug blocks (or what is missing in treated cells).
2. Say which later steps cannot happen.
3. End with the outcome: fewer, or non-infectious, new particles; fewer helper T-cells infected.

| Clue in the question | Step affected |
|---|---|
| Virus cannot attach; no viral RNA enters the cell | Binding or entry |
| Viral RNA present but no viral DNA, or incomplete DNA strands | Reverse transcription |
| Viral DNA present, but not in the chromosomes | Integration |
| Long uncut viral proteins; particles not infectious | Protease |

**Selectivity:** uninfected cells do not use the viral reverse transcriptase, integrase or protease, so inhibiting them harms host metabolism less. **Antibiotics** do not work on viruses: no cell wall, ribosomes or metabolism to target.

## Treatment data patterns

- On treatment: viral load falls; helper T-cell count rises.
- Treatment stopped: viral load rebounds, because integrated DNA in host chromosomes was never removed.
- Conclusion: drugs control the infection; they do not cure it.

## Exam traps

- "HIV attacks the immune system." Name CD4 receptors on helper T-cells.
- "The virus makes its envelope." The envelope comes from the host plasma membrane.
- "Integrase makes DNA." Reverse transcriptase makes DNA; integrase inserts it.
- "Viral RNA joins the chromosome." Only DNA is integrated.
- "HIV kills the patient." Other infections do, because the immune response is weakened.
- Naming a drug instead of explaining the blocked step.
- Forgetting that the host provides ribosomes, nucleotides, amino acids and ATP.

## Quick self-test

1. State the genetic material of HIV.
2. Name the three enzymes inside the HIV capsid.
3. Where does the HIV envelope come from?
4. Which molecule on the host cell does HIV bind to?
5. Why must viral RNA be converted into DNA?
6. What does integrase do?
7. What does the word "may" in "the viral DNA may be transcribed" tell you?
8. What provides the ribosomes used to make viral proteins?
9. A drug stops protease working. What effect does this have on new particles?
10. In treated cells, viral RNA is present but no viral DNA. Which step is blocked?
11. Explain why antibiotics do not treat HIV.
12. Why does viral load rise again when a patient stops taking HIV drugs?

### Answers

1. RNA.
2. Reverse transcriptase, integrase and protease.
3. The plasma membrane of the host cell it last left.
4. The CD4 receptor on helper T-cells.
5. Only DNA can be integrated into a host chromosome and transcribed by the host cell.
6. It inserts the double-strand viral DNA into a host chromosome.
7. Integrated viral DNA is not necessarily expressed straight away; an infected cell can carry it without producing virus.
8. The host cell.
9. Long viral proteins are not cut into working proteins, so particles that assemble are not infectious.
10. Reverse transcription.
11. Viruses are acellular, with no cell wall, ribosomes or metabolism of their own for antibiotics to act on.
12. Viral DNA integrated in host chromosomes is not removed by the drugs; once treatment stops it can be transcribed and new particles made.

## Frequently asked questions

**Which cells does HIV infect?**
HIV binds to CD4 receptors on helper T-cells, the cells that initiate the body's response to infections.

**Do I need to know the names of HIV drugs?**
No. The specification asks you to explain drug effects when you are given information about them.
