## Questions

**1.** State the accounting concept applied in each of the following situations.

**(a)** The owner's private car insurance is not recorded in the business's books. **[1]**
**(b)** A highly skilled team of staff is not shown as an asset. **[1]**
**(c)** Land is shown at its purchase price of £150 000, although an estate agent values it at £210 000. **[1]**
**(d)** Every transaction is recorded with a debit and a credit of equal value. **[1]**

**2.** State **two** concepts that explain why inventory is valued at the lower of cost and net realisable
value. **[2]**

**3.** Brookmoor Kitchenware holds the following inventory at its year end.

| Item | Units | Cost per unit £ | Expected selling price per unit £ | Further costs per unit before sale £ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pans | 80 | 25 | 40 | 0 |
| Knife sets | 50 | 30 | 36 | 8 (packaging and delivery) |
| Mixers (damaged) | 12 | 90 | 110 | 30 (repairs) |

Calculate the value of closing inventory to be shown in the statement of financial position. **[4]**

**4.** Ashdown Crafts sent goods with a selling price of £7 200 to a customer on sale or return. The whole £7 200
was recorded as a credit sale. Before the year end the customer accepted goods with a selling price of £2 400;
the rest are still held by the customer and have not been accepted. Ashdown Crafts uses a mark-up of 20% on
cost. Draft profit for the year is £29 600.

**(a)** Calculate the corrected profit for the year. **[2]**
**(b)** State the change needed to trade receivables and to closing inventory. **[2]**
**(c)** State the accounting concept that requires this adjustment. **[1]**

**5.** Kelso Print bought a new printing machine. The invoice shows:

| Item | £ |
|---|---|
| Machine | 32 000 |
| Delivery | 450 |
| Installation | 1 150 |
| One-year maintenance contract | 1 200 |
| Ink cartridges | 380 |

**(a)** Calculate the amount to be debited to the machinery account. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the amount to be charged to the income statement as revenue expenditure. **[1]**
**(c)** The bookkeeper debited the maintenance contract to the machinery account. Explain the effect on profit
for the year, ignoring depreciation, and name the concept breached. **[2]**

**6.** A sole trader has always used reducing balance depreciation for vehicles. Profit is low this year, so the
owner wants to switch to straight line, which gives a lower charge. Explain whether this is acceptable. **[4]**

**7.** A wholesaler has decided to stop trading in three months' time. Explain how this decision affects the
valuation of its non-current assets and inventory in its statement of financial position. **[4]**

**8.** A firm of architects with annual revenue of £3 million buys a £35 office kettle. Explain how it should be
recorded, and why a much larger purchase might be treated differently. **[3]**

**9.** On 20 December a furniture maker receives a £2 000 deposit for a table to be made and delivered in
February. The year end is 31 December. Explain how the deposit is treated in this year's financial statements.
**[3]**

**10.** Ridgeway Cycles has a draft profit for the year of £41 250. The following matters have not been dealt
with correctly.

(i) A workshop compressor costing £3 600 was bought during the year and debited to repairs. Equipment is
depreciated at 25% per annum on cost, with a full year's charge in the year of purchase.
(ii) Goods with a selling price of £4 800 were sent on sale or return and recorded as sales. The customer has not
yet accepted them. Mark-up is 60% on cost.
(iii) Closing inventory includes 30 helmets at their cost of £42 each. They now sell for £35 each, and selling
costs are £3 per helmet.
(iv) The owner's private mobile phone bill of £540 was paid from the business bank account and debited to
telephone expenses.
(v) Wages of £620 for the final week of the year have not been recorded.

Prepare a statement of corrected profit, stating the concept for each adjustment. **[12]**

**11.** The owner of a small retail business says: "Prudence makes accounts too pessimistic. I would rather
follow accruals and show what really happened." Evaluate the importance of the prudence concept when preparing
financial statements. **[8]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** Business entity [1]
**(b)** Money measurement [1]
**(c)** Cost [1]
**(d)** Duality [1]
*Examiner insight:* Give one concept per part; listing two usually earns nothing if either is wrong.

**2.** Prudence: inventory is not shown at more than it will realise, so foreseeable losses are recognised [1].
Cost: inventory is not shown above cost, because profit on unsold goods is not yet realised [1]. (Accept
accruals, matching the cost of unsold goods to the next period, as an alternative.)
*Examiner insight:* "State" needs only the names, but a brief reason removes any doubt about which concept you mean.

**3.** Knife sets: NRV = 36 − 8 = £28, lower than cost £30, so 50 × 28 = £1 400 [1]
Mixers: NRV = 110 − 30 = £80, lower than cost £90, so 12 × 80 = £960 [1]
Pans: NRV £40 is above cost £25, so 80 × 25 = £2 000; lower of cost and NRV applied to each item separately [1]
Total = 2 000 + 1 400 + 960 = **£4 360** [1]
*Examiner insight:* Comparing totals (cost £4 580, NRV £5 560) and using £4 580 loses the method marks; one wrong NRV still allows own-figure credit for the total.

**4. (a)** Goods not accepted = 7 200 − 2 400 = £4 800 at selling price; cost = 4 800 ÷ 1.2 = £4 000 [1]
Corrected profit = 29 600 − 4 800 + 4 000 = **£28 800** [1]
**(b)** Trade receivables **reduce by £4 800** [1]; closing inventory **increases by £4 000** [1]
**(c)** **Realisation**: revenue is not earned until the customer accepts the goods [1]
*Examiner insight:* The £2 400 already accepted is a genuine sale; removing all £7 200 loses the accuracy mark in (a), though (b) can still earn own-figure credit.

**5. (a)** 32 000 + 450 + 1 150 [1] = **£33 600** [1]
**(b)** 1 200 + 380 = **£1 580** [1]
**(c)** Expenses are understated by £1 200, so **profit is overstated by £1 200** [1]; the **accruals** concept
is breached because the cost of a service used this year has not been matched against this year's revenue [1].
*Examiner insight:* Delivery and installation are capital because they get the asset ready for use; that reason, not "it is expensive", earns credit.

**6.** The change is not acceptable [1]. The **consistency** concept requires the same depreciation method to be
used each year [1], so profits can be compared over time [1]. A method may be changed only if the new method gives
a fairer view of how the vehicles are used up, not to raise reported profit [1].
*Examiner insight:* A bare conclusion scores one mark; the rest need the concept and its purpose explained.

**7.** The **going concern** concept no longer applies [1]. Non-current assets should be shown at the amount they
are expected to realise on sale, not at cost less depreciation [1], which is likely to be lower in a forced
sale [1]. Inventory should be valued at what it is expected to raise in the closure, applying **prudence** where
this is below cost [1].
*Examiner insight:* Naming going concern earns one mark; the rest need what replaces cost less depreciation.

**8.** The kettle is charged to the income statement as an expense rather than capitalised and depreciated [1],
because £35 is **immaterial** to a firm of this size: no user's decision would change [1]. A purchase large enough
to affect users' decisions, such as a £12 000 server, would be capitalised and depreciated over its life [1].
*Examiner insight:* Link materiality to the firm's size; "it is cheap" alone earns at most one mark.

**9.** No revenue is recognised this year, because the table has not been made or delivered [1]; under
**realisation** revenue is earned when the goods pass to the customer [1]. The £2 000 is shown as a **current
liability** (payment received in advance, within other payables) in the statement of financial position [1].
*Examiner insight:* Without saying where the £2 000 appears in the statement of financial position, the final mark is lost.

**10.**

```
                                                    £
Draft profit for the year                        41 250
(i)   Add compressor wrongly charged to repairs    3 600
      Less depreciation 3 600 x 25%                (900)
(ii)  Less unrealised profit 4 800 - 3 000        (1 800)
(iii) Less inventory write-down 30 x (42 - 32)      (300)
(iv)  Add private phone bill (drawings)              540
(v)   Less wages accrued                            (620)
Corrected profit for the year                    41 770
```

(i) Add back £3 600, deduct depreciation £900 [1]; cost: capital items are recorded as assets at cost (accept accruals) [1]
(ii) Cost = 4 800 ÷ 1.6 = £3 000, so deduct £1 800 [1]; realisation: the goods are not sold until accepted [1]
(iii) NRV = 35 − 3 = £32, write-down 30 × 10 = £300 [1]; prudence (lower of cost and NRV) [1]
(iv) Add back £540 [1]; business entity: the owner's private expense is drawings [1]
(v) Deduct £620 [1]; accruals: the wages relate to this year [1]
Statement laid out from draft profit with each adjustment shown as an addition or deduction [1]
Corrected profit **£41 770** [1]
*Examiner insight:* Each adjustment has a figure mark and a concept mark, and the total is marked on own figures, so one slip costs one mark.

**11.** **For prudence:** it stops profit being overstated, so the owner does not take drawings from profit that may
never be earned [1]; lenders and suppliers can trust that inventory and trade receivables are not shown above
what they will realise [1]; recognising foreseeable losses early, such as a likely irrecoverable debt, warns
users sooner [1].
**Against excessive prudence:** overdone, it understates profit, which misleads as much as overstating [1];
low figures this year make next year look better, undermining comparison [1]; over-cautious provisions can be
used to smooth profits, an ethical concern [1].
**Link to accruals:** the two usually work together (depreciation, inventory); they conflict only when a loss is
foreseen, and then prudence normally takes priority [1].
**Judgement:** prudence matters because users need figures that are not overstated, but it should give a
realistic view, not a pessimistic one; it is not an alternative to accruals [1].
*Examiner insight:* A balanced list with no supported final judgement is usually capped below full marks.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using selling price as NRV without deducting costs to complete or sell.
- Valuing inventory on totals instead of item by item.
- Removing all sale or return goods when some have already been accepted.
- Adding sale or return goods back to inventory at selling price instead of cost.
- Forgetting depreciation when a wrongly expensed asset is capitalised.
- Treating a customer deposit as revenue when received.
