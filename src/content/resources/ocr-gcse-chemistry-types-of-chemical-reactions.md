## Oxidation and reduction by oxygen (C3.3a)

- **Oxidation** is the **gain of oxygen**.
- **Reduction** is the **loss of oxygen**.

The two always happen together, in a **redox** reaction.

Two more terms:

- An **oxidising agent** gives oxygen to another substance. It oxidises the other substance and is **itself reduced**.
- A **reducing agent** takes oxygen from another substance. It reduces the other substance and is **itself oxidised**.

### Worked example 1: extracting tin

Heating tin(IV) oxide with carbon gives tin:

SnO₂ + 2C → Sn + 2CO

1. Tin(IV) oxide, SnO₂, ends up as tin, Sn. It has **lost oxygen**, so **tin(IV) oxide is reduced**.
2. Carbon, C, ends up as carbon monoxide, CO. It has **gained oxygen**, so **carbon is oxidised**.
3. Carbon took the oxygen, so it is the **reducing agent**; tin(IV) oxide supplied it, so it is the **oxidising agent**.

Name the whole substance: "tin(IV) oxide is reduced", not "tin is reduced".

## Oxidation and reduction by electrons (C3.3b, Higher Tier only)

Everything here is **Higher Tier only**.

Many redox reactions involve no oxygen, so a wider definition uses electrons:

- **Oxidation**: electrons are **lost**.
- **Reduction**: electrons are **gained**.

**OIL RIG** (Oxidation Is Loss, Reduction Is Gain) helps you remember which is which. In electron terms, an oxidising agent **accepts** electrons (and is reduced), and a reducing agent **gives** electrons (and is oxidised).

### Worked example 2: zinc in silver nitrate solution

Zn + 2Ag⁺ → Zn²⁺ + 2Ag

Split the ionic equation into two half equations:

- Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻. Each zinc atom **loses two electrons**, so **zinc is oxidised**. Zinc is the reducing agent.
- 2Ag⁺ + 2e⁻ → 2Ag. Each silver ion **gains one electron**, so **silver ions are reduced**. Silver ions are the oxidising agent.

Check: electrons lost (2) = electrons gained (2).

The same idea covers a non-metal displacement such as Cl₂ + 2Br⁻ → 2Cl⁻ + Br₂. Chlorine gains electrons (reduced, the oxidising agent). Bromide ions lose electrons (oxidised).

## Acids and alkalis in water (C3.3c)

- **Acids** form **hydrogen ions, H⁺**, when they dissolve in water.
- Solutions of **alkalis** contain **hydroxide ions, OH⁻**.

- HNO₃ → H⁺ + NO₃⁻
- H₂SO₄ → 2H⁺ + SO₄²⁻
- KOH → K⁺ + OH⁻

The specification lists two misconceptions here. First, the H⁺ ions do **not** stay part of the acid molecule: in solution they are separate and move freely. Second, alkalis are **not** less corrosive than acids: concentrated sodium hydroxide solution damages skin and eyes badly. Wear eye protection with both.

## Neutralisation (C3.3d and C3.3e)

**Neutralisation** is an acid reacting with an alkali or a base to form a **salt plus water**. A **base** is a substance that neutralises an acid, such as a metal oxide or metal hydroxide. An **alkali** is a soluble base.

- acid + alkali: KOH + HNO₃ → KNO₃ + H₂O
- acid + base: MgO + H₂SO₄ → MgSO₄ + H₂O

The metal gives the first part of the salt's name; the acid gives the second:

| Acid | Salt ending | Example salt |
|---|---|---|
| Hydrochloric acid, HCl | chloride | potassium chloride, KCl |
| Nitric acid, HNO₃ | nitrate | potassium nitrate, KNO₃ |
| Sulfuric acid, H₂SO₄ | sulfate | magnesium sulfate, MgSO₄ |

### The ionic equation

In any aqueous neutralisation, the reaction that actually happens is:

**H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l)**

In KOH + HNO₃, the K⁺ and NO₃⁻ ions are unchanged, so they are left out. Every acid–alkali neutralisation in water reduces to this one equation.

## Acids with carbonates and metals (C3.3f)

Two general patterns:

- acid + metal carbonate → salt + water + carbon dioxide
- acid + metal → salt + hydrogen (only **some** metals; copper, silver and gold do not react with dilute acids)

### Worked example 3: writing balanced equations

**(a) Sodium carbonate with nitric acid.**

1. Products: sodium nitrate, water, carbon dioxide.
2. Formulae: Na₂CO₃ + HNO₃ → NaNO₃ + H₂O + CO₂
3. Two Na on the left, so put 2 in front of NaNO₃. That needs two NO₃, so put 2 in front of HNO₃.
4. **Na₂CO₃ + 2HNO₃ → 2NaNO₃ + H₂O + CO₂**. Check H: 2 = 2. Check O: 3 + 6 = 6 + 1 + 2 = 9.

**(b) Iron with dilute sulfuric acid**, forming iron(II) sulfate.

**Fe + H₂SO₄ → FeSO₄ + H₂**, balanced as written.

You will see fizzing in both.

## Making a pure, dry salt (PAG C7)

The specification's practical suggestion for C3.3d is the production of a pure dry sample of a salt (PAG C7). Here is the method for **magnesium sulfate** from magnesium oxide and dilute sulfuric acid:

1. Gently warm some dilute sulfuric acid in a beaker.
2. Add magnesium oxide powder a spatula at a time, stirring, until some solid stays undissolved. The **excess** base makes sure all the acid has reacted.
3. **Filter** to remove the leftover magnesium oxide. The filtrate is magnesium sulfate solution.
4. Boil away part of the water from the filtrate in an evaporating basin, stopping when crystals begin to appear at the edge.
5. Leave it to cool so crystals grow. Filter them off and **dry** them between sheets of filter paper or in a warm oven.

Excess solid can be filtered off; excess acid would stay in the solution and contaminate the salt.

## Dilute, concentrated, weak and strong (C3.3g, Higher Tier only)

Everything here is **Higher Tier only**. The specification flags a misconception: thinking strength and concentration mean the same thing.

- **Concentrated** and **dilute** describe the **amount of substance** of acid in a given volume of solution (the ratio of amount of acid to volume). Concentrated means a large amount per dm³; dilute means a small amount.
- **Strong** and **weak** describe the **degree of ionisation**. Hydrochloric, nitric and sulfuric acids are **strong**: in water every molecule ionises (**complete ionisation**). Ethanoic, citric and carbonic acids are **weak**: only some of their molecules ionise (**partial ionisation**).

A useful model: picture 400 molecules of a weak acid in water. If only 4 of them have split into ions, the degree of ionisation is 4 ÷ 400 × 100 = 1%. For a strong acid, every one of the 400 is ionised. So you can have a dilute strong acid or a concentrated weak acid.

### Worked example 4: comparing concentrations (CM3.3i)

Solution A has 0.40 mol of hydrochloric acid in 500 cm³. Solution B has 0.15 mol in 100 cm³. Which is more concentrated?

1. Convert volumes to dm³: 500 cm³ = 0.500 dm³; 100 cm³ = 0.100 dm³.
2. A: 0.40 ÷ 0.500 = **0.80 mol/dm³**.
3. B: 0.15 ÷ 0.100 = **1.5 mol/dm³**.
4. **B is more concentrated**, even though it contains less acid in total.

Estimate first (M1d): B has over a third as much acid in a fifth of the volume, so it should be more concentrated.

## pH and hydrogen ion concentration (C3.3h, C3.3i, C3.3j)

**C3.3h (both tiers):** relative acidity and alkalinity are measured by **pH**. The scale usually runs from 0 to 14. pH 7 is **neutral**. Below 7 is acidic: the lower the number, the more acidic. Above 7 is alkaline: the higher the number, the more alkaline.

**C3.3i and C3.3j are Higher Tier only.** A neutral solution has equal amounts of H⁺ and OH⁻. An acidic solution has more H⁺ than OH⁻. The **higher the hydrogen ion concentration, the lower the pH**. The specification works in whole-number pH values, and the rule is:

**Each time the H⁺ concentration increases by a factor of 10, the pH decreases by 1.**

### Worked example 5: factor of ten (Higher Tier only)

**(a)** A solution changes from pH 4 to pH 1. The pH has dropped by 3, so the H⁺ concentration has increased by 10 × 10 × 10 = **1000 times**.

**(b)** 20 cm³ of a strong acid at pH 2 is diluted with water to 2000 cm³. The volume is 100 times larger, so the H⁺ concentration is 100 times smaller. 100 = 10 × 10, so the pH rises by 2, to **pH 4**.

### pH titration curves (Higher Tier only)

When an alkali is added from a burette to an acid while a pH probe records the pH, the readings make a **titration curve**. These values are for 25.0 cm³ of a strong acid with a strong alkali of equal concentration:

| Alkali added (cm³) | 0 | 10 | 20 | 24 | 24.9 | 25.0 | 25.1 | 26 | 30 | 50 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| pH | 1 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 7 | 10 | 11 | 12 | 13 |

The pH rises **slowly** at first, because plenty of H⁺ remains. Near exact neutralisation it jumps **steeply** through pH 7 over a tiny volume. Then excess OH⁻ makes the solution alkaline and the pH **levels off** high. The middle of the steep part marks the volume that neutralises the acid, 25.0 cm³ here.

## Measuring pH (C3.3k, PAG C6)

The specification lists determining the pH of unknown solutions and use of pH probes as PAG C6 practical suggestions.

- **Universal indicator** (solution or paper): add a few drops, or dip the paper, and match the colour against the chart. Roughly: red for strong acid, orange and yellow for weaker acid, green at 7, blue for weak alkali, purple for strong alkali. Colours vary a little between brands, so always use the chart that comes with it. It gives only an approximate whole-number pH, and the colour judgement is subjective.
- **pH meter or pH probe**: calibrate it in buffer solutions of known pH, rinse the probe with distilled water between samples, dip it in and read the display. It gives a more precise value, works in coloured solutions, and with a data logger can record pH continuously during a titration.

## Common errors

- Calling the oxidising agent "oxidised". The oxidising agent gets **reduced**.
- Naming "tin" instead of "tin(IV) oxide" as the substance reduced.
- (Higher Tier only) Writing Zn + 2e⁻ → Zn²⁺. Electrons lost appear on the **right**.
- Leaving water or carbon dioxide out of acid + carbonate products.
- Using "weak" to mean "dilute" (Higher Tier only).
- Saying a fall of 2 in pH means twice as much H⁺. It is 100 times (Higher Tier only).
