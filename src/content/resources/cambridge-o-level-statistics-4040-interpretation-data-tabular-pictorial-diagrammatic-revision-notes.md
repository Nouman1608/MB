These notes condense section 2.4 of the Cambridge O Level Statistics (4040) syllabus for examination in 2025, 2026 and 2027: "Interpretation of data presented in tabular, pictorial or diagrammatic form". The worked examples are in the [interpretation study guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-statistics-4040-interpretation-data-tabular-pictorial-diagrammatic/), and the [interpretation practice questions](/resources/cambridge-o-level-statistics-4040-interpretation-data-tabular-pictorial-diagrammatic-practice/) give exam-style drill.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Interpret | Say what the figures mean in the context of the question |
| Compare | Make a statement about both groups together, using figures from each |
| Median | The middle value; the line inside the box of a box-and-whisker diagram |
| Interquartile range | Q3 − Q1; the width of the box; the spread of the middle half |
| Range | Maximum − minimum; affected by a single extreme value |
| Skewed | Values bunched at one end with a long tail towards the other |
| Proportion | A part as a fraction or percentage of its own group total |
| Association | Two things tend to occur together; not proof that one causes the other |

## Reading rules by form

| Form | To get a count | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| Table | Read the cell | Units; counts or percentages; which total |
| Pictogram | Symbols × key | Part-symbols |
| Pie chart | (angle ÷ 360) × total | Total may need finding from a known sector |
| Comparative pie charts | Total₂ = Total₁ × (r₂/r₁)² | Angles compare shares, not amounts |
| Bar or dual bar chart | Height from the scale | Scale steps; key |
| Sectional bar chart | Top reading − bottom reading | Do not read the top of a section as its size |
| Percentage bar chart | Percentage × group total | Total needed from elsewhere |
| Venn diagram | Add the regions named | "Only" against "both" against "at least one" |
| Stem-and-leaf | Count leaves | Key and units |
| Box-and-whisker | Quarters of the data | Shows quartiles, not the mean or n |

## Worked reminders

**Total from a known sector.** A 75° sector represents 30 people. Then 1° represents 0.4 people, and the total is 360 × 0.4 = 144.

**Sectional bar chart.** Section tops at 14, 31 and 50: sections of 14, 17 and 19.

**Box-and-whisker quarters.** If Q3 = 68 for 200 pupils, about 50 pupils scored more than 68.

**Comparing shares and amounts.** Chart A (total 900): sector 120° = 300 items, a share of 33.3%. Chart B (total 400): sector 162° = 180 items, a share of 45%. B has the larger share; A has the larger amount.

## Writing a comparison

1. Compare an **average** (usually the median): "The median for Group P (42) is higher than for Group Q (36)."
2. Compare a **spread** (usually the IQR): "Group Q is more consistent; its IQR is 8 against 15."
3. Put it in **context**: "So Group P scored higher on the whole, but Group Q's scores were more alike."

Use figures from both groups in each statement.

## Valid conclusions checklist

- Does the claim use proportions when the groups differ in size?
- Does it go beyond the sample to a wider population without justification?
- Does it claim cause from a diagram that shows only association?
- Does it treat an average as describing every individual?
- Does it rely on a range distorted by one extreme value?

## Exam traps

- Comparing two pie chart angles as if both charts had the same total.
- Quoting the top of a sectional bar as a section's size.
- Comparing only medians and ignoring spread.
- Saying "Group A is better" without saying in what way.
- Giving figures with no conclusion, or a conclusion with no figures.
- Saying a box-and-whisker diagram shows the mean.
- Treating "at least one" in a Venn diagram as "exactly one".

## Quick self-test

1. A 60° sector represents 25 cars. How many cars does the whole pie chart represent?
2. In a pie chart of 360 people, how many are shown by a 45° sector?
3. A sectional bar has section tops at 12, 27 and 40. How large is the middle section?
4. A percentage bar chart shows 15% of a group of 340. How many is that?
5. Comparative pie charts have radii 3 cm and 6 cm. The smaller represents 500. What does the larger represent?
6. Venn regions: A only 14, both 6, B only 11, neither 9. How many are in A?
7. Using question 6, how many are in at least one set?
8. A box-and-whisker diagram for 80 pupils has Q1 = 34. About how many pupils scored 34 or less?
9. Box P has IQR 12, box Q has IQR 20. Which data set is more consistent?
10. A stem-and-leaf diagram has 21 leaves. Which value is the median?
11. Why might the range be a poor measure of spread for a data set with one very large value?
12. A diagram shows that pupils who sleep more score higher. Can you conclude that sleep causes higher scores?

### Answers

1. 1° = 25/60 cars; total = 360 × 25/60 = 150 cars.
2. 45/360 × 360 = 45 people.
3. 27 − 12 = 15.
4. 0.15 × 340 = 51.
5. 500 × (6/3)² = 2000.
6. 14 + 6 = 20.
7. 14 + 6 + 11 = 31.
8. About 20 (a quarter of 80).
9. P, because its interquartile range is smaller.
10. The 11th value.
11. One extreme value makes the range large even if the other values are close together.
12. No. The diagram shows association only; other factors may explain it.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is it enough to quote the two medians?**
No. A comparison needs a statement linking them ("higher", "lower"), figures from both, and usually a spread comparison as well.

**How precise should values read from a diagram be?**
As precise as the scale allows. State that the value is read from the diagram if it is an estimate.
