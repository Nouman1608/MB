This guide covers section 11.3 of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Geography (9696) syllabus for examination in 2025 and 2026. The syllabus lists "factors affecting the location of manufacturing and related service industry (land, labour, capital, markets, materials, technology, economies and diseconomies of scale, inertia, transport, government policies)"; "industrial agglomeration, functional linkages, the industrial estate and the export processing zone (EPZ)"; and "the informal sector of manufacturing and services: causes, characteristics, location and impact". It is part of Topic 11, Production, location and change, in Paper 4.

The [Topic 11 study guide](/resources/a-level-geography-9696-production-location-and-change/) defines each factor and uses Sialkot, the Chittagong EPZ and Kenya's jua kali as examples. This page adds tools for analysing location (the material index and the location quotient), a classification of industries by their pull factors, and a different zone example. Condense it with the [11.3 revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-manufacturing-and-related-service-industry-revision-notes/) and test it with the [11.3 practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-manufacturing-and-related-service-industry-practice/).

## Classifying industries by their main pull

| Type | Main pull | Reason | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Material-oriented | Raw materials | Bulk-reducing: the product weighs much less than the inputs | Ore smelting, sugar refining, cement |
| Market-oriented | Consumers | Bulk-gaining or perishable: the product is heavier, bulkier or more fragile | Brewing and bottling, bakeries, furniture |
| Labour-oriented | Workforce | Labour is a large share of costs, or special skills are needed | Garment assembly; research and design |
| Footloose | Many locations possible | Light inputs and outputs, so transport costs matter little | Electronics assembly, software, many services |

The classification explains why the same factor matters differently across industries. In an exam, decide which type the industry in the question belongs to before ranking factors.

## Weber's material index

Alfred Weber measured the pull of materials with the **material index (MI)**:

MI = weight of localised materials ÷ weight of finished product

Localised materials are found only in some places (ore, coal); ubiquitous materials are available almost everywhere (water, air) and do not pull industry.

- **MI greater than 1:** the process loses weight, so the least-cost location is near the materials.
- **MI less than 1:** the product gains weight from ubiquitous materials, so the least-cost location is near the market.

### Worked example

*A fictional plant uses 3.2 tonnes of ore and 1.6 tonnes of coal to make 1 tonne of metal. A second plant uses 0.15 tonnes of malt and hops plus water to make 1 tonne of bottled drink.*

- Metal: MI = (3.2 + 1.6) ÷ 1 = **4.8**, so the plant is pulled to the materials.
- Drink: MI = 0.15 ÷ 1 = **0.15**, because water is ubiquitous; the plant is pulled to the market.

*Evaluation:* the index assumes transport cost depends only on weight and distance. In practice cost per tonne-km falls with distance and varies by mode; break-of-bulk points such as ports can be cheaper than either the material site or the market, which is why many steelworks were built on coasts to receive imported ore.

## The ten factors: points beyond definitions

- **Land:** large, flat, cheap sites with room to expand; greenfield sites are often on the urban edge, while brownfield sites reuse old industrial land.
- **Labour:** cost, skill, availability, productivity and labour relations all matter. Low wages are worthless if productivity is lower still.
- **Capital:** money is mobile, so capital rarely fixes a location directly, but access to investors, banks and state funds varies between regions.
- **Markets:** size and accessibility of the market; just-in-time supply chains pull suppliers close to assembly plants.
- **Materials:** strongest pull in bulk-reducing industries (see the MI above); weakening as inputs become lighter and more processed.
- **Technology:** containerisation cut the cost of moving goods; the internet allows design, finance and support services to sit far from factories.
- **Economies of scale:** internal economies (bulk buying, specialised machines) lower unit costs inside a firm; external economies come from location among other firms.
- **Diseconomies of scale:** congestion, high land prices and labour shortages push firms out of large agglomerations.
- **Inertia:** sunk investment, skilled labour and linkages hold firms in place after the original advantage has gone.
- **Transport:** cost, speed, reliability, and access to ports, airports and motorway junctions.
- **Government policies:** grants, tax relief, zones, tariffs, local-content rules, planning controls and infrastructure.

## Agglomeration and linkages

Agglomeration gives two kinds of external economy:

- **localisation economies**, from firms in the same industry clustering (shared suppliers, specialist labour, exchange of ideas);
- **urbanisation economies**, from locating in a large city (transport, banking, universities, a large market).

Clusters can grow by **cumulative causation**: new firms attract suppliers and workers, which attract more firms. Diseconomies eventually slow growth or push firms to the edge of the region.

**Functional linkages** for a car assembly plant:

- *backward:* tyres, seats, glass, electronics, steel pressings;
- *forward:* dealers, exporters, car-hire firms;
- *horizontal:* firms making different components for the same car;
- *service:* logistics, maintenance, testing, finance, design.

**Measuring clustering: the location quotient (LQ).** LQ = (share of a region's jobs in an industry) ÷ (share of national jobs in that industry). An LQ above 1 means the industry is more concentrated in the region than nationally.

*Example (fictional data):* electronics provides 12% of jobs in region R but 3% nationally, so LQ = 12 ÷ 3 = **4.0**, a strong cluster.

## Industrial estates and EPZs

| Feature | Industrial estate | Export processing zone |
|---|---|---|
| Purpose | Provide serviced sites for any firms | Attract export-oriented investment |
| Trade rules | Normal customs and tax rules | Duty-free import of inputs; output mainly exported |
| Incentives | Ready infrastructure, sometimes low rents | Tax holidays, simplified procedures, sometimes relaxed labour rules |
| Typical firms | Small and medium local firms, warehouses | Foreign-owned assembly plants |
| Linkages | Often strong with the local economy | Often weak, because most inputs are imported |

*Example:* Mexico's **maquiladora** programme, begun in 1965 along the border with the United States, allowed factories to import components duty-free, assemble them and export the finished goods, mostly to the United States. It brought large numbers of jobs to border cities such as Ciudad Juárez and Tijuana. Critics point to low wages, weak links to Mexican suppliers and dependence on demand in one market. A **special economic zone (SEZ)** is a wider version: China designated Shenzhen an SEZ in 1980, covering a whole city area rather than a fenced estate.

## The informal sector in manufacturing and services

The term was popularised in the early 1970s by Keith Hart's work in Ghana and an International Labour Organization report on Kenya.

- **Manufacturing examples:** metalworking, furniture, clothing, recycling plastic and metal into new goods.
- **Service examples:** street vending, transport (motorcycle taxis), repairs, domestic work, waste picking.
- **Links with the formal sector:** formal firms subcontract to home-based workers; informal traders sell factory goods. The two sectors are connected, not separate.
- **Location:** where customers and materials are, so near markets, transport terminals, roadsides, homes in informal settlements, and on waste sites.
- **Impact:** a source of jobs and incomes for migrants and the poor; cheap goods and services; recycling. But earnings are low and uncertain, work can be unsafe, and governments lose tax revenue.

## Annotated model answer

*Question: Explain why many industries are now described as footloose. [5]*

> A footloose industry is not tied to a particular location by materials or markets (**definition**). Its inputs and products are light and high in value, such as electronic components, so transport is a small share of costs (**weight and value**). Containerisation and air freight have lowered transport costs further (**technology**). Digital communications allow design and management to be separated from production (**ICT**). As a result, firms choose among many places, often on labour costs or government incentives (**consequence for location**).

## Common mistakes

- Treating all ten factors as equally important; rank them for the industry in the question.
- Writing that the MI is about volume or value; it is about weight.
- Confusing localisation and urbanisation economies.
- Describing an EPZ only as a fenced area; its defining features are trade and tax rules.
- Treating the informal sector as separate from the formal economy.
- Ignoring "related service industry", which the syllabus names.

## Exam technique

- **Explain** location: name the factor, give its effect, then link to the industry ("because the process loses weight, the plant is near the ore").
- **Suggest** from a map or table: use the resource and choose two or three factors that fit it.
- **Assess** or **Evaluate**: compare factors, and say how their importance has changed over time or differs between industries.
