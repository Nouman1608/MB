Condensed notes for section 6.4 (Business and ethical issues) of the Cambridge IGCSE Business 0264 syllabus for examination in 2027, 2028 and 2029, content statement 6.4.1 Ethical issues. A supply-chain example, a stakeholder table and a model answer are in the [business and ethical issues study guide](/resources/cambridge-igcse-business-0264-business-and-ethical-issues/). Test yourself with the [business and ethical issues practice questions](/resources/cambridge-igcse-business-0264-business-and-ethical-issues-practice/). The combined [ethical issues and pressure groups notes](/resources/igcse-business-0264-business-and-ethical-issues-and-pressure-groups-revision-notes/) also cover section 6.5.

All examples are invented. Money is in dollars.

## Syllabus points

- Ethical issues which may affect businesses, e.g. child labour, paying fair wages to employees, paying fair prices to suppliers, using suppliers who do not damage the environment.
- How businesses may respond to ethical issues.
- Advantages and disadvantages of a business being ethical.

## Key ideas

- **Ethics:** moral principles about right and wrong.
- **Ethical decision:** doing what is morally right, even when a cheaper or more profitable option exists.
- **Ethics vs law:** the law sets a minimum, with penalties; ethics goes beyond it, by choice.
- A business can face ethical issues through its **suppliers**, not just its own actions.

## The four named issues

| Issue | What it involves | Who is affected |
|---|---|---|
| Child labour | Children doing harmful work or kept out of school, often in a supplier's workplace | Children and families; the business's reputation |
| Fair wages to employees | Paying enough to live on, not just the lowest possible | Employees; owners (costs); customers (prices) |
| Fair prices to suppliers | Paying prices that cover a supplier's costs, especially small suppliers | Suppliers and their workers; owners |
| Suppliers who do not damage the environment | Choosing suppliers that avoid pollution and waste | Communities near suppliers; future generations |

## How businesses respond

| Response | Effect |
|---|---|
| Obey the law only | Lowest cost; open to criticism |
| Ethical policy | Clear rules for managers and staff |
| Inspect suppliers | Finds problems; costs time and money |
| Require suppliers to improve, with deadlines | Keeps supply; may improve workers' lives; supplier may not change |
| Switch suppliers or materials | Quick break with the problem; often higher cost |
| Tell customers | May win sales; claims must be true |

Note: dropping a supplier that uses child labour may not help the children; requiring change can be an ethical alternative.

## Advantages of being ethical

- Better reputation and brand image.
- May attract customers who care how products are made; possibly higher prices.
- More loyal customers.
- Easier to recruit and keep motivated employees.
- More reliable, cooperative suppliers.
- Less risk of scandal, boycotts and campaigns against the business.

## Disadvantages

- Higher costs: wages, supplier prices, inspections.
- Lower profit unless prices rise; higher prices may lose price-sensitive customers.
- Less ethical competitors may undercut on price.
- Benefits can be slow and hard to measure; costs are certain and continue each year.
- False or exaggerated ethical claims damage reputation.

## Calculation pattern

**Extra yearly cost** = extra cost per unit x units per year.
**Effect on profit** = old profit - extra cost (if prices do not change).

Worked reminder: a café pays $0.40 more per kilogram for fairly traded coffee and uses 2,500 kg a year.

```
0.40 x 2,500 = $1,000 extra a year
```

If its profit was $26,000, it becomes 26,000 - 1,000 = $25,000.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Ethics | Moral principles about what is right and wrong |
| Ethical issue | A situation where a business must choose between what is morally right and what is cheaper or more profitable |
| Child labour | Employing children in work that harms them or stops their schooling |
| Fair wage | A wage that reflects the work and lets the employee live decently |
| Fair price | A price that covers a supplier's costs and lets it pay its workers properly |
| Supply chain | The businesses that provide the materials and products a business sells |
| Ethical policy | A written statement of how a business will behave on ethical issues |

## Exam traps

- "Ethical" does not mean "legal". The law is the minimum.
- Say *how* reputation leads to sales or loyalty; "good image" alone is weak.
- Name a stakeholder and the effect on them.
- Costs of being ethical usually continue every year.
- In "consider" questions, weigh both sides using the case and decide.

## Quick self-test

1. Define an ethical decision.
2. Give two examples of ethical issues named in the syllabus.
3. Explain the difference between a legal requirement and an ethical choice.
4. How can a retailer be linked to child labour without employing children?
5. Give two ways a business could respond to an ethical issue.
6. State two advantages of a business being ethical.
7. State two disadvantages of a business being ethical.
8. A firm pays $0.25 more per unit to an ethical supplier on 80,000 units. Calculate the extra yearly cost.
9. Using Question 8, profit was $150,000. What is profit if prices do not change?
10. Which stakeholders gain from fair prices to suppliers?
11. Why might dropping a supplier that uses child labour not help the children?
12. Why may a business with price-sensitive customers find being ethical harder?

### Answers

1. A decision based on what is morally right, even when a cheaper or more profitable option is available.
2. Any two: child labour; fair wages to employees; fair prices to suppliers; using suppliers who do not damage the environment.
3. A legal requirement must be followed or the business faces penalties; an ethical choice goes beyond the law and is made voluntarily.
4. One of its suppliers, or a supplier's supplier, may use child labour to make products the retailer sells.
5. Any two: write an ethical policy; inspect suppliers; require improvement; switch suppliers; tell customers; respond to concerns.
6. Any two: better reputation; more customers or loyalty; motivated staff; reliable suppliers; less risk of boycotts.
7. Any two: higher costs; lower profit; higher prices may lose customers; competitors may undercut; slow benefits.
8. 0.25 x 80,000 = $20,000.
9. 150,000 - 20,000 = $130,000.
10. The supplier and its workers, and possibly the business through more reliable supply.
11. The children may lose their income and move to other, possibly worse, work.
12. Its higher costs may force price rises, and its customers may switch to cheaper rivals.
