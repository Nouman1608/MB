## What 3.3.14 asks of you

| Outcome | In short |
|---|---|
| 1 | Explain the aim of processes with **no solvent** and **non-hazardous starting materials** |
| 2 | Explain the aim of **fewer steps** and **high percentage atom economy** |
| 3 | Plan a route of **four steps or fewer**, built only from **9620 reactions** |

No required practical is listed under 3.3.14. Required practical 4 (distil a product, 3.3.5.2) and
Required practical 10 (make an organic solid and check how pure it is, 3.3.9.2) are the practical
skills a synthesis draws on.

## Key formulas

| Quantity | Formula |
|---|---|
| % atom economy | Mr of the wanted product × 100, divided by the total Mr of all reactants |
| % yield | actual amount of product ÷ theoretical maximum × 100 |
| Overall yield | step yields as decimals multiplied together, × 100 |

## Outcome 1 in bullet points

**No solvent**
- No energy or raw materials spent making and transporting it.
- No separation step (often distillation) to remove it from the product.
- No solvent waste and no recovery plant.
- No flammable or toxic vapour to leak.

**Non-hazardous starting materials**
- Less spent on sealed storage, transport rules, protective kit, training and emergency plans.
- Lower risk to workers, neighbours and the environment if something goes wrong.
- 9620 hazards to quote: KCN (very toxic; acid releases HCN gas), epoxyethane (extremely flammable,
  explosive with air, toxic, carcinogenic), chlorine and (chloromethyl)benzene (toxic, irritant).
- Aspirin: ethanoic anhydride is chosen over ethanoyl chloride because it is cheaper, less corrosive, less
  violent with water and releases no HCl.

## Outcome 2 in bullet points

- Every step loses some product, so the overall yield drops with each step added.
- Every step needs its own reagents, energy, separation, time and waste disposal.
- High atom economy: most reactant atoms end up in the product, so less by-product and less waste.
- Addition = 100% atom economy. Substitution and elimination are always below 100%.
- A useful by-product (one that can be sold) softens a low atom economy.

## Must-know distinctions

| Pair | The difference |
|---|---|
| Atom economy vs yield | Atom economy is calculated from the equation; yield from the mass you collect |
| Cl₂ + UV on benzene vs on methylbenzene | Benzene: addition to the ring. Methylbenzene: substitution on the side chain |
| Ring Cl vs side-chain Cl | Ring Cl is not replaced by nucleophiles; side-chain Cl reacts like a halogenoalkane |
| NaOH(aq) vs KOH in ethanol | Aqueous favours substitution (alcohol); ethanolic favours elimination (alkene) |
| Excess NH₃ vs excess halogenoalkane | Excess NH₃ gives mainly the primary amine; otherwise further substitution follows |
| Nitrile reduction vs NH₃ route to an amine | Nitrile route adds a carbon and gives one amine only |

## Carbon–carbon bond forming steps

Outside polymerisation, four reactions in 9620 join carbon to carbon:

1. Halogenoalkane + KCN (nitrile).
2. Aldehyde or ketone + KCN, then dilute acid (hydroxynitrile).
3. Friedel–Crafts acylation (acyl chloride + AlCl₃).
4. Friedel–Crafts alkylation (chloroalkane + AlCl₃).

## Not in the 9620 content

- Steam hydration of an alkene.
- Turning an alcohol directly into a halogenoalkane.
- Making an acyl chloride or an acid anhydride.
- Electrophilic substitution beyond a single substituent (the specification limits this to
  monosubstitution).

A route that needs one of these is not acceptable as a 9620 synthesis.

## Method in steps

```
1  Draw start and target; mark groups; count carbons.
2  More carbons in the target? Choose a C–C step from the list.
3  Work back from the target one reaction at a time.
4  Check the position of each group (major product, ring vs side chain).
5  Each step: reagent + conditions + product name or structure.
6  Count steps (no more than four) and check every reaction is in 9620.
```

## Worked reminders

**Atom economy of a cyanide step.** Relative atomic masses: C = 12.0, H = 1.0, K = 39.1, N = 14.0, Cl = 35.5.

```
C₆H₅CH₂Cl + KCN → C₆H₅CH₂CN + KCl
Sum of reactants = 126.5 + 65.1 = 191.6
% atom economy  = 117.0 ÷ 191.6 × 100 = 61.1%
```

**Spot the broken route.** A student plans: benzene + Cl₂ in UV light → chlorobenzene → warm NaOH(aq)
→ an alcohol. Both steps fail. UV light makes chlorine **add** to benzene, giving C₆H₆Cl₆, not
chlorobenzene. And even if chlorobenzene were available, the ring C–Cl bond is strengthened by overlap
of a chlorine lone pair with the delocalised ring, so hydroxide does not substitute it. A workable 9620
alternative to put an OH group near a ring is methylbenzene + Cl₂ (UV), then NaOH(aq), which gives
phenylmethanol, C₆H₅CH₂OH.

## Quick self-test

1. State two benefits of a process that uses no solvent.
2. Give one reason for avoiding hazardous starting materials, with a 9620 example of such a material.
3. Calculate the % atom economy for making ethylamine: CH₃CH₂Cl + 2NH₃ → CH₃CH₂NH₂ + NH₄Cl.
   (Relative atomic masses: C = 12.0, H = 1.0, N = 14.0, Cl = 35.5)
4. A route has three steps with yields of 92%, 88% and 61%. Calculate the overall yield.
5. Route P has two steps (80% and 55%). Route Q has one step (50%). Which gives more product?
6. Name the four reactions in 9620, other than polymerisation, that form a new C–C bond.
7. Give the reagent and condition to convert methylbenzene into (chloromethyl)benzene.
8. Give the reagent and catalyst to make ethylbenzene from benzene.
9. State the reagent that converts epoxyethane into ethane-1,2-diol.
10. Give the reagent that converts a ketone into a secondary alcohol.

### Answers

1. Any two: no energy to make, transport or remove it; no solvent waste; no flammable or toxic vapour.
2. Hazardous materials need costly controls and put people at risk; for example KCN, which is very toxic.
3. Sum = 64.5 + 2(17.0) = 98.5; % atom economy = 45.0 ÷ 98.5 × 100 = **45.7%**.
4. 0.92 × 0.88 × 0.61 = 0.4939, so **49.4%**.
5. Route P: 0.80 × 0.55 = 0.44, so 44%. **Route Q** (50%) gives more, and with fewer steps.
6. Halogenoalkane + KCN; aldehyde or ketone + KCN then dilute acid; Friedel–Crafts acylation;
   Friedel–Crafts alkylation.
7. **Chlorine**, in **UV light**.
8. **Chloroethane**, with an **AlCl₃** catalyst.
9. **Water**.
10. **NaBH₄** in aqueous solution.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using a reaction that is not in 9620, such as steam hydration or alcohol to halogenoalkane.
- Writing AlCl₃ when the side chain of methylbenzene is to be chlorinated; UV light is needed.
- Planning substitution of a chlorine atom attached directly to a benzene ring.
- Multiplying step yields as percentages (80 × 55) instead of decimals, or adding them.
- Dropping a coefficient from the atom economy denominator, such as the 2 in 2NH₃.
- Reagent with no condition: "KOH" alone does not show whether substitution or elimination is wanted.
- Ignoring the major product: HBr on propene gives mainly 2-bromopropane, not 1-bromopropane.
- Giving "safer" and "less dangerous" as two separate reasons; they make one point.
- Forgetting that KCN with an aldehyde or unsymmetrical ketone gives a racemic mixture.
