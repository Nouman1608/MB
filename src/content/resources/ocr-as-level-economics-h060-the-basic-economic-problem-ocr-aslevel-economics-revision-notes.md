## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Scarcity | Wants exceed the resources available to satisfy them |
| Basic economic problem | Scarcity forces choices about what to produce, how, and for whom |
| Need | Something required for survival or to avoid serious harm |
| Want | Something desired beyond a need; wants are unlimited |
| Economic good | A good that uses scarce resources to produce, so it has an opportunity cost |
| Free good | A good available without using scarce resources, with no opportunity cost |
| Positive statement | A statement that can be tested against evidence |
| Normative statement | A statement containing a value judgement about what ought to be |
| Economic agents | Households, firms and government: the decision-makers in an economy |
| Factors of production | Land, labour, capital and enterprise |
| Factor rewards | Rent, wages, interest and profit, in the same order |
| Sustainability | Meeting present needs without reducing future generations' ability to meet theirs |

## Condensed recall

**Scarcity**

- Permanent and universal: rich and poor economies both face it.
- Not the same as a shortage, which is a temporary excess of demand over supply at a given price.
- Applies to time as well as goods: an hour spent on one task cannot be spent on another.

**The three questions**

- What to produce: which goods, in what quantities.
- How to produce: which combination of factors and techniques.
- For whom: how output is shared.

**Goods**

- Classify by resource use, not by price.
- "Free at the point of use" goods (public library loans, state schooling) are economic goods.
- A free good can become an economic good when it has to be collected, cleaned or transported.

**Statements**

- Positive: testable; can be false and still positive.
- Normative: contains a judgement; look for "should", "fair", "too much", "deserve", but test the whole sentence.
- Policy uses both: positive analysis predicts effects; normative judgement chooses between them.

**Agents and factors**

- Households supply factors and consume.
- Firms combine factors to produce.
- Government taxes, spends, provides some goods and sets rules.
- Capital is man-made equipment, not money.
- Enterprise organises the other factors and bears risk; its reward is profit.

**Sustainability**

- Using a resource faster than it renews raises future scarcity.
- Example: a water store of 900 million cubic metres with recharge of 30 million and extraction of 45 million a year falls by 15 million a year and lasts 60 years.
- Responses (charges, quotas, new technology) all carry present costs.

## Classification drill

| Item | Answer |
|---|---|
| Air in an open field | Free good |
| Oxygen in a hospital cylinder | Economic good |
| A council park with free entry | Economic good |
| Seawater for a sailor at sea | Free good |
| "Taxes on sugar reduce consumption" | Positive |
| "Sugar should be taxed more heavily" | Normative |
| A pizza oven in a restaurant | Capital |
| A chef's skill | Labour |
| The plot the restaurant stands on | Land |

## Exam traps

- Defining scarcity as "a lack of goods" without the comparison between wants and resources.
- Calling something free because no price is charged.
- Calling a positive statement normative because it seems wrong.
- Writing that money is capital.
- Giving the factor of production when the question asks for its reward.
- Mentioning sustainability without linking it to future choices.
- In multiple-choice questions, picking "can be proven true" for a positive statement; the test is whether it can be checked, not whether it is true.

## Linking to later topics

- Opportunity cost is the cost of each choice scarcity forces.
- The market economy and the allocation of resources are ways of answering the three questions.
- Market failure and sustainability return in the externalities topic.

## Self-test

1. Define scarcity.
2. How does scarcity differ from a shortage?
3. State the three questions every economy must answer.
4. Is a free school meal an economic good or a free good? Why?
5. Give one example of a free good.
6. Is "Raising the minimum wage will reduce youth employment" positive or normative?
7. Is "The minimum wage is too low" positive or normative?
8. Name the reward to capital.
9. Which factor of production organises the others and bears risk?
10. Why is money not a factor of production?
11. Define sustainability.
12. A forest grows by 2,000 tonnes of timber a year and loggers cut 3,500 tonnes. Is this sustainable?

### Answers

1. The situation in which wants exceed the resources available to satisfy them.
2. Scarcity is permanent and universal; a shortage is a temporary market situation where quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied at a given price.
3. What to produce, how to produce it, and for whom.
4. An economic good: food, cooks and kitchens use scarce resources, even though the pupil pays nothing.
5. Air in an open field, or seawater far out at sea.
6. Positive: it is a prediction that evidence can test.
7. Normative: "too low" is a value judgement.
8. Interest.
9. Enterprise.
10. Money is used to buy capital goods; it does not itself produce anything.
11. Meeting present needs without reducing the ability of future generations to meet theirs.
12. No: the stock falls by 1,500 tonnes a year, so future supply is reduced.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can a statement be positive if it turns out to be wrong?**
Yes. A positive statement is one that can be tested; being false does not change its type.

**Are needs the same in every country?**
No. The boundary between needs and wants changes with time, place and the way people earn a living.
