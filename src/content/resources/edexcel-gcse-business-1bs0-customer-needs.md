## What 1.2.1 asks you to know

The specification heading is "Identifying and understanding customer needs". It lists:

| Strand | Specification wording |
|---|---|
| What customer needs are | price, quality, choice, convenience |
| Why identifying and understanding customers matters | generating sales, business survival |

## Part 1: the four needs, looked at closely

### Price
Customers want to pay a price they think is fair for what they receive. That is not always the lowest price. A customer buying everyday items may want the cheapest option, while a customer buying a wedding cake may accept a high price if the quality is right. The question to ask is: how sensitive are these customers to price?

### Quality
Quality means the product or service meets or beats what the customer expects. Expectations depend on the price and the situation: a £2 umbrella is not expected to last as long as a £40 one. For a service, quality includes reliability, skill and how staff treat customers.

### Choice
Customers want a range to choose from: sizes, flavours, colours, times or options. Choice helps a business serve customers whose tastes differ. Too much choice raises costs, because more stock must be held and more items may go unsold.

### Convenience
Customers want buying to be easy: a nearby location, long opening hours, quick service, online ordering, delivery or simple payment. Convenience often matters most when customers are short of time.

## Part 2: needs differ between customers and situations

The same product can be bought for different reasons by different people. Even one customer can have different needs at different times: a parent buying a quick weekday dinner wants convenience and a low price; the same parent buying for a birthday wants quality and choice. A business that understands this can target the right needs at the right time.

### Worked example: Gannet Bay Fish Bar

Gannet Bay Fish Bar is in a seaside town. The owner asks 250 customers which need matters most to them: 150 are local residents and 100 are holiday visitors.

| Need | Locals | % of locals | Visitors | % of visitors | All customers | % of all |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Price | 66 | 44% | 12 | 12% | 78 | 31.2% |
| Quality | 39 | 26% | 41 | 41% | 80 | 32.0% |
| Choice | 15 | 10% | 9 | 9% | 24 | 9.6% |
| Convenience | 30 | 20% | 38 | 38% | 68 | 27.2% |
| Total | 150 | 100% | 100 | 100% | 250 | 100% |

Working for one cell: locals choosing price = 66 ÷ 150 × 100 = 44%.

**What the data shows.**
- Locals care most about price (44%). Visitors care least about price (12%) and most about quality (41%) and convenience (38%).
- The "all customers" column suggests price and quality matter equally (31.2% and 32.0%). That hides the real story, because the two groups want different things.
- Choice is low for both groups, so a short menu is unlikely to lose sales.

**What the owner could do.** In winter, when most customers are locals, a lower-priced meal deal could protect sales. In summer, when visitors arrive, the owner could stress fresh, locally caught fish (quality) and add a quick takeaway hatch (convenience). The data cannot prove these changes will work, but it gives evidence for them.

## Part 3: trade-offs between needs

A small business rarely leads on all four needs at once, because meeting one often costs money that pushes up price.

| Improving this need | Usually means | Which can conflict with |
|---|---|---|
| Quality | Better materials or more skilled staff | Low price |
| Choice | More stock and more waste | Low price |
| Convenience | Longer hours, delivery, more staff | Low price |
| Low price | Cheaper inputs, fewer staff | Quality, choice, convenience |

So the decision is about which needs this business's customers value most. Strong answers name the trade-off and say why the chosen need suits the customers in the case.

## Part 4: why identifying and understanding customers matters

### Generating sales
A business that knows its customers' needs can offer what they want, at a suitable price, in a convenient way. Customers are more likely to buy, to come back and to recommend the business to others. It also avoids wasting money on products or features customers do not value.

### Business survival
New and small businesses have limited cash. If they misjudge needs, unsold stock and low sales can quickly turn into losses and closure. Needs also change over time (linking to 1.1.1, The dynamic nature of business), so a business that keeps checking what customers want can adapt before sales fall. Understanding customers is not a one-off task at start-up.

## Annotated model answer

**Question (Analyse):** Analyse the importance to Gannet Bay Fish Bar of identifying and understanding its customers' needs. **[6]**

> The survey shows locals and visitors want different things: 44% of locals put price first, but only 12% of visitors do (evidence from the case). If the owner set one price all year, she could lose local customers in winter when visitors are few (consequence). By offering a cheaper meal deal in winter she could keep locals buying, which protects sales in the quiet season (development). Visitors care more about quality and convenience (evidence). A takeaway hatch in summer could serve more visitors in less time, generating extra sales when demand is highest (development). Understanding both groups therefore helps the fish bar survive the quiet months and earn more in the busy ones (link to the specification's two reasons).

## Common mistakes

- Listing all four needs instead of identifying which matters most to the customers in the case.
- Treating "price" as always meaning "cheapest".
- Using the combined figure when the case gives separate groups.
- Forgetting that meeting one need can raise costs and price.
- Writing "it helps sales" with no chain of reasoning.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Identify** a need from a table: pick the figure, quote it.
- **Calculate** percentages from survey counts, showing the division.
- **Outline** one need in context: the need, then why it matters to these customers.
- **Analyse:** use data or case details, then follow through to sales or survival.
- **Justify:** choose between two changes, such as a price cut or a delivery service, using which need the customers rank highest.
