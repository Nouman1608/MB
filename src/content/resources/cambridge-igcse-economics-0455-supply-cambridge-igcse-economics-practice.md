These questions cover section 2.4 (supply) of the Cambridge IGCSE Economics 0455 syllabus for examination in 2026. All firms, products and figures are invented. Revise first with the [supply study guide](/resources/cambridge-igcse-economics-0455-supply-cambridge-igcse-economics/) and the [supply revision notes](/resources/cambridge-igcse-economics-0455-supply-cambridge-igcse-economics-revision-notes/).

## Section A: Short-answer questions

**1.** Which of the following would cause a contraction in the supply of cotton shirts? **[1]**

A: A rise in the wages of textile workers

B: A fall in the price of cotton shirts

C: A new tax on cotton shirts

D: A new, faster sewing machine

**2.** A government introduces a subsidy for producers of bicycles. What happens to the supply curve for bicycles? **[1]**

A: It shifts to the left

B: It shifts to the right

C: There is a movement up the curve

D: There is a movement down the curve

**3.** Define supply. **[2]**

**4.** Distinguish between a contraction in supply and a decrease in supply. **[2]**

**5.** Two firms supply olive oil. At a price of 8 per litre, Firm M supplies 300 litres and Firm N supplies 450 litres. At a price of 10, Firm M supplies 380 litres and Firm N supplies 520 litres.

**(a)** Calculate market supply at each price. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the percentage extension in market supply when the price rises from 8 to 10. **[2]**

**6.** State **two** reasons why a supply curve slopes upward. **[2]**

## Section B: Structured questions

**7.** A firm supplies 600 units at a price of 15 and 500 units at a price of 12. The government places an indirect tax of 3 per unit on the product.

**(a)** State the price the firm now needs to supply 600 units. **[1]**

**(b)** State the quantity the firm now supplies at a price of 15. **[1]**

**(c)** Describe how the supply curve has moved. **[2]**

**8.** Explain, using a supply diagram, the effect of a rise in the price of fertiliser on the supply of rice. **[5]**

**9.** Explain how new technology can increase the supply of a product. **[3]**

**10.** Farmers in a region can grow either coffee or tea on the same land. The world price of tea rises sharply. Analyse the effect on the supply of coffee from the region. **[4]**

**11.** Explain **two** natural events that could decrease the supply of fish, and say why each has this effect. **[4]**

**12.** Discuss whether a subsidy is the best way for a government to increase the supply of electric buses. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** **B** [1]. Only a fall in the product's own price causes a contraction; A and C decrease supply, and D increases it. **[1]**

**2.** **B** [1]. A subsidy reduces producers' costs, so more is supplied at every price. **[1]**

**3.** The willingness and ability of producers to sell a product [1] at a given price over a given period of time [1]. **[2]**

**4.** A contraction is a fall in quantity supplied caused by a fall in the product's own price: a movement down the supply curve [1]. A decrease is a fall in supply at every price caused by a change in a condition of supply, such as higher costs: a shift of the curve to the left [1]. **[2]**

**5. (a)** At 8: 300 + 450 = **750 litres** [1]; at 10: 380 + 520 = **900 litres** [1].

**(b)** Extension = 900 − 750 = 150 [1]; 150 ÷ 750 × 100 = **20%** [1]. **[4]**

**6.** Any two: a higher price raises the profit on each unit [1]; a higher price covers the higher costs of producing extra units, or attracts new firms into the market [1]. **[2]**

**7. (a)** 15 + 3 = **18** [1].

**(b)** **500 units**, the amount it used to supply at a price of 12 [1].

**(c)** The supply curve shifts to the left (upward by 3 at each quantity) [1], so supply has decreased: less is supplied at every price [1]. **[4]**

**8.** Diagram with price on the vertical axis and quantity on the horizontal axis, original supply curve S1 [1] and new curve S2 to the left [1]. Fertiliser is a cost of producing rice [1], so a rise in its price reduces the profit on each unit and farmers are willing to supply less at every price [1]; at the original price, quantity supplied falls from Q1 to Q2, shown on the diagram [1]. **[5]**

**9.** New technology raises the output from the same inputs [1], which lowers the cost of producing each unit [1], so producers are willing and able to supply more at every price and the supply curve shifts right [1]. **[3]**

**10.** Tea becomes more profitable than coffee [1], so some farmers switch their land and workers from coffee to tea [1]. At every price of coffee, less is now supplied [1], so the supply of coffee decreases and its supply curve shifts to the left [1]. **[4]**

**11.** Any two developed events. Storms or rough seas [1] keep fishing boats in port, so fewer fish can be caught and sold at every price [1]. Disease among fish stocks, or a rise in sea temperature that drives fish away [1], reduces the number of fish available to catch, so supply decreases [1]. **[4]**

**12.** For a subsidy: it lowers producers' costs, so more buses are supplied at every price [1]; it acts quickly on existing manufacturers [1]. Against: it costs the government money that has an opportunity cost, such as spending on schools [1]; firms may keep part of the subsidy as profit rather than expanding output [1]; other measures, such as funding research into battery technology or removing taxes on bus parts, might increase supply more cheaply over time [1]. Conclusion: a subsidy is likely to work fastest, but whether it is best depends on its cost and on whether producers can expand output; combining it with support for technology may be more effective [1]. **[6]**

*Examiner insight:* A strong "Discuss" answer compares the named policy with at least one alternative and reaches a reasoned conclusion.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calling a price-caused change an "increase" or "decrease" in supply.
- Shifting supply right for a tax, or left for a subsidy.
- Diagrams without S1, S2 and the original and new quantities.
- Saying "costs rise" without explaining the link to profit and willingness to supply.
- In Question 10, shifting the coffee curve because of a change in coffee demand rather than the price of tea.
