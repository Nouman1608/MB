## What the specification asks for

| Statement | What you must do |
|---|---|
| Explain what is meant by monetary policy and how it can be used to achieve economic objectives | Define it and link interest rate changes to objectives |
| Analyse how monetary policy can affect growth, employment and price stability | Build chains of reasoning to each objective |
| Evaluate the effects of monetary policy on consumer spending, borrowing, saving and investment | Weigh how strong each effect is and what it depends on |

## What monetary policy is, and who runs it

**Monetary policy** is the use of interest rates, and sometimes the supply of money, to influence spending in the economy. It is run by a country's **central bank**.

In the UK, the **Bank of England** sets the key interest rate, **Bank Rate**. Its Monetary Policy Committee has been independent of the government in setting rates since 1997. The government sets the target, **2% CPI inflation**, and the Bank decides how to meet it. Independence means decisions are not timed to win elections, which helps people believe inflation will stay low.

## From Bank Rate to the high street

1. The Bank of England changes Bank Rate.
2. Commercial banks and building societies change the rates they charge on loans and mortgages and pay on savings.
3. Households and firms change how much they borrow, save, spend and invest.
4. Total demand in the economy changes.
5. Output, employment and prices respond.

Steps 2 and 3 are where policy can be weakened. Lenders do not always pass on the full change, and many borrowers are on fixed rates for several years.

## Effects on the objectives

| | Rate rise (contractionary) | Rate cut (expansionary) |
|---|---|---|
| Growth | Less spending and investment, so output grows more slowly | More spending and investment, so output grows faster |
| Employment | Firms need fewer workers, so unemployment may rise | Firms hire more, so unemployment falls |
| Price stability | Lower demand eases demand-pull inflation | Higher demand can push inflation up |

The table shows the central trade-off: a rise that brings inflation down may cost growth and jobs; a cut that helps jobs may raise inflation. The central bank has to judge which risk is greater.

**Cost-push inflation is harder.** If inflation comes from a rise in world energy prices, higher rates cannot lower the price of oil. They can only reduce demand elsewhere, which slows growth, so the central bank may accept inflation above target for a while.

## Worked example 1: effect across the economy

In Country M, 2 million households have variable-rate mortgages averaging £150,000. The central bank raises rates by 1 percentage point, and lenders pass it on in full.

- Extra interest per household = 150,000 × 0.01 = £1,500 a year, or **£125 a month**.
- Across the economy = 2 million × £1,500 = **£3bn a year** less to spend on other things.

The £3bn falls on variable-rate borrowers alone. Households on fixed rates feel the rise only when their deal ends, so the full effect on spending builds up over several years.

## Worked example 2: investment

A firm can borrow £500,000 to expand and expects a return of 5.5% a year, £27,500.

| Interest rate | Yearly interest | Gain after interest | Decision |
|---|---|---|---|
| 6.0% | £30,000 | −£2,500 | Do not invest |
| 4.5% | £22,500 | +£5,000 | Invest |

A cut from 6.0% to 4.5% turns this project from loss-making into profitable. Across many firms, more projects clear the cost of borrowing, so investment rises. But if the firm expects sales to fall, it may lower its expected return and still not invest.

## Worked example 3: savers when rates and inflation differ

Bank Rate is 5%, a savings account pays 4%, and inflation is 6%. The real return is 1.04 ÷ 1.06 − 1 = **−1.9%**. Even after a series of rate rises, savers can lose purchasing power if inflation is higher still.

## Evaluating effects on spending, borrowing, saving and investment

| Effect | What makes it stronger | What makes it weaker |
|---|---|---|
| Consumer spending | High household debt on variable rates; a large rate change | Most mortgages fixed; strong confidence or wage growth |
| Borrowing | Credit-funded purchases such as cars and furniture | Essential borrowing that cannot be put off |
| Saving | Savings rates follow Bank Rate closely | Households already saving little; banks slow to raise savings rates |
| Investment | Projects funded by loans; firms confident about demand | Firms funding projects from profits; weak expected sales |

**Further limits:**

- **Time lags.** Changes take many months to feed through, often a year or more, so the central bank must act on forecasts.
- **Confidence.** In a deep downturn, firms and households may not borrow however cheap credit is.
- **Winners and losers.** A rise helps savers and hurts borrowers; a cut does the reverse, which affects the distribution of income.
- **One rate for the whole country.** A rate right for a booming region may be too high for a struggling one.

**Strengths:** decisions can be made quickly, at regular meetings; changes can be small and reversed; independence supports confidence that inflation will stay near target.

## Annotated model answer

*Evaluate the effect of a rise in interest rates on investment.*

"Higher interest rates increase the cost of borrowing, so for firms that borrow to buy machinery, fewer projects earn more than the interest cost. **[chain]** Investment falls, which reduces demand now and the economy's capacity later. **[consequence]** However, firms that fund investment from retained profit are less affected, and if firms expect strong sales, a small rise may not change their plans. **[limits]** Firms with fixed-rate loans also feel the change only when they borrow again. **[timing]** So a rise is likely to reduce investment most among small, debt-funded firms, and its effect depends on the size of the rise and on business confidence." **[judgement]**

## Common mistakes

- Calling monetary policy "government spending" or confusing it with fiscal policy.
- Saying the government sets Bank Rate. The Bank of England does, to meet a target set by the government.
- Assuming every borrower is affected immediately.
- Forgetting that a rate rise helps savers.
- Claiming higher rates directly cut cost-push inflation.

## Exam technique

- **Explain**: define monetary policy and name the tool (interest rates).
- **Analyse**: follow every step from rate change to the objective named; do not jump from "rates rise" to "inflation falls".
- **Evaluate**: name the group (consumers, borrowers, savers, firms), give conditions that strengthen or weaken the effect, and decide.
