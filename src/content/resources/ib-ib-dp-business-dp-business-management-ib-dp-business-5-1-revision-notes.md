## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Operations management | Planning, organising and controlling the process that turns inputs into outputs |
| Inputs | Resources used: materials, labour, equipment, energy, information |
| Transformed inputs | Inputs changed by the process, such as raw materials or customers being served |
| Transforming inputs | Inputs that do the changing and are reused, such as staff and machines |
| Outputs | The goods or services delivered to customers |
| Value added | Selling price minus the cost of bought-in inputs |
| Goods | Tangible products that can be stored |
| Services | Intangible activities produced and consumed at the same time |

## The role in one list

* Decide what and how much to produce, and with what capacity.
* Choose the production method (section 5.2).
* Secure inputs and manage stock.
* Control quality.
* Control costs and waste.
* Deliver on time.
* Act ethically and sustainably in sourcing, working conditions and resource use.

## Value added

Value added = price − cost of bought-in inputs. It is not profit: wages, rent and other costs must still be covered.

Ways to raise it:

* Cut input costs or waste.
* Increase what customers will pay, through quality, design, service or branding.

## Goods versus services

| Goods | Services |
|---|---|
| Tangible | Intangible |
| Can be stored | Cannot be stored |
| Made, then sold | Produced and consumed together |
| Customer usually absent | Customer present and involved |
| Checked before sale | Quality depends on each interaction |

Implication: service operations must match staff to demand as it happens; goods producers can use stock as a buffer.

## Performance aims (standard framework)

| Aim | Customer sees |
|---|---|
| Quality | Product right first time |
| Speed | Fast delivery |
| Dependability | Promises kept |
| Flexibility | Choice and changes accommodated |
| Cost | Low price or good value |

Trade-offs: more flexibility usually raises cost; faster delivery may need more staff; tighter quality checks may slow output. The role is to prioritise the aims that matter to the target customers.

## Links to other functions

* Marketing: promises must be deliverable.
* Finance: capital for equipment; cash tied up in stock.
* HR: method of production sets skills and staffing.
* Conceptual themes for this unit: change, creativity, ethics, sustainability.

## Worked mini-example

Ravensworth Laundry (invented) charges 14.00 to wash and press a bag of clothes. Detergent, water, energy and packaging cost 3.20 per bag.

* Value added = 14.00 − 3.20 = **10.80** per bag.
* At 2,500 bags a month, value added = **27,000**.
* Service features: customers drop off and collect, so opening hours and turnaround time are part of the product; capacity on a quiet Tuesday cannot be stored for a busy Saturday.

## Applying the role: what to stress in different cases

* **Manufacturer facing rising input prices:** cost control, waste reduction and supplier choice.
* **Service business with uneven demand:** matching staff to busy and quiet periods, since capacity cannot be stored.
* **Premium brand:** quality and dependability, even at a higher cost per unit.
* **Business under ethical scrutiny:** sourcing standards, working conditions and energy use, which affect reputation as well as cost.

## Exam traps

* Describing operations as factory work only.
* Calling value added "profit".
* Listing responsibilities with no reference to the case organisation.
* Claiming an organisation can be fastest, cheapest and most flexible at once.
* Forgetting the effect of operations on other functions.

## Quick self-test

1. Define operations management.
2. Give two examples of inputs for a restaurant.
3. Distinguish between transformed and transforming inputs.
4. A chair sells for 120, and bought-in materials cost 45. Calculate the value added.
5. Why is value added not the same as profit?
6. Give one way operations can increase value added.
7. State two differences between goods and services.
8. Why can a hotel not store unused rooms?
9. Name the five performance aims.
10. Give an example of a trade-off between flexibility and cost.
11. Explain one link between operations and marketing.
12. Give one way sustainable operations can also reduce cost.

### Answers

1. Planning, organising and controlling the process that turns inputs into outputs that customers value.
2. Any two: ingredients, chefs, kitchen equipment, energy, the premises.
3. Transformed inputs are changed by the process (ingredients become meals); transforming inputs do the changing and are reused (chefs, ovens).
4. 120 − 45 = 75.
5. Other costs, such as wages and rent, must still be paid out of value added before profit is made.
6. Reduce waste or input costs, or improve quality or design so customers pay more.
7. Any two: goods are tangible, services intangible; goods can be stored, services cannot; services are produced and consumed at the same time; customers are involved in services.
8. A room-night is a service; if it is not sold that night, the chance to sell it is lost.
9. Quality, speed, dependability, flexibility, cost.
10. Offering many custom options means frequent changeovers, which slow production and raise cost per unit.
11. Marketing promises such as delivery times or quality must be met by operations, or customers will be disappointed.
12. Using less energy or wasting fewer materials lowers input costs as well as environmental harm.

## Frequently asked questions

**Does operations management apply to service businesses?**
Yes. Every organisation transforms inputs into outputs, whether goods or services. In services, staff and customers are often part of the process itself.

**What is value added in operations management?**
It is the difference between the selling price and the cost of bought-in inputs. Operations raise it by cutting input costs or making the output worth more to customers.
