## Key definitions

- **Metacognition:** thinking about your own thinking.
- **Conditioning:** simple learning that can operate without cognitive processes.
- **Classical conditioning:** learning by association between stimuli.
- **Operant conditioning:** learning from consequences (reinforcement and punishment).
- **Schema:** an organised mental framework from experience.
- **Dual processing model:** fast intuitive and slow analytic thinking.
- **Anchoring bias:** judgement pulled towards an initial value.
- **Confirmation bias:** favouring information that supports what you already believe.
- **Cognitive load theory (content section):** working memory is limited, so learning is harder when too much must be processed at once.

## Study notes

**Anchoring (Tversky and Kahneman, 1974).** A wheel of fortune stopped at 10 or 65. Participants then estimated the percentage of African countries in the United Nations. Median estimates were about 25 and about 45. Strength: clear cause and effect. Limitation: artificial setting, participants may guess because they lack knowledge.

**Schema (Bartlett, 1932).** Participants repeatedly recalled a folk tale from another culture. Recall became shorter and more conventional. Limitation: loosely controlled procedure and qualitative scoring.

**Social learning (Bandura, 1961).** Children who watched an adult attack a Bobo doll copied the aggression. Limitation: artificial setting, ethical concern about exposing children to aggression.

**Biological factor (Maguire et al., 2000).** Taxi drivers had a larger posterior hippocampus, related to years of driving. Limitation: correlational, so the direction of the effect cannot be proved.

**Cultural factor (Masuda and Nisbett, 2001).** Japanese participants noticed more background and relationships in underwater scenes, American participants focused on the main fish. Limitation: students are not representative of whole cultures, and there is a risk of the ecological fallacy.

**Environmental factor (Loftus and Palmer, 1974).** "Smashed" gave higher speed estimates than "hit", and more false reports of broken glass a week later. Limitation: a film is less emotional than a real event.

**Comparing two models of memory.**

| Point | Multi-store model | Working memory model |
|---|---|---|
| Short-term memory | One store | Several components (central executive, phonological loop, visuospatial sketchpad, later an episodic buffer) |
| Long-term memory | One store | Linked to working memory |
| Strength | Explains primacy and recency effects | Explains dual-task findings |
| Weakness | Oversimplifies rehearsal | Central executive is vague |

**Improving a process.** Spaced practice, retrieval practice, elaborative rehearsal, mnemonics, the cognitive interview. For each, state the evidence and whether it works outside the lab.

## The experiment practical

- Type: true or quasi-experiment. Minimum sample for student-led work is five participants.
- Examples in the guide (suggestions only): Loftus and Palmer (1974), Masuda and Nisbett (2001), Tversky and Kahneman (1974), music and memory (Mozart effect).
- Know the independent and dependent variables, hypothesis, design (independent measures, repeated measures or matched pairs), controls, sampling technique, ethics and one limitation.
- True experiment: random allocation. Quasi-experiment: groups already exist (such as two cultures), so causality is weaker.

## Concept links to prepare

- **Causality:** experiments versus correlational brain imaging.
- **Bias:** cognitive biases in participants; sampling bias; researcher expectation.
- **Measurement:** recall versus recognition; operationalising attention or memory.
- **Perspective:** biological, cognitive and cultural explanations of the same process.
- **Responsibility:** misleading questions, use of children, deception.
- **Change:** which strategies produce lasting improvement.

## Common mistakes

- Naming a study without linking it to a learning objective.
- Comparing models without a clear similarity and difference.
- Treating conditioning as the whole of learning.
- Using only one concept, or none.

## Self-test

1. Distinguish classical from operant conditioning.
2. What do the two groups in the anchoring study show?
3. Why is Maguire et al. (2000) correlational?
4. State two differences between the multi-store and working memory models.
5. What is one strength and one limitation of Bandura (1961)?
6. Which experimental design would you use to compare memory in two cultures, and what is the weakness?
7. Name two strategies to improve memory.

**Answers:** 1. Classical is learning by association between stimuli; operant is learning from consequences. 2. An irrelevant starting number shifted judgements, showing anchoring. 3. Brain size was measured, not manipulated, so we cannot know whether driving caused growth or larger hippocampi led people to become drivers. 4. One short-term store versus several components; passive store versus active processing system. 5. Strength: control and clear cause and effect; limitation: artificial setting and ethics. 6. A quasi-experiment, because culture cannot be assigned; participants are not randomly allocated, so causality is weaker. 7. Spaced practice and retrieval practice (also mnemonics).

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need to learn all of the named studies?**
No. The guide names studies only as practical suggestions. Choose well-evidenced examples for each learning objective and know how to evaluate them.

**Is "memory" required?**
No. The guide says "one or more of attention, memory, perception or language". Memory is a convenient choice because it has the most evidence.

**What links this context to the HL extensions?**
The culture, motivation and technology extensions each have a learning and cognition strand, such as the role of culture on learning, the role of motivation in enhancing a cognitive process and the effect of technology on cognition.
