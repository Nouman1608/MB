This guide covers **4.6.1 Rate of reaction** of the AQA GCSE Chemistry (8462) specification, for teaching from
September 2016. It sits in topic 4.6, The rate and extent of chemical change, and is assessed on Paper 2.
Almost all of it is for both tiers. Two parts are **Higher tier only**: using moles and mol/s for the rate,
and calculating the gradient of a tangent to find the rate at a specific time. Required practical 5 (effect
of concentration on rate) belongs here.

## 4.6.1.1 Calculating rates of reaction

The **rate** of a reaction tells you how fast reactants are used up or products are formed. You find it by
measuring the **quantity of reactant used** or **product formed** over **time**.

**mean rate of reaction = quantity of reactant used ÷ time taken**

**mean rate of reaction = quantity of product formed ÷ time taken**

The quantity can be a **mass in grams** or a **volume in cm³**, so the units of rate are **g/s** or **cm³/s**.
At **Higher tier** the quantity can also be in **moles**, giving **mol/s**.

### Worked example 1: mean rate from a volume

*A reaction produces 54 cm³ of gas in 90 seconds. Calculate the mean rate.*

```
mean rate = 54 cm3 / 90 s = 0.60 cm3/s
```

### Worked example 2: mean rate from a loss in mass

*A flask loses 1.50 g in mass in 250 seconds as carbon dioxide escapes. Calculate the mean rate.*

```
mean rate = 1.50 g / 250 s = 0.0060 g/s
```

### Worked example 3 (Higher tier only): rate in mol/s

*0.12 g of magnesium (Ar = 24) is used up in 50 seconds. Calculate the mean rate in mol/s.*

```
moles Mg = 0.12 / 24 = 0.0050 mol
mean rate = 0.0050 mol / 50 s = 0.00010 mol/s  (1.0 x 10^-4 mol/s)
```

### Measuring the quantity

| Method | What is measured | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Gas syringe | Volume of gas against time | Accurate; must be airtight |
| Inverted measuring cylinder in water | Volume of gas | Less precise |
| Balance | Loss in mass as gas escapes | Cotton wool in the flask neck stops spray |
| Turbidity (disappearing cross) | Time for cross to vanish | Subjective; for precipitates |
| Colour change | Time for a colour to appear or fade | Use a colorimeter for better results |

### Graphs of rate

A graph of **quantity of product against time** (or reactant against time):

- The **steepest** part is at the start, when the rate is fastest, because reactant concentrations are highest.
- The line becomes **less steep** as reactants are used up, so the rate falls.
- The line becomes **flat** when one reactant is used up. The reaction has stopped.

### Worked example 4: rates over intervals

| Time / s | 0 | 20 | 40 | 60 | 80 | 100 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Volume of gas / cm³ | 0 | 30 | 48 | 58 | 63 | 64 |

Rate over each 20 s interval = change in volume ÷ 20:

| Interval | Rate / cm³/s |
|---|---|
| 0 to 20 s | 30 ÷ 20 = 1.5 |
| 20 to 40 s | 18 ÷ 20 = 0.9 |
| 40 to 60 s | 10 ÷ 20 = 0.5 |
| 60 to 80 s | 5 ÷ 20 = 0.25 |
| 80 to 100 s | 1 ÷ 20 = 0.05 |

The rate falls steadily, and the reaction is almost finished by 100 s. The mean rate over the whole 100 s is
64 ÷ 100 = 0.64 cm³/s. The final volume (64 cm³) shows the total product, and a faster or slower reaction
gives the same final volume if the amounts are unchanged.

### Tangents and the rate at a point

The **slope** of a tangent drawn to a curve is a measure of the **rate at one moment**. To draw it, place a
ruler so it touches the curve at the chosen time without cutting across it, and draw a line. Make a large
right-angled triangle on the line.

**Higher tier only:** calculate the gradient = change in y ÷ change in x. If the tangent at 30 s passes
through (10 s, 25 cm³) and (50 s, 49 cm³):

```
gradient = (49 - 25) / (50 - 10) = 24 / 40 = 0.60 cm3/s
```

At Foundation, you need to draw tangents and use their slope as a measure of rate (a steeper tangent means a
faster rate).

## 4.6.1.2 Factors that affect the rate

Five factors change the rate of a reaction:

1. **Concentration** of reactants in solution
2. **Pressure** of reacting gases
3. **Surface area** of solid reactants
4. **Temperature**
5. **Catalysts**.

You must recall how changing these affects the rate. Increasing the first four makes the reaction faster.

### Required practical 5: concentration

This is an investigation in which you develop a hypothesis, such as "doubling the concentration of acid
doubles the rate". There are two methods:

- **Gas volume:** magnesium ribbon with hydrochloric acid of different concentrations, collecting hydrogen in a
  gas syringe and timing, for example, the first 30 seconds.
- **Turbidity:** sodium thiosulfate with hydrochloric acid, timing how long it takes for a cross under the flask to
  disappear as sulfur forms.

Keep the temperature, volume and mass the same, and change only concentration. Dilute the stock solution
with water to get different concentrations. Repeat and calculate a mean.

## 4.6.1.3 Collision theory and activation energy

**Collision theory:** reactions can occur only when reacting particles **collide** with **sufficient energy**.
The minimum energy needed is the **activation energy**.

| Change | Effect on particles | Effect on rate |
|---|---|---|
| Increase concentration or pressure | More particles in the same volume, so collisions are more **frequent** | Faster |
| Increase surface area | More particles exposed to attack, so more frequent collisions | Faster |
| Increase temperature | Particles move **faster**, so collisions are more **frequent** and **more energetic** | Faster |

For temperature, remember **two** reasons. Faster particles collide more often, and a bigger fraction of
collisions has energy at least equal to the activation energy. The second reason matters most.

**Simple proportionality:** if the concentration is doubled, there are twice as many particles in a given
volume, so about twice as many collisions per second and about double the rate.

### Surface area to volume ratio

Smaller pieces of a solid have a larger **surface area to volume ratio**. For cubes:

| Cube side | Surface area | Volume | Surface area : volume |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 cm | 6 × 1² = 6 cm² | 1 cm³ | 6 : 1 |
| 2 cm | 6 × 2² = 24 cm² | 8 cm³ | 3 : 1 |

Cutting one 2 cm cube into eight 1 cm cubes keeps the volume at 8 cm³, but raises the total surface area from
24 cm² to 8 × 6 = 48 cm². The ratio doubles from 3 : 1 to 6 : 1, so the rate roughly doubles. Powders react faster
than lumps for this reason.

## 4.6.1.4 Catalysts

A **catalyst** changes the rate of a reaction but is **not used up**. It provides an **alternative pathway** with
a **lower activation energy**, so more collisions are successful. **Enzymes** are catalysts in living things.

- A catalyst is **not shown in the chemical equation** as a reactant or product. This is how you identify one.
- Different reactions need different catalysts, and you do not need to learn named catalysts beyond those in the
  specification.
- On a **reaction profile**, the catalysed pathway has a **lower peak**. The reactants and products are at the
  same levels, so the **overall energy change is unchanged**.

## Exam technique

- Give units for the rate every time (g/s, cm³/s, or mol/s at Higher tier).
- In "explain" questions, name the factor, the effect on **collision frequency**, and the effect on rate.
- For temperature, say collisions are more frequent **and** more energetic.
- For a catalyst, say it provides an alternative pathway with a **lower activation energy**.
- Read graphs carefully: a steeper line means a faster rate, and equal final heights mean equal amounts of product.

## Common mistakes

- Saying a higher concentration means "more energy". It means more particles per unit volume.
- Forgetting that the rate falls over time because the reactants are used up.
- Saying a catalyst "lowers the energy of the reactants" or "is used up".
- Using the total time rather than the time for a given quantity of product.
- Drawing a tangent that cuts through the curve.
- Mixing up rate (how fast) with extent (how much product).
