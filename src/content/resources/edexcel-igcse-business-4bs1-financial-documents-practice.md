These questions cover section 3.4 Financial documents (points 3.4.1 and 3.4.2) of the Pearson Edexcel International GCSE in Business (4BS1) specification, Issue 2, February 2026. As in the specification, you are asked to read and interpret statements, not to construct them. Revise with the [financial documents study guide](/resources/edexcel-igcse-business-4bs1-financial-documents/) and the [financial documents revision notes](/resources/edexcel-igcse-business-4bs1-financial-documents-revision-notes/) first if needed. Every business is invented and amounts are in dollars. Give percentages to 1 decimal place.

## Questions

### Section A: shorter questions

**1.** Define cost of sales. **[2]**

**2.** Give the term used in the exam for each of these older terms.

**(a)** Stock. **[1]**
**(b)** Creditors. **[1]**
**(c)** Balance sheet. **[1]**

**3.** Rennick Outdoor had sales of 340,000, cost of sales of 204,000 and other operating expenses of 98,000. **(Calculation)**

**(a)** Calculate gross profit. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate operating profit. **[1]**

**4.** Classify each item as a non-current asset, current asset, current liability or non-current liability. **[6]**

| Item | Classification |
|---|---|
| Shop fittings | ? |
| Trade receivables | ? |
| Bank overdraft | ? |
| Bank loan repayable in 10 years | ? |
| Inventory | ? |
| Trade payables | ? |

**5.** Explain why a bank would study a business's statement of comprehensive income before agreeing to a loan. **[3]**

**6.** Outline one difference between gross profit and operating profit. **[2]**

**7.** Lark & Finch Ltd has the following items on its statement of financial position: property 300,000; equipment 85,000; inventory 32,000; trade receivables 21,000; cash 7,000; trade payables 26,000; bank overdraft 9,000; bank loan repayable after six years 120,000. **(Calculation)**

**(a)** Calculate non-current assets. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate current assets. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate current liabilities. **[1]**
**(d)** Calculate capital employed. **[2]**
**(e)** Calculate equity. **[1]**

### Section B: longer questions

**8.** Bellamy Gym's statement of comprehensive income shows:

| | Year 1 | Year 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Sales | 410,000 | 452,000 |
| Cost of sales | 82,000 | 90,400 |
| Gross profit | 328,000 | 361,600 |
| Other operating expenses | 290,000 | 341,600 |
| Operating profit | 38,000 | 20,000 |

**(a)** Calculate the percentage change in sales. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage change in operating profit. **[2]**
**(c)** Analyse one decision Bellamy Gym's managers could make using this statement. **[4]**

**9.** Hesketh Electrical's statement of financial position shows current assets of 104,000, of which inventory is 88,000 and cash 4,000. Current liabilities are 70,000. Analyse what these figures suggest about Hesketh Electrical. **[4]**

**10.** Pinecroft Bakery has two sections. The owner wants to expand one of them.

| | Café | Wholesale to shops |
|---|---|---|
| Sales | 180,000 | 260,000 |
| Cost of sales | 72,000 | 182,000 |
| Other operating expenses for the section | 96,000 | 49,000 |

**(a)** Calculate the gross profit of each section. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the operating profit of each section. **[2]**
**(c)** Justify which section Pinecroft Bakery should expand. **[6]**

**11.** Corbin Hardware has asked a supplier for 60 days' credit. Its statement of financial position shows property of 500,000, current assets of 52,000 (including inventory of 40,000) and current liabilities of 47,000. Evaluate how useful the statement of financial position is to the supplier in making its decision. **[8]**

**12.** **(a)** State the purpose of a statement of financial position. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain one limitation of using a statement of comprehensive income to decide whether to open a new branch. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** The direct cost of the goods or services that a business has sold [1], such as the materials used or the goods bought for resale [1]. **[2]**

**2. (a)** Inventory [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** Trade payables [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** Statement of financial position [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* The specification states that the assessments use IAS terminology only, so these are the words you will meet in the paper.

**3. (a)** 340,000 − 204,000 = **136,000** [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** 136,000 − 98,000 = **38,000** [1]. **[1]**

**4.** Shop fittings: non-current asset [1]. Trade receivables: current asset [1]. Bank overdraft: current liability [1]. Bank loan repayable in 10 years: non-current liability [1]. Inventory: current asset [1]. Trade payables: current liability [1]. **[6]**

**5.** The statement shows whether the business makes an operating profit [1], so the bank can judge whether there is enough profit to pay the interest on the loan [1]. A business with falling or negative profit is more likely to miss repayments, so the bank may refuse the loan or ask for security [1]. **[3]**

**6.** Gross profit is sales minus cost of sales only [1], while operating profit also deducts other operating expenses such as rent and administration wages, so it is lower [1]. **[2]**

**7. (a)** 300,000 + 85,000 = **385,000** [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** 32,000 + 21,000 + 7,000 = **60,000** [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** 26,000 + 9,000 = **35,000** [1]. **[1]**
**(d)** 385,000 + 60,000 − 35,000 [1] = **410,000** [1]. **[2]**
**(e)** 410,000 − 120,000 = **290,000** [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* The bank loan is a non-current liability, so it is not deducted when finding capital employed. It is part of the long-term finance.

**8. (a)** (452,000 − 410,000) ÷ 410,000 × 100 [1] = **10.2%** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** (20,000 − 38,000) ÷ 38,000 × 100 [1] = **−47.4%** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Other operating expenses rose by 51,600, more than the 33,600 rise in gross profit [1], so the managers could investigate which running costs grew, such as staff wages or energy [1]. If, for example, extra instructors were hired but did not attract enough new members, they could reduce instructor hours [1], which would cut expenses and raise operating profit back towards the Year 1 level [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* The gross profit line is healthy and growing; the problem lies below it. Pinpointing where in the statement the change happens is what "analyse" rewards.

**9.** Most current assets are inventory: 88,000 of 104,000 [1], and cash is only 4,000, while 70,000 must be paid within a year [1]. Inventory has to be sold, and customers must pay, before it becomes cash [1], so Hesketh may struggle to pay suppliers on time and could need an overdraft or have to sell stock at lower prices [1]. **[4]**

**10. (a)** Café: 180,000 − 72,000 = **108,000** [1]. Wholesale: 260,000 − 182,000 = **78,000** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Café: 108,000 − 96,000 = **12,000** [1]. Wholesale: 78,000 − 49,000 = **29,000** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** For the café: it earns the higher gross profit, 108,000, because selling prices are high compared with ingredient costs [1]. Against the café: its running costs of 96,000, such as serving staff and seating space, leave only 12,000 operating profit [1]. For wholesale: it makes more than twice the operating profit, 29,000 [1], and running costs are low because there is no seating or table service [1]. Against wholesale: gross profit is lower on each sale, so it depends on large orders from a small number of shops, and losing one customer would hurt [1]. Judgement: for example, expand wholesale, because it turns sales into operating profit more effectively, provided the bakery can find more shop customers [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* A section with high gross profit is not automatically the better one. The operating profit line shows what is left after the costs of running each section.

**11.** Useful: the statement shows current assets of 52,000 against current liabilities of 47,000 [1], so Corbin has only slightly more short-term assets than short-term debts [1]. Most of the current assets are inventory, 40,000, so only 12,000 is in cash and receivables [1], which suggests Corbin may find it hard to pay a supplier on time [1]. The property of 500,000 shows the business has valuable assets [1], although these cannot quickly be turned into cash to pay a supplier [1]. Limited: it shows one date only, so it does not show whether Corbin is profitable or how quickly it sells its inventory [1]. Judgement: for example, it is useful as a warning about short-term payments, but the supplier should also look at the statement of comprehensive income and Corbin's past payment record before offering 60 days [1]. **[8]**

**12. (a)** To show what a business owns and owes, and how it is financed, on a particular date [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** It shows past results [1], but a new branch depends on future sales in a different location [1], so past profit may not predict whether the branch will succeed, and the statement does not show whether there is enough cash to fund it [1]. **[3]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Deducting non-current liabilities when finding capital employed.
- Calculating a percentage change on the new figure instead of the old one.
- Treating high gross profit as proof that a section or business is doing well.
- Calling inventory "cash" when judging whether bills can be paid.
- Using older terms instead of the IAS terms.
