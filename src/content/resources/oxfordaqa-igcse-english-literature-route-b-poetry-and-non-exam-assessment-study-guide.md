Route B of OxfordAQA International GCSE English Literature combines written work on poetry with a piece of work completed outside exam conditions. That means two different skills: responding to poetry under time pressure, including a poem you have never seen, and building a considered piece of writing over a longer period. This guide covers the methods for each. Because the set poems, question wording and assessment rules are fixed by the specification, check those details with your teacher; here you will find the skills that transfer to any poem or task.

## Where this fits in the specification

The OxfordAQA International GCSE English Literature specification (9275) is organised into routes, which combine different components, and Route B includes poetry and non-exam assessment. The three parts of this topic are poetry, unseen poetry and the non-exam assessment. For general advice on pacing and revision across the qualification, see the guide on [exam preparation for OxfordAQA International GCSE English Literature](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-english-literature-exam-preparation/).

## Studied poetry

When you study a set group of poems, you can prepare in depth. For each poem, build a short profile.

1. **Situation**: who speaks, to whom, and what happens or is described.
2. **Theme and attitude**: what the poem is about and what the speaker feels.
3. **Form and structure**: stanza pattern, rhyme, rhythm, line length, punctuation, and any shift in tone or direction.
4. **Language**: key images, word choices, sound effects and repeated words.
5. **Context**: what you need to know about the writer, setting or period to understand the poem, if anything.
6. **Links**: other poems in the group that share or contrast with its theme or methods.

If the question asks you to compare poems, choose the poem to compare that fits the question best, not the one you prefer. A comparison is stronger when organised by an idea, such as how each poet presents memory, and when it explains why the methods differ.

### Writing a paragraph on a poem

Aim for a clear claim, brief evidence, analysis of method and an explanation of effect. For example, if you were writing about a poem in which a speaker looks back on childhood, you might say: "The speaker's use of short, unpunctuated lines near the end creates a sense of memories rushing back without control, so the reader feels the pull of the past as much as the speaker does." Notice that the sentence names a technique, explains its effect and connects it to the poem's subject. The sentence is invented to show the method and is not taken from a set poem.

## Unseen poetry

An unseen poem is one you have not studied. The examiners are testing whether you can apply skills to something new, so you are not expected to know the poem, the writer or the context. What you need is a calm, systematic approach.

### A five-step approach

1. **Read the poem twice**, once for sense and once for detail. Look at the title, which often gives a clue.
2. **Annotate quickly**: circle repeated words, underline striking images, mark where the tone changes.
3. **Decide what the poem is about** and how the speaker feels, in one or two sentences.
4. **Choose three or four points** about language, form and structure, each linked to meaning.
5. **Write** in an organised way, with a short introduction, developed paragraphs and a brief conclusion.

If a question asks you to compare two unseen poems, plan around two or three shared ideas, such as the poets' attitudes to nature, and show how each uses language and structure differently.

### Worked example: an invented unseen opening

Suppose the opening lines read: "The tide went out and took the town with it, / leaving the pier like a finger pointing nowhere." You might comment that the tide taking "the town" is a surprising metaphor which suggests loss on a large scale, and that the simile comparing the pier to a "finger pointing nowhere" gives the landscape a sense of aimlessness. The line break after "with it" leaves the reader momentarily with the idea of everything being taken before the image of the pier, a structural choice that mimics the way loss is felt first and understood afterwards. These lines are made up for practice; the aim is to show how to turn a detail into analysis.

### Common unseen-poetry traps

- Searching for a hidden meaning and ignoring the surface meaning.
- Listing techniques without effects.
- Spending the whole time on the first stanza.
- Writing about what the poem is not.

## Non-exam assessment

The non-exam assessment is work you complete in school over a period, not under timed exam conditions. Follow the instructions given by your teacher about the task, the texts, the word limit and the rules on supervision and drafting. The following skills are likely to help in any case.

### Planning

Start by reading the task carefully and turning it into a question you can answer. Make a plan with a thesis, a sequence of points and the evidence for each. Choose evidence from across the text so the answer shows development, not just a good opening.

### Drafting and revising

Write a full draft, then revise it for argument before you edit for accuracy. Ask whether each paragraph supports your thesis, whether your analysis explains effects, and whether the introduction and conclusion do their jobs. Check quotations against the text and keep within the word limit.

### Using context and critical ideas

If you use context, mention only what you are sure of, and use it to explain a choice in the text. If you refer to another reader's view, put it in your own words and acknowledge where it came from.

### Authenticity

Work you submit must be your own. Do not copy from the internet, study guides or other students. Keep your notes and drafts, and follow any declaration you are asked to sign.

## Planning your time across the topic

Practise the three skills separately. For studied poetry, revise by theme and by poem profile. For unseen poetry, practise one new poem each week under timed conditions. For the non-exam assessment, work backwards from the deadline and set milestones for the plan, the first draft and the final check.

## Common mistakes

- Memorising an answer to a poem rather than learning to answer the question.
- Treating the speaker as the poet in every case.
- Describing a poem's meaning without analysing methods.
- Leaving the non-exam assessment to the last minute.
- Writing context facts that do not link to the poem.
- Using long quotations in place of analysis.

## Exam technique

Read each question carefully and underline the key words. Use short quotations. In an unseen response, show your thinking by explaining the effect of each detail you choose. In your non-exam work, keep your focus on the task. Always finish with a brief conclusion that answers the question.

## Frequently asked questions

**What does Route B involve?**
Paper 1, then Paper 2b (1 hour 30 minutes, 60 marks, 40%, open book: anthology poetry and an unseen poem) and a non-exam assessment.

**What is the 9275 non-exam assessment?**
One extended response to a prose fiction text of your choice that is not on the set-text list, worth 30 marks and 20%.

**Is Paper 2b open book?**
Yes, according to the specification's assessment overview.


## Check your understanding

1. What is the first thing to do with an unseen poem? (Read it twice, noticing the title and the surface meaning.)
2. Why do examiners set unseen poems? (To test whether you can apply analytical skills to something new.)
3. Name three aspects of poetic form you could comment on. (For example stanza pattern, rhyme, rhythm, line length or punctuation.)
4. How should a comparison of two poems be organised? (Around ideas, moving between the poems.)
5. What should follow a quotation in a poetry answer? (Analysis of the method and its effect.)
6. How should you use context in a poetry answer? (Only where it explains a choice in the poem, and only with facts you are sure of.)
7. What is the purpose of keeping drafts for non-exam assessment? (They show your work is your own and developed over time.)
8. Why plan the non-exam assessment backwards from the deadline? (So that planning, drafting and checking all have enough time.)
