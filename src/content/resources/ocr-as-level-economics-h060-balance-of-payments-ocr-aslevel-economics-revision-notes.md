## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Balance of payments | Record of transactions between a country's residents and the rest of the world over a period |
| Credit | A flow of money into the country (exports, income received) |
| Debit | A flow of money out of the country (imports, income paid) |
| Current account | Trade in goods and services plus primary and secondary income |
| Trade in goods | Exports and imports of tangible products |
| Trade in services | Exports and imports of intangibles: tourism, finance, insurance, transport, education |
| Balance of trade | Goods balance plus services balance |
| Primary income | Cross-border wages, interest, profits and dividends |
| Secondary income | Cross-border transfers with nothing in return: remittances, aid, contributions to international bodies |
| Financial account | Cross-border purchases of assets and borrowing: direct investment, portfolio investment, loans |
| Capital account | Small capital transfers, such as debt forgiveness |
| Current account deficit | Debits exceed credits on the current account |
| Imbalance | A large or persistent current account deficit or surplus |
| Sustainable position | A balance that can be maintained without a forced adjustment |

## Building the current account

1. Goods balance = goods exports − goods imports.
2. Services balance = service exports − service imports.
3. Balance of trade = goods balance + services balance.
4. Current account = balance of trade + primary income balance + secondary income balance.
5. Share of GDP = current account balance ÷ GDP × 100.

**Quick example (Country Dovra, bn).** Goods: credits 88, debits 101. Services: credits 40, debits 31. Primary income: credits 12, debits 10. Secondary income: credits 3, debits 7.

- Goods −13; services +9; balance of trade −4.
- Primary income +2; secondary income −4.
- Current account: −4 + 2 − 4 = **−6 bn**. With GDP of 400 bn, that is **1.5% of GDP**.

**Missing-figure method.** Write the full equation with the unknown, then solve. If the current account is −10, goods −15, services +8 and secondary income −5, then primary income = −10 + 15 − 8 + 5 = **+2**.

## Classifying transactions

| Transaction (from the home country's view) | Component | Entry |
|---|---|---|
| A foreign airline buys aircraft parts from a home firm | Trade in goods | Credit |
| A home family takes a holiday abroad | Trade in services | Debit |
| A home bank earns interest on a loan to a foreign firm | Primary income | Credit |
| A foreign-owned supermarket chain sends profits to its parent | Primary income | Debit |
| The government gives aid to another country | Secondary income | Debit |
| A foreign firm builds a new factory at home | Financial account, not current | Inflow |

Remember: the investment itself is never current account. Only the profit or interest it later generates is.

## Sustainability checklist

- **Size:** relate the balance to GDP, never judge from the money figure alone.
- **Persistence:** a one-off deficit in a boom differs from a decade of deficits.
- **Financing:** long-term direct investment is steadier than short-term borrowing.
- **Use:** imports of capital goods can raise future capacity; a consumer import boom does not.

## Causes of imbalances

| Cyclical (often temporary) | Structural (often lasting) |
|---|---|
| A boom at home raises import spending | Low productivity and high unit labour costs |
| A recession abroad cuts demand for exports | Weak non-price competitiveness: quality, design, reliability |
| A temporary rise in import prices, such as fuel | A persistently strong exchange rate |
| | Heavy foreign ownership, causing primary income debits |

**Relative inflation rule of thumb.** If home prices rise faster than partners' prices with an unchanged exchange rate, home goods become relatively dearer: 1.05 ÷ 1.01 − 1 = 4.0% dearer when home inflation is 5% and partners' inflation 1%. The effect on the balance depends on the price elasticity of demand for exports and imports.

## Consequences of imbalances

- **Deficit:** net exports reduce AD, so growth and employment are lower, other things equal; downward pressure on the currency; assets sold or debt taken on abroad, so primary income debits rise later.
- **Surplus:** net exports add to AD; upward pressure on the currency; acquisition of foreign assets brings future income; possible friction with deficit countries.
- **Judgement:** consequences depend on size, persistence, financing and the state of the economy. A deficit is less pressing when the economy is near full employment and growing.

## Exam traps

- Calculating debits minus credits instead of credits minus debits.
- Stopping at the balance of trade when asked for the current account.
- Putting foreign direct investment into the current account.
- Classing tourism by foreign visitors as an import.
- Calling interest and dividends "secondary income": they are returns to factors, so primary income.
- Concluding "deficits are bad" without the sustainability tests.
- Ignoring which component drives the balance in the data.

## Self-test

1. Define the balance of payments.
2. Name the four components of the current account.
3. A home university charges fees to an overseas student. Which component, and credit or debit?
4. Profits sent abroad by a foreign-owned car plant: which component, and which entry?
5. Goods balance −20 bn, services +14 bn. What is the balance of trade?
6. Balance of trade −6 bn, primary income +3 bn, secondary income −5 bn. Calculate the current account balance.
7. A current account deficit of 24 bn on GDP of 800 bn: what percentage of GDP is it?
8. The current account is −12 bn, goods −18, services +10, primary income −1. Find the secondary income balance.
9. Why must a current account deficit be matched by a net inflow on the financial and capital accounts?
10. Give two features that make a deficit more sustainable.
11. Explain one cyclical and one structural cause of a deficit.
12. Give one reason why a persistent surplus may be a problem.

### Answers

1. A record of all transactions between the residents of a country and the rest of the world over a period.
2. Trade in goods, trade in services, primary income and secondary income.
3. Trade in services; credit.
4. Primary income; debit.
5. −20 + 14 = **−6 bn**.
6. −6 + 3 − 5 = **−8 bn**.
7. 24 ÷ 800 × 100 = **3.0%**.
8. −12 = −18 + 10 − 1 + secondary income, so secondary income = **−3 bn**.
9. Spending abroad beyond earnings must be paid for by selling assets or borrowing from abroad, which are financial account inflows; the accounts sum to zero apart from errors and omissions.
10. Any two of: small relative to GDP; short-lived; financed by long-term direct investment; spent on capital goods that raise future output.
11. Cyclical: a domestic boom raises imports. Structural: low productivity raises unit labour costs, making exports uncompetitive.
12. Any one of: it means residents consume less than the economy produces; upward pressure on the currency hurts exporters; partners run matching deficits and may retaliate.
