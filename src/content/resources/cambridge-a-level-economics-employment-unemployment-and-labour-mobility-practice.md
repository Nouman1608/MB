## Questions

**1.** Define full employment. **[2]**

**2.** In Country D the labour force is 12.5 million and 11.7 million people are employed. Calculate the unemployment rate. **[2]**

**3.** Country A has a labour force of 15.0 million and 1.0 million unemployed people: 0.45 million structurally, 0.20 million frictionally and 0.35 million cyclically.
**(a)** Calculate the unemployment rate. **[1]**
**(b)** Treating the natural rate as frictional plus structural unemployment, calculate it as a percentage of the labour force. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the cyclical unemployment rate in percentage points. **[1]**

**4.** In a labour market, w is the hourly wage in cu (currency units) and quantities are in thousands of workers. Demand is L = 900 − 30w and supply is L = 30w − 300.
**(a)** Find the market-clearing wage and the number employed there. **[2]**
**(b)** A legal minimum wage of 26 is then set. Calculate employment, the labour supplied and unemployment. **[3]**
**(c)** Explain whether this unemployment is voluntary or involuntary, and why cutting the minimum wage back to 20 would not create jobs for all of it. **[3]**

**5.** Explain hysteresis in the labour market. **[4]**

**6.** Explain two factors that could raise a country's natural rate of unemployment. **[4]**

**7.** A job pays 10 cu an hour for 40 hours a week and is taxed at 15%. Out-of-work benefit is 150 cu a week.
**(a)** Calculate the replacement rate. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain how a rise in the replacement rate could affect voluntary unemployment. **[2]**

**8.** A worker can earn 6,000 cu a year more in another city, but pays 3,000 cu a year more in rent. Moving costs 15,000 cu once.
**(a)** Calculate the payback period. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain two factors, other than cost, that limit geographical mobility. **[4]**

**9.** The government of Country G raises spending by 0.8 billion cu. The marginal propensity to consume is 0.75, and there are no taxes or imports. Each extra worker produces 40,000 cu a year. Cyclical unemployment is 200,000.
**(a)** Calculate the multiplier, the rise in national income and the number of jobs created. **[4]**
**(b)** Explain why the fall in unemployment might be smaller than this. **[3]**

**10.** A wage subsidy costs 4,000 cu per subsidised job, but half of those jobs would have existed anyway. A training scheme costs 2,500 cu per trainee, and 25% of trainees gain a job they would not otherwise have found.
**(a)** Calculate the cost per additional job for each policy. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain one reason why the cheaper policy may still be less effective. **[2]**

**11.** The table shows Country E, whose working-age population is 22.0 million in every year. Figures are in millions.

| Year | Employed | Unemployed |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 15.0 | 0.9 |
| 2 | 14.1 | 1.8 |
| 3 | 14.3 | 1.6 |
| 4 | 14.8 | 1.2 |
| 5 | 15.2 | 1.0 |

**(a)** Calculate the unemployment rate in years 2 and 5. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate the employment rate in years 1 and 5. **[2]**
**(c)** The year 5 unemployment rate is above that of year 1. Explain two possible reasons. **[4]**

**12.** Assess the view that a government should rely on supply-side measures, rather than changes in aggregate demand, to cut unemployment. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** Full employment is the position where all those prepared and able to work at the prevailing wage can obtain a job [1], although some frictional and structural (natural) unemployment remains [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* "No unemployment" is a common slip. Naming what remains is what separates a full definition from a partial one.

**2.** Unemployed = 12.5 − 11.7 = 0.8 million [1]. Rate = 0.8 ÷ 12.5 × 100 = **6.4%** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the labour force as the denominator. A bare percentage with no working leaves the reader unable to follow your steps.

**3. (a)** 1.0 ÷ 15.0 × 100 = **6.7%** [1]
**(b)** Natural = 0.45 + 0.20 = 0.65 million [1]; 0.65 ÷ 15.0 × 100 = **4.3%** [1]
**(c)** 6.7 − 4.3 = **2.3 percentage points** (0.35 ÷ 15.0 × 100) [1] **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Keep unrounded figures until the end. Rounding 6.7 and 4.3 first still gives 2.4, which drifts from the exact 2.3.

**4. (a)** 900 − 30w = 30w − 300 → 60w = 1,200 [1] → w = **20**, employment = **300 thousand** [1]
**(b)** Employment = demand = 900 − 780 = **120 thousand** [1]; labour supplied = 780 − 300 = **480 thousand** [1]; unemployment = 480 − 120 = **360 thousand** [1]
**(c)** The 360 thousand are willing to work at 26 and cannot find jobs, so it is involuntary [1]. At 20 there would be no involuntary unemployment [1]. A fall from 26 to 20 raises employment only from 120 to 300 thousand, while labour supplied falls from 480 to 300, because 180 thousand would not offer labour at 20 [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Read employment from the demand curve at the floor, not from supply. Candidates who quote the supply figure as employment overstate jobs.

**5.** Hysteresis is unemployment that persists after the original cause has ended [1] because the equilibrium rate itself has risen [1]. A long spell of joblessness makes skills decay and contacts fade, and employers may treat the gap as a warning sign [1], so cyclical unemployment becomes structural and stays high after demand recovers [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Link cause to consequence. Listing "skills decay" with no step to the higher natural rate is a description, not an explanation.

**6.** Factor 1, for example generous long-lasting benefits [1]: they raise the reservation wage and lengthen search, so voluntary and frictional unemployment rise [1]. Factor 2, for example a skills mismatch [1]: workers laid off from declining industries lack the skills for vacancies, so structural unemployment rises [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Two developed factors tend to earn more than four listed ones. Say why each moves the natural rate, not just that it does.

**7. (a)** Net pay = 10 × 40 × (1 − 0.15) [1] = 340 cu [1]; replacement rate = 150 ÷ 340 × 100 = **44.1%** [1]
**(b)** A higher replacement rate narrows the gap between benefit and pay [1], so some workers refuse low-paid jobs or search longer, raising voluntary unemployment [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Use net pay (after tax) as the denominator. Using gross pay gives a different rate.

**8. (a)** Net annual gain = 6,000 − 3,000 = 3,000 cu [1]; payback = 15,000 ÷ 3,000 [1] = **5.0 years** [1]
**(b)** Family and community ties [1] raise the non-money cost of moving, so some workers stay despite a pay gain [1]. Poor information about vacancies elsewhere [1] means workers may not know the gain exists [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* "Other than cost" rules out housing and moving costs. Repeating them gains nothing.

**9. (a)** Multiplier = 1 ÷ (1 − 0.75) = **4** [1]; rise in income = 0.8bn × 4 = **3.2 billion cu** [1]; jobs = 3.2bn ÷ 40,000 [1] = **80,000** [1]
**(b)** In practice taxes and imports leak from the circular flow (the question assumed none), so the multiplier would be smaller [1]. Some firms raise prices or hours rather than hiring [1]. Unemployed workers may lack the skills for the vacancies, so structural unemployment remains [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* "Fewer jobs" needs a reason tied to the model. Naming leakages or a skills mismatch is a causal point.

**10. (a)** Subsidy: 4,000 ÷ 0.50 = **8,000 cu** [1]. Training: 2,500 ÷ 0.25 = **10,000 cu** [1]. The subsidy is cheaper per additional job [1].
**(b)** A subsidy may end the job when payments stop [1], whereas training raises productivity and lasting employability [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Divide by the share who gain, not multiply. The cost per additional job is always at least the cost per participant.

**11. (a)** Year 2 labour force = 15.9; rate = 1.8 ÷ 15.9 × 100 = **11.3%** [1]. Year 5 labour force = 16.2 [1]; rate = 1.0 ÷ 16.2 × 100 = **6.2%** [1]
**(b)** Year 1: 15.0 ÷ 22.0 × 100 = **68.2%** [1]. Year 5: 15.2 ÷ 22.0 × 100 = **69.1%** [1]
**(c)** Participation has risen (labour force 15.9 to 16.2 million), so more people are looking for work, and employment has not grown as fast [2]. Hysteresis may have raised the natural rate after the year 2 downturn, through skill loss among the long-term unemployed [2]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the data. Quoting the rise in the labour force turns a general point into an evaluated one.

**12.** Supply-side policies (training, incentives, mobility help) tackle structural, frictional and voluntary unemployment and so lower the natural rate [1]; they shift LRAS right [1]. Training raises skills and occupational mobility, which helps where jobs exist but workers lack the skills [2]. Demand-side policy raises AD and reduces cyclical unemployment quickly [1]. Beyond the natural rate it causes an inflationary gap, so the gain is temporary [1]. Evaluation: supply-side effects are slow, costly and uncertain [1]; in a deep recession with hysteresis, demand-side action is needed first [1]. The best choice depends on the type of unemployment, so a mix is likely to work best [1] and a reasoned judgement follows [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* A judgement that depends on the type and cause of unemployment, supported by your earlier analysis, tends to read better than a list of pros and cons.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Defining full employment as zero unemployment.
- Dividing unemployment by the entire population instead of the labour force.
- Placing a wage floor below the equilibrium wage on a diagram.
- Reading employment from supply rather than demand at a wage floor.
- Treating all benefits-related unemployment as involuntary.
- Listing mobility factors without saying which type of mobility they affect.
- Applying demand-side policy to structural unemployment without comment.
- Concluding without a judgement about which policy suits which cause.
