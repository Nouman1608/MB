The [full syllabus guide](/resources/ib-dp-economics-syllabus-guide/) sets out what each paper
weighs and the command-term hierarchy DP Economics uses. These notes turn that into an exam-day
plan -- diagram practice, evaluation-writing strategy, and what changes on HL's policy paper --
with a worked scenario and a checklist.

## Papers 1 and 2 (SL: three hours total, 70% of the grade)

Both papers test definitions, diagram construction, short-answer application and extended
essay-style evaluation. Because Economics is graded heavily on **evaluation** -- weighing up
arguments, not just describing them -- the highest-value exam preparation is not re-reading
definitions that are already secure, but deliberately practising argument-and-counter-argument
paragraphs: state a position, support it with theory and a diagram, then weigh a genuine
counter-argument or real-world limitation before reaching a judgement.

**Diagram practice deserves dedicated time on its own.** Nearly every unit is assessed partly
through the ability to draw, label and interpret a diagram correctly under exam conditions, and an
unlabelled diagram or a shift drawn in the wrong direction typically loses the marks attached to it
even when the surrounding written explanation is accurate. Practise sketching each major diagram
type (supply-and-demand shifts, aggregate demand/supply, trade and exchange-rate diagrams) from
memory, correctly labelled, until it takes under a minute -- this frees up thinking time in the exam
for the written analysis and evaluation that actually earns most of the marks.

## HL Paper 3: the policy paper

HL students sit a third paper -- a policy paper -- taking total external assessment to four hours
forty-five minutes and 80% of the HL grade. Paper 3 tests the ability to apply economic theory and
diagrams to a specific, often data-rich policy scenario, which means the exam-preparation priority
here shifts from recalling theory in the abstract to **applying it quickly to unfamiliar data and
policy contexts** -- practise reading a short policy scenario, identifying which unit(s) of the
syllabus it draws on, and building a diagram-supported answer under timed conditions, rather than
only revising Paper 3 content the same way as Papers 1 and 2.

## Worked practice scenario: building an evaluate-style answer

A Paper 2-style question asks: "Evaluate the view that indirect taxes are an effective way to
correct the market failure caused by a negative production externality."

```
Step 1 -- state the theory:     A negative production externality means the firm's
                                 private cost of production is lower than the true
                                 social cost, so the free market over-produces
                                 relative to the socially optimal quantity.

Step 2 -- diagram-supported
argument FOR:                   An indirect tax raises the firm's private cost
                                 closer to the social cost, shifting the supply
                                 curve left, reducing output toward the socially
                                 optimal quantity and internalising the
                                 externality -- assuming the tax is set at the
                                 correct level.

Step 3 -- counter-argument:     In practice, the government may not know the
                                 external cost precisely, so the tax could be set
                                 too high or too low; also, if demand for the good
                                 is highly price inelastic, the tax may raise
                                 revenue without significantly reducing quantity,
                                 doing little to correct the externality itself.

Step 4 -- reasoned judgement:   Indirect taxes can be effective where the
                                 externality's cost is reasonably well known and
                                 demand is not highly inelastic, but their
                                 effectiveness depends heavily on accurate
                                 information and the good's elasticity -- other
                                 tools (regulation, tradable permits) may suit
                                 cases where these conditions do not hold.
```

This state-theory, argue-for, counter-argue, judge structure -- always ending on an explicit
judgement rather than stopping at the analysis -- is the shape "evaluate" and "to what extent"
questions reward across every unit of the syllabus, not just externalities.

## Before/during exam checklist

- **Before the exam**: practise sketching every major diagram type from memory, correctly labelled,
  until it is fast and automatic; practise full evaluate-style paragraphs (state, argue for,
  counter-argue, judge) rather than only reviewing definitions; HL students should specifically
  practise applying theory to short, data-rich policy scenarios for Paper 3.
- **During Papers 1 and 2**: draw a diagram whenever a question's theory can be shown visually, and
  label every axis, curve and shift explicitly; on extended-response questions, always end with an
  explicit judgement, not just a list of points.
- **During HL Paper 3**: read the policy scenario fully before answering, and identify which
  syllabus unit(s) it draws on before building a diagram-supported response.
- **On every paper**: match the depth of your answer to the command term used -- "define"/"state"
  needs a precise short answer; "explain" needs a reasoned chain of cause and effect; "evaluate"/"to
  what extent" needs a weighed judgement.

## Self-test

1. Why does Economics reward practising evaluation specifically, rather than only content recall?
2. Why does an unlabelled diagram typically lose marks even if the written explanation is correct?
3. In the worked scenario, what does the counter-argument step add that the "argument for" step
   does not?
4. How does HL Paper 3's exam-preparation priority differ from Papers 1 and 2?
5. What four steps does a strong evaluate-style answer follow?

**Answers:** 1. Because Economics is graded heavily on evaluation -- weighing up arguments and
reaching a judgement, not just describing or applying theory -- so deliberate practice at
constructing weighed arguments pays off disproportionately compared with re-reading secure content.
2. Because diagrams are assessed as part of the answer in their own right; an unlabelled diagram or
one with a shift in the wrong direction loses the marks attached to it regardless of how accurate
the surrounding written explanation is. 3. It introduces a genuine limitation or opposing
consideration (e.g. imperfect government information, inelastic demand) that the first step's
one-sided argument does not address, which is necessary to reach a balanced judgement. 4. Paper 3
requires applying theory quickly to unfamiliar, often data-rich policy scenarios under time
pressure, rather than recalling and applying theory in the more general way Papers 1 and 2 test. 5.
State the relevant theory, build a diagram-supported argument for the claim, introduce a genuine
counter-argument or limitation, then reach an explicit, reasoned judgement.
