This guide covers section **3.5.1 Identifying and understanding customers** of OxfordAQA International GCSE Business (9225), part of Topic 5, Marketing. The specification asks you to understand the **importance of identifying and satisfying customer needs**, including in **local, national and international markets**, so that the business can provide a product or service that customers will buy. This is the starting point for all marketing: the later subtopics on segmentation, market research and the marketing mix all depend on knowing who the customers are and what they want. All businesses and figures in this guide are invented.

For the whole topic, see the [Marketing study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-business-marketing/). Condensed notes are in the [revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-business-9225-identifying-and-understanding-customers-revision-notes/) and questions are in the [practice set](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-business-9225-identifying-and-understanding-customers-practice/).

## What is marketing?

**Marketing** is the process of identifying, anticipating and satisfying customer needs profitably. The key words are "identifying" (finding out who the customers are and what they want), "satisfying" (providing it) and "profitably" (earning enough from sales to cover costs and make a profit). A business that produces what customers do not want will fail, however well it makes the product.

## Who are the customers?

A **customer** is a person or organisation that buys a business's goods or services. There are two broad types.

| Type | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Consumers | Individuals or households buying for personal use | Families buying food, a student buying a phone |
| Business customers | Organisations buying to use or resell | A hotel buying towels, a shop buying stock from a wholesaler |

Their needs differ. A consumer may care about style and price, while a business customer may care more about reliability, bulk discounts and delivery dates.

A business's **target market** is the group of customers it aims to sell to. Few products suit everyone, so businesses decide which customers matter most. (Segmentation is covered in the next subtopic.)

## Customer needs

**Customer needs** are the things customers want a product or service to do for them, or provide. They can be:

- **Functional:** the product works properly, is safe, durable and does the job (a raincoat keeps out the rain).
- **Price-related:** customers want value for money, or a price they can afford.
- **Convenience:** easy to find, buy and use, delivered quickly, with simple payment.
- **Emotional or social:** a product that gives a sense of status, belonging or enjoyment (a famous brand of trainers).
- **Service needs:** helpful advice, aftercare, quick replies and easy returns.

Needs change over time as tastes, incomes, technology and fashions change. A business must keep checking, since needs that were true last year may not be true next year.

### Why identifying customer needs is important

| Reason | Explanation |
|---|---|
| To sell products that customers will buy | Reduces the risk of making something nobody wants |
| To increase sales and revenue | A product that fits needs sells more and often at a higher price |
| To build customer satisfaction and loyalty | Satisfied customers come back and recommend the business |
| To stay ahead of competitors | A business that understands customers better can design better products or services |
| To use resources wisely | Money and effort are spent on what customers value, reducing waste |
| To spot changes and new opportunities | Noticing a trend early can create a new product or market |

### What happens if a business gets it wrong?

- Unsold stock and wasted production costs.
- Falling sales and profit as customers choose rivals.
- Poor reputation and negative reviews.
- Lost investment in design, advertising and equipment.

**Worked example 1: a product that missed the need.** A small company develops a premium bottled water for $1.50 a bottle and makes 20,000 bottles at a cost of $1.00 each. Local customers prefer the $0.80 supermarket brand, and only 6,000 bottles sell.

```
Costs   = 20,000 x $1.00 = $20,000
Revenue = 6,000 x $1.50  = $9,000
Loss    = $20,000 - $9,000 = $11,000
```

The company had not identified what customers wanted: cheap water for everyday use. Finding out first could have saved $11,000.

## How businesses find out what customers want

Businesses collect information on customers in several ways. (Market research methods are the subject of 3.5.3.)

- **Sales records:** what sells well, when and to whom.
- **Customer feedback:** complaints, reviews, comment cards, social media.
- **Surveys and interviews:** asking customers what they think.
- **Observation:** watching how customers behave in the shop or on the website.
- **Competitor analysis:** seeing what rivals offer and how customers respond.

**Worked example 2: using a survey.** A café asks 400 customers whether they would buy a vegetarian menu. 260 say yes.

```
Percentage interested = 260 / 400 x 100 = 65%
```

With 65% interested, a vegetarian menu looks promising. If the answer had been 26% (104 of 400), the café would need to think again. The result is still only a sample, and what people say they will do may differ from what they do.

## Local, national and international markets

The specification refers to three kinds of market, and customer needs can differ between them.

| Market | Meaning | What businesses need to understand |
|---|---|---|
| Local | Customers in the town or region | Local tastes, nearby competition, what residents can afford, community links |
| National | Customers across the whole country | Differences between regions, national tastes and habits, distribution across the country, national advertising |
| International | Customers in other countries | Language, culture, religion, laws, income levels, climate, standards and local rivals |

### Local markets

A local business can know its customers personally. A baker in a small town may know that customers prefer a certain loaf. The market is small, so the business may depend on a few customers and be hurt if a rival opens nearby.

### National markets

A national market is bigger, which allows higher sales, but customers are more varied. Businesses may offer different products in different regions, for example spicier food in one region. They need to reach all customers through shops, websites and national advertising.

### International markets

Selling abroad can bring many more customers but is harder, because customers in other countries may have different needs.

- **Language:** product labels, instructions and advertising must be translated.
- **Culture and religion:** a product or advert acceptable in one country may offend in another. Food may need to follow religious rules.
- **Income:** a price that is normal in one country may be too high in another.
- **Laws and standards:** safety rules, labelling and electrical plugs differ.
- **Climate and lifestyle:** a thick coat or air-conditioner designed for one climate may not suit another.

A business that assumes foreign customers want exactly the same product may fail. Many adapt the product, price, advertising or packaging for each market.

**Worked example 3: is adapting worth it?** Zesta Snacks sells in its home market. To sell abroad it must translate its labels ($5,000) and create a new flavour liked by local customers ($7,000). It expects to sell 3,200 extra units, earning $4 profit on each.

```
Adaptation cost = $5,000 + $7,000 = $12,000
Extra profit    = 3,200 x $4 = $12,800
Net gain        = $12,800 - $12,000 = $800
```

The net gain of $800 is small, so the business should be sure of its sales forecast. In later years, when there is no translation cost to pay again, the gain should be higher.

**Worked example 4: sales by market.** A company's annual sales were $120,000 locally, $300,000 nationally and $80,000 internationally.

```
Total sales = 120,000 + 300,000 + 80,000 = $500,000
Local = 24%, National = 60%, International = 16%
```

The company relies on its national market for 60% of its sales, which suggests it understands those customers best. It might aim to grow its international share, but would first need to research those customers' needs.

## Satisfying customer needs

Understanding customers is only useful if the business acts on it. A business satisfies customers by:

- designing products with the right features, quality and price
- offering good service before, during and after the sale
- making products easy to find and buy
- communicating accurately, so that customers know what is on offer
- listening to feedback and improving.

Satisfaction shows in repeat purchases, positive reviews and few complaints.

## Common mistakes

- Writing that customers' needs are "always the same". They change over time and between markets.
- Saying marketing is only advertising. Identifying needs comes first.
- Treating international customers as identical to home customers.
- Describing the importance of customers without linking it to sales, profit or loyalty.
- Confusing consumers (buying for personal use) with business customers.
- Giving a list of reasons without applying them to the business in the question.

## Exam technique for this section

For **define** and **state** questions, keep to the meaning of customer needs or the market. For **explain** questions, give a reason with a consequence: "if a business does not identify customer needs, it may make products that do not sell, so it will lose money". For **calculate** questions, show percentages and costs step by step. For **evaluate** questions, such as whether a business should sell abroad, compare the extra sales with the costs of adapting and the risk of misunderstanding customers' needs, then give a reasoned conclusion.
