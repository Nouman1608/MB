Assignment B of 4ET1 Component 3 is one coursework essay of about 650–800 words on a literary heritage
set text, marked out of 30 (AO1 10, AO2 10, AO4 10). Section A practises choosing and using context
within a tight word count. Section B gives full assignment tasks in the specification's style, with
indicative content. Choose the task on the text you have studied.

## Section A: Context and task skills

**1.** Rewrite this as a Component 3 Assignment B task: "Write about Lady Macbeth." **[2]**

**2.** A student's essay on *Great Expectations* opens with a 150-word paragraph about Dickens's
childhood and the history of London. Give two reasons why this is a weak use of an 800-word essay.
**[2]**

**3.** For a task on social rank in *Pride and Prejudice*, a student has collected four contexts. Choose
the **two** most useful and explain your choice. **[3]**

- (a) The entail of family property to male heirs
- (b) The Napoleonic Wars, fought while Austen was writing
- (c) The importance of rank, manners and connections among the landed gentry
- (d) The invention of the steam locomotive

**4.** Write one sentence of reception context about Capulet's treatment of Juliet when she refuses to
marry Paris. **[2]**

**5.** A teacher reading a draft wants to help. Give one thing the teacher may do and one thing the
teacher may not do. **[2]**

## Section B: Assignment B tasks (30 marks each)

**6.** How does Shakespeare present Lady Macbeth's power in *Macbeth*? You must consider language,
form and structure and refer to the context of the play. **[30]**

**7.** Explore the ways in which Shakespeare presents mercy and justice in *The Merchant of Venice*.
You must consider language, form and structure and refer to the context of the play. **[30]**

**8.** How does Austen present social rank in *Pride and Prejudice*? You must consider language, form
and structure and refer to the context of the novel. **[30]**

**9.** How does Dickens present crime and punishment in *Great Expectations*? You must consider
language, form and structure and refer to the context of the novel. **[30]**

**10.** "In *Romeo and Juliet*, the lovers are destroyed by fate rather than by the choices people
make." To what extent do you agree? You must consider language, form and structure and refer to the
context of the play. **[30]**

## Answers

**1.** For example: "How does Shakespeare present Lady Macbeth's influence over her husband in
*Macbeth*? You must consider language, form and structure and refer to the context of the play." [1
for a single, arguable focus; 1 for including the AO2 and AO4 requirements]

**2.** Any two [1 each]: the context is not linked to any moment in the novel, so it earns little AO4
credit; biography counts only where it sheds light on the text; it uses nearly a fifth of the word
count before any analysis (AO2) begins; it does not answer the task.

**3.** (a) and (c) [1]. The entail explains why rank and money matter so much to the Bennet family
[1]; the gentry's concern with rank and connections explains characters such as Lady Catherine and
Mr Collins and Darcy's first proposal [1]. (b) and (d) are not closely linked to the novel's
treatment of rank.

**4.** For example: "A modern audience may find Capulet's threat to throw Juliet out shocking, while
many in Shakespeare's audience would have accepted a father's right to choose his daughter's husband,
so the scene's effect depends on when it is watched." [1 for a specific moment, 1 for comparing
audiences]

**5.** May: give general advice on how the work could be improved [1]. May not: correct errors,
rewrite sections or provide detailed fixes [1].

### Level guidance for Questions 6–10

Mark on a best-fit basis using the five-level grid. Level 3 shows "sound" knowledge with relevant
context. Level 4 shows "detailed awareness" of the relationship between text and context. Level 5
integrates context "convincingly" within a perceptive, well-evidenced argument. Credit any valid
alternative to the points below.

### Question 6: indicative content

| AO | Points a strong answer might make |
|---|---|
| AO1 (10) | Lady Macbeth's response to the letter; her persuasion of Macbeth before the murder; her control after it, including the return of the daggers; her loss of influence as Macbeth acts alone; the sleepwalking scene and her death reported offstage |
| AO2 (10) | Her soliloquy calling on spirits to remove her womanly feelings; commanding imperatives and questions that challenge Macbeth's manhood; the shift from controlled verse to broken prose in the sleepwalking scene; structure that moves her from the centre to the margin of the play |
| AO4 (10) | Jacobean expectations of wifely obedience, which make her power unsettling; belief in evil spirits, which gives her invocation real menace; tragedy's pattern of rise and fall applied to her as well as to Macbeth; modern readings that see her as a woman with no legitimate outlet for ambition |

### Question 7: indicative content

| AO | Points a strong answer might make |
|---|---|
| AO1 (10) | The bond and its terms; Shylock's insistence on the law in the trial; Portia's plea for mercy; her use of the bond's wording to defeat him; the punishments imposed on Shylock, including forced conversion |
| AO2 (10) | Portia's speech on mercy in formal verse, with imagery of rain falling from heaven; repeated legal language of "bond" and law; the trial scene as the play's structural climax; the shift to Belmont's music and comedy afterwards, which leaves the harshness of the verdict unresolved |
| AO4 (10) | Christian teaching that set mercy above strict justice; prejudice against Jewish people in Elizabethan England; Christian disapproval of lending money at interest; modern audiences who question whether the Christians show the mercy they preach |

### Question 8: indicative content

| AO | Points a strong answer might make |
|---|---|
| AO1 (10) | Darcy's pride at the first ball; Mr Collins's respect for Lady Catherine; Lady Catherine's visit to Elizabeth; Darcy's first proposal and its insults to her family; the Gardiners as respectable people in trade whom Darcy comes to value |
| AO2 (10) | Ironic narration that exposes snobbery; Mr Collins's long, flattering speech as comedy; the confrontation with Lady Catherine as a structural test of Elizabeth's independence; Darcy's letter as a turning point that changes both characters' views of rank |
| AO4 (10) | The landed gentry's concern with rank, land and connections; the entail of Longbourn; growing wealth from trade in the period; comedy of manners as a form that satirises social behaviour |

### Question 9: indicative content

| AO | Points a strong answer might make |
|---|---|
| AO1 (10) | The escaped convict on the marshes in the opening chapters; the soldiers' pursuit; Jaggers and his office near Newgate; Magwitch's return and Pip's horror; Magwitch's trial and death |
| AO2 (10) | Gothic imagery of the misty marshes and the prison ships; Pip's retrospective narration, which shows his changing judgement of Magwitch; the structure in which the convict of the first chapters turns out to be Pip's benefactor; contrast between Magwitch's loyalty and the cold respectability of others |
| AO4 (10) | Victorian transportation of convicts to Australia and the death penalty for an illegal return; harsh prisons and public attitudes to criminals; the gentleman as a social ideal that Magwitch's money undermines; the novel's first publication in weekly parts, using suspense around the benefactor |

### Question 10: indicative content

| AO | Points a strong answer might make |
|---|---|
| AO1 (10) | The Prologue's "star-crossed" lovers; Romeo's sense of foreboding before the Capulet feast; Friar Lawrence's plan and the undelivered letter; the hasty choices of Romeo, Juliet, Tybalt and Capulet; a judgement that weighs fate against human decisions |
| AO2 (10) | Imagery of stars and fortune; the Prologue's sonnet telling the ending in advance, so the audience watches inevitability unfold; the compressed time scheme that increases haste; dramatic irony in the tomb scene |
| AO4 (10) | Elizabethan beliefs in fortune, astrology and providence; a father's authority over his daughter's marriage and the honour code behind the feud as human causes; tragedy as a genre that blends a hero's choices with forces beyond control |

A "to what extent" answer should reach a judgement, for example that Shakespeare uses fate to frame
the play but makes the characters' haste and the feud the forces that actually close it.
