## What fiscal policy is

Fiscal policy means changing government spending and taxes to steer aggregate demand and the wider economy. It sits within demand management, alongside monetary policy. Unlike monetary policy, it is set by the government itself.

### Sources of revenue

The guide lists four sources.

- **Direct taxes** are paid by the person or firm that bears the burden, such as income tax and corporate profits tax.
- **Indirect taxes** are collected from a seller but passed on in prices, such as a sales tax or a tax on fuel.
- **Sales of goods and services by state-owned enterprises**, for example fares from a publicly owned railway.
- **Sale of government assets**, for example selling land or shares in a public company. This is usually a one-off receipt, so it is a weak basis for planning permanent spending.

### Types of expenditure

- **Current expenditure** pays for the day-to-day running of government: wages, supplies, upkeep.
- **Capital expenditure** buys or builds assets that last, such as roads, schools and hospitals.
- **Transfer payments** pass money from taxpayers to recipients with no output produced in return, such as unemployment benefits and pensions.

### Worked example: a budget table

Veldara is an illustrative economy. All figures are in billions of veld.

| Revenue | Amount | Expenditure | Amount |
|---|---|---|---|
| Income tax | 210 | Current | 310 |
| Corporate profits tax | 90 | Capital | 75 |
| Sales tax | 150 | Transfer payments | 105 |
| State-owned enterprise sales | 12 | | |
| Sale of a government asset | 8 | | |

- Total revenue = 210 + 90 + 150 + 12 + 8 = 470.
- Direct tax revenue = 210 + 90 = 300. Indirect tax revenue = 150.
- Total expenditure = 310 + 75 + 105 = 490.
- Budget balance = revenue − expenditure = 470 − 490 = −20. Veldara runs a deficit of 20 billion veld.

The 8 from the asset sale will not recur. Without it the shortfall is 28.

## Goals of fiscal policy

The guide names six goals. For each, you should be able to say what the government does and how it works.

| Goal | Typical fiscal action |
|---|---|
| Low and stable inflation | Cut spending or raise taxes when demand is too strong |
| Low unemployment | Raise spending or cut taxes when demand is weak |
| A stable environment for long-term growth | Predictable tax rules; capital spending on infrastructure |
| Reduced business cycle fluctuations | Counter-cyclical spending and tax changes |
| Equitable distribution of income | Progressive taxes and transfer payments |
| External balance | Policies that restrain spending on imports, or support exports, to move the current account towards balance |

Goals can conflict: a tax cut that cuts unemployment may add to inflation.

## Expansionary and contractionary policy

**Expansionary fiscal policy** raises government spending, cuts taxes, or both. It shifts AD to the right. It targets a deflationary (recessionary) gap, where actual output is below potential.

**Contractionary fiscal policy** cuts government spending, raises taxes, or both. It shifts AD to the left. It targets an inflationary gap, where actual output is above potential.

The guide asks for diagrams in two schools of thought, and the shape of AS is what separates them.

### Keynesian view

The Keynesian AS curve is flat when there is a lot of spare capacity, slopes upwards as capacity tightens, and becomes vertical at full employment. The economy can settle at an equilibrium below full employment, and the guide notes that deflationary gaps can persist. Government action is therefore a way to speed up recovery.

**To draw it:** axes are price level (vertical) and real GDP (horizontal). Draw AS flat, then rising, then vertical at potential output Yf. Draw AD1 cutting the flat part at Y1, left of Yf. Shift AD to AD2.

Output rises towards Yf and the price level rises little, because AS is flat there. If AD shifted well beyond Yf, only prices would climb, which is why over-shooting is a risk.

### Monetarist/new classical view

In this view LRAS is vertical at potential output, and the economy returns to it automatically. Expansionary fiscal policy shifts AD right, and in the short run output rises along SRAS. Wages and other costs then adjust, SRAS shifts left, and output returns to potential at a higher price level. The long-run result is inflation without extra output. 

**To draw it:** draw a vertical LRAS at Yf and a rising SRAS1 crossing it. Draw AD1 cutting SRAS1 at Yf, then shift AD right to AD2. Output moves above Yf. Then shift SRAS left to SRAS2, and the new equilibrium is back at Yf with a higher price level.

On both diagrams, label the axes, every curve, the old and new equilibrium, and potential output.

## The Keynesian multiplier (HL only)

An injection into the circular flow causes a larger rise in GDP, because one person's spending becomes another's income and part of it is spent again. The size depends on how much leaks out in each round.

The guide gives two forms.

- Simple form: **multiplier = 1 / (1 − MPC)**, where MPC is the marginal propensity to consume.
- Fuller form: **multiplier = 1 / (MPS + MPT + MPM)**, where MPS is the marginal propensity to save, MPT to tax and MPM to import.

The two agree when saving is the only leakage, because then 1 − MPC = MPS. When tax and imports also take a share of extra income, use the fuller form.

Change in GDP = multiplier × change in the injection. The injection may be investment, government spending or exports.

### Worked example 1: the fuller form

Veldara has MPS = 0.08, MPT = 0.17 and MPM = 0.15. Government spending rises by 24 billion veld.

```
Leakages: 0.08 + 0.17 + 0.15 = 0.4
Multiplier: 1 divided by 0.4 = 2.5
GDP effect: 2.5 times 24 = 60
```

GDP rises by 60 billion veld. Working backwards, a recessionary gap of 60 needs an injection of 60 / 2.5 = 24.

### Worked example 2: the simple form

A different closed economy has MPC = 0.8 and no taxes. Investment rises by 3.6.

```
Multiplier    = 1 / (1 - 0.8) = 1 / 0.2 = 5
Change in GDP = 5 x 3.6 = 18
```

A cut of 4 in government spending would lower GDP by 5 × 4 = 20.

### Worked example 3: a cut

Veldara's inflationary gap is 40 and the multiplier is 2.5. A cut in government spending of 40 / 2.5 = 16 closes the gap on this model.

The calculation assumes leakage rates stay fixed and treats the whole effect as real output. In practice some appears as higher prices, especially near full employment.

## Effectiveness of fiscal policy

The guide lists constraints and strengths, then asks you to judge how well fiscal policy delivers growth, low unemployment and low, stable inflation. "Evaluate" asks you to weigh strengths against limitations.

### Constraints

- **Political pressure.** Tax rises and spending cuts are unpopular, while tax cuts and spending rises are easy to sell. A needed contraction may be blocked.
- **Time lags.** Recognising a problem, passing a policy and spending the money all take time, so policy meant for a slump can arrive during a recovery.
- **Sustainable debt.** Deficits add to the national debt. Higher debt means higher interest payments, which use up future budgets and may deter lenders.
- **Crowding out (HL only).** Borrowing to finance a deficit competes with private borrowers for funds. Interest rates can rise, which cuts private investment.

### Crowding out in numbers (HL only)

Veldara raises spending by 20 and borrows it all. Interest rates rise, and private investment falls by 8. The net injection is 20 − 8 = 12. With a multiplier of 2.5, GDP rises by 12 × 2.5 = 30, not the 20 × 2.5 = 50 that ignoring crowding out would suggest.

On an AD/AS diagram, show it in two steps. AD shifts right because of government spending, then shifts back part of the way as investment falls. The final AD sits to the right of where it began but to the left of the first shift. In the monetarist/new classical extreme the offset is complete.

### Strengths

- **Targeting.** Spending can be aimed at a sector, a region or a group, such as a depressed area. A single interest rate cannot do this.
- **Effective in a deep recession.** When confidence is low and households and firms will not borrow even at low interest rates, government spending adds demand directly.

### Automatic stabilisers (HL only)

The guide lists progressive taxes and unemployment benefits. They need no new decision, so they avoid the lags above. When incomes fall, tax payments fall faster than incomes and benefit payments rise, which softens the drop in disposable income. In a boom the effect reverses and restrains demand.

Example. Veldara's income tax is 10% on the first 100 and 30% above that. A household's income falls from 180 to 140.

```
Tax at 180 = 0.10 x 100 + 0.30 x 80 = 10 + 24 = 34
Tax at 140 = 0.10 x 100 + 0.30 x 40 = 10 + 12 = 22
Tax falls by 12
Disposable income: 146 falls to 118, a drop of 28
```

Income fell by 40, but disposable income fell by only 28, so 30% of the shock was absorbed. Under a flat 20% tax the tax would fall from 36 to 28, an 8 reduction, and disposable income would drop by 32.

### Growth, unemployment and inflation

Fiscal policy can support growth through infrastructure and by lifting demand in a slump. It can reduce cyclical unemployment when output is below potential, but does little for unemployment caused by a skills mismatch. It can restrain inflation by cutting demand, at the cost of output and jobs.

## Common errors

- Mixing up direct and indirect taxes, or treating a transfer payment as spending on goods and services.
- Using one diagram for both the Keynesian and the monetarist/new classical view.
- Stopping at the multiplier and forgetting to multiply by the injection.
- Describing crowding out as an effect that happens whenever the government spends, rather than when it borrows and interest rates rise.
- Listing strengths and limits with no judgment about which matters more.
