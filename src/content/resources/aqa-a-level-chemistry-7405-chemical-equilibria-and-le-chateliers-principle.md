## Dynamic equilibrium

In a reversible reaction the products can react to re-form the reactants. In a **closed system** (nothing enters or leaves), the reaction reaches a state of **dynamic equilibrium**, in which:

- the **rate of the forward reaction equals the rate of the reverse reaction**, and
- the **concentrations** of reactants and products **remain constant**.

"Dynamic" means that both reactions are still going on. The system looks unchanged because the rates are equal, not because the reactions have stopped. This distinguishes it from a reaction that has finished.

Equilibrium can be approached from either side. Starting with only the reactants, or only the products, the same equilibrium mixture forms at the same temperature.

The **position** of equilibrium describes the relative amounts of reactants and products. If the position lies to the right, there is more product at equilibrium. If it lies to the left, there is more reactant.

## Le Chatelier's principle

**When a system at equilibrium is subjected to a change, the position of equilibrium shifts to oppose the change.**

The principle predicts the direction of the shift. It does not explain the rates, and it says nothing about how fast the new equilibrium is reached.

### Concentration

| Change | Shift | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Increase concentration of a reactant | To the right (products) | Uses up the added reactant |
| Decrease concentration of a reactant | To the left | Replaces some of the removed reactant |
| Increase concentration of a product | To the left | Uses up the added product |
| Remove a product | To the right | Replaces the removed product |

Removing a product as it forms is one way to increase the yield of a reversible reaction.

### Pressure (gases only)

A higher pressure shifts the position towards the side with **fewer moles of gas**, which lowers the pressure. A lower pressure shifts it towards the side with more moles of gas. If the numbers of moles of gas are equal on both sides, pressure has no effect on the position.

### Temperature

| Change | Shift | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Increase temperature | In the **endothermic** direction | Absorbs the added heat |
| Decrease temperature | In the **exothermic** direction | Releases heat |

For a forward reaction with ΔH negative (exothermic), a higher temperature shifts the position to the left and reduces the yield of the products.

### Catalyst

A catalyst has **no effect** on the position of equilibrium. It speeds up the forward and reverse reactions equally, so the equilibrium is reached sooner but the composition is the same.

## Worked examples

### Worked example 1: the Haber process

N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) ⇌ 2NH₃(g)  ΔH = −92 kJ mol⁻¹

The forward reaction is exothermic and the number of moles of gas falls from 4 to 2.

| Change | Effect on position | Explanation |
|---|---|---|
| Increase pressure | Right | Fewer moles of gas on the right (2 against 4) |
| Increase temperature | Left | The reverse reaction is endothermic and absorbs the added heat |
| Remove NH₃ | Right | Replaces the ammonia removed |
| Add iron catalyst | None | Rates of both reactions increase equally |
| Add more N₂ | Right | Uses up the added nitrogen |

### Worked example 2: a colour change

The ions in cobalt(II) chloride solution are in equilibrium:

[Co(H₂O)₆]²⁺(aq) + 4Cl⁻(aq) ⇌ [CoCl₄]²⁻(aq) + 6H₂O(l)

The hexaaqua complex is pink and the tetrachloro complex is blue.

Adding concentrated hydrochloric acid increases the concentration of Cl⁻, so the equilibrium shifts to the right and the solution turns blue. Adding water dilutes the chloride, so the equilibrium shifts to the left and the solution turns pink. The forward reaction is endothermic, so heating a pink solution makes it more blue.

### Worked example 3: no effect of pressure

H₂(g) + I₂(g) ⇌ 2HI(g)

There are 2 moles of gas on each side, so a change in pressure does not shift the position of equilibrium. It changes the rate at which equilibrium is reached.

### Worked example 4: effect on the rate and the position

Increasing the temperature of an exothermic reaction at equilibrium increases both the rate of the forward and reverse reactions. The reverse reaction increases more, because it has the higher activation energy, so the new equilibrium lies further to the left. The rate and position are different ideas: a higher temperature gives a faster approach to a position with less product.

## Industrial compromise

In an industrial process the manufacturer wants a **high yield**, a **high rate** and a **low cost**. These aims often conflict.

### Worked example 5: ammonia (Haber process)

The forward reaction is exothermic and has fewer moles of gas.

- A **low temperature** would favour the product, but the rate would be too slow, so the process would be uneconomic.
- A **high pressure** favours the product and increases the rate, but very high pressure needs strong, expensive equipment and uses a lot of energy for compression, and is a safety hazard.

A **compromise** is used: a temperature of about 450 °C, a pressure of about 200 atmospheres, and an iron catalyst. These give a moderate yield (the equilibrium mixture contains a fairly low percentage of ammonia) at an acceptable rate. Unreacted nitrogen and hydrogen are recycled, and the ammonia is removed by cooling and liquefying, so the overall conversion is high.

### Worked example 6: ethanol from ethene

C₂H₄(g) + H₂O(g) ⇌ C₂H₅OH(g)  ΔH = −45 kJ mol⁻¹

Three moles of gas become two, and the reaction is exothermic. Yield favours low temperature and high pressure. In industry a compromise of about 300 °C and 60–70 atmospheres is used with a phosphoric acid catalyst, because lower temperature gives too slow a rate, and a higher pressure would increase costs and cause polymerisation of ethene.

### Writing a compromise answer

A good answer states: (1) what the conditions that favour the yield are; (2) why they are not used in full (rate, cost, safety); (3) the compromise chosen; (4) the role of the catalyst.

## Common mistakes

- Writing that the reactions "stop" at equilibrium.
- Not stating that the system is closed.
- Saying that the position shifts to "increase" the change instead of "oppose" it.
- Applying pressure arguments to a reaction with no change in the moles of gas.
- Saying that a catalyst increases the yield.
- Forgetting that temperature changes both the rate and the position.
- Mentioning only the effect on yield and not on rate when discussing compromise.
- Counting solids and liquids in the moles of gas.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Describe** equilibrium: forward and reverse rates equal; concentrations constant; closed system.
- **Predict**: state the direction of the shift, and give the reason in terms of Le Chatelier.
- **Explain** a compromise: yield, rate and cost.
- **State** the effect of a catalyst: none on the position, but the equilibrium is reached sooner.

## Frequently asked questions

**Does a change in concentration change the value of Kc?**
No. Kc changes only with temperature. The position shifts so that the ratio of concentrations returns to the same value (see the next subtopic).

**Does Le Chatelier's principle tell me how fast equilibrium is restored?**
No. It tells you only the direction of the shift.

**Why does a high pressure not help if the moles of gas are equal?**
The system has no way to reduce the pressure by shifting, so the position is unchanged.
