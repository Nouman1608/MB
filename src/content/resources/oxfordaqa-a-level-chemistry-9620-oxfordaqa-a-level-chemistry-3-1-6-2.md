This guide covers **3.1.6.2 Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution** from the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Chemistry (9620) specification, Version 5.3. It is International AS content, examined in Unit 2. The specification names the **Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution of molecular energies in gases**, and you must be able to **draw and interpret distribution curves for different temperatures**.

The [kinetics overview](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-kinetics/) lists the drawing rules and shows how to count squares. This page explains **why** the curve has each feature, gives a checklist for spotting faulty sketches, and works through interpretation questions, including what happens when the number of molecules changes. ## What the curve represents

In a sample of gas, molecules collide constantly and exchange energy at random. At any instant, some have little energy, many have a moderate amount and a few have a lot. The **Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution** is a graph of this spread:

- horizontal axis: **energy** of the molecules;
- vertical axis: **number of molecules** with that energy (sometimes labelled "fraction of molecules");
- the **area under the curve** represents the **total number of molecules** in the sample.

It describes a gas at a **fixed temperature**. The specification applies it to gases only.

## Why each feature is there

| Feature | Reason |
|---|---|
| Starts at the origin | No molecule has zero energy; very few have very low energy |
| Rises to one peak | Most molecules have energies near the middle of the range |
| Peak = most probable energy | The energy possessed by the largest number of molecules |
| Not symmetrical; long tail at high energy | Energy cannot be less than zero, but there is no upper limit, so the curve is stretched to the right |
| Tail approaches but never touches the energy axis | A few molecules always have very high energy; there is no maximum |
| Mean energy lies to the right of the peak | The long high-energy tail pulls the average up |
| Area fixed for a given sample | The number of molecules does not change |

**Activation energy on the curve.** Ea is drawn as a vertical line at an energy well out in the tail. The area to the right of this line represents the molecules with energy **≥ Ea**, the ones that can react on collision. For most reactions this is a small fraction of the total.

## Curves for different temperatures

For the **same sample** at a **higher** temperature:

1. the peak moves to **higher energy** (to the right);
2. the peak is **lower**;
3. the curve is **flatter and wider**;
4. the **total area is the same**, because the number of molecules has not changed;
5. the two curves **cross once**: at low energies the cooler curve is higher, and at high energies the hotter curve is higher;
6. the area beyond Ea is **larger**.

Points 2 and 3 follow from point 4. If the molecules are spread across a wider range of energies, and the total is fixed, fewer can have any one energy, so the peak must be lower.

At a **lower** temperature the opposite applies: a taller, narrower curve with its peak to the left, and less area beyond Ea.

## Checklist for spotting faulty sketches

When a question shows a curve or asks you to judge one, check:

- Does it start at the origin?
- Is it asymmetric, with the long tail on the high-energy side?
- Does the tail stay above the axis rather than touching or crossing it?
- For two temperatures, is the hotter curve's peak lower and to the right?
- Do the two curves enclose the same area (no hotter curve sitting above the cooler one everywhere)?
- Is Ea marked on the energy axis, not the number axis?

### Worked example 1: evaluating a sketch

A student draws two curves for one gas sample at 300 K and 350 K. The 350 K curve has its peak to the right of the 300 K curve, but higher, and it is above the 300 K curve at every energy. Identify the errors and explain them.

**Answer.**

- The 350 K peak should be **lower**, not higher.
- The 350 K curve cannot be above the 300 K curve at every energy: it would then enclose a **larger area**, implying more molecules. The same sample has the same number of molecules, so the areas must be equal and the curves must cross.

### Worked example 2: reading proportions

For one gas sample, 0.8 % of molecules have E ≥ Ea at temperature T₁ and 2.9 % at temperature T₂.

- T₂ is the **higher** temperature, because more of the distribution lies beyond Ea.
- The proportion with E ≥ Ea has increased by 2.9 ÷ 0.8 ≈ **3.6 times**.
- If collision frequency hardly changes, the number of successful collisions per second, and so the rate, increases by roughly this factor. The [effect of temperature on rate](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-1-6-3/) builds on exactly this reasoning.

## Same temperature, different number of molecules

The shape and position of the curve depend on **temperature**. The **height** of the curve also depends on how many molecules are in the sample.

### Worked example 3

A sealed flask of gas at 298 K has more of the same gas pumped in, and the temperature is returned to 298 K. Describe the new curve.

- The temperature is unchanged, so the **peak stays at the same energy** and the shape is the same.
- There are more molecules, so the curve is **higher at every energy** and the **total area is larger**.
- The **fraction** of molecules with E ≥ Ea is unchanged, but the **number** with E ≥ Ea increases in proportion.

This contrasts with heating, where the area stays the same and the peak moves. Being clear about which change keeps the area fixed is a common source of marks.

## Interpreting the energies

- **Most probable energy**: read at the peak.
- **Mean energy**: higher than the most probable energy, because of the tail.
- Increasing temperature increases **both**; it increases the mean kinetic energy of the molecules.

## Common mistakes

- Labelling the vertical axis "energy" or "rate"; it is the number (or fraction) of molecules.
- Drawing a symmetrical, bell-shaped curve.
- Letting the tail touch or cross the energy axis.
- Drawing the hotter curve with a taller peak.
- Making the hotter curve enclose more area than the cooler one.
- Putting Ea on the left side of the curve, near the peak, for a typical reaction.
- Saying heating raises Ea; Ea does not change with temperature.

## Exam technique

- **Draw** or **sketch**: label both axes, start at the origin, keep the tail above the axis, and mark Ea and the area beyond it if relevant.
- For two temperatures, **label** each curve with its temperature and make the peak positions and heights clearly different.
- **Interpret**: refer to areas ("the area to the right of Ea is larger") rather than to the height of the curve at Ea alone.
- **Explain** a feature: give the physical reason, such as "no molecules have zero energy" or "the number of molecules is unchanged".

## Frequently asked questions

**Why does the Maxwell–Boltzmann curve start at the origin?**
No molecules in a gas have zero energy, so the number of molecules at zero energy is zero. Very few molecules have very low energies.

**Why is the peak lower at a higher temperature?**
The number of molecules, and so the area under the curve, is unchanged. At the higher temperature the energies are spread over a wider range, so fewer molecules have any one energy and the peak is lower.

**Does the curve ever touch the energy axis at high energy?**
No. There is no maximum energy, so a small number of molecules always have very high energies and the tail approaches the axis without reaching it.
