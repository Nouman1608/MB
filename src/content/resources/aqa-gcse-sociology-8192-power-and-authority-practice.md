## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** A hereditary monarch's authority is best described by Weber as:

A. Charismatic
B. Rational-legal
C. Traditional
D. Informal

**[1]**

**2.** Power based on force or the threat of force is called:

A. Authority
B. Coercion
C. Legitimacy
D. Consensus

**[1]**

**3.** Define the term "authority". **[2]**

**4.** Describe what Weber meant by charismatic authority. **[2]**

**5.** Identify and explain **one** feature of a bureaucracy. **[3]**

**6.** Explain the difference between formal and informal sources of power, using an example of each. **[3]**

### Section B: longer and structured questions

**7.** Read the item.

> The Greenway Climate Collective (a fictional campaign group) was founded by Tolu, a gifted speaker whose videos attract thousands of followers. After Tolu stepped down, members wrote a constitution, elected a committee and set up a membership fee.

**(a)** Using the item, identify the type of authority Tolu held and explain why. **[2]**
**(b)** Using the item, explain what Weber would call the change that happened after Tolu stepped down. **[2]**

**8.** Read the item.

> At Riverside High (a fictional school), the deputy head can put pupils in detention under the behaviour policy. In Year 10, a group led by a popular pupil, Jordan, decides which classmates are invited to weekend events. Pupils who annoy Jordan are ignored by the group for days.

Using the item, explain **two** different sources of power. **[4]**

**9.** Explain **one** strength and **one** weakness of rational-legal authority. **[4]**

**10.** Explain how Marxists and feminists would each criticise the functionalist view of power and authority. **[4]**

**11.** Discuss how far sociologists would agree that power in the UK is shared fairly between different groups. **[12]**

**12.** Discuss how far sociologists would agree that Weber's three types of authority are still useful for understanding power today. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** C. Traditional [1] **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* a title and a formal role can tempt students towards rational-legal, but inherited status is the key to traditional authority.

**2.** B. Coercion [1] **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* coercion and authority are the two forms of power; learn them as a pair.

**3.** Power that is accepted as legitimate [1], so people obey because they believe the person or rule has the right to be obeyed, not because they are forced [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* "power" alone is not enough; the mark is for legitimacy.

**4.** Authority based on devotion to a leader who is believed to have exceptional personal qualities [1], so people obey because of who the leader is rather than because of rules or tradition [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* "a nice or popular person" misses the idea of devotion to exceptional qualities.

**5.** One feature is a hierarchy of offices [1], in which each official is supervised by the one above [1]. This means orders pass down a clear chain of command, so everyone knows who has authority over whom [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* written rules, specialisation, impersonality and appointment on merit are equally valid features.

**6.** Formal power comes from an official position, law or rule, such as a police officer arresting someone under the law [1]. Informal power comes from personal influence, relationships or money, such as peer pressure from friends [1]. The difference is that formal power is written into rules and backed by formal sanctions, whereas informal power is not [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* the third mark needs an explicit statement of difference, not just two examples.

**7. (a)** Tolu held charismatic authority [1], because people followed Tolu for exceptional personal qualities as a gifted speaker, not because of any rule or position [1].
**(b)** Weber would call it the routinisation of charisma [1]: after the leader left, the group turned personal devotion into lasting rules, an elected committee and a constitution, which is rational-legal authority [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* name the process and link it to the constitution or election in the item.

**8.** First, the deputy head has a formal source of power [1], because the right to give detention comes from the school's written behaviour policy and her official position [1]. Second, Jordan has an informal source of power [1], because Jordan holds no official role but controls invitations, and the group ignoring pupils is an informal sanction [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* the item gives you a sanction for each type; using them shows application.

**9.** Strength: rational-legal authority is based on shared impersonal rules, so the same rules apply to everyone [1], which makes decisions predictable and fairer in principle, and power passes smoothly to a new post-holder [1]. Weakness: rules can become rigid "red tape" [1], so officials may follow procedures even when they produce unfair or unhelpful results for individuals [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* another weakness is that authority can be questioned if the rules are seen as unfair.

**10.** Marxists argue that power follows ownership of the means of production [1], so what functionalists call legitimate authority is really the product of ruling class ideology that makes rules benefiting owners look fair [1]. Feminists argue that formal positions of power have mostly been held by men [1], so authority serves male interests rather than the whole society, as functionalists claim [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* begin with the functionalist claim being criticised (power used for collective goals) to make the contrast clear.

**11.** The real exam marks extended answers using levels of response, not point by point. The points below show indicative creditworthy content.

- Pluralists argue power is spread among competing groups, such as parties, pressure groups and unions [1].
- Elections allow citizens to remove governments, so no group holds power permanently [1].
- Functionalists see power as a resource for collective goals, made legitimate by value consensus [1].
- Rational-legal authority means rules apply equally to everyone [1].
- Against: Marxists argue power follows ownership of the means of production [1].
- Political power comes from economic power, so wealthy owners have more influence [1].
- Informal power, such as money and media ownership, can shape decisions outside formal rules [1].
- Feminists point to male domination of Parliament, business and religious organisations [1].
- Walby's structures of patriarchy include the state and paid work [1].
- Weber: status and party mean power is not only economic, so the picture is more complex than Marx suggests [1].
- Women's and minority groups' share of formal positions has grown, supporting some pluralist claims [1].
- Judgement: power is more widely spread than in the past, but class and gender still affect access to it, so it is not shared fairly [1]. **[12]**

*Examiner insight:* "fairly" is the key word; your judgement must say whether the spread is fair, not just whether it exists.

**12.** The real exam marks extended answers using levels of response, not point by point. The points below show indicative creditworthy content.

- Weber described traditional, charismatic and rational-legal authority [1].
- Rational-legal authority describes modern institutions such as Parliament, courts and police [1].
- Bureaucracy runs schools, hospitals and large companies, showing rational-legal authority is widespread [1].
- Traditional authority still exists, for example in the monarchy [1].
- Charismatic authority is seen in religious leaders and leaders of social movements [1].
- The routinisation of charisma explains how movements become organisations [1].
- Weber's types are ideal types, so real leaders can mix them, which makes the model flexible [1].
- Against: the types do not explain informal power, such as peer pressure or media influence [1].
- Marxists argue the types describe forms of legitimacy but hide the economic basis of power [1].
- Feminists argue the types ignore how gender shapes who gets to hold authority [1].
- Online influence and celebrity may create new forms of power that do not fit neatly into the three types [1].
- Judgement: the types remain useful for classifying why people obey, but must be combined with other perspectives to explain who holds power and why [1]. **[12]**

*Examiner insight:* "still useful" asks for contemporary examples; give at least one modern case for each type.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using "power" and "authority" as the same word.
- Calling a monarch's authority rational-legal.
- Giving examples of formal and informal power without stating the difference.
- Describing the item without naming Weber's terms.
- Leaving out the feminist perspective.
