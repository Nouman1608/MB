This guide covers one sub-section of OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Business (9725), Version 1.1: **3.1.2.2 Marketing data**, in Unit 1 Business and markets (International AS). The tables of advantages and disadvantages for each research method are already set out in the combined [marketing objectives and marketing data guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-business-marketing-objectives-and-plans-and-marketing-data/); this page goes further into the judgements the specification asks for: choosing a method for a situation, judging a sample, and using correlation and confidence intervals to make decisions. Condense it afterwards with the [marketing data revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-business-9625-9725-marketing-data-9725-revision-notes/) and test yourself with the [marketing data practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-business-9625-9725-marketing-data-9725-practice/).

Every business and figure here is invented.

## What the specification asks for

**Market research**

- Advantages and disadvantages of primary research: surveys/questionnaires, interviews, focus groups, observations.
- Advantages and disadvantages of secondary research: websites/social media, academic journals, government publications, media articles.
- The value of qualitative and quantitative data.
- Advantages and disadvantages of random, stratified and quota samples, and the value of sampling.
- The value of primary and secondary market research.
- Influences on choosing a form of research: cost, time required, reliability of findings.

**Interpretation of marketing data**

- Interpreting and using marketing data in decision making and planning.
- The sign and size of a correlation value (positive or negative, strong or weak).
- The concept of confidence levels and confidence intervals, for example the significance of a 95% confidence level.

You are not asked to calculate a correlation coefficient or a confidence interval; you are asked to understand and use them.

## Start from the decision, not the method

Research is worth paying for only if it reduces the risk of a decision. So the first question in any case is: **what does the business need to know, and how sure does it need to be?**

| Decision | What it needs to know | Sensible first step |
|---|---|---|
| Is the market for plant-based snacks growing? | Market size and trend | Secondary: government publications, media articles |
| Which of two pack designs do shoppers prefer? | Preference, with numbers | Primary: survey with closed questions |
| Why are customers cancelling subscriptions? | Reasons and feelings | Primary: interviews or a focus group |
| Do shoppers notice the new shelf display? | Actual behaviour | Primary: observation |

**Quantitative data** (numbers) answers "how many" and "how much", and can be compared over time or between groups. **Qualitative data** (opinions, reasons) answers "why". Closed questions such as "Would you buy this? Yes/No" produce quantitative data; open questions such as "What would make you switch?" produce qualitative data. A firm that only collects numbers may know that sales are falling without knowing why; a firm that only collects opinions from a few people cannot be sure those views are typical.

## Judging secondary sources

Secondary data is cheap and fast but was collected for someone else's purpose. Four checks:

1. **Who produced it?** A government statistics office has large samples and no product to sell; a trade body promoting its industry may be optimistic.
2. **When?** Data from three years ago may miss a recent change in tastes.
3. **For what purpose?** Academic journals test ideas rigorously but rarely match one firm's product or region.
4. **How representative?** Social media comments come from the customers who choose to post, often the most delighted or the most annoyed.

## Cost, time and reliability: a worked choice

**Case.** Larkspur Teas, a small online tea seller with a marketing budget of $5,000, wants to know whether customers would pay more for compostable tea bags. It must decide within three weeks, before ordering packaging.

- A national survey firm could interview 1,000 tea drinkers: reliable, but costs more than the whole budget and takes six weeks. **Rejected on cost and time.**
- A focus group of eight customers is cheap and fast and would reveal reasons, but eight people cannot show what proportion of customers would pay more. **Useful but not enough on its own.**
- An online questionnaire emailed to Larkspur's 6,000 past customers, with a small discount for replying, is cheap and quick. If 600 reply, the sample is large enough to give a reasonably reliable percentage. **Best fit**, with one weakness: people who reply may care more about the environment than those who do not.

The judgement weighs all three influences and names the remaining risk. That is the pattern examiners reward.

## Sampling

**Value of sampling.** Asking every customer is usually too slow and costly. A well-chosen sample gives results close to the whole population at a fraction of the cost. The larger and more representative the sample, the more reliable the findings, but each extra respondent adds cost.

**Worked example: a stratified sample.** Haverly Leisure Centre has 2,000 members: 900 students, 700 working adults and 400 retired members. It wants a sample of 80.

```
Students:       900 ÷ 2,000 × 80 = 36
Working adults: 700 ÷ 2,000 × 80 = 28
Retired:        400 ÷ 2,000 × 80 = 16
Check: 36 + 28 + 16 = 80
```

Within each group, members are then chosen at random. The sample mirrors the membership, so a view held mainly by retired members is neither over- nor under-represented.

**Comparing the three samples at Haverly.**

- **Random**: every member has an equal chance. Fair and simple with a full membership list, but by chance it might pick, say, only 8 retired members.
- **Stratified**: guarantees 36, 28 and 16, but needs accurate data on each member's group.
- **Quota**: staff at reception stop members until they have 36, 28 and 16. Quick and cheap, but staff choose whom to approach, and people who visit at quiet times may be missed.

## Interpreting correlation

A correlation value runs from −1 to +1.

- **Sign**: positive means the two variables rise together; negative means one rises as the other falls.
- **Size**: close to +1 or −1 is strong; close to 0 is weak.

| Variables (one business) | Value | Reading |
|---|---|---|
| Weekly online advertising spend and weekly orders | +0.81 | Strong positive |
| Price of a sandwich and daily sandwiches sold | −0.67 | Moderately strong negative |
| Daily temperature and hot chocolate sales | −0.85 | Strong negative |
| Staff training hours and complaints | −0.15 | Weak negative |

**Using it.** A strong positive link between advertising and orders supports a bigger advertising budget, but only within the range of spending already tried, and correlation does not prove cause: both may rise in the same weeks because of a seasonal event. A weak value does not prove there is no relationship; the effect may be delayed or masked by other factors.

## Interpreting confidence levels and intervals

A sample gives an estimate for the whole market. The **confidence interval** is the range in which the true value is likely to lie. The **confidence level** says how sure we can be: at 95%, if the research were repeated many times, about 95 out of 100 intervals worked out this way would contain the true value. A higher level (99%) gives a wider interval; a bigger sample gives a narrower one.

**Worked example.** Brackwell Crisps surveyed 600 shoppers about two new flavours. A flavour is worth launching only if more than 40% of shoppers would buy it.

| Flavour | Sample result | 95% confidence interval |
|---|---|---|
| Smoked paprika | 46% would buy | 42% to 50% |
| Lime and chilli | 41% would buy | 37% to 45% |

- Smoked paprika: the whole interval is above 40%, so Brackwell can be 95% confident it clears the threshold.
- Lime and chilli: the sample result is above 40%, but the interval runs down to 37%, so the true figure could be below the threshold.
- The intervals overlap (42% to 45%), so the survey alone cannot prove smoked paprika is more popular, though it is the safer launch.

The lesson: a single headline figure from a sample is not enough; the interval shows how much the decision could be wrong.

## Annotated model answer

**Question:** Explain **one** disadvantage to Larkspur Teas of using a quota sample.

> Interviewers fill each quota by choosing whom to ask **[method]**, so they may approach the friendliest-looking customers at a tea fair **[how bias arises]**. Those customers may be keener on premium products than Larkspur's typical online buyer, so the share willing to pay more for compostable bags could be overstated and Larkspur might set the price too high **[consequence in context]**.

## Common mistakes

- Saying primary research is always more reliable: a small, badly chosen primary sample can be worse than good government data.
- Treating qualitative data as less useful; it answers different questions.
- Calling a correlation of −0.8 "weak" because it is negative.
- Claiming that correlation proves one variable causes the other.
- Treating a 95% confidence level as meaning the result is 95% accurate.
- Recommending the most expensive method without checking the budget and deadline in the case.

## Exam technique

- **Explain** a method's advantage by naming the data it produces and why that matters for this decision.
- **Analyse** a choice of method through cost, time and reliability, using figures from the case.
- **Evaluate** by weighing the methods and stating what extra research would reduce the remaining risk.
- When given a correlation value, always state both sign and strength, then the business implication.
