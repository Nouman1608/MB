## The carbonyl group and nucleophiles

Aldehydes, ketones, carboxylic acids and their derivatives all contain the **carbonyl group**, C=O, which nucleophiles attack.

- **Aldehyde:** the C=O carbon carries at least one hydrogen atom. The group is written –CHO and the name ends in **-al**.
- **Ketone:** the C=O carbon is bonded to two other carbon atoms. The name ends in **-one**.

Oxygen pulls the shared electrons towards itself, so the bond is polarised as Cδ+=Oδ−. A nucleophile (a lone-pair donor) bonds to the δ+ carbon. At the same time one of the carbon–oxygen bonds breaks and its electron pair moves on to the oxygen, which becomes O⁻. Adding H⁺ to that oxygen finishes the reaction. Two groups have joined across the old C=O, so the process is **nucleophilic addition**.

The carbonyl carbon has three regions of electron density around it, so it is **trigonal planar** (bond angles near 120°). Keep this in mind for the enantiomer section.

## Oxidation of aldehydes

An aldehyde has a hydrogen on its carbonyl carbon. Oxidation replaces that C–H with C–OH, so **aldehydes are readily oxidised to carboxylic acids**:

```
RCHO + [O] → RCOOH
(CH₃)₂CHCH₂CH₂CHO + [O] → (CH₃)₂CHCH₂CH₂COOH     4-methylpentanal → 4-methylpentanoic acid
```

A ketone has no hydrogen on its carbonyl carbon, and oxidising it would mean breaking a C–C bond, so mild oxidising agents leave it unchanged. The two tests rely on this.

## Tests: Fehling's solution and Tollens' reagent

Both are mild oxidising agents. Warm the sample with the reagent in a hot water bath rather than over a flame, since many carbonyl compounds are flammable.

| Reagent | Active species | Aldehyde | Ketone |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fehling's solution | Blue copper(II) complex in alkaline solution | Red precipitate of copper(I) oxide, Cu₂O | Remains a blue solution |
| Tollens' reagent | Diammine silver(I) ion, [Ag(NH₃)₂]⁺ | Silver mirror on the inside of the tube | Stays colourless; no silver forms |

In a positive test the aldehyde is the reducing agent. The reagents are alkaline, so the acid formed is present as its carboxylate ion. Section 3.2.5.5 of the specification also names the reduction of [Ag(NH₃)₂]⁺ to silver as the way Tollens' reagent separates aldehydes from ketones; the half-equation is [Ag(NH₃)₂]⁺ + e⁻ → Ag + 2NH₃. The [transition metals guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-transition-metals/) covers that redox side.

### Worked example 1: choosing and describing a test

Two colourless liquids are known to be 4-methylpentanal and 2-methylpentan-3-one. Both have the formula C₆H₁₂O. Describe how Tollens' reagent tells them apart.

1. Warm a little of each liquid with fresh Tollens' reagent in a hot water bath.
2. 4-methylpentanal: a silver mirror forms, because the aldehyde is oxidised to 4-methylpentanoate and Ag⁺ is reduced to Ag.
3. 2-methylpentan-3-one: no silver forms; the mixture stays colourless, because the ketone is not oxidised.

Give an observation for **both** liquids: "no silver mirror", not "no reaction".

### Worked example 2: identifying an aldehyde from the silver formed

A saturated, non-cyclic aldehyde has the general formula CₙH₂ₙO. Warming 0.750 g of one such aldehyde with excess Tollens' reagent gives 1.62 g of silver. Find its molecular formula. (Ar: Ag = 107.9; C = 12.0, H = 1.0, O = 16.0)

Each Ag⁺ gains one electron, and turning one –CHO group into –COOH releases two electrons. So 1 mol of aldehyde gives 2 mol of Ag.

```
n(Ag)       = 1.62 ÷ 107.9 = 0.01501 mol
n(aldehyde) = 0.01501 ÷ 2   = 0.007507 mol
Mr          = 0.750 ÷ 0.007507 = 99.9, so 100 to 3 s.f.
14n + 16 = 100  →  n = 6
```

The aldehyde is **C₆H₁₂O**. Any hexanal isomer fits, for example 2-ethylbutanal, (CH₃CH₂)₂CHCHO.

## Reduction with NaBH₄

Aqueous NaBH₄, sodium tetrahydridoborate(III), reduces both kinds of carbonyl compound:

- an **aldehyde** gives a **primary alcohol**
- a **ketone** gives a **secondary alcohol**

Write overall equations with **[H]** standing for the reductant. Two [H] are needed, one for the carbon and one for the oxygen:

```
(CH₃CH₂)₂CHCHO + 2[H] → (CH₃CH₂)₂CHCH₂OH             2-ethylbutanal → 2-ethylbutan-1-ol
(CH₃)₂CHCOCH(CH₃)₂ + 2[H] → (CH₃)₂CHCH(OH)CH(CH₃)₂    2,4-dimethylpentan-3-one → 2,4-dimethylpentan-3-ol
```

### The mechanism

Show the nucleophile as the **hydride ion**, drawn with its lone pair (H:⁻), as the specification asks.

### Worked example 3: reduction of 2-methylpentan-3-one

Outline the mechanism when aqueous NaBH₄ reduces 2-methylpentan-3-one, CH₃CH₂COCH(CH₃)₂.

1. Draw the ketone with the C=O bond labelled Cδ+ and Oδ−.
2. First arrow: starts at the H⁻ lone pair and ends on the carbonyl carbon.
3. Second arrow: from the double bond on to the oxygen atom. This gives an alkoxide ion, with the negative charge on O.
4. Third arrow: an O⁻ lone pair bonds to a hydrogen of a water molecule (add an arrow from that O–H bond on to the water's oxygen), or you can show H⁺ instead of water.

```
          Oδ−                        O⁻                         OH
          ‖                          |                          |
C₂H₅ – Cδ+ – CH(CH₃)₂   →   C₂H₅ – C – CH(CH₃)₂   →   C₂H₅ – C – CH(CH₃)₂
          ↑                          |            + H₂O         |
         :H⁻                         H                          H
```

The product is **2-methylpentan-3-ol**, a secondary alcohol, as expected from a ketone.

## Hydroxynitriles from KCN and dilute acid

Treat an aldehyde or ketone with **potassium cyanide and then dilute acid** and you get a **hydroxynitrile**. The new carbon carries both –OH and –C≡N, and the chain is one carbon longer than before. The specification asks for the **overall equation written with HCN**:

```
(CH₃CH₂)₂CHCHO + HCN → (CH₃CH₂)₂CHCH(OH)CN          3-ethyl-2-hydroxypentanenitrile
(CH₃)₃CCOCH₃ + HCN → (CH₃)₃CC(OH)(CH₃)CN            2-hydroxy-2,3,3-trimethylbutanenitrile
```

**Naming tip.** The –C≡N carbon is C1. In the first product the chain is C≡N, CH(OH), CH, then one ethyl group (C4–C5); the other ethyl is a substituent on C3.

### Worked example 4: the mechanism with 4-methylpentanal

4-methylpentanal, (CH₃)₂CHCH₂CH₂CHO, is treated with KCN and then dilute acid. Outline the mechanism.

1. Show Cδ+=Oδ− on the aldehyde group.
2. KCN supplies cyanide ions. The attacking lone pair is the one on the **carbon atom** of :C≡N⁻; draw the arrow from it to the δ+ carbon.
3. Second arrow: double bond on to oxygen. The intermediate is (CH₃)₂CHCH₂CH₂CH(CN)O⁻.
4. Third arrow: the negatively charged oxygen uses a lone pair to pick up H⁺, which the dilute acid supplies.

```
     Oδ−                   O⁻                    OH
     ‖                     |                     |
R – Cδ+ – H   →   R – C – H   →(+H⁺)→   R – C – H        R = (CH₃)₂CHCH₂CH₂–
     ↑                     |                     |
    :CN⁻                   CN                    CN
```

The product is **2-hydroxy-5-methylhexanenitrile**.

### Worked example 5: yield of a hydroxynitrile

3.00 g of 3,3-dimethylbutan-2-one (C₆H₁₂O) gives 2.79 g of 2-hydroxy-2,3,3-trimethylbutanenitrile (C₇H₁₃NO). Find the percentage yield. (Ar: C 12.0, O 16.0, H 1.0, N 14.0)

```
Mr(ketone)  = 72.0 + 12.0 + 16.0 = 100.0;  n = 3.00 ÷ 100.0 = 0.0300 mol
Mr(product) = 84.0 + 13.0 + 14.0 + 16.0 = 127.0
maximum mass = 0.0300 × 127.0 = 3.81 g
yield = 2.79 ÷ 3.81 × 100 = 73.2 %
```

## Why the product can be a pair of enantiomers

From [optical isomerism](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-optical-isomerism/) (section 3.3.7.1): an asymmetric carbon (one bonded to four different groups) is a chiral centre, its two non-superimposable mirror-image forms are enantiomers, and a mixture of equal amounts of them is a racemate that has no overall effect on plane-polarised light.

**The explanation, point by point:**

1. The carbonyl carbon and the three atoms joined to it lie in one plane.
2. The cyanide ion can reach that carbon from above the plane or from below it.
3. Neither face is favoured, so both approaches happen equally often.
4. When the new C(OH)(CN) carbon ends up with four different groups, attack from the two faces gives the two enantiomers.
5. The two enantiomers are made in a 50 : 50 ratio. That racemate has no net effect on plane-polarised light.

This is why the specification names **aldehydes and unsymmetrical ketones**. In each, the two groups already on the C=O carbon differ, and adding OH and CN makes four different groups. (Methanal is the exception among aldehydes: its two groups are both H.)

### Worked example 6: racemate or not?

| Starting compound | Groups on the C=O carbon | Groups on the product carbon | Mixture of enantiomers? |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2-ethylbutanal | H and CH(C₂H₅)₂ | H, OH, CN, CH(C₂H₅)₂ | Yes |
| 3,3-dimethylbutan-2-one | CH₃ and C(CH₃)₃ | CH₃, OH, CN, C(CH₃)₃ | Yes |
| 2,4-dimethylpentan-3-one | CH(CH₃)₂ and CH(CH₃)₂ | two identical CH(CH₃)₂ groups | No: symmetrical ketone, no chiral centre |

## The hazards of using KCN

- Potassium cyanide is **highly toxic** by ingestion, skin contact or inhaled dust.
- Acid converts cyanide ions into **hydrogen cyanide**, a highly toxic gas that is very volatile.

So use a fume cupboard, gloves and eye protection, and add the dilute acid slowly. KCN is used rather than HCN because, as an ionic solid, it supplies plenty of CN⁻; HCN is a weak acid that ionises only slightly, and a dangerous gas.

## Common errors

- Starting the cyanide arrow at N. Carbon's lone pair does the attacking, which is why a new C–C bond forms.
- Leaving out the arrow from C=O to oxygen, which leaves carbon with five bonds.
- Writing 1[H] in reduction equations; the C=O needs 2[H].
- Using a 1 : 1 ratio of aldehyde to silver in calculations; it is 1 : 2.
- Saying a symmetrical ketone gives enantiomers.
- Writing "KCN is toxic" without the HCN released when acid is added.
