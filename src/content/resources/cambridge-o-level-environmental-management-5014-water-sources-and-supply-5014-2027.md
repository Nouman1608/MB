Sub-topic 3.1 of the Cambridge O Level Environmental Management (5014) syllabus for 2027 to 2029 traces water from the natural cycle to a safe supply. It has ten learning statements, more than any other part of Topic 3, and it mixes recall (naming oceans, defining potable water) with two "discuss" statements on desalination and multipurpose dams. This guide works through each statement in turn and shows how to turn knowledge into marks.

## The water cycle as a system

Think of the water cycle as **stores** (where water is held) linked by **transfers** (how it moves). The syllabus names one salt water store, the oceans, and four fresh water stores: ice sheets and glaciers, ground water, the atmosphere, and lakes and rivers. Most of the Earth's water is salt water, and most fresh water is locked in ice, which is why usable fresh water is limited even on a "blue planet".

The nine transfers are easiest to learn as a journey:

1. **Evaporation**: liquid water at the sea or land surface becomes water vapour.
2. **Transpiration**: water vapour is lost from the leaves of plants.
3. **Condensation**: water vapour cools and forms droplets, making clouds.
4. **Precipitation**: rain, snow or hail falls from clouds.
5. **Interception**: leaves and branches catch precipitation before it reaches the ground.
6. **Infiltration**: water soaks into the soil surface.
7. **Through-flow**: water moves sideways through the soil towards a river.
8. **Ground water flow**: water moves slowly through permeable rock deep underground.
9. **Surface run-off**: water flows over the ground surface when it cannot soak in.

"Interpret" means you may be given a diagram, a description or data and asked what it shows. Three pairs are often confused. Interception happens above the ground on vegetation; infiltration happens at the soil surface. Through-flow is in the soil; ground water flow is in rock. Evaporation is from water surfaces; transpiration is from plants. If a question asks how a change in land use affects the cycle, follow the water: fewer trees means less interception and transpiration, so more water reaches the ground quickly, infiltration falls on compacted soil and surface run-off rises.

## Sources of fresh water

- **Atmosphere**: rain and snow can be collected directly, for example from roofs into tanks, or as snowmelt in mountain regions.
- **Surface water**: rivers, lakes and reservoirs. A reservoir is an artificial lake behind a dam. Surface water is easy to reach but is exposed to pollution and evaporation.
- **Ground water**: water stored in an **aquifer**, a layer of permeable rock, reached by digging or drilling **wells**. It is often cleaner than surface water, because it has been filtered through rock, but it refills slowly.
- **Oceans**: sea water made fresh in **desalination plants**.

## The five oceans

The syllabus asks you to identify the Pacific, Atlantic, Indian, Southern and Arctic Oceans. On a world map, the Pacific is the largest and lies between Asia and Australia to the west and the Americas to the east. The Atlantic separates the Americas from Europe and Africa. The Indian Ocean lies south of Asia, between Africa and Australia. The Southern Ocean surrounds Antarctica, and the Arctic Ocean surrounds the North Pole.

## Making water potable

Potable water is water that is safe to drink. Untreated river water may carry litter, sediment and microorganisms, so it passes through four stages:

1. **Screening**: water passes through metal screens or grids that remove large floating objects such as branches, plastic and dead animals.
2. **Sedimentation**: water is held still in tanks so that suspended particles settle to the bottom as sludge.
3. **Filtration**: water passes through beds of sand and gravel that trap the remaining fine particles.
4. **Chlorination**: a small, controlled amount of chlorine is added to kill bacteria and other microorganisms, so the water stays safe in the pipes.

In a sequence question, each stage is usually worth a mark only if you say what it removes or does, so "filtration" alone is weaker than "filtration through sand removes fine particles".

## Comparing access to potable water

Statement 3.1.6 uses the command word **compare**, so answers need both sides and a linking word such as "whereas".

- **Water-rich and water-poor regions**: places with high, reliable rainfall or large rivers and aquifers have more water than deserts or regions with long dry seasons. Water-poor does not always mean poor access: a wealthy desert country can buy desalinated water, whereas a low-income country with plenty of rain may still lack treatment works.
- **Urban and rural regions**: towns usually have piped, treated supplies because many people live close together, so each kilometre of pipe serves more customers. Rural people are scattered, so they more often depend on wells, rivers or rainwater that may be untreated, and they may walk long distances to collect it.
- **Global inequalities**: access to safe water and to sewage treatment is lowest in low-income countries. The two are linked, because untreated sewage pollutes the rivers and wells people drink from.

## Desalination

| | Distillation | Reverse osmosis |
|---|---|---|
| How it works | Sea water is heated until it boils; salt stays in the liquid; the steam (pure water) is cooled and condensed | Sea water is put under high pressure and pushed through a membrane |
| What separates the salt | Change of state: salt does not evaporate | The membrane lets water molecules through but stops most ions and other molecules |
| Main energy need | Heat | Pumping to high pressure |

**Benefits**: an almost unlimited source for coastal places; supply does not depend on rainfall, so it is reliable in drought; it reduces pressure on over-used aquifers and rivers.

**Limitations**: large energy use, so water is expensive and, if fossil fuels are burned, carbon dioxide is released; very salty waste water (**brine**) returned to the sea can harm marine organisms; plants are costly to build and maintain; they suit coastal areas, and pumping water inland adds cost.

## Multipurpose dams

A multipurpose dam is built to serve several uses at once. The syllabus lists eight: **flood control**, **hydro-electric power**, **irrigation**, **storage of water**, **transport** on the reservoir, **recreation**, **tourism** and **fish farming**.

Benefits follow from these uses: a reliable water supply in dry seasons, renewable electricity, more farmland irrigated, fewer floods downstream, and new income from fishing and tourism. Limitations include flooding of farmland, homes and habitats when the reservoir fills; people forced to resettle; sediment trapped behind the dam, so farmland downstream loses fertile silt and the reservoir slowly fills up; fish migration blocked; high construction cost; and water lost by evaporation from a large surface.

There is also a conflict between uses. For flood control, managers keep the reservoir partly empty so it can absorb a flood; for storage and hydro-electric power, they want it full. A strong "discuss" answer can point out this tension.

## Worked example: sizing a desalination plant

*Figures invented for practice.* A coastal town has 48,000 people who each use 150 litres of water per day. A reverse osmosis plant turns 45% of the sea water it takes in into fresh water.

1. Daily demand = 48,000 × 150 = 7,200,000 litres.
2. Convert: 1 m³ = 1,000 litres, so demand = 7,200 m³ per day.
3. Sea water intake = 7,200 ÷ 0.45 = 16,000 m³ per day.
4. Brine returned to the sea = 16,000 − 7,200 = 8,800 m³ per day.

The final line is the useful one for a "discuss" answer: more brine is produced than fresh water, which is why disposal is a real limitation.

## Annotated model answer

*Question: Discuss the benefits and limitations of building a multipurpose dam on a large river.* **[6]**

> The dam stores water in a reservoir, so towns and farms have a reliable supply in the dry season, and irrigation can increase crop yields. ✔ *(benefit, developed)*
> Water flowing through turbines generates hydro-electric power without burning fuel. ✔ *(benefit)*
> Holding back flood water protects settlements downstream. ✔ *(benefit)*
> However, the reservoir floods valley land, so villagers lose homes and farmland and must be resettled. ✔ *(limitation, developed)*
> Sediment is trapped behind the dam, so downstream fields no longer receive fertile silt. ✔ *(limitation)*
> Overall the dam is worthwhile only if the displaced people are compensated fairly, because they bear the costs while others gain the benefits. ✔ *(judgement)*

This scores well because it is balanced, each point is explained rather than listed, and it ends with a reasoned judgement.

## Common mistakes

- Writing "evaporation" for water lost from leaves, which is transpiration.
- Putting chlorination first, or describing sedimentation as "filtering".
- Saying reverse osmosis "boils" water; only distillation uses heat to separate the salt.
- In "compare" questions, describing two places separately with no comparative words.
- Listing dam uses when the question asks you to discuss; each use needs a consequence.

## Exam technique

**Identify** and **define** need short, exact answers. **Describe** needs the steps or features in order. **Interpret** means using the diagram or data in front of you, quoting labels or figures. **Compare** needs similarities or differences stated together. **Discuss** needs at least two benefits, at least two limitations and a short judgement linked to the context in the question.
