## Atoms and nuclide notation (7.2, 7.3)

An atom has a tiny, dense nucleus of **protons** (charge +1) and
**neutrons** (charge 0), with **electrons** (charge −1) around it. A
neutral atom has equal numbers of protons and electrons.

A nuclide is written as the chemical symbol with the mass number at the
top left and the atomic number at the bottom left, as in ¹⁴₆C:

- **A, the mass (nucleon) number:** protons + neutrons
- **Z, the atomic (proton) number:** protons only
- number of neutrons = A − Z

So ¹⁴₆C has 6 protons and 14 − 6 = 8 neutrons. **Isotopes** are atoms of
the same element (same Z) with different numbers of neutrons (different
A). Carbon-12 (¹²₆C) and carbon-14 (¹⁴₆C) are isotopes; carbon-14 is
unstable and radioactive.

## The radiations (7.4, 7.5, 7.7)

Unstable nuclei emit radiation **randomly**: you cannot predict when a
particular nucleus will decay, only the pattern for a very large number.

| | Alpha (α) | Beta-minus (β⁻) | Gamma (γ) | Neutron (n) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Nature | 2 protons + 2 neutrons (a helium nucleus) | Fast electron from the nucleus | Electromagnetic wave | Neutron |
| Symbol in equations | ⁴₂He or ⁴₂α | ⁰₋₁e or ⁰₋₁β | ⁰₀γ | ¹₀n |
| Charge | +2 | −1 | 0 | 0 |
| Ionising | Strongly | Moderately | Weakly | Not compared in the specification |
| Stopped by | Paper, skin, a few cm of air | A few mm of aluminium | Greatly reduced by thick lead or concrete | Not compared in the specification |
| Change in A | −4 | 0 | 0 | −1 |
| Change in Z | −2 | +1 | 0 | 0 |

The link between ionising and penetrating power is cause and effect:
alpha ionises so strongly that it loses its energy within a short
distance, so it penetrates least. Gamma rarely interacts, so it travels
much further.

In beta-minus decay, a **neutron in the nucleus changes into a proton**
and the electron is emitted. That is why Z rises by 1 while A stays the
same. Gamma emission only removes energy from the nucleus, so it usually
follows alpha or beta decay.

## Balancing nuclear equations (7.8)

The top numbers must add up to the same total on each side (mass
conserved), and so must the bottom numbers (charge conserved).

**Alpha:** ²²⁶₈₈Ra → ²²²₈₆Rn + ⁴₂He. Check: 226 = 222 + 4; 88 = 86 + 2.

**Beta-minus:** ⁹⁰₃₈Sr → ⁹⁰₃₉Y + ⁰₋₁e. Check: 90 = 90 + 0; 38 = 39 + (−1).

**Neutron emission (illustrative nucleus X):** ⁸⁷₃₆X → ⁸⁶₃₆X + ¹₀n. Check:
87 = 86 + 1; 36 = 36 + 0. The element is unchanged because Z is
unchanged; a lighter isotope of the same element is left.

## The penetration practical (7.6)

The specification allows radioactive sources or a simulation. With real
sources:

1. Measure the background count rate with no source present.
2. Place the source a fixed distance from a Geiger-Müller tube and record the count rate.
3. Put sheets of paper, then aluminium of increasing thickness, then lead between the source and the tube, recording the count rate each time.
4. Subtract the background from every reading.
5. The absorber that brings the corrected count rate to about zero identifies the radiation.

Safety: handle sources with tongs, keep them pointed away from people,
minimise the time they are out of their lead-lined box, and keep your
distance.

## Detection and background (7.9, 7.10)

Ionising radiation can be detected with **photographic film** (it darkens
when exposed) or a **Geiger-Müller tube** connected to a counter.

**Background radiation** is always present. The specification asks for
sources from **Earth and space**:

- **From Earth:** radioactive rocks and soil, building materials made from them, and **radon gas**, which seeps out of the ground; radioactive isotopes in food and drink, such as potassium-40.
- **From space:** **cosmic rays**, high-energy radiation from the Sun and beyond, mostly absorbed by the atmosphere, so exposure is higher at altitude.

Human activity, such as medical X-rays and nuclear industry, adds a
smaller amount.

## Activity and half-life (7.11 to 7.13)

The activity of a source **decreases with time** because fewer unstable
nuclei remain. The **half-life** is the time taken for the number of
unstable nuclei, or the activity, to fall to half. It is **different for
different isotopes**, from fractions of a second to billions of years.

**Graphical method.** Plot activity (or corrected count rate) against
time and draw a smooth curve. Pick a value on the activity axis, read the
time; halve the value, read the time again; the difference is one
half-life. Repeat from a different starting value and take the average.

**Worked example.** *Illustrative figures.* From a decay curve, the
activity falls from 900 Bq to 450 Bq between t = 0 and t = 3.2 h, and from
600 Bq to 300 Bq between t = 1.9 h and t = 5.1 h. Both intervals are
3.2 h, so the half-life is **3.2 h**. After four half-lives (12.8 h), the
activity is 900 ÷ 2⁴ = 900 ÷ 16 ≈ 56 Bq.

## Uses in industry and medicine (7.14)

The choice of source depends on the radiation's penetration and the
isotope's half-life.

- **Smoke detectors (alpha):** alpha ionises the air in a small chamber so a tiny current flows; smoke absorbs the alpha, the current drops and the alarm sounds. Alpha cannot escape the casing.
- **Thickness monitoring of paper or foil (beta):** partly absorbed, so the count changes with thickness.
- **Finding leaks in underground pipes (gamma tracer):** a short half-life gamma source is added to the fluid; a detector above ground finds where the count rises.
- **Sterilising medical equipment and food (gamma):** gamma passes through packaging and kills bacteria. The item does not become radioactive.
- **Medical tracers (gamma, short half-life):** detected from outside the body, then decays quickly.
- **Radiotherapy (gamma):** carefully aimed beams kill cancer cells.

## Contamination, irradiation and dangers (7.15, 7.16)

**Irradiation** is exposure to radiation from a source outside the body;
it stops when you move away or the source is removed. **Contamination**
means radioactive material gets onto or into something, such as dust on
skin or a gas breathed in; it keeps irradiating until removed. Alpha
sources are the most dangerous inside the body, because all their
strongly ionising radiation is absorbed by nearby cells.

Ionising radiation can **damage cells and tissue**, and can cause
**mutations** in DNA, which may lead to cancer.

**Radioactive waste** stays dangerous for a time that depends on its
half-life; some waste remains active for thousands of years. Risks are
reduced by sealing waste in containers, shielding it (concrete, lead,
water), storing it securely, often deep underground in stable rock, and
monitoring the site. Workers reduce their dose by limiting time, keeping
their distance and using shielding.

## Common mistakes

- Writing that beta is "an electron from the shells". It comes from the nucleus.
- Balancing only the top numbers of a nuclear equation.
- Calling half-life "half the time it takes to decay completely".
- Forgetting to subtract background before finding a half-life.
- Saying an irradiated object becomes radioactive.
- Stating alpha is harmless; it is very dangerous if inside the body.

## Exam technique

- **Complete** (nuclear equations): check both rows add up, and name the new element only if the atomic number is given or can be found.
- **Determine** (half-life from a graph): draw construction lines on the graph and show two readings.
- **Explain** (choice of source): give the radiation type **and** a reason based on penetration or half-life.
- **Describe** (contamination versus irradiation): define both and give the key difference, whether the material is transferred.
