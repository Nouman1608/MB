## Section A — Knowledge and terms

**1.** State how Paper 3 Section B differs in assessment from the drama section of Paper 1. **[2]**

**2.** Define **naturalism** in drama and name **one** set play for Paper 3 Section B that is usually
described as naturalistic. **[2]**

**3.** Explain what **structural dramatic irony** means in a play whose scenes are not in chronological
order. **[2]**

**4.** Explain what **doubling** is and one way it can add meaning in performance. **[2]**

## Section B — Extracts and evaluation

**5.** Read this **original** opening stage direction.

> *A kitchen in a terraced house, 1983. Everything is clean, and everything is old. A calendar on the
> wall is three months behind. The radio is on, low, tuned to a station that no one in the house now
> listens to. MOTHER sits at the table with her coat on, as though she has just come in, or is about to
> leave, and has forgotten which.*

Explain how this stage direction establishes **mood and situation** before any dialogue. **[6]**

**6.** Explain **two** challenges a director faces in staging the stage direction in Question 5, and how
the choices might shape an audience's response. **[4]**

**7.** Read this **original** extract, from a play that moves between two time periods.

> *(2019. The same factory canteen, now empty except for stacked chairs. LENA, older, enters alone.)*
>
> LENA: They took the clock. Thirty years it told us when to eat, and they took it.
>
> *(She sets one chair down, the one she always sat in, and sits.)*

The audience has already seen a crowded, noisy scene in this canteen set in 1989. Explain how the
dramatist uses **structure and staging** to create meaning here. **[6]**

**8.** Read this **original** extract.

> *(A public square. A huge portrait of the PRESIDENT. Drummers. The CROWD cheers on cue as an
> OFFICIAL raises his hand, and stops when he lowers it.)*
>
> OFFICIAL: The President thanks you for your spontaneous joy.

Explain how the dramatist uses **satire** in this extract. **[4]**

**9.** A student writes: "Some people think the ending of the play is hopeful but others don't." Explain
why this earns little AO5 credit, and rewrite it as a stronger evaluated point using a play you have
studied or a general example. **[4]**

**10.** Explain how **two different critical approaches** could interpret the same scene of economic
conflict between workers in a modern play, and how a candidate might judge between them. **[4]**

**11.** Explain **three** features of a strong (b) answer on a passage from a play with a non-linear
structure. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** Paper 3 Section B assesses **AO5**, the discussion and evaluation of other interpretations, as
well as AO1 to AO4 [1]; the objectives are weighted **20% each**, whereas Paper 1 assesses only AO1 to
AO4 at 25% each [1].

**2.** Naturalism presents **everyday life realistically**, with ordinary speech, realistic settings and
characters shaped by their environment [1]. *Long Day's Journey Into Night* [1].

**3.** The audience **already knows outcomes** that the characters in earlier scenes do not [1], so lines
and actions in those scenes **gain a second meaning** and are watched with foreknowledge [1].

**4.** Doubling is **one actor playing more than one role** [1]. It can draw links between characters,
for example making a point about **class or power** when the same actor plays an officer and a prisoner
[1].

**5.** "Everything is clean, and everything is old" presents **care alongside decline** [1]. The calendar
"three months behind" suggests **time has stopped**, perhaps since a loss [1]. The radio tuned to a
station "no one in the house now listens to" implies an **absent person** [1], created through a sound
the audience hears [1]. The MOTHER in her coat "has forgotten which" presents **disorientation or grief**
[1], establishing the situation of a household suspended after a change before any word is spoken [1].

**6.** Some details are **hard to convey visually**, such as the calendar being three months behind or
whose radio station it is [1]; a director might make the calendar prominent or use the radio's sound to
draw focus, guiding the audience to notice absence [1]. The MOTHER's uncertainty about **arriving or
leaving** must be shown through stillness or gesture [1]; whether she is played as lost or as
deliberately frozen changes whether the audience reads grief or resistance [1].

**7.** The **structural contrast** with the crowded 1989 scene makes the emptiness of 2019 felt as loss
[1] [1]. The audience's memory of the earlier scene creates **structural irony**: we know what the
canteen was [1]. The missing clock is a **symbol** of the routine and community taken away [1]. Lena
setting down "the one she always sat in" is a **single, deliberate action** [1] that turns a habit into
an act of remembrance [1].

**8.** The crowd cheers "on cue" and stops on a signal, **exposing enforced enthusiasm** [1]. The visual
dominance of the **huge portrait** presents the cult of the leader [1]. The OFFICIAL's phrase
"spontaneous joy" is **ironic**, as the staging has shown the joy to be controlled [1], so the audience
laughs at the regime while recognising its menace [1].

**9.** It **reports** two views without explaining them, testing them, or judging between them [1] [1].
A stronger version: "The ending can be read as hopeful because the characters finally speak openly, but
its final image of separation suggests that understanding comes too late; the staging of that image
makes the bleaker reading more convincing." [1] [1] (One mark for testing against the text, one for a
judgement.)

**10.** A **Marxist or economic** reading would see the conflict as produced by **class and the pressures
of industry**, with characters' hostility directed sideways rather than at those with power [1]. A
reading focused on **race, identity or community** might stress how economic fear becomes **division
between groups** [1]. A candidate judges by **testing each against the text**: which scenes, lines or
staging choices support it [1], then states which reading accounts for **more of the play** [1].

**11.** **Locate** the passage both in the script and in the play's **timeline**, so the reader knows what
the audience already knows [1] [1]. Analyse how **structural dramatic irony** shapes the passage [1] [1].
**Link outward** to the scene from the other time period that the passage echoes or contrasts, and
evaluate how a staging choice could heighten that link [1] [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Discussing modern plays as if they were novels, with no reference to staging.
- Treating a non-linear play as if it were told in order.
- Mentioning alternative readings without testing or judging them.
- Skipping long stage directions in plays that depend on them.
