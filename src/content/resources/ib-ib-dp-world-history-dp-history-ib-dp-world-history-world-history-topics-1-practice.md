## Section A

**1.** State **two** agricultural changes that supported population growth in western Europe between about 1000 and 1300. **[2]**

**2.** Explain why the spread of Champa rice was important for Song China. **[2]**

**3.** Identify **three** obligations a peasant might owe a lord on a medieval manor. **[3]**

**4.** Outline the purpose of the Statute of Labourers (1351) and explain why it largely failed. **[3]**

**5.** Identify **two** goods exchanged in the trans-Saharan trade and **one** state that grew rich from it. **[3]**

**6.** Explain **two** ways in which learning in the Abbasid caliphate influenced Europe. **[4]**

**7.** Define the *waqf* and give **one** example of an institution it might fund. **[2]**

---

## Section B

**8.** Explain **two** ways in which Song China's economy became more commercialised. **[4]**

**9.** Explain why Mongol rule in the thirteenth century encouraged long-distance trade, and **one** way it also spread danger along trade routes. **[4]**

**10.** Compare and contrast the roles of religious institutions in the economy of **two** societies, each chosen from a different region. **[6]**

**11.** "The Black Death changed the lives of European peasants for the better." Explain **two** arguments for and **two** against this view. **[8]**

**12.** Discuss the impact of trade on **two** societies, each chosen from a different region, between 750 and 1400. **[15]**

**13.** To what extent was the Black Death the main cause of social and economic change in **one** society between 1347 and 1400? **[15]**

## Answers

**1.** Any two, 1 mark each: the heavy plough for clay soils (1); the three-field rotation, leaving less land fallow (1); clearing of forests and draining of marshes for new farmland (1); greater use of horses with the horse collar (1); spread of water mills (1). **[2]**

**2.** It ripened quickly and resisted drought, so two crops a year could be grown in the south (1). This supported population growth and helped shift China's economic centre to the Yangzi region (1). **[2]**

**3.** Any three, 1 mark each: labour services on the lord's land (week work or boon work at harvest) (1); rents in money or produce (1); fees such as a payment on inheriting land (1); use of the lord's mill or oven for a fee (1); tithes to the Church (1); attendance at the manor court (1). **[3]**

**4.** It aimed to keep wages at pre-plague levels and to force able-bodied people to accept work (1). It largely failed because labour was so scarce that lords and employers competed for workers and paid more despite the law (1). Peasants also moved to find better terms, and enforcement bred resentment that fed into the revolt of 1381 (1). **[3]**

**5.** Goods, 1 mark each up to 2: gold, salt, cloth, copper, slaves, horses (1)(1). State, 1 mark: Ghana, Mali or Songhai (1). **[3]**

**6.** 2 marks for each developed way: Arabic translations of Greek works such as Aristotle reached Europe through Latin translations made in Spain and Sicily, shaping university teaching (2); works by Muslim scholars, such as Ibn Sina's *Canon of Medicine*, became standard texts in European medicine (2); mathematical ideas, including Hindu-Arabic numerals and algebra, spread to Europe through Arabic texts (2). **[4]**

**7.** A *waqf* is a permanent charitable endowment under Islamic law, usually of land or property, whose income funds a religious or public purpose (1). Example: a mosque, a *madrasa* (school), a hospital, a fountain or a caravanserai (1). **[2]**

**8.** 2 marks for each developed way: farmers grew crops for sale rather than subsistence, including tea, sugar and silk, supplying growing cities such as Kaifeng and Hangzhou (2); paper money and expanding markets made exchange easier over long distances (2); maritime trade grew through southern ports, linking China to South-East Asia and the Indian Ocean (2); the state taxed commerce and ran monopolies, encouraging monetisation (2). **[4]**

**9.** Encouragement, up to 3 marks: one political authority across much of Eurasia made routes safer (1); Mongol rulers protected and invested in merchants (1); a relay system of post stations supported travel and communication (1); they moved artisans and experts across the empire, spreading skills (1). Danger, 1 mark: the routes also carried disease, and the plague reached the Black Sea from Central Asia in the 1340s (1). **[4]**

**10.** Up to 6 marks, needing both similarity and difference, for example Latin Christendom and the Abbasid caliphate. Similarities (up to 2): in both, religious institutions held property that produced income (1); both funded education and care of the poor (1). Differences (up to 4): the Church was a great landowner collecting tithes from all, while Islamic endowments were created by individual donors for specific purposes (1); monasteries ran estates directly, whereas *waqf* income was managed by trustees (1); the Church condemned lending at interest, while Islamic merchants used partnerships that shared profit and risk (1); the papacy was a single international authority, whereas the *ulama* had no single hierarchy (1). **[6]**

**11.** 2 marks for each developed argument. For: labour shortage raised wages and lowered rents in many areas, despite the Statute of Labourers (2); lords commuted labour services and leased land, so serfdom declined and many peasants gained land and freedom (2). Against: mortality and repeated outbreaks brought grief, disruption and fear (2); lords and governments tried to enforce old obligations and imposed poll taxes, causing hardship and repression after 1381 (2). **[8]**

**12.** Marked holistically using the 15-mark markbands; the indicative content is a guide, not a checklist. *For example, Song China (Asia and Oceania) and Mali (Africa and the Middle East).* Song China: maritime and internal trade, paper money, urban growth, commercialised agriculture, state revenue; limits: agriculture remained the base and merchants' status stayed low. Mali: control of the gold trade gave rulers revenue and prestige, trade towns grew, Islam spread with merchants and learning flourished in Timbuktu; limits: dependence on trade made the state vulnerable to shifts in routes. Comparison: trade transformed urban life and state wealth in both, but in China it built on a huge agricultural economy, while in Mali it was the main source of royal power. A strong answer defines "impact" (economic, political, cultural), compares within paragraphs and reaches a reasoned judgement. **[15]**

**13.** Marked holistically using the 15-mark markbands. *For example, England.* Case for: sudden mortality created labour shortage; higher wages and lower rents; commutation and leasing; the end of many labour services; social tension culminating in 1381; challenges to clerical authority. Case against: commercialisation and commutation were under way before 1348; the poll taxes of 1377-1381 and the war with France were the immediate causes of the revolt; serfdom survived in many places to 1400; famine in 1315-1322 had already disrupted growth. A strong answer weighs the plague as an accelerator against longer-term trends and reaches a judgement, for example that the plague made existing changes faster and more widespread rather than causing them. **[15]**

## Frequently asked questions

**Can I use England and France in Question 12?**
No: both are in Europe, and the question asks for different regions.

**How much detail on dates do I need?**
Enough to anchor change over time. A few precise dates, such as 1348 and 1381, show secure knowledge.
