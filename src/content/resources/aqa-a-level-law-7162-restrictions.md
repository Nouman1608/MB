This guide covers **Restrictions** in section 3.5 Human Rights of AQA A-level Law (7162). The
specification lists "Article 8.2, Article 10.2 and Article 11.2: restrictions on the rights under Article
8.1, Article 10.1 and Article 11.1" and "General requirements relating to restrictions". It is the stage
of almost every Paper 3 human rights scenario where the marks for application and evaluation are won or
lost. For the option as a whole, see the [Human Rights study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-law-human-rights-study-guide/).

## The common structure of the second paragraphs

Articles 8, 10 and 11 share a pattern. The first paragraph states the right; the second permits
interference if three requirements are met:

1. the interference is **in accordance with the law** (Article 8.2) or **prescribed by law** (Articles
   10.2 and 11.2);
2. it pursues one of the **legitimate aims** listed in that paragraph; and
3. it is **necessary in a democratic society** for that aim.

The burden is on the state to justify the interference. If any requirement fails, the right is breached.

### Differences between the three paragraphs

| | Article 8.2 | Article 10.2 | Article 11.2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Opening words | "There shall be no interference by a public authority ... except" | Exercise "carries with it duties and responsibilities" and may be subject to "formalities, conditions, restrictions or penalties" | "No restrictions shall be placed ... other than" |
| Aims shared by all three | National security, public safety, prevention of disorder or crime, protection of health or morals, protection of the rights and freedoms of others (10.2 phrases the last as protection of the reputation or rights of others) | | |
| Extra aims | Economic well-being of the country | Territorial integrity; preventing disclosure of information received in confidence; maintaining the authority and impartiality of the judiciary | None, but the last sentence allows lawful restrictions on members of the armed forces, the police or the administration of the state |

The list of aims is **exhaustive**. Article 18 adds that restrictions may be applied only for the
purposes for which they are prescribed.

## Requirement 1: prescribed by law

The measure must have a basis in domestic law, and the law must have a certain **quality**. In **Sunday
Times v United Kingdom (1979)** the Court said the law must be **adequately accessible** and formulated
with **sufficient precision** to enable a citizen to regulate their conduct, if need be with advice.
Common law can satisfy this; the contempt rules in Sunday Times did.

The requirement fails where there is no legal basis (**Malone v United Kingdom, 1984**, telephone
tapping) or where the law is so broad it gives no protection against arbitrary use. In **Gillan and
Quinton v United Kingdom (2010)** the stop and search power in sections 44 to 47 of the Terrorism Act
2000, which needed no reasonable suspicion, was not "in accordance with the law" under Article 8 because
it was insufficiently circumscribed and lacked adequate safeguards against abuse. The power was later
repealed and replaced by a narrower one under the Protection of Freedoms Act 2012.

## Requirement 2: legitimate aim

The state must identify an aim from the relevant list. This stage is rarely decisive because the aims
are broad, but an answer must still name the aim that fits the facts. Article 18 is the safeguard
against a stated aim hiding an improper purpose.

## Requirement 3: necessary in a democratic society

In **Handyside v United Kingdom (1976)** the Court explained that "necessary" is not synonymous with
"indispensable", nor as flexible as "reasonable" or "useful". It implies a **pressing social need**. The
reasons given by the state must be **relevant and sufficient**, and the interference must be
**proportionate** to the legitimate aim pursued.

### Proportionality in English courts

Domestic courts apply a structured test. In **Bank Mellat v HM Treasury (No 2) (2013)** Lord Reed set out
four questions:

1. Is the objective sufficiently important to justify limiting a fundamental right?
2. Is the measure rationally connected to the objective?
3. Could a less intrusive measure have been used without unacceptably compromising the objective?
4. Balancing the severity of the effects on the rights of the persons affected against the importance
   of the objective, does the measure strike a fair balance?

Question 3 is often where a ban fails: conditions, a warning or a narrower order may achieve the same aim.

## The margin of appreciation

The European Court allows states a **margin of appreciation**, recognising that national authorities are
often better placed to judge local needs. Its width varies.

- **Wide** where there is no European consensus or the issue is moral, social or economic: Handyside
  (morals), **A, B and C v Ireland (2010)** (abortion law), Hatton v United Kingdom (2003) (night flights).
- **Narrow** where a particularly important aspect of identity or existence is at stake, such as intimate
  private life in Dudgeon v United Kingdom (1981), or where political speech is restricted.

The margin is an international doctrine. Domestic courts do not apply it as such, but they give weight to
Parliament's judgment in areas of social policy, sometimes called a **discretionary area of judgment**.

## General limits on restrictions

- **Article 17** stops anyone using Convention rights to destroy the rights of others. In **Norwood v
  United Kingdom (2004)** a man convicted for displaying a poster attacking Muslims could not rely on
  Article 10; his application was inadmissible.
- **Article 15** allows derogation from these Articles in war or a public emergency threatening the life
  of the nation, to the extent strictly required.
- **Article 14** requires restrictions to be applied without discrimination.

## When two qualified rights conflict

Where Article 8 meets Article 10, as in privacy claims against the press, neither has automatic priority.
In **Re S (A Child) (2004)** Lord Steyn set out four propositions: neither Article has precedence; where
their values conflict, there must be an **intense focus on the comparative importance** of the specific
rights claimed; the justifications for interfering with each must be considered; and the
proportionality test must be applied to each. Campbell v MGN (2004) applied this balancing.

## Worked example

*Facts: A city council bans a planned march against a new incinerator, using a power under a local Act,
because the route passes a hospital. The organisers offered to change route.*

```
Right:        Article 11.1 (and 10.1): peaceful assembly and expression.
By law?       A statutory power that is published and clear: likely satisfied
              (Sunday Times test).
Aim:          Public safety; protection of the rights of others (patients).
Necessary?    Pressing social need: some disruption near a hospital, yes.
              Less intrusive means: re-routing was offered, so a total ban
              fails Bank Mellat question 3.
Conclusion:   Likely breach of Article 11, because the ban was
              disproportionate when a condition would have met the aim.
```

## Common mistakes

- Applying the 8.2 test to Article 5, which is a limited right with its own exhaustive list.
- Stating the three requirements without applying each to the facts.
- Using "necessary" to mean merely "useful" or "sensible"; Handyside says otherwise.
- Ignoring less restrictive alternatives, which is the usual reason a measure fails.
- Treating the margin of appreciation as something an English court applies.
- Giving Article 10 automatic priority over Article 8 or the reverse (Re S).

## Exam technique

- **Advise** and **Consider**: take the three requirements in order, then the proportionality questions,
  and reach a conclusion. Say which requirement decides the case.
- **Discuss**: evaluate the flexibility of the tests, for example whether a wide margin of appreciation
  gives too little protection, or whether the Bank Mellat questions give judges too much power over
  policy.
