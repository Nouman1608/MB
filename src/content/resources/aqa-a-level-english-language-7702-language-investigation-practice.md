These questions practise the skills of 3.3.1 Language Investigation in AQA A-level English Language (7702). The
investigation itself is your own coursework; these tasks rehearse its design and writing decisions. All student
plans, drafts and figures below are **original** and hypothetical, written for this page.

## Section A

**1.** Turn this aim into **three** research questions, each pointing to a language level and a data set: *"To
explore how estate agents' online listings make ordinary houses sound desirable."* **[3]**

**2.** Explain the difference between a research question and a hypothesis, and give **one** advantage of research
questions for language data. **[3]**

**3.** A student plans to compare a 40-minute recording of her friends with a 5-minute recording of her
grandparents. Explain the problem and suggest a solution. **[3]**

**4.** Rewrite this questionnaire item so it is neutral: *"Do you agree that text-speak is ruining young people's
writing?"* Explain why your version is better. **[3]**

**5.** Explain the observer's paradox, and suggest **two** ways a student recording a family conversation could
reduce it. **[3]**

**6.** Distinguish reliability from validity, using an example from an investigation into hedges in group talk.
**[4]**

## Section B

**7.** Rewrite this methodology sentence as an evaluative account: *"I collected ten restaurant reviews from a
website and looked at the adjectives."* **[5]**

**8.** In a hypothetical coding table, Speaker A produces 18 tag questions in 60 turns and Speaker B produces 6 in 40
turns. Calculate each as a percentage of turns, and explain what further analysis is needed before drawing a
conclusion. **[5]**

**9.** Rewrite this conclusion so that it is appropriately cautious: *"This proves that girls use more emoji than
boys."* Explain **two** changes you made. **[4]**

**10.** A student is investigating how a primary school's newsletters address parents. Write a paragraph analysing
the contextual influences she should consider. **[5]**

**11.** A student's analysis section is organised as "Newsletter 1", "Newsletter 2", "Newsletter 3". Explain why this
is weak and propose a better structure. **[4]**

**12.** Evaluate this investigation plan and suggest improvements: *"I want to look at how social media has changed
language. I will screenshot some posts and say what is interesting about them."* **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** Any three suitable questions, 1 mark each [1] [1] [1], for example: Which evaluative adjectives and
pre-modifiers are used in the noun phrases describing rooms (lexis and grammar, listings)? How is the reader
addressed through second-person pronouns and imperatives ("Imagine your evenings here")? How does listing structure
(headline, bullet features, closing call to action) differ between low- and high-priced properties (discourse,
two price bands)?

**2.** A research question asks what the data shows ("How do commentators use ellipsis?") [1]; a hypothesis predicts
a specific result to be tested ("Commentator A will use more ellipsis") [1]. Research questions allow complex or
unexpected patterns to emerge, which suits language data that rarely gives yes or no answers [1].

**3.** The recordings differ greatly in length, so counts and patterns are not comparable [1], and differences could
reflect length or setting rather than age [1]. Solution: record similar lengths in similar settings, or analyse
matched 5-minute extracts and normalise counts [1].

**4.** Example: *"How, if at all, do you think using abbreviations in messages affects young people's formal writing?
Choose: improves it / no effect / harms it / not sure."* [1] It removes the loaded terms "text-speak" and "ruining"
[1] and no longer presupposes harm, offering a range of responses [1].

**5.** The observer's paradox (Labov) is that researchers want to study natural speech, but being observed or recorded
makes speech less natural [1]. Ways (one mark each): record for longer and analyse later sections, once speakers
relax [1]; record in a familiar routine setting, such as a family meal, where the activity absorbs attention [1].

**6.** Reliability is whether the method gives consistent results [1], for example applying a clear definition of
"hedge" so that every instance is coded the same way [1]. Validity is whether the method measures what the question
asks [1], for example checking whether a "sort of" is really hedging or just a filler, so that counts reflect the
function being studied [1].

**7.** Credit, 1 mark each: states the method precisely (ten reviews of restaurants in one city, from one site, over
one period) [1]; justifies the choice (public, comparable texts with a shared purpose) [1]; states the approach to
analysis (coding evaluative adjectives as positive or negative, then examining their position in noun phrases) [1];
identifies a limitation (one website's house style; small sample) [1]; suggests an improvement (two websites, or
matched professional and customer reviews) [1].

**8.** Speaker A: 18 ÷ 60 × 100 = 30% of turns [1]. Speaker B: 6 ÷ 40 × 100 = 15% of turns [1]. Further analysis: the
function of each tag question (seeking confirmation, softening, inviting a turn) [1]; context and roles of the
speakers (for example, whether A is leading the discussion) [1]; caution about generalising from two speakers [1].

**9.** Example: *"In this data, the girls sampled used emoji more often than the boys, which may suggest a difference
in how the groups signal tone, though the small sample limits generalisation."* [2] Changes, 1 mark each: replaced
"proves" with cautious language ("may suggest", "in this data") [1]; acknowledged the limit of the sample [1].

**10.** Credit, 1 mark each: audience, parents with a range of backgrounds and reading habits, which encourages
accessible lexis [1]; purpose, informing and maintaining goodwill, which explains positive evaluative language [1];
the institutional relationship, a school addressing parents as partners but with authority over rules, shown through
inclusive "we" alongside modals of obligation [1]; genre and mode, a printed or emailed newsletter with headings and
dates [1]; wider context, such as school policies or events that shape topics and tone [1].

**11.** Organising by text leads to description of each newsletter and leaves comparison and patterns to the reader
[1] [1]. A better structure organises by research question or feature, for example "Forms of address", "Modality and
requests", "Representation of the school", with evidence from all three newsletters in each section [1] [1].

**12.** Credit, 1 mark each: the focus is too broad (all social media, all language) [1]; "changed" implies a time
comparison but no earlier data is planned [1]; "some posts" gives no selection criteria, so data is not comparable
[1]; "say what is interesting" has no analytical framework or levels [1]; improvement: narrow to one platform,
feature and group, for example emoji use in two age groups' posts on one topic [1]; improvement: define the sample,
coding categories and research questions, and consider consent and anonymisation [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Research questions that are just the aim reworded.
- Methodology rewrites that add detail but no justification or limitation.
- Percentages presented as conclusions without analysing function or context.
- "Proves" and "all" in conclusions drawn from small samples.
