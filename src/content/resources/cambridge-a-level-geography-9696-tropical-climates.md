## The two climate types

| | Humid tropical (rainforest) | Seasonally humid tropical (savanna) |
|---|---|---|
| Typical latitude | Within about 5-10° of the equator | Roughly 5-20° north and south |
| Köppen class | Af (and Am where a monsoon gives a short dry season) | Aw |
| Temperature | Hot all year | Hot all year, hottest just before the rains |
| Rainfall | No true dry season | Wet season in the high-sun period, dry season in the low-sun period |
| Regions | Amazon basin, Congo basin, much of Indonesia and Malaysia | Llanos and much of central Brazil, East and West Africa away from the equator, northern Australia |

In the Köppen scheme, tropical (A) climates have a coolest month with a mean temperature of at least 18 °C. **Af** climates have at least 60 mm of rain in every month. **Aw** climates have a dry season in the low-sun months, with the driest month well under 60 mm. **Am** (monsoon) climates have a short dry season, but annual rainfall is so high that the soil stays moist enough for forest.

## The Hadley cell: the engine of tropical climates

The equator receives the most solar energy per unit area through the year, because the sun is high in the sky and day length is about 12 hours in every month. The intense heating drives a large circulation, first proposed by George Hadley in 1735:

1. **Rising air near the equator.** Heated air rises by convection, cools adiabatically and condenses, forming towering cumulonimbus clouds and heavy rain. Surface pressure is low.
2. **Poleward flow aloft.** At the top of the troposphere (around 15 km in the tropics) the air spreads towards the poles.
3. **Sinking air near 30°N and 30°S.** The air cools by radiating energy to space and converges aloft, then sinks. Sinking air warms adiabatically, its relative humidity falls and clouds evaporate. This forms the **subtropical anticyclones**, high-pressure cells with clear skies.
4. **Return flow at the surface.** Air flows back towards the equator as the **trade winds**. The Coriolis effect deflects them, so they blow from the north-east in the northern hemisphere and from the south-east in the southern hemisphere.

Where the two sets of trade winds meet, near the equator, is the **intertropical convergence zone**. Light and variable winds there gave it the old sailors' name "the doldrums".

## Why the ITCZ moves, and why unevenly

The ITCZ follows the zone of greatest surface heating, which moves north and south with the overhead sun between the Tropics of Cancer and Capricorn. Three details matter in exam answers:

- **Lag.** Land and sea take time to heat, so the ITCZ reaches its furthest position about one to two months after the solstice.
- **Land versus ocean.** Land heats and cools faster than water, so the ITCZ swings much further over continents than over oceans. Over South Asia in July it can lie well north of 20°N; over the eastern Pacific it moves only a few degrees.
- **Hemisphere bias.** The northern hemisphere has more land, so on average across the year the ITCZ lies slightly north of the equator.

The movement explains the **seasonality of rainfall**. Places near the equator are under or close to the ITCZ for most of the year, so rain falls in every month, often with two peaks near the equinoxes when the ITCZ passes overhead. Further from the equator, a place is reached by the ITCZ only once in the high-sun season and spends the rest of the year under the subtropical high, giving a single wet season. The further from the equator, the shorter and less reliable the wet season.

## Subtropical anticyclones

When the ITCZ moves away from a savanna location, the subtropical high extends towards it. Sinking air produces a **temperature inversion** a few kilometres up, which acts as a lid on convection, so even when the air near the ground is warm and humid, tall clouds cannot form. Dry trade winds, such as the harmattan blowing from the Sahara across West Africa between about November and March, bring dust and low humidity.

## Monsoons

A monsoon is a seasonal reversal of wind direction. The **South Asian monsoon** is the strongest example:

- **Summer (wet).** The land heats far faster than the Indian Ocean, and the Tibetan Plateau heats the air above it. A large low-pressure area forms over northern India and Pakistan, and the ITCZ is pulled far north. South-east trade winds cross the equator, are deflected by the Coriolis effect and arrive as south-westerlies, carrying moist air onshore. Uplift over the Western Ghats and the Himalayas produces very heavy orographic rain. In the upper atmosphere, the subtropical westerly jet stream shifts north of the Himalayas, allowing the monsoon circulation to establish. The normal onset date in Kerala, in the far south-west of India, is around 1 June.
- **Winter (dry).** The land cools below the ocean temperature, high pressure builds over central Asia, and winds blow offshore from the north-east. Most of India is dry.

Monsoons also occur in West Africa, where moist south-westerly winds from the Gulf of Guinea bring the summer rains, and in northern Australia. A monsoon is therefore an intensified, land-driven form of the ITCZ shift.

## Annual and diurnal variations

**Humid tropical.** Mean monthly temperatures stay within a degree or two of each other because day length and sun angle barely change. The **diurnal range**, often 8-12 °C, exceeds the annual range. A typical day: clear morning; strong heating; cumulus clouds building by late morning; convective thunderstorms in the afternoon; clearing in the evening. High humidity and cloud limit night-time cooling.

**Seasonally humid tropical.** The annual temperature range is larger. Temperatures peak just before the rains, when the sun is high and skies are still clear; once the wet season starts, cloud and evaporation lower daytime maxima. The diurnal range is largest in the dry season, when clear skies allow strong daytime heating and rapid night-time cooling, and smallest in the cloudy wet season. Rainfall is also more **variable** from year to year, so droughts and late onsets are common.

## Worked example: reading climate data

The table shows two fictional stations.

| Month | J | F | M | A | M | J | J | A | S | O | N | D |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| P (1°N) temperature (°C) | 26.6 | 26.9 | 27.3 | 27.5 | 27.6 | 27.2 | 26.9 | 27.0 | 27.1 | 27.3 | 27.0 | 26.7 |
| P rainfall (mm) | 250 | 230 | 290 | 300 | 240 | 170 | 160 | 180 | 190 | 230 | 260 | 270 |
| Q (12°N) temperature (°C) | 24.0 | 26.0 | 29.0 | 32.0 | 31.0 | 28.0 | 26.0 | 25.5 | 26.5 | 28.0 | 27.0 | 24.5 |
| Q rainfall (mm) | 0 | 0 | 5 | 20 | 70 | 130 | 210 | 260 | 150 | 40 | 2 | 0 |

- **Annual range:** P = 27.6 − 26.6 = **1.0 °C**; Q = 32.0 − 24.0 = **8.0 °C**.
- **Annual rainfall:** P = **2 770 mm**; Q = **887 mm**.
- **Pattern at P:** every month has more than 150 mm, so there is no dry season (Af). Rain peaks in March-April and November-December, when the ITCZ passes overhead near the equinoxes.
- **Pattern at Q:** a single wet season from May to September, with a peak of 260 mm in August, and four months with 5 mm or less (Aw). The hottest month is April, before the rains; temperatures fall as cloud arrives in June.
- **Explanation:** P is under the ITCZ for most of the year; Q is reached by the ITCZ only in the northern summer and lies under the subtropical high for the rest of the year.

## Annotated model answer

*Question: Explain why the hottest month in many savanna areas occurs before the wettest months. [5]*

> In the months before the wet season the sun is close to overhead, so insolation is intense (**cause of heating**). Skies are still clear under the subtropical high, so little radiation is reflected or absorbed by cloud (**clear skies**). The ground is dry, so almost all energy heats the surface rather than evaporating water (**energy budget**). When the ITCZ arrives, thick cloud cuts incoming radiation (**change with the rains**), and evaporation from wet ground uses energy as latent heat, so temperatures fall even though the sun is still high (**cooling effect**).

## Common mistakes

- Saying the ITCZ "follows the sun" exactly, without lag or the land-ocean difference.
- Describing a savanna as having a "winter". It is hot all year; the seasons are wet and dry.
- Forgetting the Coriolis effect when explaining trade wind and monsoon directions.
- Explaining the monsoon only by land-sea heating, without the ITCZ shift.
- Reading a climate graph without quoting the range, totals and wettest and driest months.

## Exam technique

- In the 10-mark structured question, data-response parts usually need figures from the resource; quote values with units.
- **Explain** a pattern by linking it to the circulation: ITCZ, subtropical high or monsoon.
- **Compare** two stations with linked statements ("whereas…").
- In 20-mark essays, evaluation might ask how far the ITCZ explains tropical climates; weigh it against monsoons, relief and ocean currents.
