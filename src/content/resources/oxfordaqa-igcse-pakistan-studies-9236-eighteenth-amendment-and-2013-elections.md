Key development 3.1.2.2 of the 9236 specification, Political developments, 1999-2022, includes
"changes under Asif Ali Zardari, including the 18th and 19th Amendments; the 2013 elections, and the
policies of the Pakistan Muslim League (PML) led government". This guide covers that content point
in depth: the decade from 2008 to 2018. It is part of the compulsory topic examined in Paper 1
Section B. The full topic, from 1947 to 2022, is summarised in the
[Pakistan into the 21st Century study guide](/resources/igcse-oxfordaqa-pakistan-studies-pakistan-into-the-21st-century/).

## How Zardari came to power

- **December 2007:** Benazir Bhutto, leader of the Pakistan Peoples Party (PPP), was assassinated in
  Rawalpindi during the election campaign.
- **February 2008:** the PPP won the most seats in the general election, and **Yousaf Raza Gilani**
  became Prime Minister.
- **August 2008:** Pervez Musharraf resigned as President under threat of impeachment.
- **September 2008:** **Asif Ali Zardari**, Benazir Bhutto's widower and co-chairman of the PPP, was
  elected President.

Zardari was President, not Prime Minister. At first he kept the strong powers Musharraf had given the
presidency; the 18th Amendment then gave many of them up.

## Changes under Zardari

**Restoring the judiciary (2009).** After a long campaign by lawyers and opposition parties, Chief
Justice Iftikhar Chaudhry, dismissed by Musharraf, was restored in March 2009.

**Help for the provinces and regions.**

- The **7th National Finance Commission (NFC) Award (2009)** gave the provinces a larger share of
  federal revenue and, for the first time, divided it using several criteria, not population alone,
  which helped smaller provinces such as Balochistan.
- The **Aghaz-e-Huqooq-e-Balochistan** package (2009) offered jobs, funds and political measures to
  address grievances in Balochistan.
- The **Gilgit-Baltistan Empowerment and Self-Governance Order (2009)** gave the region an elected
  assembly and a chief minister.

**Social protection.** The **Benazir Income Support Programme (BISP)**, set up in 2008, made regular
cash payments to poor families, paid to women.

### The 18th Amendment (April 2010)

Passed with the support of all the main parties, the 18th Amendment was the largest change to the
1973 Constitution since its adoption. Its main effects:

- **Parliamentary democracy restored.** The President lost the power to dissolve the National
  Assembly at his own discretion, a power used to dismiss several governments in the 1990s. The Prime
  Minister became the chief executive.
- **Provincial autonomy.** The Concurrent Legislative List, under which both federal and provincial
  governments could make laws, was abolished, so subjects such as education and health passed to the
  provinces. The Council of Common Interests was strengthened, and provinces were given a share in
  ownership of oil and gas in their territory.
- **Khyber Pakhtunkhwa.** The North-West Frontier Province was renamed, recognising Pashtun identity.
- **Right to education.** A new Article 25A made free and compulsory education a right for children
  aged 5 to 16.
- **Protection against military rule.** Suspending or holding the constitution in abeyance was added
  to the definition of high treason, and courts could not validate such acts.
- **Appointments.** Judges were to be appointed through a Judicial Commission and a parliamentary
  committee; the Chief Election Commissioner through consultation between the Prime Minister and the
  Leader of the Opposition.
- **Third term.** The bar on anyone serving a third term as Prime Minister was removed.

### The 19th Amendment (2010-11)

The Supreme Court raised concerns about the new method of appointing judges. Parliament responded
with the 19th Amendment, which gave senior judges a greater role in the Judicial Commission and
changed the procedure of the parliamentary committee. It showed parliament and the judiciary
adjusting the balance between them.

**Further reform.** The 20th Amendment (2012) strengthened the Election Commission and set rules for
caretaker governments during elections.

**Challenges.** The government faced militant violence, including the 2009 military operation in
Swat; the 2010 floods; serious electricity shortages (load-shedding); economic difficulty that led
to an IMF loan in 2008; and tension with the United States after the 2011 raid in Abbottabad. In 2012
Gilani was disqualified after being convicted of contempt of court for refusing to ask Swiss
authorities to reopen cases against Zardari; Raja Pervaiz Ashraf replaced him.

## The 2013 elections

The general election of **11 May 2013** was a landmark:

- for the first time, an elected civilian government had completed its full five-year term and handed
  power to another elected government;
- the **PML-N** won the most seats and **Nawaz Sharif** became Prime Minister for a third time, which
  the 18th Amendment's removal of the third-term bar had made possible;
- **Pakistan Tehreek-e-Insaf (PTI)** emerged as a major party and formed the government of Khyber
  Pakhtunkhwa;
- Zardari completed his term as President in September 2013 and handed over to Mamnoon Hussain.

## Policies of the PML-N government (2013-18)

| Area | Policies |
|---|---|
| Energy | New power projects, including coal-fired and hydel plants, and imports of liquefied natural gas (LNG), to reduce load-shedding |
| Economy and CPEC | An IMF programme from 2013; the China-Pakistan Economic Corridor launched in 2015, with roads, power stations and the port of Gwadar |
| Security | Operation Zarb-e-Azb in North Waziristan (2014); the National Action Plan after the Army Public School attack in Peshawar (December 2014); military courts under the 21st Amendment; the Karachi operation |
| Infrastructure | Motorways and urban transport such as metro bus systems |
| Tribal areas | The 25th Amendment (2018) merged the Federally Administered Tribal Areas into Khyber Pakhtunkhwa |

**Outcome.** After the Panama Papers revelations in 2016, the Supreme Court disqualified Nawaz Sharif
in July 2017. **Shahid Khaqan Abbasi** of the PML-N served as Prime Minister until the government
completed its term in 2018.

## Judging the decade

Extended questions often ask how successful these governments were. Weigh the evidence on both sides.

| Achievements | Limits and problems |
|---|---|
| Parliamentary democracy restored by the 18th Amendment | Provinces were slow to build capacity for newly devolved subjects such as health and education |
| Provinces strengthened through the NFC Award and devolution | Balochistan's grievances were not resolved by the 2009 package |
| First full-term handover between elected governments (2013) | Two Prime Ministers, Gilani and Sharif, were removed by the Supreme Court |
| Reduced militant violence after Zarb-e-Azb and the National Action Plan | Military courts raised concerns about rights and due process |
| More electricity and CPEC investment after 2013 | Rising debt and dependence on IMF programmes |

A good judgement explains which side weighs more and why, rather than listing both.

## Worked example

**Question:** Explain why the 18th Amendment strengthened democracy in Pakistan. **[4]**

```
Point 1   removed the President's discretionary power to dissolve
          the National Assembly
Explain   elected governments could no longer be dismissed in this
          way, as several had been in the 1990s, so power rested with
          parliament and the Prime Minister

Point 2   made suspending the constitution an act of high treason
Explain   this aimed to deter military takeovers and protect
          constitutional government
```

## Common mistakes

- **Calling Zardari Prime Minister.** He was President from 2008 to 2013.
- **Mixing the 18th and 19th Amendments.** The 19th mainly adjusted judicial appointments.
- **Saying 2013 was Pakistan's first election.** It was the first transfer of power from one elected
  government that completed its term to another.
- **Listing PML-N policies without judging them.** Extended questions expect evaluation.

## Exam technique

Section B mixes multiple choice, short answers and extended responses. For 9-mark questions, weigh
achievements against problems and reach a judgement; dates and named policies turn assertion into
evidence.
