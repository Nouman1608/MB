This guide covers subtopic **3.7, Dot-and-cross diagrams**, from Topic 3, Chemical bonding, of Cambridge International AS & A Level Chemistry 9701, 2025–2027 series. It is AS Level content. The [Chemical Bonding: Shapes and Intermolecular Forces](/resources/as-chemical-bonding-shapes-and-intermolecular-forces/) guide shows a handful of examples; this page gives a checking method that works for any species and an electron inventory for every compound the syllabus names. Condensed notes are in the [dot-and-cross diagrams revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-chemistry-9701-as-dot-and-cross-diagrams-revision-notes/), and you can practise with the [dot-and-cross diagrams practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-chemistry-9701-as-dot-and-cross-diagrams-practice/).

## Syllabus coverage

Candidates should be able to use dot-and-cross diagrams to illustrate **ionic, covalent and coordinate bonding**, including the representation of **any compounds stated in 3.4 and 3.5**. Dot-and-cross diagrams may include species with atoms which have an **expanded octet** or species with an **odd number of electrons**.

That brings in: NaCl, MgO and CaF₂ (from 3.2, as ionic examples); H₂, O₂, N₂, Cl₂, HCl, CO₂, NH₃, CH₄, C₂H₆, C₂H₄, SO₂, PCl₅, SF₆, NH₄⁺ and Al₂Cl₆ (from 3.4); and BF₃, H₂O and PF₅ (from 3.5).

## Conventions examiners expect

- Use **dots** for the electrons of one atom and **crosses** for the electrons of another. With three or more elements, a third symbol (such as an open circle) can be used, or electrons can be distinguished by atom.
- Show **outer-shell electrons only**, unless the question asks for all shells.
- For **covalent** bonds, place each shared pair in the overlap between the two atoms, one dot and one cross.
- For **coordinate** bonds, the shared pair is **two dots** (or two crosses): both electrons from the donor.
- For **ions**, draw each ion separately inside **square brackets** with its **charge** at the top right. Show the transferred electron(s) with the symbol of the atom they came from.
- Circles for shells are optional; pairs must be clearly grouped.

## A method that prevents errors

1. **Count** the outer electrons of every atom. Add one per negative charge, subtract one per positive charge. This is the **total** your diagram must show.
2. **Decide the bonds.** Typical numbers of covalent bonds: H 1, halogens 1, O 2, N 3, C 4. Period 3 central atoms may form more (P up to 5, S up to 6).
3. **Place the shared pairs**, then **fill lone pairs** on outer atoms.
4. **Check each atom**: H has 2; Period 2 atoms have 8 (boron in BF₃ is the exception with 6); Period 3 central atoms may have 10 or 12.
5. **Recount** the total. If it is odd, one electron must be **unpaired**.

As an illustration, hydrogen chloride (H electron shown as •, chlorine electrons as x):

```
       x x
  H •x Cl x x
       x x
```

The shared pair (• and x) sits between H and Cl; chlorine's other six electrons form three lone pairs, giving it eight in total, and hydrogen has two.

## Worked example 1: carbon dioxide

Carbon has 4 outer electrons (crosses); each oxygen has 6 (dots). Total 16. Each C=O double bond contains **two shared pairs**, each made of one cross and one dot: two crosses from carbon and two dots from that oxygen. Each oxygen keeps four dots as two lone pairs. Carbon has eight electrons (four pairs, all bonding); each oxygen has eight.

A common error is drawing one shared pair between C and each O; that gives carbon only four electrons.

## Worked example 2: the ammonium ion

Nitrogen 5 + four hydrogens 4 − 1 for the positive charge = 8 electrons, four pairs.

- Three N–H bonds: one nitrogen dot and one hydrogen cross each.
- The fourth N–H bond: **two nitrogen dots**, because the H⁺ that joined had no electrons. This is the coordinate bond.
- Draw the whole ion inside square brackets with a **+** charge outside.

## Worked example 3: Al₂Cl₆

Total: 2 × 3 (Al) + 6 × 7 (Cl) = 48 electrons.

- Four **terminal** chlorine atoms each form one normal Al–Cl bond (one Al electron, one Cl electron) and keep three lone pairs.
- Two **bridging** chlorine atoms each form one normal bond to one aluminium and one **coordinate bond** to the other aluminium, using a pair of chlorine electrons. Each bridging chlorine keeps two lone pairs.
- Each aluminium ends with four bonding pairs: eight electrons.

Check: 8 bonds × 2 = 16, terminal lone pairs 4 × 6 = 24, bridging lone pairs 2 × 4 = 8; total 48.

## Worked example 4: an odd-electron species, nitrogen monoxide

Nitrogen 5 + oxygen 6 = **11**, an odd number, so one electron must be unpaired. One accepted diagram shows an N=O double bond (two shared pairs), two lone pairs on oxygen (giving it eight), and one lone pair plus **one single unpaired electron** on nitrogen (giving it seven). The same logic applies to the methyl radical, CH₃: carbon has three shared pairs and one unpaired electron, seven in total.

## Ionic dot-and-cross diagrams

| Compound | Cation drawn as | Anion drawn as |
|---|---|---|
| NaCl | [Na]⁺ with no outer electrons shown | [Cl]⁻ with 7 crosses and 1 dot |
| MgO | [Mg]²⁺ | [O]²⁻ with 6 crosses and 2 dots |
| CaF₂ | [Ca]²⁺ | two separate [F]⁻ ions (or 2[F]⁻), each with 7 crosses and 1 dot |

The cation has lost its outer shell, so either show nothing in its outer shell or show the full shell beneath (eight electrons for Na⁺, Mg²⁺ and Ca²⁺). Never draw a shared pair between the ions: that would show covalent bonding.

## Common mistakes

- Drawing ions with shared pairs, or forgetting the brackets and charges.
- Writing CaF₂ as one F²⁻ ion instead of two F⁻ ions.
- Showing a coordinate bond with one dot and one cross.
- Giving boron eight electrons in BF₃.
- Forcing an octet onto nitrogen in NO, which leaves the electron count wrong.
- Leaving out lone pairs on outer atoms, for example on Cl in PCl₅.
- Not adjusting the electron total for the charge on an ion.

## Exam technique

- Read whether the question says "outer electrons only".
- Make your dots and crosses large and clearly paired; examiners must be able to count them.
- When asked to show a coordinate bond, add a label or arrow pointing to it.
- Use the total-electron check before you move on; it takes seconds and catches most errors.
