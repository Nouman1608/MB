## Definitions to know word for word

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Population | A group of organisms of the same species, occupying a particular space at a particular time, that can potentially interbreed |
| Gene pool | The complete set of alleles, for every gene, in a population at a given time |
| Allele frequency | How often an allele occurs in the gene pool, as a proportion or percentage |
| Evolution | A change in the allele frequencies in a population |
| Natural selection | Differential survival and reproduction because of predation, disease and competition; advantageous alleles are passed on more |
| Genetic drift | Chance change in allele frequency, important in small populations |
| Species | Organisms able to interbreed and produce fertile offspring |

## Hardy-Weinberg at a glance

| Symbol | Meaning |
|---|---|
| p | Frequency of one allele (usually the dominant) |
| q | Frequency of the other allele (usually the recessive) |
| p + q | 1 |
| p² | Homozygous dominant genotype |
| 2pq | Heterozygous genotype |
| q² | Homozygous recessive genotype (the recessive phenotype) |
| p² + 2pq + q² | 1 |
| p² + 2pq | Dominant phenotype |

**The principle** predicts that allele frequencies stay the same from generation to generation. **Conditions:** a large population, random mating, and an absence of mutation, migration and selection.

### Method in steps

1. Find q²: it is the recessive phenotype frequency (or homozygous recessive genotype frequency).
2. q = √(q²).
3. p = 1 − q.
4. Calculate the required frequency from p², 2pq or q².
5. For a number of individuals, multiply by the population size.
6. Check that p² + 2pq + q² adds up to 1.

**Worked reminder 1 (from allele frequency).** In a population of 2000, q = 0.15. Then p = 0.85. p² = 0.7225, 2pq = 0.255, q² = 0.0225. Numbers: 1445 homozygous dominant, 510 heterozygous, 45 homozygous recessive. Total 2000.

**Worked reminder 2 (from the recessive phenotype).** A recessive condition affects 81 in 10 000. q² = 0.0081, so q = 0.09 and p = 0.91. Carriers: 2 × 0.91 × 0.09 = 0.1638, which is 1638 per 10 000.

**Worked reminder 3 (from genotype counts).** Count alleles. If 60 of 200 alleles are A, p = 0.30, q = 0.70. Do not count individuals.

### Common wrong turns

- Using the dominant phenotype frequency as p² (it is p² + 2pq).
- Forgetting to square-root.
- Reporting q when asked for carriers.
- Applying the equation to a population where a condition clearly fails, without comment.

## Sources of variation

| Cause | Detail |
|---|---|
| Mutation | Primary source of new alleles |
| Meiosis | Crossing over; independent segregation of homologous chromosomes |
| Random fertilisation | Which gamete fuses with which is a matter of chance |
| Environment | Diet, light, temperature and disease affect expression; phenotype = genotype + environment |

For 23 pairs, independent segregation gives 2²³ = 8 388 608 gametes.

## Natural selection: the explanation in steps

1. The population contains variation, arising from mutation, meiosis and random fertilisation.
2. A selection pressure (predation, disease, competition) acts.
3. Individuals with the advantageous phenotype leave more offspring.
4. Those offspring inherit the advantageous allele.
5. The allele frequency in the gene pool rises across generations.

**Worked reminder 4.** A population of 800 has q = 0.20 for a recessive allele (p = 0.80). Genotypes: AA 512, Aa 256, aa 32. If all aa die before breeding: survivors = 768; a alleles = 256 of 1536; q becomes 0.167. The allele falls, but slowly, because it is hidden in Aa.

## Selection types

| Feature | Stabilising | Directional | Disruptive |
|---|---|---|---|
| Favoured | The mean | One extreme | Both extremes |
| Acts against | Both extremes | The other extreme | The mean |
| Mean | Unchanged | Moves | May stay, but the curve has two peaks |
| Range | Narrows | Shifts | Widens |
| Environment | Stable | Changing | Varied |

## Genetic drift versus selection

| Feature | Natural selection | Genetic drift |
|---|---|---|
| Cause | Differential survival and reproduction linked to phenotype | Chance in which alleles are passed on |
| Direction | Towards advantageous alleles | None |
| Population size | Any | Matters in small populations |
| Result | Adaptation | Loss or fixation of alleles with no advantage |

**Why small only.** A population of 10 diploids has 20 copies of a gene. Losing 2 copies by chance changes the frequency by 2 ÷ 20 = 0.10. In a population of 10 000 (20 000 copies) the same loss is 0.0001.

## Speciation

| Feature | Allopatric | Sympatric |
|---|---|---|
| Barrier | Geographical (river, mountain range, sea) | None geographical; same area |
| Isolating mechanism | Physical separation | Seasonal, behavioural, ecological or chromosome-number change (polyploidy) |
| Gene flow | Stopped by the barrier | Stopped by reproductive isolation |
| Result | Gene pools diverge until fertile interbreeding fails | Same |

**Answer frame:** separation, independent mutation, different selection and drift, change in allele frequencies, inability to interbreed and produce fertile offspring. Long-term repetition of this gives the diversity of species.

## Linking the ideas

Hardy-Weinberg is the baseline: if all five conditions hold, allele frequencies stay constant and there is no evolution. Each real process breaks one condition. Mutation adds new alleles, migration moves alleles in or out, selection removes some genotypes more than others, non-random mating changes genotype frequencies, and small size lets drift act. Evolution is what you see when the frequencies change, so the equation lets you detect it and then explain it.

## Reading data and graphs

- **Allele frequency over generations.** A steady rise or fall points to selection. Irregular changes, especially in a small population, point to drift. Frequencies that stay flat fit Hardy-Weinberg conditions.
- **Distribution curves.** Compare the curve after selection with the one before. Narrower with the same mean: stabilising. Mean has moved: directional. Two peaks with a dip in the middle: disruptive.
- **Observed against expected genotypes.** Calculate p and q from the data, work out the expected numbers, then compare. An excess of homozygotes (a deficit of heterozygotes) fits non-random mating. A change in q between generations fits selection, migration, mutation or drift.
- **Percentage change.** (new value minus old value) divided by the old value, times 100. A fall of an allele from 0.50 to 0.35 is a decrease of 30%.

## Quick self-test

1. Define a population.
2. What is a gene pool?
3. State the Hardy-Weinberg conditions.
4. In a population, q = 0.4. Calculate the frequency of heterozygotes.
5. A recessive trait appears in 4 of every 10 000 individuals. Find q.
6. Which type of selection narrows the range of a trait?
7. Why is drift unimportant in a large population?
8. Name two ways a sympatric population can become reproductively isolated.
9. Why must "fertile" be in the definition of a species?
10. A population of 500 has 80 aa individuals. What is the frequency of the recessive allele?
11. What does it mean to say evolution is a change in allele frequencies?
12. Name the two genetic sources of variation produced during sexual reproduction, other than mutation.

### Answers

1. A group of organisms of the same species occupying a particular space at a particular time that can potentially interbreed.
2. The complete set of alleles, for every gene, in a population at a given time.
3. Large population, random mating, and no mutation, migration or selection.
4. p = 0.6, so 2pq = 2 × 0.6 × 0.4 = **0.48**.
5. q² = 0.0004, so q = **0.02**.
6. Stabilising selection.
7. Chance events involve a tiny fraction of the gene pool and average out, so frequencies barely change.
8. Any two of: breeding or flowering at different times; different courtship behaviour; chromosome-number change (polyploidy).
9. Some different species can mate, but their offspring are sterile, so the gene pools remain separate; fertility shows that gene flow is possible.
10. q² = 80 ÷ 500 = 0.16, so q = **0.4**.
11. The proportion of copies of each allele in the population's gene pool differs between generations.
12. Meiosis (crossing over and independent segregation) and random fertilisation.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing evolution of individuals rather than a change in a population's allele frequencies.
- Leaving out "fertile" or "interbreed" in species definitions.
- Using p² for the dominant phenotype, or q for the recessive phenotype frequency.
- Not stating which Hardy-Weinberg condition is broken when a result does not fit.
- Describing selection without saying that the allele is passed on.
- Treating drift as adaptive.
- Giving a geographical barrier as the cause of sympatric speciation.
- Reading a graph with two peaks as stabilising rather than disruptive selection.
- Giving a count of individuals when a frequency was asked for.
