## Section A

**1.** State the three dimensions combined in the Human Development Index. **[3]**

**2.** Outline what a Lorenz curve that bows far from the diagonal shows about a country's population. **[2]**

**3.** Suggest why a country might rank higher on HDI than on GNI per capita. **[2]**

**4.** Explain why a strong correlation between income and fertility does not prove that income causes lower fertility. **[2]**

**5.** Outline how megacity growth can reinforce a core-periphery pattern. **[2]**

## Section B

**6.** The table shows invented data for six countries.

| Country | GNI per capita (thousand dollars) | Life expectancy (years) |
|---|---|---|
| A | 0.8 | 58 |
| B | 3.0 | 66 |
| C | 5.5 | 64 |
| D | 12.0 | 74 |
| E | 20.0 | 77 |
| F | 40.0 | 82 |

**(a)** Calculate Spearman's rank correlation coefficient. Show your working. **[3]**

**(b)** Describe the relationship shown. **[1]**

**(c)** Suggest one reason why Country C does not fit the general pattern. **[1]**

**(d)** Outline one limitation of this analysis. **[1]**

**7.** In an invented country, the two most densely populated regions together hold 70% of the population on 15% of the land.

**(a)** State the cumulative point this gives on a Lorenz curve. **[1]**

**(b)** Suggest two physical factors that might explain the concentration. **[2]**

**(c)** Suggest one human factor that might explain the concentration. **[1]**

**8.** The table shows invented regional data.

| Region | Share of population (%) | Share of GDP (%) |
|---|---|---|
| Core | 25 | 45 |
| Periphery | 75 | 55 |

**(a)** Calculate GDP per person relative to the national average for each region. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate how many times greater output per person is in the core than in the periphery. **[1]**

**(c)** Explain one likely consequence of this gap for population distribution. **[2]**

**9.** Explain how natural increase and migration combine to drive the growth of a named megacity. **[4]**

## Section C

**10.** "Physical factors explain where people live, but economic development explains where they are moving to." Discuss this statement with reference to examples. **[10]**

**11.** Examine the usefulness of different measures for comparing the economic development of countries. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** A long and healthy life (life expectancy) [1]; knowledge (years of schooling) [1]; a decent standard of living (GNI per capita) [1]. **[3]**

**2.** The population is highly concentrated [1]: a large share of the people live on a small share of the land, as in a country where most people live in a few densely populated regions [1]. **[2]**

**3.** It has invested heavily in health and education [1], so its life expectancy and schooling scores are higher than its average income alone would predict [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Both may be linked to other factors that rise with income, such as female education, urbanisation and falling infant mortality [1], which are the more direct causes of lower fertility; correlation shows association, not mechanism [1]. **[2]**

**5.** Investment, jobs and infrastructure concentrate in the megacity, attracting migrants from the periphery [1]; the periphery loses young workers and the core gains further labour, markets and investment, widening the gap (cumulative causation) [1]. **[2]**

**6. (a)** Ranks for GNI (highest = 1): A 6, B 5, C 4, D 3, E 2, F 1. Ranks for life expectancy: A 6, B 4, C 5, D 3, E 2, F 1 [1]. Differences: 0, 1, -1, 0, 0, 0; sum of d squared = 2 [1]. rs = 1 - (6 x 2) / (6 x 35) = 1 - 12 / 210 = **0.94** [1].

**(b)** A very strong positive relationship: higher income is consistently associated with longer life expectancy [1].

**(c)** For example, conflict, a major epidemic or inequality may lower life expectancy despite moderate income [1].

**(d)** For example, six countries is a very small sample, or correlation does not show the cause [1]. **[6]**

**7. (a)** 15% cumulative area, 70% cumulative population [1].

**(b)** Any two, for example: fertile soils on deltas or river plains [1]; reliable water supply [1]; flat land suitable for farming and building [1]; a moderate climate [1]. Maximum [2].

**(c)** For example, a coastal capital and port with most of the country's jobs and infrastructure [1]. **[4]**

**8. (a)** Core: 45 / 25 = **1.8**; periphery: 55 / 75 = **0.73** [2].

**(b)** 1.8 / 0.733 = **about 2.5 times** [1].

**(c)** Higher incomes and more jobs in the core attract migrants from the periphery [1], so the core's share of the population rises and the periphery loses young adults, increasing concentration [1]. **[5]**

**9.** Credit any named megacity, for example Dhaka:

- Migrants arrive from rural areas, pushed by low farm incomes and river erosion or flooding, and pulled by jobs such as garment work [1] [1].
- Most migrants are young adults, so the city's population has a high share of people of child-bearing age [1].
- Births to these residents produce natural increase that adds to growth alongside new migration [1]. **[4]**

**10.** Indicative content:

- **Physical factors and distribution:** climate, water, relief and soil explain long-term patterns, such as Egypt's population along the Nile and the sparsely populated Sahara [1] [1].
- **Limits of physical explanation:** human factors such as history, trade and political centres also shape distribution, for example coastal colonial ports [1].
- **Economic development and movement:** rural-urban migration to cores and megacities follows jobs and incomes [1]; core-periphery gaps drive internal migration, as in Brazil's south-east [1].
- **Limits of economic explanation:** forced migration from conflict or hazards follows safety, not development [1]; physical hazards and climate change also drive movement [1].
- **Interaction:** economic development can overcome physical constraints through irrigation, air conditioning and transport [1]; and policy, such as moving a capital, redirects movement [1].
- **Judgement:** for example, the statement is broadly useful as a generalisation, but both distribution and movement reflect physical and human factors together [1]. **[10]**

**11.** Indicative content:

- **GNI per capita:** widely available, comparable, linked to income groups [1]; hides inequality and says nothing about health or education [1].
- **Purchasing power parity:** adjusts for living costs, improving comparison [1].
- **HDI:** adds life expectancy and schooling, giving a broader view [1]; still a national average, and the weighting of dimensions is a choice [1].
- **Demographic indicators:** fertility, infant mortality and life expectancy track development and relate to population change [1].
- **Regional measures:** GDP per person by region shows core-periphery inequality that national averages hide [1].
- **Data quality:** informal economies and weak statistics reduce reliability [1].
- **Statistical analysis:** Spearman's rank can test links between measures but not causes [1].
- **Judgement:** for example, no single measure is sufficient; a combination of income, human development and regional data is most useful [1]. **[10]**

*Examiner insight:* Paper 2 extended answers reward named examples and an explicit judgement on the statement or measures in the question.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Ranking one variable highest-first and the other lowest-first.
- Forgetting to square the rank differences.
- Treating national averages as describing every region.
- Writing "physical factors" without naming any place.
