Edexcel International A Level Economics (YEC11) is modular, with four externally assessed units each
worth 80 marks and 25% of the full IAL. Their internal composition is published in detail and is worth
knowing precisely. **Units 1 (Markets in Action) and 2 (Macroeconomic Performance and Policy)** each
run 1 hour 45 minutes and contain six multiple-choice questions (6 marks), five short-answer questions
(20 marks), a five-part data-response question from a source booklet (34 marks), and one 20-mark essay
chosen from two. **Units 3 (Business Behaviour) and 4 (Developments in the Global Economy)** each run
2 hours and contain six multiple-choice questions (6 marks), a five-part data-response question (34
marks), and two 20-mark essays chosen from three. These notes complement the site's guides to
[Markets in Action](/resources/a-level-edexcel-economics-markets-in-action/) and
[Measuring Economic Growth](/resources/a-level-edexcel-economics-economic-growth/).

## The A2 units replace short answers with a second essay

Units 1 and 2 give 20 marks to short-answer questions and 20 to one essay. Units 3 and 4 drop the
short-answer section entirely and require two essays instead -- 40 marks of extended writing.
**Exam-preparation priority**: the step up at A2 is a writing-stamina step as much as a content step.
Practise producing two 20-mark essays in one sitting, since the second essay is where fatigue and poor
time management show.

## The data-response question is 34 marks -- the largest single block on every unit

At 34 of 80 marks, the data-response question outweighs any essay and is present on all four units.
**Exam-preparation priority**: give it proportionate time -- roughly 45 minutes of a 105-minute paper,
or 50 of 120 -- and practise it as a five-part unit rather than as isolated parts. Its later parts
typically carry most of its marks and depend on the extract having been read carefully at the start.

## Six marks of multiple choice should take about six minutes

Multiple-choice questions in economics often conceal a diagram shift or a two-step calculation, but
they are still worth one mark each. **Exam-preparation priority**: cap the time spent. Spending eight
minutes on six marks costs the essay far more than it gains, and there is no partial credit to be
earned by persistence.

## Essay choice should be made on evidence, not on topic comfort

Units 1 and 2 offer one essay from two; Units 3 and 4 offer two from three. **Exam-preparation
priority**: choose on the specific examples, data or diagrams you can bring, not on which title reads
most comfortably. An economics essay is carried by the quality of its chains of reasoning and its
evidence, and a title you find slightly less appealing but can support with a named policy and a
labelled diagram is the better choice.

## Diagrams are arguments, not decoration

At this level a correctly labelled diagram can carry several marks and can replace a paragraph of
prose. **Exam-preparation priority**: practise drawing the core diagrams quickly and accurately, with
axes labelled, curves labelled, and the shift or movement clearly indicated -- then practise referring
to the diagram in the text ("as shown by the shift from S1 to S2") rather than leaving it to speak
for itself.

## Worked routine: a 20-mark essay under 25 minutes

The routine below is an original model written for this resource, not a reproduction of any official
past paper or mark scheme.

```
Minutes 0-3: plan
Decide the position. Note two arguments for, one against, one
diagram, and the factor the judgement will turn on.

Minutes 3-6: introduction and definition
Define the key term precisely, and state the position. Do not
narrate the question back.

Minutes 6-15: two developed arguments
Each: claim -> mechanism, link by link -> consequence. Insert the
diagram at the point it does work, and reference it in the prose.

Minutes 15-21: evaluation
The strongest counter-argument, then what the answer depends on --
elasticity, time horizon, magnitude, or the assumption of ceteris
paribus.

Minutes 21-25: conclusion
A judgement, with the reason, referring back to the criterion you
identified at minute 3.
```

The evaluation block is where marks concentrate at A2. An essay that argues one side thoroughly and
adds a sentence of qualification at the end scores below one that treats the counter-argument
seriously and identifies the condition on which the conclusion turns.

## Before/during exam checklist

- **Before the exams**: practise two 20-mark essays in one sitting for A2 units; practise the
  five-part data-response question as a whole; drill the core diagrams for speed and accuracy.
- **During any unit**: cap multiple choice at about a minute per mark and move on; give the
  data-response question roughly 45-50 minutes.
- **When choosing essays**: choose on the examples, data and diagrams you can supply.
- **In every essay**: define the key term, build linked causal chains, reference the diagram in the
  prose, treat the counter-argument seriously, and conclude on a stated criterion.

## Self-test

1. What changes in the question mix between the AS units and the A2 units?
2. Which single question block is largest, and how much time should it get?
3. On what basis should an essay title be chosen?
4. What distinguishes strong evaluation from weak qualification?

**Answers:** 1. The five short-answer questions (20 marks) are dropped and a second 20-mark essay is
added, so A2 units require 40 marks of extended writing rather than 20. 2. The five-part data-response
question at 34 of 80 marks -- roughly 45 minutes of a 105-minute unit, or 50 of a 120-minute unit.
3. On the specific examples, data and diagrams you can bring to it, rather than on which title feels
most comfortable. 4. Strong evaluation takes the counter-argument seriously and identifies the
specific condition -- elasticity, time horizon, magnitude -- on which the conclusion depends; weak
qualification adds a hedging sentence at the end.
