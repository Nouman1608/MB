**This guide covers the final strand of 3.2.2 Social influence in OxfordAQA International GCSE
Psychology (9218), Version 1.1: "Prosocial and antisocial behaviour in crowds: identification and
explanation of how social factors (social loafing, deindividuation and culture) and dispositional
factors (personality and morality) affect collective behaviour."** The
[Conformity and Obedience study guide](/resources/igcse-oxfordaqa-psychology-conformity-and-obedience/)
covers the first two strands and notes that collective crowd behaviour is treated separately; this is
that resource.

## Key definitions

- **Collective behaviour:** how people act when they are part of a group or crowd, rather than alone.
- **Prosocial behaviour:** behaviour that benefits others, such as helping, sharing, comforting or
  rescuing.
- **Antisocial behaviour:** behaviour that harms others or breaks social norms, such as violence,
  vandalism, looting or abuse.

Crowds can produce both. The same football crowd that chants abuse at rival fans may raise money for
a charity or help an injured spectator. The specification asks you to explain which factors push a
crowd one way or the other.

## Social factor 1: social loafing

**Social loafing** is the tendency for people to put in **less effort** when working as part of a group
than when working alone, because their individual contribution cannot be identified.

In the late nineteenth and early twentieth century, Ringelmann found that when men pulled on a rope
in groups, the force produced per person fell as the group grew. Some of this could be poor
coordination. Latané, Williams and Harkins (1979) separated the two causes. Participants clapped and
shouted as loudly as they could, alone or in groups, and the noise produced per person fell as the
group got bigger. Some participants were blindfolded and wore headphones playing noise, and believed
others were shouting with them when they were actually shouting alone. They still produced less
noise, so the drop was partly **reduced effort**, not only poor coordination.

**Why it happens:** in a crowd, each person's effort is hidden, so there is less to gain from working
hard and less risk of blame for not doing so. Each person may also expect others to do the work.

**Effect on crowd behaviour:** social loafing makes **prosocial** collective action less likely. In a
crowd at an emergency, each person may contribute less to helping, clearing a space or raising the
alarm, which links to diffusion of responsibility in bystander behaviour. It is reduced when
individual contributions are **identifiable**, when the task is **meaningful** to the person, and in
small, close-knit groups.

## Social factor 2: deindividuation

**Deindividuation** is a psychological state in which a person in a crowd loses their sense of
individual identity and self-awareness, and feels less personally responsible for what they do.
Two conditions encourage it: **anonymity** (wearing masks or uniforms, darkness, a large crowd) and
**high arousal** (noise, excitement, alcohol).

In a deindividuated state, people pay less attention to their own values and are less worried about
being judged, so normal restraints weaken.

**Evidence for antisocial effects**
- **Zimbardo (1969):** female students who wore hoods and lab coats and were not called by name
  gave electric shocks (none were real) for about twice as long as students who wore name labels and
  could be identified.
- **Diener and colleagues (1976):** on Halloween in the United States, children who were trick-or-
  treating were left alone with a bowl of sweets and told to take only one. Children who were
  anonymous and in groups were most likely to take more than one.
- **Mann (1981):** in newspaper reports of people threatening to jump from buildings, crowds were more
  likely to "bait" the person (shout encouragement to jump) when the crowd was large, when it was
  dark and when the crowd was far away from the person: all conditions of anonymity.

**Deindividuation is not always antisocial.** Johnson and Downing (1979) dressed participants in
outfits resembling nurses' uniforms or in robes resembling those of the Ku Klux Klan. Those in nurses'
uniforms gave fewer shocks than participants in their own clothes. This suggests deindividuation
makes people follow the **norms of the situation or group** more closely. If those norms are
prosocial (a charity fun run in fancy dress), deindividuation can increase prosocial behaviour.

## Social factor 3: culture

**Culture** shapes what a crowd treats as normal and how individuals relate to their group.

- **Individualist and collectivist cultures.** In cultures that emphasise the group over the
  individual (collectivist), people tend to see group goals as their own. Karau and Williams (1993)
  reviewed many studies and found that social loafing was **smaller** in collectivist cultures than in
  individualist ones.
- **Cultural norms for crowds.** Whether a crowd at a protest, a festival or a religious gathering
  behaves peacefully depends heavily on what the culture, and the crowd's own sense of itself,
  treats as acceptable.
- **Shared identity in emergencies.** Research by Drury and colleagues with survivors of emergencies
  has found that people in crowds frequently help strangers rather than panic, especially when the
  shared danger gives them a sense of common identity. This challenges the older view that crowds
  are naturally irrational or selfish.

## Dispositional factor 1: personality

Individuals bring stable traits into a crowd, and these affect whether they join in with what others
are doing.

- People with **aggressive** or **impulsive** traits, or who enjoy excitement and risk, may be more
  likely to start or join antisocial crowd behaviour.
- People high in **empathy** or **agreeableness** are more likely to help others in a crowd and less
  likely to join in harm.
- People with a strong sense of **personal identity** and self-awareness may be less affected by
  deindividuation, because they keep thinking about their own values even when anonymous.

## Dispositional factor 2: morality

**Morality** refers to a person's own principles about right and wrong. Someone with strong moral
values that they apply consistently is more likely to:

- **refuse** to take part in looting, violence or abuse even when the crowd around them is doing it;
- **act prosocially**, for example by protecting a victim or calling for help, even when others are
  not.

Morality works against deindividuation: if a person's moral standards are central to who they are,
anonymity in a crowd removes the fear of being caught but not the internal sense that an action is
wrong.

## Worked example: applying the factors

**Question.** After a city's team wins a cup final, thousands of masked fans celebrate in the streets
late at night. Some smash shop windows. Others ignore the vandalism, and a few try to stop it. Use
your knowledge of social and dispositional factors to explain this collective behaviour. [6]

> *Deindividuation.* The fans are **anonymous**, wearing masks in a large crowd at night, and highly
> **aroused** by the win, so they may lose self-awareness and feel less personally responsible,
> making vandalism more likely **[1] [1]**.
>
> *Social loafing.* Fans who ignore the vandalism may feel that their own effort to stop it would make
> little difference in such a large crowd and that others will deal with it **[1]**.
>
> *Dispositional factors.* The fans who smash windows may have more **aggressive or impulsive
> personalities** **[1]**. The few who try to stop it may have strong **moral** principles that they
> keep applying even when anonymous **[1]**, showing that dispositional factors can override the
> effects of the crowd **[1]**.

## Evaluating explanations of crowd behaviour

- **Support:** laboratory and field evidence (Zimbardo; Diener; Latané, Williams and Harkins) supports
  social loafing and deindividuation.
- **Deindividuation and norms:** Johnson and Downing show anonymity does not automatically cause
  aggression; the norms of the group matter, which helps explain prosocial crowds.
- **Laboratory artificiality:** shock-giving tasks and clapping experiments are far from real riots or
  rescues, so ecological validity is limited.
- **Ethics:** studies using apparent electric shocks raise issues of deception and distress.
- **Social versus dispositional:** not everyone in a crowd behaves in the same way, so situational
  factors alone cannot explain collective behaviour; personality and morality explain the
  differences between individuals.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating crowds as always antisocial.** The specification asks about prosocial and antisocial
  behaviour.
- **Confusing social loafing with deindividuation.** Loafing is reduced effort when contributions are
  hidden; deindividuation is loss of self-awareness and personal responsibility.
- **Listing anonymity as a separate factor.** Here anonymity is a cause of deindividuation (it also
  appears as a conformity factor elsewhere in 3.2.2).
- **Describing culture vaguely.** Name a specific difference, such as lower social loafing in
  collectivist cultures.
- **Forgetting the scenario.** Link each factor to a detail of the crowd described.

## Exam technique

- **Identify:** use the specification's terms: social loafing, deindividuation, culture,
  personality, morality.
- **Explain:** give the mechanism (hidden effort; loss of self-awareness; cultural norms; stable
  traits; personal principles).
- **Apply:** connect every point to the scenario's details.
- **Evaluate:** support with research, then add a limitation or alternative explanation.
