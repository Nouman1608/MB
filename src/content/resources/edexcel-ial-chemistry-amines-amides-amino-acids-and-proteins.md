## 19.1 Naming and drawing

A primary amine has one carbon on the nitrogen (R–NH₂), a secondary amine two, a tertiary amine three. The specification uses common names: **butylamine** is CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂NH₂ and **phenylamine** is C₆H₅NH₂. A secondary amine such as CH₃CH₂NHCH₃ is named N-methylethylamine; the "N-" shows the methyl group sits on nitrogen.

**Amides** contain –CONH–. A primary amide ends –CONH₂: CH₃CONH₂ is ethanamide. When a carbon group replaces one hydrogen on nitrogen, the name gains an N- prefix: CH₃CH₂CONHCH₃ is N-methylpropanamide.

**Amino acids** have an –NH₂ and a –COOH group. In the 2-amino acids that build proteins both groups sit on the same carbon: H₂NCH(R)COOH. With R = CH₂CH₃ the systematic name is 2-aminobutanoic acid.

### Worked example 1: three formulae for one amide

Draw N-ethylethanamide three ways.

1. Structural: CH₃CONHCH₂CH₃.
2. Displayed: every bond shown.

```
    H   O       H   H
    |   ‖       |   |
H — C — C — N — C — C — H
    |       |   |   |
    H       H   H   H
```

3. Skeletal: a zigzag line with N in the chain, =O on the second carbon and the H on nitrogen drawn in.

## 19.2 and 19.3 Reactions and basicity of amines

### Why amines are bases

The nitrogen lone pair accepts a proton; the more available it is, the stronger the base.

- **Butylamine is stronger than ammonia.** The alkyl group pushes electron density onto nitrogen (positive inductive effect).
- **Phenylamine is weaker than ammonia.** The lone pair overlaps with the ring's delocalised π system, so it is less available.

Order of base strength: **phenylamine < ammonia < butylamine**.

### Why short amines mix with water

N–H bonds and the nitrogen lone pair hydrogen bond with water, so small amines such as butylamine are miscible. Phenylamine dissolves only slightly: the large ring cannot hydrogen bond.

### The five reactions in 19.2

| Reagent | Butylamine | Phenylamine |
|---|---|---|
| Water | Equilibrium: C₄H₉NH₂ + H₂O ⇌ C₄H₉NH₃⁺ + OH⁻; alkaline solution | Same equilibrium; only weakly alkaline |
| Acid (HCl) | C₄H₉NH₂ + HCl → C₄H₉NH₃⁺Cl⁻ (butylammonium chloride) | C₆H₅NH₂ + HCl → C₆H₅NH₃⁺Cl⁻; the oily amine dissolves, and reappears with alkali |
| Halogenoalkane, e.g. bromoethane | Nucleophilic substitution: C₄H₉NH₂ + C₂H₅Br → C₄H₉NHC₂H₅ + HBr; further substitution gives tertiary amines and quaternary ammonium salts | Same type of substitution |
| Ethanoyl chloride | Fast at room temperature: CH₃COCl + 2C₄H₉NH₂ → CH₃CONHC₄H₉ + C₄H₉NH₃⁺Cl⁻ (N-butylethanamide) | CH₃COCl + 2C₆H₅NH₂ → CH₃CONHC₆H₅ + C₆H₅NH₃⁺Cl⁻ (N-phenylethanamide) |
| Copper(II) ions | Pale blue precipitate of Cu(OH)₂, then in excess a deep blue solution, [Cu(C₄H₉NH₂)₄(H₂O)₂]²⁺ | Also acts as a ligand through its lone pair |

The copper reaction mirrors aqueous ammonia (17.22): deprotonation first, then ligand exchange in excess. In the ethanoyl chloride equation the second mole of amine removes the HCl formed; without excess amine you see steamy fumes.

### Worked example 2: finding the concentration of butylamine

A 25.0 cm³ portion of aqueous butylamine needs 18.40 cm³ of 0.150 mol dm⁻³ hydrochloric acid, using methyl orange. Find the concentration in mol dm⁻³ and g dm⁻³ (Mr of butylamine = 73.0).

1. n(HCl) = 0.150 × 18.40 ÷ 1000 = 0.00276 mol.
2. Ratio 1 : 1, so n(C₄H₉NH₂) = 0.00276 mol.
3. Concentration = 0.00276 ÷ 0.0250 = **0.110 mol dm⁻³**.
4. Mass concentration = 0.1104 × 73.0 = **8.06 g dm⁻³**.

Methyl orange suits a weak base–strong acid titration (end point below pH 7).

## 19.4 and 19.5 Making amines

### Primary aliphatic amines (19.4)

**From halogenoalkanes.** Heat the halogenoalkane with **excess concentrated ammonia in ethanol**, in a sealed tube (under pressure):

CH₃(CH₂)₃Cl + 2NH₃ → CH₃(CH₂)₃NH₂ + NH₄Cl

Excess ammonia cuts down further substitution to secondary and tertiary amines.

**By reducing nitriles.** Use **lithium tetrahydridoaluminate(III) in dry ether**, or hydrogen gas over nickel:

CH₃CH₂CH₂CN + 4[H] → CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂NH₂

This gives only the primary amine, and (via a nitrile made from a halogenoalkane and cyanide) adds one carbon.

### Aromatic amines (19.5)

Reflux the nitro-compound with **concentrated hydrochloric acid and tin**. The product is the salt of the amine, so add sodium hydroxide solution to release the free amine:

C₆H₅NO₂ + 6[H] → 2H₂O + C₆H₅NH₂

### Worked example 3: yield of a substituted phenylamine

7.88 g of 1-chloro-4-nitrobenzene (C₆H₄ClNO₂, Mr 157.5) is reduced. After purification, 4.59 g of 4-chlorophenylamine (C₆H₆ClN, Mr 127.5) is collected. Find the percentage yield.

1. n(nitro-compound) = 7.88 ÷ 157.5 = 0.0500 mol.
2. One mole of nitro-compound gives one mole of amine: theoretical mass = 0.0500 × 127.5 = 6.375 g.
3. Yield = 4.59 ÷ 6.375 × 100 = **72.0%**.

## 19.6 Azo dyes

**Step 1: diazotisation.** Nitrous acid, HNO₂, is unstable, so make it in the flask from sodium nitrite and hydrochloric acid, **below 10 °C** (ice bath):

C₆H₅NH₂ + HCl + HNO₂ → C₆H₅N₂⁺Cl⁻ + 2H₂O

The product is benzenediazonium chloride. Above about 10 °C it decomposes, giving off nitrogen gas and forming phenol.

**Step 2: coupling.** Add the cold diazonium solution to **phenol dissolved in sodium hydroxide solution**. A yellow-orange solid forms: an azo dye.

C₆H₅N₂⁺ + C₆H₅OH → C₆H₅N=NC₆H₄OH + H⁺

The –N=N– azo group links two benzene rings; delocalisation across the molecule makes it coloured.

## 19.7 Amides from acyl chlorides

Acyl chlorides react quickly at room temperature with ammonia, giving benzamide: C₆H₅COCl + 2NH₃ → C₆H₅CONH₂ + NH₄Cl; and with amines to give N-substituted amides (see the 19.2 table).

## 19.8 and 19.9 Polymers containing nitrogen or hydrogen-bonding groups

### Condensation polymers (19.8 i)

In condensation polymerisation, monomers join and a small molecule (water or hydrogen chloride) is lost at each link.

**Nylon-6,10** forms from hexane-1,6-diamine, H₂N(CH₂)₆NH₂, and decanedioyl dichloride, ClOC(CH₂)₈COCl. Each amide link releases HCl. Repeat unit:

–[NH(CH₂)₆NHCO(CH₂)₈CO]–

With the dicarboxylic acid instead, water is lost. The numbers give the carbons in the diamine, then in the acid part.

**Proteins** are condensation polymers of 2-amino acids. The –COOH of one amino acid and the –NH₂ of the next form a **peptide link** (an amide link, –CONH–), releasing water.

### Addition polymers (19.8 ii)

These form by opening a C=C; nothing is lost.

- **Poly(propenamide)** from propenamide, CH₂=CHCONH₂. Repeat unit: –[CH₂–CH(CONH₂)]–.
- **Poly(ethenol)**: repeat unit –[CH₂–CH(OH)]–. The notional monomer, ethenol, is unstable, so the polymer is made from poly(ethenyl ethanoate) by replacing its ethanoate groups with –OH.

Show extension bonds through the brackets and leave no C=C.

## 19.10 Properties and hydrogen bonding

**Polyamides.** N–H on one chain hydrogen bonds to C=O on the next. With London forces along the chains, this gives strong fibres with fairly high melting temperatures.

**Poly(ethenol).** Every second carbon carries an –OH group. These form hydrogen bonds with water, so the polymer dissolves. Uses:

- **soluble laundry bags**: hospital linen goes into the washing machine still in its bag, so staff do not handle it;
- **liquid-detergent capsules (liquitabs)**: the film dissolves in the wash and releases the detergent.

Leaving some ethanoate groups in the chain changes the hydrogen bonding, which lets manufacturers adjust how readily the film dissolves.

## 19.11 Experiments with amino acids

### Acidity, basicity and zwitterions (19.11 i)

In the solid and in neutral solution, an amino acid exists mainly as a **zwitterion**: the –COOH has given its proton to the –NH₂ in the same molecule.

H₃N⁺–CH(R)–COO⁻

- Add acid: the –COO⁻ is protonated, giving H₃N⁺CH(R)COOH.
- Add alkali: the –NH₃⁺ loses a proton, giving H₂NCH(R)COO⁻.

Experiments: show that the amino acid neutralises both dilute hydrochloric acid and sodium hydroxide; note its high melting temperature (an ionic lattice of zwitterions).

### Worked example 5: identifying an amino acid

0.721 g of an amino acid H₂NCH(R)COOH reacts with exactly 28.00 cm³ of 0.250 mol dm⁻³ sodium hydroxide (1 : 1). Identify R.

1. n(NaOH) = 0.250 × 28.00 ÷ 1000 = 0.00700 mol.
2. Mr = 0.721 ÷ 0.00700 = 103.0.
3. H₂NCH(R)COOH minus R is C₂H₄NO₂ (74.0), so R = 29.0, which is C₂H₅.
4. The acid is **2-aminobutanoic acid**.

### Plane-polarised light (19.11 ii)

Pass plane-polarised monochromatic light through the solution in a polarimeter. Every amino acid found in proteins except glycine has a chiral carbon. One enantiomer on its own turns the plane of polarisation. A glycine solution, or a racemic mixture, causes no overall rotation.

### Peptide bond formation (19.11 iii)

Two amino acids can join in two orders, so glycine and 2-aminobutanoic acid give two dipeptides, each with Mr = 75.0 + 103.0 − 18.0 = 160.0. Heating with hydrochloric acid breaks peptide links again.

## 19.12 Core Practical 15: analysis of unknowns

The specification gives only the title, "Analysis of some inorganic and organic unknowns". Unit 6 may ask you to recall and interpret tests for ions, gases and organic functional groups from the whole course:

| Looking for | Test | Positive result |
|---|---|---|
| CO₃²⁻ / HCO₃⁻ | Dilute acid; limewater | Gas turns limewater milky |
| SO₄²⁻ | Acidified barium chloride | White precipitate |
| NH₄⁺ | Warm with NaOH(aq) | Ammonia given off: damp red litmus goes blue |
| Cl⁻, Br⁻, I⁻ | HNO₃ then AgNO₃(aq); then ammonia | White / cream / yellow precipitates |
| Group 1 and 2 cations | Flame test | Characteristic colour |
| Transition metal ions | NaOH(aq), NH₃(aq), then excess | Coloured precipitates; some dissolve |
| C=C | Shake with bromine water | Orange colour disappears |
| –OH (dry sample) | PCl₅ | Steamy HCl fumes |
| Aldehyde | Fehling's or Benedict's; Tollens' | Brick-red precipitate; silver mirror |

Record each observation in full (start and end colours, precipitate colour), then deduce.

## Common errors

- Ranking phenylamine above ammonia as a base.
- Forgetting the sodium hydroxide step after tin and hydrochloric acid.
- Letting the diazonium solution warm above 10 °C, or coupling with phenol in acid.
- Leaving a C=C in an addition repeat unit, or omitting the water or HCl lost in condensation.
- Saying glycine rotates plane-polarised light.
