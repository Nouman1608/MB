## What an NMR spectrum shows

Placed in a strong magnetic field, ¹H and ¹³C nuclei take in radio-frequency energy, and the frequency each one absorbs depends on its **environment**. Atoms related by symmetry are **equivalent** and share one signal. A signal's position is its **chemical shift**, δ, in ppm from a reference at δ = 0. Nearby O, N, Cl or C=O moves signals to higher δ.

## Chemical shift values used on this page

The specification says NMR chemical shift values will be provided on the Data Sheet in examinations; use those in the exam. On this page, use these typical ranges.

| Carbon environment | δ / ppm | Proton environment | δ / ppm |
|---|---|---|---|
| C–C (alkyl) | 5–55 | R–CH₃, R–CH₂–R, R₃CH | 0.7–1.8 |
| C–Cl, C–Br | 30–70 | H–C–C=O | 2.0–2.7 |
| C–N | 30–65 | H–C–N, H–C on a benzene ring | 2.3–3.0 |
| C–O | 50–90 | H–C–Cl, H–C–Br | 3.0–4.5 |
| C=C (alkene) | 115–150 | H–C–O | 3.3–4.5 |
| aromatic C | 110–160 | alkene C=C–H | 4.5–6.5 |
| C≡N | 110–125 | aromatic H | 6.5–8.2 |
| C=O in acids, esters, amides | 160–185 | aldehyde H–C=O | 9.4–10.0 |
| C=O in aldehydes, ketones | 190–220 | carboxylic acid O–H | 10.0–12.5 |
| | | alcohol O–H, amine N–H | 0.5–5.0 (variable) |

## 6.3.2(a): Carbon-13 NMR

Assessed carbon-13 spectra are **proton decoupled**: each carbon environment gives one line, with no splitting. Line heights do not show how many carbons share an environment, so count lines, not heights.

**Method:** count the lines (environments); fewer lines than carbons means symmetry; assign each line a type; write every structure that fits.

### Worked example 1: an ester or an acid?

Compound A, C₅H₁₀O₂, gives carbon-13 lines at δ 170.6, 67.6, 21.9 and 21.4.

**Count.** Four lines for five carbons: two carbons are equivalent.

**Types.** δ 170.6 is a C=O in an acid, ester or amide. δ 67.6 is a carbon bonded to oxygen. δ 21.9 and 21.4 are two different alkyl carbons.

**Rule out.** An acid such as 3-methylbutanoic acid also has four environments, but it has no line at δ 50–90. So A is an ester.

**Possible structures.** Three C₅H₁₀O₂ esters have four environments: propan-2-yl ethanoate, CH₃COOCH(CH₃)₂; methyl 2-methylpropanoate, (CH₃)₂CHCOOCH₃; and 2-methylpropyl methanoate, HCOOCH₂CH(CH₃)₂.

Carbon-13 data alone cannot separate them. If A's proton spectrum has a 3H singlet at δ 2.0, that CH₃ is next to C=O, so A is **propan-2-yl ethanoate**. Methyl 2-methylpropanoate would show its 3H singlet (OCH₃) at δ 3.3–4.5, and 2-methylpropyl methanoate has no 3H singlet.

## 6.3.2(b): High resolution proton NMR

**(i) Number of signals** = number of proton environments.

**(ii) Chemical shift** gives the type of environment. Recognise aromatic protons (δ 6.5–8.2) from their shift; you are not expected to analyse their splitting.

**(iii) Relative peak area** is proportional to the number of protons. It may be shown as an **integration trace** (divide each step height by the smallest) or as ratio numbers.

**(iv) Splitting.** A signal is split by the non-equivalent protons on the adjacent carbon atoms. By the **n + 1 rule**, n adjacent non-equivalent protons split a signal into n + 1 peaks.

| Pattern | Peaks (n + 1) | Neighbouring non-equivalent H (n) |
|---|---|---|
| singlet | 1 | none |
| doublet | 2 | one |
| triplet | 3 | two |
| quartet | 4 | three |
| quintet | 5 | four |
| sextet | 6 | five |
| septet | 7 | six |

Add up the protons on all adjacent carbons. Equivalent protons do not split each other. O–H and N–H protons usually give broad singlets and do not split neighbours.

**(v) Structure.** Combine the evidence and check the total proton count against the formula.

### Worked example 2: reading the splitting

Compound B, C₆H₁₂O₂, gives this proton spectrum.

| δ / ppm | Relative area | Splitting |
|---|---|---|
| 0.93 | 3 | triplet |
| 1.38 | 2 | sextet |
| 1.60 | 2 | quintet |
| 2.04 | 3 | singlet |
| 4.06 | 2 | triplet |

**Environments.** Five signals; areas total 12, matching the formula.

**Shifts.** δ 2.04 (3H, singlet) is CH₃ next to C=O with no neighbouring H, so CH₃CO–. δ 4.06 (2H) is a CH₂ on oxygen.

**Splitting.** The δ 4.06 triplet has 2 adjacent H. The δ 1.60 quintet has 4 adjacent H, so it sits between two CH₂ groups. The δ 1.38 sextet has 5 adjacent H: a CH₂ on one side and CH₃ on the other. The δ 0.93 triplet is a CH₃ next to a CH₂.

**Structure.** The chain is O–CH₂–CH₂–CH₂–CH₃ joined to CH₃CO–. B is **butyl ethanoate, CH₃COOCH₂CH₂CH₂CH₃**.

## 6.3.2(c): Predicting a spectrum

Reverse the method: for each environment give the shift range, relative area and splitting.

### Worked example 3: methyl 3-chloropropanoate, ClCH₂CH₂COOCH₃

**Carbon-13.** Four carbons, all different, so **four lines**: C=O at δ 160–185, OCH₃ at δ 50–90, CH₂Cl at δ 30–70 and CH₂ next to C=O at δ 5–55.

**Proton.** Three environments, area ratio **2 : 2 : 3**.

| Protons | δ range | Area | Splitting |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cl–CH₂– | 3.0–4.5 | 2 | triplet (2 adjacent H) |
| –CH₂–C=O | 2.0–2.7 | 2 | triplet (2 adjacent H) |
| –O–CH₃ | 3.3–4.5 | 3 | singlet (no adjacent H) |

The OCH₃ is a singlet: its neighbouring carbon (C=O) has no hydrogen.

## 6.3.2(d): TMS, deuterated solvents and D₂O

**(i) Tetramethylsilane, TMS, Si(CH₃)₄,** is the standard. Its signal is defined as δ = 0. It is used because:

- all 12 hydrogens are equivalent (and all four carbons are), so it gives one sharp signal
- its signal lies away from almost all organic signals
- it is inert and volatile, so it does not react with the sample and is easily removed afterwards.

**(ii) Deuterated solvents** such as CDCl₃ are used because deuterium, ²H, does not absorb in the region recorded for ¹H. A solvent containing ordinary hydrogen would give a huge signal that swamps the sample.

**(iii) D₂O exchange.** O–H and N–H shifts vary, so run the spectrum, shake the sample with D₂O and run it again. These protons swap for deuterium:

R–OH + D₂O ⇌ R–OD + HDO

R–NH₂ + 2D₂O ⇌ R–ND₂ + 2HDO

The O–H or N–H signal disappears from the second spectrum, which identifies it. C–H protons do not exchange.

### Worked example 4: confirming an amine

2-methylpropan-2-amine, (CH₃)₃CNH₂, gives a 9H singlet at δ 1.1 and a broad 2H singlet near δ 1.3. After shaking with D₂O only the 9H singlet remains, so the 2H signal was NH₂. The 9H signal is a singlet because the central carbon has no hydrogen.

## 6.3.2(e): Combining analytical data

Each technique adds evidence:

- **elemental analysis** gives the empirical formula (2.1.3(c))
- **the mass spectrum** gives Mr from the molecular ion, and fragments (4.2.4(f) and (g))
- **the IR spectrum** gives functional groups, especially O–H, N–H and C=O
- **carbon-13 NMR** gives the carbon environments
- **proton NMR** gives the proton environments, their numbers and their neighbours.

You will not be asked to interpret mass spectra of organic halogen compounds.

### Worked example 5: from percentages to a structure

Compound C contains 50.8% C, 8.5% H and 40.7% O by mass. Relative atomic masses: O 16.0, C 12.0, H 1.0. The data are:

- mass spectrum: m/z 118 (very small), 73, 45 (tallest) and 29
- IR: broad absorption at 3450 cm⁻¹, strong absorption at 1735 cm⁻¹
- carbon-13: five lines, at δ 175.7, 66.8, 61.7, 20.4 and 14.1
- proton: δ 1.29 (3H, triplet), 1.41 (3H, doublet), 2.9 (1H, broad singlet, gone after D₂O), 4.23 (2H, quartet), 4.28 (1H, quartet).

**Empirical formula.** C: 50.8 ÷ 12.0 = 4.233. H: 8.5 ÷ 1.0 = 8.5. O: 40.7 ÷ 16.0 = 2.544. Dividing by 2.544 gives 1.664 : 3.342 : 1. Multiplying by 3 gives 4.99 : 10.03 : 3, so **C₅H₁₀O₃**, with Mr = 118.0.

**Molecular formula.** The molecular ion at m/z 118 matches: C₅H₁₀O₃.

**IR.** 3450 cm⁻¹ (broad) is an alcohol O–H, not the very broad acid band. 1735 cm⁻¹ is C=O.

**Carbon-13.** δ 175.7 is an ester C=O. δ 66.8 and 61.7 are two different C–O carbons. δ 20.4 and 14.1 are two alkyl carbons.

**Proton.** The δ 1.29 triplet (3H) and δ 4.23 quartet (2H) are an ethyl group on oxygen, –OCH₂CH₃. The δ 1.41 doublet (3H) and δ 4.28 quartet (1H) are CH₃–CH< with the CH on oxygen. The δ 2.9 signal vanishes with D₂O, so it is the O–H.

**Fragments.** m/z 45 fits CH₃CH(OH)⁺, m/z 73 fits COOCH₂CH₃⁺ and m/z 29 fits CH₂CH₃⁺.

**Structure.** C is **ethyl 2-hydroxypropanoate, CH₃CH(OH)COOCH₂CH₃**.

## Common errors

- Counting carbon-13 line heights as numbers of carbons.
- Using n instead of n + 1, so a CH₂ next to a CH₃ is called a triplet.
- Forgetting to add the protons on **both** adjacent carbons.
- Splitting an OCH₃ signal because "the oxygen is next to something": count H on adjacent **carbons** only.
- Trying to work out splitting for aromatic protons, which you are not expected to analyse.
- Saying D₂O makes the O–H peak "move" rather than disappear.
