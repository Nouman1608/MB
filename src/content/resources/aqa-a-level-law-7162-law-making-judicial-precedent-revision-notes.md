Condensed notes on **Law making: judicial precedent** in AQA A-level Law (7162) section 3.1: the doctrine, the hierarchy including the Supreme Court, stare decisis, ratio and obiter, law reporting, following, overruling and distinguishing, and evaluation. For full explanation and a worked application, see the [Judicial Precedent study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-law-7162-law-making-judicial-precedent/).

## Core vocabulary

- **Stare decisis:** stand by what has been decided; like cases alike.
- **Ratio decidendi:** the legal principle on which the decision rests, applied to the material facts. **Binds.**
- **Obiter dicta:** other remarks, including hypothetical points. **Persuasive.**
- **Binding / persuasive / original** precedent.

**Obiter becoming law:** *R v Howe* (1987) said obiter that duress should not be a defence to attempted murder; *R v Gotts* (1992) adopted it.

**Persuasive sources:** lower courts; obiter; dissenting judgments; the Privy Council; other common law countries.

## Hierarchy summary

| Court | Binds | Own decisions |
|---|---|---|
| Supreme Court (from 2009) | All below | May depart: Practice Statement 1966 |
| Court of Appeal (Civil) | All below | Bound, except *Young* exceptions |
| Court of Appeal (Criminal) | Criminal courts below | Bound, but more flexible where liberty at stake (*R v Taylor*, 1950; *R v Gould*, 1968) |
| Divisional Courts | High Court and below | Generally bound |
| High Court | County Court, magistrates | Persuasive between High Court judges |
| Crown, County, magistrates' | None | Not bound |

## Practice Statement 1966

- Lord Gardiner: depart "when it appears right to do so".
- Still applies to the Supreme Court: *Austin v Southwark LBC* (2010).
- Uses: *Conway v Rimmer* (1968); *Herrington* (1972) overruling *Addie v Dumbreck* (1929); *Shivpuri* (1986) overruling *Anderton v Ryan* (1985); *R v G* (2003) overruling *Caldwell* (1981).
- Restraint: *Knuller v DPP* (1972) declined to overrule *Shaw v DPP*.

## *Young v Bristol Aeroplane* (1944): three exceptions

1. Two conflicting Court of Appeal decisions: choose one.
2. Earlier decision inconsistent with a later House of Lords or Supreme Court decision.
3. Earlier decision made **per incuriam** (overlooking a statute or binding case).

*Davis v Johnson* (1979): Lord Denning's attempt to free the Court of Appeal rejected by the House of Lords.

## Privy Council

- Normally persuasive: *The Wagon Mound (No 1)* (1961) followed in England.
- *R v James* (2006): Court of Appeal followed *AG for Jersey v Holley* (2005) over the House of Lords.
- *Willers v Joyce* (2016): Privy Council may direct that its decision represents English law.

## Law reporting

- Year Books (from the late thirteenth century) → private reports → Incorporated Council of Law Reporting (1865), *The Law Reports*.
- Neutral citations since 2001; judgments published online.
- **Reasons:** accurate record of ratio; advice to clients; identifying binding authority; consistency and certainty; accessibility.

## Operation of precedent

| Operation | Who | What | Case |
|---|---|---|---|
| Following | Any court | Applies precedent on similar material facts | Routine |
| Distinguishing | Any court | Material facts differ, so ratio not applied | *R v Wilson* (1996) distinguishing *R v Brown* (1993) |
| Overruling | Higher court, or Supreme Court of its own decision | Earlier rule in a different case declared wrong | *Herrington* overruling *Addie* |

**Not on this list:** reversing (same case changed on appeal).

## Evaluation

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Certainty | Rigidity |
| Consistency, fairness | Complexity, volume |
| Flexibility (distinguishing, Practice Statement) | Slow: depends on cases reaching senior courts |
| Practical, real-case rules | Retrospective effect of overruling |
| Saves time | Undemocratic judge-made law |
| Detailed rules | Illogical fine distinctions |

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Stare decisis | Standing by decided cases |
| Ratio decidendi | The binding legal principle of a decision |
| Obiter dicta | Non-binding remarks made by the way |
| Persuasive precedent | A decision a court may, but need not, follow |
| Original precedent | A decision on a point never decided before |
| Per incuriam | Made in error, overlooking relevant law |
| Distinguishing | Avoiding a precedent because material facts differ |
| Overruling | A higher court declaring an earlier rule wrong |
| Neutral citation | A publisher-independent case reference |

## Exam traps

- Obiter treated as binding.
- Practice Statement applied to the Court of Appeal.
- Overruling confused with reversing.
- *Young* exceptions named but not explained.
- Privy Council said always to bind.
- Advantages listed with no judgement.

## Self-test

1. Define ratio decidendi and obiter dicta.
2. How did obiter in *R v Howe* become law?
3. Which court replaced the House of Lords, and when?
4. What did *Austin v Southwark* confirm?
5. Which case overruled *Addie v Dumbreck*?
6. State the three *Young* exceptions.
7. Why does the Criminal Division have extra flexibility?
8. What did *R v James* (2006) show about the Privy Council?
9. Give three reasons for law reporting.
10. Which court can distinguish?
11. How does overruling differ from reversing?
12. Give two disadvantages of precedent.

**Answers:** 1. Ratio: the legal principle on which the decision is based, which binds; obiter: other remarks, which are persuasive only. 2. *R v Gotts* (1992) adopted it, holding duress unavailable for attempted murder. 3. The Supreme Court, in 2009, under the Constitutional Reform Act 2005. 4. That the Practice Statement 1966 continues to apply to the Supreme Court. 5. *British Railways Board v Herrington* (1972). 6. Conflicting Court of Appeal decisions; inconsistency with a later House of Lords or Supreme Court decision; earlier decision made per incuriam. 7. Because a person's liberty is at stake, so it may depart where the law was misapplied (*R v Taylor*; *R v Gould*). 8. That the Court of Appeal may follow a Privy Council decision over a House of Lords decision in exceptional circumstances, here where nine judges had sat to settle the law. 9. Any three: accurate record of ratio; advice to clients; finding binding authority; consistency; accessibility. 10. Any court. 11. Overruling declares an earlier rule in a different case wrong; reversing changes the outcome of the same case on appeal. 12. Any two: rigidity; complexity; slow development; retrospective effect; undemocratic; illogical distinctions.
