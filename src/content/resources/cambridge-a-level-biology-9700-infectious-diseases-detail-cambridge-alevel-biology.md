This guide covers sub-topic **10.1 Infectious diseases** of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Biology 9700 syllabus for examination in 2025, 2026 and 2027. It is AS Level content, examined on Paper 1 and Paper 2 and assumed on Paper 4.

The [infectious diseases study guide](/resources/a-level-biology-9700-infectious-diseases/) tabulates the pathogens, routes and control measures. This page shows how to think with them: tracing each disease as a chain of transmission, seeing which link each control measure breaks, using the type of pathogen to predict what will and will not work, and building a "discuss" answer that earns the higher marks. Condense it with the [infectious diseases revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-biology-9700-infectious-diseases-detail-cambridge-alevel-biology-revision-notes/) and test yourself with the [infectious diseases practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-biology-9700-infectious-diseases-detail-cambridge-alevel-biology-practice/).

## The vocabulary you will be marked on

- **Pathogen**: an organism or virus that causes disease.
- **Transmissible**: able to pass from one host to another. This is what separates an infectious disease from, say, sickle cell anaemia or lung cancer.
- **Host**: the organism the pathogen lives in.
- **Vector**: an organism that carries a pathogen from one host to another without itself being the cause of the disease. Only malaria, of the four, has a vector.
- **Carrier**: an infected person who shows no symptoms but can pass the pathogen on. Carriers matter in cholera and in latent infections.

Pathogen names are marked strictly: genus with a capital letter, species in lower case, both in italics when typed (underlined when handwritten). Learn all seven: *Vibrio cholerae*; *Plasmodium falciparum*, *P. malariae*, *P. ovale*, *P. vivax*; *Mycobacterium tuberculosis*, *M. bovis*; and human immunodeficiency virus (HIV).

## Why the type of pathogen matters

| Type | Disease | What follows for control |
|---|---|---|
| Bacterium (prokaryote, has a cell wall and its own ribosomes) | Cholera, TB | Antibiotics can be used; resistance can evolve |
| Protoctist (eukaryote, lives inside human cells for part of its time) | Malaria | Antibiotics are not the treatment; antimalarial drugs are; parasite is hidden inside liver cells and red blood cells, making vaccines difficult |
| Virus (no cell, no metabolism of its own) | HIV/AIDS | Antibiotics have no effect; antiviral drugs slow replication but do not cure |

Questions often give you an unfamiliar disease and its pathogen type and ask you to suggest control methods. Use this table to decide.

## Transmission as a chain

Every infectious disease can be traced as a chain: **source → way out of the host → route → way into the next host**. Control works by breaking one link.

### Cholera

- Source: an infected person (or carrier) with *V. cholerae* in the intestine.
- Out: in faeces.
- Route: water or food contaminated with sewage, or unwashed hands.
- In: swallowed; the bacteria survive the stomach in large enough numbers to reach the small intestine.
- Links to break: sewage treatment (route), clean or chlorinated water (route), food hygiene and hand-washing (route), treating patients so they shed fewer bacteria (source).

### Malaria

- Source: an infected person with *Plasmodium* in their blood.
- Out: taken up when a female *Anopheles* mosquito feeds.
- Route: the mosquito (vector).
- In: injected with the mosquito's saliva at its next blood meal. Also by transfusion of infected blood, shared needles, or across the placenta.
- Links to break: kill mosquitoes or prevent breeding (vector); nets and repellents (way in); drugs to clear parasites (source); screening donated blood (route).

### Tuberculosis

- Source: a person with **active** TB in the lungs. Many infected people have **latent** TB: the bacteria are present but contained, there are no symptoms, and they are not infectious. Latent infection can become active if immunity is weakened, for example by HIV.
- Out: droplets released by coughing, sneezing or talking.
- Route: air, especially in crowded, poorly ventilated rooms.
- In: inhaled. *M. bovis* instead enters in unpasteurised milk or undercooked meat from infected cattle.
- Links to break: treat active cases quickly (source), isolate infectious patients (route), ventilation and less crowding (route), BCG vaccination (way in), pasteurise milk and test cattle (*M. bovis* source).

### HIV

- Source: an infected person; the virus is in blood, semen, vaginal fluids and breast milk.
- Out and route: exchange of these body fluids: unprotected sex, sharing needles, transfusion of unscreened blood, and mother to child across the placenta, at birth or in breast milk.
- In: the virus enters the bloodstream and infects **T-helper lymphocytes**, eventually weakening the immune system so that AIDS develops.
- Links to break: condoms (route), needle exchange (route), blood screening (route), anti-retroviral therapy lowering the amount of virus in an infected person (source), treatment in pregnancy (mother-to-child route).

## Discussing prevention and control

"Discuss" means presenting points for and against, or factors that help and hinder. For each control method, ask three questions:

- **Biological**: does the pathogen or vector have features that make the method fail (resistance, mutation, hiding inside cells, carriers, latent infection, breeding sites)?
- **Social**: does it depend on people's behaviour, education, housing, conflict, migration or stigma?
- **Economic**: who pays for it, and can a poorer country sustain it?

### Annotated model answer

*Question: Discuss the factors that make tuberculosis difficult to control.* **[5]**

> Treatment requires several antibiotics taken for many months **(biological, 1)**, and patients who feel better may stop early, which allows drug-resistant strains to be selected **(social and biological, 1)**. People with latent TB have no symptoms, so they are not identified, but their infection may later become active, especially if they are also infected with HIV **(biological, 1)**. TB spreads easily in crowded, poorly ventilated housing, which is common among people on low incomes **(social, 1)**. Supervised treatment, contact tracing and testing cattle for *M. bovis* are effective but expensive, so they are harder to sustain where health budgets are small **(economic, 1)**.

Each sentence names a factor, says which type it is, and links it to why control is harder. That linking is what lifts a list into a discussion.

## Worked example: comparing rates, not counts

A health team records cholera cases in two districts after only District B has its water supply chlorinated. The figures are invented for practice.

| District | Population | Cases in the year |
|---|---|---|
| A (no chlorination) | 48 000 | 216 |
| B (chlorinated) | 125 000 | 150 |

```
A: 216 ÷ 48 000 × 1000 = 4.5 cases per 1000 people
B: 150 ÷ 125 000 × 1000 = 1.2 cases per 1000 people
A ÷ B = 4.5 ÷ 1.2 = 3.75 times higher in A
```

District B has fewer cases in total, but the fair comparison is the rate per 1000 people. A good conclusion also notes limits: the districts may differ in sewage treatment, crowding or reporting of cases, so chlorination may not be the only reason.

## Common mistakes

- Calling the mosquito the pathogen, or *Plasmodium* a bacterium.
- Saying TB is spread "by touch" or cholera "through the air".
- Saying HIV "kills the immune system" without naming T-helper cells.
- Treating everyone with TB bacteria as infectious; latent TB is not.
- Listing control methods without the factor that helps or limits each one.
- Comparing case numbers between populations of different sizes.
- Describing the stages of the malarial life cycle, which the syllabus says are not expected.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **State**: the exact pathogen name and type, no explanation.
- **Explain** (transmission): source, how the pathogen leaves, route, how it enters.
- **Discuss**: for each method, the factor and its effect; use all three types of factor when the question asks for them.
- **Suggest**: apply pathogen type and transmission route to unfamiliar diseases.
