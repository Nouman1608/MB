## 4.1.3(a) and (b): Bonding and shape

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Alkene | Unsaturated hydrocarbon with a C=C bond |
| σ-bond | Orbitals overlap directly between the two bonded atoms |
| π-bond | Sideways overlap of adjacent p-orbitals; electron density above and below the bonding carbons |
| Restricted rotation | The π-bond stops the carbons rotating about C=C |
| Shape at each C=C carbon | Trigonal planar, about 120° |

Why 120°: each C=C carbon has three regions of electron density (the double bond counts as one). They repel equally to be as far apart as possible. You do not need hybridisation.

## 4.1.3(c) and (d): Stereoisomerism

| Word | What it means |
|---|---|
| Stereoisomers | Identical structural formula; atoms arranged differently in space |
| E/Z isomerism | Stereoisomerism from restricted rotation about C=C, needing two different groups on each C=C carbon |
| Cis–trans isomerism | Special case of E/Z where two of the substituent groups on each C=C carbon are the same |

### Method in steps: does a molecule have E/Z isomers?

> 1. Find every C=C.
> 2. For each C=C carbon, write its two groups.
> 3. If either carbon has two identical groups (=CH₂, =CCl₂, =C(CH₃)₂), that C=C gives no isomers.
> 4. If both carbons have two different groups, that C=C can be E or Z.
> 5. With more than one such C=C, list the combinations and remove any that are identical because the molecule is symmetrical.

### Method in steps: assigning E or Z by CIP rules

> 1. On each carbon separately, compare the atoms bonded directly to it. Higher atomic number wins.
> 2. Tie? Compare the atoms one bond further out, highest first.
> 3. Higher-priority groups on the same side: **Z**. Opposite sides: **E**.

Atomic numbers you will use most: I 53, Br 35, Cl 17, F 9, O 8, N 7, C 6, H 1.

**Small reminder.** 1-iodopropene, ICH=CHCH₃. Left carbon: I beats H. Right carbon: CH₃ beats H. Iodine and methyl on the same side gives the Z isomer, which is also cis because each carbon carries an H.

E = trans and Z = cis are only reliably equivalent when each C=C carbon has an H. With heavier groups, rank them by CIP every time. You will not be asked to rank multiple bonds inside R groups.

## 4.1.3(e): Reactivity

The π-bond has a relatively low bond enthalpy, so it breaks more easily than the σ-bond. Estimate: π ≈ (C=C) − (C–C) from mean bond enthalpies. The π electrons are exposed above and below the plane, which attracts electrophiles.

## 4.1.3(f): Addition reactions

| Reagent | Condition | Product | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| H₂ | Ni catalyst, heat | Alkane | hept-2-ene → heptane |
| Cl₂ or Br₂ | Room temperature | Dihaloalkane | oct-2-ene + Br₂ → 2,3-dibromooctane |
| HCl, HBr or HI | Room temperature | Haloalkane | dec-5-ene + HBr → 5-bromodecane |
| H₂O(g) | Acid catalyst, e.g. H₃PO₄ | Alcohol | ethene + H₂O → ethanol |

**Bromine test (PAG7).** Add bromine water and shake. Alkene present: **orange to colourless**. No C=C: stays orange.

## 4.1.3(g) and (h): Electrophile and mechanism

- **Electrophile:** an electron pair acceptor.
- **Heterolytic fission:** a bond breaks and one atom takes both electrons, giving a cation and an anion.

### Method in steps: electrophilic addition with H–X

> 1. Draw the alkene and H–X with δ+ on H and δ− on X.
> 2. Arrow 1 starts at the C=C bond and ends at H.
> 3. Arrow 2 starts at the H–X bond and ends at X.
> 4. Draw the carbocation (+ on the carbon that did not gain H) and X⁻ with a lone pair.
> 5. Arrow 3 goes from the lone pair on X⁻ to the C⁺.
> 6. Draw the haloalkane.

**With Br₂:** the π electrons induce a dipole in Br–Br; the nearer Br is δ+. Same three arrows. The intermediate may be shown as a carbocation or a bromonium ion; the specification accepts either.

## 4.1.3(i): Markownikoff's rule

Stability: **tertiary > secondary > primary** carbocation. H–X adding to an unsymmetrical alkene gives a major product from the more stable carbocation. No explanation of the stability order is needed.

### Method in steps: predicting the major product

> 1. Number the two C=C carbons.
> 2. Put H on the first carbon and + on the second. Classify the carbocation.
> 3. Swap and classify again.
> 4. The more substituted carbocation leads to the major product; X ends up on that carbon.

**Small reminder.** 2-methylpent-2-ene, (CH₃)₂C=CHCH₂CH₃, with HI. + on C2 is tertiary; + on C3 is secondary. Major product: 2-iodo-2-methylpentane.

## 4.1.3(j): Addition polymers

### Method in steps: monomer to repeat unit

> 1. Draw the monomer with all four groups around C=C.
> 2. Open the double bond to a single bond.
> 3. Add one extension bond on each side and put the unit in square brackets.

### Method in steps: polymer to monomer

> 1. Find the shortest repeating section: two main-chain carbons.
> 2. Put back the C=C between them and keep their side groups.

## 4.1.3(k) and (l): Waste polymers

| Approach | Benefit |
|---|---|
| Combustion for energy | Energy from waste is used, not buried in landfill |
| Organic feedstock | Polymers broken into small molecules for new plastics and chemicals; less crude oil used |
| Removing toxic products | HCl from burning PVC is neutralised before gases are released |
| Biodegradable polymers | Broken down by microorganisms; less persistent waste; often made from plant sources |
| Photodegradable polymers | Bonds break when light is absorbed; less persistent waste |

## Must-know distinctions

- **σ vs π:** σ overlaps directly between nuclei and allows rotation; π overlaps sideways and prevents it.
- **E/Z vs cis–trans:** every cis–trans pair is an E/Z pair, but not every E/Z pair can be named cis or trans.
- **Cis vs Z:** cis compares identical groups; Z compares higher-priority groups. They can disagree.
- **Heterolytic vs homolytic:** heterolytic gives ions (alkene addition); homolytic gives radicals (alkane substitution in 4.1.2).
- **Biodegradable vs photodegradable:** microorganisms versus light.

## Quick self-test

Data: C = 12.0, H = 1.0, Br = 79.9; molar gas volume at RTP = 24.0 dm³ mol⁻¹.

1. Define the term electrophile.
2. State the bond angle around each carbon of a C=C bond and name the shape.
3. Which of hex-1-ene, hex-2-ene and 2-methylpent-2-ene has E/Z isomers?
4. FCH=CHI has F and I on opposite sides of the double bond. Is it E or Z?
5. State the reagent and catalyst that convert hept-2-ene into heptane.
6. What volume of H₂, at RTP, is used up when 3.36 g of but-1-ene is fully hydrogenated?
7. Calculate the mass of bromine that reacts with 4.20 g of hex-2-ene.
8. Give the major product of but-1-ene with HI and the type of carbocation it forms from.
9. Draw the repeat unit of the polymer made from but-1-ene.
10. A polymer section is –CH₂–CH(CH₂CH₂CH₃)–CH₂–CH(CH₂CH₂CH₃)–. Name the monomer.
11. Name the toxic gas formed when PVC is burned and say how it is removed.

### Answers

1. An electron pair acceptor.
2. About 120°; trigonal planar.
3. Hex-2-ene only. Hex-1-ene has =CH₂; in 2-methylpent-2-ene, C2 carries two CH₃ groups.
4. **E** (F beats H, I beats H; they are on opposite sides). It is also trans.
5. Hydrogen, with a nickel catalyst.
6. Mr C₄H₈ = 56.0, so 3.36 g is 0.0600 mol, which takes up 0.0600 mol H₂: 0.0600 × 24.0 = **1.44 dm³**.
7. Mr C₆H₁₂ = 84.0. Amount = 4.20 ÷ 84.0 = 0.0500 mol. Mr Br₂ = 159.8. Mass = 0.0500 × 159.8 = **7.99 g**.
8. **2-iodobutane**, from the secondary carbocation (the primary one gives the minor product, 1-iodobutane).
9. –[CH₂–CH(CH₂CH₃)]– with an extension bond on each side.
10. **Pent-1-ene**, CH₂=CHCH₂CH₂CH₃.
11. Hydrogen chloride; the waste gases are passed through a base, which neutralises it.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "the π-bond is between the two carbons" instead of above and below them.
- Defining an electrophile as "something that attacks a double bond" rather than an electron pair acceptor.
- Claiming E/Z isomers for an alkene with two identical groups on one C=C carbon.
- Using size or relative mass for CIP priority instead of atomic number.
- Naming cis–trans and E/Z as if they always agree.
- Starting a curly arrow at a carbon atom, or ending the first arrow on the C=C instead of the H.
- Missing the lone pair on the halide ion, or the dipole on H–X.
- Putting the halogen on the wrong carbon because the carbocations were not compared.
- Leaving C=C in a repeat unit, or giving a four-carbon repeat unit for a simple polymer.
- Saying "clear" for the bromine test result.
