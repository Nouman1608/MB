## Questions

**1.** State **two** factors that could increase the demand for a country's currency. **[2]**

**2.** Use the illustrative rate £1 = €1.20.

**(a)** Convert £450 into euros. **[1]**
**(b)** Convert €708 into pounds. **[1]**
**(c)** A traveller changes £800 into euros. The bank takes a £16 fee first. Calculate the euros received. **[2]**

**3.** The table shows the demand for and supply of pounds in a currency market (illustrative).

| Rate (euros per £) | 1.05 | 1.10 | 1.15 | 1.20 | 1.25 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pounds demanded (millions) | 120 | 100 | 80 | 60 | 40 |
| Pounds supplied (millions) | 40 | 60 | 80 | 100 | 120 |

**(a)** Identify the rate at which the market clears and the quantity of pounds traded. **[2]**
**(b)** Fewer tourists visit Country A, so demand falls by 40 million pounds at every rate. Find the new equilibrium rate and calculate the percentage change in the rate. **[3]**
**(c)** State whether the pound has appreciated or depreciated, and name the curve that shifted. **[1]**

**4.** Illustrative average rates, euros per pound: year 1, 1.18; year 2, 1.22; year 3, 1.30; year 4, 1.24; year 5, 1.12.

**(a)** Calculate an index for years 3 and 5 with year 1 = 100. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage change from year 3 to year 5. **[1]**
**(c)** Describe the trend and suggest one cause using demand and supply. **[2]**

**5.** The pound depreciates. Explain the likely effect on **(a)** a furniture maker that exports most of its output **[2]**, and **(b)** a firm that imports machine parts. **[2]**

**6.** A machine maker in Country A sells 600 machines a year at £2,500 each to a country that uses dollars. The rate rises from £1 = 1.40 dollars to £1 = 1.75 dollars (illustrative).

**(a)** Calculate the price of one machine in dollars before and after. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage change in the dollar price. **[1]**
**(c)** Demand for the machines has a price elasticity of −0.8. Work out how many are now sold. **[2]**
**(d)** Calculate the change in revenue in pounds, assuming the pound price is unchanged. **[2]**

**7.** Explain **two** factors that have driven globalisation. **[4]**

**8.** Explain how a country's development can be measured, using one indicator each for health, education and technology. **[6]**

**9.** Country M has GDP of 72 billion dollars and 18 million people. Country N has GDP of 150 billion dollars and 25 million people. Life expectancy is 71 years in M and 66 in N.

**(a)** Calculate GDP per capita for each country. **[2]**
**(b)** M's GDP grows by 3.5% and its population by 1.5%. Calculate the percentage growth in GDP per capita. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why GDP per capita alone may not show that N is more developed than M. **[2]**

**10.** Evaluate whether a fall in the value of a country's currency benefits both consumers and producers. **[8]**

**11.** Evaluate the costs and benefits of globalisation to workers, producers and consumers in a developed country, including the effect on sustainability. **[8]**

**12.** Evaluate the costs and benefits of globalisation to workers, producers and consumers in a less developed country, including the effect on sustainability. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** Two of: higher overseas demand for the country's exports; higher interest rates attracting overseas savers; more foreign tourists or investment; expectations that the currency will rise. One for each factor [1] [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* name factors that raise demand for the currency itself. Saying "the currency becomes stronger" restates the result and does not give a cause.

**2. (a)** 450 × 1.20 = **€540** [1].
**(b)** 708 ÷ 1.20 = **£590** [1].
**(c)** 800 − 16 = £784 [1]; 784 × 1.20 = **€940.80** [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* show the direction of the operation. A clear working line for each part makes any slip easy to spot and fix.

**3. (a)** Demand equals supply at 80 million pounds [1], at a rate of **1.15 euros per pound** [1].
**(b)** New demand is 80, 60, 40, 20, 0 [1]. It equals supply at 60 million, at a rate of **1.10** [1]. Change = (1.10 − 1.15) ÷ 1.15 = **−4.3%** [1].
**(c)** The pound has **depreciated** because the **demand curve** for pounds shifted left [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* give both the number and the word. "The rate falls" is less complete than saying the pound depreciates because the demand curve moved.

**4. (a)** Year 3: 1.30 ÷ 1.18 × 100 = **110.2** [1]. Year 5: 1.12 ÷ 1.18 × 100 = **94.9** [1].
**(b)** (1.12 − 1.30) ÷ 1.30 = **−13.8%** [1].
**(c)** The pound rose to year 3, then fell, ending below its year 1 level [1]. A possible cause is lower overseas demand for exports, shifting demand for pounds left, or higher supply as residents bought more imports [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* back a description of a trend with numbers taken from the data, and treat any cause as a suggestion, since data alone does not prove why a rate moved.

**5. (a)** A depreciation makes the exporter's goods cheaper in foreign currency [1], so overseas demand and sales may rise, increasing revenue [1].
**(b)** The importer must pay more pounds for each unit of foreign currency [1], so its costs rise and it may raise prices or earn lower profit [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* link the exchange rate to price first, then to quantity or cost. A statement that "exports increase" with no reason is less convincing.

**6. (a)** Before: 2,500 × 1.40 = 3,500 dollars [1]. After: 2,500 × 1.75 = **4,375 dollars** [1].
**(b)** (4,375 − 3,500) ÷ 3,500 = **+25%** [1].
**(c)** Quantity change = −0.8 × 25 = −20% [1]. New quantity = 600 × 0.8 = **480** [1].
**(d)** Before: 600 × 2,500 = £1,500,000 [1]. After: 480 × 2,500 = £1,200,000, a fall of **£300,000** [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* apply elasticity to the percentage price change, not to the price itself, and state which currency each price or revenue is in.

**7.** Two factors, each named and explained. For example, lower transport costs [1] make it cheaper to ship goods to distant markets, so trade grows [1]. Improved communications technology [1] allows firms to manage production and sell across borders [1]. Trade agreements and multinational companies are other valid factors. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* a factor needs a link to more cross-border trade or investment. A bare list of factors gives little to reward beyond naming them.

**8.** Health: life expectancy or doctors per 1,000 people [1] shows health and access to care [1]. Education: average years of schooling or literacy [1] shows skills and opportunity [1]. Technology: internet users per 100 people [1] shows access to modern tools [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* say what each indicator shows about living standards, not only what it is called.

**9. (a)** M: 72 billion ÷ 18 million = **4,000 dollars** [1]. N: 150 billion ÷ 25 million = **6,000 dollars** [1].
**(b)** 1.035 ÷ 1.015 − 1 = **2.0%** (1.97%) [2].
**(c)** GDP per capita is an average and ignores how income is shared [1]. N's life expectancy is lower, which suggests poorer health, so higher income does not guarantee higher development [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* use the data given. A reference to M's higher life expectancy supports the argument better than a general comment.

**10.** Producers gain: exports are cheaper abroad, so sales rise [1]. Consumers gain indirectly if extra demand creates jobs and incomes [1]. Producers lose if they buy imported inputs, because costs rise [1]. Consumers lose: imports and holidays abroad cost more, which can raise prices [1]. It depends on elasticity of demand for exports, the share of imported inputs, the size and length of the fall, and whether rivals' currencies also fall [2]. Judgement: it favours exporters more than consumers, so "both" is true only in part, as consumers who buy imports lose [2]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* an answer that sets out gains and losses for each group, then reaches a supported conclusion, is better placed than one that only lists effects.

**11.** Benefits: producers reach larger markets and cut costs by importing inputs, and consumers gain lower prices and more choice [1]; workers in export industries gain jobs [1]. Costs: low-skilled workers may lose jobs or face lower wages as production moves abroad [1]; some local producers close [1]. Sustainability: economic, if retraining lets workers move into new industries [1]; social, because job losses can harm communities [1]; environmental, because longer transport adds emissions [1]. Judgement: the gains are widespread but the costs fall on particular groups and places, so the balance depends on the pace of change and the support given [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* name each group and its gain or loss, and bring sustainability in as a test of the answer, not as a separate list.

**12.** Benefits: investment and technology arrive with multinational firms [1]; workers may earn more than in farming, and consumers gain cheaper goods [1]. Costs: local producers face strong competition and may rely on a few exports [1]; low pay, long hours or poor safety [1]. Sustainability: economic, through dependence on one export or a foreign employer [1]; social, through urban crowding or inequality [1]; environmental, through pollution and resource use [1]. Judgement: the outcome depends on regulation, the types of jobs created and time, so the balance is favourable only where standards are enforced [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* keep evaluation specific to a less developed country, with a reason for each claim, and finish with a judgement on balance.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Multiplying instead of dividing when converting into pounds.
- Quoting a percentage change without showing the calculation.
- Describing a trend without figures from the table.
- Saying a depreciation always helps without considering imports and elasticity.
- Listing development indicators without saying what each shows.
- Using GDP per capita as if it measured fairness.
- Writing a one-sided answer to an evaluation question.
- Treating sustainability as an add-on and not applying it to the points made.
