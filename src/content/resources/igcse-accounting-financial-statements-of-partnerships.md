## Types of partnership business

A partnership is a business owned by two or more people. What it does decides which statements it prepares.

- **Trading:** buys and sells goods, for example a garden supplies shop. Revenue less cost of sales gives gross profit.
- **Service:** sells skills or time, for example a firm of surveyors. There is no cost of sales, so expenses come straight off revenue.
- **Manufacturing:** makes what it sells. A manufacturing account comes first, and its cost of production replaces purchases in the statement of profit or loss.
- **Combination:** for example a bakery that also resells coffee machines. It uses the statements for each activity.

Every type ends at profit for the year; the appropriation account then shares it.

## Advantages and disadvantages

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| More capital is available from several owners | Every partner has unlimited liability for the debts of the business |
| Skills are pooled and decisions can be discussed | A disagreement can slow decisions or split the partners |
| Workload and risk are shared | Profit has to be shared, so each owner may earn less than a sole trader would |

When a question asks you to advise someone, link the point to their facts: an owner short of money gains from extra capital; one with a house at stake may fear unlimited liability.

## The partnership agreement

The partnership agreement is the set of terms the partners accept. It matters because the accounts depend on it: without agreed terms nobody can tell what interest, salary or profit each partner should receive, and arguments follow. Typical contents:

- the capital each partner will invest;
- the profit-sharing ratio, also used for losses;
- the rate of interest on capital, if any;
- the rate of interest charged on drawings, if any;
- partners' salaries;
- the rate of interest on partners' loans;
- how and when drawings may be taken.

If a question gives no term for an item, do not invent one.

## What the appropriation account is for

The statement of profit or loss measures what the business earned. The **appropriation account** shows how that profit is divided among the partners under the agreement. It gives the figures to post to the current accounts.

| Item | Where it goes | Effect on the partner's current account |
|---|---|---|
| Interest on a partner's loan | Charged as a business expense, above profit for the year | Credit (if not paid in cash) |
| Interest on capital | Deducted in the appropriation account | Credit |
| Partners' salaries | Deducted in the appropriation account | Credit |
| Interest on drawings | Added in the appropriation account | Debit |
| Balance left over | Divided using the agreed ratio | Credit for a profit, debit for a loss |

A loan is a liability, so its interest is a business expense. Interest on capital and salaries are shares of profit, so they come after profit for the year. Drawings themselves are not in the appropriation account: they are debited to the current accounts.

$20,000. Salaries: Elwick

## Capital and current accounts

The **capital account** records what the partner has invested for the long term. It gives a stable measure of each person's stake and is the base for interest on capital. It normally stays the same from year to year and changes only when capital is put in or taken out.

The **current account** is the working account between the partner and the business. It collects everything that changes during the year: interest and salary earned, the share of profit or loss, drawings and interest on drawings. It can be a credit balance (the business owes the partner) or a debit balance (the partner has taken more than their entitlement). So the capital account is long term and fixed, the current account is yearly and moving.

In the statement of financial position, show each partner's capital and current account in columns with totals, as in example 1.

## Common errors

- Putting loan interest in the appropriation account instead of expenses.
- Subtracting interest on drawings instead of adding it.
- Posting drawings to a fixed capital account.
- Sharing a loss equally when the ratio is different.
- Starting from profit before the adjustments and loan interest.

## Frequently asked questions

**What does a partnership agreement usually include?**
It usually sets out each partner's capital, the profit-sharing ratio (also used for losses), interest on capital, interest on drawings, partners' salaries, interest on partners' loans and how drawings may be taken. If a question gives no term for an item, do not invent one.

**What is the purpose of a partnership appropriation account?**
The appropriation account shows how the profit for the year is divided among the partners under the agreement. It starts from profit for the year and gives the figures posted to the partners' current accounts.

**What is excluded from section 5.2 Partnerships in Cambridge IGCSE Accounting 0452?**
The syllabus excludes questions on ending a partnership, altering the profit-sharing ratio and limited liability partnerships. Financial statements are also not prepared when a partner joins or leaves.

**Why is interest on a partner's loan not in the appropriation account?**
A partner's loan is a liability of the business, so its interest is a business expense deducted before profit for the year. Interest on capital and salaries are shares of profit, so they come after profit for the year.

**What is the difference between a partner's capital account and current account?**
The capital account records the long-term investment and normally stays the same unless capital is introduced or withdrawn. The current account collects interest, salary, the share of profit or loss, drawings and interest on drawings during the year.
