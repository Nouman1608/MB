This guide teaches sub-topic 1(f) Ionic bonding from Topic 1, Principles of chemistry, of the Pearson Edexcel International GCSE Chemistry (4CH1) specification, Issue 3 (September 2024). It covers points 1.37 to 1.43. None of them carries a C reference, so all can be examined on Paper 1 and Paper 2.

The [ionic, covalent and metallic bonding study guide](/resources/edexcel-igcse-chemistry-4ch1-ionic-covalent-and-metallic-bonding/) covers all three bonding sub-topics together. This page spends longer on ionic bonding alone: ions from every group the specification lists, a wider range of formulae, more dot-and-cross practice and full-mark explanations of lattice properties. ## How ions form (1.37)

An **ion** is a charged particle formed when an atom loses or gains electrons. The nucleus does not change, so the number of protons stays the same.

- **Metal atoms lose** their outer-shell electrons and form **positive ions** (cations).
- **Non-metal atoms gain** electrons to complete their outer shell and form **negative ions** (anions).

In both cases the ion has a full outer shell, the same electronic configuration as a noble gas.

**Worked example 1.** Describe how a nitrogen atom and a sulfur atom form ions.

```
nitrogen: 7 protons, configuration 2,5
  gains 3 electrons -> 7 protons, 10 electrons -> N3-, configuration 2,8

sulfur: 16 protons, configuration 2,8,6
  gains 2 electrons -> 16 protons, 18 electrons -> S2-, configuration 2,8,8
```

Ion names change for non-metals: nitrogen forms **nitride**, oxygen **oxide**, sulfur **sulfide**, chlorine **chloride**.

Several different ions can share one configuration. Na⁺, Mg²⁺, Al³⁺, F⁻, O²⁻ and N³⁻ are all 2,8, like neon, but each has a different number of protons, so each has a different charge.

## The charges to learn (1.38)

| Source | Charge | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Group 1 metals | 1+ | Li⁺, Na⁺, K⁺ |
| Group 2 metals | 2+ | Mg²⁺, Ca²⁺ |
| Group 3 metals | 3+ | Al³⁺ |
| Group 5 non-metals | 3− | N³⁻ |
| Group 6 non-metals | 2− | O²⁻, S²⁻ |
| Group 7 non-metals | 1− | F⁻, Cl⁻, Br⁻, I⁻ |
| Other metal ions | Learn individually | Ag⁺, Cu²⁺, Fe²⁺, Fe³⁺, Pb²⁺, Zn²⁺ |
| Other ions | Learn individually | H⁺, OH⁻, NH₄⁺, CO₃²⁻, NO₃⁻, SO₄²⁻ |

The group rule works because the charge equals the number of electrons lost (group number) or gained (8 minus group number). The ions in the last two rows cannot be worked out this way. Roman numerals in a name, such as iron(III), give the charge of the metal ion.

## Writing formulae (1.39)

The total positive charge must equal the total negative charge, because an ionic compound has no overall charge. Put brackets around a compound ion (OH⁻, NH₄⁺, CO₃²⁻, NO₃⁻, SO₄²⁻) when you need more than one.

**Worked example 2.** Write the formulae.

```
calcium nitrate:     Ca2+ and NO3-     need 2 x NO3-      -> Ca(NO3)2
ammonium sulfate:    NH4+ and SO4 2-   need 2 x NH4+      -> (NH4)2SO4
aluminium oxide:     Al3+ and O2-      2 x (+3) = 3 x (-2) -> Al2O3
copper(II) hydroxide: Cu2+ and OH-     need 2 x OH-       -> Cu(OH)2
silver carbonate:    Ag+ and CO3 2-    need 2 x Ag+       -> Ag2CO3
```

**Worked example 3 — reading a formula.** Name Fe₂O₃ and identify its ions.

```
3 oxide ions: 3 x (-2) = -6
2 iron ions must give +6, so each is +3
ions: Fe3+ and O2-; name: iron(III) oxide
```

Writing CuOH₂ instead of Cu(OH)₂ is wrong: without brackets the 2 applies only to the hydrogen.

## Dot-and-cross diagrams (1.40)

A dot-and-cross diagram shows electrons being **transferred**. Show only the outer electrons. Use dots for the electrons from one element and crosses for the other, so a reader can trace where each electron went. Each ion is drawn in square brackets with its charge at the top right.

**Worked example 4 — magnesium chloride, MgCl₂.**

```
before:  Mg has 2 outer electrons (x x)
         each Cl has 7 outer electrons (7 dots)

transfer: Mg gives one electron to each of two Cl atoms

after:   [Mg]2+   with no outer electrons shown (its outer shell is now the full second shell)
         2 x [Cl]-   each with 8 outer electrons: 7 dots + 1 cross
```

Two chlorine atoms are needed because magnesium loses two electrons and each chlorine atom gains only one. The ratio of ions in the diagram must match the formula.

**Worked example 5 — aluminium fluoride, AlF₃.** Aluminium (Group 3) loses 3 electrons; each fluorine (Group 7) gains 1. So one Al³⁺ ion and three F⁻ ions are drawn, each F⁻ with 7 dots and 1 cross in its outer shell.

Common marking points: correct number of each ion; correct charges on every ion; eight electrons in each anion's outer shell, with the transferred electron shown as the other symbol.

## Electrostatic attraction and lattices (1.41, 1.42)

**Ionic bonding** is the strong electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions. In a solid the ions form a **giant ionic lattice**: a regular three-dimensional arrangement in which every ion is surrounded by ions of opposite charge and attracted to them in all directions.

**Model answer.** Explain why potassium bromide has a high melting point. **[3]**

> Potassium bromide has a giant ionic lattice [1]. There are strong electrostatic attractions between the oppositely charged K⁺ and Br⁻ ions [1]. A lot of energy is needed to overcome the many attractions throughout the lattice [1].

Avoid "breaking molecules" or "intermolecular forces": an ionic compound has no molecules. Compounds with more highly charged ions, such as magnesium oxide (Mg²⁺ and O²⁻), have stronger attractions between ions and usually higher melting points than compounds such as sodium chloride (Na⁺ and Cl⁻).

## Conductivity (1.43)

| State | Conducts? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Solid | No | Ions are held in fixed positions in the lattice |
| Molten | Yes | Ions are free to move and carry charge |
| Aqueous solution | Yes | Ions are free to move and carry charge |

You must know the pattern for 1.43; the full explanation is developed in electrolysis (1.56C, Paper 2 only). The mobile particles are **ions**, not electrons.

## Common mistakes

- Saying non-metals "lose" electrons, or that ions form by gaining or losing protons.
- Writing "chlorine ion" instead of chloride ion.
- Dropping brackets: Ca(NO₃)₂, not CaNO₃₂.
- Drawing shared pairs between ions in a dot-and-cross diagram: that is covalent.
- Explaining high melting points with "strong covalent bonds" or "intermolecular forces".
- Saying solid ionic compounds do not conduct "because there are no ions".

## Exam technique

- **Describe** ion formation: say which atom loses or gains how many electrons, and give the resulting charge and configuration.
- **Draw**: brackets, charges, correct ratio of ions, outer electrons only, dots and crosses distinguished.
- **Explain** a property: name the structure, the attraction, and the energy needed.
- **Write the formula**: show the charge balance if working is asked for.
