This guide covers the first two key ideas of section 3.1.3.2, Coastal landscapes, in Section C of Unit 1 of the OxfordAQA International GCSE Geography (9230) specification, Version 4.3: "The coast is shaped by a number of physical processes" and "Distinctive coastal landforms are the result of rock type, structure and physical processes." Section C is compulsory for every candidate. For the whole unit, start with the [Living with the Physical Environment study guide](/resources/igcse-oxfordaqa-geography-living-with-the-physical-environment/). Coastal management, the third key idea, is not covered here.

## What the specification lists

- "Wave types and characteristics."
- Coastal processes: "weathering processes – mechanical, chemical"; "mass movement – sliding, slumping and rock falls"; "erosion – hydraulic power, abrasion and attrition"; "transportation – longshore drift"; "deposition – why sediment is deposited in coastal areas".
- "How geological structure and rock type influence coastal forms."
- Landforms from erosion: "headlands and bays, cliffs and wave cut platforms, caves, arches and stacks".
- Landforms from deposition: "beaches, sand dunes, spits and bars".
- "Examples should be used from the region and integrated into the teaching of the content." The region for this section is the Middle and Far East, so add your teacher's named regional examples to each landform you revise.

## Waves

Waves are created by wind blowing over the sea surface. Their size depends on the **strength of the wind**, **how long it blows** and the **fetch**, the distance of open water over which it blows. A long fetch gives larger, more powerful waves.

As a wave reaches shallow water, friction with the sea bed slows its base, the wave becomes steeper and it breaks. Water rushing up the beach is the **swash**; water draining back is the **backwash**.

| Feature | Constructive waves | Destructive waves |
|---|---|---|
| Height and shape | Low, gently sloping | High, steep |
| Frequency | Fewer per minute | More per minute |
| Swash and backwash | Strong swash, weak backwash | Weak swash, strong backwash |
| Effect | Carry material up the beach and build it up | Drag material down the beach and erode it |
| Typical conditions | Calm weather, distant storms | Storms, strong local winds |

## Weathering

Weathering is the breakdown of rock **in place**, without movement. It weakens cliffs so that erosion and mass movement work faster.

- **Mechanical weathering** breaks rock into pieces without changing its chemistry. In **freeze-thaw**, water enters cracks, freezes and expands, widening the crack; repeated cycles break fragments off. In **salt weathering**, seawater evaporates in cracks and salt crystals grow, forcing the rock apart.
- **Chemical weathering** changes the minerals in the rock. Rainwater is slightly acidic because it absorbs carbon dioxide, and it slowly dissolves rocks such as limestone and chalk; this is called **carbonation**.

## Mass movement

Mass movement is the downhill movement of material under gravity.

- **Sliding:** a block of rock or soil moves down a slope along a flat surface, often after heavy rain.
- **Slumping:** saturated material, often clay, moves down a curved surface with a rotational movement, leaving a stepped, curved scar on the cliff.
- **Rock falls:** fragments break away from a steep cliff face and fall, often after freeze-thaw weathering or undercutting by the sea.

## Erosion

Erosion is the wearing away and removal of rock by the sea. The specification names three processes:

- **Hydraulic power:** the force of waves crashing against the cliff, including air compressed into cracks; when the wave retreats, the air expands explosively, widening the crack.
- **Abrasion:** waves throw sand and pebbles against the cliff, wearing it away like sandpaper.
- **Attrition:** rocks and pebbles carried by the waves knock against each other, becoming smaller and more rounded.

Abrasion and hydraulic power erode the cliff; attrition reduces the size of the sediment.

## Transportation: longshore drift

Longshore drift moves sediment **along** the coast.

1. The prevailing wind makes waves approach the beach at an angle.
2. The swash carries sediment up the beach at the same angle.
3. The backwash drags it straight back down the beach, at right angles to the coast, under gravity.
4. Repeated many times, sediment moves in a zigzag along the beach in the direction of the prevailing wind.

## Deposition

Deposition happens when the sea loses energy and can no longer carry its sediment. It is likely:

- in sheltered areas such as bays, where wave energy is spread out;
- where constructive waves dominate, so swash is stronger than backwash;
- where the coastline changes direction, so longshore drift carries sediment into deeper, calmer water;
- where the supply of sediment is large, for example near river mouths;
- behind obstacles such as groynes.

## How geology influences coastal forms

**Rock type.** Resistant rocks such as granite or chalk erode slowly and tend to form high, steep cliffs and headlands. Less resistant rocks such as clay and sands erode quickly, forming lower, gentler cliffs and bays, and are prone to slumping.

**Structure.** The arrangement of rock layers matters as much as their strength.
- On a **discordant coast**, bands of hard and soft rock run at right angles to the coastline. The soft rock erodes back into bays and the hard rock is left as headlands.
- On a **concordant coast**, the bands run parallel to the coastline, so a single resistant band protects the softer rock behind it; where the sea breaks through, a small, rounded cove may form.
- **Joints and faults** are lines of weakness that erosion exploits, and the **dip** of the rock layers affects the steepness and stability of the cliff.

## Erosional landforms

**Headlands and bays.** On a discordant coast, the less resistant rock erodes faster and forms bays; the resistant rock projects out to sea as headlands. Once headlands stand out, waves bend around them and concentrate energy on them, while bays become sheltered areas of deposition, often with beaches.

**Cliffs and wave-cut platforms.**
1. Destructive waves attack the base of the cliff between high and low tide.
2. Hydraulic power and abrasion cut a **wave-cut notch**.
3. The notch deepens and the overhanging rock above becomes unsupported.
4. The overhang collapses, and the cliff retreats inland.
5. Repeated over time, a gently sloping rocky **wave-cut platform** is left at the foot of the cliff, exposed at low tide.

**Caves, arches and stacks.**
1. Waves erode a weakness, such as a joint or fault, in a headland by hydraulic power and abrasion, widening it into a **cave**.
2. The cave is eroded deeper until it breaks through the headland, forming an **arch**.
3. The base of the arch is eroded and weathering weakens its roof, which eventually collapses.
4. The remaining isolated column of rock is a **stack**.
5. Further erosion at its base may reduce the stack to a low **stump**.

## Depositional landforms

**Beaches.** Accumulations of sand or shingle between the low-tide line and the highest point reached by storm waves, built mainly by constructive waves in sheltered places such as bays. Sand beaches are usually gently sloping; shingle beaches are steeper because the larger material lets the backwash drain into it.

**Sand dunes.** Where a wide beach dries at low tide, onshore winds blow sand inland. It collects against obstacles such as driftwood, forming small **embryo dunes**. Plants such as marram grass, with long roots and tolerance of salt and burial, colonise and trap more sand, so dunes grow. Further inland, dunes become older, higher and more vegetated, and the soil gains organic matter, so a wider range of plants can grow.

**Spits.** A spit is a long, narrow ridge of sand or shingle joined to the land at one end.
1. Longshore drift carries sediment along the coast.
2. Where the coastline changes direction, for example at a river mouth or bay, the sediment is carried into the calmer, deeper water and deposited.
3. The ridge extends out to sea.
4. Changes in wind direction can curve the end, forming a **recurved** tip.
5. Sheltered, calm water behind the spit may let fine sediment settle, forming **salt marsh**.

**Bars.** If a spit grows across a bay and joins the land on the other side, it forms a **bar**, trapping a shallow **lagoon** behind it.

## Worked example: a 4-mark answer

**Question:** Explain how a stack is formed. [4 marks]

**Model answer:** "Waves attack a weakness such as a crack in a headland, and hydraulic power and abrasion widen it into a cave. Erosion continues until the cave cuts right through the headland, forming an arch. The base of the arch is eroded and its roof is weakened by weathering until it collapses. This leaves an isolated pillar of rock separated from the headland, which is a stack."

Each sentence is one stage, with the process named. Examiners expect the sequence in order and the processes identified, not just the landform names.

## Common mistakes

- **Confusing weathering and erosion.** Weathering is breakdown in place; erosion is removal by the sea.
- **Calling longshore drift erosion.** It is a transport process.
- **Mixing up attrition and abrasion.** Attrition makes sediment smaller; abrasion wears away the cliff.
- **Skipping stages** in sequences such as cave, arch, stack.
- **Generic answers.** Include named examples from the region where you can.
