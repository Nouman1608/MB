This guide goes deeper into sub-topic **6.1 Photosynthesis** of the Cambridge IGCSE Biology 0610 syllabus for examination in 2026, 2027 and 2028. Statements 1 to 9 are Core, so every candidate needs them. Statement 10 (the balanced equation) and statement 11 (limiting factors) are Supplement, so they are **Extended only** and appear on Papers 2 and 4. Photosynthesis (rate and limiting factors) and the use of hydrogencarbonate indicator are both named experimental contexts for Paper 5 and Paper 6.

The [plant nutrition study guide](/resources/igcse-biology-0610-plant-nutrition/) introduces the whole topic, including the starch-test method and a lamp-distance data set. This page builds on it with the reasoning examiners reward: precise energy wording, explanation chains for mineral ions, planning with controls, a temperature data set, a four-tube indicator problem and limiting factors in real situations. Condense it afterwards with the [photosynthesis revision notes](/resources/cambridge-igcse-biology-0610-photosynthesis-revision-notes/) and test yourself with the [plant nutrition practice questions](/resources/igcse-biology-plant-nutrition-practice/).

## Energy: the wording that scores

Photosynthesis is an energy transfer, not energy creation. Chlorophyll **absorbs light** and **transfers energy from light into energy in chemicals**, which is then stored in the carbohydrates made. Three phrases lose marks every series:

- "Chlorophyll makes energy." Energy cannot be made.
- "Plants get their food from the soil." Plants take mineral ions and water from the soil, but they make their own carbohydrate in the leaves.
- "Light is a raw material." The raw materials are carbon dioxide and water; light is the energy source, and light and chlorophyll are conditions written over the arrow.

Follow each raw material to its source: carbon dioxide diffuses in from the air through the stomata; water is absorbed by root hair cells and carried up in the xylem. Follow each product: glucose is used or converted in the plant; oxygen diffuses out through the stomata or is used in the plant's own respiration.

### Reading the balanced equation (Extended only)

The numbers in front of each formula give the ratio of molecules: six carbon dioxide and six water molecules are used for every one glucose molecule made, and six oxygen molecules are released.

**Worked example.** A leaf takes in 42 molecules of carbon dioxide during photosynthesis. How many glucose molecules can it make, and how many oxygen molecules are released?

- Glucose: 42 ÷ 6 = **7 molecules**.
- Oxygen: one oxygen for each carbon dioxide, so **42 molecules**.

## What the plant does with the carbohydrate

The five uses in 6.1.5 are a chain, not a list of separate facts. Glucose made in a leaf can be used straight away in **respiration** to provide energy. Much of it is converted to **sucrose** for transport in the phloem to other parts of the plant. At its destination the sugar is turned into **starch** as an energy store (for example in a seed or storage organ), into **cellulose** to build the walls of new cells, or into **nectar** in flowers, which attracts insects for pollination.

Starch is a good store because it is insoluble, so it stays inside cells. Sucrose suits transport because it is soluble and travels in the phloem sap.

## Mineral ions: explanation chains

"Explain the importance" needs a chain of at least two links, ending in an effect on the plant.

| Ion | Link 1 | Link 2 | Effect of a shortage |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nitrate | needed to make amino acids | amino acids make proteins, needed for new cells | poor, stunted growth |
| Magnesium | needed to make chlorophyll | chlorophyll absorbs light for photosynthesis | yellow leaves; less light absorbed, so less carbohydrate is made |

A model answer for magnesium [3]: magnesium ions are needed to make chlorophyll [1]; without enough chlorophyll the leaves are yellow and absorb less light [1]; so less energy is transferred for photosynthesis and less carbohydrate is made for growth [1].

## Planning the "need for" investigations (6.1.7)

Each investigation removes one requirement from part of a plant and compares it with a part that has everything. The comparison is only valid if the plant was **destarched**, and it is good practice to test one leaf straight after destarching to show that no starch is left.

**Worked example: carbon dioxide.** Two destarched potted plants are each sealed in a clear plastic bag in bright light for six hours. Bag A contains a dish of sodium hydroxide solution, which absorbs carbon dioxide. Bag B contains a dish of water instead. A leaf from each plant is then tested with iodine.

- Expected results: leaf from A stays **orange-brown**; leaf from B turns **blue-black**.
- Why B has water, not an empty space: the only difference between the bags is the carbon dioxide absorber, so the result can only be caused by the lack of carbon dioxide.
- Keep the same: plant species and size, light intensity, temperature and time.

An "appropriate control" is identical to the test except for the one factor being investigated. Saying "a control was used" without describing it earns nothing.

## Rate of photosynthesis: a temperature data set

A student collects the gas given off by pondweed in a gas syringe for 5 minutes at each temperature, keeping light intensity and sodium hydrogencarbonate concentration the same.

| Temperature / °C | Volume of gas in 5 min / cm³ | Rate / cm³ per min |
|---|---|---|
| 10 | 0.6 | 0.12 |
| 20 | 1.4 | 0.28 |
| 30 | 2.3 | 0.46 |
| 40 | 1.1 | 0.22 |

Working for 30 °C: 2.3 ÷ 5 = 0.46 cm³ per min.

A full description quotes the trend and the data: the rate increases from 0.12 to 0.46 cm³ per min between 10 °C and 30 °C, then falls to 0.22 cm³ per min at 40 °C. The explanation for the fall is that the enzymes controlling photosynthesis are denatured at high temperature. The optimum lies somewhere near 30 °C, but with readings 10 °C apart you can only say it is between 20 °C and 40 °C; more readings between those temperatures would locate it.

For carbon dioxide concentration, the independent variable is the concentration of sodium hydrogencarbonate solution around the pondweed. Keep the lamp at a fixed distance and stand the tube in a water bath at one temperature.

## Hydrogencarbonate indicator: four-tube problem

Four sealed tubes contain orange-red indicator. After several hours in bright light:

| Tube | Contents | Colour | Reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | pondweed | purple | photosynthesis faster than respiration, so carbon dioxide falls |
| 2 | pondweed, tube covered in foil | yellow | respiration only, so carbon dioxide rises |
| 3 | small water snail | yellow | animals respire but cannot photosynthesise |
| 4 | no organism | orange-red | control: shows the indicator alone does not change |

If tube 1 is moved into dim light, the colour may stay orange-red because carbon dioxide taken in by photosynthesis equals carbon dioxide released by respiration. A tube holding both a snail and pondweed in light could turn purple or stay orange-red, depending on whether the plant removes carbon dioxide faster than both organisms release it.

## Limiting factors in different conditions (Extended only)

Statement 11 asks you to apply the idea to environments, not just graphs. The limiting factor is the one in shortest supply at that moment.

| Situation | Likely limiting factor | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Just after dawn | light intensity | light is still dim; temperature and carbon dioxide are adequate |
| Bright, frosty winter morning | temperature | plenty of light, but enzymes work slowly in the cold |
| Warm, sunny midday in a field | carbon dioxide concentration | light and temperature are high; carbon dioxide in air is low |
| Plants on a woodland floor in summer | light intensity | the tree canopy absorbs most of the light |
| Closed glasshouse on a sunny afternoon | carbon dioxide concentration | the plants have used up much of the carbon dioxide inside |

To justify the answer, say what would happen if the factor were increased: only increasing the limiting factor raises the rate.

## Common mistakes

- Writing "chlorophyll absorbs light energy and turns it into glucose": glucose is made from carbon dioxide and water, not from light.
- Giving "glucose for energy" without the word respiration.
- Saying nitrate is needed "for growth" with no mention of amino acids or proteins.
- Describing a control as "a plant with nothing done to it" when it must be identical apart from the factor tested.
- Describing a rate graph without quoting figures from the data.
- Extended: naming two limiting factors at once; at any one point only one factor limits.

## Exam technique for 6.1

- **Describe** results: give the trend and quote values, with units.
- **Explain** results: give the biological reason (enzymes, energy transfer, gas exchange).
- **Suggest** a limiting factor: use the clues in the question (time of day, season, closed space).
- **Calculate** a rate: divide the volume or count by the time, and give the unit, such as cm³ per min.
