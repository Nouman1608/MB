This guide goes deeper on one section of the OxfordAQA International GCSE Physics (9203) specification, for exams May/June 2018 onwards: **3.7.4 Nuclear fusion**. The specification marks this section P, so it is assessed in the full Physics award only. The qualification is untiered.

The broader [half-life, fission and fusion guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-physics-half-life-contamination-nuclear-fission-and-fusion/) sets fusion beside half-life and fission. Here the focus is narrower: how to write fusion equations, what "mass converted into energy" really means, why the conditions must be so extreme, and how all of this connects to stars. ## Light nuclei and heavier nuclei

"Light" means a small mass number. The nuclei that fuse most readily are the isotopes of hydrogen and helium:

| Nucleus | Symbol | Protons | Neutrons |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hydrogen-1 | ¹₁H | 1 | 0 |
| Hydrogen-2 | ²₁H | 1 | 1 |
| Hydrogen-3 | ³₁H | 1 | 2 |
| Helium-3 | ³₂He | 2 | 1 |
| Helium-4 | ⁴₂He | 2 | 2 |

The three hydrogen nuclei are isotopes of one element: the same number of protons, different numbers of neutrons. A fusion reaction always makes a product nucleus that is heavier than either nucleus that went in. Sometimes a single particle, such as a neutron (¹₀n) or a proton (¹₁H), is released as well.

A quick test: two small nuclei on the left and one larger nucleus on the right means fusion.

## Balancing fusion equations

Fusion equations obey the same rule as decay equations: the total mass number and the total atomic number are the same on both sides.

**Worked example 1.** Two helium-3 nuclei fuse to form helium-4 and two identical particles, X. Identify X.

³₂He + ³₂He → ⁴₂He + 2X

1. Mass numbers on the left: 3 + 3 = 6. On the right, helium-4 accounts for 4, so the two X particles share 6 − 4 = 2. Each X has a mass number of 1.
2. Atomic numbers on the left: 2 + 2 = 4. Helium-4 accounts for 2, so the two X particles share 2. Each X has an atomic number of 1.
3. A particle with mass number 1 and atomic number 1 is a proton, ¹₁H.

Check: 3 + 3 = 4 + 1 + 1 and 2 + 2 = 2 + 1 + 1. Both sides balance.

**Worked example 2.** Two hydrogen-2 nuclei fuse: ²₁H + ²₁H → ³₂He + Y. Mass number of Y = 4 − 3 = 1. Atomic number of Y = 2 − 2 = 0. Y is a neutron, ¹₀n.

Notice the coefficient trap in example 1: "2X" means two particles, so divide the leftover numbers by two. Students who forget this give X a mass number of 2.

## Where the energy comes from

Statement 3.7.4 b says that some of the mass of the smaller nuclei is converted into energy. This can look like a contradiction, because the mass numbers balance. The resolution is that mass number and mass are not the same thing.

- **Mass number** counts particles: protons plus neutrons. Fusion does not create or destroy protons or neutrons, so the count is conserved.
- **Mass** is the actual amount of matter. When nuclei fuse, the total mass of the products is very slightly less than the total mass of the nuclei that joined.

That small decrease in mass is the mass that has been converted into energy. The energy is carried away mainly as kinetic energy of the product particles, which share it as they fly apart.

**Worked example 3 (mass data supplied).** For the reaction ²₁H + ³₁H → ⁴₂He + ¹₀n, the masses in atomic mass units (u) are: hydrogen-2 2.01355 u, hydrogen-3 3.01550 u, helium-4 4.00151 u, neutron 1.00866 u.

1. Mass before = 2.01355 + 3.01550 = 5.02905 u.
2. Mass after = 4.00151 + 1.00866 = 5.01017 u.
3. Decrease = 5.02905 − 5.01017 = 0.01888 u.
4. As a percentage of the starting mass: 0.01888 ÷ 5.02905 × 100 = 0.375%.

Only about 0.4% of the mass disappears, yet this is the source of all the energy released, while the mass numbers still balance (2 + 3 = 4 + 1). No equation linking mass and energy is needed for this specification.

## Why the conditions must be extreme

Every nucleus contains protons, so every nucleus is positively charged. Two nuclei therefore repel each other, and the repulsion becomes stronger as they get closer. To fuse, they must get very close indeed. Statement 3.7.4 c says this repulsion must be overcome, and that this happens at very high temperatures and pressures.

**High temperature.** Temperature is linked to how fast particles move. At an extremely high temperature, nuclei move so fast that they have enough kinetic energy to approach one another despite the repulsion. At lower temperatures they slow, stop and are pushed apart before they can join.

**High pressure.** A very high pressure packs a large number of nuclei into a small volume. Close, fast collisions then happen very often, so fusion takes place at a high enough rate to release a large amount of energy.

Temperature makes each collision energetic enough; pressure makes energetic collisions frequent. A full explanation names both jobs.

**Larger charges need hotter conditions.** A helium nucleus carries a charge of +2, twice that of a hydrogen nucleus. Two helium nuclei therefore repel more strongly than two hydrogen nuclei, and fusing them needs an even higher temperature. This is why the [life cycle of a star guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-physics-life-cycle-of-a-star/) links a hotter core to the creation of heavier nuclei.

## Annotated model answer

**Question:** Explain why two hydrogen nuclei will fuse only at a very high temperature and pressure. [4 marks]

**Answer:** Both nuclei are positively charged, so they repel each other (1). To fuse, they have to get very close, so this repulsion must be overcome (1). At a very high temperature the nuclei move very fast, so they have enough kinetic energy to get close despite the repulsion (1). At a very high pressure the nuclei are packed close together, so collisions are frequent and fusion happens often enough (1).

*Why it scores:* each sentence makes one creditworthy point, and the temperature and pressure points each say what the condition achieves. An answer that only says "it needs to be hot to give the nuclei energy" scores one mark at most, because it never mentions the charge or the repulsion that the energy is needed to overcome.

## Fusion in stars

Statement 3.7.4 d connects this section to space physics. During the main sequence, hydrogen nuclei fuse to form helium nuclei in the core of a star. The core is where the temperature and density are greatest, so it is where most fusion takes place. Gravity squeezes the star's material towards the centre, which is what produces such extreme conditions there.

The energy released keeps the core hot and leaves the star as light and heat. In more massive stars the core becomes hot enough for helium nuclei to fuse into carbon, and further fusion builds nuclei such as nitrogen and oxygen. Fusion in stars is the source of their energy and of the naturally occurring elements.

## Fission and fusion side by side

| Feature | Fission | Fusion |
|---|---|---|
| Nuclei involved | One large, unstable nucleus (uranium-235 or plutonium-239) | Two light nuclei, such as isotopes of hydrogen or helium |
| What happens | The nucleus splits into two smaller nuclei | The nuclei join into one heavier nucleus |
| What starts it | The nucleus absorbs a neutron | Very high temperature and pressure overcome the repulsion |
| Particles released | Two or three neutrons | Sometimes a neutron or a proton |
| Where you meet it in 9203 | Nuclear reactors | Stars |

Both processes release energy because the products have slightly less mass than the starting nuclei.

## Common mistakes

- Defining fusion as "atoms joining". It is nuclei that join; the electrons play no part.
- Saying the mass number decreases in fusion. Mass numbers balance; it is the mass that decreases slightly.
- Writing that the nuclei "attract" or need to "collide hard". Name the repulsion between positive charges.
- Giving temperature without saying what it does (kinetic energy, speed) or why that matters (overcoming repulsion).
- Forgetting to divide by two when an equation shows two identical product particles.
- Saying stars produce energy by burning hydrogen. Burning is a chemical reaction; stars release energy by nuclear fusion.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **State** or **define**: one precise sentence. "Two light nuclei join to form a heavier nucleus."
- **Complete** an equation: write both numbers for the missing particle and check both totals.
- **Explain**: give the cause and the effect, joined by "so" or "because". For conditions, chain charge, repulsion, kinetic energy and collision frequency.
- **Compare**: make each point about both processes in the same sentence, for example "fission splits a large nucleus, whereas fusion joins two light nuclei".

## Frequently asked questions

**Is nuclear fusion in the OxfordAQA Combined Science course?**
No. Section 3.7.4 is marked P in the 9203 specification, so it is assessed only in the full International GCSE Physics award.

**If mass numbers balance, how can mass be converted into energy?**
Mass number counts protons and neutrons, which are conserved. The actual mass of the products is slightly less than the mass of the nuclei that fused, and that difference is converted into energy.
