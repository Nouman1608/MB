## Section A

**1.** Identify three features of economic policy under the Harding and Coolidge administrations. **[3]**

**2.** State two reasons why the stock market boom of 1927-29 was unstable. **[2]**

**3.** Give three structural weaknesses of the American banking system in the 1920s. **[3]**

**4.** Identify three ways in which the Depression affected families and minorities. **[3]**

**5.** State two reasons why Hoover refused to give direct federal relief to individuals. **[2]**

**6.** Identify three measures of the Hundred Days and the problem each addressed. **[3]**

**7.** Explain two reasons why Roosevelt's court-packing plan failed. **[4]**

## Section B

**8.** Explain why Hoover's policies failed to end the Depression. **[10]**

**9.** "Speculation was the main cause of the Great Crash of 1929." How far do you agree? **[20]**

**10.** Read the two invented sources below. Compare Sources A and B as evidence about the Works Progress Administration.

*Source A (invented)* -- From a letter by an unemployed bricklayer in Pittsburgh to the President, 1936. "For two years I stood in the relief line and felt like a beggar. Now I am building a school, and on Saturday I bring home pay. My boys see their father go to work again. God bless you for giving a man his self-respect."

*Source B (invented)* -- From an editorial in a business newspaper, 1936. "The WPA is the greatest vote-buying machine in history. Men are paid to rake leaves and lean on shovels, and the bill is sent to the taxpayer. Every relief office is a Democratic campaign office. Private industry cannot hire when government takes its profits in taxes." **[15]**

**11.** Read the two further invented sources below, together with Sources A and B in Question 10. How far do Sources A to D support the view that the New Deal had brought recovery by 1938?

*Source C (invented)* -- From a report by a federal official in a cotton county of the South, 1937. "The landowners have received their crop payments and are buying tractors. The tenants they no longer need have been put off the land. Many families are on the roads, and the relief rolls here are longer than in 1934."

*Source D (invented)* -- From a radio address by a Democratic senator, October 1938. "Look back five years. The banks are open and your savings are insured. The farmer's prices have risen. The old have the promise of a pension and the worker the right to his union. The setback of this past year shows only that we cut spending too soon." **[25]**

**12.** Explain why the Social Security Act was passed in 1935. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** Any three, 1 mark each: tax cuts, especially on high incomes, under Mellon (1); high protective tariffs such as Fordney-McCumber (1922) (1); minimal regulation of business and the stock market (1); reduced federal spending and balanced budgets (1); opposition to farm price support, shown by Coolidge's vetoes of McNary-Haugen (1). **[3]**

**2.** Any two, 1 mark each: share prices rose far faster than company profits, so they were overvalued (1); many buyers bought on the margin, so a fall would force sales (1); investment trusts and brokers' loans spread borrowed money through the market (1); there was little regulation of share issues or trading practices (1). **[2]**

**3.** Any three, 1 mark each: many small independent banks with little capital (1); low cash reserves relative to deposits (1); no federal insurance of deposits, so customers ran to withdraw at the first rumour (1); banks lent heavily for speculation or invested depositors' money in shares (1); rural banks were weakened by farm debts throughout the 1920s (1). **[3]**

**4.** Any three, 1 mark each: unemployment left families without income and local relief ran out (1); evictions and foreclosures led to Hoovervilles (1); marriages and births were delayed and some men left home to look for work (1); African Americans were often "last hired, first fired" (1); Mexican Americans faced pressure to leave and deportation (1); women were dismissed to keep jobs for men (1). **[3]**

**5.** Any two, 1 mark each: he believed federal handouts would destroy self-reliance ("rugged individualism") (1); he believed relief was the responsibility of local government and private charity (1); he feared an unbalanced federal budget would damage confidence (1). **[2]**

**6.** Any three, 1 mark each, for a measure correctly matched to a problem: Emergency Banking Act -- bank failures and loss of confidence (1); CCC -- unemployed young men (1); FERA -- the collapse of state and local relief (1); AAA -- low farm prices from overproduction (1); TVA -- poverty, flooding and lack of electricity in the Tennessee valley (1); NIRA -- falling prices and wages in industry and the need for jobs on public works (1); Glass-Steagall -- risky banking and the lack of deposit insurance (1). **[3]**

**7.** Two reasons, 2 marks each: many Democrats, as well as Republicans, saw it as an attack on the independence of the Court and the Constitution, so Congress would not pass it (2); the Court itself began upholding New Deal laws in 1937 -- for example on minimum wages -- removing the urgency (2); the death of Senate majority leader Joseph Robinson in 1937 weakened the bill's management in Congress (2); Roosevelt's justification that the justices were too old and overworked was seen as dishonest (2). **[4]**

**8.** Indicative content. **Belief in voluntarism:** Hoover asked business to keep wages and investment up, but firms facing falling demand cut both. **Limits on relief:** by refusing direct federal relief he left the burden on local governments and charities that had run out of money. **The RFC:** it lent to banks, railways and insurers, but cautiously, and critics said it helped the powerful rather than the unemployed; the 1932 relief law came late. **Tariffs and trade:** the Hawley-Smoot Tariff (1930) worsened the international slump that hurt American exports. **Fiscal orthodoxy:** the tax increase of 1932, intended to balance the budget, reduced demand. **Confidence:** the Bonus March eviction and his public optimism ("prosperity is just around the corner" was the phrase attached to his administration) made him seem out of touch. **Linking:** his measures addressed credit for institutions rather than demand from consumers, so they could not reverse the collapse in spending. **[10]**

**9.** Indicative content. **For speculation:** rising share prices from 1927 bore little relation to profits; margin buying meant that small falls forced sales; investment trusts and brokers' loans spread borrowed money; when prices fell in October 1929 margin calls turned decline into panic. **Against:** speculation needed other conditions -- easy credit in 1927 and the absence of regulation of share issues; the Federal Reserve's tightening in 1928-29 raised borrowing costs; signs of economic slowdown by mid-1929 (falling construction, car sales and industrial output) undermined confidence; unequal distribution of wealth meant much money flowed into shares rather than consumption; farm and old-industry problems showed the boom's base was narrow. **Judgement:** strong answers explain that speculation was the immediate mechanism of the Crash but that a decline in confidence in the economy triggered it, and weak regulation allowed it. **[20]**

**10.** Indicative content. **Similarities:** both recognise that the WPA provided paid work in place of relief; both connect it with politics -- A thanks the President, B accuses it of buying votes. **Differences:** A says the work was real and useful (building a school) and restored self-respect; B says it was pointless ("rake leaves") and wasteful. A sees benefits for the worker; B sees costs for business and the taxpayer. **Explaining:** A is a beneficiary writing to Roosevelt, possibly hoping the programme continues; B is a business newspaper in an election year, hostile to taxes and government competition with private employers. Knowledge: the WPA built schools, roads and public buildings and employed millions, but accusations of political use of relief were made in some states; the 1936 election saw business opinion largely opposed to Roosevelt. **[15]**

**11.** Indicative content. **Support:** D lists banks open and insured, higher farm prices, pensions and union rights; A shows a worker back in employment. Knowledge: GDP and industrial output rose substantially from 1933 to 1937; bank failures almost ended after 1933; farm incomes rose. **Challenge:** C shows AAA payments benefiting landowners while tenants were evicted and relief rolls grew; D itself admits a "setback" in 1937-38; B claims government spending prevented private hiring. Knowledge: the 1937-38 recession brought a sharp rise in unemployment; unemployment remained high until wartime production; sharecroppers and many African Americans gained least. **Provenance:** D is a politician's broadcast before the 1938 mid-term elections; C is an official report, less obviously partisan; B is hostile opinion. **Judgement:** the sources support partial recovery -- in banking, farm prices and confidence -- but not full recovery by 1938, which came only with rearmament. **[25]**

**12.** Indicative content. **Need:** the Depression had shown that old people, the unemployed and dependent children had no protection when savings and local relief were exhausted; state old-age pensions, where they existed, were small and poorly funded. **Political pressure:** the Townsend movement's demands for generous pensions and Huey Long's "Share Our Wealth" campaign attracted millions and threatened Roosevelt's support from the Left. **Roosevelt's aims:** he wanted permanent reform rather than temporary relief, and a contributory system funded by payroll taxes that a future Congress could not easily abolish. **Frances Perkins:** the Labor Secretary chaired the committee that drafted the plan. **Timing:** after Court defeats and with the 1936 election approaching, the second New Deal turned to lasting reform. **Linking:** the Depression created the need, but pressure from the Left turned need into legislation in 1935. **[10]**
