## Section A

**1.** Identify three features of the lives of Black South Africans around 1948. **[3]**

**2.** Identify three apartheid laws passed between 1949 and 1953 and state what each did. **[3]**

**3.** Explain two ways in which the government suppressed opposition in the 1950s. **[4]**

**4.** Explain two reasons why the ANC turned to armed struggle in 1961. **[4]**

**5.** Give three features of life in the Bantustans. **[3]**

**6.** Identify three reforms introduced by P.W. Botha. **[3]**

**7.** Give three reasons why Britain did not use force against Rhodesia after UDI. **[3]**

**8.** Identify three terms of the Lancaster House Agreement. **[3]**

---

## Section B

**9.** Evaluate the effectiveness of opposition to apartheid in the years 1948-59. **[30]**

**10.** "Black Consciousness was the most significant form of resistance to apartheid in the years 1960-77." Assess this view. **[30]**

**11.** Assess the roles of de Klerk and Mandela in ending apartheid. **[30]**

**12.** "The guerrilla war was the main reason for the end of white minority rule in Zimbabwe." Assess this view. **[30]**

---

## Answers

**1.** Any three, 1 mark each: most land was reserved for whites, with Africans confined to overcrowded reserves (1); migrant labour separated men from families, especially in mining (1); pass laws restricted movement and residence in towns (1); urban Africans lived in townships and squatter camps with poor housing and services (1); job colour bars kept Africans in low-paid work (1); Africans had no vote in national elections (1). **[3]**

**2.** Any three, 1 mark each (law and effect): Prohibition of Mixed Marriages Act (1949) banned marriage between whites and other groups (1); Population Registration Act (1950) classified everyone by race (1); Group Areas Act (1950) assigned separate residential areas (1); Suppression of Communism Act (1950) allowed banning of opponents (1); Bantu Authorities Act (1951) set up tribal authorities in the reserves (1); Reservation of Separate Amenities Act (1953) segregated public facilities (1); Bantu Education Act (1953) placed African schooling under state control with an inferior curriculum (1). **[3]**

**3.** Two ways, 2 marks each: banning orders under the Suppression of Communism Act restricted leaders' movement, meetings and publication (2); mass arrests during the Defiance Campaign, followed by laws increasing penalties for civil disobedience (2); the Treason Trial (1956-61) tied up 156 leaders in court for years (2); police raids and violence at meetings and protests (2). **[4]**

**4.** Two reasons, 2 marks each: the Sharpeville massacre showed that peaceful protest would be met with lethal force (2); the banning of the ANC in 1960 removed legal means of opposition (2); the state's refusal to respond to the 1961 stay-at-home and its use of emergency powers convinced leaders that non-violence had failed (2). **[4]**

**5.** Any three, 1 mark each: overcrowding and poor land (1); poverty and dependence on migrant labour in white areas (1); forced removals added to their populations (1); rule by chiefs and leaders dependent on Pretoria (1); loss of South African citizenship for people assigned to "independent" homelands (1); lack of international recognition (1). **[3]**

**6.** Any three, 1 mark each: legalisation of Black trade unions (1979) (1); the Tricameral Parliament for whites, Coloureds and Indians (1984) (1); repeal of the Mixed Marriages and Immorality Acts (1985) (1); abolition of the pass laws (1986) (1); some relaxation of petty apartheid (1). **[3]**

**7.** Any three, 1 mark each: fear that British troops would refuse to fight white "kith and kin" (1); the cost and distance of military action (1); the risk of South African involvement (1); Wilson's narrow parliamentary majority and domestic opinion (1); belief that sanctions would bring Smith down quickly (1). **[3]**

**8.** Any three, 1 mark each: a ceasefire and the return of guerrillas to assembly points (1); a new constitution with 20 of 100 seats reserved for whites for seven years (1); protection of property rights, with land transfers on a willing-seller, willing-buyer basis (1); a British governor, Lord Soames, to supervise elections (1); a Commonwealth monitoring force (1). **[3]**

**9.** Indicative content. **Effective:** the ANC Youth League's Programme of Action (1949) transformed the ANC into a mass movement; the Defiance Campaign (1952) grew ANC membership greatly; the Freedom Charter (1955) built a multiracial alliance and lasting programme; the women's march (1956) showed broad mobilisation. **Limits:** none of the main apartheid laws was repealed; the government answered with bannings, harsher penalties and the Treason Trial; divisions led to the PAC split (1959); white voters increased their support for the National Party. **Criterion:** effectiveness in changing policy, against effectiveness in building organisation and support. **Judgement:** ineffective in stopping apartheid, but effective in creating the organisations, leaders and ideas that later resistance used. AO1 15 + AO2 15 = **[30]**

**10.** Indicative content. **For:** Black Consciousness revived internal resistance after the crushing of the ANC and PAC in the early 1960s; SASO and Biko built confidence among students and young workers; it inspired the Soweto uprising (1976), the largest challenge since Sharpeville; Biko's death drew international condemnation and the mandatory arms embargo. **Against:** the PAC's anti-pass campaign and Sharpeville (1960) produced the decisive international reaction; MK's armed struggle and the Rivonia Trial made Mandela a global symbol; the external ANC kept the movement alive; workers' strikes in Durban (1973) showed a different, union-based resistance. **Judgement:** strong answers distinguish significance in reviving resistance (Black Consciousness) from significance in international impact (Sharpeville, Rivonia). AO1 15 + AO2 15 = **[30]**

**11.** Indicative content. **De Klerk:** the decision of February 1990 to unban the ANC and release Mandela; repeal of apartheid laws; the 1992 referendum; willingness to negotiate away white minority power. **Mandela:** authority to keep the ANC committed to negotiation; reassurance to whites; restraint after Boipatong and Hani's assassination, when he appealed for calm on television. **Limits on their roles:** both acted under pressure -- township revolt, the UDF and unions, economic crisis, sanctions and the end of the Cold War; other leaders, such as Cyril Ramaphosa and Roelf Meyer, negotiated details; Buthelezi's Inkatha and extremists threatened the process. **Judgement:** the leaders shaped how and when apartheid ended peacefully, while structural pressures explain why it ended. AO1 15 + AO2 15 = **[30]**

**12.** Indicative content. **For:** ZANLA's rural campaign after 1972 and Mozambique's independence in 1975 stretched Rhodesian forces; conscription, casualties and the cost of the war drove white emigration; the attacks on Viscount airliners and cross-border raids showed escalation; the Internal Settlement failed to end the fighting. **Other factors:** sanctions, though evaded, raised costs; South Africa and the USA pressed Smith to accept majority rule in 1976; Britain refused to recognise Muzorewa; the front-line states pressed Mugabe and Nkomo to accept Lancaster House; Carrington's diplomacy. **Judgement:** the war made white rule unsustainable, but diplomacy and regional pressure determined that it ended through a negotiated settlement in 1979-80. AO1 15 + AO2 15 = **[30]**
