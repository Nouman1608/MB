This guide covers the "Initial preparation and research proposal" section of Component 4, the Cambridge Research Report, in the Cambridge International AS & A Level Global Perspectives & Research (9239) syllabus for 2026-2028. The syllabus advises candidates "to choose a topic that engages them strongly and is of real importance to them", and requires that "each candidate must write a research proposal and discuss this with their teacher". The matching assessment points include "analyse their research question (AO1)" and "select and analyse relevant concepts (AO1)". For the component as a whole, read the [Cambridge Research Report study guide](/resources/a-level-global-perspectives-9239-component-4-cambridge-research-report/) first. This page goes further into the work you do before you start the report itself.

## Why the preparation stage matters

The syllabus says: "It is important that candidates spend sufficient time on this preparatory stage." The proposal is **not externally marked**, but it is submitted with your report and it decides almost everything that follows. A weak question cannot produce a strong report, however well you write. A good question allows you to meet the assessment criteria: methods, concepts, sources, perspectives, judgement and reflection.

| What the proposal fixes | Which later marks it affects |
|---|---|
| Research question | analysing the question; judgement; conclusions |
| Key concepts | selecting and analysing concepts |
| Outline of the debate | building and comparing perspectives |
| Example sources | using and assessing sources |

## What the syllabus says the proposal must contain

The research proposal "should outline":

1. the **research question**;
2. the **key concepts** which are relevant to the question;
3. an **outline of the debate** to be addressed;
4. **examples of sources** which will be used.

The candidate must discuss the proposal with the teacher, "to ensure that their research question, concepts, initial thoughts on how they intend to argue for or against the question, and sources are appropriate". The teacher must review it to confirm "whether the concepts and debate are relevant and that the research question chosen gives the candidate scope to meet the assessment criteria", and formally approves it **before the candidate starts work on the research**. A completed proposal form, with the teacher's comments, is submitted to Cambridge International along with the materials for assessment.

## Step 1: choose a topic that matters to you

The syllabus advises a topic that engages you strongly and is of real importance to you. You will work on it for months, and the report must be written for a non-specialist reader, so it helps if you can explain why it matters.

Test your topic with these questions.

- **Interest.** Will I still want to read about this in six months?
- **Importance.** Why does this matter to people beyond me?
- **Debate.** Do informed people disagree about it?
- **Evidence.** Is there enough evidence for me to find and use?
- **Scale.** Can I do it justice in up to 5000 words?

The syllabus tells teachers to check that the topic "is not too large, nor too complex". A topic such as "the economy" is too large. A topic such as "bicycle lanes on one street in my town" may be too narrow for a range of perspectives.

## Step 2: turn the topic into a question

The title of the report **must take the form of a question**. The syllabus warns that "formulating a good research question takes time and careful consideration".

Look at its own example. "Industrialisation" is a topic, not a question. "Has industrialisation been environmentally damaging?" is a question, "but it would not be suitable because it suggests a one-sided, preconceived view and does not lead directly to a debate". "Is industrialisation a good thing?" is appropriate "because this question could lead to establishing and evaluating different perspectives".

Use these tests to judge a question.

| Test | Ask | Problem if the answer is no |
|---|---|---|
| Form | Is it a question? | a topic is not enough |
| Open | Could reasonable people give different answers? | a loaded question prevents debate |
| Debate | Will it produce competing perspectives with their own evidence? | the report becomes a description |
| Evidence | Can I find enough evidence on each side? | the argument will be thin |
| Scope | Can I answer it in the word limit? | too large or too narrow |
| Audience | Can I explain it to a non-specialist? | the language will be too technical |

### Examples (invented)

| Weak | Problem | Improved |
|---|---|---|
| Social media | topic only | Does social media improve or harm young people's wellbeing? |
| Why is fast fashion harmful? | loaded: assumes the answer | Is fast fashion an acceptable way to meet demand for clothing? |
| How many people use electric cars in Norway? | purely factual | Should governments subsidise electric cars? |
| Is plastic bad? | vague | Should single-use plastic packaging be banned in supermarkets? |

## Step 3: identify the key concepts

The syllabus defines key concepts as "the terms or ideas which are relevant to the topic they have chosen". Its examples: "for a project on the representation of women in poetry, key concepts might be gender and feminism", and for "different explanations for the movement of fluids in science, key concepts might be the conservation of energy and Brownian motion".

To find yours, ask: which words in my question need to be explained before I can answer it? For the question "Should governments subsidise electric cars?" the key concepts might be **subsidy**, **externality**, **sustainability** and **equity**. For each concept you should be able to:

- define it in your own words;
- show how different perspectives use it differently;
- explain why it matters to answering the question.

## Step 4: outline the debate

The proposal should say, in a few lines, what the main positions are and how you expect to argue. For example:

- **Perspective A** argues that subsidies speed up adoption and cut emissions.
- **Perspective B** argues that subsidies mainly help wealthier households and that public transport is a better use of the money.
- **Your initial line:** subsidies can be justified only if they are targeted.

Say that this line is **initial**. Your view may change during the research, and the research log is the place to record this.

## Step 5: choose example sources

List the kinds of source you expect to use, with a few specific examples: government reports, academic journals, statistical databases, news analysis, interviews. The syllabus asks for "examples of sources which will be used", so show the teacher that real material exists on each perspective.

## A model proposal (fictional)

| Section | Entry |
|---|---|
| Research question | Should governments subsidise electric cars? |
| Key concepts | subsidy; externality; sustainability; equity |
| Outline of the debate | Supporters argue that subsidies speed up adoption and reduce emissions. Critics argue that subsidies mainly help wealthier buyers and that public transport offers a fairer use of funds. I will compare the evidence and assumptions of each and reach a supported judgement. |
| Example sources | national transport statistics; two academic studies on adoption rates; a government policy report; reports from environmental and consumer organisations |

## How the teacher helps, and what they must not do

The teacher "ensures an understanding of the task, monitors progress, and responds to requests for advice on research and writing in general, but not to guide specific content". The teacher should help you to identify an appropriate topic and develop a suitable question, to consider alternative perspectives, to consider whether enough evidence exists, to select appropriate methods and to consider research ethics. Teachers and consultants must **not** teach specific topics or offer their own information and views, undertake any research for you, correct any part of your notes or drafts, or prepare any part of your report.

## Explaining the question in your report

The syllabus says: "The report should explain how and why the question was developed. For example, why is the question worth asking, and how did it develop during the research process?" In practice, write a short section near the start that covers:

1. why the question matters;
2. how you moved from a broad topic to this question;
3. any changes you made and why.

The research log gives you the evidence for item 3.

## Common mistakes

- **Choosing a topic for its sound**, not for the debate it contains.
- **A loaded question**, which assumes an answer.
- **A question that is only factual**, so there are no perspectives to compare.
- **Too many key concepts**, which spreads the report thin.
- **Listing sources without perspectives**, so the proposal shows no debate.
- **Starting work before the teacher has approved the proposal.**
- **Treating the proposal as fixed.** It may develop, and the report should explain how.

## Checklist before you submit the proposal

- The title is a question and it is open.
- Two or more perspectives can be named.
- Three or four key concepts are identified.
- At least one source for each perspective is named.
- Your initial line of argument is stated and marked as provisional.
- Your teacher has approved it, and any safety or ethical concerns are addressed.
