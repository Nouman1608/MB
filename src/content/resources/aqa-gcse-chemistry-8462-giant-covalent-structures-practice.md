These questions cover section **4.2.2.6 Giant covalent structures** of the AQA GCSE Chemistry (8462)
specification, for teaching from September 2016 onwards (version 1.2). The content is examined on Paper 1 at
Foundation and Higher Tier, and no question here tests Higher-tier-only content. Diagrams are described in
words.

Learn the content first in the [giant covalent structures study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-chemistry-8462-giant-covalent-structures/)
and the [revision notes](/resources/aqa-gcse-chemistry-8462-giant-covalent-structures-revision-notes/).

## Section A

**1.** **(a)** Name **two** substances that have giant covalent structures. **[2]**
**(b)** State the state of a giant covalent structure at room temperature. **[1]**

**2.** Silicon dioxide has a very high melting point.

**(a)** Name the type of bond that is broken when silicon dioxide melts. **[1]**
**(b)** State whether silicon dioxide conducts electricity when solid. **[1]**

**3.** In silicon dioxide, each silicon atom is bonded to four oxygen atoms and each oxygen atom is bonded to two
silicon atoms.

**(a)** Show that the ratio of silicon atoms to oxygen atoms is 1 : 2. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why SiO₂ is not the formula of a molecule. **[1]**

**4.** Four diagrams are described below.

- Diagram A: an atom of oxygen bonded to two atoms of hydrogen.
- Diagram B: a network of atoms, each joined to four others, continuing in all directions.
- Diagram C: a regular pattern of alternating positive and negative ions.
- Diagram D: a long chain section in brackets with bonds through the brackets and *n*.

**(a)** Which diagram shows a giant covalent structure? **[1]**
**(b)** Which diagram shows an ionic compound? **[1]**
**(c)** Name the type of substance shown in diagram D. **[1]**

## Section B

**5.** Explain why silicon dioxide has a very high melting point. **[3]**

**6.** Carbon dioxide and silicon dioxide are both oxides of Group 4 elements.

**(a)** Describe the structure of carbon dioxide. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain why carbon dioxide is a gas at room temperature while silicon dioxide is a solid. **[4]**

**7.** The table shows data for four substances.

| Substance | Melting point / °C | Conducts when solid? | Conducts when molten? |
|---|---|---|---|
| S | 1710 | No | No |
| T | 770 | No | Yes |
| U | -114 | No | No |
| V | 1535 | Yes | Yes |

**(a)** Which substance is a metal? Give a reason. **[2]**
**(b)** Which substance is a giant covalent structure? Give **two** reasons. **[3]**
**(c)** Which substance is ionic? Give **two** reasons. **[3]**

**8.** A student draws part of the silicon dioxide structure in two dimensions.

**(a)** State **two** limitations of a two-dimensional diagram of this structure. **[2]**
**(b)** Give **one** reason why the diagram does not show a molecule of silicon dioxide. **[1]**

**9.** Sodium chloride melts at 801 °C and silicon dioxide melts at 1710 °C. Both are solids at room temperature.

**(a)** State the type of bonding in each. **[2]**
**(b)** Suggest why silicon dioxide has the higher melting point, in terms of bonds. **[2]**
**(c)** Describe one way to show experimentally that sodium chloride is different from silicon dioxide. **[2]**

**10.** Explain, in terms of structure and bonding, the difference between a polymer and a giant covalent
structure. You should refer to melting. **[5]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** Any two [1] [1]: **diamond**, **graphite**, **silicon dioxide**. **[2]**
**(b)** **Solid** [1]

**2. (a)** **Covalent** bonds [1]
**(b)** **No** [1]

**3. (a)** Each oxygen atom is shared between two silicon atoms, so each silicon has 4 ÷ 2 = **2** oxygen atoms
[1]; so the ratio Si : O is 1 : 2 [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** It is a **giant structure** with no separate molecules, so the formula shows the ratio of atoms only [1]

**4. (a)** **Diagram B** [1]
**(b)** **Diagram C** [1]
**(c)** A **polymer** [1]

**5.** Silicon dioxide has a giant covalent structure [1]; all the atoms are linked by strong covalent bonds
and these must be broken to melt it [1]; there are many of these bonds, so a very large amount of energy is
needed [1]. **[3]**

**6. (a)** **Small molecules** [1]
**(b)** Marking points, up to 4:
- Carbon dioxide has weak intermolecular forces between molecules [1].
- Little energy is needed to overcome them, so it is a gas [1].
- Silicon dioxide has a giant covalent structure with strong covalent bonds in all directions [1].
- A very large amount of energy is needed to break the covalent bonds, so it is a solid with a very high melting
  point [1]. **[4]**

**7. (a)** **V** [1]; it conducts when solid and when molten [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** **S** [1]; very high melting point [1]; does not conduct when solid or molten [1]. **[3]**
**(c)** **T** [1]; high melting point [1]; conducts only when molten [1]. **[3]**

**8. (a)** Any two [1] [1]: it cannot show the **3D arrangement**; it shows only a **small part** of the structure;
it does not show the electrons in the bonds; atom sizes are not to scale. **[2]**
**(b)** The structure is a continuous **network**, not separate molecules [1].

**9. (a)** Sodium chloride: **ionic**; silicon dioxide: **covalent** [1] [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** In silicon dioxide the bonds that must be broken are **covalent bonds**, which are strong and there are
many [1]; in sodium chloride they are electrostatic attractions between ions [1], so more energy is needed for
silicon dioxide. **[2]**
**(c)** Test whether each conducts when molten or dissolved in water [1]; sodium chloride conducts but silicon
dioxide does not [1]. **[2]**

**10.** Marking points, up to 5:
- A polymer is made of separate very large molecules [1].
- Atoms inside the molecules are joined by strong covalent bonds [1].
- Between polymer molecules there are relatively strong intermolecular forces [1], which are overcome when it
  melts or softens [1].
- A giant covalent structure is one continuous network of atoms joined by strong covalent bonds [1], and these
  must be broken to melt it, so its melting point is much higher [1]. **[5]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Referring to intermolecular forces in silicon dioxide.
- Not saying that covalent bonds must be broken to melt a giant covalent structure.
- Treating SiO₂ as a molecule.
- Using only one piece of evidence when two are required.
- Saying silicon dioxide has "weak bonds" because it is brittle or a glass.
