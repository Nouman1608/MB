Condensed notes on the Services content (4.15 on this site) in the area of study Productive quality (fit for purpose), OCR A Level Business H431, Version 2.1 (January 2026), final assessment summer 2027. This is the Services heading under Productive quality, not the one under The production process. Full explanations and two worked examples are in the [services in productive quality study guide](/resources/ocr-a-level-business-h431-services-productive-quality-ocr-alevel-business/). Test yourself with the [services in productive quality practice questions](/resources/ocr-a-level-business-h431-services-productive-quality-ocr-alevel-business-practice/). All figures are invented.

## What the specification lists

- What is meant by 'services'; the process by which services are provided.
- Importance of the provision of services to the UK economy.
- Ways in which the quality of service provision can be monitored or measured.
- Impact of poor customer service on a business and its stakeholders.
- Ways in which the quality of customer service can be improved.
- Importance of customer service to a business and its stakeholders.

## Core recall

### Features of a service

| Feature | Meaning | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Intangible | Cannot be touched or inspected beforehand | Customers rely on reputation and reviews |
| Inseparable | Produced and consumed together | Staff behaviour is part of the product |
| Variable | Quality depends on who delivers and when | Hard to keep consistent |
| Perishable | Cannot be stored | Unused capacity is lost revenue |

### The service process

1. Booking or enquiry.
2. Arrival and diagnosis or need.
3. Delivery (front-of-house contact plus back-office work).
4. Handover and payment.
5. Follow-up and feedback.

- Inputs: staff, premises, equipment, information. Output: the result and the experience.

### Services and the UK economy

- The largest part of UK output and jobs, around four-fifths in recent years.
- Employment across many skill levels.
- Tax revenue.
- Exports, such as finance, insurance and education.
- Support for other sectors (transport, finance, advertising).
- Risks: reliance on a few sectors; sensitive to consumer spending; some low-paid or insecure jobs.

### Measuring and monitoring service quality

| Method | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|
| Customer surveys | Comparable over time | Low response; mood effects |
| Complaints and compliments | Show specific failures | Many unhappy customers never complain |
| Mystery shoppers | Detailed, objective | Few visits; costly |
| Repeat custom and retention | Shows loyalty | Slow to show problems |
| Online reviews | Quick; public | Unrepresentative; bias |
| Operational measures (waiting time, answer speed) | Precise | Show speed, not feelings |
| Staff feedback | Early warning | May be biased |

Use rates (complaints per 1,000 customers), not raw numbers, when volumes change.

### Impact of poor customer service

- Business: lost customers, damaged reputation, cost of complaints, low morale, lost market share.
- Owners: lower profit. Employees: stress and risk to jobs. Customers: wasted time and money. Suppliers: fewer orders. Community: lost jobs if the business declines.

### Improving customer service

- Training.
- Empowerment of front-line staff.
- Better systems (online booking, tracking, customer records).
- Recruiting for attitude; rewards for good service.
- Acting on feedback.
- Clear targets (for example answer calls in 20 seconds).
- Enough staff at busy times.

Costs and risks: training time and staff turnover; empowerment may cause inconsistency; systems can fail.

## Worked reminder

Harbour Hotel (invented).

- Complaints per 1,000 guests: 96 ÷ 4,800 × 1,000 = **20**; 81 ÷ 5,400 × 1,000 = **15**. A 25% fall in the rate.
- Check-in wait 11 minutes against a target of 8: 3 minutes, or **37.5%** above target.
- 3% of 4,800 guests lost = 144; revenue lost 144 × £220 = **£31,680**; profit lost at 35% = **£11,088**.
- Improvement costing £15,000 keeps 96 guests: 96 × £220 × 35% = **£7,392** a year; payback **2.0 years**.

## When customer service matters most

- Many close rivals offering similar products.
- Customers who pay for personal attention (hotels, banking, health care).
- Repeat custom is the main source of profit.
- Reputation spreads quickly online.
- It matters less where price is the main factor, as in a discount store.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Service | An activity done for a customer where the benefit is the activity |
| Intangible | Cannot be touched |
| Perishable | Cannot be stored |
| Customer service | Help and support given to customers before, during and after a sale |
| Mystery shopper | A trained person who poses as a customer and scores the service |
| Empowerment | Giving staff the authority to solve problems themselves |
| Retention | The share of customers who stay with a business |

## Exam traps

- Do not treat a service like a stored product; use its four features.
- Use a rate of complaints when customer numbers change.
- A low number of complaints is not proof of satisfaction.
- Evaluate each method of measurement, not just list it.
- Include the cost of improving service and compare it with the cost of lost customers.
- Mention employees as stakeholders of poor service.

## Self-test

1. Explain what is meant by saying a service is perishable.
2. Give two other features that make service quality hard to control.
3. Name two reasons why services matter to the UK economy.
4. A shop has 60 complaints from 2,000 customers. Calculate complaints per 1,000 customers.
5. Give one strength and one weakness of mystery shoppers.
6. Why can online reviews be misleading?
7. A business loses 5% of 3,000 customers, each worth £40 profit. Calculate the profit lost.
8. Give two ways to improve customer service.
9. Give one cost of empowering front-line staff.
10. Give two stakeholders affected by poor customer service, and how.

### Answers

1. It cannot be stored for later, so unused capacity, such as an empty seat, is lost income.
2. Any two from: intangible; inseparable; variable.
3. Any two: employment; output and tax revenue; exports; support for other sectors.
4. 60 ÷ 2,000 × 1,000 = **30 per 1,000**.
5. Strength: detailed, objective evidence. Weakness: only a few visits, and costly.
6. They come from a self-selected group, often people who are very happy or very unhappy, and may be biased or false.
7. 5% × 3,000 = 150 customers; 150 × £40 = **£6,000**.
8. Any two: training; empowerment; better systems; acting on feedback; clear targets.
9. Decisions may be inconsistent and refunds or discounts may cost more.
10. Employees (stress, risk to jobs); owners (lower profit); also customers or suppliers.
