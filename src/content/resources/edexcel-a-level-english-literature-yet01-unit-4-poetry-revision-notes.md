## Metaphysical Poets: recall

| Poem | Poet | Remember |
|---|---|---|
| "The Flea" | John Donne | A seduction argument built on a conceit; the flea as marriage bed and temple |
| "A Valediction of Weeping" | John Donne | Parting lovers; tears as coins and worlds in an extended conceit |
| Holy Sonnets | John Donne | Religious sonnets that argue with God and with death |
| "To His Coy Mistress" | Andrew Marvell | Three-part argument ("Had we...", "But...", "Now therefore..."); carpe diem |
| "The Definition of Love" | Andrew Marvell | Love defined by impossibility; geometric conceits |
| "A Letter to her Husband, Absent upon Public Employment" | Anne Bradstreet | Married love expressed through the sun and seasons |

## Romantics: recall

| Poem | Poet | Remember |
|---|---|---|
| "London" | William Blake | The walking witness; "charter'd"; "mind-forg'd manacles" |
| "Lines Written in Early Spring" | William Wordsworth | Joy in nature interrupted by grief at "what man has made of man" |
| "Tintern Abbey" | William Wordsworth | Blank-verse meditation; memory, nature and the moral self |
| "Ode to a Nightingale" | John Keats | Desire to escape into the bird's song; return to the "sole self" |

## Victorians: recall

| Poem | Poet | Remember |
|---|---|---|
| *In Memoriam* VII ("Dark house, by which once more I stand") | Alfred Tennyson | Grief at the friend's house; bleak dawn |
| "My Last Duchess" | Robert Browning | Dramatic monologue; the Duke reveals more than he intends |
| "Remember" | Christina Rossetti | Sonnet; the turn from "remember me" to "forget and smile" |
| "Echo" | Christina Rossetti | Longing for a lost love in dreams |
| "The Darkling Thrush" | Thomas Hardy | Bleak landscape at the century's end; the thrush's unexplained hope |
| "Drummer Hodge" | Thomas Hardy | A young soldier buried far from home in the Boer War |

These are examples, not complete lists; revise every prescribed poem for your movement.

## Forms

| Form | Feature | Typical effect |
|---|---|---|
| Sonnet | Fourteen lines and a turn | Argument, then a shift |
| Ode | Elevated address to a subject | Praise, meditation, longing |
| Dramatic monologue | One speaker, an implied listener | Self-revelation; irony |
| Elegy | Lament | Grief moving towards or away from consolation |
| Conceit | Extended, surprising comparison | Wit and argument |
| Blank verse | Unrhymed iambic pentameter | Meditative, conversational seriousness |

## Critical views to test

- Samuel Johnson on the Metaphysicals: "heterogeneous ideas... yoked by violence together".
- T. S. Eliot (1921): the Metaphysicals fused thought and feeling before a "dissociation of sensibility".
- Wordsworth's Preface to *Lyrical Ballads*: poetry as "the spontaneous overflow of powerful feelings".
- Romanticism as retreat into self, or as political engagement.
- Victorian poetry as consolation, or as the poetry of doubt.

## Paragraph frame for a movement essay

1. Topic sentence that tests the statement.
2. Main poem: short quotation, method, effect.
3. Supporting or contrasting reference to a second prescribed poem.
4. Context (often the movement itself) explaining the choice.
5. Interpretation weighed: does a critical view or alternative reading hold?
6. Link back to the statement.

**Example topic sentence:** *"Keats's ode seems to confirm that Romantic poets turn inward, yet its
return to the 'sole self' is presented as loss, not triumph, which complicates the statement."*

## Statement method

1. Unpack key terms.
2. Agree, disagree or qualify.
3. Choose three or four poems: some supporting, some complicating.
4. Analyse form and language in depth for each.
5. Integrate literary and historical context and critical views.
6. Judge.

## Traps

- One poem only.
- Too many poems, none analysed closely.
- Forcing every poem to fit the movement label.
- Context as biography.
- Critics listed but not evaluated.
- Writing about non-prescribed poems.
- Paraphrase in place of analysis of older language.

## Self-test

1. Which four AOs are assessed in Section B?
2. Name the three movements.
3. Which editor's collection is prescribed for the Romantics?
4. What is a conceit? Give a Metaphysical example.
5. What is the structure of the argument in "To His Coy Mistress"?
6. What does "charter'd" suggest in Blake's "London"?
7. What is a dramatic monologue? Name a prescribed example.
8. What did Johnson say about the Metaphysical poets?
9. What did Wordsworth call poetry in the Preface to *Lyrical Ballads*?
10. How many poems should you aim to discuss?
11. Which type of context is especially central in Section B?
12. Is comparison with Shakespeare or Unit 3 texts rewarded?

**Answers:** 1. AO1, AO2, AO3, AO5. 2. Metaphysical Poets, The Victorians, The Romantics. 3. David Wright
(*English Romantic Verse*, Penguin Classics). 4. An extended, surprising comparison; for example the
flea as marriage bed and temple in Donne's "The Flea". 5. Three parts: "Had we...", "But...", "Now
therefore...". 6. Legally owned and controlled, implying restriction. 7. A poem spoken by one character
to an implied listener; "My Last Duchess". 8. That their "heterogeneous ideas are yoked by violence
together". 9. "The spontaneous overflow of powerful feelings". 10. Three or four, analysed in depth,
with brief references to others. 11. Literary context: the movement itself. 12. No; AO4 is not
assessed.
