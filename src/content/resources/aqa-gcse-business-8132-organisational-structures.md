## What the specification asks for

The content headings are "Organisational structures", "Appropriateness of organisational structures" and "Centralisation and decentralisation". Students should be able to understand:

- "internal organisational structures, span of control, chain of command, delayering and delegation"
- "why businesses have internal organisational structures, including an understanding of different job roles and responsibilities throughout the business"
- "the impact that having a tall or flat organisational structure has on how a business is managed"
- "how organisational structure may affect the different ways of communication"

## Why businesses have a structure

An organisational structure sets out how jobs are arranged: who does what, who has authority over whom, and how information moves. It is often shown as an organisation chart, with the most senior roles at the top and lines joining each manager to the people who report to them.

A clear structure means:

- everyone knows their role and responsibilities, so tasks are not missed or duplicated;
- staff know who to report to and who to ask for decisions;
- managers know who they are responsible for;
- the business can see where new roles are needed as it grows;
- staff can see possible routes to promotion.

## Job roles and responsibilities

| Role | Main responsibilities |
|---|---|
| Directors | Set the overall aims and strategy of the business; answerable to the owners |
| Senior managers | Turn strategy into plans for a function, such as marketing or operations; manage budgets |
| Middle managers and supervisors | Organise day-to-day work in a department or team; check that targets are met |
| Team leaders | Lead a small group of workers, often while doing the same work |
| Operational staff | Carry out the day-to-day tasks: making products, serving customers, processing orders |
| Support staff | Provide services that keep the business running, such as IT, cleaning or administration |

Authority and responsibility increase towards the top. Decisions at the top affect the whole business and the long term; decisions lower down affect daily tasks.

## The key terms

**Chain of command:** the line of authority from the top of the business to the bottom. Instructions pass down it and information passes up it. The longer the chain, the more people a message passes through.

**Span of control:** the number of employees who report directly to one manager. A narrow span means a manager supervises a few people closely; a wide span means a manager supervises many people, each with more independence.

**Delegation:** passing authority to carry out a task, and to make the decisions involved, to someone lower in the structure. The manager remains accountable for the outcome. Delegation frees managers to concentrate on bigger decisions and gives staff responsibility, which can motivate them, but only works if the staff have the skills and the manager trusts them.

**Delayering:** removing one or more levels of management from the structure. It shortens the chain of command and widens spans of control. It saves salary costs and speeds up communication, but remaining managers have more people to manage, and staff lose possible promotion routes.

## Tall and flat structures

A **tall** structure has many levels of hierarchy, a long chain of command and narrow spans of control. A **flat** structure has few levels, a short chain of command and wide spans of control.

### Impact on how the business is managed

| | Tall | Flat |
|---|---|---|
| Supervision | Close; managers can check work and support each person | Lighter; staff work more independently |
| Decision making | Slower, as decisions may need approval at several levels | Quicker, with fewer levels to pass through |
| Management costs | Higher, with more managers to pay | Lower |
| Promotion | Many steps, giving staff something to aim for | Fewer steps, so less chance of promotion |
| Staff responsibility | Less, as decisions are made higher up | More, which can motivate skilled staff |
| Manager workload | Manageable, with few direct reports | Heavy, with many direct reports |

## How structure affects communication

Communication in a business can be:

- **vertical:** up and down the chain of command, for example a manager giving instructions or a worker reporting a problem;
- **horizontal:** between people at the same level, such as two department managers agreeing a plan;
- **formal:** through official channels, such as meetings, reports and emails;
- **informal:** through unofficial contact, such as conversations in the staff room.

Structure shapes each of these:

- In a **tall** structure, vertical messages pass through many levels. Each step adds delay, and the message can be changed or misunderstood as it is passed on. Senior managers may become out of touch with what customers and workers are saying.
- In a **flat** structure, messages reach their destination through fewer people, so communication is faster and more accurate. However, a manager with a wide span has less time to talk with each employee individually.
- Horizontal communication can be harder in a large structure with separate departments, because each department works through its own chain of command.

## Centralisation and decentralisation

**Centralised:** important decisions are made by senior managers, often at head office.

- Benefits: consistent decisions across the business; senior managers see the whole picture; tighter control of costs and brand.
- Drawbacks: slower decisions; less understanding of local conditions; staff lower down may feel less trusted.

**Decentralised:** authority to make decisions is passed down to managers in branches, regions or departments.

- Benefits: quicker decisions; local managers understand their customers; staff are motivated by responsibility.
- Drawbacks: decisions may be inconsistent between branches; harder for head office to control; local managers need training.

## Worked example: changing a structure

Figures and business invented. Merrow Garden Centres has 8 centres. Each centre has a centre manager, 2 supervisors and 20 staff (10 per supervisor). The 8 centre managers report to 2 regional managers, who report to the managing director.

Levels: managing director → regional managers → centre managers → supervisors → staff = 5 levels.

Spans of control: managing director 2; each regional manager 4; each centre manager 2; each supervisor 10.

The managing director removes the regional manager level. Now:

- Levels fall from 5 to 4.
- The managing director's span rises from 2 to 8.
- Two regional managers' salaries are saved. If each costs £48,000 a year, the saving is £96,000.

At the same time, the business decentralises: centre managers can now set local promotions and order plants suited to their area.

Analysis: decisions about local stock are made quicker by people who know their customers; communication from the managing director reaches centres through one fewer level; and the salary saving is large. Risks: the managing director now has 8 direct reports and less time for each; centres may run inconsistent promotions that confuse customers; and supervisors lose a possible promotion route. A good judgement might say the change suits Merrow if centre managers are experienced, but it should keep central control of pricing and the brand so customers get the same experience in every centre.

## Appropriateness

No single structure is best. Consider:

- **Size:** a small business can be flat; a large one usually needs more levels.
- **Type of work:** routine work suits wider spans; complex or risky work may need closer supervision.
- **Skills of staff:** skilled, experienced staff can work with wide spans and delegated decisions.
- **Speed of change in the market:** fast-changing markets favour flat, decentralised structures that decide quickly.
- **Need for consistency:** a brand that must be identical everywhere may keep key decisions centralised.

## Exam technique

- **Define or state:** give a precise meaning, such as span of control as direct reports to one manager.
- **Calculate:** count levels and spans from a description, showing your working.
- **Explain:** structure feature → effect on management or communication → effect on the business.
- **Analyse and evaluate:** weigh benefits and drawbacks for the business's size, staff and market, then judge whether the structure or change is appropriate.

## Common mistakes

- Confusing span of control with the number of levels.
- Saying delayering means making workers redundant. It removes management levels.
- Saying flat structures are always better.
- Mixing up delegation (passing tasks and authority to individuals) with decentralisation (passing decision-making power through the structure).
- Forgetting that the manager who delegates remains accountable.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the difference between span of control and chain of command?**
Span of control is the number of people reporting directly to one manager. The chain of command is the line of authority from the top of the business to the bottom.

**What is delayering?**
Removing one or more levels of management. It shortens the chain of command and widens spans of control.

**Is a flat structure better than a tall one?**
Not always. Flat structures are cheaper and faster, but managers with wide spans have less time for each employee, and there are fewer promotion opportunities.
