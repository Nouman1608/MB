This guide covers globalisation within the HL extension **Culture** of IB DP Psychology (first assessment 2027). The 2027 guide notes that cultural diffusion has raised questions about the effect of globalization on mental and physical health, identity and the nature of relationships. The HL extensions are assessed only at HL, on Paper 3. The 2019 course had "the influence of globalization on individual attitudes, identities and behaviour" as its HL extension, and this guide was written around that wording. The [sociocultural approach study guide](/resources/ib-dp-psychology-sociocultural-approach-study-guide/) covers enculturation, acculturation and cultural dimensions, which this extension builds on. Here you will find what globalisation means for psychology, three lines of research you can use, and how to evaluate them. The 2019 course had its last examination session in November 2026.

## What globalisation means in psychology

**Globalisation** is the process by which societies become more interconnected through trade, migration, travel, mass media and digital communication. Economists and sociologists study its effect on economies and institutions. The IB extension asks a narrower, psychological question: how does contact with a global culture change what individuals believe (attitudes), who they think they are (identities) and what they do (behaviour)?

Two ideas from the core help here:

- **Acculturation** usually describes change after migration. Globalisation means people can experience contact with another culture without moving at all, because global media, brands and online networks reach them at home.
- **Social identity** suggests that people can belong to several groups at once. Globalisation adds a new, very large potential in-group: a global or cosmopolitan identity.

## Identity: Arnett (2002)

Jeffrey Arnett's article "The psychology of globalization" (2002) is a theoretical review rather than a single study. He proposed several consequences for identity, especially among adolescents and young adults:

- **Bicultural identity.** Many young people develop two identities: one rooted in their local culture and family, and one linked to a global culture, spread through media, music, technology and the English language. They may switch between them depending on the setting.
- **Identity confusion.** For some young people, especially where the local and global cultures are very different, neither identity feels secure. Arnett linked this to problems such as a sense of alienation.
- **Self-selected cultures.** Some people react to globalisation by joining groups that share values they choose, which may be traditional or religious as well as global.
- **A longer emerging adulthood.** As education lengthens and life choices widen, the period of exploring identity extends into the twenties in more societies.

**Evaluation.** Arnett's ideas are useful for organising research, but they are descriptive and broad, and much of the evidence he drew on came from different kinds of sources. A theory that predicts integration, confusion or a return to tradition can be hard to test, because almost any outcome can be fitted to it. Use it to frame an answer and support it with studies.

## Globalisation-based acculturation

Chen, Benet-Martínez and Bond (2008) extended acculturation research to people who stay at home but are exposed to a global culture, calling this **globalization-based acculturation**. In research in Hong Kong, they found that **bicultural identity integration**, the sense that one's two cultural identities fit together rather than conflict, was associated with better psychological adjustment. This links Arnett's bicultural identity to Berry's acculturation model: integration of two identities seems to be associated with well-being whether the contact comes from migration or from globalisation. The research is correlational, so it cannot show that integration causes better adjustment; well-adjusted people may also find it easier to integrate their identities.

## Behaviour and attitudes to the body: Becker et al. (2002)

Becker and colleagues used a natural experiment in Fiji. Television, carrying mainly programmes from the USA, Australia and the UK, became widely available in one province in 1995. Traditional Fijian culture had valued a robust body size and discouraged dieting.

**Method.** Two samples of adolescent girls were surveyed: one shortly after television arrived and one three years later. Both completed a standard questionnaire on eating attitudes. In the later sample, some girls were also interviewed about television.

**Findings.** The proportion of girls with scores indicating disordered eating attitudes rose markedly between the two samples, and self-induced vomiting to control weight was reported in the later sample but not the earlier one. In interviews, girls described admiring television characters and wanting to look like them, linking slimness to success and career opportunities.

**Evaluation.** A natural experiment allowed the researchers to study a real cultural change that could never be manipulated. The interviews add credibility, because girls themselves linked their attitudes to television. However, the two samples were different girls, other changes in Fiji (economic development, schooling, other contact with Western culture) happened at the same time, and the measures were self-report. The questionnaire was developed in Western countries, which raises the question of whether it measures the same thing in Fiji: an etic measure applied to a new culture.

## Behaviour: global identity and cooperation

Buchan and colleagues (2009) studied participants in six countries using a public goods game. Each person could keep money, contribute to a local group or contribute to a global group whose members came from several countries. Participants whose lives were more exposed to globalisation, and those living in more globalised countries, tended to contribute more to the global group, and the researchers linked this to a stronger sense of identification with a global community.

This study is useful because it measures **behaviour** (real choices about money), not just attitudes, and because it used a standard procedure in very different societies. Limitations: an economic game is an artificial setting, and "exposure to globalisation" is a broad variable that may be connected to education or income.

## Evaluating research on globalisation

| Issue | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Defining the variable | "globalisation" includes media, migration, trade and technology; studies usually measure only one |
| Causation | cultures change for many reasons at once, so a single cause is hard to isolate |
| Natural experiments | high realism, but no random allocation and many confounding variables |
| Measurement | questionnaires designed in one culture may not be valid in another (etic versus emic) |
| Change over time | effects may differ by generation; cross-sectional data cannot show individual change |
| Researcher bias | a Western research team may interpret local change as "Westernisation" too readily |

## Worked example: planning a 15-mark discussion

Paper 3 Question 4 (15 marks) gives at least three sources and a stated claim, and asks you to discuss the validity of the claim using the sources and your own knowledge. The plan below is for a claim of that kind without the source booklet, so it shows the knowledge side of the answer.

**Claim:** "Exposure to global media changes eating behaviour." Discuss the validity of this claim.

1. **Focus:** define globalisation, choose eating behaviour and state the claim: exposure to global media can change attitudes to the body and eating.
2. **Knowledge:** Arnett's bicultural identity as the framework; how global media might act through social comparison and observational learning, linking back to social cognitive theory.
3. **Research:** Becker et al. (2002) as the main study; Buchan et al. (2009) to show that globalisation can also produce positive behaviour, such as cooperation beyond one's country.
4. **Discussion:** the natural experiment's realism against its confounding variables; self-report and an etic measure; the risk of assuming change is always negative.
5. **Conclusion:** evidence suggests that globalisation can influence behaviour, but the size and direction of the effect depend on the behaviour, the local culture and the individual's identity.

## Common mistakes

- Writing an economics answer about trade instead of a psychology answer about individuals.
- Treating globalisation as the same thing as acculturation after migration.
- Assuming every effect of globalisation is harmful.
- Describing Becker et al. (2002) without discussing confounding variables.
- Answering at HL with core material only, such as Berry's model, without the extension research.

## Exam technique

The HL extensions are assessed on Paper 3 (1 hour 45 minutes, 30 marks, HL only). It has four source-based questions on quantitative and qualitative findings that focus on one extension, here culture: one issue that limits the interpretation of a source (3 marks), analysis of the findings of one source with a conclusion (6 marks), a research consideration such as credibility, bias or transferability (6 marks), and a synthesis of at least three sources to discuss the validity of a claim (15 marks). Knowledge of the extension is assessed through its application to unseen material, so prepare at least two studies with a strength and a limitation for each, and practise reading graphs, tables and qualitative extracts. **Discuss** needs a balanced view, **evaluate** a judgement on the strength of the evidence and **to what extent** a conclusion about degree. Name the attitude, identity or behaviour in your first paragraph, and return to it in every paragraph.
