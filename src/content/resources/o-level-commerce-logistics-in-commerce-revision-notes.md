Logistics is the part of commerce that gets goods from producer to consumer: transporting them and
storing them in warehouses along the way. Cambridge O Level Commerce (7100, 2026-2028) Topic 4 has two
sections, 4.1 Transport and 4.2 Warehousing. Questions usually give a business situation and ask you to
recommend a transport method or warehouse, so revise by practising decisions, not just lists.

For full explanations use the [logistics study guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-commerce-logistics-in-commerce-study-guide/);
practise with the [logistics practice questions](/resources/o-level-commerce-logistics-in-commerce-practice/).

## How does transport add value?

Transport makes goods useful by delivering them at the right **time**, to the right **place**, in the right
**quantity**. A crate of mangoes on a farm is worth little to a city shopper until transport brings it to
the market before it ripens too far.

## Transport methods in one line each

- **Road** (vans, trucks, couriers) - the only method that reaches the customer's door; best for short,
  flexible journeys.
- **Rail** (freight trains) - heavy loads over long land distances; needs road at each end.
- **Air** (freight planes) - fastest for long distances; the most expensive per kilogram.
- **Sea** (ferry, tanker, container ship, cargo liner) - cheapest for large international loads; slowest.
- **Pipeline** - continuous movement of liquids and gases; very costly to build, cheap to run.

## How do you choose a transport method?

Ask six questions - they are the six factors in the syllabus.

| Question | Factor | Points towards |
|---|---|---|
| Is it perishable, fragile, bulky, liquid or valuable? | Nature of goods | Air for perishables; sea or rail for bulky; pipeline for liquids |
| How soon is it needed? | Urgency | Air or courier |
| Can the price of transport be justified by the value of goods? | Cost | Sea or rail for low-value goods |
| Is there a risk of theft or damage? | Security | Air, sealed containers |
| How far is it going? | Distance | Road for short; sea or air for international |
| Will the carrier deliver on time? | Reliability of transporter | A carrier with a good record |

**Decision practice**

| Situation | Best method | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Cement from a works to building sites across one city | Road (trucks) | Short distance, many destinations |
| Iron ore from a mine to a coastal steelworks 800 km away | Rail | Heavy, bulky, long overland journey |
| Spare part for a grounded aircraft on another continent | Air | Extremely urgent, small and valuable |
| 10 000 washing machines from Korea to Brazil | Sea, in containers | Large volume, international, not urgent |
| Natural gas from a field to a power station | Pipeline | Continuous flow of a gas |

## What is containerisation and transshipment?

Goods are packed into **standard-sized sealed containers** that are lifted between ships, trains and
trucks without unpacking. **Transshipment** is moving containers from one ship or vehicle to another at
a hub - for example from a giant ocean vessel to a smaller feeder ship serving a minor port.

- **For**: quick crane loading; less theft and damage; lower labour and insurance costs; easy multi-mode
  transport.
- **Against**: expensive containers, ships and port equipment; small ports may be excluded; empty
  containers must be returned.

## Why are warehouses needed?

- **Seasonal production** - storing a harvest (such as wheat) to sell through the year.
- **Demand** - building stock before peaks such as school terms or festivals.
- **Protecting supply** - buffer stock if a delivery is delayed.
- **Price stability** - steady release prevents sharp price swings.
- **Logistics postponement** - finishing, labelling or packing goods only when orders arrive.

| Warehouse | When to use it |
|---|---|
| Bonded | Imported goods awaiting payment of customs duty, or for re-export |
| Cold storage | Food, flowers, medicines needing low temperatures |
| Cash and carry | Small retailers buying in bulk and transporting goods themselves |
| Regional distribution centre (RDC) | A retail chain supplying all its shops in a region from one hub |

## How does automation help warehouses?

**Robotics** move and pick goods; **automated sorting** routes parcels by scanner; **voice picking** guides
workers by headset. Benefits: lower labour costs, less human error, better picking accuracy, better
inventory control and better customer service.

**Worked evaluation.** A medium-sized wholesaler considers robots. Benefits: fewer picking errors and
faster dispatch. Costs: high purchase price, maintenance and training. Judgement: worthwhile if order
volumes are high enough for savings in labour and errors to cover the investment.

## Common mistakes and exam traps

- Recommending a method without using the scenario's facts (distance, urgency, nature of goods).
- Saying pipelines can carry solid goods.
- Calling transshipment "moving goods into containers".
- Writing that a cash and carry delivers to the retailer - retailers collect the goods.
- Mentioning automation without a specific benefit from the syllabus list.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why is road transport used in almost every supply chain?** It is the only method that can deliver to the
customer's door, so goods arriving by rail, sea or air usually finish their journey by road.

**What is a regional distribution centre?** A large warehouse that receives goods from many suppliers and
sends combined deliveries to a retail chain's shops in one region.

**How many factors affect the choice of transport in 7100?** Six: nature of goods, urgency, cost, security,
distance and reliability of transporter.
