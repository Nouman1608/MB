## Section A

**1.** Explain how Unit 2's Section B differs from Unit 1's Section B. **[4]**

**2.** Write a one-sentence thesis in response to: *"Discuss the view that power in conversation always belongs to the person with the highest status."* **[2]**

**3.** Rewrite this sentence in an academic register: *"Labov proved that rich people in New York say their r's more, which is basically snobby."* **[4]**

**4.** Give **three** hedging expressions and explain why hedging matters in this essay. **[4]**

**5.** Explain the difference between using "found" and "claims" when reporting research. **[2]**

## Section B

The extract below (original, invented) is the kind of Section A data the essay must connect with. It is from a staff-room conversation at a school. HEAD is the head teacher; NQT is a newly qualified teacher; LIZ is a long-serving teacher.

```
HEAD:  so (.) the trip (.) are we still on for Thursday
NQT:   I think so (.) I've sent the forms out but um (.) I'm not sure
       everyone's replied yet
LIZ:   they never do (.) chase them on Wednesday (.) works every time
HEAD:  good shout Liz (.) yes (.) do that
NQT:   okay (.) thanks
```

**6.** Identify **two** features of the extract that could be used as evidence in an essay about power in conversation, and explain what each shows. **[6]**

**7.** Write a paragraph arguing that status in this extract comes from experience as well as from position. Use the data and **one** concept or study. **[8]**

**8.** Explain how the extract could be used to **complicate** the claim that the head teacher holds all the power. **[4]**

**9.** Write a plan for an essay responding to: *"Discuss the view that people mainly use language to fit in with the groups they belong to."* Include a thesis, three paragraph claims each with evidence, a counter-argument, and a conclusion. **[10]**

**10.** Evaluate how far Milroy's Belfast findings would support the view in Question 9. **[4]**

**11.** A student concludes an essay: *"In conclusion, language is used for lots of different things in groups, as shown above."* Explain **two** weaknesses and rewrite the conclusion. **[5]**

## Answers

**1.** Unit 1's Section B is **directed writing**: a new text written to a brief [1], **transforming** the Section A source material [1]. Unit 2's Section B is an **academic essay** [1] that **argues** about an aspect of language and social group behaviour, using the Section A data as evidence [1].

**2.** Credit a thesis that **answers the question directly** [1] and **takes a qualified position on "always"** [1], e.g. *"Status often brings conversational power, but the data show that expertise, humour and relationships allow lower-status speakers to exercise power too, so 'always' overstates the case."*

**3.** Example: *"Labov (1966) found that post-vocalic /r/ was more frequent among staff in higher-status New York department stores, suggesting that this pronunciation carried overt prestige."* Credit **"found" instead of "proved"** [1], **accurate description of the study** [1], **precise terminology** (post-vocalic /r/, overt prestige) [1], and **removal of informal, evaluative language** ("basically snobby") [1].

**4.** Any three: suggests, may indicate, appears to, to some extent, tends to [1] [1] [1]. Hedging matters because **data from one text cannot prove a general claim**, so claims must be stated with appropriate caution [1].

**5.** "Found" presents a result as **evidence from research** [1]; "claims" signals that a view is **contested or not established**, showing the writer's critical stance [1].

**6.** Any two, three marks each. **The head teacher's opening question** ("are we still on for Thursday") sets the topic, showing **topic control** linked to her role [1] [1] [1]. **The newly qualified teacher's hedges and filler** ("I think so", "um", "I'm not sure") show **deference and uncertainty**, attending to the head's negative face [1] [1] [1]. Alternatively, Liz's imperative "chase them on Wednesday", which gives advice without hedging.

**7.** Credit: **a topic sentence that makes the claim** [1]; **quoted data**, e.g. Liz's unhedged imperative "chase them on Wednesday" and the confident "works every time" [1] [1]; **analysis** that Liz speaks with authority despite not being the head, and the head **ratifies** it ("good shout Liz") [1] [1]; **a concept or study applied accurately**, e.g. Wareing's influential power, or communities of practice in which experienced members hold status [1]; **a hedged interpretation** [1]; **a link back to the essay's question** [1].

**8.** Although the head **opens the topic and gives the final instruction** ("do that") [1], the **solution comes from Liz**, who advises without being asked [1]. The head **adopts Liz's suggestion** rather than imposing her own [1], showing power shared between positional authority and experience [1].

**9.** **Thesis**: language is often used to fit in, but "mainly" overlooks its use to stand out, claim status and manage relationships [1] [1]. **Claim 1**: convergence and in-group lexis show speakers fitting in (Giles; Section A data) [1] [1]. **Claim 2**: speakers also use language to stand out or rank within groups (status, instrumental power; Section A data) [1] [1]. **Claim 3**: speakers move between groups and styles, so "the groups they belong to" is plural (accommodation; Eckert) [1] [1]. **Counter-argument**: covert prestige and network studies show strong pressure to conform, supporting the view [1]. **Conclusion**: a judgement that fitting in is a major but not the main function [1].

**10.** Milroy found that speakers in **dense, close-knit networks maintained local vernacular forms** [1], which **supports** the view: language use reflects pressure to conform to the group [1]. However, the study concerns **particular communities** and network strength varies [1], and it does not show that fitting in is the **main** function, since speakers in looser networks shift more [1].

**11.** Weaknesses: it **does not answer the question** or make a judgement [1]; it is **vague and informal** ("lots of different things", "as shown above") [1]. Rewrite: credit **a direct judgement on the question** [1], **reference to the strongest evidence** [1], and **precise academic register** [1]. Example: *"The data suggest that speakers frequently use language to fit in, as convergence and shared lexis show; however, the same speakers use it to claim status and negotiate roles, so the view holds only in part."*

## Where marks are usually lost

- Restating the Section A analysis without making an argument.
- Overclaiming from a short extract.
- Misreporting studies or using "proved".
- Ignoring the qualifying word in the question.
- Conclusions that summarise instead of judging.
