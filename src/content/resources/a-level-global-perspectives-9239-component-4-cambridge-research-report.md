This guide covers Component 4, the Cambridge Research Report, for Cambridge International AS & A Level Global Perspectives & Research (9239). It follows the Cambridge International AS & A Level Global Perspectives & Research (9239) syllabus for examination in 2026, 2027 and 2028, Version 3 (published December 2025), drawing on section 4 (Details of the assessment) and the assessment objectives in section 2. Component 4 is taken only on an A Level route. It is a research report of up to 5000 words plus a research log, externally marked by Cambridge International, worth 85 marks and 50% of the A Level.

Use it with the [9239 subject hub](/boards/cambridge/a-level/global-perspectives/), the [9239 checklist](/checklists/cambridge/a-level/global-perspectives/), the [revision notes for this component](/resources/a-level-global-perspectives-9239-component-4-cambridge-research-report-revision-notes/) and the [practice questions for this component](/resources/a-level-global-perspectives-9239-component-4-cambridge-research-report-practice/).

The syllabus weights Component 4 as AO1 65%, AO2 20% and AO3 15%. It lists eleven things you are assessed on, each tagged to an AO. The detailed level descriptors used by markers are not printed in the syllabus; your teacher will confirm how they are applied.

## Choosing and framing the research question

The syllabus asks for a topic "that engages them strongly and is of real importance to them". The report must be built around one question that allows in-depth research. The title of the report must take the form of a question. Your report should also explain how and why the question was developed: why it is worth asking, and how it changed during the research.

A good question leads straight into a debate between perspectives. The syllabus gives an example. "Industrialisation" is a topic, not a question. "Has industrialisation been environmentally damaging?" is a question, but it is one-sided. "Is industrialisation a good thing?" works, because it opens up competing views.

**Key terms**
- **Research question:** the single question your whole report answers.
- **Key concepts:** the terms or ideas relevant to your topic. The syllabus example pairs a project on women in poetry with gender and feminism.
- **Scope:** how wide the question is. The teacher must check it is "not too large, nor too complex".

**Evaluation.** A broad question gives plenty of sources but risks a shallow report. A very narrow one may not have enough evidence; your teacher should support you to consider "whether enough evidence exists". Your best question sits between the two, and you should say in the report why you narrowed it.

**Worked paragraph (from an introduction).** "My first idea was 'Is fast fashion bad?', but early reading in my log showed that 'bad' meant different things to economists and environmental groups. I narrowed the question to 'Should governments limit fast-fashion production?' This keeps the economic and environmental perspectives in direct competition, because each side answers a policy question rather than a moral one. It also gives a clear test for my conclusion: whether limits would do more good than harm."

That paragraph shows knowledge (the original question), application (the specific change), analysis (why the new wording creates a debate) and evaluation (how the conclusion will be judged). The fast-fashion paragraphs in this guide follow one invented candidate; the sources they describe are illustrative, not real publications.

## The research proposal

Before you begin research, you complete a research proposal form. The syllabus says it should outline:

- the research question
- the key concepts which are relevant to the question
- an outline of the debate to be addressed
- examples of sources which will be used.

You discuss the proposal with your teacher. The teacher confirms that the concepts and debate are relevant, that the question gives you scope to meet the assessment criteria, and that the research does not raise safety issues. The teacher formally approves it before you start. The form, with the teacher's comments, is sent to Cambridge with your report and log, but it is not externally marked.

**Evaluation.** The proposal is not marked, so it is tempting to rush it. That is a mistake. A weak proposal often leads to a weak question, and the question shapes every later mark.

## Perspectives, evidence and the literature review

The syllabus defines a **perspective** as "a coherent world view which is a response to an issue", made up of argument, evidence and assumptions, and possibly shaped by context. Assessment criteria ask you to "build and compare contrasting perspectives".

**Evidence** can be primary, secondary or both.
- **Primary evidence** is first-hand: historical documents, literary texts, artefacts, scientific observation, interviews and surveys.
- **Secondary evidence** is material such as the arguments and opinions of specialists, which reflect on or synthesise primary evidence.

The syllabus says you should "explore the strengths and potential weaknesses of all types of evidence".

**Literature review.** "Reviewing the secondary literature in the field" is the first research method the syllabus lists. In practice, you survey what specialists have argued, group their views into perspectives, and note where they disagree. A literature review is not a list of summaries. Each source should be placed in a perspective and assessed for credibility: who wrote it, where and when (its **provenance**), what evidence it uses, and what it assumes.

**Evaluation.** Secondary sources give you expert depth quickly, but they can share hidden assumptions, and a review built from one type of source (for example, only campaign groups) will be lopsided. Primary evidence lets you test a claim yourself, but a small, local sample rarely supports a general conclusion.

**Worked paragraph (main body).** "Industry reports argue that limiting production would cost jobs in garment-exporting countries. Their evidence is employment data, which is relevant, but the reports are written for trade bodies, so they have a reason to stress costs. Environmental groups argue the reverse, using waste-volume data. Both sides assume that production levels, not consumer behaviour, drive the problem. Neither perspective weighs this assumption, which suggests that a third view, focused on consumer demand, deserves attention."

## Research methods and methodology

The syllabus lists methods you might use:

- reviewing the secondary literature in the field
- close analysis of texts
- other qualitative methods (for example ethnography, interviews, focus groups)
- analysis and evaluation of statistical or other numerical data
- other quantitative methods (for example surveys, experiments)
- a mixture of the above.

**Methodology** is "the study of the reasons why particular sets of methods are appropriate for specific academic subject areas". You must explain your choice and use of methods, explain how you carried out the research, and consider the methodology behind them and its impact on your report.

**Key terms (general research vocabulary)**
- **Qualitative:** evidence in words or meanings; depth over number.
- **Quantitative:** evidence in numbers; patterns across many cases.
- **Sample:** the people or items you actually study.
- **Representative:** a sample that reflects the wider group you want to talk about.
- **Bias:** a systematic lean in who is asked, how, or how answers are read.
- **Research ethics:** the conventions you follow to protect participants, such as consent and confidentiality. The syllabus asks teachers to help you "consider research ethics and conventions".

**Evaluation of common methods**

| Method | Strength | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Literature review | Access to expert arguments and large data sets | Depends on what others chose to study |
| Interviews | Depth; you can follow up answers | Few people; answers shaped by the interviewer |
| Survey | Many responses; easy to compare | Shallow answers; sampling bias |
| Close analysis of texts | Detailed reading of language and meaning | Interpretations can be contested |

**Worked paragraph (methods section).** "I chose a literature review rather than a survey. A survey of students at my school would tell me what one age group in one city thinks, which says little about government policy. Policy questions are usually studied through economic and environmental data, so secondary analysis fits the discipline. The cost is that I depend on the data others collected, so I compare sources that measure the same thing in different ways."

## The research log

The log is worth 10 marks. You use it throughout to record information, plan actions, and reflect on findings and decisions. Decisions might include the wording of your question, your choice of methods, and judgements made as findings came in. It can hold reminders, ongoing evaluation, and notes on how teacher feedback changed your thinking.

Rules printed in the syllabus:
- The log must be a separate document from the report.
- It can be in note form or a mix of notes and continuous prose.
- It is not counted in the 5000 words, but it "should not be seen as a way to extend that word limit".
- You do not need to record every action.

**Evaluation.** A log full of dates and "found three articles" records but does not reflect. A strong entry says what you found, why it matters and what you will do next.

**Worked log entry.** "Week 6. Two interview answers contradict the survey data on cost. Possible reason: interviewees all run shops, survey was shoppers. Decision: keep both, treat as separate perspectives, flag the sample difference in methods section. Check with teacher whether question still works."

## Analysis, judgement and reflection

Several criteria sit here: analyse your research question, select and analyse relevant concepts, use and assess sources, make judgements supported by evidence, reflect on how alternative perspectives influenced your argument, and reflect on your conclusions.

A supported conclusion weighs the perspectives. It says which is stronger and why, using the quality of evidence and the strength of reasoning. It does not invent a balance that the evidence does not show.

**Reflection (AO2)** has two parts in the A Level objectives: acknowledging perspectives and their effect on your own standpoint, and reflecting on the "scope, nature and limitations" of your own report.

**Worked paragraph (conclusion).** "The evidence suggests limited, targeted production limits would reduce waste with smaller job losses than industry reports predict, because the employment data they cite covers all garment work, not only fast fashion. My judgement is cautious. I relied on secondary data from a few countries, and my early view that limits were obviously right changed once I read the employment evidence. Further research could compare countries that have tried such limits."

## Structure, referencing and the word limit

- The report must be in continuous prose and must not exceed 5000 words.
- An accurate word count must be clearly stated.
- The title and bibliographical references are not included in the word count. Work beyond 5000 words will not be marked.
- It should have an introduction, main body and conclusion, and may use sub-headings.
- It must include a bibliography, with full references for any quotations.
- Many reports use Harvard, Chicago or APA referencing. Whatever system you choose, use it consistently.
- Write for a non-specialist reader, and define terms.

You submit three items: the report, the research log, and the research proposal form. All work is submitted electronically. A Declaration of Authenticity is completed for each candidate, and Cambridge uses plagiarism detection software.

**The teacher's role.** Teachers help you choose a topic and scale, monitor your log, and teach research skills, referencing and academic honesty. They must not teach specific topics or give their own views on them, research for you, correct notes or drafts, or write any part of the report. During drafting they give only brief summative comments.

## Common errors

- A title that is a topic or statement, not a question.
- A question that already assumes its answer, so only one perspective is possible.
- Describing sources one by one instead of grouping them into perspectives.
- Naming methods without explaining why they suit the question, or ignoring methodology.
- A log that lists actions but never reflects or records decisions.
- Moving material into the log to get around the word limit.
- Mixing referencing styles, or quoting without a full reference.
- A conclusion that summarises both sides without a judgement.
- Specialist language left undefined for a non-specialist reader.
