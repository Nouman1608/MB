The specification lists: "Differences between types of AI"; "Algorithms: issues of power and inequality"; "The extent to which people and organisations are using AI and effects of this"; "Emerging technologies that merge the human and machine/technology"; and "Robots and cyborgs".

## Differences between types of AI: a fuller picture

**Origins.** Alan Turing's paper "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" (1950) proposed the "imitation game", now called the Turing test: a machine counts as intelligent if a human questioner cannot reliably tell it from a person. The term "artificial intelligence" was coined for a 1956 workshop at Dartmouth College organised by John McCarthy and colleagues.

**How systems work.**
- **Rule-based (symbolic) AI** follows rules written by people, as in the "expert systems" of the 1980s that encoded specialists' knowledge as if-then rules. It is transparent but brittle when situations do not fit the rules.
- **Machine learning** finds patterns in large sets of examples rather than following written rules. In **supervised learning**, the system is trained on examples labelled by humans (emails marked "spam" or "not spam").
- **Deep learning** uses many-layered neural networks and drives most recent advances in image recognition, translation and text generation. **Large language models**, the basis of chatbots released to the public from late 2022, are trained on vast amounts of text to predict likely next words.

**By capability.** Narrow AI (one task) and artificial general intelligence (human-level across tasks, which does not exist) are in the unit guide. Philosopher Nick Bostrom (*Superintelligence*, 2014) discussed a hypothetical **superintelligence** far beyond human ability and the risks if its goals differed from ours.

**Why the differences matter sociologically.** Rule-based systems carry the biases of the people who wrote the rules; machine-learning systems carry the biases in their **training data**. Both look neutral, which makes their decisions harder to challenge.

## Algorithms: power and inequality

Additional cases and concepts:

- **Criminal justice**: in 2016 the journalism organisation ProPublica analysed COMPAS, a tool used in some US courts to predict reoffending, and reported that Black defendants who did not reoffend were more likely than white defendants to have been labelled high risk. The company disputed the analysis, and the case started a wide debate about what "fair" means for an algorithm.
- **Recruitment**: in 2018 Reuters reported that Amazon had abandoned an experimental hiring tool after finding it downgraded CVs containing the word "women's", because it had been trained on past hiring dominated by men.
- **Education**: in England in 2020, when exams were cancelled during the pandemic, an algorithm used to standardise A-level grades was withdrawn after protests that it downgraded many students, with critics arguing it disadvantaged students at historically lower-performing schools.
- **Welfare**: in the Netherlands, thousands of families were wrongly accused of fraud over childcare benefits, with risk profiling that included nationality among its factors; the government resigned over the scandal in January 2021.

**Concepts.**
- **The New Jim Code**: Ruha Benjamin (*Race After Technology*, 2019) argued that new technologies can reproduce old racial hierarchies while appearing objective.
- **The black box**: Frank Pasquale (*The Black Box Society*, 2015) argued that the secrecy of corporate and state algorithms prevents people from knowing or contesting how decisions about them are made.
- **Feedback loops**: if predictive policing sends more officers to an area, more crime is recorded there, which "confirms" the prediction.

**Evaluation.** Supporters argue that algorithms can be audited and corrected more easily than human prejudice, and that human decision-makers are also biased. Critics reply that scale, opacity and the appearance of objectivity make algorithmic bias harder to see and resist.

## The extent of AI use and its effects

**Extent.** AI is used in search, translation, recommendations, fraud detection, medical imaging, customer service, logistics, policing and education. Use is uneven between countries, sectors and social groups.

**Work.** Carl Frey and Michael Osborne (2013) estimated that 47% of US jobs were at high risk of computerisation. Later studies that looked at the separate tasks within jobs, rather than whole occupations, produced much lower estimates, because most jobs mix tasks machines can and cannot do. Generative AI has since extended the debate to office, creative and professional work.

**Hidden labour.** Mary Gray and Siddharth Suri (*Ghost Work*, 2019) showed that many "automated" services rely on low-paid workers who label data and check outputs through online platforms. Journalists have reported on content moderators and data labellers in countries such as Kenya doing this work for AI companies.

**Material costs.** Kate Crawford (*Atlas of AI*, 2021) argued that AI is an extractive industry, depending on mined minerals, energy-hungry data centres, cheap labour and data taken from users.

**Evaluation.** Benefits include faster diagnosis, translation that widens access to information and help for disabled users. Effects depend on who owns the systems and who bears the costs, so a Marxist reading stresses profit and control, while a more optimistic reading stresses productivity and access.

## Merging human and machine

- **Medical technologies**: the first fully implanted pacemaker (Sweden, 1958); cochlear implants; deep brain stimulation for Parkinson's disease; advanced prosthetic limbs; brain-computer interfaces, including the first human implant by the company Neuralink in 2024.
- **Self-experimentation**: Kevin Warwick, a cybernetics professor, had a chip implanted in his arm in 1998 to open doors and switch on lights.
- **Contested meanings**: some members of Deaf communities have opposed routine cochlear implants for children, arguing that deafness is a cultural identity with its own language, not a defect to be fixed. This shows that "repair" and "enhancement" are socially defined.
- **Transhumanism** seeks to enhance human abilities through technology; **posthumanism** questions the idea of a fixed, separate "human". N. Katherine Hayles (*How We Became Posthuman*, 1999) traced how information came to be seen as separable from the body.

## Robots and cyborgs

- **Industrial robots**: Unimate, the first industrial robot arm, began work at a General Motors plant in 1961.
- **Care robots**: Japan has developed robots for elder care, such as Paro, a robotic baby seal used with people with dementia. Turkle (*Alone Together*) questioned whether robotic companionship is a substitute for human care.
- **Military uses**: armed drones and debates at the UN about autonomous weapons that select targets without human control.
- **Theory**: Haraway's cyborg (in the unit guide) and Latour's actor-network theory, which treats robots and algorithms as actants that shape outcomes alongside humans.

## Annotated model answer

*Extract from a 12-mark answer: Evaluate the view that algorithms reinforce social inequality.*

> Algorithms learn from past data, so they can reproduce past discrimination, as with Amazon's abandoned hiring tool that downgraded CVs mentioning "women's" **[knowledge and evidence]**. In my society, banks use automated credit scoring, which may disadvantage people with informal incomes **[application]**. Pasquale's "black box" shows that people cannot see or contest these decisions, which strengthens the institutions that use them **[analysis]**. However, biased tools have been audited and withdrawn, as in England's 2020 grading case, so algorithms can be challenged; the key factor is who controls and scrutinises them **[evaluation and judgement]**.

## Common mistakes

- Writing "AI" without saying which type or which use.
- Treating AGI or superintelligence as existing technology.
- Giving the 47% job estimate as fact without the task-based criticism.
- Describing cyborgs only as science fiction; use real medical cases.
- Ignoring hidden human labour behind "automated" systems.

## Exam technique

For **differences between types** questions, contrast on one clear basis (how they work, or what they can do). **Explain** questions on algorithms need the mechanism (training data, feedback loop, opacity). **Evaluate** answers should balance benefits against power and inequality, and use current own-society examples, such as AI in banking, schools or policing.
