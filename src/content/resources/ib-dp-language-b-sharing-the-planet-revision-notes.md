Sharing the planet is one of the five compulsory themes set out in the
[full syllabus guide](/resources/ib-dp-language-b-syllabus-guide/), and -- like all five -- it is
examined through unseen material on Paper 2, so it cannot be safely under-revised relative to the
others. These notes go deeper into this theme's recommended topics and how to prepare for it in the
target language, alongside the [subject overview](/resources/ib-dp-language-b-subject-guide/) and
[assessment revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-language-b-assessment-revision-notes/) already on the
site.

## What this theme actually asks

Sharing the planet explores the challenges and opportunities faced by individuals and communities in
the modern world, and asks what environmental and social challenges the world faces, how they might
be overcome, and what challenges and benefits globalization brings. Unlike a theme built around
personal or cultural experience, this theme leans toward issues and debate, which changes the kind of
language you need: opinion, cause-and-effect, and comparison structures matter more here than for a
theme like Identities.

## Recommended topics and target-language vocabulary to build

- **The environment** -- practise vocabulary for climate, natural resources, conservation and
  environmental impact, plus opinion language for discussing environmental policy.
- **Human rights** -- practise vocabulary for rights, freedoms, justice and equality, plus the more
  formal register this topic often calls for.
- **Peace and conflict** -- practise vocabulary for cooperation, negotiation, tension and resolution.
- **Equality** -- practise vocabulary for comparison and contrast (more/less, similarity/difference),
  since equality-focused texts frequently compare groups, regions or outcomes.
- **Globalization** -- practise vocabulary for cause and effect (because of, as a result, this leads
  to), since globalization texts often trace chains of consequence across borders.
- **Ethics** -- practise vocabulary for expressing and justifying an opinion or moral position.
- **Urban and rural environments** -- practise vocabulary for comparing settings and describing
  change over time.

## Why comprehension practice matters as much as speaking practice for this theme

Paper 2's combined 50% weighting for listening and reading comprehension is worth as much as the
writing task and internal oral combined, as the full syllabus guide notes -- yet revision time tends
to default toward speaking and writing output. Sharing the planet is a theme where unseen texts
(news reports, opinion pieces, documentary-style audio) are especially likely to appear, since the
theme is inherently issue-driven and current-affairs adjacent. Build comprehension practice
specifically around authentic or course-level materials discussing environmental, social or global
issues in the target language, not just vocabulary lists.

## Preparing Paper 1's writing task for this theme

If a Paper 1 writing prompt draws on Sharing the planet, expect the syllabus's specified text types
(such as a blog post, an article, a formal letter or a report) to require the more formal,
argument-structured register this theme often calls for -- practising an opinion-based article or a
formal letter arguing for a specific environmental or social position, in the target language's real
conventions, is more targeted preparation than only reviewing topic vocabulary in isolation.

## Preparing the individual oral around this theme

At SL, the individual oral's visual stimulus could be an image relevant to any of the five themes,
including Sharing the planet -- practise describing and discussing an environmental, human-rights or
globalization-related image spontaneously in the target language. At HL, if either of your two
studied literary works engages with issues from this theme, that connection is worth identifying and
preparing explicitly ahead of the assessment.

## How to approach it

Because all five themes are compulsory and examined through unseen material, build genuine breadth
across Sharing the planet's seven recommended topics rather than concentrating only on the one or two
your class discussed most. Because this theme leans toward opinion, cause-and-effect and comparison
language more than some of the other four themes, make sure your target-language toolkit for
expressing and justifying a position, and for tracing cause and consequence, is genuinely fluent, not
just recognised passively when reading.

## Self-test

1. Name the seven recommended topics within the Sharing the planet theme.
2. Why does this theme lean more heavily on opinion and cause-and-effect language than a theme like
   Identities might?
3. Why is comprehension practice (listening and reading) worth as much dedicated revision time as
   speaking and writing practice for this theme specifically?
4. Give one example of a text type Paper 1 might ask you to produce if a prompt draws on this theme.

**Answers:** 1. The environment, human rights, peace and conflict, equality, globalization, ethics,
and urban and rural environments. 2. Because the theme is built around issues, challenges and debate
(environmental policy, globalization's effects, human rights), which are naturally discussed using
opinion, comparison and cause-and-effect language more than personal or descriptive language. 3.
Because Paper 2's combined listening and reading weighting (50%) equals the writing task and internal
oral combined, and this issue-driven theme is especially likely to appear in unseen news, opinion or
documentary-style comprehension material. 4. Any valid example specified by the syllabus, such as a
blog post, an article, a formal letter, or a report, arguing or informing on an environmental, social
or global issue.
