Condensed notes for **sub-topic 2.2 Outsourcing** in Cambridge O Level Commerce (7100). The syllabus statement is: *"the growth of outsourcing: contract manufacturing and logistics"*. For explanations and a costed example, see the [outsourcing study guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-commerce-7100-outsourcing/).

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Outsourcing | Paying another business to carry out an activity the business could do itself |
| In-house | Carrying out the activity with the business's own staff, premises and equipment |
| Client | The business that outsources the work |
| Contractor (provider) | The business paid to do the work |
| Contract manufacturing | Paying another firm to make products, or parts, to the client's design and specification, usually sold under the client's brand |
| Contract logistics | Paying a specialist to manage storage and movement of goods: warehousing, picking and packing, delivery, returns and documents |
| Fixed cost | A cost that does not change with output, such as warehouse rent |
| Variable cost | A cost that rises and falls with output, such as a fee per order |

## The two growth areas

**Contract manufacturing**

- What it is: the contractor makes; the client designs, brands and sells.
- Examples: a bottling plant filling a new juice brand's drinks; an assembler building earphones designed by an electronics brand.
- Why it has grown: low start-up costs for new brands; specialist machinery and skills; economies of scale; flexibility of output; focus on core activities; easier management through digital communication and global supply chains.

**Contract logistics**

- What it is: a provider stores, packs, moves and delivers the client's goods, and may handle returns and export paperwork.
- Why it has grown: the rise of **ecommerce** and parcel deliveries; customer expectations of fast delivery and tracking; complex cross-border trade; costs that rise and fall with sales.

## Benefits versus risks

| Benefits | Risks |
|---|---|
| No factory, warehouse or vehicles to buy | Less control over quality and timing |
| Specialist expertise and equipment | Dependence on one contractor |
| Lower cost per unit | Leak or copying of designs and data |
| Costs vary with output | Reputation damage from contractor's conduct |
| Managers focus on design and selling | Slower communication across distance and time zones |

**Link to 1.2.2:** in-house wins on *control on quality and information*; outsourcing wins on *accessing expertise and cost savings*.

## Calculation pattern

When a question gives figures:

1. Add in-house costs (rent, wages, vehicles, packaging) for the period.
2. Multiply the provider's charge by the number of units or orders.
3. Compare totals, or divide each total by units for cost per unit.
4. Comment: fixed in-house costs make outsourcing relatively cheaper when output is low.

*Invented example:* in-house costs $6,000 for 1,500 orders = $4.00 per order; provider charges $3.20 per order = $4,800, so outsourcing saves $1,200.

## Exam traps

- **Outsourcing is not the same as moving abroad.** The contractor can be in the same country.
- **Contract manufacturing is not a franchise.** No outlet or brand licence is involved; the contractor only produces.
- **Contract logistics is more than transport.** Storage, packing, returns and documents are included.
- **Balance is needed.** "Should the business outsource?" requires benefits, risks and a decision.
- **Fit the product.** Quality control matters most for food, medicines and premium goods.

11. A provider charges $2.75 per order. How much does it charge for 1,200 orders?

## Self-test answers

1. Paying another business to carry out an activity the business could do itself.
2. Contract manufacturing and logistics.
3. Paying another firm to make products, or parts of them, to the client's own design and specification.
4. Any three: warehousing; picking and packing orders; transport and delivery; handling returns; export documents and customs clearance.
5. Online sellers must deliver many small parcels quickly and handle returns; providers already have warehouses, vehicles and tracking, so it is cheaper than building this in-house.
6. Any two: lower start-up costs; access to specialist machinery and skills; economies of scale; flexible output; focus on core activities; easier management through digital communication.
7. Control on quality, or control on information.
8. Accessing expertise, or cost savings.
9. Any two: less control over quality or timing; dependence on the contractor; leaked designs or data; reputation damage; communication delays.
10. It avoids the large cost of buying premises and machinery, so it can launch with little capital and use the contractor's expertise.
11. 1,200 x $2.75 = $3,300.
12. $5,400 / 1,200 = $4.50 per order; the provider ($2.75 per order, $3,300 in total) is cheaper by $2,100 a month.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is outsourcing always cheaper?** No. A provider's charges may exceed in-house costs when output is high and the business already owns its premises and equipment; this is why figures should be compared.

**Where else does outsourcing appear in the course?** The meaning and benefits of in-house and outsourced production are in 1.2.2, and logistics elements are in 1.1.2 and Topic 4.
