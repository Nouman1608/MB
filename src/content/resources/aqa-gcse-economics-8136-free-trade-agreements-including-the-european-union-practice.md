These questions cover **section 3.2.4.3 Free-trade agreements including the European Union** in the AQA GCSE Economics (8136) specification for first teaching from September 2017: the arguments for and against free trade, and the significance and benefits of free-trade agreements such as the EU. There are no tiers. Countries, firms and prices are invented; UK and EU dates are real. For explanations, see the [free-trade agreements study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-economics-8136-free-trade-agreements-including-the-european-union/) and the [free-trade agreements revision notes](/resources/aqa-gcse-economics-8136-free-trade-agreements-including-the-european-union-revision-notes/).

## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** A government limits the number of tonnes of steel that can be imported each year. This is an example of: **[1]**

A: A tariff

B: A quota

C: A subsidy

D: A free-trade agreement

**2.** Which **one** of the following best describes a customs union? **[1]**

A: Members remove tariffs between themselves and each sets its own tariffs on outside goods

B: Members remove tariffs between themselves and charge a common tariff on outside goods

C: Members share a single currency

D: Members ban all imports from outside

**3.** State **one** reason why a government may protect a strategic industry. **[1]**

**4.** An imported television costs £400 and a home-made one costs £440. The government places a 15% tariff on imported televisions.

**(a)** Calculate the price of the imported television after the tariff. **[1]**

**(b)** State which television is now cheaper. **[1]**

**(c)** 50,000 imported televisions are sold after the tariff. Calculate the tariff revenue. **[1]**

**5.** A UK bakery imports 30 tonnes of flour a month at £500 a tonne. An 8% tariff is placed on the flour. Calculate the extra cost to the bakery over a year. **[2]**

**6.** Two members of a free-trade agreement charge different tariffs on a £50 good from outside: Member X charges 4% and Member Y charges 18%. Calculate the price of the good in each member. **[2]**

**7.** State the year in which the UK held a referendum on leaving the EU, and the date on which it left. **[2]**

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**8.** Explain how removing tariffs could benefit UK firms that import components. **[3]**

**9.** Explain the infant industry argument for protecting a domestic industry. **[3]**

**10.** Explain why non-tariff barriers, such as customs paperwork, can be especially costly for small exporting firms. **[3]**

**11.** An invented country's government is considering a 25% tariff on imported steel to protect its own steelworks. Its car industry uses large amounts of steel.

**(a)** Explain **one** benefit of the tariff for the country's steel industry. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain **one** cost of the tariff for the country's car makers and their customers. **[3]**

**12.** Lorvia is an invented small country. Its machinery firms are efficient and export widely, but its farms have high costs. Tariffs on imports currently provide a noticeable share of government revenue. Lorvia has been invited to join a large regional free-trade agreement.

Evaluate whether Lorvia should join. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** **B** [1]. A quota limits quantity; a tariff is a tax. **[1]**

**2.** **B** [1]. The common external tariff is what distinguishes a customs union. **[1]**

**3.** Any one: to avoid relying on other countries for vital goods such as food, energy or defence equipment in a crisis or conflict [1]. **[1]**

**4. (a)** 400 × 1.15 = **£460** [1].

**(b)** The home-made television (£440) [1].

**(c)** 0.15 × 400 × 50,000 = **£3,000,000** [1]. **[3]**

**5.** Tariff per month = 0.08 × 500 × 30 = £1,200 [1]. Over a year: 1,200 × 12 = **£14,400** [1]. **[2]**

**6.** Member X: 50 × 1.04 = **£52** [1]. Member Y: 50 × 1.18 = **£59** [1]. **[2]**

*Examiner insight:* This gap is why a free-trade area needs rules of origin: otherwise the good would enter through X and move to Y tariff-free.

**7.** Referendum: 2016 (23 June) [1]. Left the EU: 31 January 2020 [1]. **[2]**

**8.** Without tariffs, imported components are cheaper [1], so the firms' costs of production fall [1]. They can cut prices to sell more, invest, or earn higher profits, which makes them more competitive at home and abroad [1]. **[3]**

**9.** A new industry is small, so it has high average costs and cannot yet benefit from economies of scale [1]. Established foreign firms can undercut it, so it may fail before it grows [1]. Temporary protection, such as a tariff, lets it grow until its costs fall enough to compete, after which protection should be removed [1]. **[3]**

**10.** Each shipment needs forms, checks and proof of where goods were made, which cost time and money [1]. These costs are largely fixed per shipment, so they are a bigger share of the value of small orders [1]. Small firms also cannot afford specialist staff to handle the paperwork, so some stop exporting [1]. **[3]**

**11. (a)** Imported steel becomes 25% dearer [1], so buyers switch to home-made steel and the steelworks sells more, protecting jobs [1].

**(b)** Car makers must pay more for steel, whether imported or from a domestic producer that can now raise its price [1]. Their costs rise, so they raise car prices or accept lower profits [1]; customers pay more, and exports of cars may fall because they are less competitive abroad [1]. **[5]**

**12.** *Reasons to join:*

- Lorvia's efficient machinery firms gain tariff-free access to a large market, so exports and jobs rise [1].
- Larger sales bring economies of scale, lowering costs further [1].
- Consumers gain cheaper imports and more choice [1].
- Firms outside may invest in Lorvia to serve the whole agreement area [1].

*Reasons not to join:*

- High-cost farms face competition from cheaper members' produce, so farms may close and rural workers lose jobs [1].
- The government loses tariff revenue, so it must raise other taxes or cut spending [1].
- Lorvia gives up some control over its own trade policy [1].

*Judgement:* Lorvia should join, because its strongest industry gains a large market and consumers gain lower prices, which are likely to outweigh the losses [1]; it should use part of the gains to support farm workers to retrain and replace lost tariff revenue with other taxes [1]. **[9]**

*Examiner insight:* Use every detail of the case: efficient exporters, high-cost farms and tariff revenue each point to a different group.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Confusing tariffs (taxes) with quotas (limits).
- Describing a customs union without the common external tariff.
- Forgetting that tariffs raise firms' costs of imported inputs.
- Giving wrong dates for the UK's EU membership.
- Evaluating with consumers only, ignoring producers, workers and government.
