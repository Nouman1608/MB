## Section A

**1.** Three stars are observed: one blue, one red and one yellow. List them in order of increasing surface temperature. **[1]**

**2.** State what property of a star determines its colour. **[1]**

**3.** Name the stage in the life of a Sun-like star that comes immediately before it becomes a white dwarf. **[1]**

**4.** Explain why a star remains stable for a long time during its main sequence stage. **[3]**

**5.** State the stage that a star much more massive than the Sun passes through immediately after its main sequence stage. **[1]**

## Section B

**6.** Describe how a star of similar mass to the Sun forms from a nebula and evolves to the end of its life. **[6]**

**7.** Describe how the evolution of a star much more massive than the Sun differs from that of a Sun-like star after the main sequence stage. **[4]**

**8.** A student says: "Red stars must be the hottest stars, because red means hot." Evaluate this statement. **[2]**

**9.** Two stars have the same apparent brightness when seen from Earth. Star X is much further away than star Y.

**(a)** State which star gives out more light. Explain your answer. **[2]**

**(b)** State which star has the lower (more negative) absolute magnitude. **[1]**

**(c)** Explain why astronomers use absolute magnitude, rather than apparent brightness, to compare stars. **[2]**

**10.** Describe how to draw the axes of a Hertzsprung–Russell diagram, including the direction in which each quantity increases. **[4]**

**11.** The table gives data for three stars.

| Star | Surface temperature / K | Absolute magnitude |
|---|---|---|
| J | 3500 | −4 |
| K | 12 000 | +11 |
| L | 5500 | +5 |

**(a)** Identify the type of each star. **[3]**

**(b)** Explain why star K is dim even though it is hot. **[1]**

**(c)** Explain why star J is bright even though it is cool. **[1]**

**12.** The Sun is currently a main sequence star. Describe how its position on the HR diagram will change during the rest of its life. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** Red, yellow, blue [1].

**2.** Its surface temperature [1].

**3.** Red giant [1].

**4.** Gravity pulls the star's material inwards [1]; the energy released by nuclear fusion in the core produces an outward pressure [1]; these are balanced, so the star neither collapses nor expands [1].

**5.** Red supergiant [1].

**6.** Any six:
- a nebula is a cloud of gas (mainly hydrogen) and dust [1]
- gravity pulls it together and it heats up as it collapses [1]
- when hot and dense enough, hydrogen nuclei fuse into helium [1]
- it becomes a main sequence star, stable because gravity is balanced by outward pressure from fusion [1]
- when the hydrogen in the core runs out, the outer layers expand and cool [1]
- it becomes a red giant [1]
- the outer layers drift away into space [1]
- the hot, dense core is left as a white dwarf, which cools [1]

maximum 6, with the four stages in the correct order needed for full marks.

**7.** A massive star becomes a red supergiant, whereas a Sun-like star becomes a red giant [1]. The massive star then explodes as a supernova, whereas a Sun-like star loses its outer layers gently [1]. The massive star's core becomes a neutron star [1], or a black hole if the star is massive enough, whereas a Sun-like star ends as a white dwarf [1].

**8.** The statement is wrong: red stars are the coolest stars [1]; colour shifts from red to blue as surface temperature increases, so blue stars are the hottest [1].

**9. (a)** Star X [1]: it looks as bright as Y despite being much further away, so it must give out more light [1].
**(b)** Star X [1].
**(c)** Apparent brightness depends on distance as well as the light given out [1]; absolute magnitude compares stars as if they were all at the same standard distance, so the comparison is fair [1].

**10.** Vertical axis: absolute magnitude (or luminosity) [1], with brighter stars at the top, so magnitude becomes more negative going up [1]. Horizontal axis: surface temperature (or colour) [1], increasing from right to left, so hot (blue) stars are on the left [1].

**11. (a)** J: red giant (or supergiant) [1]; K: white dwarf [1]; L: main sequence star [1].
**(b)** It is very small, so it gives out little light in total despite its high temperature [1].
**(c)** It is very large, so it gives out a lot of light in total despite its low temperature [1].

**12.** It will stay on the main sequence for a long time [1], then move up and to the right as it becomes a red giant (brighter, cooler) [1], and finally move down and to the left as it becomes a white dwarf (dimmer, hotter) [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing stages without the causes that link them (Question 6).
- Writing two separate life cycles instead of direct comparisons (Question 7).
- Saying the more distant star in Question 9 is dimmer because it is further away: they look equally bright.
- Drawing the temperature axis the wrong way round (Question 10).
- Placing white dwarfs on the right because they "die out".

Statement 8.12P asks you to draw its main components, so you should be able to sketch the axes and the four main regions.
