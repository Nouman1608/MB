## Core outcomes at a glance

1. Organisms are classified into groups by the **features they share**.
2. A **species** is a group of organisms that can reproduce to produce **fertile offspring**.
3. The **binomial system** is an internationally agreed system: two-part name showing **genus** and **species**.
4. **Construct and use** dichotomous keys based on **identifiable features**.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Classification | Sorting organisms into groups by shared features |
| Species | A group of organisms that can reproduce to produce fertile offspring |
| Fertile | Able to reproduce |
| Genus | A group of closely related species; the first word of a binomial name |
| Binomial system | The internationally agreed two-part scientific naming of species |
| Dichotomous key | A series of paired, contrasting statements used to identify organisms |
| Common ancestor (Extended) | An ancestral species from which two or more groups have evolved |
| Base sequence (Extended) | The order of bases (A, T, C, G) along a DNA strand |

## Species: the fertile test

- Can they produce offspring? Not enough.
- Are the **offspring fertile**? Then same species.
- Horse × donkey → mule (infertile) → horses and donkeys are **different** species.
- Very different-looking dog breeds produce fertile puppies → **same** species.

## Binomial names: the rules

| Rule | Example |
|---|---|
| Genus first, capital letter | *Panthera* |
| Species second, lower case | *leo* |
| Italics in print | *Panthera leo* |
| Underlined by hand | Panthera leo, underlined |
| Genus may be shortened after first use | *P. leo* |

**Same genus = closely related:** *Panthera leo* (lion) and *Panthera tigris* (tiger).

**Why use it:** one name per species worldwide; common names differ between places and languages.

## Building a key: five steps

1. List visible features of every organism.
2. Choose a feature that splits the group in two.
3. Write two opposite statements (has / has not; more than / fewer than a stated number).
4. Repeat until each organism stands alone.
5. Check: *n* organisms need *n* − 1 pairs; run every organism through.

**Good features:** number of legs; wings present or absent; leaf edge smooth or toothed; leaves simple or divided; shell present or absent.

**Poor features:** "large", "dark", "pretty", features that vary with age or season, features you cannot see on the specimen.

## Using a key

- Always start at step 1.
- Choose one statement at each step and follow its instruction.
- Record the route if asked: 1 → 3 → 4 → name.

## Extended only: outcomes 5 to 7

- Classification aims to reflect **evolutionary relationships**: groups share a **common ancestor**.
- **DNA base sequences** are used as a means of classification.
- **More recent common ancestor → more similar base sequences.**
- Distant common ancestor → less similar base sequences.
- Appearance can mislead: dolphins (mammals) and sharks (fish) look alike because both live in water.

### Method: comparing sequences

1. Line up the sequences base by base.
2. Count the positions that differ.
3. Percentage identical = (total bases − differences) ÷ total bases × 100.
4. Fewest differences = most closely related.

**Worked reminder:** two 25-base sequences differ at 3 positions: (25 − 3) ÷ 25 × 100 = **88%** identical.

## Exam traps

- Species definition without "fertile" scores zero.
- *Homo Sapiens* is wrong: the species word is lower case.
- The second word is the **species**, not the family.
- A key step must offer exactly **two** choices.
- (Extended) Not "the same DNA": **more similar base sequences**.
- (Extended) Not "evolved from each other": **share a more recent common ancestor**.

## Quick self-test

1. State the basis on which organisms are classified into groups.
2. Define a species.
3. Why are horses and donkeys classified as different species?
4. In *Canis lupus*, which word is the genus?
5. Give two rules for writing a binomial name.
6. Why is the binomial system described as internationally agreed?
7. How many pairs of statements does a key for seven organisms need?
8. Why is "large or small" a poor feature for a key?
9. Two species are in the same genus. What does this suggest about them?
10. (Extended) What do classification systems aim to reflect?
11. (Extended) Species A and B differ at 2 of 40 bases; A and C differ at 9. Which is more closely related to A?
12. (Extended) Calculate the percentage of bases identical for A and C in question 11.

### Answers

1. The features that they share.
2. A group of organisms that can reproduce to produce fertile offspring.
3. Their offspring (mules) are infertile.
4. *Canis*.
5. Any two: genus has a capital letter; species is lower case; italics in print; underlined by hand.
6. Every country uses the same name for the same species, so there is no confusion between languages.
7. Six.
8. It is a matter of opinion and depends on what you compare with, so different people would answer differently.
9. They are closely related.
10. Evolutionary relationships.
11. B, because its base sequence is more similar to A's.
12. (40 − 9) ÷ 40 × 100 = **77.5%**.

## Frequently asked questions

**Which parts of 1.2 are Extended only?**
Outcomes 5 to 7: classification reflecting evolutionary relationships and the use of DNA base sequences.

**Do I need to know classification levels such as order and family?**
The 0610 outcomes name genus and species only, so focus on those and on how keys and DNA comparisons work.
