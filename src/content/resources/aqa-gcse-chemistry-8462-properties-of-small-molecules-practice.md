These questions cover section **4.2.2.4 Properties of small molecules** of the AQA GCSE Chemistry (8462)
specification, for teaching from September 2016 onwards (version 1.2). The content is examined on Paper 1 at
Foundation and Higher Tier, and no question here tests Higher-tier-only content.

Learn the content first in the [properties of small molecules study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-chemistry-8462-properties-of-small-molecules/)
and the [revision notes](/resources/aqa-gcse-chemistry-8462-properties-of-small-molecules-revision-notes/).

## Section A

**1.** **(a)** Name the forces that are overcome when liquid oxygen boils. **[1]**
**(b)** State whether the forces in part (a) are weak or strong compared with covalent bonds. **[1]**

**2.** Carbon dioxide is made of small molecules.

**(a)** Give **one** property that you would expect it to have because of its structure. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain why carbon dioxide does not conduct electricity. **[2]**

**3.** The table gives the boiling points of three alkanes.

| Alkane | Formula | Boiling point / °C |
|---|---|---|
| Ethane | C₂H₆ | -89 |
| Propane | C₃H₈ | -42 |
| Butane | C₄H₁₀ | -1 |

**(a)** Describe the trend in boiling point as the number of carbon atoms increases. **[1]**
**(b)** Propane melts at -188 °C. Predict the state of propane at -60 °C. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain the trend in terms of intermolecular forces. **[2]**

**4.** A student says that when ice melts, the covalent bonds between hydrogen and oxygen break.
Explain why the student is wrong. **[2]**

## Section B

**5.** The table shows the properties of four substances.

| Substance | Melting point / °C | Boiling point / °C | Conducts when liquid? |
|---|---|---|---|
| E | -101 | -34 | No |
| F | 801 | 1413 | Yes |
| G | 1410 | 3265 | No |
| H | -7 | 59 | No |

**(a)** Which **two** substances consist of small molecules? **[2]**
**(b)** Explain how you can tell. **[2]**
**(c)** Which of the two substances in part (a) has the larger molecules? Give a reason. **[2]**

**6.** Chlorine, bromine and iodine are all made of diatomic molecules. At 20 °C chlorine is a gas, bromine is a
liquid and iodine is a solid.

**(a)** Explain this difference in terms of molecular size and intermolecular forces. **[3]**
**(b)** Iodine melts at 114 °C. Explain why this is a much lower melting point than that of sodium chloride. **[3]**

**7.** Methane, CH₄, boils at -162 °C. Decane, C₁₀H₂₂, boils at 174 °C.

**(a)** State which substance has the stronger intermolecular forces. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain your answer. **[2]**
**(c)** Decane melts at -30 °C. Give the state of each at 20 °C. **[2]**

**8.** A student heats ice in a beaker until it boils and then holds a cold plate over the steam.

**(a)** State what is overcome when the water boils. **[1]**
**(b)** State what is seen on the plate and the name of this change of state. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why water is **not** broken down into hydrogen and oxygen during this experiment. **[2]**

**9.** Compare the electrical conductivity of sodium chloride (molten), hydrogen chloride (liquid) and iron
(solid). Explain each result in terms of the particles present. **[6]**

**10.** A chemist is given an unlabelled solid which melts at 115 °C and does not conduct electricity when solid
or when molten.

**(a)** State which of the two substances, sulfur (melting point 115 °C) or sodium chloride (melting point
801 °C), the solid is more likely to be. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain your answer in terms of structure and bonding. **[3]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** **Intermolecular forces** (between the oxygen molecules) [1]
**(b)** **Weak** [1]

**2. (a)** Any one: low melting point and boiling point; it is a gas at room temperature [1]
**(b)** The molecules have **no overall electric charge** [1]; there are no ions or free electrons to carry charge
[1]. **[2]**

**3. (a)** The boiling point **increases** [1]
**(b)** **Liquid**: -60 °C is above -188 °C and below -42 °C [1]
**(c)** Bigger molecules have **stronger** intermolecular forces [1] so more energy is needed to overcome them [1].
**[2]**

**4.** The covalent bonds inside the water molecules **stay intact** [1]; only the **weak intermolecular forces
between** the molecules are overcome [1]. **[2]**

**5. (a)** **E and H** [1] [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** They have **low melting points and boiling points** [1]; they do not conduct when liquid, so they are not
ionic or metallic [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** **H** [1]; it has higher melting and boiling points, so stronger intermolecular forces [1], which go with
larger molecules. **[2]**

**6. (a)** Molecular size increases from chlorine to bromine to iodine [1]; the intermolecular forces get
stronger as the molecules get larger [1]; more energy is needed to overcome them, so the boiling and melting
points rise and the state at 20 °C changes from gas to liquid to solid [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** Iodine is made of small molecules with **weak intermolecular forces** [1]; sodium chloride has a giant
lattice of ions with **many strong electrostatic bonds** [1]; far less energy is needed to melt iodine [1].
**[3]**

**7. (a)** **Decane** [1]
**(b)** Decane molecules are **larger** [1], so the forces between them are stronger and more energy is needed to
boil it [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Methane: **gas**; decane: **liquid** (20 °C is above its melting point and below 174 °C) [1] [1]. **[2]**

**8. (a)** The **weak intermolecular forces** between water molecules [1]
**(b)** **Liquid droplets** (water) form on the plate [1]; the change of state is **condensing** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** The **covalent bonds** within each water molecule are strong and are **not** overcome [1]; so the
molecules stay as H₂O and only separate from each other [1]. **[2]**

**9.** Marking points, up to 6:
- Molten sodium chloride conducts [1] because it contains ions that are free to move [1].
- Liquid hydrogen chloride does not conduct [1] because it consists of molecules with no overall charge and no
  free ions or electrons [1].
- Solid iron conducts [1] because it has delocalised electrons that are free to move [1]. **[6]**

**10. (a)** **Sulfur** [1]
**(b)** Sulfur is made of small molecules with weak intermolecular forces, so little energy is needed to melt it
and its melting point is low [1]; the molecules have no overall charge, so it does not conduct in either state
[1]; sodium chloride is a giant ionic lattice that melts at a much higher temperature and conducts when molten
[1]. **[3]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "the bonds break" for a small-molecule substance without specifying which forces.
- Saying the covalent bonds are weak.
- Giving the trend in boiling point without linking to molecular size.
- Forgetting the state at a stated temperature needs a comparison.
- Explaining non-conduction with "no electrons" (electrons are present, but they are not free).
