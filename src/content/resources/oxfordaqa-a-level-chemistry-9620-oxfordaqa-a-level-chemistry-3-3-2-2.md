This guide goes deeper into **3.3.2.2 Modification of alkanes by cracking**, part of section 3.3.2 Alkanes in the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Chemistry (9620) specification, Version 5.3. It is International AS content, assessed in Unit 2 (Organic 1 and Physical 1) and available again in the synoptic Unit 5. Questions combine three things: the conditions and products of the two types of cracking, balanced equations, and an economic argument.

## What the specification asks for

- Cracking involves breaking C–C bonds in alkanes.
- Thermal cracking takes place at high pressure and high temperature and produces a high percentage of alkenes (mechanism not required).
- Catalytic cracking takes place at a slight pressure, high temperature and in the presence of a zeolite catalyst, and is used mainly to produce motor fuels and aromatic hydrocarbons (mechanism not required).
- Explain the economic reasons for cracking alkanes.

The specification gives the conditions in words, so learn the words exactly; you are not asked for numerical temperatures or pressures.

## What cracking does

Cracking breaks long-chain alkane molecules into shorter molecules. It is a **chemical** change: **C–C bonds break**. (Fractional distillation, in 3.3.2.1, is the physical change that only separates molecules.)

Why at least one product must be an alkene:

- A chain alkane has 2n + 2 hydrogen atoms for n carbons.
- If it splits into two pieces, the pieces together have the same number of hydrogens, but two alkanes would need 2n + 4.
- There are not enough hydrogen atoms, so at least one product is an **alkene** (CₙH₂ₙ), with a C=C bond.

A single alkane can crack in many ways, so real cracking gives a mixture of products. Exam questions specify the products to make the equation definite.

## The two types

| | Thermal cracking | Catalytic cracking |
|---|---|---|
| Temperature | high | high |
| Pressure | **high** | **slight** |
| Catalyst | none | **zeolite** |
| Main products | **high percentage of alkenes** | **motor fuels** and **aromatic hydrocarbons** |
| Mechanism | not required | not required |

A **zeolite** is a solid aluminosilicate with a honeycomb-like structure of tiny pores, which gives it a very large surface area. Because it is a catalyst, catalytic cracking can work at a slight pressure. Its products include branched alkanes, cycloalkanes and aromatic hydrocarbons (compounds containing a benzene ring), which are used in motor fuels.

**How to choose in a question:** if a company wants **alkenes** (for polymers or other chemicals), choose **thermal** cracking. If it wants **motor fuels** or **aromatic hydrocarbons**, choose **catalytic** cracking.

## Balancing cracking equations

Method:

1. Write the formula of the starting alkane, CₙH₂ₙ₊₂.
2. Write the formulas of the products you are given.
3. Subtract carbons and hydrogens **separately** to find what is left.
4. Check each product fits CₙH₂ₙ₊₂ (alkane) or CₙH₂ₙ (alkene).

### Worked example 1: three products

*Hexadecane, C₁₆H₃₄, cracks to give octane, butene and ethene only, with twice as many ethene molecules as butene molecules. Write the equation.*

```
C₁₆H₃₄ → C₈H₁₈ + C₄H₈ + 2C₂H₄
Carbon:   8 + 4 + 2(2) = 16  ✓
Hydrogen: 18 + 8 + 2(4) = 34 ✓
```

### Worked example 2: finding a missing product

*Tetradecane, C₁₄H₃₀, cracks to give octane and only one other product, Z, three molecules of which form per molecule of tetradecane. Identify Z.*

```
C₁₄H₃₀ → C₈H₁₈ + 3Z
Carbon left:   14 − 8 = 6, so each Z has 2 C
Hydrogen left: 30 − 18 = 12, so each Z has 4 H
Z = C₂H₄, ethene (fits CₙH₂ₙ)
```

## Calculating product masses

Cracking equations are used like any other equation in mole calculations.

### Worked example 3: mass of ethene

*Decane cracks: C₁₀H₂₂ → C₆H₁₄ + 2C₂H₄. Calculate the maximum mass of ethene from 1.00 kg of decane, and the percentage of the product mass that is ethene. (Ar: C = 12.0, H = 1.0)*

```
Mr(C₁₀H₂₂) = 142.0          Mr(C₂H₄) = 28.0
n(decane)  = 1000 ÷ 142.0 = 7.042 mol
n(ethene)  = 2 × 7.042 = 14.08 mol
mass       = 14.08 × 28.0 = 394 g

% of product mass as ethene = (2 × 28.0) ÷ 142.0 × 100 = 39.4 %
```

All the mass of the reactant ends up in the products, so the percentage of the product mass that is ethene is set by the equation.

## The economic reasons for cracking

This is the "explain" part of the specification. Build the argument in steps.

1. **Supply is fixed by the crude oil.** Fractional distillation gives each fraction in proportions set by the composition of the crude oil used.
2. **Demand does not match supply.** Typically there is more of the long-chain (heavy) fractions than the market needs, and not enough of the short-chain fractions used as motor fuels.
3. **Short-chain products are worth more.** High demand means the lighter fractions, such as petrol, sell for a higher price than the surplus heavy fractions.
4. **Cracking converts low-value surplus into high-value products.** Breaking long chains makes more of the motor-fuel fractions.
5. **Alkenes are valuable feedstock.** Alkenes are far more reactive than alkanes, because of the C=C bond, so they are used to make polymers (such as poly(ethene)) and other chemicals. Crude oil itself contains very few alkenes.

### Worked example 4: interpreting supply and demand

The figures below are invented to show the method.

| Fraction | Supply from distillation / % | Demand / % |
|---|---|---|
| Gases | 4 | 6 |
| Petrol and naphtha | 22 | 34 |
| Kerosene | 12 | 10 |
| Diesel | 22 | 24 |
| Heavy fuel oil and residue | 40 | 26 |

*Use the data to explain why the refinery cracks some of its heavy fuel oil.*

> Heavy fuel oil is produced in excess: supply is 40 % but demand is only 26 % [1]. Petrol and naphtha are in short supply: 22 % supplied against 34 % demanded [1]. Cracking breaks the long-chain molecules in the surplus heavy fraction into shorter molecules that can be used as petrol, which is in higher demand and so is more valuable [1]. It also produces alkenes, which are useful for making polymers [1].

Quote numbers from the table, identify surplus and shortage, then link cracking to value.

## Common mistakes

- Saying cracking "separates" alkanes. It breaks C–C bonds; it is a chemical reaction.
- Mixing up the conditions: catalytic cracking has a **slight** pressure, not high pressure; thermal cracking has **no** catalyst.
- Saying thermal cracking makes "motor fuels" and catalytic cracking makes "alkenes" (the reverse of the specification).
- Writing a cracking equation with two alkane products and no alkene.
- Writing the alkene product with a formula that fits CₙH₂ₙ₊₂.
- Giving only "to make more petrol" for an economic explanation, without the supply and demand reasoning and the value argument.
- Describing a zeolite as a "zeolite enzyme" or naming platinum as the cracking catalyst.

## Exam technique for OxfordAQA command words

- **State** (conditions): use the specification wording: "high temperature, high pressure" or "slight pressure, high temperature, zeolite catalyst".
- **Write an equation**: check carbon and hydrogen separately, and check each formula against its general formula.
- **Explain** (economic reasons): supply versus demand, value of the products, usefulness of alkenes.
- **Calculate**: use moles and the mole ratio from the cracking equation.
