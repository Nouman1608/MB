These notes cover three sub-sections of the AQA A-level Economics (7136) specification, version 1.3: the circular flow of income, aggregate demand and aggregate supply analysis, and the determinants of aggregate demand. The PDF numbers them 4.2.2.1 to 4.2.2.3; the AQA website and the site map call them 3.2.2.1 to 3.2.2.3. They stand alone, so you can revise from this page and the [practice questions](/resources/aqa-a-level-economics-circular-flow-ad-and-as-analysis-and-determinants-of-practice/). Every country and figure is invented and illustrative. Course pages: [AQA A-level Economics hub](/boards/aqa/a-level/economics/), [checklist](/checklists/aqa/a-level/economics/) and [free diagnostics](/diagnostics/).

## 1. National income, nominal and real

National income measures the value of the output a country produces in a period. The same total can be found as income earned, output produced or expenditure made. You need the idea, not the detail of how the accounts are built.

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Nominal income | Measured at the prices of the year in which it is produced |
| Real income | Nominal income adjusted for price changes, so it measures the volume of output |
| Price index (deflator) | Average price level, with the base year set at 100 |

Method: real income = nominal income / (index / 100).

Worked example (Country A, illustrative). Year 1: nominal income 800, index 100. Year 2: nominal income 891, index 108.

- Real income in year 2 = 891 / 1.08 = **825**.
- Nominal growth = 891 / 800 = 1.11375, so 11.4%.
- Real growth = 825 / 800 = 1.03125, so **3.1%**. Most of the nominal rise was price rise.
- If the population rises from 20.0 million to 20.4 million, real income per head moves from 800 / 20.0 = 40.00 to 825 / 20.4 = 40.44 (units of income per million people). That is a rise of **1.1%**.

Real national income is the usual indicator of economic performance because it shows whether more is being produced. Limits: it ignores how income is shared, unpaid work, the black economy, quality changes, working hours and environmental damage. Per head figures matter when the population is changing.

## 2. The circular flow of income

Households supply labour and other factors to firms and receive income. They spend it on the output of firms. So income = output = expenditure, and the flow goes round.

| | Items | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Withdrawals (W) | Saving (S), taxation (T), import spending (M) | Income leaves the flow |
| Injections (J) | Investment (I), government spending (G), exports (X) | Spending enters the flow |

- **Equilibrium national income**: planned injections equal planned withdrawals, so the flow is steady and income is not changing.
- **Full employment income**: the level at which the economy's resources are fully used. Equilibrium can settle below it (spare capacity). Spending that pushes demand beyond it cannot raise real output further, so it puts pressure on prices.
- J = W is the condition. It is S + T + M = I + G + X. It does not say S = I.

Worked example (Country A). Injections: I 60, G 70, X 80, so J = 210. Withdrawals at income Y: S = 0.10Y, T = 0.15Y, M = 0.10Y, so W = 0.35Y.

| Income Y | S | T | M | W | Compare with J = 210 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 500 | 50 | 75 | 50 | 175 | W < J: income rises |
| 600 | 60 | 90 | 60 | 210 | W = J: equilibrium |
| 700 | 70 | 105 | 70 | 245 | W > J: income falls |

Effect of changes (same country):

- Government spending rises by 35, so J = 245. Equilibrium: 0.35Y = 245, so Y = **700**. Income rises by 100.
- Instead, the saving rate rises from 0.10 to 0.13, so W = 0.38Y. With J = 210, Y = 210 / 0.38 = **552.6**. Income falls by about 47.4.
- If full employment income is 650, the first change takes equilibrium (700) above capacity. The extra spending cannot become extra real output, so the price level is likely to rise.

Chain: injection up, firms' sales up, output and incomes up, households spend part again and withdraw part, until W has risen to equal the new J. A fall in an injection or rise in a withdrawal runs the other way.

## 3. AD and AS analysis

Axes: price level (vertical), real output or real GDP (horizontal). AD slopes down, short-run AS slopes up, long-run AS is vertical.

**Movement or shift?** A change in the price level moves you along the curves. A change in anything else shifts a curve.

| Curve | Shifts when | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| AD | C, I, G or (X - M) changes at each price level | Confidence falls, interest rates rise, a trading partner enters recession |
| Short-run AS | Production costs change | Wage rates, raw material prices, business taxes, productivity, energy costs |
| Long-run AS | Productive capacity changes | Technology, productivity of labour and capital, attitudes and enterprise, mobility of factors, incentives |

Distinguish short from long run: a cost rise such as dearer fuel can shift short-run AS without altering capacity. Lasting changes in the quantity or quality of resources shift long-run AS.

**Underlying economic growth** is a rightward shift in long-run AS. Short-run growth from using spare capacity is a movement towards it.

**Macroeconomic equilibrium** is where AD meets AS: a price level and a level of real output.

Worked example (Country A, price index and real output in units).

| Price level | AD | Short-run AS |
|---|---|---|
| 100 | 640 | 440 |
| 110 | 600 | 500 |
| 120 | 560 | 560 |
| 130 | 520 | 620 |
| 140 | 480 | 680 |

AD: Y = 1,040 - 4P. Short-run AS: Y = 6P - 160. Equilibrium: 1,040 - 4P = 6P - 160, so P = **120**, Y = **560**. Long-run AS at 600 means output is 40 below capacity, a gap of 40 / 600 = 6.7%. That gap is demand-deficient (cyclical) unemployment, because labour is idle for lack of spending.

- **Demand-side shock**: AD falls by 60 at each price. Y = 980 - 4P. New equilibrium: P = **114**, Y = **524**. Output falls 6.4%, prices fall 5.0%.
- **Supply-side shock**: short-run AS falls by 60 at each price. Y = 6P - 220. With the original AD, P = **126**, Y = **536**. Prices rise 5.0% and output falls 4.3%.
- **Growth**: long-run AS moves from 600 to 640, a rise of 6.7% in capacity.

Reading shocks: a demand shock moves price and output in the same direction; a supply shock moves them in opposite directions.

**Global events** affect a domestic economy through exports and imports (AD), imported raw materials and energy (short-run AS), exchange rates, confidence, and flows of finance and technology. A slump abroad shifts AD left; a world commodity price rise shifts short-run AS left.

## 4. The determinants of aggregate demand

AD is total planned spending on a country's output at a given price level: **AD = C + I + G + (X - M)**.

| Component | Main determinants |
|---|---|
| Consumption (C) | Real disposable income, interest rates, wealth, consumer confidence, availability of credit, taxes |
| Investment (I) | Interest rates, expected profit and business confidence, growth of demand (accelerator), corporation tax, access to credit, technology |
| Government spending (G) | Policy choices, the budget deficit, the stage of the cycle (benefit payments), demographic pressures |
| Exports (X) | Foreign incomes, exchange rate, relative prices and quality, trade barriers |
| Imports (M) | Domestic income, exchange rate, relative prices, quality, trade barriers |

**Net exports** in numbers (Country A): C 520, I 130, G 160, X 190, M 170. AD = 520 + 130 + 160 + (190 - 170) = **830**. Depreciation lifts X to 205 and cuts M to 165. Net exports become 40 and AD = **850**, a rise of 2.4%. Higher net exports add to AD, output and jobs. Costs: dearer imports, inflation if near capacity, and a gain that depends on how responsive trade is.

**The basic accelerator.** Investment depends on the rate of change of demand for output, not the level. Illustration (capital stock needs to be twice output; you will not be asked to calculate this):

| Year | Output | Growth | Net investment |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 100 | - | - |
| 2 | 110 | 10.0% | 20 |
| 3 | 120 | 9.1% | 20 |
| 4 | 126 | 5.0% | 12 |
| 5 | 126 | 0% | 0 |
| 6 | 120 | -4.8% | negative |

Output is still rising in year 4, yet investment has fallen by 8. So a slowdown in growth can hit investment, and with it AD, hard.

**Determinants of saving.** Saving is disposable income not spent. Income, interest rates, wealth, job security and expected inflation, access to credit, age structure and attitudes all affect it. Worked example: disposable income 400, consumption 340, saving **60**, so 15% of disposable income is saved.

**Saving versus investment**

| | Saving | Investment |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | Income not spent | Spending on capital goods |
| Made by | Households (also firms and government) | Mainly firms |
| Circular flow | Withdrawal | Injection |
| Motive | Future consumption, security | Expected profit |

They are decided by different people, so planned saving and planned investment may differ. Saving that is not matched by injections lowers national income.

## 5. Quick self-test

1. Nominal income is 660 and the price index is 110. Find real income.
2. Real income rises from 600 to 618. Find real growth.
3. Injections are 150 and withdrawals are 0.25Y. Find equilibrium income.
4. If full employment income is 640, how far below it is your answer to question 3?
5. C 300, I 80, G 90, X 100, M 110. Find AD.
6. State net exports in question 5.
7. Disposable income 250, consumption 215. Find saving as a fraction of disposable income.
8. Which curve shifts if wage costs rise, and in which direction?
9. Does a rise in the price level move AD or shift it?
10. Explain why investment can fall when growth merely slows.

### Answers

1. 660 / 1.10 = **600**.
2. 18 / 600 = **3.0%**.
3. 0.25Y = 150, so Y = **600**.
4. 640 - 600 = **40** below.
5. 300 + 80 + 90 + (100 - 110) = **460**.
6. 100 - 110 = **-10** (a deficit).
7. 35 / 250 = **0.14**.
8. Short-run AS shifts **left**.
9. A **movement along** AD.
10. Investment depends on the rate of change of demand; when the rate falls, firms need fewer new machines, so net investment falls even as output rises.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using nominal figures when real is asked, or dividing by the index without the 100.
- Writing S = I as the equilibrium condition instead of J = W.
- Moving a curve for a change in the price level.
- Shifting AD for a cost shock, or short-run AS for a demand shock.
- Leaving axes and curves unnamed, or giving no equilibrium labels.
- Treating saving and investment as the same thing.
- Describing net exports as exports only.
- Stating a conclusion about a shock with no reference to spare capacity or the time period.
