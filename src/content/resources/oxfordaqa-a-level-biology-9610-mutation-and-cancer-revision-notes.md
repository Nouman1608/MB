These notes condense section 3.2.11 (Mutation and cancer) of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Biology (9610) specification, Version 5.1, in Unit 2. For worked mutation predictions, risk data and drug examples, use the [mutation and cancer study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-biology-9610-mutation-and-cancer/); then try the [mutation and cancer practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-biology-9610-mutation-and-cancer-practice/).

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Gene mutation | A change in the base sequence of DNA |
| Mutagen | A physical or chemical agent that increases the frequency of mutations |
| Base substitution | One base is replaced by another |
| Base deletion | One base is lost from the sequence |
| Degenerate code | Most amino acids are coded for by more than one triplet |
| Tumour | A mass of cells formed by uncontrolled cell division |
| Proto-oncogene | A gene that stimulates cell division |
| Oncogene | A mutated proto-oncogene that stimulates cells to divide too quickly |
| Tumour suppressor gene | A gene that slows cell division |

## Mutations (3.2.11.1)

- Arise spontaneously during DNA replication.
- Mutagens raise the rate: for example ultraviolet light, ionising radiation such as X-rays, some chemicals in tobacco smoke.
- A mutation in a body cell is copied to all cells produced from it by mitosis.

| Mutation | Possible outcomes |
|---|---|
| Substitution | Same amino acid (degenerate code); one amino acid changed; a stop codon, ending the chain early |
| Deletion | Reading frame shifts; every triplet after the deletion changes; usually many amino acids change |

**Method for predictions:** split into triplets from the start; make the change; re-split from the start; translate with the codons given; compare.

**Worked reminder.** mRNA AUG AAA GGU UAA codes Met-Lys-Gly. With AAA → AAG (both Lys), there is no change. With GGU → UGU (Cys), Gly is replaced by Cys. With AAA → UAA (stop), the chain is just Met.

**Sequence to function:** new amino acid sequence → different primary structure → may change folding and tertiary structure → for an enzyme, active site shape may change → substrate no longer fits.

## Tumours (3.2.11.2)

| | Benign | Malignant |
|---|---|---|
| Growth | Slow | Rapid |
| Capsule | Often present | Absent |
| Invasion | Does not invade surrounding tissue | Invades surrounding tissue |
| Spread | Stays in one place | Cells spread in blood or lymph and form secondary tumours |
| Cells | Resemble normal cells | Less like normal cells, often larger nuclei |

Benign tumours can still be harmful by pressing on organs.

## Control of division

| Gene | Normal role | After mutation |
|---|---|---|
| Proto-oncogene | Stimulates division | Becomes an oncogene: division too fast |
| Tumour suppressor gene | Slows division | Inactivated: division increases |

Several mutations usually accumulate in one cell line before a malignant tumour forms; risk rises with age and exposure to mutagens.

## Risk-factor data for cancers

- Compare rates per 100 000, not raw numbers.
- Calculate ratios or percentage increases.
- Describe the trend, then evaluate: confounding variables, sample size, self-reported data, a plausible mechanism (mutagens damage DNA).
- Correlation does not prove cause.

## Cancer drugs and the cell cycle

| Drug blocks | Stage | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| DNA replication | S phase | Cell cannot complete the cycle |
| Spindle formation | Mitosis | Chromatids not separated; cells build up at metaphase |

- Cancer cells divide often, so more are affected.
- Fast-dividing normal cells (hair follicles, gut lining) are also affected: side effects.

## Exam traps

- "Every mutation changes the protein." Degenerate code.
- Re-splitting triplets from the mutation instead of from the start.
- "A deletion changes one amino acid." It shifts the frame.
- "A tumour suppressor gene mutation makes it overactive." It is inactivated.
- "Oncogenes are normal genes." Proto-oncogenes are normal; oncogenes are mutated.
- "Benign tumours are harmless." They can press on organs.
- "Cancer drugs only affect cancer cells."

## Quick self-test

1. Define a gene mutation.
2. When do spontaneous mutations arise?
3. Give two examples of mutagens.
4. Why might a base substitution have no effect on a protein?
5. Explain why a base deletion usually has a larger effect than a substitution.
6. mRNA: AUG UUU GGA UAA. Codons: AUG Met; UUU, UUC Phe; GGA, GGU Gly; UAA stop. Predict the effect of UUU → UUC.
7. Using the same mRNA, predict the effect of deleting the fifth base.
8. Give two differences between benign and malignant tumours.
9. What is an oncogene?
10. Explain how inactivating a tumour suppressor gene can lead to a tumour.
11. Why do drugs that block spindle formation affect cancer cells more than most normal cells?
12. Why can anticancer drugs cause hair loss?

### Answers

1. A change in the base sequence of DNA.
2. During DNA replication.
3. Any two: ultraviolet light; ionising radiation such as X-rays; some chemicals in tobacco smoke.
4. The genetic code is degenerate, so the new triplet may code for the same amino acid.
5. A deletion shifts the reading frame, changing every triplet after it; a substitution changes only one triplet.
6. **No change**: UUC also codes for Phe.
7. New sequence AUG UUG GAU AA...: from codon 2 onwards every triplet changes, so the amino acids after Met change (the codons given do not include UUG and GAU, but they are no longer Phe and Gly), and the original stop codon is lost.
8. Any two: malignant tumours grow faster; malignant tumours invade surrounding tissue; malignant tumour cells spread to form secondary tumours; benign tumours are often encapsulated; malignant cells look less like normal cells.
9. A mutated proto-oncogene that stimulates cells to divide too quickly.
10. Its protein no longer slows cell division, so the rate of division increases; the cell and its descendants divide uncontrollably, forming a tumour.
11. Cancer cells divide more often, so a larger proportion are in mitosis at any time.
12. Hair follicle cells divide frequently, so they are also affected by drugs that act on dividing cells.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need to learn the genetic code?**
No. The specification says you will be provided with suitable information about the genetic code when predicting the effect of a mutation.

**Are mutations in body cells passed on to children?**
No. A mutation in a body cell is passed to the cells produced from it by mitosis, which is how it can lead to a tumour, but not to offspring.
