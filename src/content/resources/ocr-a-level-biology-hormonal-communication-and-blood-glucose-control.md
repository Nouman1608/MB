## 5.1.4(a) Endocrine communication

A **hormone** is a chemical messenger made by an **endocrine gland**, a gland with no duct. Hormones pass straight into the blood. Communication has three stages:

1. **Secretion.** The gland cells release the hormone into the blood capillaries around them, usually by exocytosis.
2. **Transport.** The blood carries the hormone to every part of the body. Every tissue is reached, but only some respond.
3. **Detection.** **Target cells** carry **receptors** with a shape complementary to the hormone. Other cells ignore it.

Water-soluble hormones (insulin, glucagon, adrenaline) cannot cross the phospholipid bilayer. Their receptor is in the cell surface membrane, and it triggers an internal signalling pathway, often with a second messenger. Steroid hormones (cortisol, aldosterone) are lipid-soluble, so they cross the membrane and bind receptors inside the cell. The complex alters gene transcription, so the response is slower.

Compared with the nervous system, endocrine communication is slower, lasts longer and acts widely.

## 5.1.4(b) The adrenal glands

There are two adrenal glands, one above each kidney. Each has an outer **cortex** and an inner **medulla**.

| Region | Hormones | Main functions |
|---|---|---|
| Cortex | **Mineralocorticoids**, mainly aldosterone | Raise sodium ion reabsorption and potassium ion loss in the kidney tubules, which helps maintain blood volume and blood pressure |
| Cortex | **Glucocorticoids**, mainly cortisol | Raise blood glucose (including by gluconeogenesis), reduce inflammation, help with long-term stress |
| Medulla | **Adrenaline** and noradrenaline | Short-term "fight or flight" preparation |

The medulla is stimulated directly by sympathetic neurones, so it links the nervous and endocrine systems.

Adrenaline raises heart rate, widens the bronchioles, diverts blood to muscles and away from the gut, and triggers **glycogenolysis**, so more glucose is available for respiration. It raises blood glucose, so it opposes insulin.

## 5.1.4(c) Histology of the pancreas

The pancreas is a mixed gland with two tissues.

- **Exocrine tissue** makes up most of the organ. Clusters of cells called **acini** (singular acinus) make digestive enzymes, which leave through ducts into the small intestine. Acinar cells contain zymogen granules and stain darkly.
- **Endocrine tissue** forms the **islets of Langerhans**: small, pale-staining patches scattered through the acini. Islets have no ducts. They have a rich capillary network, so hormones enter the blood quickly.

Two cell types in the islets matter for this topic.

| Cell type | Hormone made | Triggered by |
|---|---|---|
| Alpha cells | Glucagon | Low blood glucose |
| Beta cells | Insulin | High blood glucose |

With a routine stain you can find the islets, but you cannot tell alpha from beta cells. That needs special stains or antibody labelling.

### Examining and drawing a section (PAG1, HSW4)

1. On low power, find a pale islet among the darker acini.
2. Make a **tissue plan**: outlines only, with no individual cells.
3. Switch to high power and make a **cell drawing** of a small area, showing a few cells and nuclei.
4. Draw large, in pencil, with unbroken lines and no shading.
5. Add ruled label lines that do not cross.
6. Add a title, then record the magnification and a scale bar.

**Worked example: magnification from a scale bar.** Your drawing of an islet is 48 mm across. The scale bar beside it is 20 mm long and is labelled 100 micrometres (µm).

1. Actual width = 48 ÷ 20 × 100 = **240 µm**.
2. Magnification = drawn length ÷ actual length = 20 mm ÷ 0.100 mm = **×200**.

Convert units first: 1 mm is 1000 µm.

## 5.1.4(d) Regulating blood glucose

Too little glucose starves cells. Too much lowers the water potential of the blood. In a healthy fasting person the concentration is roughly 4 to 6 mmol dm⁻³.

### Negative feedback with insulin and glucagon

Beta and alpha cells detect blood glucose directly. The liver and muscle are the main effectors.

| | Blood glucose too high | Blood glucose too low |
|---|---|---|
| Detected by | Beta cells | Alpha cells |
| Hormone | Insulin released | Glucagon released |
| Response | More glucose enters cells; liver and muscle convert glucose to glycogen; more glucose is respired | Liver converts glycogen to glucose and makes glucose from non-carbohydrates |
| Result | Blood glucose falls | Blood glucose rises |

This is **negative feedback**: the response reverses the original change, and as the level returns to normal the stimulus weakens, so less hormone is released. Two opposing hormones give finer control.

### The role of the liver

The liver receives blood from the gut through the hepatic portal vein, so it sees absorbed glucose first. Its cells (hepatocytes) carry out three processes:

- **Glycogenesis**: glucose to glycogen, lowering blood glucose.
- **Glycogenolysis**: glycogen to glucose, raising blood glucose.
- **Gluconeogenesis**: glucose made from non-carbohydrate sources such as amino acids and glycerol, raising blood glucose.

Insulin promotes glycogenesis. Glucagon promotes glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis. In muscle and fat, insulin also increases the number of glucose transporters in the surface membrane.

Glucagon binds to a receptor on the hepatocyte surface, which activates **adenylyl cyclase**. This makes cyclic AMP (cAMP) from ATP, and cAMP acts as a second messenger that starts an enzyme cascade. Adrenaline uses the same type of pathway. The signal is amplified at each step.

### Control of insulin secretion in beta cells

Beta cells secrete insulin only when glucose is high. The mechanism links glucose metabolism to the membrane.

1. Glucose enters the beta cell by facilitated diffusion through a glucose transporter. More glucose outside means more glucose inside.
2. The cell respires it, so the **ATP** concentration rises.
3. ATP closes **potassium ion channels** in the surface membrane. Potassium ions can no longer leave.
4. The inside of the cell becomes less negative: the membrane **depolarises**.
5. Depolarisation opens **voltage-gated calcium ion channels**. Calcium ions diffuse in.
6. The rise in calcium ion concentration makes vesicles containing insulin fuse with the surface membrane. Insulin is released by **exocytosis**.

At low glucose, little ATP is made, the potassium channels stay open, the membrane stays polarised, the calcium channels stay shut and little insulin is released.

**Worked example: islet data.** Isolated islets were kept in solutions of different glucose concentration, and insulin released was measured as picomoles per islet per hour.

| Glucose / mmol dm⁻³ | 2 | 5 | 8 | 11 | 14 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Insulin released | 0.4 | 0.5 | 1.8 | 4.6 | 5.0 |

At 5 mmol dm⁻³, adding a drug that closes potassium channels gave 4.2. At 11 mmol dm⁻³, adding a calcium channel blocker gave 0.6.

(i) Percentage increase from 5 to 11 mmol dm⁻³: (4.6 − 0.5) ÷ 0.5 × 100 = **820%**.

(ii) Mean change from 8 to 11 mmol dm⁻³: (4.6 − 1.8) ÷ 3 = **0.93 pmol islet⁻¹ h⁻¹ per mmol dm⁻³**.

(iii) The potassium channel blocker raised release 4.2 ÷ 0.5 = **8.4 times** at low glucose, so closing potassium channels is enough to cause release.

(iv) The calcium blocker cut release by (4.6 − 0.6) ÷ 4.6 × 100 = **87%**, so calcium entry is needed.


## 5.1.4(e) Type 1 and Type 2 diabetes mellitus

In diabetes mellitus blood glucose stays too high (hyperglycaemia). Symptoms include thirst, frequent urination and weight loss. Glucose appears in the urine when it exceeds what the kidney tubules can reabsorb.

| | Type 1 | Type 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Cause | Autoimmune destruction of beta cells, so little or no insulin is made. Genetic factors and an environmental trigger are thought to contribute | Target cells respond less to insulin (insulin resistance); beta cells may later fail to keep up |
| Risk factors | Family history | Obesity, inactivity, poor diet, age, family history and ethnic background |
| Insulin level | Very low | Normal or high at first |
| Main treatment | Insulin injections or a pump, blood glucose monitoring, carbohydrate management | Diet change, weight loss and exercise; drugs if needed; insulin later in some patients |

**Worked example: reading fasting data.** Three adults were tested after fasting.

| Person | Blood glucose / mmol dm⁻³ | Insulin / pmol dm⁻³ |
|---|---|---|
| P | 4.9 | 58 |
| Q | 13.8 | 6 |
| R | 9.6 | 210 |

P has normal values. Q has high glucose and almost no insulin, which fits Type 1: the beta cells are not making it. R has high glucose and a high insulin level, 210 ÷ 58 = **3.6 times** that of P. Insulin is present but not working properly, which fits Type 2 insulin resistance. 

### Treatments used

Type 2 drugs include **metformin**, which reduces glucose production by the liver and makes cells more responsive to insulin, and **sulfonylureas**, which close the potassium channels in beta cells and so stimulate insulin release (the same step as in the data above). 

Type 1 is the specification's example of medical technology regulating a hormonal system. A sensor measures glucose and insulin is delivered by injection or a pump. Some systems link the two so that the dose adjusts automatically, mimicking negative feedback.

## 5.1.4(f) Potential treatments

### Insulin from genetically modified bacteria

Insulin once came from pigs and cattle. Human insulin is now made by genetically modified bacteria.

1. Obtain the human insulin gene without introns, because bacteria cannot remove them. It can be made from mRNA by reverse transcriptase or built from the known sequence.
2. Cut the gene and a plasmid with the same **restriction enzyme**, giving matching sticky ends, and join them with **DNA ligase**.
3. Insert the recombinant plasmid into bacteria. A marker gene, for example for antibiotic resistance, shows which took it up.
4. Grow the bacteria in a fermenter so that they express the gene.
5. Extract and purify the insulin, which must be in its active form.

Advantages: the insulin has the human amino acid sequence, so it is less likely to trigger an immune response; supply is large and reliable; and no animal tissue is needed, which avoids ethical and religious objections.

### Stem cells

**Stem cells** can divide and differentiate. The idea is to grow them into beta cells or islet tissue and transplant these into a patient, so that the body regulates insulin itself. The evidence for this is still developing: trials in people have reported patients who no longer needed insulin injections, but the patients also needed immunosuppressant drugs, and it is not yet routine care.

- **Benefit:** a possible cure for Type 1 rather than lifelong management.
- **Rejection:** donor-derived cells are rejected unless the immune system is suppressed. Cells made from the patient's own cells would avoid this.
- **Autoimmunity:** the immune attack that destroyed the original beta cells could target the new ones.
- **Ethics and safety:** embryonic stem cells raise objections, and uncontrolled division could cause tumours.

## Common errors

- Writing that the pancreas "converts glucose to glycogen". The liver and muscle do that, under the influence of insulin.
- Saying insulin enters cells. It binds to a surface receptor.
- Muddling the beta cell sequence: glucose, ATP, potassium channels close, depolarisation, calcium channels open, exocytosis.
