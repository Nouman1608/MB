This guide covers subtopic **3.2, Ionic bonding**, from Topic 3, Chemical bonding, of Cambridge International AS & A Level Chemistry 9701, 2025–2027 series. It is AS Level content. The broader [Chemical Bonding: Electronegativity, Ionic and Metallic Bonds](/resources/as-chemical-bonding-electronegativity-ionic-and-metallic-bonds/) guide introduces the definition; this page goes further into how to describe ionic bonding precisely, how to work out ion ratios, and how to explain differences in the strength of ionic lattices. When you have worked through it, use the [ionic bonding revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-chemistry-9701-as-ionic-bonding-revision-notes/) and the [ionic bonding practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-chemistry-9701-as-ionic-bonding-practice/).

## Syllabus coverage

**3.2 Ionic bonding.** Candidates should be able to:

1. define ionic bonding as the electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions (positively charged cations and negatively charged anions)
2. describe ionic bonding including the examples of sodium chloride, magnesium oxide and calcium fluoride

Two things follow from this wording. First, the definition is about **attraction between ions**, not about electron transfer: transfer is how the ions form, but the bond is the attraction that exists afterwards. Second, "including" means the three named compounds are the minimum. You can be asked to describe the bonding in any analogous compound, such as lithium oxide or potassium sulfide, using the same method.

## The definition, word by word

A full-mark definition has three parts:

- **electrostatic attraction** (the force is between charges, not a shared pair of electrons)
- between **oppositely charged ions**
- named as **cations** (positive) and **anions** (negative)

The attraction acts **in all directions**. A sodium ion does not "belong" to one particular chloride ion; it attracts every chloride ion around it and repels the sodium ions further away. That is why ionic compounds exist as **giant lattices** rather than as separate molecules, and why "a molecule of sodium chloride" is wrong. The formula NaCl is the simplest ratio of ions in the lattice.

## How the ions form

Ionic bonding is favoured when the two elements have a **large difference in electronegativity**, typically a reactive metal with a reactive non-metal. The metal atom loses its outer-shell electrons and the non-metal atom gains electrons, so that both ions usually end up with the electron configuration of a noble gas.

At AS Level you should write the configurations in full sub-shell notation, not only as 2,8,1-style shell numbers.

| Atom | Configuration | Ion | Configuration of ion | Same as |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Na | 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s¹ | Na⁺ | 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ | neon |
| Cl | 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁵ | Cl⁻ | 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ | argon |
| Mg | 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² | Mg²⁺ | 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ | neon |
| O | 1s² 2s² 2p⁴ | O²⁻ | 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ | neon |
| Ca | 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ 4s² | Ca²⁺ | 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ | argon |
| F | 1s² 2s² 2p⁵ | F⁻ | 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ | neon |

Notice that Na⁺, Mg²⁺, O²⁻ and F⁻ are **isoelectronic**: they all have ten electrons. They are not the same size, because their nuclear charges differ (+11, +12, +8 and +9). With the same number of electrons, a larger nuclear charge pulls the electrons closer, so Mg²⁺ is smaller than Na⁺, and O²⁻ is larger than F⁻.

## The three named examples

**Sodium chloride, NaCl.** Each sodium atom transfers its single 3s electron to a chlorine atom, which completes its 3p sub-shell. The ions are Na⁺ and Cl⁻ in a 1 : 1 ratio. In the lattice, each Na⁺ is surrounded by six Cl⁻ ions and each Cl⁻ by six Na⁺ ions.

**Magnesium oxide, MgO.** Each magnesium atom transfers both 3s electrons to one oxygen atom, filling its 2p sub-shell. The ions are Mg²⁺ and O²⁻, again in a 1 : 1 ratio, and they adopt the same six-around-six arrangement as sodium chloride.

**Calcium fluoride, CaF₂.** Each calcium atom loses its two 4s electrons, but a fluorine atom needs only one electron to fill its 2p sub-shell. So one calcium atom gives one electron to **each of two** fluorine atoms. The ions are Ca²⁺ and F⁻ in a 1 : 2 ratio. In the lattice each Ca²⁺ is surrounded by eight F⁻ ions and each F⁻ by four Ca²⁺ ions; the numbers differ because there are twice as many fluoride ions.

The ratio always comes from **charge balance**: the total positive charge must equal the total negative charge.

## Worked example 1: describing an analogous compound

**Question.** Describe the bonding in lithium oxide. Include the electron configurations of the ions formed. [4]

**Model answer, annotated.**

- Each lithium atom (1s² 2s¹) loses its 2s electron to form Li⁺ (1s²). *(one mark: cation and its configuration)*
- An oxygen atom (1s² 2s² 2p⁴) gains two electrons, one from each of two lithium atoms, to form O²⁻ (1s² 2s² 2p⁶). *(one mark: anion, configuration and the source of the electrons)*
- So the ratio is two Li⁺ to one O²⁻, giving Li₂O. *(one mark: ratio linked to charge)*
- The ionic bond is the electrostatic attraction between the oppositely charged Li⁺ and O²⁻ ions, acting in all directions in a giant lattice. *(one mark: the definition, applied)*

Note that Li⁺ has the configuration of helium, not neon. Students who learn "ions get eight outer electrons" often go wrong here.

## Worked example 2: deducing a formula

**Question.** Magnesium reacts with nitrogen to form an ionic nitride. Deduce its formula and explain your reasoning. [2]

Magnesium forms Mg²⁺; nitrogen (1s² 2s² 2p³) gains three electrons to form N³⁻. The lowest whole-number ratio that balances the charges is 3 × (+2) = +6 and 2 × (−3) = −6, so the formula is **Mg₃N₂**. Six electrons are transferred in total, from three magnesium atoms to two nitrogen atoms.

## What makes an ionic lattice stronger

The syllabus does not ask you to calculate lattice energies at AS (that is Topic 23, A Level). You are still expected to explain physical properties from bonding in Topic 4, so you need the qualitative rule:

**The electrostatic attraction between ions is stronger when the ions have a larger charge and a smaller radius.**

Compare sodium chloride with magnesium oxide:

- Mg²⁺ and O²⁻ each carry a double charge, compared with single charges on Na⁺ and Cl⁻.
- Mg²⁺ is smaller than Na⁺, and O²⁻ is smaller than Cl⁻, so the ions in MgO are closer together.

Both factors make the attraction in MgO much stronger, so far more energy is needed to separate the ions. Magnesium oxide melts at about 2850 °C, while sodium chloride melts at 801 °C. When you explain this, always name **both** factors and say they increase the strength of the **electrostatic attraction between the ions**; "MgO has stronger bonds" earns nothing on its own.

## Ionic bonding and electronegativity

Using the Pauling values in the data booklet (Na 0.9, Mg 1.3, Ca 1.0, O 3.5, F 4.0, Cl 3.0), the differences are 2.1 for NaCl, 2.2 for MgO and 3.0 for CaF₂. All three are large, which is why each is treated as ionic. The syllabus states that covalent character in ionic compounds is not assessed, so you will not be asked to explain why some compounds are "partly" ionic.

## Common mistakes

- **Defining ionic bonding as "the transfer of electrons".** Transfer forms the ions; the bond is the electrostatic attraction between them.
- **Writing "NaCl molecules".** Ionic compounds are giant lattices; the formula is a ratio.
- **Getting the CaF₂ transfer wrong.** One calcium atom gives one electron to each of two fluorine atoms, not two electrons to one fluorine atom.
- **Assuming every ion has eight outer electrons.** Li⁺ has the helium configuration, 1s².
- **Explaining melting points with charge only.** Ionic radius is the second factor and is expected.
- **Saying the ions "want" a full shell.** Describe what happens to the electrons and the attraction that results.

## Exam technique for 3.2 command words

- **Define**: give the formal wording, including "electrostatic attraction" and "oppositely charged ions".
- **Describe**: say which atom loses how many electrons, which gains them, the ions formed with their charges, and the attraction between them. A labelled dot-and-cross diagram (subtopic 3.7) is often accepted as part of a description.
- **Explain** (usually about properties): link the strength of the attraction to ion charge and size, then to the energy needed.
- **Deduce**: show the charge balance that leads to the formula.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is the ionic bond the same thing as electron transfer?**
No. Electron transfer is how the ions are formed. The ionic bond is the electrostatic attraction between the oppositely charged ions in the lattice.

**Why does CaF₂ have twice as many fluoride ions as calcium ions?**
A calcium ion carries a 2+ charge and a fluoride ion a 1− charge, so two fluoride ions are needed to balance one calcium ion.

**Do I need lattice energy for subtopic 3.2?**
No. Lattice energy and Born–Haber cycles are A Level content in Topic 23. At AS you explain lattice strength qualitatively using ion charge and ionic radius.
