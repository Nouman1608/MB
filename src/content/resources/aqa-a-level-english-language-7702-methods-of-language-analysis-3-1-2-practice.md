These questions practise 3.1.2 Methods of language analysis from AQA A-level English Language (7702). Section B uses
two **original** texts written for this page.

**Text D**: the opening of a charity appeal email.

> *"Every night this winter, somewhere in our city, a child will go to bed hungry. It doesn't have to be this way.
> For just £5 a month, you could put a hot meal on the table of a family who has run out of options. Will you help
> us tonight?"*

**Text E**: a transcript of two friends planning a trip. Key: (.) micropause; (2) pause in seconds; capitals mark
stress; a square bracket opening two lines marks overlap.

> JO: so are we doing Saturday or (.) or what
> SAM: I mean I CAN do Saturday [but
> JO: [but you don't WANT to
> SAM: no it's not that (2) it's just my mum's got this thing

## Section A

**1.** Identify the word class of each word in bold: *"She **quickly** **opened** the **ancient** **door**."* **[4]**

**2.** Analyse the structure of the noun phrase *"the first warm evening of the summer"*, naming the head, the
determiner, the pre-modifiers and the post-modifier. **[4]**

**3.** Identify the sentence function and the sentence structure of each: (a) *"Stay where you are."* (b) *"Although
it rained, the match continued."* (c) *"What a day!"* **[6]**

**4.** Explain the difference between *"The window was broken"* and *"The window was being broken"*, using the terms
voice and aspect. **[3]**

**5.** Divide *"unhelpfulness"* into morphemes, and classify each as free or bound, stating whether each bound morpheme
is a prefix or a suffix. **[4]**

**6.** A speaker answers *"Did you enjoy the film?"* with *"Well, the popcorn was nice."* Explain the implicature using
one of Grice's maxims. **[3]**

**7.** Explain two features that show the cohesion of this sequence: *"Mia bought a bike. It was blue. She rode it
everywhere."* **[2]**

## Section B

**8.** Identify and describe **two** grammatical features in Text D, explaining how each contributes to its purpose.

## Answers

**1.** "quickly": adverb [1]; "opened": verb (dynamic, past tense) [1]; "ancient": adjective [1]; "door": noun
(concrete, common) [1].

**2.** Head: "evening" [1]. Determiner: "the" [1]. Pre-modifiers: "first" (an ordinal number functioning as a modifier)
and "warm" (adjective) [1]. Post-modifier: the prepositional phrase "of the summer" [1].

**3.** (a) Imperative function [1]; simple structure, one main clause [1]. (b) Declarative function [1]; complex
structure, a subordinate clause beginning "Although" plus a main clause [1]. (c) Exclamative function [1]; minor
sentence, as it has no main verb [1].

**4.** Both are in the passive voice: the subject "the window" is affected by the action and the agent is not named
[1]. The first is a simple past form with no progressive aspect, presenting the breaking as a completed event [1]; the second uses the past
progressive, presenting the breaking as ongoing at a point in the past [1].

**5.** un- + help + -ful + -ness [1]. "help" is a free morpheme [1]. "un-" is a bound prefix [1]. "-ful" and "-ness" are
bound suffixes [1].

**6.** The answer flouts the maxim of relation (or quantity), because it comments on the popcorn rather than the film
[1]. The listener assumes the speaker is still cooperating, so infers an implied meaning [1]: the speaker did not
enjoy the film but avoids saying so directly, protecting the other person's face [1].

**7.** Anaphoric pronoun reference: "It" and "it" refer back to "a bike", and "She" to "Mia" [1]. Chronological
ordering of simple declaratives links the events in sequence, so the reader reads them as a connected narrative [1].

**8.** Two features, each 1 mark for accurate description and 1 for function. The modal verb "could" in "you could
put a hot meal" [1] presents giving as a possibility within the reader's power, flattering their agency without
commanding [1]. The future "will go to bed hungry", introduced by the fronted adverbials "Every night this winter,
somewhere in our city" [1], presents hunger as certain and close at hand, creating urgency [1]. (Also creditable:
the simple declarative "It doesn't have to be this way" as a pivot from problem to solution; the interrogative
closing.)

**9.** Up to 5 points, 1 mark each: concrete, domestic lexis ("bed", "hot meal", "table") makes hunger tangible and
familiar [1]; the noun "child" is singular and unnamed, representing an individual the reader can picture while
remaining any child [1]; "hungry" is plain and unhedged, avoiding euphemism [1]; the idiom "run out of options"
represents the family as having tried, pre-empting blame [1]; the minimising adverb "just" with "£5 a month" frames
the cost as small [1].

**10.** Up to 5 points, 1 mark each: "It doesn't have to be this way" presupposes that the situation can be changed
[1]; "a family who has run out of options" presupposes that families have exhausted other help [1]; the final
interrogative is a direct request performed as a question, an indirect speech act that softens the imposition on
the reader's negative face [1]; it is not purely rhetorical, since it seeks an answer in action (a donation) [1];
"tonight" adds deictic immediacy, implying that delay has consequences [1].

**11.** Up to 6 points, 1 mark each: Jo's opening discourse marker "so" and the trailing "or (.) or what" show
informal topic initiation between equals [1]; Sam's stress on "CAN" signals a contrast and implies reluctance [1]; Jo
overlaps and completes Sam's turn with "[but you don't WANT to", showing close familiarity and the confidence to
voice an inference [1]; Sam's "no it's not that" is a repair of Jo's interpretation, followed by a 2-second pause
suggesting hesitation before giving a reason [1]; Jo's "oh right" acknowledges the reason and the quick shift to
"Sunday then" shows cooperative accommodation [1]; the closing adjacency pair (proposal and acceptance, "Sunday's
perfect yeah") resolves the exchange with mutual agreement, showing a symmetrical, friendly relationship [1].

**12.** Example rewrite [1]: "Text D frames the problem in a complex declarative, with the fronted adverbials 'Every
night this winter, somewhere in our city', before ending on a direct interrogative, 'Will you help us tonight?',
which turns the appeal into a personal request that expects an answer in action." Improvements, 1 mark each up to
3: it names structures precisely instead of relying on length [1]; it quotes evidence [1]; it explains the effect
in relation to purpose and audience instead of the vague "more effective" [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Mislabelling the question in Text D as purely rhetorical when it requests action.
- Describing Text E's pause or stress without saying what it implies about attitude.
- Naming word classes correctly but not saying why the pattern matters.
- Confusing tense with aspect, or the passive with the past.
