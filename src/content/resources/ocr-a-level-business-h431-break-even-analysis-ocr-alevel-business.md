## The terms and formulae

| Term | Meaning | Formula |
|---|---|---|
| Contribution per unit | What each unit adds towards fixed costs and profit | Price − variable cost per unit |
| Total contribution | Contribution per unit × quantity | |
| Break-even output | The output at which total revenue equals total cost, so profit is zero | Fixed costs ÷ contribution per unit |
| Break-even revenue | Revenue at break-even | Break-even output × price |
| Margin of safety | How far sales can fall before a loss | Actual (or budgeted) output − break-even output |
| Margin of safety (%) | | Margin of safety ÷ actual output × 100 |
| Target level of profit | The output needed for a chosen profit | (Fixed costs + target profit) ÷ contribution per unit |
| Profit at any output | | (Contribution per unit × output) − fixed costs |

### Worked example 1: the main calculations

Cobb Candles sells scented candles at £12. The variable cost of each is £5. Fixed costs are £42,000 a year. The business plans to sell 8,000 candles.

- **Contribution per candle** = £12 − £5 = **£7**.
- **Break-even output** = £42,000 ÷ £7 = **6,000 candles**.
- **Break-even revenue** = 6,000 × £12 = **£72,000**.
- **Margin of safety** = 8,000 − 6,000 = **2,000 candles**, which is 2,000 ÷ 8,000 × 100 = **25%** of planned sales.
- **Profit at 8,000** = (£7 × 8,000) − £42,000 = £56,000 − £42,000 = **£14,000**.
- **Target profit of £21,000**: output = (£42,000 + £21,000) ÷ £7 = **9,000 candles**.

Interpretation: Cobb needs to sell 6,000 candles before it makes a penny of profit. Each candle sold above that adds £7. Sales can fall by a quarter before the business starts to lose money. To reach the £21,000 target, it must sell 1,000 more than planned.

## The graph

A break-even chart has output on the horizontal axis and money (£) on the vertical axis. To construct it for Cobb Candles:

1. Draw the **fixed cost line**: horizontal at £42,000.
2. Draw the **total cost line**: starts at £42,000 when output is zero, rising by £5 for each candle.
3. Draw the **total revenue line**: starts at the origin, rising by £12 for each candle.
4. Mark where revenue and total cost cross: the break-even point.
5. Label the loss area (left of the point) and the profit area (right of it).
6. Mark planned output (8,000) and the margin of safety as the gap between planned output and break-even output.

Points to plot:

| Output | Fixed cost | Total cost | Total revenue | Profit or loss |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | £42,000 | £42,000 | £0 | −£42,000 |
| 2,000 | £42,000 | £52,000 | £24,000 | −£28,000 |
| 4,000 | £42,000 | £62,000 | £48,000 | −£14,000 |
| 6,000 | £42,000 | £72,000 | £72,000 | £0 |
| 8,000 | £42,000 | £82,000 | £96,000 | £14,000 |
| 10,000 | £42,000 | £92,000 | £120,000 | £28,000 |

The lines cross at 6,000 candles and £72,000. The vertical gap between revenue and total cost at 8,000 candles is £14,000, which is the profit. The graph shows the profit or loss at any output at a glance, which a formula does not.

## Worked example 2: changes in costs and price

Starting with break-even of 6,000 candles (contribution £7):

| Change | New contribution | New break-even | Effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fixed costs rise to £49,000 | £7 | 49,000 ÷ 7 = 7,000 | Margin of safety falls to 1,000 |
| Variable cost rises to £5.50 | £6.50 | 42,000 ÷ 6.50 = 6,462 (rounded up) | Break-even up by 462 |
| Price rises to £13 | £8 | 42,000 ÷ 8 = 5,250 | Break-even down by 750 |
| Price falls to £11 | £6 | 42,000 ÷ 6 = 7,000 | Margin of safety falls to 1,000 |

Break-even is rounded up to a whole unit because a part-candle cannot be sold. Higher costs raise the break-even point and shrink the margin of safety. A higher price lowers break-even, but only if demand holds.

## Worked example 3: price rise and demand

Cobb considers raising its price to £13. Break-even falls to 5,250, which looks like good news. But suppose a higher price cuts sales from 8,000 to 7,000.

- Profit at £13 and 7,000 candles = (£8 × 7,000) − £42,000 = £56,000 − £42,000 = **£14,000**.
- This equals the profit at £12 and 8,000 candles (£14,000).

The price rise leaves profit unchanged, but at a lower output, so the business works less hard for the same profit, and its break-even point is lower (5,250 against 6,000). However, the margin of safety is 7,000 − 5,250 = 1,750, or 25% of 7,000, so it is the same percentage as before. If sales at £13 fell below 7,000, profit would be lower than at £12 and 8,000. Whether the rise is worthwhile depends on how sensitive customers are to price.

## Worked example 4: stepped fixed costs

Cobb's workshop can produce 10,000 candles. For a higher output it must rent a second workshop, which adds £18,000 a year. Fixed costs are therefore **stepped**: £42,000 up to 10,000 candles, and £60,000 above.

- Profit at 10,000 candles = £7 × 10,000 − £42,000 = **£28,000**.
- Profit at 12,000 candles = £7 × 12,000 − £60,000 = £84,000 − £60,000 = **£24,000**.

Producing 2,000 more candles lowers profit by £4,000, because the step in fixed costs is not yet covered. The second workshop's extra cost of £18,000 needs 18,000 ÷ 7 = 2,572 extra candles to repay (rounded up from 2,571.4). Output must reach (£60,000 + £28,000) ÷ £7 = 12,572 candles (rounded up) to match the £28,000 earned at 10,000. A **stepped fixed cost** is a fixed cost that stays constant over a range of output, then jumps to a new level when capacity is exceeded. It means the break-even model is only valid within a range of output.

## Worked example 5: a special order

A hotel chain offers to buy 1,500 candles at £8 each. Cobb is producing 8,000 and can make 10,000 with its existing workshop. Variable cost remains £5.

- Contribution per candle = £8 − £5 = £3.
- Extra contribution = 1,500 × £3 = **£4,500**, assuming fixed costs do not rise.
- Total output would be 9,500, within capacity.

Cobb should usually **accept** if: there is spare capacity, fixed costs do not rise, the order does not make existing customers want a lower price, and the extra work does not harm quality. It should **refuse or negotiate** if regular customers would find out and demand £8, or if the work uses capacity that could be sold at £12. If 1,500 of Cobb's regular £12 customers switched to the lower price, it would lose 1,500 × £4 = £6,000, more than the £4,500 gained. The special order is a short-run decision; in the long run every price must cover all costs.

## Lowering break-even

| Method | How it works | Risk |
|---|---|---|
| Raise price | Raises contribution per unit | Demand may fall |
| Cut variable cost | Cheaper materials or buying in bulk raises contribution | Quality and brand may suffer |
| Cut fixed costs | Smaller premises, fewer managers | Less capacity or service |
| Change the product mix | Sell more of the high-contribution items | Customers may not want them |

To **recommend and justify**, choose the method that gives the biggest effect with the least risk to demand. For Cobb, cutting fixed costs by £5,000 gives break-even of 37,000 ÷ 7 = 5,286 candles; raising contribution by £0.50 through cheaper wax gives 42,000 ÷ 7.5 = 5,600. The fixed cost cut has the larger effect (a fall of 714 candles against 400), but the cheaper wax risks the reputation of the product. Judgement: cut fixed costs first, because the business keeps its quality; use the wax saving only if it is invisible to customers.

## Evaluating usefulness

**Advantages.** Simple and quick; shows the risk level; helps with pricing and with loan applications (lenders can see the margin of safety); allows "what if" tests.

**Limitations.** Assumes one product at one price; assumes all output is sold; assumes variable cost per unit is constant (but bulk discounts change it); ignores stepped costs and time; depends on accurate forecasts; says nothing about market demand.

**Stakeholders.** Owners use it to set targets; lenders to judge risk; employees to see whether jobs are secure; managers to price.

A balanced answer says that break-even analysis is a good guide for a small business with a simple product, but is a poor substitute for market research.

## Common mistakes

- Using price instead of contribution to find break-even.
- Forgetting to round break-even output up.
- Mixing up break-even and margin of safety.
- Drawing the total cost line starting from zero instead of from fixed costs.
- Ignoring stepped costs in a long-range answer.

## Exam technique

Always write the formula, substitute, and state the unit. For graphs, label the axes, the lines and the break-even point. For "evaluate", link a change to the margin of safety and the profit, and give a judgement.
