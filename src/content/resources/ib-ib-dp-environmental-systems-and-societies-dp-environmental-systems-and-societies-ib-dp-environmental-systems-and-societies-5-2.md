## The food system

A **food system** includes every stage from farm to plate and beyond: inputs (seed, fertiliser, water, energy, labour), production, processing, packaging, transport, retail, consumption and waste disposal. Thinking in systems terms helps in two ways:

- Impacts occur at every stage, not only on the farm. Refrigeration, processing and cooking all use energy; packaging becomes waste.
- A change at one stage affects others. Cutting household waste reduces the land, water and fertiliser needed upstream.

Agriculture is the largest human use of land: crops and grazing together occupy a large share of the planet's habitable land. That is why food choices are central to biodiversity loss (Topic 3), water use (Topic 4) and greenhouse gas emissions (Topic 6).

## Nitrogen: essential and leaky

Nitrogen limits crop growth on most farms. The **Haber-Bosch process**, developed in the early twentieth century, made it possible to manufacture ammonia from atmospheric nitrogen on an industrial scale. Synthetic nitrogen fertiliser now supports a large part of global food production, but it is energy-intensive to make, and much of what is applied is not taken up by crops. The surplus is lost:

- as **nitrate** leached into groundwater and rivers, contributing to eutrophication (see the 4.4 guide);
- as **nitrous oxide**, a powerful greenhouse gas, released by soil bacteria;
- as **ammonia** gas to the air, which forms fine particles that harm health.

### Worked example: nitrogen use efficiency

A fictional wheat field receives 180 kg of nitrogen per hectare as fertiliser. The harvested grain contains 99 kg of nitrogen per hectare.

```
nitrogen use efficiency (NUE) = N in harvest ÷ N applied × 100
                              = 99 ÷ 180 × 100 = 55 %
nitrogen surplus              = 180 − 99 = 81 kg per hectare

after precision application cuts fertiliser to 140 kg ha⁻¹
with the same harvest:
NUE     = 99 ÷ 140 × 100 = 70.7 %
surplus = 140 − 99 = 41 kg per hectare
```

**Interpretation.** Almost half the surplus is removed by matching the amount and timing of fertiliser to crop needs, with no loss of yield. Methods include soil testing, splitting applications through the season, GPS-guided spreading and growing legumes or cover crops that supply or capture nitrogen. The calculation simplifies reality, because some nitrogen also comes from the soil, manure and the air, and some surplus is stored in soil organic matter rather than lost.

## Yield and land

Higher yields mean less land is needed for the same food. This is the basis of the land-sparing argument met in 3.3.

### Worked example: land needed for a harvest

A region needs 12 000 t of maize a year. Compare two systems with fictional yields.

```
low-input system:   yield 2.0 t ha⁻¹ → 12 000 ÷ 2.0 = 6 000 ha
high-input system:  yield 7.5 t ha⁻¹ → 12 000 ÷ 7.5 = 1 600 ha
land saved by intensifying = 6 000 − 1 600 = 4 400 ha (73.3 %)
```

**Interpretation.** Intensification could spare 4 400 ha for habitat. But the high-input system uses more fertiliser, pesticide, water and energy per hectare, and higher profits can encourage farmers to clear more land rather than spare it. A full answer weighs the land saved against the pollution and inputs, and asks whether the spared land is actually protected.

## The Green Revolution

From the 1940s to the 1970s, high-yielding varieties of wheat and rice were bred and spread, together with irrigation, synthetic fertilisers and pesticides. The plant breeder Norman Borlaug, who developed high-yielding, disease-resistant dwarf wheat, received the Nobel Peace Prize in 1970. Countries such as India and Mexico greatly increased grain production.

| Benefits | Costs |
|---|---|
| Large rise in grain output; famine risk reduced in many regions | Heavy use of fertiliser and pesticide, polluting water and harming non-target species |
| Less land needed per tonne of grain | Groundwater depletion from irrigation |
| Lower food prices for urban consumers | Fewer crop varieties grown, reducing genetic diversity |
| | Wealthier farmers who could afford inputs gained most; some smaller farmers fell into debt |

The Green Revolution is a key case for "to what extent" questions, because it shows technology raising food security while creating environmental and social problems.

## Genetically modified crops

A **genetically modified (GM)** crop has had its DNA altered directly, often by inserting a gene from another species. Two widely grown types:

- **Bt crops** (for example Bt cotton and maize) contain a gene from the bacterium *Bacillus thuringiensis* that makes a protein toxic to certain insect pests, reducing the need for insecticide sprays.
- **Herbicide-tolerant crops** survive spraying with a particular herbicide, making weed control simpler.

Arguments for: higher or more reliable yields, less insecticide, possible drought tolerance or added nutrients. Arguments against: pests and weeds can evolve resistance; gene flow to wild relatives; reliance on patented seed from a few companies; herbicide-tolerant crops can increase herbicide use. Value systems shape the debate: technocentrists tend to see GM as a tool for food security, while ecocentrists stress precaution and control by farmers.

## Pollinators and pesticides

Many fruit, vegetable and seed crops depend on insect pollinators. Habitat loss, disease and pesticides have contributed to declines in wild bees and other pollinators in many regions. **Neonicotinoid** insecticides have drawn particular concern because they are taken up throughout the plant, including pollen and nectar. In 2018 the European Union banned outdoor use of three neonicotinoids. **Integrated pest management** reduces reliance on chemicals by combining crop rotation, resistant varieties, natural predators and targeted spraying only when pest numbers pass a threshold.

## Food security and food sovereignty

**Food security** (defined in the Topic 5 guide) is about access to enough safe, nutritious food. **Food sovereignty**, a concept promoted by the international peasant movement La Via Campesina, is the right of peoples to define their own food and farming systems, favouring local production, small farmers and control over seed and land. The two can conflict: cheap imported food may improve food security for city dwellers while undermining local farmers and sovereignty.

## Models of sustainable farming

| Model | Main idea | Example practices |
|---|---|---|
| Sustainable intensification | Higher yields from the same land with fewer environmental costs | Precision fertiliser, improved varieties, efficient irrigation |
| Regenerative agriculture | Rebuild soil health and biodiversity | No-till, cover crops, diverse rotations, managed grazing |
| Agroforestry | Trees grown with crops or livestock | Shade-grown coffee, alley cropping, shelter belts |
| Organic farming | No synthetic fertilisers or pesticides | Manure, compost, legumes, biological pest control |
| Polyculture | Several crops grown together | Intercropping maize with beans |

Each has trade-offs. Organic and low-input systems often yield less per hectare, so they need more land; sustainable intensification may still depend on fossil-fuel inputs. Strong answers choose the model that fits a named context.

## Model answer: evaluate

**Question.** Evaluate the use of Bt crops to increase food production. **[4]**

**Answer.** Bt crops produce their own insecticidal protein, so losses to target pests fall and yields can rise [1]. Farmers spray less insecticide, reducing harm to non-target species and costs [1]. However, pests can evolve resistance if Bt crops are grown widely without refuges of non-Bt plants, and seed must be bought each year from a few companies [1]. Bt crops can raise production where target pests are a major problem, but only with resistance management and fair access to seed [1].

## Common mistakes

- Treating the Green Revolution as wholly positive or wholly negative.
- Calculating NUE upside down (applied ÷ harvested).
- Assuming that intensification automatically spares land for nature.
- Writing that GM crops "contain pesticides" without explaining the Bt protein.
- Confusing food security with food sovereignty.
- Recommending one sustainable model everywhere, without context.

## Exam technique

- **Calculate**: show the formula, the substitution and units (t ha⁻¹, kg ha⁻¹, %).
- **Evaluate**: for each strategy give a benefit, a cost, who gains and loses, and a judgement.
- **Discuss**: bring in at least two value systems in essays on GM, organic farming or diets.
- **To what extent**: on whether technology can feed the world sustainably, weigh the Green Revolution evidence and conclude.
