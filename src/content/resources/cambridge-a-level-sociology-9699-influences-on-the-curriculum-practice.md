These questions cover section 5.3, *Influences on the curriculum*, of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Sociology (9699) syllabus for 2024–2026, assessed on Paper 3 – Education. All schools, countries and policies in the items are fictional.

Revise first with the [influences on the curriculum study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-sociology-9699-influences-on-the-curriculum/) and the [influences on the curriculum revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-sociology-9699-influences-on-the-curriculum-revision-notes/).

## Section A – Short-answer and explanation questions

**1.** What is meant by the "social construction of knowledge"? **[2]**

**2.** Identify the four features Young (1971) gave for high-status school knowledge. **[2]**

**3.** Distinguish between Bernstein's concepts of classification and framing. **[4]**

**4.** What is meant by the "null curriculum"? Give an example. **[3]**

**5.** Explain two ways in which economic demands may influence the content of the curriculum. **[6]**

**6.** Read the item.

> At Harrowfield Academy, a fictional school, girls must wear skirts, boys are asked to carry equipment for sports day, and girls are asked to help in the school office. Science textbooks name twelve scientists, all of them men.

Using the item, explain two ways in which the curriculum may be gendered. **[6]**

**7.** Read the item.

> After independence, the fictional country of Marabá kept its former colonial power's language as the only language of secondary teaching and examination. History lessons begin with the colonial period.

Explain two ways in which this curriculum could be described as ethnocentric. **[6]**

**8.** Explain how the concept of cultural capital can be used to explain why some pupils find the curriculum easier to access than others. **[6]**

## Section B – Arguments against a view and essays

**9.** "The hidden curriculum simply prepares pupils for the world of work."

Using sociological material, give two arguments against this view. **[8]**

**10.** "Schools should teach every pupil only the knowledge that is relevant to their own lives."

Using sociological material, give two arguments against this view. **[8]**

**11.** Evaluate the view that the content of the curriculum reflects the interests of powerful groups. **[26]**

**12.** Evaluate the view that the curriculum today plays a major part in reproducing social inequality. **[26]**

## Answers

**1.** Which knowledge is selected for schools, how it is divided and how it is ranked are the result of human decisions and power [1], not fixed by the nature of knowledge itself [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Literate and individualistic [1]; abstract and unrelated to everyday life [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Classification is the strength of the boundaries between subjects [1]; strong classification keeps subjects separate, as in a collection code [1]. Framing is the degree of control teachers and pupils have over what is taught and how [1]; strong framing leaves little choice over content and pace [1]. **[4]**

**4.** The knowledge a curriculum leaves out altogether [1], which teaches pupils that such knowledge does not count [1]. Example: a history syllabus that omits a minority group's past, or oral and local knowledge absent from exams [1]. **[3]**

**5.** Way 1: governments promote subjects linked to growth, such as science and computing [1], drawing on human capital ideas that education is an investment in productivity [1]; less "economic" subjects such as the arts may lose time or funding [1].
Way 2: vocational courses are designed around employers' needs [1], so content follows the skills of particular industries [1]; Marxists argue this can prepare some pupils mainly for low-paid work [1]. **[6]**

**6.** Way 1: the hidden curriculum reinforces gender roles [1]. Dress codes and allocating physical tasks to boys and office tasks to girls teach that some work is masculine and some feminine [1], which may shape later subject and career choices [1].
Way 2: formal content omits women [1]. A science textbook naming only male scientists presents science as a male field [1]; Spender (1982) argued women's contributions are left out, which may discourage girls from seeing themselves in science [1]. **[6]**

**7.** Way 1: language [1]. Teaching and examining only in the former colonial language puts pupils who speak local languages at a disadvantage [1], and, as Ngũgĩ wa Thiong'o argued, implies their own language is unsuitable for serious knowledge [1].
Way 2: history [1]. Beginning history with the colonial period treats the colonial power's arrival as the start of history that matters [1], leaving pre-colonial societies in the null curriculum and centring one group's perspective [1]. **[6]**

**8.** Cultural capital is the knowledge, tastes and language valued by dominant groups (Bourdieu) [1]. The curriculum is built from the same culture, such as formal language and canonical literature [1]. Pupils from advantaged homes meet this culture before school [1], so lessons feel familiar and they appear naturally able [1]. Others must learn both the content and the codes it assumes [1]. Exams then turn home advantage into qualifications, so access looks like ability but reflects background [1]. **[6]**

**9.** Argument 1: functionalists see the hidden curriculum as teaching wider shared values [1]. Following common rules teaches pupils to cooperate with strangers and feel part of society (Durkheim) [1], so it prepares pupils for citizenship as well as work [1]. Its role is social, not only economic [1].
Argument 2: feminists argue it also teaches gender roles [1]. Dress codes and divided classroom tasks reproduce ideas of masculinity and femininity [1], which shape family and personal life, not just jobs [1]. So the view is too narrow about what the hidden curriculum teaches [1]. **[8]**

**10.** Argument 1: Young (2008) argues all pupils have a right to powerful knowledge [1], specialised knowledge that lets them think beyond their own experience [1]. Keddie found lower streams received everyday content while higher streams received abstract knowledge [1], so a "relevant only" curriculum could deny disadvantaged pupils the knowledge elites take for granted [1].
Argument 2: it would weaken shared values [1]. Functionalists argue a common curriculum builds solidarity across backgrounds [1]; teaching each group only its own experience could divide society [1] and reduce pupils' access to a common national conversation [1]. **[8]**

**11.** Marked by levels of response; this is indicative content.

- **Knowledge (AO1):** social construction of knowledge; Young (1971) on high-status knowledge; Bernstein's classification and framing; Apple (1979); Gramsci's hegemony; Freire's banking concept; ethnocentric and gendered curricula; Bourdieu on cultural capital.
- **Application (AO2):** language of instruction; contested history syllabuses; status of academic versus vocational routes; male-centred science content.
- **Analysis and evaluation (AO3):** functionalist view that a shared curriculum benefits all; economic content may serve everyone's employability; curricula have diversified; teachers and pupils reinterpret content in class; Young (2008) argues high-status knowledge is still powerful for all.
- **Judgement:** for example, powerful groups shape selection, but the knowledge selected can still empower those given access to it.

**Level guidance:** top answers use several factors and weigh power against alternative explanations to reach a reasoned judgement; middle answers describe factors with limited evaluation; low answers assert bias without evidence.

**12.** Marked by levels of response; this is indicative content.

- **For the view:** cultural capital and symbolic advantage (Bourdieu); Keddie on differentiated knowledge; ethnocentric content and the null curriculum; gendered routes and content; hidden curriculum preparing different classes for different work.
- **Against the view:** material factors and family background may matter more than content; reforms have widened content and made core subjects compulsory; powerful knowledge can be a route to mobility; pupils resist and reinterpret content.
- **Evaluation:** separate the formal, hidden and null curriculum; compare societies; consider whether inequality arises from the curriculum or from unequal access to it.
- **Judgement:** for example, the curriculum contributes to reproduction alongside home and in-school factors rather than as the main cause.

**Level guidance:** top answers link curriculum mechanisms explicitly to patterns of inequality and weigh them against other causes; middle answers describe curriculum bias; low answers make general claims.
