This guide goes deeper into sub-topic 3.2 of Option C, Extreme environments, in IB Diploma Programme Geography (first assessments 2019): physical processes and landscapes. The [3.2 revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-geography-extreme-environments-physical-processes-and-landscapes-revision-notes/) already cover weathering, the glacier as a system, the main glacial and periglacial landforms, pingos, patterned ground, dune types, alluvial fans, playas, mesas and the effect of aspect in mountains. This page adds the processes underneath those landforms: how glaciers actually move, the smaller features that reveal ice direction, ice wedges and thermokarst, the debate about how much of a desert is shaped by water rather than wind, and the idea of equifinality. Two worked calculations, glacier mass balance and wind transport, show how to handle data. Practise with the [3.2 practice questions](/resources/ib-ib-dp-geography-dp-geography-ib-dp-geography-3-2-practice/); the [Extreme environments study guide](/resources/ib-ib-dp-geography-extreme-environments-study-guide/) gives the overview.

## What this sub-topic asks you to do

- Explain the processes of weathering, erosion, transport and deposition in cold and hot arid environments.
- Explain the formation of characteristic landforms in glacial, periglacial and arid landscapes.
- Recognise that present landscapes may reflect past conditions.

## How glaciers move

Ice moves downhill under gravity in two main ways.

- **Internal deformation.** Under its own weight, ice crystals slowly deform and slide past each other, so the glacier behaves like a very stiff fluid. This happens in all glaciers and is greatest where ice is thick and steep.
- **Basal sliding.** Where the base of the glacier is at its melting point, a thin film of meltwater lets the ice slide over its bed. Pressure from the ice above lowers the melting point slightly, so ice can melt on the upstream side of a bump and refreeze on the downstream side, a process called **regelation**.

The type of flow varies along a glacier.

- **Extending flow** occurs where the bed steepens: the ice speeds up, thins and stretches, opening crevasses.
- **Compressing flow** occurs where the bed flattens: the ice slows and thickens, which can increase erosion of the bed.

This explains why corrie floors are over-deepened and why rock steps and **ribbon lakes** occur along glacial troughs: erosion is concentrated where ice is compressed or where weaker rock is crossed.

## Worked example 1: mass balance from stakes

Glaciologists measure mass balance by drilling stakes into the ice and recording change over a year. The figures below are invented and given as water equivalent.

| Stake position | Winter accumulation (m) | Summer ablation (m) | Net balance (m) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Upper (accumulation zone) | 2.4 | 1.1 | +1.3 |
| Middle | 1.6 | 1.9 | -0.3 |
| Lower (near the snout) | 0.8 | 3.4 | -2.6 |

Mean net balance across the three stakes = (1.3 - 0.3 - 2.6) / 3 = -1.6 / 3 = **-0.53 m**. The glacier is losing mass overall, so it will thin and its snout is likely to retreat, even though the upper part is still gaining ice. The **equilibrium line**, where net balance is zero, lies between the upper and middle stakes, slightly above the middle stake. If summers warm further, the equilibrium line rises, the accumulation zone shrinks and the deficit grows.

(Real glaciers use many stakes weighted by the area each represents; a simple mean is an approximation.)

## Smaller features that show ice direction

| Feature | How it forms | What it tells you |
|---|---|---|
| Striations | Rock fragments in the base of the ice scratch the bedrock | Direction of ice movement (parallel to the scratches) |
| Roche moutonnee | Ice smooths the upstream side of a rock outcrop by abrasion and plucks the downstream side, leaving a jagged face | Ice flowed from the smooth side towards the rough side |
| Crag and tail | A resistant rock mass (the crag) shelters weaker rock or deposits behind it (the tail) | Ice flowed from the crag towards the tail |
| Drumlin | Streamlined mound of till moulded under moving ice | The steeper, blunter end usually faces up-ice |

These features let geographers reconstruct the movement of ice sheets that have long since melted.

## More periglacial processes

- **Ice wedges.** In winter, intense cold makes frozen ground contract and crack. In spring, meltwater runs into the cracks and freezes. Repeated over many years, the ice grows into a wedge, and networks of wedges form **ice-wedge polygons** on flat ground.
- **Nivation.** Under and around patches of snow that last into summer, freeze-thaw and meltwater weaken and remove material, gradually deepening a hollow. Some nivation hollows may later develop into corries if glaciers form.
- **Blockfields** (felsenmeer). Flat upland surfaces covered by angular boulders broken by repeated freeze-thaw.
- **Solifluction lobes.** Tongue-shaped bulges on slopes where saturated active-layer soil has flowed slowly downhill over frozen ground.
- **Thermokarst.** When ice-rich permafrost thaws, the ground collapses unevenly, creating hummocks, hollows and lakes. Thermokarst is increasing as the Arctic warms, linking this sub-topic to the futures sub-topic.

## Desert landscapes: wind or water?

Wind is the most visible process in deserts, but water often does more geomorphic work.

**Why wind matters:** where sand is plentiful and vegetation sparse, wind builds dunes, removes fine material by deflation, and abrades rock.

**Worked example 2: wind speed and transport.** The rate at which wind moves sand rises roughly with the **cube** of wind speed above the threshold needed to start movement.

```
Transport ratio = (speed ratio) cubed
Wind twice as strong: 2 cubed = 8 times the transport
Wind three times as strong: 3 cubed = 27 times the transport
```

So most sand moves in a few strong wind events, as most coastal change happens in storms.

**Why water matters:** rain is rare but intense, and there is little vegetation or soil to absorb it. Sheet floods spread across gently sloping surfaces and flash floods carve wadis. Many large desert landforms are the work of water:

- **Pediments**: gently sloping rock surfaces at the foot of mountain fronts, thought to be shaped by sheet flooding and the retreat of the mountain front.
- **Inselbergs**: isolated steep-sided hills rising above plains, such as Uluru in central Australia, left where resistant rock has survived while surrounding rock was weathered and removed.
- **Alluvial fans and playas** (see the revision notes).

Some of this water work happened under wetter past climates: the Sahara had large lakes and rivers within the last ten thousand years or so, so some landforms are **inherited**.

## Equifinality

**Equifinality** means that different processes can produce similar-looking landforms. Two examples:

- A rounded hollow on a hillside could be a nivation hollow, a small corrie or a landslide scar.
- Flat-topped desert hills could result from a resistant caprock (mesas), or from past erosion surfaces cut under a different climate.

The idea matters for exam answers: when you identify a landform from a photograph, give the evidence that supports your interpretation (shape, sediments, orientation) rather than assuming a single cause.

## Annotated model answer

*Examine the relative importance of wind and water in shaping hot desert landscapes.* **(sample conclusion)**

"Wind is important where sand supply is large, building dunes and abrading rock, and because transport rises with the cube of wind speed, a few strong events do most of this work **[case for wind, quantified]**. Yet water, though rare, is often the more powerful agent: intense storms on bare ground produce sheet floods and flash floods that cut wadis and build alluvial fans, and many pediments and inselbergs owe their form to water **[case for water]**. Some of this was done under wetter past climates, so present processes are not the full explanation **[inheritance]**. Overall, water is generally the dominant long-term shaper of desert relief, with wind most important in sand seas **[judgement that varies by setting]**."

## Common mistakes

- Saying glaciers move only by sliding; internal deformation occurs in all glaciers.
- Averaging mass balance without saying what the sign means for the glacier.
- Reading a roche moutonnee the wrong way round.
- Assuming wind is the main agent in all deserts.
- Explaining every landform by a single process, ignoring equifinality and inheritance.

## Exam technique

- **Explain** a landform with numbered steps and the conditions needed at each step.
- **Examine** relative importance by giving both sides, quantifying where possible, and concluding with a judgement that depends on setting.
- **Describe** a landscape photograph using shape, scale, materials and orientation before interpreting it.
- Use correct terms: internal deformation, basal sliding, regelation, nivation, thermokarst, pediment, equifinality.
