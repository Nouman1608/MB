## Definition and formulae

**PES** = the responsiveness of quantity supplied to a change in the good's own price.

```
PES = % change in quantity supplied / % change in price
% change = (new - original) / original x 100

Rearranged:
% change in Qs = PES x % change in P
% change in P  = % change in Qs / PES
```

Quick check: if price rises 20% and PES is 0.5, quantity supplied rises 10%. To raise output 12% with PES 3, price must rise 4%.

## Values

| PES | Term | Supply curve | Example context |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | Perfectly inelastic | Vertical | Seats at one performance |
| 0 to 1 | Inelastic | Steep | Tree crops, fresh fish in the short run |
| 1 | Unitary | Any straight line through the origin | Teaching example |
| above 1 | Elastic | Shallow | Factory goods with spare capacity |
| infinite | Perfectly elastic | Horizontal | Digital copies at near-zero extra cost |

**Sign:** normally **positive**, because price and quantity supplied move in the same direction. This separates PES from PED.

**Relative percentages:** the coefficient says whether quantity changed by more or less, in percentage terms, than price. A PES of 1.5 means quantity changed by one and a half times the percentage change in price.

## Factors affecting PES

Each one answers: **how fast and how cheaply can output change?**

- **Time:** momentary (no change), short run (some factors fixed), long run (all factors variable). Longer time, more elastic.
- **Spare capacity:** idle machines and workers make supply more elastic.
- **Stocks:** storable goods are more elastic; perishable goods less.
- **Factor mobility:** easily switched resources make it more elastic; specialised inputs less.
- **Production time:** quick-to-make goods are more elastic; tree crops, ships and buildings less.
- **Ease of entry:** easy entry makes market supply more elastic in the long run.

## Implications (2.3.5)

- **Inelastic supply:** a rise in demand mainly raises **price**; shortages last; prices are volatile.
- **Elastic supply:** a rise in demand mainly raises **quantity**; price changes little.
- Firms can make supply more elastic by holding stocks, keeping spare capacity or training flexible staff, but each has a cost.
- Policy link: if supply is inelastic, a large price rise is needed to call forth extra output.

**Worked comparison (invented figures).** Demand for two goods rises so that 20% more is wanted at the old price. Good A has perfectly inelastic supply: quantity cannot change, so the whole adjustment is a higher price. Good B has perfectly elastic supply: firms supply 20% more at the old price, so price does not change at all. Real goods lie between these limits, and the closer PES is to zero, the more of the adjustment falls on price.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Quantity supplied | The amount firms are willing and able to sell at a given price |
| Coefficient | The number calculated from the PES formula |
| Spare capacity | Unused productive resources a firm could bring into use |
| Perishable | Unable to be stored without spoiling |
| Factor mobility | How easily resources move between uses |
| Momentary period | So short that output cannot be changed |
| Short run | At least one factor of production is fixed |
| Long run | All factors of production can be varied |

## Exam traps

- Using the new value as the base for a percentage change.
- Inverting the formula (price change over quantity change).
- Calling PES 0.8 "elastic".
- Saying time is a factor without explaining what firms can change as time passes.
- Drawing perfectly elastic supply as vertical.
- Mixing up PES (producers) with PED (consumers).
- Listing factors without applying them to the product in the question.

## Self-test

1. Define price elasticity of supply.
2. Price rises from 8 cu to 10 cu and quantity supplied rises from 400 to 460. Calculate PES.
3. Is the supply in Question 2 elastic or inelastic?
4. PES is 1.8 and price falls by 5%. Calculate the change in quantity supplied.
5. Output must rise 9%. PES is 0.3. By what percentage must price rise?
6. Why is PES normally positive?
7. What shape is a supply curve with PES of zero?
8. Give two reasons why the supply of new housing is inelastic in the short run.
9. Why does holding stocks make supply more elastic?
10. Why does supply become more elastic in the long run?
11. Demand for a good with very inelastic supply rises. What happens mainly: a change in price or in quantity?
12. Give one cost a firm faces in making its supply more elastic.

**Answers**

1. The responsiveness of quantity supplied to a change in the good's own price.
2. Price change = 2 / 8 x 100 = 25%; quantity change = 60 / 400 x 100 = 15%; PES = 15 / 25 = 0.6.
3. Inelastic, because 0.6 is between 0 and 1.
4. 1.8 x (-5%) = -9%: quantity supplied falls by 9%.
5. 9 / 0.3 = 30%.
6. A higher price makes supplying more profitable, so price and quantity supplied move in the same direction.
7. Vertical.
8. Building takes a long time, and land with permission to build is limited (also: skilled labour and materials may be scarce).
9. The firm can sell from existing stock straight away when price rises, without waiting for new production.
10. Firms can change all factors, including building new capacity, and new firms can enter the market.
11. Mainly a rise in price, because quantity supplied cannot increase much.
12. Storage costs for stocks, or the cost of idle machines and workers kept as spare capacity.
