To represent something is to present it in a particular way, and every literary text makes choices about whom and what to show, from what angle and with what emphasis. Unit 4 of OxfordAQA International A Level English Literature invites you to examine these choices. The skill is to see a portrayal as a construction, not a mirror: someone has decided how a character, group or society will appear, and those decisions carry values. This guide teaches that analytical approach. Your specification gives the exact task, texts and format, so check it for those details.

## What is representation?

Representation means the way a text portrays a person, group, place, event or idea. A writer who represents a worker, a child, an immigrant or a ruler does so through choices of language, structure, viewpoint and form. Those choices may support or challenge common assumptions of the time. Questions to ask include:

- **Who is represented**, and who is missing or silent?
- **Who is doing the representing**: a narrator, speaker or character, and what is their position and authority?
- **What is emphasised and what is left out?**
- **What attitude does the text invite**: sympathy, admiration, fear, laughter, suspicion?
- **Is the representation stable**, or does the text complicate or undermine it?

A representation is not the same as reality, and a text may represent the same group differently at different moments.

## Methods of representation

### Voice and viewpoint

First-person narration lets a character represent themselves and others subjectively, which may be revealing or unreliable. Third-person narration may seem objective, yet can still guide judgement through selection and tone. In poetry, the speaker's voice and the person addressed shape how the subject is seen. In drama, representation comes through dialogue, action and staging, and through what other characters say about someone who is absent.

### Language

Diction, imagery and tone carry attitudes. Compare, for instance, a description that uses animal imagery for a group with one that uses imagery of nature or domesticity; the choice shapes how the group is seen. Consider labels and names, repetition, euphemism and the level of formality. Note who is given direct speech and who is described only through others' words.

### Structure and form

Where a character appears, how often, and in what sequence all matter. A figure introduced late or placed at the margins of a story is represented differently from one who occupies the centre. Form matters as well: a poem's compression, a novel's scale or a play's visible presence on stage each offers different ways of showing people.

### Setting and symbol

Places and objects can represent communities or ideas, for example a house standing for family or a city for modernity. Look for how setting is described, from whose perspective, and with what implications.

## Context and interpretation

Representation always takes place in a context: the period, culture and circumstances in which a text was written, and also the contexts in which it is read now. Considering context means asking how attitudes of the time, such as ideas about class, gender, race, empire or religion, shape or are challenged by the text. It does not mean attaching a fact to your analysis. Show how a feature of the text relates to something in its context, and consider that different readers, in different times and places, may interpret the representation differently.

Be careful with historical claims. Include only facts you are sure of, and use them to illuminate the text. If you refer to critical or alternative readings, present them accurately and weigh them against the evidence in the text.

## Writing about representation

### A method

1. **Define the representation** in a sentence: who or what is shown, and with what attitude.
2. **Choose evidence** that shows the representation in different parts of the text.
3. **Analyse methods**: voice, language, structure, form, setting.
4. **Consider effect and meaning**: what the representation makes the reader think or feel, and what values it reveals.
5. **Bring in context and other readings** where they deepen the argument.
6. **Reach a judgement**: is the representation straightforward, ambiguous or critical of the attitudes it shows?

### A short worked example

Consider an invented question: "How is a marginalised character represented?" A paragraph might run: "The writer places the character at the edge of the narrative, noticed only when other characters need something from them. This structural marginality, together with the narrator's habit of describing them through their usefulness, invites the reader to share the community's casual dismissal. Yet at one moment the narration shifts to the character's own thoughts, and the sudden fullness of the language exposes how much the earlier descriptions left out. The representation therefore both reproduces and criticises the attitudes of the society it depicts." This paragraph makes a claim, shows a method (structure, voice), identifies an effect and ends with a nuanced judgement, with no reliance on a real quotation.

## Connecting texts

If the unit asks you to link more than one text, build your discussion around points of connection, such as how each text represents power, family or outsiders. Move between texts within paragraphs and explain how differences of form, period or viewpoint produce different representations. A link is stronger when it explains why a difference exists.

## Common mistakes

- Treating a character as a real person rather than a constructed representation.
- Assuming a narrator's view is the writer's view.
- Describing what a group is like in the story without analysing how the text presents them.
- Adding context as a separate paragraph.
- Offering a single, fixed interpretation and ignoring ambiguity.
- Making sweeping claims about a period or group without evidence.

## Exam technique

Read the task carefully and identify the representation you are asked to examine. Keep a thread of argument, supported by short, accurate quotations or references. Use the key terms of the question in your opening and conclusion. Show that you can see more than one way of reading a portrayal. Leave time to check expression.

## Frequently asked questions

**What are the Unit 4 options in 9675?**
Unit 4a is a 2 hour 30 minute exam on unseen texts (prose, including literary non-fiction, and poetry, 25 marks each); Unit 4b is a non-exam assessment.

**What does Unit 4b require?**
Two essays of 1,250 to 1,500 words, each on a different text, one poetry and one prose, assessed by teachers and moderated.

**How much is Unit 4 worth?**
50 marks and 30% of the International A-level on either route.


## Check your understanding

1. What does it mean to say a text represents something? (It presents a person, group, place or idea in a particular way through choices.)
2. Why is a representation not the same as reality? (It is constructed by a writer through selection, emphasis and viewpoint.)
3. Name two methods writers use to shape representation. (For example voice and viewpoint, language, structure or setting.)
4. Why does it matter who is silent or missing in a text? (Absences show whose views are left out and what the text values.)
5. How should context be used? (To explain how attitudes of the time shape or are challenged by the text, linked to specific features.)
6. Why should you not treat the narrator as the writer? (The narrator is a constructed voice with a limited or biased view.)
7. How would you write about an ambiguous representation? (Show how the text both supports and complicates the attitude, with evidence for each.)
8. What makes a link between texts strong? (It explains why similarities or differences exist, not just that they do.)
