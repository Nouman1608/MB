This guide goes deeper into section **3.1.1 Business and objectives** of AQA AS Business (7137), part of the AQA AS and A-level Business specification (Version 1.1, November 2025) for first teaching from September 2026. The section has three content areas:

- **Entrepreneurs**: reasons for setting up in business (financial and non-financial); characteristics of entrepreneurs (risk taker, resilient, focused, passionate, innovative, adaptable); challenges of setting up in business; the change in role of entrepreneurs as the business grows.
- **Business planning and decisions**: the purpose and value of a business plan; the meaning and importance of competitiveness; influences on business decisions (objectives, risk, reward, resources, market conditions, ethics, opportunity costs); the importance of risks and rewards in business decision making; ethical dilemmas in decision making, including profit versus ethics.
- **Business objectives**: the purpose and value of setting business objectives; characteristics of effective objectives: Specific, Measurable, Accountable, Realistic, Time specific (SMART).

The specification places this section in Unit 3.1, examined in AS Paper 1, and states that AS Paper 2 also draws on sections 3.1.1 and 3.1.2. The broader [AQA AS What is business? guide](/resources/aqa-as-level-business-what-is-business/) gives an overview of 3.1.1 and 3.1.2 together. This page works through each content point in more depth, with applied examples. ## Entrepreneurs

### Reasons for setting up in business

| Financial reasons | Non-financial reasons |
|---|---|
| Earning a higher income than as an employee | Independence: being your own boss |
| Building an asset that could later be sold | Turning a passion or hobby into work |
| Profit as a reward for taking risk | Flexibility over hours and location |
| Escaping unemployment or insecure work | Solving a problem or meeting a social need |

The reasons matter because they shape the objectives. An entrepreneur who started for flexibility may be content with a modest profit; one who wants to build and sell a company will chase growth.

### Characteristics and why they matter

Link each characteristic to a challenge it helps with.

| Characteristic | How it helps |
|---|---|
| Risk taker | Willing to invest savings and give up a salary when success is uncertain |
| Resilient | Recovers from rejected loan applications, lost customers or early losses |
| Focused | Keeps working towards the goal instead of being distracted by every new idea |
| Passionate | Sustains long hours and persuades customers, staff and investors |
| Innovative | Finds a new product, process or way of reaching customers that gives an edge |
| Adaptable | Changes the plan when market conditions or customer feedback demand it |

Some characteristics can become weaknesses. Too much appetite for risk can lead to borrowing that the business cannot repay; too much focus can stop an entrepreneur noticing that the plan is failing.

### Challenges of setting up in business

- **Raising finance**, since a new business has no trading record to show lenders.
- **Winning customers** from established rivals with known brands.
- **Managing cash flow**, as costs are paid before revenue arrives.
- **Lacking skills** in areas such as accounting, marketing or employment law.
- **Workload and uncertainty**, with income unpredictable at first.

### How the role changes as the business grows

At the start the entrepreneur does almost everything: making, selling, buying and keeping the accounts. As the business grows the role shifts:

1. **From doing to managing**: recruiting staff and delegating tasks.
2. **From managing to leading**: setting direction, building culture and making strategic decisions.
3. Possibly **stepping back**: appointing professional managers or selling the business.

The challenge is letting go. An entrepreneur who insists on approving every decision becomes a bottleneck that slows growth.

## Business planning and decisions

### Purpose and value of a business plan

A business plan sets out what the business will do, who its customers are, how it will compete, and its financial forecasts. Its **purposes** are to:

- test whether the idea is likely to work before money is committed
- persuade lenders and investors to provide finance
- set objectives and give a benchmark for measuring progress.

Its **value** is limited by the quality of its forecasts. Sales estimates for a new business are often guesses, plans go out of date quickly, and a well-presented plan can create false confidence. A plan is most valuable when it is reviewed and updated as the business learns.

### Competitiveness

Competitiveness is the ability of a business to win and keep customers against its rivals. It can come from **price** (lower costs allowing lower prices) or from **non-price** factors such as quality, design, service, brand and convenience. It matters because a business that cannot compete loses sales, sees margins squeezed and eventually fails; a competitive business can grow and charge more.

### Influences on business decisions

The specification names seven influences. Turning each into a question makes it easy to apply.

| Influence | Question to ask |
|---|---|
| Objectives | Does the option help achieve what the business is aiming for? |
| Risk | What could go wrong, how likely is it, and how bad would it be? |
| Reward | What is the potential gain, in profit or other terms? |
| Resources | Does the business have the finance, staff, skills and time? |
| Market conditions | Is demand growing or shrinking? How strong is competition? |
| Ethics | Is the option morally right, and how will stakeholders view it? |
| Opportunity cost | What is the next best alternative given up? |

### Worked example: applying the influences

Ottoline runs one bakery and has £45,000 in savings. She can either open a second shop (cost £60,000, expected extra profit £18,000 a year) or buy a larger oven (cost £20,000) to supply cafés, expected to add £7,000 a year.

- **Objectives**: she wants growth, which the second shop serves more fully.
- **Reward**: £18,000 a year compared with £7,000.
- **Risk**: the shop depends on a new location attracting customers; the oven builds on existing customers and is lower risk.
- **Resources**: the shop needs £15,000 more than her savings, so she must borrow; the oven can be paid from savings.
- **Market conditions**: two new cafés have opened nearby, increasing demand for wholesale bread.
- **Opportunity cost**: choosing the shop means giving up the oven's £7,000 a year; choosing the oven means giving up the chance of a larger return.

A reasoned conclusion might be that the oven suits her current resources and risk, and its profits could help fund the shop later. Another student could justify the shop. The marks come from using the influences, not from the choice itself.

### Risks and rewards

Higher potential rewards usually come with higher risk. Decision makers differ in how much risk they will accept, which depends on their personality, the finance at stake and the consequences of failure. A business owner with personal savings at stake may avoid a risky project that a large company with many products would accept.

### Ethical dilemmas: profit versus ethics

An ethical dilemma arises when the option that earns most profit is not the morally right one. Examples include buying from a cheaper supplier with poor working conditions, or keeping quiet about a product fault. Acting ethically often raises costs in the short term, but can protect reputation, attract customers and staff, and avoid legal problems. A good answer recognises both sides.

## Business objectives

### Purpose and value

Objectives give direction, help coordinate staff, motivate, allow progress to be measured and provide a basis for decisions. Their value depends on how well they are set.

### SMART, as the specification defines it

AQA's specification defines SMART as **Specific, Measurable, Accountable, Realistic, Time specific**. Note that "A" here is **Accountable**: someone is responsible for achieving the objective.

**Worked example.** "We want to sell more" becomes: "The sales manager will increase online revenue from £40,000 to £50,000 a month by the end of next June."

- Specific: online revenue, not "sales" in general.
- Measurable: a £10,000 (25%) rise.
- Accountable: the sales manager.
- Realistic: depends on the market; a 25% rise in about a year may be achievable for a growing website.
- Time specific: by the end of next June.

## Exam technique: AQA's command words

The specification lists the command words used and the mark tariffs for AS: **Define** (2 marks, knowledge only), **Explain** (4 marks, a concept applied to the case), **Analyse** (6 marks, an issue analysed in context), **Assess** (9 marks, arguments for and against with a judgement) and **Evaluate** (15 marks, two options or courses of action and a recommendation). Match the depth of your answer to the command word.

## Common mistakes

- Listing entrepreneurial characteristics without linking them to a challenge in the case.
- Treating a business plan as a guarantee of success.
- Naming the influences on decisions without applying them to the options.
- Writing "Achievable" instead of "Accountable" for the A in SMART.
- Describing an ethical dilemma without weighing profit against ethics.
