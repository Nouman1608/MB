In the exam you choose one of two tasks, linked to the reading sources, and write a full response for
a given audience and purpose. This set practises the parts of that skill: decoding tasks, adapting
register, using sources and controlling form.

## Section A — Short questions

**1.** Identify the audience(s), purpose(s) and form in this task: *"Write an article for your school
website informing parents about the new homework policy and persuading students to give it a fair
try."* **[4]**

**2.** Rewrite this sentence in a formal register suitable for a letter to a local council: *"Loads of
people are fed up 'cause the park's a total mess."* **[3]**

**3.** A source says: *"Fourteen minutes to work instead of forty."* Show how you could use this idea in
a speech without copying it, and explain what you changed. **[3]**

**4.** Give **three** features you would include in a leaflet advising new students how to settle into
school. **[3]**

**5.** Explain the difference between "Yours sincerely" and "Yours faithfully". **[2]**

**6.** Write an opening sentence for a speech to Year 7 students about joining after-school clubs, and
explain **two** choices you made. **[3]**

## Section B — Longer questions

**7.** Task: *"Write a letter to your head teacher arguing that the school should open its library at
weekends for local families."*
**(a)** Plan four paragraphs, including one counter-argument. **[4]**
**(b)** Write the opening paragraph. **[4]**

**8.** Task: *"Write a speech for a community meeting persuading both young people and older residents
to volunteer at a new community garden."*
**(a)** Explain how you would address **both** audiences. **[4]**
**(b)** Write the final paragraph of the speech. **[4]**

**9.** Task: *"Write a review of a place to eat in your area for a website read by teenagers and their
parents."*
**(a)** Explain how a review's structure differs from that of a persuasive article. **[3]**
**(b)** Write two paragraphs of the review, one praising and one criticising. **[6]**

**10.** Task: *"Write a report for your school council on the problem of litter in the school grounds,
with recommendations."*
**(a)** Give a heading structure for the report. **[4]**
**(b)** Write the recommendations section. **[4]**

**11.** This original paragraph comes from an article for a local newspaper persuading readers to
support a new bus route. Improve it and explain **four** changes. **[8]**
*"buses are good. They help people get places and they are better for the environment, people should
use them more. The new route is a really really good idea and everyone should support it because its
good."*

**12.** Using ideas from the source extracts below (both original), write the opening two paragraphs of
a blog post for young people **explaining** the debate about cycle lanes and **persuading** them to
take part in the council's consultation. **[8]**
*Extract 1: "The council says a public consultation will run until the end of the month."*
*Extract 2: "Who will carry your grandmother to hospital at two in the morning? Not a bicycle."*

## Answers

**How these are marked.** The bracketed credits show one way to reach each tariff. For writing tasks,
a response of the same quality taking a different approach earns the same marks.

**1.** Audiences: parents [1] and students [1]. Purposes: to inform (parents) and to persuade
(students) [1]. Form: an article for a school website [1].

**2.** For example: *"Many residents are concerned about the poor condition of the park."* Credit
[1] for removing slang ("loads", "fed up", "total mess"), [1] for the full form "because" or
restructured sentence without contraction, [1] for precise formal vocabulary ("residents",
"concerned", "condition").

**3.** For example: *"Imagine cutting a forty-minute crawl through traffic to a quarter of an hour
on two wheels."* [1]. Changes [2]: the fact is turned into direct address with "Imagine" to suit a
speech, and the figures are rephrased as vivid images ("crawl", "on two wheels") to persuade rather
than simply report.

**4.** Any three, [1] each: a clear title; sub-headings for each area of advice; short sections or
bullet points; a friendly, reassuring tone with direct address; a final section on where to get help.
Maximum 3.

**5.** "Yours sincerely" is used when the letter begins with the recipient's name, such as "Dear Mr
Hassan" [1]; "Yours faithfully" is used when the recipient is not named, as in "Dear Sir or Madam" [1].

**6.** Sentence [1], for example: *"Hands up if you have ever wished the school day had a bit more
fun in it."* Two choices explained [2]: an imperative and direct address get a young audience involved
immediately; the informal phrase "a bit more fun" suits Year 7 students while staying accurate.

**7. (a)** One mark per purposeful paragraph: the proposal and the main reason (families without
quiet study space) [1]; benefits for students' learning and reading [1]; benefits for the school's
relationship with the community [1]; counter-argument on staffing and security, answered with a
volunteer rota and limited opening hours [1].
**(b)** Credit up to 4: formal greeting and register [1]; clear statement of purpose [1]; a reason or
preview of points [1]; accuracy [1]. Example: *"Dear Mr Daniels, I am writing to propose that the
school library opens on Saturday mornings for local families. Many students in our area have nowhere
quiet to read or study at home, and opening the library would support their learning while bringing
the school and the community closer together."*

**8. (a)** Credit up to 4: pitch a respectful, semi-formal register that suits both groups [1]; use
inclusive language such as "all of us" [1]; address each group directly with reasons that suit them,
for example new skills and experience for young people, company and sharing knowledge for older
residents [1]; show how the groups benefit from each other, giving one shared purpose [1].
**(b)** Credit up to 4: a memorable close [1]; addresses both audiences [1]; a clear call to action
[1]; accuracy [1]. Example: *"So to the young people here tonight: bring your energy. To our older
neighbours: bring your experience. Together we can turn an empty lot into a garden that feeds,
teaches and connects us all. Sign up at the table by the door. Thank you."*

**9. (a)** A review gives an overview, then evaluates strengths and weaknesses fairly [1], and ends
with a recommendation or rating [1]; a persuasive article argues for one side and ends with a call to
action, so it does not need balance [1].
**(b)** Credit up to 6: one paragraph of specific praise [2]; one paragraph of specific criticism
[2]; a register that suits both teenagers and parents [1]; accuracy [1]. Example: *"Bright Bowl serves
the best value lunch on the high street. The rice bowls arrive quickly, generously filled and properly
hot, and at a price students can afford without emptying a week's pocket money. However, the
experience is let down by the seating. Six stools along a narrow counter are not enough at lunchtime,
and families with young children will find nowhere comfortable to sit."*

**10. (a)** One mark each: a title stating the subject [1]; an introduction or purpose section [1];
findings section(s), for example where and when litter collects [1]; recommendations [1].
**(b)** Credit up to 4: numbered or clearly separated recommendations [1]; practical, specific
actions [1]; formal, impersonal register [1]; accuracy [1]. Example: *"Recommendations: 1. Place two
additional bins beside the canteen exit, where most litter was found. 2. Introduce a weekly
form-group rota for a ten-minute litter pick. 3. Display results in assemblies to encourage
improvement."*

**11.** Improved paragraph [4], for example: *"Every morning, hundreds of commuters sit in traffic
on the ring road, burning fuel and time. The new bus route offers a better way: one bus can replace
dozens of cars, cutting congestion and pollution at once. If we want cleaner air and quicker journeys,
we should support it."* Four changes explained [4], one mark each: capital letter and comma splice
corrected and "its" corrected to "it's" or removed (W4); vague "good" replaced with specific benefits
(W1); a vivid opening image engages newspaper readers (W1); sentence structures varied and ideas
linked logically with a conditional final sentence as a call to action (W2/W3).

**12.** Credit up to 8: explains both sides of the debate clearly [2]; persuades young readers to take
part in the consultation [2]; transforms the source ideas rather than copying them [2]; register and
features suit a blog for young people, with accurate writing [2]. Example: *"Should our city's main
roads make room for cycle lanes? It depends who you ask. Supporters say protected lanes would make
journeys safer and quicker; critics, including some taxi drivers, argue that bicycles can't replace
cars for emergencies or bad weather, and worry about the loss of road space."*
*"Here's the thing: the council actually wants to know what you think, and the consultation closes at
the end of the month. These are the roads you'll be using for years. Five minutes online now could
shape them. Don't let everyone else decide for you."*

## Where marks are usually lost

- Naming only one audience or one purpose when the task has two.
- Copying source sentences instead of transforming them.
- Model answers that slip into slang in a formal task.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I have to use the sources in Section B?**
The specification says the writing is linked with the reading sources. Use their ideas where they help
your purpose, always in your own words.

**Which forms should I practise?**
Practise letters, articles, speeches, leaflets, reports, reviews and blogs, so that any form in either
task is familiar.
