This guide covers the effectiveness of treatment, part of "Prevention and treatment" in the health and well-being context of IB DP Psychology (first assessment 2027). There students study the explanation and effectiveness of one or more biological treatments and one or more psychological treatments of mental health disorders, and the guide lists major depressive disorder among the disorders that can be studied. "Effectiveness of treatment" is also one of the related terms for the concept of change. This page is about how researchers decide whether a treatment works, what can make a result misleading, and how to use that knowledge in an extended response. Major depressive disorder is used as the example throughout.

## Efficacy and effectiveness

Two words are often confused:

- **Efficacy** is whether a treatment works under ideal, controlled conditions, such as a randomised controlled trial with carefully selected patients and trained therapists.
- **Effectiveness** is whether it works in ordinary clinical practice, with patients who may have several conditions, therapists of varying skill and less regular attendance.

A treatment can show strong efficacy and weaker effectiveness. The learning objective uses "effectiveness" broadly, but naming the distinction is a quick way to show critical thinking.

## How effectiveness is measured

**Outcome measures.** Most trials use a symptom rating scale, either completed by a clinician (such as the Hamilton Rating Scale for Depression) or by the patient (such as the Beck Depression Inventory). Results are then summarised in different ways:

| Outcome | Meaning | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Response | a substantial reduction in symptoms, often defined as a fall of half or more on a scale | shows improvement, but the person may still be unwell |
| Remission | symptoms have fallen to a level close to normal | a stricter, more meaningful target |
| Relapse | symptoms return after improvement | shows whether gains last once treatment ends |
| Drop-out | patients leave before the end | can signal side effects or a treatment people find hard to accept |

A treatment that produces quick response but frequent relapse may be less useful than one that is slower but longer lasting.

**Comparison groups.** A change in symptoms means little without a comparison, because depression often lifts with time (spontaneous remission) and people tend to enter trials when they feel worst, so scores improve anyway (regression to the mean). Trials therefore compare treatment with a **placebo**, a **wait-list control**, "treatment as usual" or another active treatment.

**Randomisation and blinding.** Random allocation spreads participant variables across groups. In drug trials, double-blind procedures prevent expectations from affecting outcomes. Psychological therapies cannot be double-blind, because both client and therapist know what is being delivered, although the person rating symptoms can be kept blind.

## A four-arm trial: TADS (2004)

The **Treatment for Adolescents with Depression Study** (TADS Team, 2004) is useful because it compared a biological treatment, a psychological treatment and their combination in one trial.

**Method.** Adolescents aged 12 to 17 with major depressive disorder were randomly allocated to one of four conditions for 12 weeks: fluoxetine (an SSRI), cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT), fluoxetine combined with CBT, or a placebo pill.

**Findings.** At 12 weeks the combination produced the greatest improvement. Fluoxetine alone was better than placebo. CBT alone was not clearly better than placebo at this point. Later reports from the same study found that the active treatments became more similar over longer follow-up.

**What it shows about assessing effectiveness.** The conclusion depends on **when** outcomes are measured: a 12-week result favoured medication, while longer follow-up narrowed the gap. The placebo arm only controlled the medication conditions; the CBT-alone group was not blind, which makes the comparison harder to interpret. And the sample of adolescents limits generalisation to adults.

## Relapse as an outcome: Hollon et al. (2005)

**DeRubeis et al. (2005)** found cognitive therapy as effective as medication for moderate to severe depression after 16 weeks, as the revision notes record. **Hollon et al. (2005)** followed the patients who had responded. Those who had responded to cognitive therapy and then stopped it were less likely to relapse than those who had responded to medication and were then switched to placebo, and about as likely to stay well as those who continued medication.

This illustrates why one measurement point is not enough. A treatment's effect during treatment and its effect after treatment ends are different questions, and the answer to the second may favour psychological treatment.

## Sources of bias in treatment research

- **Publication bias.** Trials with positive results are more likely to be published. Turner et al. (2008) compared antidepressant trials registered with the US Food and Drug Administration with the published literature and found that positive trials were almost all published, while many negative trials were either unpublished or reported in a way that made them look positive. Reading only published studies would therefore overestimate effectiveness. Kirsch et al. (2008) avoided this by analysing the regulator's data, including unpublished trials.
- **Researcher allegiance.** Luborsky et al. (1999) reported that comparisons between psychotherapies tend to favour the therapy the researchers prefer. A study run by the developers of a treatment should be read with this in mind.
- **Attrition.** If the patients who did badly drop out, the remaining group looks better than the treatment really is. Analysing all randomised patients ("intention to treat") reduces this problem.
- **Sampling.** Trials often exclude people with other disorders, suicidal thoughts or substance use, so trial samples may be less ill or less complex than clinic patients, which lowers effectiveness in practice.
- **Culture.** Most trials are run in Western countries; outcome scales and therapies may need adapting before results can be generalised.

## Meta-analysis

A **meta-analysis** combines the results of many trials to estimate an average effect. Its strengths are a large combined sample and a view across different settings. Its weaknesses are that it is only as good as the studies included ("garbage in, garbage out"), that publication bias can distort the pool, and that trials with different patients, doses and measures are being averaged. Kirsch et al. (2008) shows the value of including unpublished data; it also shows that a single average can hide differences, since the advantage over placebo was larger in severe depression.

## Worked example: planning a 15-mark extended response

**Question:** "Evaluate research on the effectiveness of two treatments for one disorder, with reference to the concept of change." **[15]**

1. **Focus:** major depressive disorder; one biological treatment (SSRIs) and one psychological treatment (CBT); state that judging effectiveness depends on how and when it is measured, and link this to the concept of change (intervention and the effectiveness of treatment).
2. **Knowledge:** outline each treatment briefly; define efficacy, effectiveness, response, remission and relapse.
3. **Research:** TADS (2004) for a direct four-way comparison; DeRubeis et al. (2005) and Hollon et al. (2005) for comparison during and after treatment; Kirsch et al. (2008) for the placebo question.
4. **Critical thinking:** time of measurement; blinding is impossible for therapy; publication bias and allegiance; trial samples versus clinic patients; adolescent and adult findings differ.
5. **Conclusion:** both treatments show benefits; medication may act faster, while CBT may give more lasting protection against relapse; combination can be best in the short term; no single trial settles the question.

## Common mistakes

- Describing what CBT involves for most of the essay instead of evaluating evidence about whether it works.
- Treating a fall in symptoms as proof of effectiveness without a comparison group.
- Ignoring the time point at which outcomes were measured.
- Claiming therapy trials are double-blind.
- Quoting response rates or percentages you are not sure of; describe the direction of the finding instead.

## Exam technique

**Evaluate** asks for a judgement about how strong the evidence is, so for each study say what it shows and how much weight it deserves. **Discuss** asks for a range of arguments, and **compare and contrast** for similarities and differences between two treatments, referring to both throughout. Always name the disorder. Organise the essay around questions (does it work better than placebo, does it last, does it work in practice) rather than study by study.
