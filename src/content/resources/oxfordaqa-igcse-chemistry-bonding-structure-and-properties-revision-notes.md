These revision notes condense section 3.2.2 of OxfordAQA's International GCSE Chemistry (9202)
specification (Version 6.2, which applies to exams May/June 2018 onwards): how bonding and structure are related
to the properties of substances, statements a to g. There are no tiers in this
qualification, so all of it is for you. The fuller teaching, with worked examples, is in the
[study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-chemistry-bonding-structure-and-properties/); to practise
exam-style answers, use the
[practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-chemistry-bonding-structure-and-properties-practice/).
Unsure where your gaps lie? A free [diagnostic](/diagnostics/) takes about ten minutes.

## The two questions behind every property

1. **Melting and boiling**: what forces must be overcome, and are they strong or weak?
2. **Conducting electricity**: are there charged particles, and are they free to move?

Answer both for a structure and you can explain any property in this section.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Giant ionic lattice | Regular repeating 3D arrangement of oppositely charged ions |
| Electrostatic attraction | Force between opposite charges; in a lattice it acts in all directions |
| Simple molecule | Small group of atoms joined by covalent bonds, e.g. H₂O, NH₃ |
| Intermolecular forces | Weak forces between separate molecules |
| Giant covalent structure (macromolecule) | Atoms all joined by strong covalent bonds in a continuous network |
| Delocalised electrons | Outer-shell electrons free to move through a whole metal structure |

## Summary table

| Structure | Examples | Forces overcome on melting | Melting point | Solid conducts? | Liquid conducts? |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Giant ionic | Sodium chloride | Many strong electrostatic attractions between ions | High | No | Yes (also when dissolved) |
| Simple molecular | H₂, O₂, N₂, Cl₂, HCl, H₂O, NH₃, CH₄ | Weak intermolecular forces only | Relatively low | No | No |
| Giant covalent | Diamond, graphite, silica (SiO₂) | A vast number of covalent bonds | Very high | No (graphite: yes) | – |
| Metallic | Copper, iron, sodium | Strong attraction between ions and delocalised electrons | Usually high | Yes | Yes |

## 3.2.2 a: ionic compounds have high melting points

- Structure: **giant ionic lattice**. In sodium chloride, every ion has six nearest neighbours of the
  opposite charge. The specification asks only for sodium chloride's structure.
- Forces: **strong electrostatic attraction**, ion to oppositely charged ion, **in all directions**.
- Property: **high melting and boiling points**, since a **large amount of energy** is needed to
  break the **many strong** bonds.

**Method in steps: "Explain why ionic compound X has a high melting point."**

1. Name the structure: giant ionic lattice.
2. Name the force: strong electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions.
3. Say there are many of them, in all directions.
4. Link to energy: overcoming them takes a lot of energy.

## 3.2.2 b: ionic compounds conduct when melted or dissolved

| State | Ions free to move? | Conducts? |
|---|---|---|
| Solid | No, fixed in lattice | No |
| Molten | Yes | Yes |
| Dissolved in water | Yes | Yes |

The charge carriers are **ions**, never electrons.

## 3.2.2 c, d and e: simple molecules

- **c**: gases, liquids or solids with **relatively low** melting and boiling points.
- **d**: melting or boiling overcomes the **weak intermolecular forces** between molecules. The
  **covalent bonds are not broken**. Intermolecular forces are weak compared with covalent bonds.
- **e**: no electrical conduction, because the molecules have **no overall electric charge**.

**Small worked reminder.** Use these values for methane: energy to boil 1 mol = 8 kJ; each C–H bond
needs 413 kJ per mol to break. One CH₄ molecule has four C–H bonds, so breaking all of them needs
4 × 413 = **1652 kJ** per mol. That is 1652 ÷ 8 ≈ **207 times** the boiling energy, which shows
boiling cannot be breaking covalent bonds.

**Method in steps: state at a given temperature.**

1. Below the melting point: **solid**.
2. Between melting point and boiling point: **liquid**.
3. Above the boiling point: **gas**.

Take care with negative numbers: −196 °C is lower than −7 °C.

## 3.2.2 f: giant covalent structures

- Every atom is linked to other atoms by **strong covalent bonds**, throughout the structure.
- Examples to know: **diamond**, **graphite** (both forms of carbon) and **silicon dioxide (silica)**.
- Property: **very high melting points**, because a very large number of strong covalent bonds must
  be broken.
- There are **no intermolecular forces** to mention, because there are no separate molecules.
- Graphite conducts and is soft; those details sit in section 3.2.3.

**Recognising structures from diagrams.**

| Clue in the diagram | Structure |
|---|---|
| Alternating + and − particles in a regular pattern | Giant ionic |
| Positive ions with "e⁻" or a sea of electrons between them | Metallic |
| Small, separate groups of bonded atoms with space between them | Simple molecular |
| Atoms joined by bonds that continue to the edge in every direction | Giant covalent |

## 3.2.2 g: metals conduct heat and electricity

- Metals contain **delocalised electrons** that can move through the whole structure.
- Moving electrons carry **charge** (electrical conduction) and **energy** (thermal conduction).
- Conduction depends on the **ability of electrons to move** through the metal.

## Model answer: comparing two structures

*Explain why sodium chloride melts at about 801 °C but ice melts at 0 °C.* A strong answer covers
both substances and links each to energy:

- Sodium chloride is a giant ionic lattice. Melting it means overcoming many strong electrostatic
  attractions between oppositely charged ions, acting in all directions.
- Ice is made of simple H₂O molecules. Melting it overcomes only the weak intermolecular forces
  between molecules; the O–H covalent bonds stay intact.
- Far more energy is needed to overcome the forces in sodium chloride, so its melting point is much
  higher.

A one-sided answer that only describes sodium chloride is incomplete. A comparison question wants a
point about each substance, plus the energy link.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Covalent bond vs intermolecular force.** A covalent bond is inside a molecule and is strong. An
  intermolecular force is between molecules and is weak. Melting a simple molecular solid overcomes
  only the second.
- **Simple molecular vs giant covalent.** Both are covalent. Simple molecular substances have low
  melting points; giant covalent substances have very high ones. The difference is whether there
  are separate molecules.
- **Ions vs electrons as charge carriers.** Molten or dissolved ionic compounds conduct by moving
  ions. Metals conduct by moving electrons.
- **"Strong bonds" vs "a lot of energy".** Strong forces are the cause; the high melting point
  follows because overcoming those forces takes a lot of energy. Write both.

## Quick self-test

1. What type of force holds the ions together in sodium chloride?
2. How many chloride ions surround each sodium ion in solid sodium chloride?
3. Why does solid calcium oxide not conduct electricity?
4. Give two conditions under which an ionic compound conducts.
5. Use these values: substance X melts at −7 °C and boils at 59 °C. State its state at 25 °C.
6. Use these values: substance Y melts at −210 °C and boils at −196 °C. State its state at 25 °C.
7. Use these values: substance Z melts at 1083 °C and boils at 2567 °C, and conducts as a solid.
   Identify its structure type.
8. When liquid ammonia boils, which forces are overcome?
9. Use these values for oxygen: energy to boil 1 mol = 7 kJ; energy to break the O=O bonds in 1 mol
   of O₂ = 498 kJ. Roughly how many times larger is the bond-breaking energy?
10. Why does liquid methane not conduct electricity?
11. Name three giant covalent substances listed in the specification.
12. Which particles carry the current in a metal wire?

### Answers

1. Strong electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions (Na⁺ and Cl⁻), acting in all
   directions.
2. Six.
3. Its ions are locked in place in the lattice, so none can travel to carry charge.
4. When melted (molten) and when dissolved in water.
5. 25 °C is between −7 °C and 59 °C, so **liquid**.
6. 25 °C is above −196 °C, so **gas**.
7. High melting point and conduction as a solid: **metallic**.
8. The weak intermolecular forces between ammonia molecules. The N–H covalent bonds are not broken.
9. 498 ÷ 7 ≈ **71 times**.
10. Methane molecules have no overall electric charge, so there are no charged particles to carry a
    current.
11. Diamond, graphite and silicon dioxide (silica).
12. Delocalised electrons.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Claiming that melting or boiling a molecular substance breaks its covalent bonds.
- Calling intermolecular forces "weak bonds" without making clear they are **between** molecules.
- Saying electrons move in a molten or dissolved ionic compound; the ions move.
- Explaining "high melting point" with "strong bonds" alone and never mentioning the large amount of
  energy needed to overcome them.
- Leaving out "in all directions" or "oppositely charged" when describing ionic bonding in a lattice.
- Describing silicon dioxide as made of SiO₂ molecules, or comparing it with carbon dioxide by
  "stronger intermolecular forces".
- Claiming a solid ionic compound contains "no ions". The ions are there; they just cannot travel.
- Misreading negative temperatures when deciding states from melting and boiling point data.
- For metals, saying "free ions" carry the current instead of delocalised electrons.
