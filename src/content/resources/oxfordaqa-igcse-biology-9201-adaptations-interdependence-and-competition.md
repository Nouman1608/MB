This guide goes deeper into section **3.3.2 Adaptations, interdependence and competition** of the OxfordAQA
International GCSE Biology (9201) specification, Version 5.2, for exams in May/June 2018 onwards. The qualification
is untiered and any part of the specification may be assessed on either paper. The specification marks the parasite
adaptations at the end of this section as Biology-only content.

The existing [Energy transfer, biomass pyramids and adaptations study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-biology-energy-transfer-biomass-pyramids-adaptations-and/)
covers 3.3.1 and 3.3.2 together. This page concentrates on 3.3.2 and adds new evidence: a nest-site competition
data set, an animal to analyse from information, the growth of an extremophile, and parasite features. Afterwards,
use the [Energy transfer, biomass pyramids and adaptations revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-biology-energy-transfer-biomass-pyramids-adaptations-and-revision-notes/)
and the [Adaptations, interdependence and competition practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-biology-9201-adaptations-interdependence-and-competition-practice/).

## What 3.3.2 asks you to know

| Statements | Content |
|---|---|
| a | Organisms need a supply of materials from their surroundings and from other living organisms |
| b, c | Plants compete for light, space, water and nutrients from the soil; animals compete for food, mates and territory |
| d | Organisms, including microorganisms, have adaptations that let them survive in the conditions where they normally live |
| e | Extremophiles live in very extreme environments: high salt, high temperature or high pressure |
| f | Adaptations are structural, behavioural or functional |
| Biology only | How parasites such as fleas, tapeworms and malaria parasites are adapted to their hosts |

When you are given appropriate information, you must be able to **suggest how animals and plants are adapted to their
environment**. The skill is to read the description and link each feature to a need.

## Interdependence

Organisms depend on one another. A plant provides food and shelter for animals, animals may pollinate plants or
spread seeds, and decomposers return nutrients to the soil. To survive and reproduce, an organism requires a supply
of materials from its surroundings (abiotic factors such as light, water and temperature) and from **other living
organisms there** (biotic factors such as food and mates).

A change in one species can therefore affect many others. If a predator is removed, its prey may increase, then
eat more of their own food source, and so on through a community.

## Competition

When two organisms need the same limited resource, they **compete**. The more individuals there are per unit of area,
the less each one gets of the resource.

| Organisms | Compete for |
|---|---|
| Plants | Light, space, water and nutrients from the soil |
| Animals | Food, mates and territory |

Competition occurs **between** species (for example, two kinds of weed) and **within** a species, where it is often
stronger because the individuals have identical needs.

### Worked example 1: nest sites and breeding success

Invented data. A student counted the fledglings from five nests in each of two woodland plots. Plot A has 2 breeding
pairs per hectare. Plot B has 8 breeding pairs per hectare.

| Nest | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Plot A (fledglings) | 4 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 3 |
| Plot B (fledglings) | 2 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 1 |

```
mean, plot A = (4 + 3 + 4 + 5 + 3) ÷ 5 = 19 ÷ 5 = 3.8
mean, plot B = (2 + 1 + 2 + 3 + 1) ÷ 5 = 9 ÷ 5 = 1.8
difference = 3.8 − 1.8 = 2.0
percentage reduction = 2.0 ÷ 3.8 × 100 = 52.6 %  (3 s.f.)
```

Interpretation: in the more crowded plot, pairs compete for food and territory, so fewer chicks survive on average. The
data come from only five nests per plot, so the means are indicative. A fair test would also need plots with similar
habitat, so that food supply and shelter are not different for other reasons.

## Adaptations

An **adaptation** is a feature that enables an organism to survive in the conditions in which it normally lives. The
specification classifies them into three kinds.

| Kind | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Structural | The way an organism is shaped, or coloured | White fur in snowy places; a thick layer of fat; spines on a cactus |
| Behavioural | What an organism does | Migration; huddling together for warmth |
| Functional | Related to processes such as reproduction and metabolism | Making very concentrated urine to save water; producing large numbers of eggs |

A common exam skill is to classify each feature. If it is a body shape or colour, it is structural; if it is an action,
behavioural; if it is about how the body works inside, functional.

### Worked example 2: suggesting adaptations from information

Invented stimulus. "The desert jerboa is a small rodent. It has long hind legs, large ears and pale sandy fur. It
spends the day in a cool burrow and feeds at night. Its kidneys produce very small volumes of concentrated urine."

| Feature | Kind | How it suits a hot, dry desert |
|---|---|---|
| Pale sandy fur | Structural | Camouflage against sand; reflects some sunlight |
| Large ears | Structural | Large surface area from which heat can be lost |
| Long hind legs | Structural | Allow fast, hopping escape from predators over loose sand |
| Spends the day in a burrow, active at night | Behavioural | Avoids the heat of the day and reduces water loss |
| Concentrated urine | Functional | Saves water, which is scarce in a desert |

For each feature, answer in three steps: *name the feature, say what it allows, link it to the environment*.

### Worked example 3: another environment

Invented stimulus. "An Arctic hare has thick white fur, short ears, a rounded body and lives in snow. It eats woody
plants in winter."

- Thick fur (structural): traps air and reduces heat loss to the cold.
- White fur (structural): camouflage against snow, so predators find it less easily.
- Short ears and a rounded body (structural): a smaller surface area for the volume, so less heat is lost.
- Eating woody plants when little else is available (behavioural): the hare can survive when food is scarce.

## Extremophiles

Some organisms live in environments that are very extreme: high levels of salt, high temperatures or high
pressures. These organisms are called **extremophiles**. They are often microorganisms, and they show that
adaptations may be at a cellular or molecular level.

### Worked example 4: an extremophile in a hot spring

Invented data. A bacterium was grown at different temperatures. Its rate of cell division was measured in divisions
per hour.

| Temperature (°C) | 40 | 60 | 80 | 95 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Divisions per hour | 0.2 | 1.1 | 2.4 | 0.3 |

```
fastest growth: 2.4 divisions per hour at 80 °C
compared with 40 °C: 2.4 ÷ 0.2 = 12 times faster
fall from 80 °C to 95 °C: (2.4 − 0.3) ÷ 2.4 × 100 = 87.5 %
```

Interpretation: this bacterium grows best at 80 °C, which is a high temperature, so it is likely to be an extremophile.
Its proteins and enzymes must be adapted to work at high temperatures (a functional adaptation). Growth falls at
95 °C, which suggests that even this organism has an upper limit. Notice that the question does not ask you to name
a specific organism, only to interpret the data.

## Parasites (Biology only)

A **parasite** lives on or in a host and takes food from it, harming the host. The specification names three
examples, and says you should be able to **explain how some parasites are adapted for living on or inside their
hosts**.

| Parasite | Where it lives | Typical adaptation features (explain with information given) |
|---|---|---|
| Fleas | Amongst the hair of mammals | Small, flattened body to move between hairs; legs for clinging or jumping; mouthparts for taking blood |
| Tapeworms | Inside the intestines of mammals | Hooks or suckers to attach to the gut wall; a long, flat body with a large surface area for absorbing digested food; a tough outer covering; produces large numbers of eggs |
| Malaria parasites | Single-celled organisms that cause malaria in humans | Single-celled and very small; live and reproduce inside the cells of the host, where they are protected and have a supply of food |

These features are examples of the sort of information a question may give you. The skill is to link each feature to
the place the parasite lives and the way it feeds. For example, a tapeworm has no need to digest its own food because
the host has already digested it; the parasite absorbs the soluble food from the gut.

## Common mistakes

- Giving a feature without its purpose, such as "white fur" with no mention of camouflage or heat.
- Writing that animals "choose" or "decide" to adapt. Adaptations are features that already help organisms survive.
- Saying that an individual animal adapts during its lifetime in the sense of changing its genes.
- Mixing structural and functional adaptations; check whether the feature is shape or process.
- Confusing competition with predation. Competition is for the same resource.
- Naming only "food" as what animals compete for, and forgetting mates and territory.
- Assuming extremophiles are always animals; many are microorganisms.

## Exam technique

- **Suggest** questions give information; use it. Quote the feature, then link it to the environment.
- **Compare** adaptations of two organisms with paired points: "the polar bear has ..., whereas the camel has ...".
- For data on competition, calculate means and a percentage change, and state the resource being competed for.
- When asked to **explain**, always give the reason a feature is useful, not just a description.
