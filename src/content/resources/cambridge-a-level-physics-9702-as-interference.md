This guide covers subtopic **8.3 Interference** of Cambridge International AS & A Level Physics 9702, 2025–2027 series (AS Level). The broader [Superposition study guide](/resources/as-physics-superposition/) introduces all four parts of Topic 8; this page goes further into the interference outcomes alone: how path difference becomes phase difference, why each classroom demonstration gives coherent sources, what happens when the conditions for fringes are not met, and how to get an accurate wavelength from a double-slit measurement. For quick recall use the [Interference revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-physics-9702-as-interference-revision-notes/), and for exam-style questions the [Interference practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-physics-9702-as-interference-practice/).

## What 8.3 asks you to do

The syllabus lists four outcomes. Candidates should be able to:

- understand the terms **interference** and **coherence**
- show an understanding of experiments that demonstrate **two-source interference** using water waves in a ripple tank, sound, light and microwaves
- understand the **conditions** required if two-source interference fringes are to be observed
- recall and use **λ = ax/D** for double-slit interference using light

Interference is the principle of superposition (8.1) applied to two sources whose waves overlap. Diffraction (8.2) matters here too: each slit or gap must spread its wave out so that the two sets of waves actually meet.

## Interference and coherence

**Interference** is the superposition of waves from two (or more) coherent sources, giving a stable pattern of points where the resultant amplitude is a maximum (constructive interference) and points where it is a minimum (destructive interference).

Two sources are **coherent** when they have a **constant phase difference**. This requires the same frequency, and therefore, for waves in the same medium, the same wavelength. "Same frequency" alone is not enough: two separate lamps filtered to the same colour still emit in short, random bursts, so the phase difference between them changes many millions of times a second and any pattern averages out.

The word "stable" is the point. Waves from any two sources superpose wherever they meet; interference is only *observed* when the pattern stays fixed in space long enough to be seen or measured.

## From path difference to phase difference

At a point P, the **path difference** is the difference between the distances from each source to P. For two sources that oscillate **in phase**:

```
path difference = nλ           →  constructive (maximum)
path difference = (n + ½)λ     →  destructive (minimum)
n = 0, 1, 2, ...
```

Path difference converts to phase difference because one whole wavelength corresponds to one complete cycle:

```
phase difference = (path difference / λ) × 2π   (in radians)
phase difference = (path difference / λ) × 360° (in degrees)
```

So a path difference of 1.5λ gives a phase difference of 3π rad, which is equivalent to π rad: the waves arrive in antiphase.

**Sources in antiphase.** If the two sources oscillate exactly out of phase (for example, one loudspeaker's leads reversed), the conditions swap: a path difference of nλ now gives a **minimum** and (n + ½)λ gives a **maximum**. The central line, equidistant from both sources, becomes a line of minima. Questions sometimes test this by reversing the connections to one speaker and asking what changes at a given point.

**Intensity at a maximum.** If each wave arriving at a maximum has amplitude A, the resultant amplitude is 2A. Intensity is proportional to amplitude squared, so the intensity there is four times that from one source alone, while at a minimum it is zero (for equal amplitudes). Energy is not created: it is redistributed from the minima to the maxima.

## Conditions for observable fringes

For a clear two-source pattern you need:

1. **Coherent sources**: a constant phase difference. With light, this is achieved by splitting light from one source into two parts, as the double slit does, or by using a laser.
2. **Overlap of the two waves**: each slit must be narrow enough for diffraction to spread its light into the region where the other slit's light also goes.
3. **Similar amplitudes**: if one wave is much stronger, the minima are not dark, and contrast is poor. The minimum amplitude is the *difference* of the two amplitudes.
4. **A measurable fringe spacing** for light: since λ is tiny, x = λD/a is only visible if a is small (a fraction of a millimetre) and D is large (metres).

For transverse waves such as light and microwaves, the two waves must also oscillate in the same plane to cancel fully; light from one source through two slits satisfies this automatically.

## The four demonstrations: what makes each source pair coherent

| Wave | How the two sources are made | Why they are coherent | What is detected |
|---|---|---|---|
| Water | Two dippers fixed to one vibrating bar in a ripple tank | Both driven by the same motor, so same frequency and fixed phase | Lines of calm water (minima) radiating outwards between rough regions |
| Sound | Two loudspeakers connected to one signal generator | Same electrical signal drives both | Loud and quiet positions found with a microphone and oscilloscope, or by ear |
| Light | One laser (or one monochromatic source and single slit) illuminating a double slit | Both slits are lit by the same wavefront | Bright and dark fringes on a screen |
| Microwaves | One transmitter aimed at a metal sheet with two gaps | Both gaps are fed by the same wave | Rising and falling meter reading as the receiver probe is moved |

Microwaves used in school laboratories have wavelengths of a few centimetres, so gap separations of several centimetres work, and the pattern can be mapped by hand. Moving the receiver from one maximum to the next changes the path difference by exactly one wavelength, so the wavelength can be found by measuring path lengths with a metre rule.

## The double-slit equation

For light through two slits of separation a, the spacing x between adjacent bright fringes on a screen at distance D is:

```
λ = ax / D       or       x = λD / a
```

The equation relies on D being much larger than a, so that the angles to the fringes are small and the fringes are evenly spaced near the centre. Read the relationships directly from it:

- x is proportional to λ: red light gives wider fringes than blue light.
- x is proportional to D: moving the screen further away spreads the fringes.
- x is inversely proportional to a: bringing the slits closer together spreads the fringes.

**White light.** Each wavelength produces its own fringe pattern with its own spacing. At the centre the path difference is zero for every colour, so the central fringe is white. Away from the centre the patterns separate: each bright fringe has a blue edge nearer the centre and a red edge further out, and after a few fringes the colours overlap and the pattern fades to uniform illumination.

**One slit covered.** There is no second source, so there is no interference; the screen shows only the single-slit diffraction pattern, a broad central patch.

## Worked example 1: measuring a wavelength

A student uses slits of separation 0.80 mm and a screen 2.00 m away. She measures the distance across **ten** fringe spacings as 13.5 mm. Calculate the wavelength.

```
x = 13.5 mm / 10 = 1.35 mm = 1.35 × 10⁻³ m
λ = ax / D = (0.80 × 10⁻³)(1.35 × 10⁻³) / 2.00
λ = 5.4 × 10⁻⁷ m  (540 nm)
```

*Annotation.* Measuring across ten spacings rather than one reduces the percentage uncertainty in x by a factor of about ten, because the uncertainty in locating the two end fringes is shared over ten spacings. Count *spacings*, not fringes: from the 1st to the 11th bright fringe is ten spacings. The answer is given to two significant figures because a is given to two.

## Worked example 2: path difference with sound

Two loudspeakers connected to the same signal generator emit sound of wavelength 0.50 m in phase. A microphone at point P is 4.20 m from one speaker and 4.95 m from the other. Determine what is detected at P.

```
path difference = 4.95 − 4.20 = 0.75 m
0.75 / 0.50 = 1.5 wavelengths
```

A path difference of 1.5λ, for sources in phase, gives destructive interference: P is at a **minimum** (quiet). If the leads to one speaker are now reversed, the sources are in antiphase, so the same point becomes a **maximum**.

*Annotation.* The sound at P is quiet, not silent: the microphone is at different distances from the two speakers, so the two amplitudes arriving there are not quite equal, and sound reflected from walls also reaches P.

## Common mistakes

- **Defining coherence as "same frequency" or "in phase".** The required phrase is a *constant* phase difference. Coherent sources need not be in phase.
- **Counting fringes instead of fringe spacings** when dividing a measured distance.
- **Unit slips in λ = ax/D.** Slit separation and fringe spacing are usually given in millimetres; convert both to metres before substituting.
- **Forgetting to check whether the sources are in phase or in antiphase** before applying the path-difference conditions.
- **Saying energy is destroyed at minima.** It is redistributed to the maxima.
- **Using λ = ax/D for a diffraction grating.** The grating has its own equation; see [the diffraction grating guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-physics-9702-as-the-diffraction-grating/).

## Exam technique for the command words

- **State** or **define** (coherence, interference): one precise sentence. "Constant phase difference" earns the coherence mark.
- **Explain** (how fringes form): name superposition, give the path-difference condition for a bright fringe and for a dark fringe, and link each to the phase difference on arrival.
- **Describe** (an experiment): name the source, how two coherent sources are made, the detector, what is measured and what is calculated from it.
- **Calculate** or **determine**: write λ = ax/D, convert units, substitute, and give the answer to a sensible number of significant figures with a unit.
- **Suggest** (why minima are not completely dark or silent): unequal amplitudes, reflections or background noise.
