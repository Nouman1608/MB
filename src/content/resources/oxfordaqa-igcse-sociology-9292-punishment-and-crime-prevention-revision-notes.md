**These notes cover "Aims and types of punishment" and "Crime prevention: types of policing,
surveillance"** in 3.2.2.3 of OxfordAQA International GCSE Sociology (9292). They condense the
[punishment and crime prevention study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-sociology-9292-punishment-and-crime-prevention/);
the earlier parts of 3.2.2 are in the [Socialisation revision notes](/resources/igcse-oxfordaqa-sociology-socialisation-revision-notes/)
and the [formal and informal social control revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-sociology-9292-formal-and-informal-social-control-revision-notes/).

## Six aims of punishment

| Aim | One-line definition | Quick example |
|---|---|---|
| Retribution | Punishment the offender deserves, in proportion to the crime | Long sentence for serious violence |
| Deterrence | Discouraging crime through fear of punishment (individual and general) | Heavy, publicised fines |
| Rehabilitation | Reforming the offender | Education or drug treatment |
| Incapacitation | Removing the ability to offend | Prison; driving ban |
| Reparation | Repairing the harm | Compensation; unpaid work |
| Denunciation | Public expression of society's disapproval | Formal condemnation in court |

**Conflicts between aims:** retribution may demand a harsh sentence that harms rehabilitation; a
short sentence may neither deter nor reform.

## Types of punishment

- Fines and compensation orders.
- Community sentences (unpaid work, supervision).
- Probation and suspended sentences.
- Imprisonment.
- Restorative justice (offender meets victim and makes amends).
- Corporal and capital punishment, lawful in some countries and abolished in many others.

## Perspectives on punishment

- **Durkheim (functionalist):** punishment expresses collective outrage, reaffirms shared values and
  marks the boundaries of acceptable behaviour.
- **Marxist:** punishment falls hardest on the working class; crimes of the powerful are less often
  prosecuted or receive fines rather than prison.
- **Foucault,** *Discipline and Punish* (1975): a shift from physical spectacle to **discipline**
  through observation; the **panopticon** (Bentham's design) makes people watch themselves.

- **Interactionist:** punishment depends on who is labelled (Becker); prison can become a master status.
- **Feminist:** double deviance for women who break gender norms; domestic abuse historically treated
  less seriously.

## Types of policing

| Type | Key idea | Main criticism |
|---|---|---|
| Policing by consent | Authority rests on public trust (Metropolitan Police, 1829) | Trust varies between communities |
| Community policing | Local officers, relationships, problem-solving | Resource-intensive |
| Zero tolerance | Enforce minor offences; "broken windows" (Wilson and Kelling, 1982) | Can damage community relations |
| Intelligence-led / predictive | Data used to target places and people | Data may reproduce bias |
| Situational crime prevention | Reduce opportunities by design (Clarke) | Displacement |

## Surveillance

- Forms: CCTV, number-plate recognition, facial recognition, online monitoring, data from phones and
  payments.
- **Zuboff,** *The Age of Surveillance Capitalism* (2019): companies collect behavioural data to
  predict and influence behaviour for profit; control extends beyond the state.
- Debates: prevention or detection; displacement; privacy and power; unequal targeting.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| General deterrence | Punishing one offender to warn others |
| Individual deterrence | Punishing an offender to stop them reoffending |
| Restorative justice | Bringing offender and victim together to repair harm |
| Displacement | Crime moving to another place, time or target after prevention measures |
| Panopticon | A prison design in which inmates may always be watched without knowing when |
| Surveillance capitalism | Zuboff's term for profiting from data about human behaviour |
| Broken windows | The idea that visible disorder encourages more serious crime |

## Model paragraph: evaluation

"Situational crime prevention, such as better lighting and CCTV, can reduce opportunistic crime in a
particular place. However, critics argue that it often leads to displacement, as offenders move to
less protected areas, and it does nothing about the causes of crime such as poverty. In [a named
society], the spread of CCTV in city centres could be judged by whether crime fell overall or simply
moved."

## Exam traps

- Confusing deterrence (fear) with incapacitation (inability).
- Calling restorative justice "soft" without explaining its aims.
- Zero tolerance with no mention of broken windows or criticism.
- Foucault or Zuboff named without their argument.
- Surveillance reduced to CCTV only.
- No named society in an extended answer.

## Self-test

1. Name the six aims of punishment.
2. What is the difference between individual and general deterrence?
3. What is incapacitation? Give an example.
4. What is restorative justice?
5. Give one example of how two aims of punishment might conflict.
6. What did Durkheim argue punishment does for society?
7. What is the panopticon, and why did Foucault use it?
8. What is policing by consent?
9. What is the "broken windows" idea, and who proposed it?
10. What is displacement?
11. What did Zuboff argue about surveillance?
12. Give two criticisms of surveillance as a means of crime prevention.

**Answers:** 1. Retribution, deterrence, rehabilitation, incapacitation, reparation, denunciation.
2. Individual deterrence aims to stop the punished offender reoffending; general deterrence warns
others. 3. Removing an offender's ability to offend, for example imprisonment or a driving ban.
4. Bringing offender and victim together so the offender understands the harm and makes amends.
5. For example, a harsh sentence for retribution may reduce the chance of rehabilitation.
6. It expresses collective outrage, reaffirms shared values and marks the boundaries of acceptable
behaviour. 7. Bentham's prison design in which inmates may always be watched without knowing when;
Foucault used it to show how people under possible observation begin to discipline themselves.
8. Police authority resting on public co-operation and trust. 9. That visible disorder encourages
more serious crime; Wilson and Kelling (1982). 10. Crime moving to another place, time or target
after prevention measures. 11. That companies collect behavioural data to predict and influence
behaviour for profit, extending control beyond the state. 12. Any two: displacement; it may detect
rather than prevent; invasion of privacy; unequal targeting of groups or areas; it does not address
causes of crime.
