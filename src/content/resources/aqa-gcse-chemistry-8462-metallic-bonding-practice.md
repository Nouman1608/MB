## Section A

**1.** This question is about the structure of metals.

**(a)** Describe the arrangement of atoms in a metal. **[1]**
**(b)** State which electrons in a metal atom become delocalised. **[1]**
**(c)** Name the type of force that holds the structure together. **[1]**

**2.** Magnesium has the electronic structure 2,8,2.

**(a)** How many delocalised electrons does each magnesium atom provide? **[1]**
**(b)** Give the charge on the ions in solid magnesium. **[1]**

**3.** Give **two** features of a correctly drawn diagram of metallic bonding. **[2]**

**4.** Three diagrams are described.

- **R:** large particles labelled 1− alternating with small particles labelled 1+.
- **S:** particles labelled 3+ in rows, with e⁻ symbols between them.
- **T:** particles of two different sizes, all labelled 2+, in rows, with e⁻ symbols between them.

Identify the **two** diagrams that show metallic giant structures. **[2]**

**5.** A student writes: "In a metal, the atoms lose their outer electrons, so the whole metal becomes
positively charged." Explain why the student is wrong. **[2]**

**6.** Solder is an alloy of tin and copper. Name the type of bonding in solder and explain how you know. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** Describe the structure and bonding in solid calcium. **[4]**

**8.** A diagram shows part of the structure of sodium, with 16 Na⁺ ions.

**(a)** How many delocalised electrons should the diagram show for this part to be neutral? **[1]**
**(b)** Explain why a piece of sodium has no overall charge. **[1]**
**(c)** A diagram of part of the structure of aluminium shows 10 Al³⁺ ions. How many delocalised electrons
balance this charge? **[1]**

**9.** Explain why metallic bonding is strong, even though the delocalised electrons are free to move. **[3]**

**10.** Compare the metallic bonding in sodium (2,8,1) with the metallic bonding in aluminium (2,8,3). **[4]**

**11.** Give **three** limitations of a two-dimensional diagram of metallic bonding. **[3]**

**12.** A student describes copper as "a lattice of copper atoms held together by covalent bonds, with
electrons fixed between pairs of atoms". Explain what is wrong with this description, and give a correct
description of the structure and bonding in copper. **[6]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** A **giant structure** of atoms arranged in a **regular pattern** (layers) [1]
**(b)** The **outer-shell** electrons [1]
**(c)** **Electrostatic** attraction [1]

**2. (a)** **2** [1]
**(b)** **2+** (Mg²⁺) [1]

**3.** Any two [1] [1]: **positive ions** labelled with their charge; ions in a **regular arrangement**
(rows); **electrons between the ions**, not in shells; a key labelling delocalised electrons. **[2]**

**4.** **S** [1] and **T** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* T is an alloy: two kinds of metal particle still give metallic bonding. R has negative
ions, so it is ionic.

**5.** The delocalised electrons **stay within the metal structure**; they are shared, not lost [1]. The
negative charge of the electrons **balances** the positive charge of the ions, so the metal is neutral [1].
**[2]**

**6.** **Metallic** [1]; tin and copper are both **metals**, and alloys have metallic bonding [1]. **[2]**

**7.** **Giant** structure of calcium atoms in a **regular pattern** [1]. The **outer-shell electrons** (two
per atom) are **delocalised** and free to move through the structure [1], leaving **positive** calcium ions
(Ca²⁺) [1]. There is **strong electrostatic attraction** between the positive ions and the delocalised
electrons [1]. **[4]**

**8. (a)** **16** [1]
**(b)** The total negative charge of the delocalised electrons **equals** the total positive charge of the
ions [1].
**(c)** 10 × 3 = **30** [1]

**9.** The delocalised electrons are **attracted to the positive metal ions** [1]; each electron is
attracted to **many ions** and each ion to many electrons [1]; these electrostatic attractions act
**throughout the whole giant structure**, so the bonding is strong [1]. **[3]**

**10.** Similarity: both are electrostatic attractions between **positive metal ions** and **delocalised
electrons** in a giant structure [1]. Differences: sodium provides **1** delocalised electron per atom,
aluminium provides **3** [1]; sodium forms **Na⁺** ions, aluminium forms **Al³⁺** ions [1]; so aluminium has
**more delocalised electrons** for each ion (and more charge on each ion) than sodium [1]. **[4]**

**11.** Any three [1] [1] [1]: shows only **one layer** of a 3D structure; shows electrons in **fixed
positions** when they move; shows only a **small part** of a giant structure; ion **sizes and spacing** may
not be to scale. **[3]**

**12.** Indicative content, one mark per point, up to 6:

Errors:
- the bonding is **metallic**, not covalent [1]
- the electrons are **delocalised and free to move**, not fixed between pairs of atoms [1]
- the bonding does not involve pairs of atoms; each ion is attracted to many electrons [1]

Correct description:
- a **giant structure** of copper atoms in a **regular pattern** [1]
- the **outer-shell electrons** are delocalised and move through the whole structure [1]
- leaving **positive copper ions** [1]
- held by **strong electrostatic attraction** between the positive ions and delocalised electrons [1]

**[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Both parts of the question must be answered; a correct description with no comment on
the errors cannot reach full marks.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "sea of ions" instead of a sea of delocalised electrons.
- Describing the electrons as lost or transferred.
- Leaving out "positive" when naming what the electrons are attracted to.
- Classifying an alloy diagram as ionic because it shows two kinds of particle.
- Saying the bonds are weak because the electrons move.
