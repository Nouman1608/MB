This guide covers section **3.3.1 Business objectives and strategy** in Unit 3 (Business decisions and strategy) of the Pearson Edexcel International Advanced Subsidiary/Advanced Level in Business (YBS11), Issue 1. Unit 3 moves from separate business functions to the strategic view: where the business wants to go, how it will get there, and what in its environment helps or blocks it. All businesses and figures are invented.

For the unit as a whole, see the [Business decisions and strategy study guide](/resources/edexcel-ial-business-business-decisions-and-strategy/). Condensed notes are in the [Business objectives and strategy revision notes](/resources/edexcel-a-level-business-ybs11-business-objectives-and-strategy-revision-notes/) and questions in the [Business objectives and strategy practice questions](/resources/edexcel-a-level-business-ybs11-business-objectives-and-strategy-practice/).

## What the specification lists

1. **Corporate objectives:** developing corporate objectives from a mission statement or corporate aims; critical appraisal of mission statements.
2. **Theories of corporate strategy:** Ansoff's Matrix; Porter's Strategic Matrix; the aim of portfolio analysis; the effect of strategic and tactical decisions on human, physical and financial resources.
3. **SWOT analysis:** internal strengths and weaknesses; external opportunities and threats.
4. **Impact of external influences:** PESTLE; the changing competitive environment; Porter's five forces.

Unit 3 is a two-hour paper with 80 marks: Section A has short and extended responses (40 marks), and Sections B and C each have one 20-mark essay. The strategy models in this section are used in all three, so you need to apply them to a given business rather than recite them.

## From mission to objectives to strategy

A **mission statement** sets out why the business exists and what it stands for, in general terms. **Corporate aims** are the long-term goals that follow from it. **Corporate objectives** turn those aims into specific, measurable targets with a time limit. **Corporate strategy** is the long-term plan to achieve them, and **tactics** are the short-term actions that carry the plan out.

| Level | Example for a regional bus company |
|---|---|
| Mission | "To connect our region with safe, reliable and affordable travel" |
| Aim | Become the region's preferred public transport provider |
| Objective | Raise market share from 12% to 15% within three years |
| Strategy | Expand into neighbouring towns and add a night service |
| Tactic | Run a three-month discount fare on the new routes |

**Worked example 1.** The bus company operates in a regional market worth $400m a year. At 12% share, its sales are 0.12 x 400 = **$48m**. The market is expected to grow by 5% a year.

- Market in three years = 400 x 1.05 x 1.05 x 1.05 = **$463.05m**.
- Sales needed at 15% share = 0.15 x 463.05 = **$69.46m**.
- Increase needed = 69.46 / 48 = a rise of **44.7%** in three years, about **13.1% a year** compound.

This shows that an apparently small gain of three percentage points of share needs sales to grow more than two and a half times faster than the market (13.1% against 5%). An objective like this is useful because it can be measured and tested against the business's resources.

**Critical appraisal of a mission statement.** A good statement is clear, distinctive, realistic and understood by staff and customers. Weaknesses include vague wording ("to be the best"), no link to measurable objectives, being the same as every rival's, or failing to match what the business really does (for instance, claiming to put staff first while cutting wages). Mission statements can motivate staff and guide decisions, but they are only useful if managers act on them.

## Ansoff's Matrix

Ansoff's Matrix links growth strategies to the risk of moving into new markets or new products.

| | Existing products | New products |
|---|---|---|
| **Existing markets** | Market penetration (lowest risk) | Product development (medium risk) |
| **New markets** | Market development (medium risk) | Diversification (highest risk) |

- **Market penetration:** sell more of the same product to the same customers, through promotion, price cuts or loyalty schemes. The bus company's discount fare on existing routes is an example.
- **Market development:** sell existing products in new markets, such as new regions or customer groups. Extending into neighbouring towns is an example.
- **Product development:** launch new products in existing markets, such as a night bus or an app.
- **Diversification:** new products in new markets, such as a coach-tour holiday business. It spreads risk if the existing market declines, but the business has the least experience.

The strategy a business selects should depend on its resources, its attitude to risk and the state of the existing market. A business in a saturated market may be forced to develop new products or markets, while a business with spare capacity may prefer penetration.

## Porter's Strategic Matrix

Porter's Strategic Matrix (generic strategies) shows how a business can gain competitive advantage by combining the **source of advantage** (low cost or differentiation) with the **scope of the market** (broad or narrow).

| | Low cost | Differentiation |
|---|---|---|
| **Broad market** | Cost leadership: lowest prices across a mass market (a discount supermarket) | Differentiation: distinctive features, quality or brand across the market (a premium car maker) |
| **Narrow segment** | Cost focus: low cost within a niche (a budget airline on a few routes) | Differentiation focus: a distinctive offer for a niche (a luxury boutique hotel) |

A business that tries to do everything can become "stuck in the middle", with neither the lowest cost nor a distinctive offer. Cost leadership requires scale, efficient operations and tight control; differentiation requires investment in design, quality and brand, which customers must be willing to pay for.

## Portfolio analysis

**Portfolio analysis** examines the mix of products or business units to see whether the whole range is balanced. Its aim is to guide decisions on which products to invest in, maintain, harvest or drop, so that cash earned by mature products can fund the growth of new ones. The Boston Matrix is the best known tool. It classes products by **market growth** and **relative market share**, where relative share is the product's share divided by the share of its largest rival.

**Worked example 2.** Product A has 24% of its market and its largest rival has 12%: relative share = 24 / 12 = **2.0**. Product B has 6% against a rival's 18%: relative share = 6 / 18 = **0.33**. If both markets are growing quickly, A is a **star** (strong share in a growing market) and B is a **question mark** (weak share in a growing market). A mature product with high relative share is a **cash cow**; a low-share product in a declining market is a **dog**.

The strength of the approach is that it forces a view of the whole portfolio. Its limits are that market growth and share are not the only factors, that it ignores links between products, and that labelling a product a "dog" could make staff give up on something that is still profitable.

## Strategic and tactical decisions and resources

**Strategic decisions** are long term, high risk and affect the whole business, such as entering a new country. They require major commitment of finance (for capital and funding), physical resources (new sites or machinery) and human resources (recruitment, training or restructuring), and they are hard to reverse. **Tactical decisions** are short term and operate within the strategy, such as a promotion or a staffing rota. They use existing resources and are easier to adjust. A strategy fails if tactics do not support it, and tactics fail if the strategy is unclear.

## SWOT analysis

SWOT classes factors as **internal** (strengths and weaknesses, which the business controls) or **external** (opportunities and threats, which it does not). A sound SWOT is specific, prioritised and linked to action: use strengths to seize opportunities, correct weaknesses that leave the business open to threats. A common mistake is to place an external factor, such as a new competitor, under weaknesses. Its limits are that it is a snapshot, subjective, and does not by itself say what to do.

## PESTLE and Porter's five forces

**PESTLE** classes external influences as **political** (government stability, trade policy), **economic** (growth, interest rates, exchange rates), **social** (demographics, tastes), **technological** (innovation, automation), **legal** (employment, consumer and competition law) and **environmental** (climate, sustainability rules). Its value is breadth. Its limit is that listing factors is not analysis: you must say which matter most to this business and why.

**Porter's five forces** examines the competitive pressure in an industry:

1. **Rivalry** between existing firms (many, similar in size, slow growth means fierce rivalry).
2. **Threat of new entrants** (low barriers such as small capital needs mean high threat).
3. **Threat of substitutes** (alternative products meeting the same need).
4. **Bargaining power of suppliers** (few suppliers or unique inputs give them power).
5. **Bargaining power of buyers** (few large customers, or low switching costs, give them power).

The more intense the forces, the lower the industry's profits. The model supports the changing competitive environment: new technology can lower entry barriers, and mergers can alter buyer or supplier power.

## Common mistakes

- Treating a mission statement as an objective: objectives must be measurable and time-limited.
- Confusing market development (existing product, new market) with product development (new product, existing market).
- Describing Boston Matrix labels without using relative market share and growth.
- Putting external factors in the strengths and weaknesses boxes of a SWOT.
- Listing PESTLE headings with no link to the business in the source.
- Treating strategy and tactics as the same thing.

## Exam technique

For **analyse** questions, apply one model to the business and trace the chain, such as "new entrants threaten the firm because capital costs are low, which would cut prices and market share". In **assess** and essay answers, weigh which strategy fits the business's resources and risk, and use evidence such as a calculated share or growth target. Strong judgements say what the choice depends on, for instance the firm's cash reserves or the market's growth rate.
