This study guide goes deeper into section 4.4, Measures, of topic 4 (Geometry and trigonometry) in the Pearson Edexcel International GCSE Mathematics A (4MA1) specification, Issue 2 (November 2017). Section 4.4 is Foundation Tier content. The Higher Tier entry says "See Foundation Tier", so it is needed on both tiers, and compound measures in particular turn up inside longer Higher questions.

| 4.4 E | measure an angle to the nearest degree |

| 4.4 G | use compound measure such as speed, density and pressure (note: formula for pressure will be given) |

## 4.4 A: Reading scales

Never assume each small mark is worth 1. Find the value of one division first:

```
value of one division = (difference between two labelled marks) ÷ (number of gaps between them)
```

Count **gaps**, not marks. Between two labels with four small marks in between there are five gaps.

**Worked example 1.** Three instruments:

```
Kitchen scale: labels 200 g and 300 g, 5 gaps  -> 100 ÷ 5 = 20 g per gap
  pointer on the 3rd mark after 200 g          -> 200 + 3 × 20 = 260 g
Thermometer: labels −10 °C and 0 °C, 5 gaps    -> 10 ÷ 5 = 2 °C per gap
  reading 2 marks below 0                      -> 0 − 2 × 2 = −4 °C
Speedometer: labels 40 and 60 km/h, 4 gaps     -> 20 ÷ 4 = 5 km/h per gap
  needle halfway between 2nd and 3rd marks     -> 40 + 2.5 × 5 = 52.5 km/h
```

Below zero, values decrease as you move away from 0. When a pointer sits between marks, estimate to a fraction of a division.

## 4.4 B: Time intervals

In the 24-hour clock, 00:00 is midnight and 12:00 is noon. Afternoon times add 12 to the hour: 4:15 pm is 16:15. The two cases that catch people out are the twelve o'clock hours: 12:35 am is 00:35, but 12:35 pm is 12:35.

**Worked example 2.** A film starts at 7:40 pm and lasts 2 hours 25 minutes. When does it finish? Give the answer in both clocks.

```
7:40 pm + 2 h = 9:40 pm
9:40 pm + 20 min = 10:00 pm, + 5 min = 10:05 pm
24-hour clock: 22:05
```

**Worked example 3.** How long is it from 08:52 to 13:17?

```
08:52 -> 09:00    8 min
09:00 -> 13:00    4 h
13:00 -> 13:17    17 min
total             4 h 25 min
```

Subtracting 13.17 − 8.52 on a calculator gives 4.65, which means nothing: there are 60 minutes in an hour, not 100.

## 4.4 C: Sensible estimates

An estimate compares an unknown measure with something whose size you know. Useful reference sizes:

| Reference | Approximate measure |
|---|---|
| Height of a classroom door | 2 m |
| Height of an adult | 1.5 m to 1.9 m |
| Width of a fingernail | 1 cm |
| Mass of a bag of sugar | 1 kg |
| Capacity of a teaspoon | 5 ml |
| Capacity of a large bottle of water | 1.5 to 2 litres |

**Worked example 4.** A picture shows a man standing next to a flagpole. The flagpole is about 4 times his height. Estimate the height of the flagpole.

Take the man as about 1.8 m tall. 4 × 1.8 = 7.2, so the flagpole is about **7 m** tall. Give a rounded value with a unit, and state the assumption you made.

Choosing a sensible unit is part of the skill. The length of a pencil is measured in cm, the distance between cities in km and the mass of a lorry in tonnes.

## 4.4 D and E: Angles and bearings

### Measuring an angle

1. Put the centre of the protractor exactly on the vertex.
2. Line up the zero line with one arm of the angle.
3. Read from the scale that starts at 0° on that arm. Most protractors have an inner and an outer scale, and reading the wrong one gives 180° minus the true angle.
4. Check the type: an acute angle must be less than 90°, an obtuse angle between 90° and 180°.

For a **reflex** angle (between 180° and 360°), measure the smaller angle on the other side and subtract from 360°. If the smaller angle is 128°, the reflex angle is 360° − 128° = 232°.

### Three-figure bearings

A bearing is measured **from north**, **clockwise**, and written with **three figures**. Due east is 090°, south is 180°, west is 270°. The compass points in between are also bearings: NE is 045°, SE is 135°, SW is 225° and NW is 315°.

The word "from" tells you where to stand. "The bearing of B from A" means stand at A, face north, and turn clockwise until you face B.

**Worked example 5.** The bearing of B from A is 305°. Find the bearing of A from B.

The two bearings differ by 180°. Since 305° is more than 180°, subtract:

```
305° − 180° = 125°
```

If the first bearing is less than 180°, add 180° instead. The north lines at A and B are parallel, so this rule is really the fact that co-interior (allied) angles add up to 180°.

**Worked example 6.** Ship P is on a bearing of 040° from a port, and ship Q is on a bearing of 115° from the same port. The angle between the two directions, at the port, is 115° − 40° = **75°**.

## 4.4 F: Average speed, distance and time

```
average speed = total distance ÷ total time
distance = speed × time
time = distance ÷ speed
```

Time must be in hours for km/h, and in seconds for m/s. Convert minutes to hours by dividing by 60.

**Worked example 7.** A coach travels 45 km in 40 minutes, then 30 km at an average speed of 60 km/h. Find the average speed for the whole journey.

```
second part: time = 30 ÷ 60 = 0.5 h = 30 min
total distance = 45 + 30 = 75 km
total time = 40 + 30 = 70 min = 70/60 h
average speed = 75 ÷ (70/60) = 64.285... = 64.3 km/h (3 s.f.)
```

The first part was at 45 ÷ (40/60) = 67.5 km/h. Averaging the two speeds, (67.5 + 60) ÷ 2 = 63.75, is **wrong**, because the coach spent different lengths of time at each speed.

**Worked example 8.** How long does it take to travel 84 km at 48 km/h? 84 ÷ 48 = 1.75 h = **1 h 45 min** (0.75 × 60 = 45).

### Converting speed units

```
km/h -> m/s: × 1000 ÷ 3600 (the same as ÷ 3.6)
m/s -> km/h: × 3600 ÷ 1000 (the same as × 3.6)
```

So 15 m/s = 54 km/h and 90 km/h = 25 m/s. A speed in m/s is always the smaller number.

## 4.4 G: Density and pressure

```
density = mass ÷ volume         common units g/cm³ or kg/m³
pressure = force ÷ area         common units N/m² or N/cm²
```

The pressure formula is given in the question; the density formula is not, so learn it.

**Worked example 9 (density).** A metal block is a cuboid 4 cm by 5 cm by 10 cm. The metal has density 8.9 g/cm³. Find the mass of the block in kilograms.

```
volume = 4 × 5 × 10 = 200 cm³
mass = density × volume = 8.9 × 200 = 1780 g = 1.78 kg
```

**Worked example 10 (mixing).** 300 cm³ of liquid A (density 0.8 g/cm³) is mixed with 200 cm³ of liquid B (density 1.2 g/cm³). Find the density of the mixture.

```
mass of A = 0.8 × 300 = 240 g
mass of B = 1.2 × 200 = 240 g
density = total mass ÷ total volume = 480 ÷ 500 = 0.96 g/cm³
```

Do not average 0.8 and 1.2: that gives 1.0, which ignores the different volumes.

**Worked example 11 (pressure).** A box has a weight of 120 N. It rests on a face measuring 0.5 m by 0.4 m. It is turned onto a face measuring 0.4 m by 0.3 m. Find the pressure in each position.

```
first face:  area = 0.5 × 0.4 = 0.2 m²   pressure = 120 ÷ 0.2 = 600 N/m²
second face: area = 0.4 × 0.3 = 0.12 m²  pressure = 120 ÷ 0.12 = 1000 N/m²
```

The same force on a smaller area gives a greater pressure.

## Exam technique

- **"Work out"** needs method shown: write the formula, substitute, then evaluate.
- **"Give your answer in ..."** means a unit conversion is part of the marks. Check the final unit before you stop.
- **"Estimate"** means round to sensible values and state any assumption.

## Common mistakes

- Counting marks instead of gaps when finding the value of one division.
- Treating 1 h 20 min as 1.2 h; it is 1⅓ h, about 1.33 h.
- Averaging two speeds or two densities instead of using totals.
- Measuring a bearing anticlockwise or from the wrong point.
- Reading the inner protractor scale when the angle starts on the outer zero.
- Mixing units, such as a mass in kg with a volume in cm³.
