These revision notes condense rights and social protest for IB MYP Individuals and Societies, aligned to the International Baccalaureate Organization, Middle Years Programme Subject Brief -- *Individuals and societies*, from 2014. They suit MYP years 4 and 5 (no SL/HL split). "Rights and social protest" is a topic the brief lists for the on-screen examinations; MYP has no prescribed content list, so schools choose their own case studies, and these notes give you a reliable core.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition to learn |
|---|---|
| Human rights | Rights every person has because they are human; universal, inalienable, indivisible |
| Civil rights | Rights a state guarantees its citizens in law (equal treatment, fair trial, the vote) |
| De jure / de facto | In law / in practice |
| Segregation | Enforced separation of groups, e.g. in schools, transport, housing |
| Apartheid | South Africa's system of racial separation and white minority rule, built from 1948 |
| Suffrage | The right to vote; universal suffrage means all adults can vote |
| Suffragist / suffragette | Lawful campaigner (NUWSS) / militant campaigner (WSPU) |
| Boycott | Refusing to buy, use or take part in something to apply pressure |
| Sit-in | Occupying a place and refusing to leave to challenge a rule |
| Civil disobedience | Openly breaking an unjust law and accepting the penalty |
| Direct action | Acting to cause change directly rather than asking authorities |
| Sanctions / embargo | Economic or trade penalties / a ban on trade in certain goods |

## UDHR facts

| Item | Fact |
|---|---|
| Adopted | 10 December 1948, UN General Assembly, Paris |
| Vote | 48 for, 0 against, 8 abstentions (58 UN members) |
| Abstainers included | USSR and five other Eastern European members, South Africa, Saudi Arabia |
| Drafting | Commission chaired by Eleanor Roosevelt |
| Length | 30 articles |
| Status | A declaration, not a binding treaty |
| Binding follow-up | ICCPR and ICESCR, adopted 1966, in force 1976 |
| Together called | The International Bill of Human Rights |

**Articles to quote:** 2 non-discrimination; 3 life, liberty, security; 7 equality before the law; 9 no arbitrary arrest; 13 freedom of movement; 19 expression; 20 peaceful assembly; 21 take part in government and vote; 23 work and equal pay; 26 education.

## Methods: one example each

| Method | Example |
|---|---|
| Legal challenge | *Brown v. Board*, 1954 |
| Economic boycott | Montgomery, 1955–56; international boycotts of South Africa |
| Sit-in | Greensboro, 1960 |
| Mass march | March on Washington, 1963 |
| Petitions and lobbying | NUWSS |
| Militant action | WSPU window-smashing and arson |
| Armed struggle | Umkhonto we Sizwe, from 1961 |
| International pressure | UN arms embargo, 1977 |

Gene Sharp (1973) listed 198 non-violent methods in three groups: protest and persuasion, non-cooperation, non-violent intervention.

## Method in steps

**Judging whether a movement was effective**

1. State its aims.
2. Test de jure change: which laws changed, and when?
3. Test de facto change: did daily life change?
4. Separate short-term from long-term results.
5. Weigh the factors: methods, leadership, unity, media, economic pressure, government response, international pressure, outside events.
6. Reach a judgement that answers the question's wording ("to what extent", "how far").

**Evaluating a source on protest**

1. Origin: who produced it, when, and were they involved?
2. Purpose: to inform, persuade, justify or record?
3. Value: what can it reliably show for this inquiry?
4. Limitation: what can it not show, and why? Use the same origin or purpose fact.

## Worked reminders

A fictional petition needs 120,000 signatures and has 84,000.

```
84,000 ÷ 120,000 × 100 = 70% of the target
```

A fictional march grew from 2,500 to 11,000 people.

```
(11,000 − 2,500) ÷ 2,500 × 100 = 340% increase
```

Divide by the **starting** value. For rates already in percent, a change from 25% to 65% is **40 percentage points**.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Human rights vs civil rights.** Everyone's by being human vs guaranteed by a state's law.
- **De jure vs de facto.** The 15th Amendment (1870) gave a legal right to vote; the Voting Rights Act (1965) tackled the practice.
- **Declaration vs treaty.** The UDHR (1948) is not binding; the 1966 covenants bind states that ratify them.
- **Suffragists vs suffragettes.** NUWSS lawful methods vs WSPU militancy.
- **Non-violent vs violent.** The ANC used non-violent mass action until 1960, then added armed struggle from 1961.
- **Boycott vs strike.** Consumers withhold spending vs workers withhold labour.

## Linking to concepts

- Key concept: **change**; global context: *fairness and development*.
- Related concept: **causality** for "why did it succeed?" questions.
- *Identities and relationships* fits how movements build group identity.
- *Orientation in space and time* fits turning points such as 1948, 1964 and 1994.

## Quick self-test

1. Distinguish human rights from civil rights.
2. When, where and by which body was the UDHR adopted?
3. What percentage of the 58 UN members voted in favour of the UDHR? Give 3 significant figures.
4. Why is the UDHR not legally binding, and which two treaties followed in 1966?
5. Which UDHR article protects peaceful assembly?
6. What did *Brown v. Board of Education* (1954) decide?
7. How long did the Montgomery Bus Boycott last, and who led it?
8. What happened at Sharpeville on 21 March 1960, and how did the government respond?
9. Why did students protest in Soweto on 16 June 1976?
10. Which women gained the vote in Britain in 1918, and what changed in 1928?
11. Name Gene Sharp's three groups of non-violent methods.
12. Registration rose from 25% to 65%. Give the change correctly.

### Answers

1. Human rights belong to every person by being human; civil rights are guaranteed to citizens by a state's law.
2. 10 December 1948, in Paris, by the UN General Assembly.
3. 48 ÷ 58 × 100 = **82.8%**.
4. It is a General Assembly declaration, not a treaty; the ICCPR and ICESCR.
5. Article 20.
6. Segregated public schools were unconstitutional.
7. 381 days; Martin Luther King Jr.
8. Police killed 69 people protesting against the pass laws; the ANC and PAC were banned.
9. Against Afrikaans being imposed as a language of teaching.
10. Women over 30 with a property qualification; in 1928 women got the vote at 21, on equal terms with men.
11. Protest and persuasion; non-cooperation; non-violent intervention.
12. A rise of **40 percentage points**.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calling the UDHR international law or a treaty.
- Writing "all women got the vote in 1918".
- Confusing suffragists and suffragettes, or treating the WSPU as non-violent.
- Describing the anti-apartheid struggle as only non-violent, or forgetting the role of international pressure.
- Saying a law "ended discrimination" without testing whether practice changed.
- Judging success without stating the movement's aims first.
- Generic source comments ("it is biased") not tied to the author's origin or purpose.
- Saying "a 40% rise" when a rate went from 25% to 65%.
- Vague dates ("in the 1960s") where a precise, well-known date exists, such as 2 July 1964.
