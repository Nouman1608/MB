This page is a guide to OxfordAQA International GCSE Chemistry (9202) past papers. It covers the November 2022 series in detail, with notes on what the examiners saw written by us from OxfordAQA's Report on the examination for each paper, and it lists the June 2023 and June 2024 series so you know which papers exist. We do not host any papers. Every paper, mark scheme and report belongs to OxfordAQA and is available from the board's own site.

One thing to know before you start: we have not printed any grade thresholds on this page. We could not retrieve a grade-boundary document for any 9202 series, and we do not estimate or recall numbers. Each series section below says so. For the boundaries themselves, use the board's own results and grade-boundary information.

## Where to get the official papers

- [OxfordAQA International GCSE Chemistry (9202) qualification page](https://www.oxfordaqa.com/qualifications/international-gcse-chemistry/)

The qualification page is the starting point for the specification, assessment details and the exam materials OxfordAQA publishes for this subject. Past papers, mark schemes and examiner reports for recent series are released through the board, and some materials may require a centre login.

Use the three documents together. Sit the paper first, under timed conditions, then mark it strictly with the mark scheme, which shows what each mark needs. After that, read the Report on the examination for that paper. It explains why answers that looked reasonable still lost marks, and it names the questions that separated stronger and weaker candidates. The notes for each series below are a quick way into that report, not a replacement for it.

Check which specification a paper was set on. The 9202 specification this page follows is version 6.2, for teaching from September 2016 and for exams from May/June 2018 onwards. It is a linear qualification taken at the end of the course. The assessment is two written papers, Paper 1 and Paper 2, each lasting 90 minutes and worth 90 marks, with equal weighting. Any part of the specification may be assessed on either paper, and there is no separate practical exam, so practical skills are tested inside the written papers. The three series covered here all fall within that specification's life, but always check the version on a paper's front cover before relying on a question from a different year.

## What the November 2022 reports keep returning to

The November 2022 reports for the two papers share several threads. They are worth reading before you work through any 9202 paper, because the same habits cost marks in every series.

**Say what the particles or bonds are doing, not what the diagram looks like**

- Candidates described a picture of a structure when the question was about the strength of the bonds in it.
- In electricity and conduction answers, ions and delocalised electrons were swapped. Conduction in a molten compound is by mobile ions, and conduction in a metal is by delocalised electrons that move through the structure.
- Ionic structures need the idea of a giant lattice with forces acting in every direction, and answers often left that out.

**Calculations are a strength, planning and evaluation are not**

- Straightforward calculations (percentages, formula masses, reacting masses, a rate from a graph) were done well in both papers.
- Calculations that needed a mole ratio, a multiplication by the number of molecules or a link to the formula mass tripped up more candidates.
- Planning a practical method was hard on Paper 1, and the evaluation of data from a table or graph, including a reasoned conclusion, separated the top scripts on Paper 2.

**Precision of wording**

- Several marks were lost for answers that were nearly right but missing a key word, such as the idea of a minimum energy, the surroundings in a heat-loss answer, or the fact that something is free to move through a structure.
- Comparisons need comparative language, such as a lower temperature rise or fewer bubbles, rather than a bare description.

## November 2022

Both papers were sat in November 2022 and both have a Report on the examination. The Paper 1 report says candidates performed much as in recent series, with a wide spread of marks. It singles out calculations as a strength and planning a practical investigation as a repeat weakness. The Paper 2 report describes a paper with some low-demand opening material that went well and several high-demand, evaluative questions that only the strongest candidates handled fully.

**Where marks were lost**

Paper 1:

- Separation and purity: reasoning about a chromatography-style result drifted into the wrong property, and some candidates thought a pure substance would melt over the widest range or at the highest temperature.
- Giant structures: layered carbon was recognised, but the number of bonds each atom forms was often wrong, and some answers described the diagram rather than the bond strength.
- Reaction rate: very few candidates linked a faster rate to more particles in a given volume, and some believed particles gain energy at higher concentration. A mass-time graph was read as if the reaction had stopped when it had only slowed.
- Planning: method plans often failed to keep the amount of solid and the volume of acid the same, and the way of collecting the gas was weak.
- Industrial chemistry of ammonia: the conditions were poorly known, the recycling of unreacted gases was often missed, and the cost benefit of a catalyst was stated without being explained. A mole-ratio calculation lost marks when the number of ammonia molecules was not scaled.
- Organic chemistry: confusion between alcohols and esters, drawing a displayed formula, and the weak-acid idea that acids ionise in water. An empirical formula was usually found, but the step to the molecular formula via the formula mass was often missing.
- Metal extraction and electrolysis: displacement was named but not described, ions were confused with delocalised electrons in the molten state, half-equations were mostly not completed, and the wearing away of the positive electrode was noticed but not explained. Aluminium was sometimes shown as diatomic.
- Energy changes: the word minimum was missing from activation energy answers, labelling activation energy and enthalpy change on a profile was the least secure part, and bond breaking and making were not linked to the exothermic result. A gas-volume calculation went wrong when the molar ratio was ignored.

Paper 2:

- Atomic structure and the periodic table: the opening questions were answered well. Marks were lost on the corrected atom diagram when the numbers of each particle were not stated.
- Periodic table and bonding: describing an ionic compound often left out the giant structure, and the forces in all directions were rarely mentioned.
- Polymers and carbon compounds: reasoned comparison of polymer properties separated the grades, with weaker answers giving no conclusion or only quoting the table. Completing a repeating unit and a percentage taken from a graph were each done by fewer than half.
- Energy from fuels: why results were lower than expected needed the surroundings, not just heat loss. Showing that two quantities are not directly proportional needed numbers, and the energy calculation often used the wrong mass.
- Metals and extraction: an evaluation of phytomining needed the calculation of mass per area before a conclusion, and only a quarter reached the top level. The conductivity of copper needed delocalised electrons that are free to move through the structure, not just throughout it.
- Reactivity and salts: comparative observations were missing in some answers, burning and glowing splints were confused, and a high-demand mass-loss question was hard because the small mass of hydrogen was not turned into a small loss.
- Analysis and precipitation: the extended question on identifying ions was good for those who described several tests. The method for obtaining the mass of a precipitate lost marks when washing and drying were left out. Almost nobody explained why conductivity fell to zero, because electrons were mentioned instead of ions.
- Equilibrium and yield: reasons for a lower yield than expected were often limited to one. Pressure and temperature effects on yield were described from the figure but seldom explained.

## How to use this page

1. Pick a series and sit Paper 1 and Paper 2 under timed conditions, 90 minutes each.
2. Mark each paper strictly with the official mark scheme.
3. Read the notes for that series here, then the full Report on the examination. Write down each mark you lost for a reason listed there.
4. Fix weak topics with the revision notes linked above, then try the paper again after a gap.
5. Compare your raw mark with the board's boundaries for that series only as a rough guide.

## Frequently asked questions

**Where can I find OxfordAQA International GCSE Chemistry 9202 past papers?**
OxfordAQA publishes past papers, mark schemes and examiner reports through its own website, starting from the 9202 qualification page. Some materials may need a centre login.

**How many papers are there in 9202 and what do they cover?**
There are two papers, each 90 minutes and 90 marks, with equal weighting. Any part of the specification can appear on either paper, and there is no separate practical exam.

**Why does this page not show grade thresholds?**
We could not retrieve a grade-boundary document for any 9202 series, and we do not estimate numbers. Use the board's own grade-boundary information for the series you are practising.

**What do examiners say candidates lose marks on most in November 2022?**
Mole-ratio steps in calculations, planning a fair method, explaining rate in terms of particles in a given volume, mixing up mobile ions and delocalised electrons, and answers that are close but miss a key word.

**Can grade thresholds tell me what mark I need next time?**
No. Thresholds change every series and should only be used as a rough guide for the series they belong to, never to predict future ones.
