Group behaviour is the first of the two aspects of the **human relationships** context in IB Psychology (first assessment 2027). The guide's idea is that people are social and become part of groups gradually, by learning acceptable behaviours and norms. It lists six areas of study: acculturation, compliance techniques, conformity, cultural dimensions, social identity theory and social learning. This guide teaches each one with a definition, a worked example, evaluation and a link to the concepts. The guide names no compulsory studies here, so the studies below are well-established examples you can swap for your own.

Related: [overview of the context](/resources/ib-dp-psychology-2027-human-relationships/), [revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-psychology-2027-human-relationships-group-behaviour-revision-notes/) and [practice set](/resources/ib-dp-psychology-2027-human-relationships-group-behaviour-practice/).

## 1. Acculturation

**Acculturation** is the process of change that happens when people from one culture live in sustained contact with another culture. The guide asks for "different acculturation strategies and their effect on behaviour and mental health". It also lists models of acculturation as a sociocultural term, with the application "to explain the experience of immigrants, refugees or other people taking an extended stay in another culture".

**Berry's model** is the standard answer. It asks two questions about a person's situation: *Do I want to keep my own culture?* and *Do I want contact with the new culture?* The four combinations are the four strategies.

| Strategy | Keep own culture? | Contact with new culture? | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Integration | Yes | Yes | A student keeps speaking Urdu at home and joins the school football team |
| Assimilation | No | Yes | A family adopts the local language and customs and drops its own |
| Separation | Yes | No | A community lives mainly within its own networks and avoids the new culture |
| Marginalisation | No | No | A person feels cut off from both cultures |

**Effects on behaviour and mental health.** Moving between cultures can cause **acculturative stress**: pressure from language barriers, discrimination, loss of social support and conflicting expectations. Berry and colleagues (2006) studied young people from immigrant families in many societies and reported that an integration profile was generally linked to better psychological and social adaptation than marginalisation. That fits the common prediction: integration gives the person two sources of support and identity, while marginalisation gives none.

**Worked example.** A teenager moves from Karachi to Leeds. At home the family eats together, uses Urdu and respects elders. At school she wants friends who play sport after class. If she chooses integration, she has a home network and a school network. If her school treats her as an outsider and her family forbids school activities, she may drift towards marginalisation. A strong answer says which factors push her towards each strategy (school climate, family flexibility, discrimination) and why mental health would differ.

**Evaluation.**
- The model is simple and useful, but real people shift between strategies depending on the setting (home, work, online).
- The strategy is not always a free choice. A host society that pressures people to assimilate, or excludes them, restricts the options.
- Research is mostly correlational and self-reported, so it cannot show whether integration causes good mental health or well-adjusted people choose integration.
- **Concepts:** change, perspective and responsibility (avoiding stigma when reporting results on immigrant groups).

## 2. Conformity

**Conformity** is a change in behaviour or belief to match the group, because of real or imagined group pressure. Two reasons are usually distinguished (Deutsch and Gerard, 1955).
- **Normative influence:** going along to be liked or avoid rejection. Behaviour changes, belief may not.
- **Informational influence:** going along because you think the group knows better, especially when the situation is unclear. Belief often changes too.

**Asch (1951, 1956).** In the line-judgement experiments, a participant and several confederates judged which comparison line matched a standard line. The confederates gave the same wrong answer on set trials. About a third of responses on these critical trials followed the majority, and about three-quarters of participants conformed at least once. Some participants never did. Asch also found that conformity dropped when one confederate gave the correct answer, which shows that an ally reduces pressure.

**Sherif (1935).** Participants judged how far a point of light seemed to move in a dark room (the autokinetic effect). Alone, estimates varied. In a group, estimates converged to a group norm, an example of informational influence in an ambiguous task.

**Worked example.** A student in a group chat is asked whether a classmate's joke was acceptable. Everyone else replies "hilarious". The student privately thinks it was unkind but replies "ha". This is normative influence, public compliance without private acceptance. If the student is new to the school and unsure of its norms, informational influence also plays a part.

**Evaluation.**
- Strength: laboratory control gives clear cause and effect, and the findings have been repeated in many versions.
- Limitation: a line task is artificial, and the stakes are low (low ecological validity). Early samples were mostly American male students, so there is cultural and gender bias.
- Ethics: deception and stress for participants, so debriefing is essential.
- Culture: a meta-analysis by Bond and Smith (1996) of conformity studies using the Asch task in several countries found higher levels in collectivist than individualist cultures, which links conformity to cultural dimensions.
- **Concepts:** causality, bias, responsibility and measurement (is a line task a valid measure of real social pressure?).

## 3. Cultural dimensions

**Cultural dimensions** are measurable ways in which national or cultural groups differ in values. The best-known set comes from Hofstede, who analysed surveys of employees of one multinational company across many countries. The first four dimensions were:
- individualism versus collectivism (the weight given to personal goals versus group goals);
- power distance (acceptance of unequal power);
- uncertainty avoidance (discomfort with ambiguity);
- masculinity versus femininity (competition and assertiveness versus care and cooperation).

Later work added long-term orientation and indulgence versus restraint.

**Using them to understand group behaviour.** A collectivist culture might be expected to show more conformity and stronger in-group loyalty, while an individualist culture might reward standing out. Bond and Smith's finding on conformity is the usual link. A high power distance culture may show deference to a group leader, which affects how decisions are made at work or school.

**Worked example.** Two companies run the same brainstorming meeting. In one country, junior staff rarely contradict a manager. In another, challenge is expected. Hofstede's power distance gives a prediction, but a good answer adds that individuals vary and that organisational culture matters as much as national culture.

**Evaluation.**
- Useful framework for cross-cultural comparison, easy to test.
- The data were collected from one company in the late twentieth century, so they may not generalise to whole societies or to today.
- Dimensions describe averages, so applying them to an individual risks stereotyping (the ecological fallacy).
- It is an etic approach (an outside, comparative view). An emic study of one group may reveal meanings the dimensions miss.
- **Concepts:** perspective, measurement and bias.

## 4. Social identity theory

**Social identity theory (SIT)**, developed by Tajfel and Turner, explains why belonging matters. It proposes three processes.
1. **Categorisation:** we sort people into groups (including ourselves).
2. **Identification:** we adopt the identity of our group and take pride from it.
3. **Comparison:** we compare our group with others, and to feel good we favour the in-group.

The result is **in-group favouritism** and, sometimes, hostility to the out-group.

**Tajfel et al. (1971), the minimal group studies.** Schoolboys were assigned to groups on a trivial basis, such as a supposed preference for a type of painting. They then allocated points to anonymous others. They consistently gave more to members of their own group, even when this gave them no personal gain. The study suggests that mere categorisation is enough to produce bias.

**Changing group behaviour with SIT.** If conflict comes from competition between groups, then reducing it means changing identities and goals. In Sherif's Robbers Cave study (1954), two groups of boys at a summer camp developed hostility during competition. Tasks that needed both groups to cooperate toward shared (superordinate) goals reduced the conflict. Modern applications include mixed-group projects in schools and shared-identity messages in organisations.

**Worked example.** Two year groups compete in a sports day and start insulting each other online. A teacher applies SIT: replace year-group competition with mixed teams (new categorisation), give a joint goal (raise funds for a school charity), and praise the whole school as the in-group (a broader identity).

**Evaluation.**
- Strength: explains discrimination without needing prior conflict, and generates practical interventions.
- Limitation: minimal group studies are artificial, and the points allocated may reflect demand characteristics. The theory says little about why some people identify more strongly than others.
- Ethics of the camp study: boys were deceived and put in conflict, so protection from harm matters.
- **Concepts:** change, causality, bias (in-group bias) and responsibility.

## 5. Compliance techniques

**Compliance** is going along with a direct request. Unlike conformity, it needs no group pressure, only a request. The guide asks you to apply one or more techniques to change group behaviour.

| Technique | How it works | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Foot-in-the-door | A small request first, then a larger one | A club asks for a one-minute survey, then asks for a monthly donation |
| Door-in-the-face | A large request is refused, then a smaller one is made | A coach asks for a whole weekend of volunteering, then for one afternoon |
| Low-ball | Agreement is gained first, then the cost is raised | A group agrees to a free trip, then costs are added |
| That's-not-all | An offer is improved before the person answers | A stall adds a free item before you decide |

**Evidence.** Freedman and Fraser (1966) found that householders who first agreed to a small request were far more likely to agree to a large one later than those asked only the large request. Cialdini and colleagues (1975) found that people asked first for a very large favour and then a smaller one agreed to the smaller one much more often than people asked only for the smaller favour. The usual explanations are self-perception (I am the kind of person who helps) and reciprocity (they made a concession, so should I).

**Worked example (applying to change a group).** A school wants more students to join a recycling scheme. It first asks everyone to sign a short pledge (foot-in-the-door), then asks signers to join a weekly rota. The principle is that small commitments change self-image.

**Evaluation.**
- Strength: real-world field experiments, so higher ecological validity than many lab studies.
- Limitation: effects vary by culture and situation, and some replications show small effects.
- Responsibility: the same techniques can manipulate people, so a researcher who publishes them has a duty to consider misuse. Cost-benefit analysis applies.
- **Concepts:** change, causality, responsibility.

## 6. Social learning

**Social learning theory (SLT)**, by Bandura, says that people learn by observing models. Four processes are needed: **attention**, **retention**, **reproduction** and **motivation**. Motivation depends on vicarious reinforcement, seeing a model rewarded or punished. In the Bobo doll studies (1961), children who watched an adult attack an inflatable doll were more likely to imitate that aggression than those who saw a non-aggressive model.

**Learning group norms.** SLT helps explain how newcomers learn what is acceptable in a group. A new employee watches colleagues and copies how they speak and behave. Children in a playground see which actions earn status.

**Changing group behaviour.** Use respected role models. For example, a school asks a popular older student to model kind online behaviour, makes the benefits visible (friends respond positively) and gives younger students practice.

**Evaluation.**
- Strength: ties together observation, imitation and reinforcement and has wide practical use.
- Limitation: the Bobo studies were artificial, with children as participants, and the doll is not a real person. Imitation in the lab may not become real aggression.
- The theory underplays biological factors and individual choice.
- **Concepts:** change, causality, responsibility.

## Putting the six together

For a longer answer, link them. A newcomer acculturates (strategy), learns norms by social learning, sees the group as an in-group (SIT), conforms to keep acceptance, is exposed to compliance requests, and does all of it in a cultural frame described by dimensions. Good Section C answers show how two of these interact, for example how SIT explains why conformity is stronger inside a valued in-group.

## Common mistakes

- Listing Berry's four strategies without effects on mental health or any example.
- Confusing normative influence (public change) with informational influence (private belief change).
- Presenting cultural dimensions as facts about every person from a country.
- Describing a study at length without saying what it shows or how strong it is.
- Writing "SIT causes discrimination" instead of "SIT explains in-group favouritism through categorisation, identification and comparison".
