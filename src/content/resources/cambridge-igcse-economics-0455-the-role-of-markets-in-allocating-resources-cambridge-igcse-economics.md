## 2.2.1 How a market system works

A **market** brings together buyers and sellers of a product so that they can trade. In a **market system**, most decisions about the use of resources are made through markets rather than by a central authority.

### Two kinds of market

| Market | Buyers | Sellers | Price |
|---|---|---|---|
| Product market | Households (consumers) | Firms | The price of a good or service |
| Factor market | Firms | Households, which own labour, land and capital | Wages, rent and interest |

The two kinds of market are linked. Households earn incomes by selling factor services to firms, then spend those incomes buying products from firms. A change in one market therefore spreads to the other: when consumers buy more of a product, firms need more workers and materials to make it.

### Equilibrium and disequilibrium in brief

- **Equilibrium:** at the current price, the quantity buyers want equals the quantity sellers offer, so there is no pressure on the price to change.
- **Disequilibrium:** at the current price, the two quantities differ. A **shortage** (demand greater than supply) pushes the price up; a **surplus** (supply greater than demand) pushes it down.

The detailed schedules and diagrams belong to price determination (2.5). For 2.2 the key idea is that disequilibrium sets prices moving, and moving prices are what shift resources.

### Worked example: resources follow a change in tastes

In the town of Kessan (invented), people switch from petrol scooters to electric bicycles.

1. **Product market, electric bicycles:** demand rises. At the old price of 400 there is a shortage, so shops raise the price to 460, a rise of 60 ÷ 400 × 100 = **15%**.
2. **Profit signal:** a bicycle that costs 340 to make now earns 460 − 340 = **120** profit instead of 60. Profit per bicycle has doubled.
3. **Product market, petrol scooters:** demand falls. Surplus stock builds up and sellers cut prices, so scooter profits fall.
4. **Factor markets:** bicycle makers want more mechanics and offer higher wages, say 15 an hour instead of 12. Mechanics move from scooter workshops to bicycle workshops. Floor space once used to store scooters is rented to bicycle firms.
5. **Result:** labour, capital and land have moved from scooters to bicycles. No one ordered the move; price and profit changes caused it.

## 2.2.2 The three key allocation decisions

Because resources are scarce and wants are unlimited, every economy must decide:

1. **What to produce:** which goods and services, and in what quantities.
2. **How to produce:** which combination of factors and which methods.
3. **For whom to produce:** who receives the output.

These questions apply to any economic system. A market system answers them through prices; a planned system answers them by government decision; a mixed system uses both. Section 2.2 concentrates on the market answer.

## 2.2.3 How the price mechanism answers the questions

The **price mechanism** is the system in which the forces of demand and supply set prices, and those prices guide the decisions of buyers and sellers.

### What to produce

Consumers "vote" with their spending. Products that buyers want more of rise in price and become more profitable, so firms switch resources into them. Products buyers want less of fall in price and lose profit, so resources leave. The Kessan example above is this process in action.

### How to produce

Firms want the lowest cost per unit, so they compare the prices of factors.

**Worked example: packing tea.** A tea firm must pack 10,000 boxes a week.

| Method | Workers needed | Weekly wage per worker | Machine hire per week | Total weekly cost |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Labour-intensive | 10 | 300 | 0 | 10 × 300 = 3,000 |
| Capital-intensive | 2 | 300 | 2,200 | 2 × 300 + 2,200 = 2,800 |

1. Cost per box: labour-intensive 3,000 ÷ 10,000 = **0.30**; capital-intensive 2,800 ÷ 10,000 = **0.28**. The firm chooses the machine.
2. If the weekly wage were 200 instead, the costs would be 10 × 200 = 2,000 and 2 × 200 + 2,200 = 2,600. Labour-intensive packing becomes cheaper.

The price of each factor, set in factor markets, answers "how". Where labour is plentiful and cheap, labour-intensive methods tend to be chosen; where wages are high, firms substitute capital for labour.

### For whom to produce

Products go to buyers who are willing and able to pay the market price. The price **rations** a scarce product among those who want it.

**Worked example: rationing by price.** A concert hall in Kessan has 5 seats left. Eight people want one seat, and each would pay up to the amount shown.

| Buyer | P | Q | R | S | T | U | V | W |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Most willing to pay | 90 | 75 | 70 | 60 | 55 | 50 | 40 | 30 |

1. At a price of 40, seven people (all except W) want a seat. There is a shortage of 7 − 5 = 2 seats.
2. The price rises. At 55, exactly five people (P, Q, R, S, T) are willing to pay, so 5 seats meet 5 buyers.
3. The seats go to those willing and able to pay at least 55. U, V and W miss out, even though U might enjoy the concert just as much but have less money.

This shows both the strength and the weakness of the price mechanism. It allocates scarce goods quickly without queues or officials, but it allocates them according to ability to pay, which depends on the distribution of income.

### Why prices work as signals

- **They carry information.** A rising price tells producers that consumers value a product more, without anyone collecting survey data.
- **They create incentives.** Higher profits attract firms and factors; lower profits push them out.
- **They ration.** When supply is limited, price rises until the quantity wanted matches the quantity available.

## Limits worth noting

The price mechanism does not always lead to the best allocation for society. Goods that people cannot be charged for may not be produced; some products harm people who are not part of the trade; and output follows money rather than need. These are developed under market failure (2.10) and the mixed economic system (2.11).

## Common mistakes

- Describing what, how and for whom without linking each to price or profit changes.
- Forgetting factor markets: resources move because wages, rent and interest change too.
- Saying that the price mechanism gives products to "those who need them most". It gives them to those willing and able to pay.
- Writing that the government decides what is produced in a market system.
- Mixing up shortage and surplus when explaining why prices change.

## Exam technique for the 0455 command words

- **Identify** the three questions by name: what, how, for whom.
- **Explain** a reallocation as a chain: change in demand → shortage or surplus → price change → profit change → resources move.
- **Calculate** costs per unit for two production methods, then state which method the firm chooses and why.
- **Analyse** a source on a changing market by following the chain into factor markets.
- **Discuss** whether markets allocate resources well: speed and incentives on one side, ability-to-pay and missing products on the other, then a judgement.
