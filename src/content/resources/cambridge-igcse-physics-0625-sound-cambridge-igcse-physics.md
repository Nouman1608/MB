## How sound is produced and travels

Every sound comes from something **vibrating**: a guitar string, the cone of a loudspeaker, the vocal cords, a struck tuning fork. The vibrating surface pushes on the air particles next to it, which push on their neighbours, and so on. The particles themselves only vibrate **backwards and forwards about a fixed position**, in the same direction as the wave travels: sound is a **longitudinal** wave. Energy moves from the source to your ear; the air does not.

**Extended only.** As the surface moves forward it squeezes the particles together, making a **compression** (a region of higher pressure). As it moves back it leaves the particles spread out, making a **rarefaction** (a region of lower pressure). A sound wave is a series of compressions and rarefactions moving away from the source. The wavelength is the distance from the centre of one compression to the centre of the next.

**A medium is needed.** Because sound is passed on by particles, it cannot travel through a vacuum. The standard demonstration: an electric bell rings inside a sealed glass jar. As a vacuum pump removes the air, the sound becomes fainter until it can hardly be heard, even though the hammer can still be seen striking the bell. Letting air back in restores the sound. (Light still crosses the vacuum, which is why you can see the bell.)

**Extended only: speed in different materials.** In general sound travels fastest in **solids**, more slowly in **liquids** and most slowly in **gases**. In a solid the particles are close together and strongly linked, so each vibration is passed on quickly. Typical values to use when a question gives them: about 340 m/s in air, about 1500 m/s in water and several thousand metres per second in steel.

## The audible range and ultrasound

Humans can hear frequencies from about **20 Hz to 20 000 Hz** (20 kHz). Sound with a frequency **above 20 kHz** is **ultrasound**: it is still sound (a longitudinal mechanical wave), just too high-pitched for humans to hear.

You can use v = fλ from section 3.1 with sound. With v = 340 m/s, a 20 Hz sound has λ = 340 / 20 = 17 m, and a 20 000 Hz sound has λ = 340 / 20 000 = 0.017 m (1.7 cm).

## Loudness and pitch

| Change | Effect | On a display of the wave |
|---|---|---|
| Larger amplitude | **Louder** sound | Taller peaks, same spacing |
| Smaller amplitude | Quieter sound | Shorter peaks |
| Higher frequency | **Higher pitch** | More waves across the same width |
| Lower frequency | Lower pitch | Fewer, more widely spaced waves |

Amplitude and frequency are independent: a sound can be loud and low, or quiet and high. When comparing two traces drawn on the same scales, compare peak height for loudness and the number of complete waves for pitch. Plucking a guitar string harder increases the amplitude (louder) but not the frequency; shortening or tightening the string increases the frequency (higher pitch).

## Measuring the speed of sound in air

The syllabus asks for a method that measures a **distance and a time**. Two standard versions:

**Method 1: two observers.** Observer A stands at least 100 m away from observer B, in an open space, with the distance measured using a long tape or trundle wheel. A makes a sound that can also be seen, such as banging two blocks together above the head or firing a starting pistol. B starts a stopwatch on seeing the action (light arrives almost instantly) and stops it on hearing the sound. Speed = distance / time. Repeat several times and average, then swap positions to cancel any effect of wind. A long distance matters because the reaction time is a large fraction of a short time.

**Method 2: echoes.** Stand a measured distance (for example 85 m) from a large flat wall and clap. An echo is sound **reflected** from the wall, so the sound travels **to the wall and back**: total distance = 2 × 85 = 170 m. Single echo times are too short to measure well, so clap in a steady rhythm so that each clap coincides with the echo of the one before, and time many intervals.

### Worked example 1

A student 85 m from a wall claps in time with the echoes. Another student times 20 intervals as 10.2 s. Calculate the speed of sound.

```
time for one interval = 10.2 / 20 = 0.51 s
distance in that time = 2 × 85 = 170 m
speed = 170 / 0.51 = 333 m/s   (330 m/s to 2 s.f.)
```

The answer lies in the expected range of 330–350 m/s, which is a useful check. Forgetting the factor of 2 gives 167 m/s, which should immediately look wrong.

## Uses of ultrasound (Extended only)

All three uses work the same way: a short pulse of ultrasound is sent out, part of it reflects from a boundary, and the time for the echo to return is measured. Then:

**distance to the boundary = (speed × time) ÷ 2**

- **Non-destructive testing:** a pulse is sent into a metal part. The far side returns an echo; a crack or hollow inside returns an extra, **earlier** echo. The part is checked without being cut or damaged.
- **Medical scanning of soft tissue:** pulses partly reflect at each boundary between tissues, and the echo times are used to build an image, for example of a fetus.
- **Sonar:** a ship sends pulses down and times echoes from the sea bed or a shoal of fish.

### Worked example 2 (Extended)

A steel rail section is 0.30 m long. An ultrasound pulse sent in at one end returns from the far end after 1.0 × 10⁻⁴ s. A second, smaller echo returns after 4.0 × 10⁻⁵ s. Find the speed of sound in the steel and the depth of the flaw.

```
speed = (2 × 0.30) / 1.0 × 10⁻⁴ = 6000 m/s
flaw depth = (6000 × 4.0 × 10⁻⁵) / 2 = 0.12 m from the end
```

Ultrasound is used for these tasks because its short wavelength gives detailed reflections and, in medicine, because it is not ionising.

## Common mistakes

- Calling sound transverse, or saying the air particles travel from the source to the ear.
- Forgetting the factor of 2 for echoes.
- Using a short distance (a few metres) in the two-observer method.
- Linking loudness to frequency, or pitch to amplitude.
- Saying ultrasound is "not sound" or that it is an electromagnetic wave.
- Saying sound travels faster in air than in water.

## Exam technique

- **Describe** a method: name the apparatus, what is measured (distance and time), how, and the calculation. Add one improvement such as repeats or a larger distance.
- **Explain** why sound cannot cross a vacuum: link it to particles passing on the vibration.
- **Calculate:** write speed = distance / time, decide whether the distance is there and back, and include units.
- **Compare** two traces: comment on both amplitude and frequency, with the effect on loudness and pitch.
