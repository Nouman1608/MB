These questions practise Criterion B (investigating patterns) from the IB *Mathematics guide for the MYP* (first assessment 2022). Section A has short pattern and proof questions. Section B has small investigations that ask you to find a rule, verify it, and justify or prove it. A real Criterion B task is more open than these; the parts here show each step so you can check your own method. The marks are Marlbridge practice tariffs. Read the [Criterion B study guide](/resources/ib-ib-myp-mathematics-myp-mathematics-ib-myp-mathematics-assessment-criteria-b-investigating-patterns/) and the [Criterion B revision notes](/resources/ib-ib-myp-mathematics-myp-mathematics-ib-myp-mathematics-assessment-criteria-b-investigating-patterns-revision-notes/) first.

## Questions

### Section A: patterns and short proofs

**1.** The sequence 3, 8, 15, 24, 35, … continues in the same way.
**(a)** Find a general rule for the nth term. **[2]**
**(b)** Find the 20th term. **[1]**

**2.** The sequence 2, 6, 18, 54, … continues in the same way.
**(a)** Find a general rule for the nth term. **[2]**
**(b)** Which term is equal to 4374? **[2]**

**3.** The fractions 1/3, 2/5, 3/7, 4/9, … continue in the same way.
**(a)** Write down a rule for the nth fraction. **[1]**
**(b)** Show that every fraction in the pattern is less than 1/2. **[2]**

**4.** A student notices that 3, 7 and 11 fit the rule 4n − 1 and writes "so the rule is true for every term". Explain what is missing from the student's work. **[2]**

**5.** The sums of the first n odd numbers are 1, 1 + 3, 1 + 3 + 5, ….
**(a)** Find a rule for the sum of the first n odd numbers. **[1]**
**(b)** By considering (n + 1)² − n², justify your rule. **[2]**

### Section B: investigations

**6.** Squares are made in a row from matchsticks. One square uses 4 matchsticks, two squares use 7 and three squares use 10.
**(a)** Find a rule for the number of matchsticks M needed for n squares. **[2]**
**(b)** Verify your rule for 10 squares without using the rule to count. **[1]**
**(c)** Justify your rule from the structure of the arrangement. **[2]**
**(d)** Find the largest number of squares that can be made with 200 matchsticks. **[2]**

**7.** The triangular numbers are 1, 3, 6, 10, …, formed by rows of 1, 2, 3, 4, … dots.
**(a)** Find a rule for the nth triangular number Tₙ. **[2]**
**(b)** Prove that the sum of two consecutive triangular numbers, Tₙ + Tₙ₊₁, is always a square number. **[3]**

**8.** A large cube is built from n × n × n small cubes and its outside is painted.
**(a)** For n = 3 and n = 4, find how many small cubes have exactly 3, 2, 1 and 0 painted faces. **[2]**
**(b)** Find general rules, in terms of n, for the number of small cubes with exactly 3, 2, 1 and 0 painted faces, for n ≥ 2. **[4]**
**(c)** Verify that your rules account for every small cube when n = 5. **[2]**

**9.** A diagonal is drawn across a rectangle of m × n unit squares, where m and n have no common factor greater than 1. The number of squares the diagonal passes through is recorded.

| m × n | 1 × 2 | 2 × 3 | 3 × 4 | 2 × 5 | 3 × 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Squares crossed | 2 | 4 | 6 | 6 | 7 |

**(a)** Find a rule for the number of squares crossed. **[2]**
**(b)** Predict the number for a 4 × 5 rectangle and state how you would verify it. **[1]**
**(c)** A 2 × 4 rectangle gives 4 squares, not the 5 your rule predicts. Explain why. **[2]**

**10.** A sheet of paper 0.08 mm thick is cut in half, the pieces are stacked, and the stack is cut in half again, and so on.
**(a)** Write down a rule for the number of pieces after n cuts. **[1]**
**(b)** Find the height of the stack after 12 cuts. **[2]**
**(c)** Find the least number of cuts for the stack to be more than 1 m high. **[2]**

**11.** A strip of n unit squares in a row contains rectangles of different lengths. A strip of 3 squares contains 6 rectangles (three of length 1, two of length 2 and one of length 3).
**(a)** Find the number of rectangles in strips of 1, 2 and 4 squares. **[1]**
**(b)** Find a rule for a strip of n squares. **[2]**
**(c)** By thinking about the vertical lines in the strip, justify your rule. **[2]**

**12.** Consider S(n) = 1/(1 × 3) + 1/(3 × 5) + … + 1/((2n − 1)(2n + 1)).
**(a)** Find S(1), S(2) and S(3) as fractions in their lowest terms. **[2]**
**(b)** Make a conjecture for S(n). **[1]**
**(c)** Use the fact that 1/((2k − 1)(2k + 1)) = ½(1/(2k − 1) − 1/(2k + 1)) to prove your conjecture. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** **(a)** Second differences are constant at 2, so the rule contains n² [1]; the leftovers 2, 4, 6, 8, 10 are 2n, so **n² + 2n** [1]
**(b)** 20² + 2(20) = **440** [1] **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Writing the rule as n(n + 2) shows a structure: each term is one less than (n + 1)².

**2.** **(a)** Constant ratio 3 [1]; first term 2, so **2 × 3ⁿ⁻¹** [1]
**(b)** 3ⁿ⁻¹ = 2187 = 3⁷ [1], so n − 1 = 7 and it is the **8th term** [1] **[4]**

**3.** **(a)** **n/(2n + 1)** [1]
**(b)** n/(2n + 1) < 1/2 is equivalent to 2n < 2n + 1 (multiplying both sides by the positive 2(2n + 1)) [1], which is true for every n, so every fraction is less than 1/2 [1] **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Testing a few fractions only verifies the claim; reducing it to a statement that is always true proves it.

**4.** The rule has only been tested on the terms used to find it [1]; it needs verifying on a new term and justifying (for example, each step adds 4) or proving for every n [1] **[2]**

**5.** **(a)** The sums are 1, 4, 9, 16, so **n²** [1]
**(b)** (n + 1)² − n² = 2n + 1 [1], which is the (n + 1)th odd number, so adding the next odd number always takes one square number to the next; starting from 1 = 1², the sums stay square [1] **[3]**

**6.** **(a)** Difference 3 [1]; M = 3n + 1 [1]
**(b)** For 10 squares, draw or count: 1 left-hand stick plus 3 sticks for each square gives 31, and 3(10) + 1 = 31 [1]
**(c)** The first square needs a left-hand stick [1]; each square then adds a top, a bottom and a right-hand side, which is 3 sticks per square, giving 3n + 1 [1]
**(d)** 3n + 1 ≤ 200, so n ≤ 66.3 [1]; the largest whole number is **66 squares** (199 matchsticks) [1] **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* The justification must connect the 3 and the 1 to parts of the drawing, not just restate that the difference is 3.

**7.** **(a)** Second differences are constant at 1, or each Tₙ is half of an n by (n + 1) rectangle of dots [1], so **Tₙ = n(n + 1)/2** [1]
**(b)** Tₙ + Tₙ₊₁ = n(n + 1)/2 + (n + 1)(n + 2)/2 [1] = (n + 1)(n + n + 2)/2 = (n + 1)(2n + 2)/2 [1] = **(n + 1)²**, a square number for every n [1] **[5]**

**8.** **(a)** n = 3: 8, 12, 6, 1 [1]. n = 4: 8, 24, 24, 8 [1]
**(b)** 3 faces: **8** (the corners) [1]. 2 faces: **12(n − 2)** (along each of 12 edges, excluding corners) [1]. 1 face: **6(n − 2)²** (the middle of each of 6 faces) [1]. 0 faces: **(n − 2)³** (the hidden inner cube) [1]
**(c)** n = 5: 8 + 12(3) + 6(9) + 27 = 8 + 36 + 54 + 27 [1] = 125 = 5³ [1] **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Linking each rule to corners, edges, faces and the interior is the justification. Checking that the four counts add to n³ is a strong verification.

**9.** **(a)** In each case the count is one less than m + n [1], so the rule is **m + n − 1** [1]
**(b)** 4 + 5 − 1 = **8**; verify by drawing a 4 × 5 grid and counting the squares the diagonal enters [1]
**(c)** 2 and 4 have common factor 2 [1]; the diagonal passes exactly through the grid point at its midpoint, so it moves into a new square diagonally without crossing a separate line, and one square is saved [1] **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* A failing case is useful evidence: it shows the condition on m and n is part of the rule's domain.

**10.** **(a)** **2ⁿ** pieces [1]
**(b)** 0.08 × 2¹² = 0.08 × 4096 [1] = 327.68 mm, about **328 mm** [1]
**(c)** 0.08 × 2ⁿ > 1000 means 2ⁿ > 12 500 [1]; 2¹³ = 8192 is too small and 2¹⁴ = 16 384 is enough, so **14 cuts** [1] **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Convert 1 m to 1000 mm before comparing.

**11.** **(a)** 1, 3 and 10 [1]
**(b)** The results 1, 3, 6, 10 are the triangular numbers [1], so **n(n + 1)/2** [1]
**(c)** A strip of n squares has n + 1 vertical lines, and each rectangle is fixed by choosing two of them as its ends [1]; there are (n + 1) × n ordered choices, and each rectangle is counted twice, giving n(n + 1)/2 [1] **[5]**

**12.** **(a)** S(1) = 1/3 [1]; S(2) = 1/3 + 1/15 = 2/5 and S(3) = 2/5 + 1/35 = 3/7 [1]
**(b)** **S(n) = n/(2n + 1)** [1]
**(c)** Writing each term as ½(1/(2k − 1) − 1/(2k + 1)) gives ½[(1 − 1/3) + (1/3 − 1/5) + … + (1/(2n − 1) − 1/(2n + 1))] [1]; the middle terms cancel, leaving ½(1 − 1/(2n + 1)) [1] = ½ × 2n/(2n + 1) = **n/(2n + 1)** [1] **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* This is a proof because it works for every n. Question 3 showed the same fractions stay below 1/2, which fits: the sum approaches 1/2 but never reaches it.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Verifying with a case that was used to find the rule.
- Justifying with "the difference is 3" instead of explaining where the 3 comes from.
- Stopping at first differences when the pattern is quadratic, cubic or exponential.
- Forgetting the condition that makes a rule work, such as m and n having no common factor.
- Mixing units when an exponential pattern is compared with a target, such as millimetres and metres.
- Leaving a proof as a list of examples.

## Frequently asked questions

**How much should I write when justifying a rule?**
Enough to connect each part of the rule to the structure of the situation. For the matchsticks, say where the 1 and each 3 come from in the drawing.

**Why do some questions include a case where the rule fails?**
A counter-example shows the limits of a rule. Explaining why it fails, such as a diagonal passing through a grid corner, strengthens an investigation and shows the domain of the rule.
