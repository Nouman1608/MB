## Why keep a control account

The sales ledger holds one account for each customer. The purchases ledger holds one account for each supplier. A control account is a single ledger account that summarises all of them, using totals from the books of prime entry instead of individual postings.

Its purposes:

- It gives the **total trade receivables** (sales ledger control) or **total trade payables** (purchases ledger control) at any date, without listing every customer or supplier.
- It acts as a **check**. A total built from the books of prime entry is a second, independent figure for the same balance, so a business that builds both can see whether its ledger postings look reasonable.
- It helps **control**. When one person keeps the control account and another keeps the individual accounts, it is harder for an error or a fraud to pass unnoticed.
- It supplies the figure for trade receivables or trade payables that goes into the statement of financial position.
- It is useful for finding a **missing figure**, such as credit sales, when the other totals are known.

## Where each entry comes from

Control accounts are built from totals of the books of prime entry, not from the individual customer or supplier accounts.

| Item | Usual source | Sales ledger control | Purchases ledger control |
|---|---|---|---|
| Credit sales | Sales journal | Debit | |
| Credit purchases | Purchases journal | | Credit |
| Returns | Sales returns journal / purchases returns journal | Credit | Debit |
| Receipts from customers / payments to suppliers | Cash book (bank column) | Credit | Debit |
| Cash discounts | Cash book (discount columns) | Credit (discount allowed) | Debit (discount received) |
| Irrecoverable debts written off | General journal | Credit | |
| Dishonoured cheque | Cash book | Debit (customer's cheque) | Credit (our cheque) |
| Interest on overdue accounts | General journal | Debit (charged to customer) | Credit (charged by supplier) |
| Contra entries | General journal | Credit | Debit |
| Refunds | Cash book | Debit (paid to customer) | Credit (received from supplier) |

Cash discount is different from trade discount. Trade discount is deducted on the invoice, so it never appears in a control account; only the net invoice value is in the sales or purchases journal.

## The two layouts

**The rule.** A customer normally owes you, so the sales ledger control account normally has a debit balance. A supplier is normally owed by you, so the purchases ledger control account normally has a credit balance. Increases go on the side of the normal balance. Decreases go on the other.

**Debit and credit balances within each account.** A few customers have credit balances, for example after an overpayment or a returned item that was already paid for. A few suppliers have debit balances, for example after you overpay. The syllabus lists "debit and credit within each account", so show both kinds of balance.

- Sales ledger control: opening debit balances on the debit side, opening credit balances on the credit side. At the end, the closing credit balances are carried down on the debit side and the closing debit balances on the credit side, so the totals agree.
- Purchases ledger control: the same idea, with the sides reversed. Opening credit balances on the credit side, opening debit balances on the debit side.

**Refunds.** If a customer has a credit balance and you pay them back, you debit the sales ledger control account, which removes the credit balance. If a supplier has a debit balance and refunds you, you credit the purchases ledger control account.

**Dishonoured cheques.** A customer's cheque that the bank will not pay means the customer still owes you, so you debit the sales ledger control account. If your own cheque to a supplier is dishonoured, you still owe the supplier, so you credit the purchases ledger control account.

## Worked example 1: sales ledger control account

Brackenfell Paints sells on credit. For March the books of prime entry give these totals.

- Opening balances: customers owing 18,400 (debit) and customers in credit 260.
- Credit sales 41,750; sales returns 1,350.
- Cheques and bank transfers from customers 36,900; cash discounts allowed 740.
- Irrecoverable debts written off 620.
- A customer's cheque for 480 was dishonoured.
- Interest of 150 was charged on overdue accounts.
- Contra with the purchases ledger 900.
- Refund of 260 paid to a customer whose account was in credit.
- Closing balances: customers in credit 90; the debit balances are not yet known.

| Sales ledger control account | | | |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Debit** | **Amount** | **Credit** | **Amount** |
| Balance b/d (debit balances) | 18,400 | Balance b/d (credit balances) | 260 |
| Credit sales | 41,750 | Bank (receipts) | 36,900 |
| Bank (dishonoured cheque) | 480 | Discount allowed | 740 |
| Interest on overdue accounts | 150 | Sales returns | 1,350 |
| Bank (refund) | 260 | Irrecoverable debts | 620 |
| Balance c/d (credit balances) | 90 | Purchases ledger control (contra) | 900 |
| | | Balance c/d (debit balances) | 20,360 |
| | **61,130** | | **61,130** |

Method: add the debit side (61,130). Add the known credit items (40,770). The difference, 20,360, is the closing debit balance, which is the trade receivables figure. The balances brought down next period are debit 20,360 and credit 90.

## Worked example 2: purchases ledger control account

Hollinger Timber buys on credit. For June the totals are:

- Opening balances: suppliers owed 27,300 (credit) and suppliers who owe the business 340 (debit).
- Credit purchases 52,600; purchases returns 2,090.
- Payments to suppliers 47,250; cash discounts received 1,180.
- Contra with the sales ledger 1,100.
- Interest of 210 charged by suppliers on overdue accounts.
- A supplier refunded the 340 debit balance.
- The business's cheque for 600 to a supplier was dishonoured.
- Closing debit balances 120; the credit balances are not yet known.

| Purchases ledger control account | | | |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Debit** | **Amount** | **Credit** | **Amount** |
| Balance b/d (debit balances) | 340 | Balance b/d (credit balances) | 27,300 |
| Bank (payments) | 47,250 | Credit purchases | 52,600 |
| Discount received | 1,180 | Interest on overdue accounts | 210 |
| Purchases returns | 2,090 | Bank (refund received) | 340 |
| Sales ledger control (contra) | 1,100 | Bank (dishonoured cheque) | 600 |
| Balance c/d (credit balances) | 29,210 | Balance c/d (debit balances) | 120 |
| | **81,170** | | **81,170** |

Method: add the credit side (81,170), add the known debit items (51,960), and the balancing figure, 29,210, is the closing credit balance. That is the trade payables figure.

## Worked example 3: finding a missing figure

Marwick Tools has opening trade receivables of 9,600. During the month it received 31,800 from customers, allowed discounts of 560, accepted returns of 940 and wrote off 300 as irrecoverable. Closing trade receivables are 11,250. Find credit sales.

Credit sales go on the debit side, so they are the balancing figure.

- Credit side items: 31,800 + 560 + 940 + 300 + 11,250 = 44,850
- Debit side must equal 44,850. Opening balance is 9,600.
- Credit sales = 44,850 - 9,600 = **35,250**

You can use the same method for any one missing item. Put in every known figure, total the side with more information, and find the difference.

## Digital recording

Many businesses record transactions in accounting software. The syllabus asks about the effect on preparing and using control accounts.

- **Preparation.** The software adds up the journals and ledger postings itself, so the control account can be produced automatically at any date. Totals are faster and arithmetic mistakes are rarer. You may see a control account as a system report rather than something you build by hand.
- **Use.** Up-to-date totals of trade receivables and trade payables are available whenever managers need them, for example before chasing overdue accounts or deciding on a supplier payment.
- **Limits.** The software cannot tell whether the data entered is right. A wrong amount, a wrong customer or a missed document still produces a control account that looks complete. Access controls, passwords and backups matter because anyone who can change the data can change the totals.

## Common errors

- Putting the dishonoured cheque on the credit side. A customer's dishonoured cheque is a debit in the sales ledger control account.
- Putting interest on overdue accounts on the wrong side for suppliers. Interest charged to you increases what you owe, so it is a credit in the purchases ledger control account.
- Forgetting that the contra appears in both control accounts, on opposite sides.
- Including trade discount as if it were a cash discount.
- Mixing up the sides when a closing credit balance in the sales ledger is carried down. It goes on the debit side.
- Using the cash sales figure from the cash book. Only credit sales belong in the account.
