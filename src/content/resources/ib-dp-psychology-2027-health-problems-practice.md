> **These are original questions written for Marlbridge** on the health problems strand of the health and well-being context (first assessment 2027). They are not past-paper questions. The scenarios and the data in Question 6 are invented. Question 9 uses the 15-mark tariff of the concept-based extended response; other tariffs are for practice.

Related: [health problems study guide](/resources/ib-dp-psychology-2027-health-problems/) and [revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-psychology-2027-health-problems-revision-notes/).

## Section A

**1.** Outline one factor that may explain a change in the prevalence of a health problem in a population over time. **[3]**

**2.** Explain one factor that may explain a difference in the prevalence of a health problem between two populations. **[4]**

**3.** Explain the role of social learning in one health problem. **[4]**

**4.** Explain how stress may contribute to one health problem. **[4]**

## Section B

**5.** Read the scenario (invented for practice).

> Danya, 15, started smoking after joining a new group of friends. Her older cousin, whom she admires, also smokes and is popular. Danya says that she smokes mostly during exam weeks because it "calms her down".

**(a)** Use social learning theory to explain why Danya started smoking. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain one role of stress in Danya's behaviour. **[3]**

**6.** Read the summary of a constructed study.

> A researcher asked 200 adolescents (aged 14 to 16, from one city) to complete a questionnaire about daily social media use and a 10-item scale of compulsive use. The mean score on the scale was 24 out of 50 for those who used social media for more than four hours a day and 14 out of 50 for those who used it for less than one hour. The researcher concluded that heavy use causes addiction.

**(a)** Identify the type of study. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain why the researcher's conclusion about causality is not justified. **[3]**
**(c)** Suggest one way to improve the study to test a causal claim. **[3]**

**7.** Explain one limitation of using body mass index (BMI) to measure obesity in a population. **[3]**

**8.** Evaluate the use of social learning theory to explain one health problem. **[6]**

## Section C

**9.** Evaluate research on health problems in terms of how well it explains the prevalence of one health problem. Refer to at least two concepts in your answer. **[15]**

## Answers

**1.** Any one factor, outlined and linked to the change. For example, a rise in the availability of cheap energy-dense food **[1]** means people consume more calories **[1]**, and the prevalence of obesity rises **[1]**. Other factors: reduced physical activity, marketing, spread of smartphones, changes in law or price (for drug misuse).

**2.** Any one factor, for example income. In a lower-income population, healthy food may cost more relative to income and safe places for exercise may be limited **[1]**, so obesity may be more common **[1]**. In a higher-income country, the opposite might hold, depending on food costs and culture **[1]**. A caution about differences in measurement or sampling between the populations **[1]**. Other factors: cultural norms, policy, access to healthcare.

**3.** Social learning is learning by observing and imitating models **[1]**. Example: children copy eating habits they see in parents and friends **[1]**, or teenagers start smoking after seeing admired models rewarded with status (vicarious reinforcement) **[1]**. Link to the health problem with a developed point, such as attention to high-status models and motivation to gain acceptance **[1]**.

**4.** Stress activates the stress response and raises cortisol **[1]**; chronic stress can affect appetite, fat storage or the immune system (direct route) **[1]**. Stress may also lead to coping behaviours such as comfort eating, smoking, drinking or excessive social media use (indirect route) **[1]**. Link to a named health problem **[1]**.

**5.** (a) Danya pays attention to a model she admires (her cousin) **[1]** who is rewarded with popularity (vicarious reinforcement) **[1]**. She is motivated to imitate to gain acceptance in the new group **[1]**.

(b) Stress acts as a trigger for coping behaviour **[1]**. Danya smokes in exam weeks to feel calmer, which is negatively reinforcing (it relieves tension) **[1]**, so the behaviour is repeated and may become a habit **[1]**.

**6.** (a) Correlational study (survey) **[1]**.

(b) The study shows an association, not cause **[1]**. Bidirectional ambiguity: compulsive users may spend more time online, or adolescents who are already lonely may use social media more **[1]**. Third variables such as loneliness or sleep problems might explain both, and there was no manipulation or control of use **[1]**.

(c) Any one, up to **[3]**. Run an experiment in which one randomly allocated group limits use and the other does not, and compare scores over time **[1]**, so that cause and effect can be tested **[1]**, with a control of extraneous variables or a follow-up measure at several time points **[1]**. Alternatively, a longitudinal design measuring use and compulsion over several years.

**7.** BMI is weight divided by height squared and does not distinguish fat from muscle **[1]**, so muscular people may be classified as overweight **[1]**. It also does not show where fat is stored, and thresholds may not suit all ethnic groups, so prevalence figures may over- or underestimate the problem **[1]**.

**8.** Description (up to 2): learning by observation, imitation, vicarious reinforcement, four conditions **[1]**; application, such as children copying parents' eating or peers' smoking **[1]**. Evaluation (up to 4): strengths include explaining clustering in families and peers and supporting practical prevention such as role-model programmes **[1]**; experiments (advertising and snacking) support causality **[1]**; limitations include underplaying biological and genetic factors **[1]**, short-term lab measures that may not predict real long-term behaviour **[1]**, and selection effects (people choose similar friends) **[1]**. Award up to **[6]**.

**9.** Markband guide: 13 to 15 for well-developed critical analysis, links between concepts throughout and a reasoned conclusion; 10 to 12 for good analysis lacking development; lower bands for description. Indicative content, one mark per developed point up to the markband:
- Prevalence change over time points to environmental factors (food environment, activity, marketing) since genes change slowly **[2]**.
- Differences between populations: income, culture, policy, with the caution that definitions and sampling vary **[2]**.
- Social learning explains how patterns spread through families, peers and media, but selection effects limit causal claims **[2]**.
- Stress explains changes in behaviour under pressure, with direct and indirect routes, but evidence is often correlational **[2]**.
- Causality: factors interact, and natural experiments or controlled trials give stronger evidence than surveys **[2]**.
- Measurement: BMI, self-report scales, changing definitions affect the figures **[2]**.
- Bias and responsibility: sampling bias, stigma, and the duty to avoid blaming individuals **[2]**.
- Change: if environment explains prevalence, policy change may be more effective than individual education **[2]**.
- A conclusion that weighs the explanations, for example that environmental factors best explain population change while social learning and stress explain how it works in individuals **[1]**.

## Where marks are usually lost

- **Question 2:** stating a difference without explaining how the factor produces it.
- **Question 5:** describing the theory but not applying it to Danya.
- **Question 6(b):** saying "correlation is not causation" without explaining why in this study.
- **Question 9:** discussing causes and not the evidence about prevalence, or using only one concept.
