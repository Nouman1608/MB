Section A of Paper 3 Language Analysis in Cambridge International AS & A Level English Language (9093) asks you to
explain how English has changed from the Early Modern period to the present, using three pieces of evidence at once.
This guide covers what the section requires, the features and processes you need to recognise, the named theories,
and how to read the two kinds of quantitative data the paper always includes.

## What the question gives you

Section A is one compulsory question worth 25 marks, half of Paper 3. You respond to three texts:

- a **prose text** of about 300-400 words, written at any time from the Early Modern English period (beginning
  c.1500) to the present day, in any of the forms listed for the AS papers (letters, diaries, advertisements,
  travel writing and so on);
- an **n-gram graph**;
- a **word table of corpus data**.

You analyse how the prose text **exemplifies ways in which English has changed**, supporting your answer with both
data sources and with your wider study. The question assesses AO2, AO4 and AO5: writing effectively, understanding
linguistic issues, concepts and approaches, and analysing and synthesising language data. Dictionaries are not
allowed.

## Knowing the period: features by language level

The syllabus asks for "the chronology and essential features" of English from Early Modern to Contemporary English,
and lists the levels to examine. Learn at least one feature per level that marks an older text.

| Level | Features that mark an older text |
|---|---|
| Graphology | Long s (ſ) in medial positions, capitalised common nouns, ornate typefaces, ampersands |
| Orthography | Variable spelling before dictionaries ("musick", "publick", "shew"); interchangeable u/v and i/j; "ye" with y standing for an older letter for "th" |
| Phonology | Spellings that hint at older pronunciations; rhymes that no longer work |
| Morphology | Third-person "-eth" (hath, doth, maketh); second-person "-est" (thou knowest) |
| Grammar | "Thou/thee" alongside "you"; older past forms; multiple negation in early texts |
| Syntax | Questions and negatives without "do" ("Know you not?", "I fear not the storm"); long periodic sentences with heavy subordination |
| Lexis | Archaic words; Latinate borrowings; words still in use but with older meanings |
| Semantics | Meanings that have broadened, narrowed, ameliorated or pejorated |
| Pragmatics | Elaborate politeness in letters ("your most humble and obedient servant"); deferential forms of address |

Three landmarks help you place a text. The spread of **printing** from the late fifteenth century encouraged more
fixed spellings. **Samuel Johnson's** *Dictionary of the English Language* (1755) became a reference point for spelling
and meaning. Eighteenth-century **prescriptive grammars**, such as Robert Lowth's (1762), promoted rules that
stigmatised forms like multiple negation. A text with variable spelling and "-eth" endings is probably early; one with
standardised spelling but very formal syntax and politeness formulas may be eighteenth or nineteenth century.

## Processes of change: the concept vocabulary

The syllabus lists the concepts and terminology below. Each needs a definition and an example you can produce under
pressure.

**Word formation**

- **Etymology**: the origin and history of a word.
- **Derivation**: forming a new word by adding affixes ("kind" to "kindness").
- **Inflection**: grammatical endings that mark tense, number or person; English has lost many over time.
- **Compounding**: joining words ("smartphone").
- **Blending**: fusing parts of words ("smog" from smoke and fog).
- **Acronym**: initial letters pronounced as a word ("scuba", "laser").
- **Conversion**: a word changing class without changing form (the noun "text" used as a verb).
- **Backformation**: removing a supposed affix to create a new word ("edit" from "editor", "burgle" from "burglar").
- **Borrowing**: taking words from other languages ("bungalow", "tsunami", "algebra").
- **Telescoping**: compressing a phrase into a shorter word, as "goodbye" developed from "God be with you".
- **Coalescence**: separate forms merging into one, as "to-day" became "today" and "any one" became "anyone"; the term
  is also used for two sounds merging in speech.

**Semantic change**

- **Broadening**: meaning widens ("holiday", once a holy day, now any day off).
- **Narrowing**: meaning becomes more specific ("meat", once food in general).
- **Amelioration**: meaning becomes more positive ("nice", once meaning foolish).
- **Pejoration**: meaning becomes more negative ("villain", once a farm labourer).

## Theories and theorists

The syllabus names these theories. Use them to explain **why** a change happened, not just to label it.

- **Random fluctuation theory**: some changes have no single identifiable cause; forms drift in and out of use, rather
  as fashions do.
- **Cultural transmission theory**: language is passed from one generation to the next through social learning, not
  biology, so each generation can alter what it passes on.
- **Theory of lexical gaps**: new words are created or borrowed to fill gaps where a concept has no name, which
  explains much technological vocabulary.
- **Substratum theory**: when a population shifts to a new language, features of its earlier language persist in the
  new one, as with features of Irish in the English of Ireland.
- **Functional theory**: language changes to meet the communicative needs of its users, so new contexts (the internet,
  new professions) produce new forms.
- **Tree model**: associated with August Schleicher, it presents languages as branches descending from common
  ancestors, as English descends within the Germanic branch.
- **Wave model**: associated with Johannes Schmidt, it presents changes spreading outward from a centre across
  neighbouring varieties, like ripples, which explains shared features that cut across family branches.

A useful contrast for attitudes: **prescriptivism** treats change as decline; **descriptivism** records it without
judgement. Jean Aitchison's image of language as a "crumbling castle" names a prescriptive fear that she challenges.

## Reading the data

### N-gram graphs

An n-gram graph plots how often a word or phrase appears in a large collection of printed texts, year by year. The
horizontal axis shows time; the vertical axis shows **relative frequency**, the proportion of all words in that year's
texts. The syllabus suggests comparisons of related words (variant spellings, synonyms), parts of speech, inflections
("hath" against "has") and collocations.

When reading a graph:

1. State what is plotted and the time span.
2. Describe the **trend** for each line: rise, fall, crossover point, plateau.
3. Give approximate dates and use comparative language ("by around 1800, the newer form overtakes...").
4. **Interpret**: link the trend to a process (standardisation, borrowing, semantic change) and a theory.
5. **Qualify**: the corpus is of printed texts, so it over-represents formal writing; older scans can misread long s as
   "f"; a fall in frequency is not the same as a change in meaning.

### Corpus word tables

A word table lists data drawn from a corpus, such as the **collocates** of a word (words that frequently occur near it)
in different periods, or **synonym lists**. Collocates are evidence of meaning: if "awful" collocates with "majesty"
and "power" in older data but with "weather" and "mistake" in recent data, its meaning has shifted from
"awe-inspiring" towards "very bad", an example of pejoration and of weakening into an intensifier.

## Worked example: synthesising three sources

**Prose extract** (original, written for this guide in the style of a seventeenth-century letter):

> *Sir, I have receiv'd your Letter, and do most heartily thank you for the Care you shew'd my Son. He hath been
> sicke these three Daies, yet the Physick worketh well, and I doubt not he shall be recover'd ere Sunday.*

**Data (described, invented for this example):** an n-gram graph shows "shew" falling steadily while "show" rises,
crossing over in the eighteenth century; a word table shows "physic" collocating with "doctor", "remedy" and "purge"
in older texts, while recent collocates of "medicine" include the same words.

**Analysis paragraph:**

> The extract's orthography marks it as pre-standard: "shew'd", "sicke" and "Daies" show the variable spelling common
> before Johnson's *Dictionary*, and the n-gram data confirm that "shew" gave way to "show" during the eighteenth
> century. The steady, one-directional curve fits a cultural transmission explanation better than random
> fluctuation: once printers and dictionary-makers fixed one spelling, later generations learned it as the correct
> form. Grammatically, "hath" and "worketh" retain the "-eth" inflection that "-s" later replaced, while "I doubt
> not" negates without "do", a syntax that now sounds archaic. Lexically, "Physick" here means medicine, a sense that
> survives today only in "physician"; the word table shows the collocates that once clustered around "physic" now
> clustering around "medicine", evidence of one word replacing another in the same semantic space.

The paragraph moves between all three sources, names features precisely and uses a theory to explain the data rather
than to decorate the answer.

## Common mistakes

- Feature-spotting archaic forms without dating or explaining them.
- Describing the n-gram graph without interpreting it, or interpreting it without describing it.
- Treating n-gram data as complete evidence of all English use.
- Naming a theory without connecting it to a specific change in the data.
- Calling older forms "wrong" or "mistakes".
- Writing about the prose text and the data in separate halves of the essay.

## Exam technique

Open with a short overview placing the text in time, with reasons. Organise paragraphs by **language level or
process**, each combining evidence from the prose text, the graph or the table, and a concept or theory. Quote
sparingly and precisely. End with a judgement about which processes the text best exemplifies. Because AO4 and AO5
dominate, every paragraph should show both conceptual knowledge and handling of data.
