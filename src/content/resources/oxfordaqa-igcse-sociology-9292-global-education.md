This guide covers **3.1.2.5 Global education** in Section B of Paper 1 of OxfordAQA International
GCSE Sociology (9292). The specification lists: "Literacy rates: global rates and comparisons between
countries", "Differences in educational provision around the world", "Effects of poverty on schools
and education", "Gender and other inequalities in access to education" and "Inequalities in access to
digital education". The [Education study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-sociology-9292-education/)
summarises this section in a paragraph; this page develops each statement. Because the specification
asks students to apply the course to "different societies around the world", this is one of the most
international parts of Paper 1, and Section C of the same paper may use data on "an unseen issue of
global sociological significance".

## Literacy rates

**Literacy** is the ability to read and write. UNESCO, the United Nations body that collects
international education data, defines a literate person as one who can read and write, with
understanding, a short simple statement about their everyday life. The **adult literacy rate** is the
percentage of people aged 15 and over who meet this definition.

**Global patterns.** Literacy rates are close to universal in many high-income countries and are
lowest in parts of sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia. Within countries, rates are usually lower in
rural areas, among older people and among poorer groups. UNESCO data have consistently shown that
women make up the majority of adults who lack basic literacy.

**Comparing countries.** When you compare literacy data:

- check the **age group** (adults 15+, or young people 15–24);
- compare **male and female** rates, and the size of the gap;
- look at **change over time**: youth literacy is often much higher than adult literacy where
  schooling has recently expanded;
- remember that literacy is measured differently in different places (self-report in a census, or a
  test), which affects comparability.

### Freire and literacy

**Paulo Freire**, one of the sociologists named for 3.1.2, worked on adult literacy programmes in
Brazil. In *Pedagogy of the Oppressed* (1968), he argued that learning to read should help people
understand and question the conditions of their lives, developing **critical consciousness**, rather
than simply teaching them to repeat words. For Freire, literacy is political: it can either keep
people passive or help them change their situation.

## Differences in educational provision

**Provision** means what education is available and on what terms. It varies in:

| Aspect | Variation between societies |
|---|---|
| Compulsory years | From a few years of primary school to education until 18 |
| Cost | Free state schooling, or fees, uniforms and materials paid by families |
| Teachers | Numbers of trained teachers, class sizes, teacher absence |
| Buildings and resources | Classrooms, textbooks, electricity, water and toilets |
| Language | Whether teaching is in children's home language or an official or colonial language |
| Type of school | State, private, faith and international schools, as in 3.1.2.1 |
| Stages | Access to pre-school, secondary and tertiary education |

International agreements set common goals. The Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948) states a
right to education, with elementary education free and compulsory. The United Nations' Sustainable
Development Goal 4, agreed in 2015, aims to "ensure inclusive and equitable quality education" for
all.

## Effects of poverty on schools and education

Poverty affects education at the level of the **family**, the **school** and the **country**.

- **Family:** children may work to support the household; fees, uniforms and books may be
  unaffordable; hunger and illness reduce concentration and attendance; there may be no light or quiet
  space for homework.
- **School:** underfunded schools have large classes, few trained teachers, shortages of textbooks,
  and poor buildings, sometimes without electricity or toilets.
- **Country:** governments with low tax revenue or high debt spend less on education; conflict and
  displacement close schools.

These effects connect to the **material** explanations of attainment in 3.1.2.4, now applied between
countries rather than within one.

## Gender and other inequalities in access

**Gender.** In some societies girls have less access to education than boys. Reasons include:

- expectations that girls do domestic work or care for siblings;
- early marriage;
- families with limited money prioritising sons;
- safety concerns about the journey to school;
- a lack of separate toilets and sanitary facilities, which can keep adolescent girls away;
- laws or policies restricting girls' schooling, as in Afghanistan, where the Taliban have barred
  girls from secondary schools since returning to power in 2021.

In other societies the pattern is different: boys may leave school early to earn money, and in many
higher-income countries girls now outperform boys in examinations. Feminist perspectives (3.1.2.2)
help to explain how gender expectations shape access and choices.

**Other inequalities** include:

- **Disability:** inaccessible buildings, lack of trained support and stigma.
- **Rural location:** long distances, fewer secondary schools, teacher shortages.
- **Ethnicity, caste and language:** discrimination, or teaching only in a dominant language.
- **Refugees and conflict:** displaced children may miss years of schooling.
- **Social class:** families able to pay for private schooling or tuition gain advantages.

These inequalities **intersect**: a girl with a disability in a poor rural area faces several barriers
at once.

## Inequalities in access to digital education

The **digital divide** is the gap between those who have access to digital technology and the skills
to use it, and those who do not. For education, it involves:

- **Devices:** whether each child has a computer or phone, or must share.
- **Connection:** internet access and cost; mobile signal in rural areas.
- **Electricity:** without a reliable supply, devices cannot be used.
- **Skills and support:** parents' ability to help; teachers' training in online teaching.

During the **COVID-19** school closures of 2020, many schools moved to online learning. Children with
devices and internet at home could continue; many without them could not, widening existing gaps both
within and between countries. Online learning (3.1.2.1) can widen access, but only where the digital
divide has been addressed.

## Worked example 1: a short-answer question

*Explain **one** way in which poverty can affect children's access to education. [3 marks]*

> Poor families may need children to work (1), for example on farms or in family businesses, to
> contribute to household income (1), so these children attend school irregularly or leave early (1).

## Worked example 2: planning an extended answer

*Discuss how far gender is the most important cause of inequality in access to education around the
world. Use examples from your own society or a society you know about. [12 marks]*

- **For gender:** early marriage, domestic labour, safety, sanitation; legal restrictions in
  Afghanistan; feminist perspectives.
- **Other causes:** poverty and child labour; rural location; disability; conflict; the digital
  divide; in some societies boys are the group more likely to leave early.
- **Intersection:** gender barriers are strongest when combined with poverty and rural location.
- **Application:** a named society, for example the pattern of school completion there.
- **Judgement:** gender matters greatly in some societies, but poverty is often the underlying factor
  that turns gender expectations into exclusion.

## Common mistakes

- Using "literacy" loosely without the UNESCO definition or age group.
- Comparing countries without stating which figures and which year.
- Treating gender inequality as the same everywhere.
- Describing the digital divide only as "no internet", ignoring devices, electricity and skills.
- Listing effects of poverty without explaining how each affects education.
- No example from a named society when the question asks for one.
