This guide covers **3.1.2 Price determination in a competitive market** in AQA AS Economics (7135), specification version 1.3. It is the second sub-topic of 3.1 The operation of markets and market failure and is examined on Paper 1. The aim here is to show how the six parts of 3.1.2 work together as one toolkit, using a single invented market, rather than treating demand, elasticity and supply as separate lists. All markets, countries and figures below are invented.

## The one idea that links every part

A competitive market price is the outcome of buyers' and sellers' plans meeting. Every question in this sub-topic asks one of three things:

1. **What has shifted?** A change in a non-price factor shifts a whole curve. A change in the good's own price moves along a curve.
2. **By how much do price and quantity change?** That depends on the elasticities. A given shift in demand mainly raises price when supply is inelastic, and mainly raises quantity when supply is elastic.
3. **What happens elsewhere?** Through the links in 3.1.2.6, the new price becomes a non-price factor in some other market.

## Worked example: one market, all six parts

Country N's market for oat drinks (litres per week, thousands; price in N$) has:

```
Demand:  Qd = 300 - 8P
Supply:  Qs = -60 + 12P
Equilibrium: 300 - 8P = -60 + 12P  ->  360 = 20P  ->  P = 18,  Q = 156
```

**Step 1, a shift in demand (3.1.2.1).** Incomes rise and social media coverage makes plant-based drinks fashionable. Both are non-price determinants, the second being one of the social and emotional influences the specification mentions. Demand rises by 40 at every price: Qd = 340 - 8P.

```
340 - 8P = -60 + 12P  ->  400 = 20P  ->  P = 20,  Q = 180
Price: +2 on 18 = +11.1%      Quantity: +24 on 156 = +15.4%
```

**Step 2, disequilibrium and adjustment (3.1.2.5).** At the old price of 18, the new quantity demanded is 340 - 144 = 196 while quantity supplied is still 156. There is excess demand of 40. Shortages let sellers raise prices; as the price rises, quantity supplied extends and quantity demanded contracts until they meet at 20. Saying which curve shifts and which quantity "extends" or "contracts" is what turns a description into an explanation.

**Step 3, the supply response (3.1.2.3 and 3.1.2.4).** Firms supply more because the higher price makes extra output more profitable. Measured along the supply curve:

```
PES = %change in quantity supplied / %change in price = 15.4 / 11.1 = 1.38
```

Supply is price elastic, so more of the demand increase shows up as extra output than as higher price. If producers had little spare capacity or few stocks, PES would be lower and the same demand shift would raise price more.

**Step 4, revenue (3.1.2.2).** Total revenue rises from 18 x 156 = 2,808 to 20 x 180 = 3,600 (thousand N$). Be careful: this is **not** evidence that demand is price inelastic. Revenue rose because demand shifted, not because of a movement along a fixed demand curve. The PED and revenue rule applies only to a movement along one demand curve.

**Step 5, PED on the new demand curve.** Suppose a supermarket then cuts the price from 20 to 19. On Qd = 340 - 8P, quantity demanded rises from 180 to 188.

```
PED = %change in Qd / %change in P = (8/180 x 100) / (-1/20 x 100)
    = 4.44 / -5.0 = -0.89
Revenue: 20 x 180 = 3,600  ->  19 x 188 = 3,572 (falls)
```

Demand is price inelastic here, so a price cut lowers revenue, as the rule predicts.

**Step 6, linked markets (3.1.2.6).** Oat drinks and dairy milk are substitutes. A positive XED means the higher price of oat drinks raises demand for dairy milk, so the dairy demand curve shifts right. Oats themselves face **derived demand**: the demand for oats rises because demand for the final product rose. Oats also have **composite demand**, since they are wanted for porridge, animal feed and drinks; more oats used for drinks leaves fewer for other uses, so the price of oats for porridge makers may rise and their supply curve shifts left.

## The five linkages in 3.1.2.6

| Linkage | Meaning | Chain to learn |
|---|---|---|
| Joint demand | Goods bought together (complements) | Price of printers falls, demand for ink cartridges rises |
| Demand for substitutes | Goods that meet the same want | Price of rail fares rises, demand for coach travel rises |
| Composite demand | One good wanted for several different uses | More land used for housing, less land for farming, so farm rents rise |
| Derived demand | Demand for an input comes from demand for the output | Demand for new homes rises, demand for bricklayers and bricks rises |
| Joint supply | Producing one good automatically produces another | More beef produced, more hides supplied, so the price of leather falls |

Joint supply is the linkage most often confused with joint demand. In joint supply the link runs through **producers**; in joint demand it runs through **consumers**.

## Annotated model paragraph

*Question style: explain how a fall in the price of electric cars is likely to affect the market for petrol.*

> Electric cars and petrol cars are substitutes, while petrol cars and petrol are in joint demand. A fall in the price of electric cars causes an extension of demand for electric cars and reduces the demand for petrol cars, so fewer petrol cars are bought over time. Because petrol is in joint demand with petrol cars, the demand curve for petrol shifts to the left. At the original price there is now excess supply, so petrol retailers cut prices until a new equilibrium is reached with a lower price and quantity. The size of the price fall depends on PES: if refineries cannot quickly cut output, supply is inelastic and most of the adjustment falls on price.

Why it works: it names two different links, uses the precise words "extension" and "shifts", explains the move to the new equilibrium through excess supply, and finishes with an elasticity condition that adds evaluation.

## Simultaneous shifts

When both curves shift, one of price or quantity is certain and the other depends on the size of the shifts.

| Demand | Supply | Price | Quantity |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rises | Rises | Uncertain | Rises |
| Rises | Falls | Rises | Uncertain |
| Falls | Rises | Falls | Uncertain |
| Falls | Falls | Uncertain | Falls |

In a data question, use the figures given to decide which shift was larger rather than leaving the answer as "uncertain".

## Common mistakes

- Writing "demand rises" when the price of the good itself changes. That is an extension of demand along the curve.
- Using a positive sign for PED, or calling a PED of -0.89 elastic because 0.89 is "close to 1".
- Concluding demand is inelastic because revenue rose after a shift in demand.
- Reading XED of +0.4 as complements. A positive XED means substitutes; a negative XED means complements.
- Treating an inferior good as a low-quality good. It is defined only by a negative YED.
- Describing joint supply as two goods "sold together". It means one production process creates both.
- Stopping at "price rises" without the excess demand that causes the rise.

## Exam technique for AQA AS Paper 1

Paper 1 has a Section A of 20 multiple-choice questions and a Section B of data-response questions, with a choice of one from two contexts. For this sub-topic:

- **Calculate** items in Section A often hinge on the percentage change. Divide the change by the original value and keep the sign.
- **Explain** questions need a chain: cause, shift or movement, disequilibrium, new equilibrium.
- **Using the data** means quoting a figure from the extract, with its units, and saying what it shows.
- **Evaluate** or **discuss** questions on a market need a condition that changes the answer, most often an elasticity, the time period or the size of competing shifts, then a judgement.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the difference between a shift in demand and an extension of demand?**
A shift moves the whole curve because a non-price factor such as income or the price of a substitute has changed. An extension is a movement down the existing curve because the good's own price has fallen.

**What is composite demand?**
Composite demand exists when one good is wanted for several different uses, so more of it used in one market leaves less for the others.
