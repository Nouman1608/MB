These notes condense section 2.4, Price, of the OCR GCSE (9-1) Economics specification (J205), Version 2.0, for first assessment in 2019 (component J205/01). The specification is not tiered. The worked honey market, with single and double shifts, is in the [2.4 study guide](/resources/ocr-gcse-economics-j205-price-ocr-gcse-economics/); then try the [2.4 practice questions](/resources/ocr-gcse-economics-j205-price-ocr-gcse-economics-practice/). All figures are invented.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Equilibrium price | The price at which quantity demanded equals quantity supplied |
| Equilibrium quantity | The quantity bought and sold at the equilibrium price |
| Shortage (excess demand) | Quantity demanded is greater than quantity supplied at a price |
| Surplus (excess supply) | Quantity supplied is greater than quantity demanded at a price |
| Market forces | The pressures of demand and supply that move price towards equilibrium |
| Price mechanism | The way prices signal, give incentives and ration to allocate resources |
| Efficient distribution of resources | Resources used to make what people most want, at least cost, without waste |

## Price as worth

- Price reflects what buyers will pay and what it costs sellers to supply.
- Price measures worth at the margin, so a useful but plentiful good (tap water) can be cheap and a scarce one (a rare painting) dear.
- Resources move towards products with higher prices and profits, so they go to uses consumers value most.

## Reaching equilibrium

```
Price below equilibrium → shortage → buyers compete, price rises
   → demand contracts, supply extends → equilibrium

Price above equilibrium → surplus → unsold stock, price falls
   → demand extends, supply contracts → equilibrium
```

**Worked reminder.** At £8, demand is 500 and supply 300: shortage of **200**, so price rises. At £10, demand is 400 and supply 400: equilibrium.

## The price mechanism

| Function | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Signalling | Price changes tell buyers and sellers what is happening in the market |
| Incentive | Higher prices make supplying more profitable, encouraging more output |
| Rationing | Price shares limited goods between buyers willing and able to pay |

Limitation: rationing by price favours those with higher incomes.

## Four single shifts

| Change | At old price | Price | Quantity |
|---|---|---|---|
| Demand right | Shortage | Up | Up |
| Demand left | Surplus | Down | Down |
| Supply right | Surplus | Down | Up |
| Supply left | Shortage | Up | Down |

## Double shifts

| Both shift | Quantity | Price |
|---|---|---|
| Demand right, supply right | Up | Depends on sizes |
| Demand left, supply left | Down | Depends on sizes |
| Demand right, supply left | Depends | Up |
| Demand left, supply right | Depends | Down |

Always check the data to decide the uncertain one.

## Analysis order

1. Which curve shifts, and why.
2. Which direction.
3. Shortage or surplus at the old price, with figures.
4. New equilibrium price and quantity.
5. Effect on consumers and producers.

## Allocation chain

Demand rises for product A and falls for product B → A's price rises, B's falls → A becomes more profitable, B less → factors move from B to A → resources follow consumers' wants.

## Diagram checklist

- Axes: Price, Quantity.
- D1, S1, P1, Q1 with dotted lines.
- Shifted curve labelled, arrow for direction.
- P2 and Q2 marked; analysis refers to them.

## Evaluation points on markets and price

- **Strength:** prices respond quickly to changes in tastes and costs, so resources move without central planning.
- **Strength:** the incentive function rewards producers who supply what consumers want.
- **Weakness:** rationing by price can leave people on low incomes without essentials.
- **Weakness:** adjustment takes time; workers and firms in declining industries bear the cost while resources move.

## Exam traps

- Saying "demand falls" when a price rise causes a contraction, not a shift.
- Guessing the price change in a double shift.
- No figures from the table in a data question.
- Describing the price mechanism without an example.
- Unlabelled diagrams.

## Quick self-test

1. Define equilibrium price.
2. At £6, demand is 120 and supply 80. Name and size the imbalance.
3. What happens to price in question 2, and why?
4. State the three functions of the price mechanism.
5. Explain why tap water can be cheaper than a luxury perfume.
6. Supply of a good increases. State the effect on price and quantity.
7. Demand decreases and supply decreases at the same time. Which change is certain?
8. Give one weakness of rationing by price.
9. A market moves from £4 and 600 units to £5 and 700 units. Calculate the percentage change in revenue.
10. Explain how a rise in the price of a product acts as an incentive.
11. List what a demand and supply diagram should show before any shift.

### Answers

1. The price at which quantity demanded equals quantity supplied.
2. Shortage (excess demand) of 120 − 80 = **40**.
3. Price rises: buyers compete for limited goods and sellers can charge more, until demand and supply are equal.
4. Signalling, incentive and rationing.
5. Water is plentiful, so the price of one more unit is low; perfume is scarcer relative to the demand for it.
6. Price falls; quantity rises.
7. Quantity falls; the effect on price depends on the size of each shift.
8. People on low incomes may be unable to buy essential goods.
9. Revenue: 4 × 600 = £2,400 → 5 × 700 = £3,500; (3,500 − 2,400) ÷ 2,400 × 100 = **+45.8%**.
10. It raises profit per unit, so firms produce more and new firms enter.
11. Labelled Price and Quantity axes, curves D1 and S1, and the equilibrium P1 and Q1 marked with dotted lines.
