These notes condense sub-topic 1(f) Ionic bonding (points 1.37 to 1.43) of the Pearson Edexcel International GCSE Chemistry (4CH1) specification, Issue 3 (September 2024). None of the points has a C reference, so all can appear on Paper 1 and Paper 2. Worked examples and model answers are in the [ionic bonding study guide](/resources/edexcel-igcse-chemistry-4ch1-ionic-bonding/); test yourself with the [ionic bonding practice questions](/resources/edexcel-igcse-chemistry-4ch1-ionic-bonding-practice/).

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Ion | A charged particle formed when an atom (or group of atoms) loses or gains electrons |
| Cation | A positive ion |
| Anion | A negative ion |
| Ionic bond | The strong electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions |
| Giant ionic lattice | A regular three-dimensional arrangement of oppositely charged ions |
| Compound ion | An ion made of more than one atom, such as SO₄²⁻ or NH₄⁺ |

## Forming ions (1.37)

| Atom | Change | Ion | Ion configuration |
|---|---|---|---|
| Li (2,1) | Loses 1 e⁻ | Li⁺ | 2 |
| Ca (2,8,8,2) | Loses 2 e⁻ | Ca²⁺ | 2,8,8 |
| Al (2,8,3) | Loses 3 e⁻ | Al³⁺ | 2,8 |
| N (2,5) | Gains 3 e⁻ | N³⁻ | 2,8 |
| O (2,6) | Gains 2 e⁻ | O²⁻ | 2,8 |
| Cl (2,8,7) | Gains 1 e⁻ | Cl⁻ | 2,8,8 |

- Protons never change; only electrons.
- Metals → positive; non-metals → negative.
- Ions have full outer shells (noble gas configurations).

## Charges (1.38)

```
Group 1: 1+   Group 2: 2+   Group 3: 3+
Group 5: 3-   Group 6: 2-   Group 7: 1-
Ag+   Cu2+   Fe2+   Fe3+   Pb2+   Zn2+
H+    OH-    NH4+   CO3 2-   NO3-   SO4 2-
```

Roman numeral = charge on the metal ion: lead(II) is Pb²⁺; iron(III) is Fe³⁺.

## Formulae (1.39)

Method:

1. Write both ions with charges.
2. Find the numbers of each ion that make total charge zero.
3. Write the cation first; use brackets for more than one compound ion.

| Compound | Ions | Formula |
|---|---|---|
| Potassium sulfide | K⁺, S²⁻ | K₂S |
| Zinc nitrate | Zn²⁺, NO₃⁻ | Zn(NO₃)₂ |
| Aluminium sulfate | Al³⁺, SO₄²⁻ | Al₂(SO₄)₃ |
| Ammonium carbonate | NH₄⁺, CO₃²⁻ | (NH₄)₂CO₃ |
| Lead(II) bromide | Pb²⁺, Br⁻ | PbBr₂ |
| Calcium hydroxide | Ca²⁺, OH⁻ | Ca(OH)₂ |

## Dot-and-cross checklist (1.40)

- Elements limited to Groups 1, 2, 3 with Groups 5, 6, 7.
- Outer electrons only.
- Dots for one element, crosses for the other.
- Each ion in square brackets with its charge.
- Correct ratio of ions (it must match the formula).
- Each anion shows 8 outer electrons: its own plus those gained.

Example: lithium oxide, Li₂O → two [Li]⁺ and one [O]²⁻ with 6 dots + 2 crosses.

## Lattices and properties (1.41 to 1.43)

| Property | Explanation |
|---|---|
| High melting and boiling points | Giant lattice; many strong electrostatic attractions between oppositely charged ions; lots of energy needed to overcome them |
| Higher melting point with higher ion charges | Stronger attraction between, for example, Mg²⁺ and O²⁻ than Na⁺ and Cl⁻ |
| Solid does not conduct | Ions held in fixed positions |
| Molten or aqueous conducts | Ions free to move and carry charge |

## Answer frames

- **Describe how [compound] forms (1.37):** "Each [metal] atom loses [n] electron(s) to form [M]ⁿ⁺. Each [non-metal] atom gains [m] electron(s) to form [X]ᵐ⁻. [Ratio] atoms are needed so the electrons lost equal the electrons gained."
- **Why high melting point (1.42):** "Giant ionic lattice. Strong electrostatic attractions between oppositely charged ions. Much energy needed to overcome the many attractions."
- **Why conducts only when molten or dissolved (1.43):** "In the solid the ions are in fixed positions. When molten or in solution the ions are free to move and carry the charge."
- **Why the ion has this charge:** "It has [p] protons and [e] electrons, so the charge is p − e."

Reminder: aluminium oxide forms when two Al atoms each lose 3 electrons (6 in total) and three O atoms each gain 2 (6 in total), giving Al₂O₃.

## Exam traps

- "Chlorine ion" → write chloride ion.
- Ions form by losing or gaining **electrons**, never protons.
- No brackets: Al₂SO₄₃ is wrong; Al₂(SO₄)₃ is right.
- Shared pairs in an ionic diagram → that shows covalent bonding.
- Missing charges or brackets on ions in diagrams.
- "Intermolecular forces" or "molecules" in an ionic explanation.
- "Electrons move" in molten ionic compounds → it is the ions.
- Writing NH₄ for ammonium without the charge when an ion is asked for.

## Self-test

1. Describe how a calcium atom becomes a calcium ion.
2. Give the charge on the ion formed by an element in Group 6.
3. Give the electronic configuration of a sulfide ion.
4. Name two ions from the specification list that have the configuration 2,8.
5. Write the formula of magnesium nitrate.
6. Write the formula of iron(II) sulfate.
7. Write the formula of sodium nitride.
8. Name the compound with formula CuCO₃ and give the charge on its metal ion.
9. State the ratio of ions drawn in a dot-and-cross diagram for calcium fluoride.
10. State what is meant by an ionic bond.
11. Explain why sodium chloride does not conduct electricity when solid.
12. Explain why magnesium oxide has a very high melting point.

### Answers

1. It loses its two outer electrons to form Ca²⁺ (2,8,8).
2. 2−.
3. 2,8,8.
4. Any two of: Na⁺, Mg²⁺, Al³⁺, N³⁻, O²⁻, F⁻.
5. Mg(NO₃)₂.
6. FeSO₄.
7. Na₃N.
8. Copper(II) carbonate; Cu²⁺ (2+).
9. One Ca²⁺ to two F⁻.
10. The strong electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions.
11. The ions are held in fixed positions in the lattice, so they cannot move to carry charge.
12. It has a giant ionic lattice with many strong electrostatic attractions between Mg²⁺ and O²⁻ ions; a lot of energy is needed to overcome them.
