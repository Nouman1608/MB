## Questions

### Section A: short-answer questions

**1.** Which sociologist argued that education must weld "a mass of individuals into a united whole"?

A. Parsons
B. Durkheim
C. Illich
D. Willis

**[1]**

**2.** Which type of school is funded by the state but independent of local authority control?

A. Academy
B. Private school
C. Community school
D. Home school

**[1]**

**3.** Identify **two** types of state school. **[2]**

**4.** Describe what Parsons meant by achieved status, and explain how it links to meritocracy. **[3]**

**5.** Identify and explain **one** way in which education may facilitate social mobility. **[4]**

**6.** Read Item A.

> **Item A (fictional).** After their son was bullied at secondary school, Mr and Mrs Adeyemi decided to educate him at home. Mrs Adeyemi, a former teacher, plans his lessons, and he joins a local group of home-educated children for science once a week.

- **(a)** Identify **one** reason, from Item A, why the family chose home schooling. **[1]**
- **(b)** Explain **one** concern sociologists might raise about home schooling. **[3]**

**7.** Explain **one** difference between Durkheim's and Parsons' views of education. **[3]**

**8.** Explain how a Marxist sociologist might view private schools. **[4]**

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**9.** Read Item B.

> **Item B (fictional).** A researcher wanted to find out whether citizenship lessons help to build social cohesion. She handed out a questionnaire during a citizenship lesson to 150 Year 10 pupils at three schools, with the class teacher in the room. Almost all pupils agreed that "the lessons help us feel part of one community".

- **(a)** From Item B, examine **one** weakness of the research. **[2]**
- **(b)** Identify **one** research method, other than questionnaires, that could be used to study how schools foster social cohesion, and explain **one** disadvantage of using it. **[4]**

**10.** Explain **two** ways in which feminists might criticise the functionalist view of the functions of education. **[4]**

**11.** Discuss how far sociologists would agree that the main function of education is to serve the needs of the economy. **[12]**

**12.** Discuss how far sociologists would agree that Durkheim's view of education as the transmission of norms and values applies to schools today. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** B [1]. The phrase comes from the specification's summary of *Moral Education* (1925). **[1]**

**2.** A [1]. Academies are free to attend; private schools are funded mainly by fees. **[1]**

**3.** Academy [1]; grammar school [1]. *Also credit:* community school; free school; state faith school; comprehensive. **[2]**

**4.** Achieved status is a position gained through one's own effort and ability, such as qualifications [1], rather than ascribed status fixed at birth [1]. Parsons argued schools operate on meritocratic principles, so pupils are rewarded for merit, and the status they achieve decides their future roles [1]. **[3]**

**5.** Identify: qualifications open access to better-paid jobs [1]. Explain: in a meritocracy, pupils who work hard and gain good qualifications can move into professional jobs [1], whatever their parents' class [1]. For example, a pupil whose parents left school at 16 may become the first in the family to go to university and enter a higher-status job, which is upward intergenerational mobility [1]. **[4]**

**6. (a)** The son was bullied at secondary school [1].

**(b)** Home-schooled children may have fewer chances for secondary socialisation with a wide range of peers [1], so they may have less practice cooperating with people who are neither kin nor friends, which Durkheim saw as a key part of schooling [1]. Home schooling also depends on parents' time, money and education, as in Item A where the mother is a former teacher, so it may be easier for middle-class families and could widen inequality [1]. **[4]**

**7.** Durkheim emphasised education's role in building social cohesion by transmitting shared norms and values [1], for example through history lessons and strict rules [1], whereas Parsons emphasised meritocracy, with schools judging pupils by universalistic standards and selecting them for future roles [1]. **[3]**

**8.** Marxists argue that private schools reproduce class inequality [1]. Wealthy families can buy an education with more resources and smaller classes [1], and pupils gain cultural capital and social networks that help them into elite universities and top jobs [1]. This means privilege is passed on from one generation to the next, while the system still appears meritocratic, which legitimises inequality [1]. **[4]**

**9. (a)** One weakness, examined. Pupils filled in the questionnaire in a citizenship lesson with the teacher present [1], so they may have given answers they thought the teacher wanted, which reduces the validity of the findings [1]. *Also credit:* agreement with one statement does not show how pupils actually behave towards each other. **[2]**

**(b)** Identify: non-participant observation of lessons and break times [1]. Disadvantage: pupils and teachers may change their behaviour because they know they are being watched [1], so the researcher may see more cooperation than normally happens [1], which means the data may not give a valid picture of how far schools really foster cohesion [1]. *Also credit:* unstructured interviews, with the disadvantage that they are time-consuming, so samples are small. **[6]**

**10.** First, feminists argue that the economy function has been gendered [1]: girls and boys are steered into different subjects, so they enter differently paid jobs, and education reproduces gender inequality rather than simply allocating roles by ability [1]. Second, they argue that the shared values schools pass on have often reflected men's interests [1], for example through a curriculum and hidden curriculum that present traditional gender roles as normal, so "social cohesion" may mean accepting patriarchy [1]. **[4]**

**11.** The real exam marks extended answers using levels of response, not point by point. The points below show indicative creditworthy content.

- Education teaches literacy, numeracy and specialist skills that modern economies need [1].
- Qualifications allow role allocation, sorting people into suitable jobs [1].
- Vocational courses and apprenticeships prepare people for particular jobs [1].
- Parsons argues schools select individuals for their future roles [1].
- Durkheim argued complex societies need people with specialist skills [1].
- Against: Durkheim saw the major function as transmitting norms and values to build cohesion [1].
- Social mobility is another function, allowing talent to rise in a meritocracy [1].
- Marxists argue education serves capitalism, not the economy as a neutral whole [1].
- Marxists also argue it reproduces class inequality and legitimises it through the myth of meritocracy [1].
- Feminists argue gendered subject choice means education serves a gendered labour market [1].
- Home schooling and de-schooling show some people reject the idea that schooling is mainly for work [1].
- Judgement: economic functions are central, but cohesion and mobility also matter, and whose economy is served is disputed, so "main" depends on perspective [1]. **[12]**

*Examiner insight:* Show the three functions working together before deciding whether one is "main".

**12.** The real exam marks extended answers using levels of response, not point by point. The points below show indicative creditworthy content.

- Durkheim saw the major function of education as transmitting society's norms and values [1].
- History lessons link the individual to society and build commitment to the group [1].
- School teaches cooperation with people who are neither kin nor friends [1].
- Strict rules teach self-discipline and show misbehaviour harms society [1].
- For: citizenship lessons and the promotion of British values in England support his view [1].
- School rules, uniforms and assemblies still teach shared norms [1].
- Against: in a diverse society it is harder to say which values are shared [1].
- Marxists argue the norms passed on are ruling-class norms [1].
- Feminists argue the values taught have reflected men's interests [1].
- Willis's lads rejected school values, so transmission is not always successful [1].
- Schools today stress exam results and skills for work, so the economic function may now matter more [1].
- Judgement: schools still transmit norms and values, but these are contested and not always accepted, so Durkheim's view applies only in part [1]. **[12]**

*Examiner insight:* Apply Durkheim to a modern example, such as citizenship lessons, rather than restating his ideas.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Linking Durkheim with meritocracy or universalistic standards; those are Parsons' ideas.
- Calling an academy a private school.
- In 6(b), giving a concern without explaining why it matters sociologically.
- In 12-mark answers, giving functionalist views only.
