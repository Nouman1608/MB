## Syllabus coverage

Candidates should be able to:

1. explain the origin of pressure in a gas in terms of collisions between gas molecules and the wall of the container
2. understand that ideal gases have zero particle volume and no intermolecular forces of attraction
3. state and use the ideal gas equation pV = nRT in calculations, including in the determination of Mr

## Where gas pressure comes from

Gas molecules move rapidly and randomly. When a molecule hits the wall of its container, it changes direction, so its momentum changes, and the wall exerts a force on it (and it on the wall). Pressure is the **total force of these collisions per unit area** of wall.

This picture explains the qualitative behaviour you will use:

- **More molecules in the same volume** → more frequent collisions with the walls → higher pressure.
- **Higher temperature** → molecules move faster → they hit the walls more often and with more force → higher pressure.
- **Smaller volume** (same amount and temperature) → molecules hit the walls more often → higher pressure.

## The ideal gas model

An **ideal gas** is a model with two assumptions named in the syllabus:

- the particles have **zero volume** (they are treated as points), and
- there are **no intermolecular forces of attraction** between them.

Real gases behave most like this model at **low pressure** and **high temperature**. At low pressure the molecules are far apart, so their own volume is a tiny fraction of the container volume and they rarely come close enough to attract. At high temperature they move fast enough that brief attractions have little effect.

Real gases deviate most at **high pressure** (molecules are close together, so their volume and attractions both matter) and **low temperature** (molecules move slowly, so attractions pull them together and reduce the pressure). Gases with stronger intermolecular forces, such as ammonia with hydrogen bonding, deviate more than gases with very weak forces, such as helium.

## The ideal gas equation

    pV = nRT

| Symbol | Quantity | SI unit |
|---|---|---|
| p | pressure | pascal, Pa (1 kPa = 1000 Pa) |
| V | volume | m³ (1 dm³ = 10⁻³ m³; 1 cm³ = 10⁻⁶ m³) |
| n | amount of gas | mol |
| R | molar gas constant | 8.31 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ |
| T | temperature | kelvin, K (T/K = θ/°C + 273) |

Every calculation fails if the units are not SI. Convert before substituting.

Because n = m/M, the equation can be rearranged to give relative molecular mass directly:

    Mr = mRT / pV        (m in g gives M in g mol⁻¹)

With density ρ = m/V:

    Mr = ρRT / p         (ρ in g m⁻³)

## Worked example 1: pressure in a container

**Question.** 0.0500 mol of a gas is held in a 2.00 dm³ flask at 25 °C. Calculate the pressure.

T = 298 K; V = 2.00 × 10⁻³ m³.

p = nRT/V = (0.0500 × 8.31 × 298) / (2.00 × 10⁻³) = **6.19 × 10⁴ Pa** (61.9 kPa).

## Worked example 2: Mr of a volatile liquid

The syllabus names the **determination of Mr**. A common method uses a gas syringe in an oven:

1. A small hypodermic syringe containing the liquid is weighed.
2. A few drops are injected through a seal into a heated gas syringe, where the liquid evaporates completely.
3. The small syringe is reweighed; the difference is the mass of liquid used.
4. The volume of vapour, the temperature of the oven and the atmospheric pressure are recorded.

**Question.** 0.200 g of a liquid gives 62.0 cm³ of vapour at 100 °C and 101 kPa. Calculate its Mr.

T = 373 K; V = 62.0 × 10⁻⁶ m³; p = 1.01 × 10⁵ Pa.

n = pV/RT = (1.01 × 10⁵ × 62.0 × 10⁻⁶) / (8.31 × 373) = 6.262 / 3099.6 = 2.020 × 10⁻³ mol

Mr = 0.200 / (2.020 × 10⁻³) = **99.0**

This is consistent with dichloroethane, C₂H₄Cl₂ (Mr = 99.0).

**Why the method can give errors.** If some liquid does not evaporate, or some vapour condenses on cooler parts of the syringe, the measured volume is too small, n is too small and the Mr calculated is too **high**. If the vapour is near its boiling point, intermolecular attractions make it less ideal, which also reduces the volume. The method works best for liquids that boil well below the oven temperature.

## Worked example 3: identifying a gas from its density

**Question.** A gas has density 1.25 g dm⁻³ at 273 K and 101 kPa. Calculate its Mr and suggest its identity.

ρ = 1.25 g dm⁻³ = 1250 g m⁻³.

Mr = ρRT/p = (1250 × 8.31 × 273) / (1.01 × 10⁵) = **28.1**

The gas could be nitrogen, N₂ (28.0), or carbon monoxide, CO (28.0). Density alone cannot tell them apart.

## Worked example 4: volume of gas from a reaction

**Question.** 5.00 g of calcium carbonate (Mr = 100.1) is fully decomposed. The carbon dioxide is collected at 20 °C and 100 kPa. Calculate its volume in dm³.

n(CaCO₃) = 5.00 / 100.1 = 0.04995 mol, and CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂, so n(CO₂) = 0.04995 mol.

V = nRT/p = (0.04995 × 8.31 × 293) / (1.00 × 10⁵) = 1.216 × 10⁻³ m³ = **1.22 dm³**

## Checking an answer

At 273 K and 101 kPa, one mole of ideal gas occupies 8.31 × 273 / 1.01 × 10⁵ = 0.0225 m³, about 22.5 dm³ (the data booklet quotes 22.4 dm³ mol⁻¹ at s.t.p., and 24.0 dm³ mol⁻¹ at room conditions). If your answer for 1 mol at ordinary conditions is near 0.02 dm³ or 20 000 dm³, you have missed a unit conversion.

## Common mistakes

- **Using °C instead of K.** Always add 273.
- **Using cm³ or dm³ instead of m³.** Multiply cm³ by 10⁻⁶ and dm³ by 10⁻³.
- **Using kPa instead of Pa.** Multiply by 1000.
- **Rounding too early.** Keep at least four figures until the final answer, then give three significant figures, as in the data.
- **Saying ideal gas molecules have "small" volume.** The assumption is **zero** volume.
- **Stating that real gases deviate at low pressure.** They deviate at **high** pressure.
- **In an Mr experiment, forgetting which direction an error moves Mr.** A smaller measured volume gives a larger Mr.

## Exam technique

- **Explain** the origin of pressure: mention molecules, collisions with the wall and force per unit area.
- **State** the ideal gas assumptions: the two named in the syllabus are zero particle volume and no intermolecular forces.
- **Calculate**: write the rearranged equation first, show the converted units, then substitute. Method marks are often available even if the final answer is wrong.

## Frequently asked questions

**Which value of R should I use?**
Use R = 8.31 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹, which is given in the data booklet. It requires SI units throughout.

**Does pV = nRT work for real gases?**
It is an approximation for real gases. It is closest at low pressure and high temperature, where real gases behave most like an ideal gas.
