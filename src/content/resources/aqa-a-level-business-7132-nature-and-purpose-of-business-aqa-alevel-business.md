This guide goes deeper into section **3.1.1 Understanding the nature and purpose of business** of the AQA AS and A-level Business specification (7132, for first teaching from September 2023). The specification lists four content points:

- **why businesses exist**, with business objectives such as profit, growth, survival, cash flow, and social and ethical objectives
- **the relationship between mission and objectives**
- **why businesses set objectives**
- **the measurement and importance of profit**, including revenue (also turnover and sales), fixed costs, variable costs and total costs.

The combined [nature, purpose and business forms guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-understanding-the-nature-and-purpose-of-business-and-understanding-different/) defines each term. This page goes further on three things the definitions leave out: what makes a mission useful or empty, how a business decides which objective comes first, and how sensitive profit is to each of its parts. Companion pages: [nature and purpose revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-nature-and-purpose-of-business-aqa-alevel-business-revision-notes/) and [nature and purpose practice questions](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-nature-and-purpose-of-business-aqa-alevel-business-practice/). All businesses and figures are invented.

## Why businesses exist: two answers to give

A complete answer to "why does this business exist?" has two halves.

1. **What it does for customers.** It meets a need or want that people will pay for, turning inputs into an output worth more than the inputs cost.
2. **What it does for its owners and founders.** It might provide an income, a return on capital, independence, or a social result they care about.

The second half explains why objectives differ. A plc owned by investors exists largely to generate returns. A social enterprise exists to tackle a social or environmental problem and uses trading to fund it. A family firm may exist to provide secure work for the family and pass the business on. The same activity, such as running a café, can serve any of these purposes.

## Mission and objectives

A **mission** sets out the purpose and direction of the business in broad, qualitative terms. **Objectives** are the targets that turn the mission into something that can be measured and achieved within a set time.

### What makes a mission useful

A mission is useful when it:

- is specific enough to rule some options **out** (a mission to offer "the freshest local food" makes importing cheap frozen stock hard to justify)
- is understood and believed by staff, so it guides everyday decisions
- stays stable while objectives change year to year.

### Why missions are sometimes criticised

- Many are vague enough to fit any business ("to delight our customers"), so they guide nothing.
- They can be written for publicity rather than to direct decisions.
- If objectives and rewards push in a different direction, staff follow the rewards, not the mission.

### The relationship in practice

The mission should come first and the objectives should follow from it. Two businesses in the same market show the link:

| | Business A | Business B |
|---|---|---|
| Mission | "Affordable bikes for every family" | "The most advanced racing bikes in the world" |
| Likely objectives | Grow unit sales 15% in two years; cut unit cost 5% | Win a premium price; launch two new frames a year |
| Decision this favours | Larger factory, simpler designs | Research spending, specialist staff |

When an answer asks about the relationship, show that the objectives follow from the mission and that a decision can be judged against both.

## The objectives the specification names

| Objective | Typical measurable target | Often the priority when... |
|---|---|---|
| Profit | Profit of £150,000 this year; or a satisfactory level that keeps owners content | the business is established and owners want returns |
| Growth | Revenue up 20% in three years; ten new outlets | the market is expanding or owners want scale |
| Survival | Break even within 18 months; avoid closure | the firm is new, or trading conditions are poor |
| Cash flow | Keep a minimum bank balance of £20,000 every month | the firm is short of cash or expanding quickly |
| Social and ethical | Halve waste to landfill by 2028; buy 40% of inputs locally | the owners value it, or customers and staff expect it |

**Profit maximising or satisfactory profit?** Some owners aim for the highest possible profit. Others accept a level that is "good enough", because pushing for the maximum would need more risk, longer hours or decisions they find unethical. Managers who are not owners may also prefer a satisfactory profit combined with growth, which raises their status.

## What decides which objective comes first?

- **Age of the business.** New firms put survival and cash flow first; established firms can aim for profit or growth.
- **Ownership.** Shareholders in a plc may press for profit and dividends; a sole trader may value independence; a social enterprise puts its social aim first.
- **Market conditions.** Strong competition or falling demand pushes survival up the list.
- **The economy.** In a downturn, cash flow often becomes the priority.
- **Finance.** A business that cannot raise funds cannot aim for fast growth.
- **Stakeholder pressure.** Customers, staff or campaigners may push social and ethical objectives forward.

Objectives often conflict. Growth uses cash. Ethical sourcing raises costs. Strong answers name the conflict in the case and say which objective should win **and why**.

## Why businesses set objectives

Objectives give direction and coordinate departments, motivate staff, provide a yardstick for measuring performance, help choose between options and make managers accountable.

They have limits too. An unrealistic target can demotivate. A fixed target can stop managers responding when conditions change. A narrow target, such as a sales figure, can encourage behaviour that harms other aims, for example heavy discounting that hits profit. This is why many businesses review their objectives regularly.

## Measuring profit: how sensitive is it?

Profit = revenue − total costs, where revenue = price × quantity sold and total costs = fixed costs + variable costs.

The useful analysis point is **which part of the calculation matters most**. Take Holloway Print (monthly figures): price £8 per item, variable cost £3 per item, fixed costs £20,000, sales 6,000 items.

Profit = (8 × 6,000) − (3 × 6,000) − 20,000 = 48,000 − 18,000 − 20,000 = **£10,000**.

Now change one thing at a time by 10%:

| Change (others unchanged) | New profit | Change in profit |
|---|---|---|
| Price up 10% to £8.80 | 52,800 − 18,000 − 20,000 = £14,800 | +48% |
| Variable cost up 10% to £3.30 | 48,000 − 19,800 − 20,000 = £8,200 | −18% |
| Sales volume down 10% to 5,400 | 43,200 − 16,200 − 20,000 = £7,000 | −30% |
| Fixed costs up 10% to £22,000 | 48,000 − 18,000 − 22,000 = £8,000 | −20% |

**What this shows.** A 10% change in any one part changes profit by much more than 10%, because profit is a small figure left over after large costs. Price has the biggest effect here because every extra penny goes straight to profit. But the price row assumes sales do not fall; if customers react to the rise, the volume row shows how quickly that gain could disappear. This kind of reasoning turns a calculation into analysis.

## Why profit matters, and to whom

- **Owners and shareholders**: reward for risk, and the source of dividends.
- **The business itself**: retained profit is internal finance for investment.
- **Lenders and investors**: evidence the business can repay and is worth backing.
- **Employees**: profit can support pay rises, bonuses and job security.
- **Government**: profit is taxed.

Profit is not the same as cash. A profitable business can still fail if customers pay late or it ties up cash in stock.

## Worked example: an annotated analysis answer

**Case.** Marram Coffee, a growing chain with the mission "great coffee, sourced fairly", has set an objective to open 12 new cafés in two years. Cash is tight and a cheaper, uncertified coffee supplier has offered a 15% discount.

**Analyse one conflict between Marram's objectives.**

*Model answer, with notes.* Marram's growth objective needs cash to fit out 12 cafés, *(objective from the case)* and the cheaper supplier would free cash by cutting the cost of its main input. *(link)* However, buying uncertified coffee would contradict the "sourced fairly" mission and any ethical objective that follows from it. *(conflict with mission)* If customers chose Marram partly because of fair sourcing, switching could damage the brand and reduce sales in the very cafés it is opening, undermining growth itself. *(chain of consequences back to the first objective)*

## Exam technique

- **Define**: precise wording; for example, fixed costs do not change with output in the short run.
- **Calculate**: show working; state units and whether the answer is a profit or a loss.
- **Explain / Analyse**: build a chain from the case fact to the effect on the business.
- **Assess / Evaluate**: weigh at least two sides and reach a judgement that says what it depends on.
- In multiple-choice questions on profit, check whether the figures are per unit or totals before choosing.

## Common mistakes

- Giving "to make a profit" as the only reason a business exists.
- Treating mission and objectives as the same thing.
- Listing objectives without saying which matters most in the case and why.
- Classing wages as fixed or variable without thinking about how staff are paid.
- Calculating profit without commenting on what drives the change.
- Assuming profit means the business has cash.
