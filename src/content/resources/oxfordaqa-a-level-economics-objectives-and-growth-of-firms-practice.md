## Questions

**1.** Explain what is meant by satisficing. **[3]**

**2.** A cheese dairy in Country H has demand P = 80 − 2Q and total cost TC = 120 + 8Q + Q², where Q is tonnes a week and money is in £.

**(a)** Find the output, price and profit when the dairy maximises profit. **[4]**
**(b)** Find output, price and profit if the dairy maximises sales revenue instead. **[3]**
**(c)** Explain one reason why managers might prefer the output in (b). **[2]**

**3.** A tailor in Country J has fixed costs of £600 a week and variable costs of £25 per garment, and makes 80 garments a week.

**(a)** At a price of £30, show whether the tailor should keep producing in the short run. **[3]**
**(b)** At a price of £20, show what the tailor should do. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the lowest price at which the tailor stays in business in the long run. **[1]**

**4.** Explain two reasons why ownership is separated from control in large firms. **[4]**

**5.** A company's managers receive a bonus of 3% of revenue. Plan X gives revenue of £8.0m and profit of £1.5m. Plan Y gives revenue of £9.5m and profit of £0.9m.

**(a)** Calculate the bonus under each plan. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why the bonus may conflict with shareholders' interests, and suggest one change. **[4]**

**6.** A state water authority in Country K faces demand P = 20 − 0.01Q, marginal cost of £4 per unit and fixed costs of £4,800.

**(a)** Find the output, price and profit when the authority maximises profit. **[4]**
**(b)** The authority must break even. Show that output of 1,200 units at £8 achieves this. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the subsidy needed if price equals marginal cost. **[2]**

**7.** In a market worth £600m, Firm M sells £90m and Firm N sells £66m. M makes 9m units at a total cost of £63m, and N makes 6m units at a total cost of £42m.

**(a)** Calculate their combined market share after a merger. **[2]**
**(b)** The merger removes £6m of costs. Calculate the percentage fall in average cost per unit. **[3]**

**8.** A target firm has 30m shares at £1.80. A bidder offers a 25% premium. Calculate the total price, the premium paid in £m, and the years of £4.5m annual savings needed to recover the premium. **[4]**

**9.** A coffee roaster buys 300,000 kg of beans a year at £5.00 a kg. The importer's margin is £1.00 a kg. Buying the importer would add £0.30 a kg of running cost.

**(a)** Name the type of integration. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the yearly saving. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain one risk of this integration. **[2]**

**10.** Firm S earns profits (£m) of 8, 2, 9, 3 over four years. Firm T earns 3, 8, 2, 7.

**(a)** Calculate the combined profit each year and the range for S, T and the combined firm. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain why a conglomerate merger might be chosen even with no cost savings. **[2]**

**11.** Evaluate whether a large listed company is likely to pursue profit maximisation. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** Satisficing means aiming for a satisfactory outcome, not the maximum [1]. The decision maker sets a target, such as a minimum profit that keeps shareholders content [1]. Once it is met, effort goes to other aims, such as market share or a quieter life [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* A definition scores better with a short example of a target than with a bare statement.

**2. (a)** MR = 80 − 4Q and MC = 8 + 2Q [1]. Set equal: 72 = 6Q, so **Q = 12** [1]. P = 80 − 24 = **£56** [1]. TR = 672, TC = 120 + 96 + 144 = 360, so profit = **£312** [1].
**(b)** MR = 0, so Q = 20 [1]. P = 40 and TR = **£800** [1]. TC = 120 + 160 + 400 = 680, so profit = **£120** [1].
**(c)** Revenue is £128 higher than in (a) [1]. If pay or status depends on size and sales, managers gain although profit is £192 lower [1].
*Examiner insight:* Show the MR equation before solving; an answer with only the final output gives the reader nothing to follow if the arithmetic slips.

**3. (a)** TR = 30 × 80 = £2,400 and variable cost = £2,000 [1]. Contribution £400 pays part of the £600 fixed cost, so the loss is £200 [1]. Shutting down loses £600, so it **keeps producing** [1].
**(b)** TR = £1,600 is below variable cost £2,000, so the loss is £1,000 [1]. That exceeds £600, so it should **shut down** [1].
**(c)** 600 ÷ 80 + 25 = **£32.50** [1].
*Examiner insight:* Compare the loss from producing with the loss from shutting; stating only "revenue is below total cost" misses the short-run test.

**4.** Growth needs more capital than a few owners can provide, so shares are sold to many investors [1], which spreads ownership and passes day-to-day decisions to others [1]. Large firms need specialist managers with technical skill [1]. Limited liability makes it easier to attract many small investors, none of whom can control the firm alone [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Give reasons for the divorce, not its consequences; a second reason that only restates the first gains little.

**5. (a)** X: 0.03 × 8.0m = **£240,000** [1]. Y: 0.03 × 9.5m = **£285,000** [1].
**(b)** Managers gain £45,000 by choosing Y [1], but profit falls by £0.6m, so shareholders lose [1]. Managers know more about the firm than owners, so the choice is hard to monitor [1]. Link the bonus to profit or share price, or award share options [1].
*Examiner insight:* Use both plans' figures to show the conflict; a general statement about managers' motives is weaker than a comparison of £45,000 with £0.6m.

**6. (a)** MR = 20 − 0.02Q; set equal to 4 [1]. **Q = 800** [1]. P = 20 − 8 = **£12** [1]. TR = 9,600, TC = 4,800 + 3,200 = 8,000, so profit = **£1,600** [1].
**(b)** TR = 8 × 1,200 = £9,600 [1]. TC = 4,800 + 4 × 1,200 = £9,600, so profit is zero [1].
**(c)** P = 4 gives Q = 1,600 [1]. TR = 6,400 and TC = 11,200, so subsidy = **£4,800** [1].
*Examiner insight:* Check that your break-even price lies on the demand curve; testing it against the demand curve shows you understand the objective.

**7. (a)** M: 90 ÷ 600 = 15% and N: 66 ÷ 600 = 11% [1]. Combined **26%** [1].
**(b)** Costs are 63 + 42 = £105m for 15m units, so £7.00 each [1]. After the saving: 99 ÷ 15 = **£6.60** [1]. Fall = 0.40 ÷ 7.00 = **5.7%** [1].
*Examiner insight:* A percentage change needs the original value as the base; dividing by the new figure gives a different answer.

**8.** Offer price = 1.80 × 1.25 = £2.25 [1]. Total price = 30m × 2.25 = **£67.5m** [1]. Premium = 30m × 0.45 = **£13.5m** [1]. 13.5 ÷ 4.5 = **3 years** [1].
*Examiner insight:* State what the payback ignores, such as integration costs; a short comment on that gains credit that the number alone cannot.

**9. (a)** Vertical (backward) integration [1].
**(b)** Net saving per kg = 1.00 − 0.30 = £0.70 [1]. 0.70 × 300,000 = **£210,000** [1].
**(c)** The roaster may be tied to an inefficient supplier [1], or lose flexibility to buy cheaper beans elsewhere [1].
*Examiner insight:* Name the type precisely: "vertical" with direction beats "integration" alone.

**10. (a)** Combined: 11, 10, 11, 10 [1]. Ranges: S 9 − 2 = 7, T 8 − 2 = 6 [1], combined 11 − 10 = **1** [1].
**(b)** Profits move in opposite directions, so the combined firm is less exposed to downturns in one market [1]. Stable profit helps survival and lowers the firm's risk to lenders [1].
*Examiner insight:* Put the ranges next to each other; the stability is clear only when compared.

**11.** Traditional theory assumes MR = MC and profit maximisation because owners want returns and firms must earn normal profit to survive [1]. In a large listed firm ownership is separate from control [1], so managers may pursue sales, growth or status, giving a principal-agent problem [1]. Shareholders can respond with profit-linked pay, share options, independent directors and the threat of takeover [2]. Competitive pressure also makes slack costly, which pushes towards profit [1]. Information is limited, so managers may satisfice, reaching a target without maximising [1]. Short-run revenue or market-share aims may support later profit, so objectives differ by time period [1]. Judgement: the answer depends on market power, how widely shares are spread and how well controls work [1]; with strong competition and good controls profit becomes the dominant aim, while a dominant firm with weak shareholder oversight is more likely to satisfice [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* Evaluation gains credit when it names conditions and reaches a supported conclusion; a list of arguments for and against with no verdict is weaker.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Stating MR = MC but not calculating MR from the demand equation.
- Mixing up profit-maximising and revenue-maximising outputs.
- Shutting a firm down because it makes a loss, when revenue covers variable cost.
- Ignoring that normal profit is included in costs.
- Describing a merger as horizontal when the firms are at different stages.
- Calculating a percentage change from the wrong base.
- Giving the benefits of growth with no costs.
- Evaluating with a list and no judgement.
