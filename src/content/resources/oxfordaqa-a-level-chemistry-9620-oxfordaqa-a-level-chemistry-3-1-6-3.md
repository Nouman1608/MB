## What rate of reaction means

**Rate of reaction**: the change in the amount or concentration of a reactant or product **per unit time**.

When concentration is measured in mol dm⁻³ and time in seconds, the unit is **mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹**. Rates are always given as positive values: a reactant's concentration falls while a product's rises, but both describe the same rate.

```
mean rate over an interval = change in concentration ÷ time taken
```

### Worked example 1: a mean rate from concentration data

In a reaction A → products, [A] falls from 0.80 mol dm⁻³ to 0.62 mol dm⁻³ in the first 90 s.

```
change in [A] = 0.80 − 0.62 = 0.18 mol dm⁻³
mean rate = 0.18 ÷ 90 = 2.0 × 10⁻³ mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹
```

### Other ways of following a reaction

Not every experiment measures concentration directly. Common measurements and the rate they give:

| What is measured | Rate expressed as |
|---|---|
| Volume of gas collected | cm³ s⁻¹ |
| Loss of mass as a gas escapes | g s⁻¹ |
| Time for a fixed amount of reaction (a solid to disappear, a precipitate to hide a mark) | relative rate = 1 ÷ time, in s⁻¹ |

The last method works because a reaction that takes half the time to reach the same point has, on average, twice the rate.

## The qualitative effect of temperature

Increasing the temperature **increases** the rate of reaction; decreasing it **decreases** the rate. The effect is large: a rise of only a few kelvin often produces a clearly measurable increase in rate.

### Worked example 2: comparing rates from times

A fixed length of magnesium ribbon is added to excess hydrochloric acid at different temperatures, and the time for the ribbon to disappear is recorded (illustrative results):

| Temperature / °C | 20 | 30 | 40 | 50 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Time / s | 160 | 82 | 41 | 22 |
| Relative rate, 1/t / s⁻¹ | 0.00625 | 0.0122 | 0.0244 | 0.0455 |

In these results the relative rate roughly **doubles for each 10 K rise**, and increases about **seven-fold** from 20 °C to 50 °C (0.0455 ÷ 0.00625 = 7.3). The temperature in kelvin has risen by only about 10 % (293 K to 323 K). So a small fractional change in temperature produces a large change in rate. Explaining this is the core of the subtopic.

For a fair test, the same length of ribbon, the same volume and concentration of acid, and the same container are used; only the temperature changes.

## Explaining the large effect

Heating a reaction mixture has two effects.

**1. Molecules move faster, so they collide more often.** This increases the collision frequency, but only by a few per cent for a 10 K rise near room temperature. On its own it cannot explain a doubling of rate.

**2. A much larger proportion of molecules have energy ≥ Ea.** The Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution moves to higher energies. The activation energy is fixed, and it lies far out in the tail of the curve. Because the tail is small and steep, shifting the curve to the right **increases the area beyond Ea by a large factor**. Many more collisions now have enough energy to react.

**The second effect is the main one.** The number of successful collisions per second rises sharply, so the rate rises sharply.

### Worked example 3: building a full-mark explanation

**Question.** Use the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution to explain why a small increase in temperature can lead to a large increase in the rate of a reaction between two gases.

**Annotated answer.**

1. Sketch two curves for the same gas, labelled T₁ and a higher T₂, with T₂'s peak lower and to the right, both starting at the origin and with equal areas [the diagram carries marks of its own].
2. Mark Ea on the energy axis, well to the right of both peaks, and shade the area beyond Ea under each curve [shows what is being compared].
3. At the higher temperature the molecules have more energy on average, so the distribution shifts to higher energy [describes the change].
4. Ea does not change, and the area beyond Ea is much larger for T₂, so a much greater proportion of molecules have energy ≥ Ea [the key point, stated with "proportion"].
5. So a much greater proportion of collisions are successful, and there are many more successful collisions per second [links to collision theory].
6. Collisions also become slightly more frequent, but this is a minor effect compared with the increase in the proportion with energy ≥ Ea [ranks the two effects].

## The reverse: cooling

Cooling has the opposite effect. The distribution shifts to lower energies and the area beyond Ea shrinks by a large factor, so the rate falls sharply. This is why food kept in a refrigerator spoils far more slowly than at room temperature: the reactions that cause spoilage slow down.

## What temperature does **not** change

- **The activation energy**: Ea is a property of the reaction route, not of the temperature.
- **The number of molecules**: the area under the distribution is unchanged.
- **The route of the reaction**: only a catalyst provides an alternative route.

## Common mistakes

- Defining rate as "how fast a reaction is" or "the time taken", rather than change per unit time.
- Giving rates as negative numbers for a reactant.
- Explaining the temperature effect only through more frequent collisions.
- Writing "more molecules have the activation energy" instead of "**energy equal to or greater than** the activation energy".
- Saying heating lowers the activation energy.
- Drawing the higher-temperature curve with a higher peak or a larger area.
- Using 1/t as if it were in mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹; it is only a relative rate.

## Exam technique

- **Define** rate with "change in concentration ... per unit time" and give the unit if asked.
- **Calculate**: show the change and the time, and give the unit.
- **Explain** using the distribution: two labelled curves, Ea marked, and the words "greater proportion of molecules with energy ≥ Ea", then "more successful collisions per second".
- If asked to compare the two effects of heating, state clearly that the energy effect is **much larger** than the collision-frequency effect.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the rate of a reaction?**
Rate of reaction is the change in concentration (or amount) of a reactant or product per unit time. With concentration in mol dm⁻³ and time in seconds, the unit is mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹.

**Why does a small temperature rise cause a large increase in rate?**
The Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution shifts to higher energy, and because the activation energy lies far out in the tail, the proportion of molecules with energy equal to or greater than Ea increases by a large factor. Collision frequency increases only slightly.

**Does temperature change the activation energy?**
No. The activation energy stays the same; what changes is the proportion of molecules with enough energy to overcome it.
