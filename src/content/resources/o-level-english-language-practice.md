## Section A

**1.** State the **three** things you must identify before beginning any directed writing task. **[3]**

**2.** Explain the difference between a summary and a paraphrase. **[2]**

## Section B

**3.** You are asked to write a speech to your school persuading students to volunteer at a local shelter.

**(a)** State the register you would use and justify it. **[2]**
**(b)** Identify **three** features of a speech you must include. **[3]**
**(c)** Explain **two** persuasive devices suitable for this audience. **[4]**

**4.** Explain the approach to a summary question in **five** steps. **[5]**

**5.** For a descriptive composition entitled "The Market at Dawn":

**(a)** Explain how you would structure it. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain how to use the five senses effectively, and one common mistake to avoid. **[4]**

**6.** Explain how to answer a comprehension question worth 3 marks, and explain why quoting a whole sentence often loses marks. **[4]**

---

## Answers

**1.** The **purpose** — to persuade, inform, advise or argue [1]; the **audience** — who will read or hear it [1]; the **form** — letter, speech, report, article, each with its own conventions [1].

**2.** A **summary** selects **only the relevant points and compresses them**, so it is much shorter than the original [1]. A **paraphrase restates the whole passage in different words** at roughly the same length [1].

**3. (a)** **Semi-formal** [1] — the audience is fellow students, so it should be warm and direct rather than stiff, but it is a formal school occasion, so slang would undermine credibility [1].
**(b)** A **direct opening address** to the audience ("Good morning, everyone") [1]; **signposting between sections** so listeners can follow without rereading [1]; a **clear, memorable closing call to action** [1].
**(c)** Any two, 2 marks each: **rhetorical questions** — invite the audience to answer privately and feel they reached the conclusion themselves [1] [1]; **the rule of three** — memorable in speech, where listeners cannot go back and reread [1] [1]; **personal anecdote** — makes the shelter concrete and emotionally real rather than an abstraction [1] [1]; **inclusive pronouns** ("we", "our school") — creates shared responsibility [1] [1].

**4.** **Read the question carefully and underline exactly what is being asked for** — many marks are lost by summarising the wrong thing [1]. **Read the passage and underline only the relevant points**, ignoring examples and repetition [1]. **List the points in your own words**, checking none is a duplicate [1]. **Write them as continuous prose in one paragraph**, linked with connectives, not as a list [1]. **Check the word count and cut examples and adjectives first**, since only the points earn marks [1].

**5. (a)** Use a **clear time or spatial progression** — for example from the empty square before dawn, through the arrival of the traders, to the first customers [1]; **begin with a striking image rather than an explanation** [1]; **end by returning to the opening image, changed**, which gives a sense of shape [1].
**(b)** Use **sight, sound, smell, touch and taste**, but **do not attempt all five in every paragraph** [1]; **choose one or two per moment and develop them precisely** — "the sting of woodsmoke" rather than "it smelled nice" [1]; anchor each to a **specific, concrete detail** rather than a general statement [1]. Common mistake: turning the description into a **narrative with a plot**, which loses the descriptive focus [1].

**6.** Identify **how many separate points the mark allocation requires** — 3 marks usually means three distinct points [1]. **Answer in your own words** wherever the question asks you to explain, using short quotations only as evidence [1]. Keep each point **brief and directly answering the question asked** [1].
Quoting a whole sentence loses marks because it **does not demonstrate understanding** — the examiner cannot tell whether you have identified which part of the sentence answers the question, and "own words" questions explicitly require you to show comprehension by rewording [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Ignoring the specified audience and writing in the wrong register.
- Writing a summary as a bulleted list instead of continuous prose.
- Including examples and repetition in a summary when only points earn marks.
- Lifting whole sentences from the passage in "own words" questions.
