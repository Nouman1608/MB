Producing clear and coherent text is the first strand of 3.1.2 Writing in AQA GCSE English Language (8700). It is
about control: choosing the right voice for a reader, organising ideas so that each follows from the last, and
keeping the whole piece consistent from first line to last. It underpins Section B of both papers. For quick recall
use the [clear and coherent writing revision notes](/resources/aqa-gcse-english-language-8700-producing-clear-and-coherent-text-revision-notes/),
and to practise use the
[clear and coherent writing practice questions](/resources/aqa-gcse-english-language-8700-producing-clear-and-coherent-text-practice/).

## What the specification includes

The strand lists:

- writing effectively for different purposes and audiences: to describe, narrate, explain, instruct, give and respond
  to information, and argue;
- selecting vocabulary, grammar, form, and structural and organisational features judiciously to reflect audience,
  purpose and context;
- using language imaginatively and creatively;
- using information provided by others to write in different forms;
- maintaining a consistent point of view;
- maintaining coherence and consistency across a text.

It is assessed by **AO5**: communicate clearly, effectively and imaginatively, selecting and adapting tone, style and
register for different forms, purposes and audiences; organise information and ideas, using structural and
grammatical features to support coherence and cohesion. **AO6** (vocabulary, sentence structures, spelling and
punctuation) is marked alongside it. In each paper's Section B, 24 marks go to content and organisation and 16 to
technical accuracy.

| Paper | Section B task | Purposes most often in play |
|---|---|---|
| Paper 1 | Narrative or descriptive writing from a choice of scenario, prompt or image | Describe, narrate |
| Paper 2 | A non-fiction text giving your viewpoint, with audience, purpose and form specified | Argue, explain, persuade, give and respond to information |

## Purpose shapes everything

Each purpose pulls the writing towards different choices:

| Purpose | Typical choices |
|---|---|
| Describe | Sensory detail, precise nouns and verbs, controlled focus, often present tense |
| Narrate | A sequence of events, a viewpoint character, pace control, a clear shape |
| Explain | Logical order, causal connectives ("because", "as a result"), examples |
| Instruct | Imperatives, numbered or ordered steps, second person |
| Give and respond to information | Accurate facts, neutral or measured register, clear signposting |
| Argue | A clear position, reasons and evidence, counter-argument and rebuttal |

A single text can mix purposes. An article arguing for a later school day may explain research, describe a tired
morning and narrate a short anecdote, but the controlling purpose stays argument.

## Audience, form and register

**Register** is the level of formality. Matching it to the reader is the clearest sign of control. Compare two
openings with the same purpose:

- *To a head teacher (letter):* "I am writing to ask you to reconsider the decision to close the library at
  lunchtime."
- *To fellow students (speech):* "Raise your hand if the library has ever saved your lunchtime. Exactly."

Both are appropriate; neither would suit the other audience. Register is not only vocabulary: sentence length,
contractions, pronouns ("you" or "one") and humour all move it.

**Form** brings conventions. A letter needs an appropriate greeting and sign-off; an article benefits from a headline
and perhaps a subheading; a speech addresses listeners directly and signals structure aloud; a leaflet may use
subheadings. Use the conventions the form expects, but do not let layout replace content: a headline earns little
if the article beneath it is disorganised.

## Coherence and cohesion

These two words are often confused.

- **Coherence** is whether the whole text makes sense as a sequence: the ideas are in a logical order and the reader
  always knows why one paragraph follows another.
- **Cohesion** is the set of linguistic devices that bind sentences and paragraphs together.

Cohesive devices include:

- **topic sentences** that announce each paragraph's idea;
- **discourse markers** ("however", "consequently", "at first", "meanwhile") that signal the relationship between
  ideas;
- **pronoun reference** ("this", "they") that points back clearly, never ambiguously;
- **lexical chains**, where related words (*storm, thunder, drenched*) keep a thread running;
- **deliberate repetition** of a key word or image to tie the opening to the ending.

A text can be cohesive but incoherent: every sentence linked with "furthermore" while the ideas jump randomly. Aim
for both: plan the logic first, then signal it.

## Maintaining a consistent point of view

In narrative, consistency means keeping the same narrative person (first or third) and tense unless a change is
deliberate and signalled. A story that slips from "I" to "she", or from past to present in the middle of a paragraph,
loses the reader's trust.

In argument, consistency means keeping your stance. Acknowledging a counter-argument is a strength; drifting into
agreeing with it is not. Signal concessions clearly: "Some argue that..., but this ignores...".

## Using information provided by others

The specification includes turning information from others into new forms. In practice, this might mean using facts
from a briefing to write a leaflet, or drawing ideas from the Paper 2 reading sources into your own article. Select
only what serves your purpose, rewrite it in your register, and integrate it rather than copying a list.

## Worked example: improving coherence

**Draft paragraph (original):**

> School uniform should be scrapped. My cousin's school has no uniform. Uniform is expensive. Some people say it stops
> bullying. Also it is uncomfortable. Students would be happier.

**Revised paragraph (annotated):**

> School uniform should be scrapped, and cost is the strongest reason **[topic sentence states the paragraph's
> idea]**. A full set of branded items can stretch a family's budget, especially when a growing child needs new ones
> each year **[development of one idea]**. Supporters argue that uniform prevents bullying about clothes **[counter-
> argument signposted]**; however, pupils who want to single someone out will find another target, from shoes to
> phones **[rebuttal with discourse marker]**. If uniform does not protect pupils, it is hard to justify what it costs
> the families who pay for it **[final sentence links back to the topic]**.

The revision removes the anecdote that did no work, groups the ideas, uses "however" to show the relationship
between counter-argument and rebuttal, and ends where the topic sentence began. The comfort point has been saved for
its own paragraph.

## Common mistakes

- **Ignoring the audience named in the task**, so the register is wrong from the first line.
- **Treating form as layout only**, with a headline but no structure beneath it.
- **Lists of points** with no development or links.
- **Overusing one discourse marker**, especially "also" or "furthermore".
- **Slipping tense or person** in narrative.
- **Drifting stance** in argument after a counter-argument.
- **Copying information** from a source rather than reshaping it.

## Exam technique

- Underline the **audience, purpose and form** in the Paper 2 task before planning.
- Spend a few minutes planning a **sequence** of paragraphs, each with a one-line idea.
- Write the **first and last lines** of the plan first, so the ending can echo the opening.
- After writing, check three things: register matches the reader; tense and person are consistent; every paragraph
  connects to the one before.
