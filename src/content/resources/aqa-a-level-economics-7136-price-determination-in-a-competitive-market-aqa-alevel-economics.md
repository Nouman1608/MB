## One market, step by step

The specification wants you to "use demand and supply diagrams to help them to analyse causes of changes in
equilibrium market prices" and to "apply their knowledge of the basic model of demand and supply to a variety
of real-world markets". A reliable sequence:

1. **Identify the market** and the event.
2. **Decide which curve moves** and why. Ask: does the event change buyers' willingness to buy at each price
   (demand), or the cost or willingness to sell at each price (supply)? A change in the good's own price is a
   movement along a curve, never a shift.
3. **Show the direction of shift** and the new equilibrium.
4. **Explain the adjustment:** at the old price there is excess demand or excess supply, and that is what
   moves the price.
5. **Use elasticity** to say how far price and quantity change.
6. **Follow the effect** into related markets if the question asks.

## The adjustment process in words

At the initial price after a rightward shift in demand, quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied: **excess
demand**. Buyers compete for limited stock and sellers find they can raise prices. As price rises, quantity
demanded contracts along the new demand curve and quantity supplied extends along the supply curve, because
higher prices imply higher profits and give producers the incentive to expand output. The process stops where
the two are equal. With **excess supply**, unsold stock leads sellers to cut prices until the surplus is
cleared. Markets are in **disequilibrium** whenever planned demand and planned supply differ at the current
price.

## Worked example: equilibrium algebra with two shifts (invented)

The weekly market for bicycle helmets in Country H:

- Demand: Qd = 1,200 − 50P
- Supply: Qs = −300 + 100P

**Initial equilibrium.** Set Qd = Qs: 1,200 − 50P = −300 + 100P, so 1,500 = 150P, **P = 10** and **Q = 700**.

**Disequilibrium check.** If the price were stuck at 8, Qd = 1,200 − 400 = 800 and Qs = −300 + 800 = 500:
excess demand of **300** helmets, so the price would be bid up.

**Event 1: a cycling safety campaign raises demand** to Qd = 1,500 − 50P. Now 1,500 − 50P = −300 + 100P, so
**P = 12** and **Q = 900**.

Price elasticity of supply over this movement: quantity rises from 700 to 900, which is 200 ÷ 700 = **28.6%**;
price rises from 10 to 12, which is **20%**. PES = 28.6 ÷ 20 = **1.43**, elastic.

**Event 2 instead: a cheaper plastic shifts supply right** to Qs = 100P with the original demand.
1,200 − 50P = 100P gives **P = 8** and **Q = 800**. Along the demand curve, price falls 20% and quantity
rises 100 ÷ 700 = 14.3%, so PED = 14.3 ÷ (−20) = **−0.71**, inelastic. Total revenue falls from 10 × 700 =
7,000 to 8 × 800 = 6,400, as an inelastic PED predicts when price falls.

**Both events together.** 1,500 − 50P = 100P gives **P = 10** and **Q = 1,000**. Quantity rises by 300 but the
price is unchanged.

The general lesson: when both curves shift right, **quantity certainly rises, but the effect on price depends
on the relative size of the shifts**. When demand shifts right and supply shifts left, **price certainly rises,
but the effect on quantity is uncertain**. Say which variable is ambiguous and why.

| Demand | Supply | Price | Quantity |
|---|---|---|---|
| right | right | uncertain | rises |
| left | left | uncertain | falls |
| right | left | rises | uncertain |
| left | right | falls | uncertain |

## Elasticity decides how a shift shows up

The specification asks you to "interpret numerical values" of elasticities. In market analysis that means
predicting the split between price and quantity effects.

- **A demand shift with inelastic supply** (PES below 1) mainly raises **price**. Short-run supply of
  concert tickets, fresh fish or housing is like this.
- **A demand shift with elastic supply** mainly raises **quantity**. Manufactured goods with spare capacity
  and stocks are like this.
- **A supply shift with inelastic demand** mainly changes **price**, and total revenue moves in the same
  direction as price.
- **A supply shift with elastic demand** mainly changes **quantity**, and total revenue moves the opposite way to
  price.

### Interpreting income and cross elasticity values (invented data)

| Good | Elasticity | Value | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Restaurant meals | YED | +1.8 | normal good; demand grows faster than income |
| Bread | YED | +0.3 | normal good; demand grows much more slowly than income |
| Bus travel | YED | −0.4 | inferior good; demand falls as incomes rise |
| Tea and coffee | XED | +0.6 | substitutes, but not close ones |
| Games consoles and games | XED | −1.3 | strong complements |
| Umbrellas and bread | XED | 0 | unrelated |

Use these in context. If incomes in Country H are forecast to grow 4%, restaurant demand rises by about
4 × 1.8 = **7.2%** and bus travel falls by about 4 × 0.4 = **1.6%**, ceteris paribus.

## Interrelationships between markets (3.1.3.6)

The specification names five relationships. One fresh example of each:

- **Joint demand:** electric cars and home chargers are bought together. A subsidy that lowers car prices raises
  demand for chargers.
- **Competitive demand:** train and coach travel between two cities compete. A rail strike raises demand for
  coach seats and their price.
- **Composite demand:** a good wanted for more than one use. Milk is demanded for drinking and for cheese
  making. Higher demand for cheese diverts milk from the drinking market, so supply of drinking milk shifts left
  and its price rises.
- **Derived demand:** demand for a factor or input depends on demand for the final product. Rising demand for
  holidays raises demand for pilots and airport staff.
- **Joint supply:** producing one good yields another. Refining crude oil produces petrol and also bitumen for
  roads; higher petrol demand raises refinery output, increasing bitumen supply and lowering its price.

In a chain answer, name the relationship, move one curve in the second market, and state the result for its
price and quantity.

## Assumptions of the model

The specification wants awareness of the assumptions behind the competitive model:

- Many buyers and sellers, none able to set the price alone.
- Prices are free to adjust, without controls or long contracts.
- Buyers and sellers know the prices on offer.
- **Ceteris paribus:** only the stated determinant changes.

Real markets break these in different ways. Housing rents are often fixed for a year, so prices adjust slowly.
Electricity is supplied by a small number of firms. When an assumption fails, the direction of a prediction
may still be right, but the speed or size of the change may differ: a useful evaluation point.

## Common errors

- Shifting the demand curve because the good's own price changed.
- Claiming a definite price change when both curves shift.
- Calculating elasticity with absolute changes rather than percentage changes.
- Forgetting the sign: negative PED and negative XED carry meaning, and a negative YED identifies an inferior
  good.
- Stating that higher demand "causes" higher supply. Higher demand raises price, which causes an **extension**
  of supply along the curve.
- Naming a related market without saying which curve moves in it.

## Exam technique

- **Calculate:** write the formula, show the percentage changes and state the answer with a sign.
- **Explain, with a diagram:** label axes (price, quantity), curves (D₁, D₂, S) and equilibria (P₁, Q₁, P₂,
  Q₂), and describe the excess demand or supply that moves the price.
- **Analyse:** go one step further than the diagram, for example to total revenue or a related market.
- **Assess / Evaluate:** weigh the size of elasticities, the time period and the realism of the model's
  assumptions, then reach a judgement.
