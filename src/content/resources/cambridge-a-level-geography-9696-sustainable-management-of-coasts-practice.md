## Section A

**1.** Define the term "managed realignment". **[2]**

**2.** Explain how rock armour reduces cliff erosion. **[2]**

**3.** State two disadvantages of a recurved sea wall. **[2]**

**4.** Outline what is meant by "coastal squeeze". **[3]**

**5.** Explain why groynes can increase erosion elsewhere in a sediment cell. **[3]**

**6.** Outline two ways that dune regeneration schemes stabilise coastal dunes. **[4]**

## Section B

**7.** The fictional town of Porthallow is protected by a beach that is nourished with 400,000 m³ of sand every 5 years at a cost of $15 per m³. An alternative is a rock revetment costing $28 million to build, with maintenance of $0.4 million a year.

**(a)** Calculate the cost of beach nourishment over 30 years, assuming the first placement is in year 0 and the last in year 25. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the cost of the revetment over 30 years. **[2]**

**(c)** Suggest two factors other than cost that should influence the choice. **[4]**

**8.** A Shoreline Management Plan for the fictional Felbridge coast proposes "no active intervention" for a 4 km stretch of farmland cliffs and "hold the line" for the 1 km seafront of Felbridge town.

**(a)** Suggest why different policies are proposed for the two stretches. **[3]**

**(b)** Suggest how two different groups might respond to the "no active intervention" policy. **[4]**

**9.** Explain how mangrove restoration can reduce coastal flood risk. **[5]**

**10.** Explain why sea-level rise makes "hold the line" policies harder to sustain. **[5]**

**11.** With reference to a case study, describe the problems of managing a stretch of coastline. **[6]**

**12.** "Soft engineering is always more sustainable than hard engineering." With reference to examples, how far do you agree? **[20]**

## Answers

**1.** Allowing the shoreline to move landward in a controlled way [1], usually by breaching or removing old defences so low-value land floods to form intertidal habitat behind a new set-back defence [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Large boulders at the cliff foot absorb and dissipate wave energy through the gaps between them [1], so less energy reaches the cliff and undercutting is reduced [1]. **[2]**

**3.** It is very expensive to build and maintain [1]. Reflected waves scour the beach in front, lowering it and risking undermining the wall [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Intertidal habitats such as saltmarsh or mangrove would naturally migrate landward as sea level rises [1]. Fixed defences or development behind them block that migration [1], so the habitat is squeezed into a narrowing strip and lost [1]. **[3]**

**5.** Groynes trap sediment carried by longshore drift on their updrift side [1]. Less sediment passes downdrift, so beaches there narrow [1]. Narrow beaches absorb less wave energy, so cliffs or defences downdrift erode faster [1]. **[3]**

**6.** Sand fences reduce wind speed so blowing sand is deposited and trapped [1], building the dune up [1]. Planting marram grass binds sand with its roots and traps more sand as it grows upward [1], while boardwalks and fencing keep visitors off fragile dune surfaces so vegetation is not trampled [1]. **[4]**

**7. (a)** Placements in years 0, 5, 10, 15, 20 and 25 = 6 placements [1]. Each costs 400,000 x 15 = $6 million, so total = 6 x 6 = $36 million [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** Maintenance = 0.4 x 30 = $12 million [1]. Total = 28 + 12 = $40 million [1]. **[2]**

**(c)** The effect on the sediment cell, since a revetment would stop sediment supply from the protected cliff while nourishment adds sediment [1], so other beaches may be affected [1]. The value of the beach for tourism, which nourishment preserves but a revetment may reduce [1], affecting local jobs [1]. **[4]**

**8. (a)** The town has high-value property, infrastructure and jobs, so the benefits of defence exceed the costs [1]. The farmland has low economic value per kilometre, so defending it would cost more than the land is worth [1]. Allowing the farmland cliffs to erode keeps sediment supplied to beaches elsewhere in the cell, including the town's [1]. **[3]**

**(b)** Farmers and landowners are likely to oppose it, because they lose land and income without compensation [1] and may campaign for defences or payment [1]. Conservation groups may support it, because natural cliffs expose geology and supply sediment to beaches and habitats [1], and natural retreat avoids coastal squeeze [1]. **[4]**

**9.** Mangrove roots and trunks create friction that slows tidal currents and storm surges [1]. Wave height falls as waves pass through the forest [1]. Roots trap sediment, so the land surface can build up [1], which helps it keep pace with sea-level rise [1]. The belt of trees keeps floodwater back from settlements behind, reducing damage [1]. **[5]**

**10.** Higher sea level brings deeper water to the defences, so larger waves reach them [1]. Storm surges overtop defences more often, so walls must be raised [1]. Beaches in front narrow and lower, exposing the structure to scour [1]. Coastal squeeze removes saltmarsh that once absorbed energy [1]. Costs rise year after year, so benefits may no longer justify the expense [1]. **[5]**

**11.** Credit a named stretch of coast with relevant detail. For example: problems are identified with named locations [1]; a physical cause such as weak geology or high wave energy is explained [1]; a human cause such as interrupted sediment supply or development on the coast is explained [1]; the consequences for people (homes, roads, jobs) are given [1]; consequences for habitat or the environment are given [1]; supporting data such as retreat rates, dates or costs are included [1]. **[6]**

**12.** The real exam marks this essay with levels of response, not point by point. The points below are indicative creditworthy content.

*Introduction:* define hard and soft engineering [1] and the tests of sustainability: environmental, economic, social and long term [1].

*Soft more sustainable:* works with natural processes, so it does not starve downdrift beaches [1]. The Sand Engine used natural spreading of a single placement [1]. Managed realignment at Medmerry reduced maintenance and created intertidal habitat [1]. Mangrove restoration absorbs wave energy and can build land with rising sea level [1]. Lower whole-life cost in many places [1].

*Soft less sustainable:* nourishment must be repeated and depends on a sediment source [1]. Realignment loses land, so landowners lose out [1]. Soft methods cannot protect dense settlement where there is no space, as in Malé [1].

*Hard more sustainable in some places:* gives reliable protection for high-value land and infrastructure [1]. Malé's sea wall protects a capital city on a low island [1]. Long-lived structures can have favourable cost-benefit ratios where property values are high [1].

*Hard less sustainable:* groynes and walls move problems along the sediment cell, as at Mappleton [1]. Sea walls cause beach scour and coastal squeeze [1]. Costs rise with sea level [1].

*Judgement:* "always" is too strong [1]. Sustainability depends on the value of land, sediment cell effects and time scale [1]. Combined schemes, such as rock structures with nourishment, may be most sustainable [1]. A reasoned final view on "how far" with named examples [1]. **[20]**
