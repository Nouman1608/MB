Condensed notes on section **3.4.2 Analysing operational performance** of AQA A-level Business (7132, for first teaching from September 2023). The full explanations and the Marsden Ceramics worked example are in the [operational performance study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-analysing-operational-performance/); exam-style questions are in the [operational performance practice set](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-analysing-operational-performance-practice/).

## Specification content

- The interpretation, calculation and use of operations data in operational decision making and planning.
- You should be able to calculate: labour productivity; unit costs (average costs); capacity; capacity utilisation.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Labour productivity | Output per worker (or per hour) in a period |
| Unit cost (average cost) | Total cost divided by total output |
| Fixed costs | Costs that do not change with output in the short run |
| Variable costs | Costs that change with output |
| Capacity | Maximum output possible in a period with existing resources |
| Capacity utilisation | Actual output as a percentage of maximum output |
| Under-utilisation | Using much less than full capacity |
| Benchmarking | Comparing performance with rivals or best practice |

## Formulas

| Measure | Formula |
|---|---|
| Labour productivity | total output ÷ number of employees |
| Labour productivity per hour | total output ÷ total hours worked |
| Total cost | fixed costs + (variable cost per unit × output) |
| Unit cost | total cost ÷ output |
| Capacity utilisation (%) | actual output ÷ maximum output × 100 |
| Spare capacity | maximum output − actual output |

Always give the **unit** (units per worker, £ per unit, %).

## Worked example to remember

Output 48,000 plates a month; capacity 60,000; 60 employees working 160 hours each; fixed costs £90,000; variable cost £3 per plate.

- Productivity: 48,000 ÷ 60 = **800 per employee**; hours = 9,600, so **5 per hour**.
- Total cost: £90,000 + 48,000 × £3 = £234,000; unit cost = **£4.875**.
- Capacity utilisation: 48,000 ÷ 60,000 × 100 = **80%**.
- Output rises to 54,000: productivity **900**; total cost £252,000; unit cost **£4.67**; utilisation **90%**.
- At 60,000: total cost £270,000; unit cost **£4.50**.

Why unit cost falls: the same fixed costs are spread over more units.

## Second worked example: reading two years of data

| | Year 1 | Year 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Output | 3,600 | 3,780 |
| Employees | 12 | 14 |
| Maximum capacity | 4,800 | 4,800 |
| Total costs | £288,000 | £321,300 |

- Productivity: 3,600 ÷ 12 = **300**; 3,780 ÷ 14 = **270**, a fall of 10%.
- Unit cost: £288,000 ÷ 3,600 = **£80**; £321,300 ÷ 3,780 = **£85**, a rise of 6.25%.
- Utilisation: 3,600 ÷ 4,800 = **75%**; 3,780 ÷ 4,800 = **78.75%**.
- Reading: output rose 5% but staff rose by two, so productivity fell and unit cost rose. Spare capacity remains. Possible causes: staff hired ahead of demand or not yet trained. The numbers raise the question; they do not answer it alone.

## Interpreting capacity utilisation

| Level | Meaning | Consequences |
|---|---|---|
| Low | Under-utilised | High unit costs; wasted resources; scope for new orders |
| About 80 to 90% | Efficient with some flexibility | Lower unit costs; can handle small extra demand |
| 100% | Full capacity | No flexibility; risk of late delivery, breakdown, turned-away orders; overtime or outsourcing costs |

No single "ideal" figure: depends on whether demand is steady or seasonal and on the cost of failing to supply.

## Interpreting productivity and unit costs

- Compare **over time**, **against rivals** and **against targets**.
- Falling productivity: poor morale, absence, weak training, old equipment, inexperienced workers.
- Rising unit cost: higher material prices, lower output (fixed costs spread thinner), wage rises, waste.
- If wages rise by less than productivity, labour cost per unit falls.

## Using the data

- **Capacity:** high utilisation suggests investing; low suggests closing a line or finding new customers.
- **Staffing:** productivity shows workers needed for a given output.
- **Pricing and orders:** unit cost is a floor for price; check an order's price against it.
- **Improvement:** trends and benchmarks show where to focus.

## Limits of the data

- Averages hide variation between shifts or products.
- Productivity measures quantity, not quality.
- Allocating fixed costs between products is a judgement.
- Data is historical; short periods may mislead.
- Automation can raise labour productivity while adding capital cost.

## Exam traps

- Unit cost is **total** cost divided by output, including fixed costs.
- Multiply by 100 for capacity utilisation.
- 100% utilisation is not always best.
- Match the productivity measure (per worker or per hour) to the data.
- Always interpret the number you calculate.

## Self-test

1. Define "capacity utilisation".
2. A firm makes 7,500 units with 25 workers. Calculate labour productivity.
3. Its fixed costs are £12,000 and variable costs are £4 per unit. Calculate total cost at 7,500 units.
4. Calculate unit cost at 7,500 units.
5. Its maximum capacity is 10,000 units. Calculate capacity utilisation.
6. Output rises to 9,000 units. Calculate the new unit cost.
7. Explain why unit cost fell in question 6.
8. Give two consequences of operating at 100% capacity.
9. Labour productivity falls from 120 to 108 units per worker. Calculate the percentage change.
10. Give two possible causes of falling productivity.
11. Give one limitation of using productivity data alone to judge performance.
12. A firm has capacity of 50,000 units and spare capacity of 12,000 units. Calculate capacity utilisation.

### Answers

1. Actual output as a percentage of the maximum possible output.
2. 7,500 ÷ 25 = **300 units per worker**.
3. £12,000 + (7,500 × £4 = £30,000) = **£42,000**.
4. £42,000 ÷ 7,500 = **£5.60**.
5. 7,500 ÷ 10,000 × 100 = **75%**.
6. Total cost = £12,000 + 9,000 × £4 = £48,000; unit cost = £48,000 ÷ 9,000 = **£5.33** (to two decimal places).
7. Fixed costs of £12,000 are spread over more units.
8. Any two: no flexibility; late deliveries or turned-away orders; risk of breakdown; staff stress; overtime or outsourcing costs.
9. (108 − 120) ÷ 120 × 100 = **−10%**, a fall of 10%.
10. Any two: low morale; absence; poor training; old equipment; inexperienced workers.
11. Productivity measures quantity, not quality; or it hides variation between shifts or products.
12. Actual output = 50,000 − 12,000 = 38,000; 38,000 ÷ 50,000 × 100 = **76%**.
