> **HL only. These are original questions written for Marlbridge** to practise Regional study 1 of Africa and the Middle East (750 to 1291). They are not reproduced exam questions and do not copy the layout or mark tariffs of the real Paper 3, which has essay questions only. Use them with the [study guide](/resources/ib-dp-history-2028-abbasids-and-crusades/) and [revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-history-2028-abbasids-and-crusades-revision-notes/). Source extracts are constructed for practice.

## Section A: knowledge and short answers

**1.** Give two reasons why the Umayyad caliphate was weak by the 740s. **[2]**

**2.** Explain why the foundation of Baghdad in 762 mattered for the Abbasid state. **[3]**

**3.** Identify three groups that held real power in the Abbasid lands by 1055. **[3]**

## Section B: working with an argument

*Constructed for practice, not a real source:* "The Franks did not take Jerusalem because their armies were stronger. They took it because the lords of Syria preferred to guard their own cities than to join together." (a modern textbook paragraph)

**4.** Explain what argument this extract makes about the First Crusade. **[3]**

**5.** Suggest two pieces of evidence from outside the extract that could be used to challenge it. **[4]**

*Constructed for practice, not a real source:* "Baghdad was not lost because of one enemy. It was emptied from within, by governors who kept the taxes and soldiers who obeyed their captains before their caliph." (a paraphrase of a historian's view)

**6.** Examine how far this view of the end of Abbasid power fits the evidence you have studied. **[6]**

## Section C: essay questions

**7.** To what extent was the success of Salah ad-Din against the Crusader states due to his own leadership? **[15]**

**8.** Evaluate the claim that the Crusades had a greater impact on the Middle East than the Mongol invasions. **[15]**

## Answers

**1.** Any two, one mark each: rivalry between Arab tribal groups divided the army; non-Arab converts (*mawali*) resented Arab privilege; Shi'a supporters of the Prophet's family opposed Umayyad legitimacy; revolts and civil war after 744 drained resources. **[2]**

**2.** One mark for the founding (al-Mansur, a new capital on the Tigris). Up to two more for development: it moved the centre east towards Iraq and Persia and away from Syria; it sat on trade routes and rich farmland, giving revenue; it became the centre of court, administration and learning. **[3]**

**3.** One mark each for three of: the Buyids (holding Baghdad from 945 and controlling the caliph); the Seljuks (entering Baghdad in 1055); the Fatimids in Egypt; Turkic military commanders; autonomous governors or dynasties in the east or Egypt. **[3]**

**4.** One mark for the claim that Muslim disunity, not Frankish strength, explains the capture of Jerusalem. One for the reason offered: Syrian lords put their local interests first. One for noting it is a comparison (strength against disunity) that downgrades military factors. **[3]**

**5.** Up to two marks for each piece of evidence, with detail. For example: the crusaders defeated much larger forces and kept together through the siege of Antioch in 1098 and the hardships of the march (strength and determination); Byzantine guidance and the fall of Nicaea in 1097 helped them; Fatimid Egypt took advantage of Seljuk weakness to recapture Jerusalem in 1098, which shows Muslim powers were not just guarding their own cities; naval support from Italian cities supplied sieges. **[4]**

**6.** Level guide: 1 to 2 marks for description of the Abbasid end only; 3 to 4 for partial examination with some evidence; 5 to 6 for a judgement on fit. Credit points that support the view (provincial autonomy by Tahirids, Saffarids and Tulunids; Turkic commanders and the Samarra years; the Buyids in 945) and points that challenge it (the Fatimid religious challenge; the Zanj revolt; the Seljuk and Mongol conquests as outside blows; the 1258 sack ended an institution that had already weakened). A top answer concludes that the view fits the long process well but underplays external and religious factors. **[6]**

**7.** Marking points, up to 15 in total, using the paper's markband logic (understanding of the argument, evaluation of perspectives, judgement):
- up to 3 for understanding the argument (leadership against other causes, with Salah ad-Din's career: Egypt 1171, Syria, Hattin 1187, Jerusalem);
- up to 4 for evidence for the claim: unifying Egypt and Syria, the use of jihad as a rallying idea, the victory at Hattin where he lured the Frankish army away from water;
- up to 4 for challenging factors: the groundwork of Zengi and Nur ad-Din, Frankish divisions and mistakes before Hattin, the support of the Egyptian and Syrian economies;
- up to 4 for a judgement consistent with the argument, for example that leadership was important but built on earlier unity, and that Richard I and his army still held the coast in 1192, so success was limited. **[15]**

**8.** Marking points, up to 15 in total:
- up to 3 for understanding "impact" and framing a comparison (political, economic, social, cultural);
- up to 5 for the Crusades: Crusader states and later Muslim reaction, trade, massacre in Jerusalem 1099, longer-term memory;
- up to 4 for the Mongols: the sack of Baghdad in 1258, destruction of the caliphate and cities, and the response of the Mamluks at Ain Jalut (1260);
- up to 3 for a judgement with criteria. A strong answer may argue the Mongols had the greater immediate impact on central Muslim lands, while the Crusades had a long cultural and political memory, and that the two are not easily separated since the Mamluks gained power fighting both. **[15]**
