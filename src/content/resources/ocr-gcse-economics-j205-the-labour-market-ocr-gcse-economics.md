## What the specification asks

Learners should be able to:

- explain the role and operation of the labour market, including the interaction between workers and employers;
- analyse the determination of wages through supply and demand, including factors affecting the supply and demand of labour;
- explain and calculate gross and net pay, including deductions through income tax, national insurance and pension contributions.

The specification's quantitative skills include calculating income, including gross and net pay.

## How workers and employers interact

The labour market is a factor market. **Workers supply labour; employers demand it; the wage is its price.** The interaction happens at several stages.

| Stage | What employers do | What workers do |
|---|---|---|
| Recruitment | Advertise vacancies, set the wage and conditions | Search for jobs, compare offers |
| Selection | Interview and test applicants | Apply and show their skills |
| Agreement | Offer a contract of employment | Accept, refuse or negotiate |
| Employment | Pay wages, provide training, set hours | Provide effort and skills; may ask for pay rises or change jobs |
| Bargaining | Negotiate with individuals or with a trade union representing many workers | Negotiate alone or through a trade union |

The labour market's role is to match people to jobs: to move workers into the occupations and places where employers most need them. When this works, firms find the skills they need and workers earn incomes. When it does not, there are unfilled vacancies in some jobs and unemployment in others.

### Bargaining power

The outcome of the interaction depends on bargaining power:

- Workers have more power when their skills are scarce, when unemployment is low (employers must compete for staff), or when they bargain together through a trade union.
- Employers have more power when many people can do the job, when unemployment is high, or when they are the main employer in an area.

## Wage determination through supply and demand

- **Demand for labour** slopes downwards: at a lower wage, employers want to hire more workers. Labour demand is derived from demand for the product the workers make.
- **Supply of labour** slopes upwards: a higher wage attracts more people into the occupation.
- The **equilibrium wage** is where the number of workers employers want equals the number willing to work.

**Worked example: care workers in Eastmoor (invented).**

| Hourly wage (£) | Demand for workers | Supply before | Supply after |
|---|---|---|---|
| 10 | 1,000 | 400 | 700 |
| 11 | 900 | 600 | 900 |
| 12 | 800 | 800 | 1,100 |
| 13 | 700 | 1,000 | 1,300 |
| 14 | 600 | 1,200 | 1,500 |

- At first the equilibrium is **£12 an hour with 800 workers**.
- A new free training course qualifies 300 more people at every wage, so labour supply shifts right.
- At £12 there are now 1,100 willing workers for 800 jobs: a surplus of 300. Wage offers fall until the market clears at **£11 with 900 workers**.

```
Wage change       = (11 − 12) ÷ 12 × 100 = −8.3%
Employment change = (900 − 800) ÷ 800 × 100 = +12.5%
```

**Consequences:** employers fill more posts at lower cost; more people are employed in care; but each care worker earns less per hour. The analysis chain is the same as for any market: **cause → curve and direction → surplus or shortage at the old wage → new wage and employment → who gains and who loses.**

### Factors affecting the demand for labour

- **Demand for the product:** more customers mean more staff are needed (derived demand).
- **Productivity:** more productive workers are worth more to an employer.
- **Price and ability of machines:** if machines become cheaper and can do the job, demand for labour may fall; if machines make workers more productive, demand may rise.
- **Costs of employing:** if the cost of each worker to the firm rises, firms may hire fewer.

### Factors affecting the supply of labour

- **Skills and qualifications needed:** long or costly training reduces supply.
- **Wages in other occupations:** better pay elsewhere draws workers away.
- **Working conditions and non-wage benefits:** hours, safety, flexibility, holidays and job security.
- **Size of the working population:** affected by migration, the age structure and how many people choose to work.

## Explaining why pay differs between jobs

Use the supply and demand framework for every comparison, not a list of opinions.

| Question to ask | High pay if... | Low pay if... |
|---|---|---|
| How many people can do it? | Few, because training is long or talent rare | Many, because few skills are needed |
| How much value does the work add? | High, so demand is strong | Low, so demand is weaker |
| How attractive is the job? | Dangerous or unsociable, so extra pay is needed | Pleasant or flexible, so people accept less |
| How strong is bargaining power? | Workers are organised or scarce | Workers are numerous and unorganised |

**Worked comparison.** An airline pilot earns more than a café assistant because supply of pilots is limited by long, expensive training and strict standards, while demand is high because each flight earns an airline a large revenue. Many people can work in a café with little training, so supply is large relative to demand.

## Gross pay and net pay

- **Gross pay** is total earnings before deductions.
- **Net pay** (take-home pay) is what the worker receives after deductions.

```
Net pay = gross pay − income tax − national insurance − pension contributions
```

Income tax and national insurance are paid to the government. Pension contributions are saved for the worker's retirement.

**Illustrative rules for Country B (invented, not real UK rates):**

- Income tax: first £12,000 tax-free; 20% on income from £12,000 to £50,000; 40% on income above £50,000.
- National insurance: 10% on income from £12,000 to £50,000; 2% above £50,000.
- Pension: 5% of gross pay.

**Worked example 1: annual salary of £28,000.**

```
Taxable income = 28,000 − 12,000 = £16,000
Income tax     = 16,000 × 0.20 = £3,200
NI             = 16,000 × 0.10 = £1,600
Pension        = 28,000 × 0.05 = £1,400
Net pay        = 28,000 − 3,200 − 1,600 − 1,400 = £21,800
Per month      = 21,800 ÷ 12 = £1,816.67
```

**Worked example 2: annual salary of £60,000.**

```
Income tax: 20% band = (50,000 − 12,000) × 0.20 = £7,600
            40% band = (60,000 − 50,000) × 0.40 = £4,000
            Total = £11,600
NI:         (38,000 × 0.10) + (10,000 × 0.02) = 3,800 + 200 = £4,000
Pension:    60,000 × 0.05 = £3,000
Net pay     = 60,000 − 11,600 − 4,000 − 3,000 = £41,400
Net as % of gross = 41,400 ÷ 60,000 × 100 = 69%
```

**A pay rise of £1,000 for this worker** is all in the top band: extra tax £400, extra NI £20, extra pension £50. Net pay rises by £1,000 − £470 = **£530**. The worker on £28,000 keeps a larger share of their whole pay (77.9%) than the worker on £60,000 (69%), because higher income is taxed at a higher rate.

**Converting an hourly wage.** £14.50 an hour for 37.5 hours a week over 52 weeks gives gross annual pay of 14.50 × 37.5 × 52 = **£28,275**.

## Common mistakes

- Treating the labour market as a product market: here workers are the **sellers**.
- Shifting the labour demand curve for a change in the wage; a wage change is a movement along it.
- Explaining pay differences with "some jobs are harder" and no supply or demand reasoning.
- Applying the 40% rate to the whole income instead of only the part above the threshold.
- Forgetting that the pension is a deduction from gross pay, even though the worker keeps it as savings.
- Mixing weekly, monthly and annual figures.

## Exam technique

- **Explain the role of the labour market:** matching workers to jobs, with the wage as the price.
- **Analyse wage changes:** name the curve that shifts, give the surplus or shortage at the old wage, then the new wage and employment.
- **Calculate pay:** set out each deduction on its own line, label the bands, and state the time period of the answer.
