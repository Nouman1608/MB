## Questions

**1.** State **two** ways in which a business can behave ethically. **[2]**

**2.** Define **ethical behaviour**. **[2]**

**3.** Identify **two** environmental considerations named in the specification, other than recycling. **[2]**

**4.** Trentmoor Bakery uses 60,000 paper boxes a year. Recycled boxes cost 6p more each. The bakery's yearly profit is £48,000. Calculate the extra yearly cost of recycled boxes, and express it as a percentage of profit. **[4]**

**5.** Explain **one** benefit and **one** drawback to a business of behaving ethically. **[4]**

**6.** Brantham Coaches runs five diesel minibuses. Replacing them with electric ones would cost £140,000 each instead of £95,000. Each electric minibus would cost £6,000 a year to run, against £14,000 for a diesel one. Analyse the trade-off between sustainability and profit in this decision. **[6]**

**7.** Gransden Joinery has a loan of £90,000 and an average overdraft of £20,000. The loan rate rises from 5% to 7% and the overdraft rate from 11% to 14%. Calculate the total extra interest the business pays in a year. **[4]**

**8.** Explain **two** ways in which higher interest rates could reduce Gransden Joinery's sales of fitted kitchens. **[4]**

**9.** The level of employment in the town where Trentmoor Bakery trades rises sharply. Analyse how this could affect the bakery. **[4]**

**10.** Discuss how the demand for fitted kitchens and the demand for basic bread might each change if household incomes fall. **[6]**

**11.** Gransden Joinery's yearly timber bill is £30,000. Timber from a certified sustainable source would cost 15% more. Profit is £42,000 before this change, and interest costs are about to rise by £2,400 a year (see question 7). Evaluate whether Gransden should switch to the sustainable timber. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** Any two, one mark each: paying suppliers a fair price [1]; honest advertising and labelling [1]; fair pay and safe conditions for staff, or fair treatment of customers (accept similar fair and honest actions). **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Each way must show fairness or honesty; a vague word like "being nice" gives nothing to credit, and two versions of the same idea count once.

**2.** Acting in ways that stakeholders consider [1] to be both fair and honest [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A definition needs both ideas; "fair" alone, or "legal", leaves out part of the meaning.

**3.** Any two of: traffic congestion, disposing of waste, noise pollution, air pollution [1] [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Naming the specification's own areas is safest; a general phrase such as "protecting nature" may not match what the question asks for.

**4.** Extra cost: 60,000 × 6p = 360,000p [1] = **£3,600** [1]. Percentage of profit: 3,600 ÷ 48,000 × 100 [1] = **7.5%** [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Convert pence to pounds before comparing with profit, and write the unit with each answer.

**5.** Benefit: a better reputation with customers who value fairness, so loyalty and sales may rise [1] and the business may find it easier to recruit staff [1]. Drawback: ethical choices such as fair supplier prices cost more [1], which lowers short-run profit or forces higher prices [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Each of the two points needs a reason that explains it, not just a statement; "good for the business" alone gains little.

**6.** Extra upfront cost: 5 × (£140,000 − £95,000) = £225,000 [1]. Yearly running saving: 5 × (£14,000 − £6,000) = £40,000 [1]. Time to recover the extra cost: £225,000 ÷ £40,000 = about 5.6 years [1]. Sustainability benefit: lower emissions that help with global warming, and a better image [1]. Profit cost: a large outlay now, which may strain cash and lower profit in the short run [1]. Judgement: worthwhile only if the firm can fund the cost and expects to keep the vehicles beyond about 5.6 years, because the benefit comes later [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Analysis improves when the figures are used in the argument; a general line about "saving the planet" without numbers stays at the level of description.

**7.** Loan interest before: £90,000 × 5% = £4,500; after: £90,000 × 7% = £6,300; extra £1,800 [1]. Overdraft before: £20,000 × 11% = £2,200 [1]; after: £20,000 × 14% = £2,800 [1]. Extra £600. Total extra = £1,800 + £600 = **£2,400** a year [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Show each rate calculation; a wrong total can still gain credit for correct working, but a bare wrong figure cannot.

**8.** Customers who borrow to pay for a kitchen face higher repayments [1], so some delay or cancel the purchase [1]. Households with mortgages or loans have less spare income after paying more interest [1], so they spend less on non-essential home improvements [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Each way needs a link from the rate rise to customers' behaviour, not just "people spend less".

**9.** More people have jobs and wages, so more households can afford to buy bread and cakes, raising demand [1] [1]. The bakery may find it harder to recruit staff [1] and may need to pay more to attract and keep them, which raises costs [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A chain of cause and effect (jobs, then incomes, then sales) is stronger than naming an effect alone, and one effect on demand plus one on human resources shows breadth.

**10.** Fitted kitchens: expensive, and usually put off when incomes fall [1], so demand is likely to fall sharply [1]. Bread: a basic item that households keep buying [1], so demand falls little, though some may trade down to cheaper loaves [1]. Business response: Gransden might offer cheaper ranges or finance deals, while the bakery may promote value lines [1]. Judgement: the fall is likely to hit the kitchen business harder because it sells a high-cost, non-essential item [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Discussion questions reward a contrast between products and a short conclusion; treating both products the same loses the comparison.

**11.** Extra timber cost: £30,000 × 15% = £4,500 [1]. Profit after timber and interest: £42,000 − £4,500 − £2,400 = £35,100, a fall of about 16% [1]. For switching: a better reputation with environmentally aware customers [1]; protects the supply of a scarce resource for the long term [1]. Against: profit is already under pressure from interest, and £6,900 of extra cost is a large share of profit [1]; if customers will not pay more, the cost may not be recovered [1]. If it raises prices, sales of non-essential kitchens could fall [1]. Judgement: switching is sensible if Gransden can pass part of the cost to customers who value sustainability [1] or phase the change in over time [1]; with interest costs rising and demand sensitive to income, a gradual move is the more prudent decision. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* An evaluation needs figures from the case, both sides and a judgement with a reason; a one-sided list of benefits stays in the lower levels of response.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "legal" instead of "fair and honest" when defining ethical behaviour.
- Giving only benefits or only costs of an environmental or sustainable choice.
- Not using the figures in the case when analysing or evaluating.
- Forgetting to convert pence to pounds, or leaving off the unit.
- Explaining why interest rates change when the question asks about the effect.
- Saying "people have less money" without linking it to a purchase decision.
- Treating all products as equally sensitive to a change in incomes.
- Finishing an evaluate answer with a summary instead of a judgement.
