Pearson Edexcel International GCSE History 4HI1 is assessed by two externally assessed written papers
of identical size: each lasts 1 hour 30 minutes, carries 60 marks and is worth 50%. Their internal
structure differs. On **Paper 1: Depth Studies**, candidates answer two questions on two depth studies
chosen from eight named options -- among them the French Revolution, the unification of Italy, Germany
under dictatorship, the USSR under dictatorship, superpower relations, US civil rights, and South
Africa under apartheid. On **Paper 2: Investigation and Breadth Studies**, candidates answer one
historical investigation chosen from five named options plus one breadth study in change chosen from
eight. Both papers are available as paper-based or on-screen assessment. These notes complement the
site's guides to
[Paper 1 Depth Studies](/resources/igcse-edexcel-world-history-paper-1-depth-studies/) and
[Paper 2 Investigation and Breadth Studies](/resources/igcse-edexcel-world-history-paper-2-investigation-breadth-studies/).

## Know exactly which four options you are entered for

Between the two papers a candidate is examined on two depth studies, one investigation and one breadth
study -- four selections drawn from three separate menus. **Exam-preparation priority**: write your
four down and revise only those. This sounds obvious and is routinely got wrong, because textbooks and
online material cover options your centre has not taught. Time spent on the Russian Revolution when
your centre teaches apartheid is time entirely wasted.

## Depth and breadth are opposite skills and need opposite notes

A depth study rewards dense, specific knowledge of a short period: names, dates, particular policies,
particular incidents. A breadth study in change rewards the ability to see a long arc -- what changed,
what stayed the same, what drove each. **Exam-preparation priority**: make two kinds of notes. For
depth, detailed chronological notes; for breadth, a timeline with turning points marked and a
one-line "what changed here" against each. Revising a breadth study using depth-study methods produces
a candidate who knows a great deal and cannot answer the question asked.

## Ninety minutes for sixty marks, split across two questions per paper

Each paper's 60 marks are divided between two substantial answers -- roughly 45 minutes each, including
planning. **Exam-preparation priority**: practise complete answers to a 40-minute limit. Both papers
have exactly the same shape in this respect, so the pacing practice transfers between them.

## The historical investigation rewards handling evidence, not narrating the topic

Paper 2's investigation component is about working with historical material -- what it shows, how far
it can be trusted, what it does and does not establish. **Exam-preparation priority**: practise
evaluating sources you have not seen before, and specifically practise saying what a source is good
evidence *for* given who produced it, rather than dismissing it as biased. Provenance is information,
not a disqualification.

## Change questions need causes ranked, not listed

Breadth study questions in change typically ask how far something changed, or what most drove a
change. Both demand a judgement between factors. **Exam-preparation priority**: for each breadth
study, rehearse ranking three or four causes against each other and being able to justify the ranking.
A list of causes with no relative weighting answers a different question from the one asked.

## Worked routine: a change-and-continuity answer

The routine below is an original model written for this resource, not a reproduction of any official
past paper, source booklet or mark scheme.

```
Step 1 - state the overall judgement in one sentence:
"Between [dates], X changed substantially in [respect], but
continuity persisted in [respect]."

Step 2 - evidence the change:
Two specific, dated pieces of evidence. Named policies, named
figures, quantities where you have them.

Step 3 - evidence the continuity:
At least one specific example of what did NOT change. This is the
half most often missing, and it is half the question.

Step 4 - weigh them:
Which mattered more, and for whom? Change for a governing elite is
not the same as change for a rural population -- saying so is a
mark-earning distinction.

Step 5 - conclude by returning to step 1:
Confirm or qualify the opening judgement, with the reason.
```

Step 3 is the reliable differentiator. A question about change is also a question about what stayed
the same, and answers that only evidence change have addressed half of it.

## Before/during exam checklist

- **Before the exams**: write down the four options you are actually entered for and revise only
  those; keep depth-study notes detailed and chronological, and breadth-study notes as a timeline of
  turning points; rehearse ranking causes for each breadth study; practise source evaluation on
  unseen material.
- **During either paper**: budget about 45 minutes per answer, including planning.
- **On the investigation**: say what each source is good evidence for, given its provenance, rather
  than dismissing it as biased.
- **On change questions**: evidence both change and continuity, then weigh them, then conclude.

## Self-test

1. How many options is a candidate examined on across the two papers, and from how many menus?
2. How should depth-study notes differ from breadth-study notes?
3. What half of a change question is most often missing from answers?
4. Why is calling a source biased not, by itself, an evaluation?

**Answers:** 1. Four -- two depth studies, one historical investigation and one breadth study in
change -- drawn from three separate lists of named options. 2. Depth notes should be dense and
chronological, carrying names, dates and specific policies; breadth notes should be a long timeline
with turning points marked and a one-line statement of what changed at each. 3. Continuity: specific
evidence of what did *not* change over the period, which is half of what a change question asks. 4.
Because provenance is information rather than a disqualification -- the mark comes from saying what
the source is good evidence for, given who produced it and why.
