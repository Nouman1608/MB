This guide covers section 3.1.2 Resource allocation of the OxfordAQA International GCSE Economics (9214) specification, for exams from May/June 2025 onwards. In the specification's words, students "look at the role of markets in allocating scarce resources", then explore "the different economic sectors" and conclude "with a look at benefits and costs of specialisation, the division of labour and how this naturally leads to exchange."

This page goes further on 3.1.2: how prices move resources between uses, how one firm takes part in both kinds of market, how to read sector data by output, and why specialisation leads to exchange. ## 3.1.2.1 How markets allocate scarce resources

Because resources are scarce, something must decide which uses they go to. In a market, **price** does this job in three ways.

1. **Signal.** A rising price tells producers that buyers want more of a product.
2. **Incentive.** A higher price means higher profit, so producers move land, labour and capital into making it.
3. **Rationing.** A higher price means buyers who value the product less stop buying, so the limited supply goes to those most willing and able to pay.

### Worked example 1: resources move between uses

In Country M, demand for electric bicycles rises as fuel becomes more expensive. The price of an electric bicycle rises from 800 to 920.

```
Percentage rise = (920 - 800) / 800 x 100 = 15%
```

- **Signal and incentive**: bicycle makers earn more on each sale, so they expand. A factory that made petrol scooters, whose sales are falling, switches its machinery and workers to electric bicycles.
- **Factor markets respond**: makers need more engineers, so wages for engineers rise and more people train for that work.
- **Rationing**: at 920, some buyers who would have paid 800 choose a cheaper ordinary bicycle instead.

No government plan ordered these changes; millions of separate decisions by buyers and sellers moved resources from scooters to electric bicycles. That is the role of markets in allocating scarce resources. An evaluation can add that this process takes time and leaves out people who cannot afford the higher price, which links forward to market failure in 3.1.6.

### Factor markets and product markets

| | Product market | Factor market |
|---|---|---|
| What is traded | Goods and services | Land, labour, capital and enterprise |
| Usual buyer | Households (consumers) | Firms (producers) |
| Usual seller | Firms | Households, which own the factors |
| Name of the price | The price of the product | Rent, wages, interest |

### Worked example 2: one firm, both markets

Oakline Furniture makes chairs.

- It **rents** a workshop from a landowner: the factor market for land, paying rent.
- It **hires** carpenters: the labour market, a factor market, paying wages.
- It **borrows** to buy machinery: capital is supplied in return for interest.
- It **sells** chairs to households: the product market, at the price of a chair.

The households that buy the chairs may include the carpenters themselves, spending the wages they earned. That is why the two kinds of market are linked: income earned in factor markets is spent in product markets.

## 3.1.2.2 Economic sectors and how they change

- **Primary**: extracting natural resources (farming, fishing, mining, forestry).
- **Secondary**: manufacturing, processing and construction.
- **Tertiary**: services (retail, transport, banking, health, education, tourism).

A **chain of production** shows the sectors working together. Cocoa beans are grown by a farmer (primary), made into chocolate in a factory (secondary), then transported, advertised and sold in a shop (tertiary). Each stage adds value.

### Worked example 3: reading sector data by output

The specification asks how relative sizes "may change over time". Sizes can be measured by **employment** or by **output**. Country R's output (billion):

| Sector | Year 1 output | Year 1 share | Year 25 output | Year 25 share |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Primary | 60 | 30% | 50 | 10% |
| Secondary | 70 | 35% | 150 | 30% |
| Tertiary | 70 | 35% | 300 | 60% |
| Total | 200 | 100% | 500 | 100% |

```
Secondary output change = (150 - 70) / 70 x 100 = +114% (to the nearest whole number)
Secondary share change  = 30 - 35 = -5 percentage points
Primary output change   = (50 - 60) / 60 x 100 = -16.7%
```

The secondary sector more than doubled its output but its share still fell, because the tertiary sector grew even faster. Always check whether a question asks about the **size** of a sector or its **share**. Common reasons for a shift towards the tertiary sector include rising incomes (people spend more on services such as holidays and healthcare), machinery replacing farm workers, and cheaper imports of manufactured goods.

### Goods and services

A **good** is tangible and can be stored; a **service** is intangible and is usually consumed as it is provided. A restaurant meal mixes both: the food is a good, but the cooking, serving and setting are a service, so the restaurant is in the tertiary sector.

## 3.1.2.3 Specialisation, division of labour and exchange

**Specialisation** is concentrating on a limited range of tasks, products or services. Its types are specialisation by **individuals** (occupations), **firms** (product ranges), **regions** and **countries**. **Division of labour** is specialisation within a firm: production is split into tasks, each done by a different worker.

### Why individuals and producers specialise

- Repeating one task builds skill and speed.
- No time is lost switching between tasks and tools.
- Specialised machinery can be used for each task.
- Training is quicker when a worker learns one task.
- People and firms can concentrate on what they are best at.

### Costs and benefits of the division of labour

| | Benefits | Costs |
|---|---|---|
| Worker | Becomes skilled and fast; may earn more as a specialist | Repetitive work can be boring and lower motivation; narrow skills make it harder to find a new job if the task is automated |
| Firm | Higher output per worker; lower cost per unit; easier recruitment and training | Bored workers make more mistakes; if one stage stops, the whole line can stop; workers cannot easily cover for each other |

### Worked example 4: how specialisation leads to exchange

Two villages each have 10 workers. In one day:

| | Fish per worker | Baskets per worker |
|---|---|---|
| Coast village | 8 | 2 |
| Hill village | 2 | 6 |

```
No specialisation (each village puts 5 workers on each task):
  Coast: 5 x 8 = 40 fish,  5 x 2 = 10 baskets
  Hill:  5 x 2 = 10 fish,  5 x 6 = 30 baskets
  Total: 50 fish, 40 baskets
Specialisation (Coast fishes, Hill weaves):
  Coast: 10 x 8 = 80 fish
  Hill:  10 x 6 = 60 baskets
  Total: 80 fish, 60 baskets
```

Total output of both goods rises. But Coast now has no baskets and Hill no fish, so they **must exchange**. That is why the specification says specialisation "naturally leads to exchange". Exchange is usually made easier by using money rather than swapping goods directly.

### Countries that specialise in a narrow range

Benefits: a country can produce large quantities of what it is best at, earn export revenue, and gain lower costs from producing on a large scale. Costs: dependence on a few products leaves it exposed to falls in world demand or price, to bad weather or disease, and to resources running out.

### Worked example 5: a tourism island

Island S earns 2,000 million a year from exports, 70% of it from tourism. A global downturn cuts tourist spending by 30%.

```
Tourism earnings = 0.70 x 2,000 = 1,400; fall = 0.30 x 1,400 = 420
New total = 2,000 - 420 = 1,580; fall in total = 420 / 2,000 x 100 = 21%
```

Evaluation: the island may still be right to specialise if it has natural advantages, such as beaches, that other industries could not use as well; it could reduce the risk by using tourism earnings to develop fishing or financial services.

## Exam technique

- **Knowledge (AO1)**: precise definitions, such as the specification's own wording for a market.
- **Application (AO2)**: name the market, the sector or the worker in the case.
- **Analysis and evaluation (AO3)**: chains (price rises → profit rises → resources move), then judgement on time taken, who gains and who loses.

## Common mistakes

- Describing a market only as a physical place.
- Reversing buyers and sellers in factor markets: firms buy, households sell.
- Confusing a fall in a sector's share with a fall in its output.
- Placing a restaurant or a shop in the secondary sector.
- Listing benefits of specialisation without explaining why it creates the need to exchange.
