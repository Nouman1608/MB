Analysis turns raw measurements into evidence. For OCR A Level Biology A (H420) section 1.1.3 you must be able to process, analyse and interpret qualitative and quantitative results, reaching valid conclusions where appropriate. You must use suitable mathematical skills on quantitative data, use significant figures appropriately, and plot and interpret graphs, including choosing and labelling axes and measuring gradients and intercepts. Most of the marks for data questions are earned by showing a correct method and then saying what the answer means in biological terms.

This guide covers learning outcomes 1.1.3(a) to (d) of the OCR A Level Biology A specification, Version 4.1 (April 2026), first assessment 2025, in Module 1, Development of practical skills in biology. It goes into more detail than the module-wide [planning, implementing, analysing and evaluating study guide](/resources/ocr-a-level-biology-planning-implementing-analysing-and-evaluating/). Use the [analysis revision notes](/resources/ocr-a-level-biology-h420-analysis-ocr-alevel-biology-revision-notes/) for quick recall and the [analysis practice questions](/resources/ocr-a-level-biology-h420-analysis-ocr-alevel-biology-practice/) to test yourself.

## Processing quantitative results (a and b)

**Step 1: check the raw data.** Look for values that do not fit the pattern. Decide whether a result is anomalous before you calculate a mean, and say which result you left out and why.

**Step 2: calculate a mean** for each value of the independent variable: add the repeats and divide by their number.

**Step 3: convert to a rate** if the measurement was taken over a period of time. Rate = change in quantity ÷ time.

**Step 4: describe the spread.** The range (largest minus smallest) is simple. The standard deviation is a better measure when you have enough values, and it is less affected by one outlying result. The formula is provided in the assessment where needed.

> s = √[ Σ(x − x̄)² ÷ (n − 1) ]

**Worked example 1.** A student collects oxygen from a catalase reaction at 30 °C for 2 minutes. Three repeats give 8.0, 8.5 and 9.0 cm³.

- Mean = (8.0 + 8.5 + 9.0) ÷ 3 = **8.5 cm³**.
- Rate = 8.5 ÷ 2.0 = **4.25 cm³ min⁻¹**.
- Deviations from the mean: −0.5, 0, +0.5. Squares: 0.25, 0, 0.25. Sum = 0.50. Divide by n − 1 = 2 to get 0.25. Square root: **s = 0.50 cm³**.

The value of s shows the typical spread of a repeat around the mean. A mean of 8.5 with s = 0.50 tells you more than a mean on its own.

**Percentage change** = (new − original) ÷ original × 100. A fall from 3.10 g to 2.79 g is −10.0 %.

**Ratios.** Surface area : volume and phenotypic ratios are examples. A ratio is simplified by dividing both parts by the smaller one.

## Significant figures (c)

A result cannot be more precise than the least precise measurement used to produce it. Report a calculated value to the **same number of significant figures as the least precise piece of data**, or one more.

- 17.6 ÷ 3.0 = 5.8667... The 3.0 has two significant figures, so give **5.9**.
- A mass of 2.40 g (three significant figures) divided by a volume of 15 cm³ (two significant figures) gives 0.16 g cm⁻³ (two significant figures), not 0.1600.
- Do not round in the middle of a calculation. Round once, at the end.
- Zeros: in 0.0050 there are two significant figures (5 and the final 0). In 2.40 there are three.

## Statistical tests (b)

The specification lists three tests you must be able to select and use. Formulae and critical-value tables are provided in the assessment where needed. You must know **which test to use** and **what the result means**.

| Test | Use it when | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Chi-squared (χ²) | Comparing **observed** counts with **expected** counts in categories | Phenotype ratios in a genetic cross |
| Student's t-test | Comparing the **means** of two sets of continuous data, to see if the difference is significant | Mean leaf length in two habitats |
| Spearman's rank correlation | Testing for **association** between two variables that are measured on a scale | Number of species against canopy cover |

**The null hypothesis** says there is no difference (or no correlation) and that any apparent difference is due to chance. If the calculated value is **greater than or equal to** the critical value at p = 0.05, you reject the null hypothesis; the result is significant at the 5 % level. If it is less, you do not reject it.

**Worked example 2 (chi-squared).** A cross is expected to give a 3 : 1 ratio. Of 80 offspring, 62 show the dominant trait and 18 the recessive. Expected values: 60 and 20.

χ² = Σ (O − E)² ÷ E = (62 − 60)² ÷ 60 + (18 − 20)² ÷ 20 = 0.067 + 0.20 = **0.27**.

There are two categories, so the degrees of freedom are 2 − 1 = 1. The critical value at p = 0.05 for 1 degree of freedom is 3.84. As 0.27 is less than 3.84, the difference between the observed and expected numbers is not significant. The results fit the 3 : 1 ratio.

**Worked example 3 (Spearman's rank).** At six sites, the percentage canopy cover and the number of ground plant species are:

| Cover / % | 10 | 25 | 40 | 55 | 70 | 85 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Species | 14 | 12 | 13 | 8 | 7 | 3 |

Rank each variable from lowest to highest. The cover ranks are 1 to 6. The species ranks are 6, 4, 5, 3, 2 and 1. The differences d are −5, −2, −2, 1, 3 and 5, and d² are 25, 4, 4, 1, 9 and 25. Σd² = 68.

rₛ = 1 − 6Σd² ÷ n(n² − 1) = 1 − (6 × 68) ÷ (6 × 35) = 1 − 408 ÷ 210 = **−0.94**.

This is a strong negative correlation. For n = 6 the critical value at p = 0.05 is 0.886, so the correlation is significant (the sign is ignored when comparing). The conclusion is that species number tends to fall as canopy cover rises. It does not prove that shade is the cause; other factors, such as leaf litter or soil moisture, could also change with cover.

**Worked example 4 (t-test).** The lengths of leaves in two habitats have these means and standard deviations (five leaves each): shade, 12.14 mm, s = 0.32; sun, 13.16 mm, s = 0.29.

t = (13.16 − 12.14) ÷ √(0.32² ÷ 5 + 0.29² ÷ 5) = 1.02 ÷ 0.193 = **5.3** (to 2 s.f.).

There are (5 + 5) − 2 = 8 degrees of freedom. The critical value at p = 0.05 for 8 degrees of freedom is 2.31. As 5.3 is greater than 2.31, the difference is significant. The two means differ, and chance alone is unlikely to explain it.

## Graphs (d)

**Choosing the graph.**

- **Line graph (with or without a line of best fit):** both variables are continuous, such as rate against temperature.
- **Bar chart:** the independent variable is categorical, such as habitat type.
- **Scatter graph:** a test of association between two measured variables, as with a Spearman's test.
- **Histogram:** the frequency of values in continuous classes.

**Rules for plotting**

1. Independent variable on the **x-axis**, dependent variable on the **y-axis**.
2. Label each axis with the **quantity and the unit**, such as "Temperature / °C".
3. Choose a **linear scale** that uses more than half of the grid in each direction, with sensible steps such as 1, 2, 5 or 10. Do not use awkward steps such as 3 or 7.
4. Plot points accurately with small crosses or dots.
5. For a clear trend, draw a **line of best fit**: a smooth line or straight line through the points, with roughly equal points above and below. If the pattern between points is not known, join the points with straight lines using a ruler.

**Gradient and intercept.** For a straight-line graph, the gradient is the change in y divided by the change in x, with the units of y over the units of x.

**Worked example 5.** On a graph of oxygen volume against time, the line of best fit passes through (30 s, 4.5 cm³) and (150 s, 22.5 cm³).

Gradient = (22.5 − 4.5) ÷ (150 − 30) = 18.0 ÷ 120 = **0.15 cm³ s⁻¹**, which is the rate (or 9.0 cm³ min⁻¹). Use two points on the line that are far apart, not two data points.

The **intercept** is where the line crosses the axis. An intercept of zero for volume at zero time shows that no gas was present at the start.

**Rate from a curve.** If the graph is a curve, such as the volume of product against time for an enzyme-catalysed reaction, the rate at a given time is the gradient of the **tangent** to the curve at that point. If a tangent at t = 0 passes through (0, 0) and (20 s, 16 cm³), the **initial rate** is 16 ÷ 20 = **0.80 cm³ s⁻¹**.

## Reaching valid conclusions (a)

A conclusion must be supported by the data. A good conclusion:

1. states the **relationship** shown by the data (as the independent variable increases, the dependent variable...),
2. **quotes values** from the data,
3. is expressed as "supports" or "does not support" rather than "proves",
4. **explains** the result using biological knowledge, and
5. says whether any **statistical test** shows the result to be significant.

**Correlation is not causation.** A correlation shows that two variables change together. It does not show that one causes the other.

## Exam technique

- **Show your working.** A correct method with an arithmetic slip still earns method marks.
- **Give the unit** and an appropriate number of significant figures.
- **Say what the number means** after you calculate it.
- **State the null hypothesis** in a statistics question, and compare the calculated value with the critical value.
- **Use the whole grid** and label the axes when plotting.

## Common mistakes

- Using the data points, rather than the line of best fit, to find a gradient.
- Putting the independent variable on the y-axis.
- Taking a mean that includes an anomalous result without comment.
- Quoting a result to many more significant figures than the data support.
- Saying a significant correlation proves one variable causes the other.
- Dividing by n instead of n − 1 for a standard deviation.
- Forgetting the units of a gradient.
