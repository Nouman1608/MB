This guide covers **Paper 2 – Prose and Unseen**, an AS Level paper for
Cambridge International A Level English Literature 9695, 2024–2026
series. Literature syllabuses are organised around named papers and
set texts rather than a numbered topic taxonomy, and the set-text list
rotates by examination year — this guide covers the paper's stable
structure rather than any specific text, since candidates should
always check the current set-text grid for their own exam series.

## Where this fits in 9695

AS Level candidates take Paper 1 (Drama and Poetry) and Paper 2
(Prose and Unseen) — together these two papers make up the standalone
AS Level award. A Level candidates additionally take Paper 3
(Shakespeare and Drama) and Paper 4 (Pre- and Post-1900 Poetry and
Prose). Paper 2 is distinctive within the AS award because, unlike
Paper 1, one of its two sections is not based on a set text at all —
Section B assesses unseen prose, testing a different skill from
detailed knowledge of a studied novel.

## Syllabus coverage

**CAMBRIDGE INTERNATIONAL A LEVEL ENGLISH LITERATURE 9695 — PAPER 2
PROSE AND UNSEEN**

- 2.1 Section A: Prose — answering on a set prose text, testing
knowledge of narrative technique, character and theme
- 2.2 Section B: Unseen — responding to an unseen prose passage,
testing close reading and analysis without prior knowledge of the
source text

## How to approach it

Because Paper 2 combines a studied text (Section A) with a completely
unfamiliar one (Section B), preparation for the two sections looks
quite different and both need dedicated practice time. For Section A,
building genuine familiarity with the set prose text — its structure,
its narrative voice, how character and theme develop across the whole
novel rather than in isolated extracts — pays off directly, since
strong answers draw on the text as a whole rather than treating it as
a series of disconnected moments. Keeping track of how a theme or
character develops chronologically, rather than only revising
memorable individual scenes, tends to produce answers with a stronger
overall argument.

Section B tests a genuinely different skill: responding to an unseen
extract under timed conditions with no prior knowledge of the source
novel. The priority here is close, line-level reading — noticing how
narrative voice, structure, and specific word choices shape a reader's
impression of character or event within the extract itself, rather
than speculating about plot or context beyond what the passage
actually gives. Because there is no set text to fall back on, practice
should focus on building a repeatable method: a first read for overall
sense and tone, a second read annotating specific language choices and
their effects, then planning a response structured around a small
number of well-developed points rather than a long list of surface
observations.

A common weakness across both sections is describing what a text does
—"the writer uses long sentences"— without explaining the effect that
technique has on a reader, or with the theme, character or mood being
discussed. Strong answers on this paper consistently pair an
observation about technique with a specific, textually-grounded
explanation of its effect, in both the studied Section A response and
the unseen Section B response. Because Section B carries no benefit
from memorised quotations or pre-prepared arguments, timed practice
responding to genuinely new extracts — not just reviewing extracts
already seen in class — is the most reliable preparation available for
that section specifically.

Time management across the paper also deserves deliberate practice:
since Section A rewards depth of textual knowledge built up over the
whole course, while Section B rewards a fast, reliable close-reading
method applied to fresh material, rehearsing both sections together
under exam timing (not just revising each separately) helps candidates
judge realistically how long each response actually needs, rather than
running out of time on the section they find more comfortable at the
expense of the other.

## Worked example: annotating an unseen extract

Consider an unseen extract opening with short, clipped sentences
describing a character walking through an empty house. A weak response
might note "the writer uses short sentences to create tension." A
stronger response identifies the specific effect: the short,
fragmented sentences slow the pace of the description, mirroring the
character's own hesitant movement through unfamiliar space, and the
absence of any other sound described in the passage isolates the
reader's attention on each small noise the character notices,
building a quiet unease before anything is stated directly. The
difference between the two responses is not extra knowledge — it is
the habit of asking, after noting any technique, "what does this
actually do to the reader's impression, here, in this specific
passage" rather than stopping at naming the device.

## Quick revision checklist

- Track how your Section A text's themes and characters develop
  across the whole novel, not only in a handful of favourite scenes.
- For Section B, rehearse a repeatable close-reading method: read for
  overall sense, annotate language and structure, then plan before
  writing.
- Always pair an observation about technique with its specific effect
  on the reader — never leave a device named but unexplained.
- Practise both sections together under full exam timing so pacing
  reflects the real paper, not each section revised in isolation.
- For Section B specifically, resist the temptation to guess at
  context beyond the passage — analyse what is actually on the page.
