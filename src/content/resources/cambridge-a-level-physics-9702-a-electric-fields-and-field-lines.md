This guide covers subtopic **18.1 Electric fields and field lines** of Cambridge International AS & A Level Physics 9702, 2025–2027 series (A Level). The broader [Electric Fields study guide](/resources/a-physics-electric-fields/) introduces the whole of Topic 18; this page goes further into the foundations: what "field of force" means, why the definition of field strength is worded as it is, how to use F = qE with signs and directions, and how to draw field-line patterns that earn the marks. For quick recall use the [Electric fields and field lines revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-physics-9702-a-electric-fields-and-field-lines-revision-notes/), and for exam-style questions the [Electric fields and field lines practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-physics-9702-a-electric-fields-and-field-lines-practice/).

## What 18.1 asks you to do

Candidates should be able to:

- understand that an electric field is an example of a **field of force** and **define electric field as force per unit positive charge**
- **recall and use F = qE** for the force on a charge in an electric field
- **represent an electric field by means of field lines**

Everything else in Topic 18 (uniform fields, Coulomb's law, point-charge fields and potential) builds on these three ideas.

## A field of force

A **field of force** is a region of space in which an object with a particular property experiences a force without contact. In a gravitational field (Topic 13) the property is mass; in an electric field it is **charge**. Any charged object creates an electric field around itself, and any other charged object placed in that field experiences a force.

The field is a property of each point in space, whether or not a charge is placed there. This lets you separate two questions: "what field does this arrangement of charges produce?" and "what force does a given charge feel in that field?"

## Defining electric field strength

> The electric field strength at a point is the **force per unit positive charge** acting on a small stationary charge placed at that point.

```
E = F / q        unit: N C⁻¹
```

Each part of the wording has a reason:

- **Per unit charge** makes E independent of the charge used to measure it. Doubling the test charge doubles the force, but F/q stays the same.
- **Positive** fixes the direction. Electric field strength is a **vector**, and its direction is defined as the direction of the force on a positive charge.
- **Small** test charge: a large charge would push or pull on the charges producing the field and rearrange them, changing the very field being measured.

## Using F = qE

Rearranging the definition gives the force on any charge q in a field E:

```
F = qE
```

- For a **positive** charge, the force is **in the direction** of the field.
- For a **negative** charge, such as an electron, the force is **opposite** to the field.
- This applies in any electric field: uniform or not, the E used is the field strength at the charge's position.

A good habit is to calculate the magnitude with positive numbers, then decide the direction from the sign of the charge, rather than carrying a negative sign through the arithmetic.

## Representing fields with field lines

Field lines are a map of the field. The rules:

1. Lines show the **direction of the force on a positive charge**; mark each line with an arrow.
2. Lines **start on positive charges and end on negative charges** (or continue to infinity).
3. Lines **never cross**: at any point the field has only one direction.
4. The **closer together** the lines, the **stronger** the field.
5. Lines meet the surface of a conductor **at right angles**. If the field had a component along the surface, the free charges in the conductor would move until that component vanished.

### Patterns you should be able to draw

| Arrangement | Pattern |
|---|---|
| Isolated positive point charge (or charged sphere) | Straight radial lines pointing **outwards**, spreading apart with distance |
| Isolated negative point charge | Radial lines pointing **inwards** |
| Equal and opposite charges | Lines leave the positive charge, curve round and end on the negative; densest between the charges |
| Two equal positive charges | Lines repel and bend away from each other; a **neutral point** midway where the field is zero, with no lines passing through it |
| Oppositely charged parallel plates | Evenly spaced, parallel lines from + plate to − plate between the plates (uniform field), bowing outwards at the edges |
| Point charge near an oppositely charged flat plate | Radial near the charge, curving to meet the plate at right angles |

The spreading of radial lines shows the field getting weaker with distance; the even spacing between plates shows a constant field strength. Subtopics 18.2 and 18.4 put numbers to each.

## Combining fields

Because E is a vector, the field at a point due to several charges is the **vector sum** of the separate fields. In a line, add with signs; at right angles, use Pythagoras. A **neutral point** is where the separate fields cancel exactly.

## Worked example 1: comparing electric and gravitational forces on an electron

An electron is in a uniform field of strength 3.5 × 10⁴ N C⁻¹. Calculate the electric force on it and its acceleration, and compare the electric force with its weight.

```
F = qE = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 3.5 × 10⁴ = 5.6 × 10⁻¹⁵ N   (opposite to E)
a = F/m = 5.6 × 10⁻¹⁵ / 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ = 6.1 × 10¹⁵ m s⁻²
weight = mg = 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ × 9.81 = 8.9 × 10⁻³⁰ N
F / weight ≈ 6 × 10¹⁴
```

*Annotation.* The weight is smaller by a factor of about 10¹⁴, which is why questions about electrons and ions in electric fields ignore gravity. State this assumption when you make it.

## Worked example 2: a charged oil drop held stationary

A tiny oil drop of mass 2.0 × 10⁻¹⁵ kg carries a charge of +3e. Calculate the field strength needed to hold it stationary, and state the direction of the field.

```
for equilibrium: qE = mg
E = mg / q = (2.0 × 10⁻¹⁵ × 9.81) / (3 × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹) = 4.1 × 10⁴ N C⁻¹
```

The electric force must act upwards to balance the weight. The charge is positive, so the force is in the field direction: the **field points upwards**.

*Annotation.* Here gravity is not negligible, because the drop is around 10¹⁵ times heavier than an electron. If the drop had a negative charge, the field would have to point downwards.

## Worked example 3: proton and alpha particle in the same field

A proton and an alpha particle are placed in the same uniform field. The alpha particle has twice the charge and about four times the mass of the proton. Compare the forces and accelerations.

*Answer.* F = qE, so the force on the alpha particle is **twice** the force on the proton. Acceleration = qE/m, so the alpha particle's acceleration is 2/4 = **half** that of the proton. Both forces are in the direction of the field, because both particles are positive.

## Common mistakes

- **Leaving out "positive"** in the definition of E: the direction of the field depends on it.
- **Drawing field lines without arrows**, or with arrows pointing into a positive charge.
- **Drawing lines that cross** or that start and end in empty space.
- **Spacing radial lines evenly** at all distances: they must spread out.
- **Forgetting the force on an electron is opposite to the field.**
- **Drawing lines meeting a conductor at an angle** other than 90°.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Define** (electric field strength): "force per unit positive charge (acting on a small stationary charge)". Give E = F/q only as a support.
- **State** (the direction of a field): the direction of the force on a positive charge.
- **Draw** or **sketch** field lines: at least four or five lines per charge, arrows on every line, correct spacing, right angles at conductors, symmetry where the arrangement is symmetric.
- **Calculate**: use F = qE, then a = F/m if motion is asked for; state the direction separately.
- **Explain** (why gravity can be ignored): compare mg with qE numerically.
