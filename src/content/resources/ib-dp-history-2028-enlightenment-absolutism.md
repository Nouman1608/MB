**This page is for HL only.** Regional study 3, Enlightenment and Absolutism (1600 to 1800), is one of twelve studies in the Europe option, examined on HL Paper 3 (two essays from different regional studies). The guide states that questions about rulers will require you to refer to **one enlightened despot**. Suggested examples include Catherine the Great of Russia, Frederick the Great of Prussia, Charles III of Spain and Joseph II, Holy Roman Emperor. This guide uses **Catherine II of Russia** as the main case and mentions the others for comparison.

## Emergence of Enlightenment ideas

The Enlightenment was an intellectual movement, strongest in the eighteenth century, that put reason, evidence and criticism ahead of tradition and authority. It was not one doctrine, and its thinkers disagreed.

*Intellectual influences.* Ancient writers, such as Greek and Roman philosophers, gave models of rational inquiry. The Renaissance revived them and promoted humanism. The **Scientific Revolution** showed that natural laws could be discovered: Copernicus and Galileo challenged old ideas about the heavens, and Newton's *Principia* (1687) showed a single law explaining motion. Francis Bacon urged learning from experiment, while Descartes stressed reasoning. John Locke argued that knowledge comes from experience and that governments rest on consent and protect natural rights.

*Political conditions.* Wars of religion and civil conflict, such as the English Civil War and the Glorious Revolution of 1688, made people think hard about the limits of royal power. Strong states with growing bureaucracies and armies also needed ideas about how to organise rule, and some thinkers believed reform from above was the best way to improve society.

*Religious factors.* The **Reformation** encouraged individual reading of scripture and weakened the Church's monopoly over truth, while religious wars made many writers favour toleration. Some Enlightenment writers attacked the Church and superstition, such as Voltaire; others, such as Locke, wanted a reasonable Christianity. Do not describe the movement as simply anti-religious.

*Role of individuals.* **Montesquieu** wrote *The Spirit of the Laws* (1748), urging the separation of powers. **Voltaire** campaigned for toleration and justice. **Rousseau** wrote *The Social Contract* (1762), placing sovereignty in the people. **Diderot** edited the *Encyclopédie* (published 1751 to 1772). **Kant** answered the question "What is Enlightenment?" in 1784 with the call to "dare to know".

## Developments during the Enlightenment era

*Economic change.* Trade expanded with colonies and overseas markets. Early industrial change began in Britain, including improved steam engines in the 1760s and 1770s. New economic theories appeared, such as Adam Smith's *The Wealth of Nations* (1776), which favoured freer markets, and the Physiocrats in France, who saw land as the source of wealth.

*Social change.* Cities grew, with London approaching a million people by 1800. A reading public met in coffee houses, salons, clubs and learned societies. Literacy grew, particularly among the middle classes and in towns, but remained limited among peasants.

*Science and technology.* Academies and societies, such as the Royal Society of London, promoted experiment. Advances included work on electricity, chemistry and medicine, such as smallpox inoculation.

*Agriculture.* Improvements such as new crops, crop rotation, selective breeding and enclosure raised output in parts of western Europe. These changes helped feed larger populations but also pushed some small farmers off the land. Do not claim all of Europe changed at once; eastern Europe stayed heavily dependent on serf labour.

## Impact of Enlightenment ideas

*Political ideas.* Thinkers criticised absolute monarchy and divine right, and proposed limited government, rule of law and separation of powers. At the same time many believed a wise ruler could carry out reform, which produced the idea of the **enlightened despot**: a monarch who kept full authority but used it to promote reason, law, education and the general welfare.

*Social ideas.* Writers argued for equality before the law, toleration and individual rights. Beccaria's *On Crimes and Punishments* (1764) attacked torture and the death penalty. Some criticised slavery, though others accepted it.

*Economic ideas.* Physiocrats and Adam Smith challenged state control and monopolies. Rulers took what suited them, such as promoting trade and agriculture, but ignored what threatened their power.

*Women's rights.* Women were prominent as hosts of salons and as readers and writers. A few argued for equal education and rights, such as Mary Wollstonecraft's *A Vindication of the Rights of Woman* (1792) and Olympe de Gouges's declaration of 1791. Most thinkers, including Rousseau, still thought women's role belonged in the home, so the Enlightenment's impact here was limited.

## Maintenance of power by one enlightened despot: Catherine II of Russia

Catherine, born a German princess, took the throne in a coup against her husband Peter III in 1762 with the help of guards regiments, including the Orlov brothers. Her position as a foreign-born usurper made legitimacy a constant concern.

- *Use of ideas to legitimize rule.* She corresponded with Voltaire and Diderot, bought Diderot's library and drew on Montesquieu and Beccaria in her *Nakaz* (Instruction) of 1767. This showed her as a reasonable ruler, and brought praise from European writers.
- *Centralization of power.* The Provincial Reform of 1775 divided Russia into more provinces with more officials, extending central control. She secularized church lands in 1764, taking revenue for the state.
- *Use of force.* The **Pugachev revolt** (1773 to 1775), led by a Cossack claiming to be Peter III, shook her rule. After it was crushed, she depended more on the nobility and strengthened local government. Writers who criticized serfdom, such as Radishchev (1790), were punished.
- *Image and depiction.* Portraits, medals and the equestrian statue of Peter the Great (unveiled in 1782) linked her to Russia's greatness. She promoted herself as "Mother of the Fatherland" and patron of culture.

## Domestic impact: Catherine

*Political, administrative and legal reform.* The Legislative Commission of 1767 to 1768 gathered delegates from several social groups but achieved no new code and was dissolved, and the Nakaz stayed unenforced as law. The Charter to the Nobility (1785) confirmed noble privileges and freed nobles from compulsory service, so the reforms strengthened the noble class.

*Arts and education.* She built the Hermitage collection, supported theatre and writing, and founded the Smolny Institute (1764) for girls' education.

*Religion.* She secularized church lands, and tolerated Muslims, Jews and other faiths within limits.

*Economy, agriculture and environment.* She encouraged trade, settlement of southern lands and the growth of towns. Serfdom spread to new areas and became harsher in practice, so agricultural reform was limited. Historians often cite this as the most striking gap between her image and her actions.

## Foreign policy: Catherine

*Conflict and territory.* Wars with the Ottomans brought gains on the Black Sea, including the Treaty of Kuchuk Kainarji (1774) and the annexation of Crimea (1783). Russia took a large share of Poland in the partitions of 1772, 1793 and 1795, with Prussia and Austria.

*Trade and diplomacy.* She signed a commercial treaty with Britain in 1766, encouraged trade through new Black Sea ports and led the League of Armed Neutrality in 1780. In diplomacy she balanced Prussia and Austria.

*Colonial policy.* Russian traders pushed into Alaska and the North Pacific, and Catherine's government encouraged settlement in "New Russia" in the south.

## Comparing other despots

- **Frederick II of Prussia** (1740 to 1786) called himself the first servant of the state, promoted religious toleration and legal reform (a law code appeared after his death), but kept an army-based state and the power of the nobility.
- **Joseph II** (sole ruler 1780 to 1790) issued the Edict of Toleration (1781), abolished personal serfdom, and reformed church and law, but his rapid changes provoked revolts in Hungary and the Austrian Netherlands.
- **Charles III of Spain** (1759 to 1788) carried out administrative and economic reform and expelled the Jesuits in 1767, though the Esquilache riots of 1766 showed popular opposition.

## Interpretations to use

Historians disagree about whether "enlightened despotism" is a useful label. Some argue rulers used Enlightenment ideas as decoration for state-building and war. Others say the ideas shaped real reforms, especially in law and religion. A strong essay judges how far the ruler was driven by principle and how far by power and need.

## Frequently asked questions

**Must I use Catherine?**
No. Choose one despot and use only that ruler, as the questions require.

**Was the Enlightenment unified?**
No. Writers disagreed about religion, government and economy.

**Is "enlightened despot" a contradiction?**
It can look like one. The best essays examine the tension between reform and absolute power.
