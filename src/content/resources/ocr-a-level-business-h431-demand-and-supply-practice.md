Revise first with the [demand and supply study guide](/resources/ocr-a-level-business-h431-demand-and-supply/) and the [demand and supply revision notes](/resources/ocr-a-level-business-h431-demand-and-supply-revision-notes/).

## Section A: short questions

**1.** Define equilibrium price. **[2]**

**2.** Distinguish between a contraction of demand and a decrease in demand. **[2]**

**3.** State **two** determinants of supply, other than the product's own price. **[2]**

**4.** Larkspur Motors sells petrol cars. The price of electric cars falls sharply. Explain the likely effect on demand for Larkspur's cars. **[3]**

**5.** Fernhollow Studios makes video games for one brand of console. The console's manufacturer cuts its price. Explain the likely effect on demand for Fernhollow's games. **[3]**

**6.** Explain why a shortage puts upward pressure on the market price. **[3]**

## Section B: structured questions

**7.** Bramblegate Bakery supplies trays of vegan pastries to cafés. The weekly market is described by QD = 2,000 − 50P and QS = 150P − 400, where P is the price per tray in pounds.

**(a)** Calculate the equilibrium price and quantity. **[2]**

**(b)** The bakeries' trade association recommends a price of £10. Calculate the size of the resulting shortage. **[2]**

**(c)** A rise in the price of plant-based butter reduces supply by 200 trays at every price. Calculate the new equilibrium. **[3]**

**(d)** Describe how you would draw a diagram showing the change in part (c). **[3]**

**8.** The weekly market for bottles of Haldane Orchards' pressed apple juice is QD = 1,000 − 20P and QS = 30P − 250 (P in pence per bottle, quantities in hundreds of bottles). A rival brand launches a cheaper juice, reducing demand by 100 at every price. At the same time, a bumper harvest increases supply by 50 at every price.

**(a)** Calculate the original equilibrium price and quantity. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the new equilibrium price and quantity. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain why the price was certain to fall but the change in quantity was not. **[3]**

**(d)** Calculate the percentage change in total spending in this market. **[2]**

**9.** Kestle Bakeries faces a 25% rise in the price of flour, its main ingredient. It sells bread to supermarkets, which can buy from four other large bakeries. Analyse **two** price and output decisions Kestle might take in response. **[6]**

**10.** Pinemarsh Fruit grows strawberries. An unusually warm spring has brought the whole crop forward, so every grower is harvesting at once and the market has excess supply. Evaluate the effect of this excess supply on Pinemarsh. **[8]**

**11.** Northcrag Outdoor sells ski clothing through shops in three UK cities and online. Evaluate which factor is likely to have the greatest effect on demand for its products over the next five years. **[9]**

**12.** Tallis Audio's new wireless headphones sold out within a week of launch, and it cannot raise output for three months. Evaluate whether this shortage is good or bad for the business. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** The price at which quantity demanded equals quantity supplied [1], so the market clears and there is no pressure for the price to change [1]. **[2]**

**2.** A contraction of demand is a fall in quantity demanded caused by a rise in the product's own price, a movement along the curve [1]. A decrease in demand is a fall at every price caused by another determinant, such as lower incomes, which shifts the curve left [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Any two from: costs of production [1]; technology [1]; number of sellers; indirect taxes or subsidies; weather; expected future profit. **[2]**

**4.** Electric cars are substitutes for petrol cars [1]. When they become cheaper, some buyers switch to them [1], so demand for Larkspur's cars decreases and the curve shifts left [1]. **[3]**

**5.** Consoles and games are complements [1]. A cheaper console means more consoles are bought [1], so more owners want games and demand for Fernhollow's games increases (shifts right) [1]. **[3]**

**6.** At a price below equilibrium, buyers want more than sellers offer [1]. Some buyers are willing to pay more, so sellers can raise price without losing sales [1]. As price rises, quantity demanded contracts and quantity supplied extends until the gap closes at equilibrium [1]. **[3]**

**7. (a)** 2,000 − 50P = 150P − 400, so 2,400 = 200P [1]; P = **£12**, Q = **1,400 trays** [1].

**(b)** At £10: QD = 1,500 and QS = 1,100 [1]; shortage = **400 trays** [1].

**(c)** New supply QS = 150P − 600 [1]; 2,000 − 50P = 150P − 600, so 2,600 = 200P [1]; P = **£13**, Q = **1,350 trays** [1].

**(d)** Price on the vertical axis and quantity on the horizontal, with D and S crossing at £12 and 1,400 [1]. Draw S1 to the left of S, parallel to it [1]. Mark the new equilibrium at £13 and 1,350, with dashed lines to both axes, and label the rise in price and fall in quantity [1]. **[10]**

*Examiner insight:* Write the new supply equation before solving; it earns a method mark even if the arithmetic slips.

**8. (a)** 1,000 − 20P = 30P − 250, so 1,250 = 50P [1]; P = **25p**, Q = **500** hundred bottles [1].

**(b)** QD = 900 − 20P and QS = 30P − 200; 1,100 = 50P [1]; P = **22p**, Q = **460** hundred bottles [1].

**(c)** A decrease in demand pushes price down, and an increase in supply also pushes price down, so price must fall [1]. The demand fall reduces quantity while the supply rise increases it [1], so the change in quantity depends on which shift is larger; here the demand effect dominates and quantity falls [1].

**(d)** Original spending = 25 × 50,000 = £12,500; new = 22 × 46,000 = £10,120 [1]; change = −2,380 ÷ 12,500 × 100 = **−19.0%** [1]. **[9]**

*Examiner insight:* Watch the units: prices in pence and quantities in hundreds must be converted before you find spending in pounds.

**9.** Decision 1: raise price to protect margin [1]. Because the supermarkets can buy from four rivals, they may switch if Kestle alone raises price [1], so this works best if the rivals face the same flour cost and also raise prices [1]. Decision 2: hold price and accept a lower margin [1], keeping its contracts and volume of output [1], but if the cost rise lasts, profit may fall below an acceptable level, so Kestle may need to cut costs elsewhere or reduce output of the least profitable lines [1]. **[6]**

**10.** Excess supply pushes the market price down [1], so Pinemarsh's revenue per punnet falls [1]. Strawberries are perishable, so fruit not sold quickly is wasted [1], adding to losses because picking costs have already been paid [1]. However, lower prices may raise quantity bought, so total sales volume could hold up [1]. Pinemarsh may reduce the effect by freezing fruit or selling to jam makers [1]. The effect is short-lived, since the glut ends when the early crop is picked [1]. Judgement: a serious but temporary hit to profit; how serious depends on whether Pinemarsh has storage or processing outlets [1]. **[8]**

**11.** Possible factors: incomes [1], since ski holidays and clothing are luxuries bought less when incomes fall [1]; tastes and trends [1], such as whether skiing stays fashionable or outdoor clothing becomes everyday wear [1]; weather and climate [1], since poor snow seasons reduce trips and demand for kit [1]; prices of substitutes such as cheaper online sellers [1]. Judgement: incomes are likely to matter most over five years because a downturn could cut spending on luxury trips across all its markets, whereas Northcrag can partly influence tastes through its own promotion [1]; but if snow seasons keep shortening, climate could become the deciding factor [1]. **[9]**

**12.** Good: the sell-out signals strong demand and may create publicity that builds the brand [1], and Tallis could raise price to increase margin while stock is scarce [1]. Bad: every unmet order is a lost sale [1], and frustrated customers may buy rival headphones and not return [1]. Three months is long enough for rivals to fill the gap [1], and a price rise could look like exploitation and harm reputation [1]. Judgement: good in the short run [1], but it turns bad if the shortage lasts the full three months, so Tallis should take pre-orders to keep customers committed [1]. **[8]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "demand fell" when price rose: that is a contraction.
- Treating complements as substitutes, or the reverse.
- Forgetting to rewrite the shifted equation before solving.
- Ignoring units, such as pence or hundreds of items.
- Stating with certainty how both price and quantity change in a double shift.
- Evaluating without a judgement that names the deciding condition.
