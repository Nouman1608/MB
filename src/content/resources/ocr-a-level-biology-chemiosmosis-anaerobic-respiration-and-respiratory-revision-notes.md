## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Chemiosmosis | Synthesis of ATP driven by the flow of protons down an electrochemical gradient through ATP synthase |
| Electron transport chain | Carriers in a membrane that pass electrons down energy levels; energy released pumps H⁺ |
| ATP synthase | Enzyme and channel in the membrane; H⁺ flow through it joins ADP and inorganic phosphate |
| Oxidative phosphorylation | ATP made using electrons from reduced NAD and FAD, with oxygen as final acceptor |
| Photophosphorylation | ATP made using the electron transport chain driven by light energy |
| Fermentation step | Reaction that turns reduced NAD back into NAD so glycolysis can continue |
| Respiratory quotient (RQ) | CO₂ produced ÷ O₂ consumed |

## 5.2.2(h) Chemiosmosis in steps

Oxidative phosphorylation (inner mitochondrial membrane, cristae):

1. Reduced NAD and reduced FAD donate electrons to the electron transport chain.
2. Electrons move down the carriers. Released energy pumps H⁺ from the matrix to the intermembrane space.
3. An electrochemical gradient forms: more H⁺ in the intermembrane space.
4. H⁺ flows back to the matrix through ATP synthase. ADP + Pi → ATP.
5. Oxygen accepts electrons and H⁺ at the end of the chain, forming water.

Photophosphorylation (thylakoid membrane): light excites electrons in chlorophyll, the chain pumps H⁺ from the stroma into the thylakoid space, and H⁺ flows back to the stroma through ATP synthase.

**Must-know distinctions**

| | Mitochondrion | Chloroplast |
|---|---|---|
| H⁺ pumped into | Intermembrane space | Thylakoid space |
| Electrons come from | Reduced NAD and FAD | Chlorophyll excited by light |
| Final acceptor | Oxygen | NADP (non-cyclic) |
| ATP made in | Matrix | Stroma |

**If oxygen is absent:** electrons cannot leave the chain, reduced NAD is not reoxidised, the gradient is not built, and ATP synthesis by chemiosmosis stops.

**Worked reminder.** A pH difference of 1.0 across a membrane is a ten-fold difference in H⁺ concentration (10¹ = 10), and 0.5 is about 3.2-fold (10^0.5).

## 5.2.2(i) Anaerobic respiration

| | Mammals | Yeast |
|---|---|---|
| Starting point | Pyruvate from glycolysis | Pyruvate from glycolysis |
| Steps | Pyruvate + reduced NAD → lactate + NAD | Pyruvate → ethanal + CO₂, then ethanal + reduced NAD → ethanol + NAD |
| Enzyme (first step) | Lactate dehydrogenase | Pyruvate decarboxylase |
| Gas released | None | CO₂ |
| Reversible? | Lactate can be converted back to pyruvate (in the liver) | Ethanol formation is not reversible |
| Net ATP | 2 (glycolysis only) | 2 (glycolysis only) |

**Why the NAD step matters:** glycolysis uses NAD. Without a way to regenerate it, glycolysis stops and ATP production stops.

**Benefits:** ATP can be produced quickly with no oxygen, such as in muscle during sprinting; yeast and some other organisms can live in low oxygen conditions.

**Why the yield is lower:**
- Pyruvate is not oxidised any further, so the link reaction and Krebs cycle do not happen.
- Reduced NAD is used to make lactate or ethanol, so it does not supply the electron transport chain.
- Energy remains in lactate or ethanol.
- Only substrate-level phosphorylation in glycolysis makes ATP: 2 per glucose, compared with about 30 aerobically.

**Yeast practical.** Oil on the surface excludes oxygen. Rate can be measured from gas volume, bubble count or colour change of a redox indicator. Control: same yeast mass, temperature and glucose concentration, with boiled yeast as a negative control.

## 5.2.2(j) Energy values

| Substrate | Approx. energy / kJ g⁻¹ | Route in |
|---|---|---|
| Carbohydrate | 17 | Glycolysis |
| Lipid | 37 | Glycerol to triose phosphate; fatty acids to acetyl CoA |
| Protein | 17 | Deamination, then pyruvate, acetyl CoA or Krebs cycle intermediate |

Lipid gives more energy per gram because it has more hydrogen per molecule, so more reduced NAD and FAD are made and more H⁺ is pumped.

## 5.2.2(k) Respiratory quotient

RQ = CO₂ produced ÷ O₂ consumed. No units.

| RQ | Suggests |
|---|---|
| About 1.0 | Carbohydrate |
| About 0.9 | Protein |
| About 0.7 | Lipid |
| Above 1.0 | Some anaerobic respiration, or an organic acid substrate |

**Method in steps for an equation**
1. Write the balanced equation.
2. Take moles of CO₂ made and moles of O₂ used.
3. Divide CO₂ by O₂.

**Worked reminder.** Glucose: C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O. RQ = 6 ÷ 6 = 1.0.

## 5.2.2(l) Respirometer practicals

**Method in steps**
1. Put the organisms on gauze above soda lime or potassium hydroxide solution, which absorbs CO₂.
2. Connect a capillary tube with a drop of coloured fluid. Use a control tube with beads or dead organisms.
3. Equilibrate in a water bath, seal, and record the drop's position at set times.
4. Repeat and calculate a mean.

**Calculations**
- Volume of O₂ used = π r² × distance moved by the drop (r is the capillary radius, not the diameter).
- Rate = volume ÷ time. Divide by mass for rate per gram.
- RQ = (movement with absorber − movement without absorber) ÷ movement with absorber.

**Worked reminder.** The drop moves 25 mm in 5 min in a capillary of radius 0.4 mm. Volume = π × 0.4² × 25 = 12.6 mm³. Rate = 12.6 ÷ 5 = 2.5 mm³ min⁻¹.

**Factors:** temperature (rate rises then falls as enzymes denature), substrate concentration (rate rises then levels off), type of substrate (compare using RQ).

## Explaining inhibitors and changes

Questions often change one thing and ask for the effect. Work through the chain in order.

| Change | Effect on proton gradient | Effect on ATP and oxygen use |
|---|---|---|
| Electron transport chain blocked | Gradient falls as no more H⁺ is pumped | Less ATP; less oxygen used; reduced NAD builds up |
| ATP synthase blocked | H⁺ cannot flow back, so the gradient rises and slows pumping | Less ATP; less oxygen used |
| Membrane made leaky to H⁺ | Gradient cannot be kept | Oxygen use continues but little ATP is made; more energy is lost as heat |
| No oxygen | Chain backs up, so no pumping | Aerobic ATP stops; fermentation takes over |

**Reading a rate from a graph.** Rate is the gradient. Take two points far apart on a straight section, and divide the change in volume by the change in time. For a curve, draw a tangent at the time asked. Data supplied: if gas collected rises from 4.2 cm³ to 10.2 cm³ over 6 min on a straight section, rate = 6.0 ÷ 6 = 1.0 cm³ min⁻¹. Give units every time.

**Explain-question frame.** Name the structure or stage, say what happens there, and then say what the result is for ATP. For example: "Oxygen is the final acceptor, so electrons keep flowing, so H⁺ keeps being pumped, so ATP synthase keeps making ATP."

## Quick self-test

1. In which part of a mitochondrion is ATP synthase found?
2. What is the final electron acceptor in oxidative phosphorylation, and what is formed?
3. Which side of the inner mitochondrial membrane has the higher H⁺ concentration?
4. Why does glycolysis stop if lactate is not formed in a working muscle?
5. Name the two products of anaerobic respiration in yeast.
6. Why does anaerobic respiration give a much lower ATP yield than aerobic respiration?
7. A person needs 555 kJ from a lipid. Taking lipid as 37 kJ g⁻¹, what mass is needed?
8. Calculate the RQ of seedlings that take in 1.2 cm³ of oxygen and release 0.9 cm³ of carbon dioxide in the same time.
9. What does an RQ of 0.7 suggest about the substrate?
10. In a respirometer, why is potassium hydroxide solution or soda lime included?
11. A drop moves 18 mm in 6 min in a capillary of radius 0.5 mm. State the volume of oxygen used per minute, to 2 significant figures.
12. Why can a yeast culture growing only by fermentation not be given an RQ?

### Answers

1. The inner mitochondrial membrane (it is embedded in the cristae).
2. Oxygen; it combines with electrons and H⁺ to form water.
3. The intermembrane space side.
4. NAD is not regenerated, so glycolysis cannot continue.
5. Ethanol and carbon dioxide.
6. Only glycolysis makes ATP (2 per glucose); pyruvate is not oxidised further, and reduced NAD is used to make lactate or ethanol rather than to drive the electron transport chain.
7. 555 ÷ 37 = 15 g.
8. 0.9 ÷ 1.2 = 0.75.
9. Mainly lipid.
10. It absorbs the carbon dioxide produced, so the movement of the drop shows oxygen used only.
11. π × 0.5² × 18 = 14.1 mm³; 14.1 ÷ 6 = 2.4 mm³ min⁻¹.
12. It takes in no oxygen, so dividing carbon dioxide by oxygen would mean dividing by zero.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Saying "protons" where the question wants the idea of a proton gradient across a membrane, or writing "electrons flow through ATP synthase".
- Not saying which side of the membrane the H⁺ is pumped to.
- Writing that oxygen is "used up" in the Krebs cycle.
- Saying anaerobic respiration "produces ATP from lactate" instead of explaining NAD regeneration.
- Leaving out the units of the rate, or not dividing by the mass or the time.
- Using the capillary diameter in π r².
- Writing the RQ ratio upside down.
- Giving a "mixture of substrates" without saying why the RQ lies between 0.7 and 1.0.
