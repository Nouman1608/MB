This guide teaches section 18.3 Conservation from Topic 18, Classification, biodiversity and conservation, in the Cambridge International AS & A Level Biology 9700 syllabus for examination in 2025, 2026 and 2027. It covers all six learning outcomes. This is A Level content, examined on Paper 4, where it often appears as extended answers built around an unfamiliar endangered species.

The [Classification, biodiversity and conservation study guide](/resources/a-level-biology-9700-classification-biodiversity-and-conservation/) lists the main points for each outcome. This page explains the biology behind them, so that you can apply them to any species: why small populations are especially vulnerable, the step-by-step logic of assisted reproduction, what makes a seed bank or frozen zoo work, and how IUCN and CITES differ. Afterwards use the [Conservation revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-biology-9700-conservation-revision-notes/) and the [Conservation practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-biology-9700-conservation-practice/).

## Why populations become extinct (18.3.1)

Each named cause reduces either survival or reproduction, so the death rate exceeds the birth rate for many generations.

- **Climate change**: temperature and rainfall patterns shift. Species with narrow tolerances, or that cannot disperse, lose suitable habitat; food sources may appear at the wrong time; corals bleach in warmer water. Mountain species can only move uphill until there is nowhere left.
- **Competition**: a species that uses the same resources more efficiently takes food, space or light, so the weaker competitor's numbers fall. This is often caused by an introduced species.
- **Hunting by humans**: removes adults faster than they are replaced. Species with long lives, late maturity and few offspring, such as large mammals, recover slowly.
- **Degradation and loss of habitats**: clearing, draining, pollution and building remove food, shelter and breeding sites. **Fragmentation** splits one large population into small isolated ones.

### Why small populations are at greater risk

Explain answers score more highly when they link cause to genetics:

1. A small population has **low genetic diversity**: fewer alleles of each gene.
2. **Genetic drift** (17.2) has a bigger effect, so alleles can be lost by chance.
3. **Inbreeding** becomes likely, increasing homozygosity, so harmful recessive alleles are expressed.
4. With fewer alleles, the population is less likely to contain individuals able to survive a new disease or environmental change.
5. A single event (fire, storm, disease outbreak) can kill all remaining individuals.

## Why maintain biodiversity (18.3.2)

An outline answer should range across different kinds of reason:

| Kind of reason | Examples |
|---|---|
| Ethical | Other species have a right to exist; humans have a responsibility not to cause extinctions |
| Ecosystem services | Pollination of crops, nutrient recycling by decomposers, water purification, soil formation, flood control |
| Ecological | Each species has a niche; losing one, especially a keystone species, disrupts food webs and can cause further losses |
| Resources | Food, timber, fibres, fuel; wild relatives of crops carry alleles for disease resistance; sources of new medicines |
| Economic and social | Ecotourism, recreation, cultural and aesthetic value |

## Conservation of endangered species (18.3.3)

**Conservation in place (in situ)** protects the habitat as well as the species. **Conservation elsewhere (ex situ)** keeps organisms or their genetic material away from their natural habitat.

### Conserved areas: national parks and marine parks

- Protect whole ecosystems, so every species in them benefits, along with the interactions between them.
- Laws restrict hunting, fishing, logging, farming and building; rangers enforce them.
- Marine parks may ban fishing in some zones, allowing fish stocks to recover and spill over into surrounding waters.
- Problems: enforcement is costly; parks may be too small or isolated for wide-ranging animals; conflict with local people who depend on the land.

### Zoos

- **Captive breeding**: breeding records (studbooks) track every animal's ancestry, so pairings maximise genetic diversity and avoid inbreeding. Animals or gametes are moved between zoos.
- **Reintroduction**: captive-bred animals are released into protected habitat. The Arabian oryx, extinct in the wild by the early 1970s, was reintroduced to Oman in the 1980s from captive-bred herds.
- **Research** into behaviour, diet and reproduction; **education** of visitors.
- Limits: small founder populations; animals may lose natural behaviours; reintroduction fails if the original threat (hunting, habitat loss) remains.

### Botanic gardens

Grow and propagate rare plants, including by tissue culture; keep living collections for research; reintroduce plants to restored habitats; educate the public.

### Seed banks

1. Seeds are collected from many individuals and populations, to capture genetic diversity.
2. They are cleaned and **dried** to a low water content.
3. They are stored at low temperature (around −20 °C), which slows metabolism and decay so seeds stay viable for decades or longer.
4. Samples are tested for germination at intervals; if viability falls, plants are grown and fresh seed collected.

Seeds are small, so huge numbers of species can be stored cheaply in little space. Some seeds cannot survive drying or freezing, so those species must be kept as living plants or by other methods.

### Frozen zoos

Store sperm, eggs, embryos and tissue samples in **liquid nitrogen** (about −196 °C). Genetic material from animals that died long ago can be used in assisted reproduction, adding alleles back into a small population. Sperm can be transported between countries far more easily than live animals.

## Assisted reproduction (18.3.4)

Many endangered mammals breed poorly in captivity, or the best genetic matches live in different zoos. The syllabus names three linked techniques.

**IVF (in vitro fertilisation)**

1. The female is given **hormones** that stimulate several follicles to develop at once.
2. Mature **eggs are collected** from her ovaries.
3. **Sperm** is collected from a male (or thawed from a frozen zoo).
4. Eggs and sperm are mixed in a dish ("in vitro") and **fertilisation** occurs.
5. Embryos are grown in culture for a few days and checked.

**Embryo transfer**

6. Embryos are placed into the **uterus** of a female whose reproductive cycle has been matched by hormone treatment, or **frozen** for later use.

**Surrogacy**

7. The female carrying the embryo is a **surrogate**: not the genetic mother. She may be a less valuable female of the same species, or a female of a closely related, more common species.

**Advantages**: one female's eggs can give many offspring; the genetic mother can produce more eggs instead of spending months pregnant; animals that will not mate, or live far apart, can still breed; frozen gametes increase genetic diversity. **Limits**: costly, low success rates, and little is known about the reproductive biology of many rare species.

## Invasive alien species (18.3.5)

An **alien** species has been introduced, deliberately or accidentally, outside its natural range. It is **invasive** if it spreads and harms native species, ecosystems or the economy. Reasons to control them:

- **Competition**: grey squirrels, introduced to Britain from North America, out-compete native red squirrels for food.
- **Disease**: grey squirrels also carry squirrelpox virus, which kills red squirrels.
- **Predation**: the Nile perch, introduced to Lake Victoria, contributed to the loss of many native cichlid fish species.
- **Poisoning**: cane toads, introduced to Australia in 1935 to control beetle pests in sugar cane, are toxic to native predators that eat them.
- **Habitat change**: water hyacinth forms mats on lakes such as Lake Victoria, blocking light and reducing oxygen in the water beneath.
- **Few natural predators or pathogens** in the new area, so numbers rise unchecked.
- **Economic harm**: crop and fisheries losses, blocked waterways.

## IUCN and CITES (18.3.6)

| | IUCN | CITES |
|---|---|---|
| What it is | International Union for Conservation of Nature: an organisation of governments and non-governmental groups | Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species of Wild Fauna and Flora: an agreement between governments |
| Main role | Assesses the conservation status of species and publishes the **Red List of Threatened Species** | Controls **international trade** in listed species and their products |
| How | Categories from Least Concern through Near Threatened, Vulnerable, Endangered and Critically Endangered to Extinct in the Wild and Extinct | **Appendix I**: commercial trade banned; **Appendix II**: trade allowed with permits; **Appendix III**: protected in at least one country that asks others to help |
| Outcome | Evidence to prioritise conservation; advice to governments; supports conservation projects | Reduces hunting and collecting driven by trade (e.g. ivory, skins, rare plants) |

## Common mistakes

- Listing a cause of extinction without explaining how it lowers survival or reproduction.
- Saying zoos "protect the habitat". Only conserved areas do that.
- Calling IVF "cloning". IVF combines a sperm and an egg.
- Describing surrogacy as the surrogate "donating eggs". She carries another female's embryo.
- Confusing IUCN (assesses status) with CITES (controls trade).
- Defining any introduced species as invasive. It must spread and cause harm.

## Exam technique

- **Explain** (18.3.1, 18.3.5): give the cause, then the mechanism, then the effect on population size.
- **Outline** (18.3.2, 18.3.3, 18.3.6): short points, each with a distinct idea; one per mark.
- **Describe** (18.3.4): steps in order, naming hormones, collection, fertilisation, culture and transfer.
- **Suggest** with an unfamiliar species: use the stem (diet, breeding rate, range) to choose the most relevant threat and conservation method.
