## 4.1.3(a) and (b): The double bond and its shape

An alkene is an unsaturated hydrocarbon: it contains at least one C=C double bond, so more atoms can be added to it. The double bond is made of two different kinds of covalent bond.

- **σ-bond:** formed by orbitals overlapping directly between the two bonding carbon atoms, on the line joining the nuclei.
- **π-bond:** formed by the sideways overlap of a p-orbital on each carbon. Its electron density lies above and below the two carbons rather than on the line between them.

Because the π-bond depends on the two p-orbitals staying parallel, the carbons cannot twist about the C=C axis without breaking it. This is **restricted rotation**. A single C–C bond is a σ-bond only, so free rotation is possible there.

Each carbon in C=C has three regions of electron density: two single bonds and the double bond, which counts as one region. These repel as far apart as possible, so the arrangement around each carbon is **trigonal planar** with bond angles of about **120°**. The two carbons and the four atoms attached to them all lie in one plane.

## 4.1.3(c) and (d): Stereoisomerism in alkenes

**Stereoisomers** share one structural formula, yet their atoms are arranged differently in space.

**E/Z isomerism** is stereoisomerism caused by restricted rotation about a C=C bond. It needs two things:

1. a C=C bond (so the groups cannot swap sides by rotation), and
2. **each** C=C carbon must carry two **different** groups.

If either carbon carries two identical groups, such as =CH₂ or =C(CH₃)₂, no E/Z isomers exist.

**Cis–trans isomerism** is a special case of E/Z isomerism in which two of the substituent groups on each C=C carbon are the same. Cis means those identical groups are on the same side; trans means opposite sides.

### CIP priority rules

To name an isomer E or Z, rank the two groups on each carbon separately.

1. Look at the atom joined directly to the C=C carbon. Higher atomic number means higher priority. So Br > Cl > O > C > H.
2. If those atoms are the same, move out one bond and compare the sets of atoms attached there, highest first. Ethyl (C attached to C, H, H) beats methyl (C attached to H, H, H).
3. **Z** (zusammen): the higher-priority groups are on the same side. **E** (entgegen): they are on opposite sides.

### Worked example 1: when cis is not Z

3-methylpent-2-ene is CH₃CH=C(CH₃)CH₂CH₃. Name the isomer in which the two methyl groups are on the same side.

1. C2 carries CH₃ and H; C3 carries CH₃ and CH₂CH₃. Each carbon has two different groups, so E/Z isomers exist.
2. Both carbons carry CH₃, so cis–trans naming also applies: methyls on the same side means **cis**.
3. CIP at C2: C beats H, so CH₃ has priority.
4. CIP at C3: both groups start with C. Move out: ethyl has (C, H, H), methyl has (H, H, H). Ethyl wins.
5. The priority groups are the CH₃ on C2 and the ethyl on C3. In this isomer the CH₃ on C3 is on the same side as the C2 methyl, so the ethyl is on the opposite side. The isomer is **E**.

So this molecule is cis and E at the same time. This is why the specification warns that E = trans and Z = cis only holds reliably when each C=C carbon carries a hydrogen.

### Worked example 2: counting stereoisomers

How many stereoisomers does hexa-2,4-diene, CH₃CH=CH–CH=CHCH₃, have?

1. At C2=C3, C2 has CH₃ and H and C3 has H and CH=CHCH₃, so this bond can be E or Z. The same holds for C4=C5.
2. Combinations: (2E,4E), (2Z,4Z), (2E,4Z) and (2Z,4E).
3. The molecule is symmetrical, so (2E,4Z) turned round is (2Z,4E): one compound.
4. Answer: **three** stereoisomers.

## 4.1.3(e): Why alkenes are reactive

The π-bond has a relatively low bond enthalpy, so little energy is needed to break it, and its exposed electron density attracts attacking species. Alkenes therefore react far more readily than alkanes.

### Worked example 3: estimating the π-bond enthalpy

Use these values: average bond enthalpy of C=C is 612 kJ mol⁻¹; of C–C is 347 kJ mol⁻¹. Estimate the bond enthalpy of the π-bond.

1. C=C is one σ-bond plus one π-bond. Take the σ-bond as similar to a C–C single bond.
2. π-bond ≈ 612 − 347 = **265 kJ mol⁻¹**.
3. This is less than the 347 kJ mol⁻¹ needed for the σ-bond, so the π-bond breaks first when an alkene reacts.

## 4.1.3(f): Addition reactions

In each reaction the π-bond breaks and two atoms or groups add, one to each C=C carbon. The product is saturated.

| Reagent | Conditions | Product type | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| H₂ | nickel catalyst, heat | alkane | hex-2-ene + H₂ → hexane |
| Br₂ or Cl₂ | room temperature | dihaloalkane | pent-2-ene + Br₂ → 2,3-dibromopentane |
| HBr, HCl or HI | room temperature | haloalkane | hex-3-ene + HCl → 3-chlorohexane |
| H₂O (steam) | acid catalyst, e.g. H₃PO₄ | alcohol | but-2-ene + H₂O → butan-2-ol |

For example, but-2-ene with steam: CH₃CH=CHCH₃ + H₂O → CH₃CH(OH)CH₂CH₃.

### The bromine test for unsaturation (PAG7)

Shake the sample with bromine water. If the sample contains C=C, the orange colour disappears and the mixture becomes **colourless**, because bromine adds across the double bond. A saturated compound leaves the orange colour unchanged. Record "orange to colourless", not "clear", which describes transparency rather than colour.

## 4.1.3(g) and (h): Electrophiles and the mechanism

An **electrophile** is an **electron pair acceptor**. It is attracted to the π-bond's electron density. The δ+ hydrogen in H–Br and a δ+ bromine in polarised Br₂ both act as electrophiles.

Electrophilic addition involves **heterolytic fission**: a covalent bond breaks and both bonding electrons go to one atom, forming ions.

### Worked example 4: hex-3-ene with hydrogen chloride

H–Cl is polar, with H δ+ and Cl δ−.

1. Curly arrow from the C=C bond to the H; curly arrow from the H–Cl bond to the Cl. The H bonds to C3, leaving a carbocation with the positive charge on C4, plus Cl⁻.
2. Curly arrow from a lone pair on Cl⁻ to the positive carbon.
3. The product is 3-chlorohexane.

Show the dipole, the carbocation's positive charge and the lone pair on Cl⁻.

**With a halogen**, the π-bond's electron density induces a dipole in the approaching Br₂. The nearer Br becomes δ+ and is attacked; Br–Br breaks heterolytically, giving a carbocation (or bromonium ion; the specification accepts either) and Br⁻, which bonds to the positive carbon. Pent-2-ene gives 2,3-dibromopentane.

## 4.1.3(i): Markownikoff's rule

When H–X adds to an unsymmetrical alkene, two carbocations are possible. The major product comes from the **more stable** carbocation. Stability order: **tertiary > secondary > primary**.

### Worked example 5: 2-methylbut-1-ene with HBr

2-methylbut-1-ene is CH₂=C(CH₃)CH₂CH₃.

1. If H adds to C1, the positive charge sits on C2, bonded to three other carbons: a **tertiary** carbocation.
2. If H adds to C2, the positive charge sits on C1, bonded to one carbon: a **primary** carbocation.
3. The tertiary carbocation is more stable, so it forms more often.
4. Major product: **2-bromo-2-methylbutane**. Minor product: 1-bromo-2-methylbutane.

## 4.1.3(j): Addition polymers

Many alkene monomers join through their C=C bonds to form a long saturated chain.

### Worked example 6: repeat unit from a monomer

Propenenitrile is CH₂=CHCN.

1. Draw the C=C with its four groups, then change C=C to C–C with a bond out of each end.
2. Repeat unit: **–[CH₂–CH(CN)]–**, with extension bonds through the brackets and CN as a side branch.

### Worked example 7: monomer from a polymer section

A section reads –CH(CH₃)–CH(CH₃)–CH(CH₃)–CH(CH₃)–.

1. Every main-chain carbon is the same, so the repeat unit is two adjacent ones: –CH(CH₃)–CH(CH₃)–.
2. Put C=C between them and remove the extension bonds: CH₃CH=CHCH₃.
3. Monomer: **but-2-ene**.

## 4.1.3(k) and (l): Waste polymers and alternatives

Cheap oil-derived polymers are unreactive, so they persist in landfill. Processing waste makes better use of resources.

- **Combustion for energy:** burning waste polymers generates heat for electricity, so less fuel is extracted.
- **Organic feedstock:** waste polymers are broken into small molecules used to make new plastics and other organic chemicals, reducing demand for crude oil.
- **Removing toxic waste products:** burning PVC and other halogenated plastics releases toxic, acidic hydrogen chloride. Passing the waste gases through a base neutralises the HCl.

**Biodegradable polymers** are broken down by microorganisms into small molecules such as water and carbon dioxide; many are made from plant material, reducing dependence on oil. **Photodegradable polymers** contain bonds that break when they absorb light, so the polymer breaks up. Both reduce persistent plastic waste.

## Common errors

- Giving 109.5° at a C=C carbon instead of 120°.
- Claiming E/Z isomers for a molecule with =CH₂ at one end, such as but-1-ene.
- Ranking CIP groups by size or mass instead of by atomic number of the first atom.
- Assuming cis always means Z; check the priorities each time.
- Drawing the first curly arrow from a carbon atom rather than from the double bond, or omitting the halide ion's lone pair.
- Writing the repeat unit with a C=C still in it, or putting a side group into the main chain.
