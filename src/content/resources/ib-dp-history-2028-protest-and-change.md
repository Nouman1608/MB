The focused study **Protest and change** is one of the five focused studies in IB DP History (first assessment 2028). Both examples must be studied: **feminism in the USA (1960 to 1979)** and **revolution in Tunisia (1989 to 2015)**. The study has three inquiry questions and is assessed through sources in paper 1. This guide gives the historical context for each example and shows how to use it when reading sources. It applies to SL and HL.

## How paper 1 uses this study

Paper 1 (1 hour 15 minutes, 24 marks) uses three sources and one inquiry question. The three static questions ask you to explain how the content of sources A and B can be used (6 marks), analyse how the context of source C influences its use (6 marks) and examine the perspectives in all three sources (12 marks). The grounding content is the background that lets you read the sources with understanding.

## Inquiry question 1: What led to the emergence of protest movements?

### Feminism in the USA

The grounding content names reactions to domesticity, including the participation of women in the workforce; the impact of changing access to contraception; and the influence of early feminist literature.

After the Second World War, many American women had worked in factories and offices during the war, and by the 1960s the proportion of women in paid work had risen. Yet the dominant image in the media was the contented housewife, and women were mostly paid less than men and excluded from many professions. Many educated women felt frustrated at the gap between ideals and reality. The approval of the first oral contraceptive pill in 1960 gave women greater control over family planning, though access varied by marital status and by state law. Books played a part. Simone de Beauvoir's *The Second Sex* (1949, English translation 1953) analysed women's subordination, and Betty Friedan's *The Feminine Mystique* (1963) described what she called "the problem that has no name" among middle-class housewives. Historians note that the movement also drew on the experience of women in the civil rights movement and the New Left, who found that they were often given supporting roles.

### Revolution in Tunisia

The grounding content names repression and censorship by the Ben Ali regime, failed economic reform and unemployment, and the influence and spread of revolutionary ideas.

Zine El Abidine Ben Ali took power in 1987, removing Habib Bourguiba, and ruled through the ruling party and a strong security apparatus. Opposition parties and independent media were restricted, the internet was censored, and critics faced surveillance, arrest and torture. Ben Ali's regime was also associated with corruption, particularly among relatives of the president and his wife. Tunisia had achieved some economic growth and had a relatively educated population, but unemployment was high among young graduates, and inland regions such as Sidi Bouzid were poorer than the coast. Reforms did not create enough jobs, and food prices rose in 2010. Ideas also spread: satellite television, such as Al Jazeera, and the internet carried news and criticism, and activists drew on a wider tradition of protest and trade-union organising.

## Inquiry question 2: How did the protest movement challenge authority?

### Feminism in the USA

The grounding content names protest and activism; the role of groups including the National Organization for Women (NOW), the National Women's Political Caucus and organisations affiliated to the Women's Liberation Movement; and the role of mass media.

NOW was founded in 1966 by Betty Friedan, Pauli Murray and others to press for equality, and it lobbied for legal change, including enforcement of the employment provisions of the Civil Rights Act of 1964 and an Equal Rights Amendment to the Constitution. The National Women's Political Caucus, founded in 1971 with figures including Bella Abzug, Gloria Steinem and Shirley Chisholm, aimed to elect more women to office. The wider Women's Liberation Movement was made up of many small groups that used consciousness-raising meetings, and it protested events such as the Miss America pageant in 1968. In August 1970 women held the Women's Strike for Equality, which saw marches in several cities. The media played an ambiguous part. Coverage spread the movement's ideas and attracted new members, but it also mocked activists. Publications such as *Ms.* magazine (founded in 1971) gave women their own platform.

### Revolution in Tunisia

The grounding content names the December Revolution, the Ennahda Party and Nidaa Tounes, and the role of social media.

On 17 December 2010 a young street vendor, Mohamed Bouazizi, set himself on fire in Sidi Bouzid after a confrontation with local officials. His act sparked protests in the town that spread across the country, led by unemployed young people and supported by trade unionists, lawyers and others. The security forces killed many protesters. Social media, mainly Facebook and videos shared online, spread images and organised protests, and the regime's ability to censor them was limited. On 14 January 2011, Ben Ali fled the country. Historians use the label "revolution" for the events, and also debate how far the changes were deep.

After Ben Ali's fall, political parties formed or re-emerged. Ennahda, an Islamist party led by Rached Ghannouchi, won the largest share of the vote in the October 2011 election for a Constituent Assembly. Nidaa Tounes, founded in 2012 by Beji Caid Essebsi, brought together secular and other political figures, and won the largest share in the 2014 parliamentary elections, after which Essebsi became president.

## Inquiry question 3: What changes did the protest movement achieve, and with what limitations?

### Feminism in the USA

The grounding content names political and legal changes, including Title IX (1972) and *Roe v. Wade* (1973); economic changes, including equal pay and employment rights; and the experiences of marginalised women, including African American and working-class women.

Title IX of the Education Amendments of 1972 banned sex discrimination in federally funded education, which had a large effect on women's participation in college and in school sports. The Supreme Court's decision in *Roe v. Wade* in 1973 recognised a constitutional right to abortion in the first stages of pregnancy. The Equal Pay Act of 1963 and Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964 prohibited pay discrimination and employment discrimination, though enforcement was uneven. Congress passed the Equal Rights Amendment in 1972 but it fell short of the number of states needed for ratification, and opposition campaigns such as that led by Phyllis Schlafly were influential. Many Black women and working-class women argued that the movement concentrated on the concerns of white, middle-class women. Groups such as the Combahee River Collective (1974) developed Black feminist analysis of the combined effects of race, class and gender.

### Revolution in Tunisia

The grounding content names political and legal changes, including the ousting of Ben Ali and the 2014 Constitution; social changes, including youth radicalisation and terrorism; and the role and status of women.

After the Constituent Assembly was elected in 2011, the political process was sometimes tense, with the assassinations of two opposition politicians, Chokri Belaid and Mohamed Brahmi, in 2013. A national dialogue, led by civil society groups including the main trade union, helped to resolve the crisis, and the Constitution was adopted in January 2014. It guaranteed freedoms and equality, and committed the state to protecting women's rights. Tunisian women already held rights under the Personal Status Code of 1956, and after 2011 these were defended in debates about the constitution. Limitations were serious. Economic problems and youth unemployment continued, and some young people were drawn to extremist groups. Terrorist attacks in 2015, at the Bardo Museum in Tunis and at a tourist resort at Sousse, damaged the economy. Historians therefore describe Tunisia as the most successful of the 2011 uprisings in the region, but with incomplete economic change.

## Using sources

- US sources include speeches, NOW documents, magazine articles, posters, photographs of marches and legal texts. Be alert to who is speaking: a Friedan speech, a Schlafly pamphlet and a Black feminist statement represent different perspectives.
- Tunisian sources include social media posts, photographs, newspaper reports, speeches and later academic analysis. A regime statement will emphasise order, while an activist's post will emphasise injustice.
- Always ask what was meant to be achieved. A call to action, a news report and a retrospective analysis serve different purposes.

## Linking to the concepts

- **Cause and consequence:** conditions (repression, domesticity, unemployment) and actors (organisations and individuals).
- **Continuity and change:** legal change did not always bring social change.
- **Perspectives:** supporters, opponents and those excluded.
- **Significance:** how these movements shaped politics in their countries.

## Common mistakes

- Treating the Tunisian uprising as caused only by social media.
- Describing the US movement as a single, united organisation.
- Forgetting that the Equal Rights Amendment was not ratified.
- Writing about the events instead of the sources.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is the Tunisian revolution the same as the "Arab Spring"?** It began the wave of protests in the region in 2010 to 2011, but the course of each country differed.

**Do I need to know about women's rights in Tunisia before 2011?** The 1956 Personal Status Code is useful background for the role and status of women.
