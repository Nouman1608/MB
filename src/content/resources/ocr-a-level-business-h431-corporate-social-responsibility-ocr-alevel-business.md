## Layers of responsibility

CSR means a business accepting responsibility for the effects of its activities on society, the environment and all its stakeholders, going beyond what the law requires. A helpful way to explain it, which the specification does not name, is to think of four layers:

1. **Economic:** make a profit, so the business survives, pays staff and rewards owners.
2. **Legal:** obey the law on employment, health and safety, consumer protection and the environment.
3. **Ethical:** do what is right where the law is silent, such as paying suppliers on time or refusing to use suppliers with poor working conditions.
4. **Discretionary:** contribute to the community voluntarily, through donations, volunteering or local projects.

Only the third and fourth layers are CSR in the strict sense, because the first two are required. This matters in exam answers: obeying a minimum wage law is not CSR; paying above it by choice is.

### Two views of the purpose of business

- **Shareholder view.** The economist Milton Friedman argued in 1970 that the social responsibility of business is to increase its profits within the rules of the game. On this view, CSR spending is the owners' money and should be justified by returns.
- **Stakeholder view.** A business depends on employees, customers, suppliers and the community, so it should balance their interests, not only the owners'.

You do not need to name either view, but using the contrast helps explain why CSR causes conflict between stakeholders.

## Policy and profile

| | CSR policy | CSR profile |
|---|---|---|
| What it is | The written commitments a business makes and how it will carry them out | How responsible the business is seen to be, based on its record |
| Controlled by | The business | Customers, media, employees, campaigners and investors, based on evidence |
| Example | "We will pay all staff at least £1 an hour above the legal minimum and audit every supplier yearly" | Reviews, news stories, staff opinions, independent ratings |

A policy can improve a profile only if actions follow. A gap between the two is the source of **greenwashing**: claims of environmental or social responsibility that the business's actions do not support. Greenwashing can leave a profile worse than having no policy at all.

**Advantages of a strong profile** for the business: customer loyalty, a possible price premium, easier recruitment and retention, investor appeal and lower risk of boycotts. For stakeholders: better pay and conditions, safer products, fairer supplier terms, less pollution. **Disadvantages:** cost, high expectations that are hard to sustain, closer scrutiny, and greater damage when something goes wrong.

## The conflict with profit and other objectives

The conflict arises because most CSR actions cost money now, while the benefits are uncertain and come later.

### Worked example 1: a CSR investment against a profit target

Hollins Laundry Services washes linen for hotels. It earns operating profit of £420,000 a year on revenue of £3.5 million. The directors propose two CSR actions:

- A water-recycling system costing £180,000, which will cut water and energy bills by £30,000 a year.
- Paying all 85 staff £1,200 a year more than now, a cost of 85 × £1,200 = £102,000 a year.

**Effect on profit in year 1** (treating the £180,000 as a cost in year 1 for simplicity): 420,000 − 180,000 − 102,000 + 30,000 = **£168,000**, a fall of £252,000, which is 60% of current profit.

**Effect in later years**, with no further outlay: 420,000 − 102,000 + 30,000 = **£348,000**, still £72,000 (about 17%) below today.

**Payback on the recycling system alone:** 180,000 ÷ 30,000 = **6 years**.

To restore profit to £420,000 in later years, Hollins needs an extra £72,000 of profit. If each extra £1 of revenue brings 30p of contribution, it needs 72,000 ÷ 0.30 = **£240,000** more revenue, about 6.9% of current sales.

**Interpreting the figures.** On the numbers alone, CSR clearly conflicts with profit. Evaluation brings in what the figures omit: higher pay may cut staff turnover and recruitment costs; hotel chains with their own environmental targets may prefer a laundry that recycles water, so the revenue gain may be realistic; and rising water prices would shorten the payback. The conflict is sharp in year 1 and narrower later, and its size depends on how customers respond.

### Conflict with objectives other than profit

- **Growth:** strict supplier standards may limit which suppliers or markets a business can use.
- **Cost leadership:** a business competing on the lowest price has little room for extra cost.
- **Survival:** in a downturn, CSR spending may be cut first, damaging a profile built over years.
- **Shareholder returns:** dividends may fall in the short run, which matters more where shareholders want income now.

## When CSR matters most

The specification asks you to evaluate the importance of CSR. Importance varies with:

- **How visible the business is to consumers.** A consumer brand selling on trust gains or loses more from its profile than a firm supplying parts to other businesses, although business customers increasingly check suppliers too.
- **The nature of the industry.** Where risks to people or the environment are high, as in food, clothing supply chains, chemicals or transport, a failure is more likely and more damaging.
- **Ownership.** A family owner may accept lower returns for values; shareholders in a plc may expect CSR to pay its way.
- **Competitive position.** If rivals all have strong profiles, CSR is a minimum expectation rather than an advantage.
- **Financial strength.** A business with thin margins may not be able to afford major CSR spending, whatever its intentions.

## Worked example 2: recommending how to improve a CSR profile

**Case.** Penmore Haulage runs 60 lorries. Last year it was fined after diesel leaked from its depot into a stream, and a local news story followed. It has £150,000 available. Three options are proposed.

| Option | Cost | Credibility | Link to the problem |
|---|---|---|---|
| A: Sponsor the local football club | £40,000 a year | Low: looks like a cover-up | None |
| B: Rebuild the depot's fuel storage, fund a stream clean-up and publish an independent environmental audit each year | £130,000, then £10,000 a year for audits | High: addresses the cause and can be checked | Direct |
| C: Replace 10 lorries with lower-emission models over time | £150,000 deposit | Medium | Indirect |

**Recommendation: option B.** It deals directly with the cause of the damage, so it reduces the risk of another leak and another fine, which protects profit as well as the profile. Publishing an independent audit makes the claim checkable, which avoids the charge of greenwashing that option A would invite. It costs £130,000, within the £150,000 available, leaving £20,000 towards the first audits.

**Why not the others?** Sponsorship does nothing about the leak and could be seen as an attempt to buy goodwill. Lower-emission lorries are valuable but address a different issue and use all the cash.

**Condition.** Option C could follow once the depot problem is solved, perhaps funded as lorries are replaced in the normal cycle. The recommendation depends on Penmore's customers caring about environmental record; if most choose purely on price, the profile matters less, but the cost of further fines still justifies option B.

## Common mistakes

- Calling legal compliance CSR.
- Treating a CSR policy as if it were the same as a good profile.
- Assuming CSR always lowers profit, or always raises it; the effect depends on time period and customer response.
- Recommending a popular CSR action that does not link to the business's actual problem.
- Ignoring how CSR affects different stakeholders differently.

## Exam technique

"Explain" needs a point and its effect, for example CSR raises costs, so profit margin falls in the short run. "Evaluate" requires both sides weighed and a judgement about this business. "Recommend and justify" means a clear choice, reasons from the case, a rejected alternative and a condition. Use the case figures: a CSR cost expressed as a percentage of profit is more convincing than "it is expensive".
