Section **3.1.2.4 Social control** in OxfordAQA International AS/A level Sociology (9690) is
examined in Unit 2 Section A. It is one of the most concept-heavy parts of the course. The
specification lists formal and informal control; positive and negative sanctions; consensus and
conflict views; "Stigmatised identities; othering"; agencies of informal control (families,
schools, peers, media, religion, workplace) and formal control ("government/laws; police and other
enforcement agencies; criminal justice system"); "Aims and types of punishment"; "Crime prevention:
types of policing, surveillance"; "Repressive and ideological state apparatuses, interpellation;
hegemony"; and functionalist, Marxist, feminist and social action/interactionist perspectives.
Goffman, Stanley Cohen and Zuboff, named for 3.1.2, all help here. The unit overview is in the
[Exploring Sociology study guide](/resources/a-level-oxfordaqa-sociology-exploring-sociology/).

## Formal and informal control, and sanctions

**Social control** is the set of processes that encourage people to conform to norms. **Formal
control** is exercised by bodies with official authority to enforce written rules; **informal
control** comes from everyday relationships and unwritten norms.

Sanctions can be crossed with this distinction to make a four-cell grid, a quick way to generate
examples:

| | Positive (reward) | Negative (punishment) |
|---|---|---|
| **Formal** | Award, promotion, certificate, reduced sentence for good behaviour | Fine, imprisonment, suspension from school, dismissal |
| **Informal** | Praise, smiles, likes on a post, acceptance by friends | Gossip, ridicule, ignoring someone, ostracism |

**Agencies.** Informal: families (parental approval), schools (teacher praise, detentions), peers
(acceptance or exclusion), media (shaming of public figures), religion (community expectations,
ideas of sin), workplace (colleagues' approval, appraisal). Formal: government and laws, police and
other enforcement agencies (customs, regulators), and the criminal justice system (courts, prisons,
probation).

## Consensus and conflict views

- **Consensus** (functionalist) views see control as protecting shared values that benefit everyone.
- **Conflict** views (Marxist and feminist) see control as protecting the interests of powerful groups.

## Stigmatised identities and othering

Erving Goffman's *Stigma* (1963) defines stigma as an attribute that is "deeply discrediting",
reducing a person from a whole person to a "tainted, discounted one". He described three types:
physical differences, perceived flaws of character (for example a criminal record) and "tribal"
stigma of race, nation or religion. He distinguished the **discredited**, whose stigma is visible,
from the **discreditable**, who can hide it and must manage information about themselves. Stigma is a
form of informal control: fear of stigma deters deviance, and those stigmatised face exclusion.

**Othering** is defining a group as fundamentally different from, and inferior to, "us". Edward
Said's *Orientalism* (1978) showed how Western writing constructed "the East" as exotic and
backward, justifying colonial rule. Stanley Cohen's *Folk Devils and Moral Panics* (1972) showed how
media reaction to clashes between Mods and Rockers in 1960s England turned young people into
**folk devils**, leading to calls for tougher control. Othering legitimises harsher treatment of
the othered group by police, courts and the public.

## Aims and types of punishment

| Aim | Meaning | Typical forms |
|---|---|---|
| **Retribution** | Offenders deserve punishment in proportion to the harm done | Prison terms matched to seriousness |
| **Deterrence** | Discourage the offender (individual) and others (general) from offending | Visible, certain punishment |
| **Incapacitation** | Remove the ability to offend | Imprisonment, driving bans, in some states the death penalty |
| **Rehabilitation** | Change the offender so they do not reoffend | Education, therapy, drug treatment |
| **Reparation / restorative justice** | Repair harm, often by meeting victims | Community payback, victim-offender conferences |
| **Denunciation** | Express society's disapproval | Public sentencing, criminal record |

**Types of punishment** include fines, community sentences, probation, imprisonment, corporal
punishment and capital punishment; their use differs sharply between societies.

**Durkheim** argued punishment's main function is to reaffirm the **collective conscience**,
uniting law-abiding citizens against the offender. He contrasted **repressive law** in simpler
societies, which punishes, with **restitutive law** in complex societies, which aims to restore
things to how they were. **Michel Foucault** (*Discipline and Punish*, 1975) traced a shift from
**sovereign power**, displayed through public bodily punishment, to **disciplinary power**,
which works through surveillance and self-discipline. He used Jeremy Bentham's design for a
**panopticon** prison, where inmates could be watched at any time without knowing when, so they
learn to police themselves.

## Crime prevention: policing and surveillance

**Types of policing**
- **Community policing**: officers build relationships and trust in local areas.
- **Zero-tolerance policing**: strict enforcement of minor offences, based on James Q. Wilson and
  George Kelling's **broken windows** argument (1982) that visible disorder invites more serious
  crime; associated with New York in the 1990s, and criticised for targeting poor and minority
  communities.
- **Intelligence-led and predictive policing**: using data to target likely offenders, places and
  times.

**Situational crime prevention**, associated with Ronald Clarke, reduces opportunities for crime
through target hardening (locks, barriers), surveillance and design. Critics say crime may simply be
displaced elsewhere.

**Surveillance** ranges from CCTV and facial recognition to monitoring of online activity. Thomas
Mathiesen's **synopticon** describes the many watching the few, for example through media and
smartphone filming of police. Shoshana Zuboff's *surveillance capitalism* extends the debate to
corporations collecting behavioural data, which can be shared with states.

## State apparatuses, interpellation and hegemony

**Louis Althusser** (1970) distinguished:
- **Repressive state apparatuses (RSAs)**, which control through force or its threat: police,
  courts, prisons and army.
- **Ideological state apparatuses (ISAs)**, which control through ideas: education, family,
  religion, media.

**Interpellation** is Althusser's term for the way ideology "hails" individuals and turns them into
subjects who recognise themselves in the role offered. His example is a police officer calling out
"Hey, you there!": the person who turns around has recognised that the call is addressed to them.
Ideology works when people accept identities, such as "good student" or "loyal employee", as their
own.

**Antonio Gramsci**'s concept of **hegemony** describes rule through **consent** rather than force:
the ruling class wins acceptance for its worldview as "common sense", through civil society
(churches, schools, media). Force remains in reserve. Because hegemony must be won, it can be
challenged by **counter-hegemonic** ideas, which gives more room for resistance than Althusser's
model.

## Perspectives on social control

| Perspective | Argument | Sociologists |
|---|---|---|
| **Functionalist** | Control maintains shared values and order; punishment strengthens solidarity; strong social bonds prevent deviance | Durkheim; Travis Hirschi's social bond theory (1969): attachment, commitment, involvement, belief |
| **Marxist** | Control protects capitalism; the law is enforced selectively against the working class; ideology secures consent | Althusser; Gramsci |
| **Feminist** | Women face distinctive, often informal, control at home, in public and at work, which limits their freedom | Frances Heidensohn's *Women and Crime* (1985) |
| **Social action / interactionist** | Control creates deviance through labelling; agents of control use discretion and stereotypes | Howard Becker's *Outsiders* (1963); Goffman; Stanley Cohen |

## Worked example: annotated 8-mark answer

*Explain the difference between repressive and ideological state apparatuses.*

> **Method of control.** Repressive state apparatuses, such as police, courts and prisons, control
> people through force or the threat of force (**define**). Ideological state apparatuses, such as
> schools, the family, religion and media, control through ideas that make inequality seem natural
> (**contrast**). Althusser argued that ISAs are more important in stable capitalist societies
> because they secure obedience without visible coercion (**analysis**).
>
> **Visibility and interpellation.** RSAs are visible and can provoke resistance, whereas ISAs work
> through interpellation, as people accept identities such as "hard-working employee" as their
> own (**develop**). This means people may police themselves, so control seems voluntary
> (**consequence**).

## Common mistakes

- Confusing informal control with positive sanctions: informal sanctions can be negative.
- Attributing hegemony to Althusser or interpellation to Gramsci.
- Listing aims of punishment without types, or vice versa.
- Describing stigma without Goffman's terms (discredited, discreditable, spoiled identity).
- Forgetting the feminist perspective, which the specification names.

## Exam technique

Use the four-cell sanctions grid to generate examples quickly. In evaluation questions, set
consensus views against conflict views and add the interactionist point that control can create
the deviance it targets. Apply concepts to a named society: its policing model, punishments or
surveillance systems.
