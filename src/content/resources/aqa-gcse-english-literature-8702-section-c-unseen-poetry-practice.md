## Section A: short questions

**1.** Which assessment objectives does the 24-mark unseen poetry question assess, and why should you
not spend time on context? **[2]**

**2.** Identify the tone of this original line and name two words that create it: *"You left the gate
unlatched again, as if the world were safe."* **[3]**

**3.** An unseen poem is titled "Inheritance" and describes a woman sorting her grandmother's
kitchen drawers. Explain two ways the title could guide your interpretation. **[2]**

**4.** These are the last two lines of an original poem: *"I said I didn't mind. I said it twice."*
Explain how the structure of these lines suggests the speaker's real feelings. **[2]**

**5.** Improve this sentence so that it analyses rather than spots a feature: *"The poet uses
enjambment in line 6."* The line reads: *"and still I waited by the door / long after he had gone."*
**[3]**

**6.** A student writes: *"The poet is clearly writing about her own divorce."* Explain why this is
unhelpful and how to rephrase it. **[2]**

**7.** Rewrite this sentence so that it compares methods for the 8-mark question: *"Poem 1 is about a
mother and Poem 2 is about a father."* **[3]**

## Section B: unseen poems

Read the two original poems, then answer Questions 8 to 10.

> *Original poem 1, "Night Shift"*
> My mother leaves as we come in from school,
> her coat already buttoned to the throat,
> a kiss that tastes of toothpaste and of rush.
> The kettle she has boiled is still too hot.
>
> All night the house is ours. We fight, we watch
> the shows she wouldn't let us. Then we wait,
> pretending not to, ears turned to the street,
> until the key, the cough, the dragging feet.
>
> At breakfast she is bright as she can be.
> She asks about our day. She doesn't sit.
> Her eyes are somewhere left behind at work,
> and so, I think, are other bits of her.

> *Original poem 2, "Early"*
> Before the light, my father's boots
> go softly down the stairs.
> The front door clicks. The engine coughs.
> And then the street is theirs:
>
> the foxes and the bin men and
> the long, unhurried dark.
> I lie awake and count to ten
> and listen for the park
>
> where every morning, so he says,
> he stops to watch it rise.
> I've never asked him if it's true.
> I'd rather keep the lie.

**8.** In "Night Shift", how does the poet present the speaker's feelings about their mother? **[24]**

**9.** In both "Night Shift" and "Early" the speakers describe a parent whose work takes them away
from home. What are the similarities and/or differences between the methods the poets use to present
the speakers' feelings? **[8]**

**10.** Write the first two sentences of your answer to Question 8 as an overview that would guide
the rest of the response. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** AO1 and AO2 [1]. Context is not assessed in this question, and the poem is unseen, so time is
better spent analysing language, form and structure [1].

**2.** Tone: anxious, protective or gently reproachful [1]. "Unlatched" suggests vulnerability or
carelessness [1]; "as if the world were safe" implies the speaker believes it is not, adding fear to
the reproach [1].

**3.** The title suggests that more than objects are being passed down, perhaps habits, memories or
identity [1]. It could also create irony or sadness if the drawers reveal how little remains, or
something unexpected, so the reader looks for what is really inherited [1].

**4.** The two short, end-stopped sentences create a flat, clipped rhythm that sounds forced [1].
The repetition "I said... I said it twice" suggests the speaker is trying to convince themselves,
implying they did mind [1].

**5.** Example: *"The enjambment after 'door' leaves the reader waiting at the line break, just as the
speaker waits,"* [1] *"and the delayed phrase 'long after he had gone' reveals"* [1] *"that the
waiting was hopeless, making the loss feel prolonged."* [1]

**6.** It guesses at biography that cannot be known from an unseen poem and shifts focus away from
the text [1]. Rephrase as: *"The speaker presents the end of a relationship as..."*, focusing on the
speaker and the poet's methods [1].

**7.** Example: *"Both poets present a parent through their absence,"* [1] *"but whereas 'Night
Shift' uses domestic objects such as the still-hot kettle,"* [1] *"'Early' relies on sounds heard
from bed, like the door that 'clicks'."* [1]

**8.** AQA marks this holistically against level descriptors. The weighting below is a guide only.

**AO1 [12]**, a critical response with apt references:

- An overview: the speaker's feelings move from missing the mother, to anxious waiting, to sad
  recognition of what the job costs her.
- Personal response to the mixture of love, resentment, worry and pity.
- Precise, short references used to support each point.
- A judgement on how the final stanza changes the reader's understanding of the earlier ones.

**AO2 [12]**, analysis of language, form and structure:

- "Buttoned to the throat" suggests the mother is already sealed off and in work mode, slightly
  stifled.
- The kiss "of toothpaste and of rush" mixes care with hurry; the abstract "rush" as a taste is
  striking and shows how little time she has.
- The still-hot kettle is a domestic trace of her presence just missed.
- "All night the house is ours" sounds like freedom, but the volta-like "Then we wait" reveals worry.
- "Pretending not to" shows the children hiding their anxiety; enjambment carries the reader into the
  waiting.
- The list "the key, the cough, the dragging feet" is heard, not seen, building relief and concern
  through sound; the only rhyming couplet, "street" and "feet", closes the stanza on her return.
- "Bright as she can be" suggests effort and strain behind her cheerfulness.
- Short sentences, "She doesn't sit.", show restlessness and exhaustion.
- The final metaphor of her eyes "left behind at work" and "other bits of her" presents the job as
  taking parts of her, a gentle but sad ending; "I think" shows the speaker's tentative, growing
  understanding.
- Regular stanzas suggest the routine repeats nightly, so the situation is ongoing.

**9.** Award one mark per developed comparative point, up to 8:

- Both poems present the parent through traces and sounds rather than direct description [1].
- "Night Shift" uses domestic objects (the kettle, the coat); "Early" uses sounds heard from bed (the
  door "clicks", the engine "coughs") [1].
- Both personify or animate the world around the absent parent: the engine "coughs" in "Early", and
  the night house becomes the children's territory in "Night Shift" [1].
- "Night Shift" uses three stanzas that follow a day's cycle from afternoon to breakfast; "Early"
  follows a single early morning [1].
- "Early" uses a regular ballad-like rhyme scheme and short lines that feel childlike; "Night Shift"
  uses longer, mostly unrhymed lines that sound more reflective [1].
- Both endings reveal the speaker's understanding: "Night Shift" with the metaphor of parts of the
  mother left at work; "Early" with the admission "I'd rather keep the lie" [1].
- The half rhyme of "rise" and "lie" in "Early" breaks the pattern, hinting at doubt, whereas the
  couplet "street"/"feet" in "Night Shift" marks relief [1].
- Overall, "Night Shift" presents worry and pity, while "Early" presents a protective choice to keep
  believing in a comforting story [1].

**10.** Award marks as follows:

- A clear statement of the speaker's main feelings about the mother [1].
- A sense of development, such as from longing and worry to sad understanding [1].
- A link to method, such as domestic detail or the final metaphor, that sets up the analysis [1].

## Using these questions well

Do Questions 1 to 7 as a warm-up. Then set a timer: about 30 to 35 minutes for Question 8 and 10 to
12 for Question 9, without looking at the answers. When you mark your work, check that your opening
gave an overview, that every quotation was followed by analysis, and that your 8-mark answer compared
methods in both poems in every point.
