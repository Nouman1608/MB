This guide covers subtopic **17.3 Inheritance** of Cambridge O Level Biology 5090, 2026-2028 series. The syllabus asks you to describe inheritance and define an allele; to use the terms dominant, recessive, phenotype, genotype, homozygous and heterozygous; to use genetic diagrams and Punnett squares for monohybrid crosses, with ratios limited to 1 : 1 and 3 : 1; to explain why observed ratios often differ from expected ones; to explain codominance using ABO blood groups; to describe sex determination; to describe gene and chromosome mutations (sickle cell anaemia and Down's syndrome); and to state the sources of genetic variation and the effects of radiation and chemicals on mutation.

The [Inheritance topic guide](/resources/o-level-biology-5090-inheritance/) covers the whole topic. This page goes deeper, with every step of the genetic diagrams shown. Use the [17.3 practice questions](/resources/cambridge-o-level-biology-5090-inheritance-practice/) to test yourself.

## Key terms

**Inheritance** is the transmission of genetic information from generation to generation.

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Allele | An alternative form of a gene |
| Dominant | An allele that shows its effect in the phenotype even when only one copy is present |
| Recessive | An allele that shows its effect only when two copies are present (no dominant allele) |
| Phenotype | The observable features of an organism |
| Genotype | The alleles an organism has for a gene |
| Homozygous | Having two identical alleles of a gene |
| Heterozygous | Having two different alleles of a gene |
| Pure-breeding | Homozygous individuals that always produce offspring like themselves when bred together |

Letters are used for alleles. The dominant allele is shown with a capital letter and the recessive with the same letter in lower case. In peas, let **R** be the allele for round seeds and **r** the allele for wrinkled seeds. The genotypes are:

| Genotype | Description | Phenotype |
|---|---|---|
| RR | Homozygous dominant | Round |
| Rr | Heterozygous | Round |
| rr | Homozygous recessive | Wrinkled |

Two **identical homozygous** individuals that breed together will be **pure-breeding**: RR x RR gives only RR, and rr x rr gives only rr.

## Monohybrid crosses

A monohybrid cross follows the inheritance of one gene. Set out every cross in the same way.

1. State the key (what the letters mean).
2. Write the parental phenotypes and genotypes.
3. Write the gametes (each gamete carries one allele).
4. Draw the Punnett square to show all combinations.
5. State the genotypes and phenotypes of the offspring, and the ratio.

### Worked example 1: heterozygous x heterozygous (3 : 1)

*Cross two round-seeded pea plants that are both heterozygous.*

Key: R = round, r = wrinkled.

Parents: Rr x Rr. Gametes: R or r from each parent.

| | R | r |
|---|---|---|
| **R** | RR | Rr |
| **r** | Rr | rr |

Offspring genotypes: 1 RR : 2 Rr : 1 rr. Phenotypes: 3 round : 1 wrinkled. The **phenotype ratio is 3 : 1**, and the probability that an offspring is wrinkled is 1 in 4, or 25 per cent.

### Worked example 2: heterozygous x homozygous recessive (1 : 1)

*Cross a heterozygous round plant with a wrinkled plant.*

Parents: Rr x rr. Gametes: R or r from one parent; r only from the other.

| | r | r |
|---|---|---|
| **R** | Rr | Rr |
| **r** | rr | rr |

Offspring: 2 Rr : 2 rr, which is 1 round : 1 wrinkled. The **phenotype ratio is 1 : 1**.

### Worked example 3: finding the unknown genotype

*A round pea plant is crossed with a wrinkled plant. Half the offspring are wrinkled. What is the genotype of the round parent?*

The wrinkled plant is rr. Wrinkled offspring (rr) must have received an r from each parent, so the round parent carries an r. Because it is round it also has R. So the round parent is **Rr**. If the round parent had been RR, all offspring would be round.

## Why observed ratios differ from expected ratios

A 3 : 1 ratio is a probability, not a guarantee. At fertilisation, which sperm fertilises which egg is random, like tossing a coin. With a large number of offspring the ratio is close to the expected value. With a small number it can differ a lot.

### Worked example 4: expected and observed

*A cross of Rr x Rr produced 40 offspring: 33 round and 7 wrinkled.*

1. Expected: 3/4 round = 30 and 1/4 wrinkled = 10.
2. Observed: 33 and 7. The numbers differ from the expected, but only by chance.
3. Observed ratio = 33 : 7, about 4.7 : 1.

With 4 offspring, the result could easily be 4 : 0 or 2 : 2. This is why genetic ratios are only reliable with large numbers.

## Codominance and ABO blood groups

In **codominance**, both alleles are expressed in the phenotype of a heterozygous individual, with neither being dominant over the other. The ABO blood groups are controlled by one gene with three alleles: **I^A**, **I^B** and **I^o**. (They are written IA, IB and Io here.) IA and IB are codominant. Io is recessive to both.

| Phenotype | Possible genotypes |
|---|---|
| Group A | IAIA or IAIo |
| Group B | IBIB or IBIo |
| Group AB | IAIB |
| Group O | IoIo |

### Worked example 5: ABO cross

*A man with group A (heterozygous) and a woman with group B (heterozygous) have children. Predict the possible blood groups.*

Parents: IAIo x IBIo. Gametes: IA or Io; IB or Io.

| | IB | Io |
|---|---|---|
| **IA** | IAIB | IAIo |
| **Io** | IBIo | IoIo |

The offspring could be group AB, A, B or O, in a ratio of 1 : 1 : 1 : 1. Each has a 25 per cent probability. Group AB shows codominance: both alleles are expressed.

## Sex determination in humans

Humans have 23 pairs of chromosomes. One pair is the **sex chromosomes**. Females are **XX** and males are **XY**.

| | X | X |
|---|---|---|
| **X** | XX | XX |
| **Y** | XY | XY |

(Mother's gametes across the top, father's down the side.) Each child has a 50 per cent chance of being a girl (XX) and 50 per cent of being a boy (XY), a 1 : 1 ratio. The sex of the child is decided by the father's sperm, because the sperm carries either X or Y, whereas all eggs carry X.

## Mutation

**A gene mutation is a random change in the base sequence of DNA.** An example is **sickle cell anaemia**. A change in one gene leads to a faulty haemoglobin protein, which makes red blood cells curve into a sickle shape. These cells carry oxygen less well and can block capillaries.

**A chromosome mutation is a change in the chromosome number or structure.** An example is **Down's syndrome**, where a person has **47 chromosomes** instead of 46, with an extra copy of one chromosome.

**Ionising radiation** (such as X-rays and gamma rays) and **some chemicals** increase the rate of mutation.

## Sources of genetic variation

Four things produce genetic variation in a population: **mutation**, **meiosis** (which shuffles genes into gametes), **random mating** (any male may mate with any female) and **random fertilisation** (any sperm may fertilise any egg).

## Worked example 6: a family tree question

*Two parents with normal skin pigmentation have a child with albinism, a recessive condition. Let N be the allele for normal pigmentation and n the allele for albinism.*

1. The child has albinism, so the child's genotype is nn. The child received one n from each parent.
2. Both parents have normal pigmentation, so each has at least one N. They also each carry an n. So both parents are **Nn** (heterozygous carriers).
3. The cross Nn x Nn gives 1 NN : 2 Nn : 1 nn. The probability that another child has albinism is 1 in 4, or 25 per cent.
4. The probability that another child has normal pigmentation is 3 in 4, or 75 per cent. Two thirds of those with normal pigmentation are carriers (2 out of 3).

This is a common type of reasoning: from the phenotype of the child you work backwards to the genotypes of the parents.

## Comparing the types of mutation

| Feature | Gene mutation | Chromosome mutation |
|---|---|---|
| What changes | The base sequence of DNA in a gene | The number or structure of chromosomes |
| Example | Sickle cell anaemia | Down's syndrome |
| Effect | A different protein may be made | The genes are in the wrong number; development is affected |
| Chromosome number | Unchanged (46 in a human) | Changed (47 in Down's syndrome) |

Mutations are **random**: they can happen anywhere in the DNA, and nobody can say which gene will be changed. Most mutations in body cells are not passed on to offspring; only mutations in the cells that make gametes are inherited.

## Writing a full genetic diagram

A complete answer in an exam has a recognisable layout, and each element can earn a mark.

- **Key:** which letter stands for which allele and which is dominant.
- **Parent phenotypes and genotypes:** for example "round Rr x wrinkled rr".
- **Gametes:** circled or listed, one allele each.
- **Punnett square or lines:** every combination shown.
- **Offspring genotypes and phenotypes:** counted and written as a ratio.

Even if you make a mistake in one stage, the other stages can still earn marks, provided that they follow from what you have written.

## Common mistakes

- Writing the gametes of Rr as "Rr". Each gamete has one allele: R or r.
- Writing the ratio as 3 : 1 for genotypes. The phenotype ratio is 3 : 1; the genotype ratio is 1 : 2 : 1.
- Saying that a recessive allele is rare or weak. It is only masked when a dominant allele is present.
- Forgetting the key.
- Saying that the 3 : 1 ratio always appears in small families.
- Writing "heterozygous" when "homozygous" is meant.
- Saying that Down's syndrome has 45 chromosomes. It has 47.
- Writing that the mother determines the sex of the child. The sperm does.

## Command words

- **Define**: give the exact definition of a term such as allele.
- **Use**: apply the terms in a genetic diagram.
- **Predict**: use a Punnett square to give the ratios.
- **Explain**: give the reason, such as why ratios differ from expected.
