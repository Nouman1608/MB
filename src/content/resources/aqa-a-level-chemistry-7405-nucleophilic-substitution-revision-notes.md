## What you must be able to do

- Explain why halogenoalkanes are attacked by nucleophiles (**polar C–X bond**).
- Give the reagents, conditions and products for substitution with **OH⁻, CN⁻ and NH₃**.
- **Outline the mechanism** for each of these reactions.
- **Explain** why carbon–halogen **bond enthalpy** sets the rate of reaction.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Halogenoalkane | Alkane with one or more H atoms replaced by a halogen atom |
| Polar bond | Bond where the shared pair is unequally shared, giving δ+ and δ− ends |
| Nucleophile | Species that donates a lone pair to form a new covalent bond |
| Substitution | One atom or group replaced by another |
| Hydrolysis | Breaking a bond using water or hydroxide ions |
| Curly arrow | Shows the movement of an electron pair, from the pair to where it goes |
| Bond enthalpy | Energy needed to break one mole of a bond in gaseous molecules |

## Why the reaction happens

Halogens are more electronegative than carbon, so the C–X bond is polar: **Cᵟ⁺–Xᵟ⁻**. The δ+ carbon attracts a nucleophile. The nucleophile forms a bond to carbon, and the halogen leaves as X⁻.

## The three reactions

| Nucleophile | Reagent and conditions | Product | Example equation |
|---|---|---|---|
| OH⁻ | Aqueous NaOH or KOH, warm or reflux | Alcohol | CH₃CH₂Br + OH⁻ → CH₃CH₂OH + Br⁻ |
| CN⁻ | KCN in ethanol and water, reflux | Nitrile | CH₃CH₂Br + CN⁻ → CH₃CH₂CN + Br⁻ |
| NH₃ | Excess NH₃ in ethanol, sealed tube, heat | Primary amine | CH₃CH₂Br + 2NH₃ → CH₃CH₂NH₂ + NH₄Br |

Points to remember:

- Hydroxide must be **aqueous**. Ethanolic hydroxide leads to elimination.
- Cyanide adds **one carbon** to the chain. Bromoethane gives propanenitrile.
- Ammonia must be in **excess**, so that the amine product does not react further.
- Ammonia needs two molecules per halogenoalkane: one substitutes, one removes H⁺.

## Mechanism checklist

1. Draw the halogenoalkane with the **dipole** (δ+ on C, δ− on X).
2. Arrow 1 starts on the **lone pair** of the nucleophile and ends on the δ+ carbon.
3. Arrow 2 starts on the **C–X bond** and ends on X.
4. Show the product and the halide ion with its **negative charge**.

Which atom donates the lone pair?

| Nucleophile | Atom with the lone pair |
|---|---|
| OH⁻ | Oxygen |
| CN⁻ | Carbon |
| NH₃ | Nitrogen |

For ammonia, the first product is R–NH₃⁺. A second NH₃ takes an H⁺ to form R–NH₂ and NH₄⁺.

## Rate and bond enthalpy

| Bond | Mean bond enthalpy / kJ mol⁻¹ (approx.) | Relative rate |
|---|---|---|
| C–F | 467 | Extremely slow |
| C–Cl | 346 | Slowest of the three |
| C–Br | 290 | Intermediate |
| C–I | 228 | Fastest |

**Rate order: iodoalkane > bromoalkane > chloroalkane.**

Model explanation: the C–X bond must break in the reaction. The C–I bond is the weakest, so it needs the least energy to break, and the reaction has the lowest activation energy, so it is fastest.

Do not use polarity as the reason. C–Cl is the most polar of these bonds but reacts the slowest.

## Silver nitrate rate experiment

| Step | Detail |
|---|---|
| Reaction | R–X + H₂O → R–OH + H⁺ + X⁻ |
| Detection | Ag⁺(aq) + X⁻(aq) → AgX(s) |
| Solvent | Ethanol, so the halogenoalkane and water mix |
| Controls | Same carbon skeleton, volumes, concentrations and temperature |
| Measure | Time for the precipitate to appear |
| Colours | AgCl white, AgBr cream, AgI yellow |

The shortest time is the fastest hydrolysis, which is the weakest C–X bond.

## Quick calculations

- Moles of halide from a precipitate: n(halogenoalkane) = n(X⁻) = n(AgX).
- Percentage yield = actual mass ÷ theoretical mass × 100.
- Theoretical mass = moles of limiting reagent × Mᵣ of product (1 : 1 here).

Example: 0.0500 mol of bromoethane gives at most 0.0500 mol of propanenitrile, which is 0.0500 × 55.0 = 2.75 g.

## Exam traps

- Arrow 1 drawn from the H of OH⁻, not from the lone pair on O.
- Arrow 2 drawn from X to C instead of from the bond to X.
- Missing dipole, missing lone pair, or no charge on the halide ion.
- Rate trend explained by polarity.
- "Ethanolic" and "aqueous" swapped.
- Open flask for ammonia (it escapes; use a sealed tube).
- Calling the nucleophile "an electron-loving species".
- Cyanide attacking through nitrogen.
- Ignoring the ethanol solvent or the controls in the rate experiment.

## Self-test

1. What is a nucleophile?
2. Why is the carbon in a C–Br bond δ+?
3. Give the reagent and conditions to convert 1-bromopropane into propan-1-ol.
4. Name the product of bromoethane with KCN, and say how the chain length changes.
5. Why is ammonia used in large excess?
6. State where arrow 1 starts and ends in the hydroxide mechanism.
7. Which atom in CN⁻ attacks the carbon?
8. Put 1-chlorobutane, 1-bromobutane and 1-iodobutane in order of reactivity.
9. Explain the order in question 8.
10. What colour is the precipitate from 1-bromobutane in the silver nitrate experiment?
11. Why is ethanol added in that experiment?
12. How many moles of silver chloride form from 0.0300 mol of 1-chloropropane after complete hydrolysis?

### Answers

1. A species that donates a lone pair of electrons to form a covalent bond.
2. Bromine is more electronegative than carbon, so the bonding pair is drawn towards bromine.
3. Aqueous sodium or potassium hydroxide, warmed or heated under reflux.
4. Propanenitrile; the chain is one carbon longer.
5. To prevent the amine formed from reacting with more halogenoalkane, and because the second ammonia removes H⁺.
6. From the lone pair on oxygen to the δ+ carbon.
7. Carbon.
8. Iodo (fastest), then bromo, then chloro (slowest).
9. The C–I bond is the weakest, so it breaks most easily; bond enthalpy, not polarity, decides.
10. Cream.
11. It lets the halogenoalkane and aqueous silver nitrate mix into one solution.
12. 0.0300 mol.
