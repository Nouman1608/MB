This guide covers section **3.1.3 Setting business aims and objectives** of OxfordAQA International GCSE Business (9225), part of Topic 1, Business in the real world. The specification asks you to understand the main aims and objectives of businesses: **survival, profit maximisation, growth (domestic and international), market share, customer satisfaction, social and ethical objectives (including religious and cultural obligations) and shareholder value**. You need to explain the role of objectives in running a business, why objectives differ between businesses and change over time, how public sector objectives differ, how success can be measured in ways other than profit, why governments encourage enterprise, and why new businesses fail. All businesses and figures are invented.

For the whole topic, see the [Business in the Real World study guide](/resources/igcse-oxfordaqa-business-business-in-the-real-world/). Condensed notes are in the [revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-business-9225-setting-business-aims-and-objectives-revision-notes/) and questions are in the [practice set](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-business-9225-setting-business-aims-and-objectives-practice/).

## Aims and objectives

An **aim** is the long-term goal of a business, such as becoming the best-known coffee chain in the region. An **objective** is a specific target that helps the business move towards the aim, such as opening three new shops this year. Objectives are most useful when they are clear, measurable and have a deadline. "Increase sales by 10% in the next 12 months" is better than "sell more".

### Why set objectives?

- **Direction:** everyone knows what the business is trying to achieve.
- **Motivation:** staff work towards targets, and meeting them gives a sense of achievement.
- **Decision making:** managers can compare options against the objective. If the objective is growth, they may accept lower profit now to open a new branch.
- **Measuring success:** results can be compared with targets to show whether the business is succeeding.
- **Coordination:** departments such as marketing, finance and operations can work together towards the same goal.
- **Support for finance:** lenders and investors often ask what the business is aiming to achieve.

## The main objectives in the specification

| Objective | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Survival | Keeping the business going, typical in the early years or in hard times | A new cafe aims to cover its costs for the first year |
| Profit maximisation | Making as much profit as possible, by raising revenue or cutting costs | A shoe manufacturer raises prices and cuts waste |
| Growth (domestic and international) | Increasing the size of the business in its home country or abroad | A bakery opens a second shop; a clothing firm starts selling in another country |
| Market share | The percentage of total market sales that the business holds | A phone seller wants a larger share of phone sales |
| Customer satisfaction | Meeting or beating customers' expectations | A hotel aims for 90% of guests to rate it good or excellent |
| Social and ethical objectives | Behaving responsibly towards people, communities and the environment, including religious and cultural obligations | A shop that closes on a day of worship, or sources fair-trade goods |
| Shareholder value | Increasing the value of the owners' shares and dividends | A company aims to raise the share price and dividend each year |

### Market share calculation

Market share is calculated as:

market share (%) = business sales / total market sales x 100

**Worked example 1: market share.** Brightway sells $2.4 million of bicycles in a market worth $12 million.

```
Market share = 2.4 / 12 x 100 = 20%
```

Brightway sets an objective of 25% market share. If the market stays at $12 million, it needs sales of 25% x $12 million = $3.0 million, an increase of $0.6 million. If the market grows, it needs more.

**Worked example 2: growth.** Brightway's sales rise from $800,000 to $920,000 in a year.

```
Growth = (920,000 - 800,000) / 800,000 x 100 = 15%
```

Growth does not always bring profit, since costs may rise faster than revenue, so growth and profit objectives can conflict.

## Why objectives differ between businesses

The specification names three reasons:

- **Size.** A small business often wants to survive and earn enough to live on. A large business may aim to become the market leader.
- **Level of competition.** Where many rivals compete, a business may focus on market share or customer satisfaction. A business with little competition may feel able to aim for high profit.
- **Type of business.** A profit-making business may aim for profit and growth. A not-for-profit organisation such as a charity measures success by the number of people it helps.

## Why objectives change

As a business develops, its objectives change.

**Larger, more established businesses** may aim to become the dominant firm in the market, expand internationally, increase shareholder value and meet higher ethical and environmental expectations. **A start-up** often aims first to survive and build a customer base.

Objectives may also change when the business environment changes. In a recession a firm may switch from growth to survival. New laws or customer concern about the environment may lead a firm to add environmental objectives, such as cutting plastic packaging by half.

**Worked example 3: a changing objective.** Zola Juice started with a street stall. Year one: survive by covering costs of $30,000. Year three: profit of $55,000 and four stalls, so the objective changes to growth by adding two more stalls and selling to local shops. Year seven: a national company with shareholders, whose objectives are to raise market share to 15% and sell to another country. The business is the same idea, but the objectives have changed as the business has grown.

## Public sector objectives

Public sector organisations are owned by the government and usually exist to provide a service rather than make a profit. Their objectives might be to provide healthcare to everyone, to keep costs within the budget set by the government, to provide the service to a standard, or to reduce waiting times. They are judged on how well they meet these targets rather than how much profit they make.

## Using objectives to judge success

Comparing results with objectives shows whether the business is succeeding. Success is not just profit. A business could be judged successful if it:

- reaches its market share target
- has a high level of customer satisfaction
- survives its first three years
- keeps its staff, and meets its ethical or environmental targets
- helps the community, for a not-for-profit organisation.

A business making a loss might still judge itself successful if its main objective is growth and sales are rising quickly.

## Government support for enterprise

Governments often encourage new businesses because they **create jobs**, increase **tax revenue**, provide **new products** and strengthen the economy. Support can take the form of:

- **grants or cheap loans** to start up
- **training and advice** for new entrepreneurs
- **tax breaks** or lower taxes on small firms
- **business support centres** and help with planning.

## Why new businesses fail

Many start-ups close within a few years. Typical reasons are:

- **Poor cash flow:** money runs out before enough customers have paid.
- **Lack of planning or market research:** the owner assumes demand without checking.
- **Poor management:** inexperienced owners make costly mistakes.
- **Too much competition:** rivals have a head start or lower prices.
- **Too little finance:** the owner underestimates start-up costs.
- **Changes in the economy or the law:** costs rise or customers spend less.

**Worked example 4: a failing start-up.** Tomas opens a juice bar and sells 200 juices a day at $3, so revenue is 200 x $3 = $600 a day. His daily costs are $520. The profit is $80 a day. A rival opens next door and Tomas loses 40% of his customers. His sales fall to 120 juices, which brings in $360 a day, below his $520 costs, giving a daily loss of $160. Without a plan for extra finance, the business is unlikely to survive.

## Common mistakes

- Saying every business aims to maximise profit. Many want to survive or grow first, and not-for-profit organisations do not aim for profit at all.
- Confusing an aim with an objective. An objective is specific and measurable.
- Giving objectives without linking them to the size, competition or type of the business in the case.
- Assuming a business with a loss has failed. Its objective may be growth or survival.
- Forgetting that objectives can conflict. A firm that raises wages and pays for fair-trade supplies may earn less profit.

## Exam technique for this section

For **explain** questions, state the objective and give a reason it suits the business: a new restaurant may aim for survival because it needs to build customers and cannot afford losses. For **analyse** questions, link objectives to decisions and results. For **evaluate** questions, such as whether profit is the best measure of success, weigh profit against other measures and reach a judgement based on the type and stage of the business.
