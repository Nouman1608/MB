## 3.3.1 a: the Sun as the energy source

Almost all the energy in most communities began as radiation from the Sun. Plants and algae absorb light and use it in photosynthesis (see the [bioenergetics guide](/resources/igcse-oxfordaqa-biology-bioenergetics/)). They transfer only **about 1%** of the incident light energy. That energy is stored in the substances that make up their cells: glucose, starch, cellulose, proteins and lipids.

Why so little? Typical reasons are that some light is reflected, some passes straight through leaves, some lands on parts without chloroplasts, and some wavelengths are not absorbed. These reasons are common explanations rather than a required list, so the number matters more than the reasons.

**Worked example 1.** A field receives 3.2 × 10⁶ kJ of light energy per m² in a year. Plants store about 1% of it.

Energy stored = 3.2 × 10⁶ × 0.01 = **3.2 × 10⁴ kJ per m²** (32 000 kJ).

## 3.3.1 b: why only about 10% moves up

Only approximately 10% of the biomass from each trophic level is transferred to the level above. The rest is lost for two reasons:

- some materials and energy are always lost in waste materials (faeces, urine and, for plants, shed leaves that are not eaten);
- respiration supplies all the energy needed for living processes, including movement. Much of that energy is eventually transferred to the surroundings as heat.

Another point often used in answers is that some parts of an organism are not eaten, such as bones, shells or roots. Remember that the respired carbon leaves as carbon dioxide, so it is no longer biomass.

**Worked example 2.** Biomass in a pond was measured per hectare: algae 4 800 kg, water fleas 520 kg, minnows 61 kg.

Algae to water fleas: 520 ÷ 4 800 × 100 = 10.83, so **10.8%**.
Water fleas to minnows: 61 ÷ 520 × 100 = 11.73, so **11.7%**.

Both values are close to 10%, which fits the rule. The rule is an approximation, and real values vary between ecosystems.

**Worked example 3.** Using the 1% and 10% figures from example 1, the energy stored by plants is 3.2 × 10⁴ kJ per m². The next levels hold about:

| Level | Energy stored (kJ per m²) |
|---|---|
| Producers | 32 000 |
| Primary consumers (10%) | 3 200 |
| Secondary consumers (10%) | 320 |
| Tertiary consumers (10%) | 32 |

Because so little is left after three transfers, food chains rarely have more than four or five trophic levels.

## 3.3.1 c: pyramids of biomass

![Biomass pyramid with producer base and narrowing trophic levels, accompanied by the approximate 1% solar capture and 10% transfer values.](/images/learning/biology/bio-b16-biomass-energy-transfer-pyramid.svg)


A pyramid of biomass has one horizontal bar for each trophic level, with the producers at the base. Each bar is drawn to scale, so its length is proportional to the biomass at that level. The bars are centred on a vertical line.

### Constructing a pyramid

1. Choose a scale that lets the largest value fit on the page.
2. Divide every value by the scale to get each bar's length.
3. Draw the bars, producers at the bottom, all the same height, centred.
4. Label each bar with the organism or trophic level, and give the units and the scale.

**Worked example 4.** Grassland biomass (dry mass, kg per hectare): grass 3 000, grasshoppers 360, frogs 45.

Use a scale of 1 mm = 50 kg.

- Grass: 3 000 ÷ 50 = **60 mm**
- Grasshoppers: 360 ÷ 50 = **7.2 mm**
- Frogs: 45 ÷ 50 = **0.9 mm**

The frog bar is under 1 mm. That is correct, not a mistake: the pyramid is narrow because each transfer is about 10%. Here the transfers are 360 ÷ 3 000 = 12.0% and 45 ÷ 360 = 12.5%.

### Interpreting a pyramid

- A wide base and narrowing bars show that biomass falls at each level.
- The ratio of one bar to the one below is the percentage transferred.
- The biomass is usually dry mass, because water content varies and dry mass is a better measure of the material that has been built.
- Say what the pyramid represents. A bar shows the biomass at the time of sampling.

## 3.3.1 d: efficiency of food production (B)

Each extra stage in a food chain loses most of the biomass and energy. Reducing the number of stages therefore increases the proportion of the producers' energy that reaches humans. Eating plants directly (one step) is more efficient than feeding the plants to livestock and eating the meat (two steps).

**Worked example 5.** A hectare of crop stores 7 500 MJ of edible energy.

- Eaten directly by people: 7 500 MJ.
- Fed to cattle, which retain about 10%: 7 500 × 0.1 = **750 MJ** in the meat.

If a person needs 10 MJ per day, the crop feeds 7 500 ÷ 10 = **750 person-days**, but the meat feeds 750 ÷ 10 = **75 person-days**. Cutting out the cattle stage gives ten times as much human food from the same land.

A balanced answer also notes that some land (steep hillsides, poor soil) suits grazing animals better than crops, and that meat supplies protein. The question usually asks only for the efficiency argument.

## 3.3.2 a to c: needs and competition

To survive and reproduce, organisms need a supply of materials from their surroundings and from other living organisms.

| | Plants | Animals |
|---|---|---|
| From surroundings | Light, carbon dioxide, water, mineral ions, space | Oxygen, water, shelter, space |
| From other organisms | Pollination and seed dispersal by animals | Food (plants or other animals), mates |

Where supplies are limited, organisms **compete**.

- **Plants** compete for light and space, and for water and nutrients (mineral ions) from the soil.
- **Animals** compete for food, mates and territory.

Competition can be between members of the same species or of different species. Organisms also depend on each other: a predator needs prey, and a flowering plant may need an insect to carry its pollen. This is **interdependence**.

**Worked example 6.** Mustard seedlings were grown in trays at four densities. The mean dry mass per plant was measured after four weeks.

| Seedlings per tray | Mean dry mass per plant (g) | Total dry mass per tray (g) |
|---|---|---|
| 10 | 0.92 | 9.2 |
| 20 | 0.61 | 12.2 |
| 40 | 0.34 | 13.6 |
| 80 | 0.19 | 15.2 |

Total mass = number × mean mass (for example 80 × 0.19 = 15.2 g).

Percentage decrease in mean mass from 10 to 80 seedlings: (0.92 − 0.19) ÷ 0.92 × 100 = **79.3%**.

Explanation: crowded seedlings compete for light, water and mineral ions, so each plant has less to photosynthesise and grow with. The total per tray rises only slowly (9.2 g to 15.2 g) while seedling number rises eightfold, so each extra plant adds less and less.

## 3.3.2 d to f: adaptations

An **adaptation** is a feature that enables an organism, including a microorganism, to survive in the conditions in which it normally lives. There are three kinds.

| Type | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Structural | Shape, size or colour | A flattened flea body; a white coat in snow; a thick waxy cuticle |
| Behavioural | What the organism does | Migration; emperor penguins huddling; sheltering in burrows at midday |
| Functional | Processes such as reproduction and metabolism | Concentrated urine in a desert rodent; producing very many eggs; flowering soon after rain |

The types overlap. Hibernation is a behaviour, but it works by lowering metabolic rate. Give the type the question asks about, with a reason.

### Extremophiles

Some organisms live where conditions are extreme, such as very high salt levels, high temperatures or high pressures. These are **extremophiles**. Examples: archaea in salt lakes that tolerate salt concentrations that would make water leave the cells of most organisms; bacteria such as *Thermus aquaticus* in hot springs at around 70 °C; and microorganisms living deep in the ocean under great pressure. Their proteins and cell structures work in conditions that would damage those of most organisms.

### Using information to suggest adaptations

You may be given facts about an animal or plant and asked how it is adapted. Use three steps: identify the **problem** the habitat creates, name the **feature**, then say how it **helps survival**.

**Worked example 7.** A sand-dune grass has leaves that roll inwards, and its stomata sit in pits lined with hairs.

- Problem: dunes are windy and dry, so water vapour is easily lost.
- Feature: rolled leaves and hair-lined pits trap a layer of moist air.
- Benefit: this slows the diffusion of water vapour out of the leaf, so less water is lost by transpiration.

That is a structural adaptation.

## Parasite adaptations (B)

A parasite lives on or in a host and gains food or shelter at the host's expense.

| Parasite | Where it lives | Adaptations and why they help |
|---|---|---|
| Flea | Among the hair of mammals | Body flattened from side to side so it moves between hairs; claws grip hair; piercing mouthparts feed on blood; strong legs for jumping between hosts |
| Tapeworm | Inside the intestines of mammals | Hooks and suckers on the head hold it to the gut wall; a thick outer covering resists digestive enzymes; a long flat body gives a large surface area to absorb digested food; no digestive system is needed; segments full of eggs give huge reproductive output |
| Malaria parasite | In humans (single-celled) | Small enough to live inside liver and red blood cells; reproduces rapidly inside them; passed between people by a mosquito, which is its vector |

For a full-mark answer, join feature to function: say what the feature is, then how it helps the parasite feed, stay attached or spread.

## Common errors

- Writing that energy is "used up" or "lost". It is transferred to the surroundings as heat.
- Saying 10% of animals are eaten. The 10% refers to biomass (or energy) passing to the next level.
- Drawing a pyramid with unequal bar heights, no scale or no labels.
- Claiming that animals compete for light, or that plants compete for mates. Match the resource to the organism.
- Describing a feature without saying how it helps survival.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why do food chains rarely have more than four or five trophic levels?**
Only about 10% of the biomass passes to each level above, so after three or four transfers too little is left to support another level.

**Why is eating crops directly more efficient than eating meat?**
Each extra stage in a food chain loses about 90% of the biomass, so fewer stages means more of the producers' biomass reaches humans. A balanced answer also notes that some land suits grazing better than crops and that meat supplies protein.

**What are the three types of adaptation?**
Adaptations can be structural (a body feature such as camouflage colouring), behavioural (what the organism does, such as huddling or migrating) or functional (body processes, such as producing many eggs). The types can overlap.

**Why is dry mass used for pyramids of biomass?**
Water content varies, so dry mass is a better measure of the material an organism has actually built. A bar shows the biomass at the time of sampling.

**Which sections of OxfordAQA 9201 Biology does this topic cover?**
Section 3.3.1 covers energy transfer, pyramids of biomass and the efficiency of food production, and section 3.3.2 covers needs, competition and adaptations, including extremophiles and parasites.
