Condensed recall for one angle in the 9675 Unit 2 content: "the significance of the natural world,
its beauty and possibly its despoliation". It supports the poetry essay set out in the
[Unit 2 Section B study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-english-literature-9675-unit-2-section-b/);
the full discussion and a model paragraph are in the
[natural world and despoliation study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-english-literature-9675-natural-world-and-despoliation/).

## The idea in one box

- Three parts to the phrase: **significance**, **beauty**, **despoliation**.
- "Possibly" means damage may be present, implied or absent; each is arguable.
- Section B is **one essay, 25 marks, open book**, usually on more than one poem.
- Integrate poems around a shared question rather than writing about them one by one.

## Four relationships with nature

| Relationship | What the poem does | Example from the selections |
|---|---|---|
| Teacher | Landscape shapes mind and conscience | "Tintern Abbey" |
| Mirror | Landscape reflects mood, loss or an age | "Neutral Tones", "Desert Places", "The Darkling Thrush" |
| Victim | Human action damages the place | "Nutting", "A Brook in the City" |
| Indifferent force | Nature ignores or defeats human hopes | "The Convergence of the Twain", "The Wood-Pile" |

## Key poems by poet

**Wordsworth**
- "Tintern Abbey": remembered landscape as lasting moral support.
- "Nutting": a boy's destruction of a hazel bower, followed by a sense of pain and a warning.
- "Michael": land threatened by debt and the pull of the city; the unfinished sheepfold.
- "Composed Upon Westminster Bridge": beauty found in a city at dawn.

**Frost**
- "A Brook in the City": a stream buried by building.
- "The Wood-Pile": abandoned labour decaying in a frozen swamp.
- "Out, Out –": a farm saw kills a boy amid mountain beauty.
- "Desert Places": snow as outer and inner emptiness.

**Hardy**
- "The Darkling Thrush": winter landscape at the century's turn; a song of hope the speaker doubts.
- "Neutral Tones": a drained winter pond as the image of a dead love.
- "Channel Firing": gunnery disturbing the coastal dead.
- "In Time of 'The Breaking of Nations'": rural life outlasting war.

**Heaney**
- "Bogland": the bog as a preserving, downward-reaching ground.
- Bog poems: land that keeps bodies and history.
- "Requiem for the Croppies": growth from rebels' graves.
- "The Toome Road": armoured vehicles intruding on a country road.

## Pairings to revise

- "Nutting" with "Tintern Abbey": nature as victim and as teacher in one poet.
- "A Brook in the City" with "The Wood-Pile": people burying nature; nature reclaiming people's work.
- "Channel Firing" with "The Darkling Thrush": war and history read through landscape.
- "Bogland" with "The Toome Road": land preserving history; land intruded on by force.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning | Use it to say |
|---|---|---|
| Despoliation | Spoiling or plundering of a place | How human action damages nature |
| Pastoral | Writing that idealises rural life | Whether a poem trusts or questions rural peace |
| Pathetic fallacy | Landscape given human feeling | How setting mirrors a speaker's state |
| Personification | Human qualities given to nature | Why damage reads as injury |
| Sublime | Awe mixed with fear before vast nature | Nature as overwhelming force |
| Volta | A turn in a poem's argument | Where delight becomes guilt, or despair hope |
| Semantic field | A group of related words | Violence against growth |
| Elegy | A poem of mourning | Loss of a place or a way of life |

## Methods to look for

- Verbs of damage (tear, bury, fire) against verbs of growth or rest.
- A structural turn from delight to regret, or from bleakness to hope.
- Sound: harsh consonants for destruction; slower sounds for stillness.
- Viewpoint: child, remembering adult, passer-by, the dead.
- Final lines that leave nature unchanged by, or indifferent to, the human event.

## Exam traps

- Describing scenery without arguing its significance.
- Assuming every nature poem celebrates nature.
- Ignoring "possibly": a poem with no damage can still be discussed.
- Biographical context with no link to a line.
- Separate mini-essays on each poem instead of an integrated comparison.

## Self-test

1. Quote the phrase from the 9675 Unit 2 content that names this angle.
2. What does "despoliation" mean?
3. Why does the word "possibly" matter in the specification's phrase?
4. Name the four relationships with nature used in these notes.
5. What happens in Wordsworth's "Nutting"?
6. What threatens the land in "Michael"?
7. How does "A Brook in the City" present despoliation?
8. What does "The Wood-Pile" suggest about human labour and nature?
9. How does "Channel Firing" connect war and landscape?
10. What does the bog do in Heaney's "Bogland"?
11. Define a volta and explain its use for this angle.
12. Give one good pairing for an essay on this angle and say why it works.

## Answers

1. "The significance of the natural world, its beauty and possibly its despoliation".
2. The spoiling, plundering or destruction of a place.
3. Not every poem shows damage, so a poem may present it, imply it or leave it out, and each is
   arguable.
4. Teacher, mirror, victim, indifferent force.
5. A boy finds an untouched hazel bower, tears it down, then feels a sense of pain, and the poem warns
   that there is a spirit in the woods.
6. Debt and the pull of the city, which draws the shepherd's son away; the sheepfold is left
   unfinished.
7. A stream is buried under urban building, and the poem asks whether it still troubles the city.
8. Abandoned work is slowly reclaimed by decay, so nature absorbs human effort without regard for it.
9. Naval gunnery wakes the dead in a coastal churchyard, so war disturbs the land and its history.
10. It preserves the past and reaches downward, unlike a prairie that spreads outward.
11. A turn in a poem's argument or mood; it often marks the shift from delight to regret or from
    bleakness to hope.
12. Any justified pairing, for example "Nutting" with "Tintern Abbey", showing the same poet presenting
    nature as victim and as teacher.
