This guide covers section 11.4 of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Geography (9696) syllabus for examination in 2025 and 2026. It is a case study: candidates "must study the industrial policy for one country's manufacturing and consequent changes in the character, location and organisation of its manufacturing, showing some of the issues faced and evaluate the attempted solutions". It is part of Topic 11, Production, location and change, in Paper 4.

The [Topic 11 study guide](/resources/a-level-geography-9696-production-location-and-change/) gives Malaysia as a full case study. This page shows how to organise any 11.4 case study around the three syllabus words "character, location and organisation", and works through South Korea as a second example. Condense it with the [11.4 revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-the-management-of-change-in-manufacturing-industry-revision-notes/) and practise with the [11.4 practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-the-management-of-change-in-manufacturing-industry-practice/).

## Unpacking the syllabus wording

| Phrase | What to include |
|---|---|
| "industrial policy" | Deliberate government action: plans, incentives, zones, protection, state firms, credit, training |
| "character" | What is made, how (labour- or capital-intensive), and at what value |
| "location" | Where manufacturing is, and how the pattern shifted |
| "organisation" | Who owns and runs it: state, private conglomerates, small firms, foreign TNCs |
| "issues faced" | Problems that arose: debt, inequality, pollution, labour conflict, competition |
| "evaluate the attempted solutions" | Judge what worked, for whom, and for how long |

A strong case study shows how each policy changed all three of character, location and organisation, rather than narrating dates.

## Types of industrial policy

| Policy type | What it does | Typical tools |
|---|---|---|
| Import substitution | Builds industry to supply the home market | Tariffs, quotas, licences |
| Export orientation | Makes industry compete in world markets | Export zones, tax relief, cheap credit for exporters |
| Sector targeting | Chooses strategic industries | State loans, state-owned firms, research funding |
| Regional policy | Spreads industry to lagging regions | Grants, new industrial complexes, relocating public bodies |
| Restructuring | Manages decline or upgrading | Retraining, closures, support for new sectors |

## Worked case study: South Korea

### Starting point

In the early 1960s South Korea was a low-income, largely rural economy with few natural resources. From 1962 the government ran a series of five-year economic development plans.

### Phase 1: export-oriented light industry (1960s)

- **Policy:** the state encouraged exports with cheap credit, tax relief and a favourable exchange rate. The Masan Free Export Zone was established in 1970 to attract foreign assembly firms.
- **Character:** labour-intensive goods such as textiles, clothing, footwear, wigs and plywood.
- **Location:** concentrated around Seoul and the port of Busan, where labour and port access were greatest.
- **Organisation:** many private firms, with the state directing bank credit towards exporters.

### Phase 2: heavy and chemical industry (1970s)

- **Policy:** the Heavy and Chemical Industry drive announced in 1973 targeted steel, shipbuilding, machinery, electronics, chemicals and non-ferrous metals.
- **Character:** capital-intensive heavy industry replaced light industry as the policy priority.
- **Location:** new planned industrial complexes on the south-east coast. The state steel company POSCO, founded in 1968, began producing at Pohang in 1973; Ulsan grew as a centre of shipbuilding, cars and petrochemicals; Changwon specialised in machinery; Gumi, inland, in electronics.
- **Organisation:** the state favoured large family-controlled business groups, the **chaebol** (such as Hyundai, Samsung, LG and Daewoo), with loans and licences.

### Phase 3: technology and upgrading (1980s onwards)

- **Policy:** support shifted towards research, education and high-technology sectors; import protection was gradually reduced.
- **Character:** semiconductors, consumer electronics, displays, cars and ships became leading exports; value added per worker rose.
- **Location:** high-technology production clustered in the Seoul metropolitan region, especially in Gyeonggi province south of Seoul, close to research institutes and skilled graduates.
- **Organisation:** chaebol became global TNCs, opening factories abroad.

### Issues faced and attempted solutions

| Issue | What happened | Attempted solution | Judgement |
|---|---|---|---|
| Debt and over-expansion | Heavily indebted chaebol were exposed in the 1997 Asian financial crisis; South Korea took an IMF rescue package, and Daewoo collapsed in 1999 | Restructuring: firms sold or merged, debt limits, stronger bank regulation | Recovery was quick, but job losses and greater insecurity of employment followed |
| Rising wages and labour conflict | Democratisation in 1987 brought strong trade unions and wage rises | Firms automated and moved labour-intensive work abroad, for example phone assembly in Vietnam | Kept firms competitive, but reduced manufacturing jobs at home |
| Regional imbalance | Industry and population concentrated on the Seoul–Busan axis; the south-west lagged | Regional complexes, relocation of public bodies, the new administrative city of Sejong | Some decentralisation of government, little of high-technology industry |
| Decline in old sectors | Shipbuilding downturns in the 2010s; General Motors closed its Gunsan car plant in 2018 | Designated crisis areas with support for workers and new firms | Softened the impact, but single-industry towns stayed vulnerable |
| Pollution | Heavy industry polluted coastal air and water | Environmental regulation and cleaner technology | Improved conditions over time, though air quality remains a concern |

### Evaluation

- **Character:** highly successful. Policy moved the country from cheap labour-intensive goods to high-value manufacturing within a few decades.
- **Location:** policy deliberately built new coastal complexes, but market forces and agglomeration have kept the most advanced industry around Seoul, so regional imbalance persists.
- **Organisation:** state support created world-class firms, but concentration in a few chaebol brought financial risk, as 1997 showed.
- **Overall:** a strong case of successful sector targeting, with weaker results for regional and employment goals.

## How to evaluate an industrial policy

| Criterion | Test |
|---|---|
| Growth | Did output, exports and value added rise? |
| Upgrading | Did the country move to higher-value products? |
| Distribution | Did benefits reach lagging regions and ordinary workers? |
| Resilience | Could firms survive crises and competition? |
| Ownership | Did domestic firms gain capability, or remain branch plants? |
| Environment | Were pollution costs controlled? |

Comparison helps judgement. Malaysia (in the Topic 11 guide) relied more on foreign TNCs, whereas South Korea built domestic conglomerates; that difference in organisation is a good basis for an evaluative paragraph.

## Annotated model paragraph

*Question: Evaluate the success of one country's industrial policy in changing the location of its manufacturing.*

> South Korea's 1973 heavy industry drive created new industrial locations on the south-east coast, such as Pohang for steel and Ulsan for ships and cars (**policy changes location, with named places**). These coastal sites suited imported raw materials and exports, so the policy worked with location factors rather than against them (**analysis**). However, later high-technology industry clustered south of Seoul near research and graduates (**counter-evidence**), and regional policy has not shifted it (**limit of policy**). Policy therefore changed location most when it matched transport advantages, and least when it tried to move knowledge-based industry (**judgement**).

## Common mistakes

- Narrating dates with no reference to character, location or organisation.
- Writing about trade or TNCs in general instead of the country's industrial policy.
- Ignoring issues, or listing them without the attempted solutions.
- Judging success only by growth, without distribution or resilience.
- Using two countries as equal case studies; use one, and compare briefly.

## Exam technique

- **Describe the changes**: organise by phase and by the three syllabus words.
- **Explain**: link each change to a policy and to location factors from 11.3.
- **Evaluate**: use criteria, vary the judgement by goal (growth, regions, workers), and conclude.
