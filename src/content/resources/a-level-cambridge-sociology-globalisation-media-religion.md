This guide covers **Paper 4 Globalisation, Media and Religion**, an A
Level-only paper in Cambridge International AS & A Level Sociology
(9699). AS Level candidates study Paper 1 and Paper 2; the full A
Level adds Paper 3 and Paper 4. Paper 4 has three named sections, and
candidates answer questions on 2 of the 3.

## Where this fits in 9699

Paper 4 is the final paper of the full A Level and brings together
three substantive areas — globalisation, media, and religion — that
each apply the theoretical concepts and research methods introduced
in Paper 1 (Socialisation, Identity and Methods of Research) to a
specific area of contemporary social life. Because candidates choose
2 of the 3 sections, Paper 4 allows some specialisation within the
qualification, though the syllabus still expects candidates to draw on
the same underlying sociological concepts and research methods
knowledge that runs through the whole course.

## Syllabus coverage

**CAMBRIDGE A LEVEL SOCIOLOGY (9699) — PAPER 4: GLOBALISATION, MEDIA
AND RELIGION**

- 4.1 Section A: Globalisation — the causes and consequences of
globalisation for societies, culture and identity
- 4.2 Section B: Media — the role and influence of media in society,
including its relationship to identity, culture and social control
- 4.3 Section C: Religion — the role of religion in society, and
sociological explanations for religious belief, organisation and
change

## How to approach it

Because candidates answer on only 2 of these 3 sections, a sensible
strategy is to study all three to a working level early in the course,
then narrow to genuinely deep revision on two sections closer to the
exam, keeping in mind that unexpectedly difficult questions on a
"safe" section can make a well-prepared third section valuable as a
fallback. For Globalisation (4.1), strong answers move beyond treating
globalisation as simply "the world becoming more connected" and can
discuss specific consequences for identity and culture — for instance,
tension between global cultural influences and the persistence or
revival of local and national identities — supported by named
sociological perspectives on globalisation rather than general
observation alone.

Media (4.2) rewards being able to discuss more than one theoretical
explanation of media influence — for example, contrasting views that
see media as shaping audience attitudes and behaviour directly against
views that see audiences as more actively selecting and interpreting
media content — and applying whichever perspective a question
foregrounds to a specific, named example rather than discussing media
influence only in the abstract. A frequent weakness is asserting that
media "affects behaviour" without engaging with the actual debate
among competing explanations the syllabus expects candidates to know.

Religion (4.3) similarly rewards weighing competing sociological
explanations against each other — for instance, whether religion
primarily functions to maintain social cohesion, or whether it can
also be a source of social change and conflict — rather than
describing a single view as though it were uncontested. Candidates
should also be ready to discuss secularisation (the extent to which
religion's social influence has declined) as a genuinely debated
question, with evidence and counter-evidence, rather than an assumed
fact.

Across all three sections, Paper 4 is best revised by consistently
linking back to research methods: for any claim about globalisation,
media or religion, being able to suggest how a sociologist might
actually research that claim, and to evaluate the strengths and
limitations of that method, connects this paper back to the methods
content examined in Paper 1 and demonstrates the kind of joined-up
sociological thinking this qualification rewards at its highest
level.

## Worked example: applying methods to a media question

A typical Paper 4 question might ask candidates to discuss the
influence of social media on how young people form their identities.
A weak answer describes social media's features and asserts that it
"has a big influence." A stronger answer selects a specific
sociological perspective on media influence, applies it directly to
identity formation among young people (for instance, discussing how
curated self-presentation online might reshape ideas of a stable,
singular identity), and then briefly considers how a sociologist could
investigate this claim — noting a suitable method and one limitation
of it for researching something as private and platform-dependent as
social media use. Linking a theoretical claim to a research method in
this way, even briefly, signals the joined-up understanding this paper
rewards, rather than treating Paper 4's content as disconnected from
Paper 1's methods.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- Discussing globalisation as a purely economic phenomenon without
  engaging with its cultural and identity-related consequences, which
  this paper specifically examines.
- Presenting one theory of media influence as simply true, rather than
  as one of several competing sociological explanations to be weighed.
- Treating secularisation as a settled fact rather than a genuinely
  debated question with evidence on more than one side.
- Answering with no reference at all to research methods, when Paper 4
  questions frequently reward candidates who connect a substantive
  claim to how it could actually be studied.

A final point worth building into revision is currency: all three
sections deal with rapidly changing areas of social life, so
supporting an answer with a specific, plausible contemporary example
(rather than only textbook cases from decades past) tends to make an
argument feel more genuinely engaged with the sociological debate
being assessed.
