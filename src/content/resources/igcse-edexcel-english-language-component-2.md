This guide covers **Component 2 Poetry and Prose Texts and Imaginative
Writing**, for Pearson Edexcel International GCSE English Language A
(4EA1), first teaching 2016, current specification Issue 6, 2023.
Component 2 assesses 40% of the total qualification, split evenly
between reading (20%) and writing (20%), and is the examined alternative
to Component 3's non-exam assessment.

## Where this fits in 4EA1

Component 1 (Non-fiction Texts and Transactional Writing) is compulsory
for every candidate; students then take EITHER Component 2 (the exam
route covered here) OR Component 3 (a coursework-style alternative
covering the same content areas). Where Component 1 works with
non-fiction, Component 2 shifts to poetry and prose fiction, and pairs
that reading with imaginative rather than transactional writing.

## Syllabus coverage

**PEARSON EDEXCEL INTERNATIONAL GCSE ENGLISH LANGUAGE A (4EA1) — COMPONENT 2**

- Section A: Poetry and Prose Texts — one 45-minute, 30-mark essay
question on a poetry or prose text from Part 2 of the Pearson Edexcel
International GCSE English Anthology, made available in the examination;
assessed against AO1 (reading and understanding a variety of texts,
selecting and interpreting information, ideas and perspectives) and AO2
(understanding and analysing how writers use linguistic and structural
devices to achieve their effects)
- Content preparation — studying the poetry and prose provided in Part 2
of the Anthology, developing the ability to read substantial, demanding
extended texts and the skills of inference and analysis; reading
additional prose fiction from a range of genres and cultures beyond the
Anthology itself is recommended
- Section B: Imaginative Writing — one 45-minute, 30-mark imaginative
writing task, chosen from three given prompts; assessed against AO4
(communicating effectively and imaginatively, adapting form, tone and
register for specific purposes and audiences) and AO5 (writing clearly,
using a range of vocabulary and sentence structures, with accurate
spelling, grammar and punctuation)
- Using what is learned from reading — the specification explicitly
expects students to use what they have learned about the writer's craft
from their reading of fiction to inspire and influence their own
imaginative writing, including developing planning and proofreading
skills

## How to approach it

Because Section A's question is set on a specific poem or prose text
from Part 2 of the Anthology (made available in the exam itself), deep
familiarity with the Anthology's Part 2 selections matters more here
than broad unseen-text practice — candidates know in advance which texts
could be examined, so systematic preparation across all of Part 2, not
just a favourite few, pays off directly.

Section B's imaginative writing task rewards applying craft techniques
observed in Section A's reading — narrative viewpoint, structuring for
effect, precise and evocative vocabulary — rather than treating the two
sections as unrelated. Since the specification explicitly links reading
of fiction to inspiring imaginative writing, practise noticing a
specific technique in a studied text and then trying it out in your own
writing, rather than revising reading and writing skills separately.

With 45 minutes and 30 marks for each section, time discipline matters:
spend proportionate time planning the imaginative writing task (a brief
outline of structure and ending) before drafting, since AO4's
requirement to adapt form, tone and register for a specific purpose is
difficult to achieve in unplanned, first-draft prose.

## Common mistakes

Rushing Section A's analysis to leave more time for Section B, when both sections carry equal marks and equal recommended time.

Writing a Section A response that only summarises the poem or prose
extract's content (AO1) without analysing how the writer's language and
structural choices create effect (AO2). Choosing an imaginative writing
prompt based on which sounds most impressive rather than which the
candidate can sustain well for 30 marks. Neglecting planning in Section
B and producing writing that drifts without a clear structure or ending.
Applying insights from Anthology texts studied for Component 1
(non-fiction) as if they were relevant analytical tools for Component 2's
poetry and prose, when the two components draw on different genres and
skills.

## Worked example: reading for Section B inspiration

A candidate studies an Anthology short story that builds tension through
short, fragmented sentences during its climax. When later attempting an
imaginative writing task about a character facing a sudden crisis, the
candidate deliberately shortens sentence length at the story's turning
point to mirror that same technique.

```
Studied technique:  short, fragmented sentences at a moment of high
                     tension, mirroring a character's racing thoughts
Applied in writing:  "The door creaked. Footsteps. Closer. She froze."
                     -- short sentences used deliberately, not as an
                     accident of rushed drafting
```

This is exactly the kind of technique transfer the specification asks
for when it says students should use what they have learned about the
writer's craft to influence their own imaginative writing -- a
technique consciously borrowed and adapted, not just similar writing
produced by coincidence.

## Quick revision checklist

- Prepare systematically across all of Part 2 of the Anthology, not just
a few favourite poems or extracts.
- Practise identifying a specific writer's technique in Section A texts,
then applying a similar technique in Section B writing.
- Plan the imaginative writing task briefly before drafting, with a
clear ending in mind.
- Keep Section A analysis (AO1/AO2) and Section B craft (AO4/AO5)
mutually reinforcing rather than treating them as unrelated halves of
the paper.
