These questions cover **Section L: Data presentation and interpretation** (L1 to L4) of the
**AQA A-level Mathematics (7357) specification**, version 1.3, for A-level exams from June 2018
onwards. The specification lists Section L under Paper 3. A calculator is required in every
7357 paper, so every question here assumes you have one with statistics functions. Standard
deviations use the divisor n unless a question says otherwise.

## Questions

**1.** In a grouped frequency table, the class 20 ≤ m < 35 has frequency 27.

**(a)** Calculate the frequency density of this class. **[1]**
**(b)** On the histogram, the bar for 35 ≤ m < 40 has frequency density 3.6. Find the frequency
of that class. **[1]**

**2.** On a histogram, the bar for the class 40 ≤ x < 50 is 2 cm wide and 6 cm tall. It
represents 24 people. Another bar is 3 cm wide and 2.5 cm tall. Find the number of people it
represents. **[3]**

**3.** For a sample of 25 values, Σx = 1150 and Σx² = 53 800. Calculate the mean and the
standard deviation. **[3]**

**4.** Across a group of towns there is strong positive correlation between the number of
cafés and the number of bookshops. A councillor says that opening more cafés will cause more
bookshops to open. Comment on this claim. **[2]**

**5.** The commuting times, in minutes, of two groups of workers are summarised below.

| | Minimum | Q₁ | Median | Q₃ | Maximum |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Group A | 12 | 20 | 26 | 35 | 58 |
| Group B | 15 | 24 | 28 | 31 | 40 |

**(a)** Compare the commuting times of the two groups, making two comparisons in context. **[2]**
**(b)** An outlier is defined as a value more than 1.5 × IQR above Q₃. Show that the maximum
of Group A is an outlier. **[2]**

**6.** The reaction times, in milliseconds, of 10 people are:

245, 262, 238, 251, 270, 244, 259, 248, 330, 255

An outlier is defined as a value more than 2 standard deviations from the mean. Use your
calculator to find the mean and standard deviation, and identify any outliers. **[4]**

**7.** The lengths of 60 phone calls are shown.

| Length t (min) | 0 ≤ t < 2 | 2 ≤ t < 5 | 5 ≤ t < 10 | 10 ≤ t < 20 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Frequency | 14 | 22 | 18 | 6 |

Estimate the mean and standard deviation of the call lengths. **[4]**

**8.** On 20 days a café records the maximum temperature, T °C, and the number of cold drinks
sold, D. The temperatures range from 12 °C to 28 °C. The regression line of D on T is
D = 4.2T − 18.

**(a)** Interpret the value 4.2 in context. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain why the line should not be used to predict sales on a day with a maximum
temperature of 2 °C. **[2]**
**(c)** The data were collected from two branches, one on a beach and one in a city centre. The
scatter diagram shows two separate clusters of points. Explain why a single regression line may
be misleading here. **[2]**

**9.** A researcher has 30 values with Σx = 1512 and Σx² = 79 272.

**(a)** Calculate the mean and standard deviation. **[3]**
**(b)** While cleaning the data, the researcher finds that one value was typed as 95 instead of
59. Calculate the corrected mean and standard deviation. **[3]**

**10.** Ages of visitors to a museum are grouped into classes of widths 5, 10, 20 and 40 years.
A student draws a bar chart with frequency on the vertical axis and bars of matching widths.

**(a)** Explain why this diagram is misleading and state what should be plotted instead. **[2]**
**(b)** Suggest a suitable diagram for comparing the ages of visitors in two different years.
**[1]**

**11.** The masses of 80 parcels are shown.

| Mass m (kg) | 0 ≤ m < 1 | 1 ≤ m < 2 | 2 ≤ m < 4 | 4 ≤ m < 7 | 7 ≤ m < 10 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Frequency | 12 | 20 | 28 | 15 | 5 |

**(a)** Calculate the frequency density of each class. **[2]**
**(b)** Estimate the number of parcels with masses between 1.5 kg and 5 kg. **[2]**
**(c)** A parcel is chosen at random from these 80. Find the probability that its mass is at
least 4 kg. **[1]**
**(d)** Estimate the mean and standard deviation of the masses. **[3]**
**(e)** Use linear interpolation to estimate the median mass. **[2]**

**12.** A class of 15 students had a test mean of 62 and standard deviation of 5. A second
class of 10 students had a mean of 70 and standard deviation of 4.

**(a)** Find the mean of all 25 scores. **[2]**
**(b)** Find the standard deviation of all 25 scores. **[4]**
**(c)** Explain why the combined standard deviation is larger than that of either class. **[1]**
**(d)** A histogram of all 25 scores is drawn. Suggest what feature it might show. **[1]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** 27 ÷ 15 = **1.8** [1]
**(b)** 3.6 × 5 = **18** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* write the class width you used; a wrong width with no working leaves nothing to credit.

**2.** Area of the first bar = 2 × 6 = 12 cm² for 24 people [1], so 1 cm² represents 2 people
[1]. Second bar: 3 × 2.5 = 7.5 cm², so 7.5 × 2 = **15 people** [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* using bar heights alone (6 for 24, so 2.5 for 10) ignores width and gives a wrong answer.

**3.** x̄ = 1150 ÷ 25 = **46** [1]. σ² = 53 800/25 − 46² = 2152 − 2116 = 36 [1], so **σ = 6** [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* show Σx²/n − x̄² before the square root; a bare σ with an arithmetic slip earns no method credit.

**4.** Correlation does not imply causation, so the claim is not supported by the data [1].
A third variable, such as the population of the town, could increase both the number of cafés
and the number of bookshops [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* naming a plausible third variable in context is stronger than repeating "correlation is not causation".

**5. (a)** The median for Group B (28 minutes) is higher than for Group A (26 minutes), so
Group B's commutes are slightly longer on average [1]. Group A's IQR (15 minutes) is larger than
Group B's (7 minutes), so Group A's commuting times are more varied [1].
**(b)** IQR for A = 35 − 20 = 15, so the limit is 35 + 1.5 × 15 = 57.5 [1]. 58 > 57.5, so 58
is an **outlier** [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* each comparison needs the numbers and a sentence about commuting; "B is higher" alone is not an interpretation.

**6.** Mean = 2602 ÷ 10 = **260.2 ms** [1]. σ = **24.9 ms** (3 s.f.) [1]. Limits:
260.2 ± 2 × 24.94, giving 210.3 and 310.1 [1]. 330 > 310.1, so **330 ms is an outlier**; no
value is below 210.3 [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* keep the unrounded σ for the limits; state both limits so it is clear you checked for low outliers too.

**7.** Midpoints 1, 3.5, 7.5, 15; Σf = 60, Σfx = 316, Σfx² = 2646 [1].
x̄ = 316 ÷ 60 = **5.27 minutes** (3 s.f.) [1].
σ² = 2646/60 − (316/60)² = 16.36... [1], so **σ = 4.05 minutes** (3 s.f.) [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* using class upper boundaries instead of midpoints gives the wrong sums and loses the accuracy marks.

**8. (a)** For each 1 °C rise in maximum temperature, about 4.2 more cold drinks are sold [1].
**(b)** 2 °C is outside the observed range of 12 °C to 28 °C [1]. This is extrapolation, so
the linear relationship may not hold there and the prediction is unreliable [1].
**(c)** The two branches form distinct sections of the population with different sales
patterns [1]. One line through both clusters may show a correlation that does not hold within
either branch, so each branch should be analysed separately [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* in (b) quote the data range; "it is too cold" without reference to extrapolation misses the key point.

**9. (a)** x̄ = 1512 ÷ 30 = **50.4** [1]. σ² = 79 272/30 − 50.4² = 2642.4 − 2540.16 = 102.24 [1],
so **σ = 10.1** (3 s.f.) [1].
**(b)** New Σx = 1512 − 95 + 59 = 1476 and new Σx² = 79 272 − 95² + 59² = 73 728 [1].
x̄ = 1476 ÷ 30 = **49.2** [1]. σ = √(73 728/30 − 49.2²) = √36.96 = **6.08** (3 s.f.) [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* you must update Σx² by subtracting 95² and adding 59²; adjusting only Σx leaves the standard deviation wrong.

**10. (a)** With unequal class widths, the area of each bar should represent frequency, but
here wide classes look larger than they are [1]. Plot **frequency density** (frequency ÷ class
width) on the vertical axis to draw a histogram [1].
**(b)** **Box plots** for the two years, drawn on the same scale [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* "use a histogram" alone is not enough in (a); say what goes on the vertical axis.

**11. (a)** Frequency densities: 12, 20, 14, 5 and 1.67 (3 s.f.) [1] for all five correct, [1]
for using frequency ÷ class width.
**(b)** 0.5 × 20 + 28 + 1 × 5 [1] = **43 parcels** [1].
**(c)** (15 + 5) ÷ 80 = **0.25** [1].
**(d)** Midpoints 0.5, 1.5, 3, 5.5, 8.5: Σfx = 245 and Σfx² = 1115 [1].
x̄ = 245 ÷ 80 = **3.06 kg** (3 s.f.) [1]. σ = √(1115/80 − 3.0625²) = **2.14 kg** (3 s.f.) [1].
**(e)** The median is the 40th value. Cumulative frequencies 12, 32, 60, so it lies in
2 ≤ m < 4 [1]. Median ≈ 2 + (8/28) × 2 = **2.57 kg** (3 s.f.) [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* in (b) only part of the 1–2 and 4–7 classes counts; adding whole classes is a common way to lose both marks.

**12. (a)** Totals: 15 × 62 = 930 and 10 × 70 = 700 [1]. Mean = 1630 ÷ 25 = **65.2** [1].
**(b)** For class 1, Σx² = 15(5² + 62²) = 58 035 [1]. For class 2, Σx² = 10(4² + 70²) = 49 160
[1]. Combined σ² = 107 195/25 − 65.2² = 36.76 [1], so **σ = 6.06** (3 s.f.) [1].
**(c)** The class means differ by 8 marks, so the combined scores include the gap between the
two groups as well as the spread within each one [1].
**(d)** Two peaks (two separate clusters), one for each class, because the data come from two
distinct sections [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* rebuild Σx² from σ² + x̄² for each group; averaging the two standard deviations is a method error.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Bar heights treated as frequencies on histograms with unequal class widths.
- The area scale not found before reading a frequency from a histogram drawn to a scale.
- Whole classes counted when a question asks about part of a class.
- Σx² not updated when a value is corrected, added or removed.
- Combined standard deviations found by averaging the separate ones.
- Outlier limits not stated, so the conclusion is unsupported.
- Comparisons given without context, or with two location measures and no spread measure.
- Correlation described as causation.
- Predictions trusted outside the range of the data.
- Rounded values used in later steps, which shifts the final answer.
