## What operations management is

**Operations management** is the management of the process that turns inputs (materials, labour, equipment, information) into outputs (goods and services) that customers want. Its **objectives** are the targets the operations function sets for how well this is done. They are the operations equivalent of the marketing objectives covered in the [marketing objectives study guide](/resources/ocr-a-level-business-h431-marketing-objectives/).

## The main objectives

| Objective | What it means | Example target (invented) |
|---|---|---|
| **Cost** | Produce at a low cost per unit | Cut full cost per unit by 8% in two years |
| **Quality** | Meet the standard customers expect, with few defects | Keep the defect rate below 2% |
| **Speed** | Deliver quickly, with short lead times | Dispatch 95% of orders within 48 hours |
| **Dependability** | Deliver on time and as promised | 96% of orders delivered on the promised date |
| **Flexibility** | Change the product, volume or timing quickly | Switch between two models in under an hour |
| **Efficiency and capacity utilisation** | Use resources fully, with little waste | Run at 85% of capacity |
| **Environmental sustainability** | Reduce waste, energy use and emissions | Cut waste from 6% to 3% of materials |
| **Innovation** | Develop new products or processes | Launch two process improvements a year |
| **Customer service** | Support and respond to customers | Answer complaints within 24 hours |

Businesses rarely pursue all of them at once. A budget airline stresses cost and dependability; a luxury watchmaker stresses quality and a craft process; an emergency repair firm stresses speed and flexibility.

## From business objective to operations target

Operations objectives follow from the business's objectives and from what customers value (the **order winners**, such as price or speed, and the **qualifiers**, the minimum standards needed to be considered at all).

### Worked example 1: turning a cost aim into operations targets

Fenner Furniture makes 10,000 chairs a year. Its corporate objective is to raise profit. Operations management sets an objective to cut the full cost per chair by **8%** in two years.

**Starting point.** Variable cost is **£40** (materials £24, labour £16). Fixed costs are **£225,000** a year.

1. Fixed cost per chair = £225,000 ÷ 10,000 = **£22.50**.
2. Full cost per chair = £40 + £22.50 = **£62.50**.
3. Target = £62.50 × 0.92 = **£57.50**, a cut of **£5.00**.

**Possible actions and their effect.**

- *Cut material waste.* Material spend is 10,000 × £24 = £240,000, of which 6% is wasted (£14,400). Reducing waste to 3% saves £240,000 × 0.03 = **£7,200**, or **£0.72** a chair.
- *Raise output to 12,500 chairs* using spare machine time. Fixed cost per chair falls to £225,000 ÷ 12,500 = **£18.00**, a saving of **£4.50**.

**Result.** New full cost = (£40 − £0.72) + £18.00 = **£57.28**, which is below the £57.50 target. Waste reduction alone would deliver only 14% of the target saving (£0.72 of £5.00). Most of the gain comes from using capacity better, which requires sales to rise by a quarter. That means operations and marketing must work together, and the cost target depends on a marketing objective being met. If demand rose only to 11,000 chairs, fixed cost per chair would be £225,000 ÷ 11,000 = £20.45 and the full cost would be £40 − £0.72 + £20.45 = **£59.73**, still above the £57.50 target.

This shows **how** objectives are used: they turn a general aim into measurable targets, show which actions matter most, and expose dependencies between departments. It shows **why** they matter to the business objective: if the cost target is missed, profit is likely to be lower than planned.

## Trade-offs between objectives

Improving one objective often costs another.

### Worked example 2: speed against cost

Fenner offers standard delivery in ten days at a price of **£85** per chair. Variable cost is **£40**, so the contribution is **£45**. A 48-hour express service needs overtime and faster transport, raising variable cost to **£48**. Fenner prices the express service at **£99**.

1. Contribution per express chair = £99 − £48 = **£51**.
2. Compared with standard: £51 − £45 = **£6 more**.
3. The extra cost of speed is £48 − £40 = **£8**, and the extra price is £99 − £85 = **£14**. Fenner needs a price premium of **more than £8** just to cover the cost of speed.

If 3,000 chairs are sold as express, the total contribution is 3,000 × £51 = **£153,000**, against 3,000 × £45 = **£135,000** as standard, a gain of **£18,000**. But this assumes customers will pay the £14 premium. If only half of them do and the rest buy standard, the gain falls. Speed also reduces flexibility for other orders when overtime is used, and fatigue may reduce quality. The trade-off must therefore be chosen to suit the customers that matter.

## Why clarity matters

Clear objectives:

- give everyone in operations the same direction (for example, the quality objective tells the line what defect rate is acceptable);
- allow performance to be measured and compared with targets, so problems are noticed;
- help managers decide trade-offs and priorities;
- link operations to other functions (for example, capacity targets must match marketing forecasts);
- support stakeholders such as lenders and customers who want evidence of reliability.

Unclear objectives lead to conflicting priorities (one team cutting cost while another raises quality), wasted effort and no way to judge success.

## Usefulness and limits

| Stakeholder | Usefulness | Possible problem |
|---|---|---|
| Owners and shareholders | Shows how operations supports profit | Cost targets may be pursued at the expense of quality |
| Managers | Focus and a basis for measuring performance | Targets may be missed for reasons outside operations' control |
| Employees | Clear expectations; chance of reward | Pressure; stress if targets seem unrealistic |
| Customers | Reliable quality, delivery and service | Cost-cutting can reduce service |
| Suppliers | Predictable order levels | Pressure to cut prices or deliver faster |
| Communities | Environmental objectives reduce harm | Costs may be passed to local suppliers |

General limits: objectives can conflict; they rely on forecasts; measuring one aspect can lead people to neglect others; and they may become out of date when demand or technology changes. Their usefulness is highest when they are few, measurable and reviewed regularly.

## Recommending objectives

To **recommend and justify** objectives for a business:

1. Identify what customers value (the order winners and qualifiers).
2. Link to the business objective and to marketing's plans.
3. Choose two or three priority objectives, with numbers and dates.
4. Explain the trade-offs accepted.
5. State how success will be measured and the main risk.

Example: for a meal-delivery start-up that promises hot food in 30 minutes, the recommended objectives might be speed (95% of orders within 30 minutes), quality (complaints under 2%) and cost (delivery cost per order cut by 10% in a year). Speed and quality are the order winners, but the cost target protects profit as the company grows. The risk is that pressure on speed may harm food quality.

## Common mistakes

- Listing objectives without saying which matter most to the business.
- Writing vague objectives such as "improve quality" with no measure.
- Forgetting trade-offs: all objectives cannot be maximised at once.
- Ignoring the link with marketing and finance objectives.
- Describing operations as production only, when it includes services.

## Exam technique

For "explain", define the objective and give a measurable example. For "evaluate usefulness", give benefits and limits for the business and at least one stakeholder, and judge. For "recommend and justify", choose objectives, give numbers, explain why they fit the case and state the trade-off.
