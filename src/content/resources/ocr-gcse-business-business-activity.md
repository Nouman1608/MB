## 1.1 Enterprise and entrepreneurship

**Business activity** exists to satisfy the needs of customers. **Enterprise** is the process of doing this: spotting an opportunity, developing an idea for a business and then turning it into something customers will buy.

Take Yarrowmoor Kayak Hire, an invented business. Nkechi Adeyemi notices that visitors to a reservoir can watch the water but cannot get onto it. That is the **opportunity**. Her **idea** is a hut renting kayaks by the hour. The **customer need** she satisfies is a wish for an afternoon activity on the water.

The specification lists four **characteristics of an entrepreneur**.

- **Creativity**: coming up with an idea or a better way of meeting a need.
- **Risk taking**: committing money and time without knowing the result.
- **Determination**: carrying on through slow early sales and setbacks.
- **Confidence**: believing in the idea and persuading others to back it.

**Risk and reward.** Nkechi puts in £14 000 of savings. The risk is that visitors are too few and she loses part or all of it, as well as the pay she gave up. The reward is the profit she hopes to make, which she forecasts at £9 000 in the first year, plus being her own boss. The larger the possible reward, the more risk an entrepreneur usually has to accept. A good answer names both sides and links them to the business in the case study.

## 1.2 Business planning

**Planning** means deciding in advance what the business will do and how. Its purposes are to **reduce risk** and to **help the business to succeed**: problems are found on paper, not after money has been spent.

A **business plan** is a written document setting out the idea and how it will work. The specification names four uses (the first four below); reducing risk is also one of its two purposes of planning.

1. **Identifying markets.** Writing the plan forces Nkechi to say who will rent kayaks, such as families and walking groups, and how many.
2. **Obtaining finance.** A lender or investor can see that the owner has thought it through, which makes backing more likely.
3. **Identifying the resources needed to operate.** The plan lists the kayaks, buoyancy aids, a hut, insurance and staff.
4. **Achieving aims and objectives.** The plan turns a goal such as covering costs by month six into steps and checkpoints.
5. **Reducing risk.** Weak points show up while they are cheap to fix.

**How useful is a plan?** It does not guarantee success. Forecasts can be wrong, a plan takes time to write, and a rigid owner may ignore a changed market. It is most useful when seeking finance or starting up, and less useful if never revisited. When asked about usefulness, give a benefit, a limit and a judgement.

## 1.3 Business ownership

| Type | Owned by | Liability |
|---|---|---|
| Sole trader | One person | Unlimited |
| Partnership | Two or more people | Unlimited |
| Private limited company (Ltd) | Shareholders | Limited |
| Public limited company (plc) | Shareholders | Limited |

**Unlimited liability** means the owner's personal assets can be used to pay business debts. **Limited liability** means a shareholder can lose no more than the amount they invested.

Sundrell & Rudge is an invented print studio run as a partnership. It owes £60 000 and its assets are worth £45 000. The shortfall is 60 000 - 45 000 = £15 000. Because liability is unlimited, the partners' personal assets can be called on. If they share equally under their agreement, that is £7 500 each. Had the studio been a private limited company in which each partner invested £10 000, each could lose at most £10 000, and creditors would bear the unpaid £15 000.

**Features to compare.** The specification names these four types but does not list their features. The points below, and the definitions of merger and takeover in section 1.6, come from general business knowledge, not from the specification text.

- **Sole trader**: easy to set up, the owner keeps all profit and takes all decisions, but carries unlimited liability and has few sources of finance.
- **Partnership**: more people bring skills and money, but profit is shared and partners may disagree.
- **Private limited company (Ltd)**: limited liability, shares sold privately, with more rules to follow than a sole trader.
- **Public limited company (plc)**: shares may be sold to the public, so large sums can be raised, but ownership is spread and the business has more reporting to do.

**Suitability in context.** Nkechi, starting small with her own money, suits a sole trader. A family business wanting protection for its savings may prefer a Ltd. An established manufacturer that needs millions to build a new site may choose to become a plc. In an evaluation, match the type to the stage, size and finance needs, not to a favourite.

## 1.4 Business aims and objectives

An **aim** is a long-term goal. An **objective** is a target that helps reach it. The specification lists five.

- **Profit**: earning more revenue than costs.
- **Survival**: staying in business, common for start-ups.
- **Growth**: becoming larger in sales, output or sites.
- **Providing a service**: meeting a need for its own sake, as with a charity or community group.
- **Market share**: the part of total market sales a business wins. In its standard form, market share (%) = firm's sales ÷ total market sales × 100.

Stormgate Sportswear plc (invented) sells £1.4 million in a market of £11.2 million. Market share = 1.4 ÷ 11.2 × 100 = **12.5%**.

**Why objectives change.** A business changes as it evolves. Yarrowmoor Kayak Hire will first aim to survive the winter. If it does, it can aim for profit, then for a second hut. Competitors, the economy and the owner's own ambitions can also move the target.

**Why businesses differ.** Calder Valley Community Transport (invented) runs minibuses for people without cars. It values providing a service and breaking even, while a plc such as Stormgate answers to shareholders who expect profit and growth. Size, ownership and the owner's motives all change the objective.

## 1.5 Stakeholders in business

A **stakeholder** is someone, or a group, with a stake in a business, either because it affects them or because they can influence it.

| Group | Type | Role and typical objective |
|---|---|---|
| Owners | Internal | Provide money and take the risk; want profit and growth |
| Employees | Internal | Do the work; want fair pay, security and good conditions |
| Customers | External | Buy the output; want quality, value and service |
| Suppliers | External | Provide inputs; want regular orders and prompt payment |
| Government | External | Sets rules and collects taxes; wants compliance and jobs |
| Local community | External | Lives near the business; wants jobs and minimal disturbance |

**Effect of business on stakeholders.** If Farrowdale Preserves (invented) opens a night shift, owners gain output, employees gain extra hours, suppliers gain bigger orders, and neighbours may suffer lorry noise.

**Effect of stakeholders on a business.** Customers can stop buying after poor service. Employees can leave, or work harder when treated well. Suppliers can refuse credit. The government can change rules. The community can complain and damage the firm's image.

**Conflict.** Interests often clash. A pay rise helps employees but cuts profit for owners. Expansion pleases owners but may upset the community. For a high-mark answer, say which stakeholder matters more in this case and why.

## 1.6 Business growth

**Organic growth** comes from inside, using the business's own resources. The specification lists four forms.

- Increasing output: Farrowdale Preserves lifts production from 8 000 to 10 200 jars a month, growth of (10 200 - 8 000) ÷ 8 000 × 100 = **27.5%**.
- Gaining new customers, for example by selling to a new town.
- Developing new products.
- Increasing market share.

Organic growth is slower but lower risk and easier to control.

**External growth** joins with another business. In a **merger** two firms agree to combine. In a **takeover** one firm buys another, which may be unwelcome to the target. Three directions are named.

- **Horizontal**: joining a business at the same stage of production. Mardelby Furniture (invented) buys a rival furniture maker.
- **Vertical**: joining a business at a different stage in the chain. Backward means buying a supplier, such as a timber merchant. Forward means buying a customer, such as a showroom.
- **Diversification**: moving into a new market or product, for example the furniture maker buying a garden-lighting firm.

External growth is fast and can win market share or a supply, but it costs money, the businesses may not blend, and staff can resist change. When you evaluate, link the type to the objective.

## How to answer section 1 questions

Follow the command words in the specification. **Identify** and **state** need knowledge only. **Explain** needs knowledge applied to the case. **Analyse** needs a logical chain of reasoning. **Evaluate** and **discuss** mean weighing both sides and reaching a supported judgement. **Recommend** needs a supported judgement. Use the business named in the stimulus and its numbers.

## Common errors

- Thinking limited liability cancels the debt. The company still owes it; the shareholder's own loss is capped.
- Treating a plc as owned by the government.
- Assuming every objective is profit.
- Mixing up merger and takeover, or horizontal and vertical.
- Listing stakeholders without saying how each is affected.
- Describing a business plan without saying why it helps this business.
