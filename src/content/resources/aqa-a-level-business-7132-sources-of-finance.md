This guide goes deeper into section **3.5.3 Making financial decisions: sources of finance** of the AQA AS and A-level Business specification (7132, for first teaching from September 2023). The specification's content is:

- **internal and external sources of finance**, including debt factoring, overdrafts, retained profits, share capital, loans, venture capital and crowd funding
- **the advantages and disadvantages of different sources of finance for short- and long-term uses**.

The wider [financial management study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-financial-management/) covers the whole topic. Companion pages: [revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-sources-of-finance-revision-notes/) and [practice questions](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-sources-of-finance-practice/). All businesses and figures are invented.

## Why businesses need finance

A business needs money to **start up**, to cover **working capital** (the day-to-day costs between paying suppliers and receiving payment), to **expand** (new premises, equipment, takeover) and to survive **cash flow gaps**. The right source depends on how much is needed, for how long, how quickly, at what cost and how much control the owners are willing to share. A basic rule is to match **short-term needs with short-term finance** and **long-term needs with long-term finance**. Using an overdraft to buy a factory would be risky, because the bank can demand repayment at short notice.

## Internal and external sources

| | Internal | External |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | Raised from within the business | Raised from outside the business |
| Examples | Retained profit, owner's savings, sale of assets, reducing inventory, collecting receivables faster | Overdraft, bank loan, share issue, venture capital, crowd funding, debt factoring, trade credit, leasing, grants |

## Short-term sources

### Overdraft

A facility allowing the business to spend more than it has in its bank account, up to an agreed limit.

- **Advantages**: flexible; interest is charged only on the amount used; quick to arrange; suited to cash flow gaps.
- **Disadvantages**: interest rates are often high; the bank can withdraw it at short notice; not suitable for long-term needs.

*Example.* A shop with an overdraft at 12% a year borrows £15,000 for 30 days. Interest = £15,000 × 12% × 30 ÷ 365 = **£147.95** (to the nearest penny), a small cost for a short gap.

### Debt factoring

A specialist firm (the factor) buys the business's trade receivables (invoices owed by customers), paying most of the value at once and the rest when the customer pays, less a fee.

*Example.* Penrose Packaging has £80,000 of invoices outstanding. The factor advances 85% immediately and charges a 2.5% fee on the invoice value.

- Advance = £80,000 × 0.85 = **£68,000**.
- Fee = £80,000 × 0.025 = **£2,000**.
- Remaining balance paid when customers pay = £80,000 − £68,000 − £2,000 = **£10,000**.

- **Advantages**: quick cash from sales already made; improves cash flow without taking a loan; some factors also collect the debts, saving administration.
- **Disadvantages**: the fee reduces profit; customers may think the business has cash problems; the business loses control over how customers are chased; only available where there are invoices.

### Trade credit

Delaying payment to suppliers (taking longer to pay) is free finance in the short term, but may harm supplier relationships or lose discounts for early payment.

## Long-term and medium-term sources

### Retained profit

Profit kept in the business rather than paid out as dividends.

- **Advantages**: no interest or repayment; no outside control; no cost of arranging.
- **Disadvantages**: only available if the business is profitable; amounts may be too small for large projects; shareholders get lower dividends; the money could have earned more elsewhere (opportunity cost).

### Share capital

Money raised by selling shares in a company. A private limited company sells shares to chosen investors; a public limited company can sell to the public on the stock market.

- **Advantages**: does not have to be repaid; no interest; can raise very large sums (especially for plcs); no pressure on cash flow, since dividends are optional.
- **Disadvantages**: owners lose some control and share future profits; dividends are expected; a flotation is costly and slow; there is a risk of takeover for a plc; issuing more shares reduces existing shareholders' percentage ownership.

*Example.* A company issues 50,000 shares at £2 each, raising 50,000 × £2 = **£100,000**. If it pays a 10p dividend per share, the dividend cost is 50,000 × £0.10 = **£5,000** a year, though it is not obliged to pay.

### Loans

Borrowing a fixed sum, repaid with interest over an agreed period, usually from a bank. Loans may be **secured** on assets (such as property) or unsecured.

- **Advantages**: large sums available; fixed repayment schedule helps planning; owners keep full control; interest is a tax-deductible expense.
- **Disadvantages**: interest and repayments must be made even if profit is low; security may be required, so assets are at risk; high borrowing (gearing) increases risk; interest rate changes can raise costs on a variable loan.

*Example.* A £100,000 loan at 8% interest costs £100,000 × 8% = £8,000 a year in interest while the full amount is outstanding. Over a five-year interest-only loan, that is £40,000 of interest in total. If the money funds a project adding £15,000 a year to profit before interest, the project still adds £7,000 a year after interest, so borrowing makes sense. If profit before interest were only £6,000, the loan would cause a loss of £2,000 a year.

### Venture capital

Investment from specialist firms or wealthy individuals (business angels) in young, higher-risk businesses with strong growth potential, in return for a share of ownership (equity) and often a say in management.

- **Advantages**: large sums for businesses banks may refuse; the investor brings expertise and contacts; no repayments or interest.
- **Disadvantages**: owners give up part of the ownership and control; investors expect high returns and an exit (sale or flotation) within a few years; the founders must share future profits.

*Example.* A venture capital firm invests £500,000 for 30% of the company. This values the company at 500,000 ÷ 0.30 = about **£1.67 million** after the investment. If the company later sells for £6 million, the investor's share is 30% × £6m = **£1.8 million**. The founders have funded growth but given up almost a third of the reward.

### Crowd funding

Raising small sums from many people, usually through an online platform. Types include **reward-based** (backers get a product or perk), **equity-based** (backers get shares) and **donation or loan-based** models.

- **Advantages**: access to finance without banks; tests demand for a product and creates a customer base and publicity; reward-based schemes do not give up ownership.
- **Disadvantages**: the target may not be reached (in many platforms the money is returned if the target fails); campaign takes time and marketing effort; ideas are made public so rivals can copy them; equity crowd funding adds many small shareholders; delivering rewards costs money.

### Other long-term sources

Leasing (spreading the cost of equipment), sale and leaseback of property, selling unused assets, and grants from government or local bodies (often for specific purposes, such as job creation) are also used.

## Choosing between sources: summary table

| Need | Likely suitable | Why | Less suitable |
|---|---|---|---|
| Short cash gap | Overdraft, debt factoring, trade credit | Quick, flexible, short-term | Share issue, long loan |
| Replace machinery | Loan, leasing, retained profit | Long life asset matches long-term finance | Overdraft |
| Fast expansion | Share capital, venture capital, retained profit | Large sums with less cash pressure | Overdraft |
| Start-up with an untested idea | Owner's savings, crowd funding, business angel | Banks wary of risk; tests demand | Large bank loan |
| Established plc, major takeover | Share issue, large loan | Needs very large sums | Overdraft |

## Factors in the decision

- **Amount needed** and **time** over which it is needed.
- **Cost**: interest, fees, dividends given up.
- **Risk and gearing**: too much debt risks failure if profit falls or interest rates rise.
- **Control**: share issues and venture capital dilute ownership.
- **Business's size, legal form and age**: a sole trader cannot issue shares; a start-up has no profit history.
- **Availability and speed**: a bank may refuse high-risk lending.
- **Purpose**: matching the life of the finance to the life of the use.
- **Technology**: online platforms have made crowd funding and quick online lending accessible.
- **Economic conditions**: interest rates and investors' confidence affect cost and availability.

## Technique for exam questions

- Identify the **purpose and time-scale** in the case first, then pick the source that fits.
- Give an advantage and a disadvantage that are relevant to the business, not a generic list.
- Use figures from the case to compare costs.
- For evaluation, state what the choice depends on, for example the owner's willingness to lose control, or whether profit is strong enough to carry interest.

## Common mistakes

- Treating share capital as a loan that must be repaid.
- Recommending an overdraft for a long-term purchase.
- Forgetting that retained profit has an opportunity cost.
- Listing crowd funding as one thing, when equity and reward types differ.
- Ignoring the effect of interest on profit.
- Saying venture capital has "no cost" because there is no interest.
