## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Inflation | A sustained rise in the general level of prices |
| Rate of inflation | The percentage rise in the price level, usually over 12 months |
| Price stability | Low and steady inflation; the UK target is 2% CPI inflation |
| Deflation | A fall in the general price level (a negative inflation rate) |
| Disinflation | Inflation falling but still above zero: prices rise more slowly |
| Purchasing power | The quantity of goods and services a sum of money can buy |
| Real value | A money value adjusted for inflation |

## How inflation is measured: the CPI

- The **Consumer Price Index (CPI)** is published every month by the Office for National Statistics (ONS).
- The ONS records the prices of a "basket" of several hundred goods and services that a typical household buys.
- Each item is **weighted** by its share of household spending, so a 10% rise in the price of something people spend a lot on (such as food) moves the index more than a 10% rise in something they rarely buy.
- The index is set to 100 in a **base year**. A reading of 120 means prices are 20% higher than in the base year.
- The headline inflation rate compares the index with the same month a year earlier.
- The basket is updated each year as spending patterns change. Other UK measures exist (CPIH, which adds owner-occupiers' housing costs, and the older RPI), but the government's target uses the CPI.

## Calculations you must be able to do

**1. Inflation rate from CPI figures.** CPI rises from 120.0 to 123.6.
(123.6 − 120.0) ÷ 120.0 × 100 = 3.6 ÷ 120 × 100 = **3.0%**.

**2. Making an index.** A basket costs £400 in the base year and £418 a year later.
Index = 418 ÷ 400 × 100 = **104.5**, so prices rose 4.5%.

**3. A weighted index.** Food has a weight of 0.4 and its price index is 110. Transport has a weight of 0.6 and its price index is 105.
Overall index = 0.4 × 110 + 0.6 × 105 = 44 + 63 = **107**. The larger weight pulls the answer towards transport.

**4. Index points versus per cent.** CPI goes from 130.0 to 133.9. That is 3.9 index points but 3.9 ÷ 130 × 100 = **3.0%**. Index points only equal per cent when the starting value is 100.

**5. A price next year.** An item costs £80 and inflation is 2.5%. Next year: 80 × 1.025 = **£82**.

**6. Real versus nominal.**
- A worker's pay rises 5% while inflation is 3%. Real pay rises by about 2% (exactly 1.05 ÷ 1.03 = 1.019, so 1.9%).
- Savings earn 4% interest while inflation is 6%. The real return is about **−2%**: the saver can buy less at the end of the year despite having more pounds.
- £100 when inflation is 4% buys, a year later, what 100 ÷ 1.04 = £96.15 bought at the start.

## Causes of inflation

| Cost-push | Demand-pull |
|---|---|
| Firms' costs rise, so they raise prices to protect profit | Total demand rises faster than the economy can increase output |
| Higher wages not matched by higher productivity | Low interest rates encouraging borrowing and spending |
| Dearer raw materials, energy and food | Tax cuts or more government spending |
| A fall in the pound, making imported inputs dearer | Rising confidence, or a boom in demand for exports |
| Higher indirect taxes such as VAT | Most likely when the economy is near full capacity |

**UK example.** CPI inflation reached 11.1% in October 2022, its highest for about 40 years. Much of this was cost-push: energy and food prices rose sharply after Russia's invasion of Ukraine.

## Consequences of inflation for different groups

| Group | Effect of high or unexpected inflation |
|---|---|
| Savers | Lose if the interest rate is below inflation, because the real value of savings falls |
| Borrowers | Gain, because the real value of what they owe falls (but lenders may raise interest rates) |
| People on fixed incomes (some pensioners, benefit claimants) | Lose purchasing power unless payments rise with prices |
| Workers | Lose if wages rise more slowly than prices; may push for higher pay |
| Firms | Face rising costs, uncertainty and the cost of changing prices; planning and investment become harder |
| Exporters | Lose competitiveness if UK prices rise faster than in other countries |
| Government | Tax revenue rises as prices and incomes rise, but spending on index-linked benefits and pensions also rises |

Low, stable inflation helps everyone plan. **Deflation** can also cause harm: people may delay spending while waiting for lower prices, and the real burden of debt rises.

## Government policies to manage inflation

- **Monetary policy:** the Bank of England raises Bank Rate. Borrowing becomes dearer and saving more rewarding, so spending falls and demand-pull inflation eases.
- **Fiscal policy:** higher taxes or lower government spending reduce demand.
- **Supply-side policies:** training, competition and investment raise productivity, so costs and prices rise more slowly over time. This helps with cost-push inflation but works slowly.
- **Conflicts:** cutting demand may slow growth and raise unemployment. Raising interest rates cannot lower the world price of oil or gas, so it works less well against cost-push inflation.

## Exam traps

- "Inflation fell from 6% to 3%" means prices are **still rising**, only more slowly. Prices fall only with deflation.
- Inflation is a rise in the **general** price level, not in the price of one good.
- Work the percentage change from the **earlier** CPI figure.
- Index points are not percentages unless the starting value is 100.
- Inflation hurts savers only when interest is below inflation. Say so.
- Match the policy to the cause: demand-side policies suit demand-pull inflation best.

## Quick self-test

1. Define inflation and the rate of inflation.
2. Why are items in the CPI weighted?
3. CPI rises from 150.0 to 154.5. Find the rate of inflation.
4. A basket costs £250 in the base year and £262 a year later. Find the index.
5. Inflation falls from 4% to 2%. What happens to prices?
6. Give two causes of cost-push inflation.
7. Pay rises by 2% while inflation is 5%. What happens to real pay?
8. Why do borrowers tend to gain from unexpected inflation?
9. Name one group that loses from inflation and explain why.
10. Explain how a rise in interest rates can reduce inflation.

### Answers

1. Inflation is a sustained rise in the general price level. The rate of inflation is the percentage rise in the price level over a period, usually a year.
2. So that items households spend more on have a bigger effect on the index.
3. 4.5 ÷ 150 × 100 = 3.0%.
4. 262 ÷ 250 × 100 = 104.8.
5. They still rise, but more slowly.
6. Any two: higher wages, dearer raw materials or energy, a weaker pound making imports dearer, higher indirect taxes.
7. It falls by about 3% (exactly 1.02 ÷ 1.05 = 0.971, a fall of 2.9%).
8. The real value of what they owe falls, so repaying the debt costs less in terms of goods.
9. For example, savers: if interest is below inflation, their savings buy less. Or people on fixed incomes: their income buys less.
10. Borrowing costs more and saving pays more, so households and firms spend less; lower demand reduces upward pressure on prices.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing that lower inflation means lower prices.
- Calculating the change from the later figure, or quoting index points as a percentage.
- Saying "everyone loses" from inflation, with no named group.
- Mixing up cost-push and demand-pull, or giving the wrong policy for the cause.
- Forgetting real values: a pay rise is not a gain if prices rise faster.
