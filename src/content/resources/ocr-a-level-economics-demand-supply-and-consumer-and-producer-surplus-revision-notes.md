## Key definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Market | Wherever buyers and sellers make contact to trade |
| Sub-market | A distinct part of a market with its own buyers, sellers and price |
| Demand | Quantities consumers want and can afford at each price, other things equal |
| Market demand | All buyers' quantities totalled, price by price |
| Supply | Quantities producers choose to offer at each price, other things equal |
| Market supply | Sum of individual firms' supplies at each price |
| Consumer surplus | Gap between the most buyers would pay and the price paid; area beneath the demand curve, above the price |
| Producer surplus | Gap between the price received and the least sellers would accept; area above the supply curve, under the price |

## Types of demand and supply

| Type | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Derived demand | Wanted for what it helps make | Bricks from demand for houses |
| Joint demand | Complements bought together | Cameras and memory cards |
| Composite demand | One good, several uses; add the uses | Milk for drinking, cheese, yoghurt |
| Competitive demand | Substitutes | Bus and train travel |
| Joint supply | One process, two products | Beef and hides |
| Composite supply | One good supplied to several uses | Oil to fuel and plastics |
| Competitive supply | Resources switch between products | Land for wheat or barley |

Joint demand and joint supply are not the same thing. The first is about buyers, the second about producers. Composite demand sums uses; it does not mean complements.

## Why demand slopes downwards

**Marginal utility.** Extra satisfaction falls with each extra unit. Consumers buy while marginal utility is at least the price, so a lower price means more units. Across goods, spending is best when marginal utility per pound is equal. If it is higher for one good, buy more of that one.

**Income and substitution effects.** A price fall has two effects.
1. Substitution: the good is cheaper relative to others, so switch towards it.
2. Income: real income rises, so more can be bought. For a normal good this adds to the extra quantity.

## Movement or shift?

| | Movement along | Shift of the curve |
|---|---|---|
| Cause | Change in the good's own price | Any other factor |
| Demand names | Extension (price down), contraction (price up) | Increase (right), decrease (left) |
| Supply names | Extension (price up), contraction (price down) | Increase (right), decrease (left) |

**Shifters of demand:** income (normal or inferior), prices of substitutes and complements, tastes and advertising, population, expectations, credit and interest rates, weather, government action.

**Shifters of supply:** costs of production, technology, indirect taxes, subsidies, prices of other goods, number of firms, weather and natural events, expectations, regulation.

Method for a shift question:
1. Name the factor and the curve it moves.
2. Say right or left, and increase or decrease.
3. Draw it: label axes, original and new curves, and the change in quantity at a given price.

Worked reminder (illustrative). Demand is Q = 60 − 4P. At £5, Q = 60 − 20 = 40. A rise in incomes makes demand Q = 72 − 4P, so at £5, Q = 52. That is an increase in demand of 12 at every price, a shift right. If instead price had fallen to £4 on the original curve, Q = 44: an extension.

Chain for a written answer: a factor changes, so the amount wanted (or offered) differs at each price, so the curve moves, so the quantity at a given price changes. Keep each link explicit.

## Consumer surplus: method

Picture the axes: price is the vertical scale, quantity the horizontal one. The demand curve falls from its intercept on the price axis. A horizontal line at the price meets it at the quantity bought. Consumer surplus is the triangle above that line and below the curve, back to the vertical axis.

Straight-line demand P = a − bQ, price p.
1. Find quantity bought: Q = (a − p) ÷ b.
2. Surplus = ½ × Q × (a − p).
3. For a price change, find the change in surplus, or split it into a rectangle (old quantity × price fall) and a triangle (extra quantity × price fall × ½).

Worked reminder (illustrative). Demand is P = 30 − 5Q, Q in hundreds.

| Price (£) | Quantity | Surplus working | Surplus |
|---|---|---|---|
| 15 | 3 | ½ × 3 × 15 | 22.5 |
| 10 | 4 | ½ × 4 × 20 | 40.0 |

The rise is 17.5 (a 77.8 per cent increase). Check: rectangle 3 × 5 = 15 plus triangle ½ × 1 × 5 = 2.5 gives 17.5.

**Evaluating a change in consumer surplus:**
- The size depends on the price change and the slope of the demand curve.
- Demand curves are estimates, and money values treat every pound alike across rich and poor.
- Check why price moved: a rise caused by higher demand can still raise surplus.
- Cheaper harmful goods may cut wider welfare.
- Sellers who charge different prices to different buyers keep some surplus.
- A lower price from lower quality gives a smaller real gain.

## Producer surplus: method

Straight-line supply P = c + dQ, price p.
1. Find quantity supplied: Q = (p − c) ÷ d.
2. Surplus = ½ × Q × (p − c).
3. For a price change, rectangle (the lower of the two quantities × price change) plus triangle (½ × extra quantity × price change).

Worked reminder (illustrative). Supply is P = 4 + 3Q, Q in hundreds.

| Price (£) | Quantity | Surplus working | Surplus |
|---|---|---|---|
| 13 | 3 | ½ × 3 × 9 | 13.5 |
| 10 | 2 | ½ × 2 × 6 | 6.0 |

A fall of £3 cuts surplus by 7.5. Check: rectangle 2 × 3 = 6 plus triangle ½ × 1 × 3 = 1.5 gives 7.5.

**Evaluating a change in producer surplus:**
- Surplus is not profit, because fixed costs still have to be paid.
- Price rises from higher demand help more than rises from higher costs.
- A cost-saving technology may lower the price and the surplus per unit while volume rises, so work out the net effect.
- The curves are models and cannot be read exactly in real markets.

## Must-know distinctions

- Contraction (price rises) is not a decrease in demand (a leftward shift).
- Movement is caused by own price; a shift is caused by everything else.
- Adding market demand: add quantities across, not prices.
- Surplus rectangle: existing units gain. Surplus triangle: new units gain.
- Derived demand is about inputs; composite demand is about uses.

## Quick self-test

1. Three firms supply 12, 18 and 25 units at £5. What is market supply at £5?
2. Demand for tyres depends on demand for cars. Name this type of demand.
3. Good A gives 20 utils at £4. Good B gives 18 utils at £3. Which should the consumer buy more of?
4. Demand is P = 40 − 8Q. Calculate consumer surplus at a price of £24.
5. Price falls to £16. Calculate the new consumer surplus and the change.
6. Split that change into a rectangle and a triangle.
7. Supply is P = 3 + 2Q. Calculate producer surplus at a price of £11.
8. Price falls to £9. Calculate the new producer surplus and the fall, split into rectangle and triangle.
9. A tax on producers is raised. Is this a movement or a shift of supply, and in which direction?
10. Give one example of joint supply and one of competitive supply.

### Answers

1. 12 + 18 + 25 = **55 units**.
2. **Derived demand**.
3. B has 18 ÷ 3 = 6 utils per pound and A has 20 ÷ 4 = 5, so buy **more of B**.
4. Q = (40 − 24) ÷ 8 = 2; surplus = ½ × 2 × 16 = **£16**.
5. Q = (40 − 16) ÷ 8 = 3; surplus = ½ × 3 × 24 = 36; change = 36 − 16 = **£20 rise**.
6. Rectangle 2 × 8 = 16; triangle ½ × 1 × 8 = 4; total **20**.
7. Q = (11 − 3) ÷ 2 = 4; surplus = ½ × 4 × 8 = **£16**.
8. Q = (9 − 3) ÷ 2 = 3; surplus = ½ × 3 × 6 = 9; fall = **£7**, made of rectangle 3 × 2 = 6 and triangle ½ × 1 × 2 = 1.
9. A **shift left** (a decrease in supply), because tax is not the good's own price.
10. Joint: beef and hides. Competitive: a farmer's land used for wheat or barley (other valid examples gain credit).

## Where marks are usually lost

- Confusing a contraction with a decrease in demand, or an extension with an increase.
- Drawing a shift without labelling the axes, both curves and the new quantity.
- Adding prices instead of quantities when building market demand.
- Using the wrong height for the surplus triangle.
- Leaving out the rectangle when surplus changes with price.
- Listing shifters without saying which curve moves and in which direction.
- Describing consumer surplus in words only, with no reference to the area on the diagram.
- Stopping at "surplus rises" in an evaluation question, without weighing who gains, why price moved and what the model leaves out.
