Sub-topic 5.3 of the Cambridge O Level Environmental Management 5014 syllabus (2027-2029) has only two outcomes, but they carry a lot of content. Outcome 5.3.1 asks you to describe ten named strategies for conserving "the biodiversity and genetic resources of natural ecosystems". Outcome 5.3.2 asks you to **discuss** their benefits and limitations, which is where the marks for extended answers lie. This guide explains how each strategy works, what it protects and what it cannot do, and shows how to build a discussion answer that compares strategies rather than listing them.

For the whole of Topic 5, see the [ecosystems, biodiversity and fieldwork study guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-environmental-management-ecosystems-biodiversity-and-fieldwork-study-guide/).

## Two things being conserved

The syllabus wording names two targets, and good answers keep them apart.

- **Biodiversity**: the variety of living things, including the number of different species and the variety of habitats.
- **Genetic resources**: the genes carried by wild species and varieties, which may be valuable in future for crops, livestock or medicines.

Some strategies protect both at once (a national park keeps whole habitats with their species and their genes). Others protect genes but not habitats (a seed bank). Saying which target a strategy protects is a quick way to show understanding.

## The ten strategies

### Protecting species where they live

**(a) Sustainable harvesting of wild plant and animal species.** People take wild plants, fish or animals at a rate no greater than the population can replace, using limits such as quotas, closed seasons, minimum sizes or protected breeding areas. *Benefit:* people keep an income and a food supply, so they have a reason to look after the resource. *Limitation:* setting a safe limit needs reliable population data, and limits must be enforced.

**(b) Sustainable forestry.** Timber is taken in ways that keep the forest standing, for example by felling selected trees, replanting, and leaving enough mature trees to keep the habitat and its seed sources. *Benefit:* long-term timber supply with the habitat retained. *Limitation:* it is slower and less profitable in the short term than clear-felling, and needs monitoring.

**(c) National parks and reserves.** Areas are protected by law, with limits on building, farming, hunting or extraction. *Benefit:* the whole ecosystem is conserved, including species no one has yet studied. *Limitation:* parks need rangers and funding; local people may lose land or income; animals that move beyond the boundary are unprotected.

**(d) Wildlife corridors.** Strips of habitat link separated areas, such as two forest reserves divided by farmland. *Benefit:* animals can move to find food and mates, so small populations are no longer isolated and keep their genetic variety. *Limitation:* land for corridors may be expensive or disputed, and narrow corridors can be crossed by roads or used by poachers.

**(g) Ecotourism.** Visitors pay to see wildlife in natural areas, and the income supports conservation and local jobs. *Benefit:* living animals and standing forest become worth more than poached animals or cleared land. *Limitation:* visitors disturb wildlife and need roads, lodges and waste disposal; income falls if tourist numbers drop.

### Storing or breeding species away from where they live

**(e) Seed banks.** Seeds are dried and stored at low temperature so they stay alive for many years. *Benefit:* a very large number of plant varieties can be stored cheaply in a small space, protecting genetic resources against loss in the wild. *Limitation:* not all seeds survive drying and freezing; it does not protect the habitat; stored seeds do not evolve with changing conditions.

**(f) Zoos and captive breeding.** Endangered animals are kept and bred, sometimes for release back into the wild. *Benefit:* can save a species that is close to extinction and supports research and education. *Limitation:* expensive; only small numbers can be kept, so genetic variety is limited; released animals may not survive if the habitat has not been restored.

### Controlling people through law and cooperation

**(h) International cooperation against animal trade.** Countries work together, sharing information and policing borders, to stop illegal trade in wild animals and their parts. *Benefit:* tackles a cause of decline that no country can stop alone. *Limitation:* depends on every country taking part and enforcing it; high prices on the illegal market keep the trade profitable.

**(i) Regulation of trade in vulnerable and endangered species.** Trade is banned or allowed only with permits. The international agreement best known for this is CITES, the Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species of Wild Fauna and Flora. *Benefit:* reduces demand-driven harvesting. *Limitation:* permits can be forged and smuggling continues where enforcement is weak.

**(j) Classifying organisms in terms of threat level.** Species are placed in categories: **threatened** (at risk of extinction if threats continue), **endangered** (at high risk of extinction in the wild) and **extinct** (no living individuals remain). *Benefit:* shows which species need action most urgently, so limited money is directed well. *Limitation:* classification alone protects nothing; many species have never been assessed.

## How strategies work together

No single strategy deals with every threat. Think in layers:

1. **Protect the habitat** (parks, reserves, corridors, sustainable forestry).
2. **Reduce the pressure** (sustainable harvesting, trade regulation, international cooperation).
3. **Keep a back-up** (seed banks, zoos and captive breeding).
4. **Decide priorities** (threat classification).
5. **Pay for it and win local support** (ecotourism, sustainable harvesting income).

A strong discussion answer shows that one layer fails without another: a reserve does little if poachers can sell what they take, and captive breeding has no purpose if there is no habitat to release animals into.

## Worked example 1: evaluating a corridor with data

*The figures are invented for practice.* Two forest reserves were joined by a corridor in 2016. A survey counted one primate species in both reserves together.

| Year | Total count |
|---|---|
| 2016 | 240 |
| 2021 | 276 |
| 2026 | 312 |

Change 2016-2026 = 312 - 240 = **72**. Percentage increase = 72 / 240 x 100 = **30%**.

The data support the corridor's success, but a careful answer adds that the increase could have other causes (for example, less hunting) and that a single species is limited evidence. That is the kind of judgement "discuss" and "suggest" questions reward.

## Worked example 2: annotated model answer

**Question:** Discuss the benefits and limitations of seed banks and national parks for conserving genetic resources. **[6]**

**Answer:** Seed banks store the seeds of many plant varieties at low temperature, so genetic material is kept safe even if the plant dies out in the wild *(benefit)*. They are relatively cheap per species stored and take little space *(benefit)*. However, they protect only plants, some seeds cannot survive storage, and the habitat is not protected *(limitations)*. National parks protect whole ecosystems, so they conserve the genes of every species present, including animals and unstudied species *(benefit)*. But they need funding and enforcement, and conflict with local people over land can lead to illegal use *(limitation)*. Overall, national parks protect more genetic resources because they keep species evolving in their habitat, while seed banks are a valuable back-up *(judgement)*.

Why it scores well: both strategies get a benefit and a limitation, the answer stays focused on genetic resources, and it ends with a reasoned conclusion.

## Common mistakes

- Saying zoos or seed banks "protect the habitat". They protect species or genes away from the habitat.
- Mixing up (h) and (i): (h) is countries cooperating against the animal trade; (i) is regulating trade in vulnerable and endangered species, including plants.
- Describing ecotourism as having no downside.
- Defining endangered as "rare". The categories are about the risk of extinction.
- Listing strategies in a discuss question without benefits, limitations and a conclusion.

## Exam technique

**Describe** a strategy: say what is done and how it conserves biodiversity or genes. **Discuss** benefits and limitations: give at least one of each for each strategy named, then a judgement that depends on the threat (habitat loss, poaching, trade). **Suggest** questions often give a case, such as a species declining because of poaching: match the strategy to the threat named in the information.

## Check your understanding

1. Which strategy links isolated habitats? *Wildlife corridors.*
2. Give one limitation of captive breeding. *Small numbers mean limited genetic variety.*
3. What does the classification "extinct" mean? *No living individuals of the species remain.*
4. How does ecotourism give local people a reason to conserve wildlife? *It brings income and jobs that depend on wildlife staying alive.*
5. A population rises from 150 to 195. Calculate the percentage increase. *45 / 150 x 100 = 30%.*
