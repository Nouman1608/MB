## Definitions to learn word for word

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Ion | A charged particle formed when an atom loses or gains electrons |
| Ionic bond | Strong electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions |
| Giant ionic lattice | A regular 3-D arrangement of oppositely charged ions |
| Covalent bond | A shared pair of electrons between two atoms |
| Why a covalent bond holds | Electrostatic attraction between the shared pair and the nuclei of both atoms |
| Intermolecular forces | Weak forces of attraction between molecules |
| Metallic bonding (Paper 2 only) | Electrostatic attraction between positive metal ions and delocalised electrons |

## 1(f) Ionic bonding

### Forming ions (1.37)

- Metals **lose** outer electrons → **positive** ions.
- Non-metals **gain** electrons → **negative** ions.
- The ion has a full outer shell (a noble gas configuration). Protons do not change.

| Atom | Configuration | Ion | Ion configuration |
|---|---|---|---|
| Li | 2.1 | Li⁺ | 2 |
| Mg | 2.8.2 | Mg²⁺ | 2.8 |
| Al | 2.8.3 | Al³⁺ | 2.8 |
| K | 2.8.8.1 | K⁺ | 2.8.8 |
| N | 2.5 | N³⁻ | 2.8 |
| O | 2.6 | O²⁻ | 2.8 |
| Cl | 2.8.7 | Cl⁻ | 2.8.8 |

### Ion charges (1.38)

| 1+ | 2+ | 3+ | 1− | 2− | 3− |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Group 1, Ag⁺, H⁺, NH₄⁺ | Group 2, Cu²⁺, Fe²⁺, Pb²⁺, Zn²⁺ | Group 3, Fe³⁺ | Group 7, OH⁻, NO₃⁻ | Group 6, CO₃²⁻, SO₄²⁻ | Group 5 (N³⁻) |

Group 1 → 1+, Group 2 → 2+, Group 3 → 3+. Group 5 → 3−, Group 6 → 2−, Group 7 → 1−. Roman numerals give the metal ion charge: iron(II) = Fe²⁺, iron(III) = Fe³⁺.

### Method: writing a formula (1.39)

```
1. Write both ions with charges.
2. Find the lowest common multiple (LCM) of the charges.
3. Number of each ion = LCM / its charge.
4. Bracket any group ion used more than once.
5. Check: total + charge = total - charge.
```

Small reminder: Ca²⁺ and NO₃⁻ → LCM 2 → one Ca²⁺, two NO₃⁻ → Ca(NO₃)₂.

### Ionic dot-and-cross (1.40)

- Only Groups 1, 2, 3 combined with Groups 5, 6, 7 are required.
- Show only outer electrons; dots for one element, crosses for the other.
- Show **transfer**: square brackets round each ion, charge outside the bracket at the top right.
- Negative ion: 8 outer electrons, with the gained ones drawn as the other symbol.
- Number of ions drawn must match the formula (e.g. two Na⁺ for one O²⁻).

### Structure and properties (1.41-1.43)

| Property | Reason |
|---|---|
| High melting and boiling points | Many strong electrostatic attractions between oppositely charged ions throughout the lattice need a lot of energy to overcome |
| Solid does **not** conduct | Ions held in fixed positions |
| Molten or aqueous **does** conduct | Ions free to move and carry charge |

## 1(g) Covalent bonding

### Covalent dot-and-cross (1.46)

- Overlap the outer-shell circles; each shared pair goes in the overlap.
- Each atom (except H) ends with 8 outer electrons; H ends with 2.
- Draw every lone pair.
- Check: total outer electrons = 2 × shared pairs + 2 × lone pairs.

| Molecule | Shared pairs | Lone pairs | Outer electrons |
|---|---|---|---|
| H₂ | 1 | 0 | 2 |
| O₂ | 2 (double) | 4 | 12 |
| N₂ | 3 (triple) | 2 | 10 |
| Cl₂ | 1 | 6 | 14 |
| HCl | 1 | 3 | 8 |
| H₂O | 2 | 2 | 8 |
| NH₃ | 3 | 1 | 8 |
| CO₂ | 4 (two double) | 4 | 16 |
| CH₄ | 4 | 0 | 8 |
| C₂H₆ | 7 | 0 | 14 |
| C₂H₄ | 6 (one double) | 0 | 12 |
| CH₃Cl | 4 | 3 | 14 |

The list in the specification covers: H₂, O₂, N₂, the halogens, the hydrogen halides, water, ammonia, carbon dioxide, and organic molecules with up to two carbon atoms (including methane, ethane, ethene and molecules containing halogen atoms).

### Simple molecular substances (1.47-1.48)

- Strong covalent bonds **within** molecules; weak intermolecular forces **between** molecules.
- Melting or boiling overcomes only the intermolecular forces → little energy → gases, liquids, or low-melting solids.
- Higher relative molecular mass → stronger intermolecular forces → higher melting and boiling points (in general).

Small reminder (halogens, Mr from A_r F = 19, Cl = 35.5, Br = 80, I = 127):

| | F₂ | Cl₂ | Br₂ | I₂ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mr | 38 | 71 | 160 | 254 |
| State at 25 °C | gas | gas | liquid | solid |

### Giant covalent structures (1.49-1.50)

- Network of strong covalent bonds throughout → melting **breaks covalent bonds** → high melting and boiling points; solids.

| | Diamond | Graphite | C60 fullerene |
|---|---|---|---|
| Structure type | Giant covalent | Giant covalent (layers) | Simple molecular |
| Bonds per carbon | 4 | 3 | 3 |
| Hardness | Very hard | Soft, slippery | Soft |
| Why | Rigid 3-D network | Weak forces between layers; layers slide | Weak intermolecular forces |
| Electrical conductivity | None | Conducts | Poor |
| Why | No free electrons | One delocalised electron per C, moves along layers | Electrons cannot move from molecule to molecule |

### Conductivity (1.51)

Covalent compounds do not usually conduct electricity. Graphite is the exception to learn.

## 1(h) Metallic bonding -- Paper 2 only

### 2-D lattice diagram (1.52C)

- Regular, closely packed rows of circles, each labelled + (or 2+ for magnesium): **positive ions**.
- Electrons (e⁻) drawn between the ions: **delocalised electrons**.
- Both labels are needed.

### Bonding and properties (1.53C-1.54C)

| Property | Explanation |
|---|---|
| Bonding | Electrostatic attraction between positive metal ions and delocalised electrons |
| Conducts electricity (solid and liquid) | Delocalised electrons free to move through the structure and carry charge |
| Malleable | Layers of positive ions slide over each other; attraction to the delocalised electrons is kept |
| Usually high melting point | Many strong attractions to overcome |

## Must-know distinctions

- **Ionic diagram vs covalent diagram:** brackets and charges (transfer) vs overlapping circles (sharing). Never mix them.
- **Simple molecular vs giant covalent:** melting overcomes intermolecular forces vs breaks covalent bonds.
- **Graphite vs C60:** both have three bonds per carbon, but graphite is a giant layered structure that conducts; C60 is molecular and a poor conductor.
- **Ionic vs metallic conduction:** ions must be free to move (molten or aqueous only) vs delocalised electrons move (solid too).
- **Lose vs gain:** metals lose electrons; nothing gains protons.

## Quick self-test

1. Give the electronic configuration of the sulfide ion.
2. Write the formula of zinc hydroxide.
3. Write the formula of copper(II) nitrate.
4. Write the formula of potassium sulfate.
5. In forming potassium sulfide, how many electrons does each potassium atom lose, how many does the sulfur atom gain, and what is the formula?
6. How many shared pairs and lone pairs are in a molecule of hydrogen bromide?
7. How many shared pairs and lone pairs are in a molecule of dichloromethane, CH₂Cl₂?
8. How many shared pairs of electrons are in a molecule of ethane?
9. Explain why chlorine is a gas at room temperature but iodine is a solid.
10. Explain why C60 is soft although diamond is very hard.
11. Explain why solid potassium bromide does not conduct but its aqueous solution does.
12. (Paper 2 only) State what is meant by metallic bonding.

### Answers

1. **2.8.8** (sulfur 2.8.6 gains two electrons).
2. **Zn(OH)₂**.
3. **Cu(NO₃)₂**.
4. **K₂SO₄**.
5. Each K loses **1**; S gains **2**; formula **K₂S**.
6. **1 shared pair**, **3 lone pairs** (on Br).
7. **4 shared pairs**, **6 lone pairs** (three on each Cl). Check: 4 + 2 + 14 = 20 = 2 × 4 + 2 × 6.
8. **7** (one C–C and six C–H).
9. Both are simple molecular. Iodine has a higher Mr (254 vs 71), so its intermolecular forces are stronger and need more energy to overcome.
10. C60 is simple molecular: only weak intermolecular forces hold the molecules together. Diamond is giant covalent: every carbon is held by four strong covalent bonds in a rigid network.
11. In the solid the ions are fixed in the lattice. In solution the ions are free to move and carry charge.
12. The electrostatic attraction between positive metal ions and delocalised electrons.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "electrostatic attraction between atoms" in an ionic bonding answer. It is between **oppositely charged ions**.
- Missing the "shared pair" wording: "atoms share electrons" alone is vaguer than the definition, which needs a **pair** of electrons.
- Forgetting brackets: Fe₂(SO₄)₃ written as Fe₂SO₄₃, or (NH₄)₂CO₃ as NH₄2CO₃.
- Putting the charge inside the bracket, or omitting it, on an ionic dot-and-cross diagram.
- Drawing too few ions, e.g. one Na⁺ with O²⁻.
- Leaving lone pairs off N, O or Cl in a covalent diagram.
- "Covalent bonds break when it boils" for a simple molecular substance.
- Explaining graphite's softness with weak covalent bonds rather than weak forces between layers.
- In metals, saying "free electrons and positive atoms": write **positive ions** and **delocalised electrons**.
- Saying ionic compounds conduct because "electrons move". It is the ions.
