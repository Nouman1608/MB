## Why the Doppler effect happens (3.8.3 a)

Think of a source sending out wave crests at a steady rate.

- **Source still.** Each crest spreads out from the same point. The crests are evenly spaced, and every observer measures the emitted wavelength.
- **Source moving towards you.** Between one crest and the next, the source moves closer to you. Each new crest starts from a point nearer to you, so the crests arrive bunched together. The observed wavelength is shorter.
- **Source moving away.** Each new crest starts from further away, so the crests are spread out. The observed wavelength is longer.

The speed of the wave through the medium (or through space, for light) does not change. Since wave speed = frequency × wavelength, a shorter wavelength at the same speed means more crests arrive each second: a higher frequency. A longer wavelength means a lower frequency.

| Source | Observed wavelength | Observed frequency |
|---|---|---|
| Moving towards the observer | Decreases | Increases |
| Moving away from the observer | Increases | Decreases |

The specification names light, sound and microwaves as possible wave sources. The effect is the same idea for each, because it depends only on the motion of the source relative to the observer.

**Worked example 1: sound from a moving source.** A train horn emits sound of frequency 450 Hz. Sound travels at 340 m/s. As the train moves away, a listener hears 420 Hz.

1. Emitted wavelength = 340 ÷ 450 = 0.756 m.
2. Observed wavelength = 340 ÷ 420 = 0.810 m.
3. Increase = 0.810 − 0.756 = 0.054 m.

The listener hears a lower frequency and a longer wavelength, as expected for a source moving away.

## Red shift (3.8.3 b)

For light, a longer wavelength means a shift towards the red end of the visible spectrum. That is why an increase in the observed wavelength of light from a receding source is called **red shift**.

How do astronomers know the wavelength has changed? Elements produce light at particular, fixed wavelengths, measured in laboratories on Earth. Light from a galaxy shows the same pattern of wavelengths, but every one is longer by a similar fraction. The pattern is recognisable, so the shift can be measured.

The specification's three observations:

1. There is an observed increase in the wavelength of light from most distant galaxies.
2. The further away the galaxies, the faster they are moving.
3. The further away the galaxies, the bigger the observed increase in wavelength.

**Worked example 2: reading galaxy data.** A line has a laboratory wavelength of 656.3 nm. The table shows its observed wavelength in light from four galaxies.

| Galaxy | Distance (million light-years) | Observed wavelength (nm) |
|---|---|---|
| A | 50 | 658.6 |
| B | 120 | 661.9 |
| C | 200 | 665.7 |
| D | 330 | 671.8 |

1. Increases: A 2.3 nm, B 5.6 nm, C 9.4 nm, D 15.5 nm.
2. Every galaxy shows an increase, so every galaxy is moving away.
3. The increase grows with distance, so the more distant galaxies are moving away faster.

These are illustrative values, rounded, chosen to show the pattern; a data question will supply its own figures. Notice that galaxy D is about 6.6 times as far as A and its shift is about 6.7 times as large: the shift rises roughly in proportion to distance.

## From red shift to an expanding universe

Red shift alone tells you galaxies are moving away. The reasoning that leads to the Big Bang model goes further:

1. Light from most distant galaxies is red-shifted, so they are moving away from us.
2. More distant galaxies move away faster.
3. This pattern is what you would see if **space itself is expanding**, carrying the galaxies apart. Galaxies with more space between them separate faster.
4. If the universe is expanding now, then in the past everything was closer together.
5. Tracing the expansion back leads to a very small initial point. This is the **Big Bang model**: the universe began from a very small initial point and has been expanding since.

Notice what the evidence does: it supports the model. It does not prove it. Examiners credit careful wording such as "suggests" and "supports".

An important point about step 3: observers in any galaxy would see the same pattern. It does not mean we are at the centre of the universe.

## Cosmic microwave background radiation (3.8.3 c, d)

CMBR is a form of electromagnetic radiation filling the universe. It is in the microwave region of the spectrum and arrives from every direction. It comes from radiation that was present shortly after the beginning of the universe.

The specification states that the Big Bang theory is currently the only theory that can explain the existence of CMBR. So CMBR is a second, separate line of evidence:

| Evidence | What it shows | How it supports the Big Bang |
|---|---|---|
| Red shift of distant galaxies | Galaxies are moving apart, faster with distance | Space is expanding, so it was once very small |
| CMBR | Radiation fills the whole universe | It is explained as radiation left from shortly after the beginning; no other current theory explains it |

Scientists believe the universe began with a "big bang" about 14 thousand million years ago (1.4 × 10¹⁰ years).

## Annotated model answer

**Question:** Explain how observations of distant galaxies and of CMBR support the Big Bang theory. [6 marks]

**Answer:** Light from most distant galaxies has a longer wavelength than expected: it is red-shifted (1). This shows the galaxies are moving away from us (1). The further away a galaxy is, the bigger its red shift, so the faster it is moving away (1). This suggests that space itself is expanding, so in the past the universe must have been much smaller, starting from a very small initial point (1). CMBR is microwave radiation that fills the universe (1). It is explained as radiation left over from shortly after the beginning of the universe, and the Big Bang theory is currently the only theory that can explain it (1).

*Why it scores:* it gives the observation, the deduction and the link to the model for red shift, then does the same for CMBR. A list of facts without "so" links, or an answer that discusses only red shift, caps at about half marks.

## Common mistakes

- Saying red shift means the galaxy "looks red" or "turns red". The wavelengths are longer; the light need not appear red.
- Saying the speed of light changes. The wavelength and frequency change; the wave speed does not.
- Getting the direction wrong: moving away gives longer wavelength and lower frequency.
- Writing "the Big Bang was an explosion from a point in space". The model is that space itself expands.
- Claiming red shift proves the Big Bang. It provides evidence that supports it.
- Describing CMBR as radiation from stars, or as sound. It is electromagnetic radiation from shortly after the beginning.
- Quoting the age as 14 million years. It is about 14 thousand million years.

## Exam technique

- **State** the Doppler effect: a change in observed wavelength and frequency when the source moves relative to the observer.
- **Describe** red shift: increase in wavelength of light from distant galaxies; bigger for more distant galaxies.
- **Explain** how red shift is evidence: observation, deduction (moving away, faster with distance), conclusion (space expanding, small starting point).
- **Calculate**: use wave speed = frequency × wavelength, and give the change in wavelength as observed minus emitted.
- **Evaluate** a claim: say what the evidence supports and what it does not show.

## Frequently asked questions

**Does red shift mean galaxies are moving away from Earth in particular?**
No. Most distant galaxies are moving away from us, but the expansion of space means observers in any galaxy would see the same pattern.

**Why is CMBR important evidence?**
The Big Bang theory is currently the only theory that can explain the existence of CMBR, which comes from radiation present shortly after the universe began.
