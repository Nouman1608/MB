## Section A (practise using a passage from your studied Elizabethan/Jacobean tragedy)

**1.** Using a passage of your choice, explore how the playwright presents the beginnings of the tragic hero's downfall. **[25]**

**2.** Using a passage of your choice, explore how far the playwright presents the tragic hero as isolated from those around them. **[25]**

## Section B (practise using your studied later dramatic tragedy)

**3.** "A tragic hero's downfall is always their own fault." Discuss this view in relation to your studied later dramatic tragedy. **[25]**

**4.** "Tragedy depends on the audience's sympathy for the central character." Discuss this view in relation to your studied later dramatic tragedy. **[25]**

---

## Answers (worked approach — marks depend on your studied plays)

**1.** A top-band response opens with **close analysis of the passage's** language and dramatic method [10], identifies a specific marker of the hero's developing hamartia or error of judgement within that passage [6], and connects the moment outward to the **play's tragic structure** as a whole, considering how an audience would experience the moment in performance [9].

**2.** A strong response identifies specific textual markers of isolation within the chosen passage -- through soliloquy, staging (positioning on stage, other characters' physical distance), or dialogue that signals the hero is misunderstood or alone in their perspective [10], analyses how the playwright's specific dramatic method creates this sense of isolation for an audience [8], and considers whether this isolation is self-inflicted or imposed by circumstance, connecting the point to the play's wider tragic arc [7].

**3.** A strong essay engages critically with the claim rather than simply agreeing or disagreeing [4], weighs the hero's **own responsibility** (hamartia, choices, flaws) against **external forces** (fate, other characters, circumstance) using specific evidence from across the play [14], and reaches a sustained, evidence-based judgement about where the balance lies, ideally informed by an awareness of critical debate about tragedy as a genre [7].

**4.** A strong essay considers what specifically generates audience sympathy for the tragic hero at different points in the play (their own suffering, their self-awareness, an admirable quality undermined by their flaw) [10], weighs this against moments where the hero's actions might alienate audience sympathy instead [8], and reaches a reasoned judgement about whether sympathy is essential to how the play achieves its tragic effect, or whether other elements (spectacle, catharsis, structure) matter just as much [7].

## How to adapt these questions to your own set texts

Because this resource deliberately does not name specific plays, the most effective way to use it is to substitute your own studied Elizabethan/Jacobean tragedy and later dramatic tragedy into each question exactly as worded, then write a full timed response (roughly 35-40 minutes per question, reflecting the 25-mark allocation relative to the paper's overall timing). Before writing a Section A response specifically, select your practice passage deliberately -- choose a moment genuinely relevant to the question asked (the hero's downfall beginning, or a moment of isolation) rather than a generic favourite passage, since close analysis anchored to a well-chosen passage is what the question actually rewards.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Section A answers that don't engage closely with the specific passage given, drifting into general essay-writing.
- Section B answers that assert a position without weighing the counter-view using textual evidence.
- Treating "tragic hero" and "hamartia" as labels to apply rather than ideas to debate against the specific play.
- Ignoring staging and performance, treating the play purely as printed text.
- Answering the given claim as a yes/no question rather than a genuine debate, missing the "discuss this view" instruction's expectation of critical engagement with more than one position.
- Writing a response that could apply to almost any tragedy in the genre, rather than one clearly rooted in the specific details, structure and language of the play actually studied.

## Building an exam-ready evidence bank in advance

Rather than searching for suitable passages or quotations under exam pressure, prepare a short bank of key moments from both your Elizabethan/Jacobean tragedy and your later dramatic tragedy well before the exam -- each one briefly annotated with which tragic concept (hamartia, catharsis, isolation, fate versus responsibility, audience sympathy) it best demonstrates. Versatile evidence, capable of supporting more than one type of question, is more valuable revision material than evidence tied to only a single possible essay title, since the specific claim or focus a question takes cannot be predicted in advance.

## Why command words matter as much as content knowledge

"Explore" and "discuss this view" both invite genuinely analytical, argument-driven responses rather than descriptive plot summary or one-sided assertion -- a response that narrates the passage or simply agrees with the given claim, however accurately, will not reach the top band against either command word. Treat every practice question as requiring a sustained critical argument, built from specific textual evidence and, where relevant, awareness of critical debate about tragedy as a dramatic form, rather than a set of separate observations about the play.
