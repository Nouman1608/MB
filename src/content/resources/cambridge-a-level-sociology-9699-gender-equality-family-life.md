Section 4.1, *Gender equality and experiences of family life*, begins the second half of Paper 2 in Cambridge International AS & A Level Sociology (9699), *Family roles and changing relationships*. It asks whether men and women are equal inside the family, and whether family life is good or bad for the people in it. The syllabus links it to the key concepts *Power, control and resistance* and *Inequality and opportunity*.

The [Paper 2 family guide](/resources/a-level-cambridge-sociology-the-family/) outlines this section; this page supplies the studies and arguments. Revise with the [4.1 revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-sociology-9699-gender-equality-family-life-revision-notes/) and practise with the [4.1 practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-sociology-9699-gender-equality-family-life-practice/). Feminism's general critique of functionalist and Marxist theories is covered in the [3.1 study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-sociology-9699-perspectives-role-of-family/).

## What the syllabus lists

- Different feminist perspectives on equality and power in the family, including liberal, radical and Marxist feminist.
- Conjugal roles and debates about gender equality in the family, including housework, childcare, power and emotion work.
- Debates about whether the experience of family life is positive or negative for family members.

## Three feminist perspectives on equality and power

| | Liberal feminist | Radical feminist | Marxist feminist |
|---|---|---|---|
| Main cause of inequality | Socialisation, attitudes and unequal laws | Patriarchy: men as a group dominate women | Capitalism, which uses women's unpaid and low-paid labour |
| View of change | Real progress through education, employment and law; men gradually doing more | Little real change; the family remains a key site of male power | Change limited while capitalism needs domestic labour |
| Solution | Further reform: equal pay, parental leave, changing attitudes | Radical change to or separation from patriarchal family structures | Ending capitalism alongside challenging gender roles |
| Example | Equal pay and anti-discrimination laws as steps forward | Christine Delphy and Diana Leonard (1992): men benefit from women's unpaid work | Fran Ansley (1972): wives absorb men's work frustrations |

**Evaluating each.** Liberal feminists are criticised by radical feminists for mistaking legal equality for real equality. Radical feminists are criticised for treating all men and all women as uniform groups and for ignoring evidence of change. Marxist feminists are criticised for linking everything to capitalism, though gender inequality exists in non-capitalist societies too.

## Conjugal roles

**Conjugal roles** are the roles of partners in a marriage or cohabiting relationship. Elizabeth Bott (*Family and Social Network*, 1957) distinguished:

- **Segregated conjugal roles**: separate tasks and separate leisure, such as a breadwinner husband and a homemaker wife.
- **Joint conjugal roles**: shared tasks, decisions and leisure.

### The march of progress view

Michael Young and Peter Willmott's *The Symmetrical Family* (1973) argued that families were becoming **symmetrical**: roles not identical but similar, with both partners working, sharing domestic tasks and spending leisure together at home. They linked this to women's employment, smaller families, better homes and new technology.

### Critics of the symmetrical family

- **Ann Oakley** (*The Sociology of Housework*, 1974) argued that Young and Willmott exaggerated men's contribution: a husband was counted as helping if he did a single household task a week, such as washing up once. In her own interviews with London housewives, only a minority of husbands took a high share of housework.
- **The dual burden**: women in paid work still do most housework, so they carry two jobs.
- **Arlie Hochschild** (*The Second Shift*, 1989), studying dual-earner couples in the USA, described women working a "second shift" at home after paid work.
- **Childcare**: fathers' involvement has grown, but critics argue men often take on the more enjoyable parts, such as play, while mothers keep the routine and responsibility.

## Power in the family

**Decision-making.** Stephen Edgell (*Middle-Class Couples*, 1980) found that important decisions, such as moving house or major finances, were made by husbands alone or jointly with husbands having the final say, while wives made more frequent but less important decisions, such as food and children's clothes.

**Money management.** Jan Pahl (*Money and Marriage*, 1989) studied how couples control money, from allowance systems (one partner gives the other a set sum) to pooling. Even where money was pooled, men often had more say over large spending, so pooling does not guarantee equal power.

**Domestic violence.** R. Emerson Dobash and Russell Dobash (*Violence Against Wives*, 1979) argued that men's violence against wives was a means of control, triggered when husbands felt their authority was challenged. Radical feminists see domestic violence as an extreme expression of patriarchal power.

## Emotion work

Hochschild's *The Managed Heart* (1983) introduced the idea of managing feelings as a form of work. In families, **emotion work** means managing the emotional well-being of others: remembering birthdays, calming conflicts, listening to worries. Jean Duncombe and Dennis Marsden (1995) found that women felt they did most of the emotion work in their relationships, in addition to paid work and housework, a **triple shift**.

## Is family life positive or negative?

### Positive views

- **Functionalists**: Parsons saw the family stabilising adult personalities and giving emotional security; Ronald Fletcher (1966) argued the modern family meets members' needs more fully than before.
- **March of progress**: Young and Willmott saw family life becoming more equal and home-centred.
- **Personal-life perspectives**: many people describe family as their main source of love, identity and support, including relationships beyond the nuclear family.

### Negative views

- **The dark side**: Edmund Leach's 1967 Reith Lectures (*A Runaway World?*) argued that the isolated nuclear family, cut off from wider kin, becomes inward-looking and a source of tension and discontent.
- **R.D. Laing** (*The Politics of the Family*, 1971) argued that family interaction can damage members psychologically through manipulation and control.
- **Domestic violence and child abuse** show the family can be a dangerous place.
- **Feminists**: the dual burden, triple shift and unequal power mean family life is experienced differently by men and women.

### Evaluating the debate

Experience depends on gender, age, class, ethnicity and family type. The same family may be positive for some members and negative for others. Avoid treating "the family" as having one experience.

## How far has equality advanced? Weighing the evidence

A balanced conclusion separates three things that are easy to blur:

- **Attitudes**: in many societies more people now say housework and childcare should be shared. Attitudes have often changed faster than behaviour.
- **Time spent**: men's hours of housework and childcare have risen in many studies, but usually from a low base, and women's hours remain higher.
- **Responsibility**: who plans, remembers and worries. Even where tasks are shared, the "mental load" of organising family life often stays with women, which links to emotion work.

Variation matters too. Dual-earner couples with similar incomes tend to share more than couples where one partner earns much more; expectations differ between cultures and religious traditions; and roles can change across the life-course, for example when children arrive or partners retire.

## Worked example: two explanations

*Question (practice): Explain two reasons why gender inequality in housework may continue.*

> **Reason 1: gender socialisation.** Girls are often still socialised into caring and domestic tasks and boys less so, so as adults women may feel responsible for housework and men may see themselves as "helping". This is why Oakley found that even husbands who did some tasks did not take responsibility for them.
>
> **Reason 2: economic inequality.** Men on average still earn more than women in many societies, so when a couple decides who reduces paid work for childcare, it is often the woman. Less paid work then justifies her doing more domestic work, reinforcing the pattern.

Why it works: each reason is named, explained and supported with a concept or study, and both link back to housework.

## Common mistakes

- Treating feminism as one view; the syllabus names three strands.
- Using Young and Willmott without Oakley's criticism.
- Discussing housework only, when the syllabus also names childcare, power and emotion work.
- Assuming the family is either wholly positive or wholly negative.
- Forgetting to say which society or group a study examined.
