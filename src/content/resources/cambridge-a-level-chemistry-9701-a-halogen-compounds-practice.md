## Section A

**1.** State the reagent and catalyst needed to make bromobenzene from benzene. **[2]**

**2.** Write the equation for the formation of chlorobenzene from benzene. **[1]**

**3.** Name the two main organic products when methylbenzene reacts with chlorine in the presence of AlCl₃. **[2]**

**4.** State whether (chloromethyl)benzene, C₆H₅CH₂Cl, is a halogenoarene. Explain your answer. **[1]**

**5.** State what is observed when chlorobenzene is warmed with aqueous ethanolic silver nitrate. **[1]**

## Section B

**6.** Chloroethane and chlorobenzene are each heated under reflux with aqueous sodium hydroxide.

**(a)** Write an equation for the reaction that occurs with chloroethane, and name the organic product. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain, in terms of bonding, why chlorobenzene does not react under these conditions. **[3]**

**(c)** Suggest one further reason, not related to bond strength, why OH⁻ does not attack the carbon bonded to chlorine in chlorobenzene. **[1]**

**7.** A student claims that the C–Cl bond in chlorobenzene is unreactive because it is non-polar. Evaluate this claim. **[2]**

**8.** Compound E has the formula ClC₆H₄CH₂Cl, with one chlorine on the ring and one on the side-chain.

**(a)** Predict the organic product when E is heated under reflux with aqueous sodium hydroxide. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain why only one chlorine atom is replaced. **[2]**

**(c)** E is warmed with aqueous ethanolic silver nitrate. State what is seen, and calculate the mass of precipitate formed if 0.0100 mol of E reacts completely at the side-chain. (Ar: Ag = 107.9) **[2]**

**9.** Methylbenzene can be chlorinated to give three different compounds of formula C₇H₇Cl.

**(a)** Give the names of the two isomers formed when AlCl₃ is used. **[2]**

**(b)** Give the name of the isomer formed in ultraviolet light without a catalyst, and name the type of mechanism. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain why no significant amount of 3-chloromethylbenzene forms. **[1]**

**10.** 11.7 g of benzene is chlorinated using Cl₂ and AlCl₃, and 12.0 g of chlorobenzene is obtained.

**(a)** Calculate the theoretical mass of chlorobenzene. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the percentage yield. **[1]**

**11.** A student plans to make phenol by heating chlorobenzene under reflux with aqueous sodium hydroxide.

**(a)** Explain why this plan will not work. **[1]**

**(b)** State the starting material and the two steps, with conditions, used in the syllabus route to phenol. **[3]**

**12.** Explain the role of AlCl₃ in the chlorination of benzene, using equations to show how it generates the electrophile and is regenerated. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** Br₂ [1]; AlBr₃ (anhydrous) [1].

**2.** C₆H₆ + Cl₂ → C₆H₅Cl + HCl [1].

**3.** 2-chloromethylbenzene [1] and 4-chloromethylbenzene [1].

**4.** No: the chlorine is bonded to the sp³ carbon of the side-chain, not to a carbon in the ring [1].

**5.** No precipitate forms (the solution stays clear) [1]. No chloride ions are released, because the C–Cl bond in chlorobenzene is not broken under these conditions.

**6. (a)** CH₃CH₂Cl + NaOH → CH₃CH₂OH + NaCl [1]; ethanol [1].

**(b)** A lone pair on the chlorine is in a p orbital that overlaps with the delocalised π system of the ring [1]. The lone pair is partly delocalised, giving the C–Cl bond partial double-bond character [1], so the bond is shorter and stronger than in chloroethane and harder to break [1].

**(c)** The electron-rich π system repels the approaching nucleophile (or: substitution at a ring carbon would disrupt the stable delocalised ring) [1].

**7.** The claim is wrong: chlorine is more electronegative than carbon, so the C–Cl bond in chlorobenzene is still polar [1]. The low reactivity is due to the stronger C–Cl bond (lone-pair overlap with the π system) and the repulsion of nucleophiles by the ring's π electrons [1].

**8. (a)** ClC₆H₄CH₂OH, (4-chlorophenyl)methanol [1].

**(b)** The side-chain chlorine is on an sp³ carbon and is substituted by OH⁻ like a halogenoalkane [1]; the ring chlorine's C–Cl bond is strengthened by overlap of its lone pair with the π system, so it is not substituted [1].

**(c)** A white precipitate (of AgCl) [1]; 0.0100 × (107.9 + 35.5) = 0.0100 × 143.4 = 1.43 g [1].

**9. (a)** 2-chloromethylbenzene [1] and 4-chloromethylbenzene [1].

**(b)** (Chloromethyl)benzene, C₆H₅CH₂Cl [1]; free-radical substitution [1].

**(c)** The –CH₃ group directs electrophilic substitution to the 2- and 4-positions [1].

**10. (a)** n(C₆H₆) = 11.7 / 78.0 = 0.150 mol [1]; Mr(C₆H₅Cl) = 112.5, so theoretical mass = 0.150 × 112.5 = 16.9 g [1].

**(b)** 12.0 / 16.875 × 100 = 71.1% [1].

**11. (a)** Chlorobenzene does not undergo nucleophilic substitution with aqueous hydroxide under these conditions, because its C–Cl bond is strengthened by delocalisation and the ring repels the nucleophile [1].

**(b)** Start from phenylamine [1]; react it with HNO₂ (NaNO₂ and dilute acid) below 10 °C to form the diazonium salt [1]; then warm the diazonium salt with water to give phenol [1].

**12.** AlCl₃ is a halogen carrier: it accepts a lone pair from Cl₂ and polarises it, generating the electrophile: Cl₂ + AlCl₃ → Cl⁺ + AlCl₄⁻ [1]. The Cl⁺ is attacked by the ring, and H⁺ is lost from the intermediate [1]. The catalyst is regenerated: H⁺ + AlCl₄⁻ → HCl + AlCl₃, so it is not used up overall [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using UV light when a ring-substituted halogenoarene is wanted.
- Classifying (chloromethyl)benzene as a halogenoarene.
- Saying the C–Cl bond in chlorobenzene is non-polar.
- Giving "benzene is stable" without the p-orbital overlap and bond-strength argument.
- Forgetting to show the AlCl₃ catalyst being regenerated.
- Proposing hydrolysis of chlorobenzene as a route to phenol.
