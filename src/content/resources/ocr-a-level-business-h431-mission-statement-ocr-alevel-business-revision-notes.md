## The four specification points

1. Explain the nature and purpose of a mission statement.
2. Recommend and justify a suitable mission statement for a business.
3. Evaluate the advantages and disadvantages to a business of having one.
4. Evaluate the impact of changing a mission statement on a business and its stakeholders.

## Core recall

### Nature

- A short, public statement of why the business exists, what it does and for whom.
- Qualitative and open-ended: no figures, no deadline.
- Top of the planning chain: mission, then aims, then objectives, then strategy, then tactics.
- Revised rarely, usually after a major event.

### The four parts a statement can contain

| Part | Question it answers | Effect on decisions |
|---|---|---|
| Purpose | Why do we exist? | Sets what success means |
| Scope | Which products, customers, places? | Rules out moves outside the scope |
| Values | What do we believe in? | Guides choices between options of similar profit |
| Standards of behaviour | How do our people act? | Shapes service, training and targets |

More parts means more guidance, but also less flexibility.

### Purpose: who it is for

- **Employees:** shared direction, consistency across sites, culture, recruitment.
- **Customers:** part of the brand and the reason to choose the business.
- **Investors and lenders:** a signal of priorities and of the kind of return to expect.
- **Suppliers and community:** what the business will and will not do, for example local sourcing.

### Five-point test for a draft statement

1. Specific: could a rival use it word for word?
2. Memorable: one or two sentences.
3. Restrictive: does it rule anything out?
4. Accurate: does behaviour match it?
5. Durable: will it outlast this year's objectives?

### When it matters most (evaluation factors)

| Factor | Mission matters more when... | Mission matters less when... |
|---|---|---|
| Size and spread | Many sites, remote teams | Owner runs everything directly |
| Growth stage | New staff arriving fast | Stable workforce, mature business |
| Customer choice | Customers choose on values | Customers choose on price alone |
| Market change | Broad wording keeps options open | Narrow wording ties the firm to one product or place |
| Credibility | Actions match words | Actions contradict words, causing cynicism |

### Changing a mission statement

Triggers: takeover or new owners, new markets, a scandal, a change in customer values, a change of strategy.

Judge impact by:

- **Size of change:** new wording only, or a new purpose and scope?
- **Business:** fit with strategy, rebranding and training costs, risk to reputation.
- **Stakeholders:** who gains, who loses, and how much power each has.
- **Time:** short-run disruption against long-run focus.
- **Communication:** a change that is explained and phased does less damage.

## Quick worked reminder

Ferrand Optical (invented) has nine shops and a home-visit service. "To be the leading opticians in the UK" is generic and unrealistic. "To raise sales by 15% by 2028" is an objective. "To look after the eyesight of people in our market towns, in our shops or in their homes, with time and care" names purpose, scope and a standard, fits what Ferrand does and would rule out cutting appointment times. Limitation: "time and care" must be turned into measurable objectives.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Mission statement | Short statement of the purpose of a business, what it does and for whom |
| Vision | The future position a business hopes to reach |
| Aim | A broad intention that follows from the mission |
| Objective | A measurable, time-bound target that serves an aim |
| Values | The beliefs that guide how a business behaves |
| Scope | The products, customers or places a business serves |
| Corporate culture | The shared values and ways of working in a business, which a mission can reinforce |
| Stakeholder | Any person or group with an interest in, or affected by, the business |

## Exam traps

- A figure or a date means it is an objective, not a mission.
- "Recommend and justify" needs a choice, reasons from the case, a rejected alternative and a condition.
- A mission is not proof of good behaviour; evidence of actions matters more.
- Changing a mission is not only a wording change if purpose or scope changes.
- Do not list stakeholders; say which are affected most and why.
- Small businesses may gain little from a formal statement; say so if the case is a sole trader.

## Self-test

1. State two features of the nature of a mission statement.
2. Give the order of the planning chain from mission to tactics.
3. Name the four parts a mission statement can contain.
4. Why is "To double profit by 2030" not a mission statement?
5. Give one purpose of a mission statement for employees and one for investors.
6. State the five-point test for a draft statement.
7. Why might a mission statement matter more to a business with 40 sites than to a sole trader?
8. Explain how a mission statement can become a disadvantage.
9. Give two events that might cause a business to change its mission statement.
10. A bakery chain changes its mission from "local, handmade bread" to "affordable bread for every household". Name one stakeholder who gains and one who may lose.
11. What should a judgement on the impact of a changed mission depend on?
12. Why should a recommended mission statement be followed by objectives?

### Answers

1. Any two: short; qualitative, with no figures; open-ended; sets purpose; public.
2. Mission, aims, objectives, strategy, tactics.
3. Purpose, scope, values, standards of behaviour.
4. It is measurable and time-bound, so it is an objective; it says nothing about purpose or values.
5. Employees: a shared sense of direction, so decisions across teams are consistent. Investors: a signal of priorities and the kind of return to expect.
6. Specific, memorable, restrictive (rules something out), accurate (matches behaviour), durable.
7. Managers cannot supervise every decision at 40 sites, so a shared purpose guides staff; a sole trader makes the decisions directly.
8. If actions contradict the words, staff and customers become cynical and reputation suffers; narrow wording can also stop the business taking good opportunities.
9. Any two: a takeover or new owners; entry to new markets; a scandal; a change in customer values; a change of strategy.
10. Gains: price-sensitive customers, or shareholders if sales rise. May lose: local suppliers of special ingredients, staff with craft skills, or loyal customers who valued handmade bread.
11. The size of the change, its fit with strategy, the costs, which stakeholders are affected and how powerful they are, the time period and how well the change is communicated.
12. Because a mission is not measurable; objectives turn it into targets that can be checked.
