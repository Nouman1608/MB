## Section A

**1.** Distinguish between a positive statement and a normative statement. **[2]**

**2.** Classify each statement as positive or normative. **[4]**

- "The fictional country of Arnsby had a budget deficit of 3% of GDP last year."
- "Arnsby's government ought to balance its budget."
- "A tax on plastic bags in Arnsby will reduce their use by half."
- "Arnsby's tax system is too generous to high earners."

**3.** Define *ceteris paribus*. **[2]**

**4.** State **two** reasons why economic models simplify reality. **[2]**

**5.** Outline what is meant by refutation of a hypothesis. **[2]**

**6.** Identify the economist and the century associated with the idea of the "invisible hand". **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** An economist in Arnsby proposes: "Higher fuel prices, ceteris paribus, reduce car journeys." The data show:

| Month | Fuel price (arns per litre) | Car journeys (millions) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 2.00 | 50 |
| 2 | 2.30 | 46 |
| 3 | 2.53 | 47 |

**(a)** Calculate the percentage change in fuel price and in car journeys between months 1 and 2. **[2]**

**(b)** In month 3 a large factory opened outside the capital, creating thousands of new commuter journeys. Explain whether the month 3 data refute the hypothesis. **[3]**

**8.** Explain why it is difficult for economists to test hypotheses in the way natural scientists can. **[4]**

**9.** Explain the difference between the Keynesian view and the monetarist or new classical view of whether governments should manage aggregate demand. **[4]**

**10.** Explain how behavioural economics challenges a common assumption of economic models. **[3]**

**11.** Explain how one issue, a sugar tax, can be the subject of both positive and normative economics. **[4]**

**12.** "Economic models are of little use because they are based on unrealistic assumptions." Discuss this view. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** A positive statement is about what is, was or will be and can be tested against evidence [1]. A normative statement contains a value judgement about what ought to be and cannot be settled by evidence alone [1].

**2.** Budget deficit of 3%: positive [1]. Ought to balance its budget: normative [1]. Tax will cut use by half: positive, as a testable prediction [1]. Too generous to high earners: normative [1].

**3.** "Other things being equal" [1]: all variables except the one being studied are held constant so its effect can be isolated [1].

**4.** Any two: to isolate the key relationship [1]; to make clear predictions that can be tested [1]; because the real economy has too many variables to include at once.

**5.** When empirical evidence contradicts a hypothesis [1], the hypothesis is rejected or revised [1].

**6.** Adam Smith [1], 18th century (*The Wealth of Nations*, 1776) [1].

**7. (a)** Fuel price: (2.30 - 2.00) / 2.00 × 100 = **15%** [1]. Journeys: (46 - 50) / 50 × 100 = **-8%** [1].

**(b)** Price rose again by (2.53 - 2.30) / 2.30 = 10% while journeys rose slightly, which appears to contradict the hypothesis [1]. But the factory opening means ceteris paribus did not hold: demand for journeys increased for another reason [1]. So the data do not cleanly refute the hypothesis; more evidence, for example from areas without the factory, is needed [1].

**8.** Economists cannot usually run controlled experiments on whole economies [1]. Many variables change at the same time, so ceteris paribus rarely holds in observed data [1]. Human behaviour is shaped by values, institutions and expectations, which can change over time [1]. So results are provisional and economists must rely on models and careful use of data [1].

**9.** Keynes argued that aggregate demand can stay too low for long periods, leaving lasting unemployment [1], so governments should actively manage demand, for example through spending [1]. Monetarist and new classical economists argued the economy tends to return to its potential output [1], so demand management cannot permanently lower unemployment and may simply raise inflation [1].

**10.** Many models assume consumers act rationally to maximise utility [1]. Behavioural economics finds systematic departures, such as biases and rules of thumb [1], so predictions based on rational choice may be wrong and policies such as nudges may change behaviour [1].

**11.** Positive: the tax's effect on prices, consumption and revenue can be measured [1], for example whether sugary drink sales fell [1]. Normative: whether the tax is fair to low-income households, or whether the state should influence diets at all, rests on value judgements [1]. Positive evidence informs the debate but cannot settle the normative question [1].

**12.** For the view: models rely on assumptions such as rational behaviour and perfect information that often do not hold [1]; ceteris paribus rarely holds, so predictions can fail [1]; models may leave out values and institutions that matter [1]. Against the view: simplification is deliberate, isolating relationships that would be lost in detail [1]; models are judged by how well they predict, and many, such as demand and supply, predict the direction of change well [1]; models give a shared framework and diagrams for analysing policy [1]; they can be revised when evidence refutes them [1]. Judgement: models are useful when their assumptions suit the question and their limits are acknowledged, so the claim that they are of "little use" overstates the case [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calling a prediction normative because it might be wrong.
- Forgetting to explain why ceteris paribus failed in a data question.
- Naming economists without explaining their ideas.
- Writing one-sided answers to "discuss" questions about models.
