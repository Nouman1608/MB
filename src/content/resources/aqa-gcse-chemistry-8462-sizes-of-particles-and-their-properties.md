This guide covers **4.2.4.1 Sizes of particles and their properties** of the AQA GCSE Chemistry (8462)
specification, for teaching from September 2016 onwards (version 1.2). It is part of topic 4.2.4, Bulk and
surface properties of matter including nanoparticles, which is content for the separate Chemistry qualification
and is assessed on Paper 1 at Foundation and Higher Tier. No statement in it is marked (HT only), and there is no
required practical. The subsection includes several maths skills: standard form, order of magnitude estimates,
ratios, and surface areas and volumes of cubes.

## Particle sizes

The specification groups particles by their diameter.

| Group | Diameter range | In metres | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nanoparticles | 1 to 100 nm | 1 × 10⁻⁹ m to 1 × 10⁻⁷ m | Smaller than fine particles |
| Fine particles (PM2.5) | 100 to 2500 nm | 1 × 10⁻⁷ m to 2.5 × 10⁻⁶ m | |
| Coarse particles (PM10) | up to the 10⁻⁵ m range | 2.5 × 10⁻⁶ m to 1 × 10⁻⁵ m | Often called dust |

The unit **nm** means **nanometre**: 1 nm = 1 × 10⁻⁹ m, or one billionth of a metre. The specification states that
nanoscience structures are of the order of **a few hundred atoms**.

### Converting between nm and metres

To change nanometres to metres, multiply by 10⁻⁹. To change metres to nanometres, divide by 10⁻⁹ (or multiply
by 10⁹).

| Quantity | In nm | In metres |
|---|---|---|
| Lower limit of fine particles | 100 nm | 1 × 10⁻⁷ m |
| Upper limit of fine particles | 2500 nm | 2.5 × 10⁻⁶ m |
| An atom (about 0.1 nm across) | 0.1 nm | 1 × 10⁻¹⁰ m |
| A typical nanoparticle (30 nm) | 30 nm | 3 × 10⁻⁸ m |

An atom is roughly 0.1 nm across, so a 100 nm nanoparticle is about 1000 atoms across, and a 1 nm particle is
about 10 atoms across. Those figures are consistent with "a few hundred atoms" for the more usual smaller
nanoparticles.

## Surface area to volume ratio

The **surface area to volume ratio** (SA : V) tells you how much surface there is for each unit of volume. It
matters because chemical reactions and many other effects happen **at the surface** of a particle.

For a cube with sides of length *L*:

- surface area = 6 × *L*² (six faces, each *L* × *L*);
- volume = *L*³;
- surface area to volume ratio = 6 *L*² ÷ *L*³ = 6 ÷ *L*.

So when *L* gets smaller, the ratio gets bigger. If the side decreases by a factor of 10, the ratio increases by
a factor of 10.

### Worked example 1: a cube of side 2 cm and one of side 0.2 cm

```
Cube 1:  L = 2 cm
         surface area = 6 x 2 x 2 = 24 cm2
         volume       = 2 x 2 x 2 = 8 cm3
         SA : V = 24 / 8 = 3 per cm

Cube 2:  L = 0.2 cm
         surface area = 6 x 0.2 x 0.2 = 0.24 cm2
         volume       = 0.2 x 0.2 x 0.2 = 0.008 cm3
         SA : V = 0.24 / 0.008 = 30 per cm

30 / 3 = 10.  The side fell by a factor of 10, so the ratio rose by a factor of 10.
```

### Worked example 2: nanoscale cubes

*Calculate the surface area to volume ratio of cubes of side 100 nm and 10 nm. By what factor does it change?*

```
L = 100 nm:  SA = 6 x 100 x 100 = 60 000 nm2;  V = 100^3 = 1 000 000 nm3;  ratio = 0.06 per nm
L = 10 nm:   SA = 6 x 10 x 10 = 600 nm2;       V = 10^3 = 1000 nm3;        ratio = 0.6 per nm
0.6 / 0.06 = 10: the ratio is 10 times larger for the smaller cube.
```

Notice that a 10 nm particle has a ratio 10 times that of a 100 nm particle, which is itself far larger than a
cube of side a few millimetres. That is why nanoparticles are so different from bulk material.

### Why this matters

Imagine breaking a block of a substance into many tiny pieces. The total volume (and mass) stays the same, but the
total surface area becomes much larger. More of the atoms are at a surface where they can interact with other
substances.

## Why nanoparticles may have different properties

Nanoparticles may have properties different from the same material in bulk because of their **high surface area
to volume ratio**.

- A large fraction of the atoms are at the surface, where they can react or take part in processes.
- A **smaller quantity** of the material may be needed to be effective, because a given mass has a much larger
  surface area than the same mass of bulk material.

For example, a nanoparticle catalyst can work with a smaller mass than a catalyst in larger pieces, because more
of its surface is available to the reactants.

You do not need to learn specific examples or specific properties beyond those in the specification. Use the
information in the question and the idea of the high surface area to volume ratio.

## Comparing "nano" with atoms and molecules

| Object | Typical size | Nanoscale? |
|---|---|---|
| Atom | About 0.1 nm | Smaller than the nanoscale |
| Small molecule (such as water) | About 0.3 nm | Smaller than the nanoscale |
| Large molecule such as a polymer | Several nm or more | Can be within the nanoscale |
| Nanoparticle | 1 to 100 nm | Yes |
| Fine particle (PM2.5) | 100 to 2500 nm | No |

### Worked example 3: order of magnitude

*A fine particle has a diameter of 2500 nm and a nanoparticle has a diameter of 25 nm. About how many times larger
is the fine particle? Give your answer as an order of magnitude.*

```
2500 / 25 = 100
Order of magnitude: 10^2, so about a hundred times larger.
```

### Worked example 4: standard form

*Write 30 nm in metres in standard form.*

```
30 nm = 30 x 10^-9 m = 3 x 10^-8 m
```

### Worked example 5: comparing with an atom

*An atom has a diameter of about 1 × 10⁻¹⁰ m. A nanoparticle has a diameter of 1 × 10⁻⁸ m. How many atoms
would fit across the nanoparticle?*

```
1 x 10^-8 / 1 x 10^-10 = 1 x 10^2 = 100 atoms
```

### Worked example 6: classifying particles

*Particle X has a diameter of 8 × 10⁻⁸ m. Particle Y has a diameter of 1.5 × 10⁻⁶ m. Classify each.*

```
X: 8 x 10^-8 m = 80 nm, between 1 and 100 nm   -> nanoparticle
Y: 1.5 x 10^-6 m = 1500 nm, between 100 and 2500 nm -> fine particle (PM2.5)
```

## Common mistakes

- Saying "nanoparticles are 1 to 100 m". The unit is nanometres.
- Writing that the surface area to volume ratio **decreases** as the particle size decreases. It increases.
- Using the wrong factor: a factor of 10 smaller side gives a factor of 10 larger ratio, not 100 or 1000.
- Forgetting that surface area is in length squared and volume in length cubed.
- Making a standard form error when converting nm to metres (10⁻⁹, not 10⁻⁶ or 10⁹).
- Claiming that nanoparticles are made of different materials. They are the same material in a different size,
  with different properties.

## Exam technique

- **State a size range:** 1 to 100 nm; fine particles 100 to 2500 nm.
- **Calculate a ratio:** write the surface area, the volume, then divide. Show each step.
- **Explain a different property:** high surface area to volume ratio, so more of the atoms are exposed, so
  smaller amounts are needed.
- **Convert units carefully:** write 1 nm = 1 × 10⁻⁹ m beside the working.
