Content tested: "The problem of maintaining détente" and "The end of the Cold War in the USSR, Central and Eastern Europe".

## Section A: knowledge (3-mark questions)

**1.** Give three features of the SALT I agreement and the ABM Treaty of 1972. **[3]**

**2.** Give three features of Solidarity in Poland in 1980-81. **[3]**

**3.** Give three features of Gorbachev's reforms of glasnost and perestroika. **[3]**

**4.** Give three events of 1989 that ended Soviet control in Eastern Europe. **[3]**

## Section B: explanation and judgement

**5.** Explain one way in which Nixon's policy towards China helped to bring about détente. **[6]**

**6.** Explain one way in which the Helsinki Accords of 1975 affected the Soviet bloc. **[6]**

**7.** Explain one way in which the Strategic Defence Initiative affected relations between the USA and the USSR. **[6]**

**8.** Explain two reasons why détente broke down between 1979 and 1983. **[9]**

**9.** Explain two reasons why the Soviet Union decided to withdraw from Afghanistan. **[9]**

**10.** "Gorbachev was the main reason why communist rule in Eastern Europe ended in 1989." How far do you agree? **[12]**

**11.** "Reagan's policies were the main reason why the Cold War ended." How far do you agree? **[12]**

**12.** "Détente in the 1970s failed to reduce the danger of nuclear war." How far do you agree? **[12]**

## Answers

**How these guides work.** 3-mark questions: [1] per relevant point. 6-mark: way identified [1], precise knowledge [2], explanation of how it affected the development [2], further developed detail or consequence [1]. 9-mark: each of two ways or reasons [4] (identified [1], precise knowledge [1], explained link [2]) plus [1] for accurate, well-sequenced detail. 12-mark: the given factor [3], at least one other factor [4], comparison in a line of reasoning [3], supported judgement [2].

**1.** Any three, [1] each: both were signed at the Moscow summit in May 1972 by Nixon and Brezhnev; the ABM Treaty limited each side's anti-ballistic missile systems; SALT I froze the number of strategic missile launchers for five years; it did not limit the number of warheads on each missile; they were the first major arms limitation agreements; they showed that both sides accepted a rough equality.

**2.** Any three, [1] each: it began with a strike at the Gdansk shipyard in August 1980; it was led by Lech Walesa; it was the first independent trade union in the eastern bloc; it had about ten million members; it was supported by the Catholic Church and Pope John Paul II; it demanded political freedoms as well as better pay; martial law was imposed in December 1981 and the union was banned.

**3.** Any three, [1] each: glasnost meant openness and allowed criticism and discussion; censorship was relaxed; perestroika meant restructuring the economy; it allowed some elements of the market and private enterprise; the aim was to modernise the Soviet economy; the changes revealed the failures of the system and encouraged demands for more change.

**4.** Any three, [1] each: Solidarity won the semi-free elections in Poland in June 1989; Hungary opened its border with Austria; East German protests forced Honecker to resign in October 1989; the Berlin Wall was opened on 9 November; the Velvet Revolution ended communist rule in Czechoslovakia; Gorbachev refused to use force.

**5.** Way identified [1]: it put pressure on the USSR to seek agreements. Precise knowledge [2]: after the Sino-Soviet split, Nixon visited China in February 1972, only months before he visited Moscow. Explanation [2]: the Soviet leaders feared a USA-China alignment against them, so they were more willing to sign arms limitation treaties and trade agreements to prevent it. Further development [1]: the USA could therefore negotiate from a stronger position, and Nixon and Brezhnev signed SALT I and the ABM Treaty at the Moscow summit.

**6.** Way identified [1]: it encouraged dissidents who criticised Soviet rule. Precise knowledge [2]: in the Helsinki Accords (1975) the USSR and its allies promised to respect human rights, in return for recognition of the post-war frontiers. Explanation [2]: groups in the Soviet bloc, such as Charter 77 in Czechoslovakia, used the promises to challenge their governments, which found it hard to refuse. Further development [1]: Western governments could also criticise human rights abuses.

**7.** Way identified [1]: it raised tension and mistrust. Precise knowledge [2]: Reagan announced SDI in 1983, a space-based defence against missiles that critics called "Star Wars". Explanation [2]: the USSR feared that if the USA could shoot down missiles, it could attack without fear of retaliation, which would upset the balance of deterrence, and the USSR could not afford a matching programme. Further development [1]: at Reykjavik in 1986 Reagan refused to give up SDI and the two sides failed to agree on large cuts.

**8.** Reason one [4]: the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan [1]; Soviet troops entered in December 1979 [1]; so Carter withdrew SALT II from the Senate, banned grain exports and led a boycott of the 1980 Moscow Olympics, and trust collapsed [2]. Reason two [4]: the arms build-up and the election of Reagan [1]; the USSR deployed SS-20 missiles in Europe and Reagan, elected in 1980, increased defence spending and sent Pershing and cruise missiles to Europe [1]; so each side felt threatened, the arms race restarted and the language became hostile ("evil empire") [2]. Accurate, well-sequenced detail [1], for example SDI in 1983.

**9.** Reason one [4]: the cost of the war [1]; the Soviet army could not defeat the Mujahideen, who were supported by the USA, and about fifteen thousand soldiers died [1]; so the war was unpopular, drained the economy and damaged morale [2]. Reason two [4]: Gorbachev's new policies [1]; he needed to reduce military spending to reform the economy and improve relations with the West [1]; so he decided that the war could not be won and withdrew troops in 1988-89 [2]. Accurate, well-sequenced detail [1], for example Soviet isolation after the Olympic boycotts.

**10.** Given factor [3]: Gorbachev's glasnost and perestroika; his refusal to use force and the end of the Brezhnev Doctrine; his talks with Reagan and cuts in military spending. Other factors [4]: Solidarity in Poland and its victory in 1989; the pressure of mass protests in East Germany and Czechoslovakia; the economic weakness of the Soviet bloc; Reagan's arms build-up; the cost of Afghanistan; nationalism. Comparison [3]: Gorbachev created the conditions by refusing to intervene, but the revolutions were driven by people in each country and by economic failure; his reforms also encouraged demands which he could not control. Judgement [2]: for example, Gorbachev was the most important single reason because without his decision not to use force the regimes might have been defended, but he acted because of the economic weakness of the USSR.

**11.** Given factor [3]: Reagan's defence spending and SDI forced the USSR to compete; he spoke of an "evil empire"; he later negotiated with Gorbachev, leading to the INF Treaty; he deployed missiles in Europe. Other factors [4]: Gorbachev's reforms and new thinking; the economic weakness of the USSR; the cost of Afghanistan; Solidarity and popular movements; the influence of Western Europe and the Helsinki Accords. Comparison [3]: Reagan increased the pressure and then negotiated, but the USSR was already in economic difficulties, and Gorbachev's decisions ended the Cold War. Judgement [2]: for example, Reagan's policies were a contributing factor, but the decisive changes came from within the USSR and eastern Europe.

**12.** Given factor [3]: SALT I did not limit warheads; both sides continued to build weapons; the arms race went on with SS-20s; détente collapsed in 1979; fears increased in the early 1980s. Other factors [4]: the ABM Treaty and SALT I were the first arms control agreements; summits and the hotline improved communication; the Helsinki Accords reduced tension in Europe; Ostpolitik; there was no direct clash between the superpowers in the 1970s. Comparison [3]: détente reduced the risk of confrontation in the 1970s and created a framework of agreements, but it did not stop the arms race and it collapsed, so the danger returned. Judgement [2]: for example, détente reduced the immediate danger by establishing talks and rules, but it failed to reduce the weapons, so the statement is only partly true.
