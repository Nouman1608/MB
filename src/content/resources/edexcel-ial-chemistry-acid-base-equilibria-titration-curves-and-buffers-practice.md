Assume 298 K and Kw = 1.00 × 10⁻¹⁴ mol² dm⁻⁶. Use the data given in each question.

## Questions

**1.** Define a buffer solution, and name the two types of species present in an acidic buffer. **[2]**

**2.** 20.0 cm³ of 0.150 mol dm⁻³ nitric acid is titrated with 0.120 mol dm⁻³ potassium hydroxide. Calculate the volume of alkali at the equivalence point, the starting pH, and state the pH at the equivalence point. **[3]**

**3.** Use these indicator ranges: methyl orange 3.1–4.4; bromothymol blue 6.0–7.6; phenolphthalein 8.2–10.0; alizarin yellow 10.1–12.0.

**(a)** A titration curve has a steep section from pH 7.0 to pH 11.0. Choose the indicator and explain why the others are unsuitable. **[2]**
**(b)** Another curve has a steep section from pH 2.9 to pH 6.6. Choose the indicator and give the type of titration. **[2]**
**(c)** A student titrates propanoic acid with aqueous ammonia using phenolphthalein. Her three titres differ by more than 2 cm³. Explain this result and suggest a better way to find the equivalence volume. **[2]**

**4.** 50.0 cm³ of 0.240 mol dm⁻³ weak acid HM (Ka = 3.2 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³) is mixed with 30.0 cm³ of 0.160 mol dm⁻³ NaOH. Calculate the pH of the mixture. **[5]**

**5.** A mixture of aqueous ammonia and ammonium chloride is a buffer. Explain, with equations, how it resists a change in pH when small amounts of (i) acid and (ii) alkali are added. **[4]**

**6.** 25.0 cm³ of sodium carbonate solution is titrated with 0.0950 mol dm⁻³ HCl. The curve has two steep sections; the first is centred on 13.20 cm³.

**(a)** Write ionic equations for the reactions in each stage. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the concentration of the sodium carbonate, and state the volume at the second steep section. **[2]**
**(c)** Name a suitable indicator for each end point. **[2]**

**7.** A weak acid HR has Ka = 4.5 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³. Its sodium salt NaR has Mr = 110.0.

**(a)** Calculate the mass of NaR that must be dissolved in 200 cm³ of 0.125 mol dm⁻³ HR to give a buffer of pH 4.60. State one assumption. **[5]**
**(b)** Instead, equal volumes of 0.150 mol dm⁻³ HR and a NaR solution are mixed. Calculate the concentration of NaR solution needed for pH 4.60. **[2]**

**8.** In Core Practical 11, a student finds Ka for a weak monoprotic acid.

**(a)** Describe how the student could prepare a half-neutralised solution of the acid, starting from the acid, NaOH solution of unknown concentration and phenolphthalein. **[3]**
**(b)** The half-neutralised solution has pH 4.12. Calculate Ka. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why the student does not need to know the concentration of either solution, and state why the pH meter is first calibrated. **[2]**

**9.** 20.0 cm³ of 0.0750 mol dm⁻³ weak acid HG (pKa = 4.31) is titrated with 0.0600 mol dm⁻³ NaOH.

**(a)** Calculate the pH before any alkali is added, stating the assumption made. **[3]**
**(b)** State the pH when 12.50 cm³ of NaOH has been added. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the pH after 30.0 cm³ of NaOH has been added. **[3]**
**(d)** Suggest why the solution in the flask is alkaline at the equivalence point. **[1]**

**10.** Blood and many foods contain buffers.

**(a)** Write the equilibrium for the main buffer in blood. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain how this buffer limits the rise in pH if a small amount of base enters the blood. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why some foods contain added buffers. **[2]**

**11.** 250 cm³ of a buffer contains 0.120 mol dm⁻³ weak acid HA (Ka = 2.6 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³) and 0.0900 mol dm⁻³ NaA.

**(a)** Calculate the pH of the buffer. **[2]**
**(b)** The buffer receives 3.00 cm³ of 2.00 mol dm⁻³ HCl. Find the new pH. **[3]**
**(c)** Find the pH if that HCl went into 250 cm³ of pure water instead. **[2]**
**(d)** The original buffer is diluted to 500 cm³ with water. State and explain the effect on pH. **[2]**

**12.** A diprotic weak acid H₂X is titrated with NaOH. Steep sections are centred on 11.0 cm³ and 22.0 cm³. The pH is 2.90 at 5.50 cm³ and 6.10 at 16.50 cm³. State pKa₁ and pKa₂ and explain how you identified each. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** A solution whose pH changes very little when a small amount of acid or alkali is added [1]. It contains a weak acid and its conjugate base (or a salt of the weak acid) [1].
*Examiner insight:* "keeps the pH constant" overstates what a buffer does; say the pH changes only slightly, and only for small additions.

**2.** Moles HNO₃ = 0.0200 × 0.150 = 3.00 × 10⁻³ mol; volume = 3.00 × 10⁻³/0.120 = **25.0 cm³** [1]. Starting pH = −log 0.150 = **0.82** [1]. Equivalence pH = **7** (strong acid with strong base) [1].
*Examiner insight:* quote pH to two decimal places unless told otherwise.

**3. (a)** **Phenolphthalein**: its range lies wholly within pH 7.0–11.0 [1]. Bromothymol blue starts below 7.0, alizarin yellow ends above 11.0 and methyl orange changes far too early [1].
**(b)** **Methyl orange** [1]; strong acid with weak base [1].
**(c)** With no steep section, the colour changes gradually over several cm³ [1]; use a pH meter, plot the curve and read the equivalence volume from it [1].
*Examiner insight:* refer to the whole range lying inside the steep section, not to the equivalence pH alone.

**4.** Moles HM = 0.0500 × 0.240 = 0.0120 mol [1]; moles OH⁻ = 0.0300 × 0.160 = 4.80 × 10⁻³ mol [1]. HM left = 7.20 × 10⁻³ mol, M⁻ = 4.80 × 10⁻³ mol [1]. [H⁺] = 3.2 × 10⁻⁵ × 7.20/4.80 = 4.8 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³ [1]; pH = **4.32** [1].
*Examiner insight:* show the subtraction; a ratio built from starting moles is wrong.

**5.** (i) Added H⁺ reacts with ammonia [1]: NH₃ + H⁺ → NH₄⁺ [1]. (ii) Added OH⁻ reacts with ammonium ions [1]: NH₄⁺ + OH⁻ → NH₃ + H₂O [1].
*Examiner insight:* an explanation with no equations is unlikely to earn full credit; write one equation for each addition.

**6. (a)** CO₃²⁻ + H⁺ → HCO₃⁻ [1]; HCO₃⁻ + H⁺ → H₂O + CO₂ [1].
**(b)** Moles HCl = 0.01320 × 0.0950 = 1.254 × 10⁻³ mol = moles CO₃²⁻; concentration = 1.254 × 10⁻³/0.0250 = **0.0502 mol dm⁻³** [1]. Second steep section at **26.40 cm³** [1].
**(c)** **Phenolphthalein** for the first [1]; **methyl orange** for the second [1].
*Examiner insight:* use the first end point (1 : 1) for the carbonate moles; using the second without halving doubles the answer.

**7. (a)** [H⁺] = 10^-4.60 = 2.51 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³ [1]. [R⁻] = Ka × [HR]/[H⁺] [1] = 4.5 × 10⁻⁵ × 0.125/2.51 × 10⁻⁵ = 0.224 mol dm⁻³ [1]. Moles = 0.224 × 0.200 = 0.0448 mol [1]; mass = 0.0448 × 110.0 = **4.93 g**, assuming the volume does not change on dissolving [1].
**(b)** Ratio [R⁻]/[HR] = Ka/[H⁺] = 1.79, unchanged by mixing equal volumes [1]; [NaR] = 1.79 × 0.150 = **0.269 mol dm⁻³** [1].
*Examiner insight:* in (b), halving only one concentration gives a wrong ratio.

**8. (a)** Titrate a measured portion (e.g. 25.0 cm³) of the acid with NaOH using phenolphthalein to the first permanent pink [1]. Take an identical portion of acid [1] and add exactly half the titre of NaOH (or mix the neutralised solution with an equal portion of acid) [1].
**(b)** pKa = pH = 4.12 [1]; Ka = 10^-4.12 = **7.6 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³** [1].
**(c)** At half-neutralisation [HA] = [A⁻], so they cancel in the Ka expression whatever their values [1]. Calibration with buffers of known pH makes the readings accurate [1].
*Examiner insight:* name the apparatus (pipette, burette); "add some NaOH" is too vague.

**9. (a)** Ka = 10^-4.31 = 4.90 × 10⁻⁵ [1]; [H⁺] = √(4.90 × 10⁻⁵ × 0.0750) = 1.92 × 10⁻³, pH = **2.72** [1]; assume [HG] at equilibrium equals 0.0750 because dissociation is slight [1].
**(b)** 12.50 cm³ is half of the 25.0 cm³ equivalence volume, so pH = pKa = **4.31** [1].
**(c)** Excess OH⁻ = (30.0 − 25.0) × 10⁻³ × 0.0600 = 3.00 × 10⁻⁴ mol [1]; [OH⁻] = 3.00 × 10⁻⁴/0.0500 = 6.0 × 10⁻³ mol dm⁻³ [1]; pH = 14.00 − 2.22 = **11.78** [1].
**(d)** The salt's anion G⁻ is a weak base that accepts protons from water, forming OH⁻ [1].
*Examiner insight:* in (c), divide by the total volume (50.0 cm³), not the volume of alkali.

**10. (a)** H₂CO₃ ⇌ H⁺ + HCO₃⁻ [1].
**(b)** H₂CO₃ + OH⁻ → HCO₃⁻ + H₂O [1]; H₂CO₃ and HCO₃⁻ far exceed the base added, so their ratio and the pH (close to 7.4) barely change [1].
**(c)** Bacterial or fungal activity can change the pH of food [1]; a buffer resists the change, slowing deterioration [1].
*Examiner insight:* name the actual species, HCO₃⁻ and H₂CO₃; "the buffer soaks up the base" is too vague.

**11. (a)** [H⁺] = 2.6 × 10⁻⁵ × 0.120/0.0900 = 3.47 × 10⁻⁵ [1]; pH = **4.46** [1].
**(b)** Moles: HA 0.0300, A⁻ 0.0225; H⁺ added 6.00 × 10⁻³ [1]. HA 0.0360, A⁻ 0.0165 [1]. [H⁺] = 2.6 × 10⁻⁵ × 0.0360/0.0165 = 5.67 × 10⁻⁵; pH = **4.25** [1].
**(c)** [H⁺] = 6.00 × 10⁻³/0.253 = 0.0237 mol dm⁻³ [1]; pH = **1.62** [1].
**(d)** pH **stays almost the same** [1]: doubling the volume halves both concentrations, which leaves [HA]/[A⁻] at 0.120/0.0900 [1].
*Examiner insight:* in (b), work in moles so the change in volume cancels.

**12.** pKa₁ = **2.90**: 5.50 cm³ is half the first equivalence volume, so [H₂X] = [HX⁻] [1]. pKa₂ = **6.10** [1]: 16.50 cm³ is midway between the equivalence points, so [HX⁻] = [X²⁻] [1].
*Examiner insight:* explain each value with the pair of species at equal concentration.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Assuming every equivalence point is at pH 7.
- Picking an indicator that begins or ends its colour change outside the steep section.
- Using starting moles, not moles left after reaction, in a buffer ratio.
- Inverting the ratio in [H⁺] = Ka × [HA]/[A⁻].
- Taking pKa at the equivalence volume instead of half of it.
- Buffer explanations with no equation.
- Forgetting the two-step stoichiometry for diprotic species.
