## How Paper 3 is set out

Section 2.2 of the specification prints the structure. Paper 3 is a written exam of 2 hours, 96 marks in total, worth 33.3% of the A-level. It assesses compulsory content 8 (Issues and debates in Psychology) and "one from option 1, 9–11, one from option 2, 12–14, one from option 3, 15–17".

- **Section A:** multiple choice, short answer and extended writing, 24 marks. The specification does not name a topic for Section A, but Sections B to D are the three options, so Section A is where the compulsory issues and debates content sits; your teacher will confirm.
- **Section B:** one topic from option 1 (Relationships, Gender, Cognition and development), 24 marks.
- **Section C:** one topic from option 2 (Schizophrenia, Eating behaviour, Stress), 24 marks.
- **Section D:** one topic from option 3 (Aggression, Forensic Psychology, Addiction), 24 marks.

The approximate Paper 3 weightings in section 5.2 are AO1 9-12%, AO2 5-8% and AO3 15-17% of the whole A-level. So evaluation carries the most weight on this paper. The specification does not print command words or mark schemes; your teacher will confirm how each question type is marked.

One instruction matters more than any other here: you are "expected to illustrate" issues and debates answers "with knowledge and understanding of topics studied elsewhere in the specification". Every point below should end in an example from your other topics.

## Gender and culture in psychology

**Universality** is the claim that a finding applies to all people, whatever their gender or culture. **Bias** is a systematic distortion that makes a finding less true for some groups.

**Gender bias**

- **Androcentrism:** treating male thinking and behaviour as the normal standard, so female behaviour is judged as different or lacking.
- **Alpha bias:** exaggerating differences between men and women, often presenting them as fixed.
- **Beta bias:** ignoring or minimising real differences, often by studying men and assuming the results apply to women.

*Alpha bias example.* Freud argued that girls develop a weaker superego than boys because they do not experience castration anxiety. That treats a supposed difference as natural and makes women seem morally inferior.

*Beta bias example.* Early research on the fight-or-flight response used mostly male animals and men. Taylor et al. (2000) proposed "tend and befriend" as a pattern more typical of females under stress, linked to the hormone oxytocin.

*Androcentrism example.* Kohlberg built his theory of moral development from interviews with boys. Gilligan (1982) argued that it rated a care-based way of reasoning, which she linked to women, as less developed. But a meta-analysis by Jaffee and Hyde (2000) found only small sex differences in care and justice orientations. That is a warning that critics can also exaggerate differences.

**Cultural bias**

- **Ethnocentrism:** judging other cultures by the standards of your own and treating your own as normal.
- **Cultural relativism:** the view that behaviour can only be understood in the cultural context where it happens.
- **Etic and emic** (Berry, 1969): an etic approach studies a culture from outside, looking for universals; an emic approach studies it from inside. An **imposed etic** happens when a tool built in one culture is used as if it were universal.

*Example.* Ainsworth's Strange Situation was developed in the USA. Using it elsewhere can be an imposed etic, because "secure" behaviour reflects American norms of independence. Bond and Smith (1996) analysed 133 Asch-type conformity studies from 17 countries and found higher conformity in collectivist countries. Henrich, Heine and Norenzayan (2010) argued that most research samples come from "WEIRD" (Western, educated, industrialised, rich and democratic) societies.

**Evaluation.** Awareness of bias has improved research: more mixed samples, cross-cultural replication and reflexivity (researchers reflecting on their own values). But removing bias completely is hard, because the tools themselves come from a culture. Also, cultural relativism taken too far can stop you from finding genuine universals, such as basic facial expressions of emotion.

**Worked paragraph (application to a fictional study).** *A fictional study of revision anxiety at Larkfield College used 300 students, 255 of them women (85%), and concluded that "students" cope by seeking support.* This risks beta bias: a mainly female sample is generalised to all students, ignoring possible sex differences (knowledge -> application). If men are less likely to seek support, the conclusion would misdescribe them and could shape support services that suit only women (analysis). Taylor et al. (2000) made a similar argument about stress research built on male samples; however, the researchers could test the claim by recruiting a balanced sample and comparing the sexes (evaluation).

## Free will and determinism

**Free will** is the idea that people can choose their behaviour, within limits. **Determinism** is the idea that behaviour is caused by forces we do not control.

- **Hard determinism:** all behaviour has causes; free will is an illusion.
- **Soft determinism:** behaviour has causes, but people still have some conscious choice within those limits. Cognitive and social learning explanations often take this position.
- **Biological determinism:** behaviour is caused by genes, hormones, brain structure or neurochemistry (for example, genetic and neural explanations of OCD).
- **Environmental determinism:** behaviour is caused by learning from the environment, as in Skinner's operant conditioning.
- **Psychic determinism:** behaviour is caused by unconscious forces and early experience, as in Freud's theory.

**The scientific emphasis on causal explanations.** Science looks for cause and effect. A lab experiment manipulates an independent variable to see if it causes a change in a dependent variable. This assumes behaviour is determined, so it can be predicted and controlled.

**Evaluation.**

- Supporting determinism: Libet et al. (1983) found brain activity (the readiness potential) began a fraction of a second before people reported deciding to move. Soon et al. (2008) used fMRI to predict simple choices several seconds before awareness.
- Against hard determinism: the legal system holds people responsible for their actions, which assumes some free will. Hard determinism can also be unfalsifiable, because any behaviour can be called "caused" after the event.
- Soft determinism offers a middle position, but critics say it does not explain how choice and cause fit together.

**Worked paragraph.** Biological determinism explains OCD through genes and abnormal brain functioning (knowledge). For a person who checks locks many times a night, this would mean the behaviour is caused, not chosen (application). A strength is that it reduces blame and supports drug treatment; a limitation is that drug treatment does not work for everyone, and cognitive therapy, which assumes people can change their thinking, also helps some people with OCD (analysis and evaluation). Soft determinism may fit the evidence better: biology sets a vulnerability, but some control remains.

## The nature-nurture debate

**Nature** means heredity: the influence of genes inherited from parents. **Nurture** means environment: everything from prenatal conditions to culture. The specification asks about the relative importance of each.

- **Heritability coefficient:** a number from 0 to 1 showing how much of the variation in a trait across a population is associated with genetic variation. It says nothing about one individual.
- **Concordance rate:** how often both twins in a pair share a trait. Higher concordance in identical (MZ) than non-identical (DZ) twins suggests a genetic influence.
- **Interactionist approach:** behaviour comes from nature and nurture working together, so asking "which one?" is often the wrong question.

**Examples.** Bowlby's monotropic theory sees attachment as innate (nature); learning theory explains it through conditioning (nurture). Bouchard et al. (1990) studied identical twins reared apart and estimated that around 70% of the variation in IQ in their sample was associated with genetic variation. The **diathesis-stress model** is interactionist: a vulnerability (diathesis) leads to a disorder only if a stressor triggers it. **Phenylketonuria (PKU)** is a genetic condition whose harmful effects can be prevented by a special diet, so the outcome depends on genes and environment together. **Epigenetics** studies how experience can change whether genes are switched on or off, without changing the DNA sequence.

**Evaluation.** Twin studies help separate the two influences, but MZ twins may also be treated more alike than DZ twins, which inflates genetic estimates. Interactionism fits the evidence well but is harder to test, because the influences cannot be cleanly separated. Extreme positions also raise concerns: a strong nature view can be used to argue that groups cannot change.

## Holism and reductionism

**Reductionism** explains behaviour by breaking it into smaller parts. **Holism** says behaviour must be studied as a whole, because the whole is different from the sum of its parts (an idea from Gestalt psychology, shared by humanistic psychology).

**Levels of explanation.** The same behaviour can be explained at different levels, for example:

1. **Socio-cultural:** social norms and group influence.
2. **Psychological:** thoughts, emotions and learning.
3. **Physical or biological:** brain areas, neurotransmitters and genes.

- **Biological reductionism:** explaining behaviour at the lowest physical level, such as neurotransmitters or genes.
- **Environmental (stimulus-response) reductionism:** explaining behaviour as learned links between a stimulus and a response, as in behaviourism.

**Evaluation.** Reductionism allows clear, testable hypotheses and has led to treatments such as drugs. But it may miss meaning and context: Asch's findings on conformity cannot be explained by studying one person's neurons. Holism captures real-life complexity, but it is hard to test and can be vague. A good answer often argues that different levels are useful for different questions.

**Worked paragraph.** Behaviourism explains a phobia as a conditioned link between a stimulus and fear (knowledge). For someone afraid of lifts, this reduces the fear to one learned association (application). It allows a precise treatment, systematic desensitisation, which targets that link (analysis). However, it ignores the person's thoughts about being trapped, which a cognitive or holistic account would include, and some people develop phobias with no remembered conditioning event (evaluation).

## Idiographic and nomothetic approaches

The terms come from the philosopher Wilhelm Windelband. Allport (1937) made them widely known in personality psychology.

- **Idiographic approach:** studies the individual in depth to understand what makes them unique. It uses qualitative methods such as case studies and unstructured interviews.
- **Nomothetic approach:** studies groups to find general laws that apply to many people. It uses quantitative methods, experiments and statistics.

**Examples.** Freud's case studies and humanistic psychology are idiographic. The case of Phineas Gage, a railway construction foreman whose personality was reported to change after an iron rod passed through his frontal lobe in 1848, is an idiographic source that later fed general theories of brain function. Milgram's obedience research and behaviourist laws of learning are nomothetic.

**Evaluation.** The idiographic approach gives rich detail and can challenge a general law with one clear case, but it is hard to generalise and relies on subjective interpretation. The nomothetic approach is scientific, replicable and useful for prediction, but it can lose sight of the individual. In practice the two work together: case studies suggest hypotheses that nomothetic research then tests.

## Social sensitivity in psychological research

Sieber and Stanley (1988) described socially sensitive research as studies with potential social consequences for the participants or for the group of people the research represents. They identified issues at four points:

1. **The research question:** asking whether a group differs on some trait can itself cause harm.
2. **Conduct of the research:** protecting participants, for example through confidentiality.
3. **The institutional context:** who funds the research and how the findings will be used.
4. **Interpretation and application:** how findings may shape policy or public opinion.

**Evaluation.** Socially sensitive research can benefit the groups studied, for example by showing the value of early support. Avoiding it entirely could leave important questions unanswered. But researchers often cannot control how others use their findings. Bowlby's theory of maternal deprivation, for example, was used in public debate to argue that mothers of young children should not go out to work.

## Common errors

- Mixing up alpha bias (exaggerates differences) and beta bias (minimises them).
- Treating soft determinism as "a bit of free will" without saying that behaviour is still caused.
- Saying a heritability of 0.5 means half of one person's trait comes from genes.
- Writing a description of reductionism with no levels of explanation and no example from another topic.
- Treating idiographic and nomothetic as rivals with no link between them.
- Writing about general ethics (consent, deception) when the question asks about social sensitivity and wider consequences.
