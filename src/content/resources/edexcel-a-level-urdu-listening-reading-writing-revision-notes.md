## Format at a glance

- **2 hours 15 minutes, 60 marks.**
- **Section A — Listening Comprehension** (recommended 45 minutes) — no translation or extended-writing component.
- **Section B — Listening, Reading and Writing** (recommended 1 hour 30 minutes) — all three skills combined in one section.
- No translation component at all — unlike Papers 1 and 2, which each pair translation with one other skill.

## What genuinely distinguishes Paper 3

Papers 1 and 2 each test **translation** (into English; into Urdu) alongside one other skill. Paper 3 has **no translation component** — it combines listening with reading and writing instead, integrating all three within Section B rather than testing them separately.

## Section A technique

Entirely Urdu-in, Urdu-out — no English-translation step. Prioritise **genuine listening practice** (news broadcasts, interviews, spoken narrative) over reading-based revision alone, since listening and reading comprehension speeds develop through different practice. Technique: **read the question before the audio plays** wherever the format allows, so you know what specific information to listen for.

## Section B technique

Carries the **larger share of time** (1hr 30 of 2hr 15 total) and combines three skills — allocate practice time proportionately; don't treat it as an afterthought after over-practising Section A. Practise **taking accurate notes** from a listening/reading source, then **using those notes to construct a written response** — treat note-taking and writing as one connected process, not two disconnected stages.

## Why translation strength doesn't transfer automatically

Translation proficiency (Papers 1/2) and real-time listening comprehension (Paper 3) draw on **different processing speeds and skills**. A candidate strong at translation is not automatically strong at Paper 3's listening demands — dedicated listening practice is needed regardless of translation strength.

## Exam-day strategy

Practise **full timed sittings of Paper 3 specifically**, not isolated drills — the real difficulty is managing 2hr 15min across two sections with different skill demands, not any single skill alone. Section A has **no reading/writing fallback** if a passage is missed — build the habit of staying with the current question rather than dwelling on a missed detail, since the listening audio continues regardless.


## Worked example: reading the question before the audio plays

Section A presents a listening passage about a community event, followed by a question asking "what time does the event start, and what should attendees bring?" A candidate who reads this question before the audio plays knows in advance to listen specifically for a time expression and a list of items, and can mentally flag those two pieces of information as soon as they occur in the audio, rather than trying to process and retain the entire passage before knowing what will actually be asked. A candidate who has not read the question first must either try to remember everything (increasing the risk of missing the specific detail needed) or hope the passage replays, which is not always possible depending on the exam format. This is why the technique of reading ahead, wherever the format permits it, is worth building into practice from the start rather than treating it as an optional shortcut.

## Building a realistic Section B practice routine

Because Section B combines listening or reading with writing, a practice routine that only ever writes in response to a text the candidate has read calmly and repeatedly (rather than a single listening pass, or a reading passage under realistic time pressure) does not build the actual skill Section B tests. A more realistic practice routine plays or presents the source material once (or with the number of repeats the real exam allows), requires notes to be taken during or immediately after that single exposure, and then requires a written response built from those notes alone, without returning to re-read or re-listen to the original source freely. Practising under this constraint, even occasionally, surfaces gaps in note-taking technique that untimed, repeat-access practice will not reveal.

## Exam traps

- Assuming translation strength (from Papers 1/2 practice) transfers directly to Paper 3's listening demands.
- Over-preparing Section A's shorter component while treating Section B as an afterthought, against the paper's actual time/mark weighting.
- Treating note-taking and writing as disconnected stages in Section B, instead of one connected process.
- Dwelling on a missed listening detail instead of staying with the current question — the audio does not wait.
- Practising only one register of Urdu audio (e.g. only formal news-style), rather than varying speed and register.
