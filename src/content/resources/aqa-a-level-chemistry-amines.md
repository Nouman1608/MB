## What an amine is

An amine is a compound based on ammonia, NH₃, in which one or more hydrogen atoms are replaced by alkyl or
aryl groups. Amines are classified by the **number of carbon atoms bonded to the nitrogen**:

| Type | General formula | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Primary | RNH₂ | CH₃CH₂NH₂, ethylamine |
| Secondary | R₂NH | (CH₃CH₂)₂NH, diethylamine |
| Tertiary | R₃N | (CH₃)₃N, trimethylamine |
| Quaternary ammonium ion | R₄N⁺ | (CH₃CH₂)₄N⁺, tetraethylammonium |

Unlike alcohols, you count carbons on the nitrogen. (CH₃)₂CHNH₂ is **primary**: N carries one carbon.

An **aromatic** amine has N bonded directly to a benzene ring, as in **phenylamine**, C₆H₅NH₂; an
**aliphatic** amine has N bonded to an alkyl chain.

You will also see substitutive names such as ethanamine for ethylamine.

The **lone pair** on N explains this whole section: it accepts a proton (base) and attacks a δ+ carbon
(nucleophile). A quaternary ion has used its lone pair, so it is neither.

## 3.3.11.1 Preparation

### Route 1: ammonia and a halogenoalkane

Reagent and conditions: excess ammonia in ethanol, heated in a sealed container.

```
CH3CH2Br + 2NH3 → CH3CH2NH2 + NH4Br
```

The mechanism is in the halogenoalkanes guide. The weakness: the product amine is also a nucleophile, so
it reacts with more halogenoalkane, giving a mixture (see 3.3.11.3). Excess ammonia makes the primary
amine the main product, but the mixture still needs separating.

### Route 2: reduction of nitriles

A nitrile, R–C≡N, is reduced to a primary amine, RCH₂NH₂. The specification names the reaction type but
not a reagent. Two common choices are LiAlH₄ in dry ether, or hydrogen with a nickel catalyst.

```
CH3CN + 4[H] → CH3CH2NH2          (reducing agent written as [H])
CH3CN + 2H2  → CH3CH2NH2          (hydrogen, Ni catalyst)
```

The C≡N carbon becomes the CH₂ on N, so propanenitrile gives propylamine.

Advantages over route 1: only the primary amine forms. After the halogenoalkane-plus-cyanide reaction,
it also gives an amine with **one more carbon** than the starting halogenoalkane.

**Worked example 1 (atom economy of the two routes to ethylamine).** Use Ar: H = 1.0, C = 12.0, N = 14.0,
Br = 79.9. Compare route 1 (bromoethane + ammonia) with route 2 (ethanenitrile + hydrogen).

Step 1: Mr values. CH₃CH₂NH₂ = 45.0; CH₃CH₂Br = 108.9; NH₃ = 17.0; CH₃CN = 41.0; H₂ = 2.0.
Step 2: route 1 uses CH₃CH₂Br + 2NH₃, total Mr = 108.9 + 34.0 = 142.9.
Atom economy = 45.0 ÷ 142.9 × 100 = **31.5%**.
Step 3: route 2 uses CH₃CN + 2H₂, total Mr = 41.0 + 4.0 = 45.0.
Atom economy = 45.0 ÷ 45.0 × 100 = **100%**.
Step 4: route 1 also loses yield to further substitution, which atom economy does not show.

### Aromatic amines: reduction of nitro compounds

Benzene is nitrated (3.3.10.2) to nitrobenzene, and the nitro group is then reduced. A common method is tin and concentrated
hydrochloric acid, heated under reflux. In acid the product forms as its salt, so sodium hydroxide is then
added to release the free amine.

```
C6H5NO2 + 6[H] → C6H5NH2 + 2H2O
```

Count the [H]: two O atoms leave as 2H₂O (four H) and two H end up on N, so six [H] in total.

The specification says aromatic amines made this way are **used in the manufacture of dyes**.

**Worked example 2 (percentage yield).** 12.3 g of nitrobenzene gives 6.51 g of phenylamine. Use Ar:
H = 1.0, C = 12.0, N = 14.0, O = 16.0. Calculate the percentage yield.

Step 1: Mr C₆H₅NO₂ = 123.0; Mr C₆H₅NH₂ = 93.0.
Step 2: moles of nitrobenzene = 12.3 ÷ 123.0 = 0.100 mol.
Step 3: the mole ratio is 1 : 1, so the theoretical mass = 0.100 × 93.0 = 9.30 g.
Step 4: percentage yield = 6.51 ÷ 9.30 × 100 = **70.0%**.

## 3.3.11.2 Base properties

### Amines are weak bases

A Brønsted–Lowry base is a proton acceptor. The nitrogen lone pair forms a dative bond to H⁺. In water only
a small fraction of the amine reacts, so an amine is a **weak base**:

```
CH3CH2NH2 + H2O ⇌ CH3CH2NH3+ + OH−
```

The OH⁻ ions make the solution alkaline. With a strong acid the reaction goes to completion and forms a
salt:

```
CH3CH2NH2 + HCl → CH3CH2NH3+Cl−      (ethylammonium chloride)
```

Adding NaOH to the salt removes H⁺ from RNH₃⁺ and gives back the amine (the same step that releases
phenylamine after the tin and acid reduction).

### Comparing base strength

The specification asks you to compare three species. The order is:

**primary aliphatic amine > ammonia > primary aromatic amine**

for example, ethylamine > ammonia > phenylamine.

The rule is: the **more available the lone pair on N**, the more readily it accepts H⁺, so the stronger the
base.

- **Primary aliphatic amine.** Alkyl groups are electron-releasing (they have a positive inductive effect).
  They push electron density onto N, so the lone pair is more available than in ammonia. Stronger base
  than NH₃.
- **Ammonia.** No electron-releasing group on N. It is the reference point.
- **Primary aromatic amine.** The lone pair on N overlaps with the delocalised π system of the benzene ring
  and is partly delocalised into it. Less electron density is available on N, so it is a weaker base than
  NH₃.

**Worked example 3 (explain an order).** Explain why propylamine is a stronger base than phenylamine.

Step 1: both accept H⁺ using the lone pair on N.
Step 2: the propyl group is electron-releasing, increasing electron density on N.
Step 3: in phenylamine the N lone pair is delocalised into the benzene ring, decreasing electron density on N.
Step 4: the lone pair is more available in propylamine, so it accepts a proton more readily.

## 3.3.11.3 Nucleophilic properties

A nucleophile is an electron pair donor. The amine lone pair attacks an electron-deficient carbon, so
amines take part in the same nucleophilic substitution as ammonia.

### Substitution with halogenoalkanes: a ladder of products

Each product still has a lone pair on N, so it can attack another halogenoalkane:

```
NH3      + RX → RNH2 (primary)      + HX
RNH2     + RX → R2NH (secondary)    + HX
R2NH     + RX → R3N  (tertiary)     + HX
R3N      + RX → R4N+ X− (quaternary ammonium salt)
```

In practice the HX is taken up by more base as an ammonium salt.

To favour the **primary amine**, use excess ammonia. To favour the **quaternary ammonium salt**, use excess
halogenoalkane. The quaternary ion has no H on N to lose and no lone pair left, so the sequence stops there.

**Worked example 4 (outline the mechanism for ethylamine + bromomethane).** Describe each arrow.

Step 1: a curly arrow from the lone pair on N of CH₃CH₂NH₂ to the δ+ carbon of CH₃Br.
Step 2: a curly arrow from the C–Br bond to Br. The bond breaks heterolytically and Br⁻ leaves.
Step 3: the intermediate is CH₃CH₂N⁺H₂CH₃, with a positive charge on N.
Step 4: a second ethylamine removes H⁺ from N⁺ (arrow from its lone pair to the H; arrow from the N–H bond
to N⁺).
Products: **N-methylethylamine**, CH₃CH₂NHCH₃ (a secondary amine), and CH₃CH₂NH₃⁺Br⁻.

### Quaternary ammonium salts as cationic surfactants

A quaternary ammonium salt with one **long hydrocarbon chain**, such as CH₃(CH₂)₁₅N⁺(CH₃)₃ Br⁻, behaves as a
surfactant. The ionic N⁺ head is attracted to water and the long chain is not. Because the head group is
positive it is called a **cationic** surfactant, and it is attracted to negatively charged surfaces.

### Addition–elimination with acyl chlorides

Ammonia and primary amines react with acyl chlorides, RCOCl, to give amides. The reaction is fast at room
temperature.

```
CH3COCl + 2NH3       → CH3CONH2 + NH4Cl                   (ethanamide)
CH3COCl + 2CH3NH2    → CH3CONHCH3 + CH3NH3+Cl−            (N-methylethanamide)
```

A primary amine gives an **N-substituted amide**; the N- shows the alkyl group is on nitrogen. The second
mole of base takes up HCl as a salt.

**Worked example 5 (outline the mechanism for ethanoyl chloride + methylamine).**

Step 1 (addition): a curly arrow from the lone pair on N of CH₃NH₂ to the δ+ carbon of C=O. A second arrow
from the C=O double bond to O, giving O⁻.
Step 2: the intermediate has a tetrahedral carbon bonded to O⁻, Cl, CH₃ and N⁺H₂CH₃.
Step 3 (elimination): a curly arrow from the lone pair on O⁻ back to form C=O again, and an arrow from the
C–Cl bond to Cl, which leaves as Cl⁻.
Step 4: a base (Cl⁻, or a second CH₃NH₂) removes H⁺ from N⁺, with an arrow from the N–H bond to N.
Product: CH₃CONHCH₃, N-methylethanamide.

The carbonyl carbon is strongly δ+ because both O and Cl are more electronegative than carbon.

### Acid anhydrides

Acid anhydrides, (RCO)₂O, give the same amides, more slowly. The by-product is a carboxylic acid instead
of HCl:

```
(CH3CO)2O + NH3     → CH3CONH2 + CH3COOH
(CH3CO)2O + CH3NH2  → CH3CONHCH3 + CH3COOH
```

With excess base the acid is then neutralised to its salt. The specification does not ask for the
anhydride mechanism.

## Common errors

- Classifying (CH₃)₂CHNH₂ as secondary. Count carbons on N, not on the carbon next to N.
- Writing the nitrile reduction with 2[H]. C≡N to CH₂–NH₂ needs 4[H].
- Using 3[H] or 4[H] for nitrobenzene. It needs 6[H] and gives 2H₂O.
- Explaining phenylamine's weakness as "the benzene ring is electron-withdrawing" with no mention of the
  lone pair being delocalised into the ring.
- Stopping the substitution mechanism at the N⁺ intermediate without the deprotonation step.
- Naming CH₃CONHCH₃ "methylethanamide" without the N-, which places the methyl group on carbon.
