## 1. Budgetary control

A budget is a plan, stated in money terms, for a coming period. Budgetary control compares actual results with the budget, investigates differences and takes action. The syllabus asks for advantages and disadvantages to the organisation.

**Advantages:** it forces planning ahead; it co-ordinates departments (production cannot plan without the sales budget); it communicates targets; it sets a benchmark so managers can concentrate on large differences; it supports decisions about resources; it can motivate staff.

**Disadvantages:** preparing and revising budgets takes time and money; forecasts can be wrong, so budgets can mislead; a fixed plan can be rigid when conditions change; managers may add slack or spend unused budgets; targets seen as unfair can demotivate; a focus on money may hide quality, customer and staff issues.

## 2. Spreadsheets

**Advantages:** formulas recalculate every linked budget instantly when an assumption changes, so what-if questions are quick; arithmetic is more accurate; templates can be reused each period; charts and summaries are easy to produce.

**Disadvantages:** a wrong formula or hard-coded number can pass unnoticed and spread through linked sheets; weak version control and access control; files can be lost or altered without a record; staff need skills; a polished layout can hide poor assumptions.

## 3. The master budget

The master budget brings the functional budgets together into a summary: usually the budgeted statement of profit or loss, the budgeted statement of financial position and the cash budget. Management and owners can then see whether the plan achieves the organisation's objectives.

## 4. Limiting factors and preparing the budgets

A limiting factor (principal budget factor) is the resource or condition that restricts activity, such as customer demand, skilled labour, material supply, machine capacity or cash. Identify it first and build the other budgets around it. If demand is 1,300 units but capacity is 1,200, the sales budget is capped at 1,200 unless capacity is increased, for example by overtime, subcontracting or new machinery. The budget starts from the constraint, not from the wish.

Normal order: sales, production, purchases and labour, receivables and payables, cash, then the statement of profit or loss, then the statement of financial position.

### Worked example: Ormesby Planters, April to June

- Sales units: March 900, April 1,000, May 1,200, June 1,400, July 1,300. Selling price 40 per planter. Of sales, 25% are for cash and 75% on credit; credit customers pay in the following month.
- Closing finished goods are 20% of next month's sales; 200 units on 1 April.

## 5. Flexible budgets

A fixed budget is set for one planned activity level and is not changed. If actual output differs, comparing actual costs with it is unfair: higher output naturally costs more. A flexible budget restates the budget for the actual activity level. Variable items are recalculated per unit; fixed items stay the same within the normal range of activity.

**Benefits over fixed budgeting:** like-for-like comparison; clearer responsibility, since volume effects are removed; better cost control and planning; more realistic targets, so managers accept them.

### Worked example: Sutterby Lamps

Planned sales and output were 2,000 lamps; 2,300 were made and sold. Selling price was 50 per lamp. Budgeted cost per lamp: materials 12, labour 10, variable production overhead 3 and variable selling expense 2. Fixed production overhead 12,000 and fixed administration and selling costs 14,000.

| | Fixed budget 2,000 | Flexed budget 2,300 | Actual 2,300 | Difference F / (A) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Revenue | 100,000 | 115,000 | 112,700 | (2,300) |
| Materials | 24,000 | 27,600 | 29,900 | (2,300) |
| Labour | 20,000 | 23,000 | 22,700 | 300 |
| Variable production overhead | 6,000 | 6,900 | 7,500 | (600) |
| Fixed production overhead | 12,000 | 12,000 | 12,400 | (400) |
| Variable selling | 4,000 | 4,600 | 4,800 | (200) |
| Fixed administration and selling | 14,000 | 14,000 | 14,300 | (300) |
| Profit | 20,000 | 26,900 | 21,100 | (5,800) |

Against the fixed budget, profit looks 1,100 better. Against the flexed budget it is 5,800 worse. The extra 300 lamps explain a rise of 6,900 (300 x 23 contribution); the cost and price differences explain the rest.

## 6. Causes of differences

Differences between actual and flexed figures are not caused by volume. Possible causes: supplier price changes or discounts; poorer or better quality material; waste and spoilage; labour overtime, pay awards or slower work; machine breakdowns; selling price cuts or discounts to win sales; inflation in overheads; budget errors. Causes can be linked: cheaper low-quality material may give an adverse usage difference, and a price cut might lift volume. Adverse means profit is lower than the flexed budget; favourable means higher.

## 7. The two reconciliation statements

**Flexible budgeted cost of production to actual cost of production**: production costs are materials, labour, variable production overhead and fixed production overhead. Selling and administration costs are excluded.

| Sutterby Lamps | Amount |
|---|---|
| Flexible budgeted cost of production (2,300 lamps) | 69,500 |
| Materials | 2,300 adverse |
| Labour | 300 favourable |
| Variable production overhead | 600 adverse |
| Fixed production overhead | 400 adverse |
| Actual cost of production | 72,500 |

Check: 69,500 + 2,300 - 300 + 600 + 400 = 72,500.

**Flexible budgeted profit to actual profit**: start with 26,900, add favourable and deduct adverse differences, including revenue and selling costs: 26,900 - 2,300 (revenue) - 2,300 + 300 - 600 - 400 - 200 - 300 = 21,100.

## 8. Behavioural aspects

Budgets influence people, so the process matters.

- **Targets:** too easy and staff coast; too hard and they give up. Stretching but achievable targets work best.
- **Participation:** managers who help set the budget are more likely to accept it. Imposed budgets can cause resentment, though top-down control is quicker and keeps goals aligned with the organisation.
- **Incentives:** bonuses linked to budget achievement can motivate but also encourage slack, short-term cuts in training, maintenance or quality, or year-end spending just to use up budgets.
- **Controllability:** judge managers on costs they can control.

## 9. Decisions and non-financial factors

Recommendations need a balance of reasons and a justified conclusion. In Sutterby's case, higher volume was good, but material cost and price differences need investigation. Non-financial factors include staff morale, customer satisfaction, product quality, supplier relationships, environmental impact, reputation and long-term sustainability. A cut that improves the budget this year can harm these.

## Common errors

- Production budget: forgetting to add closing and subtract opening inventory.
- Putting depreciation in the cash budget; it is not a cash flow.
- Flexing fixed costs, or leaving variable costs unflexed.
- Calling a difference favourable because actual cost is higher than the fixed budget.
- Cash budget: using credit sales when cash is received a month later.
