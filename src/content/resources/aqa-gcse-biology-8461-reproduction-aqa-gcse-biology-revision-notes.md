## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Gamete | Sex cell (sperm, egg, pollen) with a single set of chromosomes |
| Meiosis | Cell division that forms four genetically different gametes |
| Fertilisation | Fusion of gametes, restoring the full chromosome number |
| Clone | Genetically identical offspring from asexual reproduction |
| DNA | Polymer of two strands forming a double helix |
| Gene | Small section of DNA coding for a sequence of amino acids (a protein) |
| Genome | The entire genetic material of an organism |
| Nucleotide | Sugar + phosphate + one of four bases (Biology only) |
| Allele | Different form of a gene |
| Genotype / phenotype | Alleles present / characteristic expressed |
| Homozygous / heterozygous | Two identical alleles / two different alleles |

## Sexual versus asexual

- **Sexual:** two parents; fusion of gametes; mixing of genetic information; variation; meiosis.
- **Asexual:** one parent; no fusion; no mixing; clones; mitosis only.

## Meiosis in four lines

1. Genetic information copied.
2. Cell divides **twice**.
3. **Four** gametes, each with a **single set** of chromosomes.
4. All gametes **genetically different**.

Fertilisation restores the full number → mitosis → more cells → cells differentiate.

## Advantages (Biology only)

| Sexual | Asexual |
|---|---|
| Variation | One parent |
| Survival advantage if the environment changes (natural selection) | Saves time and energy (no mate) |
| Humans can speed up selection by selective breeding | Faster |
| | Many identical offspring when conditions are favourable |

Both methods: malarial parasites (asexual in humans, sexual in mosquitoes); fungi (spores, plus sexual);
plants (seeds, plus runners in strawberries or bulb division in daffodils).

## Human genome: why it matters

- Finding genes linked to diseases.
- Understanding and treating inherited disorders.
- Tracing past human migration.

## DNA structure (Biology only)

- Four nucleotides; each = sugar + phosphate + base (A, C, G or T).
- Backbone of alternating sugar and phosphate; bases attached to sugars.
- **Higher tier only:** A–T and C–G pairing; three bases code for one amino acid; protein made on ribosomes from a
  template, carrier molecules bring amino acids; the chain folds into a unique shape (enzymes, hormones,
  collagen); most mutations have little or no effect, a few change the protein's shape; non-coding DNA switches
  genes on and off.

## Inheritance

- Dominant: shown with one copy. Recessive: shown only with two copies.
- Single-gene examples: fur colour in mice; red-green colour blindness.
- Most characteristics involve **multiple genes**.
- Ratios and probabilities are **predictions**; real numbers vary.

| Cross | Offspring ratio |
|---|---|
| AA × aa | All Aa (all dominant phenotype) |
| Aa × Aa | 1 AA : 2 Aa : 1 aa → 3 dominant : 1 recessive |
| Aa × aa | 1 Aa : 1 aa → 1 : 1 |

## Setting out a genetic cross

1. Choose letters: capital for dominant, lower case for recessive (same letter).
2. Write the **parents' genotypes**.
3. Write each parent's possible **gametes** (one allele each).
4. Draw the **Punnett square**.
5. List offspring **genotypes**, then **phenotypes**.
6. Give the **ratio**, **fraction**, **percentage** or **probability** the question asks for.

Worked reminder: Bb × bb → Bb, Bb, bb, bb → 1 : 1 → probability of bb = 0.5.

## Family tree rules

- Two unaffected parents with an affected child → the allele is **recessive**; both parents are carriers.
- An affected child always has at least one affected parent if the allele is **dominant**.
- A person with a dominant phenotype may be homozygous or heterozygous; check their children for clues.

## Disorders and sex

- **Polydactyly:** dominant allele; extra fingers or toes.
- **Cystic fibrosis:** recessive allele; disorder of cell membranes; carriers are heterozygous.
- **Embryo screening:** judge economic, social and ethical issues from given information.
- **Sex:** 23 pairs; one pair are sex chromosomes; XX female, XY male; 50% chance of each.

## Exam traps

- Meiosis gives **four** cells, not two; they are **different**.
- Gametes have **one set**, not "half a set".
- Allele ≠ gene.
- Recessive does not mean rare.
- 3 : 1 is a probability, not a guarantee.
- Only the father's gamete decides sex (X or Y sperm).
- Construction of Punnett squares for prediction is **Higher tier only**; interpreting them is for everyone.

## Self-test

1. How many gametes does one cell produce in meiosis?
2. Why must gametes have a single set of chromosomes?
3. Give two advantages of asexual reproduction.
4. How do strawberry plants reproduce asexually?
5. Define genome.
6. Give one reason why studying the human genome is important.
7. What three parts make up a nucleotide?
8. (Higher tier only) Which base pairs with C?
9. What is the probability of a recessive phenotype from Aa × Aa?
10. Is cystic fibrosis caused by a dominant or a recessive allele?
11. What are the sex chromosomes of a male?
12. Two unaffected parents have a child with a disorder. What does this show about the allele?

### Answers

1. Four.
2. So that fertilisation restores the normal number of chromosomes.
3. Any two: one parent; time and energy efficient; faster; many identical offspring in favourable conditions.
4. By runners.
5. The entire genetic material of an organism.
6. Any one: finding genes linked to disease; understanding and treating inherited disorders; tracing human migration.
7. A sugar, a phosphate group and a base.
8. G.
9. 0.25 (25%, 1 in 4).
10. Recessive.
11. XY.
12. It is recessive, and both parents are carriers.
