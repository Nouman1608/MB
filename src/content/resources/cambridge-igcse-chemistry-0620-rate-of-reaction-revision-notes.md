Condensed notes for subtopic **6.2 Rate of reaction**, Cambridge IGCSE Chemistry 0620, 2026–2028. Items marked *(Extended)* are Supplement content. For full explanations and a worked graph example, see the [rate of reaction study guide](/resources/cambridge-igcse-chemistry-0620-rate-of-reaction/); to test yourself under exam conditions, use the [rate of reaction practice questions](/resources/cambridge-igcse-chemistry-0620-rate-of-reaction-practice/).

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Rate of reaction | How quickly a reactant is used up or a product forms |
| Mean rate | Change in amount of product (or reactant) ÷ time taken |
| Catalyst | Increases the rate of a reaction and is unchanged at the end |
| Enzyme | A biological catalyst |
| Activation energy, Ea *(Extended)* | Minimum energy colliding particles need to react |
| Collision frequency *(Extended)* | Number of collisions per second |
| Limiting reactant | The reactant that runs out first and so stops the reaction |

## The five factors at a glance

| Factor | Change that speeds up the reaction | *(Extended)* Why |
|---|---|---|
| Concentration | Higher concentration of a solution | More particles per unit volume, so more frequent collisions |
| Pressure | Higher pressure of a gas | More particles per unit volume, so more frequent collisions |
| Surface area | Smaller pieces / powder | More particles exposed, so more frequent collisions at the surface |
| Temperature | Higher temperature | Particles have more kinetic energy: more frequent collisions **and** a greater proportion of collisions with energy ≥ Ea |
| Catalyst | Add a catalyst or enzyme | Lowers Ea, so a greater proportion of collisions have enough energy |

Removing a catalyst returns the rate to its uncatalysed value.

## Collision theory in four lines *(Extended)*

1. Particles must collide to react.
2. The collision must have energy at least equal to Ea.
3. Rate depends on how **frequent** successful collisions are.
4. Factors change either the collision frequency, or the proportion of collisions with energy ≥ Ea, or both (temperature does both).

## Catalysts

- Core: increase rate; chemically unchanged at the end; can be reused.
- *(Extended)* Decrease the activation energy of the reaction.
- A catalyst does not change the amount of product formed.

## Practical methods

**Gas formed:** measure volume at regular time intervals.

- Gas syringe, or
- Upturned measuring cylinder or burette full of water.

**Change in mass:** flask on a balance, cotton wool plug in the neck; record the falling mass as gas escapes.

Typical reactions:

```
Mg + 2HCl → MgCl2 + H2                 (gas volume; H2 too light for mass loss)
CaCO3 + 2HCl → CaCl2 + H2O + CO2       (gas volume or mass loss)
```

**Apparatus to name:** stop-watch, balance, gas syringe, measuring cylinder, thermometer.

## Evaluating methods *(Extended)*

- Mass loss is unsuitable for hydrogen: the mass change is too small to measure accurately.
- Carbon dioxide dissolves slightly in water, so collecting it over water gives a low reading; a gas syringe avoids this.
- Gas lost while the bung is put in gives low early readings.
- Control temperature with a water bath; keep volumes and concentrations of other reactants the same.
- Repeat and take a mean; identify anomalies.

## Reading rate graphs

- **Gradient = rate.** Steeper means faster.
- Curve steepest at the start, then less steep as reactants are used up.
- Flat section: reaction finished; the limiting reactant has run out.
- Same amount of limiting reactant → same final volume or mass, whatever the rate.
- Doubling the amount of limiting reactant → double the final volume.
- Mean rate between two times = difference in readings ÷ time interval, with units (cm³/s, g/s).

**Comparing two curves**

| New curve is... | Means... |
|---|---|
| Steeper, same final level | Faster, same amount of limiting reactant (e.g. powder, warmer, catalyst) |
| Less steep, same final level | Slower, same amount of limiting reactant (e.g. lumps, cooler) |
| Lower final level | Less limiting reactant used |
| Higher final level | More limiting reactant used |

## Exam traps

- "More collisions" is not enough; write **more frequent collisions** or **more collisions per second**.
- Temperature *(Extended)*: two marks usually need both the frequency point and the energy (Ea) point.
- A catalyst does **not** give particles more energy; it lowers Ea.
- Bigger lumps = **smaller** surface area.
- Rate affects how quickly product forms, not how much forms.
- Pressure only affects reactions involving **gases**.
- Mass of the flask **falls** in a mass-loss experiment because gas escapes; it does not rise.
- Give units with every rate value.

## Self-test

1. State the effect on rate of using magnesium powder instead of ribbon.
2. State two properties of a catalyst (Core).
3. *(Extended)* Define activation energy.
4. *(Extended)* Explain, using collision theory, why increasing the concentration of an acid increases the rate.
5. *(Extended)* Give the two reasons a higher temperature increases the rate.
6. Name two pieces of apparatus used to measure the volume of a gas produced.
7. 45 cm³ of gas is collected in the first 30 s. Calculate the mean rate.
8. Why does a volume–time graph level off?
9. *(Extended)* Why is measuring mass loss unsuitable for the reaction of magnesium with acid?
10. A reaction is repeated with the same mass of marble chips but more concentrated acid (acid still in excess). How do the two curves differ?
11. *(Extended)* How does a catalyst increase the rate?
12. What is an enzyme?

**Answers**

1. Rate increases (powder has a larger surface area).
2. It increases the rate; it is chemically unchanged at the end of the reaction.
3. The minimum energy that colliding particles must have to react.
4. More acid particles per unit volume, so more frequent collisions between acid particles and the other reactant, so the rate increases.
5. Particles move faster so collisions are more frequent; a greater proportion of collisions have energy equal to or above Ea.
6. Gas syringe; upturned measuring cylinder (or burette) filled with water.
7. 45 ÷ 30 = 1.5 cm³/s.
8. A reactant (the limiting reactant) has been used up, so no more gas is produced.
9. Hydrogen has a very low mass, so the change in mass is too small to measure accurately.
10. The more concentrated acid gives a steeper initial curve that levels off sooner, at the same final volume.
11. It lowers the activation energy, so a greater proportion of collisions have enough energy to react.
12. A biological catalyst.
