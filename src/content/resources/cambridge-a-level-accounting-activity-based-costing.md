## Traditional absorption versus ABC

The syllabus presents ABC as a contrast to the traditional methods in 2.2. Absorption costing shares production overheads using a single basis such as direct labour hours. That works when every product consumes overhead in proportion to labour time. It misleads when a product is made in small batches, needs many inspections or generates many orders, because those costs have nothing to do with labour hours.

ABC links overheads to the activities that cause them. Each product then picks up overhead according to how many times it uses each activity.

## Cost drivers

A **cost driver** is the factor that causes the cost of an activity to be incurred. If the factor doubles, the cost of the activity tends to rise too. The driver also measures how much of the activity each product uses, which is what lets you share the cost fairly.

| Activity | Possible cost driver |
|---|---|
| Changing a machine between batches | Number of set-ups |
| Checking quality | Number of inspections |
| Buying materials | Number of purchase orders |
| Running machines | Machine hours |
| Processing customer orders | Number of orders despatched |

## The ABC method in five steps

1. **Group overheads into cost pools**, one for each activity.
2. **Identify the cost driver** for each pool.
3. **Calculate a cost driver rate** for each pool: pool cost divided by total driver quantity.
4. **Allocate overhead to each product**: driver rate multiplied by the product's use of the driver. Divide by units made to get overhead per unit.
5. **Add direct costs** to find total cost per unit, then add the mark-up or margin to find the selling price.

### Worked example 1: Calderfell Plastics

Calderfell Plastics makes the Standard crate (S) and the Custom crate (C). It makes 24,000 S and 3,000 C. Direct materials are 7.50 per S and 12.00 per C. Direct labour is 0.25 hours per S and 1.00 hour per C at 16 per hour. Machine time is 0.20 hours per S and 0.40 hours per C. Overheads are 198,000. Prices are set at cost plus a 25% mark-up on cost.

**Steps 1 to 3: pools, drivers and rates**

| Activity pool | Cost | Driver | S use | C use | Total | Rate |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Machine set-ups | 72,000 | Set-ups | 60 | 140 | 200 | 360 |
| Quality inspections | 54,000 | Inspections | 300 | 240 | 540 | 100 |
| Machine running | 48,000 | Machine hours | 4,800 | 1,200 | 6,000 | 8 |
| Purchase orders | 24,000 | Orders placed | 90 | 30 | 120 | 200 |

**Step 4: overhead allocated**

| Pool | S | C |
|---|---|---|
| Set-ups | 21,600 | 50,400 |
| Inspections | 30,000 | 24,000 |
| Machine running | 38,400 | 9,600 |
| Purchase orders | 18,000 | 6,000 |
| Total | 108,000 | 90,000 |
| Per unit | 4.50 | 30.00 |

The two totals add to 198,000, so all overhead has been shared out.

**Traditional comparison.** Labour hours are 6,000 (S) plus 3,000 (C) = 9,000. The absorption rate is 198,000 / 9,000 = 22.00 per hour. S takes 0.25 × 22 = 5.50 and C takes 1.00 × 22 = 22.00.

**Step 5: unit cost and selling price**

| Per unit | S traditional | S ABC | C traditional | C ABC |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Direct materials | 7.50 | 7.50 | 12.00 | 12.00 |
| Direct labour | 4.00 | 4.00 | 16.00 | 16.00 |
| Overhead | 5.50 | 4.50 | 22.00 | 30.00 |
| Total cost | 17.00 | 16.00 | 50.00 | 58.00 |
| Price at 25% mark-up | 21.25 | 20.00 | 62.50 | 72.50 |

Custom crates are made in small runs, so they use 70% of set-ups (140 of 200) while accounting for only 33% of labour hours. Traditional costing hides this and under-costs C by 8.00 per unit.

## How the method changes cost and profit

Changing the absorption method does not change total overheads. It changes how they are split between products, and so each product's reported cost and profit.

Suppose Calderfell's current prices were set using the traditional costs: 21.25 for S and 62.50 for C. Machine hours give an alternative traditional basis: 198,000 / 6,000 = 33 per hour, so S takes 0.20 × 33 = 6.60 and C takes 0.40 × 33 = 13.20.

| Per unit | Labour hours | Machine hours | ABC |
|---|---|---|---|
| S overhead | 5.50 | 6.60 | 4.50 |
| C overhead | 22.00 | 13.20 | 30.00 |
| S profit at 21.25 | 4.25 | 3.15 | 5.25 |
| C profit at 62.50 | 12.50 | 21.30 | 4.50 |

Total profit is 139,500 under all three methods, because every unit made was sold. The method only moves profit between products. The reported profit of C ranges from 4.50 to 21.30 per unit, which could lead a manager to the wrong decision.

**Effect when inventory is left unsold.** Total profit for the period does change if production and sales differ, because overhead carried in closing inventory is not charged to the period. Suppose Calderfell sells 21,000 S and 2,500 C. Closing inventory is 3,000 S and 500 C, valued at full cost:

| Basis | Closing inventory value |
|---|---|
| Labour hours | 3,000 × 17.00 + 500 × 50.00 = 76,000 |
| Machine hours | 3,000 × 18.10 + 500 × 41.20 = 74,900 |
| ABC | 3,000 × 16.00 + 500 × 58.00 = 77,000 |

A higher closing inventory means less cost is charged against sales, so profit is higher. ABC profit is 1,000 above the labour hours profit and 2,100 above the machine hours profit.

## Uses of ABC

- **More accurate product costs**, because overhead follows the activities that cause it.
- **Better pricing**: prices reflect the real cost of complex or low-volume products.
- **Spotting products that look profitable but are not**, as with C above.
- **Cost control**: managers can target the drivers, for example cutting set-ups by making longer production runs.
- **Support for decisions** on product range, pricing and process changes.

## Limitations

- **Cost and time**: identifying pools, drivers and collecting data is expensive, and the system must be kept up to date.
- **Judgement**: choosing pools and drivers is subjective, and some costs, such as factory rent, have no clear driver and are still shared arbitrarily.
- **Fixed costs do not fall automatically**: if set-ups fall by 40, the saving appears only when the resources (staff hours, equipment) are actually removed.
- **Historical data**: costs are based on past activity levels, which may not suit changed conditions.
- **Limited gain for some businesses**: where overheads are small or products use activities in similar proportions, ABC and traditional results will be close.

## Choosing a cost driver

A good driver passes these tests:

1. **Cause and effect**: more of the driver really does mean more cost in that pool.
2. **Measurable**: the quantity can be recorded for each product.
3. **Available at reasonable cost**: records already exist or are cheap to create.
4. **Differs between products**: if every product uses the activity in the same proportion, any driver gives the same answer.

For a pool covering invoicing, the number of invoices issued is better than direct labour hours, because invoices cause the work.

## Worked example 2: a pricing recommendation

Calderfell's competitor sells a similar Custom crate at 66.00. Managers ask what to do about C.

- ABC total cost of C is 58.00. A 25% mark-up gives 72.50, which is above the rival's price.
- At the old price of 62.50, profit is 4.50 per unit, or 13,500 for 3,000 units.
- At 66.00, profit is 8.00 per unit. As a mark-up on cost this is 8.00 / 58.00 = 13.8%. Profit is 24,000 for the same volume.
- If larger batches cut C set-ups from 140 to 100, and the cost of those 40 set-ups (40 × 360 = 14,400) is genuinely removed, C overhead falls to 75,600, or 25.20 per unit. Total cost becomes 53.20 and profit at 66.00 is 12.80 per unit, or 38,400.

**Recommendation.** Set the price at 66.00 and move to larger batches. This keeps C competitive and raises profit. Balance this against non-financial factors: larger batches mean customers wait longer for custom orders, which could damage relationships, and the saving depends on reducing staff hours. Review results after one quarter. Paper 4 expects recommendations supported by a balance of reasons, so always give a reason on each side before you decide.

## Common errors

- Dividing a pool by units produced instead of by driver quantity.
- Using labour hours as the driver for every pool, which reproduces traditional costing.
- Forgetting to divide product overhead by units to reach overhead per unit.
- Adding the mark-up to overhead only, instead of to total cost.
- Claiming ABC changes total overhead. It changes the split between products, and total profit only where inventory changes.
- Describing a driver as "the activity". The driver is the measure of the activity.
