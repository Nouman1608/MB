This guide covers sub-topic C.2, Wave model, in theme C (Wave behaviour) of the IB *Physics guide* (first assessment 2025). C.2 is the same for standard level (SL) and higher level (HL). It sets up the language of waves used throughout theme C: the wave phenomena of C.3, the standing waves of C.4 and the Doppler effect of C.5.

The [travelling waves and Doppler effect study guide](/resources/ib-dp-physics-travelling-waves-and-the-doppler-effect/) introduces each C.2 idea with one example. This page goes further on the skills that examiners test with data: extracting λ, T and A from two kinds of graph, predicting what changes when a wave crosses into a new medium, separating particle motion from wave motion, and placing a wave in the electromagnetic spectrum from its frequency. Afterwards use the [C.2 revision notes](/resources/ib-ib-dp-physics-dp-physics-ib-dp-physics-c-2-revision-notes/) and the [C.2 practice questions](/resources/ib-ib-dp-physics-dp-physics-ib-dp-physics-c-2-practice/).

## The wave model in one sentence

A travelling wave transfers **energy** from one place to another without any **net transfer of matter**. Every quantity in C.2 describes either the **pattern** (wavelength, wave speed) or the **oscillation at one point** (amplitude, period, frequency).

| Quantity | Symbol, unit | Describes | Read it from |
|---|---|---|---|
| Amplitude | A, m | Maximum displacement from equilibrium | Either graph (peak height) |
| Wavelength | λ, m | Distance between neighbouring points in phase | Displacement–position graph only |
| Period | T, s | Time for one oscillation | Displacement–time graph only |
| Frequency | f, Hz | Oscillations per second, f = 1/T | From T |
| Wave speed | v, m s⁻¹ | Speed of the pattern | v = fλ = λ/T |

## Reading the two graphs

A **displacement–position** graph is a snapshot: every particle at one instant. A **displacement–time** graph follows one particle over time. They look alike, so check the horizontal axis label before reading anything off.

**Worked example 1.** A snapshot of a wave on a string shows crests at x = 0.20 m, 0.80 m and 1.40 m, with peak displacement 4.0 mm. A displacement–time graph for one point on the string shows maxima at t = 0.010 s and t = 0.035 s.

- λ = 0.80 − 0.20 = **0.60 m** (crest to neighbouring crest, from the position graph).
- T = 0.035 − 0.010 = **0.025 s** (from the time graph), so f = 1/0.025 = **40 Hz**.
- v = fλ = 40 × 0.60 = **24 m s⁻¹**.
- A = **4.0 mm** from either graph.

Common error: taking "λ" from the time graph. A time graph never shows a distance.

## Particle speed is not wave speed

Each particle oscillates about its equilibrium position. A particle at a crest or trough is momentarily **at rest**; a particle passing through equilibrium moves **fastest**. The wave speed, by contrast, is constant in a uniform medium.

For the wave in Worked example 1, each particle moves with simple harmonic motion (C.1), so its maximum speed is

v_max = ωA = 2πfA = 2π × 40 × 4.0 × 10⁻³ ≈ **1.0 m s⁻¹**

while the pattern moves at 24 m s⁻¹. The two speeds are unrelated quantities.

**Direction of particle motion.** On a snapshot, imagine the pattern shifted slightly in the direction of travel. A point that is about to receive a higher part of the profile is moving up. For a wave moving to the right, particles on the **leading** slope of a crest (in front of it) are moving up; particles behind the crest are moving down.

## Crossing into a new medium

The **source** sets the frequency; the **medium** sets the speed. So when a wave crosses a boundary:

- f stays the same (each oscillation arriving at the boundary produces one oscillation on the other side);
- v changes to the speed in the new medium;
- λ = v/f changes in proportion to v.

**Worked example 2: sound into water.** A 500 Hz sound in air (v = 340 m s⁻¹) enters water (v = 1500 m s⁻¹).

- λ in air = 340/500 = **0.68 m**.
- λ in water = 1500/500 = **3.0 m**.
- Frequency is still 500 Hz, so the pitch heard by a swimmer is unchanged.

**Worked example 3: light into glass.** Light of wavelength 600 nm in a vacuum enters glass, where its speed is 2.0 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹.

- f = c/λ = 3.00 × 10⁸ / 6.00 × 10⁻⁷ = **5.0 × 10¹⁴ Hz** (unchanged in the glass).
- λ in glass = v/f = 2.0 × 10⁸ / 5.0 × 10¹⁴ = 4.0 × 10⁻⁷ m = **400 nm**.

The colour depends on frequency, so the light does not change colour in the glass even though its wavelength is shorter.

## The nature of sound

Sound is a **longitudinal mechanical wave**. The source pushes on neighbouring particles, making **compressions** (higher pressure, particles closer together) and **rarefactions** (lower pressure, particles further apart). Sound therefore needs a medium and cannot travel through a vacuum. It generally travels faster in liquids than in gases, and faster still in solids, because particles are more strongly coupled.

**Worked example 4: an echo.** A ship's sonar pulse returns from the sea bed 0.36 s after it is sent. The speed of sound in seawater is 1500 m s⁻¹.

- The pulse travels down and back, so distance = vt = 1500 × 0.36 = 540 m.
- Depth = 540/2 = **270 m**.

## The nature of electromagnetic waves

Electromagnetic (EM) waves are **transverse** oscillations of electric and magnetic fields, at right angles to each other and to the direction of travel. No medium is needed: all EM waves travel at **c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹** in a vacuum, and more slowly in materials such as glass or water.

### Placing a wave in the spectrum

Use λ = c/f, then compare with the orders of magnitude (data booklet values, with overlapping boundaries):

| Region | Approximate wavelength |
|---|---|
| Radio | 10⁻¹ m and longer |
| Microwave | 10⁻³ to 10⁻¹ m |
| Infrared | 10⁻⁶ to 10⁻³ m |
| Visible | about 4 × 10⁻⁷ to 7 × 10⁻⁷ m |
| Ultraviolet | 10⁻⁸ to 10⁻⁷ m |
| X-ray | 10⁻¹¹ to 10⁻⁸ m |
| Gamma | 10⁻¹² m and shorter |

**Worked example 5.** Identify the region for f = 1.0 × 10¹⁰ Hz and for f = 1.0 × 10¹⁸ Hz.

- λ = 3.0 × 10⁸ / 1.0 × 10¹⁰ = **0.030 m** → microwave.
- λ = 3.0 × 10⁸ / 1.0 × 10¹⁸ = **3.0 × 10⁻¹⁰ m** → X-ray.

## Mechanical and EM waves compared

| | Mechanical | Electromagnetic |
|---|---|---|
| What oscillates | Particles of a medium | Electric and magnetic fields |
| Medium | Required | Not required |
| Type | Transverse (string, water surface) or longitudinal (sound) | Transverse only |
| Speed | Depends on the medium | c in vacuum; slower in matter |
| Example | Sound, seismic waves, waves on a string | Radio, light, X-rays |

## Common mistakes

- Reading wavelength off a displacement–time graph, or period off a displacement–position graph.
- Measuring wavelength from crest to trough (that is λ/2).
- Saying frequency changes when a wave enters a new medium.
- Confusing the particle's maximum speed with the wave speed.
- Describing sound as "particles travelling from the source to the ear".
- Claiming all EM waves travel at c in every medium; c is the vacuum speed.
- Forgetting to halve the total distance in echo problems.

## Exam technique

- **State** and **outline**: give the definition or feature with no padding (for example, "longitudinal: oscillations parallel to the direction of energy transfer").
- **Determine** and **calculate**: show the reading from the graph, the equation, the substitution and the unit.
- **Describe** particle motion: say direction, when the particle is at rest and when it is fastest.
- **Compare**: give similarities and differences in matching pairs, as in the table above.
