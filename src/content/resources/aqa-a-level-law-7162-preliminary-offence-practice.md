These questions test the section 3.2 row on the preliminary offence of attempt. In Section B, identify the full offence first, then the actus reus, mens rea and any impossibility point.

---

## Section A

**1.** State the actus reus and mens rea of attempt under s1(1) Criminal Attempts Act 1981. **[2]**

**2.** Explain what Lord Lane meant by "embarked on the crime proper" in *R v Gullefer* (1990). **[2]**

**3.** Explain how the roles of judge and jury are divided under s4(3) Criminal Attempts Act 1981. **[2]**

**4.** State the mens rea of attempted murder and give an authority. **[2]**

**5.** Explain the effect of s1(2) and s1(3) Criminal Attempts Act 1981. **[3]**

**6.** Explain why a person cannot be guilty of attempted battery. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** Explain, using **two** cases, how the courts have decided whether an act is more than merely preparatory. **[5]**

**8.** Rhys plans to rob a petrol station. He puts on a balaclava and walks towards the door carrying a knife, but sees a police car and runs away before entering. Advise Rhys on attempted robbery. **[6]**

**9.** Sana, intending to kill her business partner Tom, puts poison in his coffee. Tom notices a strange taste and does not drink it. Advise Sana on attempted murder. **[5]**

**10.** Uri, intending to injure Vic seriously but not to kill him, stabs at him with a knife and misses. Explain which offences Uri may have attempted. **[5]**

**11.** Wes buys a white powder believing it to be cocaine and intending to sell it. It is in fact baking powder. Explain whether Wes can be guilty of an attempt. **[5]**

**12.** Evaluate the law on attempts. **[10]**

---

## Answers

**1.** Actus reus: doing an act which is more than merely preparatory to the commission of the offence [1]. Mens rea: intention to commit the full offence [1].

**2.** The defendant is guilty of an attempt once he has gone beyond mere preparation and has actually begun to commit the offence itself [1]. This may be before the last act needed to complete the crime [1].

**3.** The judge decides whether there is evidence on which the jury could find that the defendant's act was more than merely preparatory [1]; if there is, the jury decides whether it actually was [1].

**4.** An intention to kill; an intention to cause GBH is not enough [1] (*R v Whybrow*, 1951) [1].

**5.** Section 1(2) provides that a person may be guilty of attempt even though the facts make the commission of the offence impossible [1]. Section 1(3) provides that the defendant is judged on the facts as he believed them to be [1]. Together they mean factual impossibility is not a defence, as confirmed in *R v Shivpuri* (1987) [1].

**6.** Section 1(4) applies the offence of attempt only to offences triable on indictment [1]. Assault and battery are summary offences, so there is no offence of attempting them [1].

**7.** The test is whether the defendant has embarked on the crime proper (*R v Gullefer*, 1990) [1]. In *R v Campbell* (1991) a man near a post office with an imitation gun and a note had not attempted robbery, because he had not entered [1]. In *R v Jones* (1990) a man who got into a car and pointed a loaded gun at the victim had attempted murder, even though he had not pulled the trigger [1]. These cases show the last act is not needed [1], but that being near the scene, even fully equipped, may still be preparation (credit *Geddes*, *Tosti* or *Boyle and Boyle*) [1].

**8.** Robbery is an indictable offence, so it can be attempted [1]. Rhys must have done an act more than merely preparatory, having embarked on the crime proper (*R v Gullefer*, 1990) [1]. His facts are close to *R v Campbell* (1991), where a man outside a post office with an imitation gun had not attempted robbery because he had not entered [1], and to *R v Geddes* (1996) [1]. Putting on a balaclava and walking towards the door with a knife is arguably still preparation, though the jury could find he was close to execution [1]. He clearly intended to rob, but on the authority of *Campbell* he is unlikely to be guilty of attempted robbery [1].

**9.** Murder can be attempted [1]. Putting poison in the coffee is more than merely preparatory: Sana has done all she needs to do to commit the offence [1], and even the last act is not required (*A-G's Reference (No 1 of 1992)*) [1]. She intended to kill, which is the required mens rea (*R v Whybrow*, 1951) [1]. The fact that Tom did not drink the coffee is irrelevant, so she is likely to be guilty of attempted murder [1].

**10.** Attempted murder requires an intention to kill (*R v Whybrow*, 1951) [1], so as Uri intended only serious injury he is not guilty of attempted murder [1]. He may be guilty of attempted s18: stabbing at Vic is more than merely preparatory [1], and he intended to cause GBH, which is the mens rea of s18 [1]. Attempted s18 carries the same maximum as s18, which is life (s4(1)) [1].

**11.** Under s1(2) a person may be guilty of attempt even though the offence is impossible [1], and under s1(3) he is judged on the facts as he believed them [1]. Wes believed the powder was cocaine and intended to supply it [1]. In *R v Shivpuri* (1987) the House of Lords held a man guilty of an attempted drug offence where the substance was harmless, overruling *Anderton v Ryan* (1985) [1]. Buying the powder with the intention of selling it may also need to be more than merely preparatory to the supply offence, which is for the jury, but impossibility is no defence [1].

**12.** Levels-based. **Actus reus:** "more than merely preparatory" is vague and depends on the jury [1], so similar cases produce different results, such as *R v Campbell* (1991) and *R v Geddes* (1996) compared with *R v Tosti* (1997) [1]. This conflicts with maximum certainty [1]. Requiring the defendant to have embarked on the crime proper can stop the police from intervening early, as in *Geddes*, where a man equipped to kidnap was not guilty [1]. **Mens rea:** requiring intention is consistent with the fault principle [1], but requiring an intention to kill for attempted murder (*R v Whybrow*, 1951) is narrower than murder [1]. **Impossibility:** *R v Shivpuri* (1987) means defendants cannot escape on technicalities [1], but critics say this punishes a guilty mind with a harmless act [1]. **Reform:** the Law Commission consulted in 2007 on reforms including a separate offence of criminal preparation, but no change has been made [1]. A reasoned conclusion [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Not naming the full offence being attempted.
- Requiring the last act.
- Using GBH intent for attempted murder.
- Treating impossibility as a defence.
