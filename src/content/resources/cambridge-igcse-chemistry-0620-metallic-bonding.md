This guide covers subtopic **2.7 Metallic bonding** in Topic 2, Atoms, elements and
compounds, for Cambridge IGCSE Chemistry 0620 (2026–2028 series). It expands the short
section in [Ionic, Covalent and Metallic Bonding](/resources/ionic-covalent-and-metallic-bonding/)
into a full account of the model, how to draw it and how to write the explanations
examiners credit.

## Who needs this subtopic

**Subtopic 2.7 is Supplement only.** The Core column is empty, so Core candidates are not
assessed on metallic bonding. Extended candidates must be able to do both outcomes:

1. Describe metallic bonding as the **electrostatic attraction between the positive ions in
   a giant metallic lattice and a 'sea' of delocalised electrons**.
2. Explain, in terms of structure and bonding, the properties of metals:
   (a) **good electrical conductivity**; (b) **malleability and ductility**.

Core candidates still meet the *properties* of metals as facts in Topic 9 (Metals); what
they do not need is this bonding explanation.

## Building the model

Metals are elements whose atoms have few outer-shell electrons, which they lose easily. In a
piece of solid metal:

1. Each metal atom **releases its outer-shell electrons**. A sodium atom (2,8,1) releases
   one electron and becomes Na⁺; a magnesium atom (2,8,2) releases two and becomes Mg²⁺.
2. The **positive ions** pack together in **regular layers**, forming a **giant metallic
   lattice** that extends throughout the metal.
3. The released electrons are not given to any other atom. They spread through the whole
   lattice as a **'sea' of delocalised electrons**, free to move between the ions.
4. The **metallic bond** is the **electrostatic attraction** between the positive ions and
   the sea of delocalised electrons around them.

**Delocalised** means not fixed to any one atom or bond. That is the difference from
covalent bonding, where a shared pair belongs to a particular bond between two atoms, and
from ionic bonding, where electrons are transferred to particular non-metal atoms.

Because each positive ion is attracted to the electron sea all around it, rather than to one
particular neighbour, the bonding acts in **all directions**. This is the key to
malleability, below.

## How to draw the structure

A diagram that earns credit shows:

- **Regular rows** of circles, each labelled **+** (or 2+ for a Group II metal) — these are
  the **positive ions**, not atoms.
- **Electrons** (e⁻ or dots) **scattered between** the ions, not attached to any one ion.
- Labels: "positive ion" and "delocalised electron".

Typical errors: drawing negative ions among the positive ones, which turns the diagram into
an ionic lattice; or drawing electrons in pairs between two ions, which looks covalent.

## Explaining good electrical conductivity

An electric current is a flow of charged particles. In a metal:

- the **delocalised electrons are free to move** through the lattice;
- when a voltage is applied, they **move through the metal**, carrying charge, from the
  negative end towards the positive end.

This works in the solid and also when a metal is molten, because the electron sea is present
in both states. Compare this with:

| Substance | Charge carriers | Conducts when solid? |
|---|---|---|
| Metal | Delocalised electrons | Yes |
| Graphite | Delocalised electrons | Yes |
| Ionic compound | Ions | No — only molten or aqueous |
| Simple molecular | None | No |
| Diamond, SiO₂ | None | No |

Metals and graphite are the only solids on the 0620 course that conduct, and in both cases
the reason is delocalised electrons.

## Explaining malleability and ductility

- **Malleable** — can be hammered or pressed into shape (for example into thin sheets)
  without breaking.
- **Ductile** — can be drawn out into wires.

Both properties have the same explanation:

1. The positive ions are arranged in **layers**.
2. When a force is applied, **layers of ions slide over one another**.
3. The **delocalised electrons move with the ions**, so the attraction between ions and
   electrons is **maintained in the new positions**.
4. The metal **changes shape without breaking**.

The phrase that turns a description into an explanation is step 3: the bonding is not
broken because the electron sea is still there after the layers move.

## Worked model answers

**Question:** *Describe metallic bonding.* **[3]**

> A **giant lattice of positive (metal) ions** ✓ surrounded by a **sea of delocalised
> electrons** ✓. The bond is the **electrostatic attraction between the positive ions and
> the delocalised electrons** ✓.

**Question:** *Explain why copper is used for electrical wiring, in terms of structure and
bonding.* **[3]**

> Copper has **delocalised electrons** ✓ that are **free to move** through the lattice and
> carry charge ✓, so it is a good conductor. Its layers of positive ions **can slide over
> one another without the bonding breaking**, so it is **ductile** and can be drawn into
> wires ✓.

Notice that the wiring answer uses *both* outcomes: conductivity explains why copper
works in a wire; ductility explains why it can be made into one.

## Common mistakes

- **"Metal atoms" in the lattice.** They are **positive ions**.
- **"Free ions carry the current."** In a metal, electrons carry the current; the ions stay
  in the lattice.
- **"Electrons are shared between atoms."** That describes covalent bonding.
- **"The metallic bonds break when the metal is bent."** The point of the explanation is
  that they do not.
- **Missing the word "attraction".** The bond is an electrostatic attraction, not the
  electrons themselves.
- **Writing "layers of electrons slide".** It is the layers of **ions** that slide.

## Exam technique: command words

- **Describe** metallic bonding: three parts — positive ions, giant lattice, sea of
  delocalised electrons — linked by "electrostatic attraction".
- **Explain** conductivity: "delocalised electrons" + "free to move" + "carry charge".
- **Explain** malleability or ductility: "layers of ions" + "slide" + "attraction
  maintained / bonding not broken".
- **Suggest** a use: pick the property the use needs, then give its structural reason.

## Quick checklist

- Exact syllabus description of metallic bonding
- Labelled diagram: regular positive ions, scattered delocalised electrons
- Conductivity: delocalised electrons move and carry charge
- Malleability and ductility: layers slide, electrons move with them, bonding maintained

## Frequently asked questions

**Do Core candidates need to know metallic bonding?**
No. Subtopic 2.7 has no Core outcomes. Core candidates learn the physical properties of
metals in Topic 9 but are not asked to explain them with this model.

**Is "sea of electrons" enough, or must I say "delocalised"?**
Use both: "a sea of delocalised electrons" is the syllabus phrase, and "delocalised" is the
word that explains why the electrons can move.

**Why do metals conduct when solid but ionic compounds do not?**
In a metal the charge carriers are electrons, which are free to move in the solid. In an
ionic compound the carriers are ions, which are fixed in the solid lattice.
