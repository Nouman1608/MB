## Questions

**1.** Name the most suitable instrument, and give its resolution, for measuring (i) the diameter of a wire of about 0.60 mm, (ii) the diameter of a steel ball of about 12 mm, (iii) the height from which a ball is dropped, about 1.5 m. **[3]**

**2.** Vernier calipers show 27 mm on the main scale, with the fourth Vernier mark aligned with a main-scale mark. State the reading, the uncertainty in this single reading and the percentage uncertainty. **[3]**

**3.** A micrometer is closed on nothing and reads -0.03 mm. It is then used on a wire. The sleeve shows 0.5 mm and the thimble line at 38.

**(a)** State the uncorrected reading. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the diameter of the wire. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why the answer is given to two decimal places. **[1]**

**4.** A voltmeter reads 0.04 V when disconnected from any circuit.

**(a)** State the type of error and why repeating does not remove it. **[1]**
**(b)** Correct the readings 0.52 V and 0.96 V. **[1]**
**(c)** Find the percentage of the first reading that the error represents. **[1]**

**5.** A student times a ball falling 0.80 m through oil. The times are 2.41 s, 2.38 s, 2.44 s, 3.10 s and 2.40 s.

**(a)** Identify the anomalous reading and suggest a cause. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the mean of the remaining readings. **[1]**
**(c)** Give the uncertainty in the mean as half the range, and the percentage uncertainty. **[2]**

**6.** A long steel wire is stretched with slotted masses of up to 8 kg to find the Young modulus. Identify two hazards and a precaution for each. **[4]**

**7.** In a falling-ball experiment the time for a ball to fall through glycerol is measured at different temperatures.

**(a)** State three variables to control. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain why temperature is the independent variable and not just a control here. **[1]**

**8.** A student loads a spring and records these results. Masses: 50 g, 100 g, 150 g and 200 g. Extensions: 1.0 cm, 2.1 cm, 3.0 cm and 4.05 cm. A ruler marked in mm is used.

**(a)** Comment on the number of readings. **[2]**
**(b)** Comment on the range of the loads. **[2]**
**(c)** Comment on the significant figures. **[2]**

**9.** A student measures the potential difference V across a cell for different currents I. Currents: 0.10, 0.20, 0.30, 0.40 and 0.50 A. Potential differences: 1.44, 1.38, 1.32, 1.62 and 1.20 V.

**(a)** Use the points at 0.10 A and 0.50 A to find the gradient, then predict V at 0.40 A. **[2]**
**(b)** Comment on the recorded value at 0.40 A. **[1]**
**(c)** Suggest two checks to make before deciding what to do with the point. **[2]**

**10.** A student measures the extension of a hanging spring by holding a metre rule beside it. Suggest two improvements, each with the reason it helps. **[4]**

**11.** Plan an experiment to determine the acceleration of free fall using a steel ball, an electromagnet release and an electronic timer. Describe the apparatus and procedure, how you will control the variables, how you will deal with a hazard, how you will use the data, and one source of systematic error. Comment briefly on one benefit or risk of the physics in a wider context. **[10]**

**12.** A student plans to find the resistivity of a constantan wire of diameter about 0.45 mm. She measures the diameter once with a ruler marked in mm, uses a single length of 50 cm, leaves the current on throughout, and records the ammeter reading as 1 A.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage uncertainty in the diameter using half the ruler's resolution, and compare it with a micrometer measurement (resolution 0.01 mm). **[3]**
**(b)** Comment on the single length. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain the problem with leaving the current on. **[2]**
**(d)** Comment on the ammeter reading. **[1]**
**(e)** Describe how she should use the data. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** (i) Micrometer screw gauge, 0.01 mm [1] (ii) Vernier calipers, 0.1 mm [1] (iii) metre rule (or tape), 1 mm [1] **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* answers that give the instrument and its resolution together are easier to credit than the instrument name alone.

**2.** Reading = 27 + 4 × 0.1 = **27.4 mm** [1]. Uncertainty = half the resolution = **0.05 mm** [1]. Percentage = 0.05 ÷ 27.4 × 100% = **0.18%** [1] **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* write the percentage to one or two significant figures; a long string of digits adds nothing.

**3.** **(a)** 0.5 + 0.38 = **0.88 mm** [1]
**(b)** The zero error is negative, so the true diameter is larger [1]: 0.88 + 0.03 = **0.91 mm** [1]
**(c)** The micrometer resolves 0.01 mm, so readings are recorded to the nearest 0.01 mm [1] **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* the direction of the correction is the usual slip; say whether the error is positive or negative before correcting.

**4.** **(a)** Systematic, because every reading is shifted by the same amount [1]
**(b)** 0.52 − 0.04 = **0.48 V** and 0.96 − 0.04 = **0.92 V** [1]
**(c)** 0.04 ÷ 0.52 × 100% = **7.7%** [1] **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* name the type of error and give the reason in the same sentence, since "systematic" alone is a label rather than an explanation.

**5.** **(a)** 3.10 s is far from the others [1]; the stopwatch may have been started or stopped late (reaction time), or the ball touched the side [1]
**(b)** Mean = (2.41 + 2.38 + 2.44 + 2.40) ÷ 4 = **2.41 s** [1]
**(c)** Half range = (2.44 − 2.38) ÷ 2 = **0.03 s** [1]; percentage = 0.03 ÷ 2.41 × 100% = **1.2%** [1] **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* give a reason for dropping a reading, and make clear which readings the mean uses.

**6.** Hazard 1: the wire may snap and whip [1]; precaution: goggles and a cloth over the wire, or keep to loads within the limit [1]. Hazard 2: heavy masses may fall on feet [1]; precaution: a sand tray under the masses and feet kept clear [1] **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* each hazard is better paired with its own precaution than listed together.

**7.** **(a)** Any three of: the same ball [1]; the same liquid sample and depth [1]; the same distance timed [1] (any other control linked to fall time also earns credit)
**(b)** The viscosity of glycerol changes with temperature, so it affects the fall time and is the variable being studied [1] **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* controls need to be linked to the effect on the fall time, not named alone.

**8.** **(a)** Only four readings [1]; too few to see the pattern or judge whether the last point is anomalous, so use at least six [1]
**(b)** The range is small, 50 g to 200 g [1]; a wider range (until near the limit of proportionality) would fix the gradient better [1]
**(c)** Extensions should have the same number of decimal places as the ruler's resolution [1]; 4.05 cm cannot be read on a mm ruler, so should be 4.0 or 4.1 cm [1] **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* tie each comment to the data, such as the value 4.05 cm, and say what would improve it.

**9.** **(a)** Gradient = (1.20 − 1.44) ÷ 0.40 = **-0.60 V A⁻¹** [1]; at 0.40 A the line gives 1.44 − 0.60 × 0.30 = **1.26 V** [1]
**(b)** The recorded 1.62 V is 0.36 V above the line, probably the digits of 1.26 transposed [1]
**(c)** Re-read the meters and repeat the reading [1]; check the connections and the calculation for slips [1] **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* checking and repeating usually comes before excluding, and the check should be named.

**10.** Use a set square against the ruler to make sure it is vertical [1], as a tilted rule overestimates the extension [1]. Put a pointer or marker on the spring and read at eye level [1] to avoid parallax [1] **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* "be more careful" is not credited; the improvement should name apparatus and say what it fixes.

**11.** Apparatus: electromagnet and clamp, steel ball, trapdoor or light gate, timer, metre rule or tape, stand [1]. Measure height h from the base of the ball to the trapdoor, with the rule checked vertical [1]. Release by switching off the magnet so the timer starts, and stop it at impact [1]. Repeat each height three times and average the times [1]. Use at least six heights over a wide range [1]. Hazard: falling ball and stand, so use a clamped stand with a soft landing or a tray [1]. Data: plot h against t²; since h = ½gt², the gradient = g ÷ 2 [1] and g = 2 × gradient [1]. Systematic error: residual magnetism delays release, so every measured time is too large and g is underestimated; fit a non-magnetic spacer or use a light gate release to reduce it [1]. Context: accurate g values help in geophysical surveys that locate dense rocks, but fieldwork has energy costs [1] **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* lists of apparatus alone gain little; credit builds up when each stage says how the quantity is measured and how it is used.

**12.** **(a)** Ruler uncertainty = 0.5 mm [1]; percentage = 0.5 ÷ 0.45 × 100% = **111%** [1]; micrometer: 0.005 ÷ 0.45 × 100% = **1.1%**, so it is about one hundred times smaller [1]
**(b)** One length gives no trend; take several lengths and plot R against l [1]
**(c)** The wire heats, so its resistivity changes, and readings drift [1]; use a low current and switch off between readings [1]
**(d)** One significant figure is too coarse; record to the meter's resolution, for example 0.52 A [1]
**(e)** Plot R against l; the gradient equals ρ ÷ A [1]; ρ = gradient × (π × d² ÷ 4) [1] **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* working out the percentage uncertainty makes the case for the micrometer far more persuasive than the statement that it is better.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Giving an instrument name with no resolution when the question asks for both.
- Missing the sign of a zero error and correcting the wrong way.
- Calling a fixed offset random, or saying repeating removes it.
- Quoting readings to more decimal places than the instrument can show.
- Listing hazards with no precaution paired to each one.
- Describing controls without saying why they affect the result.
- Naming an improvement without the apparatus, or "more accurate" without the instrument.
- Describing a graph without saying what the gradient represents.
- Removing an anomalous point without a check.
