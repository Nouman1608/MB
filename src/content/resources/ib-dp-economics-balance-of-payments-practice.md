## Questions

**1.** Distinguish between a credit item and a debit item in the balance of payments, giving one example of each. **[3]**

**2.** The fictional country Orvane has these figures, in billions of orvs. Goods: exports 310, imports 285. Services: exports 70, imports 96. Income: credits 40, debits 55. Current transfers: credits 22, debits 12.

**(a)** Calculate the balance on goods, services, income and current transfers. **[4]**
**(b)** Calculate the current account balance. **[1]**

**3.** State the account and item, and whether each is a credit or a debit for Orvane. **[4]**

- A foreign company builds a factory in Orvane.
- Orvanians working abroad send money to their families at home.
- Orvane's central bank buys foreign currency, adding to its reserves.
- An Orvane resident pays a foreign hotel for a holiday.

**4.** Orvane has a current account balance of -6 and a capital account balance of +2. Net FDI is +7, net portfolio investment is -5 and net official borrowing is +3 (billions of orvs).

**(a)** Calculate the balance on the financial account. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the balance on reserve assets. **[2]**
**(c)** State whether reserves rose or fell. **[1]**

**5.** Explain, with a numerical example, why the balance of payments as a whole sums to zero. **[4]**

**6.** (HL only) Orvane's currency, the orv, is held at a rate above its equilibrium rate and the country has a current account deficit. Draw a currency market diagram and explain the link between the deficit and the exchange rate. **[5]**

**7.** (HL only) Explain how a rise in Orvane's interest rates might change the financial account, the exchange rate and the current account. **[4]**

**8.** (HL only) The fictional country Kelmar has exports of 250 and imports of 280 (billions of kels). The kel depreciates by 5%. Assume export prices in kels and import prices in foreign currency do not change.

**(a)** State the Marshall-Lerner condition. **[1]**
**(b)** The price elasticity of demand for exports is 0.8 and for imports is 0.5. Show that the condition is met. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the new current account balance. **[3]**
**(d)** In the short run the elasticities are 0.3 and 0.2. Calculate the new balance and explain what this shows about the J-curve. **[2]**

**9.** (HL only) Discuss expenditure switching and expenditure reducing as methods of correcting a persistent current account deficit. **[8]**

**10.** (HL only) Explain two implications of a persistent current account surplus. **[4]**

**11.** (HL only) Kelmar has run a current account deficit of 30 a year for ten years. GDP is 600. Most of the deficit is financed by inflows of portfolio investment.

**(a)** Calculate the deficit as a percentage of GDP. **[1]**
**(b)** Evaluate the implications of this persistent deficit. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** A credit item brings money into the country, for example an export sale [1]. A debit item sends money out of the country, for example an import purchase [1]. The difference is the direction of the money flow: in for credits, out for debits [1].
*Examiner insight:* "Distinguish" asks for a difference, so state both definitions clearly before the examples.

**2. (a)** Goods = 310 - 285 = **+25** [1]. Services = 70 - 96 = **-26** [1]. Income = 40 - 55 = **-15** [1]. Current transfers = 22 - 12 = **+10** [1].
**(b)** 25 - 26 - 15 + 10 = **-6**, a deficit of 6 billion orvs [1].
*Examiner insight:* Showing each balance as credits minus debits makes the working easy to follow, and the sign carries the surplus or deficit.

**3.** Financial account, FDI, credit [1]. Current account, current transfers, credit [1]. Financial account, reserve assets, debit [1]. Current account, trade in services, debit [1].
*Examiner insight:* Name the account and the item as well as the direction, because the question asks for all three.

**4. (a)** Financial account = -(-6 + 2) [1] = **+4** [1].
**(b)** Reserve assets = 4 - (7 - 5 + 3) [1] = 4 - 5 = **-1** [1].
**(c)** A debit means reserves **rose** by 1 [1].
*Examiner insight:* Check the final answer against the zero-sum rule: all four financial items should add to the financial account.

**5.** Each transaction is recorded twice, once as a credit and once as a debit [1]. Example: Orvane exports goods worth 8, a credit on the current account [1]. The foreign buyer pays, so an Orvane bank gains a foreign deposit, an outflow of financial capital recorded as a debit of 8 on the financial account [1]. Credits equal debits, so the accounts sum to zero, and a deficit on one account is matched by a surplus on another [1].
*Examiner insight:* A numerical example is asked for, so give the amount and the account for both sides.

**6.** Axes: exchange rate on the vertical axis, quantity of orvs on the horizontal axis [1]. Downward-sloping demand (exports) and upward-sloping supply (imports), with the equilibrium labelled [1]. The held rate is marked above equilibrium [1]. At that rate supply exceeds demand, matching the deficit, and puts downward pressure on the orv [1]. If the rate floats, depreciation makes exports cheaper abroad and imports dearer, narrowing the deficit [1].
*Examiner insight:* A diagram earns more when each curve, axis and rate is labelled and the explanation refers to the labels.

**7.** Higher interest rates attract portfolio investment, raising the financial account surplus [1]. Foreign investors demand more orvs, so demand shifts right [1] and the orv appreciates [1]. Exports become dearer and imports cheaper, so the current account worsens [1].
*Examiner insight:* Follow the chain link by link and end with the current account, since the question names all three accounts.

**8. (a)** A depreciation improves the current account only if the sum of the price elasticities of demand for exports and imports is greater than 1 [1].
**(b)** 0.8 + 0.5 = 1.3, which is greater than 1 [1].
**(c)** Exports = 250 × (1 + 0.8 × 0.05) = 250 × 1.04 = **260** [1]. Imports = 280 × 1.05 × (1 - 0.5 × 0.05) = 280 × 1.05 × 0.975 = **286.65** [1]. Balance = 260 - 286.65 = **-26.65**, better than -30 [1].
**(d)** Exports = 250 × 1.015 = 253.75; imports = 280 × 1.05 × 0.99 = 291.06; balance = **-37.31** [1]. The deficit widens at first, then narrows as elasticities rise, which is the J-curve [1].
*Examiner insight:* Show how each volume change is applied to the price change, because a bare final figure gives no way to credit sound working.

**9.** Switching redirects spending to home output through tariffs, quotas, subsidies or depreciation [1]. It acts quickly on imports and exports [1]. Limits: retaliation by trading partners [1]; depreciation depends on elasticities (Marshall-Lerner) and raises import prices [1]. Reducing cuts demand through higher taxes, lower spending or higher interest rates [1]. It cuts imports directly [1] but lowers output, jobs and growth [1]. Judgement: the better method depends on the cause of the deficit, so switching suits a competitiveness problem if elasticities are high, and reducing suits a consumption boom [1].
*Examiner insight:* "Discuss" calls for both methods weighed against each other, so finish with a reasoned judgement tied to a cause.

**10.** Two implications, each explained, earn two each. Exchange rates: strong foreign demand for the currency pushes it up, which raises export prices abroad [2]. Consumption and investment: output sold abroad is not used at home, so consumption and investment may be lower than they could be [2]. Other valid answers: inflation (export demand adds to aggregate demand) and employment (export jobs, but appreciation can cost some of them).
*Examiner insight:* Naming an implication is not enough, so add one step of reasoning to each.

**11. (a)** 30 / 600 = **5.0%** [1].
**(b)** Exchange rate: persistent deficit pressure may weaken the currency, but portfolio inflows can support it for now [1]. Interest rates may have to stay high to attract inflows, raising borrowing costs [1]. Portfolio inflows can reverse quickly if confidence falls, causing a sharp depreciation [1]. Ten deficits of about 30 add to external liabilities, so debt and interest payments grow [1]. Foreign ownership of assets rises and income flows out [1]. A credit rating agency may judge the country riskier, raising future borrowing costs [1]. Demand management is constrained, since faster growth pulls in imports [1]. If the imports are capital goods, growth may rise, but if they are consumer goods it does not [1]. Judgement: 5.0% for ten years financed by short-term flows is a risk, and the verdict depends on what the imports funded [1].
*Examiner insight:* Evaluation improves when you use the case details (size, length, financing) to decide how serious the deficit is.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "deficit in the balance of payments" without naming an account.
- Reversing credits and debits, so balances carry the wrong sign.
- Taking the financial account as equal to the current account instead of minus the current plus capital accounts.
- Treating a rise in reserves as a credit.
- Not showing working in calculations with elasticities and depreciation.
- Drawing a currency diagram without labelled axes, curves or equilibrium.
- Saying depreciation always cures a deficit, without elasticities or time.
- Listing implications of a deficit with no judgement about size, cause or financing.
