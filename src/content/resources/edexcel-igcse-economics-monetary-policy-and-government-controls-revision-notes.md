## Key definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Monetary policy | Changes to interest rates (and related tools) by the central bank to influence the economy |
| Interest rate | The price of borrowing money and the reward for saving, as a percentage |
| Policy (base) rate | The rate set by the central bank; lenders build their own rates on it |
| Central bank | The public body that sets the policy rate, usually aiming at an inflation target |
| Transmission mechanism | The route from a rate change to spending, output, jobs and prices |
| Asset purchasing | The central bank creates money and buys assets, mainly government bonds |
| Yield | The return on a bond, equal to its yearly income divided by its price |
| Regulation | Rules on how firms must behave, enforced by a public body |
| Legislation | Laws that make acts legal or illegal |
| Fine | A money penalty for breaking a law or rule |
| Pollution permit | A (usually tradable) right to release a set amount of pollution |

## Central bank decisions

- Inflation above target or the economy overheating: raise the rate.
- Inflation below target or the economy weak: cut the rate.
- Independence: less pressure from elections (advantage), but unelected officials decide (disadvantage).

## Transmission mechanism

### A rate rise, step by step

1. Borrowing costs rise and saving pays more.
2. Consumers spend less; firms invest less.
3. Currency tends to rise, so exports fall and imports rise.
4. Aggregate demand (AD) falls.
5. Inflation slows, growth slows, unemployment may rise.

A rate cut runs the other way: AD rises, growth rises, unemployment falls, and inflation may rise.

### Consumers and businesses

| Group | Rate rises | Rate falls |
|---|---|---|
| Borrowers (consumers) | Pay more, spend less | Pay less, spend more |
| Savers | Earn more, may save more | Earn less, may spend more |
| Firms | Borrowing dearer, less investment, higher costs | Borrowing cheaper, more investment, lower costs |

### Effects on objectives

| Objective | Rate rise | Rate cut |
|---|---|---|
| Inflation | Falls | May rise |
| Growth | Slows | Rises |
| Unemployment | May rise | May fall |
| Current account | Tends to worsen (stronger currency) | Tends to improve (weaker currency) |

## Method reminders with numbers

### Extra interest on a variable-rate loan

Extra interest = loan x change in rate. A loan of 60,000 with the rate up from 5% to 7%: 60,000 x 0.02 = 1,200 a year, or 100 a month.

### Investment decision

Compare yearly profit before interest with interest cost. A loan of 40,000 for a project earning 2,600 a year:

```
Rate 4%: interest 1,600, net +1,000  -> go ahead
Rate 8%: interest 3,200, net -600    -> do not go ahead
```

### Inflation rate

Inflation = (new CPI - old CPI) / old CPI x 100. CPI 140 to 144.2: (4.2 / 140) x 100 = 3.0%.

### Real interest rate

Roughly nominal rate minus inflation. A rate of 5% with inflation of 3% is about 2%. With inflation of 6%, it is about −1%.

### Exchange rate and prices

```
Currency rises: 1 A-unit = 5.00 B-units -> 5.50 B-units
Export priced 40 A-units: 200 B-units -> 220 B-units (up 10%)
Import priced 110 B-units: 22 A-units -> 20 A-units
```

Foreign price of exports = home price x exchange rate. Home price of imports = foreign price / exchange rate.

### Bond yield

Yield = yearly income / price x 100. A bond paying 3 a year at a price of 100 yields 3%. When its price rises to 120, it yields 3 / 120 = 2.5%. Buying bonds raises prices and so lowers yields.

## Asset purchasing: what to know

- Used when the policy rate is already very low.
- New money buys bonds, which raises bond prices, lowers yields, and gives banks more to lend.
- It aims to lower borrowing costs and lift spending.
- Risks: inflation if too much money is created; higher prices for shares and property, which benefit existing owners; hard to reverse.

## Limits of monetary policy

- Time lags before spending changes.
- Weak confidence: people may not borrow even at low rates.
- Cost-push inflation is not cured by dearer borrowing.
- Fixed-rate borrowers respond slowly.
- Fighting inflation can raise unemployment; cutting rates can raise inflation.

## Government controls: advantages and disadvantages

| Control | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|
| Regulation | Clear standards; stops harm directly; can be updated | Compliance costs, heavier for small firms; inspection costs; one rule for all |
| Legislation | Covers everyone; legal force; can ban the worst harm | Slow to pass; policing is costly; black markets |
| Fines | Make harm costly; raise revenue | Work only if caught; may be treated as a cost of business |
| Pollution permits | Known total pollution; cheapest cuts happen first; incentive to innovate | Cap may be too high; monitoring needed; large firms may buy up permits |

### Fines: the deterrence test

Expected fine = chance of being caught x fine. A firm saves 50,000 by dumping; chance of capture 20%; fine 150,000. Expected fine = 0.2 x 150,000 = 30,000. This is less than 50,000, so the fine does not deter. To deter, the fine must exceed 50,000 / 0.2 = 250,000.

### Permits: the trading test

A firm must emit 60 tonnes less than it does now. Cutting costs 40 a tonne (2,400). Buying 60 permits at 25 each costs 1,500. Buying is cheaper by 900, so the firm buys. The firm with the lowest cutting cost sells.

## Building an evaluation

For an extended answer, make a point, develop it with "so" links, then weigh it.

- **Chain:** rate rises, so a variable-rate mortgage costs more, so disposable income falls, so consumption falls, so AD falls, so demand-pull inflation eases.
- **Judgement words:** it depends on the cause of inflation, how much of the debt is variable, how long the time lag is, and how confident people are.
- **Short run versus long run:** higher rates may cut inflation quickly but raise unemployment for a while.
- **Conclusion:** choose a view and give the main reason, for example "raising rates is more effective for demand-pull inflation than for cost-push".
- **Controls:** compare two. Permits suit a cap on total pollution; fines suit deterrence only if detection is likely; a ban in law suits the most dangerous acts.

## Must-know distinctions

- Interest rate change versus asset purchasing: the first changes the price of borrowing directly; the second works by buying bonds when rates cannot fall much more.
- Regulation versus legislation: regulation is rules on conduct; legislation is law. Both need enforcement.
- Fine versus permit: a fine punishes after the act; a permit sets a limit before it.
- Nominal versus real interest rate: real subtracts inflation.

## Quick self-test

1. Define monetary policy.
2. A loan of 100,000 has its rate cut from 6% to 4%. What is the change in yearly interest?
3. State two ways a rate rise reduces consumer spending.
4. Why might a rate cut raise inflation?
5. A firm borrows 50,000 at 6%. The project earns 2,500 a year before interest. Should it go ahead?
6. Explain why a higher rate can raise the exchange rate.
7. Give one reason asset purchasing is used.
8. Give one disadvantage of asset purchasing.
9. State one advantage and one disadvantage of pollution permits.
10. A firm saves 20,000 by breaking a rule. The chance of capture is 10% and the fine is 100,000. Does the fine deter?

### Answers

1. The central bank's use of interest rate changes (and related tools) to influence the economy.
2. 100,000 x 0.02 = 2,000 less a year.
3. Variable loans and mortgages cost more, and saving becomes more rewarding.
4. Cheaper borrowing raises AD. If output cannot rise, firms raise prices.
5. Interest = 3,000, which is more than 2,500, so the net result is −500. No.
6. Foreign savers buy the currency to earn the better return, so demand for it rises.
7. Rates are already very low, so cutting them further is not possible.
8. It may cause inflation, or raise asset prices for owners.
9. Advantage: total pollution is capped. Disadvantage: the cap may be set too high.
10. Expected fine = 0.1 x 100,000 = 10,000, less than 20,000. It does not deter.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing the effect of a rate rise without saying which way spending, AD or inflation moves.
- Missing the link between interest rates and the exchange rate when asked about the current account.
- Leaving out the "so" steps in a chain of reasoning.
- Treating a lower rate as always good, with no mention of inflation.
- Confusing a lower rate with asset purchasing.
- Giving only advantages of a control, with no disadvantage or judgement.
- Dropping the units or the time period (a year or a month) in a calculation.
- Judging a policy without saying what it depends on, such as the cause of inflation.
