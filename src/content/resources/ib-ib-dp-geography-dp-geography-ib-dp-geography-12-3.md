This guide goes deeper into sub-topic 12.3 of Unit 5, Human development and diversity, part of the Higher Level extension of IB Diploma Programme Geography (first assessments 2019), examined in Paper 3. The [12.3 revision notes](/resources/ib-ib-dp-geography-local-responses-to-global-interactions-revision-notes/) already classify local responses, define key terms, and give examples including Plachimada, Standing Rock, geographical indications, Bhutan's tourism policy, Kuapa Kokoo and Transition Towns. This page adds a way of placing responses on a spectrum, two further located cases, an explanation of why responses are often contested within a community, and a method for scoring responses against criteria. Practise with the [12.3 practice questions](/resources/ib-ib-dp-geography-dp-geography-ib-dp-geography-12-3-practice/); the [Human development and diversity study guide](/resources/ib-ib-dp-geography-human-development-diversity-study-guide/) gives the unit overview.

## What this sub-topic asks you to do

- Explain the different ways local communities respond to global interactions.
- Examine why responses vary between places and between groups within a place.
- Evaluate the success of local responses, using located examples.
- Link each response back to the global force that prompted it.

## A spectrum of responses

Rather than sorting responses into fixed boxes, place them on a spectrum from embracing global interaction to rejecting it.

| Position | Response | Typical form |
|---|---|---|
| Embrace | Active adoption | Using global markets, platforms or investment to raise incomes |
| Shape | Negotiation | Accepting investment on conditions: local jobs, environmental standards, benefit-sharing |
| Filter | Selective protection | Accepting some flows while limiting others, such as visitor caps or local-content rules |
| Resist | Opposition | Protest, legal action and boycotts aimed at stopping a project or practice |

A community may move along the spectrum over time, for example from acceptance to resistance as costs become clear, and different groups in one place may occupy different positions. Placing a case on the spectrum, and explaining why it sits there, gives an answer structure.

## Resistance against a transnational oil company: Ogoniland, Nigeria

Oil extraction in the Niger Delta brought revenue to the Nigerian state and to multinational companies, while local communities experienced oil spills, gas flaring and damage to farmland and fishing waters. In Ogoniland, the Movement for the Survival of the Ogoni People, led by the writer Ken Saro-Wiwa, campaigned from the early 1990s for compensation, environmental clean-up and a share of oil revenue. Shell withdrew its operations from Ogoniland in 1993. In 1995 Saro-Wiwa and eight other Ogoni activists were executed by the military government after a trial that was widely condemned internationally.

The case shows resistance achieving part of its aim, stopping operations locally, at terrible human cost, under a state whose interests were tied to oil revenue. It also shows a local campaign gaining global attention through international NGOs and media, a link back to Unit 4's networks.

## Filtering a global flow: cities managing tourism

Global tourism has grown with cheaper flights and online booking platforms, bringing income to historic cities but also crowding, rising rents and the loss of residents. Some local governments have responded by filtering the flow rather than rejecting it.

- **Venice** introduced an entry fee for day-trippers on selected days in 2024, aiming to reduce peak crowding by the visitors who spend least time and money in the city.
- **Barcelona** has restricted licences for new hotels and tourist apartments in its most visited districts, responding to residents' concerns about housing and congestion.

These are local responses to a global force, but they raise evaluative questions: do fees reduce visitor numbers or only raise revenue? Do restrictions protect residents or push tourism into other neighbourhoods? Whose voices shaped the policy: residents, businesses or visitors?

## Responses through rights: free, prior and informed consent

Some communities respond to global investment by asserting legal rights. The UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples, adopted in 2007, sets out the principle of **free, prior and informed consent**: indigenous communities should be consulted, given full information and able to agree or refuse before projects affect their lands. In practice its strength depends on whether national governments write it into law and courts enforce it. It moves a local response from protest towards negotiation, because it gives communities a formal place in decisions rather than leaving them to react afterwards.

## Why responses are contested within a place

A community is rarely united. Different groups gain or lose from the same global interaction:

- **Workers** employed by a foreign firm may support it, while **neighbours** bearing pollution oppose it.
- **Property owners** renting to tourists gain, while **tenants** facing higher rents lose.
- **Young people** may welcome global culture and jobs, while **older residents** seek to protect traditions.
- **Local politicians** may prioritise investment and tax revenue over campaigners' concerns.

The term **NIMBY** ("not in my back yard") is sometimes used for residents who accept a facility in general but oppose it locally. Use it carefully: it can dismiss genuine concerns about who bears costs. A strong answer names the groups, explains their positions and shows how power between them shaped the outcome.

## Worked example: scoring two responses

Two invented responses are scored from 1 (weak) to 5 (strong) against four criteria.

```
Criterion                     Response A: protest      Response B: negotiated
                              against a mine           benefit-sharing deal
Achieved its stated aim              4                         3
Benefits spread widely               2                         4
Lasting over time                    3                         4
Avoided harm to participants         2                         5
Total (out of 20)                   11                        16
```

Response B scores higher overall, though Response A did more to achieve its immediate aim. Change the weighting, for example by counting "achieved its stated aim" twice, and the totals become 15 and 19, so B still leads, but the gap narrows from 5 to 4 points. The value of the method is that it makes judgements explicit; its weakness is that the scores are subjective, so you should justify each one with evidence when you use the method in an essay.

## Annotated model answer

*Evaluate the success of one local response to global interactions that you have studied.* **(sample paragraph)**

"In Ogoniland, the campaign led by Ken Saro-Wiwa achieved one aim when Shell withdrew its operations in 1993 **[success against a stated aim]**. It also drew international attention to oil pollution in the Niger Delta **[wider impact through global networks]**. However, the cost was extreme: Saro-Wiwa and eight other activists were executed in 1995, and environmental damage remained long after operations ceased **[limits and human cost]**. The response was therefore successful in halting local extraction but not in securing clean-up or a share of revenue, showing that local resistance has limited power when state and corporate interests align **[judgement linked to power]**."

## Common mistakes

- Describing a response without stating the global interaction it responds to.
- Treating a community as having a single view.
- Judging success by one criterion, such as whether a project was stopped.
- Using NIMBY as a label instead of analysing who bears costs.
- Choosing examples that are national policy when the question asks about local responses.

## Exam technique

- **Evaluate** a response against stated criteria: aims, distribution of benefits, durability and costs.
- **Examine** why responses vary by comparing power, dependence, legal rights and connection to wider networks.
- **Discuss** needs the views of at least two groups within a place.
- **To what extent** answers on local power need a position on how far local action can change global forces.
- Keep one detailed resistance case and one detailed filtering or negotiating case, with dates.
