Condensed notes for sub-topic 1.3 of Cambridge O Level Geography (2217), 2026 series.

## Reading a pyramid

1. **Check the axis**: numbers or percentages?
2. **Overall shape**: wide base (young) or straight sides and wide top (ageing)?
3. **Irregularities**: bulges and notches, and male–female differences.
4. **Cause**: birth rate, death rate, life expectancy or migration.
5. **Consequence**: what it means for services, jobs and government spending.

| Feature | Indicates |
|---|---|
| Wide base | High birth rate |
| Sides narrow quickly | High death rate, lower life expectancy |
| Narrow base | Low or falling birth rate |
| Wide top | Long life expectancy, ageing |
| Bulge of working-age males | Labour immigration (e.g. UAE) |
| "Waist" of young adults | Emigration from a source area |
| More elderly women than men | Women's longer life expectancy |

## Structures by development level

- **Low income**: wide base, concave sides. Example: **Niger**, very young population.
- **Middle income**: narrowing base, bulge of young adults (possible demographic dividend).
- **High income**: straight sides, wide top. Example: **Japan**, more than a quarter aged 65+,
  population shrinking.

## Formulas

```
dependency ratio   = (0-14 + 65+) / (15-64) x 100
youth dependency   = (0-14) / (15-64) x 100
old-age dependency = (65+) / (15-64) x 100
```

Divide by the **15–64 group**, not the whole population.

## Impacts

**Youthful**
- Pressure on schools, child health care, vaccination.
- Future need for jobs and housing; unemployment or emigration risk.
- Population momentum: growth continues as many reach childbearing age.

**Ageing**
- Pension, health and care costs rise; fewer taxpayers.
- Labour shortages, especially care and manual work.
- Positives: grandparent childcare, volunteering, spending from savings.

**Responses to ageing:** raise retirement age, more women and older people in work, pro-natalist
incentives, immigration (Japan's new skilled-worker visa, 2019), technology and robotics.

## Worked example: describe, then explain

*Figure 1 is a population pyramid for Country A. Describe its shape and suggest reasons for it.*

Model answer: "The pyramid has a wide base, with about 8% of the population in each of the male
and female 0–4 bars, so the population is youthful. The sides narrow quickly after age 15 and
fewer than 2% of each sex are over 70, giving a concave, triangular shape. The wide base suggests a
high birth rate, perhaps because children are valued as farm labour and contraception is hard to
obtain. The rapid narrowing suggests a high death rate and low life expectancy, because health
care, clean water and sanitation are limited."

Why it scores: an overall statement first, figures read from the axis with the unit (%), and each
reason tied to a specific feature of the shape. Compare this with a list of bars, which earns
little.

## Comparing two pyramids

- Use comparative language: *wider base than*, *whereas*, *both*, *in contrast*.
- Compare the **same feature** in each: base, middle, top, then any irregularity.
- Quote one figure from each pyramid for every point you make.
- Finish with what the difference means, for example a higher youth dependency in one country and
  a higher old-age dependency in the other.

## Case study: a country with a high dependent population

**Pakistan: high youth dependency**
- Shape: broad base that is narrowing only slowly; a large share of people are under 15 and a
  small share are 65 and over.
- Reasons: fertility has fallen since the 1990s but remains well above replacement level,
  especially in rural areas; early marriage and lower female education in some regions; falling
  infant mortality means more children survive to adulthood.
- Implications: demand for school places, teachers and child health care is greater than supply,
  and large numbers of children are out of school; a big cohort enters the labour market every
  year, so job creation is a priority.
- Opportunity and risk: if young adults find productive work, the bulge can become a
  **demographic dividend**; if not, it brings unemployment and pressure to emigrate (link to 1.2,
  Pakistan to the Gulf states).
- Responses: family planning through community health workers (the Lady Health Worker
  Programme, begun in the 1990s), wider girls' education and skills training.

**Japan: high old-age dependency** (the global contrast): very low birth rate and one of the
longest life expectancies in the world, so the working-age group is shrinking while pension and
care costs rise; responses are listed above.

## Command words for 1.3

- **Describe**: shape, proportions and figures only, no reasons.
- **Explain / give reasons**: birth rate, death rate, life expectancy or migration, each linked to
  a feature.
- **Suggest**: a sensible reason for unfamiliar data; use tentative language such as "may" or
  "probably".
- **Implications**: consequences for people and government, ideally positive and negative.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Population structure | The make-up of a population by age and sex |
| Population pyramid | Back-to-back bar chart of age and sex groups |
| Cohort | A group of people born in the same period |
| Dependants | People aged under 15 and 65 and over |
| Economically active | People aged 15–64 who are of working age |
| Dependency ratio | Dependants per 100 people of working age |
| Ageing population | A rising proportion of people aged 65 and over |
| Population momentum | Continued growth because many young people reach childbearing age |
| Sex ratio | Number of males per 100 females |
| Youth dependency ratio | People aged 0–14 per 100 people aged 15–64 |
| Old-age dependency ratio | People aged 65 and over per 100 people aged 15–64 |
| Demographic dividend | Economic boost when a large working-age group has relatively few dependants |
| Replacement level | Fertility of about 2.1 children per woman, which keeps a population stable without migration |
| Life expectancy | Average number of years a newborn baby is expected to live |

## Exam traps

- Listing bars with no overall statement.
- Treating numbers as percentages.
- Missing migration as a cause.
- Wrong denominator in the dependency ratio.
- Ageing answers with problems only and no responses.
- Saying a pyramid "shows the birth rate": it shows structure, and rates are inferred from it.
- Treating every dependant as economically inactive: many people over 65 work, and in some
  low-income countries children work too, so the ratio is only an approximation.
- Calling a youthful population "bad" with no mention of a possible demographic dividend.

## Self-test

1. 0–14 = 30%, 15–64 = 60%, 65+ = 10%. Calculate the dependency ratio.
2. Using the same data, calculate youth and old-age dependency.
3. A pyramid has far more men than women aged 25–44. Suggest a cause.
4. Give two impacts of a youthful population.
5. Why might a country with a falling birth rate still be growing?
6. Why is the dependency ratio only an approximate measure of the burden on workers?
7. Give two implications of Pakistan's youthful population structure for its government.
8. What is a demographic dividend, and what is needed to achieve it?
9. A Gulf city's pyramid has a narrow 0–14 group but a very wide male 25–44 group. Suggest why.
10. Give two differences you would expect between the pyramids of Niger and Japan.
11. 65+ = 28% and 15–64 = 59%. Calculate the old-age dependency ratio.

**Answers:** 1. (30 + 10) ÷ 60 × 100 = **66.7**. 2. Youth 30 ÷ 60 × 100 = **50**; old-age
10 ÷ 60 × 100 = **16.7**. 3. Immigration of male labour migrants, for example to a Gulf state.
4. Any two: pressure on schools, demand for child health care, future need for jobs and housing,
possible future unemployment. 5. Population momentum: a large number of young people are entering
childbearing age, so total births remain high even if each family is smaller. 6. Some people aged
15–64 do not work (students, unemployed, people caring at home), while some under 15 or over 65 do
work; it also ignores unpaid work such as childcare. 7. Any two: building schools and training
teachers; child health care and vaccination; creating jobs for young people entering the labour
market; housing for future households. 8. A boost to economic growth when the working-age share is
large and dependants are relatively few; it needs jobs, education and good health care so that
young adults are productive. 9. Many male labour migrants come without their families, so there
are few children and far more men than women of working age. 10. Any two: Niger has a much wider
base; Japan has a much wider top; Niger's sides are concave while Japan's are nearly straight;
Japan has many more elderly women than men. 11. 28 ÷ 59 × 100 = **47.5**.
