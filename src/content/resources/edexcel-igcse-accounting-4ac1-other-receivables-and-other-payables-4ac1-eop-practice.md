These questions cover section 5.4, Other receivables and other payables, in Topic 5 of the Pearson Edexcel International GCSE in Accounting (4AC1) specification, Issue 1, October 2016. Most start from trial balance notes, the Paper 2 setting for Topic 5. Amounts are in dollars, written without a currency sign, and every business is invented. Teaching is in the [other receivables and other payables study guide](/resources/edexcel-igcse-accounting-4ac1-other-receivables-and-other-payables-4ac1-eop/) and the [other receivables and other payables revision notes](/resources/edexcel-igcse-accounting-4ac1-other-receivables-and-other-payables-4ac1-eop-revision-notes/).

## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** State whether each item is an other receivable or an other payable.
**(a)** Insurance paid in advance. **[1]**
**(b)** Rent received in advance from a tenant. **[1]**
**(c)** Commission earned but not yet received. **[1]**

**2.** Wages paid during the year were 26,400. At the start of the year 480 was owing, and at the end 550 is owing. Calculate the wages expense for the year. **[2]**

**3.** A business with a year end of 31 December pays insurance of 3,600 on 1 November for twelve months. Calculate the insurance expense for the year and the other receivable at the year end. **[3]**

**4.** Explain why it is necessary to account for other payables at the year end. **[2]**

**5.** Rent received in a trial balance is 7,800. It includes 650 for next year. State the rent received in the income statement and how the 650 is shown in the statement of financial position. **[2]**

**6.** Distinguish between other receivables and other payables. **[2]**

### Section B: structured questions

**7.** Calder Bakery has a year end of 31 December 2027. Its trial balance includes rent 13,200, heat and light 4,750, advertising 2,600 and interest received 480. Notes:
- Rent for December, 1,100, has not been paid.
- Heat and light of 420 is owing.
- Advertising includes 600 for a campaign in January 2028.
- Interest of 120 is due to the bakery but has not been received.

**(a)** Calculate the figure for each item in the income statement. **[4]**
**(b)** Calculate total other receivables and total other payables. **[2]**
**(c)** Profit before these adjustments was 22,300. Calculate the adjusted profit. **[2]**

**8.** Renwick Studio has a year end of 31 December 2027. On 1 January 2027 insurance of 720 was prepaid. On 1 April 2027 it paid 3,360 for twelve months' insurance.
**(a)** Calculate the insurance prepaid at 31 December 2027. **[2]**
**(b)** Prepare the insurance account for 2027, showing the balance brought down on 1 January 2028. **[5]**

**9.** Renwick Studio lets a storeroom for 400 a month. On 1 January 2027 the tenant owed the rent for December 2026. During 2027 the studio received 5,600. At 31 December 2027 the tenant had paid January 2028's rent in advance.
**(a)** Calculate the rent receivable for 2027. **[2]**
**(b)** Prepare the rent receivable account for 2027, showing the balance brought down on 1 January 2028. **[5]**

**10.** A trainee calculates the profit of Dunmere Florists as 30,450. These errors are found:
1. Wages owing of 760 were left out.
2. Insurance prepaid of 540 was added to the insurance expense instead of deducted.
3. Commission received of 300 for next year was included in this year's income.

**(a)** Calculate the corrected profit. **[5]**
**(b)** State the effect of error 1 on current liabilities. **[1]**

**11.** Before year-end adjustments, a business has current assets of 26,000 and current liabilities of 13,000. The adjustments create other receivables of 1,400 and other payables of 2,600.
**(a)** Calculate the current ratio after the adjustments, to two decimal places. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why the ratio has changed. **[2]**

**12.** The owner of Dunmere Florists says: "I only want expenses recorded when they are paid, because that is what the bank statement shows." Explain why the owner's approach would give misleading financial statements, and advise the owner. **[5]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** Other receivable [1].
**(b)** Other payable [1].
**(c)** Other receivable [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Ask whether the business owes or is owed; "in advance" does not always mean receivable.

**2.** 26,400 − 480 + 550 [1] = **26,470** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* The opening 480 was last year's cost, paid this year, so it comes off.

**3.** 3,600 ÷ 12 = 300 a month [1]. November and December are used: expense **600** [1]. Ten months prepaid: other receivable **3,000** [1]. **[3]**

**4.** Under the accruals concept, expenses belong to the period in which they are used, whether paid or not [1]; without other payables, expenses would be too low, profit too high, and liabilities the business really owes would be left out of the statement of financial position [1]. **[2]**

**5.** Rent received 7,800 − 650 = **7,150** [1]; 650 is shown as an other payable under current liabilities [1]. **[2]**

**6.** Other receivables are amounts owed to the business or paid by it in advance (not by trade customers), shown as current assets [1]; other payables are amounts the business owes (not to trade suppliers), including income received in advance, shown as current liabilities [1]. **[2]**

**7. (a)** Rent 13,200 + 1,100 = **14,300** [1]. Heat and light 4,750 + 420 = **5,170** [1]. Advertising 2,600 − 600 = **2,000** [1]. Interest received 480 + 120 = **600** [1].
**(b)** Other receivables 600 + 120 = **720** [1]. Other payables 1,100 + 420 = **1,520** [1].
**(c)** 22,300 − 1,100 − 420 + 600 + 120 [1] = **21,500** [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Write the sign of each adjustment; a list of bare figures hides the method.

**8. (a)** 3,360 ÷ 12 = 280 a month [1]. January to March 2028 is 3 months: 3 × 280 = **840** [1].
**(b)**

**Insurance**

| Debit | Amount | Credit | Amount |
|---|---:|---|---:|
| 1 Jan Balance b/d | 720 | 31 Dec Income statement | 3,240 |
| 1 Apr Bank | 3,360 | 31 Dec Balance c/d | 840 |
| | 4,080 | | 4,080 |
| 2028 1 Jan Balance b/d | 840 | | |

Opening balance 720 [1]; bank 3,360 [1]; balance c/d 840 [1]; transfer 3,240 [1]; balance b/d on the debit side [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* Check the transfer: old policy January to March 720, plus 9 × 280 = 2,520 for April to December, gives 3,240.

**9. (a)** Of the 5,600 received, 400 was for December 2026 and 400 is for January 2028 [1]. Rent receivable = 5,600 − 400 − 400 = **4,800**, which is 12 × 400 [1].
**(b)**

**Rent receivable**

| Debit | Amount | Credit | Amount |
|---|---:|---|---:|
| 1 Jan Balance b/d (owed) | 400 | Bank | 5,600 |
| 31 Dec Income statement | 4,800 | | |
| 31 Dec Balance c/d (in advance) | 400 | | |
| | 5,600 | | 5,600 |
| | | 2028 1 Jan Balance b/d (in advance) | 400 |

Opening debit balance [1]; bank 5,600 [1]; transfer 4,800 [1]; balance c/d on the debit side [1]; balance b/d on the credit side [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* The closing balance is a liability (an other payable), so it is brought down as a credit.

**10. (a)** Error 1: profit falls by 760 [1]. Error 2: the 540 was added when it should have been deducted [1], so expenses are 1,080 too high and profit rises by 1,080 [1]. Error 3: profit falls by 300 [1]. Corrected profit = 30,450 − 760 + 1,080 − 300 = **30,470** [1].
**(b)** Current liabilities (other payables) are understated by 760 [1]. **[6]**

**11. (a)** Current assets 26,000 + 1,400 = 27,400; current liabilities 13,000 + 2,600 = 15,600 [1]. Ratio 27,400 ÷ 15,600 = **1.76 : 1** [1].
**(b)** Other payables (2,600) increased current liabilities by more than other receivables (1,400) increased current assets [1], so the ratio fell from 2.00 : 1 to 1.76 : 1, showing less liquidity than the unadjusted figures suggested [1]. **[4]**

**12.** The accruals concept says expenses belong to the period in which they are used, not the period of payment [1]. If only payments are recorded, unpaid bills such as wages owing are left out, so expenses are too low and profit too high [1], while amounts paid for next year are wrongly charged this year, so profit can also be understated [1]. The statement of financial position would leave out other payables owed and other receivables paid in advance, so it would not show what the business owes and owns [1]. Advice: keep the bank records, but adjust for amounts owing and paid in advance at the year end so profit is fair and comparable [1]. **[5]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Adding a prepayment because the note says the expense "includes" it.
- Treating income received in advance as an other receivable.
- Forgetting the opening balance when calculating the year's figure.
- Using the whole payment instead of apportioning months.
- Bringing a balance down on the wrong side.
- Forgetting that an error of direction counts twice.
