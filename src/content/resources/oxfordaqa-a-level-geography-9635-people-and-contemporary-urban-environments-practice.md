## Questions

**1.** Define counter-urbanisation and give one reason why it happens. **[2]**

**2.** Explain the difference between a megacity and a world city. **[3]**

**3.** The table shows air temperatures along a transect across Velmora, a fictional city, at 22:00 on a calm, clear night.

| Site | Rural west | Outer suburb | Inner residential | CBD | City park | Rural east |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Temperature (°C) | 14.2 | 16.0 | 17.8 | 19.1 | 16.4 | 14.0 |

**(a)** Calculate the urban heat island intensity, using the highest urban temperature minus the mean of the two rural sites. Show your working. **[2]**
**(b)** Suggest why the city park is cooler than the CBD. **[3]**
**(c)** Explain why urban heat islands are usually strongest on calm, clear nights. **[4]**

**4.** The table shows household waste in two fictional cities: Harrowby, a high-income city, and Kasenda, a lower-income city.

| | Harrowby | Kasenda |
|---|---|---|
| Waste generated (kg per person per day) | 1.6 | 0.5 |
| Organic and food (%) | 28 | 58 |
| Paper and card (%) | 24 | 9 |
| Plastics (%) | 14 | 10 |
| Glass (%) | 7 | 2 |
| Metals (%) | 5 | 2 |
| Other (%) | 22 | 19 |

**(a)** Compare the waste of the two cities. **[4]**
**(b)** Suggest reasons for the differences, linked to economic characteristics and lifestyles. **[4]**

**5.** The table shows malaria cases in four districts of Nandoro, a fictional tropical city.

| District | Description | Dry season cases per 1,000 per month | Wet season cases per 1,000 per month | Households storing water in open containers (%) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Lagoon Edge | Informal, low-lying, beside wetland | 4.2 | 18.9 | 71 |
| Old Town | Dense, intermittent piped water | 2.8 | 9.8 | 64 |
| New Heights | Planned, high-income, on a ridge | 0.6 | 1.5 | 12 |
| Ring Road Estates | Peri-urban, beside irrigated plots | 3.5 | 14.0 | 48 |

**(a)** Calculate the ratio of wet season to dry season cases for Lagoon Edge. **[1]**
**(b)** Analyse the data to explain the variation in malaria between the districts. **[6]**

**6.** Outline how wind in a city centre with tall buildings differs from wind in the surrounding countryside. **[3]**

**7.** Read the description of Corrin Wharf, a fictional district of a fictional port city.

> Corrin Wharf was a dock district. Its warehouses closed when shipping moved to a new container port. Over ten years, warehouses have been converted into loft flats, galleries and cafés, and rents have tripled. At the waterfront, a new apartment complex has a single gated entrance, security guards and private gardens. Many long-term tenants have moved to cheaper districts.

Identify two types of new urban landscape shown in Corrin Wharf, and explain how each may affect social segregation. **[6]**

**8.** Outline one strategy for managing urban drainage problems and explain how it reduces flood risk. **[4]**

**9.** With reference to one named non-communicable disease, explain how its distribution within urban areas is linked to the physical and socio-economic environment. **[6]**

**10.** With reference to a specified urban area, assess the relative merits of incineration and landfill as approaches to waste disposal. **[9]**

**11.** "Truly sustainable cities are only possible in wealthy countries." With reference to two contrasting urban areas, to what extent do you agree? **[16]**

## Answers

**1.** Counter-urbanisation is the movement of people from cities to small towns and rural areas [1]. One reason: a wish for a quieter environment and cheaper housing, made possible by car ownership or remote working [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* the definition must show movement away from the urban area as a whole; moving to the suburbs is suburbanisation and scores nothing here.

**2.** A megacity is defined by size: more than 10 million people [1]. A world city is defined by function: a command role in the global economy through finance, headquarters or media [1]. The two can differ, as a city may be a world city with fewer than 10 million people, such as Singapore [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* the third mark needs the contrast made explicit, ideally with a real example of one without the other.

**3. (a)** Rural mean = (14.2 + 14.0) / 2 = 14.1 °C [1]. Intensity = 19.1 − 14.1 = 5.0 °C [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Vegetation and soil allow evapotranspiration, which uses energy and cools the air [1]. Grass and trees store less heat than concrete and tarmac [1]. There is less waste heat from traffic and buildings in the park than in the CBD [1]. **[3]**
**(c)** On clear nights, rural surfaces lose heat quickly by long-wave radiation to the sky [1]. Urban surfaces release heat stored during the day, so the city cools more slowly [1]. Tall buildings trap outgoing radiation between them [1]. In calm conditions, wind does not mix the warm urban air with cooler rural air, so the contrast stays high [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* in calculations, show each step; a correct final figure with no working can lose a mark if it is wrong by rounding.

**4. (a)** Harrowby generates 3.2 times as much waste per person as Kasenda [1]. Kasenda's waste is dominated by organic material, 58% compared with 28% [1]. Harrowby has a much higher share of paper and card, 24% compared with 9% [1]. Dry recyclables (paper, plastics, glass, metals) make up 50% of Harrowby's waste but only 23% of Kasenda's [1]. **[4]**
**(b)** Higher incomes in Harrowby mean more consumption of packaged goods, so more paper, plastic and glass [1]. More processed and convenience food produces packaging rather than food scraps [1]. In Kasenda, more food is bought fresh and cooked at home, so organic waste dominates [1]. In Kasenda, informal collectors may remove valuable items such as metals and plastics before formal collection [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* "compare" needs a direct link between the two cities in each point, with data from both sides.

**5. (a)** 18.9 ÷ 4.2 = 4.5 [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** All districts have more cases in the wet season, as rain creates standing water for *Anopheles* breeding [1]. Lagoon Edge has the highest wet-season rate, 18.9, because it is low-lying and beside wetland, so drainage is poor [1]. New Heights has the lowest, 1.5, less than a tenth of Lagoon Edge, as its ridge site drains well [1]. Open water storage shows a positive link with cases: 71% in Lagoon Edge against 12% in New Heights [1]. Old Town has high storage, 64%, but a lower wet-season rate than Ring Road Estates, so storage is not the only factor [1]. Ring Road Estates' irrigated plots provide breeding sites, and higher incomes in New Heights may allow screens and nets [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* analysis marks need figures quoted from the table and at least one anomaly, not a description of each row.

**6.** Average wind speed is lower in the city because the rough surface creates friction [1]. Between tall buildings, wind is funnelled along streets, so speeds can be higher locally (canyon effect) [1]. Tall buildings create eddies and sudden gusts, so direction is more variable [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* "outline" needs each difference stated with its cause in a few words; one cause for all three points earns one mark.

**7.** Gentrified area: warehouse conversions with rising rents and long-term tenants leaving [1]. This increases segregation by income, as lower-income households move to cheaper districts [1]. It can also change the cultural character of the area, so some groups no longer feel it is for them [1]. Fortress development: the gated waterfront complex with guards and private gardens [1]. This separates high-income residents physically from the rest of the district [1]. It reduces everyday contact between groups and turns public waterfront into private space [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* application marks need details from the extract (rents, gates, tenants leaving), not a textbook definition.

**8.** Strategy: sustainable urban drainage systems (SuDS), such as permeable paving and retention ponds [1]. Permeable surfaces let water infiltrate rather than run off [1]. Ponds and swales store water and release it slowly [1]. This lengthens lag time and lowers peak discharge in drains and rivers [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* link the strategy to a hydrological effect (lag time or peak discharge) to reach full marks.

**9.** Named disease: asthma, a long-term inflammatory condition of the airways [1]. Physical link: rates are higher near busy roads, where nitrogen dioxide and particulates are concentrated [1]. Physical link: calm conditions and temperature inversions trap pollution in valleys and street canyons [1]. Socio-economic link: deprived inner areas often have damp, mouldy housing, a known trigger [1]. Socio-economic link: lower-income households are more likely to live beside main roads, as housing there is cheaper [1]. Lifestyle link: smoking in the home increases risk, so rates can cluster where smoking is more common [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* a named disease is required; "respiratory illness" in general is too broad to gain the first mark.

**10.** This question is marked by levels of response in the real exam; the points below are indicative. Example: Singapore. Context: very little land, so landfill space is scarce [1]. Incineration cuts waste volume by up to 90%, according to the National Environment Agency [1]. Heat from incineration is recovered to generate electricity [1]. Residual ash still needs disposal, at the offshore Semakau Landfill [1]. Incineration is costly and needs emission controls to limit air pollution [1]. Landfill is cheaper to build and run [1]. But landfill produces methane and leachate and takes land Singapore lacks [1]. Semakau's space is finite, which pushes policy towards recycling and reuse of ash [1]. Judgement: incineration is better suited to Singapore because land scarcity outweighs its higher cost, but neither method reduces waste at source [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* "assess" needs a judgement tied to the named place's conditions; generic pros and cons without a city cap the answer low.

**11.** This question is marked by levels of response in the real exam; the points below are indicative creditworthy content for a balanced answer comparing, for example, Singapore and Lagos.

Introduction: define sustainability across natural, physical, social and economic dimensions [1]. Set the criterion: whether a city can meet present needs without harming future ones [1].

Agree: wealth funds infrastructure. Singapore can afford incineration with energy recovery [1] and road pricing since 1998 linked to strong public transport [1]. High-income states can plan land use because they control land, as in Singapore's public housing [1]. In Lagos, rapid informal growth has outpaced planning, so waste goes to landfill such as Olusosun [1]. Flooding and drainage failures show the physical dimension under stress [1].

Disagree: wealthy cities often have the largest ecological footprints, drawing resources from far beyond their area [1]. So wealth can make a city liveable but less sustainable globally [1]. Lower-income cities have strengths: high public transport use and recovery of materials by informal recyclers [1]. Lagos has invested in bus rapid transit from 2008 and the Blue Line from 2023 [1]. Social sustainability can be weak in wealthy-looking projects: Eko Atlantic creates an enclave beside informal settlements [1]. Eviction of Makoko residents shows economic growth can undermine social sustainability [1].

Evaluation: "truly sustainable" may not exist anywhere, as every large city depends on outside resources [1]. Governance and planning may matter more than wealth alone [1]. Conclusion: wealth makes some dimensions easier, but no city has achieved all four, so the statement is only partly true [1]. **[16]**
*Examiner insight:* top-level answers judge each of the dimensions of sustainability separately and use detailed evidence from both case studies.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Giving a correct calculation with no working, or wrong units.
- Describing each row of a data table instead of identifying patterns and anomalies.
- Using general definitions in application questions instead of details from the extract.
- Answering "compare" questions with two separate descriptions.
- Naming a disease group rather than one specific non-communicable disease.
- Discussing incineration and landfill in general when a specified urban area is required.
- Writing case studies as fact lists without linking to sustainability or residents' experience.
- Ending an extended answer with no judgement, or with one not supported by earlier points.
