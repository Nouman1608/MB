These questions practise 3.1.3 Children's language development from AQA A-level English Language (7702). Both data
sets are **original**, written for this page.

**Data 1**: Ella (3;2) and her father building a tower of bricks. Key: (.) micropause.

> ELLA: I putted the red one on top
> FATHER: you put the red one on top (.) wow (.) what shall we put next
> ELLA: the bwoo one (.) no (.) the gween one
> FATHER: the green one (.) good choice
> ELLA: it falled down (.) why it falled down
> FATHER: because it was too tall wasn't it (.) shall we try again
> ELLA: yes (.) me do it

**Data 2**: the opening of a story by Ravi (7;4), with his spelling and punctuation kept.

> *"One day a boy cald Sam went to the park and he saw a dragun and the dragun was sad. Sam sed why are you sad. The
> dragun sed I have no frends. And then Sam was his frend and they plaid all day."*

---

## Section A

**1.** Define a virtuous error and give an example from Data 1. **[2]**

**2.** Identify the phonological process in Ella's "bwoo" for "blue" and "gween" for "green", and explain what it
shows. **[3]**

**3.** Explain the difference between overextension and underextension, with an example of each. **[4]**

**4.** Classify each utterance using Halliday's functions: (a) "me do it" (b) "why it falled down" (c) "I putted the
red one on top". **[3]**

**5.** Explain what Berko's "wug" test showed and why it matters for theories of acquisition. **[3]**

**6.** Describe the typical sequence of development in children's questions, and place Ella's "why it falled down" in
it. **[3]**

## Section B

**7.** Analyse the father's language in Data 1 as child-directed speech. **[6]**

**8.** Analyse Ella's grammatical development as shown in Data 1. **[6]**

**9.** Analyse Ravi's spelling in Data 2, classifying the types of error. **[5]**

**10.** Using Kroll's model, explain which stage of writing development Data 2 best fits, with evidence. **[4]**

**11.** Analyse what Data 2 shows about Ravi's knowledge of the story genre. **[5]**

**12.** "Children learn language mainly by imitating the adults around them." Evaluate this view, referring to both
data sets and to theories and research. **[10]**

---

## Answers

**1.** A virtuous error is a logical mistake made by over-applying a grammatical rule to an irregular form [1], for
example "putted" or "falled", where the regular past-tense "-ed" is added to irregular verbs [1].

**2.** Substitution, specifically gliding: Ella replaces the liquids /l/ and /r/ with the easier glide /w/ [1]. The
same replacement in both words, each time in a consonant cluster after a first consonant, shows that /l/ and /r/ are
not yet produced in clusters [1]. This shows patterned, systematic simplification of adult targets rather than
random error [1].

**3.** Overextension is using a word for a wider range of things than adults do [1], such as "apple" for all round
fruit [1]. Underextension is using a word for a narrower range [1], such as "shoe" only for the child's own shoes
[1].

**4.** (a) Regulatory or personal: Ella asserts control over the activity and her own role [1]. (b) Heuristic:
finding out why [1]. (c) Representational: giving information about what she did [1].

**5.** Children shown a made-up creature called a "wug" produced the plural "wugs" for two of them [1], although they
could never have heard that word before [1]. This shows children internalise rules rather than only imitating, which
supports nativist and rule-learning views and challenges behaviourist accounts [1].

**6.** Questions typically develop from rising intonation on a declarative ("daddy gone?"), to wh-words without
subject-auxiliary inversion, to inversion with an auxiliary ("why did it fall down?") [1] [1]. "why it falled down"
uses a wh-word without inversion or auxiliary "did", placing Ella at the middle stage [1].

**7.** Up to 6 points, 1 mark each: recast "you put the red one on top" models the irregular past without explicit
correction [1]; praise ("wow", "good choice") provides positive reinforcement of effort and participation [1];
wh-question "what shall we put next" hands Ella the turn and invites a choice [1]; recast "the green one" models the
target pronunciation [1]; tag question "wasn't it" invites agreement and models a complex structure [1]; the
explanatory "because it was too tall" answers Ella's heuristic question, extending her understanding, and "shall we
try again" scaffolds continued play [1].

**8.** Up to 6 points, 1 mark each: past-tense "-ed" applied consistently ("putted", "falled") shows that she has
acquired the regular rule [1] and over-generalises it to irregular verbs [1]; determiners and pre-modification are
secure ("the red one", "the gween one") [1]; she uses a wh-question but without auxiliary "did" or inversion [1];
"me do it" uses the object pronoun in subject position and omits an auxiliary or modal, a remnant of telegraphic
speech [1]; overall she is in the post-telegraphic stage, with complete clauses and some immature forms [1].

**9.** Up to 5 points, 1 mark each: phonetic spellings that represent sounds accurately ("sed", "cald") [1];
the unstressed vowel spelled as heard ("dragun" for "dragon") [1]; omission of a
silent letter ("frends", missing "i") [1]; over-generalised or analogical spelling of the past "-ed" ending as heard
("plaid" for "played", "cald" for "called") [1]; correct spelling of high-frequency words ("went", "park", "sad",
"day") shows sight vocabulary is secure [1].

**10.** Consolidation best fits [1]: writing closely resembles speech, with chaining by "and" and "and then" [1]; there
is little punctuation of speech and limited sentence variety [1]; but awareness of genre (a story opening, a
problem and resolution) suggests movement towards differentiation [1].

**11.** Up to 5 points, 1 mark each: the formulaic opening "One day" shows awareness of a conventional story opening
[1]; introduction of a named character with a post-modifier ("a boy cald Sam") [1]; a clear problem ("the dragun was
sad... I have no frends") and resolution ("Sam was his frend") show narrative structure [1]; direct speech is
included, though without speech marks, showing awareness that stories contain dialogue [1]; the closing summary
("they plaid all day") echoes a typical happy ending [1].

**12.** Up to 10 points, 1 mark each:
- states the behaviourist position (Skinner): imitation and reinforcement [1];
- evidence for: the father's praise ("good choice") can be read as reinforcement [1];
- evidence for: Ravi's written story reproduces conventions he has heard and read ("One day") [1];
- evidence against: "putted" and "falled" are virtuous errors no adult models [1];
- links to Chomsky's nativist view and the LAD, and Berko's wug test [1];
- the father recasts rather than corrects, and Ella does not immediately adopt his forms, suggesting imitation is
  not straightforward [1];
- interactionist view (Bruner's scaffolding, Vygotsky's zone of proximal development): wh-questions and turn
  handovers support development [1];
- cognitive point: Ella's "why" questions reflect developing understanding of cause (Piaget) [1];
- writing development (spelling by sound) shows rule-based hypotheses rather than copying [1];
- a qualified judgement: imitation plays a part, especially in vocabulary and conventions, but rule formation and
  interaction explain more of the data [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calling "gween" a virtuous error: it is phonological, not grammatical.
- Ignoring the father's turns in Data 1.
- Commenting only on Ravi's spelling and missing genre and structure.
- Evaluating theories with no reference to the data.
- Ending the essay without a judgement.
