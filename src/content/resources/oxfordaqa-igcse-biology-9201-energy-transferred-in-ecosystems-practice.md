These questions cover section **3.3.1 Energy transferred in ecosystems** of the OxfordAQA International GCSE
Biology (9201) specification, Version 5.2, for exams in May/June 2018 onwards. The qualification is untiered. All
data are invented for practice. A calculator is useful for questions 3 to 8. Question 10 tests content that the
specification marks as Biology-only.

Learn the content first in the [Energy transferred in ecosystems study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-biology-9201-energy-transferred-in-ecosystems/)
and the [Energy transferred in ecosystems revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-biology-9201-energy-transferred-in-ecosystems-revision-notes/).
For mixed ecology questions, use the [Energy transfer, biomass pyramids and adaptations practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-biology-energy-transfer-biomass-pyramids-adaptations-and-practice/).

## Section A

**1.**

**(a)** State the source of energy for most communities of living organisms. **[1]**
**(b)** State approximately what percentage of the incident light energy plants and algae transfer for photosynthesis. **[1]**

**2.** Give **two** reasons why only about 10 % of the biomass at one trophic level is transferred to the level above. **[2]**

**3.** A meadow receives 2.8 × 10⁶ kJ of light energy per m² each year.

**(a)** Calculate the energy transferred by the plants for photosynthesis, using the specification figure. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the energy that would be expected in the primary consumers. **[1]**

**4.** The table shows the dry mass of organisms in a food chain, in kg per hectare.

| Stage | Grass | Locusts | Frogs |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dry mass (kg per hectare) | 5 400 | 610 | 58 |

**(a)** Calculate the percentage of biomass transferred from grass to locusts. Give your answer to 3 significant figures. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage of biomass transferred from locusts to frogs. Give your answer to 3 significant figures. **[2]**
**(c)** State why dry mass is used instead of fresh mass. **[1]**

## Section B

**5.** A pyramid of biomass is drawn for a food chain: producers 3 600 kg, primary consumers 420 kg, secondary consumers 36 kg (dry mass per hectare). Use a scale of 1 cm to 300 kg.

**(a)** Calculate the length of each bar. **[3]**
**(b)** Describe how you would draw and label the pyramid. **[3]**
**(c)** Calculate the percentage of biomass transferred from the primary to the secondary consumers. **[2]**

**6.** Explain why the biomass of each level in a pyramid of biomass is smaller than that of the level below. **[2]**

**7.** A cow eats grass containing 800 kJ of energy. The energy is distributed as follows: 280 kJ in faeces and urine, 410 kJ transferred by respiration, and the rest stored in new body material.

**(a)** Calculate the energy stored in new body material. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage of the energy eaten that is stored. Give your answer to 3 significant figures. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain how the figure in part (b) is related to the statement that only about 10 % is transferred. **[2]**

**8.** A farmer's field produces 3.0 × 10⁷ kJ of edible crop energy per year. One person needs 9.0 × 10³ kJ per day.

**(a)** Calculate the energy a person needs in one year (365 days). **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate how many people could be fed if everyone ate the crop directly. Give your answer to the nearest whole number. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate how many people could be fed if the crop were fed to cattle and the people ate the beef (10 % of the energy reaches the beef). Give your answer to the nearest whole number. **[2]**

**9.** Explain why food chains rarely have more than four or five stages. **[2]**

**10.** (Biology only) A farmer keeps chickens indoors in warm, restricted conditions. Evaluate this method in terms of the efficiency of food production. **[3]**

**11.** Explain why a pyramid of biomass is more useful than a count of the numbers of organisms when comparing the amount of food available to each stage. **[2]**

**12.** Explain what happens to the light energy that reaches a field of wheat, and why the amount of energy available to a hawk eating mice that eat the wheat is very small. **[6]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** The Sun (radiation from the Sun) [1]
**(b)** About 1 % [1]

**2.** Some materials and energy are always lost in waste materials (faeces and urine) [1]; respiration supplies all the energy needs for living processes, including movement, and much of this is transferred to the surroundings [1]

**3. (a)** 1 % of 2.8 × 10⁶ [1] = 0.01 × 2 800 000 = **2.8 × 10⁴ kJ** (28 000 kJ) [1]
**(b)** 10 % of 2.8 × 10⁴ = **2.8 × 10³ kJ** (2 800 kJ) [1]

**4. (a)** 610 ÷ 5 400 × 100 [1] = **11.3%** [1]
**(b)** 58 ÷ 610 × 100 [1] = **9.51%** [1]
**(c)** Fresh mass includes a variable amount of water (dry mass gives a fair comparison of biomass) [1]

**5. (a)** Producers 3 600 ÷ 300 = **12.0 cm** [1]; primary consumers 420 ÷ 300 = **1.4 cm** [1]; secondary consumers 36 ÷ 300 = **0.12 cm** (1.2 mm) [1]
**(b)** Draw horizontal bars of equal height [1]; centre them on a vertical line with the producers at the bottom and the secondary consumers at the top [1]; label each bar and write the scale [1]
**(c)** 36 ÷ 420 × 100 [1] = **8.57%** (8.6%) [1]

**6.** Some of the biomass and energy is lost in waste materials [1]; respiration supplies energy for living processes including movement, and much of the energy is transferred to the surroundings, so less is available to the next level [1]

**7. (a)** 800 − 280 − 410 = **110 kJ** [1]
**(b)** 110 ÷ 800 × 100 [1] = **13.8%** [1]
**(c)** The 13.8% stored is the part available to the next consumer [1]; it is close to the approximate figure of 10 % because the rest is lost in waste (35 %) and respiration (about 51 %) [1]

**8. (a)** 9.0 × 10³ × 365 = **3.285 × 10⁶ kJ** (3 285 000 kJ) [1]
**(b)** 3.0 × 10⁷ ÷ 3.285 × 10⁶ [1] = 9.13, so **9 people** [1]
**(c)** 10 % of 3.0 × 10⁷ = 3.0 × 10⁶ kJ [1]; 3.0 × 10⁶ ÷ 3.285 × 10⁶ = 0.91, so **1 person** (about 1) [1]

**9.** Only about 10 % of the biomass is passed on at each stage [1]; so by the fourth or fifth stage there is too little energy left to support another stage [1]

**10.** Benefit: less energy is lost in respiration for movement and keeping warm, so more energy is stored in growth [1]; this makes food production more efficient [1]; drawback (any one): animal welfare concerns, or the cost of heating and buildings [1]

**11.** Biomass shows the mass of living material, which relates to the energy stored [1]; numbers do not allow for the size of organisms, for example one tree and many insects, so they can be misleading [1]

**12.** Marking points, 1 mark each [6]:
- only about 1 % of the incident light energy is transferred by the wheat in photosynthesis
- this energy is stored in the substances that make up the cells of the wheat
- only about 10 % of the biomass is transferred from the wheat to the mice
- some energy and materials are lost from the mice in waste materials
- respiration supplies energy for living processes such as movement, and much of it is transferred to the surroundings
- the loss is repeated from mice to hawk, so only about 10 % of the mice's biomass (about 1 % of the wheat's biomass) reaches the hawk

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using 10 % instead of 1 % for the Sun-to-producer step.
- Writing that energy is "used up" or "lost" without saying it is transferred to the surroundings.
- Giving one reason for loss instead of two.
- Dividing the lower level by the upper level in a percentage transfer.
- Drawing a pyramid with no scale or with bars of different heights.
- Discussing farming efficiency without a benefit and a drawback.
