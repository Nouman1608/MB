## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Fetch | Distance of open water over which the wind blows; longer fetch allows bigger waves |
| Wave energy | Rises with the square of wave height; set by wind speed, duration and fetch |
| Wave refraction | Bending of wave crests in shallow water, concentrating energy on headlands and spreading it in bays |
| Swash / backwash | Uprush of water up the beach / return flow down the beach under gravity |
| High energy wave | Steep, frequent; backwash stronger than swash; removes sediment |
| Low energy wave | Low, long wavelength; swash stronger than backwash; builds beaches |
| Hydraulic action | Force of water and compressed air in joints and cracks |
| Cavitation | Collapse of air bubbles creating shock waves against rock |
| Corrasion / abrasion | Rock fragments thrown or dragged against the coast |
| Solution | Chemical dissolving of rock, especially limestone and chalk |
| Attrition | Sediment particles colliding and becoming smaller and rounder |
| Sub-aerial processes | Weathering and mass movement acting on the land above wave reach |
| Sediment cell | Stretch of coast, usually bounded by headlands, within which sediment moves between sources and sinks with little exchange outside |
| Longshore drift | Movement of sediment along the shore by oblique swash and perpendicular backwash |
| Swash-aligned beach | Beach facing the dominant waves, little net drift |
| Drift-aligned beach | Beach aligned to the direction of longshore drift |
| Tombolo | Sediment ridge linking an island to the mainland |
| Barrier beach | Ridge of sediment above high tide enclosing a lagoon |
| Flocculation | Clay particles clumping in salt water so they settle faster |
| Eustatic change | Global change in sea level from a change in ocean water volume |
| Isostatic change | Local rise or fall of the land relative to the sea |
| Zooxanthellae | Photosynthetic algae living inside coral tissue |
| Bleaching | Corals expelling zooxanthellae under stress, mainly heat, and turning white |

## Process to landform sequences

- **Wave-cut platform:** erosion at high-tide zone -> notch -> overhang collapses -> cliff retreats -> platform widens -> waves lose energy crossing it -> retreat slows (negative feedback).
- **Cave -> arch -> stack -> stump:** hydraulic action and corrasion enlarge joints on a headland; roof weakened from below by waves and from above by weathering and mass movement.
- **Spit:** longshore drift continues past a change in coastline direction -> sediment builds out -> refraction curves the end -> shelter behind allows saltmarsh. Several recurves = compound spit.
- **Tombolo:** deposition in the wave shadow behind an island, or a spit or bar growing until it reaches the island.
- **Dunes:** dry sand blown from a wide beach -> embryo dunes at obstacles -> marram grass traps and binds sand -> fore and yellow dunes -> grey dunes as humus builds up.
- **Saltmarsh:** slack water and flocculation deposit mud -> pioneer salt-tolerant plants trap more -> surface rises -> fewer tidal floods -> more plant species -> creeks drain the marsh.
- **Mangroves:** tropical equivalent of saltmarsh; roots slow tidal flow, trap sediment and absorb wave energy.
- **Submergent coasts (sea level rising relative to land):** rias, fjords, landward-rolling barrier beaches.
- **Emergent coasts (land rising relative to sea):** raised beaches and relict cliffs.

## Case studies at a glance

| Example | Syllabus point it serves | Key facts | One evaluation point |
|---|---|---|---|
| Holderness, eastern England | 8.1 sediment cells, 8.4 management | Glacial till cliffs from Flamborough Head to Spurn Head; average retreat about 1.5 m a year; at least 23 settlements lost since Roman times | Erosion here supplies sediment to Spurn Head, so protecting cliffs has costs elsewhere |
| Mappleton | 8.4 hard engineering | 1991 scheme, about £2 million: two rock groynes, rock revetment, reduced cliff gradient | Village protected and beach wider, but erosion increased just south of the scheme |
| Spurn Head | 8.2 spits, 8.4 no active intervention | Over 5 km long, built from Holderness sediment; a tidal island since the December 2013 surge cut the road; old defences not being replaced | Shows a decision to let natural processes run where assets are few |
| Hurst Spit, Hampshire | 8.2 spits, 8.4 soft and hard engineering | Hook-shaped shingle spit with saltmarsh behind; nearly breached in 1989; rebuilt 1996-97 with dredged shingle and rock revetment | Now protected, but no longer a fully natural landform |
| Chesil Beach, Dorset | 8.2 barrier beach / tombolo, swash alignment | 29 km to the Isle of Portland; Fleet lagoon behind; pebbles graded along its length | Debate over whether it is a tombolo or a landward-rolled barrier beach |
| Great Barrier Reef, Australia | 8.3 barrier reef, threats, management | Marine park under a 1975 Act; World Heritage 1981; no-take zones raised from 4.6% to 33.3% in 2004; mass bleaching including 1998, 2002, 2016, 2017, 2022, 2024 | Zoning reduces local pressures but cannot prevent marine heatwaves |
| Sundarbans, Bangladesh and India | 8.2 mangroves, 8.4 soft protection | About 10,000 km² of mangrove in the Ganges-Brahmaputra-Meghna delta, about 60% in Bangladesh; natural barrier to cyclone surges | Threatened by shrimp farming, salinity and embankments, so its protective role is weakening |
| Darwin's subsidence theory (1842) | 8.3 reef formation | Fringing -> barrier -> atoll as a volcanic island sinks | Supported by 1950s Enewetak drilling, but Pleistocene sea level change also shapes reefs |

## Conditions for coral growth

- Warm water, typically 23-29°C, rarely below about 18°C.
- Light for zooxanthellae, so mostly within roughly the top 30 m.
- Clear water with little sediment.
- Normal sea salinity, so few reefs near large river mouths.
- Wave action for oxygen and food, with only brief exposure at low tide.
- Result: most reefs between about 30°N and 30°S; few on western continental coasts with cold currents and upwelling.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Cavitation vs hydraulic action:** bubble collapse vs water and air forced into cracks.
- **Corrasion vs attrition:** sediment eroding the coast vs sediment eroding itself.
- **Swash-aligned vs drift-aligned:** waves parallel to shore vs waves at an angle.
- **Simple vs compound spit:** one recurve or none vs several old recurves.
- **Offshore bar vs barrier beach:** submerged or low-tide ridge vs ridge above high tide enclosing a lagoon.
- **Eustatic vs isostatic:** change in sea vs change in land.
- **Fringing vs barrier reef:** little or no lagoon vs wide, deep lagoon separating reef and coast.
- **Hard vs soft engineering:** structures that resist the sea vs approaches that work with natural processes.

## Mini essay plan

**Question style:** "To what extent can hard engineering provide sustainable management of a stretch of coastline?" (20 marks)

1. **Introduction:** define hard engineering and sustainable management (protecting people and assets now without creating larger problems later or elsewhere). Name your case study and say it lies within a sediment cell.
2. **For:** at Mappleton, rock groynes and a revetment halted the loss of the village and widened the beach. Hard defences protect high-value assets reliably, which matters where people cannot easily move.
3. **Against:** groynes trap sediment, so cliffs downdrift erode faster. Defences are costly to maintain. Protecting cliffs cuts the sediment supply to spits and beaches further along the cell.
4. **Alternatives:** soft engineering (nourishment, dune or mangrove protection) and no active intervention, as at Spurn Head. Each has its own limits.
5. **Viewpoints:** residents, farmers losing land, conservation groups and governments with limited budgets judge success differently.
6. **Judgement:** hard engineering is sustainable only at a local scale and for high-value sites. At the scale of the whole cell it often moves the problem. Mixed strategies planned for the whole cell are more sustainable.

## Quick self-test

1. What three factors control wave size?
2. Why does wave refraction concentrate erosion on headlands?
3. Name the marine erosion process that involves bubbles collapsing.
4. Give two sub-aerial processes that act on cliffs.
5. What is a sediment cell?
6. What makes a spit "compound"?
7. Why does flocculation matter in estuaries?
8. Give one landform of a submergent coast and one of an emergent coast.
9. Why do reef-building corals need clear, shallow water?
10. In what year were the Great Barrier Reef no-take zones extended, and to what share of the park?
11. What happened to the cliffs south of Mappleton after 1991, and why?

### Answers

1. Wind speed, wind duration and fetch.
2. Waves slow first in the shallow water off headlands, so crests bend and wave energy converges on the headland.
3. Cavitation.
4. Any two: weathering (for example salt crystallisation or wetting and drying), rockfall, sliding, slumping.
5. A stretch of coast, usually between headlands, within which sediment moves between sources, transfers and sinks with little exchange beyond it.
6. It has several recurved ridges along its landward side, each marking an earlier end of the spit.
7. Clay particles clump in salt water and settle faster, building mudflats that saltmarsh or mangroves colonise.
8. Submergent: ria or fjord. Emergent: raised beach or relict cliff.
9. Their zooxanthellae photosynthesise, so they need light; sediment and depth cut out light.
10. 2004; 33.3% of the park.
11. They eroded faster, because the groynes trapped sediment moving south, leaving less beach to protect the cliffs downdrift.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing a landform sequence with no processes named at each stage.
- Writing "the waves erode the cliff" without naming the erosion process.
- Mixing up corrasion and attrition, or cavitation and hydraulic action.
- Ignoring sub-aerial processes when explaining cliff retreat.
- Drawing a spit or tombolo diagram with no labels for drift direction, wave approach or recurves.
- Listing coral growth conditions with no explanation of why each matters.
- Treating every threat to reefs as "global warming" and ignoring local pressures such as runoff and anchor damage.
- Case studies with no place names, dates or schemes, or with facts from the wrong place.
- Essays that describe hard and soft engineering but never judge whether they are sustainable.
