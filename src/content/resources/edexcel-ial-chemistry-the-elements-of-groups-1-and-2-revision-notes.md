## Trends down the groups (8.11, 8.12)

**Ionisation energy falls** down Group 1 and down Group 2. Give all three reasons:

- an extra shell each time, which places the outer electron further out;
- more inner-shell shielding;
- these outweigh the larger nuclear charge.

**Reactivity rises** (Li → K; Mg → Ba) because the outer electron or electrons are lost more easily.
The metals act as reducing agents: Na → Na⁺ + e⁻; Ca → Ca²⁺ + 2e⁻.

## Equations to know (8.13, 8.14)

| With | Group 1 | Group 2 |
|---|---|---|
| O₂ | 4Li + O₂ → 2Li₂O; 2Na + O₂ → Na₂O₂ (excess O₂); K + O₂ → KO₂ | 2Ca + O₂ → 2CaO (same pattern for Mg, Sr, Ba) |
| Cl₂ | 2K + Cl₂ → 2KCl | Sr + Cl₂ → SrCl₂ |
| H₂O | 2Li + 2H₂O → 2LiOH + H₂ | Ba + 2H₂O → Ba(OH)₂ + H₂; Mg + H₂O(g) → MgO + H₂ |
| Oxide + water | K₂O + H₂O → 2KOH | SrO + H₂O → Sr(OH)₂ |
| Oxide + acid | Li₂O + H₂SO₄ → Li₂SO₄ + H₂O | MgO + 2HNO₃ → Mg(NO₃)₂ + H₂O |
| Hydroxide + acid | NaOH + HCl → NaCl + H₂O | Ca(OH)₂ + 2HCl → CaCl₂ + 2H₂O |

**Observations worth a mark:** sodium melts into a silvery ball; potassium burns with a lilac flame on
water; magnesium burns with a dazzling white light; calcium in water gives steady fizzing and a white
suspension.

**Oxidation number of oxygen:** oxide −2, peroxide (O₂²⁻) −1, superoxide (O₂⁻) −½.

## Solubility (8.15)

| Down Group 2 | Hydroxides | Sulfates |
|---|---|---|
| Trend | more soluble | less soluble |
| Least soluble | Mg(OH)₂ | BaSO₄ |
| Most soluble | Ba(OH)₂ | MgSO₄ |

Memory hook: the two trends run in opposite directions, and barium sits at the extreme of each.

## Thermal stability (8.16)

**Method in steps for an "explain" answer:**

1. Name the cation's charge and size (ionic radius).
2. Say the cation polarises (distorts) the electron cloud of the large anion.
3. Say this weakens the C–O or N–O bonds in the anion.
4. Conclude: smaller or more highly charged cation → more polarising → decomposes more easily.

**Decomposition products at a glance**

| Compound | Products on heating |
|---|---|
| Group 2 carbonate | oxide + CO₂ |
| Li₂CO₃ | Li₂O + CO₂ |
| Na₂CO₃, K₂CO₃ | no change at Bunsen temperatures |
| Group 2 nitrate | oxide + NO₂ + O₂ |
| LiNO₃ | Li₂O + NO₂ + O₂ |
| NaNO₃, KNO₃ | nitrite (MNO₂) + O₂ only |

Order of stability: MgCO₃ < CaCO₃ < SrCO₃ < BaCO₃, and the nitrates follow the same order. Group 1 salts beat Group 2 salts because the
cation has half the charge and is larger.

## Flame colours (8.17, 8.18)

| Li⁺ | Na⁺ | K⁺ | Ca²⁺ | Sr²⁺ | Ba²⁺ | Mg²⁺ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| crimson | yellow-orange | lilac | brick red | crimson red | pale green | none visible |

**Why colours form:** electrons absorb heat energy and jump to higher levels, then drop back, emitting
light. The energy gaps are fixed for each element, so the frequencies (and colour) are characteristic.

**Procedure in steps:** clean nichrome wire in concentrated HCl until it gives no colour → moisten
with acid → pick up solid → hold in the hottest part of a non-luminous flame → record the colour.

## Gas tests and decomposition practical (8.18)

| Gas | Test or identifying feature |
|---|---|
| CO₂ | turns limewater milky |
| O₂ | relights a glowing splint |
| NO₂ | brown gas; turns damp blue litmus red (acidic) |

Fair comparison: same amount in moles, same tube, same flame and height, same volume of limewater.
Time to cloudiness or to the first brown fumes measures how easily each salt decomposes.

## Ion tests (8.19)

- **Carbonate / hydrogencarbonate:** dilute acid → fizzing → limewater milky.
  CO₃²⁻ + 2H⁺ → CO₂ + H₂O; HCO₃⁻ + H⁺ → CO₂ + H₂O.
- **Sulfate:** dilute HCl then BaCl₂(aq) → white precipitate. Ba²⁺ + SO₄²⁻ → BaSO₄.
  The acid removes carbonate, which would also give a white precipitate.
- **Ammonium:** NaOH(aq) and warm → ammonia, which turns damp red litmus blue and gives white smoke
  with HCl fumes. NH₄⁺ + OH⁻ → NH₃ + H₂O; NH₃ + HCl → NH₄Cl.

## Titration and uncertainty (8.20 to 8.23)

| Quantity | Formula |
|---|---|
| Amount from solution | n = c × V (V in dm³) |
| Concentration | c = n ÷ V |
| Mass concentration | g dm⁻³ = c × M |
| Percentage uncertainty | (uncertainty ÷ measured value) × 100 |
| Titre uncertainty | 2 × uncertainty of one burette reading |
| Overall uncertainty | add percentage uncertainties |

**Indicators:** methyl orange red → orange as alkali goes in (yellow in alkali); phenolphthalein
colourless → pink as alkali goes in.

**Method in steps for a titration calculation:**

1. Pick concordant titres and take their mean.
2. Moles of the known solution = c × V.
3. Use the equation ratio to get moles of the unknown.
4. Scale up if only a portion of a solution was titrated.
5. Divide by volume in dm³; multiply by M for g dm⁻³.

**Core Practical 3:** titrate a pipetted portion of hydrochloric acid with sodium hydroxide of known
concentration. **Core Practical 4:** weigh a solid acid by difference, dissolve, transfer with rinsings
to a volumetric flask, make up to the mark, invert to mix, then titrate the sodium hydroxide against it.

**Cutting uncertainty:** bigger masses and titres; rinse burette and pipette with their own solution;
read the bottom of the meniscus at eye level; add dropwise near the end point; swirl constantly.

## Quick self-test

1. Give two reasons why the first ionisation energy of potassium is lower than that of sodium.
2. Write an equation for potassium burning in oxygen and give the oxidation number of oxygen in the
   product.
3. Write an equation for magnesium reacting with steam.
4. Which Group 2 hydroxide is least soluble, and which Group 2 sulfate is least soluble?
5. Write an equation for the thermal decomposition of lithium nitrate.
6. Explain in one sentence why barium carbonate is more thermally stable than calcium carbonate.
7. Calculate the volume of CO₂ (in cm³, at room temperature and pressure) from complete
   decomposition of 0.843 g of MgCO₃ (Mr 84.3). Use 24.0 dm³ mol⁻¹.
8. Which ion gives a crimson red flame in Group 2?
9. A solution of KOH has concentration 0.0850 mol dm⁻³. Give its concentration in g dm⁻³ (Mr 56.1).
10. 20.0 cm³ of 0.0500 mol dm⁻³ H₂SO₄ is neutralised by 16.00 cm³ of NaOH. Find c(NaOH).
11. A titre is 18.30 cm³ and each burette reading is ±0.05 cm³. Find the percentage uncertainty.
12. Describe the test for ammonium ions, with the result.

### Answers

1. Potassium has one more occupied shell, so its outer electron is more distant from the nucleus,
   and inner electrons shield it more.
2. K + O₂ → KO₂; oxygen is −½.
3. Mg + H₂O → MgO + H₂
4. Mg(OH)₂; BaSO₄.
5. 4LiNO₃ → 2Li₂O + 4NO₂ + O₂
6. Ba²⁺ is larger than Ca²⁺ with the same charge, so it polarises the carbonate ion less.
7. n = 0.843 ÷ 84.3 = 0.0100 mol; V = 0.0100 × 24 000 = 240 cm³.
8. Sr²⁺.
9. 0.0850 × 56.1 = 4.77 g dm⁻³.
10. n(H₂SO₄) = 1.00 × 10⁻³ mol; n(NaOH) = 2.00 × 10⁻³ mol; c = 2.00 × 10⁻³ ÷ 0.01600 = 0.125 mol dm⁻³.
11. (0.10 ÷ 18.30) × 100 = 0.55%.
12. Warm with NaOH(aq); the gas turns damp red litmus blue (and forms white smoke with HCl fumes).

## Where marks are usually lost

- Stopping at "more shells" for the ionisation energy trend without linking it to a weaker attraction
  for the outer electron.
- Writing MO₂ or M₂O for Group 2 oxides, or an unbalanced 2Na + O₂ → Na₂O.
- Giving H₂ and Mg(OH)₂ for magnesium with steam; with steam the product is MgO.
- Describing "a precipitate" in the sulfate test without the colour, or leaving out the acid.
- Using dry litmus for ammonia; the paper must be damp, because ammonia acts as an alkali only in
  solution.
- Forgetting that NO₂ is identified by its brown colour and acidic reaction, not by a splint test.
- Mixing up the Group 1 and Group 2 nitrate equations, or giving NO₂ from sodium nitrate.
- Writing "the atom polarises the anion"; it is the cation that does it.
- Including the rough titre in the mean, or quoting the mean to fewer decimal places than the readings.
- Forgetting to scale up when only 25.0 cm³ of a 250 cm³ solution was titrated.
- Adding absolute uncertainties of different quantities instead of percentage uncertainties.
