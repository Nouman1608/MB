## Syllabus coverage

**CAMBRIDGE INTERNATIONAL AS & A LEVEL PHYSICS 9702 — AS Level, 7.2 Transverse and longitudinal waves**

Candidates should be able to:

- compare transverse and longitudinal waves
- analyse and interpret graphical representations of transverse and
longitudinal waves

## Comparing the two types

| Feature | Transverse | Longitudinal |
|---|---|---|
| Oscillation of particles | Perpendicular to the direction of energy transfer | Parallel to the direction of energy transfer |
| Regions in the wave | Crests (peaks) and troughs | Compressions and rarefactions |
| Examples | All electromagnetic waves; waves on a stretched string or rope | Sound waves; push-pull waves along a spring |
| Can be polarised? | Yes | No |
| Shared properties | Both transfer energy without transferring the medium; both obey v = fλ; both reflect, refract, diffract and interfere | |

Water waves in a ripple tank are treated as transverse at this level. A
long spring can carry either type, depending on whether the end is moved
sideways (transverse) or pushed and pulled along the axis (longitudinal), so
it is the motion of the source relative to the direction of travel that
decides the type.

A **compression** is a region where the particles are closer together than
normal (higher pressure and density); a **rarefaction** is a region where
they are further apart than normal (lower pressure and density).

## Two kinds of graph, the same shape

Both types of wave are drawn as sine curves, and this is the source of most
confusion. Always read the axes first.

**Displacement–distance graph** (a snapshot at one instant):

- horizontal axis: distance along the wave
- shows the displacement of **every particle** at one moment
- gives the **wavelength** (distance for one complete cycle) and the
**amplitude**

**Displacement–time graph** (one particle over time):

- horizontal axis: time
- shows how **one particle's** displacement changes
- gives the **period** (and so the frequency) and the **amplitude**

To find the wave speed you need one of each, or one graph plus one other
quantity: v = λ/T.

**Worked example.** A displacement–time graph for one air particle in a sound
wave shows a period of 2.5 ms. The speed of sound is 340 m s⁻¹.

```
f      = 1 / T = 1 / 2.5 x 10^-3 = 400 Hz
lambda = v / f = 340 / 400 = 0.85 m
```

## Reading a transverse snapshot: which way is a particle moving?

On a displacement–distance graph of a transverse wave travelling to the
right, each particle will next have the displacement that the particle
**just behind it** (on the source side) has now. So:

- a particle on the **front slope** of a crest (the side facing the direction
of travel) is moving **up**
- a particle on the **back slope** of a crest is moving **down**
- particles at a crest or a trough are momentarily **at rest**
- particles at zero displacement are moving **fastest**

A quick check is to imagine the whole curve shifted slightly in the direction
of travel and see whether each point's displacement rises or falls.

## Reading a longitudinal snapshot: where are the compressions?

For a longitudinal wave, the graph does **not** show the shape of the wave.
The vertical axis is displacement *along* the direction of travel, plotted
upwards for convenience. Take positive displacement as "to the right".

At a point where the graph crosses zero going **downwards** (from positive to
negative):

- particles just to the left are displaced right, towards the point
- particles just to the right are displaced left, towards the point
- so the particles crowd together: this is the centre of a **compression**

At a point where the graph crosses zero going **upwards**, particles on each
side are displaced away from the point: the centre of a **rarefaction**.

**Worked example.** A displacement–distance graph for a sound wave is a sine
curve that starts at zero at x = 0, rises to a positive maximum at
x = 0.20 m, returns to zero at x = 0.40 m, reaches a negative maximum at
x = 0.60 m, and returns to zero at x = 0.80 m. Positive displacement is in the
direction of travel.

```
x = 0      zero crossing, upward       -> rarefaction
x = 0.40 m zero crossing, downward     -> compression
x = 0.80 m zero crossing, upward       -> rarefaction
wavelength = 0.80 m (rarefaction to rarefaction)
```

Three results to remember: the particles at the centre of a compression or a
rarefaction have **zero displacement**; the particles with **maximum
displacement** lie midway between a compression and a rarefaction, where the
spacing is normal; and the distance from one compression to the next is one
wavelength.

**Pressure graphs.** A sound wave can also be shown as a pressure–distance
graph. Pressure is greatest at compressions, where the displacement is zero,
so for a sinusoidal wave the pressure curve is shifted by a quarter of a
wavelength from the displacement curve. A question that gives a pressure
graph is asking you to find compressions at the pressure maxima directly.

## The oscilloscope trap

A microphone connected to a CRO shows a sine-shaped trace for a sound wave.
This does **not** mean sound is transverse. The trace is a graph of the
microphone's output voltage against time, which follows the pressure changes
at one point. It is a displacement–time style graph, not a picture of the
wave.

## Exam technique

- **Compare**: make each point a matched pair ("in a transverse wave…, whereas
in a longitudinal wave…"). Two separate descriptions often lose the
comparison mark.
- **Describe the motion of a particle**: give the direction relative to the
direction of energy transfer and say it oscillates about a fixed position.
- **Interpret a graph**: name the axis you used and state what one cycle on
it represents (wavelength or period).
- **Mark a compression on a graph**: give the position and justify it using
the displacements of neighbouring particles.

## Common mistakes

- Reading a wavelength from a displacement–time graph, or a period from a
displacement–distance graph.
- Marking compressions at the crests of a longitudinal displacement graph.
- Saying particles in a longitudinal wave move along with the wave.
- Defining a transverse wave as one that "moves up and down" without
reference to the direction of energy transfer.
- Treating a CRO trace of sound as evidence that sound is transverse.

## Quick revision checklist

- Transverse: oscillation perpendicular to energy transfer; longitudinal:
parallel
- Compressions and rarefactions; crests and troughs
- Displacement–distance gives λ; displacement–time gives T
- Transverse snapshot: front slope of crest rising, back slope falling
- Longitudinal snapshot: compression at a downward zero crossing (positive
displacement in the direction of travel)
- Only transverse waves can be polarised

## Frequently asked questions

**How can you tell a displacement–distance graph from a displacement–time
graph?**
Look at the horizontal axis. Distance means a snapshot of many particles;
time means the history of one particle.

**Where are the compressions on a displacement–distance graph of a sound
wave?**
At the zero-displacement points where neighbouring particles are displaced
towards each other: with positive displacement in the direction of travel,
where the graph crosses zero going downwards.

**Is a water wave transverse?**
At this level, water surface waves in a ripple tank are treated as
transverse.
