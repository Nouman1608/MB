This guide covers subtopic **17.2 DNA** of Cambridge O Level Biology 5090, 2026-2028 series. The syllabus asks you to describe the structure of a DNA molecule (two strands coiled into a double helix, each a chain of nucleotides, each nucleotide containing a base A, T, C or G, bonds between base pairs holding the strands together, and A always pairing with T and C with G); to define a gene as a length of DNA that codes for a protein; to explain that DNA controls cell function by controlling the production of proteins, including enzymes; to state that the sequence of bases in a gene determines the sequence of amino acids in a protein; and to understand that different sequences of amino acids give proteins different shapes.

## Where DNA is found

DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid) is the molecule that carries genetic information. In a cell with a nucleus it is found in the **chromosomes** in the nucleus. A chromosome is a very long DNA molecule with proteins. A human cell nucleus contains 46 chromosomes, and each is a single DNA molecule that is a very long chain.

## The structure of a DNA molecule

DNA has a shape called a **double helix**: like a twisted ladder.

1. There are **two strands** coiled together.
2. Each strand is a chain of **nucleotides**.
3. Each nucleotide contains a **base**. There are four bases: **A, T, C and G**. The full names are not required.
4. The two strands are held together by **bonds between pairs of bases**.
5. The bases always pair up in the same way: **A with T**, and **C with G**. This is called **complementary base pairing**.

Think of the helix as a ladder. The two sides are the strands made of nucleotides. The rungs are the pairs of bases, held together by bonds in the middle. You need not remember the detail of the sugar and phosphate parts of a nucleotide.

### Complementary base pairing

| Base | Always pairs with |
|---|---|
| A | T |
| T | A |
| C | G |
| G | C |

This is a fixed rule. It means that if you know the sequence of bases along one strand, you can work out the sequence along the other.

### Worked example 1: writing the complementary strand

*One strand of a DNA molecule has the base sequence A T G C C A T G. Write the sequence on the other strand.*

| Strand 1 | A | T | G | C | C | A | T | G |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Strand 2 | T | A | C | G | G | T | A | C |

Each base is replaced by its partner: A to T, T to A, G to C, C to G.

### Worked example 2: percentages of bases

*In a sample of DNA, 28 per cent of the bases are A. Find the percentages of T, C and G.*

1. A pairs with T, so the percentage of T is also 28 per cent.
2. A + T = 28 + 28 = 56 per cent.
3. The remaining bases are C and G: 100 - 56 = 44 per cent.
4. C pairs with G, so they are equal: 44 divided by 2 = 22 per cent each.

Answer: T = 28 per cent, C = 22 per cent, G = 22 per cent. The rule is that the amount of A equals the amount of T, and the amount of C equals the amount of G.

### Worked example 3: counting pairs

*A short length of DNA has 20 bases on one strand. How many base pairs are there, and how many bases are there in total on both strands?*

Each base on one strand pairs with a base on the other strand, so there are 20 base pairs. The total number of bases is 20 x 2 = 40.

## Genes

**A gene is a length of DNA that codes for a protein.** A chromosome contains many genes arranged along its length. A single gene is a short stretch of the DNA molecule, and each gene has its own sequence of bases. A cell does not use all of its genes all the time; the genes in use depend on the type of cell.

## How DNA controls cell function

**DNA controls cell function by controlling the production of proteins.** This includes **enzymes**, which are proteins. Enzymes control the chemical reactions in the cell, and other proteins give structure or carry out other functions, such as haemoglobin carrying oxygen. Because proteins decide what a cell is able to do, the genes that code for them control the activity of the cell and, in the end, the features of the organism.

The steps to learn are:

1. A gene in the DNA contains the instructions to make a particular protein.
2. The cell uses the instructions to make the protein.
3. The protein (for example an enzyme) carries out its job in the cell.
4. The activity of the cell, and its features, depend on which proteins it makes.

### Bases, amino acids and shapes

- A protein is made of a chain of **amino acids**.
- The **sequence of bases** in a gene determines the **sequence of amino acids** in the protein.
- A **different sequence** of amino acids gives a protein a **different shape**.
- The shape of a protein decides its function. For example, the shape of the active site of an enzyme fits its substrate.

So a change to the base sequence of a gene can change the order of amino acids, which can change the shape of the protein. If the shape changes, the protein may not work properly. This is the link to mutation, which is dealt with in the inheritance subtopic: sickle cell anaemia is caused by a change in a gene that changes the haemoglobin protein.

### Worked example 4: the chain of cause and effect

*Explain how a gene controls the production of an enzyme such as amylase.*

- The gene is a length of DNA with a specific sequence of bases. [1]
- The sequence of bases determines the sequence of amino acids in the enzyme. [1]
- That sequence of amino acids gives the enzyme its particular shape, including the shape of its active site. [1]
- The enzyme then catalyses its reaction, so the gene controls the function of the cell. [1]

### Different cells, same DNA

Almost all the body cells of one person contain the same DNA. A muscle cell and a nerve cell have different features because they use different genes, and so they make different proteins. This idea links DNA to specialised cells.

## Putting numbers on the genetic code

A chromosome may contain thousands of genes, and each gene may be hundreds or thousands of bases long. Even a short gene has a huge number of possible base sequences. For a short stretch of just 4 bases, with 4 possible bases at each position, there are 4 x 4 x 4 x 4 = 256 different sequences. A gene of 300 bases has far more. This is why a cell can make so many different proteins from just four bases: the information is in the order, in the same way that the 26 letters of the alphabet can spell a very large number of words.

### Worked example 5: the effect of a change

*A section of a gene has the sequence A A G C T T. A mutation changes the third base from G to T, giving A A T C T T. Which statements are correct?*

- The sequence of bases has changed. **Correct.**
- The sequence of amino acids in the protein might change. **Correct** (it may, because the base sequence decides the amino acid sequence).
- The protein will definitely stop working. **Not necessarily.** If the change alters the shape of an important part such as an active site, the protein may not work, but some changes have little or no effect.

This is the kind of careful wording the exam rewards: use "may" and "could" when the outcome depends on the change.

## DNA, genes and inheritance

When cells divide by mitosis, the DNA is copied first, so that each daughter cell has the same genes. Sperm and egg cells carry half of the parent's DNA, and a zygote receives one set from each parent. The offspring therefore inherits a mixture of genes. This is explained further in the inheritance subtopic. Here it is enough to know that DNA is the material that is passed from one generation to the next, and that the information is the order of its bases.

## A model to remember

Think of DNA as a cookbook. The cookbook (the chromosome) contains many recipes (genes). Each recipe is written using only four letters (bases). A recipe gives instructions for making one dish (a protein). The cell reads only the recipes that it needs. Cooks (the cell) do the work, but the cookbook decides which dishes can be made. Like all models, this has limits: the DNA does not leave the nucleus, and a book does not copy itself before a cell divides. Use the model to remember the roles, but use correct biological terms in answers.

## Common mistakes

- Saying that bases pair A with C or T with G. A pairs with T, and C with G.
- Writing that a gene "codes for a trait" without saying it codes for a protein.
- Saying that DNA makes proteins directly. It controls their production.
- Writing "the shape of DNA is a single helix". It is a double helix.
- Saying that the chromosome is a gene. A chromosome contains many genes.
- Mixing up bases and amino acids. The bases are in DNA; the amino acids are in proteins.
- Saying that the amino acid sequence is unimportant. It decides the shape.
- Writing that the bonds are between bases on the same strand. They are between the two strands.

## Command words

- **Describe**: state the structure of the DNA molecule.
- **Define**: give the definition of a gene.
- **Explain**: link base sequence, amino acid sequence, shape and function.
- **State**: give the base-pairing rule.
