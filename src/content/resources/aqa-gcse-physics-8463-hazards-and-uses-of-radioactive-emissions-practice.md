These questions cover section **4.4.3 Hazards and uses of radioactive emissions and of background
radiation** of the AQA GCSE Physics (8463) specification, a physics only section examined on Paper 1 at both
tiers. Nothing here is Higher tier only. A calculator is allowed throughout. All dose figures are invented
for practice.

Learn the content first in the
[hazards and uses study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-physics-8463-hazards-and-uses-of-radioactive-emissions/)
and the
[hazards and uses revision notes](/resources/aqa-gcse-physics-8463-hazards-and-uses-of-radioactive-emissions-revision-notes/).

## Section A

**1.** State what is meant by background radiation. **[1]**

**2.** Give the two man-made sources of background radiation named in the specification. **[2]**

**3.** Radiation dose can be given in millisieverts or sieverts.

**(a)** Convert 0.75 mSv into sieverts. **[1]**
**(b)** Convert 0.0042 Sv into millisieverts. **[1]**

**4.** A miner works underground in a town where an office worker also lives. Suggest why the miner's yearly
radiation dose may be higher than the office worker's. **[2]**

**5.** A radioactive isotope has a half-life of 1.2 × 10⁵ s. Calculate its half-life in hours. Give your answer
to 2 significant figures. **[2]**

**6.** State the two uses of nuclear radiation in medicine that are named in the specification. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** A student measures the background count-rate in a laboratory as 0.40 counts/s. She then measures the
count-rate from a source every 10 minutes. Her readings have **not** been corrected for background.

| Time (min) | 0 | 10 | 20 | 30 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Measured count-rate (counts/s) | 64.4 | 32.4 | 16.4 | 8.4 |

**(a)** Calculate the corrected count-rate at each time. **[2]**
**(b)** Use your answers to determine the half-life of the source. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain why using the uncorrected readings would give a half-life that is too long. **[2]**

**8.** A sample of nuclear waste contains three isotopes.

| Isotope | Half-life |
|---|---|
| X | 30 s |
| Y | 8.0 days |
| Z | 2.4 × 10⁴ years |

**(a)** Each isotope starts with the same number of unstable nuclei. State which isotope has the highest
activity at first, and give a reason. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain which isotope decides how long the waste must be stored securely. **[2]**
**(c)** The count-rate from isotope Y is 480 counts/s when the waste is produced. Calculate its count-rate
32 days later. **[2]**

**9.** A hospital wants to destroy part of a patient's overactive thyroid gland from the inside. The thyroid
absorbs iodine, so the source is given to the patient in a drink. Three isotopes are available.

| Isotope | Radiation emitted | Half-life |
|---|---|---|
| A | Alpha | 2 years |
| B | Beta | 8 days |
| C | Gamma | 6 hours |

**(a)** Evaluate which isotope is most suitable. Give reasons for rejecting the other two. **[4]**
**(b)** Explain why the patient's body is a source of radiation for some weeks after treatment, but not for
years. **[2]**

**10.** The table shows invented yearly doses for people in one country.

| Exposure | Dose |
|---|---|
| Average yearly background radiation | 2.5 mSv |
| Extra yearly dose for an airline pilot from cosmic rays | 3.0 mSv |
| One tracer scan of the heart | 4.5 mSv |

**(a)** Calculate the total yearly dose for an airline pilot. Give your answer in sieverts. **[2]**
**(b)** A patient refuses the scan, saying: "That radiation is far more dangerous than anything I am
normally exposed to." Use the data to evaluate the patient's view. **[4]**

**11.** A tumour deep inside a patient's body is treated with beams of gamma rays from a source outside the
body. The beams are aimed at the tumour from several different directions.

**(a)** Explain why the beams are aimed from several directions rather than from one. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain why gamma radiation is used rather than alpha radiation. **[2]**
**(c)** Give one precaution that protects hospital staff during the treatment. **[1]**

**12.** A nuclear accident releases fallout containing isotopes with a wide range of half-lives. Explain why
the hazard near the accident is very high at first, why it falls quickly over the first weeks, and why
parts of the area may still need to be closed many years later. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** Radiation that is around us all of the time, from natural and man-made sources [1]

**2.** Fallout from nuclear weapons testing [1]; fallout from nuclear accidents [1]
*Examiner insight:* "Nuclear power stations" or "X-rays" do not match the specification's two man-made
sources.

**3. (a)** 0.75 ÷ 1000 = **7.5 × 10⁻⁴ Sv** (0.00075 Sv) [1]
**(b)** 0.0042 × 1000 = **4.2 mSv** [1]

**4.** Underground, the miner is surrounded by rock, which contains radioactive isotopes [1]; some rocks
release radon gas, which builds up in enclosed spaces, so the miner receives more background radiation at
work [1]

**5.** 1.2 × 10⁵ ÷ 3600 [1]; = 33.3, so **33 hours** [1]

**6.** Exploring (imaging) internal organs [1]; controlling or destroying unwanted tissue, such as a tumour [1]

**7. (a)** Subtract 0.40 from each reading [1]; 64.0, 32.0, 16.0 and 8.0 counts/s [1]
**(b)** The corrected count-rate halves every 10 minutes, so the half-life is **10 minutes** [1]
**(c)** The background adds the same 0.40 counts/s to every reading and does not decay [1]; so the measured
values fall by less than half in each 10 minutes (64.4 has not yet fallen to 32.2 by 10 minutes), making
the half-life appear longer [1]
*Examiner insight:* The error grows as the source's count-rate falls, because background becomes a larger
share of each reading.

**8. (a)** **X** [1]; it has the shortest half-life, so its nuclei decay at the greatest rate [1]
**(b)** **Z** [1]; its half-life is 24 000 years, so it stays radioactive, and hazardous, for tens of
thousands of years after X and Y have decayed [1]
**(c)** 32 ÷ 8.0 = 4 half-lives [1]; 480 → 240 → 120 → 60 → **30 counts/s** [1]

**9. (a)** **B** is most suitable [1]; beta is ionising enough to destroy thyroid cells, and its short range
in tissue keeps most of the dose inside the gland [1]; A's half-life of 2 years would leave the patient
radioactive for years, so healthy tissue would be exposed far longer than needed [1]; C's gamma radiation
mostly passes out of the body, so it would give little dose to the thyroid and would irradiate other people
near the patient; its 6-hour half-life is also too short for a treatment [1]
**(b)** The half-life of 8 days means the activity halves every 8 days, so after some weeks only a small
fraction of the isotope remains [1]; it is short compared with years, so the activity is negligible long
before then (the body also removes some of it) [1]
*Examiner insight:* "Evaluate" needs a clear choice and a reason against each rejected option; a choice on
its own scores one mark.

**10. (a)** 2.5 + 3.0 = 5.5 mSv [1]; 5.5 ÷ 1000 = **0.0055 Sv** [1]
**(b)** The scan dose is 4.5 ÷ 2.5 = 1.8 times the yearly background dose [1]; this is less than the extra
dose a pilot receives in a year and a half (4.5 ÷ 3.0 = 1.5 years), which pilots accept as part of the job
[1]; so the scan increases the risk of harm only by a small amount, and the patient's perceived risk is
larger than the data show [1]; the benefit of diagnosing a heart problem outweighs this small extra risk,
so the view is not supported [1]

**11. (a)** The beams cross at the tumour, so the tumour receives the dose from every beam [1]; each region of
healthy tissue is crossed by only one beam [1]; so the tumour receives a large enough dose to destroy it
while healthy tissue receives a smaller dose and less damage [1]
**(b)** Alpha cannot penetrate the skin, so it could not reach a deep tumour from outside the body [1];
gamma is very penetrating, so it reaches the tumour [1]
**(c)** Any one: staff leave the room or stand behind shielding while the beam is on; they wear dose
badges to monitor exposure; the source is stored in a shielded container [1]

**12.** Short half-life isotopes are present at first, and a short half-life means a high activity, so the
hazard is very high [1]; the fallout contaminates the area: radioactive atoms are on or in the ground,
buildings and plants, so the hazard continues while those atoms decay [1]; over days and weeks the short
half-life isotopes pass through many half-lives [1]; so their activity falls to a small fraction and the
hazard drops quickly [1]; isotopes with long half-lives decay slowly, so after many years most of their
nuclei have still not decayed [1]; their activity is lower but lasts, so contaminated parts of the area
stay hazardous and may have to remain closed [1]
*Examiner insight:* AQA marks 6-mark answers by level. The top level needs each time period linked to
half-life and activity; "the radiation lasts a long time" on its own stays at the lowest level.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Converting between mSv and Sv with the wrong power of ten.
- Reading the half-life from uncorrected counts.
- Saying the isotope with the longest half-life is the most active.
- Choosing a source for its radiation type but ignoring its half-life.
- Quoting a dose from the table without comparing it with anything.
- Ending an evaluation without a judgement.
