Section B of Unit 1 (WET01) is an essay on one post-2000 novel. The specification is explicit that "it
is the writers' use of narrative that should be taught alongside the critical appreciation of the text
and consideration of any contextual influences". This guide shows what that means for each of the five
set novels, how to handle the statement each question gives you, and how to bring in context without
losing the text. The [Unit 1 overview](/resources/a-level-edexcel-english-literature-post-2000-poetry-and-prose/)
covers the paper as a whole.

## What Section B covers

| Detail | Section B: Post-2000 Prose |
|---|---|
| Task | One essay from a choice of two on your chosen novel |
| Question type | A statement that you must respond to, considering relevant contextual factors |
| Format | Open book |
| Marks | 25 of the paper's 50 |
| AOs | AO1, AO2, AO3 |

The set novels are *Brooklyn* (Colm Tóibín), *The Kite Runner* (Khaled Hosseini), *Life of Pi* (Yann
Martel), *Purple Hibiscus* (Chimamanda Ngozi Adichie) and *The White Tiger* (Aravind Adiga). The list is
fixed by specification issue, so confirm it with your teacher.

## What the specification asks you to study

**Key features of prose fiction:** characterisation; form and structure; grammatical structure;
imagery, symbols and motifs; language choices; voice; theme; and use of dialogue. The learning
outcomes add reading for detail of how writers "use and adapt language, form and structure", the
contexts in which prose texts have been written and how they influence meaning, and how attitudes and
values are expressed.

"Grammatical structure" is the feature students most often overlook: sentence length, tense, the
use of the second person, and how syntax creates a voice.

## Narrative methods in the five novels

| Novel | Narrative method to analyse | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| *Brooklyn* | Restrained third-person narration closely tied to Eilis's perception | Feelings are often implied rather than stated, so silences and omissions carry meaning |
| *The Kite Runner* | First-person retrospective narration by Amir | The adult narrator judges his younger self; guilt shapes what he tells and when |
| *Life of Pi* | A frame narrative with an author figure, Pi's first-person story, and a final interview offering a second version | The structure asks the reader to choose between stories |
| *Purple Hibiscus* | First-person narration by Kambili, with a non-chronological structure that opens on Palm Sunday, moves back to the months before it, then forward to the present | A quiet, constrained voice that changes as she does |
| *The White Tiger* | Balram's letters to the Chinese Premier over several nights | A confident, comic, unreliable voice addressing an outsider |

## Responding to the statement

Each question gives a statement. You are not asked to agree; you are asked to **test** it.

1. **Define the key terms.** If the statement says a character is "a victim", decide what victimhood
   means in this novel.
2. **Take a position**, which can be partial: "largely true, but...".
3. **Build evidence** from across the novel: the opening, a turning point, the ending.
4. **Show how methods shape the issue**: the reader's view of the claim depends on how the story is
   told.
5. **Use context to explain** why the novel presents the issue this way.
6. **Conclude with a judgement** that answers the statement directly.

## AO3: context for post-2000 novels

The specification's guidance on context lists the writer's life and situation (only where relevant),
the historical setting, social and cultural contexts, literary context, and contexts of reception.

For these novels, context usually means **contemporary concerns**: globalisation and inequality,
migration and belonging, religion and authority, political violence, and how stories shape truth.
Context is strongest when it explains a choice: for example, why a novel set in the past might speak to
readers in the 2000s, or how a narrator's position in society shapes what he can say.

| Novel | Contexts worth knowing |
|---|---|
| *Brooklyn* | Irish emigration to the United States in the 1950s; Catholic community life; women's work and marriage |
| *The Kite Runner* | Afghanistan from the monarchy to the Soviet invasion and Taliban rule; ethnic division between Pashtun and Hazara; the Afghan diaspora in the United States |
| *Life of Pi* | Religious pluralism; zoos and the human relationship with animals; storytelling, faith and truth |
| *Purple Hibiscus* | Post-colonial Nigeria; political instability and military rule; Catholicism and Igbo tradition |
| *The White Tiger* | India's economic growth and the outsourcing industry; class and caste; servants and masters; corruption |

## Worked example

Statement (original): *"Balram is more a product of his society than a villain." Explore this view of
The White Tiger.*

**Plan:**

```
Thesis:   The novel invites the reader to see Balram as a product of a corrupt
          system, but his own confident narration makes that view harder to accept.
Para 1:   The "Darkness" and the "Light" - Balram's account of village poverty and
          servitude explains, but does not excuse, what follows (AO2 + AO3: rural
          poverty, class)
Para 2:   The Rooster Coop metaphor - the system keeps servants loyal; Balram presents
          the murder as escape from it (AO2: extended metaphor; AO3: servant culture)
Para 3:   The hit-and-run and the forced confession - the novel's clearest evidence
          that the powerful use the poor (AO2: structure, turning point)
Para 4:   Form - letters to a foreign leader: Balram performs success and markets
          himself, which makes him a less reliable witness to his own innocence (AO2: voice)
Para 5:   The ending - entrepreneur in Bangalore; the reader's discomfort at his
          lack of remorse and the likely cost to his family (AO1: judgement)
Conclusion: both - the society creates him, but the narration makes us judge him
```

**Model paragraph (paragraph 4):**

> Adiga's choice of form complicates any simple claim that Balram is only a product of his society.
> Because the novel is written as letters to the Chinese Premier, Balram is always performing: he
> presents himself as a self-made entrepreneur explaining the "real" India to a powerful outsider, and
> his comic confidence draws the reader in. Yet the same voice that exposes the corruption of masters
> and politicians also justifies a murder with unsettling ease. The epistolary form therefore makes the
> reader both an ally and a judge: we accept his diagnosis of an unequal society, written at a time of
> celebrated economic growth, while recognising that his account is designed to persuade.

What earns credit: AO1 argument tied to the statement; AO2 analysis of form and voice; AO3 context
(economic growth and inequality) used to explain a narrative choice, not added as background.

## Common mistakes

- **Plot summary** in place of analysis of narrative methods.
- **Historical background paragraphs** that never return to the novel.
- **Ignoring the statement**, or agreeing with it in the introduction and then forgetting it.
- **Treating the narrator as the author**, especially with first-person narrators such as Amir, Kambili
  and Balram.
- **Quoting long passages** from the open-book text with little comment.
- **Writing only about character**: structure, voice and symbols are what AO2 rewards.

## Exam technique

- Read both questions; choose the statement you can test across the whole novel.
- Plan for five minutes: thesis, four or five points, ending.
- Use the open book for **short, precise** quotations you have already located during revision.
- Make each paragraph's topic sentence answer the statement.
- Finish with a judgement that weighs the statement, not a summary.
