## Section A

**1.** Give **three** causes of the 1848 revolutions in Europe. **[3]**

**2.** Name the key figures in the unification of Italy and state the role of each. **[4]**

## Section B

**3.** Explain why the 1848 revolutions largely failed. **[8]**

**4.** Explain the role of Bismarck in the unification of Germany, referring to **three** wars. **[9]**

**5.** Explain the following causes of the First World War: the alliance system, militarism, and the crisis in the Balkans. **[9]**

**6.** "The First World War was caused primarily by the alliance system." How far do you agree? **[12]**

---

## Answers

**1.** Any three: **economic distress** following poor harvests and the food shortages of the "hungry forties" [1]; **demands for liberal constitutions and an extension of the franchise** from the growing middle class [1]; **nationalist demands for independence or unification**, particularly in Italy, Germany and the Habsburg lands [1]; the **example of the February revolution in France**, which spread by imitation [1].

**2. Mazzini** — the ideological inspiration, founder of Young Italy, who promoted a republican vision of unity [1]. **Garibaldi** — the military leader whose Expedition of the Thousand conquered Sicily and Naples [1]. **Cavour** — Prime Minister of Piedmont, who provided the diplomatic strategy and the French alliance [1]. **Victor Emmanuel II** — King of Piedmont, who became the first king of a united Italy [1].

**3.** The revolutionaries were **divided among themselves** [1]: liberals wanted constitutional reform while radicals wanted a republic and social change, and **once the initial concessions were won the middle classes withdrew** rather than see the revolution go further [1] [1]. **Nationalist movements conflicted with one another**, as in the Habsburg Empire where Hungarian and Slav aspirations clashed [1]. Crucially, the **armies remained loyal to their monarchs** [1], so once the initial shock passed the rulers had the force to crush the risings [1]. **Austria and Russia intervened** to restore conservative order, notably in Hungary and Italy [1]. The revolutionaries also **lacked the support of the peasantry**, who returned to quiescence once feudal dues were abolished [1].

**4.** Bismarck pursued a policy of **realpolitik**, using war deliberately as an instrument to unite Germany under **Prussian** rather than Austrian leadership [1] [1].
**The Danish War (1864)** — Prussia and Austria jointly seized Schleswig and Holstein [1]; the **joint administration was deliberately designed to create friction with Austria** [1].
**The Austro-Prussian War (1866)** — Bismarck provoked the quarrel over the duchies into war [1]; the swift Prussian victory at Sadowa **excluded Austria from German affairs** and produced the North German Confederation under Prussian control [1].
**The Franco-Prussian War (1870–71)** — Bismarck used the **Ems Telegram to provoke France into declaring war** [1], which brought the **southern German states in on Prussia's side out of national feeling** [1]; the German Empire was proclaimed at Versailles in January 1871 [1].

**5. The alliance system** divided Europe into **two armed camps** — the Triple Alliance and the Triple Entente [1]; this meant a **local quarrel could escalate into a general war**, as obligations pulled each power in [1]; it also created a climate of suspicion in which every diplomatic move was read as hostile [1].
**Militarism** — the **arms race**, particularly the Anglo-German naval rivalry over dreadnoughts, raised tension and expenditure [1]; **detailed war plans such as the Schlieffen Plan** made rapid mobilisation essential [1], so once mobilisation began the timetable **removed the room for diplomacy** [1].
**The Balkans** — the **decline of Ottoman power** created a vacuum that Austria-Hungary and Russia both sought to fill [1]; **Serbian nationalism**, backed by Russia, threatened the multi-ethnic Habsburg Empire [1]; the **assassination at Sarajevo in June 1914** gave Austria the pretext it wanted, and the alliance obligations did the rest [1].

**6. Agreeing:** the alliances **converted an Austro-Serbian quarrel into a continental war** [1]; Russia's obligation to Serbia, Germany's "blank cheque" to Austria and France's commitment to Russia meant that **no power could stay out** [1]; the rigidity of the system removed the flexibility that had kept the peace since 1871 [1].
**Disagreeing:** the alliances were **defensive in intent and had defused earlier crises** — Morocco in 1905 and 1911, Bosnia in 1908 — without war, so they were not automatically fatal [1]. **Militarism and the mobilisation timetables** arguably mattered more: the Schlieffen Plan meant Germany had to attack France to fight Russia, which turned an eastern crisis into a western war [1] [1]. **Nationalism** in the Balkans supplied the actual spark and the underlying instability [1]. **Imperial rivalry** over colonies and markets had already poisoned relations [1]. Some historians emphasise **individual decisions in July 1914** — Austrian determination to punish Serbia and German willingness to risk war — over structural causes [1].
**Judgement:** the alliance system was the **mechanism by which a local war became a general one, but not its cause** [1] [1]. Without the Balkan crisis there would have been nothing for the alliances to escalate, and without the mobilisation plans there would have been time to negotiate; the alliances are best seen as a necessary but not sufficient condition [1] [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Narrating events rather than explaining causes.
- Attributing German unification entirely to Bismarck's planning.
- Listing causes of the war without linking them.
- Not reaching a supported judgement in the "how far" question.
