Condensed notes on section **3.5.2 Analysing financial performance** of AQA A-level Business (7132, for first teaching from September 2023). The full explanations and worked examples are in the [financial performance study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-analysing-financial-performance/); exam-style questions are in the [financial performance practice set](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-analysing-financial-performance-practice/).

## Specification content

- Constructing and analysing budgets and cash flow forecasts; variance analysis; adverse and favourable variances; the value of budgeting.
- Constructing and interpreting break-even charts: break-even output, margin of safety, contribution per unit, total contribution; effects of changes in price, output and cost; the value of break-even analysis.
- Analysing profitability: gross profit, profit from operations and profit for the year margins.
- Analysing timings of cash inflows and outflows: payables and receivables.
- The use of data for financial decision making and planning.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Budget | Financial plan for a future period |
| Variance | Difference between budget and actual |
| Favourable variance | Raises profit (higher revenue or lower cost than budget) |
| Adverse variance | Reduces profit (lower revenue or higher cost than budget) |
| Cash flow forecast | Prediction of receipts, payments and closing balance each month |
| Contribution per unit | Price − variable cost per unit |
| Break-even output | Output where total revenue equals total cost |
| Margin of safety | Actual output − break-even output |
| Receivables | Money owed to the business by customers |
| Payables | Money the business owes suppliers |

## Budgets

**Value:** targets; control of spending; coordination; early warning through variances; allocation of resources; motivation when agreed. **Limits:** inaccurate forecasts; time-consuming; rigid; managers may pad budgets or spend to the limit; focus on the budget can harm quality.

**Variances:** revenue above budget is favourable; revenue below is adverse; costs above budget are adverse; costs below are favourable. Investigate the cause (for example a favourable labour variance may only reflect lower output).

Example: budget profit £100,000; actual £61,000. Revenue £30,000 adverse; materials £15,000 adverse; labour £6,000 favourable; total variance £39,000 adverse.

## Cash flow forecast

- Closing balance = opening balance + receipts − payments.
- Next month's opening balance = this month's closing balance.
- Example: opening £10,000; January net −£6,000 gives £4,000; February net −£7,000 gives −£3,000; March net +£15,000 gives £12,000. February needs an overdraft, delayed payments or faster collection.
- **Value:** early warning; supports loans; times big purchases. **Limits:** based on assumptions; uncertainty over sales and customer payments; needs updating.

## Break-even

| Formula | |
|---|---|
| Contribution per unit | price − variable cost per unit |
| Total contribution | contribution per unit × output |
| Profit | total contribution − fixed costs |
| Break-even output | fixed costs ÷ contribution per unit |
| Margin of safety (units) | actual output − break-even output |
| Output for target profit | (fixed costs + target profit) ÷ contribution per unit |

**Worked example.** Price £40; variable cost £22; fixed costs £90,000.

- Contribution £18; break-even **5,000 units** (revenue £200,000).
- At 6,500 units: margin of safety **1,500 units** (23.1%); total contribution £117,000; profit **£27,000**.
- Target profit £36,000 needs **7,000 units**.

**Chart:** output on the horizontal axis, £ on the vertical axis. Fixed cost line is horizontal; total cost starts at fixed costs and rises with variable cost; revenue starts at zero. The lines cross at break-even; margin of safety is the horizontal gap between actual output and break-even.

| Change | Effect on break-even |
|---|---|
| Higher price | Lower (revenue line steeper) |
| Lower price | Higher |
| Higher variable cost | Higher (total cost line steeper) |
| Higher fixed costs | Higher (cost lines shift up) |

**Value:** simple; shows risk; helps pricing and targets. **Limits:** assumes all output sells; constant prices and costs; single product; ignores demand.

## Profit margins

- Gross profit margin = gross profit ÷ revenue × 100
- Operating profit margin = operating profit ÷ revenue × 100
- Profit for the year margin = profit for the year ÷ revenue × 100

Compare with earlier years, targets and competitors. Example: gross margin 44% to 40% (higher purchase costs or price cuts) with operating margin steady at 10% means overheads fell as a percentage of revenue.

## Timing of cash

- **Receivables rise:** cash position weakens.
- **Payables rise:** cash position improves, but supplier relationships may suffer.
- Customers on 60 days' credit and suppliers paid in 30 days leave a 30-day gap to fund.
- Seasonal sales, tax bills and equipment purchases affect timing.

## Using financial data

Decide on orders (contribution), prices, finance and project choices; judge managers (variances). Limits: historical, may be inaccurate, hides non-financial factors, needs market context.

## Exam traps

- Costs above budget are **adverse**.
- Break-even uses contribution **per unit**.
- Include fixed costs in the total cost line.
- Name the profit margin you mean.
- A forecast opening balance is the previous closing balance.

## Self-test

1. Budgeted revenue is £80,000 and actual is £86,000. State the variance and whether it is favourable or adverse.
2. Budgeted materials cost is £30,000 and actual is £33,000. State the variance and type.
3. Opening balance £5,000, receipts £22,000, payments £25,000. Calculate the closing balance.
4. Price £15, variable cost £9. Calculate contribution per unit.
5. Fixed costs are £24,000. Calculate break-even output.
6. Sales are 5,000 units. Calculate the margin of safety.
7. Calculate profit at 5,000 units.
8. How many units are needed for a profit of £12,000?
9. Fixed costs rise by £6,000. What is the new break-even output?
10. Revenue £400,000; gross profit £140,000; operating profit £48,000. Calculate the gross and operating margins.
11. Give one limitation of break-even analysis.
12. Why does a rise in receivables worry a business?

### Answers

1. £6,000 favourable.
2. £3,000 adverse.
3. £5,000 + £22,000 − £25,000 = **£2,000**.
4. £15 − £9 = **£6**.
5. 24,000 ÷ 6 = **4,000 units**.
6. 5,000 − 4,000 = **1,000 units**.
7. 5,000 × £6 = £30,000 − £24,000 = **£6,000**.
8. (24,000 + 12,000) ÷ 6 = **6,000 units**.
9. 30,000 ÷ 6 = **5,000 units**.
10. Gross: 140 ÷ 400 × 100 = **35%**. Operating: 48 ÷ 400 × 100 = **12%**.
11. Any one: assumes all output is sold; assumes constant prices and costs; ignores demand; single product.
12. Customers are taking longer to pay, so cash comes in later and the business may be unable to pay its own bills.
