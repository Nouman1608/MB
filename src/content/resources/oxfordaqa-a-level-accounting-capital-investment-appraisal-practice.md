These questions cover **3.2.3 Capital investment appraisal** of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Accounting (9615) specification, Version 1.2, for International A-level exams from May/June 2026 onwards. The topic belongs to the International A-level only and is assessed in Paper 4: Accounting for analysis and decision making. Money is in dollars, the firms are made up, and present values are to the nearest dollar.

## Questions

**1.** (multiple choice) Tamberlow Plastics is appraising a new moulding machine. Which **one** of these should be left out of the project cash flows? **[1]**

A. Operator training in year 1
B. Lower energy bills
C. A share of existing factory rent charged to the machine
D. The machine's scrap value at the end of its life

**2.** (multiple choice) At a 15% cost of capital, which factor applies to cash received at the end of year 2? **[1]**

A. 0.870
B. 0.756
C. 0.658
D. 0.850

**3.**

**(a)** State the meaning of the term cost of capital. **[1]**
**(b)** Give **two** reasons why 1,000 received in three years' time is worth less than 1,000 received today. **[2]**

**4.** Abberley Cargo is buying a fleet of electric cargo bikes for 46,800. The bikes will generate net cash inflows of 14,400 a year. How long is the payback period? Give your answer in years and months. **[2]**

**5.** Orrinsby Joinery is appraising a computer-controlled router.

**(a)** The router costs 64,000 plus 1,500 delivery, paid now. The old panel saw will be sold now for 5,500. Last month Orrinsby paid 2,000 for a feasibility study. Calculate the year-0 net cash flow. **[2]**
**(b)** The forecast for year 1 shows: receipts from extra orders 92,000; timber 31,000; extra wages 18,500; depreciation 12,000; share of head-office costs 4,000; interest on the loan for the router 3,600; maintenance contract 2,900. Calculate the net cash flow for year 1. **[3]**

**6.** Calvering Dairy is considering a yoghurt line costing 132,000. Net cash inflows are forecast as: year 1 30,000; year 2 42,000; year 3 44,000; year 4 40,000; year 5 28,000. At its 9% cost of capital, the factors for years 1 to 5 are 0.917, 0.842, 0.772, 0.708 and 0.650.

**(a)** Calculate the payback period to the nearest month. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate the net present value. **[4]**
**(c)** State, with a reason, whether the line should be bought on financial grounds. **[1]**

**7.** Hepscott Gym plans to buy rowing machines for 88,000. They will bring in net cash inflows of 31,500 a year for four years, then be sold for 6,000. The cost of capital is 15%. Discount factors: year 1 0.870, year 2 0.756, year 3 0.658, year 4 0.572. Calculate the net present value and state whether the gym should buy the machines on financial grounds. **[5]**

**8.** Explain **one** benefit and **one** limitation for Hepscott Gym of using net present value to appraise the rowing machines. **[4]**

**9.** Mossbrae Nurseries is considering a polytunnel costing 50,000, with net cash inflows of 16,000, 18,000, 20,000 and 14,000 in years 1 to 4.

**(a)** Calculate the net present value at a cost of capital of 6%. Discount factors: 0.943, 0.890, 0.840, 0.792. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the net present value at a cost of capital of 16%. Discount factors: 0.862, 0.743, 0.641, 0.552. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain what your answers show about the cost of capital in investment decisions. **[2]**

**10.** Gadsworth Coaches can fund one project. Its cost of capital is 11%, giving factors of 0.901, 0.812, 0.731, 0.659 and 0.593 for years 1 to 5.

| Year | Project X: electric coach | Project Y: refurbish two diesel coaches |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | (180,000) | (110,000) |
| 1 | 30,000 | 45,000 |
| 2 | 40,000 | 42,000 |
| 3 | 55,000 | 38,000 |
| 4 | 60,000 | 20,000 |
| 5 | 70,000 | 10,000 |

Gadsworth mainly carries pupils to two schools. One school will favour low-emission operators when its contract is renewed in year 3. The electric coach needs a charging point and driver training, and its maker is new to the market. Gadsworth's cash balance is low.

**(a)** Calculate the payback period of each project to the nearest month. **[4]**
**(b)** Calculate the net present value of each project. **[4]**
**(c)** Recommend which project Gadsworth should choose, using financial and non-financial factors. **[4]**

**11.** Yarrowdale Pottery is considering an automated glazing line costing 120,000. The accountant forecasts net cash inflows of 36,000 a year for five years. At a 12% cost of capital she calculates a net present value of 9,780 and a payback period of 3 years 4 months. Discount factors: year 1 0.893, year 2 0.797, year 3 0.712, year 4 0.636, year 5 0.567.

A rival is launching cheaper factory-glazed tableware, so inflows in years 3 to 5 could be 25% lower. The line would replace five hand-glazers; two could be retrained as operators. Yarrowdale markets its pieces as "hand-finished". The line would cut glaze waste and energy use. Spare parts come from overseas and take six weeks. Yarrowdale runs a bank overdraft.

**(a)** Calculate the net present value if inflows in years 3 to 5 are 25% lower than forecast. **[3]**
**(b)** Evaluate whether Yarrowdale Pottery should invest in the glazing line. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** **C** -- the rent is paid anyway, so it is not an extra cash flow [1]
*Examiner insight:* Ask of each option: would this cash move only because of the project?

**2.** 1 ÷ 1.15² = 1 ÷ 1.3225 = **B, 0.756** [1]
*Examiner insight:* A and C are the year-1 and year-3 factors.

**3. (a)** The return that providers of finance (lenders and owners) require, so the minimum return a project must earn [1]
**(b)** Any two: money now can be invested to earn a return [1]; inflation erodes later money [1]; a future receipt might not arrive.
*Examiner insight:* Cost of capital is a percentage return, not the price of the asset.

**4.** 46,800 ÷ 14,400 = 3.25 years [1]; 0.25 × 12 = 3 months, so **3 years 3 months** [1]
*Examiner insight:* Convert the decimal to months; "3 years 25 months" is a common slip.

**5. (a)** 64,000 + 1,500 − 5,500 [1] = **(60,000)**; the 2,000 study is sunk and is excluded [1]
**(b)** Exclude depreciation, head-office costs and interest [1]; 92,000 − (31,000 + 18,500 + 2,900) = 92,000 − 52,400 [1] = **39,600** [1]
*Examiner insight:* Name the items you leave out, so your reasoning is visible.

**6. (a)** Cumulative: (102,000), (60,000), (16,000), then 24,000 at the end of year 4 [1]; 16,000 ÷ 40,000 × 12 = 4.8 months [1]; **3 years 5 months** [1]
**(b)** Present values (cash flow × factor): 27,510; 35,364 [1]; 33,968; 28,320; 18,200 [1]; total 143,362 [1]; NPV = 143,362 − 132,000 = **11,362** [1]
**(c)** The NPV is positive, so the line earns more than 9%: buy it on financial grounds [1]
*Examiner insight:* Divide 16,000 by year 4's inflow of 40,000, not the cumulative 24,000.

**7.** Year 4 cash flow = 31,500 + 6,000 = 37,500 [1]; present values 27,405, 23,814, 20,727 and 37,500 × 0.572 = 21,450 [1]; total 93,396 [1]; NPV = 93,396 − 88,000 = **5,396** [1]; positive, so buy the machines on financial grounds [1]
*Examiner insight:* Discount the resale value with year 4's factor; never add it at full value.

**8.** Benefit: NPV discounts each inflow at the 15% cost of capital [1], so the gym knows the machines beat that return even after later inflows lose value [1]. Limitation: it depends on the 15% rate being right [1]; a higher true rate could push the NPV below zero [1].
*Examiner insight:* Link each point to the gym; a one-word "accurate" or "complicated" is not developed.

**9. (a)** 15,088 + 16,020 + 16,800 + 11,088 = 58,996 [1]; NPV = **8,996** [1]
**(b)** 13,792 + 13,374 + 12,820 + 7,728 = 47,714 [1]; NPV = **(2,286)** [1]
**(c)** A higher cost of capital shrinks later inflows' present values, turning accept into reject [1]. So the decision depends on estimating the rate correctly, which is hard [1].
*Examiner insight:* Use both results: the switch from positive to negative is the point.

**10. (a)** X cumulative: (150,000), (110,000), (55,000), then 5,000 [1]; 3 years + 55,000 ÷ 60,000 × 12 = 11 months, so **3 years 11 months** [1]. Y cumulative: (65,000), (23,000), then 15,000 [1]; 2 years + 23,000 ÷ 38,000 × 12 = 7.3 months, so **2 years 7 months** [1]
**(b)** X present values 27,030 + 32,480 + 40,205 + 39,540 + 41,510 = 180,765 [1]; NPV = **765** [1]. Y present values 40,545 + 34,104 + 27,778 + 13,180 + 5,930 = 121,537 [1]; NPV = **11,537** [1]
**(c)** Y has the higher NPV and pays back 16 months sooner, which suits a low cash balance [1]. X barely covers 11%, so a small forecast shortfall makes it negative [1]. But the school's low-emission preference could cut Y's inflows from year 3 [1]. Choose Y unless that contract is at real risk; then X, after checking the new maker's reliability [1].
*Examiner insight:* Weigh a figure against a non-financial point, then commit to a choice.

**11. (a)** Years 3 to 5 inflow = 36,000 × 0.75 = 27,000 [1]; present values 32,148 + 28,692 + 19,224 + 17,172 + 15,309 = 112,545 [1]; NPV = **(7,455)** [1]
**(b)**
- Forecast NPV 9,780 is positive: the line returns more than 12% [1].
- The margin is thin (about 8% of the outlay); the 25% fall gives (7,455) [1].
- Payback of 3 years 4 months is late in a five-year life; 3 years 9 months in the lower case [1].
- With an overdraft, a 120,000 outlay strains liquidity and adds finance costs [1].
- Three hand-glazers lose their jobs, hurting morale and local standing [1].
- Automation weakens the "hand-finished" selling point as a cheaper rival arrives, making the lower forecast likelier [1].
- In favour: less waste and energy, and a more consistent finish [1].
- Six-week waits for spare parts could halt production [1].
- Judgement: the NPV rests on optimistic forecasts and risks the brand, so do not invest now; revisit with local servicing and evidence customers accept machine glazing [1].
*Examiner insight:* Use your answer to (a), and tie it to the brand and competition facts.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Question 1: rejecting scrap value because it arrives late.
- Question 5: deducting depreciation or loan interest as if they were cash payments.
- Question 6: writing 0.4 of a year as 4 months; the fraction must be multiplied by 12.
- Question 7: adding the 6,000 resale value without discounting it.
- Question 10: comparing undiscounted totals instead of the NPVs.
- Question 11: listing points for and against with no final decision.
