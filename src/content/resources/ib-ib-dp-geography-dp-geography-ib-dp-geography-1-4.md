This guide goes deeper into sub-topic 1.4 of Option A, Freshwater, in IB Diploma Programme Geography (first assessments 2019): water management futures. The [1.4 revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-geography-freshwater-water-management-futures-revision-notes/) already set out supply-side and demand-side strategies, the principles of integrated water resources management, the costs and benefits of large dams with the Three Gorges example, and the Colorado, Murray-Darling and Singapore cases, along with wetlands and managed aquifer recharge. This page adds tools for thinking about the **future**: projecting when demand overtakes supply, the special problem of fossil groundwater, a framework for judging dams, a shrinking lake, and how river basin organisations try to manage shared water. Practise with the [1.4 practice questions](/resources/ib-ib-dp-geography-dp-geography-ib-dp-geography-1-4-practice/); the [Freshwater option study guide](/resources/ib-ib-dp-geography-freshwater-study-guide/) gives the overview.

## What this sub-topic asks you to do

- Evaluate strategies for managing water sustainably, including large multipurpose schemes and integrated basin management.
- Consider the future of groundwater, lakes and wetlands.
- Judge how far management can secure water for the future under growing demand and a changing climate.

## Projecting the gap between supply and demand

Future water security depends on how fast demand grows relative to reliable supply. A simple projection shows why demand management matters.

**Worked example (invented city).** Reliable supply is 500 million cubic metres a year. Demand is 400 million cubic metres and growing by 2 per cent a year.

```
Demand after n years = 400 x 1.02 to the power n
Demand exceeds supply when 1.02 to the power n > 500 / 400 = 1.25
n > log(1.25) / log(1.02) = 11.3, so in year 12

If demand management cuts growth to 1 per cent a year:
n > log(1.25) / log(1.01) = 22.4, so in year 23
```

Halving the growth rate roughly doubles the time before a shortfall. That buys time to develop new supply, but it does not remove the need for it. Two further points belong in an evaluative answer:

- Reliable supply itself may fall. If a warmer climate reduces river flow in the dry season, the 500 figure shrinks and the gap arrives sooner.
- Averages hide drought years. A city can face shortage in a dry year long before average demand exceeds average supply, so planners design for drought conditions, not the mean.

## Groundwater: renewable and fossil

Groundwater supplies a large share of the world's irrigation and drinking water, and its future is less visible than that of rivers.

- **Unconfined aquifers** are recharged from the surface above them, so they respond to rainfall and to pollution within years.
- **Confined aquifers** lie between impermeable layers and are recharged slowly, often from distant outcrops.
- **Fossil groundwater** was stored under wetter past climates and receives almost no recharge today. Using it is closer to mining a resource than managing a renewable one.

**Example: Libya's Great Man-Made River.** Libya pipes water from the Nubian Sandstone Aquifer System beneath the Sahara to its coastal cities. The aquifer is shared with Egypt, Sudan and Chad and is largely fossil water. The scheme supplies a dry country with water it could not otherwise obtain, but every cubic metre withdrawn is a cubic metre that will not return on any human timescale, so its long-term future depends on how quickly the stored water is drawn down and on cooperation between the countries sharing it.

The sustainability test for any aquifer: compare annual abstraction with annual recharge. If abstraction is larger, water tables fall, pumping costs rise, wells fail, land may subside and coastal aquifers may suffer saline intrusion.

## A framework for judging large dams

The revision notes list the benefits and costs of a large dam. To evaluate one, organise those points by **stakeholder** and **time**.

| Stakeholder | Short-term effect | Long-term effect |
|---|---|---|
| Urban users and industry | Reliable water and electricity | Supply depends on the reservoir not silting up |
| Farmers downstream | Irrigation and flood control | Loss of fertile silt that floods once delivered |
| Displaced communities | Loss of homes, land and heritage | Outcome depends on compensation and resettlement |
| Fishers and ecosystems | Changed flow and blocked migration | Delta erosion, lost wetland habitat |
| Government | Large debt or investment | Revenue from power; political prestige |

In 2000 the **World Commission on Dams** published *Dams and Development*. It found that large dams had often delivered important benefits but at social and environmental costs that were frequently unacceptable, borne largely by displaced people and downstream communities. It recommended assessing all options before choosing a dam and involving affected people in decisions. That is a useful authority to cite when you argue that the way a dam is planned matters as much as the dam itself.

## Lakes under pressure: Lake Chad

Lake Chad in the Sahel is shared by Chad, Cameroon, Niger and Nigeria. It is shallow, so its area responds strongly to changes in inflow, and it shrank dramatically from the 1960s. Drier decades in the Sahel reduced inflow from its main river, the Chari, and growing irrigation withdrawals added pressure. The **Lake Chad Basin Commission**, founded in 1964, coordinates the member states and has studied proposals to transfer water from the Congo basin, which raise questions of cost, environmental effects and who controls the water.

What the case teaches about water futures:

- Shallow lakes are highly sensitive to climate variability and to upstream use.
- Shrinking water affects fishing, farming and herding livelihoods, and can push people into competition for what remains.
- Large engineering solutions such as inter-basin transfers are proposed where local demand management alone cannot restore the lake.

## River basin organisations

Integrated drainage basin management needs an institution that spans the basin. The **Mekong River Commission**, set up in 1995 by Cambodia, Laos, Thailand and Vietnam, shares data and reviews proposed developments on the lower Mekong. China and Myanmar, upstream, are dialogue partners rather than members, so dams built on the upper river in China sit outside the Commission's agreement. The example shows both the value of a basin body (shared data, a forum for disagreement) and its central weakness: it is only as strong as its membership and its powers. Where the most powerful upstream state stays outside, integrated management of the whole basin is incomplete.

## Restoring wetlands at scale

The **Comprehensive Everglades Restoration Plan**, authorised by the US Congress in 2000, aims to restore more natural water flow through the Everglades in Florida after a century of drainage for farming and cities. It shows that restoration is possible but slow and expensive, and that it must balance the needs of farms, cities and the ecosystem.

## Annotated model answer

*To what extent can integrated drainage basin management secure future water supplies?* **(sample conclusion)**

"Integrated management offers the best framework for future security because it treats the basin as one system, so upstream and downstream needs, groundwater and ecosystems are considered together **[strength linked to the concept]**. The Murray-Darling plan shows it can return water to rivers once over-allocation is recognised **[located evidence]**. However, its success depends on institutions with power over the whole basin: on the Mekong, the absence of the upstream state from the Commission limits what can be achieved **[limitation with a contrasting example]**. It also cannot create water; where demand is projected to exceed supply, as in fast-growing dry cities, it must be combined with demand reduction and new sources **[recognises limits]**. Integrated management is therefore necessary but not sufficient **[clear judgement]**."

## Common mistakes

- Treating all groundwater as renewable; fossil aquifers are effectively finite.
- Projecting future demand without mentioning that supply may also change.
- Listing dam costs and benefits without saying who bears each.
- Assuming a river basin organisation manages the whole basin when key states are not members.
- Writing about "the future" without any time scale or scenario.

## Exam technique

- **Evaluate** a strategy with criteria: sustainability, equity, cost, effectiveness and adaptability to climate change.
- **To what extent** needs a clear position supported by at least two located examples.
- **Discuss** future scenarios by setting out more than one possible outcome and what each depends on.
- When given projections, comment on their assumptions: constant growth rates, unchanged supply, average rather than drought conditions.
