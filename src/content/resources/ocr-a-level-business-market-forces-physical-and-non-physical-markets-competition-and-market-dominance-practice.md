## Questions

**1.** Define the term market dominance. **[2]**

**2.** Distinguish between a merger and an acquisition. **[3]**

**3.** (a) Explain one reason why a firm might enter a market. **[2]**
(b) Analyse one barrier to entry and one barrier to exit in a market. **[4]**

**4.** Ormsgill Bakery is a village shop. In 2024 its sales revenue was £420,000 in a local market worth £2.4m. In 2025 a rival bakery-café opened nearby. Ormsgill's sales revenue rose to £432,000 and the market was worth £2.7m.

**(a)** Calculate Ormsgill's market share in each year. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate the growth of the market from 2024 to 2025. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain why Ormsgill's market share fell although its sales revenue rose. **[2]**

**5.** Distinguish between a physical and a non-physical market, including online and digital. **[3]**

**6.** Thrushwell Books sells a paperback at £18. Each book costs £9 to buy. A shop would have fixed costs of £9,000 a month. An online store would have fixed costs of £2,500 a month and a delivery and packing cost of £2.50 a book.

**(a)** Calculate the monthly break-even output for the shop and for the online store (to the next whole book). **[4]**
**(b)** Calculate the monthly output at which the two would give equal profit, and say what this shows. **[2]**

**7.** Stavordale Foods has 34% of a regional grocery market. It merges with Pell Fresh, which has 22%. The next three firms have 18%, 14% and 12%.

**(a)** Calculate the combined market share. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain how the merger could lead to a dominant firm. **[4]**

**8.** Explain how market dominance is restricted and regulated in the UK. **[5]**

**9.** Brinsworth Orchards sells 70% of its apples to Stavordale Foods, which now holds 56% of the regional grocery market. Evaluate the impact of this dominant firm on Brinsworth Orchards. **[8]**

**10.** Thrushwell Books is an independent bookseller with a shop in a market town and a small website. Evaluate the impact of competition at local, national and global levels on Thrushwell Books. **[9]**

**11.** Ormsgill Bakery (Question 4) faces a new bakery-café charging about 10% less on average, and a supermarket in-store bakery on the edge of the village. Ormsgill has loyal regular customers and sells only from its shop. The owner is choosing between cutting prices by 10% and launching online ordering with local delivery. Recommend and justify which response Ormsgill should choose. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** One firm (or a very small group of firms) holds a very large share of the market [1] and has power over price, output or rivals as a result [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Answers that give only "a big firm" tend to earn less than ones that mention power over the market.

**2.** A merger is where two firms agree to combine into one business [1]. An acquisition is where one firm buys control of another [1]. The difference is that a merger is a joining by agreement, while an acquisition is one firm taking over the other, possibly against its wishes [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* A distinction needs the contrast stated; two separate definitions can leave the final point unearned.

**3.** (a) A firm may enter because profits in the market are high [1], which offers a return that its current market does not [1]. **[2]**
(b) Barrier to entry, for example high start-up costs [1], which deter small firms without finance, so fewer rivals appear [1]. Barrier to exit, for example a long lease [1], which keeps a failing firm in the market and the competition intense [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Analysis points score best when each barrier is followed by a consequence for the market, not just named.

**4.** (a) Market share = sales revenue ÷ market size × 100 [1]. 2024: 420,000 ÷ 2,400,000 × 100 = **17.5%** [1]. 2025: 432,000 ÷ 2,700,000 × 100 = **16.0%** [1]. **[3]**
(b) (2.7 − 2.4) ÷ 2.4 × 100 = **12.5%** [1]. **[1]**
(c) Ormsgill's sales revenue grew by only 2.9% [1], while the market grew by 12.5%, so rivals took most of the extra demand [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Showing the formula with figures before the answer helps a reader give credit even if a slip follows.

**5.** A physical market is one where buyers and sellers meet in a place, such as a shop [1]. A non-physical market has no shared place and trades by website, app or other digital means [1]. Online means trading over the internet, while digital is wider and includes digital products and app-based trading [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* The words "including online and digital" ask for both terms to be separated, not just one.

**6.** (a) Shop contribution = 18 − 9 = £9 [1]; break-even = 9,000 ÷ 9 = **1,000 books** [1]. Online contribution = 18 − 9 − 2.50 = £6.50 [1]; break-even = 2,500 ÷ 6.50 = 384.6, so **385 books** [1]. **[4]**
(b) 9q − 9,000 = 6.5q − 2,500, so q = 6,500 ÷ 2.5 = **2,600 books** [1]. Below 2,600 the online store earns more profit; above it the shop earns more, because its contribution per book is higher [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A comparison of break-even points scores better when it goes on to say which channel suits the expected volume.

**7.** (a) 34 + 22 = **56%** [1]. **[1]**
(b) The merger removes a rival from the market [1]. The combined share is over three times that of the next largest rival (56 ÷ 18 = 3.1) [1]. A larger firm may gain economies of scale and lower costs [1], and it may have greater power over suppliers and prices, so rivals struggle to compete [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the figures from the stem to show scale; a general statement that mergers create dominance is not enough.

**8.** In general terms, the UK has a competition authority and sector regulators that set and apply competition rules [1]. They can investigate proposed mergers [1] and can block them or allow them with conditions [1]. They can investigate anti-competitive behaviour, such as agreeing prices or abusing a strong position [1], and penalise firms found to have broken the rules [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Describe the powers in general terms; naming exact thresholds or Acts you are unsure of can cost credibility.

**9.** Dependence: 70% of Brinsworth's output goes to one buyer [1], so it has little bargaining power and may have to accept lower prices [1]. Benefit: a large, regular order gives stable sales revenue and planning certainty [1], and it may let Brinsworth afford investment [1]. Risk: if Stavordale delists Brinsworth, 70% of sales revenue is lost [1]. Brinsworth could reduce the risk by finding other buyers or selling direct to customers [1]. Judgement: the impact is probably more negative than positive in the long term, because the power imbalance is large [1]; the result depends on whether Brinsworth can build other outlets quickly [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Evaluation earns credit when the judgement depends on a named factor in the case, such as the 70% share.

**10.** Local: a rival bookshop or a charity shop nearby attracts shoppers and can force a price cut [1]; loyal customers and personal service reduce the effect [1]. National: chains and supermarkets use scale to offer lower prices and wider range [1], and Thrushwell's small website cannot match their marketing [1]. Global: global online retailers offer wide choice, fast delivery and easy price comparison [1], which can pull away customers who are price sensitive [1]. Stakeholders: lost sales revenue may threaten jobs and the owner's profit, while customers gain from lower prices [1]. Judgement: global online competition is likely to be the greatest threat because customers compare prices easily and Thrushwell has little pricing power [1]; the effect is smaller if Thrushwell's customers value local events and advice [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* Naming all three levels is only the start; the judgement must say which matters most and why.

**11.** Price cut option: a 10% cut means Ormsgill needs about 11% more customers just to keep the same sales revenue (1 ÷ 0.9 = 1.11) [1], so profit will probably fall [1], and rivals may cut again [1]. Online option: it reaches new customers beyond the village [1], and loyal customers may like delivery [1], but there are set-up costs and delivery costs, and fresh bread may be harder to deliver [1]. Recommendation: choose online ordering and local delivery, not the price cut [1]. Justification: the market is growing at 12.5%, so Ormsgill needs a way to reach extra demand, not just defend existing sales [1]. Risk: if online orders stay low, the fixed costs will not be covered, so test it with a small trial first [1]. Judgement: the choice is better because it adds a channel rather than giving away margin, as long as the extra volume is high enough to cover the extra costs [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* A recommendation needs a choice, a reason tied to the case data and a weakness; options listed without a choice tend to limit the credit.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Defining market dominance as being large, without power over price, output or rivals.
- Giving two definitions for a distinction question without stating the difference.
- Naming a barrier to entry or exit without saying what it does to the market.
- Judging a firm on sales revenue growth alone and ignoring market growth.
- Forgetting delivery or returns when calculating online contribution.
- Stopping a calculation at break-even without interpreting what it means.
- Describing regulation using invented Acts or limits.
- Writing balanced points but no judgement in an evaluate question.
- Recommending an option without a justification from the case data.
