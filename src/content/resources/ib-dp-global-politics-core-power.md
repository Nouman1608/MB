## Defining power

The guide's starting point is that power can be seen as an **ability to effect change**. It adds a second view: power is not a thing someone owns but a feature of **relations between people** inside a social organisation. Much of the debate below is a choice between them.

### Power as capacity or as relationship

- **Capacity view:** power is what an actor *has* -- troops, money, votes, ideas. You can count it and compare it.
- **Relational view:** power exists only *between* actors. A large army gives no power over a state that does not fear it.

A related distinction is **resources versus outcomes**. An actor can hold vast resources and still fail to get what it wants. The United States kept troops in Afghanistan for almost 20 years. Yet the Taliban took Kabul on 15 August 2021 as US forces withdrew. Resources did not turn into the outcome sought.

### Thinkers the guide suggests

The guide names eleven thinkers who might be useful. You need not know them all; each offers a different way in.

| Thinker | Core idea about power | Useful for |
|---|---|---|
| Steven Lukes | *Power: A Radical View* (1974) sets out three dimensions: winning open decisions; keeping issues off the agenda; and shaping what people want so conflict never appears | Seeing hidden power |
| Joseph Nye | Coined "soft power" in *Bound to Lead* (1990): getting others to want what you want through attraction. Later developed "smart power" | Hard, soft and smart power |
| John Mearsheimer | Offensive realist: power means material capability, above all military strength and the wealth and population behind it; great powers seek to maximise relative power | Capacity view, hard power |
| Hannah Arendt | *On Violence* (1970): power is the human ability to act together; it belongs to a group. Violence is not power and appears where power is being lost | Power with |
| Michel Foucault | Power is exercised, not owned. It runs through institutions, knowledge and everyday rules (e.g. prisons, schools, medicine) and produces behaviour | Diffuse, structural power |
| Antonio Gramsci | Hegemony: a ruling group stays dominant mainly through consent built in culture, education and the media, not force alone | Cultural power |
| bell hooks | Criticised equating power with domination; valued power as the capacity to act and transform, and saw the margins as a site of resistance | Power to, power with |
| Eric Liu | Power is the capacity to get others to act as you would want; ordinary citizens must learn how power works in order to use it | Civic power |
| Walter Mignolo | Decolonial thinker: a "colonial matrix of power" still ranks knowledge, peoples and economies, favouring the West | Critique of dominant definitions |
| Hanna Pitkin | *Wittgenstein and Justice* (1972): separates "power to" (a capacity) from "power over" (a relation to others) | Power to / power over |
| Yaqing Qin | *A Relational Theory of World Politics* (2018): actors exist in webs of relations; power includes the ability to shape and use those relations | Relational power |

Lukes' first dimension is Robert Dahl's 1957 idea: A has power over B if A gets B to do what B would not otherwise do. His second draws on Peter Bachrach and Morton Baratz, who pointed to "non-decisions" that keep issues off the agenda.

## Hard, soft and smart power

**Hard power** is **coercion**: using force, threats or penalties to make others act. The guide's examples are military interventions and economic sanctions.

- Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine in February 2022 is military hard power.
- The sanctions that the EU, the UK, the US and others imposed on Russia in response are economic hard power.

**Soft power** is **persuasion** and attraction. The guide's examples are cultural influence, ideology and economic aid. Nye names three sources: a country's culture, its political values and foreign policies that others see as legitimate.

- South Korea's cultural reach (the Korean Wave) grew through K-pop and film. *Parasite* became the first non-English-language film to win the Oscar for Best Picture in 2020.

**Smart power** is an eclectic mix of hard and soft power, chosen to fit the aim. The guide's examples are approaches to climate change and negotiations.

- The Joint Comprehensive Plan of Action (2015) on Iran's nuclear programme came after years of sanctions (hard) combined with multilateral diplomacy (soft).
- The EU leads by example in climate talks (soft), but its Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism, which began a reporting phase in October 2023, puts a charge on carbon-heavy imports (economic pressure).

### Worked example: classifying economic aid

**Task:** China's Belt and Road Initiative (launched 2013) funds ports, railways and power plants abroad. Classify it.

1. **Identify the resource.** Loans, investment and construction -- economic resources.
2. **Ask how it works.** If partners join because they want the projects, it attracts: soft power.
3. **Test for coercion.** Critics argue that debt can become leverage. They cite Sri Lanka's 2017 lease of Hambantota port to a Chinese company for 99 years. Other researchers dispute the "debt-trap" label and say borrowing governments made their own choices.
4. **Conclude.** The same aid can be soft or hard depending on how it is used and seen. That is a limit of the hard/soft split, and it is worth saying so in an answer.

## Structural and relational power

**Structural power** comes from an actor's **position** in a system of rules or institutions. The guide's examples are UN Security Council membership and heads of state.

- Under Article 27 of the UN Charter, substantive Security Council decisions need nine votes and no veto from the five permanent members (China, France, Russia, the UK and the US). On 25 February 2022 Russia vetoed a draft resolution on its invasion of Ukraine. The position, not the argument, decided the outcome.
- A head of state holds power from the office. A US president can veto bills passed by Congress, whoever holds the post.
- The term is often linked to Susan Strange, who saw power in control of four structures: security, production, finance and knowledge.

**Relational power** comes from **ties between actors**. The guide's examples are alliances and collective civil movements.

- NATO's Article 5 treats an attack on one member as an attack on all. Finland (2023) and Sweden (2024) joined after Russia's 2022 invasion to gain that relationship.
- Fridays for Future grew from one school strike in Sweden in August 2018 into a global youth movement. Its power came from numbers and networks, not formal position.

Structures and relations interact. After Russia's veto, 141 states voted in the General Assembly on 2 March 2022 to demand Russian withdrawal. That was relational pressure working around a structural block. In April 2022 the Assembly also agreed that it will meet after any veto is cast.

## Economic, political, social and cultural power

| Type | Rests on | Real example |
|---|---|---|
| Economic | Control of money, markets, trade and supply chains | US controls on exporting advanced chips to China (October 2022); China's export controls on gallium and germanium (2023) |
| Political | Authority to make and enforce binding decisions | Security Council resolutions; a legislature passing law; the EU setting rules for its single market |
| Social | Networks, norms, status and the ability to mobilise people | The International Campaign to Abolish Nuclear Weapons, awarded the 2017 Nobel Peace Prize |
| Cultural | Shaping values, identity and taste | Hollywood, Bollywood and the Korean Wave; global reach of the English language |

These overlap: sanctions are economic *and* hard, and cultural power often feeds soft power. Say which type dominates in your case, and why.

## Power to, power over, power with

- **Power over** is control of others: getting them to do what they would not choose. Sanctions and military threats fit here. This is Dahl's and Lukes' focus.
- **Power to** is the capacity to act or achieve something: a state's ability to vaccinate its population, or citizens' ability to vote. Pitkin separates it from power over.
- **Power with** is collective power built through cooperation. The term is usually traced to Mary Parker Follett in the 1920s, and it echoes Arendt. The Alliance of Small Island States (formed 1990) pushed for the 1.5°C goal. The Paris Agreement (2015) commits parties to pursue efforts to limit warming to 1.5°C.

## Using the classifications as tools

The guide asks you to judge these categories, not just use them.

**Advantages**
- They give precise vocabulary instead of "X is powerful".
- They reveal non-military power, such as a youth movement or a film industry.
- Comparing types shows why a strong actor can fail.

**Limitations**
- Categories blur. Aid, trade and sanctions shift between soft and hard.
- Soft power is hard to measure. It lives in how others see you, and governments do not fully control it.
- Most terms come from Western, state-centred scholarship. Mignolo and Qin argue that this hides colonial hierarchies and relational ways of thinking.
- Lukes' third dimension warns that the most effective power may leave no visible trace to classify.

## How this is examined

Paper 1 (1 hour 15 minutes; 30% at SL, 20% at HL) is a source-based paper on the core topics: four sources, four compulsory structured questions, 25 marks. One source is always non-textual. Power questions may ask you to explain a term, compare sources or evaluate a claim. Paper 2 section B essays integrate the core topics with the thematic studies. Know the command terms: *distinguish* (AO2) means make the differences clear; *evaluate* (AO3) means weigh strengths and limitations.

## Common errors

- Treating all economic power as soft power. Sanctions are coercive; the guide lists them as hard power.
- Calling smart power "a mix" without saying which tools were combined and for what aim.
- Confusing structural power with power from structures of the economy only. The guide's examples are institutional positions such as a permanent seat.
- Mixing up power over and power with. A coalition of equals is power with; a coalition used to pressure an outsider may also be power over that outsider.
- Naming a thinker without applying the idea to your case.
- Vague examples. Give the actor, the action and the year.
