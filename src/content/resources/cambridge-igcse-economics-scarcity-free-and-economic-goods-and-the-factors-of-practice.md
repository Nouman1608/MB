## Questions

**1.** Define the economic problem. **[2]**

**2.** Explain how the economic problem affects a producer. **[3]**

**3.** The government of Country B (invented) expects tax revenue of 120 units. It wants to spend 70 on health, 55 on education and 25 on defence.

**(a)** Calculate the shortfall between planned spending and revenue. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why borrowing to cover the shortfall does not remove the economic problem. **[2]**

**4.** Explain the difference between a free good and an economic good. **[3]**

**5.** State whether each of the following is best described as a free good or an economic good, and give a reason.

**(a)** Air on an open hillside. **[1]**
**(b)** Water piped to homes in a large city. **[1]**
**(c)** Vaccinations given at no charge to patients at a clinic. **[2]**

**6.** For each of the following, give the factor of production it represents and the payment it earns: a field, a delivery van, a driver employed by a firm, and the person who set up the firm and bears its risk. **[4]**

**7.** A small tailor (invented) has yearly revenue of 90,000. It pays wages of 40,000 and rent of 12,000. It has a loan of 50,000 at an interest rate of 6%.

**(a)** Calculate the yearly interest paid. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the profit. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate profit as a percentage of revenue, to one decimal place. **[1]**

**8.** Explain **two** influences on the geographical mobility of labour. **[4]**

**9.** A worker in Town X could earn 3,000 a year more in Town Y. Housing in Y costs 1,200 more a year. Moving costs 5,400 once.

**(a)** Calculate the worker's net gain each year. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the number of years needed to recover the cost of moving. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain **one** reason why the worker may still choose not to move. **[2]**

**10.** A mill (invented) begins the year with 300 looms. Looms lose 10% of their number each year through wear. The mill buys 24 new looms.

**(a)** Calculate depreciation. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate net investment. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the closing stock of looms. **[1]**
**(d)** Explain how output could rise even though the stock of looms fell. **[3]**

**11.** Country B has a working-age population of 5.0 million and a labour force of 3.1 million. A year later, working-age population is 5.2 million and the labour force is 3.4 million.

**(a)** Calculate the participation rate (labour force as a percentage of working-age population) in each year, to one decimal place. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage growth of the labour force, to one decimal place. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain **two** causes of an increase in the quantity of labour. **[4]**
**(d)** Discuss whether a larger labour force will necessarily raise output. **[5]**

**12.** A region (invented) has 5,000 hectares of farmland producing 2.5 tonnes per hectare. Each year erosion removes 2% of the farmland. The region reclaims 60 hectares. Fertiliser then raises output to 3.0 tonnes per hectare.

**(a)** Calculate the farmland area after erosion and reclamation. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate total output before and after the changes. **[2]**
**(c)** Discuss whether raising the quality of land is a more reliable way of raising farm output than raising the quantity of land. **[5]**

## Answers

**1.** Resources are finite [1] and wants are unlimited, so choices have to be made [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Both halves of the definition are needed; saying only "there is not enough" does not show the contrast between resources and wants.

**2.** A producer has limited inputs such as finance, workers and machinery [1]. It wants to raise output and profit without limit [1]. It must choose what to produce and which inputs to use, giving up other uses of the same inputs [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* A producer-specific answer links the idea to firms' resources. A generic definition of scarcity does not answer "how does it affect a producer".

**3. (a)** Planned spending = 70 + 55 + 25 = 150 [1]. Shortfall = 150 − 120 = **30 units** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Borrowing has to be repaid, using resources in the future [1]. Total resources are still limited, and wants stay unlimited, so choices remain [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the addition before the subtraction so the shortfall can be traced. For the explanation, say what happens in the future, not just that borrowing is "limited".

**4.** A free good is not scarce, so no resources are given up to obtain it [1] and there is no price [1]. An economic good is scarce, uses resources to supply and has an opportunity cost [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* A question on the difference between two things needs both sides. Writing only one definition is incomplete, however well written.

**5. (a)** **Free good**: it is available in unlimited quantity and uses no resources to supply [1].
**(b)** **Economic good**: pipes, plants and workers are used to supply it [1].
**(c)** **Economic good** [1] because doctors, equipment and vaccines use resources even though the patient pays nothing [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A zero price to the user is not enough to call something free. The reason must refer to resources used or not used.

**6.** Field: land, rent [1]. Delivery van: capital, interest [1]. Driver: labour, wages [1]. Person who set up the firm: enterprise, profit [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* The factor and its reward are two separate points, so check that both are given and that they match.

**7. (a)** Interest = 50,000 × 0.06 [1] = **3,000** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Profit = 90,000 − (40,000 + 12,000 + 3,000) [1] = 90,000 − 55,000 = **35,000** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** 35,000 ÷ 90,000 × 100 = **38.9%** [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the subtraction of all three costs and write each step, so that your method is visible even if a figure slips.

**8.** Any two influences, each with an explanation, for example:
Cost of moving and housing: moving is expensive and housing may be dearer in a new area [1], so a worker with a small wage gain does not move [1].
Family and community ties: workers with children in school or relatives nearby are reluctant to leave [1], so labour stays where it is even when jobs are better elsewhere [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* "Explain" needs a reason and a consequence, so naming an influence alone is not enough. Keep to geographical mobility rather than occupational.

**9. (a)** Net gain = 3,000 − 1,200 [1] = **1,800 a year** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Years = 5,400 ÷ 1,800 [1] = **3 years** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Any one reason, developed: family ties or a partner's job in Town X [1] outweigh a net gain that takes three years to repay [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A good answer to a part like (c) uses the figures. A reason tied to the three-year payback shows more than a general statement about "not wanting to move".

**10. (a)** 300 × 0.10 = **30** [1].
**(b)** 24 − 30 = **−6** [1].
**(c)** 300 + 24 − 30 = **294 looms** [1].
**(d)** The new looms may be of a better design or newer technology [1], so each loom produces more [1], and quality gains can outweigh the fall in quantity, so total output rises [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* In (d) the key idea is quality versus quantity. State both, then say which dominates.

**11. (a)** Year 1: 3.1 ÷ 5.0 × 100 = **62.0%** [1]. Year 2: 3.4 ÷ 5.2 × 100 = **65.4%** [1].
**(b)** (3.4 − 3.1) ÷ 3.1 × 100 = **9.7%** [1].
**(c)** Any two, each explained: population growth or immigration adds people of working age [1], so more can join the labour force [1]. Higher participation, for example more people taking jobs because of flexible hours [1], means a bigger share of the working-age population is working or seeking work [1].
**(d)** Argument for: more workers can produce more, if they have tools and land [1]. Argument against: if capital and land do not rise, each worker has less to work with and output per worker may fall [1]. Skills matter: untrained workers may add little [1]. Depends on whether the jobs exist or workers stay unemployed [1]. Judgement: output rises only if the extra labour is matched by capital, skills and demand [1]. **[12]**
*Examiner insight:* An evaluation needs a judgement that follows from the arguments. Listing points for and against, without saying what output depends on, stops short of that.

**12. (a)** Erosion = 5,000 × 0.02 = 100 hectares [1]. Net change = 60 − 100 = −40 [1]. New area = **4,960 hectares** [1].
**(b)** Before: 5,000 × 2.5 = **12,500 tonnes** [1]. After: 4,960 × 3.0 = **14,880 tonnes** [1].
**(c)** Quality: a 20% rise in yield (2.5 to 3.0) more than offsets a 0.8% loss of area, so output rose by 19.0% [1]. Quality gains work with the land already owned, whereas new land is limited and reclaiming it is costly [1]. Against: fertiliser can harm soil in the long run and raises costs [1], and erosion continues unless it is stopped [1]. Judgement: raising quality is more reliable provided it is sustainable, but the area must also be protected [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the figures from part (b) in the discussion. A conclusion that names a condition ("provided it is sustainable") reads as a judgement rather than a repeat of earlier points.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Stating one half of a definition when both parts are needed.
- Treating a zero price as proof of a free good.
- Giving the right factor but the wrong reward.
- Writing that money is capital.
- Forgetting that profit is what remains after all other payments.
- Listing influences on mobility without explaining the effect.
- Mixing up geographical and occupational mobility.
- Giving a quantity change when the question asks about quality, or the reverse.
- Reporting a calculation without the working or without units.
- Ending an evaluation without a conclusion that depends on stated conditions.
