## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** From which structure is ADH released into the blood?

A: Hypothalamus

B: Anterior pituitary gland

C: Posterior pituitary gland

D: Collecting duct **[1]**

**2.** State where urea is produced in the body and the process that produces it. **[2]**

**3.** Which substance is normally **absent** from the glomerular filtrate of a healthy person?

A: Glucose

B: Urea

C: Plasma proteins such as albumin

D: Sodium ions **[1]**

**4.** In a model of glucagon signalling, one active protein kinase A molecule activates 20 enzyme molecules. Each of these activates 20 molecules of a second enzyme, and each of those activates 20 molecules of the final enzyme.

**(a)** Calculate the number of final enzyme molecules activated. **[1]**

**(b)** State the importance of this effect. **[1]**

**5.** Describe how a test strip shows the concentration of glucose in a sample. **[3]**

**6.** Explain why blood in the glomerulus is at a high hydrostatic pressure. **[2]**

### Section B: longer questions

**7.**

**(a)** Name the part of the nephron in which ultrafiltration takes place. **[1]**

**(b)** Describe and explain how the structure of the glomerulus and Bowman's capsule is related to ultrafiltration. **[4]**

**8.**

**(a)** Explain how glucose is reabsorbed from the filtrate in the proximal convoluted tubule. **[4]**

**(b)** A drug inhibits the sodium–potassium pumps of PCT cells. Suggest why glucose then appears in the urine. **[2]**

**9.** A student drinks 1 dm³ of water in a few minutes.

**(a)** Explain how the volume of urine produced changes over the next hour. **[4]**

**(b)** A person cannot make ADH. Suggest and explain the effect on their urine. **[2]**

**10.** Describe how glucagon binding to a liver cell leads to the breakdown of glycogen. **[5]**

**11.** The blood glucose concentration of a person was measured after a meal eaten at time 0.

| Time / min | 0 | 30 | 60 | 90 | 120 | 150 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Blood glucose / mmol dm⁻³ | 5.0 | 7.8 | 6.9 | 5.8 | 4.9 | 5.0 |

**(a)** Calculate the mean rate of fall in blood glucose between 30 and 90 minutes. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain how insulin causes the fall between 30 and 120 minutes. **[4]**

**(c)** Suggest and explain why blood glucose rises slightly between 120 and 150 minutes. **[2]**

**12.**

**(a)** Explain why it is important to keep core body temperature within narrow limits. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain why having two hormones with opposite effects gives finer control of blood glucose than a single hormone. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** C [1]

**2.** In the liver [1], by deamination of excess amino acids [1].

**3.** C [1]

**4.**

**(a)** 20 × 20 × 20 = **8000** [1]

**(b)** The signal is amplified, so a very small concentration of hormone causes a large, rapid release of glucose [1].

**5.** Glucose oxidase on the strip catalyses the oxidation of glucose, producing hydrogen peroxide [1]. Peroxidase catalyses the reaction of hydrogen peroxide with a colourless chromogen to form a coloured compound [1]. The colour is compared with a chart: the more glucose, the darker the colour [1].

**6.** The afferent arteriole bringing blood in is wider than the efferent arteriole taking it away [1]. Blood enters faster than it can leave, so pressure builds in the glomerular capillaries [1].

**7.**

**(a)** Bowman's capsule (with the glomerulus) [1]

**(b)** The capillary endothelium has pores, so plasma passes out but blood cells are held back [1]. The basement membrane acts as a filter, holding back large molecules such as plasma proteins [1]. Podocytes have extensions with filtration slits between them, so filtrate passes easily into the capsule [1]. The glomerulus is a knot of many capillaries, giving a large surface area for filtration [1].

**8.**

**(a)** Sodium–potassium pumps in the basal membrane actively move Na⁺ out of the PCT cell into the blood, using ATP [1]. This keeps the Na⁺ concentration in the cell low [1]. Na⁺ diffuses into the cell from the lumen through cotransporter proteins that carry glucose with it, against the glucose concentration gradient [1]. Glucose then moves out of the cell into the blood by facilitated diffusion [1].

**(b)** Without the pumps, no Na⁺ gradient is kept between lumen and cell, so the cotransporters cannot move glucose into the cells [1]. Glucose stays in the filtrate and passes on to the urine [1].

**9.**

**(a)** Water is absorbed into the blood, so blood water potential rises [1]. Osmoreceptors in the hypothalamus detect this, and less ADH is released from the posterior pituitary [1]. Aquaporins are removed from the collecting duct membranes, so they become less permeable to water [1]. Less water is reabsorbed, so a large volume of dilute urine is produced, peaking within the hour [1].

**(b)** The collecting duct walls stay of low permeability to water, as few aquaporins are inserted [1]. Little water is reabsorbed, so large volumes of dilute urine are produced all the time [1].

**10.** Glucagon binds to a receptor on the cell surface membrane, causing a conformational change [1]. This activates a G-protein, which stimulates adenylyl cyclase [1]. Adenylyl cyclase converts ATP to cyclic AMP, the second messenger [1]. cAMP activates protein kinase A, which starts an enzyme cascade in which enzymes are activated by phosphorylation, amplifying the signal [1]. The final enzyme is activated and catalyses the breakdown of glycogen to glucose [1].

**11.**

**(a)** (7.8 − 5.8) ÷ 60 [1] = **0.033 mmol dm⁻³ min⁻¹** [1]

**(b)** Raised blood glucose is detected by β cells, which secrete insulin [1]. Insulin binds to receptors on muscle cells, causing more glucose transporter proteins (GLUT4) to be inserted into the cell surface membrane, so more glucose enters [1]. In liver cells, insulin activates enzymes that phosphorylate glucose and convert it to glycogen, so more glucose is taken up [1]. Glucose is removed from the blood faster than it is absorbed from the gut [1].

**(c)** Glucose fell below the set point at 120 minutes, which was detected by α cells, which secreted glucagon [1]. Glucagon caused liver cells to break down glycogen and release glucose, raising blood glucose: negative feedback [1].

**12.**

**(a)** Enzymes work fastest near their optimum temperature [1]. Too high a temperature denatures enzymes; too low slows metabolic reactions [1].

**(b)** One hormone can actively lower the concentration and the other can actively raise it [1]. So a change in either direction is corrected quickly, rather than depending on a single hormone's level simply falling [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Saying the posterior pituitary detects water potential.
- Writing "the kidney absorbs more water" with no mention of the collecting duct or aquaporins.
- Describing ultrafiltration as active transport.
- Missing out cAMP or the G-protein in the glucagon pathway.
- Giving the effects of insulin without naming the target cells.
- Confusing glycogen with glucagon.
