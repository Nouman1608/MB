## Key definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Market failure | A market misallocates resources |
| Public expenditure | Government spending on goods, services, transfers and subsidies |
| Indirect tax | A tax on spending; adds to firms' costs and shifts supply up by the tax |
| Subsidy | A payment to producers that lowers costs and shifts supply down |
| Maximum price | A legal ceiling; binding when set below equilibrium |
| Minimum price | A legal floor; binding when set above equilibrium |
| Buffer stock | A store bought and sold to hold a price within a band |
| Tradable pollution permit | A right to emit a set amount, which can be bought and sold under a cap |
| Property rights | Enforceable ownership or use rights over a resource |
| Government failure | Intervention that leads to a worse allocation of resources and a fall in economic welfare |

## The tools at a glance

| Tool | How it works | Main gains | Main risks |
|---|---|---|---|
| Indirect tax | Raises cost, cuts output | Corrects overproduction; raises revenue | Hard to set at the right level; regressive; weak if demand is inelastic |
| Subsidy | Lowers cost, raises output | Corrects underproduction | Costs taxpayers; may be set too high; waste |
| Maximum price | Holds price below equilibrium | Lower price for those who can buy | Shortage, queues, black market, lower quality |
| Minimum price | Holds price above equilibrium | Higher income for sellers | Surplus, cost of buying it, higher prices for buyers |
| Buffer stock | Buys when price is low, sells when high | Stabler prices | Storage cost; stock may run out or grow |
| Pollution permits | Cap on total pollution, tradable | Certain total pollution; lowest-cost cuts | Cap may be wrong; price uncertain; monitoring |
| Property rights | Owners protect what they own | Removes the cause of some externalities | Hard to define or enforce |
| State provision | Government funds or supplies | Overcomes free riding; wider access | Taxpayer cost; weak cost control |
| Regulation | Rules, limits and bans | Direct and certain | Costly to enforce; ignores firms' cost differences |

## Method: tax or subsidy calculation

1. Find the original equilibrium by setting Qd = Qs.
2. Shift supply. For a tax t, write (P − t) wherever P appears in the supply equation; for a subsidy s, write (P + s).
3. Find the new price and quantity from the shifted equation.
4. Consumers' burden (or gain) is the change in the market price. Producers' share is the tax (or subsidy) minus that change.
5. Revenue (or cost) is the amount per unit times the new quantity.

**Worked reminder (inelastic demand).** Qd = 100 − 4P, Qs = 16P − 20, tax 5.

```
Before: 100 − 4P = 16P − 20  →  P = 6, Q = 76     PED = −4 × 6/76 = −0.32
Supply after tax: Qs = 16(P − 5) − 20 = 16P − 100
After:  100 − 4P = 16P − 100  →  P = 10, Q = 60
Consumers pay 4 of the 5 (80%). Revenue = 5 × 60 = 300. Quantity falls 21.1%.
```

Demand is inelastic, so consumers bear most of the tax and output falls proportionately less than price rises: good for revenue, weak for correcting an externality.

**Rule for a small tax:** consumers' share = PES ÷ (PES + the size of PED). With PES 1.5 and PED −0.5, consumers bear 1.5/2.0 = 75%.

## Method: evaluating any intervention

Use this chain: (1) name the failure; (2) explain how the tool works; (3) analyse effects on consumers, producers, taxpayers and third parties; (4) give a cost or risk, including a possible government failure; (5) judge, using size, elasticity, time and information as your reasons.

Useful evaluation words: depends on elasticity; depends on how accurately the failure is measured; short run versus long run; fairness (a value judgement); cost of administration; compared with a different tool.

## Equity: taxes and spending

A progressive tax takes a larger share of income as income rises. **Worked reminder.** Tax is 0% up to 5,000 and 30% above.

```
Income 15,000: tax 30% × 10,000 = 3,000  (average rate 20%)   after tax 12,000
Income 45,000: tax 30% × 40,000 = 12,000 (average rate 26.7%)  after tax 33,000
Ratio before tax 3.0; after tax 33,000/12,000 = 2.75
```

Spending on benefits, health and education raises low incomes further. Evaluation: higher marginal rates may reduce incentives, and how much redistribution is fair is a value judgement.

## Buffer stock rule

Set a floor and a ceiling. If the free-market price would fall below the floor, buy and add to stock. If it would rise above the ceiling, sell from stock. If the price is inside the band, do nothing. Check whether stock is enough: when it runs out, the ceiling cannot be defended. A floor above the long-run price makes stocks pile up and storage costs rise.

## Permit rule

The cap fixes total pollution. The firms with the lowest abatement cost cut the most and sell permits to the firms with the highest cost. The permit price settles near the abatement cost of the marginal firm. If the permit price is 15, a firm whose cost is 12 per tonne cuts more and sells; a firm whose cost is 20 buys.

## Government failure

| Source | Chain of reasoning |
|---|---|
| Inadequate information | Government misjudges the external benefit or cost, so the policy overshoots or undershoots and creates a new welfare loss |
| Inappropriate or conflicting objectives | One aim (low prices, popularity) undermines another (the environment, budget); short-term focus |
| Administrative costs | Monitoring and enforcement use resources, which reduces the net benefit |
| Corruption | Resources go to the connected, not to the best use |
| Unintended consequences | Behaviour changes in ways the policy did not plan, such as black markets |

Judge by comparing the size of the market failure with the likely size of the government failure. The specification wants you to see that intervention can raise welfare and that it can lower it.

## Must-know distinctions

- Tax per unit versus change in price: the tax is the vertical gap between supply curves; the price rise is the consumers' share.
- Maximum price (shortage) versus minimum price (surplus).
- Market failure (the reason to act) versus government failure (the risk of acting).
- Specific tax (fixed amount per unit) versus a percentage tax (the supply curve pivots).
- Progressive tax (rising average rate) versus regressive (falling average rate).

## Quick self-test

1. A specific tax of 2 raises the market price from 10 to 11.5. How much do consumers bear, and what percentage is that?
2. After the tax in question 1 the quantity sold is 80. What is the tax revenue?
3. A subsidy of 3 per unit lowers the market price from 12 to 10.5. What price do producers effectively receive, and by how much have they gained?
4. Qd = 160 − 8P and Qs = 12P − 40. Find equilibrium, then the shortage if a maximum price of 8 is set.
5. In the same market a minimum price of 12 is set. Find the surplus and the cost of the government buying it all.
6. A buffer stock authority has a band of 20 to 30. What does it do if the price would be 15, and if it would be 35?
7. The permit price is 15. A firm's abatement cost is 20 per tonne. Does it buy or sell permits?
8. State two sources of government failure.
9. A tax of 4 is added to a good priced at 20. Consumers bear 75%. What is the percentage rise in the price paid?
10. Give one unintended consequence of a binding price ceiling.

### Answers

1. The price rose by 1.5, so consumers bear 1.5, which is 75% of the tax. Producers bear 0.5.
2. 2 × 80 = 160.
3. 10.5 + 3 = 13.5, up from 12 by 1.5.
4. 160 − 8P = 12P − 40, so P = 10, Q = 80. At 8: Qd = 96, Qs = 56, shortage 40.
5. At 12: Qd = 64, Qs = 104, surplus 40; cost 40 × 12 = 480.
6. At 15 it buys, because the price is below the floor of 20. At 35 it sells from stock, if it has any.
7. It buys, because buying at 15 is cheaper than cutting at 20.
8. Any two of: inadequate information; inappropriate or conflicting objectives; administrative costs; corruption.
9. Consumers bear 75% × 4 = 3, and 3/20 = 15%.
10. A black market, or reduced quality, or rationing by queue.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Drawing the tax as a shift of demand, or labelling the gap between old and new price as the tax.
- Forgetting that a tax raises price by less than the tax unless supply is perfectly elastic or demand perfectly inelastic.
- Describing a policy without saying who gains and who loses.
- Giving a list of tools with no judgement about which suits this market and why.
- Using the phrase "government failure" without a source and a chain of reasoning.
- Treating the price ceiling as helping everyone, and ignoring the shortage.
- Quoting an elasticity without saying what it means for the outcome.
- Stating that inequality is unfair as fact; fairness is a value judgement.
- Dropping working in calculations, such as the shifted supply equation.
