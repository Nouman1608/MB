## Definitions to know

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| GDP | Money value of a country's output over one year |
| Real GDP | GDP adjusted for price changes |
| Real GDP per head | Real GDP divided by population |
| HDI | A 0-to-1 index combining health, education and income |
| Absolute poverty | Income too low to buy basic necessities (food, water, shelter) |
| Relative poverty | Income far below the typical income of the same society |
| Progressive tax | Tax whose rate rises as income rises |
| National minimum wage | Legal lowest hourly pay |
| State benefits | Payments from government to households, such as unemployment or old-age support |
| Income distribution | How total income is shared among households |

## Formulas and rules

| Item | Rule |
|---|---|
| Real GDP | Nominal GDP / price index x 100 |
| Price index | Nominal GDP / real GDP x 100 |
| Real GDP per head | Real GDP / population |
| Growth per head | Real GDP growth factor / population growth factor, minus 1 |
| GDP by spending | C + I + G + (X - M) |
| Average tax rate | Tax paid / income x 100 |
| Relative poverty line | A chosen fraction (for example 50% or 60%) of median income |
| Quintile income | Share x 5 x mean income |

## Worked reminders

**Real and per head.** Nominal GDP 880 billion, price index 110, population 20 million.
Real GDP = 880 / 110 x 100 = 800 billion. Per head = 800 billion / 20 million = **40,000**.

**Growth per head.** Real GDP up 3%, population up 1%: 1.03 / 1.01 - 1 = **1.98%**. If both rise by the same percentage, per head stays unchanged.

**Progressive tax.** Illustrative: 0% on the first 8,000, then 25% on the rest.
Income 16,000: tax = 0.25 x 8,000 = 2,000, average rate 12.5%.
Income 40,000: tax = 0.25 x 32,000 = 8,000, average rate 20%.
Because the share taken climbs from 12.5% to 20%, the tax is progressive.

**Relative line.** Median income 18,000, line at 60%: 0.6 x 18,000 = **10,800**. A household on 10,500 is relatively poor.

## 5.1.1 Measuring living standards

| | Real GDP per head | HDI |
|---|---|---|
| Measures | Average output and income | Health, education and income together |
| Components | C, I, G, X - M, adjusted for prices, divided by population | Life expectancy, years of schooling, income per head |
| Advantages | Simple; available often; comparable over time and between countries | Wider than income; one number to rank; shows rich is not always developed |
| Disadvantages | Average hides inequality; leaves out unpaid and hidden activity, environment, health, leisure | Still an average; leaves out environment, freedom, inequality; equal weights are a judgement |

**Method for "Discuss which measure is better":**
1. State what each measures.
2. Give one strength and one weakness of each.
3. Say that neither shows how evenly income is shared.
4. Conclude: the HDI is broader, but you need both plus distribution data.

**Simplified HDI reminder.** The real index is not calculated in the course, but the idea is: scale each part to 0-1, then combine. Country H (invented) has life expectancy 68 years, with minimum 25 and maximum 85, so the health index is (68 - 25) / 60 = 0.717. With education 0.55 and income 0.48, the equal-weight average is (0.717 + 0.55 + 0.48) / 3 = **0.582**. A country with higher income per head can score lower if its health or schooling is worse.

**Must-know distinctions.**
- Nominal means current prices; real means adjusted for price changes.
- Total means all output; per head means divided by population.
- Average means a mean across everyone; it is not the same as what a typical household receives.
- Poverty means low income and unmet needs; inequality means unequal shares. A country can have equality at a low level, or inequality alongside little poverty.

## 5.1.2 Why living standards and distribution differ

**Between countries:** natural resources; capital and technology; education and health; stable, honest institutions; trade links and geography; population growth; debt.

**Within a country:** ownership of land and wealth; skills and pay gaps; city versus countryside; discrimination; the tax and benefit system.

**Chain:** low education, so low productivity, so low wages, so low consumption and tax revenue, so little money for schools. This is a cycle that policy must break.

**Distribution reminder.** Mean income per head is 10,000. The poorest 20% receive 6% of income and the richest 20% receive 45%.
Poorest group: 0.06 x 5 x 10,000 = 3,000 each. Richest group: 0.45 x 5 x 10,000 = 22,500 each. The richest earn 45 / 6 = **7.5 times** as much.
If a benefit of 1,200 goes to a poorest household, its income rises to 4,200, a rise of 1,200 / 3,000 = **40%**, which narrows the gap.

**Within or between?** Differences *between* countries are about national output, institutions and resources. Differences *within* a country are about who owns assets, who is educated and how the tax and benefit system treats each group. Say which one the question is asking about before you answer.

## 5.2.1 Absolute versus relative poverty

| | Absolute | Relative |
|---|---|---|
| Line | Fixed cost of basics | Share of median income, moves with society |
| Falls when | Incomes rise above basic needs | Gap to the typical household narrows |
| Can exist in a rich country? | Yes, but rarer | Yes, easily |
| Rises in a boom? | Unlikely | Yes, if the poor gain less than the median |

## 5.2.2 Causes of poverty

- **Unemployment:** loses wage; long spells erode skills.
- **Low wages:** low skills, low productivity, weak bargaining power, part-time work.
- **Illness:** cost of treatment and lost earnings; can limit education.
- **Age:** pensioners on small pensions; children and elderly people depend on others.
- Also acceptable: lack of education, large families, discrimination, conflict, no land or capital.

## 5.2.3 Policies: one-line evaluation each

| Policy | How it helps | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Growth | More jobs and tax revenue | Gains may go to the rich; slow; environmental cost |
| Education | Higher skills and wages | Costly; years before results; poorest may not attend |
| State benefits | Quick, direct income | Cost to taxpayers; may weaken work incentives |
| Progressive taxation | Funds spending; narrows after-tax gaps | May reduce effort; avoidance; cost of collection |
| National minimum wage | Raises pay of low earners | May cut jobs if set above the market wage |

**Minimum wage reminder.** Market wage 5, demand = supply = 900. Minimum set at 7: demand 700, supply 1,100.
Unemployed = 1,100 - 700 = 400. Jobs lost = 900 - 700 = 200.
Pay bill rises from 900 x 5 = 4,500 to 700 x 7 = 4,900, but some workers lose out.

## Quick self-test

1. Define real GDP per head.
2. GDP is 600 billion and the population 12 million. Calculate GDP per head.
3. Nominal GDP is 330 and the price index 125. Calculate real GDP.
4. Nominal GDP is 242 and real GDP 200. Calculate the price index.
5. Real GDP rises from 100 to 102.5 and population from 40 million to 41 million. Calculate the change in real GDP per head.
6. Name the three dimensions of the HDI.
7. The richest 20% receive 50% of income; the poorest 20% receive 5%. By how many times is the richer group's income larger?
8. Using the tax bands in the worked reminder, calculate tax and average rate on 22,000.
9. Median income is 18,000 and the line is 60%. Is a household on 10,900 relatively poor?
10. State two causes of poverty.
11. Explain one reason why a national minimum wage might increase unemployment.
12. Explain why income can rise but a household can still fall into relative poverty.

### Answers

1. Real GDP divided by population, so output adjusted for inflation and shared across the number of people.
2. 600 billion / 12 million = **50,000**.
3. 330 / 125 x 100 = **264**.
4. 242 / 200 x 100 = **121**.
5. 102.5 / 100 = 1.025 and 41 / 40 = 1.025, so per head stays the same: **0%**.
6. Health, education and income.
7. 50 / 5 = **10 times**.
8. Tax = 0.25 x (22,000 - 8,000) = **3,500**; average rate = 3,500 / 22,000 = **15.9%**.
9. Line = 10,800. 10,900 is above it, so **no**, but only just.
10. Any two: unemployment, low wages, illness, age (also lack of education and others).
11. If the minimum wage is above the market wage, firms demand fewer workers while more people want jobs; labour costs rise and some firms cut hours or jobs.
12. If median income rises faster, the relative poverty line rises faster than the household's income, so it can drop below the line.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using nominal figures when the question asks for real values.
- Forgetting to divide by population when asked for "per head".
- Describing HDI as a measure of happiness or including wealth only.
- Mixing up absolute and relative poverty, or saying relative poverty cannot occur in rich countries.
- Defining progressive tax as "rich pay more" rather than "higher proportion".
- Giving policies as a list with no chain of reasoning.
- Evaluation that only repeats advantages: always add a limit, a cost or a time lag.
- Calculating a percentage change against the wrong base year.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the difference between absolute and relative poverty?**
Absolute poverty is not having enough income to buy basic necessities such as food, clean water, shelter and clothing. Relative poverty is having much less income than most others in the same society, such as below a set fraction of median income.

**Can relative poverty exist in rich countries?**
Yes. Relative poverty is measured against incomes in the same society, so it can exist in any country, and it can even rise while absolute poverty falls.

**What causes poverty?**
The syllabus names unemployment, low wages, illness and age. Other causes include little education, large families, discrimination, conflict and a lack of land or capital.

**Which policies reduce poverty and redistribute income?**
Policies include promoting economic growth, improving education, more generous state benefits, progressive taxation and a national minimum wage. Each has limits, such as cost, time lags or reduced work incentives.
