Section A tests the knowledge that Question 4 needs. Section B is a one-hour source paper on case study 2, Richard I, using the sources below. Questions 8 to 12 carry the Paper 1 tariffs of 3, 2, 4, 6 and 9 marks.

**Source A** -- *Invented for practice:* extract from a verse chronicle written by a crusader in Richard's army, completed some years after his return.

> The king rode up and down the ranks and would let no man break from the line, though the Turks rained arrows on us and the Hospitallers begged to charge. Only when he gave the word did the knights ride out, and then the enemy scattered like sheep. No prince of Christendom ever led an army so wisely.

**Source B** -- *Invented for practice:* description of a modern illustration in a school textbook. Richard, in shining armour and much larger than the figures around him, stands on a hill looking towards a distant walled city labelled "Jerusalem". Behind him, small exhausted soldiers sit by empty supply carts. The caption reads: "So near, and yet so far."

**Source C** -- *Invented for practice:* extract from a biography of Saladin written by a member of his court.

> The king of the Franks sent to the Sultan many times, seeking peace, for he feared for his lands across the sea, where the king of France was stirring. The Sultan answered with courtesy, but he would not give up Jerusalem, for it is sacred to us. In the end the Franks kept the coast and our people kept the Holy City.

**Source D** -- *Invented for practice:* extract from a modern historian's study of Angevin kingship, published in 2010.

> To judge Richard by the years he spent in England is to misread his position. He was lord of lands from the Scottish border to the Pyrenees, and the crusade was a duty his contemporaries expected of a great prince. His decision not to besiege Jerusalem was not a failure of nerve but a sober judgement that a garrison could never hold it once the army sailed home.

## Section A

**1.** State **two** ways in which Temüjin increased his power before 1206. **[2]**

**2.** Outline the decimal organisation of the Mongol army. **[3]**

**3.** Explain why the Otrar incident of 1218 led to war with the Khwarazmian Empire. **[3]**

**4.** Explain **two** reasons why the Mongols were able to take walled cities in Jin China. **[4]**

**5.** Identify **three** ways in which Richard I raised money for the Third Crusade or for his wars. **[3]**

**6.** Explain why the execution of Muslim prisoners after the fall of Acre in 1191 is debated by historians. **[3]**

**7.** Compare **one** strength and **one** weakness of Genghis Khan and Richard I as military leaders. **[4]**

## Section B

**8.** What, according to Source A, were the reasons for Richard's success at the Battle of Arsuf? **[3]**

**9.** What is the message conveyed by Source B? **[2]**

**10.** With reference to its origin, purpose and content, analyse the value and limitations of Source C for a historian studying the negotiations that ended the Third Crusade. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** Any two, 1 mark each: alliance with Toghrul (Ong Khan) of the Kereit (1); alliance with his sworn brother Jamukha (1); victories over rival peoples such as the Tatars, Kereit or Naiman between 1202 and 1204 (1); rewarding loyal followers regardless of birth (1); dividing defeated tribes among his own units (1). **[2]**

**2.** Men were grouped in tens (*arban*), hundreds (*jaghun*), thousands (*mingghan*) and ten thousands (*tumen*) (1). Each unit had its own commander answerable to the one above, giving a clear chain of command (1). Units mixed men from different tribes, which reduced old kin loyalties and bound soldiers to Genghis Khan (1). **[3]**

**3.** A caravan sent with Mongol backing was killed at Otrar by the Khwarazmian governor (1). When Genghis Khan sent envoys to demand redress, the Shah had an envoy executed (1). Killing an envoy was an unforgivable insult in Mongol eyes, and the Mongols already had reasons to expand westward, so the incident became the trigger for invasion in 1219 (1). **[3]**

**4.** 2 marks for each developed reason: the Mongols recruited and forced captured Chinese and Muslim engineers to build catapults and other siege engines (2); they blockaded and starved cities while their cavalry ravaged the surrounding countryside, as at Zhongdu, which fell in 1215 (2); the Jin were weakened by defections of commanders and subjects and by war on other fronts, and some garrisons surrendered (2). **[4]**

**5.** Any three, 1 mark each: the Saladin tithe of 1188 (1); selling offices, lands and privileges at his accession (1); selling to William of Scotland a release from the obligations Henry II had imposed (1); taxation to pay the ransom of 150,000 marks (1); plunder and payments from Sicily and Cyprus (1). **[3]**

**6.** Supporters of military necessity argue that the exchange of prisoners stalled and Richard could not feed or guard them while marching south (1). Critics see it as a massacre of about 2,700 people who had surrendered under terms (1). The evidence is partisan: crusader and Muslim chroniclers explain the delay and the killing differently, so historians weigh motives that the sources present in opposite ways (1). **[3]**

**7.** 1 mark for each valid strength and weakness, up to 2 for each leader. Genghis Khan: strength, organisation and mobility of a mass cavalry army (1); weakness, at first could not take walled cities, or reliance on terror that destroyed the wealth he conquered (1). Richard I: strength, tactical discipline and use of the fleet for supply, as at Arsuf (1); weakness, could not hold Jerusalem without a permanent army, or long absence from his lands (1). **[4]**

**8.** 1 mark per point, up to 3: Richard kept the army in formation under attack (1); he refused to let knights charge early (1); he chose the moment to charge, which broke the enemy (1); he was personally present, riding along the ranks (1). **[3]**

**9.** 1 mark per point, up to 2: Richard came close to Jerusalem but did not take it (1); his army was exhausted and short of supplies, which held him back (1); the heroic image of Richard contrasts with the weakness of his position (1). **[2]**

**10.** Markbands (summarised from the guide's Question 2 descriptors): **3-4 marks**, explicit discussion of both value and limitations, linked to origin, purpose and content; **1-2 marks**, limited, one-sided, or not linked to the negotiations.
- *Value:* origin, a member of Saladin's court with access to the Sultan's diplomacy; purpose, to record Saladin's life and honour him; content shows Richard repeatedly seeking peace, the Sultan's refusal to give up Jerusalem and the final division of coast and city.
- *Limitations:* written to praise Saladin, so it presents his courtesy and firmness favourably and Richard as the supplicant; it gives a religious explanation for the outcome and says little about Saladin's own military difficulties. **[4]**

**11.** Markbands: **5-6 marks**, detailed comparison and contrast with explicit links throughout; **3-4 marks**, some similarities and differences, possibly unbalanced; **1-2 marks**, superficial or sources described separately.
- *Comparisons:* both link Richard's choices to his wider lands (C, the king of France "stirring"; D, lands "to the Pyrenees"); both imply Jerusalem was not simply captured by force.
- *Contrasts:* C presents Richard as asking for peace from weakness, D as making a "sober" military judgement; C stresses Saladin's religious refusal to give up the city, D stresses the problem of holding it after the army left; C presents the outcome as a Muslim success, D as a sensible limit to crusading aims. **[6]**

**12.** Markbands: **7-9 marks**, focused response using the sources and accurate own knowledge, with effective synthesis; **4-6 marks**, mostly focused, uses sources and some own knowledge; **1-3 marks**, limited focus, sources described or own knowledge only.
- *From the sources:* A (discipline at Arsuf), B (supply problems and the failure to reach Jerusalem), C (diplomacy and pressure from France), D (realistic judgement over Jerusalem).
- *Own knowledge, strengths:* capture of Messina and Cyprus as bases; siege of Acre (July 1191); march down the coast with the fleet; Arsuf (September 1191); relief of Jaffa (1192); Treaty of Jaffa securing the coast and pilgrim access.
- *Own knowledge, limits:* quarrels with Philip II and Leopold of Austria; the execution of prisoners at Acre; failure to recover Jerusalem; the costs of the crusade and his capture on the way home.
- *Judgement:* for example, Richard was an outstanding field commander whose political aims were limited by the size of his army and by events in France. **[9]**

## Frequently asked questions

**Why is Section B set on Richard I rather than Genghis Khan?**
Paper 1 sources are set on one case study. Use Section A to cover Genghis Khan and ask your teacher for a Mongol source set.

**Can I use Source D's historian as own knowledge?**
No. Material given in the sources is source material; own knowledge is what you bring from your study of the case.
