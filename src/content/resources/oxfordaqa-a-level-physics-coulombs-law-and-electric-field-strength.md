## Coulomb's law (3.8.1)

In a vacuum, point charges Q₁ and Q₂ separated by r push or pull on each other with forces of equal size and opposite direction:

F = Q₁Q₂ / (4πε₀r²)

It has the same inverse-square form as Newton's law of gravitation. The force acts along the line joining the charges. Like charges repel and unlike charges attract. The equation gives magnitude only: decide direction from the signs of the charges.

ε₀ is the permittivity of free space. The factor 1/(4πε₀) is about 8.99 × 10⁹ N m² C⁻². Air can be treated as a vacuum: it changes the force by a negligible amount, so you use ε₀ for charges in air.

For a charged sphere, the charge may be considered to be at the centre. So r is the distance from the centre of the sphere to the other charge.

### Worked example 1: force between two charges

Two small spheres carry +4.8 nC and −3.2 nC. Their centres are 6.0 cm apart in air. Find the force on each.

F = (4.8 × 10⁻⁹ × 3.2 × 10⁻⁹) / (4π × 8.85 × 10⁻¹² × 0.060²)

Numerator: 1.536 × 10⁻¹⁷ C². Denominator: 4π × 8.85 × 10⁻¹² × 3.6 × 10⁻³ = 4.00 × 10⁻¹³.

F = **3.8 × 10⁻⁵ N**. The charges are unlike, so each sphere is pulled towards the other. By Newton's third law both forces have this magnitude.

### Worked example 2: scaling

The force between two charges is 0.80 mN. One charge is doubled and the separation is tripled. Find the new force.

Doubling a charge doubles F. Tripling r divides F by 3² = 9. New force = 0.80 × 2/9 = **0.18 mN**.

### Worked example 3: a charged sphere

A conducting sphere of radius 2.5 cm carries +60 nC. A point charge of −5.0 nC is held 10 cm from the surface of the sphere. Find the force on the point charge.

The charge on the sphere acts as if at its centre, so r = 2.5 + 10 = 12.5 cm = 0.125 m.

F = (60 × 10⁻⁹ × 5.0 × 10⁻⁹) / (4π × 8.85 × 10⁻¹² × 0.125²) = **1.7 × 10⁻⁴ N**, towards the sphere (unlike charges).

Using 0.10 m by mistake gives a force 1.6 times too large.

### Gravitational and electrostatic forces between subatomic particles

For an electron and a proton, the same r appears in both forces, so it cancels in the ratio:

Fₑ / F_g = [e² / (4πε₀r²)] ÷ [G mₑ mₚ / r²] = e² / (4πε₀ G mₑ mₚ)

Substituting: (1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹)² = 2.56 × 10⁻³⁸ in the numerator. The denominator is 4π × 8.85 × 10⁻¹² × 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ × 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ × 1.67 × 10⁻²⁷ = 1.13 × 10⁻⁷⁷.

Ratio = **2.3 × 10³⁹**.

For a separation of 5.3 × 10⁻¹¹ m, the electrostatic force is 8.2 × 10⁻⁸ N and the gravitational force is 3.6 × 10⁻⁴⁷ N. Both forces fall as 1/r², so the ratio is the same at any separation. Gravity is negligible between subatomic particles.

## Electric field lines (3.8.2)

An electric field is a region where a charge experiences a force. You show it with field lines. The rules are:

- An arrow on each line shows which way a positive test charge would be pushed.
- Lines leave positive charges and arrive at negative ones (an isolated charge has lines running to or from infinity).
- The lines never cross.
- Where the lines are closer together, the field is stronger.

Three patterns to know:

| Situation | Field line pattern |
|---|---|
| Isolated positive point charge | Straight lines pointing radially outwards, spreading out so the field weakens with distance |
| Isolated negative point charge | The same lines pointing radially inwards |
| Two oppositely charged parallel plates | Parallel, equally spaced lines from the positive plate to the negative plate, running at right angles to the plates. They curve outwards only near the edges |

Equally spaced parallel lines show a uniform field (ignoring the edges).

## Electric field strength E = F/Q (3.8.2)

The electric field strength at a point is the force per unit positive charge placed at that point:

E = F / Q

E is a vector that points the way a positive charge would be pushed. Its unit is N C⁻¹, which is equivalent to V m⁻¹. For a negative charge the force is opposite to E, so the force on −Q is −QE.

Example: a +2.0 nC test charge feels 6.0 × 10⁻⁵ N, so E = 6.0 × 10⁻⁵ / 2.0 × 10⁻⁹ = **3.0 × 10⁴ N C⁻¹**.

## Uniform field: E = V/d

Between two parallel plates with potential difference V and separation d, the field is uniform and its magnitude is

E = V / d

### Derivation

Move a charge Q from one plate to the other through a field of strength E. The force on it is F = QE, and it moves a distance d along the field.

Work done = Fd. The work done per unit charge is the potential difference, so the work done is also QΔV.

So Fd = QΔV. Substituting F = QE gives QEd = QΔV, and cancelling Q:

**E = ΔV / d**

Write out each step: work = force × distance, work = QΔV, and F = QE.

### Worked example 4: balancing a droplet

A tiny oil droplet of mass 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁵ kg hangs stationary between horizontal plates 1.5 cm apart with a p.d. of 480 V. Find the charge on the droplet.

E = V/d = 480 / 0.015 = 3.2 × 10⁴ V m⁻¹.

For equilibrium, the electric force equals the weight: QE = mg.

Q = mg/E = (1.6 × 10⁻¹⁵ × 9.81) / 3.2 × 10⁴ = **4.9 × 10⁻¹⁹ C**.

This is about 3.1 times e, so the droplet carries about three excess electrons. The force acts upwards, so the upper plate is positive and the droplet is negative.

## Particle crossing a uniform field

A particle enters the plates with speed v at right angles to the field. There is no force along its original direction, so that speed stays constant. A constant force acts across the field, so it accelerates in that direction. This matches a projectile in a uniform gravitational field: the path is a parabola between the plates, then a straight line after it leaves.

The steps are:

1. Field: E = V/d.
2. Force: F = QE.
3. Acceleration across the field: a = F/m.
4. Time between the plates: t = L/v (L is the plate length).
5. Deflection: y = ½at² (starting from zero sideways speed).
6. Sideways speed on leaving: v_y = at, and the angle to the original direction is tan⁻¹(v_y/v).

### Worked example 5: a proton in a field

A proton enters the gap between two horizontal plates at 6.0 × 10⁵ m s⁻¹, parallel to the plates. The plates are 4.5 cm long and 2.4 cm apart, with a p.d. of 450 V.

1. E = 450 / 0.024 = 1.875 × 10⁴ V m⁻¹.
2. F = eE = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 1.875 × 10⁴ = 3.0 × 10⁻¹⁵ N, towards the negative plate.
3. a = F/m = 3.0 × 10⁻¹⁵ / 1.67 × 10⁻²⁷ = 1.8 × 10¹² m s⁻².
4. t = 0.045 / 6.0 × 10⁵ = 7.5 × 10⁻⁸ s.
5. y = ½ × 1.796 × 10¹² × (7.5 × 10⁻⁸)² = **5.1 mm** towards the negative plate.
6. v_y = at = 1.3 × 10⁵ m s⁻¹, so the angle is tan⁻¹(1.347 × 10⁵ / 6.0 × 10⁵) = **13°** to the original direction.

The proton's weight is 1.6 × 10⁻²⁶ N, about 10¹¹ times smaller than the electric force, so weight is ignored. Check that the deflection is below 12 mm (half the gap), or the particle hits a plate. A lighter particle with the same charge is deflected further, because a = QE/m.

## Radial field: E = Q/(4πε₀r²)

Put a small test charge q a distance r from a source charge Q. Coulomb's law gives F = Qq/(4πε₀r²). Dividing by q:

E = Q / (4πε₀r²)

- E is inversely proportional to r². Doubling the distance cuts the field to a quarter.
- For a charged sphere, r is measured from the centre and the equation applies outside the sphere.
- The direction is away from a positive Q and towards a negative Q.

### Worked example 6

A sphere carries −35 nC. Find the field strength 0.30 m from its centre, and the force on a +4.0 nC charge placed there.

E = 35 × 10⁻⁹ / (4π × 8.85 × 10⁻¹² × 0.30²) = **3.5 × 10³ N C⁻¹**, directed towards the sphere.

F = qE = 4.0 × 10⁻⁹ × 3.497 × 10³ = **1.4 × 10⁻⁵ N**, towards the sphere.

## Common errors

- Taking r to the surface of a sphere rather than to its centre.
- Forgetting to square r.
- Using E = V/d for a radial field, or E = Q/(4πε₀r²) between parallel plates.
- Leaving the separation in centimetres or the charge in nC.
- Giving the force on an electron in the direction of E. The electron is negative, so the force is opposite to E.
- Drawing the path between the plates as a circle. It is a parabola.
- Saying field lines show a moving charge's path. They show the direction of the force.
