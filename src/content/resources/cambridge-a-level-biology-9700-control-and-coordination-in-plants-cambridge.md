## How plants coordinate responses

Plants have no nervous system and no muscles, but they still coordinate responses in two ways that this section illustrates:

- **Electrical signals**: plant cells can produce action potentials that spread from cell to cell. The Venus fly trap uses them for one of the fastest movements in the plant kingdom.
- **Plant hormones** (plant growth regulators): chemicals such as auxin and gibberellin, made in one place and acting on cells elsewhere, usually by changing growth or gene expression.

Hormonal responses in plants are usually slow (hours to days) because they work through growth; the fly trap is the exception that shows electrical signalling at work.

## The Venus fly trap

### The structure

The trap is a **modified leaf** with two **lobes** joined along a midrib. On the inner surface of each lobe are a few stiff **sensitive hairs** (trigger hairs). The edges carry stiff spines that interlock when the trap shuts. Glands on the inner surface secrete digestive enzymes.

### The response, step by step

1. An insect bends a sensitive hair. Cells at the base of the hair are deformed, and **ion channels open** in their membranes.
2. Ions move into the cells, causing a **receptor potential**. If it reaches threshold, an **action potential** is generated.
3. A single stimulation does not close the trap. A **second stimulation** (the same hair again, or another hair) within a short time, often quoted as about 20 seconds, is needed.
4. The action potentials **spread across the lobes** from cell to cell.
5. Closure: it is thought that **hydrogen ions are pumped** into the walls of cells in the outer layers of the lobes, loosening the walls. **Water moves rapidly into these cells by osmosis**, so they expand.
6. The lobes, held under tension in a **convex** shape, suddenly flip to a **concave** shape, and the trap **snaps shut** in a fraction of a second.
7. As the trapped insect struggles, it keeps stimulating the hairs; the lobes press tightly together, sealing the trap, and digestive enzymes are secreted.

### Why the two-touch rule matters

Requiring two stimulations within a short time means the trap rarely closes for a raindrop or a piece of debris that touches one hair once. Each closure uses a lot of energy and the trap takes many hours to reopen, so false closures are costly. A living insect moving inside the trap is very likely to touch hairs more than once.

### Comparing with a mammal reflex

Both use receptor potentials, a threshold and action potentials. But in the fly trap there are no neurones and no muscles: the signal passes between ordinary cells, and the movement is produced by **rapid changes in cell water content and cell wall properties**, not by contractile proteins.

## Auxin and elongation growth

Auxin is made in growing tips (for example the tip of a shoot or coleoptile) and moves to the **zone of elongation** behind the tip. There, it causes cells to elongate by the **acid growth** mechanism:

1. Auxin binds to a **receptor** in the target cell.
2. This stimulates **proton pumps** (H⁺-ATPases) in the cell surface membrane, which use ATP to pump **H⁺ out of the cytoplasm into the cell wall**.
3. The cell wall is **acidified** (its pH falls).
4. At the lower pH, proteins in the wall called **expansins** are activated. They loosen the links between **cellulose microfibrils**.
5. The cell has a low water potential, so **water enters by osmosis**. Turgor pressure pushes on the loosened wall, which stretches.
6. Because the microfibrils mostly wrap around the cell, it stretches **lengthways**: the cell elongates.

### Worked example 1: testing acid growth

Illustrative data. Segments cut from the elongation zone of coleoptiles, each 10.0 mm long, were floated in different solutions for 6 hours.

| Solution | Final length / mm | Increase / % |
|---|---|---|
| Buffer at pH 7 | 10.4 | 4 |
| Buffer at pH 4.5 | 11.8 | 18 |
| Auxin in buffer at pH 7 | 11.6 | 16 |
| Auxin + proton pump inhibitor at pH 7 | 10.5 | 5 |

Percentage increase = (final − initial) ÷ initial × 100; for example (11.8 − 10.0) ÷ 10.0 × 100 = 18 %.

*Interpretation.* Acid alone (pH 4.5) causes almost as much elongation as auxin, which supports the idea that auxin works by acidifying the wall. When proton pumps are inhibited, auxin has almost no effect, which supports the idea that auxin acts **by stimulating the pumps**. In a "suggest" question, also note limitations: segments from a single set of seedlings, no repeats or variation shown, and a short time span.

## Gibberellin and the germination of barley

A barley grain contains an **embryo**, a large **endosperm** full of starch, and a thin **aleurone layer** of living cells around the endosperm.

1. The grain **absorbs water** (imbibition), which activates the embryo.
2. The embryo produces **gibberellin**, which diffuses to the **aleurone layer**.
3. In aleurone cells, gibberellin causes the breakdown of **DELLA proteins**. DELLA proteins normally inhibit a factor needed for transcription (details in section 16.3).
4. With DELLA proteins removed, the gene for **amylase** is transcribed and translated.
5. Amylase is secreted into the **endosperm**, where it hydrolyses **starch to maltose**; maltose is then broken down to glucose.
6. The sugars are absorbed by the embryo and used in **respiration** (to release energy) and to build new cells for growth.

### Worked example 2: designing and reading a germination test

Illustrative experiment. Barley grains were cut in half. Halves were placed, cut side down, on agar containing starch for 48 hours, then the agar was flooded with iodine solution. A clear zone around a half means starch has been hydrolysed.

| Grain half | Gibberellin added? | Clear zone? |
|---|---|---|
| With embryo | No | Yes |
| Without embryo | No | No |
| Without embryo | Yes | Yes |
| Without embryo, aleurone layer removed | Yes | No |

*Reasoning.* The embryo half produces amylase activity without help, but the embryo-free half needs added gibberellin, so the **embryo is the source of gibberellin**. With the aleurone removed, gibberellin has no effect, so the **aleurone layer is where amylase is made** in response to gibberellin.

## Common mistakes

- Saying the fly trap closes because "muscles contract". Plants have no muscles; closure depends on changes in cell water content and wall properties.
- Forgetting that **two** stimulations are needed.
- Writing that auxin pumps protons into the cell. Protons are pumped **out of the cytoplasm into the cell wall**.
- Saying that low pH "breaks down" the cell wall. It **loosens** the links between microfibrils; the wall still stretches under turgor.
- Saying gibberellin hydrolyses starch. Gibberellin causes **amylase** to be made; amylase hydrolyses starch.
- Placing amylase production in the embryo or endosperm; it is made in the **aleurone layer**.

## Exam technique

- **Describe** the fly trap response: hair bent → receptor potential → action potential → two stimulations → closure.
- **Explain** closure or elongation: name the ion movement, the effect on the cell wall and the role of water entering by osmosis.
- **Describe** the role of gibberellin: list the steps in order from imbibition to sugar use, naming the aleurone layer and DELLA proteins.
- With experimental data, calculate percentage changes from the starting value and say which hypothesis the results support.
