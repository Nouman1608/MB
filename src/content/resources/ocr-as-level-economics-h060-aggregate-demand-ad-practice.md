## Section A: Short questions

**1.** Which **one** of the following would shift the AD curve to the right? **[1]**

A: A rise in the general price level

B: An appreciation of the exchange rate

C: A fall in business confidence

D: A cut in the rate of income tax

**2.** Which **one** of the following explains a movement along the AD curve rather than a shift? **[1]**

A: A rise in government spending on schools

B: A fall in the price level that raises the real value of savings

C: A recession in the country's main export market

D: A fall in house prices

**3.** In Country P, C = 700, I = 160, G = 240, X = 300 and M = 350. Calculate aggregate demand. **[1]**

**4.** Household income in Country Q rises from 2,000 to 2,200, and consumption rises from 1,700 to 1,850. Calculate the marginal propensity to consume. **[2]**

**5.** Explain the interest rate effect as a reason why the AD curve slopes downwards. **[3]**

**6.** State **two** factors, other than interest rates, that influence the level of investment. **[2]**

## Section B: Structured and extended questions

**7.** Country R has the consumption function C = 40 + 0.75Y.

**(a)** Calculate consumption and the average propensity to consume when Y = 800. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate consumption and the average propensity to consume when Y = 1,200. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain what happens to the share of income consumed as income rises. **[1]**

**8.** Explain, with the aid of a diagram, the effect on aggregate demand of a fall in the exchange rate. **[4]**

**9.** The government of Country S pays every household a one-off sum of 500 currency units. Explain **two** reasons why consumption may rise by much less than the total paid. **[4]**

**10.** The table shows invented survey data for Country T.

| Quarter | Consumer confidence index | Business investment (bn cu) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 104 | 52 |
| 2 | 97 | 49 |
| 3 | 88 | 44 |
| 4 | 86 | 43 |

**(a)** Calculate the percentage change in business investment between quarter 1 and quarter 4. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain how falling confidence could reduce aggregate demand, using the data. **[3]**

**11.** Explain how rising house prices can increase consumption even when household incomes do not change. **[3]**

**12.** Evaluate the view that changes in income are the main determinant of changes in consumption. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** D [1]. A tax cut raises disposable income, so consumption rises at every price level. **[1]**

**2.** B [1]. A change in the price level is shown by a movement along AD. **[1]**

**3.** 700 + 160 + 240 + 300 - 350 = **1,050** [1]. **[1]**

**4.** MPC = change in C / change in Y = 150 / 200 [1] = **0.75** [1]. **[2]**

**5.** A higher price level means people need more money to buy the same goods [1]. Demand for money rises and interest rates rise [1]. Borrowing becomes dearer, so consumption and investment fall, and the quantity of real output demanded falls [1]. **[3]**

**6.** Any two from: expected future demand or profits [1]; business confidence [1]; technology; corporation tax or investment allowances; the cost of capital goods. **[2]**

**7. (a)** C = 40 + 600 = **640** [1]; APC = 640 / 800 = **0.80** [1].

**(b)** C = 40 + 900 = **940** [1]; APC = 940 / 1,200 = **0.78** (to two decimal places) [1].

**(c)** The share consumed falls, because each extra unit of income adds only 0.75 to consumption while the fixed 40 becomes a smaller share of income [1]. **[5]**

**8.** A fall in the exchange rate makes exports cheaper in foreign currency and imports dearer in domestic currency [1]. Export volumes rise and import volumes fall, so net exports rise [1]. The diagram shows AD shifting right from AD1 to AD2, with price level and real output axes labelled [1]. The size of the effect depends on how responsive demand for exports and imports is to price [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* An unlabelled diagram earns little. Label both axes and both curves, and add an arrow for the shift.

**9.** Reason 1: households smooth consumption over time [1]; a one-off payment is treated as temporary, so much of it is saved or used to repay debt [1]. Reason 2: part of any extra spending goes on imports [1], which is a leakage, so domestic consumption rises by less [1]. Other valid reasons: precautionary saving when confidence is low; higher-income households have a lower MPC. **[4]**

**10. (a)** (43 - 52) / 52 x 100 = **-17.3%** [1].

**(b)** Confidence fell from 104 to 86 over the year [1]. Less confident households save more and borrow less, cutting C, while firms expecting weaker demand postpone investment, which fell from 52 to 43 billion [1]. Both components fall, so AD shifts left [1]. **[4]**

**11.** Higher house prices raise household wealth, so owners feel better off and save less [1]. They can also borrow more against the higher value of their homes [1]. Consumption rises at every level of income, so the consumption function and AD shift up [1]. **[3]**

**12.** Award up to 10 marks across knowledge, application, analysis and evaluation.
- Income is the main source of spending power, so consumption rises with income: the consumption function C = a + bY [1].
- With an MPC below 1, a rise in income raises consumption by a predictable fraction [1].
- But permanent and temporary changes differ: one-off gains are largely saved [1].
- Wealth effects can raise consumption without any rise in income, for example from rising house or share prices [1].
- Interest rates and credit affect borrowing and saving: easy credit lets spending exceed income [1].
- Confidence and expectations of future income can change saving behaviour even when current income is stable [1].
- Distribution matters: lower-income households have a higher MPC [1].
- In normal times income changes explain most of the trend in consumption [1], but in booms and crises other factors can dominate [1].
- Judgement: income is the main long-run determinant, but short-run changes often reflect wealth, credit and confidence, so policymakers cannot rely on income alone [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* Strong answers distinguish the long-run link, which is close, from short-run swings, where other factors often dominate.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Shifting AD when the price level changes.
- Using "price" and "quantity" axes.
- Confusing MPC (change over change) with APC (level over level).
- Forgetting that imports reduce AD.
- Treating any income change as fully spent.
- Asserting that confidence matters without linking it to a component of AD.
