The questions here practise **1.1.1 Planning** in OCR A Level Chemistry A (H432), Version 3.1 (May 2026), for first assessment in 2017: experimental design and apparatus choice (a), controlled variables (b) and judging whether a method is appropriate (c). Section A has short questions; Section B has longer planning and evaluation questions. Use a molar gas volume of 24.0 dm³ mol⁻¹ where needed.

Read the [1.1.1 study guide](/resources/ocr-a-level-chemistry-h432-ocr-a-level-chemistry-1-1-1/) and [1.1.1 revision notes](/resources/ocr-a-level-chemistry-h432-ocr-a-level-chemistry-1-1-1-revision-notes/) first. See also the [OCR chemistry hub](/boards/ocr/a-level/chemistry/).

## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** State the meaning of each term.

**(a)** Independent variable. **[1]**

**(b)** Controlled variable. **[1]**

**2.** Choose the most suitable apparatus for each task and give a reason.

**(a)** Adding sodium hydroxide solution to an acid until an indicator changes colour. **[1]**

**(b)** Adding about 10 cm³ of dilute acid that will be in excess. **[1]**

**(c)** Making 250 cm³ of a standard solution. **[1]**

**3.** Suggest why carbon dioxide can be collected over water, but ammonia cannot. **[2]**

**4.** A student dissolves 2.65 g of anhydrous sodium carbonate (Mr = 106.0) and makes the solution up to 250.0 cm³.

**(a)** Calculate the concentration of the solution. **[2]**

**(b)** 25.0 cm³ portions will be titrated with 0.200 mol dm⁻³ hydrochloric acid in a 50 cm³ burette. Na₂CO₃ + 2HCl → 2NaCl + H₂O + CO₂. Show that the expected titre is suitable. **[2]**

**5.** Choose a suitable indicator for each titration.

**(a)** Ethanoic acid with sodium hydroxide. **[1]**

**(b)** Hydrochloric acid with aqueous ammonia. **[1]**

### Section B: longer questions

**6.** A student plans to collect the oxygen from 10.0 cm³ of hydrogen peroxide solution, of about 0.50 mol dm⁻³, decomposed by a manganese(IV) oxide catalyst, in a 100 cm³ gas syringe.

2H₂O₂(aq) → 2H₂O(l) + O₂(g)

**(a)** Calculate the expected volume of oxygen. **[3]**

**(b)** The student decides to use hydrogen peroxide of twice the concentration instead. Evaluate this change and suggest how the plan should be altered. **[2]**

**7.** A student investigates how the concentration of hydrochloric acid affects the time taken for a 2 cm length of magnesium ribbon to react completely. The acid is in excess.

**(a)** Identify the independent and dependent variables. **[1]**

**(b)** State three variables that must be controlled, and how each is controlled. **[3]**

**(c)** The student has 2.00 mol dm⁻³ hydrochloric acid, distilled water and two burettes. Describe how to prepare five different concentrations, each with a total volume of 20.0 cm³. **[2]**

**8.** A student plans to measure the enthalpy change of neutralisation by mixing 25 cm³ of 1.0 mol dm⁻³ hydrochloric acid with 25 cm³ of 1.0 mol dm⁻³ sodium hydroxide in a glass beaker. The temperature is measured with a thermometer that reads to the nearest 1 °C, before mixing and at its highest. Taking the enthalpy change as about −57 kJ mol⁻¹, the expected temperature rise is about 6.8 °C.

Evaluate whether this method is appropriate, and suggest improvements. **[5]**

**9.** A solid, HA, is a monobasic acid with Mr thought to be about 120. Plan an experiment to determine its Mr by titration with 0.100 mol dm⁻³ sodium hydroxide. Give quantities, show that your titre will be suitable, and explain how the Mr is calculated. **[6]**

**10.** Two unlabelled bottles contain propanal and propanone. Plan a test-tube method to identify which is which, including the expected observations and one safety precaution. **[4]**

**11.** The rate of the reaction between propanone and iodine in acid can be followed in two ways.

Method A: remove 10 cm³ samples at intervals, add each to sodium hydrogencarbonate solution to stop the reaction, then titrate the remaining iodine with sodium thiosulfate.

Method B: place the reaction mixture in a colorimeter and record the absorbance continuously.

Explain which method is more appropriate for measuring how the concentration of iodine changes with time. **[3]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** The variable that is deliberately changed by the experimenter [1]

**(b)** A variable, other than the independent variable, that could affect the dependent variable and is kept constant [1]

Total: 2

**2. (a)** A **burette**, because it measures any volume added up to its capacity, to ±0.05 cm³ per reading [1]

**(b)** A **measuring cylinder**: the acid is in excess, so a precise volume is not needed [1]

**(c)** A **250.0 cm³ volumetric flask**, which holds one exact volume when filled to the mark [1]

Total: 3

**3.** Carbon dioxide is only slightly soluble in water, so little of it dissolves as it is collected [1]. Ammonia is very soluble in water, so most of it would dissolve and the volume collected would be far too low [1]

Total: 2

**4. (a)** n = 2.65 ÷ 106.0 = 0.0250 mol [1]; c = 0.0250 ÷ 0.2500 = **0.100 mol dm⁻³** [1]

**(b)** n(Na₂CO₃) in 25.0 cm³ = 2.50 × 10⁻³ mol, so n(HCl) = 5.00 × 10⁻³ mol [1]; titre = 5.00 × 10⁻³ ÷ 0.200 = 0.0250 dm³ = **25.0 cm³**, about half the burette's capacity, so it is suitable [1]

Total: 4

*Examiner insight:* A "show that" on suitability needs both the number and the comparison with the burette's capacity.

**5. (a)** **Phenolphthalein** (weak acid, strong base) [1]

**(b)** **Methyl orange** (strong acid, weak base) [1]

Total: 2

**6. (a)** n(H₂O₂) = 0.50 × 0.0100 = 5.0 × 10⁻³ mol [1]; n(O₂) = 2.5 × 10⁻³ mol [1]; volume = 2.5 × 10⁻³ × 24 000 = **60 cm³** [1]

**(b)** Doubling the concentration would give about 120 cm³ of oxygen, more than the syringe holds, so the plunger could be pushed out and the volume would not be measured [1]. Use 5.0 cm³ of the more concentrated solution (or keep the original solution, or use a larger syringe) [1]

Total: 5

**7. (a)** Independent: concentration of hydrochloric acid. Dependent: time for the magnesium to react completely [1]

**(b)** Any three, each with a method: temperature, using a water bath or room-temperature solutions checked with a thermometer [1]; volume of acid, measured with a burette [1]; length of magnesium ribbon, cut to 2.0 cm with a ruler from the same roll and cleaned the same way [1]; the same flask and the same swirling.

**(c)** Mix acid and water so that the total volume is always 20.0 cm³ [1]: 4.0, 8.0, 12.0, 16.0 and 20.0 cm³ of acid, made up with 16.0, 12.0, 8.0, 4.0 and 0.0 cm³ of water, giving 0.40, 0.80, 1.20, 1.60 and 2.00 mol dm⁻³ [1]

Total: 6

*Examiner insight:* Keeping the total volume the same is what makes each concentration directly comparable. Changing the volume of acid alone would change the concentration and the amount together.

**8.** The temperature change is small: each reading is ±0.5 °C, so ΔT has an uncertainty of ±1 °C, about 15% of 6.8 °C [1]. A glass beaker conducts heat away, so the measured rise will be too small [1]. Judgement: the method is not appropriate as it stands, because both the uncertainty and the heat loss are large [1]. Improvements: use a thermometer or probe reading to 0.1 °C [1]; use an insulated polystyrene cup with a lid, and record the temperature at intervals so the maximum can be found by extrapolating the cooling curve (using 2.0 mol dm⁻³ solutions would also double ΔT) [1]

Total: 5

**9.** Quantities: for a titre of about 25 cm³, n(HA) in 25.0 cm³ = 25.0 × 10⁻³ × 0.100 = 2.50 × 10⁻³ mol, so 250.0 cm³ needs 0.0250 mol, about 0.0250 × 120 = **3.0 g** of HA [1]. Weigh about 3.0 g by difference to ±0.01 g, dissolve in distilled water in a beaker, transfer with washings to a 250.0 cm³ volumetric flask, make up to the mark and invert to mix [1]. Pipette 25.0 cm³ into a conical flask and add phenolphthalein [1]. Titrate with 0.100 mol dm⁻³ NaOH from a 50 cm³ burette to the first permanent pale pink; do a rough titre and then repeat until concordant [1]. Processing: n(NaOH) = 0.100 × mean titre ÷ 1000 = n(HA) in 25.0 cm³; multiply by 10 for the whole flask; Mr = mass weighed ÷ n(HA) [1]. Safety: sodium hydroxide is corrosive to eyes, so wear eye protection [1]

Total: 6

*Examiner insight:* The calculation of the 3.0 g is what shows the plan was designed, not guessed. Phenolphthalein suits a strong base with either a strong or a weak acid, so it is a safe choice when the acid's strength is unknown.

**10.** Add a few cm³ of each liquid to separate test tubes of Tollens' reagent [1]. Warm the tubes in a hot water bath for a few minutes [1]. Propanal, an aldehyde, gives a silver mirror; propanone, a ketone, gives no change [1]. Safety: both liquids are flammable, so heat with a water bath, not a naked flame [1]

Total: 4

**11.** Method B is more appropriate [1]. It records the iodine concentration continuously without removing samples, so many readings are obtained and the reaction is not disturbed [1]. Method A takes time to remove and quench each sample, so the exact time of each reading is uncertain, and it gives only a few points [1]

Total: 3

## Where marks are usually lost

- Naming apparatus without a size, or without saying why it is suitable.
- No calculation of the expected reading in a planning answer.
- Controlled variables named without saying how they are controlled.
- Changing the total volume when preparing a set of concentrations.
- "Evaluate" answers with no judgement.
- Safety precautions that are not linked to a specific hazard.
