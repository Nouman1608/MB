Section 8.1 of Cambridge IGCSE ICT 0417 covers the physical dangers of using ICT equipment. The syllabus names four safety issues: "electrocution from spilling drinks near electrical equipment and touching live cables, fire from sockets being overloaded or equipment overheating, tripping over trailing cables, heavy equipment falling and injuring people". It then asks for "the causes of these safety issues and strategies for preventing them". Questions are usually set in a room, such as an office, a classroom or a call centre, and expect you to connect a hazard to its cause and to a specific prevention.

The [safety and security study guide](/resources/cambridge-igcse-ict-safety-and-security-study-guide/) summarises all of Topic 8 in one table per section. This page explains *why* each cause leads to the hazard, which is what turns a one-mark answer into a two-mark one. For quick recall see the [physical safety revision notes](/resources/cambridge-igcse-ict-0417-physical-safety-revision-notes/), and practise with the [physical safety practice questions](/resources/cambridge-igcse-ict-0417-physical-safety-practice/).

## Safety is not the same as health

Section 8.1 is about **accidents**: sudden events that injure people, such as a shock, a fire, a fall or a crush injury. Health problems caused by long-term use of ICT, such as back pain, eye strain or repetitive strain injury, belong to section 5.2. If a question asks about safety, do not answer with posture or screen breaks.

## Electrocution

### Causes

- **Spilling drinks near electrical equipment.** Water and most drinks conduct electricity. If liquid gets into a computer, keyboard, socket or extension lead, it can create a path for current to flow where it should not, including through the person touching the equipment.
- **Touching live cables.** A cable is safe only while its insulation is intact. Insulation can be damaged by cables being trapped under desks, chewed, bent sharply, stretched or simply worn with age, exposing the live wire inside. Opening the case of equipment while it is plugged in also exposes live parts.

### Prevention strategies

- **Do not allow drinks (or food) near computers**, and enforce this as a rule in ICT rooms.
- **Check cables regularly** for damaged insulation and replace them, rather than repairing them with tape.
- **Do not open the case** of equipment while it is connected to the mains; leave repairs to trained technicians.
- **Use a residual current breaker (RCB)**, which cuts the power very quickly if it detects current leaking to earth, reducing the chance of a fatal shock.
- **Keep cables away from water**, such as sinks, and from places where they can be trapped or crushed.

## Fire

### Causes

- **Overloaded sockets.** Every socket and extension lead is designed to carry a maximum current. Plugging too many devices into one socket, or plugging one extension lead into another, can draw more current than the wiring is designed for. The wires heat up and can melt their insulation and set fire to nearby materials.
- **Equipment overheating.** Computers, printers and projectors produce heat and rely on fans and vents to remove it. If vents are blocked by papers, walls or dust, or if fans fail, components can get hot enough to cause a fire. Rooms full of equipment with poor ventilation make this worse.

### Prevention strategies

- **Do not overload sockets**: use enough wall sockets, and do not plug extension leads into each other.
- **Keep vents clear** and leave space around equipment; clean dust from fans and vents.
- **Ensure good ventilation or cooling**, such as air conditioning in rooms with many computers.
- **Switch equipment off** when it is not in use, especially overnight.
- **Have a CO2 (carbon dioxide) fire extinguisher** in ICT rooms. Water must not be used on electrical fires because it conducts electricity.
- **Fit smoke detectors** and make sure fire exits are clear.

## Tripping over trailing cables

### Causes

Cables for power, network and peripherals often run across the floor from desks to wall sockets. If they cross walkways or hang loosely, people catch their feet on them. As well as injuring the person, a trip can pull equipment off a desk and damage cables, which links to the electrocution and falling-equipment hazards.

### Prevention strategies

- **Use cable ducts** or covers to enclose cables that must cross the floor.
- **Route cables along walls** or under desks, not across walkways.
- **Tie or clip cables together** so they do not trail.
- **Use wireless connections** (Wi-Fi, Bluetooth) to reduce the number of cables.
- **Position desks near sockets** so long cables are not needed.

## Heavy equipment falling and injuring people

### Causes

Monitors, printers, projectors and servers can be heavy. They can fall if placed on desks that are too weak or unstable, if they overhang the edge of a desk, if they are stacked on top of each other, or if someone trips over a cable connected to them and pulls them off.

### Prevention strategies

- **Use strong, sturdy desks and tables** that can take the weight.
- **Place equipment well back from the edge** and do not let it overhang.
- **Use large enough desks** so that equipment is not crowded or stacked.
- **Fix heavy equipment** in place where needed, such as wall-mounted projectors on secure brackets.
- **Do not stack** heavy devices on top of each other.

## Worked example: inspecting an ICT room

*An office manager finds the following in a room with eight computers. For each, name the hazard and give a prevention strategy.*

| Observation | Hazard | Prevention |
|---|---|---|
| A cup of coffee next to a keyboard | Electrocution, if the drink is spilled into the equipment | Ban drinks from the computer desks |
| One wall socket with a four-way extension lead plugged into another four-way lead | Fire from overloading | Use more wall sockets; never plug one extension lead into another |
| A printer pushed against the wall with its vents blocked by boxes | Fire from overheating | Move the boxes and leave space round the vents |
| Network cables running across the doorway | Tripping | Put the cables in a cable duct or reroute them along the wall |
| A large monitor overhanging the edge of a small table | Heavy equipment falling | Use a larger, sturdy desk and place the monitor away from the edge |
| A power cable with tape wrapped round a split | Electrocution from a live cable | Replace the damaged cable |

## Annotated model answer

*Question: explain how overheating can cause a fire in a computer room and give two ways of preventing it.* **[4]**

"Computers produce heat, which fans remove through vents. If the vents are blocked or the fans fail, the components get very hot and can set fire to nearby materials **[cause explained, 2 marks]**. This can be prevented by keeping the vents clear of papers and dust **[prevention 1]** and by providing air conditioning or good ventilation in the room **[prevention 2]**."

## Common mistakes

- Giving a hazard without its cause, or a precaution without the hazard it prevents.
- Answering a safety question with health issues such as eye strain.
- Suggesting a water extinguisher for an electrical fire.
- Writing "be careful" or "take care" as a strategy. Name the action.
- Saying "use fewer cables" without saying how, such as using wireless connections.

## Exam technique

**State** or **identify** a hazard: name it precisely ("electrocution", not "danger"). **Describe** a cause: say what happens. **Explain** a cause: say why it leads to harm, for example "water conducts electricity". For "give a strategy" questions, match each strategy to the hazard in the question, and avoid repeating the same strategy for two hazards.
