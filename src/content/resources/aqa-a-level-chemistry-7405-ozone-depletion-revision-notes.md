These notes condense section **3.3.3.3 Ozone depletion** of the AQA AS and A-level Chemistry specification (7404/7405), for teaching from September 2015. It is AS content, examined on Paper 2 at AS and A-level. Explanations and worked examples are in the [Ozone depletion study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-ozone-depletion/), and exam-style questions are in the [Ozone depletion practice questions](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-ozone-depletion-practice/). ## What you must be able to do

- State why **ozone** in the upper atmosphere is beneficial.
- Explain how **chlorine atoms** form from **CFCs**.
- Use equations to show how chlorine atoms **catalyse** the decomposition of ozone.
- **Appreciate** how research by different groups led to legislation, and that chemists developed chlorine-free alternatives.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Ozone, O₃ | A form of oxygen in the upper atmosphere that absorbs ultraviolet radiation |
| CFC | Chlorofluorocarbon: a compound of carbon, chlorine and fluorine only |
| Free radical | A species with an unpaired electron, shown with a dot (Cl•) |
| Homolytic fission | A bond breaks and each atom takes one electron of the pair |
| Catalyst | Speeds up a reaction and is regenerated, so it is not used up |
| Propagation | A step in which a radical reacts and another radical forms |
| Termination | A step in which two radicals combine and the chain ends |

## Ozone in the upper atmosphere

- Forms naturally: O₂ is split by ultraviolet radiation, then O• + O₂ → O₃.
- Beneficial because it **absorbs ultraviolet radiation**, protecting living things.

## CFCs: uses and properties

| Use | Property that made CFCs useful |
|---|---|
| Refrigerants | Easily liquefied and vaporised |
| Aerosol propellants | Non-toxic and non-flammable |
| Solvents | Chemically unreactive |

The same lack of reactivity lets them reach the stratosphere unchanged.

## Formation of chlorine atoms

Ultraviolet radiation breaks the **C–Cl bond** (weaker than C–F):

```
CCl₂F₂ → •CClF₂ + Cl•
CCl₃F  → •CCl₂F + Cl•
```

This is **homolytic fission**. Chlorine radicals, Cl•, are formed.

## The catalytic cycle

```
Cl• + O₃ → ClO• + O₂
ClO• + O₃ → 2O₂ + Cl•
Overall:  2O₃ → 3O₂
```

- The Cl• used in the first step is **regenerated** in the second.
- Cl• and ClO• cancel in the overall equation.
- One Cl• can destroy a very large number of ozone molecules.
- The chain ends only at a termination step where radicals combine.

## Why bond enthalpy matters here

| Bond | Mean bond enthalpy / kJ mol⁻¹ (approx.) | Broken by ultraviolet radiation in the stratosphere? |
|---|---|---|
| C–Cl | 346 | Yes |
| C–F | 467 | No |

This is why CFCs release chlorine, not fluorine, atoms.

## Research and legislation

| Stage | Point to make |
|---|---|
| Hypothesis | Chemists proposed that CFCs could release chlorine in the stratosphere and destroy ozone |
| Evidence | Measurements showed large falls in ozone over Antarctica |
| Agreement | Different research groups obtained consistent results |
| Action | International legislation banned CFCs as solvents and refrigerants |
| Solution | Chlorine-free replacements were developed |

Independent confirmation by different groups makes the evidence reliable and persuaded governments to act.

## Replacements

- Compounds containing C–F bonds but **no C–Cl bonds**, for example hydrofluorocarbons.
- They cannot release chlorine atoms, so they do not catalyse ozone decomposition.
- Some are greenhouse gases, so chemists continue to search for better options.

## Writing equations: checklist

1. Put a dot on every radical, on the correct atom (Cl•, ClO•).
2. Show the condition (ultraviolet radiation) over the arrow for bond breaking.
3. Balance atoms: ClO• + O₃ gives 2O₂ + Cl•.
4. For an overall equation, cancel what appears on both sides.
5. For a termination step, show two radicals forming one molecule.

## Quick calculation

n(ozone destroyed) = n(Cl•) × number of cycles × 2, since each cycle destroys two O₃ (2O₃ → 3O₂).

Example: 0.0100 mol of Cl• in 500 cycles destroys 0.0100 × 500 × 2 = 10.0 mol of ozone, which is 10.0 × 48.0 = 480 g.

## Exam traps

- "CFCs destroy ozone" rather than "chlorine atoms from CFCs destroy ozone".
- Breaking the C–F bond.
- Cl⁻ instead of Cl•.
- Including Cl• or ClO• in the overall equation.
- Describing CFCs as harmful because they are reactive.
- Muddling the ozone layer with the greenhouse effect.
- Leaving out ultraviolet radiation as the condition.
- Saying chlorine catalyses the reaction "because it is a radical" without saying it is regenerated.

## Self-test

1. Why is ozone in the upper atmosphere beneficial?
2. Name the type of compound CCl₂F₂ is.
3. Why were CFCs used as refrigerants and solvents?
4. Write an equation for the formation of a chlorine atom from CCl₃F.
5. Why does the C–Cl bond break rather than the C–F bond?
6. What is meant by homolytic fission?
7. Write the two propagation steps for ozone destruction.
8. Write the overall equation.
9. Explain why chlorine atoms act as a catalyst.
10. Why do CFCs reach the stratosphere?
11. Why did legislation follow research by different groups?
12. Why can CH₂FCF₃ not deplete ozone in this way?

### Answers

1. It absorbs harmful ultraviolet radiation.
2. A chlorofluorocarbon (CFC).
3. They were unreactive, non-toxic, non-flammable and easily liquefied.
4. CCl₃F → •CCl₂F + Cl•, with ultraviolet radiation.
5. C–Cl is weaker (about 346 kJ mol⁻¹) than C–F (about 467 kJ mol⁻¹).
6. A covalent bond breaks and each atom takes one of the shared electrons, forming two radicals.
7. Cl• + O₃ → ClO• + O₂, then ClO• + O₃ → 2O₂ + Cl•.
8. 2O₃ → 3O₂.
9. It is used in the first step and regenerated in the second, so it is not used up.
10. They are unreactive in the lower atmosphere so are not removed there and diffuse upwards.
11. Consistent results from independent groups gave strong evidence of the cause.
12. It has no C–Cl bond, so no chlorine atoms form.
