## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Define the term aesthetics, as part of the design mix. **[2]**

**2.** A product's sales are rising quickly and it has just started to make a profit. Which **one** phase of the product life cycle is it most likely to be in? **[1]**

A: Introduction

B: Growth

C: Maturity

D: Decline

**3.** Which **two** of the following are extension strategies? **[2]**

A: Launching a new flavour

B: Closing the factory

C: Selling the product in a new country

D: Raising fixed costs

E: Stopping all advertising

**4.** Ashgrove Scooters sold 2,000 scooters in Year 1, 3,400 in Year 2 and 5,100 in Year 3.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage change in sales from Year 1 to Year 2. **[2]**

**(b)** Identify the phase of the product life cycle the scooter was in during Years 1 to 3. **[1]**

**5.** Pebble Cases has launched a phone case that survives a drop of three metres. It comes in twelve colours with a soft-touch finish. State **one** feature of the case's aesthetics. **[1]**

**6.** Winmill Backpacks sells 2,000 backpacks a year at £45 each. Each costs £18 to make. A waterproof lining would add £5 to unit cost; the price would rise to £52 and sales are forecast to fall to 1,900. Calculate the change in total contribution. **[3]**

**7.** Explain **one** reason why it is important for a business to differentiate its product. **[3]**

### Section B: longer questions

**8.** Tilly's Teddies makes handmade bears from wool and sells them at craft markets for £24. Outline **one** way the cost element of the design mix may limit the design of Tilly's bears. **[2]**

**9.** Explain **one** risk to a business of using an extension strategy. **[3]**

**10.** Glenmore Crisps sells a cheese and onion flavour that has been in maturity for six years. Three larger rivals sell similar crisps in the same shops, often on special offer. Analyse how differentiating its product could help Glenmore Crisps. **[6]**

**11.** Sparrow Games makes a classic board game that is now in decline. The owner can afford one extension strategy.

Option A: a new edition with a free companion app. It costs £20,000 to develop and is forecast to add 6,000 sales, each with a contribution of £5.

Option B: sell the existing game in a new overseas market through a distributor. It costs £8,000 to arrange and is forecast to add 3,000 sales, each with a contribution of £4.50.

Justify which option Sparrow Games should choose. **[9]**

**12.** Kinross Bikes sells 9,000 commuter bikes a year, each with a contribution of £120. The bike is in maturity. A redesign costing £60,000 would add a lighter frame and new colours, raising unit cost by £4 and allowing a price rise of £10. Sales are forecast to rise by 10%. Several rivals have recently launched electric commuter bikes.

Evaluate whether Kinross Bikes should go ahead with the redesign. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** How a product appeals to the senses [1], such as its look, feel, sound, smell or taste [1]. **[2]**

**2.** B [1]. **[1]**

*Examiner insight:* In introduction the product is usually still making a loss; in maturity sales level off rather than rise quickly.

**3.** A [1]. C [1]. **[2]**

**4. (a)** (3,400 − 2,000) ÷ 2,000 × 100 [1] = **70%** [1]

**(b)** Growth [1] **[3]**

*Examiner insight:* Year 3 also shows a large rise (50%), which confirms growth across the period.

**5.** Twelve colours / the soft-touch finish [1]. **[1]**

*Examiner insight:* Surviving a three-metre drop is function, not aesthetics.

**6.** Current contribution = (£45 − £18) × 2,000 = £54,000 [1]. New contribution = (£52 − £23) × 1,900 = £55,100 [1]. Change = **+£1,100** [1]. **[3]**

*Examiner insight:* Remember to add the £5 to the unit cost before calculating the new contribution per unit.

**7.** A differentiated product gives customers a reason to choose it over similar rivals [1]. Because customers value the difference, they may be willing to pay a higher price [1], so the business can earn more per unit without having to compete only on price [1]. **[3]**

**8.** Wool and handmade labour are expensive, so adding extra features such as embroidered details would raise Tilly's cost per bear [1]; because craft market buyers may not pay more than about £24, she may have to keep designs simple to keep a profit on each bear [1]. **[2]**

**9.** The strategy costs money to develop and promote [1], but the extra sales it brings are only a forecast [1], so if customers do not respond the business may spend more than it earns and lose money on a product that is declining anyway [1]. **[3]**

**10.** Glenmore's crisps are similar to those of three larger rivals, so shoppers have little reason to choose them [1]. Larger rivals can afford frequent special offers, so if Glenmore competes only on price its profit per pack will be squeezed [1]. Differentiating, for example with a distinctive local recipe, thicker cut or eye-catching packaging, gives customers a reason to pick Glenmore from the shelf [1]. This could protect or increase its market share during maturity, when competition is strongest [1]. Customers who value the difference may pay a little more, so Glenmore would not have to match every special offer [1]. However, rivals may copy a new flavour quickly, so the advantage may not last unless Glenmore keeps innovating [1]. **[6]**

*Examiner insight:* An Analyse answer uses the case: the three larger rivals and their special offers explain why differentiation matters to Glenmore in particular.

**11.** Option A extra contribution = 6,000 × £5 = £30,000 [1]; net gain = £30,000 − £20,000 = **£10,000** [1]. Option B extra contribution = 3,000 × £4.50 = £13,500 [1]; net gain = £13,500 − £8,000 = **£5,500** [1]. Option A gains £4,500 more [1]. A companion app modernises the game and may appeal to younger buyers, which could lift sales for more than one year [1]. However, Option A risks more money: if sales were half the forecast it would earn £15,000 and lose £5,000, whereas Option B would earn £6,750 and lose only £1,250 [1]. Option B also opens a new market, spreading risk, while Option A still sells only in the shrinking home market [1]. **Judgement:** choose Option A, because it gives the larger gain and refreshes the product itself, as long as the owner can afford the higher outlay; if cash is tight, Option B is the safer choice [1]. **[9]**

*Examiner insight:* Testing each option against a weaker outcome shows the examiner you understand that forecasts can be wrong.

**12.** Current total contribution = 9,000 × £120 = £1,080,000 [1]. New contribution per bike = £120 + £10 − £4 = £126 [1]. New sales = 9,000 × 1.1 = 9,900, so new total = 9,900 × £126 = £1,247,400 [1]. Gain = £167,400, or £107,400 after the £60,000 redesign cost [1]. For: the redesign improves function (a lighter frame) and aesthetics (new colours), refreshing a product in maturity before it declines [1]. Even if sales did not rise at all, the extra £6 a bike would earn £54,000, almost covering the £60,000 cost [1]. Against: rivals' electric bikes may draw commuters away, so sales could fall rather than rise by 10% [1]. A lighter frame and colours may not be enough to compete with electric models, so the money might be better spent developing an electric bike [1]. **Conclusion:** go ahead, because the redesign covers its cost even with modest sales and protects the bike in maturity, but Kinross should treat it as a short-term extension while it plans an electric model, since that is where the market appears to be moving [1]. **[9]**

*Examiner insight:* Evaluate answers that test the forecast, here by asking what happens if sales do not rise, give the conclusion a firmer basis.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Confusing function with aesthetics when selecting from a case.
- Forgetting to add a design change to unit cost before working out contribution.
- Identifying a phase without quoting the figures that show it.
- Choosing an extension strategy on extra sales alone, without subtracting its cost.
- Writing about differentiation in general with no link to the rivals in the case.
