## What this sub-topic asks you to do

- Know and use measures of food security, nutrition and health.
- Describe and explain global and regional patterns in those measures.
- Explain how food and health measures are related.
- Evaluate the strengths and limits of each measure.

## Crude rates and age standardisation

A **crude death rate** is deaths per 1,000 people per year. It is easy to calculate but depends heavily on age structure: an older population has more deaths even if health care is excellent. Comparing crude rates between a youthful and an ageing population can therefore suggest the wrong conclusion.

**Age standardisation** applies each population's age-specific death rates to the same standard population, so differences in age structure are removed.

### Worked example 1: two invented regions

| Age group | Region A population (thousands) | Region A deaths per 1,000 | Region B population (thousands) | Region B deaths per 1,000 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0-39 | 700 | 1 | 400 | 1.5 |
| 40-64 | 250 | 5 | 350 | 6 |
| 65 and over | 50 | 50 | 250 | 45 |

**Step 1, crude rates.** Deaths in A = 700 + 1,250 + 2,500 = 4,450 in a population of 1,000,000, so the crude rate is **4.45 per 1,000**. Deaths in B = 600 + 2,100 + 11,250 = 13,950, a crude rate of **13.95 per 1,000**. Region B looks about three times less healthy.

**Step 2, standard population.** Apply both sets of age-specific rates to a standard population of 500,000 aged 0-39, 300,000 aged 40-64 and 200,000 aged 65 and over.

- Region A: 500 + 1,500 + 10,000 = 12,000 deaths, or **12.0 per 1,000**.
- Region B: 750 + 1,800 + 9,000 = 11,550 deaths, or **11.55 per 1,000**.

**Interpretation.** Once age structure is removed, Region B's rate is slightly **lower**. Its high crude rate was caused by its older population, and its older people actually die at a lower rate (45 per 1,000 against 50). This is why international health comparisons use age-standardised rates, and why you should check which kind of rate a source gives before drawing conclusions.

## Health and income: the Preston curve

Plotting life expectancy against income per person for many countries gives a characteristic shape, first described by Samuel Preston in 1975:

- At **low incomes**, life expectancy rises steeply with income, because extra money buys food, clean water, sanitation and basic health care, which prevent many deaths from infectious disease and undernutrition.
- At **higher incomes**, the curve flattens: further income adds little to life expectancy, because the remaining causes of death are mainly chronic diseases of older age.
- Over time the **whole curve has shifted upwards**: a country at a given income today tends to have a higher life expectancy than a country at the same income decades ago, thanks to cheaper vaccines, antibiotics and public-health knowledge.

Countries well above the curve achieve more health than their income predicts, often through strong primary care, education and public health; countries below it may suffer from conflict, epidemics or inequality. Using the curve shows that income matters, but that policy and technology matter too.

## Grading a food crisis

Prevalence measures such as undernourishment describe long-term conditions. For sudden crises, agencies use a phase scale. The **Integrated Food Security Phase Classification (IPC)** grades areas into five phases:

1. Minimal
2. Stressed
3. Crisis
4. Emergency
5. Catastrophe or famine

Famine is declared only when thresholds for household food shortage, acute malnutrition in children and death rates are all passed, so the word has a technical meaning rather than being a general term for hunger. Phase scales are designed to trigger action early; their limits are that they need reliable field data, which is hardest to collect in conflict zones where crises are often worst.

## Anthropometric measures in more detail

Child stunting and wasting are defined against international growth standards. A child is **stunted** if their height for age is more than two standard deviations below the median of the World Health Organization Child Growth Standards; wasting uses weight for height in the same way. Using a standard reference makes rates comparable between countries. However:

- Measurements need trained staff and accurate equipment.
- Ages are uncertain where births are not registered, which affects height-for-age.
- Rates describe children, so adult undernutrition or obesity must be measured separately.

## Mapping food and health data

Choosing the wrong map is a common source of error.

| Data | Best map | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Rates or percentages (undernourishment %, infant mortality per 1,000) | Choropleth | Shading suits values that are already relative to population |
| Absolute numbers (number of undernourished people) | Proportional symbols | Shading counts would make large countries look worst simply because they are large |
| Movement (spread of an outbreak, food trade) | Flow lines | Show direction and volume |

Choropleth maps have their own limit: the whole area is shaded with one value, hiding variation inside it. A national choropleth can show a country as moderately food-secure while one region is in crisis.

## Combining indicators

No single measure covers food security, nutrition and health. A small **dashboard** of indicators is more convincing.

### Worked example 2: reading a dashboard

| Indicator (invented district data) | Value | What it suggests |
|---|---|---|
| Undernourishment | 9% | Moderate calorie shortfall |
| Child stunting | 34% | Long-term undernutrition, probably high |
| Child wasting | 3% | No recent acute crisis |
| Adult obesity | 18% | Diet transition under way |
| Under-five mortality | 48 per 1,000 live births | Weak health services or high infection |

Reading across: calorie availability alone looks only moderately poor, but high stunting shows that children have suffered for years, probably through poor diet quality and repeated infection (utilisation, not just availability). Low wasting rules out a current emergency. The obesity figure points to a double burden. The judgement is that the district's main problem is chronic diet quality and health care rather than a lack of food, so a response focused on food aid would be poorly targeted.

## Annotated model answer

*Evaluate life expectancy as a measure of a population's health.* **(sample paragraph)**

"Life expectancy is widely available and summarises mortality at all ages in one figure, so it allows comparison between countries and over time **[strength]**. It also responds strongly to income at low levels of development, as the Preston curve shows **[links to a pattern]**. However, it measures length of life, not health during life, so it misses years lived with illness, which healthy life expectancy or DALYs capture **[limitation with alternative]**. As a national average it can hide large differences between regions and income groups **[scale limitation]**. It is therefore a useful headline measure, but needs to be combined with morbidity data **[judgement]**."

## Common mistakes

- Comparing crude death rates between countries with different age structures.
- Shading absolute numbers on a choropleth map.
- Using "famine" loosely for any food shortage.
- Treating one indicator as a complete picture of food security or health.
- Assuming higher income always means better health, ignoring countries above and below the Preston curve.

## Exam technique

- **Describe** a pattern by quoting values with units, naming regions and noting anomalies.
- **Distinguish** two measures by stating what each records, such as crude and age-standardised rates.
- **Evaluate** a measure with what it shows, what it hides, and a judgement on its usefulness for a stated purpose.
- **Suggest** reasons for an anomaly using income, policy, conflict or data quality.
- Check whether data are rates or numbers before interpreting them.
