## Section A -- short questions

**1.** Explain the purpose of damages for breach of contract. **[2]**

**2.** Distinguish expectation loss from reliance loss, giving an authority for reliance loss. **[3]**

**3.** Explain when a claimant can recover for disappointment or distress caused by a breach of contract. **[4]**

**4.** Explain how The Heron II (1969) developed the test for remoteness of damage. **[3]**

**5.** A buyer agreed to buy 300 chairs at £40 each. The seller failed to deliver. On the delivery date the market price was £46; a month later it was £52. Calculate the buyer's damages and explain your answer. **[3]**

**6.** Explain the nature of specific performance and why it is not available for an ordinary sale of goods. **[3]**

**7.** Explain the difference between rescission and specific restitution. **[3]**

**8.** Explain why the court granted an injunction in Warner Brothers v Nelson (1937) but refused one in Page One Records v Britton (1968). **[4]**

## Section B -- scenario and essay questions

**9.** *Original scenario.* Zara pays Sunquest Travel £3,600 for a two-week "luxury spa retreat" for herself and her partner. The hotel is under construction, the spa is closed and the pool is empty. Zara spends £250 on taxis to reach a spa elsewhere. She also claims £5,000 because she missed a business opportunity in her home town while on holiday. Advise Zara on the damages she can recover. **[10]**

**10.** *Original scenario.* Glenmore Distillery agrees to buy a rare 18th-century copper still from Hamid, a private collector, for £70,000, intending to display it in its visitor centre. Hamid then receives a better offer and refuses to deliver. Glenmore also discovers that Hamid had told it, before the contract, that the still had been used by a famous distiller; this was untrue. Advise Glenmore on the equitable remedies available. **[12]**

**11.** *Original scenario.* Fieldway Ltd contracts with Coolpack Ltd to install a cold store at Fieldway's food factory by 1 August. Coolpack knows Fieldway supplies supermarkets but is not told of a special export contract worth £60,000 profit that depends on the cold store. Installation is two months late. Fieldway loses £8,000 normal profit and the export contract. Advise Fieldway on the extent of its recoverable loss. **[8]**

**12.** "Damages fail to compensate claimants fully for breach of contract." Evaluate this statement. **[15]**

## Answers

**1.** Damages are compensatory: they aim to put the claimant, so far as money can, in the position they would have been in had the contract been performed (1), as stated in Robinson v Harman (1848); they are not intended to punish the defendant (1). **[2]**

**2.** Expectation loss protects the benefit of the bargain, such as lost profit or the difference in value (1). Reliance loss compensates expenditure wasted because of the breach, putting the claimant back where they were before the contract (1). Authority: Anglia Television v Reed (1972), where wasted production costs, including pre-contract spending, were recovered (1). **[3]**

**3.** The general rule is that damages are not recoverable for disappointment or injured feelings (Addis v Gramophone Co, 1909) (1). An exception applies where the object of the contract was to provide pleasure, relaxation or peace of mind (1), as with the holiday in Jarvis v Swans Tours (1973) (1), or where peace and quiet was an important object of the contract, as in Farley v Skinner (2001), or where the breach caused physical discomfort (Watts v Morrow, 1991) (1). **[4]**

**4.** Hadley v Baxendale (1854) allowed natural losses and those in the contemplation of both parties (1). In The Heron II the House of Lords said the loss must be of a kind that the defendant, when contracting, should have realised was "not unlikely" to result from breach (1). This is a higher degree of probability than the "reasonably foreseeable" test in tort, so contract recovery is narrower (1). **[3]**

**5.** Damages are measured at the date delivery should have occurred: 300 × (£46 − £40) = £1,800 (1). The market rule under s51 Sale of Goods Act 1979 assumes the buyer can buy replacements at the market price on that date (1). Any extra cost from waiting until the price reached £52 is a failure to mitigate and is not recoverable (1). **[3]**

**6.** Specific performance is an equitable, discretionary order compelling a party to perform their obligations (1). It is available only where damages are inadequate (1); ordinary goods can be bought elsewhere, so money is a sufficient remedy (1). **[3]**

**7.** Rescission sets the whole contract aside and restores the parties to their pre-contract positions (1), for example after misrepresentation (1). Specific restitution is an order to return a particular item of property, rather than its value, where money would not be an adequate substitute (1). **[3]**

**8.** In Warner Brothers v Nelson the injunction restrained the actress from working for another film company in breach of her exclusive contract (1); it enforced a negative promise and left her free to earn a living in other ways (1). In Page One Records v Britton the injunction would in practice have forced the group to continue working with their manager (1), which would amount to indirectly compelling personal services, so it was refused (1). **[4]**

**9.** Indicative content (1 mark per point):

- Damages aim to put Zara where performance would have put her (Robinson v Harman) (1).
- Difference in value between the holiday promised and the holiday provided is recoverable (1).
- Non-pecuniary loss: the contract's object was pleasure and relaxation, so damages for disappointment are available (Jarvis v Swans Tours) (1).
- Her partner's disappointment may be reflected because the contract was made for both (Jackson v Horizon Holidays) (1).
- The £250 taxi costs are a reasonable step to reduce her loss, so are recoverable as a consequence of mitigation (1); they arise naturally from the breach (Hadley, first limb) (1).
- The £5,000 business opportunity: causation is doubtful because she chose to go on holiday, and the loss flows from her absence, not the breach (1).
- It is also too remote: Sunquest did not know of it at the time of contracting (Hadley, second limb; Victoria Laundry) (1).
- Recovery would also be inconsistent with the bargain, since she would have missed the opportunity even with perfect performance (1).
- Conclusion: difference in value, distress for both, and taxi costs; not the £5,000 (1). **[10]**

**10.** Indicative content (1 mark per point):

- Equitable remedies are discretionary and available where damages are inadequate (1).
- Specific performance: a rare 18th-century still is unique, so damages would not let Glenmore buy an equivalent (1); compare Falcke v Gray (1859) on rare goods (1).
- Limits: this is not a personal service, needs no constant supervision, and mutuality exists (1); Glenmore must not delay and must have clean hands (1).
- Hamid's better offer is not hardship of the kind that leads to refusal (contrast Patel v Ali, 1984) (1).
- Alternatively, an injunction could restrain Hamid from selling to the other buyer pending trial (1).
- The untrue statement about the famous distiller may be a misrepresentation that induced the contract (1).
- Rescission would set aside the contract and restore the parties (1); bars include affirmation (Long v Lloyd), lapse of time (Leaf v International Galleries) and third-party rights (1).
- Glenmore must elect: it cannot both rescind and obtain specific performance (1).
- Conclusion: if Glenmore still wants the still, specific performance is the most suitable remedy; if not, rescission (1). **[12]**

**11.** Indicative content (1 mark per point):

- Remoteness: Hadley v Baxendale, two limbs (1).
- Normal profit of £8,000: a supplier to a factory knows delay will cause loss of ordinary trading profit, so it arises naturally (1) and is recoverable (Victoria Laundry) (1).
- The export contract was not communicated, so it was not in the parties' contemplation (1) and is too remote, as with the dyeing contracts in Victoria Laundry (1).
- Parsons v Uttley Ingham: if the type of loss (lost profits) is within contemplation, the extent need not be (1); but Victoria Laundry treats exceptional contracts as a separate category requiring knowledge (1).
- Conclusion: £8,000 recoverable; £60,000 not, subject to mitigation (1). **[8]**

**12.** Indicative content:

- Knowledge (up to 6): purpose (Robinson v Harman); measures of expectation, reliance and non-pecuniary loss (Ruxley; Anglia TV; Jarvis; Farley v Skinner); limits of causation, remoteness and mitigation (Hadley; Victoria Laundry; The Heron II; The Achilleas; British Westinghouse).
- Analysis (up to 3): explain how each limit can leave part of a real loss with the claimant, for example uncommunicated special profits or distress outside the pleasure-contract exception (Addis).
- Evaluation (up to 6): for the statement, remoteness, the limits on non-pecuniary loss and the costs of litigation leave real losses unrecovered, and money cannot replace unique property. Against, the limits are justified because they reflect the risks each party accepted when contracting, they encourage disclosure of special circumstances, and equitable remedies fill gaps where damages are inadequate. A reasoned conclusion, for example that full compensation is not the aim; fair allocation of foreseeable risk is. **[15]**
