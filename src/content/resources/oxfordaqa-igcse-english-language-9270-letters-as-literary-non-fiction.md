**This guide covers one of the forms named for Paper 1 Section A of OxfordAQA International GCSE
English Language (9270).** The specification says the passages "will be literary non-fiction eg
autobiography, biography, letters, memoir and travel/adventure writing". The
[Section A literary non-fiction study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-english-language-9270-section-a-literary-non-fiction/)
covers the section as a whole and lists letters in a table; this page explains what makes a letter
different from the other forms, what to look for when the unseen text is a letter, and how to write
about it under each reading objective.

## What makes a letter different

Every literary non-fiction text has a real writer and a real experience. A letter adds three things
the other forms do not have.

1. **A named reader.** A memoir is written for anyone; a letter is written to someone. The writer's
   relationship with that person shapes every choice: what to explain, what to leave out, how
   formal to be, what to joke about.
2. **A moment of writing.** A letter is written at a particular time, often close to the events. The
   writer may not yet know how things will turn out, which creates a different kind of tension from
   a memoir written with hindsight.
3. **A purpose in the relationship.** A letter usually wants something from its reader: reassurance,
   forgiveness, news, money, sympathy, permission. Working out that purpose is often the key to the
   writer's perspective (R3).

## The conventions and what they reveal

Letters have a recognisable shape. Each convention can carry meaning.

| Convention | What to notice | What it can reveal |
|---|---|---|
| Place and date | Where and when the letter was written | Distance from home; how soon after an event |
| Greeting | "Dear Mother", "My dearest", "Sir" | The relationship and its formality |
| Opening | News first, apology first, a question | What the writer thinks matters most |
| Direct address | "you", questions to the reader, imperatives | Intimacy, persuasion, anxiety |
| Body | Order of topics, digressions | What the writer delays or avoids |
| Sign-off | "Your loving son", "Yours faithfully", a nickname | The relationship the writer wants to confirm |
| Postscript (PS) | An afterthought below the signature | Often the real feeling, added last |

A postscript deserves special attention. Writers sometimes put their most important or most
difficult point there, as if it were an afterthought.

## Kinds of letter you may meet

- **Letters home** from a traveller, soldier, student or migrant. Look for the gap between the
  writer's experience and what they choose to tell a worried family.
- **Letters to a friend or partner**, where private language, shared jokes and references the reader
  would understand appear without explanation.
- **Formal letters** to an official, an employer or a public figure. Register is controlled and
  purpose is explicit.
- **Open letters** written to one person but intended to be read publicly. The named addressee and
  the wider audience may both be targets.
- **A sequence of letters**, divided into sections on the paper. Track how the relationship or the
  situation changes from letter to letter.

Because the paper divides the text into sections, a sequence of letters fits the format naturally,
and the whole-text question may ask how the writer's feelings change across them.

## Reading a letter: what each objective looks like

**R1 (select and use).** Questions may ask what you learn about the writer's situation. Use the
details the writer gives, but remember that the writer has selected them for a particular reader.

**R2 (collate and synthesise within the text).** Gather details from different parts of the letter,
or different letters, into one point: for example, everything the writer says about money, spread
across three paragraphs.

**R3 (interpret ideas and perspectives).** Ask what the writer wants the reader to think and feel,
and what the writer may be hiding. A cheerful letter from a hard place often protects the reader.

**R4 (explain and evaluate methods).** Analyse how the writer adapts language to the reader: pet
names, formal phrases, humour, reassurance, questions, short sentences that break through the
politeness.

## The two audiences

When you analyse a letter, there are two readers: the person it was written to, and you. Strong
answers distinguish them. The writer's reassurance may comfort the addressee, while the reader of
the exam text notices the strain behind it. Phrases such as "for the recipient… but for us as
readers…" let you analyse both effects.

## Worked example

The following is an **original letter written for this guide**.

> *Harbour Road, 3 March*
>
> *Dear Mum,*
>
> *The room is fine, honestly. It has a window, and if I lean out far enough I can see a corner of the
> sea, which is more than the advertisement promised. The other lodgers are quiet. I have learned to
> make soup that lasts four days. Please don't send money: I am managing, and you need it more than I
> do. Tell Dad the job is going well.*
>
> *Your loving son,*
> *Daniel*
>
> *PS. If you could send my winter coat, I'd be grateful. No hurry.*

**Question:** How does the writer try to reassure his mother, and what does the letter suggest about
his real situation?

**Model answer (extract):**
Daniel's letter is built to reassure. He opens with "The room is fine, honestly", and the adverb
"honestly" answers a worry before his mother has expressed it, which suggests he expects her to doubt
him. Small positive details, such as the "corner of the sea", turn a cramped room into something
almost charming, and his joke that this is "more than the advertisement promised" keeps the tone
light. Yet the details also reveal hardship: soup that "lasts four days" implies he cannot afford
better food, and the instruction "Please don't send money" sounds like a refusal he has had to make
before. The postscript is the most revealing part. Placed after the loving sign-off and played down
with "No hurry", the request for his winter coat shows that he is cold and that his pride stops him
asking directly. For his mother, the letter offers comfort; for us, its careful cheerfulness exposes
the difficulty it is meant to hide.

*Why it works:* it analyses specific words and conventions (the adverb, the postscript), interprets
the gap between surface and situation (R3), and distinguishes the two audiences.

## Common mistakes

- **Ignoring the addressee.** Every choice in a letter depends on who will read it.
- **Taking reassurance at face value.** Ask what the writer is protecting the reader from.
- **Skipping the conventions.** Greetings, sign-offs and postscripts are evidence.
- **Treating a sequence of letters as one block.** Track change between them.
- **Writing about letters in general.** Analyse this writer, this reader, this moment.

## Exam technique

Before answering, note in the margin: who is writing, to whom, when, and why. Then read each section
with that context in mind. For the whole-text question, compare the opening and closing of the
letter or sequence and explain how the relationship or the writer's feelings have moved. Leave time
for Section B, which carries the same 40 marks.
