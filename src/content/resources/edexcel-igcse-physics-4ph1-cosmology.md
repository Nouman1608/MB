This guide covers sub-topic **8(d) Cosmology** of the Pearson Edexcel
International GCSE in Physics (4PH1), Issue 4 specification: statements
8.13P to 8.18P. **Every statement in this sub-topic is Physics-only**, so
it is assessed on Paper 2P only. A shorter treatment of the whole topic is
in the [Astrophysics study guide](/resources/edexcel-igcse-physics-astrophysics/);
the wave ideas used here (wavelength, frequency, the visible spectrum) are
in [Waves](/resources/edexcel-igcse-physics-waves/).

## What the specification asks for

- **8.13P** describe the past evolution of the universe and the main arguments in favour of the Big Bang theory
- **8.14P** describe evidence that supports the Big Bang theory (red-shift and cosmic microwave background (CMB) radiation)
- **8.15P** describe that if a wave source is moving relative to an observer, there will be a change in the observed frequency and wavelength
- **8.16P** use the equation relating change in wavelength, reference wavelength, velocity of a galaxy and the speed of light
- **8.17P** describe the red-shift in light received from galaxies at different distances away from the Earth
- **8.18P** explain why the red-shift of galaxies provides evidence for the expansion of the universe

## The past evolution of the universe (8.13P)

The Big Bang theory describes this history:

1. About 13.8 billion years ago, the universe was **extremely small, hot and dense**.
2. It began to **expand**, and as it expanded it **cooled**.
3. As it cooled, the first particles formed, then the nuclei and atoms of the lightest elements, mainly hydrogen and helium.
4. Gravity pulled matter together into clouds that formed the first **stars** and **galaxies**.
5. The universe is **still expanding** today.

The Big Bang was not an explosion of matter into empty space from one
place. Space itself has been expanding everywhere at once, carrying the
galaxies apart.

## The Doppler effect (8.15P)

When a wave source moves relative to an observer, the waves are
squashed together in front of the source and stretched out behind it.

- **Source moving towards the observer:** waves arrive closer together, so the observed **wavelength is shorter** and the observed **frequency is higher**.
- **Source moving away from the observer:** waves arrive further apart, so the observed **wavelength is longer** and the observed **frequency is lower**.

You hear this with sound: an ambulance siren sounds higher in pitch as it
approaches and lower as it moves away. The same happens with light.
Light from a source moving away is shifted towards longer wavelengths,
the red end of the visible spectrum: this is **red-shift**. Light from a
source moving towards us is shifted towards the blue end (blue-shift).

## Red-shift in galaxies

Elements absorb light at particular wavelengths, producing dark lines in
a spectrum. In the laboratory, with a stationary source, these lines
appear at known wavelengths: the **reference wavelengths**, λ₀. In light
from distant galaxies, the same pattern of lines appears, but every line
is shifted to a longer wavelength. The pattern is recognisably the same,
so we know it is the same element; the shift tells us the galaxy is
moving away.

## The red-shift equation (8.16P)

```
change in wavelength / reference wavelength = velocity of a galaxy / speed of light
(λ − λ₀) / λ₀ = Δλ / λ₀ = v / c
```

- λ is the observed wavelength; λ₀ is the reference (laboratory) wavelength.
- Δλ = λ − λ₀ (positive for red-shift).
- c = 3.0 × 10⁸ m/s.
- The wavelengths can be in any unit, as long as both use the same one.

**Worked example 1 (finding v).** *Illustrative figures.* A line with a
reference wavelength of 486 nm is observed at 495.72 nm in light from a
galaxy.

```
Δλ = 495.72 − 486 = 9.72 nm
Δλ / λ₀ = 9.72 / 486 = 0.020
v = 0.020 × 3.0 × 10⁸ = 6.0 × 10⁶ m/s, moving away
```

**Worked example 2 (finding the observed wavelength).** A galaxy recedes
at 1.5 × 10⁷ m/s. Predict where a line with λ₀ = 410 nm will appear.

```
v / c = 1.5 × 10⁷ / 3.0 × 10⁸ = 0.050
Δλ = 0.050 × 410 = 20.5 nm
λ = 410 + 20.5 = 430.5 nm
```

Remember to add Δλ to λ₀ when a question asks for the observed
wavelength, not just the change.

## Red-shift and distance (8.17P)

Measurements of many galaxies show two patterns:

- **Almost all distant galaxies are red-shifted**: they are moving away from us.
- **The further away a galaxy is, the greater its red-shift**: more distant galaxies are moving away faster.

## Why red-shift shows the universe is expanding (8.18P)

If every distant galaxy is moving away, and the most distant ones are
moving fastest, the simplest explanation is that **space itself is
expanding**. Think of dots drawn on a balloon being inflated: every dot
moves away from every other, and dots that start further apart separate
faster, because there is more stretching space between them. An observer
on any dot sees the same pattern, so Earth is not at the centre. There is
no centre on the surface of the balloon.

Running this expansion backwards in time means that galaxies were once
much closer together. Ultimately the whole universe was in a tiny, hot,
dense state, which is the Big Bang.

## Cosmic microwave background radiation (8.14P)

The **CMB** is weak microwave radiation that reaches Earth from **every
direction** in space, with almost the same intensity in all directions.

The Big Bang theory explains it: the early universe was extremely hot
and filled with high-energy radiation. As the universe expanded, this
radiation was stretched to much longer wavelengths, and it is now
detected as microwaves, the remnant "afterglow" of the hot early
universe. The Big Bang theory predicts this radiation; the rival Steady
State theory, in which the universe has always existed in the same
state, gives no explanation for it, which is why the discovery of the CMB
was strong evidence for the Big Bang.

## Main arguments for the Big Bang (8.13P, 8.14P)

| Evidence | Observation | Why it supports the Big Bang |
|---|---|---|
| Red-shift | Light from distant galaxies is red-shifted, more so for more distant galaxies | The universe is expanding, so it must have been smaller, hotter and denser in the past |
| CMB radiation | Microwave radiation from every direction, nearly uniform | It is the stretched, cooled radiation left from the hot, dense early universe |

## Common mistakes

- Saying red-shift means the light "turns red". The whole spectrum shifts towards longer wavelengths.
- Using the observed wavelength as the denominator. It is the reference wavelength, λ₀.
- Stating that red-shift shows galaxies moving away from Earth because Earth is at the centre.
- Describing the CMB without saying what it is evidence of, or calling it "microwaves from stars".
- Saying the Big Bang was an explosion at a point in space.
- Mixing up the direction of frequency change: a receding source gives a **lower** frequency.

## Exam technique

- **Describe** (8.15P): give both the wavelength and the frequency change, and say whether the source is approaching or receding.
- **Calculate** (8.16P): subtract first to get Δλ, then divide by λ₀; give v in m/s.
- **Explain** (8.18P): link three ideas: all galaxies red-shifted → further ones faster → space expanding.
- **Describe evidence** (8.14P): for each piece of evidence, state the observation, then what it shows.
