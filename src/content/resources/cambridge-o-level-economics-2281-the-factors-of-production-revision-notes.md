These are condensed notes for section 1.2, *The factors of production* (1.2.1 definitions and rewards; 1.2.2 mobility; 1.2.3 quantity and quality), of the Cambridge O Level Economics 2281 syllabus for examination in 2026. For full explanations and worked examples, use the [study guide on the factors of production](/resources/cambridge-o-level-economics-2281-the-factors-of-production/). Then try the [practice questions on the factors of production](/resources/cambridge-o-level-economics-2281-the-factors-of-production-practice/). All examples are invented.

## Key definitions

| Term | Definition to learn |
|---|---|
| Factors of production | The resources used to produce goods and services |
| Land | Natural resources used in production |
| Labour | Human effort, physical and mental, used in production |
| Capital | Man-made goods used to produce other goods and services |
| Enterprise | Organising the other factors and taking the risks of production |
| Occupational mobility | How easily a factor moves between uses or jobs |
| Geographical mobility | How easily a factor moves between places |
| Depreciation | Capital wearing out or becoming out of date |

## Factor, reward, quick test

| Factor | Reward | Quick test |
|---|---|---|
| Land | Rent | Provided by nature? |
| Labour | Wages | Human effort? |
| Capital | Interest | Made by people, used to make something else? |
| Enterprise | Profit | Organises and bears risk? |

Memory line: **L**and–**R**ent, **L**abour–**W**ages, **C**apital–**I**nterest, **E**nterprise–**P**rofit.

## The nature of each factor in one line

- **Land:** fixed in total amount; quality can be improved; renewable (forests, fish) or non-renewable (oil, minerals).
- **Labour:** quantity (workers × hours) and quality (skills, education, health).
- **Capital:** defined by use (a taxi is capital, a family car is not); depreciates; building it uses resources now for more output later.
- **Enterprise:** combines factors, makes decisions, bears risk; profit can be negative.

**Worked reminder: classify.** An invented printing shop: the premises' land (land, rent); two printers and a guillotine (capital, interest); three staff (labour, wages); the owner who took out a loan and runs the shop (enterprise, profit).

## Mobility by factor

| Factor | Geographical mobility | Occupational mobility |
|---|---|---|
| Land | None: cannot be moved | Some: use can change (farm to housing), limited by quality and rules |
| Labour | Varies: housing, family, moving costs, information, language, rules | Varies: skills, training time and cost, ability, licences |
| Capital | High for vans, laptops; none for ports, dams, factory buildings | High for general-purpose capital; low for specialised machines |
| Enterprise | Varies, like labour | Usually high: organising skills transfer between industries |

**Chain to write.** Lack of skills (occupational immobility) and high housing costs (geographical immobility) → workers cannot take vacancies elsewhere → unemployment persists in one region while jobs go unfilled in another.

## Quantity and quality: causes of change

**Land**

- Quantity rises: new discoveries, land reclamation.
- Quantity falls: depletion, erosion, flooding.
- Quality: irrigation, fertilisers and drainage improve it; over-farming and pollution worsen it.

**Labour**

- Quantity: population size, age structure, retirement and school-leaving ages, migration, the proportion who choose to work.
- Quality: education, training, healthcare, nutrition, experience.

**Capital**

- Quantity: rises with investment, falls with depreciation.
- Quality: improved by new technology.

**Enterprise**

- Quantity: ease of starting a business, government support, tax on profit, attitudes to risk.
- Quality: business education, experience, advice.

**Worked reminder: labour force (invented).** Working-age population 8 million; 55% in the labour force → 4.4 million. Net immigration adds 0.2 million workers → 4.6 million, a rise of 4.5% (0.2 ÷ 4.4 × 100).

## Must-know distinctions

- Quantity (how many) versus quality (how productive).
- Occupational (between jobs) versus geographical (between places).
- Capital (goods used in production) versus money (used to buy factors).
- Consumer goods (used up by households) versus capital goods (used by producers).
- Rent as a factor reward (for land) versus everyday rent for a flat.

## Exam traps

- Swapping rewards: capital–profit or enterprise–interest.
- Calling money or savings "capital".
- Saying land is completely immobile. It is geographically immobile but can change use.
- Treating all capital as mobile or all as immobile: it depends on the type.
- Describing an increase in training as an increase in the quantity of labour.
- Listing influences on mobility without saying which kind of mobility they affect.

## Quick self-test

1. Define capital.
2. State the reward to enterprise.
3. Why is a taxi capital but a family car is not?
4. Give **two** examples of land.
5. Define occupational mobility.
6. Give **two** influences on the geographical mobility of labour.
7. Explain why land is geographically immobile but can be occupationally mobile.
8. Give an example of capital that is geographically immobile.
9. State **two** causes of an increase in the quantity of labour.
10. State **two** ways the quality of labour can be improved.
11. What reduces the quantity of capital over time?
12. A country's labour force is 5 million. It rises by 0.25 million. Calculate the percentage increase.

### Answers

1. Man-made goods used to produce other goods and services.
2. Profit.
3. The taxi is used to produce a service; the family car is used for the household's own consumption.
4. Any two: fields, forests, minerals, fish, rivers, oil.
5. How easily a factor can move from one use or job to another.
6. Any two: housing costs, family ties, cost of moving, information about jobs, language or culture, rules on working elsewhere.
7. It cannot be moved, but its use can be changed, for example from farming to housing.
8. Any one: a dam, a port, a factory building, a railway line.
9. Any two: population growth, net immigration, a higher retirement age, more people choosing to work.
10. Any two: education, training, better healthcare, better nutrition, experience.
11. Depreciation (wearing out or becoming out of date).
12. 0.25 ÷ 5 × 100 = 5%.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the difference between quantity and quality of labour?**
Quantity is the number of workers and the hours they work. Quality is how productive each worker is, which depends on skills, education, health and experience.

**Is all capital geographically mobile?**
No. Portable capital such as vans and laptops is mobile, but fixed capital such as a port, a dam or a factory building cannot be moved.
