These questions cover section 2.7 (Industrial/employee relations) of the IB *Business management guide*, first assessment 2024. The whole section is HL only. Learn the method from the [2.7 study guide](/resources/ib-dp-business-2-7/) and the [2.7 revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-business-2-7-revision-notes/). All businesses and figures are invented, and money is in dollars.

## Questions

### Section A: short-answer questions

**1.** Define the term *collective bargaining*. **[2]**

**2.** Distinguish between a work-to-rule and strike action. **[2]**

**3.** State whether each of these is an employee or an employer approach to conflict: (i) lockout, (ii) work-to-rule, (iii) changes of contract. **[3]**

**4.** Explain one advantage to an employer of a single-union agreement. **[2]**

**5.** Explain one reason why a threat of redundancies may fail to end a dispute. **[2]**

**6.** Distinguish between conciliation and arbitration. **[2]**

### Section B: structured questions

Questions 7 to 10 use the case below.

Wexcombe Care runs 12 care homes with 600 care workers paid an average of 28,000 a year. Most care workers belong to one of three unions. Wexcombe wants to introduce 12-hour shifts to reduce agency staff costs. The unions say longer shifts will harm safety and family life, and they have announced a work-to-rule. Care workers' skills are in short supply locally, and residents cannot be left without care.

**7.** Identify two sources of conflict at Wexcombe. **[2]**

**8.** The unions also want a 4% pay rise, and Wexcombe has offered 2%.

**(a)** Calculate the annual cost of the union claim and of Wexcombe's offer. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the annual gap per care worker. **[1]**

**9.** Analyse the bargaining power of Wexcombe's care workers. **[6]**

**10.** Evaluate whether Wexcombe should respond to the work-to-rule by changing employees' contracts to introduce 12-hour shifts. **[8]**

**11.** Ardlow Logistics has had three strikes in four years. Its managers are considering a no-strike agreement and greater employee participation through a works council. Discuss which of these would better reduce future conflict. **[8]**

**12.** Explain two reasons why a lockout may be a high-risk approach for an employer. **[4]**

## Answers

### Section A

**1.** Negotiation between an employer and representatives of employees, such as a trade union [1], over pay and conditions for the whole group rather than one person at a time [1]. **[2]**

**2.** In a work-to-rule staff keep working but do only what their contract and rules require, withdrawing goodwill such as overtime [1]; in strike action staff stop work completely and normally lose pay [1]. **[2]**

**3.** (i) Employer [1]. (ii) Employee [1]. (iii) Employer [1]. **[3]**

**4.** The employer negotiates with one union instead of several [1], so bargaining is quicker and simpler, and there is less risk of rival unions making competing claims [1]. **[2]**

**5.** If staff have scarce skills or unemployment is low [1], they may not believe the threat or may find other jobs, so the threat hardens resistance or causes skilled staff to leave [1]. **[2]**

**6.** Conciliation uses a neutral third party to help both sides reach their own agreement [1], whereas in arbitration the third party hears both sides and gives a ruling [1]. **[2]**

### Section B

**7.** Any two: change in working conditions (12-hour shifts) [1]; safety concerns [1]; effect on family life [1]; pay [1]. Maximum two. **[2]**

**8. (a)** Payroll = 600 × 28,000 = 16,800,000; claim = 4% = **672,000** [1]; offer = 2% = **336,000** [1]

**(b)** (672,000 − 336,000) ÷ 600 = **560 per care worker** [1] **[3]**

**9.** Care workers' skills are in short supply locally [1], so Wexcombe cannot easily replace them, which strengthens their position [1]. Residents cannot be left without care, so the service cannot be stored or delayed [1]; any action has an immediate effect, adding to their power [1]. Most belong to unions, so action is collective, though three unions may not act together, which weakens them [1]. However, care workers may be reluctant to harm vulnerable residents, which limits how far they will go, so their power is strong but constrained [1]. **[6]**

**10.** For: changing contracts would achieve the 12-hour shifts quickly [1] and cut agency staff costs, which is the business objective [1]. Against: the workforce has scarce skills, so imposed contracts may cause resignations that raise agency costs instead [1]; unions may escalate from a work-to-rule to strike action [1], which is especially harmful because residents need continuous care [1]; safety concerns raised by staff may be valid, and an incident could damage Wexcombe's reputation [1]. Alternative: collective bargaining, perhaps with conciliation, or a trial of 12-hour shifts in one or two homes [1]. Judgement: Wexcombe should not change contracts now, because its bargaining position is weak and the risk to residents is high; it could reconsider only if negotiation fails and a trial shows no safety harm [1]. **[8]**

**11.** No-strike agreement: protects continuity of service after three strikes in four years [1]; but staff give up their strongest pressure, so unions will want something in return, such as binding arbitration [1]; it does not remove the causes of conflict, so grievances may show up in other ways, such as low morale [1]. Works council: gives employees a voice in decisions before they are made [1], which addresses conflict caused by staff feeling unheard [1]; but decisions take longer and managers share some control [1]. Judgement: the works council better reduces future conflict because it tackles causes rather than banning one response [1]; a no-strike agreement could follow once trust has improved, linked to a fair dispute procedure [1]. **[8]**

**12.** Reason 1: output stops completely during a lockout [1], so the employer loses revenue and customers may move to rivals [1]. Reason 2: it is a hostile act that damages trust [1], so morale and cooperation may stay low after the dispute ends, and skilled staff may leave [1]. **[4]**

## Where marks are usually lost

* Labelling approaches to the wrong side.
* Confusing conciliation and arbitration.
* Analysing bargaining power without using facts from the case.
* Recommending an employer approach without considering how employees will respond.
* Treating a no-strike agreement as a cure for the causes of conflict.
