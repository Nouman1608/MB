## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Which **one** of the following is most likely to increase the demand for pounds?
A: UK households buy more imported cars.
B: More students from the USA pay fees to UK universities.
C: UK firms build new factories overseas.
D: UK interest rates are cut. **[1]**

**2.** The exchange rate changes from £1 = $1.25 to £1 = $1.40. Which **one** statement is correct?
A: The pound has depreciated, so UK exports are cheaper in the USA.
B: The pound has appreciated, so UK exports are cheaper in the USA.
C: The pound has appreciated, so imports from the USA are cheaper in the UK.
D: The pound has depreciated, so imports from the USA are dearer in the UK. **[1]**

**3.** A student changes £350 into Japanese yen at £1 = ¥185. The bureau first deducts 2% commission from the pounds. Calculate how many yen the student receives. **[2]**

**4.** A UK shopper buys a laptop priced at $864 from a US website. The exchange rate is £1 = $1.35. Calculate the price of the laptop in pounds. **[2]**

**5.** State **two** reasons why UK residents supply pounds on the foreign exchange market. **[2]**

**6.** The value of the pound against the euro falls from €1.25 to €1.15. Calculate the percentage change in the value of the pound. **[2]**

**7.** Explain what is meant by a depreciation of a currency. **[2]**

### Section B: structured questions

**8.** Draw a demand and supply diagram and use it to analyse the likely effect of a cut in UK interest rates on the value of the pound. **[6]**

**9.** The table shows the average value of Country K's currency, the krone, against the dollar (illustrative).

| Year | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $ per krone | 1.48 | 1.52 | 1.31 | 1.27 | 1.36 |

**(a)** Identify the year-to-year change with the largest fall and calculate it as a percentage, to one decimal place. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage change in the value of the krone from Year 1 to Year 5, to one decimal place. **[2]**
**(c)** Analyse **one** possible reason for the change between Year 4 and Year 5. **[2]**

**10.** Calderbrook Ceramics, a UK firm, sells 50,000 mugs a year in the USA at £6 each. Each mug uses glaze bought from the USA for $2.40, plus £2.50 of other UK costs. The pound appreciates from £1 = $1.20 to £1 = $1.50. The firm keeps its price at £6.

**(a)** Calculate the dollar price of a mug before and after the change. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the cost of the glaze per mug in pounds before and after the change. **[2]**
**(c)** Price elasticity of demand for the mugs in the USA is −1.2. Calculate the firm's total profit before and after the change and analyse the result. **[4]**

**11.** Explain **two** effects of an appreciation of the pound on UK consumers. **[4]**

**12.** Evaluate whether a depreciation of the pound is good for UK producers. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** **B** [1]. US students need pounds to pay UK fees, so demand for pounds rises. A and C increase the supply of pounds; D makes holding pounds less attractive. **[1]**

**2.** **C** [1]. Each pound buys more dollars, so dollar-priced imports cost fewer pounds. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Use SPICED: Strong Pound, Imports Cheap, Exports Dear. Options A and B pair an appreciation with cheaper exports, which is the wrong way round.

**3.** Pounds after commission = 350 × 0.98 = £343 [1]. Yen = 343 × 185 = **¥63,455** [1]. **[2]**

**4.** 864 ÷ 1.35 [1] = **£640** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A pound buys more than one dollar, so the pound figure must be smaller than the dollar figure. Multiplying gives £1,166.40, which fails this check.

**5.** Any two, one mark each: to pay for imports of goods or services [1]; to pay for holidays abroad [1]; to save or invest overseas, such as buying foreign shares or building a factory abroad [1]; speculation that the pound will fall [1]. **[2]**

**6.** (1.15 − 1.25) ÷ 1.25 × 100 [1] = **−8.0%** [1]. **[2]**

**7.** A fall in the value of a currency against another currency [1], so one unit buys less foreign currency, for example £1 falling from $1.30 to $1.20 [1]. **[2]**

**8.**
- Diagram: vertical axis labelled as the price of the pound, for example "$ per £", and horizontal axis "quantity of pounds" [1].
- Original demand and supply curves with the equilibrium rate marked [1].
- Demand curve shifted left and/or supply curve shifted right, with the new curve labelled [1].
- New, lower equilibrium rate shown [1].
- Analysis: lower interest rates reduce the return on saving in the UK, so fewer foreign savers buy pounds and demand falls [1].
- UK savers move money to countries with higher returns, selling pounds, so supply rises, and the pound depreciates [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Full marks need a link from interest rates to the decisions of savers, not just "the pound falls". If you show both shifts, the new quantity can be left roughly where it was.

**9. (a)** Year 2 to Year 3 [1]: (1.31 − 1.52) ÷ 1.52 × 100 = **−13.8%** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** (1.36 − 1.48) ÷ 1.48 × 100 [1] = **−8.1%** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** The krone rose by about 7.1% [1]. One reason, with a chain: for example, higher interest rates in Country K attracted foreign savers, raising demand for the krone; or rising demand for Country K's exports meant buyers needed more krone [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* "The krone went up" earns nothing on its own. Quote the figures, then give a cause that works through demand or supply.

**10. (a)** Before: 6 × 1.20 = **$7.20** [1]. After: 6 × 1.50 = **$9.00** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Before: 2.40 ÷ 1.20 = **£2.00** [1]. After: 2.40 ÷ 1.50 = **£1.60** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)**
- Profit per mug rises from 6 − 2.00 − 2.50 = £1.50 to 6 − 1.60 − 2.50 = £1.90 [1].
- The dollar price rises 25%, so quantity falls by 1.2 × 25% = 30%, from 50,000 to 35,000 mugs [1].
- Total profit falls from 50,000 × 1.50 = £75,000 to 35,000 × 1.90 = **£66,500** [1].
- Analysis: demand is price elastic, so the loss of sales outweighs the saving on imported glaze, and the appreciation harms the firm overall [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Strong answers notice both effects of the appreciation: dearer exports and cheaper imported inputs. The figures then decide which one wins.

**11.** Effect 1: imported goods become cheaper, because each pound buys more foreign currency [1], so consumers' real incomes rise and they can buy more, or more variety, for the same spending [1]. Effect 2: holidays abroad become cheaper [1], because hotels and meals priced in foreign currency cost fewer pounds [1]. (Also credit: lower inflation from cheaper imports, which protects purchasing power; or a risk to consumers who work in export industries, whose jobs may be less secure.) **[4]**

**12.**
- Benefit: UK exports become cheaper in foreign currency [1], so sales abroad may rise, increasing revenue and possibly profit and jobs [1].
- Benefit: firms competing with imports at home gain, because imported rivals become dearer [1].
- Benefit: exporters may instead keep foreign prices the same and receive more pounds per sale, widening profit margins [1].
- Cost: firms using imported raw materials or components face higher costs in pounds [1], which squeezes margins or forces price rises [1].
- Evaluation: the gain depends on the price elasticity of demand for exports and on the share of costs that are imported, and on how long the depreciation lasts [1].
- A supported judgement, for example: good for exporters with elastic demand and mainly UK costs, but harmful for producers that rely on imported inputs and sell mainly at home [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* "Producers" are not one group. Splitting them into exporters, import-competing firms and firms using imported inputs is the quickest way to build a balanced answer and a real judgement.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Converting in the wrong direction, or forgetting to deduct commission first.
- Percentage changes calculated from the new value instead of the old one.
- Shifting demand for pounds when UK residents' spending abroad changes (it is supply).
- Describing data with no figures, or calculating with no description of the pattern.
- Ignoring imported inputs when judging the effect on a firm.
- Ending an evaluation with no judgement or no named condition.
