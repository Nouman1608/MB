## Questions

**1.** Define the standard enthalpy change of atomisation of an element, and write an equation, with state symbols, for the standard enthalpy change of atomisation of bromine. **[3]**

**2.** This question is about the electron affinities of sulfur.

**(a)** Define the first electron affinity of an element. **[2]**
**(b)** Write equations, with state symbols, for the first and second electron affinities of sulfur. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why the second electron affinity of sulfur is endothermic. **[1]**

**3.** **(a)** Define lattice energy as used in this specification. **[2]**
**(b)** Write an equation for the lattice energy of magnesium bromide. **[1]**
**(c)** Define the enthalpy change of hydration of an ion, and write an equation for the hydration of the calcium ion. **[2]**

**4.** Use these values to construct a Born-Haber cycle for barium iodide, BaI₂, and calculate its lattice energy.

ΔfH(BaI₂) = −602; ΔatH(Ba) = +180; first ionisation energy of Ba = +503; second ionisation energy of Ba = +965; ΔatH(I) = +107; first electron affinity of I = −295. **[6]**

**5.** Calculate the first electron affinity of bromine from these values: ΔfH(CsBr) = −406; ΔatH(Cs) = +76; first ionisation energy of Cs = +376; ΔatH(Br) = +112; lattice energy of CsBr = −645. **[3]**

**6.** The table gives lattice energies (kJ mol⁻¹).

| Compound | Experimental (Born-Haber) | Theoretical (electrostatic) |
|---|---|---|
| NaBr | −747 | −733 |
| AgBr | −903 | −816 |
| MgI₂ | −2327 | −1944 |

**(a)** Identify the compound with the greatest degree of covalent bonding. Justify your choice with a calculation. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain, in terms of the ions present, why this compound shows the most covalent character. **[3]**

**7.** Use these values to draw a labelled energy level diagram for dissolving calcium bromide, CaBr₂, and calculate its enthalpy change of solution.

Lattice energy of CaBr₂ = −2176; ΔhydH(Ca²⁺) = −1616; ΔhydH(Br⁻) = −332. **[4]**

**8.** Lithium bromide dissolves exothermically: ΔsolH(LiBr) = −49. The lattice energy of LiBr is −805 and ΔhydH(Li⁺) = −522. Calculate ΔhydH(Br⁻). **[3]**

**9.** **(a)** Explain why the enthalpy change of hydration of Mg²⁺ is more exothermic than that of Na⁺. **[2]**
**(b)** Predict, with a reason, which of KF and KCl has the more exothermic lattice energy. **[2]**

**10.** Data at 298 K for dissolving two salts:

| Salt | ΔsolH / kJ mol⁻¹ | ΔSsystem / J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ |
|---|---|---|
| NaBr | −0.6 | +54.6 |
| CaF₂ | +19.9 | −149.4 |

**(a)** Calculate ΔStotal for dissolving each salt at 298 K. **[3]**
**(b)** Use your answers to predict the solubility of each salt. **[1]**
**(c)** Suggest why ΔSsystem for CaF₂ is negative. **[1]**

**11.** In Unit 2 you met the trends in solubility of the Group 2 hydroxides and sulfates. Explain both trends in terms of lattice energy and enthalpy change of hydration. **[6]**

**12.** Strontium oxide, SrO, is an ionic solid. Use these values:

ΔfH(SrO) = −592; ΔatH(Sr) = +164; first ionisation energy of Sr = +550; second ionisation energy of Sr = +1064; ΔatH(O) = +249; first electron affinity of O = −141; lattice energy of SrO = −3223.

**(a)** Write equations, with state symbols, for the atomisation of oxygen and for the second electron affinity of oxygen. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the second electron affinity of oxygen. **[3]**
**(c)** Explain the sign of your answer to (b). **[1]**
**(d)** The theoretical lattice energy of SrO is −3189. Comment on the bonding in SrO. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** The enthalpy change when one mole of gaseous atoms is formed from the element in its standard state [1], under standard conditions (100 kPa and a stated temperature, usually 298 K) [1]. ½Br₂(l) → Br(g) [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* the equation must start from liquid bromine and produce one mole of atoms; Br₂(l) → 2Br(g) shows twice the enthalpy change and is unlikely to be accepted.

**2. (a)** The enthalpy change when each atom in one mole of gaseous atoms [1] gains one electron to form one mole of gaseous 1− ions [1].
**(b)** S(g) + e⁻ → S⁻(g) [1]; S⁻(g) + e⁻ → S²⁻(g) [1].
**(c)** The electron is added to a negative ion, so energy is needed to overcome the repulsion [1].
*Examiner insight:* "gaseous" is needed for both the atoms and the ions in (a); state symbols on every species are needed in (b).

**3. (a)** The enthalpy change when one mole of an ionic solid is formed [1] from its gaseous ions [1].
**(b)** Mg²⁺(g) + 2Br⁻(g) → MgBr₂(s) [1]
**(c)** The enthalpy change when one mole of gaseous ions dissolves in water to form one mole of aqueous ions [1]. Ca²⁺(g) + aq → Ca²⁺(aq) [1].
*Examiner insight:* describing lattice energy as breaking the solid into ions reverses the sign, so it does not match this specification's exothermic definition.

**4.** Cycle: Ba(s) + I₂(s) → Ba(g) → Ba⁺(g) → Ba²⁺(g); I₂(s) → 2I(g) → 2I⁻(g); then Ba²⁺(g) + 2I⁻(g) → BaI₂(s), with ΔfH linking the elements to BaI₂(s) [1]. All species carry state symbols and arrows point the right way [1]. Barium steps: 180 + 503 + 965 = +1648 [1]. Iodine steps: 2(107) + 2(−295) = −376 [1]. ΔfH = 1648 − 376 + lattice energy, so lattice energy = −602 − 1272 [1] = **−1874 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* both ionisation energies of barium and two moles of iodine atoms and electrons must appear; a single iodine term is a common way to lose two marks.

**5.** −406 = 76 + 376 + 112 + EA + (−645) [1]; EA = −406 − 76 − 376 − 112 + 645 [1] = **−325 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* writing the full Hess equation before rearranging makes sign errors far easier for you, and the marker, to trace.

**6. (a)** **MgI₂** [1]: its experimental value is 383 kJ mol⁻¹ (about 20%) more exothermic than theory, compared with 87 for AgBr and 14 for NaBr [1].
**(b)** Mg²⁺ is small and doubly charged, so it has a high charge density and high polarising power [1]. I⁻ is large, so its outer electrons are far from the nucleus and easily distorted [1]. The cation pulls anion electron density into the region between the ions, giving partial covalent bonding [1].
*Examiner insight:* an answer that only says "MgI₂ is more covalent" without the polarising power of the cation and the polarisability of the anion gains little in (b).

**7.** Diagram: Ca²⁺(g) + 2Br⁻(g) at the top; CaBr₂(s) 2176 below; Ca²⁺(aq) + 2Br⁻(aq) 2280 below the gaseous ions [1]. Hydration total: −1616 + 2(−332) = −2280 [1]. ΔsolH = +2176 − 2280 [1] = **−104 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* the lattice energy arrow must point from the gaseous ions down to the solid; draw ΔsolH from the solid to the aqueous ions so the sign is clear.

**8.** ΔsolH = −(lattice energy) + ΔhydH(Li⁺) + ΔhydH(Br⁻) [1]. −49 = 805 − 522 + ΔhydH(Br⁻) [1]. ΔhydH(Br⁻) = **−332 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* a positive answer here should prompt you to recheck signs, since hydration is always exothermic.

**9. (a)** Mg²⁺ has a higher charge [1] and a smaller radius than Na⁺, so it attracts the δ− oxygen atoms of water molecules more strongly [1].
**(b)** **KF** [1]: F⁻ is smaller than Cl⁻, so the ions are closer and the electrostatic attraction is stronger [1].
*Examiner insight:* mention both factors in (a); naming only charge or only radius usually earns one of the two marks.

**10. (a)** NaBr: ΔSsurroundings = −(−600) / 298 = +2.0; ΔStotal = 54.6 + 2.0 = **+56.6 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹** [1]. CaF₂: ΔSsurroundings = −19 900 / 298 = −66.8 [1]; ΔStotal = −149.4 − 66.8 = **−216.2 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹** [1].
**(b)** NaBr is soluble (ΔStotal positive); CaF₂ is insoluble (ΔStotal negative) [1].
**(c)** Small, doubly charged Ca²⁺ and small F⁻ ions hold water molecules in an ordered shell, which outweighs the disorder gained when the lattice breaks up [1].
*Examiner insight:* ΔsolH must be converted to J mol⁻¹ before dividing by T; mixing units gives a value out by a factor of 1000.

**11.** Sulfates become less soluble down the group [1]. The sulfate ion is large, so as the cation radius increases, lattice energy becomes only slightly less exothermic [1]. Cation hydration enthalpy becomes much less exothermic, so ΔsolH becomes more endothermic [1]. Hydroxides become more soluble down the group [1]. The hydroxide ion is small, so increasing cation size makes lattice energy much less exothermic [1]. Lattice energy falls faster than hydration enthalpy, so ΔsolH becomes more favourable [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* each trend needs both quantities compared; describing how lattice energy changes without saying what happens to hydration enthalpy leaves the explanation incomplete.

**12. (a)** ½O₂(g) → O(g) [1]; O⁻(g) + e⁻ → O²⁻(g) [1].
**(b)** −592 = 164 + 550 + 1064 + 249 + (−141) + EA₂ + (−3223) [1]. Known steps sum to −1337 [1], so EA₂ = −592 + 1337 = **+745 kJ mol⁻¹** [1].
**(c)** Positive (endothermic): the incoming electron is repelled by the negative O⁻ ion [1].
**(d)** The experimental value is only 34 kJ mol⁻¹ (about 1%) more exothermic than the theoretical value [1], so SrO is close to purely ionic with very little polarisation [1].
*Examiner insight:* in (d) a comment needs evidence; quoting the size of the difference and linking it to the ionic model is what turns an opinion into an answer.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Defining lattice energy as an endothermic break-up instead of the formation of one mole of solid from gaseous ions.
- Using Br₂(l) → 2Br(g) or Cl₂(g) → 2Cl(g) for atomisation, which describes two moles of atoms.
- Forgetting the second ionisation energy of a Group 2 metal.
- Using one electron affinity and one atomisation term for a compound with two anions.
- Missing state symbols, especially (g) on ions in lattice and electron affinity equations.
- Dividing a ΔsolH in kJ by T and adding it directly to an entropy in J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹.
- Assuming endothermic dissolving means the salt is insoluble.
- Explaining covalent character without referring to polarisation of the anion by the cation.
- Giving the Group 2 solubility trends without comparing changes in lattice and hydration enthalpies.
