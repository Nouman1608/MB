Work only from the data printed in each question. Enthalpy changes are in kJ mol⁻¹ and entropies in J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹.

## Questions

**1.** State what is meant by the standard enthalpy change of atomisation of an element. Write an equation, including state symbols, representing ΔatH for iodine. **[3]**

**2.** This question is about the electron affinities of sulfur.

**(a)** Define the first electron affinity of an element. **[2]**
**(b)** Write equations, with state symbols, for the first and second electron affinities of sulfur. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why the second electron affinity of sulfur is endothermic. **[1]**

**3.** **(a)** Define lattice energy as it is used in this specification. **[2]**
**(b)** Write an equation for the lattice energy of potassium oxide. **[1]**
**(c)** Define the enthalpy change of hydration of an ion, and write an equation for the hydration of the aluminium ion. **[2]**

**4.** Use these values to construct a Born-Haber cycle for barium iodide, BaI₂, and calculate its lattice energy.

ΔfH(BaI₂) = −602; ΔatH(Ba) = +180; first ionisation energy of Ba = +503; second ionisation energy of Ba = +965; ΔatH(I) = +107; first electron affinity of I = −295. **[6]**

**5.** Lithium fluoride has ΔfH(LiF) = −616 and a lattice energy of −1046. Calculate the first electron affinity of fluorine, given ΔatH(Li) = +159, first ionisation energy of Li = +520 and ΔatH(F) = +79. **[3]**

**6.** The table compares lattice energies (kJ mol⁻¹) for three iodides.

| Compound | Experimental (Born-Haber) | Theoretical (electrostatic) |
|---|---|---|
| CsI | −604 | −599 |
| AgI | −889 | −778 |
| MgI₂ | −2320 | −1950 |

**(a)** Identify the iodide with the greatest degree of covalent bonding. Justify your choice with a calculation. **[2]**
**(b)** All three compounds contain the same anion. Explain why the extent of covalent character differs so much between CsI and MgI₂. **[3]**

**7.** Draw a labelled energy level diagram for dissolving barium chloride, BaCl₂, and use it to calculate the enthalpy change of solution.

Lattice energy of BaCl₂ = −2056; ΔhydH(Ba²⁺) = −1341; ΔhydH(Cl⁻) = −364. **[4]**

**8.** Caesium fluoride dissolves exothermically: ΔsolH(CsF) = −37. The lattice energy of CsF is −745 and ΔhydH(F⁻) = −506. Calculate ΔhydH(Cs⁺). **[3]**

**9.** **(a)** Explain why ΔhydH(Ca²⁺) is far more exothermic than ΔhydH(K⁺). **[2]**
**(b)** Predict, with a reason, which of calcium oxide and calcium sulfide has the more exothermic lattice energy. **[2]**

**10.** Data at 298 K for dissolving two salts:

| Salt | ΔsolH / kJ mol⁻¹ | ΔSsystem / J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ |
|---|---|---|
| MgSO₄ | −91.2 | −209.6 |
| CaF₂ | +19.9 | −149.6 |

**(a)** Calculate ΔSsurroundings and ΔStotal for dissolving each salt at 298 K. **[4]**
**(b)** Use your answers to predict the solubility of each salt in water. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain why ΔSsystem is negative for both salts, and why MgSO₄ dissolves even so. **[2]**

**11.** In Unit 2 you learned how the solubility of the Group 2 hydroxides and sulfates changes down the group. Explain both trends in terms of lattice energy and enthalpy change of hydration. **[6]**

**12.** Calcium oxide, CaO, is an ionic solid. Use these values:

ΔfH(CaO) = −635.1; ΔatH(Ca) = +178.2; first ionisation energy of Ca = +589.8; second ionisation energy of Ca = +1145.4; ΔatH(O) = +249.2; first electron affinity of O = −141.0; lattice energy of CaO = −3401.

**(a)** Write equations, with state symbols, for the atomisation of oxygen and for the second electron affinity of oxygen. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the second electron affinity of oxygen. **[3]**
**(c)** Explain the sign of your answer to (b). **[1]**
**(d)** A theoretical value of −3371 has been calculated for the lattice energy of CaO. Comment on the bonding in CaO. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** The enthalpy change for producing 1 mol of gaseous atoms [1] from the element in its standard state, under standard conditions [1]. ½I₂(s) → I(g) [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* the equation must start from solid iodine and make one mole of atoms; I₂(s) → 2I(g) represents twice the quantity and would not normally gain the equation mark.

**2. (a)** The enthalpy change when 1 mol of gaseous atoms [1] each gain one electron to form 1 mol of gaseous 1− ions [1].
**(b)** S(g) + e⁻ → S⁻(g) [1]; S⁻(g) + e⁻ → S²⁻(g) [1].
**(c)** S⁻ is already negative, so it repels the incoming electron and energy has to be put in [1].
*Examiner insight:* in (a), "gaseous" is needed for both the atoms and the ions; in (b), every species needs a state symbol.

**3. (a)** The enthalpy change when 1 mol of an ionic solid is made [1] from its gaseous ions [1].
**(b)** 2K⁺(g) + O²⁻(g) → K₂O(s) [1]
**(c)** The enthalpy change as 1 mol of an ion in the gas phase is hydrated, giving 1 mol of that ion in aqueous solution [1]. Al³⁺(g) + aq → Al³⁺(aq) [1].
*Examiner insight:* describing lattice energy as breaking a solid into ions gives the reverse process, so it does not match this specification's exothermic definition and loses the definition marks.

**4.** Cycle: Ba(s) → Ba(g) → Ba⁺(g) → Ba²⁺(g); I₂(s) → 2I(g) → 2I⁻(g); then Ba²⁺(g) + 2I⁻(g) → BaI₂(s), with ΔfH linking Ba(s) + I₂(s) to BaI₂(s) [1]. Every species has a state symbol and every arrow points the correct way [1]. Barium steps: 180 + 503 + 965 = +1648 [1]. Iodine steps: 2(107) + 2(−295) = −376 [1]. −602 = 1648 − 376 + lattice energy, so lattice energy = −602 − 1272 [1] = **−1874 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* both barium ionisation energies and two moles of iodine atoms and electrons must appear; using a single iodine term is a quick way to lose two marks.

**5.** −616 = 159 + 520 + 79 + EA + (−1046) [1]; the known steps total −288, so EA = −616 + 288 [1] = **−328 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* writing the whole Hess equation before rearranging makes a sign slip easy for you, and the examiner, to spot.

**6. (a)** **MgI₂** [1]: its experimental value is 370 kJ mol⁻¹ (19.0%) more exothermic than theory, against 111 (14.3%) for AgI and 5 (0.8%) for CsI [1].
**(b)** Mg²⁺ is small and doubly charged, so it has a much higher charge density and polarising power than Cs⁺ [1]. I⁻ is large, so its electron cloud is easily distorted [1]. Mg²⁺ draws iodide electron density into the region between the ions, giving partial covalent bonding; large, singly charged Cs⁺ barely does this [1].
*Examiner insight:* since the anion is the same throughout, the marks in (b) depend on comparing the two cations; a general statement that "MgI₂ is more covalent" earns little.

**7.** Diagram: Ba²⁺(g) + 2Cl⁻(g) at the top; BaCl₂(s) 2056 below it; Ba²⁺(aq) + 2Cl⁻(aq) 2069 below the gaseous ions [1]. Hydration total: −1341 + 2(−364) = −2069 [1]. ΔsolH = +2056 − 2069 [1] = **−13 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* the lattice energy arrow points down from the gaseous ions to the solid; drawing ΔsolH from the solid to the aqueous ions makes its sign obvious.

**8.** ΔsolH = −(lattice energy) + ΔhydH(Cs⁺) + ΔhydH(F⁻) [1]. −37 = 745 + ΔhydH(Cs⁺) − 506 [1]. ΔhydH(Cs⁺) = **−276 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* hydration is always exothermic, so a positive answer is a signal to recheck your signs before moving on.

**9. (a)** Ca²⁺ has a 2+ charge, double that of K⁺ [1], and a smaller radius, so it pulls the partially negative oxygen ends of water molecules in more strongly [1].
**(b)** **Calcium oxide** [1]: O²⁻ is smaller than S²⁻ with the same charge, so the ions sit closer together and attract more strongly [1].
*Examiner insight:* in (a) give both charge and radius; naming just one factor usually earns only one of the two marks.

**10. (a)** MgSO₄: ΔSsurroundings = −(−91 200) / 298 = +306.0 [1]; ΔStotal = −209.6 + 306.0 = **+96.4 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹** [1]. CaF₂: ΔSsurroundings = −19 900 / 298 = −66.8 [1]; ΔStotal = −149.6 − 66.8 = **−216.4 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹** [1].
**(b)** MgSO₄ is soluble (ΔStotal positive); CaF₂ is insoluble (ΔStotal negative) [1].
**(c)** Both salts contain small or doubly charged ions that hold many water molecules in ordered shells, which outweighs the disorder gained as the lattice breaks up [1]. Dissolving MgSO₄ is strongly exothermic, so the large positive ΔSsurroundings more than cancels the negative ΔSsystem [1].
*Examiner insight:* convert ΔsolH to J mol⁻¹ before dividing by T; leaving it in kJ makes ΔSsurroundings a thousand times too small and reverses the MgSO₄ conclusion.

**11.** Sulfates become less soluble down the group [1]. The sulfate ion is large, so as the cation radius increases the lattice energy becomes only slightly less exothermic [1]. Cation hydration enthalpy becomes much less exothermic, so ΔsolH becomes more endothermic [1]. Hydroxides become more soluble down the group [1]. The hydroxide ion is small, so a larger cation makes the lattice energy much less exothermic [1]. Lattice energy falls faster than hydration enthalpy, so ΔsolH becomes more favourable [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* each trend needs the two quantities compared; saying how lattice energy changes without saying what happens to hydration leaves the explanation incomplete.

**12. (a)** ½O₂(g) → O(g) [1]; O⁻(g) + e⁻ → O²⁻(g) [1].
**(b)** −635.1 = 178.2 + 589.8 + 1145.4 + 249.2 + (−141.0) + EA₂ + (−3401) [1]. The known steps total −1379.4 [1], so EA₂ = −635.1 + 1379.4 = **+744.3 kJ mol⁻¹** [1].
**(c)** Positive (endothermic): the incoming electron is repelled by the negatively charged O⁻ ion [1].
**(d)** The experimental value is only 30 kJ mol⁻¹ (0.9%) more exothermic than the theoretical value [1], so CaO is very close to purely ionic, with little polarisation of the oxide ion [1].
*Examiner insight:* a "comment" needs evidence from the data; quoting the size of the gap and linking it to the ionic model is what earns both marks in (d).

## Where marks are usually lost

- Treating lattice energy as an endothermic break-up instead of the formation of 1 mol of solid from gaseous ions.
- Writing I₂(s) → 2I(g) or Cl₂(g) → 2Cl(g) for atomisation, which describes two moles of atoms.
- Leaving out the second ionisation energy of a Group 2 metal.
- Using one electron affinity and one atomisation term for a compound with two anions.
- Missing state symbols, especially (g) on ions in lattice energy and electron affinity equations.
- Dividing a ΔsolH in kJ by T and adding the result straight to an entropy in J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹.
- Assuming that endothermic dissolving means a salt is insoluble.
- Explaining covalent character without mentioning polarisation of the anion by the cation.
- Giving the Group 2 solubility trends without comparing changes in both lattice and hydration enthalpies.
