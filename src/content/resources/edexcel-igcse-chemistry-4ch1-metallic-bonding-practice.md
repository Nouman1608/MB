These questions cover sub-topic 1(h) Metallic bonding of the Pearson Edexcel International GCSE Chemistry (4CH1) specification, Issue 3 (September 2024): points 1.52C to 1.54C. Every point has a C reference, so all of this sub-topic is **Paper 2 only**. Revise with the [metallic bonding study guide](/resources/edexcel-igcse-chemistry-4ch1-metallic-bonding/) and the [metallic bonding revision notes](/resources/edexcel-igcse-chemistry-4ch1-metallic-bonding-revision-notes/).

## Section A: short questions

**1.** State what is meant by delocalised electrons. **[1]**

**2.** Name the two types of particle that attract each other in metallic bonding. **[2]**

**3.** State the charge on the ions in a lattice of potassium. **[1]**

**4.** State the meaning of the term malleable. **[1]**

**5.** Give **two** physical properties typical of metals, other than malleability. **[2]**

## Section B: structured questions

**6.** A student draws a 2-D diagram of the lattice in sodium. Her diagram shows rows of circles labelled "Na atom", with no charges, and a few electrons drawn inside some of the circles.

**(a)** Identify **two** errors in her diagram. **[2]**
**(b)** Describe how the diagram should be corrected so it shows metallic bonding. **[2]**

**7.** Magnesium is used in fireworks and in lightweight alloys.

**(a)** Describe the structure and bonding in magnesium. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain why magnesium conducts electricity. **[2]**
**(c)** Magnesium has a melting point of 650 °C and sodium has a melting point of 98 °C. Suggest an explanation for this difference. **[2]**

**8.** Copper is drawn into wires and hammered into sheets.

**(a)** Explain why copper can be hammered into sheets without breaking. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain why copper is a good conductor of heat. **[1]**

**9.** The table shows some properties of four substances.

| Substance | Melting point / °C | Conducts when solid? | Conducts when molten? | Effect of hammering |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | 1085 | Yes | Yes | Flattens |
| B | 3652 (sublimes) | Yes | — | Crumbles into flakes |
| C | 2852 | No | Yes | Shatters |
| D | 17 | No | No | — |

**(a)** Identify the substance that has metallic bonding. Explain your choice using **two** properties from the table. **[3]**
**(b)** Substance B conducts as a solid but is not a metal. Suggest the identity of B. **[1]**
**(c)** Identify the substance that is ionic. Give a reason. **[2]**

**10.** Aluminium and aluminium oxide both have high melting points, but only aluminium conducts electricity as a solid.

**(a)** Describe the type of bonding in aluminium oxide. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain why solid aluminium conducts electricity but solid aluminium oxide does not. **[3]**

**11.** Explain, in terms of structure and bonding, why metals typically have high melting points and are malleable. **[5]**

## Answers

**1.** Electrons that are not attached to any one atom and are free to move through the structure [1].

**2.** Positive metal ions [1] and delocalised electrons [1].

**3.** 1+ [1].

**4.** Can be hammered or bent into shape without breaking [1].

**5.** Any two [1 each]: good electrical conductor; good thermal conductor; high melting point / high boiling point; shiny when freshly cut.

**6. (a)** Any two [1 each]: the particles should be labelled as positive ions, not atoms; the ions should show a + charge; the electrons should be between the ions, not inside them; there are too few electrons.
**(b)** Label each circle Na⁺ (positive ion) [1]. Draw many electrons in the spaces between all the ions, labelled "delocalised electrons" [1].

**7. (a)** A giant lattice of positive magnesium ions (Mg²⁺) in regular layers [1], surrounded by delocalised electrons [1]. Metallic bonding is the strong electrostatic attraction between the positive ions and the delocalised electrons [1].
**(b)** It has delocalised electrons [1] that are free to move through the structure and carry charge [1].
**(c)** Magnesium ions have a higher charge (2+ compared with 1+) / each magnesium atom gives two delocalised electrons [1], so the electrostatic attraction is stronger and needs more energy to overcome [1].

**8. (a)** The copper ions are arranged in layers [1]. When hammered, the layers slide over each other [1]. The delocalised electrons still attract the ions in their new positions, so the bonding is not broken [1].
**(b)** The delocalised electrons move through the metal and transfer energy [1].

**9. (a)** A [1]. It conducts when solid (delocalised electrons) [1] and flattens when hammered, showing it is malleable [1].
**(b)** Graphite (carbon) [1].
**(c)** C [1]: it does not conduct as a solid but does when molten (ions free to move only in the liquid), with a high melting point [1].
*Marking note:* In (a), conduction when molten alone does not identify a metal, because ionic compounds also conduct when molten.

**10. (a)** Ionic bonding (electrostatic attraction between Al³⁺ and O²⁻ ions) [1].
**(b)** Aluminium has delocalised electrons that can move and carry charge [1]. In aluminium oxide the ions are held in fixed positions in the lattice [1], and there are no delocalised electrons, so there are no charged particles free to move [1].

**11.** One mark per point, up to 5:
- Metals have a giant lattice of positive ions and delocalised electrons [1].
- Metallic bonding is the strong electrostatic attraction between the positive ions and the delocalised electrons [1].
- There are many of these strong attractions, so a lot of energy is needed to overcome them, giving high melting points [1].
- The positive ions are arranged in layers that can slide over each other when a force is applied [1].
- The delocalised electrons still attract the ions after the layers move, so the metal changes shape instead of breaking [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Saying "ions carry the current" in a metal.
- Drawing atoms instead of positive ions in the lattice.
- Leaving out why a metal does not break when its layers slide.
- Identifying a metal from "conducts when molten" alone.
- Calling metallic bonding an attraction "between metal atoms".
