## 5.1 The nuclear model of the atom

### The atom (5.1.1)

In the nuclear model, an atom is a tiny central **nucleus** carrying positive charge, with negatively charged **electrons** orbiting it. Almost all of the atom's volume is empty space.

**Alpha-particle scattering.** In this experiment a narrow beam of alpha particles is directed onto an extremely thin metal foil and detectors record where the particles come out. The results and what they show:

| Observation | Conclusion |
|---|---|
| Most particles go straight through, almost undeviated | Most of the atom is empty space and the nucleus is tiny |
| A few particles are deflected through large angles, some almost straight back | A small nucleus holds most of the mass: only something heavy could turn an alpha particle round |
| Deflected particles are repelled | The nucleus is positively charged, like the alpha particles |

Give all three conclusions when asked for evidence, and link "dense" to particles bouncing back.

### The nucleus (5.1.2)

The nucleus contains **protons** (positive) and **neutrons** (no charge). Together they are called nucleons.

- **Proton number Z** (atomic number): the number of protons.
- **Nucleon number A** (mass number): protons and neutrons added together.
- Neutrons = A − Z.

A **nuclide** is a particular kind of nucleus with a given Z and A. It is written ᴬ_ZX, for example ⁶³₂₉Cu, where the top number is A and the bottom number is Z.

An atom is neutral because protons and electrons are equal in number. An atom becomes a **positive ion** by losing electrons and a **negative ion** by gaining electrons. The nucleus does not change. A magnesium ion Mg²⁺ made from ²⁴₁₂Mg has 12 protons, 12 neutrons and 10 electrons.

**Isotopes** are nuclei of the same element (same Z) with different numbers of neutrons (different A). An element can have more than one isotope. Chlorine-35 and chlorine-37 both have 17 protons, but 18 and 20 neutrons.

**Worked example 1.** Copper has two common isotopes, ⁶³₂₉Cu and ⁶⁵₂₉Cu. State how they are alike and different.
- Same Z = 29, so both have 29 protons and (when neutral) 29 electrons.
- Neutrons: 63 − 29 = 34 and 65 − 29 = 36.
- They differ only in neutron number, so they are isotopes of copper.

## 5.2.1 Detecting radioactivity

- **Alpha particles:** a **cloud chamber** shows alpha particles as short, thick, straight tracks of condensed vapour along the path of ionisation. A **spark counter** has a charged wire above a metal gauze; an alpha particle ionises the air and a spark jumps across.
- **Beta particles and gamma radiation:** a **Geiger-Müller (GM) tube** connected to a counter. Radiation entering the tube ionises the gas and each event gives an electrical pulse that is counted.

The **count rate** is the number of counts detected per unit time, in counts per second or counts per minute.

### Background radiation

A GM tube with no source nearby still counts. This is **background radiation**. Significant sources:

- cosmic rays arriving from space
- radon, a gas found in the air
- rocks and buildings
- the food and drink we consume

To find the **corrected count rate** from a source, subtract the background count rate from the measured count rate. Make sure both are per the same time unit first.

**Worked example 2.** A GM tube records 156 counts in 2 minutes with a source in place. With the source removed it records 18 counts in 3 minutes. Find the corrected count rate.
- Measured rate: 156 ÷ 2 = 78 counts per minute.
- Background rate: 18 ÷ 3 = 6 counts per minute.
- Corrected rate: 78 − 6 = **72 counts per minute**.

## 5.2.2 The three types of emission

Unstable nuclei give out radiation. The emission is **spontaneous** (it needs no outside trigger) and **random in direction**.

| | Alpha (α) | Beta (β) | Gamma (γ) |
|---|---|---|---|
| What it is | 2 protons and 2 neutrons (a helium nucleus) | Fast electron from the nucleus | Very high-frequency electromagnetic wave |
| Relative ionising effect | Strongest | Weaker | Weakest |
| Relative penetrating power | Least: stopped by paper or a few centimetres of air | Moderate: stopped by a few millimetres of aluminium | Greatest: only reduced by thick lead or concrete |

Beta here means β⁻, the electron. Strong ionisation and weak penetration go together: an alpha particle loses its energy quickly because it ionises so many atoms along a short path.

**Deflection.** Alpha particles are positive, beta particles are negative and gamma rays are uncharged.
- In an **electric field**, alpha bends towards the negative plate, beta towards the positive plate, and gamma does not bend. Beta bends much more than alpha because its mass is far smaller.
- In a **magnetic field**, alpha and beta curve in opposite directions (they are opposite charges moving the same way) and gamma goes straight.

## 5.2.3 Radioactive decay

Decay happens when an unstable nucleus changes and gives out alpha particles, beta particles and/or gamma radiation. Decay is **spontaneous** and **random**: you cannot predict when a given nucleus will decay, only the chance that it will in a given time. This is why we describe large samples by half-life.

### Decay equations

Write each particle in nuclide notation and make the top numbers balance and the bottom numbers balance.

- **Alpha:** ⁴₂He. A falls by 4, Z falls by 2.
- **Beta:** ⁰₋₁e. A is unchanged, Z rises by 1 (a neutron changes into a proton).
- **Gamma:** no change to A or Z. It often accompanies alpha or beta decay, carrying away surplus energy.

**Worked example 3.** Write equations for (a) alpha decay of americium-241, ²⁴¹₉₅Am, and (b) beta decay of carbon-14, ¹⁴₆C.
- (a) Daughter A = 241 − 4 = 237, Z = 95 − 2 = 93: ²⁴¹₉₅Am → ²³⁷₉₃Np + ⁴₂He.
- (b) A stays 14, Z = 6 + 1 = 7: ¹⁴₆C → ¹⁴₇N + ⁰₋₁e.
- Check (b): top 14 = 14 + 0; bottom 6 = 7 + (−1).

**Worked example 4.** Uranium-238, ²³⁸₉₂U, emits an alpha particle, and the product then emits a beta particle. Find the nuclide after both decays.
- After alpha: ²³⁴₉₀Th.
- After beta: Z = 90 + 1 = 91, A = 234, giving ²³⁴₉₁Pa.

## 5.2.4 Fission and fusion

**Fusion** is the joining of two smaller nuclei to form a larger nucleus, releasing energy. Fusion of hydrogen into helium is the energy source of stars.

**Fission** happens when a nucleus such as uranium-235 absorbs a neutron, becomes unstable and splits into **daughter nuclei**, releasing two or more neutrons and energy. One possible reaction is

²³⁵₉₂U + ¹₀n → ¹⁴¹₅₆Ba + ⁹²₃₆Kr + 3 ¹₀n + energy

Balance: the mass numbers give 236 on the left and 141 + 92 + 3 = 236 on the right; the proton numbers give 92 on the left and 56 + 36 = 92 on the right.

### Chain reaction and the reactor

Each fission releases neutrons, and these can be absorbed by other uranium-235 nuclei, each causing a further fission. This is a **chain reaction**. If it were uncontrolled, the rate of fission would grow rapidly. In a reactor it is controlled:

| Part | Action |
|---|---|
| Moderator (for example graphite or water) | Slows fast neutrons so they are far more likely to be absorbed and cause fission |
| Control rods (for example boron or cadmium) | Absorb neutrons; moved in to slow the reaction and out to speed it up |
| Coolant | Flows through the core, takes thermal energy away and carries it to where steam is made to drive turbines |

To keep the power steady, on average one neutron from each fission must go on to cause one more fission.

## 5.2.5 Half-life

The **half-life** of an isotope is how long it takes for half of the nuclei of that isotope present, whatever the sample size, to decay. The background-corrected count rate also halves in this time.

**Worked example 5.** A sample gives these readings. Background is 20 counts per minute throughout.

| Time (min) | 0 | 15 | 30 | 45 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Measured (counts per minute) | 660 | 340 | 180 | 100 |

- Correct each reading: 640, 320, 160, 80.
- Each 15 minutes the rate halves, so the half-life is **15 minutes**.
- After 60 minutes (four half-lives) the corrected rate is 640 ÷ 16 = 40 counts per minute.
- A sample of mass 2.4 g of the isotope would have 2.4 ÷ 16 = 0.15 g left after the same time.

Reading a **decay curve**: pick a starting value, find half of it, read the time between the two. Repeat from a second starting point to check the half-life is the same.

### Carbon-14 dating

Living things take in carbon, including a small constant fraction of radioactive carbon-14, so the proportion in a living organism stays steady. Once the organism dies, its intake stops and the carbon-14 left in it decays, with a half-life of 5730 years. By comparing the carbon-14 activity of an old wooden object with that of living wood, you can estimate its age.

**Worked example 6.** A wooden tool has one quarter of the carbon-14 activity of living wood. Estimate its age.
- One quarter means two halvings.
- Age = 2 × 5730 = **11 460 years**.

### Choosing an isotope for a use

Match the **type of radiation** (penetration and ionisation) and the **half-life** (long enough to last, or short enough to be safe) to the job.

| Application | Radiation and half-life reasoning |
|---|---|
| Household fire (smoke) alarm | Alpha: ionises the air in a small chamber so a small current flows; smoke soaks up alpha particles, so the current drops and the alarm goes off. Weak penetration is safe indoors; long half-life means no frequent replacement |
| Irradiating food to kill bacteria | Gamma: penetrates packaging and food; food is not made radioactive and bacteria are killed |
| Sterilising equipment | Gamma: passes through sealed packs and kills microorganisms on surgical tools |
| Measuring and controlling thickness | Sheet material: the radiation must be partly absorbed. Thin aluminium foil suits beta; a thick steel plate needs gamma. Long half-life keeps readings steady |
| Diagnosing and treating cancer | Gamma: a gamma emitter swallowed or injected can be tracked outside the body, and a short half-life means it soon disappears; a focused beam of gamma from several directions delivers a high dose to a tumour and a lower dose to tissue around it |

## 5.2.6 Safety precautions

Ionising radiation damages living cells. Effects include **cell death**, **mutations** (changes to DNA, which may be passed on) and **cancer**.

To use, move and store radioactive materials safely:
- **Reduce exposure time:** keep the source out only while needed.
- **Increase distance:** handle with long tongs, never by hand, and stay well back.
- **Use shielding:** keep sources in lead-lined containers; use lead or concrete walls to absorb radiation.

## Common errors

- Writing that alpha is "the most penetrating" because it is the biggest. It is the most ionising and the least penetrating.
- Forgetting to subtract background before halving counts.
- Writing the proton number on top in nuclide notation.
- Saying fission "uses up atoms" without a neutron being absorbed and further neutrons released.
- Saying a short half-life means the sample decays "faster in one go". It means the activity falls by half in less time.
