These notes condense section 5.4, Other receivables and other payables, from Topic 5 of the Pearson Edexcel International GCSE in Accounting (4AC1) specification, Issue 1, October 2016. In Paper 2 these adjustments arrive as notes under a trial balance. Worked examples are in the [other receivables and other payables study guide](/resources/edexcel-igcse-accounting-4ac1-other-receivables-and-other-payables-4ac1-eop/); exam-style questions are in the [other receivables and other payables practice questions](/resources/edexcel-igcse-accounting-4ac1-other-receivables-and-other-payables-4ac1-eop-practice/). Amounts are in dollars without a sign; all figures are invented.

## The three outcomes

- 5.4 a: explain why it is necessary to account for other receivables and payables.
- 5.4 b: distinguish between other receivables and payables.
- 5.4 c: calculate and record them in the books of account.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning | Where it goes |
|---|---|---|
| Accrued expense | Expense used this period, not yet paid | Other payable (current liability) |
| Prepaid expense | Paid this period for a later period | Other receivable (current asset) |
| Accrued income | Income earned this period, not yet received | Other receivable (current asset) |
| Income received in advance | Received this period for a later period | Other payable (current liability) |
| Time apportionment | Splitting a payment by months either side of the year end | Gives the adjustment |

The specification's glossary: other receivables replace "prepayments"; other payables replace "accruals".

## Why it is necessary (5.4 a)

- Accruals concept: match expenses and income to the period used or earned.
- Without adjustment, profit is wrong.
- Without adjustment, the statement of financial position leaves out real assets and liabilities.

## The formulae

Expense for the year:

- cash paid − opening accrual + closing accrual
- \+ opening prepayment − closing prepayment

Income for the year:

- cash received − opening accrued income + closing accrued income
- \+ opening income in advance − closing income in advance

## Note phrases to adjustments

| Note says | Do this |
|---|---|
| "owing", "unpaid", "due but not paid" (expense) | Add to expense; other payable |
| "includes ... for next year", "paid in advance" (expense) | Deduct from expense; other receivable |
| "due but not received" (income) | Add to income; other receivable |
| "includes ... for next year", "received in advance" (income) | Deduct from income; other payable |

## Ledger sides

| Balance | Carried down on | Brought down on |
|---|---|---|
| Expense prepaid | Credit | Debit |
| Expense accrued | Debit | Credit |
| Income accrued | Credit | Debit |
| Income in advance | Debit | Credit |

The transfer to the income statement is the balancing figure: on the credit side of an expense account, the debit side of an income account.

## Reminders

- Electricity: paid 4,200; opening owing 350; closing owing 410. Expense 4,200 − 350 + 410 = 4,260.
- Insurance: 1,800 paid 1 September for 12 months; year end 31 December. 150 a month; 4 months used, expense 600; other receivable 1,200.
- Rent received: 9,600 received, includes 800 for next year. Income 8,800; other payable 800.

## Effects

| Adjustment | Profit | Statement of financial position |
|---|---|---|
| Accrued expense | Down | Current liabilities up |
| Prepaid expense | Up | Current assets up |
| Accrued income | Up | Current assets up |
| Income in advance | Down | Current liabilities up |

The adjustments also change working capital and the current ratio.

## Grouping in the statement of financial position

- List each item's adjustment, then add the receivables together and the payables together.
- Example: insurance prepaid 1,200 and interest due 150 give other receivables of 1,350 (current assets). Wages owing 900 and rent received in advance 800 give other payables of 1,700 (current liabilities).
- Never net one total against the other: they sit in different sections.

## Exam traps

- Reading "includes" as a reason to add.
- Treating income in advance as an other receivable.
- Charging the whole payment when part covers later months.
- Missing the opening balance in a ledger account.
- An error of direction moves profit by twice the amount.
- Showing other receivables and other payables as one net figure instead of separately.
- Using older terms when the question uses other receivables and other payables.

## Self-test

1. Wages paid 31,500; 700 owing at the year end; none at the start. Calculate the wages expense.
2. Name the item: the business has received rent for next month.
3. Insurance of 2,400 is paid on 1 April for 12 months. The year end is 31 December. Calculate the expense and the other receivable.
4. Commission received 2,100; 350 is still due. Calculate commission income.
5. State the side on which an accrued expense is brought down in the ledger.
6. Opening prepayment 600; paid 5,000; closing prepayment 750. Calculate the expense.
7. Draft profit 20,000. Adjust for wages owing 900 and insurance prepaid 400.
8. Give the specification's term for an expense paid in advance.
9. A prepayment of 500 is added to the expense instead of deducted. State the effect on profit.
10. Explain, in one sentence, why an accrued expense is a liability.
11. Opening income in advance 200; received 6,000; closing accrued income 300. Calculate the income.

### Answers

1. 31,500 + 700 = 32,200.
2. Income received in advance: an other payable.
3. 200 a month; April to December is 9 months, so the expense is 1,800 and the other receivable 600.
4. 2,100 + 350 = 2,450.
5. The credit side.
6. 600 + 5,000 − 750 = 4,850.
7. 20,000 − 900 + 400 = 19,500.
8. Other receivable.
9. Profit is understated by 1,000 (twice 500).
10. The business has used the service and still owes the money for it.
11. 6,000 + 200 + 300 = 6,500.
