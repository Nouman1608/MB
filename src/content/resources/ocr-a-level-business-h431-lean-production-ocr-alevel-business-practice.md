## Questions

### Section A: short-answer questions

**1.** Explain what is meant by lean production. **[2]**

**2.** Explain what is meant by kaizen. **[2]**

**3.** Marlow Coffee buys 40,000 litres of milk a year at £0.90 a litre. It wastes 8% of the milk. Calculate the annual cost of the wasted milk. **[2]**

**4.** Explain **one** reason why a business using just in time (JIT) needs reliable suppliers. **[2]**

**5.** Which **one** of the following is an example of cell production?

A: Ordering stock when it falls to a set level.

B: A small team that assembles a complete unit, with machines placed next to each other.

C: Inspecting all products at the end of the line.

D: Producing a large batch before the order arrives.

**[1]**

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**6.** Marlow Coffee has 14 cafes. The owners introduce weekly kaizen meetings, and set a target of cutting milk waste from 8% to 5%. Training the café teams will cost £2,400.

**(a)** Calculate the annual saving if the target is met. **[3]**

**(b)** Calculate the payback period on the training, in years to one decimal place. **[2]**

**(c)** Evaluate the usefulness of kaizen to Marlow Coffee. **[8]**

**7.** Tilby Textiles makes curtains in a factory where each stage is in a separate area. Workers carry rolls of fabric between areas and often wait for the next stage to be ready. Stock of finished curtains is high and sells slowly. Tilby's managers are considering cell production and JIT.

**(a)** Explain **two** kinds of waste that Tilby Textiles has. **[4]**

**(b)** Recommend which method of lean production Tilby Textiles should introduce first. Justify your answer. **[8]**

**8.** A manufacturer reduced the value of its stock from £54,000 to £21,000 using lean methods. Holding stock costs 18% of its value a year.

**(a)** Calculate the annual saving in holding costs. **[2]**

**(b)** Evaluate the impact of lean production on the stakeholders of this manufacturer. **[9]**

**9.** Explain how time-based management could help a business to win customers. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** Lean production is an approach to operations that aims to use fewer resources for the same output [1] by reducing waste at every stage while keeping quality [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Kaizen is a method of continuous improvement [1] using many small changes, usually suggested by the employees who do the work [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Cost of milk = 40,000 × £0.90 = £36,000 [1]. Waste = 8% × £36,000 = **£2,880** [1]. **[2]**

**4.** There is no buffer stock [1], so a late or faulty delivery stops production or sales [1]. **[2]**

**5.** **B** [1]. **[1]**

**6. (a)** Waste at 8% = £2,880. Waste at 5% = 5% × £36,000 = £1,800 [1] [1]. Saving = **£1,080** a year [1]. **[3]**

**(b)** Payback = £2,400 ÷ £1,080 [1] = 2.2 years [1]. **[2]**

**(c)** Benefits: staff who make the drinks know where milk is wasted, so their suggestions are likely to be practical [1]. Many small changes, such as measuring pours or ordering by sales pattern, can add up across 14 cafes [1]. Involvement may improve motivation and reduce staff turnover [1].
Limits: the saving of £1,080 a year is small compared with the £36,000 milk bill, and a payback of 2.2 years is slow for a small project [1]. Meetings take staff time from serving customers, which is a cost not included in the £2,400 [1]. If managers do not act on ideas, staff will stop making them [1]. Cutting waste too far may harm quality if staff are tempted to serve smaller drinks [1].
Judgement: kaizen is useful because it costs little and the benefits are not just milk: it can find other wastes and build a culture of improvement [1]. It should be judged by its wider effect on costs and morale, not the milk saving alone. **[8]**

**7. (a)** Any two with explanation, 2 marks each. Transport waste: workers carry fabric between areas [1], which takes time that adds no value to the customer [1]. Waiting: workers wait for the next stage [1], so labour is paid but not producing [1]. Excess stock: finished curtains sell slowly [1], tying up cash and space and risking damage or fashion changes [1]. **[4]**

**(b)** Cell production should come first [1]. It would place the stages together in small teams, which reduces carrying and waiting [1], the first two problems described [1]. It also needs no change to suppliers [1].
JIT would reduce finished stock, but it is a poor fit at present: the problem is that curtains are made faster than they sell, so JIT would first need better forecasting of demand [1], and it would not cure the waiting inside the factory [1].
Cell production has costs: moving machines, retraining staff to be multi-skilled and possible disruption to output during the move [1].
Judgement: start with cell production, because it directly addresses the waste in the factory and carries less risk than JIT; then reduce finished stock by making to order once flow is faster [1]. **[8]**

**8. (a)** Fall in stock = £54,000 − £21,000 = £33,000 [1]. Saving = 18% × £33,000 = **£5,940** [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** Owners: lower holding costs of £5,940 a year and £33,000 of cash freed for other uses [1]. They also face the risk that low stock leads to stoppages if suppliers fail.
Employees: wider skills and more involvement if methods such as kaizen are used [1]; they may fear redundancy if the changes reduce the labour needed, and may feel pressure to keep improving [1].
Customers: better quality and quicker delivery if lean works [1]; but may find products out of stock if the stock is too low [1].
Suppliers: they may gain a long-term partnership, but must deliver small loads more often and meet tight quality standards [1].
Evaluation: the effect depends on how reliable the suppliers are and how the changes are introduced [1]. Judgement: the benefits to owners are clear and measurable, but the long-term gain depends on the co-operation of the workforce, so management should consult staff and avoid redundancies where possible [1]. **[9]**

**9.** Time-based management reduces the time from order to delivery [1], so customers receive products sooner than from rivals [1]. Faster development of new products lets the business reach the market before competitors [1], and a reputation for speed may allow it to charge a higher price or win repeat orders [1]. **[4]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing that lean production means making redundancies.
- Treating JIT as the only lean method.
- Defining a method without explaining how it reduces waste.
- Recommending JIT without checking supplier reliability.
- Evaluating with benefits only, and no costs or risks.
