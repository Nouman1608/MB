These notes condense the [globalisation study guide](/resources/igcse-global-perspectives-0457-globalisation/); read that first if anything here is new.

They cover the Cambridge IGCSE Global Perspectives (0457) syllabus for examination in 2025, 2026 and 2027, Version 2 (published April 2025), sections 2 to 4. Globalisation is one topic on the section 3 topic list, which is used for Component 1 Written Exam, Component 2 Individual Report and Component 3 Team Project. The syllabus says knowledge of content is not assessed, so these notes show how to use the topic to practise the skills, not facts to memorise.

## What the syllabus assesses

| Assessment objective | Weighting in the qualification | Where it counts |
|---|---|---|
| AO1 Research, analysis and evaluation | 68% | 100% of Component 1; 67% of Component 2; 36% of Component 3 |
| AO2 Reflection | 15% | 0% of Component 1; 8% of Component 2; 36% of Component 3 |
| AO3 Communication and collaboration | 17% | 0% of Component 1; 25% of Component 2; 28% of Component 3 |

Component 1 is 1 hour 25 minutes, 70 marks, four compulsory questions on a source insert: Question 1 (18 marks) analyses information, arguments and perspectives; Question 2 (16 marks) evaluates research or evidence and suggests how to test a claim; Question 3 (16 marks) judges the quality of arguments; Question 4 (20 marks) assesses actions using all sources.

Command words printed in the syllabus: **Analyse, Describe, Evaluate, Explain, Identify, Suggest.**

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Issue (syllabus) | An important subject or problem for discussion |
| Perspective (syllabus) | A viewpoint on an issue based on evidence and reasoning |
| Research (syllabus) | The systematic study of a global issue to find evidence and develop understanding |
| Evidence (syllabus) | Information about a global issue that helps to develop understanding or prove that something is true or false |
| Argument (syllabus) | A series of statements containing reasons and evidence which support a claim about a global issue |
| Globalisation (working definition) | Growing connection of economies, cultures and people through trade, investment, technology, travel and communication |
| Supply chain | The series of firms and places a product passes through from raw material to buyer |
| Multinational company | A firm that operates in more than one country |
| Tariff | A tax on imported goods |
| Glocalisation | Global products or ideas adapted to suit local cultures |
| Vested interest | A personal stake (often money or status) in what others believe |
| Generalisation | A claim stretched from a few cases to all cases |

## Examples at a glance

Real examples you can research. You will not be asked to recall them; they help you practise evaluating evidence.

| Example | What happened | Use it to show | One evaluation point |
|---|---|---|---|
| *Ideal X*, 1956 | Malcolm McLean's ship carried the first containers | A cause: cheaper shipping | One invention rarely explains a trend alone |
| WTO, 1 January 1995 | Organisation set up to oversee rules of trade between members | A cause: agreed trade rules | Rules do not guarantee equal benefits |
| *Ever Given*, March 2021 | Ship blocked the Suez Canal for six days | A consequence: fragile supply chains | One event shows a risk, not its frequency |
| Chip shortage, 2020–2021 | Shortage of computer chips made in a few places forced car makers to cut output | A consequence: dependence on few suppliers | Pandemic demand changes were also a cause, not only globalisation |
| Global minimum tax, 2021 | More than 130 countries agreed, through the OECD, to a 15% minimum tax rate for large multinationals | A course of action | Each country must pass its own law, so results vary |
| KOF Globalisation Index | ETH Zurich index scoring economic, social and political globalisation | Measuring the topic | Results depend on the indicators chosen |
| Friedman (2005) vs Stiglitz (2002) | Two books, broadly supportive and broadly critical | Experts disagree | Both are arguments, not neutral data |

## Must-know distinctions

- **Topic vs issue.** Globalisation is a topic. "Should my country tax imported steel?" is an issue.
- **Cause vs consequence.** Cheaper shipping is a cause; cheaper clothes in shops are a consequence.
- **Fact vs opinion.** "The factory closed in 2024" can be checked. "Closing it was a disgrace" cannot.
- **Claim vs evidence.** A claim is what someone wants you to accept; evidence is what is offered to support it.
- **Prediction vs fact.** "Prices will fall" is about the future, so it cannot yet be checked.
- **Vested interest vs bias.** A vested interest is a stake in the outcome; bias is a one-sided presentation. A vested interest makes bias more likely but does not prove it.
- **Local vs national vs global.** Use all three levels where the question allows; the Team Project must start local.
- **Perspective vs opinion.** A perspective rests on evidence and reasoning; an opinion may not.

## Perspectives at a glance

| Who | Likely perspective | Why they might hold it |
|---|---|---|
| Shopper on a low income | Welcomes cheaper imported goods | Lower prices stretch a tight budget |
| Worker in an older factory | Worried about jobs moving abroad | Their income depends on the factory staying open |
| Owner of an exporting firm | Supports open trade | New markets mean more sales |
| Small local shop owner | Wary of global chains | Competes with firms that can charge less |
| Environmental campaigner | Questions long supply chains | Transport over long distances adds to pollution |
| Government | Balances all of these | Must weigh jobs, prices, trade partners and votes |

Remember: these are typical positions, not fixed rules. A low-income shopper may also be a factory worker. Show that you understand the reasoning behind each view before you judge it (AO2 asks you to consider perspectives "objectively and with empathy").

## Quick checks for any globalisation source

1. **Who wrote it, and what do they gain?** Look for a vested interest.
2. **What is the claim?** Write it in one line.
3. **What evidence is given?** Is it data, personal experience, expert view or none?
4. **How big and fair is the sample?** One town or one group cannot speak for a country.
5. **Is the language loaded?** Words such as "flooding", "ruined" or "destroy" signal bias.
6. **Does the conclusion go further than the evidence?** This is where most marks for evaluation come from.

## Mini plan: a typical extended answer

*Question type (Component 1, Question 4 style):* "Which course of action would be most effective in reducing the harm caused by global supply chains? Use all the sources and explain your judgement."

1. **Name two or three actions from the sources** (for example, safety agreements, tariffs, consumer labelling).
2. **For each action:** who would carry it out, what it would change, the evidence in the sources that it could work, and one weakness or side effect.
3. **Compare.** Which action reaches the cause rather than the symptom? Which affects most people? Which is realistic?
4. **Judge.** Pick one action and say why it beats the others, using source evidence.
5. **Qualify.** State one condition that would make your chosen action work better (for example, enforcement).

Reflection is not credited in Component 1, so do not spend time on how your view changed. Save that for the Individual Report and the Reflective Paper.

## Quick self-test

1. Does the 0457 syllabus assess knowledge of globalisation?
2. Give the syllabus definition of a perspective.
3. Turn the topic "globalisation" into an issue that divides opinion.
4. "Container shipping made goods cheaper to move." Cause or consequence of globalisation?
5. "All global brands wipe out local culture." Which of the eight Component 1 terms fits best?
6. An exporters' union praises a new trade deal. What should you note about the source?
7. Which assessment objective has 0% weighting in Component 1?
8. What word limit applies to the Individual Report?
9. Which levels of perspective does the syllabus say Component 1 sources present?
10. Give one drawback of tariffs as a course of action.

### Answers

1. No. The syllabus states that knowledge of content is not assessed; the topic is a context for the skills.
2. A viewpoint on an issue based on evidence and reasoning.
3. For example: "Should countries limit cheap imports to protect local jobs?"
4. A cause: it helped trade grow.
5. Generalisation (it stretches some cases to all).
6. A vested interest: its members gain from the deal, so check for one-sided evidence.
7. AO2 Reflection.
8. 1500–2000 words, not counting the bibliography or reference list.
9. Personal, local and/or national, and global.
10. Higher prices for consumers, or other countries responding with their own tariffs.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing what you know about globalisation instead of using the source material in Component 1.
- Saying "this source is biased" without naming who gains and quoting the loaded words.
- Accepting a percentage without asking how big the sample was and who was asked.
- Treating a single event as proof of a general pattern.
- Calling a perspective "wrong" instead of explaining the reasoning behind it.
- Listing courses of action without comparing them and reaching a judgement.
- Choosing an Individual Report question that does not divide opinion or has no global impact.
- Picking a Team Project issue that is national or global, not local.
- In the Reflective Paper, describing the action but not evaluating how effective it was.
