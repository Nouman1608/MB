This guide covers section 3.1.4.2, River landscapes, in Section D (Physical landscapes: Hot deserts or River landscapes) of Unit 1 of the OxfordAQA International GCSE Geography (9230) specification, Version 4.3. In Section D, students study **either** hot desert landscapes **or** river landscapes. The key ideas are: "The shape of river valleys changes as rivers flow downstream"; "Distinctive fluvial landforms result from different physical processes"; and "Different management strategies can be used to protect river landscapes from the effects of flooding." For the whole unit, start with the [Living with the Physical Environment study guide](/resources/igcse-oxfordaqa-geography-living-with-the-physical-environment/). Examples should come from the Middle and Far East; see the [Middle and Far East study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-geography-9230-physical-landscapes-middle-far-east/) for the rivers.

## What the specification lists

- The long profile and the changing cross profile of a river and its valley.
- **Fluvial processes:** erosion (hydraulic action, abrasion, attrition, solution, vertical and lateral erosion), transport (traction, saltation, suspension, solution), and why rivers deposit sediment.
- **Landforms of erosion:** interlocking spurs, waterfalls and gorges.
- **Landforms of erosion and deposition:** meanders and ox-bow lakes.
- **Landforms of deposition:** levées, flood plains and estuaries.
- Physical and human factors affecting flood risk; hydrographs.
- The costs and benefits of hard and soft engineering.
- **An example** of a river flood management scheme in the region.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Long profile | A cross-section of a river from source to mouth, showing how its gradient changes |
| Cross profile | A cross-section across a river channel and valley |
| Discharge | The volume of water passing a point each second, measured in cumecs (m³ per second) |
| Hydraulic action | The force of water pushing air into cracks and breaking up rock |
| Abrasion | Wearing away by the load carried by the river |
| Attrition | Pieces of the load knocking against each other and becoming smaller and rounder |
| Solution | Dissolving of soluble rock, such as limestone, in river water |
| Traction | Rolling of the biggest stones along the bed |
| Saltation | Bouncing of small stones along the bed |
| Suspension | Fine material carried within the flow |
| Floodplain | The flat land either side of a river that floods when the river overflows |
| Hydrograph | A graph showing river discharge over time after a rainstorm |

## Long profile and cross profile

The **long profile** is a smooth curve, steep near the source and gentle near the mouth. The river's job changes along its length.

| Stage | Gradient | Valley cross profile | Channel | Dominant process |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Upper course | Steep | Narrow, V-shaped valley with steep sides | Narrow, shallow, rocky, bumpy | Vertical erosion |
| Middle course | Gentler | Wider valley, gentler slopes | Wider and deeper | Lateral erosion and transport; some deposition |
| Lower course | Very gentle | Very wide, flat floodplain | Widest and deepest, smooth | Deposition |

Discharge increases downstream as tributaries add water. Sediment gets smaller and rounder because of attrition.

## Fluvial processes

**Erosion.**

- **Hydraulic action:** the force of the water on the banks and bed.
- **Abrasion:** the load scrapes the bed and banks, like sandpaper.
- **Attrition:** the load itself is broken down.
- **Solution:** slightly acidic water dissolves rock such as limestone.
- **Vertical erosion** cuts the bed downwards, mainly in the upper course. **Lateral erosion** cuts the banks sideways, mainly in the middle and lower course.

**Transportation.** Large stones are rolled along the bed (traction), small stones bounce (saltation), fine silt and clay are carried in the flow (suspension), and dissolved minerals are carried in solution.

**Deposition.** A river deposits its load when it loses energy. Reasons include a fall in discharge in dry weather, shallow water, slower flow on the inside of bends, where a river meets a lake or the sea, and when the load is too heavy for the flow.

## Landforms of the upper course

### Interlocking spurs

In the upper course, the river is powerful enough to cut down but not sideways. It winds around the hillsides, eroding the ground by vertical erosion. Spurs of higher land project into the valley and overlap or "interlock" like the teeth of a zip.

### Waterfalls and gorges

A waterfall forms where a river flows over a band of hard rock lying over softer rock. The soft rock is eroded faster by abrasion and hydraulic action, creating a step. Water plunges over the edge and erodes a deep **plunge pool** at the base. The hard rock above is undercut and eventually collapses. This repeats, and the waterfall **retreats** upstream. The steep-sided, narrow valley left behind is a **gorge**. In the region, examples include Tiger Leaping Gorge on the upper Yangtze (the Jinsha River) in China, and the Hukou Waterfall on the Yellow River.

## Landforms of the middle and lower course

### Meanders and ox-bow lakes

A meander is a bend in a river. Water flows faster on the outside of a bend, where the channel is deeper, so the outer bank is eroded to form a **river cliff**. On the inside, the water is slower and shallower, so material is deposited to form a **slip-off slope** (point bar). Over time the bend becomes more pronounced. The Jingjiang section of the middle Yangtze is famous for its tight meanders.

An **ox-bow lake** forms when the neck of a tight meander is eroded away, often during a flood, so the river takes a shortcut. Deposition seals off the ends of the old bend, leaving a curved lake on the floodplain.

### Floodplains and levées

A **floodplain** is the flat area on either side of the river. It is formed by lateral erosion (as meanders move across the valley) and by deposition of silt when the river floods. A **levée** is a raised bank beside the river. When a river floods, the water slows as it leaves the channel and drops its coarser material first, right next to the bank, building up natural embankments over many floods. On the lower Yellow River, silt has built up so much that the channel lies above the surrounding plain between levées.

### Estuaries

An **estuary** is the wide, tidal mouth of a river where fresh water meets the sea. Mud and silt are deposited by the slow-moving water and by the tides, forming mudflats and salt marshes. The Yangtze estuary at Shanghai is a major example.

## Flood risk: physical and human factors

| Factor | Effect on flooding |
|---|---|
| Precipitation | Heavy or prolonged rain, or rapid snowmelt, increases flood risk |
| Geology | Impermeable rock (such as clay or granite) causes quick runoff; permeable rock (chalk, limestone) absorbs water |
| Relief | Steep slopes send water to the river quickly; flat land holds water longer |
| Land use | Urban surfaces (concrete, tarmac) are impermeable and have drains that speed runoff; deforestation reduces interception; farming on slopes can increase runoff |

## Hydrographs

A storm hydrograph shows rainfall and discharge over time. Key features:

- **Base flow:** the normal flow in the river.
- **Rising limb:** discharge increases after rain.
- **Peak discharge:** the highest flow.
- **Lag time:** the time between peak rainfall and peak discharge.
- **Falling limb:** discharge decreases.

A **flashy** hydrograph has a short lag time, a steep rising limb and a high peak. It is typical of steep, impermeable, urban or deforested catchments after heavy rain. A **flatter** hydrograph has a long lag time and a lower peak. It is typical of gentle, permeable, vegetated catchments.

## Flood management

| Strategy | Type | Benefits | Costs and drawbacks |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dams and reservoirs | Hard | Store floodwater and release it slowly; generate power; provide water | Very expensive; flood land and displace people; trap sediment |
| Straightening (channelisation) | Hard | Speeds water through town | Moves flood problem downstream; damages habitats |
| Embankments | Hard | Raise the channel capacity to protect towns | Expensive; can fail with disastrous results; cause higher floods downstream |
| Flood relief channels | Hard | Divert excess water around towns | Costly and use land |
| Flood warnings and preparation | Soft | Cheap; give time to evacuate and move belongings | Do not prevent the flood; need good communication |
| Flood plain zoning | Soft | Stops building in high-risk areas; cheap | Restricts development; hard to apply to existing homes |
| Planting trees | Soft | Intercepts rain and slows runoff; improves habitat | Slow to work; needs large areas |
| River restoration | Soft | Returns channels to natural form; creates wetlands | Needs land; may flood some farmland |

## An example scheme: the Three Gorges Dam, Yangtze, China

Your teacher may use a different scheme from the region, such as one on the Yellow River or the Mekong. The same questions apply. Check these details.

- **Why it was needed:** The Yangtze has a long history of devastating floods, which caused very heavy loss of life and damage in the 20th century, and millions of people live on its floodplain. China also needed a large source of electricity for its growing economy.
- **The scheme:** a very large concrete gravity dam, about 2.3 km long and around 185 m high, built across the Yangtze in Hubei province. Construction began in the 1990s, the main wall was finished in 2006 and the project was fully operating by 2012. The reservoir behind the dam stretches for hundreds of kilometres upstream. It has a huge power-generating capacity, over 20 000 MW.
- **Social issues:** more than a million people were relocated, and many towns and villages and cultural sites were flooded. Some resettled people received poorer land or lost livelihoods.
- **Economic issues:** much electricity is generated without burning coal; flood control protects farmland and cities downstream; larger ships can navigate further upstream, boosting trade. However the cost was very high, and some communities lost farming and fishing income.
- **Environmental issues:** the reservoir traps sediment, reducing the silt that fertilises the downstream floodplain and rebuilds the delta; it changed habitats and affects fish migration; landslides along the reservoir banks are a risk; water quality is a concern. But the dam reduces coal burning and carbon emissions.

## Worked example: a 6-mark answer

**Question:** Assess the benefits and costs of a river flood management scheme you have studied. [6 marks]

**Model answer:** "The Three Gorges Dam on the Yangtze stores floodwater in its reservoir and releases it slowly, which protects millions of people and farmland on the floodplain downstream. It also generates large amounts of hydroelectric power, so it supports industry and reduces the use of coal, and bigger ships can travel further upstream. However, over a million people had to be relocated and some lost their land and livelihoods. The dam traps sediment, so the downstream floodplain and delta receive less fertile silt, and the habitat of fish and other wildlife is changed. The project cost a very large amount of money. Overall, the benefits in flood protection and power are major, but the social and environmental costs are significant and fall on different groups."

The answer deals with social, economic and environmental effects, and ends with a judgement.

## Common mistakes

- **Describing the shape of a landform without explaining how it formed.** Give a step-by-step sequence.
- **Mixing up erosion and transport.** Attrition is erosion of the load; saltation is transport.
- **Saying that a waterfall "moves back" without undercutting.** Explain the plunge pool and collapse.
- **Calling the inside of a meander "erosion".** The inside bend is deposition.
- **Giving a hydrograph answer without values.** Quote peak discharge and lag time with units.
- **Naming a scheme with no details.** Give location, features and impacts.
