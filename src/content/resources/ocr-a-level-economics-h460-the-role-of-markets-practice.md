## Section A: Short questions

**1.** Which **one** of the following is an example of composite demand? **[1]**

A: Cars and petrol

B: Wheat used to make bread, animal feed and biofuel

C: Tea and coffee

D: Lamb and wool

**2.** Which **one** of the following would cause an extension of the supply of strawberries? **[1]**

A: A fall in the wages of fruit pickers

B: Good weather during the growing season

C: A rise in demand for strawberries, raising their price

D: A subsidy paid to strawberry growers

**3.** A good is said to be non-rejectable. What does this mean? **[1]**

A: Consumers cannot be prevented from using it once it is provided

B: One person's use does not reduce the amount available to others

C: Individuals cannot choose to opt out of consuming it once it is provided

D: The cost of supplying one more user is zero

**4.** Explain **two** advantages of using money rather than barter as a medium of exchange. **[4]**

**5.** Explain how diminishing marginal utility helps to explain why a demand curve slopes downwards. **[3]**

**6.** The price of tea rises by 8% and, as a result, the quantity of coffee demanded rises by 2%. Calculate the cross elasticity of demand for coffee with respect to the price of tea, and explain what it shows. **[3]**

## Section B: Structured and extended questions

**7.** In the market for an invented garden fertiliser, demand is Qd = 800 − 4P and supply is Qs = 2P − 100, where P is the price in cu per bag and Q is thousands of bags a month.

**(a)** Calculate the equilibrium price and quantity. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the point price elasticity of demand and the point price elasticity of supply at equilibrium. **[2]**

**(c)** Calculate consumer surplus at equilibrium. **[2]**

**(d)** The government sets a maximum price of 120 cu. Calculate the resulting shortage. **[2]**

**8.** Production of the fertiliser in question 7 pollutes rivers. The marginal external cost is 30 cu per bag at every output.

**(a)** Calculate the socially optimal price and quantity. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the welfare loss at the free-market output. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain **one** reason why a tax of 30 cu per bag might not achieve the social optimum in practice. **[2]**

**9.** Beef and leather are in joint supply. Using a supply and demand diagram for leather, explain the effect on the leather market of a large rise in demand for beef. **[4]**

**10.** Explain how asymmetric information can cause market failure in the market for used cars. **[4]**

**11.** Evaluate the view that specialisation and the division of labour solve the problem of scarcity. **[10]**

**12.** Evaluate the view that government intervention to correct market failure will always improve the allocation of resources. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** B [1]. One good wanted for several different uses. A and C are joint and competitive demand; D is joint supply. **[1]**

**2.** C [1]. A higher price causes a movement along the supply curve. A, B and D shift the supply curve. **[1]**

**3.** C [1]. A is non-excludability, B non-rivalry, D zero marginal cost. **[1]**

**4.** Advantage 1: money removes the need for a double coincidence of wants [1], so traders no longer have to search for someone who both has what they want and wants what they offer, cutting the time and cost of exchange [1]. Advantage 2: money is a common unit of account [1], so goods have one price rather than a separate rate of exchange against every other good, making comparison easy [1]. Other valid advantages: money is divisible; it is a store of value, so sales and purchases can happen at different times. **[4]**

**5.** Each extra unit consumed gives less additional satisfaction than the one before [1]. A rational consumer buys a unit only if its marginal utility is at least worth the price paid [1]. So the consumer will buy more units only if the price falls, giving the inverse relationship between price and quantity demanded [1]. **[3]**

**6.** XED = 2 ÷ 8 [1] = **+0.25** [1]. The positive sign shows tea and coffee are substitutes, and the low value shows they are weak substitutes [1]. **[3]**

**7. (a)** 800 − 4P = 2P − 100, so 6P = 900 and P = 150 cu [1]; Q = 800 − 600 = 200 thousand bags [1].

**(b)** PED = −4 × 150 ÷ 200 = **−3.0** (price elastic) [1]. PES = 2 × 150 ÷ 200 = **1.5** (price elastic) [1].

**(c)** Demand meets the price axis at P = 200 [1]. Consumer surplus = ½ × 200 × (200 − 150) = **5,000 thousand cu**, or 5 million cu a month [1].

**(d)** At 120 cu: Qd = 800 − 480 = 320 and Qs = 240 − 100 = 140 [1]. Shortage = 320 − 140 = **180 thousand bags** [1]. **[8]**

**8. (a)** MSC lies 30 above supply: Qs = 2(P − 30) − 100 = 2P − 160. Setting 800 − 4P = 2P − 160 gives P = 160 cu [1] and Q = 160 thousand bags [1].

**(b)** Over-production is 200 − 160 = 40 thousand bags [1]. Welfare loss = ½ × 40 × 30 = **600 thousand cu** [1].

**(c)** The marginal external cost is hard to measure, so the tax may be set too high or too low [1]; if it is wrong, output will still differ from the optimum, creating a new welfare loss [1]. Alternative: firms may evade the tax or relocate production. **[6]**

**9.** The rise in demand for beef raises its price, so farmers raise more cattle [1]. Because leather is jointly supplied, more leather is produced automatically, shifting the supply curve for leather to the right [1]. A diagram with price and quantity of leather on the axes shows supply shifting right along an unchanged demand curve [1]. The price of leather falls and the quantity traded rises [1]. **[4]**

**10.** Sellers of used cars know more about each car's condition than buyers do, which is asymmetric information [1]. Buyers cannot tell good cars from poor ones, so they will pay only a price reflecting average quality [1]. Owners of good cars find this price too low and withdraw from the market, so the average quality of cars offered falls [1]. Fewer mutually beneficial trades take place than with full information, so resources are misallocated: a market failure [1]. **[4]**

**11.** Award up to 10 marks across knowledge, application, analysis and evaluation.
- Define specialisation and division of labour: concentrating on a narrow range of tasks or products [1].
- Workers become more skilled through repetition and time is not lost switching tasks [1].
- Output per worker rises, so more wants can be met from the same resources [1].
- Specialisation makes machinery worthwhile, which further raises productivity [1].
- Application: an example, such as a manufacturing line or a country specialising in what it produces relatively cheaply [1].
- But scarcity remains: wants are unlimited, so higher output reduces scarcity rather than removing it [1].
- Specialisation requires exchange, and hence money and well-functioning markets [1].
- Repetitive work can lower motivation, and narrow skills can lead to structural unemployment when demand changes [1].
- Heavy dependence on others creates risk if supply chains are disrupted [1].
- Judgement: specialisation eases the problem of scarcity considerably but cannot solve it, with a reasoned conclusion [1]. **[10]**

**12.** Award up to 10 marks across knowledge, application, analysis and evaluation.
- Market failure means the free market misallocates resources, for example through externalities, information failure or public goods [1].
- Intervention can correct this: a tax equal to the external cost moves output to the social optimum [1].
- A subsidy or state provision can raise consumption of merit goods and provide public goods [1].
- Information provision and regulation can tackle information failure directly [1].
- But government failure is possible: external costs and benefits are hard to measure [1].
- Intervention has an opportunity cost in public money and administrative costs [1].
- Unintended consequences: price controls cause shortages; high taxes encourage evasion and black markets [1].
- Political pressures or short time horizons may lead to poorly targeted policy [1].
- The outcome depends on the tool chosen and the quality of information [1].
- Judgement: intervention can improve allocation but not always; the net gain must exceed the risk of government failure, with a reasoned conclusion [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* Answers that compare the welfare loss from the market failure with the likely cost of government failure show genuine evaluation.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calling a price-driven movement a shift of supply.
- Calculating point elasticity away from the equilibrium.
- Using the market price instead of the external cost as the height of the welfare loss triangle.
- Mixing up the four public good characteristics.
- Claiming specialisation removes scarcity.
- Treating intervention as automatically beneficial.
