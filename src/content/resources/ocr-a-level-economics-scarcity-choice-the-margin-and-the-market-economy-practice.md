## Questions

**1.** Goods and wants.

**(a)** Distinguish between an economic good and a free good. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why a clinic run by the government and free to patients is still an economic good. **[2]**
**(c)** Distinguish between a need and a want, using one example of each. **[2]**

**2.** Country B (illustrative) has the following statements made about it.

**(a)** Classify each as positive or normative: (i) output fell by 2% last year; (ii) the government should spend more on railways; (iii) a higher fuel tax will reduce petrol use; (iv) income differences are unfair. **[4]**
**(b)** Explain why statement (iii) is positive even if it turns out to be wrong. **[1]**

**3.** A farm in Country C pays rent of £24,000, wages of £78,000 and interest of £12,000, and earns a profit of £36,000.

**(a)** Calculate wages as a percentage of the total reward to all four factors. **[2]**
**(b)** Identify the factor that earns the £36,000 and explain its role. **[2]**
**(c)** Identify the factor that earns rent. **[1]**

**4.** A printing firm's total cost (TC) is £300 at 0 units, then £340, £375, £415 and £470 at 1, 2, 3 and 4 units.

**(a)** Calculate the marginal cost of each unit. **[2]**
**(b)** The fixed cost rises by £100. State the new marginal cost of the third unit and explain. **[2]**

**5.** A firm sells at a price that falls as it sells more: £50, £46, £42, £38, £34 and £30 for 1 to 6 units. Marginal cost is a constant £22 and fixed cost is £40.

**(a)** Calculate total revenue and marginal revenue for each quantity. **[2]**
**(b)** Find the profit-maximising quantity and explain using marginal values. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the profit at that quantity. **[2]**

**6.** A consumer's total utility from 1 to 6 slices of pizza is 40, 70, 90, 100, 104 and 104. One unit of utility is valued at £0.10 and a slice costs £1.50.

**(a)** Calculate the marginal utility of each slice. **[2]**
**(b)** State how many slices a rational consumer buys and explain. **[2]**
**(c)** Name the pattern shown by the marginal utility values. **[1]**

**7.** An aquifer in Country D holds 5,400 million cubic metres. It recharges by 120 million a year and 300 million a year are extracted.

**(a)** Calculate the net change in the stock each year. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate how many years until the aquifer is empty if nothing changes. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the percentage cut in extraction needed to hold the stock level. **[2]**
**(d)** Explain how depletion affects scarcity and choice. **[2]**

**8.** Explain the role of households, firms and the government in an economy. **[6]**

**9.** Evaluate the strengths and weaknesses of the market economy. **[12]**

**10.** Evaluate the role of government within the market economy. **[10]**

**11.** Evaluate the extent to which the marginal concept and the assumption of rationality help explain how economic agents behave. **[10]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** An economic good uses scarce resources to supply [1]; a free good needs none and has no price [1].
**(b)** Staff, buildings and equipment are used [1]; taxpayers meet the cost even though patients do not pay [1].
**(c)** A need is required to survive, such as food [1]; a want is desired beyond that, such as a games console [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Part (b) gains credit when it names the resources used. "It is paid for by tax" alone does not show scarcity.

**2. (a)** (i) positive [1]; (ii) normative [1]; (iii) positive [1]; (iv) normative [1].
**(b)** It can be tested against evidence, so it can be true or false [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* A "should" or "unfair" signals a value judgement. Answers lose credit by calling a statement normative because it seems untrue.

**3. (a)** Total reward = 24,000 + 78,000 + 12,000 + 36,000 = £150,000 [1]; 78,000 ÷ 150,000 × 100 = **52%** [1].
**(b)** Enterprise [1]; it organises the other factors and bears the risk [1].
**(c)** Land [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the total before the division. A bare percentage gives nothing to credit if it is wrong.

**4. (a)** MC = change in TC for one more unit [1]: **£40, £35, £40, £55** [1].
**(b)** **£40** [1]; the extra £100 is added to every TC, so the differences are unchanged [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Mixing up total and marginal cost is the usual error. Say that the marginal value is a difference.

**5. (a)** TR = 50, 92, 126, 152, 170, 180 [1]; MR = 50, 42, 34, 26, 18, 10 [1].
**(b)** **4 units**: the fourth has MR £26, above MC £22 [1]; the fifth has MR £18, below MC £22, so it loses profit [1].
**(c)** TC = 40 + 22 × 4 = £128 [1]; profit = 152 − 128 = **£24** [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Justify the quantity with MR and MC for the units either side. Stating "MR = MC" with no figures is weaker.

**6. (a)** MU = change in total utility [1]: **40, 30, 20, 10, 4, 0** [1].
**(b)** Marginal benefit of the third is £2.00, above £1.50 [1]; the fourth is £1.00, below £1.50, so **3 slices** [1].
**(c)** Diminishing marginal utility [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Convert utility to money before comparing it with the price. Comparing 20 utils with £1.50 mixes units.

**7. (a)** 120 − 300 = **−180 million** a year [1].
**(b)** 5,400 ÷ 180 [1] = **30 years** [1].
**(c)** Extraction must fall to 120 [1]; (300 − 120) ÷ 300 × 100 = **60%** [1].
**(d)** Water becomes scarcer for future users and costlier to obtain [1]; a choice must be made between current use and future supply, perhaps limiting uses or finding substitutes [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* Part (d) gains credit when it links both scarcity and choice to the data, not when it lists general environmental harm.

**8.** Households supply factors, mainly labour [1], and buy goods and services with the income [1]. Firms combine factors to produce goods and services [1] and pay households for the factors [1]. Government provides goods and services, such as public services [1], and taxes, regulates and redistributes [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Each agent needs a role and a development. A list of agent names with no activity earns little.

**9.** Strength: prices signal changes in demand [1], so firms earn more by supplying what is wanted and resources move without planning [1]. Strength: profit and wages reward efficiency and effort, which drives innovation [1] and costs fall as firms compete [1]. Weakness: where costs fall on third parties, such as pollution, the market overproduces [1], so prices do not show the full cost [1]. Weakness: reward follows ownership and skill, so those with little are left with little [1], and inequality can persist [1]. Weakness: dominant firms restrict output and raise price [1], and markets can neglect long-run needs such as sustainability [1]. The judgement depends on how severe the failure is and how well the market works in the case [1]. Conclusion: the market is a strong way of allocating most goods, but needs correction where it fails, so a market with targeted intervention is better than either extreme [1]. **[12]**
*Examiner insight:* Strong answers weigh the points against each other and reach a reasoned view. Two lists of bullet points leave the evaluation undone.

**10.** The government gives a legal framework and property rights, which markets need [1]. It corrects externalities with taxes, subsidies or regulation [1], moving output closer to the level society wants [1]. It redistributes income through tax and benefits [1] and promotes competition [1]. However, it lacks full information, so a tax may be set wrongly [1], and policies can have unintended effects or cost more than they gain (government failure) [1]. The balance depends on the size of the failure and the time lag [1]. Intervention is justified where a specific failure is large and the policy is targeted, but not where the cost of the policy exceeds the harm [2]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* Name a failure and a matching policy, then weigh it against a limit on the policy. Treating intervention as automatically beneficial gains little.

**11.** The margin is the point of deciding on one more or one less unit [1]. A firm adds output while marginal revenue is at least marginal cost [1], and ignores fixed and sunk costs when it does [1]. Marginal values are hard to measure in practice [1], and some choices are all-or-nothing [1]. Rationality assumes agents maximise a goal with the information available [1]. It predicts firm decisions well when the goal is clear, as with profit [1], but households often lack information or follow habit [1]. Overall the concepts are most useful where choices can be varied by degrees and goals are clear, and weaker for one-off choices [2]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* Strong answers link both concepts to a named agent and weigh the extent. Defining terms and stopping there leaves "extent" unanswered.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Defining a free good as "free to the consumer" rather than needing no resources.
- Calling a statement normative because it is wrong, or positive because it has a number in it.
- Quoting a total or average where a marginal value is asked for.
- Not converting utility to money before comparing it with a price.
- Treating capital as money.
- Giving percentages without showing the total used.
- Listing strengths and weaknesses of the market with no weighing against each other.
- Treating government intervention as always beneficial, with no mention of government failure.
