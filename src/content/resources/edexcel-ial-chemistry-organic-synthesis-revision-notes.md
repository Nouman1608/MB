## Key definitions

- **Empirical formula:** the elements in a compound in their lowest whole-number ratio.
- **Molecular formula:** the full count of each kind of atom in a molecule; a whole-number multiple of the empirical formula.
- **Molecular ion (M⁺):** the ion of the whole molecule; its m/z gives Mr.
- **Grignard reagent:** an organomagnesium halide, RMgX, made from a halogenoalkane and magnesium in dry ether.
- **Reflux:** heating with a vertical condenser so vapour returns to the flask.
- **Steam distillation:** distilling a volatile, water-immiscible compound with steam, below 100 °C.
- **Recrystallisation:** purifying a solid by dissolving it in minimum hot solvent and letting it crystallise on cooling.

## 20.1 Structure from data: method in steps

1. Combustion: n(C) = n(CO₂); n(H) = 2 × n(H₂O); mass O = sample − C − H.
2. Percentage composition: divide each % by Ar, then divide by the smallest.
3. Scale the empirical formula to Mr from M⁺.
4. Tests and IR give functional groups. NMR gives the skeleton.
5. Check the structure fits **every** piece of data.

### Evidence and what it tells you

| Evidence | Points to |
|---|---|
| Effervescence with Na₂CO₃(aq) | COOH |
| Orange precipitate with 2,4-DNPH | C=O in aldehyde or ketone |
| Silver mirror (Tollens') or red-brown precipitate (Fehling's) | aldehyde |
| Pale yellow precipitate with iodine and alkali | CH₃CO– or CH₃CH(OH)– |
| Orange to green with acidified dichromate(VI) | primary or secondary alcohol, or aldehyde |
| Steamy fumes with PCl₅ | OH (alcohol or acid) |
| IR very broad band about 2500–3300 cm⁻¹ | O–H of a carboxylic acid |
| IR broad band about 3200–3550 cm⁻¹ | O–H of an alcohol |
| IR strong band about 1700–1750 cm⁻¹ | C=O |
| ¹³C NMR: number of peaks | number of carbon environments |
| ¹H NMR peak areas | ratio of H atoms in each environment |
| ¹H NMR splitting | n + 1 peaks, where n = H atoms on adjacent carbons |

Use the chemical shift values the question supplies. Typical regions: alkyl CH about δ 0.9–1.7; CH next to C=O about δ 2.0–2.6; CH on C–O about δ 3.3–4.2; aromatic H about δ 6.5–8.0; CHO about δ 9–10; COOH about δ 10–12.

## 20.2 Grignard reagents

Make: RX + Mg → RMgX, in **dry ether**. Use: add the second reactant in dry ether, then hydrolyse with **dilute acid**.

| Reagent with RMgX | Product | Alcohol class |
|---|---|---|
| CO₂ | RCOOH | (acid, not alcohol) |
| HCHO | RCH₂OH | primary |
| R′CHO | RCH(OH)R′ | secondary |
| R′COR″ | RC(OH)R′R″ | tertiary |

Why it works: the C bonded to Mg is δ−, so it attacks the δ+ carbon of C=O, making a new C–C bond.

Why dry: water reacts with RMgX to give the alkane RH, destroying the reagent.

### Working backwards to a Grignard pair

1. Find the carbon carrying OH (or COOH).
2. Break one C–C bond to that carbon.
3. One fragment becomes RMgX; the other becomes the C=O compound (or CO₂).
4. Check the carbon count adds up.

## 20.3 Problem solving

### Predicting properties (20.3 i)

Name every group. Predict each group's reactions and its effect on boiling temperature and solubility (hydrogen bonding from O–H or N–H). Give the reason with each prediction.

### Route planning (20.3 ii)

1. Compare start and target: carbon count, functional groups, positions.
2. Work backwards one step at a time.
3. Give reagents **and** conditions for every step, and name each intermediate.
4. Up to four steps; the shorter route usually wins on overall yield.
5. Use any reaction the question gives you exactly as described.

### Conversions often needed

| Change | Reagents and conditions |
|---|---|
| alcohol → halogenoalkane | PCl₅, or KBr with 50% H₂SO₄, or red P with I₂ |
| halogenoalkane → alcohol | NaOH(aq), heat under reflux |
| halogenoalkane → nitrile (+1 C) | KCN in ethanol, heat under reflux |
| nitrile → amine | LiAlH₄ in dry ether |
| halogenoalkane → amine | excess ammonia in ethanol, heat in a sealed tube |
| 1° alcohol → aldehyde | K₂Cr₂O₇/H₂SO₄, distil as it forms |
| 1° alcohol → acid; 2° → ketone | K₂Cr₂O₇/H₂SO₄, heat under reflux |
| C=O → alcohol | LiAlH₄ in dry ether |
| acid → acyl chloride | PCl₅ |
| benzene → nitrobenzene → phenylamine | conc. HNO₃/conc. H₂SO₄ below 55 °C; then Sn/conc. HCl, then NaOH |

### Practical choices and hazards (20.3 iii, iv)

Pick the technique for the job. Then pair each hazard with a matching control: flammable → no flames, water bath or electric heater; corrosive → gloves and eye protection; toxic vapour → fume cupboard; water-reactive → dry apparatus.

## 20.4 Core Practical 16: aspirin

Equation: C₇H₆O₃ + (CH₃CO)₂O → C₉H₈O₄ + CH₃COOH

| Stage | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Excess ethanoic anhydride, acid catalyst, warm in a water bath | Make the reaction go to completion at a useful rate |
| Add cold water | Destroy excess anhydride |
| Cool in ice; filter under reduced pressure | Crystallise and collect crude product |
| Recrystallise from minimum hot solvent | Remove soluble impurities |
| Dry; weigh; melting temperature | Yield and purity |

Pure aspirin melts sharply at about 135–136 °C. A lower, broad range shows impurity (often unreacted 2-hydroxybenzoic acid) or water.

## 20.5 Techniques at a glance

| Technique | Removes or achieves | Key detail |
|---|---|---|
| Reflux | long heating without losing volatile substances | vertical condenser, anti-bumping granules |
| Washing with water | water-soluble impurities | separating funnel |
| Washing with Na₂CO₃(aq) | acid impurities | release CO₂ pressure |
| Solvent extraction | moves product into a second solvent | several small portions beat one large |
| Recrystallisation | soluble and insoluble impurities from a solid | minimum hot solvent, hot filtration, cool, cold wash |
| Drying | water from an organic liquid | anhydrous MgSO₄ or CaCl₂ until clear |
| Distillation | separates by boiling temperature | bulb level with side arm |
| Steam distillation | volatile, water-immiscible product that would decompose | distils below 100 °C |
| Melting temperature | purity of a solid | sharp and matches data if pure |
| Boiling temperature | identity and purity of a liquid | steady reading during distillation |

## Must-know distinctions

- **Reflux vs distillation:** reflux keeps everything in the flask; distillation removes the volatile part.
- **Washing vs extraction:** washing removes impurities from the product layer; extraction moves the product out of the mixture.
- **Grignard + methanal vs other aldehydes:** methanal gives a primary alcohol; other aldehydes give secondary alcohols.
- **Empirical vs molecular formula:** you need Mr to go from one to the other.

## Quick self-test

1. A compound contains C 48.6%, H 8.1% and O 43.2% by mass. M⁺ is at m/z = 74. Find the molecular formula.
2. Complete combustion of 0.430 g of a compound of C, H and O gives 1.100 g of CO₂ and 0.450 g of H₂O. Find the empirical formula.
3. Name the Grignard reagent made from iodomethane.
4. Name the product when propylmagnesium bromide reacts with CO₂, then dilute acid.
5. Name the product when methylmagnesium iodide reacts with propanal, then dilute acid.
6. Which carbonyl compound gives a primary alcohol with a Grignard reagent?
7. Why must the ether be dry?
8. Calculate the mass of 2-hydroxybenzoic acid needed to make 2.70 g of aspirin at 100% yield (Mr 138.0 and 180.0).
9. Why is cold water added after the aspirin reaction?
10. Which technique purifies a volatile, water-immiscible liquid that decomposes near its boiling temperature?
11. A proton NMR peak is a triplet. How many H atoms are on adjacent carbons?

### Answers

1. Moles: 4.05 : 8.1 : 2.7 = 1.5 : 3 : 1 = 3 : 6 : 2. C₃H₆O₂ has Mr 74.0, so **C₃H₆O₂**.
2. n(C) = 0.0250 mol (0.300 g); n(H) = 0.0500 mol (0.050 g); O = 0.080 g = 0.0050 mol. Ratio 5 : 10 : 1, **C₅H₁₀O**.
3. **Methylmagnesium iodide**, CH₃MgI.
4. **Butanoic acid**.
5. **Butan-2-ol**.
6. **Methanal**.
7. Water reacts with the Grignard reagent, giving an alkane.
8. 2.70 ÷ 180.0 = 0.0150 mol; 0.0150 × 138.0 = **2.07 g**.
9. To destroy the excess ethanoic anhydride.
10. **Steam distillation**.
11. **Two**.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Leaving out oxygen by difference in combustion data.
- Rounding mole ratios too early, so 1.5 becomes 2.
- Writing "Grignard in water" or leaving out "dry ether".
- Giving the acid step before the addition step in a Grignard reaction.
- Reagents without conditions (reflux, distil, sealed tube, temperature).
- Naming a reagent, such as "dichromate", without the acid.
- Saying a melting temperature is "higher" when impure; it is lower and over a range.
- Controls that do not answer the stated hazard.
- A recrystallisation description with no "minimum volume of hot solvent".
