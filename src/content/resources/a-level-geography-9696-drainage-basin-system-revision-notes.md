Condensed recall for **1.1 The drainage basin system** (inputs, stores, flows and outputs). For full explanations, read the [Core Physical Geography study guide](/resources/a-level-cambridge-geography-core-physical-geography/).

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Drainage basin | Area drained by a river and its tributaries, bounded by a watershed |
| Watershed | High ground separating one basin from the next |
| Open system | Exchanges matter and energy with its surroundings |
| Interception | Rain caught by vegetation before reaching the ground |
| Throughfall | Rain dripping from leaves to the ground |
| Stemflow | Water running down stems and trunks to the ground |
| Infiltration | Water entering the soil surface |
| Infiltration capacity | The maximum rate at which a soil can absorb water |
| Percolation | Water moving down from soil into the rock below |
| Throughflow | Water moving downslope through the soil |
| Overland flow | Surface runoff, when rainfall exceeds infiltration or the ground is saturated |
| Groundwater flow | Slow movement of water through saturated rock |
| Baseflow | Slow groundwater flow into the channel |
| Water table | Upper surface of the saturated zone in rock or soil |
| Aquifer | Permeable rock that stores and transmits groundwater |
| Evapotranspiration | Evaporation plus transpiration from plants |

## The system in one line

**Input:** precipitation. **Stores:** interception, surface, soil moisture, groundwater, channel. **Flows:** stemflow, infiltration, percolation, throughflow, overland flow, baseflow, channel flow. **Outputs:** evapotranspiration and discharge to the sea (or a lake).

## Following a raindrop

1. Rain falling on a forest is first **intercepted** by leaves. Some evaporates straight back to the air (interception loss); the rest reaches the ground as **throughfall** or **stemflow**.
2. At the surface, water either **infiltrates** or, if the ground cannot absorb it, collects in hollows as **surface storage** or runs off as **overland flow**.
3. In the soil it is held as **soil moisture**, taken up by roots and **transpired**, moves sideways as **throughflow**, or **percolates** into the rock.
4. In permeable rock it joins **groundwater storage** and moves slowly as **groundwater flow**, feeding the river as **baseflow**, often for weeks or months after rain.
5. All routes end in **channel storage** and channel flow, and leave the basin as discharge, or return to the atmosphere by evapotranspiration.

The faster routes (overland flow, then throughflow) produce the quick response to storms; the slow routes (groundwater) keep rivers flowing in dry weather.

## Two kinds of overland flow

- **Infiltration-excess (Hortonian) overland flow:** rainfall intensity is greater than the infiltration capacity, even though the soil below is not full. Common on bare, compacted, baked or urban surfaces and in intense storms.
- **Saturation-excess overland flow:** the soil is already full, so any further rain runs off. Common at the foot of slopes and near channels after long periods of rain.

Knowing the difference lets you explain why the same storm produces more runoff after a wet week than after a dry one.

## Water balance

Precipitation = evapotranspiration + runoff, plus or minus the change in storage. If storage is drawn down in a dry spell, runoff is less than precipitation minus losses; if stores fill, the opposite holds. Over a long period the change in storage is close to zero.

**Worked example:** a basin receives 900 mm of precipitation in a year, and evapotranspiration is 500 mm. If storage is unchanged, runoff = 900 - 500 = **400 mm**. If soil and groundwater stores fell by 50 mm over the year (a dry year), runoff = 900 - 500 + 50 = **450 mm**, because water drawn from storage adds to river flow.

**Soil moisture budget terms** (for interpreting graphs):

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Field capacity | Soil holds as much water as it can against gravity |
| Soil moisture utilisation | Plants draw on stored water when evapotranspiration exceeds precipitation |
| Soil moisture deficit | Soil below field capacity, so plants may suffer |
| Recharge | Rainfall exceeds evapotranspiration and refills the soil |
| Surplus | Soil is full, so extra water becomes runoff |

In a temperate climate the soil typically reaches deficit in late summer and recharges in autumn.

## What controls the routes water takes

- **Rock and soil:** permeable rock and sandy soil favour infiltration and percolation; impermeable clay or rock favours overland flow.
- **Vegetation:** more interception and transpiration, and roots open up the soil.
- **Slope:** steep slopes shed water quickly as overland flow.
- **Antecedent conditions:** saturated, frozen or baked ground cannot absorb water.
- **Rainfall intensity:** heavy rain can exceed the infiltration rate.
- **Temperature:** high temperatures raise evapotranspiration; snowfall is stored until it melts.
- **Season:** deciduous trees intercept far less in winter.

## Annotated model paragraph

*"Explain how vegetation affects flows in the drainage basin."*

> Vegetation intercepts rain, so less reaches the ground and some evaporates (**store and output**). Roots open pathways that raise infiltration (**flow**). Transpiration draws down soil moisture, so more rain can be absorbed next time (**store**). Together these reduce overland flow and slow water reaching the channel (**outcome**).

## Exam traps

- A stage in the system is either a store or a flow: do not call infiltration a store.
- Evapotranspiration is an output from the basin, not just from the channel.
- "Open system" needs a reason: inputs and outputs cross the basin boundary.
- Do not confuse infiltration (into the soil) with percolation (down into rock).
- Throughflow is in the soil; groundwater flow is in rock. Keep the depths straight.
- Infiltration is a process; infiltration capacity is a rate.

## Self-test

1. What separates one drainage basin from its neighbour?
2. Name two outputs of the drainage basin system.
3. Difference between throughflow and overland flow?
4. Why is a basin an open system?
5. Give two factors that increase overland flow.
6. State the water balance equation.
7. What is the difference between infiltration-excess and saturation-excess overland flow?
8. Precipitation 750 mm, evapotranspiration 450 mm, storage unchanged: what is runoff?
9. What is field capacity?
10. Which flow keeps rivers flowing in a drought?
11. Why does a deciduous woodland intercept less in winter?

**Answers:** 1. The watershed. 2. Evapotranspiration and river discharge. 3. Throughflow moves through the soil; overland flow moves across the surface. 4. Precipitation enters and water and energy leave across its boundary. 5. Impermeable rock or saturated ground; intense rainfall; steep slopes; sparse vegetation. 6. Precipitation = evapotranspiration + runoff, plus or minus change in storage. 7. Infiltration-excess: rain falls faster than the soil can absorb it. Saturation-excess: the soil is already full. 8. 300 mm. 9. The maximum water a soil can hold against gravity. 10. Baseflow from groundwater. 11. The trees have lost their leaves.
