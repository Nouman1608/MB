Section 12.3 of Cambridge International A Level Sociology (9699) asks whether religion in
post-modern societies is disappearing or simply changing shape. The syllabus has five content
points: whether there has been a growth in privatised religion; the concept of spiritual shopping
and its contribution to understanding religiosity today; debates about whether resacralisation is
a feature of post-modernity; the significance of new religious movements (NRMs) and New Age ideas;
and explanations for the growth of fundamentalist religions, including cultural defence, cultural
transition, disengagement, marginality and religious revival.

The [secularisation, feminism and post-modern religion guide](/resources/a-level-cambridge-sociology-influence-of-religion/)
introduces Lyon, Hervieu-Léger, the Kendal Project and Wallis's typology. This guide explains what
"post-modernity" adds to the analysis, develops each syllabus point with further studies, and
shows how to use the five explanations of fundamentalism evaluatively.

## Syllabus coverage

Cambridge International AS & A Level Sociology 9699 (2024–2026), Paper 4 Section C (Religion),
heading 12 *The influence of religion*, sub-topic 12.3:

- Whether there has been a growth in privatised religion.
- Spiritual shopping and its contribution to understanding religiosity today.
- Whether resacralisation is a feature of post-modernity.
- The significance of NRMs and New Age ideas for understanding religion today.
- Explanations for the growth of fundamentalist religions: cultural defence, cultural transition,
  disengagement, marginality and religious revival.

Paper 4 essays are worth 35 marks each and must explore contrasting views or evidence. The section
links to the key concept of *Inequality and opportunity* through the analysis of fundamentalism.

## What does "post-modernity" add?

Post-modern theorists describe a society marked by fragmentation, choice, consumption and
media-saturated culture. Jean-François Lyotard (1979) described post-modernity as "incredulity
towards metanarratives": grand stories claiming to explain everything, including religious ones,
lose authority. Applied to religion, this produces three predictions:

1. Belief becomes individual and chosen rather than inherited.
2. Religious ideas are mixed, borrowed and consumed like other cultural products.
3. Religion does not vanish; it reappears in new forms and places.

These predictions give an alternative to secularisation (section 12.1): the same fall in
attendance may mean *relocation*, not decline.

## Growth in privatised religion?

Thomas Luckmann's *The Invisible Religion* (1967) argued that religion was not disappearing but
moving from public institutions into the private sphere, where individuals construct their own
systems of meaning. Supporting evidence includes personal prayer, meditation and spiritual
practice outside organisations, and the use of media and the internet. Christopher Helland
distinguished **religion online** (organisations sending information to followers, top-down) from
**online religion** (people creating religious practice and community interactively). Online
religion fits the idea of privatised, self-directed belief.

**Evaluation:** private belief is hard to measure, so claims of growth rest on limited evidence;
Bruce argues privatised faith is weaker because it lacks the social support that keeps belief
plausible; and in many societies religion remains public and political (sections 11.3 and 12.1).

## Spiritual shopping

Spiritual shopping describes individuals selecting beliefs and practices that suit them from a
wide market. Its contribution to understanding religiosity is to explain why measures based on
membership may miss real spiritual activity.

- **Danièle Hervieu-Léger** links this to **cultural amnesia**: religion was once handed down as a
  "chain of memory" through families and communities. As that chain breaks, people no longer
  inherit a fixed religion and instead assemble their own.
- **Wade Clark Roof**, studying the American baby-boom generation, described a "spiritual
  marketplace" of seekers moving between traditions.
- **David Lyon** connects choice to consumer culture and media, which "disembed" religion from
  particular places and communities.

**Evaluation:** spiritual shopping may describe affluent, educated Western groups more than
religious life globally; Bruce sees pick-and-mix belief as evidence of secularisation, because
loosely held beliefs have little effect on behaviour and are rarely passed on to children.

## Is resacralisation a feature of post-modernity?

*Resacralisation* means the return of the sacred: renewed interest in religion and spirituality.
The syllabus spells the term "resacrilisation"; either spelling is understood. Evidence includes:

- **Pentecostal and charismatic growth.** David Martin's *Tongues of Fire* (1990) traced rapid
  Pentecostal expansion in Latin America.
- **Holistic spirituality.** The Kendal Project found it growing, though from a small base.
- **NRMs, New Age and fundamentalism** (below).
- **Religion in public life**, from faith-based political movements to religious responses to
  national crises.

**Debate:** is this resacralisation a *feature* of post-modernity (a response to uncertainty,
choice and the collapse of metanarratives), or simply evidence that secularisation was never
universal? Growth in the global South may be driven by migration to cities and insecurity, which
are features of modernisation rather than post-modernity.

## NRMs and New Age ideas

**What NRMs show about religion today**

- **Eileen Barker**, *The Making of a Moonie* (1984), studied the Unification Church. She found
  that most people who attended its recruitment workshops did not join, challenging claims that
  members were "brainwashed". This suggests membership is a choice, even in demanding groups.
- **Explanations for joining:** marginality and relative deprivation (Wallis's world-rejecting
  movements appeal to those on the edges of society); social change and anomie; and a search for
  meaning or self-improvement (world-affirming movements appeal to successful people seeking
  more).
- **Dynamics:** H. Richard Niebuhr (1929) argued that sects tend to be short-lived or to become
  denominations once the founding generation passes.

**New Age ideas.** Paul Heelas, in *The New Age Movement* (1996), identified **self-spirituality**
(the sacred is found within the self) and **detraditionalisation** (personal experience replaces
external authority) as the core of New Age thought. These features make New Age ideas a clear
example of post-modern religion: chosen, individual and suspicious of institutions.

**Significance:** NRMs and New Age groups are small in membership, so their significance lies less
in numbers than in what they reveal: a persistent demand for meaning, and a shift from obedience
to self-development.

## Explanations for the growth of fundamentalism

Fundamentalist movements typically claim a return to the literal truth of sacred texts, draw sharp
boundaries between believers and outsiders, and react against modern relativism, while often
using modern media skilfully.

| Explanation | Core idea | Fits best when |
|---|---|---|
| **Cultural defence** | Religion becomes a focus of group identity against an external threat (Bruce) | A nation or minority feels dominated by an outside power |
| **Cultural transition** | Religion helps people cope with the upheaval of migration or rapid change and keeps identity | Migrants in a new society; rural migrants to cities |
| **Disengagement** | Believers withdraw from a society they see as corrupt, creating separate communities | Groups rejecting secular or permissive culture |
| **Marginality** | Deprived or excluded groups turn to movements offering certainty and hope | Poverty, unemployment, political exclusion |
| **Religious revival** | Renewal movements respond to perceived moral decline and reassert tradition | Periods of rapid moral or social change |

**Other theorists to add:**

- **Anthony Giddens:** fundamentalism is "tradition defended in the traditional way", a reaction
  to globalisation and uncertainty.
- **Zygmunt Bauman:** post-modern freedom brings anxiety; fundamentalism appeals to those who want
  certainty instead of endless choice.
- **Manuel Castells**, *The Power of Identity* (1997): fundamentalism as **resistance identity**,
  formed by groups who feel excluded by global change, contrasted with **project identity**,
  which builds new identities to transform society.
- **Bruce** distinguishes fundamentalism in the West, often a reaction to internal diversity and
  secular culture, from movements elsewhere reacting to change imposed from outside.

**Evaluating the explanations:** they overlap (marginalised migrants may experience cultural
transition and marginality together); they are better at explaining why people join than why
movements grow at particular times; and the label "fundamentalism" can be applied loosely to very
different groups, so essays should define it and use specific cases.

## Worked example: applying the five explanations

*Original practice question:* "Marginality is the main reason for the growth of fundamentalist
religions." Evaluate this view. **[35]**

**Plan:** define fundamentalism; explain marginality with Weber's theodicy of disprivilege and
support from deprived urban populations; test it against cultural defence (membership spanning
all classes in a threatened nation), cultural transition (migrants who are not necessarily poor),
disengagement (educated believers withdrawing by choice) and revival; add Castells and Bauman for
global and post-modern context; conclude that marginality explains part of the appeal, but the
growth of movements that recruit across classes shows that identity and uncertainty matter as
much as deprivation.

## Common mistakes

- Describing NRMs with Wallis's typology but never saying why they matter today.
- Treating spiritual shopping as automatically disproving secularisation.
- Listing the five fundamentalism explanations with no evaluation of where each fits.
- Using "post-modern" to mean simply "modern" or "recent".
- Labelling groups as fundamentalist without defining the term.

## Command words in Paper 4

**Evaluate** essays in 12.3 reward judgement about *significance*: how widespread a change is,
whom it affects and whether it shows change or decline.
