A **warehouse** is a building used to store goods between production and sale. Sub-topic 4.2 of Cambridge O Level Commerce (7100) covers the **functions of warehousing** in the supply chain, the **different types of warehouse**, the **uses of automation** in warehousing and the **benefits of warehouse automation**.

Production and consumption rarely match exactly. Farmers harvest once a year, but people buy food all year. Factories may make goods steadily, but demand may peak at a festival. A warehouse fills the gap by holding **stock**, the goods a business has ready to sell.

## Functions of warehousing in the supply chain

| Function | Explanation | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Link with seasonal production** | Goods made or harvested at one time of year are stored and sold throughout the year | Rice harvested in autumn is stored and sold until the next harvest |
| **Link with demand** | Goods are made in advance and stored so they are ready when customers want them, including seasonal peaks | Toys made through the year and held ready for the December rush |
| **Protecting supply** | A reserve of stock means that a delay by a supplier or a sudden rise in demand does not leave shelves empty | A shop keeps a spare month of stock in case of a delivery strike |
| **Price stability** | Stock is stored when plentiful and released when scarce, so prices do not rise and fall sharply | Wheat is stored after a large harvest and sold after a poor one |
| **Logistics postponement** | Final packaging, labelling or customisation is delayed until the customer's order is known, so stock is held in a general form | A warehouse holds plain mobile phone cases and prints the customer's chosen design only after the order arrives |

### Why logistics postponement matters

Suppose a company sells the same product with labels in ten languages for ten countries. If it labels everything at the factory, it must guess how many to label for each country, and unsold stock in the wrong language is wasted. With postponement, the warehouse keeps unlabelled stock and adds the right label when an order arrives. This reduces waste and makes the business more flexible.

## Types of warehouse

| Type | What it is | Used by |
|---|---|---|
| **Bonded warehouse** | A secure warehouse approved by customs, where imported goods are stored **without paying customs duty** until they are released for sale | Importers of goods with high duties, such as tobacco, alcohol and perfumes |
| **Cold storage** | A warehouse kept at low temperatures with refrigeration or freezing | Fresh food, frozen food, medicines and flowers |
| **Cash and carry** | A warehouse where trade customers, such as small retailers and caterers, choose goods, pay at once in cash or by card and carry them away | Small shops, restaurants, market traders |
| **Regional distribution centre (RDC)** | A large warehouse serving a region, receiving goods from many suppliers and sending them to shops in that region | Supermarket chains and large retailers |

### Bonded warehouse in more detail

When goods enter a country, customs duty is normally due. In a bonded warehouse the duty is delayed until the goods leave for the home market. If the goods are exported to another country instead, no duty is paid. This helps the importer's cash flow because it does not pay duty on goods that have not yet been sold.

*Worked example.* An importer brings in 1,000 bottles of perfume worth $50 each. The import duty is 20%.

- Value = 1,000 × 50 = $50,000.
- Duty = 20% × 50,000 = $10,000.

If all the perfume is stored in a bonded warehouse and only 250 bottles are released in the first month, duty is paid on 250 × 50 = $12,500, which is 20% × 12,500 = **$2,500**. The importer delays paying the other $7,500 until the rest is sold, improving cash flow.

### Regional distribution centres

An RDC receives full loads from many suppliers and sends mixed loads to shops in its region. Shops get all their goods in one delivery rather than many from different suppliers. This reduces the number of lorries on the road and the cost of transport, and shops can re-order stock quickly. Large retailers can also use an RDC to carry out logistics postponement.

## Automation in warehousing

**Automation** means using machines and computer systems to carry out tasks that people once did.

| Use of automation | How it works |
|---|---|
| **Robotics** | Robots move goods, lift pallets, fetch shelves or load vehicles. Some follow lines or sensors on the floor |
| **Automated sorting** | Conveyor belts, scanners and diverters read the barcode on each item and send it to the right place |
| **Voice picking** | Workers wear headsets and receive spoken instructions on what to pick and where it is, and confirm each pick by speaking, so their hands are free |

### Benefits of warehouse automation

| Benefit | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **Lower labour costs** | Fewer workers are needed to move and sort goods |
| **Less human error** | Machines do not get tired or distracted |
| **Improved picking accuracy** | The right item and quantity are chosen more often |
| **Improved inventory control** | The system records every movement, so the stock count is up to date and shortages are spotted early |
| **Improved customer service** | Orders are picked and sent faster and more accurately, so customers receive the right goods on time |

### Disadvantages

Automation is expensive to buy and install, needs skilled staff to maintain it, may cause job losses, and stops if the computer system or power fails.

### Worked example: automation sums

A warehouse picks 4,000 orders a month. Human pickers make errors on 1.5% of orders. After automation the error rate is 0.2%. Each error costs $18 to correct.

1. Errors before = 1.5% × 4,000 = 60.
2. Errors after = 0.2% × 4,000 = 8.
3. Fewer errors = 60 − 8 = 52.
4. Saving = 52 × 18 = **$936 a month**.

The warehouse also employed 12 pickers at $2,400 a month each, which cost 12 × 2,400 = $28,800. After automation it needs 5 pickers: 5 × 2,400 = $12,000. The labour saving is 28,800 − 12,000 = **$16,800 a month**.

## Case study: a supermarket chain

*Invented example.* A supermarket chain has 60 shops in one region.

1. Suppliers deliver fresh and packaged goods to a **regional distribution centre**, where goods from many suppliers are combined.
2. Fruit, dairy and frozen foods are held in **cold storage** areas at the right temperature.
3. Orders from each shop are picked by **automated sorting**: scanners read the barcodes and conveyors send each item to the right delivery bay. Pickers wearing headsets use **voice picking** for the items that need to be picked by hand.
4. Lorries take mixed loads to the shops, so each shop receives one delivery instead of dozens.
5. Stock records update every time an item moves, so the chain can **protect supply** by re-ordering before shelves empty.

The chain gains lower transport and labour costs, fewer errors and better availability for customers. It loses flexibility if the system fails, and it must pay for expensive equipment.

## Common mistakes

- Describing a warehouse only as "a place to keep things" without a function.
- Saying a bonded warehouse means customs duty is never paid. It is paid when the goods are released for home sale.
- Confusing cash and carry (for trade customers) with a supermarket (for the public).
- Describing automation as "using computers" without a named use such as robotics, automated sorting or voice picking.
- Writing "less mistakes" without saying what is more accurate, such as picking.

## Exam technique

- **Name the function or type**, then **explain** with a reason and a short example.
- **For automation**, link the benefit to the warehouse: picking accuracy, labour cost, stock records.
- **Calculate** percentages carefully: find the percentage of the right total.
