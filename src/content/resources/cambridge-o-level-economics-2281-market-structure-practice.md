Teaching is in the [market structure study guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-economics-2281-market-structure/) and the [market structure revision notes](/resources/cambridge-o-level-economics-2281-market-structure-revision-notes/).

## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Define a monopoly. **[2]**

**2.** Which is a barrier to entry?

A: A large number of small firms in the market

B: A patent held by the existing firm

C: Low start-up costs

D: Buyers who compare prices carefully

**[1]**

**3.** Which is most likely in a market with a high number of firms?

A: High long-run profit for each firm

B: Little choice for consumers

C: Prices close to firms' costs

D: One firm acting as price maker

**[1]**

**4.** The table shows annual sales in a market for cement.

| Firm | Sales ($ million) |
|---|---|
| A | 150 |
| B | 50 |
| C | 30 |
| D | 20 |

**(a)** Calculate the market share of each firm. **[2]**

**(b)** State whether Firm A has monopoly power, giving a reason. **[1]**

**5.** State three characteristics of a monopoly market. **[3]**

**6.** A single firm sells phone repairs in a town at $10 each. Its cost is $6 per repair and it sells 500 a month. Three more firms open; the price falls to $7, and each of the four firms sells 150 repairs a month.

**(a)** Calculate the first firm's monthly profit before and after the new firms open. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the percentage change in the price of a repair. **[1]**

### Section B: structured questions

**7.** Explain how a high number of firms in a market affects price and quality. **[4]**

**8.** Explain two barriers to entry that could protect a monopoly. **[4]**

**9.** A monopoly sells 10,000 units a month at $4 each; its cost is $3 per unit. It raises the price to $5 and sales fall to 8,000.

**(a)** Calculate its profit before and after the price rise. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain why consumers are worse off. **[2]**

**10.** Explain why a water supply network is often provided by a single firm. **[3]**

**11.** Explain two disadvantages of competitive markets for consumers. **[4]**

**12.** Discuss whether a monopoly is always against the interests of consumers. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** A market with a single supplier [1], or one firm so dominant that it controls supply, with no close substitutes and barriers to entry [1]. **[2]**

**2.** B [1]. A patent gives the existing firm the sole legal right to make the product. **[1]**

**3.** C [1]. Rivalry keeps prices close to costs. A, B and D describe monopoly. **[1]**

**4. (a)** Total sales $250 million [1]. A 60%, B 20%, C 12%, D 8% [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** Yes: with 60% of sales, three times its nearest rival, it is dominant and likely able to influence the price [1]. **[1]**

**5.** Any three: one seller or a dominant seller; no close substitutes; barriers to entry; the firm is a price maker; high profit can last in the long run [1 each]. **[3]**

**6. (a)** Before: (10 − 6) × 500 = $2,000 [1]. After: (7 − 6) × 150 = $150 [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** (7 − 10) ÷ 10 × 100 = −30% [1]. **[1]**

*Examiner insight:* This is the long-run effect of entry: consumers pay much less and buy more (600 repairs instead of 500), while each firm's profit is competed away.

**7.** Price: if one firm charges more than its rivals, buyers switch to cheaper firms [1], so each firm must keep its price close to its costs to keep customers [1]. Quality: firms try to attract buyers with better products or service [1], because buyers can easily move to a rival that offers more for the same price [1]. **[4]**

**8.** First: high start-up costs [1], such as building a factory or network, mean new firms cannot raise enough finance to enter [1]. Second: economies of scale [1]: the established firm produces so much that its cost per unit is lower than a small entrant could achieve, so it can undercut any newcomer's price [1]. (Credit also: patents and licences, control of resources or outlets, brand loyalty.) **[4]**

**9. (a)** Before: (4 − 3) × 10,000 = $10,000 [1]. After: (5 − 3) × 8,000 = $16,000 [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** Those who still buy pay $1 more per unit, so they have less to spend on other things [1]; 2,000 units that consumers valued at $4 are no longer bought, so some buyers go without [1]. **[2]**

**10.** Building a network of pipes costs a great deal [1]. Once one network exists, it can serve every household, and a second network would roughly double the cost without supplying more water [1], so one firm can supply the market at lower cost than several: a natural monopoly [1]. **[3]**

**11.** First: each firm is small, so it cannot gain economies of scale [1], and its costs and prices may be higher than a large firm's would be [1]. Second: low profits leave little money for research [1], so fewer new products or improvements may be developed [1]. (Credit also: trivial choice; quality cut where buyers cannot check it.) **[4]**

**12.** Against consumers: with no rivals, a monopoly can raise prices and restrict output, as in question 9 [1]; consumers have little choice [1]; lack of competition may make it complacent about quality and costs [1]. For consumers: economies of scale may give lower costs, and lower prices if passed on [1]; lasting profit can fund research, and in natural monopolies a single network avoids wasteful duplication [1]. Judgement: a monopoly is not always against consumers' interests; it is less harmful when it is regulated, faces substitutes or the threat of entry, or is a natural monopoly, and more harmful when it is unregulated and protected by strong barriers [1]. **[6]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Drawing diagrams that the syllabus does not require.
- Saying "many firms" without explaining the effect on price, quality, choice or profit.
- Assuming a monopoly can raise its price without losing any sales.
- Calling a firm a monopoly without checking market share or substitutes.
- Naming a barrier to entry without explaining why it stops new firms.
- Giving a one-sided answer when asked to discuss.
