Breadth Study B7, "The Middle East: conflict, crisis and change, 1917–2012", is one of eight breadth
studies in change in Section B of Pearson Edexcel International GCSE History (4HI1) Paper 2. It follows
the Arab–Israeli conflict from the Balfour Declaration and the British Mandate in Palestine, through the
creation of Israel and the wars of 1948, 1956, 1967 and 1973, to the peace efforts of Camp David and Oslo,
the Intifadas and the Gaza War of 2008–09. Questions focus on change and continuity across long periods.

## Syllabus coverage

The specification (Issue 5, February 2026) names five **themes**: international involvement; disputed
territorial claims; military conflicts — the key wars; political and diplomatic attempts at lasting peace;
and the role of terrorism. The (c) essay normally draws on two or more key topics. The five **key topics** are:

1. **Build-up of tension in Palestine, 1917–46** — the Balfour Declaration, Jewish immigration, clashes
   between Jews and Palestinians, the Peel Commission, Arab and Jewish terrorism and the King David Hotel
   (1946).
2. **The creation of Israel, the war of 1948–49 and the Suez Crisis of 1956** — Britain hands the problem to
   the UN, the partition plan, the first Arab–Israeli War and Israeli victory, the refugee problem and
   terrorism, the Law of Return; Nasser and the Suez Canal, the British, French and Israeli attack, the UN
   and the superpowers, and the results.
3. **Tension and conflict, 1956–73** — Nasser and Arab aims, the Six-Day War (1967) and its impact, Arafat
   and the PLO, Black September and the Munich Olympics (1972), the Cold War, and the Yom Kippur War (1973).
4. **Diplomacy, peace then wider war, 1973–83** — Kissinger's shuttle diplomacy, the Camp David Agreements
   and the roles of Sadat, Begin and Carter, and the war in Lebanon (1982–83) and the PLO.
5. **Attempts to find a lasting peace, 1987–2012** — the first Intifada, the motives of Arafat, Rabin,
   Netanyahu, Hamas, Hezbollah and the USA, the Oslo Accords (1993), Rabin's assassination, the second
   Intifada (2000), the Roadmap (2003) and the Gaza War (2008–09).

## Why did tension build in Palestine, 1917–46?

In the **Balfour Declaration** (November 1917) the British government supported "a national home for the
Jewish people" in Palestine, while promising not to prejudice the rights of existing non-Jewish
communities. Britain ruled Palestine under a League of Nations **mandate** from the early 1920s. **Jewish
immigration** grew, especially after Hitler came to power in 1933, alarming the Arab majority, who feared
losing their land. **Clashes** included riots in 1920–21 and 1929 and the **Arab Revolt** of 1936–39. The **Peel
Commission** (1937) proposed partition for the first time; Arabs rejected it. Britain's 1939 White Paper
restricted immigration, angering Zionists. Both sides used **terrorism**: Arab groups attacked Jewish
settlements and British forces, while Jewish groups such as the Irgun and Lehi attacked British targets. In
July 1946 the Irgun bombed the **King David Hotel** in Jerusalem, the British headquarters, killing 91 people.

## How was Israel created, and why did it win in 1948–49?

Exhausted and unable to keep order, Britain **handed the problem to the UN** in 1947. The **UN partition plan**
(November 1947) proposed separate Jewish and Arab states, with Jerusalem under international control; Jewish
leaders accepted it and Arab leaders rejected it. Israel declared independence on **14 May 1948**, and armies
from Egypt, Jordan, Syria, Lebanon and Iraq invaded. **Israel won** because its forces were better organised
and motivated, gained arms from Czechoslovakia during a truce, and faced Arab armies that were divided and
poorly coordinated. The 1949 armistices left Israel with more territory than the partition plan had offered.
Around 700,000 Palestinians fled or were driven out, creating a lasting **refugee problem** in camps in
neighbouring states, which fed later **terrorism** and raids. Israel's **Law of Return** (1950) gave every Jew
the right to settle there.

## What were the causes and results of the Suez Crisis (1956)?

Egypt's leader **Nasser** wanted to build the Aswan High Dam and lead the Arab world. When the USA and Britain
withdrew their offer to fund the dam (July 1956), Nasser **nationalised the Suez Canal** to use its revenues.
Britain and France, the canal's main shareholders and users, secretly agreed with **Israel**, which wanted to
stop guerrilla raids from Egyptian territory, that Israel would invade Sinai and Britain and France would
then intervene to "separate" the sides. The attack began in late October. The **USA** opposed it and put
financial pressure on Britain, the **USSR** threatened intervention, and the **UN** called for a ceasefire and
sent an emergency force. Britain and France withdrew. **Results:** Nasser became a hero of Arab nationalism,
Britain's world role was shown to be weakened, the Soviet Union gained influence in Egypt, and Israel gained
access to the Gulf of Aqaba.

## Why did conflict continue, 1956–73?

Nasser and other Arab leaders aimed to defeat Israel. In May 1967 Nasser asked UN troops to leave Sinai and
closed the Straits of Tiran to Israeli shipping. On 5 June Israel struck first, destroying Arab air forces on
the ground. In the **Six-Day War** it captured Sinai and Gaza from Egypt, the West Bank and East Jerusalem from
Jordan and the Golan Heights from Syria. Israel's success came from surprise, air superiority and military
skill. The war left Israel occupying large Arab populations; Arab states were humiliated, and UN Resolution
242 called for withdrawal in return for peace.

**Palestinian resistance** grew. **Yasser Arafat**, leader of Fatah, became chairman of the **PLO** in 1969. After
fighting with the Jordanian army in **Black September** (1970), the PLO was driven out of Jordan. A group named
Black September killed eleven Israeli athletes and coaches at the **Munich Olympics** (1972). The **Cold War**
shaped the conflict: the USSR armed Egypt and Syria, the USA armed Israel. On **Yom Kippur** (6 October 1973)
Egypt and Syria attacked; early success came from surprise and Soviet anti-aircraft and anti-tank missiles,
but Israel recovered with a US airlift of weapons and crossed the Suez Canal. Arab oil producers used an oil
embargo against Israel's supporters. The war restored Arab pride and made Israel more open to negotiation.

## How did diplomacy lead to peace, then wider war, 1973–83?

US Secretary of State **Henry Kissinger**'s **shuttle diplomacy** (1973–75) produced disengagement agreements
between Israel and Egypt and Syria. Egypt's President **Sadat** visited Jerusalem in 1977. At **Camp David**
(1978), hosted by US President **Carter**, Sadat and Israeli Prime Minister **Begin** agreed frameworks that led to
the Egypt–Israel peace treaty (1979): Israel returned Sinai and Egypt recognised Israel. Other Arab states
expelled Egypt from the Arab League, and Sadat was assassinated in 1981. In 1982 Israel **invaded Lebanon** to
destroy the PLO's bases; the PLO was forced to leave Beirut for Tunis. The massacre of Palestinians at the
Sabra and Shatila camps by Lebanese Christian militia, and attacks on Western peacekeepers in 1983, widened the
conflict.

## How did the search for peace develop, 1987–2012?

The **first Intifada** (from December 1987), a mass Palestinian uprising in the occupied territories, drew world
attention; **Hamas** was founded during it and rejected Israel's existence. Arafat, weakened and seeking
statehood, and Israeli Prime Minister **Rabin**, seeking security, signed the **Oslo Accords** (1993) with US
backing: mutual recognition and limited Palestinian self-rule in Gaza and parts of the West Bank. Rabin was
**assassinated** by an Israeli opponent of the deal in 1995, and **Netanyahu** (Prime Minister from 1996) slowed
the process. The **second Intifada** (from 2000) brought suicide bombings and Israeli military operations.
**Hezbollah**, founded in Lebanon in the 1980s, attacked Israel from the north. The **Roadmap for Peace** (2003),
backed by the USA, EU, UN and Russia, failed over violence and settlements. In the **Gaza War** (December
2008–January 2009) Israel attacked Hamas in Gaza; over a thousand Palestinians and thirteen Israelis were killed.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the best theme for a (c) essay?** International involvement (Britain, the UN, the USA and USSR) or
disputed territory, which both span the whole period.

**Why did Oslo fail to bring lasting peace?** Extremists on both sides, Rabin's assassination, settlements,
terrorism and disputes over Jerusalem and refugees.

**Why was Suez significant?** It ended Britain's role as the dominant power in the region.
