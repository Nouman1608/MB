Content tested: "Printing before the 15th Century, including innovations in Asia"; Gutenberg and the press; "the economies of book production; the development of printing industry, typefaces and layouts; new professions and opportunities, including for women; vernacular languages, copyright and intellectual property; the impact on the Renaissance and Reformation in Europe; increased literacy and its impact on society and politics; libraries and universities; the printing revolution and global scientific progress."

## Section A: knowledge (3-mark questions)

**1.** Give three printing innovations made in Asia before c1440. **[3]**

**2.** Give three features of Gutenberg's printing system. **[3]**

**3.** Give three new jobs or opportunities created by the printing industry. **[3]**

**4.** Give three ways in which printers or authors tried to protect their right to print a work, c1450-c1750. **[3]**

## Section B: explanation and judgement

**5.** Explain one way in which printing changed the cost of producing books. **[6]**

**6.** Explain one way in which printing affected vernacular languages. **[6]**

**7.** Explain one way in which printing affected the Renaissance. **[6]**

**8.** Explain two ways in which the printing revolution affected politics in the years c1500-c1800. **[9]**

**9.** Explain two reasons why printing spread so quickly across Europe after c1450. **[9]**

**10.** "The Reformation was the most important consequence of the printing revolution." How far do you agree? **[12]**

**11.** "Gutenberg was the most important factor in the printing revolution." How far do you agree? **[12]**

## Answers

**How these guides work.** 3-mark questions: [1] per relevant point. 6-mark: way identified [1], precise knowledge [2], explanation [2], further developed detail or consequence [1]. 9-mark: each of two ways or reasons [4] (identified [1], precise knowledge [1], explained link [2]) plus [1] for accurate, well-sequenced detail. 12-mark: the given factor [3], at least one other factor [4], comparison in a line of reasoning [3], supported judgement [2].

**1.** Any three, [1] each: woodblock printing in China; the *Diamond Sutra* of 868, the earliest dated printed book; printed paper money and calendars in China; Bi Sheng's clay movable type in the 1040s; metal movable type in Korea, used for the *Jikji* (1377); paper-making.

**2.** Any three, [1] each: metal type cast in a hand mould; an alloy of lead, tin and antimony; a screw press adapted from wine or oil presses; oil-based ink; the use of paper; reusable letters arranged into pages.

**3.** Any three, [1] each: typefounder; compositor; pressman; proofreader; illustrator or engraver; bookbinder; bookseller; publisher; widows running printing businesses, such as Charlotte Guillard in Paris; wider audiences for authors, including women writers.

**4.** Any three, [1] each: privileges from rulers giving sole rights to print a work, for example in Venice; registration of titles with the Stationers' Company in England (chartered 1557); royal licensing of printers; the Statute of Anne (1710), giving authors copyright for a fixed term.

**5.** Way identified [1]: the cost of each copy fell sharply. Precise knowledge [2]: hand-copying a book could take a scribe months, whereas Gutenberg's metal type and screw press, in use by the 1450s, could print hundreds of copies once a page was set. Explanation [2]: although printers paid high start-up costs for type, presses and paper, those costs were spread over a large print run, so each book was far cheaper than a manuscript. Further development [1]: cheaper books reached merchants, craftsmen and students who could never have afforded manuscripts, helping literacy to rise.

**6.** Way identified [1]: vernacular languages were spread and standardised. Precise knowledge [2]: printers found large markets for books in everyday languages; Luther's German New Testament (1522) and Tyndale's English New Testament (1526) were widely read. Explanation [2]: because the same printed spellings and words reached readers across a whole region, printing encouraged a standard written form of each language, rather than many local versions. Further development [1]: this strengthened national identities and reduced the dominance of Latin outside the Church and universities.

**7.** Way identified [1]: classical and humanist texts spread quickly and accurately. Precise knowledge [2]: printers such as Aldus Manutius in Venice produced editions of ancient Greek and Latin authors; Erasmus published a Greek New Testament in 1516. Explanation [2]: because every copy was identical, scholars across Europe could read and compare the same texts and correct errors, so Renaissance ideas spread beyond Italy far faster than by hand-copying. Further development [1]: small, portable editions made the classics available to students and readers outside the great libraries.

**8.** Way one [4]: wider spread of political news and argument [1]; printed newspapers appeared in German cities in the early 1600s, and pamphlet wars accompanied the English Civil War [1]; so more people could follow and take part in political debate, not only rulers and nobles [2]. Way two [4]: governments tried to control printing [1]; licensing, the Stationers' Company and censorship such as the Catholic Index of Prohibited Books (1559) [1]; so printing became a political battleground, with rulers using it for propaganda while trying to suppress criticism [2]. Accurate, well-sequenced detail [1], for example the spread of Enlightenment ideas through the *Encyclopédie* (1751-72).

**9.** Reason one [4]: profit [1]; once a page was set, many copies could be sold, and merchants invested in presses, selling books at fairs such as Frankfurt [1]; so printing became a business that spread wherever there were customers and finance [2]. Reason two [4]: the technology was easy to copy and move [1]; trained printers from Mainz moved to other cities, and by 1500 presses were working in more than two hundred towns, with Venice a major centre [1]; so knowledge of the process spread with skilled workers [2]. Accurate, well-sequenced detail [1], for example the availability of paper or demand from universities and the Church.

**10.** Given factor [3]: Luther's writings were printed in huge numbers after 1517; illustrated pamphlets reached people who could barely read; vernacular Bibles let people read scripture for themselves; the Church could not stop the spread of ideas. Other consequences [4]: the Renaissance, with accurate classical texts and Erasmus's Greek New Testament; scientific progress, from Vesalius and Copernicus (1543) to journals (1665) and Newton (1687); rising literacy and newspapers; standardised vernacular languages; new jobs; copyright. Comparison [3]: the Reformation was the most dramatic short-term consequence and split Western Christianity, but the scientific and literacy changes developed over a longer period and affected all later technological change. Judgement [2]: for example, the Reformation was the most important consequence in the sixteenth century, but over the whole period c1440-c1800 the spread of scientific knowledge and literacy was more important.

**11.** Given factor [3]: Gutenberg combined metal type cast in a hand mould, a screw press, oil-based ink and paper into a workable system; the Gutenberg Bible (c1455) showed what it could do. Other factors [4]: earlier innovations in Asia, including woodblock printing and movable type in China and Korea; the spread of paper from China through the Islamic world; business and finance, such as Fust's investment; demand from universities, the Church and merchants; skilled printers who spread the process; later innovators such as Aldus Manutius. Comparison [3]: Gutenberg's system was the key breakthrough for Europe, but it depended on paper and existing press technology, and it spread because printing was profitable and in demand. Judgement [2]: for example, Gutenberg was the most important individual, but the economic demand for books was the most important factor in turning his invention into a revolution.
