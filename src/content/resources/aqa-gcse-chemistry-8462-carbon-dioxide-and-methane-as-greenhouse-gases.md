This guide covers **4.9.2 Carbon dioxide and methane as greenhouse gases** of the AQA GCSE Chemistry (8462)
specification, for teaching from September 2016. It sits in topic 4.9, Chemistry of the atmosphere, and is
assessed on Paper 2 at both Foundation and Higher Tier. It is not chemistry only, and there is no Higher tier
only statement. A lot of the marks here come from **extended, evaluative writing**, so practise using evidence.

Use the [carbon dioxide and methane revision notes](/resources/aqa-gcse-chemistry-8462-carbon-dioxide-and-methane-as-greenhouse-gases-revision-notes/)

to test yourself. The [chemistry of the atmosphere study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-chemistry-8462-chemistry-of-the-atmosphere/)

## 4.9.2.1 Greenhouse gases and the greenhouse effect

**Greenhouse gases** in the atmosphere **maintain temperatures on Earth high enough to support life**. The
specification names three: **water vapour, carbon dioxide and methane**. Without them the Earth would be far
colder.

### The greenhouse effect: step by step

You must describe the effect in terms of **short and long wavelength radiation** interacting with matter.

1. The Sun emits **short wavelength radiation** (visible light and some ultraviolet and infrared), which passes
   through the atmosphere.
2. The Earth's **surface absorbs** this radiation and warms.
3. The warm surface **emits long wavelength infrared radiation**.
4. **Greenhouse gas molecules absorb** some of the long wavelength radiation, instead of letting it all escape to
   space.
5. They **re-radiate** it in **all directions**, so some goes **back towards the Earth's surface**, keeping it
   warmer than it would otherwise be.

More greenhouse gas molecules mean more long wavelength radiation is absorbed and returned, so the average
temperature rises. Say "**absorb and re-radiate long wavelength (infrared) radiation**". Do not say the gases
"trap heat like a blanket" without the wavelength idea, and do not mix this up with the ozone layer.

## 4.9.2.2 Human activities that increase greenhouse gases

Some human activities increase the amount of **carbon dioxide** and **methane** in the atmosphere. You must recall
**two activities for each**.

| Gas | Human activities |
|---|---|
| **Carbon dioxide** | **Burning fossil fuels** (in power stations, vehicles and homes); **deforestation** (fewer trees to take in carbon dioxide, and burning or rotting wood releases it); also making cement |
| **Methane** | **Farming of cattle** (digestion releases methane); **growing rice** in paddy fields (microbes produce methane); **rubbish decomposing in landfill sites**; leaks from gas extraction |

### Scientific evidence and uncertainty

Based on **peer-reviewed evidence**, many scientists believe that human activities will cause the temperature of
the Earth's atmosphere to **increase at the surface**, which will lead to **global climate change**.

But it is **difficult to model** such a complex system. This leads to:

- **simplified models**
- **speculation**
- **opinions in the media** that may use only part of the evidence and may be **biased**.

You should be able to:

1. **Evaluate the quality of evidence** in a report. Ask: Is it peer reviewed? Is it from a reliable source? How
   much data is there, over what period? Does the writer have a reason to be biased? Is the claim based on
   measurements, or on opinion?
2. **Describe uncertainties** in the evidence, such as natural variation in climate, the difficulty of modelling
   all the factors, and limited records from long ago.
3. **Recognise the importance of peer review** (other scientists check the work before publication) and
   communicating results to **a wide range of audiences** in a clear way.

### Worked example 1: evaluating a claim

*A newspaper says "Scientists prove that carbon dioxide has no effect on temperature", based on one year of data
from one town.*

- **One year** is too short to show a long-term trend, and **one town** is not global.
- The article **gives no peer-reviewed source** and may be biased.
- Global temperature data over decades from many sites, checked by other scientists, is **much better evidence**.

## 4.9.2.3 Global climate change

An **increase in average global temperature** is a major cause of **climate change**. There are several potential
effects. You must **describe briefly four** and **discuss the scale, risk and environmental implications**.

| Potential effect | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **Rising sea levels** | Ice caps and glaciers melt and warm water expands, so coastal areas flood and land is lost |
| **Extreme weather** | More frequent or severe storms, floods, droughts and heatwaves |
| **Changes in rainfall** | Some regions wetter, others drier, which affects water supplies and crop growing |
| **Loss of habitats and species** | Species cannot adapt or move fast enough, so biodiversity falls and ranges change |
| **Changes in crop yields** | Different regions become more or less suitable for growing food |

To **discuss scale, risk and implications**, link them: sea level rise affects very large numbers of people living
on coasts (scale), the risk is of flooding and loss of homes and farmland, and the environmental implications
include loss of coastal habitats such as wetlands.

## 4.9.2.4 The carbon footprint and its reduction

The **carbon footprint** is the **total amount of carbon dioxide and other greenhouse gases emitted over the full
life cycle of a product, service or event**.

It can be reduced by **reducing emissions of carbon dioxide and methane**. Ways to do so include:

| Action | How it helps |
|---|---|
| Use **renewable** energy (wind, solar, hydroelectric) or **nuclear** power instead of fossil fuels | Less carbon dioxide from power generation |
| Improve **energy efficiency** (insulation, efficient appliances) | Less fuel needed |
| Use **public transport**, walk, cycle or share cars; use electric vehicles with low-carbon electricity | Fewer fossil fuels burned |
| **Plant trees** and prevent deforestation | More carbon dioxide taken in by photosynthesis |
| **Carbon capture and storage** | Carbon dioxide from power stations is stored underground |
| **Capture methane** from landfill and use it as fuel; reduce food waste; change livestock feed | Less methane released |
| **Buy local products**, reduce packaging and food waste | Lower footprints across the product's life cycle |

### Why actions may be limited

- **Cost** of new technology and changing infrastructure.
- **Public attitudes**: people may be unwilling to change their lifestyle.
- **Lack of agreement** between countries, so international action is hard.
- **Technical limits**: renewables depend on weather, and carbon capture is not yet widespread.
- **Economic needs**: developing countries may rely on fossil fuels for growth.

### Worked example 2: a footprint question

*A company says that switching from air to sea transport lowers its carbon footprint. State what a full
footprint includes.*

It includes the emissions from **every stage**: obtaining raw materials, manufacturing, packaging, transport, use
and disposal. The change reduces only the transport part, so the full footprint may drop by less than the
transport saving alone.

## Exam technique

- For the greenhouse effect, use: **short wavelength in, long wavelength out, absorbed and re-radiated**.
- Name **two human activities** for each gas, and connect each to carbon dioxide or methane.
- For extended answers, give **advantages and limits**, **evidence** and a **reasoned conclusion**.
- When evaluating a source, comment on **peer review, sample size, time period, bias and data type**.
- Use the term **carbon footprint** to include other greenhouse gases, not only carbon dioxide.

## Common mistakes

- Confusing the greenhouse effect with the hole in the ozone layer.
- Saying greenhouse gases reflect sunlight. They absorb and re-radiate infrared.
- Saying the greenhouse effect is entirely harmful. Without it, Earth would be too cold for life.
- Giving only one human activity per gas.
- Assuming one weather event proves climate change.
- Saying "the scientists agree" without referring to peer-reviewed evidence.
