## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Which **one** of the following is an example of internalisation? Choose **one** letter.

A Laughing at a joke you do not find funny because your friends laugh
B Becoming vegetarian after flatmates explain their reasons, and staying vegetarian after moving out
C Wearing a uniform because school rules require it
D Agreeing with a teacher in class but arguing against the view at home

**[1]**

**2.** In Asch's research, which **one** change produced the largest fall in conformity? Choose **one** letter.

A Increasing the majority from three to fifteen confederates
B Making the comparison lines more similar in length
C Including one confederate who gave the correct answer
D Telling the participant that the confederates were students

**[1]**

**3.** Outline the agentic state explanation of obedience. **[3]**

**4.** Explain how commitment and flexibility can increase the influence of a minority. **[4]**

**5.** Outline Moscovici et al.'s (1969) research into minority influence. **[4]**

### Section B: applied and data questions

**6.** Leah is a trainee at Ashdown Bank. Her branch manager tells her to tell customers that a savings account has "no risk", which Leah believes is misleading. She does it, telling a friend, "It's not my decision; I'm just doing what the manager says."

Using **two** explanations for obedience, explain Leah's behaviour. **[4]**

**7.** A psychologist ran an Asch-type study with 80 participants, each tested in one group-size condition. The percentage of critical trials on which participants conformed is shown below.

| Number of confederates | 1 | 2 | 3 | 6 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Conformity on critical trials (%) | 4 | 12 | 30 | 33 |

**(a)** Identify the independent variable and the dependent variable. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate how many times greater conformity was with three confederates than with one. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain what the results suggest about the effect of group size on conformity. **[2]**

**8.** At Fernley School, Jo and Kai see classmates sharing answers to an online test. Kai joins in. Jo refuses after her friend Amir also says no. Jo says, "What I get in this test is down to me."

Use your knowledge of resistance to social influence to explain why Jo resisted and Kai did not. **[4]**

**9.** In a field study, a researcher asked 50 passers-by to pick up litter while wearing a park warden's uniform, and asked a different 50 while in casual clothes. 38 people obeyed the uniformed researcher and 18 obeyed the casually dressed researcher.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage who obeyed in each condition. **[2]**
**(b)** Use the legitimacy of authority to explain the difference. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain **one** ethical issue raised by this study. **[2]**

**10.** Explain **one** strength and **one** limitation of Milgram's research into situational variables affecting obedience. **[4]**

### Section C: extended writing

**11.** Evaluate research into the effects of unanimity and task difficulty on conformity. **[8]**

**12.** Discuss situational explanations of obedience. Refer to Leah's behaviour in question 6 in your answer. **[16]**

## Answers

**1.** B [1] **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* B is the only option where the change is private and lasts without the group; A and D are compliance, and C is obedience to a rule.

**2.** C [1] **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* A dissenter cut conformity to about 5.5%; adding confederates beyond three made little difference.

**3.** People obey when they see themselves as an agent carrying out the wishes of an authority figure [1]. They shift from the autonomous state, where they feel responsible, to the agentic state, where responsibility is passed to the authority (the agentic shift) [1]. Binding factors, such as not wanting to seem rude, keep them in this state even when they feel moral strain [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Three distinct points are needed; repeating "they feel less responsible" in different words scores once.

**4.** Commitment: when a minority makes personal sacrifices for its view, the majority sees that it is sincere and confident [1], so they pay closer attention to the argument (the augmentation principle) [1]. Flexibility: a minority that repeats its view rigidly may seem dogmatic and be dismissed [1]; one that accepts reasonable compromise while keeping its core position seems more reasonable and persuades more people [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Two marks per factor: what it is, then why it increases influence.

**5.** Groups of six female participants judged the colour of 36 blue slides that varied in brightness [1]. Two confederates were the minority [1]. When the minority consistently called every slide "green", participants gave "green" answers on 8.42% of trials [1], compared with 1.25% when the minority was inconsistent, showing that consistency increases minority influence [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* The comparison between consistent and inconsistent conditions earns the final mark; one figure alone does not show the effect.

**6.** Legitimacy of authority: the branch manager is higher in the bank's hierarchy, so Leah accepts that he has the right to give her orders [1]; as a trainee she has learned to defer to managers' instructions [1]. Agentic state: Leah's comment "it's not my decision" shows she sees herself as the manager's agent [1], passing responsibility to him, so she obeys despite believing the claim is misleading (moral strain) [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Each explanation needs a quotation or detail from the scenario to reach full marks.

**7. (a)** IV: the number of confederates (group size) [1]. DV: the percentage of critical trials on which participants conformed [1].
**(b)** 30/4 = 7.5 times [1]
**(c)** Conformity rises sharply as the majority increases from one to three [1], but adding more confederates beyond three makes little further difference (30% to 33%), as Asch found [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* For (c), describe both the rise and the levelling off; "more people means more conformity" earns one mark at most.

**8.** Jo has an internal locus of control: "down to me" shows she believes outcomes depend on her own actions [1], so she takes responsibility and is less influenced by others [1]. Amir's refusal gives her social support, showing that resistance is possible and that she will not be the only one [1]. Kai has no such support and may have an external locus of control, so he conformed to the majority [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Both explanations named in the specification are expected; one explained well with scenario detail can reach three marks.

**9. (a)** Uniform: 38/50 × 100 = 76% [1]. Casual: 18/50 × 100 = 36% [1].
**(b)** The uniform signals that the researcher holds a legitimate role with the right to give orders in the park [1], so people accept the instruction as coming from an authority and obey more often [1].
**(c)** Deception: passers-by did not know they were in a study, so they could not give informed consent [1]; they could not be told of their right to withdraw, though the request was harmless and they could be debriefed afterwards [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* An ethical issue needs explaining in the context of this study, not just naming.

**10.** Strength: the variations were controlled laboratory experiments, changing one variable at a time [1], so changes in obedience, such as the fall to 20.5% with orders by telephone, can be attributed to that variable [1]. Limitation: participants may have realised the shocks were fake, as Orne and Holland (1968) argued [1], so the studies may measure willingness to play along rather than genuine obedience, lowering internal validity [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Sheridan and King (1972) can be used to counter the limitation in a longer answer.

**11.** The real exam marks extended answers with levels of response, not point by point. These indicative points show the content a strong answer could use.

- Asch found that one dissenting confederate reduced conformity to about 5.5%, showing that unanimity is key to majority pressure [1].
- A dissenter giving a different wrong answer also reduced conformity, so breaking unanimity, not just hearing the right answer, frees people to be independent [1].
- Making the lines more similar increased conformity, consistent with informational social influence when a task is ambiguous [1].
- Strength: high control over variables allows cause and effect to be established [1].
- Limitation: the line task is trivial, so findings may not generalise to situations with real consequences [1].
- Limitation: Perrin and Spencer (1980) found much less conformity among UK engineering students, so the findings may reflect 1950s America [1].
- Limitation: the samples were American male students, so the results may not apply to other groups [1].
- Judgement: the variables are well supported in the laboratory, but their size in everyday life is uncertain [1].

**[8]**
*Examiner insight:* "Evaluate" needs judgement; describing the variations alone stays in the lowest band.

**12.** The real exam marks extended answers with levels of response, not point by point. A strong answer might use this plan.

*AO1 (description)*

- Agentic state: people see themselves as agents of an authority, not responsible for their actions [1].
- The agentic shift is the move from the autonomous to the agentic state [1].
- Binding factors and moral strain explain why people continue despite anxiety [1].
- Legitimacy of authority: hierarchies give some people the right to command, learned from childhood [1].
- Milgram's baseline: 65% of participants went to 450 V [1].
- Proximity: obedience fell to 40% with the learner in the same room and 20.5% with telephoned orders [1].
- Location: obedience fell to 47.5% in a run-down office [1].
- Uniform: obedience fell to 20% when an ordinary member of the public gave orders; Bickman (1974) found more obedience to a guard's uniform [1].

*AO3 (evaluation and application)*

- Leah's "not my decision" fits the agentic state, and her deference to the manager fits legitimacy [1].
- Hofling et al. (1966) found 21 of 22 nurses ready to give a dangerous dose, supporting legitimacy in a real setting [1].
- Rank and Jacobson (1977) found most nurses refused when they knew the drug and could consult, so obedience depends on the details of the situation [1].
- Milgram's participants showed great distress, which the agentic state does not fully explain if they had passed on responsibility [1].
- Orne and Holland (1968) questioned whether participants believed the shocks were real, but Sheridan and King (1972) found high obedience with real shocks [1].
- Situational explanations cannot explain why some people disobey in the same situation; the Authoritarian Personality offers a dispositional alternative [1].
- Situational accounts help explain real cases of destructive obedience, giving them practical value, but they risk being used to excuse harmful acts [1].
- Judgement: situational factors explain much of the overall level of obedience, while dispositional factors may explain who resists [1].

**[16]**
*Examiner insight:* The question asks for Leah to be used; an essay that never applies the theory to her cannot reach the top band.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing about identification instead of the two types of conformity named in Version 1.4.
- Swapping normative and informational social influence.
- Describing the agentic state without the shift of responsibility.
- Giving scenario answers that never mention the people or details in the scenario.
- Quoting Milgram percentages for the wrong variation.
- Naming an ethical issue without linking it to the study.
- Leaving out a final judgement in 8- and 16-mark answers.
