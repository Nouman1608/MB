Key question 3.2 of Cambridge O Level Sociology (2251) asks *What attempts have been made to reduce
social inequalities?* The syllabus names three kinds of attempt (legislation, welfare states and the
work of non-governmental organisations) and then asks how successful sociologists think they have
been. Description earns only part of the credit: Paper 1 Question 3 rewards answers that explain *how*
a measure is supposed to improve life chances and then weigh the criticisms from the New Right,
Marxists, feminists and critics of NGOs.

The [O Level guide to tackling inequality and globalisation](/resources/o-level-sociology-reducing-inequality-and-globalisation/)
gives an overview of 3.2 and 3.3 together. This guide explains the mechanisms in more detail, with a
wider range of examples.

## What the syllabus covers

- **3.2.1 Legislation**, for example progressive taxation, redistribution of wealth through benefits, a
  minimum wage, equal opportunities laws and other government measures.
- **3.2.2 Welfare states** and their impact on life chances: free and/or universal education,
  unemployment benefits, pensions, low-cost housing, free healthcare, universal basic income.
- **3.2.3 The global work of NGOs**: reducing poverty, providing medical assistance, promoting
  business, improving education.
- **3.2.4 Sociological views of success**: New Right criticisms of the welfare state and the
  underclass; the culture of poverty and the dependency culture; Marxist criticisms of the welfare
  state; criticisms of NGOs; feminist criticisms of equality legislation.

## Legislation: how each measure is meant to work

**Progressive taxation** takes a higher *percentage* of higher incomes. An invented illustration: if
income up to 20,000 is taxed at 10% and income above that at 30%, a person earning 15,000 pays 1,500
(10% of their income), while a person earning 60,000 pays 2,000 plus 12,000, which is 14,000 (about
23% of their income). The revenue can fund services used by everyone, so the gap in living standards
narrows. Taxes on spending, by contrast, often take a larger share of poorer people's income, because
they spend more of what they earn.

**Redistribution through benefits** transfers resources to those in need. **Means-tested** benefits go
only to people below an income limit, which targets help but can stigmatise claimants and discourage
take-up. **Universal** benefits go to everyone in a category, such as all pensioners, which avoids
stigma but is more expensive. Some schemes are **conditional**: Brazil's Bolsa Família, launched in
2003, paid poor families on condition that children attended school and had health check-ups, linking
immediate relief to longer-term life chances.

**A minimum wage** sets a legal floor under pay, such as the National Minimum Wage introduced in the
United Kingdom in 1999. It raises the incomes of the lowest paid, many of whom are women, young people
and migrants. Critics argue employers may cut jobs or hours, and it does not help the large number of
people in informal work in many countries.

**Equal opportunities laws** outlaw discrimination. Britain's Equality Act 2010 brought earlier laws
together and protects characteristics including age, disability, race, religion or belief and sex.

**Other government measures** include reserved places or quotas for disadvantaged groups. India's
constitution provides for reservations in education, government jobs and legislatures for Scheduled
Castes and Scheduled Tribes, an attempt to counter inequality rooted in caste.

## Welfare states and life chances

A **welfare state** is a system in which the government takes responsibility for citizens' basic
welfare. In Britain the Beveridge Report (1942) identified "five giants" to be defeated: Want, Disease,
Ignorance, Squalor and Idleness. Each provision named in the syllabus targets one or more of these.

| Provision | Giant tackled | Life chance improved |
|---|---|---|
| Free/universal education | Ignorance | Qualifications, employment, social mobility |
| Unemployment benefits | Want, Idleness | Income security between jobs |
| Pensions | Want | Income and health in old age |
| Low-cost housing | Squalor | Safe, secure homes; better health |
| Free healthcare | Disease | Health and life expectancy regardless of income |
| Universal basic income | Want | A guaranteed floor of income for all |

**Universal basic income (UBI)** is an unconditional regular payment to every individual, whether or
not they work. It is mostly trialled rather than fully adopted. Finland's experiment in 2017–2018 paid
2,000 unemployed people a monthly sum without conditions; participants reported better wellbeing,
while the effect on employment was small. Supporters say UBI removes stigma and the poverty trap;
critics point to its cost and argue it may reduce the incentive to work.

## NGOs: four kinds of work

Non-governmental organisations are independent, non-profit bodies, from small local charities to large
international organisations.

- **Reducing poverty:** BRAC, founded in Bangladesh in 1972, runs programmes for the very poor,
  including support for families to build savings and small enterprises.
- **Providing medical assistance:** Médecins Sans Frontières, founded in France in 1971, provides
  emergency medical care in conflict zones and disasters.
- **Promoting business:** the Grameen Bank, founded by Muhammad Yunus in Bangladesh, pioneered
  **microcredit**, small loans to poor people (mostly women) without collateral; Yunus and the bank
  shared the Nobel Peace Prize in 2006.
- **Improving education:** The Citizens Foundation in Pakistan, founded in 1995, runs schools in
  low-income areas.

## How successful have these attempts been?

**New Right.** Charles Murray argued that generous welfare creates an **underclass** of people who
depend on benefits, with weak family ties and little attachment to work. The solution is to cut
benefits and encourage self-reliance. *Response:* most benefit claimants move in and out of work, and
poverty is often caused by low pay, illness or unemployment rather than attitudes.

**Culture of poverty and dependency culture.** Oscar Lewis argued that people in long-term poverty
develop fatalistic values that they pass on to their children, keeping them poor. The related idea of
a **dependency culture** claims that welfare discourages people from helping themselves. *Response:*
critics argue values are a reasonable reaction to blocked opportunities, so changing opportunities
changes behaviour.

**Marxists.** The welfare state does not challenge the ownership of wealth. It keeps workers healthy
and educated enough to be productive, and calms discontent that might lead to revolution. Benefits are
set low, and the wealthy can avoid taxes, so inequality persists. *Response:* welfare has
substantially improved health, literacy and life expectancy for working-class people.

**Critics of NGOs.** NGOs may depend on foreign donors and follow their priorities; they are not
elected and may lack accountability to the people they serve; they can duplicate or undermine state
services; and they treat symptoms rather than structural causes. Microcredit has also been criticised
when borrowers take on debts they cannot repay.

**Feminists.** Equality laws have opened education and work to women, but they cannot change unpaid
domestic labour, the "dual burden" of paid and unpaid work, or the glass ceiling. Feminists also
argued that the Beveridge model assumed a male breadwinner and a dependent wife, building gender
inequality into the welfare state.

## Worked example: an annotated "explain three" answer (practice)

*Explain three ways a welfare state may improve life chances.*

> First, free education allows children from poor families to gain qualifications, which opens routes
> to better-paid work and upward social mobility. Second, free healthcare means people receive
> treatment according to need rather than income, so illnesses are treated earlier and life
> expectancy rises. Third, unemployment benefits provide an income when people lose their jobs, which
> prevents families falling into absolute poverty and losing their homes.

**Why it works:** each point names a provision, explains the mechanism and links it to a specific
life chance.

## Common mistakes

- Listing measures without explaining the mechanism by which they reduce inequality.
- Confusing the New Right (welfare does too much) with Marxists (welfare does too little).
- Describing NGOs as part of the government.
- Ignoring 3.2.4 entirely: questions on success need criticisms.
- Assuming a law works because it exists; judge its effects.

## Exam technique

- **Explain** (parts c and d): measure, mechanism, life chance.
- **Discuss** (part e): one view in depth, such as the New Right, with a response.
- **Evaluate** (part f): set the achievements of welfare or NGOs against the criticisms and conclude.
