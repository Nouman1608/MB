## What this sub-topic asks you to do

- Describe and explain the characteristics of urban places, including their population, density and economic activities.
- Explain patterns of land use and of residential and economic areas within cities.
- Compare urban areas of different sizes, functions and levels of development.
- Apply and evaluate models of urban structure.

## What counts as urban?

Countries define "urban" differently. Some use a minimum population, which ranges from a few hundred people to tens of thousands; others use density, administrative status or the share of workers outside farming. Comparisons of urbanisation between countries therefore carry hidden error.

Within one country, a city's population also depends on its boundary. Three definitions are common:

| Definition | What it includes | Typical effect on population |
|---|---|---|
| City proper | The area governed by the city's own administration | Smallest; may exclude most suburbs |
| Urban agglomeration | The continuous built-up area, whatever the administrative boundaries | Larger |
| Metropolitan area | The built-up area plus surrounding places linked by daily commuting | Largest |

### Worked example 1: one city, three populations

Invented data for a single city:

| Definition | Population | Area (km squared) | Density (people per km squared) |
|---|---|---|---|
| City proper | 2,100,000 | 300 | 7,000 |
| Urban agglomeration | 6,800,000 | 1,700 | 4,000 |
| Metropolitan area | 9,500,000 | 9,500 | 1,000 |

The same city could be listed as a city of about 2 million or of about 9.5 million, and its density as 7,000 or 1,000 people per square kilometre. Rankings of the world's largest cities change depending on which definition they use. Before comparing cities, check the definition, and say so in your answer.

## Measuring density

- **Gross density** divides population by the total area of a district, including roads, parks and industry.
- **Net residential density** divides population by the area used for housing only.

A district of 5 km squared with 60,000 residents has a gross density of 60,000 / 5 = 12,000 people per km squared. If only 3 km squared is housing, the net density is 60,000 / 3 = 20,000 people per km squared. A neighbourhood can appear crowded or spacious depending on which measure is used, so planners specify it.

### Density gradients

In many cities, density is highest near the centre and declines outwards. Colin Clark (1951) showed that this decline is often roughly **exponential**: density falls by a similar proportion for each equal step outwards, rather than by a fixed amount.

Invented transect data:

| Distance from centre (km) | 0 | 2 | 4 | 6 | 8 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Density (people per km squared) | 20,000 | 12,000 | 7,200 | 4,320 | 2,592 |

Each 2 km step multiplies density by 0.6 (12,000 / 20,000 = 0.6; 7,200 / 12,000 = 0.6), which is the exponential pattern. Over time, suburbanisation and better transport **flatten** the gradient: central densities fall and outer densities rise. In many cities of lower-income countries, central densities remain very high and informal settlements at the edge add a second peak, so the gradient is not a smooth curve. Use these departures to evaluate models.

## Inside the central business district

The CBD contains the **peak land value intersection**, the most accessible and most expensive point in the city, usually where the main roads or transit lines meet. Land use is sorted outwards from it:

- **Core:** department stores, flagship shops, banks and head offices, which need maximum footfall or prestige and can pay the highest rents.
- **Frame:** land-hungry or lower-rent uses such as car parks, warehouses, wholesale trade, hospitals and transport terminals.
- **Vertical zoning:** within tall buildings, shops occupy the ground floor where passing trade is greatest, with offices and sometimes housing above.

The CBD is not fixed. It can migrate towards higher-status districts (the zone of assimilation) and leave run-down areas behind it (the zone of discard). Out-of-town retail parks and edge-of-city office parks have reduced the dominance of the CBD in many car-oriented cities.

## Economic activity in urban areas

Cities differ in the mix of their economic activities:

- **Formal sector** jobs are registered and taxed, with legal protections.
- **Informal sector** work, such as street vending, waste picking and home-based manufacturing, is often the main source of income in fast-growing cities of lower-income countries.
- **Knowledge-based activities**, such as finance, design and research, cluster in world cities and in specialised districts because firms benefit from being close to skilled workers and to each other.

The mix shapes land use: informal trading occupies streets and pavements, while knowledge clusters drive up office rents and housing costs.

## From city to megalopolis

As cities grow, their built-up areas and commuter belts can merge. Jean Gottmann (1961) used the term **megalopolis** for the chain of cities along the north-east coast of the United States, from Boston to Washington. Similar urban corridors are often described elsewhere, for example along Japan's Pacific coast. Within such regions, many smaller centres function together, so models built around a single CBD fit poorly.

## Annotated model answer

*Explain why urban population densities vary within a city.* **(sample paragraph)**

"Density is usually highest near the centre because accessibility makes land expensive, so housing is built upwards in flats to spread land cost over more households **[bid-rent mechanism]**. Further out, cheaper land allows larger plots and detached homes, so density falls, often by a similar proportion for each kilometre, as Clark's gradient describes **[pattern named]**. However, the pattern is distorted where informal settlements occupy unused land at the edge, or where planning rules such as height limits keep central density low **[exceptions]**. Density therefore reflects land value, transport, planning and inequality together **[synthesis]**."

## Common mistakes

- Comparing city populations without checking the definition used.
- Confusing gross and net density.
- Describing the density gradient as a straight line when it is usually exponential.
- Treating the CBD as uniform rather than as a core and frame.
- Applying single-centre models to urban regions with several centres.

## Exam technique

- **Describe** an urban pattern by quoting values and naming zones or districts.
- **Explain** variation with land value, accessibility, history, physical site and planning.
- **Compare** two cities by stating similarities and differences on the same criteria.
- **Evaluate** a model by testing it against a named city and stating where it fits and fails.
