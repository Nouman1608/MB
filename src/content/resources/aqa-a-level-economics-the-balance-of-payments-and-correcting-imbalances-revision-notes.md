## Definitions to know

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Balance of payments | Record of transactions between residents and the rest of the world over a period |
| Current account | Goods, services, primary income, secondary income |
| Capital account | Small capital transfers and non-produced assets (such as rights to natural resources) |
| Financial account | Investment and lending flows, plus changes in reserves |
| Current account deficit | Payments out exceed earnings in on the current account |
| Current account surplus | Earnings in exceed payments out |
| Primary income | Net profits, interest, dividends and cross-border wages |
| Secondary income | One-way transfers: remittances, aid, subscriptions to international bodies |
| FDI | Investment giving lasting interest and control in a foreign business |
| Portfolio investment | Buying foreign shares or bonds without control |
| Expenditure-reducing | Policies that cut total demand, and so imports |
| Expenditure-switching | Policies that move spending from imports to domestic output |

Memory line: the accounts always sum to zero (with errors and omissions), so a "deficit" means the current account.

## Structure of the current account

```
Current account = goods balance
                + services balance
                + primary income balance
                + secondary income balance
Trade balance   = goods balance + services balance
```

Check the sign of each: exports and income received are credits; imports and income paid are debits. A deficit can arise even when the trade balance is positive if income paid abroad is large.

**Worked reminder.** Country C: goods 140 exports, 175 imports; services 88 and 61; primary income 30 received, 47 paid; secondary income 9 received, 21 paid. Balances: −35, +27, −17, −12. Current account = **−37**. Trade balance = −35 + 27 = −8.

## Influences on the current account

| Factor | Effect on the balance | Key evaluation |
|---|---|---|
| Higher productivity | Lowers unit costs, raises price competitiveness | Slow to build; wages may rise as fast |
| Higher relative inflation | Exports dearer, imports cheaper in comparison: worsens | Offset if the currency falls |
| Currency depreciation | Exports cheaper abroad, imports dearer at home | Needs elasticities summing to more than 1; time lags |
| Faster domestic growth | Imports rise with income | Cyclical, may reverse in a slowdown |
| Non-price competitiveness | Quality and design hold demand at higher prices | Hard to measure |

**Unit labour cost** = wage per hour ÷ output per hour. Country D pays 15 per hour for 5 units: 3.00. Wages rise 4% and output per hour 8%: 15 × 1.04 ÷ (5 × 1.08) = 2.889, a fall of **3.7%**.

**Relative inflation.** Home 3%, partners 5%: 1.03 ÷ 1.05 − 1 = **−1.9%**. Home goods become relatively cheaper, which supports the current account.

**Exchange rate arithmetic.** A fall from 0.80 to 0.72 foreign units per home unit is a 10% depreciation (0.72 ÷ 0.80 − 1). The other way round, a foreign good that cost 1 / 0.80 = 1.25 home units now costs 1 / 0.72 = 1.39: a rise of 11.1%.

**Marshall-Lerner and the J-curve.** Depreciation improves the balance if the export and import price elasticities of demand, added together, come to more than 1. In the short run both are low, so the balance may worsen before improving. (The specification does not name these, but they support evaluation.)

**Worked reminder: depreciation.** Exports 200, imports 260 (balance −60). Currency falls 5%; export elasticity 1.0, import elasticity 0.6, sum 1.6. Export volume +5%: 200 × 1.05 = 210. Import prices +5% and volume −3%: 260 × 1.05 × 0.97 = 264.81. New balance = 210 − 264.81 = **−54.81**, an improvement of 5.19. State the assumptions: full pass-through to import prices, home-currency export prices unchanged, supply able to meet extra demand.

**Cyclical or structural?** A deficit that appears in a boom and shrinks in a slowdown is cyclical, so waiting may be enough. A deficit that persists through the cycle points to weak competitiveness and needs supply-side action.

## Investment flows

| | FDI | Portfolio |
|---|---|---|
| Control | Yes | No |
| Time horizon | Long | Short, easily reversed |
| Benefits | Capital, technology, jobs, skills | Funds domestic firms and government |
| Risks | Profits flow out later; foreign control | Sudden outflow, currency falls, interest rates rise |

Share example: a country receives 30 of FDI and 10 of portfolio inflows: FDI is 30 ÷ 40 = 75%. A higher FDI share usually means a more stable way of financing a deficit.

## Policies: method in steps

**Choosing and judging a policy**

1. Name the policy and say whether it reduces or switches expenditure.
2. Give the mechanism in steps (for example: higher tax, lower disposable income, lower consumption, fewer imports).
3. Quantify if data allow (multiplier, import share).
4. Say which other objectives are hit.
5. Judge using size, speed, elasticities, retaliation and the cause of the imbalance.

**Multiplier reminder.** Multiplier = 1 ÷ (marginal propensity to save + marginal rate of tax + marginal propensity to import). With 0.1, 0.1 and 0.3 the multiplier is 2. A spending cut of 25 lowers income by 50 and imports by 0.3 × 50 = **15**.

| Type | Examples | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| Expenditure-reducing | Tax rises, spending cuts, higher interest rates | Does not rely on elasticities | Costs output and jobs |
| Expenditure-switching | Depreciation, tariffs, quotas, export subsidies | Keeps demand up | Elasticities, retaliation, inflation |
| Supply-side | Training, infrastructure, innovation | Tackles root cause | Slow, uncertain |

For a surplus, the reverse: expand demand, let the currency rise, open the market to imports.

## Significance and global implications

- **Larger deficits matter more** when persistent, financed by short-term capital, caused by uncompetitiveness, and when the country cannot borrow in its own currency.
- **A deficit may be benign** when it finances investment that raises future output.
- **A surplus** may signal strength or weak domestic demand; it can fuel currency tension and inflation.
- **Global:** if a major deficit economy cuts demand and surplus economies do not expand, world demand falls (a contractionary bias). Large currency moves can start retaliation. Capital flows may reverse and shift interest rates worldwide.
- **Shared adjustment** (surplus countries expanding demand) lowers the cost.

## Must-know distinctions

- Current account against balance of payments as a whole.
- Trade balance against current account.
- Primary against secondary income: payment for a factor of production against a transfer.
- FDI against portfolio.
- Reducing against switching.
- Cyclical against structural imbalance.

## Quick self-test

1. Name the four parts of the current account.
2. Country E has goods −22, services +30, primary income −9, secondary income −6. Find the current account balance.
3. A deficit of 27 occurs in an economy with GDP of 600. Express it as a percentage of GDP.
4. Wages per hour are 15 and output per hour is 5. Wages rise 4% and output per hour 8%. Find the percentage change in unit labour cost.
5. Home inflation is 3% and partner inflation 5%. Find the change in relative prices.
6. State one difference between FDI and portfolio investment.
7. A spending cut of 25 with multiplier 2 and marginal propensity to import 0.3: find the fall in imports.
8. A currency moves from 0.80 to 0.72 foreign units. State the percentage depreciation.
9. Give one expenditure-switching and one expenditure-reducing policy.
10. Why might depreciation fail to improve the current account?

### Answers

1. Goods, services, primary income, secondary income.
2. −22 + 30 − 9 − 6 = **−7** (a deficit).
3. 27 ÷ 600 = **4.5%**.
4. 15 × 1.04 ÷ (5 × 1.08) = 2.889 against 3.00, so **−3.7%**.
5. 1.03 ÷ 1.05 − 1 = **−1.9%** (home goods relatively cheaper).
6. FDI gives control and lasting interest; portfolio gives no control and is easily reversed.
7. Income falls 25 × 2 = 50; imports fall 0.3 × 50 = **15**.
8. 0.72 ÷ 0.80 − 1 = **−10%**.
9. Switching: tariff, quota or depreciation. Reducing: higher income tax or lower government spending.
10. The sum of the price elasticities of demand for exports and imports may be below 1, or lags (J-curve) delay the gain; domestic inflation may also erode the advantage.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "deficit" without saying which account.
- Putting the sign of a debit the wrong way round, so a deficit becomes a surplus.
- Quoting a percentage of GDP without showing the division.
- Saying a weaker currency "makes exports cheaper" without linking it to elasticity and the current account.
- Treating FDI and portfolio investment as the same thing.
- Describing a policy without the step-by-step mechanism.
- Ignoring side effects on growth, unemployment and inflation when asked about other objectives.
- Concluding that every deficit is bad, with no judgement of size, cause or financing.
- Describing global effects only for the home country.
