## Questions

**1.** State two features of a perfectly competitive market. **[2]**

**2.** Distinguish between monopolistic competition and oligopoly in terms of (i) the number and size of firms and (ii) barriers to entry. **[4]**

**3.** Identify the main type of barrier to entry (legal, market, cost or physical) in each case. **[4]**

**(a)** A firm holds the only licence to run a ferry route.
**(b)** A well-known brand spends heavily on advertising each year.
**(c)** One company owns every quarry in a region that contains the only limestone deposit.
**(d)** A new firm would need equipment worth 40 million cu to compete.

**4.** The table shows sales in Country D's tinned fish market. Total sales are 300 million cu.

| Firm | P | Q | R | S | T | All others |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sales (million cu) | 60 | 45 | 30 | 30 | 15 | 120 |

**(a)** Calculate the three-firm concentration ratio. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the four-firm concentration ratio. **[1]**
**(c)** Firms Q and R merge. Calculate the new three-firm concentration ratio. **[2]**

**5.** A rail line has a fixed cost of 1,200,000 cu a year and a variable cost of 3 cu per passenger. Annual demand is 300,000 passengers.

**(a)** Calculate average cost if one firm runs the line. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate average cost if two firms each carry half the passengers. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why this market might be a natural monopoly. **[2]**

**6.** Country E's glass market demands 600,000 tonnes a year. In market A the minimum efficient scale (MES) is 150,000 tonnes. In market B it is 400,000 tonnes.

**(a)** Calculate how many firms could operate at MES in each market. **[2]**
**(b)** State which market is more likely to be supplied by one firm and explain why. **[2]**

**7.** A new firm buys machinery for 800,000 cu, which could be resold for 320,000 cu. It also spends 150,000 cu on staff training. If it closes early it must pay a contract termination fee of 60,000 cu.

**(a)** Calculate the sunk cost of the outlay. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the sunk cost as a percentage of the total outlay, to one decimal place. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain how sunk costs and the termination fee act as barriers to exit, and how this may affect entry. **[3]**

**8.** Market W has firm shares of 48%, 20%, 12% and 9%, with 11% held by many small firms. Market Z has firm shares of 25%, 23%, 21% and 20%, with 11% held by many small firms.

**(a)** Calculate CR4 for each market. **[2]**
**(b)** State CR1 for each market. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain why the markets differ although their CR4 values are equal. **[3]**

**9.** Explain why a firm in monopolistic competition has some control over its price but not much. **[4]**

**10.** Evaluate whether a high concentration ratio shows that a market is uncompetitive. **[8]**

**11.** The table shows the market shares (%) in Country A's paper market in year 1 and year 5.

| Firm | A | B | C | D | All others |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Year 1 | 34 | 26 | 14 | 9 | 17 |
| Year 5 | 38 | 27 | 12 | 8 | 15 |

**(a)** Calculate the two-firm concentration ratio in each year. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the change in the three-firm concentration ratio, in percentage points. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the percentage increase in firm A's market share between year 1 and year 5, to one decimal place. **[2]**
**(d)** Evaluate whether the data show that barriers to entry have risen. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** Any two of: many buyers and sellers; a homogeneous (identical) product; free entry and exit; perfect information; firms are price takers. One feature [1], a second different feature [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Two distinct features earn two separate credits, so avoid giving the same feature in two wordings, such as "many firms" and "lots of sellers".

**2.** Monopolistic competition has many small firms [1], while oligopoly has a few large firms [1]. Entry into monopolistic competition is fairly free [1], while oligopoly has significant barriers to entry [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A distinction needs both sides of the comparison. Describing only oligopoly leaves half the credit unearned.

**3.** (a) Legal [1]. (b) Market [1]. (c) Physical [1]. (d) Cost [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Naming the type is enough here. In longer answers, add the mechanism, because the label alone explains little.

**4.** **(a)** CR3 = (60 + 45 + 30) / 300 x 100 [1] = **45%** [1].
**(b)** CR4 = (60 + 45 + 30 + 30) / 300 x 100 = 165 / 300 = **55%** [1].
**(c)** After the merger the firms are QR 75, P 60, S 30 and T 15. The three largest are 75, 60 and 30 [1], so CR3 = 165 / 300 x 100 = **55%** [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Show which firms form the top three after the merger. The merged firm is the largest, so it is easy to add the wrong firms.

**5.** **(a)** Average cost = 1,200,000 / 300,000 + 3 [1] = 4 + 3 = **7 cu** [1].
**(b)** Each firm carries 150,000, so average cost = 1,200,000 / 150,000 + 3 [1] = 8 + 3 = **11 cu** [1].
**(c)** A second firm duplicates the fixed cost of 1,200,000 cu [1], so average cost rises from 7 cu to 11 cu. Average cost is still falling at the output of the whole market, so one firm supplies at lower cost [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Support the explanation with the figures you calculated. A bare statement that fixed costs are high is weaker than a comparison of 7 cu with 11 cu.

**6.** **(a)** Market A: 600,000 / 150,000 = **4 firms** [1]. Market B: 600,000 / 400,000 = **1.5**, so only one firm can reach MES [1].
**(b)** **Market B** is more likely to have one supplier [1]. A second firm would produce about 200,000 tonnes, below MES, so its average cost would be higher [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A result of 1.5 firms needs interpreting. Say that it means one firm at MES, not 1.5 firms.

**7.** **(a)** Machinery loss = 800,000 − 320,000 = 480,000 [1]. Add training 150,000: sunk cost = **630,000 cu** [1].
**(b)** 630,000 / 950,000 x 100 = **66.3%** [1].
**(c)** Sunk costs cannot be recovered on leaving, so leaving means a loss and the firm may stay in the market [1]. The termination fee adds 60,000 cu, taking the cost of early exit to 690,000 cu [1]. Entrants expect to face these costs if they fail, so the risk deters entry too [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Link exit barriers back to entry. Many answers define the barrier but stop before saying what it does to new firms.

**8.** **(a)** Market W: 48 + 20 + 12 + 9 = **89%** [1]. Market Z: 25 + 23 + 21 + 20 = **89%** [1].
**(b)** CR1 is **48%** in W and **25%** in Z [1].
**(c)** In W one firm holds 48% and the other leaders are much smaller, so that firm is close to a dominant position [1]. In Z four firms are almost equal and must watch each other closely [1]. CR4 does not show how shares are divided, so look at CR1 or individual shares as well [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the figures from both markets in the explanation. Answers that only say "CR does not show distribution" miss the application.

**9.** Products are differentiated by brand, quality or location [1], so some customers prefer this firm and it can raise its price without losing every sale [1]. There are many rivals selling close substitutes [1], and entry is fairly free, so a high price makes customers switch and attracts new firms [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Both parts of the question need an answer: why there is some control, and why it is limited. A one-sided answer leaves half the question unanswered.

**10.** Definition and argument, up to 8 points:
- A concentration ratio is the combined market share of the n largest firms [1].
- A high figure means a few firms hold most sales, so they may have market power and set prices above competitive levels [1].
- Barriers to entry may protect them from new rivals [1].
- Fewer rivals mean less pressure to cut prices or improve quality [1].
- However, CR hides how the share is divided, since four equal rivals compete differently from one dominant firm [1].
- It depends on how the market is defined, and ignores imports and foreign rivals [1].
- If barriers are low, new firms can enter, so the high figure may overstate market power [1].
- Judgement: a high CR is a warning sign and not proof. It should be supported by evidence on barriers, prices and entry [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Evaluation gains credit when it ends in a supported judgement. A list of limitations with no conclusion usually earns less than a shorter answer that weighs them.

**11.** **(a)** Year 1: 34 + 26 = **60%** [1]. Year 5: 38 + 27 = **65%** [1].
**(b)** Year 1 CR3 = 34 + 26 + 14 = 74% [1]. Year 5 CR3 = 38 + 27 + 12 = 77%, so the change is **+3 percentage points** [1].
**(c)** Rise = 38 − 34 = 4 points, and 4 / 34 x 100 [1] = **11.8%** [1].
**(d)** Rising CR is consistent with higher barriers, as leaders keep their position and others lose share [1]. But it could result from leaders offering better products or lower prices, with no barriers involved [1]. The rise is small and covers only two points in time, so a trend is not established [1]. Evidence on new entrants, firm numbers, profits and costs is needed before concluding anything [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* Keep percentage points and percentage change apart. Answers that mix them tend to go wrong on both parts.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Defining a structure but not applying the features of number, product, entry and information.
- Naming a barrier with no mechanism, or no link to the number of firms.
- Giving a concentration ratio with no n, or adding the wrong firms.
- Dividing by the total of the leading firms rather than the whole market.
- Mixing percentage points with percentage change.
- Treating a high CR as proof of high prices, with no evaluation.
- Treating a natural monopoly as a firm with high costs, when it has the lowest average cost at market scale.
- Stating a result such as 1.5 firms without interpreting it.
- Giving a list of evaluation points with no judgement.
