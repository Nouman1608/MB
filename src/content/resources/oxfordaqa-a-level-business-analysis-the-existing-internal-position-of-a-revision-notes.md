## 1. Product portfolio: the Boston Matrix

**Product portfolio** = all the products a business sells. Portfolio analysis shows whether the range is balanced and where marketing spending should go.

| Category | Market growth | Market share | Cash position | Usual marketing plan |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Star | High | High | Earns a lot, spends a lot | Hold: keep investing |
| Cash cow | Low | High | Generates surplus cash | Harvest: minimal spending |
| Question mark | High | Low | Uses cash, earns little | Build or drop |
| Dog | Low | Low | Earns little | Divest, or keep if it supports other lines |

**Method in steps: using the matrix in an answer**

1. Place each product using the growth and share data given.
2. Say what each position means for cash.
3. Link to a marketing decision (build, hold, harvest, divest).
4. Judge: is the portfolio balanced? What does it not tell you (profit, the market definition, future change)?

## 2. The financial statements

**Income statement** (a period): revenue − cost of sales = **gross profit**; − operating expenses = **operating profit**; − interest − taxes = **net profit**.

**Statement of financial position** (one date): total assets (non-current plus current) less total liabilities (current plus non-current) gives **net assets**, which equal **total equity** (share capital + retained earnings).

**Capital employed = non-current liabilities + total equity.**

**Amending the statements: what moves together**

| Event | Statement of financial position | Income statement |
|---|---|---|
| Long-term loan used to buy machinery | Non-current assets up; non-current liabilities up | No change until interest is paid |
| Inventory written off | Inventory down; retained earnings down | Cost of sales up; profits down |
| Customer debt will not be paid | Trade receivables down; retained earnings down | Expenses up; operating profit down |
| New shares issued for cash | Cash up; share capital up | No change |
| Dividend paid | Cash down; retained earnings down | No change (paid out of net profit) |

## 3. Ratio formulas

| Group | Ratio | Formula | Unit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Profitability | Gross profit margin | 100 × gross profit ÷ revenue | % |
| | Operating profit margin | 100 × operating profit ÷ revenue | % |
| | Net profit margin | 100 × net profit ÷ revenue | % |
| | ROCE | 100 × operating profit ÷ capital employed | % |
| Liquidity | Current ratio | current assets ÷ current liabilities | ratio (e.g. 1.5) |
| | Acid test ratio | (current assets − inventory) ÷ current liabilities | ratio |
| Gearing | Gearing | non-current liabilities ÷ capital employed × 100 | % |
| Efficiency | Payables days | trade payables ÷ cost of sales × 365 | days |
| | Receivables days | trade receivables ÷ revenue × 365 | days |
| | Inventory turnover | cost of sales ÷ average inventory | times a year |
| Shareholder | Dividend per share | total dividends ÷ number of shares | pence or £ |
| | Dividend yield | dividend per share ÷ market share price × 100 | % |

Average inventory = (opening inventory + closing inventory) ÷ 2.

**Reading the direction**

- Higher margins and ROCE: better use of revenue and capital.
- Liquidity too low: risk of not paying short-term debts. Too high: idle cash or excess inventory.
- Gearing above about 50% is often called high, but it depends on interest rates and the stability of profits.
- Lower receivables days and higher inventory turnover usually help cash flow.
- Higher payables days help cash but can annoy suppliers.
- A rising dividend yield can mean a bigger dividend or a falling share price.

## 4. Using ratios well

**Value:** quick comparison over time and between firms; shows strengths and weaknesses; useful to managers, lenders and investors.

**Three tests before you judge a ratio**

1. **Trend:** how has it moved over several years?
2. **Benchmark:** how does it compare with rivals or the industry average?
3. **Context:** what explains it? The business environment (recession, interest rates, competition) and the firm's objectives and strategy (a growth push may cut ROCE for a while).

**Limitations of financial reporting**

- Historic: shows the past, not the future.
- Leaves out non-financial factors such as staff morale, brand reputation and environmental impact.
- A year-end statement may not be typical; one-off items distort ratios.

## 5. Profit centres

**Profit centre:** a section (branch, product range, division) whose revenue and costs are both recorded, so its profit can be measured.

| Value | Problems |
|---|---|
| Shows which sections perform well or badly | Shared overheads must be split, often arbitrarily |
| Motivates managers judged on results | Centres may compete rather than cooperate |
| Supports decisions to invest, cut or close | Short-term profit may be chased at the cost of long-term aims |
| Aids delegation | Extra cost of recording and monitoring |

**Must-know:** a centre showing a loss after overheads may still make a positive contribution. Closing it loses that contribution unless the overheads also go.

## 6. Business culture

**Culture:** the shared values, attitudes and behaviour in a business.

**Influences (spec list):** leadership and employee values; leadership style; mission; reward systems; organisational structure; customer expectations; business size.

**Must-know distinctions**

| Pair | Meaning | Impact |
|---|---|---|
| Positive vs negative | Trust, openness, customer focus vs blame, fear, corner-cutting | Engagement, productivity and retention vs turnover, mistakes, ethical risk |
| Strong vs weak | Values widely shared vs values differ between groups | Consistency and less supervision, but resistance to change vs inconsistency, but flexibility |

**Changing culture**

- *Reasons:* new leader or owner, merger or takeover, poor performance, scandal, new customer expectations, a new strategy the old culture blocks.
- *Problems:* slow; resistance (strongest in strong cultures); key staff may leave; fails unless rewards, structure and leaders' behaviour change too.

## Quick self-test

1. A product has 12% market growth and a relative market share of 0.3. Which Boston Matrix category is it?
2. Revenue £450,000; cost of sales £279,000. Calculate the gross profit margin.
3. Operating profit £96,000; non-current liabilities £150,000; total equity £450,000. Calculate ROCE.
4. Using question 3's data, calculate gearing.
5. Current assets £84,000 (including inventory £35,000); current liabilities £56,000. Calculate the current and acid test ratios.
6. Trade receivables £27,000; revenue £450,000. Calculate receivables days.
7. Cost of sales £279,000; opening inventory £30,000; closing inventory £32,000. Calculate inventory turnover.
8. Total dividends £2.52 million; 36 million shares; share price £1.75. Calculate dividend per share and dividend yield.
9. In question 3, the business takes a further £50,000 long-term loan to buy equipment. Calculate the new gearing.
10. State two non-financial factors that financial statements leave out.
11. Give one reason a strong culture might hinder a business.
12. A business with an autocratic leader and individual sales bonuses is likely to have what kind of culture?

### Answers

1. **Question mark** (high growth, low share).
2. Gross profit = 450,000 − 279,000 = £171,000; margin = 171,000 ÷ 450,000 × 100 = **38%**.
3. Capital employed = 150,000 + 450,000 = £600,000; ROCE = 96,000 ÷ 600,000 × 100 = **16%**.
4. Gearing = 150,000 ÷ 600,000 × 100 = **25%**.
5. Current ratio = 84,000 ÷ 56,000 = **1.5**; acid test = (84,000 − 35,000) ÷ 56,000 = **0.875**, about **0.88**.
6. 27,000 ÷ 450,000 × 365 = **21.9 days**.
7. Average inventory = (30,000 + 32,000) ÷ 2 = £31,000; turnover = 279,000 ÷ 31,000 = **9 times**.
8. Dividend per share = 2,520,000 ÷ 36,000,000 = **£0.07 (7p)**; yield = 0.07 ÷ 1.75 × 100 = **4%**.
9. Non-current liabilities = £200,000; capital employed = £650,000; gearing = 200,000 ÷ 650,000 × 100 = **30.77%**.
10. Any two: staff morale, brand reputation, environmental impact.
11. Shared values make staff resist new ways of working when the market changes.
12. A controlled, competitive culture: staff follow instructions and compete with each other rather than share ideas, because leadership style and reward systems both push that way.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using total assets, or equity alone, in place of capital employed for ROCE or gearing.
- Including inventory in the acid test ratio.
- Using cost of sales for receivables days or revenue for payables days.
- Using closing inventory when opening and closing figures are both given.
- Giving a percentage answer with no % sign, or a ratio in days with no unit.
- Stating "ROCE fell" without explaining why or what it means for the business in the case.
- Judging a single year's ratio without a trend, benchmark or link to strategy.
- Recommending the closure of a loss-making profit centre without checking its contribution to overheads.
- Placing products in the Boston Matrix but not linking the result to a marketing decision.
- Listing culture factors without showing how each one shapes behaviour in the case business.
