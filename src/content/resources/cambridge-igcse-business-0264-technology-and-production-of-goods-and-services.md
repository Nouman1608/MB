This guide goes deeper on section 4.2 (Technology and production of goods and services) of the Cambridge IGCSE Business 0264 syllabus for examination in 2027, 2028 and 2029. Its one content statement, 4.2.1 The use of technology in the production of goods and services, lists three points: "how technology is changing production methods through automation and mechanisation, e.g. Computer-Aided Manufacture (CAM) and 3D Printing"; "how technology is improving productivity in the service sector, e.g. contactless payments"; and "advantages and disadvantages of changes in technology for businesses and employees". The broader [technology and production guide](/resources/igcse-business-0264-technology-and-production-of-goods-and-services-and-related-topics/) defines the terms with worked examples on CAM, 3D printing and contactless payment. This page goes further: a full decision on whether to automate, service-sector technology beyond payments, and how to write about employees in a balanced way. ## What the syllabus asks for

| Point in 4.2.1 | What you must be able to do |
|---|---|
| Automation and mechanisation, e.g. CAM and 3D printing | Explain how each changes the way a product is made, and recognise it in a case |
| Technology and productivity in services, e.g. contactless payments | Explain how technology lets service staff serve more customers, or serve them better |
| Advantages and disadvantages for businesses and employees | Weigh the effects on *both* groups, using the data, and reach a judgement |

## A quick ladder of production methods

It helps to see the methods as steps.

1. **Hand production:** people use hand tools; slow, flexible, skilled.
2. **Mechanisation:** machines do the physical work, but a person operates each machine. A baker using an electric dough mixer is an example.
3. **Automation:** machines or computer-controlled systems carry out tasks with little or no human involvement once set up. A bottling line that fills, caps and labels without anyone handling the bottles is an example.

CAM and 3D printing are both technologies a business might use as it moves up the ladder. CAM means computers control the machines making the product. 3D printing builds an object layer by layer from a digital design, which suits one-off items, replacement parts and prototypes.

## Worked example: should Strathmore Bottling automate?

Strathmore Bottling fills and labels bottles of spring water. It currently uses a mechanised line: 20 workers operate filling and labelling machines, and output is 48,000 bottles a week. Each worker earns $500 a week.

A supplier offers an automated line costing $350,000, with maintenance of $30,000 a year. With it, Strathmore would need 12 workers (to supervise the line, check quality and handle deliveries) and output would rise to 60,000 bottles a week.

**Step 1: labour productivity.** The syllabus gives the formula: output per period divided by number of employees.

```
Before: 48,000 / 20 = 2,400 bottles per worker per week
After:  60,000 / 12 = 5,000 bottles per worker per week
Increase: (5,000 - 2,400) / 2,400 x 100 = 108.3%
```

**Step 2: wage bill.**

```
Before: 20 x 500 = $10,000 a week
After:  12 x 500 = $6,000 a week
Saving: $4,000 a week, or 4,000 x 52 = $208,000 a year
```

**Step 3: compare with the cost of the line.** After maintenance, the yearly saving is 208,000 - 30,000 = $178,000. The line costs $350,000, so the savings would cover its cost in just under two years (350,000 / 178,000 = 1.97 years), assuming Strathmore can sell the extra bottles.

**Step 4: the factors the numbers leave out.**

| For automating | Against automating |
|---|---|
| Wage cost per bottle falls sharply | Eight workers lose their jobs or must be moved; morale among those who stay may fall |
| Output rises by 12,000 bottles a week, which can meet growing demand | The extra output is only worth having if customers will buy it |
| Filling is more consistent, so fewer wrongly filled bottles | A breakdown stops the whole line; with fewer staff, there is no manual back-up |
| Frees remaining staff from repetitive work | $350,000 is a large sum; Strathmore may need a loan and pay interest |

**Judgement.** If demand for Strathmore's water is rising, the automated line looks worthwhile: productivity more than doubles and the cost is covered in about two years. Strathmore should manage the change carefully, for example by retraining some workers to supervise the line and offering redundancy pay to others. If demand is flat, much of the gain comes only from cutting jobs, and the case is weaker.

## Technology in the service sector

The syllabus gives contactless payments as an example. The same idea, more customers served per member of staff, applies to many other technologies.

| Technology | How it raises service productivity | Possible drawback |
|---|---|---|
| Contactless and mobile payments | Each payment takes seconds, so queues move faster | Fees to the card provider; risk if the system goes down |
| Online booking systems | Customers book themselves at any hour, so receptionists spend less time on the phone | Some customers, often older ones, prefer to call |
| Self-service checkouts and kiosks | One assistant can supervise several tills | Theft and errors can rise; some customers dislike them |
| Ordering by phone or tablet at the table | Waiting staff spend less time taking orders and more time serving food | Less personal contact; the system must be reliable |
| Online banking | Customers pay bills and move money without visiting a branch | Fewer branch jobs; cyber-security costs |

### Worked example: Calloway Bistro

At Calloway Bistro, a waiter spends about 4 minutes taking each table's order on paper. With ordering on customers' own phones, the waiter's time per table falls to about 1.5 minutes (checking the order and answering questions).

```
Tables a waiter can take orders from per hour, paper: 60 / 4   = 15
Tables a waiter can take orders from per hour, phone: 60 / 1.5 = 40
```

The bistro can either serve more tables with the same staff at busy times, or use the freed time to give better service. The risk is that regular customers who enjoy chatting to staff may feel the bistro has become less friendly.

## Writing about employees

Questions often ask about effects on employees, and many answers say only "they lose their jobs". Better answers show both sides and link them to the case.

- **Job losses or fewer hours** where machines replace tasks, as at Strathmore.
- **Retraining:** some workers learn to programme, supervise or repair the new equipment. This can mean more skilled and better-paid roles.
- **Less heavy, dangerous or boring work** as machines take over lifting, cutting or repetitive tasks.
- **Worry and lower motivation** during the change, especially if employees are not told what is happening.
- **Changed skills:** workers who were skilled at hand production may find their skills are no longer valued.

## Annotated model answer

Question: *Explain one disadvantage to employees of a hotel introducing online check-in.*

Model answer: Fewer reception staff will be needed, because guests check themselves in on their phones instead of queuing at the desk. Some receptionists may have their hours cut or be made redundant, which reduces their income and job security.

Why it earns credit: the disadvantage is clear (fewer staff needed), linked to how the technology works in a hotel (guests check in on their phones), and followed by the effect on the employee (lower income and security).

## Exam technique

The command words in the 0264 syllabus include **calculate**, **define**, **identify**, **state**, **outline**, **explain**, **consider** and **justify**.

- **Calculate:** use the labour productivity formula, state the units ("bottles per worker per week") and show the working.
- **Explain:** state an effect, link it to the specific technology in the case, then give the consequence.
- **Consider / justify:** separate effects on the business and on employees, weigh both, then decide. Mention a condition, such as whether demand is rising.

## Common errors

- Using "automation" and "mechanisation" as if they meant the same thing.
- Describing 3D printing as suitable for mass production of millions of identical items.
- Writing about factory technology when the question asks about the service sector.
- Answering about the business when the question asks about employees, or the reverse.
- Stating that technology "saves money" without saying which cost falls and what new costs appear.
