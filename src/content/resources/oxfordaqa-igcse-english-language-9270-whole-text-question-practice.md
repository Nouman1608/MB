## The text: "The Lamp-Mender of Kessel Street" (original biography)

**Section 1**

> Nobody in Kessel Street remembers when Josef Halt arrived, only that his shop was already there
> when they were children: a narrow room between a bakery and a locksmith's, with a window so crowded
> with broken lamps that the light inside was always yellow and second-hand. Children dared each other
> to touch the glass. Josef, it was said, could hear a crack in a lampshade from the back of the shop.

**Section 2**

> He was not a friendly man. He charged what he liked, and he liked to charge a great deal. When
> customers complained, he held the repaired lamp up to the light without a word, and they paid. His
> neighbours called him the Owl, partly for his round spectacles and partly for the hours he kept;
> his window glowed long after the bakery had closed.

**Section 3**

> In the winter the power station failed, and the street went dark for eleven nights. On the second night
> the baker's wife knocked on Josef's door to ask whether he had candles to sell. He had something
> better. From the shelves at the back of the shop, where he kept the lamps nobody had collected, he
> brought out oil lamps by the dozen, filled them, trimmed them, and set one on every doorstep in
> Kessel Street. He did not charge for them. When the power returned, nobody saw him collect the lamps.
> They were simply gone, one morning, like frost.

**Section 4**

> Josef died the following spring. The shop was cleared within a week. But in Kessel Street, if you
> walk down it now on a winter evening, you will see that several houses keep an oil lamp in the front
> window, unlit, polished, as if waiting. Ask why, and people will tell you it is in case the lights
> go out again. Ask who they are waiting for, and they will not answer.

## Section A — Short questions

**1.** From Section 1, list **four** things you learn about Josef's shop. **[4]**

**2.** Explain the effect of the phrase "the light inside was always yellow and second-hand". **[2]**

**3.** In Section 2, what impression does the writer give of Josef's character? Use **two**
quotations. **[4]**

**4.** Explain the two reasons given for the nickname "the Owl". **[2]**

**5.** Explain the effect of the simile "like frost" in Section 3. **[2]**

## Section B — Longer questions

**6.** How does the writer use language in Section 3 to present Josef's generosity? **[6]**

**7.** How does the writer present Josef across the **whole text**? **[8]**

**8.** How has the writer structured the text to interest the reader? **[8]**

**9.** Trace how the presentation of **light** develops across the whole text. **[6]**

**10.** A student said: "By the end, the street understands Josef better than it did while he was
alive." To what extent do you agree? Refer to the **whole text**. **[10]**

**11.** Write the **overview sentence** you would use to begin an answer to Question 7, and explain why
it is effective. **[3]**

## Answers

**How these are marked.** Short questions are point-marked as shown. For the longer questions, the
bracketed credits show one way to reach the tariff; other valid points of the same quality earn the
same credit.

**1.** Any four, [1] each: it is narrow; it is between a bakery and a locksmith's; the window is
crowded with broken lamps; the light inside is yellow; it has been there as long as anyone remembers;
children dare each other to touch the glass.

**2.** "Second-hand" suggests the light has passed through old, broken lamps and is worn or reused [1],
which creates a slightly mysterious, shabby atmosphere around the shop and its owner [1].

**3.** Impression [2]: unfriendly and greedy, but highly skilled [1 for each of two impressions]. Two
quotations [2]: "He was not a friendly man"; "he liked to charge a great deal"; "held the repaired
lamp up to the light without a word".

**4.** His round spectacles look like an owl's eyes [1], and he keeps late hours, like a nocturnal
bird [1].

**5.** Frost disappears silently in the morning without anyone seeing it go [1], so the simile makes
Josef's collection of the lamps quiet and almost magical, showing he wanted no thanks [1].

**6.** Credit up to 6: the contrast between "candles to sell" and "something better" shows he gives
more than is asked [2]; the list of verbs "filled them, trimmed them, and set one" shows care and
effort [2]; the short sentence "He did not charge for them" stands out against Section 2's greed and
surprises the reader [2].

**7.** Credit up to 8: Section 1 presents him as a near-legend, half-frightening to children [2];
Section 2 presents him as unfriendly and greedy, yet skilled [2]; Section 3 is the turning point, in
which his free gift of light reveals generosity [2]; Section 4 presents him through his absence, as
the lamps in the windows suggest he is missed and honoured [2].

**8.** Credit up to 8: the opening begins with communal memory ("Nobody… remembers"), creating
mystery [2]; Section 2 builds a negative impression that the reader expects to be confirmed [2];
Section 3 reverses it through the power cut, a crisis that tests the community [2]; the ending moves
to the present tense and the unanswered question, leaving the reader with an open, reflective
close [2].

**9.** Credit up to 6: in Section 1 the light is "yellow and second-hand", linked to brokenness and
mystery [2]; in Section 3 light becomes a gift, as he sets a lamp on every doorstep in the darkness
[2]; in Section 4 the unlit, polished lamps in windows make light a symbol of memory and hope [2].

**10.** Credit up to 10. A judgement [2]; supporting points such as the reversal of Section 3, which
shows the street discovering his kindness [2], and the lamps kept in windows as a sign of respect
[2]; qualifying points such as the refusal to answer "who they are waiting for", which suggests the
street still cannot fully explain or speak of him [2]; a conclusion weighing both, for example that
the street understands his kindness but not the man [2].

**11.** A sentence that names the movement across the whole text [2], for example: "The writer
presents Josef first as a mysterious, unfriendly figure and then, through one act of generosity, as
a man whose kindness the street only recognises after his death." Explanation [1]: it sets up an
argument about change that the paragraphs can follow.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Answering Question 7 from Section 2 alone, where the impression is most vivid.
- Retelling the story in order for Question 8 without naming structural choices.
- In Question 10, agreeing completely without weighing the unanswered question at the end.
