## 17.1 Variation

**Variation** is the differences between individuals of the same species.

- **Continuous variation** gives a **range of phenotypes between two extremes**, for example body length and body mass. It is caused by **genes and the environment**. A mass of 61.3 kg is possible, so any value in the range can occur.
- **Discontinuous variation** gives a **limited number of phenotypes with no intermediates**, for example ABO blood groups, and seed shape (round or wrinkled) and seed colour in peas. It is **usually caused by genes only**.

### Investigating variation (17.1.5)

1. **Continuous:** measure one feature of many individuals of one species, such as the length of 24 leaves from one tree, using a ruler in mm. Group the values into equal classes and draw a **histogram** (bars touching), because the data are continuous.
2. **Discontinuous:** count individuals in each category, such as round and wrinkled seeds from a sample of pea pods. Draw a **bar chart** (bars of equal width, not touching), because the data are in categories.
3. Use a large sample, and measure the same way each time.

### Worked example 1: which chart?

Leaf lengths (mm) were sorted into classes: 35–44: 2 leaves; 45–54: 7; 55–64: 10; 65–74: 5.

- These are lengths, so any value is possible: **continuous** variation. Plot a **histogram** with class on the x-axis and number of leaves on the y-axis.
- The data peak in the middle class and tail off at both ends. That shape is typical of continuous variation.
- The leaves share similar genes, yet differ in length. This suggests the **environment** (light, water, position on the tree) also affects length.

## 17.2 DNA

### Structure of DNA (17.2.1)

- **Two strands** coiled together to form a **double helix**.
- Each strand is a **chain of nucleotides**.
- Each nucleotide contains a **base**: A, T, C or G (full names are not required).
- **Bonds between pairs of bases** hold the two strands together.
- The bases always pair in the same way: **A with T** and **C with G**.

### Genes and proteins (17.2.2–17.2.5)

- A **gene** is a length of DNA that codes for a protein.
- DNA controls cell function by **controlling the production of proteins**, including **enzymes**. Enzymes control the reactions in a cell, so the genes a cell uses decide what it does.
- The **sequence of bases** in a gene determines the **sequence of amino acids** needed to make a specific protein.
- **Different sequences of amino acids give different shapes** to protein molecules. Shape decides function: the active site of an enzyme and the binding site of an antibody both depend on it (see [Enzymes](/resources/enzymes/)).

### Worked example 2: base pairing

**(a)** One strand reads **ATCGGA**. Write the other strand.
Pair each base: A–T, T–A, C–G, G–C, G–C, A–T. Answer: **TAGCCT**.

**(b)** In a DNA molecule, 30% of the bases are A. Find the percentage of C.
A pairs with T, so T = **30%**. A + T = 60%, leaving 40% for C + G. C pairs with G, so they are equal: C = **20%**.

## 17.3 Inheritance

**Inheritance** is the transmission of genetic information from generation to generation.

### Key terms (17.3.2–17.3.3)

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Allele | An alternative form of a gene |
| Genotype | The alleles an organism has, e.g. Rr |
| Phenotype | The observable features, e.g. round seeds |
| Homozygous | Two identical alleles of a gene, e.g. RR or rr |
| Heterozygous | Two different alleles of a gene, e.g. Rr |
| Dominant | An allele expressed in the phenotype when one copy is present (capital letter) |
| Recessive | An allele expressed only when no dominant allele is present (small letter) |

### Genetic diagrams and Punnett squares (17.3.4)

Lay out every cross the same way:

1. Choose a letter; capital for dominant, small for recessive.
2. Write the **parental phenotypes** and **genotypes**.
3. Write the **gametes**: one allele of the gene in each gamete.
4. Combine gametes in a **Punnett square**.
5. List **offspring genotypes**, **phenotypes** and the **ratio**.

### Worked example 3: a 3 : 1 cross

In peas, the allele for round seeds (R) is dominant to the allele for wrinkled seeds (r). Two heterozygous plants are crossed.

```
Parental phenotypes:  round   x   round
Parental genotypes:    Rr     x    Rr
Gametes:             R or r      R or r

          |  R  |  r
      R   | RR  | Rr
      r   | Rr  | rr
```

Offspring genotypes: 1 RR : 2 Rr : 1 rr. RR and Rr are both round, so the phenotypic ratio is **3 round : 1 wrinkled**.

### Worked example 4: a 1 : 1 cross

The same heterozygous plant (Rr) is crossed with a wrinkled plant (rr). Gametes: R or r from the first parent, only r from the second.

```
          |  R  |  r
      r   | Rr  | rr
```

Offspring: 1 Rr : 1 rr, so **1 round : 1 wrinkled**.

### Observed v expected ratios (17.3.5)

Each fertilisation is a **chance** event: which gamete meets which is random. A predicted ratio is what you expect **on average**. With **small numbers of offspring**, chance has a big effect, so the observed ratio often differs from the expected one.

### Worked example 5

The cross in Worked example 3 gave just 8 seeds: 5 round and 3 wrinkled. Expected: 8 × 3/4 = **6 round** and 8 × 1/4 = **2 wrinkled**. The observed ratio is 5 : 3, about **1.67 : 1**, not 3 : 1. The sample is small, and fertilisation is random, so this is not surprising. With 2400 seeds you would expect very close to 1800 round and 600 wrinkled.

### Pure-breeding (17.3.6)

Two **identical homozygous** individuals that breed together will be **pure-breeding**: RR × RR gives only RR; rr × rr gives only rr. Every offspring has the same phenotype as the parents.

### Codominance: ABO blood groups (17.3.7)

In **codominance**, both alleles in a heterozygote are expressed in the phenotype. The ABO gene has three alleles: **Iᴬ**, **Iᴮ** and **Iᵒ**. Iᴬ and Iᴮ are codominant; both are dominant to Iᵒ.

| Genotype | Blood group (phenotype) |
|---|---|
| IᴬIᴬ or IᴬIᵒ | A |
| IᴮIᴮ or IᴮIᵒ | B |
| IᴬIᴮ | AB (both alleles expressed) |
| IᵒIᵒ | O |

### Worked example 6

A man of group AB (IᴬIᴮ) and a woman of group O (IᵒIᵒ) have children. Gametes: Iᴬ or Iᴮ from the man; only Iᵒ from the woman.

```
          |  Iᴬ   |  Iᴮ
     Iᵒ   | IᴬIᵒ  | IᴮIᵒ
```

Children: **1 group A : 1 group B**. None can be AB or O.

### Sex determination (17.3.8)

Human females are **XX** and males are **XY**. Every egg carries an X. Half the sperm carry X and half carry Y.

```
          |  X  |  Y      (sperm)
  egg X   | XX  | XY
```

The ratio is **1 female : 1 male**, so each child has an equal chance of being either sex. The sperm decides the sex.

### Mutation (17.3.9–17.3.12)

- A **gene mutation** is a **random change in the base sequence of DNA**. Example: **sickle cell anaemia**. A change in the base sequence of the gene for haemoglobin changes the amino acid sequence, so the haemoglobin has a different shape. Red blood cells can become sickle-shaped. This links straight back to 17.2.4–17.2.5.
- A **chromosome mutation** is a **change in chromosome number or structure**. Example: **Down's syndrome**, where a person has **47 chromosomes instead of 46**.
- **Sources of genetic variation** in populations: **mutation, meiosis, random mating and random fertilisation**.
- **Ionising radiation** (for example X-rays and gamma rays) and **some chemicals** increase the **rate** of mutation.

## 17.4 Selection

### Natural selection (17.4.1–17.4.2)

1. There is **variation** within a population.
2. Organisms produce **many offspring**, more than the environment can support.
3. There is a **struggle for survival**, including **competition for resources** such as food and mates.
4. Individuals **better adapted** to the environment are more likely to survive and **reproduce**.
5. They **pass on their alleles** to the next generation.

Repeated over many generations, the alleles for the useful feature become more common. In this way the **inherited features of a population evolve over time**.

### Antibiotic-resistant bacteria (17.4.3)

Within a population of bacteria, a few may be resistant to an antibiotic because of a mutation. The antibiotic kills the others; the resistant bacteria survive, reproduce and pass on the resistance allele. Repeated use builds up a **resistant strain**. **MRSA** (methicillin-resistant *Staphylococcus aureus*) is such a strain, resistant to many commonly used antibiotics. Limiting antibiotic use is covered in [Disease and immunity](/resources/o-level-biology-5090-disease-and-immunity/).

### Artificial selection (17.4.4–17.4.5)

**Artificial selection** (selective breeding):

1. Humans **select** animals or plants with **desirable features**.
2. They **cross** these to produce the next generation.
3. They **select the offspring** showing the desirable features.
4. They **repeat** this over **many generations**.

It is used to produce **economically important** plants and animals: crops with high yield or disease resistance, cattle that give more milk, and sheep with more wool.

| | Natural selection | Artificial selection |
|---|---|---|
| Who or what selects | The environment | Humans |
| Features favoured | Those that help survival and reproduction | Those useful to humans |

## Common errors

- Writing "gene" when you mean "allele". A gene for seed shape has two alleles, R and r.
- Putting both alleles in one gamete. A gamete carries **one** allele of each gene.
- Expecting exactly 3 : 1 in a small sample. Ratios are chances, not fixed numbers.
- Pairing A with C or G with T. Only **A–T** and **C–G**.
- Saying the mother decides the sex of a child. Every egg carries X; the sperm decides.
- Writing AB as "IAB" or "incomplete dominance". Use **IᴬIᴮ** and the term **codominance**.
- Saying the antibiotic "causes" the mutation. Resistant bacteria were already there; the antibiotic selects them.
- Calling a continuous-variation feature "caused by genes only".
