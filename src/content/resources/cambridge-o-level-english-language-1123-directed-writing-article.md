This guide covers the **article**, one of the five forms named for **Section A, Directed Writing**, of Cambridge O Level English Language 1123 Paper 2. The syllabus says candidates "use, develop and evaluate the information in the text(s) to create a discursive/argumentative/persuasive speech, email, report, letter or **article**", writing "about 250–350 words". The [directed writing study guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-english-language-1123-directed-writing/) lists the article's features as "headline, engaging opening; developed paragraphs, a viewpoint, a strong ending; suited to the publication's readers". This page shows how to produce each of those, how an article differs from a speech and a report, and how to make source material read like journalism rather than a summary.

## What an article is

An article is written to be **read by a general audience** who have chosen to pick up a publication: a school magazine, a local newspaper, a community newsletter or a website. Unlike a letter or a report, it is not addressed to one decision-maker. Readers can stop at any moment, so an article has to win their attention and keep it.

Compared with the other forms:

| | Speech | Report | Article |
|---|---|---|---|
| Audience | listeners present at an occasion | a named decision-maker | readers of a publication |
| Opening | greeting and hook | title and purpose | headline and hook |
| Register | spoken, direct | impersonal, objective | set by the publication; lively but controlled |
| Structure | signposted for listeners | headings and recommendations | developed paragraphs, a clear line of argument |
| Ending | call to action | recommendations | a memorable final line, often returning to the opening |

## The publication sets the register

**W4**, "use register appropriate to context", depends on where the article will appear. The task will say.

| Publication | Readers | Register |
|---|---|---|
| School magazine | students, some teachers and parents | lively, inclusive, humorous where it fits; standard English, no slang |
| Local newspaper | adults in the community | clear, measured, more formal; balanced where the issue is contested |
| Community or club newsletter | members with a shared interest | friendly, practical |
| Website or online magazine | a wide audience | engaging, concise paragraphs, still accurate |

A school magazine article can say "we" and address readers as "you"; a newspaper article might refer to "residents" and "young people".

## Headlines and hooks

A **headline** tells readers what the article is about and makes them want to read it. It should be short and specific. Techniques include a question, a play on words, alliteration or a striking claim, but clarity matters more than cleverness.

- Weak: "An article about volunteering"
- Better: "Should volunteering be compulsory?"
- Stronger: "Forced to care? The debate over compulsory volunteering"

A **subheading** (a short line under the headline) can add the angle: "Year 10 will soon have to give two hours a week to the community. Not everyone is convinced."

The **hook** is the opening paragraph. Options:

| Hook | Example (original) |
|---|---|
| Scene | "It is 7.30 on a Saturday morning, and fifteen-year-old Bilal is already sorting tins at the food bank." |
| Question | "Can you make someone kind by making it compulsory?" |
| Surprising fact from the sources | "From September, every Year 10 student will owe the community two hours a week." |
| Short quotation from a source | "'It changed how I see my town,' says one volunteer." |

## Building the body

An article needs a **viewpoint**: even a balanced article reaches a judgement. Each paragraph should develop one aspect of the issue:

1. **Topic sentence** that moves the argument on.
2. **Source material**, reworked in your own words, or a short, attributed quotation.
3. **Development**: an example, consequence or application to the readers.
4. **Evaluation**: how convincing is the claim, and who is making it?

Short quotations, clearly attributed, are a natural feature of articles ("as the head teacher put it, 'a habit for life'"). Keep them brief: the reading marks reward your use and evaluation of the material, not copying.

## Endings

A strong ending gives the reader something to remember. Options:

- **Return to the opening** scene or question, now answered.
- **A final judgement** in one short sentence.
- **A challenge** to the readers.

Avoid "In conclusion" (an essay habit) and avoid simply repeating the headline.

## Worked example

Sources (original, shortened):

> **Text 1, from the head teacher's announcement:** From September, all Year 10 students will
> complete two hours of community volunteering each week, at a local charity, care home or
> environmental project. Volunteering builds responsibility and confidence, and universities and
> employers value it. It will become "a habit for life".
>
> **Text 2, from a student's post on the school forum:** I already volunteer at the animal shelter
> because I want to. Making it compulsory turns kindness into homework. Students with part-time jobs
> or caring responsibilities at home will struggle to find two more hours. Charities need committed
> helpers, not people counting the minutes.

**Task:** *Write an article for your school magazine giving your views on compulsory volunteering.*

**Model article (shortened):**

> **Forced to care? The debate over compulsory volunteering**
>
> It is 7.30 on a Saturday morning, and Bilal is already sorting tins at the food bank. Nobody made
> him come. From September, though, every Year 10 student will have to give two hours a week to the
> community, and the school is divided about whether that is a good thing.
>
> The head teacher's case is persuasive. Volunteering builds confidence and responsibility, and
> universities and employers do value it. For students who would never think of walking into a care
> home, being required to go once could open a door they did not know was there.
>
> But the claim that it will become "a habit for life" is a hope rather than a fact, and the
> students it would affect most have a point. Some already work part-time or look after younger
> brothers and sisters; two more hours a week is not a small ask. And charities themselves need
> helpers who want to be there, not students watching the clock.
>
> So perhaps the answer is not compulsion but choice. If the school offered volunteering as one option
> among several, with time set aside in the timetable rather than added on top, more students might
> try it for the right reasons.
>
> Bilal, for one, thinks the scheme could work, as long as students can choose where they go. "You
> can't force someone to care," he says. "But you can give them the chance."

**Commentary.** The headline poses the issue; the hook is a scene that introduces a real person's angle. Each paragraph moves the argument: the case for, the case against with **evaluation** of the head's prediction, then a **developed** compromise (timetabled choice) that neither source proposed. The short, attributed quotations are brief. The ending returns to the opening scene. Register suits a school magazine: lively, inclusive, standard English. A full answer would be a little longer, around 300 words. Note that Bilal is an invented example of the kind of detail an article might use; in the exam, take care that any examples you invent are plausible and consistent with the sources.

## Common mistakes

- **No headline**, or a headline that only labels the topic.
- **A greeting** ("Hello readers"), which belongs to a speech.
- **Essay habits**: "In conclusion", "Firstly, secondly, thirdly".
- **Long quotations** copied from the sources.
- **No viewpoint**: a list of facts with no judgement.
- **Wrong register** for the publication: slang in a newspaper, stiffness in a school magazine.
- **Inventing facts** that contradict the sources.

## Exam technique

Underline the publication and its readers in the task before you read the sources. Draft three possible headlines in your plan and choose the clearest. Decide your viewpoint and your ending first, then plan four or five paragraphs that lead to it. Keep to about 300 words, and finish by checking spelling, punctuation and grammar.
