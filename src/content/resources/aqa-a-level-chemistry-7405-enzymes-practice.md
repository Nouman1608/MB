These questions cover section **3.3.13.3 Enzymes** of the AQA AS and A-level Chemistry specification (7404/7405), for teaching from September 2015. It is A-level only content, examined on Paper 2 and Paper 3. Learn the ideas first in the [Enzymes study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-enzymes/) and the [Enzymes revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-enzymes-revision-notes/). Use Aᵣ values H = 1.0, C = 12.0, N = 14.0, O = 16.0.

## Questions

**1.** State what type of molecule an enzyme is. **[1]**

**2.** Describe how an enzyme acts as a catalyst, using the terms active site and substrate. **[4]**

**3.** Explain why enzymes are specific to a particular reaction. **[2]**

**4.** Explain what is meant by a stereospecific active site. **[2]**

**5.** An enzyme catalyses the reaction of one enantiomer of an amino acid but not the other enantiomer. Explain why. **[3]**

**6.** A drug molecule is an enzyme inhibitor.

**(a)** Explain how it reduces the rate of the reaction the enzyme catalyses. **[3]**

**(b)** Suggest why it is important that the drug has the correct three-dimensional shape. **[1]**

**7.** A chiral drug is made as a racemic mixture, but only one enantiomer fits the active site of the target enzyme.

**(a)** State what is meant by a racemic mixture. **[1]**

**(b)** Suggest why a pharmaceutical company might prefer to make only the active enantiomer. **[2]**

**8.** Describe how computers are used in the design of enzyme inhibitor drugs. **[3]**

**9.** State two advantages of using computers to help design drugs. **[2]**

**10.** State the types of interaction that can hold a substrate in the active site of an enzyme. **[2]**

**11.** 20.0 g of a racemic mixture of a chiral compound (Mᵣ = 100.0) is added to an enzyme that reacts with only one enantiomer.

**(a)** Calculate the mass of the enantiomer that can react. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the maximum amount, in mol, of the enantiomer that can react. **[1]**

**12.** An enzyme can convert 5.0 × 10² molecules of substrate per second at each active site. Calculate the amount of substrate, in mol, converted in 120 s by 3.0 × 10⁻⁹ mol of enzyme, assuming one active site per molecule of enzyme. **[2]**

**13.** Explain how the tertiary structure of a protein is related to the function of an enzyme. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** A protein [1]. **[1]**

**2.** The substrate binds to the active site of the enzyme [1]; forming an enzyme–substrate complex [1]; the enzyme provides an alternative route with a lower activation energy, so the reaction occurs more quickly [1]; the products leave and the enzyme is unchanged and can be used again [1]. **[4]**

**3.** The active site has a specific shape [1]; and specific groups arranged to bond to the substrate, so only a molecule that fits and bonds can react [1]. **[2]**

**4.** The active site is chiral, because it is made of chiral amino acids folded into a specific 3D shape [1]; so it can bind to only one stereoisomer (enantiomer) of a substrate [1]. **[2]**

**5.** The active site has binding groups arranged in a particular 3D pattern [1]; the groups around the chiral carbon of one enantiomer match this pattern [1]; the other enantiomer has the same groups arranged as a mirror image, so they cannot all bond to the active site at the same time [1]. **[3]**

**6. (a)** The drug has a shape and groups similar to the substrate [1]; it binds in the active site and blocks it [1]; so the substrate cannot bind, and the reaction is slower [1]

**(b)** It must fit the stereospecific active site and bond to its groups [1]. **[4]**

**7. (a)** An equal mixture of two enantiomers [1]

**(b)** The other enantiomer may be inactive and so wasteful [1]; it may have unwanted effects (or take up space and cost) [1]. **[3]**

**8.** Computers are used to model the three-dimensional structure of the enzyme's active site [1]; they model candidate drug molecules and predict how well they fit and bind [1]; the best candidates are then made and tested in the laboratory [1]. **[3]**

**9.** Any two from: many molecules can be tested without synthesis; saves time; saves cost; allows the design to be improved; may reduce laboratory or animal testing [2]. **[2]**

**10.** Hydrogen bonding [1]; ionic attractions (and London forces) [1]. **[2]**

**11. (a)** 20.0 ÷ 2 = **10.0 g** [1]

**(b)** 10.0 ÷ 100.0 = **0.100 mol** [1]. **[2]**

**12.** n per second = 3.0 × 10⁻⁹ × 5.0 × 10² = 1.5 × 10⁻⁶ mol s⁻¹ [1]; in 120 s: 1.5 × 10⁻⁶ × 120 = **1.8 × 10⁻⁴ mol** [1]. **[2]**

**13.** The tertiary structure is the overall 3D folding, held by hydrogen bonds and S–S bonds [1]; it forms the active site with a specific shape and arrangement of groups [1]; if the tertiary structure changes, the shape of the active site changes and the substrate no longer fits, so the enzyme stops working [1]. **[3]**

*Examiner insight:* Link each statement to the active site: its shape, its groups, and whether the substrate or drug can bond to it.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing that the enzyme is used up.
- Saying the wrong enantiomer is "the wrong size".
- Writing that an inhibitor destroys the enzyme.
- Not stating that the active site is chiral.
- Forgetting that a computer models and predicts but does not replace laboratory testing.
- Confusing the substrate with the inhibitor.
