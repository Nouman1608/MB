## 1.4.4 Labour productivity

**Labour productivity** measures how much output labour produces. The usual measure is output per labour hour.

Productivity = units produced ÷ labour hours worked

Labour cost per unit = total labour cost ÷ units produced

**Example.** In week 1 a workshop made 4,800 units in 600 labour hours. In week 2 it made 5,400 units in 640 hours. Labour costs 12 an hour.

| | Week 1 | Week 2 |
|---|---:|---:|
| Units | 4,800 | 5,400 |
| Hours | 600 | 640 |
| Units per hour | 8.00 | 8.44 |
| Labour cost (hours × 12) | 7,200 | 7,680 |
| Labour cost per unit | 1.50 | 1.42 |

Productivity rose by (8.4375 − 8) ÷ 8 × 100 = 5.5%, and the labour cost per unit fell from 1.50 to 1.42, even though total labour cost rose by 480. Higher productivity lowers unit cost when the hourly rate stays the same.

Productivity can rise through better training, better machinery, better motivation or better organisation of work. It can fall through poor materials, idle time, machine breakdown or low morale. Do not assume that a rise in total labour cost is bad. It may be caused by more output.

## 1.4.5 Methods of remuneration

### Day work (time rate)

The employee is paid for the hours worked, whatever the output. Overtime is usually paid at a higher rate, such as time and a half (1.5 times the normal rate).

- **Advantages:** simple to calculate; income is predictable for the worker; encourages care over quality.
- **Disadvantages:** no direct reward for effort, so output may be low; needs supervision.

### Piecework

The employee is paid a fixed amount for each unit completed. Many schemes add a **guaranteed minimum** wage so that the employee is not penalised by materials shortages or breakdowns.

- **Advantages:** pay is linked to output, so it motivates workers; labour cost per unit is fixed; less supervision of effort.
- **Disadvantages:** quality may fall; pay is uncertain; workers may resist new methods; it needs good records of output.

### Individual bonus scheme

A basic wage is paid for the time worked. A **bonus** is added when the employee completes the work in less than the **allowed time**. The bonus is a share of the time saved, valued at the hourly rate. In the example below the worker receives 50% of the time saved.

### Group bonus scheme

A bonus is paid for the output or time saved by a team. It is shared equally or in proportion to basic pay. It encourages co-operation, but an individual who works hard may gain little if others do not.

### A worked comparison

A worker is paid 14 an hour for a 40-hour week. A standard rate of 12 units an hour is used to set allowed time. Piecework is paid at 1.20 per unit with a guaranteed minimum of 480 a week. The bonus scheme gives 50% of the value of time saved.

| Output in the week | Allowed time | Time saved | Day work | Piecework | Bonus scheme |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 360 units | 30 hours | 0 | 560 | 480 (guarantee) | 560 |
| 480 units | 40 hours | 0 | 560 | 576 | 560 |
| 600 units | 50 hours | 10 hours | 560 | 720 | 630 |

Workings. Allowed time = output ÷ 12. For 600 units: 600 ÷ 12 = 50 hours, so time saved = 50 − 40 = 10 hours. Bonus = 10 × 14 × 50% = 70, so pay = 560 + 70 = 630. Piecework: 600 × 1.20 = 720; 480 × 1.20 = 576; 360 × 1.20 = 432, which is below the guarantee, so 480 is paid.

Labour cost **per unit** for the same three outputs:

| Output | Day work | Piecework | Bonus scheme |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 360 units | 1.56 | 1.33 | 1.56 |
| 480 units | 1.17 | 1.20 | 1.17 |
| 600 units | 0.93 | 1.20 | 1.05 |

At low output the guarantee makes piecework cost more than the rate suggests (480 ÷ 360 = 1.33). At high output day work gives the lowest cost per unit to the employer (560 ÷ 600 = 0.93) but the worker earns the least. The bonus scheme shares the saving: the employer's cost per unit falls to 1.05 and the worker gains 70.

## 1.4.6 Employee earnings and employer cost

**Gross pay** is the total earned before deductions. **Net pay** is what the employee receives after deductions such as income tax and the employee's pension contribution. The **employer cost** is more than gross pay because the employer also pays its own contributions.

**Example (rates invented for practice).** An employee works 40 hours at 14 and 5 hours of overtime at time and a half.

| | $ |
|---|---:|
| Basic pay 40 × 14 | 560.00 |
| Overtime 5 × 14 × 1.5 = 5 × 21 | 105.00 |
| **Gross pay** | **665.00** |
| Income tax at 20% | (133.00) |
| Employee pension at 4% | (26.60) |
| **Net pay** | **505.40** |

The employer also pays social security at 10% of gross pay (66.50) and a pension contribution of 5% (33.25).

| | $ |
|---|---:|
| Gross pay | 665.00 |
| Employer's social security 10% | 66.50 |
| Employer's pension 5% | 33.25 |
| **Total employer cost** | **764.75** |

The employee's tax and pension are *not* an extra cost to the employer: they are part of gross pay, kept back and paid to the authorities on the employee's behalf. Employer cost = gross pay + the employer's own contributions.

In costing, the part of the pay that relates to production time is **direct labour**. Time spent idle, in training or on supervision is **indirect labour** and goes into overheads.

## Choosing a remuneration method

Match the method to the work:

- **Day work** suits work that is hard to measure or where quality matters more than speed.
- **Piecework** suits repetitive work with easily counted units.
- **Individual bonus** suits work where time standards can be set and effort affects the result.
- **Group bonus** suits work done by a team, where individual output cannot be measured.

An evaluation answer should consider the effect on cost per unit, quality, morale, supervision cost and fairness.

## Common mistakes

- Treating overtime as paid at the normal rate.
- Applying the guaranteed minimum after adding a bonus, or forgetting the guarantee altogether.
- Confusing time taken with time allowed. Time saved is allowed time − actual time.
- Giving the whole value of the time saved to the worker when the scheme shares it.
- Including the employee's deductions in the employer cost as extra expenses.
- Leaving out the employer's own contributions in the employer cost.
- Calculating labour cost per unit by dividing by hours instead of units.
- Saying that a method is "best" without a context.

## Exam technique

- **Calculate** questions need separate lines for basic pay, overtime, bonus, gross pay, deductions and net pay.
- Show the working for time saved before the bonus.
- When **comparing** methods, use numbers for employer and employee, then give a judgement.
- **Explain** a disadvantage with a consequence, such as "piecework may reduce quality because workers rush, so the business faces more rejects and higher costs".
