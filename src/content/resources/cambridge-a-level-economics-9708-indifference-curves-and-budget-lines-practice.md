## Section A

**1.** Define a budget line. **[2]**

**2.** An indifference curve is usually drawn convex to the origin because:

A: the consumer's income is fixed

B: the marginal rate of substitution diminishes along the curve

C: the prices of both goods are constant

D: one of the goods is inferior **[1]**

**3.** A consumer has an income of 90. Good X costs 6 and good Y costs 3. Calculate the intercepts and the slope of the budget line. **[2]**

**4.** Describe the effect on a budget line of each change.

**(a)** A rise in the consumer's income, with prices unchanged. **[1]**

**(b)** A fall in the price of good Y, with income and the price of X unchanged. **[1]**

**5.** Define the income effect of a price change. **[2]**

**6.** After a fall in the price of good X, the substitution effect raises the quantity demanded by 4 units and the income effect lowers it by 6 units. Good X is:

A: a normal good

B: an inferior good that is not a Giffen good

C: a Giffen good

D: a good with perfectly inelastic demand **[1]**

## Section B

**7.** A consumer has an income of 120. The price of X is 6 and the price of Y is 4.

**(a)** Calculate the intercepts and the slope of the budget line. **[2]**

**(b)** The price of Y rises to 6. Calculate the new intercepts. **[1]**

**(c)** Starting again from the original prices, income and both prices all rise by 20%. Explain the effect on the budget line and on the consumer's choice. **[2]**

**8.** The price of good X falls from 5 to 4. At the original optimum the consumer buys 12 units. At the compensated point (new prices, original indifference curve) they would buy 14 units. At the new optimum they buy 17 units.

**(a)** Calculate the substitution effect, the income effect and the price effect. **[3]**

**(b)** State whether X is a normal, inferior or Giffen good, and explain your answer. **[2]**

**9.** With the aid of an indifference curve diagram, explain the income and substitution effects of a rise in the price of an inferior good that is not a Giffen good. **[6]**

**10.** Explain why a consumer at a point where the budget line cuts an indifference curve is not maximising satisfaction. **[3]**

**11.** Assess whether indifference curve analysis is more useful than marginal utility theory in explaining consumer behaviour. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** A line showing all combinations of two goods that a consumer can just afford [1] with a given income and given prices [1].

**2.** **B** [1]. As the consumer has more X, they are willing to give up less Y for each extra unit of X.

**3.** Intercepts: 90 ÷ 6 = **15 X** and 90 ÷ 3 = **30 Y** [1]. Slope = 6 ÷ 3 = **2**, ignoring the sign [1].

**4.** **(a)** A parallel shift outward, with the slope unchanged [1].

**(b)** The line pivots outward around the X intercept, so the Y intercept rises [1].

**5.** The change in the quantity demanded of a good [1] caused by the change in the consumer's real income (purchasing power) that results from the price change [1].

**6.** **C** [1]. The income effect works against the substitution effect, so the good is inferior, and it is larger, so quantity falls when price falls.

**7.** **(a)** 120 ÷ 6 = **20 X** and 120 ÷ 4 = **30 Y** [1]; slope = 6 ÷ 4 = **1.5** [1].

**(b)** 120 ÷ 6 = **20 X** and 120 ÷ 6 = **20 Y** [1].

**(c)** New income 144 with prices 7.2 and 4.8 gives intercepts of 20 X and 30 Y, so the budget line does not move [1]. Real income and relative prices are unchanged, so the consumer's optimum combination stays the same [1].

**8.** **(a)** Substitution effect = 14 − 12 = **+2 units** [1]. Income effect = 17 − 14 = **+3 units** [1]. Price effect = 17 − 12 = **+5 units** [1].

**(b)** X is a **normal good** [1], because the income effect of the rise in real income increases the quantity demanded, working in the same direction as the substitution effect [1].

**9.** Diagram: original budget line BL1 tangent to IC1 at A; after the price rise, BL2 pivots inward on the Y axis and is tangent to a lower curve IC2 at C; a compensated line BLc parallel to BL2 is tangent to IC1 at B [1].

Substitution effect: X is now relatively dearer, so moving along IC1 from A to B reduces the quantity of X [1]; it always works against the price change [1].

Income effect: the price rise lowers real income [1]; because the good is inferior, lower real income increases the quantity demanded, so the move from B to C raises the quantity of X [1].

Because the good is not a Giffen good, the income effect is smaller than the substitution effect, so C lies to the left of A and the overall quantity demanded falls [1].

**10.** Where the budget line cuts the curve, the MRS differs from the price ratio [1], so the rate at which the consumer is willing to swap the goods differs from the rate at which the market lets them swap [1]. By moving along the budget line towards the tangency, the consumer can reach a higher indifference curve with the same income [1].

**11.** For indifference curve analysis: it needs consumers only to rank bundles, not to measure utility in units, which is more realistic [1]; it separates the income and substitution effects, explaining inferior and Giffen goods [1]; it shows how changes in income and in each price shift the budget line [1].

Against, or limits shared with utility theory: both assume rational, informed consumers with stable preferences [1]; indifference curves cannot be observed, so the model is hard to test [1]; it shows only two goods [1]; marginal utility theory gives a simpler explanation of a downward-sloping demand curve that is often enough [1].

Judgement: indifference curve analysis is more useful for explaining why demand responds to price in different ways for different goods, though both models share unrealistic assumptions about rational behaviour [1].
