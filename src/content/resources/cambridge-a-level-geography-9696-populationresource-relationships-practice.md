## Section A

**1.** Define the term "food security". **[2]**

**2.** Define the term "carrying capacity". **[2]**

**3.** Distinguish between overpopulation and underpopulation. **[2]**

**4.** Explain why a country can be food secure at national level while some of its households are not. **[2]**

**5.** Explain how war can cause food shortages. **[3]**

**6.** State **two** consequences of food shortages for young children. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** A fictional region has 250 000 hectares of cropland, with average grain yields of 3.2 tonnes per hectare per year. Each person needs about 0.2 tonnes of grain a year.

**(a)** Calculate the carrying capacity of the region. Show your working. **[2]**

**(b)** The region's population is 4.6 million. Calculate the annual grain shortfall in tonnes. Show your working. **[2]**

**(c)** Suggest **two** ways the region could close the shortfall. **[2]**

**8.** The table compares two fictional countries.

| | Country X | Country Y |
|---|---|---|
| Population density (people per km²) | 450 | 60 |
| Income level | High | Low |
| Food imports (% of consumption) | 60 | 15 |
| Undernourished population (%) | Under 2.5 | 30 |

Using the data, explain why population density is not a reliable indicator of overpopulation. **[4]**

**9.** Evaluate the role of the Green Revolution in improving food security. **[6]**

**10.** Explain how climatic hazards limit the population an area can sustain. **[4]**

**11.** Explain how output per head changes as population grows, with reference to the concept of optimum population. **[5]**

**12.** "Technology will always allow food production to keep pace with population growth." Discuss this view. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** A situation in which all people, at all times, have physical and economic access to sufficient, safe and nutritious food [1] to meet their dietary needs for an active and healthy life [1]. **[2]**

**2.** The maximum population an area can support [1] sustainably with its resources and technology, without degrading the environment [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Overpopulation is when there are too many people for the resources and technology available, so living standards are lower than they could be [1]. Underpopulation is when there are too few people to use the resources fully, so a larger population could raise living standards [1]. **[2]**

**4.** National food supply may be enough in total [1], but poor households may be unable to afford food or reach markets, so they lack access [1]. **[2]**

**5.** Fighting destroys crops, livestock, stores and infrastructure such as roads and irrigation [1]. Farmers are displaced or conscripted, so land is not planted [1]. Sieges and blockades cut off trade and food aid, so local shortages become famine [1]. **[3]**

**6.** Two from: stunted growth [1]; weakened immunity and greater risk of disease and death [1]. Accept impaired brain development or lower school attendance in place of either. **[2]**

**7. (a)** Production = 250 000 x 3.2 = 800 000 tonnes [1]; capacity = 800 000 / 0.2 = 4.0 million people [1].

**(b)** Requirement = 4 600 000 x 0.2 = 920 000 tonnes [1]; shortfall = 920 000 - 800 000 = 120 000 tonnes [1].

**(c)** Two from: raise yields with irrigation, improved seed or fertiliser [1]; import grain paid for by exports [1]. Accept reducing post-harvest losses or bringing new land into cultivation in place of either. **[6]**

**8.** Country X has 7.5 times the density of Y but almost no undernourishment [1], because its high income lets it import 60% of its food [1]. Country Y is sparsely populated, yet 30% of its people are undernourished, because low incomes and limited technology restrict both production and imports [1]. So overpopulation depends on the balance between people, resources, technology and trade, not on density [1]. **[4]**

**9.** High-yielding varieties of wheat and rice, with irrigation and fertilisers, greatly raised yields in parts of Asia and Latin America from the 1960s [1], increasing food availability and helping prevent famine as populations grew [1]. However, the benefits went mainly to farmers who could afford seeds, fertiliser and water, widening rural inequality [1]. Heavy irrigation lowered water tables and caused salinisation in places [1], and fertiliser and pesticide use polluted water [1]. Overall it improved availability greatly but did less for access and sustainability, so its contribution to food security was large but incomplete [1]. **[6]**

**10.** Droughts reduce harvests and kill livestock, so the food an area produces falls [1]. Floods and tropical cyclones destroy crops and stores and damage soils and infrastructure [1]. Repeated hazards erode savings and assets, so households cannot recover between events [1]. The population that can be supported at a given standard of living is therefore lower and less stable than average conditions suggest [1]. **[4]**

**11.** At low population, there are too few workers to use resources fully, so output per head is low [1]. As population grows, specialisation and fuller use of resources raise output per head [1]. Output per head reaches a maximum at the optimum population [1]. Beyond this, each extra person adds less to output than they consume [1], so output per head falls and the area becomes overpopulated [1]. **[5]**

**12.** For the view: global food production has so far grown faster than population [1]. The Green Revolution and later advances in seed, irrigation and mechanisation raised yields greatly [1]. Boserup argued that population pressure itself drives innovation [1]. New technologies, such as genetically modified crops and controlled-environment farming, may raise output further [1].

Against the view: yield growth has slowed in some regions, and degradation of soil and water limits further gains [1]. Climate change brings more frequent droughts, floods and heat stress that reduce yields [1]. Technology raises availability but does not ensure access for the poor, so shortages persist [1]. War and other constraints can override technological gains [1].

Judgement: technology has kept global production ahead of population so far [1], but "always" is too strong, because environmental limits, climate change and unequal access mean technology alone cannot guarantee food security [1]. **[10]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Defining food security without the idea of access.
- Mixing up tonnes and people in carrying capacity calculations.
- Describing data on two countries separately without drawing the comparison.
- Evaluating the Green Revolution with only benefits or only costs.
- Agreeing with an "always" statement without challenging its absolute wording.
