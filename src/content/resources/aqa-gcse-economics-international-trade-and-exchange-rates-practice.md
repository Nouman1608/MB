## Questions

**1.** Explain **two** benefits to the UK of international trade. **[4]**

**2.** State **one** type of service and **one** type of good that the UK exports. **[2]**

**3.** Explain what is meant by global interdependence and give **one** risk it creates for the UK. **[3]**

**4.** Define an exchange rate. **[1]**

**5.** Explain how an increase in demand for UK exports affects the value of the pound. **[3]**

**6.** Explain why a rise in UK interest rates might cause the pound to appreciate. **[3]**

**7.** The exchange rate changes from £1 = €1.10 to £1 = €1.25.
**(a)** A UK-made bicycle costs £400. Calculate its price in euros before and after the change. **[2]**
**(b)** A French wine costs €20. Calculate its price in pounds after the change, to the nearest penny. **[1]**
**(c)** State whether the pound has appreciated or depreciated. **[1]**

**8.** Explain how a depreciation of the pound could affect a UK firm that imports most of its raw materials. **[3]**

**9.** Explain how a depreciation of the pound could affect UK consumers. **[3]**

**10.** Explain why a recession in the UK's main trading partners could increase unemployment in the UK. **[3]**

**11.** A UK car maker exports half its output and imports many of its parts. Evaluate whether a fall in the value of the pound would benefit the firm. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** Any two developed: lower prices [1] because competition from imports pushes UK firms to keep prices down [1]; more choice [1] as consumers can buy goods not made in the UK [1]; larger markets for UK firms [1], so they can sell more and create jobs [1]. **[4]**

**2.** Service: e.g. financial services, insurance, education or tourism [1]. Good: e.g. cars, machinery or medicines [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Economies rely on one another for goods, services, markets and investment [1]. Risk, developed: a fall in demand abroad reduces UK exports [1], so UK firms cut output and jobs [1]. **[3]**

**4.** The price of one currency in terms of another [1]. **[1]**

**5.** Foreign buyers need pounds to pay for UK goods [1], so demand for pounds rises [1], causing the pound to appreciate [1]. **[3]**

**6.** Saving in UK banks earns a higher return [1], so foreign savers buy pounds to deposit [1]; demand for pounds rises and the pound appreciates [1]. **[3]**

**7. (a)** Before: 400 × 1.10 = **€440** [1]; after: 400 × 1.25 = **€500** [1].
**(b)** 20 ÷ 1.25 = **£16.00** [1].
**(c)** Appreciated [1]. **[4]**

**8.** Each pound buys less foreign currency, so imported materials cost more in pounds [1]; the firm's costs rise [1], so it must either raise prices, possibly losing sales, or accept lower profit [1]. **[3]**

**9.** Imports become dearer [1], so consumers pay more for imported goods and foreign holidays [1]; if firms pass on higher import costs, the general price level may rise, reducing real incomes [1]. **[3]**

**10.** Incomes fall in those countries, so they buy fewer UK exports [1]; UK exporters produce less [1] and need fewer workers, so unemployment rises [1]. **[3]**

**11.** *Benefits:*
- Its exports become cheaper abroad [1], so foreign sales may rise [1], raising revenue and possibly profit [1].
- Half of output is exported, so the gain on that side is large [1].

*Costs:*
- Imported parts cost more in pounds [1], raising costs [1].
- If buyers abroad do not respond much to lower prices, the extra sales may be small [1].

*Judgement:* Whether the firm gains depends on the balance between higher export sales and higher parts costs; with half of output exported it may gain overall, but only if imported parts are a smaller share of its costs [1] [1]. **[9]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Reversing the effects of an appreciation and a depreciation.
- Multiplying instead of dividing when converting a foreign price into pounds.
- Not finishing the chain: "the pound falls" without explaining what happens to export prices or sales.
- Ignoring that a firm can be both an exporter and an importer.

## Frequently asked questions

**How do you convert a foreign price into pounds?**
Divide the foreign price by the exchange rate. Going from pounds to a foreign currency, multiply; going from a foreign currency to pounds, divide.

**What is a common mistake with appreciation and depreciation?**
Reversing their effects. A strong pound makes imports cheaper and exports dearer; a weak pound does the opposite.

**How far should I take an exchange rate chain of reasoning?**
Finish the chain: say what happens to export or import prices and then to sales, rather than stopping at the pound falls.

**Can a firm be affected by the exchange rate as both an exporter and an importer?**
Yes. A firm that exports and also buys imported materials gains on one side and loses on the other when the exchange rate changes, so consider both.
