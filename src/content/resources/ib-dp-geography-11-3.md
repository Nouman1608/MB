## What this sub-topic asks you to do

- Explain how human factors, including political decisions, culture and technology, influence global interactions.
- Explain how physical factors, including location, relief, climate and hazards, influence them.
- Examine the interaction between human and physical factors.
- Evaluate how far physical geography still matters in a world of time-space compression.

## The cost of distance: a worked example

Being landlocked or remote raises the cost of every unit traded. Figures are invented.

```
A machine part costs 100 dollars at a factory overseas.

Coastal Country C
  Sea freight to its port               5 dollars
  Cost before tariff                    105 dollars
  Tariff of 10%                         10.50 dollars
  Landed cost                           115.50 dollars

Landlocked Country L
  Sea freight to a neighbour's port     5 dollars
  Overland transport and transit fees  10 dollars
  Cost before tariff                    115 dollars
  Tariff of 10%                         11.50 dollars
  Landed cost                           126.50 dollars

Difference: 126.50 - 115.50 = 11 dollars, or 11 / 115.50 x 100 = 9.5% more
```

Two lessons follow. First, a physical disadvantage becomes a cost through human arrangements: transit fees, border delays and the quality of a neighbour's roads and railways. Second, because the tariff is charged on a value that already includes transport, the extra cost is multiplied. If a trade agreement removed the tariff, the landed costs would become 105 and 115 dollars, narrowing the gap to 10 dollars. Human decisions can therefore reduce, though not remove, the effect of a physical constraint.

## Physical events that break networks

Global networks are efficient but tightly linked, so a physical event in one place can disrupt flows worldwide.

- **Volcanic ash, Iceland, 2010.** The eruption of Eyjafjallajökull sent ash into the atmosphere over Europe, and much of European airspace was closed for several days in April 2010. Millions of passengers were stranded and air freight was disrupted far beyond Europe.
- **Floods, Thailand, 2011.** Severe flooding inundated industrial estates north of Bangkok, which held a large share of the world's production of hard-disk drives and many components for car makers. Shortages and higher prices spread through global electronics and vehicle supply chains.
- **Earthquake and tsunami, Japan, 2011.** The Tohoku disaster closed component plants and disrupted car and electronics production in other countries that depended on Japanese parts.

These cases show that physical geography still matters even where technology has reduced the friction of distance: concentration of production in hazard-exposed places turns a local event into a global disruption.

## Climate and the location of digital infrastructure

It is tempting to say that the internet makes physical location irrelevant. In fact, the data centres that store and process information use large amounts of electricity, much of it for cooling. Some technology companies have built data centres in cool northern locations with plentiful renewable electricity: Facebook opened a large data centre at Luleå in northern Sweden in 2013, citing the cold climate and hydroelectric power. Physical factors therefore influence the geography of digital flows, through climate, energy supply and the routes of submarine cables.

## Seasonality and access

Physical conditions can limit access for part of the year. Many ports in northern Russia freeze in winter, but Murmansk, warmed by the North Atlantic Drift, remains ice-free, which made it strategically important. Monsoon rains can cut roads in parts of South Asia, while low rainfall can restrict canal and river traffic, as on the Rhine during dry summers, when barges must carry lighter loads. Seasonality is a physical influence that answers often overlook.

## Language, time zones and services

Flows of services respond to influences that are partly human and partly physical. India's information technology and business-process services grew rapidly from the 1990s, serving clients in the USA and the UK. Widespread English among graduates, a human influence rooted partly in colonial history, lowered the cost of communication. Position on the globe also mattered: the time difference allowed work handed over at the end of a Western working day to be continued overnight, so firms could offer round-the-clock service. Fibre-optic connections made the arrangement possible, showing how technology, language and location combine.

## Trade creation and trade diversion

A trading bloc lowers barriers between members, but its effects depend on where trade would otherwise have come from.

| Effect | Meaning | Who gains or loses |
|---|---|---|
| Trade creation | Members now buy from a lower-cost member instead of producing at higher cost at home | Consumers gain; inefficient domestic producers lose |
| Trade diversion | Members switch from a cheaper non-member to a more expensive member because the member faces no tariff | Non-member exporters lose; the bloc may become less efficient overall |

Using these terms lets you evaluate a bloc's influence on interactions rather than simply stating that it "increases trade".

## How human and physical factors interact

Few influences act alone. A useful structure for an extended answer is to show how human action modifies physical constraints:

1. **Physical constraint:** a mountain barrier, a coastline lacking natural harbours, a hazard-prone location.
2. **Human response:** a tunnel or railway, a dredged port, building codes and supply-chain diversification.
3. **Outcome:** the constraint is reduced, removed or remains, depending on investment, technology and politics.

Switzerland's Alpine rail tunnels and Singapore's land reclamation show physical limits overcome by investment. Landlocked states whose neighbours restrict transit show physical disadvantage made worse by politics.

## Annotated model answer

*To what extent has time-space compression reduced the importance of physical influences on global interactions?* **(sample paragraph)**

"For information and finance, physical distance now matters little: data and payments move between continents almost instantly **[evidence for the view]**. For bulky goods, however, transport costs still rise with distance and terrain, so landlocked states face higher landed costs than coastal ones **[counter-evidence by type of flow]**. Physical events also still disrupt networks, as the closure of European airspace after the 2010 Icelandic eruption showed **[a second limit]**. Time-space compression has therefore reduced physical influences very unevenly, most for weightless flows and least for goods **[judgement differentiated by flow]**."

## Common mistakes

- Treating physical factors as deciding outcomes without human mediation.
- Assuming digital flows have no physical geography.
- Saying a trading bloc always increases total trade, ignoring diversion.
- Forgetting that tariffs charged on value including transport magnify distance costs.
- Using only one scale, when influences may act differently locally and globally.

## Exam technique

- **Examine** the relative importance of human and physical factors by showing how they interact.
- **To what extent** answers should differentiate between types of flow: goods, people, money and data.
- **Discuss** trading blocs from the viewpoint of members, non-members and consumers.
- **Explain** a physical disruption as a chain from event to supply chain to wider impact.
- In calculations, show every step and comment on what the result means for interaction.
