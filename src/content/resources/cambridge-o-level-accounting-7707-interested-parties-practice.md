Revise first with the [interested parties study guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-accounting-7707-interested-parties/) and the [interested parties revision notes](/resources/cambridge-o-level-accounting-7707-interested-parties-revision-notes/). All organisations and figures are invented, and amounts are in dollars.

## Section A: Short-answer questions

**1.** State **two** interested parties outside a business who use its accounting information. **[2]**

**2.** Explain **one** decision a supplier makes using a customer's accounting information. **[2]**

**3.** Which party would be most interested in the surplus or deficit shown in an income and expenditure account?

A: shareholders

B: club members

C: a tax authority

D: suppliers

**[1]**

**4.** Explain why a bank would be interested in a business's acid test ratio. **[2]**

**5.** State **one** decision managers make using accounting information. **[1]**

**6.** Explain why employees are interested in the accounts of the business they work for. **[2]**

## Section B: Structured questions

**7.** Thornbury Engineering Ltd provides the following information.

| | 2027 | 2028 |
|---|---|---|
| Profit for the year | $84,000 | $96,000 |
| ROCE | 12.00% | 15.00% |
| Current ratio | 1.60 : 1 | 1.10 : 1 |
| Acid test ratio | 0.90 : 1 | 0.50 : 1 |
| Trade payables turnover | 40 days | 68 days |
| Ordinary dividends paid | $20,000 | $60,000 |

Suppliers allow Thornbury 45 days' credit.

**(a)** Explain how a person considering buying shares in Thornbury would use this information. **[4]**

**(b)** Explain how a supplier would use this information. **[3]**

**(c)** Explain the conflict of interest between Thornbury's shareholders and its lenders shown by these figures. **[3]**

**8.** Sita owns a shop and has asked her bank for a loan of $20,000 to refit it. Her latest accounts show a profit for the year of $18,000 after interest of $1,200 on an existing loan of $15,000. Her current ratio is 2.20 : 1 and her acid test ratio is 1.10 : 1. She owns her premises, which are recorded at $120,000.

**(a)** Identify **three** items of information the bank would use, and explain why each matters. **[6]**

**(b)** Advise the bank whether to grant the loan. **[2]**

**9.** Riverside Rowing Club's income and expenditure account for 2028 shows subscriptions of $12,800, a surplus on the regatta of $2,100 and other expenditure of $17,300. The committee asks members to approve a 20% rise in subscriptions.

**(a)** Calculate the club's surplus or deficit for 2028. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the subscription income after a 20% rise and the resulting surplus or deficit if all other figures stay the same. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain **two** other pieces of information members would want before voting. **[4]**

**10.** Tremont Mills Ltd is the largest employer in a small town.

**(a)** Explain how the tax authority uses Tremont's accounts. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain **one** other way the government or town council might use Tremont's accounts. **[2]**

**(c)** State **one** item a local environmental group might look for in Tremont's accounts. **[1]**

**11.** A hospital plans to buy scanning equipment from a supplier that will also service it for five years. Explain why the hospital would look at the supplier's accounts, and which information it would use. **[4]**

**12.** A shop owner says, "My managers do not need financial statements, because they run the business every day." Discuss this view. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** Any two: banks [1]; investors and lenders [1]; suppliers; customers; governments or tax authorities; public or environmental bodies. **[2]**

*Examiner insight:* Managers and owners are inside the business; club members are inside a club.

**2.** Whether to give the business credit, and how much [1], judged from how long the business takes to pay its suppliers (trade payables turnover) and its liquidity, because slow payment risks the supplier not being paid on time [1]. **[2]**

**3.** B [1]. **[1]**

**4.** The acid test ratio shows whether liquid assets cover current liabilities without selling inventory [1], so the bank can judge whether the business is likely to pay interest and repay an overdraft or loan on time [1]. **[2]**

**5.** Any one: setting selling prices; controlling expenses; deciding how much inventory to hold; deciding credit terms for customers; planning expansion [1]. **[1]**

**6.** Accounts show whether the business is profitable and has enough cash [1], which tells employees whether their jobs are secure and whether a pay rise may be affordable [1]. **[2]**

**7. (a)** ROCE rose from 12% to 15%, so the company earns a better return on the capital used [1], which makes the shares more attractive [1]. Dividends rose from $20,000 to $60,000, so shareholders receive more income [1]. However, liquidity has fallen sharply (acid test 0.50 : 1), which increases the risk that the company runs short of cash, so the investor must weigh return against risk [1].

**(b)** Thornbury now takes 68 days to pay against 45 days allowed [1], and its acid test ratio has fallen to 0.50 : 1 [1], so the supplier may reduce Thornbury's credit limit, ask for earlier payment or refuse further credit [1].

**(c)** Shareholders benefit from the higher dividend of $60,000 [1], but paying it out used cash at a time when liquidity was falling [1]. Lenders would prefer the cash to stay in the company so that interest and loan repayments can be met [1]. **[10]**

**8. (a)** Any three, each explained: profit for the year of $18,000 [1], which shows Sita can afford interest on a larger loan [1]; the acid test ratio of 1.10 : 1 [1], which shows she can meet current liabilities from liquid assets [1]; the premises at $120,000 [1], which could be offered as security [1]; existing borrowing of $15,000 and interest of $1,200, which show how much she already owes; the current ratio of 2.20 : 1, which shows healthy working capital; the trend of profits over several years.

**(b)** The bank should grant the loan [1], because profit comfortably covers the existing interest, liquidity is sound and the premises provide security (also credit a reasoned case for asking for a forecast of extra sales from the refit first) [1]. **[8]**

**9. (a)** 12,800 + 2,100 - 17,300 = **deficit of $2,400** [1].

**(b)** Subscriptions = 12,800 x 1.2 = $15,360 [1]. Result = 15,360 + 2,100 - 17,300 = **surplus of $160** [1].

**(c)** Any two, each explained: the accumulated fund and bank balance [1], to see whether reserves can cover a deficit without a rise [1]; the breakdown of expenditure [1], to see whether any costs, such as boat repairs, could be cut instead [1]; whether membership numbers might fall if fees rise, which would reduce the expected income; previous years' results, to see whether the deficit is a one-off. **[7]**

**10. (a)** It uses the profit shown to calculate or check the tax the company must pay [1], and checks that income has not been left out of the records [1].

**(b)** Any one, explained: to judge whether the company is likely to continue [1], so that local jobs and the town's economy are secure [1]; for national statistics on output and wages, which help in setting policy.

**(c)** Any one: spending on pollution control or waste disposal; environmental fines; plans to close or expand the mill [1]. **[5]**

**11.** The hospital needs the supplier to keep trading for five years to provide servicing and spare parts [1]. It would look at the supplier's profit trend [1] and its liquidity ratios [1], because a business making losses or short of cash may close and leave the equipment without support [1]. **[4]**

**12.** For the owner's view: managers see daily records such as sales, inventory and costs, so they know more detail than the annual statements show [1]. Against: statements bring all the figures together, so managers can see overall profit and the financial position [1]. Ratios from the statements help managers compare with last year and with other businesses [1]. They show where action is needed, such as rising receivables days or falling margins [1]. Managers also use them to plan, for example setting budgets and deciding whether to expand [1]. Conclusion: managers do need financial statements, alongside their detailed records, to make informed decisions [1]. **[6]**
