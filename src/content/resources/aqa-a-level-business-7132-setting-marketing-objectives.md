This guide goes deeper into section **3.3.1 Setting marketing objectives** of the AQA AS and A-level Business specification (7132, for first teaching from September 2023). The specification asks you to know:

- **the value of setting marketing objectives**
- the main kinds of marketing objective: **sales volume and sales value, market size, market and sales growth, market share and brand loyalty**.

Throughout section 3.3 you should also consider technology (for example digital marketing and social media), ethical and environmental influences, market conditions and competition, how marketing decisions improve competitiveness, and links with other functions. The wider [marketing management study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-marketing-management/) covers the whole topic. This page concentrates on one question: what is a marketing objective, and how does a business choose and judge one? Companion pages: [revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-setting-marketing-objectives-revision-notes/) and [practice questions](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-setting-marketing-objectives-practice/). All businesses and figures are invented.

## What a marketing objective is

A **marketing objective** is a specific target for the marketing function to achieve in a stated period. It sits below the business's corporate objectives and turns them into marketing terms. If the corporate objective is "grow profit by 20%", the marketing objective might be "increase sales volume by 15% within a year".

Marketing objectives are different from marketing strategies and tactics:

- the **objective** says *where* the business wants to be (for example, a 20% market share)
- the **strategy** says *how* it will get there (for example, target a new segment)
- the **tactics** are the day-to-day actions (for example, a social media campaign in March).

Students often confuse the three. If an answer begins "the business should lower its price", that is a tactic, not an objective.

## The value of setting marketing objectives

Objectives are valuable because they:

1. **Give direction.** Everyone in marketing, and in other functions, knows what they are working towards.
2. **Allow decisions to be judged.** A business that aims for 12% market share can check whether a campaign worked. Without a target there is nothing to compare results against.
3. **Support motivation and accountability.** A clear target can be shared with a team and used in appraisal.
4. **Coordinate functions.** A volume target tells operations how much to produce, tells finance how much working capital is needed and tells HR how many staff to plan for.
5. **Help allocate scarce resources.** A limited budget is spent on activities that serve the objective.

There are also limits. A target set without good market research may be unrealistic. A target that is too narrow can distort behaviour: a team chasing sales volume may cut prices so far that profit falls. Objectives set for one year may also encourage short-term thinking, and external events such as a recession can make them unachievable.

A well-set objective is often described as **SMART**: specific, measurable, achievable, realistic and time-bound. "Sell more" is weak. "Raise sales volume by 8% in the next 12 months" is strong.

## The main marketing objectives

### Sales volume and sales value

**Sales volume** is the number of units sold. **Sales value** (revenue) is the money received: volume multiplied by average selling price.

The two can move in different directions. A discount campaign may raise volume while sales value stays flat or falls. A premium product may sell fewer units but earn more revenue. Choosing between them is a real decision:

- Volume suits a business trying to fill spare capacity, gain scale economies or build a customer base.
- Value suits a business that wants revenue growth without extra production, or one selling a premium brand.

### Market size

**Market size** is the total sales of all firms in the market. It can be measured by volume or by value. A business may set an objective to *grow the market itself*, for example by persuading non-users to try the product. This is most common when a firm already holds a large share, because then most of the benefit of a bigger market comes to it.

### Market growth and sales growth

**Market growth** shows how fast the market is expanding:

> market growth (%) = (change in market size ÷ original market size) × 100

**Sales growth** is the same calculation for one firm's sales. Comparing the two is revealing. If a market grows 10% and a firm's sales grow 17%, the firm is winning customers from rivals. If the firm's sales grow 6% in the same market, it is growing but losing ground.

### Market share

**Market share** is the proportion of total market sales that one firm holds:

> market share (%) = (firm's sales ÷ total market sales) × 100

Use sales of the same kind in both parts of the fraction, either both by value or both by volume. A high share can bring buying power over suppliers, brand strength and economies of scale. But market share is a relative measure, so a firm can grow its sales and still see its share fall.

### Brand loyalty

**Brand loyalty** is the extent to which customers keep buying a brand rather than switching. It can be shown by repeat purchase rates, membership of loyalty schemes, or survey measures of customers who would recommend the brand. High loyalty means more stable revenue, less need to spend on winning new customers, and some freedom on price. Loyalty is built over years, so it suits objectives for long-run stability rather than a quick sales boost.

## Worked example: Tidewell Bikes

Tidewell Bikes sells electric bicycles at an average price of £500 in a market worth £80 million in 2025.

| | 2025 | 2026 |
|---|---|---|
| Market size (value) | £80m | £88m |
| Tidewell sales value | £12.0m | £14.08m |
| Tidewell sales volume | 24,000 | 28,160 |
| Survey of 4,000 customers: repeat buyers | 1,200 | 1,440 |

**Step 1: market growth.** (88 − 80) ÷ 80 × 100 = **10%**.

**Step 2: sales growth.** (14.08 − 12.0) ÷ 12.0 × 100 = **17.3%** (to one decimal place).

**Step 3: market share.**

- 2025: 12.0 ÷ 80 × 100 = **15%**
- 2026: 14.08 ÷ 88 × 100 = **16%**

**Step 4: volume check.** £14.08m ÷ £500 = 28,160 bikes, so the price stayed the same and the growth came entirely from selling more units.

**Step 5: loyalty.** Repeat buyers rose from 1,200 ÷ 4,000 = **30%** to 1,440 ÷ 4,000 = **36%**.

**Analysis.** Tidewell grew faster than its market (17.3% against 10%) and its share rose by one percentage point. Notice the wording: share rose by *one percentage point*, from 15% to 16%, which is a rise of about 6.7% in proportion. In an exam, say "percentage points" when you compare two percentages. The higher repeat rate suggests the growth is not just from discounting, which would normally bring in one-off buyers. A sensible next objective might be a 17% share in 2027, with the market assumed to grow by a further 8%.

## Choosing objectives: influences

The best objective for a business depends on:

- **Corporate objectives.** A business aiming for survival may prioritise sales value and cash. One aiming for dominance may prioritise share.
- **Stage of the product life cycle.** Launch often favours awareness and sales volume. A mature product often favours share defence and loyalty.
- **Market conditions.** In a fast-growing market, sales growth is easy but share may still fall. In a shrinking market, holding share may be the realistic goal.
- **Competition.** A strong rival may make a share target harder.
- **Resources and capacity.** There is no point setting a volume objective operations cannot supply.
- **Ethical and environmental influences.** A business that promotes sustainability may set objectives around responsible products rather than sheer volume, and objectives that depend on aggressive selling to vulnerable customers carry reputational risk.
- **Technology.** Digital data lets firms measure loyalty, conversion and sales growth by customer segment far more precisely than in the past.

## Objectives in conflict

Objectives can clash. Volume targets may push a business to cut prices, which reduces sales value or loyalty to a premium brand. A market share target may require heavy promotion that reduces profit in the short run. Good answers recognise that objectives need to be consistent with each other and with the objectives of finance and operations.

## Technique for exam questions

- **Define**: give a precise meaning, ideally with the formula.
- **Calculate**: write the formula, show working, and give the unit (% or £m).
- **Analyse**: build a chain, for example "market share rose, so the firm has more buying power with suppliers, which may reduce unit costs".
- **Evaluate**: weigh the benefits of the objective against its limits, and make a judgement based on the business's situation, for example its life cycle stage or capacity.

## Common mistakes

- Confusing objectives with strategies or tactics.
- Using different bases for market share, such as the firm's volume divided by market value.
- Writing "market share rose by 1%" when the change is one percentage point.
- Assuming higher sales volume always means higher sales value.
- Forgetting that a falling market share can occur while sales rise.
- Setting an objective without reference to capacity or finance.
