Section 3.2.4.3 of the AQA GCSE Economics (8136) specification has two content points: "Free-trade" and "Free-trade agreements such as the EU". The additional guidance asks you to understand "the arguments for and against free-trade" and "the significance and benefits of free-trade agreements, such as the EU". This guide sets out each argument as a chain you can use in an answer, explains how far different kinds of agreement go, and shows with numbers how removing a barrier changes prices and costs for UK consumers and firms.

## Free trade and the barriers it removes

Free trade means trade between countries without barriers imposed by governments. The main barriers are:

- **tariffs:** taxes on imports, which raise their price;
- **quotas:** limits on the quantity of a good that can be imported;
- **non-tariff barriers:** product rules, paperwork, border checks and subsidies to domestic firms, which make importing harder or dearer.

### Worked example: a tariff on an input (invented)

A UK bicycle maker imports aluminium frames at £80 each from outside any trade agreement. A 20% tariff raises the cost to 80 × 1.20 = **£96**. On 5,000 bicycles a year, the tariff adds 16 × 5,000 = **£80,000** to the firm's costs. If a free-trade agreement removes the tariff, the firm saves that £80,000, which it can use to cut prices, invest or raise profit. Tariffs affect producers as well as consumers, because many imports are materials and components.

## Arguments for free trade

1. **Lower prices for consumers.** No tariffs → imports enter at the price foreign producers charge → competition keeps domestic prices down too.
2. **More choice.** Consumers can buy goods from anywhere in the world.
3. **Specialisation and higher world output.** Each country concentrates on what it produces best → more total output → higher living standards.
4. **Larger markets for exporters.** Tariff-free access → more sales → economies of scale → lower average costs.
5. **Efficiency and innovation.** Foreign competition pushes domestic firms to cut costs and improve quality.
6. **Cheaper inputs.** Firms can buy materials and components from the cheapest suppliers, lowering their costs.

## Arguments against free trade (the case for protection)

1. **Protecting jobs.** Cheaper imports → domestic firms lose sales → closures → structural unemployment, often concentrated in particular regions.
2. **Infant industries.** A new industry has high costs until it grows; without protection it may be beaten by established foreign rivals before it can compete.
3. **Strategic industries.** A country may want to produce its own food, energy or defence equipment so it is not dependent on others in a crisis.
4. **Unfair competition.** Foreign firms may sell below cost (dumping) or benefit from very low labour or environmental standards.
5. **Government revenue.** Tariffs raise money for the government.

When evaluating, notice that most arguments against free trade protect **producers**, while most arguments for it benefit **consumers** and **exporters**. Protection also risks **retaliation**: if one country imposes tariffs, its trading partners may respond with tariffs on its exports.

## How far do agreements go?

| Type of agreement | What members do |
|---|---|
| Free-trade agreement or area | Remove or reduce tariffs and quotas on trade between members; each member keeps its own tariffs on goods from outside |
| Customs union | As above, plus a **common external tariff**: all members charge the same tariff on goods from outside |
| Single market | As above, plus common rules on products and free movement of goods, services, capital and people |

**Why customs unions need fewer checks.** In a free-trade area, members charge different tariffs on outsiders. Suppose member A charges 5% on bicycles from outside and member B charges 20%. Without controls, an outside producer could ship bicycles to A and then on to B tariff-free, avoiding B's tariff. So free-trade agreements use **rules of origin**: only goods made mainly within the members trade tariff-free, and firms must prove where their goods come from. A customs union, with one common external tariff, does not need these checks between members.

## The European Union and the UK

The **European Union** is a customs union and a single market of 27 member states. Goods, services, capital and people move freely between members, product rules are shared, and goods from outside face a common external tariff.

Key UK dates:

- **1 January 1973:** the UK joined the European Economic Community, which later became the EU.
- **23 June 2016:** a referendum voted for the UK to leave.
- **31 January 2020:** the UK left the EU, with a transition period until 31 December 2020.
- **From 1 January 2021:** UK–EU trade has been under the Trade and Cooperation Agreement.

**Significance for the UK.** The EU, taken as a whole, is the UK's largest trading partner: it is a large, wealthy market close to the UK, and many UK supply chains run through it. While the UK was a member, UK exporters sold into the single market with no tariffs, no customs declarations and the same product rules.

**What changed after leaving.** The Trade and Cooperation Agreement keeps trade in goods free of tariffs and quotas where goods meet its rules of origin. But the UK is no longer in the customs union or the single market, so traders face customs declarations, checks and different product rules. These non-tariff barriers add costs, especially for small firms. In return, the UK can set its own tariffs and make its own trade agreements, such as those with Australia and New Zealand.

## Benefits of free-trade agreements for members

- **Exporters** gain tariff-free access to a larger market, so they can grow and gain economies of scale.
- **Consumers** pay lower prices for imports from members and have more choice.
- **Firms** can build supply chains across borders without paying tariffs each time a component crosses.
- **Inward investment** rises, as firms from outside locate inside the agreement to serve the whole market.
- **Bargaining power**: a group negotiates trade deals with the rest of the world more strongly than one country.

**Costs to weigh:** members give up some control over their trade policy; industries that cannot compete with other members may decline; and goods from cheaper producers outside the agreement still face tariffs.

## Annotated model answer

*Question (original):* Explain **one** benefit to a UK car maker of a free-trade agreement with a group of countries. **[3]**

*Model answer:* The agreement removes tariffs on cars sold to member countries [identifies the change]. UK cars therefore sell at lower prices in those markets than before, so they compete better against local brands and sales rise [develops the effect on demand]. With higher output, the car maker spreads its fixed costs over more cars, lowering average cost and raising profit [links to a benefit for the firm].

## Common mistakes

- Defining free trade as trade that "costs nothing".
- Treating a free-trade agreement, a customs union and a single market as the same thing.
- Giving only the consumer view; tariffs also change firms' costs of materials.
- Stating that UK–EU trade now faces tariffs on all goods. Under the Trade and Cooperation Agreement, most goods meeting the rules of origin trade tariff-free; the main new costs are checks and paperwork.
- Ignoring retaliation when recommending tariffs.

## Exam technique

- **Explain** questions: name the barrier removed, then show the effect on price, sales or costs, and on the named group.
- **Evaluate** questions: separate consumers, exporters, firms competing with imports, workers and government; weigh gains against losses; and reach a judgement that fits the case.
