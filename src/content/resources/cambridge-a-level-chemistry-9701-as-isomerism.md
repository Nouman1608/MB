This guide covers subtopic **13.4, Isomerism: structural isomerism and stereoisomerism**, from Topic 13
of Cambridge International AS & A Level Chemistry 9701, 2025–2027 series. It is AS Level content. The
[Introduction to Organic Chemistry study guide](/resources/organic-mechanisms-an-introduction/) defines
each type of isomer in a paragraph; this page shows how to apply the definitions, how to find every
isomer of a formula without missing or repeating one, and how to spot cis/trans and chiral centres in
unfamiliar structures, including rings.

## What the syllabus asks for

Candidates should be able to:

1. describe structural isomerism and its division into **chain**, **positional** and **functional group**
   isomerism
2. describe stereoisomerism and its division into **geometrical (cis/trans)** and **optical** isomerism
   (E/Z names are acceptable but not required)
3. describe cis/trans isomerism in alkenes and explain its origin in **restricted rotation** due to the
   π bond
4. explain what a **chiral centre** is and that it gives rise to two optical isomers (**enantiomers**);
   compounds can contain more than one chiral centre, but meso compounds and the term diastereoisomers
   are not required
5. identify chiral centres and cis/trans isomerism in a given structure, **including cyclic compounds**
6. **deduce the possible isomers** for a known molecular formula

## The family tree

**Isomers** are compounds with the same molecular formula but different arrangements of atoms.

- **Structural isomers** have different structural formulae: the atoms are joined in a different order.
- **Stereoisomers** have the same structural formula but a different arrangement of atoms in space.

## Structural isomerism

**Chain isomers** differ in the carbon skeleton. C₅H₁₂ has three: pentane, CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂CH₃;
2-methylbutane, CH₃CH(CH₃)CH₂CH₃; and 2,2-dimethylpropane, C(CH₃)₄. Branching changes physical
properties: the more branched isomers have fewer points of contact between molecules, weaker
instantaneous dipole–induced dipole forces and lower boiling points.

**Positional isomers** have the same skeleton and the same functional group, but the group sits at a
different position. Examples: 1-chlorobutane and 2-chlorobutane; pentan-2-one and pentan-3-one;
but-1-ene and but-2-ene.

**Functional group isomers** have different functional groups. Common pairs at AS Level:

| Molecular formula | Isomer 1 | Isomer 2 |
|---|---|---|
| C₃H₆O | propanal (aldehyde) | propanone (ketone) |
| C₃H₆O₂ | propanoic acid (carboxylic acid) | methyl ethanoate (ester) |
| C₄H₈ | but-1-ene (alkene) | cyclobutane (cycloalkane) |

Functional group isomers have different chemical reactions, which is how tests tell them apart: for
example, propanoic acid fizzes with sodium carbonate and methyl ethanoate does not.

## Geometrical (cis/trans) isomerism

A C=C double bond contains a π bond formed by sideways overlap of p orbitals. Rotating one end of the
double bond would twist the p orbitals out of alignment and break the π bond, so there is **restricted
rotation**. Groups attached to the two carbons are locked on the same side or on opposite sides.

**The test.** A C=C shows cis/trans isomerism only if **each** carbon of the double bond carries two
**different** groups.

- But-2-ene, CH₃CH=CHCH₃: each carbon has H and CH₃, so **cis** (both CH₃ on the same side) and
  **trans** (opposite sides) isomers exist.
- But-1-ene, CH₂=CHCH₂CH₃: the first carbon carries two hydrogens, so there is **no** cis/trans
  isomerism.
- 2-methylpropene, (CH₃)₂C=CH₂: both carbons carry two identical groups, so **no** cis/trans isomerism.

**Rings.** A ring also prevents rotation, because turning one ring carbon would mean breaking the ring.
In 1,2-dimethylcyclohexane the two methyl groups can lie on the same face of the ring (cis) or on
opposite faces (trans). The test is the same idea: each of the two ring carbons must carry two different
groups (here H and CH₃, plus its place in the ring).

Cis and trans isomers are different compounds with different physical properties, such as boiling and
melting points.

## Optical isomerism and chiral centres

A **chiral centre** is a carbon atom bonded to **four different atoms or groups**. Such a molecule has a
mirror image that cannot be superimposed on it, just as a left hand cannot be superimposed on a right
hand. The two mirror-image forms are **optical isomers**, or **enantiomers**.

To show enantiomers, draw the chiral carbon with two bonds in the plane of the paper, one wedge (towards
you) and one dash (away), then draw its mirror image beside it. Swapping any two groups on a chiral
carbon converts one enantiomer into the other.

**How to judge "four different groups".** Look at the whole group, not just the first atom.

- Butan-2-ol, CH₃CH(OH)CH₂CH₃: C2 carries H, OH, CH₃ and CH₂CH₃, so it is a chiral centre.
- Propan-2-ol, CH₃CH(OH)CH₃: C2 carries two CH₃ groups, so it is **not** chiral.
- In a ring, follow each path round the ring. In methylcyclohexane, C1 carries H, CH₃ and two ring
  paths that are identical, so it is not chiral. In 2-methylcyclohexanone, C2 carries H, CH₃, the ring
  path via C=O and the ring path via CH₂; these four are different, so C2 is a chiral centre.

**More than one chiral centre.** 3-methylpentan-2-ol, CH₃CH(OH)CH(CH₃)CH₂CH₃, has two: C2 (H, OH, CH₃
and the rest of the chain) and C3 (H, CH₃, C₂H₅ and CH(OH)CH₃). The syllabus only asks you to recognise
that a molecule can contain more than one.

How enantiomers are told apart, using plane-polarised light, is A Level content: see the
[Optical Isomerism and Chirality guide](/resources/a-optical-isomerism-and-chirality/).

## Worked example 1: deducing every isomer of C₄H₈

**Question.** Deduce all the isomers of C₄H₈, including stereoisomers.

**Method.** C₄H₈ is CₙH₂ₙ, so it is either an alkene or a cycloalkane. Work through each systematically.

1. **Straight-chain alkenes:** the C=C can start at C1 or C2. That gives but-1-ene and but-2-ene.
2. **Branched alkenes:** a three-carbon chain with a methyl branch gives 2-methylpropene. (Putting the
   double bond elsewhere in this skeleton gives the same molecule.)
3. **Cycloalkanes:** a four-membered ring, cyclobutane; or a three-membered ring with a methyl group,
   methylcyclopropane.
4. **Stereoisomers:** test each alkene. Only but-2-ene has two different groups on both C=C carbons, so
   it has cis and trans forms. None of the molecules has a carbon with four different groups.

**Answer: six isomers.** But-1-ene, cis-but-2-ene, trans-but-2-ene, 2-methylpropene, cyclobutane and
methylcyclopropane.

## Worked example 2: finding stereoisomerism in a given structure

**Question.** Pent-3-en-2-ol is CH₃CH(OH)CH=CHCH₃. Identify the types of stereoisomerism it shows and
state how many stereoisomers exist.

**Answer.** C2 carries H, OH, CH₃ and CH=CHCH₃: four different groups, so C2 is a **chiral centre**
and the molecule shows **optical isomerism**. At the C=C, C3 carries H and CH(OH)CH₃ and C4 carries H
and CH₃: two different groups on each, so it shows **cis/trans isomerism**. Each of the cis and trans
forms has two enantiomers, so there are **four stereoisomers**.

## Common mistakes

- **Saying C=C "cannot rotate" without the reason.** Credit needs the π bond: rotation would break the
  sideways overlap of p orbitals.
- **Claiming cis/trans isomers for but-1-ene or any C=CH₂ end.** Check both carbons.
- **Calling a carbon chiral when two of its groups are identical further along**, such as the two
  CH₂CH₃ groups on C3 of pentan-3-ol.
- **Counting the same isomer twice** by numbering from the wrong end: 3-chlorobutane is 2-chlorobutane.
- **Forgetting cycloalkanes** when deducing isomers of a CₙH₂ₙ formula.
- **Drawing mirror images that are actually identical** because the carbon is not chiral. Check for four
  different groups first.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Describe** an isomer type: give the definition and one named example.
- **Explain** cis/trans isomerism: restricted rotation, caused by the π bond, plus two different groups on
  each carbon.
- **Identify** a chiral centre: mark it with an asterisk on the structure and, if asked to justify, list
  its four groups.
- **Deduce** isomers: work systematically (chain length, then branching, then position, then other
  functional groups or rings, then stereoisomers) and give a name or clear structure for each.
