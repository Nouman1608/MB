This guide covers topic 1.7 Urbanisation in Theme 1 (Population and settlement) of the Cambridge IGCSE Geography (0460) syllabus for examination in 2025 and 2026. The syllabus asks you to identify and suggest reasons for rapid urban growth, referring to the physical, economic and social factors that cause rural depopulation and movement to major cities, and to describe the impacts of urban growth on both rural and urban areas, with possible solutions. Its guidance names the effects on people and on the natural environment, the characteristics of squatter settlements, and strategies to reduce the negative impacts. A case study of a rapidly growing urban area in a developing country, and migration to it, is required. The [Urbanisation revision notes](/resources/igcse-geography-0460-urbanisation-revision-notes/) summarise causes and consequences with a Mumbai case study; this guide adds a factor-by-factor explanation of rural depopulation, impacts on the countryside as well as the city, an evaluation of strategies and a second case study, Dhaka. Practise with the [Urbanisation practice questions](/resources/cambridge-igcse-geography-0460-urbanisation-practice/).

## Urbanisation and urban growth

- **Urbanisation** is an increase in the **proportion** of a country's population living in urban areas.
- **Urban growth** is an increase in the **number of people** living in towns and cities, or in their area.

A city can grow without the country urbanising, if the rural population grows just as fast. The United Nations estimates that more than half of the world's population has lived in urban areas since 2007, and most urban growth now happens in Asia and Africa. A **megacity** is an urban area of more than 10 million people.

Rapid urban growth has two causes: **rural-urban migration** and **natural increase** in the city, because migrants are mostly young adults who then have children in the city.

## Why people leave rural areas: three groups of factors

The syllabus names physical, economic and social factors. Explain each as a chain from the factor to the decision to leave.

**Physical factors**

- **Drought and desertification** cause crop failure, so families lose food and income.
- **Floods, cyclones and river erosion** destroy fields, homes and livestock; on some large rivers, erosion removes whole villages.
- **Soil erosion and exhaustion** lower yields year after year.

**Economic factors**

- **Low and unreliable farm incomes**, especially when crop prices fall.
- **Land shortage**: plots are divided between children over generations until they are too small to support a family.
- **Mechanisation** of commercial farms reduces the number of labourers needed.
- **Debt** to moneylenders after a poor harvest can force a family to sell land.

**Social factors**

- Few secondary schools, hospitals, piped water or electricity in rural areas.
- Limited opportunities for young people, and the expectation that one family member will earn in the city.
- Conflict or insecurity in some rural regions.

**Pull factors of the city** are the mirror image: more jobs and higher wages in factories, construction and services (formal and informal); access to education and healthcare; and the hope of a better life, reinforced by relatives who have already moved.

## Impacts on rural areas

| Positive | Negative |
|---|---|
| Remittances sent home pay for food, schooling, housing and farm inputs | **Rural depopulation**: young adults leave, so the population becomes older and more female |
| Less pressure on land, so remaining farmers may have larger plots | Labour shortages at busy times such as planting and harvest, so output may fall |
| Returning migrants bring ideas and skills | Services such as schools and clinics may close as the population falls |

**Solutions in rural areas** aim to reduce the push factors: irrigation and drought-resistant crops; small loans (microfinance) to start businesses; rural electrification, roads and mobile phone coverage; and building schools and clinics so that families do not have to move to reach services.

## Impacts on urban areas: people and the natural environment

**On people**

- **Housing shortage**: the city cannot build homes as fast as people arrive, so squatter settlements grow.
- **Unemployment and the informal sector**: there are not enough formal jobs, so many people work as street sellers, rickshaw drivers or waste pickers, with low and insecure incomes.
- **Overloaded services**: water, sanitation, schools and hospitals cannot keep up.
- **Traffic congestion** and long journeys to work.
- **Health risks** from crowding, poor sanitation and polluted air.

**On the natural environment**

- **Air pollution** from vehicles, factories, brick kilns and burning waste.
- **Water pollution** when untreated sewage and industrial waste enter rivers.
- **Loss of farmland, wetlands and forest** as the city spreads.
- **Waste**: uncollected rubbish blocks drains and increases flooding.

Urban growth also has benefits: a large workforce attracts investment, and cities produce a large share of national income.

## Characteristics of squatter settlements

- Built on land the residents do not own or rent legally, often **hazardous or unwanted** land: steep slopes, flood plains, beside railways or rubbish dumps.
- Homes made from **makeshift materials** such as corrugated iron, plastic sheeting, wood and mud, sometimes later rebuilt in brick.
- **Very high density**, with narrow paths instead of roads.
- **Few services**: shared water points and toilets, no sewers, illegal electricity connections, irregular waste collection.
- Residents risk **eviction** because they lack legal title.
- But they are often **active communities** with small businesses, workshops and recycling, and many residents have lived there for years and gradually improved their homes.

## Strategies and how well they work

| Strategy | How it works | Strengths | Limitations |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Self-help schemes** | Residents are given materials, loans or legal title to improve their own homes | Cheap; keeps communities together; residents gain security | Slow; quality varies; does not create new land |
| **Site-and-service schemes** | Government prepares plots with water, sanitation and roads; residents build homes | Basic services from the start; affordable | Sites are often far from jobs |
| **Upgrading in place** | Paths paved, drains, water and toilets added within an existing settlement | Improves health without moving people | Very crowded sites limit what can be added |
| **Redevelopment into flats** | Settlement cleared and replaced with apartment blocks | Better-quality housing | Some residents may not qualify; flats may not suit home-based work |
| **Transport and pollution controls** | Metro systems, bus lanes, cleaner fuels, waste collection | Tackle city-wide problems | Expensive; growth can outpace them |
| **Rural development** | Reduce push factors, as above | Slows migration at its source | Takes a long time; city pull remains strong |

## Case study: Dhaka, Bangladesh

**Growth and migration.** Dhaka, the capital of Bangladesh, is one of the fastest-growing megacities in the world, with a metropolitan population of more than 20 million. Many migrants come from rural districts affected by flooding, river erosion and cyclones, and from areas where landholdings are too small to support families. The main pull is work: Bangladesh's garment industry, its largest export industry, has many factories in and around Dhaka, and many of their workers are young women from rural areas. Others work in construction, transport and informal trade.

**Impacts on people.** A large share of the population lives in informal settlements. Korail, beside a lake close to the wealthy districts of Gulshan and Banani, is one of the largest; it lies on government-owned land and has suffered repeated fires and threats of eviction. Traffic congestion is severe. Working conditions in the garment industry became an international issue after the collapse of the Rana Plaza factory building at Savar, near Dhaka, in April 2013, which killed more than 1,100 people; it led to a legally binding safety agreement between brands and unions and to factory inspections.

**Impacts on the natural environment.** The Buriganga River, on which the old city stands, has been heavily polluted by sewage and industrial waste, including from tanneries. Brick kilns around the city add to severe air pollution in the dry season. Wetlands and low-lying land that once absorbed floodwater have been filled for building.

**Responses.** Tanneries were relocated from the Hazaribagh area of the city to an industrial estate at Savar in 2017, although pollution has been reported at the new site. Dhaka's first metro rail line opened in December 2022 to reduce congestion. Upgrading projects have improved water points, paths and drains in some informal settlements.

**Evaluation.** Responses have been limited by the speed of growth, the cost of land and the scale of need. As in Mumbai, migration continues because the gap in opportunities between the city and the countryside remains large.

## Model answer: explain a cause of rural depopulation

*Question: Explain how physical factors can cause rural-urban migration.*

> Physical hazards can destroy rural livelihoods (knowledge). In Bangladesh, river erosion and floods wash away fields and homes (application), so families lose both their food supply and their income and have little choice but to move (analysis). Many go to Dhaka, where garment factories and construction offer work, so the physical push combines with an economic pull (analysis). Physical factors are often the trigger, but economic factors such as small plots and low incomes explain why so many people cannot recover and stay (evaluation).

## Common errors

- Defining urbanisation as "cities getting bigger"; it is an increase in the proportion of people living in urban areas.
- Giving only economic push factors when the syllabus names physical and social factors too.
- Describing impacts on the city but not on the rural areas people leave.
- Describing squatter settlements only negatively, missing their community and economic activity.
- Naming strategies without evaluating them.

## Exam technique

- **Suggest reasons**: give a factor, then the consequence that makes people leave.
- **Describe characteristics** of squatter settlements: materials, site, density, services, legal status.
- **Evaluate strategies**: say who benefits, who does not, and what limits success.
- **Case study answers**: name the city, where migrants come from, why, the impacts and the responses.
