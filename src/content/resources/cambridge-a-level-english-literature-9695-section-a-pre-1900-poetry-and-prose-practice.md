## Section A — Knowledge and terms

**1.** State the **form rule** that governs a candidate's two answers on Paper 4. **[2]**

**2.** A candidate plans to answer on Shakespeare's *Selected Sonnets* in Section A. State what form of
text they must answer on in Section B, and give **one** 2026 example. **[2]**

**3.** Define an **intrusive narrator** and explain how its commentary can contribute to a novel's
meaning. **[2]**

**4.** Describe the structure of a **Shakespearean sonnet** and explain the usual function of its final

## Section B — Period-style passages and evaluation

**5.** Read this **original** passage, written in the manner of a nineteenth-century novel.

> Mr Harrow had been told so often that he was a generous man that he had come, in time, to believe it,
> and it would have astonished him to learn that his generosity had never yet cost him anything he
> wanted. He gave freely of advice, of which he had a great deal, and of opinions, of which he had
> more; and if he gave less freely of money, it was only, he felt, because money was so often misused.

Explain how the narrator uses **irony** to present Mr Harrow. **[6]**

**6.** In the passage in Question 5, explain how the **sentence structure** of the final sentence
contributes to its effect. **[3]**

**7.** Read this **original** sonnet, written in the Shakespearean form.

> When I am gone, let no one keep my name
> in stone, or speak it slowly at the door;
> I would not have my absence turned to fame,
> nor be remembered better than before.
> Let others build their monuments of praise
> and hang their portraits where the light is kind;
> I ask for nothing past my counted days
> but that you live, and leave the grief behind.
> And yet, if some late evening you should find
> my book left open where we used to read,
> and pause, and mark the page, and call to mind
> the voice that read it — that is all I need.
> So I forbid you grief, and in one breath
> confess I hope you'll grieve me after death.

Explain how the **volta** and the **final couplet** change the meaning of the poem. **[6]**

**8.** Explain how the sonnet in Question 7 uses **contrast** in the second quatrain. **[3]**

**9.** Explain **two** opposed readings of the Wife of Bath as a character, and how a candidate could
evaluate them. **[4]**

**10.** A candidate writes: "Austen's heroines only care about getting married, which seems shallow to a
modern reader." Explain the weakness of this comment, and suggest how context and AO5 could improve it.
**[4]**

**11.** Explain **three** features of a strong (a) essay on a long pre-1900 novel. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** A candidate must write **one essay on a poetry text and one on a prose text** [1], with **one
answer from each section** (pre-1900 and post-1900) [1].

**2.** They must answer on a **prose** text in Section B [1]; for 2026, for example, *Waiting for the
Barbarians*, Mansfield's *Selected Stories* or *Beloved* [1].

**3.** An intrusive narrator **comments directly on events or characters**, sometimes addressing the
reader [1]. The commentary can **generalise from a character's experience** to a wider claim about human
nature or society, making it part of the novel's argument [1].

**4.** **Three quatrains and a closing couplet**, usually rhymed ABAB CDCD EFEF GG [1] [1]. The couplet
often **resolves, summarises or twists** the argument of the quatrains [1].

**5.** The narrator reports that Mr Harrow "had come, in time, to believe" he was generous, exposing
**self-deception shaped by flattery** [1] [1]. The clause that his generosity "had never yet cost him
anything he wanted" **undercuts** the praise with a dry fact [1]. The parallel of advice "of which he had a
great deal" and opinions "of which he had more" mocks him through **ironic balance** [1]. The final
clause moves into **free indirect style** ("it was only, he felt") so his excuse is given in his own
reasoning [1], letting the reader see through it while the narrator never openly condemns him [1].

**6.** The sentence builds through a **balanced, periodic structure**, listing what he gives freely
before arriving at money [1]. The semicolon creates a **pause before the reversal** [1], so the excuse,
delayed until the end, lands as the comic climax of the sentence [1].

**7.** The first two quatrains **forbid memorial and grief**, presenting the speaker as self-effacing [1].
The volta at "And yet" in line 9 **turns towards a private wish** [1]: the speaker imagines being
remembered through an intimate, ordinary scene of reading [1]. The final couplet **openly admits the
contradiction**, forbidding grief "and in one breath" hoping for it [1] [1], so the poem ends by
revealing the human need its opening tried to deny [1].

**8.** The quatrain contrasts **public monuments** and portraits [1] with the speaker's **modest wish**
for the beloved's life [1], so public fame is rejected in favour of private, living love [1].

**9.** One reading sees the Wife as an **anti-feminist caricature** who confirms clerical stereotypes of
women [1]. Another sees her as **challenging male authority**, setting her experience against what
authorities have written [1]. A candidate evaluates by **testing each against the text**, such as her use
of the authorities' own arguments [1], and judging which reading, or what balance, the poem's structure
best supports [1].

**10.** The comment **judges by modern norms** without considering the economic and social position of
women in the period, and **dismisses** rather than analyses [1] [1]. Context could explain that
**marriage determined women's security** and status [1]; AO5 could weigh a reading of the marriage plot
as **conservative** against one that finds it **ironic** about those pressures [1].

**11.** A **positioned thesis** answering the question's terms [1] [1]. Moments selected from **across the
whole novel, including the ending**, so development is shown rather than a few famous scenes [1] [1].
**Narrative method and evaluated AO5** in each paragraph, not just character and plot [1] [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Breaking the poetry-and-prose form rule across the paper.
- Missing irony in an older narrator's voice.
- Labelling the sonnet form without tracking how the argument moves through it.
- Judging older texts by modern standards without analysis or context.
