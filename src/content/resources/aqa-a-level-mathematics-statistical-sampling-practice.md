These questions cover **Section K: Statistical sampling** (K1) of the **AQA A-level Mathematics
(7357) specification**, version 1.3, for A-level exams from June 2018 onwards. Section K is
Paper 3 content in the specification. A calculator is required in every 7357 paper, so every
question here assumes you have one. Questions 9 to 11 are extended problems that combine several
parts of K1.

## Questions

**1.** A cinema chain wants to know how satisfied its 15,000 loyalty-card holders are. It emails a
questionnaire to 300 of them. State the population and the sample. **[2]**

**2.** A factory makes fireworks. Give two reasons why it tests a sample of each batch rather than
carrying out a census. **[2]**

**3.** A raffle has 840 tickets, numbered 1 to 840. Describe how to use a random number generator
to choose a simple random sample of 50 tickets. **[3]**

**4.** To find out how much time adults in a city spend reading, a student asks the first 40 adults
who leave the central library on a Saturday afternoon.

**(a)** Name this sampling method. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain why the results are unlikely to be representative, stating the likely effect on
the estimate. **[2]**

**5.** A warehouse has 960 parcels on an ordered dispatch list. A systematic sample of 40 parcels
is to be checked.

**(a)** Find the sampling interval. **[1]**
**(b)** The random start is 15. List the first three parcel numbers chosen and the last one.
**[2]**
**(c)** Explain why this is not a simple random sample. **[1]**

**6.** A tennis club has 84 junior, 196 adult and 70 senior members.

**(a)** Find how many of each group should be in a stratified sample of 25. **[3]**
**(b)** State one advantage of stratified sampling over simple random sampling here. **[1]**

**7.** A random sample of 40 bags of flour is taken from a batch of 2,000 bags. 6 of the sampled
bags have a mass below 500 g. The total mass of the 40 sampled bags is 20,180 g.

**(a)** Estimate the number of bags in the batch with a mass below 500 g. **[2]**
**(b)** Estimate the mean mass of a bag in the batch. **[1]**
**(c)** The supplier claims fewer than 1 in 10 bags in a batch are below 500 g. Comment on this
claim using the sample. **[2]**

**8.** Two students each take a random sample of 12 pupils from the same sixth form and record the
hours of paid work done last week. Ana's sample mean is 3.2 hours. Ben's is 3.9 hours.

**(a)** Explain why the two sample means are different. **[2]**
**(b)** Suggest two ways the students could get a more reliable estimate of the population mean.
**[2]**

**9.** A town council wants the views of the town's 24,000 adult residents on a new bus route.
Three methods are suggested:

- **P:** interview adults waiting at the bus station on one weekday morning;
- **Q:** choose a simple random sample of adults from the electoral register and post them a
  questionnaire;
- **R:** split the town into its six wards and take a stratified sample by ward.

**(a)** Give two criticisms of method P. **[3]**
**(b)** Give one advantage and two disadvantages of method Q. **[3]**
**(c)** Explain why the council might prefer R to Q. **[2]**

**10.** A factory runs three shifts. The morning shift makes 240 items, the afternoon shift 180
items and the night shift 120 items per day. A quality manager wants a stratified sample of 45
items from one day's output.

**(a)** Find how many items to take from each shift. **[2]**
**(b)** Describe how the items from the morning shift should be chosen. **[2]**
**(c)** The numbers of defective items found are 3 (morning), 2 (afternoon) and 4 (night). Show
that the estimated proportion of defective items in the day's output is 20%. **[3]**
**(d)** Comment on the night shift's results. **[1]**
**(e)** A colleague suggests a quota sample instead, taking the first items available from each
shift. Explain why this could give a biased estimate. **[2]**

**11.** A researcher wants the views of Year 12 pupils in a region with 40 secondary schools.

**(a)** Describe how a cluster sample could be taken. **[2]**
**(b)** Give one advantage of cluster sampling here and one reason the estimates may be less
reliable than from a stratified sample by school. **[2]**
**(c)** State the sampling frame needed for a simple random sample of Year 12 pupils in the
region. **[1]**

## Answers

**1.** Population: **all 15,000 loyalty-card holders** [1]. Sample: **the 300 card holders sent
the questionnaire** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* the population must be the exact group in the question; "cinema customers"
loses the mark because it is wider than the card holders.

**2.** Testing destroys the firework, so a census would leave nothing to sell [1]. A sample is
quicker and cheaper to test [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* each reason must be a separate point tied to the context; two versions of
"cheaper" earn one mark only.

**3.** Use the numbers 1 to 840 as the sampling frame (the ticket numbers) [1]. Generate random
integers from 1 to 840 with a calculator or computer [1]. Select the matching tickets, ignoring
repeats, until 50 are chosen [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* "pick 50 at random" scores nothing; the marks are for the frame, the random
number generation, and dealing with repeats.

**4. (a)** **Opportunity sampling** [1].
**(b)** Library users are more likely to read than other adults [1], so the time spent reading
will be **overestimated** [1].
*Examiner insight:* a critique needs the group over-represented and the direction of the effect;
"it is biased" alone gets no credit.

**5. (a)** k = 960 ÷ 40 = **24** [1].
**(b)** First three: **15, 39, 63** [1]. Last: 15 + 39 × 24 = **951** [1].
**(c)** Once the start is chosen, the other parcels are fixed, so not every possible sample of 40
can be chosen [1].
*Examiner insight:* in (b) using 40 × 24 instead of 39 × 24 for the last item is a common slip;
the 40th item is r + 39k.

**6. (a)** Total = 84 + 196 + 70 = 350 [1]. Juniors 84/350 × 25 = **6**, adults 196/350 × 25 =
**14** [1], seniors 70/350 × 25 = **5** [1].
**(b)** Each age group is represented in proportion to its size [1].
*Examiner insight:* show the fraction for each stratum; three bare whole numbers with no working
risk losing the method mark.

**7. (a)** Sample proportion 6/40 = 0.15 [1]. Estimate 0.15 × 2000 = **300 bags** [1].
**(b)** 20180 ÷ 40 = **504.5 g** [1].
**(c)** The sample proportion 0.15 is more than 0.1, so the sample does not support the claim
[1]. But it is only one sample of 40, and another sample could give a different proportion, so
this is not conclusive [1].
*Examiner insight:* in (c) one mark is for comparing with the claim and the other for a
sensible caveat about sampling variability; stating "the claim is false" overstates the evidence.

**8. (a)** The samples contain different pupils [1], and with small samples chance differences
between samples are large (sampling variability) [1].
**(b)** Use a larger sample [1]. Combine the two samples into one of 24 [1].
*Examiner insight:* "they made a mistake" earns nothing; credit comes from the idea that
different random samples give different results, linked to the small sample size.

**9. (a)** Only bus users are asked, so support for a new bus route is likely to be
overestimated [1]. Residents who drive, walk or cycle are under-represented [1]. One weekday
morning misses people who travel at other times, such as shift workers [1].
**(b)** Advantage: every adult on the register has an equal chance of selection, so there is no
selection bias [1]. Disadvantages: postal questionnaires may get a low response rate [1]; those
who reply may hold stronger views than those who do not [1].
**(c)** Views may differ between wards, for example near and far from the proposed route [1].
Stratifying by ward makes sure each ward is represented in proportion to its adult population
[1].
*Examiner insight:* in (a) three marks need three distinct, context-linked points; repeating
"not representative" in different words earns one mark at most.

**10. (a)** Total 540. Morning 240/540 × 45 = **20**, afternoon **15** [1], night **10** [1].
**(b)** Number the 240 morning items [1]. Use random numbers to select 20, ignoring repeats [1].
**(c)** Estimated defective: morning 3/20 × 240 = 36, afternoon 2/15 × 180 = 24, night 4/10 × 120
= 48 [1]. Total 36 + 24 + 48 = 108 [1]. 108/540 = 0.2 = **20%** [1].
**(d)** The night shift's sample defect rate (40%) is much higher than the others (15% and
about 13%), which suggests a problem on that shift worth investigating, though it is based on only
10 items [1].
**(e)** The first items available are not chosen at random [1], so if, for example, the first
items of a shift are made while machines are warming up, they may have a different defect rate
from the rest [1].
*Examiner insight:* (c) is a "show that", so each step must appear; quoting 9/45 = 0.2 without
explaining why it matches the weighted estimate risks losing marks.

**11. (a)** Choose some of the 40 schools at random [1]. Survey all (or a random sample of) Year 12
pupils in the chosen schools [1].
**(b)** Advantage: it is cheaper because researchers visit only a few schools [1]. Less reliable:
schools may differ from each other, and unchosen schools contribute nobody [1].
**(c)** A list of all Year 12 pupils in the region's 40 schools [1].
*Examiner insight:* in (a) the random choice of clusters must be stated; choosing "nearby"
schools turns it into an opportunity sample and loses the mark.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing the population too broadly or too narrowly for the context.
- Writing "choose at random" without a sampling frame, numbering or a random number generator.
- Leaving out "ignore repeats" when selecting by random numbers.
- Getting the last systematic item wrong by using n × k instead of (n − 1) × k.
- Stratum sizes that do not add up to the sample size after rounding.
- Calling a criticism "biased" without saying who is over-represented and which way the
  estimate moves.
- Treating a sample estimate as proof about the population.
- Describing quota sampling as random within groups.
- Giving two criticisms that are really the same point.
