**How to use this set.** Both passages are original, written for practice. Section A builds
individual skills; Section B asks for longer analysis of the passages and for planning.

**Passage 1: from "The Lake"** *(original passage)*

> My father did not speak until the boat was well out on the lake, where the town had become a
> smudge of roofs behind us. He baited my hook before his own, the way he always had, though I was
> fifteen now and could have done it myself. The water slapped against the hull in small, impatient
> sounds.
>
> "Your mother says you want to go to the city," he said at last, watching his line and not me.
>
> I said nothing. Somewhere a fish rose, and the rings spread out until they reached us, and faded.
>
> "Well," he said. He reeled in, slowly, though nothing was biting. "It's a long way to row back."
>
> I did not know whether he meant the lake.

**Passage 2: from "The New Teacher"** *(original passage)*

> Mr Okafor arrived on a Tuesday, which was already suspicious. Nobody starts anything on a Tuesday.
> He wrote his name on the board in letters so neat they looked printed, and then, instead of telling
> us to be quiet, he simply waited. I timed him: forty seconds. By the end even Daniel had stopped
> talking, though he pretended it was his own idea. I decided at once that I did not like Mr Okafor.
> I decided it again at lunch, and once more on the bus home, which should have told me something.

## Section A

**1.** Identify the narrative voice of Passage 2 and explain **one** effect it has. **[2]**

**2.** In Passage 1, "The water slapped against the hull in small, impatient sounds." Name the
method and explain its effect. **[2]**

**3.** Explain what it means to "place" a printed passage, and why doing so earns credit. **[2]**

**4.** Give **three** ways a writer can reveal character, with an example of each from Passage 1 or
Passage 2. **[3]**

**5.** Explain the irony in this original sentence: "She thanked him for the lovely gift and put it in
the drawer with the others." **[2]**

**6.** Using Passage 2, explain the difference between a writer "showing" and "telling" the reader
about a character. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** Read Passage 1.
**(a)** How does the writer present the relationship between the father and the narrator? **[8]**
**(b)** Explore the significance of the final sentence. **[3]**

**8.** Read Passage 2. How does the writer make the narrator's attitude to Mr Okafor both amusing and
revealing? **[8]**

**9.** Write the introduction to an answer on: "How does the writer create tension in Passage 1?" **[4]**

**10.** A student wrote this paragraph about Passage 1:

> "The father and son go fishing on the lake. The father baits the hook. Then he asks about the city
> and the son does not answer. A fish jumps. The father reels in his line and says it is a long way to
> row back."

Identify **three** weaknesses in the paragraph and rewrite **one** of its sentences as analysis. **[5]**

**11.** If Passage 1 came from a novel, explain how your approach would change if the question asked
about the father's feelings across the whole text instead of in this passage. **[4]**

**12.** Write a five-point plan for the question: "How does the writer vividly present Mr Okafor in
Passage 2?" **[5]**

## Answers

**1.** First-person narration [1]. Any one effect [1]: we see Mr Okafor only through the narrator's
biased view; the narrator's own stubborn, comic personality, comes through; the reader can see
more than the narrator admits.

**2.** Personification [1]: the water seems "impatient", reflecting the tension and the narrator's
unease while the father stays silent, and the harsh "slapped" makes the silence feel uncomfortable [1].

**3.** Placing means briefly saying where the passage comes in the text and what has led to it [1]; it
shows knowledge of the whole text (AO1) and helps explain why the moment matters (AO2) [1].

**4.** Any three, [1] each, with an example: action (the father baits the narrator's hook first,
showing protectiveness); speech (the father's indirect comment about rowing back); description or
narrator's comment (Mr Okafor's letters "so neat they looked printed"); other characters' reactions
(Daniel stops talking); a character's thoughts (the narrator decides he dislikes Mr Okafor).
Maximum 3.

**5.** "Lovely" and the thanks suggest she values the gift [1], but putting it "in the drawer with the
others" reveals that she does not use it and that he gives such gifts often, so her politeness hides
indifference [1].

**6.** Telling: the narrator states "I decided at once that I did not like Mr Okafor" [1]. Showing:
the writer lets us see Mr Okafor's calm authority through his actions, waiting silently until "even
Daniel" stops talking, so we judge him differently from the narrator [1].

**7. (a)** Any four developed points, [2] each:
- The father's silence until they are "well out on the lake" suggests he finds it hard to talk and
  needs distance from home to raise a difficult subject [2].
- Baiting the son's hook "the way he always had" shows lasting care, but "though I was fifteen now"
  hints that the father is not ready to see his son grow up [2].
- "watching his line and not me" shows the father avoiding eye contact, so the conversation is
  awkward and emotional [2].
- The son's silence ("I said nothing") shows reluctance or guilt; the two communicate through what
  they do not say [2].
- The fish's rings that "spread out until they reached us, and faded" suggest the news of leaving
  spreading between them and fading into acceptance [2].
- Reeling in "slowly, though nothing was biting" suggests the father's sadness or his wish to end the
  conversation gently [2].
Maximum 8.
**(b)** The narrator realises the father's words may have a second meaning [1]: "a long way to row
back" may refer to the distance to the city or the difficulty of coming home once he has left [1]. The
short final line leaves the reader with the same uncertainty, so the ending is open and poignant [1].

**8.** Any four developed points, [2] each:
- The narrator's opening judgement that a Tuesday start is "already suspicious" is comically absurd,
  showing prejudice before Mr Okafor has done anything [2].
- The over-confident generalisation "Nobody starts anything on a Tuesday" reveals the narrator's
  stubborn, childish logic [2].
- Timing him, "forty seconds", is a funny detail that also shows the narrator is fascinated despite
  themselves [2].
- The comment that Daniel "pretended it was his own idea" shows the class being won over without
  admitting it [2].
- The repetition of "I decided" three times is humorous and suggests the narrator is trying to
  convince themselves [2].
- "which should have told me something" is an older narrator looking back, hinting that the narrator came to
  like Mr Okafor, so the passage is ironic [2].
Maximum 8.

**9.** A clear answer to the question [1]; reference to the main sources of tension, such as silence
and the unspoken subject [1]; at least one brief, exact quotation [1]; an indication of the argument
to follow [1]. For example: "The writer creates tension through what the father and son cannot say to
each other. The father waits until they are 'well out on the lake' before speaking, and even then
watches 'his line and not me', so the reader senses a painful subject that both avoid. I will show how
silence, small actions and the ambiguous final line keep this tension unresolved."

**10.** Any three weaknesses, [1] each: it retells the plot in order; it uses no quotations; it names
no methods; it does not explain effects; it does not answer a question or make an argument. Maximum
3. A rewritten sentence with an exact quotation and analysis of effect [2], for example: "The father's
remark that 'it's a long way to row back' sounds practical, but it may also warn his son how hard it
will be to return once he leaves."

**11.** Any two developed points, [2] each:
- You would choose several key moments from across the novel, from beginning to end, not only this
  scene [2].
- You would need to recall short quotations from memory, because only relevant passages are printed
  and the text cannot be taken into the exam [2].
- You would trace how the father's feelings change and explain what causes the change [2].
- The answer would be organised as an argument about his feelings, not as a summary of the plot in
  order [2].
Maximum 4.

**12.** One mark for each of five relevant, sequenced points [5], for example:
1. Introduction: Mr Okafor is presented vividly through the biased, comic eyes of a narrator who
   resists him.
2. His precise, controlled actions: letters "so neat they looked printed".
3. His unusual authority: he "simply waited" and silenced the class, even Daniel.
4. The narrator's resistance: repeated "I decided" shows the effect Mr Okafor has on the narrator.
5. Ending and irony: "which should have told me something" suggests Mr Okafor will matter to the narrator;
   conclusion on how humour makes the portrait memorable.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Retelling the passage instead of analysing it.
- Missing what lies beneath the dialogue.
- Ignoring the narrator's viewpoint and the ending of the passage.
- Long quotations that are not then analysed.
