These notes condense the **mental health disorders** strand of the health and well-being context (SL and HL, first assessment 2027). The guide says to understand one or more of major depressive disorder, eating disorders, post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), obsessive-compulsive disorders or anxiety disorders. Use one disorder in depth (depression is the example here). Read the [study guide](/resources/ib-dp-psychology-2027-mental-health-disorders/), then try the [practice set](/resources/ib-dp-psychology-2027-mental-health-disorders-practice/).

## The four areas at a glance

| Area | Learning objective (summarised) | Key idea | Example evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Biological explanations | One or more explanations of one disorder | Genes, neurotransmitters, stress system, brain structure | Twin studies (Sullivan, Neale and Kendler 2000); review by Moncrieff et al. (2022) |
| Cognitive models | The value of cognitive models | Negative schemas, cognitive triad, attributional style | Beck's model; learned helplessness |
| Cultural differences | Factors explaining differences in prevalence between cultures or populations; examples of cultural differences in approaches | Risk, expression, stigma, measurement, diagnostic bias | Becker et al. (2002) Fiji; Kleinman on somatisation |
| Environmental factors | Role of environmental factors | Life events, adversity, trauma, isolation | Brown and Harris (1978); Felitti et al. (1998) |

## Key definitions

- **Mental health disorder:** a pattern of thinking, feeling or behaviour that causes significant distress or impairment, identified by a classification system.
- **Prevalence:** the proportion of a population with a disorder at a time or over a period.
- **Heritability:** the proportion of variation in a population explained by genetic differences.
- **Diathesis-stress model:** a disorder results from a vulnerability combined with stress.
- **Monoamine hypothesis:** depression is linked to low levels of monoamine neurotransmitters such as serotonin.
- **Schema:** an organised framework of beliefs from experience.
- **Cognitive triad:** negative views of the self, the world and the future.
- **Somatisation:** expressing psychological distress through bodily symptoms.
- **Imposed etic:** applying a concept from one culture to another as if it were universal.
- **Stigma:** negative labelling and discrimination.

## Biological explanations

- **Genetics:** identical twins are more alike than non-identical twins; estimates of heritability for depression are around a third to two-fifths. Not 100 per cent, so the environment matters.
- **Neurotransmitters:** the monoamine idea was supported by drugs that raise serotonin, but a large review found no consistent evidence of low serotonin as a cause. A drug working does not prove its target was the cause.
- **Stress system and brain structure:** raised cortisol and smaller hippocampal volume in some groups; correlational.
- **Evaluate:** reductionist; twin studies assume equal environments; direction of cause unclear in imaging.
- **Link:** diathesis-stress is the bridge to environmental factors.

## Cognitive models

- **Beck:** negative schemas are activated by stress; the cognitive triad; biases such as overgeneralising keep depression going.
- **Attributional style:** internal, stable, global explanations of bad events.
- **Other disorders:** anxiety (threat bias), eating disorders (distorted beliefs about weight), PTSD (intrusive memory), OCD (misinterpreting intrusive thoughts).
- **Value:** testable, practical (the basis for CBT), connects thought to feeling and behaviour.
- **Limitations:** correlational (thoughts may be a symptom), may ignore biology and social causes.

## Cultural differences

| Possible reason for different prevalence | Example |
|---|---|
| Real differences in risk | Conflict, poverty, isolation |
| Cultural expression | Somatisation in some settings |
| Stigma and help-seeking | Low reporting where stigma is high |
| Measurement and diagnostic bias | Tools made in the West applied elsewhere |
| Gender or cultural bias | Different diagnoses for similar symptoms |

- **Fiji (Becker et al., 2002):** disordered eating attitudes rose after television arrived. Natural experiment, no random allocation.
- **Approaches differ:** spiritual, family and biomedical explanations; healers and elders as well as doctors.
- **Evaluate:** prevalence data are not exactly comparable; etic research gives comparisons, emic research gives meaning.

## Environmental factors

- **Brown and Harris (1978):** severe life events and difficulties, with vulnerability when there was no close confiding relationship.
- **Childhood adversity (Felitti et al., 1998):** more adverse experiences, more risk of later problems; retrospective self-report.
- **Trauma:** required for PTSD, but not everyone exposed develops it.
- **Evaluate:** real-world evidence, but exposure cannot be randomised; memory bias.

## Linking to the concepts

- **Causality:** genes, brain differences and thoughts are mostly correlational; interaction among factors.
- **Bias:** gender and cultural bias in diagnosis; cognitive bias as a feature of the disorder.
- **Measurement:** symptom checklists, prevalence figures, self-report scales.
- **Perspective:** biological, cognitive and sociocultural accounts; etic and emic.
- **Change:** prevention can target thinking, relationships or conditions.
- **Responsibility:** avoiding stigma, careful interpretation of prevalence.

## A model answer plan (Section B style)

Scenario: a student has low mood after repeated exam failure and thinks "I will never succeed".
1. Name the model: Beck's cognitive model, negative schema and the cognitive triad (future).
2. Apply: failure activates the schema, producing negative views of self and future.
3. Add a second area: environmental stress (exams) combined with vulnerability (diathesis-stress).
4. Evaluate briefly: the link may run in both directions.

## Common mistakes

- Using treatment evidence to answer a question about explanation.
- Saying "depression is caused by low serotonin".
- Comparing prevalence between countries as if methods were identical.
- Describing a model without saying what it is useful for.
- Not naming the disorder you are using.

## Self-test

1. What does "diathesis-stress" mean?
2. Why does a drug raising serotonin not prove that low serotonin causes depression?
3. List the three parts of the cognitive triad.
4. Give two reasons why recorded prevalence of a disorder may differ between countries.
5. What type of study was Becker et al. (2002) in Fiji, and what is one limitation?
6. Name one vulnerability factor identified by Brown and Harris (1978).
7. Why is Felitti et al. (1998) open to memory bias?

**Answers:** 1. A vulnerability (genetic or other) combined with stress produces the disorder. 2. A treatment can work through effects unrelated to the original cause, and the effect may take weeks, so cause cannot be inferred. 3. Negative views of the self, the world and the future. 4. Any two: real differences in risk, cultural expression, stigma, measurement tools, diagnostic bias. 5. A natural experiment (before and after television); no random allocation, so other social changes may be involved. 6. Lack of a close confiding relationship (also loss of a mother in childhood or several young children at home). 7. Adults reported childhood experiences retrospectively, and recall may be inaccurate.
