This study guide covers **the HL essay** in IB Diploma Programme Language A: Language and
Literature. It is aligned to the IB Diploma Programme subject brief *Language A: language and
literature* and draws on all three areas of exploration: Readers, writers and texts; Time and space;
and Intertextuality: connecting texts. It follows the current IB subject brief (first assessments for
SL and HL 2021). The essay is **HL only**: SL students do not write it.

## What the subject brief says

The brief gives these facts about the HL essay, and only these:

- It is a **written coursework component**.
- It is a **1,200–1,500 word essay**.
- It is written **on one literary work or a non-literary body of work studied** in the course.
- It is worth **20% of the final grade at HL**.
- It is listed with Paper 1 and Paper 2 under **external** assessment. The individual oral is the
  internal component.

Your teacher will share the assessment criteria, the drafting and feedback rules and your school's
timeline. Ask early what counts towards the word count, and whether you may use the same work in
more than one component.

The brief's assessment objectives say what a good essay shows. You must **know,
understand and interpret** the work and its contexts; **analyse and evaluate** "ways in which the use
of language creates meaning"; and **communicate** "ideas in clear, logical and persuasive ways".

## Choosing a work

You write about one work you studied in class. The examples on this page use Charlotte Perkins
Gilman's short story "The Yellow Wall-Paper" (1892), because it is out of copyright and short enough
to check. Your own essay will be on a work your class studied, so treat Gilman as a model only.

A good choice passes four tests.

1. **You have a question about it.** Something puzzles you, or you disagree with a common reading.
2. **It has depth in one place.** You can find five or more precise moments that bear on your
   question.
3. **You can analyse it.** You can say *how* the writer's choices create effects.
4. **It fits the word limit.** 1,500 words is short. A narrow question on a rich work beats a broad
   question on anything.

If you choose a **non-literary body of work**, the same tests apply. Ask your teacher to confirm what
counts as a body of work for your class. On this page the example is a set of related texts from one
public-information campaign. Your essay then asks how the texts work *together*.

## From topic to line of inquiry

A **line of inquiry** is the single question your whole essay answers. A topic names an area. A line
of inquiry asks something arguable about it and points to *how* the writer does it.

| Level | Example on Gilman |
|---|---|
| Topic | Marriage in "The Yellow Wall-Paper" |
| Better | How is the husband presented? |
| Line of inquiry | How does Gilman use the narrator's polite, self-correcting voice to expose the control she cannot name directly? |

The third version names a feature (the narrator's voice), an effect (exposing control) and a
tension (she cannot say it outright).

### Using the three areas of exploration

The areas of exploration give three ways in:

- **Readers, writers and texts**: how the writer's choices of language, form and structure make
  meaning. *How does the first-person diary form shape what we are allowed to know?*
- **Time and space**: how the contexts in which a text was written and is read change its meaning.
  *How does a story written when "rest" was a medical instruction read to someone today?*
- **Intertextuality: connecting texts**: how a work uses, bends or answers the conventions of other
  texts and genres. *The narrator herself mentions "a haunted house". How does Gilman borrow Gothic
  conventions and then turn them towards an ordinary marriage?*

Your essay is on one work, so connecting texts here means how that work handles conventions, not a
comparison. Comparison of two works is the job of Paper 2.

### Worked example: narrowing a question

**Start:** "I want to write about control in 'The Yellow Wall-Paper'."

**Step 1: find the evidence first.** Re-read the opening pages and log every moment where the
narrator's wishes meet her husband's decisions:

- She wanted a downstairs room, "but John would not hear of it".
- When she says the house feels strange, "he said what I felt was a draught, and shut the window."
- "He is very careful and loving, and hardly lets me stir without special direction."
- "I have a schedule prescription for each hour in the day".

**Step 2: look for the pattern.** Each time, John's control arrives in the language of care. The
narrator reports it calmly, then adds a small doubt or apology: she feels "basely ungrateful not to
value it more."

**Step 3: ask the question the pattern raises.** *How does Gilman make John's control sound like
kindness, and how does the narrator's voice let the reader see through it?*

**Step 4: write a working thesis.** Gilman presents John's control through the vocabulary of love
and medicine, so the narrator cannot resist it without seeming ungrateful; her apologetic diary
voice records each restriction faithfully, which lets the reader judge what she cannot.

That thesis can be argued in three body sections: the language of care; the narrator's
self-correction; and the room as a physical form of the same control.

## Research and evidence

Your main evidence is the work itself. Build an **evidence log** as you re-read. For each entry note:

- the exact quotation, copied carefully, with page or line reference from your class edition;
- the feature (word choice, syntax, narrative voice, image, layout, sound);
- the effect on meaning, in one sentence;
- which part of your thesis it supports.

Secondary sources, such as criticism or background on the period, are optional support. Read them
after your own reading, so the argument stays yours. If a critic's idea
shapes your point, credit it, even if you do not quote it. Record full details of every source the
moment you use it: author, title, publisher or website, date, and page.

For a non-literary body of work, log features across the texts: where the same slogan, image, form
of address or statistic appears, and how its effect changes from one text to the next.

## Drafting

Plan to the word limit before you write. A worked budget for a 1,380-word target:

| Part | Words |
|---|---|
| Introduction | 160 |
| Three body sections × 360 | 1,080 |
| Conclusion | 140 |
| **Total** | **1,380** |

That leaves 120 words of room under 1,500. When you cut, cut summary and repetition, not analysis.

A workable sequence:

1. Write the thesis and a one-line claim for each body section.
2. Draft the body first; write the introduction last.
3. Read the draft against your line of inquiry. Delete any paragraph that does not answer it.
4. Check every quotation against the text, word for word.
5. Revise for precision: replace "shows" and "portrays" with verbs that say what the language does
   (undercuts, softens, repeats, withholds).
6. Use the feedback your teacher gives within your school's process, then make the final version
   entirely your own work.

## Structure

**Introduction.** Name the work and writer, state the line of inquiry, and give your thesis. Add
only the context the argument needs. Two or three sentences of plot summary are the most you need.

**Body paragraphs.** Each one makes a claim that moves the thesis forward. A reliable build:

1. **Claim**: one sentence, linked to the line of inquiry.
2. **Evidence**: a short embedded quotation.
3. **Analysis**: what the specific words, syntax or form do, and why that matters.
4. **Development**: a second piece of evidence, a contrast, or a wider effect.
5. **Link**: how this moves the argument on.

**Conclusion.** Answer the line of inquiry directly. Say what the argument adds up to. Do not bring
in new evidence.

### Model body paragraph (original text written for Marlbridge)

> Gilman makes John's control almost invisible by dressing it in the language of care. When the
> narrator tells him the house feels strange, "he said what I felt was a draught, and shut the
> window." The sentence is flat and orderly, and its two clauses give John both the diagnosis and the
> action. Her feeling is translated into a physical cause and then shut out, literally. Because the
> narrator reports this without comment, the reader supplies the judgement she withholds: her
> perception has been corrected, not heard. The same pattern returns when she calls him "very
> careful and loving" in the same breath as admitting that he "hardly lets me stir without special
> direction". Love and restriction sit side by side in one sentence, and the narrator never lets
> herself separate them. That refusal is the story's quiet argument: control that sounds like
> kindness is the hardest kind to name.

The claim answers the line of inquiry, the quotations are short and embedded, each is followed by
analysis of specific words or syntax, and the last sentence moves the argument on.

## Referencing

- **Quote exactly.** Copy spelling, punctuation and capitals from your edition. Mark any word you
  leave out with an ellipsis (…) and any word you add with square brackets.
- **Embed quotations.** Make them part of your grammar: *She calls him "very careful and loving"*.
  Avoid dropping a quotation in as its own sentence.
- **Keep quotations short.** A few words that carry the feature you analyse are stronger than a
  long passage.
- **Give a location.** Add the page (or line, or text number for a body of work) in the style your
  school uses, such as MLA or APA.
- **List your sources.** End with a works cited or bibliography list that includes the work itself
  and every secondary source.
- **Credit ideas too.** Paraphrasing a critic without a citation is still using their work.

## Common errors

- **A topic, not a question.** "Women in the story" gives you nothing to prove. Ask *how* and *why*.
- **Retelling the plot.** If a paragraph would make sense to someone who has not read the work, it
  is probably summary.
- **Naming a technique without its effect.** "Gilman uses repetition" earns little until you say
  which words repeat and what the repetition does.
- **Context as a history lesson.** Use context only when it changes how a line is read.
- **Mis-quoting.** One changed word can alter the meaning you analyse. Check every quotation.
- **Going over 1,500 words.** Plan to a budget and cut summary first.
