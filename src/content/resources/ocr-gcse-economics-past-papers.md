This page is a guide to OCR GCSE (9-1) Economics (J205) past papers from June 2024 to June 2026. For each series you get the grade thresholds, copied from OCR's grade-boundary documents, and for June 2025 and June 2024 a short account of what the examiners saw, written by us from OCR's examiner reports. We do not host any papers. Every paper, mark scheme and report is on OCR's own site or its teacher platform.

- [OCR grade boundaries page](https://www.ocr.org.uk/administration/grade-boundaries/)

OCR publishes question papers and mark schemes through its past paper finder, and grade boundaries on the page above. Examiner reports for each series are listed on the assessment page for the qualification. The full question papers and mark schemes that the reports refer to are held on Teach Cambridge, OCR's teacher site, so ask your teacher or exams officer if you cannot open them.

Use the three documents together. Sit the paper first, under timed conditions. Mark it strictly with the mark scheme, which tells you what each mark needs. Then read the examiner report for that paper: it tells you why answers that looked right still lost marks. Our notes for each series below are a quick way into that report.

Check which specification a paper was set on. J205 is a linear qualification, first assessed in 2019 and still current, and it is not tiered. Paper 1 (J205/01, Introduction to Economics) and Paper 2 (J205/02, National and International Economics) are each 80 marks, 1 hour 30 minutes and 50% of the qualification, so 160 marks in total. Candidates sit both. In the reports we read, each paper has a Section A of 20 multiple-choice questions and a Section B of three case-study questions, each made up of four 2-mark parts and two 6-mark parts.

## June 2025

The June 2025 reports describe a paper that most candidates found accessible, with solid recall of basic terms and good use of data on Paper 2. The difference between grades lay in reading the question fully, drawing and using diagrams, and evaluation that matched the point before it.

**Where marks were lost – J205/01 (Introduction to Economics)**

- Reading the question: some candidates fastened on one or two words and wrote answers that were only marginally relevant. A few appeared to run out of time, and some left the extended parts blank.
- Multiple choice: scarcity, competitive against oligopolistic markets, the importance of time in supply, price elasticity and the role of price were the weaker areas. Candidates who changed an answer were more likely to swap a right answer for a wrong one, and some answers were not written clearly.
- Price elasticity of supply: many candidates confused elastic with inelastic supply, wrote about demand instead, or described the idea without saying that it compares percentage changes.
- Terminology: factor and product markets were confused with the primary, secondary and tertiary sectors, and cost and price were used as if they were the same.
- Diagrams: marks were lost for diagrams that were poorly drawn or unlabelled, for showing a movement along a curve when a shift was needed, and for shifting the wrong curve.
- Six-mark answers: candidates answered about the business instead of the consumer, or about tickets and concerts instead of the number of workers asked about. Evaluation was often a summary of earlier points, and evaluation on a question about competition needed a view on how far it helps consumers.
- Arithmetic and units: percentage slips, and answers that left out the word million.

**Where marks were lost – J205/02 (National and International Economics)**

- Multiple choice: government spending was confused with debt interest, the costs of globalisation were mis-chosen, and some candidates did not see that a question asked for an effect on the whole economy.
- Units: the pound sign, percentage sign or the word million were often left off numerical answers.
- Data questions: candidates described trade in goods rising when the data showed a deficit, did not use the terms deficit and surplus, or rewrote the case-study material with no analysis.
- Confusions: the trade balance was mixed up with government finances, and exchange rates with interest rates. Some candidates used a macroeconomic diagram for a taxation question that needed a market diagram, and thought indirect taxes shift demand.
- Explanations: a reason was often given without being explained, for example how a subsidy works.
- Evaluation: the usual loss was a point that did not match the point before it (for example, opportunity cost mentioned with no link to the benefit just analysed), or a conclusion that only summarised. Some candidates analysed problems when the question asked for benefits.
- GDP per capita was sometimes treated as a measure of wealth distribution, or as a figure per household or region.

## June 2024

The June 2024 reports again single out terminology and evaluation. Candidates scored well when they used the case study in a chain of reasoning and finished with a justified judgement, and poorly when they rewrote the source, asserted rather than explained, or left out symbols such as the percentage sign.

**Where marks were lost – J205/01 (Introduction to Economics)**

- Multiple choice: several items were missed because a key word in the question was overlooked, and others showed gaps on net pay, the difference between production and productivity, falling prices in a housing market and the role of the central bank.
- Definitions: a definition of a fall in supply was often a rephrasing of the question, and oligopoly was confused with monopoly. A comparison with a competitive market needed a precise difference, such as how many firms there are.
- Mixing up related terms: oil and petrol were used as if they were the same, price elastic and price inelastic were confused, and revenue and profit were used interchangeably.
- Diagrams: up to a tenth of the marks depended on drawing an accurate, labelled diagram. Some answers referred to only one of demand and supply when both were asked for, or did not link the diagram to the written analysis.
- Scarcity and trend questions: candidates described oil as limited without linking that to price, and repeated the data month by month instead of stating the trend and its size.
- Six-mark answers: judgements were often just concluding statements, and the type of evaluation needed changed from one case study to the next.
- Applying knowledge: candidates could say what profit is but not what it is used for, and answers that did not use the facts of the business stayed in the lower levels. Product market was explained vaguely, and a small number drew demand curves that shifted the wrong way or sloped upwards.

**Where marks were lost – J205/02 (National and International Economics)**

- Multiple choice: a bold instruction in a question was missed, and rates of change against absolute changes caught some candidates.
- Explaining against describing: candidates described the direction of unemployment rather than giving a reason, and described a government budget as bigger or smaller rather than a smaller deficit or a move towards surplus.
- Misreading the question: some wrote about how unemployment was brought down rather than its effect on the budget, and some confused higher unemployment with fewer workers being available.
- Units: the percentage sign was often left off calculated answers, and a fifth of the population in the income data was misidentified.
- Taxation: the key property of a progressive tax, that it takes a higher percentage of income as people earn more, was missed, and the link from tax revenue to help for lower earners was often incomplete.
- Analyse and evaluate questions: evaluation was written where only analysis was asked for, and a pair of points (one good, one bad) was not enough on its own to count as evaluation. A bare word such as inflation was too vague for an economic objective.
- Interest rates and the exchange rate: the direction of the effect on the supply of pounds was sometimes confused.

## What examiners keep saying

Across the two series with reports, the same points come up on both papers.

- Read the whole question and the extract. Underline who or what the question is about (the consumer, the owners, the number of workers) and keep your answer there.
- Be precise. Price inelastic demand, a deficit and a surplus, factor and product markets, and revenue and profit are all pairs that were mixed up.
- Include units and symbols: the pound sign, the percentage sign and the word million.
- Draw the diagram when asked, label it, and shift the right curve. Use it in your writing.
- In the six-mark questions, build a chain of reasoning from the data. For evaluation, make each evaluative point answer the point before it, and finish with a judgement that adds something rather than repeating your answer.
- Write your multiple-choice answers clearly and do not leave any blank.

## Frequently asked questions

**Where can I find OCR GCSE Economics (J205) past papers?**
OCR publishes question papers and mark schemes through its past paper finder. Grade boundaries are on OCR's grade boundaries page, and examiner reports are listed on the assessment page for the qualification.

**Which J205 papers should I sit?**
Both. Paper 1 (Introduction to Economics) and Paper 2 (National and International Economics) are compulsory, each 80 marks and 1 hour 30 minutes. There are no tiers.

**How are the grade thresholds used?**
The overall grade comes from adding the two paper marks, out of 160, and comparing the total with that series' boundaries. The paper rows show how each paper performed on its own.

**What mistakes do J205 examiners report most often?**
Not reading the question fully, mixing up related terms such as elastic and inelastic or deficit and surplus, leaving out units, and evaluation that only summarises.

**Can grade thresholds tell me what mark I need next time?**
No. Thresholds change every series and should only be used as a rough guide for the series they belong to, never to predict future ones.
