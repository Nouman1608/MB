These questions cover **3.1.6.4 Effect of concentration and pressure** of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Chemistry (9620) specification, Version 5.3. It is International AS content, assessed in Unit 2. All experimental data are invented for practice. Use R = 8.31 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ and a molar gas volume of 24.0 dm³ mol⁻¹ at room conditions.

## Section A

**1.** State what is meant by the term *rate of reaction*. **[1]**

**2.** State what is meant by *collision frequency*. **[1]**

**3.** Explain why marble chips react faster with 2.0 mol dm⁻³ hydrochloric acid than with 0.5 mol dm⁻³ hydrochloric acid at the same temperature. **[3]**

**4.** Explain why the rate of a reaction decreases as the reaction proceeds. **[2]**

**5.** A student writes: "A more concentrated solution reacts faster because its particles have more energy, so a larger proportion of collisions succeed." Identify **two** errors in this statement. **[2]**

**6.** Sodium thiosulfate solution reacts with dilute hydrochloric acid. Explain why changing the pressure above the reaction mixture has no significant effect on the rate. **[1]**

## Section B

**7.** Hydrogen and nitrogen monoxide react in the gas phase at 300 K.

**(a)** Calculate the amount, in mol, of gas molecules in 1.00 dm³ of a gas at 100 kPa and 300 K. **[2]**

**(b)** The mixture is compressed at 300 K until its pressure is 400 kPa. State the factor by which the amount of each gas per dm³ increases. **[1]**

**(c)** Explain the effect of this compression on the rate of reaction. **[3]**

**8.** A student adds 0.0729 g of magnesium ribbon (Ar = 24.3) to 40.0 cm³ of 0.800 mol dm⁻³ hydrochloric acid and measures the volume of hydrogen with a gas syringe.

Mg(s) + 2HCl(aq) → MgCl₂(aq) + H₂(g)

**(a)** Calculate the amount, in mol, of magnesium. **[1]**

**(b)** Show that the hydrochloric acid is in excess. **[2]**

**(c)** Calculate the final volume of hydrogen, in cm³, at room conditions. **[1]**

**(d)** The experiment is repeated with 40.0 cm³ of 0.400 mol dm⁻³ acid and the same mass of magnesium. Describe how the volume–time curve differs from the first, and explain the difference in the initial gradient. **[2]**

**(e)** The experiment is repeated again with only 10.0 cm³ of 0.400 mol dm⁻³ acid. Calculate the new final volume of hydrogen and state how the initial gradient compares with that in (d). **[2]**

**9.** 3.00 g of powdered calcium carbonate (Mr = 100.1) is added to 25.0 cm³ of hydrochloric acid on a balance, and the loss in mass is followed.

CaCO₃(s) + 2HCl(aq) → CaCl₂(aq) + H₂O(l) + CO₂(g)

Experiment P uses 2.00 mol dm⁻³ acid; experiment Q uses 1.00 mol dm⁻³ acid.

**(a)** Show that the acid is the limiting reagent in both experiments. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the total mass of carbon dioxide (Mr = 44.0) lost in each experiment. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain why the initial rate of mass loss is greater in P than in Q. **[3]**

**10.** A mixture of two reacting gases is held in a sealed steel vessel of fixed volume at constant temperature.

**(a)** Argon, which does not react, is pumped into the vessel, so the total pressure doubles. Explain why the rate of reaction does not change. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain how the rate would change if instead the original mixture were transferred to a vessel of half the volume at the same temperature. **[2]**

**11.** A Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution is drawn for the gas molecules in 1 dm³ of a reaction mixture. The concentration of the gas is then doubled at constant temperature. Describe how the curve changes, and explain why the rate increases even though the proportion of molecules with energy at least Ea is unchanged. **[3]**

**12.** Compare how an increase in temperature and an increase in concentration each increase the rate of a reaction in solution. **[4]**

**13.** A student investigates the reaction between sodium thiosulfate and hydrochloric acid by timing how long a cross under the flask takes to disappear. The acid concentration and the total volume are kept constant. Results (invented):

| [Na₂S₂O₃] / mol dm⁻³ | 0.050 | 0.100 | 0.150 | 0.200 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Time, t / s | 96 | 48 | 33 | 24 |

**(a)** The value of 1/t can be used as a measure of the rate. Calculate 1/t, in s⁻¹, for the 0.050 and 0.200 mol dm⁻³ solutions. **[2]**

**(b)** Describe the trend shown by the results and explain it in terms of collisions. **[2]**

**(c)** State **two** variables, other than the acid concentration and total volume, that the student should keep constant. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** The change in concentration (or amount) of a reactant or product per unit time [1]

**2.** The number of collisions between reactant particles per unit time (in a given volume) [1]

**3.** The more concentrated acid has more H⁺ ions per unit volume [1]; so collisions between H⁺ ions and the marble surface are more frequent [1]; so more collisions with energy at least Ea occur per second, giving a higher rate [1]
*Examiner insight:* "More collisions" without "per second" or "more frequent" does not show a rate.

**4.** Reactants are used up, so their concentrations fall [1]; collisions become less frequent, so there are fewer successful collisions per unit time [1]

**5.** The particles do **not** have more energy: energy depends on temperature, not concentration [1]; the **proportion** of successful collisions is unchanged; it is the **frequency** of collisions (successful ones included) that rises [1]

**6.** The reactants are in solution; liquids hardly compress, so pressure does not change their concentrations or collision frequency [1]

**7. (a)** n/V = p/(RT) = 100 000 ÷ (8.31 × 300) = 40.1 mol m⁻³ [1]; = **0.0401 mol** in 1.00 dm³ [1]

**(b)** **4** (four times) [1]

**(c)** The same molecules occupy a smaller volume, so there are more molecules per unit volume [1]; the molecules collide more frequently [1]; more collisions with energy at least Ea occur per second, so the rate increases [1]
*Examiner insight:* The temperature is constant, so do not mention extra energy.

**8. (a)** n(Mg) = 0.0729 ÷ 24.3 = **3.00 × 10⁻³ mol** [1]

**(b)** HCl needed = 2 × 3.00 × 10⁻³ = 6.00 × 10⁻³ mol [1]; HCl present = 0.800 × 0.0400 = 0.0320 mol, which is more than needed [1]

**(c)** n(H₂) = 3.00 × 10⁻³ mol; volume = 3.00 × 10⁻³ × 24 000 = **72.0 cm³** [1]

**(d)** The curve starts less steeply but levels off at the same final volume, 72.0 cm³ (0.0160 mol HCl is still in excess) [1]; the lower acid concentration means fewer H⁺ ions per unit volume, so less frequent collisions with the magnesium [1]

**(e)** n(HCl) = 0.400 × 0.0100 = 4.00 × 10⁻³ mol, now limiting; n(H₂) = 2.00 × 10⁻³ mol, so final volume = **48.0 cm³** [1]; initial gradient is the **same** as in (d), because the acid concentration is the same [1]
*Examiner insight:* Volume of acid changes the amount, not the concentration. Only concentration sets the starting rate here.

**9. (a)** HCl needed for all the CaCO₃ = 2 × (3.00 ÷ 100.1) = 2 × 0.0300 = 0.0599 mol [1]; P has 2.00 × 0.0250 = 0.0500 mol and Q has 1.00 × 0.0250 = 0.0250 mol, both less than 0.0599 mol, so the acid is limiting in both [1]

**(b)** P: n(CO₂) = 0.0500 ÷ 2 = 0.0250 mol, mass = **1.10 g** [1]; Q: n(CO₂) = 0.0125 mol, mass = **0.550 g** [1]

**(c)** The acid in P has twice as many H⁺ ions per unit volume [1]; so collisions between H⁺ ions and the calcium carbonate surface are more frequent [1]; so more successful collisions occur per second [1]

**10. (a)** The amount of each reacting gas per unit volume is unchanged (the volume and the amounts of reactants are the same) [1]; so the collision frequency between reactant molecules, and the rate, are unchanged; only the total pressure rises [1]

**(b)** The concentration of each reacting gas doubles [1]; collisions between reactant molecules become more frequent, so the rate increases [1]

**11.** The curve is twice as tall at every energy, with the peak at the same energy (same shape) [1]; the number of molecules with energy at least Ea per dm³ doubles, even though the fraction does not [1]; so more collisions with energy at least Ea occur per second, and the rate increases [1]

**12.** Temperature: the molecules gain kinetic energy, so a much larger proportion have energy at least Ea [1]; collisions also become slightly more frequent [1]. Concentration: more particles per unit volume make collisions more frequent [1]; the proportion of collisions with energy at least Ea is unchanged [1]
*Examiner insight:* A comparison needs both factors and the difference between them: temperature mainly changes the energy fraction, concentration changes only the frequency.

**13. (a)** 0.050 mol dm⁻³: 1/96 = **0.0104 s⁻¹** [1]; 0.200 mol dm⁻³: 1/24 = **0.0417 s⁻¹** [1]

**(b)** As the thiosulfate concentration increases, the time falls and the rate (1/t) increases [1]; more thiosulfate ions per unit volume give more frequent collisions, so more successful collisions per second [1]

**(c)** Any two from: temperature [1]; the same cross and flask (depth of solution) or same observer [1]; the volume of acid used
*Examiner insight:* The question asks for qualitative interpretation. Do not claim a mathematical relationship from four invented points; that analysis belongs to rate equations.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Missing the time link ("per second", "more frequent") in a collision explanation.
- Saying concentration or pressure gives particles extra energy.
- Applying a pressure argument to a reaction in solution.
- Predicting the final amount of product without checking the limiting reagent.
- Confusing the volume of a solution with its concentration.
