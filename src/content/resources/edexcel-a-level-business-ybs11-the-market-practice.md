## Sources

> **Source 1: Aldmere Oils**
>
> Aldmere Oils buys olive oil in bulk and bottles it under its own brand
> for supermarkets. In its national market, weekly demand is
> Qd = 1,200 − 40P and supply is Qs = 200 + 60P, where P is the price per
> litre in $ and Q is thousands of litres. A drought in the main growing
> regions has cut the harvest, and supply has become Qs = −100 + 60P.
> Aldmere then raised the price of its 1-litre bottle from $8.00 to $9.20,
> and monthly sales fell from 50,000 to 46,000 bottles. Aldmere's oil has
> won several taste awards, and many customers buy it every week. The
> government is considering a subsidy for each litre bottled locally.
>
> **Source 2: Paviour Travel**
>
> Paviour Travel sells luxury guided tours at $1,800 (6,000 bookings a
> year) and budget coach day trips at $150 (20,000 bookings a year).
> Market research estimates the YED of luxury tours at +2.2 and of budget
> coach trips at −0.5, and the PED of luxury tours at −1.4. Average
> incomes in Paviour's main market are forecast to fall by 3% next year.

## Section A

Questions 1 to 6 refer to Source 1.

**1.** Define the term "external shock". **[2]**

**2.** Calculate the equilibrium price and quantity in the olive oil market before and after the drought. **[4]**

**3.** Calculate the price elasticity of demand for Aldmere's 1-litre bottle. **[4]**

**4.** Explain why Aldmere's revenue from the 1-litre bottle rose after the price increase. **[4]**

**5.** Explain **one** factor that may make demand for Aldmere's oil price inelastic. **[4]**

**6.** Analyse the likely effects on the olive oil market of a government subsidy for each litre bottled locally. **[6]**

## Section B

Questions 7 to 12 refer to Source 2.

**7.** Define the term "inferior good". **[2]**

**8.** Calculate the expected change in annual revenue from luxury tours if incomes fall by 3%, assuming prices do not change. **[4]**

**9.** Explain why demand for Paviour's budget coach trips may have a negative YED. **[4]**

**10.** Analyse how Paviour could use its YED estimates to plan for the forecast fall in incomes. **[6]**

**11.** Discuss whether Paviour should cut the price of its luxury tours by 5% next year to protect revenue. **[8]**

**12.** Assess the usefulness to Paviour Travel of its PED and YED estimates. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** An unexpected event outside the control of businesses [1] that suddenly changes demand or supply in a market, such as a drought cutting a harvest [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Before: 1,200 − 40P = 200 + 60P, so 1,000 = 100P, giving **P = $10** [1] and Q = 1,200 − 400 = **800 thousand litres** [1]. After: 1,200 − 40P = −100 + 60P, so 1,300 = 100P, giving **P = $13** [1] and Q = 1,200 − 520 = **680 thousand litres** [1]. **[4]**

**3.** % change in quantity = (46,000 − 50,000) ÷ 50,000 × 100 = **−8%** [1]. % change in price = (9.20 − 8.00) ÷ 8.00 × 100 = **+15%** [1]. PED = −8 ÷ 15 [1] = **−0.53** (to 2 d.p.) [1]. **[4]**

**4.** Demand is price inelastic (PED −0.53) [1], so the 15% price rise was proportionally larger than the 8% fall in sales [1]. Revenue rose from 50,000 × $8.00 = $400,000 [1] to 46,000 × $9.20 = $423,200 a month, an increase of $23,200 [1]. **[4]**

**5.** Brand loyalty [1]: Aldmere's taste awards suggest customers see it as better than other oils [1], and many buy it every week out of habit [1], so they are reluctant to switch when its price rises, which keeps the fall in sales small [1]. *(Also creditable: all rivals face the same drought-driven cost rise, so there are few cheaper substitutes; oil takes a small share of a household's income.)* **[4]**

**6.** A subsidy lowers bottlers' costs per litre [1], so supply shifts to the right [1]. At the old price there would be a surplus, so the equilibrium price falls [1] and the equilibrium quantity rises [1]. This would partly offset the drought, which shifted supply left [1], helping Aldmere sell more bottles, although supermarkets may push for the lower cost to be passed on, so Aldmere's margin may not rise by the full subsidy [1]. **[6]**

**7.** A good for which demand falls as consumers' incomes rise [1], so it has a negative income elasticity of demand [1]. **[2]**

**8.** % change in bookings = YED × % change in income = 2.2 × −3 = **−6.6%** [1]. New bookings = 6,000 × 0.934 = **5,604** [1]. Revenue falls from 6,000 × $1,800 = $10,800,000 to 5,604 × $1,800 = $10,087,200 [1], a fall of **$712,800** [1]. **[4]**

**9.** As incomes rise, customers can afford to replace a day trip with a longer or more comfortable holiday [1], so demand for the budget trip falls [1]. When incomes fall, households give up more expensive holidays and choose cheap day trips instead [1], so demand rises as income falls, which is what a negative YED shows [1]. **[4]**

**10.** With a 3% fall in income, luxury bookings are predicted to fall 6.6% [1], so Paviour can reduce the number of luxury tours it books with hotels and guides, avoiding paying for unused capacity [1]. Budget trips are predicted to rise by 0.5 × 3 = 1.5%, to about 20,300 bookings [1], so Paviour can plan extra coaches and drivers for them [1]. It could also shift marketing spending towards budget trips [1], helping to offset part of the lost luxury revenue, since the gain of about $45,000 on budget trips is far smaller than the $712,800 lost on luxury tours [1]. **[6]**

**11.** **For:** luxury demand is price elastic (PED −1.4), so a 5% cut should raise bookings by about 7% [1]. Starting from the forecast 5,604 bookings, that gives about 5,996 bookings at $1,710, so revenue of about $10.25m rather than $10.09m [1]. This would protect revenue and keep guides and partner hotels busy during a downturn [1].
**Against:** revenue is not profit: each extra tour carries costs such as hotels and guides [1], so the extra $166,000 of revenue may not cover the extra costs [1]. A price cut may also weaken the luxury image that justifies a $1,800 price [1], and customers may expect the lower price to stay once incomes recover [1].
**Judgement:** a targeted offer, such as discounts on tours that are not yet full, may protect revenue with less risk to the brand than a general cut; the decision depends on the cost of each additional booking [1]. **[8]**

**12.** **Useful:** the YED estimates let Paviour forecast that luxury bookings fall about 6.6% when incomes fall 3% [1], so it can plan capacity and cash flow in advance [1]. They show the two products move in opposite directions, so the portfolio partly protects Paviour in a downturn [1]. The PED estimate shows that a price cut would raise luxury revenue [1], which helps it decide how to respond [1].
**Limitations:** the estimates come from market research, so they may be inaccurate, especially if the sample was small [1]; elasticities can change over time, for example if new rivals appear [1]; the income forecast itself may be wrong [1]; the estimates ignore other factors such as rivals' prices or exchange rates [1].
**Judgement:** the estimates are useful as a starting point for planning, but Paviour should update them often and test them against actual bookings rather than base large decisions on them alone [1]. **[10]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Solving for price and then forgetting to substitute back to find quantity.
- Calculating percentage changes from the new figure rather than the original.
- Saying a subsidy shifts demand, or that a drought shifts demand.
- Multiplying YED by the income change but forgetting the sign, so luxury bookings appear to rise.
- Treating a revenue gain as a profit gain without considering the cost of extra bookings.
- Accepting research estimates without questioning how reliable they are.
