## Questions

**1.** (a) Calculate the pH of a solution with a hydrogen ion concentration of 5.0 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the hydrogen ion concentration of a buffer at pH 9.0. **[1]**
**(c)** How many times greater is the hydrogen ion concentration at pH 5 than at pH 8? **[1]**

**2.** Describe how, according to the induced-fit model, an enzyme lowers the activation energy of a reaction. **[4]**

**3.** A mutation changes one amino acid in the active site of an enzyme and the enzyme stops working. Explain why. **[3]**

**4.** A protease from a bacterium that lives in hot springs was mixed with a cloudy protein suspension at different temperatures. The time taken for the suspension to clear was recorded.

| Temperature (°C) | 40 | 50 | 60 | 70 | 80 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Time to clear (s) | 300 | 150 | 75 | 50 | 120 |

**(a)** Calculate the rate at 70 °C as 1 ÷ time, in s⁻¹. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage change in rate between 70 °C and 80 °C. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain the changes in rate from 40 °C to 70 °C and from 70 °C to 80 °C. **[3]**

**5.** One enzyme reaction was run three ways: without inhibitor, with inhibitor X and with inhibitor Y. Rates are in arbitrary units.

| Substrate concentration | 0.5 | 1 | 2 | 4 | 8 | 16 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| No inhibitor | 1.6 | 2.7 | 4.0 | 5.3 | 6.4 | 7.1 |
| Inhibitor X | 0.6 | 1.1 | 2.0 | 3.2 | 4.6 | 5.8 |
| Inhibitor Y | 0.8 | 1.3 | 2.0 | 2.7 | 3.2 | 3.6 |

**(a)** Identify which inhibitor is competitive and which is non-competitive. Use calculations of percentage reduction in rate to support your answer. **[4]**
**(b)** Explain how the non-competitive inhibitor reduces the rate. **[2]**

**6.** A student has a 3.0% enzyme stock and wants 20 cm³ of each diluted solution.
**(a)** Calculate the volume of stock and the volume of water needed for a 1.5% solution. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why every solution must have the same final volume. **[1]**
**(c)** With excess substrate, predict the shape of a graph of rate against enzyme concentration. **[1]**

**7.** A student followed the formation of a product at a fixed temperature.

| Time (s) | 0 | 15 | 30 | 45 | 60 | 90 | 120 | 180 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Product (mmol dm⁻³) | 0.00 | 0.23 | 0.39 | 0.51 | 0.59 | 0.69 | 0.74 | 0.79 |

A tangent drawn at time 0 passes through (0, 0.00) and (30, 0.53).
**(a)** Calculate the initial rate, to 2 significant figures, with units. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the mean rate over the first 60 s, to 2 significant figures. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain why the mean rate is lower than the initial rate. **[3]**

**8.** (a) Compare the lock-and-key model with the induced-fit model of enzyme action. **[3]**
**(b)** State whether each enzyme works inside or outside the cell that made it: (i) catalase in a liver cell; (ii) amylase in saliva. **[2]**

**9.** Plan an investigation of how pH affects the speed at which catalase breaks down hydrogen peroxide, giving the variable you would measure and how you would use your results. **[7]**

**10.** A student measured the volume of oxygen from a catalase reaction. Each syringe reading has an uncertainty of ±0.5 cm³, so a volume found by difference has an uncertainty of ±1.0 cm³.
**(a)** The student collected 8.0 cm³. Calculate the percentage uncertainty. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage uncertainty if 20.0 cm³ were collected with the same syringe. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain why the student should repeat each measurement. **[2]**

**11.** Pepsin, a protease secreted into the stomach, is fully active at pH 2.0. Tested at pH 6.8 it showed no activity.
**(a)** Calculate the hydrogen ion concentration at pH 2.0 and at pH 6.8. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate how many times greater the concentration is at pH 2.0, to 2 significant figures. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain, using tertiary structure, why pepsin has no activity at pH 6.8. **[4]**
**(d)** Explain why pepsin is classified as an extracellular enzyme. **[1]**

## Answers

**1.** (a) pH = -log₁₀(5.0 × 10⁻⁵) = **4.3** [1]
**(b)** [H⁺] = 10⁻⁹·⁰ = **1.0 × 10⁻⁹ mol dm⁻³** [1]
**(c)** Three pH units, so 10³ = **1000 times** [1]
*Examiner insight:* Show the substitution into the formula and give the unit for concentration, so that the working is easy to follow. **[3]**

**2.** The substrate attaches at the active site, giving an enzyme-substrate complex [1]. Before binding, the active site is not an exact fit [1]. It shifts slightly in shape during binding and wraps around the substrate [1]. This strains bonds in the substrate (or holds two substrates close together), so less energy is needed to start the reaction [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Answers gain credit for saying the active site changes shape, not just that substrate fits. State the effect on activation energy at the end.

**3.** The amino acid sequence (primary structure) determines the tertiary structure [1]. A different amino acid changes the shape or charge of the active site [1]. The substrate is no longer complementary, so an enzyme-substrate complex cannot form [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Link the sequence to tertiary structure, then to the active site. Denaturation is a different process, so writing "it was denatured" does not answer this question.

**4.** (a) 1 ÷ 50 = **0.020 s⁻¹** [1]
**(b)** Rate at 80 °C = 1 ÷ 120 = 0.0083 s⁻¹ [1]. Change = (0.0083 - 0.020) ÷ 0.020 × 100 = **-58%** [1]
**(c)** Up to 70 °C, more kinetic energy gives more collisions and more enzyme-substrate complexes per second [1]. Above 70 °C, bonds holding the tertiary structure break [1]. The active site changes shape, and some enzyme molecules are denatured, so the rate falls [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* A percentage change needs a sign or the word fall. Explain both halves of a rise-then-fall curve, because a one-sided explanation can only earn part of the marks.

**5.** (a) X at substrate concentration 0.5: (1.6 - 0.6) ÷ 1.6 × 100 = 62.5% [1]. At 16: (7.1 - 5.8) ÷ 7.1 × 100 = 18% [1]. X has a smaller effect as substrate rises and is heading for the same maximum, so X is **competitive** [1]. Y: 50% at 0.5 and about 49% at 16, an almost constant percentage reduction with a lower plateau, so Y is **non-competitive** [1]
**(b)** It binds to a site other than the active site [1]. This alters the tertiary structure and the shape of the active site, so fewer substrates can bind, and extra substrate cannot overcome this [1] **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Quote figures from the table for both inhibitors. Saying only "the curve is lower" gives no evidence for the type.

**6.** (a) Stock volume = (1.5 ÷ 3.0) × 20 = **10.0 cm³** [1]. Water = 20 - 10.0 = **10.0 cm³** [1]
**(b)** The same final volume keeps the substrate concentration the same, so enzyme concentration is the only variable changed [1]
**(c)** A straight line through the origin (rate proportional to enzyme concentration) [1] **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Both the stock volume and the water volume are asked for, so give both. A graph prediction should say where the line passes.

**7.** (a) Gradient = 0.53 ÷ 30 = 0.01767 [1]. Rate = **0.018 mmol dm⁻³ s⁻¹** [1]
**(b)** Mean rate = 0.59 ÷ 60 = **0.0098 mmol dm⁻³ s⁻¹** [1]
**(c)** The rate is greatest at time 0, because substrate is most plentiful then [1]. The rate falls as substrate is used up (and product builds up) [1]. The mean averages this decrease, so it is lower than the rate at time 0 [1] **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Give the unit and the stated number of significant figures. For the explanation, link the falling gradient to the changing substrate concentration.

**8.** (a) In lock and key, the active site is a rigid shape that is complementary to the substrate [1]. In induced fit, the active site changes shape as the substrate binds [1]. This puts strain on the substrate and further lowers activation energy, which lock and key does not explain [1]
**(b)** (i) Inside (intracellular) [1] (ii) outside (extracellular), because it is secreted by the salivary glands [1] **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* A comparison needs both models side by side, saying what each does.

**9.** Independent variable: pH, using at least five buffer solutions across a range of values [1]. Dependent variable: the volume of oxygen collected, using a gas syringe or an inverted measuring cylinder, recorded at set time intervals [1]. Control: the same volume and concentration of hydrogen peroxide, the same volume of catalase source, and a constant temperature in a water bath [1]. Repeat at each pH and calculate a mean [1]. Plot volume against time for each pH and draw a tangent at time 0 to find the initial rate [1]. Plot the initial rate against pH [1]. Safety: wear eye protection because hydrogen peroxide can irritate [1] **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* A plan should say how each variable is kept constant. Naming a control variable without saying how it is controlled is less secure than giving the method.

**10.** (a) 1.0 ÷ 8.0 [1] × 100 = **12.5%** [1]
**(b)** 1.0 ÷ 20.0 × 100 = **5.0%** [1]
**(c)** Repeats show whether results are reliable and let an anomaly be identified and left out [1]. A mean reduces the effect of random error [1] **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Percentage uncertainty is absolute uncertainty divided by the measurement, not the other way round. Larger readings from the same apparatus give a smaller percentage uncertainty.

**11.** (a) [H⁺] at pH 2.0 = **1.0 × 10⁻² mol dm⁻³** [1]. At pH 6.8 = **1.6 × 10⁻⁷ mol dm⁻³** [1]
**(b)** (1.0 × 10⁻²) ÷ (1.6 × 10⁻⁷) = **6.3 × 10⁴ times** [1]
**(c)** The pH is far from the optimum, so hydrogen ion concentration changes [1]. This disrupts ionic and hydrogen bonds in the enzyme [1]. The tertiary structure changes, so the active site changes shape [1]. The substrate is no longer complementary, so no enzyme-substrate complexes form [1]
**(d)** It is secreted by cells and acts outside them, in the stomach [1] **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* In pH explanations, name the bonds and the structure level affected. Saying "pH denatures it" without a mechanism leaves out the reasoning that earns marks.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using "killed" or "dead" for an enzyme in place of "denatured".
- Saying the substrate "fits exactly" and ignoring induced fit.
- Explaining only the rising part of a temperature graph and not the fall.
- Forgetting units, or the number of significant figures asked for.
- Quoting a percentage change without a sign.
- Naming a control variable without saying how it is kept constant.
- Describing inhibitors without saying where each binds.
- Drawing a tangent at the wrong place instead of at time 0.
