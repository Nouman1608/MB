These revision notes cover the core prescribed topic "Framing global politics" (also titled "Systems and interactions in global politics") in IB Diploma Programme Global Politics. It follows the IB Diploma Programme *Global politics guide* (first assessment 2026, published February 2024), the course examined from the May 2026 session onwards. The guide lists three items of prescribed content here -- stakeholders and actors; systems (structures and dynamics); legal frameworks, norms and institutions -- and all three are common to SL and HL.

## Core definitions

- **Actor** -- an individual or group with the agency and means to exercise power on an issue.
- **Stakeholder** -- anyone affected by an issue, whether or not they can act on it. Every actor involved is a stakeholder; not every stakeholder is an actor.
- **Political system** -- a set of actors plus the rules and patterns that govern their interaction, at any scale.
- **Structure** -- the stable arrangement of a system: where authority sits, how decisions are made, how power is distributed.
- **Dynamics** -- how interactions within a system change over time.
- **Legal framework** -- a body of binding rules (treaties, customary international law, constitutions, statutes).
- **Norm** -- a shared expectation of appropriate behaviour, written into law or not.
- **Institution** -- (1) a formal organisation or agency; (2) a social structure of rules and norms that shapes and constrains behaviour.

## The actor types -- recall table

The guide lists every type below. Learn the row, and have one contemporary example of your own for each.

| Actor type | What it is | Typical sources of influence | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| States | Sovereign units with population, territory, government | Law-making, force, money, treaty-making, IGO membership | Any of the 193 UN member states |
| Subnational and local governments | Regions, provinces, federal units, cities | Local law, services, own international networks | Scottish Parliament (devolved, 1999); C40 Cities |
| IGOs | Organisations whose members are states | Pooled resources, rule-setting, legitimacy | UN, WTO, African Union, ASEAN |
| Organized civil society (incl. NGOs) | Non-profit groups independent of government | Expertise, monitoring, campaigning, service delivery | Amnesty International; Médecins Sans Frontières |
| Private actors/companies | Firms, local to multinational | Investment, jobs, lobbying, control of technology | Multinational corporations, platform firms |
| Social movements | Broad, loose networks seeking change | Numbers, protest, framing, moral pressure | Fridays for Future (from 2018) |
| Resistance movements | Groups opposing a ruling or occupying authority | Armed or unarmed pressure, local support | Label is contested -- report who uses it |
| Political parties | Organisations competing for office | Votes, seats, government posts | Governing and opposition parties |
| Interest and pressure groups | Organised groups influencing policy, not seeking office | Lobbying, membership, funding, strikes | Trade unions, business associations |
| Political leaders | Individuals whose position or standing shapes decisions | Office, personal authority, media profile | Heads of government; movement leaders |
| Formal and informal political forums | Settings where actors meet and decide | Agenda-setting, bargaining, statements | Formal: UN General Assembly. Informal: G7, G20 |
| The media | News organisations and social platforms | Agenda-setting, framing, reach | Public broadcasters; social media platforms |
| Other individual and collective actors | Everyone else who shapes an issue | Varies | Diasporas, whistleblowers, expert networks |

## Must-know distinctions

| Pair | The difference in one line |
|---|---|
| Actor vs stakeholder | Can act vs is affected (may be both) |
| IGO vs NGO | Members are states vs independent of government |
| Social movement vs pressure group | Loose network seeking wide change vs organised group seeking specific policy influence |
| Pressure group vs political party | Influences those in office vs seeks office itself |
| Formal vs informal forum | Founding rules and set membership vs no treaty or permanent secretariat, non-binding outcomes |
| Hard law vs soft law | Binding (treaties) vs non-binding (declarations, resolutions, guidelines) |
| Norm vs law | Shared expectation vs binding rule; a norm may later become law |
| Structure vs dynamics | How the system is arranged vs how interactions in it change |
| Institution (sense 1) vs (sense 2) | Organisation vs pattern of rules and norms |

## Method in steps: analysing actors in a source

Use this on any Paper 1 source.

```
1. Issue and scale   -> What is the issue? Global, regional, national, local?
2. Actors            -> Who can act? Label each with its type from the guide.
3. Stakeholders      -> Who is affected but cannot act? Say why they cannot.
4. System            -> What structure (authority, decision rule, distribution
                        of power) limits or enables each actor?
5. Rules and norms   -> Which laws, norms or institutions shape the options?
                        Do any of them cause the issue?
6. Context           -> Would the same type of actor have more or less power
                        and legitimacy in a different system?
```

## Systems in brief

- **Scale** (the guide's list): global, international, regional, national, subnational, local, community.
- **Structure questions:** Is there an authority above the actors? (Internationally, no world government.) What is the decision rule -- majority, consensus, veto? Who dominates?
- **Dynamics questions:** Who is cooperating, competing or forming alliances? Which rules are being tested? Has a new actor entered?
- **Key idea from the guide:** structures and dynamics regulate interactions and limit possible courses of action. Similar actors can have very different power and legitimacy in different systems.

Small reminders:

- UN Security Council: five permanent members with a veto -- a structural reason a resolution can fail despite wide support.
- WTO dispute settlement: only member governments are parties -- companies need their government to act.

## Legal frameworks, norms and institutions in brief

- **UN Charter (1945):** Article 2(4) bans the threat or use of force against another state; Article 51 keeps the right of self-defence.
- **Vienna Convention on Diplomatic Relations (1961):** diplomatic immunity.
- **Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948):** a General Assembly resolution, so soft law, yet highly influential.
- **Paris Agreement (2015):** a treaty, but each party's contribution is self-set and meeting it is not legally binding.
- Rules, norms and institutions can **shape**, **limit** or **cause** political issues. Name which one you mean.

## Command terms you will meet on this topic

From the guide's glossary:

- **Identify** (AO1) -- provide an answer from a number of possibilities.
- **Outline** (AO1) -- give a brief account or summary.
- **Explain** (AO2) -- give a detailed account including reasons or causes.
- **Distinguish** (AO2) -- make clear the differences between two or more items.
- **Compare and contrast** (AO3) -- similarities and differences, referring to both throughout.
- **Evaluate** (AO3) -- weigh up strengths and limitations.
- **To what extent** (AO3) -- consider the merits of an argument, with a clear, supported conclusion.

## Quick self-test

1. Give the precise difference between an actor and a stakeholder.
2. Name three actor types from the guide that are not states.
3. What makes an organisation an IGO rather than an NGO?
4. Give one formal and one informal political forum.
5. Distinguish a social movement from an interest or pressure group.
6. What are the two senses of "institution" in the guide?
7. Is the Universal Declaration of Human Rights hard or soft law? Why?
8. Which article of the UN Charter prohibits the use of force against another state, and which preserves self-defence?
9. Give one structural feature of the UN Security Council that limits action.
10. Why should you avoid calling a resistance movement "terrorists" as a statement of fact?
11. List the seven scales the guide names.
12. Give one way a rule or institution can cause, rather than just shape, a political issue.

### Answers

1. An actor has the agency and means to act on an issue; a stakeholder is affected by it, whether or not they can act.
2. Any three of: subnational and local governments, IGOs, organized civil society/NGOs, private actors/companies, social movements, resistance movements, political parties, interest and pressure groups, political leaders, political forums, the media, other individual and collective actors.
3. An IGO's members are states and it is set up by agreement between them; an NGO is independent of government.
4. Formal: UN General Assembly (or any body with founding rules and set membership). Informal: G7 or G20.
5. A social movement is a broad, loosely organised network seeking wide change, often by protest; a pressure group is an organised body seeking specific policy influence, usually by lobbying.
6. (1) Formal organisations or agencies; (2) social structures of rules and norms that shape and constrain behaviour.
7. Soft law: it was adopted as a General Assembly resolution, not a binding treaty.
8. Article 2(4) prohibits force; Article 51 preserves self-defence.
9. The five permanent members each hold a veto, so one of them can block a resolution.
10. The label is contested: supporters and opponents describe the same group differently. You should say who uses which label.
11. Global, international, regional, national, subnational, local, community.
12. For example, a border agreement that divides a community, or trade rules that one group sees as unfair, can themselves be the source of the dispute.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using "actor" and "stakeholder" as synonyms, so the answer never shows who is affected but voiceless.
- Naming an actor type without saying what that actor can actually do in the source's context.
- Treating an actor type as having fixed power -- for example, assuming all NGOs are weak -- instead of linking power to the system.
- Describing "the UN" as one actor when the source is really about a specific organ, such as the Security Council or General Assembly.
- Forgetting the second meaning of "institution", which loses easy explanation marks on norms and rules.
- Stating that soft law is irrelevant rather than explaining how it works through legitimacy and pressure.
- Adopting one side's label for a contested group rather than reporting it.
- Offering an example with no explanation: the guide expects contextualisation, not a bare name.
- Relying on old historical cases instead of contemporary ones from roughly the past two decades.
