This guide covers one subtopic of the Cambridge IGCSE Physics 0625 syllabus (for examination in 2026, 2027 and 2028): **5.2 Radioactivity**, in five parts: detection, the three types of emission, radioactive decay, half-life and safety precautions. Core content is for every candidate; content marked **Extended only** is Supplement material, examined on Papers 2 and 4. The [Nuclear physics study guide](/resources/igcse-physics-0625-nuclear-physics/) gives the full summary tables and worked half-life examples; this page focuses on the reasoning behind identifying radiation, choosing isotopes and handling data.

## Background radiation and count rate

**Background radiation** is the ionising radiation present around us all the time. The sources that make a significant contribution are **radon gas** in the air, **rocks and buildings**, **food and drink**, and **cosmic rays** from space.

Radiation is measured with a **detector connected to a counter**. The **count rate** is the number of counts divided by the time: 1350 counts in 5.0 minutes is 270 counts/minute, or 4.5 counts/s.

**Extended only.** Measure the background count rate with the source removed (ideally over a long time, because emission is random) and subtract it from every reading to get the **corrected count rate**.

## Identifying α, β and γ

| | Alpha (α) | Beta (β) | Gamma (γ) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nature | Helium nucleus: 2 protons + 2 neutrons | Fast electron from the nucleus | Electromagnetic radiation |
| Relative ionising effect | Strongest | Medium | Weakest |
| Penetration | Stopped by paper or a few cm of air | Stopped by a few mm of aluminium | Reduced, not stopped, by thick lead or concrete |

Emission is **spontaneous** (it cannot be triggered or stopped by temperature or chemical changes) and **random in direction**.

**Reading absorption data.** Compare the count rate after each absorber with the one before:

- A large drop when **paper** is added → **α** is present.
- A further large drop when **aluminium** replaces the paper → **β** is present.
- A count still well above background after the aluminium, reduced by lead → **γ** is present.
- No change with paper → no α.

**Explaining ionising effect (Extended only).** Alpha particles have the **largest charge** (+2) and a **large kinetic energy**, and move relatively slowly, so they interact strongly with every atom they pass, ionise heavily and lose their energy in a short distance. That is also why they penetrate least. Gamma radiation is **uncharged**, so it interacts rarely, ionises least and penetrates furthest.

**Deflection (Extended only).** In an **electric field**, α bends towards the negative plate and β towards the positive plate; β bends **more** because its mass is far smaller. In a **magnetic field** at right angles to their motion, α and β curve in **opposite** directions (use Fleming's left-hand rule, remembering β is negative), with β curving more. Gamma is **not deflected** by either field.

## Radioactive decay

Radioactive decay is a change in an **unstable nucleus** that can result in the emission of α-particles, β-particles and/or γ-radiation. It is **spontaneous** and **random**: you cannot predict when a particular nucleus will decay. In α-decay or β-decay the nucleus becomes that of a **different element**; γ emission alone leaves the element unchanged.

**Extended only.** Isotopes may be unstable because the nucleus has **too many neutrons** and/or is **too heavy**. Decay makes the nucleus **more stable**. In β-decay a **neutron changes into a proton and an electron**; the electron is emitted, so the number of excess neutrons falls.

| Emission | Change in A | Change in Z | Symbol |
|---|---|---|---|
| α | −4 | −2 | ⁴₂He |
| β | 0 | +1 | ⁰₋₁e |
| γ | 0 | 0 | γ |

### Worked example 1 (Extended)

Radon-222 (Z = 86) decays by α emission to polonium (Po). Strontium-90 (Z = 38) decays by β emission to yttrium (Y). Write the equations.

```
²²²₈₆Rn → ²¹⁸₈₄Po + ⁴₂He        top: 222 = 218 + 4;  bottom: 86 = 84 + 2
⁹⁰₃₈Sr  → ⁹⁰₃₉Y  + ⁰₋₁e         top: 90 = 90 + 0;    bottom: 38 = 39 + (−1)
```

## Half-life

The **half-life** of a particular isotope is the time taken for **half the nuclei of that isotope** in any sample to decay. Each half-life halves the count rate (and the number of undecayed nuclei), whatever the starting value.

**Core method:** count the halvings. A count rate falls from 1280 to 160 counts/minute in 15 minutes: 1280 → 640 → 320 → 160 is 3 half-lives, so the half-life is 15 ÷ 3 = **5.0 minutes**. After n half-lives the fraction remaining is (1/2)ⁿ: after 4 half-lives, 1/16 remains.

### Worked example 2 (Extended)

A source gives 660 counts/minute; 9.0 hours later it gives 100 counts/minute. The background is 20 counts/minute. Find the half-life.

```
corrected: 660 − 20 = 640;  100 − 20 = 80
640 → 320 → 160 → 80  = 3 half-lives
half-life = 9.0 / 3 = 3.0 hours
```

Using the raw readings (660 to 100) gives 2.7 halvings and a wrong answer; this is exactly the trap the Supplement outcome tests. On a decay curve that levels off above zero, the level it approaches is the background.

## Choosing an isotope (Extended only)

Every choice has **two** parts: the **type of radiation** (does it penetrate as far as needed, and is it absorbed where it should be?) and the **half-life** (long enough to do the job, short enough not to leave a lasting hazard where that matters).

| Use | Radiation | Half-life | Reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| Smoke alarm | α | Long (years) | Ionises the air in the detector but is stopped by the casing; long half-life gives a steady count for the alarm's life |
| Irradiating food; sterilising equipment | γ | Long | Penetrates packaging to kill bacteria; source lasts |
| Thickness of paper or thin sheet | β | Long | Partly absorbed, so the count changes with thickness (α absorbed completely, γ barely at all) |
| Diagnosing cancer (tracer) | γ | Short (hours) | Detected outside the body; activity falls quickly, limiting the dose |
| Treating cancer | γ | Long (for an external source) | Penetrates to the tumour from beams aimed at it |

Americium-241, an α emitter with a half-life of about 430 years, is the usual smoke-alarm example.

## Safety precautions

Ionising radiation can cause **cell death**, **mutations** and **cancer**. In schools and hospitals, sources are handled with **tongs**, held **away from the body** and pointed away from people, used for the **shortest time** possible, and stored in **lead-lined containers** in a locked, labelled cupboard.

**Extended only.** Every precaution reduces exposure in one of three ways: **reducing the time** of exposure, **increasing the distance** between source and living tissue, or using **shielding** to absorb the radiation. When asked to explain a precaution, name which of the three it uses.

## Common mistakes

- Saying γ is stopped by lead; thick lead only reduces it.
- Saying a nucleus "decays faster when heated".
- Defining half-life as half the time for the whole sample to decay.
- Halving the time instead of the count rate.
- Leaving background in the data for an Extended half-life calculation.
- Choosing an isotope by radiation type alone, without the half-life.

## Exam technique

- **State** the nature of a radiation precisely: "helium nucleus", "electron from the nucleus", "electromagnetic radiation".
- **Deduce** from absorption data: give a reason for including **and** for ruling out each type.
- **Calculate** half-life: show each halving, or read two points from a curve with construction lines.
- **Explain** a use: one point on radiation type, one on half-life.
- **Explain** a precaution (Extended): time, distance or shielding.
