This guide covers subtopic **1.2 Specialised cells, tissues and organs** of Cambridge O Level Biology 5090, 2026–2028 series. The syllabus asks you to "understand that cells can become specialised and that their structures are related to their specific functions, as illustrated by examples covered in the syllabus", to understand "the terms cell, tissue, organ, organ system and organism", and to "state and use the formula magnification = image size ÷ actual size".

The [Cell Structure and Organisation guide](/resources/cell-structure-and-organisation/) introduces specialised cells, the hierarchy and the formula. This page goes deeper: it collects the specialised cells that later topics of 5090 name, shows how to write a three-part structure-function answer, practises classifying structures into levels of organisation, and extends magnification to scale bars and image size. Use the [1.2 revision notes](/resources/cambridge-o-level-biology-5090-specialised-cells-tissues-and-organs-revision-notes/) for recall and the [1.2 practice questions](/resources/cambridge-o-level-biology-5090-specialised-cells-tissues-and-organs-practice/) to test yourself.

## How cells become specialised

A newly formed cell is usually unspecialised. The syllabus describes **stem cells** (in a later topic) as unspecialised cells that divide by mitosis to produce daughter cells that can become specialised for specific functions. As a cell specialises, it develops a particular shape and particular contents. The phrase "examples covered in the syllabus" means the specialised cells you meet in other topics are the ones you can be asked about here.

## The specialised cells named across 5090

| Cell | Where it appears in the syllabus | Key structural features | How each feature helps |
|---|---|---|---|
| Root hair cell | Transport in plants; movement into and out of cells | Long, thin extension; many mitochondria | Large surface area for uptake of water and ions; energy from respiration for active transport of ions |
| Xylem vessel | Transport in plants | Thick walls with lignin; no cell contents; cells joined end to end with no cross walls | Strength and support; an empty, continuous tube lets water and mineral ions flow freely |
| Palisade mesophyll cell | Plant nutrition | Many chloroplasts; near the upper surface of the leaf | Absorbs as much light as possible for photosynthesis |
| Guard cell | Plant nutrition | Pair of cells around a stoma that change shape | Open and close the stoma, controlling gas exchange and water loss |
| Red blood cell | Transport in humans | Contains haemoglobin | Carries oxygen |
| Phagocyte | Transport in humans | Can change shape to engulf | Engulfs and digests pathogens |
| Lymphocyte | Transport in humans | Features not required | Produces antibodies |
| Ciliated cell | Gas exchange | Cilia on the surface that beat | Moves mucus, with trapped particles and pathogens, away from the lungs |
| Goblet cell | Gas exchange | Secretes mucus | Mucus traps particles and pathogens |
| Sperm cell | Reproduction | Flagellum; many mitochondria; enzymes in the acrosome | Swims to the egg; energy for swimming; enzymes digest a way into the egg |
| Egg cell | Reproduction | Energy stores; jelly coat that changes at fertilisation | Supplies energy for early development; changed coat prevents more sperm entering |

You do not need features beyond those the syllabus lists for each cell. For sperm and egg cells, the syllabus states the limits: flagellum, mitochondria and acrosome enzymes; energy stores and the jelly coat.

## Writing a structure-function answer

The mark scheme usually gives one mark for the feature and one for how it helps. Use a three-part sentence:

**feature → what it does → why that matters for the function**

> *Weak:* "Root hair cells have a long extension."
>
> *Strong:* "Root hair cells have a long, thin extension, **which gives a large surface area**, **so more water and mineral ions can be absorbed from the soil**."

> *Weak:* "Sperm cells have mitochondria."
>
> *Strong:* "Sperm cells have many mitochondria, **which carry out aerobic respiration to release energy**, **so the flagellum can move and the sperm can swim to the egg**."

## Cell, tissue, organ, organ system, organism

| Level | Definition | Plant example | Animal example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cell | The basic unit of a living organism | Palisade mesophyll cell | Ciliated cell |
| Tissue | A group of similar cells working together to perform a shared function | Xylem; palisade mesophyll | Muscle tissue; blood |
| Organ | A structure made of several tissues working together to perform a specific function | Leaf; root; stem | Heart; stomach; lung |
| Organ system | A group of organs working together to perform a body function | Shoot system (stems, leaves, flowers) | Digestive system; circulatory system |
| Organism | A complete living thing | A bean plant | A human |

### Worked example: classifying structures

*Classify each as a cell, tissue, organ or organ system: guard cell, heart, phloem, leaf, digestive system, sperm cell, lung, xylem.*

| Cell | Tissue | Organ | Organ system |
|---|---|---|---|
| Guard cell, sperm cell | Phloem, xylem | Heart, leaf, lung | Digestive system |

**Reasoning to show.** Xylem and phloem contain many similar cells doing one job, so they are tissues. A leaf contains several tissues (epidermis, mesophyll, xylem, phloem), so it is an organ. A common error is calling the leaf a tissue or the heart an organ system.

## Magnification: beyond the basic formula

```
magnification = image size / actual size
```

The [Cell Structure and Organisation guide](/resources/cell-structure-and-organisation/) shows how to find magnification and actual size. Three further question types appear often.

**1. Small cells measured in micrometres.** A drawing of a red blood cell has a diameter of 30 mm. The actual diameter is 7.5 µm. Find the magnification.

```
convert: 7.5 µm = 7.5 / 1000 = 0.0075 mm
magnification = 30 / 0.0075 = x4000
```

**2. Using a scale bar.** A photomicrograph has a scale bar 20 mm long labelled 50 µm. A cell on the image measures 48 mm. Find the actual length of the cell.

```
magnification = image length of bar / actual length of bar
              = 20 mm / 0.05 mm = x400
actual size of cell = 48 / 400 = 0.12 mm = 120 µm
```

You can also reason by proportion: the cell is 48 ÷ 20 = 2.4 times the bar's length, so it is 2.4 × 50 = 120 µm. Both routes give the same answer.

**3. Finding image size.** A guard cell is 40 µm long. How long would it be in a drawing at ×500?

```
image size = actual size x magnification
           = 0.04 mm x 500 = 20 mm
```

**Checks before you write the answer.**

- Are image size and actual size in the same unit before dividing?
- For a magnified image, is the image size larger than the actual size?
- Is the answer sensible? Most animal and plant cells are tens of micrometres across; a "cell" calculated as several millimetres long probably means a unit error.
- Magnification has no unit; write it with × in front.

## Common mistakes

- Giving a feature without its effect, or an effect without the feature.
- Adding features the syllabus does not list for sperm and egg cells, while missing the ones it does.
- Calling xylem vessels living cells; they have no cell contents.
- Calling blood or xylem an organ, or a leaf a tissue.
- Measuring the scale bar and forgetting to convert the label from µm to mm.
- Rounding too early in a multi-step magnification calculation.

## Command words

- **State**: a short answer, such as one feature.
- **Describe**: give the features, for example of a sperm cell.
- **Explain**: link each feature to how it helps the cell's function.
- **Calculate**: show the formula, the substitution with units converted, and the answer.
