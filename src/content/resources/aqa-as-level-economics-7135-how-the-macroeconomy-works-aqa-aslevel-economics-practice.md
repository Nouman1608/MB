These questions cover **3.2.2 How the macroeconomy works** in AQA AS Economics (7135), specification version 1.3. Multiplier questions use the AS method, from an injection and the resulting change in national income; no question asks for an accelerator calculation. All countries and figures are invented. Revise first with the [3.2.2 study guide](/resources/aqa-as-level-economics-7135-how-the-macroeconomy-works-aqa-aslevel-economics/) and the [3.2.2 revision notes](/resources/aqa-as-level-economics-7135-how-the-macroeconomy-works-aqa-aslevel-economics-revision-notes/).

## Section A: short questions

**1.** State the identity that links national income, national output and national expenditure. **[1]**

**2.** Which one of the following is a withdrawal from the circular flow of income? **[1]**

A: Exports

B: Investment

C: Taxation

D: Government spending

**3.** Investment rises by 30 bn and national income rises by 90 bn as a result. Calculate the multiplier. **[1]**

**4.** The multiplier in an economy is 2.4. Calculate the initial increase in government spending needed to raise national income by 72 bn. **[2]**

**5.** Distinguish between saving and investment. **[2]**

**6.** State two changes that would shift the short-run aggregate supply curve to the left. **[2]**

## Section B: structured questions

**7.** In Country Z this year (bn): investment 150, government spending 200, exports 250, saving 180, taxation 220, imports 230.

**(a)** Calculate total injections and total withdrawals. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain what is likely to happen to national income. **[2]**

**(c)** In a later year exports rise by 40 bn and national income rises by 64 bn as a result. Calculate the multiplier. **[1]**

**8.** A machine-tool maker's customers had sales of 100, 110, 125 and 130 thousand units in four successive years. Explain, using the accelerator process, why the customers' investment in new machine tools might fall in the fourth year although their sales still rose. **[4]**

**9.** Explain, using an AD/AS diagram, the likely effect of a large rise in world commodity prices on a country's price level and real output. **[6]**

**10.** Explain how two determinants of long-run aggregate supply can increase an economy's normal capacity level of output. **[4]**

**11.** A government spends 50 m on a new hospital in a small town.

**(a)** Explain why national income is likely to rise by more than 50 m. **[4]**

**(b)** Explain why the increase in the town's own income is likely to be smaller than the increase in national income. **[2]**

**12.** Country Q's exports rise by 20 bn after a trading partner recovers from recession. The multiplier is estimated at 1.5, and the economy is already close to its normal capacity level of output. Evaluate whether the rise in exports will significantly increase real output. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** National income = national output = national expenditure [1]. *Total: 1.*

**2.** **C** [1]. Taxation takes income out of the flow; the others are injections. *Total: 1.*

**3.** 90 / 30 = **3** [1]. *Total: 1.*

**4.** Initial change = change in income / multiplier [1] = 72 / 2.4 = **30 bn** [1]. *Total: 2.*

**5.** Saving is the part of income that is not spent on consumption, a withdrawal from the circular flow [1]. Investment is spending by firms on capital goods such as machinery and buildings, an injection [1]. *Total: 2.*

**6.** Any two [1 each]: a rise in money wage rates not matched by productivity; higher raw material prices; higher business taxation; a fall in productivity. *Total: 2.*

**7. (a)** Injections = 150 + 200 + 250 = **600 bn** [1]. Withdrawals = 180 + 220 + 230 = **630 bn** [1].

**(b)** Withdrawals exceed injections by 30 bn, so less is being spent in the flow than is being earned [1]. National income is likely to fall until withdrawals fall to equal injections at a new, lower equilibrium [1].

**(c)** 64 / 40 = **1.6** [1]. *Total: 5.*

**8.** The accelerator says investment depends on the rate of change of sales, not their level [1]. Sales grew by 10 thousand in year 2 and 15 thousand in year 3, so firms needed many extra machines to meet growing demand [1]. In year 4 sales grew by only 5 thousand, so fewer additional machines were needed [1]. Investment, which covers the extra capacity, therefore falls even though sales are at a record level [1]. *Total: 4.*

**9.** Commodities such as oil and metals are inputs, so their higher prices raise firms' production costs [1]. SRAS shifts to the left [1]. Diagram: AD unchanged, SRAS shifting left from SRAS1 to SRAS2, new equilibrium at a higher price level and lower real output [1]. The price level rises: cost-push pressure [1]. Real output falls, so unemployment may rise [1]. This is a negative supply-side shock from the global economy; LRAS need not move if the rise does not change the economy's capacity [1]. *Total: 6.*

**10.** Technology: new methods let the same workers and machines produce more [1], so capacity rises and LRAS shifts right [1]. Factor mobility: if workers can move more easily to jobs or regions where they are needed, fewer resources sit idle or in low-productivity uses [1], so the economy can produce more and normal capacity output rises [1]. Other acceptable determinants: productivity, attitudes, enterprise, economic incentives. *Total: 4.*

**11. (a)** The 50 m becomes income for builders and suppliers [1]. They spend part of it, which becomes income for shopkeepers and others [1]. Each round is smaller because some income is withdrawn as saving, taxes and imports [1]. The sum of all rounds is larger than the initial 50 m: this is the multiplier process [1].

**(b)** Much of the extra income in each round is spent on goods and services from outside the town, so it leaks from the local economy [1]. Less is passed on within the town, so the local multiplier is smaller than the national one [1]. *Total: 6.*

**12.** With a multiplier of 1.5, AD could rise by up to 20 x 1.5 = 30 bn [1]. With spare capacity this would mean a similar rise in real output and jobs [1]. But the economy is close to normal capacity, so the extra AD meets shortages of labour and materials [1]. Firms raise prices, so more of the AD increase shows up in a higher price level and less in output [1]. A rising price level makes exports less competitive, partly reversing the gain [1]. The effect also depends on whether firms invest to raise capacity; if the recovery abroad looks lasting, investment could shift LRAS right over time [1]. A multiplier of 1.5 is fairly small, so even with spare capacity the effect would be moderate [1]. Judgement: the rise in exports is unlikely to raise real output much in the short run; it is more likely to add to inflation unless capacity grows [1]. *Total: 8.*

## Where marks are usually lost

- Dividing the injection by the income change instead of the other way round.
- Calling saving an injection.
- Explaining the accelerator without the rate of change of sales.
- Showing a cost shock as a shift of AD.
- Assuming the full multiplier effect is on real output even near full capacity.
