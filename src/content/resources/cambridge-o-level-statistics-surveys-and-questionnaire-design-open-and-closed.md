## 1. General ideas of surveys

A **survey** collects information from people by asking them questions. The group you want to know about is the **population**. A **census** asks every member. A **sample survey** asks a selected part of the population, chosen using a **sampling frame** (the list the sample is drawn from).

A survey usually follows this order:

1. State the aim, for example "find out how pupils travel to school".
2. Decide the population and how to choose a sample.
3. Decide how to ask: face to face, by telephone, by post, or online.
4. Write and pilot the questionnaire.
5. Collect the data and record non-response.
6. Process, display and interpret the results.

A **questionnaire** is the written set of questions. It may be completed by the respondent alone or read out by an interviewer. Each way of asking has a cost. An interviewer can explain a question but may influence the answer. A postal or online form is cheap but many people do not return it.

## 2. Closed questions

A **closed question** gives the respondent a fixed set of answers to choose from. Common forms:

- **Yes/No**: "Do you own a bicycle?"
- **Multiple choice**: "How do you usually travel to school?" with options such as walk, cycle, bus, car.
- **Rating scale**: "How satisfied are you with the school library?" with options from very satisfied to very dissatisfied.
- **Banded numbers**: "How many hours of sleep did you have last night?" with bands.

### Advantages

- Quick to answer, so more people finish the form.
- Answers are already in categories, so tallying is fast and cheap.
- Every respondent answers on the same terms, so results are easy to compare and display.

### Disadvantages

- The respondent may find no option that fits, so they guess or leave it blank.
- The options come from the researcher, who may miss an important answer.
- Little detail: you learn what people chose but not why.

### Good response options

Response options must be:

- **Exhaustive**: every possible answer has an option. Add "Other (please state)" or "Don't know" when you cannot list everything.
- **Mutually exclusive**: no answer fits two options.
- **Clear and in a sensible order**: rating scales run in one direction and are balanced, with as many positive options as negative ones.

**Worked example 1: faulty bands.** A sports club records the ages, in whole years, of its members using the options "Under 16", "16–25", "25–40", "45 or over".

- Fault 1: 25 appears in two options, so the options are not mutually exclusive.
- Fault 2: ages 41 to 44 appear in no option, so the options are not exhaustive.
- Corrected options: "Under 16", "16–25", "26–40", "41 or over".

Check the corrected list: every whole age from 0 upwards belongs to exactly one option.

**Worked example 2: a closed question with a rating scale.** 40 members answer "How satisfied are you with the changing rooms?"

| Response | Number | Percentage |
|---|---|---|
| Very satisfied | 9 | 22.5% |
| Satisfied | 15 | 37.5% |
| Neither | 8 | 20% |
| Dissatisfied | 5 | 12.5% |
| Very dissatisfied | 3 | 7.5% |
| Total | 40 | 100% |

Each percentage is (number ÷ 40) × 100. Members who were satisfied or very satisfied: 9 + 15 = 24, and 24/40 = 60%. This is a clean result because every answer is already in a category. It cannot tell you what is wrong with the changing rooms.

## 3. Open questions

An **open question** has no fixed answers. The respondent writes or says what they like, for example "What would you change about the school canteen?"

### Advantages

- The respondent can give an answer the researcher did not think of.
- It gives detail and reasons, which can explain a pattern found in closed questions.
- It is useful at an early stage, when you do not yet know what the likely answers are.

### Disadvantages

- Slower to answer, so people skip it or write very little.
- Answers vary in length and wording, so they are hard to compare.
- The answers must be sorted into categories before you can count them, which takes time and relies on judgement.

### Processing open answers: coding

To analyse open answers, read them and sort them into categories. This is called **coding**. Choose categories after reading the answers, make them mutually exclusive, and keep an "Other" group small.

**Worked example 3: coding.** 24 pupils answer "What would you most like to change about the canteen?" After reading the answers, the researcher sorts them like this:

| Category | Frequency | Percentage | Pie chart angle |
|---|---|---|---|
| Prices | 7 | 29.2% | 105° |
| Queues | 6 | 25% | 90° |
| Menu variety | 5 | 20.8% | 75° |
| Seating | 3 | 12.5% | 45° |
| Other | 3 | 12.5% | 45° |
| Total | 24 | 100% | 360° |

Working for prices: percentage = 7/24 × 100 = 29.2% (1 d.p.); angle = 7/24 × 360° = 105°. The angles add to 360°, which is the check.

Coding loses information. The pupil who wrote "the chips are always cold and cost too much" is placed in one category only, or counted twice if you decide to allow that. State your rule before you count.

### Choosing between them

| | Closed | Open |
|---|---|---|
| Time to answer | Short | Longer |
| Time to analyse | Short | Long |
| Detail and reasons | Little | A lot |
| Unexpected answers | Not possible | Possible |
| Comparing respondents | Easy | Harder |

Many questionnaires use mostly closed questions with one or two open questions at the end, so that the closed questions give counts and the open ones give reasons.

## 4. Writing good questions

A badly worded question produces **bias**: the answers are pushed away from the truth. Watch for these faults.

| Fault | Example | Better version |
|---|---|---|
| **Leading**: wording suggests the answer | "Don't you agree the new timetable is much better?" | "How would you rate the new timetable?" with a balanced scale |
| **Loaded**: emotive words | "Should the council waste money on cycle lanes?" | "Should the council spend money on cycle lanes?" |
| **Double-barrelled**: two questions in one | "Is the café cheap and clean?" | Ask about price and cleanliness separately |
| **Vague**: words with no fixed meaning | "Do you exercise often?" with options "Often", "Sometimes" | "How many days did you exercise last week?" with number bands |
| **Hard to understand**: jargon or long sentences | "Do you engage in extracurricular provision?" | "Do you go to any school clubs?" |
| **Hypothetical or memory-heavy** | "What will you spend next year?" | Ask about a recent, short period |

Other points of design:

- Put easy, interesting questions first. Put sensitive ones (such as income) late, and give bands rather than asking for an exact figure.
- Keep the questionnaire short and give a short introduction saying who is asking, why, and that answers are confidential.
- Give clear instructions such as "tick one box" or "tick all that apply".
- Leave out any question you do not need for the aim.

## 5. The pilot survey

A **pilot survey** is a small trial of the questionnaire on a few people before the main survey. The pilot group should be like the real respondents. It checks:

- whether questions are understood in the way you meant
- whether the response options are exhaustive and mutually exclusive
- how long the form takes
- whether the instructions are clear
- whether the answers can be coded and counted

The pilot lets you correct problems before you spend time and money on the full sample.

**Worked example 4: using pilot results.** A school trials a form on 10 pupils. Three pupils leave the transport question blank, because their route is a mix of bus and walking. Two ask what "sedentary" means. One notes that the form takes 20 minutes.

- Blank answers: add an option, for example "Other (please state)", and make clear whether the question allows one answer or several.
- "Sedentary": replace it with plain words, such as "sitting down".
- Length: cut questions that do not serve the aim.

Then the changed form is used in the main survey.

## 6. Response and non-response

Some people will not reply. The **response rate** is the number of usable replies divided by the number of people asked, as a percentage.

**Worked example 5: response rate.** 320 forms are handed out. 184 are returned, but 8 are spoiled and cannot be used.

- Usable forms = 184 − 8 = 176
- Response rate = 176/320 × 100 = 55%

A low response rate is a problem when the people who did not reply differ from those who did. This is **non-response bias**. Reminders, a shorter form, and a prepaid return envelope can raise the rate. A larger sample does not remove bias caused by bad questions or non-response.

## Common errors

- Writing "closed questions are better" without a reason. Always say what the question is for.
- Offering overlapping bands such as 10–20 and 20–30, or leaving gaps.
- Saying a pilot survey is a smaller version of the final survey. It is a test of the questionnaire, and its purpose is to find faults.
- Listing "gives more detail" as an advantage of closed questions. Detail is the advantage of open questions.
- Forgetting that open answers must be coded before they can be counted.
- Calling a question leading when it is only unclear. Name the exact fault and rewrite the question.
- Rewriting a leading question but leaving the loaded word in the new version.
