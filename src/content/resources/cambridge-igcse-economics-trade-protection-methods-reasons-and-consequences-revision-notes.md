## Core definitions

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Protection | Government action to shield home producers from foreign competition |
| Tariff | A tax on imported goods |
| Import quota | A limit on the quantity (or value) of a good that can be imported in a period |
| Subsidy | A payment from the government to producers that lowers their costs |
| Embargo | A ban on trade in a good or with a country |
| Infant industry | A new industry with small output and high unit costs |
| Declining industry | An industry whose output and employment are falling over time |
| Strategic industry | An industry a country needs for security or survival, such as food, energy or defence |
| Dumping | Selling abroad below cost, or below the price in the exporter's home market |
| Retaliation | One country responding to another's protection with its own |

## 6.2.4 Methods of protection

### Effect on a market: one set of reminders

Country A buys tea at a world price of 4 per kg. It buys 1 000 000 kg and makes 200 000 kg itself, so it imports 800 000 kg.

A 25% tariff:

1. Tariff per kg: 25% of 4 = 1.
2. New price: 4 + 1 = 5.
3. Quantity demanded falls to 900 000 kg. Home supply rises to 300 000 kg.
4. Imports: 900 000 − 300 000 = 600 000 kg.
5. Revenue: 600 000 × 1 = 600 000.
6. Imports fell by 200 000 kg, which is 200 000 / 800 000 × 100 = 25%.
7. Home output rose by 100 000 / 200 000 × 100 = 50%.
8. Consumer spending moved from 1 000 000 × 4 = 4 000 000 to 900 000 × 5 = 4 500 000.

A quota of 600 000 kg gives the same price and quantities. The gap of 1 per kg (600 000 in total) goes to licence holders unless the government charges for licences.

A subsidy of 0.50 per kg to home growers makes them act as if the price were 4.50. Assuming supply rises steadily, supply at 4.50 is 200 000 + half of 100 000 = 250 000 kg. Cost: 250 000 × 0.50 = 125 000. Consumers still pay 4 and buy 1 000 000 kg, so imports are 750 000 kg.

### Summary table

| Method | How it works | Price to consumers | Who gains | Who loses |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Tariff | Taxes imports | Rises | Home firms, government | Consumers, foreign exporters |
| Import quota | Caps import quantity | Rises | Home firms, licence holders | Consumers, foreign exporters beyond the cap |
| Subsidy | Cuts home costs | Stays low | Home firms | Taxpayers |
| Embargo | Bans trade | Import unavailable | Home firms | Consumers, the banned exporter |

### Method steps for a protection calculation

1. Find the new price (price × (1 + tariff rate)), or the subsidy-adjusted supply price.
2. Read off or work out quantity demanded and home supply at that price.
3. Imports = quantity demanded − home supply.
4. Revenue or cost = quantity × amount per unit (tariff revenue uses imports; subsidy cost uses home output).
5. Percentage change = change / original × 100.

## 6.2.5 Reasons for protection

| Reason | Argument | Main weakness |
|---|---|---|
| Infant industry | New firms need time to grow and cut unit costs | May never become competitive; protection can become permanent |
| Declining industry | Slows job losses and gives time to retrain | Costly for consumers; delays the move of resources to better uses |
| Strategic industry | Keeps home capacity for security or food supply | Higher prices; may protect more than is needed |
| Avoidance of dumping | Stops unfair pricing that destroys home firms | Hard to prove; a low price can simply reflect low costs |

### Chain of reasoning: infant industry

Small output leads to high unit cost. High unit cost means the firm cannot match the foreign price. It exits before growing. Protection raises the foreign price, the firm survives, output grows, unit cost falls, and the firm can compete without help. So the case is strongest when protection is temporary and has a stated end date.

### Chain of reasoning: declining industry

Imports are cheaper, so home sales fall. Firms cut jobs. Workers lose income, and a town that relies on the industry suffers. A quota slows the fall. The cost is higher prices for everyone who buys the good.

### Cost per job calculation

Quota raises the price of cloth by 1.20 per metre on 3 000 000 metres bought. Extra spending: 1.20 × 3 000 000 = 3 600 000. It protects 800 jobs. Cost per job: 3 600 000 / 800 = 4 500 per year. Compare that with the cost of unemployment benefit or retraining.

### Dumping check

Cost 70, home price 85, export price 60. Loss per unit abroad: 70 − 60 = 10. Export price below home price: (85 − 60) / 85 × 100 = 29.4% lower. Both tests point to dumping. A tariff of 25 would lift the export price from 60 to 85.

## 6.2.6 Consequences of protection

### Effectiveness checklist

- Imports fall by the amount aimed for.
- Home output and jobs rise.
- Home firms improve rather than relax.
- Trading partners do not retaliate.
- The measure can be enforced (not undermined by smuggling).
- The cost to consumers is acceptable.

In the tea example the price rose by 25% and quantity demanded fell by 10%. Price elasticity of demand is −10 / 25 = −0.4, which is inelastic. A tariff on a good with inelastic demand raises more revenue and cuts total consumption less, so consumers bear most of the cost.

### Impact table

| | Gains | Losses |
|---|---|---|
| Home consumers | None directly | Higher prices, less choice |
| Home producers | Sales, profit, jobs | Less pressure to cut costs |
| Home government | Tariff revenue | Subsidy bill; lost goodwill |
| Trading partners' exporters | None | Lost sales, jobs and revenue |
| Home exporters | None | Lost sales if partners retaliate |

### Trading partner calculation

An exporter in Country H sends 5 000 000 of goods to Country A. A tariff cuts sales by 30%. Loss: 5 000 000 × 0.30 = 1 500 000. New exports: 3 500 000.

### Evaluation reminders

- Protection helps a few producers and workers a lot, and harms many consumers a little each. Use this contrast.
- Duration, size of protection, elasticity of demand and retaliation all change the answer.
- Finish with a judgement and a reason, not a list.

## Must-know distinctions

- Tariff and quota raise price. A subsidy does not.
- Tariff revenue goes to the government. Quota revenue goes to licence holders unless licences are sold.
- Embargo is a total ban. A quota allows some trade.
- Dumping is below cost or below the exporter's home price. A low price alone is not dumping.
- The cost of protection falls on consumers and taxpayers at home, and on exporters abroad.

## Quick self-test

1. Define a tariff.
2. Country A imposes a 15% tariff on a good with a world price of 20. What is the new price?
3. Imports after the tariff are 40 000 units and the tariff is 3 per unit. Calculate revenue.
4. Imports fall from 800 000 to 600 000. Calculate the percentage fall.
5. Give the four reasons for protection in the syllabus.
6. Who collects the gap in price under a quota?
7. A tariff raises price by 30% and quantity demanded falls by 15%. Calculate price elasticity of demand.
8. A quota costs consumers 2 400 000 extra and saves 600 jobs. Calculate the cost per job.
9. Give one effect of protection on a trading partner.
10. Explain why a subsidy may be better for consumers than a tariff.

### Answers

1. A tax on imported goods.
2. 20 × 1.15 = 23.
3. 40 000 × 3 = 120 000.
4. 200 000 / 800 000 × 100 = 25%.
5. Infant industry, declining industry, strategic industry, avoidance of dumping.
6. Licence holders, often foreign exporters or importers, unless the government sells the licences.
7. −15 / 30 = −0.5, inelastic.
8. 2 400 000 / 600 = 4 000.
9. Lost export sales, so lower income and jobs in its export industries (or the risk of retaliation).
10. A subsidy lowers home firms' costs without raising the price consumers pay. A tariff raises the price.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calling a quota a tax, or saying it raises revenue by default.
- Applying the tariff to the whole quantity demanded instead of the imports only.
- Writing a percentage change over the new value instead of the original.
- Describing only home gains, with nothing on trading partners or on consumers.
- Defining dumping as "cheap imports" without reference to cost or the home price.
- Repeating a reason instead of explaining why it matters for that industry.
- Ending an evaluation with no judgement.
