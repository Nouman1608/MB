## 4.10.1.1 Using the Earth's resources and sustainable development

Humans use the Earth's resources to provide **warmth, shelter, food and transport**.

- **Natural resources**, supplemented by **agriculture**, provide food, timber, clothing and fuels.
- **Finite resources** from the Earth, oceans and atmosphere are processed to provide energy and materials.

| Type of resource | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Finite** | Cannot be replaced once used, or takes millions of years | Crude oil, coal, metal ores, natural gas |
| **Renewable** | Can be replaced at the rate we use them | Wood (if trees are replanted), crops, wind, solar |

Whether a resource is finite or renewable depends on **how fast it is replaced compared with how fast it is
used**. Timber is renewable only when new trees are planted as fast as old ones are cut.

### Natural products replaced or supplemented

Many natural products are **supplemented or replaced** by **agricultural and synthetic products**:

| Natural product | Agricultural or synthetic alternative |
|---|---|
| Natural rubber | Synthetic rubber |
| Wool and cotton | Nylon and polyester fibres |
| Timber | Plastics, composites, farmed timber |
| Natural food flavours | Synthetic flavourings |
| Natural dyes | Synthetic dyes |

### Sustainable development

**Sustainable development** is development that **meets the needs of current generations without compromising
the ability of future generations to meet their own needs**. Chemistry helps by improving agricultural and
industrial processes and providing new products. Chemists aim to **minimise the use of limited resources, energy,
waste and environmental impact**.

### Worked example 1: orders of magnitude

*A mineral has world reserves of 4.5 × 10⁹ tonnes. World use is 3 × 10⁷ tonnes per year. Estimate how many years
the reserves will last at that rate.*

```
years = 4.5 x 10^9 / 3 x 10^7 = 1.5 x 10^2 = 150 years
```

The answer is an **order of magnitude** estimate, because use will change. A comparison of two quantities to the
nearest power of ten is used "to evaluate the significance of data": 10⁶ compared with 10³ is 1000 times greater.

## 4.10.1.2 Potable water

**Potable water** is water that is **safe to drink**. It needs **sufficiently low levels of dissolved salts and
microbes**. Potable water is **not pure water** in the chemical sense, because it contains dissolved substances.
Pure water is only H₂O.

### Treating fresh water in the UK

In the UK, rain provides water with **low levels of dissolved substances** (fresh water) that collects in the
ground, lakes and rivers. Most potable water is produced by:

1. **Choosing an appropriate source** of fresh water (reservoirs, rivers, aquifers).
2. **Passing it through filter beds** to remove solid particles. Fine sand and gravel filter out insoluble
   matter.
3. **Sterilising** to kill microbes. Agents include **chlorine, ozone or ultraviolet light**.

| Step | Reason |
|---|---|
| Choose a source | Low levels of salts and pollution from the start |
| Filter beds | Remove insoluble particles |
| Sterilise | Kill bacteria and other microbes that cause disease |

### Treating salty water: desalination

If supplies of fresh water are limited, **desalination** of salty water or sea water may be needed. Methods:

- **Distillation:** boil the water, condense the steam. The salts stay behind.
- **Reverse osmosis:** water is forced through a **membrane** that lets water through but holds back dissolved
  salts.

Both processes need **large amounts of energy**, so they are expensive and used where fresh water is scarce.

### Required practical 8: analysing and purifying water

You analyse and purify samples from different sources (for example sea water, river water, rock salt solution):

1. **pH:** test with universal indicator or a pH meter.
2. **Dissolved solids:** evaporate a measured volume of the sample to dryness and weigh the residue.
3. **Purification by distillation:** heat the sample in a flask; condense the vapour in a condenser; collect the
   distilled water.

### Worked example 2: dissolved solids

*25.0 cm³ of a water sample is evaporated and leaves 0.0125 g of solid. Calculate the mass of dissolved solids in
g/dm³.*

```
volume in dm3 = 25.0 / 1000 = 0.0250 dm3
mass per dm3  = 0.0125 / 0.0250 = 0.50 g/dm3
```

Distilled water leaves no residue, so it is much purer. Test with silver nitrate and nitric acid: sea water gives a
white precipitate (chloride ions), but distilled water does not.

## 4.10.1.3 Waste water treatment

Urban lifestyles and industry produce large amounts of **waste water** that need treatment before it goes back
into the environment.

- **Sewage and agricultural waste water:** removal of **organic matter** and **harmful microbes**.
- **Industrial waste water:** removal of organic matter and **harmful chemicals**.

### Stages of sewage treatment

1. **Screening and grit removal:** screens remove large objects and grit settles out.
2. **Sedimentation:** solids settle to form **sewage sludge**, leaving a liquid called **effluent**.
3. **Anaerobic digestion of the sludge:** bacteria break down the sludge without oxygen, producing biogas
   (methane), which can be used as fuel.
4. **Aerobic biological treatment of the effluent:** air is bubbled in so bacteria can break down organic
   matter.

### Comparing the ease of obtaining potable water

| Source | Ease | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Ground (fresh) water | Easiest | Low in dissolved salts; filter and sterilise |
| Waste water | Harder | Needs several stages to remove organic matter, microbes, chemicals |
| Salt water | Hardest | Needs desalination, with large energy use |

## 4.10.1.4 Alternative methods of extracting metals (Higher tier only)

The Earth's **metal ores are limited**. Copper ores are becoming **scarce**, so new ways of extracting copper from
**low-grade ores** are used: **phytomining** and **bioleaching**. They avoid traditional mining, which involves
digging, moving and disposing of large amounts of rock.

| Method | How it works |
|---|---|
| **Phytomining** | Plants absorb metal compounds from the soil. The plants are **harvested and burned**, and the **ash** contains metal compounds. |
| **Bioleaching** | **Bacteria** feed on the ore and produce a **leachate solution** containing metal compounds. |

The metal is obtained from the compounds, for example copper from a solution of copper compounds by
**displacement** using **scrap iron** (iron is more reactive than copper) or by **electrolysis**.

Fe + CuSO₄ → FeSO₄ + Cu

### Evaluating the methods

| Advantage | Disadvantage |
|---|---|
| Works on low-grade ores | Slow (plants take time to grow; bacteria take time to work) |
| Less damage to land than mining; lower energy | Needs a lot of land (phytomining) |
| Fewer waste rocks | Leachate can pollute water if it escapes |

## Exam technique

- Always distinguish **potable** from **pure**.
- Give **reasons** for each stage of water treatment, not just a list.
- For desalination, mention **large amounts of energy**.
- For sewage, name stages in the correct order.
- For evaluation, use the data given and give an advantage, a disadvantage and a conclusion.
- Use **orders of magnitude** for quick comparisons between large numbers.

## Common mistakes

- Saying potable water is pure, or that it has no dissolved substances.
- Saying chlorine is added to remove salt.
- Saying filter beds remove microbes. They remove solids; sterilising kills microbes.
- Saying anaerobic digestion needs oxygen.
- Mixing up phytomining (plants) and bioleaching (bacteria).
- Giving "burned" as a step in bioleaching.
