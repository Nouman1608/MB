## 1.4.1 Definition and drawing

A **production possibility curve** shows the maximum combinations of two goods (or two groups of goods) that an economy can produce when all its resources are fully and efficiently used, with a given state of technology.

The model rests on three assumptions: only two goods are shown, the quantity and quality of resources are fixed, and technology is fixed. If any of these change, the curve itself moves.

### Drawing checklist

- Label each axis with the good and its unit, for example "Medical services (thousand treatments)" and "Cars (thousands)".
- Plot each combination from the schedule and join the points with a smooth curve, not a series of straight segments, unless the question gives a straight-line PPC.
- Make the curve meet both axes. The intercepts show the maximum of each good when all resources make only that good.
- Label the curve "PPC" and any point you discuss with a letter.
- When showing a shift, label the original curve PPC1 and the new curve PPC2, and draw an arrow from one to the other.

### Building a curve from a schedule

Country Velora (invented) can produce these combinations of food and clothing in a year.

| Point | Food (million tonnes) | Clothing (million units) |
|---|---|---|
| V | 50 | 0 |
| W | 48 | 10 |
| X | 42 | 20 |
| Y | 30 | 30 |
| Z | 0 | 40 |

Putting clothing on the horizontal axis and food on the vertical axis gives a sketch like this:

```
Food
 50 V
 48  W
 42     X
 30         Y
  0                 Z   Clothing
  0  10  20  30  40
```

The curve slopes down from left to right, showing that more clothing means less food. It is bowed outward (concave to the origin) because some resources are better at one good than the other.

### Why the shape matters

| Shape | What it shows | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Bowed outward | Increasing opportunity cost | Resources are specialised; moving more of them to one good draws in factors less suited to it |
| Straight line | Constant opportunity cost | Resources are equally good at producing either good |

## 1.4.2 Points under, on and beyond the curve

| Location | What it tells you | Typical cause or example |
|---|---|---|
| On the curve | All resources fully and efficiently used; productive efficiency | Any point V to Z |
| Under the curve | Some resources unemployed or used inefficiently | Unemployment, idle factories, poor organisation |
| Beyond the curve | Unattainable with current resources and technology | Only reachable after an outward shift or through trade |

**Worked example: a point under the curve.** Velora produces 20 million units of clothing and 36 million tonnes of food (point U).

1. At 20 million units of clothing, point X shows 42 million tonnes of food is possible.
2. Food lost through unused resources = 42 − 36 = **6 million tonnes**.
3. As a percentage of possible food output = 6 ÷ 42 × 100 = **14.3%**.
4. Moving from U to X raises food output with no loss of clothing. Using idle resources has no opportunity cost in terms of the other good.

A point beyond the curve, such as 30 million units of clothing with 40 million tonnes of food, cannot be produced now because point Y shows only 30 million tonnes of food at that clothing level.

## 1.4.3 Movements along the curve

A movement along the curve is a reallocation of fully used resources from one good to the other. Because the economy is already on the curve, gaining more of one good always means giving up some of the other.

**Opportunity cost of a movement = amount of the good lost ÷ amount of the good gained.**

**Worked example: Velora's increasing cost.**

| Move | Clothing gained (million) | Food lost (million tonnes) | Food lost per million units of clothing |
|---|---|---|---|
| V to W | 10 | 2 | 0.2 |
| W to X | 10 | 6 | 0.6 |
| X to Y | 10 | 12 | 1.2 |
| Y to Z | 10 | 30 | 3.0 |

Each extra 10 million units of clothing costs more food. The first workers moved are those best suited to textile work; the last are farmers on the best land, whose skills suit food much better.

**A movement across several steps.** From W to Y, clothing rises by 20 million and food falls by 48 − 30 = 18 million tonnes. The opportunity cost is 18 ÷ 20 = **0.9 million tonnes of food per million units of clothing** on average over the move.

**Reading the cost the other way.** Moving from Y back to X gains 12 million tonnes of food and loses 10 million units of clothing. The opportunity cost of one million tonnes of food is 10 ÷ 12 = **0.83 million units of clothing**.

## 1.4.4 Shifts of the curve

A shift means the economy's productive capacity has changed. A movement along the curve is a choice; a shift is a change in what is possible.

### Causes

| Direction | Causes |
|---|---|
| Outward | More factors of production (discovery of resources, a larger labour force, net investment in capital); better quality of factors (education, training, better health); improved technology |
| Inward | Natural disasters, war, emigration of workers, disease reducing the workforce, depletion of non-renewable resources, capital wearing out without replacement |

### Types of shift

- **Outward shift of the whole curve:** both intercepts move out. A general improvement, such as a better-educated labour force, raises capacity for both goods.
- **Pivot on one axis:** only one intercept moves. New irrigation that raises Velora's maximum food from 50 to 60 million tonnes leaves maximum clothing at 40 million units. Growth in food capacity = (60 − 50) ÷ 50 × 100 = **20%**.
- **Inward shift:** a drought that cuts maximum food to 40 million tonnes moves the curve in on the food axis, a fall of (50 − 40) ÷ 50 × 100 = **20%**.

### Consequences for growth

An outward shift is **economic growth** in the sense of greater potential output. Its consequences:

- More of both goods can be produced, so living standards can rise.
- The economy can increase output of one good without cutting the other, compared with before the shift.
- Higher incomes can bring higher tax revenue for public services.
- Growth may bring costs not shown on the curve, such as pollution or the depletion of resources, which can later shift the curve inward.

An inward shift lowers potential output: fewer goods and services, lower incomes and possibly higher unemployment.

### Moving towards the curve is not the same as a shift

If unemployed workers find jobs, the economy moves from a point under the curve towards it. Output rises, but capacity has not changed. Only a change in the quantity or quality of resources, or in technology, shifts the curve.

## Common mistakes

- Unlabelled axes, or axes labelled "X" and "Y" instead of the goods.
- Describing a fall in unemployment as an outward shift.
- Dividing the wrong way: the good lost goes on top, the good gained on the bottom.
- Showing a pivot when the cause affects both goods, or a full shift when the cause affects only one.
- Saying a point beyond the curve is "inefficient". It is unattainable.
- Forgetting that a bowed curve means increasing opportunity cost.

## Exam technique for the 0455 command words

- **Define:** use "maximum", "two goods", "all resources fully and efficiently used".
- **Draw** (often inside an "Explain" question with "using a diagram"): label axes, curves and points; show the shift with an arrow.
- **Calculate:** show the subtraction and division, and give the unit of the good lost per unit of the good gained.
- **Identify:** from data, say whether a point is under, on or beyond the curve, giving the comparison figure.
- **Explain:** link the cause to the change in resources and then to the curve. "New technology raises output from the same resources, so maximum output of both goods rises, so the PPC shifts outward."
- **Discuss:** weigh consequences, such as more output against environmental costs, and conclude.
