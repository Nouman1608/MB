This guide covers subtopic **1.5 Business objectives and stakeholder objectives** of Cambridge O Level Business Studies 7115 (2026 series). The combined [Types of Business Organisation and Business Objectives study guide](/resources/o-level-business-types-of-business-organisation-and-business-objectives-and-stakeholder/) lists the main objectives and stakeholder groups. This page goes deeper into **why** objectives change, how to measure them, how to analyse a stakeholder conflict rather than just name it, and how private and public sector aims differ in practice.

## What the syllabus asks for

- **1.5.1** Businesses can have several objectives and the importance of them can change:
  - the need for business objectives and the importance of them
  - different business objectives, for example survival, growth, profit and market share
  - objectives of social enterprises
- **1.5.2** The role of stakeholder groups involved in business activity:
  - main internal and external stakeholder groups
  - objectives of different stakeholder groups
  - how these objectives might conflict with each other, using examples
- **1.5.3** Differences in the objectives of private sector and public sector enterprises.

## Why a business needs objectives

An objective is a target the business aims to achieve. Objectives matter because they:

- give managers and workers a **clear direction**, so everyone works towards the same targets
- help **decision-making**: a choice can be tested against whether it helps meet the objective
- allow **performance to be measured**, by comparing results with the target
- can **motivate** employees who know what they are aiming for
- help departments **coordinate**, for example marketing and production planning for the same sales target.

## The main objectives and how to measure them

| Objective | What it means | How it can be measured |
|---|---|---|
| Survival | Staying in business, especially in the first months or in a crisis | Enough cash to pay bills; still trading |
| Growth | Becoming larger | Sales revenue, number of outlets, number of employees |
| Profit | Revenue exceeding costs | Profit for the year compared with last year |
| Market share | The business's sales as a percentage of total market sales | Business sales ÷ total market sales × 100 |

**Worked example 1 (invented figures).** Lumen Lamps sold $420,000 of lamps in a market worth $3.5 million. The next year it sold $480,000 in a market worth $4.8 million.

```
Year 1 market share = 420,000 ÷ 3,500,000 × 100 = 12%
Year 2 market share = 480,000 ÷ 4,800,000 × 100 = 10%
Sales growth = (480,000 − 420,000) ÷ 420,000 × 100 = 14.3%
```

Lumen achieved growth in sales but **lost** market share, because the market grew faster than it did. Whether that is a success depends on which objective it set.

## Why the importance of objectives changes

A business can pursue several objectives, but their priority shifts with circumstances.

- **New business**: survival comes first; without it no other objective is possible.
- **Established business with loyal customers**: growth or market share may become the priority.
- **A new competitor enters**: survival or protecting market share may return to the top.
- **An economic downturn**: falling demand may push survival above growth.
- **Before seeking investors or a loan**: profit may be stressed to show the business is a good risk.
- **After public criticism**: social or environmental targets may rise in importance.

## Objectives of social enterprises

A **social enterprise** is a business whose main purpose is to meet a social or environmental need, using profit to support that purpose. Its objectives are often described under three headings:

- **social**: for example, providing jobs for people who find work hard to get, or affordable services for a community
- **environmental**: for example, recycling waste or reducing pollution
- **financial**: making enough profit to survive and reinvest in the social purpose, rather than maximising profit for owners.

**Example (invented).** Second Thread repairs and resells donated clothes and employs young people leaving care. Its targets might be: train 12 young people a year (social), keep 20 tonnes of clothing out of landfill (environmental), and cover its costs with a small surplus (financial).

## Stakeholders and their objectives

A **stakeholder** is any person or group with an interest in the activities of a business.

| Group | Internal or external | Typical objectives |
|---|---|---|
| Owners or shareholders | Internal | Profit, dividends, rising value of the business |
| Managers | Internal | Higher salaries, status, a growing business, job security |
| Employees | Internal | Fair pay, job security, safe conditions, training and promotion |
| Customers | External | Good quality, fair prices, reliable service, choice |
| Suppliers | External | Regular orders, prompt payment, fair prices |
| Lenders such as banks | External | Loans repaid on time with interest |
| Government | External | Tax revenue, jobs, businesses obeying the law |
| Local community | External | Jobs, little pollution or noise, support for local projects |

## Analysing stakeholder conflict

Naming two groups is not enough. Explain what the business plans to do, what each group wants, and why one gains while the other loses.

**Worked example 2 (invented business).** Marlow Dairy plans to replace 30 milking staff with automated machines.

- **Shareholders** gain: lower wage costs should raise profit and dividends.
- **Employees** lose: jobs are cut, and remaining workers may fear further cuts.
- **Local community** may lose: less spending in local shops, but may gain if the dairy stays competitive and does not close.
- **Customers** may gain if lower costs allow lower milk prices.

A good judgement says whose objective the business is likely to put first and why (for example, shareholders, because they own the business), and how the conflict might be reduced, such as offering retraining to some staff to maintain machines.

Not every decision causes conflict. Higher quality may please customers and, through more sales, shareholders too. Recognising when objectives **align** is also analysis.

## Private sector and public sector objectives

| | Private sector enterprises | Public sector enterprises |
|---|---|---|
| Main aim | Usually profit, with survival, growth and market share | Providing a service to the public |
| Pricing | Set to make a profit | May be kept low or free at the point of use |
| Who they answer to | Owners or shareholders | The government and, through it, the public |
| Other aims | Social goals if they support reputation and sales | Serving all areas, protecting jobs, staying within the budget the government sets |

The difference is about **priorities**, not absolutes: a public corporation may still be asked to cover its costs, and a private business may set environmental targets.

## Common mistakes

- Saying a business has only one objective.
- Calculating market share from the business's sales alone, without the market total.
- Treating growth in sales as growth in market share.
- Listing stakeholders without stating each group's objective.
- Stating that public sector enterprises "never care about money".

## Exam technique for 7115 command words

- **Identify / State**: name an objective or a stakeholder group.
- **Calculate**: show the market share or growth formula with figures.
- **Explain**: link the objective or stakeholder to the case, such as "as a new café facing two competitors, survival is the priority because…".
- **Consider / Justify**: weigh two stakeholder groups and decide whose objective is more important for this decision.
