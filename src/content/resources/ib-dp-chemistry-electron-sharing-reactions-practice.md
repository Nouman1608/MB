## Questions

**1.** Define the term radical and explain why radicals are highly reactive. **[2]**

**2.** Using valence electron counts, identify which of the following species are radicals: CN, CN⁻, ClO, H₂O₂, CHCl₂. **[3]**

**3.** The species H•, NO, C₂H₆•⁺ and CO₂•⁻ all have an unpaired electron.

**(a)** State whether each one is an atom, a neutral molecule, a cation or an anion. **[2]**
**(b)** Show by counting valence electrons that C₂H₆⁺ and CO₂⁻ must be radicals. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why the chloride ion, Cl⁻, is an ion but not a radical. **[1]**

**4.** Chlorine reacts with alkanes in ultraviolet light.

**(a)** Write an equation for the homolytic fission of chlorine. **[1]**
**(b)** Describe how single-barbed arrows are drawn to show this process. **[2]**
**(c)** State the name of this step in the chain reaction and one condition, other than UV light, that can cause it. **[1]**

**5.** Radicals can combine as well as form.

**(a)** State the process that is the reverse of homolytic fission and write an equation for one example of it involving bromine radicals. **[2]**
**(b)** Chlorine radicals in the stratosphere can break down ozone, O₃, but not oxygen, O₂. Suggest what this indicates about the oxygen–oxygen bonds in the two allotropes, and explain your answer in terms of bonding. **[2]**

**6.** Cyclobutane, C₄H₈, reacts with chlorine in UV light to give chlorocyclobutane, C₄H₇Cl.

**(a)** Write the two propagation steps that form chlorocyclobutane. **[2]**
**(b)** Write three possible termination steps. **[3]**
**(c)** State the molecular formula of the hydrocarbon formed in one of your termination steps. **[1]**

**7.** Octane reacts with bromine in UV light. One product is 2-bromooctane, CH₃CHBr(CH₂)₅CH₃, formed with hydrogen bromide. Use these average bond enthalpies (kJ mol⁻¹): C–H 414, Br–Br 193, C–Br 285, H–Br 366.

**(a)** Calculate ΔH for each of the two propagation steps. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate ΔH for the overall reaction from your answers to (a), and confirm it using the bonds broken and made in the overall equation. **[2]**
**(c)** State the sign of ΔH for every termination step and explain why. **[1]**

**8.** Alkanes are often described as kinetically stable but thermodynamically unstable.

**(a)** Explain this description. **[2]**
**(b)** Give two features of alkane molecules that account for their low reactivity. **[2]**

**9.** Propane is mixed with bromine vapour and exposed to UV light. The products include 1-bromopropane, 2-bromopropane, 2,2-dibromopropane and some hydrocarbons of formula C₆H₁₄.

**(a)** Write two propagation steps that convert 2-bromopropane into 2,2-dibromopropane. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why two monobromo products form. **[2]**
**(c)** Write an equation for the formation of hexane in this mixture. **[1]**

**10.** A sample of 6.01 g of 2,4-dimethylpentane, (CH₃)₂CHCH₂CH(CH₃)₂, is reacted with chlorine in UV light. After separation, 3.15 g of 3-chloro-2,4-dimethylpentane, C₇H₁₅Cl, is collected.

**(a)** Deduce how many monochloro structural isomers 2,4-dimethylpentane can form, and name them. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage yield of 3-chloro-2,4-dimethylpentane. **[3]**
**(c)** The yield is far below the theoretical maximum. Using the radical mechanism, explain two causes. **[2]**
**(d)** Suggest how the proportion of monochloro products could be increased. **[1]**

**11.** Chlorotrifluoromethane, CClF₃, is a CFC. In the stratosphere it absorbs UV radiation. Use these values (kJ mol⁻¹): C–Cl 324, C–F 492.

**(a)** Write an equation for the homolytic fission that takes place, and show by counting valence electrons that the carbon-containing product is a radical. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why fluorine radicals are not typically released. **[2]**
**(c)** The chlorine radicals then react in a cycle: Cl• + O₃ → ClO• + O₂, followed by ClO• + O → Cl• + O₂. Classify these steps and explain why a single chlorine radical can destroy many ozone molecules. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** A molecular entity with an unpaired electron [1]. The unpaired electron makes it react readily to pair up, by removing an atom from a molecule or combining with another radical [1] **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* "A species with a single electron" is too vague; the key word is unpaired.

**2.** CN: 4 + 5 = 9, odd, **radical** [1]; ClO: 7 + 6 = 13, odd, **radical** [1]; CHCl₂: 4 + 1 + 14 = 19, odd, **radical**, while CN⁻ (10) and H₂O₂ (14) are even and not radicals [1] **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the electron count for each species; a bare list of names shows no reasoning, so a single slip leaves nothing creditworthy.

**3. (a)** H• **atom**; NO **neutral molecule** [1]; C₂H₆•⁺ **cation**; CO₂•⁻ **anion** [1]
**(b)** C₂H₆⁺: 2 × 4 + 6 − 1 = **13**, odd [1]; CO₂⁻: 4 + 2 × 6 + 1 = **17**, odd [1]
**(c)** Cl⁻ has 8 valence electrons, all paired (a full octet), so there is no unpaired electron [1]
*Examiner insight:* Remember to adjust the count for charge: subtract for a positive charge and add for a negative one.

**4. (a)** **Cl₂ → 2Cl•** [1]
**(b)** Each arrow has a single barb (half arrowhead) and starts at the centre of the Cl–Cl bond [1]; one arrow goes to each chlorine atom, showing one electron moving to each [1]
**(c)** **Initiation**; heat (high temperature) [1]
*Examiner insight:* Dots on the radicals are part of the equation; Cl₂ → 2Cl is not the same answer.

**5. (a)** Bond formation between two radicals, each giving one electron (a termination step) [1]; **Br• + Br• → Br₂** [1]
**(b)** The oxygen–oxygen bonds in O₃ are weaker than the bond in O₂ [1]; O₃ has resonance, so each O–O bond has a bond order of about 1.5, while O₂ has a full double bond [1]
*Examiner insight:* For (b), "ozone is less stable" does not answer the question; compare bond strength and give the bonding reason.

**6. (a)** Cl• + C₄H₈ → C₄H₇• + HCl [1]; C₄H₇• + Cl₂ → C₄H₇Cl + Cl• [1]
**(b)** Cl• + Cl• → Cl₂ [1]; C₄H₇• + Cl• → C₄H₇Cl [1]; C₄H₇• + C₄H₇• → C₈H₁₄ [1]
**(c)** **C₈H₁₄** (two four-membered rings joined by a C–C bond) [1]
*Examiner insight:* Each propagation step must have one radical on each side; count the dots before moving on.

**7. (a)** Step 1, Br• + C₈H₁₈ → C₈H₁₇• + HBr: +414 − 366 = **+48 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]. Step 2, C₈H₁₇• + Br₂ → C₈H₁₇Br + Br•: +193 − 285 = **−92 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]
**(b)** +48 + (−92) = **−44 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]; broken 414 + 193 = 607, made 285 + 366 = 651, so 607 − 651 = −44 kJ mol⁻¹, which agrees [1]
**(c)** Negative (exothermic): a bond forms and no bond breaks [1]
*Examiner insight:* Write "bonds broken minus bonds made" and keep the sign on every value; a dropped sign turns the endothermic first step into an exothermic one.

**8. (a)** The products of combustion are much lower in energy, so reaction with oxygen is very exothermic (thermodynamically unstable) [1]; but the activation energy is high, so at room temperature the reaction does not happen without a spark or flame (kinetically stable) [1]
**(b)** C–C and C–H bonds are strong [1]; the bonds are essentially non-polar because C and H have similar electronegativities, so nucleophiles and electrophiles are not attracted [1]
*Examiner insight:* "Kinetically stable" refers to rate, not to energy content; link it to activation energy, not to ΔH.

**9. (a)** Br• + CH₃CHBrCH₃ → CH₃C•BrCH₃ + HBr [1]; CH₃C•BrCH₃ + Br₂ → CH₃CBr₂CH₃ + Br• [1]
**(b)** Propane has hydrogens in two environments, on the end CH₃ groups and on the central CH₂ [1]; Br• can remove either, giving 1-bromopropane or 2-bromopropane [1]
**(c)** Two propyl radicals combine in a termination step: CH₃CH₂CH₂• + CH₃CH₂CH₂• → CH₃(CH₂)₄CH₃ [1]
*Examiner insight:* In (a) the second bromine goes on the carbon that carries the dot, so show the radical's structure, not just C₃H₆Br•.

**10. (a)** Three hydrogen environments (the four CH₃ groups, the two CH carbons, the central CH₂), so **three** isomers [1]: **1-chloro-2,4-dimethylpentane, 2-chloro-2,4-dimethylpentane and 3-chloro-2,4-dimethylpentane** [1]
**(b)** M(C₇H₁₆) = 100.20 g mol⁻¹, n = 6.01 ÷ 100.20 = 0.05998 mol [1]; M(C₇H₁₅Cl) = 134.64 g mol⁻¹, so maximum mass = 0.05998 × 134.64 = 8.08 g [1]; yield = 3.15 ÷ 8.08 × 100 = **39.0 %** [1]
**(c)** Chlorine can replace hydrogens in the other two environments, giving the other isomers [1]; further substitution gives dichloro and more highly substituted products, and termination gives other hydrocarbons [1]
**(d)** Use an excess of the alkane so that Cl• is more likely to meet alkane than chloroalkane [1]
*Examiner insight:* Carry unrounded values for the moles and the maximum mass, then round only the percentage at the end.

**11. (a)** CClF₃ → CF₃• + Cl• [1]; CF₃: 4 + 3 × 7 = 25, odd, so it is a radical [1]
**(b)** The C–F bond (492) is 168 kJ mol⁻¹ stronger than C–Cl (324) [1], so the UV radiation reaching the stratosphere has enough energy to break C–Cl but not C–F [1]
**(c)** Both are propagation steps, because each uses one radical and produces one radical [1]; the Cl• used in the first step is regenerated in the second [1]; so the cycle repeats many times until a termination step removes the radical [1]
*Examiner insight:* Say that the chlorine radical is regenerated; that is the point the explanation of the chain needs.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Leaving the dot off a radical, or putting it on the wrong atom.
- Giving a halogen radical a negative charge.
- Showing homolytic fission with a full curly arrow or with one arrow only.
- Writing a propagation step that produces H• instead of HX.
- Listing only Br• + Br• → Br₂ when several termination steps are asked for.
- Forgetting further substitution or position isomers when explaining a mixture of products.
- Mixing up kinetic and thermodynamic stability.
- Rounding moles early in yield calculations.
- Comparing O₃ and O₂ without mentioning bond order or bond strength.
