This guide covers **Natural Resources – An Issue of Sustainability**,
the second of eight sections in Paper 2 The Environment of Pakistan,
Cambridge O Level Pakistan Studies (2059), 2026 series. It follows on
directly from Marlbridge's existing guide to Section 1 (The Land of
Pakistan), and shares an identical eight-section structure and content
with sibling Cambridge IGCSE Pakistan Studies (0448), independently
confirmed against 2059's own official syllabus PDF for this guide.

## Where this fits in 2059

Pakistan Studies 2059 splits into two compulsory papers: Paper 1, The
History and Culture of Pakistan, and Paper 2, The Environment of
Pakistan. Paper 2 is assessed through three questions (25 marks each)
from a choice of five, each requiring the interpretation and analysis
of resources — diagrams, graphs, maps, photographs and written
material — with part (d) of every question specifically testing
analysis skills. Section 2 builds directly on the map and topography
knowledge from Section 1: understanding where Pakistan's mountains,
rivers and plains are located is assumed knowledge for explaining how
its natural resources are distributed and exploited.

## Syllabus coverage

**CAMBRIDGE O LEVEL PAKISTAN STUDIES 2059 — PAPER 2, SECTION 2: NATURAL RESOURCES – AN ISSUE OF SUSTAINABILITY**

- 2(a) Water — identifying the Mangla, Tarbela and Warsak dams and two
  example barrages on a map; the importance of water as a resource for
  agricultural, industrial and domestic use, including how supplies
  are obtained, maintained and controlled; the reasons for and
  consequences of the Indus Water Treaty; the causes of and solutions
  to water supply problems (including pollution); water's value as a
  development resource; how water supply issues can lead to conflict
- 2(b) Forests — the different types of forest and their main
  locations; the physical and human factors controlling forest
  distribution; the reasons for plantation development in the Indus
  Plain and afforestation on mountain slopes and plateaux; forests'
  value as a development resource and the importance of sustainability;
  the effects of deforestation (soil erosion, silting, flooding) and
  possible solutions
- 2(c) Mineral resources — the main locations of limestone, gypsum and
  rock salt extraction; the main metallic and non-metallic mineral
  resources, their extracted quantities and reserves; the extent to
  which they can be exploited; environmental problems caused by mineral
  extraction; the benefits and sustainability of developing mineral
  resources; the main imported minerals and their sources and
  quantities
- 2(d) Fish — fishing methods in marine and inland waters, including
  fish farms; examples of fish caught in marine and inland waters and
  reared on fish farms; the fishing ports of the Balochistan and Sindh
  coasts; the uses of the fish caught; improvements in fishing methods
  and processing; the problems facing the fishing industry and the
  possibilities for its further development and sustainability

## Water (2a): the most exam-heavy sub-section

Water combines map-identification skills (locating the three named
dams and identifying barrages) with genuinely evaluative content — the
Indus Water Treaty is the single most exam-significant item in this
sub-section, since it requires candidates to explain both *why* the
treaty exists and *what consequences* have followed from it, not just
state that it exists. Practise a balanced answer structure for water
supply problems: name a specific cause (for example, industrial
pollution or over-extraction), pair it with a specific solution, and be
ready to explain how water issues can create conflict between regions
or user groups competing for the same supply.

## Forests (2b)

The plantation-versus-afforestation distinction is the detail most
often blurred by candidates: plantations in the Indus Plain are new
forest planted specifically for commercial or resource use, while
afforestation on mountain slopes and plateaux is typically aimed at
preventing soil erosion and managing water flow. Both physical factors
(climate, terrain) and human factors (deforestation for agriculture or
fuel) should be paired when explaining forest distribution, since
"explain" questions here consistently expect both sides of the
explanation rather than one alone.

## Mineral resources (2c)

This sub-section rewards precision with numbers and locations over
general description — know specifically where limestone, gypsum and
rock salt are extracted, and be able to distinguish Pakistan's
metallic from non-metallic mineral resources. The "evaluate the
benefits... and understand the sustainability of extraction" bullet is
where analysis-focused marks are won: pair a named benefit (for
example, export revenue or industrial input) with a named environmental
cost, rather than listing benefits alone.

## Fish (2d)

The marine-versus-inland distinction runs through this entire
sub-section: marine fishing draws on the Balochistan and Sindh coasts,
while inland fishing includes rivers, lakes and fish farms. Improvements
in fishing methods and processing techniques should be learned as a
pair with the sustainability challenges the industry faces — the
syllabus explicitly links "improvements" with "problems facing the
fishing industry," so an answer describing improvements without also
addressing ongoing sustainability problems is incomplete.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating the Indus Water Treaty as a fact to state** rather than an
  issue to explain, including its reasons and consequences.
- **Confusing plantations with afforestation** — one is Indus Plain
  commercial planting, the other is mountain/plateau erosion
  prevention.
- **Giving generic "pollution is bad" answers on mineral extraction**
  instead of naming specific environmental problems tied to specific
  resources.
- **Discussing fishing methods without addressing sustainability**,
  when the syllabus explicitly pairs the two.

## How to approach it

Build a single resource-by-resource table across all four
sub-sections — water, forests, minerals, fish — with columns for
location, use, and sustainability issue, since Paper 2 exam questions
frequently ask candidates to evaluate the sustainability of a named
resource specifically. Revisit Section 1's map work (dams, rivers,
coastal regions) alongside this section, since almost every Section 2
sub-topic asks candidates to locate a feature before analysing it.
