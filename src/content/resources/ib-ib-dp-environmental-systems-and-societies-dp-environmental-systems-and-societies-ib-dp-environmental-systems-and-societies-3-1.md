Subtopic 3.1 Biodiversity and evolution opens Topic 3 Biodiversity and conservation of IB Diploma
Programme Environmental Systems and Societies (ESS), first assessment 2026. The IB's public subject
brief names Topic 3 but not its subtopics, so this title follows the course's provisional checklist;

The [biodiversity and conservation study guide](/resources/ib-dp-ess-biodiversity-conservation/)
defines the three levels of biodiversity, separates richness from evenness, works Simpson's index,
lists the four steps of natural selection, and outlines speciation, plate tectonics and mass
extinctions. This guide goes deeper on the evolutionary half: where variation comes from, natural
selection observed in real populations, the different ways populations become isolated, why some
groups diversify rapidly, how plate movements have shaped where species live, and what made each mass
extinction different from the present one.

## What 3.1 asks you to do

- Explain biodiversity at genetic, species and habitat levels and why the levels are linked.
- Explain how natural selection acting on variation leads to evolution.
- Explain speciation through isolation, and the role of plate tectonics.
- Describe mass extinctions and compare them with present-day biodiversity loss.

## Why genetic diversity matters

Genetic diversity is the raw material for evolution. A population with many different alleles is
more likely to contain individuals that survive a new disease, a change in climate or a new predator.
A population with low genetic diversity, such as one descended from a few survivors, can be wiped out
by a single pressure it cannot adapt to. This is why conservation programmes track the genetic
diversity of small populations, and why crop varieties grown as near-identical plants are vulnerable
to a single pathogen.

## Where variation comes from

- **Mutation:** random changes in DNA create new alleles. Most are neutral or harmful; a few are
  beneficial in a particular environment.
- **Sexual reproduction:** meiosis shuffles alleles into new combinations, and fertilisation joins
  alleles from two parents, so offspring differ from each other and from their parents.

Variation that is not inherited, such as a scar or a skill learned during life, cannot be selected,
because it is not passed on.

## Natural selection in real populations

### Industrial melanism in the peppered moth

In Britain, the peppered moth (*Biston betularia*) has a pale form and a dark (melanic) form. During
the 19th century, soot from industry darkened tree bark around cities, and the dark form became
common in those areas, while the pale form remained common in cleaner countryside. After air
pollution was reduced in the 20th century, the pale form increased again. The explanation is
selective predation by birds: moths that contrast with their background are more likely to be seen
and eaten.

### Resistance

Bacteria exposed repeatedly to an antibiotic, and insects exposed to an insecticide, evolve
resistance within years. The chemical does not create resistance; it kills susceptible individuals,
so the rare resistant ones leave more offspring.

### Worked example 1: tracking a change (fictional data)

A moth population is sampled in an industrial valley.

| Year | 1880 | 1900 | 1920 | 1960 | 2000 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Dark moths caught | 30 | 210 | 410 | 380 | 60 |
| Total moths caught | 400 | 420 | 450 | 400 | 410 |

```
Percentage dark in 1880 = 30 / 400 × 100 = 7.5 %
Percentage dark in 1920 = 410 / 450 × 100 = 91.1 % (3 s.f.)
Percentage dark in 2000 = 60 / 410 × 100 = 14.6 % (3 s.f.)
```

Use percentages, not raw counts, because the sample sizes differ. The rise then fall matches the
increase and later reduction in soot. A strong answer adds the mechanism: dark moths were better
camouflaged on sooty bark, so fewer were eaten, so they left more offspring carrying the allele for
dark colour; when bark became paler again, the advantage reversed.

## Isolation and speciation

Speciation needs gene flow between populations to stop. The main isolating mechanisms are:

- **Geographical isolation:** a physical barrier, such as a sea, river or mountain range, separates
  populations. Speciation following geographical separation is called **allopatric speciation**.
- **Temporal isolation:** populations breed at different times of day or year.
- **Behavioural isolation:** differences in courtship songs, displays or scents mean individuals no
  longer recognise each other as mates.

Temporal and behavioural isolation can operate between populations living in the same area,
producing **sympatric speciation**.

### Adaptive radiation

When a single ancestral species colonises an area with many unoccupied niches, its descendants can
diversify rapidly into many species, each adapted to a different niche. Darwin's finches on the
Galápagos Islands are the classic example: descendants of one colonising species evolved different
beak shapes suited to different foods on different islands. Adaptive radiation also followed mass
extinctions, as surviving groups filled emptied niches; mammals diversified after the end-Cretaceous
extinction.

### Gradual or sudden?

Some lineages appear in the fossil record to change slowly and steadily (gradualism). Others show
long periods of little change interrupted by rapid bursts (punctuated equilibrium, proposed by Niles
Eldredge and Stephen Jay Gould in 1972). Both patterns can be explained by natural selection acting at
different rates as environments change.

## Plate tectonics and the distribution of species

- **Separation of land masses** isolates populations. The break-up of the southern supercontinent
  Gondwana left Australia isolated for a long time, which is why marsupials diversified there with
  little competition from placental mammals.
- **Joining of land masses** allows species to spread and compete. The formation of the Isthmus of
  Panama, roughly 3 million years ago, connected North and South America and allowed land mammals to
  move in both directions; at the same time it separated marine populations in the Atlantic and
  Pacific.
- **Mountain building** creates new habitats along altitudinal gradients and new barriers.

## The five mass extinctions

| Event | Approximate time | Leading explanation |
|---|---|---|
| End-Ordovician | about 444 million years ago | Glaciation and falling sea level |
| Late Devonian | about 372 million years ago | Ocean oxygen loss and climate change |
| End-Permian | about 252 million years ago | Massive volcanic eruptions (Siberian Traps), warming and ocean acidification; the largest known extinction |
| End-Triassic | about 201 million years ago | Volcanic activity as the supercontinent Pangaea began to split |
| End-Cretaceous | about 66 million years ago | Asteroid impact, with volcanic activity also implicated |

### How the present differs

Many scientists describe the present as a possible sixth mass extinction. Two differences stand out.
First, the cause is the activity of a single species, through habitat loss, overexploitation,
invasive species, pollution and climate change. Second, the rate of loss is happening over centuries
rather than over many thousands of years. The fossil record shows recovery of diversity after each
event, but it took millions of years each time.

## Annotated answer

*Original question.* **Explain how a new species could arise from a population of lizards on an
island chain. [5]**

> A storm carries a few lizards to a second island, so the two populations are **geographically
> isolated** and gene flow stops. The founding group carries only some of the original alleles. On
> the new island, food and predators differ, so **different selection pressures** act: for example,
> lizards with longer legs may escape a ground predator better. Individuals with favourable
> **heritable variations** survive and reproduce more, so **allele frequencies change** over many
> generations. Eventually the populations differ so much that, even if they met, they could **not
> interbreed to produce fertile offspring**: a new species has formed.

*Why it scores well:* every marking point is present, in order: isolation, no gene flow, different
pressures, selection on heritable variation, allele frequencies, reproductive incompatibility.

## Common mistakes

- Saying organisms evolve "because they need to".
- Using raw counts instead of percentages when sample sizes differ.
- Forgetting that isolation must stop gene flow.
- Confusing adaptive radiation with speciation in general.
- Giving mass extinction causes without approximate times or the scale of loss.

## Exam technique

"Explain" evolution questions need the full chain from variation to changed allele frequencies.
"Compare" the present with past extinctions needs at least one similarity and one difference.
"Outline" plate tectonics needs the geological event and its biological effect.
