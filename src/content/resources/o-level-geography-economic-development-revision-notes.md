## The seven subtopics, all needing a named case study

| Subtopic | Case study required |
|---|---|
| 3.1 Development | A transnational corporation and its global links |
| 3.2 Food production | A farm/agricultural system; a food-shortage country/region |
| 3.3 Industry | An industrial zone or factory |
| 3.4 Tourism | An area where tourism is important |
| 3.5 Energy | Energy supply in a country/area |
| 3.6 Water | Water supply in a country/area |
| 3.7 Environmental risks | An area with environmental risk from development |

**One case study can support more than one subtopic** if it genuinely
fits (e.g. the same transnational corporation for 3.1 and 3.3) — but
each still needs concrete, place-specific facts, not a vague example.

## 3.1 Development — indicators and sectors

| Indicator type | Example |
|---|---|
| Economic | GNP per capita |
| Social | Literacy rate, life expectancy |
| Composite | HDI (avoids single-indicator distortion) |

**Sectors shift with development**: primary (extraction) →
secondary (manufacturing) → tertiary/quaternary (services,
research/information) — explain the *shift*, don't just recall the
diagram. **Globalisation**: weigh positives (investment, jobs)
against negatives (loss of local control, environmental cost).

## 3.2–3.3 Food production and industry — same systems approach

Both use **inputs → processes → outputs** (industry's outputs
include waste). For farming: natural inputs (relief, climate, soil) +
human inputs (economic, social) determine commercial/subsistence,
arable/pastoral/mixed, intensive/extensive classification.

**Food shortage causes**: natural (drought, floods, storms, pests) vs
economic/political (low investment, poor transport, conflict).

**Industry location factors**: land, labour, raw materials, fuel and
power, transport, markets, political factors.

## 3.4–3.6 Tourism, energy, water

| Theme | Structure |
|---|---|
| Tourism | Growth (physical/human attractions) → impact (benefits vs disadvantages) → management (sustainability) |
| Energy | Non-renewable, renewable, nuclear, fuelwood — **evaluate** nuclear and renewable specifically |
| Water | Supply methods (reservoirs/dams, wells/boreholes, desalination); use split (agriculture/domestic/industrial) shifts with development |

Energy and tourism are the two subtopics singled out for
**evaluation**, not just description — always weigh both sides.

## 3.7 Environmental risks — the theme's synthesis

Named threats: **soil erosion, desertification, enhanced global
warming, pollution** (water, air, noise, visual). This subtopic is
most often examined by **linking back to an earlier process** (e.g.
industry's waste output, or energy's fossil-fuel use) — revise it as
a synthesis, not a standalone list.

## Worked example: evaluating an energy source

A question asks you to evaluate a country's decision to expand
nuclear power generation.

```
Benefit:     Low ongoing carbon emissions once built, and a
             reliable, high-output power source not dependent on
             weather conditions (unlike wind or solar).
Drawback:    Very high upfront construction cost and long build
             time, plus the unresolved long-term problem of
             radioactive waste disposal and the (low-probability
             but high-impact) risk of a serious accident.
Balanced
conclusion:  Nuclear power can be a genuine solution to rising
             energy demand with lower emissions than fossil fuels,
             but the decision depends heavily on a country's ability
             to fund the high upfront cost and manage waste safely
             over the long term -- exactly the kind of weighed
             judgement an "evaluate" command word is asking for,
             rather than a one-sided list of advantages.
```

The same weighed structure -- a genuine benefit, a genuine drawback,
then a conclusion that depends on context -- transfers directly to
tourism evaluation questions too.

## Exam traps

- Having only a vague, generic case-study example rather than real,
place-specific facts you can write under exam conditions.
- Reciting the primary→secondary→tertiary sector shift as a diagram
without explaining *why* it happens as development proceeds.
- Describing energy or tourism content one-sidedly when the command
word asks for evaluation.
- Treating food production and industry as unrelated to each other,
rather than recognising both use the same inputs-processes-outputs
structure.
- Forgetting environmental risks (3.7) connects back to processes
covered earlier in the theme, rather than being a standalone list of
named threats.

## Case study reuse: a practical revision shortcut

Given six of the seven subtopics demand a named case study, it is
far more efficient to deeply learn 3-4 well-chosen examples that each
span multiple subtopics than to memorise seven completely separate,
shallow examples. A single large transnational corporation, for
instance, can typically supply concrete detail for 3.1 (global links),
3.3 (industrial location factors) and potentially 3.7 (environmental
risk from its operations) if you learn its specific facts -- named
locations, named products, specific employment or investment figures
-- in enough depth to answer any of those three subtopics' questions
convincingly.

## Worked example: linking industry to an environmental risk

A question asks you to explain how industrial development can lead to
an environmental risk covered in 3.7, using your named case study.

```
Process (3.3):     A named industrial zone processes raw materials,
                    generating waste water and airborne emissions as
                    by-products of production.
Environmental
risk (3.7):         If untreated, the waste water discharged into a
                    nearby river causes water pollution, harming
                    aquatic life and reducing water quality for
                    downstream users; airborne emissions contribute
                    to air pollution and, at a larger scale, to
                    enhanced global warming.
Why this
matters for
revision:            This is exactly the "link back to an earlier
                    process" structure 3.7 questions reward -- naming
                    the pollution type alone, without tracing it back
                    to the industrial process that caused it, would
                    only partially answer the question.
```

Practising this same link-back structure with your own named case
study for food production (e.g. overuse of fertiliser causing water
pollution) or energy (e.g. fossil fuel extraction causing land
degradation) prepares you for whichever specific pairing a 3.7
question happens to draw on.

## Self-test

1. Which subtopics are singled out for evaluation rather than
description alone?
2. Name the four production sectors in order of typical development
progression.
3. What structure do both food production and industry share?
4. Name the four environmental risks specified in 3.7.
5. Why might the same case study be usable for more than one
subtopic?

**Answers:** 1. Energy and tourism. 2. Primary, secondary, tertiary,
quaternary. 3. Inputs, processes and outputs (industry's outputs
additionally include waste). 4. Soil erosion, desertification,
enhanced global warming, pollution (water, air, noise, visual).
5. Because a single real-world example, such as a transnational
corporation, can genuinely illustrate more than one subtopic's
content (e.g. both development's global links and industry's
location factors), provided it is supported with concrete,
place-specific detail relevant to each.
