## 2.9.1 Definition and features

A **market economic system** is an economic system in which resources are allocated through the price mechanism, with resources owned mainly by the private sector, and with little government intervention.

### Six features

| Feature | What it means |
|---|---|
| Private ownership | Households and firms own land, capital and businesses |
| Price mechanism | Demand and supply set prices, which signal where resources should go |
| Profit motive | Firms produce what they expect to be profitable |
| Consumer choice | Households decide what to buy with their incomes |
| Competition | Many firms compete for customers, pushing down costs and prices |
| Limited role for government | The state mainly protects property, enforces contracts, keeps law and order and defends the country |

### How it answers the three questions

- **What to produce:** goods that consumers are willing and able to buy at a price that gives firms a profit.
- **How to produce:** the method that gives the lowest cost, given the prices of factors.
- **For whom:** those with the income to pay, so the distribution of income decides who gets the output.

### No pure market economies exist

Every real country has some government provision, at least of defence, courts and police. Economies differ in how far they rely on markets. In many countries, everyday goods such as food, clothing and phones are allocated almost entirely by markets, while services such as schooling and roads are provided partly or wholly by the state. The question in an exam is usually how far a market system works well, not whether a country is a pure example.

## 2.9.2 Advantages

Each advantage follows from a feature. Explain it as a chain.

1. **Efficient use of resources.** Competition and the profit motive push firms to keep costs low, so resources are not wasted. A firm that wastes resources has higher costs and loses customers to rivals.
2. **Responds to consumer wants.** When tastes change, prices change, profits change and resources move. No planning office is needed to decide that more electric scooters and fewer petrol scooters are wanted.
3. **Wide choice.** Many firms offer different products, designs and prices, so consumers can find what suits them.
4. **Incentives to innovate.** Firms that develop better products or cheaper methods earn higher profits, so new technology spreads.
5. **Incentives to work and invest.** People keep most of what they earn, so effort and enterprise are rewarded.
6. **Less government cost.** Few officials are needed to run the allocation of resources, so taxes can be lower.

**Worked example: responding to wants.** In Lumo (a mainly market economy, invented), demand for solar panels rises sharply.

1. The price of a panel rises from 200 to 240, while cost per panel stays at 170.
2. Profit per panel rises from 200 − 170 = 30 to 240 − 170 = **70**, more than doubling.
3. Existing firms expand and four new firms enter within a year. Output rises from 50,000 to 80,000 panels, a rise of 30,000 ÷ 50,000 × 100 = **60%**.
4. As supply grows, the price falls back to 210. Consumers receive many more panels, and the extra profit attracted the resources needed to make them.

## 2.9.2 Disadvantages

Each disadvantage also follows from a feature.

1. **Inequality of income and wealth.** Output goes to those who can pay. People who own valuable resources or skills earn much more than those who do not, and the gap can widen over time.
2. **Some useful goods are not provided.** Goods that people cannot be made to pay for, such as street lighting, are not profitable to supply.
3. **Under-consumption of beneficial goods.** Some people may buy too little education or health care, because they cannot afford it or do not recognise its benefit.
4. **Over-consumption of harmful goods.** Products such as tobacco may be over-consumed, and firms may pollute because they do not pay for the damage done to others.
5. **Abuse of market power.** If one firm comes to dominate a market, it may raise prices and reduce choice.
6. **Unemployment of resources.** When industries decline, workers and capital may not move easily to new uses, so some stay unemployed.

These problems are developed as market failure in section 2.10.

**Worked example: who gets the output?** In Lumo, the richest 10% of households earn 40% of total income, and the poorest 10% earn 2%.

1. The richest group's share is 40 ÷ 2 = **20 times** that of the poorest group.
2. If total household income is 900 billion, the richest 10% earn 0.40 × 900 = **360 billion** and the poorest 10% earn 0.02 × 900 = **18 billion**.
3. Spending power follows income, so producers have a strong incentive to make goods the richest households want, even if poorer households lack basic goods.

## Comparing economies that rely on markets to different degrees

| Feature | Lumo (relies heavily on markets, invented) | Varn (relies less on markets, invented) |
|---|---|---|
| Income tax on high earners | 15% | 45% |
| Health care | Mostly bought privately or through private insurance | Mostly provided free at the point of use by the state |
| Choice of products | Very wide | Wide in shops; limited in state-run services |
| Gap between richest and poorest 10% | 20 times | 6 times |
| Pace of new firms starting | Fast | Slower |

The comparison shows the trade-off. Greater reliance on markets tends to bring stronger incentives and more choice, but also wider inequality and less provision of services that people cannot afford. A country does not choose "market" or "not market"; it chooses how far to rely on markets in each part of the economy.

## Building a judgement

When asked to discuss whether a market economic system is desirable, use three tests.

1. **Which products?** For everyday goods such as clothes and food, markets usually work well. For goods such as defence, or where external costs are large, they work badly.
2. **Which people?** Those with skills and assets tend to gain most; those on low incomes may be left without essentials.
3. **Which goal?** If the goal is efficiency and choice, markets score well. If the goal is fairness, they score less well.

A strong conclusion says which advantage or disadvantage matters most in the context given, and why.

## Common mistakes

- Defining the market system as "no government at all". Government still enforces laws and property rights.
- Listing advantages and disadvantages without the feature that causes each one.
- Saying a market economy gives goods to those who "need" them most.
- Treating all disadvantages as permanent: competition laws or taxes can reduce some of them, which is why most economies are mixed.
- Using real-country examples with figures you cannot be sure of. Invented figures, clearly labelled, are safer.

## Exam technique for the 0455 command words

- **Define:** "resources allocated through the price mechanism, mainly privately owned, with little government intervention".
- **Describe:** give features such as private ownership, profit motive, consumer choice and competition.
- **Explain:** link a feature to an advantage or disadvantage in a chain of two or three steps.
- **Analyse:** use data such as income shares or numbers of new firms to show how the system is working.
- **Discuss:** weigh at least two advantages against two disadvantages and conclude with the condition the judgement depends on.
