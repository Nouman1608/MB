## Section B

**7.** Explain why the magnesium ion is smaller than the sodium ion, and why the chloride ion is larger than both. **[4]**

**8.** Explain why the melting point increases from sodium to aluminium. **[3]**

**9.** The melting point of silicon is very much higher than that of phosphorus.

**(a)** Explain the high melting point of silicon. **[2]**

**(b)** A student writes: "Phosphorus has a low melting point because the P–P bonds are weak." Explain why this is incorrect. **[2]**

**10.** Place the elements phosphorus, sulfur, chlorine and argon in order of decreasing melting point and explain the order. **[3]**

**11.** Describe and explain the variation in electrical conductivity of the elements from sodium to argon. **[5]**

**12.** The elements of Period 2, lithium to neon, show a similar pattern of melting points to Period 3.

**(a)** Predict which Period 2 element has the highest melting point, giving a reason based on its structure. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain why nitrogen has a much lower melting point than carbon, although both are non-metals. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** Atomic radius decreases [1]. The nuclear charge increases while the electrons are added to the same shell [1], and shielding stays roughly constant, so the outer electrons are attracted more strongly and pulled closer to the nucleus [1].

**2.** 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ [1]. P³⁻ or Cl⁻ [1].

**3.** Al³⁺ < Mg²⁺ < Cl⁻ < S²⁻ [1].

**4. (a)** Giant metallic lattice [1].

**(b)** Giant covalent (macromolecular) lattice [1].

**(c)** Simple molecular (S₈ molecules) [1].

**5.** Silicon [1]. Its conductivity increases on heating [1].

**6.** Instantaneous dipole–induced dipole (id-id, London) forces between atoms [1].

**7.** Na⁺ and Mg²⁺ have the same number of electrons (10, in two shells) [1]. Mg²⁺ has a greater nuclear charge (12 protons compared with 11), so its electrons are pulled closer and it is smaller [1]. Cl⁻ has 18 electrons in three shells [1]; the extra occupied shell makes it larger than both cations despite its higher nuclear charge [1].

**8.** Na, Mg and Al all have giant metallic lattices [1]. Across the three, the cation charge increases and the cation radius decreases [1], and the number of delocalised electrons per atom increases from 1 to 3, so the electrostatic attraction between the cations and delocalised electrons (the metallic bonding) becomes stronger and more energy is needed to melt the solid [1].

**9. (a)** Silicon has a giant covalent lattice [1], so melting requires many strong covalent bonds to be broken [1].

**(b)** The P–P covalent bonds are strong and are not broken when phosphorus melts [1]. Phosphorus is simple molecular, so melting only overcomes the weak id-id forces between P₄ molecules [1].

**10.** S > P > Cl > Ar [1]. All have only weak id-id forces between their particles [1]; these are stronger for particles with more electrons, S₈ (128) > P₄ (60) > Cl₂ (34) > Ar (18) [1].

**11.** Na, Mg and Al are good conductors, increasing from Na to Al [1], because they have delocalised electrons free to move [1], and the number of delocalised electrons per atom increases from 1 to 3 [1]. Silicon is a semiconductor: it has no delocalised electrons, but a few electrons gain enough energy to move [1]. P, S, Cl and Ar do not conduct, because there are no mobile electrons or ions [1].

**12. (a)** Carbon [1]: it forms a giant covalent lattice in which many strong covalent bonds must be broken to melt it [1].

**(b)** Nitrogen is simple molecular (N₂) [1], so only weak id-id forces between molecules are overcome on melting, whereas carbon requires many covalent bonds to be broken [1].

**(c)** The same pattern (rise across the metals, a peak at the Group 14 element, then a sharp fall) repeats in each period [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Saying that the drop in melting point after silicon happens because bonds get weaker.
- Describing phosphorus or sulfur as having weak covalent bonds.
- Treating ionic radius as one continuous trend.
- Omitting silicon's semiconductor behaviour from conductivity answers.
- Giving one factor only when explaining the metallic melting point trend.
