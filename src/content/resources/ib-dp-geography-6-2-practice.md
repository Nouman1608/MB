## Section A

**1.** Distinguish between a cumulative S-curve and an epidemic curve. **[2]**

**2.** State the four stages of spatial diffusion described by Hagerstrand. **[2]**

**3.** Explain what is meant by effective distance in the spread of disease. **[2]**

**4.** Explain why dengue is increasingly an urban disease. **[2]**

**5.** Outline why export restrictions by a major grain exporter can raise world food prices. **[2]**

**6.** Describe the spatial pattern of cases typically produced by a food-borne outbreak from one contaminated product. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** The table shows invented rice data, in millions of tonnes.

| | Country T | Country U |
|---|---|---|
| Production | 1.5 | 12.0 |
| Imports | 3.5 | 0.5 |
| Exports | 0.0 | 4.5 |

**(a)** Calculate the import dependency ratio for each country. **[2]**

**(b)** The world rice price rises from 400 to 600 currency units per tonne. Calculate the increase in Country T's import bill, assuming import volume is unchanged. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain why Country U is less vulnerable to this price rise. **[2]**

**8.** The table shows the week in which an invented infection was first recorded in five places.

| Place | Population | Distance from source (km) | Week of first case |
|---|---|---|---|
| Port city (source) | 1,500,000 | 0 | 1 |
| Inland capital | 3,000,000 | 550 | 2 |
| Market town | 40,000 | 30 | 3 |
| Second inland city | 500,000 | 700 | 3 |
| Mountain village | 1,500 | 45 | 8 |

**(a)** Identify the evidence of hierarchical diffusion. **[2]**

**(b)** Identify the evidence of contagious diffusion. **[1]**

**(c)** Suggest why the mountain village was reached last. **[2]**

**9.** Explain how two land-use changes can increase the risk of malaria. **[4]**

**10.** Explain how livestock production can contribute to the spread of a disease such as avian influenza. **[4]**

## Section C

**11.** Examine the factors that influence the speed and direction of the spread of an infectious disease. **[10]**

**12.** "Globalised food systems make food more secure but health less secure." Discuss this statement. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** A cumulative S-curve plots the total number of cases to date, rising slowly, then steeply, then levelling off [1]; an epidemic curve plots new cases in each period, rising to a peak and then falling [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Primary, diffusion, condensing and saturation stages, in that order [2]; award [1] if all four are named in the wrong order. **[2]**

**3.** Effective distance reflects how well connected places are by transport, not how far apart they are in kilometres [1]; places linked by frequent flights or major roads are "closer" for disease spread than nearby places with poor links [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Its *Aedes* mosquito vector breeds in small amounts of standing water in containers, such as water-storage drums and discarded tyres [1], which are common in densely populated urban areas, especially where piped water and waste collection are poor [1]. **[2]**

**5.** A small number of countries supply much of the traded grain, so a restriction removes a large share of supply from the world market [1]; importers compete for the remaining supply, pushing prices up, and other exporters may follow with their own restrictions [1]. **[2]**

**6.** Cases are scattered across many places at about the same time [1], following the distribution routes of the product rather than forming a cluster around one source area [1]. **[2]**

**7. (a)** Country T: 3.5 / (1.5 + 3.5 - 0.0) x 100 = 3.5 / 5.0 x 100 = **70%** [1]. Country U: 0.5 / (12.0 + 0.5 - 4.5) x 100 = 0.5 / 8.0 x 100 = **6.25%** [1].

**(b)** Before: 3.5 million x 400 = 1,400 million; after: 3.5 million x 600 = 2,100 million [1]. Increase = **700 million currency units** [1].

**(c)** Country U produces far more than it consumes and imports only 6.25% of its supply [1], so the higher price adds little to its import bill and, as a net exporter, it may earn more from its exports [1]. **[6]**

**8. (a)** The inland capital, 550 km away, and the second inland city, 700 km away, were reached by weeks 2 and 3 [1], before or at the same time as smaller places much closer to the source, showing spread down the settlement hierarchy [1].

**(b)** The market town, only 30 km from the source, was reached in week 3, consistent with spread outwards to nearby places by contact [1].

**(c)** It is small and probably poorly connected by transport [1], so few people travel between it and infected places; mountains may also act as a partial (permeable) barrier [1]. **[5]**

**9.** Two land-use changes explained, two marks each:

- Irrigation and dam building create areas of standing water [1] in which *Anopheles* mosquitoes breed, increasing vector numbers near settlements [1].
- Forest clearance can leave pools in tracks and cleared ground [1] and brings people such as farmers and miners into contact with mosquito habitats [1].

Accept other valid changes, such as poorly drained urban expansion. **[4]**

**10.** Large numbers of birds kept close together allow a virus to pass quickly between them [1]. Trade in live birds, eggs and poultry products moves the virus between farms, regions and countries [1]. Live-bird markets bring birds from many sources into contact with each other and with people [1]. Contact between domestic flocks and wild birds, which migrate long distances, introduces the virus to new areas [1]. **[4]**

**11.** Indicative content:

- **Nature of the disease:** mode of transmission (contact, water, vector, air) and how long people are infectious before symptoms [1].
- **Transport networks:** air and road links create hierarchical and relocation diffusion, and effective distance matters more than kilometres [1] [1].
- **Population density:** crowded cities, camps and informal settlements allow contagious spread [1].
- **Environment:** climate and land use control vector-borne disease; sanitation controls water-borne disease, as with cholera in Haiti from 2010 [1].
- **Barriers:** physical barriers, quarantine, border controls and vaccination [1]; absorbing versus permeable barriers [1].
- **Health systems and behaviour:** surveillance, treatment and trust, as in the Ebola outbreak of 2014 to 2016 [1].
- **Stage of the outbreak:** spread slows at saturation as fewer people are susceptible [1].
- **Judgement:** for example, transport connections usually decide direction, while density and health systems decide speed [1]. **[10]**

**12.** Indicative content:

- **Food more secure:** trade allows food-deficit countries to import staples and smooths local harvest failures [1]; cold chains reduce spoilage [1].
- **Food less secure:** concentration of exporters means shocks spread globally, as in the 2007 to 2008 price spike and the 2022 disruption of Black Sea exports [1] [1]; import-dependent countries are most exposed [1].
- **Health less secure:** food-borne outbreaks spread along supply chains [1]; livestock trade spreads diseases such as avian influenza [1]; intensive livestock systems raise concern about antimicrobial resistance [1].
- **Health more secure:** improved diet diversity and food safety standards in global supply chains [1].
- **Judgement:** for example, globalised systems raise average security but concentrate and spread risk for both food and health, so the statement is only partly true [1]. **[10]**

*Examiner insight:* answers that test a statement against evidence on both sides, and say for whom it is true, reach the higher mark bands.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Leaving exports out of the import dependency calculation.
- Explaining spread only by straight-line distance.
- Confusing new cases with cumulative cases.
- Saying mosquitoes cause malaria or dengue.
