## 1 Marketing objectives and strategy

**Marketing objectives (specification list):** increase market share, increase revenue, build a brand.

**Marketing mix:** the combination of marketing decisions used to meet the objectives. Standard four Ps: product, promotion, price, place. The mix must be consistent and tied to the objective.

**Product life cycle (standard stages):** development, introduction, growth, maturity, decline.

| Stage | Sales | Typical response |
|---|---|---|
| Development | None, costs only | Research and testing |
| Introduction | Low, slow rise | Awareness-building, often high or low launch price |
| Growth | Rising fast | Widen distribution, build brand |
| Maturity | Flat | Competitive pricing, extension strategies |
| Decline | Falling | Harvest, replace or withdraw |

**Extension strategies:** new packaging, new market, new use, new promotion, price change, new variant. Judge them by whether they fix the cause of the stalled sales.

**Boston Matrix: method in steps**

1. Find market growth for each product.
2. Calculate relative market share (standard form): product's share ÷ largest rival's share.
3. Compare with the cut-offs given (growth line, and 1.0 for share).
4. Classify: star, cash cow, question mark or dog.
5. Make the portfolio decision: use cash cows to fund stars and question marks, and decide whether to invest in, harvest or drop the rest.

| | Relative share above 1 | Relative share below 1 |
|---|---|---|
| High growth | Star | Question mark |
| Low growth | Cash cow | Dog |

**Market types**

| Market | Key features | Typical strategy |
|---|---|---|
| Mass | Many customers, standard product | Scale, low costs, wide distribution, broad media |
| Niche | Small specific segment | Specialist product, higher price, targeted promotion |
| B2B | Few buyers, large orders, several decision-makers | Personal selling, relationships, technical detail |
| B2C | Many individual buyers | Brand, social media, retail presence, emotion |

**Customer loyalty:** built through quality, after-sales service, loyalty schemes, personalisation, a trusted brand and ease of purchase. Benefits: repeat sales, lower cost of winning sales, support for premium prices.

## 2 Product/service design

**Design mix:** function, aesthetics, cost/economic manufacture.

- Function: does the job reliably.
- Aesthetics: appearance, feel, packaging.
- Cost/economic manufacture: can be made at a cost that allows a profit at an acceptable price.

**Social trends**

| Trend | Design response | Likely effect |
|---|---|---|
| Concern over resource depletion | Waste minimisation, re-use, recycling | Redesign cost; less material later; appeals to green buyers |
| Ethical sourcing | Fair-trade or audited suppliers | Higher input cost; stronger brand |

## 3 Promotion and branding

**Types of promotion (standard forms):** advertising, sales promotion, personal selling, public relations, sponsorship, direct and digital marketing.

**Types of branding (standard forms):** corporate (family), individual, own-label.

**Benefits of strong branding (specification list):** added value; ability to charge premium prices; reduced price elasticity of demand.

**Ways to build a brand (specification list):** USPs/differentiation, advertising, sponsorship, social media.

**Social trends:** viral marketing, social media, emotional branding.

| Term | Meaning | Main risk |
|---|---|---|
| Viral marketing | Customers share content themselves | No control of the message |
| Social media | Platform promotion and two-way contact | Public complaints |
| Emotional branding | Appeals to feelings, not features | Can seem insincere if the product does not match |

## 4 Pricing

| Strategy | Core idea | Best when |
|---|---|---|
| Cost plus | Unit cost + mark-up % | Costs stable, little differentiation |
| Skimming | High launch price, lowered later | Strong USPs, inelastic demand, strong brand |
| Penetration | Low launch price | Elastic demand, rivals many, scale economies |
| Predatory | Below cost to force out a rival | Firm can afford losses |
| Competitive | Close to rivals' prices | Many similar rivals, maturity |
| Psychological | Prices such as £4.99 | Visible shelf prices |

**Factors in choosing (specification list):** number of USPs/amount of differentiation; price elasticity of demand; level of competition; strength of brand; stage in the product life cycle; costs and the need to make a profit.

**Social trends:** online sales (flexible, direct pricing) and price comparison sites (price transparency, so demand becomes more elastic for similar goods).

## 5 Distribution

| Channel | Route |
|---|---|
| Four stage | Producer → wholesaler → retailer → consumer |
| Three stage | Producer → retailer → consumer |
| Two stage | Producer → consumer |

Shorter channel: more income per unit and more control for the producer, but it takes on storage, delivery and service. Longer channel: wider reach with less effort, but each middleman takes a margin.

## Formulas (standard forms)

| Item | Formula |
|---|---|
| Market share | firm's sales ÷ total market sales × 100 |
| Relative market share | product's share ÷ largest rival's share |
| Cost-plus price | unit cost × (1 + mark-up %) |
| Price elasticity of demand | (% change in quantity demanded) ÷ (% change in price) |
| Added value | price charged − cost of materials and services bought in |
| Revenue | price × quantity |

## Worked reminders

**Share target.** A business has sales of £2.4m in a £30m market, so its share is 2.4 ÷ 30 × 100 = 8%. A target of 10% in a market forecast at £32m needs sales of 0.10 × 32 = £3.2m. That is £0.8m more, a rise of 0.8 ÷ 2.4 × 100 = 33.3%.

**Relative share.** A product has 6% of the market and the largest rival has 24%. Relative share = 6 ÷ 24 = 0.25. With low growth it is a dog, and the question to ask is whether it supports sales of other products before deciding to drop it.

**Growth rate.** Sales rise from 40,000 to 46,000 units: (46,000 − 40,000) ÷ 40,000 × 100 = 15%. In a 10% growth market that is faster than the market, so share is rising.

**Elasticity and revenue.** If demand is elastic, a price rise cuts quantity by a larger percentage and revenue falls. If it is inelastic, revenue rises. Branding aims to move demand towards inelastic.

## Must-know distinctions

- Market share (the firm's slice of the whole market) versus relative market share (compared with the largest rival).
- Skimming (high then lower) versus penetration (low to gain share).
- Predatory (below cost, aimed at a rival) versus competitive (in line with rivals).
- Corporate branding (one name for all) versus individual branding (name per product).
- Mark-up (percentage of cost) versus margin (percentage of price).
- Added value (price less bought-in inputs) versus profit (after all costs).

## Quick self-test

1. Define the term marketing mix.
2. Veldtmann Audio sells 45,000 speakers in a market of 360,000. Calculate its market share.
3. A product has unit cost £30 and a 50% mark-up. Calculate the cost-plus price.
4. A product with unit cost £42 sells for £63. Calculate the mark-up as a percentage of cost.
5. A product has an 18% share, the largest rival has 36%, and market growth is 14% with a cut-off of 10%. Calculate relative market share and classify it.
6. A price rises 10% and quantity demanded falls 4%. Calculate price elasticity of demand and state the effect on revenue.
7. Demand has a price elasticity of −1.5. By how much does quantity fall if price rises 6%?
8. A producer sells at £50 to a wholesaler, who adds 30%, and the retailer adds 60%. Give the consumer price.
9. State the three elements of the design mix.
10. State two ways of building a brand and two social trends in pricing.

### Answers

1. The combination of marketing decisions (product, promotion, price, place) a business uses to meet its marketing objectives.
2. 45,000 ÷ 360,000 × 100 = **12.5%**.
3. 30 × 1.5 = **£45**.
4. (63 − 42) ÷ 42 × 100 = **50%**.
5. 18 ÷ 36 = **0.5**. Share is below 1 and growth is above the cut-off, so a **question mark**.
6. −4 ÷ 10 = **−0.4**, inelastic, so revenue **rises**.
7. −1.5 × 6 = **−9%**, so quantity falls by 9%.
8. 50 × 1.3 = £65; 65 × 1.6 = **£104**.
9. Function, aesthetics, cost/economic manufacture.
10. Any two of USPs/differentiation, advertising, sponsorship, social media; and online sales and price comparison sites.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Giving a definition of the Boston Matrix categories without using the figures to classify the product.
- Using the wrong base for relative market share (dividing by total market, not the largest rival).
- Applying a mark-up to the selling price instead of the unit cost.
- Dropping the negative sign or the unit in elasticity answers, or not saying what the value means.
- Choosing a pricing strategy without linking it to a factor from the specification list, such as elasticity, competition or stage in the life cycle.
- Listing the four Ps for the business without saying how they meet the stated objective.
- Treating extension strategies as always successful, with no judgement about the cause of the stalled sales.
- Ignoring that a shorter distribution channel moves costs and tasks on to the producer.
- Writing about viral marketing or social media in general terms, with no link to the product and its target market.

## Frequently asked questions

**What are the four categories of the Boston Matrix?**
Stars have high relative market share in a high-growth market, cash cows have high share in a low-growth market, question marks have low share in a high-growth market, and dogs have low share in a low-growth market.

**What is the difference between price skimming and penetration pricing?**
Price skimming sets a high launch price, lowered later, and suits a new product with strong USPs. Penetration pricing sets a low launch price to gain market share and suits elastic demand.

**What is predatory pricing?**
Predatory pricing sets a price below cost to push a rival out of the market. It is not normal competition and suits only a firm that can survive the losses.

**What are the distribution channels in Edexcel IAL Business?**
A four-stage channel runs producer to wholesaler to retailer to consumer, a three-stage channel cuts out the wholesaler, and a two-stage channel sells direct from producer to consumer.
