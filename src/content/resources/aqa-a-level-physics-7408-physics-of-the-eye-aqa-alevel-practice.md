These questions cover sub-section 3.10.1 Physics of the eye of the AQA AS and A-level Physics specification (7407/7408, AS and A-level exams June 2016 onwards), part of the Medical physics option (A-level only, Paper 3 Section B).

Revise from the [physics of the eye study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-physics-7408-physics-of-the-eye-aqa-alevel/) and [physics of the eye revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-physics-7408-physics-of-the-eye-aqa-alevel-revision-notes/) first. Mixed option questions are in the [Medical physics practice questions](/resources/aqa-a-level-physics-medical-physics-practice/).

Use the "real is positive" sign convention. Take the normal near point as 0.25 m.

## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** State three differences between rods and cones. **[3]**

**2.** Explain why the spatial resolution of the eye is poorer in dim light. **[2]**

**3.** Define the power of a lens and the dioptre. **[2]**

**4.** Explain how the eye keeps a sharp image on the retina as an object moves closer. **[2]**

**5.** Explain how the eye distinguishes colours. **[2]**

### Section B: structured questions

**6.** A converging lens has a focal length of 0.20 m.

**(a)** An object is placed 0.30 m from the lens. Calculate the image distance and the magnification, and state whether the image is real or virtual. **[2]**
**(b)** The object is moved to 0.12 m from the lens. Calculate the new image distance and magnification, and state whether the image is real or virtual. **[2]**

**7.** A short-sighted person has a far point of 0.80 m and a near point of 0.15 m.

**(a)** Calculate the power of the lens that corrects their distant vision. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate their near point when wearing this lens. **[2]**
**(c)** Describe a ray diagram showing how the lens corrects their distant vision. **[2]**

**8.** A long-sighted person has a near point of 0.75 m.

**(a)** Calculate the power of the lens needed for them to read a book held 0.25 m away. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why the image formed by this lens must be virtual. **[1]**

**9.** In a simplified model, the eye's lens is 0.022 m from the retina. Calculate:

**(a)** the power of the eye when focused on a very distant object; **[1]**
**(b)** the power when focused on an object at the normal near point; **[1]**
**(c)** the change in power the eye must provide. **[1]**

**10.** A person 1.8 m tall stands 12 m from an observer. Using the model eye with the retina 0.020 m behind the lens, calculate the height of the image on the retina. **[2]**

**11.** A question states that adjacent cones in the fovea are 3.0 × 10⁻⁶ m apart and that the retina is 0.020 m from the eye's lens.

**(a)** Estimate the minimum angle between two point objects that can just be resolved. **[2]**
**(b)** Estimate the greatest distance at which two dots 3.0 mm apart can be seen as separate. **[1]**

**12.** Explain what astigmatism is, how it is corrected, and the information given in a prescription for it. **[3]**

**13.** An older person can focus clearly on distant objects but their near point has moved to 0.50 m.

**(a)** Calculate the power of reading glasses that let them read at 0.25 m. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why they cannot see distant objects clearly while wearing these glasses. **[1]**

## Answers

**1.** Any three, one mark each: rods are more sensitive and work in dim light, cones need bright light; there is one type of rod but three types of cone; cones give colour vision, rods do not; cones are concentrated in the fovea, rods are absent from its centre and found towards the periphery; each foveal cone has its own nerve connection while many rods share one [3].

**2.** In dim light only rods respond [1]; many rods share a single nerve fibre, so two images falling on rods connected to the same fibre produce one signal and cannot be distinguished [1].

**3.** Power = 1/focal length [1]; one dioptre is the power of a lens of focal length 1 m (unit m⁻¹) [1].

**4.** The ciliary muscles change the shape of the lens [1]; making it more powerful (shorter focal length) so the image of the closer object still forms on the retina (accommodation) [1].

**5.** There are three types of cone, each most sensitive to a different range of wavelengths [1]; the brain compares the relative responses of the three types to perceive colour [1].

**6. (a)** 1/v = 1/0.20 − 1/0.30, v = **+0.60 m**, **real** [1]; m = 0.60/0.30 = **2.0** [1]
**(b)** 1/v = 1/0.20 − 1/0.12, v = **−0.30 m**, **virtual** [1]; m = 0.30/0.12 = **2.5** [1]

**7. (a)** u = ∞, v = −0.80 m [1]; P = 1/(−0.80) = **−1.25 D** (diverging) [1]
**(b)** Image at v = −0.15 m: 1/u = −1.25 + 1/0.15 = 5.42 [1]; u = **0.18 m** [1]
**(c)** Parallel rays from a distant object pass through the diverging lens and spread out as if from the far point, 0.80 m in front of the eye [1]; the eye then focuses these rays on the retina instead of in front of it [1].

**8. (a)** u = 0.25 m, v = −0.75 m [1]; P = 4.0 − 1.33 = **+2.7 D** [1]
**(b)** The image must be at the near point on the same side of the lens as the object, so the eye can focus on it; an image on the same side as the object is virtual [1].

**9. (a)** P = 1/0.022 = **45 D** [1]
**(b)** P = 1/0.25 + 1/0.022 = **49 D** [1]
**(c)** **4.0 D** [1]

**10.** m = v/u = 0.020/12 [1]; image height = 1.8 × 0.020/12 = **3.0 × 10⁻³ m** [1]

**11. (a)** Minimum separation on the retina ≈ 2 × 3.0 × 10⁻⁶ = 6.0 × 10⁻⁶ m (one unstimulated cone between) [1]; angle ≈ 6.0 × 10⁻⁶/0.020 = **3.0 × 10⁻⁴ rad** [1]
**(b)** Distance = 3.0 × 10⁻³/3.0 × 10⁻⁴ = **10 m** [1]

**12.** The cornea (or lens) has different powers in different planes, so lines in one orientation are in focus while those at right angles are blurred [1]; it is corrected with a cylindrical lens, which has power in one plane only [1]; the prescription gives the spherical power and cylindrical power in dioptres and the axis of the cylinder in degrees [1].

**13. (a)** u = 0.25 m, v = −0.50 m [1]; P = 4.0 − 2.0 = **+2.0 D** [1]
**(b)** The glasses add converging power, so light from distant objects is focused in front of the retina [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Dropping the minus sign for virtual images.
- Leaving distances in centimetres when finding power.
- Swapping u and v in hypermetropia calculations.
- Saying rods give better resolution because they are more sensitive.
- Describing an astigmatism prescription without the axis.
