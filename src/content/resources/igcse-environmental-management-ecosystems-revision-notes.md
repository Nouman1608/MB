Topic 5 of Cambridge IGCSE Environmental Management (0680) covers how ecosystems work, why forests
matter, how biodiversity is conserved and how fieldwork data is collected. Expect definitions,
food-web and pyramid interpretation, the 10% energy rule, and "discuss" questions on conservation
strategies and sampling methods. Fieldwork skills are also tested through source material on Paper 2.

For full explanations use the
[ecosystems study guide](/resources/cambridge-igcse-environmental-management-ecosystems-biodiversity-and-fieldwork-study-guide/);
practise with the [ecosystems practice questions](/resources/igcse-environmental-management-ecosystems-practice/).

## Key ecosystem terms (5.1.1)

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Habitat | The place where an organism lives |
| Species | A group of organisms that can breed to produce fertile offspring |
| Population | All the organisms of one species in an area |
| Community | All the populations of different species in an area |
| Niche | The role of a species in its ecosystem |
| Prey / predator | The organism eaten / the organism that hunts and eats it |
| Apex predator | A predator at the top of a food chain with no natural predators |

**Biotic** (living): producers, primary, secondary and tertiary consumers, decomposers.
**Abiotic** (non-living): temperature, water, oxygen, carbon dioxide, salinity, light, pH.

**Biotic interactions (5.1.3)**: native vs invasive species; competition (for food, light, space);
predation; pollination.

## How do pollination, photosynthesis and respiration fit together?

- **Pollination**: transfer of pollen from an anther to a stigma, by insects or wind; leads to
  fertilisation, then seed and fruit formation. (Flower structure is not required.)
- **Photosynthesis**: carbon dioxide + water → glucose + oxygen, using sunlight energy captured by
  chlorophyll.
- **Aerobic respiration**: glucose + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water, releasing energy.

Notice that the two word equations are reverses of each other: an easy check in the exam.

## How does energy flow through an ecosystem? (5.1.6-5.1.7)

- Only about **10%** of energy passes from one trophic level to the next.
- The rest is lost in movement, respiration, digestion and excretion.
- **Pyramid of energy**: always pyramid-shaped, because energy is lost at each level.
- **Pyramid of numbers**: can be inverted, for example one tree supporting many caterpillars.

**Worked example.** Grass fixes 20 000 kJ. Energy reaching each level at 10%:
grasshoppers 2 000 kJ → frogs 200 kJ → snakes 20 kJ. The tertiary consumer receives 0.1% of the
producer's energy, which is why food chains rarely have more than four or five levels.

## The carbon cycle (5.1.9)

| Process | Carbon moves |
|---|---|
| Photosynthesis | Atmosphere → plants |
| Respiration | Organisms → atmosphere |
| Feeding | Plants → animals |
| Decomposition | Dead matter → atmosphere and soil |
| Fossil fuel formation | Dead organisms → coal, oil, gas (over millions of years) |
| Combustion | Fuels and wood → atmosphere |

## Why are forests cleared, and why does it matter? (5.2)

**Causes**: logging and timber; subsistence and commercial farming; roads and settlements; rock, ore and
mineral extraction; hydro-electric power stations (flooding of valleys); climate change.

**Impacts**: habitat loss; loss of biodiversity; soil erosion and desertification; silting of rivers and
flooding; global warming; changes to rainfall patterns; genetic depletion.

**Sustainable management of forests** = balancing the needs of the environment, wildlife and humans
while conserving forests for future generations. It is needed because forests:
- regulate climate as carbon sinks and stores;
- support the water cycle through interception, water uptake, transpiration and reduced surface run-off;
- control floods and prevent soil erosion;
- are a genetic resource and supply food, medicine and raw materials;
- offer recreation, ecotourism and education.

## How can biodiversity be conserved? (5.3)

| Strategy | Benefit | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Sustainable harvesting | Income continues without wiping out the species | Quotas are hard to enforce |
| Sustainable forestry | Timber with replanting | Replanted forest is less diverse than the original |
| National parks and reserves | Protect whole habitats | Need funding and guards; may displace local people |
| Wildlife corridors | Link fragmented habitats so animals can move and breed | Land is costly; corridors can be narrow |
| Seed banks | Store genetic material for the future | Seeds do not adapt to changing conditions in storage |
| Zoos and captive breeding | Rebuild numbers of endangered species | Expensive; reintroduction can fail |
| Ecotourism | Funds conservation and local jobs | Visitors can disturb wildlife |
| International cooperation against animal trade; regulation of trade | Reduces poaching demand | Illegal trade continues |
| Threat classification (threatened, endangered, extinct) | Targets effort at the species most at risk | Data for many species is incomplete |

## How do you plan a reliable fieldwork investigation? (5.4)

**Scientific method (5.4.1)**: aim and hypothesis → plan methods (sampling strategy and technique,
questionnaires, surveys, pilot study) → identify independent, dependent and control variables → repeats
and replicates → record → analyse → identify and process anomalies → conclude.

| Strategy | How | Benefit | Limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Random | Coordinates from random numbers | Avoids bias | May miss parts of the area |
| Systematic | Samples at regular intervals, e.g. every 5 m along a transect | Shows change along a gradient | Can miss patterns between the intervals |

| Technique | Used for |
|---|---|
| Pitfall trap | Ground-dwelling invertebrates |
| Pooter | Small insects, sucked into a container |
| Sweep net | Insects in long grass |
| Quadrat | Plants and slow-moving animals (percentage cover or count) |
| Transect | Change across an area, with quadrats along a line |
| Aerial photography and drones | Large or inaccessible areas, such as forest cover |
| Automated sampling | Camera traps or sensors collecting data continuously |

## Common mistakes and exam traps

- Confusing population (one species) with community (all species).
- Writing that energy is "used up" - it is transferred and lost as heat and in life processes.
- Calling a pyramid of energy inverted; only pyramids of numbers can be.
- Giving "more samples" as the only improvement: say repeats increase reliability and explain why.
- Forgetting that a seed bank conserves genetic resources, not habitats.

## Frequently asked questions

**What does the 10% rule mean?**
About 10% of the energy at one trophic level passes to the next. The rest is
lost in movement, respiration, digestion and excretion.

**What is the difference between random and systematic sampling?**
Random sampling chooses sample points by
chance to avoid bias. Systematic sampling places them at regular intervals, often along a transect.

**Which conservation strategies are on the 0680 syllabus?**
Ten are named, from sustainable harvesting and
national parks to seed banks, ecotourism, trade regulation and threat classification.
