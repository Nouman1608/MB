Revise first with the [HR performance study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-analysing-human-resource-performance/) and the [HR performance revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-analysing-human-resource-performance-revision-notes/).

## Section A: Short-answer questions

**1.** A business has an average of 150 employees and 18 of them leave during the year. What is its labour turnover? **[1]**

A: 8.3%

B: 12%

C: 18%

D: 83%

**2.** A firm's total labour cost is £90,000 and it produces 15,000 units. What is its labour cost per unit? **[1]**

A: £0.17

B: £5

C: £6

D: £16.67

**3.** Define the term "labour productivity". **[2]**

**4.** Explain **one** cost to a business of high labour turnover. **[3]**

**5.** Aldwick Care Homes has an average of 120 staff. During the year 30 staff left. Employee costs were £3.0 million and revenue was £5.0 million. Replacing each leaver costs £2,800.

**(a)** Calculate the labour turnover. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate employee costs as a percentage of revenue. **[2]**

**(c)** Calculate the total cost of replacing the leavers. **[1]**

**6.** Brampton Works made 300,000 units with 100 employees in Year A, at a total labour cost of £2.4 million. In Year B it made 330,000 units with 105 employees at a total labour cost of £2.73 million.

**(a)** Calculate labour productivity in each year, to the nearest whole unit. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate labour cost per unit in each year, to the nearest penny. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain why labour cost per unit rose even though productivity rose. **[2]**

**7.** State **two** limitations of using labour turnover data to judge HR performance. **[2]**

## Section B: Structured questions

**8.** Analyse **two** possible causes of rising labour turnover in a restaurant chain. **[6]**

**9.** A hotel group is deciding whether to invest in staff development. Assess how useful HR data would be in helping it decide. **[9]**

**10.** Analyse how a manufacturer could use labour cost per unit when deciding where to open a new factory. **[6]**

**11.** Explain **two** ways in which a fall in employee costs as a percentage of turnover could be misleading. **[4]**

**12.** Evaluate whether labour turnover is the most important measure of a business's HR performance. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** **B** [1]. 18 ÷ 150 × 100 = 12%. **[1]**

**2.** **C** [1]. £90,000 ÷ 15,000 = £6. **[1]**

**3.** The amount of output produced by each employee [1] in a given period, calculated as total output divided by the number of employees [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Each leaver has to be replaced, which costs money in advertising, recruitment and interviews [1]. New employees also need induction and training and are less productive at first [1], so output and service quality fall and the business pays for lost knowledge and experience [1]. **[3]**

**5. (a)** 30 ÷ 120 [1] × 100 = **25%** [1].

**(b)** £3.0m ÷ £5.0m [1] × 100 = **60%** [1].

**(c)** 30 × £2,800 = **£84,000** [1]. **[5]**

**6. (a)** Year A: 300,000 ÷ 100 = **3,000** [1]. Year B: 330,000 ÷ 105 = **3,143** [1].

**(b)** Year A: £2,400,000 ÷ 300,000 = **£8.00** [1]. Year B: £2,730,000 ÷ 330,000 = **£8.27** [1].

**(c)** Labour cost per employee rose from £24,000 to £26,000, a rise of 8.3% [1], which is larger than the 4.8% rise in productivity, so the extra pay more than cancelled the gain in output per employee [1]. **[6]**

**7.** Any two: it does not show why staff leave [1]; it does not separate good leavers from poor ones or planned from unplanned [1]; it is an average that hides differences between departments [1]. **[2]**

**8.** Cause 1: low pay or poor conditions compared with competitors [1]. If rival restaurants pay more or offer better shifts, staff will move [1], so turnover rises as staff join competitors [1]. Cause 2: poor management or lack of progression [1]. Staff who feel unvalued, face heavy workloads or see no chance of promotion will look elsewhere [1], which is common where there are few training or career opportunities [1]. **[6]**

**9.** For: data on labour turnover and exit interviews could show whether staff leave for lack of development [1]. Productivity figures before and after past training show whether development raises output [1]. Employee costs and labour cost per unit show if the group can afford the investment [1]. Comparing hotels shows where training is most needed [1]. Against: data shows what is happening but not why, so it may point to the wrong cause [1]. Past data may not predict the future; benefits of development, such as service quality and loyalty, are hard to measure [1]. Averages hide differences between jobs [1]. Judgement: HR data is a useful starting point for deciding where to invest but should be combined with staff surveys, exit interviews and guest satisfaction scores [1], and the investment should be trialled and its results measured [1]. **[9]**

**10.** Labour cost per unit combines wages and productivity [1], so it is a better guide than wages alone, as a lower-paid location with low productivity could cost more per unit [1]. The manufacturer could estimate it for each location by dividing the expected labour cost by output [1]. A lower figure suggests lower costs and so greater profit [1]. It should also consider the availability of skilled workers and training costs [1], transport and supplier links, and the possible effect on reputation of moving jobs [1]. **[6]**

**11.** Way 1: the ratio may fall because turnover (revenue) has risen through price increases rather than because the workforce is more efficient [1], so there is no real improvement in efficiency [1]. Way 2: the ratio may fall because the business has cut staff, which can harm service quality and increase pressure on remaining staff [1], leading to higher turnover and lost sales later [1]. **[4]**

**12.** For: turnover is easy to calculate and compare over time and with competitors [1]. High turnover is costly because of recruitment, training and lost knowledge [1]. It is a sign of problems such as poor pay or management [1]. It affects service quality, particularly where customers value relationships with staff [1]. Against: it shows only that staff leave, not why [1]. Some turnover is healthy [1]. It says nothing about output; productivity and labour cost per unit show whether the workforce is efficient [1]. A business with low turnover could still have low productivity [1]. Employee costs as a percentage of revenue show affordability [1]. Factors: the type of industry, since retail and hospitality expect higher turnover, and the business's objectives [1]. Judgement: turnover is important, particularly for businesses relying on skilled staff, but it is not the most important on its own [1]; a balanced view uses turnover with productivity, labour cost per unit and engagement measures [1]. **[12]**

*Examiner insight:* After every calculation, say what the figure means and what else you would want to know.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Dividing leavers by the wrong number instead of the average workforce.
- Mixing up employee costs as a percentage of turnover with labour cost per unit.
- Treating all turnover as bad.
- Forgetting that pay rises can cancel out productivity gains.
- Giving a figure without interpretation.
- Using HR data without noting its limits.
