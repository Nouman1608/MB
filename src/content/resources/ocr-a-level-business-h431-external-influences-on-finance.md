This guide covers the **External influences on accounting and finance** content in the Area of Study **Accounting and finance within the external environment** of the OCR A Level Business (H431) specification, Version 2.1 (January 2026), final assessment summer 2027. It is numbered 6.13 on this site; the specification does not number its content. The [accounting and financial considerations study guide](/resources/ocr-a-level-business-accounting-and-financial-considerations/) covers the whole topic. This page goes further on this one heading: each of the eight influences the specification lists, how it changes accounting and finance decisions, and four worked examples with figures showing how to judge the size of an effect. All businesses and figures are invented.

## What the specification asks for

One statement: learners should be able to **evaluate** the following influences on accounting and finance: social, legal, ethical, environmental, economic, political, technological and international. "Evaluate" means weighing the size and direction of each effect on the business and its stakeholders, and reaching a judgement.

## How to evaluate an influence

For each influence, link it to a specific accounting or finance **decision**: which source of finance to use, how to price, whether to invest, how to forecast cash, how to report profit. Then ask:

1. **What changes?** (cost of finance, availability of funds, costs, revenue, reporting rules)
2. **How big is it?** Put a figure on it.
3. **How fast and how lasting?**
4. **Can the business respond?** Does it have reserves, alternative suppliers, or options to change finance?
5. **Who gains and loses?** Owners, lenders, employees, customers, the government.

## Social influences

Changes in attitudes and lifestyles affect finance decisions. Examples: **ethical investing** (investors prefer businesses with a good record on society), which can make funds cheaper for responsible firms and harder to obtain for those with a poor image; **crowdfunding**, where many individuals back a project they like; changes in **spending habits** (online shopping, subscription models) that change sales patterns and cash timing; and **demand for transparency**, where customers and staff expect fair pay and honest reporting. A business that ignores social attitudes can lose customers and investors.

## Legal influences

The law sets what must be reported and how. Companies must prepare accounts that give a true and fair view, file them publicly and have them audited if above a size threshold. Law also covers company law, insolvency rules, taxes, and rules on late payment of invoices. Compliance costs time and money, but it reassures lenders and investors. Failure risks fines, loss of reputation and, for directors, personal consequences. Small firms may be exempt from some requirements, which lowers their cost.

## Ethical influences

Ethics concern what is right, beyond what the law requires. Examples: honest reporting (not "window dressing" the accounts to show a better position at the year end); fair treatment of suppliers (paying invoices on time rather than stretching credit); open dealing with investors; and responsible tax behaviour. Ethical conduct builds trust and a good reputation with lenders and customers but may cost more in the short term, for example paying a small supplier in 14 days instead of 60. Unethical conduct that is discovered can lead to a loss of trust, and legal action.

## Environmental influences

Environmental concerns affect costs, investment and funding. A business may need to invest in cleaner equipment, buy carbon offsets or pay environmental taxes. Lenders and investors increasingly look at environmental risk, and some offer lower rates for green projects. Green investment can cut running costs, for example by saving energy, but it needs cash upfront.

### Worked example 1: a green investment

Dunmoor Dairy considers solar panels for its creamery, costing £150,000 and expected to save £18,000 a year in electricity.

- Simple payback = £150,000 ÷ £18,000 = **8.3 years**.
- If the panels last 25 years, the saving over their life is 25 × £18,000 = £450,000, three times the cost (before allowing for the time value of money).

The investment competes for funds with other projects, so Dunmoor must choose a source of finance (a loan at a lower green rate, or retained profit) and judge the payback against its needs. A green image may also help sales to supermarkets that ask suppliers about their emissions. Judgement: worthwhile if funds are available at a reasonable cost and the panels' life is long; less so if cash is tight and the 8.3 years is too long.

## Economic influences

Economic conditions change the cost and availability of finance, demand, costs and risk. Key factors: **interest rates**, **inflation**, **economic growth or recession**, **unemployment** and **exchange rates** (covered under international).

### Worked example 2: a rise in interest rates

Calder Components has a variable-rate loan of £600,000. The interest rate rises from 5% to 7%. Operating profit is £210,000.

- Interest before = 5% × £600,000 = £30,000. After = 7% × £600,000 = **£42,000**.
- Extra interest = **£12,000**, a rise of 40%.
- Interest cover falls from 210,000 ÷ 30,000 = **7.0 times** to 210,000 ÷ 42,000 = **5.0 times**.

Effects: profit before tax falls by £12,000; the cost of other finance and of new investment rises, which makes projects less attractive (higher discount rates reduce net present value); customers' borrowing costs rise, which may cut demand. Calder could respond by repaying part of the loan from retained profit, fixing the rate, or delaying investment. Judgement: cover of 5.0 times is still comfortable, so the rise is manageable, but a business with cover of 2 would be at risk.

### Worked example 3: recession and bad debts

Wrenfield Supplies sells £2,000,000 a year on credit. In normal times bad debts are 0.5% of sales (£10,000). In a recession they rise to 2% (£40,000).

- Extra bad debts = £40,000 − £10,000 = **£30,000**, which reduces profit directly.
- Cash is also lower, because customers pay late as well as defaulting.

Responses: tighter credit checks, credit insurance, factoring (with its fee), or shorter credit terms. The cost is that tight terms may lose sales. Banks may lend less in a recession, making an overdraft harder to obtain, so the cash problem and the funding problem occur together.

## Political influences

Government decisions on tax, grants, regulation and public spending affect finance. Changes in **corporation tax** change profit after tax and funds available for dividends and investment. **Grants and subsidies** can lower the cost of investment. Government spending can create or cut demand for contractors.

### Worked example 4: a corporation tax rise

Fenwick Labs has profit before tax of £260,000. The tax rate rises from 20% to 25% (an invented change).

- Tax before = £52,000; profit after tax = **£208,000**.
- Tax after = £65,000; profit after tax = **£195,000**.
- Fall = £13,000, or 6.25% of profit after tax.

With dividends of £100,000, retained profit falls from £108,000 to £95,000. Effects: less retained profit to finance growth, so more reliance on borrowing; possibly lower dividends; and a fall in earnings per share. A business may respond by claiming available tax allowances for investment (legally planning), delaying payments, or raising prices. Judgement: a 5-point tax rise matters, but it is smaller than most changes in sales or costs, and applies to all rivals.

## Technological influences

Technology changes how accounts are kept and how finance is raised. **Accounting software and automation** reduce the time and error in bookkeeping and allow real-time reports, but cost money and need training. **Online banking and payments** speed up collection. **Crowdfunding and online lenders** provide new sources of finance, and **data analytics** improves forecasting. **Cyber security** risks (fraud, data theft) create costs and the need for controls. Small firms may gain most from cloud accounting; larger firms from automating routine work.

## International influences

Trading across borders brings **exchange rate risk**, different tax and reporting rules, access to overseas funds, and global competition for investment.

### Worked example 5: a fall in the pound

A UK importer buys $500,000 of goods a year and sells them for £700,000.

- At £1 = $1.25 the cost is $500,000 ÷ 1.25 = **£400,000**; gross profit = £300,000 (42.9% of revenue).
- If the pound falls to £1 = $1.10 the cost is $500,000 ÷ 1.10 = **£454,545**; gross profit = £245,455 (35.1%).
- Extra cost = **£54,545**, a rise of 13.6%.

The importer could raise prices, fix an exchange rate in advance with its bank, find a UK supplier, or accept lower profit. Exporters gain from the same fall, because overseas customers find UK goods cheaper. Judgement: the importer is exposed; a fixed-rate agreement would give certainty at the cost of missing any benefit if the pound rises.

## Putting it together

Influences link. A rise in interest rates (economic) may follow a government decision (political), reduce demand (economic), increase bad debts and make borrowing harder. A top answer picks the two or three most important influences for the case, sizes them, and judges which matters most and why.

## Common mistakes

- Listing all eight influences with one line each.
- Describing an influence without linking it to a finance decision.
- Stating an effect without a figure or comparison.
- Treating influences as threats only.
- Ignoring whether the business can respond.

## Exam technique

State the influence, link it to a finance decision, give a figure or example, weigh the effect on a stakeholder and finish with a judgement on which influence matters most and what could change that view.
