This guide goes deeper into **section 10.4 Finance and accounting strategy** of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Business 9609 syllabus for examination in 2026, 2027 and 2028. It is the final A Level section and draws on everything in Topic 10, so it often appears in essays and in the evaluative parts of case-study questions.

The [investment appraisal and finance and accounting strategy guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-business-investment-appraisal-and-finance-and-accounting-strategy/) introduces the annual report and shows how a share issue and a loan change gearing. This page takes the analysis further: it traces each strategic decision through to earnings per share, investor ratios and liquidity, and shows how to judge the results. Afterwards, use the [finance and accounting strategy revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-business-9609-finance-and-accounting-strategy-revision-notes/) and the [finance and accounting strategy practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-business-9609-finance-and-accounting-strategy-practice/).

All businesses are invented. Figures are in $000 unless stated.

- assessment of business performance over time and against competitors
- the impact of accounting data including ratio results on business strategy

- the impact of changes in dividend strategy on ratio results
- the impact of business growth on ratio results
- the impact of other business strategies on ratio results

## 10.4.1 Financial statements in developing strategy

Accounting data feeds strategy in three ways:

1. **Where are we now?** Profitability, liquidity and gearing show the business's strengths and weaknesses, a starting point for any SWOT analysis.
2. **What can we afford?** Retained profit, cash and existing borrowing set limits on how ambitious a strategy can be and how it must be financed.
3. **Is it working?** Comparing results with targets each year shows whether the strategy should continue, change or stop.

**Example.** A retailer's accounts show one division earning a 14% profit margin and another 2%. Strategic options follow directly: invest more in the strong division, restructure the weak one, or sell it and use the cash to reduce debt.

**The annual report.** A company's annual report typically contains the audited financial statements with notes, statements from the chair and directors on performance and strategy, the auditor's report, and information on governance and on wider issues such as environmental impact. Its usefulness:

| User | Most useful parts | Caution |
|---|---|---|
| Managers | Comparison with targets and previous years | Little detail on competitors |
| Shareholders and potential investors | Profit, dividends, directors' plans | Directors' statements are written to present the company favourably |
| Lenders | Gearing, existing debts, cash | Data is out of date by the time it is published |
| Employees | Profitability and expansion plans, as signs of job security | Totals hide what happens at individual sites |
| Competitors | Margins, growth, strategy | Key details are often left out deliberately |

## Performance over time: look for the trend

A single year can mislead; three or more years show direction.

| Ratio | Year 1 | Year 2 | Year 3 | Rival, Year 3 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Profit margin | 11% | 10% | 8% | 10% |
| ROCE | 16% | 14% | 11% | 13% |
| Current ratio | 1.6 | 1.4 | 1.1 | 1.5 |
| Gearing | 25% | 33% | 42% | 30% |

The trend is consistent: profitability falling, liquidity weakening and borrowing rising. Against the rival, the business is now worse on every measure. Strategic implications: control costs or raise prices to rebuild margins, and avoid further borrowing until liquidity recovers. A strategy of rapid debt-financed expansion would be risky.

## 10.4.2 Debt or equity: a full comparison

**Thornbury Logistics plc** has: profit from operations 2,400; issued shares 6,000; reserves 9,000; non-current liabilities 5,000 at 6% interest (300 a year); 10,000 thousand shares; tax at 25% of profit after interest.

Base position: capital employed = 20,000; ROCE = 12%; gearing = 25%; profit for the year = (2,400 − 300) × 0.75 = 1,575; EPS = 1,575 ÷ 10,000 = 15.75 cents.

Thornbury needs 4,000 for new depots expected to add 600 to profit from operations. Option A borrows at 6% (extra interest 240). Option B issues 2,000 thousand new shares at $2.00.

| After expansion | Option A: loan | Option B: shares |
|---|---|---|
| Profit from operations | 3,000 | 3,000 |
| Interest | 540 | 300 |
| Profit for the year (75%) | 1,845 | 2,025 |
| Number of shares (thousands) | 10,000 | 12,000 |
| EPS | 18.45 cents | 16.9 cents |
| Capital employed | 24,000 | 24,000 |
| ROCE | 12.5% | 12.5% |
| Gearing | 9,000 ÷ 24,000 = 37.5% | 5,000 ÷ 24,000 = 20.8% |

**Interpretation.**

- **ROCE is identical**, because it uses profit before interest and the same capital employed.
- **Debt gives higher EPS** (18.45 against 16.9 cents), because the depots earn 15% on the new money (600 ÷ 4,000) while the loan costs only 6%, and the profit is not shared with new shareholders.
- **Debt raises risk.** Gearing rises to 37.5% and interest to 540 a year. If profit from operations fell back to 2,400, interest would take 22.5% of it, compared with 12.5% before.
- **Equity dilutes control** and spreads profit across more shares, but leaves the business better able to borrow in future.

**Judgement.** Debt suits Thornbury if its profits are stable and interest rates are unlikely to rise; equity suits it if profits are volatile or more expansion is planned soon.

## Dividend strategy and ratio results

Thornbury's current dividend is 630 (6.3 cents a share); the share price is $2.20.

| | Current | Higher payout (945) |
|---|---|---|
| Dividend cover (1,575 ÷ dividend) | 2.5 times | 1.67 times |
| Dividend yield (dividend per share ÷ price) | 6.3 ÷ 220 = 2.9% | 9.45 ÷ 220 = 4.3% |
| Retained profit | 945 | 630 |

A higher payout raises yield at today's price and may attract income-seeking investors. But less profit is retained, so reserves and cash grow more slowly, current and acid test ratios are weaker than they would otherwise be, and more borrowing may be needed for future investment, raising gearing. In practice the share price may also change, so the yield will move.

## Business growth and ratio results

| Growth route | Likely short-term effects on ratios |
|---|---|
| Organic expansion (new branches) | Expenses rise before revenue, so margins and ROCE fall at first; inventory and receivables rise, weakening liquidity |
| Acquisition financed by debt | Gearing rises; ROCE may fall if the price paid is high relative to the target's profit |
| Acquisition financed by shares | Gearing falls; EPS may fall if the acquired profit is small relative to new shares issued |
| Rapid sales growth on credit | Receivables days and cash pressure rise; overtrading risk |

## Other strategies and ratio results

| Strategy | Effect on ratios |
|---|---|
| Sale and leaseback of property | Cash rises, improving liquidity; if the cash repays loans, gearing and capital employed fall, which can raise ROCE; rent becomes a new expense, reducing profit margin |
| Cost-cutting programme | Margins and ROCE improve, unless quality falls and revenue drops |
| Price cuts to win market share | Gross margin falls; inventory turnover may rise |
| Just-in-time inventory | Inventory turnover rises; less cash is tied up in stock; the acid test, which excludes inventory, is not directly affected |
| Offering longer credit to customers | Revenue may rise; receivables days rise and cash falls |

## Limitations of published accounts and ratio analysis

- **Historical**: they show the past, not the future.
- **Window dressing**: managers may time transactions to flatter year-end figures, for example collecting debts just before the year end.
- **Estimates**: inventory values and depreciation rely on judgement.
- **Comparability**: different accounting policies, year ends or business models make rival comparisons unreliable.
- **Non-financial factors**: staff skills, brand strength, customer satisfaction and environmental impact are missing.
- **Inflation**: figures in different years are not in the same real terms.
- **Ratios explain nothing alone**: they show *what* changed, not *why*.

## Annotated model answer

*Evaluate whether Thornbury should finance its expansion with a loan.*

> "A loan gives higher EPS, 18.45 cents against 16.9 cents with a share issue, because the depots earn 15% on the money while interest costs only 6%. Existing shareholders also keep control. However, gearing would rise from 25% to 37.5% and interest to $540,000 a year, so if the depots underperform, more of a smaller profit goes on interest. A share issue keeps gearing at 20.8%, leaving room to borrow later. On balance a loan is better if Thornbury's profits are stable and interest rates are not expected to rise, because the gain in EPS is significant; if demand for logistics is volatile, the lower risk of equity is worth the dilution."

## Common mistakes

- Saying a loan changes ROCE when profit from operations and capital employed are the same under both options.
- Ignoring EPS dilution from a share issue.
- Treating a higher dividend as costless.
- Discussing growth without saying which ratios it affects and in which direction.
- Listing limitations of ratios without linking them to the decision in the question.

## Exam technique

**Analyse** questions want the chain: strategy → which figure changes → which ratio changes → what it means for the business or a stakeholder. **Evaluate** and **assess** questions want both sides with figures, a judgement and the condition it depends on, such as stability of profit, interest rates or what shareholders want.
