## Questions

**1.** State what is meant by a mixed economic system. **[2]**

**2.** In Country W, the demand for rented flats is Qd = 700 − 20P and the supply is Qs = −200 + 25P, where P is the monthly rent in hundreds and Q is the number of flats.

**(a)** Calculate the equilibrium rent and quantity. **[2]**
**(b)** The government sets a maximum rent of 16. Calculate the shortage of flats. **[3]**
**(c)** Explain one problem the maximum rent may cause for people looking for a flat. **[2]**

**3.** In Country X, the demand for cassava is Qd = 800 − 25P and the supply is Qs = −100 + 20P, where P is the price per tonne and Q is thousands of tonnes.

**(a)** Work out the equilibrium price and quantity. **[2]**
**(b)** A minimum price of 24 is set. Calculate the surplus. **[3]**
**(c)** Calculate the cost to the government of buying the whole surplus. **[1]**
**(d)** Calculate farmers' total revenue before the policy and after it, if the government buys the surplus. **[2]**

**4.** In Country V, labour demand is Ld = 1000 − 50W and labour supply is Ls = −200 + 70W, where W is the hourly wage and L is hundreds of workers.

**(a)** Calculate the equilibrium wage and quantity of labour. **[2]**
**(b)** A minimum wage of 12 is set. Work out how many workers are left unemployed. **[3]**
**(c)** Calculate the total pay of employed workers before and after, and state what this shows about the policy. **[3]**

**5.** In Country Y, the market for its currency, the zeta, has demand Qd = 600 − 40P and supply Qs = −100 + 60P, where P is foreign units per zeta and Q is millions of zeta.

**(a)** Find the equilibrium exchange rate and the quantity of zeta traded. **[2]**
**(b)** The central bank fixes the rate at 8. Calculate the surplus of zeta. **[3]**
**(c)** Calculate the foreign currency the central bank must spend to hold the rate at 8. **[2]**
**(d)** Explain one effect of the rate of 8 on the country's exporters. **[2]**

**6.** In a market in Country U, Qd = 900 − 30P and Qs = −150 + 20P, where P is the price per unit and Q is units.

**(a)** Calculate the equilibrium price and quantity. **[2]**
**(b)** A tax of 5 per unit is placed on sellers. Find the new price and quantity. **[3]**
**(c)** Calculate how much of the tax is borne by consumers and by producers per unit. **[2]**
**(d)** Calculate the government's tax revenue. **[1]**
**(e)** Calculate price elasticity of demand and of supply at the original equilibrium (to 2 decimal places) and use them to explain why producers bear the larger share. **[3]**

**7.** In Country T, Qd = 390 − 10P and Qs = −90 + 20P for a product with external benefits.

**(a)** Find the market equilibrium before any subsidy. **[2]**
**(b)** The government pays a subsidy of 3 per unit to sellers. Find the new price and quantity once the subsidy is paid. **[3]**
**(c)** Calculate the change per unit in the price paid by consumers and the amount received by producers. **[2]**
**(d)** Calculate the cost to the government. **[1]**

**8.** A government owns a loss-making bus company. It loses 30 a year and could be sold for 450.

**(a)** Calculate how many years of losses equal the sale price. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain two likely benefits of privatising the company. **[4]**
**(c)** Explain one reason why privatisation might not benefit passengers. **[2]**

**9.** A government limits the amount of smoke that factories may release.

**(a)** Identify the market failure this regulation tackles. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain two difficulties in applying the regulation. **[4]**

**10.** A government has a budget of 360 to spend on clinics, which cost 24 each, and classroom blocks, which cost 45 each.

**(a)** Calculate the maximum number of clinics it could build. **[1]**
**(b)** It builds 6 clinics. Calculate how many whole classroom blocks it can then afford. **[3]**
**(c)** Calculate the opportunity cost of one extra clinic in classroom blocks, to 2 decimal places. **[2]**

**11.** Country A puts a tax on sweetened biscuits, which it treats as a demerit good. Before the tax a packet costs 50 and 250 packets a month are sold. The tax is 6 a packet and sellers pass on all of it. Price elasticity of demand is −0.4.

**(a)** Find the percentage rise in the price. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the new quantity sold. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the tax revenue. **[1]**
**(d)** Evaluate whether the tax is an effective way to reduce consumption. **[5]**

## Answers

**1.** Resources are allocated through private-sector markets and the price mechanism [1], with the government (public sector) also making allocation decisions [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Name both sectors in the definition, saying "private and public" explicitly.

**2. (a)** 700 − 20P = −200 + 25P gives 900 = 45P [1], so **P = 20 and Q = 300** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** At 16, Qd = 700 − 320 = 380 [1] and Qs = −200 + 400 = 200 [1]. **Shortage = 180 flats** [1]. **[3]**
**(c)** Only 200 flats are supplied but 380 wanted, so some tenants cannot find a flat [1] and may pay illegal extra payments, because demand for 200 flats would support a rent of 25 (700 − 20P = 200) [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A named problem needs a short reason connected to the numbers. "Black markets" alone is only a label.

**3. (a)** 800 − 25P = −100 + 20P gives 900 = 45P [1], so **P = 20 and Q = 300** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Qd = 800 − 600 = 200 [1] and Qs = −100 + 480 = 380 [1]. **Surplus = 180** thousand tonnes [1]. **[3]**
**(c)** 24 × 180 = **4,320** [1]. **[1]**
**(d)** Before: 20 × 300 = **6,000** [1]. After: 24 × 380 = **9,120** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Farmers sell their whole supply once the government buys the surplus, so use Qs, not Qd.

**4. (a)** 1000 − 50W = −200 + 70W gives 1,200 = 120W [1], so **W = 10 and L = 500** hundreds [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Ld = 1000 − 600 = 400 [1] and Ls = −200 + 840 = 640 [1]. Unemployment = 240 hundreds = **24,000 workers** [1]. **[3]**
**(c)** Before: 10 × 500 = 5,000 [1]. After: 12 × 400 = 4,800 [1]. Total pay falls although the hourly wage rises, because the fall in jobs outweighs the higher wage, so some workers lose [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* The units matter here. The workings are in hundreds, so the final answer should be converted to workers.

**5. (a)** 600 − 40P = −100 + 60P gives 700 = 100P [1], so **P = 7 and Q = 320** million [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Qd = 600 − 320 = 280 [1] and Qs = −100 + 480 = 380 [1]. **Surplus = 100 million zeta** [1]. **[3]**
**(c)** The bank must buy 100 million zeta [1] at 8 each, so 8 × 100 = **800 million** foreign units [1]. **[2]**
**(d)** Foreign buyers must pay more foreign currency for each zeta [1], so exports become dearer and sales and revenue fall [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Say which currency is spent: the central bank pays out foreign currency, not zeta.

**6. (a)** 900 − 30P = −150 + 20P gives 1,050 = 50P [1], so **P = 21 and Q = 270** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** New supply Qs = −150 + 20(P − 5) = −250 + 20P [1]. Then 1,150 = 50P [1], so **P = 23 and Q = 210** [1]. **[3]**
**(c)** Consumers: 23 − 21 = **2** [1]. Producers: 5 − 2 = **3** [1]. **[2]**
**(d)** 5 × 210 = **1,050** [1]. **[1]**
**(e)** PED = 30 × 21 / 270 = **2.33** (in size) [1]. PES = 20 × 21 / 270 = **1.56** [1]. Demand is more elastic, so consumers cut purchases sharply when the price rises and sellers cannot pass on as much, so producers bear more [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Link the burden to the relative elasticities. Calculating them without a comparison leaves the explanation unfinished.

**7. (a)** 390 − 10P = −90 + 20P gives 480 = 30P [1], so **P = 16 and Q = 230** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** New supply Qs = −90 + 20(P + 3) = −30 + 20P [1]. Then 390 − 10P = −30 + 20P gives **P = 14** [1] and **Q = 250** [1]. **[3]**
**(c)** Consumers pay 14, which is **2 less** [1]. Producers receive 14 + 3 = 17, which is **1 more** [1]. **[2]**
**(d)** 3 × 250 = **750** [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Producers receive the market price plus the subsidy, not the market price alone.

**8. (a)** 450 / 30 = **15 years** [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** Benefit one: the profit motive gives managers an incentive to cut costs [1], which may improve efficiency and service [1]. Benefit two: the government receives 450 from the sale [1], and it stops paying annual losses, so funds can go to other uses [1]. **[4]**
**(c)** A private owner may cut unprofitable routes or raise fares to make a profit [1], so passengers in remote areas may lose their service [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Develop each benefit or drawback with a because-chain rather than listing a bare point.

**9. (a)** **External cost** (negative externality) [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** Difficulty one: monitoring is costly, because inspectors are needed to measure emissions [1], and firms may hide breaches if penalties are weak [1]. Difficulty two: compliance raises costs [1], so firms may raise prices, cut jobs or relocate [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* This asks about the policy, so avoid describing the harm smoke does.

**10. (a)** 360 / 24 = **15 clinics** [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** 6 × 24 = 144 [1]. 360 − 144 = 216 [1]. 216 / 45 = 4.8, so **4 whole blocks** [1]. **[3]**
**(c)** 24 / 45 [1] = **0.53 blocks** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Round down for whole buildings, and show the division so the reader sees where 4 came from.

**11. (a)** (6 / 50) × 100 = **12%** [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** Quantity change = −0.4 × 12 = −4.8% [1]. New quantity = 250 × 0.952 = **238 packets** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** 6 × 238 = **1,428** [1]. **[1]**
**(d)** The tax reduces sales by only 12 packets, 4.8%, because demand is inelastic [1]. It raises 1,428, which could fund health spending [1]. Consumers on low incomes pay a larger share of their income, so it is regressive [1]. Other measures such as information campaigns or limits on sales to children could work alongside it [1]. Judgement: the tax is effective as a revenue source but weak at cutting consumption unless the rate is higher or substitutes are available [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the figures from parts (a) to (c) as evidence for your conclusion, and avoid a one-sided answer.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using the equilibrium quantity instead of the gap between demand and supply at the controlled price.
- Treating a minimum price as a shortage or a maximum price as a surplus.
- Using Qd instead of Qs for revenue when the government buys the surplus.
- Shifting supply by the tax or subsidy in the wrong direction when forming the new equation.
- Giving the producer's receipt after a tax as the market price.
- Leaving out units when the working is in hundreds.
- Listing advantages and drawbacks of privatisation without explaining why they happen.
- Ending an evaluation without a judgement tied to the case.
