## 3.3.5.1 Classifying alcohols

Find the carbon atom that carries the –OH group. Count how many other carbon atoms are bonded to it.

| Class | Carbons bonded to the C–OH carbon | H atoms on the C–OH carbon | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Primary | one for carbon-attached primary alcohols; methanol (CH₃OH) is a conventional primary-alcohol exception with no other carbon attached | two for carbon-attached primary alcohols; methanol has three | pentan-1-ol, CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂CH₂OH |
| Secondary | two | one | cyclopentanol, C₅H₉OH (–OH on a ring carbon) |
| Tertiary | three | none | 2-methylpentan-2-ol, (CH₃)₂C(OH)CH₂CH₂CH₃ |

The number of hydrogen atoms on the C–OH carbon is what controls oxidation, so it is worth writing it in.

## 3.3.5.1 Oxidation with acidified potassium dichromate(VI)

The specification names **acidified potassium dichromate(VI)** as a suitable oxidising agent: K₂Cr₂O₇
solution made acidic with dilute H₂SO₄. When the alcohol is oxidised, the orange dichromate(VI) ions are reduced to green
chromium(III) ions. So a colour change from **orange to green** shows that oxidation has happened.

You may write the oxygen supplied by the oxidising agent as **[O]**. The specification states that equations
showing [O] as the oxidant are acceptable.

**The three outcomes:**

- **Primary alcohol → aldehyde → carboxylic acid.** Each step uses one [O]. The first step also makes water.
- **Secondary alcohol → ketone.** One [O]; water also forms. Ketones are not oxidised further by this reagent.
- **Tertiary alcohol: not easily oxidised.** There is no hydrogen atom on the C–OH carbon to remove, so the
  mixture stays orange.

### Worked example 1: products and equations

Give the organic product, an equation using [O] and the observation for each alcohol warmed with acidified
potassium dichromate(VI).

**Pentan-1-ol (primary).** The –CH₂OH group becomes –CHO, then –COOH. The chain stays five carbons long.

```
CH₃(CH₂)₃CH₂OH + [O]  → CH₃(CH₂)₃CHO + H₂O       pentanal
CH₃(CH₂)₃CHO + [O]    → CH₃(CH₂)₃COOH           pentanoic acid
CH₃(CH₂)₃CH₂OH + 2[O] → CH₃(CH₂)₃COOH + H₂O     overall
```

Observation: orange to green.

**Cyclopentanol (secondary).** The –CH(OH)– ring carbon becomes C=O.

```
C₅H₉OH + [O] → C₅H₈O + H₂O       cyclopentanone
```

Observation: orange to green.

**2-methylpentan-2-ol (tertiary).** No reaction under these conditions. Observation: the mixture **stays
orange**.

Check every [O] equation by counting atoms on both sides.

## 3.3.5.1 How the method decides aldehyde or acid

A primary alcohol gives two possible products, so you control the outcome with the apparatus and the amounts.

| Product wanted | What you do | Why it works |
|---|---|---|
| **Aldehyde** | Use the alcohol in excess (a limited amount of oxidising agent), heat gently, and **distil off the aldehyde as soon as it forms** | The aldehyde boils at a lower temperature than the alcohol, because aldehyde molecules cannot hydrogen bond to each other. It leaves the flask before it can be oxidised again |
| **Carboxylic acid** | Use **excess** oxidising agent and **heat under reflux**, then distil the acid out at the end | In reflux the vapour condenses and runs back into the flask, so any aldehyde made is trapped with the excess dichromate and is converted to the acid |

### Worked example 2: choosing the set-up

A technician has pentan-1-ol and wants (a) a sample of pentanal and (b) a sample of pentanoic acid.
Describe the method for each.

- **(a)** Put acidified potassium dichromate(VI) in a flask with the pentan-1-ol in excess. Warm gently with
  the flask connected to a condenser set for distillation (sloping down to a collecting vessel). Pentanal is
  the most volatile organic substance in the flask, so it distils over as it forms and escapes further
  oxidation.
- **(b)** Mix pentan-1-ol with excess acidified potassium dichromate(VI). Heat under reflux (condenser
  vertical, open at the top) until the reaction is complete, then rearrange the apparatus and distil off the
  pentanoic acid.

Pair each method with its reason: **distil immediately** for the aldehyde, **reflux with excess oxidising
agent** for the acid.

## 3.3.5.1 Telling aldehydes from ketones

An aldehyde still has an H atom on its carbonyl carbon, so it is easy to oxidise; a ketone has none and resists
oxidation. Fehling's solution and Tollens' reagent are both mild oxidising agents that react only with the
aldehyde, and the specification names both.

| Reagent | Method | Result: aldehyde | Result: ketone |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Fehling's solution** (blue, contains Cu²⁺ complexes) | Warm in a water bath | Blue solution forms a **red precipitate** of copper(I) oxide, Cu₂O | Stays **blue** |
| **Tollens' reagent** (contains [Ag(NH₃)₂]⁺) | Warm in a water bath | A **silver mirror** coats the glass | **No change** |

Each positive result means the aldehyde has become a carboxylic acid (as its salt, since both reagents are
alkaline), while the metal ion is reduced: Cu²⁺ to copper(I), Ag⁺ to silver. Use a water bath, not a naked
flame, because many aldehydes and ketones are flammable. The silver complex in Tollens' reagent appears again
in the [9620 transition metal chemistry guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-transition-metals/).

### Worked example 3: identifying two liquids

Two colourless liquids are pentanal and cyclopentanone. Explain how Fehling's solution distinguishes them.

1. Warm a few drops of each with Fehling's solution in a water bath.
2. Pentanal: the blue solution gives a red precipitate, because the aldehyde is oxidised.
3. Cyclopentanone: the solution stays blue, because ketones are not oxidised by Fehling's solution.
4. Equation for the positive result: CH₃(CH₂)₃CHO + [O] → CH₃(CH₂)₃COOH.

Always give an observation for **both** compounds. "No reaction" is not an observation; "stays blue" is.

## 3.3.5.2 Elimination

Alkenes can be formed from alcohols by **acid-catalysed elimination**. Heat the alcohol with concentrated
phosphoric acid (concentrated sulfuric acid also works) as the catalyst, and a molecule of water is removed. This is also called dehydration.

1. The –OH oxygen uses one of its lone pairs to bond to an H⁺ ion supplied by the acid. This gives a protonated
   alcohol with a positive oxygen, R–OH₂⁺.
2. The C–O bond breaks heterolytically. Both electrons go to the oxygen, and a water molecule leaves. A
   carbocation forms on the carbon that held the –OH.
3. A carbon **adjacent** to C⁺ gives up a proton: the pair of electrons from its C–H bond becomes the second
   bond of C=C. Because H⁺ comes back out, the acid is not used up; it is a catalyst.

Draw each curly arrow from a lone pair or a bond, never from a positive charge or from H⁺ itself.

Which alkenes form from 2-methylpentan-2-ol with hot concentrated phosphoric acid?

- Step 1: C2 becomes the positive carbon. It carries two methyl groups and a propyl group.
- Step 2: look at each neighbouring carbon that has a hydrogen atom. The two methyl carbons are equivalent;
  C3 is different.
- Step 3: losing H⁺ from a methyl carbon gives **2-methylpent-1-ene**, CH₂=C(CH₃)CH₂CH₂CH₃.
- Step 4: losing H⁺ from C3 gives **2-methylpent-2-ene**, (CH₃)₂C=CHCH₂CH₃.
- Step 5: check for E/Z isomers. C1 of the first alkene carries two hydrogens and C2 of the second carries
  two methyl groups, so neither shows E/Z isomerism.

Answer: **two alkenes**, 2-methylpent-1-ene and 2-methylpent-2-ene.

### Polymers without crude oil

The specification notes that alkenes made this way can be used to produce addition polymers **without using
monomers derived from crude oil**. If the alcohol comes from a non-petroleum source (for example, from plant
material), the alkene and the polymer made from it do not depend on crude oil.

## Required practical 4: distil a product from a reaction

Distillation separates a volatile product from a reaction mixture. In this topic it is how you collect an
aldehyde before it is oxidised further, and how you collect an alkene from an elimination.

**Points of technique to explain:**

- **Anti-bumping granules** in the flask give smooth boiling.
- **Thermometer bulb level with the side arm** that leads to the condenser, so it reads the temperature of
  the vapour that is distilling.
- **Condenser water in at the bottom and out at the top**, so the condenser stays full of cold water.
- **Heat with an electric heater or a water bath**, not a naked flame, because organic vapours are
  flammable.
- **Never heat a sealed system**: the collecting vessel must be open to the air (or vented).
- **Collect the fraction** that distils close to the boiling point of the product you want.

### Worked example 5: percentage yield of a distilled product

6.16 g of pentan-1-ol (Mr 88.0) is oxidised and the pentanal (Mr 86.0) is distilled off as it forms. The
student collects 2.71 g of pentanal. Calculate the percentage yield.

```
n(pentan-1-ol)       = 6.16 ÷ 88.0 = 0.0700 mol
theoretical n(pentanal) = 0.0700 mol            (1 : 1 ratio)
actual n(pentanal)   = 2.71 ÷ 86.0 = 0.03151 mol
percentage yield     = 0.03151 ÷ 0.0700 × 100 = 45.0 %
```

Answer: **45.0 %**. Losses include incomplete reaction, further oxidation and liquid left in the apparatus. For more mole practice, see the
[9620 moles and equations guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-amount-of-substance/).

## Common errors

- Counting all the carbons in the molecule to classify an alcohol, instead of only those bonded to the C–OH
  carbon.
- Saying "reflux" for an aldehyde, or "distil" without saying the aldehyde is removed **as it forms**.
- Explaining the low boiling point of an aldehyde as "weaker bonds". It is the absence of hydrogen bonding
  between aldehyde molecules.
- Writing that a tertiary alcohol "turns green". It stays orange.
- Giving "no reaction" for the ketone in Fehling's or Tollens' tests instead of an observation.
- Starting the first elimination arrow at H⁺. It starts at a lone pair on oxygen.
- Forgetting a second alkene, or E/Z isomers, when the C–OH carbon has two different neighbours with H atoms.

## Frequently asked questions

**How do you classify an alcohol as primary, secondary or tertiary?**
Find the carbon atom that carries the –OH group and count how many other carbon atoms are bonded to it: one for primary, two for secondary and three for tertiary. Counting every carbon in the molecule is a common mistake.

**How do you oxidise a primary alcohol to a carboxylic acid?**
Mix the alcohol with excess acidified potassium dichromate(VI) and heat under reflux until the reaction is complete, then distil off the acid. To stop at the aldehyde instead, keep the alcohol in excess and distil the aldehyde as it forms.

**How do Fehling's solution and Tollens' reagent tell aldehydes from ketones?**
Both are mild oxidising agents that react only with aldehydes, because an aldehyde has a hydrogen on its carbonyl carbon. Fehling's solution turns from blue to a red precipitate and Tollens' reagent gives a silver mirror, while a ketone gives no change.

**Can alkenes from alcohols be used to make polymers without crude oil?**
Yes. The OxfordAQA 9620 specification notes that alkenes made by eliminating water from alcohols can be used to make addition polymers without monomers derived from crude oil, provided the alcohol itself comes from a non-petroleum source such as plant material.
