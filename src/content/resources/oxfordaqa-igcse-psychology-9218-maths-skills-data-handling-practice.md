estimation, arithmetic means and significant figures, from OxfordAQA International GCSE Psychology

## Section A — Short questions

**1.** Which **one** of the following is equal to 0.35?
A 3/5
B 7/20
C 35/1000
D 3.5% **[1]**

**2.** Give 0.007384 to **two** significant figures. **[1]**

**3.** In a perception study, 45 of 60 participants saw the ambiguous figure as a number. Express this
as a fraction in its simplest form and as a percentage. **[2]**

**4.** In an observation, 14 shoppers helped a person who had dropped their bags and 56 did not.
Write the ratio of helpers to non-helpers in its simplest form. **[1]**

**5.** Using your answer to question 4, calculate the percentage of all shoppers who helped. **[2]**

**6.** Estimate the number of participants who conformed in a sample of 396, if 21% conformed. Show
the rounded values you used. **[2]**

## Section B — Longer questions

**7.** A researcher measured reaction times (in seconds) in a dual-task condition: 2.31, 1.87, 2.06,
2.45, 1.98, 2.20.
**(a)** Calculate the mean reaction time. **[2]**
**(b)** Give your answer to an appropriate number of significant figures, and justify your choice.
**[2]**

**8.** In a memory study, the mean number of words recalled rose from 40 in the first trial to 52 after
practice.
**(a)** State the increase in the number of words. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage increase. **[2]**

**9.** In an invented replication of a conformity study, 20 participants each completed 12 critical
trials. They conformed on 66 trials in total.
**(a)** Calculate the total number of critical trials. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage of critical trials on which participants conformed. **[2]**
**(c)** Give the fraction of critical trials on which they conformed, in its simplest form. **[1]**

**10.** Two conditions in a study had different numbers of participants. In condition A, 8 of 32
participants reported the smoke. In condition B, 5 of 20 reported it. A student says condition A had
more reporting. Use ratios or percentages to explain whether the student is right. **[3]**

**11.** A student calculated the mean of 48, 52, 47, 53 and 50 as 500. Use estimation to explain how the
student could tell this is wrong, and give the correct mean. **[3]**

**12.** In a study of the serial position effect, a researcher recorded these percentages of
participants recalling the first word, the middle word and the last word of a list: 82.35%, 29.41%
and 76.47%. There were 17 participants.
**(a)** Calculate how many participants recalled the middle word. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why giving these percentages to four significant figures is not appropriate for this
sample, and suggest a more appropriate number. **[2]**

---

## Answers

**1.** **B** [1]. (7 ÷ 20 = 0.35.)

**2.** 0.0074 [1].

**3.** 45/60 = **3/4** [1]; 3 ÷ 4 × 100 = **75%** [1].

**4.** 14 : 56 = **1 : 4** [1].

**5.** Total parts = 1 + 4 = 5, so 1/5 helped [1]; 1/5 × 100 = **20%** [1]. (Check: 14 ÷ 70 × 100 =
20%.)

**6.** Rounded values: 400 × 0.2 [1] = about **80** participants [1]. (Exact: 396 × 0.21 = 83.16.)

**7. (a)** Total = 2.31 + 1.87 + 2.06 + 2.45 + 1.98 + 2.20 = 12.87 [1]; mean = 12.87 ÷ 6 = 2.145
seconds [1].
**(b)** **2.15 seconds** (3 s.f.) [1], because the data are recorded to three significant figures, so
the mean should not claim greater precision [1].

**8. (a)** 52 − 40 = **12 words** [1].
**(b)** 12 ÷ 40 [1] × 100 = **30%** [1].

**9. (a)** 20 × 12 = **240** [1].
**(b)** 66 ÷ 240 [1] × 100 = **27.5%** [1].
**(c)** 66/240 = **11/40** [1].

**10.** Condition A: 8 : 24 = 1 : 3, or 8 ÷ 32 × 100 = 25% [1]. Condition B: 5 : 15 = 1 : 3, or
5 ÷ 20 × 100 = 25% [1]. The proportions are the same, so the student is wrong; A had more people
reporting only because it had more participants [1].

**11.** All the values are close to 50, so the mean must also be close to 50 [1]; 500 is ten times too
big, suggesting the student divided incorrectly or misplaced a digit [1]. Correct mean:
250 ÷ 5 = **50** [1].

**12. (a)** 29.41 ÷ 100 × 17 = 4.9997 [1], so **5 participants** [1].
**(b)** With only 17 participants, each person is worth about 6% of the sample, so four significant
figures implies a precision the data cannot support [1]; two or three significant figures (for example
29% or 29.4%) would be more appropriate [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using the wrong total, such as dividing by the non-helpers instead of everyone.
- Rounding too early in multi-step calculations.
- Giving more significant figures than the data justify, or not the number asked for.
- Forgetting the unit on a mean.
- Not showing working, which can earn marks even if the final answer is wrong.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can I use a calculator?**
Check the instructions on your paper. Practise both with and without one, and always estimate to check.

**How many significant figures should I give if the question does not say?**
Match the precision of the data given; two or three significant figures is usually sensible.
