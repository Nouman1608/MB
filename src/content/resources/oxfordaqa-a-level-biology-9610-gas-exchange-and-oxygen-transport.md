This guide goes deeper on one section of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Biology (9610) specification, Version 5.1: **3.1.5 Gas exchange and the transport of oxygen in living organisms**, in Unit 1, The diversity of living organisms. Unit 1 is part of the International AS and counts towards the full International A-level.

The section follows a logical chain. Size determines surface area to volume ratio, the ratio determines whether diffusion alone can supply oxygen, and when it cannot, organisms evolve specialised gas exchange surfaces and a transport system. ## Surface area to volume ratio

As an organism gets bigger, its volume (which determines its demand for oxygen) increases faster than its surface area (which determines its supply by diffusion). The ratio of surface area to volume falls.

**Worked example 1.** Compare cubes with sides of 1, 2, 4 and 8 cm.

| Side / cm | Surface area / cm2 | Volume / cm3 | Surface area : volume |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 6 | 1 | 6 : 1 |
| 2 | 24 | 8 | 3 : 1 |
| 4 | 96 | 64 | 1.5 : 1 |
| 8 | 384 | 512 | 0.75 : 1 |

Each time the side doubles, the surface area is multiplied by 4 and the volume by 8, so the ratio halves.

A small organism, for example a single-celled organism, has a large ratio, so diffusion across its surface meets its needs. A larger organism has a smaller ratio and a longer diffusion distance to its core. Organisms respond in two ways, as the specification notes:

- **Body shape.** Flat or thin shapes (such as a flatworm or a leaf) keep a high ratio. Compact shapes in cold climates reduce heat loss.
- **Systems.** Large organisms develop specialised exchange surfaces and a **mass transport** system, which moves substances in bulk over long distances, for example a circulatory system carrying oxygen.

## Gas exchange systems

All gas exchange surfaces share features: a **large surface area**, a **thin** barrier, a **steep concentration gradient** (maintained by ventilation or blood flow) and, for a living tissue, moisture to dissolve gases.

### A single-celled organism

Oxygen diffuses directly through the cell-surface membrane. The ratio is large and the diffusion distance is short, so no special system is needed.

### An insect

Insects have a **tracheal system**. Air enters through openings called **spiracles** on the body surface, and passes into branching tubes called **tracheae**, which end in very small **tracheoles** that reach individual cells. Oxygen diffuses along the tubes and into the cells without being carried by blood. The tracheole ends contain fluid. During activity, lactic acid from anaerobic respiration lowers the water potential of the cells and fluid is drawn into the cells by osmosis, which brings more air closer to the cells. Large insects also use body movements, such as abdominal pumping, to ventilate the system.

**Compromise.** Open spiracles lose water vapour. Insects can close their spiracles to limit water loss and open them for gas exchange. The insect's waxy exoskeleton also cuts water loss across the body surface.

### The leaf of a dicotyledonous plant

Carbon dioxide diffuses into the leaf through **stomata**, which are pores in the epidermis, controlled by pairs of guard cells. Inside, the spongy mesophyll has large air spaces that let the gas diffuse to the cell surfaces of the mesophyll cells, which have a large total surface area. Oxygen from photosynthesis leaves by the reverse route. Open stomata also lose water vapour by evaporation.

**Compromise.** Stomata close when water is scarce, which cuts water loss but also reduces carbon dioxide uptake.

### Xerophytes

A **xerophyte** is a plant adapted to dry conditions. Features reduce water loss from the leaf:

- a thick waxy cuticle;
- stomata in sunken pits, trapping a humid layer of air;
- rolled leaves, enclosing the stomata in a humid space;
- hairs that trap moist air close to the surface;
- a reduced leaf surface area, for example spines or needles.

Each feature increases the diffusion distance or reduces the water potential gradient. Each feature also reduces carbon dioxide uptake, which is the compromise.

### The human gas exchange system

Air passes down the **trachea**, into two **bronchi**, then into smaller **bronchioles** and ends in the **alveoli** in the **lungs**.

**The alveolus** is the gas exchange surface:

- the wall is a single layer of flattened epithelial cells, so the diffusion distance is short;
- the capillary wall is also one cell thick;
- there are millions of alveoli, giving a very large total surface area;
- a dense network of capillaries carries oxygen away and brings carbon dioxide, which keeps the concentration gradients steep;
- ventilation keeps the oxygen concentration in the alveoli high.

**Breathing mechanism.**

| | Inspiration | Expiration |
|---|---|---|
| Diaphragm | Contracts and flattens | Relaxes and rises |
| External intercostal muscles | Contract, raising the ribs | Relax, lowering the ribs |
| Thoracic volume | Increases | Decreases |
| Pressure in the thorax | Falls below atmospheric | Rises above atmospheric |
| Air movement | Into the lungs | Out of the lungs |

Quiet expiration is passive. In forced expiration the internal intercostal muscles contract and the abdominal muscles help.

### Lung disease and data

You must interpret data on the effect of lung disease on gas exchange and ventilation. Three measurements recur:

- **Tidal volume:** the volume of air in each breath.
- **Ventilation rate:** the number of breaths per minute. Pulmonary ventilation = tidal volume x breathing rate.
- **FVC** (forced vital capacity) and **FEV1** (the volume forced out in the first second). FEV1 / FVC is expressed as a percentage.

**Worked example 2.** A healthy person has an FVC of 5.0 dm3 and an FEV1 of 4.0 dm3. A person with fibrosis has an FVC of 2.8 and an FEV1 of 2.4. A person with asthma has an FVC of 3.8 and an FEV1 of 2.1.

1. Healthy: 4.0 / 5.0 x 100 = 80 %.
2. Fibrosis: 2.4 / 2.8 x 100 = 85.7 %.
3. Asthma: 2.1 / 3.8 x 100 = 55.3 %.
4. In fibrosis, the lung tissue is scarred and less elastic, so the lung cannot expand fully. FVC is low, but the air that can be moved leaves quickly, so the ratio stays high.
5. In asthma, the airways narrow, so air leaves slowly, which lowers FEV1 more than FVC and the ratio is low.

**Worked example 3.** A resting person has a tidal volume of 0.5 dm3 and breathes 15 times per minute. Pulmonary ventilation = 0.5 x 15 = 7.5 dm3 per minute.

### Unfamiliar exchange surfaces

You may be asked about an animal you have not studied, such as a fish gill or an amphibian skin. Use the checklist: surface area, thickness, gradient, ventilation or blood flow, and moisture. State the feature, then link it to rate of diffusion.

## Haemoglobin and the transport of oxygen

### Circulation

Blood flows from the heart in **arteries**, through **capillaries** and back in **veins**. You need names only for the coronary arteries and for the vessels entering and leaving the heart and liver: aorta, vena cava, pulmonary artery, pulmonary vein, hepatic artery, hepatic vein and hepatic portal vein.

### Haemoglobin

Haemoglobin is a protein with four polypeptide globin chains, each associated with an iron-containing **haem** group. It is found in red blood cells. Each haem group binds one oxygen molecule, so one haemoglobin molecule can carry four.

The **oxygen dissociation curve** plots the percentage saturation of haemoglobin with oxygen against the partial pressure of oxygen.

- At the lungs, the partial pressure of oxygen is high and haemoglobin is nearly saturated.
- In respiring tissues the partial pressure is low and haemoglobin releases oxygen.
- The curve is S-shaped, because after the first oxygen molecule binds, the shape of the haemoglobin changes, making the next one easier to bind. A small drop in partial pressure then releases a large amount of oxygen.

**Effect of carbon dioxide (Bohr effect).** A high partial pressure of carbon dioxide in respiring tissues lowers the affinity of haemoglobin for oxygen, so the curve shifts to the right. Haemoglobin releases more oxygen to the tissues that need it most.

### Different haemoglobins

Haemoglobins are a group of chemically similar molecules found in many organisms, with different oxygen-transporting properties. A haemoglobin with a **higher affinity** has a curve to the left. It loads at a low partial pressure of oxygen, which suits organisms in low-oxygen environments, for example a fetus (which takes oxygen from its mother's blood), or an animal that lives at high altitude or burrows underground. A haemoglobin with a **lower affinity** has a curve to the right and unloads readily, which suits an animal with a high metabolic rate, such as a small, active bird.

## Common mistakes

- Saying a larger organism has "less surface area". It has more surface area but less in proportion to its volume.
- Writing that insects have blood that carries oxygen to the cells.
- Forgetting that the diaphragm flattens as it contracts.
- Saying air is "sucked in". The pressure inside the thorax falls and the higher external pressure pushes air in.
- Confusing a left shift and a right shift: left means higher affinity.
- Claiming xerophytes have no stomata.
- Saying the Bohr effect raises the affinity of haemoglobin for oxygen. It lowers it.

## Exam technique

- **Use "ratio" in size questions** and give a calculation if data are provided.
- **Use the checklist** for any unfamiliar surface.
- **Describe curve shifts with data**: quote the percentage saturation at a stated partial pressure.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need to remember the cube table?**
No, but you must be able to calculate surface area, volume and the ratio for a shape quickly.

**Why is the oxygen dissociation curve S-shaped?**
Because haemoglobin's shape changes as each oxygen binds, making further binding easier.
