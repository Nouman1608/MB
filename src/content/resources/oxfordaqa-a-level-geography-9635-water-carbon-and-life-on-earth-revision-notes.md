## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Open system | Energy and matter cross the boundary (a drainage basin) |
| Closed system | Only energy crosses the boundary (close to true for the global cycles) |
| Positive feedback | A change sets off effects that amplify it |
| Negative feedback | A change sets off effects that reduce it |
| Dynamic equilibrium | Inputs and outputs balance, so stores stay roughly steady |
| Interception | Precipitation caught by vegetation before it reaches the ground |
| Stemflow / throughfall | Water running down stems / dripping from leaves to the ground |
| Infiltration / percolation | Water entering soil / moving down into rock |
| Throughflow | Water moving sideways through soil |
| Overland flow | Water moving across the surface when soil cannot absorb it |
| Baseflow | River flow fed slowly by groundwater |
| Water balance | P = Q + E ± ΔS |
| Lag time | Time from peak rainfall to peak discharge |
| Ablation / accumulation | Loss of ice by melting, sublimation and calving / gain of ice from snowfall |
| Carbon sequestration | Long-term capture of carbon in a store such as ocean sediment or soil |
| Biological pump | Plankton fix carbon; their remains sink to the deep ocean |
| Carbon budget | The balance of carbon entering and leaving stores |
| Albedo | The share of incoming sunlight a surface reflects |

## Store sizes to quote

| Water (USGS) | Share of all water |
|---|---|
| Oceans | about 96.5% |
| Ice caps, glaciers, permanent snow | about 1.74% |
| Groundwater | about 1.69% |
| Atmosphere | about 0.001% |

| Carbon (year 2000 estimates) | GtC |
|---|---|
| Sedimentary rocks | tens of millions |
| Oceans | about 38,400 |
| Fossil fuels | about 4,130 |
| Soils | about 1,500 |
| Living biomass | about 600 to 1,000 |
| Atmosphere | about 720 then; now nearer 890 |

The 890 figure comes from 421 ppm x 2.12 GtC per ppm. Round store sizes in exams and say they are estimates.

## Case studies and evidence at a glance

| Example | What | Key facts | One evaluation point |
|---|---|---|---|
| Amazon rainforest | Tropical rainforest water and carbon cycles | About 5.5 million km² of forest, 60% in Brazil; about half of rainfall recycled from the forest; about 110 billion tonnes of carbon | Store sizes are estimates with wide uncertainty |
| Amazon deforestation | Human change | 27,772 km² cleared in 2004, 4,571 km² in 2012; about 80% linked to cattle; about 20% of the forest lost by 2022 | The fall after 2004 shows policy can work, but it was not sustained |
| Gatti and colleagues (2021) | Aircraft carbon dioxide measurements over Amazonia | The south-eastern Amazon had become a net carbon source | Based on aircraft profiles at four sites, not the whole forest |
| River Eden, Cumbria | Local catchment, flooding and supply | January 2005: up to 180 mm upstream in a day, about 2,700 Carlisle homes flooded; December 2015, Storm Desmond: defences overwhelmed | Carlisle's defences were breached in 2015, so defences alone are not enough |
| Haweswater Reservoir | Water supply in the Eden basin | About 25% of North West England's water; filled by 1935 | Storage secures supply but moves water out of the basin |
| Sleipner, Norway | Carbon capture and storage | About 1 million tonnes of carbon dioxide a year into the Utsira formation since 1996 | Proven, but tiny next to global emissions |
| Mauna Loa record | Atmospheric carbon dioxide | Annual mean 315.98 ppm (1959) to 421.08 ppm (2023) | One site, but matches global records |

## Must-know distinctions

- **Store vs flow:** groundwater is a store; groundwater flow is a transfer.
- **Open vs closed system:** a drainage basin is open; the global water cycle is close to closed.
- **Infiltration-excess vs saturation-excess overland flow:** rain falls faster than soil can absorb it, versus soil already full of water.
- **Peak discharge vs bankfull discharge:** the highest flow in a storm versus the flow that just fills the channel; flooding happens only when discharge exceeds bankfull.
- **Fast vs slow carbon cycle:** photosynthesis and respiration take days to decades; rock weathering and sedimentation take millions of years. Burning fossil fuels moves carbon from slow to fast.
- **GtC vs gigatonnes of carbon dioxide:** multiply GtC by 44/12 (about 3.67) to get carbon dioxide.
- **Natural vs enhanced greenhouse effect:** the natural effect keeps Earth habitable; the enhanced effect is the extra warming from human emissions.
- **Mitigation vs adaptation:** reducing carbon transfers to the atmosphere versus coping with the effects. The specification's human interventions point is about mitigation.

## Feedback chains to learn

- **Ice-albedo (positive):** warming → ice melts → darker surface → more heat absorbed → more warming.
- **Water vapour (positive):** warming → more evaporation → more water vapour, a greenhouse gas → more warming.
- **Permafrost (positive):** warming → thaw → decomposition releases carbon dioxide and methane → more warming.
- **Rainforest drying (positive):** deforestation → less evapotranspiration → less rain → more fire → carbon released.
- **Carbon dioxide fertilisation (negative):** more carbon dioxide → faster photosynthesis → more uptake.
- **Weathering (negative, very slow):** warmer and wetter → faster chemical weathering → carbon dioxide drawn down.

## Mini essay plan

*"Human activity is now the main driver of change in the carbon cycle." To what extent do you agree?*

1. **Introduction:** define the carbon cycle as linked stores and flows; separate natural and human drivers; state your line (agree for the fast cycle over recent decades).
2. **Human drivers:** burning hydrocarbons moves carbon from the lithosphere to the atmosphere; deforestation, as in the Amazon, and farming release biosphere carbon. Use the Mauna Loa rise from 315.98 to 421.08 ppm.
3. **Scale and speed:** human transfers happen in decades; natural slow-cycle transfers take millions of years.
4. **Natural drivers still matter:** wildfires and volcanic activity release carbon; seasonal photosynthesis creates the yearly Mauna Loa cycle.
5. **Interaction:** human warming can trigger natural feedbacks, such as permafrost thaw and forest drying, so the line between natural and human is blurred.
6. **Conclusion:** agree largely for the last century and for the atmosphere store; over geological time natural processes dominate. Judge by timescale and store.

## Quick self-test

1. Name the four water stores listed in the specification.
2. Give the water balance equation.
3. What is stemflow?
4. Define lag time.
5. Give two drainage basin features that make a hydrograph flashy.
6. Name two ways carbon is sequestered in oceans.
7. Convert 5 GtC into gigatonnes of carbon dioxide.
8. A glacier gains 1.8 million m³ and loses 2.2 million m³ of water equivalent in a year. What is its mass balance?
9. Why does most carbon in a tropical rainforest sit in the vegetation rather than the soil?
10. Give one positive and one negative feedback in the carbon cycle.
11. Name one human intervention designed to reduce carbon transfers to the atmosphere.
12. How many cubic metres of water fall when 12 mm of rain falls on 3 km²?

### Answers

1. Lithosphere, hydrosphere, cryosphere and atmosphere.
2. P = Q + E ± ΔS (precipitation = runoff + evapotranspiration ± change in storage).
3. Intercepted water running down plant stems and trunks to the ground.
4. The time between peak rainfall and peak discharge.
5. Any two: steep slopes, impermeable rock, high drainage density, small round basin, urban surfaces, saturated soil.
6. The physical pump (carbon dioxide dissolving in cold water that sinks) and the biological pump (plankton remains sinking); also shells forming sediments.
7. 5 x 44/12 = about 18.3 gigatonnes of carbon dioxide.
8. 1.8 − 2.2 = −0.4 million m³: a negative balance, so the glacier shrinks.
9. Warm, wet conditions speed up decomposition, so dead matter is broken down fast and nutrients are taken back into plants quickly.
10. Positive: permafrost thaw releasing carbon. Negative: carbon dioxide fertilisation of plants.
11. Any one: afforestation, carbon capture and storage, peatland restoration, reduced tillage, forest protection such as REDD+.
12. 12 x 3 x 1,000 = 36,000 m³.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calling a drainage basin a closed system.
- Listing stores and flows without naming the direction of the transfer.
- Quoting store sizes with no units, or mixing GtC and gigatonnes of carbon dioxide.
- Explaining hydrograph shape with one factor only, with no link to infiltration or throughflow.
- Treating Honister Pass rainfall as if it fell in the Eden basin.
- Rainforest answers that describe deforestation but never say what happens to interception, evapotranspiration or carbon stores.
- Evaluating human interventions as simply "good" without judging scale, cost or permanence.
- Essays with no judgement on "to what extent".
