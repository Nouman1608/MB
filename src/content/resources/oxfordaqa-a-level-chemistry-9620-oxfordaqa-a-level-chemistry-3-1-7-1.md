This guide goes deeper on point **3.1.7.1 Chemical equilibria and Le Chatelier's principle** of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Chemistry (9620) specification, Version 5.3. The specification states that in a reversible reaction at equilibrium "forward and reverse reactions proceed at equal rates" and "the concentrations of reactants and products remain constant"; that Le Chatelier's principle predicts the effects of temperature, pressure and concentration on the position of equilibrium in homogeneous reactions; and that "a catalyst does not affect the position of equilibrium". Students must also "explain why, for a reversible reaction used in an industrial process, a compromise temperature and pressure may be used". It is International AS content, assessed in Unit 2.

## How a system reaches dynamic equilibrium

Mix the reactants of a reversible reaction in a closed system:

- At the start, reactant concentrations are high, so the **forward rate** is high; there is no product, so the **reverse rate** is zero.
- As reactants are used up, the forward rate falls. As products build up, the reverse rate rises.
- Eventually the two rates are **equal**. From then on, every second, as much product is made as is used up, so all concentrations stay **constant**.

This is **dynamic** equilibrium: both reactions continue. Two misconceptions to avoid:

- The concentrations are **constant**, not **equal**. An equilibrium mixture can be mostly products or mostly reactants.
- The reaction has **not stopped**. If a labelled atom is added as one product, it soon appears in the reactants too.

A **closed system** is needed so nothing escapes. If a gaseous product can leave, the reverse reaction cannot keep pace and equilibrium is never reached.

## Le Chatelier's principle

If a factor affecting a system at equilibrium is changed, the position of equilibrium shifts in the direction that **opposes** (minimises) the change.

The **position of equilibrium** describes the composition of the equilibrium mixture. "Shifts to the right" means more products and less reactants than before. The shift reduces the effect of the change but never cancels it completely.

### Concentration

Adding a reactant (or removing a product) shifts the position to the right; adding a product (or removing a reactant) shifts it to the left.

**Worked example 1: chromate and dichromate**

```
2CrO₄²⁻(aq) + 2H⁺(aq) ⇌ Cr₂O₇²⁻(aq) + H₂O(l)
   yellow                    orange
```

- **Add dilute acid**: [H⁺] rises; the position shifts right to use up some of the added H⁺; the solution turns more **orange**.
- **Add sodium hydroxide**: OH⁻ reacts with H⁺ to form water, lowering [H⁺]; the position shifts left to replace some H⁺; the solution turns more **yellow**.

The second case is a favourite: the added substance is not in the equation, so you must first say what it does to a species that **is** in the equation.

### Pressure (gas equilibria)

Increasing the total pressure shifts the position towards the side with **fewer moles of gas**, which reduces the pressure. If both sides have the same number of moles of gas, pressure has no effect on the position.

**Worked example 2: what you see when a gas is compressed**

```
N₂O₄(g) ⇌ 2NO₂(g)        ΔH = +57 kJ mol⁻¹
colourless    brown
```

A sealed syringe of the equilibrium mixture is quickly compressed to half its volume at constant temperature.

1. **Immediately** the mixture looks **darker**: the same NO₂ molecules are in half the volume, so [NO₂] doubles.
2. **Then** the colour fades somewhat: the higher pressure shifts the position to the left, the side with 1 mole of gas rather than 2, converting some NO₂ to N₂O₄.
3. **Finally** the mixture is still darker than at the start: the shift only partly opposes the change.

Compare H₂(g) + I₂(g) ⇌ 2HI(g). There are 2 moles of gas on each side, so compression does not shift the position. The purple colour of iodine still darkens, but only because every concentration has increased; there is no later fading.

### Temperature

Increasing the temperature shifts the position in the **endothermic** direction, which absorbs heat; decreasing it shifts the position in the **exothermic** direction.

For N₂O₄ ⇌ 2NO₂ the forward reaction is endothermic (ΔH = +57 kJ mol⁻¹), so heating turns the mixture **browner** and cooling makes it paler. Only a temperature change also changes the value of the equilibrium constant; that is developed in the [Kc guide (3.1.7.2)](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-1-7-2/).

### Catalysts

A catalyst speeds up the forward and reverse reactions equally, because it lowers both activation energies by the same amount. Equilibrium is reached **sooner**, but the position of equilibrium is **unchanged**. See the [catalysts guide (3.1.6.5)](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-1-6-5/).

## Industrial compromise conditions

Industry wants **lots of product, quickly, cheaply and safely**. Rate and yield can pull in opposite directions, so the chosen conditions are compromises.

### Worked example 3: making methanol

```
CO(g) + 2H₂(g) ⇌ CH₃OH(g)        ΔH = −91 kJ mol⁻¹
```

**Temperature.**

- The forward reaction is exothermic, so a **low** temperature shifts the position right and gives a **higher yield**.
- But at a low temperature the rate is too slow, even with a catalyst.
- A **moderate** temperature is therefore chosen: a lower yield than at a low temperature, but methanol is produced fast enough to be economic.

**Pressure.**

- There are 3 moles of gas on the left and 1 on the right, so a **high** pressure shifts the position right and increases the yield. It also increases the rate, because the gases are more concentrated.
- But very high pressures need thick-walled vessels and powerful compressors, which are expensive to build and run and carry a greater safety risk.
- A **moderately high** pressure is chosen, balancing yield and rate against cost and safety.

**Catalyst.** It gives an acceptable rate at the moderate temperature but does not change the yield.

**Unreacted gases** are separated from the methanol and recycled, so over time most of the reactants are converted even though each pass does not reach a high yield.

## Common mistakes

- Saying that at equilibrium the concentrations of reactants and products are **equal**.
- Saying the reaction **stops** at equilibrium.
- Giving a direction of shift without the reason (for example, not naming "fewer moles of gas" or "endothermic direction").
- Saying a catalyst increases yield, or that it shifts the equilibrium.
- In compromise questions, discussing yield only and forgetting rate, cost or safety.
- Applying the pressure rule by counting moles of all species, including liquids and solids; count **gas** moles only.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **State and explain the effect of X on the yield**: (1) effect (increases or decreases); (2) direction the position shifts; (3) reason from the equation (gas moles or sign of ΔH) and that the shift opposes the change.
- **Explain why a compromise temperature is used**: high yield needs low temperature (exothermic); low temperature is too slow; moderate temperature gives acceptable yield at acceptable rate.
- **Explain why a compromise pressure is used**: higher pressure increases yield (and rate), but expense of equipment and energy, and safety, limit it.
- **Describe what you would see**: name the colour change and link it to the species whose concentration changes.
