## How chromatography separates a mixture

Every type of chromatography has two phases. The **stationary phase** stays in place: the solid on a TLC
plate, the solid packed in a column, or the solid (or liquid-coated solid) in a GC column. The **moving
phase** flows past it: a liquid solvent in TLC and CC, and a gas in GC. (Many books call it the mobile phase.)

Each component is shared between the two phases. In the specification's words, separation depends on the
**balance between solubility in the moving phase and retention by the stationary phase**.

- A component that is very soluble in the moving phase and weakly held by the stationary phase spends most
  of its time moving. It travels far (TLC), leaves the column first (CC) or has a short retention time (GC).
- A component that is strongly held by the stationary phase travels a short distance or comes out later.

Two components separate only if their balances differ. If they are the same, you see one spot or one peak.

### Worked example 1: predicting the order of separation

A mixture is run on a TLC plate whose stationary phase is polar. The solvent is much less polar. Compound J
can form hydrogen bonds; compound K cannot and is only weakly polar. Which has the larger Rf value?

1. The polar stationary phase interacts more strongly with J, because J can hydrogen bond.
2. So J is retained more strongly and spends less time in the moving phase.
3. K is held only weakly and dissolves well in the less polar solvent.
4. K moves further up the plate, so **K has the larger Rf value**.

The answer depends on both phases. With a much more polar solvent, J would dissolve better in the moving
phase and its Rf value would rise. Always explain using both phases.

## Thin-layer chromatography (TLC)

In TLC, a plate is coated with a thin layer of solid, the stationary phase. Small spots of each sample go on a
pencil line near the bottom (the **baseline**), and the plate stands in a shallow layer of solvent in a covered
container. The solvent rises up the plate. Near the top, you remove the plate and mark the **solvent front**
at once.

Coloured substances, such as many transition metal complexes, show as visible spots. Colourless ones must be
located: the specification names ultraviolet light and developing agents such as ninhydrin for amino acids
(section 3.3.13.2). The apparatus list (AT i) allows thin-layer or paper chromatography; the Rf method is the
same for both.

### Calculating Rf values

```
Rf = distance moved by the spot from the baseline
     ---------------------------------------------
     distance moved by the solvent front from the baseline
```

- Measure both distances from the baseline, and measure to the **centre** of the spot.
- Rf has **no units** and lies between 0 and 1.
- An Rf value only applies to one stationary phase, one solvent and one set of conditions.

### Worked example 2: calculating Rf values

The solvent front is 7.20 cm above the baseline. Two spots have centres 1.80 cm and 4.68 cm above it.

```
Spot 1: Rf = 1.80 / 7.20 = 0.25
Spot 2: Rf = 4.68 / 7.20 = 0.65
```

The Rf values are **0.25** and **0.65**. The second component is less strongly retained (or more soluble in
the solvent) than the first.

### Worked example 3: identifying substances with standards

A crushed analgesic tablet is dissolved and spotted on a TLC plate. Beside it, on the same baseline, are three
pure standards, A, B and C. The solvent front ends 6.40 cm above the baseline.

| Spot | Distance from baseline / cm | Rf |
|---|---|---|
| Standard A | 1.92 | 0.30 |
| Standard B | 3.52 | 0.55 |
| Standard C | 4.80 | 0.75 |
| Tablet, spot 1 | 1.92 | 0.30 |
| Tablet, spot 2 | 4.80 | 0.75 |

1. Divide each distance by 6.40 cm (shown in the table).
2. Tablet spot 1 matches A; tablet spot 2 matches C; nothing matches B.
3. Conclusion: the tablet **contains A and C**, and no B was detected.

Standards go on the same plate because Rf values change with the solvent, the stationary phase and the
conditions; side by side, sample and standard get identical treatment. A match is good evidence, not proof:
two substances can share an Rf value in one solvent. Repeating in a different solvent is a further test.

## Column chromatography (CC)

A vertical column is packed with a solid, the stationary phase. The mixture is added at the top and solvent is
run down through the column. Weakly retained components that are very soluble in the solvent move down
fastest and leave the bottom first; strongly retained ones follow later. Because the components leave one
after another, you can collect them in separate containers (fractions) and obtain each one separately.

For J and K from worked example 1, on a column of the same polar solid with the same solvent, **K leaves first**.
The reasoning is the same as for TLC; only the direction of solvent flow differs.

## Gas chromatography (GC)

The column is packed with a solid, or with a solid coated by a liquid. A gas, the moving phase, is passed
through under pressure at high temperature. The sample is injected, vaporises and is carried through. A
detector at the end records each component as it emerges, giving a peak.

The **retention time** is the time from injection until a component reaches the detector. Strongly retained
components have long retention times. Retention times depend on the column, its temperature and the gas
flow, so you compare them with standards run under the same conditions. Raising the column temperature makes
components spend more time in the gas, so retention times get shorter.

### Worked example 4: identifying components by retention time

Standards run on the same column, temperature and gas flow give retention times of 3.2 min (D), 4.5 min (E)
and 7.8 min (F). A sample gives peaks at 3.2 min and 7.8 min only.

The peaks match D and F, so the sample **contains D and F**; no E is detected. Two substances can share a
retention time on one column, so a match is strong evidence, not certainty. That is one reason GC is combined
with mass spectrometry.

## Mass spectrometry of the components separated by GC

In GC-MS, the gas leaving the column passes into a mass spectrometer. GC has already separated the mixture,
so the components arrive one at a time and each gives its own mass spectrum. The molecular ion gives the
relative molecular mass, and a high-resolution m/z value fixes the molecular formula (section 3.3.6.2).

How a mass spectrometer works is in the [atomic structure guide](/resources/a-level-aqa-chemistry-atomic-structure/);
precise-mass calculations are taught in the [organic analysis guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-organic-analysis/).

### Worked example 5: GC-MS

One GC peak gives a molecular ion at m/z = 100.0635. Which of C₆H₁₂O, C₅H₈O₂, C₄H₈N₂O and C₅H₁₂N₂ is it?
Use H = 1.0078, C = 12.0000, N = 14.0031 and O = 15.9949.

```
C6H12O  : 6(12.0000) + 12(1.0078) + 15.9949             = 100.0885
C5H8O2  : 5(12.0000) + 8(1.0078) + 2(15.9949)           = 100.0522
C4H8N2O : 4(12.0000) + 8(1.0078) + 2(14.0031) + 15.9949 = 100.0635
C5H12N2 : 5(12.0000) + 12(1.0078) + 2(14.0031)          = 100.0998
```

Only **C₄H₈N₂O** matches. GC separated this component; mass spectrometry identified its formula.

## Required practical 12: separation of species by TLC

The specification lists AT a (recording measurements), AT i (thin-layer or paper chromatography) and AT k
(safe handling of chemicals). A typical method:

1. Wear gloves and handle the plate by its edges.
2. Draw a **pencil** baseline about 1 cm from the bottom. Pencil does not dissolve in the solvent; ink would
   dissolve and separate.
3. With a fine capillary tube, put small, concentrated spots of each sample and standard on the baseline,
   spaced apart. Let them dry.
4. Add solvent to a depth **below the baseline**, so the samples do not dissolve into the solvent pool.
5. Stand the plate in the container and add a lid, which keeps the air saturated with solvent vapour so
   solvent does not evaporate from the plate.
6. Near the top, remove the plate and mark the solvent front in pencil straight away.
7. Dry the plate, then locate colourless spots (for example under ultraviolet light).
8. Measure from the baseline to each spot centre and to the solvent front. Calculate Rf values and compare
   with the standards.

Safety (AT k): check the hazards of the solvent and any developing agent first. Organic solvents are often
flammable, so keep them away from flames, and use a fume cupboard if vapours are harmful. Do not look
directly at an ultraviolet lamp.

## Common errors

- Measuring from the bottom edge of the plate, or to the top of a spot, instead of baseline to spot centre.
- Writing Rf with units, or getting a value above 1 by dividing the wrong way round.
- Comparing an Rf value with one from a different solvent or plate.
- Explaining separation with one phase only, or by "how heavy" a substance is.
- Stating that GC uses a liquid moving phase.
- Saying a single matching Rf value or retention time "proves" identity.
