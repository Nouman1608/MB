These notes condense section **3.3.2.2 Modification of alkanes by cracking** of the AQA AS and A-level Chemistry specification (7404/7405), for teaching from September 2015. It is AS content, examined on Paper 2. Explanations and worked examples are in the [Modification of alkanes by cracking study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-modification-of-alkanes-by-cracking/), and exam-style questions are in the [Modification of alkanes by cracking practice questions](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-modification-of-alkanes-by-cracking-practice/). The wider topic is in the [Alkanes revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-alkanes-revision-notes/).

## What you must be able to do

- State that **cracking breaks C–C bonds** in alkanes.
- Give the **conditions and products** of thermal and catalytic cracking (mechanism not required).
- Explain the **economic reasons** for cracking.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Cracking | Breaking C–C bonds in long-chain alkanes to give shorter alkanes and alkenes |
| Thermal cracking | High pressure and high temperature, no catalyst |
| Catalytic cracking | Slight pressure, high temperature, zeolite catalyst |
| Zeolite | Crystalline aluminosilicate catalyst with a large surface area |
| Feedstock | Raw material for the chemical industry |
| Aromatic hydrocarbon | Hydrocarbon containing a benzene ring |

## Conditions and products

| | Thermal cracking | Catalytic cracking |
|---|---|---|
| Pressure | High (about 7000 kPa) | Slight |
| Temperature | High (about 700 to 1200 K) | High (about 720 K) |
| Catalyst | None | Zeolite |
| Main products | High percentage of alkenes | Motor fuels (branched, cyclic alkanes) and aromatic hydrocarbons |
| Energy use | Higher | Lower |

## Writing and checking equations

```
C₁₀H₂₂ → C₈H₁₈ + C₂H₄
C₁₆H₃₄ → C₈H₁₈ + 2C₄H₈
C₁₄H₃₀ → C₁₀H₂₂ + 2C₂H₄
```

Rules:

1. Carbon atoms must balance.
2. Hydrogen atoms must balance.
3. At least one product is an alkene (CₙH₂ₙ).
4. The other product(s) are usually shorter alkanes (CₙH₂ₙ₊₂).

Finding a missing product: subtract the carbon and hydrogen of the known products. Dodecane C₁₂H₂₆ giving C₄H₁₀ leaves C₈H₁₆.

## Why alkenes must form

The starting alkane is saturated. When it splits, there is not enough hydrogen to make every product saturated, so at least one product has a C=C double bond.

## Economic reasons for cracking

1. **Demand.** Crude oil has more long-chain hydrocarbons than the market needs. There is greater demand for petrol and other short-chain fuels.
2. **Value.** Cracking changes less useful, surplus fractions into more valuable molecules.
3. **Alkenes.** The alkenes are feedstock for polymers and other chemicals.
4. **Fuel quality.** Catalytic cracking gives branched and aromatic hydrocarbons that make better motor fuels.
5. **Lower costs.** Catalytic cracking uses lower temperature and pressure, so energy and plant costs are lower.

## Worked examples

**Mass and volume.** 100 g of decane, C₁₀H₂₂ (Mᵣ 142.0), is cracked: C₁₀H₂₂ → C₈H₁₈ + C₂H₄.

```
n(C₁₀H₂₂) = 100 ÷ 142.0 = 0.704 mol
n(C₂H₄) = 0.704 mol
mass = 0.704 × 28.0 = 19.7 g
volume = 0.704 × 24.0 = 16.9 dm³
```

**Atom economy for ethene.** In C₁₀H₂₂ → C₈H₁₈ + C₂H₄: 28.0 ÷ 142.0 × 100 = 19.7%. In C₁₄H₃₀ → C₁₀H₂₂ + 2C₂H₄: 56.0 ÷ 198.0 × 100 = 28.3%.

## Comparing with fractional distillation

| Feature | Fractional distillation | Cracking |
|---|---|---|
| Type of change | Physical | Chemical |
| Bonds broken | None (London forces overcome) | C–C bonds |
| Separates by | Boiling point | Not a separation: makes new molecules |
| Purpose | Separate crude oil into fractions | Convert long chains into shorter, more useful ones |

## What happens to the products

- Alkenes (ethene, propene) go on to make polymers and many chemicals.
- Branched and aromatic hydrocarbons are blended into petrol.
- The mixture is separated by fractional distillation.

## Describing the two processes in an answer

**Thermal:** long-chain alkanes are heated to a high temperature under high pressure. C–C bonds break and a mixture rich in alkenes forms.

**Catalytic:** the vapour of long-chain alkanes passes over a hot zeolite catalyst at a slight pressure. The products are branched and cyclic alkanes and aromatic hydrocarbons for motor fuels. The catalyst is regenerated by burning off carbon deposits.

## Quick calculation routine

1. Balance the cracking equation.
2. Convert the mass of the reactant to moles (mass ÷ Mᵣ).
3. Use the equation ratio to find moles of the product.
4. Convert to mass (moles × Mᵣ) or volume (moles × 24.0 dm³ mol⁻¹ at room temperature and pressure).

## Products and their uses

| Product | Source | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Ethene | Thermal cracking | Poly(ethene), ethanol, other chemicals |
| Propene | Thermal cracking | Poly(propene) |
| Branched alkanes | Catalytic cracking | Components of petrol |
| Cycloalkanes | Catalytic cracking | Components of petrol |
| Aromatic hydrocarbons | Catalytic cracking | Petrol and chemical feedstock |
| Short-chain alkanes | Both | Fuels, feedstock |

## Exam traps

- Cracking given as a physical separation.
- Unbalanced equations.
- No alkene in the products.
- High pressure for catalytic cracking.
- Catalyst not named as a zeolite.
- Reasons given as "to make more crude oil" instead of supply and demand.
- Cracking said to make longer chains.
- Mechanism written out in full (not required).

## Self-test

1. What bond breaks in cracking?
2. State the conditions for thermal cracking.
3. State the catalyst for catalytic cracking.
4. What is the main product of thermal cracking?
5. What are the main products of catalytic cracking?
6. Why is at least one alkene always formed?
7. Complete: C₁₂H₂₆ → C₈H₁₈ + ?
8. Give one economic reason for cracking.
9. Why is catalytic cracking cheaper to run?
10. What is an alkene used for after cracking?
11. What is a zeolite?
12. How does cracking differ from fractional distillation?

### Answers

1. C–C bonds.
2. High pressure and high temperature.
3. A zeolite.
4. Alkenes.
5. Motor fuels (branched and cyclic alkanes) and aromatic hydrocarbons.
6. The starting molecule is saturated, so splitting it leaves too little hydrogen for all products to be saturated.
7. C₄H₈.
8. Supply of long-chain fractions exceeds demand; demand for short-chain fuels is higher.
9. It uses a lower temperature and lower pressure.
10. Feedstock for polymers and other chemicals.
11. A crystalline aluminosilicate catalyst with a large surface area.
12. Cracking is a chemical change that breaks C–C bonds; fractional distillation is a physical separation.
