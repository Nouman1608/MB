This guide covers subtopic **19.4 Effects of humans on ecosystems** of Cambridge O Level Biology 5090, 2026-2028 series. The syllabus asks you to outline the causes and describe the consequences of deforestation (on biodiversity, extinction, soil, flooding and atmospheric carbon dioxide); to describe the impacts of over-harvesting and of introducing non-native species; and to describe the harmful effects of water pollution by untreated sewage and nitrogen-containing fertilisers leading to eutrophication, air pollution by the greenhouse gases carbon dioxide and methane contributing to global warming, pollution by insecticides and herbicides, and non-biodegradable plastics.

The [Relationships of organisms topic guide](/resources/o-level-biology-5090-relationships-of-organisms-with-one-another-and-with-the-env/) covers the whole topic. This page goes deeper on each effect, with the chains of cause and effect in order. Use the [19.4 practice questions](/resources/cambridge-o-level-biology-5090-effects-of-humans-on-ecosystems-practice/) to test yourself.

## Deforestation

**Deforestation** is the permanent removal of forests, usually by cutting or burning.

### Causes

- Clearing land for **agriculture**, for crops and for cattle.
- **Timber** for building, furniture and fuel.
- Land for **housing** and **roads** as the human population grows.
- Mining and the building of dams.

### Consequences

| Effect | Explanation |
|---|---|
| Loss of biodiversity | Habitats are destroyed, so the many species that live there lose their homes and food, and numbers fall |
| Extinction | Species that live only in one forest can disappear for ever when it is cleared |
| Loss of soil | Tree roots hold soil together; without them, rain washes the topsoil away (soil erosion), which makes the land less fertile |
| Flooding | Trees take up water and slow its flow; without them, rainwater runs quickly into rivers, which flood |
| Higher carbon dioxide | Fewer trees means less photosynthesis, so less carbon dioxide is removed from the air; burning the trees releases carbon dioxide; decomposers also respire and release carbon dioxide |

### Worked example 1: forest loss

*A forest of 800 km2 loses 6 per cent of its area each year because of clearance. What area is lost in one year, and how much remains after the first year?*

1. Area lost = 6 divided by 100 x 800 = 48 km2.
2. Area remaining = 800 - 48 = 752 km2.
3. After the second year, 6 per cent of 752 is 45.12 km2, leaving 706.88 km2.

## Over-harvesting

**Over-harvesting** is taking plants or animals from the wild faster than they can reproduce. Examples are overfishing, hunting and the collection of wild plants.

- The population of the species falls, perhaps so far that it cannot recover and it becomes extinct.
- The loss affects the food chain: predators lose food and the prey of the over-harvested species may increase.
- It reduces biodiversity.

## Introducing non-native species

A **non-native species** (introduced or alien species) is a species brought to an area where it did not live before. It may have no natural predators or diseases there, so its population can grow quickly. The effects are:

- it **competes** with native species for food, water, light and space;
- it may **eat** native species (predation) or spread **disease**;
- native species may decline or become extinct, so biodiversity falls.

Example: a plant or animal brought to an island with no natural enemies can spread across it and push out the local species.

## Water pollution: eutrophication

Untreated **sewage** and **nitrogen-containing fertilisers** that run off fields into rivers and lakes can cause **eutrophication**. The syllabus gives the sequence. Learn it in order.

1. **Increased availability of nitrate and other ions** in the water, from fertiliser run-off and sewage.
2. **Increased growth of producers.** Algae and plants grow rapidly (an algal bloom), which covers the surface and blocks light to plants below.
3. Plants below the surface do not get enough light, so they **die**; many algae also die as competition and nutrients run out. **Increased decomposition** after the death of producers.
4. **Increased aerobic respiration by decomposers**, which feed on the large amount of dead material and use up oxygen.
5. **Reduction in dissolved oxygen** in the water.
6. **Death of organisms that need dissolved oxygen**, such as fish and invertebrates.

### Worked example 2: explaining a fish kill

*After heavy rain, a river next to farmland had a green layer of algae. Two weeks later, many fish were found dead. Explain.*

- Fertiliser from the fields was washed into the river, increasing the concentration of nitrate ions.
- The algae grew rapidly because of the increased nitrate.
- The algae died, and decomposers fed on the dead algae.
- The decomposers carried out aerobic respiration in large numbers, using up the dissolved oxygen.
- With less dissolved oxygen, fish could not respire and died.

Note that it is the **bacteria** respiring, not the algae dying, that removes the oxygen.

## Air pollution: greenhouse gases and global warming

**Carbon dioxide** and **methane** are **greenhouse gases**. They trap some of the heat energy that would otherwise escape from the Earth into space, so the atmosphere warms. This is the enhanced greenhouse effect.

| Gas | Main human sources |
|---|---|
| Carbon dioxide | Burning fossil fuels, deforestation |
| Methane | Cattle and rice paddies, landfill sites, leaks from gas production |

The result is **global warming**, an increase in the mean temperature of the Earth. The likely effects are:

- melting of polar ice and glaciers, and a rise in sea level, causing coastal flooding;
- changes in weather patterns, with more extreme events such as droughts, floods and storms;
- changes to habitats, so some species may not survive or may have to move;
- shifts in the areas in which crops can be grown.

### Worked example 3: a rise in carbon dioxide

*The concentration of carbon dioxide in the air was 315 parts per million in 1960 and 420 parts per million in 2023. Calculate the percentage increase.*

1. Increase = 420 - 315 = 105 parts per million.
2. Percentage = 105 divided by 315 x 100 = 33.3 per cent (to one decimal place).

## Pollution by insecticides and herbicides

**Insecticides** kill insects and **herbicides** kill weeds. They protect crops, but they can have harmful effects.

- They can kill **non-target** organisms, such as bees and other pollinators, and so reduce biodiversity.
- They can wash into rivers and lakes and harm water life.
- Some chemicals are not broken down and **accumulate** in food chains. The concentration in the animals at the top, such as birds of prey, can become high enough to harm their health and breeding.
- Pests can become resistant to insecticides by natural selection, so stronger chemicals may be needed.

## Non-biodegradable plastics

Plastics are **non-biodegradable**: decomposers cannot break them down, so they stay in the environment for hundreds of years.

- In **aquatic** ecosystems, animals can become entangled in plastic or swallow it, which can block their digestive systems and cause starvation. Plastic breaks into very small pieces that enter food chains.
- In **terrestrial** ecosystems, plastic waste builds up in landfill sites and on land, may release harmful chemicals, and can harm animals that eat it.

## Summary table of effects

| Human activity | Main effect on ecosystems |
|---|---|
| Deforestation | Loss of habitat, biodiversity and soil; flooding; more carbon dioxide in the air |
| Over-harvesting | Population of the species falls; risk of extinction |
| Introducing a non-native species | Competition, predation or disease harm native species |
| Sewage and fertiliser in water | Eutrophication; fish and other organisms die |
| Burning fossil fuels; cattle; landfill | Greenhouse gases; global warming |
| Insecticides and herbicides | Non-target species harmed; chemicals pass along food chains |
| Plastics | Persist for centuries; entangle and are swallowed by animals |

## Ways in which the effects can be reduced

Although the syllabus asks you for the harmful effects, it helps to know the links to the conservation subtopic. Eutrophication can be reduced by treating sewage before it is released and by using fertiliser carefully so that less runs off into water. Deforestation can be reduced by protecting forests and replanting trees. Greenhouse gas emissions can be reduced by burning less fossil fuel. Over-harvesting can be limited by quotas. Pollution by plastics can be reduced by recycling and by reducing the use of single-use plastics.

### Worked example 4: choosing the best explanation

*Which statement best explains why fish die in a eutrophic lake?*

- "The fertiliser poisons the fish." **Weak**: the main cause is a lack of oxygen, not poisoning.
- "The algae use up all the oxygen." **Incomplete**: algae produce oxygen in the light.
- "Dead algae are decomposed by bacteria, which use up the dissolved oxygen in aerobic respiration, so the fish cannot get enough oxygen." **Best**: it names the decomposers, the respiration and the loss of oxygen.

Learn to write the full chain, rather than short statements, as the marks are given for each link.

## Common mistakes

- Writing that trees "make" oxygen as the only effect of deforestation. Give the consequences named in the syllabus.
- Saying that algae use up the oxygen in eutrophication. Decomposers do, through aerobic respiration.
- Mixing up the order of the eutrophication stages.
- Writing that methane and carbon dioxide cause a hole in the ozone layer. They cause global warming.
- Saying that global warming is caused by the Sun getting hotter.
- Writing that plastic "rots" slowly. It is non-biodegradable.
- Giving only one effect when the question asks for the "consequences".

## Command words

- **Outline**: give the main causes briefly.
- **Describe**: give the effects, in order where appropriate.
- **Explain**: give the cause-and-effect chain for eutrophication or global warming.
