## Specification in one line each

- Halogenoalkanes contain **polar bonds**: Cδ+–Xδ−.
- They undergo substitution with the nucleophiles **OH⁻, CN⁻ and NH₃**.
- Outline each **mechanism** with curly arrows.
- Explain why **C–X bond enthalpy** controls the rate.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Halogenoalkane | Alkane with one or more H atoms replaced by F, Cl, Br or I |
| Nucleophile | Electron pair donor that attacks an electron-deficient atom |
| Nucleophilic substitution | Nucleophile replaces the halogen, which leaves as X⁻ |
| Leaving group | The halide ion that departs with the C–X bonding pair |
| Nitrile | Compound with –C≡N; named with the suffix -nitrile, counting the C of C≡N as carbon 1 |
| Primary amine | R–NH₂, made from a halogenoalkane and excess ammonia |
| Bond enthalpy | Energy needed to break one mole of a given covalent bond in the gas phase (mean values used for C–X) |
| Activation energy | Minimum energy a collision needs for reaction to occur |

## Recall: the three reactions

| Reagent and conditions | Attacking atom | Equation (1-bromopropane shown) | Product named |
|---|---|---|---|
| Aqueous NaOH/KOH, warm under reflux | O of OH⁻ | CH₃CH₂CH₂Br + OH⁻ → CH₃CH₂CH₂OH + Br⁻ | propan-1-ol |
| KCN in ethanol/water, reflux | C of CN⁻ | CH₃CH₂CH₂Br + CN⁻ → CH₃CH₂CH₂CN + Br⁻ | butanenitrile |
| Excess NH₃ in ethanol, heat in sealed tube | N of NH₃ | CH₃CH₂CH₂Br + 2NH₃ → CH₃CH₂CH₂NH₂ + NH₄Br | propylamine |

### Arrows to draw

- **OH⁻ or CN⁻ (one step, two arrows):** lone pair on the attacking atom → δ+ C; C–X bond → X.
- **NH₃ (two stages, four arrows):**
  - Stage 1: N lone pair → δ+ C; C–X bond → X. Gives R–NH₃⁺.
  - Stage 2: lone pair of a second NH₃ → H on N⁺; N–H bond → N. Gives R–NH₂ and NH₄⁺.

### Naming after cyanide substitution

The chain gains one carbon. Count from the nitrile carbon.

- Bromoethane → propanenitrile, CH₃CH₂CN.
- 2-Chloropropane → 2-methylpropanenitrile, (CH₃)₂CHCN.
- 1-Iodo-3-methylbutane → 4-methylpentanenitrile, (CH₃)₂CHCH₂CH₂CN.

## Recall: bond enthalpy and rate

| C–X | Bond enthalpy (one data set) / kJ mol⁻¹ | Rate of substitution |
|---|---|---|
| C–F | 467 | slowest |
| C–Cl | 346 | slow |
| C–Br | 290 | faster |
| C–I | 228 | fastest |

Chain of reasoning for full marks:

1. Down the group, the halogen atom is larger and the C–X bond longer.
2. The bond enthalpy decreases.
3. Less energy is needed to break C–X, so the activation energy is lower.
4. More collisions have at least the activation energy, so the rate is higher.
5. Polarity falls down the group, so polarity would predict the opposite order: it is not the controlling factor.

**Comparison experiment (synoptic):** warm each halogenoalkane with aqueous ethanolic silver nitrate. The order of precipitates appearing is AgI (yellow) first, then AgBr (cream), then AgCl (white).

## Exam traps

- Cyanide attacks through **carbon**, not nitrogen.
- The arrow to the halogen starts **on the C–X bond**.
- Show the **lone pair** on the nucleophile and the **dipole** on C–X.
- Ammonia needs **excess** (so the amine product is less likely to react further) and **two** NH₃ per halogenoalkane.
- Positive charge in the ammonia intermediate sits on **N**.
- "C–I is more polar" is false; C–I is the least polar C–X bond.
- Write "aqueous" for OH⁻ substitution; ethanolic hydroxide shifts the balance towards elimination (3.3.3.2).
- Nitrile names: 2-bromobutane gives 2-methylbutanenitrile, not pentanenitrile.

## Self-test

1. Why is the carbon of a C–Br bond attacked by nucleophiles?
2. Which atom of the cyanide ion forms the new bond to carbon?
3. Give the reagent and conditions for converting 1-iodobutane into butan-1-ol.
4. Name the organic product when 1-chloro-2-methylpropane reacts with KCN.
5. Write the overall equation for bromoethane reacting with excess ammonia.
6. Why is the ammonia used in excess?
7. Describe the two arrows in stage 2 of the ammonia mechanism.
8. Put the C–F, C–Cl, C–Br and C–I bonds in order of increasing rate of substitution.
9. Explain why chloroethane is hydrolysed more slowly than iodoethane.
10. Why does bond polarity not explain the reactivity order?
11. 1-Chlorobutane (Mr 92.5, 4.625 g) is hydrolysed and gives 2.96 g of butan-1-ol (Mr 74.0). Calculate the percentage yield.
12. Which halogenoalkane gives a yellow precipitate first with aqueous ethanolic silver nitrate: 1-bromobutane or 1-iodobutane?

### Answers

1. Bromine is more electronegative than carbon, so the carbon is **δ+ (electron deficient)** and attracts lone pairs.
2. The **carbon** atom (it carries the lone pair used).
3. **Aqueous NaOH (or KOH)**, warm under **reflux**.
4. **3-Methylbutanenitrile**, (CH₃)₂CHCH₂CN.
5. **CH₃CH₂Br + 2NH₃ → CH₃CH₂NH₂ + NH₄Br**
6. The amine product has a lone pair and could attack more halogenoalkane; **excess ammonia** makes reaction with NH₃ much more likely.
7. Arrow from the **lone pair of a second NH₃ to an H on N⁺**; arrow from that **N–H bond onto the nitrogen**.
8. **C–F < C–Cl < C–Br < C–I** (slowest to fastest).
9. The C–Cl bond enthalpy is **higher** than C–I, so more energy is needed to break it, the **activation energy is higher** and fewer collisions succeed.
10. Polarity decreases from C–F to C–I, so it predicts fluoroalkanes would react fastest; the **observed order is the reverse**.
11. n(1-chlorobutane) = 0.0500 mol; n(butan-1-ol) = 0.0400 mol; yield = **80.0 %**.
12. **1-Iodobutane** (silver iodide is yellow and the C–I bond breaks fastest).
