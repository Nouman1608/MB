## 2.1 Motivating employees

| Theorist | Core idea |
|---|---|
| Maslow | Hierarchy of human needs |
| Taylor | Money is the primary motivator; workers driven by financial reward |
| Herzberg | Motivators (job satisfaction) vs hygiene factors (dissatisfaction avoidance) |

Keep two separate lists, since scenario questions often ask for one
of each:

- **Financial**: wage, salary, bonus, commission, profit sharing.
- **Non-financial**: job enrichment, job rotation, teamworking,
training, promotion opportunities.

Benefits of a motivated workforce: higher **labour productivity**,
lower **absenteeism**, lower **labour turnover**.

## 2.2 Organisation and management

Read and draw organisational charts: **levels of hierarchy**, **span
of control** (how many report to one manager), **chain of command**
(the reporting line). Tall structures = many levels, narrow spans;
short/flat structures = few levels, wide spans.

**Management functions**: planning, organising, coordinating,
commanding, controlling. **Delegation** = passing authority down while
balancing trust and control.

| Leadership style | Feature |
|---|---|
| Autocratic | Manager makes decisions alone |
| Democratic | Employees consulted, input valued |
| Laissez-faire | Employees given freedom to decide |

A **trade union** represents employee interests; membership affects
both the employee (representation, collective bargaining) and the
employer (negotiating with a collective body rather than individuals).

## 2.3 Recruitment, selection and training

- **Internal vs external recruitment** — internal is cheaper/faster
and motivates existing staff; external brings fresh ideas/skills.
- Main stages: identify vacancy → job description/person spec →
advertise → shortlist → interview/select.
- **Part-time vs full-time**: weigh flexibility/cost against
availability/commitment.
- **Training types**: induction (settling in), on-the-job (learning
while working), off-the-job (external courses) — know a benefit and
limitation of each.
- **Dismissal vs redundancy**: dismissal is for the employee's own
conduct/performance; redundancy is when the *role* is no longer
needed (automation, falling demand) — not the person's fault.
- **Legal controls**: contracts, unfair dismissal, discrimination,
health and safety, minimum wage.

## 2.4 Internal and external communication

Communication methods have benefits/limitations, including
IT-based methods (email, video call) — always pair a benefit with a
limitation rather than listing in isolation. Know common **barriers**
to communication (noise, jargon, poor channel choice, hierarchy
length) and how a business can reduce them (training, choosing an
appropriate method for the message, shortening reporting lines).

## The exam's favourite instruction: "recommend and justify"

This phrasing recurs across motivation methods, leadership style, who
to employ, redundancy decisions, and communication method. It signals
Cambridge wants you to **apply** a concept to the scenario's specific
details (company size, product, workforce, budget) — not recite a
generic definition that could apply to any business. Always anchor
your answer in a detail actually given in the question.

## Worked example: recommend and justify a leadership style

A scenario describes a small design studio where staff are highly
skilled, creative, and expect independence in how they complete
projects.

```
Style recommended: Democratic, leaning toward laissez-faire
Justification:      Skilled, creative staff generally respond better
                     to consultation and autonomy than to close
                     direction; an autocratic style risks reducing
                     motivation and creative input from employees
                     whose expertise the business specifically needs.
Caveat to mention:   Full laissez-faire risks a lack of coordination
                     on shared deadlines, so some democratic
                     structure (regular check-ins, shared decisions
                     on direction) is usually the safer recommendation
                     than pure laissez-faire.
```

Notice the structure: state the recommendation, justify it using a
detail from the scenario (skilled, creative, expects independence),
then acknowledge a limitation. This three-part shape — recommend,
justify with scenario detail, acknowledge a limitation — is the
reliable way to score full marks on "recommend and justify" questions
across this entire topic, not just leadership style.

## Exam traps

- Naming Maslow, Taylor or Herzberg without being able to use the
theory to explain *why* a specific motivation method would or
wouldn't suit a given workforce.
- Listing training types or recruitment options without pairing each
with a benefit *and* a limitation.
- Confusing dismissal (employee's fault) with redundancy (role no
longer needed).
- Answering "recommend and justify" questions with generic, scenario-free
reasoning.
- Treating Topic 2 in isolation from Topic 1 — a question on span of
control or delegation may assume you already know the business's
ownership structure (sole trader, partnership, limited company) from
Topic 1.

## Self-test

1. Name the three motivation theorists specified and their core idea.
2. What is the difference between dismissal and redundancy?
3. List the three leadership styles and one feature of each.
4. What does "span of control" mean, and how does it differ between
tall and short organisational structures?
5. Why does "recommend and justify" wording require more than a
textbook definition?

**Answers:** 1. Maslow (hierarchy of needs), Taylor (money as primary
motivator), Herzberg (motivators vs hygiene factors). 2. Dismissal
relates to the employee's own conduct or performance; redundancy
occurs when the role itself is no longer needed, regardless of the
employee's performance. 3. Autocratic (manager decides alone),
democratic (employees consulted), laissez-faire (employees given
freedom to decide). 4. The number of people reporting directly to one
manager; tall structures have narrow spans of control across many
hierarchy levels, short/flat structures have wide spans across fewer
levels. 5. Because it specifically tests applying a general concept to
the scenario's own details (company size, product, workforce, budget)
rather than reciting a definition that could apply to any business.
