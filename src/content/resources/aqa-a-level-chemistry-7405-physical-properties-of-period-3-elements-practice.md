## Questions

**1.** Describe the trend in atomic radius from sodium to chlorine and explain it. **[3]**

**2.** Write an equation, with state symbols, to represent the first ionisation energy of magnesium. **[1]**

**3.** Explain why the first ionisation energy of aluminium is lower than that of magnesium. **[2]**

**4.** Explain why the first ionisation energy of sulfur is lower than that of phosphorus. **[2]**

**5.** Explain the general increase in first ionisation energy from sodium to argon. **[3]**

**6.** Explain why magnesium has a higher melting point than sodium. **[3]**

**7.** Explain why silicon has a very high melting point. **[2]**

**8.** Phosphorus exists as P₄ molecules and sulfur as S₈ molecules. Explain why sulfur has a higher melting point than phosphorus. **[2]**

**9.** Explain why argon has a lower melting point than chlorine. **[2]**

**10.** Three consecutive elements in Period 3 have first ionisation energies 738, 578 and 789 kJ mol⁻¹.

**(a)** Identify the three elements. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain why the middle element has the lowest value. **[2]**

**11.** A student writes: "When phosphorus melts, the P–P covalent bonds break, so it has a low melting point because the bonds are weak." Identify the error and give the correct explanation. **[3]**

**12.** A student says: "The first ionisation energy of aluminium is lower than that of magnesium because aluminium has an extra shell." Identify the error. **[2]**

**13.** Describe the shape of a graph of melting point against atomic number from sodium to argon and explain the sharp fall between silicon and phosphorus. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** Atomic radius decreases from sodium to chlorine [1]; the nuclear charge increases (more protons) while the electrons are added to the same shell, with similar shielding [1]; so the outer electrons are pulled closer to the nucleus [1]. **[3]**

**2.** Mg(g) → Mg⁺(g) + e⁻ [1]. **[1]**

**3.** The outer electron of aluminium is in a 3p sub-level, which is higher in energy than the 3s sub-level of magnesium [1]; it is also shielded slightly by the 3s electrons, so less energy is needed to remove it [1]. **[2]**

**4.** In sulfur two electrons share one 3p orbital (3p⁴), whereas in phosphorus the three 3p electrons are in separate orbitals [1]; repulsion between the paired electrons makes the sulfur electron easier to remove [1]. **[2]**

**5.** Nuclear charge increases across the period [1]; the electron is removed from the same shell, with similar shielding, and the atomic radius decreases [1]; so the attraction between the nucleus and the outer electron increases and more energy is needed [1]. **[3]**

**6.** Both have giant metallic lattices of positive ions and delocalised electrons [1]; magnesium ions have a 2+ charge, are smaller, and each atom provides two delocalised electrons, so the attraction between ions and electrons is stronger [1]; more energy is needed to overcome it, so the melting point is higher [1]. **[3]**

**7.** Silicon has a giant covalent (macromolecular) structure [1]; many strong covalent bonds must be broken, which needs a lot of energy [1]. **[2]**

**8.** S₈ molecules are larger and have more electrons than P₄ molecules, so the London forces between them are stronger [1]; more energy is needed to overcome them [1]. **[2]**

**9.** Argon exists as single atoms and chlorine as Cl₂ molecules [1]; argon atoms have fewer electrons than Cl₂ molecules, so the London forces are weaker and less energy is needed to melt it [1]. **[2]**

**10. (a)** Magnesium, aluminium, silicon [1]

**(b)** The outer electron of aluminium is in a 3p sub-level, which has higher energy than the 3s sub-level of magnesium [1], so it is easier to remove despite the greater nuclear charge [1]. **[3]**

**11.** The covalent bonds within P₄ molecules do not break when phosphorus melts [1]; the forces that must be overcome are the weak London forces between the molecules [1]; weak forces need little energy, so the melting point is low [1]. **[3]**

**12.** Aluminium does not have an extra shell; both aluminium and magnesium have their outer electrons in the third shell [1]; the difference is that the aluminium electron is in a 3p sub-level, which has higher energy than 3s [1]. **[2]**

*Examiner insight:* "Extra shell" is a common wrong answer. Within a period, no new shell is added.

**13.** The melting point rises from sodium to a maximum at silicon [1]; it then falls sharply at phosphorus and stays low for sulfur, chlorine and argon [1]. The fall happens because the structure changes from giant covalent in silicon to simple molecular in phosphorus [1]; only weak London forces must be overcome, rather than strong covalent bonds [1]. **[4]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Saying covalent bonds break when a simple molecular element melts.
- Writing "extra shell" for the Mg to Al fall.
- Giving "half-filled stability" instead of electron pair repulsion for P to S.
- Not stating that the electrons are in the same shell when explaining atomic radius.
- Comparing metals by "bond strength" without mentioning ionic charge, size and delocalised electrons.
