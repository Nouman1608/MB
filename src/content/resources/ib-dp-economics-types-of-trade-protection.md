## The set-up that every diagram shares

All the diagrams use one market, one world price and one domestic demand and supply pair. Price is on the vertical axis and quantity on the horizontal axis.

- **D** is domestic demand and **S** is domestic supply.
- **Pw** is the world price. Under free trade, domestic buyers and sellers face Pw.
- If Pw is below the no-trade price, the country imports the gap between quantity demanded and quantity supplied.
- The diagrams assume the country is a price taker, so its own trade policy does not change Pw. This is a modelling choice, not a fact about every economy.

Surplus ideas carry over from [competitive market equilibrium](/resources/ib-dp-economics-competitive-market-equilibrium/). Welfare here means consumer surplus plus producer surplus plus government revenue.

**Fictional market for the worked examples:** shirts in Brandovia. Quantities are in thousands, and prices are in currency units.

- Demand: Qd = 180 − 3P
- Supply: Qs = 4P − 30
- No-trade equilibrium: 180 − 3P = 4P − 30, so 210 = 7P, giving P = 30 and Q = 90.
- World price: Pw = 20.

At Pw = 20, Qd = 180 − 60 = 120 and Qs = 80 − 30 = 50. Imports are 120 − 50 = **70**. Import expenditure is 20 × 70 = **1 400**.

## Tariffs

A tariff is a tax on imported goods. A **specific** tariff is a fixed amount per unit. An **ad valorem** tariff is a percentage of the price: 25% of a world price of 20 is 5 per unit. Either way, the domestic price of the imported good becomes Pw plus the tariff.

### Reading the tariff diagram

Draw a second horizontal line above Pw at Pw + t, labelled Pt. Four quantities now matter:

| Label | Meaning | Brandovia value |
|---|---|---|
| Qs under free trade | Domestic output before the tariff | 50 |
| Qs with tariff | Domestic output after the tariff | 70 |
| Qd with tariff | Domestic consumption after the tariff | 105 |
| Qd under free trade | Domestic consumption before the tariff | 120 |

Effects, in the order the guide lists them:

1. **Price.** Rises from Pw to Pt, because importers pass the tax on.
2. **Production.** Domestic output rises along S, from 50 to 70. Higher prices make more domestic supply worthwhile.
3. **Consumption.** Falls along D, from 120 to 105.
4. **Imports.** Fall from 70 to 35, so the gap between the two new quantities is the new import volume.
5. **Expenditure.** Consumers spend Pt × Qd. Spending on imports splits into payments to foreign sellers and tax paid to the government.
6. **Revenues.** Domestic producers receive Pt on every unit. The government collects t on every imported unit.
7. **Welfare.** Consumer surplus falls by a trapezoid between Pw and Pt. Producer surplus rises. The government gains revenue. Two triangles are lost to everyone.

Label the areas on your diagram:

| Area | Shape | What it is |
|---|---|---|
| a | Trapezoid beside S, between Pw and Pt | Gain in producer surplus |
| b | Triangle between S and the Pt line | Welfare loss from inefficient extra domestic production |
| c | Triangle between D and the Pt line | Welfare loss from reduced consumption |
| d | Rectangle between the new quantities | Government revenue |

The consumer surplus loss is a + b + c + d.

### Worked example (HL only): Brandovia tariff of 5

A tariff of 5 per shirt is imposed. Then Pt = 25.

1. New quantities: Qd = 180 − 75 = 105 and Qs = 100 − 30 = 70. Imports = 105 − 70 = **35**.
2. Government revenue = 5 × 35 = **175**.
3. Consumer surplus loss = ½ × (120 + 105) × 5 = **562.5**.
4. Producer surplus gain = ½ × (50 + 70) × 5 = **300**.
5. Welfare loss, production triangle = ½ × 5 × (70 − 50) = **50**.
6. Welfare loss, consumption triangle = ½ × 5 × (120 − 105) = **37.5**.
7. Check: 300 + 175 + 50 + 37.5 = 562.5, which equals the consumer loss.
8. Net welfare change = 300 + 175 − 562.5 = **−87.5**, the same as −(50 + 37.5).

Spending changes too. Consumers spend 25 × 105 = 2 625 instead of 2 400. Domestic producers' revenue rises from 1 000 to 25 × 70 = 1 750. Of the 875 spent on imports, 175 is tax and **700** goes to foreign sellers, down from 1 400.

## Quotas

An import quota is a limit on the quantity of a good that may be imported in a period. The government may allocate the right to import by licence. Because supply from abroad is capped, the domestic price rises until domestic buyers want exactly the quota quantity beyond what domestic firms supply.

### Quota diagram

Keep D, S and Pw. Draw the quota as a horizontal gap of fixed width between S and D. Slide that gap up until it fits between the two curves. The price at which it fits is the new domestic price, Pq. Mark the quota quantity as the width of that gap.

Effects on price, production and consumption match a tariff of the same size. The difference is the rectangle d. A tariff pays it to the government. Under a quota it is **quota rent**: the gap between Pq and Pw on every imported unit, collected by whoever holds the import licences.

### Worked example (HL only): Brandovia quota of 35

Limit imports to 35. Find the price where Qd − Qs = 35.

1. 180 − 3P − (4P − 30) = 35, so 210 − 7P = 35 and 7P = 175, giving **P = 25**.
2. Qd = 105 and Qs = 70, so imports are 105 − 70 = 35. The quota binds.
3. Quota rent = (25 − 20) × 35 = **175**.
4. Consumer loss, producer gain and the two triangles are the same as for the tariff: **562.5, 300, 50 and 37.5**.

The quota gives the same outcome as a tariff of 5. The only difference is who holds the 175. Domestic importers who hold the licences keep it in the country, as private income. If foreign sellers hold the licences, it leaves the country. Net welfare change for the country is then −87.5 minus 175 = **−262.5**.

A second difference: a tariff fixes the price gap and lets the quantity adjust. A quota fixes the quantity and lets the price adjust. If demand rises, imports increase under a tariff but not under a quota.

## Subsidies and export subsidies

A subsidy is a payment from the government to a producer, usually per unit. In trade, two cases matter.

### Subsidy to domestic producers (competing with imports)

The subsidy shifts S down by the amount of the subsidy, equivalent to a shift right. Consumers still buy at Pw, since imports are available at that price. Producers receive Pw plus the subsidy per unit.

Effects: consumer price unchanged, domestic production up, imports down, consumption unchanged. Government spending equals the subsidy times the new output.

**Worked example (HL only): Brandovia subsidy of 5 per shirt**

1. Producers receive 20 + 5 = 25, so Qs = 100 − 30 = **70**. Consumers still pay 20, so Qd = **120**.
2. Imports = 120 − 70 = **50**, down from 70. Import expenditure falls from 1 400 to 1 000.
3. Government cost = 5 × 70 = **350**.
4. Producer surplus gain = ½ × (50 + 70) × 5 = **300**.
5. Consumer surplus is unchanged.
6. Net welfare change = 300 − 350 = **−50**. This equals the production triangle, ½ × 5 × (70 − 50) = 50.

Output reaches the same 70 as under the tariff, but consumers are not hurt. Taxpayers pay instead.

### Export subsidy

An export subsidy is a payment on each unit sold abroad. Take the fictional grain market of Tessaly. Demand is Qd = 100 − 2P and supply is Qs = 3P − 20, in thousand tonnes. The world price is 30.

- Free trade: Qd = 100 − 60 = 40 and Qs = 90 − 20 = 70. Exports are **30**.
- With a subsidy of 5, exporters earn 35 on every unit exported, so they will not sell at home for less. The domestic price rises to **35**.
- New quantities: Qd = 100 − 70 = 30 and Qs = 105 − 20 = 85. Exports rise to 85 − 30 = **55**.

**HL only calculation:**

1. Government cost = 5 × 55 = **275**.
2. Consumer surplus loss = ½ × (40 + 30) × 5 = **175**.
3. Producer surplus gain = ½ × (70 + 85) × 5 = **387.5**.
4. Net welfare change = 387.5 − 175 − 275 = **−62.5**.
5. Check with the triangles: ½ × 5 × (85 − 70) = 37.5 and ½ × 5 × (40 − 30) = 25, which sum to 62.5.

Home consumers pay more, producers gain, and taxpayers fund the subsidy.

## Administrative barriers

The guide names **standards and regulations** here, at an evaluation depth. These are non-tax rules that make it harder or dearer to sell an imported product. Examples (all fictional in use):

- A labelling rule that requires packaging in the local language.
- A safety standard, tested in domestic laboratories.
- A licence that importers must hold.
- Slow customs paperwork or inspections at the border.

Each raises the cost of selling the import or limits how much can be sold. If the extra cost is per unit, the diagram looks like a tariff. The difference is that nobody collects revenue: the money is spent on testing, paperwork or waiting.

**Worked example: Brandovia compliance cost of 4**

Importers must pay 4 per shirt for local testing, so the consumer price becomes 24.

1. Qd = 180 − 72 = 108 and Qs = 96 − 30 = 66. Imports = **42**.
2. Compliance spending = 4 × 42 = **168**, which is a pure cost and not revenue to anyone.
3. Consumer surplus loss = ½ × (120 + 108) × 4 = 456. Producer surplus gain = ½ × (50 + 66) × 4 = 232.
4. Net welfare change = 232 − 456 = **−224**, made up of 168 of compliance cost and triangles of 32 and 24.

Evaluation matters because a standard can have two purposes. It may protect buyers from unsafe or misleading products. It may also shelter domestic firms. A good answer weighs how well the rule achieves a genuine goal against how much it restricts trade, and whether a less restrictive rule would do the job. Reasons for trade control belong to sub-topic 4.3.

## Common errors

- Shading the tariff revenue as a triangle. It is a rectangle: tariff per unit times imports after the tariff.
- Using free-trade imports to find revenue. Use the smaller import figure after the tariff.
- Forgetting that a producer subsidy leaves the consumer price unchanged in the import-competing case.
- Putting the quota rent in the government's pocket without saying who holds the licences.
- Drawing an export subsidy as a shift in demand. It is a payment to producers.
- Leaving out axes, curves, Pw or Pt.
