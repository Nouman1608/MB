These notes condense the [research methods study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-sociology-8192-research-methods/); go there for full explanations and worked paragraphs.

They cover section 3.7, Sociological research methods, of the AQA GCSE Sociology (8192) specification, Version 1.2 (June 2026), for GCSE exams 2019 onwards: research design, qualitative and quantitative methods, different types of data, primary and secondary sources, interpretation of data, practical issues and ethical issues. Methods are not a separate paper. Paper 1 (The sociology of families and education) and Paper 2 (The sociology of crime and deviance and social stratification) both assess "relevant areas of social theory and methodology", so expect methods questions set in any topic.

## What the specification asks you to do

- Identify, describe and explain methods and methodological issues.
- Explain advantages and disadvantages of a method **for a specific area of research**.
- Understand research design, including practical difficulties and ethical issues.
- Judge the relevance and usefulness of primary and secondary sources.
- Interpret data in a variety of forms.

## Key terms

| Term | One-line definition |
|---|---|
| Aim | General statement of what a study wants to find out |
| Hypothesis | Testable prediction about a relationship between two things |
| Pilot study | Small trial run to test the method before the main study |
| Population | Whole group the researcher is interested in |
| Sampling frame | List of the population from which a sample is drawn |
| Random sample | Everyone has an equal chance of selection |
| Systematic sample | Every *n*th name on the sampling frame |
| Quota sample | Set numbers from each group, chosen by the researcher, to match the population |
| Snowball sample | Participants recruit further participants |
| Representative | Sample shares the population's characteristics, so findings can be generalised |
| Closed / open question | Fixed choice of answers / free answer in the respondent's words |
| Structured / unstructured interview | Standard questions in fixed order / flexible guided conversation |
| Focus group | Group interview in which participants discuss a topic |
| Participant / non-participant observation | Researcher joins the group / watches without joining |
| Covert observation | Group does not know it is being studied |
| Ethnography | Detailed study of a group's way of life, usually by participant observation |
| Case study | In-depth study of one setting, group or event |
| Longitudinal study | Follows the same group over a period of time |
| Content analysis | Systematic study of documents or media content |
| Reliability | Repeating the method gives the same results |
| Validity | Data give a true picture of what is studied |
| Triangulation | Checking findings with more than one method or source |
| Mixed methods research | Combining quantitative and qualitative methods in one study |
| Official statistics | Numerical data produced by government and its agencies |
| Primary / secondary data | Collected first-hand by the researcher / already existing, produced by others |
| Trend | General direction of data over time |
| Informed consent | Agreeing to take part with full knowledge of what the research involves |
| Confidentiality | Keeping participants' identities and information private |

## Methods at a glance

| Method | Data type | Main strength | Main weakness | Topic where it fits well |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Questionnaire (closed questions) | Quantitative | Large samples, cheap, reliable | Low depth; response rates may be low | Attitudes to marriage across age groups |
| Structured interview | Mostly quantitative | Higher response rate than postal questionnaires | Interviewer bias; cost | Household surveys of income |
| Unstructured interview | Qualitative | Depth, rapport, valid | Hard to compare or repeat | Experiences of offenders |
| Focus group | Qualitative | Shows how views form in discussion | One person can dominate | Parents' views on school choice |
| Participant observation | Qualitative | Sees real behaviour | Time; observer effect; ethics if covert | Pupil subcultures |
| Non-participant observation | Qualitative or quantitative | Researcher stays detached | Group may act differently when watched | Classroom interaction |
| Official statistics | Quantitative, secondary | National, regular, free | Definitions set by government; unrecorded events | Exam results by gender over time |

## Studies at a glance (methods focus)

All details below are as the specification's Appendix B summarises them.

| Who | What they did | Finding (in brief) | One methods evaluation point |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ball (1981) | Three years as participant observer in a south coast comprehensive | Raised questions about selection and socialisation in banded and mixed ability cohorts | One school: rich detail, limited generalisation |
| Willis (1977) | Observation, participant observation, group discussions, informal interviews, diaries; Midlands school | Counter culture among working-class boys | Saw school from students' view; small group |
| Halsey, Heath and Ridge (1980) | Sample of over 8,000 males born 1913-1952 | Clear class inequalities in staying on and university | Excluded females |
| Carlen (1988) | Unstructured interviews with 39 women aged 15-46 | Crime more likely when the class and gender deals fail | Relatively small sample |
| Townsend (1979) | Questionnaires to over 2,000 households, more than 6,000 individuals; deprivation index | More than 22% in poverty in 1968-69 on his measure | Index items may show choice, not poverty |
| Young and Willmott (1973) | Large-scale survey: nearly 2,000 people interviewed in Greater London and surrounding areas | Symmetrical family | Feminists questioned whether "symmetry" existed |
| Devine (1992) | Interviews with Vauxhall Luton workers and their wives, late 1980s | Did not find evidence to support privatised instrumentalism | Revisiting the same site allowed comparison over time |

## Must-know distinctions

- **Reliability vs validity.** Reliable = repeatable. Valid = true to life. Questionnaires lean reliable; unstructured interviews and participant observation lean valid.
- **Aim vs hypothesis.** An aim is broad; a hypothesis predicts and can be tested.
- **Random vs representative.** Random describes how people are chosen; representative describes the result.
- **Quota vs snowball.** Quota fills set numbers for each group; snowball grows through contacts.
- **Overt vs covert.** Overt risks the observer effect; covert risks breaking informed consent.
- **Primary vs secondary.** Who collected the data, and for what purpose.
- **Official vs non-official statistics.** Who produced the figures, not whether they are accurate.
- **Mixed methods vs triangulation.** Mixed methods combines quantitative and qualitative; triangulation checks findings across methods or sources.
- **Practical vs ethical issues.** Practical = time, cost, access. Ethical = consent, confidentiality, harm.
- **Percentage vs percentage points.** 20% to 30% is a 10 percentage point rise, which is a 50% increase.

## Mini essay plan

*Typical extended question: "Discuss how far participant observation is the best method for studying processes within schools."*

1. **Define and apply.** Participant observation: the researcher joins the group. In a school this means sitting in lessons or spending time with pupils.
2. **For (validity).** Records what pupils and teachers do, not what they claim. Ball's three years in one comprehensive and Willis's observation of working-class boys in a Midlands school show banding, teacher expectations and counter culture as they happen.
3. **For (hard-to-reach views).** Pupils resistant to school may not answer a questionnaire honestly; trust built over time gets beneath the surface.
4. **Against (practical).** Time and access: the head teacher usually has to agree; an adult cannot pass as a pupil, so covert work is rarely possible.
5. **Against (reliability and representativeness).** One school or class cannot be repeated or generalised.
6. **Against (ethics).** Pupils are under 18: parental consent, confidentiality of named teachers, harm from being labelled in a report.
7. **Alternatives.** Questionnaires on setting and self-image give comparable data across many schools; official statistics show attainment by set or exclusion rates.
8. **Judgement.** Best for *why* and *how*; weaker for *how common*. A mixed methods design is likely to be most useful.

## Quick self-test

1. Define a hypothesis.
2. What is the main purpose of a pilot study?
3. A researcher picks every 20th pupil from a school list. Which sampling method is this?
4. Which sampling method would suit a study of people in an unlicensed street-racing scene, and why?
5. Give one strength of closed questions.
6. What is the difference between participant and non-participant observation?
7. Name the three practical issues listed in the specification.
8. Name the three ethical issues listed in the specification.
9. Give one way to protect confidentiality.
10. A fictional researcher posts 400 questionnaires and receives 112 back. What is the response rate?
11. Why might official statistics on a topic change even if behaviour does not?
12. Is a researcher's own interview transcript primary or secondary data?

### Answers

1. A testable statement predicting a relationship between two things.
2. To test the method on a few people and fix problems before the main study.
3. Systematic sampling.
4. Snowball sampling: there is no sampling frame, and members can introduce the researcher to others they trust.
5. Answers are easy to count and compare, so results are reliable.
6. In participant observation the researcher joins the group; in non-participant observation they watch without joining.
7. Time, cost and access.
8. Consent, confidentiality and harm to participants.
9. Anonymise names and places, or store data securely.
10. 112 / 400 x 100 = 28%.
11. A change in definitions, recording rules or reporting can change the figures.
12. Primary data.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Giving a general strength of a method without linking it to the topic in the question.
- Writing "random samples are representative" instead of "give everyone an equal chance".
- Mixing up reliability and validity, or using "valid" to mean "good".
- Calling unstructured interviews quantitative because they can be large-scale.
- Forgetting to show working in a percentage calculation, or giving no units.
- Describing the trend in a table without quoting any figures.
- Answering an ethics question with practical issues (cost, time) or the reverse.
- Saying covert observation is "unethical" and stopping; explain which ethical issue and how it might be addressed.
- Treating official statistics as either perfectly accurate or completely useless.
- Retelling a study's findings when the question asks about its method.
