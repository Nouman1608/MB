These questions cover **Information asymmetries** for the OCR *AS Level Economics (H060)* specification, for first assessment in 2016, Component 01, Microeconomics. The current specification names information provision as an AS intervention, with asymmetric information and moral hazard named at A Level, so confirm the scope with your teacher. The mark totals are our own and every market and figure is invented. Explanations are in the [information asymmetries study guide](/resources/ocr-as-level-economics-h060-information-asymmetries-ocr-aslevel-economics/) and the [information asymmetries revision notes](/resources/ocr-as-level-economics-h060-information-asymmetries-ocr-aslevel-economics-revision-notes/).

## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Define asymmetric information. **[2]**

**2.** Which of the following is an example of moral hazard?

A: A buyer cannot tell whether a used car has a fault

B: A driver takes less care after buying comprehensive insurance

C: A firm advertises a new product heavily

D: A consumer ignores a health warning on a label **[1]**

**3.** Good used bikes are worth £800 to buyers and faulty ones £400. Buyers believe 25% of bikes for sale are faulty but cannot tell which. Calculate the most an uninformed buyer would pay. **[1]**

**4.** Distinguish between information failure in general and asymmetric information. **[2]**

**5.** State two ways in which sellers or buyers can reduce an information asymmetry without government intervention. **[2]**

**6.** An insurer expects a 2% chance of a £5,000 claim on each policy. After cover is bought, the chance rises to 3%.

**(a)** Calculate the expected claim before and after. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the percentage rise in the expected claim. **[1]**

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**7.** In an invented market for a "brain-boosting" drink, buyers' perceived MPB = 60 − 2Q, but their true MPB if fully informed would be 48 − 2Q. MPC = MSC = 12 + Q, with Q in thousand cans and prices in pence.

**(a)** Calculate the market output. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the output if consumers were fully informed. **[1]**

**(c)** Calculate the welfare loss. **[2]**

**(d)** Explain why this is a market failure. **[2]**

**8.** Explain how asymmetric information can cause the market for used goods to shrink. **[4]**

**9.** Explain two examples of moral hazard and one way to reduce each. **[4]**

**10.** Explain, with the aid of a diagram, why people may underconsume regular dental check-ups. **[5]**

**11.** Evaluate the effectiveness of information provision as a way to correct information failure. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** Asymmetric information exists when one party to a transaction [1] has more or better information than the other, for example a seller who knows about hidden faults [1]. **[2]**

**2.** **B** [1]. The driver's behaviour changes after the agreement, which the insurer cannot observe. **[1]**

**3.** 0.75 × 800 + 0.25 × 400 = **£700** [1]. **[1]**

**4.** Information failure is any situation where people lack the information needed to make choices in their own interest, which may affect both sides [1]. Asymmetric information is a particular form in which one side knows more than the other [1]. **[2]**

**5.** Any two, one mark each: warranties or guarantees [1]; independent inspections, customer reviews and reputation, or insurance excesses and no-claims discounts [1]. **[2]**

**6. (a)** Before: 0.02 × 5,000 = £100; after: 0.03 × 5,000 = £150 [1].

**(b)** 50 ÷ 100 × 100 = **50%** [1]. **[2]**

**7. (a)** 60 − 2Q = 12 + Q, so Q = **16** thousand cans [1].

**(b)** 48 − 2Q = 12 + Q, so Q = **12** thousand cans [1].

**(c)** At Q = 16, true MPB = 16p and MPC = 28p, a gap of 12p [1]. Welfare loss = ½ × 4 × 12 = **24**, which is 4 thousand cans × 12p × ½ = **£240** [1].

**(d)** Consumers overestimate the benefit, so they buy 4 thousand cans more than they would if informed [1]. Each of those cans costs more to produce than its true value to the buyer, so resources are misallocated [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Read the welfare loss from the true benefit curve, not the perceived one.

**8.** Sellers know more than buyers about quality [1]. Buyers who cannot tell good from poor items offer only an average price [1]. Owners of good items value them above this price and withdraw [1]. The share of poor items rises, prices fall further, and mutually beneficial trades in good items stop, so the market shrinks [1]. **[4]**

**9.** Insurance: people who are insured may take less care of their property [1]; an excess makes them pay part of any loss, so they keep some incentive to take care [1]. Employment: workers on fixed pay may put in less effort when not observed [1]; performance-related pay or monitoring links reward to effort [1]. **[4]**

**10.** Diagram with true MPB above perceived MPB, both sloping down, and one cost curve [1]; market output where perceived MPB = MPC is less than the informed output where true MPB = MPC [1]; welfare loss shaded between the outputs, between true MPB and MPC [1]. People underestimate the benefit because problems develop slowly and are not visible early [1], so they buy fewer check-ups than they would if fully informed, and the lost check-ups were worth more than they cost [1]. **[5]**

**11.** Award up to 10 marks across knowledge, application, analysis and evaluation.
- Information provision, such as labels, campaigns and published ratings, narrows the gap between perceived and true benefit [1], moving consumption towards the informed level [1].
- It is relatively cheap compared with taxes or bans and leaves consumers free to choose [1].
- It can protect buyers in markets where sellers know more, for example required disclosure of charges on financial products [1].
- But people may ignore, distrust or misunderstand information [1], especially when habits or advertising are strong [1].
- Information can be costly to collect and the government may itself be poorly informed [1].
- It does little about moral hazard, where the problem is hidden behaviour rather than missing facts [1].
- It may need to be combined with regulation or legislation to be effective [1].
- Judgement: information provision is most effective when the information is simple, trusted and acted on quickly, and least effective for long-term or habitual choices, where stronger measures may be needed [1]. **[10]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Confusing moral hazard with hidden quality before a deal.
- Not saying who has the extra information.
- Drawing the welfare loss against the perceived benefit curve.
- Treating information provision as automatically effective.
- Leaving units off answers.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the difference between asymmetric information and moral hazard?**
Asymmetric information is a gap in knowledge between the two sides, often about quality before a deal. Moral hazard is a change in behaviour after the deal, possible because the other side cannot observe it.

**Is information provision always the best response?**
No. It is cheap and preserves choice, but people may ignore it, so serious or long-term harms may need regulation or legislation as well.
