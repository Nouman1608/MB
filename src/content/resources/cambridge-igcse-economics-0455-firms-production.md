## 3.6.1 Demand for factors of production

A firm demands land, labour, capital and enterprise only because it wants to produce something people will buy. Each influence the syllabus names can be turned into a short chain.

| Influence | Chain to learn |
|---|---|
| Demand for the product | More sales → more output needed → more of each factor demanded |
| Price of different factors | One factor dearer → firm substitutes towards the relatively cheaper factor |
| Availability | Factor scarce → hard to hire, price rises → firm uses less or uses a substitute |
| Productivity | Factor produces more per unit of cost → firm prefers it |

### Combining influences

Exam data often shows two influences at once. Work out each effect, then the overall result.

**Worked example 1 (invented).** A shoe firm employs 60 workers, each making 25 pairs a day (1,500 pairs). Demand for its shoes rises by 20%, and new equipment raises output per worker to 30 pairs.

```
Pairs needed = 1,500 x 1.20 = 1,800
Workers needed = 1,800 / 30 = 60
```

Higher demand on its own would need 1,800 / 25 = 72 workers. Higher productivity on its own would cut the need to 1,500 / 30 = 50. Together they cancel out, so the firm still employs 60. An answer that only says "demand rose, so more workers" misses the second influence.

## 3.6.2 Labour-intensive and capital-intensive production

### When does a switch become worthwhile?

A firm compares the cost per unit of each method. Because wages and machine costs change over time, the best method can change too.

**Worked example 2 (invented).** A packaging firm must pack 10,000 boxes a week.

- **Method H (by hand):** 25 workers, each paid a weekly wage W.
- **Method M (machine):** a machine leased for 6,000 a week plus 5 operators, each paid W.

```
Weekly cost of H = 25W
Weekly cost of M = 6,000 + 5W
Equal when 25W = 6,000 + 5W, so 20W = 6,000 and W = 300
```

| Weekly wage | Cost of H | Cost of M | Cheaper method |
|---|---|---|---|
| 200 | 5,000 | 7,000 | H (labour-intensive) |
| 300 | 7,500 | 7,500 | Equal |
| 400 | 10,000 | 8,000 | M (capital-intensive) |

While wages are below 300 a week, packing by hand is cheaper. As wages rise above 300, the machine becomes cheaper. This explains why, as an economy develops and wages rise, firms tend to move from labour-intensive towards capital-intensive methods.

### Reasons that are not about cost alone

- **Nature of the product:** hair cutting, care for the elderly, tailoring and fine craft work need human judgement and personal contact, so they stay labour-intensive even where wages are high.
- **Quality and consistency:** machines produce identical goods, which matters for medicines or car parts.
- **Access to finance:** a small firm may be unable to borrow enough to buy machinery.
- **Flexibility:** a firm whose orders vary from week to week can adjust the hours of workers more easily than it can change the size of a machine.
- **Skills available:** capital-intensive methods need workers able to operate and repair machines.
- **Government aims:** in economies with high unemployment, governments may encourage labour-intensive methods.

### Advantages and disadvantages: linking to the firm

Rather than recite a table, link each point to the firm in the question.

| Firm | Better method | Main reason | Main drawback |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bottled water plant with steady, high sales | Capital-intensive | Low cost per unit at high output; consistent quality | Large fixed cost if sales fall |
| Wedding-cake maker | Labour-intensive | Each order is different | Output limited by staff time; wage bill rises with orders |
| Tea estate in a low-wage economy | Labour-intensive | Labour is cheap and plentiful; leaves must be picked selectively | Productivity per worker is low |

## 3.6.3 Production and productivity

**Production** is the total output of goods and services. **Productivity** is output per unit of input over a period. Productivity can be measured for every factor:

```
Labour productivity   = output / number of workers (or hours worked)
Capital productivity  = output / number of machines
Land productivity     = output / area of land (for example tonnes per hectare)
```

**Worked example 3 (invented).** A rice farm uses 40 hectares and 16 workers.

| | Year 1 | Year 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Output (tonnes) | 160 | 210 |
| Hectares | 40 | 50 |
| Workers | 16 | 15 |
| Tonnes per hectare | 4.0 | 4.2 |
| Tonnes per worker | 10.0 | 14.0 |

Production rose by 50 / 160 x 100 = 31.25%. Land productivity rose by 5% and labour productivity by 40%. Part of the extra production came from more land, and part from each worker and each hectare producing more, perhaps because of better seed, fertiliser or a new harvesting machine.

### Four combinations to recognise

| Production | Productivity | Typical cause |
|---|---|---|
| Rises | Rises | Better technology or training |
| Rises | Falls | More workers hired, each producing less |
| Falls | Rises | Fewer workers, the least productive leave, or machines replace staff |
| Falls | Falls | Lower demand, idle workers kept on |

### Influences

**Production** depends on the quantity of factors used, how productive they are, and demand for the product.

**Productivity** depends on:

- **Quality of labour:** education, training, experience, health and motivation.
- **Quantity and quality of capital:** modern machinery and technology for each worker.
- **Organisation:** division of labour, good management, efficient layout.
- **Quality of land and materials:** fertile soil, reliable inputs.
- **Working conditions:** safe, comfortable workplaces reduce errors and absence.

## Annotated model answer

**Question (invented):** Discuss whether a clothing firm should switch from labour-intensive to capital-intensive production. [6]

> Machines would raise output per worker, so with steady large orders the cost per garment would fall [advantage linked to output]. Garments would be more consistent in quality, reducing returns [second advantage]. However, the machines are expensive, and if orders fall the firm still has to pay for them [disadvantage]. If the firm makes many different styles in small numbers, workers can adjust more easily than machines [disadvantage linked to the product]. The firm should switch if wages are rising and orders are large and predictable, but stay labour-intensive if its orders are small and varied [judgement with conditions].

## Common mistakes

- Treating production and productivity as the same thing.
- Calculating productivity using the wrong input, such as dividing by machines when asked for output per worker.
- Saying capital-intensive methods are always cheaper. They are cheaper only at high output or high wages.
- Forgetting that demand for factors is derived from demand for the product.
- Ignoring the nature of the product when explaining a method choice.
