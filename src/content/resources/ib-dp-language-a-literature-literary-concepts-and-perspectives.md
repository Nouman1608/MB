This guide covers **Course concepts and critical perspectives** for IB Diploma Programme Language A:
Literature, at SL and HL. It follows the current IB subject brief (first assessments for SL and HL
2021). The course has no numbered subtopics, so this unit maps to two areas of exploration named in
the brief: **Readers, writers and texts** and **Intertextuality: connecting texts**. Everything here
applies equally at SL and HL.

## What the brief says

The subject brief says the course explores "the creativity of writers and readers" and "the
performative and transformative potential of literary creation and response". Through close
analysis, students "will consider their own interpretations as well as the critical perspectives of others,
to explore how such positions are shaped by cultural belief systems and to negotiate meanings for
texts."

Three more phrases matter here. One aim is to "develop skills in interpretation, analysis and
evaluation". Assessment objective 2 includes "relationships among different texts" and "ways in
which texts may offer perspectives on human concerns". Assessment objective 3 asks you to
communicate "ideas in clear, logical and persuasive ways".

The brief does not print a numbered list of course concepts, and it names no school of criticism.
This page uses only the ideas the brief itself names. Your teacher will share the full guide's
wording.

## The concepts the brief names

| Concept | Where the brief names it | Question to ask of any text |
|---|---|---|
| Creativity | "the creativity of writers and readers" | What did the writer make, and what must the reader make from it? |
| Perspective | "a variety of perspectives"; "the critical perspectives of others" | Whose view does the text give? Whose is missing? Where do I read from? |
| Culture | "cultural contexts"; "cultural belief systems" | Which beliefs does the text take for granted, and which does it question? |
| Transformation | "the performative and transformative potential of literary creation and response" | What does the text change: a form, a source, a reader's view? |
| Communication | "communicate and collaborate in a confident and creative way"; "ideas in clear, logical and persuasive ways" | How does the text reach its reader, and how will I put my reading across? |

A concept is not a theme. A theme is what a text is about: war, love, loss. A concept is a question
you can bring to any text. "London", below, is not about perspective, but asking about perspective
shows something: the speaker walks and listens, and the people who suffer never speak for
themselves.

## Critical perspectives: lenses for reading

A critical perspective is a set of questions a reader brings to a text, shaped by beliefs about what
matters in literature and in life. The brief does not list which perspectives to use. The ones below
are widely used in literary study. You do not need theorists' names. You need the questions.

| Perspective | Main question | Tends to notice | Risk if overused |
|---|---|---|---|
| Formalist (close reading) | How do form, structure and language make meaning? | Patterns, sound, imagery, shifts | Ignores context and the reader |
| Reader-response | What does a reader do with the text, and why may readers differ? | Gaps, ambiguity, first reactions | "Any reading goes" |
| Historical and biographical | What could the text mean when and where it was written? | Period details, the writer's life | Treats the text as a mere document |
| Marxist (class) | How does the text show money, work and power? | Labour, ownership, institutions | Forcing every text into economics |
| Feminist (gender) | How does the text present gender and the power between people? | Who speaks, who is looked at | Judging the past only by today's values |
| Postcolonial | How does the text present empire, and whose voices are heard? | Centre and margin, language, place | Reading every foreign setting as political |
| Ecocritical | How does the text present nature and human use of it? | Land, animals, weather, ownership of place | Ignoring the human story |

Two rules. First, a lens is a tool, not a verdict: "a Marxist reading suggests" is useful; "this is
a Marxist poem" is not. Second, choose the lens the evidence invites. A poem full of owners and
workers invites a class reading. Forcing a lens onto a text that gives it no evidence produces a weak
interpretation.

## Worked example: three readings of Blake's "London"

Public-domain text: William Blake, "London", from *Songs of Experience* (1794). Blake's engraved
text, with spelling and punctuation modernised.

> I wander through each chartered street,\
> Near where the chartered Thames does flow,\
> And mark in every face I meet,\
> Marks of weakness, marks of woe.
>
> In every cry of every man,\
> In every infant's cry of fear,\
> In every voice, in every ban,\
> The mind-forged manacles I hear:
>
> How the chimney-sweeper's cry\
> Every blackening church appals,\
> And the hapless soldier's sigh\
> Runs in blood down palace-walls.
>
> But most, through midnight streets I hear\
> How the youthful harlot's curse\
> Blasts the new-born infant's tear,\
> And blights with plagues the marriage hearse.

### Reading 1: formalist

- "Every" appears seven times in the first three stanzas. Suffering is not one person's bad luck;
  it is everywhere.
- "Mark" appears three times, as noun and verb: the speaker marks (notices) faces that are marked.
  Seeing and damage are tied together.
- The poem is heard more than seen: "I hear" twice, and "cry", "sigh", "curse". Tight four-line
  stanzas with alternate rhyme hold a city of noise in a rigid pattern.
- It ends on an oxymoron, "the marriage hearse": a new life together is already a funeral.

*Interpretation:* London is a city where every life is damaged, and the relentless repetition makes
the reader feel trapped inside the pattern.

### Reading 2: Marxist and historical

- "Chartered" means granted by legal charter to owners. Even the river is owned.
- Chimney-sweeps in Blake's time were often small children sent up narrow flues. Their cry
  "appals" the "blackening church": the church is shamed, and blackened by soot and guilt, yet acts
  on nothing.
- "The hapless soldier's sigh / Runs in blood down palace-walls": the soldier suffers and the palace
  is stained. Power rests on the poor.

*Interpretation:* the poem accuses church and state of living off suffering they could relieve.

### Reading 3: reader-response

A modern reader may take "mind-forged manacles" as self-imposed limits: the habits of thought that
hold us back. This shows the creativity of readers; the phrase still works more than two centuries
later. But test it. The manacles are heard "In every voice, in every ban". A ban is a prohibition or
a curse, made by others. So the manacles are forged by a society's ideas, not only by the individual.

### Evaluating the three readings

| Test | Formalist | Marxist and historical | "Self-imposed limits" |
|---|---|---|---|
| Evidence | Strong: precise patterns | Strong: "chartered", church, palace | One phrase only |
| Coverage | Explains form, not the targets | Explains stanzas 1--3; weaker on stanza 4 | Ignores "ban", church, palace |
| Context | Neutral | Fits the 1790s | Present-day focus, unacknowledged |

The negotiated reading combines the first two. The repetition enacts the trap that institutions and
ideas build. The third reading's instinct survives in a better form: the manacles are mental, but
they are made socially. That move, keeping what holds up and dropping what does not, is what the
brief means by "negotiate meanings for texts".

## Building an interpretation in six steps

1. **Notice** a detail: a word, image, pattern or shift.
2. **Ask** the question it raises. Why is the Thames "chartered"?
3. **Pick** the lens the detail invites.
4. **Claim** something arguable, with a concept in it.
5. **Test** the claim against the whole text and a rival reading.
6. **Write** it clearly and tentatively: "This suggests...", "A reader focused on class might
   argue..., yet...".

## Evaluating interpretations: five tests

Use these on your own readings, on classmates' readings and on critics.

1. **Evidence.** Is the claim built on precise quotations, not on a general impression?
2. **Coverage.** Does it account for the whole text, including details that do not fit?
3. **Coherence.** Do its parts agree with each other?
4. **Context.** Is it plausible for when the text was written? Reading through present-day values
   is allowed, but say that you are doing it.
5. **Counter-reading.** Does it name and answer a serious alternative?

An interpretation is not wrong because it is unusual. It is weak when it fails these tests.

*Mini example.* Claim: "Blake hates London." Evidence: the speaker never says so. Coverage: his
sympathy for "the new-born infant's tear" points at systems, not at the city. Improved claim:
"Blake presents London as a place ruined by those who own and govern it."

## Connecting texts through concepts and perspectives

Intertextuality asks about "relationships among different texts". A shared lens gives two works a
shared question. Read two works through a gender lens and ask of each: who speaks, and who is looked
at? The comparison is then about how each work answers, not two summaries side by side. A concept
works the same way. "Culture" is especially useful for a work studied in translation, which comes
from different "cultural belief systems" and may be received differently by you than by its first
readers.

## Common errors

- **Labelling instead of reading.** "This is a feminist novel" states a category; it does not
  interpret a single line.
- **Saying the writer holds the lens.** Blake could not be a Marxist: Marx was born in 1818. The lens
  belongs to the reader.
- **Adding a lens only in the conclusion.** A perspective should shape the argument from the thesis.
- **One lens, no test.** A reading that ignores the details it cannot explain fails the coverage test.
- **"It is open to interpretation."** That is not an interpretation. Say which readings are possible
  and which is best supported.
- **Critic as proof.** Quoting a critic does not settle a point. Test the critic's view like any other.
- **Concept confused with theme.** "The theme is perspective" says little; ask whose perspective, and
  how the text controls it.
