## Names (4.2 A)

A polygon is a closed shape made of straight sides.

| Sides | Name |
|---|---|
| 3 | triangle |
| 4 | quadrilateral |
| 5 | pentagon |
| 6 | hexagon |
| 8 | octagon |
| 10 | decagon |

A **regular** polygon has all sides equal **and** all angles equal. A rhombus has equal sides but is not regular unless its angles are also equal (when it is a square).

## Quadrilaterals (4.2 B and 4.2 C)

A diagonal splits any quadrilateral into two triangles, so **the angles of a quadrilateral add up to 360°**.

**Worked example 1.** A quadrilateral has angles 85°, (2x + 5)°, (x + 40)° and (3x − 10)°. Find x and the largest angle.

```
85 + 2x + 5 + x + 40 + 3x − 10 = 360     angles in a quadrilateral add up to 360°
6x + 120 = 360
x = 40
angles: 85°, 85°, 80°, 110°;  largest 110°
```

### Properties of the special quadrilaterals

| Shape | Sides | Angles | Diagonals | Lines of symmetry | Rotational order |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Parallelogram | opposite sides equal and parallel | opposite angles equal; neighbouring angles add to 180° | bisect each other | 0 | 2 |
| Rectangle | opposite sides equal and parallel | all 90° | equal; bisect each other | 2 | 2 |
| Rhombus | all equal; opposite sides parallel | opposite angles equal | bisect each other at right angles; bisect the angles | 2 | 2 |
| Square | all equal; opposite sides parallel | all 90° | equal; bisect each other at right angles; bisect the angles | 4 | 4 |
| Trapezium | exactly one pair of parallel sides | angles between the parallel sides, on the same side, add to 180° | no general property | 0 (1 if isosceles) | 1 |
| Kite | two pairs of equal neighbouring sides | one pair of opposite angles equal | cross at right angles; one bisects the other | 1 | 1 |

A square is a special rectangle and a special rhombus; a rectangle and a rhombus are special parallelograms. So "a square is a rhombus" is true, but "a rhombus is a square" is not always true.

**Worked example 2 (parallelogram).** In a parallelogram, two neighbouring angles are (3y + 12)° and (y + 28)°. Find both angles.

```
3y + 12 + y + 28 = 180     neighbouring angles of a parallelogram add up to 180°
                           (they are allied angles between parallel sides)
4y = 140,  y = 35
angles: 117° and 63°
```

**Worked example 3 (kite).** In kite ABCD, AB = AD and CB = CD. Angle BAD = 110° and angle BCD = 50°. Find angle ABC.

The equal angles of a kite are the pair between unequal sides: angle ABC = angle ADC.

```
2 × angle ABC = 360 − 110 − 50 = 200
angle ABC = 100°
```

## The angle sum of any polygon (4.2 E)

Pick one vertex and draw diagonals to every other vertex you can. An n-sided polygon splits into **(n − 2) triangles**, so

```
sum of interior angles = (n − 2) × 180°
```

This is the same as the specification's "(2n − 4) right angles", because (2n − 4) × 90 = (n − 2) × 180.

**Worked example 4.** Four angles of a pentagon are 100°, 110°, 95° and 120°. Find the fifth.

```
pentagon: (5 − 2) × 180 = 540°
fifth angle = 540 − (100 + 110 + 95 + 120) = 540 − 425 = 115°
```

## Regular polygons (4.2 D)

At each vertex, the interior angle and the exterior angle lie on a straight line, so they add up to 180°. Walking once round any polygon turns you through 360°, so **the exterior angles add up to 360°**. For a regular polygon with n sides:

```
exterior angle = 360° ÷ n
interior angle = 180° − exterior angle
n = 360° ÷ exterior angle
```

**Worked example 5.** (a) Find the interior angle of a regular decagon. (b) A regular polygon has interior angles of 165°. How many sides has it? (c) Can a regular polygon have interior angles of 130°?

```
(a) exterior = 360 ÷ 10 = 36°;  interior = 180 − 36 = 144°
(b) exterior = 180 − 165 = 15°;  n = 360 ÷ 15 = 24 sides
(c) exterior = 50°;  360 ÷ 50 = 7.2, not a whole number, so no
```

Working through the exterior angle is quicker and less error-prone than using the interior angle sum.

**Worked example 6 (combining facts).** ABCDEFGH is a regular octagon. Find angle BAC.

```
interior angle ABC = 180 − 360 ÷ 8 = 135°
AB = BC (regular), so triangle ABC is isosceles
angle BAC = (180 − 135) ÷ 2 = 22.5°
```

## Congruence (4.2 F and 4.2 G)

Two shapes are **congruent** if they are exactly the same shape and size: one would fit exactly on top of the other, possibly after turning it over. A reflection or a rotation of a shape is congruent to the original; an enlargement with a scale factor other than 1 is not, because the size changes.

Examples:

- A diagonal of a parallelogram splits it into two congruent triangles.
- The two diagonals of a rectangle split it into four triangles: opposite pairs are congruent.
- Two squares are congruent only if their sides are equal.

At 4MA1 this section asks you to recognise and use congruence as "same shape and size"; it does not list formal congruence conditions.

## Common mistakes

- Using 180° for the angle sum of a quadrilateral.
- Using n × 180 or (n − 1) × 180 for the angle sum of a polygon.
- Dividing 360 by n to get the interior angle; that gives the exterior angle.
- Calling every four-sided shape with equal sides a square.
- Mixing up which kite angles are equal: they are between the unequal sides.
- Thinking a reflected copy is not congruent because it "faces the other way".

## Exam technique

- **"Work out the size of each interior angle"** of a regular polygon: show 360 ÷ n, then 180 − that.
- **"Show that"** a polygon has a given number of sides: work from the angle to n, and state the conclusion.
- **"Explain why"** a regular polygon cannot have a given angle: show that 360 ÷ exterior angle is not a whole number.
- **"Give a reason"**: use phrases such as "angles in a quadrilateral add up to 360°" and "opposite angles of a parallelogram are equal".
- Sketch and label the shape, marking equal sides and parallel sides.
