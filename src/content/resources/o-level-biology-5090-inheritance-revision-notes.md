## 17.1 Variation

- **Variation**: differences between individuals of the same species.

| | Continuous | Discontinuous |
|---|---|---|
| Phenotypes | A range between two extremes | A limited number, no intermediates |
| Examples | Body length, body mass | ABO blood groups; seed shape and seed colour in peas |
| Cause | Genes **and** environment | Usually genes only |
| Graph | Histogram (bars touch) | Bar chart (bars do not touch) |

**Method in steps: investigating variation (17.1.5)**

1. Pick one species and one feature.
2. Continuous feature (e.g. leaf length): measure many individuals with the same instrument and units; group into equal classes; draw a histogram.
3. Discontinuous feature (e.g. pea seed shape): count the number in each category; draw a bar chart.
4. Use a large sample so the pattern is clear.

## 17.2 DNA

**Structure (17.2.1)**

- Two strands coiled into a **double helix**.
- Each strand is a **chain of nucleotides**.
- Each nucleotide contains a **base**: A, T, C or G.
- **Bonds between pairs of bases** hold the strands together.
- Pairing rule: **A with T, C with G**. Always.

**From gene to protein (17.2.2–17.2.5)**

- **Gene**: a length of DNA that codes for a protein.
- DNA controls cell function by controlling the **production of proteins, including enzymes**.
- **Base sequence** in a gene → **amino acid sequence** of a specific protein.
- Different amino acid sequences → **different protein shapes** → different functions.

**Worked reminder.** 18% of the bases in a DNA molecule are G. So C = 18%. That leaves 100 − 36 = 64% for A + T, so A = T = **32%**.

## 17.3 Inheritance

**Definitions**

- **Inheritance**: the transmission of genetic information from generation to generation.
- **Allele**: an alternative form of a gene.
- **Genotype**: the alleles present (e.g. Tt). **Phenotype**: the observable features (e.g. tall).
- **Homozygous**: two identical alleles (TT or tt). **Heterozygous**: two different alleles (Tt).
- **Dominant**: expressed when one copy is present. **Recessive**: expressed only when no dominant allele is present.
- **Pure-breeding**: two identical homozygous individuals that breed together will be pure-breeding.
- **Codominance**: both alleles in a heterozygote are expressed in the phenotype.

**Method in steps: any monohybrid cross (17.3.4)**

1. Key: capital letter = dominant allele, same small letter = recessive allele.
2. Parental phenotypes, then parental genotypes.
3. Gametes: one allele each, circled or listed.
4. Punnett square.
5. Offspring genotypes → phenotypes → ratio.

**The two ratios you need**

| Cross | Offspring genotypes | Phenotypic ratio |
|---|---|---|
| Heterozygous × heterozygous (Tt × Tt) | 1 TT : 2 Tt : 1 tt | **3 dominant : 1 recessive** |
| Heterozygous × homozygous recessive (Tt × tt) | 1 Tt : 1 tt | **1 dominant : 1 recessive** |
| Homozygous × same homozygous (TT × TT or tt × tt) | All the same | Pure-breeding |

**Worked reminder.** Tt × Tt gives 240 seeds. Expected dwarf = 240 × 1/4 = **60**.

**Observed v expected (17.3.5).** Fertilisation is random, so the ratio is a probability. Small numbers of offspring often give ratios far from 3 : 1 or 1 : 1. Larger samples come closer.

**Worked reminder.** Tt × tt gives 100 plants: 52 tall and 48 dwarf. Observed ratio = 52 ÷ 48 = **1.08 : 1**. Expected 1 : 1 (50 : 50). The small difference is due to chance.

**Spot the cross from the offspring**

- All offspring show the dominant phenotype, and one parent is homozygous recessive → the other parent is almost certainly homozygous dominant.
- Some offspring show the recessive phenotype → **both** parents must carry at least one recessive allele.
- About half dominant, half recessive → one parent heterozygous, the other homozygous recessive.

**ABO blood groups (17.3.7)**

| Genotype | Phenotype |
|---|---|
| IᴬIᴬ, IᴬIᵒ | A |
| IᴮIᴮ, IᴮIᵒ | B |
| IᴬIᴮ | AB |
| IᵒIᵒ | O |

Iᴬ and Iᴮ are codominant; Iᵒ is recessive to both. Two parents of group A and group B can have a child of group O only if both are heterozygous (IᴬIᵒ and IᴮIᵒ).

**Sex determination (17.3.8)**

- Female **XX**, male **XY**.
- Eggs: all X. Sperm: half X, half Y.
- XX × XY → **1 female : 1 male**. Each child has an equal chance of either sex, whatever the earlier children were.

**Mutation and variation (17.3.9–17.3.12)**

| | Gene mutation | Chromosome mutation |
|---|---|---|
| Definition | Random change in the base sequence of DNA | Change in chromosome number or structure |
| Example | Sickle cell anaemia | Down's syndrome: 47 chromosomes instead of 46 |

- Sickle cell anaemia: changed base sequence in the gene for haemoglobin → changed amino acid sequence → haemoglobin of a different shape → red blood cells can become sickle-shaped.
- **Sources of genetic variation**: mutation, meiosis, random mating, random fertilisation.
- **Ionising radiation and some chemicals increase the rate of mutation.**

## 17.4 Selection

**Natural selection: five points (17.4.1)**

1. **Variation** within the population.
2. **Many offspring** produced.
3. **Struggle for survival**, including **competition for resources**.
4. Individuals **better adapted** to the environment survive and **reproduce**.
5. They **pass on their alleles** to the next generation.

Over many generations the inherited features of the population change: it **evolves** (17.4.2).

**Antibiotic-resistant bacteria (17.4.3)**

Mutation gives a few bacteria resistance → antibiotic kills non-resistant bacteria → resistant ones survive and reproduce → resistance allele passed on → resistant strain, e.g. **MRSA**. Link: [Disease and immunity](/resources/o-level-biology-5090-disease-and-immunity-revision-notes/).

**Artificial selection: four points (17.4.4)**

1. Humans **select** individuals with **desirable features**.
2. **Cross** them.
3. **Select offspring** with the desirable features.
4. **Repeat over many generations**.

Role (17.4.5): producing economically important plants and animals, such as high-yield or disease-resistant crops and cattle with high milk yield.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Gene v allele**: a gene codes for a protein; an allele is one form of that gene.
- **Genotype v phenotype**: letters v what you see.
- **Homozygous v heterozygous**: same pair v different pair.
- **Dominance v codominance**: one allele hidden v both alleles expressed.
- **Continuous v discontinuous**: range v categories; genes and environment v genes only.
- **Gene mutation v chromosome mutation**: base sequence v chromosome number or structure.
- **Natural v artificial selection**: environment selects v humans select.

## Quick self-test

1. Define variation.
2. Is body mass an example of continuous or discontinuous variation? What causes it?
3. One DNA strand reads TTACGC. Write the complementary strand.
4. In a DNA molecule, 18% of the bases are G. What percentage are A?
5. Define a gene.
6. Explain why changing the base sequence of a gene can stop an enzyme working.
7. A plant of genotype Tt is crossed with a plant of genotype tt. State the expected phenotypic ratio. (T = tall, t = dwarf.)
8. A cross between two Tt plants gives 300 tall and 98 dwarf offspring. Calculate the observed ratio of tall to dwarf.
9. Give the genotype of a person with blood group AB, and name the type of inheritance shown.
10. How many chromosomes are present in the body cells of a person with Down's syndrome?
11. State four sources of genetic variation in populations.
12. State the four steps of artificial selection.

### Answers

1. Differences between individuals of the same species.
2. **Continuous**; caused by genes and the environment.
3. **AATGCG**.
4. C = 18%, so A + T = 64%; A = **32%**.
5. A length of DNA that codes for a protein.
6. The base sequence sets the amino acid sequence; a different sequence gives a different shape, so the active site may no longer fit the substrate.
7. **1 tall : 1 dwarf**.
8. 300 ÷ 98 = **3.06 : 1** (close to the expected 3 : 1).
9. **IᴬIᴮ**; **codominance**.
10. **47**.
11. Mutation, meiosis, random mating, random fertilisation.
12. Select individuals with desirable features; cross them; select offspring with the desirable features; repeat over many generations.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Giving gametes with two alleles, or forgetting to show gametes at all.
- Stating a genotypic ratio (1 : 2 : 1) when the question asks for a phenotypic ratio (3 : 1).
- Using two different letters for the alleles of one gene (e.g. T and d).
- Writing blood group alleles as A, B and O instead of Iᴬ, Iᴮ and Iᵒ.
- Saying a small family "should" show an exact 3 : 1 ratio.
- Listing natural selection points but leaving out "pass on their alleles".
- Saying bacteria "become resistant" because they are exposed to the antibiotic, rather than resistant ones being selected.
- Mixing up artificial selection (humans choose) with natural selection (the environment chooses).
- Describing Down's syndrome as a gene mutation.

## Frequently asked questions

**Which DNA bases pair together?**
In DNA, A pairs with T and C pairs with G. Writing A–G or C–T pairs is a common error.

**Why do observed ratios differ from expected ratios?**
Fertilisation is random, so a ratio from a genetic cross is a probability. Small numbers of offspring often give ratios far from 3 : 1 or 1 : 1, and larger samples come closer.

**How does sickle cell anaemia show the effect of a gene mutation?**
A changed base sequence in the gene for haemoglobin changes the amino acid sequence, giving haemoglobin of a different shape, so red blood cells can become sickle-shaped.

**What is the difference between a gene and an allele?**
A gene is a length of DNA that codes for a protein. Alleles are the alternative forms of that gene.
