## Section A: knowledge and concepts

**1.** Explain the difference between retribution and justice. **[2]**

**2.** Explain what is meant by poetic justice and why a crime writer might deny it. **[3]**

**3.** Explain why a detective who acts outside the law can unsettle a reader. **[2]**

**4.** For one set text, explain how the presentation of a legal or religious institution shapes the
text's view of justice. **[4]**

**5.** Give two ways in which a wrongful accusation of an innocent character can contribute to a crime
text's meaning. **[2]**

## Section B: an original extract and essay skills

**Extract (original).** A nineteenth-century courtroom. Tom Harker, a farm labourer, is accused of
stealing a sheep; his landlord, Mr Vane, gives evidence.

> The magistrate did not look up from his papers while Mr Vane spoke. When Harker tried to say that
> the sheep had strayed through a broken fence, and that the fence was Mr Vane's to mend, the clerk
> told him to be silent, and the magistrate wrote something and underlined it twice. It was over
> before the church clock finished striking eleven. Outside, Harker's wife was waiting with the baby,
> and Mr Vane raised his hat to her, very civilly, as he passed.

**6.** Analyse how the writer presents the legal system in the extract. **[6]**

**7.** Explain how the final sentence affects the reader's view of justice. **[3]**

**8.** Explain how the extract could be linked to Dickens's presentation of the law in *Oliver Twist*.
**[3]**

**9.** Plan an essay for the question: "Crime texts reassure readers that the guilty will be punished."
Use one set text and list five elements of your plan. **[5]**

**10.** Read this student paragraph on *The Rime of the Ancient Mariner*: "The Mariner shoots the
albatross so he gets punished. This is fair because killing is wrong." Explain two weaknesses and
write an improved topic sentence. **[4]**

**11.** Compare how two different kinds of justice operate in one set text, giving a moment for each.
**[4]**

**12.** For one set text, explain how far its ending restores order. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** Retribution is punishment as repayment for a wrong, focused on the offender [1]; justice is
broader, including fairness to victims and the innocent and a proper process, not only punishment
[1]. **[2]**

**2.** Poetic justice is an outcome in which virtue is rewarded and vice punished [1]. A writer may
deny it to unsettle the reader, to criticise a society whose institutions fail [1], or to suggest
that moral order is not guaranteed in the world the text depicts [1]. **[3]**

**3.** The detective becomes judge as well as investigator, so the reader must decide whether private
judgement can be trusted [1], and the absence of law raises the question of whether the resolution is
truly just [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Institution identified with an accurate reference [1]; how it is presented [1]; what this shows
about justice [2]. For example: in *Oliver Twist*, Mr Fang's court [1] judges Oliver without hearing
him [1], which satirises a system that punishes the poor and helpless [1], so justice in the novel
must come from Brownlow's private goodness as much as from the law [1]. Credit the Inquisition in
"The Pit and the Pendulum", the church in "The Confessional", or the Party in *Death of a Red
Heroine*. **[4]**

**5.** Any two, [1] each: it shows the legal system as fallible or unjust; it builds suspense and
sympathy as the reader fears an innocent will suffer; it misdirects the reader away from the real
criminal; it exposes prejudice in those who accuse. **[2]**

**6.** Any six, [1] each:

- The magistrate "did not look up from his papers" shows indifference to the accused as a person.
- Mr Vane's evidence is heard while Harker's defence is silenced, so the court favours status.
- Harker's explanation about the broken fence shifts responsibility to the landlord, which the court
  will not hear.
- The clerk's order to be silent shows the institution enforcing powerlessness.
- The magistrate writing and underlining "twice" suggests a decision made with emphasis but without
  listening.
- "Over before the church clock finished striking eleven" shows the speed of judgement and links law
  to the established order.
- The calm, reported style mirrors the official process, making the injustice seem routine.
- The ironic gap between the court's procedure and fairness invites the reader to judge the system.

**7.** Any three, [1] each: the wife and baby show who will suffer from the sentence; Mr Vane's
civil gesture is ironic because his courtesy hides his role in the injustice; "very civilly" shows
power preserved through manners; the reader is left with unease rather than the relief of a just
resolution. **[3]**

**8.** Link identified [1]; explanation with reference to the novel [2]. For example: Mr Fang's
court in *Oliver Twist* also judges a powerless defendant without listening [1]; Dickens uses satire
to show the law punishing the poor [1], and Bumble's verdict that the law is "a ass" shares the
extract's ironic view [1]. **[3]**

**9.** Any five, [1] each: a one-sentence judgement on "reassure"; the moment of exposure and how it is
presented; the punishment or its absence; any satire or failure of institutions; the fate of the
innocent; the ending and the reader's response (relief or unease); a relevant context tied to a
moment; a counter-argument. **[5]**

**10.** Weakness one [1]: it treats the punishment as simple cause and effect without analysing how it
is presented. Weakness two [1]: "fair because killing is wrong" is a moral opinion that ignores the
poem's disproportionate, supernatural punishment and its religious meaning. Improved topic sentence
[2] for an argued, precise claim, [1] for a partial improvement. Example: "Coleridge makes the
Mariner's punishment so much greater than his crime that justice in the poem appears less a matter
of fairness than of a moral order the Mariner must learn to love." **[4]**

**11.** Two kinds of justice named [1]; a moment for each [1]; comparison of how they operate [2].
For example, in *Macbeth*: internal punishment in the sleepless guilt of the Macbeths [1] and
private retribution in Macduff's victory [1]; guilt destroys from within while Macduff's act
restores public order [1], so the play suggests that conscience punishes but only action can heal
the kingdom [1]. **[4]**

**12.** A judgement on how far order is restored [1], supported by reference to the ending [1] and a
qualification [1]. For example: *Oliver Twist* restores order for Oliver, who gains a home and an
identity, and punishes Fagin; but Nancy is dead and the system that harmed Oliver remains, so the
order is personal rather than social. **[3]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Assuming justice always means arrest and trial.
- Moral opinions about punishment in place of analysis of the text's choices.
- Ignoring satire of legal and religious institutions.
- Treating endings as neutral rather than as shaped effects on the reader.
