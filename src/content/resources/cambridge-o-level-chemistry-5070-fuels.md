Section 11.3 of the Cambridge O Level Chemistry 5070 syllabus (2026–2028) has seven learning outcomes. You must be able to name the fossil fuels coal, natural gas and petroleum; name methane as the main constituent of natural gas; state that hydrocarbons "contain hydrogen and carbon only" and that petroleum is a mixture of them; describe fractional distillation of petroleum; describe how four properties of the fractions change from the bottom to the top of the column; and name the uses of eight fractions. 5070 has no Core/Extended split, so every outcome is examined for every candidate.

The broader [Petroleum, Alkanes and Alkenes guide](/resources/petroleum-alkanes-and-alkenes/) covers this section alongside alkanes and cracking. This guide goes deeper into the column itself: why separation happens, why each fraction is still a mixture, how to explain the four trends using forces between molecules, and how to answer data questions about fractions.

## Fossil fuels and hydrocarbons

The three **fossil fuels** named in the syllabus are **coal**, **natural gas** and **petroleum** (crude oil). The main constituent of natural gas is **methane**, CH₄.

A **hydrocarbon** is a compound that contains **hydrogen and carbon only**. The word "only" carries the mark. Ethanol, C₂H₅OH, contains carbon and hydrogen, but it also contains oxygen, so it is not a hydrocarbon.

**Petroleum is a mixture of hydrocarbons.** Its molecules range from very small, such as methane, to very large, with long carbon chains. As a mixture it has no single boiling point, and in that form it has few uses. Separating it into fractions turns it into a set of useful products.

## How fractional distillation works

Fractional distillation separates liquids in a mixture using differences in their **boiling points**. For petroleum it works like this:

1. Petroleum is heated strongly so that most of it **vaporises**.
2. The vapour enters the bottom of a tall **fractionating column**.
3. The column has a **temperature gradient**: it is hottest at the bottom and coolest at the top.
4. Vapours rise up the column. As each substance reaches a level where the temperature is **below its boiling point**, it **condenses** to a liquid and is collected there.
5. Substances with **low** boiling points stay as vapour longer and condense **high** in the column, or leave the top as gas. Substances with **high** boiling points condense **low** in the column; the largest molecules never vaporise and are collected at the base.

Each **fraction** is a mixture of hydrocarbons with boiling points within a similar range, and similar numbers of carbon atoms. It is not a single pure compound. That is why an exam answer should say "fractions are collected" rather than "pure compounds are collected".

## Trends from bottom to top

The syllabus limits the trends to four, all going from the bottom to the top of the column:

| Moving up the column | Change |
|---|---|
| Chain length | decreases |
| Volatility | higher (evaporates more easily) |
| Boiling point | lower |
| Viscosity | lower (flows more easily) |

### Explaining the trends

All four trends come from one cause. Hydrocarbon molecules are held together in the liquid by weak forces between molecules (intermolecular forces, as in section 2.5). The **longer the chain**, the **stronger** these forces.

- Stronger forces need more energy to overcome, so longer-chain molecules have **higher boiling points**.
- For the same reason they evaporate less readily: **lower volatility**.
- Stronger forces between long, tangled chains make the liquid **thicker**, so long-chain fractions have **higher viscosity**.

At the top of the column the opposite is true: short chains, weak forces, low boiling points, high volatility and runny liquids or gases.

**Worked example 1.** Fraction P is collected near the top of a fractionating column and fraction Q near the bottom. Compare P and Q in terms of chain length, boiling point and viscosity, and explain why P is collected higher up. [4]

*Annotated answer.* "P has shorter chains than Q [1]. P has a lower boiling point and lower viscosity than Q [1]. P's shorter molecules have weaker forces between them, so less energy is needed to separate them [1]. P stays as a vapour until it reaches the cooler upper part of the column, where the temperature falls below its boiling point and it condenses [1]."

The final mark depends on linking condensation to the temperature in the column. "P is lighter so it rises" does not score: both fractions enter as vapour; the difference is where each condenses.

## The fractions and their uses

The syllabus lists eight fractions and one use for each. They are given here in order from the top of the column to the bottom.

| Position | Fraction | Use named in the syllabus |
|---|---|---|
| Top | Refinery gas | gas used in heating and cooking |
| | Gasoline / petrol | fuel used in cars |
| | Naphtha | chemical feedstock |
| | Kerosene / paraffin | jet fuel |
| | Diesel oil / gas oil | fuel used in diesel engines |
| | Fuel oil | fuel used in ships and home heating systems |
| | Lubricating oil | lubricants, waxes and polishes |
| Bottom | Bitumen | making roads |

A memory aid for the order, top to bottom: **R**eally **G**ood **N**ew **K**ids **D**on't **F**ear **L**earning **B**itumen (refinery gas, gasoline, naphtha, kerosene, diesel, fuel oil, lubricating oil, bitumen).

### Matching properties to uses

The uses make sense once you link them to the trends.

- **Refinery gas** has the shortest molecules and is a gas at room temperature, so it can be piped or bottled for cooking.
- **Gasoline** is highly volatile, so it vaporises easily and mixes with air inside a car engine.
- **Naphtha** is not mainly burned. It is a **chemical feedstock**: the raw material for making other chemicals, including by cracking (section 11.5).
- **Lubricating oil** is viscous, so it stays in place between moving parts instead of draining away.
- **Bitumen** is very viscous and solid when cool, so it binds stones together in road surfaces.

**Worked example 2.** Four unnamed fractions, W, X, Y and Z, are collected from a column. Their boiling ranges are given. (The figures are invented for practice.)

| Fraction | Boiling range / °C |
|---|---|
| W | 250–350 |
| X | below 25 |
| Y | 40–110 |
| Z | 160–250 |

(a) State which fraction is collected highest in the column. (b) State which fraction is the most viscous. (c) X is a gas at room temperature. Suggest its name and use.

*Answer.* (a) X, because it has the lowest boiling points, so it stays as vapour until the coolest part of the column [1]. (b) W, because it has the highest boiling points and therefore the longest chains [1]. (c) Refinery gas [1], used as gas for heating and cooking [1].

Data questions like this never need memorised boiling ranges. Rank the fractions by the numbers given, then apply the trends.

## Common mistakes

- Defining a hydrocarbon as "a compound containing hydrogen and carbon" without "only".
- Saying fractions are pure substances.
- Writing that the column is hottest at the top.
- Reversing the trends: at the top, chains are **shorter**, volatility **higher**, boiling points and viscosity **lower**.
- Explaining position in the column by mass ("heavy molecules sink") instead of boiling point and condensation.
- Giving naphtha's use as "fuel for cars" (that is gasoline) or kerosene's use as "cooking gas" (that is refinery gas).
- Confusing **volatility** (how easily a liquid evaporates) with **viscosity** (how thick it is).

## Exam technique: the command words

- **Name**: the fossil fuel, the fraction or the use, exactly as the syllabus words it.
- **State**: a definition; include "only" for hydrocarbons and "mixture" for petroleum.
- **Describe** (fractional distillation): heat and vaporise; temperature gradient; vapours rise and condense at different heights according to boiling point; fractions collected.
- **Explain** (trends): link chain length to the strength of forces between molecules, then to the property.
