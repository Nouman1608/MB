All businesses and figures are invented, and all money is in Sereno's home currency, shown as $. Section B questions refer to the case study about Sereno Furniture.

> **Case study: Sereno Furniture**
>
> Sereno Furniture is based in a developing country. Its owner started it
> to be her own boss. For years it was the only furniture maker in its
> region, selling 3,000 wooden chairs a year locally at $40 each. It also
> exports 5,000 chairs a year to retailers in a developed country. Each
> chair costs Sereno $25 to make; a similar chair made in the developed
> country costs $48. A new rival has now opened in Sereno's region,
> selling chairs at $34. If Sereno matches this price, it expects to sell
> 2,800 chairs a year locally. If it keeps its price at $40, it expects
> local sales to fall to 2,100 chairs. Sereno has no insurance, and one
> retailer buys all of its exports.

## Section A

**1.** In which **one** of these situations is a business most likely to face minimal competition? **[1]**
- A. A café on a busy high street
- B. The only pharmacy on a remote island
- C. A clothing shop in a large shopping centre
- D. An online bookshop

**2.** Which **one** of the following is an activity that helps a business minimise risk? **[1]**
- A. Launching without market research
- B. Taking out insurance
- C. Relying on a single customer
- D. Spending all of its cash reserves

**3.** Define the term "competition". **[2]**

**4.** State **two** reasons why entrepreneurs choose to run businesses. **[2]**

**5.** Explain **one** reason why all businesses face uncertainty. **[2]**

## Section B

**6.** Calculate Sereno's yearly local sales revenue before the rival opened, and if it matches the rival's price. **[3]**

**7.** Calculate Sereno's cost advantage per chair over the developed-country maker, and express it as a percentage of the developed-country maker's cost. **[2]**

**8.** Explain how lower cost advantage helps Sereno compete in the developed country's market. **[4]**

**9.** Explain **two** ways in which the arrival of the new rival could affect Sereno. **[4]**

**10.** Analyse **two** activities Sereno could use to reduce its risks. **[6]**

**11.** Evaluate whether Sereno should cut its local price to $34 to compete with the new rival. Use your answer to Question 6 and the cost figures in the case study. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** **B** [1]. **[1]**

**2.** **B** [1]. **[1]**

**3.** Rivalry between businesses [1] that are trying to sell to the same customers [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Any two: to earn a profit or higher income [1]; to be independent and be their own boss [1]; to turn an idea or passion into a product; to gain flexibility; to help others. **[2]**

**5.** Customers' tastes can change, for example with fashion or new technology [1], so a business cannot be sure its products will still sell in future, which makes its sales and profit uncertain [1]. *(Also creditable: competitors' actions, economic changes, new laws, unexpected events, each with its effect.)* **[2]**

**6.** Before: 3,000 × $40 = **$120,000** [1]. Matching the price: 2,800 × $34 = **$95,200** [1], a fall of $24,800 [1]. **[3]**

**7.** Cost advantage = $48 − $25 = **$23** a chair [1]. As a percentage = $23 ÷ $48 × 100 = **47.9%** (1 d.p.) [1]. **[2]**

**8.** Sereno makes each chair for $25, almost half the developed-country maker's $48 [1], because wages and other costs are lower in its country [1]. This means Sereno can sell to retailers at a price below what local makers can match while still making a profit [1], which helps it win the export orders for 5,000 chairs a year [1]. **[4]**

**9.** **Lower sales or prices:** customers who used to have no choice can now buy from the rival at $34 [1], so Sereno will either sell fewer chairs or have to cut its price, reducing revenue [1].
**Pressure to improve:** to keep customers, Sereno may need to improve quality, designs or delivery [1], which could raise costs but make it a stronger business [1]. **[4]**

**10.** **Insurance** [1]: Sereno has none, so a fire in its workshop could destroy its stock and equipment [1]; insurance would cover the loss, helping it survive an event it cannot predict [1].
**Spreading risk across customers** [1]: one retailer buys all 5,000 exported chairs [1], so finding more export customers would mean that losing one buyer would not wipe out most of its export sales [1]. **[6]**

**11.** **For cutting the price:** Sereno keeps more customers, selling 2,800 chairs rather than 2,100 [1]; revenue is higher, $95,200 against 2,100 × $40 = $84,000 [1]; keeping customers may stop the rival growing and becoming a bigger threat [1].
**Against cutting the price:** profit is what matters. At $34, profit per chair is $9, so profit = 2,800 × $9 = $25,200 [1]; at $40, profit per chair is $15, so profit = 2,100 × $15 = $31,500 [1]. Keeping the price earns $6,300 more profit with fewer chairs to make [1].
**Judgement:** Sereno should keep its price at $40, because it earns more profit even though it sells fewer chairs [1]. It could compete in other ways, such as quality or its reputation as the established maker [1]. This depends on the sales forecasts being accurate; if far more customers switched to the rival, cutting the price could become the better choice [1]. **[9]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Comparing revenue only and missing that profit is higher at the original price.
- Writing the cost advantage as $48 − $25 but not using it to explain lower prices.
- Saying the rival will "take all of Sereno's customers" without using the figures in the case.
- Naming risk-reducing activities without linking them to Sereno's lack of insurance or single export buyer.
- Forgetting that a business with no competition still faces the risk of new rivals.
