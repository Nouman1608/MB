Responding to spoken language is the second strand of 3.1.3 Spoken language in AQA GCSE English Language (8700). The
specification's wording is brief: "listening to and responding appropriately to any questions and feedback". Its
description of the assessment adds a detail that is easy to miss: students respond "appropriately to questions and to
feedback, asking questions themselves to elicit clarification". The strand is assessed by **AO8**: listen and respond
appropriately to spoken language, including to questions and feedback on presentations. For recall, use the
[responding revision notes](/resources/aqa-gcse-english-language-8700-responding-to-spoken-language-revision-notes/),
and to practise, use the
[responding practice questions](/resources/aqa-gcse-english-language-8700-responding-to-spoken-language-practice/).

## Where it happens

Questions and feedback come "as part of, or following, the presentation". In a debate or dialogue they may be woven
through; after a talk they usually follow it. Either way, this part of the assessment cannot be scripted, which is why
it shows real understanding of your topic.

## What the criteria reward

The endorsement is graded on a competency basis: every criterion for a grade must be met. The listening and
responding criterion steps up like this:

| Grade | Criterion for responding |
|---|---|
| Pass | Listens to questions or feedback and provides an appropriate response in a straightforward manner |
| Merit | Listens to questions or feedback, responding formally and in some detail |
| Distinction | Listens to questions or feedback, responds perceptively and, if appropriate, elaborates with further ideas and information |

Three words carry the difference. **Formally** means keeping the register of the presentation even when the exchange
feels like a conversation. **In some detail** means more than a one-line answer: a reason or an example. **Perceptively**
means seeing what lies behind a question, such as the doubt or assumption that prompted it, and answering that.
**Elaborates** means adding new ideas or information that the presentation did not include.

## Kinds of questions and feedback

Recognising the type of question tells you what kind of answer it needs.

| Type | Example (original, after a talk on banning cars from a high street) | What it needs |
|---|---|---|
| Clarification | "What did you mean by a 'trial period'?" | A clear, concise explanation |
| Extension | "How would deliveries work?" | New detail beyond the talk |
| Challenge | "Isn't this unfair on people who can't walk far?" | Acknowledgement, then a reasoned reply |
| Hypothetical | "What if shops lost money during the trial?" | A considered "if... then" answer |
| Personal | "Would you shop there more yourself?" | An honest answer linked back to the argument |
| Feedback | "Your second section was hard to follow." | Thanks, reflection and a constructive response |

## Active listening

Active listening is the "listens" part of every criterion. It means:

- **hearing the whole question** before planning an answer;
- **identifying its core**: often one key word ("unfair", "cost", "evidence");
- **checking** if it is unclear, by asking your own question to elicit clarification: "Do you mean the cost to
  shops or to the council?" This is exactly what the specification describes, and it shows control rather than
  weakness;
- **watching the questioner**, so you can tell whether your answer has satisfied them.

## Shapes for good answers

**For a straightforward or extension question:**

```
Direct answer -> reason or explanation -> example -> link back to the presentation's point
```

**For a challenge:**

```
Acknowledge the point fairly -> concede what is true -> respond with reasoning or evidence -> restate your position
```

**For feedback:**

```
Thank the speaker -> show you understand the comment -> respond (explain, accept or offer an improvement)
```

These shapes stop you from giving one-word answers on the one hand and wandering speeches on the other.

## Worked example: three answers to one challenge

**Question (original):** "Isn't a car ban unfair on disabled people who need to park close to the shops?"

**Answer A (straightforward):**

> "Yes, that's a fair point. They could still come on other days."

*Comment:* relevant and polite, but brief and undeveloped: it fits a straightforward response.

**Answer B (formal and in some detail):**

> "That's an important point. I think the ban should include an exemption for blue-badge holders, so that they can
> still park on the side streets nearest the shops. Other towns have done something similar."

*Comment:* formal, with a reason and a proposed solution; this is a response "in some detail".

**Answer C (perceptive, with elaboration):**

> "You're right that a ban could make the high street less accessible, and that would undermine the whole point,
> which is to make it a place for everyone. So I'd want disabled residents involved in planning the trial, not just
> consulted afterwards. Beyond parking, a ban could actually help some disabled people: wheelchair users I spoke to
> said that cars parked on the pavement are one of their biggest problems. So the question isn't only access by car,
> but access for everyone once they arrive."

*Comment:* it sees the principle behind the question (fairness and inclusion), concedes honestly, and **elaborates
with further ideas and information** that the presentation did not include, reframing the issue. That is what
"perceptively" looks like in practice.

## Preparing for questions you cannot predict

- **Know more than you present.** Keep reserve material: an extra example, a counter-argument you left out, a source.
- **List likely challenges** to your view and draft the first sentence of each reply.
- **Practise with a partner** who asks unexpected questions, including hostile ones.
- **Prepare a holding phrase** that keeps the register formal while you think: "That's a question I'd like to think
  about carefully."

## Common mistakes

- **Answering a question that was not asked**, because you started planning before the questioner finished.
- **One-word or yes/no answers** with no development.
- **Defensiveness** when challenged, or dismissing the question.
- **Dropping the register**, slipping into casual speech once the "talk" part is over.
- **Repeating the presentation** instead of adding something new.
- **Not asking for clarification** when a question is unclear, and guessing instead.
- **Ignoring feedback**, or simply saying "OK" without engaging with it.

## Technique for the criteria

- Pause briefly before answering; it shows you have listened.
- Use the questioner's key word in the first sentence of your answer.
- Give at least one reason and one example in every answer.
- When challenged, concede something genuine before responding.
- Look for the chance to add new information: it is the clearest sign of elaboration.
