Key question 5.3 of Cambridge O Level Sociology (2251) asks *What factors help to explain differences
in educational achievement and experience?* It is the largest key question in the Education topic:
four patterns, a separate list of explanations for class, ethnicity and gender, a list of reasons for
global differences, and a requirement to judge them all. The skill that earns credit is sorting each
explanation by *where* it operates (home, school or society) and *how* it affects achievement.

The key question says **achievement and experience**. Experience means what school is like for a
pupil: being labelled, feeling included in the curriculum, being bullied, being placed in a low set.
Use both words in your answers.

## The patterns (5.3.1)

In many societies, broad patterns recur: pupils from middle-class backgrounds on average achieve more
than working-class pupils; some ethnic groups achieve above and some below the average; girls now
outperform boys in many countries at school-leaving level, although boys and men still dominate some
subjects and careers; and achievement varies greatly between richer and poorer countries. Patterns
differ between societies, so treat them as tendencies, not rules, and do not quote figures you cannot
support.

## Explanations based on social class (5.3.2)

**Outside school (home and society):**

- **Material factors**: lack of money for devices, internet, books, quiet space, trips and tuition;
  poor diet and housing affect health and attendance.
- **Cultural and social factors**: Barry Sugarman (1970) argued that working-class subculture valued
  **immediate gratification** over deferred rewards and held a **fatalistic** outlook, discouraging
  long study. Critics say these are realistic responses to poverty, not a separate culture.
- **Cultural capital**: Pierre Bourdieu argued that middle-class children inherit knowledge, tastes
  and confidence that schools reward.
- **Home/community factors**: J.W.B. Douglas's *The Home and the School* (1964) linked achievement to
  the level of parental interest in a child's education, which he found higher in middle-class
  families. Critics point out that working-class parents may care just as much but have less time.
- **Linguistic factors**: Basil Bernstein distinguished the **restricted code** (short, context-bound
  speech) from the **elaborated code** (detailed, explicit speech). Schools use the elaborated code,
  which middle-class children learn at home.
- **Compensatory education**: programmes that give extra resources to disadvantaged children, such as
  **Operation Head Start** in the USA (from 1965) and **Sure Start** in the UK. They show that
  governments accept home disadvantage matters.

**Inside school:**

- **In-school factors**: David Hargreaves (1967) found that boys in lower streams, labelled as
  failures, formed an **anti-school subculture** that gave them status by breaking rules. Pupils in
  higher streams more often formed **pro-school subcultures**.
- **Selective and private education**: wealthier families can buy places in private schools or move
  near high-performing schools; entry tests may favour children with tuition.
- **Labelling theory**: Howard Becker found that teachers judged pupils against an image of the
  **ideal pupil**, which middle-class pupils fitted best. Ray Rist (1970) observed a kindergarten
  teacher seating children by her early judgement of their ability, which matched their social class.
  Robert Rosenthal and Lenore Jacobson (*Pygmalion in the Classroom*, 1968) found that pupils
  teachers were told would "spurt" ahead often did, suggesting a **self-fulfilling prophecy**.
- **Marxist explanations**: the myth of meritocracy and the reproduction of class (Bowles and Gintis;
  Willis), which tie the in-school and out-of-school factors to capitalism.

## Explanations based on ethnicity (5.3.3)

- **Institutional racism**: discrimination built into the routine policies of an organisation rather
  than individual prejudice. The term was given wide public attention by the Macpherson Report (1999)
  into the police investigation of Stephen Lawrence's murder. In education, David Gillborn and Deborah
  Youdell (2000) argued that schools focused effort on pupils seen as likely to pass, a form of
  **educational triage**, and that some minority pupils were less often placed in that group.
- **Ethnocentric curriculum**: a curriculum that reflects the culture and history of the majority
  group, which can leave minority pupils feeling excluded.
- **Role models**: few teachers or school leaders from minority groups.
- **Linguistic factors**: pupils learning in a second language may be underestimated or placed in
  lower sets.
- **Cultural, material and social factors**: higher rates of poverty in some minority groups;
  strong educational aspirations in some families and communities.
- **Home/community factors**: family support, supplementary schools and community expectations can
  raise achievement.
- **In-school factors and ethnic subcultures**: Mary Fuller (1984) studied Black girls in a London
  comprehensive who worked hard for qualifications while refusing to seek teachers' approval. Tony
  Sewell (1997) described different responses among Black boys, including rebels, conformists,
  retreatists and innovators.
- **Labelling theory**: Bernard Coard (1971) argued that the British school system wrongly labelled
  many West Indian children as less able. Labels may lead to lower sets and lower expectations.

## Explanations based on gender (5.3.4)

- **Differential gendered socialisation and social control**: girls are often more closely supervised,
  and some argue they learn habits such as neatness and persistence that suit coursework and study.
- **Teacher expectations**: teachers may expect girls to be hard-working and boys to be disruptive, or
  expect boys to be stronger in science.
- **Cultural and social factors**: Sue Sharpe's study of girls' priorities found a shift between the
  1970s and the 1990s from love and marriage towards jobs and careers, linked to changing
  opportunities for women.
- **Access to education**: in some societies girls have less access because of poverty, early
  marriage, safety on the way to school or law.
- **In-school factors and gendered subcultures**: "laddish" cultures in which working hard is seen as
  unmasculine may lower some boys' achievement; Máirtín Mac an Ghaill (1994) described "macho lads"
  who rejected school values.
- **Labelling theory**: boys labelled as troublemakers may be excluded or placed in lower sets.
- **Feminist explanations**: patriarchy shapes subject choice, expectations and careers; feminists
  note that girls' higher grades have not removed male dominance in high-status careers.

## Global differences (5.3.5)

- **Availability of schools**: rural areas may have few schools, especially secondary schools.
- **Poverty/wealth**: families may need children's labour or cannot afford fees, uniforms or books.
- **Access to education for girls**: early marriage, safety and cultural expectations; restrictions on
  girls' secondary schooling in Afghanistan since 2021 are a current example.
- **Class sizes**: very large classes limit individual attention.
- **Norms, values and cultural differences**: the value placed on education, and on educating girls,
  varies.
- **Internet and digital resources**: access to online learning and information is unequal between
  and within countries.

## Weighing the explanations (5.3.6)

| Explanation type | Strength | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Material | Clear link between poverty and resources | Does not explain why some poor pupils succeed |
| Cultural (Sugarman, Bernstein) | Explains attitudes and language | Can blame working-class culture rather than schools or poverty |
| Cultural capital | Links home culture to school rewards | Hard to measure |
| In-school and labelling | Shows schools themselves create inequality | Can be deterministic; pupils may reject labels (Fuller) |
| Institutional racism | Explains patterns no single teacher intends | Does not explain differences between minority groups |
| Feminist | Explains subject choice and careers | Less able to explain boys' lower attainment |
| Global | Shows structural barriers | Varies greatly within countries as well as between them |

**Interaction:** class, ethnicity and gender combine. A working-class boy from a minority group may
face several factors at once, so the strongest answers show how explanations overlap.

## Worked example: an annotated "explain" answer

**Question:** Explain **two** in-school factors that may affect the achievement of working-class
pupils.

"One factor is **labelling**. Teachers may judge working-class pupils as less able because they do
not fit the image of the ideal pupil (Becker) **[point and evidence]**. The pupil may then be placed in
a lower set and receive less challenging work, so the label becomes a self-fulfilling prophecy
**[mechanism]**. A second factor is **anti-school subcultures**. Pupils placed in lower streams may
gain status by rejecting school rules instead (Hargreaves, 1967) **[point and evidence]**, which lowers
their effort and results further **[effect]**."

## Common mistakes

- Treating material and cultural factors as the same; cultural capital is not money.
- Explaining ethnicity patterns as if all minority groups achieve the same.
- Using "girls are cleverer" rather than a sociological explanation.
- Forgetting *experience*: exclusion, labelling and curriculum content matter even where grades are
  similar.
- Describing global differences without explaining *why* each factor affects achievement.

## Exam technique for 2251 command words

- **Explain** answers need factor → mechanism → effect on achievement.
- **Discuss** a factor such as labelling by setting out evidence and its limits.
- **Evaluate** a claim like "home factors matter more than school factors" by weighing both sides and
  judging.
