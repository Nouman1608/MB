This guide covers **2.3 Weather processes and phenomena** in Topic 2, Atmosphere and weather, of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Geography (9696) syllabus for 2025 and 2026. The syllabus content is:

- **Atmospheric moisture processes:** evaporation, condensation, freezing, melting, deposition and sublimation.
- **Causes of precipitation:** convection, frontal and orographic uplift of air, and radiation cooling.
- **Types of precipitation:** clouds, rain, hail, snow, dew and fog.

It is examined in Paper 1, often through diagrams of air rising over mountains, temperature-height graphs or cloud photographs. The [2.3 weather processes and phenomena revision notes](/resources/a-level-geography-9696-weather-processes-phenomena-revision-notes/) give the change-of-state table, lapse rates and a stability example; this guide adds humidity calculations, a full orographic temperature sequence, frontal weather and the link between each cause and each type of precipitation. Test yourself with the [2.3 practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-weather-processes-and-phenomena-practice/).

## Moisture in the air: the measures you need

- **Absolute humidity:** the mass of water vapour in a given volume of air, in grams per cubic metre.
- **Saturation (capacity):** the maximum vapour the air can hold at a given temperature. Warm air can hold much more than cold air.
- **Relative humidity:** the vapour present as a percentage of the maximum the air could hold at that temperature.
- **Dew point:** the temperature at which air becomes saturated if cooled without adding or removing vapour.

Relative humidity can change without any change in moisture: cooling air raises it, warming air lowers it. This is why relative humidity is usually highest at dawn and lowest in the afternoon.

## Worked example 1: relative humidity and the dew point

*Use these illustrative saturation values: air at 30°C can hold 30 g/m³ of vapour; at 20°C it can hold 17 g/m³. A parcel of air at 30°C contains 18 g/m³.*

Relative humidity at 30°C = 18 / 30 x 100 = **60%**.

If the parcel cools to 20°C, it can hold only 17 g/m³, but it contains 18 g/m³. It has therefore passed its dew point (just above 20°C), and about 18 - 17 = **1 g/m³** must condense as droplets. The cooling, not any added moisture, caused saturation.

## The six changes of state and their energy

All six processes in the syllabus involve **latent heat**. Changes that make water more ordered (vapour to liquid, liquid to ice, vapour to ice) **release** heat to the surroundings; changes in the other direction **absorb** it.

- **Evaporation** and **condensation** are the pair that matter most for weather. Evaporation over warm oceans stores huge amounts of energy in vapour; condensation in rising air releases it, warming the air and helping it keep rising. This is why storms such as tropical cyclones and thunderstorms strengthen when condensation is intense.
- **Freezing** and **melting** matter in clouds that extend above the freezing level and in snowfall. Melting snow absorbs heat, which can chill the air below it.
- **Deposition** turns vapour directly into ice: it forms **frost** on cold surfaces and ice crystals in high cloud. **Sublimation** turns ice directly into vapour, as when snow disappears in dry, sunny, windy weather without melting.

Condensation needs **condensation nuclei**: tiny particles of salt, dust, smoke or pollution on which droplets form. Some nuclei, such as sea salt, attract water and allow condensation to begin even slightly below 100% relative humidity.

## Four ways air is cooled to its dew point

Three causes in the syllabus work by **uplift**. Rising air expands as pressure falls, and expansion cools it without heat being lost to the surroundings (**adiabatic cooling**). The fourth, **radiation cooling**, works without uplift.

- **Convection:** the sun heats the ground unevenly; a warm, buoyant parcel of air rises, cools, and reaches its condensation level, forming cumulus cloud. If the atmosphere is unstable, the cloud grows into **cumulonimbus**, with heavy showers, thunder and sometimes hail. Convection peaks in the afternoon, after the ground has warmed.
- **Frontal uplift:** where warm and cold air masses meet, the less dense warm air rises over the cold air.
- **Orographic uplift:** air forced to rise over a mountain barrier cools and produces cloud and rain on the windward side.
- **Radiation cooling:** on clear, calm nights the ground loses heat by longwave radiation, and the air in contact with it cools to its dew point. This produces **dew**, **frost** (by deposition when below 0°C) and **radiation fog**, but rarely rain, because the cooling affects only a shallow layer of air.

## Frontal weather in sequence

At a **warm front**, warm air rises gently over a wedge of cold air along a shallow slope. The cloud sequence, as the front approaches, runs from high, thin **cirrus** to thickening **altostratus** and then low, grey **nimbostratus**, bringing a long period of steady, moderate rain or snow. At a **cold front**, cold air pushes under warm air along a steep slope, forcing it upwards rapidly. This produces towering **cumulonimbus**, short bursts of heavy rain, gusty winds and sometimes hail, followed by clearer skies and a drop in temperature. The contrast in slope explains the contrast in precipitation: gentle uplift gives prolonged, light-to-moderate rain; rapid uplift gives intense, short-lived showers.

## Worked example 2: orographic uplift and the rain shadow

*Air at sea level is 20°C on the windward side of a 3000 m mountain range. Its condensation level is 1000 m. Use 10°C per km for the dry adiabatic lapse rate (DALR) and 6°C per km for the saturated adiabatic lapse rate (SALR). Calculate its temperature at the summit and on returning to sea level on the leeward side, assuming all condensed water falls as precipitation on the windward slope.*

- 0 to 1000 m, unsaturated, cools at the DALR: 20 - 10 = **10°C** at cloud base.
- 1000 to 3000 m, saturated, cools at the SALR: 10 - (2 x 6) = **-2°C** at the summit.
- Descending 3000 m on the leeward side, now unsaturated, warms at the DALR: -2 + (3 x 10) = **28°C** at sea level.

The air arrives 8°C warmer than it started, and much drier, because latent heat was released during condensation on the windward side and the moisture fell as rain. The leeward side is a **rain shadow**, and the warm, dry descending wind is a **föhn** effect.

## Types of precipitation and what forms each

| Type | How it forms | Main cause |
|---|---|---|
| Cloud | Visible droplets or ice crystals formed when air cools to its dew point | Any uplift |
| Rain | Droplets grow until heavy enough to fall, by merging or by melting of ice crystals | Any uplift |
| Hail | Ice pellets cycled up and down in strong updrafts, gaining layers | Convection, cold fronts |
| Snow | Ice crystals clump into flakes and reach the ground without melting | Uplift with air near or below 0°C |
| Dew | Vapour condenses directly on cold surfaces | Radiation cooling |
| Fog | Cloud at ground level; radiation fog on calm nights, advection fog where moist air crosses a cold surface | Radiation or contact cooling |

Strictly, cloud, dew and fog are not precipitation because nothing falls, but the syllabus lists them together, so learn them as **types** of condensation product.

## Annotated model answer

*"Explain why convectional rainfall is often heavy but short-lived." [5]*

> Strong surface heating, often in the afternoon, makes air buoyant so that it rises rapidly **(trigger)**. As it rises it cools adiabatically and reaches its dew point, and condensation releases latent heat, keeping the parcel warmer than its surroundings so it continues to rise **(instability)**. This builds tall cumulonimbus clouds with strong updrafts, in which droplets and ice grow quickly, giving intense rain or hail **(why heavy)**. Each cell covers a small area and its downdrafts of cold, rain-filled air soon cut off the supply of warm, rising air, so the storm dies out within an hour or two **(why short-lived)**.

## Common mistakes

- Saying condensation cools the air; it releases latent heat and warms it.
- Confusing deposition (vapour to ice) with sublimation (ice to vapour).
- Describing radiation cooling as a cause of heavy rain; it gives dew, frost and fog.
- Using the SALR for descending air on the leeward side; it is unsaturated and warms at the DALR.
- Calling all fog "radiation fog".
- Describing cold-front weather as long, steady rain.

## Exam technique

- **Calculate** temperature changes in steps, stating which lapse rate applies in each stage and why.
- **Explain** the formation of a type of precipitation: name the cooling mechanism, the condensation process and the growth process.
- **Compare** frontal and convectional rainfall: compare intensity, duration, area covered and cloud type directly.
- Label diagrams with processes (adiabatic cooling, condensation, latent heat release), not only with features.
