## 2.1.1(a) and (b): isotopes and particles

Isotopes of an element are atoms that share a proton number but contain different numbers of neutrons, giving them different masses. Same proton number means same element. Same electron arrangement means the same chemistry. Properties that depend on mass, for example density, are not identical.

| Quantity | Rule |
|---|---|
| Protons | atomic number |
| Neutrons | mass number minus atomic number |
| Electrons, neutral atom | atomic number |
| Electrons, positive ion | atomic number minus the charge |
| Electrons, negative ion | atomic number plus the charge |

Worked reminders:

- ⁵⁶Fe³⁺ (atomic number 26): 26 protons, 56 − 26 = 30 neutrons, 26 − 3 = 23 electrons.
- ³²S²⁻ (atomic number 16): 16 protons, 32 − 16 = 16 neutrons, 16 + 2 = 18 electrons.

Changing the charge changes only the electron count. It never changes protons or neutrons.

### Models of the atom (HSW1 and HSW7)

The specification links this section to two ideas about how science works:

- Different models explain different phenomena. The Bohr model, with electrons in shells, explains periodic properties.
- Accepted models change as evidence is used to accept or reject them.

A short story you can use: the plum-pudding picture had electrons embedded in a positive sphere. Alpha-particle scattering evidence, where most particles went straight through and a few were deflected strongly, supported a small, dense, positive nucleus surrounded by mostly empty space. Shell models then explained patterns across the periodic table.

## 2.1.1(c): the two definitions

Learn both. The specification says definitions are required.

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Relative isotopic mass | The mass of an atom of an isotope compared with 1/12th the mass of an atom of carbon-12 |
| Relative atomic mass | The mean mass of an atom of the element, weighted by the abundance of each isotope as it occurs, compared with 1/12th the mass of an atom of carbon-12 |

Carbon-12 is the standard for atomic masses. Both quantities are ratios, so they have no units. The phrase "weighted mean" is what separates the second definition from the first. Say which isotope or element you are describing.

## 2.1.1(d): mass spectrometry calculations

You do not need to know how a mass spectrometer works. You do need to read the data. The specification limits this to ions with single charges, so the m/z value of a peak can be taken as the relative isotopic mass of that isotope.

- Peak position (m/z) gives the relative isotopic mass.
- Peak height (or the percentage or relative abundance given) tells you how much of that isotope there is.

Method in steps:

1. List each m/z value with its abundance.
2. Multiply each mass by its abundance.
3. Add up all the products.
4. Divide that sum by the combined abundance (100 when the figures are percentages).
5. Give the answer to a sensible number of significant figures. Relative atomic mass has no unit.

Worked reminder, using invented data for an element Y: peaks at m/z 46, 47 and 48 with abundances 12.0%, 28.0% and 60.0%.

> (46 × 12.0 + 47 × 28.0 + 48 × 60.0) / 100  
> = (552 + 1316 + 2880) / 100  
> = 4748 / 100  
> = 47.48, which is 47.5 to 3 s.f.  

The result must fall between the smallest and largest isotopic mass, closer to the most abundant isotope. If not, recheck the arithmetic.

Working backwards: if only two isotopes exist, let one abundance be x and the other 100 − x, then solve. The relative atomic mass must lie between the two isotopic masses.

## 2.1.1(e): Mr and relative formula mass

| Substance type | Term to use | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Simple molecules | Relative molecular mass, Mr | SO₂, P₄ |
| Giant structures, including ionic compounds | Relative formula mass | Ca(NO₃)₂ |

The specification says the definitions of these two terms are not required. Knowing which term goes with which type of substance is what is expected. Either one is found by summing the relative atomic masses of every atom shown in the formula.

Worked reminders, with Ar values supplied: Ca = 40.1, N = 14.0, O = 16.0, H = 1.0, C = 12.0.

- Ca(NO₃)₂: 40.1 + 2 × (14.0 + 3 × 16.0) = 40.1 + 124.0 = 164.1.
- (NH₄)₂CO₃: 2 × (14.0 + 4 × 1.0) + 12.0 + 3 × 16.0 = 36.0 + 12.0 + 48.0 = 96.0.

Multiply everything inside a bracket by the number outside it.

## 2.1.2(a): ionic formulae

### Charges from the periodic table

| Group | Typical ion |
|---|---|
| 1 | 1+ |
| 2 | 2+ |
| 13 | 3+ |
| 15 | 3− |
| 16 | 2− |
| 17 | 1− |

Metals lose electrons to form positive ions. Non-metals gain electrons to form negative ions. The size of the charge matches the electrons an atom must lose or gain to end up with a full outer shell. The specification says charges on ions other than the ones above and the named ions below will be provided.

### Named ions to recall

| Name | Formula |
|---|---|
| Nitrate | NO₃⁻ |
| Carbonate | CO₃²⁻ |
| Sulfate | SO₄²⁻ |
| Hydroxide | OH⁻ |
| Ammonium | NH₄⁺ |
| Zinc | Zn²⁺ |
| Silver | Ag⁺ |

The specification adds that "nitrate" and "sulfate" should be assumed to mean NO₃⁻ and SO₄²⁻.

Method in steps:

1. Note the formula and charge of the cation and of the anion.
2. Choose the lowest whole-number amounts of each so that positive and negative charges cancel.
3. Show those amounts as subscripts; a subscript of 1 is not written.
4. Enclose a polyatomic ion in brackets when more than one is needed.

Worked reminders:

- Magnesium nitride: Mg²⁺ and N³⁻. Three Mg²⁺ give +6 and two N³⁻ give −6, so **Mg₃N₂**.
- Ammonium sulfate: NH₄⁺ and SO₄²⁻. Two NH₄⁺ balance one SO₄²⁻, so **(NH₄)₂SO₄**.

## 2.1.2(b): balanced and ionic equations

A complete equation has correct formulae, balanced atoms and charges, and state symbols: (s), (l), (g) or (aq).

Method in steps:

1. Write the correct formula for every species. Do not change a formula to make the equation balance.
2. Balance by changing only the large numbers in front.
3. Leave hydrogen and oxygen until last if they appear in several species.
4. Add state symbols.

Ionic equations:

1. Start from the full equation, state symbols included.
2. Show each dissolved strong electrolyte, (aq), as separate ions.
3. Delete any ion that appears unchanged on both sides: these are the spectator ions.
4. What remains is the ionic equation; confirm that atoms and charges both balance.

Worked reminder, with an unfamiliar reaction given in words: aqueous barium chloride and aqueous sodium sulfate form a white precipitate of barium sulfate.

> BaCl₂(aq) + Na₂SO₄(aq) → BaSO₄(s) + 2NaCl(aq)  
> Ba²⁺(aq) + SO₄²⁻(aq) → BaSO₄(s)  

Spectator ions: Na⁺ and Cl⁻. Charge on the left is 2+ plus 2−, which is 0, matching the neutral solid on the right.

Other patterns to know how to apply: acid plus base gives salt plus water; acid plus carbonate gives salt, water and carbon dioxide; ammonium ions with hydroxide ions on warming give ammonia and water.

## Must-know distinctions

- Relative isotopic mass is for one isotope. Relative atomic mass is a weighted mean for the element.
- Relative molecular mass is for simple molecules. Relative formula mass is for giant structures.
- Mass number is protons plus neutrons. Atomic number is protons only.
- A coefficient changes the amount. A subscript changes the substance.
- A full equation shows everything. An ionic equation shows only what changes.

## Quick self-test

1. State the numbers of protons, neutrons and electrons in ²⁷Al³⁺ (atomic number 13).
2. Why do isotopes of one element have the same chemical behaviour?
3. Define relative atomic mass.
4. A sample has two isotopes: 10 with abundance 20.0% and 11 with abundance 80.0%. Calculate the relative atomic mass.
5. Which term, relative molecular mass or relative formula mass, applies to P₄O₁₀? Which applies to Ca(NO₃)₂?
6. Calculate the relative formula mass of (NH₄)₂SO₄ (N = 14.0, H = 1.0, S = 32.1, O = 16.0).
7. Predict the ion formed by an atom of a Group 16 element.
8. Write the formula of aluminium hydroxide.
9. Balance: C₆H₁₄(g) + O₂(g) → CO₂(g) + H₂O(l).
10. Balance, with state symbols: aluminium and aqueous hydrochloric acid form aqueous aluminium chloride and hydrogen gas.

### Answers

1. 13 protons, 27 − 13 = 14 neutrons, 13 − 3 = 10 electrons.
2. Their atoms contain identical numbers of protons, so the electron arrangement is identical.
3. The mean mass of an atom of the element, weighted by isotope abundance, compared with 1/12th the mass of an atom of carbon-12.
4. (10 × 20.0 + 11 × 80.0) / 100 = (200 + 880) / 100 = 10.8.
5. P₄O₁₀ is a simple molecule: relative molecular mass. Ca(NO₃)₂ is ionic and giant: relative formula mass.
6. 2 × (14.0 + 4 × 1.0) + 32.1 + 4 × 16.0 = 36.0 + 32.1 + 64.0 = 132.1.
7. A 2− ion.
8. Al³⁺ and OH⁻ give Al(OH)₃.
9. 2C₆H₁₄(g) + 19O₂(g) → 12CO₂(g) + 14H₂O(l).
10. 2Al(s) + 6HCl(aq) → 2AlCl₃(aq) + 3H₂(g).

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "the average mass" for relative atomic mass instead of "weighted mean", or leaving out carbon-12.
- Defining relative isotopic mass without saying it is for an isotope.
- Subtracting the charge from the electron count for an anion, instead of adding it.
- Dividing by the number of peaks instead of the total abundance.
- Using relative molecular mass for an ionic compound.
- Missing brackets round a polyatomic ion, so that a subscript applies only to its last atom.
- Balancing an equation by altering a formula.
- Leaving out state symbols, or writing a soluble ionic compound as (s) in an ionic equation.
- Leaving spectator ions in an ionic equation, or writing an ionic equation whose charges do not balance.
