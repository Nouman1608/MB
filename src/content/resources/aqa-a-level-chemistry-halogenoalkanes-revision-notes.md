## Definitions and key ideas

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Halogenoalkane | An alkane with one or more H atoms replaced by halogen atoms |
| Nucleophile | An electron pair donor that attacks an electron-deficient (δ+) carbon |
| Nucleophilic substitution | A nucleophile replaces the halogen, which leaves as a halide ion |
| Elimination | H and X are removed from neighbouring carbons, forming a C=C |
| Base (in elimination) | OH⁻ accepting a proton (H⁺) from the carbon next to C–X |
| CFC | Chlorofluorocarbon: a compound of carbon, chlorine and fluorine only |
| Curly arrow | Movement of an electron pair, from a lone pair or bond to where the pair ends up |

**Why halogenoalkanes react.** The halogen is more electronegative than carbon, so the C–X bond is polar:
C is δ+ and X is δ−. Nucleophiles attack the δ+ carbon. Alkanes have no δ+ carbon, so halogenoalkanes are
much more reactive. Uses: refrigerants, solvents and pharmaceuticals. Some uses are restricted because of the
effect of CFCs on the atmosphere.

## Reagents, conditions and products

| Nucleophile | Reagent and conditions | Product | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| OH⁻ | NaOH or KOH(aq), warm (reflux) | alcohol | 1-chloropropane → propan-1-ol |
| CN⁻ | KCN in aqueous ethanol, reflux | nitrile (chain +1 C) | 1-bromopropane → butanenitrile |
| NH₃ | excess NH₃ in ethanol, heat in sealed container | primary amine | 1-iodobutane → butylamine |
| OH⁻ as base | KOH in ethanol, hot, reflux | alkene | 2-bromopropane → propene |

Equations to know the pattern of:

```
R–X + OH⁻  → R–OH + X⁻
R–X + CN⁻  → R–CN + X⁻
R–X + 2NH3 → R–NH2 + NH4X
```

## 3.3.3.1 Mechanisms in steps

> **Method: nucleophilic substitution (OH⁻ or CN⁻)**
> 1. Draw the halogenoalkane and label C as δ+ and X as δ−.
> 2. Arrow 1: from a lone pair on the nucleophile (O of OH⁻, C of CN⁻) to the δ+ carbon.
> 3. Arrow 2: from the C–X bond to X.
> 4. Products: the substituted compound and X⁻.

> **Method: nucleophilic substitution with NH₃**
> 1. Arrow from the N lone pair to the δ+ carbon; arrow from C–X to X.
> 2. Show the intermediate R–NH₃⁺ (charge on N) and X⁻.
> 3. A second NH₃: arrow from its lone pair to an H on N⁺; arrow from that N–H bond onto N.
> 4. Products: R–NH₂ and NH₄⁺ (NH₄X overall).

**Why excess ammonia?** The amine has a lone pair and can itself act as a nucleophile. Excess NH₃ reduces
further substitution. Formation of secondary and tertiary amines and quaternary ammonium salts is in
[3.3.11 Amines](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-amines/) (A-level only).

**Naming nitriles.** Count all carbons, including the C of C≡N. CH₃CH₂CH₂CN is butanenitrile.

*Worked reminder.* 1-chloro-2-methylpropane, (CH₃)₂CHCH₂Cl, with KCN gives (CH₃)₂CHCH₂CN. The longest chain
now has four carbons including the nitrile carbon (numbered C1), with a methyl group on C3, so the product is
**3-methylbutanenitrile**.

## Bond enthalpy and rate

```
C–X bond enthalpy:  C–F > C–Cl > C–Br > C–I
rate of substitution:  iodo > bromo > chloro > fluoro
```

- The C–X bond must break, so the **weaker** bond reacts **faster**.
- Polarity predicts the opposite order (C–F most polar), so polarity is **not** what controls the rate.

### Test-tube hydrolysis (suggested activity)

| Halogenoalkane | Precipitate with AgNO₃ | Appears |
|---|---|---|
| iodoalkane | yellow (AgI) | first |
| bromoalkane | cream (AgBr) | second |
| chloroalkane | white (AgCl) | last |

- Water is the nucleophile; the released X⁻ forms AgX with Ag⁺.
- Ethanol is used as a common solvent so the halogenoalkane and aqueous silver nitrate mix.
- Control: same temperature (water bath), same volumes, same chain length.

### Preparing a chloroalkane (suggested activity)

> **Method: purify a liquid organic product**
> 1. React (e.g. 2-methylpropan-2-ol with concentrated HCl) in a separating funnel; vent often.
> 2. Remove the aqueous layer.
> 3. Wash with sodium hydrogencarbonate solution to remove acid (vent the CO₂), then remove the aqueous layer.
> 4. Dry with an anhydrous salt such as anhydrous sodium sulfate until clear.
> 5. Distil and collect the fraction near the product's boiling point.

## 3.3.3.2 Elimination

> **Method: elimination mechanism**
> 1. Arrow 1: from the OH⁻ lone pair to an H on a carbon **next to** the C–X carbon.
> 2. Arrow 2: from that C–H bond to between the two carbons (new C=C).
> 3. Arrow 3: from the C–X bond to X.
> 4. Products: alkene + H₂O + X⁻.

Substitution and elimination happen **concurrently**. Conditions shift the balance:

| Favours | Solvent | Temperature | OH⁻ acts as |
|---|---|---|---|
| Substitution (alcohol) | water | warm | nucleophile |
| Elimination (alkene) | ethanol | hot | base |

If the neighbouring carbons are different, more than one alkene can form. Check each for E–Z isomerism.

## 3.3.3.3 Ozone depletion

- Ozone forms naturally in the upper atmosphere and **absorbs UV radiation**.
- UV breaks the **C–Cl** bond in CFCs (C–F is stronger), giving Cl• atoms.
- Cl• catalyses ozone decomposition:

```
Cl•  + O3 → ClO• + O2
ClO• + O3 → 2O2  + Cl•
overall: 2O3 → 3O2
```

- Cl• is regenerated, so one atom destroys many O₃ molecules. This contributes to the ozone hole.
- Research by different groups in the scientific community gave evidence for legislation banning CFCs as
  solvents and refrigerants. Chemists developed **chlorine-free** alternatives such as HFCs.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Nucleophile vs base.** A nucleophile donates a pair to carbon; a base accepts H⁺. OH⁻ can do both.
- **Polarity vs bond enthalpy.** Polarity explains *why* nucleophiles attack; bond enthalpy explains the
  *rate order*.
- **Substitution vs elimination products.** Same reagent (KOH), different products: alcohol vs alkene.
- **Aqueous vs ethanolic KOH.** Water favours substitution; ethanol and heat favour elimination.
- **C–Cl vs C–F in CFCs.** Only the weaker C–Cl bond is broken by UV to release radicals.

## Quick self-test

1. Name the product when 1-bromopropane is refluxed with KCN in aqueous ethanol.
2. Calculate Mr of that product (C = 12.0, H = 1.0, N = 14.0).
3. Give the reagent and conditions to convert 1-chloropropane into propan-1-ol.
4. Write the equation for bromoethane with ammonia.
5. Which of 1-chloropentane and 1-iodopentane hydrolyses faster? Explain in one sentence.
6. State the role of OH⁻ when 2-bromopropane forms propene.
7. Where does the first curly arrow start in the reaction of CN⁻ with a halogenoalkane?
8. Write the equation for the formation of a chlorine atom from CCl₃F.
9. Write the two equations showing Cl• catalysing ozone decomposition.
10. In the overall reaction 2O₃ → 3O₂, how many moles of O₂ form from 0.030 mol of O₃?
11. What colour precipitate forms when 1-bromobutane is warmed with silver nitrate in ethanol?

### Answers

1. **Butanenitrile**, CH₃CH₂CH₂CN.
2. C₄H₇N: 4(12.0) + 7(1.0) + 14.0 = **69.0**.
3. Aqueous NaOH (or KOH), warm, under reflux.
4. CH₃CH₂Br + 2NH₃ → CH₃CH₂NH₂ + NH₄Br.
5. **1-iodopentane**: the C–I bond has a lower bond enthalpy than C–Cl, so it breaks more easily.
6. **Base**: it accepts a proton from a carbon next to the C–Br carbon.
7. On the **lone pair on the carbon** of CN⁻, pointing to the δ+ carbon.
8. CCl₃F → •CCl₂F + Cl•.
9. Cl• + O₃ → ClO• + O₂; ClO• + O₃ → 2O₂ + Cl•.
10. 0.030 × 3/2 = **0.045 mol**.
11. **Cream** (AgBr).

## Where marks are usually lost

- Arrow from the H of OH⁻, or from the negative sign with no lone pair shown, instead of from the oxygen
  lone pair.
- Arrow drawn from X to C on the C–X bond (wrong direction).
- Missing δ+ and δ− labels when the question asks for the dipole.
- Ammonia mechanism stopped at R–NH₃⁺, or the second NH₃ step left out.
- Using 1 NH₃ instead of 2 NH₃ in the balanced equation.
- Giving "ethanolic" conditions for substitution, or "aqueous" for elimination.
- Explaining the rate order by electronegativity or polarity instead of C–X bond enthalpy.
- Removing an H from the C–X carbon itself in elimination, rather than from the neighbouring carbon.
- Writing ozone equations where Cl• is not regenerated, or with ClO without the radical dot.
- Saying UV breaks the C–F bond in CFCs.
