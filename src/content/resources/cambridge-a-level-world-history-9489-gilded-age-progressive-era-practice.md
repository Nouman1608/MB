## Section A

**1.** Identify three ways in which the Civil War and its legislation encouraged industrial growth after 1870. **[3]**

**2.** State two ways in which government policy helped business in the Gilded Age. **[2]**

**3.** Identify two features of the "Boss" system in American cities. **[2]**

**4.** State three differences between the Knights of Labor and the American Federation of Labor. **[3]**

**5.** Give three grievances of farmers that led to the Granger movement. **[3]**

**6.** Explain why the assassination of President Garfield led to civil service reform. **[3]**

**7.** Identify four limits of the Progressive Movement's achievements by 1920. **[4]**

## Section B

**8.** Explain why there were frequent financial "Panics" in the period 1873-1907. **[10]**

**9.** "Theodore Roosevelt was the most successful Progressive President." How far do you agree? **[20]**

**10.** Read the two invented sources below. Compare Sources A and B as evidence about living conditions in American cities in the 1890s.

*Source A (invented)* -- From a report by a settlement-house worker in Chicago, 1893. "In one rear tenement I counted eleven people in two rooms, with one window and no water above the yard. The children work at sewing until midnight. Three families share a privy. Typhoid comes every summer, and the landlord says the city will not make him mend the drains."

*Source B (invented)* -- From a speech by a manufacturer to a business club in New York, 1895. "Our cities are the wonder of the world. Electric light, streetcars and running water reach more homes every year. The immigrant who arrives with nothing finds work on the day he lands. If some live poorly, it is because they spend on drink what they should spend on rent." **[15]**

**11.** Read the two further invented sources below, together with Sources A and B in Question 10. How far do Sources A to D support the view that rapid urbanisation was the main reason for the growth of the Progressive Movement?

*Source C (invented)* -- From a magazine article by an investigative journalist, 1903. "The city council meets in a saloon. Every franchise for the streetcars and the gas works has been sold, and the price has been paid in cash to the ward boss. Reform will not come until the citizens throw out the men who sell their city."

*Source D (invented)* -- From a pamphlet by a women's temperance society, 1905. "We have closed saloons in a hundred towns, and we have learned that the vote is the weapon we lack. Women who run homes and schools and hospitals are told they may not choose the men who govern them. We ask for the ballot to clean our cities and protect our children." **[25]**

**12.** Explain why the 19th Amendment was ratified in 1920. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** Any three, 1 mark each: the Homestead Act (1862) encouraged settlement of the West, creating demand for farm machinery and goods (1); the Pacific Railway Act (1862) and land grants led to the transcontinental railroad, completed 1869 (1); railroad building consumed vast amounts of iron, steel and coal (1); wartime demand had built up Northern industry and capital (1); high wartime tariffs were kept after the war, protecting industry (1). **[3]**

**2.** Any two, 1 mark each: high protective tariffs shielded US manufacturers from foreign competition (1); land grants to railroad companies (1); little regulation of business, working hours or monopolies (1); courts often ruled against unions and in favour of property rights (1); the army was used against strikes such as Pullman (1894) (1). **[2]**

**3.** Any two, 1 mark each: party machines such as Tammany Hall controlled city government (1); bosses traded jobs, contracts and help for poor (often immigrant) voters in return for their votes (1); bribes and kickbacks on public contracts, as with Boss Tweed in New York (1). **[2]**

**4.** Any three, 1 mark each: the Knights admitted unskilled workers, women and African Americans, while the AFL organised skilled craft workers (1); the Knights had a broad reform programme, while the AFL concentrated on wages, hours and conditions ("bread and butter") (1); the Knights declined after the Haymarket affair of 1886, while the AFL, founded that year under Gompers, grew (1); the Knights were one national body, the AFL a federation of separate craft unions (1). **[3]**

**5.** Any three, 1 mark each: falling crop prices (1); high railroad freight charges and storage costs at grain elevators (1); debt and high interest rates (1); high tariffs on the manufactured goods they bought (1); a feeling that bankers and railroads had political power farmers lacked (1). **[3]**

**6.** 1 mark each: Garfield was shot in 1881 by a disappointed office-seeker (1); this exposed the spoils system, in which government jobs were handed out as party rewards (1); Congress passed the Pendleton Act (1883), requiring some federal jobs to be filled by competitive examination (1). **[3]**

**7.** Any four, 1 mark each: segregation and Jim Crow laws continued and federal offices were segregated under Wilson (1); Native Americans and many immigrants gained little (1); enforcement of anti-trust and regulatory laws was often weak, and courts struck some down (1); women won the vote only in 1920 and remained unequal in pay and work (1); rural poverty and farmers' problems persisted (1); child labour was not ended -- the 1916 federal law was struck down in 1918 (1). **[4]**

**8.** Indicative content. **Over-expansion:** railroads were built ahead of demand, often on borrowed money; the collapse of Jay Cooke's bank, heavily invested in railroads, began the Panic of 1873, and railroad failures helped trigger 1893. **Weak banking system:** there was no central bank to act as a lender of last resort; thousands of small banks held little reserve, so runs spread quickly. **Money supply:** disputes over silver and gold left the currency inelastic; in 1893 fears for the Treasury's gold reserve undermined confidence. **Speculation:** stock-market and trust speculation drove booms that ended in crashes; in 1907 the failure of the Knickerbocker Trust led to runs that J.P. Morgan had to organise private bankers to stop. **Linking:** rapid, unregulated growth created the risks, while the absence of a central bank turned failures into panics -- which is why 1907 led to the Federal Reserve Act of 1913. **[10]**

**9.** Indicative content. **For Roosevelt:** he changed the presidency, using it as a "bully pulpit"; he intervened in the 1902 coal strike as a broker rather than strike-breaker; the Northern Securities case (1904) revived the Sherman Act; the Hepburn Act (1906) strengthened railroad regulation; the Pure Food and Drug Act and Meat Inspection Act (1906); conservation of millions of acres of forest. **For Taft:** more anti-trust suits than Roosevelt, including the break-up of Standard Oil (1911); the Mann-Elkins Act (1910); the 16th Amendment passed Congress under him -- but the Payne-Aldrich Tariff and the Ballinger-Pinchot affair split the party. **For Wilson:** the Underwood Tariff and federal income tax, the Federal Reserve Act (1913), the Clayton Act and Federal Trade Commission (1914), and laws on child labour and the eight-hour day for railway workers (1916). **Against all three:** segregation under Wilson; limited enforcement. **Judgement:** strong answers set criteria -- changing the role of the presidency, lasting legislation, scope of reform. Roosevelt was the most important in changing the presidency; Wilson's legislation was the most lasting. **[20]**

**10.** Indicative content. **Similarities:** both accept that some city dwellers live poorly; both refer to immigrants and work. **Differences:** A describes severe overcrowding, child labour, poor sanitation and disease; B stresses improvements -- electricity, transport, water -- and blames poverty on drink. A blames landlords and the city; B blames the poor. **Explaining:** A is a settlement-house worker (in the tradition of Hull House, 1889) recording what she saw in order to press for reform; B is a manufacturer addressing fellow businessmen, defending a system that benefits them, in the language of individual responsibility and Social Darwinism. Knowledge: Riis's *How the Other Half Lives* (1890) supports A's description; B's improvements were real but reached the middle class first. A is more reliable on conditions in poor districts; B on the attitudes of employers. **[15]**

**11.** Indicative content. **Support:** A shows the overcrowding, disease and child labour of fast-growing cities; C links urban franchises to corruption; D asks for the vote "to clean our cities". Knowledge: by 1920 more Americans lived in urban than rural areas; city reformers introduced commission and city-manager government (Galveston, 1901). **Challenge:** C stresses political corruption as a cause in itself; D points to temperance and women's demand for a political role as separate drivers; B suggests many contemporaries saw cities as successes. Knowledge: other causes included the muckrakers (Tarbell on Standard Oil, Sinclair on meatpacking), the concentration of wealth in trusts, and repeated banking panics. **Provenance:** C is a muckraking article seeking reform; D is a campaigning pamphlet. **Judgement:** the sources support the view to a large extent, but suggest urbanisation mattered mainly because it concentrated other problems -- corruption, drink, poverty -- where reformers could see and attack them. **[25]**

**12.** Indicative content. **Long campaign:** the National American Woman Suffrage Association under Carrie Chapman Catt pursued a state-by-state and then federal strategy; several western states had already granted women the vote, so women formed a voting bloc that parties could not ignore. **Militant pressure:** Alice Paul's National Woman's Party picketed the White House from 1917, and the arrest and treatment of the pickets drew sympathy. **The First World War:** women's work in industry and war service strengthened their claim; it was hard to fight for democracy abroad while denying it at home. **Wilson's conversion:** the President endorsed the amendment in 1918 as a war measure. **Progressive context:** suffrage fitted Progressive aims of cleaner, more democratic politics, and the temperance movement had shown women's organising power. **Linking:** decades of organisation created the support; the war and Wilson's backing gave Congress the reason to pass it in 1919, and Tennessee's ratification in August 1920 completed it. **[10]**
