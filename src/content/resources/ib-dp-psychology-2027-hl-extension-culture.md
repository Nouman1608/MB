This page is **higher level (HL) only**. The role of culture in shaping human behaviour is one of three HL extensions (with motivation and technology), and the extensions are assessed in **Paper 3**, which SL students do not sit. The extension is meant to extend your understanding of the four contexts, so you apply it inside health and well-being, human development, human relationships and learning and cognition. The guide prints areas of inquiry, not named studies, so the studies below are widely used examples. Teachers and students are encouraged to develop their own areas of inquiry.

Related: [revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-psychology-2027-hl-extension-culture-revision-notes/) and [practice set](/resources/ib-dp-psychology-2027-hl-extension-culture-practice/).

## What the guide says

Culture plays a key role in explaining differences in behaviour across populations. Psychologists consider a person's **local culture**, the culture they are **enculturated** into and the **global culture**. Cultural diffusion has raised questions about the effect of **globalisation** on mental and physical health, identity and relationships.

Two research problems are named.

- **Operationalising variables** in the study of culture is difficult. Researchers must avoid the **ecological fallacy**: assuming that because a person belongs to a cultural group, they share that group's norms. Group averages say little about an individual.
- **Researcher bias.** The guide's key example is **imposed etic bias**: applying findings from one culture to another. Its example is taking findings on modesty in one African culture and applying them to a South American culture because both are agricultural societies.

The concept of **perspective** is central, including Indigenous perspectives on health and well-being.

## Areas of inquiry in each context

| Context | Areas of inquiry printed in the guide |
|---|---|
| Health and well-being | The role of culture in health and well-being; in diagnosing and treating mental health issues; cross-cultural comparisons of the prevalence of mental health issues |
| Human development | The role of cultural dimensions in children's social and cognitive development; the extent Western models of development can explain child development in Indigenous cultures (or vice versa); the role of culture in developing the self |
| Human relationships | Strategies for developing and maintaining cross-cultural relationships; the role of culture and strategies in cross-cultural communication |
| Learning and cognition | The role of culture on learning and cognition; the role of bilingualism on other cognitive processes; the extent learning and cognition across cultures are similar and different |

These are examples. In Paper 3 the sources will reflect one extension inside one context, for example the role of culture in group dynamics.

## Tools you need from the content section

The sociocultural approach terms in the guide are your main tools for this extension.

- **Cultural dimensions.** Hofstede proposed dimensions on which national cultures differ, including individualism and collectivism, power distance and uncertainty avoidance. They allow cross-cultural comparison but describe national averages, so the ecological fallacy is a risk.
- **Etic and emic.** Etic compares cultures using the same measures; emic studies a culture from the inside. Imposed etic bias occurs when a measure created in one culture is used as if it applied in another.
- **Enculturation.** The learning of one's own culture's norms from childhood.
- **Models of acculturation.** Berry's model describes four strategies for people adapting to a new culture: integration, assimilation, separation and marginalisation. The learning objective links acculturation strategies to behaviour and mental health.
- **Social identity theory** and **cognitive dissonance** are also listed terms.

## Example evidence by context

These are examples of the kind of evidence you can use. Check the details with your teacher.

**Health and well-being.** Prevalence rates of mental health disorders differ between populations. Explanations include real differences in risk factors, differences in help-seeking and stigma, and cultural differences in how distress is expressed and diagnosed. A diagnostic category developed in one culture may fit poorly in another, which is an imposed etic problem. Indigenous models of health, such as Durie's Te Whare Tapa Whā from Aotearoa New Zealand, describe well-being as four connected walls (spiritual, mental, family and physical), in contrast to a model that treats mental health as an individual brain state. Becker and colleagues studied girls in Fiji after television arrived in 1995 and reported increases in disordered eating attitudes by 1998. It is often used as evidence for the effect of globalisation, but it is correlational and cannot isolate television as the cause.

**Human development.** Markus and Kitayama (1991) proposed that many Western cultures encourage an independent self and many East Asian cultures an interdependent self. Attachment research by van IJzendoorn and Kroonenberg (1988) compared the distribution of attachment types across countries and found that secure attachment was the most common in all countries studied, but the proportions of other types varied. Questions to ask: does a Western model of development (such as one based on a single caregiver) apply in cultures with shared caregiving?

**Human relationships.** Cross-cultural communication differs in directness, personal space and the role of silence. Strategies for cross-cultural relationships include learning the other's norms, checking assumptions and using shared goals. Acculturation strategy affects relationships with the host community.

**Learning and cognition.** Masuda and Nisbett (2001) found that Japanese and American participants attended to scenes differently, which suggests holistic and analytic styles (see the learning and cognition guide). Bilingualism and cognition: some studies reported a bilingual advantage in executive control, but later large studies and replication attempts have been mixed, so claims should be cautious.

## Indigenous and insider perspectives

The guide stresses the perspectives of Indigenous people, and it describes how an emic approach led to a growing Indigenous psychology. This matters for method as well as content. Community-led research asks the community what questions matter, uses local language and concepts, and shares findings back. Qualitative methods such as interviews, talking circles and observation are common, so Paper 3 questions on these studies are likely to ask about credibility and transferability rather than statistics. When you evaluate such work, do not treat the lack of a large sample as a weakness in itself. Ask instead whether the researcher described the context in enough detail, checked interpretations with participants and reflected on their own position (reflexivity).

## How to write about culture well

- Name the cultural factor (for example a cultural dimension, a language, a norm or a level of acculturation) rather than writing "culture" in general.
- Say whether the study is etic, emic or a mixture, and what that means for the claim.
- Use cautious wording: "in these samples", "on average", "was associated with".
- Link to at least one concept. Perspective, bias and measurement are the usual choices.
- Show that the Western findings are one perspective rather than the default.

## Research problems to discuss in Paper 3

Paper 3 emphasises interpretation of graphs, data analysis, research considerations (credibility, bias, transferability) and synthesis. For culture, prepare these points.

1. **Ecological fallacy.** A bar chart showing that country A has a higher mean on a dimension than country B tells you about averages, not about any one person.
2. **Imposed etic.** Check whether the measure was designed in one culture, translated and used in another.
3. **Translation and equivalence.** A word in one language may not mean the same in another, and response styles (for example avoiding extremes) differ across cultures.
4. **Sampling.** Students in two universities are not representative of two nations.
5. **Cultural bias and researcher bias.** The researcher's own culture shapes the question, the method and the interpretation. Reflexivity and involving local researchers help.
6. **Cause.** Cross-cultural comparisons are quasi-experimental: culture cannot be assigned. Other differences (wealth, education, climate) may explain results.
7. **Transferability** (qualitative research). Interviews in one community give rich insight but may not transfer; the researcher should describe the context in enough detail for readers to judge.
8. **Responsibility.** Avoid stereotyping and stigmatising groups, and report uncertainty.

## A worked mini-analysis (constructed for practice)

A researcher gave the same questionnaire on preference for working alone to 200 students at a university in country X and 200 in country Y. Mean scores out of 10 were 7.2 and 5.4. She concluded that "people in country X prefer to work alone".

- **Data analysis:** country X students scored 1.8 points higher on average. Without standard deviations or a statistical test, we do not know if the difference is statistically significant or overlap is large.
- **Limitation of the conclusion:** the sample is students, so "people in country X" overgeneralises; the ecological fallacy also applies to individuals.
- **Bias:** the questionnaire was written in one language and translated, which may be imposed etic.
- **Better conclusion:** among these university students, those in country X reported a stronger preference for working alone, but the finding may not apply to others.

## Common mistakes

- Treating a national average as a description of every individual.
- Using "culture" as the explanation without saying which cultural factor and how it acts.
- Describing Western findings as universal.
- Naming a cultural dimension without applying it to the data.
- Writing a Paper 3 answer from general knowledge only, with no reference to the sources.
