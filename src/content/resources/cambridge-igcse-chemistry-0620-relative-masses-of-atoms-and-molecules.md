This guide covers subtopic **3.2 Relative masses of atoms and molecules** in Topic 3,
Stoichiometry, for Cambridge IGCSE Chemistry 0620 (2026–2028 series). It is all **Core**
content, so every candidate needs it. The overview in
[Formulae, Equations and the Mole](/resources/formulae-equations-and-the-mole/) gives one
Mr example; this page builds the skill through brackets, larger formulae and proportion
calculations.

## What the syllabus asks for

**Core** (there is no Supplement content in 3.2)

1. Describe **relative atomic mass, Ar**, as the average mass of the isotopes of an element
   compared to 1/12th of the mass of an atom of carbon-12.
2. Define **relative molecular mass, Mr**, as the sum of the relative atomic masses.
   **Relative formula mass, Mr**, is used for ionic compounds.
3. Calculate **reacting masses in simple proportions**. Calculations **will not involve the
   mole concept**.

Point 3 is important for Core candidates: you are expected to scale masses using
proportion, not to calculate amounts in moles. (Extended candidates meet the mole in
subtopic 3.3, but the proportion method below remains a valid way to answer.)

## Relative atomic mass, Ar

Atoms are far too light to weigh in grams in a useful way, so chemists compare them with a
standard. The standard is the **carbon-12 atom**, which is given a mass of exactly 12.
Every other element's Ar says how heavy its atoms are compared with **1/12th** of the mass
of a carbon-12 atom.

Most elements are a mixture of **isotopes** — atoms with the same number of protons but
different numbers of neutrons. Ar is the **average** mass of these isotopes, allowing for
how common each one is. That is why some Ar values are not whole numbers.

**Chlorine as an example.** Chlorine has two isotopes, chlorine-35 and chlorine-37. Its Ar
is 35.5. This lies between 35 and 37 but closer to 35, which tells you chlorine-35 is the
more common isotope. (Calculating Ar from isotope abundances is a Supplement skill in
subtopic 2.3, Isotopes.)

Ar values come from the Periodic Table supplied in the exam. Ar has **no units**, because
it is a comparison.

| Element | Ar | Element | Ar |
|---|---|---|---|
| H | 1 | Mg | 24 |
| C | 12 | S | 32 |
| N | 14 | Cl | 35.5 |
| O | 16 | Ca | 40 |
| Na | 23 | Fe | 56 |
| Al | 27 | Cu | 64 |

## Relative molecular and formula mass, Mr

**Mr is the sum of the relative atomic masses** of all the atoms in the formula.

- For molecules (CO₂, H₂SO₄, C₂H₅OH) it is called **relative molecular mass**.
- For ionic compounds (NaCl, CaCO₃) it is called **relative formula mass**, because there
  are no molecules. The symbol is Mr in both cases.

**Method:** list each element, multiply its Ar by the number of its atoms, add.

### Worked example 1: sulfuric acid, H₂SO₄

```
H: 2 × 1  = 2
S: 1 × 32 = 32
O: 4 × 16 = 64
Mr = 98
```

### Worked example 2: brackets — ammonium sulfate, (NH₄)₂SO₄

The subscript 2 after the bracket doubles everything inside it.

```
N: 2 × 1 × 14 = 28
H: 2 × 4 × 1  = 8
S: 1 × 32     = 32
O: 4 × 16     = 64
Mr = 132
```

A quick alternative: the NH₄ group is 18, so 2 × 18 = 36; the SO₄ group is 96;
36 + 96 = 132.

More results to check your method: Ca(OH)₂ = 74 · Mg(NO₃)₂ = 148 · Fe₂O₃ = 160 ·
Al₂(SO₄)₃ = 342 · C₆H₁₂O₆ = 180.

## Reacting masses in simple proportions

A balanced equation tells you the **ratio of masses** that react and form, once you
multiply each Mr by its coefficient in the equation. Total mass on the left always equals
total mass on the right (conservation of mass). You can then **scale** the masses up or
down like a recipe.

**Method**

1. Write the balanced equation.
2. Under the two substances you need, write **coefficient × Mr**. Ignore the others.
3. Scale: find the factor that turns the known mass in the equation into the mass in the
   question, and apply it to the unknown.
4. Keep units the same throughout: grams with grams, tonnes with tonnes.

### Worked example 3: heating magnesium carbonate

*What mass of magnesium oxide forms when 21 g of magnesium carbonate is heated?*

```
MgCO3 → MgO + CO2
Mr:      84     40
84 g of MgCO3 gives 40 g of MgO
scale factor: 21 ÷ 84 = 0.25
mass of MgO = 40 × 0.25 = 10 g
check: CO2 = 44 × 0.25 = 11 g; 10 + 11 = 21 g ✓
```

### Worked example 4: coefficients matter — iron from iron(III) oxide

*Calculate the mass of iron that can be made from 40 tonnes of iron(III) oxide.*

```
Fe2O3 + 3CO → 2Fe + 3CO2
1 × 160          2 × 56 = 112
160 tonnes of Fe2O3 gives 112 tonnes of Fe
scale factor: 40 ÷ 160 = 0.25
mass of iron = 112 × 0.25 = 28 tonnes
```

Forgetting the 2 in front of Fe would give 14 tonnes — exactly half the right answer.

### Worked example 5: proportion from experimental data

*1.2 g of copper forms 1.5 g of copper(II) oxide. What mass of copper(II) oxide forms from
4.8 g of copper?*

No equation is needed: 4.8 ÷ 1.2 = 4, so the mass of oxide is 4 × 1.5 = **6.0 g**. The
syllabus calls this "simple proportions" because the masses scale together.

## Common mistakes

- **Using the atomic (proton) number instead of Ar** — e.g. 8 for oxygen instead of 16.
- **Ignoring brackets**: Ca(OH)₂ is 74, not 58.
- **Ignoring coefficients** in reacting-mass questions.
- **Adding units to Ar or Mr**: they have none.
- **Using Cl = 35 or 36** instead of 35.5 from the Periodic Table.
- **Mixing units**, such as grams on one side and kilograms on the other.

## Exam technique: command words

- **Describe** Ar: include *average*, *isotopes* and *1/12th of the mass of a carbon-12
  atom* — each is a likely marking point.
- **Define** Mr: "the sum of the relative atomic masses" (of the atoms in the formula).
- **Calculate**: show each step — the equation ratio, the scale factor and the answer with
  units. Method marks are available even if the final number slips.
- **Show that**: work to the given value and state it clearly at the end.

## Quick checklist

- Ar described with carbon-12 as the standard
- Ar values read from the Periodic Table, including Cl = 35.5
- Mr calculated with brackets
- Reacting masses scaled from a balanced equation or from given data
