> **Syllabus edition note.** This guide follows the Cambridge O Level Environmental Management 5014
> syllabus for exams in 2027, 2028 and 2029.

Sub-topic 1.5 turns from where energy comes from to how people can use it more wisely. The
[energy resources, conservation and fracking guide](/resources/o-level-cambridge-environmental-management-energy-resources/)
lists the eight strategies with a one-line description each. This page explains how each strategy saves
energy, separates the strategies that **use less** from those that **supply differently**, and works
through the calculations and "discuss" answers the sub-topic generates.

## What the syllabus asks

- **1.5.1** Describe strategies for the management of energy resources, limited to: reducing consumption;
  insulation; energy-efficient devices including vehicles and electrically propelled vehicles; exploiting
  existing energy resources; education on energy conservation; transport policies; battery storage;
  development of new energy resources.
- **1.5.2** Describe the development of new energy resources, limited to: **blue hydrogen fuel** (produced
  from natural gas); **green hydrogen fuel** (produced using renewable resources); **ground source heat
  pumps** (transfer of heat from the ground for heating); **air source heat pumps** (transfer of heat from
  the air for heating).
- **1.5.3** Discuss the benefits and limitations of strategies for the management of energy resources.

## Two families of strategy

| Use less energy | Supply or store energy differently |
|---|---|
| Reducing consumption | Exploiting existing energy resources |
| Insulation | Battery storage |
| Energy-efficient devices and vehicles | Development of new energy resources |
| Education on energy conservation | |
| Transport policies | |

Strategies in the first column cut demand, so fewer resources are used at all. Those in the second column
make better use of supply, often helping renewable resources replace fossil fuels.

## The eight strategies explained

**Reducing consumption.** Switching off lights and devices not in use, turning heating down by a degree,
drying clothes outside, and making fewer journeys. It costs nothing to begin, but it depends entirely on
people choosing to change.

**Insulation.** Materials that slow the escape of heat from a building, or its entry in hot climates: roof
(loft) insulation, filled cavity walls, double-glazed windows and draught-proofing. Less heat lost means
less fuel or electricity used for heating or cooling. It costs money to fit, but the saving continues for
many years.

**Energy-efficient devices, including vehicles and electrically propelled vehicles.** An efficient device
does the same job with less energy: LED lamps instead of filament lamps, well-rated fridges and washing
machines, and vehicles that travel further on each litre of fuel. **Electrically propelled vehicles**
(electric cars, buses, trains and hybrids) use electric motors, which waste less energy than petrol and
diesel engines and release no exhaust gases where they are driven. They are only as low-carbon as the
electricity that charges them.

**Exploiting existing energy resources.** Making fuller use of resources that are already available, for
example improving the efficiency of existing power stations or recovering more of the fuel from fields and
mines already in production, rather than opening new ones.

**Education on energy conservation.** Campaigns, school lessons, energy labels on appliances and smart
meters that show how much energy is being used help people to understand how and why to save energy.
Education supports every other strategy.

**Transport policies.** Government measures to cut fuel use: reliable and affordable public transport,
cycle lanes, car-sharing lanes, park-and-ride schemes, and charges or taxes on driving into city centres.
They work only if the alternatives to driving are convenient.

**Battery storage.** Batteries store electricity produced when supply is greater than demand, such as solar
power at midday or wind power at night, and release it later. Storage does not create energy; it solves the
main limitation of solar and wind, which is that their output does not match the times people need
electricity.

**Development of new energy resources.** New resources add supply that can be cleaner or more secure. The
syllabus names four.

## The four new energy resources

### Blue hydrogen and green hydrogen

Hydrogen can be burned, or used in a fuel cell to generate electricity, and the only product is water, so
no carbon dioxide is released **when it is used**. The colour describes how the hydrogen is **made**:

| | Blue hydrogen | Green hydrogen |
|---|---|---|
| Produced from | Natural gas | Water, using electricity from renewable resources |
| Carbon dioxide in production | Produced; intended to be captured and stored | Little or none |
| Main benefit | Uses existing gas supplies and is cheaper to make at present | Can be made with very low emissions |
| Main limitation | Still uses a finite fossil fuel; emissions if the carbon dioxide is not captured, and methane can leak | Needs large amounts of renewable electricity; currently more expensive |

Both share limitations: hydrogen must be compressed or cooled for storage and transport, and new pipelines,
filling stations and vehicles are needed.

### Ground source and air source heat pumps

A heat pump moves heat from a cooler place to a warmer one, using electricity to do so.

- A **ground source heat pump** circulates fluid through pipes buried in the ground, where the temperature
  stays fairly steady through the year. The fluid absorbs heat from the ground; the pump concentrates it to
  heat a building and its water.
- An **air source heat pump** uses a unit outside the building to absorb heat from the outside air, even in
  cold weather, and transfers it indoors.

The benefit is that a heat pump delivers more heat energy to the building than the electrical energy it
uses. Limitations: high cost to install; ground source systems need land or deep boreholes for the pipes;
air source pumps work less well in very cold weather; both need electricity, so their carbon emissions
depend on how that electricity is generated.

## Worked example: energy savings

*Invented figures.* A house needs 9000 kWh of heat a year. A direct electric heater supplies 1 kWh of heat
for each kWh of electricity. An air source heat pump supplies 3 kWh of heat for each kWh of electricity.

```
electric heater: 9000 / 1 = 9000 kWh of electricity
heat pump:       9000 / 3 = 3000 kWh of electricity
saving = 9000 - 3000 = 6000 kWh
percentage saving = 6000 / 9000 x 100 = 66.7%
```

*Invented figures.* Insulation costs 1200 currency units to fit and saves 300 units a year on heating.

```
payback time = 1200 / 300 = 4 years
```

After the payback time, the saving is pure gain. A payback question is often followed by "suggest why
some households do not insulate": the upfront cost, renting rather than owning, or planning to move.

## Model "discuss" answer

*Question (original):* Discuss the benefits and limitations of transport policies as a strategy for
managing energy resources in a large city. [6]

*Model answer:* Transport policies such as better public transport, cycle lanes and charges for driving
into the centre can reduce the number of cars, so less petrol and diesel is burned. One bus or train
carries many passengers using less fuel per person than separate cars, and less traffic also reduces air
pollution and congestion. Charges raise money that can be spent on improving public transport. However,
the policies only work if public transport is reliable, affordable and goes where people need it; otherwise
people keep driving. New rail lines and bus fleets are expensive, and driving charges can be unpopular and
fall most heavily on people on low incomes. Cycling is less practical in very hot or wet climates or for
long journeys. Overall, transport policies are effective in a large, crowded city where public transport
can be made convenient, especially when combined with education and electrically propelled buses.

## Common mistakes

- Saying battery storage "produces" energy. It stores surplus electricity for later use.
- Mixing up the hydrogen colours: blue from natural gas, green using renewable resources.
- Writing that hydrogen fuel releases carbon dioxide when burned. The product is water; emissions come
  from producing blue hydrogen.
- Saying heat pumps only work in volcanic areas. That is a limitation of geothermal electricity.
- Calling electric vehicles "zero-carbon" without considering how the electricity is generated.
- Answering "discuss" with benefits only, or with no judgement.

## Exam technique

- **Describe** a strategy: what is done and how it reduces energy use or improves supply.
- **Describe** a new resource: use the syllabus wording (from natural gas; using renewable resources;
  transfer of heat from the ground or air).
- **Discuss**: benefits, limitations and a judgement that depends on the place or people in the question.
- **Calculate**: show working and use the original value as the base for percentages.
