## Questions

### Section A: short-answer questions

**1.** Which **one** of the following is most likely to be a variable cost for a furniture maker? **[1]**

A: Rent of the workshop

B: Timber used in each table

C: Insurance for the building

D: The manager's monthly salary

**2.** Which **one** of the following is the correct formula for the break even point in units? **[1]**

A: fixed cost ÷ sales price

B: fixed cost ÷ (sales price − variable cost)

C: (sales price − variable cost) ÷ fixed cost

D: total costs ÷ variable cost

**3.** Define the term margin of safety. **[1]**

**4.** Aled borrows £7,500 to buy a coffee machine and repays £8,700 in total. Calculate the interest on the loan as a percentage. **[2]**

**5.** Explain **one** effect on a business's break even level of output of an increase in its fixed costs. **[3]**

### Section B: case-study questions

**7.** *Heronsgate Hampers* sells food hampers at £45 each. The variable cost is £27 a hamper. Fixed costs are £5,400 a month. It budgets to sell 400 hampers a month.

**(a)** Calculate the break even level of output. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the margin of safety in hampers. **[1]**

**(c)** Calculate the margin of safety in pounds of revenue. **[1]**

**(d)** Calculate the budgeted profit. **[2]**

**(e)** A supplier raises the variable cost to £30 a hamper. Calculate the new break even level of output. **[2]**

**(f)** Analyse the impact of the supplier's price rise on Heronsgate Hampers. **[6]**

**8.** *Copse End Candles* breaks even at 480 candles a month. Each candle sells for £14 and fixed costs are £4,320 a month. Calculate the variable cost per candle. **[2]**

**9.** The owner of Heronsgate Hampers (variable cost back at £27) is considering two plans.

Plan A: move to a larger unit, adding £1,200 a month to fixed costs, and buy in bulk to cut the variable cost to £24 a hamper. Sales are expected to stay at 400 a month.

Plan B: raise the price to £50 a hamper, with sales forecast to fall to 360 a month.

Justify which plan the owner should choose. **[9]**

**10.** A break even diagram for *Mossgiel Mugs* shows the total costs line meeting the vertical axis at £1,500. The total revenue line passes through £3,000 at an output of 250 mugs.

**(a)** State the fixed costs. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the selling price of one mug. **[1]**

## Answers

**1.** B: Timber used in each table [1]. **[1]**

**2.** B: fixed cost ÷ (sales price − variable cost) [1]. **[1]**

**3.** The amount by which actual or budgeted sales are above the break even level of sales [1]. **[1]**

**4.** (£8,700 − £7,500) ÷ £7,500 × 100 [1] = **16%** [1]. **[2]**

**5.** Higher fixed costs mean there is more to cover before the business makes a profit [1]. Each unit still contributes the same amount (price minus variable cost), so more units must be sold to break even [1]. The break even level of output therefore rises, and the margin of safety falls if sales do not change [1]. **[3]**

**6.** 200 bars: revenue £1,000, total costs £1,300, loss £300 [1]. 300 bars: revenue £1,500, total costs £1,500, profit £0 (break even) [1]. 400 bars: revenue £2,000, total costs £1,700, profit £300 [1]. **[3]**

**7. (a)** £5,400 ÷ (£45 − £27) = £5,400 ÷ £18 [1] = **300 hampers** [1].

**(b)** 400 − 300 = **100 hampers** [1].

**(c)** 100 × £45 = **£4,500** [1].

**(d)** Revenue £45 × 400 = £18,000; total costs £5,400 + (£27 × 400) = £16,200 [1]. Profit = **£1,800** [1].

**(e)** £5,400 ÷ (£45 − £30) = £5,400 ÷ £15 [1] = **360 hampers** [1].

**(f)** Contribution per hamper falls from £18 to £15 [1], so the break even level rises from 300 to 360 hampers [1]. The margin of safety shrinks from 100 to 40 hampers, so a small fall in sales would push Heronsgate into a loss [1]. Budgeted profit falls from £1,800 to £600 (400 × £15 − £5,400) [1]. The owner could raise the price to restore contribution, but customers may buy fewer hampers [1], or look for a cheaper supplier, which could affect the quality of the food [1]. **[14]**

**8.** Contribution = £4,320 ÷ 480 = £9 [1]. Variable cost = £14 − £9 = **£5** [1]. **[2]**

**9.** Plan A: fixed costs become £6,600 and contribution £45 − £24 = £21 [1]. Break even = £6,600 ÷ £21 = 314.3, so 315 hampers [1]. Profit at 400 = 400 × £21 − £6,600 = £1,800, the same as now [1]. The margin of safety falls to 85 hampers and fixed costs are higher even if sales drop [1].

Plan B: contribution = £50 − £27 = £23 [1]. Break even = £5,400 ÷ £23 = 234.8, so 235 hampers [1]. Profit at 360 = 360 × £23 − £5,400 = £2,880 [1], and the margin of safety is 125 hampers [1].

Judgement: the owner should choose Plan B, because it gives £1,080 more profit and a much wider margin of safety, with no extra fixed costs; the risk is that sales fall below the forecast of 360 at the higher price, so the owner should test the price first [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* Calculating profit, break even and margin of safety for both plans gives the judgement solid support. Plan A only makes sense if growth beyond 400 hampers is expected, because the bigger unit adds capacity.

**10. (a)** **£1,500**, where the total costs line meets the vertical axis at zero output [1].

**(b)** £3,000 ÷ 250 = **£12** [1]. **[2]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using price instead of contribution as the divisor.
- Rounding 314.3 down to 314, which leaves the business short of break even.
- Forgetting to add fixed costs into total costs in a table.
- Giving margin of safety in hampers when pounds were asked for.
- In part 7(f), giving figures without saying what they mean for the business.
