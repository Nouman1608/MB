This guide covers subtopic 7.3, Solid waste, in Topic 7 of IB Diploma Programme Environmental Systems and Societies, first assessment 2026. The [Topic 7 study guide](/resources/ib-dp-ess-natural-resources-energy-waste/) introduces solid domestic waste (SDW), the waste hierarchy, the pollution management model and a landfill lifespan calculation. This page goes further: it shows how waste composition changes with income, compares a linear and a circular economy, explains how modern landfill and incineration are engineered, and treats plastics and electronic waste as special cases. It ends with the policy tools that change behaviour.

## Sources and types of solid waste

SDW is only part of the solid waste a society produces. Others include:

- **commercial** waste from shops and offices
- **industrial** waste from manufacturing, some of it hazardous
- **construction and demolition** waste, often the largest stream by mass
- **agricultural** waste, such as crop residues and manure
- **electronic waste (e-waste)**, discarded devices containing metals, plastics and toxic substances

Classify waste in two further ways. **Biodegradable** waste (food, garden waste, paper) is broken down by decomposers; **non-biodegradable** waste (most plastics, glass, metals) persists. **Hazardous** waste (batteries, solvents, medical waste) needs separate handling.

### How composition changes with income

As incomes rise, waste per person usually rises, and its make-up changes. In lower-income areas the largest share is often organic (food and garden waste). In higher-income areas a larger share is paper, card, plastics and packaging, reflecting more processed and packaged goods. This matters for management: a city with mostly organic waste gains most from composting, while one with mostly packaging gains most from reduction and recycling.

## Worked example 1: planning diversion from landfill

A fictional town of 50,000 people produces 22,000 t of SDW a year with this composition: food and garden 40%, paper and card 20%, plastics 12%, glass 7%, metals 4%, textiles 5%, other 12%.

```
Waste per person = 22,000,000 kg ÷ 50,000 = 440 kg per year (about 1.2 kg per day)

Masses: organics 8,800 t; paper 4,400 t; plastics 2,640 t;
        glass 1,540 t; metals 880 t; textiles 1,100 t; other 2,640 t

Plan: compost 70% of organics; recycle 60% of paper, glass and metals
Composted  = 0.70 × 8,800 = 6,160 t
Recycled   = 0.60 × (4,400 + 1,540 + 880) = 0.60 × 6,820 = 4,092 t
Diverted   = 6,160 + 4,092 = 10,252 t  →  10,252 ÷ 22,000 × 100 = 46.6%
Still sent to disposal = 22,000 − 10,252 = 11,748 t
```

Notice that composting does most of the work because organics are the largest fraction. The plan does nothing about plastics, which suggests the next target.

### Contamination

Recycling figures can mislead. If kerbside collections gather 5,000 t of "recyclables" but 18% is contaminated (food-soiled card, wrong plastics), then 900 t is rejected and only 4,100 t is recycled. Clear labelling, simpler sorting rules and separate collections cut contamination.

## Linear and circular economies

A **linear economy** follows take, make, use, dispose: raw materials become products that become waste. A **circular economy** designs waste out by keeping products and materials in use for as long as possible through durable design, repair, reuse, remanufacturing and recycling, and by returning biological materials safely to the soil. The related **cradle-to-cradle** idea, set out by William McDonough and Michael Braungart, treats every material as a future input rather than a waste.

In systems terms, a circular economy turns waste outputs into inputs, so the system needs fewer inputs of natural capital and releases less pollution. It links 7.3 directly to the sustainable use of resources in 7.1.

## How disposal and recovery are engineered

### Sanitary landfill versus open dumps

An **open dump** is uncontrolled: waste is tipped on bare ground, leachate soaks into groundwater, methane escapes, and fires, pests and disease are common. A **sanitary landfill** controls these flows:

- a clay or plastic **liner** under the waste stops leachate escaping
- **leachate collection pipes** drain the liquid for treatment
- **gas wells** collect methane, which can be flared or burned to generate electricity
- **daily cover** of soil reduces smells, pests and windblown litter
- a final **cap** and monitoring continue after closure

Each feature is a control at the second level of the pollution management model: it limits release of the pollutant rather than preventing the waste.

### Incineration with energy recovery

Burning waste reduces its volume greatly and can generate electricity or heat. Its outputs need control: flue gases are cleaned to remove acid gases, particulates and dioxins; **bottom ash** can sometimes be used in construction; **fly ash** from the filters is hazardous and must go to secure landfill. Incineration also needs a steady supply of waste to pay for the plant, which can discourage reduction and recycling.

### Composting and anaerobic digestion

**Composting** breaks organic waste down aerobically into a soil improver. **Anaerobic digestion** breaks it down without oxygen in a sealed tank, producing **biogas** (mainly methane, used as fuel) and **digestate** (a fertiliser). Both keep organic waste out of landfill, where it would release methane uncontrolled.

## Special cases

### Plastics

Most conventional plastics are non-biodegradable. In the environment they fragment into **microplastics**, which are eaten by organisms and can move along food chains. Ocean currents gather floating plastic in accumulation zones within the great ocean gyres. "Biodegradable" and "compostable" plastics often break down only under industrial composting conditions, so they are not a simple solution if they end up in the sea or in landfill.

### E-waste

E-waste contains valuable metals such as copper and gold, and toxic substances such as lead and mercury. Where devices are exported and broken up informally, for example by burning cables to recover copper, workers and nearby communities are exposed to toxic fumes and contaminated soil. The **Basel Convention** (adopted 1989) controls the movement of hazardous waste between countries. Formal e-waste recycling recovers metals safely and reduces the need for new mining.

## Policy tools

| Tool | How it changes behaviour | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Landfill tax | Makes disposal dearer, so recycling and composting become competitive | Can encourage illegal dumping (fly-tipping) |
| Pay-as-you-throw | Households pay by the amount of residual waste | Needs monitoring; may burden large, low-income households |
| Deposit-return scheme | A refundable deposit on bottles and cans encourages their return | Covers only some materials; needs collection points |
| Extended producer responsibility | Producers pay for collecting and treating their products at end of life | Costs may pass to consumers; needs enforcement |
| Bans on single-use items | Removes some items from the waste stream entirely | Substitutes may have their own impacts |
| Separate collections and education | Makes correct sorting easy and cuts contamination | Depends on public participation |

Tools that act at the top of the waste hierarchy (reduce, reuse) tackle causes; those at the bottom manage outcomes.

## Common mistakes

- Treating recycling as the top of the hierarchy. Reduction and reuse come first.
- Calling all incineration "waste of energy". With energy recovery it is above disposal on the hierarchy, but below recycling.
- Describing a landfill without its controls, or treating every landfill as an open dump.
- Saying biodegradable plastics solve marine litter.
- Ignoring contamination when quoting recycling rates.
- Calculating diversion without converting percentages to masses first.

## Exam technique

- **Calculate** waste per person, masses from percentages, diversion rates or lifespans: keep units (t, kg per person per year, %).
- **Explain** a strategy: name its level in the pollution management model and its place in the hierarchy.
- **Evaluate** a waste plan: effectiveness for the waste composition in the data, cost, public participation, and side effects such as fly-tipping or air emissions, then a judgement.
- **Discuss** with perspectives: an ecocentric view favours reduction and changing consumption; a technocentric view may favour energy-recovery technology.
