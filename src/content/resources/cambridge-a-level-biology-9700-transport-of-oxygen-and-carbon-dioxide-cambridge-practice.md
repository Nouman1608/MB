These questions cover sub-topic **8.2 Transport of oxygen and carbon dioxide** of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Biology 9700 syllabus for examination in 2025, 2026 and 2027 (AS Level, Paper 1 and Paper 2 style). The number in square brackets after the last part of each question is the total for the whole question. All data are invented for practice. Calculators are allowed.

## Section A

**1.** Where is carbonic anhydrase found in the blood?

A: in plasma

B: in red blood cells

C: on the surface of capillary endothelium

D: in white blood cells **[1]**

**2.** Which correctly describes the Bohr shift?

A: the dissociation curve moves left when pCO₂ rises

B: the dissociation curve moves right when pCO₂ rises

C: the dissociation curve moves right when pO₂ rises

D: haemoglobin's affinity for oxygen rises when pCO₂ rises **[1]**

**3.** Name the compound formed when hydrogen ions combine with haemoglobin, and state **one** consequence of its formation. **[2]**

**4.** State **two** ways in which carbon dioxide is carried in the plasma. **[2]**

**5.** Describe the shape of the oxygen dissociation curve of adult haemoglobin. **[2]**

## Section B

**6.** Describe how carbon dioxide produced by respiring cells is carried to the lungs. Include the roles of carbonic anhydrase, haemoglobin and the plasma. **[6]**

**7.** Describe the chloride shift and explain why it is important. **[4]**

**8.** Explain why the oxygen dissociation curve of adult haemoglobin is S-shaped. **[3]**

**9.** The table shows the percentage saturation of haemoglobin at different partial pressures of oxygen.

| pO₂ / kPa | 2 | 4 | 6 | 8 | 10 | 12 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Saturation / % | 22 | 58 | 79 | 89 | 94 | 97 |

Fully saturated blood carries 20 cm³ of oxygen per 100 cm³.

**(a)** Blood leaves the lungs at 12 kPa and reaches a resting tissue at 6 kPa. Calculate the volume of oxygen released per 100 cm³ of blood.

**(b)** During exercise, tissue pO₂ falls to 2 kPa. Calculate how many times more oxygen is released per 100 cm³ than in (a).

**(c)** Explain the importance of the shape of the curve shown by your answers. **[6]**

**10.** At a pO₂ of 4 kPa, the percentage saturation of haemoglobin was 58 % when pCO₂ was 5.3 kPa and 41 % when pCO₂ was 9.3 kPa.

**(a)** Name the effect shown by these results.

**(b)** Explain how the higher pCO₂ causes the lower saturation.

**(c)** Explain the importance of this effect in an actively contracting muscle. **[6]**

**11.** Describe what happens to hydrogencarbonate ions, chloride ions, haemoglobinic acid and carbaminohaemoglobin when blood reaches the lungs. **[4]**

**12.** A drug inhibits carbonic anhydrase. Suggest **two** effects on the transport of gases in the blood, and explain each. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** **B** [1]. Total: 1 mark.

**2.** **B** [1]. Total: 1 mark.

**3.** Haemoglobinic acid [1]; any one of: oxyhaemoglobin releases its oxygen; free H⁺ are removed so blood pH does not fall much [1]. Total: 2 marks.

**4.** As hydrogencarbonate ions in solution [1]; as dissolved carbon dioxide [1]. Total: 2 marks.

**5.** S-shaped (sigmoid) [1]; shallow at low pO₂, steep in the middle, levelling off near 100 % at high pO₂ [1]. Total: 2 marks.

**6.** Any six from: carbon dioxide diffuses into red blood cells; carbonic anhydrase catalyses the reaction of CO₂ with water to form carbonic acid; carbonic acid dissociates into H⁺ and HCO₃⁻; H⁺ combine with haemoglobin to form haemoglobinic acid; some CO₂ combines with haemoglobin (amine groups) to form carbaminohaemoglobin; HCO₃⁻ diffuse out into the plasma (chloride shift); most CO₂ is carried as HCO₃⁻ in plasma; a small amount is dissolved in plasma [6]. Total: 6 marks.

**7.** Hydrogencarbonate ions diffuse out of red blood cells into the plasma [1]; chloride ions diffuse into the red blood cells [1]; this keeps the cell electrically neutral (balances the charge) [1]; so HCO₃⁻ can keep leaving and more CO₂ can be converted and taken up [1]. Total: 4 marks.

**8.** The first oxygen molecule binds with difficulty, so the curve is shallow at low pO₂ [1]; binding of the first oxygen changes the shape of haemoglobin, making it easier for further oxygen molecules to bind, so the curve is steep [1]; at high pO₂ most binding sites are occupied, so the curve levels off [1]. Total: 3 marks.

**9. (a)** 20 × (0.97 − 0.79) [1] = **3.6 cm³** [1].

**(b)** 20 × (0.97 − 0.22) = 15.0 cm³ [1]; 15.0 ÷ 3.6 = **4.2 times** [1].

**(c)** Tissue pO₂ lies on the steep part of the curve [1]; so a small fall in pO₂ (when respiration increases) releases a large extra volume of oxygen to the cells that need it [1]. Total: 6 marks.

**10. (a)** The Bohr shift (or Bohr effect) [1].

**(b)** More CO₂ enters red blood cells and forms carbonic acid (via carbonic anhydrase), releasing more H⁺ [1]; H⁺ combine with haemoglobin to form haemoglobinic acid [1]; which reduces the affinity of haemoglobin for oxygen, so less is bound [1].

**(c)** Contracting muscle respires fast and produces a lot of CO₂ [1]; so haemoglobin releases more oxygen there, supporting aerobic respiration [1]. Total: 6 marks.

*Examiner insight:* "CO₂ binds to haemoglobin instead of oxygen" does not earn the explanation marks; the mechanism is via H⁺ and reduced affinity.

**11.** Hydrogencarbonate ions move back into red blood cells [1]; chloride ions move out into the plasma [1]; haemoglobinic acid releases H⁺, which combine with HCO₃⁻ to form carbonic acid, broken down to CO₂ and water by carbonic anhydrase [1]; carbaminohaemoglobin releases carbon dioxide, which diffuses into the alveoli [1]. Total: 4 marks.

**12.** Any two effects, each with its explanation [4]: less CO₂ converted to HCO₃⁻, so less CO₂ is carried as hydrogencarbonate and more stays dissolved or as carbaminohaemoglobin, reducing the CO₂ carried; fewer H⁺ formed, so less haemoglobinic acid, so less oxygen released in tissues (a smaller Bohr effect); slower release of CO₂ in the lungs, because conversion of HCO₃⁻ back to CO₂ is also slower. Total: 4 marks.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Saying CO₂ binds to the iron in haem.
- Moving chloride ions the wrong way.
- Describing the Bohr shift as "down" or as competition between CO₂ and O₂.
- Reading saturation values from the wrong axis or using percentages as volumes.
- Explaining the curve's importance without naming lungs and tissues.
