They cover the concepts used in preparing financial statements, comparing sole traders, partnerships and limited companies, and sole traders' statements. ## Formulas and rules

| Item | Rule |
|---|---|
| Expense charged | Paid + owing at end - paid in advance at end (and adjust for opening balances) |
| Income earned | Received + income due - income received in advance |
| Straight line depreciation | (Cost - residual value) / years of use |
| Reducing balance | Percentage x net book value at start of year |
| Provision for doubtful debts | Percentage x receivables after irrecoverable debts; charge only the change |
| Net book value | Cost - provision for depreciation |
| Profit or loss on disposal | Proceeds - net book value |
| Inventory | Lower of cost and net realisable value |
| Goods taken for own use | Debit drawings, credit purchases, at cost |
| Closing capital | Opening capital + net profit - drawings |
| Gross profit | Revenue - cost of sales |
| Net profit | Gross profit + other income - expenses |

FIFO, AVCO and LIFO inventory valuation are stated as not examined.

## Where each adjustment goes

| Adjustment | Income statement | Statement of financial position |
|---|---|---|
| Other payables | Increases the expense | Current liabilities |
| Other receivables | Reduces the expense | Current assets |
| Income due | Increases income | Current assets (other receivables) |
| Income received in advance | Reduces income | Current liabilities |
| Depreciation | Expense | Reduces non-current assets to net book value |
| Irrecoverable debt | Expense | Reduces trade receivables |
| Provision change | Expense (or reduction of expense if the provision falls) | Trade receivables shown net |
| Closing inventory | Deducted in cost of sales | Current assets |
| Own goods | Reduces purchases | Increases drawings, reduces capital |

## Method in steps

1. Underline each adjustment and note the accounts affected.
2. Calculate each adjusted figure separately.
3. Build the income statement: revenue, cost of sales (trading business only), gross profit, other income, expenses, net profit.
4. Build the statement of financial position with sub-headings: non-current assets, current assets, current liabilities, non-current liabilities, then capital.
5. Check that net assets equal closing capital.

A service business has no inventory and no cost of sales. Its income statement runs from revenue to expenses.

## Worked reminders (new figures)

Rent paid is 7,800 and 650 relates to next year, so the charge is 7,150. Commission received is 3,100 and 240 is due, so income is 3,340.

An asset cost 12,000 with residual value 2,000 and four years of life: straight line gives 2,500 a year. Reducing balance at 25% gives 3,000 in year 1 and 2,250 in year 2, leaving net book value 6,750.

Trade receivables are 9,000 and 250 is irrecoverable. The remaining 8,750 at 2% gives a provision of 175. If the old provision was 120, charge 55. If it was 220, the provision falls by 45 and this is a reduction in expenses.

A machine cost 9,000 and has provision for depreciation of 6,300, so net book value is 2,700. Sold for 2,100, it makes a loss on disposal of 600.

## Must-know distinctions

- Irrecoverable debt (a debt that will not be paid, an expense) versus provision for doubtful debts (an estimate against remaining receivables).
- Drawings (owner takes value out, reduces capital) versus expenses (running costs of the business).
- Capital expenditure (non-current assets, spread through depreciation) versus revenue expenditure (day-to-day costs, charged in full).
- Other payables (owed) versus other receivables (paid early).
- Income due (earned, not yet received) versus income received in advance (received, not yet earned).

## Business types compared

| | Sole trader | Partnership | Limited company |
|---|---|---|---|
| Owners | One | Two or more | Shareholders |
| Liability | Unlimited | Usually unlimited | Limited |
| Capital | Owner's funds and borrowing | Partners' funds, more than one source | Shares and loans, can be large |
| Control | Owner alone | Shared | Directors act for shareholders |
| Strength | Full profit and quick decisions | Shared skills and workload | Limited liability, continuity |
| Weakness | Risk and limited capital | Disputes, shared risk | Rules, cost, loss of control |

Distinguish share capital from loan capital for companies: shareholders own a company and share in its profit, while lenders are owed repayment and interest.

## Layout to learn

```
Income statement for the year ended ...
Revenue
Less cost of sales: opening inventory + purchases (less own use)
   + carriage inwards - closing inventory
Gross profit
Add other income (income due, income received in advance adjusted)
Less expenses (each adjusted for payables, receivables, depreciation,
   irrecoverable debts, change in provision)
Net profit

Statement of financial position at ...
Non-current assets (cost, depreciation, net book value)
Current assets (inventory, trade receivables net, other receivables, bank)
Current liabilities (trade payables, other payables, income in advance)
Non-current liabilities (loans repayable later)
Capital: opening + net profit - drawings
```

## A second full reminder: Penmore Trading

Revenue is 40,000. Opening inventory is 3,000, purchases are 26,000 (including 500 of goods the owner took), carriage inwards is 400 and closing inventory is 4,200. Wages are 6,000, rent is 2,400 with 200 still owing, depreciation is 800, and insurance paid is 900 with 150 relating to next year.

| Line | Working | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Cost of sales | 3,000 + 25,500 + 400 - 4,200 | 24,700 |
| Gross profit | 40,000 - 24,700 | 15,300 |
| Rent charge | 2,400 + 200 | 2,600 |
| Insurance charge | 900 - 150 | 750 |
| Total expenses | 6,000 + 2,600 + 800 + 750 | 10,150 |
| Net profit | 15,300 - 10,150 | 5,150 |

Gross profit is 38.25% of revenue and net profit is about 12.9%. On the statement of financial position, 200 appears in current liabilities and 150 in current assets. The 500 of own goods does not appear as an expense: it is in drawings.

## Comment starters

- "Net profit rose, but drawings were higher still, so capital fell."
- "Gross profit as a share of revenue fell, which may suggest lower selling prices or higher purchase costs."
- "Net current assets are positive, so the business can cover short-term debts, although much of the total is inventory."
- "Depreciation reduces profit but not cash, so profit and bank balance can move differently."

## Quick self-test

1. Which concept explains deducting an expense paid in advance?
2. Electricity paid in the year is 2,640. 310 is owing at the end and 250 was owing at the start. What is the charge?
3. An asset has cost 14,000 and provision for depreciation of 3,500. Calculate depreciation at 20% reducing balance.
4. Receivables after writing off a debt are 8,750, the provision is 2% and the old provision is 120. What is charged?
5. Goods cost 2,400 and could be sold for 1,900 net. At what value are they included in inventory?
6. The owner takes goods that cost 280. State the double entry.
7. Opening capital is 15,000, net profit 9,800 and drawings 7,200. Find closing capital.
8. Revenue is 58,000 and cost of sales 39,500. Find gross profit and the gross profit percentage to 1 decimal place.
9. Give two disadvantages of being a sole trader.
10. An asset cost 9,000, has provision for depreciation of 6,300 and is sold for 2,100. State the profit or loss.
11. Fees of 240 are earned but unpaid. Where does this appear in the statement of financial position?
12. Give one advantage of a limited company over a partnership.

### Answers

1. Accruals.
2. 2,640 + 310 - 250 = 2,700.
3. Net book value is 10,500, so 20% gives 2,100.
4. 175 - 120 = 55 charged.
5. 1,900, the lower figure.
6. Debit drawings 280, credit purchases 280.
7. 15,000 + 9,800 - 7,200 = 17,600.
8. 18,500; 18,500 / 58,000 = 31.9%.
9. Unlimited liability; capital limited to what the owner can raise.
10. Net book value 2,700, proceeds 2,100, loss of 600.
11. Other receivables, in current assets.
12. Limited liability for shareholders (or easier to raise capital from shares).

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using the whole provision as the charge instead of the change.
- Adding a prepaid expense instead of deducting it.
- Leaving goods for own use in purchases, or treating them as an expense.
- Showing inventory at selling price or ignoring a lower net realisable value.
- Applying the reducing balance percentage to cost every year instead of net book value.
- Putting income received in advance among assets.
- Showing profit on disposal as a deduction, or loss as income.
- Giving a list of features with no link to the business in the question.
- Stating a figure when asked to comment, without saying what it means.
