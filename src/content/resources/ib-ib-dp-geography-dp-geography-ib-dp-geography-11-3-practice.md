## Section A

**1.** Distinguish between trade creation and trade diversion. **[2]**

**2.** State two physical factors that can limit a country's participation in global trade. **[2]**

**3.** Outline one way in which climate influences the location of data centres. **[2]**

**4.** Define the friction of distance. **[1]**

**5.** Identify one human-made structure that has reduced a physical barrier to global interaction. **[1]**

## Section B

**6.** A product costs 200 dollars at a factory. Transport to a coastal country costs 10 dollars; transport to a landlocked country costs 30 dollars. Both countries charge a tariff of 5% on the cost including transport.

**(a)** Calculate the landed cost in each country. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the percentage by which the landlocked country's landed cost exceeds the coastal country's. **[1]**

**(c)** Suggest two human factors that could reduce the landlocked country's extra cost. **[2]**

**7.** Using the gravity model, interaction = (P1 x P2) / d squared, with populations in millions and distance in hundreds of kilometres:

**(a)** Calculate the interaction between City J (6 million) and City K (2 million), 400 km apart. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the new value if a high-speed railway halves the effective distance between them. **[1]**

**(c)** Explain why replacing distance with travel time can improve the model. **[2]**

**8.** Explain how a natural hazard in one country can disrupt global supply chains, using an example. **[3]**

**9.** Explain why physical factors still influence global interactions despite time-space compression. **[3]**

## Section C

**10.** Examine the relative importance of human and physical influences on a country's participation in global interactions. **[10]**

**11.** Discuss the effects of trading blocs on global interactions. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** Trade creation is when members of a bloc buy from a lower-cost member instead of producing at higher cost at home [1]; trade diversion is when members switch from a cheaper non-member supplier to a more expensive member because the member faces no tariff [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Being landlocked, with no direct access to the sea [1]; mountain or desert barriers that raise transport costs [1]. (Also credit lack of natural harbours, seasonal ice or exposure to hazards.) **[2]**

**3.** Data centres need large amounts of energy for cooling [1], so a cold climate reduces cooling costs, as at Luleå in northern Sweden, where a large data centre opened in 2013 [1]. **[2]**

**4.** The idea that distance increases the time, cost or effort of interaction between places, so interaction tends to fall as distance rises [1]. **[1]**

**5.** For example, the Suez or Panama Canal, or an Alpine rail tunnel [1]. **[1]**

**6. (a)** Coastal: (200 + 10) x 1.05 = **220.50 dollars** [1]. Landlocked: (200 + 30) x 1.05 = **241.50 dollars** [1].

**(b)** (241.50 - 220.50) / 220.50 x 100 = 21 / 220.50 x 100 = **9.5%** [1].

**(c)** A transit agreement with neighbours that lowers fees and border delays [1]; investment in a railway or road to a neighbouring port [1]. (Also credit a trade agreement removing the tariff.) **[5]**

**7. (a)** (6 x 2) / 4 squared = 12 / 16 = **0.75** [1].

**(b)** d = 2, so 12 / 2 squared = 12 / 4 = **3.0**, four times the original [1].

**(c)** Two places the same distance apart can be very differently connected, depending on transport links [1], so travel time or cost better represents the real effort of interaction, which is what the model aims to predict [1]. **[4]**

**8.** Production of many components is concentrated in a few places to save costs [1]. When a hazard strikes one of those places, factories close and parts cannot be shipped [1]. In 2011, floods in Thailand inundated industrial estates producing a large share of the world's hard-disk drives, causing shortages and higher prices for manufacturers worldwide [1]. **[3]**

**9.** Time-space compression has reduced the cost of moving information and money far more than the cost of moving bulky goods, which still depends on distance and terrain [1]. Physical events such as volcanic ash, floods and droughts still disrupt transport networks [1], and climate and energy resources influence where infrastructure such as data centres is located [1]. **[3]**

**10.** Indicative content; award one mark per valid, developed point up to 10:

- Identifies human influences: policy, trade agreements, stability, infrastructure, language, colonial ties [1].
- Identifies physical influences: coastal access, relief, climate, resources, hazards [1].
- Landlocked states face higher landed costs, but the size of the penalty depends on transit agreements and neighbours' infrastructure [1].
- Switzerland shows that human factors can outweigh a physical disadvantage [1].
- Strategic location, such as Singapore's on the Strait of Malacca, gives an advantage that policy then builds on [1].
- Resource endowment draws investment and trade but can create dependence [1].
- Physical hazards disrupt participation, as in Thailand's 2011 floods [1].
- Human and physical factors interact: investment modifies physical constraints [1].
- Differentiates by type of flow: physical factors matter more for goods than for data [1].
- Conclusion: a supported judgement, for example that human factors usually decide how far physical conditions limit participation [1]. **[10]**

**11.** Indicative content; award one mark per valid, developed point up to 10:

- Defines a trading bloc and gives examples such as the EU single market or USMCA [1].
- Lower tariffs and shared rules increase trade between members [1].
- Trade creation lets consumers buy from more efficient producers [1].
- Trade diversion can shift purchases away from cheaper non-members [1].
- Blocs can attract foreign investment from firms seeking access to a large market [1].
- Some blocs allow free movement of people, increasing migration and tourism [1].
- Leaving a bloc, as the UK did in 2020, adds friction to trade with former partners [1].
- Non-members, including many lower-income countries, may be disadvantaged [1].
- Uses accurate examples [1].
- Conclusion: a supported judgement, for example that blocs intensify interactions within them while reshaping, rather than simply increasing, global flows [1]. **[10]**

*Examiner insight:* "Discuss" answers on blocs gain credit for considering several viewpoints: members, non-members, producers and consumers.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Applying the tariff only to the factory price, not the cost including transport.
- Forgetting to square the distance in the gravity model.
- Explaining physical influences as if human decisions played no part.
- Saying a trading bloc simply "increases trade" with no mention of diversion.
