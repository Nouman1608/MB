This guide goes deeper into section 5.3 Factors of production of the Pearson Edexcel International GCSE in Business (4BS1) specification, Issue 2, February 2026. Its single point, 5.3.1, is "Changing relationships between enterprise, capital, land, and labour", with the named detail "difference between capital-intensive and labour-intensive activities". The section is short in the specification, but questions on it are usually applied: why a business changes its mix of inputs, and what that change means for its costs, risks and workers.

The [factors of production and quality study guide](/resources/edexcel-igcse-business-factors-of-production-and-quality/) defines the four factors with worked cost tables. This page goes further into **why** the mix changes, how to decide which mix is cheaper, and how to judge the wider effects. ## The four factors as a system

| Factor | What it is in a business | Example in a bakery |
|---|---|---|
| Land | Natural resources and the sites a business uses | The shop premises; water; the wheat grown for flour |
| Labour | People's physical and mental effort and skills | Bakers, counter staff, delivery drivers |
| Capital | Man-made resources used to produce other goods: equipment, machinery, vehicles, tools, software | Ovens, mixers, the delivery van, the till system |
| Enterprise | The person or people who organise the other three, make decisions and take the risk | The owner who decides what to bake and invests her savings |

Two points about how they relate:

1. **They work together.** No factor produces anything alone: an oven needs a baker, a baker needs premises, and someone has to decide what to bake.
2. **They can partly replace each other.** A business can make the same output with more machines and fewer people, or the reverse. This is what "changing relationships" refers to.

In this topic **capital** means physical and technological resources, not money. Money pays for capital, but it is not itself a factor that produces goods.

## Capital-intensive and labour-intensive activities

- A **capital-intensive** activity uses a high proportion of capital relative to labour, so spending on machinery and technology makes up a large part of costs.
- A **labour-intensive** activity uses a high proportion of labour relative to capital, so wages make up a large part of costs.

You judge which description fits from the **balance of costs**, not from the size of the business. A huge consultancy firm can be labour-intensive; a small automated print shop can be capital-intensive.

### Worked example 1: measuring the balance

| | Sundara Spa | Orbis Bottling |
|---|---|---|
| Total yearly costs | 300,000 | 600,000 |
| Wages | 210,000 | 45,000 |
| Equipment and machinery costs | 30,000 | 330,000 |

Wages as a share of costs: Sundara 210,000 ÷ 300,000 × 100 = **70%**; Orbis 45,000 ÷ 600,000 × 100 = **7.5%**.
Equipment as a share of costs: Sundara 30,000 ÷ 300,000 × 100 = **10%**; Orbis 330,000 ÷ 600,000 × 100 = **55%**.

Sundara is labour-intensive: its service is personal treatments, which people must provide. Orbis is capital-intensive: filling and capping bottles is repetitive and suits machines. The specification gives no formula or cut-off percentage for this, so use cost shares to compare, and explain why the activity suits that mix.

## Why the relationships change

| Driver | How it shifts the mix | Example |
|---|---|---|
| New technology | Machines and software can do tasks people did, so capital replaces labour | Self-service checkouts in supermarkets |
| Rising wages | Labour becomes dearer relative to machines, so capital becomes more attractive | A packing firm automates after a minimum wage rise |
| Shortage of skilled labour | If workers cannot be found, capital fills the gap | A farm buys harvesting machinery when seasonal pickers are scarce |
| Growth in scale | Larger output spreads the high fixed cost of machinery over more units | A growing bakery replaces hand mixing with an industrial mixer |
| Cost and availability of land | Expensive sites push businesses to use land more intensively or move | A retailer closes city-centre shops and sells online from a cheaper warehouse site |
| Customer expectations | Some customers pay more for a personal or handmade product, keeping labour central | A tailor keeps hand finishing as a selling point |

Enterprise changes too. When capital replaces labour, the owner commits large sums up front and takes on costs that must be paid whatever sales are, so the risk carried by the entrepreneur rises.

### Worked example 2: is a machine cheaper than people?

Harrowgate Farm employs 14 seasonal fruit pickers for a 10-week harvest at 400 a week each. A harvesting machine costs 85,000 and lasts 5 years, so its cost spread across its life is 85,000 ÷ 5 = 17,000 a year. Fuel and maintenance cost 9,000 a year, and it needs 3 operators for the 10 weeks at 450 a week each.

- Labour-intensive method: 14 × 10 × 400 = **56,000** a year.
- Capital-intensive method: 17,000 + 9,000 + (3 × 10 × 450 = 13,500) = **39,500** a year.
- Yearly saving from the machine: 56,000 − 39,500 = **16,500**.

**At what wage would the methods cost the same?** The capital-intensive method costs 39,500, and hand picking needs 14 × 10 = 140 worker-weeks. 39,500 ÷ 140 = **282.14** a week. If pickers' wages were below about 282, hand picking would be cheaper. This shows the core idea of "changing relationships": the cheaper mix depends on the **relative** cost of labour and capital, and it shifts when wages, machine prices or technology change.

**Beyond the numbers:** the machine may damage delicate fruit, lowering quality; it must be paid for even in a poor harvest; and 11 seasonal jobs disappear, which may affect the local community. On the other hand, it does not depend on finding 14 workers each year.

## Impact of each type of activity

| | Capital-intensive | Labour-intensive |
|---|---|---|
| Cost structure | High fixed costs; low cost per unit at high output | Mostly variable wage costs; cost per unit changes less with output |
| Output | High volume, consistent | Lower volume, more varied or personal |
| Flexibility | Hard to change products or cut costs if demand falls | Easier to adjust staffing and tasks |
| Quality | Consistent, but a fault repeats | Can be tailored; depends on individual skill |
| Risk to the owner | Higher: large investment and fixed costs | Lower fixed costs; exposed to wage rises and staff shortages |
| Workforce | Fewer, often more technically skilled staff | More staff; skills range widely |

Labour-intensive activities often suit services, handmade or custom products, and places where wages are low relative to machinery. Capital-intensive activities suit standardised products in large volumes, and places where labour is costly or scarce.

## Annotated model answer

*Evaluate whether Harrowgate Farm should replace its seasonal pickers with a harvesting machine.*

> The machine would cut yearly harvesting costs from 56,000 to 39,500, a saving of 16,500, and the farm would no longer depend on recruiting 14 pickers every year. However, it costs 85,000 up front, which is a large risk for a farm if harvests are poor, because the machine must still be paid for. Machines can also bruise fruit, so if the farm sells high-quality fruit to customers who pay a premium, hand picking may protect its prices. The saving also depends on wages: the machine is only cheaper while pickers earn more than about 282 a week. I would recommend the machine if the farm grows fruit for processing, where appearance matters less, but keep hand picking for premium fruit.

Why it works: it uses the calculated saving and the break-even wage, names costs and risks on both sides, and gives a conditional judgement tied to the type of product.

## Exam technique

- **Define** questions: use the word "proportion" or "relative to" in definitions of capital- and labour-intensive.
- **Calculate**: cost shares (cost ÷ total × 100) and comparisons of the total cost of two methods.
- **Explain** a change in the mix: the driver (wages rise) → the response (more machinery) → the effect (lower unit cost, fewer jobs).
- **Evaluate**: costs, quality, flexibility, risk and workforce effects, then a judgement.

## Common mistakes

- Treating capital as money.
- Saying a business is capital-intensive because it is big.
- Ignoring enterprise: changes in the mix change the risk the owner carries.
- Recommending automation on cost alone, without quality, flexibility or risk.
- Forgetting that land includes natural resources, not only buildings.
