These questions cover **3.1.3.6 Bond polarity** of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Chemistry (9620) specification, Version 5.3. It is International AS content, examined in Unit 1. Section A has short recall and application questions; Section B has longer, structured questions. Use these Pauling electronegativity values where needed: H 2.1, C 2.5, N 3.0, O 3.5, F 4.0, Na 0.9, Si 1.8, S 2.5, Cl 3.0. If a point is unfamiliar, read the [bond polarity study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-1-3-6/) or the [bond polarity revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-1-3-6-revision-notes/) first.

## Questions

### Section A

**1.** State what is meant by a polar covalent bond. **[2]**

**2.** Explain why electronegativity increases from carbon to fluorine across Period 2. **[2]**

**3.** Copy each bond and add partial charges.

**(a)** C=O **[1]**

**(b)** N–H **[1]**

**(c)** S–Cl **[1]**

**4.** Put the bonds C–H, N–H, O–H and F–H in order of increasing polarity. Show how you decided. **[2]**

**5.** State whether the bond in each molecule is polar, and explain your answer.

**(a)** H₂ **[1]**

**(b)** HCl **[1]**

**6.** From the list CF₄, CHF₃, SiF₄ and CH₃F, identify the two molecules that have a permanent dipole. **[2]**

### Section B

**7.** Ammonia, NH₃, and nitrogen trifluoride, NF₃, are both pyramidal.

**(a)** State the partial charge on the nitrogen atom in each molecule. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain why both molecules have a permanent dipole. **[3]**

**8.** Xenon tetrafluoride, XeF₄, and sulfur tetrafluoride, SF₄, each have four fluorine atoms bonded to a central atom.

**(a)** Name the shape of each molecule. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain which of the two molecules has a permanent dipole. **[4]**

**9.** 1,2-Dichloroethene, ClCH=CHCl, is a flat molecule with bond angles of about 120° around each carbon. It exists as two isomers. In isomer A the two chlorine atoms are on the same side of the double bond; in isomer B they are on opposite sides.

**(a)** Explain why isomer A has a permanent dipole. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain why isomer B has no permanent dipole, even though it contains polar bonds. **[2]**

**10.** A student runs a thin stream of liquid from a burette past a charged plastic rod.

**(a)** Describe what the student would see with water and with hexane, and explain each observation. **[3]**

**(b)** The stream of water bends towards the rod whether the rod is positively or negatively charged. Explain why. **[2]**

**11.** This question compares sodium chloride, hydrogen chloride and chlorine.

**(a)** Calculate the electronegativity difference for the bond in each substance and use the values to describe the type of bonding in each. **[3]**

**(b)** Explain why the electron distribution in the H–Cl bond is unsymmetrical. **[2]**

**12.** The chloromethanes are CH₄, CH₃Cl, CH₂Cl₂, CHCl₃ and CCl₄. All are tetrahedral or close to tetrahedral.

**(a)** Draw CHCl₃, showing partial charges on the carbon and chlorine atoms. **[1]**

**(b)** Identify which of the five molecules have a permanent dipole, and explain why CCl₄ does not but CHCl₃ does. **[4]**

## Answers

### Section A

**1.** A covalent bond between atoms with different electronegativities [1], in which the shared pair of electrons is unevenly distributed, giving δ+ and δ− ends [1] **[2]**

**2.** The nuclear charge increases across the period while the shielding stays about the same [1]; the atoms get smaller, so the bonding pair is attracted more strongly [1] **[2]**

**3. (a)** C δ+, O δ− [1]

**(b)** N δ−, H δ+ [1]

**(c)** S δ+, Cl δ− [1] **[3]**

**4.** Differences: C–H 0.4, N–H 0.9, O–H 1.4, F–H 1.9 [1]; order of increasing polarity: **C–H < N–H < O–H < F–H** [1] **[2]**

**5. (a)** Not polar: both atoms are the same, so they have equal electronegativity and share the pair equally [1]

**(b)** Polar: chlorine is more electronegative than hydrogen, so the pair is pulled towards chlorine [1] **[2]**

**6.** CHF₃ [1] and CH₃F [1] **[2]**

*Examiner insight:* CF₄ and SiF₄ have four identical outer atoms arranged tetrahedrally, so their bond dipoles cancel.

### Section B

**7. (a)** NH₃: nitrogen is δ− [1]; NF₃: nitrogen is δ+ [1]

**(b)** The N–H and N–F bonds are polar because the atoms differ in electronegativity [1]; each molecule is pyramidal because of the lone pair on nitrogen [1]; the three bond dipoles all point on the same side of the nitrogen, so they do not cancel [1] **[5]**

*Examiner insight:* The direction of the dipole is opposite in the two molecules, but both are polar; the mark is for the shape-based reason, not the direction.

**8. (a)** XeF₄: square planar [1]; SF₄: see-saw [1]

**(b)** All the Xe–F and S–F bonds are polar [1]; in XeF₄ the four F atoms are in a square, so the dipoles are equal and opposite in pairs and cancel [1]; in SF₄ the lone pair makes the shape unsymmetrical [1], so the dipoles do not cancel and **SF₄** has a permanent dipole [1] **[6]**

**9. (a)** The C–Cl bonds are polar (Cl δ−) [1]; both C–Cl dipoles point to the same side of the molecule, so they do not cancel [1]

**(b)** The two C–Cl dipoles are equal and point in opposite directions [1]; so they cancel, and the C–H dipoles cancel in the same way [1] **[4]**

**10. (a)** Water: the stream is deflected towards the rod [1], because water molecules have a permanent dipole [1]; hexane: no noticeable deflection, because hexane molecules have no permanent dipole [1]

**(b)** The polar molecules turn so that their oppositely charged end faces the rod [1]; the attraction to that end is then greater than the repulsion from the other end, which is further away [1] **[5]**

**11. (a)** NaCl: 3.0 − 0.9 = 2.1; HCl: 3.0 − 2.1 = 0.9; Cl₂: 0 [1]; NaCl ionic (very large difference) and Cl₂ non-polar covalent (no difference) [1]; HCl polar covalent (intermediate difference) [1]

**(b)** Chlorine is more electronegative than hydrogen [1]; so it attracts the shared pair more strongly and the pair is closer to chlorine, making Cl δ− and H δ+ [1] **[5]**

**12. (a)** Tetrahedral CHCl₃ with C δ+ and each Cl δ− [1]

**(b)** CH₃Cl, CH₂Cl₂ and CHCl₃ have permanent dipoles; CH₄ and CCl₄ do not [1]; in CCl₄ the four C–Cl dipoles are identical and arranged symmetrically, so they cancel [1]; in CHCl₃ one Cl is replaced by H [1]; the C–H dipole is much smaller than a C–Cl dipole, so the dipoles no longer cancel [1] **[5]**

*Examiner insight:* CH₄ is non-polar because C–H bonds are almost non-polar and the shape is symmetrical; say both reasons if asked.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Defining a polar bond without mentioning electronegativity difference.
- Reversing partial charges, especially on nitrogen.
- Concluding a molecule is polar just because its bonds are polar, or non-polar just because it has no lone pairs.
- Ignoring the effect of different outer atoms on symmetry.
- Saying dipoles cancel without naming the shape.
