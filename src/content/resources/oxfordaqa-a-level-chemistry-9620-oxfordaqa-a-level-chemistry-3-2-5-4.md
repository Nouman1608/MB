This guide goes deeper into **3.2.5.4 Formation of coloured ions**, part of section 3.2.5 Transition metals in the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Chemistry (9620) specification, Version 5.3. ## What the specification asks for

- Transition metal ions can be identified by their colour.
- Colour arises when some wavelengths of visible light are absorbed and the remaining wavelengths are transmitted or reflected.
- d electrons move from the ground state to an excited state when light is absorbed.
- The energy difference between the ground state and the excited state is given by ΔE = hν = hc/λ.
- Changes in oxidation state, co-ordination number and ligand alter ΔE, and this leads to a change in colour.
- The absorption of visible light is used in spectroscopy.
- A simple colorimeter can be used to determine the concentration of coloured ions in solution.

## Where the colour comes from

White light contains all visible wavelengths, from about 400 nm (violet) to about 700 nm (red). When it passes through a solution of a transition metal complex, some wavelengths are absorbed. The eye sees the mixture of wavelengths that are **transmitted** (for a solution) or **reflected** (for a solid). So the colour you see is **not** the colour absorbed; it is roughly the complementary colour.

| Light mainly absorbed | Colour seen |
|---|---|
| red | blue-green |
| orange | blue |
| yellow | violet |
| green | purple |
| blue | orange |
| violet | yellow |

Hexaaquacopper(II) ions absorb mainly at the red–orange end of the spectrum, so the solution looks pale blue.

### Why a d electron can absorb visible light

In an isolated ion, the five 3d orbitals have the same energy. When ligands bond to the ion, the d orbitals are no longer all at the same energy: some are raised above others. A d electron in a lower-energy orbital (the **ground state**) can absorb a photon and move to a higher-energy d orbital (an **excited state**). Only a photon whose energy exactly matches the gap is absorbed:

**ΔE = hν = hc/λ**

- ΔE = energy gap, in J (for one ion)
- h = Planck constant, 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J s
- ν = frequency of the light absorbed, in Hz (s⁻¹)
- c = speed of light, 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹
- λ = wavelength absorbed, in m

For many complexes the gap corresponds to visible light, so the ion is coloured. A larger ΔE means a higher frequency and a **shorter** wavelength is absorbed.

### Ions that are not coloured

A d-to-d transition needs at least one d electron and at least one space in a higher d orbital. Ions with an empty 3d sub-level, such as Sc³⁺ and Ti⁴⁺ (3d⁰), or a full one, such as Zn²⁺ (3d¹⁰), cannot absorb visible light in this way, so their compounds are white and their solutions colourless. This is why the incomplete d sub-level is linked to colour in 3.2.5.1.

## Worked example 1: from wavelength to energy

A complex ion absorbs most strongly at 500 nm. Calculate ΔE for one ion and for one mole of ions. (h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J s; c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹; L = 6.02 × 10²³ mol⁻¹.)

```
λ  = 500 nm = 500 × 10⁻⁹ m = 5.00 × 10⁻⁷ m
ΔE = hc/λ = (6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ × 3.00 × 10⁸) ÷ 5.00 × 10⁻⁷
   = 3.98 × 10⁻¹⁹ J            (one ion)
per mole = 3.978 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 6.02 × 10²³ = 2.39 × 10⁵ J mol⁻¹
         = 239 kJ mol⁻¹
```

Green light (about 500 nm) is absorbed, so the solution would look purple.

## Worked example 2: from energy to wavelength

A transition in a different complex has ΔE = 180 kJ mol⁻¹. Find the wavelength absorbed.

```
ΔE per ion = 180 × 1000 ÷ 6.02 × 10²³ = 2.99 × 10⁻¹⁹ J
λ = hc/ΔE = (6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ × 3.00 × 10⁸) ÷ 2.990 × 10⁻¹⁹
  = 6.65 × 10⁻⁷ m = 665 nm
```

The two conversions that cost marks: kJ to J (× 1000), and per mole to per ion (÷ L). Do both before using hc/λ.

## What changes the colour

ΔE depends on the metal ion, its oxidation state, the ligands and the shape. Change any of these and a different wavelength is absorbed, so the colour changes. The specification names three changes.

| Change | Example | Colour change |
|---|---|---|
| **Ligand** (co-ordination number unchanged) | [Cu(H₂O)₆]²⁺ → [Cu(NH₃)₄(H₂O)₂]²⁺ with excess ammonia | pale blue → deep blue |
| **Ligand and co-ordination number** | [Cu(H₂O)₆]²⁺ → [CuCl₄]²⁻ with concentrated HCl | blue → yellow (green while both are present) |
| **Ligand and co-ordination number** | [Co(H₂O)₆]²⁺ → [CoCl₄]²⁻ | pink → blue |
| **Oxidation state** (same ligand) | [Fe(H₂O)₆]²⁺ → [Fe(H₂O)₆]³⁺ | pale green → yellow-brown in a normal laboratory solution |
| **Oxidation state** | V³⁺(aq) → V²⁺(aq) | green → violet |

In the ammonia example, ammonia ligands produce a larger ΔE than water ligands. Absorption moves to a shorter wavelength, and the colour seen moves with it. In the chloride examples, both the ligand and the shape change (octahedral to tetrahedral), so ΔE changes.

### Worked example 3: explaining a colour change

*Question: Explain why adding concentrated hydrochloric acid to aqueous copper(II) sulfate changes the colour of the solution. (3 marks)*

> The water ligands are replaced by chloride ligands, forming [CuCl₄]²⁻ [1]. The co-ordination number changes from 6 to 4 (octahedral to tetrahedral), so the energy gap ΔE between the d orbitals changes [1]. Light of a different wavelength (frequency) is absorbed, so the colour transmitted, and therefore seen, changes [1].

Each mark is a link in a chain: what changes in the complex, what happens to ΔE, what happens to the light.

## Spectroscopy and the colorimeter

Because each complex absorbs particular wavelengths, measuring absorption identifies and measures coloured ions. A visible spectrometer plots absorbance against wavelength; the peak (λmax) is the wavelength absorbed most strongly.

A **simple colorimeter** shines light of one colour through a sample and measures how much is absorbed. For a given complex in the same cell, absorbance increases with concentration, so it can be used to find an unknown concentration:

1. Choose a **filter** that passes the colour of light the solution absorbs most (the complementary colour to the one you see).
2. **Zero** the colorimeter using a cuvette of the solvent (or of the reagent mixture without the metal ion).
3. Make **standard solutions** of known concentration and measure the absorbance of each.
4. Plot a **calibration graph** of absorbance against concentration.
5. Measure the absorbance of the unknown, treated in exactly the same way, and read its concentration from the graph.

If an ion is only faintly coloured, add a ligand that forms a more intensely coloured complex: for example, excess ammonia turns pale blue Cu²⁺(aq) into deep blue [Cu(NH₃)₄(H₂O)₂]²⁺. The standards must be treated with the same ligand.

### Worked example 4: copper in an alloy

A 0.500 g sample of a copper alloy is dissolved in acid, excess aqueous ammonia is added, and the solution is made up to 250 cm³. Its absorbance is 0.30. Standards treated in the same way give a straight line through the origin with gradient 12.0 dm³ mol⁻¹. Calculate the percentage by mass of copper. (Ar Cu = 63.5)

```
[Cu²⁺] = 0.30 ÷ 12.0 = 0.0250 mol dm⁻³
n(Cu)  = 0.0250 × 250 ÷ 1000 = 6.25 × 10⁻³ mol
mass   = 6.25 × 10⁻³ × 63.5 = 0.397 g
% Cu   = 0.397 ÷ 0.500 × 100 = 79.4 %
```

Check that the unknown's absorbance lies within the range of the standards; reading beyond the last standard assumes the line stays straight.

## Common mistakes

- Saying the solution is blue "because it absorbs blue light". It absorbs other colours and transmits blue.
- Saying light is "emitted" or that electrons "fall back and give out colour". The colour is due to absorption.
- Leaving λ in nm in hc/λ, or forgetting to divide by L for one ion.
- Explaining a colour change with "the ligand changes" and stopping there. The mark scheme wants the change in ΔE and in the wavelength absorbed.
- Choosing a colorimeter filter the same colour as the solution.
- Not zeroing with a blank, or treating the standards differently from the unknown.

## Exam technique for OxfordAQA command words

- **Explain** (colour): absorption of some visible wavelengths, d electron promoted from ground to excited state, remaining wavelengths transmitted or reflected.
- **Explain** (colour change): name what changes (ligand, co-ordination number or oxidation state), then ΔE, then the wavelength absorbed.
- **Calculate**: write ΔE = hc/λ or hν, convert units visibly, give 3 significant figures and units.
- **Describe** (colorimetry): filter, blank, standards, calibration graph, read off.
