## Definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Absolute poverty | Income below the level needed for basic necessities, fixed in real terms |
| Relative poverty | Income below a set share (often 60%) of the median in that society |
| Poverty gap | How far below the line a household's income lies |
| Progressive tax | Takes a rising percentage of income as income rises |
| Regressive tax | Takes a falling percentage of income as income rises |
| Means-tested benefit | Paid only below an income or asset threshold |
| Universal benefit | Paid to a whole category regardless of income |
| Poverty trap | Weak gain from higher earnings because benefits are withdrawn and tax is paid |
| Unemployment trap | Weak gain from taking a job at all |
| Positive v normative | A testable claim v a value judgement about what ought to be |

## Measuring poverty: method in steps

1. Put incomes in order and find the median (mean of the middle two if the number is even).
2. Relative line = chosen percentage x median.
3. Count households below the line, then divide by the total for the headcount percentage.
4. For the absolute line, use the fixed cost of the basket and ignore the median.
5. After any change, recompute both lines separately.

**Worked reminder.** Households have incomes of 900, 1,400, 2,000, 2,600 and 4,100. Median = 2,000; 60% line = 1,200; one household below it, so 20%. If all incomes rise 10%, the median is 2,200, the line is 1,320 and the lowest household (990) is still below it: relative poverty is still 20%. With an absolute line of 1,000, the lowest household was below it before (900) and after (990), so absolute poverty is unchanged here, but a 15% rise would take it to 1,035 and out.

**Key distinction:** equal-percentage growth can change the absolute line's headcount but leaves the relative headcount unchanged, unless low incomes grow faster or slower than the median.

## Causes and effects: recall grid

| Group | Points |
|---|---|
| Labour market causes | Unemployment, low pay, low skills, structural change, discrimination |
| Personal and household causes | Ill-health, disability, age, lone-parent or large households, low wealth |
| Policy and market causes | Benefits below the relative line or poorly uprated, under-consumption of merit goods, imperfect information |
| Effects on people | Poor health, lower attainment, exclusion, reduced life chances |
| Effects on the economy | Lower human capital and productivity, lost output, higher welfare cost, weak social mobility, wasted talent |
| Cycle of deprivation | Poor children, weaker education, low pay, poor adults, poor children again |

## Policies: what each does and its trade-offs

| Policy | How it helps | Costs and evaluation |
|---|---|---|
| Progressive income tax | Narrows post-tax gaps | Work incentives, avoidance, depends on rates and thresholds |
| Wealth and inheritance taxes | Tackles unequal assets | Avoidance, capital mobility, valuation difficulty, small yield |
| Cutting taxes on necessities | Helps poor households most | Costly in revenue, benefits better-off too |
| Means-tested benefits | Target the poor, lower cost | Poverty trap, stigma, non-take-up, administration |
| Universal benefits | Full coverage, simple, political support | High cost, much goes to the non-poor |
| In-work benefits | Raise returns to low-paid work | Cost, may let wages stay low |
| Minimum wage | Raises pay of those in work | Job losses if demand elastic; may raise employment under monopsony |
| Free or subsidised education, health, housing | In-kind help, merit goods | Opportunity cost, quality and rationing issues |
| Education and training | Raise productivity and pay | Slow, uncertain, high cost |
| Regional and employment policy | Creates jobs where unemployment is concentrated | Slow, may be inefficient, crowding out |
| Basic income or negative income tax | Guaranteed floor, simpler | High cost or low payment, work-incentive debate |

## Formulae and quick calculations

| Item | Method |
|---|---|
| Relative line | Share x median |
| Headcount rate | Number below line / total x 100 |
| Average tax rate | Tax / income x 100 |
| Effective marginal tax rate | Tax rate + benefit withdrawal rate on the extra pound |
| Take-up rate | Claimants / eligible households |
| Target efficiency | Money reaching the poor / total spending |
| Benefit v median | Benefit / median x 100 |

**Tax reminder.** Tax-free up to 1,200, 30% above. Income 3,000: tax = 30% x 1,800 = 540, average rate 18%. Income 6,000: tax = 30% x 4,800 = 1,440, average rate 24%. Because the percentage taken climbs, the tax is progressive.

**Trap reminder.** Tax 25% plus benefit withdrawal 55% gives an effective marginal tax rate of 80%: the household keeps 20p of each extra pound.

## Must-know distinctions

- Absolute v relative poverty: fixed real standard v share of median.
- Progressive v regressive: look at the percentage of income, not the amount of money.
- Means-tested v universal: targeting and cost against take-up and simplicity.
- Equality of opportunity v outcome: the first is about starting points, the second about final incomes.
- Positive v normative: "the policy raises employment" is testable; "the policy is fair" is a value judgement.
- Short run v long run: cash transfers help at once; education works slowly but may reduce dependency.

## Moral and political perspectives

| Perspective | Argument | Policy leaning |
|---|---|---|
| Utilitarian | Extra income gives more benefit to the poor, so redistribution raises total welfare | Progressive tax and transfers |
| Rawlsian | Design society as if you did not know your place in it, so protect the worst off | Strong safety net |
| Libertarian | People own what they earn; coercive transfers are wrong | Low tax, voluntary charity |
| Market-based | Growth and incentives reduce poverty most | Lower tax, targeted help, supply-side |
| Interventionist | Markets generate and entrench poverty | Higher tax, universal provision |

## Evaluation tools

- Compare absolute and relative aims: a policy may help one and not the other.
- Time horizon: transfers act now, supply-side policies later.
- Scale and elasticity: how large is the work-effort response, and who bears the cost?
- Alternatives and opportunity cost: what else could the money do?
- Conclude with a stated judgement tied to a stated objective, and name the value judgement behind it.

## Quick self-test

1. State what makes poverty "relative".
2. Eight households have incomes of 500, 900, 1,300, 1,500, 2,300, 2,500, 3,000 and 4,000. Find the 60% median line and the percentage below it.
3. All eight incomes rise 15%, prices are unchanged and the absolute line is 1,000. Find the percentage below each line after the rise.
4. Tax is 25% and a benefit is withdrawn at 55p per pound earned. How much of an extra 100 earned does the household keep?
5. Find the average tax rate on an income of 3,000 when income is untaxed to 1,200 and taxed at 30% above.
6. A universal payment of 250 goes to 40,000 households, of which 8,000 are poor. State the cost and the percentage reaching poor households.
7. Give one reason why low take-up weakens a means-tested benefit.
8. Why might a minimum wage raise employment?
9. A benefit of 800 is half the median of 1,600. The median rises 10% and the benefit 5%. What is the benefit as a percentage of the median?
10. Is "redistribution is fair" positive or normative?

### Answers

1. Income is low compared with a share (such as 60%) of the median in the same society.
2. Median = (1,500 + 2,300) / 2 = 1,900; line = 1,140; incomes 500 and 900 are below it: 2 of 8 = 25%.
3. New median = 2,185, so the relative line is 1,311. Incomes of 575 and 1,035 are below it: 25%. Absolute line 1,000: only 575 is below it: 1 of 8 = 12.5%.
4. Withdrawal plus tax = 80%, so the household keeps 20.
5. Tax = 30% x 1,800 = 540; 540 / 3,000 = 18%.
6. Cost = 40,000 x 250 = 10m; poor households receive 8,000 x 250 = 2m, which is 20%.
7. Eligible poor households that do not claim receive no help, so poverty persists.
8. If one employer holds wage-setting power, a minimum between the current wage and the competitive wage can raise both pay and the number hired.
9. Median = 1,760; benefit = 840; 840 / 1,760 = 47.7%. Relative poverty has deepened.
10. Normative, because it is a value judgement.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using the mean instead of the median for a relative line.
- Counting households on the line as below it.
- Assuming that growth must cut relative poverty.
- Writing "progressive" when describing the amount of tax rather than the percentage of income.
- Ignoring benefit withdrawal when explaining incentives.
- Listing causes of poverty without a chain of reasoning to a lower income.
- Giving policies one-sided: every policy needs a cost, a time lag or an incentive effect.
- Stating a political view without showing the economic mechanism.
- Ending without a judgement tied to the objective.
