This guide covers **section 7.1 Utility** of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Economics 9708 syllabus for examination in 2026, 2027 and 2028. It is A Level content, assessed in Papers 3 and 4. The syllabus lists five statements: the "definition and calculation of total utility and marginal utility", "diminishing marginal utility", the "equi-marginal principle", the "derivation of an individual demand curve", and the "limitations of marginal utility theory and its assumptions of rational behaviour".

The broader [utility, indifference curves and budget lines study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-economics-utility-indifference-curves-and-budget-lines/) introduces each idea with one example. This page goes further: it shows how to fill gaps in utility tables, proves why the equi-marginal rule maximises utility, and derives two points on a demand curve by re-solving the consumer's choice after a price change. All consumers, goods and figures are invented, and utility is measured in invented units called utils.

## 7.1.1 Total and marginal utility: calculating in both directions

**Utility** is the satisfaction a consumer gains from consuming a good or service. **Total utility (TU)** is the satisfaction from all the units consumed in a period. **Marginal utility (MU)** is the change in total utility from consuming one more unit:

MU = change in TU ÷ change in quantity

Going the other way, TU is the running total of the marginal utilities: TU of n units = MU of unit 1 + MU of unit 2 + … + MU of unit n.

**Worked example 1: completing a table.** A student drinks cups of tea in a day. Some values are given and the rest must be found.

| Cups | TU (given or found) | MU (given or found) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 30 (given) | 30 − 0 = **30** |
| 2 | 30 + 24 = **54** | 24 (given) |
| 3 | 72 (given) | 72 − 54 = **18** |
| 4 | 72 + 12 = **84** | 12 (given) |
| 5 | 90 (given) | 90 − 84 = **6** |
| 6 | 90 + 0 = **90** | 0 (given) |
| 7 | 84 (given) | 84 − 90 = **−6** |

Three points to state when you interpret a table like this:

- MU falls with each cup, which is diminishing marginal utility.
- TU rises while MU is positive, is at its maximum when MU is zero (here at 5 and 6 cups), and falls once MU is negative.
- A consumer who had to pay for tea would stop well before MU reaches zero, because each cup must be worth at least what it costs.

## 7.1.2 Why marginal utility diminishes

As a consumer has more of a good within a period, each extra unit satisfies a less urgent want. The first glass of water on a hot day relieves thirst; the fifth adds little. Diminishing marginal utility is the reason a consumer's willingness to pay for extra units falls, which is the basis for a downward-sloping demand curve. Note the time period: diminishing MU applies within a given period, not across a lifetime.

## 7.1.3 The equi-marginal principle

A consumer with a fixed income who wants to maximise total utility should arrange spending so that the marginal utility per unit of money is equal across all goods:

MU of X ÷ price of X = MU of Y ÷ price of Y

**Why it works.** Suppose the last unit of money spent on X gives 8 utils and the last unit of money spent on Y gives 5 utils. Moving one unit of money from Y to X loses about 5 utils and gains about 8, so total utility rises. Switching continues to pay until diminishing MU brings the two ratios together. When they are equal, no switch can raise total utility.

**Worked example 2: finding the best bundle.** A consumer has 22 to spend on good X (price 2) and good Y (price 4).

| Unit | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| MU of X | 20 | 16 | 12 | 10 | 8 | 6 | 4 |
| MU of X ÷ 2 | 10 | 8 | 6 | 5 | 4 | 3 | 2 |
| MU of Y | 48 | 40 | 32 | 24 | 16 | 8 | |
| MU of Y ÷ 4 | 12 | 10 | 8 | 6 | 4 | 2 | |

Method: buy units in order of the highest MU per unit of money until the income is spent. The order is Y1 (12), X1 (10), Y2 (10), X2 (8), Y3 (8), X3 (6), Y4 (6). That is 3 units of X and 4 units of Y, costing 3 × 2 + 4 × 4 = 22.

- At this bundle, the last units give 6 utils per unit of money for both goods, so the equi-marginal condition holds.
- TU = (20 + 16 + 12) + (48 + 40 + 32 + 24) = 48 + 144 = **192 utils**.
- Check against another affordable bundle: 5 X and 3 Y also costs 22, but gives (20 + 16 + 12 + 10 + 8) + (48 + 40 + 32) = 66 + 120 = 186 utils, which is less.

## 7.1.4 Deriving an individual demand curve from a price change

A demand curve shows the quantity bought at each price. Utility theory produces it by re-solving the consumer's choice at a new price.

**Worked example 3.** Keep the consumer above (income 22, price of Y 4), but let the price of X fall from 2 to 1. MU per unit of money for X now equals its MU: 20, 16, 12, 10, 8, 6, 4.

- New order: X1 (20), X2 (16), Y1 (12), X3 (12), X4 (10), Y2 (10), X5 (8), Y3 (8), X6 (6), Y4 (6).
- Spending: 6 units of X at 1 and 4 units of Y at 4, so 6 + 16 = 22.
- The last units of each give 6 utils per unit of money, so the condition holds again.

| Price of X | Quantity of X demanded |
|---|---|
| 2 | 3 |
| 1 | 6 |

Plotting these points gives a downward-sloping individual demand curve. The reasoning to write: a fall in the price of X raises the MU per unit of money of every unit of X, so the ratio for X now exceeds that for Y at the old bundle. The consumer buys more X until diminishing MU brings the ratios back into equality. The fall in price makes it worthwhile to buy units with lower marginal utility.

## 7.1.5 Limitations and the assumptions of rational behaviour

Marginal utility theory assumes that the consumer:

- aims to maximise total utility from a given income
- knows the prices and the satisfaction each unit will give
- has consistent preferences that do not change during the period
- can measure utility in units and compare it across goods
- faces a constant marginal utility of money

Each assumption gives a limitation to evaluate:

| Limitation | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Utility cannot be measured | Utils are invented; no one can say a cup of tea gives exactly 18 |
| Information is imperfect | Consumers often do not know how much they will enjoy a new product |
| Habit and impulse | Many purchases are routine or spontaneous rather than calculated |
| Social influences | Fashion, advertising and what others buy change preferences |
| Addiction | For some goods, consuming more raises the desire for more |
| Interdependent goods | The MU of a phone case depends on owning a phone, which the theory treats separately |
| Marginal utility of money not constant | A large price change alters how much each unit of money is worth to the consumer |

**Evaluation that earns credit:** the theory is unrealistic in its assumptions, but its main prediction, that consumers buy more when price falls, is widely observed. Its value lies in explaining why demand slopes downward, not in calculating real choices.

## Common mistakes

- Saying total utility falls when marginal utility falls. TU still rises while MU is positive.
- Comparing MU instead of MU per unit of money when prices differ.
- Applying the equi-marginal rule without checking that the bundle uses the whole income.
- Forgetting the time period when explaining diminishing MU.
- Listing limitations without saying how each one weakens the prediction.

## Exam technique for 9708 command words

- **Calculate:** show TU or MU workings in a table; keep units of utils.
- **Explain:** for the equi-marginal principle, give the switching argument, not just the formula.
- **Assess** and **Evaluate:** in Paper 4 essays, which are unstructured, weigh the assumptions against the theory's usefulness and reach a judgement.

Paper 3 is multiple choice, so practise finding the utility-maximising bundle quickly from a table.
