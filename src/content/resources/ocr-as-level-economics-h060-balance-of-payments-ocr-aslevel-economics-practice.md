## Section A: Short questions

**1.** A home insurance company sells a policy to a firm based abroad. In which part of the home country's current account is this recorded? **[1]**

A: Trade in goods, as a credit

B: Trade in services, as a credit

C: Primary income, as a credit

D: Secondary income, as a credit

**2.** Which **one** of the following is recorded as a secondary income debit for the home country? **[1]**

A: Interest paid on government bonds held by foreigners

B: Imports of oil

C: Money sent abroad by migrant workers living in the home country

D: A foreign firm buying a home company

**3.** A country's goods balance is −34 bn, its services balance is +21 bn, its primary income balance is −6 bn and its secondary income balance is −9 bn. Which **one** is its current account balance? **[1]**

A: −13 bn

B: −28 bn

C: −40 bn

D: +28 bn

**4.** Distinguish between primary income and secondary income, giving one example of each. **[4]**

**5.** A country's current account deficit is 45 bn and its GDP is 1,500 bn. Calculate the deficit as a percentage of GDP. **[2]**

**6.** Explain why a foreign firm building a factory in a country does not, at the time it is built, affect that country's current account balance. **[2]**

## Section B: Structured and extended questions

**7.** The table shows invented data for Country Ferro, in bn.

| Component | Year 1 credits | Year 1 debits | Year 2 credits | Year 2 debits |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Trade in goods | 210 | 236 | 214 | 262 |
| Trade in services | 96 | 71 | 101 | 72 |
| Primary income | 30 | 33 | 31 | 40 |
| Secondary income | 6 | 14 | 6 | 15 |

**(a)** Calculate the balance of trade in goods and services in each year. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the current account balance in each year. **[2]**

**(c)** Ferro's GDP is 1,050 bn in year 2. Calculate the year 2 current account balance as a percentage of GDP, to one decimal place. **[1]**

**(d)** Using the data, explain which component contributed most to the change in the current account between the two years. **[2]**

**8.** In Country Gall, the current account balance is −16 bn. The goods balance is −27 bn, the services balance is +18 bn and the primary income balance is −2 bn. Calculate the secondary income balance. **[2]**

**9.** Over a year, inflation in Country Holt is 7% while inflation in its trading partners is 3%, and Holt's exchange rate does not change.

**(a)** Calculate, to one decimal place, how much dearer Holt's goods have become relative to its partners' goods. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain why the effect of this on Holt's current account depends on price elasticity of demand. **[3]**

**10.** Explain **two** reasons why a government might aim for a sustainable balance of payments position rather than a current account balance of exactly zero. **[4]**

**11.** Country Jessa and Country Kell both have current account deficits of 3% of GDP. Jessa's deficit is financed mainly by foreign direct investment in new factories. Kell's is financed mainly by short-term borrowing to fund a consumer spending boom. Explain which deficit is more likely to be sustainable. **[4]**

**12.** Evaluate the view that a persistent current account deficit is always harmful to an economy. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** B [1]. Insurance is an intangible product sold to a non-resident, so it is an export of services. **[1]**

**2.** C [1]. A one-way transfer with nothing received in return. Option A is a primary income debit; B is trade in goods; D is a financial account item. **[1]**

**3.** B [1]: −34 + 21 − 6 − 9 = −28 bn. **[1]**

**4.** Primary income is income earned by factors of production across borders [1], such as dividends received by a home investor from a foreign company or interest on foreign bonds [1]. Secondary income is a transfer for which nothing is received in return [1], such as government aid to another country or remittances sent by migrant workers [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* The distinction is about whether a factor service is being rewarded, not about whether the money is large or small.

**5.** 45 ÷ 1,500 × 100 [1] = **3.0%** [1]. **[2]**

**6.** The purchase of the factory is an investment in an asset, recorded in the financial account as an inflow [1]. Only the income it later earns for its foreign owner, such as profit sent abroad, enters the current account as a primary income debit [1]. **[2]**

**7. (a)** Year 1: (210 − 236) + (96 − 71) = −26 + 25 = **−1 bn** [1]. Year 2: (214 − 262) + (101 − 72) = −48 + 29 = **−19 bn** [1].

**(b)** Year 1: −1 + (30 − 33) + (6 − 14) = −1 − 3 − 8 = **−12 bn** [1]. Year 2: −19 + (31 − 40) + (6 − 15) = −19 − 9 − 9 = **−37 bn** [1].

**(c)** 37 ÷ 1,050 × 100 = **3.5%** of GDP (a deficit) [1].

**(d)** The current account worsened by 25 bn [1]. The goods balance worsened by 22 bn (from −26 to −48), far more than primary income (−6), secondary income (−1) or services, which improved by 4, so trade in goods contributed most, driven by a 26 bn rise in goods imports [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* Quote the change in each balance; describing only the year 2 levels does not explain the change.

**8.** −16 = −27 + 18 − 2 + secondary income [1], so secondary income = **−5 bn** [1]. **[2]**

**9. (a)** 1.07 ÷ 1.03 − 1 [1] = **3.9%** dearer [1].

**(b)** If demand for Holt's exports is price elastic, foreign buyers switch to cheaper substitutes, so export volumes and revenue fall by proportionately more than the relative price rise [1]. If Holt's residents' demand for imports is price elastic, they switch strongly to the now relatively cheaper imports, so import spending rises [1]. If both demands are price inelastic, volumes change little and the current account may barely change, at least in the short run [1]. **[5]**

**10.** Reason 1: a small deficit can be desirable if it finances imports of capital goods that raise future productive capacity and exports [1], so forcing it to zero could reduce long-run growth [1]. Reason 2: the balance moves with the economic cycle [1], so pursuing exactly zero every year would need frequent policy changes that could destabilise output and employment [1]. Other valid reasons: a surplus is not costless; a deficit financed by stable long-term inflows can be maintained indefinitely. **[4]**

**11.** Jessa's deficit is more likely to be sustainable [1]. Direct investment is long-term and difficult to withdraw quickly, so the financing is stable [1], and new factories raise productive capacity that can later produce exports or replace imports [1]. Kell's short-term borrowing can stop or reverse suddenly, forcing a sharp correction, and consumer spending adds nothing to future capacity while the debt adds to future primary income debits [1]. **[4]**

**12.** Award up to 10 marks across knowledge, application, analysis and evaluation.
- Definition: debits exceed credits on the current account year after year [1].
- Harmful: net exports reduce AD, lowering growth and employment, other things equal [1].
- Harmful: financing needs asset sales or borrowing abroad, so primary income debits rise in later years [1].
- Harmful: downward pressure on the exchange rate raises import prices and inflation [1].
- Harmful: reliance on inflows exposes the country to a sudden loss of confidence [1].
- Not always harmful: residents enjoy a higher level of consumption than domestic output alone would allow [1].
- Not always harmful: if financed by direct investment in productive capacity, future output and exports rise [1].
- Size and persistence relative to GDP matter: a deficit of 1 to 2% of GDP is very different from one of 7% [1].
- The state of the economy matters: a deficit in a fast-growing, fully employed economy is less pressing than one in a recession [1].
- Judgement: not always harmful; it depends chiefly on how it is financed and what the spending is used for, with a reasoned conclusion [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* Strong answers name the condition that decides the verdict. Two lists of "good" and "bad" points with no conclusion stay in the lower levels.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Stopping at the balance of trade when the current account is asked for.
- Dropping the minus sign on a deficit.
- Putting direct investment into the current account.
- Explaining the effect of relative prices without mentioning elasticity.
- Judging a deficit by its money size alone, without GDP.
- Ending the evaluation without a verdict and a reason.
