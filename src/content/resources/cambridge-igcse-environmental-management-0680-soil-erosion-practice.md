## Section A

**1.** Define soil erosion. **[2]**

**2.** Name **two** causes of soil erosion other than deforestation. **[2]**

**3.** State what is meant by desertification. **[1]**

**4.** Describe contour ploughing. **[2]**

**5.** State **two** ways in which bunds reduce soil erosion. **[2]**

**6.** Explain why soil left bare after a harvest is at risk of erosion. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** A study measured soil lost in one year from plots on slopes of different angles. The data are invented for practice.

| Slope angle | Treatment | Soil lost (kg per m²) |
|---|---|---|
| 5° | Bare | 2.0 |
| 10° | Bare | 4.5 |
| 15° | Bare | 8.0 |
| 15° | Terraced | 1.2 |

**(a)** Describe the relationship between slope angle and soil lost from the bare plots. Use data in your answer. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the percentage reduction in soil lost when the 15° slope is terraced. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain why more soil is lost from steeper slopes. **[2]**

**8.** Explain how soil erosion on farmland upstream can affect people living downstream. **[4]**

**9.** Farmers on a flat, dry, windy plain have lost topsoil in dust storms. Suggest and explain **two** strategies to reduce this erosion. **[4]**

**10.** Explain how soil erosion can lead to malnutrition and the displacement of people. **[4]**

**11.** Explain how overgrazing causes soil erosion. **[3]**

**12.** Farmers with little money farm a steep hillside that receives heavy rain. Discuss the benefits and limitations of terracing and of maintaining vegetation cover as ways for them to reduce soil erosion. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** The removal of soil (especially topsoil) by water or wind [1] at a faster rate than new soil forms [1].

**2.** Any two: unsustainable agricultural practices, such as overgrazing or overcultivation [1]; farming on steep slopes [1]; bare soil [1]; wind erosion [1]; water erosion [1].

**3.** Land in dry areas becoming increasingly desert-like as topsoil and vegetation are lost [1].

**4.** Ploughing across a slope, following the contours (lines of equal height), rather than up and down it [1]; each furrow catches water, which reduces surface run-off [1].

**5.** Any two: reduce surface run-off [1]; reduce wind speed [1]; reduce wind erosion [1].

**6.** With no plants, raindrops hit the soil directly and wind reaches the surface [1]; there are no roots to bind the soil, so particles are easily carried away by run-off or wind [1].

**7. (a)** As slope angle increases, soil lost increases [1]; for example, from 2.0 kg per m² at 5° to 8.0 kg per m² at 15°, four times as much [1].

**(b)** (8.0 − 1.2) ÷ 8.0 × 100 [1] = 85% [1].

**(c)** On steeper slopes, gravity makes surface run-off flow faster [1], so the water has more energy and can carry more soil downhill [1].

**8.** Any four:
- Eroded soil is carried into rivers and deposited, causing silting [1].
- The river channel becomes shallower and holds less water, so flooding becomes more likely [1].
- Reservoirs fill with silt, storing less water for drinking, irrigation or hydro-electric power [1].
- Cloudy water harms fish, reducing catches for fishing communities [1].
- Water treatment costs rise because more sediment must be removed [1].

**9.** Two strategies, each with an explanation, 2 marks each:
- **Wind breaks**: lines of trees or hedges planted across the prevailing wind [1] reduce wind speed, so less soil is lifted, and protect crops [1].
- **Maintaining vegetation cover**: keeping crops or cover plants on the land [1]; roots bind the soil and plants shield the surface from wind [1].
- **Bunds**: low banks of earth or stones [1] reduce wind speed at ground level and so reduce wind erosion [1].
- **Addition of organic matter**: manure or crop residue [1] improves soil structure so particles stick together and are not blown away [1].

**10.** Any four, in a logical chain:
- Erosion removes topsoil containing most nutrients and organic matter [1].
- Crops grow less well, so yields fall [1].
- Families that depend on their own harvests have too little food, leading to malnutrition, or famine if shortages are widespread [1].
- When land can no longer support them, people are forced to leave [1], moving to towns or other farmland, which may put pressure on that land too [1].

**11.** Too many animals eat the vegetation faster than it can regrow [1]; the soil is left bare and trampled, with fewer roots to hold it together [1]; rain and wind then remove the loose topsoil [1].

**12.** Levels-based. Credit up to 6 from:
- **Terracing benefits**: very effective on steep slopes because flat steps reduce movement of soil and surface run-off [1]; creates level land that is easier to farm and holds water for crops [1].
- **Terracing limitations**: needs a great deal of labour and time to build [1]; walls must be maintained, and may collapse in heavy rain if neglected [1].
- **Vegetation cover benefits**: cheap, using cover crops or grass strips [1]; roots bind the soil and plants absorb water, reducing run-off [1].
- **Vegetation cover limitations**: cover plants may compete with crops for water and nutrients [1]; on a very steep slope with heavy rain, vegetation alone may not stop run-off [1].
- **Judgement**: for example, vegetation cover first because it is affordable, adding terraces over time, or both together [1].

Maximum 6; an answer covering only one strategy scores no more than 3.

## Where marks are usually lost

- **Question 1**: "soil being damaged". Erosion is removal.
- **Question 4**: "ploughing around a hill" without the link to reduced run-off.
- **Question 7(a)**: describing the trend without quoting figures.
- **Question 9**: suggesting terraces for flat land. The problem is wind, not slope.
- **Question 10**: jumping from erosion to displacement without the steps in between.
