These notes condense **3.2.5 Newton's laws of motion** from the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Physics (9630) specification. It is International AS content, assessed in Unit 1. For full explanations and worked examples, read the [Newton's laws study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-physics-9630-newtons-laws-of-motion-oxfordaqa/); for exam-style questions, use the [Newton's laws practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-physics-9630-newtons-laws-of-motion-oxfordaqa-practice/).

## The specification in one box

- Knowledge and application of the three laws of motion.
- Use of F = ma in situations where the mass is constant.

## The laws in exam wording

| Law | Statement |
|---|---|
| First | An object remains at rest or moves at constant velocity unless acted on by a resultant force |
| Second | The acceleration is proportional to the resultant force and in its direction: F = ma (constant mass) |
| Third | If A exerts a force on B, B exerts an equal and opposite force of the same type on A |

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Resultant force | The single force equal to the vector sum of all forces on an object |
| Inertia | The tendency of an object to keep its velocity; greater mass, greater inertia |
| Newton (N) | Resultant force giving 1 kg an acceleration of 1 m s⁻² |
| Free-body diagram | A diagram of one object showing only the forces acting on it |
| Normal contact force | Force from a surface, perpendicular to it |
| Tension | Pulling force in a string or rope, along its length |
| Third-law pair | Two equal, opposite forces of the same type on two different objects |

## Method: any F = ma problem

1. Draw a free-body diagram for each object (or the whole system).
2. Choose a positive direction.
3. Resultant force = (forces in + direction) − (forces in − direction).
4. Write resultant = ma.
5. For linked objects, write one equation per object, or use the whole system first, then one object.
6. Check with the object you did not use.

## Standard set-ups

| Set-up | Key equation |
|---|---|
| Push on two blocks in contact (smooth) | a = F ÷ (m₁ + m₂); contact force = m₂a on the far block |
| Tow bar | System: driving − total resistance = (m₁ + m₂)a; trailer: T − R₂ = m₂a |
| Table and hanging mass (smooth) | m₂g − T = m₂a and T = m₁a, so a = m₂g ÷ (m₁ + m₂) |
| Smooth slope at θ | a = g sin θ |
| Rough slope | ma = mg sin θ − friction |
| Constant velocity | Resultant force = 0 (driving force = resistance; drag = weight) |

## Third-law pairs: test

Ask four questions: same type? Equal size? Opposite direction? Different objects? A genuine pair passes all four.

- Earth pulls book down ↔ book pulls Earth up (gravitational).
- Table pushes book up ↔ book pushes table down (contact).
- Weight of book and normal force on book: **not** a pair (same object, different types).

## Worked reminders

- **Single object.** A 20 kg box pushed with 150 N against 90 N friction: a = (150 − 90) ÷ 20 = 3.0 m s⁻².
- **Contact force.** 8.0 N pushes 3.0 kg and 1.0 kg blocks: a = 2.0 m s⁻²; force on the 1.0 kg block = 2.0 N.
- **Pulley.** 2.0 kg on a smooth table, 2.0 kg hanging: a = 2.0 × 9.81 ÷ 4.0 = 4.9 m s⁻²; T = 2.0 × 4.9 = 9.8 N.
- **Slope.** On a smooth 30° slope, a = 9.81 × sin 30° = 4.9 m s⁻², whatever the mass.

## Exam traps

- F = ma with one force instead of the resultant.
- "Action and reaction cancel": they act on different objects.
- Weight and normal force called a third-law pair.
- Tension taken as equal to the hanging weight in an accelerating system.
- Mass used in place of weight: 5.0 kg weighs 49 N.
- First law stated without "constant velocity".

## Quick self-test

1. State Newton's first law.
2. State Newton's third law.
3. A 1500 kg car has a resultant force of 4500 N. Find its acceleration.
4. A 20 kg box is pushed with 150 N against 90 N of friction. Find its acceleration.
5. Find the weight of a 65 kg person.
6. Name the third-law partner of the weight of a book resting on a table.
7. An 8.0 N force pushes a 3.0 kg block that pushes a 1.0 kg block on a smooth floor. Find the force between the blocks.
8. A parachutist falls at constant velocity. What can you say about the forces on her?
9. A 2.0 kg block on a smooth table is pulled by a string over a pulley to a hanging 2.0 kg mass. Find the acceleration.
10. Find the acceleration of a 1.0 kg mass sliding down a smooth slope at 30°.
11. A loaded lorry and an empty lorry brake with the same force. Which stops in the shorter distance, and why?
12. Explain why the tension in the string in question 9 is less than the hanging weight.

### Answers

1. An object stays at rest or moves at constant velocity unless a resultant force acts on it.
2. If A exerts a force on B, B exerts an equal and opposite force of the same type on A.
3. 4500 ÷ 1500 = **3.0 m s⁻²**.
4. 60 ÷ 20 = **3.0 m s⁻²**.
5. 65 × 9.81 = **640 N** (638 N).
6. The **gravitational pull of the book on the Earth**.
7. a = 2.0 m s⁻², so force = 1.0 × 2.0 = **2.0 N**.
8. The resultant force is zero: **drag equals weight**.
9. 2.0 × 9.81 ÷ 4.0 = **4.9 m s⁻²**.
10. 9.81 × sin 30° = **4.9 m s⁻²**.
11. The **empty lorry**: same force on smaller mass gives a larger deceleration (a = F/m).
12. The hanging mass accelerates downwards, so the resultant force on it is downwards: weight must be larger than tension.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Free-body diagrams that include forces exerted by the object on others.
- Two-object problems solved with one equation that mixes forces on both objects.
- Laws stated in shortened form, losing "resultant", "constant velocity" or "different objects".
