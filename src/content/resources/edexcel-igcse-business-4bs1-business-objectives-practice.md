These questions cover section 1.1, Business objectives, of the Pearson Edexcel International GCSE in Business (4BS1) specification, Issue 2, February 2026. Use the [business objectives study guide](/resources/edexcel-igcse-business-4bs1-business-objectives/) and the [business objectives revision notes](/resources/edexcel-igcse-business-4bs1-business-objectives-revision-notes/) first if you need them. Every business is invented and amounts are in dollars. Show your workings and give percentages to 1 decimal place.

## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.**
**(a)** Define profit. **[1]**
**(b)** Define market share. **[1]**

**2.** Give two non-financial objectives a business may have. **[2]**

**3.** Ravi opened a bicycle repair shop last month. He says: "My main worry is having enough customers to keep the doors open." State the objective shown by Ravi's words. **[1]**

**4.** Corbin Paints had sales of 72,000. Total sales in its market were 960,000. Calculate Corbin Paints' market share. **[2]**

**5.** A business's profit fell from 25,000 to 21,000. Calculate the percentage change in profit. **[2]**

**6.** Ama is a sole trader who designs websites. Outline one reason why Ama may value independence and control more than growth. **[2]**

### Section B: structured questions

**7.** Explain one way that legislation may cause a business to change its objectives. **[3]**

**8.** Lumen Lamps sells 8,000 lamps a year at 30 each. Variable costs are 18 a lamp and fixed costs are 70,000 a year. The manager plans to cut the price to 27, expecting sales of 10,000 lamps.
**(a)** Calculate Lumen Lamps' current profit. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the expected profit after the price cut. **[2]**
**(c)** Analyse the conflict between Lumen Lamps' sales and profit objectives. **[4]**

**9.** Tanvir Tiles had revenue of 210,000 last year. The market was worth 3,000,000. Next year the market is expected to grow by 10%, and the owner wants an 8% market share.
**(a)** Calculate Tanvir Tiles' market share last year. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the revenue needed next year to reach 8%. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the percentage increase in revenue this requires. **[2]**

**10.** Oranmore Foods is a multinational with 12,000 employees in 20 countries. It buys cocoa and coffee from farmers in several of them. Analyse why Oranmore Foods might set social objectives. **[6]**

**11.** Mira has run a bakery as a sole trader for three years. Profit last year was 18,000, and her main objective has been financial security for her family. She works 60 hours a week. A bank has offered a loan to open a second shop across town, which she estimates could double her profit within three years. Evaluate whether Mira should change her objective from financial security to growth. **[8]**

**12.** Kellan Outdoor Ltd makes camping equipment and has 300 employees. Its market share is 12%. A new competitor has entered the market, and shareholders are unhappy that dividends have fallen two years running. The directors must choose one objective for next year:
- Option A: raise market share to 15% by cutting prices.
- Option B: raise profit by 10% by cutting costs.

Justify which option Kellan Outdoor Ltd should choose. **[6]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** Revenue minus total costs [1].
**(b)** A business's sales as a percentage of the total sales in its market [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Any two of: social objectives [1], personal satisfaction [1], challenge, independence and control. **[2]**

**3.** Survival [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* "State" takes its answer from the information given. "Keep the doors open" points to survival, not profit.

**4.** 72,000 ÷ 960,000 × 100 [1] = **7.5%** [1]. **[2]**

**5.** (21,000 − 25,000) ÷ 25,000 × 100 [1] = **−16%** (a fall of 16%) [1]. **[2]**

**6.** Ama may have left employment to make her own decisions about which clients and projects to take [1], and growing would mean hiring staff or taking on investors who would limit that freedom [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* "Outline" needs two linked points in context; use the facts that she works alone.

**7.** A new law can raise a business's costs [1]. For example, stricter safety rules may require new equipment or training [1], so the business may lower its profit target for the year or set a new objective of meeting the rule by a deadline [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* "Explain" is a statement with two expansion points; no context is needed.

**8. (a)** Contribution per lamp 30 − 18 = 12; 8,000 × 12 = 96,000 [1]; profit 96,000 − 70,000 = **26,000** [1].
**(b)** 27 − 18 = 9; 10,000 × 9 = 90,000 [1]; profit 90,000 − 70,000 = **20,000** [1].
**(c)** Sales rise by 2,000 lamps (25%), so the sales objective improves [1]. But each lamp now earns only 9 above its variable cost instead of 12 [1], so the higher volume does not make up for the lower price and profit falls by 6,000 [1]. Lumen Lamps must decide which matters more: if it wants to build market share against rivals, the lower profit may be worth accepting for a time; if its owners need profit, the price cut works against them [1]. **[8]**

**9. (a)** 210,000 ÷ 3,000,000 × 100 [1] = **7.0%** [1].
**(b)** Market next year 3,000,000 × 1.10 = 3,300,000 [1]; 8% × 3,300,000 = **264,000** [1].
**(c)** (264,000 − 210,000) ÷ 210,000 × 100 [1] = **25.7%** [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* A growing market makes a share target harder; the business needs a 25.7% rise in revenue to gain one percentage point of share.

**10.** Oranmore buys from farmers in many countries, so its reputation depends on how those farmers are treated [1]. A social objective, such as paying farmers a fair price or funding local schools, can protect the brand [1] and make customers in richer markets more willing to buy [1]. With operations in 20 countries, bad publicity in one place can spread quickly and damage sales everywhere [1]. Social objectives can also help secure future supplies of cocoa and coffee if farmers can afford to keep growing them [1]. However, they raise costs, so Oranmore must balance them against shareholders' expectations of profit [1]. **[6]**

**11.** For changing to growth: a second shop could double profit to about 36,000 [1], giving more income for the family in the long run [1], and Mira has three years' experience, so she knows the market [1]. Against: the loan must be repaid with interest whatever happens [1], so a slow start in the new shop could threaten the security she has built [1]; she already works 60 hours a week, so running two shops would need staff she must manage and pay [1]. As a sole trader she has unlimited liability, so the risk falls on her personally [1]. Judgement: for example, Mira should delay until the first shop's profit is steady and she has reserves, because her stated objective is her family's security and the loan puts it at risk; or growth is justified if the estimate is based on firm research [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the case figures (18,000, 60 hours, the loan) and end with a judgement linked to her objective.

**12.** Option B is the stronger choice [1]. Shareholders are unhappy after two years of falling dividends, so raising profit addresses the stakeholders who own the company [1]. Cutting costs raises profit without depending on customers buying more [1]. Option A risks a price war with the new competitor [1], which could cut profit and dividends further even if share rises [1]. Option A would only be better if the competitor threatened to take a large part of the market quickly, which the case does not show [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* "Justify" recommends one of two options using the information given. Either option can earn the marks if the case is used to support it and the other is shown to be weaker.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Choosing profit when the case points to survival or independence.
- Giving market share in dollars instead of a percentage.
- Forgetting that a growing market raises the revenue needed for a share target.
- Explaining a reason for change without the new objective it leads to.
- Ending an "evaluate" or "justify" answer without a decision.
