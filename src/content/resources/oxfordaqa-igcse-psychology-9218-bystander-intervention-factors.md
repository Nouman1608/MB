**This guide covers one strand of 3.2.2 Social influence in OxfordAQA International GCSE Psychology
(9218), Version 1.1: "Identification and explanation of how social factors (presence of others and
the cost of helping) and dispositional factors (similarity to victim and expertise) affect bystander
intervention."** The [Conformity and Obedience study guide](/resources/igcse-oxfordaqa-psychology-conformity-and-obedience/)
covers the first two strands of 3.2.2 and leaves bystander behaviour for separate pages. This is one
of them; the three named studies have their own guide,
[Studies of Bystander Behaviour](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-psychology-9218-bystander-studies/).

## What bystander intervention means

A **bystander** is someone who witnesses an event, such as an accident, a crime or a person in
distress, without being directly involved. **Bystander intervention** is when a bystander acts to
help: giving first aid, calling the emergency services or challenging an attacker. The **bystander
effect** is the finding that people are often less likely to help when other people are present.

Research on bystanders grew in the 1960s after the murder of Kitty Genovese in New York in 1964.
Early newspaper reports claimed that dozens of neighbours watched and did nothing; later
investigations showed that those reports exaggerated how many people saw the attack and how little
was done. The case still prompted psychologists to ask a real question: what makes people help, or
fail to help, in an emergency?

The specification divides the answer into two kinds of factor:

| Type of factor | Meaning | Factors named in 9218 |
|---|---|---|
| **Social** (situational) | Features of the situation the bystander is in | Presence of others; cost of helping |
| **Dispositional** | Features of the bystander as a person | Similarity to victim; expertise |

## Social factor 1: the presence of others

The more people who witness an emergency, the less likely any one of them is to help, and the longer
help takes to arrive. Three processes explain this.

**Diffusion of responsibility.** When a person is the only witness, all the responsibility for
helping falls on them. With others present, responsibility is shared, so each person feels less
obliged to act: "someone else will do it", or "someone has probably already called an ambulance".

**Pluralistic ignorance.** Many emergencies are **ambiguous**: is the person lying on the pavement ill
or asleep? People look at others to decide. If everyone is calmly waiting to see what the others do,
each person reads the calm as a sign that nothing is wrong, and the whole group wrongly concludes
there is no emergency.

**Audience inhibition.** People worry about looking foolish in front of others if they misjudge the
situation or help badly, so they hold back.

These fit into the five-step **decision model** proposed by Latané and Darley. A bystander must:

1. **notice** the event;
2. **interpret** it as an emergency;
3. **accept responsibility** for helping;
4. **know how** to help;
5. **decide** to help and act.

A "no" at any step means no help. The presence of others mainly works at step 2 (pluralistic
ignorance) and step 3 (diffusion of responsibility). Latané and Darley's smoke study tested step 2
directly.

## Social factor 2: the cost of helping

Helping is not free. Bystanders weigh up, often very quickly, the **costs of helping** against the
**costs of not helping**.

| Costs of helping | Costs of not helping |
|---|---|
| Danger of physical harm | Guilt or self-blame |
| Time and effort | Criticism or blame from others |
| Embarrassment if help is not needed | Knowing the victim may suffer |
| Unpleasantness (blood, vomit, a drunk person) | Loss of the good feeling and praise that come from helping |

Piliavin and colleagues called this the **arousal: cost-reward model**. Seeing someone in distress
produces unpleasant **arousal**. The bystander is motivated to reduce it, and chooses the action that
does so at the lowest net cost. When the cost of helping is low and the cost of not helping is high
(a sober person collapses in front of you), helping is likely. When the cost of helping is high (the
victim seems drunk and might be sick or aggressive), people may help indirectly, by fetching someone
else, or not at all. Piliavin's subway study tested this by comparing a victim with a cane and a
victim who appeared drunk.

## Dispositional factor 1: similarity to the victim

People are more likely to help someone they see as **similar to themselves**: the same group,
background or identity. Perceived similarity makes the victim feel like "one of us", increases
empathy and raises the cost of not helping (failing a member of your own group).

Similarity is classed as dispositional here because it depends on how the **bystander** sees
themselves and which groups they identify with. Levine's football supporter study showed that the
same fall by the same person attracted far more help when the victim wore the shirt of the
participant's own team. A second part of that research showed something important for exam answers:
the boundary of "similar" can change. When participants were encouraged to think of themselves as
football fans in general, rival supporters were helped too.

## Dispositional factor 2: expertise

**Expertise** means having the knowledge, skills or training that a particular emergency needs: a
nurse at a road accident, a lifeguard at a swimming pool, someone trained in first aid when a person
collapses.

Expertise increases intervention in three ways that map onto the decision model:

- **Interpretation (step 2):** experts recognise the signs of a real emergency, such as a heart attack,
  so they are less affected by pluralistic ignorance.
- **Responsibility (step 3):** people with relevant skills feel that responsibility falls on them,
  which reduces diffusion of responsibility.
- **Knowing how (step 4) and cost:** an expert knows what to do, so helping feels less risky and less
  likely to end in embarrassment, which lowers the cost of helping.

Expertise can also make a bystander more likely to **step back**: if someone more qualified is already
helping, an untrained person may judge that their help is not needed.

## How the factors interact

Exam questions often describe a scenario with several factors at once. A busy station with a sober,
injured stranger in a supporter's shirt combines a large crowd (reducing help) with low cost and
possible similarity (increasing help). A strong answer identifies each factor separately, explains
the mechanism for each, and then judges which is likely to dominate in that scenario.

## Worked example: applying the factors

**Question.** Jamal is walking through a crowded shopping centre when an elderly woman trips and
cannot get up. Dozens of shoppers walk past. Jamal has just finished a first-aid course. Use your
knowledge of factors affecting bystander intervention to explain why the other shoppers walk past and
why Jamal might help. [6]

> *Presence of others.* Because there are dozens of shoppers, **responsibility is diffused**: each
> shopper assumes someone else will help, so no one feels personally responsible **[1]**. Seeing
> others walk past calmly may also lead each shopper to decide the woman is not seriously hurt
> (**pluralistic ignorance**) **[1]**.
>
> *Cost of helping.* Shoppers may see costs such as being delayed or not knowing what to do, which
> makes walking past the cheaper option **[1]**.
>
> *Expertise.* Jamal's first-aid training means he can **recognise** whether she is injured **[1]**
> and **knows how to help**, so helping is less risky and embarrassing for him, lowering the cost
> **[1]**. His training may also make him feel that the **responsibility** falls on him rather than
> being shared with the crowd **[1]**.

Each mark comes from a named factor, its mechanism and a link to a detail in the scenario.

## Evaluating explanations of bystander behaviour

- **Supporting evidence:** the smoke study supports the effect of others' presence; the subway study
  supports the cost of helping; the football study supports similarity. Each is described in the
  [studies guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-psychology-9218-bystander-studies/).
- **Not always true:** in the subway study, larger groups of passengers did **not** reduce helping,
  probably because the emergency was clear and the victim could be seen by everyone. Diffusion of
  responsibility is strongest when the emergency is ambiguous or the bystanders cannot see each other
  helping.
- **Application:** the factors suggest practical ways to increase help, such as first-aid training
  (expertise) and, if you need help, pointing to one specific person and asking them directly (which
  removes diffusion of responsibility).
- **Social versus dispositional:** the factors work together; a full explanation of a real event
  needs both kinds.

## Common mistakes

- **Saying the bystander effect means people are selfish.** It describes how situations reduce
  helping, not a fixed trait.
- **Mixing up diffusion of responsibility and pluralistic ignorance.** Diffusion is about *who should
  act*; pluralistic ignorance is about *whether it is an emergency*.
- **Classing similarity as a social factor.** The specification lists it as dispositional.
- **Naming a factor without its mechanism.** "There were lots of people" earns little without
  explaining diffusion or pluralistic ignorance.
- **Ignoring the scenario.** In "use your knowledge" questions, every point must refer to the people
  and details given.

## Exam technique

- **Identify:** name the factor, using the specification's words.
- **Explain:** say how the factor changes the likelihood of helping, and why.
- **Use your knowledge / apply:** link every point to a detail in the scenario.
- **Evaluate:** support the factor with a named study, then give a limitation or exception.
