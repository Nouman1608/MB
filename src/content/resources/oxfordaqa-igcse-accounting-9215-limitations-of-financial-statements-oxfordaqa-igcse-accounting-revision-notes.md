These notes condense the section 3.5 statement "The limitations of financial statements and ratio analysis when assessing business performance", where "limitations will include those relating to both financial and non-financial factors". The fuller explanation and an annotated answer are in the [limitations study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-accounting-9215-limitations-of-financial-statements-oxfordaqa-igcse-accounting/); test yourself with the [limitations practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-accounting-9215-limitations-of-financial-statements-oxfordaqa-igcse-accounting-practice/). Figures are invented and in dollars.

## Two families of limitation

**Financial**: problems with the figures themselves.

- Historic: they describe a period that has ended.
- Snapshot: the statement of financial position shows one date, which may be untypical.
- Historic cost: assets bought at different times are added together; inflation is ignored.
- Estimates and policies: depreciation method, doubtful debts and inventory value involve judgement.
- Comparability: other businesses may differ in size, products, year end, policies and how owners are paid.
- Ratios show what changed, not why.

**Non-financial**: things the money measurement concept leaves out.

- Staff skills and morale; quality of management.
- Reputation, customer loyalty and service.
- Competitors, market trends, the economy, changes in law.
- Environmental and ethical conduct.
- Future plans: new products, new premises, closures.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Money measurement concept | Only items with a money value are recorded |
| Historic cost | Assets recorded at original cost, not current value |
| Inter-firm comparison | Comparing ratios with another business or an industry average |
| Trend analysis | Comparing the same business over several years |
| Snapshot | A position at one date, which may not be typical |
| Real terms | Figures adjusted to remove the effect of price rises |
| Product mix | The proportions of different goods sold, each with its own margin |

## Ratio-specific weaknesses

| Ratio | Weak spot |
|---|---|
| Gross profit margin | Changes with product mix |
| Markup | Depends on closing inventory valuation |
| Inventory turnover | Average uses only two dates |
| Profit in relation to revenue | Sole trader profit excludes any pay for the owner |
| ROCE | Old, depreciated assets make it look higher |
| Current ratio | Counts slow-moving inventory as fully liquid |
| Liquid capital ratio | One date; seasonal businesses vary |
| Receivable days | Total revenue instead of credit revenue shortens it |
| Payable days | One large invoice at the year end distorts it |

## Four distortions to remember

1. **Receivable days**: receivables 30,000; total revenue 365,000 gives 30 days, but credit revenue of 219,000 gives 50 days.
2. **ROCE and asset age**: same profit of 36,000 on capital employed of 120,000 (old assets) is 30%, but on 240,000 (new assets) is 15%.
3. **Inflation**: revenue up 10% while prices rose 15% means volume fell by about 4%.
4. **Owner's reward**: a sole trader's 20% profit margin can match a company's 4% once a director's salary is allowed for.

## Which limitation suits which user?

- **Lender**: future cash to repay; security; management experience.
- **Supplier**: payment record; likelihood of continuing to trade.
- **Employee**: job security; plans for expansion or cuts.
- **Owner or investor**: competitors, new products, market growth.

## Answer frame for "explain one limitation"

1. **Name** the limitation: "the statement of financial position is a snapshot".
2. **Link** it to a ratio or figure: "so the liquid capital ratio reflects one date only".
3. **Apply** it to the business: "a garden centre holds little inventory and more cash at the end of summer".
4. **Consequence** for the user: "so a lender could overrate its liquidity".

Four short linked sentences like these usually earn more than a list of six unconnected limitations.

## Exam traps

- "The ratios might be calculated wrongly" is not a limitation.
- Generic lists without naming the business or ratio.
- Calling reputation or morale a financial factor.
- Forgetting to use credit revenue for receivable days when it is given.
- In "evaluate" questions, giving only limitations and no usefulness or judgement.
- Saying inflation makes profit "wrong"; it makes comparisons over time misleading.

## Self-test

1. Name two financial limitations of financial statements.
2. Name two non-financial factors not shown in financial statements.
3. Which accounting concept explains why customer loyalty is not recorded?
4. Why might a business with old assets have a high ROCE?
5. Receivables 18,000; credit revenue 146,000; total revenue 219,000. Calculate receivable days using credit revenue.
6. What would the answer to question 5 be using total revenue, and why is it misleading?
7. How can product mix change gross profit margin when no prices change?
8. Why is a sole trader's profit not directly comparable with a company's?
9. Revenue rose by 5% while prices rose by 8%. Did sales volume rise or fall?
10. Why might a toy shop's current ratio at 31 December be unrepresentative?
11. Give one non-financial factor a lender would want to know about.
12. What should an evaluate answer end with?

**Answers**

1. Any two: historic data; snapshot date; historic cost ignores inflation; estimates and policies differ; ratios do not explain causes.
2. Any two: staff morale, reputation, competition, the economy, management quality, future plans.
3. Money measurement.
4. Its assets have been depreciated for years, so capital employed is low.
5. 18,000 ÷ 146,000 × 365 = 45 days.
6. 18,000 ÷ 219,000 × 365 = 30 days; it includes cash sales, so credit customers seem to pay faster than they do.
7. Selling a larger share of low-margin goods lowers the overall margin.
8. The sole trader's reward is taken as drawings, not an expense, while a company charges directors' salaries.
9. Fell.
10. After the Christmas season, inventory and cash may be unusually low or high, so the date is not typical.
11. Any one: the owner's experience, the strength of the market, loss of a major customer, new competitors.
12. A supported judgement, ideally saying what further information the user needs.
