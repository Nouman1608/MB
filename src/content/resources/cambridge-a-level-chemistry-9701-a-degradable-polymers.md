This guide covers subtopic **35.3, Degradable polymers**, from Topic 35, Polymerisation, of Cambridge
International AS & A Level Chemistry 9701, 2025–2027 series. It is A Level content. The broader
[Condensation Polymerisation and Polymer Degradability study guide](/resources/a-condensation-polymerisation-and-polymer-degradability/)
states the three ideas; this page explains each one at the level of bonds, and works through the
hydrolysis products that examiners ask you to write.

## What the syllabus asks for

Subtopic 35.3 has three learning outcomes:

1. "recognise that poly(alkenes) are chemically inert and can therefore be difficult to biodegrade"
2. "recognise that some polymers can be degraded by the action of light"
3. "recognise that polyesters and polyamides are biodegradable by acidic and alkaline hydrolysis"

It extends AS subtopic 20.1, which asks you to recognise "the difficulty of the disposal of
poly(alkene)s, i.e. non-biodegradability and harmful combustion products".

## Poly(alkenes): why they are inert

A poly(alkene) such as poly(ethene), –[CH₂CH₂]ₙ–, or poly(propene), –[CH₂CH(CH₃)]ₙ–, has a backbone
made entirely of C–C and C–H bonds. Three features make it chemically inert:

- **Strong bonds.** C–C and C–H bonds need a lot of energy to break.
- **Non-polar bonds.** Carbon and hydrogen have similar electronegativities, so there are no δ+
  carbon atoms for nucleophiles such as water or hydroxide ions to attack.
- **No functional group in the chain.** Once polymerised, the C=C of the monomer has gone; there is
  nothing left that acids, alkalis or the enzymes of microorganisms can react with.

**Biodegradable** means broken down by natural processes, such as the action of microorganisms, into
smaller molecules. Because nothing in a poly(alkene) chain can be attacked, these polymers persist in
landfill and the environment for a very long time.

**Disposal problems, from AS.** Burying poly(alkenes) uses landfill space they occupy almost
indefinitely. Burning them releases energy but can produce harmful products: carbon monoxide from
incomplete combustion, and, from poly(chloroethene), hydrogen chloride, which is toxic and acidic.

## Degradation by light

Some polymers are designed to break down when exposed to light, especially ultraviolet light in
sunlight. Bonds or groups within the chain, for example carbonyl (C=O) groups built into the
backbone, absorb the energy of the light, and bonds in the chain break. The long chains become
shorter fragments, and the material becomes brittle and breaks up.

Two limits are worth stating in an evaluation answer:

- The polymer must be **exposed to light**. Buried in landfill, it degrades very little.
- Breaking into fragments is not the same as breaking down completely; the small pieces can still
  persist.

## Polyesters and polyamides: hydrolysis

The links in these polymers are ester (–COO–) and amide (–CONH–) groups, the same functional groups
that hydrolyse in small molecules. Water, helped by acid or alkali, attacks the δ+ carbonyl carbon of
each link and breaks the C–O or C–N bond. The chain falls apart into its monomers, or into salts of
them, depending on the conditions.

**The product rule.** Hydrolysis gives back the monomers, then:

- in **acid**, any amine end is **protonated** (–NH₃⁺) and carboxylic acids stay as –COOH;
- in **alkali**, any carboxylic acid end becomes a **carboxylate salt** (–COO⁻) and amines and
  alcohols stay neutral.

| Polymer | Acid hydrolysis gives | Alkaline hydrolysis (NaOH) gives |
|---|---|---|
| PET, –[OCH₂CH₂OOCC₆H₄CO]ₙ– | HOCH₂CH₂OH + HOOCC₆H₄COOH | HOCH₂CH₂OH + NaOOCC₆H₄COONa |
| poly(lactic acid), –[OCH(CH₃)CO]ₙ– | CH₃CH(OH)COOH | CH₃CH(OH)COONa |
| nylon-6,6, –[NH(CH₂)₆NHCO(CH₂)₄CO]ₙ– | ⁺H₃N(CH₂)₆NH₃⁺ (as chloride) + HOOC(CH₂)₄COOH | H₂N(CH₂)₆NH₂ + NaOOC(CH₂)₄COONa |
| polypeptide (from amino acids) | amino acids as cations, ⁺H₃NCH(R)COOH | amino acids as anions, H₂NCH(R)COO⁻ |

Because the links can be attacked, polyesters and polyamides are described as biodegradable by acidic
and alkaline hydrolysis. In the environment the rate depends on conditions, but the key contrast with
poly(alkenes) is that a reactive group exists in every link.

## The three outcomes side by side

| Polymer type | What breaks it down | Why | Main limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| poly(alkene) | very little; persists | strong, non-polar C–C and C–H bonds; no functional group | landfill space; harmful products if burned |
| photodegradable polymer | light, especially UV | groups in the chain absorb light energy and bonds break | needs light; fragments may persist |
| polyester or polyamide | hydrolysis by acid or alkali | ester or amide links have a δ+ carbonyl carbon open to attack | rate depends on conditions |

A useful way to organise a long answer is to ask, for each polymer, "is there a bond in the chain
that something in the environment can break?" For poly(alkenes) the answer is no; for photodegradable
polymers, yes, but only with light; for polyesters and polyamides, yes, by hydrolysis of every link.

## Worked example 1: writing a hydrolysis equation for one repeat unit

**Question.** Write an equation for the complete alkaline hydrolysis of one repeat unit of PET with
NaOH(aq).

**Answer.** One repeat unit contains two ester links. Each needs one OH⁻, so two NaOH are used:

    –OCH₂CH₂OOCC₆H₄CO– + 2NaOH → HOCH₂CH₂OH + NaOOCC₆H₄COONa

*Examiner's note:* repeat-unit equations are written with the open bonds shown; the ends of the
chain are ignored. Giving the free acid, HOOCC₆H₄COOH, for alkaline conditions loses the mark.

## Worked example 2: a quantitative hydrolysis question

**Question.** Calculate the mass of NaOH needed to hydrolyse 1.00 kg of PET completely.
(Aᵣ: H = 1.0, C = 12.0, O = 16.0, Na = 23.0)

**Answer.**

- Repeat unit C₁₀H₈O₄: Mᵣ = 10(12.0) + 8(1.0) + 4(16.0) = 192.0
- n(repeat units) = 1000 / 192.0 = 5.21 mol
- 2 mol NaOH per repeat unit: n(NaOH) = 10.4 mol
- Mᵣ(NaOH) = 40.0, so mass = 10.4 × 40.0 = **417 g** (0.417 kg)

## Worked example 3: an annotated comparison answer

**Question.** Explain why nylon-6,6 is biodegradable by hydrolysis but poly(propene) is not. **[3]**

**Model answer.** Nylon-6,6 contains amide links in its chain, with a δ+ carbonyl carbon **[1]**,
which can be attacked by water in acidic or alkaline conditions, breaking the C–N bond and splitting
the chain **[1]**. Poly(propene) has only strong, non-polar C–C and C–H bonds and no functional group
in the chain, so it is chemically inert and is not hydrolysed **[1]**.

## Common mistakes

- Saying poly(alkenes) are inert "because they are saturated" without mentioning strong, non-polar
  bonds and the lack of a functional group.
- Writing that photodegradable polymers break down fully into harmless molecules.
- Giving free carboxylic acids in alkaline hydrolysis, or free amines in acid hydrolysis.
- Forgetting the diol in PET hydrolysis: it is released unchanged in both acid and alkali.
- Using one NaOH per repeat unit of PET or nylon-6,6, which each contain two links.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Recognise / state:** a one-line reason is enough: "poly(alkenes) are inert because of strong,
  non-polar C–C and C–H bonds."
- **Explain** biodegradability: identify the link, say where it is attacked, and contrast with a
  poly(alkene).
- **Give the products** of hydrolysis: name or draw both, in the form for the conditions stated.
- **Evaluate** a disposal method: give a benefit and a limitation, such as light needed for
  photodegradation, or harmful products from burning.
