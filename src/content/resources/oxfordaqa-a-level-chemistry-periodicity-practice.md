This set tests section **3.2.1 Periodicity** of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Chemistry
(9620) specification, Version 5.3, for International AS and A-level exams May/June 2020 onwards: 3.2.1.1
(Classification) together with 3.2.1.2 (the Physical properties of Period 3 elements). The whole section is International AS
content; none of it is International A-level only. Unit 1 (Inorganic 1 and Physical 1) is where the
specification examines it.

Before you start, work through the [study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-periodicity/) and the
[revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-periodicity-revision-notes/). The questions use
rounded values from standard data tables (phosphorus as white P₄, sulfur as monoclinic S₈). If you would
rather see where you stand before you begin, a free [10-minute diagnostic](/diagnostics/) will tell you.

## Questions

**1.** State the block of the Periodic Table that contains each element.

**(a)** the element with proton number 9 **[1]**
**(b)** the element with proton number 23 **[1]**
**(c)** the element with proton number 19 **[1]**

**2.** Uranium is placed in the actinide row of the Periodic Table.

**(a)** State the block that contains uranium. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain why you can classify uranium without writing out its electron configuration. **[1]**

**3.** Magnesium and aluminium are next to each other in Period 3.

**(a)** Explain why aluminium is a p-block element but magnesium is an s-block element. **[2]**
**(b)** Use your answer to (a) to explain why magnesium's first ionisation energy is higher than
aluminium's. **[2]**

**4.** The first ionisation energies of the Period 3 elements are shown below.

| Element | Na | Mg | Al | Si | P | S | Cl | Ar |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| First ionisation energy / kJ mol⁻¹ | 496 | 738 | 578 | 787 | 1012 | 1000 | 1251 | 1521 |

**(a)** Give the equation, including state symbols, that represents the first ionisation of phosphorus. **[1]**
**(b)** Name the two elements that break the rising pattern in the table. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain the general increase in first ionisation energy from sodium to argon. **[3]**
**(d)** Explain why phosphorus has a slightly higher first ionisation energy than sulfur. **[2]**

**5.** Arrange the atoms of sodium, aluminium, phosphorus and chlorine in order of increasing atomic radius.
Explain the order. **[4]**

**6.** Five Period 3 elements are labelled J, K, L, M and N. They are magnesium, silicon, phosphorus,
chlorine and argon, in no particular order. Their melting points are:

| Element | J | K | L | M | N |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Melting point / K | 1687 | 172 | 923 | 84 | 317 |

**(a)** Identify J, L and N. **[3]**
**(b)** K and M are chlorine and argon. State which is which, and explain the difference in their melting
points. **[3]**
**(c)** Explain why J has the highest melting point of the five. **[2]**

**7.** Sodium atoms are the largest in Period 3, and sodium has the lowest first ionisation energy in the
period. Explain both facts. **[3]**

**8.** Explain why magnesium has a much higher melting point than sulfur. **[4]**

**9.** The melting points of sodium, magnesium and aluminium are 371 K, 923 K and 933 K.

**(a)** Calculate the increase in melting point from sodium to magnesium. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain why the melting point increases from sodium to magnesium to aluminium. **[3]**

**10.** This question is about phosphorus, sulfur and chlorine. Use the data in questions 4 and 6, and a
melting point of 388 K for sulfur.

**(a)** Describe and explain how the first ionisation energy changes from phosphorus to sulfur, and from
sulfur to chlorine. **[4]**
**(b)** Describe and explain how the melting point changes from phosphorus to sulfur to chlorine. **[3]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** **p block**: 1s² 2s² 2p⁵, so the last electron enters 2p (fluorine) [1].
**(b)** **d block**: 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ 3d³ 4s², so the last electron enters 3d (vanadium) [1].
**(c)** **s block**: 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ 4s¹ (potassium) [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Write the configuration in filling order before you decide; for proton number 23 the
3d electrons are the last added, even though 4s is the outer shell.

**2. (a)** **f block** [1].
**(b)** The block follows from position in the Periodic Table, which is set by proton number; every element
in the actinide row is in the f block [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* An answer that only says "it is in the actinides" restates the question; link
position to proton number to make the reason count.

**3. (a)** Magnesium (3s²) has its highest-energy electron in the 3s sub-shell, so it is s block [1].
Aluminium (3s² 3p¹) has its highest-energy electron in the 3p sub-shell, so it is p block [1].
**(b)** The electron removed from aluminium is in 3p, which is higher in energy than the 3s electron
removed from magnesium [1]. The filled 3s sub-shell also partly screens the 3p electron from the nucleus; both effects reduce the energy
needed to remove it [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* In (b), say which electron is removed from each atom; an answer about "aluminium's
electrons" in general does not show the sub-shell idea.

**4. (a)** **P(g) → P⁺(g) + e⁻** [1].
**(b)** **Aluminium and sulfur** (both needed) [1].
**(c)** Nuclear charge increases across the period, as each element has one more proton [1]. The electron
always leaves the third shell, behind the same inner electrons, so shielding barely changes [1]. With a
stronger pull on it (and a smaller atom), the outer electron takes more energy to remove [1].
**(d)** In sulfur, two of the 3p electrons share one orbital [1]. These paired electrons repel each other,
so one of them is removed more easily than an unpaired 3p electron in phosphorus [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* In (c), "more protons" and "similar shielding" are separate points, so state both
explicitly rather than folding them into one phrase about a "stronger nucleus".

**5.** Increasing radius: **Cl < P < Al < Na** [1]. Proton number increases from 11 (Na) to 17 (Cl), so
nuclear charge increases [1]. All four atoms keep their outer electrons in the third shell, behind the
same ten inner electrons, so shielding hardly differs [1]. The outer electrons are pulled closer as nuclear charge rises, so the radius falls from Na
to Cl [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Check the direction asked for: "increasing" starts with the smallest atom, and a
reversed list can lose the order mark even when the explanation is right.

**6. (a)** **J is silicon** [1]; **L is magnesium** [1]; **N is phosphorus** [1].
**(b)** **K is chlorine and M is argon** [1]. Chlorine is made of Cl₂ molecules with 34 electrons each;
argon is single atoms with 18 electrons each [1]. More electrons give stronger van der Waals forces, so
more energy is needed to overcome them in chlorine, which melts at the higher temperature [1].
**(c)** Silicon is macromolecular, so melting breaks many strong covalent bonds [1]. Breaking them takes
more energy than disrupting magnesium's metallic bonding or the van der Waals forces in the other three [1].
**[8]**
*Examiner insight:* In (b), name the particles (Cl₂ molecules, Ar atoms) before comparing forces; a
comparison of "size" with no reference to electrons is hard to credit.

**7.** Sodium has the smallest nuclear charge in the period (11 protons) [1]. Its outer electron is in the
third shell with the same inner-shell shielding as the other elements, so it is the least strongly
attracted and lies furthest from the nucleus [1]. This weakly held 3s electron needs the least energy to
remove, so sodium has the lowest first ionisation energy [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* One chain explains both facts; tie the radius point and the energy point to the same
weak attraction rather than giving two unconnected statements.

**8.** Magnesium has a giant metallic structure [1]. Melting must overcome the strong attraction between
Mg²⁺ ions and the delocalised electrons [1]. Sulfur is molecular (S₈); melting only overcomes van der Waals
forces between molecules, and the S–S covalent bonds do not break [1]. Van der Waals forces are much weaker
than metallic bonding, so far less energy is needed and sulfur's melting point is lower [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Mentioning S–S bonds breaking on melting contradicts the molecular point, so leave
them out or say clearly that they stay intact.

**9. (a)** 923 − 371 = **552 K** [1].
**(b)** All three are giant metallic lattices [1]. The ion charge rises (Na⁺, Mg²⁺, Al³⁺) and the number
of delocalised electrons per atom rises (1, 2, 3), while the ions get smaller [1]. Each of these makes the
metallic bonding stronger, so melting the metal takes more energy [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* "Stronger metallic bonding" alone is a claim, not an explanation; credit comes from
giving the ion charge or delocalised electron count that makes it stronger.

**10. (a)** From phosphorus to sulfur the first ionisation energy falls slightly, from 1012 to
1000 kJ mol⁻¹ [1]. The electron taken from sulfur comes from a doubly filled 3p orbital, and repulsion
inside that pair lowers the energy needed [1]. From sulfur to chlorine it rises, to 1251 kJ mol⁻¹ [1]. Chlorine has
one more proton, with the electron removed from the same shell and similar shielding, so it is held more
strongly [1].
**(b)** The melting point rises from phosphorus (317 K) to sulfur (388 K), then falls to chlorine (172 K)
[1]. All three are molecular, so melting only overcomes van der Waals forces between molecules [1]. S₈ has
the most electrons per molecule (128), then P₄ (60), then Cl₂ (34), so the forces are strongest between S₈
molecules and weakest between Cl₂ molecules [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* "Describe and explain" asks for two things at each step: state the direction of
change, with the data, and then give the reason; a reason on its own leaves the description unanswered.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Picking the block from the sub-shell written last rather than the one filled last.
- Giving "Group 0" as a reason for argon or helium being in a particular block.
- Writing X(s) or X(aq) in a first ionisation energy equation.
- Naming only one of the two ionisation energy dips when two are asked for.
- Explaining the Mg → Al drop with electron pairing, or the P → S drop with a new sub-shell: the two
  reasons are often swapped.
- Saying atomic radius falls because "electrons are added to new shells".
- Comparing molecular elements by "size of molecule" with no mention of the number of electrons.
- Treating aluminium's p-block position as a reason for covalent bonding.
- Giving a melting point comparison that names the structure of only one element.
