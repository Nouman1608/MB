**Section A of Paper 1 in OxfordAQA International GCSE English Language (9270) tests how well you read
one unseen piece of literary non-fiction.** It is worth 40 of the paper's 80 marks. You are given a
single text for close study, divided into sections with questions, and at least one question relates
to the whole text. There is no second text and no comparison. This guide explains what kind of writing
to expect, which reading skills are assessed, and how to turn close reading into precise written
answers.

## What the specification says

- The passages are **literary non-fiction**, for example **autobiography, biography, letters, memoir
  and travel or adventure writing**.
- Students answer **all** the questions in Section A.
- The text is **divided into sections**, each with its own question or questions.
- **At least one question relates to the whole text.**

The reading skills assessed come from AO1:

| Skill | Specification wording | What it means in Section A |
|---|---|---|
| R1 | Read and understand texts, selecting and utilising material appropriate to purpose | Find the right details for the question asked, and use them, not just copy them |
| R2 | Collate and synthesise detail from one or more sources, making cross-references and comparisons as appropriate | Gather details from different parts of the one text and draw them together |
| R3 | Develop and sustain interpretations of writers' ideas and perspectives | Work out the writer's attitude and how it develops |
| R4 | Explain and evaluate how writers use linguistic, grammatical, structural and presentational features to achieve effects and engage and influence the reader | Analyse methods: words, sentences, organisation, and their effect |

## What makes literary non-fiction different

Literary non-fiction tells something true, but the writer **shapes** the experience. A memoir chooses
which moments to include; a travel writer decides what to describe first and what to leave until the
end; a letter writer adapts tone to the person addressed. Three features follow:

1. **A real "I".** Most of these texts have a first-person voice. The narrator is also the writer, but
   often an older writer looking back at a younger self. Notice the gap: "I was sure then that…"
   signals a change of view.
2. **A purpose beyond the facts.** The writer wants you to feel something about a place, person or
   event: admiration, unease, amusement, nostalgia.
3. **Selection and arrangement.** What the writer chose to put first, linger on or leave out is
   evidence of perspective.

## Reading the text in sections

Because the questions are attached to sections, read with the question in mind:

1. **Read the whole text once quickly** for the overall situation, voice and mood. Questions on one
   section are easier when you know where the text is going.
2. **For each question, re-read only the section named.** Answers that drift into other sections
   waste time and may not be credited for that question.
3. **Underline before you write.** Mark four or five short pieces of evidence that answer the
   question directly.
4. **For the whole-text question, track change.** Note the opening, any turning point and the ending,
   and ask how the writer's attitude or the reader's understanding moves.

## Analysing language: from feature to effect

The core move is the same as in any analytical answer: identify the method, quote briefly, and explain
the effect on the reader. Literary non-fiction rewards attention to:

- **Precise verbs and adjectives** that reveal attitude ("the bus *lurched*", "a *grudging* welcome").
- **Imagery** that makes an unfamiliar place vivid through a familiar comparison.
- **Contrast**, especially between expectation and reality in travel writing.
- **Humour and irony**, often aimed at the writer's younger self.
- **Direct speech**, which brings other people into the account and lets readers judge them.
- **Tone shifts**, signalled by a change of sentence length or vocabulary.

## Analysing structure

Structure means the order and shape of the text: how it opens, how it moves, where it changes focus,
and how it ends. Useful questions to ask:

- **Opening:** does the writer begin with a scene, a reflection, a line of dialogue? Why?
- **Shifts:** where does the focus move from outside to inside, from past to present, from wide view
  to detail?
- **Pace:** where does the writer slow down with detail, and where are long periods summarised in a
  sentence?
- **Ending:** does it return to the opening, offer a reflection, or leave something unresolved?

Always link a structural choice to its effect: "By delaying the name of the village until the final
paragraph, the writer keeps the reader as disorientated as she was."

## Interpreting perspective (R3)

A writer's perspective is rarely stated. You infer it from evidence:

- **Word choice with connotations** ("crumbling" vs "weathered").
- **What is praised and what is mocked.**
- **How other people are presented**, through description and their own speech.
- **The reflective voice**, where the older writer comments on the experience.

Use tentative language when a text supports more than one reading: "This *suggests*…", "The writer
*may* be implying…". Sustained interpretation (R3) means developing one idea across several pieces of
evidence, not making one guess per quotation.

## Worked example

The following is an **original passage written for this guide**, in the style of a memoir.

> *My grandmother's kitchen was not large, but in my memory it is enormous. There was the stove, black
> and patient, which she fed every morning before light. There was the table, scarred by forty years
> of knives. And there was my grandmother herself, who never sat down. I was nine. I thought she was
> simply busy. It took me thirty years to understand that she was holding the whole house together
> with her hands.*

**Question:** How does the writer present her grandmother in this section?

**Model answer (extract):**
The writer presents her grandmother as tireless and quietly powerful. The contrast between a kitchen
that was "not large" and one that is "enormous" in memory shows how much the grandmother's presence
filled the child's world. Personifying the stove as "black and patient", and saying she "fed" it,
makes the stove seem like a creature in her care, suggesting that tending the house was a constant,
living responsibility. The short sentences "I was nine. I thought she was simply busy." recreate the
child's limited understanding, and the final sentence reveals the adult's insight: the metaphor
"holding the whole house together with her hands" turns ordinary work into something heroic and
physical, so the reader shares the writer's late-arriving admiration.

*Why it works:* each point names a method, quotes briefly, and explains the effect; the answer tracks
the shift from child to adult perspective (R3) as well as language (R4).

## Common mistakes

- **Retelling the section** instead of answering how or why.
- **Feature-spotting**: "There is a simile" with no effect.
- **Quoting long sentences**: short, embedded quotations are easier to analyse.
- **Ignoring the section boundaries** set for a question.
- **Treating the narrator as a character in a novel**: in non-fiction it is the writer's real view,
  often shaped by hindsight.
- **Commenting only on language** when the question asks about structure or the whole text.

## Exam technique: typical question stems

Question wording varies from paper to paper, but stems like these are common in reading papers, and
each points to a different skill:

- **"What do you learn…" / "List…"**: select relevant details accurately (R1); brief answers are fine.
- **"Explain…"**: give the detail and say what it shows.
- **"How does the writer…"**: analyse methods and effects (R4).
- **"What impressions…" / "What do you think and feel about…"**: interpret attitudes (R3), supported
  by evidence.
- **"To what extent…" / "Evaluate…"**: weigh how successfully the writer achieves an effect across the
  text, and reach a judgement.

Divide your time by the marks shown for each question, and leave enough for Section B, which carries
the same 40 marks.
