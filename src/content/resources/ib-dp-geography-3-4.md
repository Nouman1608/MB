This guide goes deeper into sub-topic 3.4 of Option C, Extreme environments, in IB Diploma Programme Geography (first assessments 2019): extreme environments futures. The [3.4 revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-geography-extreme-environments-extreme-environments-futures-revision-notes/) already cover Arctic amplification, peak water, glacial lake outburst floods, permafrost subsidence, the carbon and ice-albedo feedbacks, new shipping routes, the Arctic Council and desert solar power. This page adds four themes that make future-focused answers more sophisticated: competing claims to the Arctic seabed, conflicts created by low-carbon development, the future of desert cities, and how engineering must adapt to a moving baseline. It ends with a method for writing scenario answers. Practise with the [3.4 practice questions](/resources/ib-dp-geography-3-4-practice/); the [Extreme environments study guide](/resources/ib-dp-geography-extreme-environments-study-guide/) gives the overview.

## What this sub-topic asks you to do

- Discuss how extreme environments may change in future, through climate change and human activity.
- Evaluate new opportunities and the risks that come with them.
- Consider how resilient different communities are, and who decides the future of these places.

## Who owns the Arctic seabed?

Under the UN Convention on the Law of the Sea, a coastal state controls seabed resources on its continental shelf. Beyond 200 nautical miles it can claim an **extended continental shelf** if it presents scientific evidence that the seabed is a natural prolongation of its land territory. Claims go to the UN Commission on the Limits of the Continental Shelf, which reviews the evidence but does not settle overlaps between states.

In the central Arctic Ocean, the **Lomonosov Ridge**, an undersea ridge crossing near the North Pole, lies at the centre of overlapping submissions by **Russia**, **Denmark** (through Greenland) and **Canada**. In 2007 a Russian submersible placed a flag on the seabed at the North Pole, an act with no legal force but strong symbolic meaning.

Why this matters for the future:

- As sea ice retreats, the economic value of seabed oil, gas and minerals may rise, even if extraction remains costly and risky.
- The legal process is slow and evidence-based, which has so far kept competition peaceful.
- Indigenous Arctic peoples are not parties to these claims between states, though organisations representing them sit on the Arctic Council as permanent participants.

## When low-carbon development creates conflict

The shift to low-carbon energy creates new demands on extreme environments: lithium and copper from deserts, wind and solar farms on open land, and hydroelectric dams in mountains. These projects can clash with existing uses.

**Fosen, Norway.** Two large wind farms were built on the Fosen peninsula, on land used for reindeer herding by Sami communities. In 2021 Norway's Supreme Court ruled that the licences for the wind farms violated the herders' rights to practise their culture under international human rights law. The turbines continued to operate after the ruling, which led to protests in 2023 and to later negotiations between the herders, the companies and the state.

The case shows a key tension for the future: a project that serves global climate goals may still impose local costs on indigenous people, so "green" development raises questions of justice as well as emissions.

## The future of desert cities

Many of the world's fastest-growing cities lie in dry regions. Their futures depend on water and heat.

- **Water.** Gulf states rely heavily on **desalination**, which secures supply but uses large amounts of energy and returns concentrated brine to the sea, raising salinity near outfalls. Inland desert cities depend on rivers and aquifers that may be over-allocated, as in the south-western United States.
- **Heat.** Rising temperatures and the urban heat island make outdoor work and life more dangerous; cities respond with shading, cooling centres, changed working hours and building design.
- **Energy.** Air conditioning and desalination raise electricity demand, but strong sunshine makes solar power a natural supply.

A resilient desert city combines diverse water sources, demand management, heat-adapted design and low-carbon energy. A vulnerable one depends on a single over-used source.

## Engineering for a moving baseline

Infrastructure in extreme environments was designed for conditions that are now changing. In permafrost regions, buildings and pipelines rest on piles frozen into the ground below the **active layer**, the layer that thaws each summer. As the climate warms, the active layer deepens.

**Worked example (invented figures).** A building's piles were designed on the assumption that the active layer would not exceed 1.2 m. The active layer is now 0.9 m and is deepening by 2 cm a year.

```
Remaining margin = 1.2 - 0.9 = 0.3 m = 30 cm
Years until the design limit is reached = 30 / 2 = 15 years
If the rate doubles to 4 cm a year: 30 / 4 = 7.5 years
```

A building expected to last 50 years may lose its safety margin within a decade or two. Adaptation options include thermosyphons that keep ground frozen, adjustable foundations, deeper piles for new buildings, and monitoring ground temperature. The wider lesson for future answers: plans based on past averages may fail, so **adaptive management** that monitors and adjusts is more robust.

## Writing a scenario answer

Questions about the future often reward answers that set out more than one possible outcome. A useful structure:

1. **Identify the drivers**: climate change, population and migration, resource demand, technology, governance.
2. **Build two or three scenarios**, for example a cooperative, well-governed future and a competitive, poorly regulated one.
3. **For each scenario**, say what happens to the environment and to different groups of people.
4. **Identify what decides** which scenario happens: emissions, agreements, enforcement, local rights.
5. **Conclude** with a judgement about which is more likely, or which is more desirable, and why.

**Example in brief: the Arctic in 2050.** In a cooperative scenario, shipping and extraction expand under strict environmental rules agreed through the Arctic Council and UNCLOS, indigenous peoples share in decisions and revenue, and spill-response capacity grows. In a competitive scenario, rising geopolitical tension weakens cooperation, extraction is poorly regulated, and indigenous communities face pollution and disruption to hunting with little say. Which future arrives depends on global emissions, relations between the Arctic states, and the strength of indigenous rights.

## Annotated model answer

*Discuss the possible futures of a cold environment you have studied.* **(sample paragraph)**

"In the Arctic, warming is reducing sea ice and deepening the active layer, which opens new shipping routes and resources but destabilises infrastructure **[drivers stated]**. In a cooperative future, shared rules and indigenous participation could allow economic gains while limiting harm **[scenario one]**; in a competitive future, unresolved seabed claims around the Lomonosov Ridge and weaker cooperation could lead to poorly regulated extraction **[scenario two with located detail]**. Indigenous communities, as the Fosen wind farm dispute in Norway shows, may bear local costs even from low-carbon projects **[equity dimension]**. The outcome is therefore uncertain and depends more on governance than on physical change alone **[judgement]**."

## Common mistakes

- Presenting one future as certain.
- Treating new opportunities as benefits only, without who bears the costs.
- Assuming low-carbon projects are automatically sustainable.
- Forgetting that infrastructure designed for past conditions may fail.
- Giving projected figures from memory; keep to trends unless the data are given.

## Exam technique

- **Discuss** futures with at least two scenarios and the factors that decide between them.
- **Evaluate** opportunities by weighing economic gains against environmental and social risks for named groups.
- **To what extent** questions about the future need a position on likelihood, not just a list of possibilities.
- Use time scales explicitly: the next decade, by mid-century, by 2100.
