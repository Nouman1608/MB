## Key definitions

- **Ideal gas equation:** pV = nRT, used with every variable in SI units.
- **Empirical formula:** the ratio of atoms of each element in a compound, in its simplest
  whole-number form.
- **Molecular formula:** how many atoms of each element are really present in the compound's formula.
- **Relationship:** molecular formula = empirical formula × a whole number, where the whole number is
  Mr ÷ empirical formula mass.

## 3.1.2.3 The ideal gas equation

### Formula and units

| Quantity | Symbol | SI unit | Common conversion |
|---|---|---|---|
| pressure | p | Pa | kPa × 10³; MPa × 10⁶ |
| volume | V | m³ | dm³ × 10⁻³; cm³ × 10⁻⁶ |
| amount | n | mol | n = m / M |
| gas constant | R | J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ | value given in the question |
| temperature | T | K | °C + 273 |

You are not expected to recall R (the specification says so). These notes use R = 8.31 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹.

### Rearrangements to know

| Find | Use |
|---|---|
| n | pV / RT |
| V | nRT / p |
| p | nRT / V |
| T | pV / nR |
| M of a gas or vapour | mRT / pV |

### Method in steps

1. List p, V, n (or m) and T from the question.
2. Convert each to SI units **before** substituting.
3. Rearrange for the unknown.
4. Substitute and calculate.
5. Convert the answer to the unit asked for (m³ to dm³: × 10³; m³ to cm³: × 10⁶).
6. Round to the number of significant figures in the least precise data.

### Worked reminder

0.0250 mol of argon is held in 600 cm³ at 300 K. Find the pressure in kPa.

- V = 600 × 10⁻⁶ m³ = 6.00 × 10⁻⁴ m³
- p = nRT / V = (0.0250 × 8.31 × 300) / (6.00 × 10⁻⁴) = 1.04 × 10⁵ Pa = **104 kPa**

### Mr of a volatile liquid in brief

- Inject a weighed mass of liquid into a heated gas syringe held above the liquid's boiling point.
- Mass injected = mass of small syringe before − after.
- Volume of vapour = final reading − starting reading.
- M = mRT / pV with T the syringe temperature and p the room pressure.
- Errors that make Mr too high: liquid not fully vaporised, vapour leaking, liquid lost from the needle
  before reweighing.

## 3.1.2.4 Empirical and molecular formula

### Method in steps

1. Masses or percentages of each element (find oxygen or a missing element by difference).
2. Divide each by Ar.
3. Divide all by the smallest.
4. Clear fractions: × 2 for a .5; × 3 for .33 or .67; × 4 for .25 or .75.
5. Write the empirical formula.
6. For the molecular formula: multiplier = Mr ÷ empirical formula mass; multiply every subscript.

### Worked reminder

A carbide contains 75.0% aluminium and 25.0% carbon by mass. (Use C = 12.0 and Al = 27.0.)

| | Al | C |
|---|---|---|
| ÷ Ar | 75.0 / 27.0 = 2.778 | 25.0 / 12.0 = 2.083 |
| ÷ smallest | 1.333 | 1.000 |
| × 3 | 4 | 3 |

Empirical formula **Al₄C₃**. Rounding 1.333 to 1 would give AlC, which is wrong.

### Metal oxide practical in brief

- Make the oxide by heating a weighed metal in a crucible, or reduce a weighed oxide in a stream of
  hydrogen.
- Heat to **constant mass** so you know the reaction is complete.
- Mass of oxygen = mass of oxide − mass of metal.
- Each mass by difference uses two balance readings, so double the reading uncertainty.

## Linking the two sections

Longer problems often join pV = nRT to formula work. The pattern is always the same: the gas data give
Mr, the composition gives the empirical formula, and Mr ÷ empirical formula mass gives the multiplier.

### Worked reminder

A gaseous fluorocarbon is 24.0% carbon and 76.0% fluorine by mass. A 0.450 g sample occupies
110 cm³ at 300 K and 102 kPa. Find its molecular formula. (Use C = 12.0 and F = 19.0.)

1. Moles of gas: n = pV / RT = (102 × 10³ × 110 × 10⁻⁶) / (8.31 × 300) = 4.50 × 10⁻³ mol.
2. Mr = 0.450 ÷ (4.50 × 10⁻³) = 100.
3. Ratio: C 24.0 / 12.0 = 2.00; F 76.0 / 19.0 = 4.00; scale to 1 : 2. Empirical formula CF₂
   (mass 50.0).
4. Multiplier = 100 ÷ 50.0 = 2. Molecular formula **C₂F₄**.

Note the order: composition alone cannot give the molecular formula, because CF₂, C₂F₄ and C₃F₆
share the same percentages. Only the Mr tells them apart.

## Significant figures and uncertainty

- Round the final answer to match the least precise piece of data (MS 1.1).
  In the reminder above the data are to 3 significant figures, so the amount is written as
  4.50 × 10⁻³ mol, keeping the final zero.
- Carry unrounded values through between steps. Round only once, at the end.
- A percentage uncertainty is (uncertainty ÷ measured value) × 100. For a 0.450 g mass found from two
  readings of ±0.001 g, that is (0.002 ÷ 0.450) × 100 = 0.44%.
- To combine uncertainties in a product or quotient such as mRT / pV, add the percentage
  uncertainties of the measured quantities.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Empirical vs molecular formula.** One is a simplest ratio, the other the true atom count; they
  coincide when the multiplier is 1.
- **Mr vs M.** Mr has no units. Molar mass M is in g mol⁻¹. M = mRT / pV gives M; quote Mr as the same
  number without units.
- **Pa vs kPa.** pV = nRT needs Pa. A value like "101" is almost certainly kPa.
- **m³ vs dm³ vs cm³.** 1 m³ = 10³ dm³ = 10⁶ cm³.
- **°C vs K.** Never put a Celsius temperature into pV = nRT.
- **Composition by mass vs percentage by mass.** Same method; with percentages you treat the values as
  masses in a 100 g sample.

## Quick self-test

1. Convert 2.5 MPa to Pa.
2. Convert 45.0 cm³ to m³.
3. Convert −23 °C to K.
4. Calculate the amount of gas in 2.00 dm³ at 100 kPa and 400 K.
5. Calculate the volume, in dm³, of 0.500 mol of gas at 500 K and 200 kPa.
6. 0.375 g of a gas occupies 210 cm³ at 300 K and 101 kPa. Calculate its Mr.
7. 0.640 g of sulfur combines with 0.960 g of oxygen. Find the empirical formula. (S = 32.1, O = 16.0)
8. A compound is 30.4% nitrogen and 69.6% oxygen by mass, with Mr = 92.0. Find its molecular formula.
   (N = 14.0, O = 16.0)
9. A hydrocarbon has empirical formula C₃H₇ and Mr = 86.0. Find its molecular formula.
10. A mole ratio comes out as C 1.00 : H 2.67. Write the empirical formula.
11. In a volatile-liquid experiment, some liquid stays as droplets. Is the calculated Mr too high or too
    low? Explain.

### Answers

1. 2.5 × 10⁶ Pa.
2. 4.50 × 10⁻⁵ m³.
3. 250 K.
4. n = (100 × 10³ × 2.00 × 10⁻³) / (8.31 × 400) = **0.0602 mol**.
5. V = (0.500 × 8.31 × 500) / (200 × 10³) = 1.04 × 10⁻² m³ = **10.4 dm³**.
6. M = (0.375 × 8.31 × 300) / (101 × 10³ × 210 × 10⁻⁶) = **44.1**.
7. S: 0.640 / 32.1 = 0.01994; O: 0.960 / 16.0 = 0.0600; ratio 1 : 3.01, so **SO₃**.
8. N: 2.171; O: 4.350; ratio 1 : 2.00, so NO₂ (46.0). 92.0 ÷ 46.0 = 2, so **N₂O₄**.
9. C₃H₇ = 43.0; 86.0 ÷ 43.0 = 2, so **C₆H₁₄**.
10. Multiply by 3: 3 : 8, so **C₃H₈**.
11. Too **high**. The vapour volume is too small, and M = mRT / pV has V on the bottom.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Substituting kPa, cm³ or °C into pV = nRT without converting.
- Converting cm³ to m³ with ÷ 1000 (that gives dm³).
- Forgetting to change m³ back into the cm³ or dm³ the question wanted.
- Rounding mole values to one or two figures before dividing, so a 1.33 ratio is missed.
- Rounding 1.5 down to 1 instead of multiplying the whole ratio by 2.
- Using the mass of oxide in place of the mass of oxygen.
- Writing down only the empirical formula when the molecular one is required.
- Showing no division of Mr by the empirical formula mass, so the multiplier is not justified.
- Giving units with Mr, or quoting more significant figures than the data support.
- Saying "heat it for longer" instead of "heat to constant mass" when asked how to make sure a reaction
  is complete.
