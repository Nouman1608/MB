These questions cover **3.2.5 Newton's laws of motion** of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Physics (9630) specification. It is International AS content, assessed in Unit 1. Section A has short questions; Section B has longer, structured questions. In every question the mass is constant. If a point is unfamiliar, read the [Newton's laws study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-physics-9630-newtons-laws-of-motion-oxfordaqa/) or the [Newton's laws revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-physics-9630-newtons-laws-of-motion-oxfordaqa-revision-notes/) first. Take g = 9.81 N kg⁻¹.

## Questions

### Section A

**1.** State Newton's second law of motion for an object of constant mass. **[1]**

**2.** Which pair of forces is a Newton's third law pair?

A: the weight of a car and the normal contact force on the car

B: the Earth's pull on the Moon and the Moon's pull on the Earth

C: the driving force on a car and the drag on the car

D: the tension in a rope and the weight of the mass hanging from it

**[1]**

**3.** A 0.25 kg puck slides across ice and slows uniformly from 6.0 m s⁻¹ to 4.0 m s⁻¹ in 2.0 s. Calculate the frictional force on it. **[2]**

**4.** A car of mass 900 kg accelerates at 2.0 m s⁻² along a level road against resistive forces of 600 N. Calculate the driving force. **[2]**

**5.** A spacecraft far from any planet or star switches off its engines while moving. State and explain how it moves afterwards. **[2]**

**6.** A helicopter hovers at a fixed height. State the resultant force on it, and use Newton's third law to explain how the rotor produces an upward force. **[2]**

**7.** A 4.0 kg mass has two horizontal forces acting on it: 10 N east and 6 N west. What is its acceleration?

A: 1.0 m s⁻² east

B: 1.5 m s⁻² west

C: 2.5 m s⁻² east

D: 4.0 m s⁻² east

**[1]**

### Section B

**8.** A locomotive of mass 8.0 × 10⁴ kg pulls two carriages, each of mass 3.0 × 10⁴ kg, along a level track. The driving force on the locomotive is 1.4 × 10⁵ N. Each of the three vehicles has a resistive force of 1.0 × 10⁴ N acting on it.

**(a)** Calculate the acceleration of the train. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the tension in the coupling between the locomotive and the first carriage. **[2]**

**(c)** Calculate the tension in the coupling between the two carriages. **[1]**

**9.** A 4.0 kg block on a rough horizontal table is joined by a light string over a smooth pulley to a 2.0 kg mass hanging freely. The frictional force on the block is 6.0 N. The system is released from rest.

**(a)** Calculate the acceleration of the system. **[3]**

**(b)** Calculate the tension in the string. **[2]**

**(c)** Calculate the speed of the hanging mass after it has fallen 0.50 m. **[1]**

**10.** A skier of mass 70 kg moves straight down a slope at 15° to the horizontal. The total resistive force on her is 60 N.

**(a)** Calculate the component of her weight acting down the slope. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate her acceleration. **[2]**

**(c)** Lower down, she moves at constant velocity. State the total resistive force on her then, and explain your answer. **[2]**

**11.** A book rests on a table, which stands on the floor.

**(a)** Name the two forces acting on the book and state the direction of each. **[2]**

**(b)** For each force in (a), state its Newton's third law partner, naming the object it acts on and the type of force. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain why the two forces in (a) are equal even though they are not a third-law pair. **[1]**

**12.** A horse pulls a cart along a level road.

**(a)** A student says: "The cart pulls back on the horse with a force equal to the horse's pull on the cart, so they cancel and the cart can never start moving." Explain why the student is wrong. **[4]**

**(b)** The forward push of the ground on the horse is 1200 N. The total resistive force on the cart is 400 N, and resistance on the horse can be ignored. The horse has mass 500 kg and the cart 300 kg. Calculate the acceleration and the pulling force between the horse and the cart. **[2]**

## Answers

### Section A

**1.** The resultant force on an object is equal to its mass multiplied by its acceleration, F = ma, and acts in the direction of the acceleration [1] **[1]**

**2.** **B** [1] **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* A, C and D are all pairs of forces acting on one object. Only B has two forces of the same type on two different bodies.

**3.** a = (4.0 − 6.0) ÷ 2.0 = −1.0 m s⁻² [1]; F = 0.25 × 1.0 = **0.25 N**, opposing the motion [1] **[2]**

**4.** Resultant = 900 × 2.0 = 1800 N [1]; driving force = 1800 + 600 = **2400 N** [1] **[2]**

**5.** It continues in a straight line at constant speed (constant velocity) [1]; there is no resultant force on it, so by the first law its velocity does not change [1] **[2]**

**6.** Resultant force = **zero** [1]; the rotor pushes air downwards, so the air pushes the rotor upwards with an equal force [1] **[2]**

**7.** **A** [1] **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Resultant = 10 − 6 = 4 N east, so a = 4 ÷ 4.0 = 1.0 m s⁻². Option C adds the forces as if they acted in the same direction.

### Section B

**8.**

**(a)** Resultant = 1.4 × 10⁵ − 3 × 1.0 × 10⁴ = 1.1 × 10⁵ N [1]; a = 1.1 × 10⁵ ÷ 1.4 × 10⁵ = **0.79 m s⁻²** [1]

**(b)** The coupling pulls both carriages (6.0 × 10⁴ kg) against 2.0 × 10⁴ N of resistance: T − 2.0 × 10⁴ = 6.0 × 10⁴ × 0.786 [1]; T = **6.7 × 10⁴ N** [1]

**(c)** T − 1.0 × 10⁴ = 3.0 × 10⁴ × 0.786, so T = **3.4 × 10⁴ N** [1] **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Isolate the part of the train behind the coupling. That way the locomotive's driving force never enters the equation.

**9.**

**(a)** Hanging mass: 2.0 × 9.81 − T = 2.0a [1]; block: T − 6.0 = 4.0a [1]; adding: 19.6 − 6.0 = 6.0a, so a = **2.27 m s⁻²** [1]

**(b)** T = 4.0 × 2.27 + 6.0 [1] = **15.1 N** [1]

**(c)** v² = 2 × 2.27 × 0.50 = 2.27, so v = **1.5 m s⁻¹** [1] **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Check (b) with the other equation: 19.6 − 15.1 = 4.5 N, and 2.0 × 2.27 = 4.5 N. A quick check like this catches sign slips.

**10.**

**(a)** 70 × 9.81 × sin 15° = **178 N** [1]

**(b)** Resultant = 178 − 60 = 118 N [1]; a = 118 ÷ 70 = **1.7 m s⁻²** [1]

**(c)** **178 N** [1]; constant velocity means zero acceleration, so the resultant force is zero and the resistive force equals the weight component down the slope [1] **[5]**

**11.**

**(a)** Weight (gravitational pull of the Earth), acting downwards [1]; normal contact force from the table, acting upwards [1]

**(b)** Partner of the weight: the gravitational pull of the book on the Earth, acting upwards on the Earth [1]; partner of the normal force: the contact force of the book pushing down on the table [1]

**(c)** The book is not accelerating, so by the first law (or F = ma with a = 0) the resultant force on it is zero [1] **[5]**

**12.**

**(a)** The two forces of the third-law pair act on different objects, the horse and the cart, so they cannot cancel [1]; whether the cart accelerates depends only on the forces acting on the cart [1]; the cart accelerates if the horse's forward pull on it is greater than the resistive forces on the cart [1]; the horse accelerates because the ground pushes it forward (the third-law partner of the horse pushing back on the ground) with more force than the cart pulls back on it [1]

**(b)** System: 1200 − 400 = 800 N on 800 kg, so a = **1.0 m s⁻²** [1]; cart: P − 400 = 300 × 1.0, so P = **700 N** [1] **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* The key phrase in (a) is "act on different objects". Without it, the explanation does not reach the source of the student's error.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using a single force in F = ma instead of the resultant.
- Treating balanced forces on one object as a third-law pair.
- Two-body problems with no separate equation for each body.
- Leaving the frictional force out of the block's equation in a pulley problem.
