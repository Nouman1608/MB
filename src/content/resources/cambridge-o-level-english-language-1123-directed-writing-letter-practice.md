## Section A

**1.** Give the correct sign-off for each salutation: (a) "Dear Editor" (b) "Dear Mr Osei". **[2]**

**2.** Rewrite this sentence in a polite but firm register for a letter to a head teacher: "Your new homework rule is totally unfair and you need to scrap it." **[2]**

**3.** Write an opening sentence for a letter to a supermarket manager complaining that the store has stopped stocking local fruit. **[2]**

**4.** Identify two features that make this sentence too informal for a letter to a council: "Honestly, the park's a total mess and loads of people are fed up." **[2]**

**5.** Write a closing request for a letter asking a sports centre to extend its weekend opening hours. **[1]**

**6.** Explain one way a letter to a newspaper editor differs from a letter to a company manager. **[1]**

## Section B

Read the two texts (original, written for this practice set).

> **Text 1: from an article in the local newspaper**
>
> The empty plot behind Station Road has been used as a dumping ground for years. The council now
> plans to turn it into a forty-space car park. Shopkeepers on Station Road welcome the idea, saying
> that customers often drive away because they cannot park. A council spokesperson said the car park
> would "bring life back to the high street".
>
> **Text 2: from a residents' group leaflet**
>
> We have a better idea: a community garden. Volunteers have already offered to clear the plot, and a
> local garden centre has promised plants. The nearest park is a mile away, and many families in the
> flats on Station Road have no garden at all. A car park would add traffic outside the primary school
> on the corner. We have asked the council to meet us, but have had no reply.

**7.** Identify one point from each text that you would use, and one claim you would evaluate, in a letter to the newspaper supporting the community garden. **[3]**

**8.** Write the opening paragraph (about 50 words) of the letter to the editor. **[3]**

**9.** Write a body paragraph (about 80 words) that evaluates the council's claim that the car park would "bring life back to the high street". **[4]**

**10.** Write a paragraph (about 60 words) that concedes the shopkeepers' concern and answers it. **[3]**

**11.** The task changes: *write a letter to the council's head of planning*. Explain two changes you would make to the letter to the editor. **[2]**

**12.** Write a letter to the editor of the local newspaper giving your views on the future of the plot. Use and evaluate ideas from both texts. Write about 250–350 words. **[25]**

## Answers

**1.** (a) "Yours faithfully" **[1]** (b) "Yours sincerely" **[1]**

**2.** For example: "I am concerned that the new homework rule places an unfair burden on students, and I would ask you to reconsider it." **[2]** One mark for a courteous register, one for keeping a clear, firm request.

**3.** For example: "I am writing as a regular customer to express my disappointment that your store no longer stocks fruit from local farms." **[2]** One mark for stating who is writing, one for the issue and view.

**4.** Any two **[2]**: the informal "Honestly" as an opener; the contraction "park's"; "total mess"; "loads of"; "fed up".

**5.** For example: "I would be grateful if you would consider opening until 8 p.m. on Saturdays and Sundays." **[1]**

**6.** For example: a letter to the editor is written for the newspaper's readers as well as the editor, so it can appeal to the community and usually refers to an article or event **[1]**.

**7.** One mark each:
- Use from Text 1: shopkeepers say customers drive away because they cannot park **[1]**.
- Use from Text 2: families in the flats have no garden, and the nearest park is a mile away **[1]**.
- Evaluate: the claim that the car park would "bring life back to the high street" is an unsupported prediction from the council, which wants the scheme **[1]**.

**8.** Model:

> Dear Editor,
>
> Your report last week on the plot behind Station Road described the council's plan for a car park.
> As a resident of Station Road, I would like to argue that a community garden would do far more for
> our street, and for the families who live on it.

Marking: correct salutation and reference to the article **[1]**; writer's role and the issue **[1]**; clear view **[1]**.

**9.** Model:

> The council claims that a car park would "bring life back to the high street". It is an appealing
> phrase, but it is a hope, not evidence: no figures are given for how many shoppers would come. Life
> on a street is not only measured in cars. A garden would bring families, volunteers and schoolchildren
> to Station Road every week, and people who walk to a garden are just as likely to stop at the shops
> on the way home.

Marking: identifies the claim **[1]**; evaluates its evidence **[1]**; develops an alternative view of "life" **[1]**; formal persuasive register **[1]**.

**10.** Model:

> I understand the shopkeepers' frustration. If customers cannot park, some will go elsewhere. However,
> forty spaces will not solve that problem for long, and the extra traffic would pass the primary
> school at the busiest times of day. A short-stay parking scheme on nearby streets might help the
> shops without losing the garden.

Marking: concedes fairly **[1]**; answers with a reason from the texts **[1]**; proposes a developed alternative **[1]**.

**11.** For example: change the salutation to the named head of planning ("Dear Mr / Ms...") with "Yours sincerely" **[1]**; make a direct, specific request, such as a meeting with the residents' group, rather than appealing to newspaper readers **[1]**.

**12.** Marked out of 25: reading (R3, R5) and writing (W1–W5).
- **Reading:** uses points from both texts; develops them (families without gardens, school traffic, volunteers and donated plants); evaluates them (the council's unsupported prediction, the shopkeepers' interest, the council's failure to reply to residents, whether forty spaces would really help) **[10]**.
- **Writing:** letter-to-the-editor conventions (salutation, reference to the article, clear view, sign-off "Yours faithfully"); a clear argument with a counter-point; formal persuasive register aimed at readers; varied vocabulary and sentences; accurate spelling, punctuation and grammar **[15]**.

Either view, garden or car park, can score fully if it is argued from the sources. Lower-level responses retell the texts, address the council instead of the editor, or lose courtesy.

## Where marks are usually lost

- **Question 2:** removing the request entirely, so the sentence becomes polite but weak.
- **Question 9:** calling the council dishonest instead of questioning its evidence.
- **Question 11:** changing only the sign-off.
- **Question 12:** writing "Dear Editor" and then addressing the council as "you" throughout.
