This guide covers **4.1, Networks**, the first half of Topic 4 in
Cambridge IGCSE Information and Communication Technology 0417. It
follows [Topic 3, Storage devices and
media](/resources/igcse-ict-storage-devices-and-media/) and covers the
hardware, connection methods and structures that make networks work,
ahead of [4.2, Network issues and
communication](/resources/igcse-ict-network-issues-and-communication/).

## The router

You need to know the operation and purpose of a router, covering three
specific functions: connecting networks and devices to the internet,
storing computer addresses (so it knows where to send incoming data),
and routing data packets -- directing each packet of data along an
appropriate path toward its destination. A router sits at the boundary
between a local network and the wider internet, making it the single
device most commonly named in questions about how a home or school
network reaches the internet.

## Common network devices

Beyond the router, you need to know the purpose of network interface
cards (NIC), hubs, bridges, and switches. An NIC is the hardware that
lets an individual device connect to a network at all. A hub broadcasts
incoming data to every device connected to it, regardless of which
device the data is actually intended for -- simple but inefficient. A
switch improves on this by learning which device is connected to which
port and sending data only to the intended recipient, making better use
of network capacity. A bridge connects two separate network segments
together so they function as one combined network.

## Wi-fi and Bluetooth

You need to know the uses of wi-fi and Bluetooth, how to connect a
device to a network using each, and the similarities and differences
between them. Both are wireless connection methods, but they serve
different purposes: wi-fi typically connects a device to a wider
network (and through it, the internet) over a larger range, while
Bluetooth typically connects two devices directly to each other over a
much shorter range, commonly for peripherals such as headphones or a
wireless keyboard, without needing a network infrastructure in
between.

## Cloud computing

You need to know the characteristics, uses and issues relating to
cloud computing, including how data is stored, managed and shared this
way, and the advantages and disadvantages of cloud storage compared
with other storage methods. Cloud storage's main advantages are
accessibility (data can be reached from any internet-connected device)
and reduced reliance on local storage hardware; its main disadvantages
are dependency on a working internet connection to access the data at
all, and reliance on a third-party provider for the data's security and
availability.

## Network environments: extranet, intranet, and the internet

You need to know the characteristics, uses and purpose of an extranet,
an intranet, and the internet, and the differences and similarities
between them. An **intranet** is a private network, internal to a
single organisation, accessible only to its own members. An
**extranet** extends a private network to allow controlled access for
specific external parties, such as trusted suppliers or customers,
while still restricting general public access. The **internet** is the
global, publicly accessible network that connects networks worldwide.
The shared thread across all three is the same underlying networking
technology; what differs is who is permitted to access it.

## Network types: LAN, WLAN and WAN

You need to know Local Area Network (LAN), Wireless Local Area Network
(WLAN), and Wide Area Network (WAN), and the differences between them.
A LAN covers a small geographic area, typically a single building or
site, connected by cabling. A WLAN is functionally the wireless
equivalent of a LAN, covering a similar small area but using wireless
connections (such as wi-fi) instead of cables. A WAN covers a much
larger geographic area, often connecting multiple LANs across
different sites, cities or countries -- the internet itself is the
largest example of a WAN.

## A worked scenario

A school wants students to access shared coursework files from any of
its classrooms, its library, and their own homes, while also letting a
small number of approved textbook suppliers upload new resources
directly. The classroom-to-library connection within the school
building is best served by a WLAN (wireless, single site). Reaching
the internet from that network requires a router, handling address
storage and packet routing to and from the wider internet -- itself a
WAN. Student access to shared coursework from home could be met either
by cloud storage (accessible from any internet-connected device
without needing to reach the school's own servers directly) or by a
remotely-accessible intranet-style system. The supplier upload access
is the clearest case for an **extranet**: controlled, limited external
access to specific resources, rather than a fully public internet
system or a fully closed internal-only intranet. Working through a
combined scenario like this, rather than revising each term in
isolation, is a good check that the distinctions above are genuinely
understood rather than just memorised.

## How to approach it

Learn the router's three named functions (connecting to the internet,
storing addresses, routing data packets) as a specific list rather
than a vague "connects to the internet" answer, since exam mark
schemes credit each function separately. Practise distinguishing hub,
switch and bridge by what happens to incoming data at each device --
broadcast to all (hub), directed to the intended recipient only
(switch), or joining two segments (bridge) -- since these three are
frequently confused with each other. For network types, anchor LAN,
WLAN and WAN to a concrete scale (a single building, the same building
wirelessly, and multiple sites) rather than abstract definitions, and
be ready to classify a described network (for example, "a school
connects its three campuses across a city") using the correct term
with a stated reason.
