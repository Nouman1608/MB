## What each section actually assesses

Component 1 is worth 90 marks in total, closed book, over 2 hours --
60% of the whole IGCSE. Section A (20 marks) is pure AO2: since the
unseen poem is printed on the paper, no memorisation is needed, and
the mark is entirely for analytical skill in explaining how meaning
and effect are created through language, form and structure. Section
B (30 marks) assesses AO2 and AO3 together, and AO3 specifically means
a response that analyses two poems well but never directly compares
them is missing marks it could otherwise have earned. Section C (40
marks) assesses AO1, an informed and textually supported argument, and
AO4, understanding of context, and rewards tracking how a theme or
character develops across the whole novel rather than focusing on one
memorable scene, with social, historical or cultural context woven
into the argument rather than added as a separate paragraph at the
end.

## Section A (practise using any unseen poem)

**1.** Choose a short unseen poem. Explore how the poet presents their central idea or feeling, referring closely to language, form and structure. **[20]**

## Section B (practise using two poems from your own anthology)

**2.** Compare how two poems from your studied anthology present a shared theme (e.g. loss, conflict, identity, nature). **[30]**

## Section C (practise using your own studied prose text)

**3.** Explore how the writer of your studied modern prose text presents the development of a central character across the novel. **[40]**

---

## Answers (worked approach — marks depend on your chosen texts)

**1.** A top-band response identifies the central idea precisely [4], then builds a sustained analysis of **language** (imagery, word choice, sound), **form** (rhyme, meter, stanza shape) and **structure** (how the poem develops or shifts) [12], with every point rooted in a short, precisely selected quotation and a clear "effect" explained, not just named [4].

**2.** The strongest answers organise **by point of comparison**, not poem-by-poem (e.g. "both poets use natural imagery, but Poem A does X while Poem B does Y") [10], analyse language/form/structure in each poem with precision [12], and make the comparison **explicit and sustained** throughout rather than only in a closing paragraph [8].

**3.** Track the character across **at least three distinct points** in the novel (beginning, a turning point, and the ending or a late development) [16], using specific, well-chosen textual evidence at each point [12], and integrate relevant **context** — social, historical, cultural — that illuminates why the character develops as they do, rather than adding context as a disconnected final paragraph [12].

## An additional practice question for each section

**4. (Section A)** Choose a different short unseen poem from question 1. Explore how the poet uses structure specifically — line length, stanza breaks, enjambment or caesura — to shape the reader's response. **[20]**

**5. (Section B)** Compare how two poems from your anthology use imagery drawn from the natural world to explore a human emotion or relationship. **[30]**

**6. (Section C)** Explore how the writer of your studied prose text uses a specific setting to reflect or influence a character's state of mind at two different points in the novel. **[40]**

### Worked approach for questions 4-6

**4.** As with question 1, this is pure AO2, but the question narrows the focus onto structure specifically, so a top-band answer should not drift into discussing imagery or word choice as its main evidence — structural features (where lines break, where a stanza ends, where a sentence overruns a line) should carry the analysis, each tied to a precise effect on pacing or emphasis.

**5.** This question again rewards point-by-point comparison over separate poem-by-poem treatment, but because it specifies imagery as the shared feature, both poems must be shown to use natural imagery in a genuinely comparable way — a strong answer identifies not just that both poems use nature imagery, but how each poet's specific choices (a storm versus a wilting flower, for instance) produce a different emotional effect.

**6.** Setting-based character analysis is a common Section C angle: track the same setting, or a contrasting pair of settings, at two clearly separated points in the novel, and use each to make an explicit claim about the character's development, rather than describing the setting for its own sake.

## Timing and exam strategy

Because the three sections are not equally weighted, time should be allocated roughly in proportion to their marks rather than split evenly across two hours: more time for Section C's 40 marks than Section B's 30, and the least time for Section A's 20, while still leaving a few minutes at the end to check all three responses. A common cause of a disappointing overall mark is spending too long perfecting Section A's shorter response and running out of time on Section C, where the largest number of marks is actually available.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Section B answers that analyse each poem in a separate half rather than comparing directly, point by point.
- Section C answers that retell the plot instead of tracking character or theme development with a clear argument.
- Context in Section C treated as a bolt-on paragraph rather than woven into the analysis.
- Uneven time management — remember the marks are not equal across sections (20/30/40).
- Writing about only one poem in Section B when direct comparison is explicitly credited by AO3.
- Forgetting that the poetry anthology and prose novel rotate by examination series — always confirm which texts apply to your own series before revising.
