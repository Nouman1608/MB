## What each section actually assesses

Component 1 is worth 90 marks in total, over 2 hours -- 60% of the whole
IGCSE. Section A (20 marks) is pure AO2: since the unseen poem is printed
on the paper, no memorisation is needed, and the mark is entirely for
analytical skill in explaining how meaning and effect are created through
language, form and structure. Section B (30 marks) assesses AO2 and AO3
together, and AO3 specifically means a response that analyses two poems
well but never directly compares them is missing marks it could otherwise
have earned; the real paper offers a choice of two questions in this
section, one naming both poems to compare and the other naming one poem
and leaving the second choice to the candidate. Section C (40 marks)
assesses AO1, an informed and textually supported argument, and AO4,
understanding of context, and rewards tracking how a theme or character
develops across the whole novel rather than focusing on one memorable
scene, with social, historical or cultural context woven into the
argument rather than added as a separate paragraph at the end; this
section also offers a choice of two questions on the real paper.

**Set texts (for your own revision, not used in the practice below):** the
16-poem anthology and the seven-title modern prose list are named in full
in the [study guide](/resources/igcse-edexcel-english-literature-poetry-and-modern-prose/)
and the [revision notes](/resources/igcse-edexcel-english-literature-poetry-and-modern-prose-revision-notes/)
— both lists are revised by specification issue, not rotated by
examination series.

## Section A (an original unseen poem, for practice)

**1.** Read the poem below, then explore how the poet presents the speaker's
relationship to the empty house, referring closely to language, form and
structure. **[20]**

> **Empty House**
>
> The rooms still hold the shapes of what we owned—  
> a paler square where the mirror hung,  
> a groove worn soft into the third stair down  
> where a hand always reached, and never wrong.
>
> I open cupboards out of habit now,  
> not looking for anything, just checking  
> that absence has a texture I can touch,  
> that emptiness is also a kind of packing.
>
> The kettle's gone. The window's cleaner light  
> falls straight across a floor no rug will soften.  
> I stand here longer than I need to, counting  
> rooms that used to argue with each other,
>
> and find the silence is not built like grief—  
> it is built like waiting, patient, unconvinced,  
> as if the house itself still expects  
> the sound of someone coming back at six.
>
> *(An original poem written for this practice exercise — not a set text.)*

## Section B (answer (a), (b), or (c) — the real anthology pairing)

**2(a).** Compare how "Letters" and "The Call" below present the experience of
distance in a relationship. **[30]**

> **Letters**
>
> She keeps the drawer half-open, always,  
> as if paper needs air to breathe—  
> five years of blue ink turning grey,  
> words she reads with her hand pressed flat  
> against each page, like checking a pulse.
>
> The postmark says a country she has never seen.  
> The handwriting says a hand she has.

> **The Call**
>
> Three rings before you answer, always three—  
> long enough for me to picture you  
> crossing whatever room you're in  
> towards a voice that travelled further  
> than either of us likes to say.
>
> We talk about the weather on both ends,  
> two skies that never once agree,  
> and neither of us mentions  
> how the line goes quiet  
> in exactly the same place, each time.

**2(b).** Compare how "Riverbank" below presents the effect of time on a
relationship with a second poem of your own choosing on a related theme.
**[30]**

> **Riverbank**
>
> You taught me the names of things that don't stay still—  
> how a river argues with its own banks,  
> wearing them down so slowly  
> neither side notices the shape it's lost.
>
> I think of us like that sometimes:  
> two banks worn smooth by staying close  
> to something that kept moving anyway,  
> carrying off small pieces neither of us mourned  
> until we counted what was left.

**2(c).** Using your own copy of the anthology, compare how Shakespeare's
"Sonnet 116" and Browning's "My Last Duchess" each present a view of love.
**[30]**

## Section C (practise using your own studied prose text — answer either (a) or (b))

**3(a).** Explore how the writer of your studied modern prose text presents the
development of a central character across the novel. **[40]**

**3(b).** Explore how the writer of your studied modern prose text uses a
specific relationship to reveal a central theme. **[40]**

---

## Answer plans (approach — marks depend on your chosen texts and poems)

**1.** A top-band response identifies the central idea precisely, then builds
a sustained analysis of **language** (imagery, word choice, sound), **form**
(rhyme, meter, stanza shape) and **structure** (how the poem develops or
shifts), with every point rooted in a short, precisely selected quotation
and a clear "effect" explained, not just named. These objectives are marked
holistically against level descriptors rather than by splitting the total
across fixed per-point figures.

**2.** Whichever option you answer, the strongest responses organise **by
point of comparison**, not poem-by-poem (e.g. "both poems use domestic
detail, but 'Letters' does X while 'The Call' does Y"), analyse
language/form/structure in each poem with precision, and make the
comparison **explicit and sustained** throughout rather than only in a
closing paragraph. For 2(c), the real pairing rewards contrasting
Shakespeare's idealised, unconditional definition of love with the way
Browning's Duke's language of possession and control reveals a
disturbing, transactional view of love masquerading as devotion — the
comparison, not just a summary of each poem's content on its own, is
what AO3 credits. For 2(b), the strength of your chosen second poem
matters: pick one with a genuine point of connection to "Riverbank" rather
than a loosely related text. As with question 1, this is marked
holistically against level descriptors, not a fixed split of marks.

**3.** Track the character or theme across **at least three distinct
points** in the novel (beginning, a turning point, and the ending or a
late development), using specific, well-chosen textual evidence at each
point, and integrate relevant **context** — social, historical, cultural —
that illuminates why the character or theme develops as it does, rather
than adding context as a disconnected final paragraph. Again, this is
marked holistically rather than against a fixed per-point split.

## An additional practice question for each section

**4. (Section A)** Read the poem below, then explore how the poet uses
structure specifically — line length, stanza breaks, enjambment or caesura
— to shape the reader's response. **[20]**

> **Low Tide**
>
> The sea goes out  
> and out  
> and out,
>
> taking with it the noise of itself,  
> leaving a hush of ridged sand,  
> a single boat listing on nothing.
>
> We walk further than the sea allowed this morning,  
> find a shoe, a bottle, a shape too broken to name—
>
> and turn back only when the light starts turning,  
> only when the first line of water  
> begins, quietly,  
> to close.
>
> *(An original poem written for this practice exercise — not a set text.)*

**5. (Section B)** Compare how "Winter Apple" below uses imagery drawn from
the natural world to explore a human relationship with a second poem of
your own choosing on a related theme. **[30]**

> **Winter Apple**
>
> It hung on past the frost, past the bare branch,  
> past every other apple's quiet fall—  
> red going brown at the shoulder,  
> skin gone loose the way skin does  
> when it has held a shape too long.
>
> I picked it in December out of stubbornness,  
> found it still sweet beneath the wrinkling,  
> and understood, biting down,  
> that some things keep their sugar  
> long after they stop looking like they should.

**6. (Section C)** Explore how the writer of your studied prose text uses a
specific setting to reflect or influence a character's state of mind at two
different points in the novel. **[40]**

### Answer plans for questions 4-6

**4.** As with question 1, this is pure AO2, but the question narrows the
focus onto structure specifically, so a top-band answer should not drift
into discussing imagery or word choice as its main evidence — structural
features (where lines break, where a stanza ends, where a sentence
overruns a line) should carry the analysis, each tied to a precise effect
on pacing or emphasis.

**5.** As with question 2, this rewards point-by-point comparison over
separate poem-by-poem treatment, but because it specifies natural imagery
as the shared feature, your chosen second poem must be shown to use it in
a genuinely comparable way — a strong answer identifies not just that both
poems use nature imagery, but how each poet's specific choices produce a
different emotional effect.

**6.** Setting-based character analysis is a common Section C angle: track
the same setting, or a contrasting pair of settings, at two clearly
separated points in the novel, and use each to make an explicit claim
about the character's development, rather than describing the setting for
its own sake.

## Timing and exam strategy

The question paper itself prints a recommended timing for each section:
35 minutes for Section A, 40 minutes for Section B, and 45 minutes for
Section C — together the full two hours. That is not a strict proportional
split of the marks (a pure 40/30/20 proportion would give Section A less
time, not more, than its 35 minutes), so use the paper's own timings
rather than reworking them from the mark scheme, and leave a few minutes
at the end to check all three responses.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Section B answers that analyse each poem in a separate half rather than comparing directly, point by point.
- Section C answers that retell the plot instead of tracking character or theme development with a clear argument.
- Context in Section C treated as a bolt-on paragraph rather than woven into the analysis.
- Uneven time management — remember the marks are not equal across sections (20/30/40).
- Writing about only one poem in Section B when direct comparison is explicitly credited by AO3.
- Practising only on invented poems and never on the real anthology pairing (question 2(c)), since the actual exam draws on set texts, not original material.
- Forgetting that the set texts (poetry anthology and prose novel) are fixed by the specification and only change when Pearson issues a revised specification, not on a series-by-series rotation — see the study guide and revision notes for the current lists, and always confirm you're revising the texts for your own specification issue.
