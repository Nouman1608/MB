**What do these questions practise?** Sections 6 (Trade) and 7 (Transport and telecommunications)
of Cambridge IGCSE Pakistan Studies 0448 Paper 2: exports, imports, GNP and GDP, trading partners
and barriers, internal and international transport, dry ports and telecommunications. Each answer
shows the points a mark scheme would credit.

## Section A: recall

**1.** Name **two** of Pakistan's main exports and **two** of its main imports. **[4]**

**2.** What is meant by a *trade deficit*? **[1]**

**3.** State the difference between GNP and GDP. **[2]**

**4.** Name the pass used by the Karakoram Highway to cross into China. **[1]**

**5.** Name **three** seaports of Pakistan. **[3]**

**6.** Name **one** trading bloc or trade agreement that includes Pakistan. **[1]**

## Section B: explanation

**7.** Explain why Pakistan's import bill rises when world oil prices rise. **[3]**

**8.** Explain why Pakistan finds it difficult to increase its share of world trade. **[5]**

**9.** Describe and explain the pattern of the road and rail network in Pakistan. **[5]**

**10.** Compare road and rail transport for moving cotton bales from Multan to Karachi. **[4]**

**11.** What is a dry port? Explain **two** benefits of dry ports. **[5]**

**12.** Explain the problems of providing telecommunications in the mountainous north of Pakistan. **[4]**

## Section C: evaluation

**13.** Evaluate the benefits and problems of building motorways in Pakistan. **[6]**

**14.** "Telecommunications are more important than roads for Pakistan's development." How far do you
agree? **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** Exports, any two: cotton yarn, cloth, garments, rice, leather, sports goods, surgical
instruments [2]. Imports, any two: crude oil or petroleum products, machinery, chemicals, palm oil,
iron and steel, tea [2].

**2.** When the value of imports is greater than the value of exports [1].

**3.** GDP is the value of goods and services produced within Pakistan [1]; GNP adds net income
earned from abroad, such as remittances from overseas workers [1].

**4.** Khunjerab Pass [1].

**5.** Keamari (Karachi Port) [1], Port Qasim [1], Gwadar [1].

**6.** SAARC, ECO, or the EU's GSP+ scheme [1].

**7.** Pakistan imports most of its oil because domestic production is small [1]. Oil is needed for
transport, industry and thermal power stations, so demand cannot be cut quickly [1]. When the price
per barrel rises, the same quantity costs more foreign exchange, widening the trade deficit [1].

**8.** Any five: exports depend heavily on textiles [1], so a fall in world demand or prices cuts
earnings [1]; competition from other low-cost countries [1]; tariffs and quotas in richer markets
[1]; strict quality and safety standards [1]; energy shortages raise production costs [1]; weak
infrastructure delays shipments [1]; changing exchange rates [1].

**9.** Pattern: dense network across the Indus Plain of Punjab and Sindh [1], main routes running
north-east to south-west from Peshawar through Lahore to Karachi [1], sparse network in
Balochistan and the north [1]. Explanation: flat land and many people and industries make building
cheap and use high on the plain [1]; mountains, deserts and sparse population raise cost and lower
demand elsewhere [1]. Maximum 5.

**10.** Rail: cheaper per tonne for heavy, bulky loads over long distance [1]; links directly to the
port [1]. Road: more flexible, door-to-door from ginning factory to dock [1]; often faster because
rail services are slow and unreliable [1]. Credit a conclusion with a reason. Maximum 4.

**11.** A dry port is an inland terminal where goods are cleared through customs and packed into
sealed containers for transport to or from a seaport [1–2]. Benefits, any two developed: exporters
inland save time because customs checks are done near their factories [1–2]; reduced congestion at
Karachi's ports [1–2]; lower transport costs because containers move by rail [1–2]. Maximum 5.

**12.** Steep, rugged relief makes laying cables and building masts difficult and costly [1];
landslides, snow and earthquakes damage equipment [1]; population is small and scattered, so few
customers pay for the investment [1]; unreliable electricity supply for masts and equipment [1].

**13.** Benefits: faster, safer travel between cities such as Lahore and Islamabad [1]; lower costs
for moving goods [1]; attract industry along routes [1]; jobs in building and services [1].
Problems: very high construction cost and foreign loans [1]; farmland lost and people displaced
[1]; tolls are too high for some users [1]; little benefit to remote areas [1]. Judgement needed.
Maximum 6.

**14.** Telecoms, for: support e-commerce, banking, call centres and distance learning [1]; cheaper
than roads to extend to remote places [1]; give farmers market prices [1]. Roads, for: goods,
raw materials and people still need physical transport [1]; roads open markets for farm produce and
allow access to schools and hospitals [1]; tourism and industry depend on them [1]. A supported
judgement, for example that the two are complementary, earns the top mark. Maximum 6.

## Frequently asked questions

**How do I earn marks on pattern questions?**
First describe the pattern with directions and named
places, then explain it with physical and human factors.

**Should I learn trade figures?**
The syllabus asks about changes in types, amounts and value, so
learn a few current figures from your course statistics and quote the year.
