## Questions

**1.** Define the emf of a cell, and explain why the terminal pd is less than the emf when the cell supplies a current. **[3]**

**2.** A 24 Ω and an 8.0 Ω resistor are in parallel, and this pair is in series with a 9.0 Ω resistor across a 7.5 V supply.
**(a)** Calculate the total resistance and the supply current. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the current in the 8.0 Ω resistor. **[2]**

**3.** A small motor runs from a 6.0 V supply and draws 0.80 A for 2.0 minutes.
**(a)** Calculate the electrical energy supplied. **[2]**
**(b)** The motor is 25% efficient. Calculate the useful energy output. **[2]**

**4.** A 20 Ω and a 30 Ω resistor are connected across a 10 V supply.
**(a)** They are in series. Calculate the power dissipated in each. **[3]**
**(b)** They are now in parallel across the same supply. Calculate the power in each. **[2]**

**5.** A 12 V supply sends 2.4 A into a junction, which splits into two parallel branches. One branch carries 1.5 A.
**(a)** Find the current in the other branch. **[1]**
**(b)** Find the resistance of the other branch. **[2]**
**(c)** Name the conservation principle used in part (a). **[1]**
**(d)** Calculate the total power from the supply and state how it compares with the power in the two branches. **[2]**

**6.** A 9.0 V supply is across a 1.2 kΩ resistor in series with a 1.8 kΩ resistor.
**(a)** Calculate the pd across the 1.8 kΩ resistor. **[2]**
**(b)** A second 1.8 kΩ resistor is now connected across the first one. Calculate the new pd across them. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why the pd changed. **[1]**

**7.** A 6.0 V supply drives an ntc thermistor and a 4.0 kΩ resistor in series. A switching circuit monitors the pd across the 4.0 kΩ resistor. The thermistor has a resistance of 8.0 kΩ at 15 °C and 2.0 kΩ at 45 °C (data supplied).
**(a)** Calculate the output pd at each temperature. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why the monitored pd rises as the temperature rises. **[2]**
**(c)** A switching circuit operates when the output reaches 3.0 V. Calculate the thermistor resistance at that point. **[2]**

**8.** A 12 V supply is across a 2.0 kΩ fixed resistor in series with a variable resistor of range 0 to 6.0 kΩ. The output is across the fixed resistor.
**(a)** State the largest and smallest output pd, with the variable resistor setting for each. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate the variable resistor setting that gives 8.0 V. **[2]**

**9.** A battery has emf 9.0 V. When it supplies 1.5 A to a resistor, the terminal pd is 8.4 V.
**(a)** Calculate the internal resistance. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the resistance of the external resistor. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the current if the terminals were joined by a wire of negligible resistance. **[1]**
**(d)** Calculate the power wasted inside the battery in the original circuit. **[1]**

**10.** Two identical cells, each of emf 1.6 V and internal resistance 0.30 Ω, supply a 1.9 Ω resistor.
**(a)** They are connected in series. Calculate the current. **[3]**
**(b)** They are connected in parallel. Calculate the total current. **[3]**

**11.** A student investigates a cell by varying the current and measuring the terminal pd.
**(a)** Describe the circuit, including the position of each meter. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain how the emf and internal resistance are found from a graph of V against I. **[2]**
**(c)** The line of best fit passes through (0.20 A, 1.82 V) and (0.80 A, 1.40 V). Calculate the internal resistance and emf. **[3]**
**(d)** Give one reason for opening the switch between readings. **[1]**

## Answers

**1.** The emf is the energy the cell supplies per coulomb of charge it drives round the circuit [1]. When a current flows, some energy per coulomb is dissipated in the cell's internal resistance (lost volts, Ir) [1]. Terminal pd = ε − Ir, which is less than ε [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Definitions of emf gain credit for "per unit charge" or "per coulomb"; "the voltage of a cell" is too vague.

**2. (a)** Parallel: 1/R = 1/24 + 1/8 = 4/24, so R = 6.0 Ω [1]. RT = 6.0 + 9.0 = 15 Ω, and I = 7.5/15 = **0.50 A** [1]
**(b)** Pd across the pair = 0.50 × 6.0 = 3.0 V [1]. I = 3.0/8.0 = **0.375 A** [1]
*Examiner insight:* Keep the pd across the parallel pair separate from the supply pd. Using 7.5 V across the 8.0 Ω resistor is the usual wrong route.

**3. (a)** t = 120 s [1]. E = IVt = 0.80 × 6.0 × 120 = **576 J** [1]
**(b)** Useful = 0.25 × 576 [1] = **144 J** [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Converting minutes to seconds is part of the working. A final answer in the wrong units undermines otherwise correct working.

**4. (a)** I = 10/50 = 0.20 A [1]. P = I²R: 0.20² × 20 = **0.80 W** [1]; 0.20² × 30 = **1.2 W** [1]
**(b)** P = V²/R: 10²/20 = **5.0 W** [1]; 10²/30 = **3.3 W** [1]
*Examiner insight:* Choose the power form that matches the quantity shared: I²R in series, V²/R in parallel. Showing the form used makes the working easy to credit.

**5. (a)** 2.4 − 1.5 = **0.9 A** [1]
**(b)** R = V/I = 12/0.9 [1] = **13 Ω** (13.3 Ω) [1]
**(c)** Conservation of charge [1]
**(d)** P = IV = 2.4 × 12 = **29 W** (28.8 W) [1]. Branches: 1.5 × 12 + 0.9 × 12 = 18 + 10.8 = 28.8 W, equal to the supply power, so energy is conserved [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* If asked to name a principle, name it and say what it applies to, such as "charge is conserved at the junction".

**6. (a)** Vout = 9.0 × 1.8/(1.2 + 1.8) [1] = **5.4 V** [1]
**(b)** Parallel pair: 1/R = 2/1.8, so R = 0.90 kΩ [1]. Vout = 9.0 × 0.90/(1.2 + 0.90) = **3.86 V** [1]
**(c)** The load draws extra current, lowering the resistance of the lower section, so it takes a smaller share of the supply pd [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* "The resistance went down" alone is incomplete; link it to the share of the supply pd.

**7. (a)** 15 °C: 6.0 × 4.0/(4.0 + 8.0) = **2.0 V** [1]. 45 °C: 6.0 × 4.0/(4.0 + 2.0) = **4.0 V** [1]
**(b)** Thermistor resistance falls as temperature rises [1], so the total resistance falls, the current rises and the 4.0 kΩ resistor's pd rises [1]
**(c)** 3.0 = 6.0 × 4.0/(4.0 + Rt) [1], so 4.0 + Rt = 8.0 and Rt = **4.0 kΩ** [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* State which component the output is across before describing a trend. Output direction depends on that choice.

**8. (a)** Largest: variable resistor at 0 Ω, output **12 V** [1]. Smallest: variable resistor at 6.0 kΩ [1], output 12 × 2.0/8.0 = **3.0 V** [1]
**(b)** 8.0 = 12 × 2.0/(2.0 + R) [1], so 2.0 + R = 3.0 and R = **1.0 kΩ** [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Pair each extreme output with the setting that produces it. A bare pair of values is incomplete.

**9. (a)** Lost volts = 9.0 − 8.4 = 0.6 V [1]. r = 0.6/1.5 = **0.40 Ω** [1]
**(b)** R = 8.4/1.5 = **5.6 Ω** [1]
**(c)** I = ε/r = 9.0/0.40 = **22.5 A** (23 A) [1]
**(d)** P = I²r = 1.5² × 0.40 = **0.90 W** [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Calculating r from 9.0/1.5 (the emf over the current) gives the total resistance, not the internal resistance.

**10. (a)** Series: ε = 3.2 V, r = 0.60 Ω [1]. I = 3.2/(1.9 + 0.60) [1] = **1.28 A** [1]
**(b)** Parallel: ε = 1.6 V, r = 0.30/2 = 0.15 Ω [1]. I = 1.6/(1.9 + 0.15) [1] = **0.780 A** [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the combined emf and internal resistance before substituting. That way your method is easy to follow.

**11. (a)** Cell, variable resistor, ammeter and switch in series [1]; voltmeter connected across the cell's terminals only [1]
**(b)** Plot V against I. V = ε − Ir [1], so the gradient is −r and the vertical intercept is ε [1]
**(c)** Gradient = (1.40 − 1.82)/(0.80 − 0.20) = −0.70 V A⁻¹, so r = **0.70 Ω** [1]. Using V = ε − Ir: ε = 1.82 + 0.70 × 0.20 [1] = **1.96 V** [1]
**(d)** Continuous current heats the cell and drains it, changing its internal resistance and emf [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* A correct r is a positive number, so give the magnitude of the gradient. Reading ε from the last data point rather than the intercept is a common slip.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Not inverting 1/R in a parallel calculation.
- Using the supply pd across a branch that only receives part of it.
- Leaving time in minutes in E = IVt.
- Choosing P = V²/R in a series circuit where the pd is not known across the resistor.
- Treating a loaded divider as unloaded.
- Describing a thermistor or LDR trend without stating which component the output is across.
- Dividing the emf by the current and calling the result the internal resistance.
- Not stating the combined emf and internal resistance for cells in series or parallel.
- Answering with the terminal pd where the emf is asked for.
