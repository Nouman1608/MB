Content tested: "collectivisation; the Five Year Plans; social and economic consequences for Kulaks, city dwellers, women, professional and industrial workers; the extent of modernisation."

## Section A: knowledge and description

**1.** Give two reasons why Stalin introduced the Five-Year Plans. **[2]**

**2.** Describe two features of collectivisation. **[4]**

**3.** Describe two features of the Stakhanovite movement. **[4]**

**4.** Describe two features of life for city dwellers in the USSR in the 1930s. **[4]**

## Section B: change, interpretations and judgement

**5.** In what ways were the lives of kulaks affected by collectivisation? Explain your answer. **[8]**

**6.** In what ways were professional workers affected by Stalin's modernisation of the USSR? Explain your answer. **[8]**

**7.** In what ways were the lives of industrial workers affected by the Five-Year Plans? Explain your answer. **[8]**

**Interpretation A** *(invented)* -- From a history of the Soviet economy written in 1987 by an American economist:
"In barely a decade a nation of wooden ploughs became a nation of steel mills and power stations. Without the factories built in the Urals in the 1930s, the Red Army could not have defeated Hitler. Whatever the cost, Stalin's plans worked."

**Interpretation B** *(invented)* -- From the memoir of a woman who grew up on a Ukrainian collective farm, published in 1983:
"They told us we were building socialism. What we built were graves. The brigade took the last of our grain in the autumn of 1932, and by spring our village was silent. My mother worked in the fields until she could not stand. I have never been able to call that progress."

**8.** Why might the authors of Interpretations A and B have a different interpretation about Stalin's modernisation of the USSR? Explain your answer using Interpretations A and B and your contextual knowledge. **[4]**

**9.** Which interpretation gives the more convincing opinion about Stalin's modernisation of the USSR? Explain your answer based on your contextual knowledge and what Interpretations A and B say. **[8]**

**10.** Which of the following was the more important reason why Stalin introduced collectivisation: the need for grain; communist ideas? Explain your answer with reference to both reasons. **[12]**

**11.** Which of the following was the more important consequence of Stalin's modernisation for the USSR by 1941: industrial growth; the suffering it caused? Explain your answer with reference to both consequences. **[12]**

## Answers

**How these guides work.** "Describe": each of two features identified [1] and developed [1]. "In what ways": each of two ways identified [1], evidenced [1] and explained as a change [2]. Question 2-style interpretation answers: each author's provenance linked to what they say [2] + [2]. Question 3-style: each interpretation tested with knowledge [3] + [3], judgement [2]. 12-mark essays: each bullet explained with evidence [4] + [4], linked judgement [4].

**1.** Any two, [1] each: to strengthen defence against attack by capitalist powers; to catch up with the industrial West; to build a socialist economy based on state ownership; to increase Stalin's power and the party's control; to produce machinery for agriculture.

**2.** Feature one [2]: private farms were merged into collective farms (*kolkhozy*) [1], which had to deliver fixed grain quotas to the state [1]. Feature two [2]: the kulaks were targeted [1]; from December 1929 Stalin called for their "liquidation as a class", and many were deported [1]. Also creditable: Machine Tractor Stations supplied machinery and party supervision; peasant resistance by slaughtering livestock; the famine of 1932-33.

**3.** Feature one [2]: it was named after Alexei Stakhanov [1], a miner reported in 1935 to have cut many times his quota of coal in one shift [1]. Feature two [2]: record-breaking workers were held up as models in propaganda [1] and rewarded with higher pay, better housing or other privileges [1]. Also creditable: targets for other workers were raised, causing resentment.

**4.** Feature one [2]: overcrowded housing [1]; many families shared one room in a communal flat or lived in barracks because housing could not keep up with migration from the countryside [1]. Feature two [2]: shortages of food and consumer goods [1]; food was rationed until 1935, and queuing for scarce goods was common [1]. Also creditable: expanding education and healthcare; harsh workplace discipline.

**5.** Way one [4]: they lost their land and property [1]; their farms, animals and tools were seized by the new collective farms [1]; so families who had been the most prosperous in their villages were left with nothing, and their way of life as independent farmers ended [2]. Way two [4]: they were deported, imprisoned or killed [1]; millions were sent to Siberia, Kazakhstan, the far north or labour camps after the call to "liquidate the kulaks as a class" in December 1929 [1]; so many died on the journey or in harsh settlements, and those who survived became forced labourers or outcasts [2].

**6.** Way one [4]: older specialists were distrusted and endangered [1]; in the Shakhty trial of 1928, engineers were accused of sabotage [1]; so engineers and managers trained before the revolution worked under suspicion and could be blamed for any failure [2]. Way two [4]: new opportunities for Soviet-trained specialists [1]; the plans needed huge numbers of engineers, technicians and managers, and a new generation trained in the 1930s was promoted quickly [1]; so young people from worker and peasant backgrounds could rise into professional jobs, although they too faced arrest if targets were missed, especially during the purges [2].

**7.** Way one [4]: harsher discipline [1]; lateness or absence could mean losing a job, housing or ration card, and labour books recorded employment from 1938 [1]; so workers lost control over when and where they worked and faced constant pressure to meet targets [2]. Way two [4]: rewards for high output [1]; wages were linked to production, and Stakhanovites gained higher pay and privileges [1]; so a skilled or record-breaking minority improved their position, while others resented rising targets [2]. Also creditable: forced labour by Gulag prisoners on projects such as the White Sea Canal; poor housing in new industrial cities such as Magnitogorsk.

**8.** A [2]: the author is an economist writing in 1987, looking at national production and the outcome of the war, so he judges modernisation by its industrial results and its contribution to victory over Hitler. B [2]: the author grew up on a Ukrainian collective farm and lived through the famine of 1932-33, losing members of her village; her personal experience of suffering leads her to judge modernisation by its human cost, which she will "never" call progress.

**9.** A tested [3]: heavy industry grew rapidly; new plants such as Magnitogorsk and the Dnieper dam were built; official figures claimed steel output rose from about 4 million tonnes in 1928 to about 18 million in 1940; industry in the Urals and Siberia supplied the Red Army after 1941. But A's "whatever the cost" ignores that figures were sometimes exaggerated and consumer goods neglected. B tested [3]: grain quotas left villages without food; the famine of 1932-33 killed millions, including in Ukraine (the Holodomor); grain was still exported; kulaks were deported. But B describes one village and does not consider industrial change. Judgement [2]: for example, A is more convincing about industry and defence, B about the countryside and the human cost; a strong answer decides which better describes "modernisation" as a whole and explains why.

**10.** The need for grain [4]: the 1927-28 grain crisis left cities short of food as peasants held back supplies; the growing industrial workforce had to be fed; grain exports paid for foreign machinery for the Five-Year Plans. Collective farms with fixed quotas made grain easier for the state to collect. Communist ideas [4]: communists saw private farming and the kulaks as capitalist; collective ownership was the socialist ideal; Stalin's call to "liquidate the kulaks as a class" shows an ideological as well as economic aim; Machine Tractor Stations extended party control. Linked judgement [4]: for example, the need for grain was more important because the timing of collectivisation followed the grain crisis and served the Five-Year Plans, while ideology shaped *how* it was done; or ideology was more important because Stalin continued exporting grain during the famine, showing control mattered more than feeding people. Credit a reasoned judgement.

**11.** Industrial growth [4]: new industries, cities and power stations; heavy industry multiplied in the 1930s; education and technical training expanded; industry moved east, which later helped the USSR survive the German invasion. Suffering [4]: the famine of 1932-33; the deportation of kulaks; forced labour in the Gulag; overcrowded city housing and rationing; harsh labour discipline; women's double burden. Linked judgement [4]: for example, industrial growth was more important for the USSR as a state because it made victory in 1941-45 possible, while the suffering was more important for the Soviet people; or the suffering was more important because it weakened agriculture for years and much of the growth depended on fear and forced labour. The judgement must compare the two directly.
