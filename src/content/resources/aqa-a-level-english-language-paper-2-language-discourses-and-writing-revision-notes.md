These notes condense the Paper 2 content on **methods of language analysis**, **language discourses** and **writing
skills** in AQA AS and A-level English Language (7701/7702), Version 1.2 (A-level subject content 3.2.2 to 3.2.4; 4.2.2 to 4.2.4 in the specification PDF).
Paper 2 is 2 hours 30 minutes, 100 marks and 40% of the A-level. Section B has a 40-mark analysis of two texts
about a language issue and a 30-mark directed writing task on the same issue.

## The answer in one paragraph

Section B tests whether you can see texts *about* language as arguments. Analyse how each text represents language,
builds an identity for its writer, positions its reader, and draws on wider discourses about language, comparing
the two texts throughout. Then write your own accessible, well-crafted piece for non-specialists on the same issue.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Discourse (about language) | A recurring way of talking about language that carries values, such as "standards are declining" |
| Prescriptivism | The view that there is a correct form of the language that people should follow |
| Descriptivism | The approach of describing how language is actually used, without judging it |
| Representation | How a text constructs an image of language, or of a group of speakers |
| Producer identity | The persona a writer builds (expert, ordinary person, guardian of standards) |
| Positioning | How a text invites the reader to take a particular view or role |
| Non-specialist audience | Readers with no training in linguistics: the target for directed writing |
| Discursive essay | An academic essay that weighs views on a language issue (Section A) |

## The six language levels (as listed in the specification)

| Level | Quick prompt for Section B |
|---|---|
| Phonetics, phonology and prosodics | Does the writer imitate or mock a pronunciation? |
| Graphology | What do headlines, images, fonts and layout signal? |
| Lexis and semantics | Which evaluative or metaphorical words frame language positively or negatively? |
| Grammar, including morphology | Are there imperatives, declaratives, modal verbs, lists, rhetorical questions? |
| Pragmatics | What is implied or presupposed? Is there irony or humour? |
| Discourse | How is the argument structured across the whole text? |

## The four questions for each text

1. How does it **represent** language?
2. What **identity** does it construct for the producer?
3. How does it **position the reader** and try to influence them?
4. How is it **connected to discourses** about language?

## Common discourses to recognise

| Discourse | Signals |
|---|---|
| Decline / deficit | Decay, disease and invasion metaphors; nostalgia; blame on youth or technology |
| Correctness | Lists of "errors"; "should" and "must"; appeals to rules |
| Natural change | Historical examples; hedged claims; references to research |
| Identity and pride | Dialect celebrated; inclusive pronouns |
| Linguistic prejudice | Anecdotes of being judged; lexis of fairness |

Useful references (use sparingly): Jean Aitchison's *The Language Web* (1997), with its "damp spoon", "crumbling
castle" and "infectious disease" metaphors for complaints about change; Deborah Cameron's *Verbal Hygiene* (1995).

## Method in steps: the 40-mark analysis

```
1. Read both texts; note genre, mode, audience, purpose, date and context.
2. Identify each writer's main view of language.
3. Find 3-4 shared points of comparison (views, methods, persona, positioning).
4. For each point: quote, name the feature, explain the effect, link to context and discourse.
5. Keep comparing throughout; finish with how the texts differ overall and why.
```

## Method in steps: the 30-mark directed writing

```
1. Check the form, audience and purpose in the task.
2. Decide your position on the issue.
3. Plan 4-5 points using ideas from the texts plus your own linguistic knowledge.
4. Explain each concept with an everyday example.
5. Use the conventions of the form (headline, direct address, sign-off) and craft an opening and ending.
```

## Worked reminder: turning jargon into accessible writing

- **Too technical:** "The variable use of non-standard grammatical forms in AAVE is rule-governed."
- **Accessible:** "When people say 'He be working', they are not making a mistake: in some varieties of English this
  pattern regularly means someone *usually* works. It follows a rule, just not the rule of standard English."

## Must-know distinctions

- **Analysis question vs directed writing:** analysing how others write about language vs writing about it yourself.
- **Section A essay vs Section B writing:** formal academic register vs a form chosen for a non-specialist audience.
- **Topic vs text:** write about how the text represents the issue, not just about the issue.

### Answers

1. Analysis of how two texts use language to present ideas about language (40); directed writing on the same topic
   (30).
2. Phonetics, phonology and prosodics; graphology; lexis and semantics; grammar including morphology; pragmatics;
   discourse.
3. How texts represent language; construct an identity for the producer; position the reader and seek to influence
   them; are connected to discourses about language.
4. One that claims there is a correct form of the language and that people should follow it.
5. A non-specialist audience.
6. The damp spoon, the crumbling castle or the infectious disease.
7. AO4 rewards connections across texts; integrated comparison shows them most clearly.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Spotting features with no link to how they present language.
- Writing two separate mini-essays instead of comparing.
- Ignoring the date and context of an older text.
- In directed writing: copying the source texts, using undefined jargon, ignoring the named form.
- Giving opinions about language without evidence.

## Frequently asked questions

**How long is AQA A-level English Language Paper 2?**
Paper 2 of AQA A-level English Language (7702) lasts 2 hours 30 minutes, is worth 100 marks and counts for 40% of the A-level. Section B contains a 40-mark analysis of two texts about a language issue and a 30-mark directed writing task on the same issue.

**What four things should you explore in each Section B text?**
Ask how the text represents language, what identity it constructs for its producer, how it positions the reader and tries to influence them, and how it is connected to wider discourses about language.

**Who is the Paper 2 directed writing task written for?**
It is written for non-specialists, so explain each language concept with an everyday example and use the conventions of the form, such as a headline or direct address, rather than technical jargon.

**Why should you compare the two texts throughout the 40-mark analysis?**
Writing two separate mini-essays instead of comparing as you go is a common way to lose marks. Find three or four shared points of comparison and keep linking the texts point by point.

**Which writers about language can I refer to in Section B?**
Useful references, used sparingly, include Jean Aitchison's The Language Web, with its damp spoon, crumbling castle and infectious disease metaphors for complaints about change, and Deborah Cameron's Verbal Hygiene.
