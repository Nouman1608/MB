This guide goes deeper into **section 7.3 Leadership** of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Business 9609 syllabus for examination in 2026, 2027 and 2028. It is A Level content, examined through case studies and essays.

The [leadership and HRM strategy guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-business-leadership-and-human-resource-management-hrm-strategy/) gives a one-paragraph summary of each theory. This page shows how to *use* the theories: how to recognise them in a case, how they differ from management styles, and how to build an evaluation. ## Leadership, management and management style

At AS Level you met management functions and the four management styles. Keep the ideas separate:

- **Management** is about organising resources to meet plans: planning, organising, directing and controlling.
- **Leadership** is about setting a direction and getting people to follow it willingly, especially through change.
- A **management style** (autocratic, democratic, laissez-faire, paternalistic) describes *how decisions are made*. A **leadership theory** tries to explain *why some leaders are effective*.

One person can manage well and lead badly. A store manager may run rotas and stock perfectly yet fail to persuade staff to accept a new way of serving customers.

## 7.3.1 Purpose, roles and qualities

**Purpose.** Leadership gives a business direction, turns objectives into something staff understand, motivates people, makes decisions under uncertainty and steers change.

**Roles at each level.** The four syllabus roles lead different people over different time scales.

| Role | Leads whom | Typical leadership task |
|---|---|---|
| Directors | The whole organisation, through senior managers | Set the vision and strategy; answer to shareholders |
| Managers | A department or site | Translate strategy into plans; win support for them |
| Supervisors | A team on the front line | Keep daily work on track; set an example; spot problems early |
| Worker representatives | Fellow employees, in dealings with management | Voice staff views; negotiate; help explain decisions to colleagues |

Notice that a worker representative leads *without* formal authority over colleagues. Their influence comes from trust, which links directly to power and influence theory.

**Qualities of a good leader.** Commonly cited qualities include vision, integrity, decisiveness, clear communication, empathy, resilience and the willingness to delegate. The exam test is relevance. A useful method is to ask what the business needs right now:

- Facing a crisis: decisiveness and calm under pressure.
- Facing major change: vision and communication.
- Leading experts: willingness to delegate and listen.
- Rebuilding trust after a scandal: integrity and openness.

## 7.3.2 Theories of leadership in depth

### Trait theory

Effective leaders share personal characteristics, such as confidence, intelligence, drive and integrity, so leadership is largely born, not made. **Use in a case:** a business recruiting a chief executive might screen for these traits. **Limits:** researchers have never agreed a single list; people with the "right" traits still fail in the wrong situation; and it implies leadership cannot be developed through training.

### Behavioural theory

Effectiveness depends on what leaders *do*, so leadership can be learned. Most behavioural approaches compare concern for the task with concern for people. A well-known example is the managerial grid of Blake and Mouton, which scores both concerns from 1 to 9: high task with low people concern is "authority-compliance", low task with high people concern is "country club", and high on both is "team management". **Use in a case:** a supervisor who chases targets but ignores stress shows a task-heavy pattern. **Limit:** it suggests one best pattern, yet what works depends on the people and the problem.

### Contingency theory

There is no one best way to lead; the effective approach depends on the situation. One example is Fiedler's model, which argued that a leader's style is fairly fixed, so the business should match the leader to the situation, judged by leader–member relations, how structured the task is and how much formal power the leader holds. **Use in a case:** a firm in crisis may need a directive leader for now; a research team may need a consultative one. **Limit:** situations change quickly, and a leader may not be able or willing to change approach, or the business may not be able to change leaders.

### Power and influence

Leaders achieve results through the power they hold over others. The five-way classification of French and Raven is the standard one:

- **Legitimate**: from the formal position.
- **Reward**: from control over pay, promotion or praise.
- **Coercive**: from the ability to punish.
- **Expert**: from knowledge or skill others respect.
- **Referent**: from being liked and admired.

**Use in a case:** ask where the leader's power comes from and what it produces. Coercive power can force compliance quickly, but people do the minimum and leave when they can. Expert and referent power win commitment and survive a change of job title.

### Transformational leadership

The leader changes how followers see their work, raising their commitment to a shared vision beyond their own self-interest. The idea is associated with Burns and was developed by Bass, who described four elements: idealised influence (a role model), inspirational motivation (a compelling vision), intellectual stimulation (encouraging new ideas) and individualised consideration (attention to each person's development). It is contrasted with **transactional** leadership, which exchanges rewards for meeting targets.

**Use in a case:** a new leader turning around a declining business with a clear vision fits this. **Limits:** success depends heavily on one person; a vision can be wrong; constant pressure to change may exhaust staff; and in stable routine work, transactional leadership may be enough.

### Telling the theories apart in a case

| Evidence in the case | Theory it points to |
|---|---|
| "She was a natural leader from the start" | Trait |
| "He was sent on a course to improve how he supports his team" | Behavioural |
| "The approach that worked in the old factory failed in the new one" | Contingency |
| "Staff obey him because he controls the bonus" | Power and influence (reward) |
| "She persuaded staff to believe in a new future for the firm" | Transformational |

## 7.3.3 Emotional intelligence (EQ)

Goleman popularised the idea that leaders need emotional intelligence as well as technical ability. The syllabus names four competencies. Learn them as **two about yourself** and **two about other people**:

| | Recognising | Managing |
|---|---|---|
| **Self** | Self-awareness | Self-management |
| **Others** | Social awareness | Social skills |

**What each looks like in practice:**

- Self-awareness: a manager admits that she becomes impatient in long meetings and asks a colleague to chair them.
- Self-management: a supervisor stays calm when a customer shouts and deals with the complaint politely.
- Social awareness: a director notices that a usually talkative employee has gone quiet and asks if something is wrong.
- Social skills: a manager settles a dispute between two departments so that both feel heard.

**Why EQ matters for a business.** Leaders with high EQ tend to handle conflict and change better, which can reduce absence and labour turnover and improve cooperation. **Limits:** EQ is hard to measure reliably; it does not replace strategic judgement or technical knowledge; and a manipulative leader can use emotional insight against staff.

## Worked example: diagnosing leadership at Fernhill Furniture

Fernhill Furniture's new managing director, Ms Osei, was appointed after three years of falling sales. She has deep knowledge of the industry, set out a plan to sell directly online, and spends time on the factory floor asking staff for ideas. Two experienced supervisors still resist the plan. When one argued with her in a meeting, she lost her temper.

- **Theory:** her vision and attention to staff ideas fit transformational leadership (inspirational motivation, intellectual stimulation).
- **Power:** expert power from her industry knowledge, plus legitimate power from her post.
- **EQ:** the outburst shows weak self-management; the supervisors' resistance may need stronger social skills to win them over.
- **Contingency view:** a business in decline may also need some directive decisions quickly, so a purely inspirational approach may be too slow.

## Annotated model answer

*Evaluate whether transformational leadership is the most suitable approach for Fernhill Furniture.*

> "Transformational leadership suits Fernhill because the business must change its whole route to market, and Ms Osei's vision and willingness to listen can build the commitment this needs. However, it depends on staff trusting her, and her loss of temper with a supervisor may damage that trust, so weak self-management is a risk to the approach. Contingency theory also suggests that falling sales may require quick directive decisions that inspiration alone cannot deliver. On balance, transformational leadership is the most suitable long-term approach, but only if Ms Osei combines it with firm decisions on urgent issues and improves her self-management, because the plan will fail if the experienced supervisors withhold their support."

The answer applies a theory, uses EQ as a counter-argument, brings in a second theory, and reaches a conditional judgement.

## Common mistakes

- Using "leadership style" and "leadership theory" as if they meant the same thing.
- Calling any popular leader transformational without evidence of vision and changed commitment.
- Listing all four EQ competencies when the case shows only one or two.
- Treating coercive power as always wrong; it may be needed briefly for safety breaches.

## Exam technique

| Command word | What it needs in 7.3 |
|---|---|
| Explain | The theory or competency plus why it affects the leader's effectiveness |
| Analyse | A chain from evidence in the case to an effect on staff and then on the business |
| Evaluate | Strengths and limits of an approach in this case, then a judgement on what success depends on |
| To what extent | A clear position on how far, with the main condition |
