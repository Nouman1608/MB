This guide goes deeper into content item 1.3.1, Business aims and objectives, in Topic 1.3 Putting a business idea into practice of the Pearson Edexcel Level 1/Level 2 GCSE (9-1) in Business (1BS0) specification, Issue 2, July 2022, part of Theme 1 (Paper 1, 1BS0/01). The [Aims, objectives, revenue, costs and profit study guide](/resources/edexcel-gcse-business-aims-objectives-revenue-costs-and-profit/) defines each financial and non-financial objective; this page concentrates on writing objectives from aims, measuring whether they have been met, handling objectives that pull against each other, and comparing two businesses' objectives in a case. All businesses and figures are invented.

Companion pages: the [1.3.1 revision notes](/resources/edexcel-gcse-business-1bs0-business-aims-and-objectives-revision-notes/) and the [1.3.1 practice questions](/resources/edexcel-gcse-business-1bs0-business-aims-and-objectives-practice/).

## What 1.3.1 asks you to know

| Strand | Specification wording |
|---|---|
| Meaning | What business aims and business objectives are |
| Financial, when starting up | survival, profit, sales, market share, financial security |
| Non-financial, when starting up | social objectives, personal satisfaction, challenge, independence and control |
| Comparison | Why aims and objectives differ between businesses |

Appendix 2 of the specification lists percentages and percentage changes among the calculations, and market share among the market data you may interpret. These are the tools for checking whether a financial objective has been met.

## Part 1: from aim to objective

An aim says where the owner wants the business to go; objectives are the measurable steps on the way. A useful objective has three features:

1. **It names what is measured**: sales revenue, number of customers, market share, profit.
2. **It has a target figure**: £96,600, 15%, 40 customers.
3. **It has a time limit**: by the end of year 2, within six months.

| Weak objective | Why it is weak | Improved version |
|---|---|---|
| "Sell more cakes" | No figure, no deadline | "Sell 250 cakes a week by the end of June" |
| "Be profitable soon" | "Soon" is not a time limit | "Make a profit in the second year of trading" |
| "Help the community" | Cannot be checked | "Employ four local school leavers within 12 months" |

Non-financial objectives can be made measurable too. Independence and control might become "keep 100% ownership for the first three years"; challenge might become "open a second stall by year 3".

## Part 2: checking progress with figures

### Worked example: Bellhaven Bakery

In its first year, Bellhaven Bakery had sales revenue of £84,000. Its year 2 objective was to increase sales revenue by 15%.

- Target revenue = £84,000 × 1.15 = **£96,600**
- Actual year 2 revenue = £92,400
- Actual increase = (£92,400 − £84,000) ÷ £84,000 × 100 = **10%**
- Shortfall against target = £96,600 − £92,400 = **£4,200**

So the objective was **not met**, even though sales grew. In an exam answer, say both things: sales rose by 10%, but this was below the 15% target.

Bellhaven also had a market share objective of 15% of the town's bread market, worth £600,000 a year.

- Market share = £92,400 ÷ £600,000 × 100 = **15.4%** (market share is business sales ÷ total market sales × 100, a standard formula)

This objective **was met**. One business can meet one objective and miss another in the same year.

### Survival as an objective
Survival means the business keeps trading. A simple check is whether revenue covers total costs and whether the business can pay its bills on time (cash is covered in 1.3.3). For a start-up, survival is often the first objective because most of its spending happens before customers are regular.

## Part 3: when objectives pull against each other

Some objectives conflict, and the owner must decide which comes first.

| Objectives | Why they conflict |
|---|---|
| Sales or market share against profit | Winning sales through price cuts or heavy promotion lowers profit per sale |
| Profit against social objectives | Paying above-average wages or buying from local suppliers raises costs |
| Independence and control against growth in sales | Growth may need an investor who wants a say in decisions |
| Financial security against challenge | A secure income comes from playing safe; a challenge means taking risks |
| Personal satisfaction against profit | An owner may keep making a favourite product that sells slowly |

Strong answers name the conflict and say which objective the owner in the case is likely to put first, using evidence such as their stage, motives or finances.

## Part 4: why aims and objectives differ between businesses

### Worked comparison

| | Bellhaven Bakery | Nettlebed Bike Workshop |
|---|---|---|
| Owner | Sole trader who borrowed to buy ovens | A community group run by volunteers |
| Stage | Second year | Fourth year |
| Main aim | Become the town's leading bakery | Get more local people cycling |
| Likely objectives | Sales growth, market share, profit to repay the loan | Social: repair 500 donated bikes a year for families on low incomes; survival by covering costs |
| Why | A loan must be repaid; the owner relies on the business for income | No owner takes a profit; the purpose is to help the community |

The reasons behind the differences:
- **Who owns the business and why they started it.** An owner depending on the business for a living needs profit and financial security; a community group may put social objectives first.
- **Stage.** New businesses often aim for survival; established ones can aim for growth or market share.
- **Finance.** A business with loans needs enough profit to make repayments; a business with investors may be pushed towards profit.
- **Size and market.** A one-person business can pursue personal satisfaction; a business facing tough competition may focus on survival.

## Annotated model answer

**Question (Explain):** Explain one reason why a new business may set survival as its main objective. **[3]**

> A new business has not yet built up regular customers (statement). This means its sales are uncertain while many of its costs, such as rent, must be paid from the first month (expansion). If it chased growth too soon, for example by spending heavily on promotion, it could run out of money and close, so staying open comes first (second expansion).

## Common mistakes

- Writing objectives with no figure or deadline.
- Saying an objective was met because the figure rose, without comparing it with the target.
- Calculating percentage change from the new figure instead of the original.
- Treating sales and profit as the same objective.
- Explaining why objectives differ without using the businesses in the case.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Calculate:** targets, percentage changes and market shares; show the base you divide by.
- **Outline:** one objective linked to the business's situation.
- **Analyse:** use figures to judge progress, then say what the owner might do next.
- **Justify:** pick which objective the business should prioritise from two given options.
- **Evaluate:** weigh conflicting objectives and reach a supported judgement.
