## The chain in one line

**Inputs → output (productivity) → costs (diminishing returns in the short run, economies of scale in the
long run) → revenue (depends on the demand the firm faces) → profit (revenue minus costs) → investment and
innovation, which change productivity and costs again.**

The specification itself makes these links. It says students "should appreciate that both the law of
diminishing returns and returns to scale explain relationships between inputs and output" and "that these
relationships have implications for costs of production".

## Productivity and specialisation

Production converts inputs into output. **Labour productivity** is output per worker (or per hour worked).
Specialisation and the division of labour raise productivity: workers repeat tasks they are suited to, build
skill, waste less time switching tasks and can be supported by specialised machinery. But specialisation means
each person produces only a small part of what they consume, so it **requires an efficient means of exchange**.
Money, as a medium of exchange, removes the need for a double coincidence of wants that barter requires.

## Worked example: a print workshop (invented)

The workshop has a fixed press and premises costing **200 a day** (fixed cost). Each worker costs **100 a day**.

| Workers | Output (posters) | Marginal product | Average product | Total cost | Average total cost | Marginal cost |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 0 | – | – | 200 | – | – |
| 1 | 20 | 20 | 20.0 | 300 | 15.00 | 5.00 |
| 2 | 50 | 30 | 25.0 | 400 | 8.00 | 3.33 |
| 3 | 90 | 40 | 30.0 | 500 | 5.56 | 2.50 |
| 4 | 120 | 30 | 30.0 | 600 | 5.00 | 3.33 |
| 5 | 140 | 20 | 28.0 | 700 | 5.00 | 5.00 |
| 6 | 150 | 10 | 25.0 | 800 | 5.33 | 10.00 |

Marginal cost per poster here is the wage divided by the marginal product (100 ÷ MP), because each extra worker
adds 100 to cost and MP posters to output.

**Reading the table.**

- The first three workers raise marginal product (20, 30, 40) as they specialise around the press.
- From the fourth worker, **marginal product falls**: this is the **law of diminishing returns**. With the press
  fixed, extra workers have less capital each.
- As marginal product falls, **marginal cost rises** (2.50, then 3.33, 5.00, 10.00). The U-shape of the
  short-run MC curve is the mirror image of the marginal product curve.
- Average total cost falls at first, because fixed cost is spread over more output and productivity is rising;
  it reaches its lowest value, 5.00, between 120 and 140 posters, then rises as diminishing returns dominate.
- The MC curve cuts the average cost curve at its minimum. Where MC is below ATC (at 120 posters), ATC is
  falling; where MC is above it (at 150), ATC is rising.

This is the short run: at least one factor, the press, is fixed. In the **long run** all factors can vary, so
the workshop could buy a second press.

## From short run to long run

| | Short run | Long run |
|---|---|---|
| Factors | At least one fixed | All variable |
| Input–output relationship | Returns to a factor (diminishing returns) | Returns to scale |
| Cost curves | SRAC, SRMC; U-shaped because of diminishing returns | LRAC; shape set by economies and diseconomies of scale |

**Returns to scale** compare a proportionate change in **all** inputs with the change in output: increasing (output
rises by a larger proportion), constant (same proportion) or decreasing (smaller proportion). If factor prices are
unchanged, increasing returns to scale mean lower long-run average cost: one source of **economies of scale**.

## Economies and diseconomies of scale

**Internal economies** arise inside a growing firm: technical (larger, more efficient machines), purchasing
(bulk discounts), managerial (specialist managers), financial (cheaper borrowing) and risk-bearing
(diversified products). **External economies** arise from the growth of the **industry or area**: a pool of
trained labour, specialist suppliers and shared infrastructure lower costs for every firm there.

**Diseconomies of scale** raise long-run average cost as the firm becomes very large: coordination and
communication problems, slower decisions, and lower motivation among workers who feel remote from management.

**LRAC shapes.** If economies are followed by diseconomies, the LRAC is U-shaped. Many industries show an
**L-shaped** LRAC: costs fall up to a certain output and then stay roughly constant over a wide range. The output
at which LRAC first reaches its minimum is the **minimum efficient scale (MES)**. If MES is large relative to
total market demand, only a few firms can operate at lowest cost, which acts as a barrier to entry and tends to
produce a concentrated industry.

## Factor prices, productivity and input choice

Costs depend on factor prices **and** productivity. A wage rise increases costs unless productivity rises too.
Firms choose the input mix that minimises cost for a given output: if wages rise relative to the cost of
machinery, they substitute capital for labour. In the workshop, if wages rose to 150 a day, every marginal cost
would rise by half, and a digital press that needs fewer workers might become worthwhile.

## Revenue

Suppose the workshop is a **price taker** that can sell posters at **6.00** each. Then average revenue (AR) and
marginal revenue (MR) both equal 6.00, and total revenue (TR) is 6 × output.

A firm with market power faces a downward-sloping demand curve. An invented example:

| Quantity | Price (AR) | TR | MR |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 10 | 10 | 10 |
| 2 | 9 | 18 | 8 |
| 3 | 8 | 24 | 6 |
| 4 | 7 | 28 | 4 |
| 5 | 6 | 30 | 2 |
| 6 | 5 | 30 | 0 |

- **AR is the firm's demand curve**, because AR = TR ÷ Q = price.
- **MR is below AR** because to sell one more unit the firm cuts the price on all units, not just the extra one.
- **TR is at its maximum where MR = 0** (at 6 units here). While MR is positive, TR rises; when MR is negative,
  TR falls.

## Profit

Profit = TR − TC. Economists include **normal profit** in costs: it is the minimum return needed to keep the
owner in the industry, the opportunity cost of their capital and enterprise. Anything above it is **abnormal
(supernormal) profit**.

**Back to the workshop at a price of 6.00** (assume TC includes normal profit):

| Output | TR | TC | Profit |
|---|---|---|---|
| 120 | 720 | 600 | 120 |
| 140 | 840 | 700 | **140** |
| 150 | 900 | 800 | 100 |

Profit is highest at 140 posters. Hiring the fifth worker adds 20 posters at a marginal cost of 5.00 each,
below the price of 6.00, so it adds to profit. The sixth adds 10 posters at 10.00 each, above the price, so it
reduces profit. This is the MC = MR reasoning used in 3.1.5.

The **role of profit** in a market economy: it signals where consumers want resources, rewards risk-taking,
provides funds for investment, and attracts new firms into profitable markets.

## Technological change

**Invention** is the creation of a new idea or product; **innovation** is turning it into a commercial product or
process. Technological change can raise productivity, cut costs, create new products and markets, and destroy
existing ones. For the workshop, cheap home printing may shrink demand for small poster orders, while
large-format digital printers lower its costs for big orders. The specification links this to **creative
destruction**: new technologies and firms replace old ones, which can reshape market structure.

## Common errors

- Confusing diminishing returns (short run, one factor fixed) with diseconomies of scale (long run, all factors
  variable).
- Drawing MC cutting AC anywhere other than at the minimum of AC.
- Saying fixed costs never change; they are fixed only with respect to output in the short run.
- Treating MR as equal to price for a firm facing a downward-sloping demand curve.
- Calling all profit "abnormal" without allowing for normal profit.
- Defining external economies as economies from outside the country rather than from the growth of the industry.

## Exam technique

- **Calculate** costs and revenues from tables: write formulas such as ATC = TC ÷ Q and MC = ΔTC ÷ ΔQ.
- **Explain** the shape of a curve with a cause: "MC rises because of diminishing marginal returns."
- **Analyse** by following the chain to profit or price.
- **Evaluate** by asking whether economies of scale are available in the industry, how large MES is, and whether
  technological change alters the cost curves.
