This guide covers subtopic **16.3 Sexual reproduction in plants** of Cambridge O Level Biology 5090, 2026-2028 series. The syllabus asks you to identify the parts of insect-pollinated and wind-pollinated flowers and relate their structure to function; to compare the flowers and pollen of the two types; to outline pollination and distinguish self-pollination from cross-pollination, and discuss their effects; to describe the growth of the pollen tube and fertilisation; to understand that ovules become seeds and the ovary becomes a fruit; to describe seed structure; to relate the features of wind-dispersed and animal-dispersed fruits to their functions; and to investigate the conditions needed for germination and describe the process, including the role of enzymes.

The [Development of organisms and continuity of life topic guide](/resources/o-level-biology-5090-development-of-organisms-and-continuity-of-life/) covers the whole topic. This page goes deeper on plants. Use the [16.3 practice questions](/resources/cambridge-o-level-biology-5090-sexual-reproduction-in-plants-practice/) to test yourself.

## The structure of an insect-pollinated flower

The flower is the organ of sexual reproduction. Learn the parts, from the outside to the centre.

| Part | Description | Function |
|---|---|---|
| Sepals | Small, green, leaf-like structures | Protect the flower while it is a bud |
| Petals | Large and often brightly coloured, with scent and sometimes nectar | Attract insects |
| Stamen (male) | Made of an anther on a filament | Makes and releases pollen |
| Anther | Top part of a stamen | Produces pollen grains (containing male gametes) |
| Filament | The stalk of a stamen | Holds the anther in a position where insects touch it |
| Carpel (female) | Made of a stigma, style and ovary | Receives pollen and contains the ovules |
| Stigma | Top of the carpel, often sticky | Receives pollen |
| Style | Stalk joining the stigma to the ovary | Holds the stigma up for pollen capture |
| Ovary | Base of the carpel | Contains the ovules |
| Ovule | Inside the ovary | Contains the female gamete (egg cell); becomes a seed after fertilisation |

## The wind-pollinated flower

You must be able to identify the anthers and stigmas of a wind-pollinated flower, for example grass.

- The **anthers** hang outside the flower on long filaments, so that wind can blow the pollen away.
- The **stigmas** are large, feathery and hang outside the flower, so that they can catch pollen from the air.
- The petals are small, green or absent, and there is no scent or nectar.

## Comparing the two types of flower

| Feature | Insect-pollinated | Wind-pollinated |
|---|---|---|
| Petals | Large, brightly coloured | Small, dull or absent |
| Scent and nectar | Present | Absent |
| Anthers | Inside the flower, firmly attached | Outside the flower, hanging loosely |
| Stigma | Small and sticky, inside the flower | Large and feathery, outside the flower |
| Pollen | Sticky or spiny; larger grains; fewer | Smooth, light, small grains; very large numbers |

The differences between the pollen grains are about how they are carried. An insect-pollinated flower makes pollen that sticks to the insect's body. A wind-pollinated flower makes a very large amount of light, smooth pollen, because most of it will not land on a stigma.

## Pollination

**Pollination is the transfer of pollen from an anther to a stigma.** The agent is either an insect or the wind in the syllabus.

- **Self-pollination** is the transfer of pollen from an anther to a stigma of the **same flower** or of another flower on the **same plant**.
- **Cross-pollination** is the transfer of pollen from an anther to a stigma of a flower on a **different plant of the same species**.

### Discussing the effects on a population

| | Self-pollination | Cross-pollination |
|---|---|---|
| Variation | Less variation; offspring are similar to the parent | More variation, since genes from two plants are combined |
| Response to environmental change | Poorer, since there are few differences for natural selection to act on | Better, since some offspring may be adapted to the new conditions |
| Reliance on pollinators | Less reliance on insects or wind; it works even if pollinators are scarce | Depends on a pollinator or the wind to carry the pollen between plants |

A discussion answer should refer to variation, the capacity to respond to change and the need for pollinators, as these are the three points named in the syllabus.

## Pollen tube and fertilisation

After pollination, the events are as follows.

1. A pollen grain lands on the stigma and absorbs substances from it.
2. The pollen grain grows a **pollen tube**, which grows down through the style towards the ovary.
3. The tube enters the ovule, usually through a small opening.
4. The nucleus of the male gamete passes down the tube into the ovule.
5. The male nucleus fuses with the nucleus of the egg cell. This is **fertilisation**, and it forms a diploid zygote.

You do not need the production of endosperm or the details of development.

## From flower to fruit

After fertilisation:

- the **ovules** develop into **seeds**;
- the **ovary** develops into a **fruit**;
- the other flower parts usually wither.

A fruit therefore contains seeds, and it protects them and helps their dispersal.

## The structure of a seed

The syllabus asks for you to investigate and describe the structure of a seed (for example a broad bean), limited to the embryo and the testa.

| Part | Function |
|---|---|
| Testa | The tough outer coat; protects the seed |
| Embryo | The young plant, made of the plumule and radicle |
| Plumule | The embryo shoot |
| Radicle | The embryo root |
| Cotyledons | Seed leaves that store food (or absorb it) for the embryo |

To investigate, soak a bean seed in water overnight so that it softens, remove the testa, open the seed along the join between the two cotyledons and look at the embryo with a hand lens.

## Dispersal

**Dispersal** is the spreading of seeds and fruits away from the parent plant. It helps the species to **colonise new areas** and **reduces competition** with the parent plant and between the seedlings for light, water and ions.

| Feature | Wind-dispersed fruit or seed | Animal-dispersed fruit or seed |
|---|---|---|
| Structure | Wings or feathery parts, light | Hooks that catch on fur, or fleshy brightly coloured fruit |
| Function | Increase surface area, so the wind carries it far | Hooks attach to animals; fleshy fruit are eaten, and seeds pass out in faeces |
| Example | Sycamore fruits with wings; dandelion with parachute of hairs | Burdock with hooks; blackberry with fleshy fruit |

## Germination

**Germination** is the start of growth of the embryo in a seed. The conditions needed are:

- **water**, to rehydrate the seed and allow enzymes to work;
- **oxygen**, for aerobic respiration to release energy;
- a **suitable temperature**, for enzymes to work.

Light is not needed for most seeds to germinate.

### The process

1. The seed absorbs water.
2. **Enzymes** are activated. They break down stored food, for example amylase breaks down starch to sugars and protease breaks down proteins to amino acids.
3. The soluble products are moved to the growing embryo.
4. They are used in respiration, which releases energy, and in making new cells.
5. The radicle emerges first and grows downwards, followed by the plumule.

### Investigating the conditions: worked example

*Set up four tubes of cress seeds on cotton wool: (1) damp, warm, air available; (2) dry, warm, air available; (3) damp, cold, air available; (4) damp, warm, with the water boiled and cooled and covered by a layer of oil (little oxygen).*

Only tube 1 is expected to germinate well. Tube 2 lacks water, tube 3 is too cold and tube 4 lacks oxygen. Each tube changes one factor, and tube 1 acts as the control. Record the number of seeds that germinate in each tube after a set time and use percentages to compare: if 18 out of 20 seeds germinate in tube 1, the percentage is 18 divided by 20 x 100 = 90 per cent.

## Common mistakes

- Mixing up pollination and fertilisation. Pollination is the transfer of pollen; fertilisation is the fusion of nuclei.
- Saying that pollen grains are female gametes. They contain the male gamete.
- Saying that the stigma produces pollen. The anther does.
- Saying that the whole flower becomes the fruit. The ovary becomes the fruit.
- Forgetting that the ovules, not the ovary, become the seeds.
- Describing wind-pollinated flowers as brightly coloured or scented.
- Saying that seeds need light or soil to germinate; they need water, oxygen and a suitable temperature.

## Command words

- **Identify**: name flower parts on a diagram.
- **Compare**: set out similarities and differences between the two types of flower.
- **Relate**: link a structure to its function.
- **Discuss**: give both sides, as with self- and cross-pollination.
- **Investigate**: plan or interpret an experiment with controls.
