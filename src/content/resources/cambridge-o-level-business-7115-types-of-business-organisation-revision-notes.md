Condensed recall for subtopic **1.4 Types of business organisation**. For full explanations and an annotated model answer, use the [Types of business organisation study guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-business-7115-types-of-business-organisation/).

## The forms at a glance

| Form | Owners | Liability | Main advantage | Main limitation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sole trader | One person | Unlimited | Full control; keeps all profit; easy to set up | Limited capital; owner bears all risk; no continuity |
| Partnership | 2 or more partners | Unlimited (usual form) | More capital and skills; shared workload | Disagreements; profits shared; unlimited liability |
| Private limited company | Shareholders, shares not sold to the public | Limited | Limited liability; continuity; owners keep control | Set-up costs; some accounts published; shares not easily sold |
| Public limited company | Shareholders, shares sold to the public | Limited | Very large sums of capital can be raised | Loss of control; takeover risk; more legal requirements |
| Franchise | Franchisee runs an outlet of the franchisor's brand | Depends on franchisee's own form | Known brand lowers risk; support given | Fees and royalties; strict rules |
| Joint venture | Two or more businesses on one project | Agreed between them | Shared cost, risk and expertise | Shared profit; disputes over control |

## Unincorporated versus limited companies

- **Unincorporated** (sole trader, partnership): no separate legal identity; owners personally responsible for debts.
- **Limited company**: separate legal entity; owns assets and signs contracts in its own name; owners have limited liability; the company continues if an owner leaves or dies.

## Risk, ownership and limited liability

- **Ownership** decides who receives the profit and who has the final say.
- **Risk** is the chance of losing money; limited liability protects personal assets but does not stop the business failing.
- **Limited liability**: owners can lose only the money they invested in shares.
- **Unlimited liability**: owners can be made to pay all business debts from personal assets.

## Separation of ownership and control

In a public limited company shareholders own it but elected directors and managers run it. Their aims can differ: directors may prefer growth, shareholders may want higher dividends.

## Choosing a form: four needs

1. **Capital**: how much money is needed?
2. **Risk**: how large are debts likely to be?
3. **Control**: how much does the owner want to keep?
4. **Growth and continuity**: how fast should it expand, and must it outlast its owners?

## Public sector organisations

- **Public corporation**: owned by the government, run by a board it appoints.
- Aims: essential service, affordable prices, services to all areas, jobs; often expected to cover costs.
- Finance: government funds, charges to users, loans.
- Weaknesses: less pressure to be efficient, political interference.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Sole trader | A business owned by one person |
| Partnership | A business owned by two or more people who share the profit |
| Partnership agreement | A document setting out partners' capital, roles and how profit is shared |
| Shareholder | A person who owns part of a company by holding its shares |
| Limited liability | Owners can lose only the amount they invested |
| Unlimited liability | Owners are personally liable for all business debts |
| Separate legal entity | A company exists in law apart from its owners |
| Continuity | The business continues if an owner leaves or dies |
| Franchise | The right to trade using another business's brand and methods, in return for fees |
| Joint venture | Two or more businesses working together on a project while keeping their own identities |
| Public corporation | A business owned and run by the government |

## Exam traps

- **Public limited company is private sector**; public corporation is public sector.
- **Limited liability does not mean low risk of failure.**
- **Partners share profit as their agreement says**, not always equally.
- **Franchisees are not employees** of the franchisor; they own their outlet.
- **A recommendation needs case facts.** "Ltd because of limited liability" alone earns little.
- **Do not recommend a plc** to someone who wants to keep control of a small business.

## Self-test

1. Define limited liability.
2. Give two features of an unincorporated business.
3. Why does a private limited company have continuity?
4. State one difference between a private and a public limited company.
5. Give one advantage of a partnership over a sole trader.
6. Give one disadvantage of being a franchisee.
7. Give one advantage to a franchisor of franchising.
8. Why might two businesses form a joint venture to enter a foreign market?
9. Explain what is meant by the separation of ownership and control.
10. Who appoints the board of a public corporation?
11. A plumber works alone, needs little equipment and wants to keep all decisions. Which form suits her?
12. Give one reason a public corporation may be less efficient than a private company.

## Answers

1. Owners can lose only the money they have invested in the business, not their personal assets.
2. No separate legal identity; unlimited liability (also: fewer legal formalities, accounts kept private).
3. It is a separate legal entity, so it continues when shares pass to new owners.
4. A public limited company can sell shares to the general public, for example on a stock exchange; a private limited company cannot.
5. Any one: more capital; shared workload; extra skills; someone to share decisions with.
6. Any one: initial fee and royalties; must follow the franchisor's rules; brand damage by other outlets affects them.
7. Rapid expansion using the franchisees' capital (also: fee and royalty income; motivated local owners).
8. To share costs and risks, and to combine one firm's product with the other's knowledge of the local market.
9. The shareholders own the company but directors and managers run it, so their aims may differ.
10. **The government**.
11. **Sole trader**: cheap to set up, low risk and she keeps full control.
12. Any one: losses may be covered by the government, so less pressure to cut costs; political interference in decisions.
