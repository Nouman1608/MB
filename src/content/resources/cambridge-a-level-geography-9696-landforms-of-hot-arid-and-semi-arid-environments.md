## Weathering and its effects

The effects of weathering matter as much as the processes, because they prepare material for wind and water.

| Process | Conditions | Effect on the landscape |
|---|---|---|
| Thermal fracture | Large daily surface temperature range; dark minerals and coarse grains | Granular disintegration produces grus (loose grains) that wind can move; angular blocks on scarps |
| Exfoliation | Repeated heating of outer layers, usually with some moisture | Rounded boulders and domes; curved sheets peeling from bare rock |
| Salt weathering | Salty groundwater, dew, playa margins, cliff bases | Honeycomb pitting (tafoni), crumbling bases of slopes, undercutting of buildings |
| Chemical weathering | Dew, rare rain, groundwater near the surface | Desert varnish, rotting of rock beneath the surface, slow release of clays |

A key evaluation point: chemical weathering is slow at the surface but may have been much more effective in the past, or at depth where moisture is retained. Deep weathering profiles under some desert plains are thought by many geomorphologists to have formed under wetter past climates.

## Wind processes and the sediment they move

Wind moves particles by three methods sorted by size.

- **Suspension:** silt and clay (finer than about 0.06 mm) can be lifted high and carried hundreds or thousands of kilometres as dust.
- **Saltation:** sand grains (roughly 0.06-2 mm) bounce in arcs, mostly within a metre or two of the ground. Each impact can knock other grains into the air.
- **Traction (surface creep):** grains too heavy to lift roll or are pushed by saltating grains striking them.

**Deflation** removes loose fine particles, lowering the surface into **deflation hollows** and leaving behind a lag of stones. **Abrasion** by saltating sand is concentrated near the ground, which helps explain undercut rocks and the shape of yardangs and zeugen.

**Desert surfaces** reflect these processes: **erg** (sand sea), **reg** (stony plain) and **hamada** (bare rock plateau). A **desert pavement** is a closely packed surface of stones. One explanation is deflation of fine material between the stones; another is that wind-blown dust accumulates *beneath* the stones, lifting them. Both may operate.

## Sand dunes: form and movement

A dune forms where sand supply, wind and a trap (an obstacle, a rough surface or a decrease in wind speed) combine. Sand saltates up the gentle windward slope and accumulates at the crest until the lee slope exceeds the **angle of repose** for dry sand, about 30-34°. Sand then avalanches down the **slip face**, so the dune advances downwind.

| Dune type | Wind regime | Sand supply | Form |
|---|---|---|---|
| Barchan | One dominant direction | Limited, on a hard surface | Crescent, horns pointing downwind |
| Transverse | One dominant direction | Plentiful | Long ridges at right angles to the wind |
| Seif (linear) | Two directions alternating | Moderate | Long ridges parallel to the overall wind |
| Star | Several directions | Plentiful | Radiating arms; grow upward rather than move |
| Parabolic | One direction, with vegetation | Moderate | U-shape with arms pointing upwind, held by plants |

Barchans move faster when they are smaller, because less sand must be moved for the dune to advance. Migrating dunes can bury roads, canals and farmland.

## Wind-sculptured rocks

**Yardangs** are streamlined ridges aligned with the dominant wind, carved from soft rock or old lake sediments by abrasion and deflation. They can range from a metre to kilometres long. **Zeugen** are flat-topped ridges where a horizontal resistant cap protects softer rock beneath; abrasion near the ground and weathering along joints cut the softer layer. Many wind-sculptured rocks were first shaped by water or weathering and later modified by wind, so they are useful in discussions of process interaction.

## Water processes: regime and floods

The **hydrological regime** of most desert channels is **ephemeral**: they flow only after rain. Some are **intermittent** (flowing in a wet season) and a few are **exogenous**, rising in wetter mountains (for example the Nile). **Episodic rainfall** produces sudden, intense flows. On bare, crusted surfaces, infiltration is low, so overland flow begins quickly.

- **Sheet floods** spread as thin, wide sheets over gentle slopes such as pediments and fans.
- **Flash floods** are confined to channels. Discharge rises in minutes, carries very high sediment loads (sometimes as debris flows), then drops quickly as water is lost by **transmission loss** into the channel bed and by evaporation.

Because flows are short and loads are high, desert rivers deposit abruptly, building fans and filling basins.

## Fluvial landforms

- **Wadis and arroyos:** steep-sided, flat-floored channels cut by flash floods. In the south-western USA, many arroyos were cut deeply in the late nineteenth century. Explanations include overgrazing, changes in rainfall intensity, and natural cycles of cutting and filling. The debate is a useful example of separating human and climatic causes.
- **Alluvial fans:** where a channel leaves a mountain front, it loses confinement and gradient, so deposition is rapid. The coarsest material is at the fan apex. Fans grow by switching channels and by debris flows. A channel cut into the head of the fan (fan-head trenching) suggests a change in sediment supply, base level or climate.
- **Pediments:** gently sloping rock surfaces at the foot of mountain fronts, often thinly covered with debris, meeting the mountain at a sharp **piedmont angle**. Proposed explanations include lateral planation by shifting channels, sheet flood erosion, and parallel retreat of the mountain front through weathering and removal of debris.
- **Bahadas:** coalescing fans along a mountain front.
- **Playas and salt lakes:** the lowest points of enclosed basins, where floodwater collects and evaporates, leaving clays and salt crusts.
- **Inselbergs:** isolated steep-sided hills above plains. **Bornhardts** are dome-shaped and massive; **kopjes** are smaller, made of piled boulders. One explanation is deep chemical weathering followed by stripping of the weathered layer to expose unweathered cores; another is long-continued scarp retreat leaving remnants.

## Relative roles of aeolian and fluvial processes

| Evidence for aeolian dominance | Evidence for fluvial dominance |
|---|---|
| Large active ergs such as the Rub' al Khali | Wadis, fans, pediments and playas are widespread even in the driest deserts |
| Dust exported across oceans | A single flash flood can move more sediment than years of wind |
| Yardangs aligned with present winds | Much dune sand originated as river or lake sediment |

In most hot deserts, sand seas cover only a minority of the area, and **fluvial landforms dominate**. Wind is most important where sand is abundant and surfaces are bare.

## Past processes and Pleistocene pluvials

Many desert landforms are partly **relict**. Evidence of wetter periods includes:

- **palaeolake shorelines** and lake sediments in now-dry basins;
- **dry valley networks** too large for present rainfall;
- **stabilised dunes** covered with vegetation and soil;
- **fossil groundwater** in aquifers such as the Nubian Sandstone, recharged in wetter times;
- **rock art** in the central Sahara, such as at Tassili n'Ajjer in Algeria, showing animals such as giraffes and cattle that need far more water than the area has now.

The best answers recognise that present processes rework inherited forms: flash floods still deepen old wadis, and wind reshapes old lake sediments into yardangs.

*Explain the formation of a barchan dune. [5]*

The thinner edges move faster than the thicker centre, so horns extend downwind, giving a crescent shape [1].

## Common mistakes

- Treating all desert landforms as wind-made.
- Mixing up saltation and traction.
- Explaining alluvial fans without loss of gradient and confinement.
- Naming pluvials without evidence.
- Forgetting that present processes still modify relict landforms.

## Exam technique

**Explain the formation** questions reward a sequence and a labelled sketch. **Assess the relative roles** questions need evidence for both processes and a judgement that varies by desert and landform.
