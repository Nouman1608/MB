This guide goes deeper into sub-topic 5.2 of Option E, Leisure, tourism and sport, in IB Diploma Programme Geography (first assessments 2019): tourism and sport at the local and national scale. It is examined in Paper 1 if your school teaches this option. The [5.2 revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-geography-leisure-tourism-sport-tourism-and-sport-at-the-local-and-national-scale-revision-notes/) already cover types of tourism, the facility hierarchy, Butler, Doxey and Plog, carrying capacity, the impact table and London 2012. This page adds what those notes leave short: where tourism concentrates inside cities and rural areas, how sports venues are located and what effects they have on their neighbourhoods, how to measure seasonality and catchment, and how national governments use tourism and sport for regional development. Test yourself with the [5.2 practice questions](/resources/ib-dp-geography-5-2-practice/); the [Leisure, tourism and sport option study guide](/resources/ib-dp-geography-leisure-tourism-sport-study-guide/) gives the overview of all four sub-topics.

## What this sub-topic asks you to do

- Explain the spatial distribution of tourism and sport facilities within cities, rural areas and countries.
- Explain the economic, social and environmental effects on host places.
- Apply and evaluate models of destination change and host attitudes.
- Judge how far local and national strategies spread benefits.

## Where tourism concentrates in a city

Urban tourism is not spread evenly. It clusters where primary and secondary resources coincide.

- **The tourist-historic city.** Ashworth and Tunbridge (1990) described how the historic core and the modern commercial centre of many European cities overlap to create a zone used mainly by visitors: monuments, museums, restaurants and hotels in a compact, walkable area. As tourism grows, this zone expands and can push out everyday shops and residents.
- **Waterfronts and former industrial land.** Old docks and riverside warehouses are converted to museums, arenas, hotels and promenades, often as part of regeneration.
- **Event and stadium districts.** Clusters of sports and entertainment venues around a transport hub.
- **Business tourism.** Conference centres and hotels cluster near airports, rail stations and the central business district.

The consequence is a sharp **local** imbalance: a few streets carry most visitors, so pressure on housing, prices and public space is intense there and almost absent a short walk away. When evaluating impacts, specify the zone, not the whole city.

## Rural tourism and honeypots

Rural visitor pressure concentrates at **honeypots**: a waterfall, a viewpoint, a village with a car park. Typical problems are footpath erosion, traffic congestion on narrow roads, litter, disturbance of livestock and wildlife, and conflict with farmers. Second-home ownership raises house prices and leaves some villages empty outside the season, weakening shops, schools and bus services.

Management tools at local scale include surfaced paths, car-park pricing and park-and-ride, signposting visitors to alternative sites (spreading demand), and planning limits on second homes. Each moves the problem as well as reducing it: spreading demand protects one site but may bring pressure to quieter ones.

## Locating sport facilities

The facility hierarchy explains **how many** people a facility needs. Location decisions also weigh:

- **Land cost and site size.** Modern stadiums need large sites, so new venues are often built on the edge of cities or on former industrial land.
- **Accessibility.** Motorway junctions and rail stations widen the catchment.
- **Planning and politics.** Many large venues are part-funded by local government in return for regeneration promises.
- **Tradition and identity.** Fans resist moving a club far from its historic home.

Many clubs have relocated from small, crowded inner-city grounds to larger new stadiums. In London, Arsenal moved in 2006 from Highbury to the Emirates Stadium, a short distance away, and the old ground was converted to housing. Relocation can lift capacity and income while redeveloping the former site.

### Effects on the surrounding area

A stadium creates **externalities**, effects felt by people who are not using it:

| Positive | Negative |
|---|---|
| Spending in nearby cafes, bars and shops on match days | Traffic and parking pressure |
| Regeneration and new transport links | Noise, litter and crowd disorder |
| Local identity and pride | Dead space on non-event days if the area has no other uses |

These effects decline with distance, so the residents of the streets nearest the venue bear most of the costs, while benefits may go to the club and the wider city.

## Measuring seasonality and catchment

### Worked example 1: seasonality

An invented coastal resort records bed-nights by quarter.

| Quarter | Bed-nights (thousands) |
|---|---|
| January to March | 20 |
| April to June | 60 |
| July to September | 150 |
| October to December | 30 |

1. Total = 20 + 60 + 150 + 30 = 260 thousand.
2. Peak share = 150 / 260 x 100 = **57.7%** in one quarter.
3. Peak-to-trough ratio = 150 / 20 = **7.5**.

A ratio this high means many jobs are seasonal, hotels close in winter and infrastructure such as water supply must be sized for a peak that lasts three months. Strategies to reduce seasonality include conferences, festivals and winter breaks for retired travellers.

### Worked example 2: catchment and distance decay

A regional stadium surveys season-ticket holders by distance zone (invented figures).

| Zone | Population | Season-ticket holders | Holders per 1,000 residents |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0-5 km | 200,000 | 9,000 | 45 |
| 5-20 km | 600,000 | 12,000 | 20 |
| 20-50 km | 1,500,000 | 6,000 | 4 |

The **absolute** number is highest in the middle zone, but the **rate** falls steadily with distance, from 45 to 4 per 1,000. Always convert counts into rates before claiming distance decay: a large outer population can hide falling participation.

## National scale: tourism and sport as policy

Governments use tourism and sport for national goals:

- **Regional development.** Tourism can bring income to peripheral regions with few other industries, such as mountain or island areas. The risk is dependence on a seasonal, low-wage sector.
- **National identity and image.** National stadiums, teams and events promote a shared identity and attract visitors.
- **Health.** National sport strategies aim to raise participation and reduce inactivity.
- **Protected areas.** National parks protect landscapes while channelling domestic tourism into them, which creates tension between access and conservation.

Domestic tourism flows usually run from large cities towards coasts, mountains and countryside, so the same national parks and coastal resorts face peak pressure in school holidays.

## Annotated model answer

*Examine the effects of a new sports stadium on its local area.* **(sample paragraph)**

"A new stadium can bring match-day spending to nearby cafes and bars and may justify new transport links, which benefit residents every day, not just on event days **[economic and infrastructure effects]**. However, its negative externalities, such as traffic, parking pressure and noise, fall mostly on the streets closest to the venue, while ticket and broadcast income goes to the club **[distribution of costs and benefits by distance]**. If the surrounding area has no other uses, it may be empty and unsafe on non-event days **[temporal dimension]**. The overall effect therefore depends on whether the stadium is part of a wider mixed-use plan or stands alone **[conditional judgement]**."

## Common mistakes

- Treating a whole city as equally affected by tourism, rather than particular zones.
- Using counts instead of rates to argue for distance decay.
- Ignoring that local strategies can move pressure to other places.
- Describing stadium impacts without saying who bears them and how far away.
- Forgetting the national scale: policy, identity and regional development.

## Exam technique

- **Explain** the distribution of facilities using threshold, accessibility, land cost and planning.
- **Examine** effects by sorting them by type, by stakeholder and by distance or time.
- **Evaluate** a model such as Butler or the tourist-historic city by testing it against a real place and stating where it fits and fails.
- In data parts, calculate rates and ratios, then interpret them geographically.
