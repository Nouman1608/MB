## What management is

**Management** is the process of getting things done through people and other resources to achieve the objectives of a business. Managers decide what the business will do, organise the resources, guide the people and check the results. Managers operate at different levels: senior managers set direction, middle managers turn plans into departmental actions, and supervisors oversee day-to-day work.

## The functions

The specification does not list the functions by name. The widest-used list comes from Henri Fayol, an early writer on management, who described planning, organising, commanding, coordinating and controlling. Other writers use "leading" or "directing" in place of commanding. These five are shown here.

| Function | What it involves | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Planning | Setting objectives and deciding how to achieve them, including forecasts, budgets and timetables | A restaurant manager plans next quarter's menu, staffing and sales target |
| Organising | Arranging resources, structure, tasks and responsibilities | Dividing the kitchen into stations and assigning staff |
| Commanding (directing, leading) | Giving instructions, motivating and guiding people | A shift manager briefs staff, sets priorities and encourages the team |
| Coordinating | Making sure that departments and people work together towards the same aim | Linking kitchen, waiting staff and deliveries so that orders are timely |
| Controlling | Monitoring results against plans and taking corrective action | Comparing actual costs with the budget and acting on the gaps |

The functions are linked as a cycle. Planning sets targets; organising and directing put the plan into action; coordinating keeps the parts working together; controlling measures results, which feed into the next round of planning. Managers rarely do one function at a time.

### Worked example 1: the control function and budgets

Linford Foods (invented) planned total costs of £480,000 for the year. Actual costs were £516,000.

| Cost heading | Planned | Actual | Variance | Variance % |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Materials | £210,000 | £224,000 | £14,000 adverse | 6.7% |
| Labour | £180,000 | £186,000 | £6,000 adverse | 3.3% |
| Marketing | £40,000 | £38,000 | £2,000 favourable | 5.0% |
| Overheads | £50,000 | £68,000 | £18,000 adverse | 36.0% |
| **Total** | **£480,000** | **£516,000** | **£36,000 adverse** | **7.5%** |

A variance is the difference between planned and actual. When costs exceed the plan, the variance is **adverse**; when they are lower, **favourable**. Variance % = variance ÷ planned × 100. For overheads: 18,000 ÷ 50,000 × 100 = 36%.

Control action is aimed where it matters: overheads are the biggest overrun both in percentage and in money (£18,000, against £14,000 for materials). A manager would find out why (for example energy prices, an unplanned repair, or poor forecasting) and decide whether it is a one-off or will continue. The marketing underspend might be good (efficient) or bad (opportunities missed), so it must be checked.

The example also shows the links between functions. A poor plan (too little allowed for overheads) leads to a variance that control highlights; the fix may involve organising (a new energy supplier) and directing (staff to cut waste).

## Constraints on management

A **constraint** is something that limits what managers can do or how well they can do it.

**Internal constraints:**

- **Finance.** A limited budget restricts investment, hiring and expansion.
- **People.** Staff may lack the skills, or resist change; labour may be hard to recruit.
- **Existing structure and systems.** A tall hierarchy may slow decisions; old IT systems may limit what can be done.
- **Organisational culture.** "That is how we do things" can block change.
- **Objectives and owners.** Shareholders may demand short-term profit, which limits long-term plans.
- **Time and information.** Managers may have too little data to decide well.
- **Existing contracts** with staff, suppliers and customers.

**External constraints:**

- **Law and regulation** (employment, health and safety, competition, consumer protection, environmental rules).
- **Competition.** Rivals' prices and products limit a business's choices.
- **The economy.** Interest rates, inflation and recession affect costs and demand.
- **Technology.** Rapid change may make plans out of date.
- **Suppliers and customers.** Powerful suppliers can set terms; customers can change their demands.
- **Trade unions and pressure groups.**
- **Ethical and social expectations.**

To **evaluate** a constraint, judge how serious it is for this business, whether it can be overcome (by borrowing, training, changing the culture, lobbying), and at what cost. Some constraints are fixed, such as the law, while others, such as culture, can be changed over time.

## Judging the success of management

Success means that managers have achieved the objectives of the business, efficiently, within the law and with the support of stakeholders. No single measure is enough. A range of evidence is used.

- **Objectives achieved:** sales growth, profit, market share, as set in the plan.
- **Financial performance:** profit margin, return on capital, cash flow, and budget variances.
- **Efficiency:** productivity, waste and cost per unit.
- **People:** labour turnover, absence, morale, skills and diversity.
- **Customers:** satisfaction, complaints and loyalty.
- **Stakeholders:** relations with suppliers, the community and regulators.
- **Long-term health:** investment, innovation and reputation, not just this year's profit.

### Worked example 2: a management scorecard

Linford Foods had sales of £2,000,000 last year. It set these targets and recorded the results.

| Objective | Target | Actual | Met? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sales growth | 8% | 5% | No |
| Profit margin | 12.0% | 11.4% | No |
| Labour turnover (maximum) | 15% | 12% | Yes |
| Customer satisfaction | 85% | 88% | Yes |

Sales target = £2,000,000 × 1.08 = £2,160,000. Actual sales = £2,000,000 × 1.05 = £2,100,000, a shortfall of £60,000.

Profit target = 12.0% × £2,160,000 = £259,200. Actual profit = 11.4% × £2,100,000 = £239,400. Shortfall = £259,200 − £239,400 = **£19,800**.

Two of four objectives were met (50%). The cost overrun in Worked example 1 helps to explain the profit shortfall.

**Evaluation.**

- Managers did well on people and customers: lower turnover and higher satisfaction than targeted. These may support future sales.
- They missed the two financial targets. The sales shortfall may be due to the economy or competition, which are not managers' fault, while the margin shortfall relates to the cost overrun, which control should have caught earlier.
- Was the target realistic? An 8% growth target may have been too ambitious given market conditions.
- Compared with rivals, a 5% rise might still be strong; the targets alone do not show this.
- The time frame matters. The business may have invested in staff and service, accepting lower profit now for stronger performance later.
- Stakeholders: owners may be disappointed; employees and customers may be pleased.

**Judgement.** Management has been partly successful. It has run a good, stable workforce and satisfied customers, but control of costs was weak. The overall verdict depends on whether the external conditions explain the sales gap and whether the cost overruns are one-off.

## Common mistakes

- Giving a list of functions without examples or links to the business.
- Describing management as the same as leadership. Leadership (covered elsewhere in this topic) is one part of directing.
- Judging success by profit alone.
- Blaming managers for external constraints, or ignoring that they affect results.
- Listing constraints without judging their importance.
- Treating variances as always bad. A favourable variance may signal under-investment.

## Exam technique

For "explain the functions", give a definition and a short example for each function. For "evaluate constraints", say which is most serious in the case, whether it can be overcome and how. For "evaluate success", compare results with objectives, consider several measures, separate what managers could control from what they could not, and give a judgement.
