These notes cover **section 3.2.2.5 Balance of payments** of the AQA GCSE Economics (8136) specification for first teaching from September 2017: "balance of trade and balance of payments", "balance of payments surpluses and deficits on the current account" and "government policies to influence the balance of payments", including "simple calculations using current account balance of payments figures". They focus on classifying flows, calculation types and the tests for whether a deficit matters. Full explanations are in the [balance of payments study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-economics-8136-balance-of-payments/); test yourself with the [balance of payments practice questions](/resources/aqa-gcse-economics-8136-balance-of-payments-practice/). Figures are invented.

## Condensed recall

- Every current account flow is a **credit** (money into the country) or a **debit** (money out).
- Follow the **money**, not the goods or people: a UK tourist in France is a services **import**.
- Four parts: trade in goods, trade in services, primary income, secondary income.
- Balance of trade = goods balance + services balance.
- Current account balance = balance of trade + net primary income + net secondary income.
- A negative balance is a **deficit**; a positive balance is a **surplus**.
- Dividing the balance by GDP shows its size relative to the economy.
- A deficit is financed by inflows elsewhere: foreign investment, borrowing from abroad or sales of assets.
- Causes come down to **competitiveness** (price and non-price) and **demand** (at home and abroad).

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Credit | A flow that brings money into the country, e.g. an export |
| Debit | A flow that takes money out of the country, e.g. an import |
| Visible trade | Trade in goods |
| Invisible trade | Trade in services, such as tourism, insurance and banking |
| Net primary income | Income earned from abroad minus income paid abroad: profits, interest, dividends, some wages |
| Net secondary income | Transfers in minus transfers out, with nothing traded in return: aid, money sent to family abroad |
| Price competitiveness | How cheap a country's goods are compared with rivals' |
| Non-price competitiveness | Quality, design, reliability and reputation |

## Classifying flows: quick checks

| Flow | Part | UK credit or debit |
|---|---|---|
| UK whisky sold in Japan | Goods | Credit |
| Chinese tourists visit Edinburgh | Services | Credit |
| UK firm pays a US software company for cloud storage | Services | Debit |
| Interest paid to foreign holders of UK bonds | Primary income | Debit |
| Profits sent home by a UK bank's branch abroad | Primary income | Credit |
| UK overseas aid | Secondary income | Debit |

## Calculation types

**1. Building the balance.** Goods: exports 95, imports 120 → −25. Services: exports 60, imports 41 → +19. Balance of trade = **−6**. Add primary income +3 and secondary income −6: current account = −6 + 3 − 6 = **−9**, a deficit.

**2. Share of GDP.** A deficit of 18 in an economy with GDP of 900 is 18 ÷ 900 × 100 = **2.0%** of GDP.

**3. Comparing years by share of GDP.** Year 1: deficit 36, GDP 1,800 → 2.0%. Year 2: deficit 30, GDP 2,000 → 1.5%. The deficit shrank relative to the economy as well as in money terms.

**4. Finding imports.** Exports of goods are 310 and the goods balance is −45. Imports = 310 + 45 = **355**.

**5. Percentage change in exports.** Exports rise from 47 to 52: (52 − 47) ÷ 47 × 100 = **10.6%**.

## Reasons for a deficit or surplus

| Pushes towards a deficit | Pushes towards a surplus |
|---|---|
| Inflation higher than trading partners' | Inflation lower than trading partners' |
| A strong currency | A weak currency |
| Rapid growth of incomes at home | Slow growth or recession at home |
| Recession in main export markets | Fast growth in main export markets |
| Poor quality or reputation of domestic goods | Strong brands and high quality |
| Small manufacturing base | Strong specialist industries the world wants |

## Does the deficit matter? Four tests

- **Size:** a large share of GDP is more serious.
- **Duration:** a deficit lasting many years suggests firms are uncompetitive.
- **Cause:** importing machinery that raises future output is less worrying than importing consumer goods.
- **Financing:** inward investment in factories is less risky than borrowing that builds up debt.

## Policies and their conflicts

| Policy type | Examples | Conflict |
|---|---|---|
| Reduce spending at home | Higher taxes, lower government spending, higher Bank Rate | Slower growth, higher unemployment |
| Switch spending to home goods | Lower exchange rate, tariffs, quotas | Higher inflation; retaliation; may break trade agreements |
| Improve competitiveness | Training, investment incentives, lower taxes on business profits | Slow to work; costs the government money |

## Exam traps

- Holidays abroad, foreign streaming subscriptions and freight paid to foreign shipping firms are **imports of services**.
- "The deficit fell from 40 to 25" means there is still a deficit.
- The current account balance is not the government's budget balance.
- A surplus on goods does not guarantee a current account surplus; check services and income too.
- Expressing a figure in billions and then as a share of GDP: keep the units the same in both.

## Self-test

1. State what is meant by a credit on the current account.
2. Classify: a French family pays for a holiday in Cornwall.
3. Classify: a UK resident buys a car made in South Korea.
4. Which part of the current account includes money sent by migrant workers to their families abroad?
5. Goods balance −70, services balance +52, net primary income −4, net secondary income −9. Find the current account balance.
6. A current account deficit of 24 in an economy with GDP of 1,200. Express it as a percentage of GDP.
7. Exports of services are 180 and the services balance is +65. Find imports of services.
8. Give two reasons why a country's current account deficit might widen during a boom.
9. Give one reason why a deficit caused by importing machinery is less worrying.
10. Explain how a fall in the exchange rate could reduce a current account deficit.
11. Give one drawback of using tariffs to reduce a deficit.
12. Why might a large surplus not be good for a country's households?

### Answers

1. A flow that brings money into the country, such as payment for an export.
2. An export of services (credit) for the UK.
3. An import of goods (debit).
4. Secondary income.
5. −70 + 52 − 4 − 9 = −31, a deficit of 31.
6. 24 ÷ 1,200 × 100 = 2%.
7. 180 − 65 = 115.
8. Rising incomes increase spending on imports; inflation may rise, making exports less price competitive; domestic firms may switch sales from exports to the home market.
9. The machinery can raise future output and exports, which may reduce the deficit later.
10. Exports become cheaper in foreign currency and imports dearer in pounds, so export sales rise and spending switches from imports to domestic goods.
11. Other countries may retaliate with tariffs on UK exports, or tariffs may raise prices for consumers and firms using imported materials.
12. It may mean households are consuming less than the country produces, so their living standards are lower than they could be.
