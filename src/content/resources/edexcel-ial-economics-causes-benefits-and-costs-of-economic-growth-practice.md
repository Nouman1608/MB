## Questions

**1.** (a) Define potential growth. **[2]**
(b) Country D's real GDP rises from 1,250 to 1,300. Calculate the rate of actual growth. **[2]**

**2.** In Country E, consumption is 600, investment 150, government spending 200, exports 170 and imports 120 in year 1. In year 2 the figures are 618, 162, 204, 178 and 130.
(a) Calculate the growth rate of real GDP between the two years. **[3]**
(b) Calculate the contribution of investment to growth, in percentage points. **[2]**

**3.** Explain two ways in which international trade can support export-led growth in a small economy. **[4]**

**4.** Country A's labour force rises from 5.0 million to 5.1 million. Output per worker rises from 30,000 to 30,750.
(a) Calculate the percentage growth in real GDP. **[3]**
(b) Explain how net migration could have contributed to this growth. **[2]**

**5.** Explain how (i) foreign direct investment and (ii) greater competition could each raise potential growth. **[6]**

**6.** Tax revenue in Country G is 28% of real GDP. Real GDP rises from 600 to 624.
(a) Calculate the extra tax revenue. **[3]**
(b) Explain how this could improve public services. **[1]**

**7.** Real GDP in Country H rises from 90 billion to 94.5 billion. Population rises from 3.0 million to 3.06 million.
(a) Calculate the percentage change in real GDP per head. **[3]**
(b) Explain why this is lower than the growth rate of real GDP. **[2]**

**8.** In Country J, growth raises national income by 50. The marginal propensity to import is 0.3. Exports rise by 8.
(a) Calculate the change in imports. **[2]**
(b) Calculate the change in net exports. **[2]**
(c) State the effect on the trade balance. **[1]**

**9.** Real GDP in Country K rises 4.5%. Emissions per unit of output fall 3%.
(a) Calculate the percentage change in total emissions. **[3]**
(b) Explain why growth may impose an environmental cost even when technology improves. **[3]**

**10.** Average monthly income by group in Country L (illustrative currency units):

| Group | Year 1 | Year 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Bottom | 9.0 | 9.18 |
| Middle | 19.0 | 19.76 |
| Top | 52.0 | 57.2 |

(a) Calculate the ratio of top to bottom income in each year. **[2]**
(b) Evaluate whether economic growth benefits all groups in society. **[8]**

**11.** Explain why growth may cause inflation, and why it does not always do so. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** (a) Potential growth is the increase in the economy's productive capacity [1], shown by a rightward shift of LRAS or the PPF [1]. (b) (1,300 - 1,250)/1,250 x 100 [1] = **4.0%** [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A definition earns credit for saying capacity rises over time; describing a rise in output alone describes actual growth. Show the base-year division even for easy arithmetic.

**2. (a)** Year 1 GDP = 600 + 150 + 200 + (170 - 120) = 1,000; year 2 = 618 + 162 + 204 + (178 - 130) = 1,032 [1]. Growth = 32/1,000 [1] = **3.2%** [1].
**(b)** 12/1,000 x 100 [1] = **1.2 percentage points** [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Net exports are exports minus imports, not exports alone. State the unit as percentage points, not per cent, for a contribution.

**3.** First: foreign demand widens the market [1], so firms gain economies of scale and lower unit costs [1]. Second: export earnings pay for imported machinery and materials [1], which raises capacity and productivity [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Each way needs a development step, not just a label. "Exports earn money" is incomplete until you say what the money does.

**4. (a)** Year 1 GDP = 5.0m x 30,000 = 150bn; year 2 = 5.1m x 30,750 = 156.825bn [1]. Change = 6.825bn [1]. 6.825/150 = **4.55%** [1].
**(b)** Net migration raises the labour force (here by 2%) [1], so more workers produce more output if they find jobs [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Adding 2% and 2.5% gives about 4.5% but the exact figure is 4.55%; show the multiplication when asked to calculate. Link migration to employed workers, not population alone.

**5.** (i) FDI adds to the capital stock [1], which raises productive capacity [1]; it may also bring technology and skills that raise productivity [1]. (ii) Competition pressures firms to cut waste and costs [1], so output per input rises [1]; firms may also invest and innovate to avoid losing customers [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Separate the two causes clearly. An answer that says "more competition is good" without linking it to costs, efficiency or innovation stays at assertion.

**6. (a)** Increase in GDP = 24 [1]; 0.28 x 24 [1] = **6.72** [1].
**(b)** The government can spend the extra revenue on health or education without raising tax rates [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Apply the tax share to the change in GDP, not to the new total. The service link should be a mechanism, not a statement that services "improve".

**7. (a)** Year 1 per head = 90bn/3.0m = 30,000; year 2 = 94.5bn/3.06m = 30,882 [1]. Change = 882 [1]. 882/30,000 = **2.94%** [1].
**(b)** Population grew 2% [1], so output is shared among more people and per-head growth is lower than the 5% growth in total [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Dividing 5% by 2% or subtracting without checking the base gives a wrong figure. Use the per head values.

**8. (a)** 50 x 0.3 [1] = **15** [1]. **(b)** 8 - 15 [1] = **-7** [1]. **(c)** The trade balance worsens (the deficit widens) [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the sign. A bare "7" loses the information that the balance moves into deficit.

**9. (a)** Emissions index = 1.045 x 0.97 [1] = 1.01365 [1]. Total emissions rise **1.4%** (to 1 d.p.) [1].
**(b)** Emissions and resource use are negative externalities not fully paid for by producers [1]. A rise in total output can outweigh cleaner production per unit [1], and growth may also deplete resources or damage habitats, with costs to future generations [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Subtracting 3 from 4.5 gives 1.5%, a plausible but inexact answer. Multiply the changes.

**10. (a)** Year 1: 52/9 = **5.78** [1]. Year 2: 57.2/9.18 = **6.23** [1].
**(b)** Analysis: the top group's income rose by 10% against 4% for the middle and 2% for the bottom [1], so the ratio widened from 5.78 to 6.23 [1]. Everyone's income rose, so absolute living standards improved for all [1]. Growth may raise tax revenue, which could fund services that help low earners [1]. Evaluation: relative inequality rose [1]; inflation or rising rents may erode the 2% gain, so it is not clear that the bottom group is better off [1]. The outcome depends on policy, such as progressive taxes, training and benefits [1]. Conclusion: growth raised all incomes but unevenly, so it benefits all in money terms only; whether this is a benefit to all depends on distribution and prices [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* A judgement that names what it depends on, using the figures in the table, shows evaluation. Repeating that growth "helps everyone" or "helps the rich" without data does not.

**11.** If AD grows faster than potential output, firms face capacity limits [1], so demand for labour and materials bids up wages and costs [1], so prices rise (demand-pull inflation) [1]. It does not always do so if growth comes from higher capacity, such as investment or productivity, so supply keeps pace with demand [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* The qualification is where the final mark comes from. State the condition under which inflation does not occur, not just that it "may not".

## Where marks are usually lost

- Dividing by the new year rather than the base year when finding a growth rate.
- Giving the contribution of a component in per cent rather than percentage points.
- Defining actual growth when asked for potential growth, or the reverse.
- Treating net migration as the same as population growth.
- Stating a benefit without a limit, or a cost without an offset.
- Subtracting percentages instead of multiplying changes, as in emissions or per head questions.
- Writing "GDP" when real GDP is needed.
- Ending an evaluation with a summary and no judgement.
