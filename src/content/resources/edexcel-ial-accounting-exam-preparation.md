Edexcel International A Level Accounting (YAC11) has the simplest modular structure in Pearson's IAL
suite: just two units rather than the usual six. Unit 1 (WAC11/01, The Accounting System and Costing)
and Unit 2 (WAC12/01, Corporate and Management Accounting) are each 3 hours, **200 marks** and 50% of
the full IAL, and both are externally assessed. Each unit is built the same way: Section A carries two
compulsory multi-part data-response questions worth 55 marks each, and Section B carries three
optional multi-part questions worth 30 marks each, chosen from four. The International Advanced
Subsidiary (XAC11) is claimed on Unit 1 alone; the full IAL requires both. These notes complement the
site's guides to
[Principles and Double Entry Bookkeeping](/resources/a-level-edexcel-accounting-principles-and-double-entry-bookkeeping/)
and
[Control Accounts and Correction of Errors](/resources/a-level-edexcel-accounting-control-accounts-and-correction-of-errors/).

## A 200-mark paper in 180 minutes is under a minute a mark

This is a much denser paper than most A-level candidates will have met. At 0.9 minutes per mark, a
55-mark Section A question has roughly 50 minutes and a 30-mark Section B question roughly 27.
**Exam-preparation priority**: rehearse against those figures explicitly, because the intuition
carried over from 100-mark papers -- where a mark is worth a minute and a half -- will leave you an
hour short. This single arithmetic fact is the most useful thing to internalise about the paper.

## The two Section A questions are compulsory and carry 110 of 200 marks

Section A alone is 55% of each unit, and neither of its questions can be avoided. **Exam-preparation
priority**: revise for full coverage of the unit rather than relying on the Section B choice as
insurance. The choice exists in Section B only, and Section B is the smaller half.

## Choose Section B questions on the whole question, not the opening part

Three questions are chosen from four, and each is multi-part. A question whose part (a) is a familiar
computation may continue into an evaluative part you are less ready for. **Exam-preparation
priority**: read all four questions to the end before choosing, and choose on the parts carrying the
most marks. Three minutes spent choosing is recovered several times over.

## Presentation and layout are mark-bearing throughout

Accounting statements are credited for correct headings, correct ordering, dates, and clearly
referenced workings. **Exam-preparation priority**: practise producing statements in full from a blank
page rather than completing pre-drawn templates, because a blank page is what the exam gives you.
Under this paper's time pressure, a rehearsed layout is also considerably faster than one improvised.

## Costing and management accounting reward interpretation, not just computation

Unit 1's costing content and Unit 2's management accounting content both lead to questions asking what
the numbers mean for a decision. **Exam-preparation priority**: for each technique you revise, be able
to state in one sentence what a manager would do differently as a result of the figure. Computation
alone earns the computation marks; the interpretation marks come from that sentence.

## Worked routine: protecting marks across a 55-mark multi-part question

The routine below is an original model written for this resource, not a reproduction of any official
past paper or mark scheme.

```
Step 1 - budget the parts before starting:
Note the marks on each part and convert to minutes at roughly
0.9 min/mark. Write the times in the margin.

Step 2 - label every statement fully:
Business name, statement name, period or date. Fast, and creditable.

Step 3 - reference workings rather than embedding them:
"Depreciation (W3)" with W3 set out separately, so a marker can
follow the method even if a figure is wrong.

Step 4 - carry your own figure forward explicitly:
"Using the closing inventory of 18,400 from (b)..." keeps the
method marks in later parts available.

Step 5 - on the evaluative part, use the numbers you calculated:
Refer to your own figures rather than to general principles. An
evaluation that quotes the computed margin is worth considerably
more than one that discusses margins in the abstract.
```

Step 1 is what makes the rest possible. On a paper this dense, an unbudgeted 55-mark question is the
most reliable way to lose the second one.

## Before/during exam checklist

- **Before the exams**: rehearse at roughly 0.9 minutes per mark, not the 1.5 typical of 100-mark
  papers; revise for full unit coverage, since Section A's 110 marks are compulsory; practise
  statements from a blank page; for each technique, prepare one sentence on what a manager would do
  differently.
- **At the start of each unit**: read all four Section B questions to the end before choosing three.
- **In every question**: budget the parts in the margin first; label statements; reference workings
  separately; carry own figures forward explicitly.
- **On evaluative parts**: quote your own computed figures rather than arguing in the abstract.

## Self-test

1. How many minutes per mark does this paper allow, and how does that compare with a typical 100-mark
   A-level paper?
2. What share of each unit is compulsory, and where is the choice?
3. Why practise statements from a blank page rather than from templates?
4. What distinguishes an evaluative answer that scores well?

**Answers:** 1. About 0.9 minutes per mark (180 minutes for 200 marks), against roughly 1.5 on a
100-mark paper -- so the familiar pacing intuition leaves a candidate badly short. 2. Section A's two
compulsory 55-mark questions are 110 of 200 marks; the choice is in Section B only, where three
questions of 30 marks are chosen from four. 3. Because the exam provides no template, so headings,
ordering and dates must be produced from memory -- and a rehearsed layout is faster under time
pressure. 4. Using the figures you have just computed rather than discussing the technique in general
terms.
