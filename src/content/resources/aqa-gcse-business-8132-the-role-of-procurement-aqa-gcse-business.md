## Procurement and logistics

**Procurement** is the process of finding and buying the goods and services a business needs: raw materials, components, stock for resale, equipment and services such as cleaning. It includes choosing suppliers, agreeing prices and terms, and placing orders.

**Logistics** is getting those goods to the right place at the right time: transport, storage and delivery, both inbound from suppliers and outbound to customers.

Done well, both raise **efficiency**, because production is not held up waiting for materials, and lower **unit costs**, because the business pays less for inputs, wastes less and spends less on storage and transport. The specification adds a warning: cutting costs must be balanced against the **quality of service**. The cheapest courier may deliver late; the cheapest supplier may be the least helpful when something goes wrong.

## Managing stock: JIT and JIC

### Just in time (JIT)

Stock arrives just before it is needed, so the business holds very little.

**Benefits:**

- Lower storage costs, as less warehouse space is needed.
- Less cash tied up in stock, which helps cash flow.
- Less waste from stock that goes out of date, is damaged or stops selling.

**Costs and risks** (the specification names the first two):

- More frequent deliveries, each with a delivery charge.
- Lost purchasing economies of scale: small orders miss bulk-buying discounts.
- Production stops if a delivery is late.
- No spare stock if demand rises suddenly.

### Just in case (JIC)

The business holds **buffer stock**: extra stock kept in reserve.

**Benefits:**

- Spare stock can satisfy unexpected demand, so sales are not lost.
- Protection if a supplier is late.
- Large orders can earn bulk-buying discounts.

**Costs:**

- Holding costs: storage space, insurance, security, handling.
- Cash tied up that could be used elsewhere.
- Risk that stock is damaged, stolen, goes out of date or becomes unfashionable.

### Evaluating JIT for a given business

JIT suits a business when:

- demand is predictable, so it knows what to order;
- suppliers are reliable and close by, so deliveries are quick;
- stock is perishable, bulky or expensive to store.

JIC suits a business when:

- demand is unpredictable or seasonal;
- running out would be very costly, for example a hospital or a car repair garage;
- suppliers are far away or unreliable;
- large discounts are available for bulk orders.

## Worked example 1: JIT or JIC?

Figures invented. Wrenfield Garden Centre sells bags of compost. It needs 2,400 bags over the spring season.

| | JIC: one order at the start | JIT: weekly orders |
|---|---|---|
| Price per bag | £3.60 (bulk discount) | £4.00 |
| Cost of compost | 2,400 × £3.60 = £8,640 | 2,400 × £4.00 = £9,600 |
| Delivery charges | 1 × £80 = £80 | 12 × £45 = £540 |
| Holding cost (extra space and covers) | £600 | £50 |
| Total | £9,320 | £10,190 |

JIC is £870 cheaper here because the bulk discount is large and compost does not spoil. Spring demand also depends on the weather, so buffer stock means the centre will not run out on a sunny weekend. However, £8,640 of cash is tied up at the start of the season, so if the garden centre has a cash shortage, JIT may still be the safer choice. Notice how the specification's points appear: reduced holding cost under JIT against more frequent deliveries and lost bulk-buying discounts.

## Choosing a supplier

The specification names three factors.

- **Price:** a lower price cuts costs and allows lower selling prices or higher profit.
- **Quality:** inputs must meet the standard customers expect; faulty materials cause waste, returns and damage to reputation.
- **Reliability:** deliveries that arrive on time, in the right quantity, every time. Essential for a business using JIT.

To analyse, weigh the factors for the business in the case. A luxury brand puts quality first. A business using JIT puts reliability first. A discount retailer may put price first, but still needs acceptable quality.

## Worked example 2: comparing suppliers

Figures invented. Calloway Pizzas uses 1,000 kg of cheese a week.

| | Supplier X | Supplier Y |
|---|---|---|
| Price per kg | £5.80 | £6.10 |
| Weekly cost | £5,800 | £6,100 |
| Late deliveries in the last 10 weeks | 3 | 0 |

When cheese arrives late, Calloway estimates it loses £400 of sales. With Supplier X, three late deliveries in ten weeks suggests about 0.3 a week, so an expected loss of 0.3 × £400 = £120 a week. Supplier X then costs about £5,800 + £120 = £5,920 a week, still £180 less than Supplier Y.

On these figures X is cheaper, but the estimate leaves out damage to Calloway's reputation when customers are turned away. If Calloway is moving to JIT, Y's reliability becomes much more valuable. This is the kind of judgement the specification asks for.

## Supply chain management

A **supply chain** is the network of businesses and activities that take a product from raw materials to the final customer: growers or extractors, manufacturers, wholesalers, transport firms and retailers.

The specification lists the value of effective supply chain management:

- **Working with suppliers to ensure that key processes are running efficiently and cost effectively:** sharing sales forecasts so suppliers can plan; agreeing delivery times; solving problems together.
- **Getting goods and services for the best price and value:** value means what you get for what you pay, including quality and service, not only the lowest price.
- **Cutting any waste and unnecessary costs to create a streamlined process and fast production times:** fewer handling stages, combined deliveries, less packaging, less idle time.

Long-term relationships with suppliers often bring benefits beyond price: priority when goods are scarce, help with new products, and flexible payment terms.

## Exam technique

- **Identify:** name a supplier factor or a stock method.
- **Explain:** link a factor to an effect, such as "unreliable supplier → late materials → production stops → orders missed".
- **Analyse:** follow the chain through to unit costs, sales or reputation.
- **Evaluate JIT for a given business:** use the business's demand pattern, its suppliers, its product and its cash position, then decide.

## Common mistakes

- Drawing or describing stock control charts. They are not required.
- Saying JIT is always cheaper. Delivery charges and lost discounts can make it dearer.
- Choosing the cheapest supplier without considering quality and reliability.
- Confusing procurement (buying) with logistics (moving and storing).
- Forgetting the specification's warning that lower costs must be balanced against quality of service.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the difference between procurement and logistics?**
Procurement is finding and buying the goods and services a business needs. Logistics is moving and storing them so they arrive where and when they are needed.

**What are the three factors in choosing a supplier?**
The specification names price, quality and reliability.

**Do I need to draw stock control charts?**
No. The specification states that students will not be asked to draw or interpret stock control charts.
