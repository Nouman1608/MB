## What 2.3 asks you to do

The syllabus sets five learning outcomes:

1. Demonstrate an understanding of the work of the **sea and wind** in
   eroding, transporting and depositing.
2. Describe and explain the formation of the **landforms**: cliffs, wave-cut
   platforms, caves, arches, stacks, stumps, bay and headland coastlines,
   beaches, spits and coastal sand dunes. This list is complete, so you must
   know every item on it.
3. Describe **coral reefs and mangrove swamps** and the conditions required
   for their development.
4. Demonstrate an understanding that coasts present **hazards** (including
   coastal erosion and tropical storms) and offer **opportunities** for people.
5. Explain what can be done to **manage the impacts of coastal erosion**.

The required case study covers **the opportunities presented by an area or
areas of coastline, the associated hazards and their management**.

## Waves: the energy behind coastal processes

Waves are formed by wind blowing over the sea surface. Their size depends on
**wind speed**, **how long the wind blows** and the **fetch** (the distance of
open water the wind blows over). When a wave reaches shallow water, friction
with the sea bed slows its base, the top topples forward and the wave breaks.
Water rushing up the beach is the **swash**; water draining back is the
**backwash**.

| | Constructive waves | Destructive waves |
|---|---|---|
| Height | Low | High |
| Frequency | Fewer per minute | More per minute |
| Swash and backwash | Swash stronger than backwash | Backwash stronger than swash |
| Effect | Build up beaches by deposition | Remove material and erode cliffs |
| Beach profile | Gentle, wide beach | Steeper, narrower beach |

## The work of the sea

**Erosion:** hydraulic action (including air compressed in cracks),
abrasion, attrition and solution. Cliffs are also weakened from above by
**weathering** (for example, rain loosening soft rock) and **mass movement**
(slumping and rock falls), which is why soft clay cliffs can retreat very
quickly.

**Transport:** as in rivers, by traction, saltation, suspension and solution.
Along the coast, **longshore drift** moves sediment in a zig-zag: swash carries
it up the beach at the angle of the waves, backwash drags it straight down the
slope under gravity.

**Deposition** happens where waves lose energy: in sheltered bays, behind
spits, where constructive waves dominate, or where longshore drift is
interrupted.

## The work of the wind

Wind moves dry sand inland from the beach, mainly by **saltation**. **Coastal
sand dunes** form where:

1. A wide beach has a large supply of sand, often with a large tidal range so
   that a wide area dries out at low tide.
2. A strong **onshore wind** blows the dry sand inland.
3. The sand meets an **obstacle** such as driftwood, a pebble ridge or a plant,
   and builds up behind it.
4. Salt-tolerant grasses colonise the sand; **marram grass**, with long roots,
   binds it together and keeps growing as it is buried.

Over time a sequence develops: small **embryo dunes** near the beach, then
taller **fore dunes** and **yellow dunes**, then older **grey dunes** further
inland where more vegetation adds humus to the sand.

## Depositional landforms in more detail

- **Beach:** an accumulation of sand or shingle between the low-tide mark and
  the highest point reached by storm waves. Sand beaches have gentle slopes;
  shingle beaches are steeper because the backwash drains away through the
  pebbles.
- **Spit:** a long, narrow ridge of sand or shingle attached to the land at
  one end. Longshore drift carries sediment past a bend in the coastline, so
  it is deposited out into open water. A change in wind or wave direction can
  curve the end into a **hook**. Sheltered water behind the spit allows mud to
  settle and a **salt marsh** to form. Spurn Head, at the southern end of the
  Holderness coast in England, is a well-known example.

## Coral reefs and mangrove swamps

**Coral reefs** are built by tiny animals (polyps) that make limestone
skeletons. They depend on algae living in their tissues, which need sunlight.
Conditions required:

- **Warm water**, usually above about 18 °C, so reefs are found mainly in
  tropical seas.
- **Shallow, clear water** so that sunlight reaches the algae; sediment from
  rivers blocks light and smothers coral.
- **Salt water**: reefs do not grow near the mouths of large rivers.
- **Well-oxygenated water**, often from wave action.

There are three types: **fringing reefs** attached to the shore, **barrier
reefs** separated from the shore by a lagoon (the Great Barrier Reef off
Queensland, Australia, is the largest reef system), and **atolls**, rings of
coral around a lagoon, as found in the Maldives. Reefs are threatened by
**coral bleaching** when water becomes too warm, by pollution and sediment, by
overfishing and by damage from tourists and boats.

**Mangrove swamps** are forests of salt-tolerant trees on tropical and
subtropical coasts. Conditions required:

- **Warm** tropical or subtropical temperatures.
- **Sheltered, low-energy coasts** such as estuaries, deltas and lagoons.
- **Muddy, tidal** areas regularly flooded by salty or brackish water.

Mangrove trees have **prop (stilt) roots** for stability in soft mud and
**breathing roots (pneumatophores)** that grow up out of the mud. The
Sundarbans in the Ganges–Brahmaputra delta (India and Bangladesh) form the
largest mangrove forest in the world, and there are extensive mangroves in the
Indus delta in Pakistan. Mangroves protect coasts from storm surges and
erosion, provide nursery areas for fish and supply timber and honey; they are
threatened by clearance for shrimp farms, agriculture and urban development.

## Hazards and opportunities

**Hazards**

- **Coastal erosion:** loss of homes, roads, farmland and businesses as
  cliffs retreat.
- **Tropical storms** (cyclones, hurricanes, typhoons): these form over warm
  oceans (above about 26 °C) between roughly 5° and 20° from the Equator. They
  bring very strong winds, torrential rain and **storm surges** that flood
  low-lying coasts. Cyclone Nargis, which struck the Irrawaddy delta in
  Myanmar in May 2008, left well over 100,000 people dead or missing, largely
  because of its storm surge.

**Opportunities**

- Fishing and aquaculture.
- Ports, trade and industry.
- Tourism and recreation on beaches and reefs.
- Flat coastal land for settlement and farming.
- Energy, including offshore oil and gas, wind and tidal power.

## Managing coastal erosion

| Hard engineering | How it works | Drawbacks |
|---|---|---|
| **Sea wall** | Concrete wall, often curved, reflects wave energy | Very expensive; can increase erosion of the beach in front |
| **Groynes** | Wooden or rock barriers across the beach trap longshore drift | Starve beaches further along the coast of sediment |
| **Rock armour** | Large boulders at the cliff foot absorb wave energy | Expensive to transport; can look unnatural |
| **Gabions** | Wire cages of rocks absorb wave energy | Wire rusts; short lifespan |
| **Offshore breakwater** | Barrier in the sea breaks waves before they reach the shore | Costly; affects boats and wildlife |

| Soft engineering | How it works | Drawbacks |
|---|---|---|
| **Beach nourishment** | Sand is added to widen the beach, which absorbs wave energy | Must be repeated regularly |
| **Dune regeneration** | Planting marram grass and fencing stabilises dunes | Areas must be closed to visitors |
| **Mangrove replanting** | Restores a natural buffer against waves and surges | Slow to grow; needs protection |
| **Managed retreat** | The coast is allowed to erode or flood in a controlled way | Landowners lose property; compensation is disputed |

Deciding which places to protect usually involves **cost–benefit analysis**:
towns, key roads and industry are more likely to be defended than isolated
farmland.

## Worked example 1: rate of cliff retreat

The figures are **illustrative**. *A road was 120 m from the cliff edge in
2000 and 84 m from it in 2024. Calculate the mean rate of retreat and estimate
how many more years the road will be safe at this rate.* [3]

Retreat = 120 − 84 = 36 m in 24 years [1]. Rate = 36 ÷ 24 = **1.5 m per year**
[1]. Time remaining = 84 ÷ 1.5 = **56 years**, assuming the rate stays the same
[1].

A good answer adds that the rate may increase, for example with stronger
storms, or decrease if defences are built.

## Worked example 2: case-study plan

*For a named coastline, describe the opportunities it offers and evaluate how
its hazards are managed.* [7]

A plan for **the Holderness coast, England**:

- **Physical background:** cliffs of soft boulder clay that slump easily and
  are attacked by powerful waves; it is among the fastest-eroding coastlines
  in Europe; eroded sediment moves south by longshore drift to Spurn Head.
- **Opportunities:** seaside towns such as Hornsea and Withernsea for tourism;
  farmland; the Easington gas terminal.
- **Hazard:** rapid cliff retreat threatens homes, roads and farms.
- **Management:** sea walls and groynes at Hornsea and Withernsea; at
  Mappleton, rock groynes and rock armour were built in 1991 to protect the
  village and its main road; rock armour protects the Easington terminal.
- **Evaluation:** the defences protect high-value places, but groynes trap
  sediment, so beaches and cliffs further south lose protection and erosion
  increases there.

## Common mistakes

- Describing longshore drift without saying that swash moves at an angle and
  backwash moves straight down the beach.
- Forgetting the role of **wind** and vegetation in sand dune formation.
- Mixing up the conditions for coral reefs (clear, salty, shallow water) with
  those for mangroves (sheltered, muddy, tidal water).
- Treating every defence as "good" without mentioning cost or the effect on
  other parts of the coast.
- Naming a coastline in a case study but giving no specific places or
  details.

## Exam technique for 2.3

- **Explain the formation of** a landform: describe the processes in order;
  for a spit, start with the direction of longshore drift.
- **Describe** conditions for coral or mangroves: state each condition
  clearly; a list of conditions gains more credit than a vague description.
- **Suggest** why a stretch of coast is eroding quickly: look in the resource
  for rock type, fetch, wave type and lack of beach.
- **Evaluate** management: weigh cost, effectiveness and effects elsewhere,
  then make a judgement.
