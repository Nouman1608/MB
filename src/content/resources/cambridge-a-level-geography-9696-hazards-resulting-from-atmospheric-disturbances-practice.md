## Section B

**7.** The central pressure of the fictional Cyclone Merin fell from 1,010 hPa to 920 hPa before landfall.

**(a)** Using an approximate rise of 1 cm for each 1 hPa fall, calculate the rise in sea level caused by low pressure alone. **[1]**

**(b)** At landfall the surge measured 4.6 m. Calculate the percentage of the surge explained by low pressure. **[1]**

**(c)** Explain what caused the rest of the surge. **[3]**

**8.** The table shows four fictional cyclones that struck the coast of Delmar.

| Cyclone | Year | Maximum sustained wind (km/h) | People evacuated | Deaths |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Arun | 1991 | 230 | 20,000 | 31,000 |
| Bela | 2002 | 210 | 150,000 | 4,200 |
| Chand | 2012 | 240 | 600,000 | 310 |
| Dalia | 2021 | 255 | 1,100,000 | 52 |

**(a)** Give the Saffir-Simpson category of Cyclone Dalia. **[1]**

**(b)** Describe the relationship between evacuation and deaths. **[3]**

**(c)** Suggest reasons, other than evacuation, for the fall in deaths. **[3]**

**9.** Explain the processes that lead to the formation and development of a tropical cyclone. **[6]**

**10.** Explain how a tropical cyclone can cause hazards far inland. **[4]**

**11.** Explain why some groups of people are more vulnerable than others to tropical cyclones. **[6]**

**12.** "Prediction and warning are now the most important ways of reducing deaths from atmospheric disturbances." How far do you agree? **[20]**

## Answers

**1.** The ring of towering cumulonimbus cloud surrounding the eye [1], where the strongest winds and heaviest rain occur [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Moving over land, which cuts off the supply of warm, moist air and adds friction [1]. Moving over cooler water or into strong vertical wind shear [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Sea surface temperatures are generally too cool for cyclones to form [1], and strong vertical wind shear tears developing storms apart [1]. **[2]**

**4.** A watch means conditions favour tornadoes over a region for the next few hours [1]. A warning means rotation has been detected or a tornado seen, so people must take shelter at once [1]. **[2]**

**5.** Strong updraughts carry water droplets far above the freezing level [1]. The droplets freeze and collect more supercooled water, growing in layers as they are carried up and down [1]. When they become too heavy for the updraught to support, they fall as hail [1]. **[3]**

**6.** The Great Plains are flat, so air masses can meet without mountain barriers [1]. Warm, moist air from the Gulf of Mexico flows north at low level [1]. Dry air from the south-west and cold air from Canada lie above or beside it, creating instability [1]. Strong jet stream winds aloft provide wind shear, favouring supercells [1]. **[4]**

**7. (a)** 1,010 - 920 = 90 hPa, so about 90 cm or 0.9 m [1]. **[1]**

**(b)** 0.9 / 4.6 x 100 = about 20% [1]. **[1]**

**(c)** Strong onshore winds drove water towards the coast and piled it up [1]. A shallow, gently sloping sea bed let the water build up [1]. The surge may have been concentrated in a funnel-shaped bay or arrived with a high tide [1]. **[3]**

**8. (a)** Category 5 (252 km per hour or more) [1]. **[1]**

**(b)** As the number evacuated rose, deaths fell [1], from 31,000 with 20,000 evacuated in 1991 to 52 with 1,100,000 evacuated in 2021 [1]. This happened even though Dalia had the strongest winds, so the relationship is not explained by storm strength [1]. **[3]**

**(c)** Better forecasting gave more warning time [1]. More cyclone shelters and stronger buildings were built [1]. Mangroves, embankments, or higher incomes may have reduced vulnerability [1]. **[3]**

**9.** Sea surface temperature of at least about 26.5°C provides heat and moisture [1]. Warm, moist air rises in a pre-existing disturbance at least about 5° from the equator [1]. Condensation releases latent heat, warming the air so it rises faster [1]. Surface pressure falls, drawing in more air [1]. The Coriolis effect deflects inflowing air into a spiral [1]. With low wind shear the storm stays vertical, an eye of sinking air forms and the storm intensifies [1]. **[6]**

**10.** Slow-moving storms drop very heavy rain over large areas [1], causing severe river floods well inland [1]. Rain on steep slopes raises pore-water pressure and triggers landslides and debris flows [1]. High winds persist for some distance inland, damaging buildings and crops before the storm weakens [1]. **[4]**

**11.** Poorer people live in weaker housing that collapses in high winds [1]. People on low-lying land or near the coast face surge flooding [1]. Older people and people with disabilities may be unable to reach shelters [1]. Women may face cultural or caring barriers to leaving home [1]. People without transport or phones may not get or act on warnings [1]. Farmers and fishers may stay to protect livestock and boats, their only assets [1]. **[6]**

**12.** The real exam marks this essay with levels of response, not point by point. The points below are indicative creditworthy content.

*Introduction:* define prediction and warning [1], and identify other approaches such as shelters, building design and land-use planning [1].

*Prediction and warning most important:* satellite tracking and models forecast cyclone tracks days ahead [1]. Odisha's evacuation before Cyclone Phailin cut deaths to under 50 [1]. Doppler radar gives minutes of tornado warning, enough to reach shelter [1]. Warnings are relatively cheap compared with engineering [1].

*Limitations:* warnings are useless if people cannot or will not act [1]. Warning fatigue reduces response [1]. Tornado warnings are very short [1]. Intensity forecasts are less reliable than track forecasts [1].

*Other measures:* cyclone shelters give people somewhere to go [1]. Building codes and safe rooms protect against wind [1]. Defences and levees reduce flooding, though Katrina showed their failure [1]. Mangroves and land-use planning reduce exposure [1].

*Interaction:* warnings work only when combined with shelters, transport and trust [1]. Deaths fall most where all measures are used together [1]. Warnings reduce deaths far more than they reduce economic losses, as Harvey's flood damage showed [1].

*Judgement:* prediction and warning are necessary but not sufficient [1]; they matter more for cyclones than tornadoes because of lead time [1]; a reasoned final view on "how far" [1]. **[20]**
