This guide teaches **section 11.1 Video editing** of Topic 11 Video and audio editing in the Cambridge International AS & A Level Information Technology 9626 syllabus for 2025, 2026 and 2027. It is AS Level content. Paper 1 Theory asks why features are used and what compression does to quality, and Paper 2 Practical asks you to carry out the edits and exports yourself. A good edit always starts with two questions: **where will the video be shown** and **who will watch it**. A clip for a phone screen, a training DVD and a website banner need different aspect ratios, text sizes, resolutions and file sizes.

## Setting up and structuring the clip

**Aspect ratio** is the ratio of width to height. 16:9 suits modern televisions and most online video. 4:3 matches older televisions and some tablets. 9:16 (tall) suits phone-first platforms. Set the ratio first, because everything else is placed inside the frame. If a source does not match, it is cropped, or the frame shows black bars.

**Trimming** removes unwanted footage from the start or end of a clip, such as the seconds before a speaker begins. **Splicing** (joining) places clips one after another on the timeline. Trimming keeps the video focused and shortens the file. Splicing builds a story from separate shots.

**Transitions** are effects between clips, such as a cross-dissolve or a wipe. They signal a change of scene and avoid an abrupt jump. **Fading** gradually changes opacity or volume: a fade in from black opens a video gently, and a fade out to black closes it. Too many different transitions look amateur, so use one or two consistently.

**Pan and zoom** effects move or scale the visible area. They bring life to a still image (a slow zoom into a photograph) and direct attention to part of the frame.

## Text: titles, subtitles, captions and credits

- **Titles** introduce the video or a section.
- **Subtitles** show the words spoken, usually in the same or another language.
- **Captions** label what is on screen, such as a speaker's name, and can include descriptions of sounds for viewers who cannot hear.
- **Credits** list who made the video and who owns the material used.

Credits should carry **appropriate source information**: the creator, the title of the work, and the licence or permission, for music, images and clips taken from elsewhere. This respects copyright and shows the viewer where material came from.

For **scrolling credits**, text moves up the screen. Use **blank lines** between groups, for example between "Music" and "Photography", so each group is easy to separate. Scroll slowly enough to read.

**Readability** means a viewer can read text in the time it is on screen. Use a large sans-serif font, strong contrast between text and background (white on dark, or a semi-transparent box behind the text), keep lines short, and leave each caption on screen for at least as long as it takes to read aloud. Keep text inside the **safe area** away from the edges, because some screens crop the frame.

## Still images

You can **extract a still image** from a clip by exporting one frame, for example for a poster or a thumbnail. You can **insert a still image** into the timeline, giving it a duration, for a title card, a logo or a photograph.

A still image rarely matches the video's shape, so it must be **resized and cropped** to match the aspect ratio.

**Worked example 1: fitting a photograph to a 16:9 video.** A photograph is 4000 pixels wide and 3000 pixels high, so its ratio is 4:3. The video is 1920 by 1080 pixels (16:9).

1. Keep the full width of 4000 pixels. A 16:9 shape that wide must be 4000 × 9 ÷ 16 = 2250 pixels high.
2. Crop the photograph from 3000 to 2250 pixels high, removing 750 pixels in total (for example 375 from the top and 375 from the bottom).
3. Resize the cropped image from 4000 × 2250 to 1920 × 1080. The scale factor is 1920 ÷ 4000 = 0.48 in both directions, so the proportions stay correct.

If you resized the original 4:3 photograph directly to 1920 × 1080 without cropping, it would be stretched and people would look wide. Always crop first, then resize.

## Audio inside a video

- **Adding audio** places a music track or narration on the timeline, often under the video clip.
- **Editing audio within a clip** includes adjusting volume, fading it in and out, and removing noise, so speech stays clear above music.
- **Removing audio** deletes the original sound, useful when the camera microphone picked up wind or when you want to replace it with a voiceover.

Audio and video must stay **in sync**: lips and sound should match. Detaching the sound to edit it, then reattaching it, can break this if you are careless.

## Speed, filters and colour correction

**Altering speed** makes a clip play faster (time-lapse, to shorten a slow process) or slower (slow motion, to show detail). A 60-second clip at 200% speed lasts 60 ÷ 2 = 30 seconds. At 50% speed it lasts 60 ÷ 0.5 = 120 seconds. Changing speed also changes the pitch and timing of the clip's original audio, so the sound is often removed or replaced.

**Filters** apply a visual effect to a clip, such as black and white, blur or sharpen. **Colour correction** fixes problems with brightness, contrast, saturation and white balance, so that footage shot in different lighting looks consistent and skin tones look natural. Matching the colour of clips that will be joined together stops the viewer noticing the cut.

## Why compression is needed

Raw video is enormous.

**Worked example 2: size of uncompressed video.** A 1920 × 1080 frame has 1920 × 1080 = 2,073,600 pixels. With 24 bits of colour per pixel, one frame holds 2,073,600 × 24 = 49,766,400 bits. At 30 frames per second that is 49,766,400 × 30 = 1,492,992,000 bits every second, about 1.49 gigabits per second or roughly 187 megabytes per second. One minute would need over 11 gigabytes. No ordinary internet connection could stream this, and it would fill a disc quickly.

**Compression** reduces the file size.

- **Lossless compression** reduces size with no loss of quality, because the original can be rebuilt exactly. The saving is modest for video.
- **Lossy compression** permanently discards information the eye is least likely to notice, giving a much smaller file. Quality falls as compression increases. Heavy compression produces blocky areas, blurred detail, banding in gradients and smeared movement.

Video compression uses two ideas. **Within a frame**, areas of similar colour are described together. **Between frames**, the system stores only what changes: if only a speaker's mouth moves against a fixed background, the background is not stored again for every frame.

Compression can also be applied by reducing **resolution** (fewer pixels per frame), by reducing the **frame rate**, or by lowering the **bit rate** (the data allowed per second). Each choice lowers quality and size together.

**Worked example 3: size from bit rate.** A video is encoded at 8 megabits per second and lasts 2 minutes (120 seconds).

1. Total bits = 8 × 120 = 960 megabits.
2. Divide by 8 to convert to megabytes: 960 ÷ 8 = 120 MB.
3. Over a 20 megabit-per-second connection it takes at least 960 ÷ 20 = 48 seconds to transfer.

Halving the bit rate to 4 megabits per second halves the file to 60 MB, but lowers quality.

## Compressing for different media

The syllabus names DVD, the internet and mobile devices. The target medium sets the **resolution and bit rate** that fit.

| Medium | Typical choice | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| DVD | Standard definition, roughly 720 × 576 pixels | A disc has fixed capacity and players expect standard definition |
| Internet | 1280 × 720 or 1920 × 1080, moderate bit rate | Balances sharpness against loading time and data use |
| Mobile devices | Lower resolution, such as 640 × 360 or 854 × 480, low bit rate | Small screens do not show extra detail; saves data allowance and storage |

These figures are typical rather than fixed rules. State the principle: **match resolution and bit rate to the screen size and the connection**.

## Export file formats

Exporting turns the edited project into a single playable file. The format chosen decides compatibility, quality and size.

| Format | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|
| MP4 | Plays on almost all devices and web browsers; good quality for a small file size | Compression is lossy; some editing software needs a licensed codec |
| AVI | Long-established; works well in older Windows programs; can hold high-quality video | Often large files; poor support on some phones and the web |
| MOV | High quality; common on Apple devices and in editing workflows | Large files; less supported on some non-Apple devices |
| WMV | Small files; plays on Windows systems | Limited support on other systems; quality lower at small sizes |

A format is a **container** that holds video, audio and information about them. The quality actually depends on the **codec** (the method used to compress the data) and the settings, as well as the container.

## Why typical features are used

The syllabus asks you to explain why features exist. Give a reason, not just a definition.

| Feature | Why it is used |
|---|---|
| Trimming and cropping | Removes unwanted material and fits the frame to the display |
| Text-based slides | Introduce topics or add information without recording footage |
| Credits | Acknowledge people and sources and meet copyright expectations |
| Captions and subtitles | Help viewers who cannot hear, who watch with the sound off or who speak another language |
| Fading effects | Make openings, endings and scene changes smooth |
| Extracting a still image | Reuses a frame for a poster, thumbnail or report |
| Inserting a still image | Adds a logo, photograph or diagram to the timeline |
| Editing audio | Keeps speech clear and the sound balanced |
| Exporting in different formats | Allows playback on the devices the audience actually uses |
| Compressing to different resolutions | Reduces file size and suits the screen and connection |

## Common mistakes

- Resizing a picture to the frame without cropping, so it is stretched.
- Saying a compressed file "loses nothing". Only lossless compression keeps all the data, and video is normally lossy.
- Naming a format without a reason. "MP4 is best" earns nothing; "MP4 plays on most devices and gives a small file" does.
- Giving the same advice for DVD, internet and mobile output, rather than linking resolution to the screen and connection.
- Forgetting source information in credits.
- Confusing subtitles (the words spoken) with captions (descriptions of the picture and sound).
- Placing light text on a light background and calling it readable.

## Exam technique

- **Describe** a feature: what it does to the clip.
- **Explain why** a feature is used: tie it to the audience, the device or the file.
- **Explain the effect of compression**: say what is lost (detail, colour accuracy, smooth motion), and link it to file size.
- **Compare formats**: give at least one advantage and one disadvantage for each format named, relating them to the scenario.
- In Paper 2, name the file and format exactly as the task asks. A correct edit saved in the wrong format earns no marks.
