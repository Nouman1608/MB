> **These are original questions written for Marlbridge** on the sociocultural approach (content) of IB DP Psychology, first assessment 2027. They are not past-paper questions, and the scenarios are invented. The guide says that Paper 1 Section A asks you to explain or describe a psychological idea and give an example, and Section B asks you to apply content to an unseen situation in one of the four contexts. Question 11 uses the 15-mark tariff of the concept-based extended response; other tariffs are for practice.

Related: [sociocultural approach study guide](/resources/ib-ib-dp-psychology-sociocultural-approach-study-guide/) and [revision notes](/resources/ib-ib-dp-psychology-sociocultural-approach-revision-notes/).

## Section A

**1.** Explain cognitive dissonance, and give one example. **[4]**

**2.** Distinguish between an emic approach and an etic approach to researching human behaviour. **[4]**

**3.** Explain what is meant by enculturation, and give one example. **[4]**

**4.** Explain the application of one compliance technique to change behaviour. **[4]**

**5.** Describe one model of acculturation. **[4]**

## Section B

**6.** Read the scenario (invented for practice; context: health and well-being).

> A group of teenagers in a school begin to vape because a few popular students do. Maya does not want to, but she feels uneasy when she refuses, and she says "just once, it's fine". She later tells a friend that she does not really like it but "everyone does it". The school plans a campaign using older students who have chosen not to vape.

**(a)** Use conformity to explain Maya's behaviour. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain why the school's plan might change the behaviour of younger students, using social learning theory. **[3]**

**7.** Read the scenario (invented for practice; context: human development).

> Six-year-old Karim has recently moved to a country where children greet adults with a handshake and eye contact. In his previous community, children greeted adults by lowering their eyes. At first he lowers his eyes and his teacher thinks he is shy. After two terms he shakes hands and looks at adults.

**(a)** Explain Karim's early behaviour using enculturation. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain how acculturation could account for the change. **[3]**

**8.** A researcher plans to study how family expectations affect career choice in one Indigenous community. Explain why an emic approach may be more appropriate than using a questionnaire developed in another country. **[4]**

**9.** An employee agrees to work late for a project she thinks is pointless. After a month she tells colleagues the project is "actually quite important". Use cognitive dissonance to explain her change in attitude. **[3]**

**10.** Evaluate social identity theory as an explanation of group behaviour. **[6]**

## Section C

**11.** Evaluate the use of the sociocultural approach to understand human behaviour. Refer to at least two concepts in your answer. **[15]**

## Answers

**1.** Cognitive dissonance is an uncomfortable mental state when two beliefs, or a belief and a behaviour, are inconsistent **[1]**. People are motivated to reduce the discomfort **[1]**, for example by changing an attitude, changing behaviour or adding a justification **[1]**. Example, any relevant one, such as a person who values health but smokes deciding that the risks are exaggerated **[1]**. Study support such as Festinger and Carlsmith (1959), in which people paid very little to describe a boring task as enjoyable later rated the task as more enjoyable than people paid more, may be credited as explanation.

**2.** An etic approach studies behaviour from outside a culture, using concepts and measures thought to apply across cultures, so it supports comparisons **[1]**. An emic approach studies a group from the insider perspective, using concepts and meanings of that group **[1]**. A limitation of etic work is that measures developed in one culture may impose its meanings on others (an imposed etic) **[1]**. Emic work gives depth and sensitivity but results may not be generalised or compared **[1]**.

**3.** Enculturation is the process by which a person learns and internalises the norms, values and behaviours of their own culture from childhood **[1]**, through family, school, peers and media **[1]**. Example, any relevant one, such as children learning when to speak to elders by watching and being corrected by parents **[1]**, with an explanation of how this is enculturation (learning, not choosing, a norm) **[1]**.

**4.** Any one technique, for example foot-in-the-door **[1]**: a small request is agreed to first **[1]**, which makes a later, larger request more likely to be agreed to because people see themselves as helpful **[1]**. Application to change behaviour, such as asking students to sign a recycling pledge before asking them to join a weekly rota **[1]**. Door-in-the-face or low-ball techniques are equally creditable.

**5.** Berry's model: acculturation strategies depend on whether a person wants to keep their own culture and whether they want contact with the new one **[1]**. Four strategies **[1]**: integration (both), assimilation (new only), separation (own only), marginalisation (neither) **[1]**. Link to the experience of immigrants, refugees or other long-stay residents, for example integration being associated with lower acculturative stress **[1]**. Credit another model if described accurately.

**6.** (a) Maya's behaviour is conformity: a change in behaviour due to real or imagined group pressure **[1]**. Normative influence: she goes along to be accepted and avoid rejection, even though she privately disagrees ("does not really like it") **[1]**, which is compliance without private acceptance. Link to the scenario detail "everyone does it" and feeling uneasy when refusing **[1]**.

(b) Social learning: students observe models and can imitate behaviour they see rewarded **[1]**. Older students who have chosen not to vape are admired models, so younger students attend to them and remember the behaviour **[1]**. Seeing them gain approval for not vaping provides vicarious reinforcement, which motivates younger students to reproduce the behaviour **[1]**.

**7.** (a) Enculturation is learning the norms of your own culture from childhood **[1]**. Karim has internalised that lowering the eyes is respectful **[1]**, and so he shows it automatically in the new setting, where it is read differently **[1]**.

(b) Acculturation is change resulting from contact between cultures **[1]**. Karim adapts to the new norms by observing classmates and receiving feedback **[1]**, which shows he has moved toward integration (keeping some of his own norms at home) or assimilation (if he drops his original norm completely) **[1]**.

**8.** An emic approach studies the community from within, using its own concepts **[1]**. Career choice may be defined differently, for instance as a family or community decision, so a questionnaire designed elsewhere may not measure the same construct (construct validity) **[1]**. Local researchers or community members can help word questions and interpret answers **[1]**, giving a more valid and ethical result, with respect for Indigenous perspectives **[1]**. Alternatively, credit a point about the limit that emic findings cannot be generalised, if it is linked to the question.

**9.** She acted in a way that conflicts with her belief ("the project is pointless") **[1]**. This creates cognitive dissonance **[1]**. Because the behaviour cannot be undone and she lacks an external justification, she changes the attitude to be consistent with the behaviour **[1]**.

**10.** Description of the theory (up to 2): groups provide part of the self-concept; categorisation, identification, comparison **[1]**; leads to in-group favouritism **[1]**. Evaluation (up to 4), any combination of: minimal group studies show categorisation alone can produce favouritism (strength) **[1]**; the theory can be applied to reduce conflict, for example through superordinate goals **[1]**; evidence comes from artificial tasks with possible demand characteristics **[1]**; explains group behaviour well but not why individuals differ in how strongly they identify **[1]**; cultural differences, such as in collectivist cultures where group identity may be stronger **[1]**. Award up to **[6]**.

**11.** Markband guide: 13 to 15 for well-developed critical analysis, links between concepts throughout and a reasoned conclusion; 10 to 12 for good analysis lacking development; lower bands for description. Indicative content, one mark per developed point up to the markband:
- The approach explains behaviour through social and cultural factors such as conformity, social identity, enculturation and cognitive dissonance **[2]**.
- Perspective: the approach provides holistic understanding that the biological and cognitive approaches miss, but each approach on its own is incomplete, and the three interact (for example cognitive biases that come from cultural experience) **[2]**.
- Bias: etic research can impose one culture's concepts on others, and samples from Western universities limit generalisation; emic approaches reduce this bias but limit comparison **[2]**.
- Causality: experiments such as conformity studies support cause and effect, but real social behaviour involves many interacting variables, and quasi-experiments of cultures cannot assign people to a culture **[2]**.
- Measurement: constructs such as culture or identity are hard to operationalise; cultural dimensions are averages and risk stereotyping **[2]**.
- Change: the approach supports strategies to change behaviour (shared goals, role models, compliance techniques) but long-term effects are less certain **[2]**.
- Responsibility: researchers must avoid stigmatising groups, respect Indigenous communities and consider deception and manipulation **[2]**.
- A conclusion that weighs the approach, for example that it is strongest when combined with other approaches and emic methods **[1]**.

## Where marks are usually lost

- **Question 1:** describing dissonance as "feeling bad" without the inconsistency.
- **Question 2:** stating a definition of each term without a contrast.
- **Question 6(b):** describing Bandura's theory without applying it to the older students.
- **Question 11:** a list of studies, with one concept and no conclusion.
