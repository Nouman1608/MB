Stock (also called inventory) is the raw materials, components, work in progress and finished goods a business holds. Holding too much ties up cash and storage space and risks waste; holding too little risks running out and losing sales. Managing stock means finding the balance.

## Bar gate stock graphs

A bar gate stock graph plots the quantity of one item in stock (vertical axis) against time (horizontal axis). Stock falls as it is used, then jumps straight up when a delivery arrives. The specification asks you to **interpret** these graphs, so learn the standard labels (these are textbook terms, not specification wording):

| Label | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Maximum stock level | The most the business wants to hold, often limited by space or cash |
| Minimum (buffer) stock | A safety level kept in case of late deliveries or a rise in demand |
| Reorder level | The stock level at which a new order is placed |
| Reorder quantity | The amount ordered each time |
| Lead time | The time between placing an order and receiving it |
| Usage rate | How much stock is used per period; the slope of the falling line |

Useful standard relationships:

- Reorder level = (usage per period × lead time) + buffer stock
- Reorder quantity = maximum stock − buffer stock, if each delivery arrives just as stock reaches the buffer

### Worked example 1: dog food at Kelda Pet Supplies

Kelda keeps a maximum of 1,200 bags of dog food and a buffer of 200 bags. It sells 200 bags a week and its supplier takes 2 weeks to deliver. The table shows what a bar gate graph would plot.

| End of week | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 (before delivery) | 5 (after delivery) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Bags in stock | 1,200 | 1,000 | 800 | 600 | 400 | 200 | 1,200 |

Reading the graph:

1. **Usage rate:** stock falls by 200 bags each week.
2. **Reorder level:** (200 × 2) + 200 = **600 bags**, reached at the end of week 3, when the order is placed.
3. **Lead time:** the delivery arrives two weeks later, at the end of week 5.
4. **Reorder quantity:** stock jumps from 200 to 1,200, so **1,000 bags** are delivered.
5. **Buffer stock:** the lowest point is 200 bags, which is never used in normal weeks.

**What if?** If demand rose to 250 bags a week with the same reorder level, stock would fall from 600 to 100 bags during the two-week lead time, cutting into the buffer. If the delivery were also a week late, stock would fall below zero and Kelda would run out. This is why examiners ask what the buffer is for, and why a change in usage should lead to a change in the reorder level.

## Just in time (JIT) stock control

JIT means ordering stock so that it arrives just when it is needed for production or sale, keeping little or no buffer stock.

| Benefits | Drawbacks |
|---|---|
| Less cash tied up in stock | Depends on very reliable suppliers |
| Lower storage costs; space freed for other uses | A late delivery can stop production or leave shelves empty |
| Less waste from stock going out of date or out of fashion | More frequent, smaller deliveries can cost more each |
| Problems with supplies are spotted quickly | Cannot easily meet a sudden rise in demand |
| | Bulk-buying discounts may be lost |

JIT suits businesses with predictable demand, nearby reliable suppliers and perishable or quickly changing products, such as a sandwich bar ordering fresh bread daily. It suits poorly a business whose suppliers are far away or unreliable, or whose demand is hard to forecast.

## The role of procurement

Procurement is the process of finding and buying the goods and services a business needs from suppliers. The specification lists what a business looks for in its **relationships with suppliers**:

- **Quality:** do supplies meet the standard the business needs? Poor quality causes faults, waste and complaints.
- **Delivery:** the specification names three parts: **cost** (who pays for delivery and how much), **speed** (how quickly orders arrive, i.e. the lead time) and **reliability** (how often they arrive on time and complete).
- **Availability:** can the supplier provide the quantities needed, including at short notice?
- **Cost:** the price of the supplies, and any discounts or payment terms.
- **Trust:** confidence that the supplier will keep its promises, which builds over time and allows closer working, such as JIT.

### Worked example 2: Granby Bike Parts chooses a supplier

Granby needs 10,000 brake cables a year, delivered in 50 deliveries. Each late delivery costs it about £120 in lost sales and rushed alternatives.

| | Supplier A | Supplier B |
|---|---|---|
| Price per cable | £4.00 | £3.70 |
| Lead time | 2 days | 7 days |
| On-time deliveries | 98% | 85% |
| Cost of cables | £40,000 | £37,000 |
| Expected late deliveries | 50 × 2% = 1 | 50 × 15% = 7.5 |
| Cost of lateness | 1 × £120 = £120 | 7.5 × £120 = £900 |
| Total | **£40,120** | **£37,900** |

Supplier B is still £2,220 cheaper. But its 7-day lead time means Granby must hold more buffer stock, which ties up cash and space, and frequent lateness could damage Granby's reputation with its own customers. If Granby wanted to move to JIT, only Supplier A's speed and reliability would make it possible. The cheapest supplier is not automatically the best one.

## Logistics and supply decisions

Logistics is the movement and storage of goods through the supply chain, from suppliers to the business and from the business to its customers. The specification asks for the impact of logistics and supply decisions on three things:

| Decision | Costs | Reputation | Customer satisfaction |
|---|---|---|---|
| Choosing the cheapest, slowest supplier | Lower purchase cost; higher stock-holding cost | Risk if delays become visible to customers | Falls if products are out of stock |
| Offering next-day delivery to customers | Higher delivery costs | Seen as fast and reliable | Rises with speed and convenience |
| Using JIT | Lower storage costs | Damaged if shelves are empty | Falls if products are unavailable |
| Sourcing from ethical or local suppliers | Often higher | Improved with customers who value it | Can rise among those customers |

The pattern: decisions that cut costs often put reputation or satisfaction at risk, and decisions that protect them usually cost more. Strong answers say which matters more to the business in the case.

## Common mistakes

- Reading the reorder level from the lowest point of the graph; the lowest point is the buffer.
- Confusing lead time (a time) with reorder quantity (an amount).
- Saying JIT means holding no stock at all ever; it means very little, arriving as needed.
- Choosing a supplier on price alone.
- Listing the procurement factors without applying them to the business in the question.

## Exam technique

- **Identify:** read maximum stock, buffer, reorder level, reorder quantity or lead time from a graph or table.
- **Calculate:** a reorder level, how long stock lasts, or the total cost of a supplier including lateness.
- **Outline:** two linked points in context, such as how a late delivery affects Kelda's customers.
- **Explain:** a general statement with two expansions, for example why JIT needs reliable suppliers.
- **Analyse:** follow a supply decision to costs, reputation and customer satisfaction.
- **Justify:** choose between two suppliers or two stock methods using figures and reliability.
- **Evaluate:** weigh cost savings against the risks of running out, and conclude on what the decision depends.
