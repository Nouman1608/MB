## The definitions

- **Finite resource**: a natural resource that is used up at a faster rate than it is replaced.
- **Sustainable management of resources**: the use of strategies that ensure the needs of the present are
  met without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs.

Check your definition has **present needs** and **future generations** in it.

## Seven strategies in one table

| Strategy | What it means | Benefit | Limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Reduce and reuse | Use less material; use items again | Saves material and the energy of reprocessing | Relies on consumers and designers changing habits |
| Recycling (accessibility, ease, education) | Recover material from waste | Less new ore mined; less waste to landfill | Collection, sorting and reprocessing cost money and energy; some items hard to separate |
| Increased efficiency of extraction | Recover more useful material per tonne of ore | Deposits last longer; less waste | New technology is expensive |
| Avoiding resource depletion | Plan rates of use to keep reserves | Resources remain for the future | Hard to agree while demand rises; may slow economic growth |
| Legislation | Laws on recycling, extraction and restoration | Applies to everyone at once | Useless unless enforced; can raise costs |
| Enforcement | Inspections, monitoring and penalties | Makes laws effective | Needs money, staff and political will; weak where corruption exists |
| Use of alternative materials | Replace a scarce material with another | Reduces demand for the scarce one | Alternative may cost more, perform worse or be finite too |

## Recycling: the three conditions

| Condition | Example measure | Why it raises recycling |
|---|---|---|
| Accessibility | Kerbside collection; bins near shops and schools | Less distance and effort to reach a collection point |
| Ease | Clearly labelled bins; fewer categories to sort | People are less likely to give up or put items in the wrong bin |
| Education | School lessons; labels on packaging; campaigns | People know what can be recycled and why it matters |

## Strategy pairs that depend on each other

- Legislation **needs** enforcement.
- Recycling **needs** accessibility, ease and education.
- Avoiding depletion **needs** alternatives and efficiency, so needs are still met.

## Percentage change pattern

- Recycling rate = mass recycled / mass used x 100.
- Percentage change = change / original x 100.
- *Invented example:* recycling rises from 300 t to 360 t: change 60 t; 60 / 300 x 100 = 20% increase.
- A rise from 30% to 36% is a rise of 6 **percentage points**, not 6%.

## "Discuss" structure

1. State the strategy and what it does.
2. Benefits, each with a reason.
3. Limitations, each with a reason.
4. Judgement: when does it work best? What must go with it?

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Finite resource | Used up faster than it is replaced |
| Sustainable management | Meeting present needs without compromising future generations |
| Reduce | Use less material in the first place |
| Reuse | Use an item again for the same or another purpose |
| Recycling | Recovering material from waste to make new products |
| Resource depletion | Using up a resource's reserves |
| Legislation | Laws |
| Enforcement | Making sure laws are obeyed |
| Alternative material | A substitute that does the same job as a scarce material |

## Exam traps

- "A resource that will run out" is not the syllabus definition of a finite resource.
- Do not stop the sustainable management definition after "present needs".
- Legislation and enforcement are **two** strategies: name both if asked for strategies.
- "Recycling is good for the environment" scores nothing: say what it reduces (new mining, waste, energy).
- Recycling still uses energy and money: it is not free of limitations.
- Alternative materials can be finite: plastics come from petroleum.
- End a "discuss" answer with a judgement.

## Self-test

1. Define a finite resource.
2. Define sustainable management of resources.
3. Why are metal ores finite?
4. Give **one** example of reducing and **one** of reusing.
5. Name the **three** conditions the syllabus links to recycling.
6. Suggest **one** way to make recycling easier.
7. How does increased efficiency of extraction help sustainability?
8. Why does legislation need enforcement?
9. Give **one** limitation of using alternative materials.
10. Recycled mass rises from 400 t to 500 t. Calculate the percentage increase.
11. Why is reuse usually better than recycling?
12. Give **one** reason why avoiding resource depletion is hard to achieve.

**Answers:** 1. A natural resource that is used up at a faster rate than it is replaced. 2. The use of
strategies that ensure the needs of the present are met without compromising the ability of future
generations to meet their own needs. 3. They form over millions of years but are mined in decades.
4. Reduce: lighter packaging or longer-lasting products. Reuse: refillable bottles or reclaimed building
stone. 5. Accessibility, ease and education. 6. Clearly labelled bins, or fewer categories to sort.
7. More useful material is recovered from each tonne of ore, so deposits last longer and less waste is
produced. 8. Without inspections and penalties people and companies can ignore the law. 9. It may cost
more, perform less well or be finite itself. 10. 100 / 400 x 100 = 25%. 11. The item does not have to be
melted and reprocessed, so energy is saved. 12. Demand keeps rising, and limits on extraction can slow
economic growth, so countries may not agree.
