This guide goes deeper on sub-topic **3.3 Macroeconomic objectives** in Unit 3 of the IB Diploma Programme *Economics guide* (first assessment 2022). Constructing a **weighted price index** is an **HL-only calculation** and is marked below. The [measuring and managing the economy guide](/resources/ib-dp-economics-macroeconomics-measuring-and-managing-the-economy/) lists the objectives, and the [measurement revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-economics-measuring-activity-unemployment-and-inflation-revision-notes/) set out the types of unemployment and causes of inflation. This page works through one country's data on every objective, explains the Phillips curve, and shows how to argue about conflicts between objectives. Consolidate with the [3.3 revision notes](/resources/ib-ib-dp-economics-dp-economics-ib-dp-economics-3-3-revision-notes/) and practise with the [3.3 practice questions](/resources/ib-ib-dp-economics-dp-economics-ib-dp-economics-3-3-practice/).

All figures are invented. The example economy is Sorvik, whose currency is the sor.

## The objectives in 3.3

- **Low unemployment**
- **Low and stable rate of inflation**
- **Economic growth**
- **A sustainable level of government debt**

For each, you need to know how it is measured, why it matters (its consequences), what causes problems, and how it relates to the others.

## Low unemployment

**Unemployment** means people of working age who are willing and able to work and actively seeking work, but without a job.

```
Unemployment rate = number unemployed / labour force x 100
```

The **labour force** is the employed plus the unemployed; it excludes people not seeking work, such as full-time students and retirees.

**Worked example.** Sorvik's labour force is 18.0 million and 1.26 million are unemployed: 1.26 / 18.0 × 100 = **7.0%**. A further 0.3 million **discouraged workers** have given up searching, so they are not counted. Including them: (1.26 + 0.3) / (18.0 + 0.3) × 100 ≈ **8.5%**. The official figure understates the problem.

### Why the official rate can mislead

- **Hidden unemployment:** discouraged workers, and people on sickness or training schemes who would otherwise be unemployed.
- **Underemployment:** part-time workers who want full-time work, and skilled workers in low-skilled jobs, count as employed.
- **Informal work:** some people counted as unemployed may be working informally.
- **Averages hide disparities** between regions, age groups, ethnic groups and genders.

### Consequences

| Economic | Personal and social |
|---|---|
| Lost output: the economy is inside its PPC | Loss of income and lower living standards |
| Lower tax revenue and higher spending on benefits | Stress, poorer health, family breakdown |
| Loss of skills the longer people are out of work | Higher crime and social unrest in affected areas |
| Wider income inequality | Lasting damage to young people's careers |

### Types and the natural rate

**Frictional** (between jobs), **seasonal** (demand varies with the season), **structural** (mismatch of skills or location after changes in the economy) and **cyclical or demand-deficient** (falling AD in a contraction). The **natural rate of unemployment** is the rate when the economy is at potential output: frictional + seasonal + structural, with no cyclical unemployment. Cyclical unemployment calls for demand-side policy; structural unemployment calls for supply-side measures such as retraining.

## Low and stable inflation

**Inflation** is a sustained rise in the general price level. **Disinflation** is a fall in the rate of inflation, so prices still rise but more slowly. **Deflation** is a sustained fall in the general price level.

Inflation is measured with a **consumer price index (CPI)**, built from a basket of goods and services bought by a typical household.

```
Inflation rate = (CPI this year - CPI last year) / CPI last year x 100
```

If Sorvik's CPI rises from 125.0 to 131.25, inflation = 6.25 / 125.0 × 100 = **5.0%**.

### Constructing a weighted price index (HL only)

Each item's price change is weighted by the share of household spending on it.

| Item | Weight | Base-year price (sors) | Current price (sors) | Price index (base = 100) | Weight × index |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Food | 40 | 5.00 | 5.40 | 108 | 4,320 |
| Housing | 35 | 800 | 840 | 105 | 3,675 |
| Transport | 25 | 2.00 | 2.24 | 112 | 2,800 |
| **Total** | **100** | | | | **10,795** |

Weighted price index = 10,795 / 100 = **107.95**, so the price level has risen by **7.95%**. Transport rose fastest (12%) but has the smallest weight, so it pulls the index up less than food.

### Problems with the CPI

- Households with different spending patterns (pensioners, students, rural families) face different inflation rates.
- Quality improvements mean a higher price may buy a better product.
- New products enter the basket only with a delay.
- Consumers switch to cheaper substitutes when prices rise, which a fixed basket ignores.

### Consequences

**Inflation:** reduces the purchasing power of incomes not linked to prices; redistributes from savers and lenders to borrowers when interest rates do not keep pace; creates uncertainty that can reduce investment; makes exports less competitive if prices rise faster than abroad.

**Deflation:** consumers may delay purchases expecting lower prices; the real burden of debt rises; firms' profits fall, so they cut investment and jobs. Deflation caused by rising productivity and AS ("good" deflation) is far less harmful than deflation caused by collapsing AD ("bad" deflation).

**Causes:** **demand-pull** inflation (AD rising faster than productive capacity) and **cost-push** inflation (rising costs shift SRAS left).

## Economic growth

Growth is measured as the percentage change in **real GDP**. Its consequences cut both ways: higher living standards, more jobs and more tax revenue, against possible inflation, a worse current account as imports rise, wider inequality if gains are unevenly shared, and environmental damage.

## Relationships between unemployment and inflation

The **short-run Phillips curve (SRPC)** shows an inverse relationship: lower unemployment comes with higher inflation, because rising AD both creates jobs and pulls up prices.

The **long-run Phillips curve (LRPC)** is vertical at the **natural rate of unemployment**. If the government pushes unemployment below the natural rate, workers come to expect higher inflation and bargain for higher wages, so the SRPC shifts upwards. Unemployment returns to the natural rate at a higher inflation rate. There is no lasting trade-off.

A negative supply shock can produce **stagflation**: rising inflation and rising unemployment together, which a single SRPC cannot show.

## A sustainable level of government debt

**Government debt** is the total amount the government owes, built up from past **budget deficits** (years when spending exceeded revenue). It is usually compared with GDP.

**Worked example.** Sorvik's debt is 540 billion sors and nominal GDP is 900 billion: a debt-to-GDP ratio of **60%**. Next year the government runs a deficit of 36 billion, so debt rises to 576 billion, while nominal GDP grows 5% to 945 billion. The ratio becomes 576 / 945 × 100 ≈ **61.0%**. With an interest rate of 4%, debt servicing costs 0.04 × 540 = 21.6 billion sors, or **2.4% of GDP**, that cannot be spent on schools or hospitals.

**Costs of high debt:** rising **debt servicing** costs with a large opportunity cost; a risk of a **credit rating downgrade**, raising borrowing costs; and **less room for fiscal policy** in a future recession.

## Conflicts between objectives

| Conflict | Reasoning |
|---|---|
| Low unemployment vs low inflation | Raising AD cuts cyclical unemployment but, near potential output, pulls up prices (SRPC trade-off) |
| Growth vs low inflation | Fast demand-led growth can create an inflationary gap |
| Growth vs sustainable debt | Borrowing to fund spending raises growth now but adds to debt |
| Growth vs current account and environment | Rising incomes draw in imports and use more resources |

Conflicts are strongest when growth is demand-led and the economy is near capacity. Supply-side growth that shifts LRAS right can reduce unemployment and inflation together.

## Common mistakes

- Calculating unemployment as a percentage of the whole population instead of the labour force.
- Confusing disinflation with deflation.
- Averaging price indices without weights in the HL calculation.
- Confusing debt (a stock) with a deficit (a yearly flow).
- Drawing a downward-sloping LRPC.

## Exam technique

- **Calculate:** write the formula, show substitution, state units and whether the figure is a rate or an index.
- **Explain** consequences as chains, for example: "unexpected inflation, so real interest rates fall, so savers lose and borrowers gain".
- **Distinguish** demand-pull from cost-push using the AD-AS diagram for each.
- **Evaluate** a policy priority by setting the cost of unemployment against the cost of inflation, using the Phillips curve and the type of unemployment, and reach a judgement that depends on the economy's position in the business cycle.
