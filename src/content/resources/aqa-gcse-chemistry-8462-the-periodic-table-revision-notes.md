## 4.1.2.1 Layout of the table

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Atomic (proton) number | Order of the modern table |
| Group | Column; same number of outer electrons; similar chemical properties |
| Period | Row; same number of occupied shells |
| Periodic | Similar properties recur at regular intervals |

**Method: position from electronic structure (first 20 elements)**

```
1. Count shells           -> period
2. Count outer electrons  -> group (1 to 7)
3. Full outer shell       -> Group 0
```

Reminder: magnesium is 2,8,2, so period 3, Group 2. Nitrogen is 2,5, so period 2, Group 5.

Working backwards: Group 3, period 3 means three shells with 3 outer electrons, 2,8,3, which is 13 electrons,
so aluminium.

## 4.1.2.2 Development of the table

| Step | What happened | Why it mattered |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Early tables in strict order of atomic weight | Incomplete; some elements put in the wrong group |
| 2 | Mendeleev left gaps | Kept similar elements together |
| 3 | Mendeleev swapped some pairs (tellurium before iodine) | Put iodine with the halogens it resembles |
| 4 | Predicted elements found (gallium, scandium, germanium) | Successful predictions supported his table |
| 5 | Isotopes discovered | Explained why atomic-weight order is sometimes wrong |

**Isotope explanation in one line:** relative atomic mass is an average over isotopes, so an element with a
lot of heavier isotopes can have a higher relative atomic mass than the element after it, even though it has
fewer protons.

Pairs to know: tellurium (52, 127.6) before iodine (53, 126.9); argon (18, 39.95) before potassium (19, 39.10).

**Science idea (WS 1.1):** a prediction that comes true supports a theory; one that fails would refute it.

## 4.1.2.3 Metals and non-metals

- **Metal:** reacts to form **positive ions**. Left and bottom of the table. Most elements.
- **Non-metal:** does **not** form positive ions. Right and top.

| | Metals | Non-metals |
|---|---|---|
| Conduct heat and electricity | Yes | No (graphite conducts) |
| Melting/boiling points | Mostly high | Mostly low |
| Solid | Malleable, shiny | Brittle, dull |
| Outer electrons | Few (usually 1 to 3) | Many (4 to 7, or full) |
| In reactions | Lose electrons | Gain or share electrons |
| Oxides | Basic | Often acidic |

**Link to position:** few outer electrons, easily lost, so left side. Further down a group, outer electrons
are further from the nucleus and lost more easily, so more metallic (Group 4: carbon non-metal; tin and lead
metals).

## 4.1.2.4 Group 0: noble gases

- Unreactive; exist as single atoms; do not easily form molecules.
- Reason: **stable arrangement of electrons**: 8 outer electrons (helium 2).
- Boiling point **increases** down the group as relative atomic mass increases.

Data to recognise (°C): He −269, Ne −246, Ar −186, Kr −152, Xe −107, Rn −62.

**Method: predicting from a trend**

```
1. Decide the direction of the trend (up or down the group).
2. Your value must lie between the neighbours.
3. If there is only one neighbour, continue the pattern of the gaps.
```

## 4.1.2.5 Group 1: alkali metals

- One outer electron, lost to form **1+ ions**.
- Reactivity **increases** down the group.
- Explanation: more shells, outer electron further from nucleus and more shielded, weaker attraction, lost
  more easily.

| Reacts with | Product | Observations (Li → Na → K, each more vigorous) |
|---|---|---|
| Oxygen | Metal oxide, M₂O | Tarnish in air; burn red (Li), yellow-orange (Na), lilac (K) |
| Chlorine | Metal chloride, MCl (white solid) | Vigorous when heated |
| Water | Metal hydroxide + hydrogen | Float and fizz; Na melts into a ball; K ignites, lilac flame |

Equations (M = Li, Na or K):

```
4M + O₂ → 2M₂O
2M + Cl₂ → 2MCl
2M + 2H₂O → 2MOH + H₂
```

Solution after reaction with water: alkaline, universal indicator turns purple.

Melting points fall down the group (°C): Li 181, Na 98, K 63.

**Worked reminder: predict rubidium's melting point.**

```
Drops: Li -> Na = 181 - 98 = 83;  Na -> K = 98 - 63 = 35
The drops are shrinking, so the next drop is smaller still (say about 20)
Prediction: about 63 - 20 = 43 °C  (any value a little below 63 °C fits)
```

The measured value is 39 °C, so the prediction is reasonable. A prediction above 63 °C breaks the trend and
scores nothing.

**Predicting reactions:** caesium, near the bottom, reacts with water even more violently than potassium and
forms caesium hydroxide, CsOH, and hydrogen.

## 4.1.2.6 Group 7: halogens

- Non-metals; **diatomic** molecules; seven outer electrons.
- Down the group: relative molecular mass, melting point and boiling point **increase**; reactivity
  **decreases**.
- Explanation (reactivity): outer shell further from nucleus, so an extra electron is attracted less strongly
  and gained less easily.

| Halogen | Mr | State at room temperature | Colour |
|---|---|---|---|
| Chlorine, Cl₂ | 71 | Gas | Pale green |
| Bromine, Br₂ | 160 | Liquid | Red-brown |
| Iodine, I₂ | 254 | Solid | Grey-black |

**Compounds:**

| Reacts with | Type of compound | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Metal | Ionic; halide ion with 1− charge | Lithium + chlorine → lithium chloride, LiCl |
| Non-metal | Covalent molecule | Hydrogen + bromine → hydrogen bromide, HBr |

**Displacement rule:** more reactive halogen + salt of a less reactive halogen → swap. Otherwise no reaction.

Reminder (not the same pair as the study guide): bromine water added to sodium iodide solution:

```
Br₂ + 2NaI → 2NaBr + I₂     solution turns from orange to brown
```

**Higher tier only:** ionic equation Br₂ + 2I⁻ → 2Br⁻ + I₂ (sodium ions are spectators).

## Must-know distinctions

- **Group 1 vs Group 7 reactivity:** Group 1 increases down (losing an electron gets easier); Group 7
  decreases down (gaining an electron gets harder).
- **Melting point vs reactivity in Group 7:** melting point depends on forces between molecules; reactivity
  depends on attraction for an extra electron. Different reasons.
- **Chlorine vs chloride:** Cl₂ is the element; Cl⁻ is the ion in a compound.
- **Atomic number vs atomic weight:** the modern table uses atomic number; Mendeleev only had atomic weights.
- **Predicted vs discovered:** Mendeleev predicted elements; other chemists discovered them.

## Quick self-test

1. What is the order of elements in the modern periodic table based on?
2. An element is in period 2, Group 6. Give its electronic structure and name.
3. Give two things Mendeleev did that earlier tables did not.
4. Why is argon placed before potassium although its relative atomic mass is higher?
5. Define a metal in terms of ions.
6. Why do noble gases not easily form molecules?
7. Predict whether radon's boiling point is above or below that of xenon.
8. Name the products when potassium reacts with water. What colour is universal indicator in the solution?
9. Write a balanced equation for lithium reacting with chlorine.
10. What type of compound forms when iodine reacts with sodium? Name it.
11. Will iodine solution react with potassium bromide solution? Explain.
12. Calculate Mr of bromine, Br₂ (Ar Br = 80).

### Answers

1. Atomic (proton) number.
2. Two shells, six outer electrons: 2,6, which is oxygen.
3. Left gaps for undiscovered elements; changed the atomic-weight order where properties required it (also:
   predicted properties of missing elements).
4. Atomic number of argon (18) is less than potassium (19). Nearly all argon is argon-40, while most potassium
   is potassium-39, so argon's average mass is higher.
5. An element that reacts to form positive ions.
6. Their atoms already have a stable arrangement of electrons (full outer shell), so they do not need to share,
   gain or lose electrons.
7. Above (−62 °C compared with −107 °C): boiling point increases down the group.
8. Potassium hydroxide and hydrogen; purple.
9. 2Li + Cl₂ → 2LiCl.
10. Ionic; sodium iodide, NaI.
11. No. Iodine is less reactive than bromine, so it cannot displace bromine.
12. 2 × 80 = 160.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Saying "more shells" for the Group 1 trend and stopping. Link it to distance, weaker attraction and an
  electron lost more easily.
- Using the Group 1 explanation word for word for Group 7. Halogens **gain** an electron, so the argument is
  about attracting an incoming electron.
- Explaining halogen melting points with shielding. Melting point is about forces between molecules.
- Writing Na₂Cl or NaCl₂. Group 1 ions are 1+ and halide ions are 1−, so the formula is NaCl.
- Forgetting to balance the water reaction: it is 2M + 2H₂O → 2MOH + H₂, not M + H₂O → MOH + H₂.
- Describing a colour change for a halogen added to a salt of a **more** reactive halogen. There is no reaction.
- Stating the noble gases have "no outer electrons" or "an empty shell". The outer shell is full.
- Giving a predicted value outside the range of its neighbours on a trend question.
- Saying isotopes have different chemical properties when explaining Mendeleev's swaps. Only their masses
  differ.
