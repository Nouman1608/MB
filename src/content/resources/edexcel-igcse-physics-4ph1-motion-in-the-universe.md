This guide covers sub-topic **8(b) Motion in the universe** of the
Pearson Edexcel International GCSE in Physics (4PH1), Issue 4
specification: statements 8.2 to 8.6. All five are core content,
examinable on both papers. A shorter summary of the whole topic is in the
[Astrophysics study guide](/resources/edexcel-igcse-physics-astrophysics/),
and the unit conversions used below are practised in
[Units in astrophysics](/resources/edexcel-igcse-physics-4ph1-units-astrophysics/).

## What the specification asks for

- **8.2** know that the universe is a large collection of billions of galaxies; a galaxy is a large collection of billions of stars; our solar system is in the Milky Way galaxy
- **8.3** understand why gravitational field strength, g, varies and know that it is different on other planets and the Moon from that on the Earth
- **8.4** explain that gravitational force causes moons to orbit planets, planets to orbit the Sun, artificial satellites to orbit the Earth, and comets to orbit the Sun
- **8.5** describe the differences in the orbits of comets, moons and planets
- **8.6** use orbital speed = (2 × π × orbital radius) / time period, v = 2πr / T

## The scale of the universe (8.2)

Put the structures in order of size, smallest first:

1. **Planet**, such as Earth, orbiting a star.
2. **Solar system**: our Sun with its planets, moons, dwarf planets, asteroids and comets.
3. **Galaxy**: a collection of **billions of stars**, held together by gravity. Our solar system is one small part of the **Milky Way** galaxy.
4. **Universe**: a collection of **billions of galaxies**.

The words "billions" in both statements are deliberate: an answer that
says a galaxy is "a group of stars" or the universe is "lots of galaxies"
may not earn full credit.

## Why g varies (8.3)

Gravitational field strength g is the gravitational force on each
kilogram of mass, in N/kg. Every object with mass attracts every other.
The strength of the field at a planet's surface depends on two things:

- **the mass of the planet or moon**: more mass, stronger field, larger g
- **the distance from its centre**: further away, weaker field, smaller g

The Moon has much less mass than Earth, so g on its surface is much
smaller: about 1.6 N/kg compared with about 10 N/kg on Earth. Jupiter has
far more mass than Earth, and g at its cloud tops is about 25 N/kg, even
though its radius is much larger. g also falls with height above Earth:
it is a little smaller at the top of a mountain and much smaller at the
height of a distant satellite. It is never zero, which is why satellites
stay in orbit rather than drifting away.

So the same object has the **same mass** everywhere, but its **weight**
(W = m × g) is different on each planet and on the Moon.

## Gravity causes orbits (8.4)

An object moving in space with no force on it would travel in a straight
line at constant speed. To move in a circle, there must be a force
**towards the centre** of the circle. In every orbit, that force is
**gravitational attraction** towards the larger body:

| Orbiting body | Orbits | Force provided by |
|---|---|---|
| Moon | A planet (for example, Earth's Moon) | Gravity of the planet |
| Planet | The Sun | Gravity of the Sun |
| Artificial satellite | The Earth | Gravity of the Earth |
| Comet | The Sun | Gravity of the Sun |

In a circular orbit, gravity acts at right angles to the motion. It
changes the **direction** of the velocity continually but not the speed.
Because velocity is changing, the orbiting body is **accelerating**
towards the centre, even at a steady speed. If gravity suddenly
disappeared, the body would fly off in a straight line along the
direction it was moving at that instant.

## Comparing orbits (8.5)

| Feature | Planets | Moons | Comets |
|---|---|---|---|
| Orbit around | The Sun | A planet | The Sun |
| Shape | Nearly circular (slightly elliptical) | Nearly circular | Highly elliptical (very elongated) |
| Speed | Nearly constant | Nearly constant | Changes greatly: fastest near the Sun, slowest far away |
| Distance from what they orbit | Nearly constant | Nearly constant | Varies enormously, from close to the Sun to far beyond the outer planets |
| Time period | From months to many years | Days to months for most | Can be very long |

Explaining the comet's changing speed earns credit: as the comet falls
towards the Sun, gravity does work on it, so gravitational potential
energy is transferred to kinetic energy and it speeds up; moving away,
kinetic energy is transferred back and it slows down.

A further pattern follows from 8.3: the further a planet is from the
Sun, the weaker the Sun's gravitational field there, so the planet moves
more slowly and, with a longer path to travel, takes much longer to
complete an orbit.

## Orbital speed (8.6)

In one orbit the body travels the circumference of a circle, 2πr, in one
time period T, so:

```
v = 2πr / T
```

- r is the orbital radius in m, measured from the centre of the body being orbited.
- T is the time period in s.
- v is in m/s.

Rearranged: **T = 2πr / v** and **r = vT / 2π**.

## Worked examples

**Example 1.** The Moon orbits Earth at r = 3.84 × 10⁸ m with
T = 27.3 days. Calculate its orbital speed.

```
T = 27.3 × 86 400 = 2.36 × 10⁶ s
v = 2π × 3.84 × 10⁸ / 2.36 × 10⁶ = 1.02 × 10³ m/s (about 1 km/s)
```

**Example 2.** Mars orbits the Sun at r = 2.28 × 10¹¹ m with
T = 687 days. Calculate its orbital speed, and compare it with Earth's
(3.0 × 10⁴ m/s).

```
T = 687 × 86 400 = 5.94 × 10⁷ s
v = 2π × 2.28 × 10¹¹ / 5.94 × 10⁷ = 2.4 × 10⁴ m/s
```

Mars is further from the Sun and moves more slowly than Earth, as
expected from the weaker gravitational field at its distance.

**Example 3 (explain, 3 marks).** *Explain why a comet's speed is
greatest when it is closest to the Sun.*

Model answer: the comet's orbit is highly elliptical, so its distance
from the Sun changes [1]. As it moves towards the Sun, the Sun's
gravitational force speeds it up: gravitational potential energy is
transferred to kinetic energy [1]. Its kinetic energy, and so its speed,
is therefore greatest at the closest point [1].

## Common mistakes

- Putting the solar system and the galaxy in the wrong order of size.
- Saying g is "the same everywhere" or "zero in space".
- Explaining an orbit with "centrifugal force pushing outwards". The only force is gravity, inwards.
- Saying a body in a circular orbit is not accelerating because its speed is constant.
- Describing comet orbits as "oval" without saying they are far more elongated than planet orbits.
- Forgetting to convert days into seconds before using v = 2πr / T.

## Exam technique

- **Explain** (8.4): name the force (gravity), its direction (towards the centre) and its effect (changes direction, keeps the body in orbit).
- **Describe the differences** (8.5): make direct comparisons ("comet orbits are much more elliptical than planet orbits"), not separate lists.
- **Calculate** (8.6): write the equation, convert to m and s, substitute, give units.
- **State** (8.2): use the exact relationships: billions of stars in a galaxy, billions of galaxies in the universe.
