## Capital and revenue expenditure (1.1.11 and 1.1.12)

**Capital expenditure** buys, or improves the earning capacity of, a non-current asset. It is recorded in the statement of financial position at cost and then written off over the asset's life through depreciation. **Revenue expenditure** keeps the business running and the asset in its present condition. It is charged in full to the statement of profit or loss for the year.

The test is purpose, not size. Does the spending bring an asset into use or add to what it can do? Or does it only maintain or run something the business already has?

### Concepts behind the treatment

- **Accruals**: a cost that benefits several years is spread over those years through depreciation, so each year's profit carries its fair share.
- **Prudence**: when it is unclear whether spending creates an asset, profit and assets are not overstated, so the cost leans towards expense.
- **Materiality**: a trivial item, such as a cheap stapler, is treated as an expense even though it lasts for years, because tracking it would cost more than the information is worth.
- **Consistency**: the same policy is applied from year to year.

### Worked example: Ironbridge Print Works

Ironbridge Print Works made these payments during 2026.

| Item | Amount | Treatment | Reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| New digital press | 48,000 | Capital | Asset acquired |
| Delivery of the press | 1,200 | Capital | Cost of bringing it to its location |
| Concrete base and wiring for the press | 2,800 | Capital | Cost of making it ready for use |
| Upgrade kit raising press speed by 40% | 3,600 | Capital | Increases earning capacity |
| Conveyancing fees on buying the workshop | 2,100 | Capital | Part of the cost of the property |
| Wages of own staff who built storage racking | 2,400 | Capital | Labour used to create an asset |
| Annual service of the old machine | 900 | Revenue | Maintains existing condition |
| Replacement rollers on the old machine | 1,500 | Revenue | Repair, not improvement |
| Repainting the workshop exterior | 1,750 | Revenue | Maintenance |
| Staple cabinet | 35 | Revenue | Not material |

Capital items total 60,100 and revenue items total 4,185. Suppose the press costs (52,000 in total) were wrongly charged as expenses. Profit for the year would be understated by 52,000, and non-current assets would be understated by the same amount. If the repainting were wrongly capitalised, profit and non-current assets would be overstated by 1,750. Questions often ask for this effect, so state both the profit and the asset.

Where one invoice mixes a repair and an upgrade, split it and treat each part separately.

## Depreciation: causes and reasons (1.1.13 and 1.1.14)

**Depreciation** spreads the cost of a non-current asset, less any expected residual value, over its useful life. It is not a cash payment and it does not build up a fund to replace the asset.

Typical causes:

- **Wear and tear** from use and the passing of time.
- **Obsolescence**: new technology or changed demand makes the asset out of date before it wears out.
- **Passage of time** for assets with a fixed term, such as a lease.
- **Depletion** of assets that are used up, such as a quarry.
- **Inadequacy**: the business outgrows the asset.

Reasons for charging it:

- To apply **accruals**, matching the cost of the asset against the revenue it helps to earn.
- To report a profit that is fairer, since a purchase charged only in year 1 would make that year's profit too low and later years' too high.
- To show non-current assets at a more realistic carrying value, which is cost less accumulated depreciation.
- To apply **prudence**, so assets are not overstated.

The carrying value is not the market value, because depreciation allocates cost rather than measuring resale price.

## Methods of depreciation (1.1.15)

### Straight line

Annual charge = (cost − residual value) ÷ useful life. Many questions give a percentage of cost instead. For a part year, charge in proportion to the months of ownership unless the question states another policy.

### Reducing balance

A fixed percentage is applied to the carrying value at the start of each year, so the charge is highest early on and residual value is not used in the calculation. Round each year's charge to the nearest dollar unless the question says otherwise.

### Revaluation

Used for small items such as tools, where tracking each one is impractical. The asset is valued at the year end.
Depreciation = opening valuation + additions − closing valuation.

Ironbridge buys a printing press on 1 January 2026 for a total of 52,000. Residual value is 4,000 and the life is 5 years.

| Year | Straight line charge | Reducing balance 40% charge | Reducing balance carrying value |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2026 | 9,600 | 20,800 | 31,200 |
| 2027 | 9,600 | 12,480 | 18,720 |
| 2028 | 9,600 | 7,488 | 11,232 |
| 2029 | 9,600 | 4,493 | 6,739 |
| 2030 | 9,600 | 2,696 | 4,043 |
| Total | 48,000 | 47,957 | |

The 40% rate brings the carrying value close to the 4,000 residual value rather than exactly onto it, which is normal when the rate is rounded.

Ironbridge also keeps small tools on a revaluation basis. They were valued at 6,400 at 1 January 2026, 1,100 of tools were bought in the year, and they are valued at 5,200 at 31 December. Depreciation is 6,400 + 1,100 − 5,200 = 2,300.

## Effect on profit of the method (1.1.17)

Using the table above, reducing balance lowers profit in 2026 by 11,200 compared with straight line (20,800 − 9,600), and by 2,880 in 2027. In 2028, 2029 and 2030 it raises profit by 2,112, 5,107 and 6,904. Total depreciation over the life is almost the same, so the methods change when profit appears, not (broadly) how much. Reducing balance suits assets that lose usefulness fastest early on; straight line suits assets used evenly. Say which fits the asset described.

A higher depreciation charge never changes cash. It lowers profit for the year and the carrying value of the asset only.

## Change of method (1.1.18)

Suppose Ironbridge had used reducing balance at 40% and decided at the start of 2029 to move to straight line over the 2 years remaining, keeping the 4,000 residual value.

- Carrying value at 1 January 2029: 11,232.
- New annual charge: (11,232 − 4,000) ÷ 2 = 3,616.
- Charge under the old method would have been 4,493, so profit for 2029 is 877 higher.

The usual approach in questions is to apply the new method to the carrying value from the date of change, without altering earlier years' profits, unless the question says otherwise. A change in method departs from consistency, so give the reason and the effect on profit.

## Ledger accounts (1.1.16)

Ironbridge depreciates plant and equipment on a straight line basis. Its old machine cost 30,000, has been owned for four years, and is depreciated at 10% of cost per year with no residual value, so its provision stood at 12,000 on 1 January 2026. The press is added to the plant account on that date.

**Plant and equipment (cost)**

| 2026 | | Dr | 2026 | | Cr |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 Jan | Balance b/d | 30,000 | 31 Dec | Balance c/d | 82,000 |
| 1 Jan | Bank | 52,000 | | | |
| | | 82,000 | | | 82,000 |

**Provision for depreciation: plant and equipment**

| 2026 | | Dr | 2026 | | Cr |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 31 Dec | Balance c/d | 24,600 | 1 Jan | Balance b/d | 12,000 |
| | | | 31 Dec | Depreciation expense | 12,600 |
| | | 24,600 | | | 24,600 |

The charge is 3,000 for the old machine plus 9,600 for the press, which is 12,600. It is debited to the depreciation expense account and then transferred to the statement of profit or loss. The statement of financial position shows plant at cost 82,000 less provision 24,600, giving a carrying value of 57,400.

For a revaluation asset the entries are simpler. Ironbridge's tools account shows opening balance 6,400, bank 1,100 on the debit side, and depreciation 2,300 and closing valuation 5,200 on the credit side. There is no separate provision account.

## Disposal of a non-current asset (1.1.19)

Ironbridge sold a van on 31 August 2026 for 9,000. The van cost 21,000 on 1 March 2023, and is depreciated at 20% of cost per year, by months, with no residual value.

- Depreciation to 31 December 2023: 21,000 × 20% × 10/12 = 3,500.
- 2024 and 2025: 4,200 each, so the provision at 31 December 2025 is 11,900.
- 2026 to the date of sale: 21,000 × 20% × 8/12 = 2,800, so the provision at disposal is 14,700.
- Carrying value at disposal: 21,000 − 14,700 = 6,300.
- Profit on disposal: 9,000 − 6,300 = 2,700.

**Disposals account: van**

| | Dr | | Cr |
|---|---|---|---|
| Van (cost) | 21,000 | Provision for depreciation | 14,700 |
| Profit on disposal (to statement of profit or loss) | 2,700 | Bank | 9,000 |
| | 23,700 | | 23,700 |

If the proceeds had been below the carrying value, the balancing figure would be a loss on disposal, which is a debit in the statement of profit or loss. The depreciation for the months before the sale is still a charge for the year. The cost and provision accounts are cleared by the same entries.

## Common errors

- Capitalising repairs, or expensing delivery and installation costs of a new asset.
- Using residual value in a reducing balance calculation.
- Forgetting to remove the disposed asset's depreciation for earlier years from the provision.
- Charging a full year on a part-year asset, or none on an asset sold in the year.
- Saying depreciation provides cash for replacement.
