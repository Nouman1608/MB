This is a complete practice paper built to the structure the 9236 specification sets for Paper 1
(1 hour 30 minutes, 77 marks). **Section A** (28 marks) is on the compulsory topic *The Pakistan movement
(1857–1947)*; **Section B** (28 marks) is on the compulsory topic *Pakistan into the 21st Century: from
independence to 2022*; **Section C** (21 marks) is on the optional topic you have studied — **either**
*Islam in the subcontinent (c700–c1831)* **or** *The Mughal Empire (c1526–c1850s)*. The specification says
Sections A and B contain multiple-choice, short and extended response questions and Section C short and
extended response questions; the individual question tariffs used here (1, 2, 4, 6 and 9 marks) follow
the board's published specimen paper.

**Answer all of Question 1 (Section A) and all of Question 2 (Section B). In Section C answer either
Question 3 or Question 4, not both.**

## Answers

Multiple-choice questions score 1 mark. "State" and "Identify" questions score 1 mark per valid point.
"Identify and explain" questions score 1 mark for each point identified and 1 further mark for its
explanation. The 6-mark and 9-mark questions are marked by **levels of response**; the level tables are
set out after the answers. Indicative content is a guide, not a checklist — other valid points should also
be credited.

**1.** **(01.1)** **B: 1906** [1].

**(01.2)** **B: Chaudhry Rahmat Ali** [1]. (Allama Iqbal proposed a Muslim state in the north-west in his
Allahabad address of 1930, but did not coin the name.)

**(01.3)** Any two, 1 mark each [2]: the new province of Eastern Bengal and Assam had a Muslim majority;
Muslims would have more influence in government there; it offered better opportunities for Muslims in
education and employment; Dhaka, as a provincial capital, would develop; it freed eastern Bengal's Muslims
from the domination of Calcutta's Hindu landlords and professionals.

**(01.4)** **C: Lord Mountbatten** [1].

**(01.5)** Any two, 1 mark each [2]: India should have dominion status; separate electorates should end
and be replaced by joint electorates; seats should be reserved for Muslims only at the centre and in
provinces where they were a minority, not in Muslim-majority provinces; the central government should hold
the residuary powers.

**(01.6)** Any two, 1 mark each [2]: the provinces were given autonomy, with elected ministers responsible
for most provincial subjects; dyarchy was ended in the provinces; it proposed an all-India federation of
the provinces and the princely states (which never came into effect); separate electorates were kept;
Sindh was separated from Bombay and made a province; the right to vote was extended to many more people;
the Governor-General and governors kept special powers.

**(01.7)** Two ways, each 1 mark for identification and 1 for explanation [2 + 2 = 4]:
- he promoted modern education (1) — he founded the Muhammadan Anglo-Oriental College at Aligarh in 1875,
  teaching modern subjects alongside Islamic studies, which produced educated Muslims able to compete for
  government posts (1);
- he worked to rebuild trust between the British and Muslims (1) — writings such as *The Causes of the
  Indian Revolt* and *The Loyal Muhammadans of India* argued that Muslims were not the enemies of British
  rule, reducing discrimination against them (1);
- he developed the idea that Hindus and Muslims were separate nations (1) — this became the basis on which
  later leaders argued for separate Muslim political rights (1).

**(01.8)** Mark by the **Analysis (6)** table. Indicative content:
- the British held Muslims mainly responsible for the uprising, so Muslims lost positions in the army and
  government service and were distrusted for decades, which weakened them economically;
- the Mughal emperor Bahadur Shah Zafar was exiled, ending the last symbol of Muslim political authority
  in India;
- the East India Company was replaced by direct rule of the Crown, and English became more important in
  government, leaving Muslims who had avoided English education further behind;
- the weakness of the Muslim position led reformers such as Syed Ahmad Khan to seek a new approach based
  on reconciliation with the British and modern education, beginning the path towards Muslim political
  organisation.

**(01.9)** Mark by the **Evaluation (9)** table. Indicative content:
- *Disagree:* Jinnah began his political career as a member of the Indian National Congress and worked for
  Hindu–Muslim unity; he was called an "ambassador of Hindu–Muslim unity" and helped bring about the
  Lucknow Pact of 1916 between Congress and the League; even his Fourteen Points of 1929 sought safeguards
  for Muslims within a federal India rather than a separate state; in 1946 the League accepted the
  Cabinet Mission Plan, which would have kept India united.
- *Agree (in part):* from the late 1930s his position changed; the experience of Congress rule in the
  provinces in 1937–39 convinced him Muslims could not be protected in a Hindu-majority India; he led the
  League in adopting the Lahore Resolution of 1940 and campaigned for Pakistan in the 1945–46 elections.
- *Judgement:* for example, the demand for a separate state was a response to changing circumstances rather
  than a lifelong aim, so the statement is largely inaccurate.

**2.** **(02.1)** **D: the Eighteenth Amendment** [1].

**(02.2)** **A: Ehsaas** [1].

**(02.3)** Any two, 1 mark each [2]: sovereignty belongs to Allah alone; authority is to be exercised by
the people through their elected representatives as a sacred trust; the principles of democracy, freedom,
equality, tolerance and social justice as taught by Islam are to be observed; Muslims are to be enabled to
live according to the teachings of Islam; minorities are to be free to profess and practise their
religions; Pakistan is to be a federation; the independence of the judiciary is to be secured; fundamental
rights are to be guaranteed.

**(02.4)** **D: Pakistan Tehreek-e-Insaf** [1].

**(02.5)** Any two, 1 mark each [2]: a National Command and Operation Centre was set up to co-ordinate
the response; lockdowns were imposed, later replaced by targeted "smart lockdowns"; schools and
educational institutions were closed; emergency cash payments were made to poor families through the
Ehsaas programme; a relief package supported businesses and the construction industry; a national
vaccination campaign began in 2021.

**(02.6)** Any two, 1 mark each [2]: close military co-operation, including the joint production of the
JF-17 Thunder aircraft; large Chinese investment in infrastructure and energy through the China–Pakistan
Economic Corridor (CPEC); the development of Gwadar port; co-operation in agriculture; China's diplomatic
support for Pakistan; strong trade links.

**(02.7)** Two changes, each 1 mark for identification and 1 for explanation [2 + 2 = 4]:
- it removed the President's power to dissolve the National Assembly (1), restoring a parliamentary
  system in which the Prime Minister and parliament hold the main executive power (1);
- it abolished the Concurrent Legislative List (1), transferring many subjects to the provinces and
  increasing provincial autonomy (1);
- it renamed the North-West Frontier Province as Khyber Pakhtunkhwa (1), recognising the identity of the
  province's Pakhtun people (1);
- it made free and compulsory education a constitutional right (1) for children aged five to sixteen (1);
- it created a judicial commission to recommend appointments of judges (1), reducing the President's
  personal influence over the courts (1).

**(02.8)** Mark by the **Analysis (6)** table. Indicative content:
- Pakistan became a key ally of the USA and received large amounts of military and economic aid, for
  example under the Kerry–Lugar–Berman Act of 2009;
- militant violence spread into Pakistan: the Tehrik-i-Taliban Pakistan was formed in 2007, and attacks
  such as that on the Army Public School in Peshawar in December 2014 killed many civilians;
- the army launched major operations against militants, such as Zarb-e-Azb in North Waziristan from 2014,
  which displaced many people from the tribal areas;
- relations with the USA were damaged by US drone strikes and by the raid in Abbottabad that killed Osama
  bin Laden in May 2011;
- the conflict damaged the economy, discouraging investment and tourism, and Pakistan continued to host
  large numbers of Afghan refugees.

**(02.9)** Mark by the **Evaluation (9)** table. Indicative content:
- *Helped:* IMF loans helped Pakistan avoid defaulting on its debts during balance-of-payments crises,
  including a Stand-By Arrangement agreed in 2008 during the world economic crisis, an Extended Fund
  Facility in 2013 and another in 2019; an IMF programme reassured other lenders and investors; reforms
  linked to the programmes aimed to widen the tax base and strengthen the independence of the State Bank
  of Pakistan.
- *Harmed:* the conditions attached to the loans required cuts to energy subsidies and higher taxes, which
  raised electricity and fuel prices; devaluation of the rupee pushed up inflation and the cost of imports;
  spending cuts limited development; repeated programmes showed that the loans did not solve Pakistan's
  underlying economic problems and increased its debts.
- *Judgement:* for example, the IMF prevented short-term crises from becoming default, but did not by
  itself create lasting economic stability.

**3.** **(03.1)** Any two, 1 mark each [2]: it is the mystical, spiritual dimension of Islam; it seeks
closeness to Allah through inner purification and love; followers learn under a spiritual guide (pir or
shaikh); Sufis belong to orders (silsilas) such as the Chishti, Suhrawardi, Qadiri and Naqshbandi; worship
includes remembrance of Allah (dhikr); Sufi saints and their shrines helped spread Islam in the
subcontinent.

**(03.2)** Two ways, each 1 mark for identification and 1 for explanation [2 + 2 = 4]:
- it brought Sindh under Muslim rule as part of the Umayyad Caliphate (1), so Sindh became known as
  *Bab-ul-Islam*, the gateway of Islam into the subcontinent (1);
- it led to the spread of the faith (1) as Arab settlers, traders and scholars came to Sindh and some of
  the local people accepted Islam (1);
- non-Muslims were allowed to keep their religion on payment of jizya (1), which showed a tolerant form of
  Muslim rule and reduced resistance (1);
- mosques were built and Islamic learning was established (1), making Sindh a centre of Muslim culture (1).

**(03.3)** Mark by the **Analysis (6)** table. Indicative content:
- he opposed the religious ideas promoted at Akbar's court, such as Din-i-Ilahi, which he believed
  corrupted Islam, and called for a return to the Sharia;
- through his letters to nobles at the court of Jahangir he tried to influence the rulers of the empire;
  he was imprisoned by Jahangir but later released;
- he taught that Sufism must follow the Sharia, bringing the Naqshbandi order and Islamic law closer
  together;
- he became known as *Mujaddid Alf Thani*, the renewer of Islam in the second millennium, and his ideas
  influenced later reformers, including Shah Waliullah, and religious developments under Aurangzeb.

**(03.4)** Mark by the **Evaluation (9)** table. Answers must refer to both bullet points. Indicative
content:
- *Translation of the Qur'an:* Persian was the language of educated Muslims, so the translation allowed
  them to read and understand the Qur'an for themselves rather than relying on scholars; it was opposed by
  some ulama at the time but was widely read; his sons later translated the Qur'an into Urdu, extending
  the work to more people; it supported his aim of uniting Muslims around the Qur'an and Hadith.
- *Political change:* he saw that the decline of the Mughal Empire left Muslims weak and divided; he wrote
  to Muslim rulers and nobles, including Ahmad Shah Abdali, urging action against the Marathas; Abdali
  defeated the Marathas at Panipat in 1761, but he returned to Afghanistan and Mughal power was not
  restored, so the political aim was only partly achieved.
- *Judgement:* for example, the translation had the more lasting effect on the religious life of Muslims,
  while his political efforts brought only short-term results.

**4.** **(04.1)** Any two, 1 mark each [2]: he built the Taj Mahal at Agra; he built the Red Fort and the
new city of Shahjahanabad in Delhi; he built the Jama Masjid in Delhi; he laid out the Shalimar Gardens in
Lahore; he extended Mughal territory in the Deccan; his reign is seen as a high point of Mughal
architecture and wealth.

**(04.2)** Two ways, each 1 mark for identification and 1 for explanation [2 + 2 = 4]:
- he introduced the mansabdari system (1), giving officials ranks that fixed their pay and the troops they
  kept, so nobles depended on the emperor rather than on inherited power (1);
- he reformed land revenue with Raja Todar Mal (1), basing tax on the measured land and its average
  produce, which gave the state a regular income (1);
- he divided the empire into provinces (subahs) (1), each with governors and officials answerable to the
  centre, making the empire easier to control (1);
- his policy of religious tolerance, including ending the jizya (1), and his alliances with Rajput rulers
  won the loyalty of many Hindu subjects (1).

**(04.3)** Mark by the **Analysis (6)** table. Indicative content:
- Robert Clive's East India Company forces defeated Nawab Siraj-ud-Daula of Bengal, helped by the betrayal
  of his commander Mir Jafar, whom the Company then made Nawab;
- the Company gained control over Bengal, one of the richest provinces of the subcontinent, and its
  revenues paid for further expansion;
- it turned the Company from a trading company into a political and military power;
- it prepared the way for later British victories, such as at Buxar in 1764, and for British expansion
  across the subcontinent at the expense of Mughal authority.

**(04.4)** Mark by the **Evaluation (9)** table. Answers must refer to both bullet points. Indicative
content:
- *Deccan campaigns:* Aurangzeb spent the last decades of his reign fighting in the Deccan; he conquered
  Bijapur and Golconda, bringing the empire to its greatest size, but the wars cost huge sums and drained
  the treasury; the Marathas kept up their resistance; his long absence from the north weakened control
  there.
- *Opposition to religious policies:* his reimposition of the jizya in 1679 and other religious policies
  alienated many Hindu subjects; the Rajputs rebelled; the execution of the Sikh Guru Tegh Bahadur made the
  Sikhs lasting enemies; Jat and other rebellions grew.
- *Links and judgement:* the two were connected — Maratha resistance was partly fuelled by religious
  opposition, and the costs of war were made worse by rebellions elsewhere; for example, an answer might
  judge the Deccan campaigns more important because they exhausted the resources the empire needed to deal
  with every other problem.

## Approaching the 9-mark questions

The 9-mark questions reward sustained, evidence-based judgement. State a clear position early rather than
leaving the judgement to a final sentence; support it with specific, accurately dated evidence rather than
general narrative; weigh it against the alternative view the question invites (did Jinnah's position
genuinely change, or was it constant; did IMF support help more than it hurt); and finish with a
conclusion that follows from the evidence you have weighed. Where the question gives two bullet points,
you must deal with **both** to reach the top level.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Treating the multiple-choice and short questions as a place to rush: they are almost a third of the
  marks in Sections A and B.
- On "identify and explain" questions, naming two points without explaining either, which caps the answer
  at 2 of the 4 marks.
- On "analyse" questions, describing events without showing how or why they led to the consequence or
  outcome asked about.
- Attributing the name "Pakistan" to Iqbal instead of Rahmat Ali.
- Treating Jinnah's demand for partition as a constant position rather than a response to changing
  circumstances.
- Answering both Section C options, or answering from the option you have not studied.

## Timing

With 90 minutes for 77 marks, allow a little over a minute a mark: about 32 minutes each for Sections A
and B and about 24 minutes for Section C.
