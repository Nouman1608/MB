## Buying behaviour

**Buyer behaviour** is how consumers decide what to buy, from where and when.

- A **planned purchase** is decided before the shopper reaches the point of sale, often after research: a laptop, a holiday, a washing machine. The buyer compares price, reviews and features, so the business needs clear information, good reviews and competitive prices.
- An **impulse purchase** is unplanned and made on the spot: a chocolate bar at the till, a sale item. It depends on display, price promotions, mood and convenience. Retailers influence it with checkout displays, "limited time" offers and store layout.

A business's decisions affect behaviour. A high price may signal quality to some buyers and put others off. A website that is slow loses planned purchases. Placing sweets at child height encourages impulse buying. **Understanding** behaviour matters because it shows when and how to reach the customer, which lowers wasted spending and improves the chance of repeat purchase. Stakeholders such as shareholders benefit from higher sales, while consumers may be affected by pressure to buy, so ethical limits matter.

## Niche and mass marketing

**Mass marketing** sells one product to a large, broad market with the same marketing mix. **Niche marketing** targets a small, specific segment with a tailored product.

### Worked example 1: niche against mass

Marlow Chocolate is considering two approaches for a new range.

| | Niche: organic single-origin bars | Mass: standard milk bars |
|---|---|---|
| Customers | 3,000 | 400,000 target, 5% buy = 20,000 |
| Average yearly spend | £220 | £35 |
| Revenue | 3,000 × £220 = **£660,000** | 20,000 × £35 = **£700,000** |
| Margin before marketing | 35% = **£231,000** | 20% = **£140,000** |
| Marketing spend | £40,000 | £90,000 |
| Contribution after marketing | £231,000 − £40,000 = **£191,000** | £140,000 − £90,000 = **£50,000** |

**Interpretation.** The mass approach earns slightly more revenue (£700,000 against £660,000) but much less contribution because its margin is lower and its marketing costs are higher. Niche customers pay more and are loyal, so they cost less to reach.

**Evaluation.** Niche marketing gives higher margins and less competition, but it depends on a small group; losing 500 customers cuts revenue by about £110,000 (500 × £220). Mass marketing spreads risk and offers growth, but it faces large rivals and price competition. The choice depends on the size of the business, the strength of the brand and the resources: a small firm usually does better to dominate a niche first.

## Marketing models

Models describe the steps a consumer passes through between hearing of a product and buying it. They help a business to plan communication.

- **AIDA:** Attention, Interest, Desire, Action. The advertisement must first attract attention, then build interest, create desire and prompt action.
- **Hierarchy of effects:** a longer sequence: awareness, knowledge, liking, preference, conviction, purchase. It shows that buyers move gradually and that different promotion is needed at each step.
- **DAGMAR** (Defining Advertising Goals for Measured Advertising Results): sets measurable communication goals, moving the audience from unawareness to awareness, comprehension, conviction and action, with a number and a date for each step.
- **ATR** (Awareness, Trial, Reinforcement): a model in which advertising makes people aware, encourages a first purchase (trial), and then reinforces the habit so that regular buyers keep buying and feel the choice is right.

### Worked example 2: following a campaign through AIDA

A campaign for Marlow's organic bars costs **£15,000** and is seen by 100,000 people.

| Stage | Rate | People |
|---|---|---|
| Attention | all who see it | 100,000 |
| Interest | 12% of 100,000 | **12,000** |
| Desire | 25% of 12,000 | **3,000** |
| Action | 30% of 3,000 | **900** |

Overall conversion = 900 ÷ 100,000 = **0.9%**. Cost per purchase = £15,000 ÷ 900 = **£16.67**.

**Using the model.** The largest loss is between attention and interest: 88% of viewers do not engage. If a clearer message raised interest from 12% to 15%, interest would rise to 15,000 and purchases to 15,000 × 25% × 30% = **1,125**, lowering the cost per purchase to £15,000 ÷ 1,125 = **£13.33**. The model therefore points to where to spend effort.

**Usefulness and limits.** The models are simple, give a common framework and encourage measurable goals (especially DAGMAR). But real buyers do not always move in order: impulse buyers skip stages, and the models say little about social influence, price or competitors. They suit advertising planning better than predicting behaviour exactly.

## Orientation and customer service

A **customer-oriented** business starts with what customers want and designs the product to fit, using research and feedback. A **product-oriented** business starts with what it can make well, then looks for buyers.

| | Customer orientation | Product orientation |
|---|---|---|
| Starting point | Customer needs and research | Engineering skill, technology or tradition |
| Benefits | Higher satisfaction and loyalty; fewer failed launches | Can lead to breakthrough products customers did not know they wanted |
| Risks | Research cost; customers may not know future needs | Product may not match demand |

Neither is always right. A technology firm may be product-led in invention but must be customer-led in design and service. **Customer service** matters because it shapes whether a buyer returns: helpful staff, fast delivery and easy returns reduce risk for planned purchases, and a poor experience loses the sale and spreads through online reviews. Good service costs money in staff and training, so the benefit must be compared with the cost.

## Segmentation

**Market segmentation** divides a market into groups with similar needs. Ways to segment include:

- **demographic:** age, gender, income, family size;
- **geographic:** region, urban or rural, climate;
- **behavioural:** how often, how much and why people buy;
- **psychographic or lifestyle:** attitudes, values and interests;
- **socio-economic:** occupation and income group.

Benefits: a message and product tailored to each group; less wasted advertising; the chance to charge higher prices to groups that value the product. Drawbacks: research costs, more product versions to make, and the risk that segments are too small.

### Worked example 3: choosing which segment to target

Fernlea Footwear has researched three segments. Expected sales are the segment's total spending multiplied by the share Fernlea believes it can win.

| Segment | People | Yearly spend each | Total spend | Achievable share | Expected sales |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Teen trainers | 40,000 | £60 | £2,400,000 | 5% | **£120,000** |
| Commuter shoes | 90,000 | £85 | £7,650,000 | 2% | **£153,000** |
| Over-60 comfort | 55,000 | £110 | £6,050,000 | 6% | **£363,000** |

The three together would give £636,000. The over-60 segment is not the largest in people, but it spends most each and faces the least competition, so it gives the highest expected sales (about 57% of the total, 363,000 ÷ 636,000). **Recommendation:** make over-60 comfort the main target, with commuter shoes second because of the segment's size. **Justification and risk:** the shares are estimates; if Fernlea could win only 2% of over-60s, expected sales would be £121,000, which would put commuter shoes ahead. The company should test the segment with research before committing.

## Common mistakes

- Describing the models without applying them to a campaign in the case.
- Treating niche marketing as "small business only".
- Saying customer orientation is always better.
- Listing segmentation bases without choosing one and explaining why.
- Using total market size alone to pick a segment, ignoring spend, share and competition.

## Exam technique

For "explain", define and give an example. For "evaluate usefulness", give the benefit and the limit of the model or method. For "recommend and justify a segmentation strategy", name the segment or segments, use numbers from the case and state a risk.
