## What makes each solid what it is

| Solid | Defining property | Other properties worth knowing |
|---|---|---|
| Cube | 6 congruent square faces | 3 pairs of parallel faces; 12 equal edges |
| Cuboid | 6 rectangular faces | Opposite faces congruent and parallel |
| Prism | The same cross-section all the way through; two congruent, parallel end faces joined by rectangles | Named after its cross-section; the edges joining the ends are parallel and equal |
| Cylinder | A prism-like solid with a circular cross-section | 2 flat circular faces, 1 curved surface |
| Pyramid | A polygon base with triangular faces meeting at one vertex, the apex | Named after its base |
| Cone | A circular base and a curved surface meeting at an apex | 1 flat face, 1 curved surface, 1 vertex |
| Sphere | Every point on the surface is the same distance from the centre | 1 curved surface; no edges or vertices |

A cube is a special cuboid, and a cuboid is a special prism (its cross-section is a rectangle). A cylinder behaves like a prism with a circular cross-section, and a cone like a pyramid with a circular base, but because they have curved surfaces they are named separately.

**Worked example 1 (counting faces).** How many faces of a regular hexagonal prism (cross-section a regular hexagon) are rectangles, and how many pairs of parallel faces does it have?

```
cross-section: hexagon, 6 sides
end faces: 2 hexagons (parallel to each other)
side faces: one rectangle for each side of the hexagon, 6 rectangles
a regular hexagon has 3 pairs of parallel sides,
so the rectangles form 3 parallel pairs
pairs of parallel faces = 1 (the ends) + 3 = 4
```

### Identifying a solid from its counts

For a prism with an n-sided cross-section: n + 2 faces, 3n edges, 2n vertices. For a pyramid with an n-sided base: n + 1 faces, 2n edges, n + 1 vertices.

**Worked example 2.** A solid has 9 faces. Its faces are polygons. Which prism and which pyramid have 9 faces, and how could edges tell them apart?

```
prism:   n + 2 = 9, so n = 7   heptagonal prism: 21 edges, 14 vertices
pyramid: n + 1 = 9, so n = 8   octagonal pyramid: 16 edges, 9 vertices
```

The face count alone cannot decide; the number of edges or vertices does.

## Plans and elevations

- **Plan:** the view from directly above.
- **Front elevation:** the view from the front.
- **Side elevation:** the view from one side (a question says which).

Each view is a flat 2D drawing. You see every edge as a line, including edges where the solid changes height or depth. Edges hidden behind the solid may be shown as dashed lines if a question asks for them.

**Worked example 3 (views to scale).** Draw the plan, front elevation and side elevation of a cuboid 4 cm long, 3 cm deep and 2 cm high.

```
plan (from above):        rectangle 4 cm by 3 cm
front elevation:          rectangle 4 cm by 2 cm
side elevation:           rectangle 3 cm by 2 cm
```

Each view uses two of the three dimensions. The length appears in both the plan and the front elevation; the height appears in both elevations. Matching lengths between views is a quick check.

**Worked example 4 (an L-shaped prism).** A prism is made of centimetre cubes. Its front face is an L shape: a bottom row 3 cubes wide, with two more cubes stacked on the left-hand cube to make a column 3 cubes tall. The prism is 2 cubes deep. Describe the three views and find the number of cubes.

```
front elevation:  the L shape: 3 wide, 3 tall on the left, 1 tall on the right

plan:             a rectangle 3 wide and 2 deep, with a line 1 cm from
                  the left edge where the top of the tall column meets
                  the lower part

side elevation    a rectangle 2 wide and 3 tall, with a horizontal line
(from the right): at height 1 cm: below it is the end of the low part,
                  above it is the end of the tall column, set further back

cubes:            cross-section 3 + 2 = 5 cubes, depth 2, so 10 cubes
```

The plan and side elevation are rectangles with extra lines. Forgetting those lines is the most common way to lose the mark: they show the edges where the height changes.

### Stacks of cubes

A plan may show the number of cubes in each stack.

**Worked example 5.** A solid is built from cubes. Looking from above, with the front at the bottom of the page, the stack heights are: back row 2, 1; front row 3, 1. Describe the front elevation and the side elevation from the right, and count the cubes.

```
front elevation: each column shows its tallest stack
  left column:  max(2, 3) = 3     right column: max(1, 1) = 1
  view: columns of height 3 and 1

side elevation from the right: each row shows its tallest stack
  front row: max(3, 1) = 3        back row: max(2, 1) = 2
  looking from the right, the front is on your left
  view: columns of height 3 (left) and 2 (right)

cubes: 2 + 1 + 3 + 1 = 7
```

### From views back to the solid

| Plan | Front elevation | Side elevation | Solid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Circle | Rectangle | Rectangle | Cylinder standing on its end |
| Circle with a dot at the centre | Triangle | Triangle | Cone standing on its base |
| Square with both diagonals | Triangle | Triangle | Square-based pyramid |
| Rectangle | Triangle | Rectangle | Triangular prism with a triangular end facing you |
| Circle | Circle | Circle | Sphere |
| Rectangle | Rectangle | Rectangle | Cuboid |

The dot in the cone's plan and the diagonals in the pyramid's plan are the edges and apex seen from above. In the pyramid's plan, the four sloping edges run from the corners of the base up to the apex, so they appear as lines from the corners to the centre.

## Isometric drawings

Isometric paper has dots arranged so that lines can be drawn in three directions: vertical, and two directions each at 30° to the horizontal. Use the paper the right way round, with the dots forming vertical columns.

Conventions:

- Vertical edges of the solid are drawn vertically.
- Edges running left to right and front to back are drawn along the two sloping directions.
- No line is drawn horizontally.
- Lengths are drawn true to scale along all three directions: a 3 cm edge is 3 dot-spaces long.
- Only visible edges are drawn.

**Method: from plan and elevations to an isometric drawing.** Read the length, depth and height from the views, mark the nearest bottom corner, draw the three edges from it, complete the faces you can see, then add any steps or cut-outs shown by the extra lines in the views. For the L-shaped prism, draw the L-shaped front face first along one sloping direction and the vertical, then slide it back 2 units along the other sloping direction and join the corners.

## Common mistakes

- Calling a pyramid a prism, or naming a prism after a side face instead of its cross-section.
- Assuming the number of faces identifies a solid on its own.
- Drawing a view in perspective instead of as a flat 2D shape.
- Leaving out internal lines in a plan or elevation where the solid changes height.
- Mixing up which side is on the left in a side elevation.
- Drawing horizontal lines on isometric paper, or using it sideways.
- Giving views with dimensions that do not match each other.

## Exam technique for OCR command words

- **Draw the plan** or **Draw the front elevation**: draw on the grid to the given scale, using a ruler, and include every visible edge.
- **Write down the name of the solid**: one precise name, such as "triangular prism", not just "prism".
- **Draw on the isometric grid**: follow the three directions; the examiner checks lengths and visible edges.
- **Work out the number of cubes** or **faces, edges, vertices**: show the counting method, for example n + 2 faces for a prism.
