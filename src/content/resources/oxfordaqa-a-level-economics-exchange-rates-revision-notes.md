## Definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Exchange rate | The value of one currency measured in units of another |
| Appreciation | Rise in a currency's value under a floating system, caused by market forces |
| Depreciation | Fall in value under a floating system, caused by market forces |
| Revaluation | Deliberate rise in the official rate (fixed or managed system) |
| Devaluation | Deliberate fall in the official rate (fixed or managed system) |
| Free float | Rate set by demand and supply with no intervention |
| Fixed rate | Rate pegged at a set value and defended by the central bank |
| Managed float | Market sets the rate, but the central bank intervenes to smooth or steer it |
| Reserves | Foreign currency and other assets the central bank can use to intervene |
| Speculation | Trading a currency to profit from an expected price change |
| Herding behaviour | Traders copying each other instead of judging fundamentals |

## Percentage changes in a rate

Percentage change = (new rate - old rate) / old rate × 100.

Reminder: 1 ar = 2.00 bel falls to 1 ar = 1.50 bel. The ar changes by (1.50 - 2.00) / 2.00 = -25%. The bel was worth 0.50 ar and is now worth 1 / 1.50 = 0.667 ar, a rise of 33.3%. A fall of 25% in one currency is a rise of 33.3% in the other, because the bases differ.

## Determination under a free float

| Demand for the home currency comes from | Supply of the home currency comes from |
|---|---|
| Foreign buyers of exports | Residents buying imports |
| Foreign tourists | Residents travelling abroad |
| Inflows of FDI and portfolio investment | Outflows of FDI and portfolio investment |
| Traders buying in expectation of a rise | Traders selling in expectation of a fall |

Diagram in words: the vertical axis shows the exchange rate (units of foreign currency per unit of home currency). Horizontal axis, quantity of home currency. Demand slopes down, supply slopes up, and they meet at the equilibrium rate.

Method in steps:

1. Identify whether demand or supply has shifted, and why.
2. State the direction of the shift.
3. State the new equilibrium: a higher or lower rate, and a larger or smaller quantity traded.
4. Name the result: appreciation or depreciation.

Worked reminder: the market for Country C's currency, the mer, is measured in kel per mer.

| Rate (kel per mer) | 0.8 | 1.0 | 1.2 | 1.4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Demand (m mer) | 60 | 50 | 40 | 30 |
| Supply (m mer) | 20 | 30 | 40 | 50 |

Equilibrium is 1.2, with 40 million mer traded. If higher interest rates add 20 million to demand at every rate, new demand is 80, 70, 60, 50. It now equals supply at 1.4. The mer has appreciated by (1.4 - 1.2) / 1.2 = 16.7%.

## Causes of changes (shift and effect)

| Cause | Curve that shifts | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Home interest rates rise relative to abroad | Demand right | Appreciation |
| Domestic inflation higher than abroad | Demand left, supply right | Depreciation |
| Faster domestic income growth | Supply right (more imports) | Depreciation |
| Higher productivity or quality | Demand right | Appreciation |
| FDI inflow rises | Demand right | Appreciation |
| Loss of confidence | Demand left, supply right | Depreciation |
| Speculators expect a rise | Demand right, supply left | Appreciation, possibly beyond fundamentals |

**Speculation and herding.** Expectation causes action, action moves the rate, and the move confirms the expectation. Herding makes this stronger: rates overshoot, then fall back sharply. Worked reminder: spend 800,000 bel on ar at 2.00 and you hold 400,000 ar. Sell at 2.10 and you receive 840,000 bel, a 5% gain.

## Government intervention

| Method | Action | Effect on the rate |
|---|---|---|
| Buy the currency | Sell reserves of foreign currency, buy home currency | Demand rises, rate rises |
| Sell the currency | Sell home currency, add to reserves | Supply rises, rate falls |
| Raise interest rates | Raises returns on home assets | Demand rises, rate rises |
| Cut interest rates | Lowers returns on home assets | Demand falls, rate falls |

Cost of defending a rate = excess supply of the currency × the target rate. If excess supply is 25 million ar at 2.00 bel per ar, the bank uses 50 million bel of reserves. Divide reserves by this figure to see how long it can last.

Limits: finite reserves, large speculative flows, conflict with domestic goals when interest rates are used, and little help against a lasting imbalance.

## Exchange rate systems

| | Free float | Fixed | Managed float |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rate set by | Market | Government, defended by intervention | Market, with intervention |
| Reserves needed | Small | Large | Moderate |
| Stability | Low | High | Moderate |
| Interest rates | Free for domestic goals | Tied to the peg | Partly constrained |
| Adjusts to shocks | Automatically | Only by devaluation or revaluation | Partly |

Evaluation: the best system depends on trade patterns, size of reserves, credibility of the authorities and how exposed the economy is to shocks.

## Effects of a change in the rate

| | Depreciation | Appreciation |
|---|---|---|
| Price paid by foreign buyers of exports | Fall | Rise |
| Price paid by home buyers of imports | Rise | Fall |
| Exporters | Gain competitiveness | Lose competitiveness |
| Firms with imported inputs | Costs rise | Costs fall |
| Consumers | Dearer imports and foreign holidays | Cheaper imports and holidays |
| Borrowers in foreign currency | Debt burden rises | Debt burden falls |
| Inflation | Tends to rise | Tends to fall |
| AD and growth | Rise if net exports rise | Fall if net exports fall |

**Elasticity condition.** After a depreciation, the trade balance improves only if the rise in export earnings exceeds the rise in import spending. High price elasticities of demand for exports and imports make that likely. Low elasticities, which are common in the short run, can worsen the balance.

Worked reminder: ar falls 10%, so exports priced in bel fall 10%. With PED of -0.6, quantity rises 6%. The bel value of exports is 0.90 × 1.06 = 0.954 of the old value, a fall of 4.6%. The ar value, with ar prices held, rises 6%.

Cost pass-through reminder: a firm with 25% of costs imported faces a 12% rise in import prices. Total costs rise 0.25 × 12 = 3%.

## Evaluating a change in the exchange rate

Build each judgement as a chain, then qualify it.

1. A depreciation makes exports cheaper for foreigners and imports dearer for residents.
2. With elastic demand, export volumes rise and import volumes fall, so net exports and AD rise.
3. With spare capacity, output and employment rise. Near full capacity, prices rise instead.
4. Dearer imported inputs raise costs and inflation, and the gain may fade if wages and prices follow.
5. Judge: the effect depends on elasticities, time, spare capacity and how far firms pass the change on.

## The IMF

- Promotes international monetary cooperation and exchange rate stability.
- Lends to members facing balance of payments difficulties, usually with conditions.
- Monitors members' economies and advises on policy.
- Provides technical assistance.
- Criticism: conditions such as spending cuts may slow growth.

## Must-know distinctions

- Appreciation and depreciation are market-driven. Revaluation and devaluation are official decisions.
- A fall in the exchange rate does not mean an improvement in the trade balance.
- Defending a rate with reserves is different from defending it with interest rates.
- A weaker currency helps exporters and hurts importers.

## Quick self-test

1. Define appreciation.
2. 1 ar = 2.00 bel falls to 1.50. By what percentage does the ar change?
3. A good costs 3,000 ar. What is its price in bel at 2.00 and at 1.50?
4. A 900-bel import: find its ar price at 2.00 and at 1.50, and the percentage rise.
5. Give two causes of a rise in demand for a currency.
6. Why can a central bank not defend a rate indefinitely using reserves?
7. State the difference between devaluation and depreciation.
8. Export PED is -0.6 and the foreign price falls 10%. Find the change in quantity and in foreign-currency revenue.
9. Name one role of the IMF.
10. A firm has 25% of its costs imported and import prices rise 12%. By how much do its costs rise?

### Answers

1. A rise in the value of a currency under a floating system, caused by market forces.
2. -25%.
3. 6,000 bel at 2.00; 4,500 bel at 1.50.
4. 450 ar at 2.00; 600 ar at 1.50; a rise of 33.3%.
5. For example higher interest rates, a rise in export demand, or an inflow of FDI.
6. Its reserves are finite and run out.
7. Devaluation is a deliberate official fall under a fixed or managed system; depreciation is a market-driven fall under a floating system.
8. Quantity rises 6%. Foreign-currency revenue changes by 0.90 × 1.06 = 0.954, so falls 4.6%.
9. For example lending to members with balance of payments problems, or monitoring economies.
10. 0.25 × 12 = 3%.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Mixing up appreciation with revaluation, or depreciation with devaluation.
- Reading a percentage change from the wrong base.
- Drawing the diagram the wrong way round, with the exchange rate on the wrong axis or the wrong currency named.
- Moving the curve without saying which one shifts and why.
- Asserting that depreciation raises exports and improves the balance with no elasticity.
- Forgetting that import prices rise after depreciation.
- Treating intervention as free and without limits.
- Ending without a judgement on size, time lags or spare capacity.
