## Section A

**1.** Define primary socialisation and secondary socialisation, giving one typical agency for each. **[4]**

**2.** Define re-socialisation and inadequate socialisation, giving one example trigger for re-socialisation. **[4]**

## Section B

**3.** Explain, using a specific mechanism for each, how **two** agencies of socialisation other than the family transmit norms and values. **[6]**

**4.** A student writes: "Socialisation happens mainly through the family." Explain why this statement is too narrow, referring to secondary socialisation and at least **one** other agency. **[6]**

**5.** Compare the functionalist and Marxist views of socialisation, giving an explicit point of contrast between them. **[8]**

**6.** Explain the feminist perspective on socialisation, giving one specific example of how an agency reinforces gender roles. **[5]**

**7.** Explain the social action/interactionist perspective on socialisation, and explain the key difference between this perspective and the functionalist view. **[6]**

**8.** Explain what is meant by the "social construction of identity", giving one example of how socialisation shapes it. **[5]**

**9.** Explain why inadequate socialisation might be linked to deviant behaviour in later life, according to at least **one** sociological perspective. **[4]**

## Answers

**1. Primary socialisation** — the **early learning of norms and values, mainly within the family** [1] [1]. **Secondary socialisation** — **later learning through wider agencies**, such as school, peers, media or the workplace [1] [1].

**2. Re-socialisation** — learning **new norms and values that replace previously learned ones**, often following a major life change, for example joining the military, migrating to a new country, or imprisonment [1] [1]. **Inadequate socialisation** — socialisation that has **failed to properly instil a society's shared norms and values** [1] [1].

**3.** Any two, 3 marks each: **school** — through the **formal curriculum**, which explicitly teaches knowledge and values, and the **"hidden curriculum"**, the implicit rules and expectations (such as punctuality, hierarchy and obedience) taught through the way school itself is organised [1] [1] [1]. **Media** — through **modelling behaviours and norms** in the content it presents, showing audiences examples of how people are expected to look, behave and relate to others [1] [1] [1]. **Peers** — through **informal approval and disapproval** within a friendship group, rewarding conformity to group norms and discouraging deviation from them [1] [1] [1].

**4.** The statement is too narrow because **secondary socialisation continues throughout life** through agencies beyond the family, so socialisation is not confined to the early, family-based stage [1] [1]. For example, **schools** transmit norms and values through both their formal curriculum and hidden curriculum [1] [1], and **media** shapes identity and behaviour through the images and role models it presents to audiences well into adulthood [1] [1].

**5.** **Functionalist:** socialisation instils **shared norms and values that create value consensus**, allowing society to function smoothly; agencies such as the family and school are seen as performing a **positive, necessary function** for social cohesion [1] [1] [1]. **Marxist:** socialisation is used by the **ruling class to instil values that benefit capitalism**, such as obedience and acceptance of inequality, preparing individuals to accept their position in the workforce; agencies such as schools are seen as **reproducing class inequality** rather than serving everyone equally [1] [1] [1]. **Point of contrast:** functionalism presents socialisation as **broadly beneficial and consensual**, while Marxism presents it as **serving the interests of one social class over another** [2].

**6.** The feminist perspective argues that socialisation **reproduces gender roles and inequality** [1] [2]. Example: **family and media agencies model different behavioural expectations for boys and girls** — for instance, media content that presents narrower or more appearance-focused role models for girls than for boys — which **reinforces a gendered division of labour and identity** as children grow up [2].

**7.** The social action/interactionist perspective argues that individuals are **not simply passive recipients of socialisation** — they **actively interpret, negotiate and sometimes resist** the norms and values presented to them by different agencies [1] [2]. **Key difference from functionalism:** functionalism treats socialisation as a broadly **structural process that successfully instils shared values in everyone**, whereas social action theory emphasises **individual agency and interpretation**, allowing for the possibility that a person rejects or reshapes the norms an agency presents to them [3].

**8.** The social construction of identity is the idea that a **person's sense of who they are is not fixed or natural, but built up over time** through their interactions with socialising agencies [2] [1]. Example: a child's sense of gender identity is **shaped gradually through family, school and media agencies modelling particular expectations of how boys and girls should behave**, rather than being something the child is simply born already knowing [2].

**9.** Some perspectives link **inadequate socialisation** — where shared norms and values have not been properly instilled — to a **weaker internalisation of the behavioural expectations that normally restrain antisocial or deviant behaviour** [2] [1]. A functionalist-influenced explanation would argue that without effective socialisation into shared values, an individual **lacks the value consensus that binds most members of society to conventional behaviour**, making deviance more likely [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Confusing primary and secondary socialisation, or omitting re-socialisation and inadequate socialisation as distinct concepts in their own right.
- Naming an agency of socialisation without explaining the specific mechanism by which it socialises individuals.
- Describing a perspective's view of socialisation in vague terms ("Marxists think it's about class") rather than explaining the specific mechanism the perspective identifies.
- Comparing two perspectives without an explicit point of contrast, rather than just placing two descriptions side by side.
- Treating identity as fixed or natural rather than explaining it as something built through interaction with socialising agencies.
