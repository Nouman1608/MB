Revise first with the [1.2 discharge relationships study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-discharge-relationships-within-drainage-basins/) and the [1.2 revision notes](/resources/a-level-geography-9696-discharge-relationships-revision-notes/).

## Section A

**1.** Define the term "lag time". **[2]**

**2.** Distinguish between the porosity and the permeability of a soil. **[2]**

**3.** Identify **two** components of an annual hydrograph. **[2]**

**4.** Explain why a heavy snowfall may produce no immediate rise in river discharge. **[3]**

**5.** A fictional basin has a total stream length of 126 km and an area of 90 km².

**(a)** Calculate its drainage density. Show your working. **[2]**

**(b)** Suggest what a high drainage density indicates about the rock or soil of a basin. **[2]**

**6.** Explain how antecedent moisture affects peak discharge. **[3]**

## Section B

**7.** The table shows rainfall and discharge on the fictional River Tallow during a storm. Peak rainfall (12 mm in one hour) occurred at 03:00.

| Time | 00:00 | 03:00 | 06:00 | 09:00 | 12:00 | 15:00 | 18:00 | 21:00 | 24:00 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Discharge (cumecs) | 5 | 5 | 9 | 21 | 38 | 31 | 20 | 13 | 9 |

**(a)** State the peak discharge and calculate the lag time. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the increase from the pre-storm discharge to the peak as a percentage. Show your working. **[2]**

**(c)** Compare the rates of change on the rising and falling limbs. **[2]**

**(d)** Suggest why discharge at 24:00 is still higher than before the storm. **[2]**

**8.** The table shows two neighbouring fictional basins that received the same storm.

| Characteristic | Basin P | Basin R |
|---|---|---|
| Area (km²) | 40 | 45 |
| Shape | Nearly circular | Elongated |
| Rock | Shale | Sandstone |
| Main land use | Grazed pasture | Woodland |
| Drainage density (km per km²) | 2.1 | 0.8 |
| Lag time (hours) | 3 | 10 |
| Peak discharge (cumecs) | 55 | 18 |

Explain the differences between the hydrograph responses of the two basins. **[6]**

**9.** The table shows mean monthly discharge for a fictional river in a tropical climate with a wet season from June to September and a hot dry season from March to May.

| Month | Jan | Mar | May | Jul | Sep | Nov |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mean discharge (cumecs) | 12 | 8 | 10 | 85 | 70 | 25 |

**(a)** Identify the period of high flow and calculate the range in mean discharge. **[3]**

**(b)** Explain how temperature and evapotranspiration contribute to the low flows from March to May. **[3]**

**10.** Explain how temperature can both increase and decrease river discharge. **[5]**

**11.** "Drainage basin characteristics are more important than climate in determining the shape of a storm hydrograph." Discuss. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** The time between peak rainfall [1] and peak discharge [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Porosity is the proportion of the soil made up of pore spaces that can hold water [1]. Permeability is the ability of the soil to let water pass through it, which depends on how large and connected the pores are [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Any two of: the high-flow season [1]; the low-flow season or baseflow period; individual flood peaks; the timing of the highest or lowest flows [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Snow is stored on the ground surface instead of infiltrating or running off [1]. Water is released only when temperatures rise above freezing and the snow melts [1], so the river responds later, possibly days or weeks after the snowfall [1]. **[3]**

**5. (a)** 126 / 90 [1] = 1.4 km per km² [1].

**(b)** The rock or soil is likely to be impermeable [1], so water cannot soak away and instead flows over the surface, cutting many channels [1]. **[4]**

**6.** If the soil is already wet from earlier rain, it has little spare storage capacity [1]. More rain therefore cannot infiltrate and becomes saturation-excess overland flow [1], which reaches the channel quickly and raises peak discharge [1]. **[3]**

**7. (a)** Peak discharge is 38 cumecs at 12:00 [1]; lag time = 12:00 - 03:00 = 9 hours [1].

**(b)** (38 - 5) / 5 x 100 [1] = 660% increase [1].

**(c)** The rising limb rises by 33 cumecs in 9 hours, about 3.7 cumecs per hour [1], whereas the falling limb drops 29 cumecs in 12 hours, about 2.4 cumecs per hour, so the rising limb is steeper [1].

**(d)** Slower throughflow from the soil continues to reach the channel after overland flow has stopped [1]. Some rain percolated into groundwater, raising the water table and so increasing baseflow [1]. **[8]**

**8.** Basin P has a shorter lag (3 against 10 hours) and a peak three times higher (55 against 18 cumecs) [1]. Shale is impermeable, so rain cannot percolate and runs off as overland flow, whereas sandstone lets water percolate and reach the river slowly as baseflow [1]. Basin P's higher drainage density means water has a shorter distance to travel before reaching a channel [1]. Its circular shape means water from all parts arrives at the outlet together, while basin R's elongated shape spreads arrival over time [1]. Grazed pasture gives little interception and may be compacted by animals, reducing infiltration [1], while woodland in basin R intercepts rain, increases evapotranspiration and raises infiltration [1]. **[6]**

**9. (a)** High flow occurs in the wet season, from about July to September [1]. Range = 85 - 8 [1] = 77 cumecs [1].

**(b)** In the hot dry season temperatures are high, so evaporation from soil and water surfaces is high [1]. Plants transpire water from the soil, so evapotranspiration removes much of the little water available [1]. Little water percolates to recharge groundwater, so the river depends on a declining baseflow and discharge reaches its lowest in March [1]. **[6]**

**10.** High temperatures increase evaporation and transpiration [1], removing water before it reaches the channel and so reducing discharge [1]. Rising temperatures can also melt stored snow and ice [1], suddenly releasing large volumes of water and increasing discharge [1]. Temperatures below freezing can freeze the ground surface, making it impermeable so that rain or meltwater runs off and discharge rises quickly [1]. **[5]**

**11.** For the view: basin characteristics are fixed or slow to change and act on every storm, so they set the basin's typical response [1]. Rock type and permeability decide whether water runs off or is stored as groundwater [1]. Drainage density and basin shape control how quickly water reaches the outlet [1]. Land use, such as urban surfaces or woodland, can change lag time and peak greatly [1].

Against the view: rainfall intensity decides whether infiltration capacity is exceeded, so the same basin can be flashy in an intense storm and subdued in light rain [1]. Antecedent moisture means the same storm gives very different peaks after wet and dry weeks [1]. Precipitation type matters, since snow may delay the response by weeks [1]. Temperature controls evapotranspiration and can freeze the ground [1].

Judgement: basin characteristics determine the general shape of a basin's hydrograph compared with other basins [1], but climate determines how one storm differs from another in the same basin, so the two interact and neither works alone [1]. **[10]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Measuring lag time from the start of rainfall instead of from peak rainfall.
- Giving a percentage change with the wrong base, such as dividing by the peak instead of the starting value.
- Comparing two basins with separate descriptions instead of comparative statements.
- Saying that a larger basin is always flashier.
- Using the word "porous" when the point being made is about permeability.
