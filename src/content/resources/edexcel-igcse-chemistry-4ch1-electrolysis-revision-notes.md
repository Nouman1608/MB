## Conduction (1.55C, 1.56C)

To conduct, a substance needs **charged particles that are free to move**.

| Substance | State | Conducts? | Reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| Covalent compound | Any (solid, liquid, solution) | No | No ions; electrons held in bonds, none free to move |
| Ionic compound | Solid | No | Ions held in fixed positions in the lattice |
| Ionic compound | Molten | Yes | Lattice broken down; ions free to move |
| Ionic compound | Aqueous solution | Yes | Ions separated by water; free to move |

In the electrolyte the **ions** move. In the wires, **electrons** move.

## Key terms (1.57C)

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Electrolysis | Breakdown of an ionic compound, molten or in solution, by a direct current |
| Electrolyte | The molten or dissolved ionic compound that conducts and is broken down |
| Cation | Positive ion; moves to the cathode |
| Anion | Negative ion; moves to the anode |
| Cathode | Negative electrode; reduction happens here |
| Anode | Positive electrode; oxidation happens here |
| Inert electrode | Does not react; graphite or platinum |

## Predicting products (1.58C)

**Molten binary compound:** metal at the cathode, non-metal at the anode.

**Aqueous solution:** water adds H⁺ and OH⁻ ions, so each electrode has a choice.

> **Method in steps -- aqueous, inert electrodes**
> 1. List all cations: the metal ion **and H⁺**.
> 2. Cathode: metal less reactive than hydrogen (Cu, Ag) → metal. Metal more reactive than hydrogen (K, Na, Ca, Mg...) → hydrogen.
> 3. List all anions: the salt's anion **and OH⁻**.
> 4. Anode: halide (Cl⁻, Br⁻, I⁻) in concentrated solution → halogen. Sulfate or nitrate → oxygen.
> 5. Work out what stays in solution from the ions not discharged.

## The four named electrolytes

| Electrolyte | Cathode | Anode | Observations | Left behind |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Molten lead(II) bromide | Lead | Bromine | Bulb lights only once molten; silvery lead; red-brown fumes | Nothing (compound used up) |
| Concentrated sodium chloride solution | Hydrogen | Chlorine | Bubbles at both; pale green gas bleaches damp litmus | Sodium hydroxide (alkaline) |
| Dilute sulfuric acid | Hydrogen | Oxygen | Bubbles at both; hydrogen volume twice oxygen volume | Sulfuric acid, more concentrated |
| Copper(II) sulfate solution | Copper | Oxygen | Pink-brown deposit; bubbles at anode; blue colour fades | Sulfuric acid (acidic) |

Lead(II) bromide must be heated in a **fume cupboard**: bromine is toxic. Very dilute sodium chloride solution gives oxygen as well at the anode.

## Half-equations (1.59C)

| Reaction | Half-equation | Type |
|---|---|---|
| Lead forms | Pb²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Pb | Reduction |
| Copper forms | Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu | Reduction |
| Silver forms | Ag⁺ + e⁻ → Ag | Reduction |
| Hydrogen forms | 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂ | Reduction |
| Bromine forms | 2Br⁻ → Br₂ + 2e⁻ | Oxidation |
| Chlorine forms | 2Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + 2e⁻ | Oxidation |
| Oxygen forms | 4OH⁻ → O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻ | Oxidation |

> **Method in steps -- writing a half-equation**
> 1. Ion on the left, product on the right (gases as molecules: H₂, O₂, Cl₂, Br₂).
> 2. Balance atoms.
> 3. Add electrons to the more positive side until total charge is equal on both sides.
> 4. Electrons on the left = gain = **reduction** (cathode). Electrons on the right = loss = **oxidation** (anode).

**OIL RIG:** Oxidation Is Loss, Reduction Is Gain (of electrons).

**Linking the two electrodes.** Electrons lost at the anode = electrons gained at the cathode. Scale the two half-equations to the same number of electrons, then read off the mole ratio of products.

| Electrolyte | Electrons | Mole ratio of products |
|---|---|---|
| Dilute sulfuric acid | 4e⁻ | 2 H₂ : 1 O₂ |
| Copper(II) sulfate | 4e⁻ | 2 Cu : 1 O₂ |
| Sodium chloride solution | 2e⁻ | 1 H₂ : 1 Cl₂ |
| Molten lead(II) bromide | 2e⁻ | 1 Pb : 1 Br₂ |

Equal moles of gas have equal volumes at the same temperature and pressure; at rtp, 1 mol = 24 dm³ = 24 000 cm³.

### Small worked reminders

**Balancing the oxygen half-equation.** Start with OH⁻ → O₂. Two O atoms are needed in O₂, but the extra H atoms must go somewhere, so water forms: 4OH⁻ → O₂ + 2H₂O. Charge on the left is 4−, on the right 0, so add 4e⁻ to the right: `4OH⁻ → O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻`.

**Sodium chloride solution, equal volumes.** Both half-equations use 2e⁻, so moles of H₂ = moles of Cl₂. If 40 cm³ of hydrogen is collected, 40 cm³ of chlorine is expected. Less is usually measured, because some chlorine dissolves in the solution.

**Silver: one electron.** Ag⁺ has a single positive charge, so `Ag⁺ + e⁻ → Ag`. For every 4 electrons, 4 Ag form at the cathode and 1 O₂ at the anode in silver nitrate solution.

### Molten vs aqueous at a glance

| Compound | Molten: cathode / anode | Aqueous, concentrated: cathode / anode |
|---|---|---|
| Sodium chloride | Sodium / chlorine | Hydrogen / chlorine |
| Potassium bromide | Potassium / bromine | Hydrogen / bromine |
| Potassium iodide | Potassium / iodine | Hydrogen / iodine |

The anode product is the same in each pair because the halide wins in concentrated solution. Only the cathode product changes, because all three metals are more reactive than hydrogen. A copper salt would give copper either way.

## The practical (1.60C)

- Graphite electrodes, low-voltage d.c. supply, solution in a cell or beaker.
- Small test tubes full of solution inverted over each electrode to collect gas.
- Record observations at each electrode, then test gases:
  - **Hydrogen:** lighted splint, squeaky pop.
  - **Oxygen:** glowing splint relights.
  - **Chlorine:** damp blue litmus turns red then white.
- Rinse electrodes between solutions. Ventilate if chlorine is produced.
- Change one thing at a time (the solution, or its concentration) and keep the rest the same.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Molten vs aqueous sodium chloride.** Molten: sodium + chlorine. Aqueous (concentrated): hydrogen + chlorine.
- **Anode vs cathode.** Anode positive, oxidation, anions arrive. Cathode negative, reduction, cations arrive.
- **Ions vs electrons.** Ions move in the electrolyte; electrons move in the external circuit.
- **"Cannot move" vs "no ions".** Solid ionic compounds have ions; they are fixed.
- **Copper(II) sulfate vs dilute sulfuric acid.** Both give oxygen at the anode. Copper sulfate gives copper at the cathode; the acid gives hydrogen.
- **Concentrated vs very dilute chloride solution.** Concentrated gives chlorine at the anode; very dilute gives oxygen too.

## Quick self-test

1. Name the two kinds of mobile charged particle that allow conduction.
2. Explain why solid potassium bromide does not conduct.
3. State the products at each electrode when molten calcium chloride is electrolysed.
4. State the products at each electrode when copper(II) chloride solution (concentrated) is electrolysed with inert electrodes.
5. State the products when sodium nitrate solution is electrolysed with inert electrodes.
6. Write the half-equation for iodide ions at the anode.
7. Write the half-equation for aluminium ions at the cathode, and state whether it is oxidation or reduction.
8. During electrolysis of dilute sulfuric acid, 50 cm³ of hydrogen is collected. What volume of oxygen forms at the same temperature and pressure?
9. Copper(II) sulfate solution is electrolysed with inert electrodes and 1.27 g of copper is deposited. Calculate the volume of oxygen at rtp. (A_r Cu = 63.5)
10. In the electrolysis of molten lead(II) bromide, 0.0100 mol of electrons pass. Calculate the mass of lead formed. (A_r Pb = 207)
11. Why does copper(II) sulfate solution become acidic during electrolysis with inert electrodes?

### Answers

1. Ions and delocalised electrons.
2. The ions are held in fixed positions in the lattice, so they cannot move to carry charge.
3. Cathode: calcium. Anode: chlorine.
4. Cathode: copper (less reactive than hydrogen). Anode: chlorine (halide in concentrated solution).
5. Cathode: hydrogen (sodium more reactive than hydrogen). Anode: oxygen (nitrate is not discharged).
6. 2I⁻ → I₂ + 2e⁻
7. Al³⁺ + 3e⁻ → Al; reduction (gain of electrons).
8. 25 cm³ (H₂ : O₂ = 2 : 1).
9. n(Cu) = 1.27 ÷ 63.5 = 0.0200 mol; n(O₂) = 0.0200 ÷ 2 = 0.0100 mol; volume = 0.0100 × 24 000 = **240 cm³**.
10. Pb²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Pb, so n(Pb) = 0.0100 ÷ 2 = 0.00500 mol; mass = 0.00500 × 207 = **1.035 g** (1.04 g to 3 s.f.).
11. Cu²⁺ and OH⁻ ions are discharged; H⁺ and SO₄²⁻ ions remain, which is sulfuric acid.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "electrons flow through the solution" instead of "ions move".
- Writing "the solid has no ions" when the point is that the ions cannot move.
- Giving sodium (or potassium) as the cathode product for an aqueous solution.
- Forgetting H⁺ and OH⁻ from water when listing the ions in a solution.
- Writing Cl or Br (atoms) instead of Cl₂ or Br₂ in half-equations.
- Electrons on the wrong side, or charges that do not balance, in an anode half-equation.
- Calling the cathode reaction oxidation, or explaining it as "loss of oxygen" instead of gain of electrons.
- Stating the gas ratio for dilute sulfuric acid the wrong way round (it is twice as much hydrogen).
- Describing chlorine's test as "turns litmus red" without the bleaching.
- Leaving out the fume cupboard or the reason for it in the lead(II) bromide experiment.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the difference between the anode and the cathode?**
The anode is the positive electrode, where anions arrive and oxidation (loss of electrons) happens. The cathode is the negative electrode, where cations arrive and reduction (gain of electrons) happens.

**What are the products of electrolysing molten and aqueous sodium chloride?**
Molten sodium chloride gives sodium at the cathode and chlorine at the anode. Concentrated sodium chloride solution gives hydrogen at the cathode and chlorine at the anode, leaving sodium hydroxide in solution.

**Why does dilute sulfuric acid give twice as much hydrogen as oxygen?**
Balancing the half-equations for the same four electrons gives 2 H₂ at the cathode for every 1 O₂ at the anode. Equal moles of gas have equal volumes at the same temperature and pressure, so the hydrogen volume is twice the oxygen volume.

**What does OIL RIG mean?**
OIL RIG stands for Oxidation Is Loss, Reduction Is Gain, referring to electrons. It is how you explain whether an electrode reaction in Edexcel International GCSE Chemistry (4CH1) is oxidation or reduction.
