This guide covers one content point of the health psychology option in IB DP Psychology (first assessment 2019): **"risk and protective factors"**, part of the topic "determinants of health". The [health psychology study guide](/resources/ib-ib-dp-psychology-health-psychology-study-guide/) introduces the biopsychosocial determinants and the health belief model, and the option's revision notes give risk and protection a single table row. This page defines the two kinds of factor, explains how they work, and gives four studies you can use, with evaluation and an essay plan. The course described here has its last examination session in November 2026.

## Definitions

- A **risk factor** is a characteristic or experience that increases the likelihood of a health problem. Examples: chronic stress, smoking, adverse experiences in childhood, social isolation.
- A **protective factor** is a characteristic or experience that reduces the likelihood of a health problem, or reduces the harm done by a risk factor. Examples: social support, a sense of control, self-efficacy, a varied social network.

Three points are worth making in any answer:

1. **Probability, not certainty.** A risk factor raises the chance of illness across a group; it does not decide what happens to one person.
2. **Interaction.** Protective factors often matter most when risk is high. A friend to talk to may make little difference in an easy week and a large one during a crisis.
3. **Levels.** Risk and protective factors can be biological (genes, immune function), psychological (appraisal, self-efficacy) or social (income, relationships), which links this content point to the biopsychosocial model.

## Two routes from a factor to illness

A risk or protective factor can affect health through two broad pathways:

- **Behavioural pathway:** the factor changes what people do. Stress may lead to smoking, drinking or poor sleep; a supportive partner may encourage someone to see a doctor or take medication.
- **Physiological pathway:** the factor changes the body directly. Chronic stress keeps the HPA axis active, and prolonged high cortisol can suppress parts of the immune response.

Good essays say which pathway a study supports, because many studies show a link without showing the route.

## Risk factor: adverse childhood experiences (Felitti et al., 1998)

**Aim.** To investigate whether abuse and household dysfunction in childhood are associated with adult health risks and disease.

**Method.** Adults attending a health-appraisal clinic run by a large health-care organisation in San Diego completed a questionnaire about adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) in several categories: psychological, physical and sexual abuse, and household dysfunction such as living with someone who misused substances, had a mental illness, was imprisoned or was violent towards the mother. Answers were compared with participants' medical records and health behaviours.

**Findings.** ACEs were common. There was a **graded relationship**: the more categories of adversity a person reported, the more likely they were to have adult risk behaviours, such as smoking and alcohol or drug misuse, and diseases, including heart disease, lung disease and liver disease.

**Evaluation.** The large sample and graded relationship are strengths: a dose-response pattern is harder to explain away than a single difference. However, the data are correlational, so childhood adversity cannot be shown to cause adult illness; poverty, for example, could contribute to both. Childhood experiences were reported retrospectively by adults, which risks inaccurate recall. The sample consisted of people with health insurance in one American city, which limits generalisation. The study suggests both pathways: risk behaviours as a behavioural route, and long-term stress as a possible physiological one.

## Risk factor: psychological stress and infection (Cohen, Tyrrell and Smith, 1991)

**Aim.** To test whether psychological stress increases susceptibility to the common cold.

**Method.** Healthy volunteers completed questionnaires measuring recent stressful life events, perceived stress and negative emotion, which were combined into a stress index. They were then given nasal drops containing a cold virus, or salt water for a control group, and kept in quarantine so that infection could be monitored.

**Findings.** Among those exposed to a virus, rates of infection and of clinical colds rose with the stress index, in a dose-response pattern. The relationship remained after controlling for factors such as age, allergies, smoking and alcohol.

**Evaluation.** Exposure to the virus was controlled, so differences in contact with infection cannot explain the result, which gives this study more internal validity than most stress research. Stress itself was measured, not manipulated, so it remains a correlational variable. Deliberately infecting participants raises ethical questions, managed through informed consent, the mild nature of the illness and medical supervision. The study supports a physiological pathway, because health behaviours were controlled.

## Protective factor: social ties (Berkman and Syme, 1979)

**Aim.** To investigate whether social relationships are associated with mortality.

**Method.** Adults in Alameda County, California, were surveyed about their social ties: marriage, contact with friends and relatives, membership of a religious group and other group memberships. Deaths were then recorded over a follow-up period of nine years.

**Findings.** People with the fewest social ties had higher death rates than those with the most, and the association remained when the researchers took account of baseline health, socio-economic status and health practices such as smoking.

**Evaluation.** The **prospective** design is a strength: social ties were measured before death, so the result cannot be caused by people who were already dying withdrawing from others. A large community sample adds to generalisability within that population. The study is still correlational, and self-reported ties may not capture the quality of relationships. **Cohen et al. (1997)**, using the viral-exposure method, found that people with more diverse social networks were less susceptible to colds, which adds experimental control over exposure to the same conclusion.

## The buffering hypothesis

Cohen and Wills (1985) distinguished two ways in which social support might protect health:

- a **main effect**: support is beneficial at all levels of stress;
- a **buffering effect**: support protects mainly when stress is high, by changing how a stressor is appraised or how people cope.

They argued that both occur, depending on how support is measured: being part of a network seems to have a general benefit, while having someone to rely on in a crisis acts as a buffer. In an essay, the buffering hypothesis shows interaction between risk and protective factors, which is a strong analytical point.

## Worked example: planning a 22-mark essay

**Question:** "Discuss risk and protective factors in relation to one health problem."

1. **Focus:** choose stress-related illness; define risk and protective factors; state that they interact.
2. **Knowledge:** behavioural and physiological pathways; the buffering hypothesis.
3. **Research:** Cohen, Tyrrell and Smith (1991) for stress as a risk factor; Felitti et al. (1998) for early adversity; Berkman and Syme (1979) and Cohen et al. (1997) for social ties as protection.
4. **Discussion:** controlled viral exposure versus correlational surveys; retrospective recall in the ACE study; prospective designs; confounding by socio-economic status; ethics of viral exposure; cultural differences in what counts as support.
5. **Conclusion:** strong, repeated evidence that stress and adversity raise risk and that social ties protect; the exact mechanisms, and how they combine for each person, are less clear.

## Common mistakes

- Saying a risk factor "causes" illness on the basis of correlational evidence.
- Listing factors without research or without explaining a pathway.
- Treating protective factors as the opposite of risk factors rather than as factors that can reduce their effect.
- Forgetting to name a health problem when the question asks for one.
- Using figures from studies that you cannot recall accurately.

## Exam technique

**Discuss** needs several arguments, supported by research, with a conclusion. **Evaluate** needs a judgement of the strength of the evidence. **Contrast** asks for differences, for example between a risk factor and a protective factor, referring to both throughout. Build each paragraph as: factor, pathway, study, what it shows, and how strong the evidence is.
