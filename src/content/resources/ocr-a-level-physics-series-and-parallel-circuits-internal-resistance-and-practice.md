These questions cover section 4.3 (Electrical circuits) of the OCR Advanced GCE in Physics A specification (H556), Version 3.0 (March 2026), for first assessment in 2017: series and parallel circuits, internal resistance and potential dividers. Revise first with the [study guide](/resources/ocr-a-level-physics-series-and-parallel-circuits-internal-resistance-and/) or the [revision notes](/resources/ocr-a-level-physics-series-and-parallel-circuits-internal-resistance-and-revision-notes/). Sources have negligible internal resistance unless a question says otherwise.

## Questions

**1.** (a) Calculate the total resistance of 6.0 Ω, 12 Ω and 4.0 Ω resistors connected in parallel. **[2]**
**(b)** This group is connected in series with a 3.0 Ω resistor across a 10 V source. Calculate the current from the source. **[2]**

**2.** State Kirchhoff's first law and second law, and for each say which conserved quantity it expresses. **[4]**

**3.** Two cells of e.m.f. 9.0 V and 3.0 V are connected in opposition in a series loop with resistors of 4.0 Ω and 2.0 Ω.

**(a)** Calculate the current. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the p.d. across the 4.0 Ω resistor. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the power dissipated in the 2.0 Ω resistor. **[1]**

**4.** A 9.0 V source in series with a 3.0 Ω resistor is connected in parallel with an 8.0 V source in series with a 2.0 Ω resistor. A 3.0 Ω resistor R is connected across the pair of branches. Calculate the current in each branch and the p.d. across R. **[6]**

**5.** A cell of e.m.f. 1.6 V supplies a current of 0.50 A. Its terminal p.d. is then 1.35 V.

**(a)** Calculate the lost volts. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the internal resistance. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the resistance of the external circuit. **[1]**
**(d)** Calculate the power wasted inside the cell. **[1]**

**6.** (a) A car battery's terminal p.d. dips when the starter motor is switched on. Explain why. **[3]**
**(b)** A 1.5 V cell has internal resistance 0.30 Ω. Calculate the current if its terminals are joined by a thick wire of negligible resistance. **[2]**

**7.** A student investigates a power supply. The table shows the readings.

| I / A | 0.20 | 0.40 | 0.60 | 0.80 | 1.00 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| V / V | 5.52 | 5.04 | 4.56 | 4.08 | 3.60 |

**(a)** Describe the circuit and procedure used to obtain these readings. **[3]**
**(b)** Use the data to determine the e.m.f. and internal resistance of the supply. **[4]**
**(c)** Explain why the student opens the switch between readings. **[1]**

**8.** Two identical cells each have e.m.f. 1.5 V and internal resistance 0.40 Ω. They supply a 2.0 Ω resistor.

**(a)** Calculate the current when the cells are in series. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate the current when the cells are in parallel. **[3]**

**9.** A 5.0 V supply is connected across a 3.3 kΩ fixed resistor in series with an NTC thermistor. The output is taken across the fixed resistor. The thermistor resistance is 10 kΩ at 10 °C and 1.0 kΩ at 50 °C.

**(a)** Calculate Vout at 10 °C and at 50 °C. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain why Vout changes in this way. **[2]**
**(c)** State the thermistor resistance at which Vout is 2.5 V. **[1]**

**10.** A 9.0 V supply is connected across resistor R1 in series with a 4.0 kΩ resistor R2. Vout is across R2 and is required to be 2.0 V.

**(a)** Calculate R1. **[3]**
**(b)** R1 is replaced by an LDR. State and explain how Vout changes when the light intensity increases. **[1]**

**11.** (a) A 12 V supply is connected across a uniform potentiometer track 80.0 cm long. Calculate the output between the slider and the zero end when the slider is 30.0 cm from that end. **[2]**
**(b)** Two 10 kΩ resistors form a potential divider across the 12 V supply. Calculate Vout across the lower resistor, first unloaded, then with a 10 kΩ load connected across it. **[4]**
**(c)** Explain why the loaded output is smaller. **[2]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** 1/R = 1/6.0 + 1/12 + 1/4.0 = 2/12 + 1/12 + 3/12 = 6/12 [1], so R = **2.0 Ω** [1]
**(b)** Total R = 2.0 + 3.0 = 5.0 Ω [1]; I = 10/5.0 = **2.0 A** [1]
*Examiner insight:* Writing the reciprocal sum before inverting shows where an error arose; leaving 0.5 as the answer is a common slip.

**2.** First law: the total current arriving at a junction equals the total current leaving [1]; this expresses conservation of charge [1]. Second law: round any closed loop the sum of e.m.f. equals the sum of p.d. [1]; this expresses conservation of energy [1]
*Examiner insight:* Name each law and the quantity it conserves; a bare list of formulae does not answer a "state" question.

**3. (a)** Net e.m.f. = 9.0 − 3.0 = 6.0 V [1]; I = 6.0/(4.0 + 2.0) = **1.0 A** [1]
**(b)** V = 1.0 × 4.0 = **4.0 V** [1]
**(c)** P = I²R = 1.0² × 2.0 = **2.0 W** [1]
*Examiner insight:* Subtracting the e.m.f.s is the point of the question; adding them gives 12 V and a different current.

**4.** Let I1 be the current from the 9.0 V branch and I2 from the 8.0 V branch, both towards R. Current in R = I1 + I2 (first law). [1]
Loop with 9.0 V branch: 9.0 = 3.0 I1 + 3.0(I1 + I2), so 9.0 = 6.0 I1 + 3.0 I2. [1]
Loop with 8.0 V branch: 8.0 = 2.0 I2 + 3.0(I1 + I2), so 8.0 = 3.0 I1 + 5.0 I2. [1]
Double the second: 16 = 6.0 I1 + 10 I2. Subtract the first: 7.0 = 7.0 I2, so I2 = **1.0 A**. [1]
Then 9.0 = 6.0 I1 + 3.0, so I1 = **1.0 A**. [1]
Current in R = **2.0 A**, so p.d. across R = 2.0 × 3.0 = **6.0 V**. [1]
*Examiner insight:* Define your currents and loop direction on the diagram before writing equations; a check that 9.0 − 3.0 − 6.0 = 0 on the first loop shows the working is consistent.

**5. (a)** Lost volts = E − V = 1.6 − 1.35 = **0.25 V** [1]
**(b)** r = 0.25/0.50 = **0.50 Ω** [1]
**(c)** R = 1.35/0.50 = **2.7 Ω** [1]
**(d)** P = I²r = 0.50² × 0.50 = 0.125, so **0.13 W** [1]
*Examiner insight:* Dividing lost volts by current, not terminal p.d. by current, is what gives r; keep each p.d. labelled.

**6. (a)** The starter motor draws a large current [1]. Lost volts Ir across the battery's internal resistance increase [1]. Terminal p.d. V = E − Ir therefore falls [1]
**(b)** I = E/r = 1.5/0.30 = **5.0 A** [1] (with R = 0, E = I(R + r)) [1]
*Examiner insight:* "The battery runs down" does not answer an explain question; link the current, the internal resistance and the equation.

**7. (a)** Put an ammeter and a rheostat in series with the supply, and wire a voltmeter straight to the supply's two terminals [1]. Change the resistance in steps and record I and V each time [1]. Plot V against I (or use the table directly) [1]
**(b)** V = E − Ir, so the vertical intercept is E and the gradient is −r [1]. Gradient from the first and last points = (3.60 − 5.52)/(1.00 − 0.20) = −2.4 V A⁻¹ [1]. So **r = 2.4 Ω** [1]. At I = 0, V = 5.52 + 0.20 × 2.4 = **6.0 V**, so **E = 6.0 V** [1]
**(c)** Current through the supply causes heating (and drains it), which could change r and E [1]
*Examiner insight:* A gradient taken from widely spaced points, with the minus sign dealt with, is more convincing than one found from neighbouring points.

**8. (a)** In series: E = 3.0 V, r = 0.80 Ω [1]. Total resistance = 2.0 + 0.80 = 2.8 Ω [1]. I = 3.0/2.8 = **1.1 A** [1]
**(b)** In parallel: E = 1.5 V, r = 0.40/2 = 0.20 Ω [1]. Total resistance = 2.2 Ω [1]. I = 1.5/2.2 = **0.68 A** [1]
*Examiner insight:* State the combined e.m.f. and combined internal resistance first, so the method is clear to a reader.

**9. (a)** At 10 °C: Vout = 3.3/(3.3 + 10) × 5.0 = **1.2 V** [1]. At 50 °C: Vout = 3.3/(3.3 + 1.0) × 5.0 = **3.8 V** [1]. Resistances can stay in kΩ because the units cancel [1]
**(b)** The thermistor's resistance falls as temperature rises [1]. The thermistor then takes a smaller share of the 5.0 V, so the fixed resistor takes a larger share [1]
**(c)** Equal shares of 2.5 V each need equal resistances: **3.3 kΩ** [1]
*Examiner insight:* Say which component the output is across; the same thermistor gives the opposite trend if Vout is taken across it.

**10. (a)** V1 = 9.0 − 2.0 = 7.0 V [1]. V1/V2 = R1/R2, so R1 = 4.0 × 7.0/2.0 [1] = **14 kΩ** [1]
**(b)** The LDR's resistance falls, so its share falls and Vout across R2 **rises** [1]
*Examiner insight:* Write the ratio form with the numbers in it before rearranging; a brief reason for the direction of change is more secure than a bare "increases".

**11. (a)** Vout = (30.0/80.0) × 12 [1] = **4.5 V** [1]
**(b)** Unloaded: Vout = 6.0 V [1]. Load in parallel with the lower resistor: 10 kΩ and 10 kΩ give 5.0 kΩ [1]. Vout = 5.0/(10 + 5.0) × 12 [1] = **4.0 V** [1]
**(c)** The load is in parallel with the lower resistor, which lowers its effective resistance [1]. It then takes a smaller share of the supply p.d. [1]
*Examiner insight:* Replace the lower resistor by the parallel combination before using the divider equation; using the unloaded resistance is the usual error.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Leaving a parallel result as 1/R, or adding resistances when the resistors are in parallel.
- Adding opposing e.m.f.s instead of subtracting.
- Using E in place of the terminal p.d. when finding the load current or resistance.
- Dropping the minus sign when relating the gradient of a V-I graph to r.
- Using two neighbouring points to find a gradient, which gives a poor estimate.
- Not stating which component Vout is across before explaining a sensor circuit.
- Giving a bare "resistance changes" explanation without saying how the share of the supply p.d. changes.
- Forgetting that a load across R2 is in parallel with R2.
