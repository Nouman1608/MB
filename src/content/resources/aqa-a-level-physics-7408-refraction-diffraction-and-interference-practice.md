These questions cover section 3.3.2 Refraction, diffraction and interference (3.3.2.1 to 3.3.2.3) of the AQA AS and A-level Physics specification (7407/7408), including Required practical 2. Section A has short questions; Section B has structured questions. Learn the content first with the [refraction, diffraction and interference study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-physics-7408-refraction-diffraction-and-interference/) and the [revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-physics-7408-refraction-diffraction-and-interference-revision-notes/). Take c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹ and the refractive index of air as 1.00.

## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** **(a)** State what is meant by coherent sources. **[1]**
**(b)** State one reason a laser is a suitable source for a double-slit experiment. **[1]**

**2.** Two loudspeakers connected to the same signal generator emit sound in phase with wavelength 0.68 m. A listener is 4.42 m from one speaker and 3.40 m from the other. Explain whether the listener hears a loud or a quiet sound. **[2]**

**3.** In a double-slit experiment with light of wavelength 589 nm and slits 0.30 mm apart, 10 fringe spacings measure 29.5 mm. Calculate the slit-to-screen distance. **[3]**

**4.** State two safety precautions when using a laser. **[2]**

**5.** Monochromatic light passes through a single narrow slit. Describe the pattern on a screen and state the effect of making the slit narrower. **[3]**

**6.** Diamond has a refractive index of 2.42.

**(a)** Calculate the speed of light in diamond. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the critical angle for a diamond–air boundary. **[1]**

### Section B: structured questions

**7.** Light from a lamp falls normally on a grating with 500 lines per mm. A spectral line produces a second-order maximum at 36.0° to the straight-through direction.

**(a)** Calculate the wavelength of the line. **[3]**
**(b)** Find the highest order of this line that can be seen. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the angle of that highest order. **[1]**

**8.** White light, containing wavelengths from 400 nm to 700 nm, falls normally on a diffraction grating.

**(a)** Describe the zero-order maximum and the first-order spectrum. **[3]**
**(b)** Show that the second- and third-order spectra overlap. **[2]**

**9.** Light travels from glass (n = 1.52) into water (n = 1.33).

**(a)** Calculate the angle of refraction for an angle of incidence of 40°. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the critical angle for the glass–water boundary. **[1]**
**(c)** State what happens to light meeting the boundary at 65°. **[1]**

**10.** A step-index fibre has a core of refractive index 1.46 and cladding of refractive index 1.44.

**(a)** Calculate the critical angle at the core–cladding boundary. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain how modal dispersion arises and why it limits how quickly pulses can be sent. **[3]**
**(c)** For a fibre 2.0 km long, calculate the difference in travel time between a ray along the axis and a ray meeting the boundary at exactly the critical angle. **[3]**

**11.** Microwaves pass through two narrow gaps in a metal sheet. A detector moved along a line parallel to the sheet records alternate high and low readings. Explain this observation. **[3]**

**12.** In Required practical 2, a laser shines normally on a grating of 300 lines per mm. The two first-order spots on a screen 1.20 m away are 0.392 m apart.

**(a)** Calculate the angle of the first-order maximum. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the wavelength of the laser light. **[2]**

**13.** Explain how Young's double-slit experiment contributed to changing ideas about the nature of light. **[2]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** Same frequency and a constant phase difference [1].
**(b)** It is monochromatic and coherent (or gives an intense, narrow beam) [1].

**2.** Path difference = 4.42 − 3.40 = 1.02 m = 1.5λ [1]. The waves arrive in antiphase and interfere destructively, so the sound is **quiet** [1].

**3.** w = 29.5/10 = 2.95 mm [1]. D = ws/λ = (2.95 × 10⁻³ × 0.30 × 10⁻³) / 589 × 10⁻⁹ [1] = **1.50 m** [1].
*Examiner insight:* Measuring across 10 spacings divides the reading error by 10; the slit separation is then often the largest source of uncertainty.

**4.** Any two of these, one mark each: never look directly into the beam or its reflections; keep the beam below eye level (or direct it away from people); remove reflective surfaces; display a warning sign [1] [1].

**5.** A bright central maximum [1] about twice the width of the other maxima, which are much less intense and equally spaced either side [1]. A narrower slit makes the central maximum wider [1].

**6. (a)** cₛ = c/n = 3.00 × 10⁸ / 2.42 = **1.24 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹** [1].
**(b)** sin θc = 1/2.42, θc = **24.4°** [1].

**7. (a)** d = 1/(500 × 10³) = 2.00 × 10⁻⁶ m [1]. λ = d sin θ / n = 2.00 × 10⁻⁶ × sin 36.0° / 2 [1] = **5.88 × 10⁻⁷ m** [1].
**(b)** d/λ = 2.00 × 10⁻⁶ / 5.88 × 10⁻⁷ = 3.4 [1], so the highest order is **3** [1].
**(c)** sin θ₃ = 3 × 5.88 × 10⁻⁷ / 2.00 × 10⁻⁶ = 0.882, θ₃ = **61.8°** [1].

**8. (a)** The zero order is white, because all wavelengths have zero path difference there [1]. The first order is a continuous spectrum [1], with violet nearest the centre and red furthest out, because θ increases with λ [1].
**(b)** The second-order red edge has n λ = 2 × 700 = 1400 nm [1]; the third-order violet edge has 3 × 400 = 1200 nm, which is smaller, so the third order begins at a smaller angle than the second order ends: they overlap [1].

**9. (a)** 1.52 sin 40° = 1.33 sin θ₂, so sin θ₂ = 0.735 [1]; θ₂ = **47.3°** [1].
**(b)** sin θc = 1.33/1.52, θc = **61.0°** [1].
**(c)** 65° is greater than the critical angle, so it is totally internally reflected [1].

**10. (a)** sin θc = 1.44/1.46, θc = **80.5°** [1].
**(b)** Rays enter the core at different angles, so they take paths of different lengths [1]. Rays on longer paths arrive later, so each pulse spreads out in time [1]. If pulses are sent too close together they overlap and cannot be distinguished, which limits the rate [1].
**(c)** Axial time = 2000 × 1.46 / 3.00 × 10⁸ = 9.73 μs [1]. Critical-angle path = 2000 / sin 80.5° = 2027 m, time 9.87 μs [1]. Difference = **0.14 μs (1.4 × 10⁻⁷ s)** [1].
*Examiner insight:* The ray that meets the boundary at the critical angle makes the smallest allowed angle with the boundary normal, so it zigzags most and travels furthest.

**11.** The gaps diffract the microwaves so the waves from each gap overlap [1]; the gaps act as coherent sources [1]. Where the path difference is a whole number of wavelengths the waves are in phase (high reading); where it is an odd number of half-wavelengths they are in antiphase (low reading) [1].

**12. (a)** Distance from centre = 0.392/2 = 0.196 m; tan θ = 0.196/1.20 [1]; θ = **9.28°** [1].
**(b)** d = 1/(300 × 10³) = 3.33 × 10⁻⁶ m [1]; λ = d sin θ = **5.37 × 10⁻⁷ m** [1].

**13.** It produced an interference pattern, which only waves can do [1], so it provided strong evidence for the wave model of light over the particle (corpuscular) model accepted at the time [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calling sources coherent without mentioning a constant phase difference.
- Using the total spot separation instead of half of it for a grating angle.
- Using lines per mm as the slit spacing.
- Giving a grating order that would need sin θ above 1.
- Writing sin θc = n₁/n₂.
- Confusing the cause of modal dispersion (paths) with material dispersion (wavelengths).

## Frequently asked questions

**How do you find the grating angle from spots on a screen?**
Halve the distance between the two spots of the same order to get the distance from the centre, then use tan θ = distance ÷ screen distance.

**Why do the second- and third-order spectra overlap?**
The angle depends on nλ. The red end of the second order has nλ = 1400 nm, which is larger than the violet end of the third order at 1200 nm, so the third order starts before the second has finished.
