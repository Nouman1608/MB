## What the specification asks for

The content statements are:

- "The production of epoxyethane by the partial oxidation of ethene and understand the hazards of this process."
- "The reactions with water and alcohols and the uses of the products formed."

You should be able to:

- "explain the high reactivity of epoxyethane"
- "write equations for the reactions of epoxyethane with water and with alcohols and outline the mechanism for these reactions"
- "explain the economic and environmental importance of products including, surfactants and antifreeze, formed in these reactions."

## The molecule

Epoxyethane, C₂H₄O, is a **three-membered ring** made of two CH₂ groups and one oxygen atom. Each carbon is bonded to the other carbon, to the oxygen and to two hydrogens. The oxygen carries two lone pairs. It is a colourless gas at room temperature.

## Production by partial oxidation

Ethene reacts with oxygen over a **silver catalyst**:

2CH₂=CH₂ + O₂ → 2C₂H₄O

"Partial" oxidation means only one oxygen atom is added to each ethene molecule. **Complete** oxidation, the combustion of ethene, also happens on the catalyst:

CH₂=CH₂ + 3O₂ → 2CO₂ + 2H₂O

That competing reaction wastes ethene and releases far more energy.

### Atom economy

All the atoms of the reactants end up in epoxyethane, so the atom economy of the partial oxidation is **100 %**. The actual yield is lower because some ethene burns.

### Hazards of the process

Each hazard links to a property or a reaction:

- **Fire and explosion.** Ethene and epoxyethane are both flammable, and mixtures of their vapours with oxygen or air can explode. The proportions of ethene and oxygen must be controlled.
- **Overheating.** Both oxidation reactions are exothermic, and combustion much more so. If the temperature rises, more combustion occurs, releasing more heat: the process must be cooled and its temperature carefully controlled.
- **Toxicity.** Epoxyethane is toxic and is classified as a carcinogen, so leaks must be prevented and the plant sealed and monitored.
- **Reactivity.** The same ring strain that makes epoxyethane useful means it reacts readily with water and other compounds, so storage must keep it away from contaminants.

## Why epoxyethane is so reactive

Two features act together:

1. **Ring strain.** In a three-membered ring the bond angles are forced to about 60°, far smaller than the normal tetrahedral angle of about 109.5° around carbon and oxygen. The bonding electron pairs are pushed close together, so the ring C–O bonds are weaker than ordinary C–O bonds. Breaking one C–O bond opens the ring and relieves the strain.
2. **Polar C–O bonds.** Oxygen is more electronegative than carbon, so each ring carbon is δ+ and attracts nucleophiles.

A nucleophile with a lone pair can therefore attack a ring carbon and break the strained C–O bond. Ordinary ethers, with unstrained C–O bonds, do not react this way.

## Reactions with water and alcohols

Both water and alcohols have lone pairs on oxygen and act as **nucleophiles**. The ring opens and the attacking oxygen becomes bonded to one CH₂, while the ring oxygen becomes an –OH group on the other.

| Reactant | Equation | Product | Main use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Water | C₂H₄O + H₂O → HOCH₂CH₂OH | ethane-1,2-diol | antifreeze; making polyesters |
| Methanol | C₂H₄O + CH₃OH → CH₃OCH₂CH₂OH | 2-methoxyethanol | solvent |
| Ethanol | C₂H₄O + C₂H₅OH → C₂H₅OCH₂CH₂OH | 2-ethoxyethanol | solvent |
| Long-chain alcohol, ROH | ROH + nC₂H₄O → R(OCH₂CH₂)ₙOH | polyether chain on R | non-ionic surfactant |

### Outlining the mechanism

**Worked example 1: epoxyethane and ethanol.**

```
Step 1   C₂H₅–O–H  →  ring CH₂ (δ+)          arrow a: lone pair on ethanol O → ring carbon
         ring C–O bond → ring O                arrow b: C–O bond → O
         gives  C₂H₅–O⁺(H)–CH₂–CH₂–O⁻
Step 2   proton transfer: H from O⁺ to O⁻      arrow c: lone pair on O⁻ → H on O⁺
                                               arrow d: O⁺–H bond → O⁺
         gives  C₂H₅–O–CH₂–CH₂–OH   (2-ethoxyethanol)
```

Marking points:

- Arrow a starts on a **lone pair** of the alcohol oxygen and ends at a **ring carbon**.
- Arrow b starts on the **ring C–O bond** and ends on the ring oxygen.
- The intermediate carries **+ on the oxygen from ethanol** and **− on the former ring oxygen**.
- The final product has an ether link and an –OH group on adjacent carbons.

For water, the same steps apply with H in place of C₂H₅, giving ethane-1,2-diol.

### Further reaction: why chains grow

Every product still contains an –OH group, so it is itself an alcohol and can attack another epoxyethane molecule. For example:

HOCH₂CH₂OH + C₂H₄O → HOCH₂CH₂OCH₂CH₂OH

This is why, when ethane-1,2-diol is wanted, a **large excess of water** is used: epoxyethane is then far more likely to meet water than diol. When a surfactant is wanted, the opposite is done on purpose, adding several epoxyethane units to one alcohol.

### Worked example 2: how many units?

*A surfactant is made from hexadecan-1-ol, C₁₆H₃₃OH (Mr 242.0), and epoxyethane (Mr 44.0). The product has Mr 594.0. How many epoxyethane units has each alcohol molecule added?*

n = (594.0 − 242.0) ÷ 44.0 = **8**. Each added unit contributes C₂H₄O and no atoms are lost, so the masses simply add.

## Economic and environmental importance

**Antifreeze.** Ethane-1,2-diol mixes completely with water and lowers its freezing point, so it is added to the cooling water of vehicle engines to stop it freezing in winter. It is also a monomer for polyesters (International A2), which gives it a second large market. It is toxic if swallowed, so spilt or discarded antifreeze is an environmental and safety hazard; it must be disposed of properly.

**Surfactants.** In R(OCH₂CH₂)ₙOH, the long hydrocarbon chain R is non-polar and attracts grease and oil, while the polyether chain ending in –OH is polar and attracts water. These non-ionic surfactants are used in detergents and cleaning products in very large quantities. Once washed into rivers they can harm aquatic life, so products that break down readily in the environment are preferred.

**Solvents.** Glycol ethers such as 2-methoxyethanol dissolve both polar and non-polar substances and are used in paints and inks.

The economic point is that one cheap starting material, ethene, is converted by a 100 % atom economy process into a reactive intermediate that leads to several high-volume products.

## Common mistakes

- Describing the reaction with water as electrophilic. Water and alcohols attack as **nucleophiles**.
- Explaining reactivity only by polarity. **Ring strain** is essential.
- Writing the ring bond angle as 90° or 109.5°; it is about **60°**.
- Losing the –OH in the product, or putting the ether and –OH groups on the same carbon.
- Stating hazards without a reason (for example "it is dangerous").
- Forgetting that products still contain –OH and can react further.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Explain the high reactivity**: ring strain (about 60° against about 109.5°), weak ring bonds, polar C–O, nucleophilic attack relieves strain.
- **Write equations**: one epoxyethane per alcohol unless the question says otherwise; check that every atom of epoxyethane is in the product.
- **Outline the mechanism**: lone pair to ring carbon, ring C–O bond to O, then proton transfer.
- **Explain the importance**: give a use, the property that makes it work, and one environmental consequence.
- **State the hazards**: name the hazard and link it to the property or competing reaction.
