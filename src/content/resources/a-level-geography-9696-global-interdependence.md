This guide covers Topic 13, Global interdependence (sections 13.1 to 13.4), of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Geography (9696) syllabus for examination in 2025 and 2026. Global interdependence is one of four Advanced Human Geography options assessed in Paper 4 (1 hour 30 minutes, 60 marks, 25% of the A Level). You answer on two of the four options; for each one you answer a 10-mark structured question and choose one of two 20-mark essays.

## 13.1 Trade flows and trading patterns

**Visible trade** is trade in physical goods: copper, cars, coffee. **Invisible trade** is trade in services and other non-physical items: tourism, banking, insurance, shipping, software. When a tourist from Germany spends money in Kenya, that is an invisible export for Kenya and an invisible import for Germany.

**Global patterns and inequalities.** Trade is concentrated in three regions: Europe, North America and East Asia. Much of it is between high-income economies, and China became the largest exporter of goods in 2009. Many low-income countries, especially in sub-Saharan Africa, have a small share of world trade and rely on a few primary commodities. Their **terms of trade** (the price of exports relative to imports) are often weak and unstable.

**Factors affecting trade:**
- **Resource endowment** -- Chile is the world's largest copper producer, so copper dominates its exports.
- **Locational advantage** -- ports on major shipping routes, such as Singapore at the Strait of Malacca, become trade hubs.
- **Colonial ties** -- the Lomé Convention (1975) gave former European colonies in Africa, the Caribbean and the Pacific (the ACP group) preferential access to European markets.
- **Trade agreements** -- the EU single market, USMCA (which replaced NAFTA in 2020) and the African Continental Free Trade Area (trading began in 2021) lower barriers between members and can divert trade away from non-members.
- **Changes in the global market** -- falling transport costs, containerisation, new demand from emerging economies and price swings in commodities.

**The WTO and free trade.** The World Trade Organization was set up on 1 January 1995, replacing GATT, which had operated since 1948. It is based in Geneva and has 166 members. Its main principles are **most-favoured nation** (treat all members equally) and **national treatment** (treat imports the same as domestic goods). It settles disputes and hosts negotiations. **Free trade** means trade without tariffs, quotas or subsidies.

*Evaluation:* the WTO has cut tariffs on manufactured goods, which helped export-led growth in East Asia. But the Doha Round, launched in 2001, has stalled, largely over agricultural subsidies in rich countries. Since 2019 its Appellate Body has not worked, because the United States blocked new appointments. Critics argue free trade can force developing countries to open markets before their industries can compete.

**Impacts of trade on exporting and importing countries.** Exporters gain foreign currency, jobs and economies of scale. But dependence on one commodity is risky. Zambia shows this. Copper makes up the large majority of its export earnings, so its exchange rate moves with the copper price. Importers gain cheaper goods and wider choice, and inputs for their own industries. But cheap imports can undercut local producers. EU agricultural export subsidies have been criticised for undercutting dairy, poultry and tomato farmers in Africa and Asia.

**Fairtrade.** The first Fairtrade label, Max Havelaar, began in the Netherlands in 1988. Fairtrade International sets standards. Certified producers receive a **Fairtrade Minimum Price**, a floor meant to cover average production costs, plus a **Fairtrade Premium** that the producer group invests in community or business projects. Products include coffee, cocoa, bananas, tea and cotton.

*Evaluation:* Fairtrade protects producers when world prices fall. But producers often sell only part of their output on Fairtrade terms, with the rest sold at market price. A 2014 study of coffee and tea in Ethiopia and Uganda found that hired workers on Fairtrade sites were not better off. Fairtrade also does not change who controls processing and branding, where most value is added.

**Worked paragraph (essay on impacts of trade):** Trade earns exporting countries foreign currency, but the benefit depends on what is exported. *(Knowledge)* Zambia relies on copper for most export earnings, so its government revenue and currency rise and fall with world copper prices. *(Application)* This means a fall in copper prices cuts income while debt repayments still fall due, making debt harder to service; Zambia defaulted in 2020. *(Analysis)* So trade benefits commodity exporters mainly in boom years; diversified exporters such as manufacturing economies gain more steadily, which suggests the type of export matters more than the volume of trade. *(Evaluation)*

## 13.2 International debt and international aid

**Causes and nature of debt.** Countries borrow to fund infrastructure, cover trade deficits or respond to shocks. Debt becomes a problem when repayments (debt service) take a large share of export earnings or government revenue. Problems include cuts to health and education spending, currency crises and loss of control over policy to lenders.

**The international debt crisis.** After the 1973 oil price rise, oil exporters deposited large sums in Western banks, which lent them on to developing countries. From 1979 interest rates in the US and Europe rose sharply, so debt service soared. In August 1982 Mexico announced it could not service its debt, and the crisis spread across Latin America. IMF loans came with **structural adjustment programmes** -- austerity, privatisation and trade liberalisation. Urban real wages in Latin America fell sharply in the 1980s, which is why it is often called the "lost decade".

**Debt relief.** The **Heavily Indebted Poor Countries (HIPC) Initiative** was launched by the IMF and World Bank in 1996 and enhanced in 1999. Countries had to write a Poverty Reduction Strategy Paper and meet conditions to reach a "completion point". The **Multilateral Debt Relief Initiative** (2005) cancelled further debts. By early 2023, 36 countries had reached the completion point and received full relief. Critics said conditions such as privatising utilities raised costs for poor households.

Debt problems have returned. Zambia received HIPC relief but defaulted on a Eurobond payment in November 2020, the first African default of the coronavirus era. It restructured its debts under the G20 **Common Framework**, with bilateral lenders in 2023 and bondholders in 2024.

**Types of aid:**
- **Relief (emergency) aid** -- short-term help after a disaster: food, shelter, medical care.
- **Development aid** -- long-term help to improve living standards: schools, roads, water supply.
- **Tied aid** -- aid given on condition that the receiver buys goods or services from the donor.
- **Bilateral aid** -- from one government to another.
- **Multilateral aid** -- from many donors pooled through an international body such as the World Bank or a UN agency.

Donors also include NGOs and charities. In 1970 the UN set a target for rich countries to give 0.7% of gross national income as official aid. Few donors meet it, and the UK cut its aid from 0.7% to 0.5% in 2021.

**Evaluating the impacts of aid.** Relief aid saves lives. Development aid can build lasting infrastructure and services. But aid can create dependency, and food aid can undercut local farmers. Tied aid returns money to donor firms and may supply unsuitable or overpriced goods. Aid can also be shaped by donor interests: UK aid for the Pergau Dam in Malaysia was linked to an arms deal and was ruled unlawful by the High Court in 1994. Multilateral aid is less tied to one donor's interests but often carries policy conditions.

**Worked paragraph (aid essay):** Tied aid can limit the benefit to receiving countries. *(Knowledge)* The Pergau Dam case showed aid being used to secure a commercial and arms deal for the donor. *(Application)* When aid is shaped by donor interests, projects may not match local priorities, and some of the money flows back to firms in the donor country. *(Analysis)* So the impact of aid depends less on its amount than on who controls it; untied, locally planned aid is more likely to reach the people who need it. *(Evaluation)*

## 13.3 The development of international tourism

**Reasons for growth:** rising incomes and paid holidays; cheaper air travel (wide-bodied jets, low-cost airlines); online booking and reviews; more destinations opening to tourists; marketing by governments. International arrivals passed 1 billion in 2012. They fell by around 70% in 2020 because of COVID-19, then recovered to about 1.4 billion in 2024, close to the 2019 level (UN Tourism figures).

**Impacts:**
- *Economic* -- jobs, foreign exchange, tax income and infrastructure. But jobs can be seasonal and low-paid, and **leakage** sends profits abroad to foreign-owned hotels and tour operators.
- *Social* -- money for heritage and cultural events. But rising rents and prices can push residents out, and local culture can become a commodity.
- *Environmental* -- entry fees can fund conservation. But tourism brings erosion, water shortages, waste and aviation emissions.

**Carrying capacity** is the maximum use a place can sustain without unacceptable damage. Geographers separate *physical* (space available), *ecological* (what ecosystems can absorb) and *perceptual* or social carrying capacity (how many people residents and visitors will accept).

**The tourism multiplier effect.** Tourist spending creates direct income in hotels and attractions. Those businesses buy local supplies and pay wages, which are spent again (indirect and induced income). Leakage reduces the multiplier, so a destination that imports its food and fuel gains less.

**Recent developments:** ecotourism, cruise tourism, medical tourism, volunteer tourism, dark tourism and adventure tourism. **Ecotourism** is responsible travel to natural areas that conserves the environment, sustains the well-being of local people and includes education. It can be undermined by greenwashing and by long-haul flights, and in some places most ecotourism revenue leaves the country.

**Butler's tourism area life cycle (1980).** R. W. Butler proposed that resorts pass through stages: exploration, involvement, development, consolidation and stagnation, followed by either decline or rejuvenation.

*Evaluation:* the model is simple and useful for planning, because it warns that growth beyond carrying capacity leads to stagnation. But stages are hard to identify at the time, and the length of each stage varies widely. Some places, such as major cities, never seem to decline. The model assumes growth is driven from outside, but governments can step in at any stage. It is descriptive rather than predictive.

**Worked paragraph (life cycle essay):** Butler's model is most useful in explaining resort decline. *(Knowledge)* It predicts stagnation once visitor numbers exceed carrying capacity. *(Application)* A resort where arrivals have levelled off while hotels age fits the stagnation stage. *(Analysis)* But the model cannot tell planners when stagnation will start or whether rejuvenation will succeed, so it works better as a description of the past than a forecast. *(Evaluation)*

## 13.4 Case study: Venice, Italy

The syllabus requires one tourist area or resort. Venice suits every part of 13.4.

**Growth and development.** Venice was a stop on the 18th-century Grand Tour and a fashionable resort in the 19th century. Mass tourism and cruise ships turned it into one of the world's busiest destinations: estimates range from 22 million to 30 million visitors a year, with up to 60,000 on peak days. Its city and lagoon became a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1987.

**Sustainability issues.** The historic centre's population fell from about 95,000 in 1980 to about 50,000 in 2021, as rents rose and homes became short-term lets. Large cruise ships caused wave damage and pollution; in 2019 a cruise ship collided with a river boat. Flooding and subsidence threaten buildings, and the November 2019 flood was the highest since 1966.

**Management:**
- Ships over 25,000 tonnes were banned from the Giudecca Canal from August 2021.
- A day-tripper access fee began on 25 April 2024 (€5 on peak days); in 2025 it covered 54 days, at €10 for late registration.
- Tour groups were limited to 25 people from June 2024.
- The MOSE flood barrier was first raised in October 2020.
- A 2017 ban on new hotels did not cover short-term rentals.

**Evaluating impacts.** *Economy:* tourism supports jobs and businesses, but income is concentrated in the tourist trade and much spending by day-trippers is small. *Society:* depopulation is the clearest sign of unsustainability. *Environment:* the cruise ban reduced one pressure, but flooding threats remain. Phone data suggested more people visited on fee days in 2024, so the fee raised money but did not yet cut numbers. Venice fits Butler's stagnation stage: demand has not fallen, but the resident community and the built environment are under pressure.

## Common errors

- Calling tourism or banking "visible" exports. Services are invisible trade.
- Treating Fairtrade as a trade agreement between countries. It is a certification scheme.
- Mixing up tied aid and multilateral aid. Tied aid is about conditions; multilateral is about the channel.
- Writing that HIPC relief ended debt problems. Zambia's 2020 default shows debt can return.
- Describing Butler's stages without evaluating the model.
- Using a whole country as the 13.4 case study when a tourist area or resort is asked for.
