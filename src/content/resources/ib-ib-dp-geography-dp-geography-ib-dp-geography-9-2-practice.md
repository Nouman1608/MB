## Section A

**1.** State the two main processes that cause global sea level to rise. **[2]**

**2.** Distinguish between a sudden-onset and a slow-onset consequence of climate change, giving an example of each. **[2]**

**3.** Define relative sea-level change. **[1]**

**4.** Outline one way in which ocean acidification affects marine life. **[2]**

**5.** Suggest one way in which a high-latitude region might gain from a warmer climate. **[1]**

## Section B

**6.** A coastal town is on land that is subsiding by 6 mm per year. Global sea level is rising by 4 mm per year.

**(a)** Calculate the relative sea-level rise per year. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the total relative rise over 25 years, giving your answer in metres. **[2]**

**(c)** Suggest one human activity that could cause the land to subside. **[1]**

**7.** The table shows invented survey data on the effects of a drought in a farming district.

| Household group | Number of households | Households that sold livestock | Households where a child left school |
|---|---|---|---|
| Irrigated farms | 150 | 15 | 3 |
| Rain-fed farms | 250 | 125 | 40 |

**(a)** Calculate the percentage of each group that sold livestock. **[2]**

**(b)** Using the data, explain how a single drought can have cascading consequences for some households. **[3]**

**8.** Explain why the consequences of glacier retreat for river flow change over time. **[3]**

**9.** Explain how a physical consequence of climate change can lead to a political dispute between countries, using an example. **[3]**

## Section C

**10.** Examine why the consequences of climate change differ between places. **[10]**

**11.** "The most serious consequences of climate change are those that happen slowly." To what extent do you agree with this statement? **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** Thermal expansion of seawater as it warms [1]; melting of land ice from glaciers and the Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets [1]. **[2]**

**2.** A sudden-onset consequence arrives as a distinct event, such as a heatwave or flood [1]; a slow-onset consequence builds gradually over years, such as sea-level rise or glacier retreat [1]. **[2]**

**3.** The change in sea level measured against the local land, combining global sea-level change with local land subsidence or uplift [1]. **[1]**

**4.** The ocean absorbs carbon dioxide, lowering its pH [1], which makes it harder for shell-building organisms such as corals, molluscs and some plankton to form their shells and skeletons [1]. **[2]**

**5.** For example, a longer growing season allowing new crops to be grown, or shipping routes opening as sea ice shrinks [1]. **[1]**

**6. (a)** 4 + 6 = **10 mm per year** [1].

**(b)** 10 x 25 = 250 mm [1] = **0.25 m** [1].

**(c)** For example, pumping groundwater from beneath the town, or extracting gas [1]. **[4]**

**7. (a)** Irrigated: 15 / 150 x 100 = **10%** [1]. Rain-fed: 125 / 250 x 100 = **50%** [1].

**(b)** Rain-fed households lost crops directly to the drought, so half had to sell livestock to buy food [1]. Selling livestock removes savings and future income, so these households are less able to cope with the next shock [1]. Some also withdrew children from school (40 of 250, or 16%, compared with 2% of irrigated households), so the drought's effects pass on to the next generation's opportunities [1]. **[5]**

**8.** In the early stages of warming, faster melting releases more water, so river flow increases [1]. As the glacier shrinks, there is less ice to melt, so meltwater, and dry-season flow, declines [1]. The glacier therefore stops acting as a store that releases water in the dry season, so the timing and reliability of supply worsen over the long term [1]. **[3]**

**9.** Warming changes where resources are found, for example as fish stocks move into cooler waters [1]. In the North Atlantic, mackerel became more abundant in Icelandic and Faroese waters [1], which led from around 2010 to a long dispute with the European Union and Norway over how catches should be shared [1]. **[3]**

**10.** Indicative content; award one mark per valid, developed point up to 10:

- Physical exposure differs with latitude: the Arctic is warming fastest because of feedbacks such as ice loss [1].
- Low-lying coasts and deltas are more exposed to sea-level rise, especially where land is subsiding [1].
- Dry regions face greater water stress when rainfall patterns shift, while some wet regions face heavier downpours [1].
- Dependence on climate-sensitive livelihoods, such as rain-fed farming, increases harm [1].
- Wealth and infrastructure change outcomes: irrigation, cooling, insurance and defences reduce losses [1].
- Governance and planning affect how well warnings and responses work [1].
- Within a place, age, gender, income and housing quality create different outcomes for different groups [1].
- Some places may gain in some respects, such as longer growing seasons at high latitudes [1].
- Uses located examples accurately, such as Shishmaref in Alaska or Jakarta's subsidence [1].
- Conclusion: a supported judgement, for example that physical exposure sets the potential for harm but human factors decide how far it becomes damage [1]. **[10]**

**11.** Indicative content; award one mark per valid, developed point up to 10:

- Defines slow-onset (sea-level rise, glacier retreat, acidification, desertification) and sudden-onset (heatwaves, floods, storms) consequences [1].
- Slow-onset change can make places permanently uninhabitable, as relocation decisions in Arctic and island communities show [1].
- Slow-onset change is hard to see, so responses are delayed and funding is harder to secure [1].
- Slow changes such as glacier retreat undermine water supply for large populations over decades [1].
- Against: sudden-onset events cause most direct deaths, as heatwaves have shown [1].
- Against: sudden events cause large economic losses and can push households into poverty at once [1].
- The two interact: slow sea-level rise makes each storm surge more damaging [1].
- Seriousness depends on the place and group: a farming household may be hit hardest by drought, a coastal city by surge flooding [1].
- Uses accurate located evidence for both sides [1].
- Conclusion: a supported judgement, for example that slow-onset change may be more serious in the long term because it is irreversible, while sudden events are more serious for loss of life now [1]. **[10]**

*Examiner insight:* "To what extent" needs a clear position. State it early, test it against the opposite view with evidence, and return to it in the final sentence.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Adding subsidence and global rise incorrectly, or forgetting to convert millimetres to metres.
- Describing survey data without calculating or quoting the values that show the difference between groups.
- Saying glacier retreat simply "reduces water" without explaining the early increase in flow.
- Treating all places as equally affected, with no reference to exposure or capacity.
