This guide covers **subtopic 3.7 Environmental Risks of Economic
Development**, the final subtopic of Theme 3 in Cambridge O Level
Geography (2217), 2026 series (Version 3). The
[Economic Development study guide](/resources/o-level-geography-economic-development/)
explains why 3.7 pulls the theme together; this page goes into each
threat, the meaning of sustainable development, and a full case study.

## What 3.7 asks you to do

| Part | What you must be able to do |
|---|---|
| Threats | Describe how economic activities may pose threats to the natural environment and people, locally and globally |
| Sustainability | Demonstrate the need for sustainable development and management |
| Conservation | Understand the importance of resource conservation |
| Case study | An area where economic development is causing environmental risk |

The threats most often examined are **soil erosion**,
**desertification**, **enhanced global warming** and **pollution** of
water and air, together with noise and visual pollution.

## The threats, activity by activity

Strong answers trace each threat back to the economic activity that
causes it. Use this chain: *activity → process → threat → effect on
people and environment*.

**Soil erosion**

- *Activities*: clearing forest for farms, ranches, mines and roads;
  ploughing steep slopes; overgrazing.
- *Process*: without roots and a plant cover, heavy rain washes topsoil
  away and wind blows dry soil off bare fields.
- *Effects*: lower crop yields, gullies, and silt that fills rivers and
  reservoirs, increasing flood risk.

**Desertification**

- *Meaning*: land in dry areas turning into desert-like conditions.
- *Activities*: overgrazing by too many animals, over-cultivation that
  exhausts soil, and cutting trees and shrubs for fuelwood, made worse
  by drought.
- *Effects*: land can no longer support crops or animals, so food
  shortages and migration follow. The Sahel, on the southern edge of
  the Sahara, is a commonly studied example.

**Enhanced global warming**

- *Process*: burning fossil fuels in power stations, factories and
  vehicles, and clearing or burning forests, releases carbon dioxide.
  Methane comes from cattle, rice paddies and landfill. These gases
  trap more heat in the atmosphere, increasing the natural greenhouse
  effect.
- *Effects (global)*: rising temperatures, melting ice and sea-level
  rise that threatens low-lying coasts and islands, more extreme
  weather, and changes to where crops can grow.

**Pollution**

| Type | Typical sources | Effects |
|---|---|---|
| Water | Untreated sewage and factory waste; fertiliser and pesticide run-off; oil spills; mining waste | Unsafe drinking water and disease; fish deaths; algal blooms from excess nutrients |
| Air | Power stations, factories, vehicles, forest burning | Breathing illnesses; smog; acid rain from sulphur and nitrogen gases |
| Noise | Airports, motorways, quarries, factories | Stress and disturbed sleep; wildlife disturbance |
| Visual | Quarries, power lines, wind farms, large factories, litter | Loss of attractive landscapes, which can also harm tourism |

**Local and global scale.** A quarry's noise and dust are local; acid
rain can cross national borders; greenhouse gas emissions are global.
Questions often ask you to distinguish these scales.

## Sustainable development and management

**Sustainable development** is development that meets the needs of
people today without reducing the ability of future generations to meet
their own needs. It balances three things: economic growth, the
well-being of people, and protection of the environment.

Management strategies you can use:

- Laws and regulation: emissions limits, protected areas, planning
  controls, fines for pollution.
- Cleaner technology: treating waste water before discharge, filters on
  chimneys, switching from coal to renewable energy.
- Better farming: terracing and contour ploughing on slopes, planting
  trees as windbreaks, controlling herd sizes, crop rotation.
- Restoring damaged land: replanting forests, landscaping old quarries.
- Monitoring: satellite images to detect illegal clearance.
- International agreements: countries agreeing targets to reduce
  greenhouse gas emissions.

## Resource conservation

**Resource conservation** means using natural resources carefully so
that they last longer and cause less damage. Key ideas:

- **Reduce, reuse, recycle**: less waste and fewer new raw materials
  mined.
- Replacing non-renewable resources with renewable ones where possible.
- Using energy and water more efficiently.
- Managing renewable resources (forests, fish stocks, soils) so they
  are not used faster than they recover.

It matters because many resources are finite, because extracting them
causes pollution and habitat loss, and because future generations will
need them.

## Case study: the Amazon rainforest in Brazil

The Amazon is the world's largest tropical rainforest, and most of it
lies in Brazil. Since the 1970s, economic development has cleared large
areas of forest.

**Economic activities**

- **Cattle ranching**: the largest single cause of clearance; forest is
  felled and burned to create pasture.
- **Commercial farming**: soya is grown on cleared land, much of it for
  export as animal feed.
- **Logging**: valuable hardwoods are removed, and logging roads open the
  forest to settlers.
- **Mining**: the Carajás iron ore mine in Pará state is one of the
  largest in the world; small-scale gold mining often uses mercury.
- **Roads and HEP**: highways such as the Trans-Amazonian Highway opened
  up the region; the Belo Monte dam on the Xingu River flooded land and
  displaced people.

**Environmental risks**

- *Soil erosion and leaching*: rainforest soils are thin and lose their
  nutrients quickly once the canopy is removed and heavy rain falls on
  bare ground.
- *Enhanced global warming*: burning and clearing release stored carbon
  dioxide, and fewer trees remain to absorb it.
- *Water pollution*: mercury from gold mining enters rivers and builds
  up in fish eaten by local people; silt from eroded land clogs rivers.
- *Loss of biodiversity*: habitats for very large numbers of plant and
  animal species are destroyed.
- *Threats to people*: indigenous communities lose land and ways of
  life, and conflicts over land occur.

**Management**

- Protected areas and indigenous territories where clearance is
  restricted.
- Brazil's Forest Code, which requires landowners in the Amazon to keep
  a large share of their land as forest.
- Satellite monitoring by Brazil's National Institute for Space
  Research (INPE) to detect clearance quickly.
- The Soy Moratorium (agreed in 2006), under which major traders agreed
  not to buy soya from newly deforested Amazon land.

**Evaluation**: deforestation fell sharply during the late 2000s when
enforcement was strong, but it has risen again in some later years,
which shows that management depends on political will and enforcement,
not only on laws.

## Worked example: data and explanation

**Illustrative data**: forest cover in a fictional district fell from
8,400 km² in 2005 to 6,300 km² in 2025.

```
Loss:              8,400 - 6,300 = 2,100 km²
Percentage loss:   2,100 / 8,400 x 100 = 25%
Average per year:  2,100 / 20 = 105 km² per year
```

A follow-up "explain" question might ask how this loss could increase
flood risk downstream: fewer trees intercept rain, more water runs
quickly over the surface, and eroded soil fills river channels, so
rivers overflow more easily.

## Common mistakes

- Naming a threat ("pollution") without the activity that causes it.
- Confusing the natural greenhouse effect with *enhanced* global
  warming.
- Describing desertification as "deserts spreading naturally" with no
  human causes.
- Defining sustainable development as "development that lasts".
- Case studies without named places, activities or schemes.

## Exam technique

- **Describe** threats with specific effects on people *and* the
  environment.
- **Explain** using the activity → process → threat chain.
- For **evaluate** or "how far" questions about management, judge how
  effective it has been and why.
