This guide covers section 1.1.5, The mixed economy, of the Pearson Edexcel International GCSE Economics (4EC1) specification, Issue 3, February 2026. It is examined in Paper 1, Microeconomics and Business Economics, with a single tier of entry. All countries, firms and figures below are invented.

## What the specification asks for

| Point | Wording |
|---|---|
| a, b | Definitions of mixed economy, public sector and private sector |
| c | Difference between the sectors in terms of ownership, control and aims |
| d | How the problems of what, how and for whom to produce are solved in the mixed economy |
| e, f | Market failure, linked to inefficient allocation of resources; why governments might need to intervene |
| g | Public goods: non-excludability, non-rivalry and the free rider problem |
| h, i | The role of each sector in producing goods and services; their relative importance in different economies |
| j, k | Definition of privatisation; its effects on consumers, workers, businesses and government |

## The mixed economy in one sentence (points a and b)

A **mixed economy** allocates resources through both the **private sector** (individuals and firms, using the price mechanism) and the **public sector** (central and local government and state-owned organisations).

It helps to picture a scale. At one end, nearly all decisions would be made by markets; at the other, nearly all would be made by government. Real economies sit somewhere between, which is why the specification asks about the *relative importance* of the two sectors.

## Ownership, control and aims (point c)

When a question asks for a difference, compare like with like in one sentence:

- **Ownership:** a private-sector firm is owned by individuals or shareholders, *whereas* a public-sector organisation is owned by the state on behalf of citizens.
- **Control:** private firms are run by owners or the managers they appoint, *whereas* public organisations are controlled by government ministers, local councils or officials they appoint.
- **Aims:** private firms usually aim for profit, *whereas* public organisations usually aim to provide a service for the public, such as fair access, even if this does not make a profit.

A point stated for one sector only ("private firms want profit") is only half a difference. The word *whereas* forces the comparison.

## What, how and for whom (point d)

| Problem | Private sector's answer | Public sector's answer |
|---|---|---|
| What to produce | Goods consumers are willing and able to pay for, signalled by prices and profit | Goods the market will not provide, such as public goods, or will provide too little of |
| How to produce | The lowest-cost method, to raise profit | Methods set by public budgets and rules, such as safety and environmental laws that also apply to private firms |
| For whom to produce | People with the income to pay | People in need, through free or subsidised services and benefits funded by taxation |

The mixed economy's answer is a combination: prices allocate most goods, and government adjusts the outcome where it judges the market's answer to be inefficient or unfair.

## Market failure and why governments intervene (points e and f)

**Market failure** occurs when the free market leads to an **inefficient allocation of resources**: too much of some goods, too little of others, or none at all of goods that society values. Section 1.1.5 links this to public goods; section 1.1.6 adds externalities.

Why governments might need to intervene:

1. **Nothing is provided.** Public goods would not be supplied at all by profit-seeking firms.
2. **The wrong quantity is provided.** Goods with external costs are over-produced and goods with external benefits are under-consumed.
3. **Basic needs go unmet.** Allocation by ability to pay may leave poorer households without essentials.

Chain to use: the market ignores some costs or benefits, or cannot charge users → resources go to the wrong uses → society's welfare is lower than it could be → government intervenes to change the allocation.

## Public goods (point g)

A **public good** is both:

- **non-excludable:** people who do not pay cannot be stopped from benefiting, and
- **non-rival:** one person's use does not reduce the amount available to others.

### Testing goods

| Good | Excludable? | Rival? | Public good? |
|---|---|---|---|
| A lighthouse | No: every ship nearby sees it | No: one ship's use leaves the light for others | Yes |
| National defence | No | No | Yes |
| A public fireworks display seen from the streets | No | No | Yes |
| A football match in a ticketed stadium | Yes: tickets | Partly: seats run out | No |
| A school place | Yes: places can be limited | Yes | No |
| A sandwich | Yes | Yes | No |

### Worked example: the free rider problem

*Invented figures.* A town of 5 000 households would enjoy a fireworks display costing 20 000. Each household values it at 6.

```
Total benefit = 5 000 × 6 = 30 000   (greater than cost of 20 000)
```

A private firm asks for voluntary payment. Because the display can be seen from the streets, most households free ride: only 1 200 pay 6 each.

```
Revenue = 1 200 × 6 = 7 200   → shortfall = 20 000 − 7 200 = 12 800
```

No firm will put on the display, although it is worth more than it costs. If the council funds it from local taxes of 20 000 ÷ 5 000 = 4 per household, each household gains 6 − 4 = 2. This is the reason public goods are usually provided by the public sector.

## The roles and relative importance of each sector (points h and i)

**Roles.** The private sector produces most consumer goods and many services. The public sector produces public goods (defence, street lighting, flood defences) and often provides or funds education, healthcare, policing and some transport. Many services are provided by both, for example private and state schools.

**Relative importance.** Economists compare economies using measures such as government spending as a share of national output, or the share of workers employed in the public sector.

*Invented data:*

| Country | Public-sector workers (millions) | Total workers (millions) | Public-sector share |
|---|---|---|---|
| L | 6.3 | 18.0 | 35% |
| M | 1.8 | 24.0 | 7.5% |

Working: 6.3 ÷ 18.0 × 100 = 35%; 1.8 ÷ 24.0 × 100 = 7.5%. Country L's public sector is far more important as an employer. Reasons such shares differ include political choices about taxation and services, the level of income (richer countries can raise more tax), and history, such as how many industries were once state-owned.

## Privatisation (points j and k)

**Privatisation** is the transfer of ownership of a business or asset from the public sector to the private sector.

### Worked example: a state telephone company

*Invented figures.* Country N sells its telephone company for 600 million. Before sale the state paid it a subsidy of 40 million a year.

| | Before | Two years after |
|---|---|---|
| Monthly line charge | 20 | 17 |
| Employees | 12 000 | 9 600 |
| Rival firms allowed | 0 | 2 |

- Price change: (17 − 20) ÷ 20 × 100 = **−15%**.
- Job change: (9 600 − 12 000) ÷ 12 000 × 100 = **−20%**, or 2 400 jobs.
- Sale proceeds equal 600 ÷ 40 = **15 years** of the old subsidy.

| Group | Effects in this case | What it depends on |
|---|---|---|
| Consumers | Lower prices and more choice, as rivals entered | Whether competition is allowed; a private monopoly could raise prices instead |
| Workers | 2 400 jobs lost; remaining staff may gain from profit-linked pay | How fast the firm grows |
| Businesses | The privatised firm now seeks profit and efficiency; rivals can enter the market | Regulation and barriers to entry |
| Government | 600 million raised once; 40 million a year saved; loss of direct control and any future profits | How the proceeds are used |

The key evaluation is that the effects depend on competition: here prices fell because two rivals entered, which would not happen if the firm kept a monopoly.

## Common mistakes

- Describing one sector only when asked for a difference.
- Calling any government-provided service a public good.
- Defining the free rider problem as people refusing to use a good. Free riders use it without paying.
- Treating market failure as "the market collapsing". It is an inefficient allocation of resources.
- Saying privatisation always lowers prices, or always raises them.

## Exam technique

- **Define / What is meant by:** two parts, such as "owned by the state" and "provides services for the public".
- **Explain:** statement plus two developments, for example non-excludable → free riders → no revenue for a firm.
- **Calculate:** show the division before multiplying by 100.
- **Assess / Evaluate:** weigh effects on different groups and say what the outcome depends on.
