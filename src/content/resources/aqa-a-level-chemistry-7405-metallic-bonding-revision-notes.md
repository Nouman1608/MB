These notes condense section **3.1.3.3 Metallic bonding** of the AQA AS and A-level Chemistry specification (7404/7405), for teaching from September 2015. It is AS content, examined on Papers 1 and 2 (and Paper 3). The [Metallic bonding study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-metallic-bonding/) explains each idea with worked examples, and the [Metallic bonding practice questions](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-metallic-bonding-practice/) give exam-style practice.

## What the specification asks

- Metallic bonding involves **attraction between delocalised electrons and positive ions arranged in a lattice**.
- Magnesium is one of the named examples of a metallic crystal in 3.1.3.4, where its melting point and conductivity are related to the bonding.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Metallic bond | Electrostatic attraction between positive ions and delocalised electrons |
| Delocalised electron | An outer electron not held by one atom, free to move through the structure |
| Lattice | Regular three-dimensional arrangement of particles |
| Giant structure | Bonding that extends through the whole crystal |
| Malleable | Can be hammered or pressed into shape without breaking |
| Ductile | Can be drawn into wires |
| Charge density | Charge relative to size; higher for small ions with high charge |

## The model in four lines

1. Each metal atom loses its outer electrons to a shared pool.
2. The structure is a lattice of **positive ions**.
3. The outer electrons are **delocalised**, free to move through the lattice.
4. The bond is the **electrostatic attraction** between the ions and the electrons, acting in all directions.

Drawing: at least three rows of circles with their charges (for example 2+), with small dots or e⁻ between them.

## Strength of metallic bonding

| Factor | Stronger bonding when |
|---|---|
| Charge on the ion | Higher (more delocalised electrons per atom) |
| Ion radius | Smaller (electrons closer to the positive nuclei) |

| Metal | Ion | Delocalised e⁻ per atom | Melting point / °C |
|---|---|---|---|
| Na | Na⁺ | 1 | 98 |
| Mg | Mg²⁺ | 2 | 650 |
| Al | Al³⁺ | 3 | 660 |

- **Across a period** (Na to Al): charge increases and ions get smaller, so the bonding is stronger and the melting point rises.
- **Down a group** (Li to K): the same charge but larger ions, so the attraction is weaker and the melting point falls.

## Properties and explanations

| Property | Explanation |
|---|---|
| High melting and boiling point | Strong electrostatic attraction throughout the lattice; much energy needed |
| Electrical conduction, solid and liquid | Delocalised electrons are free to move and carry charge |
| Thermal conduction | Delocalised electrons pass kinetic energy quickly through the lattice |
| Malleable and ductile | Layers of ions slide; delocalised electrons keep attracting the ions in the new position |
| Not soluble in water | The metallic bonding is not replaced by stronger attractions to water |

## Metals compared with ionic solids

| Feature | Metal | Ionic solid |
|---|---|---|
| Mobile particles when solid | Delocalised electrons | None; ions fixed |
| Conduction as solid | Yes | No |
| Conduction when molten | Yes | Yes (mobile ions) |
| When struck | Bends | Shatters |
| Reason | Layers slide, bonding continues | Layers shift and like charges repel |

## Worked reminders

**Describe metallic bonding.** "A lattice of positive ions held together by electrostatic attraction to delocalised electrons that are free to move throughout the structure."

**Why does magnesium melt at a higher temperature than sodium?** Mg²⁺ has a higher charge than Na⁺ and is smaller, and each magnesium atom supplies two delocalised electrons, not one. The electrostatic attraction is stronger, so more energy is needed to overcome it.

**Why does potassium melt at a lower temperature than lithium?** The ions have the same charge, but K⁺ is larger. The delocalised electrons are further from the nuclei, so the attraction is weaker.

**Why does a metal conduct when solid?** The delocalised electrons can move through the lattice when a potential difference is applied, even though the ions stay in place.

**Why does a metal not shatter when hit?** Layers slide, and the delocalised electrons continue to hold the positive ions together in the new arrangement.

## Wording that gains marks

- "positive ions" (not "atoms")
- "delocalised electrons" or "electrons free to move"
- "electrostatic attraction"
- "more energy is needed to overcome"
- "layers of ions slide over each other"

## Common mistakes

- Writing "metal atoms in a lattice" when "positive ions" is needed.
- Saying that ions carry current in a solid metal.
- Writing that metals have "free electrons" without saying that they are delocalised and that the positive ions remain fixed.
- Ignoring ion size when comparing melting points down a group.
- Saying that metallic bonds "break and reform" when a metal bends.
- Forgetting to link conduction to a **potential difference**.

## Self-test

1. Describe the bonding in a metal.
2. Define the term "delocalised electron".
3. Explain why aluminium has a higher melting point than sodium.
4. Explain why rubidium has a lower melting point than sodium.
5. Why does a metal conduct electricity when solid?
6. Explain why a metal can be bent without breaking.
7. State one similarity and one difference between the structures of magnesium and magnesium oxide.
8. A student says "In a metal, positive ions carry the current". Correct the statement.
9. How many delocalised electrons does each atom provide in calcium?
10. Why do metals conduct heat well?
11. State which would have the greater electrostatic attraction between ion and delocalised electrons: Mg or Ca? Explain.
12. Draw a labelled diagram of metallic bonding in magnesium.

### Answers

1. A lattice of positive ions with delocalised electrons that are free to move; electrostatic attraction between the ions and the electrons holds the structure together.
2. An outer electron that is not attached to a particular atom or bond but is free to move through the structure.
3. Al³⁺ has a higher charge and is smaller than Na⁺, and each atom provides three delocalised electrons instead of one, so the electrostatic attraction is stronger and more energy is needed to melt it.
4. Rb⁺ is larger than Na⁺ (more electron shells) with the same charge, so the delocalised electrons are further from the positive nuclei and the attraction is weaker.
5. Its delocalised electrons are free to move through the lattice and carry charge.
6. Layers of positive ions slide over each other, and the delocalised electrons keep the ions attracted to each other in the new positions.
7. Similarity: both are giant lattices with strong electrostatic attraction. Difference: magnesium has delocalised electrons that are mobile in the solid, while magnesium oxide has ions fixed in the lattice (so it conducts only when molten or dissolved).
8. The delocalised electrons carry the current; the positive ions stay in fixed positions in the lattice.
9. Two.
10. Delocalised electrons carry kinetic energy rapidly through the lattice.
11. Mg: Mg²⁺ is smaller than Ca²⁺, so its delocalised electrons are closer and the attraction is stronger.
12. Rows of circles labelled 2+ with small electrons (e⁻) between them, and a label for the delocalised electrons and for the attraction.
