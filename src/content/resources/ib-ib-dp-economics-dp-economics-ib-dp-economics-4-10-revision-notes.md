These notes condense sub-topic **4.10 Economic growth and/or economic development strategies** from the IB Diploma Programme *Economics guide* (first assessment 2022). It is SL and HL content. For full explanations and worked examples read the [4.10 study guide](/resources/ib-ib-dp-economics-dp-economics-ib-dp-economics-4-10/); then test yourself with the [4.10 practice questions](/resources/ib-ib-dp-economics-dp-economics-ib-dp-economics-4-10-practice/). Countries and figures are invented.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Import substitution | Protecting and supporting domestic producers so they replace imports |
| Export promotion | Supporting firms that sell abroad to grow through exports |
| Diversification | Spreading production and exports across more goods and markets |
| Social enterprise | A business with social or environmental aims that reinvests most of its profit |
| Trade liberalisation | Reducing tariffs, quotas and other trade barriers |
| Privatisation | Transfer of state-owned assets to private owners |
| Deregulation | Removal of rules that restrict business activity |
| Inward FDI | Investment by foreign firms in production facilities in the country |
| Official Development Assistance (ODA) | Aid given by governments to developing countries |
| Debt relief | Cancelling or reducing a country's debt |
| Conditionality | Policy conditions attached to multilateral loans |
| Microfinance | Small loans, savings and other financial services for people on low incomes |

## Each strategy: one strength, one limit

| Strategy | Strength | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| Import substitution | Builds domestic industry; saves foreign exchange | Protected firms may stay inefficient; consumers pay more |
| Export promotion | Earns foreign exchange; economies of scale | Depends on foreign demand; costly subsidies |
| Economic integration | Wider markets and competition | Weaker domestic firms lose out |
| Diversification | Less exposure to one price shock | Slow; needs skills and capital |
| Social enterprise | Reaches groups profit-seekers ignore | Often small and short of finance |
| Trade liberalisation | Cheaper inputs, competition, export access | Exposes infant industries |
| Privatisation | Efficiency; revenue for the state | Private monopoly risk; job losses |
| Deregulation | Lower costs of starting firms | Weaker protection for workers and environment |
| Redistribution | Cuts inequality; supports human capital | Cost; possible disincentive effects |
| Merit goods and infrastructure | Raise human capital; lower firms' costs | High up-front cost; slow returns |
| Inward FDI | Capital, jobs, skills, technology | Profit outflows; footloose firms |
| Humanitarian and development aid | Fills funding gaps; saves lives | Dependence; corruption; tied to donor goods |
| Debt relief | Frees revenue for spending | Only helps if the revenue is used well |
| World Bank and IMF | Access to finance and expertise | Conditions may cut social spending |
| Institutional change | Tackles root causes | Slow; needs enforcement |

## Matching rule

Barrier first, strategy second: primary dependence → diversification; low human capital → merit goods; weak credit and property rights → microfinance and land titling; debt → relief and export growth; corruption → institutional reform.

## Methods

**Net gain from FDI** = wages paid locally + local purchases + profit kept in the country - tax incentives given.

*Worked reminder.* A plant employs 800 workers at 4,000 a year (3.2 million), buys 6 million of local inputs, earns 20 million profit and sends 75% (15 million) abroad, so 5 million stays. A tax break costs 3 million. Net gain = 3.2 + 6 + 5 - 3 = **11.2 million a year**.

**Microfinance return** = extra income - interest - repayment of the loan.

*Worked reminder.* Loan 300 at 24%: interest 72. Extra income 45 a month = 540 a year. Left after interest and repayment: 540 - 72 - 300 = **168**.

**Debt relief effect** = change in the debt service ratio and the revenue freed.

*Worked reminder.* Debt service falls from 1.5 billion to 1.0 billion with exports of 5.0 billion: the ratio falls from 30% to 20%, freeing 0.5 billion, a 25% rise for a 2.0 billion health budget.

## Market-oriented vs interventionist

- **Market-oriented:** efficient, competitive, low fiscal cost; but under-provides merit goods and may widen inequality.
- **Interventionist:** supplies what markets will not; but risks government failure and high cost.
- **Judgement:** a mix, with the balance set by the strength of institutions and the barrier targeted.

## SDGs

Seventeen goals adopted by the United Nations in 2015 for 2030. To judge progress: compare change per year, allow for starting points, and note what the indicator leaves out (quality, distribution).

## Exam traps

- Praising a strategy that does not fit the country's barrier.
- Counting FDI's total profit as a gain to the host.
- Treating all aid as the same.
- Ignoring conditionality when discussing the IMF and World Bank.
- Comparing SDG levels without looking at rates of change.

## Quick self-test

1. Distinguish import substitution from export promotion.
2. Which strategy best targets dependence on a single export crop?
3. Give one strength and one limitation of privatisation.
4. Why might a tax holiday reduce the net gain from FDI?
5. A plant pays 2 million in wages, buys 1 million of local inputs and keeps 0.5 million of profit locally; tax incentives cost 1 million. Calculate the net gain.
6. Distinguish humanitarian aid from development aid.
7. What is conditionality?
8. A loan of 200 at 20% lets a tailor earn 30 more a month. What is left after a year once interest and the loan are repaid?
9. Give one limitation of microfinance.
10. Why does governance affect how well aid works?
11. Give one argument for an interventionist approach.
12. Country A's water access rose from 45% to 60% in ten years; Country B's from 70% to 78%. Which improved faster per year?

### Answers

1. Import substitution protects domestic producers to replace imports; export promotion supports firms to sell abroad.
2. Diversification.
3. Strength: efficiency or revenue for the state. Limitation: private monopoly, job losses or poorer service for low-income users.
4. It reduces the tax revenue the host country receives from the firm.
5. 2 + 1 + 0.5 - 1 = **2.5 million**.
6. Humanitarian aid meets emergencies in the short term; development aid funds long-term projects such as schools.
7. Policy conditions attached to loans from multilateral lenders.
8. Extra income 360; interest 40; 360 - 40 - 200 = **120**.
9. Small loan sizes, high interest rates, or repayments starting before income rises.
10. Where institutions are weak, aid may be diverted by corruption or spent on low-priority projects.
11. Markets under-provide merit goods such as education and health, so the state must provide or fund them.
12. Country A: 1.5 points a year against 0.8 for B, so A improved faster, although from a lower base.
