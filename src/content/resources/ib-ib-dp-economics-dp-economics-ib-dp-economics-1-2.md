## Why method matters in economics

Economists cannot usually run controlled laboratory experiments on whole economies. They cannot rerun a recession with a different interest rate to see what would have happened. So they rely on **logic**, **simplifying assumptions**, **models** and **observed data**, and they accept that conclusions are provisional. Knowing this lets you write the cautious, conditional judgements that evaluation questions reward.

## Positive and normative economics: the hard cases

A **positive** statement is about what is, was or will be, and can in principle be tested against evidence. A **normative** statement contains a value judgement about what ought to be. Two traps catch many students.

**Trap 1: a positive statement can be false.** "Raising the minimum wage in Velsa by 10% will cut youth employment by 40%" may be wildly wrong, but it is still positive, because data could test it.

**Trap 2: a statement with numbers can be normative.** "Velsa's government should keep inflation below 2%" contains a figure but is normative, because "should" signals a value judgement.

| Statement | Type | Signal |
|---|---|---|
| "Unemployment in Velsa was 6.1% last year." | Positive | Checkable fact |
| "If bus fares fall, more people will travel by bus." | Positive | Testable prediction |
| "Inequality in Velsa is too high." | Normative | "Too high" depends on values |
| "The tax on sugary drinks is unfair to poor households." | Normative | "Unfair" is a value judgement |
| "The tax on sugary drinks takes a larger share of income from poor households than rich ones." | Positive | Measurable claim |

The last two rows show how one issue can generate both types. Economists use positive analysis to inform normative choices, but data alone cannot settle what is fair.

## Models, assumptions and ceteris paribus

A **model** is a simplified representation of reality that isolates the relationships that matter for a question. The demand and supply diagram, the PPC and the circular flow are all models. Simplification is a strength, not a flaw, as long as the assumptions suit the question.

**Ceteris paribus** ("other things being equal") lets an economist change one variable while holding the rest constant, so its effect can be isolated. A demand curve, for example, shows how quantity demanded responds to price with income, tastes and other prices held constant.

Common simplifying assumptions include:

- **Rational behaviour:** consumers aim to maximise utility and firms aim to maximise profit. HL students later study the critique of this assumption in [behavioural economics](/resources/ib-dp-economics-critique-of-maximising-behaviour/).
- **Perfect information:** buyers and sellers know prices and quality.
- **Two goods only:** as in the PPC.

The test of a model is whether it predicts or explains well enough for the purpose, not whether it is perfectly realistic.

## Testing a hypothesis: a worked example

A **hypothesis** is a testable statement of a relationship. Economists form one through logic, test it against **empirical evidence**, and keep it only while evidence does not contradict it. Evidence that contradicts it means **refutation**: the hypothesis must be rejected or revised.

**Hypothesis:** "A rise in household income in Velsa, ceteris paribus, raises spending on restaurant meals."

**Evidence collected:**

| Year | Average household income (velsi) | Restaurant spending per household (velsi) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 40,000 | 1,200 |
| 2 | 42,000 | 1,320 |
| 3 | 44,100 | 1,150 |

- Year 1 to 2: income rose (42,000 - 40,000) / 40,000 = 5%, and restaurant spending rose (1,320 - 1,200) / 1,200 = 10%. This supports the hypothesis.
- Year 2 to 3: income rose again by 5%, but spending fell by (1,320 - 1,150) / 1,320 ≈ 12.9%. On its face this contradicts the hypothesis.

Before refuting it, check whether ceteris paribus held. Suppose a health scare closed many restaurants in year 3. Then "other things" did not stay equal, and the fall says nothing clear about the effect of income. A careful economist would look for more data, for example from regions without the closures. This is why the guide stresses that economics is a social science: real data rarely hold other things constant.

## Economic thought, era by era

The guide asks you to understand how economic ideas developed. Learn each idea with one link to the course.

### 18th century: Adam Smith and laissez faire

Adam Smith's *The Wealth of Nations* (1776) argued that individuals pursuing their own interest in competitive markets can, as if guided by an "invisible hand", promote the wider good. The policy implication is **laissez faire**: limited state interference. *Course link:* the efficiency of competitive markets in Unit 2.

### 19th century: classical and marginal ideas, and a critique

- **Classical macroeconomics and Say's law:** associated with Jean-Baptiste Say, the idea that supply creates its own demand, so general overproduction and lasting unemployment should not occur.
- **Utility and the margin:** in the 1870s, economists including William Stanley Jevons, Carl Menger and Léon Walras developed marginal analysis, which explains choices by the extra (marginal) benefit and cost of one more unit. Alfred Marshall's *Principles of Economics* (1890) brought demand and supply analysis together. *Course link:* marginal utility, marginal cost and the demand and supply model.
- **The Marxist critique:** Karl Marx argued that capitalism produces conflict between owners of capital and workers and is prone to crisis. *Course link:* debates on inequality and the role of the state.

### 20th century: Keynes and the counter-revolution

- **The Keynesian revolution:** John Maynard Keynes, in *The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money* (1936), argued that aggregate demand can be too low for long periods, leaving unemployment that markets will not quickly remove. This supported **active macroeconomic policy**. *Course link:* the Keynesian AS curve and demand-side policy in [3.2 aggregate demand and aggregate supply](/resources/ib-ib-dp-economics-dp-economics-ib-dp-economics-3-2/).
- **The monetarist and new classical counter-revolution:** Milton Friedman stressed the role of the money supply in inflation, and new classical economists such as Robert Lucas stressed rational expectations. Both argued that the economy tends towards its potential output and that demand management cannot permanently reduce unemployment. *Course link:* the vertical long-run AS curve.

### 21st century: psychology and sustainability

- **Behavioural economics:** work by Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky, and later Richard Thaler, showed systematic departures from rational choice, such as biases and rules of thumb. *Course link:* nudges and choice architecture in HL 2.4.
- **Economy, society and environment:** growing awareness that economic activity depends on the natural environment has led to interest in the **circular economy**, where materials are reused rather than discarded, and to frameworks such as Kate Raworth's "doughnut", which sets a social foundation against an ecological ceiling. *Course link:* [sustainable development](/resources/ib-dp-economics-sustainable-development/).

## Annotated model answer

**Question:** Explain why economists use the assumption of ceteris paribus. [4]

> Economists want to know the effect of one variable on another, for example the effect of price on the quantity of coffee demanded. **(identifies purpose)** In reality many variables change at once, such as incomes and the prices of substitutes. **(explains the problem)** Ceteris paribus holds those other variables constant in the model, so the effect of price alone can be isolated and shown as a movement along the demand curve. **(explains the mechanism with an example)** The limitation is that real data rarely meet this condition, so conclusions drawn from observed data need care. **(adds a qualification)**

## Common mistakes

- Classifying any statement with a number as positive.
- Treating "positive" as meaning "true" or "optimistic".
- Saying a model is useless because it is unrealistic, without asking whether it predicts well.
- Listing thinkers without the idea and the course link: names alone earn little.
- Placing Keynes in the 19th century or Smith in the 20th: learn the era table.

## Exam technique

- **Distinguish** between positive and normative: define both, then give a contrasting pair of statements on the same issue.
- **Explain** the role of models: purpose, how ceteris paribus works, one limitation.
- In **evaluate** answers anywhere in the course, flag that conclusions rest on assumptions ("if consumers respond rationally…") and on value judgements ("if equity is the priority…"). That habit comes straight from 1.2.
- Paper 1 essays on policy often reward a sentence contrasting a Keynesian view with a monetarist or new classical view.
