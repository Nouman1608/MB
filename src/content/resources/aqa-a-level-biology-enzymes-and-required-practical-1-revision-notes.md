## Key definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Enzyme | A globular protein that acts as a biological catalyst |
| Activation energy | The energy needed to start a reaction |
| Active site | The region of the enzyme where the substrate binds |
| Enzyme-substrate complex | Enzyme and substrate bound together at the active site |
| Complementary | Having a shape (and charge pattern) that matches |
| Denaturation | Loss of the tertiary structure, so the active site changes shape |
| Specificity | Each enzyme binds one substrate, or a very small group, because of its active site shape |
| Intracellular enzyme | Works inside the cell that made it |
| Extracellular enzyme | Secreted and works outside the cell |

## How enzymes work

1. Substrate collides with the active site.
2. Substrate binds, forming the **enzyme-substrate complex**.
3. The active site changes shape slightly (**induced fit**), straining the substrate bonds or bringing two substrates together.
4. The reaction takes place at a lower activation energy.
5. Product leaves. The enzyme is unchanged and can be used again.

Tertiary structure fixes the shape of the active site. A different amino acid sequence can change the tertiary structure and the active site. That is why specificity is a property of the active site.

## Models over time

| Model | Date and scientist | Active site | Limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lock and key | Emil Fischer, 1894 | Rigid, fixed shape | Does not explain strain on the substrate |
| Induced fit | Daniel Koshland, 1958 | Flexible, moulds around the substrate | The model to use in answers |

## Intracellular and extracellular enzymes

- **Intracellular:** catalase breaks down hydrogen peroxide; DNA polymerase copies DNA.
- **Extracellular:** amylase (salivary glands and pancreas) and trypsin (pancreas) digest food in the gut.
- Enzymes control reactions from the cell level (respiration) to the whole organism (digestion).

## Effects on rate

| Factor | Effect on rate | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Temperature, up to optimum | Rises | More kinetic energy, more collisions, more complexes |
| Temperature, above optimum | Falls sharply | Bonds in tertiary structure break, active site changes, denatured |
| Low temperature | Slow, not denatured | Little kinetic energy |
| pH away from optimum | Falls | Ionic and hydrogen bonds disrupted, active site changes |
| Enzyme concentration | Rises (excess substrate) | More active sites |
| Substrate concentration | Rises, then plateaus | Plateau: all active sites occupied, enzyme limits |

### Inhibitors

| Feature | Competitive | Non-competitive |
|---|---|---|
| Shape | Similar to the substrate | Not like the substrate |
| Binds at | The active site | Another site on the enzyme |
| Effect on enzyme | Blocks the active site | Changes the tertiary structure, so the active site changes |
| Raise substrate concentration | Effect reduced, same maximum reached | No recovery, lower maximum |
| Raise inhibitor concentration | Rate falls | Rate falls |

## Method in steps: pH calculation (MS 0.5)

**pH = -log₁₀ [H⁺]** with [H⁺] in mol dm⁻³.

- From concentration to pH: [H⁺] = 4.0 × 10⁻⁴ gives pH = **3.40**.
- From pH to concentration: [H⁺] = 10⁻ᵖᴴ. pH 3.5 gives 10⁻³·⁵ = **3.2 × 10⁻⁴ mol dm⁻³**.
- One unit lower in pH means ten times more H⁺. Two units means 100 times.

## Method in steps: Required Practical 1

1. Choose the **independent variable** (temperature, pH, enzyme concentration, substrate concentration or inhibitor concentration).
2. Control everything else: volumes, concentrations, pH, temperature, time.
3. Bring solutions to temperature in a water bath before mixing.
4. Measure a **rate**: volume of product against time, or time taken for a change (rate = 1 ÷ time).
5. Repeat and calculate a mean. Look for anomalies.
6. Plot rate against the independent variable.
7. Find an initial rate from a tangent at time 0.

### Tangent reminder (MS 3.6)

Product concentration rises from 0 and the tangent at time 0 passes through (0, 0) and (20 s, 5.0 mmol dm⁻³). Initial rate = 5.0 ÷ 20 = **0.25 mmol dm⁻³ s⁻¹**. Quote the unit as quantity per unit time. The mean rate over a longer period is lower, because the curve flattens.

### Uncertainty and dilution

- Percentage uncertainty = (absolute uncertainty ÷ measured value) × 100. A reading of 25.0 cm³ with ±0.5 cm³ gives **2.0%**.
- Dilution: stock volume = (required concentration ÷ stock concentration) × final volume. For 20 cm³ at 0.8% from a 2.5% stock: (0.8 ÷ 2.5) × 20 = **6.4 cm³** stock plus 13.6 cm³ water.
- Graphs: a line graph or scatter graph for a continuous variable, a bar chart for categories.
- Safety: eye protection, and take care with allergens and hydrogen peroxide.

## Reading enzyme graphs

| Graph | What to say |
|---|---|
| Rate against temperature | Rises to an optimum, then falls steeply. Explain both halves: kinetic energy first, denaturation second |
| Rate against pH | Rises to an optimum and falls on both sides. Name the bonds affected: ionic and hydrogen bonds |
| Rate against enzyme concentration | A straight line through the origin while substrate is in excess, then it levels off |
| Rate against substrate concentration | Steep at first, then a plateau at a maximum. Name the limiting factor on the plateau |
| Product against time | Steepest at the start, then flattens as substrate is used up |
| Substrate against time | Falls steeply, then levels at zero when the substrate has gone |

Describe first with figures (for example "the rate doubled from 20 to 30 units"), then explain using the enzyme-substrate complex.

## Worked reminders

**Comparing rates from times.** A suspension clears in 60 s at one temperature and in 30 s at another. Rate = 1 ÷ time, so 1 ÷ 60 = 0.017 s⁻¹ and 1 ÷ 30 = 0.033 s⁻¹. The second rate is **twice** the first.

**Percentage change.** A rate rises from 4.0 to 6.0 units: (6.0 - 4.0) ÷ 4.0 × 100 = **+50%**. A rate falls from 8.0 to 2.0 units: (2.0 - 8.0) ÷ 8.0 × 100 = **-75%**.

**Percentage inhibition.** The rate is 6.0 without inhibitor and 4.2 with it: (6.0 - 4.2) ÷ 6.0 × 100 = **30%**. If this value stays at 30% at every substrate concentration, suspect a non-competitive inhibitor. If it falls as substrate rises, suspect a competitive one.

## Answer frames

- **Explain the effect of temperature:** more kinetic energy, more frequent collisions, more enzyme-substrate complexes. Above the optimum, bonds holding the tertiary structure break, the active site is altered, the substrate is no longer complementary, the enzyme is denatured.
- **Explain the effect of pH:** change in H⁺ concentration, ionic and hydrogen bonds disrupted, tertiary structure and active site change, fewer complexes.
- **Explain inhibition:** say where the inhibitor binds, what happens to the active site, and whether more substrate helps.
- **Explain specificity:** primary structure, tertiary structure, shape of the active site, complementary to one substrate.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Lock and key and induced fit:** rigid versus flexible active site.
- **Denatured and inactive:** cold gives a slow enzyme that recovers on warming. Heat gives permanent change.
- **Competitive and non-competitive:** location of binding, and whether more substrate helps.
- **Mean rate and initial rate:** the mean averages over a period, the initial rate is the gradient at time 0.
- **Substrate-limited and enzyme-limited:** the plateau on a substrate graph is enzyme-limited.

## Quick self-test

1. State what is meant by activation energy.
2. Why is induced fit regarded as a better model than lock and key?
3. Calculate the pH of a solution with [H⁺] = 4.0 × 10⁻⁴ mol dm⁻³.
4. Calculate [H⁺] at pH 6.5.
5. How do you make 20 cm³ of 0.8% enzyme from a 2.5% stock?
6. A tangent at time 0 passes through (0, 0) and (20 s, 5.0 mmol dm⁻³). What is the initial rate?
7. How does raising substrate concentration affect a competitive inhibitor?
8. Why is an enzyme at 5 °C slow but not denatured?
9. An enzyme clears a suspension in 25 s. What is the rate?
10. Give one intracellular and one extracellular enzyme.

### Answers

1. The energy that must be supplied before a reaction can happen.
2. The active site changes shape as the substrate binds, which strains the substrate bonds and lowers activation energy further. Lock and key treats it as rigid.
3. **3.40**.
4. 10⁻⁶·⁵ = **3.2 × 10⁻⁷ mol dm⁻³**.
5. **6.4 cm³** stock plus **13.6 cm³** water.
6. 5.0 ÷ 20 = **0.25 mmol dm⁻³ s⁻¹**.
7. It reduces the inhibition, because substrate molecules out-compete the inhibitor for the active sites. The same maximum rate can be reached.
8. Molecules have little kinetic energy, so few collisions occur, but the tertiary structure is intact.
9. 1 ÷ 25 = **0.04 s⁻¹**.
10. Catalase (intracellular), amylase (extracellular).

## Where marks are usually lost

- Saying an enzyme "dies" or is "killed" by heat, when the word needed is denatured.
- Describing an active site as having the "same shape" as the substrate without saying **complementary**.
- Failing to mention **tertiary structure** when explaining denaturation or non-competitive inhibition.
- Saying a high temperature "kills" the collisions, instead of explaining bond breakage.
- Mixing up inhibitors: a non-competitive inhibitor does not bind the active site.
- Calling every plateau "denaturation" when it is a limiting factor.
- Giving a tangent gradient without units or with points too close together.
- Describing a control variable without saying how it is kept constant.
