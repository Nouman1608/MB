**This practice set covers topic 4.4, Organisational work conditions, in Specialist Option 4 of Cambridge International A Level Psychology 9990 (syllabus for 2024 to 2026).** Section A uses Paper 3 style short answers and a structured essay. Section B uses Paper 4 style scenarios with research methods. All workplaces, people and data are fictional.

Related: [Work conditions study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-psychology-9990-organisational-work-conditions/) · [Work conditions revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-psychology-9990-organisational-work-conditions-revision-notes/)

## Section A: Paper 3 style questions

**1.** Define flexitime and on-call working. **[2]**

**2.** Outline Kompier's (2006) view of the Hawthorne effect. **[3]**

**3.** Outline the findings of Oldham and Brass (1979) on open-plan offices. **[3]**

**4.** Explain the difference between an error of omission and an error of timing, with an example of each. **[4]**

**5.** Monitoring accidents can improve safety.

**(a)** Describe the study by Swat (1997). **[6]**

**(b)** Evaluate the study by Swat (1997), including a discussion of individual and situational explanations. **[10]**

## Section B: Paper 4 style questions

**6.** A factory's new machine has two identical black buttons side by side: one starts the conveyor and one stops it. Several accidents have occurred.

**(a)** Identify the type of error that the machine design encourages. **[1]**

**(b)** Suggest **two** design changes to reduce errors. **[2]**

**7.** A hospital wants to compare sleep quality in nurses on fixed and rotating shifts.

**(a)** Suggest **one** objective measure of sleep. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain **one** advantage of the objective measure over a sleep questionnaire. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain why this study would be a quasi-experiment. **[2]**

**8.** A software company moves its staff to an open-plan office and measures productivity before and after (fictional data).

| | Before the move | After the move |
|---|---|---|
| Mean tasks completed per week | 42 | 38 |

**(a)** Calculate the percentage change in productivity. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain **one** reason, other than the open-plan design, why productivity might have changed. **[2]**

**9.** A researcher observes call-centre staff to see whether better lighting increases productivity. Productivity rises during the observation period.

**(a)** Explain how the Hawthorne effect might explain this result. **[2]**

**(b)** Suggest how the researcher could reduce this problem. **[2]**

**10.** Using Fox et al. (1987), suggest how a construction firm could reduce accidents, and explain **one** problem with judging success from recorded accidents. **[4]**

**11.** Explain why a manager might prefer slow rotation to rapid rotation for a team that works nights. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** One mark each. Flexitime: employees choose their own start and finish times around fixed core hours (1). On-call: being available to work at short notice outside normal hours, e.g. a doctor called in at night (1).

**2.** Up to 3 marks. Kompier argued that the Hawthorne effect, as usually described, is a myth (1). The original studies had small groups, poor control and many changes at once, such as in pay and supervision (1), so the rise in productivity cannot be attributed to the attention of researchers, yet the story persists in textbooks (1).

**3.** Up to 3 marks. Newspaper employees who moved from a conventional office to an open-plan office were measured before and after (1). After the move, satisfaction and internal motivation fell (1). Employees reported more distraction and less privacy, which made concentration harder (1).

**4.** Two marks per error. Omission: leaving out a required step (1), e.g. not putting on safety goggles before using a grinder (1). Timing: performing an action too early or too late (1), e.g. releasing a load from a crane before it is in position (1).

**5.**

**(a)** Up to 6 marks. Swat studied the monitoring of accidents in industrial plants as part of a project begun in 1994 (1). He analysed 83 accidents recorded in 1993 (1) in four plants, including a foundry and a meat-processing plant, employing 2,964 workers (1). Accidents were classified by frequency, severity and direct and indirect causes (1). At least 45.8% involved breaches of basic housekeeping (1). A follow-up in 1994 estimated that 95% of incidents at the meat-processing plant were never reported, so Swat concluded that minor injuries and the circumstances of accidents should be recorded (1).

**(b)** Level-based: 9-10 marks for a detailed, balanced evaluation including the named debate, with a conclusion. Indicative content:

- *Individual and situational explanations*: an individual explanation blames careless workers; Swat's finding that housekeeping breaches were common supports a situational explanation, since management controls how work areas are maintained; both may interact, as workers choose whether to follow procedures.
- *Objective data*: accident records are less biased than self-report.
- *Under-reporting*: the 95% figure shows records are incomplete, so analyses of recorded accidents underestimate risk.
- *Longitudinal design*: records over time allow trends to be seen.
- *Generalisation*: four plants in one country; safety cultures differ.
- *Application*: recommends recording first-aid cases and housekeeping.
- *Conclusion*: valuable applied evidence, limited by the quality of records.

**6.**

**(a)** System error (a control design error), leading to errors of commission (1).

**(b)** One mark each: make the buttons different colours, e.g. green start and red stop (1); make them different shapes or place them apart, with a guard over the start button (1).

**7.**

**(a)** One mark for an objective measure, e.g. a wrist-worn activity monitor recording sleep duration (1).

**(b)** Up to 2 marks. It records sleep directly without relying on memory (1), whereas questionnaire answers may be inaccurate or biased by how tired nurses feel (1).

**(c)** Up to 2 marks. The researcher cannot randomly allocate nurses to shift type (1); the independent variable, fixed or rotating shift, already exists naturally (1).

**8.**

**(a)** (38 − 42) ÷ 42 × 100 (1) = −9.5%, a decrease of about 9.5% (1).

**(b)** Up to 2 marks. Other changes at the same time, such as new projects, staff leaving or a seasonal fall in demand (1), could explain the fall, because the move was not compared with a control group (1).

**9.**

**(a)** Up to 2 marks. Staff knew they were being observed (1), so they may have worked harder because of the attention, not the lighting (1).

**(b)** Up to 2 marks. Observe for a longer period so the novelty wears off, or use routinely collected productivity records without staff knowing the purpose (1); compare with a control group with no lighting change but the same observation (1).

**10.** Suggestion, up to 2: introduce a token economy in which workers earn tokens for periods without lost-time accidents and following safety rules (1), exchangeable for goods, so safe behaviour is reinforced (1). Problem, up to 2: workers may stop reporting minor accidents to keep their tokens (1), so a fall in recorded accidents may not be a real fall (1).

**11.** Up to 2 marks. Slow rotation keeps workers on nights for several weeks, so their circadian rhythms can partly adjust (1), whereas rapid rotation keeps changing the schedule so the body clock never adjusts, increasing sleepiness and accident risk (1).

## Where marks are usually lost

- In question 2, presenting the Hawthorne effect as proven.
- In question 5(a), giving Swat's figures without saying what they refer to.
- In question 8(a), dividing by the new value instead of the original.
