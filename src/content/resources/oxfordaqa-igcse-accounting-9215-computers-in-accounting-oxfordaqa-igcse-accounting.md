Section 3.5 of the OxfordAQA International GCSE Accounting (9215) specification includes "Use of computers in accounting", and the additional information sets the task: "advantages and disadvantages of using computerised software for recording accounting data compared to manual methods". The [limitations and computers guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-accounting-limitations-of-financial-statements-and-the-use-of-comp/) lists the main advantages and disadvantages. This guide explains where those points come from, stage by stage through the recording process, links them to the errors you studied in topic 2, and shows how to apply them to a business in an evaluate answer. Any figures are invented and in dollars.

Recap with the [computers in accounting revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-accounting-9215-computers-in-accounting-oxfordaqa-igcse-accounting-revision-notes/) and practise with the [computers in accounting practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-accounting-9215-computers-in-accounting-oxfordaqa-igcse-accounting-practice/).

## The recording process, manual and computerised

The stages are the same as in topic 1. What changes is who, or what, does each step.

| Stage | Manual method | Computerised software |
|---|---|---|
| Source document | Invoice written or printed, filed on paper | Invoice created in the software, or a supplier's invoice keyed in or scanned |
| Book of prime entry | Bookkeeper writes each item in the day book | Entering the invoice records it in an electronic day book |
| Posting to the ledger | Each entry copied by hand to two ledger accounts | Both sides posted automatically from one entry |
| Balancing accounts | Each account added and balanced by hand | Balances update instantly after every entry |
| Trial balance | Balances listed and totalled; may not agree | Produced at any moment; always agrees |
| Control accounts | Prepared separately as a check | Built in, because the totals come from the same entries |
| Financial statements | Prepared from the trial balance by hand | Produced from the ledger at the click of a report |

The key difference is that one entry by a person replaces several copying steps. This explains most of the advantages, and also why some errors survive.

## Which errors software prevents, and which it cannot

Accounting software posts the same amount to a debit and a credit from a single entry, and it does the arithmetic. So the trial balance always agrees. Look at what that means for the errors you met in topic 2.

| Error | Prevented by software? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Addition or balancing error | Yes | The software does all the arithmetic |
| One-sided entry | Yes | Every entry is posted to both sides |
| Different amounts on each side | Yes | A single figure is posted to both sides |
| Transposition of figures between the two sides | Yes | The figure is keyed once |
| Omission | No | If no one enters the invoice, nothing is recorded |
| Commission | No | Choosing the wrong customer's account posts to it |
| Principle | No | Coding a new machine to an expense code is accepted |
| Original entry | No | A wrong amount keyed in is posted correctly, both sides |
| Complete reversal | Partly | Most entries are made through screens that fix the sides, but a journal entry keyed the wrong way round is accepted |

The phrase "garbage in, garbage out" sums this up: the output is only as good as the data entered. A trial balance that agrees proves even less in a computerised system than in a manual one, because it would agree whatever was keyed in.

### Validation checks

Software can catch some input mistakes before they are posted. For example, it can refuse a date outside the current period, reject an account code that does not exist, or warn when an invoice number has already been used. These checks reduce errors of omission or original entry, but they cannot tell whether a correct-looking entry is true.

## Advantages, with the reason behind each

- **Speed.** One entry replaces several copying steps, so a business with many transactions saves bookkeeping time.
- **Arithmetical accuracy.** Totals, balances and the trial balance are calculated automatically.
- **Up-to-date information.** Balances are current after every entry, so the owner can see today who owes money, how much is in the bank and what profit has been made so far this year. Managers make decisions on current figures rather than waiting for the year end.
- **Reports.** Aged lists of receivables, statements of account for customers and financial statements can be produced quickly, and ratios can be calculated automatically.
- **Storage.** Records take little space, can be searched and can be backed up in another location.
- **Several users.** Different staff can enter sales and purchases at the same time.

## Disadvantages, with the reason behind each

- **Cost.** Hardware, software licences or subscriptions, updates and training are ongoing expenses that a small business may not recover.
- **Errors still occur.** Wrong data is posted just as faithfully as right data, and errors may go unnoticed because staff trust the output.
- **Security.** Records can be hacked, stolen, corrupted by a virus or changed by staff without authority.
- **Breakdown.** A power cut or system failure stops work, and records can be lost without back-ups.
- **Staff.** Employees need training, some resist change, and the business may depend on one person who understands the system.

## Controls that make software safer

A good answer on disadvantages often says how the business can reduce them.

| Risk | Control |
|---|---|
| Unauthorised access or fraud | Passwords and different access levels, so a clerk entering sales cannot change wages |
| Loss of data | Regular back-ups stored in another place |
| Undetected changes | An audit trail that records who entered or changed each item and when |
| Viruses | Up-to-date security software |
| Input errors | Validation checks, and comparing totals with source documents |

## Worked example: applying the points

*Holloway Tiles is a sole trader with about 15 sales a week, all for cash. The owner keeps her records by hand in the evenings. She is considering buying accounting software. Evaluate whether she should.*

A strong answer applies each point to Holloway Tiles:

1. **For**: the software would save her evening hours and remove arithmetic slips, and she could see her profit so far at any time.
2. **Against**: with only about 15 cash sales a week, the time saved is small; there are no credit customers, so the benefits of automatic receivables records and statements of account do not apply.
3. **Against**: a subscription and training are a real cost against a small saving. Errors of principle or omission would still depend on her care.
4. **Judgement**: at her present size, a manual system is probably adequate. If she starts selling on credit or the number of transactions rises, software would become worthwhile.

Compare a wholesaler with 400 credit customers: there, automatic posting, up-to-date receivables balances and statements of account would save a great deal of time, so the cost is easily justified.

## Common mistakes

- Saying a computer "removes all errors". It removes arithmetic and posting errors only.
- Giving advantages without saying compared with what. The specification asks for a comparison with manual methods.
- Listing points without applying them to the business's size and type of transactions.
- Forgetting that a balanced trial balance from software proves very little.
- Giving disadvantages without the controls that reduce them, when the question asks how to manage the risk.

## Exam technique

- **Explain an advantage or disadvantage**: state it, give the reason, and say why it matters to the business in the question.
- **Evaluate**: both sides, applied, then a judgement based on the business's size, number of credit transactions and budget.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can a computerised trial balance fail to agree?**
Not in normal use, because every entry is posted to both sides automatically. That is why it cannot be relied on to reveal errors.

**What is an audit trail in accounting software?**
A record of who entered or changed each transaction and when, which helps to trace errors and deters fraud.
