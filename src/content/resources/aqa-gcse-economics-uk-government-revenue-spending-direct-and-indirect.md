## Government revenue: where the money comes from

Revenue is the money the government receives. Most of it is tax. The main sources are:

- **Income tax**: paid by workers on their earnings, and by others on some other income.
- **National Insurance contributions**: paid by employees and employers on earnings. They are linked to the right to some state benefits.
- **Value added tax (VAT)**: a percentage added to the price of many goods and services.
- **Corporation tax**: paid by companies on their profits.
- **Excise duties**: taxes on particular goods such as fuel, alcohol and tobacco.
- **Local and property taxes**: for example council tax, business rates and stamp duty on property purchases.
- **Other sources**: for example capital gains tax, inheritance tax, fees and charges, and borrowing.

Borrowing is not a tax. It covers a gap when spending is more than revenue. In the UK, income tax, National Insurance and VAT are among the largest sources of revenue, and corporation tax and excise duties are smaller.

### Worked example: reading a revenue table (illustrative)

Country A's government collects the following in a year (units are fictional and in billions).

| Source | Amount | Share of total |
|---|---|---|
| Income tax | 120 | 30.0% |
| National Insurance contributions | 80 | 20.0% |
| VAT | 90 | 22.5% |
| Corporation tax | 40 | 10.0% |
| Excise duties | 30 | 7.5% |
| Other | 40 | 10.0% |
| Total | 400 | 100% |

Each share is amount divided by 400, times 100. For example VAT is 90 / 400 = 0.225, so 22.5%.

Direct taxes in the table (income tax, National Insurance, corporation tax) are 120 + 80 + 40 = 240, which is 240 / 400 = 60% of revenue. Indirect taxes (VAT and excise duties) are 90 + 30 = 120, which is 30%. The remaining 10% is other.

## Government spending: where the money goes

Public spending pays for services and transfers. The main areas are:

- **Social protection**: pensions and benefits, including support for unemployed, disabled and low-income households.
- **Health**: running hospitals and other health services.
- **Education**: schools, colleges and universities.
- **Defence**.
- **Public order and safety**: police, courts and prisons.
- **Transport**: roads, railways and other infrastructure.
- **Debt interest**: interest paid on money the government has borrowed in the past.
- **Other**: for example housing, environmental protection and running costs.

In the UK, social protection, health and education are among the largest areas.

### Worked example: spending and the deficit (illustrative)

Country A's spending in the same year, in the same fictional units:

| Area | Amount | Share of total |
|---|---|---|
| Social protection | 135 | 32.1% |
| Health | 105 | 25.0% |
| Education | 55 | 13.1% |
| Defence | 30 | 7.1% |
| Transport | 20 | 4.8% |
| Debt interest | 25 | 6.0% |
| Other | 50 | 11.9% |
| Total | 420 | 100% |

Revenue was 400 and spending was 420, so the government has a **budget deficit** of 420 - 400 = 20. It must borrow 20, which adds to the national debt and means more debt interest in later years. If revenue had been 440, it would have a surplus of 20.

Spending is a choice. Every extra unit spent on one area has an opportunity cost, because it could have been spent elsewhere or used to cut taxes.

## Direct and indirect taxes

The test is who the taxpayer is and whether they can pass the tax on.

| | Direct tax | Indirect tax |
|---|---|---|
| Who pays it to the government | The person or firm whose income or profit is taxed | A seller, who collects it and passes it to the government |
| Who bears the burden | The person or firm that pays it | Usually the consumer, through a higher price |
| Based on | Income, profit or wealth | Spending on goods and services |
| Examples | Income tax, corporation tax, National Insurance | VAT, excise duties |

Do not write that a direct tax is "paid directly to the government" as your whole answer, because a seller also pays VAT directly to the government. The point is that the person who finally bears an indirect tax is not the one who sends it in.

### Worked example: finding the tax in a price

A good costs 50 before VAT, and VAT is 20%. Price paid = 50 x 1.20 = 60, so the tax is 10.

Reverse it: a good costs 72 including 20% VAT. Divide by 1.20: 72 / 1.20 = 60 before tax, so the tax is 72 - 60 = 12. A common error is taking 20% of 72 (14.40), which is wrong because the 20% was added to the 60, not to the 72.

If VAT rises from 20% to 25%, the same good that was 60 becomes 60 / 1.20 x 1.25 = 62.50.

## Progressive and regressive taxes

These words describe how the **percentage of income** paid in tax changes as income rises. Judge them by percentages, not by pounds.

- **Progressive**: the percentage of income paid in tax rises as income rises.
- **Regressive**: the percentage of income paid in tax falls as income rises. Rich people may pay more pounds, but a smaller share of what they earn.
- **Proportional**: the same percentage at every income. This is a useful comparison but is not named in the specification.

### Worked example: a progressive income tax

Country A's income tax has an 11,000 tax-free allowance, 15% on income from 11,000 to 35,000, and 35% on income above 35,000. Each rate applies only to the slice of income in that band.

| Income | Working | Tax | Tax as % of income | Income after tax |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 9,000 | all below allowance | 0 | 0% | 9,000 |
| 26,000 | (26,000 - 11,000) x 0.15 = 2,250 | 2,250 | 8.7% | 23,750 |
| 70,000 | 24,000 x 0.15 + 35,000 x 0.35 = 3,600 + 12,250 | 15,850 | 22.6% | 54,150 |
| 130,000 | 3,600 + 95,000 x 0.35 = 3,600 + 33,250 | 36,850 | 28.3% | 93,150 |

The percentage climbs from 0% to 28.3% as income rises, so the tax is progressive. The taxpayer on 130,000 does not pay 35% of everything: the average rate (tax divided by income) stays below the top band rate.

**Marginal versus average.** Suppose a worker on 35,000 gets a 1,000 rise. The extra 1,000 is taxed at 35%, so extra tax is 350 and the worker keeps 650 more. Moving into a higher band never makes take-home pay fall, because only the extra slice is taxed at the higher rate.

### Worked example: regressive effects of indirect taxes

Indirect taxes can be regressive even when the rate is the same for everyone, because lower-income households spend more of their income on taxed goods.

*A sales tax of 20% on goods.* Household J has income 15,000 and buys 10,000 of taxable goods before tax. Tax = 10,000 x 0.20 = 2,000, which is 2,000 / 15,000 = 13.3% of income. Household K has income 90,000 and buys 45,000 of taxable goods. Tax = 9,000, which is 10.0% of income. K pays more in total but a smaller share, so the tax is regressive in its effect.

*A fixed duty of 0.50 per litre of fuel.* Household L has income 18,000 and buys 1,200 litres: tax 600, which is 3.3% of income. Household M has income 72,000 and buys 1,800 litres: tax 900, which is 1.3% of income. A fixed amount per unit takes a bigger share from the lower income.

Here M spends a smaller share of income on fuel, which is why the duty is regressive. If a tax is on a luxury that only high earners buy, it can be progressive instead.

## Using the ideas in evaluation

**Direct taxes.** Progressive income tax can reduce inequality and raises more revenue as incomes grow. Possible drawbacks: high rates may reduce the reward for extra work or encourage avoidance, and people see the tax on every payslip.

**Indirect taxes.** They are hard to avoid, collected cheaply from sellers, and taxes on goods like tobacco can discourage use and raise revenue together. Possible drawbacks: they can be regressive, and a rise in price can reduce sales, so revenue may rise by less than expected.

**A balanced answer** compares who loses and gains, gives a chain of reasoning (for example: higher fuel duty, higher prices, a bigger share of a low income lost, wider inequality) and reaches a judgement about which matters more for the government's objectives, such as raising revenue or reducing inequality.

## Common errors

- Writing "rich people pay more, so it is progressive". Test the percentage of income.
- Charging the top rate on all earnings instead of only the slice above the threshold.
- Confusing direct/indirect with progressive/regressive. They are separate pairs: VAT is indirect and can be regressive.
- Calling borrowing a form of tax, or listing it as revenue from taxpayers today.
- Taking VAT off a tax-inclusive price by subtracting 20% instead of dividing by 1.20.
- Quoting real figures from memory. Say "among the largest" unless the question gives data.

## Frequently asked questions

**What are the main sources of UK government revenue?**
Income tax, National Insurance contributions, VAT, corporation tax, excise duties, and local and property taxes such as council tax and business rates.

**What are the largest areas of UK government spending?**
Social protection, which covers pensions and benefits, health and education are among the largest areas.

**What is the difference between a direct and an indirect tax?**
A direct tax is levied on income, profit or wealth and borne by the person or firm that pays it. An indirect tax is levied on spending and is usually passed on to the consumer through a higher price.

**Why can indirect taxes be regressive?**
Lower-income households spend a larger share of their income on taxed goods, so the tax takes a bigger percentage of their income even when the rate is the same for everyone.

**What does progressive tax mean?**
A progressive tax takes a rising percentage of income as income rises, as with UK income tax bands above a tax-free allowance.
