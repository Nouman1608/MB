This strand of 3.1.1 is the analytical core of the reading questions in AQA GCSE English Language (8700). The
specification asks you to explain and illustrate "how vocabulary and grammar contribute to effectiveness and impact,
using linguistic and literary terminology accurately", "paying attention to detail", and to analyse and evaluate "how
form and structure contribute to the effectiveness and impact of a text". It is assessed chiefly by **AO2** (explain,
comment on and analyse how writers use language and structure to achieve effects and influence readers, using
relevant subject terminology), with **AO4** taking the step from explaining to judging. For quick recall use the
[revision notes on evaluating a writer's choices](/resources/aqa-gcse-english-language-8700-evaluation-of-a-writers-choice-of-vocabulary-form-grammatical-and-structural-features-revision-notes/),
and to test yourself use the
[practice questions on evaluating a writer's choices](/resources/aqa-gcse-english-language-8700-evaluation-of-a-writers-choice-of-vocabulary-form-grammatical-and-structural-features-practice/).

## The four areas of choice

### Vocabulary

Look at individual words and what they carry beyond their dictionary meaning.

- **Word class:** is the force of the sentence in a verb ("the wind *clawed*"), an adjective, or an adverb?
- **Connotation:** "home" and "house" denote the same place but carry different associations.
- **Semantic field:** a cluster of words from one area (illness, war, religion) builds an idea across a passage.
- **Register:** formal, informal, technical or archaic vocabulary signals a relationship with the reader.
- **Imagery:** metaphor, simile and personification; ask what is being compared to what, and why that comparison.

### Grammar

Grammar is about how words are arranged, and it creates effects just as vocabulary does.

- **Sentence length and type:** a simple sentence after several complex ones isolates and emphasises an idea; a long
  sentence built from many clauses can mimic breathlessness or a rush of thought.
- **Sentence function:** questions, commands and exclamations change the reader's role.
- **Tense:** the present tense can create immediacy; a sudden shift in tense can signal memory.
- **Person and pronouns:** "we" builds community; "you" makes demands of the reader.
- **Modal verbs:** "must" and "should" sound certain; "might" and "could" sound tentative.
- **Active and passive voice:** the passive ("the window was broken") can hide who acted.
- **Word order:** placing a key word at the end of a sentence gives it emphasis.

### Form

Form is the kind of text and the conventions it follows. A diary invites intimacy and immediacy; a letter addresses
a particular reader; a newspaper article aims at a wide public; a first-person narrative limits what the reader knows
to one mind. Ask how the writer uses, or breaks, the expectations of the form.

### Structure

Structure is the order and shape of the whole text, and it is what Paper 1 Question 3 assesses.

- **Openings:** what the reader is shown first and why.
- **Shifts in focus:** from wide setting to a single detail, from outside to inside, from one character to another.
- **Time:** flashbacks, flash-forwards, a slowing of pace at a key moment.
- **Repetition and patterning:** a motif that returns, or a cyclical structure that ends where it began.
- **Paragraphing:** a one-line paragraph after long ones creates a pause or a turn.
- **Endings:** resolution, cliffhanger or an unanswered question.

Structure is about the text as a sequence. A comment that could be made about a single sentence in isolation is
usually a language point, not a structural one.

## From explaining to evaluating

The specification uses two kinds of verb: *explain and illustrate* (show how a choice works, with a quotation) and
*analyse and evaluate* (judge how well it works). An explanation says what a metaphor suggests. An evaluation says
why it is the right choice at this moment, or how it shapes the reader's response to the whole passage. Evaluative
phrases include "particularly effective because", "this is the turning point of the passage because", and "the
impact is strengthened by".

## Using terminology accurately

Terminology earns credit only when it is accurate and leads somewhere. "The writer uses a verb" is accurate but
inert. "The verb 'clawed' gives the wind the violence of an animal" uses the term to open up an effect. Never name a
technique you cannot then explain, and never mislabel: calling a simile a metaphor weakens an otherwise sound point.

## Worked example

The passage below is original, written for this guide.

> *The flat was quiet. Too quiet. Ellis stood in the doorway with his key still in the lock and listened to the
> refrigerator hum its one low note. Somewhere below, a car door slammed; somewhere above, a child laughed and was
> hushed. On the table, where his sister always left the post, there was nothing at all.*
>
> *He did not call her name. He knew, already, that she had gone.*

**Language paragraph (annotated):**

> The writer uses the minor sentence "Too quiet" to make the silence threatening **[accurate term]**. Coming straight
> after the plain statement "The flat was quiet", it turns a neutral observation into a warning, as if Ellis is
> correcting himself **[effect, with attention to detail]**. The personified refrigerator, humming "its one low note",
> deepens this: the only voice in the flat belongs to a machine, so the absence of human sound is made audible
> **[explains imagery]**. This is particularly effective because the reader senses that something is wrong before
> Ellis admits it **[evaluates impact]**.

**Structure paragraph (annotated):**

> The writer moves the focus outward and then sharply inward. After the doorway, attention travels to sounds
> "below" and "above", the balanced pairing placing ordinary life all around the flat **[shift in focus]**. The focus
> then narrows to the table and the absence of post, a single concrete detail that confirms the unease **[zoom]**. The
> final, separate paragraph withholds the explanation until the last clause, "she had gone", so the passage ends on
> certainty that has been building since the second sentence **[ending, and how it relates to the opening]**.

## Common mistakes

- **Feature-spotting:** listing techniques with no effect explained.
- **Generic effects:** "it makes the reader want to read on" could be said of any text.
- **Mixing up language and structure:** analysing a metaphor in a structure question.
- **Ignoring grammar:** sentence forms and tense are often the most precise evidence available.
- **Long quotations:** a whole sentence makes it hard to analyse any single choice in detail.
- **Explaining but never evaluating** in the questions that ask how successful the writing is.
- **Inaccurate terminology**, which undermines the precision the mark scheme rewards.

## Exam technique by command word

- **How does the writer use language to...?** Choose a few rich details from the lines given; for each, give a short
  quotation, an accurate term where helpful, and a developed effect linked to the focus of the question.
- **How has the writer structured the text to interest you as a reader?** Track the whole extract from beginning to
  end; use words such as "initially", "then shifts", "by the end".
- **To what extent do you agree?** Use language and structure as evidence for a judgement, not as a separate list.
