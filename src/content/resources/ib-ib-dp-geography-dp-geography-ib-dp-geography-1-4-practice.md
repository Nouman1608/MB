## Section A

**1.** Define fossil groundwater. **[1]**

**2.** Distinguish between a confined and an unconfined aquifer. **[2]**

**3.** An aquifer receives 120 million cubic metres of recharge a year and 180 million cubic metres are pumped out. Calculate the annual change in storage and state what is likely to happen to the water table. **[2]**

**4.** State two countries that share the Nubian Sandstone Aquifer System with Libya. **[1]**

**5.** Outline one weakness of the Mekong River Commission as a body for managing the whole Mekong basin. **[2]**

## Section B

**6.** A town has a reliable supply of 60 million cubic metres a year. Demand is 48 million cubic metres and is growing by 3 per cent a year.

**(a)** Calculate demand after 5 years, to one decimal place. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate how many whole years pass before demand first exceeds supply. Show your working. **[2]**

**(c)** Suggest two reasons why a shortage may occur sooner than this projection shows. **[2]**

**7.** Explain two ways in which a large dam can affect communities living downstream. **[4]**

**8.** Explain why Lake Chad is particularly sensitive to changes in inflow. **[3]**

**9.** Outline two findings of the World Commission on Dams report of 2000. **[2]**

## Section C

**10.** Evaluate the use of groundwater as a long-term solution to water scarcity. **[10]**

**11.** Discuss the extent to which large multipurpose dam projects are a sustainable way to meet future water needs. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** Groundwater stored under past, wetter climates that receives little or no recharge today [1]. **[1]**

**2.** An unconfined aquifer is open to recharge from the surface directly above it [1]; a confined aquifer lies between impermeable layers and is recharged slowly, often from a distant outcrop [1]. **[2]**

**3.** 120 - 180 = **-60 million cubic metres a year** (storage falls) [1]. Abstraction exceeds recharge, so the water table will fall [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Any two of Egypt, Sudan, Chad [1]. **[1]**

**5.** China and Myanmar, the upstream states, are dialogue partners rather than members [1], so dams on the upper river are outside the Commission's agreement and the whole basin cannot be managed together [1]. **[2]**

**6. (a)** 48 x 1.03 to the power 5 = 48 x 1.159 = **55.6 million cubic metres** [1] [1].

**(b)** Demand exceeds supply when 1.03 to the power n > 60 / 48 = 1.25 [1]. n > log(1.25) / log(1.03) = 7.5, so demand first exceeds supply in **year 8** [1].

**(c)** Any two [1] each: reliable supply may fall if climate change reduces river flow or recharge; drought years cause shortages before average demand exceeds average supply; population or industrial growth may be faster than 3 per cent; leaks may increase as infrastructure ages. **[6]**

**7.** Any two developed points:

- The dam regulates flow and stores flood water [1], so downstream farmers and towns face fewer floods and receive irrigation water in the dry season [1].
- The reservoir traps sediment [1], so downstream floodplains lose the fertile silt that floods deposited and farmers may need more fertiliser; deltas and channels can erode [1].
- Changed flow and temperature reduce fish stocks [1], harming fishing livelihoods downstream [1].

Award 2 marks per developed point, up to 4. **[4]**

**8.** The lake is very shallow [1], so a small change in water volume causes a large change in surface area [1]. In a dry region, with high evaporation and dependence on one main river, the Chari, any fall in inflow from drought or upstream irrigation quickly shrinks the lake [1]. **[3]**

**9.** Any two [1] each: large dams had delivered important benefits such as power, irrigation and flood control; the social and environmental costs were often unacceptable and fell largely on displaced people and downstream communities; all options should be assessed before choosing a dam; affected people should be involved in decisions. **[2]**

**10.** Indicative content:

- **Strengths:** groundwater is widespread and often available where surface water is not [1]; it is buffered against short droughts because stores are large [1]; it is often cleaner than surface water, needing less treatment [1].
- **Limitations:** where abstraction exceeds recharge, water tables fall, raising pumping costs and drying wells [1]; fossil aquifers are effectively non-renewable, as with the Nubian Sandstone Aquifer System supplying Libya's Great Man-Made River [1]; over-pumping causes land subsidence and saline intrusion in coastal aquifers [1]; pollution such as nitrate is slow to clear once it reaches an aquifer [1].
- **Management possibilities:** limiting abstraction to recharge, monitoring, and managed aquifer recharge can make use sustainable [1]; shared aquifers need cooperation between states [1].
- **Conclusion:** groundwater can be a long-term solution only where use is matched to recharge; for fossil aquifers it is a temporary measure that buys time [1]. **[10]**

**11.** Indicative content:

- **Contributions to future needs:** reliable storage for dry seasons and droughts [1]; hydroelectricity with low operating emissions [1]; flood control and irrigation, as at the Three Gorges Dam on the Yangtze [1].
- **Sustainability concerns:** displacement of communities and loss of heritage [1]; sediment trapping shortens reservoir life and starves deltas [1]; evaporation losses in hot climates [1]; the World Commission on Dams found costs were often unacceptable and unevenly borne [1].
- **Future uncertainty:** if climate change reduces inflow, reservoirs may not fill, so a dam designed on past flows may underperform [1].
- **Conditions for sustainability:** assessing alternatives, fair resettlement and environmental flows can reduce harm [1].
- **Conclusion:** large dams can meet some future needs but are sustainable only where their social and environmental costs are addressed and demand management is used alongside them [1]. **[10]**

*Examiner insight:* "Sustainable" has economic, social and environmental dimensions. Answers that judge a dam on all three, over the short and long term, reach the top band.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calculating growth by adding 3 per cent of the original figure each year instead of compounding.
- Treating every aquifer as renewable.
- Describing dam costs without saying which group bears them.
- Concluding on dams without any comparison with alternatives such as demand management.
