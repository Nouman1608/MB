## Answers

**1.** The enthalpy change when one mole of electrons is added to one mole of gaseous atoms [1] to form one
mole of gaseous 1− ions [1].

**2.** Mg²⁺(g) + O²⁻(g) → MgO(s) [1].

**3.** ½Cl₂(g) → Cl(g) [1].

**4.** Positive (endothermic) [1]; the electron is being added to an O⁻ ion, which is already negative and
repels it, so energy must be supplied [1].

**5.** NaCl [1]: Cl⁻ is smaller than Br⁻, so the ions are closer together and the electrostatic attraction
is stronger [1].

**6. (a)** K(s) → K(g) [1]; K(g) → K⁺(g) + e⁻ [1].

**(b)** Sum of steps = 89 + 418 + 121 + (−349) = +279 [1]; ΔH<sub>latt</sub> = −437 − 279 =
−716 kJ mol⁻¹ [1].

**7. (a)** ΔH<sub>at</sub>(F) and EA₁(F), because CaF₂ contains two fluorine atoms (two F⁻ ions) [1].

**(b)** Sum = 178 + 590 + 1150 + 2(79) + 2(−328) = +1420 [1]; ΔH<sub>latt</sub> = −1220 − 1420 =
−2640 kJ mol⁻¹ [1].

**8.** Sum of steps to the gaseous ions = 148 + 736 + 1450 + 248 − 141 + 798 = +3239 [1];
ΔH<sub>f</sub> = 3239 + (−3841) = −602 kJ mol⁻¹ [1].

**9. (a)** −288 = 107 + 494 + 107 + EA₁(I) − 701, so −288 = 7 + EA₁(I) [1]; EA₁(I) = −295 kJ mol⁻¹ [1].

**(b)** The outer shell of iodine is further from the nucleus [1] and more shielded by inner electrons, so
the added electron is attracted less strongly despite the greater nuclear charge [1].

**10.** The oxygen atom is very small, so the added electron enters a crowded outer shell [1] and is
strongly repelled by the electrons already there; this repulsion outweighs oxygen's greater nuclear
attraction [1].

**11.** The ionic charges in MgO are 2+ and 2−, compared with 1+ and 1− in NaF [1]; the attraction depends on
the product of the charges (4 compared with 1), so with similar distances between ions the lattice energy
of MgO is much more exothermic [1].

**12.** CaO > CaF₂ > KF > KCl [1]. CaO has the highest charges (2+ and 2−); CaF₂ still has a 2+ cation, so
it is more exothermic than the potassium compounds with a 1+ cation [1]. KF is more exothermic than KCl
because F⁻ is smaller than Cl⁻, allowing the ions closer together [1].

**13. (a)** Any two for one mark each, up to [2]: atomisation of sodium; first ionisation energy of sodium;
atomisation of oxygen; second electron affinity of oxygen.

**(b)** EA₁ is exothermic (downward), because the neutral atom attracts the electron, but EA₂ is
endothermic (upward), because the electron is repelled by the negative ion [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing Cl₂(g) → 2Cl(g) for the atomisation of chlorine: it must form one mole of atoms.
- Missing the doubling of steps for MX₂ or M₂X compounds.
- Forgetting EA₂ for an oxide, or giving it a negative sign.
- Writing lattice energy as the breaking of the lattice, which reverses the sign.
- Explaining lattice energy differences without naming both charge and ionic radius, or without linking
  them to the strength of electrostatic attraction.
- Explaining the fluorine and oxygen anomalies with electronegativity instead of electron repulsion.

## Frequently asked questions

**How do I avoid sign errors in Born–Haber calculations?**
Write the Hess's law equation in full first: ΔH<sub>f</sub> equals the sum of all the other steps,
including the lattice energy. Then substitute each value with its sign and rearrange for the unknown.

**Why is the atomisation of chlorine half the Cl–Cl bond energy?**
Atomisation forms one mole of chlorine atoms, which needs only half a mole of Cl₂ molecules to be broken,
so the value is half of the 242 kJ mol⁻¹ bond energy.
