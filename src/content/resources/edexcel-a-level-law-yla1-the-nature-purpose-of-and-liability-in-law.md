This guide covers **section 1.1, The nature, purpose of and liability in law**, the first of the two sections of Paper 1 (Underlying Principles of Law and the English Legal System) in Pearson Edexcel International Advanced Level Law, Issue 4. The [Paper 1 Underlying Principles guide](/resources/a-level-edexcel-law-underlying-principles/) gives the whole paper in outline. This page goes deeper into section 1.1 alone, with the cases and exam technique you need.

## What section 1.1 covers

The specification breaks the section into fourteen content areas in five groups:

- **Rules and law (1.1.1 to 1.1.3):** how a legal rule differs from other rules through **certainty**, **enforcement** and being **sanctioned by the state**.
- **The role of law in society (1.1.4 to 1.1.7):** rights, duties, privileges, liabilities and legal personality; liability and remedies in civil law; liability and sanctions in criminal law; how civil and criminal sanctions relate.
- **Theories of punishment (1.1.8):** revenge, retribution, rehabilitation, reparation, restoration, denunciation and deterrence, linked to sentences under the **Criminal Justice Act 2003**.
- **Legal theories (1.1.9 to 1.1.11):** positivism, natural law and utilitarianism, each with its theorists.
- **Law and morality (1.1.12 to 1.1.14):** the Hart/Devlin debate and the Wolfenden Report, public and private morality, and how the courts have interpreted morality in decided cases.

## Rules and law

A club's dress code or the etiquette of a queue is a rule; it becomes **law** only with three features together.

- **Certainty.** It comes from a recognised source (an Act, delegated legislation or a binding judgment), so anyone can find out what it requires.
- **Enforcement.** It is enforced through the courts and, in criminal matters, by the police and the prosecuting authorities.
- **Sanctioned by the state.** Breaking it can lead to a state sanction, such as a fine, imprisonment or an order to pay damages.

A school rule may be certain (it is written down) and enforced (by a detention), but it is not sanctioned by the state. That is the distinction to draw in an answer.

## Rights, duties and legal personality

Law gives people **rights** (to own property, to be paid under a contract), imposes **duties** (to take reasonable care, not to steal), grants **privileges** (such as the privilege of what is said in Parliament or in court, which cannot found a defamation claim) and attaches **liabilities** (exposure to a sanction or remedy when a duty is broken).

**Legal personality** is the capacity to hold rights and duties. A human being is a **natural person**. A registered company is a **legal (artificial) person**, separate from its shareholders: in *Salomon v A Salomon & Co Ltd* [1897] the House of Lords held that the company's debts were its own, not Mr Salomon's.

## Civil and criminal liability compared

| | Civil law | Criminal law |
|---|---|---|
| Purpose | Settle disputes between individuals or organisations | Punish wrongs against society |
| Branches | Contract, tort, family, property, employment | Offences against the person, property offences, regulatory offences |
| Who brings the case | The **claimant** sues the **defendant** | The state **prosecutes** the **defendant**, usually through the Crown Prosecution Service |
| Burden and standard of proof | Claimant, on the **balance of probabilities** | Prosecution, **beyond reasonable doubt** (*Woolmington v DPP* [1935]) |
| Outcome | Defendant is **liable** or not liable | Defendant is **guilty** or not guilty |
| Response | Remedies: damages, injunction, specific performance | Sanctions: custody, community order, fine, discharge |

**How the sanctions relate (1.1.7).** A criminal sanction looks back at the wrong and serves society's interests; a civil remedy looks at the claimant's loss and tries to put them back where they were. One incident can lead to both. A shopkeeper who is punched by a customer may see the customer prosecuted for assault and may also sue for damages in tort. Because the standards of proof differ, an acquittal in the criminal case does not stop a civil claim succeeding.

## Theories of punishment and sentencing

The specification names seven theories and asks for examples and how each relates to the sentences available.

| Theory | Looks at | Typical sentence link |
|---|---|---|
| **Revenge** | Society's wish to hit back | The idea behind long custodial terms for shocking crimes; rarely openly endorsed |
| **Retribution** | Just deserts, proportionate to the offence | Sentences graded by seriousness; the mandatory life sentence for murder |
| **Denunciation** | Society's public condemnation | Exemplary sentences for crimes that cause public outrage |
| **Deterrence** | Individual (this offender) and general (others) | Custody; suspended sentences hanging over the offender |
| **Rehabilitation** | Reforming the offender | Community orders with drug rehabilitation or supervision requirements |
| **Reparation** | Making amends to the victim or community | Compensation orders; unpaid work |
| **Restoration** | Repairing the relationship between offender and victim | Restorative justice meetings |

The **Criminal Justice Act 2003** (s 142) lists the purposes a court must consider when sentencing an adult: punishment, reduction of crime (including by deterrence), reform and rehabilitation, protection of the public, and reparation. The Act does not rank them. Those purposes have since been restated in the Sentencing Act 2020; answer using the Act the specification names unless your teacher tells you otherwise.

## Legal theories of law and justice

**Positivism** says that what the law *is* and what it *ought to be* are separate questions. For **John Austin**, law is the command of a sovereign backed by a sanction. **H.L.A. Hart** refined this: law is a union of **primary rules** (which govern conduct) and **secondary rules** (which say how rules are made, changed and recognised), identified by a **rule of recognition**. Strength: certainty. Weakness: a wicked law made by the correct procedure is still law.

**Natural law** says there is a higher moral law against which human laws are measured. **St Thomas Aquinas** taught that an unjust human law is not truly law. **Lon Fuller** argued that law has an "inner morality" of procedural requirements, such as rules being public, clear and not retrospective. The 1958 Hart–Fuller debate, partly about how post-war German courts should treat informers who had relied on Nazi laws, is the classic clash between the two theories.

**Utilitarianism**, associated with **Jeremy Bentham** and **John Stuart Mill**, judges a law by its consequences: the right law produces the greatest happiness of the greatest number. Mill's **harm principle** limits the state to preventing harm to others, which links straight to the Hart/Devlin debate.

## Law and morality

The **Wolfenden Report (1957)** recommended that homosexual acts in private between consenting adults should no longer be crimes, because there is a realm of private morality that is "not the law's business". **Lord Devlin** replied that a shared morality holds society together, so the law may enforce it. **Professor Hart** replied, following Mill, that only harm to others justifies legal intervention.

**Public morality** is the shared moral code of society, such as condemnation of violence and dishonesty. **Private morality** is an individual's own choices about conduct that affects only themselves or consenting others.

**How the courts interpret morality (1.1.14).** Useful cases, grouped by the direction they point:

- **Enforcing morality:** *Shaw v DPP* [1962] (conspiracy to corrupt public morals); *R v Brown* [1993] (consent no defence to actual bodily harm in private sadomasochistic activity).
- **Respecting private choice:** *R v Wilson* [1996] (a husband who branded his initials on his wife's buttocks at her request was not guilty, distinguishing *Brown*).
- **Changing with social attitudes:** *R v R* [1991] (marital rape); *Gillick v West Norfolk and Wisbech AHA* [1985] (contraceptive advice to a competent under-16).
- **Life and death:** *Airedale NHS Trust v Bland* [1993] (withdrawing treatment); *Re A (Conjoined Twins)* [2000] (separation that would cause one twin's death).

## Worked example 1: linking theory to a sentence

*Dev, 19, has been convicted of criminal damage after spraying graffiti on a community centre. The court imposes a community order with 80 hours of unpaid work, cleaning the area he damaged. Explain which theories of punishment this sentence reflects.*

```
Reparation      unpaid work repairs harm done to the community
Rehabilitation  structured work and supervision aim to change behaviour
Deterrence      individual: breach of the order risks a harsher sentence
Not retribution in its strongest form: no custody, reflecting
                that the offence is at the lower end of seriousness
```

The answer scores because each theory is **defined, applied to Dev and tied to a feature of the sentence**, not just named.

## Worked example 2: planning an evaluation

*Evaluate the view that the law should reflect morality.*

Plan four moves: (1) define law and morality and their overlap; (2) set out Devlin and Hart, with Wolfenden as the trigger; (3) test them against cases, such as *Brown* (Devlin-style) against *Wilson* (closer to Hart); (4) conclude, for example, that the law broadly follows Hart in private conduct but still intervenes where it sees a risk of serious harm.

## Common mistakes

- Calling the claimant the "prosecutor", or saying a criminal defendant is "liable".
- Naming a theory of punishment without linking it to a sentence.
- Writing about positivism or natural law with no theorist, which the specification requires.
- Retelling Hart/Devlin without applying it to a case.

## Exam technique for the command words

The specification's command word taxonomy defines the words used in questions:

- **Identify** and **give/state**: recall only, such as naming a theory or the standard of proof.
- **Describe**: give an account that shows the meaning of a legal term.
- **Explain**: make a point and justify or exemplify it; one linked example per point.
- **Analyse**: break an issue into parts and show how they relate.
- **Assess**: weigh up factors and say which matter most.
- **Evaluate**: review the law and evidence and reach a justified conclusion.
