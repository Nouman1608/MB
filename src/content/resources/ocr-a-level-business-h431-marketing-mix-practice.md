## Questions

### Section A: short-answer questions

**1.** Explain what is meant by the marketing mix. **[2]**

**2.** Distinguish between a marketing strategy and a marketing mix. **[3]**

**3.** Explain why the four elements of the marketing mix should be consistent with each other. **[3]**

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**4.** Tarn Candles makes scented candles. A candle costs £15.00 to make. In the UK the retailer adds a 35% mark-up and VAT of 20% is added.

**(a)** Calculate the UK shelf price. **[2]**

**(b)** For export, shipping is £2.50 a candle and an import tariff of 10% applies to the cost plus shipping. Calculate the landed cost. **[2]**

**(c)** The overseas distributor adds 25%, the retailer adds 35% and 20% sales tax is added. Calculate the overseas shelf price, to the nearest penny, rounding each step to the nearest penny. **[3]**

**(d)** Calculate by what percentage the overseas shelf price is higher than the UK shelf price, to one decimal place. **[2]**

**(e)** Tarn Candles sells directly online instead, adding a 20% margin to the landed cost, and then 20% sales tax is added. Calculate the shelf price. **[2]**

**(f)** Evaluate how Tarn Candles should market its candles in the overseas market. **[9]**

**5.** Harrow Bakehouse has one bakery in a small town and sells bread, cakes and pies to local people. It plans to sell its cakes nationally through an online shop with next-day delivery. Evaluate an appropriate marketing mix for the national market. **[9]**

**6.** A global drinks company uses the same bottle design, price positioning, advertising theme and distribution approach in every country. Evaluate whether it should keep a standardised marketing mix. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** The combination of decisions a business makes about its product, price, place and promotion [1] to meet customer needs and achieve its marketing objectives [1]. **[2]**

**2.** A marketing strategy is the long-term plan for achieving marketing objectives, including which customers to target and how to compete [1]. The marketing mix is the set of tools (product, price, place and promotion) used to carry out the strategy [1]. The strategy changes slowly, while the mix can be adjusted more often [1]. **[3]**

**3.** If the elements conflict, customers receive a confusing message [1], for example a luxury product sold at a heavy discount in a discount store [1], which can damage the brand's image and make the promotion less effective [1]. **[3]**

**4. (a)** 15.00 × 1.35 × 1.20 [1] = **£24.30** [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** Tariff = 10% × (15.00 + 2.50) = £1.75 [1]; landed cost = 15.00 + 2.50 + 1.75 = **£19.25** [1]. **[2]**

**(c)** Distributor: 19.25 × 1.25 = £24.06 [1]. Retailer: 24.06 × 1.35 = £32.48 [1]. Sales tax: 32.48 × 1.20 = **£38.98** [1]. **[3]**

**(d)** (38.98 ÷ 24.30 − 1) × 100 [1] = **60.4%** [1]. **[2]**

**(e)** 19.25 × 1.20 = £23.10 [1]; 23.10 × 1.20 = **£27.72** [1]. **[2]**

**(f)** Product: adapt labels, language and scents where tastes or safety rules differ, while keeping the quality that supports the brand [1].
Price: at £38.98 through a distributor, the candle is 60.4% dearer than in the UK, which may make it too expensive against local brands [1]; selling directly online gives £27.72, which is much lower and gives Tarn a clearer price position [1].
Place: direct online selling removes the distributor and retailer margins [1], but Tarn must handle delivery, returns and customer service in another country, and the shipping cost may rise for small orders [1]; a distributor gives access to shops and local knowledge [1].
Promotion: local-language digital advertising and social media can reach targeted customers cheaply, but need knowledge of local tastes [1].
Judgement: Tarn should start with direct online sales to a few countries, adapt the labels and promotion, and test demand before using distributors, as this has a lower price and less risk, although it requires investment in delivery and customer service [2]. **[9]**

**5.** Product: the online shop needs packaging that protects cakes in transit and a longer shelf life or a range that travels well, such as fruit cakes [1]; the local reputation for freshness may need to be replaced by a quality and craft story [1].
Price: delivery and packaging costs must be covered, and national customers will compare with rival online bakers, so a premium may be justified only for distinctive products [1]; free delivery over a set spend could lift basket size [1].
Place: an online shop with next-day delivery widens the reach, but needs a reliable courier and capacity to bake to order [1]; the shop itself remains for local customers [1].
Promotion: local word of mouth no longer works nationally, so social media, search advertising and reviews are needed [1]; the cost must be affordable for a one-shop business [1].
Judgement: the national mix must be different from the local one, and the risk is that spending and complexity outgrow the business, so Harrow should test with a small range and a limited number of regions before expanding [1]. **[9]**

**6.** For standardisation: one design and campaign saves design and advertising costs [1]; a consistent image builds a recognised global brand [1]; production and distribution are simpler and cheaper [1]; consumers who travel see the same brand [1].
Against: tastes differ by country, so one product may not suit every market (sweetness, for example) [1]; incomes differ, so a single price positioning may be unaffordable in some markets or too cheap in others [1]; advertising themes may not suit cultures or laws, and may even offend [1]; distribution differs, with supermarkets in some countries and small shops in others [1].
Evaluation: standardisation is strongest for the core brand identity, such as the logo and values, and weakest for detail such as flavour, price and channel [1]. Costs saved are real but may be small compared with sales lost through a poor fit [1]. Judgement: the company should keep a standard core and adapt the mix in each country where research shows a clear difference in demand, laws or income [2]. **[12]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Rounding the shelf price at the wrong step, or adding the tariff after the distributor margin.
- Listing four Ps without applying them to the business.
- Treating the mix as identical in every market.
- Giving no judgement on whether to standardise or adapt.
