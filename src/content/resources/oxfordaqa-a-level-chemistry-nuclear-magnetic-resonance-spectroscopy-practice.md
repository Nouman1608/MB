## Questions

Use these approximate shift ranges, written for this set. In the exam, use the Chemistry data booklet.

| ¹H environment | δ / ppm | ¹³C environment | δ / ppm |
|---|---|---|---|
| Alkyl H | 0.7–1.7 | Alkyl C | 5–45 |
| H on C next to C=O | 2.0–3.0 | C next to C=O | 20–55 |
| H on C bonded to Cl or Br | 3.0–4.5 | C bonded to Cl or Br | 30–75 |
| H on C bonded to O | 3.1–4.3 | C bonded to O | 50–90 |
| Alcohol O–H (variable) | 1.0–5.0 | C=O, acid or ester | 160–185 |
| Acid COOH | 10.0–12.0 | C=O, aldehyde or ketone | 190–220 |

For infrared, take alcohol O–H as a broad absorption at about 3230–3550 cm⁻¹ and C=O at about
1680–1750 cm⁻¹.

**1.** Before a spectrum is run, a little TMS is mixed into the sample.

**(a)** Give the molecular formula of TMS. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain **two** properties that make TMS suitable as the standard. **[2]**

**2.** A chemist has three possible solvents for a ¹H NMR spectrum: CDCl₃, CCl₄ and ethanol, C₂H₅OH.
Explain why the first two are suitable and ethanol is not. **[3]**

**3.** State the number of lines in the ¹³C NMR spectrum of each compound.

**(a)** pentan-3-ol, CH₃CH₂CH(OH)CH₂CH₃ **[1]**
**(b)** 2,3-dimethylbutane, (CH₃)₂CHCH(CH₃)₂ **[1]**
**(c)** methyl 2-methylpropanoate, (CH₃)₂CHCOOCH₃ **[1]**
**(d)** 1,3-dichloropropane, ClCH₂CH₂CH₂Cl **[1]**

**4.** Compound J, C₄H₇ClO₂, is an ester. Its ¹H NMR spectrum is:

| δ / ppm | Splitting | Integration trace height / mm |
|---|---|---|
| 4.2 | quartet | 16 |
| 4.1 | singlet | 16 |
| 1.3 | triplet | 24 |

**(a)** Work out how many H atoms each signal represents. **[2]**
**(b)** Deduce the structure of J, using the splitting and the shifts. **[3]**

**5.** Predict the ¹H NMR spectrum of methoxyethane, CH₃OCH₂CH₃. For each signal give an approximate
chemical shift range, the splitting and the relative integration. **[3]**

**6.**

**(a)** Explain why chemists use several analytical techniques, rather than one, to confirm the
structure of a new compound. **[1]**
**(b)** Suggest **two** reasons why a ¹³C NMR spectrum is easier to interpret than a ¹H NMR spectrum. **[2]**

**7.** A student writes: "The CH₃ signal in a spectrum is a doublet, so the CH₃ group must be next to a
carbon with 2 hydrogen atoms." Correct the statement and explain the rule. **[2]**

**8.** Compound K has a molecular ion at m/z = 70 and the formula C₅H₁₀. It does not decolourise bromine
water. Its ¹³C spectrum has one line at δ 26, and its ¹H spectrum has one singlet. Identify K and
explain why pent-1-ene is ruled out by the NMR data. **[3]**

**9.** Compound L has the formula C₆H₁₂O₂ and the following data.

- Infrared: broad absorption at 3450 cm⁻¹ and strong absorption at 1710 cm⁻¹.
- ¹³C NMR: five lines, at δ 210, 70, 54, 32 and 29.
- ¹H NMR: four singlets.

| δ / ppm | Integration trace height / mm |
|---|---|
| 3.8 | 3 |
| 2.6 | 6 |
| 2.2 | 9 |
| 1.2 | 18 |

**(a)** Convert the trace heights into numbers of H atoms. **[2]**
**(b)** Deduce the structure of L. Explain how each set of data supports your answer. **[6]**

**10.** 1-chlorobutan-2-one, ClCH₂COCH₂CH₃, and 4-chlorobutan-2-one, CH₃COCH₂CH₂Cl, are isomers.

**(a)** Predict the ¹H NMR spectrum of 1-chlorobutan-2-one (shift, splitting, integration). **[3]**
**(b)** Do the same for 4-chlorobutan-2-one. **[3]**
**(c)** State the feature of the spectra that most clearly distinguishes the two isomers. **[1]**

**11.** Compound M, C₅H₁₁Cl, gives these spectra.

- ¹³C NMR: four lines, at δ 71, 39, 32 and 9.
- ¹H NMR:

| δ / ppm | Splitting | Integration trace height / mm |
|---|---|---|
| 1.7 | quartet | 10 |
| 1.6 | singlet | 30 |
| 1.0 | triplet | 15 |

**(a)** Work out the H count for each signal. **[2]**
**(b)** Deduce the structure of M, explaining your reasoning. **[4]**
**(c)** Explain why the ¹³C spectrum rules out 1-chloro-2,2-dimethylpropane, (CH₃)₃CCH₂Cl. **[1]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** **Si(CH₃)₄**, or SiC₄H₁₂ [1]
**(b)** All 12 H are equivalent, so it gives a single intense signal [1]. Its signal lies upfield of (lower δ than) nearly every organic signal, so the two do not clash (also accept: inert, so it does
not react with the sample; or volatile, so it is easily removed) [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Give each property with its consequence; a bare "it is inert" may not earn the mark.

**2.** CDCl₃ contains deuterium instead of ¹H, and deuterium gives no signal in a ¹H spectrum [1]. CCl₄
contains no hydrogen at all [1]. Ethanol contains ¹H and is present in large excess, so its
signals would swamp the sample's [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Calling the solvents "unreactive" misses the point: they give no ¹H signal.

**3. (a)** **3** (the two ethyl groups are equivalent) [1]
**(b)** **2** (four equivalent CH₃ and two equivalent CH) [1]
**(c)** **4** (C=O, O–CH₃, CH, and the two equivalent CH₃ on the CH) [1]
**(d)** **2** (two equivalent CH₂Cl carbons and the central CH₂) [1] **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Mark equivalent carbons on a displayed formula first; a near-miss count earns nothing.

**4. (a)** 16 : 16 : 24 = 2 : 2 : 3 [1]. 2 + 2 + 3 = 7, which equals the 7 H in C₄H₇ClO₂, so the
signals are **2H (δ 4.2), 2H (δ 4.1) and 3H (δ 1.3)** [1].
**(b)** The 2H quartet and 3H triplet are an ethyl group, CH₃CH₂– [1]. The quartet is at δ 4.2, so this
CH₂ is bonded to the ester O: –OCH₂CH₃ [1]. The 2H singlet at δ 4.1 is a CH₂ with no H neighbours,
bonded to Cl and next to C=O, so J is **ClCH₂COOCH₂CH₃ (ethyl chloroethanoate)** [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Show that the scaled ratio adds up to the formula's H count, so the method is seen.

**5.** CH₃–O: δ 3.1–4.3, **singlet** (the O has no H), integration **3** [1]. O–CH₂: δ 3.1–4.3,
**quartet** (next to CH₃), integration **2** [1]. CH₃ of the ethyl group: δ 0.7–1.7, **triplet** (next
to CH₂), integration **3** [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* A signal described without its δ range, its pattern or its H count is incomplete, and that signal's mark can be lost.

**6. (a)** Each technique gives different information, and only together do they fix one structure [1].
**(b)** ¹³C signals are not split, so each environment gives one line [1]. The ¹³C δ range is much wider
than the ¹H range, so lines rarely overlap [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* "It is easier to read" repeats the question; name the feature that makes it simpler.

**7.** A doublet means the adjacent carbon carries **1** H, not 2 [1]. A signal is split into n + 1 peaks
by n non-equivalent H on the adjacent carbons, so 2 neighbouring H would give a triplet [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* State the rule in terms of H on **adjacent** carbons; rules worded around the group's
own H are marked as wrong.

**8.** K is a saturated compound with all 5 C and all 10 H equivalent, so it is **cyclopentane** [1].
Pent-1-ene has five different carbon environments, so it would give 5 ¹³C lines, not 1 [1]. It would
also give several ¹H signals, including H on C=C at δ 4.5–6.0, not a single singlet [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* The question asks for NMR evidence; the bromine water result alone does not answer it.

**9. (a)** 3 : 6 : 9 : 18 = 1 : 2 : 3 : 6 [1]. 1 + 2 + 3 + 6 = 12, matching the 12 H of C₆H₁₂O₂, so the
signals are **1H (δ 3.8), 2H (δ 2.6), 3H (δ 2.2) and 6H (δ 1.2)** [1].
**(b)** The broad IR band at 3450 cm⁻¹ is an alcohol O–H, and 1710 cm⁻¹ is C=O [1]. The ¹³C line at δ 210
shows a ketone (or aldehyde) C=O, and there is no ¹H signal at δ 9–10, so it is a ketone [1]. The ¹³C
line at δ 70 is a carbon bonded to O, the C–OH [1]. The 6H singlet means a pair of identical methyls on a carbon bearing no H; the 1H singlet at δ 3.8 is the O–H; so the fragment is (CH₃)₂C(OH)– [1]. The 3H
singlet at δ 2.2 is CH₃CO–, and the 2H singlet at δ 2.6 is a CH₂ next to C=O with no H neighbours [1]. L is **CH₃COCH₂C(CH₃)₂OH, 4-hydroxy-4-methylpentan-2-one**, which has five carbon
environments, matching the five ¹³C lines [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Name the signal behind each deduction; "the NMR shows" links no evidence.

**10. (a)** ClCH₂: δ 3.0–4.5, **singlet** (neighbour is C=O), **2H** [1]. CH₂ of the ethyl group: δ 2.0–3.0,
**quartet**, **2H** [1]. CH₃: δ 0.7–1.7, **triplet**, **3H** [1].
**(b)** CH₃CO: δ 2.0–3.0, **singlet**, **3H** [1]. COCH₂: δ 2.0–3.0, **triplet**, **2H** [1]. CH₂Cl: δ 3.0–4.5,
**triplet**, **2H** [1].
**(c)** Splitting: only 1-chlorobutan-2-one shows a quartet (from its ethyl group); 4-chlorobutan-2-one
shows two triplets instead [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* Both isomers give three signals, so "the number of peaks" cannot earn part (c).

**11. (a)** 10 : 30 : 15 = 2 : 6 : 3 [1]. 2 + 6 + 3 = 11, matching the 11 H in C₅H₁₁Cl, so **2H, 6H and
3H** [1].
**(b)** The 2H quartet and 3H triplet are an ethyl group, CH₃CH₂– [1]. The 6H singlet is two equivalent
CH₃ groups on a carbon that has no H [1]. The ¹³C line at δ 71 is the carbon bonded to Cl, and it carries
no H, because no ¹H signal is in the 3.0–4.5 range [1]. M is **(CH₃)₂CClCH₂CH₃, 2-chloro-2-methylbutane**,
with four carbon environments [1].
**(c)** 1-chloro-2,2-dimethylpropane has only three carbon environments, so it would give 3 lines, not
4 [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* A missing signal is evidence too: no ¹H signal at 3.0–4.5 points to a tertiary chloride.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Miscounting environments because equivalent CH₃ or CH₂ groups were not spotted.
- Applying n+1 to the group's own H instead of the H on **adjacent** carbons.
- Giving a CH₂ next to C=O or O a splitting when its other neighbour carries no H.
- Stopping at the simplest integration ratio when the formula requires scaling.
- Using the NMR alone in a combined question and ignoring the infrared or molecular formula.
- Giving a structure without checking it against the molecular formula.
