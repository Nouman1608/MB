This guide covers **exclusion clauses** in section 3.4 Law of contract of AQA A-level Law (7162). The
specification requires a "basic understanding of the nature of exclusion and limitation clauses", the
"common law control of exclusion clauses: rules relating to incorporation; brief understanding of the
rules relating to construction", and "statutory control of exclusion clauses: Unfair Contract Terms Act
1977 (s2 and s3); Consumer Rights Act 2015 (s31, s57 and s65)". For the contract option in outline, start
with the [Law of contract study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-law-law-of-contract-study-guide/).

## The nature of exclusion and limitation clauses

An **exclusion clause** tries to remove a party's liability altogether ("the company accepts no
liability for loss or damage"). A **limitation clause** caps liability at a figure or a measure ("our
liability shall not exceed the price paid"). Courts treat limitation clauses a little less strictly,
because they allocate risk rather than remove it. Both may appear as a term of a contract or as a
**notice**, such as a sign in a car park.

The theory section of the specification asks about the "nature and effectiveness of exemption clauses".
They are legitimate in business, where parties use them to decide who insures against a risk, but they
can be abused by a stronger party against a weaker one who has no chance to negotiate. The law responds
in three stages, which give the structure of any problem answer.

1. **Incorporation**: is the clause part of the contract?
2. **Construction**: on its true meaning, does it cover the loss that happened?
3. **Statute**: is it allowed by UCTA 1977 (business contracts) or the CRA 2015 (consumer contracts)?

## Stage 1: incorporation

### By signature

A person who signs a contractual document is bound by its terms whether or not they read them:
**L'Estrange v Graucob (1934)**, where a café owner who signed an order form for a vending machine was
bound by a clause in small print excluding all implied warranties. The exception is
**misrepresentation of the clause's effect**: in **Curtis v Chemical Cleaning and Dyeing Co (1951)** an
assistant told the customer that a form only excluded liability for damage to beads and sequins, so the
cleaners could not rely on the wider clause when the dress was stained.

### By reasonable notice

Where there is no signature, the clause is incorporated only if reasonable notice of it was given
**before or at the time** the contract was made.

- **Timing.** In **Olley v Marlborough Court Hotel (1949)** a notice in a hotel bedroom came too late,
  because the contract was made at the reception desk. In **Thornton v Shoe Lane Parking (1971)** the
  contract was made when the customer drove up to the automatic barrier, so terms on the ticket that
  came out of the machine were too late.
- **The document must be contractual.** In **Chapelton v Barry UDC (1940)** a ticket for a deckchair
  was a mere receipt that a reasonable person would not expect to contain terms.
- **Reasonable steps.** In **Parker v South Eastern Railway (1877)** the question was whether the
  company had done what was reasonably sufficient to give notice of the conditions, not whether the
  customer actually read them.
- **The more unusual the clause, the greater the notice.** In **Spurling v Bradshaw (1956)** Denning LJ
  said some clauses would need to be "printed in red ink on the face of the document with a red hand
  pointing to it". **Interfoto Picture Library v Stiletto Visual Programmes (1989)** applied the same
  idea to an onerous term that had not been fairly brought to the other party's attention.

### By a previous course of dealing

A clause may be incorporated by a consistent and regular course of dealing. In
**McCutcheon v David MacBrayne (1964)** inconsistent previous dealings were not enough. In
**Hollier v Rambler Motors (1972)** three or four transactions over five years did not amount to a
course of dealing with a private customer.

## Stage 2: construction (brief)

- **Contra proferentem**: an ambiguous clause is read against the party relying on it. In
  **Houghton v Trafalgar Insurance (1954)** a policy excluding liability where a car carried a "load"
  in excess of its capacity did not cover carrying too many passengers. For consumer contracts the CRA
  2015 states the rule in statute: the meaning most favourable to the consumer prevails.
- **Negligence**: clear words are needed to exclude liability for negligence (**Canada Steamship Lines v
  The King, 1952**).
- **Serious breaches**: whether a clause covers even a fundamental breach is a question of construction
  of the whole contract. In **Photo Production v Securicor (1980)** a clear clause between businesses of
  equal bargaining power protected a security firm whose employee started a fire that destroyed the
  factory.

## Stage 3: statutory control

Since 1 October 2015, UCTA 1977 governs **business-to-business** contracts and the CRA 2015 governs
**trader-to-consumer** contracts. Choosing the right Act is the first marking point.

### Unfair Contract Terms Act 1977

| Section | Rule |
|---|---|
| s2(1) | A person **cannot** exclude or restrict liability for **death or personal injury** resulting from negligence, by a contract term or a notice |
| s2(2) | For **other loss or damage** caused by negligence, exclusion or restriction is valid only if it satisfies the **requirement of reasonableness** |
| s3 | Where one party deals on the other's **written standard terms of business**, the other cannot exclude or restrict liability for breach, or claim to perform substantially differently or not at all, except so far as the term is reasonable |

**The reasonableness test** (section 11) asks whether the term was fair and reasonable having regard to
the circumstances that were known, or ought reasonably to have been known, to the parties **when the
contract was made**. The party relying on the clause must prove it is reasonable. Relevant factors
include the parties' relative bargaining strength, whether the customer was offered an alternative,
whether they knew or ought to have known of the term, and, for limitation clauses, the resources of the
party relying on it and whether it could have insured.

- **George Mitchell v Finney Lock Seeds (1983)**: a clause limiting liability to the price of defective
  cabbage seed was unreasonable. The sellers could have insured, the loss to the farmers was far greater,
  and the sellers had in practice settled such claims in the past.
- **Smith v Eric S Bush (1990)**: a surveyor's disclaimer to a house buyer of modest means was
  unreasonable.
- **Watford Electronics v Sanderson (2001)**: a limitation clause negotiated between experienced
  commercial parties of similar bargaining power was reasonable; the courts should be slow to interfere.

### Consumer Rights Act 2015

| Section | Rule |
|---|---|
| s31 | A term is **not binding** on the consumer to the extent that it would exclude or restrict the trader's liability under the goods terms, including **ss9, 10 and 11** |
| s57 | A term cannot exclude liability under **s49** (reasonable care and skill); a term restricting liability under s49 or s52 is not binding if it would prevent the consumer recovering the **price paid** |
| s65 | A trader cannot by a term or a **consumer notice** exclude or restrict liability for **death or personal injury resulting from negligence**; agreeing to or knowing of such a term is not, of itself, voluntary acceptance of the risk |

Other exclusion terms in consumer contracts are subject to the Act's **fairness test**: a term is unfair
if, contrary to the requirement of good faith, it causes a significant imbalance in the parties' rights
and obligations to the detriment of the consumer, and an unfair term is not binding on the consumer.

## Worked example

*Facts: Kofi, a private individual, leaves his bicycle at Fastfix Ltd for a service. He signs a form that
says: "Fastfix accepts no liability for any loss or damage to customers' property, however caused." A
mechanic negligently leaves the bicycle outside and it is stolen. When Kofi collects his spare wheel, a
sign at the counter says Fastfix is not liable for personal injury; a loose spoke then cuts his hand.*

```
Nature:         Clause 1 is an exclusion clause in a signed form; the sign is a
                notice.
Incorporation:  Signed form, so incorporated (L'Estrange v Graucob), unless
                its effect was misrepresented (Curtis). The sign may be too
                late if seen only after the contract (Olley; Thornton).
Construction:   "However caused" is wide enough to cover negligence
                (Canada Steamship).
Statute:        Kofi is a consumer and Fastfix a trader, so the CRA 2015
                applies, not UCTA.
                Theft through careless storage is a breach of s49; under
                s57 the clause cannot exclude that liability, so it does
                not bind Kofi.
                Injury caused by negligence cannot be excluded by a term or
                a consumer notice (s65), so the sign has no effect.
Conclusion:     Fastfix is liable for the bicycle and the injury.
```

If Kofi had been a courier using the bicycle for his business, UCTA would apply instead: s2(1) would
still defeat the injury notice, but the property clause would stand or fall on the s11 reasonableness
test.

## Common mistakes

- Starting with statute before asking whether the clause was incorporated at all.
- Applying UCTA to a consumer contract, or the CRA to a contract between two businesses.
- Saying all exclusion clauses are void. Many are valid between businesses if reasonable.
- Judging reasonableness by what happened after the contract; section 11 looks at the time the contract
  was made.
- Forgetting that a signed document binds even if unread, subject to Curtis.

## Exam technique

- **Explain** questions: distinguish exclusion from limitation, and describe each stage with one case or
  section.
- **Suggest** and **Advise** questions: take each clause through incorporation, construction and
  statute in that order, identify whether the claimant is a consumer, and conclude on liability.
- **Consider** questions: develop the arguments on reasonableness or notice either way, for example
  whether a clause on the back of a ticket was given in time.
- **Discuss** questions: evaluate how effectively the common law and statute balance freedom of contract
  with protection of weaker parties.
