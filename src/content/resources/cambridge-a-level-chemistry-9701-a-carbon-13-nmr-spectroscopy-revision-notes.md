**Syllabus:** Cambridge International AS & A Level Chemistry 9701, 2025–2027, **A Level** content:
subtopic 37.3 Carbon-13 NMR spectroscopy. You must deduce carbon environments and possible structures from a
spectrum of a simple molecule, and predict or explain the number of peaks for a given molecule.

## The two facts you read from a spectrum

1. **Number of peaks = number of carbon environments.** Equivalent carbons, related by the molecule's
   symmetry, share one peak.
2. **Position of each peak (δ / ppm, relative to TMS = 0)** identifies the type of environment, read from the
   carbon-13 shift table in the syllabus data section.

Splitting and peak heights are not part of the 37.3 outcomes. Do not use them to count carbons.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning for 9701 |
|---|---|
| Carbon environment | A carbon position in the molecule, defined by what it is bonded to throughout the structure |
| Equivalent carbons | Carbons that are interchanged by a mirror plane or rotation of the molecule; they give one peak |
| Chemical shift, δ | Position of a peak in ppm relative to the TMS reference at δ = 0 |
| TMS | Tetramethylsilane, Si(CH₃)₄, the reference compound for chemical shift |
| sp³ / sp² / sp carbon | Hybridisation used to group the rows of the carbon-13 shift table |
| Possible structures | Every structure consistent with the data; there may be more than one |

## Shift regions to recognise quickly

You use the printed table in the examination, but recognising its regions speeds up your reading.

| Region / ppm | What it usually means |
|---|---|
| 0–50 | sp³ alkyl carbon (25–50 if next to an alkene or arene) |
| 30–70 | sp³ carbon bonded to a halogen (30–60), next to C=O (30–65) or bonded to oxygen (50–70) |
| 100–125 | nitrile carbon, C≡N |
| 110–160 | alkene or arene carbon |
| 160–185 | carboxylic acid or ester carbon |
| 190–220 | aldehyde or ketone carbon |

The key split: **ketone or aldehyde C=O, 190–220; acid or ester C=O, 160–185.**

## Peak counts worth knowing

**Chains**

| Molecule | Peaks | Why |
|---|---|---|
| propanone, CH₃COCH₃ | 2 | two equivalent CH₃; one C=O |
| propanal, CH₃CH₂CHO | 3 | no symmetry |
| pentan-2-one, CH₃COCH₂CH₂CH₃ | 5 | no symmetry |
| 3-methylbutan-2-one, CH₃COCH(CH₃)₂ | 4 | the two CH₃ on the CH are equivalent |
| 1-chloropropane, CH₃CH₂CH₂Cl | 3 | no symmetry |
| 2-chloropropane, CH₃CHClCH₃ | 2 | two equivalent CH₃ |
| but-2-ene, CH₃CH=CHCH₃ | 2 | the molecule is symmetrical about the C=C |
| propanenitrile, CH₃CH₂CN | 3 | no symmetry |

**Rings**

| Molecule | Peaks | Why |
|---|---|---|
| benzene | 1 | all six carbons equivalent |
| phenol | 4 | C–OH; two ortho; two meta; one para |
| benzoic acid | 5 | COOH; C1; two ortho; two meta; one para |
| 1,2-disubstituted ring, two identical groups | 3 ring | pairs: C1/C2, C3/C6, C4/C5 |
| 1,3-disubstituted ring, two identical groups | 4 ring | C1/C3, C4/C6 pair; C2 and C5 single |
| 1,4-disubstituted ring, two identical groups | 2 ring | C1/C4; the four C–H carbons |

Add any substituent carbons to the ring count: 1,3-dimethylbenzene gives 4 ring peaks plus 1 for the two
equivalent CH₃ groups, so **5** in total.

## Deduction routine

1. Compare the number of peaks with the number of carbons in the molecular formula. Fewer peaks than
   carbons means symmetry.
2. Assign each peak to a region of the table.
3. Use the 160–185 versus 190–220 split to classify any C=O group.
4. List every isomer of the formula that fits; eliminate those whose regions or counts disagree.
5. If more than one survives, say so and name the evidence that would decide (IR, proton NMR, D₂O exchange,
   or a chemical test).

## Exam traps

- Counting atoms rather than environments in substituted rings.
- Assuming the 1,2-, 1,3- and 1,4-isomers give the same count.
- Placing an ester or acid carbonyl carbon in the 190–220 region.
- Putting every carbon of an alcohol or ether in the 50–70 region; only the carbon bonded to O goes there.
- Missing a quaternary carbon (a carbon with no hydrogens, such as the central C of (CH₃)₃COH). It is still an
  environment and still gives a peak.
- Forgetting that the C≡N carbon is a separate environment in a nitrile.
- Stopping at one structure when the question asks for possible structures.

## Self-test

1. How many carbon-13 peaks does propanone give?
2. How many does 2-chloropropane give, and why is it fewer than 1-chloropropane?
3. A peak appears at 205 ppm. Which functional groups could the carbon belong to?
4. A peak appears at 172 ppm. Which functional groups could the carbon belong to?
5. How many peaks does phenol give?
6. How many peaks does 1,3-dimethylbenzene give?
7. Give the number of peaks for 1,4-dichlorobenzene and explain it.
8. Why might a peak at 55 ppm be hard to assign without the molecular formula?
9. Compound X, C₃H₆O, shows two peaks, one of them in the 190–220 region. Identify X.
10. Compound Y, C₃H₆O, shows three peaks, one of them in the 190–220 region. Identify Y.
11. Two isomers of C₄H₁₀O give two peaks each. Name them.
12. What is the reference compound for carbon-13 chemical shifts, and what δ value is it given?

### Answers

1. **2**: the two CH₃ carbons are equivalent; the C=O carbon is the second environment.
2. **2**: its two CH₃ groups are equivalent, both attached to the CHCl carbon. In 1-chloropropane all three
   carbons are different, giving 3.
3. An **aldehyde or a ketone** (the 190–220 carbonyl region).
4. A **carboxylic acid or an ester** (the 160–185 carboxyl region).
5. **4**: the carbon bearing OH, the two ortho carbons, the two meta carbons and the para carbon.
6. **5**: four ring environments (C1/C3, C2, C4/C6, C5) plus one for the two equivalent CH₃ groups.
7. **2**: the two C–Cl carbons are equivalent, and all four C–H carbons are equivalent, because the molecule
   is symmetrical across both axes of the ring.
8. Several sp³ ranges overlap there: bonded to oxygen (50–70), bonded to a halogen (30–60) and next to a
   carbonyl group (30–65). The formula shows which of these atoms are present.
9. **Propanone**, CH₃COCH₃: a ketone with two equivalent CH₃ carbons.
10. **Propanal**, CH₃CH₂CHO: an aldehyde with three different carbons.
11. **2-methylpropan-2-ol** and **ethoxyethane**.
12. **Tetramethylsilane (TMS)**, given δ = 0.
