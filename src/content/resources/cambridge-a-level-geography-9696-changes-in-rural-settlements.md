This guide covers section 6.1 of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Geography (9696) syllabus for examination in 2025 and 2026: "Contemporary issues in rural settlements in LICs, MICs and HICs, (e.g. depopulation, service provision) including the impacts of internal migration and the consequences of urban growth", together with a required case study of "a rural settlement (village or hamlet) or a rural area showing some of the issues of its development and growth (or decline) and evaluating the responses to these issues." It opens Topic 6, Settlement dynamics, in Paper 2 Core Human Geography.

The [6.1 revision notes](/resources/a-level-geography-9696-changes-in-rural-settlements-revision-notes/) cover key terms, the cycle of rural decline and an outline of rural depopulation in Japan. This guide explains why rural places change differently, adds examples from other countries, and shows how to evaluate responses. Practise afterwards with the [6.1 practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-changes-in-rural-settlements-practice/).

## Why rural places change differently

Rural settlements are not one type. The most useful way to sort them is by **accessibility to a city**:

- **Accessible rural areas** within commuting range gain people, often wealthier ones, and face pressure on housing and land.
- **Intermediate areas** may hold steady, with a mix of commuters, farmers and retirees.
- **Remote areas** lose young people and services, and age rapidly.

In the 1970s Paul Cloke built an "index of rurality" for England and Wales from measures such as population density, age structure, commuting and distance from towns, showing that rurality is a continuum rather than a simple rural-urban divide. The same idea applies in LICs and MICs: a village on a highway near a growing city is changing in very different ways from one several hours' walk from a road.

## Contemporary issues in LICs and MICs

**Depopulation and its selectivity.** Out-migration of young adults, often men, leaves villages with older people, women and children. Farm labour becomes scarce at peak seasons, and some land is left fallow.

**Remittance-led change.** Money from migrants pays for new concrete houses, school fees, motorbikes and phones. Villages can look more prosperous while their population falls, and households without migrants fall behind.

**Service provision.** Many villages lack reliable electricity, piped water, secondary schools and clinics. Low population densities mean high costs per person for each service, so governments concentrate investment in larger key villages and towns. Mobile technology has changed some services: in Kenya, the M-Pesa mobile money service, launched in 2007, lets rural households receive money from relatives in cities without travelling to a bank.

**Consequences of urban growth.** As cities expand, nearby villages are absorbed. In Delhi, many former farming villages were surrounded by the growing city. Their farmland was acquired for development, but the old village cores remained and were built up densely, often as rented housing for migrants. Former farmers became landlords, and the villages became crowded urban neighbourhoods with poor services.

**Rural employment policy.** India's National Rural Employment Guarantee Act (2005) promises up to 100 days of paid work a year to rural households on public works. One aim is to reduce distress migration by giving people paid work near home.

## Contemporary issues in HICs

**Remote decline.** Upland and island communities lose young adults to cities and universities. Schools close when pupil numbers fall, and each closure makes the place less attractive to families.

**Accessible growth.** Commuter villages gain population through counterurbanisation. House prices rise, new estates are built on the edge, and the village shop may still close because commuters shop near work.

**Second homes and holiday lets.** In attractive coastal and mountain areas, homes bought for occasional use stand empty for much of the year. Local young people are priced out, school rolls fall and shops become seasonal.

**Service decline and thresholds.** Each service needs a minimum number of users (its threshold). When population or spending falls below it, the service closes. Car ownership and online shopping lower the number of local customers even where population is stable.

## Responses and how to evaluate them

| Response | Example | Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rural employment guarantee | India, 2005 Act | Income close to home; builds local infrastructure | Wages low; delays in payment; does not stop all out-migration |
| Mobile and digital services | Mobile money in Kenya | Cheap; reaches scattered homes | Needs phone coverage and literacy; does not replace clinics or schools |
| Restricting second homes | St Ives, Cornwall: 2016 neighbourhood plan requires new-build homes to be principal residences | Keeps new housing for permanent residents | May push demand onto existing homes and raise their prices |
| Community land ownership | Isle of Eigg, Scotland: community buyout in 1997 | Local control; investment in housing and a renewable power grid (2008) | Small scale; relies on grants and volunteers |
| Key settlement policy | Concentrating services in larger villages | Services reach their thresholds | Smaller hamlets lose out and may decline faster |

When evaluating, ask three questions: **Did it work for its aim? Who gained and who lost? Is it sustainable without continuing subsidy?**

## Building the case study

The case study can be **one village or hamlet** or **a rural area**. It must show issues of development and growth (or decline), and evaluate responses. A complete case study has:

1. **Location and context:** where it is and how accessible it is to a city.
2. **Change over time:** population, age structure, economy and services at two or more dates.
3. **Causes:** internal migration, urban growth, changes in farming, tourism or second homes.
4. **Impacts:** on different groups, such as older residents, young families, farmers and incomers.
5. **Responses:** by government, local people, businesses or charities.
6. **Evaluation:** how far each response worked, for whom, and whether it is sustainable.

**Worked outline: the Isle of Eigg, Scotland (a remote HIC rural area).** For much of the twentieth century Eigg was owned by a series of private landlords, and its small population had insecure tenancies and little investment. In 1997 residents, the Highland Council and the Scottish Wildlife Trust bought the island through the Isle of Eigg Heritage Trust. The trust then invested in housing and businesses, and in 2008 the island switched on its own electricity grid powered by hydro, wind and solar generation, replacing private diesel generators. The population has grown since the buyout. **Evaluation:** community ownership reversed decline on Eigg, but the island is small, the scheme relied heavily on grants and volunteer work, and the model may be hard to copy where land is more valuable.

## Worked example: thresholds

*A village shop needs 500 regular customers to stay open. The village had 1 200 residents in 2005, of whom 50% shopped there regularly. By 2025 the population is 1 050 and only 40% shop there. Will the shop stay open?*

- 2005: 1 200 × 0.50 = **600** regular customers, above the threshold.
- 2025: 1 050 × 0.40 = **420** regular customers, below the threshold of 500.
- The shop is likely to close. Notice that the fall in the share of residents using it (from 50% to 40%), perhaps through car use and online shopping, matters more than the fall in population: at 50%, 1 050 residents would give 525 customers.

## Annotated model answer

*Question: Explain how urban growth can affect rural settlements near a city. [6]*

> As a city spreads, farmland around nearby villages is bought for housing, roads and industry (**land-use change**). Farmers may sell land and lose their livelihood, or gain capital (**economic impact, two sides**). Villages within commuting range attract wealthier residents, so house prices rise and local young people are priced out (**social impact**). In LIC and MIC cities, absorbed villages often become dense rented housing for migrants, as in Delhi, with overloaded water and sanitation (**service impact**). The village's function changes from farming to residential (**overall change**).

## Common mistakes

- Treating all rural areas as declining. Accessible areas often grow.
- Using only HIC examples. The syllabus asks for LICs, MICs and HICs.
- Describing the case study without evaluating responses.
- Explaining service closure only by population loss, ignoring car use and online shopping.
- Confusing counterurbanisation (a migration process) with suburbanisation (outward growth of the built-up area).

## Exam technique

- **Describe** change in a rural settlement from data: quote figures for two dates and identify the trend.
- **Explain** issues: link each issue to a cause, such as migration, urban growth or second homes.
- **Evaluate** responses: aim, evidence of success, winners and losers, sustainability, then a judgement.
- In Section B, a 6.1 question may combine with 5.2 on internal migration; use the migration ideas to explain rural change.
