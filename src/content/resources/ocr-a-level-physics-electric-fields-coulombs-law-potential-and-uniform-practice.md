These questions cover section 6.2 (Electric fields) of OCR A Level Physics A (H556), Version 3.0 (March 2026), first assessment 2017. Revise with the [study guide](/resources/ocr-a-level-physics-electric-fields-coulombs-law-potential-and-uniform/) and [notes](/resources/ocr-a-level-physics-electric-fields-coulombs-law-potential-and-uniform-revision-notes/). Data: g = 9.81 m s⁻²; G = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N m² kg⁻²; ε₀ = 8.85 × 10⁻¹² F m⁻¹; elementary charge 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C; electron mass 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ kg; proton mass 1.67 × 10⁻²⁷ kg.

## Questions

**1.** **(a)** Define electric field strength. **[1]**
**(b)** Describe the field lines around an isolated, positively charged sphere and say what the spacing of the lines shows. **[2]**

**2.** Two tiny charged beads carry +2.0 µC and −0.80 µC, with 0.25 m between their centres.
**(a)** Find the magnitude and direction of the force each exerts on the other. **[3]**
**(b)** Find the force when the separation is doubled. **[1]**

**3.** A uniformly charged sphere of radius 5.0 cm carries +18 nC.
**(a)** Calculate the electric field strength 0.15 m from its centre, naming the model you use. **[3]**
**(b)** Find the force on a −3.0 nC charge placed there, with its direction. **[2]**

**4.** A charge of +6.0 nC is at A and a charge of −8.0 nC is at B. A point P is 0.30 m from A and 0.40 m from B, and the lines PA and PB are at right angles. Find the magnitude of the resultant field at P and its direction relative to the line from A to P. **[6]**

**5.** In a hydrogen atom an electron orbits a proton at a distance of 5.3 × 10⁻¹¹ m.
**(a)** Calculate the electric force between them. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the gravitational force between them. **[2]**
**(c)** Find the ratio of the two forces. **[1]**

**6.** Two horizontal plates 2.5 cm apart have a p.d. of 750 V. An oil droplet carrying the charge of three excess electrons is held stationary between them.
Calculate the mass of the droplet and state which plate is positive. **[5]**

**7.** A capacitor has square plates of side 8.0 cm, 0.30 mm apart.
**(a)** Calculate its capacitance with air between the plates. **[2]**
**(b)** A material of relative permittivity 3.2 fills the gap. Calculate its permittivity and the new capacitance. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the charge stored at 12 V with the material in place. **[2]**

**8.** A proton enters midway between two horizontal plates, moving parallel to them at 1.0 × 10⁶ m s⁻¹. The plates are 5.0 cm long and 2.0 cm apart, with a p.d. of 600 V.
**(a)** Calculate the field strength between the plates. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the proton's acceleration. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the time it spends between the plates. **[1]**
**(d)** Calculate its sideways displacement when it leaves. **[2]**
**(e)** State whether it leaves the plates and find the angle of its path to the original direction. **[2]**

**9.** **(a)** Derive an expression for the capacitance of an isolated sphere of radius R in terms of R and ε₀. **[3]**
**(b)** Treating the Earth as an isolated sphere of radius 6.37 × 10⁶ m, calculate its capacitance. **[2]**

**10.** A point charge of −12 nC is fixed in air.
**(a)** Calculate the electric potential at 0.50 m and at 0.20 m from it. **[2]**
**(b)** A +2.0 nC charge is moved from 0.50 m to 0.20 m. Calculate the change in its potential energy and say what the sign shows. **[3]**
**(c)** State the potential energy of this charge at 0.20 m. **[1]**

**11.** A fixed sphere carries +5.0 µC. A +1.0 nC charge is pushed towards it from 0.80 m to 0.20 m (distances from the centre).
**(a)** Calculate the force on the charge at each distance. **[2]**
**(b)** Use a single trapezium to estimate the area under the force-distance graph. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the work done exactly using potentials. **[2]**
**(d)** Explain why the trapezium estimate differs from the exact value. **[1]**

**12.** An electron travelling at 4.0 × 10⁶ m s⁻¹ is fired from far away straight at a fixed sphere of radius 2.0 cm carrying −2.0 nC.
**(a)** Calculate the electron's initial kinetic energy. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate its distance from the sphere's centre at closest approach. **[3]**
**(c)** Calculate its speed when 1.0 m from the centre. **[3]**
**(d)** Explain why the point-charge model is valid here. **[2]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** Force per unit positive charge, E = F/q [1].
**(b)** Straight lines directed radially outwards, equally spaced [1]. Closer lines show a stronger field, so it weakens with distance [1].
*Examiner insight:* A definition needs "per unit positive charge", not just "force on a charge".

**2. (a)** F = Qq/(4πε₀r²) = (2.0 × 10⁻⁶ × 0.80 × 10⁻⁶) / (4π × 8.85 × 10⁻¹² × 0.25²) [1] = 0.23 N [1], attractive because the charges are unlike [1].
**(b)** The force falls to one quarter: 0.058 N [1].
*Examiner insight:* Give the direction in words.

**3. (a)** A uniformly charged sphere is modelled as a point charge at its centre [1]. E = Q/(4πε₀r²) = 18 × 10⁻⁹ / (4π × 8.85 × 10⁻¹² × 0.15²) [1] = 7.2 × 10³ N C⁻¹ [1].
**(b)** F = qE = 3.0 × 10⁻⁹ × 7.2 × 10³ = 2.2 × 10⁻⁵ N [1], towards the sphere because unlike charges attract [1].
*Examiner insight:* Name the model, and measure from the centre, not the surface.

**4.** E from A = 6.0 × 10⁻⁹ / (4π × 8.85 × 10⁻¹² × 0.30²) = 599 N C⁻¹, directed away from A [1]. E from B = 8.0 × 10⁻⁹ / (4π × 8.85 × 10⁻¹² × 0.40²) = 450 N C⁻¹ [1], directed towards B [1]. The fields are at right angles, so add as vectors [1]: E = √(599² + 450²) = **7.5 × 10² N C⁻¹** [1] at tan⁻¹(450/599) = **37°** to the line from A to P, turned towards B [1].
*Examiner insight:* Show each field separately before combining; adding 599 and 450 as plain numbers is a common slip.

**5. (a)** F = e²/(4πε₀r²) = (1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹)² / (4π × 8.85 × 10⁻¹² × (5.3 × 10⁻¹¹)²) [1] = 8.2 × 10⁻⁸ N [1].
**(b)** F = Gm_em_p/r² = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ × 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ × 1.67 × 10⁻²⁷ / (5.3 × 10⁻¹¹)² [1] = 3.6 × 10⁻⁴⁷ N [1].
**(c)** Ratio = 8.2 × 10⁻⁸ / 3.6 × 10⁻⁴⁷ = **2.3 × 10³⁹** [1].
*Examiner insight:* Check that both forces use the same r.

**6.** E = V/d = 750/0.025 = 3.0 × 10⁴ V m⁻¹ [1]. Charge q = 3 × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 4.8 × 10⁻¹⁹ C. In equilibrium qE = mg [1]. m = qE/g [1] = 4.8 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 3.0 × 10⁴ / 9.81 = **1.5 × 10⁻¹⁵ kg** [1]. The charge is negative, so the force is upwards, E points downwards and the **upper plate is positive** [1].
*Examiner insight:* Reason from the sign: the force on a negative charge is opposite to E.

**7. (a)** C = ε₀A/d = 8.85 × 10⁻¹² × 0.080² / 3.0 × 10⁻⁴ [1] = **1.9 × 10⁻¹⁰ F** [1].
**(b)** ε = εᵣε₀ = 3.2 × 8.85 × 10⁻¹² = **2.8 × 10⁻¹¹ F m⁻¹** [1]; C = εA/d = **6.0 × 10⁻¹⁰ F** [1].
**(c)** Q = VC = 12 × 6.04 × 10⁻¹⁰ [1] = **7.2 × 10⁻⁹ C** [1].
*Examiner insight:* Carry the unrounded capacitance into part (c).

**8. (a)** E = 600/0.020 = **3.0 × 10⁴ V m⁻¹** [1].
**(b)** a = eE/m [1] = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 3.0 × 10⁴ / 1.67 × 10⁻²⁷ = **2.9 × 10¹² m s⁻²** [1].
**(c)** t = 0.050 / 1.0 × 10⁶ = **5.0 × 10⁻⁸ s** [1].
**(d)** y = ½at² [1] = 0.5 × 2.87 × 10¹² × (5.0 × 10⁻⁸)² = **3.6 × 10⁻³ m** [1].
**(e)** 3.6 mm is less than 10 mm (half the gap), so it leaves [1]. tan θ = at/v = 1.44 × 10⁵ / 1.0 × 10⁶, so θ = **8.2°** [1].
*Examiner insight:* Find t from the horizontal motion, then compare y with half the gap.

**9. (a)** Potential at the surface (point-charge model): V = Q/(4πε₀R) [1]. C = Q/V [1] = Q ÷ Q/(4πε₀R) = **4πε₀R** [1].
**(b)** C = 4π × 8.85 × 10⁻¹² × 6.37 × 10⁶ [1] = **7.1 × 10⁻⁴ F** [1].
*Examiner insight:* A derivation shows the starting equations, then the cancellation.

**10. (a)** V = Q/(4πε₀r): at 0.50 m, V = **−216 V** [1]; at 0.20 m, V = **−540 V** [1].
**(b)** ΔV = −540 − (−216) = −324 V [1]. ΔE_p = qΔV = 2.0 × 10⁻⁹ × (−324) = **−6.5 × 10⁻⁷ J** [1]. The negative sign is a loss of potential energy: the field does the work as unlike charges move closer [1].
**(c)** E_p = Vq = −540 × 2.0 × 10⁻⁹ = **−1.1 × 10⁻⁶ J** [1].
*Examiner insight:* Keep the negative sign on Q in V.

**11. (a)** F = Qq/(4πε₀r²): at 0.80 m, **7.0 × 10⁻⁵ N** [1]; at 0.20 m, **1.1 × 10⁻³ N** [1].
**(b)** Area ≈ ½(7.0 × 10⁻⁵ + 1.12 × 10⁻³) × 0.60 [1] = **3.6 × 10⁻⁴ J** [1].
**(c)** W = q(V₂ − V₁) with V = Q/(4πε₀r): V₁ = 56.2 kV, V₂ = 225 kV [1]; W = 1.0 × 10⁻⁹ × 168.7 × 10³ = **1.7 × 10⁻⁴ J** [1].
**(d)** The curve is concave up, so the straight top of the trapezium lies above it, giving an over-estimate [1].
*Examiner insight:* Name the curve's shape and the direction of the error, not just "it is an estimate".

**12. (a)** Ek = ½mv² = 0.5 × 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ × (4.0 × 10⁶)² = **7.3 × 10⁻¹⁸ J** [1].
**(b)** At closest approach all kinetic energy is potential energy [1]: 7.29 × 10⁻¹⁸ = Qe/(4πε₀r) [1]. r = 2.0 × 10⁻⁹ × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ / (4π × 8.85 × 10⁻¹² × 7.29 × 10⁻¹⁸) = **0.39 m** [1].
**(c)** At 1.0 m, E_p = 2.88 × 10⁻¹⁸ J [1]. Ek = 7.29 × 10⁻¹⁸ − 2.88 × 10⁻¹⁸ = 4.41 × 10⁻¹⁸ J [1]. v = √(2Ek/m) = **3.1 × 10⁶ m s⁻¹** [1].
**(d)** The sphere is uniformly charged, so outside it the field is that of a point charge at its centre [1]. The electron never gets closer than 0.39 m, well outside the 2.0 cm radius [1].
*Examiner insight:* Energy conservation is the efficient route here.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Measuring r from the surface of a sphere instead of from its centre.
- Adding field magnitudes from two charges when they are not along the same line.
- Dropping the sign of Q in V = Q/(4πε₀r) and getting the wrong sign for the energy change.
- Using E = V/d for a point charge, or the inverse square law between plates.
- Using ε₀ where the material's ε is needed.
