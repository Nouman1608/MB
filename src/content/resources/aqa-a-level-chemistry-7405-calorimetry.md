This guide teaches section **3.1.4.2 Calorimetry** of the AQA AS and A-level Chemistry specification (7404/7405), for teaching from September 2015. It is AS content, examined on Papers 1 and 2 (and Paper 3). It also supports Required practical 2, the measurement of an enthalpy change. The equation q = mcΔT is simple. The marks are lost in the setup: using the right mass, the right amount in moles, the right units and sign, and evaluating the errors in the experiment. The [Energetics study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-energetics/) covers the full topic. Recall is in the [Calorimetry revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-calorimetry-revision-notes/), and questions are in the [Calorimetry practice questions](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-calorimetry-practice/).

## What the specification asks

| Content and skills | What you must be able to do |
|---|---|
| q = mcΔT, where m is the mass of the substance that has a temperature change ΔT and a specific heat capacity c | Use the equation with correct units |
| Calculate the molar enthalpy change for a reaction | Convert q into kJ mol⁻¹ with the right sign |
| Use the equation in related calculations | For example, find c, a mass, or a temperature rise |
| You will not be expected to recall the value of c | Use the value given in the question |
| Required practical 2 | Plan, carry out and evaluate an enthalpy measurement |
| Report answers to an appropriate number of significant figures | Limit the answer to the least precise measurement |

## The equation and its units

```
q = m × c × ΔT
q   heat energy transferred   J
m   mass                      g
c   specific heat capacity    J g⁻¹ K⁻¹  (water: 4.18, given)
ΔT  temperature change        K or °C (the size of a change is the same)
```

**Specific heat capacity** is the energy needed to raise the temperature of 1 g of a substance by 1 K.

The most important point is **which mass to use**. It is the mass of the substance whose temperature is measured, which is usually the **water or solution**, not the mass of the reactants you weighed (such as the magnesium or the fuel). For dilute aqueous solutions, assume the density is 1.00 g cm⁻³, so 50.0 cm³ of solution has a mass of 50.0 g.

## From q to the molar enthalpy change

1. Calculate q in joules.
2. Convert to kJ (÷ 1000).
3. Calculate the amount in moles of the substance that reacted (the limiting reagent, or the substance named in the question).
4. ΔH = q ÷ n, in kJ mol⁻¹.
5. Add the sign: **negative** if the temperature rose (exothermic), **positive** if it fell (endothermic).

### Worked example 1: neutralisation

50.0 cm³ of 1.00 mol dm⁻³ HCl is added to 50.0 cm³ of 1.00 mol dm⁻³ NaOH in a polystyrene cup. The temperature rises by 6.8 °C. Calculate the enthalpy change per mole of water formed. (c = 4.18 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹; density 1.00 g cm⁻³)

```
m = 100 g (total solution)
q = 100 × 4.18 × 6.8 = 2842 J = 2.842 kJ
n(HCl) = 1.00 × 0.0500 = 0.0500 mol  (equal to n(H₂O) formed)
ΔH = −2.842 ÷ 0.0500 = −56.8 kJ mol⁻¹
```

The temperature rose, so ΔH is negative. To two significant figures (ΔT is 6.8) the answer is −57 kJ mol⁻¹.

### Worked example 2: an endothermic dissolving

4.00 g of ammonium nitrate (Mᵣ = 80.0) is dissolved in 50.0 g of water. The temperature falls by 7.0 °C. Calculate the enthalpy change of solution per mole.

```
q = 50.0 × 4.18 × 7.0 = 1463 J = 1.463 kJ
n = 4.00 ÷ 80.0 = 0.0500 mol
ΔH = +1.463 ÷ 0.0500 = +29.3 kJ mol⁻¹
```

The temperature fell, so heat was absorbed and ΔH is positive. The mass used is that of the water (50.0 g). Some courses add the mass of the solid; follow the question.

### Worked example 3: a displacement reaction

25.0 cm³ of 1.00 mol dm⁻³ copper(II) sulfate solution is stirred with excess zinc powder, and the temperature rises by 45.0 °C. Calculate ΔH per mole of copper(II) sulfate.

```
q = 25.0 × 4.18 × 45.0 = 4702 J = 4.702 kJ
n(CuSO₄) = 1.00 × 0.0250 = 0.0250 mol   (zinc is in excess, so CuSO₄ is limiting)
ΔH = −4.702 ÷ 0.0250 = −188 kJ mol⁻¹
```

The amount in moles comes from the **limiting reagent** and not from the excess zinc.

## Combustion calorimetry

For a fuel such as an alcohol, the heat from burning is transferred to a known mass of water in a metal can (a calorimeter).

### Worked example 4: burning ethanol

A spirit burner containing ethanol heats 100 g of water in a copper can. The temperature rises from 21.0 °C to 33.5 °C. The mass of the burner falls by 0.46 g. (Mᵣ of ethanol = 46.0; c = 4.18 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹)

```
ΔT = 12.5 °C
q = 100 × 4.18 × 12.5 = 5225 J = 5.225 kJ
n(ethanol) = 0.46 ÷ 46.0 = 0.0100 mol
ΔH = −5.225 ÷ 0.0100 = −523 kJ mol⁻¹
```

The data-book value for the enthalpy of combustion of ethanol is −1367 kJ mol⁻¹, so this result is only about 38 per cent of it. This is typical of simple apparatus, for the reasons below.

## Why experimental values are less exothermic than data-book values

| Source of error | Effect on measured ΔH |
|---|---|
| Heat lost to the surroundings, the air and the can | Temperature rise is too small, so the value is less negative |
| Incomplete combustion (soot, CO) | Less heat released per mole than for complete combustion |
| Evaporation of the fuel from the wick | The mass loss is greater than the mass burned, so n is too large and ΔH too small |
| Non-standard conditions (not 298 K or 100 kPa; water vapour rather than liquid) | The product is not in its standard state, so the value differs |
| Heat absorbed by the apparatus, such as the can | Water receives less heat than was released |

**Improvements:** use a lid or draught shield; put the burner close to the can; stir the water; insulate the can; use a bomb calorimeter for better accuracy; repeat and average.

## Solution calorimetry: practical points

- Use an **insulated** vessel, such as a polystyrene cup with a lid, to reduce heat loss.
- Measure the starting temperature for a few minutes first.
- Add the second reagent, stir, and record temperature at regular intervals.
- For better results, plot temperature against time and **extrapolate the cooling curve** back to the time of mixing to find the maximum temperature rise, because the reaction is not instantaneous and heat is lost while it takes place.

## Uncertainty and significant figures

Each temperature reading has an uncertainty. A thermometer read to ±0.1 °C used twice (initial and final) gives ΔT an uncertainty of ±0.2 °C. For a rise of 6.8 °C the percentage uncertainty is 0.2 ÷ 6.8 × 100 = 2.9%.

A mass reading on a balance read to ±0.01 g has a percentage uncertainty of 0.01 ÷ 0.46 × 100 = 2.2% for 0.46 g.

Round the final answer to the number of significant figures of the **least precise** value used, or one more.

## Common mistakes

- Using the mass of the reactant, instead of the mass of the water or solution, in q = mcΔT.
- Leaving q in joules while ΔH is in kJ mol⁻¹.
- Forgetting the sign: exothermic needs a negative ΔH.
- Using the total volume in the n = cV step instead of the volume of the limiting reagent.
- Saying "heat loss" without saying which direction it affects the answer.
- Writing "human error" as an improvement or a source of error.
- Using the temperature, not the temperature change.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Calculate**: show q, the amount in moles, the division and the sign, with the units.
- **Suggest** a reason for the difference from the data-book value: give heat loss or incomplete combustion and say how it changes the result.
- **Suggest** an improvement: name a specific change (lid, insulation, stirring, draught shield) and say what it does.
- **Evaluate**: give the percentage uncertainty and compare sources of error.

## Frequently asked questions

**Does it matter if I use K or °C for ΔT?**
No. The size of a change is the same. A temperature itself must be in K only when used in equations such as pV = nRT.

**Why not use the mass of the acid and alkali separately?**
Because the temperature change applies to the whole solution. Add the volumes (and assume 1.00 g cm⁻³) unless told otherwise.

**Is the specific heat capacity of water always 4.18?**
It is given in the question. Use the value in the question.
