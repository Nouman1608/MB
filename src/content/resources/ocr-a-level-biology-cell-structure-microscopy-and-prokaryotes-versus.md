## Microscopes and what they show (a, f)

A **light microscope** shines light through a thin specimen. It can show living cells, colour and moving material, and stains add contrast. Its detail is limited because light has a relatively long wavelength.

In a **transmission electron microscope (TEM)**, a beam of electrons goes through a very thin slice of the specimen. Electrons are absorbed more by dense regions, so the image is a flat, black, white and grey picture of the inside of the cell. It shows ultrastructure such as membranes, ribosomes and the folds of a mitochondrion.

A **scanning electron microscope (SEM)** moves a beam over the surface of a specimen and collects electrons coming from it. The result is a three-dimensional-looking image of the outside of a cell or tissue. The specimen need not be sectioned, but you see the surface only.

Both electron microscopes keep the specimen in a vacuum, which kills living material, and the preparation can create **artefacts**, which are features in the image that were not in the living cell. You are not required to recall exact resolutions or magnifications for each microscope. You must be able to say which gives more detail and why.

### Magnification and resolution (f)

**Magnification** is the factor by which the image exceeds the real size of the specimen. **Resolution** is the minimum gap that lets two neighbouring points be seen apart. An electron beam has a far shorter wavelength than visible light, so electron microscopes resolve finer detail. Enlarging an image beyond the resolution of the instrument only gives a bigger, blurrier picture. This is called empty magnification.

| Feature | Light | TEM | SEM |
|---|---|---|---|
| Radiation | Light | Electron beam through specimen | Electron beam scanned over surface |
| Specimen | Thin; can be living | Ultra-thin section; dead | Whole or coated; dead |
| Image | 2D, colour possible | 2D, monochrome, internal detail | 3D appearance, surface detail |
| Resolution | Lowest of the three | Highest of the three | Better than light, usually below TEM |

## Slides, stains and drawings (b, c, d)

### Making a temporary slide (b)

1. Cut or tear a very thin piece of tissue so light can pass through, for example onion epidermis.
2. Place it flat on a clean slide in a drop of water or stain.
3. Hold a cover slip at an angle with a mounted needle and lower it slowly onto the specimen. This pushes air out and prevents bubbles.
4. Blot spare liquid with filter paper, then start on low power and move to higher power only once the specimen is in focus.

Use the coarse focus only at low power, and never move the objective towards the slide while looking down the eyepiece.

### Eyepiece graticule and stage micrometer (b)

An **eyepiece graticule** is a ruler fitted in the eyepiece whose divisions have no fixed size. Each objective lens needs its own **calibration** against a **stage micrometer**, a slide ruled in known lengths. Line up the two scales and work out how much real length one graticule division represents. Then remove the micrometer and measure the specimen in graticule divisions.

**Worked example 1.** A stage micrometer line of 100 µm lines up with 40 graticule divisions at the ×10 objective.

- One division = 100 ÷ 40 = **2.5 µm**.
- An epidermal cell spans 14 divisions, so its length is 14 × 2.5 = **35 µm**.
- At the ×40 objective, with the same eyepiece, the image is four times bigger, so one division = 2.5 ÷ 4 = **0.625 µm**. The calibration has to be repeated, or scaled, for each objective.

### Staining (c)

Most cell structures are transparent, so stains add contrast. Iodine in potassium iodide solution stains starch blue-black. Methylene blue and eosin are examples of stains for general detail, and acetic orcein stains chromosomes.

**Differential staining** uses more than one stain, or a stain that behaves differently with different structures, so that components or cell types can be told apart. In haematoxylin and eosin staining, nuclei stain blue-purple and cytoplasm pink. The **Gram stain** divides bacteria into two groups: Gram-positive cells keep the purple crystal violet and look purple, while Gram-negative cells lose it and take up the red counterstain. This difference is linked to the structure of the cell wall.

### Drawings (d)

A good biological drawing:

- uses a sharp pencil and clear continuous lines, with no shading and no colouring;
- is large and in proportion, showing only what you can see;
- has ruled label lines that do not cross and do not end in arrowheads;
- has a title and a magnification or scale bar;
- for tissue, draws the outlines of the cells in a region, not every cell, and never draws a cell that is not there.

## The magnification formula (e)

magnification = size of image ÷ size of real object

Rearranged: size of image = magnification × real size, and real size = size of image ÷ magnification. Use the same units for the image and the object, and convert only at the end. Useful conversions: 1 mm = 1000 µm and 1 µm = 1000 nm. A **scale bar** gives magnification directly: measure the bar and divide by the length it represents.

**Worked example 2.** An SEM image of a pollen grain is 62 mm across. The grain's real diameter is 31 µm.

- Convert the real size: 31 µm = 0.031 mm.
- Magnification = 62 ÷ 0.031 = **×2000**.

**Worked example 3.** In a TEM micrograph a scale bar is 25 mm long and is labelled 500 nm. A mitochondrion is 68 mm long in the image.

- Magnification = 25 mm ÷ 500 nm = 25 000 000 nm ÷ 500 nm = **×50 000**.
- Real length = 68 mm ÷ 50 000 = 0.00136 mm = **1.36 µm**, or 1360 nm.
- Check by proportion: 68 ÷ 25 × 500 nm = 1360 nm.

**Order of magnitude.** A cell about 40 µm across is about 20 times wider than a bacterium of 2 µm, which is just over one order of magnitude. A quick estimate like this lets you test whether an answer is sensible.

## Ultrastructure of eukaryotic cells (g)

| Component | Structure | Function |
|---|---|---|
| Plasma membrane | Phospholipid bilayer with proteins | Partially permeable boundary; controls exchange |
| Nucleus | Surrounded by a nuclear envelope (double membrane with pores); contains chromatin | Holds DNA; controls the activity of the cell; mRNA leaves through the pores |
| Nucleolus | Dense region inside the nucleus | Site of ribosomal RNA synthesis; ribosome subunits are put together here |
| Rough ER | Flattened sacs (cisternae) with ribosomes on the outer surface | Folds and processes proteins made on its ribosomes |
| Smooth ER | Membrane tubes without ribosomes | Makes and processes lipids |
| Golgi apparatus | Stack of flattened membrane sacs with vesicles | Modifies, sorts and packages proteins; makes lysosomes |
| Ribosomes | Two subunits of RNA and protein; no membrane | Site of protein synthesis |
| Mitochondrion | Double membrane, inner membrane folded into cristae, matrix | Aerobic respiration; makes ATP |
| Lysosome | Single membrane around hydrolytic enzymes | Digests worn-out organelles and material taken in |
| Chloroplast | Double membrane, stacks of thylakoids (grana) in stroma | Photosynthesis |
| Cell wall | Plant: cellulose fibres | Strength; resists bursting |
| Centrioles | Pair of short tubes of microtubules, at right angles, in animal cells | Involved in forming the spindle in cell division |
| Cilia and flagella | Projections from the surface, with microtubules inside | Cilia move fluid or particles over a surface; flagella move a cell |

Mitochondria and chloroplasts contain their own DNA and ribosomes. Chloroplasts are found in photosynthetic cells but not in all plant cells.

## Photomicrographs (h)

Work through a micrograph in this order: look for the scale bar, decide whether it is TEM (flat, internal) or SEM (surface, 3D), then identify structures by their features.

- **Double membrane with inner folds**: mitochondrion. A **double membrane with flattened stacks**: chloroplast.
- **Stack of curved flattened sacs with small vesicles**: Golgi apparatus.
- **Flattened sacs studded with dots**: rough ER. Dots are ribosomes.
- **Dark-staining round region with pores in its double boundary**: nucleus.
- **Cut surfaces appear as circles, ovals or long strips** because a thin section can slice an organelle at any angle. A long tube may look like a circle. Say this in a "suggest why" answer.

## Making and secreting a protein (i)

1. DNA in the nucleus is transcribed to mRNA, which leaves through a nuclear pore.
2. mRNA is translated by ribosomes on the rough ER. The growing polypeptide enters the cisternae of the rough ER, where it is folded and processed.
3. Vesicles bud off the rough ER and carry the protein to the Golgi apparatus.
4. The Golgi chemically alters the protein, for instance by attaching sugar groups, and sorts it into secretory vesicles.
5. Vesicles move to the plasma membrane and fuse with it, releasing the protein by exocytosis.

ATP from mitochondria supplies energy for this pathway.

## The cytoskeleton (j)

The **cytoskeleton** is a network of protein fibres and tubules in the cytoplasm. It has three roles in the specification.

- **Mechanical strength**: it supports the shape of the cell and holds organelles in place.
- **Transport within cells**: vesicles and organelles move along tracks, for example vesicles on microtubules.
- **Cell movement**: it enables cilia and flagella to beat and cells to change shape or move.

## Prokaryotes and eukaryotes (k)

| Feature | Prokaryote | Eukaryote |
|---|---|---|
| Size | Usually smaller | Usually larger |
| Genetic material | Circular DNA free in cytoplasm; no nucleus | Linear DNA in chromosomes inside a nucleus |
| DNA packaging | Not wound round histones | Associated with histones |
| Ribosomes | Smaller (70S) | Larger (80S) in the cytoplasm |
| Organelles | No membrane-bound organelles | Membrane-bound organelles present |
| Cell wall | Peptidoglycan (bacteria) | Cellulose in plants; chitin in fungi; absent in animals |
| Extras | Plasmids; some have a capsule | No plasmids in most; none in animals |

**Similarities**: both have a plasma membrane, cytoplasm, ribosomes and DNA as genetic material.

## Common errors

- Writing "magnification" when you mean "resolution", or the reverse.
- Mixing units in the magnification formula.
- Saying electron microscopes give a higher magnification only. The key advantage is resolution.
- Describing SEM as showing internal structure.
- Saying that prokaryotes have "no DNA", rather than no nucleus.
