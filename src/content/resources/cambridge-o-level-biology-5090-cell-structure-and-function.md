This guide covers subtopic **1.1 Cell structure and function** of Cambridge O Level Biology 5090, 2026–2028 series. The syllabus asks you to examine animal and plant cells under the microscope "using an appropriate temporary staining technique, such as methylene blue or iodine solution", draw what you see, identify named structures "on diagrams, photomicrographs or electron micrographs", describe a bacterial cell (limited to ribosomes, circular DNA and plasmids, cytoplasm, cell membrane and cell wall) and describe the functions of all these structures.

The [Cell Structure and Organisation guide](/resources/cell-structure-and-organisation/) gives the comparison table and the basic slide method. This page goes further on the parts students most often lose marks on: recognising structures in real images, writing function answers that score, identifying an unknown cell, and running and troubleshooting the practical. ## Diagrams, photomicrographs and electron micrographs

The syllabus names three kinds of image. Each shows different structures.

| Image type | How it is made | What you can usually see |
|---|---|---|
| Diagram | Drawn, simplified, labelled | All named structures, clearly outlined |
| Photomicrograph | Photograph through a light microscope | Cell wall, cell membrane (as the edge of the cytoplasm), nucleus, cytoplasm, chloroplasts, sap vacuole |
| Electron micrograph | Image from an electron microscope, much higher magnification | Mitochondria, ribosomes and the inner detail of chloroplasts and the nucleus, as well as larger structures |

Ribosomes are far too small to see in a photomicrograph. If a question shows tiny dots scattered through the cytoplasm or lining internal membranes, you are looking at an electron micrograph and the dots are ribosomes.

### Recognition clues

- **Nucleus**: the largest structure in most animal and plant cells; round or oval; often darker because it stains strongly.
- **Mitochondria**: small, sausage-shaped or oval, with an inner membrane folded into shelves. Many are present in active cells.
- **Chloroplasts**: larger than mitochondria, oval or lens-shaped, with stacks of internal membranes; found only in plant cells that photosynthesise.
- **Sap vacuole**: a large, pale, often central space in a plant cell, pushing the cytoplasm into a thin layer around the edge.
- **Cellulose cell wall**: a thick outer layer outside the cell membrane in plant cells; it gives plant cells a regular, often box-like shape.
- **Cell membrane**: a thin line at the edge of the cytoplasm. In a plant cell it is pressed against the inside of the wall and is hard to see separately.
- **Ribosomes**: tiny dots, visible only on electron micrographs.

## Functions: writing answers that score

A function answer needs a precise verb and object. "The nucleus controls the cell" is too vague for full credit. Compare:

| Structure | Weak answer | Strong answer |
|---|---|---|
| Cell membrane | Protects the cell | Controls which substances enter and leave the cell; it is partially permeable |
| Cytoplasm | Holds the organelles | Jelly-like material where many of the cell's chemical reactions take place |
| Nucleus | Controls the cell | Contains the genetic material (DNA), which controls the cell's activities |
| Ribosomes | Make things | Site of protein synthesis |
| Mitochondria | Make energy | Site of aerobic respiration, which releases energy for the cell |
| Chloroplasts | Make food | Contain chlorophyll, which absorbs light for photosynthesis |
| Sap vacuole | Stores water | Contains cell sap (water with dissolved sugars and salts); helps keep the cell firm by pressing the cytoplasm against the wall |
| Cellulose cell wall | Protects | Strong layer that supports the cell and stops it bursting when it takes in water; it is fully permeable |

Two words cost marks every year: energy is **released** by respiration, not "made" or "produced"; and the membrane is **partially permeable**, while the cell wall is **fully permeable**.

## The bacterial cell

A bacterial cell is much smaller and simpler than animal and plant cells. Learn only the structures the syllabus lists:

- **Cell wall**: supports the cell and stops it bursting. It is not made of cellulose.
- **Cell membrane**: controls what enters and leaves.
- **Cytoplasm**: where the cell's chemical reactions take place.
- **Ribosomes**: protein synthesis.
- **Circular DNA**: one large loop of DNA lying free in the cytoplasm, carrying the genes that control the cell. There is no nucleus.
- **Plasmids**: small, separate rings of DNA carrying a few extra genes.

The key structural fact: genetic material is not enclosed in a nucleus. A question that asks "where is the DNA in a bacterial cell?" wants "free in the cytoplasm, as a circular loop, with plasmids".

## Worked example: identifying an unknown cell

*Three cells are described. Identify each as animal, plant or bacterial, giving two reasons.*

- **Cell X**: has a cell wall, a nucleus and a large central vacuole, but no chloroplasts.
- **Cell Y**: has a cell wall, ribosomes and plasmids, but no nucleus.
- **Cell Z**: has a nucleus and many mitochondria, but no cell wall.

**Model answer, annotated.**

- X is a **plant cell**: it has a cell wall and a large sap vacuole, and it has a nucleus, so it is not a bacterium. *(The missing chloroplasts are a trap: plant cells that do not receive light, such as root cells and onion epidermis, have none.)*
- Y is a **bacterial cell**: it has plasmids and has no nucleus. *(The cell wall alone does not separate bacteria from plants; the absence of a nucleus does.)*
- Z is an **animal cell**: it has no cell wall, and it has a nucleus, so it is not a bacterium. *(Many mitochondria suggest an active cell, but mitochondria are found in plant cells too.)*

The skill is to choose features that rule out the other two options.

## Running the staining practical

The syllabus allows "any suitable locally available material". Useful choices include onion epidermis, the thin leaf of a pondweed such as *Elodea*, which shows chloroplasts without staining, and animal cells such as cheek cells where your school allows them.

**Method with reasons.**

1. Place the specimen flat on a clean slide in a drop of water or stain. *A thin, flat specimen lets light pass through.*
2. Add a drop of iodine solution (plant cells) or methylene blue (animal cells). *Most cell structures are colourless; the stain gives contrast so the nucleus can be seen.*
3. Lower a cover slip at an angle using a mounted needle. *This pushes air out and avoids bubbles.*
4. Remove excess liquid with a paper towel at the edge of the cover slip. *Excess stain floods the field and hides detail.*
5. Start with the lowest-power objective, focus, centre the cells, then change to a higher power and refocus with the fine focus only.

**Troubleshooting.**

| Problem | Likely cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Dark circles with thick black outlines | Air bubbles | Lower the cover slip more slowly, at an angle |
| Cells overlap and nothing is clear | Specimen too thick or folded | Use a single layer of cells, spread flat |
| Everything is dark blue or brown | Too much stain | Draw excess off with paper towel, or use less |
| Nucleus not visible | Too little stain, or wrong stain | Add stain at one edge and draw it under with paper towel at the other |

**Safety.** Iodine solution and methylene blue stain skin and clothing; wear eye protection and wash off spills.

## Common mistakes

- Labelling ribosomes on a photomicrograph; they are visible only on electron micrographs.
- Using "makes energy" for mitochondria.
- Describing the cell wall as partially permeable.
- Saying bacterial DNA is "in the nucleus" or that bacteria "have no DNA".
- Identifying a cell as a plant cell only because it has a wall; bacteria have walls too.
- Assuming every plant cell has chloroplasts.
- Drawing a nucleus in a bacterial cell.

## Command words

- **Identify**: name the structure, usually from a labelled line or letter.
- **Describe** a function: state what the structure does, precisely.
- **State**: a short answer with no explanation needed.
- **Explain**: give the reason, for example why a stain is used or why root cells lack chloroplasts.
