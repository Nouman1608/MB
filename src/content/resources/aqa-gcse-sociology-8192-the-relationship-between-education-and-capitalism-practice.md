These questions cover 3.4.2 The relationship between education and capitalism, from the AQA GCSE Sociology (8192) specification, assessed in Paper 1: the key ideas of Bowles and Gintis, the correspondence principle and alternative perspectives on it. All schools, towns and people in the items are fictional.

## Section A: short questions

**1.** Which sociologists developed the correspondence principle?

A. Durkheim and Parsons
B. Bowles and Gintis
C. Halsey, Heath and Ridge
D. Ball, Bowe and Gewirtz

**[1]**

**2.** Which term describes the lessons pupils learn from the way school is organised rather than from timetabled subjects?

A. Formal curriculum
B. Ethnocentric curriculum
C. Hidden curriculum
D. National curriculum

**[1]**

**3.** Identify two features of the workforce that, according to Bowles and Gintis, schooling creates. **[2]**

**4.** Describe what Marxists mean by the "myth of meritocracy". **[3]**

**5.** Describe one way in which the hierarchy of a school corresponds to the hierarchy of a workplace. **[3]**

**6.** Explain why, according to Bowles and Gintis, competition between pupils benefits capitalism. **[3]**

**7.** Identify and explain one criticism of the correspondence principle made by Willis. **[4]**

## Section B: longer and structured questions

**8.** Read the item.

> At Fenmoor Academy (a fictional school), pupils must stand when a teacher enters, line up in silence and carry a planner that records every late arrival. Merits are exchanged for vouchers at the end of each term. The head teacher says: "We are preparing our pupils for the world of work."

**(a)** Using the item, identify one example of the hidden curriculum. **[1]**
**(b)** Using the item and your knowledge of Bowles and Gintis, explain how Fenmoor Academy could be preparing pupils for work in a capitalist society. **[4]**

**9.** Explain one similarity and one difference between Bowles and Gintis and Willis on the relationship between education and capitalism. **[4]**

**10.** Explain how a functionalist would respond to the claim that schools prepare pupils for work. **[4]**

**11.** Explain one feminist criticism of the correspondence principle. **[3]**

**12.** Read the item.

> In Westbridge (a fictional city), a new "careers academy" works with local employers. Pupils spend one day a week on placements in warehouses, call centres and care homes. Supporters say it gives young people skills employers want. Critics note that most pupils on the scheme come from the city's poorest estates, while pupils at the city's private school are prepared for university.

Discuss how far sociologists would agree that the correspondence principle explains the relationship between education and work today. **[12]**

**13.** Discuss how far sociologists would agree that schools mainly serve the interests of capitalism. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** B. Bowles and Gintis [1] **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Durkheim and Parsons are the most common wrong answer; they are functionalists who see school preparing pupils for work fairly.

**2.** C. Hidden curriculum [1] **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* The formal curriculum is the timetabled subjects; the hidden curriculum is what the organisation of school teaches.

**3.** Any two from: hardworking, docile, obedient, highly motivated, too divided to challenge the authority of management. First feature [1]; second feature [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* "Good at their job" is not credited; use the specification's own adjectives.

**4.** Meritocracy is a system where rewards are based on ability and effort [1]. Marxists argue schools only appear to work this way, because class background is the main influence on attainment [1]. Pupils who fail are encouraged to blame themselves, so inequality looks fair and is legitimised [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* The third mark needs the consequence: the myth makes inequality look deserved.

**5.** Schools have a hierarchy with the head teacher at the top, then teachers, with pupils at the bottom [1]. Pupils learn to accept orders from those above them without questioning them [1]. This corresponds to a workplace where workers accept the authority of supervisors and managers [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Describe both halves of the correspondence; a description of school alone reaches only two marks.

**6.** Pupils are ranked against each other in tests and sets [1], so they learn to see one another as rivals rather than cooperating [1]. As adults they are too divided to act together against management, for example to demand better pay, which protects the authority of owners [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* This links directly to the specification's phrase "too divided to challenge the authority of management".

**7.** Willis rejects the idea of a direct relationship between the economy and the way schools operate [1]. He argues that education is not a particularly successful agency of socialisation [1], because the working-class lads he studied resisted school and were neither docile nor obedient [1]. Their resistance had unintended consequences, though it still prepared them for male-dominated manual work [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Strong answers note that Willis is also a Marxist, so his criticism is about the mechanism, not the conclusion.

**8. (a)** Any one: standing when a teacher enters; lining up in silence; recording late arrivals; exchanging merits for vouchers [1].
**(b)** Standing for teachers and lining up in silence teach pupils to obey those in authority [1], which corresponds to workers obeying managers [1]. Exchanging merits for vouchers teaches pupils to work for external rewards [1], just as workers work for wages rather than satisfaction, producing the motivated, docile workforce Bowles and Gintis describe [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the item's exact details; each detail should be paired with a workplace match.

**9.** Similarity: both are Marxists who argue that schooling ends up serving capitalism by producing workers for unequal jobs [1], for example the lads ended up in manual work [1]. Difference: Bowles and Gintis see a direct correspondence and successful socialisation into obedience [1], whereas Willis argues schooling is a weak agency of socialisation and pupils resist, with unintended consequences [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Comparison questions need an explicit word such as "whereas" for the difference mark.

**10.** A functionalist would agree that schools prepare pupils for work [1]. Durkheim argued that schools teach the specialist skills an advanced economy needs [1]. Parsons argued that schools select individuals for future roles on meritocratic principles, so people are matched to jobs by ability [1]. Unlike Marxists, functionalists see this as fair and beneficial to society as a whole, not exploitation [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* The last mark comes from contrasting the functionalist view with the Marxist one.

**11.** Feminists argue the correspondence principle focuses on class and ignores gender [1]. The hidden and gendered curriculum have prepared girls and boys for different roles, for example steering girls towards caring work [1]. So schools serve patriarchy as well as capitalism, which helps explain the glass ceiling in employment [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Name a mechanism (gendered or hidden curriculum), not just "it ignores women".

**12.** The real exam marks extended answers using levels of response, not point by point. The points below show indicative creditworthy content.

- The correspondence principle states that relationships at school mirror relationships at work [1].
- Bowles and Gintis argue the role of education is the reproduction of labour power [1].
- The item supports them: placements in warehouses and call centres prepare poorer pupils for routine, lower-paid work [1].
- Private school pupils being prepared for university shows class background shaping destinations, as Bowles and Gintis argue [1].
- Marxists would see the careers academy as vocationalism that channels working-class pupils into subordinate roles [1].
- The myth of meritocracy: pupils may believe they chose the route freely, so inequality looks fair [1].
- Functionalists would see the scheme as meeting the needs of the economy, matching pupils to useful roles [1].
- Supporters in the item argue it gives young people skills employers want, which fits the functionalist view [1].
- Willis would argue pupils are not simply moulded; some may resist the scheme, so outcomes are not direct [1].
- Feminists would ask whether girls are steered towards care homes and boys towards warehouses, showing a gendered correspondence [1].
- The study was based on 1970s American schools, and many jobs today reward creativity and teamwork rather than obedience [1].
- Judgement: the principle helps explain how class shapes routes into work, but it overstates pupil passivity and ignores gender, so it explains only part of the relationship today [1]. **[12]**

*Examiner insight:* The word "today" invites a point about change since the 1970s, and the item gives you a modern example to apply.

**13.** The real exam marks extended answers using levels of response, not point by point. The points below show indicative creditworthy content.

- Marxists argue that education serves the ruling class, not society as a whole [1].
- Bowles and Gintis: schools reproduce labour power through the correspondence principle [1].
- Example: pupils work for grades as workers work for wages, accepting external rewards [1].
- Schooling produces a docile, obedient workforce too divided to challenge management [1].
- They reject meritocracy, arguing class background is the main influence on attainment [1].
- The myth of meritocracy legitimises inequality, so those who fail blame themselves [1].
- Willis, also Marxist, shows capitalism may be served through resistance and unintended consequences [1].
- Functionalists argue schools serve society as a whole through shared values (Durkheim) [1].
- Parsons sees selection for future roles as fair and meritocratic [1].
- Feminists argue schools also serve patriarchy through a gendered curriculum [1].
- Interactionists argue pupils negotiate and resist meanings in school, so they are not passive [1].
- Judgement: schools reward conformity that suits employers, but evidence of resistance and gender inequality suggests capitalism is one interest among several [1]. **[12]**

*Examiner insight:* A strong judgement says "mainly" is the key word and decides whether capitalism is the main interest or one of several.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing school rules without the matching feature of work.
- Calling Willis a functionalist or saying he supports docile workers.
- Giving the functionalist view without saying why it differs from the Marxist one.
- Ignoring the item in questions that say "using the item".
- Writing "Marxists say school is bad" instead of naming the mechanism: hidden curriculum, correspondence, legitimation.
- Ending a 12-mark answer without a judgement that uses the key word in the question.
