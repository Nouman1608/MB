Learn the content first with the [accounting system study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-accounting-9706-the-accounting-system/) and the [accounting system revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-accounting-9706-the-accounting-system-revision-notes/).

## Short-answer questions

**1.** State the double entry (account debited and account credited) for each transaction.

**(a)** Goods bought on credit from Patel for $1 200. **[1]**
**(b)** Rent of $500 paid from the bank. **[1]**
**(c)** The owner took goods costing $150 for personal use. **[1]**

**2.** Identify the book of prime entry in which each of the following is first recorded.

**(a)** Goods sold for cash. **[1]**
**(b)** A credit note received from a supplier. **[1]**
**(c)** The correction of an error. **[1]**
**(d)** Goods sold on credit. **[1]**

**3.** Explain why the accounting equation always balances under double entry. **[2]**

**4.** State **two** purposes of a trial balance. **[2]**

**5.** Electricity used in December is billed and paid in January. The financial year ends on 31 December. Explain how the matching (accruals) concept applies. **[2]**

**6.** A business has assets of $85 000 and liabilities of $23 500.

**(a)** Calculate the owner's capital. **[1]**
**(b)** The owner then withdraws $4 000 from the business bank account. State the new totals for assets and capital. **[2]**

## Structured questions

**7.** Raj started a business on 1 June. His transactions in June were:

1. Paid $15 000 of his own money into a business bank account.
2. Bought shop fittings on credit from Fixit Ltd for $4 500.
3. Bought goods on credit from Zed for $3 600.
4. Cash sales of $2 100 were banked.
5. Paid rent of $800 from the bank.
6. Returned goods costing $400 to Zed.
7. Paid Zed $2 000 from the bank.
8. Withdrew $600 from the bank for personal use.

**(a)** Identify the book of prime entry for transaction 2. **[1]**
**(b)** Prepare the bank account for June, balanced at 30 June. **[4]**
**(c)** Prepare Zed's account for June, balanced at 30 June. **[3]**
**(d)** Prepare a trial balance at 30 June. **[5]**

**8.** A trader proposes the following. For each, identify the accounting concept that would be broken and explain why.

**(a)** Valuing unsold inventory, which cost $6 000, at its expected selling price of $9 000. **[2]**
**(b)** Recording the rent of the trader's own home as a business expense. **[2]**
**(c)** Changing the depreciation method each year to give a steadier profit. **[2]**

**9.** Nadia runs a market stall selling for cash only. She keeps her bank statements but no other records. Evaluate whether Nadia should keep full accounting records. **[6]**

**10.** A small wholesaler with three office staff is considering a computerised accounting system.

**(a)** State **two** advantages of a computerised system for this business. **[2]**
**(b)** State **two** disadvantages. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain how access levels and an audit trail help keep the data secure. **[2]**

**11.** State the effect (increase, decrease or no change) on total assets, total liabilities and capital of each transaction.

**(a)** Goods for resale bought on credit for $2 000. **[1]**
**(b)** A supplier paid $1 500 from the bank. **[1]**
**(c)** The owner brings in a personal vehicle worth $9 000 for business use. **[1]**

**12.** Prepare general journal entries, with narratives, for the following.

**(a)** On 1 April, Lucia starts a business with premises valued at $40 000 and $5 000 in the bank. The premises carry a loan of $15 000 that becomes a business liability. **[3]**
**(b)** On 3 April, Lucia buys a machine on credit from Mako Ltd for $7 000. **[2]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** Debit purchases; credit Patel [1]
**(b)** Debit rent; credit bank [1]
**(c)** Debit drawings; credit purchases [1] **[3]**

**2. (a)** Cash book [1]
**(b)** Purchases returns journal [1]
**(c)** General journal [1]
**(d)** Sales journal [1] **[4]**

**3.** Every transaction is recorded with an equal debit and credit, reflecting its two effects (duality) [1]. Each pair of entries changes the two sides of the equation by the same amount, or changes two items on the same side in opposite directions, so assets always equal liabilities plus capital [1]. **[2]**

**4.** It checks the arithmetic accuracy of the double entry by showing whether total debits equal total credits [1]. It provides a summary of balances from which the financial statements are prepared [1]. **[2]**

**5.** The electricity is an expense of December, the period in which it was used [1], so it is charged in that year's statement of profit or loss as an accrued expense (a liability at the year end), even though payment is made in January [1]. **[2]**

**6. (a)** 85 000 − 23 500 = $61 500 [1]
**(b)** Assets fall to $81 000 [1]; capital falls to $57 500 [1] **[3]**

**7. (a)** General journal [1]
**(b)** Bank: debit capital $15 000 and sales $2 100 (total $17 100) [1]; credit rent $800, Zed $2 000 and drawings $600 [1]; balance carried down $13 700 on the credit side, totals $17 100 each side [1]; balance brought down $13 700 debit [1].
**(c)** Zed: credit purchases $3 600 [1]; debit purchases returns $400 and bank $2 000 [1]; balance carried down $1 200 on the debit side, brought down $1 200 credit [1].
**(d)** Trial balance at 30 June:

| Account | Debit $ | Credit $ |
|---|---|---|
| Bank | 13 700 | |
| Shop fittings | 4 500 | |
| Purchases | 3 600 | |
| Rent | 800 | |
| Drawings | 600 | |
| Capital | | 15 000 |
| Fixit Ltd | | 4 500 |
| Zed | | 1 200 |
| Sales | | 2 100 |
| Purchases returns | | 400 |
| **Total** | **23 200** | **23 200** |

Debit balances correct [2]; credit balances correct [2]; totals agreeing at $23 200 [1] **[13]**

**8. (a)** Prudence (or realisation) [1]: inventory must be valued at the lower of cost and net realisable value; recording $9 000 anticipates a profit that has not yet been earned [1].
**(b)** Business entity [1]: the trader's home is private, so its rent is not a business expense; any business money used for it is drawings [1].
**(c)** Consistency [1]: methods must be applied the same way each year so profits can be compared; changing methods to smooth profit makes comparisons misleading [1]. **[6]**

**9.** Points a strong answer could make, one mark each:
- Full records would show her true profit, including costs paid in cash that never reach the bank [1].
- They would provide evidence for a loan application or tax return [1].
- They would help her spot theft or errors, such as missing takings [1].
- But with cash-only sales and no credit customers, there are no receivables to control, so some benefits are smaller [1].
- Full double entry takes time and skill, or the cost of a bookkeeper or software, which may be large compared with a small stall's profit [1].
- Justified conclusion, for example: keep at least a full cash book of all receipts and payments, moving to full records if the business grows or offers credit [1].

**[6]**

**10. (a)** Any two of: faster recording and reporting; double entry posted automatically, so fewer arithmetic errors; up-to-date balances for customers and suppliers [2]
**(b)** Any two of: cost of software, hardware and training; risk of data loss or system failure; risk of unauthorised access or viruses [2]
**(c)** Access levels let each user see or change only the parts of the system their job needs, reducing the risk of fraud or accidental change [1]; an audit trail records who entered or changed each item and when, so errors and unauthorised changes can be traced [1] **[6]**

**11. (a)** Assets increase; liabilities increase; capital no change [1]
**(b)** Assets decrease; liabilities decrease; capital no change [1]
**(c)** Assets increase; liabilities no change; capital increases [1] **[3]**

**12. (a)** Debit premises $40 000 and bank $5 000 [1]; credit loan $15 000 and capital $30 000 [1]; narrative: "Assets and liabilities at the start of the business" [1].
**(b)** Debit machinery $7 000; credit Mako Ltd $7 000 [1]; narrative: "Purchase of machine on credit" [1] **[5]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Recording cash sales or non-current assets in the wrong book of prime entry.
- Reversing debit and credit for supplier and customer accounts.
- Bringing balances down on the wrong side.
- Treating drawings of goods as a sale.
- Naming a concept without explaining why it applies.
- Evaluating without reaching a justified conclusion.
