## VSEPR — shapes and angles

Electron pairs repel and arrange as far apart as possible. **Lone pairs repel more strongly than bonding pairs**, reducing the angle by roughly 2.5° each.

| Bonding pairs | Lone pairs | Shape | Angle | Example |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | 0 | Linear | 180° | CO₂, BeCl₂ |
| 3 | 0 | Trigonal planar | 120° | BF₃ |
| 4 | 0 | Tetrahedral | 109.5° | CH₄ |
| 3 | 1 | **Pyramidal** | 107° | NH₃ |
| 2 | 2 | **Bent** | 104.5° | H₂O |
| 5 | 0 | Trigonal bipyramidal | 120° / 90° | PCl₅ |
| 6 | 0 | Octahedral | 90° | SF₆ |

Count **all** electron pairs to get the arrangement; count only **bonding** pairs to name the shape.

## Electronegativity and polarity

Electronegativity increases across a period and decreases down a group. A difference produces a **polar bond**.

A molecule with polar bonds is only **polar overall** if the dipoles do not cancel:

```
CO2   polar bonds, LINEAR    -> dipoles cancel  -> NON-POLAR
H2O   polar bonds, BENT      -> do not cancel   -> POLAR
CCl4  polar bonds, TETRAHEDRAL -> cancel        -> NON-POLAR
CHCl3 polar bonds, asymmetric -> do not cancel  -> POLAR
```

Symmetry is the deciding factor, not the number of polar bonds.

## Intermolecular forces — weakest to strongest

| Force | Present in | Strength |
|---|---|---|
| **van der Waals / London** | **All** molecules | Weakest; stronger with more electrons |
| **Permanent dipole–dipole** | Polar molecules | Intermediate |
| **Hydrogen bonding** | H bonded directly to **N, O or F** | Strongest |

Hydrogen bonding requires **both** H attached to N/O/F **and** a lone pair on the N/O/F of a neighbouring molecule.

## Consequences of hydrogen bonding

- **Anomalously high boiling points** for H₂O, NH₃, HF compared with their group trends.
- **Ice is less dense than water** — hydrogen bonds hold molecules in an open tetrahedral lattice, so it floats.
- High surface tension and high specific heat capacity of water.
- Solubility of alcohols and sugars in water.

## Exam traps

- Counting only bonding pairs when predicting the electron arrangement.
- Assuming polar bonds mean a polar molecule — check the symmetry.
- Saying "hydrogen bonding occurs because hydrogen is present" — it needs N, O or F.
- Forgetting van der Waals forces exist in **all** molecules, including polar ones.
- Claiming intermolecular forces are broken when a covalent substance decomposes — melting breaks only the intermolecular forces.

## Self-test

1. Predict the shape and bond angle of NH₃, and explain the angle.
2. Why is CO₂ non-polar despite having polar bonds?
3. State the two requirements for hydrogen bonding.
4. Why does ice float on water?
5. Rank the three intermolecular forces by strength.

**Answers:** 1. Pyramidal, 107° — four electron pairs give a tetrahedral arrangement, but the lone pair repels more strongly than the bonding pairs, compressing the H–N–H angle from 109.5°. 2. It is linear and symmetrical, so the two bond dipoles are equal and opposite and cancel. 3. Hydrogen bonded directly to N, O or F, and a lone pair on the N, O or F of a neighbouring molecule. 4. Hydrogen bonds hold the molecules in an open tetrahedral lattice with larger spacing than in liquid water, lowering the density. 5. van der Waals < permanent dipole–dipole < hydrogen bonding.
