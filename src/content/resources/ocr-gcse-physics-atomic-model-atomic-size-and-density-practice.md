## Questions

**1.** State two features of the plum pudding model of the atom. **[2]**

**2.** Alpha particles were fired at thin gold foil. Describe two observations and state what each observation shows about the structure of the atom. **[4]**

**3.** The atomic model changed again after Rutherford's nuclear model.

**(a)** Describe what Bohr changed in the model. **[2]**
**(b)** State two differences between Rutherford's nuclear model and the plum pudding model. **[2]**

**4.** Describe the atom, referring to charge, mass and size. **[3]**

**5.** An atom is drawn 80 m across for a scale model. The nucleus is about 10 000 times smaller across than the atom.

**(a)** Calculate the diameter of the nucleus in this model, in millimetres. **[2]**
**(b)** An atom has a diameter of about 1 × 10⁻¹⁰ m. Calculate how many atoms fit side by side along a line 2.0 mm long. **[2]**

**6.** A metal block measures 6.0 cm × 5.0 cm × 2.0 cm and has mass 420 g.

**(a)** Define density. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the density of the metal in g/cm³ and in kg/m³. **[3]**

**7.** (a) Mercury has a density of 13.6 g/cm³. Calculate the mass in kilograms of 250 cm³ of mercury. **[3]**
**(b)** Ethanol has a density of 790 kg/m³. Calculate the volume of 0.46 kg of ethanol, in m³ to two significant figures. **[3]**

**8.** A student finds the density of a small irregular metal object. The mass is 133.5 g. The water level in a measuring cylinder rises from 40.0 cm³ to 55.0 cm³ when the object is lowered in.

**(a)** Calculate the volume of the object. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the density in kg/m³. **[2]**
**(c)** Reference densities are: copper 8900 kg/m³, brass 8500 kg/m³, iron 7900 kg/m³. Identify the most likely metal. **[1]**
**(d)** Suggest two reasons the measured value might not match a reference value exactly. **[2]**

**9.** Water has density 1000 kg/m³. Air has density 1.2 kg/m³.

**(a)** Explain, in terms of particles, why air is much less dense than water. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate how many times denser water is than air, to two significant figures. **[2]**

**10.** A syringe holds 0.0150 m³ of a gas of density 0.80 kg/m³. The nozzle is blocked and the gas is compressed to 0.0040 m³.

**(a)** Calculate the mass of the gas. **[2]**
**(b)** State why the mass is the same after compression. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the density of the gas after compression. **[2]**

**11.** A block of ice has volume 400 cm³ and density 920 kg/m³. It melts completely to water of density 1000 kg/m³.

**(a)** Calculate the mass of the ice, in kg. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the volume of water formed, in cm³. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the percentage decrease in volume. **[2]**

**12.** A sheet of gold leaf has area 0.010 m² and thickness 1.0 × 10⁻⁷ m. The density of gold is 19 300 kg/m³.

**(a)** Calculate the volume of the leaf. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the mass of the leaf, giving your answer in milligrams to three significant figures. **[2]**
**(c)** Taking a gold atom as 1 × 10⁻¹⁰ m across, estimate how many atoms thick the leaf is. **[2]**
**(d)** Use the nuclear model to explain why most alpha particles can pass through a foil that is many atoms thick. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** The atom is a ball of positive charge [1]; electrons are embedded in / spread through it [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Give two separate features. Saying "positive ball with electrons" is incomplete unless you also state where the electrons are.

**2.** Observation 1: most alpha particles went straight through [1], so most of the atom is empty space [1]. Observation 2: a small number were deflected through large angles (a very few bounced almost straight back) [1], so the atom has a small, dense, positively charged nucleus that repels the alpha particles [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Each observation needs its own conclusion. A list of observations with a single conclusion at the end tends to gain fewer points than pairing them.

**3. (a)** Bohr placed electrons in orbits (energy levels) at fixed distances from the nucleus [1], which explained why atoms are stable / give out light only at particular wavelengths [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** In the nuclear model the positive charge and nearly all the mass are in a small central nucleus [1]; in the plum pudding model they are spread through the whole atom [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Make each difference a comparison of the two models, not a description of one model alone.

**4.** The atom has a positively charged nucleus in the centre with negatively charged electrons surrounding it [1]. Almost all the mass is in the nucleus [1]. The nuclear radius is much smaller than the radius of the atom [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Cover each of charge, mass and size in turn. Calling the nucleus "the biggest part" costs the size point.

**5. (a)** Diameter = 80 m ÷ 10 000 [1] = 0.0080 m = **8.0 mm** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Number = 2.0 × 10⁻³ ÷ 1 × 10⁻¹⁰ [1] = **2 × 10⁷ atoms** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Convert millimetres to metres before dividing. Dividing 2.0 by 1 × 10⁻¹⁰ gives a power of ten that is 1000 times too large.

**6. (a)** Density is mass per unit volume. [1]
**(b)** Volume = 6.0 × 5.0 × 2.0 = 60 cm³ [1]. Density = 420 ÷ 60 = **7.0 g/cm³** [1] = **7000 kg/m³** [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Write the unit with each answer. If a question asks for two units, show the conversion factor (× 1000) rather than just the new number.

**7. (a)** Mass = 13.6 × 250 [1] = 3400 g [1] = **3.4 kg** [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** V = m ÷ ρ = 0.46 ÷ 790 [1] = 5.82 × 10⁻⁴ [1] = **5.8 × 10⁻⁴ m³** [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Rearranging is shown by writing the new form (V = m ÷ ρ). The conversion from grams to kilograms is the step most often left out in part (a).

**8. (a)** Volume = 55.0 − 40.0 = **15.0 cm³** [1].
**(b)** Density = 133.5 ÷ 15.0 = 8.9 g/cm³ [1] = **8900 kg/m³** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** **Copper**, because 8900 kg/m³ matches the copper value. [1]
**(d)** Any two from: air bubbles on the object, making the volume read too large [1]; reading the meniscus inaccurately [1]. **[2]** An alloy rather than a pure metal would also change the density.
*Examiner insight:* For error sources, say which way the result is affected. "Human error" on its own is too vague to gain credit.

**9. (a)** In a gas the particles are far apart [1], while in a liquid they are close together / touching [1]. So a given volume of air contains far fewer particles and much less mass [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** Ratio = 1000 ÷ 1.2 [1] = 833, so **830 times** (2 s.f.) [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Explain density as a difference in spacing, not in particle size. Statements such as "the particles in a gas are lighter" do not explain the difference.

**10. (a)** Mass = density × volume = 0.80 × 0.0150 [1] = **0.012 kg** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** No gas has entered or left, so the mass is conserved. [1]
**(c)** Density = 0.012 ÷ 0.0040 [1] = **3.0 kg/m³** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the mass found in part (a) in part (c), and show the substitution so your method is clear even if an earlier value is slightly off.

**11. (a)** Volume = 400 cm³ = 4.0 × 10⁻⁴ m³ [1]. Mass = 920 × 4.0 × 10⁻⁴ = **0.368 kg** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Volume of water = 0.368 ÷ 1000 = 3.68 × 10⁻⁴ m³ [1] = **368 cm³** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Decrease = 400 − 368 = 32 cm³ [1]. Percentage = 32 ÷ 400 × 100 = **8.0%** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* The percentage change is taken relative to the original volume (the ice), not the final volume. Dividing by 368 gives a different value.

**12. (a)** Volume = area × thickness = 0.010 × 1.0 × 10⁻⁷ [1] = **1.0 × 10⁻⁹ m³** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Mass = 19 300 × 1.0 × 10⁻⁹ = 1.93 × 10⁻⁵ kg [1] = **19.3 mg** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Number = 1.0 × 10⁻⁷ ÷ 1 × 10⁻¹⁰ [1] = **about 1000 atoms** [1]. **[2]**
**(d)** The atom is mostly empty space [1], and the nucleus is tiny compared with the atom, so most alpha particles pass between the nuclei without being deflected [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* In part (b) the unit conversion (1 kg = 1 000 000 mg) is needed to reach milligrams. In part (d), name both the empty space and the small size of the nucleus.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Stating alpha scattering observations without saying what each shows about the atom.
- Mixing up the models, for example giving the plum pudding model a nucleus.
- Calling the nucleus the biggest part of the atom rather than the heaviest.
- Leaving standard form powers uncorrected when converting millimetres to metres.
- Not converting grams to kilograms, or cm³ to m³, before using the density equation.
- Treating 1 g/cm³ as 100 kg/m³.
- Using the final volume instead of the original as the base for a percentage change.
- Explaining low gas density using lighter or smaller particles instead of larger spacing.
- Giving an error source with no direction of effect on the result.
