**These notes condense the central demand of Paper 2 Section B in OxfordAQA International GCSE
English Language (9270):** writing "for audience and purpose, which could be both multiple and
diverse". For full explanation, a plan and a model paragraph, use the
[multiple audiences and purposes study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-english-language-9270-multiple-audiences-and-purposes/);
for Section B as a whole, see the
[Paper 2 Section B writing revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-english-language-9270-section-b-writing-revision-notes/).

## Two words to define

- **Multiple**: more than one audience or purpose.
- **Diverse**: audiences or purposes that differ in interest, knowledge, power or direction.

"The most successful responses will demonstrate the students' ability to respond to this demand."

## Four questions before planning

1. Who reads first, and who else will read?
2. Do the audiences agree? Which is hardest to win?
3. How many purposes, and in what order?
4. Which purpose is the main one?

## Kinds of multiple audience

| Kind | Example | Demand |
|---|---|---|
| Two named groups | Residents and local businesses | Address each group's concerns |
| Primary and secondary | Letter to the council, also published | Formal and clear |
| Expert and non-expert | Science magazine for students and teachers | Explain without talking down |
| Sympathetic and sceptical | Supporters and doubters | Encourage one, answer the other |
| Different ages | Children and parents | Friendly, but respectful to adults |

## Purpose combinations and order

- **Inform → persuade**: explain the scheme, then argue for it.
- **Explain → advise**: explain the cause, then suggest strategies.
- **Describe → evaluate**: show what it is like, then judge it.
- **Argue + reassure**: support a change while answering fears.

## Six techniques

1. **Segmented address**: "If you are a parent… If you are a student…" (two or three times only).
2. **Register for the most powerful reader**, warmed slightly for others.
3. **Anticipate the sceptic**: name the concern fairly, then answer it.
4. **An example for each audience.**
5. **Signpost the purposes**: a question or link that moves from explaining to persuading.
6. **Form features**: sub-headings for different readers in a leaflet or article.

## Using the sources

- A statistic from a report for an official reader.
- An anecdote from a blog for a general reader.
- Rework in your own words; select only what serves a named audience.
- Never copy whole sentences.

## Plan header

```
Audiences:  ______ AND ______   hardest to win: ______
Purposes:   ______ then ______  main purpose: ______
Form:       ______  conventions: ______
Register:   set by ______, warmed for ______
```

## Weak and strong compared

Task: a speech to students and parents supporting a later start to the school day.

- **Weak:** "Everyone hates getting up early, so let's start later. It will be great." Only
  students are addressed; there is no explanation and no answer to parents' worries.
- **Strong:** "For students, a later start means arriving awake rather than half asleep. For
  parents, the school's breakfast club means no change to the working day. A plan that works for
  both deserves your support." Both audiences, a benefit for each, and a call to action.

## Checking a draft

Read the draft once as each audience. After each paragraph ask: would this reader feel addressed,
respected and answered? Mark any paragraph that serves no named audience or purpose, and cut or
redirect it.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Primary audience | The reader the text is addressed to |
| Secondary audience | Others who will also read it |
| Diverse audience | Readers with different interests or views |
| Segmented address | Speaking to different groups in turn |
| Register | Level of formality |
| Counter-argument | A view the sceptical audience may hold |
| Signposting | Words or structure that show the reader where the text is going |
| Call to action | An ending that asks readers to do something |

## Writing objectives

- **W1**: form, register and vocabulary adapted to each audience and purpose.
- **W2**: short sentences for turns; longer ones to explain solutions.
- **W3**: purposes in order; moves between audiences signposted.
- **W4**: accuracy throughout, including headings and direct address.

## Exam traps

- Writing for one audience only.
- Wild switches of register.
- Listing audiences in a greeting and then ignoring them.
- Purposes mixed in random order.
- Copying the sources.
- Never meeting the sceptic.

## Self-test

1. Quote the specification's description of the focus of the Paper 2 writing task.
2. What is the difference between "multiple" and "diverse"?
3. Name the four questions to ask before planning.
4. Give an example of a primary and a secondary audience.
5. In what order do "inform" and "persuade" usually work best, and why?
6. What is segmented address, and why use it sparingly?
7. How should you set register when audiences differ?
8. How do you handle a sceptical audience?
9. How can form features help with multiple audiences?
10. How can the sources help with different audiences?
11. Which audience should usually get its own paragraph?
12. Which writing objective covers adapting register to audience?

## Answers

1. "Writing for audience and purpose, which could be both multiple and diverse."
2. Multiple means more than one; diverse means they differ from each other in interests, knowledge,
   power or direction.
3. Who reads first and who else; whether the audiences agree; how many purposes and in what order;
   which purpose is the main one.
4. For example, a letter to the council (primary) that will be published in the local paper for the
   public (secondary).
5. Inform first, then persuade, because readers need to understand the proposal before they can be
   won over.
6. Speaking directly to each group in turn; sparingly, so it shows control rather than becoming a
   list.
7. By the audience with most authority or most to lose, warmed slightly for the others.
8. Name their concern fairly, then answer it.
9. Sub-headings let different readers find what concerns them.
10. They supply material such as statistics for official readers or anecdotes for general readers,
    which you rework in your own words.
11. The audience that is hardest to win over.
12. W1.
