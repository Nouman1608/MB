## Why businesses need finance (5.1.1)

| Reason | Meaning in one line |
|---|---|
| Start-up capital | Money to open: premises, equipment, first inventory |
| Expansion or growth | Money for new sites, products or markets |
| Replacing non-current assets | Worn-out machinery, vehicles or buildings must be renewed |
| New technology | Spending now for lower costs or better quality later |
| Working capital | Money for everyday costs such as wages and materials |

**Short-term need:** about a year or less, for running costs or a gap before customers pay.
**Long-term need:** several years, for non-current assets, growth or start-up.

Rule: match the length of the finance to the length of the need.

### Working capital

- Working capital = current assets - current liabilities.
- Current assets: inventory, trade receivables, cash.
- Current liabilities: trade payables, overdraft.
- It pays everyday bills. A profitable business can still run out of working capital and fail.
- Too little: late payments, lost supplier trust, possible insolvency.
- Too much: idle money that could earn more if invested.

Small reminder: current assets £12,200 and current liabilities £7,700 give working capital of £4,500.

## Sources of finance (5.1.2)

### Internal sources

| Source | For | Against |
|---|---|---|
| Owners' investment | No interest, no outside control | Limited by savings, personal money at risk |
| Retained profit | Cheap, no repayment | Only when profitable, less paid to owners |
| Sale of unwanted assets | Cash without borrowing | One-off, may sell below value |
| Working capital | Frees cash already in the business | Leaves less cushion for bills |

### External sources

| Source | What it is | For | Against |
|---|---|---|---|
| Share capital | Company sells shares | Large sums, no repayment | Shared ownership and profits |
| Venture capital | Specialist investor takes a stake | Big sums, advice | Owners lose some control |
| Bank overdraft | Spend beyond account balance up to a limit | Flexible, interest only on amount used | Dear if kept long, can be recalled |
| Leasing | Rent an asset | Low start cost, no ownership worry | Never owned, dearer in total |
| Hire purchase | Deposit then instalments | Spread cost, own at the end | Dearer than paying cash |
| Bank loan | Fixed sum repaid with interest | Predictable repayments | Repay even in a bad year, may need security |
| Trade credit | Pay supplier later | Free if on time | Supplier may refuse future credit |
| Government grants | Money given, not repaid | No repayment | Conditions, slow to obtain |
| Crowdfunding | Many small contributions online | Tests demand, builds followers | May miss target |

### Internal against external

- Internal: cheaper and quicker, owners keep control, but the amount is limited.
- External: larger amounts, but costs, repayment pressure or lost control.

### Must-know distinctions

- **Leasing and hire purchase.** Leasing is renting with no ownership. Hire purchase ends with the business owning the asset.
- **Overdraft and loan.** An overdraft is flexible and short-term. A loan is a fixed sum for a set period.
- **Shares and loans.** Shares need no repayment but share ownership. Loans keep ownership but must be repaid with interest.
- **Grant and loan.** A grant is not repaid. A loan is.
- **Working capital and cash.** Working capital includes inventory and receivables, not only cash.
- **Retained profit and cash.** Retained profit may already be spent on assets.

## Choosing a source of finance

Method in steps:

1. Say what the money is for (purpose) and how long it is needed (length of time).
2. Check the amount required.
3. Check the legal form: only a company can issue shares.
4. Check size of business and existing loans: can it borrow more?
5. Compare the cost of each option.
6. Recommend one source and give a reason, then say why another source is weaker.

Factors to consider: size of business, legal form of business, amount required, length of time, existing loans, cost, purpose.

Quick matching guide:

| Situation | Likely sensible source |
|---|---|
| Short gap before customers pay | Overdraft or trade credit |
| Big machine, spread the cost | Hire purchase or leasing |
| Long-term expansion of a small firm | Bank loan, with owner's funds |
| Large growth plan by a company | Issue shares or venture capital |
| New idea needing proof of demand | Crowdfunding |
| Use of money already earned | Retained profit |

Treat this as a starting point. Always check the facts in the question, because they can change the answer.

## Answering the question types

| Command word | What to do |
|---|---|
| State, identify | Give the point briefly, with no explanation |
| Define | Give the meaning in a sentence |
| Calculate | Show the working, then the answer with units |
| Explain | Give a point, then a reason or consequence |
| Analyse | Give a point, develop it with a chain of reasoning, and use the case |
| Recommend and justify | Choose one source and give reasons linked to the case |
| Evaluate | Weigh both sides, then reach a judgement and say what it depends on |

A good chain for a finance answer: the source, what it gives the business, then the effect on cash, cost or control. For example: a bank loan gives a fixed sum, so the business can plan repayments, but interest must be paid even in a weak year, which reduces profit.

Use the case. If the stem mentions a sole trader, a company, an existing loan or a short gap in cash, one of your points should refer to it.

## Worked reminders

**Hire purchase.** Machine with a cash price of £6,000. Deposit £1,200 and 12 payments of £440.
Total = 1,200 + (12 x 440) = 1,200 + 5,280 = £6,480. Extra cost = £480.

**Shares.** A company with 120,000 shares issues 30,000 at £0.80.
Raised = £24,000. New holders own 30,000 / 150,000 = 20%.

**Loan interest.** £15,000 at 6% a year gives £900 interest a year, or £3,600 over four years (interest on the full amount).

**Grant share.** A £6,000 grant towards a £30,000 project covers 20%.

## Quick self-test

1. State three reasons why a business may need finance.
2. Define working capital.
3. A business has current assets of £18,600 and current liabilities of £21,000. Calculate its working capital.
4. Give one example of a short-term and one of a long-term finance need.
5. State two internal sources of finance.
6. Explain why a sole trader cannot raise money by issuing shares.
7. Give one difference between leasing and hire purchase.
8. A shop hires equipment on hire purchase: deposit £900 and 18 payments of £150. The cash price is £3,000. Calculate the extra cost.
9. Give one advantage and one disadvantage of a bank overdraft.
10. Fifty backers give an average of £80 each through crowdfunding. How much is raised?
11. State two factors that affect the choice of a source of finance.
12. A grant of £7,500 covers 25% of a project. What is the total cost?

### Answers

1. Any three of: start-up capital; expansion or growth; replacing non-current assets; investing in new technology; working capital.
2. The money a business has available for everyday expenses (current assets less current liabilities).
3. 18,600 - 21,000 = **-£2,400** (negative working capital).
4. Short-term: paying wages and suppliers. Long-term: buying a new building or machine, or expanding.
5. Any two of: owners' investment; retained profit; sale of unwanted assets; working capital.
6. Only companies have shares to sell. A sole trader's business is not a company, so there are no shares to issue.
7. With leasing the business never owns the asset. With hire purchase it owns the asset after the final payment.
8. 900 + (18 x 150) = 900 + 2,700 = 3,600. Extra cost = 3,600 - 3,000 = **£600**.
9. Advantage: flexible, interest only on the amount used. Disadvantage: expensive if used for long, and the bank can ask for it back at short notice.
10. 50 x 80 = **£4,000**.
11. Any two of: size of business; legal form; amount required; length of time; existing loans; cost; purpose.
12. 7,500 / 0.25 = **£30,000**.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Defining working capital as "cash" or "money in the bank".
- Giving an overdraft as the answer to a long-term, large-sum need.
- Saying all businesses can issue shares.
- Listing sources without choosing one when asked to recommend.
- Giving a reason with no link to the business in the case (size, legal form, existing loans, purpose).
- Writing "cheap" or "expensive" with no reason, such as interest charged or ownership given up.
- Mixing up leasing and hire purchase.
- Forgetting to subtract current liabilities, or giving a negative result the wrong sign.
- Treating a grant like a loan, or forgetting that a loan must be repaid with interest.
