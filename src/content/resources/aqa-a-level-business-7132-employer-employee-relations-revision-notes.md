Condensed notes on section **3.6.5 Making human resource decisions: improving employer-employee relations** of AQA A-level Business (7132, for first teaching from September 2023). The full explanations and the Marden Logistics worked example are in the [employer-employee relations study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-employer-employee-relations/); exam-style questions are in the [employer-employee relations practice set](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-employer-employee-relations-practice/).

## Specification content

- Influences on the extent and methods of employee involvement in decision making; employee representation methods include trade unions and works councils.
- How to manage and improve employer-employee communications and relations.
- The value of good employer-employee relations.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Employee involvement | Giving employees a voice in decisions that affect them |
| Trade union | Organisation of workers that represents members' interests |
| Collective bargaining | Union negotiates pay and conditions for all members |
| Works council | Elected employee representatives who meet management to be informed and consulted |
| Industrial action | Strike, work-to-rule, overtime ban |
| Grievance | A complaint raised by an employee |
| Mediation | Neutral person helps both sides reach agreement |
| Arbitration | Neutral person decides or proposes the settlement |
| Two-way communication | Information flows both down and up |

## Scale of involvement

Informing (notice board, briefing); consulting (survey, suggestion scheme, council); joint decision making (employee directors, negotiation); employee ownership (share schemes).

## Trade unions against works councils

| | Trade union | Works council |
|---|---|---|
| Members | Workers who join | Elected representatives of the workforce |
| Role | Negotiate pay and conditions; represent members | Consult and share information |
| Power | Can threaten industrial action | Advisory; relies on goodwill |
| Style | Negotiation, may be adversarial | Cooperative |
| For employer | One voice for many workers; early warning | Early warning; better decisions |
| Against | Strikes; pay demands; slower change | Time-consuming; may raise expectations |

## Influences on the extent of involvement

Management style (autocratic, paternalistic, democratic); skills and attitudes of staff; size of business; nature and urgency of the decision; legal requirements; union presence and history; industry and type of work; technology (online tools help; remote work makes relationships harder); labour market (scarce skills mean employers listen more).

**Benefits of involvement:** better decisions; commitment and motivation; smoother change; fewer disputes; lower turnover; ideas for savings. **Costs:** slower decisions; loss of control; unmet expectations; time and money; confidentiality; representatives may not speak for all.

## Improving communication and relations

- **Top-down:** briefings, newsletters, intranet, video updates.
- **Bottom-up:** suggestion schemes, surveys, open-door policy, one-to-ones, council representatives.
- **Feedback loop:** show what happened to suggestions.
- Train managers to listen and handle conflict; fair, clear grievance and discipline procedures; consult early on change.
- **Resolving disputes:** informal talks, negotiation, conciliation, mediation or arbitration (for example by an independent body), tribunal or industrial action as a last resort.
- **Barriers:** many layers, shift or remote working, lack of trust, overload, language and culture, managers who do not listen.

## Value of good relations

Higher motivation and productivity; lower absence and turnover; fewer disputes; smoother change; better ideas and quality; strong employer brand; better customer service; lower legal costs. Poor relations lead to strikes, resignations, absence and a damaged reputation.

## Worked example: cost of a dispute

Payroll £3m. Union asks 4% (£120,000). Employer offers 2% (£60,000). Gap **£60,000** a year.

- Strike would lose sales of £210,000 at 35% gross margin: lost gross profit **£73,500**.
- Compensation £15,000: total **£88,500**.
- Wages saved £16,000: net cost **£72,500**.
- A 3% settlement costs £90,000 a year, £30,000 more than the offer. The strike's net cost is about 2.4 times that extra annual cost.
- But a pay rise is permanent and a strike cost is one-off; the strike figure also ignores lasting damage to trust and morale.

## How to write a good answer

- Identify who is involved (union, works council, individual staff) and what they can do.
- Link the choice of involvement to management style, workforce skills and the nature of the decision.
- Use numbers to compare the cost of a dispute with the cost of a settlement.
- Weigh benefits (commitment, ideas) against costs (time, control) and say what the decision depends on.

## Exam traps

- Unions bargain (and may take industrial action); works councils consult.
- More involvement is not always better: weigh speed and control.
- Communication must be two-way.
- A pay rise is permanent; a strike is not. Compare carefully.
- Apply the answer to the workforce and management style in the case.

## Self-test

1. Define "collective bargaining".
2. Give one difference between a trade union and a works council.
3. Name two forms of industrial action.
4. Give two benefits to an employer of involving employees in decisions.
5. Give two costs of involving employees in decisions.
6. Give two influences on how much a business involves its employees.
7. Give two ways to improve upward communication.
8. What is the difference between mediation and arbitration?
9. A payroll is £2 million. A union asks for 5% and the employer offers 3%. Calculate the annual gap.
10. Give two benefits of good employer-employee relations.
11. Give one barrier to communication in a business with remote workers.
12. Why might an autocratic manager involve staff less?

### Answers

1. Negotiation of pay and conditions between an employer and a union on behalf of its members.
2. A union negotiates pay and conditions and can take industrial action; a works council mainly shares information and consults.
3. Any two: strike; work-to-rule; overtime ban; go-slow.
4. Any two: better decisions; commitment; smoother change; fewer disputes; lower turnover.
5. Any two: slower decisions; loss of control; raised expectations; time and cost; confidentiality risks.
6. Any two: management style; workforce skills; size; union presence; legal requirements; type of decision.
7. Any two: suggestion scheme; staff survey; open-door policy; one-to-one meetings; council representatives.
8. In mediation a neutral person helps both sides agree; in arbitration a neutral person decides or proposes the settlement.
9. 2% of £2,000,000 = **£40,000** a year.
10. Any two: motivation; lower turnover and absence; fewer disputes; smoother change; better customer service.
11. Any one: less face-to-face contact; difficulty building trust; messages missed; time zones.
12. An autocratic manager believes decisions should be made by managers alone and does not value or trust staff input.
