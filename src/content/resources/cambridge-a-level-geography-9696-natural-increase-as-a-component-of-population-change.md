This guide covers **4.1 Natural increase as a component of population change** in Topic 4, Population, of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Geography (9696) syllabus for 2025 and 2026. The syllabus content is:

- **Natural increase rate, birth rate and death rate, fertility rate, and infant mortality rate.**
- **Factors (social, economic, environmental and political) affecting levels of fertility and mortality.**
- **The interpretation of age/sex structure diagrams.**
- **Population structure (age, gender, dependency, and dependency ratio).**

It is examined in Paper 2 Core Human Geography, very often through a population pyramid or a table of demographic rates in Section A, and within the Population structured question in Section B. The [4.1 natural increase revision notes](/resources/a-level-geography-9696-natural-increase-revision-notes/) give the key terms, a pyramid feature table and the main calculations. This guide explains why each measure behaves as it does, shows a step-by-step method for reading pyramids and works through new calculations. Practise with the [4.1 practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-natural-increase-as-a-component-of-population-change-practice/).

## The measures, precisely

| Measure | Definition | Usual unit |
|---|---|---|
| Crude birth rate | Live births in a year per 1000 people in the total population | per 1000 |
| Crude death rate | Deaths in a year per 1000 people in the total population | per 1000 |
| Natural increase rate | Birth rate minus death rate (natural decrease if negative) | per 1000, or per cent |
| Total fertility rate | Average number of children a woman would have over her lifetime at current age-specific rates | children per woman |
| Infant mortality rate | Deaths of infants under one year old per 1000 live births in a year | per 1000 live births |

Two points are often tested:

- **Natural increase excludes migration.** Total population change = natural increase + net migration. A country can have natural decrease and still grow if net in-migration is large enough.
- **The infant mortality rate uses live births, not total population, as its base.** This makes it a sensitive indicator of health care, nutrition, sanitation and the education of mothers, which is why it is widely used as a measure of development.

## Why crude rates can mislead

Crude rates are affected by **age structure**. A country with many elderly people can have a **higher** crude death rate than a poorer country with a young population, even though people in the first country live longer. Similarly, an ageing country may have a low crude birth rate partly because a smaller share of its population is of childbearing age. The **total fertility rate** avoids this problem for births, which is why it is the best single measure of family size. **Replacement-level fertility** is about 2.1 children per woman in countries with low mortality: two children replace the parents, and the extra fraction allows for children who die before adulthood and for slightly more boys than girls being born. Where infant and child mortality are high, replacement level is higher.

## Worked example 1: rates from raw figures

*A fictional country has a population of 20 million. In one year there were 560 000 live births, 160 000 deaths and 16 800 deaths of infants under one.*

- Crude birth rate = 560 000 / 20 000 000 x 1000 = **28 per 1000**.
- Crude death rate = 160 000 / 20 000 000 x 1000 = **8 per 1000**.
- Natural increase rate = 28 - 8 = 20 per 1000 = **2.0%** a year.
- Infant mortality rate = 16 800 / 560 000 x 1000 = **30 per 1000 live births**.

Note the different bases: the first two divide by the total population, the infant mortality rate by live births.

## Factors affecting fertility and mortality

The syllabus groups factors as social, economic, environmental and political. Explain how each works, not just what it is.

**Fertility**

- **Social:** female education delays marriage, increases knowledge and use of contraception, and opens up careers, so fertility falls. Religious and cultural norms can encourage large families or discourage contraception. Where infant mortality is high, parents may have more children to ensure some survive.
- **Economic:** in farming economies, children contribute labour and support parents in old age, so large families make economic sense. In urban, industrial economies, children are costly to house and educate, and pensions reduce reliance on children, so fertility falls.
- **Environmental:** in harsh or hazard-prone environments with high child mortality, fertility tends to stay high. Access to health services, often poorer in remote rural areas, affects access to family planning.
- **Political:** governments can lower fertility through anti-natalist policies (family planning campaigns, incentives or restrictions) or raise it through pro-natalist policies (child benefits, parental leave, childcare). Laws on the minimum age of marriage also matter.

**Mortality**

- **Social:** access to health care, vaccination, clean water and sanitation; diet; lifestyle risks such as smoking and obesity in richer societies.
- **Economic:** income determines nutrition, housing quality and ability to pay for treatment; national wealth funds hospitals and public health.
- **Environmental:** disease environments (for example, malaria in parts of the tropics), natural hazards, air and water pollution, and climatic extremes such as heatwaves.
- **Political:** conflict raises mortality directly and by disrupting services; government spending on health and sanitation lowers it.

## Reading an age/sex structure diagram: a method

Work through a pyramid in this order:

1. **Overall shape:** a wide base and narrow top (youthful), straight sides (stable), or a narrow base with a wide top (ageing).
2. **Base:** compare the youngest bars with the bars just above them. A base narrower than the bar above shows births have fallen recently.
3. **Middle:** look for bulges (baby booms or in-migration of workers) and indentations (low births in the past, or losses from war, disease or out-migration).
4. **Top:** a broad top shows high life expectancy; more women than men in older bands reflects women's longer life expectancy.
5. **Sex balance:** a large excess of males in working ages usually indicates male labour in-migration, as in some Gulf states; a shortage of working-age males can indicate male out-migration.
6. **Date the anomalies:** a bar's age tells you when that cohort was born. People aged 30 to 34 in 2025 were born in 1991 to 1995.

## Worked example 2: dating an anomaly

*A fictional country's pyramid for 2025 shows a marked indentation in the 45 to 49 band in both sexes, and both neighbouring bands are much wider.*

The cohort aged 45 to 49 in 2025 was born between 1976 and 1980. A shortage in both sexes at this age suggests a fall in births in those years, perhaps due to a crisis, conflict or a short-lived policy, rather than war deaths in adulthood, which would usually affect men more. To confirm, you would look for a second, smaller indentation about 25 to 35 years later, when this smaller cohort would have had its own children.

## Dependency and the dependency ratio

**Dependency ratio** = (population aged 0 to 14 + population aged 65 and over) / (population aged 15 to 64) x 100.

*A fictional country has 6.0 million aged 0 to 14, 12.5 million aged 15 to 64 and 1.5 million aged 65 and over.*

- Dependency ratio = (6.0 + 1.5) / 12.5 x 100 = **60**.
- Youth dependency = 6.0 / 12.5 x 100 = **48**; old-age dependency = 1.5 / 12.5 x 100 = **12**.

So most of this country's dependency is young people, and its priorities will be schools, child health and future jobs. The ratio is only a guide: many 15 to 19 year olds are in education, many over-65s work, and unemployment means not every person aged 15 to 64 is earning. The **gender** structure matters too: a sex ratio far from balance affects marriage, labour supply and future births.

## Annotated model answer

*"Explain how social and economic factors cause fertility rates to fall as countries develop." [6]*

> As countries develop, more girls stay in school for longer **(social factor)**, which delays marriage and the age at first birth and increases knowledge and use of contraception **(mechanisms)**. Better health care lowers infant mortality, so parents no longer need many children to ensure some survive **(social factor and mechanism)**. Economically, more people move to cities and work in industry and services, where children do not contribute labour and are expensive to house and educate **(economic factor)**. Pensions and savings also reduce the need for children to support parents in old age **(second economic mechanism)**, so the total fertility rate falls towards replacement level **(outcome)**.

## Common mistakes

- Calculating infant mortality per 1000 people instead of per 1000 live births.
- Writing that a high crude death rate always shows poor health care.
- Including migration in natural increase.
- Describing a pyramid bar by bar without identifying the overall shape and anomalies.
- Using "birth rate" and "fertility rate" as if they were the same.
- Giving dependency ratios without saying whether youth or old-age dependency dominates.

## Exam technique

- **Calculate**: show the formula, the figures and the unit.
- **Describe** a pyramid: quote percentages for specific age bands and compare sexes.
- **Explain**: link each factor to fertility or mortality through a clear mechanism.
- **Suggest** reasons for anomalies: use the date of birth of the cohort to propose a plausible cause.
