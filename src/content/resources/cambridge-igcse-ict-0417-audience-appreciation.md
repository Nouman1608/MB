Section 9.1 of Cambridge IGCSE ICT 0417 is short in the syllabus but runs through the whole course. It has three statements: "show a clear sense of audience and purpose"; "planning ICT solutions that are responsive to and respectful of the needs of an audience"; and "analyse the needs of an audience when creating ICT solutions". In theory questions you are given an audience and asked to analyse its needs or justify design features. The same thinking guides every document, presentation, spreadsheet and web page you make in the practical topics.

The [audience study guide](/resources/cambridge-igcse-ict-audience-study-guide/) introduces the factors to consider, such as age and knowledge, and covers copyright (9.2). This page goes deeper into the three syllabus statements: how to analyse an audience, how purpose shapes a solution, and what "responsive and respectful" mean in practice. Use the [audience appreciation revision notes](/resources/cambridge-igcse-ict-0417-audience-appreciation-revision-notes/) for recall and the [audience appreciation practice questions](/resources/cambridge-igcse-ict-0417-audience-appreciation-practice/) to apply it.

## Audience and purpose work together

The **audience** is who the solution is for. The **purpose** is what it must achieve. A clear sense of both is needed, because the same audience can need very different solutions for different purposes.

| Purpose | What the solution must do | Typical features |
|---|---|---|
| **Inform** | Give facts clearly | Headings, short sections, tables, charts, neutral tone |
| **Instruct** | Help someone carry out a task | Numbered steps, one action per step, screenshots or diagrams |
| **Persuade** | Change what someone thinks or does | Strong headline, images, benefits, a clear call to action |
| **Entertain** | Hold attention and give enjoyment | Images, colour, sound or animation, lively language |

For example, parents of primary pupils might receive a **persuasive** flyer for a school fair (bright images, a strong headline and the date in large text) and an **instructional** guide to the school's online payment system (numbered steps with screenshots). The audience is the same; the purpose changes the design.

## Analysing the needs of an audience

Analysis means finding out, before designing, what the audience needs. Work through these questions and record the answers.

1. **Who are they?** Age range, roles (pupils, customers, staff, managers), and how many.
2. **What do they already know?** Their knowledge of the subject and their ICT skills decide how much explanation and what vocabulary to use.
3. **Why will they use it?** What they want to find out or do. This links to the purpose.
4. **How and where will they use it?** Printed or on screen; a phone, a desktop or a large screen in a hall; quickly in passing or in detail at a desk.
5. **Do they have particular needs?** Visual or hearing impairment, reading difficulties, limited mobility, a different first language.
6. **What are their expectations and values?** Cultural background, what they would find appropriate or offensive, and the tone they expect, formal or informal.

### How to find out

The designer can ask a sample of the audience through **questionnaires** or **interviews**, **observe** them using similar materials, look at **existing materials** that work well for them, and **test a draft** with a few members of the audience before the final version. Testing a draft matters because designers often assume the audience knows more than it does.

## Responsive to the needs of an audience

A responsive solution changes in response to what the analysis found. Each design decision should be traceable to a need.

### Content

- Reading level and vocabulary suited to the audience's knowledge, with technical terms explained or avoided.
- The right amount of detail: a summary for busy managers, step-by-step help for beginners.
- Information in the order the audience needs it, with the most important first.

### Presentation

- Font style and size suited to the age, eyesight and setting.
- Strong contrast between text and background.
- Images that support the message and that the audience will recognise.
- A consistent layout so users can find things easily.

### Format and medium

- A web page or app if the audience will use phones; a printed leaflet if many have no internet access; a presentation if the message is delivered to a group.
- Navigation that suits the users' ICT skills, such as large, clearly labelled buttons for occasional users.

## Respectful of the needs of an audience

"Respectful" adds a further requirement: the solution must not exclude, embarrass or offend the people it is for.

- **Accessibility**: include alternative text for images so screen readers can describe them; add captions or transcripts for video and audio; do not rely on colour alone to show meaning, since some users cannot distinguish certain colours; allow text to be enlarged.
- **Cultural sensitivity**: choose images, symbols, colours, gestures and examples that will not cause offence; be aware that dates, names and holidays differ between cultures.
- **Inclusive language**: avoid stereotypes, slang that excludes some readers, and language that talks down to the audience.
- **Appropriate content**: suitable for the age of the audience, with no inappropriate images or language.
- **Privacy**: do not include personal data, such as photos of named children, without permission.

## Worked example: analysing an audience and justifying a solution

*A dental clinic wants a web page to prepare children aged 5 to 8 for their first visit. Their parents will usually read it with them on a phone. Analyse the audience and describe a suitable solution.*

| Need found in analysis | Design decision |
|---|---|
| Young children cannot read long text and may feel anxious | Very short sentences in large, rounded text; a friendly cartoon tooth character explaining each step |
| Parents want practical information quickly | A separate section for parents with opening times, what to bring and a map, in a plain style |
| Most families will view it on a phone | Single-column layout, large buttons, images sized for small screens |
| Some families speak another language at home | A language choice and pictures that make sense without words |
| Some children or parents may have visual impairments | Alt text on every image; high contrast; text that can be enlarged |
| The page must not frighten children | No images of instruments or injections; calm colours; reassuring words |

The answer shows a clear sense of **audience** (children and parents), **purpose** (to reassure and inform), and solutions that are **responsive** (each decision meets a need) and **respectful** (accessibility, language and avoiding distressing images).

## Annotated model answer

*Question: a bank is creating an online guide to using its mobile app, for customers aged over 70 who have not used banking apps before. Describe how the designer should analyse the needs of this audience.* **[4]**

"The designer should find out how much experience the customers have with smartphones, for example with a short questionnaire sent with their statements **[method, linked to knowledge]**. Interviews with a few customers could show which tasks they find difficult, such as logging in **[method, linked to purpose]**. The designer should find out whether they have eyesight or dexterity difficulties, as these affect text and button size **[particular needs]**. A draft guide should then be tested with a small group and changed using their feedback **[testing with the audience]**."

## Common mistakes

- Describing a design feature with no link to a need of the named audience.
- Forgetting the purpose: a feature can suit the audience but not the purpose.
- Writing "make it colourful" or "make it easy" without saying how or why.
- Treating "respectful" only as politeness; it also means accessible, inclusive and culturally appropriate.
- Assuming every audience is like you: test with real members of the audience.

## Exam technique

**Analyse** the needs of an audience: identify the needs and explain how each was found or why it matters. **Describe** features: name the feature and its effect. **Explain** or **justify** a feature: link it to a specific need with "because". **Discuss** a design: weigh how well it meets the needs of different members of the audience and reach a judgement.
