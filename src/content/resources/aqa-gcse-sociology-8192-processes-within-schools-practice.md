These questions cover 3.4.4 Processes within schools, from the AQA GCSE Sociology (8192) specification, assessed in Paper 1: streaming, setting, mixed ability teaching, labelling and the self-fulfilling prophecy; interactionist, functionalist, feminist and Marxist views; Ball on teacher expectations and Willis on counter school cultures. All schools and people in the items are fictional.

## Section A: short questions

**1.** Which method places pupils in ability groups separately for each subject?

A. Streaming
B. Banding
C. Setting
D. Mixed ability teaching

**[1]**

**2.** Which research method did Ball use in *Beachside Comprehensive*?

A. Postal questionnaire
B. Participant observation
C. Content analysis
D. Structured interviews only

**[1]**

**3.** Define the term "self-fulfilling prophecy". **[2]**

**4.** Identify two features of the lads' counter school culture in Willis's study. **[2]**

**5.** Describe one disadvantage of streaming. **[2]**

**6.** Describe how Ball organised his study to compare banding with mixed ability teaching. **[3]**

**7.** Identify and explain one way in which teacher expectations may affect pupils' achievement. **[4]**

## Section B: longer and structured questions

**8.** Read the item.

> At Harrowgate High (a fictional school), Year 10 maths is set using a test taken in Year 7. Set 4 is entered for the foundation tier. One teacher says of set 4: "They're nice kids, but they're never going to be mathematicians." Several pupils in set 4 say they have "given up on maths".

Using the item, explain how setting at Harrowgate High could produce a self-fulfilling prophecy. **[4]**

**9.** Explain one similarity and one difference between Ball's and Willis's explanations of anti-school attitudes. **[4]**

**10.** Explain how a feminist might argue that processes within schools affect girls and boys differently. **[3]**

**11.** Identify one strength and one limitation of Willis's research methods. **[4]**

**12.** Discuss how far sociologists would agree that processes within schools are the main cause of differences in educational achievement. **[12]**

**13.** Discuss how far sociologists would agree that setting and streaming benefit all pupils. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** C. Setting [1] **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Streaming and banding both place pupils in one group for every subject.

**2.** B. Participant observation [1] **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Ball spent three years in the school; that detail is useful in evaluation questions.

**3.** A prediction about a pupil, such as a teacher's label [1], that comes true because it changes how the pupil is treated and how they see themselves [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* The definition must include the mechanism, not just "the label comes true".

**4.** Any two from: opposed to the school's values; felt superior to teachers; felt superior to conformist pupils ("ear'oles"); aimed to avoid lessons and "have a laff"; resented school controlling their time; neither docile nor obedient. First feature [1]; second feature [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Quoting the lads' own terms, such as "ear'oles", shows precise knowledge.

**5.** Pupils are placed in one group for every subject [1], so a pupil who is strong in one subject but weak in another may be held back in the subject they are good at [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Alternatively, credit the point that low streams can be labelled as failures, if the consequence is explained.

**6.** Ball studied two cohorts moving through the same school [1]. One cohort was banded by ability [1]. The other was taught in mixed ability classes, which let him compare how the two forms of grouping affected pupils [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Students often describe the findings when the question asks about the design; read the command word.

**7.** Teachers may expect less of some pupils, for example those in low sets or from working-class backgrounds [1]. Becker found teachers judged pupils against an "ideal pupil" closest to middle-class pupils [1]. Pupils far from the ideal may receive less encouragement and easier work [1], so they may lose motivation and achieve less, creating a self-fulfilling prophecy [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Ball's finding that middle-class pupils were more often placed in band 1 can replace Becker as supporting evidence.

**8.** Set 4 has been labelled as less able by a test taken years earlier [1]. The teacher's comment shows low expectations, which may lead to less demanding teaching [1]. Entry for the foundation tier limits the grades set 4 pupils can achieve [1]. Pupils saying they have "given up on maths" shows them accepting the label, so lower results may follow and appear to confirm it [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Use all three item details: the old test, the teacher's quotation and the pupils' words.

**9.** Similarity: both found working-class pupils were more likely to develop anti-school attitudes [1], which then lowered their achievement [1]. Difference: Ball links anti-school attitudes to the school's banding and the lower expectations of band 2 [1], whereas Willis shows the lads actively choosing to reject school, valuing a masculine working-class culture [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* "Whereas" signals the difference; without it, the comparison is often only implied.

**10.** Feminists argue teachers may hold different expectations of girls and boys [1], for example praising girls for being quiet and neat and tolerating boys' disruption [1]. Pupils may also be steered towards gendered subject choices, so girls and boys head towards different and unequal careers [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* The answer should be about processes inside school, not girls' and boys' results in general.

**11.** Strength: participant observation and informal interviews let Willis see school from the lads' point of view [1], producing rich, valid qualitative data [1]. Limitation: the sample was only twelve working-class boys [1], so the findings cannot be generalised to girls, other classes or other schools [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Each point needs a reason; "small sample" alone earns one mark.

**12.** The real exam marks extended answers using levels of response, not point by point. The points below show indicative creditworthy content.

- Interactionists argue that what happens in classrooms shapes achievement [1].
- Labelling can lead to a self-fulfilling prophecy that lowers achievement [1].
- Ball found middle-class pupils were more likely to be placed in band 1, with higher expectations [1].
- Band 2 pupils were most likely to become anti-school, showing polarisation [1].
- Willis showed that a counter school culture could lead working-class boys to reject school [1].
- Setting and tiered entry can limit the grades lower-set pupils can reach [1].
- Against: material deprivation, such as lack of resources at home, affects pupils before they start school [1].
- Halsey, Heath and Ridge's class gaps point to factors outside school [1].
- Cultural capital means middle-class pupils arrive with advantages [1].
- Feminists note that changes outside school, such as feminism and the job market, explain much of girls' rising achievement [1].
- Ball's study was one school and Willis's sample was twelve boys, so neither proves processes are the main cause [1].
- Judgement: in-school processes can widen gaps that begin outside school, so they are an important but not the main cause [1]. **[12]**

*Examiner insight:* The strongest answers explain how internal and external factors interact, for example how a class-based label builds on a material disadvantage.

**13.** The real exam marks extended answers using levels of response, not point by point. The points below show indicative creditworthy content.

- Setting places pupils by subject; streaming places them in one group for all subjects [1].
- Functionalists argue grouping by ability is efficient and meritocratic [1].
- Teaching can be matched to the pace of each group, helping the most able [1].
- Setting is more flexible than streaming because it reflects different strengths [1].
- Against: Ball found middle-class pupils more likely to be placed in the top band, so placement is not purely on ability [1].
- Lower bands received lower expectations and became anti-school [1].
- Labelling of low sets can produce a self-fulfilling prophecy [1].
- Lower-tier entry can cap the grades available to low sets [1].
- Marxists argue grouping reproduces class inequality, preparing pupils for unequal jobs [1].
- Feminists argue grouping and option choices can follow gender stereotypes [1].
- Mixed ability teaching can reduce polarisation, as Ball's second cohort suggests [1].
- Judgement: grouping may help pupils placed in high sets, but evidence of class bias and labelling suggests it does not benefit all pupils [1]. **[12]**

*Examiner insight:* "All pupils" is the key phrase; a strong answer asks which pupils benefit and which do not.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Confusing streaming with setting.
- Stopping a self-fulfilling prophecy answer at the label.
- Using Ball's parental choice study instead of Beachside.
- Saying Willis's lads were labelled into failure, when they actively resisted.
- Leaving out the interactionist perspective in 3.4.4 answers.
- Ignoring item details in questions that say "using the item".
