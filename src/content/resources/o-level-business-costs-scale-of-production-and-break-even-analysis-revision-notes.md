## 4.2.1 Costs: definitions and formulas

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Fixed cost | Does not change with output (in the normal range) | Rent, insurance, manager's salary |
| Variable cost | Changes directly with output | Materials, packaging, piece-rate pay |
| Total cost | Fixed costs + total variable costs | |
| Average cost | Total cost / output (cost per unit) | |
| Contribution | Revenue - variable costs (or price - variable cost per unit) | |

Formulas: total variable cost = variable cost per unit x output. Total cost = fixed costs + total variable cost. Average cost = total cost / output.

**Must-know distinction:** fixed costs per unit fall as output rises, but total fixed costs stay the same. Average cost falls because the fixed costs are spread more thinly.

**Small reminder.** Pinnowe Bags has fixed costs of 27 000 a month and variable cost of 12 per bag. At 1 500 bags: total cost = 27 000 + 18 000 = 45 000; average cost = 45 000 / 1 500 = 30. At 3 000 bags: total cost = 27 000 + 36 000 = 63 000; average cost = 21. Average cost fell by 9 because the fixed costs are shared by twice as many bags.

### Stop or continue: method in steps

1. Work out revenue and variable costs for the product.
2. Contribution = revenue - variable costs.
3. If contribution is positive and the fixed costs would still be paid, the product helps cover them, so keep it (for now).
4. If contribution is negative, the product loses money on every sale, so consider stopping.
5. Add a judgement: can the fixed costs be avoided, are there better uses of the resources, is the product needed for the range?

**Reminder.** A product earns 4 800 in revenue, with 3 900 of variable costs and 1 500 of fixed costs charged to it. Contribution is 900, but the product shows a loss of 600 once the fixed costs are included. If the 1 500 would be paid anyway, stopping increases the loss to 1 500, so continue.

## 4.2.2 Scale of production

**Economies of scale:** the fall in cost per unit that comes when the business operates on a larger scale. **Diseconomies of scale:** average cost rises when a business becomes too large.

| Economy | Memory hook | Cause |
|---|---|---|
| Purchasing | Bulk | Supplier discounts for large orders |
| Marketing | Spread | Advertising cost shared over more units |
| Financial | Borrowing | Lower interest rates for larger, lower-risk firms |
| Managerial | Specialists | Specialist managers do the job better |
| Technical | Machines | Large equipment or production lines cut unit cost at high output |

| Diseconomy | Cause | Effect on cost |
|---|---|---|
| Poor communication | Many layers, slow or distorted messages | Delays and mistakes |
| Lack of commitment from employees | Workers feel remote from decisions | Lower productivity, higher absence |
| Weak coordination | Departments or sites do not work together | Duplication and wasted effort |

**Must-know distinctions**

- Say "average cost" or "cost per unit", never just "costs", for economies of scale.
- Economies of scale come from growing the scale of production. They are not the same as a one-off price cut.
- The usual shape of the average cost curve: falls, flat at the lowest point, then rises.

## 4.2.3 Break-even analysis

**Break-even:** the level of output where total revenue equals total cost, so there is no profit and no loss.

| Item | Standard form |
|---|---|
| Contribution per unit | price per unit less variable cost per unit |
| Break-even (units of output) | fixed costs / contribution per unit |
| Margin of safety (units) | expected or actual output - break-even output |
| Margin of safety (%) | margin of safety / expected or actual output x 100 |
| Profit at an output | contribution per unit x output - fixed costs |

The syllabus does not print these formulas, so they are the standard forms. Use them.

**Reminder.** Pinnowe Bags sells at 30. Contribution = 30 - 12 = 18. Break-even = 27 000 / 18 = 1 500 bags. Expected sales are 2 100 bags, so the margin of safety = 600 bags (600 / 2 100 x 100 = 28.6%). Profit at 2 100 = 18 x 2 100 - 27 000 = 10 800.

### Drawing a break-even chart in steps

1. Axes: output across, money (costs and revenue) up.
2. Fixed costs: a horizontal line at the fixed cost figure.
3. Total costs: start at the fixed cost figure on the vertical axis (zero output). Work out total cost at a second, high output and join the points.
4. Revenue: start at the origin. Work out revenue at the same high output and join the points.
5. Mark break-even where revenue crosses total costs, and read the output straight down.
6. Label each line and both axes, and add a title.

### Reading a chart

- Break-even output: read down from the crossing point.
- Margin of safety: the horizontal gap between actual output and break-even output.
- Profit: the vertical gap between revenue and total costs, to the right of break-even.
- Loss: the vertical gap between total costs and revenue, to the left of break-even.
- Fixed costs: where the total cost line meets the vertical axis.

### What moves break-even?

| Change | Line that moves | Effect on break-even output |
|---|---|---|
| Higher fixed costs | Total cost line moves up by the same amount | Rises (moves right) |
| Higher price | Revenue line steeper | Falls (moves left) |
| Higher variable cost per unit | Total cost line steeper | Rises (moves right) |
| Lower fixed costs | Total cost line moves down | Falls (moves left) |

### Limitations of break-even analysis

- It assumes all output is sold.
- It assumes a constant selling price, so discounts are ignored.
- It assumes variable cost per unit is constant, though bulk buying can change it.
- Fixed costs are assumed to stay fixed at all outputs.
- It normally covers a single product.
- Forecasts may be inaccurate and the picture is out of date quickly.

## Quick self-test

1. Define variable costs.
2. A firm has fixed costs of 40 000 and variable costs of 30 per unit. It sells at 50. Calculate break-even output.
3. Its expected output is 2 800 units. Calculate the margin of safety in units.
4. Total cost for 5 000 units is 55 000. Calculate average cost.
5. Name the economy of scale where a bank gives a larger firm a lower interest rate.
6. Identify two causes of diseconomies of scale.
7. Fixed costs are 18 000, price is 14 and variable cost per unit is 8. Calculate break-even output.
8. What happens to break-even output if rent rises, other things equal?
9. State one limitation of break-even analysis.
10. A product has revenue of 9 000, variable costs of 7 500 and fixed costs of 2 000 that will be paid anyway. Should the product continue? Support your answer with figures.

### Answers

1. Costs that change directly with the level of output, such as materials.
2. Contribution = 50 - 30 = 20; 40 000 / 20 = **2 000 units**.
3. 2 800 - 2 000 = **800 units**.
4. 55 000 / 5 000 = **11 per unit**.
5. **Financial** economy of scale.
6. Any two from: poor communication, lack of commitment from employees, weak coordination.
7. Contribution = 14 - 8 = 6; 18 000 / 6 = **3 000 units**.
8. It rises. Rent is a fixed cost, so the total cost line moves up and break-even moves right.
9. Any one, such as: assumes all output is sold; assumes a constant price; assumes constant variable cost per unit.
10. Contribution = 9 000 - 7 500 = 1 500, which is positive, and the fixed costs of 2 000 are paid anyway. Stopping would turn a loss of 500 into a loss of 2 000, so **continue**.

## Writing it up in the exam

For a calculation, write the formula, substitute the figures, then give the answer with its unit. For a written question, make a point, say how it changes cost or profit, and link it to the business in the stem. For a decision question, give a reason on each side and finish with a clear choice. Break-even results rest on forecasts, so say what would change your decision.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Dividing fixed costs by the price rather than the contribution per unit.
- Writing "costs" instead of "average cost" for economies of scale.
- Giving a type of economy or diseconomy by name without saying how it changes cost per unit.
- Starting the fixed cost line or total cost line at the origin on a chart.
- Reading margin of safety from the wrong axis, or giving it without units.
- Stopping a product because it makes a loss, without checking contribution or whether the fixed costs would remain.
- Copying the case study's facts without applying them to the question asked.
- Listing limitations of break-even without explaining why each one matters.
