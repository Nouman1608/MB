These questions cover **3.1.3 Production, costs and revenue** in AQA AS Economics (7135), specification version 1.3. They stay within the AS content, so no question needs marginal cost or marginal revenue. All firms and figures are invented. Revise first with the [3.1.3 study guide](/resources/aqa-as-level-economics-7135-production-costs-and-revenue/) and the [3.1.3 revision notes](/resources/aqa-as-level-economics-7135-production-costs-and-revenue-revision-notes/).

## Section A: short questions

**1.** Define labour productivity. **[1]**

**2.** A factory's 25 workers produce 3,000 units a week. After a training programme, 24 workers produce 3,120 units a week. Calculate the percentage change in labour productivity. **[2]**

**3.** Distinguish between the short run and the long run in production. **[2]**

**4.** Which one of the following is an external economy of scale for a furniture maker? **[1]**

A: A discount for buying timber in bulk

B: A local college that runs courses in carpentry for the industry

C: Hiring a specialist finance manager

D: Using a larger, more efficient cutting machine

**5.** A firm has total fixed costs of 2,400 and an average variable cost of 6 when it produces 400 units. Calculate its total cost and average total cost. **[2]**

**6.** Explain why specialisation requires an efficient means of exchanging goods and services. **[3]**

## Section B: structured questions

**7.** A small brewery's total costs for outputs of 0 to 5 thousand litres a week are 50, 90, 120, 160, 220 and 300 (thousand pounds).

**(a)** State the brewery's total fixed cost. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate average total cost at outputs of 3 and 5 thousand litres. **[2]**

**(c)** Calculate average variable cost at an output of 4 thousand litres. **[1]**

**8.** A firm can sell 10, 20, 30, 40 or 50 units a day at prices of 30, 27, 24, 20 and 16 respectively. Its total costs at these outputs are 250, 380, 480, 620 and 780.

**(a)** Calculate total revenue at each output. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate profit at each output and identify the most profitable output. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain why the firm's average revenue falls as its output rises. **[2]**

**9.** A sandwich firm divides production into separate tasks: slicing, filling, wrapping and labelling. Explain two benefits and one drawback of this division of labour. **[6]**

**10.** A drinks company compares three bottling plants.

| Plant | Output (bottles a day) | Total cost (pounds a day) |
|---|---|---|
| A | 10,000 | 80,000 |
| B | 40,000 | 240,000 |
| C | 100,000 | 700,000 |

**(a)** Calculate the average cost per bottle in each plant. **[3]**

**(b)** Explain what the figures suggest about economies and diseconomies of scale. **[3]**

**11.** Explain how the growth of a technology industry in one city could lower the costs of a small software firm located there. **[4]**

**12.** Two supermarket chains plan to merge into one larger firm. Evaluate whether the merger is likely to reduce their average costs. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** Output per worker (or per worker-hour) over a period of time [1]. *Total: 1.*

**2.** Before: 3,000 / 25 = 120 units per worker; after: 3,120 / 24 = 130 units per worker [1]. Change = 10 / 120 = **+8.3%** [1]. *Total: 2.*

**3.** In the short run at least one factor of production, such as factory space, is fixed [1]. In the long run all factors can be varied, so the firm can change its scale of production [1]. *Total: 2.*

**4.** **B** [1]. The college benefits every firm in the industry and comes from the industry's growth. A, C and D are internal economies. *Total: 1.*

**5.** TVC = 6 x 400 = 2,400, so TC = 2,400 + 2,400 = **4,800** [1]. ATC = 4,800 / 400 = **12** [1]. *Total: 2.*

**6.** A specialist produces much more of one good than they need and none of the other goods they want [1]. They must trade, but barter needs a double coincidence of wants, which is slow and often impossible [1]. Money, as a generally accepted medium of exchange, lets the specialist sell to anyone and buy from anyone, so the gains from specialisation are not lost [1]. *Total: 3.*

**7. (a)** TFC = total cost at zero output = **50** [1].

**(b)** At 3: 160 / 3 = **53.33** [1]. At 5: 300 / 5 = **60** [1].

**(c)** TVC at 4 = 220 - 50 = 170, so AVC = 170 / 4 = **42.5** [1]. *Total: 4.*

**8. (a)** TR = 300, 540, 720, 800, 800 [2].

**(b)** Profit = 50, 160, 240, 180, 20 [1]. The most profitable output is **30 units**, with profit of 240 [1].

**(c)** AR equals price, and AR is the firm's demand curve [1]. To sell more units the firm must cut its price, so AR falls as output rises [1]. *Total: 6.*

**9.** Benefit 1: workers repeat one task, so they become faster and make fewer mistakes [1], raising output per worker and lowering labour cost per sandwich [1]. Benefit 2: no time is lost moving between tasks or changing equipment [1], so more sandwiches are made in each shift [1]. Drawback: repetitive work can bore staff and lower motivation [1], and if the slicing stage stops, every later stage stops too [1]. *Total: 6.*

**10. (a)** A: 80,000 / 10,000 = **8.00** [1]; B: 240,000 / 40,000 = **6.00** [1]; C: 700,000 / 100,000 = **7.00** [1].

**(b)** Average cost falls from 8 to 6 as output rises from A to B, suggesting economies of scale, for example larger, faster bottling lines [1]. It rises from 6 to 7 at C, suggesting diseconomies of scale such as coordination or communication problems in a very large plant [1]. The pattern fits a long-run average cost curve that falls and then rises, although three points cannot show exactly where costs are lowest [1]. *Total: 6.*

**11.** As the industry grows, a pool of programmers with relevant skills builds up locally [1], so the small firm spends less on recruiting and training [1]. Specialist suppliers, such as data-centre and cyber-security services, set up nearby, lowering the cost of buying these services [1]. These are external economies of scale, so the small firm's average cost falls even though it has not grown [1]. *Total: 4.*

**12.** Purchasing economies: the merged firm buys more stock, so it can negotiate larger discounts from suppliers [1], lowering the cost of each item sold [1]. Other internal economies: one head office instead of two, shared distribution centres and lorries running fuller, and lower borrowing costs [1]. Marketing costs are spread over more sales [1]. However, diseconomies of scale may follow: merging two management structures and two company cultures can cause communication and coordination problems [1], and staff who fear job losses may become less motivated [1]. Savings also depend on whether the stores overlap; if the chains operate in different regions, fewer depots can be shared [1]. Judgement: average costs are likely to fall, mainly through purchasing economies, provided the firm is not already large enough for diseconomies to outweigh them [1]. *Total: 8.*

## Where marks are usually lost

- Comparing total cost between plants instead of average cost.
- Forgetting that total fixed cost is the cost at zero output.
- Choosing the output with the highest revenue as the most profitable.
- Naming a type of economy of scale without explaining why size cuts unit cost.
- Calling a benefit from the industry's growth an internal economy.
