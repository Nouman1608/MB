This guide goes deeper into **section 10.2 Analysis of published accounts** of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Business 9609 syllabus for examination in 2026, 2027 and 2028. It is A Level content, examined through case studies and essays.

The [financial statements and analysis of published accounts guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-business-financial-statements-and-analysis-of-published-accounts/) shows how to calculate each ratio once. This page concentrates on the harder skill: **interpretation**. It follows one company over two years, explains what each change means and why it happened, and shows how improving one ratio can worsen another. Afterwards, use the [analysis of published accounts revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-business-9609-analysis-of-published-accounts-cambridge-alevel-business-revision-notes/) and the [analysis of published accounts practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-business-9609-analysis-of-published-accounts-cambridge-alevel-business-practice/).

All businesses are invented. Figures are in $000 unless stated.

## What the syllabus asks for

For each of five groups, the syllabus asks for the **meaning and importance**, **calculation and interpretation** of named ratios, and **methods of improving** them:

| Group | Ratios named |
|---|---|
| 10.2.1 Liquidity | Current ratio; acid test ratio |
| 10.2.2 Profitability | Return on capital employed; gross profit margin; profit margin |
| 10.2.3 Financial efficiency | Rate of inventory turnover; trade receivables turnover (days); trade payables turnover (days) |
| 10.2.4 Gearing | Gearing ratio |
| 10.2.5 Investment | Dividend yield; dividend cover; price/earnings ratio |

The formulas are those printed in the syllabus. Capital employed = issued shares + reserves + non-current liabilities.

## A five-step method for interpretation

1. **Calculate** accurately, with the right unit (ratio, %, times, days).
2. **Compare**: with last year, with a competitor, or with a target. A ratio alone means little.
3. **Explain the cause** using evidence from the case.
4. **State the consequence** for the business or a stakeholder.
5. **Recommend or judge**, if the question asks.

Steps 3 and 4 are where most marks for analysis are won or lost.

## The data: Dunmore Electrical plc

Dunmore sells electrical goods to trade customers on credit. In Year 2 it opened a second warehouse, financed by a new $1,000,000 loan.

| | Year 1 | Year 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue (all on credit) | 8,000 | 9,000 |
| Cost of sales | 5,200 | 6,120 |
| Gross profit | 2,800 | 2,880 |
| Profit from operations | 960 | 900 |
| Profit for the year | 720 | 675 |
| Dividends | 240 | 300 |
| Average inventory | 800 | 1,020 |
| Closing inventory | 900 | 1,140 |
| Trade receivables | 800 | 1,110 |
| Cash | 200 | 30 |
| Current liabilities | 1,000 | 1,200 |
| of which trade payables | 700 | 900 |
| Credit purchases | 5,300 | 6,360 |
| Non-current liabilities | 1,000 | 2,000 |
| Capital employed | 5,000 | 6,375 |
| Shares in issue (thousands) | 4,000 | 4,000 |
| Market price per share | $3.60 | $3.00 |

## The ratios

| Ratio | Year 1 | Year 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Current ratio | 1,900 ÷ 1,000 = 1.9 : 1 | 2,280 ÷ 1,200 = 1.9 : 1 |
| Acid test ratio | 1,000 ÷ 1,000 = 1.0 : 1 | 1,140 ÷ 1,200 = 0.95 : 1 |
| Gross profit margin | 35% | 32% |
| Profit margin | 12% | 10% |
| ROCE | 960 ÷ 5,000 = 19.2% | 900 ÷ 6,375 = 14.1% |
| Inventory turnover | 5,200 ÷ 800 = 6.5 times | 6,120 ÷ 1,020 = 6.0 times |
| Trade receivables days | 36.5 days | 45.0 days |
| Trade payables days | 48.2 days | 51.7 days |
| Gearing | 1,000 ÷ 5,000 = 20% | 2,000 ÷ 6,375 = 31.4% |
| Dividend yield | 0.06 ÷ 3.60 = 1.7% | 0.075 ÷ 3.00 = 2.5% |
| Dividend cover | 720 ÷ 240 = 3.0 times | 675 ÷ 300 = 2.25 times |
| P/E ratio | 3.60 ÷ 0.18 = 20.0 | 3.00 ÷ 0.169 = 17.8 |

Current assets are inventory + receivables + cash: 900 + 800 + 200 = 1,900 and 1,140 + 1,110 + 30 = 2,280. Earnings per share = profit for the year ÷ shares: 720 ÷ 4,000 = $0.18 and 675 ÷ 4,000 = $0.169.

## Interpreting the results

**Liquidity: hidden weakness.** The current ratio is unchanged, which looks reassuring. But the make-up of current assets has worsened: cash fell from 200 to 30 while inventory and receivables rose. The acid test has slipped below 1 : 1. Dunmore now relies on collecting receivables and selling stock to pay its debts. Lesson: always look behind an unchanged ratio.

**Profitability: growth without profit.** Revenue rose 12.5%, yet profit from operations fell. The gross margin fell three points, suggesting Dunmore cut prices to win new customers or paid more to suppliers. The profit margin fell further because the new warehouse added expenses. ROCE fell sharply for two reasons: lower profit *and* higher capital employed from the new loan. The warehouse has not yet earned a return on the money invested in it; this may improve as it reaches full use.

**Efficiency: cash tied up.** Inventory turnover slowed from 6.5 to 6.0 times, consistent with stocking a second warehouse. Receivables days rose by 8.5 days: trade customers are taking longer to pay, possibly because Dunmore offered longer credit to win sales. Payables days rose slightly, so Dunmore is also taking longer to pay suppliers, which helps cash but could strain relationships.

**Gearing: more risk.** Gearing rose from 20% to 31.4% because of the loan. Interest costs will rise, and with profit from operations already falling, Dunmore's ability to cover interest is weaker.

**Investment: mixed signals.** Dunmore raised its dividend per share from 6 cents to 7.5 cents while profit fell, so dividend cover fell to 2.25 times. The dividend yield rose to 2.5%, partly because the share price fell. The lower P/E ratio suggests investors are less optimistic about future earnings.

**Overall picture.** Dunmore has grown its sales but at the expense of margins, liquidity and financial risk. Raising the dividend while cash fell to 30 is hard to justify.

## Methods of improving, and their side-effects

Improving one ratio often worsens another. Examiners reward answers that see this.

| Method | Improves | May worsen |
|---|---|---|
| Sell excess inventory at a discount | Acid test, inventory turnover, cash | Gross profit margin |
| Tighten credit terms for customers | Receivables days, cash | Revenue, if customers go elsewhere |
| Delay payments to suppliers | Cash, liquidity | Supplier goodwill; discounts lost |
| Raise prices | Gross and profit margins | Sales volume, if demand is price elastic |
| Issue shares to repay loans | Gearing | EPS, because profit is shared over more shares; owners' control diluted |
| Cut the dividend | Cash; dividend cover | Dividend yield; possibly share price |
| Sell unused non-current assets | ROCE, cash | Future capacity |

**Recommendation for Dunmore.** Its priority is liquidity: chase overdue receivables and review credit terms, since 45 days is costly when cash is 30. Holding the dividend at Year 1's level would conserve cash. Margins should be monitored as the second warehouse fills, since the investment's return cannot yet be judged on one year.

## Annotated model answer

*Analyse the changes in Dunmore's liquidity between Year 1 and Year 2.*

> "Although the current ratio stayed at 1.9 : 1, the acid test fell from 1.0 to 0.95 : 1, showing that without selling inventory Dunmore has only 95 cents of liquid assets for each dollar of short-term debt. The cause is that cash fell from $200,000 to $30,000 while inventory rose by $240,000 to stock the new warehouse, and receivables rose because customers now take 45 days to pay rather than 36.5. As a result Dunmore could struggle to pay suppliers on time, which may damage relationships or force it into an expensive overdraft."

It compares two years, uses both ratios, explains the cause with figures and states a consequence.

## Common mistakes

- Using revenue instead of cost of sales for inventory turnover.
- Using closing inventory when average inventory is given.
- Quoting a ratio without a unit, or giving the current ratio as a percentage.
- Stating "ROCE fell" without explaining whether profit fell, capital rose, or both.
- Recommending a method of improvement without its side-effect.

## Exam technique

**Calculate**: show the formula, the figures and the unit. **Analyse**: compare, explain the cause, give the consequence. **Evaluate** or **assess** performance: weigh improving ratios against worsening ones, decide which matter most for *this* business and its stakeholders, and note that one or two years of data may not show a trend.
