## Section A: knowledge

**1.** Give two features of the Religious Settlement of 1559. **[2]**

**2.** Name two Catholic plots against Elizabeth. **[2]**

**3.** Give two ways in which Elizabeth's government responded to the Catholic missionaries. **[2]**

## Section B: interpretations, explanation and accounts

**Interpretation A** *(invented)* -- From a popular history of Tudor England written in 2010:
"English Catholics were never a serious danger to Elizabeth. Most went quietly to church, paid their fines and prayed for the queen. The plots were the work of a handful of foreign agents and hotheads, and Walsingham's spies always knew about them in advance."

**4.** How convincing is Interpretation A about the threat posed by Catholics to Elizabeth? Explain your answer using Interpretation A and your contextual knowledge. **[8]**

**Interpretation B** *(invented)* -- From a biography of Elizabeth I written in 1998:
"For nineteen years Elizabeth protected the woman who wanted her throne. Parliament begged her to act, her councillors despaired, and plot followed plot. Only when Mary's own letters proved her guilt did the queen, with deep reluctance, allow the law to take its course."

**5.** How convincing is Interpretation B about Elizabeth's treatment of Mary Queen of Scots? Explain your answer using Interpretation B and your contextual knowledge. **[8]**

**6.** Explain what was important about the Northern Rebellion of 1569. **[8]**

**7.** Explain what was important about the papal bull of 1570 that excommunicated Elizabeth. **[8]**

**8.** Explain why Elizabeth and her government saw the Puritans as a threat. **[8]**

**9.** Write an account of the ways in which the Catholic missionaries challenged Elizabeth's religious settlement. **[8]**

**10.** Write an account of how the Babington Plot led to the execution of Mary Queen of Scots. **[8]**

**11.** Write an account of how Elizabeth and her government dealt with the Puritans after 1570. **[8]**

## Answers

**How these guides work.** Interpretation questions: the interpretation's argument identified [2]; knowledge that supports it [3]; knowledge that challenges or qualifies it, leading to a judgement on how convincing it is [3]. "Explain" questions: two aspects, each with a point [1], precise evidence [1] and explanation [2]. Accounts: an accurate sequence [3], causal links [3], a clear outcome [2].

**1.** Any two, [1] each: the Act of Supremacy made Elizabeth Supreme Governor of the Church; the Act of Uniformity made church attendance compulsory; the Book of Common Prayer was used in every church; fines of twelve pence for each absence; vestments and ornaments kept to reassure Catholics.

**2.** Any two, [1] each: the Ridolfi Plot (1571); the Throckmorton Plot (1583); the Babington Plot (1586).

**3.** Any two, [1] each: the 1581 Act raised the recusancy fine to £20 a month and made converting someone to Catholicism treason; the 1585 Act ordered Jesuits and seminary priests to leave on pain of treason; priests such as Campion were hunted down and executed; Walsingham's spies watched Catholic households.

**4.** Argument [2]: A claims Catholics were never a serious danger: most conformed and the plots were small and always known in advance. Support [3]: most Catholics did conform outwardly or pay fines, and many stayed loyal during the Armada crisis of 1588; Walsingham's network did intercept the Babington letters and uncover Throckmorton. Challenge and judgement [3]: the Northern Rebellion of 1569 was led by great English nobles, not foreign agents, and needed an army to suppress it; excommunication in 1570 told Catholics they need not obey Elizabeth; the Ridolfi and Throckmorton plots involved Spanish or French invasion plans; Parliament and the Council clearly saw the threat as serious, passing the Bond of Association and the 1585 Act. A is only partly convincing: it is right about most ordinary Catholics, but underestimates the danger from noble rebels, the papacy and foreign powers, especially while Mary was alive.

**5.** Argument [2]: B claims Elizabeth protected Mary for nineteen years despite pressure from Parliament and the Council, and only reluctantly allowed her execution when Mary's guilt was proved. Support [3]: Mary was held from 1568 to 1587; Parliament demanded her execution after the Ridolfi Plot (1571-72), but Elizabeth refused; Mary's letters in the Babington Plot proved her approval; Elizabeth hesitated for months before signing the warrant and later punished Davison. Challenge and judgement [3]: "protected" overstates Elizabeth's motives: she kept Mary in custody because freeing, exiling or executing her all carried risks, not only out of kindness; Walsingham's agents deliberately let the Babington Plot develop to trap Mary, so "the law taking its course" was partly engineered. B is convincing about Elizabeth's reluctance but less convincing about her reasons.

**6.** Aspect one [4]: it showed Mary's danger as a focus for rebellion [1]; the rebels aimed to free Mary and link her to the Duke of Norfolk [1]; so Elizabeth saw that keeping Mary in England invited conspiracy, which shaped her treatment of Mary for the next eighteen years [2]. Aspect two [4]: it showed the strength of Catholic feeling in the north [1]; the earls celebrated Mass in Durham Cathedral and drew support from northern Catholics [1]; so after its collapse and the execution of hundreds of rebels, the government strengthened royal control in the north and treated Catholicism increasingly as a matter of loyalty [2].

**7.** Aspect one [4]: it forced Catholics to choose [1]; *Regnans in Excelsis* excommunicated Elizabeth and released Catholics from obedience to her [1]; so loyal Catholics now appeared to the government as potential traitors, and the period of relative tolerance in the 1560s ended [2]. Aspect two [4]: it led to harsher laws and plots [1]; the 1571 Treason Act made it treason to deny Elizabeth was the lawful queen, and the Ridolfi Plot followed in the same year [1]; so the bull encouraged plotters and justified tougher measures, leading to the laws of 1581 and 1585 [2].

**8.** Reason one [4]: they challenged her authority as Supreme Governor [1]; Puritans wanted to change the 1559 Settlement, for example by removing vestments, and some, like Thomas Cartwright, wanted to abolish bishops [1]; since bishops were appointed by the Crown, this threatened Elizabeth's control of the church and, she believed, of the state [2]. Reason two [4]: they organised independently [1]; prophesyings spread Puritan preaching, the Marprelate Tracts (1588-89) mocked the bishops, and separatists wanted to leave the church altogether [1]; so the government feared division and disorder, leading to Grindal's suspension (1577), Whitgift's enforcement of conformity and the executions of 1593 [2].

**9.** Sequence [3]: Allen founded the college at Douai in 1568; seminary priests arrived from 1574; Jesuits, including Campion and Parsons, arrived in 1580; they moved secretly between Catholic houses, hiding in priest holes. Causal links [3]: because the excommunication had told Catholics they need not obey Elizabeth, the government saw the priests as agents of a hostile pope; because the priests kept Catholicism alive and stopped Catholics drifting into conformity, they threatened the gradual success of the settlement; this led to the 1581 Act and Campion's execution, and then the 1585 Act. Outcome [2]: more than a hundred priests were executed during the reign, and Catholicism survived mainly in gentry households rather than as a mass movement.

**10.** Sequence [3]: Anthony Babington planned to kill Elizabeth, free Mary and support a foreign invasion; Walsingham's double agent Gilbert Gifford arranged for letters to be smuggled in beer barrels to Mary at Chartley; Mary's reply approving the plot was intercepted; Babington was arrested and executed; Mary was tried at Fotheringhay in October 1586. Causal links [3]: because Walsingham read every letter, he could wait until Mary committed herself in writing; because the Bond of Association (1584) and the 1585 Act allowed a trial of anyone on whose behalf a plot was made, her approval made a trial possible; the guilty verdict led Parliament to press for execution. Outcome [2]: Elizabeth signed the warrant reluctantly, Mary was executed on 8 February 1587, and Elizabeth blamed Davison for sending it.

**11.** Sequence [3]: Puritan prophesyings spread in the 1570s; Archbishop Grindal refused to suppress them and was suspended in 1577; Whitgift became Archbishop in 1583 and enforced conformity; the Marprelate Tracts appeared in 1588-89; an Act against separatists was passed in 1593, and Barrow and Greenwood were executed. Causal links [3]: because Elizabeth saw the prophesyings as a threat to her control of the church, she overruled her own archbishop; Grindal's suspension allowed a tougher policy under Whitgift; the Marprelate attacks on bishops provoked a crackdown that weakened the Puritan movement. Outcome [2]: by the 1590s the organised Puritan challenge had been contained, though Puritan ideas survived into the next reign.
