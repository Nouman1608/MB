## The unit in one line

A text's meaning depends partly on **other texts**: the ones it borrows from, the conventions of
its type or form, and the texts you place it beside.

The brief's wording to remember: assessment objective 1 includes knowing "features of particular
text types and literary forms"; assessment objective 2 includes analysing "relationships among
different texts".

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning | Quick test |
|---|---|---|
| Intertext | An earlier text that a new text relies on for part of its meaning | Would the text lose meaning if you did not know the source? |
| Allusion | A brief, indirect reference the reader should recognise | Is the source hinted at, not named or quoted? |
| Quotation | The source's exact words, usually marked | Are the words borrowed unchanged? |
| Parody | Imitation that exaggerates a style or form to mock it | Is there a target being criticised? |
| Pastiche | Imitation of a style without mockery, often as tribute or play | Is it imitation that admires? |
| Satire | Using humour, irony or exaggeration to criticise people, ideas or institutions | What is being attacked? |
| Adaptation | A text moved into a new medium or form | Has the medium or form changed? |
| Rewriting / response | A text that answers or retells another, often from a new point of view | Whose voice is added? |
| Convention | A feature readers expect of a given text type or form | Would its absence surprise a reader? |
| Text type | A kind of non-literary text: advert, speech, report, letter, blog, infographic | |
| Literary form | A kind of literary text: poetry, prose fiction, drama; sub-forms such as the sonnet | |
| Hybrid text | A text that mixes the conventions of two or more types or forms | Which two forms can you see? |
| Subversion | Using a convention against its usual purpose | What expectation is set up and then undercut? |

Parody and satire overlap but are not the same. Parody always imitates a particular style or form.
Satire attacks a target and may or may not imitate anything.

## Method in steps

### Analysing an intertextual reference

1. **Name** the source, or the kind of source ("the language of fairy tales") if you are not sure.
2. **Kept:** what is borrowed unchanged -- words, structure, form, tone?
3. **Changed:** what is new -- the context, the speaker, a reversed detail?
4. **Effect:** what the gap between the two does for meaning and for the reader.

### Analysing conventions

1. List the conventions a reader expects of this type or form.
2. Decide whether each is **followed, bent or broken**.
3. Choose the most significant bent or broken one.
4. Explain why the writer made that choice, linked to purpose and audience.

### Building a comparison

1. Find a **point of connection**: a shared issue, theme, technique or form.
2. For each text, find one precise piece of evidence on that point.
3. Write a sentence that holds both: *"Both ... but ..."* or *"Where Text A ..., Text B ..."*.
4. Explain **why** they differ: purpose, audience, form, context.
5. Repeat for two or three points, then state what the comparison reveals overall.

## Worked reminders

**Borrowing a form to mock it.** Jonathan Swift's *A Modest Proposal* (1729) is written in the calm,
reasonable form of a pamphlet offering a solution to a public problem. The proposal it makes is
monstrous. The polite, statistical form makes the cruelty of the argument stand out, so the target
is the cold way the Irish poor were discussed, not the reader.

**Parodying a genre.** Jane Austen's *Northanger Abbey* (1817) plays with the conventions of the
Gothic novel. Its heroine expects mysteries like those in the novels she reads, and ordinary life
keeps failing to supply them. The joke only works if the reader knows the genre.

**A modern allusion, in a headline.** *Original text written for Marlbridge:* "Council's new car
park: a monument to wasted millions". "Monument" borrows the language of grand, lasting buildings,
so the headline mocks the project by giving it a dignity it does not deserve.

**A literary form hosting a non-literary one.** *Original text written for Marlbridge:* a poem
titled "Terms and Conditions" in which each numbered clause is a promise between two friends. The
legal form (numbered clauses, "shall", "hereby") is cold; the content is personal. The clash
suggests that the friendship has become something to be managed rather than enjoyed.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Allusion vs quotation.** An allusion points; a quotation copies. Both need an effect.
- **Parody vs pastiche.** Parody mocks its source; pastiche imitates without mockery.
- **Text type vs literary form.** Text types are non-literary kinds of text; literary forms are
  kinds of literary text. A hybrid can mix the two.
- **Following vs subverting a convention.** Following keeps attention on the message; subverting
  pulls attention to the form itself.
- **Comparison vs contrast.** Comparison covers similarities *and* differences. A comparison built
  only of contrasts misses the point of connection that made the pairing worth studying.
- **Woven vs block comparison.** Woven: both texts in each paragraph. Block: one text, then the
  other. Woven comparison shows the connection; block comparison leaves it to the reader.
- **Influence vs intertextuality.** You do not need to prove a writer read a source. It is enough
  to show what a reader gains from making the connection -- but do not invent a source that is not
  there.

## Quick self-test

1. Define *intertext* in one sentence.
2. What is the difference between parody and pastiche?
3. Name three conventions of a news report.
4. A charity leaflet is laid out as a school report card. What is this kind of text called?
5. Name the four steps for analysing an intertextual reference.
6. What are the three things a text can do with a convention?
7. What must you find before you can compare two texts?
8. Write a comparison sentence frame that keeps both texts in one sentence.
9. How long is Paper 2 at HL, and what weighting does it carry at HL?
10. In the individual oral, what connects the literary work and the non-literary body of work?
11. Why is "both texts are about people" a weak point of connection?

### Answers

1. An earlier text that a new text depends on for part of its meaning.
2. Parody imitates in order to mock; pastiche imitates without mocking, often as tribute.
3. Any three of: headline; lead paragraph answering who/what/where/when; quotations from sources;
   third-person, factual register.
4. A hybrid text (a non-literary text borrowing the conventions of another text type).
5. Name, kept, changed, effect.
6. Follow it, bend it, break it.
7. A point of connection: a shared issue, theme, technique or form.
8. "Both texts ..., but Text A ... while Text B ..." (any frame that places evidence from both
   texts in one sentence).
9. 1.75 hours; 25% at HL (35% at SL).
10. A common global issue that both have approached.
11. It is too broad to argue from; a precise connection (an issue, technique or form) lets you
    compare specific choices.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Naming an allusion or parody without explaining what the borrowing adds to meaning.
- Inventing a precise source when only a general style is present. Say "fairy-tale conventions"
  if that is all you can support.
- Listing the conventions of a text type as a checklist instead of analysing the one that is bent
  or broken.
- Calling a text a parody when nothing is being mocked.
- Describing Text A, then Text B, and leaving the comparison to a single closing sentence.
- Choosing a point of connection so broad that no specific evidence can support it.
- Treating the non-literary text as simpler than the literary one; both are built from deliberate
  choices.
- Explaining *how* texts differ but not *why*: the difference in purpose, audience or form.
- Quoting a long passage when a short embedded phrase would carry the point.
