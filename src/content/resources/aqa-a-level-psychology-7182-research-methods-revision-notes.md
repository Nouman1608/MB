**Research methods in AQA A-level Psychology (7182) is about designing studies, controlling variables, analysing data and judging the quality of findings.** It is section 4.2.3 and topic 7 of the AQA AS and A-level Psychology (7181, 7182) specification, Version 1.4 (1 September 2025). It is listed under Paper 2: Psychology in Context (2 hours, 96 marks, 33.3% of the A-level), but research methods and maths skills are assessed on all three papers. These notes are built for fast recall and application to novel scenarios.

For full explanations read the [Research methods study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-psychology-7182-research-methods/), then test yourself with the [Research methods practice questions](/resources/aqa-a-level-psychology-7182-research-methods-practice/).

## What the specification lists

1. **Methods**: lab, field, natural and quasi-experiments; observational techniques; self-report (questionnaires, structured and unstructured interviews); correlations; content analysis; case studies.
2. **Scientific processes (4.2.3.1)**: aims and hypotheses; sampling; pilot studies; experimental designs; observational design; questionnaire and interview design; variables; control; demand characteristics and investigator effects; ethics and the BPS code; peer review; implications for the economy; reliability; validity; features of science; reporting investigations.
3. **Data handling and analysis (4.2.3.2)**: quantitative and qualitative; primary, secondary and meta-analysis; descriptive statistics and calculations; graphs and tables; distributions; correlation coefficients; levels of measurement; coding and thematic analysis.
4. **Inferential testing (4.2.3.3)**: the sign test; probability, significance and critical values; Type I and Type II errors; choosing between the named tests.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Aim | General statement of what is being investigated |
| Directional hypothesis | Predicts the direction of a difference or relationship |
| Non-directional hypothesis | Predicts a difference or relationship without direction |
| Null hypothesis | Predicts no difference or relationship |
| Operationalisation | Defining a variable in measurable terms |
| Extraneous variable | Any variable other than the IV that might affect the DV |
| Confounding variable | An extraneous variable that has affected the DV systematically |
| Counterbalancing | Varying condition order (e.g. ABBA) to balance order effects |
| Random allocation | Assigning participants to conditions by chance |
| Standardisation | Same procedures and instructions for every participant |
| Demand characteristics | Cues that reveal the aim and change participants' behaviour |
| Investigator effects | Researcher's influence on the result |
| Pilot study | Small trial run to test the procedure |
| Event sampling | Recording each occurrence of a target behaviour |
| Time sampling | Recording behaviour at fixed intervals |
| Meta-analysis | Statistical combination of many studies' findings |
| Peer review | Expert scrutiny before publication |
| Falsifiability | A theory can in principle be proved wrong |
| Paradigm | A set of shared assumptions within a science |

## Designs compared

| Design | Strength | Weakness | Fix |
|---|---|---|---|
| Repeated measures | No participant variables between conditions; fewer participants | Order effects (practice, fatigue); may guess the aim | Counterbalancing |
| Independent groups | No order effects | Participant variables; more participants | Random allocation |
| Matched pairs | Controls key participant variables; no order effects | Matching is time-consuming and never perfect | Pilot matching variable |

## Sampling compared

| Technique | Representative? | Practical? |
|---|---|---|
| Random | Potentially; free of researcher bias | Needs full population list |
| Systematic | Fairly, if the list has no pattern | Simple once list exists |
| Stratified | Most representative of sub-groups | Time-consuming |
| Opportunity | Usually not | Quick and easy |
| Volunteer | Volunteer bias | Easy; reaches many |

## Reliability and validity at a glance

| Concept | Assess by | Improve by |
|---|---|---|
| Test–retest reliability | Same test, same people, two occasions; correlate | Clearer, unambiguous questions |
| Inter-observer reliability | Two observers; correlate tallies (+0.80 or above) | Operationalised categories; training |
| Face validity | Does it look right to an expert? | Revise items |
| Concurrent validity | Compare with an established measure | Use a validated measure |
| Ecological validity | Do findings apply to real settings? | More natural tasks and settings |
| Temporal validity | Do findings still hold today? | Replicate in a new era |

## Descriptive statistics

| Measure | Use when | Weakness |
|---|---|---|
| Mean | Interval data, no extreme scores | Distorted by outliers |
| Median | Ordinal data or outliers present | Ignores exact values |
| Mode | Nominal data | May be several or none |
| Range | Quick check of spread | Distorted by extremes |
| Standard deviation | Interval data; precise spread | Harder to calculate |

**Skew rule.** Positive skew: mode < median < mean (tail to the right). Negative skew: mean < median < mode (tail to the left). Normal: all three equal.

## Choosing an inferential test

Ask: difference or correlation? Related or unrelated design? Nominal, ordinal or interval data?

| | Unrelated | Related | Correlation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nominal | Chi-squared | Sign test | Chi-squared |
| Ordinal | Mann-Whitney | Wilcoxon | Spearman's rho |
| Interval | Unrelated t-test | Related t-test | Pearson's r |

**"Less than" tests**: for the sign test, Wilcoxon and Mann-Whitney, the calculated value must be **equal to or less than** the critical value to be significant. For the others, it must be equal to or greater.

## Worked example: the sign test

Ten students rated their mood before and after a mindfulness session. Eight improved (+), one got worse (-) and one did not change (0).

1. Ignore the zero: N = 9.
2. Count the less frequent sign: S = 1.
3. For a directional hypothesis at p ≤ 0.05 with N = 9, the critical value of S is 1.
4. S (1) is equal to or less than the critical value (1), so the result is **significant**; reject the null hypothesis.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Lab vs field experiment**: setting artificial vs natural; both manipulate the IV.
- **Natural vs quasi-experiment**: IV varies naturally (event) vs IV is a participant characteristic.
- **Covert vs overt**: hidden vs known observation.
- **Event vs time sampling**: every occurrence vs fixed intervals.
- **Coding vs thematic analysis**: produces numbers vs keeps qualitative themes.
- **Type I vs Type II error**: false positive (too lenient) vs false negative (too strict).

## Quick self-test

1. Which design uses counterbalancing?
2. What is the difference between an extraneous and a confounding variable?
3. Name the five sections of a scientific report after the abstract.
4. Which measure of central tendency suits nominal data?
5. In a negative skew, which average is lowest?
6. Which test for an independent-groups design with ordinal data?
7. What is a Type I error?
8. What correlation coefficient shows a strong negative relationship: +0.2, -0.85 or -0.1?

**Answers:** (1) repeated measures; (2) an extraneous variable might affect the DV; a confounding variable has done so systematically; (3) introduction, method, results, discussion, referencing; (4) mode; (5) mean; (6) Mann-Whitney; (7) rejecting the null hypothesis when it is true (false positive); (8) -0.85.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Hypotheses missing the operationalised IV and DV.
- Explaining a sampling technique without applying it to the scenario's population.
- Saying "results are significant" without comparing calculated and critical values, or using the wrong direction rule.
- Confusing qualitative data with primary data.
- Writing "lacks validity" without saying which type and why.
