## Questions

**1.** Define the national income multiplier. **[2]**

**2.** In Country T the marginal propensities to save, to be taxed and to import are 0.08, 0.22 and 0.20.

**(a)** Calculate the value of the multiplier. **[2]**
**(b)** Investment rises by 35. Calculate the change in national income. **[1]**
**(c)** The propensity to import rises to 0.30, other things equal. Calculate the new multiplier. **[2]**

**3.** Country R's real GDP growth was +4.1% in year 1, +1.2% in year 2, -1.8% in year 3 and +0.6% in year 4. Identify the most likely stage of the economic cycle in each year. **[4]**

**4.** Country S's real GDP was 1,000 in year 0 and 1,159.3 in year 5.

**(a)** Calculate the average annual rate of growth. **[2]**
**(b)** Actual growth in year 6 is 1.2%. Compare this with your answer to (a) and state the effect on the output gap, assuming potential output grows at that average rate. **[2]**

**5.** Evaluate the view that investment in human capital is the most important factor determining a country's trend rate of growth. **[6]**

**6.** In Country W government spending rises by 15 and the marginal propensity to withdraw is 0.25. The table shows real GDP demanded and supplied at each price level.

| Price level | 100 | 105 | 110 | 115 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| AD before | 860 | 845 | 830 | 815 |
| AD after | 920 | 905 | 890 | 875 |
| SRAS | 860 | 875 | 890 | 905 |

**(a)** Calculate the multiplier. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the shift in AD and show that it matches the table. **[2]**
**(c)** State the new equilibrium and explain why real GDP rises by less than the AD shift. **[3]**

**7.** Country U's income (year 1 to year 6) is 300, 318, 342, 360, 366 and 364. The capital-output ratio is 3.

**(a)** Calculate net induced investment in years 2 to 6. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why investment falls in year 4 although income rises. **[2]**

**8.** Country V's potential output is 720 and actual output is 698.4.

**(a)** Work out the output gap as a percentage of potential output. **[2]**
**(b)** Describe how this is shown on a PPF/C diagram. **[2]**

**9.** Explain how the multiplier-accelerator model helps explain the economic cycle. **[6]**

**10.** Evaluate the causes of a negative output gap. **[8]**

**11.** Evaluate the consequences of a positive output gap for an economy. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** The eventual change in national income [1] divided by the injection (or change in autonomous AD) that started it [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Both halves of the ratio are needed: the eventual change and the starting injection. "Spending has a bigger effect" describes the idea without defining it.

**2. (a)** MPW = 0.08 + 0.22 + 0.20 = 0.50 [1]. k = 1 ÷ 0.50 = **2** [1].
**(b)** 35 × 2 = **70** [1].
**(c)** MPW = 0.08 + 0.22 + 0.30 = 0.60 [1]. k = 1 ÷ 0.60 = **1.67** [1].
*Examiner insight:* Show the addition of the three propensities. Using 1 ÷ (1 - MPC) here would ignore taxes and imports.

**3.** Year 1: boom or expansion, strong growth [1]. Year 2: downturn, growth positive but falling [1]. Year 3: recession, output falls [1]. Year 4: recovery, growth returns after the fall [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Give the stage and the evidence from the figures. "Most likely" invites a reason, since growth rates alone cannot fix the exact point.

**4. (a)** (1,159.3 ÷ 1,000)^(1/5) - 1 [1] = 1.03 - 1 = **3.0% a year** [1].
**(b)** Actual growth of 1.2% is below the 3.0% trend [1]. Output grows more slowly than potential, so a negative gap widens (or a positive gap narrows) [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the compound formula for an average over several years. Dividing total growth by 5 gives a different, less accurate figure.

**5.** Human capital raises productivity, which is the main source of growth in a mature economy [1]. Skilled workers can use new technology and adapt, which lifts output per head [1]. But workers need capital and technology to be productive, so human capital depends on other factors [1]. Institutions, infrastructure and investment may matter more in a poor economy with weak property rights [1]. Education takes years to pay off and skills may be lost through emigration [1]. Judgement: it is probably the most important in a developed economy, but its effect depends on investment and technology working with it [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Weigh one factor against the others for a stated type of economy. A list of every factor with no ranking stays at description.

**6. (a)** k = 1 ÷ 0.25 = **4** [1].
**(b)** Shift = 15 × 4 = **60** [1]. Each row of AD after is 60 above AD before [1].
**(c)** New equilibrium: price level **110**, real GDP **890** (from 100 and 860) [1]. Real GDP rises by 30, half of 60 [1]. SRAS slopes upward, so higher spending also raises the price level, which cuts the quantity demanded along the new AD curve [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Find equilibrium where AD equals SRAS in the table, and say why the gain is smaller than the shift. Quoting the multiplier as the rise in output would be wrong.

**7. (a)** Changes in income: 18, 24, 18, 6, -2 [1]. Investment: **54, 72, 54, 18, -6** [1].
**(b)** Accelerator investment depends on the change in income, not the level [1]. The change fell from 24 to 18, so investment fell from 72 to 54 even though income rose [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Refer to the figures. A general statement that investment depends on growth does not use the table.

**8. (a)** (698.4 - 720) ÷ 720 × 100 [1] = **-3.0%** [1].
**(b)** A point inside the PPF/C [1], as resources are unemployed or underused so less of both goods is produced than is possible [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Keep the sign. A negative gap means actual below potential, and the sign carries the meaning.

**9.** A rise in autonomous spending raises income through the multiplier [1]. Rising income raises investment through the accelerator [1]. This investment is an injection and raises income again, so the boom feeds itself [1]. Growth then slows as capacity or confidence limits are reached [1]. Investment falls even though income is still rising, so the downturn begins [1]. The multiplier works in reverse and a recession follows, with recovery when capital needs replacing [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Keep the links in order, each step causing the next. A list of terms with no chain stays at definition.

**10.** A fall in consumption, such as from lower confidence, reduces AD [1]. The multiplier then amplifies the initial fall [1]. A financial crisis or tight credit cuts spending and investment [1]. Falling exports, for example after a currency rise, cut AD [1]. Sticky wages and prices mean the economy adjusts slowly, so the gap persists [1]. Potential output is unobservable, so the measured gap may be wrongly estimated [1]. The cause matters: a temporary shock may close by itself, while a deep one needs policy [1]. Judgement: for most economies, a fall in private sector confidence and credit is the leading cause, magnified by the multiplier and slow wage adjustment [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Choose and defend the main cause. A list of causes, however accurate, earns less than a ranked, reasoned one.

**11.** Inflation rises as demand outruns capacity [1]. Labour shortages push up wages, adding to cost pressure [1]. Imports rise, worsening the trade balance [1]. Asset prices may form a bubble that later bursts [1]. There are benefits: low unemployment, high profits and rising tax revenue [1]. The effects depend on size and duration: a small, short gap does little harm [1]. Different groups are affected differently, such as savers and exporters [1]. Judgement: the costs usually outweigh the benefits if the gap persists, because inflation and instability follow [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Cover both sides, then judge. Costs only, with no benefit and no conditions, is one-sided.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using 1 ÷ (1 - MPC) when taxes and imports are in the question.
- Forgetting to multiply the multiplier by the injection.
- Treating the AD shift as the rise in real GDP.
- Mixing up the accelerator, which depends on the change in income, with the level of income.
- Dropping the minus sign on a negative gap.
- Listing causes or consequences without judging which matters most.
- Evaluation that says "it depends" without saying on what.
- Working from nominal rather than real figures.
