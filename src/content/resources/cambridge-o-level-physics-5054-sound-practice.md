## Section A

**1.** Describe how a vibrating loudspeaker cone produces a sound wave in the air in front of it. **[3]**

**2.** State the approximate range of frequencies audible to humans, and define ultrasound. **[2]**

**3.** Sound travels through steel, water and air. State the medium in which sound is

**(a)** fastest **[1]**
**(b)** slowest. **[1]**

**4.** A sound wave in water has a frequency of 3000 Hz and a wavelength of 0.50 m. Calculate the speed of sound in water. **[2]**

**5.** State what is meant by an echo, and give the type of surface that produces a strong echo. **[2]**

**6.** Two astronauts stand a few metres apart on the surface of the Moon, which has no atmosphere. Explain why one cannot hear a whistle blown by the other. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** An electric bell is placed inside a glass bell jar connected to a vacuum pump.

**(a)** Describe how the apparatus is used to show that sound cannot travel through a vacuum, and what is observed. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain why the bell is mounted on soft foam rather than fixed directly to the base. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain why the bell can still be seen working. **[1]**

**8.** A microphone connected to an oscilloscope picks up a steady note from a tuning fork. The oscilloscope settings are not changed.

**(a)** The note is made louder at the same pitch. Describe the change in the trace. **[1]**
**(b)** A tuning fork of higher pitch is sounded at the same loudness. Describe the change in the trace. **[1]**
**(c)** A recorder plays the same note as the first tuning fork, equally loudly. Describe how its trace compares, and name the property of sound responsible. **[2]**
**(d)** The first trace shows 4 complete waves in a time of 0.010 s. Calculate the frequency of the note. **[2]**

**9.** Two students stand 680 m apart. One bangs two wooden blocks together above her head. The other starts a stopwatch when he sees the blocks meet and stops it when he hears the sound. Four readings are 2.1 s, 1.9 s, 2.0 s and 2.0 s.

**(a)** Explain why the stopwatch is started when the blocks are seen rather than heard. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the mean time and the speed of sound. **[3]**
**(c)** Suggest **two** ways of making the result more reliable. **[2]**

**10.** A student stands 66 m from a tall wall and claps so that each clap coincides with the echo of the previous one. A friend times 30 intervals as 12.0 s.

**(a)** Calculate the speed of sound in air. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain why the friend times 30 intervals rather than one. **[1]**

**11.** A ship uses sonar. The speed of sound in sea water is 1500 m/s.

**(a)** A pulse returns from the sea bed 0.36 s after it is sent. Calculate the depth of the sea bed. **[3]**
**(b)** A shoal of fish passes 90 m below the ship. Calculate the time for the echo from the shoal to return. **[2]**
**(c)** The sonar uses ultrasound of frequency 40 kHz. Calculate its wavelength in sea water. **[2]**

**12.** Ultrasound is used to scan an unborn baby.

**(a)** Explain how the ultrasound pulses are used to build up an image. **[3]**
**(b)** An echo from a boundary returns 6.0 × 10⁻⁵ s after the pulse is sent. The speed of ultrasound in tissue is 1500 m/s. Calculate the depth of the boundary. **[2]**
**(c)** Give **one** reason why ultrasound, rather than X-rays, is used for this scan. **[1]**

**13.** Describe how ultrasound is used to clean a delicate piece of jewellery. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** The cone **vibrates backwards and forwards** [1]. Moving forward, it **pushes air particles together to form a compression** [1]; moving back, it **leaves a region where particles are spread out, a rarefaction**, and the pattern travels outwards as a longitudinal wave [1].

**2.** About **20 Hz to 20 000 Hz** [1]. Ultrasound is **sound with a frequency higher than 20 kHz** [1].

**3. (a)** **Steel** [1].
**(b)** **Air** [1].

**4.** v = f λ = 3000 × 0.50 [1] = **1500 m/s** [1].

**5.** An echo is **sound reflected from a surface** [1]. A **hard, smooth (flat) surface** such as a wall or cliff gives a strong echo [1].

**6.** Sound **needs particles (a medium) to pass on its vibrations** [1]; with **no atmosphere there are no air particles** between them, so the sound cannot travel [1].

**7. (a)** Switch on the bell and **pump the air out of the jar** [1]; the **sound heard becomes fainter until it can hardly be heard** [1], while the **hammer is still seen striking the gong**; letting air back in makes the sound return [1].
**(b)** So that **vibrations cannot travel through the solid base** to the outside, which would let sound be heard even with no air in the jar [1].
**(c)** **Light can travel through a vacuum**, so the bell is still visible [1].

**8. (a)** The trace becomes **taller (greater amplitude)**, with the **same number of waves** across the screen [1].
**(b)** **More waves are shown across the screen** (waves closer together), with the same height [1].
**(c)** Same height and same number of waves, but a **different shape of waveform** [1]; the property is **timbre (quality)** [1].
**(d)** f = number of waves ÷ time = 4 ÷ 0.010 [1] = **400 Hz** [1].

**9. (a)** Light travels so fast that it **arrives almost instantly**, so the time measured is the **time for the sound alone** to travel 680 m [1].
**(b)** Mean time = (2.1 + 1.9 + 2.0 + 2.0) ÷ 4 = **2.0 s** [1]; v = d ÷ t = 680 ÷ 2.0 [1] = **340 m/s** [1].
**(c)** Any two, 1 mark each: **take more readings and average**; **swap positions and repeat** to reduce the effect of wind; **use a greater distance** so reaction time is a smaller fraction of the time; use **several timers** and average their readings.

**10. (a)** Time per interval = 12.0 ÷ 30 = 0.40 s [1]; distance there and back = 2 × 66 = 132 m [1]; v = 132 ÷ 0.40 = **330 m/s** [1].
**(b)** The **reaction-time error is spread over 30 intervals**, so the error in each interval is much smaller [1].

**11. (a)** Distance travelled = 1500 × 0.36 = 540 m [1]; this is **there and back** [1], so depth = 540 ÷ 2 = **270 m** [1].
**(b)** t = 2d ÷ v = 2 × 90 ÷ 1500 [1] = **0.12 s** [1].
**(c)** λ = v ÷ f = 1500 ÷ 40 000 [1] = **0.0375 m** (3.75 cm) [1].

**12. (a)** A probe sends **pulses of ultrasound into the body** [1]. At each **boundary between different tissues, part of the pulse is reflected** back to the probe [1]. The **time for each echo gives the depth of the boundary**, and a computer uses the echoes to build the image [1].
**(b)** d = v × t ÷ 2 = 1500 × 6.0 × 10⁻⁵ ÷ 2 [1] = **0.045 m** (4.5 cm) [1].
**(c)** Ultrasound is **not ionising**, so it does not harm the baby's cells (X-rays can cause cell mutation) [1].

**13.** The jewellery is placed in a **bath of liquid through which ultrasound is passed** [1]; the **vibrations shake the dirt loose** from the surfaces without the need to scrub or take the item apart [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Forgetting to halve the echo time, or forgetting to double the distance in the rhythm-clapping method.
- Saying air particles travel from the source to the listener instead of vibrating about fixed positions.
- Linking pitch to amplitude or loudness to frequency.
- Describing timbre as a change in frequency rather than in the shape of the waveform.
- Leaving out the observation that the bell is still seen working in the vacuum experiment.
- Writing 20 kHz as 20 Hz, or forgetting to convert kHz to Hz before using v = f λ.
