Topic 1 -- Foundations is deliberately the first thing every ESS student studies, and, as the
[full syllabus guide](/resources/ib-dp-environmental-systems-and-societies-syllabus-guide/) explains,
its three concepts are explicitly "revisited throughout the course" rather than assessed once and set
aside. These notes go deeper into the three sub-topics than the syllabus guide's overview, alongside
the [subject overview](/resources/ib-dp-environmental-systems-and-societies-subject-guide/) and
[assessment revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-environmental-systems-and-societies-assessment-revision-notes/)
already on the site.

## 1.1 Perspectives (3 hours)

Differing individual and collective worldviews on environmental issues. The syllabus's own key term
here is the **environmental value system (EVS)** -- a particular world view or paradigm that shapes
the way an individual or group perceives and evaluates environmental issues, influenced by cultural,
religious, economic and socio-political context. Revise the three broad EVS categories used across
the course: **ecocentrism** (nature-centred, valuing the environment for its own sake), **anthropocentrism**
(human-centred, managing the environment for humanity's benefit), and **technocentrism** (a belief
that technology and human ingenuity can solve environmental problems). A strong exam answer applies
an EVS category to a specific described scenario or stakeholder, rather than only defining the three
terms in the abstract.

## 1.2 Systems (5 hours)

Systems theory as a tool for understanding environmental structures and processes. Key vocabulary to
know precisely: a **system** is an assemblage of interrelated parts that interact in a coherent way;
**inputs** and **outputs** are energy or matter crossing the system boundary; a **feedback loop** is a
mechanism where a change in a system's output affects its own input. Distinguish **negative feedback**
(counteracts and stabilises a change, moving the system back toward equilibrium) from **positive
feedback** (amplifies a change, pushing the system further from its original state) -- confusing the
two is one of the most common ESS exam errors, since "negative" here means stabilising, not "bad".
Also revise the distinction between **open systems** (exchange both energy and matter with their
surroundings), **closed systems** (exchange only energy, not matter) and **isolated systems**
(exchange neither) -- most ecosystems are treated as open systems in this course.

## 1.3 Sustainability (8 hours)

Resource management and the idea of sustainability as central to the whole course -- the largest of
Topic 1's three sub-topics by teaching hours. Core vocabulary: **natural capital** (natural resources
that provide goods and services of value to humans), **natural income** (the yield obtainable from
natural capital without depleting it), and **sustainability** itself, generally framed as meeting
present needs without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own. Revise the
distinction between **renewable natural capital** (can be replenished, e.g. forests, if harvested
within its natural income) and **non-renewable natural capital** (finite, e.g. fossil fuels, where
any use is by definition a depletion). This sub-topic also introduces environmental indicators used
to measure sustainability, such as the **ecological footprint** -- the area of land and water
required to sustainably provide all resources at the rate they are being consumed, and to
assimilate the associated waste.

## Why Topic 1 gets disproportionate revision value

Because the specification explicitly revisits perspectives, systems and sustainability throughout
every later topic, a student who has genuinely internalised this vocabulary can apply it to unfamiliar
content on water, land, atmosphere or resources without having studied that content in the same depth
as a student who has not built this foundation. Concretely: any Topic 4-8 question can, in principle,
ask you to identify the EVS underlying a described policy position, model a described environmental
process as a system with named inputs/outputs/feedback loops, or evaluate whether a described
resource-use pattern is within its natural income. Treating Topic 1 as a short warm-up rather than a
recurring toolkit is the single most common reason for descriptive, rather than analytical, ESS exam
answers.

## Common exam pitfalls

- Defining "negative feedback" as bad and "positive feedback" as good -- both terms describe the
  direction of the effect on the system (stabilising vs amplifying), not a value judgement.
- Confusing natural capital with natural income -- capital is the stock, income is the sustainable
  yield from that stock.
- Applying an EVS label without evidence from the scenario -- always justify which specific words or
  actions in a case study indicate ecocentrism, anthropocentrism or technocentrism.
- Treating "sustainable" as a simple yes/no label rather than a threshold -- a resource is being used
  sustainably only while consumption stays within its natural income; the same resource can move
  between sustainable and unsustainable use over time.

## Self-test

1. Define environmental value system (EVS) and name its three broad categories.
2. What is the difference between negative feedback and positive feedback in a system?
3. Distinguish an open system from a closed system.
4. Define natural capital and natural income, and explain how they differ.
5. What does the ecological footprint measure?
6. Explain, with one example, why Topic 1 vocabulary is expected to reappear in answers on later
   topics such as Water or Atmosphere and climate change.

**Answers:** 1. A particular world view or paradigm shaping how an individual or group perceives and
evaluates environmental issues; ecocentrism, anthropocentrism and technocentrism. 2. Negative feedback
counteracts a change and stabilises the system back toward equilibrium; positive feedback amplifies a
change, pushing the system further from its original state. 3. An open system exchanges both energy
and matter with its surroundings; a closed system exchanges only energy, not matter. 4. Natural
capital is the stock of natural resources providing goods and services of value; natural income is
the sustainable yield obtainable from that capital without depleting it -- capital is the stock,
income is the flow. 5. The area of land and water required to sustainably provide all the resources a
population consumes, and to assimilate the waste it produces, at the rate it is consuming and
producing. 6. Any well-justified example, e.g.: a question on freshwater over-extraction can be
analysed as a system with defined inputs/outputs and a feedback loop, and evaluated against whether
extraction is exceeding the resource's natural income -- both Topic 1 concepts applied directly to
Topic 4 content.
