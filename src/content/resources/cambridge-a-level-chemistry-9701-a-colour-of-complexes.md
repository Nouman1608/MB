This guide covers subtopic **28.3, Colour of complexes**, from Topic 28, Chemistry of transition elements, of
Cambridge International AS & A Level Chemistry 9701, 2025–2027 series. It is A Level content. The broader
[Transition Elements: Colour, Stereoisomerism and Stability Constants](/resources/a-transition-elements-colour-stereoisomerism-and-stability-constants/)
guide introduces the idea; this page works through the five learning outcomes one at a time, adds the
calculation that links ΔE to a wavelength, and shows how to build a full-mark explanation. When you have
finished, use the [Colour of complexes revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-chemistry-9701-a-colour-of-complexes-revision-notes/)
and the [Colour of complexes practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-chemistry-9701-a-colour-of-complexes-practice/).

## What the syllabus asks for

The five outcomes in 28.3 are:

1. define and use the terms **degenerate** and **non-degenerate** d orbitals;
2. describe the splitting of degenerate d orbitals into two non-degenerate sets, and use ΔE, in
   **octahedral** complexes (two higher, three lower) and **tetrahedral** complexes (three higher, two lower);
3. explain why transition elements form coloured compounds in terms of the **frequency of light absorbed**
   as an electron is promoted between two non-degenerate d orbitals;
4. describe, qualitatively, the effect of **different ligands** on ΔE, on the frequency absorbed and so on the
   **complementary colour** observed;
5. use the complexes of **copper(II)** and **cobalt(II)** with water, ammonia, hydroxide and chloride as
   examples of ligand exchange changing the colour.

You are expected to know how to sketch a 3dxy and a 3dz² orbital from 28.1. That sketch is what makes the
splitting pattern make sense, so revise it alongside this page.

## Degenerate and non-degenerate orbitals

**Degenerate** orbitals have exactly the same energy. In a gaseous, isolated transition-metal ion all five 3d
orbitals are degenerate: no direction in space is special, so no orbital is pushed up more than another.

When ligands approach, each donates a lone pair towards the metal. The negative charge of those lone pairs
repels the electrons in the d orbitals. Because the five d orbitals point in different directions, they are
not repelled equally. The result is two sets of orbitals at different energies: they are now
**non-degenerate**. The energy gap between the two sets is **ΔE**.

A useful one-line definition for an exam is: *"non-degenerate d orbitals are d orbitals of the same
sub-shell that have different energies because of the ligands around the metal ion."*

## Why octahedral and tetrahedral patterns are opposite

In an **octahedral** complex the six ligands sit on the x, y and z axes. Two d orbitals have their lobes
along the axes: **3dx²−y²** and **3dz²**. Their electrons point straight at the ligand lone pairs, so they are
repelled most and form the **higher set of two**. The other three, **3dxy, 3dxz and 3dyz**, have lobes
between the axes; they are repelled less and form the **lower set of three**.

In a **tetrahedral** complex the four ligands sit between the axes. Now the three orbitals with lobes between
the axes (dxy, dxz, dyz) are closer to the ligands and form the **higher set of three**, while dx²−y² and dz²
form the **lower set of two**. With only four ligands, none pointing straight at an orbital lobe, the
splitting is **smaller** than for an octahedral complex of comparable ligands.

| | Octahedral | Tetrahedral |
|---|---|---|
| Number of ligands | 6, on the axes | 4, between the axes |
| Higher-energy set | 2 (dx²−y², dz²) | 3 (dxy, dxz, dyz) |
| Lower-energy set | 3 (dxy, dxz, dyz) | 2 (dx²−y², dz²) |
| Size of ΔE | larger | smaller |

Naming the individual orbitals in each set is a helpful way to justify the pattern; the syllabus requires
the *numbers* in each set.

## From ΔE to colour: the chain of reasoning

A complete explanation of colour has four links. Mark schemes reward each one, so practise writing them in
order:

1. **Ligands split the d orbitals** into two non-degenerate sets separated by ΔE.
2. An electron in the lower set **absorbs a photon of visible light** and is **promoted** to the higher set.
3. The **frequency** of the photon absorbed is fixed by the gap: **ΔE = hf**.
4. That frequency is removed from white light, and the colour seen is the **complementary colour** of the
   light absorbed (the light that is transmitted or reflected).

Two conditions are hidden in link 2: the ion needs at least one d electron to promote, and at least one
vacancy in the upper set. That is why Ti⁴⁺ and Sc³⁺ (3d⁰) and Zn²⁺ (3d¹⁰) give white solids and colourless
solutions, while Ti³⁺ (3d¹), Cu²⁺ (3d⁹) and every ion in between can be coloured.

### Complementary colours

A simple colour wheel is enough at this level. Colours opposite each other are complementary:

| Light mainly absorbed | Colour observed |
|---|---|
| red | green-blue (cyan) |
| orange | blue |
| yellow | violet |
| green | red-purple (magenta) |
| blue | orange |
| violet | yellow |

So if a question tells you a solution absorbs mainly in the orange part of the spectrum, the solution
appears blue.

## Worked example 1: linking ΔE to a wavelength

*(The wavelength here is illustrative.)* A complex ion absorbs most strongly at a wavelength of 500 nm.
Calculate ΔE for one ion, and the energy for one mole of ions, in kJ mol⁻¹.
Use h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J s, c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹ and L = 6.02 × 10²³ mol⁻¹.

**Step 1: frequency.** f = c / λ = (3.00 × 10⁸) / (500 × 10⁻⁹) = 6.00 × 10¹⁴ Hz

**Step 2: energy per photon.** ΔE = hf = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ × 6.00 × 10¹⁴ = 3.98 × 10⁻¹⁹ J

**Step 3: energy per mole.** 3.98 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 6.02 × 10²³ = 2.39 × 10⁵ J mol⁻¹ = **239 kJ mol⁻¹**

The point to take from this: a **larger ΔE** means a **higher frequency** and a **shorter wavelength** of light
absorbed. Converting nm to m (× 10⁻⁹) is where most calculation marks are lost.

## How ligands change ΔE

Different ligands repel the d electrons by different amounts, so the same metal ion has a different ΔE with
different ligands. You are not asked to learn a full ranking; for the syllabus examples it is enough to know
that, around copper(II) and cobalt(II), **NH₃ gives a larger ΔE than H₂O**, and **Cl⁻ in a tetrahedral
complex gives a smaller ΔE than H₂O in an octahedral one**.

Three things can change ΔE, and a good answer names whichever applies:

- **the identity of the ligand** (H₂O replaced by NH₃);
- **the coordination number and shape** (six H₂O octahedral replaced by four Cl⁻ tetrahedral);
- **the oxidation state of the metal** (for example Fe²⁺ and Fe³⁺ aqua ions differ in colour).

The syllabus focuses on the first two through its copper and cobalt examples.

## The copper(II) and cobalt(II) examples

| Change | Copper(II) | Cobalt(II) |
|---|---|---|
| Aqua ion | [Cu(H₂O)₆]²⁺, pale blue, octahedral | [Co(H₂O)₆]²⁺, pink, octahedral |
| Add OH⁻(aq) | [Cu(OH)₂(H₂O)₄], pale blue precipitate | [Co(OH)₂(H₂O)₄], blue precipitate |
| Add excess NH₃(aq) | [Cu(NH₃)₄(H₂O)₂]²⁺, deep blue solution | [Co(NH₃)₆]²⁺, pale straw solution, darkens in air |
| Add concentrated HCl | [CuCl₄]²⁻, yellow-green, tetrahedral | [CoCl₄]²⁻, blue, tetrahedral |

**Worked example 2: an annotated model answer.**

*Question: Aqueous copper(II) sulfate is pale blue. When excess aqueous ammonia is added, a deep blue
solution forms. Explain the change in colour.*

> Four NH₃ ligands replace four H₂O ligands to form [Cu(NH₃)₄(H₂O)₂]²⁺ **[ligand exchange named, with
> formula]**. NH₃ splits the d orbitals by a different (larger) amount than H₂O, so ΔE changes **[effect of
> ligand on ΔE]**. A different frequency of visible light is absorbed when a d electron is promoted
> **[frequency link]**, so a different complementary colour is transmitted and the solution looks deep
> blue **[complementary colour link]**.

Notice that the answer never says "NH₃ is blue". The colour belongs to the whole complex and comes from the
light it absorbs.

**Why the cobalt chloride change is bigger.** Going from pink [Co(H₂O)₆]²⁺ to blue [CoCl₄]²⁻ changes both the
ligand and the shape. Chloride ions are larger than water molecules, so only four fit round the Co²⁺ ion; the
complex becomes tetrahedral, with the smaller tetrahedral splitting. ΔE falls, light of lower frequency is
absorbed, and the observed colour changes from pink to blue. Adding water reverses the exchange, because the
equilibrium [Co(H₂O)₆]²⁺ + 4Cl⁻ ⇌ [CoCl₄]²⁻ + 6H₂O shifts to the left.

## Common mistakes

- **Describing emission.** Transition-metal colour comes from light *absorbed* as an electron moves *up*.
  Writing that electrons "fall back and give out light" describes flame tests and loses the mark.
- **Naming the absorbed colour as the observed colour.** A complex that absorbs orange light looks blue.
- **Swapping the patterns.** Octahedral is two up, three down; tetrahedral is three up, two down.
- **Saying the d orbitals "split" in the free ion.** Splitting needs ligands; the isolated ion's d orbitals
  are degenerate.
- **Forgetting the d⁰ and d¹⁰ cases** when asked why a compound is white or colourless.
- **Unit slips** in ΔE = hf questions: nm to m, and J per ion to kJ per mole.

## Exam technique for Cambridge command words

- **Define** (degenerate, non-degenerate): one precise sentence each; no examples needed.
- **Describe** the splitting: give the number of orbitals in each set and which set is higher, for the
  geometry named. A labelled energy-level sketch with two levels and ΔE marked is quick and clear.
- **Explain** why a complex is coloured: use all four links in the chain above, in order.
- **Suggest** or **predict** a colour change: identify the new complex and its shape, then state the effect
  on ΔE and on the frequency absorbed.
- **Calculate**: show f = c/λ and ΔE = hf as separate lines, with units on every line.
