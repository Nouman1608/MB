Topic 7 of Cambridge IGCSE Environmental Management (0680) looks at where people live, how populations
are structured, why they grow or shrink, what growth does to the environment and how governments try to
manage population size. Exam questions often give a population pyramid or a map, so practise reading
data, then link it to causes, impacts and strategies.

For the full explanation use the
[human population study guide](/resources/cambridge-igcse-environmental-management-human-population-study-guide/);
practise with the [human population practice questions](/resources/igcse-environmental-management-human-population-practice/).

## Key definitions

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Population density | Number of people living in an area, usually people per km² |
| Population distribution | How people are spread over an area |
| Sparsely populated | Few people in an area |
| Densely populated | Many people in an area |
| Dependent population | Young (usually 0-14) and elderly (usually 65+) people supported by workers |
| Working population | People of working age (usually 15-64) |
| Birth rate | Measured here as children per woman |
| Death rate | Linked to life expectancy |
| Urbanisation | Growth in the proportion of people living in towns and cities |

**Worked density example**: a district of 250 km² has 75 000 people. Density = 75 000 / 250 = **300 people
per km²**.

## How do you read a population pyramid? (7.1.2)

- Each bar shows the percentage (or number) of males (left) and females (right) in an age group.
- A **wide base** means many young dependants and a high birth rate.
- A **narrow top** means few elderly people and lower life expectancy.
- **Straight sides** or a wide top mean a low birth rate and long life expectancy.
- Compare male and female bars, such as more elderly women than men because women tend to live longer.

## How do LIC and HIC structures compare? (7.1.3-7.1.4)

The World Bank classifies economies as **low-income (LICs)**, **middle-income (MICs)** and **high-income
(HICs)** countries.

| Feature | LIC pyramid | HIC pyramid |
|---|---|---|
| Shape | Triangular, wide base | Column or "beehive", narrower base |
| Young dependants | High proportion | Low proportion |
| Elderly dependants | Low proportion | High and growing proportion |
| Birth rate | High | Low |
| Life expectancy | Lower | Higher |
| Main challenge | Schools, jobs and food for a young population | Pensions and healthcare for an ageing population |

## What changes the size of a population? (7.2.1)

- **Birth rates** (children per woman): fall with access to education for girls, contraception and
  healthcare; stay high where children are needed for farm labour or as security in old age.
- **Death rates** and **life expectancy**: improve with clean water, sanitation, vaccination, healthcare and
  food supply.
- **Migration**: emigration (leaving) and immigration (arriving), driven by push factors (lack of jobs,
  conflict, hazards) and pull factors (jobs, services, safety).
- **Rural-urban migration** and **urbanisation**: people move to cities for work and services.

## What are the impacts of population growth? (7.2.2)

| Impact | Explanation |
|---|---|
| Informal urban settlements | Housing built without planning permission, often lacking water and sanitation |
| Megacities | Cities of over 10 million people |
| Resources and energy | Greater demand for food, fuel, materials and electricity |
| Sanitation and water supply | Systems overwhelmed, raising disease risk |
| Waste management | More waste than collection systems can handle |
| Land | Less land for farming and habitats |
| Facilities and infrastructure | Schools, hospitals and roads overcrowded |
| Pollution | Air, land, light, noise, visual and water pollution |
| Traffic congestion | More vehicles, longer journeys, more emissions |
| Employment | Changing opportunities, often informal work |

## How can population size be managed? (7.3)

| Strategy | Benefit | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Access to contraception | Lets people choose family size; lowers birth rate | Cost, supply in rural areas, cultural or religious objections |
| Anti-natalist policy (fewer births) | Rapid fall in birth rate, as in China's one-child policy (1980-2015) | Ageing population, gender imbalance, human rights concerns |
| Pro-natalist policy (more births) | Supports ageing countries; incentives such as child benefits | Expensive; often only a small effect on birth rate |
| Access to education | Educated women tend to have fewer children and later | Takes a generation to work |
| Access to healthcare | Lower infant mortality, so families need fewer children | Initially increases population as death rates fall |
| Control of migration | Manages labour supply and pressure on services | Can create labour shortages; ethical and political issues |

## Common mistakes and exam traps

- Confusing density (people per km²) with distribution (spread).
- Describing a pyramid without quoting figures from it.
- Saying healthcare always reduces population; at first it raises it by lowering death rates.
- Treating pro-natalist and anti-natalist as interchangeable.
- Forgetting to mention both benefits and limitations in "discuss" answers.

## Frequently asked questions

**How do I compare two pyramids?** Comment on the base, the middle and the top of each, quote a figure for
each, then explain the difference using birth rate and life expectancy.

**What is the difference between a push and a pull factor?** A push factor makes people leave a place,
such as unemployment or conflict. A pull factor attracts them to a new place, such as jobs or better
services.

**Do I need World Bank income thresholds?** No. The 0680 syllabus asks you to describe the three income
groups (LICs, MICs and HICs), not to learn the dollar values, which the World Bank updates each year.
