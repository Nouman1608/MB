This guide covers **section 3.1.3 Development**, the third of the four sections of Paper 1 (Cognition and Behaviour) in AQA GCSE Psychology (8182). The [Paper 1 Cognition and Behaviour guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-psychology-cognition-and-behaviour/) summarises the content; this page goes deeper into development alone, with each study broken into procedure, findings and evaluation, classroom applications and worked answers.

## What section 3.1.3 covers

- **Early brain development:** from simple neural structures in the womb, the development of the brain stem, thalamus, cerebellum and cortex, reflecting autonomic functions, sensory processing, movement and cognition; the roles of nature and nurture.
- **Piaget's stage theory** and the development of intelligence: assimilation and accommodation; the four stages; the role of the theory in education; reduction of egocentricity and development of conservation; **McGarrigle and Donaldson's 'naughty teddy' study** and **Hughes' 'policeman doll' study**.
- **The effects of learning on development:** **Dweck's Mindset Theory** (fixed and growth mindset); the role of praise and self-efficacy beliefs; learning styles, including verbalisers and visualisers; **Willingham's Learning Theory** and his criticism of learning styles.

## Early brain development

The brain begins as a simple tube of neural tissue in the first weeks of pregnancy and develops in a broadly bottom-to-top order, so that basic survival functions come first and complex thinking last.

| Structure | Main function | What its development makes possible |
|---|---|---|
| Brain stem | Controls automatic body processes | **Autonomic functions** such as breathing, heart rate and swallowing, needed from birth |
| Thalamus | Relays information from the senses to the cortex | **Sensory processing** |
| Cerebellum | Co-ordinates movement, balance and posture | **Movement**: sitting, crawling, walking |
| Cortex | Thinking, language, memory and planning | **Cognition**; parts at the front keep maturing into adolescence |

**Nature and nurture.** Genes (**nature**) set the order in which structures develop, which is why this sequence is broadly the same in all children. **Nurture** affects how well the brain develops: the mother's diet and health in pregnancy, avoiding harmful substances such as alcohol, and, after birth, stimulation, play and interaction, which strengthen the connections between neurons that are used. The best answer is that brain development is an **interaction** of both.

## Piaget's stage theory

Piaget saw children as active learners who build **schemas** (mental frameworks). When new information fits a schema, the child **assimilates** it; when it does not fit, the child feels uncertain and must **accommodate**, changing the schema or creating a new one.

| Stage | Approximate age | Key features |
|---|---|---|
| Sensorimotor | 0-2 | Learning through senses and actions; **object permanence** develops |
| Pre-operational | 2-7 | Language and pretend play; **egocentric**; cannot **conserve** |
| Concrete operational | 7-11 | Can conserve; logical thinking about real, concrete objects |
| Formal operational | 11+ | Abstract and hypothetical reasoning |

**Egocentrism** is the inability to see a situation from another person's point of view. **Conservation** is understanding that quantity stays the same when appearance changes: the same number of counters spread out, or the same amount of juice poured into a taller, thinner glass.

## Studies that challenge Piaget

### McGarrigle and Donaldson: the 'naughty teddy' study

**Procedure.** Young children did Piaget's number conservation task with two equal rows of counters. In one condition the experimenter deliberately spread out one row and asked whether the rows were the same. In the other, a glove puppet, "naughty teddy", messed up one row as if by accident.

**Findings.** Many more children said the rows were still the same when the change was made by teddy.

**Conclusion.** Children can conserve **earlier than Piaget claimed**. In Piaget's version, an adult deliberately changing the row and asking again may have suggested a different answer was expected.

**Evaluation.** A well-controlled comparison of the same task in two conditions. However, the teddy may have distracted children so they did not notice the change, which would make some "same" answers non-conservation.

### Hughes: the 'policeman doll' study

**Procedure.** Young children were shown a model made of two walls crossing to form four sections. After practising hiding a boy doll from one policeman doll, they had to hide the boy from **two** policemen placed so that only one section was hidden from both.

**Findings.** Most children hid the boy correctly.

**Conclusion.** Young children can take another person's point of view much earlier than Piaget's own mountain task suggested, because hiding from a policeman made **human sense** to them.

**Evaluation.** A strength is that the task was meaningful to children, which may give a truer picture of their ability. A weakness is that a hiding game may be easier than seeing exactly what another person sees, so the two tasks may not measure the same thing.

## Piaget's theory in education

- **Readiness:** match tasks to the stage; for example, younger pupils need **concrete materials** such as counters before abstract maths.
- **Discovery learning:** children learn best by exploring and finding out for themselves.
- **The teacher as facilitator:** set up activities that create a mismatch with current schemas, so accommodation can happen.

## Dweck's Mindset Theory

Carol Dweck distinguished a **fixed mindset**, the belief that ability is set and cannot change, from a **growth mindset**, the belief that ability can be developed through effort, good strategies and help from others. Pupils with a fixed mindset tend to avoid challenge and give up after failure; pupils with a growth mindset see mistakes as part of learning.

**Praise** matters. Praising ability ("you're so clever") can encourage a fixed mindset, while praising effort and strategy ("you kept trying different methods") encourages a growth mindset. **Self-efficacy** is a person's belief in their ability to succeed at a particular task; pupils who believe that effort will pay off are more likely to keep going.

**Evaluation.** The theory has clear practical uses in schools and gives teachers simple changes to try. However, some later research has found the effects of mindset interventions on grades to be smaller than early studies suggested, and mindset alone cannot overcome factors such as poor teaching or lack of resources.

## Learning styles and Willingham's criticism

**Learning styles** theory claims people learn best when teaching matches their preferred style. **Verbalisers** prefer words, spoken or written; **visualisers** prefer pictures, diagrams and charts.

**Willingham**, a cognitive psychologist, criticised this. He argued that there is little evidence that matching teaching to a learner's preferred style improves learning. People may have preferences, but what matters more is that the teaching method suits the **content**: a map is best learned visually by everyone. He also stressed that much of what we remember is stored as **meaning**, so teaching should help pupils think about what material means.

## Worked example 1: applying Piaget

> *Ava, aged 5, watches juice being poured from a short, wide glass into a tall, thin glass. She says there is now more juice. Explain Ava's answer using Piaget's theory.* [3 marks]

"Ava is in the **pre-operational stage** (2-7) [1]. She **cannot conserve**: she does not understand that the quantity stays the same when its appearance changes [1]. She focuses on the **height** of the juice in the tall glass and ignores its width, so she believes there is more [1]."

## Worked example 2: applying Dweck

> *Suggest how a teacher could use Dweck's Mindset Theory to help a pupil who says "I'm just bad at maths".* [2 marks]

"The teacher should **praise effort and strategy** rather than ability, for example 'You checked every step carefully' [1]. This encourages a **growth mindset**, so the pupil comes to believe maths ability can improve with practice and is more likely to persist [1]."

## Common mistakes

- Mixing up assimilation (fitting into an existing schema) and accommodation (changing the schema).
- Giving the wrong stage for a child's age, or the wrong ability for a stage.
- Saying the studies show Piaget was "wrong" rather than that he **underestimated** young children.
- Confusing Dweck's mindsets with learning styles: they are separate ideas.
- Calling Willingham a supporter of learning styles.
- Listing brain structures without linking each to its function.

## Exam technique for AQA command words

- **Identify / Name:** a stage, structure or term is enough.
- **Outline / Describe:** key features; for a study, procedure and findings.
- **Explain:** give reasons and link them to the scenario.
- **Evaluate:** developed strengths and weaknesses with a judgement.
- **Suggest:** apply the theory to a new situation, such as a classroom.

## Frequently asked questions

**What does the naughty teddy study show?**
That more young children could conserve number when the change looked accidental, suggesting Piaget underestimated them.

**What is Willingham's criticism of learning styles?**
That there is little evidence matching teaching to a preferred style improves learning; the method should suit the content.
