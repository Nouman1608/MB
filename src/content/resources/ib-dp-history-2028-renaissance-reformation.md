**This page is for HL only.** Regional study 2, Renaissance and Reformation (circa 1350 to 1700), is one of twelve studies in the Europe option examined on HL Paper 3, where you answer two essays from different regional studies. The guide asks you to learn the Renaissance in Italy, the Renaissance in **one other country** (not Italy), its impact on **one country**, the emergence of the Reformation, the Reformation's impact on **one country**, and the Catholic Church's response. In this guide the other country for the Renaissance is England, and the Reformation case is Germany. You can swap these for your own choices, as long as you keep the same questions.

## Emergence of the Renaissance in Italy

*Social and political conditions.* Italy was divided into city-states such as Florence, Venice, Milan and Genoa, plus the Papal States and the Kingdom of Naples. No single ruler controlled the peninsula. Rival states competed for prestige, and rulers used art, buildings and learning to show power. Wealthy families and guilds had the money and the ambition to patronize talent.

*Economic factors.* Italian cities led in trade, banking and cloth-making. Wealth from the eastern Mediterranean trade, and banking families, made spending on art possible. The Black Death (1347 to 1351) killed many, and some historians argue it changed attitudes to life, wealth and religion, though this link is debated.

*Intellectual and religious influences.* **Humanism** turned attention to the study of classical Latin and Greek writing and to human achievement. Petrarch (died 1374) is often called an early humanist. Scholars recovered texts, improved Latin and tested old documents, as Lorenzo Valla did when he showed the Donation of Constantine to be a forgery. Greek scholars and manuscripts reached Italy, especially after the fall of Constantinople to the Ottomans in 1453. The Church remained central: it was the biggest patron, and Renaissance art grew largely out of religious commissions.

*Role of individuals.* The **Medici** family of Florence ruled through wealth and influence. Cosimo de' Medici (died 1464) and his grandson Lorenzo (died 1492) supported artists, scholars and Platonic thought. In Milan the **Sforza** dukes, beginning with Francesco Sforza, who took power in 1450, employed engineers and artists, and Leonardo da Vinci worked for Ludovico Sforza. Popes such as Nicholas V, Julius II and Leo X financed rebuilding in Rome and employed Michelangelo and Raphael. Patronage was often about politics as much as taste.

**Exam point.** A strong answer will not call the Renaissance simply a "rebirth" from a dark age. Many historians now stress continuity with medieval thought, and the variation between places.

## The Renaissance in one country outside Italy: England

*Spread of ideas.* Printing, developed in Germany around 1450, made classical and humanist texts cheaper. Travel by scholars and merchants carried ideas north. William Caxton set up the first press in England in 1476. Erasmus visited England several times and spent time at an English university, and he influenced scholars such as John Colet and Thomas More.

*Social and political conditions.* Under the Tudors (from 1485), a stronger crown ended the Wars of the Roses and encouraged a court culture that valued learning. A growing educated gentry wanted grammar schools and classical education.

*Economic factors.* A rising cloth trade, a growing London and a merchant class created demand for books, schools and buildings.

*Intellectual and religious influences.* Christian humanism in England aimed to reform the Church by returning to scripture and good education. More's *Utopia* (1516) used classical models to criticise society. The Reformation, which began as an argument about religion, also opened new space for print and debate.

## The impact of the Renaissance on England

- *Literature.* The English language flowered in the sixteenth century. Poets such as Sir Thomas Wyatt adapted Italian forms like the sonnet. Playhouses opened in London from the 1570s, and playwrights including Christopher Marlowe and William Shakespeare drew on classical and Italian stories.
- *Political ideas.* More's *Utopia* questioned social inequality. Writers argued about royal power, law and the duties of rulers. Thinkers such as Machiavelli, though Italian, were widely read and argued over.
- *Science.* Francis Bacon argued for learning from experiment. William Harvey published his account of the circulation of blood in 1628. The Royal Society was founded in 1660.
- *The arts.* Court patronage brought foreign artists, such as Hans Holbein, who painted Henry VIII's court. Later architecture, such as the work of Inigo Jones in the early seventeenth century, adopted classical styles.

When you judge impact, ask: for whom? Court and urban elites felt it most; most people lived in villages and saw little of it.

## Emergence of the Reformation

*State of the Catholic Church at the start of the sixteenth century.* The Church was wealthy and powerful. Criticisms included clerics who held several offices, priests with little training, and the sale of **indulgences**, which promised reduced punishment for sin in return for payment. Humanists such as Erasmus mocked corruption but wanted reform within the Church. The Church was not collapsing, and many people were devout, so historians disagree about how weak it really was.

*Ideas and Lutheranism.* **Martin Luther**, a monk and professor at Wittenberg, argued that people were saved by faith, not works or payment, and that scripture was the highest authority. In 1517 he issued his Ninety-five Theses attacking the sale of indulgences. His ideas spread rapidly in the 1520s. The princes who protected him had political and economic reasons to support reform, such as control of church wealth.

*Response of the Church and Emperor.* The pope condemned Luther's teaching and excommunicated him in 1521. **Charles V**, Holy Roman Emperor, summoned him to the Diet of Worms in 1521, where Luther refused to retract, and the Edict of Worms outlawed him. Luther was protected by Frederick the Wise of Saxony, and hidden at the Wartburg, where he translated the New Testament into German. Charles V was distracted by wars with France and the Ottomans, so he could not enforce the edict.

*Role of the printing press.* Pamphlets, sermons and woodcuts in German carried Luther's message to many who could not read Latin. Print made the debate public and fast, though preachers and word of mouth also mattered, since many people could not read.

## Impact of the Reformation on Germany

*Religious changes.* Lutheran churches replaced Catholic practice in many German states. Services were in German, and clergy could marry. The **Peace of Augsburg** (1555) allowed each ruler of the Empire to choose between Catholicism and Lutheranism, and subjects were expected to follow ("whose realm, his religion"). Calvinism was not covered, which caused later tension.

*Social and political developments.* The **Peasants' War** (1524 to 1525) was a revolt that used religious language to demand justice; Luther condemned it, and the rebels were crushed. Princes gained power over churches and schools. Religious division weakened imperial unity.

*Economic developments.* Monastic land was taken over by princes and cities. Church revenues were redirected towards schools and poor relief in Protestant lands.

*The Thirty Years' War.* The war (1618 to 1648) began with the revolt in Bohemia and the Defenestration of Prague, and grew into a conflict involving Habsburg, Danish, Swedish and French forces. Religion mattered, but so did dynastic and territorial rivalry, which is why historians argue over whether it was mainly a religious war. It devastated parts of Germany. The Peace of Westphalia (1648) extended recognition to Calvinists and confirmed the princes' power.

## Responses of the Catholic Church: the Counter-Reformation

Historians use "Counter-Reformation" for the Church's reaction to Protestantism and "Catholic Reformation" for reforms that had begun independently. A balanced essay shows both.

- *Erasmus.* He published a Greek New Testament in 1516 and wrote *In Praise of Folly* (1511). He criticised abuses but stayed in the Church and disagreed with Luther about free will in the 1520s. His position shows that reform and rebellion were not the same thing.
- *Council of Trent (1545 to 1563).* It met in three periods. It reaffirmed Catholic doctrine on faith and works, scripture and tradition, and the seven sacraments. It also reformed clergy training and discipline by requiring bishops to reside in their dioceses and setting up seminaries.
- *Popes.* **Paul III** (1534 to 1549) approved the Jesuits in 1540, called the Council, and expanded the Roman Inquisition in 1542. **Paul IV** (1555 to 1559) was severe: the Index of Forbidden Books appeared in 1559 and Jews in Rome were confined to a ghetto. **Pius IV** (1559 to 1565) concluded the Council and issued its decrees.
- *Jesuits.* Founded by Ignatius of Loyola, they trained disciplined teachers and missionaries, ran schools and advised rulers.

## Perspectives to use

Early accounts treated the Reformation as a religious revival or a Church collapse. Later historians have stressed political, social and economic causes. Others, like some in the "confessionalization" school, argue that Catholic, Lutheran and Calvinist states built churches and governments together. Use these ideas by saying what each emphasises.

## Writing the Paper 3 essay

Pick the example the question requires and keep to it. Rank factors (religion, politics, printing, rulers' interests) and judge. Use dates and names precisely, and always address the wording of the question.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I have to use England and Germany?**
No. The guide says "one country" for each. Choose what your class taught.

**Is the Counter-Reformation the same as the Catholic Reformation?**
Not quite. Use both terms carefully and explain the difference.

**Can I discuss Calvin?**
Yes, as supporting detail, but the guide's named content focuses on Luther and the Catholic response.
