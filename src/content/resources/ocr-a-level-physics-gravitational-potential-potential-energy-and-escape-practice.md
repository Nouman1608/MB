These questions cover section 5.4.4 (Gravitational potential and energy), statements (a) to (e), of the OCR Advanced GCE in Physics A specification (H556), Version 3.0 (March 2026), for first assessment in 2017. Use G = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N m² kg⁻², Earth mass 5.97 × 10²⁴ kg and radius 6.37 × 10⁶ m, Moon mass 7.35 × 10²² kg and radius 1.74 × 10⁶ m. ## Questions

**1.** Define gravitational potential at a point in a gravitational field. **[2]**

**2.** Explain why the gravitational potential is negative at every point near a planet. **[2]**

**3.** Calculate the gravitational potential at the surface of the Earth, and at a point whose distance from the centre of the Earth is three times the Earth's radius. **[3]**

**4.** Calculate the work that must be done to move a 4.0 kg mass from the surface of the Earth to a point at a distance of three Earth radii from the centre of the Earth. **[3]**

**5.** Europa, a moon of Jupiter, has mass 4.80 × 10²² kg and radius 1.56 × 10⁶ m.

**(a)** Calculate the gravitational potential at its surface. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the escape velocity from its surface. **[2]**

**6.** A 650 kg satellite is moved from a circular path of radius 1.2 × 10⁷ m to one of radius 4.2 × 10⁷ m, both measured from the centre of the Earth.

**(a)** Calculate the change in the gravitational potential energy of the satellite. **[3]**
**(b)** State whether the satellite gains or loses gravitational potential energy. **[1]**

**7.** A 50 kg object moves directly away from the centre of the Earth, from the surface to a distance of two Earth radii from the centre.

**(a)** Describe the shape of the graph of gravitational force against distance from the centre of the Earth for this journey. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the area under this graph between the two distances and state what it represents. **[3]**

**8.** A planet has an escape velocity of 7.4 × 10³ m s⁻¹ and a radius of 3.1 × 10⁶ m. Calculate the mass of the planet. **[3]**

**9.** A small projectile is launched vertically from the surface of the Moon at 2.0 × 10³ m s⁻¹. Ignore any atmosphere.

**(a)** Calculate the greatest height above the surface that it reaches. **[5]**
**(b)** A student uses h = v²/2g with g = 1.62 N kg⁻¹ and obtains 1.23 × 10⁶ m. Explain why this is not the same as your answer to (a). **[2]**

**10.** Planet Y has mass 3.3 × 10²³ kg and radius 2.44 × 10⁶ m. Two gases are present near its surface. Their atoms have mean speeds of 5.1 × 10² m s⁻¹ (gas A) and 3.6 × 10³ m s⁻¹ (gas B).

**(a)** Calculate the escape velocity from the surface of planet Y. **[3]**
**(b)** Discuss which gas the planet is more likely to retain, and explain why some of the other gas could still escape. **[2]**

**11.** A body is released from rest at a distance of two Earth radii from the centre of the Earth. Ignore air resistance and the rotation of the Earth.

**(a)** Calculate its speed when it reaches the Earth's surface. **[4]**
**(b)** Explain why a real object released from this height would arrive more slowly. **[1]**

**12.** A spherical asteroid has radius 2.0 × 10⁵ m and a uniform density of 2.5 × 10³ kg m⁻³. The volume of a sphere is 4πr³/3.

**(a)** Show that its mass is about 8.4 × 10¹⁹ kg. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the escape velocity from its surface. **[2]**
**(c)** An astronaut can throw a stone at 12 m s⁻¹. State, with a reason, whether the stone could escape. **[1]**
**(d)** Calculate the energy needed to take a 0.50 kg stone from the surface to infinity. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** The work done per unit mass [1] in bringing a small (test) mass from infinity to that point [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Both ideas are needed: "per unit mass" and "from infinity". "Energy at a point" alone describes potential energy and does not gain credit.

**2.** Gravity is attractive, so the field does positive work as a mass moves in from infinity [1]. Potential is defined as zero at infinity, so the work done by an external agent is negative, and every nearer point is below zero [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Linking the sign to the zero at infinity and the attractive force is what earns the second mark; "because it is an attractive force" on its own is incomplete.

**3.** Vg = -GM/r [1]. At the surface: -3.982 × 10¹⁴ / 6.37 × 10⁶ = **-6.25 × 10⁷ J kg⁻¹** [1]. At 3R: one third of this, **-2.08 × 10⁷ J kg⁻¹** [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the substitution into Vg = -GM/r and keep the minus sign; a positive value is wrong even if the size is right.

**4.** W = mΔVg [1]. ΔVg = -2.084 × 10⁷ - (-6.251 × 10⁷) = +4.167 × 10⁷ J kg⁻¹ [1]. W = 4.0 × 4.167 × 10⁷ = **1.67 × 10⁸ J** [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Take final minus initial. Subtracting the other way gives a negative energy, which suggests the field does the work, contradicting a mass being moved away.

**5. (a)** Vg = -GM/r = -(6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ × 4.80 × 10²²) / 1.56 × 10⁶ [1] = **-2.05 × 10⁶ J kg⁻¹** [1]
**(b)** v = √(2GM/r) = √(2 × 3.202 × 10¹² / 1.56 × 10⁶) [1] = **2.03 × 10³ m s⁻¹** [1]
*Examiner insight:* If you have found Vg in part (a), v = √(2 × |Vg|) saves a step and an arithmetic slip.

**6. (a)** ΔE = GMm(1/r₁ - 1/r₂) [1]. = 3.982 × 10¹⁴ × 650 × (1/1.2 × 10⁷ - 1/4.2 × 10⁷) [1]. = **1.54 × 10¹⁰ J** [1]
**(b)** The energy increases by this amount, so the satellite **gains** potential energy because it moves to a higher (less negative) potential [1].
*Examiner insight:* A state question carries a single mark for the correct direction. Quote the sign of the change as well; a final answer given as a loss is wrong even with correct working.

**7. (a)** The force falls with distance in a curve [1] that is steep at first and flattens, following an inverse-square law (the force at two radii is a quarter of the force at the surface) [1].
**(b)** The area under the graph is the work done in moving the object between the two distances [1]. Area = GMm(1/R - 1/2R) = 3.982 × 10¹⁴ × 50 / (2 × 6.37 × 10⁶) [1] = **1.56 × 10⁹ J** [1]
*Examiner insight:* When asked what an area represents, name the quantity and the process ("work done by the force in moving the object between these distances") rather than just "energy".

**8.** v² = 2GM/R, so M = v²R/2G [1]. M = (7.4 × 10³)² × 3.1 × 10⁶ / (2 × 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹) [1] = **1.27 × 10²⁴ kg** [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Rearrange before substituting. Squaring 7.4 × 10³ as 7.4 × 10⁶ or squaring only the 7.4 are the common slips; check the power of ten.

**9. (a)** Energy conservation per kilogram: ½v² + Vg(surface) = Vg(highest point) [1]. Vg(surface) = -4.902 × 10¹² / 1.74 × 10⁶ = -2.8175 × 10⁶ J kg⁻¹ [1]. Total = 2.0 × 10⁶ - 2.8175 × 10⁶ = -8.175 × 10⁵ J kg⁻¹ [1]. r = GM/8.175 × 10⁵ = 5.997 × 10⁶ m from the centre [1]. Height = 5.997 × 10⁶ - 1.74 × 10⁶ = **4.26 × 10⁶ m** [1]
**(b)** The field strength decreases with height, so g is not uniform and 1.62 N kg⁻¹ applies only at the surface [1]. Using it throughout means the deceleration is overestimated, giving a height that is too small [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* The final step is converting r back into a height. Giving 6.00 × 10⁶ m as "the height" is a common loss on this type of question.

**10. (a)** v = √(2GM/r) = √(2 × 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ × 3.3 × 10²³ / 2.44 × 10⁶) [1]. = √(1.804 × 10⁷) [1] = **4.25 × 10³ m s⁻¹** [1]
**(b)** Gas A, with mean speed about 12% of the escape velocity, is far more likely to be retained, whereas gas B has a mean speed about 85% of it [1]. Atoms have a range of speeds, so some of gas B (those in the fast tail of the distribution) can exceed the escape velocity and leave over time [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* A good discussion compares numbers with the escape velocity and then makes a statement about the spread of speeds. Saying only "gas B is faster" is too thin.

**11. (a)** Energy conservation: ½v² = decrease in potential energy per kilogram = GM(1/R - 1/2R) [1]. = GM/2R = 3.982 × 10¹⁴ / (2 × 6.37 × 10⁶) = 3.126 × 10⁷ J kg⁻¹ [1]. v² = 2 × 3.126 × 10⁷ = 6.25 × 10⁷ [1]. v = **7.91 × 10³ m s⁻¹** [1]
**(b)** Air resistance transfers kinetic energy to thermal energy, so the final speed is less [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Keep the energy balance per kilogram, since the mass cancels, and say which energy falls and which rises before substituting.

**12. (a)** M = ρ × 4πr³/3 = 2.5 × 10³ × 4π × (2.0 × 10⁵)³ / 3 [1] = 8.38 × 10¹⁹ kg, which rounds to about **8.4 × 10¹⁹ kg** [1]
**(b)** v = √(2GM/r) = √(2 × 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ × 8.38 × 10¹⁹ / 2.0 × 10⁵) [1] = **2.4 × 10² m s⁻¹** [1]
**(c)** No: 12 m s⁻¹ is far below the escape velocity (about 240 m s⁻¹) [1].
**(d)** Vg = -GM/r = -2.79 × 10⁴ J kg⁻¹ [1]. Energy = m × |Vg| [1] = 0.50 × 2.79 × 10⁴ = **1.40 × 10⁴ J** [1] **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* A "show that" needs the unrounded value and the stated result; give 8.38 × 10¹⁹ kg before rounding, then use the unrounded figure in later parts.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Dropping the negative sign from Vg or E, or giving the energy to bring a mass in from infinity as positive.
- Using height above the surface rather than distance from the centre of the planet.
- Subtracting potentials in the wrong order, so the direction of the energy change is wrong.
- Rounding intermediate potentials and then subtracting two similar numbers.
- Using mgh for a large change in height, where the field is not uniform.
- Squaring or square-rooting incorrectly in v = √(2GM/r), or using g instead of GM.
- Stopping at the distance from the centre and not converting it to a height.
- Defining potential without "per unit mass" or without "from infinity".
