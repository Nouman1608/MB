## Syllabus coverage

**CAMBRIDGE INTERNATIONAL AS & A LEVEL PHYSICS 9702 — AS Level, 6.2 Elastic and plastic behaviour**

Candidates should be able to:

- understand and use the terms elastic deformation, plastic deformation and
elastic limit
- understand that the area under the force–extension graph represents the
work done
- determine the elastic potential energy of a material deformed within its
limit of proportionality from the area under the force–extension graph
- recall and use Eₚ = ½Fx = ½kx² for a material deformed within its limit of
proportionality

## Elastic deformation, plastic deformation and the elastic limit

- **Elastic deformation**: the object returns to its original shape and size
when the load is removed.
- **Plastic deformation**: the object does not return to its original shape
when the load is removed; it is permanently deformed.
- **Elastic limit**: the maximum load (or extension) beyond which the
deformation is no longer elastic.

The elastic limit and the **limit of proportionality** (from 6.1) are
different points. Proportionality concerns the *shape of the graph*: is it
still a straight line? Elasticity concerns *what happens on unloading*: does
the object return to its original length? For many metals the two points are
close together, but a material can behave elastically for a short way beyond
the limit of proportionality, following a curve on loading and returning to
zero extension on unloading.

**Reading a loading–unloading graph.** When a metal wire is loaded beyond its
elastic limit and then unloaded, the unloading line is approximately a
straight line **parallel to the original linear section**. It meets the
extension axis at a positive value: the **permanent extension**. A question
that shows the unloading line not returning to the origin is telling you the
deformation was plastic.

## Why the area under a force–extension graph is work done

Work done is force × displacement in the direction of the force. If the force
changes as the object stretches, split the extension into many small steps
∆x; over each step the force is almost constant, so the work done is F∆x — the
area of a thin strip under the graph. Adding all the strips gives the total
area. So:

> The area under a force–extension graph equals the work done in deforming
> the object.

This statement is true for **any** shape of graph, including curved sections
beyond the limit of proportionality. What changes beyond the elastic limit is
what happens to the energy. Within the elastic region, the work done is
stored as **elastic potential energy** and can be recovered. Once the
deformation is plastic, part of the work done goes into permanently
rearranging the material and is dissipated as internal energy; it is not
returned on unloading.

## Elastic potential energy within the limit of proportionality

Within the limit of proportionality the graph is a straight line through the
origin, so the area is a triangle of base x and height F:

```
Ep = (1/2) F x
and since F = k x:
Ep = (1/2) k x^2
```

Both forms are on the syllabus; use whichever matches the data given.

**Worked example.** A wire extends by 3.0 mm under a load of 90 N, within its
limit of proportionality. Ep = ½Fx = ½ × 90 × 3.0 × 10⁻³ = **0.135 J**.

Because Ep ∝ x², doubling the extension of a spring quadruples the energy
stored, while doubling the force (within the limit) also doubles the
extension and therefore quadruples the energy.

## Energy stored between two extensions

A spring that is already stretched gains energy equal to the area of a
**trapezium**, not a triangle.

**Worked example.** A spring of spring constant 400 N m⁻¹ is stretched from an
extension of 0.050 m to 0.150 m. Find the additional energy stored.

```
energy stored = (1/2) k x2^2 - (1/2) k x1^2
              = 0.5 x 400 x (0.150^2 - 0.050^2)
              = 200 x (0.0225 - 0.0025) = 4.0 J
```

The common wrong answer is ½k(x₂ − x₁)² = ½ × 400 × 0.100² = 2.0 J, which
treats the spring as if it started unstretched.

## Loading beyond the elastic limit: a worked example

A metal wire is loaded from zero to 40 N, extending 2.0 mm along a straight
line. Further loading to 50 N takes the extension to 4.0 mm along a line that
can be treated as straight. The load is then removed and the wire unloads
along a line parallel to the first section.

```
work done, linear part   = 0.5 x 40 x 2.0 x 10^-3            = 0.040 J
work done, second part   = 0.5 x (40 + 50) x 2.0 x 10^-3     = 0.090 J
total work done on wire  = 0.130 J

gradient of first section = 40 / 2.0 x 10^-3 = 2.0 x 10^4 N/m
extension recovered on unloading from 50 N = 50 / 2.0 x 10^4 = 2.5 mm
permanent extension = 4.0 - 2.5 = 1.5 mm
energy recovered = 0.5 x 50 x 2.5 x 10^-3 = 0.0625 J
energy dissipated = 0.130 - 0.0625 = 0.068 J
```

The area enclosed between the loading and unloading lines is the energy that
is not recovered. This kind of question tests all four syllabus statements at
once.

## Exam technique

- **Distinguish between elastic and plastic deformation**: refer to what
happens *when the load is removed* for both terms. Describing only one earns
half the marks.
- **Explain why the area represents work done**: mention small steps with
nearly constant force and W = F∆x.
- **Determine from a graph**: show the shape you used (triangle, trapezium or
counted squares) and the value of one square in joules.
- **Calculate**: convert millimetres to metres before multiplying, because
N × mm gives millijoules.

## Common mistakes

- Treating the limit of proportionality and the elastic limit as the same
point.
- Using Eₚ = ½kx² beyond the limit of proportionality, where the graph is no
longer a straight line.
- Using ½k(x₂ − x₁)² for energy added between two extensions.
- Saying all the work done on a plastically deformed wire is stored and can be
recovered.
- Forgetting the factor of ½ and writing Eₚ = Fx.

## Quick revision checklist

- Elastic: returns to original shape; plastic: permanent deformation; elastic
limit separates them
- Limit of proportionality (straight line ends) is not the same as the elastic
limit
- Area under F–x graph = work done, for any graph shape
- Eₚ = ½Fx = ½kx², only within the limit of proportionality
- Energy added between two extensions = ½k(x₂² − x₁²)
- After plastic deformation: unloading line parallel to loading line,
permanent extension, energy dissipated

## Frequently asked questions

**Can a material be elastic but not obey Hooke's law?**
Yes. Between the limit of proportionality and the elastic limit, the graph is
curved but the material still returns to its original length.

**Is the area under the graph always the energy stored?**
It is always the work done. It equals the elastic potential energy stored only
while the deformation is elastic.

**Why does the unloading line of a metal end at a positive extension?**
Because the wire has been plastically deformed: it has a permanent extension
once the load is removed.
