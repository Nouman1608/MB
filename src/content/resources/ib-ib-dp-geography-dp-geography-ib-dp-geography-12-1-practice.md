## Section A

**1.** Define development. **[1]**

**2.** Distinguish between the modernisation and dependency views of global trade. **[2]**

**3.** Outline what is meant by the middle-income trap. **[2]**

**4.** State what a Gini coefficient of 0 and of 1 would mean. **[2]**

**5.** Identify one example of technological leapfrogging. **[1]**

## Section B

**6.** The table shows invented income shares by quintile for Country H.

| Quintile | 1 (poorest) | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 (richest) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Share of income (%) | 4 | 8 | 13 | 20 | 55 |

**(a)** Calculate the cumulative income share for each quintile. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the Gini coefficient, showing your working. **[3]**

**(c)** Calculate the ratio of the richest quintile's share to the poorest quintile's share. **[1]**

**7.** Explain why girls' education is often seen as an effective route to wider development. **[3]**

**8.** Explain one reason why the benefits of leapfrogging technology may be unevenly shared. **[2]**

**9.** Explain how a country might escape the middle-income trap. **[3]**

## Section C

**10.** Evaluate the view that integration into the global economy is the most effective route to development. **[10]**

**11.** Examine why some groups of people within a country are less able than others to take development opportunities. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** Improvement in people's economic, social and political well-being, not only growth in income [1]. **[1]**

**2.** Modernisation theory sees global trade and investment as an opportunity that helps countries move through stages of growth [1]; dependency theory sees them as keeping peripheral countries supplying low-value goods to the core, causing underdevelopment [1]. **[2]**

**3.** A country grows quickly to middle income using cheap labour, then stalls as rising wages remove its cost advantage [1], while it lacks the skills, research and institutions to compete with high-income economies on innovation [1]. **[2]**

**4.** A Gini of 0 means perfect equality, with everyone receiving the same income [1]; a Gini of 1 means maximum inequality, with one person receiving all the income [1]. **[2]**

**5.** For example, mobile phones adopted without landlines, or mobile money such as M-Pesa in Kenya, launched in 2007 [1]. **[1]**

**6. (a)** 0.04, 0.12, 0.25, 0.45, 1.00 [1].

**(b)** Sum of adjacent pairs: (0 + 0.04) + (0.04 + 0.12) + (0.12 + 0.25) + (0.25 + 0.45) + (0.45 + 1.00) = 0.04 + 0.16 + 0.37 + 0.70 + 1.45 = 2.72 [1]. Area under the Lorenz curve = 0.1 x 2.72 = 0.272 [1]. Gini = 1 - 2 x 0.272 = 1 - 0.544 = **0.456** [1].

**(c)** 55 / 4 = **13.75** [1]. **[5]**

**7.** Educated girls tend to marry later and have smaller, healthier families [1]. Education raises women's earnings and their ability to take paid work or start businesses [1], and educated mothers invest more in their children's health and schooling, so the benefits pass to the next generation [1]. **[3]**

**8.** Leapfrogging depends on network coverage, affordable devices and digital skills, which are more available to urban, younger and wealthier people [1], so rural, older or poorer groups may gain later or not at all, widening the gap [1]. **[2]**

**9.** By investing in education and skills so that the workforce can move into higher-value activities [1]; by funding research and innovation so that firms compete on quality and technology rather than low wages [1]; and by strengthening institutions, as South Korea did when it moved into high-value manufacturing [1]. **[3]**

**10.** Indicative content; award one mark per valid, developed point up to 10:

- Defines development and integration into the global economy [1].
- For: export-led growth has raised incomes rapidly, as in East Asia [1].
- For: foreign investment brings jobs, technology and skills [1].
- For: technology flows enable leapfrogging, such as mobile money [1].
- Against: dependency theory argues integration can lock countries into low-value production [1].
- Against: exposure to price shocks and competition makes gains fragile [1].
- Against: gains may be spatially concentrated, widening inequality [1].
- Alternative routes: education, health, governance and domestic investment may matter as much [1].
- Uses accurate located evidence [1].
- Conclusion: a supported judgement, for example that integration is effective only where domestic conditions allow a country to capture value [1]. **[10]**

**11.** Indicative content; award one mark per valid, developed point up to 10:

- Location: rural and remote areas often lack infrastructure, jobs and services [1].
- Gender: women may face barriers to education, land ownership and finance [1].
- Age: older people may lack digital skills; young people may lack work experience [1].
- Ethnicity, caste or indigenous status can lead to discrimination in jobs and services [1].
- Income: poorer households cannot afford education, devices or migration costs [1].
- Disability can limit access to work and services [1].
- Legal status: informal workers and undocumented migrants may be excluded from protection [1].
- These factors overlap, so some people face several disadvantages at once [1].
- Uses accurate located examples [1].
- Conclusion: a supported judgement on which factors matter most in the context discussed [1]. **[10]**

*Examiner insight:* Unit 5 rewards answers that ask "development for whom?" Name the groups who gain and those who do not.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using income shares in the Gini calculation without cumulating them.
- Forgetting to multiply the area under the Lorenz curve by 2 before subtracting from 1.
- Presenting a theory without its criticism.
- Treating "people in a country" as one group.
