## Answers

**1.** C [1]. Differences: C–Cl 0.6; N–H 0.8; O–H 1.2; S–H 0.4. *(Total: 1 mark)*

**2.** The power of an atom to attract electrons (the bonding pair in a covalent bond) to itself [1]. *(Total: 1 mark)*

**3.** Electronegativity decreases from Be (1.6) to Mg (1.3) to Ca (1.0) [1]. *(Total: 1 mark)*

**4.** Difference = 4.0 − 1.0 = 3.0 [1]; a large difference, so ionic [1]. *(Total: 2 marks)*

**5.** C is δ+ and O is δ− (O 3.4 is more electronegative than C 2.6) [1]. *(Total: 1 mark)*

**6.** The Pauling scale is a relative scale comparing atoms with each other, so the values are pure numbers [1]. *(Total: 1 mark)*

**7. (a)** Nuclear charge increases from 11 to 17 [1]. The bonding electrons are in the third shell throughout, with the same inner shells, so shielding is roughly constant [1]. Atomic radius decreases, so the bonding pair is closer to the nucleus and attracted more strongly [1].

**(b)** The ionisation energy dip arises because sulfur's electron is removed from a paired 3p orbital, where spin-pair repulsion makes it easier to remove [1]. Electronegativity concerns the attraction of the whole atom for a bonding pair, which follows the steady increase in nuclear charge and decrease in radius, not the arrangement of one electron [1]. *(Total: 5 marks)*

**8. (a)** Bromine has an extra occupied shell, so its atomic radius is larger and the bonding pair is further from the nucleus [1]. There is more shielding by inner shells [1]. These effects outweigh bromine's greater nuclear charge, so the bonding pair is attracted less strongly [1].

**(b)** Less polar [1]: the difference for H–I is 2.7 − 2.2 = 0.5, smaller than 3.2 − 2.2 = 1.0 for H–Cl [1]. *(Total: 5 marks)*

**9. (a)** NaF 3.1; MgCl₂ 1.9; SiCl₄ 1.3; PH₃ 0.0. Two marks for all four; one mark for two or three [2].

**(b)** NaF ionic; MgCl₂ ionic (difference close to 2, and Mg is a metal); SiCl₄ polar covalent; PH₃ almost non-polar covalent. Two marks for all four; one mark for two or three [2].

**(c)** P and H have the same electronegativity (2.2), so the bonding electrons are shared almost equally [1]. *(Total: 5 marks)*

**10. (a)** Oxygen has a greater nuclear charge (8 compared with 7) [1]. The bonding electrons are in the same shell with the same inner shell, so shielding is similar [1]. Oxygen's atomic radius is smaller, so it attracts the bonding pair more strongly [1].

**(b)** Oxygen's atom is smaller, with its bonding electrons in the second shell rather than the third [1]. Oxygen has fewer inner shells, so less shielding [1]. These outweigh sulfur's greater nuclear charge, so oxygen attracts the bonding pair more strongly [1]. *(Total: 6 marks)*

**11. (a)** Electronegativity is not an energy and does not involve gaining an electron to form an ion [1]; it is the power of an atom to attract electrons (the bonding pair) to itself [1].

**(b)** Shielding stays roughly constant across a period [1]; the increase is due to increasing nuclear charge and decreasing atomic radius [1].

**(c)** Difference 3.0 − 0.8 = 2.2, a large difference, so ionic bonding [1]; X⁺Y⁻, formula XY [1]. *(Total: 6 marks)*

## Where marks are usually lost

- Confusing electronegativity with an energy change such as electron affinity.
- Explaining period trends with shielding instead of nuclear charge.
- Explaining group trends with nuclear charge alone.
- Not showing the subtraction when predicting bond type.
- Putting δ− on the wrong atom.
- Treating a difference of about 2 as an exact dividing line instead of reasoning with the values.

## Frequently asked questions

**How do I decide if a bond is ionic or covalent from electronegativity?**
Subtract the two Pauling values. A large difference (roughly 2 or more) points to ionic bonding; a smaller difference to covalent bonding, more polar as the difference grows. There is no sharp cut-off.

**Why does electronegativity decrease down a group even though nuclear charge increases?**
Each extra shell makes the atom larger and adds shielding, so the bonding electrons are further from the nucleus and feel less attraction. These effects outweigh the extra protons.
