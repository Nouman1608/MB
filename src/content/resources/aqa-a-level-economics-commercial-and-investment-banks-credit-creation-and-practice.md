## Questions

**1.** Distinguish between a commercial bank and an investment bank. **[2]**

**2.** Bank R is an invented commercial bank. Its balance sheet (£m) shows assets of reserves 30, short-term lending to other banks 50, bonds 120, loans 280 and other assets 20. Its liabilities are customer deposits 380 and wholesale borrowing 70. The remaining item is capital.

**(a)** Explain whether customer deposits are an asset or a liability of Bank R. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate Bank R's capital. **[2]**

**3.** Explain two conflicts between the objectives of a commercial bank. **[4]**

**4.** A bank receives a new deposit of £200m. It keeps 20% liquid and lends the rest, and each loan is spent and redeposited in a bank that behaves the same way.

**(a)** Calculate the amount lent in the first and second rounds. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why the amount lent falls with each round. **[2]**

**5.** Explain how a bank that carries out both retail and investment banking might increase systemic risk. **[6]**

**6.** Bank S has liquid assets of £35m and £220m of deposits repayable on demand. Most of its assets are long-term loans.

**(a)** Calculate the shortfall if 20% of those deposits are withdrawn. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain why this shortfall might lead to failure, referring to borrowing short and lending long. **[3]**

**7.** Bank T has assets of £400m, of which £240m are loans, and capital of £30m. Five per cent of its loans default and nothing is recovered.

**(a)** Calculate Bank T's capital after the defaults. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage fall in the value of its assets that would eliminate its capital. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain why a bank with more capital is less likely to fail. **[2]**

**8.** State the main role of each of the PRA, the FPC and the FCA in the UK. **[3]**

**9.** Bank U has £50m to invest. Option 1 is safe and returns 4%. Option 2 has a 55% chance of a 14% return and a 45% chance of a 12% loss.

**(a)** Calculate the expected return, in £m, of each option if Bank U bears its own losses. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate Option 2's expected return if a rescue means Bank U never makes a loss. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain what your answers show about moral hazard. **[2]**

**10.** Assess whether higher liquidity and capital requirements are enough to keep a banking system stable. **[9]**

**11.** In Country B (invented), a large bank fails. Bank lending to firms falls by £6bn and investment falls by the same amount. The multiplier is 1.4 and GDP was £300bn.

**(a)** Calculate the fall in aggregate demand, in £bn and as a percentage of GDP. **[3]**
**(b)** Evaluate the likely effects on the real economy of the failure of a large bank. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** A commercial bank takes deposits and lends to households and firms [1]. An investment bank serves large clients, for example advising on takeovers, raising finance or trading assets, earning fees and trading income [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* "Distinguish" needs both sides stated. A description of only one type of bank leaves the comparison incomplete.

**2. (a)** A liability [1], because Bank R owes the money back to depositors on demand [1].
**(b)** Total assets = 30 + 50 + 120 + 280 + 20 = £500m [1]. Capital = 500 − 380 − 70 = **£50m** [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A reason is needed after "liability". Saying that deposits are money held at the bank, not owed by it, is a common error.

**3.** Liquidity v profitability: cash and liquid assets earn little, so holding more of them lowers profit [1]; long loans earn more but cannot be turned into cash quickly [1]. Security v profitability: safe borrowers pay lower interest, so a safer loan book earns less [1]; risky lending earns more but risks default and failure [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Each conflict needs both objectives named and the reason they pull apart. Two conflicts that are really the same pair add little, so choose two different pairs.

**4. (a)** Round 1: 0.80 × 200 = **£160m** [1]. Round 2: 0.80 × 160 = **£128m** [1].
**(b)** In each round the bank keeps back 20% of the new deposit [1], so the amount available to lend shrinks each time, towards zero [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* The question asks for rounds, not a multiplier, and a multiplier calculation is not required. Show the working for each round.

**5.** Systemic risk is the danger that trouble at one institution spreads through the system [1]. Trading losses in the investment arm reduce the shared capital [1], which threatens retail deposits [1]. Ordinary savers and the payment system are then exposed [1]. Because the bank is large and interconnected, its failure would hit other banks [1], so the government may feel it must rescue it, creating moral hazard and a cost to taxpayers [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* A chain from cause to consequence gains credit. Listing "bigger, riskier" without linking the two arms of the bank does not.

**6. (a)** Withdrawals = 0.20 × 220 = £44m [1]. Liquid assets = £35m [1]. Shortfall = 44 − 35 = **£9m** [1].
**(b)** Deposits can be withdrawn immediately, but loans are long-term [1], so the bank must sell loans quickly, probably below their value (about £10m of loans sold at 90p yields £9m) [1]. The losses reduce capital, and if depositors fear failure the run grows and the bank may become insolvent [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the withdrawal figure before the subtraction. A shortfall stated alone does not show how you reached it.

**7. (a)** Loss = 0.05 × 240 = £12m [1]. Capital = 30 − 12 = **£18m** [1].
**(b)** 30 ÷ 400 = **7.5%** [1].
**(c)** Capital absorbs losses before depositors and other creditors are affected [1]. A larger cushion means a bigger fall in asset values is needed before the bank is insolvent [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Part (c) needs the mechanism: losses fall on capital first. "Capital makes banks safer" is an assertion, not an explanation.

**8.** PRA: supervises individual firms such as banks and sets standards on capital and liquidity [1]. FPC: monitors and acts to reduce risks to the financial system as a whole [1]. FCA: regulates conduct, such as treatment of customers and the working of markets [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Match each body to a different focus (firm, system, conduct). Describing all three as "regulating banks" does not show the difference between them.

**9. (a)** Option 1: 0.04 × 50 = **£2.0m** [1]. Option 2: 0.55 × 7 + 0.45 × (−6) = 3.85 − 2.70 [1] = **£1.15m** [1].
**(b)** 0.55 × 7 + 0.45 × 0 = **£3.85m** [1].
**(c)** Without a rescue, Option 1 is better (2.0 against 1.15) [1]. With a rescue, Option 2 becomes better (3.85), so the bank takes more risk because losses fall on someone else [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Moral hazard needs the comparison before and after the rescue, and a statement of who bears the loss.

**10.** Knowledge: higher liquidity requirements reduce the risk of runs [1], and higher capital requirements increase the cushion against losses [1]. Analysis: banks can then survive withdrawals and defaults without selling assets at a loss, so the chain to a credit crunch is less likely [1]. Banks are also less likely to take risks when more of their own capital is at stake, which reduces moral hazard [1]. Further analysis: lower failure risk reduces contagion between banks [1]. Evaluation: requirements raise banks' costs, so lending may fall or become dearer, which slows growth [1]. They do not remove confidence-driven runs or herd behaviour, and banks may hold risky assets that meet the rules [1]. Supervision by the PRA, system-wide action by the FPC and conduct rules also matter [1]. Judgement: requirements are necessary but not sufficient, and their value depends on how well they are set and enforced [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* A judgement that names a condition ("sufficient only if...") is stronger than a repeat of earlier points.

**11. (a)** Fall in aggregate demand = 6 × 1.4 = **£8.4bn** [1]. Percentage = 8.4 ÷ 300 [1] = **2.8%** [1].
**(b)** Chain: failure reduces confidence and capital in other banks [1], so lending tightens and a credit crunch follows [1]. Firms cut investment [1], households reduce credit-financed spending [1], aggregate demand falls [1], output falls and unemployment rises [1]. Lower incomes cause defaults and weaken banks further [1]. Evaluation: the effect is smaller if firms find other finance, if regulators and the government act quickly, or if banks hold strong capital [1]. It is larger when banks are interconnected or confidence is slow to return, and the multiplier may be bigger or smaller than assumed [1]. **[12]**
*Examiner insight:* Use Country B's figures in part (a) and refer back to them in part (b). Generic points that ignore the data are less convincing.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Putting deposits on the asset side of a balance sheet.
- Giving a result without the calculation step that leads to it.
- Mixing up liquidity (meeting payments) and capital (absorbing losses).
- Writing "moral hazard" without saying who bears the cost.
- Naming the PRA, FPC and FCA but not giving each a distinct role.
- Treating a credit multiplier calculation as required.
- Writing a one-sided answer to an assess or evaluate question.
- Stopping at bank failure without a chain to the real economy.
