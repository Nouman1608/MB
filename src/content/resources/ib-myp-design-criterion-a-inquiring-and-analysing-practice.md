This practice set covers Criterion A -- Inquiring and analysing for IB MYP Design, aligned to the International Baccalaureate Organization, Middle Years Programme Subject Brief -- *Design*, from 2014. It suits MYP years 4 and 5 (no SL/HL split). MYP has no prescribed content list; schools design their own units, and this page trains a skill named by one of the brief's four assessment criteria.

Every question trains Criterion A. Real MYP work is judged against criterion level descriptors (achievement levels 1-8), so the `[1]` points below are a revision aid, not IB marks. All people, organisations, products and data are fictional.

## Questions

**1.** (Criterion A) State the difference between primary and secondary research, and give one example of each for a digital design project. **[3]**

**2.** (Criterion A) Classify each activity as primary or secondary research: (i) timing how long users take to find a menu option; (ii) reading a published guide to colour contrast on screens; (iii) interviewing a shop owner; (iv) reading customer reviews of a rival product; (v) weighing the items a client carries; (vi) using a table of hand sizes from a reference book. **[3]**

**3.** (Criterion A) Staff at a fictional library, Elmfield Library, want a better way to run their weekly toddler story session. Distinguish between the client and the target audience here. **[3]**

**4.** (Criterion A) A student surveyed 64 Year 6 pupils who will join a fictional secondary school next year. 40 said they worried about getting lost in the new building. 56 said they can use a tablet at home.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage who worried about getting lost. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage who can use a tablet at home. **[1]**
**(c)** Use these results to justify a digital map guide for new pupils. **[3]**

**5.** (Criterion A) A student is designing a bird feeder for a fictional school garden, where squirrels empty the current feeder within a day. Research questions:
P: What colours do students like?
Q: How do squirrels reach the current feeder?
R: What materials resist the weather outdoors?
S: Where in the garden can the feeder be mounted?
Put the questions in priority order and justify your top two and your last. **[4]**

**6.** (Criterion A) A student is analysing three fictional device-charging stations for a school library.

| Station | Price | Devices charged at once | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| P | £72 | 8 | Open shelves; cables tangle |
| Q | £150 | 12 | Lockable doors; needs wall fixing |
| R | £44 | 4 | Compact; single plug |

**(a)** Calculate the cost per device slot for each station. **[2]**
**(b)** The librarian says devices must be secure while unattended. Analyse which features could inspire a solution and which should be avoided. **[3]**

**7.** (Criterion A) Eight users tried a fictional school club-finder app. Their times to find the chess club, in seconds, were: 42, 75, 38, 120, 55, 61, 47, 90.

**(a)** Calculate the mean and the range. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain what these results suggest about the need for a redesign. **[2]**

**8.** (Criterion A) A student's design brief reads: "I am going to design a pencil case. It will be blue, 200 mm long and cost under £4. Everyone needs a pencil case."
Identify three weaknesses and rewrite the brief. Invent plausible fictional evidence where needed. **[6]**

**9.** (Criterion A -- extended) A fictional food bank, Northgate Pantry, wants a digital solution so volunteers know which shelves need restocking. Volunteers now walk round with paper lists.
Produce a prioritised research plan with four research questions. For each, state primary or secondary, the method or source, and what the answer will decide. **[8]**

**10.** (Criterion A -- extended) Research for a fictional client, Mrs Adeyemi, who sells fruit from a market stall:
- Observation: she moves 14 crates each day; set-up took 25 minutes on both days observed.
- Interview: fruit bruises when crates are stacked on her barrow; she dislikes lifting crates above chest height.
- Survey: 10 of 16 traders at the market (62.5%) said stacking damaged their stock.
- Existing products: a folding stacking crate saves space but crushes soft fruit; a shelved market trolley protects fruit but will not fit in her van.

Write a design brief for this situation and show how each part is supported by the research. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** Primary research is collected first-hand by you for this project [1]. Secondary research has already been collected or published by someone else [1]. Examples: primary -- a questionnaire of the app's intended users; secondary -- published accessibility guidance on screen text size [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Examples must fit the context; "survey" with no digital link is too vague.

**2.** Primary: (i), (iii), (v). Secondary: (ii), (iv), (vi). One mark for each pair correct: (i) and (ii) [1]; (iii) and (iv) [1]; (v) and (vi) [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* The test is who collected the data, not where you read it; reviews found online are secondary, while your own online questionnaire would be primary.

**3.** The client is the library staff, such as the person who runs the session, whom you can consult [1]. The target audience is the wider group who use the solution: the toddlers and the adults who bring them [1]. The client can give feedback during design, but the audience's needs must be researched too, as they may differ [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Name the client precisely; "the library" or "people" gives nobody specific to interview.

**4. (a)** 40 ÷ 64 × 100 = **62.5%** [1]
**(b)** 56 ÷ 64 × 100 = **87.5%** [1]
**(c)** 40 of the 64 pupils surveyed (62.5%) worry about getting lost, so the need is shared by most of the audience [1]. Links the need to a consequence, such as lateness or anxiety in the first weeks [1]. 56 of 64 (87.5%) can use a tablet at home, so a digital guide reaches most users, though 8 pupils would need another format [1].
*Examiner insight:* Percentages without the sample size, or data without saying what it shows, read as description rather than justification.

**5.** Order: Q, S, R, P [1]. Q first: how squirrels reach the feeder is the core problem; a feeder that fails here fails overall [1]. S second: the mounting place sets size, fixing and the routes squirrels can take [1]. P last: colour changes appearance, not whether the feeder works [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Priority must be justified by what each answer decides; an order with no reasons earns little, even if it is sensible.

**6. (a)** P: 72 ÷ 8 = **£9.00** per slot; Q: 150 ÷ 12 = **£12.50**; R: 44 ÷ 4 = **£11.00**. Method [1]; all three correct [1].
**(b)** Q's lockable doors meet the security need, so locking is a feature to adapt [1]. P has the lowest cost per slot, but open shelves leave devices unsecured and tangled cables should be avoided [1]. Conclusion: aim for Q's security at nearer P's cost per slot; R's capacity of 4 is too small, and wall fixing should be avoided if the library cannot drill [1].
*Examiner insight:* Analysis earns credit when each feature is judged against the client's stated need and ends in a conclusion; a price table alone is description.

**7. (a)** Sum = 528 s, so mean = 528 ÷ 8 = **66 s** [1]. Range = 120 − 38 = **82 s** [1].
**(b)** A mean of over a minute to find one club suggests slow navigation for a simple task [1]. The wide range shows users differ, and 4 of 8 took over 60 s, so it is not one slow user; watching the slowest users would show where they got stuck [1].
*Examiner insight:* Give units and interpret the spread as well as the average; a mean alone can hide that half the users struggled.

**8.** Weaknesses: it starts from a chosen product, not a problem or client [1]; it gives no evidence of need ("everyone needs" is unsupported) [1]; it lists measurable requirements that belong in the specification [1].
Rewrite, for example: "Design a solution for Year 7 pupils at our school who lose equipment between lessons. In a survey, 21 of 30 Year 7 pupils (70%) had lost a pen or ruler in the last week, and observation showed cases left open in bags. Zip cases are slow to open in short lessons; pen pots do not fit in bags. The solution should keep equipment together and be quick to open at a desk." Names audience and problem [1]; evidence with sample size [1]; a product-analysis finding and a goal, with no specification details [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Keep the numbers that prove the need; requirement numbers belong in the Criterion B specification.

**9.** Model plan:

| Priority | Research question | Type | Method or source | Decides |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | How is stock checked now, and where does it go wrong? | Primary | Observe a shift; interview the coordinator | Which problem to fix first |
| 2 | What devices do volunteers have and how confident are they? | Primary | Volunteer questionnaire | Phone, shared tablet or mixed format |
| 3 | How do existing stock tools work? | Secondary | Public pages of three existing tools | Features to adapt or avoid |
| 4 | What makes screens easy to read for all users? | Secondary | Published accessibility guidance | Text size and contrast |

Four relevant questions [1] [1]; correct primary/secondary labels [1]; a suitable method or source for each [1] [1]; what each answer decides [1] [1]; a justified order, current process and users first [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* A plan is judged on whether its questions would inform the design; methods with no questions or no stated purpose cannot show planning.

**10.** Model brief: "Design a solution that lets Mrs Adeyemi move and set up 14 crates of fruit at her stall without stacking soft fruit or lifting crates above chest height. Set-up took 25 minutes on both days observed, and bruising from stacking is her main complaint; 10 of 16 traders surveyed (62.5%) report the same damage. Folding crates save space but crush fruit; a shelved trolley protects fruit but will not fit in her van. The solution should protect fruit, speed up set-up and fit in her van."

Support:
- Client and problem (bruising) named [1], taken from the interview [1].
- Lifting height included as a user need from the interview [1].
- Scale of the task from observation: 14 crates, 25 minutes [1].
- Need shown beyond one person: 10 of 16 traders (62.5%) [1].
- Crate analysis: stacking crushes fruit, so avoid stacking [1].
- Trolley analysis: van size is a known limit [1].
- Goal stated as outcomes, not a chosen product [1].
- No specification details added [1].
- Every claim traceable to a named piece of research [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* Every sentence of a strong brief traces back to research; new, unsupported claims weaken it.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Starting with a product ("an app", "a box") instead of a problem and a client.
- Quoting percentages without the sample size, or data without interpreting it.
- Research plans made of methods only, with no questions, purpose or priority.
- Calling anything found online "primary" research.
- Product analysis that describes features but never relates them to the client.
- Leaving out units, or ignoring a wide range.
- Briefs that ignore the research, or are stuffed with specification measurements.
