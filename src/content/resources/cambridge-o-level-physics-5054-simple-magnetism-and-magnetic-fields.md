## Poles and the forces between them

Every magnet has two poles, a **north-seeking (N) pole** and a
**south-seeking (S) pole**, where its magnetic effect is strongest. The
rule is short:

- **like poles repel** (N–N or S–S)
- **unlike poles attract** (N–S)

A **magnetised** object has poles of its own. An **unmagnetised** piece of
magnetic material, such as an iron nail, has no poles of its own but is
**attracted by either pole** of a magnet.

That difference gives the standard test, which is worth learning as a
model answer.

**Model answer: how can you tell whether a steel bar is a magnet?**
"Bring each end of the bar in turn up to the N pole of a known magnet. If
one end is **repelled**, the bar is a magnet, because only a magnet has a
pole that can repel. Attraction alone proves nothing, since a magnet
attracts unmagnetised magnetic material as well as an unlike pole."

Repulsion is the only sure test. Examiners often look for that reason
explicitly.

## Induced magnetism

When a piece of unmagnetised magnetic material is placed near or touching
a magnet, it becomes a magnet itself. This is **induced magnetism**. The end
nearest the magnet becomes the **opposite** pole, which is why the material
is attracted.

A chain of iron paper clips hanging from the N pole of a bar magnet shows
it: the top of the first clip becomes an S pole, its lower end an N pole,
and that induces poles in the next clip, and so on. Remove the magnet and
soft-iron clips drop apart almost at once, because soft iron keeps very
little of its induced magnetism. Steel pins would stay weakly attached to
one another, because steel keeps its magnetism.

## Magnetic and non-magnetic materials

A **magnetic material** is one that is attracted by a magnet and can be
magnetised. The magnetic materials you need are **iron, steel, nickel and
cobalt**. **Non-magnetic** materials are not attracted and cannot be
magnetised: copper, aluminium, brass, gold, wood, plastic and glass are
common examples. A frequent trap is to call all metals magnetic; aluminium
cans and copper wire are not attracted.

## Soft iron and steel: temporary and permanent magnets

| Property | Soft iron (temporary magnet) | Steel (permanent magnet) |
|---|---|---|
| Ease of magnetising | Easily magnetised | Harder to magnetise |
| Keeps its magnetism? | Loses it almost entirely when the magnetising field is removed | Keeps it |
| Typical use | Cores of electromagnets, relays and transformers | Bar magnets, compass needles, magnets in motors and loudspeakers |

**Model answer: why is the core of an electromagnet made of soft iron?**
"Soft iron is easily magnetised, so it makes the magnetic field of the
coil much stronger, and it loses its magnetism as soon as the current is
switched off, so the electromagnet can be turned off." Both halves of that
answer are needed.

## Magnetic fields

A **magnetic field** is a **region in which a magnetic pole experiences a
force**. We picture the field with **field lines**:

- the **direction** of the field at a point is the direction of the force
  on an **N pole** placed there, so lines run **from N to S** outside a
  magnet
- the **spacing** of the lines shows the **relative strength**: lines close
  together mean a strong field, lines far apart a weak one
- field lines never cross

For a single bar magnet, the lines leave the N pole, curve round the sides
and enter the S pole. They are most crowded at the poles, where the field
is strongest, and spread out with distance. When drawing it, include
several lines on each side, arrows pointing from N to S on every line, and
closer spacing near the poles.

## Plotting the field

**Method 1: plotting compass.** This shows both the pattern and the
direction.

1. Place the bar magnet on a sheet of paper and draw round it.
2. Put a small plotting compass near the N pole. Mark a dot at the end the
   needle's N points to.
3. Move the compass so that the tail of the needle is over the dot, and
   mark a new dot at the needle's N end.
4. Repeat until the line reaches the S pole, then join the dots with a
   smooth curve and add an arrow in the direction the needle pointed.
5. Start again from a different point near the N pole to draw more lines.

The compass needle is itself a tiny magnet, and its N pole is pushed along
the direction of the field, which is why the compass also gives the field
direction.

**Method 2: iron filings.** Place a sheet of paper or card over the magnet,
sprinkle iron filings thinly over it and tap the card gently. Each filing
becomes a tiny induced magnet and lines up with the field. This shows the
pattern quickly, and where it is strong, but **not the direction**; a
compass is still needed for that.

## Uses of permanent magnets and electromagnets

An **electromagnet** is a coil of wire, usually wound on a soft-iron core,
that is magnetic only while a current flows. Its strength can be changed
by changing the current, and it can be switched off.

| Permanent magnets | Electromagnets |
|---|---|
| Compass needles | Cranes lifting scrap iron and steel, released by switching off |
| Fridge and cupboard door catches and seals | Relays, where a small current switches a larger one |
| Loudspeakers and microphones | Electric door locks and bells |
| Electric motors and generators | Separating magnetic metals from other waste |

**Model answer: why is an electromagnet used, not a permanent magnet, to
lift scrap cars?** "The electromagnet can be switched off to drop the
load, and its strength can be increased by increasing the current."

## Common mistakes

- Using attraction as proof that something is a magnet. Only repulsion
  proves it.
- Saying all metals are magnetic. Only iron, steel, nickel and cobalt are
  needed for 5054.
- Drawing field lines that cross, start in mid-air or lack arrows.
- Arrows pointing from S to N outside the magnet.
- Saying iron filings show field direction.
- Mixing up the materials: soft iron is temporary, steel is permanent.

## Exam technique for Cambridge command words

- **Describe** a test or plotting method: number the steps and say what is
  observed or marked at each step.
- **Explain** a choice of material: link a property (easily magnetised,
  keeps or loses magnetism) to what the device needs.
- **Draw** a field pattern: at least six lines, evenly drawn, arrows N to
  S, crowded at the poles.
- **State** a definition: use the syllabus wording — "a region in which a
  magnetic pole experiences a force".
