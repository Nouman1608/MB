These notes condense heading 8 *Contemporary issues* of Cambridge International A Level Sociology
(9699) Paper 4. The core argument in each sub-topic is the same: globalisation creates
opportunities but distributes them unequally. Life chances may rise or fall (8.1), migration has
winners and losers (8.2), and global crime benefits the powerful while victims are often poor,
female or both (8.3). Essays should always weigh optimistic against critical perspectives.

Full explanations: [global inequality, migration and crime study guide](/resources/a-level-cambridge-sociology-globalisation-contemporary-issues/).

## Syllabus coverage

9699 (2024–2026) Paper 4 Section A, heading 8: **8.1** Globalisation, poverty and inequalities;
**8.2** Globalisation and migration; **8.3** Globalisation and crime.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Life chances** | Opportunities for education, income, health and long life |
| **Transnational organisation** | Body operating across states, e.g. UN agencies, World Bank, IMF, international NGOs |
| **Dependency theory** | Poor countries are kept poor by exploitation by rich ones (Frank, 1966) |
| **Post-colonialism** | Continuing economic and cultural influence of former colonial powers |
| **Net migration** | Immigration minus emigration |
| **Push / pull factors** | Reasons for leaving / reasons for choosing a destination |
| **Remittances** | Money migrants send home |
| **Brain drain** | Emigration of skilled workers |
| **Human trafficking** | Recruitment or movement of people by force or deception for exploitation |
| **Corporate crime** | Crime by or for a company |
| **Green crime** | Crime and harm against the environment |
| **Global criminal economy** | Networked international crime (Castells, 1998) |

## 8.1 Poverty and inequalities

**Impact on life chances**

| Area | Positive | Negative |
|---|---|---|
| Education | Global goals expanded primary enrolment; online learning | Cuts linked to debt; girls still excluded in some regions |
| Income | Export jobs, investment, remittances | Low wages, insecure work; Rana Plaza (2013) |
| Health | Vaccines, global health programmes | Brain drain of doctors; pollution from relocated industry |

**Transnational organisations**
- Supporters: Sachs, *The End of Poverty* (2005) — aid and investment break poverty traps; MDGs
  (2000–2015), SDGs (2015–2030).
- Critics: Moyo, *Dead Aid* (2009) — aid encourages corruption and dependency; Marxists — IMF and
  World Bank conditions serve Western capital.

**Explanations of inequality**
- Capitalism — Wallerstein's core / semi-periphery / periphery.
- Colonialism — extraction of resources and labour.
- Post-colonialism — neo-colonial trade and debt relationships.
- Patriarchy — Mies (1986): women's unpaid and low-paid labour subsidises global capitalism.

## 8.2 Migration

**Causes:** push (poverty, war, persecution, climate disaster); pull (jobs, wages, safety, family);
global labour demand (care, construction, technology); tourism. Lee (1966) push–pull model;
Castles and Miller (1993): migration globalised, accelerated, feminised.

**Consequences**

| Positive | Negative |
|---|---|
| Cultural diversity | Concern over scarce resources (housing, health, schools) |
| Fills labour shortages | Exploitation of irregular migrants |
| Remittances to sending countries | Brain drain from sending countries |
| Entrepreneurship and innovation | Negative perceptions, racism, moral panics |

**Who benefits?** Functionalist/neo-liberal: mutual benefit. Marxist: migrants as a reserve army of
labour that lowers wages and divides workers. Feminist: global care chains (Hochschild).

## 8.3 Crime

**Why global crime emerged:** cheaper travel, global finance, internet, weak regulation in some
states, demand in rich countries.

| Crime | Example |
|---|---|
| Human trafficking | Defined by the UN Palermo Protocol (2000) |
| Corporate crime | Bhopal gas leak, Union Carbide, India (1984); Sutherland's white-collar crime (1939) |
| Environmental crime | Illegal logging, wildlife trade, toxic waste; Deepwater Horizon (2010); White (2008) |
| Organised crime | Castells (1998); Glenny, *McMafia* (2008) |

**Who benefits?** Marxist: corporations and elites profit and avoid prosecution. Feminist:
trafficking and exploitation of women reflect patriarchy.

**Policing:** Interpol, Europol, International Criminal Court (2002), digital forensics, financial
tracking. **Challenges:** jurisdiction, corruption, encryption, power of corporations.

## Worked essay plan

*Practice question:* "Global crime mainly benefits the powerful." Evaluate this view. [35]

1. Define global crime with examples (trafficking, corporate, green).
2. Marxist support: corporate and state-corporate crime; lenient treatment of powerful offenders.
3. Feminist support: trafficking and exploitation of women.
4. Against: many global crimes are committed by marginalised groups (drug couriers, small
   smugglers) who take high risks for little gain; some victims are the powerful (cyber fraud
   against corporations).
5. Policing: international cooperation is increasing, but enforcement favours prosecuting the weak.
6. Conclusion: the powerful usually gain most, but the picture varies by crime type.

## Common mistakes and exam traps

- Writing about "development" without linking it to **globalisation**.
- Treating migration as only negative or only positive; the syllabus asks for both and for "who
  benefits".
- Forgetting tourism and global labour patterns as causes of migration.
- Ignoring the **policing** bullet in 8.3.
- Using statistics you cannot source; use named events and studies instead.

## Quick self-test

1. What did Frank mean by "development of underdevelopment"?
2. Give one argument from Moyo (2009).
3. Define net migration.
4. Name one feminist argument about migration.
5. Give two challenges in prosecuting global crime.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need statistics?** Accurate, sourced figures help, but named examples (Rana Plaza, Bhopal,
the SDGs) are safer than half-remembered numbers.

**How do 8.1 and 7.1 connect?** The "who benefits" perspectives in 7.1 supply the theory for
8.1's inequality arguments.

**Which theories suit 8.3 best?** The syllabus names Marxist and feminist perspectives on who
benefits, so build essays around them.
