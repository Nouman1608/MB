## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Entente | Friendly understanding, not a binding alliance |
| Dreadnought | All-big-gun battleship that made older ships obsolete |
| Weltpolitik | German "world policy" of colonial and naval expansion |
| Blank cheque | Germany's promise of unconditional support to Austria, July 1914 |
| Schlieffen Plan | German plan to defeat France quickly through Belgium |
| Stalemate / deadlock | Neither side able to break through |
| Attrition | Wearing the enemy down by casualties |
| Convoy | Merchant ships sailing together under naval escort |
| ANZAC | Australian and New Zealand Army Corps |

## Why deadlock? A five-point answer

1. Defensive firepower — machine guns, artillery, barbed wire.
2. Railways moved defending reserves faster than attackers could advance on foot.
3. Communication broke down once troops left their trenches.
4. New weapons (gas, early tanks) were unreliable and both sides adapted.
5. Generals' tactics — long bombardments warned the enemy and churned up ground.

## Source skills for this option

- **Features question (6):** two features, each with supporting detail — e.g. two features of the
  Moroccan Crises or of life in the trenches.
- **Cross-reference (8):** find specific agreement *and* disagreement between two sources, then
  judge "how far". Use context: a 1916 newspaper under censorship will play down losses.
- **Interpretation (16):** test the historian's view against the sources and your own knowledge,
  e.g. whether Haig was responsible for the losses on the Somme.

## Worked example: cross-referencing

*Two invented sources:* Source A, a British general's diary, says the first day of the Somme
"went well in places, though losses were heavy". Source B, a soldier's later memoir, says "whole
battalions were cut down before they reached the wire".

**Agreement:** both accept heavy losses. **Disagreement:** A suggests partial success; B suggests
total failure. **Context:** the general's position and the date of writing explain the guarded tone,
while own knowledge (about 57,000 British casualties on 1 July 1916) supports B on scale but not
on the whole front — some southern sectors did advance. **Judgement:** partly supports.

## Common mistakes and exam traps

- **Writing about usefulness or reliability on its own.** The 8-mark question is about how far one
  source supports another.
- **Treating the alliances as automatic.** Britain was not bound to fight in 1914; Belgium was the
  trigger for its entry.
- **Ignoring the Balkans.** The war began in the Balkans; know 1908–09 and 1912–13.
- **Seeing Jutland as a German victory.** Tactically perhaps, but strategically Britain kept control
  of the North Sea.
- **Forgetting the home front's role in 1918.** The blockade and unrest in Germany help explain defeat.

## Frequently asked questions

**What was the main cause of the war?**
There is no single answer; build an argument linking long-term
rivalry to the July Crisis decisions.

**Why did Gallipoli fail?**
Poor planning, delays that let the Ottomans prepare, difficult terrain and
strong Turkish defence.

**Why did the USA join in 1917?**
Unrestricted submarine warfare and the Zimmermann Telegram.
