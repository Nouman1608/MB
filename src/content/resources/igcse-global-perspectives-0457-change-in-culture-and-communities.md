This guide covers the topic **Change in culture and communities** from the Cambridge IGCSE Global Perspectives (0457) syllabus for examination in 2025, 2026 and 2027, Version 2 (published April 2025). It draws on section 2 (assessment objectives), section 3 (the topic list and key terms) and section 4 (details of the assessment and command words). The topic list is used for all three components: Component 1 Written Exam, Component 2 Individual Report and Component 3 Team Project.

**Read this first.** The syllabus states plainly that knowledge of content is not assessed. Nobody will mark you on what you know about festivals, languages or neighbourhoods. This page shows you how to use the topic to practise the skills that *are* assessed: research, analysis and evaluation, reflection, communication and collaboration.

Use it with the [revision notes for this topic](/resources/igcse-global-perspectives-0457-change-in-culture-and-communities-revision-notes/), the [practice questions for this topic](/resources/igcse-global-perspectives-0457-change-in-culture-and-communities-practice/), the [IGCSE Global Perspectives hub](/boards/cambridge/igcse/global-perspectives/) and the [0457 skills checklist](/checklists/cambridge/igcse/global-perspectives/).

## Issues at global, national and local level

The syllabus defines an **issue** as "an important subject or problem for discussion". A topic is broad. An issue is narrower and can be argued about. "Change in culture and communities" is the topic. "Should governments fund the teaching of minority languages?" is an issue.

Change in culture and communities covers how the ways people live, speak, celebrate, believe and belong change over time, and how groups of people (communities) form, change or break apart.

| Level | Example issues that divide opinion |
|---|---|
| Global | The decline of smaller languages as a few global languages spread; whether global media and brands make cultures more alike; how much traditional knowledge and practice should be protected |
| National | Which languages schools should teach in; whether public money should support traditional crafts, music or festivals; how a country should mark its history in public places |
| Local | A traditional market or meeting place closing; young people leaving a village; a neighbourhood changing as rents rise; a local festival becoming commercial |

Notice that many issues work at more than one level. Language decline is global, but it is felt in one family when a grandparent and grandchild no longer share a first language. Strong answers move between levels.

## Perspectives: who sees the change differently and why

The syllabus defines a **perspective** as "a viewpoint on an issue based on evidence and reasoning". A perspective is not just a feeling. It has reasons behind it.

Cultural change creates winners and losers, so perspectives differ sharply. For a single change, such as a historic market closing, you might find:

- **Long-standing residents** who value the market as a meeting place and part of the town's identity.
- **Younger residents** who prefer convenience, online shopping or new venues.
- **Traders** whose income depends on what happens.
- **The local council**, balancing budgets, traffic and public opinion.
- **Newcomers to the area**, who may want both the old and the new.
- **Cultural or heritage organisations**, arguing that some traditions are worth protecting for their own sake.

Ask *why* each group holds its view. The usual drivers are **values** (what people think matters), **interests** (what they gain or lose) and **experience** (what they have lived through). On contested matters such as language, religion or identity, describe each perspective fairly and in its own terms. The syllabus wants you to consider perspectives "objectively and with empathy" (AO2).

**Worked example (analysing a perspective).** *Knowledge:* An older resident says the closure of the town's tea house means "neighbours stop knowing each other". *Application:* This is a perspective based on a value, community contact, and on personal experience of meeting people there. *Analysis:* Her reasoning links a physical place to social ties: if the place goes, the ties weaken. *Evaluation:* The link is plausible, but she gives no evidence that people have stopped meeting elsewhere, for example online or at the new café, so her claim is reasonable but not yet supported.

## Causes and consequences

AO1 asks you to research issues, "their causes and consequences". In this topic, common causes of cultural and community change include:

- **Globalised media and technology** – films, music and social media cross borders quickly.
- **Movement of people** – people moving between countries or from villages to cities bring and meet new customs.
- **Economic change** – shops, jobs and rents change who can live in an area and how they spend time.
- **Government policy** – decisions about schooling, planning and funding.
- **Tourism** – visitors can fund traditions and also change them.

(Other topics on the list, such as Migration and urbanisation or Digital world, look at some of these causes in their own right. Here, focus on what they do to culture and community life.)

Consequences can be positive, negative or both, and they fall differently on different groups. A new shopping centre may bring jobs (economic consequence) but empty a traditional market (cultural consequence). Mixing of cultures can create new music, food and language, which some people welcome as creativity and others see as loss.

Build **chains**, not lists. "Rents rise → long-standing families move out → fewer children attend the local language class → the class closes" shows analysis. Four separate bullet points do not.

**Worked example (cause and consequence).** *Knowledge:* In a fictional village, the only bus to the nearest town has been cut. *Application:* Young people now move to the town to work. *Analysis:* As they leave, fewer people take part in the village's harvest festival, so older residents carry it alone. *Evaluation:* The bus cut is one cause, but the pull of jobs in the town would probably exist anyway, so it may speed up a change rather than create it.

## Judging sources and evidence

The syllabus says candidates "will benefit from an understanding of" eight terms. Learn them with examples from this topic.

| Term | Example from this topic |
|---|---|
| **Claim** | "Festivals are dying out in our region." |
| **Fact** | "The festival sold fewer tickets this year than in 2020." (can be checked) |
| **Opinion** | "The new festival music is terrible." |
| **Value** | "Tradition matters more than profit." |
| **Prediction** | "Within 20 years nobody here will speak the old dialect." |
| **Generalisation** | "Young people don't care about culture." |
| **Bias** | A report that only quotes people who agree with closing the market |
| **Vested interest** | A property developer funding a survey that supports building flats on a heritage site |

The syllabus also defines **evidence** as information that "helps to develop understanding or prove that something is true or false", and an **argument** as statements "containing reasons and evidence which support a claim".

When you evaluate a source, check:

1. **Who** produced it and whether they gain from a particular answer (vested interest).
2. **How** the evidence was gathered: sample size, who was asked, where and when.
3. **Whether** the conclusion follows. A small survey cannot prove what a whole generation thinks.
4. **Language**: words such as "dying", "destroyed" or "heart of the town" are emotive. They persuade, but they are not evidence.

Reasoning flaws to watch for in this topic: assuming old is always better (appeal to tradition), assuming new is always better (appeal to novelty), offering only two options when others exist (false dilemma), and treating one person's story as true for everyone (generalisation).

**Worked example (evaluating evidence).** *Knowledge:* An invented source says "A survey shows 80% of teenagers prefer global pop music to local music, so local music has no future." *Application:* The claim is a prediction based on a preference survey. *Analysis:* Preferring one kind of music does not mean rejecting the other; teenagers may enjoy both. *Evaluation:* We are not told who ran the survey, how many people answered or where, so the evidence is weak, and the conclusion goes far beyond it.

## Real-world examples you could research

These are accurate starting points for your own research. Remember: knowing them does not earn marks by itself. Use them to practise evaluating evidence and courses of action.

- **UNESCO Convention for the Safeguarding of the Intangible Cultural Heritage (2003).** An international agreement on protecting living traditions such as music, crafts and festivals. *Evaluation point:* critics and supporters disagree over whether listing a tradition protects it or freezes it and attracts tourism that changes it.
- **International Decade of Indigenous Languages (2022–2032),** proclaimed by the United Nations General Assembly. *Evaluation point:* a global awareness campaign depends on national and local action to make a difference.
- **Māori language revival in New Zealand.** The first kōhanga reo ("language nest" pre-schools) opened in 1982, and the Māori Language Act 1987 made Māori an official language. *Evaluation point:* this shows community-led and government action working together; judge their effects using evidence such as numbers of speakers over time.
- **Welsh language in Wales.** The Welsh Government's Cymraeg 2050 strategy (2017) set a target of a million Welsh speakers by 2050. *Evaluation point:* a target is a prediction or aim, not evidence of success; ask how speakers are counted.

## Courses of action and their effects

AO1 includes researching "possible course(s) of action". In Component 1, Question 4 asks you to "assess actions in response to the global issue". In the Individual Report you consider several and propose one.

Typical courses of action in this topic:

- **Record and document** – archives of songs, recipes or dialect words.
- **Teach and pass on** – language classes, school programmes, apprenticeships with craftspeople.
- **Create shared spaces** – community events where different generations or groups meet.
- **Fund and protect** – grants, heritage status, planning rules.
- **Adapt** – letting a tradition change so that new groups take part.

Judge each one against clear criteria: **effectiveness** (does it address the cause?), **feasibility** (cost, time, people), **who benefits and who loses**, **unintended consequences**, and **sustainability** (will it last once the funding or enthusiasm ends?). The syllabus notes that any topic can be considered with a focus on sustainability.

**Worked example (assessing an action).** *Knowledge:* A council proposes free evening classes in a fading local dialect. *Application:* This targets one cause, the lack of chances to learn it. *Analysis:* Adults who attend may use the dialect at home, so children hear it. *Evaluation:* But if few people attend, or if the dialect is never used at work or school, the classes may record it rather than keep it alive. Combined with use in local signs and events, the action would be more likely to work.

## Individual Report ideas

Component 2 needs a **global issue** that divides opinion and a **global research question** you write yourself, 1500–2000 words. Research local and/or national perspectives and global perspectives, analyse causes and consequences, consider courses of action and propose one.

Example questions (adapt; don't copy):

- Should minority languages be protected by law or left to change naturally?
- Is global popular culture making local cultures disappear?
- Should traditional festivals change to attract new audiences?
- Should cultural heritage sites put access for tourists ahead of use by local communities?

A good question names the issue, allows more than one answer and points to perspectives. "Culture" alone is a topic, not a question. Read [the Individual Report guide](/resources/igcse-cambridge-global-perspectives-individual-report/) and [Individual Report revision notes](/resources/global-perspectives-individual-report-revision-notes/) before you choose.

## Team Project ideas

Component 3 needs a **local issue**, chosen as a team of two to five. Each member researches a different aspect, such as a different perspective. You plan and carry out an action and measure its success.

Possible local issues and actions:

- Older and younger residents rarely meet → an event where elders teach a traditional skill. Measure with sign-ups and a short before-and-after survey.
- A local dialect or language is fading among children → a short illustrated phrasebook for a primary school. Measure with a quiz before and after.
- Newcomers to the area don't know local traditions → a welcome leaflet or guided walk. Measure with feedback forms.

Keep the action realistic. The syllabus says the teacher checks that a project is feasible and manageable. See the [Team Project guide](/resources/igcse-cambridge-global-perspectives-team-project/) and [Team Project revision notes](/resources/igcse-global-perspectives-team-project-revision-notes/).

## Common errors

- Writing what you know about a culture instead of using the source in front of you. Component 1 does not assess prior knowledge.
- Calling a view a "perspective" without saying whose it is and what reasons support it.
- Treating "change" as automatically good or bad. Show who gains and who loses.
- Spotting emotive language but not explaining how it affects the argument's strength.
- Calling a source "biased" without naming the interest or showing it in the text.
- Choosing an Individual Report issue that is local only; it must have global impact.
- Choosing a Team Project action you cannot measure.
- Giving a verdict on a contested cultural or religious practice as if it were fact, instead of weighing perspectives.
