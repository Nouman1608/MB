## Gas exchange in flowering plants (2.40B–2.45B, Paper 2 only)

### Diffusion does all the work (2.40B)

Plants have no lungs and no ventilation. Every gas that enters or leaves a leaf moves by **diffusion**: the net movement of particles from a region of higher concentration to a region of lower concentration. Carbon dioxide and oxygen pass through the **stomata**, spread through the **air spaces** inside the leaf, dissolve in the thin film of water on the surface of the mesophyll cells and then diffuse across the cell wall and cell membrane.

Diffusion only works because cells keep the gradient going. A photosynthesising cell uses carbon dioxide, so the concentration inside the leaf stays lower than in the air outside, and carbon dioxide keeps diffusing in.

### Two processes, opposite exchanges (2.41B)

Two processes in leaf cells exchange the same two gases in opposite directions:

| Process | When it happens | Gas taken in | Gas given out |
|---|---|---|---|
| Respiration | All the time, in every living cell | Oxygen | Carbon dioxide |
| Photosynthesis | Only in the light, in cells with chloroplasts | Carbon dioxide | Oxygen |

What you measure from a whole leaf is the **net** exchange: the result of both processes added together.

### Leaf structure (2.42B) and stomata (2.43B)

| Feature | How it helps gas exchange |
|---|---|
| Leaf is thin | Short diffusion distance from the air to every cell |
| Broad, flat shape | Large surface area for gases to enter |
| Spongy mesophyll with many air spaces | Gases spread quickly through the leaf; large internal surface area of moist cell walls |
| Many stomata, mostly in the lower epidermis | Openings that let gases in and out; fewer on the upper surface reduces water loss in direct sun |
| Moist cell surfaces | Gases dissolve before they diffuse into cells |

Each stoma is a pore between two **guard cells**, which change shape to open or close it. Stomata usually open in the light, letting carbon dioxide in for photosynthesis, and close in the dark or when the plant is short of water. Closing reduces water loss but also cuts the carbon dioxide supply. In this sub-topic, describe their role as letting gases diffuse in and out.

### Day, night and net exchange (2.44B)

Respiration never stops. Photosynthesis depends on light. So the net exchange changes with **light intensity**:

| Light intensity | Rates compared | Net exchange |
|---|---|---|
| Dark | Respiration only | Oxygen taken in, carbon dioxide given out |
| Dim (one particular intensity) | Photosynthesis = respiration | No net exchange of either gas |
| Bright | Photosynthesis > respiration | Carbon dioxide taken in, oxygen given out |

Where the two rates are equal, the carbon dioxide made by respiration is used at once by photosynthesis, and vice versa for oxygen. Both processes continue; the *net* exchange with the air is zero.

### Practical: hydrogen-carbonate indicator (2.45B)

Hydrogen-carbonate indicator changes colour with the carbon dioxide concentration of the air above it:

| Carbon dioxide compared with normal air | Indicator colour |
|---|---|
| Normal (atmospheric) level | Red |
| Higher | Orange, then yellow |
| Lower | Magenta, then purple |

**Method in outline**

1. Put the same volume of red indicator into several identical boiling tubes.
2. Place a leaf (or leaf discs of the same area) in each tube, held on gauze so it does not touch the liquid.
3. Seal each tube with a bung.
4. Cover one tube with foil (dark), one with layers of gauze or tissue (dim), and leave one uncovered (bright).
5. Set up a control tube with no leaf.
6. Keep all tubes the same distance from the lamp and at the same temperature, then compare colours after the same time.

**Worked example 1: interpreting the tubes**

After two hours the tubes look like this: foil-covered tube yellow; gauze-covered tube red; uncovered tube purple; no-leaf control red.

- Foil: in the dark the leaf only respires. Carbon dioxide is added to the air in the tube, so the indicator turns **yellow**.
- Gauze: respiration and photosynthesis happen at the same rate. There is no net change in carbon dioxide, so the indicator stays **red**.
- Uncovered: photosynthesis is faster than respiration. Carbon dioxide is removed, so the indicator turns **purple**.
- Control: it stays **red**, which shows the colour changes were caused by the leaf and not by light or the tube itself.

## Gas exchange in humans (2.46–2.50)

### Structure of the thorax (2.46)

Air enters through the nose and mouth and passes down the **trachea**. The trachea divides into two **bronchi** (one to each lung). Each bronchus divides into many smaller **bronchioles**, which end in clusters of air sacs called **alveoli**, where gas exchange happens.

The lungs sit inside the **ribcage**. The **intercostal muscles** run between the ribs. The **diaphragm** is a sheet of muscle that separates the thorax from the abdomen. Two **pleural membranes** line the outside of the lungs and the inside of the thorax, with a thin layer of fluid between them. The fluid reduces friction as the lungs move during breathing.

### Ventilation (2.47)

Ventilation moves air in and out of the lungs. Air always moves from higher pressure to lower pressure.

| Step | Breathing in (inhalation) | Breathing out (exhalation) |
|---|---|---|
| External intercostal muscles | Contract | Relax |
| Ribcage | Moves up and out | Moves down and in |
| Diaphragm | Contracts and flattens | Relaxes and domes upwards |
| Volume of thorax | Increases | Decreases |
| Pressure inside thorax | Falls below atmospheric pressure | Rises above atmospheric pressure |
| Air movement | Air is drawn in | Air is forced out |

When you breathe out hard (during exercise, for example) the internal intercostal muscles also contract to pull the ribs down faster.

Write the chain in order: muscles, volume, pressure, air movement.

### Alveoli (2.48)

Oxygen diffuses from the air in the alveoli into the blood in the capillaries. Carbon dioxide diffuses the other way. Each feature below increases the rate of that diffusion:

- **Very many alveoli**, giving a very large surface area.
- **Walls one cell thick**, and capillary walls one cell thick, so the diffusion distance is very short.
- **A dense network of capillaries** around each alveolus. Blood flow carries oxygen away and brings carbon dioxide, keeping a steep concentration gradient.
- **Ventilation** brings fresh air in and removes stale air, which also keeps the gradient steep.
- **A moist lining**, so gases dissolve before diffusing across.

Always link the feature to the effect: "thin walls give a short diffusion distance, so diffusion is faster".

### Smoking (2.49)

Tobacco smoke harms both the lungs and the circulatory system.

| Substance or effect | What it does |
|---|---|
| Tar | Contains carcinogens, which increase the risk of lung cancer. Irritates the airways. |
| Damage to cilia and extra mucus | Mucus with trapped dirt and bacteria is not swept away, so it builds up. This causes a smoker's cough and more chest infections, including bronchitis. |
| Emphysema | Walls of alveoli break down, forming fewer, larger air spaces. The surface area for gas exchange falls, so less oxygen enters the blood and the person becomes breathless. |
| Carbon monoxide | Combines with haemoglobin in red blood cells more readily than oxygen does, so the blood carries less oxygen. |
| Nicotine | Addictive. Raises heart rate and narrows blood vessels, increasing blood pressure. |

**Coronary heart disease.** The coronary arteries supply the heart muscle with oxygen and glucose. Smoking increases the risk that fatty deposits build up in their walls, narrowing them. A blood clot can then block a narrowed artery. The heart muscle beyond the blockage gets no oxygen, cannot respire aerobically, and may die: a heart attack. Carbon monoxide makes things worse because the blood that does reach the heart carries less oxygen.

### Practical: investigating breathing (2.50)

**Carbon dioxide release.** Air is breathed in through one tube of limewater and out through another, using a two-tube apparatus. The limewater that exhaled air bubbles through turns **cloudy (milky)** much sooner, which shows exhaled air contains more carbon dioxide. As a rough guide, inhaled air is about 21% oxygen and 0.04% carbon dioxide; exhaled air is about 16% oxygen and 4% carbon dioxide, and it is warmer and holds more water vapour.

**Effect of exercise.** Count breaths per minute at rest, do a set exercise (for example, step-ups for two minutes), then count again straight away. Keep the type, length and intensity of exercise the same, and repeat to get a mean.

**Worked example 2: breathing-rate data**

A student counts breaths for one minute at rest three times: 14, 15 and 16. After two minutes of skipping the counts are 26, 29 and 29. Calculate the percentage increase in mean breathing rate.

```
Mean at rest        = (14 + 15 + 16) / 3 = 15 breaths per minute
Mean after exercise = (26 + 29 + 29) / 3 = 28 breaths per minute
Increase            = 28 - 15 = 13
Percentage increase = 13 / 15 × 100 = 86.7%
```

**Explanation.** During exercise muscles respire faster, so they need more oxygen and produce more carbon dioxide. Breathing faster and deeper increases ventilation, which keeps the concentration gradients at the alveoli steep, so more oxygen diffuses into the blood and more carbon dioxide is removed.

If you count for 30 seconds, double the count to get breaths per minute: 8 breaths in 30 s is 16 per minute.

## Common errors

- Saying plants respire only at night. Respiration happens all the time; at night it is the only process.
- Saying "no gas exchange" at the compensation intensity. Write "no **net** gas exchange".
- Stating hydrogen-carbonate colours the wrong way round. Yellow means **more** carbon dioxide; purple means **less**.
- Leaving out a no-leaf control or forgetting to keep temperature constant in the indicator practical.
- Describing inhalation as "lungs expand, so air rushes in" without the muscles, volume and pressure steps.
- Writing "the diaphragm relaxes and flattens" during inhalation. It **contracts** and flattens.
- Listing alveolus features without saying how each one speeds up diffusion.
- Saying carbon monoxide "blocks the lungs". It reduces the oxygen-carrying capacity of the blood.
- Forgetting that emphysema reduces **surface area**, not just "damages the lungs".
