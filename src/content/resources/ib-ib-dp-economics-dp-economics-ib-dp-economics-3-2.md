This guide goes deeper on sub-topic **3.2 Variations in economic activity: aggregate demand and aggregate supply** in Unit 3 of the IB Diploma Programme *Economics guide* (first assessment 2022). It is SL and HL content. The [measuring and managing the economy guide](/resources/ib-dp-economics-macroeconomics-measuring-and-managing-the-economy/) introduces the AD-AS model; this page covers each determinant, solves an AD-AS equilibrium from a table, and compares how the monetarist/new classical and Keynesian views explain a recessionary gap. Consolidate with the [3.2 revision notes](/resources/ib-ib-dp-economics-dp-economics-ib-dp-economics-3-2-revision-notes/) and practise with the [3.2 practice questions](/resources/ib-ib-dp-economics-dp-economics-ib-dp-economics-3-2-practice/).

All figures are invented. The example economy is Brenmar, whose currency is the bren.

## What 3.2 covers

- **Aggregate demand (AD):** its components, why the curve slopes downwards, and what shifts it.
- **Short-run aggregate supply (SRAS)** and what shifts it.
- **Long-run aggregate supply** in the **monetarist/new classical** view (vertical at potential output) and the **Keynesian** view (a curve with three sections).
- **Equilibrium**: short-run and long-run, **recessionary (deflationary)** and **inflationary** gaps, and full employment.
- Changes in long-run aggregate supply, shown by shifts of the LRAS or Keynesian AS curve.

## Aggregate demand

AD is the total planned spending on an economy's final goods and services at each price level: **AD = C + I + G + (X - M)**.

### Why AD slopes downwards

A higher price level, other things equal, reduces the real quantity of output demanded because:

- **Wealth effect:** higher prices reduce the real value of savings and other assets, so consumption falls.
- **Interest rate effect:** higher prices raise demand for money, pushing up interest rates and reducing investment and borrowing for consumption.
- **International trade effect:** domestic goods become dearer relative to foreign goods, so exports fall and imports rise.

A change in the price level is a **movement along** AD. Any other change in spending is a **shift**.

### Determinants that shift AD

| Component | Shifts right when… |
|---|---|
| Consumption | Incomes, wealth or confidence rise; interest rates or income taxes fall; household debt is low |
| Investment | Interest rates fall; business confidence or expected demand rises; technology creates new opportunities; corporate taxes fall |
| Government spending | Government chooses to spend more, for political or economic reasons |
| Net exports | Foreign incomes rise; the currency depreciates; trading partners lower trade barriers |

Consumption is usually the largest component, so a fall in consumer confidence can shift AD substantially even if nothing else changes.

## Short-run aggregate supply

SRAS shows the real output firms are willing to produce at each price level when **wages and other resource prices are fixed**. It slopes upwards: if the price level rises while wages are fixed, profit per unit rises and firms expand output.

SRAS **shifts** when firms' costs change:

- Changes in wages or the prices of raw materials and energy.
- Changes in indirect taxes or subsidies on business.
- **Supply shocks**, such as a harvest failure or a sudden rise in oil prices (left), or a sharp fall in energy prices (right).

## Two views of long-run aggregate supply

| | Monetarist/new classical | Keynesian |
|---|---|---|
| Shape | LRAS vertical at potential output (Yp) | AS horizontal, then upward-sloping, then vertical at full-employment output |
| Why | In the long run wages and prices are flexible, so output returns to the level set by resources and technology | Wages and prices are sticky downwards, so output can stay below full employment |
| Price level and output | Only potential output matters in the long run; AD affects only the price level | With spare capacity, higher AD raises output with little or no rise in prices |

**The Keynesian AS curve in three sections:**

1. **Horizontal:** much spare capacity, so firms can expand output without raising costs.
2. **Upward-sloping:** bottlenecks appear in some industries; costs and prices rise as output grows.
3. **Vertical:** full capacity; more AD only raises prices.

### What shifts LRAS (or the Keynesian AS curve) to the right

Increases in the **quantity or quality of factors of production** (labour force growth, education and training, new capital), **better technology**, and **improved institutions** such as secure property rights or more efficient markets. These raise potential output and are the basis of long-run growth.

## Solving an AD-AS equilibrium from data

Brenmar's schedules, with real output in billions of brens:

| Price level | AD (real output demanded) | SRAS (real output supplied) | New AD after a rise in spending |
|---|---|---|---|
| 100 | 540 | 440 | 590 |
| 105 | 520 | 470 | 570 |
| 110 | 500 | 500 | 550 |
| 115 | 480 | 530 | 530 |
| 120 | 460 | 560 | 510 |

**Step 1: initial equilibrium.** AD equals SRAS at a price level of **110** and real output of **500**.

**Step 2: compare with potential output.** Brenmar's potential output (LRAS) is **520**. Actual output is 520 - 500 = **20 billion brens below** potential: a **recessionary gap**, with unemployment above its natural rate.

**Step 3: an increase in AD.** A rise in investment and government spending adds 50 at every price level (final column). The new equilibrium is at a price level of **115** and real output of **530**.

**Step 4: interpret.** Output now exceeds potential by 530 - 520 = **10**: the policy has turned a recessionary gap into an **inflationary gap**, and the price level has risen by (115 - 110) / 110 × 100 ≈ **4.5%**. A smaller boost to AD would have closed the gap with less inflation. This shows why the size of a demand shift matters, not just its direction.

## Recessionary gap: two explanations

**Monetarist/new classical view.** In a recessionary gap, unemployment is above its natural rate. Over time, workers accept lower money wages and resource prices fall. Firms' costs fall, so SRAS shifts right. The price level falls and output rises until the economy returns to long-run equilibrium at potential output. The economy **self-corrects**, so there is limited need for intervention.

**Keynesian view.** Money wages rarely fall because of contracts, minimum wage laws and worker resistance. The economy can stay in equilibrium on the horizontal section of the Keynesian AS curve, below full employment, for a long time. Without a rise in AD, the gap persists, which is the case for government action.

**Inflationary gap (monetarist/new classical):** output above potential bids up wages and resource prices, so SRAS shifts left until output returns to potential at a higher price level.

## Drawing the diagrams

1. Label the axes **price level** (vertical) and **real output** or **real GDP** (horizontal).
2. Label every curve: AD1, AD2, SRAS1, SRAS2, LRAS or Keynesian AS.
3. Mark potential output **Yp** (or full employment output **Yf**) on the horizontal axis.
4. Mark equilibrium price levels and outputs as P1, P2 and Y1, Y2, with dotted lines to the axes.
5. Show the gap between Y1 and Yp with a bracket and name it.

## Common mistakes

- Labelling the axes "price" and "quantity", which belong to a single market.
- Explaining the slope of AD with substitution between goods, which is a microeconomic idea.
- Shifting AD for a change in the price level.
- Treating a rise in wages as an AD shift; it shifts SRAS.
- Drawing the Keynesian curve and then claiming the economy always returns to full employment.
- Mixing the two models on one diagram without saying which view is being used.

## Exam technique

- **Explain using an AD-AS diagram** questions need a correctly labelled diagram and a written chain that refers to it ("AD shifts from AD1 to AD2, so real output rises from Y1 to Y2").
- **Distinguish** between the two views by naming the assumption about wage flexibility.
- **Evaluate** or **discuss** questions, for example on whether governments should close a recessionary gap, should weigh the self-correction view against the Keynesian view and reach a judgement that depends on how quickly wages adjust and how large the gap is.
- Link to policy: AD shifts connect to [fiscal policy](/resources/ib-dp-economics-fiscal-policy/) and [monetary policy](/resources/ib-dp-economics-monetary-policy/); LRAS shifts connect to [supply-side policies](/resources/ib-dp-economics-supply-side-policies/).
