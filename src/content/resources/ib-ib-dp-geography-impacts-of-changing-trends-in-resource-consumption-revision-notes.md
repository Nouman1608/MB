Condensed recall for **10.2 Impacts of changing trends in resource consumption** (Unit 3: Global
resource consumption and security). For the full explanation, read the
[Global resource consumption study guide](/resources/ib-ib-dp-geography-global-resource-consumption-study-guide/).

## Benefits and pressures

Rising consumption brings improved nutrition, health, comfort, industrial growth and development,
but also pressures.

| Type | Examples |
|---|---|
| Environmental | Land use change, deforestation, soil degradation, aquifer depletion, pollution, biodiversity loss, greenhouse gas emissions |
| Economic | Price volatility, import dependence, stranded assets as energy systems change, costs of environmental damage |
| Social and political | Unequal access, energy poverty, food insecurity, displacement, resource conflict, geopolitics of exporters and importers |

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Food security | When all people, at all times, have physical and economic access to sufficient, safe and nutritious food (the definition agreed at the 1996 World Food Summit) |
| Water security | Reliable access to enough water of acceptable quality for health, livelihoods and ecosystems |
| Energy security | Reliable, affordable access to energy |
| Energy poverty | Lack of access to modern energy services, or inability to afford them |
| Stranded assets | Investments (such as coal plants or fossil-fuel reserves) that lose value before the end of their expected life as energy systems change |
| Water-food-energy nexus | The interdependence of the three resources, so action on one affects the others |

Food security is often analysed in four dimensions: **availability** (enough food produced or
imported), **access** (people can afford or obtain it), **utilisation** (nutrition, clean water and
health allow the body to use it) and **stability** (all three hold over time).

## The water-food-energy nexus

- Farming needs water for irrigation and energy for pumps, fertiliser and machinery.
- Energy needs water: power stations need cooling water, and hydroelectric dams change river flows
  for downstream farmers.
- **Biofuels** link energy to food: maize ethanol in the USA and sugar-cane ethanol in Brazil use land
  and crops that could otherwise feed people. Biofuel demand was one of several factors blamed for the
  food price spike of 2007 to 2008, alongside oil prices, drought and export bans.
- Desalination can increase water supply but uses large amounts of energy.

## Impacts by resource: examples

- **Water: the Aral Sea.** Soviet irrigation schemes from the 1960s diverted the Amu Darya and Syr
  Darya rivers to grow cotton. The sea shrank dramatically, its fishing industry collapsed, and
  dust storms carried salt and agricultural chemicals across the region, harming health. A dam built
  in 2005 has partly restored the smaller northern part of the sea in Kazakhstan.
- **Water: aquifer depletion.** Groundwater pumped for irrigation is falling in the Ogallala aquifer
  beneath the US Great Plains and in north-west India, where the Green Revolution relied on tube wells.
- **Food: land acquisitions.** Since the 2007 to 2008 price spike, investors and governments from
  food-importing countries have leased or bought farmland abroad, including in parts of Africa,
  raising concerns about local people losing access to land.
- **Energy: import dependence.** Japan imports most of its fossil fuels; after the Fukushima nuclear
  accident in 2011 shut down its reactors, it had to import much more gas and oil. Europe's reliance
  on Russian gas became a major vulnerability in 2022.
- **Energy poverty:** several hundred million people still lack electricity, most of them in
  sub-Saharan Africa, which limits health care, education and business.

## Resource security

**Resource security** means reliable access to adequate, affordable and safe supplies. Profiles
differ: **resource-rich exporters**, **import-dependent** countries and countries facing **shortages**
because of environment, infrastructure or conflict.

Pressure in one place can affect another through trade and shared systems. A country can reduce
domestic water stress by importing water-intensive food, effectively importing **virtual water**.

Dry Middle Eastern countries such as Saudi Arabia import large amounts of grain partly for this
reason; from 2008 Saudi Arabia phased out most of its subsidised wheat growing because it was
depleting fossil groundwater.

## Causes of supply disruption

- Concentrated production.
- Transport chokepoints.
- Political instability.
- Extreme weather.

Examples: the Democratic Republic of the Congo produces the majority of the world's mined cobalt,
used in batteries, and China dominates rare-earth processing; around a fifth of the world's oil
consumption passes through the Strait of Hormuz; export bans by grain producers during price spikes
can worsen shortages elsewhere.

## Reserves and the R/P ratio

- **Reserves** are the portion of a resource that can be extracted economically with present
  technology; they change with price and technology.
- **R/P ratio** = reserves / annual production, the number of years reserves would last at current
  output.
- Example (invented figures): 360 / 12 = 30 years; with 60 more of reserves, 420 / 12 = 35 years; if
  production rises to 14, 360 / 14 = 25.7 years.
- It is a **snapshot, not a forecast**, because reserves and consumption change.

The difference between **resources** (everything that exists, including what cannot yet be extracted
economically) and **reserves** explains why R/P ratios for oil have often risen over time: higher
prices and new technology such as fracking turned resources into reserves.

## Model paragraph (examine)

*"The Aral Sea shows how a choice to maximise one resource can undermine others. Diverting rivers
increased cotton output and export earnings in the short term, but the loss of the sea destroyed
fisheries, damaged health through salty dust and altered the local climate. The benefits went to
national export income while the costs fell on communities around the former shoreline."*

## Exam traps

- Treating reserves as the total amount of a resource in the ground.
- Treating the R/P ratio as a prediction of when a resource will run out.
- Listing impacts with no located example or no sense of who is affected.
- Treating food security as only about producing enough food; access and utilisation matter as much.
- Forgetting the links between water, food and energy.

## Self-test

1. Define resource security.
2. Name two causes of supply disruption.
3. Calculate the R/P ratio for reserves of 90 units and production of 6 units a year.
4. What is virtual water?
5. Why is the R/P ratio a simplification?
6. Name the four dimensions of food security.
7. Give one example of the water-food-energy nexus.
8. What happened to the Aral Sea and why?
9. What are stranded assets?
10. Distinguish resources from reserves.
11. Why did Japan's energy imports rise after 2011?

**Answers:** 1. Reliable access to adequate, affordable and safe supplies. 2. For example concentrated
production and political instability. 3. 90 / 6 = 15 years. 4. Water embedded in traded goods, which
countries can import instead of using their own. 5. Reserves and consumption change over time.
6. Availability, access, utilisation and stability. 7. For example biofuels using crops and land, or
power stations needing cooling water. 8. It shrank dramatically because its feeder rivers were
diverted for cotton irrigation. 9. Investments that lose value early as energy systems change.
10. Resources are everything that exists; reserves are the part extractable economically now.
11. The Fukushima accident led to reactors being shut down, so more fossil fuels were imported.
