## Section A: short questions

**1.** *King Lear.* Explain the dramatic effect of Lear's line "Nothing will come of nothing" in the
opening scene, and how it gains meaning later in the play. **[3]**

**2.** *Hamlet.* "To be, or not to be, that is the question" opens a soliloquy. Explain **two** effects
of presenting this speech as a soliloquy. **[2]**

**3.** *The Taming of the Shrew.* Petruchio's request "Kiss me, Kate" recurs. Describe **two**
contrasting ways a production might stage Katherina's response, and what each suggests. **[3]**

**4.** *Measure for Measure.* The Duke spends much of the play disguised as a friar. Explain how this
creates dramatic irony. **[2]**

**5.** *King Lear.* Explain how an awareness of Jacobean anxieties about a divided kingdom might shape an
audience's response to the opening scene. **[2]**

**6.** *King Lear.* Explain **three** functions of the Gloucester subplot. **[3]**

**7.** Improve this AO5 sentence so that it evaluates the interpretation:
*"A feminist critic would say that Isabella is a victim."* **[2]**

**8.** Unpack the key terms of this statement and say what they assume:
*"Hamlet is a play about the corruption of the state, not the mind of one man."* **[2]**

## Section B: statement essays

Each essay is marked out of 25. In your answer you must consider relevant contextual factors and
different interpretations of the play.

**9. *Hamlet*.** "Claudius is a more capable ruler than Hamlet would ever be." Explore this view.
**[25]**

**10. *King Lear*.** "Lear's suffering is the consequence of his own choices." Explore this view.
**[25]**

**11. *Measure for Measure*.** "Isabella cares more about her own virtue than her brother's life."
Explore this view. **[25]**

**12. *The Taming of the Shrew*.** "Petruchio is as much changed by the play as Katherina is." Explore
this view. **[25]**

## Answers

### Section A

**1.** In the love test, the line warns Cordelia that refusing to flatter will earn her no share of the
kingdom (1); its blunt monosyllables and repetition make Lear's thinking seem rigid and transactional
(1). Later, "nothing" recurs as Lear loses power, home and reason, so the line becomes ironic: he is
reduced to the nothing he threatened (1).

**2.** Any two (1 each): the audience overhears Hamlet's private reasoning, creating intimacy; the speech
shows thought in progress rather than a conclusion; the audience knows more than the characters, who
are about to observe him; the absence of other speakers isolates him, emphasising his inwardness.

**3.** One mark for each of two contrasting stagings (2) and one for explaining what the contrast
reveals (1). Example: Katherina kisses him willingly and warmly, suggesting a genuine relationship has
developed; or she kisses him with exaggerated or ironic compliance, suggesting performance and
resistance. The choice shows how productions shape the play's attitude to marriage.

**4.** The audience knows the friar is the Duke (1), so his conversations with Claudio, Isabella and
others carry meanings they cannot see; the audience watches him test and manipulate characters who
believe they are speaking to a priest (1).

**5.** A Jacobean audience living under a king who sought to unite kingdoms might see Lear's division of
Britain as dangerous folly from the start (1), so the opening scene would feel ominous rather than
generous, and the chaos that follows as its consequence (1).

**6.** Any three (1 each): it doubles the main plot, so suffering seems universal rather than individual;
it parallels blindness and insight (Gloucester's physical blindness, Lear's moral blindness); it
explores legitimacy and inheritance through Edmund; it provides the scene at Dover, where despair is
confronted; it offers a contrasting reconciliation (Edgar and Gloucester) to compare with Lear and
Cordelia.

**7.** One mark for stating a specific interpretation with evidence (1); one for evaluating it against the
text (1). Example: *"A feminist reading sees Isabella as trapped between Angelo's lust and the Duke's
final proposal; her silence after it supports this, although productions that let her refuse him
suggest the play leaves her some agency."*

**8.** "Corruption of the state" assumes the play's focus is political and collective (Denmark, Claudius's
court) (1); "the mind of one man" refers to Hamlet's inner life; the statement assumes these are
alternatives, whereas a strong answer might argue the play links them (1).

### Section B: how to mark questions 9 to 12

Marked holistically on AO1, AO2, AO3 and AO5. The bands below are Marlbridge's illustrative guidance, not
Pearson's published mark scheme.

| Marks | Typical features |
|---|---|
| 21–25 | Sophisticated, evaluative response to the statement; perceptive analysis of dramatic methods; context and interpretations integrated and weighed |
| 16–20 | Clear argument; detailed analysis; relevant context and alternative readings discussed |
| 11–15 | Some argument; explanation of methods; context and interpretations present but undeveloped |
| 6–10 | Largely narrative; limited reference to staging, context or interpretations |
| 1–5 | Mainly retelling |

**9. *Hamlet*: indicative content.**
- AO1/AO2: Claudius's smooth public rhetoric in his first court scene; his efficient diplomacy over
  Norway; his prayer soliloquy revealing guilt; Hamlet's soliloquies of self-reproach and delay; the
  play-within-the-play as Hamlet's strategy.
- AO3: early modern ideas of kingship and legitimacy; regicide as the gravest crime; revenge tragedy
  conventions.
- AO5: Claudius played as a capable politician or a guilty usurper; readings of Hamlet as philosophical
  or as unstable; whether competence can be separated from legitimacy.

**10. *King Lear*: indicative content.**
- AO1/AO2: the love test and the banishment of Cordelia and Kent; Lear's curses on Goneril; his
  recognition in the storm ("More sinned against than sinning"); the reconciliation with Cordelia; the
  ending.
- AO3: Jacobean views of kingship, age and duty; the division of the kingdom; Tate's adapted ending as
  reception.
- AO5: Lear as tragic hero whose error brings disproportionate suffering; nihilistic readings of a
  universe without justice; productions that stress his cruelty or his frailty.

**11. *Measure for Measure*: indicative content.**
- AO1/AO2: Isabella's pleading with Angelo, including "it is tyrannous / To use it like a giant"; her
  refusal of his bargain; "More than our brother is our chastity"; her anger at Claudio's plea to live;
  her silence at the Duke's proposal.
- AO3: religious vows and the value placed on chastity; the problem-play genre; Jacobean regulation of
  sexuality.
- AO5: Isabella as rigid and self-righteous, or as a woman refusing coercion; feminist readings;
  productions that make her silence a refusal.

**12. *The Taming of the Shrew*: indicative content.**
- AO1/AO2: Petruchio's mercenary motive on arrival; his methods of "taming" (starving, denying sleep);
  the sun and moon scene; the wager; Katherina's final speech and Petruchio's response; the Induction
  framing everything as performance.
- AO3: early modern marriage and patriarchy; shrew-taming traditions; later responses and adaptations.
- AO5: the play as a romance in which both partners change, as farce, or as an exposure of patriarchal
  control; productions that play the final speech as sincere, ironic or a shared performance.
