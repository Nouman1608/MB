**This page is for HL only.** Regional study 4, The French Revolution (1774 to 1815), is one of twelve studies in the Europe option examined on HL Paper 3, where you write two essays from different regional studies. The guide has seven inquiry topics: the reasons for the revolution, its radicalization, the rise of Napoleon, social change in revolutionary and Napoleonic France, the impact of Napoleonic rule on **one country** in the French empire or French colonial empire (not France), and the decline and defeat of the First French Empire. For the country case this guide uses Spain, with notes on other options.

## Reasons for the revolution

*Intellectual, economic and social factors.* The **Enlightenment** encouraged criticism of privilege, arbitrary rule and religious intolerance. The American Revolution showed a government could be founded on written rights. These ideas reached the educated, but their influence on ordinary people was less direct, so be careful in claiming ideas alone caused revolution.

French society was divided into three **estates**: the clergy, the nobility and everyone else, the Third Estate. The first two had privileges, such as exemption from the main direct tax, the *taille*. The Third Estate included wealthy lawyers, merchants and professionals as well as peasants and urban workers. Many of the educated Third Estate resented being excluded from high office. Peasants paid dues to lords, tithes to the Church and taxes to the crown.

The key economic factor was **state bankruptcy**. France had borne the cost of wars, especially its support for the American colonists. Reform ministers such as Turgot, Necker, Calonne and Brienne tried to raise taxes or cut spending but met resistance from privileged groups. A poor harvest in 1788 and a harsh winter raised bread prices and brought hunger to cities and countryside.

*Role of Louis XVI.* Louis (king from 1774) was well-meaning but indecisive. He dismissed reforming ministers under pressure from court and nobles, and he was unwilling to act decisively when events moved quickly. Historians debate how far his character, rather than the crisis in the system, explains the revolution.

*Estates General (1789).* The Assembly of Notables (1787) refused reform, and the king agreed to summon the Estates General, which had not met since 1614. It opened on 5 May 1789 at Versailles. A dispute over voting by order (which favoured the privileged) or by head (which favoured the Third Estate) paralysed it. On 17 June the Third Estate declared itself the **National Assembly**, and on 20 June, locked out of its hall, swore the Tennis Court Oath not to disband until France had a constitution.

*National Assembly and the Declaration.* After the fall of the Bastille on 14 July, rural panic known as the Great Fear led the Assembly on 4 August to abolish feudal dues and privileges. The **Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen** (26 August 1789) stated liberty, property, security and resistance to oppression, equality before the law and national sovereignty.

## Radicalization of the revolution

*Constitution of 1791.* This created a constitutional monarchy with a Legislative Assembly. Active citizens who paid enough tax could vote, so many men were excluded, and women had no vote. The Civil Constitution of the Clergy (1790) made priests state employees and divided the Church, because many priests refused the oath.

*End of the monarchy.* In June 1791 the royal family tried to flee and were caught at Varennes, which destroyed trust in the king. War with Austria and Prussia began in April 1792 and went badly at first. On 10 August 1792 crowds attacked the Tuileries palace and the king was suspended. The September Massacres killed many prisoners. The Convention abolished the monarchy and proclaimed the republic in September 1792. **Louis XVI** was executed on 21 January 1793.

*Robespierre and the Terror (1793 to 1794).* Pressures in 1793 included foreign invasion, the revolt in the Vendée and economic hardship. The Committee of Public Safety, in which **Maximilien Robespierre** was prominent, took emergency powers. The Law of Suspects (September 1793) allowed arrest on suspicion; the Revolutionary Tribunal tried enemies; the Law of 22 Prairial (June 1794) sped up trials further. Official executions numbered in the order of seventeen thousand, with many more deaths in prisons and in the civil war. The Terror also included price controls, conscription and de-Christianization.

*Thermidorean Reaction.* On 9 Thermidor (27 July 1794) the Convention turned on Robespierre, who was executed the next day. The Terror ended and the Jacobins lost power. Historians divide over whether the Terror was a response to circumstances of war and civil war, or whether revolutionary ideology itself drove violence.

## Rise of Napoleon

*French Revolutionary Wars (1792 to 1802).* Success after 1793 created a mass citizen army and a generation of commanders. **Napoleon Bonaparte**, an artillery officer from Corsica, rose by taking Toulon in 1793 and by defending the Convention against royalists in Paris in October 1795. His Italian campaign of 1796 to 1797 brought victories, loot and the Treaty of Campo Formio (1797). His expedition to Egypt in 1798 failed militarily but he returned with his reputation intact.

*The Directory (1795 to 1799).* The Directory had five directors and two councils. It faced royalist and Jacobin opposition, financial weakness, corruption and dependence on the army. It repeatedly annulled elections to stay in power. These weaknesses explain why many were ready to accept strong leadership.

*18 Brumaire (1799).* On 9 November 1799 Napoleon, with Sieyès and his brother Lucien, ended the Directory by a coup backed by troops. He became First Consul in the Consulate, then Consul for life in 1802.

*Creation of the French Empire (1804).* Napoleon was proclaimed Emperor in May 1804 and crowned in December. A plebiscite was held, but the outcome was not in doubt. The title showed that the revolution had moved from republic to personal rule.

## Social change in revolutionary and Napoleonic France

*Domestic policies.* Napoleon claimed to preserve the gains of the revolution (equality before the law, careers open to talent, property rights) while restoring order. He founded the Bank of France (1800), reformed taxation, created the Legion of Honour (1802), set up lycées, and issued the **Civil Code** (1804). His Concordat with the pope (1801) restored the Church's role while keeping state control. Censorship and secret police limited freedom, and elections became hollow.

*Foreign policy's effects on France.* Constant war brought glory and territory at first, but also heavy conscription, taxation and loss of life. The Continental System (1806) hurt French trade as well as British. After 1812 the human cost was extreme.

*Religion.* The revolution had attacked the Church and divided the clergy; Napoleon's Concordat healed much of this division, although some radical gains of the revolution (civil registration and the loss of church land) remained.

*Women.* The revolution gave women equal inheritance rights and easy divorce (1792), yet denied them political rights. Women's clubs were banned in 1793, and Olympe de Gouges, who had written a declaration of women's rights, was executed. The Civil Code placed wives under their husbands' legal authority and limited divorce, so Napoleon reversed some gains.

## Impact of Napoleonic rule on one country: Spain

Napoleon removed the Spanish Bourbons in 1808 and placed his brother **Joseph** on the throne. The *Dos de Mayo* uprising in Madrid on 2 May 1808 began a long guerrilla war against French forces, with British help under Wellington.

*Political impact.* Joseph's rule issued reforms but lacked legitimacy. Spanish liberals met at Cádiz and wrote the Constitution of 1812, which asserted national sovereignty and limited the monarchy. After 1814 Ferdinand VII restored absolutism and cancelled it.

*Social impact.* Feudal rights and the Inquisition were abolished under French rule, but many Spaniards resented foreign control and the attack on the Church. Violence on both sides devastated communities.

*Economic impact.* The war destroyed farms and trade, and the loss of control over American colonies encouraged independence movements.

*Marginalized groups.* The abolition of the Inquisition helped religious minorities in theory, but the war left little room for reform. In other territories under French rule, such as parts of Germany, Jews were given legal equality, while in the colonial empire enslaved people in Saint-Domingue won freedom through revolt, and Napoleon's attempt to restore slavery in 1802 helped provoke the independence of Haiti in 1804.

## Decline and defeat of the First French Empire

*Coalitions.* Britain financed repeated coalitions of Austria, Russia, Prussia and others. Napoleon won great victories, such as Austerlitz (1805), but peace never lasted.

*Continental System.* The Berlin Decrees of 1806 closed European ports to British goods. It was hard to enforce, hurt Europe's economies, and led Napoleon into the Peninsular War and the break with Russia.

*Invasion of Russia (1812).* Napoleon entered Russia in June 1812 with a very large army. Russian retreat, scorched earth, disease, hunger and winter destroyed most of it. This encouraged Prussia and Austria to rejoin the coalition against him.

*Hundred Days and Waterloo.* After defeat at Leipzig in 1813 and abdication in 1814, Napoleon returned from Elba in March 1815. He was beaten at Waterloo on 18 June by Wellington and Blücher and exiled to St Helena.

## Perspectives to use

Marxist historians such as Lefebvre saw the revolution as a bourgeois revolution. Revisionists such as Cobban questioned how far the revolution was a class conflict. Furet and Schama stressed political culture and violence. On Napoleon, some see the heir of the revolution, others a dictator.
