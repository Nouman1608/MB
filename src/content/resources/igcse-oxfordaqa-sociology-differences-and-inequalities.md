**3.2.1 Differences and inequalities** is the first topic of Paper 2 (Exploring Sociology) in
OxfordAQA International GCSE Sociology (9292), Version 1.0, a new qualification first examined in
May/June 2028. It asks how sociologists classify people into groups, how those groups differ in power
and life chances, how class, gender and ethnicity are explained, and what has been tried to reduce
inequality. The specification recommends the work of **Connell, Crenshaw, du Bois, Marx, Walby and
Weber**, and credits other sociologists used appropriately.

## Syllabus coverage

- **3.2.1.1 Differences** — classifying groups by social class, status, caste, slavery, sex and
  gender, race and ethnicity, age and generation/cohort, religion, ability/disability and
  nationality; how individuals identify with groups; individual and collective identities
- **3.2.1.2 Inequalities** — inequalities in power and in life chances (employment, education,
  health, life expectancy, social mobility); intersectionality and its impacts on individuals; how
  inequalities affect people and society
- **3.2.1.3 Social class** — Marxism (classes and class conflict, exploitation, false
  consciousness); functionalism (the functional necessity of class inequality, meritocracy);
  Weberianism (status, class, party); measuring class; wealth and income, including global
  comparison and change over time; upward and downward, intragenerational and intergenerational
  social mobility and the factors affecting it
- **3.2.1.4 Gender** — feminist views of patriarchy; roles, status and power of men and women in
  different societies; sexism, sex discrimination and the glass ceiling

  enslavement; racism, institutional racism, assimilation, multiculturalism, diasporas and hybrid

- **3.2.1.6 Attempts to reduce or eliminate inequalities** — the Marxist case for abolishing
  capitalism; social democratic welfare states; development aid

## How do sociologists classify differences between people?

Sociologists group people by **social class** (economic position), **status** (social honour or
prestige), **caste** (a closed, ascribed hierarchy fixed at birth, as in the traditional Indian caste
system), **slavery** (people owned as property), **sex and gender** (biological sex versus socially
learned roles), **race and ethnicity**, **age and generation or cohort** (people born in the same
period who share experiences), **religion**, **ability or disability** and **nationality**. Some of
these positions are **ascribed** (given at birth) and others **achieved**.

People also **identify** with groups. An **individual identity** is how a person sees themselves; a
**collective identity** is a shared sense of belonging, such as a national, religious or ethnic
identity, that can unite people and mark boundaries with others.

## What are inequalities in power and life chances?

**Life chances** are a person's opportunities to achieve the good things in life. Inequalities show in
**employment** (pay, security, promotion), **education** (attainment and access to higher
education), **health**, **life expectancy** and **social mobility**. Groups with more **power** —
the ability to get their way, even against opposition — can shape laws, institutions and opportunities
to their advantage.

**Intersectionality**, a term introduced by **Kimberlé Crenshaw** (1989), argues that class, gender,
ethnicity and other differences combine, so a person's experience cannot be understood by looking at
one in isolation. Crenshaw showed that Black women could face discrimination that neither a "race"
nor a "sex" discrimination claim alone recognised. Inequalities affect individuals (stress, poorer
health, fewer opportunities) and society (lost talent, social conflict, lower trust).

## How do Marxists, functionalists and Weberians explain social class?

| Perspective | Key ideas |
|---|---|
| **Marxism (Marx)** | Society is divided into the **bourgeoisie**, who own the means of production, and the **proletariat**, who sell their labour. The bourgeoisie **exploit** workers by keeping the value they create beyond their wages, producing **class conflict**. Workers accept this because of **false consciousness** — ruling-class ideas make inequality seem natural and fair. |
| **Functionalism (Davis and Moore)** | Class inequality is a **functional necessity**: the most important jobs must carry the highest rewards to motivate the most able people to train for them. Society is a **meritocracy** in which positions are achieved by ability and effort. |
| **Weberianism (Weber)** | Inequality has three separate dimensions: **class** (economic position in the market), **status** (social honour) and **party** (political power through organised groups). A person can rank differently on each. |

**Evaluation.** Marxism explains persistent inequality and exploitation but has been criticised for
ignoring status, gender and ethnicity and for predictions of revolution that did not occur.
Functionalism explains why some jobs are rewarded, but critics point out that inherited wealth and
unequal opportunities mean society is not a true meritocracy. Weber's model captures more complexity,
which is why many sociologists use it to study modern class structures.

**Measuring social class** usually uses **occupation**, grouping jobs by pay, security and autonomy.
Other measures include income, wealth, education and people's own sense of their class. Each gives a
different picture: occupation scales can miss the very wealthy, who may not work, and people outside
paid work.

**Wealth and income.** **Income** is a flow of money (wages, benefits, interest); **wealth** is a stock
of assets (property, savings, shares). Wealth is usually distributed far more unequally than income,
both within societies and globally, where average incomes in the richest countries are many times
higher than in the poorest. Inequality changes over time with taxation, welfare and economic change.

**Social mobility** is movement up or down the class structure. **Intragenerational** mobility is
within one person's lifetime; **intergenerational** mobility compares a person with their parents.
Factors include education, family background, gender, ethnicity, the availability of jobs, and
economic change such as the growth of professional work.

## How do feminists explain gender inequality?

Feminists argue society is **patriarchal** — organised in ways that give men more power. **Sylvia
Walby** identified six structures of patriarchy: paid work, household production, culture, sexuality,
male violence and the state. She argued that patriarchy has shifted from a **private** form, based in
the home, to a **public** form, in which women are in paid work and public life but remain
disadvantaged there. **Raewyn Connell** described **hegemonic masculinity**, the dominant ideal of
manhood that ranks some men above others and above women.

The **roles, status and power** of men and women vary between societies, which shows gender is
socially constructed. **Sexism** is prejudice based on sex; **sex discrimination** is unequal treatment
because of it. The **glass ceiling** is the invisible barrier that stops women reaching the most senior
positions in employment even when they are qualified.

## How do race and ethnicity create inequality?

**Race** is a way of classifying people by supposed physical differences, now seen by sociologists as
socially constructed; **ethnicity** refers to shared culture, such as language, religion, traditions
and a sense of common origin. **Apartheid** in South Africa was a legal system of racial segregation
that controlled where people could live, work and be educated. **Colonialism** and **enslavement** have
legacies today in global inequalities, in patterns of migration and in racist ideas that were used to
justify them. **W. E. B. du Bois** described the "colour line" and the **double consciousness** of Black
Americans, who saw themselves through the eyes of a society that looked down on them.

Key terms: **racism** (prejudice and discrimination based on race); **institutional racism**
(discrimination built into the routines of organisations, even without individual intent);
**assimilation** (minority groups adopting the majority culture); **multiculturalism** (different
cultures coexisting with equal respect); **diasporas** (communities dispersed from an original homeland
who keep links with it); and **hybrid identities** (identities that blend elements of more than one
culture).

## What attempts have been made to reduce inequality?

- **Marxism** argues inequality can only be eliminated by **abolishing capitalism** and replacing it
  with common ownership of the means of production. Critics point to the inequality and lack of
  freedom in societies that claimed to follow this route.
- **Social democratic welfare states** use progressive taxation and universal services such as
  healthcare, education and benefits to reduce inequality within capitalism. They narrow gaps but are
  costly and do not remove inequality of wealth.
- **Development aid** transfers money, expertise or goods from richer to poorer countries to reduce
  global inequality. Supporters credit it with improvements in health and education; critics argue it
  can create dependency or serve donors' interests.

## Common mistakes

- Confusing **wealth** (assets) with **income** (a flow of money).
- Treating race and ethnicity as the same concept.
- Describing Weber as a Marxist: Weber added status and party as separate dimensions.
- Using "intersectionality" as a synonym for "inequality" rather than for overlapping differences.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is false consciousness?** The Marxist idea that the working class accepts beliefs that serve the
ruling class, such as the belief that inequality is fair. It explains why exploitation does not
automatically lead to revolt.

**What is the difference between intergenerational and intragenerational mobility?**
Intergenerational mobility compares a person's class with their parents'; intragenerational mobility
is movement during one person's own working life.

**Who introduced the idea of intersectionality?** Kimberlé Crenshaw, in 1989, to show how race and
gender combine in the discrimination faced by Black women.

**What did Walby mean by public patriarchy?** That women now take part in paid work and public life
but continue to face disadvantage there, rather than being confined to the home.
