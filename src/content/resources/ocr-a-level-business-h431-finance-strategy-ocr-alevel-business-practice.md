## Questions

### Section A: short-answer questions

**1.** Explain what is meant by a finance strategy. **[2]**

**2.** Identify **two** components of a finance strategy. **[2]**

**3.** A company has non-current liabilities of £900,000 and equity of £1,200,000. Calculate its gearing ratio (non-current liabilities as a percentage of capital employed). **[3]**

**4.** Which **one** of the following is most likely to be part of a finance strategy?

A: Paying this week's wages.

B: Deciding the long-term mix of loans and shares to fund expansion.

C: Recording one invoice.

D: Choosing the colour of the company logo. **[1]**

**5.** Explain **one** reason why a finance strategy should be consistent with the business's wider objectives. **[2]**

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**6.** Rowan Homes has 300,000 shares in issue, equity of £1,200,000, non-current liabilities of £400,000 and an operating profit of £300,000. Interest is £30,000 and tax is 20% of profit before tax. It plans a £500,000 project expected to add £100,000 to operating profit. Two funding options are proposed.

- **Option 1:** borrow £500,000 at 8%.
- **Option 2:** issue 100,000 new shares at £5 each.

**(a)** Calculate earnings per share before the project. **[3]**

**(b)** Calculate earnings per share under each option. **[6]**

**(c)** Calculate gearing under each option. **[4]**

**(d)** Evaluate which option Rowan Homes should choose. **[8]**

**7.** If the project adds only £20,000 to operating profit (instead of £100,000), the earnings per share are 66.7p under Option 1 and 58.0p under Option 2. Explain what this shows about the risk of each option. **[4]**

**8.** Evaluate the importance of a finance strategy to a business planning rapid growth. **[8]**

**9.** A family-owned furniture maker has gearing of 15% and interest cover of 14 times. It wants to buy a rival for £2,000,000, four times its annual profit. The family does not want to lose control. Recommend and justify a finance strategy for the purchase. **[10]**

**10.** A company pays out 90% of its profit as dividends each year but plans to double its size in three years. Analyse why this may show a lack of fit within its finance strategy. **[5]**

## Answers

**1.** A finance strategy is a long-term plan [1] for how the business will raise, use and control its money so that it can achieve its objectives [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Any two from: finance objectives; funding; investment; budgeting and control; cash and working capital management; dividend policy; risk management; reporting [1] [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Capital employed = £1,200,000 + £900,000 = £2,100,000 [1]. Gearing = 900,000 ÷ 2,100,000 [1] × 100 = **42.9%** [1]. **[3]**

**4.** **B** [1]. **[1]**

**5.** If the finance strategy does not support the objectives, funds may not be available for the growth or profit the business wants [1]; for example, a growth objective is undermined by a strategy that pays out most profit as dividends [1]. **[2]**

**6. (a)** Profit before tax = £300,000 − £30,000 = £270,000 [1]. Profit after tax = 80% × £270,000 = £216,000 [1]. EPS = £216,000 ÷ 300,000 × 100 = **72.0p** [1]. **[3]**

**(b)** Option 1: extra interest = 8% × £500,000 = £40,000, so total interest £70,000 [1]. Operating profit £400,000; profit before tax £330,000; profit after tax £264,000 [1] [1]. EPS = £264,000 ÷ 300,000 = **88.0p** [1]. Option 2: interest stays £30,000; profit before tax £370,000; profit after tax £296,000 [1]. Shares 400,000; EPS = **74.0p** [1]. **[6]**

**(c)** Base capital employed = £1,200,000 + £400,000 = £1,600,000. Option 1: non-current liabilities £900,000; capital employed £2,100,000; gearing = **42.9%** [1] [1]. Option 2: equity £1,700,000; non-current liabilities £400,000; capital employed £2,100,000; gearing = **19.0%** [1] [1]. **[4]**

**(d)** For Option 1 (loan): EPS is much higher (88.0p against 74.0p) [1], existing shareholders keep 100% control [1], and interest cover is 400,000 ÷ 70,000 = 5.7 times, still adequate [1].
For Option 2 (shares): no new interest, so gearing falls to 19.0% and interest cover is 13.3 times, giving much lower risk [1]; but existing owners' share falls to 75% (300,000 ÷ 400,000) [1] and EPS rises only slightly above the 72.0p before the project [1].
Risk: if the project underperforms, Option 1 brings EPS below the original 72.0p, and the interest still has to be paid [1].
Judgement: Option 1 is better if the profit forecast is reliable and no lender limits are broken, because it gives a much higher return; Option 2 is safer if demand for the project is uncertain [1]. Rowan should test how reliable the £100,000 forecast is before borrowing [1]. (Maximum 8 marks.) **[8]**

**7.** Under Option 1 the EPS of 66.7p is below the 72.0p before the project, so a loan makes earnings fall if the project fails [1]; interest of £70,000 must be paid whatever the profit [1]. Under Option 2 the EPS falls further, to 58.0p, because there are 100,000 more shares sharing a small profit [1]; but there is no fixed interest and gearing is much lower, so the business is safer from failing to pay its debts [1]. **[4]**

**8.** Funds: rapid growth needs money for non-current assets, stock and working capital, so the business needs a plan to obtain it at acceptable cost [1]. Risk: growth financed by debt raises gearing and exposes the business to interest rate rises [1]. Cash: fast growth can create over-trading, so cash and working capital must be planned [1].
Control: budgets and forecasts show whether growth is on target [1].
Stakeholders: lenders and investors want to see a sound plan before supplying funds [1]; employees depend on the business surviving growth [1]; owners want returns without losing control [1].
Evaluation: a strategy depends on forecasts, which are unreliable in fast growth, so it needs regular review [1]. Judgement: a finance strategy is vital for a rapidly growing business, because growth is the point at which running out of cash is most likely [1]. (Maximum 8 marks.) **[8]**

**9.** Recommendation: fund the £2,000,000 mainly through a long-term bank loan, supported by retained profit [1] and possibly a small amount of new family capital, but no outside shares [1].
Justification: the family does not want to lose control, which rules out a large share issue [1]. Gearing is only 15% and interest cover is 14 times, so there is room to borrow [1]. Suppose profit is £500,000 (£2,000,000 is four times profit): a loan of £1,500,000 at 7% would cost £105,000 a year, which is covered about 4.8 times by £500,000 of operating profit before the rival adds anything (500,000 ÷ 105,000) [1] [1]. The acquisition adds the rival's profit, so cover would be higher [1].
Risks and alternatives: rising interest rates, so fix the rate [1]; the rival may not perform, so the family should check its accounts and cash flow before buying [1]; an alternative of leasing assets or paying in stages reduces the loan [1]. Gearing would rise sharply, so a covenant limit should be negotiated [1].
Judgement: a loan-based strategy is appropriate because the business has low gearing, strong cover and wishes to retain control, but the final amount should depend on a review of the rival's accounts and on interest rates [1]. (Maximum 10 marks.) **[10]**

**10.** A growth objective needs funds for assets, stock and working capital [1]. Paying out 90% of profit leaves only 10% to retain, so the company must raise most funds from loans or new shares [1]. That raises gearing or dilutes shareholders, which may conflict with a wish to keep risk low [1]. The dividend policy and growth objective therefore contradict each other [1], so a consistent strategy would cut the payout or accept higher borrowing and plan for its risk [1]. **[5]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing finance tasks without linking them to objectives.
- Recommending a funding source without figures on gearing or earnings per share.
- Ignoring risk and the effect on control.
- Forgetting to compare with the position before the project.
- Missing a judgement.
