## The six formulae (Appendix 4)

| Ratio | Formula | Answer form | What it measures |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gross profit margin | gross profit ÷ revenue × 100 | % | Profit kept from each sale after cost of sales |
| Operating profit margin | operating profit ÷ revenue × 100 | % | Profit kept after all operating costs |
| Markup | profit per item ÷ cost per item × 100 | % | How much is added to cost to set the price |
| ROCE | operating profit ÷ capital employed × 100 | % | Return on the long-term money invested |
| Current ratio | current assets ÷ current liabilities | x : 1 | Ability to pay short-term debts from all current assets |
| Acid test ratio | (current assets − inventory) ÷ current liabilities | x : 1 | The same, without relying on selling inventory |

Appendix 4 also gives gross profit = revenue − cost of sales and operating profit = gross profit − other operating expenses. The first four are **profitability** ratios; the last two are **liquidity** ratios.

## Rearranging the formulae

Exam questions sometimes give the ratio and ask for a figure.

- **Gross profit from a margin.** Revenue 250,000 and gross profit margin 36%: gross profit = 0.36 × 250,000 = **90,000**, so cost of sales = 250,000 − 90,000 = **160,000**.
- **Selling price from a markup.** A product costs 18 and the markup is 45%: profit per item = 0.45 × 18 = 8.10, so price = 18 + 8.10 = **26.10** (or 18 × 1.45).
- **Markup versus margin on the same item.** A bag of coffee costs 7.50 and sells for 12.00. Profit per item 4.50. Markup = 4.50 ÷ 7.50 × 100 = **60%**. As a share of the selling price it is 4.50 ÷ 12.00 × 100 = 37.5%. Markup uses cost as the base, so it is always larger than the margin on the same item.

## Worked example: a full set for Fenmore Coffee Roasters

| | Year 1 | Year 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue | 500,000 | 560,000 |
| Cost of sales | 300,000 | 324,800 |
| Gross profit | 200,000 | 235,200 |
| Other operating expenses | 140,000 | 179,200 |
| Operating profit | 60,000 | 56,000 |
| Capital employed | 400,000 | 480,000 |
| Current assets | 90,000 | 120,000 |
| of which inventory | 50,000 | 84,000 |
| Current liabilities | 60,000 | 80,000 |

**Profitability**

- Gross profit margin: Year 1 200,000 ÷ 500,000 × 100 = **40.0%**; Year 2 235,200 ÷ 560,000 × 100 = **42.0%**.
- Operating profit margin: Year 1 60,000 ÷ 500,000 × 100 = **12.0%**; Year 2 56,000 ÷ 560,000 × 100 = **10.0%**.
- ROCE: Year 1 60,000 ÷ 400,000 × 100 = **15.0%**; Year 2 56,000 ÷ 480,000 × 100 = **11.7%**.

**Liquidity**

- Current ratio: Year 1 90,000 ÷ 60,000 = **1.5 : 1**; Year 2 120,000 ÷ 80,000 = **1.5 : 1**.
- Acid test: Year 1 (90,000 − 50,000) ÷ 60,000 = **0.67 : 1**; Year 2 (120,000 − 84,000) ÷ 80,000 = **0.45 : 1**.

### Interpreting the set

1. **Gross margin rose** by 2 percentage points. Fenmore is keeping more from each sale after paying for beans and roasting, perhaps from better supplier prices or a price rise.
2. **Operating margin fell** by 2 points, although gross margin rose. So the problem is below gross profit: other operating expenses rose from 140,000 to 179,200, a 28% increase against revenue growth of 12%.
3. **ROCE fell** from 15.0% to 11.7%. An extra 80,000 of capital was invested, yet operating profit fell by 4,000. The new investment is not yet paying for itself.
4. **The current ratio is unchanged**, which could look reassuring, but the **acid test fell** sharply because inventory rose from 50,000 to 84,000. More of Fenmore's short-term assets are tied up in coffee waiting to be sold, and only 0.45 of quick assets cover each 1 of short-term debt.

The story: Fenmore expanded, buying more stock and spending more on running costs, but the growth has so far reduced its return and squeezed its liquidity.

## Liquidity: concept and importance

**Liquidity** is the ability of a business to pay its short-term debts as they fall due, using assets that are cash or can quickly become cash. It matters because suppliers, employees and lenders expect payment on time; a business that cannot pay may be forced to close even if it is profitable.

Why two ratios? The current ratio counts inventory, but inventory may take months to sell and may have to be discounted. The acid test removes it, so it is a stricter test. When the two ratios move apart, as at Fenmore, the gap points straight at inventory.

Reading the figures:

- A low ratio suggests the business may struggle to pay bills on time.
- A very high ratio is not automatically good: it can mean cash or stock is sitting idle instead of being used to grow the business.
- There is no single correct figure in the specification; judge a ratio by comparing it.

## Comparing with previous years and with other businesses

The specification asks for both comparisons.

| Year 2 | Fenmore | Rival roaster |
|---|---|---|
| Gross profit margin | 42.0% | 38.0% |
| Operating profit margin | 10.0% | 13.0% |
| ROCE | 11.7% | 16.0% |
| Current ratio | 1.5 : 1 | 1.8 : 1 |
| Acid test ratio | 0.45 : 1 | 1.1 : 1 |

Fenmore is better at controlling the cost of each sale but worse at controlling running costs, earns a lower return on its capital and is less liquid.

Make comparisons fair: the rival should be a similar type and size of business, figures should cover the same period, and one-off events (such as moving premises) can distort a single year.

## Using ratios to assess performance and inform decisions (3.5.3)

Link each finding to an action:

| Finding at Fenmore | Possible decision |
|---|---|
| Expenses growing faster than revenue | Review the costs added during expansion, such as extra staff or premises |
| Inventory rising, acid test falling | Order smaller quantities more often; run promotions to sell older stock |
| ROCE below the rival's | Delay further investment until the new capacity earns more |
| Gross margin above the rival's | Protect it: keep supplier arrangements and pricing |

**Limits of ratio analysis**: ratios use past figures; they do not show causes on their own; they leave out non-financial factors such as customer satisfaction or staff morale; and a single year can be distorted.

## Annotated model answer

*Evaluate whether Fenmore Coffee Roasters is performing better in Year 2 than in Year 1.*

> In one way Fenmore has improved: its gross profit margin rose from 40.0% to 42.0%, so it keeps more from each sale after the cost of beans. However, its operating profit margin fell from 12.0% to 10.0% because expenses rose by 28% while revenue rose by only 12%. ROCE also fell from 15.0% to 11.7%, so the extra 80,000 invested has not yet earned a return. Liquidity is weaker: the acid test fell from 0.67 to 0.45 because inventory rose by 34,000. Overall Fenmore is performing worse, because operating profit, return and liquidity have all fallen; the higher gross margin shows its products are profitable, so the judgement could change if the new expenses are one-off costs of expansion.

Why it works: it uses ratios from both years, explains causes from the statements, weighs positive against negative and ends with a conditional judgement.

## Common mistakes

- Writing a liquidity ratio as a percentage, or a margin as a ratio.
- Using revenue instead of capital employed for ROCE.
- Subtracting all current assets, rather than inventory alone, in the acid test.
- Using the selling price as the base for markup.
- Saying a ratio is "good" or "bad" with no comparison.
- Describing a change ("fell by 2 points") with no cause or consequence.
