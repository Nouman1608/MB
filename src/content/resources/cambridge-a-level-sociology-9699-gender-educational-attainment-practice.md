## Section A – Short-answer and explanation questions

**1.** What is meant by "gender socialisation"? **[2]**

**2.** What did Wilkinson (1994) mean by a "genderquake"? **[2]**

**3.** Describe two of the masculinities identified by Mac an Ghaill (1994). **[4]**

**4.** Describe what Sharpe found when she compared girls' priorities in the 1970s and the 1990s. **[4]**

**5.** Read the item. The data are invented.

> In the fictional country of Lorvane, entries for an advanced physics course were 1,200 boys and 300 girls. Entries for an advanced languages course were 400 boys and 1,000 girls.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage of entries that were girls for each course. Give answers to one decimal place where needed. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain one sociological reason for the pattern shown. **[3]**

**6.** Explain two ways in which teacher expectations may affect girls' and boys' experiences in the classroom. **[6]**

**7.** Explain two reasons why some working-class boys may under-achieve, using the idea of a crisis of masculinity. **[6]**

**8.** Read the item.

> At Weybourne School, a fictional school, a group of Year 10 girls with high marks avoid answering questions in mixed classes. One explains, "If you look too keen, the boys make comments, and other girls say you're showing off."

Using the item and sociological material, explain two ways in which peer groups may shape girls' classroom behaviour. **[6]**

## Section B – Arguments against a view and essays

**9.** "Girls now do better than boys because schools have become biased in favour of girls."

Using sociological material, give two arguments against this view. **[8]**

**10.** "Gender inequality in education has now been overcome."

Using sociological material, give two arguments against this view. **[8]**

**11.** Evaluate the view that changes in wider society explain why girls now achieve more than boys. **[26]**

**12.** Evaluate the view that the under-achievement of boys is mainly caused by a crisis of masculinity. **[26]**

## Answers

**1.** The process of learning the norms, values and expectations attached to being male or female [1] through agencies such as the family, peers, media and school [1]. **[2]**

**2.** A fundamental shift in young women's values and attitudes [1] towards independence, paid work and careers rather than domestic roles [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Macho lads were working-class, anti-school boys who valued toughness and rejected teachers' authority [1], seeing schoolwork as unmasculine [1]. Real Englishmen were middle-class boys [1] who presented their achievement as effortless while doing well [1]. (Credit academic achievers or new enterprisers with accurate description.) **[4]**

**4.** In the early 1970s, working-class girls in London ranked love, marriage, husbands and children as their main priorities [1], with work seen as less important [1]. By the early 1990s, their priorities had shifted to jobs, careers [1] and being able to support themselves independently [1]. **[4]**

**5. (a)** Physics: 300 ÷ 1,500 × 100 = 20.0% girls [1]. Languages: 1,000 ÷ 1,400 × 100 = 71.4% girls [1].
**(b)** Gender socialisation and the gendered curriculum lead pupils to see physics as a male subject and languages as female [1]; subject images, textbook examples and teachers' and peers' expectations reinforce this [1], so choices follow gender even when attainment is similar, showing that patterns persist despite girls' overall improvement [1]. **[5]**

**6.** Way 1: teachers may underestimate girls [1]. Stanworth (1983) found teachers found boys easier to remember and describe, and girls underestimated their own ability compared with boys of similar attainment [1], which may lower girls' confidence in some subjects [1].
Way 2: boys receive more attention, much of it disciplinary [1]. Boys' behaviour draws teachers' focus, so classroom talk is dominated by boys [1], but disciplinary attention may reinforce anti-school identities rather than raise attainment [1]. **[6]**

**7.** Reason 1: decline of manual work [1]. Jobs that once offered working-class men a breadwinner role without qualifications have declined [1], so some boys see little point in schooling if they cannot see where it leads [1].
Reason 2: threatened identity [1]. Where masculinity is defined by toughness rather than study, as with Mac an Ghaill's macho lads, effort at school may feel like a loss of status [1]; boys may reject schoolwork to protect a masculine identity they feel is under threat [1]. **[6]**

**8.** Way 1: gendered peer policing [1]. Boys' "comments" about keen girls show how peers police acceptable femininity, similar to Lees's evidence that labels control girls' behaviour [1]; to avoid such labels, girls hide their ability in mixed classes [1].
Way 2: female peer norms [1]. Other girls calling keenness "showing off" suggests some friendship groups value not standing out, as Reay found different femininities in one class [1]; girls may balance achievement with acceptance by working privately rather than speaking up [1]. **[6]**

**9.** Argument 1: wider social change explains the shift [1]. Sharpe found girls' priorities moved from marriage to careers between the 1970s and 1990s [1], and Wilkinson's genderquake describes young women's new commitment to paid work [1]. These changes outside school give girls reasons to achieve, without any school bias [1].
Argument 2: evidence of classroom bias favours boys, not girls [1]. Stanworth found teachers underestimated girls and found boys easier to remember [1], and boys receive more classroom attention [1]. If anything, classroom processes favour boys' visibility, so bias cannot explain girls' success [1]. **[8]**

**10.** Argument 1: subject choice remains gendered [1]. Boys still dominate subjects such as physics and computing, girls subjects such as languages [1]. Radical feminists argue this channels women into lower-paid routes [1], so higher overall results have not removed inequality [1].
Argument 2: peer and classroom processes still constrain girls [1]. Lees showed labels about sexual reputation control girls' behaviour and confidence [1], and girls may hide ability to avoid peer comment [1]. Equal results can coexist with unequal experiences of schooling [1]. **[8]**

**11.** Marked by levels of response; this is indicative content.

- **Knowledge (AO1):** Sharpe (1976; 1994); Wilkinson (1994); employment and family change; equal opportunities; the crisis of masculinity; Mac an Ghaill's masculinities; socialisation (Reay, 2001); Stanworth (1983); Lees (1993).
- **Application (AO2):** patterns in a named country; subject choice data; post-industrial labour markets.
- **Analysis and evaluation (AO3):** wider change explains the timing of the reversal, which socialisation alone cannot; in-school factors and subcultures explain how the gap is maintained; class and ethnicity cut across gender; liberal and radical feminist interpretations; moral panic about boys.
- **Judgement:** for example, wider social change is the best explanation of why the gap reversed, but school processes shape its size and variation.

**Level guidance:** top answers separate explanations of change from explanations of persistence and weigh them; middle answers list factors; low answers rely on assumptions about boys and girls.

**12.** Marked by levels of response; this is indicative content.

- **For:** decline of manual work and the breadwinner role; Mac an Ghaill's macho lads; laddish cultures that reject effort; working-class boys' uncertain futures.
- **Against:** middle-class boys still achieve highly, so class matters; most boys' results have risen; girls' improvement may explain the gap more than boys' decline; teacher expectations and classroom dynamics; concern about boys as a moral panic.
- **Evaluation:** the argument fits some groups of boys better than others; it is hard to measure "crisis"; consider evidence from more than one society.
- **Judgement:** for example, the crisis helps explain some working-class boys' disengagement but not the overall gender gap.

**Level guidance:** top answers test the explanation against different groups of boys and alternative causes; middle answers describe the crisis with limited evaluation; low answers treat it as fact.
