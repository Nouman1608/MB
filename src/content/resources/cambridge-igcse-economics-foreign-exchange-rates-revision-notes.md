## Key definitions (6.3.1)

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Foreign exchange rate | How much of one currency buys a unit of another; the price of one currency in the other |
| Appreciation | A rise in the value of a currency against another |
| Depreciation | A fall in the value of a currency against another |
| Floating system | The rate is set by demand and supply in the foreign exchange market |
| Fixed system | The government or central bank sets a rate and acts to hold it |
| Foreign exchange reserves | Stocks of foreign currency held by the central bank, used to defend a fixed rate |

Setting: Country M uses the mira, Country N uses the nubi, and 1 mira = 1.50 nubi.

## Conversions and percentage changes

| Step | Rule | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Quoted currency into the other | Multiply by the rate | 75 miras × 1.50 = 112.5 nubi |
| Back again | Divide by the rate | 270 nubi ÷ 1.50 = 180 miras |
| Percentage change | (new − old) ÷ old × 100 | (1.80 − 1.50) ÷ 1.50 = +20% |

The other currency moves by a different percentage. At 1.50 one nubi is worth 1 ÷ 1.50 = 0.667 miras. After the mira falls to 1.20 it is worth 1 ÷ 1.20 = 0.833 miras. The mira fell by (1.20 − 1.50) ÷ 1.50 = −20%. The nubi rose by (0.833 − 0.667) ÷ 0.667 = +25%.

## Equilibrium in the foreign exchange market (6.3.2)

Diagram in words: vertical axis, price of the mira in nubi; horizontal axis, quantity of miras traded. The demand curve slopes down. The supply curve slopes up. They cross at the equilibrium rate.

- Demand for miras comes from foreigners buying Country M's exports, services and assets.
- Supply of miras comes from Country M's residents buying imports and foreign assets.
- Above equilibrium there is a surplus of miras, so the rate falls. Below equilibrium there is a shortage, so the rate rises.
- Under a fixed rate the same pressures exist, but the authorities buy or sell currency, or alter interest rates, to stop the rate moving.

Method for any shift question:

1. Say which currency's market you are in.
2. Say which curve moves (demand or supply) and why.
3. Say which way it moves.
4. State the new equilibrium rate: higher (appreciation) or lower (depreciation).

## Causes of fluctuations (6.3.3)

| Cause | Curve | Result for the mira |
|---|---|---|
| More exports bought by foreigners | Demand right | Appreciates |
| More imports bought by Country M | Supply right | Depreciates |
| Higher interest rates in Country M | Demand right, supply left | Appreciates |
| Lower interest rates in Country M | Demand left, supply right | Depreciates |
| Speculators expect a rise | Demand right | Appreciates |
| Speculators expect a fall | Supply right | Depreciates |
| MNC enters, builds a plant | Demand right | Appreciates |
| MNC departs, sends funds home | Supply right | Depreciates |

Reasoning for interest rates: higher rates mean a better return on savings held in miras, so foreign savers buy miras to deposit them, and residents keep more savings at home. Speculation can be self-fulfilling, because selling a currency expected to fall pushes it down.

## Consequences (6.3.4)

| Change | Export price abroad | Import price at home |
|---|---|---|
| Appreciation | Rises | Falls |
| Depreciation | Falls | Rises |

Worked reminder. A table costs 300 miras. At 1.50 a nubi buyer pays 300 × 1.50 = 450 nubi. At 1.20 the price is 300 × 1.20 = 360 nubi, a fall of 20%. A machine costs 600 nubi. At 1.50 it costs 600 ÷ 1.50 = 400 miras. At 1.20 it costs 600 ÷ 1.20 = 500 miras, a rise of 25%.

Spending rule: % change in quantity = PED × % change in price. Spending = price × quantity.

| Price change | Elastic demand | Inelastic demand |
|---|---|---|
| Price rises | Spending falls | Spending rises |
| Price falls | Spending rises | Spending falls |

Worked reminder. The depreciation cuts the nubi price of tables by 20%. Exports are 1,000 tables, PED = −1.5. Quantity rises 1.5 × 20 = 30%, to 1,300. Spending by nubi buyers goes from 1,000 × 450 = 450,000 to 1,300 × 360 = 468,000 nubi, up 4%. Demand is elastic, so lower prices raise spending.

Evaluation: effects depend on the size of the PED, on how long the change lasts (demand tends to be more elastic over time), and on what other countries do.

Method for a PED spending question:

1. Work out the new price in the buyer's currency.
2. Find the percentage change in price.
3. Multiply by PED to get the percentage change in quantity.
4. Find the new quantity and multiply by the new price.
5. Compare spending before and after, and link the result to elastic or inelastic demand.

## Floating versus fixed (6.3.5)

| | Floating | Fixed |
|---|---|---|
| For | Self-correcting; no reserves needed; interest rates free for domestic aims | Certainty for traders; supports trade and investment; can restrain inflation |
| Against | Uncertainty; large swings; speculation; depreciation raises import prices | Reserves can run out; interest rates tied to defending the rate; rate may be set wrongly |

Worked reminder. Country M fixes 1 mira = 1.50 nubi. At that rate supply of miras exceeds demand by 8 million per week. The central bank buys 8 million miras, paying 8 × 1.50 = 12 million nubi per week from reserves. With 84 million nubi in reserves it can do this for 84 ÷ 12 = 7 weeks. Then it must raise interest rates, change the fixed rate or find more reserves.

Short judgement: a small economy that trades mostly with one partner may gain from a fixed rate if it has large reserves. A large economy with varied trade may prefer to float.

## Must-know distinctions

- Appreciation and depreciation describe a market-driven change in a currency's value. Under a fixed system, the authorities may alter the fixed rate itself.
- Exports create demand for the home currency. Imports create supply of it.
- Elastic demand: spending moves opposite to price. Inelastic demand: spending moves with price.
- A fall in the rate helps exporters' prices but raises the cost of imported inputs and goods.

## Quick self-test

1. Define a foreign exchange rate.
2. At 1 mira = 1.50 nubi, convert 75 miras into nubi.
3. Convert 270 nubi into miras.
4. The rate moves from 1.50 to 1.80 nubi per mira. State the change and its percentage.
5. Households in Country M want more imports. Which curve shifts, and what happens to the mira?
6. Explain why a higher interest rate in Country M may raise the value of the mira.
7. An import costs 450 nubi. Find its price in miras at 1.50 and at 1.80, and the percentage change.
8. A table costs 300 miras. Find the nubi price at 1.50 and at 1.20, and the percentage change.
9. Imports of a good have PED = −0.6. Its price in miras rises from 400 to 500. Country M first bought 300 units. Find the new quantity and the change in spending.
10. State one advantage and one disadvantage of a fixed exchange rate.

### Answers

1. How much of one currency buys one unit of another; the price of one currency in the other.
2. 75 × 1.50 = 112.5 nubi.
3. 270 ÷ 1.50 = 180 miras.
4. An appreciation of the mira: (1.80 − 1.50) ÷ 1.50 × 100 = +20%.
5. Supply of miras shifts right (more miras sold to buy nubi). The mira depreciates.
6. Foreign savers must buy miras to deposit them, so demand rises, and residents keep more savings at home, so supply falls. The rate rises.
7. At 1.50: 450 ÷ 1.50 = 300 miras. At 1.80: 450 ÷ 1.80 = 250 miras. Change = (250 − 300) ÷ 300 × 100 = −16.7%.
8. At 1.50: 450 nubi. At 1.20: 360 nubi. Change = −20%.
9. Price rise = 25%, so quantity falls 0.6 × 25 = 15%, to 255. Spending rises from 300 × 400 = 120,000 to 255 × 500 = 127,500 miras, because demand is inelastic.
10. Advantage: certainty for traders. Disadvantage: needs reserves to defend the rate, and these can run out.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Mixing up the direction of conversion: multiply when going into the other currency, divide when coming back.
- Saying "the currency rises" without saying against which currency.
- Shifting the wrong curve: more imports shift supply of the home currency, not demand.
- Writing that a depreciation always improves the trade position, with no mention of PED or time.
- Forgetting that spending is price times quantity, and stopping at the quantity change.
- Using the same percentage for both currencies.
- Describing a fixed rate as "never changing" when the authorities can change it.
- Listing advantages and disadvantages with no judgement about which suits which economy.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is a foreign exchange rate?**
A foreign exchange rate is the price of one currency in terms of another: how much of one currency buys a unit of another.

**Who demands and supplies a currency?**
Demand for a currency comes from foreigners buying the country's exports, services and assets. Supply comes from the country's residents selling their currency to buy imports and foreign assets.

**Why do higher interest rates make a currency appreciate?**
Higher interest rates attract foreign savers, who must buy the currency first, so demand rises; residents also keep more savings at home, so supply falls. Both push the exchange rate up.

**Does a depreciation always improve a country's trade position?**
No. A depreciation makes exports cheaper abroad and imports dearer at home, but it improves the trade position most when demand for exports and imports is price elastic. In the short run demand is often less elastic.
