## Section A

**1.** Define the term "mass movement". **[2]**

**2.** Distinguish between a slide and a flow. **[2]**

**3.** Explain how heave moves soil particles downslope. **[3]**

**4.** State **two** pieces of field evidence for soil creep. **[2]**

**5.** Explain why rainsplash produces a net downslope movement of soil on a slope. **[2]**

**6.** Distinguish between sheetwash and rills. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** On a fictional clay slope, shear strength is 120 kPa and shear stress is 80 kPa in dry conditions. After a week of heavy rain, shear strength is 78 kPa and shear stress is 88 kPa.

**(a)** Calculate the factor of safety in dry and in wet conditions. Show your working. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain why heavy rain changes both shear strength and shear stress. **[3]**

**(c)** Suggest the type of mass movement most likely on this slope after the rain. **[1]**

**8.** Students measured soil moved in one wet season at three points on a fictional bare slope.

| Position | Soil moved by rainsplash (g/m²) | Soil moved by surface runoff (g/m²) |
|---|---|---|
| Upper slope | 40 | 10 |
| Middle slope | 35 | 60 |
| Lower slope | 30 | 140 |

**(a)** Describe the changes in soil movement down the slope. **[3]**

**(b)** Explain these changes. **[4]**

**9.** Explain the conditions under which rockfalls occur and their effect on slope form. **[5]**

**10.** Compare heave and flows as types of mass movement. **[6]**

**11.** Explain how vegetation affects slope processes. **[5]**

**12.** "Water is the most important factor in all slope processes." Discuss this view. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** The downslope movement of soil, rock or debris under the influence of gravity [1], as a mass rather than carried by a transporting agent such as a river [1]. **[2]**

**2.** In a slide, a coherent mass of material moves along a distinct slip surface without deforming much internally [1]. In a flow, saturated material deforms internally and moves like a viscous fluid [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Wetting or freezing makes the soil expand, lifting particles at right angles to the slope surface [1]. Drying or thawing makes the soil contract, and particles fall back vertically under gravity [1]. Each cycle leaves particles slightly further downslope, so soil moves very slowly downhill [1]. **[3]**

**4.** Two from: terracettes [1]; trees with curved bases [1]. Accept leaning walls, fences or telegraph poles, or soil banked up on the upslope side of walls, in place of either. **[2]**

**5.** Raindrops throw particles outwards in all directions [1], but on a slope particles thrown downslope travel further before landing than those thrown upslope, so more soil ends up below its starting point [1]. **[2]**

**6.** Sheetwash is a thin, unchannelled layer of water flowing across the surface [1]. Rills are small channels, a few centimetres deep, where runoff has concentrated and erodes in lines [1]. **[2]**

**7. (a)** Dry: 120 / 80 = 1.5 [1]; wet: 78 / 88 = 0.89 [1].

**(b)** Water raises pore water pressure, pushing particles apart and reducing friction, and softens clay, lowering its cohesion, so strength falls [1] [1]. Water also adds weight to the slope material, so the downslope component of stress rises [1].

**(c)** A rotational slide (slump) [1]. **[6]**

**8. (a)** Rainsplash decreases slightly downslope, from 40 to 30 g/m² [1]. Runoff increases greatly, from 10 to 140 g/m² [1]. Rainsplash is dominant on the upper slope, but runoff is dominant on the middle and lower slope [1].

**(b)** Rainsplash depends on raindrop impact, which is similar along the slope [1], so it changes little, though sealed or wetter lower soils may splash less [1]. Runoff increases downslope because water collects from all the slope above, so flow becomes deeper and faster [1], and it concentrates into rills that erode more soil [1]. **[7]**

**9.** Rockfalls occur on steep, bare rock faces [1] where joints and bedding planes are opened by freeze-thaw, pressure release or root action [1], or where the base is undercut by a river or the sea [1]. Blocks detach and fall freely, so the free face retreats while keeping its steep angle [1]. Fallen debris builds up at the foot as a scree slope at its angle of rest [1]. **[5]**

**10.** Both involve material deforming internally rather than moving as a coherent block [1]. Heave is extremely slow, only millimetres or centimetres a year [1], whereas flows can move at metres per second [1]. Heave occurs in moist but not saturated soil, through repeated expansion and contraction [1], while flows need saturated material behaving like a fluid [1]. Heave gives gradual convex slope profiles, while flows strip source areas and deposit lobes or fans [1]. **[6]**

**11.** Roots bind soil and increase its shear strength [1]. A canopy and leaf litter intercept rain and protect the surface from rainsplash [1]. Vegetation increases infiltration, reducing sheetwash and rill formation [1]. Transpiration dries the soil, lowering pore water pressure and so reducing slides and flows [1]. However, large trees add weight and can lever rock apart with roots, and wind-rocked trees can loosen soil [1]. **[5]**

**12.** For the view: water raises pore water pressure and adds weight, triggering most slides [1]. Flows need saturated material [1]. Heave depends on wetting and drying or freezing and thawing [1]. Rainsplash, sheetwash and rills are driven entirely by rainfall and runoff [1].

Against the view: rockfalls happen on dry faces, triggered by weathering, undercutting or earthquakes [1]. Slope angle and rock type and structure control where failures can happen at all [1]. Earthquakes can trigger slides in dry conditions [1].

Judgement: water is the most common trigger and is central to most processes, but gravity drives all of them and slope angle and geology set the conditions, so water is very important rather than the only factor [1]. **[8]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calculating stress divided by strength instead of strength divided by stress.
- Explaining only the effect of water on strength and forgetting its weight.
- Describing a table without quoting values.
- Explaining rockfalls without saying how the slope form changes.
- Treating "landslide" and "flow" as interchangeable.
