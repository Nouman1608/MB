Sub-topic 6.3 of the Cambridge O Level Environmental Management 5014 syllabus (2027-2029) has five outcomes: the causes of flooding (6.3.1), its impacts (6.3.2), strategies for managing those impacts before, during and after a flood (6.3.3), a discussion of the benefits and limitations of those strategies (6.3.4), and the opportunities flooding provides (6.3.5). Every list is closed, so you can prepare a reasoned explanation for each named item. This guide groups the causes by how they work, explains each impact as a chain, and shows how to write a balanced discussion of management.

For an overview of all four hazards, see the [natural hazards study guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-environmental-management-natural-hazards-study-guide/).

## The basic idea

A river floods when more water reaches it than the channel can carry, so it overflows its banks. A coast floods when the sea rises above the land behind it. Every cause in the syllabus works in one of three ways: it **sends more water**, it sends water **faster** (less soaks into the ground), or it **raises the sea**. Sorting the causes this way makes explanations quick.

## Causes of flooding (6.3.1)

**(a) Land relief.** Relief is the shape and height of the land. On steep slopes, rain runs off quickly into rivers instead of soaking in, so river levels rise fast. Flat, low-lying land such as floodplains and deltas is where the water collects, so it floods most often.

**(b) Saturated soil, compacted soil and soil erosion.**
- *Saturated* soil is already full of water after long rain, so any further rain cannot infiltrate and runs off.
- *Compacted* soil has been squashed (by machinery, animals or people), closing the pore spaces, so infiltration is slow.
- *Soil erosion* removes the soil layer that would store water, and the eroded sediment is deposited in rivers, reducing the volume the channel can hold.

**(c) Deforestation, agricultural practices and urbanisation.**
- *Deforestation* removes interception by leaves and water uptake by roots, so more rain reaches rivers quickly.
- *Agricultural practices* such as ploughing up and down slopes, overgrazing, heavy machinery and draining wetlands increase run-off and reduce storage.
- *Urbanisation* replaces soil with impermeable concrete, tarmac and roofs. Rain cannot infiltrate, and drains carry it straight to rivers, so the river rises higher and faster.

**(d) Climate change.** A *rise in sea level* puts low coasts at greater risk, and an *increase in extreme weather events* brings more intense rainfall and stronger storms.

**(e) Storm surges and tsunamis.** A storm surge is a rise in sea level as low pressure and strong winds push water onshore during a storm such as a tropical cyclone. A tsunami is a series of large waves caused when an earthquake, eruption or landslide displaces sea water. Both cause sudden coastal flooding.

### Worked example 1: urbanisation data

*The figures are invented for practice.* A river catchment covers 40 km². In 2000, 6 km² was built up; by 2025, 14 km² was built up.

- Built-up share in 2000 = 6 / 40 x 100 = **15%**.
- Built-up share in 2025 = 14 / 40 x 100 = **35%**.
- Increase in built-up area = 14 - 6 = 8 km², a percentage increase of 8 / 6 x 100 = **133%** (to three significant figures).

Interpretation: more than a third of the catchment is now impermeable, so more rain reaches the river through drains, and faster. Note the difference between the change in **share** (15% to 35%, a rise of 20 percentage points) and the percentage increase in **area** (133%). Questions often test whether you can tell them apart.

## Impacts of flooding (6.3.2)

The syllabus lists nine impacts.

| Impact | How it happens |
|---|---|
| Damage to buildings and infrastructure | Water and debris damage homes, roads, bridges, power and water supplies |
| Loss of crops | Fields are submerged; crops rot or are washed away |
| Livestock and habitats | Animals drown; habitats are flooded or covered with sediment |
| Evacuation of people and livestock | People must leave homes, often for weeks |
| Landslides | Saturated slopes fail |
| Contamination of drinking-water supplies | Floodwater mixes with sewage and chemicals and enters wells and pipes |
| Water-related disease | Contaminated water and standing water spread disease |
| Financial losses | Repairs, lost harvests, closed businesses, insurance costs |
| Human health | Drowning, injuries, illness and stress |

## Managing the impacts (6.3.3 and 6.3.4)

| Stage | Strategy | Benefit | Limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Before | **Monitoring and warning**: rain gauges and river-level sensors, weather forecasts | River floods can be forecast hours or days ahead | Flash floods and tsunamis give little warning; warnings must reach everyone |
| Before | **Land use zoning**: keep homes and key services off the land most at risk; use it for farming, parks or playing fields | Cheap; fewer people and buildings exposed | Land is scarce in growing cities; existing buildings stay at risk |
| Before | **Structure of buildings**: raised floors, homes on stilts, waterproof materials, electrics placed high | Less damage and faster recovery | Extra cost; difficult for existing or informal housing |
| Before | **Disaster preparation**: plans, drills, emergency supplies and emergency rescue teams | Organised, faster response | Needs funds and practice |
| During | **Evacuation** | Saves lives | Needs warning, transport and trust |
| During | **Shelters** on high ground | Safe refuge | Overcrowding and disease |
| After | **Rebuilding of damaged areas** | Restores homes and services; can build more flood-resistant | Slow and costly; may rebuild in the same risky place |
| After | **International aid** | Money, food, clean water, medicine | May be slow or unevenly distributed |

### Worked example 2: annotated model answer

**Question:** Discuss the benefits and limitations of land use zoning and the structure of buildings for managing the impacts of flooding in a growing city. **[6]**

**Answer:** Land use zoning keeps new housing and hospitals off the land that floods most often, so fewer people and buildings are exposed *(benefit, 1)*. It is cheap because it uses planning rules rather than construction *(benefit, 1)*. However, in a fast-growing city land is scarce, and poor families may build informal homes on the floodplain anyway *(limitation, 1)*. Improving the structure of buildings, for example raising floors and using waterproof materials, protects people who already live in risk areas *(benefit, 1)*, but it is expensive and hard to apply to existing or informal homes *(limitation, 1)*. Zoning is better for new development and building structure for existing areas, so the city needs both *(judgement, 1)*.

## Opportunities from flooding (6.3.5)

- **Deposition of nutrient-rich silt on farmland.** When a river spills onto its floodplain, the water slows and drops the fine sediment it was carrying. The silt adds nutrients and renews soil fertility, so floodplains and deltas are among the most productive farmland.
- **Recharge of surface water and ground water stores.** Floodwater refills lakes, wetlands and reservoirs, and soaks down to refill aquifers, which supply wells and irrigation in the dry season.

These opportunities help explain why so many people live and farm on floodplains despite the risk, which links back to land use zoning: removing people completely also removes the benefits.

## Common mistakes

- Writing "heavy rain causes floods" without the mechanism. Explain why the water could not soak in or why the channel could not hold it.
- Confusing saturated soil (full of water) with compacted soil (squashed, few pores).
- Leaving out storm surges and tsunamis: they are named causes.
- Mixing up percentage-point change and percentage change in data questions.
- Saying floods have no benefits. Two opportunities are named in 6.3.5.

## Exam technique

**Explain** causes using "so" chains: urbanisation → impermeable surfaces → less infiltration → rapid run-off through drains → river overflows. **Describe** a strategy by saying what is done and when (before, during or after). **Discuss** needs a benefit and a limitation for each strategy and a judgement suited to the place in the question. **Suggest** questions may give a map of a town on a river: use the map features, such as housing on low land or forest cleared upstream.

## Check your understanding

1. How does compacted soil increase flood risk? *Its closed pores slow infiltration, so more water runs off.*
2. Name the two coastal causes of flooding in 6.3.1(e). *Storm surges and tsunamis.*
3. Give one benefit of land use zoning. *Fewer people and buildings in the area at risk.*
4. A catchment is 25 km² with 5 km² built up. What percentage is built up? *20%.*
5. State the two opportunities flooding provides. *Nutrient-rich silt on farmland; recharge of surface and ground water stores.*
