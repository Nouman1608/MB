## What 2.1.3 contains

The specification groups the content under three headings.

| Heading in the specification | Bullet points |
|---|---|
| The impact of globalisation on businesses | imports: competition from overseas, buying from overseas; exports: selling to overseas markets; changing business locations; multinationals |
| Barriers to international trade | tariffs; trade blocs |
| How businesses compete internationally | the use of the internet and e-commerce; changing the marketing mix to compete internationally |

The specification does not define globalisation. The usual meaning is the growing links between the economies of different countries, so that goods, services, money and businesses move across borders more freely. Notice that every bullet is about the **impact on a business**. Examiners reward answers that say what a change means for costs, sales, prices or competitors of the business in the question, not answers that describe world trade in general.

## Imports: two different effects

Imports are goods and services bought from other countries. The specification splits them into two effects, and they push in opposite directions.

**Competition from overseas.** A UK business may find that foreign producers sell similar products more cheaply, perhaps because their wages or materials cost less. The UK business then has a choice: cut its price and accept lower profit per unit, differentiate the product so that customers will pay more, or find cheaper inputs itself.

**Buying from overseas.** The same business may become an importer: buying materials, components or finished goods from abroad because they are cheaper, better or simply not made at home. The price on the supplier's list is never the full cost. The **landed cost** (a standard term, not specification wording) adds freight, insurance and any tariff.

### Worked example 1: is the overseas supplier really cheaper?

Saltmarsh Kitchenware sells frying pans. A UK manufacturer supplies them at £9.80 each. An overseas factory quotes £6.20 a pan, plus £0.85 freight per pan and a tariff of 5% on the £6.20 value. Saltmarsh buys 5,000 pans a year.

1. Tariff per pan = 5% × £6.20 = £0.31
2. Landed cost = £6.20 + £0.85 + £0.31 = £7.36
3. Saving per pan = £9.80 − £7.36 = £2.44
4. Annual saving = £2.44 × 5,000 = **£12,200**

The figures favour importing. An answer that stops here is incomplete. Delivery from overseas takes longer, so Saltmarsh must hold more stock; a faulty shipment is harder to return; and the saving could shrink if freight costs rise. A strong conclusion weighs the £12,200 against these risks.

## Exports: selling to overseas markets

Exports are goods and services sold to customers in other countries. Reasons a growing business exports:

- The home market is saturated or highly competitive, so growth has to come from elsewhere.
- More customers means higher output, which can lower the cost of each unit.
- Selling in several countries spreads risk: a slump in one market hurts less.

Problems: the business has to understand different tastes, languages and laws; transport and paperwork add cost; payment can be slower; and the product may face a tariff when it enters the customer's country.

### Worked example 2: should the exporter absorb a tariff?

Lowther Knitwear sells jumpers to a retailer in a country outside any trade bloc that includes the UK. It charges £40 a jumper, each costs £26 to make, and the retailer orders 2,000 a year. The country introduces a 10% tariff, paid by the importing retailer.

- **Option A: keep the price at £40.** The retailer now pays £44 per jumper including the tariff and expects to cut its order to 1,500. Contribution = (£40 − £26) × 1,500 = **£21,000**.
- **Option B: cut the price to £36.40.** The retailer pays £36.40 + 10% = £40.04, almost the old cost, so it keeps ordering 2,000. Contribution = (£36.40 − £26) × 2,000 = **£20,800**.

The difference is only £200. Option A keeps profit per jumper but loses volume and possibly the relationship with the retailer. Option B protects market share at almost no extra cost. A good "Justify" answer picks one and explains why the other risk matters less to this business.

## Changing business locations

Globalisation makes it easier to move production, offices or call centres to another country. The decision is a cost-and-risk comparison.

| Reasons to relocate | Reasons for caution |
|---|---|
| Lower wages or cheaper land and energy | Redundancy and closure costs at home |
| Being inside a market to avoid its tariffs | Quality is harder to supervise at a distance |
| Closer to fast-growing customers, so delivery is cheaper and quicker | Reputation damage if UK jobs are lost |
| Access to skills or materials not available at home | Different laws, language and culture to manage |

Location decisions can also run the other way. Some businesses move work back to the UK when overseas wages rise, when shipping becomes slow or unreliable, or when customers value "made in the UK".

## Multinationals

A multinational is a business with operations, such as factories, offices or shops, in more than one country. Examiners often ask about the impact on the business or on the country that hosts it.

- **For the multinational:** a larger market, lower costs from locating where inputs are cheap, and less reliance on one economy. Against that, it is complex to co-ordinate, and bad news from one country can harm the brand everywhere.
- **For the host country:** jobs, training, tax revenue and spending with local suppliers. Against that, local businesses may struggle to compete, profits may be sent back to the head office country, and the multinational may leave if costs rise.

For a small UK business, a multinational arriving nearby can be a threat (a powerful rival, or competition for staff) or an opportunity (a large new customer).

## Barriers to international trade

**Tariffs.** A tariff is a tax on imported goods. It raises the price of imports in the country that charges it. For a UK importer, a tariff on goods coming into the UK raises costs. For a UK exporter, a tariff charged by the customer's country makes its product dearer there, as Lowther found.

**Trade blocs.** A trade bloc is a group of countries that remove or reduce barriers such as tariffs on trade between members. Members usually charge a common tariff on goods from outside the bloc; the European Union is an example. The effect depends on where the business sits:

- A business **inside** the bloc can sell to other members more easily and faces less paperwork and fewer tariffs.
- A business **outside** the bloc faces the bloc's external tariff, so it is at a price disadvantage against firms inside.

### Worked example 3: getting inside the barrier

Lowther also sells 8,000 jumpers a year into a trade bloc that charges a 12% tariff on their £40 value. The tariff bill is 12% × £40 × 8,000 = **£38,400** a year. If Lowther could run a finishing and distribution unit inside the bloc for £30,000 a year more than its current costs, and that removed the tariff, it would save £8,400 a year. In reality the rules on where goods count as made are complicated, so treat this as a simplified example of why firms relocate.

## How businesses compete internationally

**The internet and e-commerce.** A website lets a small firm reach customers worldwide without opening shops abroad. Online marketplaces handle payment and sometimes delivery. Weaknesses: shipping costs and delivery times, handling returns from abroad, and competing with thousands of other sellers for attention.

**Changing the marketing mix.** A business can sell the same product everywhere or adapt it. Adapting each element costs money but can win customers who would reject a UK version.

| Element | Example of adapting for an overseas market |
|---|---|
| Product | Change sizes, ingredients, plugs, labelling language or packaging to local rules and tastes |
| Price | Set prices for local incomes and rivals, allowing for freight and tariffs |
| Promotion | Translate adverts, use local social media platforms and avoid images that offend locally |
| Place | Use a local distributor or marketplace, or a warehouse inside the country for faster delivery |

## Common mistakes

- Treating imports only as a threat. Buying from overseas can cut a business's costs.
- Comparing an overseas supplier's list price with a UK price without adding freight and tariffs.
- Saying the exporter pays the tariff. It is charged on imports, so the importer pays, though the exporter may cut its price to compensate.
- Describing a trade bloc as a barrier for everyone. It reduces barriers for members and raises them for outsiders.
- Writing "multinationals are good for the economy" with no link to the business or country in the question.

## Exam technique for 2.1.3

- **Define:** give the meaning of a term such as tariff or multinational in one precise sentence.
- **Outline:** two linked points tied to the context, for example "The tariff raises the price of Lowther's jumpers in that country, so the retailer may order fewer."
- **Explain:** a statement plus two expansion points; these questions have no context, so stay general but connected.
- **Calculate:** show each step of a landed cost, tariff or percentage calculation.
- **Analyse:** build a chain from the global change to the impact on this business's costs, prices or sales.
- **Justify:** choose one of two options (import or buy at home, absorb or pass on a tariff) using the figures, then say why the rejected option is weaker.
- **Evaluate:** weigh the benefits against risks such as reliability and reputation and reach a conclusion that depends on stated factors.
