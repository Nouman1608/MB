## Questions

**1.** Explain why connecting a second identical resistor in series with the first increases the net resistance of a circuit. **[2]**

**2.** A student connects a second identical lamp in parallel with the first across the same cell. Explain why the net resistance of the circuit decreases. **[3]**

**3.** A 6.0 V supply is connected across three resistors in series: 5.0 Ω, 7.0 Ω and 3.0 Ω. Calculate the current and the p.d. across the 7.0 Ω resistor. **[4]**

**4.** A 20 Ω resistor and a 30 Ω resistor are connected in parallel across a 12 V supply. Calculate the supply current and the net resistance of the pair. **[4]**

**5.** A 16 V supply is connected to a 5.0 Ω resistor in series with a parallel pair of resistors. One of the pair is 12 Ω. An ammeter in the supply lead reads 2.0 A.

**(a)** Calculate the p.d. across the 5.0 Ω resistor. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the current in the 12 Ω resistor. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the resistance of the other resistor in the pair. **[2]**

**6.** A student wants to find the resistance of a filament lamp.

**(a)** Draw or describe the circuit, stating where the ammeter and the voltmeter are placed. **[3]**
**(b)** The voltmeter reads 4.5 V and the ammeter reads 0.30 A. Calculate the resistance. **[2]**

**7.** An NTC thermistor is in series with a 300 Ω fixed resistor on a 9.0 V supply. A voltmeter is connected across the fixed resistor and reads 3.0 V.

**(a)** Find the current flowing round the circuit. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the resistance of the thermistor. **[2]**
**(c)** The thermistor is heated until its resistance is 200 Ω. Calculate the new reading on the voltmeter. **[2]**

**8.** A lamp is run at 12 V and draws 2.5 A. Find its power, then the energy transferred during a 10 minute test. **[4]**

**9.** A heating element of resistance 8.0 Ω carries a current of 3.0 A. Calculate its power, and the energy transferred in 2.0 minutes. **[4]**

**10.** An electric heater has a power of 2.5 kW and runs for 4.0 hours. Calculate the energy transferred in kW h, and then in joules (1 kW h = 3 600 000 J). **[4]**

**11.** A motor connected to a 12 V supply has 45 C of charge pass through it in 9.0 s. Calculate the energy transferred, the current and the power. **[4]**

**12.** A 12 V supply is connected to a 10 Ω resistor and a 20 Ω resistor in series, for 90 s.

**(a)** State the equivalent resistance of the circuit. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the current. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the p.d. across each resistor. **[2]**
**(d)** Calculate the power in each resistor and the total power. **[3]**
**(e)** Calculate the total energy transferred in 90 s, and show that the charge passing is consistent with E = Q × V. **[2]**
**(f)** The 20 Ω resistor is replaced by a 30 Ω resistor. State and explain what happens to the current. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** Charge has to pass through both resistors in turn, so there is more opposition to the current [1]. Their resistances add, so the net resistance is greater [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* "More wire" or "resistance is used up" gains little. State the single path and the addition of opposition.

**2.** The second lamp provides an extra path for charge [1]. The p.d. across each lamp is the same as before, so the total current from the cell is larger [1]. With a larger current for the same p.d., R = V / I is smaller [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* A full answer links the extra path, the increased total current and the change in net resistance. Saying only "there is less resistance" restates the question.

**3.** Net R = 5.0 + 7.0 + 3.0 = 15 Ω [1]. I = V / R = 6.0 / 15 [1] = **0.40 A** [1]. p.d. across 7.0 Ω = 0.40 × 7.0 = **2.8 V** [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the equation and substitution. Keep the unit with the answer, and check that the three p.d.s (2.0, 2.8 and 1.2 V) add to 6.0 V.

**4.** Currents: 12 / 20 = 0.60 A and 12 / 30 = 0.40 A [1]. Supply current = 0.60 + 0.40 = **1.0 A** [1]. R = V / I = 12 / 1.0 [1] = **12 Ω** [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Both branches have the full 12 V, which is what makes the method work. A net resistance bigger than 20 Ω would be a red flag.

**5. (a)** p.d. = I × R = 2.0 × 5.0 [1] = **10 V** [1].
**(b)** p.d. across the pair = 16 − 10 = 6.0 V [1]. I = 6.0 / 12 = **0.50 A** [1].
**(c)** Current in the other resistor = 2.0 − 0.50 = 1.5 A [1]. R = 6.0 / 1.5 = **4.0 Ω** [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Part (b) needs the p.d. across the pair, not the supply p.d. Writing the intermediate value 6.0 V on the page shows your method.

**6. (a)** Lamp, ammeter and variable resistor (or cell) in series [1]. Ammeter in series with the lamp [1]. Voltmeter connected in parallel across the lamp [1].
**(b)** R = V / I = 4.5 / 0.30 [1] = **15 Ω** [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* A diagram with the voltmeter in the wrong position fails to show the correct placement, even if the equation is correct.

**7. (a)** I = V / R = 3.0 / 300 [1] = **0.010 A** [1].
**(b)** p.d. across thermistor = 9.0 − 3.0 = 6.0 V [1]. R = 6.0 / 0.010 = **600 Ω** [1].
**(c)** Net R = 300 + 200 = 500 Ω, so I = 9.0 / 500 = 0.018 A [1]. Voltmeter reading = 0.018 × 300 = **5.4 V** [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* A common slip is using the 9.0 V supply across the thermistor. State that the supply p.d. is shared between the two.

**8.** P = V × I = 12 × 2.5 [1] = **30 W** [1]. t = 10 × 60 = 600 s [1]. E = P × t = 30 × 600 = **18 000 J** [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Leaving time as 10 gives 300 J, which is wrong by a factor of 60. Convert minutes to seconds first.

**9.** P = I² × R = 3.0² × 8.0 [1] = **72 W** [1]. t = 120 s [1]. E = 72 × 120 = **8640 J** [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Square the current before multiplying by the resistance. Squaring the product I × R gives a very different value.

**10.** E = P × t = 2.5 × 4.0 [1] = **10 kW h** [1]. Energy in joules = 10 × 3 600 000 [1] = **36 000 000 J** (3.6 × 10⁷ J) [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Keep kW with hours for the first part, and convert to joules only at the end. Give each answer in the unit the question asks for.

**11.** E = Q × V = 45 × 12 [1] = **540 J** [1]. I = Q / t = 45 / 9.0 = **5.0 A** [1]. P = V × I = 12 × 5.0 = **60 W** [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* You can check the power with E / t = 540 / 9.0 = 60 W. A second route that agrees is a good way to catch an error.

**12. (a)** 10 + 20 = **30 Ω** [1].
**(b)** I = 12 / 30 [1] = **0.40 A** [1].
**(c)** 10 Ω resistor: 0.40 × 10 = **4.0 V** [1]. 20 Ω resistor: 0.40 × 20 = **8.0 V** [1].
**(d)** 10 Ω: 0.40² × 10 = **1.6 W** [1]. 20 Ω: 0.40² × 20 = **3.2 W** [1]. Total = **4.8 W**, which equals 12 × 0.40 [1].
**(e)** E = 4.8 × 90 = **432 J** [1]. Q = I × t = 0.40 × 90 = 36 C, and E = Q × V = 36 × 12 = 432 J, which agrees [1].
**(f)** The current decreases (to 12 / 40 = 0.30 A) [1] because the equivalent resistance increases for the same supply p.d. [1]. **[12]**
*Examiner insight:* In a multi-part question, set out each part separately. Visible working for each part lets the reader follow your method, even if an earlier value was wrong.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using the supply p.d. across one resistor in a series chain instead of its share.
- Adding branch resistances in parallel instead of working from branch currents.
- Placing a voltmeter in series, or an ammeter across a component.
- Forgetting to convert minutes to seconds in E = P × t, or hours to seconds in joule calculations.
- Squaring the wrong quantity in P = I² × R.
- Giving power in joules or energy in watts.
- Missing the unit, or writing a unit with the wrong case (for example mA for A without converting).
- Writing "current is used up" or "resistance is used up" in an explanation.
- Not showing substitution into the equation.
