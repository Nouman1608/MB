These notes condense content item 2.3.1 Business operations from Topic 2.3 Making operational decisions in Theme 2 of the Pearson Edexcel GCSE (9-1) Business (1BS0) specification, Issue 2, July 2022. Worked examples are in the [business operations study guide](/resources/edexcel-gcse-business-1bs0-business-operations/) and exam-style questions in the [business operations practice questions](/resources/edexcel-gcse-business-1bs0-business-operations-practice/). Businesses and figures are invented.

## The specification in one view

- **Purpose of operations:** to produce goods; to provide services.
- **Production processes:** job, batch, flow; their impact on keeping productivity up, costs down and allowing competitive prices.
- **Technology:** balancing cost, productivity, quality and flexibility.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Operations | Turning inputs (materials, labour, equipment) into goods or services |
| Job production | Making one product at a time, often to the customer's own specification |
| Batch production | Making a group of identical products together before switching to a different group |
| Flow production | Continuous production of identical products along a production line |
| Productivity | How efficiently inputs are turned into output |
| Labour productivity | Output ÷ number of workers, for a period (standard form) |
| Cost per unit | Total cost ÷ number of units produced (standard form) |
| Flexibility | How easily production can switch to different products or volumes |

## Comparing the processes

| | Job | Batch | Flow |
|---|---|---|---|
| Volume | Very low | Medium | Very high |
| Product variety | Unique | A range of standard products | One standard product |
| Skill of workers | High | Medium | Low, or machines |
| Cost per unit | Highest | Middle | Lowest |
| Flexibility | Highest | Middle | Lowest |
| Typical price | Premium | Mid-range | Low and competitive |
| Main risk | High prices limit sales | Idle time when switching between groups | Huge investment; a stoppage halts everything |

## Reading the clues in a case

| Clue in the case | Likely process | What to say about it |
|---|---|---|
| "Each order is designed with the customer" | Job | High value, high price; productivity is low but customers pay for uniqueness |
| "Makes 200 of each flavour, then cleans the machines" | Batch | Some variety at moderate cost; idle time while equipment is changed over |
| "The line runs 24 hours a day" | Flow | Very low unit cost and competitive prices, but little flexibility and a large investment |

## The chain the specification wants

Process → **productivity** (output per worker) → **cost per unit** → **competitive price**.

Example: a switch to flow raises output per worker, which spreads labour and fixed costs over more units, which lowers cost per unit, which lets the business price below rivals.

## Technology: balance the four factors

| Factor | Gain | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| Cost | Lower labour cost per unit, less waste | Purchase, maintenance, training |
| Productivity | More output per hour | Breakdowns |
| Quality | Consistency | Repeated faults until spotted |
| Flexibility | Programmable machines switch products quickly | Specialised machines do one task |

Small, varied businesses gain least; large, steady producers gain most.

## Quick calculations

**Productivity.** Tarnside Cakes has 6 bakers making 1,800 cakes a week: 1,800 ÷ 6 = **300 cakes per baker**. New mixers raise output to 2,100 with the same staff: 350 per baker, a rise of 16.7%.

**Cost per unit.** Weekly costs of £5,040 over 1,800 cakes = £2.80 a cake. If costs rise to £5,250 but output rises to 2,100: £5,250 ÷ 2,100 = **£2.50** a cake.

## Goods versus services

- Goods can be stored; services usually cannot, so service businesses plan staff hours around demand.
- Customers are often present during a service, so speed and staff manner matter as much as the result.
- Technology in services: online booking, self-service tills, automated car washes.

## Exam traps

- Describing job, batch and flow without linking them to productivity, cost and price.
- Confusing productivity with total production.
- Assuming technology always raises quality and flexibility.
- Recommending flow production for a small business with varied orders.
- Leaving services out of operations answers.

## Self-test

1. State the two purposes of business operations.
2. Define batch production.
3. Give one example of a product made by job production.
4. Why is cost per unit usually lowest in flow production?
5. A workshop has 5 workers producing 450 units a month. Calculate labour productivity.
6. Give one disadvantage of flow production.
7. Name the four factors that technology must balance, according to the specification.
8. Why might technology reduce flexibility?
9. Costs are £12,000 for 3,000 units. Calculate cost per unit.
10. A new machine raises output from 3,000 to 4,000 units while costs rise to £14,000. Calculate the new cost per unit.
11. Why can a service business not store output for busy periods?
12. Give one reason a business might choose job production despite its higher costs.

### Answers

1. To produce goods; to provide services.
2. Making a group of identical products together, then switching to make a group of a different type.
3. Any one: bespoke furniture, wedding dress, portrait, made-to-measure suit, building a house.
4. High volumes, specialised machines and repeated tasks raise productivity and spread fixed costs over many units.
5. 450 ÷ 5 = **90 units per worker**.
6. High set-up cost, little flexibility, repetitive work for staff, or a breakdown stops the whole line.
7. Cost, productivity, quality, flexibility.
8. Machines may be set up for one task, so switching to other products is slow or impossible.
9. £12,000 ÷ 3,000 = **£4**.
10. £14,000 ÷ 4,000 = **£3.50**.
11. A service is usually produced and consumed at the same time, so it cannot be made in advance.
12. Customers want a unique product and will pay a premium price, so higher costs are covered.
