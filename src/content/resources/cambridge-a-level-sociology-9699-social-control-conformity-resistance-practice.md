## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Define the term *social control*. **[2]**

**2.** Identify **two** agencies of formal social control. **[2]**

**3.** Distinguish between *deviance* and *crime*. **[4]**

**4.** Explain what is meant by *rational-legal authority*. **[3]**

**5.** Explain what is meant by *marginalisation*, using an example. **[3]**

**6.** Explain **one** difference between structuralist and interactionist views of the relationship between the individual and society. **[3]**

### Section B: structured questions

**7.** Explain **two** reasons why individuals conform to social expectations. **[6]**

**8.** Explain **two** ways in which ideology may help to maintain social order. **[6]**

**9.** Evaluate the use of covert participant observation to study a deviant youth subculture. **[8]**

**10.** *"Deviance is mainly caused by inadequate socialisation."*

- **(a)** Explain the view expressed in the quotation. **[6]**
- **(b)** Using sociological material, give **two** arguments against the view expressed in the quotation. **[10]**

**11.** "Individuals have little freedom to act against the structures of society." Evaluate this view. **[26]**

## Answers

### 1. Social control [2]

The processes and methods by which a society encourages people to conform to its norms [1], through formal agencies such as the police and courts or informal means such as family disapproval and peer pressure [1]. **[2]**

### 2. Formal agencies [2]

Any two, one mark each: police, courts, prisons, armed forces, school disciplinary systems, workplace managers enforcing written rules [1] [1]. **[2]**

### 3. Deviance and crime [4]

- Deviance is behaviour that breaks the norms of a group or society [1]; it may be legal, such as dressing very unusually [1].
- Crime is behaviour that breaks the law of a state [1]; some crimes, such as minor speeding, are so common that they may not be seen as deviant [1]. **[4]**

### 4. Rational-legal authority [3]

One of Weber's three types of authority [1]: power accepted as legitimate because it is exercised according to agreed rules and procedures [1], for example an elected government or a manager in a bureaucracy whose position comes from formal rules rather than tradition or personal charisma [1]. **[3]**

### 5. Marginalisation [3]

Being pushed to the edge of society so that a group has little voice, organisation or stake in mainstream institutions [1]. Example: long-term unemployed young people in deprived areas, or excluded minority groups [1]. Lea and Young (1984) argued that marginalised groups, lacking ways to pursue their interests, may turn to crime [1]. **[3]**

### 6. Structuralist and interactionist views [3]

Difference identified [1], explained [1], illustrated with a theory or example [1]. For example: structuralists see society shaping individuals from the top down through institutions and norms, while interactionists see individuals creating society through the meanings they build in interaction [1] [1]; Durkheim's social facts constrain behaviour, whereas Blumer argued people act on meanings that arise in interaction [1]. **[3]**

### 7. Reasons for conformity [6]

Up to 3 marks per reason: identify [1], explain [1], develop with evidence or theory [1].

- **Sanctions:** people seek rewards and avoid punishments, formal (fines, prison) or informal (ridicule).
- **Social pressure:** wish to belong or to obey; Milgram's obedience studies (1963) showed many people following an authority figure's instructions.
- **Self-interest:** people calculate that conformity protects jobs, reputations and relationships.
- **Social exchange:** conformity keeps valued exchanges of help and approval going (Blau, 1964).
- **Internalised values:** functionalists argue people conform because they believe in the norms. **[6]**

### 8. Ideology and social order [6]

Up to 3 marks per way.

- **Legitimating inequality:** ideas such as meritocracy make inequality seem fair, so the less advantaged accept their position (false consciousness).
- **Hegemony:** the ruling class wins consent through media, education and culture, so order rests on agreement rather than force.
- **Patriarchal ideology:** feminists argue that ideas about "natural" gender roles lead women to accept domestic and caring roles, maintaining gendered order.
- **Religious ideology:** Marx described religion as "the opium of the people", easing suffering and discouraging revolt. **[6]**

### 9. Covert participant observation of a youth subculture [8]

Up to 4 marks for strengths and up to 4 for limitations, applied to the context.

**Strengths:**
- Gives access to a closed group that might refuse an openly declared researcher [1].
- Members behave naturally because they do not know they are being studied, avoiding the Hawthorne effect and raising validity [1].
- The researcher experiences the group's meanings first-hand, gaining *verstehen* about why members value deviance [1].
- Produces rich qualitative data about norms and status within the group that questionnaires could not reach [1].

**Limitations:**
- Deception: members cannot give informed consent, which is a serious ethical problem [1].
- Risk to the researcher, who may be drawn into illegal or dangerous acts or face harm if discovered [1].
- Recording data is difficult without being detected, so notes rely on memory, reducing reliability [1].
- Findings from one small group are hard to generalise or repeat, limiting representativeness and reliability; the researcher may also "go native" and lose objectivity [1]. **[8]**

### 10(a). Explaining the view [6]

- The view links to functionalist and New Right ideas that people conform when norms are internalised during socialisation [1].
- If primary socialisation in the family is weak, children may not learn self-control or respect for rules [1].
- New Right writers on the "underclass" blame lone parenthood, absent fathers and welfare dependency for under-socialisation [1].
- Without internalised norms, people are guided by immediate desires, so deviance becomes more likely [1].
- Cultural deprivation arguments add that some groups pass on values that clash with mainstream norms [1].
- Application: for example, youth crime explained by lack of parental supervision and discipline [1]. **[6]**

### 10(b). Arguing against the view [10]

Up to 5 marks per argument: argument identified [1], explained [2], evidence or theory [1], evaluation or application [1].

- **Structural strain:** Merton (1938) argued deviance arises when people who have been well socialised into cultural goals, such as wealth, lack legitimate means to reach them; innovation (crime) is a response to blocked opportunity, not poor socialisation.
- **Subcultures are a different socialisation, not too little:** Cohen, and Cloward and Ohlin (1960), show that deviant youths are socialised into alternative values; Miller (1958) saw lower-class focal concerns as a distinct culture.
- **Labelling:** Becker (1963) argued deviance is created by the reactions of powerful groups; police and schools label some groups more than others, so "inadequate socialisation" may be a label itself.
- **Marxist and resistance views:** deviance can be a meaningful response to inequality (Hall and Jefferson, 1976), and crimes of the powerful are committed by people with no lack of socialisation.

Credit any two developed arguments. **[10]**

### 11. Essay: structure and individual freedom [26]

Marked by levels across AO1, AO2 and AO3.

**Indicative content for the view:**
- Durkheim's social facts constrain behaviour; suicide rates vary with social integration.
- Functionalist socialisation into shared values makes most conformity automatic.
- Marxists: class position shapes life chances and ideology limits what people think possible.
- Feminists: patriarchal structures limit women's choices in work and the family.
- Evidence of structured inequality in education, health and income.

**Indicative content against the view:**
- Interactionists: Blumer and G.H. Mead show people actively interpret and negotiate roles.
- Becker's labelling theory and Goffman's impression management show agency in everyday life.
- Resistance: subcultures and social movements show people acting against dominant structures.
- Wrong's critique of the "oversocialized" view.
- Postmodern arguments that identity is increasingly chosen.

**Evaluation:** Giddens' structuration as a synthesis; Weber's social action as a middle route; the degree of freedom varies by class, gender, ethnicity and society; structures change because individuals act differently.

**Level guidance:** top-level answers contrast structuralist and interactionist views with accurate evidence and reach a reasoned judgement; middle-level answers explain both sides with limited evaluation; low-level answers describe one view or rely on assertion.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Confusing deviance with crime.
- Giving two reasons for conformity that are really the same point.
- Listing strengths and weaknesses of a method without applying them to the group studied.
- Counter-arguments that do not address "inadequate socialisation".
- Essays that describe structuralist theories but never assess agency.
