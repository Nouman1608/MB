## The three specification statements

1. Inorganic ions occur in solution in the cytoplasm and body fluids, some in high and others in very low concentrations.
2. Each type of ion has a specific role, depending on its properties.
3. Recognise the roles of: hydrogen ions and pH; iron ions in haemoglobin; sodium ions in co-transport of glucose and amino acids; phosphate ions in DNA and ATP.

## The four ions at a glance

| Ion | Role | Where you meet it |
|---|---|---|
| H⁺ | Determines pH; affects protein shape | Enzymes (3.1.4.2); oxygen unloading (3.3.4.1); ATP synthesis (3.5, A-level) |
| Fe²⁺ | Part of the haem group of haemoglobin; binds oxygen | Mass transport in animals (3.3.4.1) |
| Na⁺ | Concentration gradient drives co-transport of glucose and amino acids | Absorption in the ileum (3.3.3); nerve impulses (3.6.2, A-level) |
| PO₄³⁻ | Component of nucleotides and of ATP | DNA and RNA (3.1.5); ATP (3.1.6); phospholipids (3.1.3) |

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Inorganic ion | A charged particle not based on carbon chains, dissolved in cytoplasm or body fluids |
| pH | −log₁₀ of the hydrogen ion concentration in mol dm⁻³ |
| Haem group | Iron-containing group in each polypeptide of haemoglobin, where oxygen binds |
| Co-transport | Movement of two substances together through one carrier protein |
| Concentration gradient | Difference in concentration between two regions |
| Phosphodiester bond | Bond linking phosphate and sugar between nucleotides |
| Phosphorylation | Adding a phosphate group to a molecule |

## pH essentials

- pH = −log₁₀[H⁺].
- Lower pH = higher [H⁺].
- One pH unit = tenfold change in [H⁺].
- Ratio of [H⁺] between two solutions = 10^(difference in pH).

Mini reminder with new numbers: [H⁺] = 1 × 10⁻³ mol dm⁻³ gives pH 3. Solutions at pH 5 and pH 8 differ in [H⁺] by 10³ = 1,000 times. [H⁺] = 2.5 × 10⁻³ gives pH 2.60.

## Hydrogen ions and proteins

Change in pH → charges on R groups change → ionic and hydrogen bonds in tertiary structure disrupted → active site shape changes → fewer enzyme-substrate complexes; extreme pH denatures.

## Iron ions

- Four polypeptides in haemoglobin, each with a haem group containing Fe²⁺.
- Each haem binds one O₂, so up to four O₂ per haemoglobin.
- Binding is reversible: loads at high partial pressure of oxygen, unloads at low.

## Sodium co-transport in the ileum

1. Na⁺ actively transported out of the epithelial cell into the blood (uses ATP).
2. Low Na⁺ inside cell; gradient from lumen into cell.
3. Na⁺ diffuses in through a co-transporter, carrying glucose or an amino acid.
4. Glucose leaves the cell into the blood by facilitated diffusion.

ATP keeps the gradient; the gradient moves the glucose.

## Phosphate ions

- DNA and RNA: one phosphate per nucleotide; phosphodiester bonds form the backbone; negative charge.
- ATP: three phosphate groups; hydrolysis releases Pi; Pi phosphorylates other molecules.
- Phospholipids: phosphate-containing head.

## Exam traps

- "High pH = more H⁺." It is fewer.
- Treating one pH unit as a small step: it is tenfold.
- "Sodium carries glucose using ATP directly." ATP maintains the Na⁺ gradient.
- "Iron carries oxygen" without saying it is in the haem group of haemoglobin.
- Forgetting phosphate in ATP or RNA.
- Listing ions without their roles in the context given.
- Saying hydrogen ions break peptide bonds when pH changes; they disrupt ionic and hydrogen bonds.

## Self-test

1. Give the formula for pH.
2. Calculate the pH of a solution with [H⁺] = 1 × 10⁻⁹ mol dm⁻³.
3. How many times greater is [H⁺] at pH 4 than at pH 6?
4. Explain why a change in pH can reduce the rate of an enzyme-controlled reaction.
5. Where is iron found in haemoglobin?
6. How many oxygen molecules can one haemoglobin molecule carry?
7. Why must oxygen binding to haemoglobin be reversible?
8. What keeps the sodium ion concentration low inside ileum epithelial cells?
9. How do sodium ions help glucose enter the epithelial cell?
10. Name two molecules in which phosphate is a component.
11. Why is DNA negatively charged?
12. Give an example of an ion present in a very low concentration that still has a major effect.

### Answers

1. pH = −log₁₀[H⁺].
2. pH 9.
3. 10² = 100 times.
4. It alters charges on R groups, disrupting ionic and hydrogen bonds in the tertiary structure, so the active site changes shape and fewer enzyme-substrate complexes form.
5. In the haem group associated with each of its four polypeptides.
6. Four.
7. So oxygen loads in the lungs and unloads in respiring tissues.
8. Active transport of sodium ions out of the cell into the blood, using ATP.
9. Sodium ions diffuse into the cell down their concentration gradient through a co-transporter protein, carrying glucose with them.
10. Any two: DNA, RNA, ATP, phospholipids.
11. Each nucleotide contains a negatively charged phosphate group in the backbone.
12. Hydrogen ions: at pH 7 their concentration is only 10⁻⁷ mol dm⁻³, yet small changes alter enzyme activity.

## Frequently asked questions

**What does "recognise the role of ions" mean in the AQA specification?**
You should identify what hydrogen, iron, sodium and phosphate ions are doing when they appear in other topics, such as enzymes, haemoglobin, absorption and ATP.

**Does ATP move glucose directly in co-transport?**
No. ATP is used to pump sodium ions out of the cell; the resulting sodium gradient drives glucose in through a co-transporter.

**What is the relationship between pH and hydrogen ion concentration?**
pH is the negative logarithm of hydrogen ion concentration, so a lower pH means more hydrogen ions and each unit is a tenfold change.
