This guide goes deeper on section 5.3 (Profit and loss) of the Cambridge IGCSE Business 0264 syllabus for examination in 2027, 2028 and 2029. The section has two content statements. 5.3.1 What is profit and why it is important covers "what profit is" and the "importance of profit to private sector businesses: reward for risk-taking, source of finance, measure of success, attract investors". 5.3.2 Statement of profit or loss covers its main features (revenue, cost of sales, gross profit, expenses, profit), simple calculations with them, and making decisions based on simple statements. The syllabus notes that candidates will not be assessed on constructing statements of profit or loss. The broader [profit and loss and statement of financial position guide](/resources/igcse-business-0264-profit-and-loss-and-statement-of-financial-position/) introduces the statement and compares two years. This page works on finding missing figures, testing the effect of a cost or price change, and using a statement to make a decision. Revise with the [profit and loss revision notes](/resources/cambridge-igcse-business-0264-profit-and-loss-revision-notes/) and practise with the [profit and loss practice questions](/resources/cambridge-igcse-business-0264-profit-and-loss-practice/).

All businesses and figures below are invented. Money is in dollars.

## What the syllabus asks for

| Content | What you must be able to do |
|---|---|
| What profit is | Explain it as what is left from revenue after all costs |
| Why profit matters to private sector businesses | Explain the four reasons named: reward for risk-taking, source of finance, measure of success, attract investors |
| Main features of a statement of profit or loss | Know what each line means |
| Simple calculations | Find any missing line from the others |
| Decisions | Use the figures to judge a change or choose between options |

## What profit is

Profit is the amount by which revenue exceeds total costs over a period. If costs are greater than revenue, the result is a **loss**. The syllabus formulae give two ways to calculate it:

```
Profit = total revenue - total costs
Profit = gross profit - expenses
```

Two misunderstandings cost marks.

- **Profit is not revenue.** A shop that sells $500,000 of goods may make little or no profit if its costs are almost as high.
- **Profit is not cash.** A business can make a profit and still run short of cash, for example if customers have not yet paid or it has spent heavily on equipment. Cash is covered in section 5.2.

## Why profit matters to a private sector business

| Reason | What it means | Who cares most |
|---|---|---|
| Reward for risk-taking | Owners risk their money; profit is their return for that risk | Sole traders, partners, shareholders |
| Source of finance | Profit kept in the business (retained profit) can pay for growth without borrowing | Managers planning expansion |
| Measure of success | Profit shows whether the business is earning more than it spends, and can be compared over time or with rivals | Owners, managers, lenders |
| Attract investors | Investors put money into businesses that are likely to give a good return | Potential shareholders or partners |

The syllabus says "private sector businesses". A public sector organisation (section 1.2) usually aims mainly to provide a service rather than profit, so these reasons apply less to it.

## The statement of profit or loss

| Line | Meaning | How it is found |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue | Money earned from selling goods or services in the period | Selling price per unit x number of units sold |
| Cost of sales | The cost of the goods that were sold, such as materials and bought-in stock | Given |
| Gross profit | Profit from trading, before other running costs | Revenue - cost of sales |
| Expenses | Other running costs, such as wages, rent, advertising and utilities | Added up from the list |
| Profit | What remains after all costs | Gross profit - expenses |

### Worked example 1: finding the missing figures

Kittering Kitchenware sold 8,500 pans at $24 each last year. Cost of sales was $119,000. Expenses were wages $38,000, rent $15,000, marketing $9,500 and utilities $4,500.

```
Revenue       = 24 x 8,500           = 204,000
Cost of sales                        = 119,000
Gross profit  = 204,000 - 119,000    =  85,000
Expenses      = 38,000 + 15,000 + 9,500 + 4,500 = 67,000
Profit        = 85,000 - 67,000      =  18,000
```

You may be asked to work backwards. If you are told revenue is $204,000 and gross profit is $85,000, then cost of sales = 204,000 - 85,000 = $119,000. If you are told profit is $18,000 and expenses are $67,000, gross profit = 18,000 + 67,000 = $85,000.

## Making decisions from a statement

### Worked example 2: a supplier raises prices

Kittering's supplier raises its prices by 8%, so cost of sales rises by 8%. Sales and expenses stay the same.

```
New cost of sales = 119,000 x 1.08 = 128,520
New gross profit  = 204,000 - 128,520 = 75,480
New profit        = 75,480 - 67,000   = 8,480
```

An 8% rise in one cost has cut profit by more than half, from $18,000 to $8,480. This happens because profit is a small amount left over from a large revenue, so a modest change in a large cost has a big effect on it.

**Option A: raise the price by $1 to $25, with sales unchanged.**

```
Revenue      = 25 x 8,500 = 212,500
Gross profit = 212,500 - 128,520 = 83,980
Profit       = 83,980 - 67,000 = 16,980
```

**Option A with sales falling by 5%.** Sales fall to 8,075 pans. Cost of sales per pan is 128,520 / 8,500 = $15.12, so cost of sales falls to 8,075 x 15.12 = $122,094.

```
Revenue      = 25 x 8,075 = 201,875
Gross profit = 201,875 - 122,094 = 79,781
Profit       = 79,781 - 67,000 = 12,781
```

**Judgement.** Even if sales fall by 5%, raising the price leaves profit ($12,781) higher than doing nothing ($8,480). The risk is that sales fall by more than 5%, for example if rivals do not raise their prices. Kittering could also look for a cheaper supplier, which would reduce cost of sales without the risk of losing customers, but might affect quality.

### Worked example 3: should a branch stay open?

Dunmore Florists has two shops.

| | Shop A | Shop B |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue | 160,000 | 95,000 |
| Cost of sales | 64,000 | 41,000 |
| Gross profit | 96,000 | 54,000 |
| Expenses | 71,000 | 58,000 |
| Profit (loss) | 25,000 | (4,000) |

Shop B makes a loss of $4,000. Before closing it, Dunmore should ask:

- **Which expenses cause the loss?** If Shop B's rent is high because the lease is new, the loss may be temporary.
- **Can expenses be cut?** Cutting $4,000 or more, for example by shorter opening hours, would remove the loss.
- **Is the trend improving?** One year's statement does not show whether Shop B is growing.
- **Would some sales move to Shop A** if B closed?

A statement of profit or loss shows the result, not the reason. Good answers use it to identify where the problem is (here, expenses are larger than gross profit) and then suggest what to check before deciding.

## Exam technique

The command words in the 0264 syllabus include **calculate**, **define**, **identify**, **state**, **outline**, **explain**, **consider** and **justify**.

- **Calculate:** write the formula in words, substitute, and give the answer with a dollar sign. Keep each step on its own line so method marks can be given if one figure is wrong.
- **Explain:** use the figures: "gross profit rose by $9,000 but expenses rose by $14,000, so profit fell".
- **Consider / justify:** compare the options with figures, add a factor the statement does not show (customers' reaction, quality, trend), then decide.

## Common errors

- Writing revenue when the question asks for profit, or the reverse.
- Subtracting expenses from revenue to get gross profit. Gross profit uses cost of sales only.
- Treating profit as the same as cash.
- Assuming sales stay the same after a price rise without saying so.
- Recommending closure of a loss-making branch without considering whether the loss can be cut.
