**Key question 2 of Core Content Option A in Cambridge O Level History 2147 asks "How was Italy unified?"** The answer
is not a single story. Between 1848 and 1870 at least four different plans for Italy competed, and the kingdom that
emerged was the work of a northern monarchy, a revolutionary general, two foreign wars and a great deal of luck. For
2147 candidates the question matters twice over: it can be set on Paper 1 as a structured question, and it is the
**Paper 2 prescribed topic for the November 2026 series**, when the whole document paper is built on it.

This guide is organised around the four official focus points. Condensed recall is in the
[Italy revision notes](/resources/cambridge-o-level-world-history-2147-unification-italy-revision-notes/), and exam-style
questions with mark schemes are in the [Italy practice questions](/resources/cambridge-o-level-world-history-2147-unification-italy-practice/).
For how Italy fits into the whole of Option A, see the
[O Level nineteenth century study guide](/resources/o-level-world-history-nineteenth-century-1848-1914/).

## What the specification sets

The four focus points are:

1. Why was Italy not unified in 1848–49?
2. How important was Garibaldi's contribution to unifying Italy?
3. Did Cavour help or hinder the unification of Italy?
4. How important for other European countries were moves towards Italian unification?

The specified content runs from Austrian influence over Italy and Italian nationalism (with the role of Mazzini),
through the events of 1848–49, Victor Emmanuel II and Cavour (the agreement of Plombières and the war with Austria in
1859), Garibaldi's invasion of Sicily and Naples, to the creation of the Kingdom of Italy and the completion of
unification by 1870.

## Four plans for one peninsula

Examiners reward candidates who see that "unification" meant different things to different Italians. Before 1848
there were four main visions:

| Plan | Champion | What Italy would look like | Main weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| Democratic republic | Giuseppe Mazzini, Young Italy (1831) | One republic made by a rising of the people | Repeated failed risings; frightened moderates and the Church |
| Federation under the Pope | Vincenzo Gioberti, *On the Moral and Civil Primacy of the Italians* (1843) | A league of existing states with the Pope as president | Collapsed when Pius IX refused to fight Austria in April 1848 |
| Expanded northern kingdom | Piedmont's House of Savoy | Piedmont enlarged by Lombardy and Venetia | Piedmont alone could not defeat Austria |
| Popular military action | Giuseppe Garibaldi | Unity won by volunteers, then handed to whoever could hold it | Depended on one leader and on others' tolerance |

Unification happened through the third plan, carried out with the energy of the fourth and borrowing the national
feeling created by the first. The second disappeared after 1848. Keeping this table in mind lets you explain *why*
particular events mattered rather than retelling them.

## Why was Italy not unified in 1848–49?

The revolutions began well. Milan expelled the Austrian garrison in the Five Days (March 1848), Daniele Manin declared
a Republic of St Mark in Venice, and Charles Albert of Piedmont declared war on Austria. Within eighteen months it had
all collapsed. The reasons connect to each other:

- **Austrian military recovery.** Marshal Radetzky withdrew to the fortresses of the Quadrilateral, regrouped and beat
  Piedmont at Custoza (July 1848) and decisively at Novara (March 1849). Charles Albert abdicated in favour of his son,
  Victor Emmanuel II.
- **Leadership divided by aims.** Charles Albert wanted territory for Piedmont; republicans in Milan and Venice did not
  trust a king. The rulers of Naples and Tuscany withdrew their troops once their own thrones were safe.
- **The Pope's withdrawal.** Pius IX's allocution of April 1848, refusing to make war on Catholic Austria, ended the
  federal plan and split Catholic opinion.
- **Foreign intervention.** When Mazzini and Garibaldi defended the Roman Republic in 1849, French troops sent by Louis
  Napoleon restored the Pope. Venice surrendered to Austria in August 1849.
- **A narrow base of support.** The movement belonged to the educated middle classes of the towns. Most peasants had no
  stake in it.

The lesson Piedmont drew was the key to what came next: Italy could not "do it by herself" against Austria, so a
foreign ally was essential. Piedmont also kept its 1848 constitution, the Statuto, which made it the natural leader for
liberals across the peninsula.

## Did Cavour help or hinder?

Camillo di Cavour became prime minister of Piedmont in 1852. The case that he **helped**:

- He modernised Piedmont with railways, trade treaties and banking, so it could afford a war.
- He sent troops to the Crimean War in 1855, earning a seat at the Congress of Paris (1856) where the Italian question
  was raised.
- At **Plombières (July 1858)** he secretly agreed with Napoleon III that France would fight Austria if Austria could be
  made to appear the aggressor, in return for Nice and Savoy.
- He provoked Austria into an ultimatum in April 1859. French and Piedmontese armies won at Magenta and Solferino, and
  Lombardy passed to Piedmont.
- In 1860 he arranged plebiscites that added Tuscany, Modena, Parma and the Romagna, and he sent the Piedmontese army
  through the Papal States to meet Garibaldi, making sure the south joined a constitutional monarchy rather than a
  republic.

The case that he **hindered**, or at least limited, unification:

- His original aim was a kingdom of northern Italy, not the whole peninsula. He regarded the south as backward and
  treated Garibaldi's expedition with suspicion.
- Giving Nice (Garibaldi's birthplace) and Savoy to France angered patriots.
- His rush to annex the south imposed Piedmontese laws, taxes and conscription, which helped cause years of
  "brigandage" and rebellion in the former Kingdom of the Two Sicilies.
- He died in June 1861 with Venetia and Rome still outside the kingdom.

A balanced judgement: Cavour helped decisively by securing French military power, but he aimed for a smaller result
than the one achieved, and his methods stored up problems for the new state.

## How important was Garibaldi's contribution?

Garibaldi sailed from near Genoa in May 1860 with about a thousand volunteers, the Redshirts, landed at Marsala in
Sicily, won at Calatafimi and took Palermo. He crossed to the mainland in August and entered Naples in September; his
forces then held the line of the Volturno against the Bourbon army. At **Teano on 26 October 1860** he met Victor
Emmanuel and handed over the south.

His contribution was huge because without it the south would not have joined in 1860 at all. But it had limits. His
success depended on the weakness of the Bourbon state and on peasant hopes of land; Cavour's army had to secure the
Papal States route; and his later attempts on Rome (Aspromonte in 1862, Mentana in 1867) failed. A strong answer
contrasts his military daring with his political dependence on the monarchy he served.

## Completing unity, 1861–70

The Kingdom of Italy was proclaimed in March 1861. **Venetia** came in 1866 after Italy joined Prussia against
Austria. Italian forces were beaten at Custoza and at sea off Lissa, but Prussia's victory forced Austria to give up
Venetia, transferred through France. **Rome** was protected by a French garrison until Napoleon III withdrew it to
fight Prussia in 1870. Italian troops entered Rome in September 1870 and it became the capital. Both final gains came
from other powers' wars.

## Why did Italy matter to other European countries?

- **Austria** lost Lombardy in 1859 and Venetia in 1866. The defeats exposed its weakness and encouraged Prussia.
- **France** gained Nice and Savoy, but Napoleon III angered French Catholics by weakening the Pope and then got
  dragged into defending Rome.
- **Prussia** gained an ally in 1866, which forced Austria to fight on two fronts.
- **Britain** welcomed a liberal Italy that reduced both Austrian and French influence, without committing troops.
- **The Papacy** lost its lands, creating a "Roman Question" that divided Catholics and the Italian state for decades.

The wider point is that Italian unification broke the 1815 settlement and showed that nationalism, backed by an army
and an ally, could redraw the map.

## Annotated model answer: a (c) question

*"Garibaldi's contribution was more important than Cavour's in unifying Italy." How far do you agree? Explain your
answer.*

> Garibaldi's contribution was essential to the shape of Italy in 1861, because his conquest of Sicily and Naples in
> 1860 added half the peninsula that Cavour had never planned to take. **[Clear point linked to the question.]** In
> four months a thousand volunteers overthrew the Bourbon kingdom, helped by peasant revolts and a weak army.
> **[Specific support.]** However, Garibaldi could not have kept the south without Cavour. The defeat of Austria in
> 1859 depended on the French army Cavour secured at Plombières, and in 1860 Cavour's troops occupied the Papal States
> so that the south was annexed to the monarchy. **[Counter-argument explained, not just stated.]** Overall Cavour's
> contribution was greater, because the decisive obstacle was Austria, and only a great-power ally could remove it;
> Garibaldi extended a process Cavour had made possible. **[Judgement with a reason.]**

The answer reaches the top level because both sides are explained with evidence and the conclusion gives a criterion
(who removed the main obstacle) rather than just picking a side.

## Common mistakes

- Treating Mazzini as the man who unified Italy. He inspired nationalism, but his republic never came about.
- Dating unification to 1861 only. Venetia (1866) and Rome (1870) complete it.
- Saying Cavour planned to unite the whole peninsula from the start.
- Forgetting that Napoleon III made a separate peace at Villafranca in July 1859, leaving Venetia Austrian.
- Narrating Garibaldi's campaign without judging his importance.

## Exam technique for 2147

- **Describe** (part a): give specific features or events. Four precise points beat a paragraph of background.
- **Explain** (part b): give reasons and show how each one led to the outcome. Two developed reasons, each with
  evidence, are better than five listed ones.
- **How far do you agree** (part c): explain both sides, then reach a judgement that says *why* one side is stronger.
- **Paper 2, November 2026**: because Italy is the prescribed topic, expect cartoons and written sources on Cavour,
  Garibaldi and Napoleon III. Ask who produced each source, when and why, and test it against what you know about
  1859–60.
