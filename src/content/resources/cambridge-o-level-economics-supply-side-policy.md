## 4.5.1 Definition

**Supply-side policy** is government action that aims to increase the economy's ability to produce goods and services, by raising the productivity and efficiency of labour, capital and markets.

Compare it with the other policies in topic 4:

| | Fiscal and monetary policy | Supply-side policy |
|---|---|---|
| Main tool | Taxes, spending, interest rates, money supply, exchange rates | Training, reforms, tax and rule changes, ownership changes |
| Main target | How much people and firms spend now | How much the economy can produce |
| Speed | Often quicker | Usually slower, because skills, investment and habits take time to change |

A simple test: if the policy makes workers more skilled, firms more willing to invest, or markets more competitive, it is supply-side.

## 4.5.2 The six measures

For each measure, trace a chain of four links: the government's action, the response of workers or firms, the effect on output or costs, and the outcome for the economy.

### Education and training

The state pays for schools, colleges and apprenticeships, or helps firms meet the cost of training staff. Workers gain skills, so output per worker (productivity) rises. Skilled workers also change jobs more easily, which helps where jobs have disappeared in one industry and appeared in another.

**Worked example (a furniture workshop, invented).** Five workers make 150 chairs a week. The weekly wage bill is 3,600.

- Output per worker before: 150 ÷ 5 = 30 chairs
- After a subsidised training course the same five workers make 180 chairs: 180 ÷ 5 = 36 chairs per worker
- Productivity change: (36 − 30) ÷ 30 × 100 = **+20%**
- Labour cost per chair before: 3,600 ÷ 150 = 24
- Labour cost per chair after: 3,600 ÷ 180 = 20
- Change: (20 − 24) ÷ 24 × 100 = **−16.7%**

Chain: the government pays for training, workers become more productive, cost per unit falls, the firm can cut prices or raise profit. Evaluation: the course costs money now and pays back later. Trained workers may leave for a rival or another country. Training is only useful if the skills match the jobs on offer.

### Labour market reforms

These remove barriers that stop workers and jobs matching. Examples include limiting the ability of trade unions to block change, making it easier for firms to hire and dismiss, improving job information, and reducing the minimum wage. A firm facing lower hiring risk may take on more staff, and a jobseeker finds vacancies faster. Evaluation: weaker job protection can leave workers insecure, and a lower minimum wage can reduce incomes for the lowest paid. If there are few vacancies, easier hiring does not create jobs on its own.

### Lower direct taxes

Direct taxes are paid directly by the person or firm from income or profit, such as income tax and corporation tax.

**Worked example 1: income tax (Country Q, invented).** A worker is offered one extra hour at 30 an hour. The tax rate on extra income is 40%, so she keeps 30 × 0.60 = 18. The rate falls to 30%, so she keeps 30 × 0.70 = 21. Reward for the extra hour rises by (21 − 18) ÷ 18 × 100 = **16.7%**. More people may accept overtime or join the labour force.

**Worked example 2: corporation tax (a firm, invented).** Profit is 80,000. At a 30% rate the firm pays 24,000 and keeps 56,000. At 20% it pays 16,000 and keeps 64,000, which is **14.3% more** to reinvest. The government loses 8,000 of revenue unless profit rises. To raise the same 24,000 at a 20% rate, profit must reach 24,000 ÷ 0.20 = **120,000**. This shows the evaluation point: a tax cut reduces revenue unless the extra activity is large.

Other evaluation points: some people may work fewer hours because they reach their target income sooner, and the cash gains are often largest for higher earners.

### Deregulation

Deregulation removes rules that restrict who may enter a market or how firms behave, for example caps on licences. New firms can join, so competition increases and firms are pushed to cut costs and improve service.

**Worked example (a town's bus services, invented).** Operators rise from 20 to 35, a rise of (35 − 20) ÷ 20 × 100 = **75%**. The average fare falls from 10 to 8.50, a fall of (8.50 − 10) ÷ 10 × 100 = **−15%**. Evaluation: rules often exist to protect safety, workers or the environment. Removing them may bring back the harm they were meant to prevent.

### Improving incentives to work and invest

Incentives to work: lower income tax, or changing the benefit system so that a person who takes a job is clearly better off than one who does not.

**Worked example (invented).** Unemployment benefit is 150 a week. A job gives 190 after tax, so the gain from working is 190 − 150 = 40. A tax cut raises the take-home pay to 205, so the gain is 205 − 150 = 55, which is **37.5% higher**. More jobseekers are likely to accept work.

Incentives to invest: grants, tax allowances for buying machines, and a stable economy that gives firms confidence. More capital per worker raises output per worker. Evaluation: grants cost the government money, and a firm will not invest if it expects weak sales whatever the incentive.

### Privatisation

Privatisation means selling state-owned firms into private ownership. The new owners seek profit, so they have a reason to cut waste, and competition may push efficiency further.

**Worked example (a state-owned water firm, invented).** It produces 300,000 units at a total cost of 9,000,000, so unit cost is 30. After sale, output is the same but total cost is 7,500,000, so unit cost is 25, a fall of **16.7%**. The government also receives the sale price.

Evaluation: a private owner with no rivals may raise prices, so the benefit depends on competition. Loss-making but socially useful services may be cut. The government loses future profits, and the sale revenue is a one-off.

## 4.5.3 Effects on macroeconomic aims

| Aim | Possible benefit | Limit or risk |
|---|---|---|
| Economic growth | Better skills, more capital and more competition raise productive capacity, so output can grow without pressure on prices | Slow to work; costs up front |
| Full employment/low unemployment | Training and labour market reform match workers to jobs; better incentives bring people into work | Does little for unemployment caused by a lack of spending |
| Stable prices/low inflation | Lower unit costs let firms hold or cut prices; more output eases shortages | Gains may be small if wages rise as fast as productivity |
| Balance of payments stability | Lower costs and better quality help exports compete and reduce imports | Rivals may improve too; imported inputs may rise |
| Redistribution of income | Training and jobs help low earners | Direct tax cuts and wage cuts can widen inequality |

**Worked example: growth and employment (Country M, invented).** Potential output is 500 billion. After a long programme of training and investment it is 520 billion: growth of (520 − 500) ÷ 500 × 100 = **4%**. The labour force is 10 million. Unemployment of 800,000 is 800,000 ÷ 10,000,000 × 100 = 8%. After labour market reform it is 600,000, or **6%**.

**Worked example: balance of payments (Country M, invented).** Exports rise from 120 to 138 (+15%) as lower costs help firms sell abroad. Imports rise from 130 to 134. The balance of trade in goods and services changes from 120 − 130 = −10 (a deficit) to 138 − 134 = **+4** (a surplus).

**Worked example: prices.** If unit costs fall by 3% and firms pass this on fully, a price index of 100 falls to 100 × 0.97 = **97**. This is a model: firms may keep the gain as profit.

## Evaluating supply-side policy

Use these ideas, and connect them to the case in front of you:

- Time lag: most measures take years to change output.
- Cost: spending on training or grants has an opportunity cost, such as fewer hospitals.
- Uncertainty: workers and firms may not respond as predicted.
- Winners and losers: lower taxes and weaker worker protection can widen inequality, so a gain in growth can conflict with redistribution.
- Fit with other policy: supply-side policy works best beside stable prices and sensible spending.

## Common errors

- Describing a spending boost (such as higher government spending) as supply-side policy.
- Stating a measure without the chain to a macroeconomic aim.
- Saying a tax cut always raises revenue.
- Mixing up direct taxes (income, profit) with indirect taxes (on spending).
- Confusing privatisation (ownership changes) with deregulation (rules change).
- Giving only advantages when asked to evaluate.
