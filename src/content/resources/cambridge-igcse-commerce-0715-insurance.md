**Insurance** is an arrangement in which a person or business pays a small regular sum, called a **premium**, to an insurance company, and in return the insurer promises to pay for certain losses. It is an **aid to trade** because it lets businesses take the risks that trade involves, such as fire, theft and accidents, without being ruined by one bad event. Section 5.3 of the Cambridge IGCSE Commerce (0715) syllabus has three parts: the **purposes of insurance** (5.3.1), the **types of insurance risk** (5.3.2) and **how enterprises make use of insurance** (5.3.3), which covers pooling, premiums, documents and brokers. This guide covers each part, with two worked examples and an annotated answer.

## What are the purposes of insurance? (5.3.1)

The syllabus lists five purposes.

| Purpose | What it means | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Risk reduction** | The insurer encourages safer behaviour, because safer firms pay lower premiums | A shop installs an alarm and gets a cheaper theft premium |
| **Compensation** | The insurer pays for losses after an insured event | A factory fire is paid for, so the owner can rebuild |
| **Financial protection** | A single large loss does not destroy the business | A lorry accident costs less than the insurance payout |
| **Business confidence** | Owners are willing to start, expand and trade, knowing losses are covered | A new exporter ships goods because cargo is insured |
| **Investment** | Insurers collect premiums and invest them; some policies also build up savings | Insurers fund business and government projects by buying shares and bonds |

Notice that insurance does not stop the loss from happening. It spreads the **financial effect** of the loss across many people.

## What types of insurance risk are there? (5.3.2)

The syllabus names seven types of risk.

| Risk | What it covers | Who needs it |
|---|---|---|
| **Consequential loss** | Lost profit and extra costs after an insured event, such as income lost while a shop is closed after a fire | Any business that would lose income during repairs |
| **Employer's liability** | Compensation to employees injured or made ill at work | Any employer |
| **Fire** | Damage to buildings and stock caused by fire | Firms with property and stock |
| **Motor** | Vehicle damage, theft and liability to others | Delivery firms, taxi firms, all drivers |
| **Premises** | Damage to buildings and fixtures, such as storm, flood or explosion | Owners of shops, offices and factories |
| **Public liability** | Compensation to customers or members of the public injured on the firm's property or by its activities | Shops, restaurants, builders |
| **Theft** | Loss of goods, cash or equipment by stealing | Retailers, warehouses and offices |

A business can have several policies at once. For example, a small bakery might insure its building (premises), its stock (fire and theft), its van (motor), its staff (employer's liability), customers who visit (public liability) and its lost income after a fire (consequential loss).

## How do enterprises make use of insurance? (5.3.3)

### Pooling of risk

Insurance works because of **pooling**. Many people or firms pay premiums into one fund. Only a few of them suffer a loss in any year, and the fund pays for those losses. The risk is shared among all the members of the pool.

Pooling works because insurers can predict, using past records, roughly how many claims will arise out of a large group. A single business cannot predict whether its own building will burn down, but an insurer can predict how many of 10,000 buildings will.

### Calculation of premiums

The **premium** is the price of insurance. Insurers set it so that the total premiums collected cover:

- the cost of **claims**;
- the insurer's **running costs**; and
- a **profit**.

Insurers look at the probability of a claim, the value of what is insured, and the type of business. A higher risk or a higher value means a higher premium. Premiums are often shown as a **percentage rate** of the value insured, so:

premium = value insured x rate

Reasons for a lower premium include safety measures such as alarms, sprinklers and good security, and a record of few claims. Reasons for a higher premium include a high-risk location, expensive contents and a history of claims.

### Insurance documents

| Document | Purpose |
|---|---|
| **Proposal form** | Completed by the person seeking cover. It gives details of the risk, such as what is being insured, its value and past claims. The insurer uses it to decide whether to offer cover and at what price. |
| **Cover note** | A temporary document that gives immediate cover until the full policy is issued. |
| **Policy** | The formal contract. It sets out what is covered, what is excluded, the sum insured, the premium and the conditions for claims. |

The usual order is: **proposal form, then cover note, then policy**. Honest answers on the proposal form matter. If information is false or hidden, the insurer may refuse to pay a claim.

### The role of an insurance broker

An **insurance broker** is an independent agent who arranges insurance between customers and insurers. The broker:

- gives advice on the type and amount of cover needed;
- compares policies from many insurers to find a suitable price;
- completes the paperwork;
- helps with claims.

The broker is usually paid by **commission** from the insurer. A broker saves the customer time and is especially valuable when the cover needed is complicated, but the customer must remember that the broker is paid by a percentage of the premium.

## Worked example 1: calculating a premium (invented figures)

*A shop owner insures stock worth $60,000. The fire premium rate is 0.5% of the value insured, and the theft rate is 0.8%. The insurer gives a 10% discount on the total because the shop has a burglar alarm.*

1. Fire premium: 60,000 x 0.5 / 100 = **$300**.
2. Theft premium: 60,000 x 0.8 / 100 = **$480**.
3. Total before discount: 300 + 480 = **$780**.
4. Discount: 780 x 10 / 100 = $78.
5. Premium payable: 780 - 78 = **$702**.

**Judgement:** the alarm cost the owner something to install, but it saves $78 a year in premium and lowers the chance of a theft loss. The owner should also check that the sum insured matches the real value of the stock, because under-insurance may lead to a part payment of claims.

## Worked example 2: how pooling covers claims (invented figures)

*An insurer covers 2,000 small shops against fire. From past records it expects 40 shops to have a claim each year, with an average claim of $15,000. It adds 20% to cover costs and profit.*

1. Expected claims: 40 x 15,000 = **$600,000**.
2. Claims cost per shop: 600,000 / 2,000 = **$300**.
3. Add 20%: 300 x 1.20 = **$360** premium per shop.

**Judgement:** each shop pays $360 a year. A shop that suffers a $15,000 fire loss recovers it, so a small certain cost replaces a large uncertain loss. This is how pooling spreads risk.

## Annotated model answer

*Question: Explain two reasons why a delivery company needs insurance.* **[4]**

> The company needs **motor insurance** [1] because its vans may be damaged or stolen, and the insurer will pay for repairs or replacement so the company can keep trading [1]. It also needs **public liability insurance** [1] because a van accident could injure members of the public, and compensation could cost more than the company could afford without help [1].

Why it scores: each reason names a type of insurance, then explains what it pays for and why this business needs it.

## What are the common mistakes in 5.3?

- Writing that insurance **prevents** losses. It pays for them.
- Mixing up **employer's liability** (employees) and **public liability** (customers and others).
- Mixing up **consequential loss** (lost profit) with direct damage covered by fire or premises insurance.
- Giving the order of documents wrongly. The proposal form comes first, then the cover note, then the policy.
- Saying a broker works for the insurer. A broker is an independent adviser, though paid by commission.
- Not giving a reason such as "alarm installed" when explaining why a premium is lower.

## Exam technique

- **Identify** or **state** a purpose or risk: give the name.
- **Describe** a document: say who completes it and why.
- **Explain** a type of insurance: name it, say what it covers and link it to the business in the case.
- **Calculate** a premium: write the formula, the figures and the answer with the unit.
- **Assess** whether to use a broker: weigh convenience and advice against commission.
