Quick recall for section 2.4 **Supply** of the Cambridge O Level Economics 2281 syllabus for examination in 2026: the definition of supply, extensions and contractions, individual and market supply "in terms of aggregation", and the conditions of supply that cause an increase or decrease. Full explanations and worked examples are in the [supply study guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-economics-2281-supply/); test yourself with the [supply practice questions](/resources/cambridge-o-level-economics-2281-supply-practice/). The wider [supply and equilibrium guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-economics-supply-and-equilibrium-and-disequilibrium-prices/) links supply to price determination. All figures are invented.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Supply | The quantity sellers are willing and able to offer for sale at each price over a given period |
| Quantity supplied | The amount offered at one particular price |
| Supply schedule | A table of prices and the quantities supplied at each |
| Supply curve | The schedule plotted with price on the vertical axis and quantity per period on the horizontal axis |
| Extension of supply | A rise in quantity supplied caused only by a rise in the product's own price; a move up the curve |
| Contraction of supply | A fall in quantity supplied caused only by a fall in the product's own price; a move down the curve |
| Increase in supply | More offered at every price; the curve shifts right |
| Decrease in supply | Less offered at every price; the curve shifts left |
| Individual supply | The supply of one seller |
| Market supply | The total of all sellers' quantities at each price (horizontal aggregation) |
| Condition of supply | Any influence other than the product's own price, such as costs, technology, taxes, subsidies, weather or the number of sellers |

## The one rule that decides most answers

Ask: **did the product's own price change, or something else?**

- Own price changed: **movement** along the curve (extension or contraction).
- Anything else changed: **shift** of the curve (increase or decrease).

A cause and its effect cannot both be the same price. "The price of rice rose, so the supply of rice increased" is wrong: the rise in price gives an extension of supply.

## Why the curve slopes upwards

- A higher price raises the revenue from each unit, so producing more becomes worthwhile.
- Extra output usually costs more per unit (overtime, less suitable land or older machines), so it needs a higher price.
- Higher prices draw in sellers who could not cover their costs at lower prices.

## Market supply in one table

Two fishing boats in an invented harbour, kilograms of fish per day:

| Price per kg ($) | Boat A | Boat B | Market supply |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4 | 50 | 0 | 50 |
| 6 | 80 | 40 | 120 |
| 8 | 110 | 70 | 180 |

Add the quantities across each row. Boat B only sails when the price is at least $6, so between $4 and $6 the market extends by 70 kg while Boat A alone adds 30.

## Conditions of supply: direction checklist

| Change | Effect on supply | Curve |
|---|---|---|
| Wages, materials or fuel become dearer | Decrease | Left |
| Better technology or higher productivity | Increase | Right |
| New or higher indirect tax on the product | Decrease | Left |
| New or higher subsidy to producers | Increase | Right |
| More firms enter the market | Increase | Right |
| Drought, flood, disease or a poor catch | Decrease | Left |
| Price of an alternative product the firm could make rises | Decrease | Left |

## Measuring a shift

- **Sideways**: compare quantities at the same price. If 500 were offered at $3 and now 420 are, supply has decreased by 80 at that price.
- **Vertically**: a subsidy of $1 per unit means each quantity can now be supplied at a price $1 lower, so the curve moves down by $1 (which is the same as a move to the right).
- **Percentages**: always divide by the original quantity. A fall from 500 to 420 is 80 ÷ 500 × 100 = 16%.

## Exam traps

- Calling a contraction a "decrease". The words are not interchangeable in this syllabus.
- Moving the supply curve when a buyer-side event happens, such as a change in incomes.
- Treating a cost rise as a movement along the curve. Costs are a condition of supply, so the curve shifts.
- Assuming a new seller adds the same amount at every price. It only adds at prices it is willing to accept.
- Leaving out the time period in a definition or on an axis label.
- Explaining a shift by saying "firms want to supply more". Give the reason: lower costs, a subsidy, better technology.

## Quick self-test

1. Define supply.
2. The price of cement rises from $8 to $10 a bag and quantity supplied rises from 600 to 750 bags a week. Name the change.
3. Calculate the percentage change in quantity supplied in question 2.
4. A new machine halves the time needed to make each pair of shoes. What happens to the supply curve for shoes?
5. Three sellers offer 20, 35 and 0 units at a price of $5. What is market supply at $5?
6. Why might a seller offer nothing at a low price but a positive quantity at a higher price?
7. A government introduces a subsidy of $2 per unit on solar panels. Describe the shift.
8. Farmers can grow cotton or wheat. The price of wheat rises. What happens to the supply of cotton?
9. Is a change in consumer tastes a condition of supply? Explain.
10. Give two reasons why the supply curve slopes upwards.
11. A tax raises each producer's costs by $0.40 per unit. At a price of $3, what quantity will producers now offer?
12. Name the change when quantity supplied falls because the product's price falls.

### Answers

1. The quantity of a product that sellers are willing and able to offer for sale at each price over a given period of time.
2. An extension of supply, a movement up the existing supply curve.
3. (750 − 600) ÷ 600 × 100 = 25%.
4. Supply increases: the curve shifts to the right, because the cost of each pair falls.
5. 20 + 35 + 0 = 55 units.
6. At the low price its revenue does not cover its costs, so it is not willing to supply; the higher price makes production worthwhile.
7. Supply increases. Each quantity can be supplied at a price $2 lower, so the curve shifts down by $2, which is a shift to the right.
8. Supply of cotton decreases (shifts left), because land is switched to the more profitable wheat.
9. No. Tastes affect buyers, so they change demand, not supply.
10. A higher price makes extra output profitable; extra units usually cost more to produce; higher prices draw in new sellers (any two).
11. The quantity they previously offered at $2.60, since they now need $0.40 more for each quantity.
12. A contraction of supply.
