## Section A

**1.** Distinguish superior judges from inferior judges, giving **one** example of each. **[2]**

**2.** State the qualification needed to become a High Court judge. **[1]**

**3.** Identify **three** ways in which a barrister's training differs from a solicitor's. **[3]**

**4.** State the function of the Legal Ombudsman. **[2]**

**5.** Explain what is meant by a challenge for cause. **[2]**

**6.** State **three** disqualifications from jury service. **[3]**

## Section B

**7.** Describe the process by which a High Court judge is selected and appointed. **[6]**

**8.** Explain how barristers and solicitors are regulated, including how a client can complain about poor service. **[8]**

**9.** Describe the role of lay magistrates in criminal and civil cases. **[6]**

**10.** Explain the selection and training of lay magistrates. **[6]**

**11.** Explain how jurors are vetted and how the jury panel may be challenged. **[6]**

**12.** "The jury should be replaced by a judge sitting alone." Assess this view. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** Superior judges sit in the senior courts and handle the most important cases and appeals, for example High Court judges or Lords Justices of Appeal [1]. Inferior judges sit in the lower courts, for example circuit judges, recorders or district judges [1].

**2.** Seven years' post-qualification legal experience, satisfying the judicial-appointment eligibility condition [1].

**3.** Any three, 1 mark each: barristers must join one of the four Inns of Court, solicitors do not [1]; barristers take the Bar course while solicitors pass SQE1 and SQE2 [1]; barristers complete pupillage while solicitors complete two years' qualifying work experience [1]; a barrister needs a law degree or a conversion course, while a solicitor may qualify with a degree in any subject or equivalent; a barrister is called to the Bar, while a solicitor is admitted to the roll.

**4.** The Legal Ombudsman is an independent body that investigates complaints about the service provided by lawyers, once the client has complained to the lawyer first [1]. It can require remedies such as an apology, a refund or reduction of fees, or compensation [1].

**5.** A challenge for cause is an objection by the prosecution or defence to an individual juror, with a stated reason [1], for example that the juror knows the defendant or a witness, or has a disqualification [1].

**6.** Any three, 1 mark each: a sentence of imprisonment of five years or more, which disqualifies for life [1]; having served a prison sentence or a community order within the period set by the Juries Act 1974 [1]; being on bail in criminal proceedings [1]; lacking mental capacity.

**7.** Vacancies are advertised by the **Judicial Appointments Commission**, set up by the Constitutional Reform Act 2005 [1]. Candidates must meet the seven-year eligibility condition and complete an application [1]. Shortlisting may include qualifying tests and assessment of references [1]. Shortlisted candidates attend a selection day with an interview and often a role-play exercise [1]. The Commission recommends one candidate on merit, having regard to diversity [1]. The Lord Chancellor may accept, reject or ask for reconsideration, and the appointment is made formally by the monarch [1].

**8.** The Legal Services Act 2007 separated representation from regulation, with the **Legal Services Board** overseeing regulators [1]. The **Bar Council** represents barristers [1], while the **Bar Standards Board** sets training standards, publishes the code of conduct and brings disciplinary proceedings for breaches [1]. The **Law Society** represents solicitors [1], while the **Solicitors Regulation Authority** sets admission and conduct standards and investigates misconduct [1], referring serious cases to the **Solicitors Disciplinary Tribunal**, which can fine, suspend or strike off [1]. A client with a service complaint must first complain to the lawyer [1]; if unresolved, the **Legal Ombudsman** can investigate and require an apology, a fee refund or compensation [1].

**9.** **Criminal**: magistrates try summary offences and either-way offences where they accept jurisdiction and the defendant consents [1]; they sentence within their powers or commit to the Crown Court for sentence [1]; they deal with early hearings in indictable cases and with bail applications [1]; they issue search and arrest warrants and warrants of further detention [1]; trained magistrates sit in the youth court [1]. **Civil**: they hear family cases in the Family Court and deal with licensing and some debt enforcement [1].

**10.** **Selection**: vacancies are advertised and applicants must be aged 18 or over and under 75 and able to sit at least 13 days a year [1]. Local advisory committees interview candidates, testing the six key qualities and judicial aptitude through case scenarios [1], and recommend suitable candidates for appointment on the monarch's behalf [1]. **Training**: new magistrates complete induction training on law, procedure and the role of the legal adviser [1], observe court sittings and are supported by a mentor during early sittings [1], and are appraised; further training is needed to chair a bench or sit in the youth or family court [1].

**11.** **Vetting**: routine police checks are made to confirm that jurors are not disqualified, upheld in *R v Mason* (1980) [1]. In national security and terrorism cases, wider authorised checks may be made under the Attorney General's guidelines [1]. **Challenges**: a challenge to the array objects to the whole panel as unfairly chosen [1]; a challenge for cause objects to an individual juror for a stated reason [1]; the prosecution may stand by a juror, moving them to the back of the panel [1]. The defence's peremptory challenge, without reasons, was abolished by the Criminal Justice Act 1988 [1].

**12.** **For replacing juries**: jurors give no reasons, so verdicts cannot be checked [1]; they may not understand complex evidence such as in fraud trials [1]; they can be influenced by media or internet research, as in *Attorney General v Fraill* (2011) [1]; jury trials are slower and more costly [1]. **Against**: juries provide public participation and are trusted as a safeguard against state power [1]; twelve randomly chosen people are less likely to be case-hardened than a single judge [1]; jury equity allows acquittal on conscience, as in *R v Ponting* (1985) [1]; a judge alone already exists for tampering cases under s44 Criminal Justice Act 2003 (*R v Twomey* (2009)), so targeted alternatives may be better than abolition [1]. **Middle routes**: a judge with lay assessors or magistrates combines expertise with participation [1]. **Conclusion**: complete replacement would lose legitimacy; limited alternatives for specific problems are a better answer [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing appointment without naming the Judicial Appointments Commission and the Constitutional Reform Act 2005.
- Giving regulators' names without their functions.
- Listing jury criticisms without a case or example.
- Ending an "assess" answer without a conclusion.
