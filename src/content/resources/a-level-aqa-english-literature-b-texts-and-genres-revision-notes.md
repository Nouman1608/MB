## Open book changes the revision priority

Because clean, unannotated copies of your set texts are permitted in
the exam, **memorising quotations word-for-word is not the priority**.
The higher-value skill is a mental map of each text: which sections
deal with which specification "elements" (crime/motive/detection, or
power/protest/control), so material can be located quickly under time
pressure.

## "Elements," not fixed genre rules

Whichever option you study, treat its named elements as a checklist to
identify and weigh in each text — not a rigid genre definition:

| Option 2A: Crime | Option 2B: Political/Social Protest |
|---|---|
| Sub-type of crime text | Sub-type of protest text |
| Settings for criminal action | Settings for power struggles |
| Crimes, criminals, motives | Pursuit of power and rebellion |
| Detection and investigation | Corruption and control |
| Restoration of moral order | Individual vs collective experience |
| Guilt, remorse, confession | Gender politics and social class |
| Detective-hero and nemesis | Structure heightening/resolving tension |
| Criminal/legal/police register | — |

For each of your three set texts, be able to name at least three
elements from your option's list and locate supporting material for
each quickly.

## The three-section structure — plan time accordingly

- **Section A** — compulsory unseen-passage question (25 marks). Apply
the *same* elements framework used for set texts.
- **Section B** — essay on one set text (25 marks).
- **Section C** — a genuinely *connective* essay across two set texts
(25 marks).

All three sections carry equal marks — do not under-revise the unseen
passage because it "isn't a memorised text."

## What makes Section C genuinely connective

The most common weakness in Section C is writing two mini-essays
side by side instead of one connected argument. A strong response
tracks **one specific element** (e.g. treatment of guilt, or the
structure of a power struggle) **across both texts**, using one text to
illuminate the other — not analysing each text in an isolated block.

## Confirm the text-list technicalities

Your three texts must include one post-2000 prose text, one poetry
text, and one further text, with one of the three pre-1900. A text can
satisfy more than one requirement simultaneously (a pre-1900 poetry
text meets both the poetry and pre-1900 requirements at once) — confirm
with your teacher exactly which three texts your centre studies and how
they map onto these requirements before building a revision plan.

## Why "elements" are labelled that way, not "genre rules"

The specification is explicit that crime writing and political/social
protest writing are treated as *elements* rather than fixed categories,
because many set texts pre-date the genre being recognised as a formal
literary tradition. This has a practical revision consequence: rather
than asking "does this text belong to the crime genre," a stronger
question is "which of the specification's named elements does this
text actually display, and how strongly." A text may show some elements
clearly (named criminals, a detective figure) while barely touching
others (restoration of moral order) — noting this unevenness, rather
than forcing a text to fit every element equally, produces a more
accurate and better-evidenced essay.

## Exam traps

- Memorising quotations instead of practising fast navigation of clean
set-text copies.
- Treating "elements of crime writing" or "elements of protest writing"
as fixed genre rules rather than a checklist to apply and weigh.
- Writing Section C as two separate text analyses rather than a
genuinely connective argument.
- Under-preparing the unseen-passage question in Section A, despite it
carrying equal marks to each essay section.
- Being unclear which of your three texts satisfies the pre-1900 and
post-2000 prose requirements.

## Recent set-text additions

AQA introduced additional optional texts to this specification for
first assessment 2027. If your course was updated to include one of
these newer additions, check with your teacher which text list
version applies to your specific examination series before relying on
older revision material that may reference only the original text
list — a mismatch here could mean revising analytical points for a
text your centre does not actually study.

## Self-test

1. Why does the open-book format reduce the value of memorising exact
quotations?
2. What should you be able to do for at least three "elements" per set
text?
3. What is the most common weakness in a Section C answer, and how is
it avoided?
4. Why should Section A's unseen-passage question not be deprioritised?
5. What three text requirements must your set-text list satisfy, and
can one text meet more than one?

**Answers:** 1. Because an unmarked copy of the text is available in
the exam, success depends on locating and analysing relevant passages
quickly rather than on word-perfect recall. 2. Name the element and
locate supporting material for it quickly within the text. 3. Writing
two separate mini-essays rather than a genuinely connective argument;
avoided by tracking one specific element across both texts, using one
to illuminate the other. 4. Because it carries the same 25 marks as
each essay section, despite feeling less "revisable" than prepared set
texts. 5. One post-2000 prose text, one poetry text, and one further
text with one of the three pre-1900; yes, a single text can satisfy
more than one requirement at once (e.g. a pre-1900 poetry text meets
both the poetry and pre-1900 requirements).
