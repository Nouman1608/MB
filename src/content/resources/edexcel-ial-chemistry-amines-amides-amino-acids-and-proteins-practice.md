## Questions

**1.** This question is about naming nitrogen compounds.

**(a)** Name H₂NCH(CH₂CH₂CH₃)COOH. **[1]**
**(b)** Name CH₃CH₂CH₂CONHCH₂CH₃. **[1]**
**(c)** Give the structural formula of propylamine and classify it as a primary, secondary or tertiary amine. **[2]**

**2.** Explain the order of base strength of ammonia, pentylamine and phenylamine. **[4]**

**3.** This question is about butylamine, C₄H₉NH₂.

**(a)** Write an equation to show why aqueous butylamine is alkaline. **[1]**
**(b)** Write an equation for the reaction of excess butylamine with ethanoyl chloride, and name the organic product containing the amide group. **[2]**
**(c)** Butylamine is added a drop at a time, until in excess, to copper(II) sulfate solution. Describe what you would see. **[2]**

**4.** Pentylamine, CH₃(CH₂)₄NH₂, can be made in two ways.

**(a)** Give the reagent and conditions to make pentylamine from 1-chloropentane. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why one reagent is used in excess. **[1]**
**(c)** Give the formula of one secondary amine that could also form in (a). **[1]**
**(d)** Give the reagent needed to make pentylamine from a nitrile. **[1]**

**5.** 0.261 g of a primary aliphatic amine, RNH₂, reacts exactly with 30.00 cm³ of 0.100 mol dm⁻³ hydrochloric acid. Calculate the Mr of the amine and deduce its molecular formula. **[4]**

**6.** Phenylamine is made from nitrobenzene.

**(a)** Give the reagents and conditions for the reduction. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why sodium hydroxide solution is added before the phenylamine is separated. **[1]**
**(c)** 6.15 g of nitrobenzene (Mr 123.0) gives 3.26 g of phenylamine (Mr 93.0). Calculate the percentage yield. **[2]**

**7.** A student makes an azo dye from phenylamine and phenol.

**(a)** Give the reagents and the temperature for converting phenylamine into benzenediazonium chloride. **[2]**
**(b)** Write an equation for this reaction. **[1]**
**(c)** State the conditions for the coupling step and give the structural formula of the dye. **[2]**
**(d)** 2.79 g of phenylamine (Mr 93.0) gives 5.05 g of dye (Mr 198.0). Assuming a 1 : 1 ratio throughout, calculate the percentage yield. **[2]**

**8.** This question is about polymers in Topic 19.

**(a)** Draw the repeat unit of poly(propenamide). **[1]**
**(b)** Draw the repeat unit of poly(ethenol). **[1]**
**(c)** Nylon-6,10 is made from hexane-1,6-diamine and decanedioyl dichloride, ClOC(CH₂)₈COCl. Draw the repeat unit and name the other product. **[2]**
**(d)** 2.32 g of hexane-1,6-diamine (Mr 116.0) reacts with 4.78 g of decanedioyl dichloride (Mr 239.0). Calculate the maximum mass of nylon-6,10 that can form (repeat unit Mr 282.0). **[2]**

**9.** **(a)** Explain why nylon fibres are strong. **[2]**
**(b)** Liquid-detergent capsules have a poly(ethenol) film. Explain why the film is useful in a washing machine. **[2]**

**10.** This question is about 2-amino-3-methylbutanoic acid (valine), (CH₃)₂CHCH(NH₂)COOH.

**(a)** Draw the zwitterion. **[1]**
**(b)** Give the structures of the main species in strongly acidic solution and in strongly alkaline solution. **[2]**
**(c)** 0.597 g of an amino acid H₂NCH(R)COOH reacts exactly with 25.50 cm³ of 0.200 mol dm⁻³ hydrochloric acid (1 : 1). Calculate its Mr and show that it could be valine. **[3]**
**(d)** Describe an experiment to show that a solution of naturally occurring valine is optically active, and give the expected result. **[2]**

**11.** **(a)** Glycine and serine, HOCH₂CH(NH₂)COOH, join to form a dipeptide. Give the structural formulae of the two possible dipeptides. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the Mr of the tripeptide formed from glycine (Mr 75.0), alanine (Mr 89.0) and serine (Mr 105.0). **[2]**

**12.** Four unlabelled samples, A to D, are tested.

- A: colourless liquid, fishy smell; turns red litmus blue; with copper(II) sulfate solution gives a pale blue precipitate that dissolves in excess A to a deep blue solution.
- B: colourless liquid; fizzes with sodium hydrogencarbonate solution; the gas turns limewater milky.
- C: colourless liquid, C₄H₈O; yellow-orange precipitate with 2,4-DNPH; no change with Fehling's solution; pale yellow precipitate with iodine in alkali.
- D: white solid; lilac flame; with dilute nitric acid then silver nitrate solution gives a cream precipitate.

**(a)** Identify the functional group in A and suggest why its solution is alkaline. **[2]**
**(b)** Identify the functional group in B. **[1]**
**(c)** Deduce the structure and name of C, using each observation. **[3]**
**(d)** Identify D. **[2]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** **2-aminopentanoic acid** [1]
**(b)** **N-ethylbutanamide** [1]
**(c)** CH₃CH₂CH₂NH₂ [1]; **primary** amine [1].
*Examiner insight:* In N-substituted amides the N- locant is part of the name, so "ethylbutanamide" alone describes a different compound.

**2.** Bases accept a proton using the lone pair on nitrogen, so strength depends on how available that lone pair is [1]. Pentylamine is the strongest: the alkyl group releases electron density onto nitrogen (positive inductive effect) [1]. Phenylamine is the weakest: the nitrogen lone pair is delocalised into the π system of the benzene ring [1]. Order: **phenylamine < ammonia < pentylamine** [1].
*Examiner insight:* Each explanation must mention the lone pair; "the ring makes it weaker" alone is unlikely to score.

**3. (a)** C₄H₉NH₂(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇌ C₄H₉NH₃⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) [1]
**(b)** CH₃COCl + 2C₄H₉NH₂ → CH₃CONHC₄H₉ + C₄H₉NH₃⁺Cl⁻ [1]; **N-butylethanamide** [1].
**(c)** A pale blue precipitate forms [1]; with more butylamine the precipitate disappears, leaving a deep blue solution [1].
*Examiner insight:* Give both stages and both colours; naming the complex ion is not an observation.

**4. (a)** Concentrated ammonia, in excess, dissolved in ethanol [1]; heated under pressure in a sealed container [1].
**(b)** Excess ammonia makes further substitution less likely [1].
**(c)** **(C₅H₁₁)₂NH** [1]
**(d)** **LiAlH₄** (dry ether solvent), or H₂ and nickel, to reduce pentanenitrile [1].
*Examiner insight:* "Ammonia" on its own is a weak answer here: the conditions (excess, ethanol, sealed tube) carry the credit.

**5.** n(HCl) = 0.100 × 30.00 ÷ 1000 = 0.00300 mol [1]; ratio 1 : 1 so n(RNH₂) = 0.00300 mol [1]; Mr = 0.261 ÷ 0.00300 = **87.0** [1]; CₙH₂ₙ₊₁NH₂: 14n + 17 = 87, so n = 5 and the formula is **C₅H₁₃N** [1].
*Examiner insight:* Show the general formula step, so the working can still earn credit if the final formula slips.

**6. (a)** Sn with concentrated HCl [1]; reflux [1].
**(b)** The product is the phenylammonium salt; NaOH removes the proton to release free phenylamine [1].
**(c)** n(nitrobenzene) = 6.15 ÷ 123.0 = 0.0500 mol, so theoretical mass = 0.0500 × 93.0 = 4.65 g [1]; yield = 3.26 ÷ 4.65 × 100 = **70.1%** [1].
*Examiner insight:* Work through moles; dividing the two masses directly is a common slip.

**7. (a)** Sodium nitrite and hydrochloric acid (to make nitrous acid) [1]; below 10 °C [1].
**(b)** C₆H₅NH₂ + HCl + HNO₂ → C₆H₅N₂⁺Cl⁻ + 2H₂O [1]
**(c)** Phenol dissolved in sodium hydroxide solution, kept cold [1]; **C₆H₅N=NC₆H₄OH** [1].
**(d)** n(phenylamine) = 2.79 ÷ 93.0 = 0.0300 mol, theoretical dye = 0.0300 × 198.0 = 5.94 g [1]; yield = 5.05 ÷ 5.94 × 100 = **85.0%** [1].
*Examiner insight:* A temperature range such as "0–5 °C" or "below 10 °C" is safer than "cold", which is vague.

**8. (a)** –[CH₂–CH(CONH₂)]– with extension bonds [1]
**(b)** –[CH₂–CH(OH)]– with extension bonds [1]
**(c)** –[NH(CH₂)₆NHCO(CH₂)₈CO]– [1]; **hydrogen chloride** [1].
**(d)** n(diamine) = 2.32 ÷ 116.0 = 0.0200 mol and n(dichloride) = 4.78 ÷ 239.0 = 0.0200 mol, so they react 1 : 1 with neither in excess [1]; mass = 0.0200 × 282.0 = **5.64 g** [1].
*Examiner insight:* Check each amide link carefully: an –OH or –Cl left on the end of a repeat unit, or a C=C left in an addition repeat unit, loses the mark.

**9. (a)** Hydrogen bonds form between N–H on one chain and C=O on a neighbouring chain [1]; with London forces along the long chains, these hold the chains firmly together [1].
**(b)** Its many –OH groups hydrogen bond with water [1], so the film dissolves in the wash and releases the detergent [1].
*Examiner insight:* Say which atoms form the hydrogen bonds; "strong intermolecular forces" alone is too vague for both marks.

**10. (a)** **(CH₃)₂CHCH(NH₃⁺)COO⁻** [1]
**(b)** Acid: (CH₃)₂CHCH(NH₃⁺)COOH [1]; alkali: (CH₃)₂CHCH(NH₂)COO⁻ [1].
**(c)** n(HCl) = 0.200 × 25.50 ÷ 1000 = 0.00510 mol = n(amino acid) [1]; Mr = 0.597 ÷ 0.00510 = **117** [1]; valine, C₅H₁₁NO₂, has Mr = 60.0 + 11.0 + 14.0 + 32.0 = 117.0, which matches [1].
**(d)** Pass plane-polarised monochromatic light through the solution in a polarimeter [1]; the plane of polarisation is rotated [1].
*Examiner insight:* In (b) the charges must be on the correct groups; a positive charge on the carboxylic acid group earns nothing.

**11. (a)** H₂NCH₂CONHCH(CH₂OH)COOH [1]; H₂NCH(CH₂OH)CONHCH₂COOH [1].
**(b)** Two water molecules are lost when three amino acids join [1]; Mr = 75.0 + 89.0 + 105.0 − 2 × 18.0 = **233.0** [1].
*Examiner insight:* Count the peptide links, not the amino acids: a chain of three has two links, so two waters are removed.

**12. (a)** **Amine** (–NH₂) [1]; the nitrogen lone pair accepts a proton from water, forming OH⁻ ions [1].
**(b)** **Carboxylic acid** (–COOH) [1]
**(c)** 2,4-DNPH shows a C=O group, and no change with Fehling's means it is a ketone, not an aldehyde [1]; the iodoform result shows a CH₃CO– group [1]; with C₄H₈O this is CH₃COCH₂CH₃, **butanone** [1].
**(d)** Lilac flame shows potassium ions [1]; cream precipitate shows bromide ions, so D is **potassium bromide, KBr** [1].
*Examiner insight:* Link each deduction to its observation; a correct name with no reasoning earns little.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Explaining base strength without the nitrogen lone pair.
- Using one mole of amine, not two, in the reaction with ethanoyl chloride.
- Giving "ammonia" without excess, ethanol and a sealed tube.
- Forgetting the NaOH step after tin and hydrochloric acid.
- Writing "cold" rather than a temperature for diazotisation.
- Repeat units with a C=C, or –OH or –Cl on the ends.
- Hydrogen bonding without naming the groups involved.
- Putting the charges on the wrong groups in amino acid ions.
- Removing the wrong number of water molecules from a peptide.
- Giving test results without a matching deduction.
