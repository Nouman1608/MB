Condensed notes on section **3.9.2 Assessing innovation** of AQA A-level Business (7132, for first teaching from September 2023). Section 3.9 is A-level only. The Fenner Components R&D example is in the [study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-assessing-innovation/); questions are in the [practice set](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-assessing-innovation-practice/).

## Specification content

- The pressures for innovation; types of innovation to include product and process innovation.
- The value of innovation.
- Ways of becoming an innovative organisation: Kaizen, research and development, intrapreneurship, benchmarking.
- How to protect innovation and intellectual property: patents and copyrights.
- The impact of an innovation strategy on the functional areas.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Innovation | The successful introduction of something new |
| Invention | The creation of a new idea; becomes innovation only when put to use |
| Product innovation | A new or improved product or service |
| Process innovation | A new or improved way of producing or delivering |
| Radical innovation | A major breakthrough |
| Incremental innovation | A series of small improvements |
| Research and development (R&D) | Planned work to discover and develop new products or processes |
| Kaizen | Continuous improvement by all employees |
| Intrapreneurship | Employees acting like entrepreneurs inside a firm |
| Benchmarking | Comparing with the best and adopting their practices |
| Intellectual property | Creations of the mind that can be owned |

## Pressures for innovation

Competition; customer expectations; technology change; product life cycles; cost pressure; regulation and social pressure; shareholder growth targets; globalisation.

## Value and limits

| Value | Limits |
|---|---|
| Competitive advantage; premium prices | Costly and uncertain |
| Higher revenue and profit | Slow payback; many failures |
| Stronger brand and image | Rivals may copy |
| New markets; lower costs | Distracts from the core business |
| Staff motivation | Needs a culture that tolerates failure |

## Methods of becoming innovative

| Method | Value | Limits |
|---|---|---|
| R&D | Patentable, unique products; long-term capability | Expensive; uncertain; years of delay |
| Kaizen | Low cost; staff ideas; quality; motivation | Small steps only; needs commitment; can plateau |
| Intrapreneurship | Uses staff creativity; keeps talent; faster | Needs risk-tolerant culture; hard to manage alongside normal work |
| Benchmarking | Realistic targets; learns from the best | Copies, does not lead; data may be unavailable; may not fit |

## Protecting IP

| Protection | Covers | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Patent | Inventions | Exclusive rights up to 20 years; costly; invention published; limited to the countries where granted |
| Copyright | Original works (software, writing, music, images) | Automatic; protects the expression, not the idea |

Others: trademarks, trade secrets, being first to market. Limits of protection: cost of applying for and enforcing rights; rivals may design around it.

## Impact on functions

| Function | Impact |
|---|---|
| Finance | R&D and launch funding; cash-flow pressure |
| Marketing | Launch, customer education, pricing, product portfolio |
| Operations | New processes, equipment, training, quality control |
| Human resources | Creative and technical recruitment; risk-tolerant culture; rewards for ideas |

## Worked mini-examples

- R&D spend 6% of £15m = **£900,000**. New products bring in 30% of revenue = **£4.5m**, so each £1 of R&D relates to £5 of new-product revenue.
- Kaizen: 100 suggestions saving £500 each = **£50,000** a year.
- Benchmarking: defect rate 4% against 1% for the best; on 20,000 units, 3% × 20,000 = 600 extra defects.

## Assessing an innovation strategy

Fit with objectives; affordability and staying power; risk of failure and how to limit it; protectability; market size and rivals' response; the firm's culture.

## Model analysis paragraph

"Kaizen could help Bryn Toys cut costs. Staff on the packing line see small wastes every day, such as time lost moving boxes. If they are encouraged to suggest changes and the best are adopted, each saving may be small, but 150 suggestions at £600 each save £90,000 a year. This improves margin without a large investment. However, the gains are incremental, so Kaizen alone is unlikely to produce a breakthrough product, and the scheme depends on managers acting on suggestions."

Notice the pattern: method, link to the case, calculation, effect on the business, and a limit.

## Command words

- **Explain / analyse:** build a chain of cause and effect.
- **Assess / evaluate:** weigh both sides and reach a judgement that states what it depends on.
- **Calculate:** show the formula and units.

## Exam traps

- Innovation is more than a new product; process innovation counts.
- Invention is not innovation.
- Kaizen is continuous and involves all staff.
- Patents protect inventions, not ideas; copyright protects expression.
- Patents last a limited time and cost money.

## Self-test

1. Distinguish innovation from invention.
2. Give an example of process innovation.
3. Give two pressures for innovation.
4. Explain Kaizen in one sentence.
5. Fifty staff suggest improvements that save an average of £400 each. Calculate the total saving.
6. Give one limit of R&D for a small firm.
7. What is intrapreneurship?
8. Give one limit of benchmarking.
9. How long does a patent typically last, at most?
10. What does copyright protect?
11. Why might a firm keep a recipe secret rather than patent it?
12. Give one impact of an innovation strategy on human resources.

### Answers

1. Invention is creating a new idea; innovation is successfully putting it to use in the market or in the business.
2. For example, a robot that packs products faster, or a new scheduling system that cuts waste.
3. Any two: competition; customer expectations; technology change; product life cycles; cost pressure; regulation.
4. Continuous improvement by all employees through many small changes.
5. 50 × £400 = **£20,000**.
6. It is expensive and uncertain, and a small firm may lack the funds or staff.
7. Employees acting like entrepreneurs inside a larger organisation, developing new ideas with support and resources.
8. Any one: it copies rather than leads; good comparison data may not be available; practices may not suit the firm.
9. Up to 20 years.
10. Original works such as writing, software, music or images (the way ideas are expressed, not the ideas themselves).
11. A patent publishes how it is made and lasts only for a time; a secret can last indefinitely if it stays secret.
12. Any one: recruiting creative and technical staff; training; a culture that accepts risk; reward systems for ideas.
