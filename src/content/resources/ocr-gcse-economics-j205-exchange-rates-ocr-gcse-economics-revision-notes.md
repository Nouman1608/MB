Condensed notes for section 4.3, Exchange rates, of OCR GCSE (9-1) Economics (J205, Version 2.0), topic 4, International trade and the global economy. The fuller explanations and worked examples are in the [exchange rates study guide](/resources/ocr-gcse-economics-j205-exchange-rates-ocr-gcse-economics/); test yourself afterwards with the [exchange rates practice questions](/resources/ocr-gcse-economics-j205-exchange-rates-ocr-gcse-economics-practice/). All figures are invented.

## The four things to be able to do

- **Draw and analyse** how supply and demand determine an exchange rate.
- **Calculate** currency conversions.
- **Analyse** recent and historical exchange rate data.
- **Evaluate** the effect of exchange rate changes on consumers and producers.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Exchange rate | The price of one currency in terms of another, e.g. £1 = €1.15 |
| Floating exchange rate | A rate set by the demand for and supply of the currency |
| Appreciation | A rise in a currency's value: £1 buys more foreign currency |
| Depreciation | A fall in a currency's value: £1 buys less foreign currency |
| Demand for pounds | Pounds wanted by people abroad to buy UK exports, visit, save or invest in the UK |
| Supply of pounds | Pounds offered by UK residents to buy imports, travel, save or invest abroad |
| Speculation | Buying or selling a currency to profit from an expected change in its value |
| Price competitiveness | How cheap a country's goods are compared with rivals' goods in the same market |

## Demand and supply in one table

| Event | Curve that moves | Pound |
|---|---|---|
| Overseas demand for UK exports rises | Demand shifts right | Appreciates |
| More foreign tourists visit the UK | Demand shifts right | Appreciates |
| UK interest rates rise | Demand right and supply left | Appreciates |
| UK households buy more imports | Supply shifts right | Depreciates |
| UK firms invest more overseas | Supply shifts right | Depreciates |
| Traders expect the pound to fall | Supply right (and demand left) | Depreciates |
| UK inflation higher than rivals' | Demand left over time | Depreciates |

### Diagram checklist

1. Vertical axis: price of the pound, e.g. "€ per £".
2. Horizontal axis: quantity of pounds.
3. D slopes down, S slopes up; label the equilibrium rate and quantity.
4. Shift the correct curve, label it D1 or S1, mark the new rate.
5. Write the chain: cause, curve, direction, new rate, appreciation or depreciation.

When both curves shift (an interest rate change), the rate moves strongly but the change in quantity can be small or zero.

## Conversion method

- **Pounds to foreign currency:** multiply by the rate. At £1 = ¥190, £75 = 75 × 190 = **¥14,250**.
- **Foreign currency to pounds:** divide by the rate. ¥9,500 = 9,500 ÷ 190 = **£50**.
- **Commission:** take the fee off first, then convert the remainder.
- **Buy and sell rates:** a bureau gives fewer units when you buy and wants more units when you sell back; the gap is its profit.
- **Percentage change in a rate:** (new − old) ÷ old × 100. From €1.10 to €1.21 the pound appreciates by **10%**.
- **Reverse rate:** 1 ÷ rate. If £1 = $1.25, then $1 = £0.80.
- **Check:** if a pound buys more than one unit of the other currency, the foreign amount should be the bigger number.

## Reading rate data

- State the overall trend, start and end values.
- Quote the highest and lowest points and when they happened.
- Calculate at least one percentage change from the figures.
- Compare currencies: the pound can rise against one and fall against another at the same time.
- Suggest a cause (interest rates, trade, confidence, political events) but say the data alone does not prove it.
- Annual averages hide movements within the year.

## Who gains and who loses

| Group | When the pound depreciates | When the pound appreciates |
|---|---|---|
| Exporters | Foreign prices fall; sales and margins can rise | Foreign prices rise; sales may fall |
| Importers and firms using imported inputs | Costs in pounds rise | Costs in pounds fall |
| Consumers | Imported goods and holidays abroad dearer | Imported goods and holidays abroad cheaper |
| UK tourist industry | More visitors from abroad | Fewer visitors from abroad |
| Workers in export industries | Jobs safer | Jobs at risk |
| General price level | Imported inflation more likely | Imported inflation less likely |

Mnemonic: **SPICED**, Strong Pound, Imports Cheap, Exports Dear.

### A depreciation gives exporters a choice

- Cut the foreign-currency price, keep the pound price, and win more sales; or
- keep the foreign-currency price, receive more pounds per item, and widen the profit margin.

## Evaluation: "it depends on"

- Price elasticity of demand for the exports or imports.
- How much of the firm's costs are imported.
- How big the change is and how long it lasts.
- Whether firms change prices at once or are locked into contracts.
- What is happening to rivals' currencies.
- Whether dearer imports push up inflation and wages, cancelling the gain.

Finish with a judgement for a named group, such as exporters or consumers, and give the condition.

## Exam traps

- Converting in the wrong direction: check the answer makes sense.
- Quoting a percentage change without saying which currency appreciated.
- Treating a 10% fall in the pound as a 10% rise in the other currency (it is 11.1%).
- Saying UK imports shift the demand for pounds: they shift supply.
- Writing that depreciation always helps exporters without elasticity or import content.
- Describing data without numbers, or numbers without a pattern.
- Ending an evaluation with no judgement, or "it depends" with no factor named.

## Self-test

1. Define appreciation.
2. Give two reasons why people abroad demand pounds.
3. Convert £240 to dollars at £1 = $1.35.
4. Convert $540 to pounds at £1 = $1.35.
5. The rate moves from $1.40 to $1.26 per pound. Calculate the percentage change in the pound's value.
6. Using Question 5, calculate the percentage change in the value of the dollar against the pound.
7. Which curve shifts when UK residents take more holidays abroad, and in which direction?
8. Explain why a rise in UK interest rates may cause the pound to appreciate.
9. A traveller changes £400 to euros at £1 = €1.15 after a £10 fee. How many euros does she receive?
10. Give one group that gains from an appreciation of the pound and explain why.
11. State two factors that decide how much a depreciation helps an exporter.
12. Explain why a depreciation may cause inflation.

### Answers

1. A rise in the value of a currency, so one unit buys more of other currencies.
2. Any two: to buy UK exports of goods or services; to visit the UK as tourists; to save or invest in the UK; speculation that the pound will rise.
3. 240 × 1.35 = **$324**.
4. 540 ÷ 1.35 = **£400**.
5. (1.26 − 1.40) ÷ 1.40 × 100 = **−10%**, a depreciation.
6. $1 was worth 1 ÷ 1.40 = £0.714 and is now 1 ÷ 1.26 = £0.794, a rise of **11.1%**.
7. The supply of pounds shifts right, because residents sell pounds to buy foreign currency.
8. Saving in pounds earns more, so foreign savers buy pounds (demand rises) and UK savers keep money at home (supply falls); the rate rises.
9. (400 − 10) × 1.15 = **€448.50**.
10. UK consumers of imported goods, because each pound buys more foreign currency, so imports cost less (also importers, firms with imported inputs, UK holidaymakers abroad).
11. Any two: price elasticity of demand for its exports; share of imported inputs; size and duration of the change; rivals' currency movements.
12. Imports and imported raw materials cost more in pounds, so firms' costs and the prices of imported goods rise.
