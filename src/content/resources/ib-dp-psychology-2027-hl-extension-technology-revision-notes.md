These notes are for **HL only**. The technology extension is assessed in Paper 3 (1 hour 45 minutes, 30 marks). They condense the IB Psychology guide for first assessment 2027. For explanations read the [study guide](/resources/ib-dp-psychology-2027-hl-extension-technology/) and then use the [practice set](/resources/ib-dp-psychology-2027-hl-extension-technology-practice/).

## Core ideas

- Technology includes mobile phones, computers, video games and artificial intelligence.
- It influences behaviour and relationships in all four contexts.
- Main concept links: **change**, **perspective**, **responsibility**; also bias, causality and measurement.
- The study of technology faces specific research problems (next section).

## The research problems named by the guide

| Problem | Meaning | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Isolating variables | Hard to separate technology use from other differences | Weak control, confounding variables |
| Sampling bias | Volunteers, students or online samples | Limits generalisability |
| Correlational designs | Most studies measure association | Bidirectional ambiguity |
| Researcher bias / confirmation bias | Expectations about harm or benefit, especially in school action research | Distorts data collection and interpretation |
| Publication bias | Positive or striking results are published more | Distorted evidence base |
| Funding | Who pays for research | Possible influence on questions and reporting |
| Replication crisis | Many findings not replicated | Limits confidence |

## Areas of inquiry (from the guide)

- **Health and well-being:** technology in prevention and treatment; technology and mental health problems; effectiveness of tele-therapy.
- **Human development:** technology and the development of self; AI to test models of development; technology and attachment.
- **Human relationships:** technology and interpersonal relationships; social media and group behaviour; technology to assist acculturation.
- **Learning and cognition:** technology in learning; technology and cognition.

## Example evidence (examples only)

| Area | Study or idea | Finding | Evaluation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Well-being | Twenge et al. (2018) | Higher screen time linked with lower well-being in adolescents | Correlational; self-report |
| Well-being | Orben and Przybylski (2019) | Technology use explained a very small part of variation in well-being (about 0.4%) | Same data type, different analysis; shows effect size matters |
| Treatment | Tele-therapy | Can widen access and be about as effective as face-to-face for several conditions | Outcome measures vary; digital exclusion |
| Relationships | Kramer et al. (2014) | Manipulating Facebook news feeds shifted users' posts slightly | Huge sample, tiny effect, indirect measure, no informed consent |
| Cognition | Sparrow et al. (2011) | People remember where to find information rather than the information | Lab task; mixed replication |
| Cognition | Ward et al. (2017) | Presence of own phone reduced cognitive capacity | Replication mixed |
| Development | AI simulations | Test whether a model of learning can work | Shows possibility, not what children actually do |

## Concepts and technology

- **Causality:** correlational data cannot show that screen time causes harm.
- **Bias:** sampling, confirmation and publication bias.
- **Measurement:** self-reported screen time is unreliable; logging data are more objective but incomplete.
- **Change:** technology changes behaviour (and behaviour change interventions use technology).
- **Perspective:** optimistic versus pessimistic views; biological, cognitive and sociocultural accounts.
- **Responsibility:** consent in online experiments, protecting privacy, reporting uncertainty.

## Paper 3 technique

| Question | Skill | Marks |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Explain one issue that limits interpretation of a source | 3 |
| 2 | Analyse findings from one source and state a conclusion | 6 |
| 3 | Credibility, bias or transferability | 6 |
| 4 | Synthesis of at least three sources for a claim | 15 |

For technology:
- Question 1: self-reported hours, correlation, scale of effect, sample.
- Question 2: report values from the graph or table; give a conclusion that matches a correlational design.
- Question 3: for credibility, ask how data were collected and whether triangulated; for bias, the researcher's expectations and funding; for transferability, whether the context resembles others.
- Question 4: use at least three sources, own knowledge (for example the replication problem and effect size), different points of view and a conclusion.

## Common mistakes

- Treating all technology as one variable.
- Stating that screen time "causes" depression from a correlation.
- Ignoring effect size.
- Forgetting ethics in online or school-based research.

## Self-test

1. Name two research problems the guide associates with the study of technology.
2. What is bidirectional ambiguity? Give a technology example.
3. Why can a statistically significant effect still be unimportant?
4. Give one ethical problem in the Kramer et al. (2014) study.
5. What is action research and why does the guide mention it?
6. Why is replication important?
7. Give one area of inquiry for technology in the human relationships context.

**Answers:** 1. For example sampling bias and correlational designs (also publication bias, confirmation bias, funding, lack of replication). 2. It is unclear which variable causes the other; for example heavy social media use may lower mood or low mood may lead to heavier use. 3. A very large sample can detect a tiny effect that has little practical meaning. 4. Users did not give informed consent and were not debriefed. 5. Research carried out by practitioners such as teachers in their own setting; the guide notes a high potential for researcher bias or confirmation bias. 6. If an effect is not found again, confidence in the original claim falls. 7. For example the impact of technology on interpersonal relationships, social media and group behaviour, or technology and acculturation.

## Frequently asked questions

**Does the guide name studies on technology?**
No. It names areas of inquiry and research problems. You choose the evidence.

**Do I need to take a side on whether technology is harmful?**
No. A strong answer weighs the evidence, noting the design, effect size and replication.

**Do SL students study technology?**
No. It is an HL extension.
