Condensed notes on section **3.5.1 Setting financial objectives** of AQA A-level Business (7132, for first teaching from September 2023). The full explanations and worked examples are in the [financial objectives study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-setting-financial-objectives/); exam-style questions are in the [financial objectives practice set](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-setting-financial-objectives-practice/).

## Specification content

- The value of setting financial objectives: return on investment; revenue, costs and profit objectives; cash flow objectives.
- The distinction between cash flow and profit.
- The distinction between gross profit, operating profit and profit for the year.
- Throughout section 3.5: technology (for example crowd funding); market conditions and competition; ethical and environmental influences; competitiveness; links with other functions.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Financial objective | Measurable target for financial performance |
| Return on investment (ROI) | Profit from an investment as a percentage of the amount invested |
| Revenue | Income from sales (price × quantity) |
| Cash flow | Money actually moving into and out of the business |
| Profit | Revenue earned minus costs incurred |
| Gross profit | Revenue − cost of sales |
| Operating profit | Gross profit − operating expenses |
| Profit for the year | Profit after interest and tax |
| Retained profit | Profit kept in the business for reinvestment |
| Profit margin | Profit as a percentage of revenue |

## Why set financial objectives

Direction; control (compare actual with target); motivation and accountability; coordination with other functions; reassurance to stakeholders (lenders, shareholders); a decision test (minimum ROI). **Limits:** unrealistic or out-of-date targets; narrow focus can harm the long term (cutting training or maintenance); figures can be manipulated.

## The five kinds of objective

| Objective | Example | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Return on investment | Target ROI of 12% | Compare with a low-risk return; ignores timing of profits |
| Revenue | Raise revenue by 8% | Does not guarantee profit |
| Costs | Cut cost of sales by 3% | Do not harm quality or morale |
| Profit | Operating margin of 10% | Total or margin; stage of life matters |
| Cash flow | Keep bank balance above £50,000 | Vital for start-ups, fast growth, seasonal firms |

**ROI (%) = profit from investment ÷ amount invested × 100.** A £200,000 press adding £30,000 profit a year gives 15%.

## Cash flow against profit

| | Cash flow | Profit |
|---|---|---|
| Credit sales | Counted when paid | Counted when sold |
| Equipment bought | Large outflow at once | Spread through depreciation |
| Loan received | Cash inflow | Not revenue |
| Importance | Survival | Long-run success |

**Worked example.** Revenue £120,000 (£40,000 unpaid); costs £90,000 (£20,000 unpaid); oven bought for £25,000 cash.

- Profit = £120,000 − £90,000 = **£30,000**.
- Cash in = £80,000; cash out = £70,000 + £25,000 = £95,000; net cash flow = **−£15,000**.
- Profitable but cash negative: a firm can fail while profitable.

## Three levels of profit

| Line | Calculation |
|---|---|
| Gross profit | Revenue − cost of sales |
| Operating profit | Gross profit − operating expenses |
| Profit before tax | Operating profit − net finance costs |
| Profit for the year | Profit before tax − tax |

**Worked example.** Revenue £2,400,000; cost of sales £1,440,000; operating expenses £720,000; interest £40,000; tax 20%.

- Gross profit £960,000 (40%).
- Operating profit £240,000 (10%).
- Profit before tax £200,000; tax £40,000.
- Profit for the year **£160,000** (6.7% margin).

What each shows: gross profit shows pricing, materials costs and production efficiency; operating profit shows how well overheads are controlled; profit for the year is what is left for shareholders.

## Influences on financial objectives

Corporate objectives; stage of life (start-up, growth, maturity); market conditions and competition; stakeholders' wants; ethical and environmental pressures; technology (crowd funding, online payments); links with other functions. Objectives can conflict (revenue growth by discounting cuts margins).

## Exam traps

- Cash flow and profit are **not** the same.
- Gross, operating and net profit are different lines; name the one you mean.
- Margins are a percentage of **revenue**.
- A loan raises cash but not profit.
- A good objective has a number and a time limit.

## Self-test

1. Define "return on investment".
2. A project costing £80,000 adds £12,000 a year to profit. Calculate ROI.
3. Give one reason why a firm might set a minimum ROI higher than bank interest.
4. Give one risk of a strict cost-cutting objective.
5. Why might a profitable business run out of cash?
6. Revenue is £500,000 and cost of sales is £300,000. Calculate gross profit and gross margin.
7. Operating expenses are £125,000. Calculate operating profit and operating margin.
8. Interest is £15,000 and tax is £12,000. Calculate profit for the year.
9. Does a bank loan increase profit? Explain.
10. Why is equipment purchase treated differently in cash flow and profit?
11. Give two reasons for setting financial objectives.
12. Give one way financial and marketing objectives could conflict.

### Answers

1. Profit gained from an investment as a percentage of the amount invested.
2. 12,000 ÷ 80,000 × 100 = **15%**.
3. Because investments carry risk; the return must exceed what could be earned safely.
4. It may reduce quality, staff morale or the future capability of the business.
5. Customers may pay late, stock or equipment may have been bought for cash, or large bills may fall due before sales are paid for.
6. £200,000; 200 ÷ 500 × 100 = **40%**.
7. £200,000 − £125,000 = **£75,000**; 75 ÷ 500 × 100 = **15%**.
8. £75,000 − £15,000 − £12,000 = **£48,000**.
9. No. A loan is a cash inflow but not revenue; only the interest is a cost that reduces profit.
10. The full price leaves the bank at once, but profit records the cost spread over the equipment's life as depreciation.
11. Any two: direction; control; motivation; coordination; reassurance to stakeholders.
12. For example, a revenue target may need discounts that reduce profit margins.
