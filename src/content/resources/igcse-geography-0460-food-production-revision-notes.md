These notes condense the [Food production study guide](/resources/igcse-geography-0460-food-production/). Read that first if a point here is new to you.

These notes cover section 3.2 Food production of the Cambridge IGCSE Geography (0460) syllabus for examination in 2025 and 2026, Version 2 (published November 2022). The section is part of Theme 3: Economic development. It is assessed mainly in Paper 1 Geographical Themes, which mixes resource-based tasks with place-specific writing. Paper 2 Geographical Skills can also set knowledge questions on topics from the three themes. When you have revised, try the [practice questions](/resources/igcse-geography-0460-food-production-practice/), and track progress on the [Cambridge IGCSE Geography hub](/boards/cambridge/igcse/geography/) and [checklist](/checklists/cambridge/igcse/geography/).

## What the syllabus asks for

- Describe and explain the main features of an agricultural system: inputs, processes and outputs.
- Know the farming types: commercial and subsistence; arable, pastoral and mixed; intensive and extensive.
- Explain how natural inputs (relief, climate, soil) and human inputs (economic, social) influence land use, and how they combine to shape scale of production, methods of organisation and products.
- Recognise the causes and effects of food shortages and describe possible solutions. Natural causes include drought, floods, tropical storms and pests. Economic and political causes include low capital investment, poor distribution and transport difficulties, and wars.
- Know the negative effects of shortages, and how they encourage food aid and measures to increase output.
- Case studies: a farm or agricultural system, and a country or region suffering from food shortages.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Agricultural system | A farm seen as inputs, processes and outputs, linked by feedback |
| Inputs | Everything a farm needs: natural (relief, climate, soil) and human (economic, social) |
| Processes | Work that turns inputs into outputs, such as ploughing, transplanting, milking, harvesting |
| Outputs | Products such as crops, milk and meat, plus waste and by-products |
| Feedback | Outputs that return as inputs, such as saved seed, manure or reinvested profit |
| Commercial farming | Producing crops or animals to sell for profit |
| Subsistence farming | Producing food mainly to feed the farmer's own household |
| Arable | Growing crops |
| Pastoral | Rearing animals |
| Mixed | Crops and animals on the same farm |
| Intensive | High inputs of labour or capital per hectare, giving high output per hectare |
| Extensive | Low inputs per hectare over a large area, giving low output per hectare |
| Land tenure | The way land is owned or rented |
| Food shortage | Not enough food produced, or people unable to obtain or afford it |
| Famine | A severe, widespread food shortage causing deaths from hunger |
| Malnutrition | Ill health from a diet lacking the right amount or balance of nutrients |
| Food aid | Food, or money for food, given by governments, the UN or charities |
| High-yielding varieties (HYV) | Crop strains bred to produce more grain, usually needing fertiliser and water |
| Appropriate technology | Simple, affordable tools that local people can build and repair |

## Case studies at a glance

| Case study | What it shows | Key facts | One evaluation point |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wet rice farming, Bangladesh (farm or agricultural system) | Subsistence, arable, intensive system | Flat Ganges-Brahmaputra delta with alluvium and monsoon rain; rice on 76% of cropped area and over 13 million farms; three seasons: aus, aman (rain-fed, harvested Nov-Dec), boro (irrigated dry season, now the leading crop); about 39.1 million tonnes in 2022-23 | Shows how natural and human inputs combine, but boro needs costly inputs, so poorer farmers gain less |
| Wheat farming, Canadian Prairies (contrast) | Commercial, arable, extensive system | Large, mechanised farms in provinces such as Saskatchewan; wheat, oats, barley, canola; early homesteads of 160 acres grew much larger; south-west prone to drought | Good contrast in capital and scale; use it as a contrast, not as your only farm case |
| Somalia 2010-2012 (country or region with food shortages) | Drought plus conflict turning shortage into famine | Worst drought in 60 years; April-June 2011 rain under 30% of the 1995-2010 average in many areas; famine declared 20 July 2011 in Lower Shabelle and Bakool; prices up to 240% higher; more than 920,000 refugees by mid-September 2011; death toll estimates about 50,000 to 260,000; famine declared over February 2012 | Shows natural and political causes interacting; death toll estimates vary, so present a range |
| Green Revolution, India (measure to increase output) | HYV seeds, fertiliser and irrigation | From the 1960s; linked to Norman Borlaug's wheat; raised wheat and rice output in states such as Punjab | Output rose, but groundwater use and input costs are criticised |

## Must-know distinctions

- **Commercial vs subsistence**: about who the food is for. Many farms do both, so describe the main purpose.
- **Intensive vs extensive**: about inputs and output per hectare, not farm wealth. A large, mechanised wheat farm is extensive.
- **Natural vs human inputs**: relief, climate and soil are natural. Capital, markets, transport, government policy, land tenure and tradition are human.
- **Food shortage vs famine**: famine is the extreme end, with widespread deaths.
- **Emergency vs long-term aid**: emergency food saves lives now; long-term free food can depress local prices.
- **Causes vs effects**: drought is a cause; malnutrition and migration are effects. Keep them in separate paragraphs.

## Solutions in brief

| Response | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|
| Emergency food aid | Saves lives quickly in famine | Costly to move; can be blocked or stolen in war zones |
| Cash transfers (hawala, mobile phones in Somalia 2011) | People buy local food, which supports local traders | Only works if food is on sale and prices are not too high |
| Irrigation (tubewells, small dams) | Allows a dry-season crop, such as boro rice | Pumps cost money and can lower water tables |
| HYV seeds and fertiliser | Large rise in output per hectare | Richer farmers benefit most; needs water and money |
| Appropriate technology (hand pumps, storage silos) | Cheap, local, cuts losses after harvest | Raises output less than large schemes |
| Early warning and pest control | Action before crops are lost | Needs funding and working transport to respond |

## Mini essay plan

*Question type: "To what extent were natural causes more important than economic and political causes of food shortage in a country or region you have studied?"*

1. **Introduction**: name the place and dates (southern Somalia, 2010-2012). Define food shortage and famine in one sentence each.
2. **Natural causes**: drought described as the worst in 60 years; April-June 2011 rain under 30% of the 1995-2010 average in many areas; livestock losses of 40-60% in some areas. Explain the chain from failed rain to lost income and food.
3. **Economic causes**: food prices up to 240% higher in the south; poor households could not buy food. Low investment meant little irrigation or storage.
4. **Political causes**: conflict involving al-Shabaab; aid restricted and some operations suspended; famine declared in Lower Shabelle and Bakool on 20 July 2011.
5. **Judgement**: drought triggered the crisis, but conflict and blocked aid explain why it became famine in southern Somalia while the same drought did not bring a famine declaration elsewhere in the region. Answer "to what extent" directly.

## Quick self-test

1. Name the three natural inputs listed in the syllabus.
2. Is "transplanting seedlings" an input, process or output?
3. Define extensive farming.
4. Give one example of feedback in a farming system.
5. Classify Bangladeshi wet rice farming using all three type pairs.
6. Which Bangladeshi rice season is grown in the dry season with irrigation?
7. Name the four natural causes of food shortage listed in the syllabus.
8. Name the three economic and political causes listed.
9. When and where did the UN declare famine in Somalia?
10. Give one disadvantage of long-term food aid.
11. What three things did the Green Revolution combine?

### Answers

1. Relief, climate and soil.
2. A process.
3. Farming with low inputs of labour or capital per hectare over a large area, giving low output per hectare.
4. Saving seed for next year, spreading manure, or reinvesting profit in machinery.
5. Mainly subsistence, arable and intensive.
6. Boro.
7. Drought, floods, tropical storms and pests.
8. Low capital investment, poor distribution or transport difficulties, and wars.
9. 20 July 2011, in Lower Shabelle and Bakool in southern Somalia.
10. Free food can lower local prices, so local farmers earn less and may grow less (or: it can create dependence).
11. High-yielding seeds, fertiliser and irrigation.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing inputs without linking them to land use, scale, organisation or products.
- Saying a farm is intensive because it is mechanised, rather than judging inputs per hectare.
- Putting labour or rainfall in the process box of a systems diagram.
- Giving only drought as a cause when the syllabus lists floods, tropical storms, pests, low capital investment, transport problems and wars.
- Case study answers with no place name, dates or figures.
- Writing about effects when the question asks for causes, or about causes when it asks for solutions.
- One-sided answers on food aid or the Green Revolution with no judgement.
- Quoting a single famine death toll as certain when estimates vary widely.
