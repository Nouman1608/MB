## Section A

**1.** Define the term digital divide. **[2]**

**2.** State **two** types of technology the digital divide applies to. **[2]**

**3.** Explain why rural areas are more likely to be affected by the digital divide than cities. **[2]**

**4.** Give **one** reason why a household with a low income may have poor access to technology. **[1]**

**5.** State what is meant by digital literacy teaching. **[2]**

## Section B

**6.** Explain how the availability of high and low performance computers can cause a digital divide. **[3]**

**7.** Describe **two** ways in which wireless connections can contribute to the digital divide. **[4]**

**8.** Describe how the digital divide may affect each of these groups.

**(a)** People over 75 years old. **[2]**

**(b)** People with a visual impairment. **[2]**

**(c)** Families in a less industrially developed country. **[2]**

**9.** A town council notices that many residents on low incomes cannot apply for services that are now available only online. Discuss how the council could reduce the effects of the digital divide. **[6]**

**10.** Describe **two** effects of the digital divide on students. **[4]**

**11.** The government of a country is deciding between investing in rural broadband cables and funding digital literacy classes. Compare the two options. **[4]**

**12.** Evaluate the statement: "The digital divide only affects people in poor countries." **[6]**

---

## Answers

**1.** The gap between people and regions that have access to modern technology and information [1] and those that have restricted or no access [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Any two from: smartphones [1]; televisions [1]; personal computers [1]; tablets [1]; laptops [1]; internet access [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Rural areas often lack infrastructure such as cables and mobile masts [1], because providers see fewer customers, so connections are slow, unreliable or unavailable [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Any one from: they cannot afford new devices [1]; they cannot afford monthly internet or data charges [1]. **[1]**

**5.** Teaching people the skills to use technology and the internet [1], including finding and judging information and staying safe online [1]. **[2]**

**6.** High performance computers can run modern software and fast connections but are expensive [1], so people on low incomes may only have older, low performance computers or a basic smartphone [1]. These may be too slow or unable to run modern software, so users cannot take part in online learning, work or services as well as others [1]. **[3]**

**7.** Any two, each with a development: rural areas may have a weak or no mobile signal [1], so people cannot get online or have slow connections [1]; mobile data is expensive and limited [1], so poorer users cannot afford video or large downloads [1]; wireless connections are less reliable in bad weather or thick buildings [1], so users have an unreliable service for work or study [1]. **[4]**

**8. (a)** Older people may lack the skills or confidence to use technology [1], so they may miss online services such as banking or health appointments [1].

**(b)** Websites may not be designed for screen readers [1], and specialist equipment is costly, so they have less access to information and services [1].

**(c)** The country may have limited electricity, networks and technology awareness [1], so families have less access to online education, work and information than those in developed countries [1]. **[6]**

**9.** Any six from: provide free computers and Wi-Fi in libraries and community centres [1], so residents without devices can apply [1]; run digital literacy classes [1], to give residents the skills and confidence [1]; offer staff help in person or by telephone [1], for those who cannot use the internet [1]; keep paper and telephone options [1]; provide low-cost or loan devices and data [1]; work with providers on better connections [1]. Judgement: free public access and help are the quickest and most direct, while skills classes bring longer-term benefits [1]. **[6]**

**10.** Any two effects, each developed: students without a device or internet cannot do online research or homework [1], so they may fall behind [1]; they cannot follow online lessons if a school closes [1], so they miss learning [1]; they miss digital skills [1], which makes it harder to find a job later [1]. **[4]**

**11.** Rural broadband fixes the lack of connection, whereas digital literacy gives people the skills to use one [1]. Broadband is very expensive and takes years, whereas classes are cheaper and can start quickly [1]. Classes do not help if people have no connection or device, whereas broadband without skills will not be used well [1]. The best approach combines both, starting with the one that removes the biggest barrier locally [1]. **[4]**

**12.** The statement is partly true: less industrially developed nations often have limited infrastructure, electricity and technology awareness [1], so large numbers of people have no access [1]. However, the divide also exists within rich countries [1], for example between cities and rural areas, between income groups, and for older people or those with disabilities [1]. Cost and skills barriers affect people in any country [1]. Conclusion: the statement is wrong because the gap exists everywhere, though its scale is greater in poorer regions [1]. **[6]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Defining the divide only as "having a computer or not".
- Naming a group without stating the specific barrier it faces.
- Treating digital literacy as a full solution without a device or connection.
- Writing "they are poor" without explaining the effect on access.
- Missing the judgement in a discuss or evaluate question.
