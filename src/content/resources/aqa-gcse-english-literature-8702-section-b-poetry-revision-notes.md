Quick-recall notes for the anthology comparison. The full method, with two original poems and a model
comparative paragraph, is in the [Poetry study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-english-literature-8702-section-b-poetry/).

## The task in one view

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| Texts | One cluster of 15 poems from *Poems Past and Present*, all studied |
| Clusters | Love and Relationships; Power and Conflict; Worlds and Lives |
| Question | One comparative question |
| Printed | One named poem only |
| From memory | The second poem, chosen by you from the same cluster |
| Marks | 30 (no AO4) |
| Split in AQA mark schemes | AO1 12, AO2 12, AO3 6 |

## Core recall

- **Compare from the first sentence.** The question is comparative; separate essays score lower.
- **Method, not just meaning.** Compare *how* poets present the idea: voice, form, structure,
  language.
- **Choose the second poem for contrast or connection**, and for quotations you can recall.
- **All fifteen poems** must be revised; any can be named.
- **Context is the smallest share** of the marks; keep it brief and linked to a method or idea.
- **Balance** the two poems, even though only one is printed.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning | Typical effect |
|---|---|---|
| Speaker / persona | The voice of the poem, not necessarily the poet | Perspective, irony |
| Dramatic monologue | One character speaks to a silent listener | Reveals more than the speaker intends |
| Sonnet | Fourteen-line poem, often with a turn (volta) | Argument, love, compression |
| Free verse | No regular metre or rhyme | Natural speech, freedom or disorder |
| Ballad | Narrative poem with regular stanzas and rhyme | Story, tradition, inevitability |
| Volta | Turn in thought or tone | Change of attitude |
| Enjambment | Sentence runs over the line end | Flow, urgency, overflow of feeling |
| Caesura | Pause within a line | Interruption, hesitation, emphasis |
| End-stopping | Line ending with punctuation | Control, finality |
| Half rhyme | Near rhyme with mismatched sounds | Unease, incompleteness |
| Sibilance | Repeated "s" sounds | Softness, menace, whispering |
| Extended metaphor | Metaphor developed across lines | Builds a theme through one image |
| Cyclical structure | Ending returns to opening | Entrapment, continuity |
| Juxtaposition | Contrasting images side by side | Highlights difference |

## One revision card per poem

For each of the fifteen poems, fill one card:

- **Speaker and situation** in one line.
- **Central idea** and whether the attitude shifts.
- **Form** and one reason the poet might have chosen it.
- **Structure**: one feature of shape and one of the ending.
- **Three short quotations**, each with a method.
- **One context point** that explains a choice.
- **Two partner poems** with a similarity and a difference noted.

## Context by period, briefly

| Period | Ideas that may be relevant |
|---|---|
| Romantic era | Nature, feeling, imagination, the individual |
| Victorian era | Duty, faith and doubt, empire, social change |
| Twentieth-century wars | Patriotism questioned, trauma, loss |
| Recent decades | Identity, migration, family, place, voice |

Use these only where they fit the poem in front of you.

## Comparative connectives

| Similarity | Difference | Weighing |
|---|---|---|
| Similarly | In contrast | More strikingly |
| Likewise | Whereas | Even more |
| Both poets | However | Less directly |
| In the same way | On the other hand | Ultimately |

## Paragraph shape

1. Comparative point that covers both poems.
2. Evidence and method from the named poem.
3. Evidence and method from the second poem.
4. Comparison of effects, ending with a judgement.
5. Context only if it explains a difference or similarity.

## Exam traps

- **Two halves, one link.** Writing separately about each poem with a comparison only at the end.
- **Theme-only comparison.** "Both are about conflict" gives nothing to analyse.
- **Unbalanced coverage** of the printed poem at the expense of the remembered one.
- **Inventing quotations** for the second poem; refer precisely to images or moments instead.
- **Spending too long** and leaving too little time for Section C.
- **Biography instead of analysis.** A poet's life matters only where it explains a choice.
- **Confusing poet and speaker**, especially in dramatic monologues.

## Self-test

1. How many poems are in a cluster, and how many must you study?
2. Which poem is printed on the paper?
3. How many marks is the question worth, and is AO4 assessed?
4. Which objective carries the smallest share of this question's marks?
5. What is a dramatic monologue?
6. What is a volta, and where is it often found?
7. Give one effect of half rhyme.
8. What is the difference between enjambment and end-stopping?
9. Name two things that make a good second poem.
10. What is an integrated comparison structure?
11. Why should you distinguish the poet from the speaker?
12. Give two comparative connectives for difference.

## Answers

1. Fifteen; you study all fifteen.
2. The named poem in the question. The second poem must be recalled.
3. 30 marks; no, AO4 is not assessed in this section.
4. AO3, context (6 of the 30 in AQA's mark schemes).
5. A poem in which one character speaks to a silent listener, often revealing more than they intend.
6. A turn in thought or tone, often found in sonnets.
7. It can create unease, incompleteness or a sense that something is slightly wrong.
8. Enjambment lets the sentence run on past the line end; end-stopping closes the line with
   punctuation.
9. Any two of: a clear link to the question's idea; a meaningful similarity and difference in
   method; quotations you can recall accurately.
10. Each paragraph compares one point across both poems rather than treating them separately.
11. Because the speaker may be a persona with views the poet does not share, especially in a dramatic
    monologue.
12. Any two of: whereas, in contrast, however, on the other hand.
