This guide teaches section **3.1.3.6 Bond polarity** of the AQA AS and A-level Chemistry specification (7404/7405), for teaching from September 2015. It is AS content, examined on Papers 1 and 2 (and Paper 3). It links two earlier ideas: the shape of a molecule and the distribution of electrons in its bonds. The key skill is to say whether a **bond** is polar, and then whether the **molecule** has a permanent dipole, which depends on the shape. The [Bonding study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-bonding/) gives a shorter overview. ## Electronegativity

**Electronegativity is the power of an atom to attract the pair of electrons in a covalent bond.** It is measured on the Pauling scale, on which fluorine, the most electronegative element, has a value of 4.0.

| Element | H | C | N | O | F | Cl | Br | I | S | P | Na | Mg |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pauling value | 2.1 | 2.5 | 3.0 | 3.5 | 4.0 | 3.0 | 2.8 | 2.5 | 2.5 | 2.1 | 0.9 | 1.2 |

The values are given when needed, but the trends should be learned.

- **Across a period** electronegativity increases. The nuclear charge rises, the atomic radius falls and the shielding stays about the same, so the nucleus attracts a bonding pair more strongly.
- **Down a group** electronegativity decreases. The bonding pair is further from the nucleus and there is more shielding from inner electron shells.

So the most electronegative elements are in the top right of the Periodic Table (excluding the noble gases): F, O, N and Cl.

### Worked example 1: explain the trend in Group 17

Explain why fluorine is more electronegative than iodine.

A fluorine atom is smaller, so its bonding pair is closer to the nucleus. It also has fewer inner shells, so there is less shielding of the nuclear attraction. Both factors mean that fluorine attracts a shared pair more strongly.

## Polar bonds

When two atoms in a covalent bond have the same electronegativity, the electron pair is shared equally and the bond is **non-polar**. This is true in all diatomic elements (H₂, Cl₂, O₂).

When the electronegativities differ, the bonding pair is pulled towards the more electronegative atom. The electron distribution is **unsymmetrical**. The more electronegative atom has a small negative charge, **δ−**, and the other has a small positive charge, **δ+**. (δ is the Greek letter delta, read as "partial".) The bond is **polar**.

```
δ+   δ−
H – Cl

δ−   δ+
O – H
```

The greater the difference in electronegativity, the more polar the bond. A very large difference (as between Na and Cl) produces a full transfer of electrons: an ionic bond. In this way covalent and ionic bonding lie along a continuum, with polar covalent bonding between them.

### Worked example 2: identify polar bonds

Use the values above to decide whether each bond is polar and mark the partial charges.

| Bond | Values | Difference | Polar? | Partial charges |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| C–H | 2.5, 2.1 | 0.4 | Slightly; usually treated as non-polar | C δ−, H δ+ (small) |
| C–Cl | 2.5, 3.0 | 0.5 | Yes | C δ+, Cl δ− |
| O–H | 3.5, 2.1 | 1.4 | Yes, strongly | O δ−, H δ+ |
| C–C | 2.5, 2.5 | 0 | No | None |
| P–H | 2.1, 2.1 | 0 | No | None |

AQA questions that ask you to use partial charges normally give the bond to a clearly more electronegative atom (O, N, F, Cl or Br). The C–H bond is treated as essentially non-polar.

## From polar bonds to polar molecules

A molecule has a **permanent dipole** if its overall charge distribution is unsymmetrical. Think of each polar bond as a pull towards the more electronegative atom. These pulls add as vectors, and the overall result depends on the shape.

- If the polar bonds are arranged so that their effects **cancel**, the molecule has no overall dipole.
- If they do **not** cancel, the molecule has a permanent dipole.

Always do it in two steps: (1) are the bonds polar? (2) is the molecule symmetrical, so that the pulls cancel?

### Worked example 3: molecules with polar bonds and a permanent dipole

**Water, H₂O.** The O–H bonds are polar, O δ− and H δ+. The shape is bent, so the pulls do not cancel. The oxygen end is δ−, and the molecule has a permanent dipole.

**Ammonia, NH₃.** Three polar N–H bonds in a pyramidal shape. The lone pair and the pyramidal shape mean the pulls do not cancel. There is a permanent dipole.

**Hydrogen chloride, HCl.** A single polar bond in a diatomic molecule. There is nothing to cancel it, so it has a permanent dipole.

**Trichloromethane, CHCl₃.** Three C–Cl bonds pull towards chlorine but the C–H bond does not pull the other way. The tetrahedral shape is not symmetrical because one position is occupied by hydrogen, so there is a net dipole.

### Worked example 4: molecules with polar bonds but no permanent dipole

**Carbon dioxide, CO₂.** Linear. Each C=O bond is polar (O δ−), but the two pulls are equal and in opposite directions, so they cancel.

**Tetrachloromethane, CCl₄.** Tetrahedral and symmetrical. Four equal C–Cl pulls cancel.

**Boron trifluoride, BF₃.** Trigonal planar. Three equal pulls at 120° cancel.

**Sulfur hexafluoride, SF₆.** Octahedral. Six equal pulls in three opposed pairs cancel.

**Phosphorus pentachloride, PCl₅.** Trigonal bipyramidal; the three equatorial pulls cancel and the two axial pulls cancel.

**trans-1,2-dichloroethene.** The two C–Cl bonds point in opposite directions across the double bond, so they cancel. The cis isomer has both on the same side and is polar.

In all of these the key phrase is "the polar bonds are arranged symmetrically, so the dipoles cancel".

### Worked example 5: comparing CHCl₃ and CCl₄

Explain why trichloromethane has a permanent dipole but tetrachloromethane does not.

Both molecules are tetrahedral and both have polar C–Cl bonds. In CCl₄ the four C–Cl bonds are identical and arranged symmetrically, so their effects cancel and there is no overall dipole. In CHCl₃ one of the positions has H, which does not pull in the opposite direction with the same force. The three C–Cl pulls are not balanced, so the molecule has an overall dipole with the chlorine side δ−.

## Why this matters

A permanent dipole gives a molecule extra attraction to neighbouring molecules (permanent dipole–dipole forces, covered in the next subtopic) and makes it more likely to dissolve in polar solvents such as water. A polar molecule also turns in an electric field, which can be shown by deflecting a thin stream of a polar liquid from a burette towards a charged rod. Non-polar liquids are not deflected.

## Common mistakes

- Saying that a molecule with polar bonds is always polar.
- Labelling δ+ and δ− on the wrong atoms, or leaving δ symbols off after drawing the bond.
- Writing that carbon dioxide is non-polar because its bonds are non-polar. The bonds are polar. The molecule is symmetrical.
- Treating the C–H bond as strongly polar.
- Describing electronegativity as "the attraction of an atom for electrons" without reference to the shared pair in a covalent bond.
- Explaining the cancelling effect without mentioning shape or symmetry.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Define** electronegativity with all the key words: power, atom, attract, pair of electrons, covalent bond.
- **Explain** why a bond is polar: give the difference in electronegativity, say which atom attracts the pair more strongly, and show δ+ and δ−.
- **Explain** why a molecule is non-polar: say the bonds are polar, but arranged symmetrically, so the dipoles cancel.
- **Deduce** whether a molecule is polar: state the shape first.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is polarity the same as electronegativity?**
No. Electronegativity belongs to an atom. Polarity is a property of a bond (difference in electronegativity) or of a molecule (shape plus bond polarity).

**Does a lone pair affect polarity?**
It affects the shape, and so whether the dipoles cancel. For example, the lone pair on nitrogen gives ammonia a pyramidal shape that is not symmetrical.

**Can a diatomic molecule of two different atoms be non-polar?**
Only if the atoms have the same electronegativity. A diatomic molecule with different atoms has one polar bond, so it is polar.
