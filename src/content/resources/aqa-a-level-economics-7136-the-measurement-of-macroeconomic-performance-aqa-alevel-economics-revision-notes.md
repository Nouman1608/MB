These notes condense **3.2.1 The measurement of macroeconomic performance** in AQA A-level Economics (7136).
For explanations and worked calculations, read the [study guide for this sub-section](/resources/aqa-a-level-economics-7136-the-measurement-of-macroeconomic-performance-aqa-alevel-economics/),
then use the [practice questions](/resources/aqa-a-level-economics-7136-the-measurement-of-macroeconomic-performance-aqa-alevel-economics-practice/).
All figures are invented.

## Objectives (3.2.1.1)

- **Main objectives:** economic growth; price stability; minimising unemployment; a stable balance of
  payments on current account.
- **Other objectives:** balancing the budget; an equitable distribution of income.
- Objectives can **conflict**, at least in the short run (for example, faster growth raising inflation or
  widening the current account deficit).
- The **importance attached** to each objective changes over time.

## Indicators (3.2.1.2)

| Indicator | What it measures | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| Real GDP | Output adjusted for inflation | Growth rate, not level, for "growth" |
| Real GDP per capita | Average output per person | Population growth can hide weak performance |
| CPI and RPI | Changes in the price level | Rate of inflation versus price level |
| Unemployment measures | People out of work and seeking work | Rate (%) versus number |
| Productivity | Output per worker or per hour | Key driver of long-run growth and living standards |
| Current account balance | Trade in goods and services plus income flows | Usually shown as % of GDP |

## Index numbers (3.2.1.3)

- **Index = (value in year ÷ value in base year) × 100.**
- **Weighted index** = Σ(weight × item index) ÷ Σ weights.
- **Percentage change between two years** = (later index − earlier index) ÷ earlier index × 100.
- **Rebasing** to year B: divide every value by the value in year B and multiply by 100.
- Underlying features of the CPI and RPI: a **basket of goods and services**, weights based on the spending of the
  **"average family"**, and a base year.

## Real and nominal

- **Real value = nominal value ÷ (price index ÷ 100).**
- Approximation: real growth ≈ nominal growth − inflation (exact only for small changes).
- **Per capita** = total ÷ population. Per capita growth ≈ growth of total − population growth.

## Unemployment and productivity

- **Unemployment rate** = unemployed ÷ labour force × 100.
- A change in a rate is given in **percentage points**; a proportional change in a number is in **per cent**.
- **Labour productivity** = output ÷ hours worked (or ÷ workers).

## The current account

| Component | Examples |
|---|---|
| Trade in goods | Exports and imports of cars, food, fuel |
| Trade in services | Tourism, finance, insurance, transport |
| Primary income | Investment income, profits and interest, wages earned abroad |
| Secondary income | Transfers: government aid, remittances |

Balance = sum of the four. A negative balance is a **current account deficit**.

## National income data (3.2.1.4)

- Used to compare living standards **over time** and **between countries**.
- **Limitations:** income distribution ignored; informal and unpaid work excluded; leisure, health and
  environment not measured; quality changes hard to capture; composition of output (health versus defence)
  ignored; data errors and revisions.
- **PPP exchange rates:** adjust for differences in the cost of living, so incomes are compared by what they
  can buy. Market exchange rates can be distorted by capital flows and speculation.

## Worked reminders (invented)

- Weights 50, 25, 25; indices 102, 110, 98: weighted index = (5,100 + 2,750 + 2,450) ÷ 100 = **103.0**.
- Index 125 to 130: change = 5 ÷ 125 = **4.0%**.
- Nominal GDP 800 to 880, price index 100 to 110: real GDP = 880 ÷ 1.10 = **800**, so real growth is **zero**.
- Unemployed 2.0 million of a 40 million labour force: **5.0%**.

## Must-know distinctions

- Nominal versus real.
- Total versus per capita.
- Price level versus rate of inflation; disinflation versus deflation.
- Percentage points versus per cent.
- Current account deficit versus budget deficit.
- Market exchange rate versus PPP exchange rate.

## Evaluation toolkit for data questions

- **Check the measure:** is the figure nominal or real, total or per capita, a level or a rate of change?
- **Check the period:** one year of data can mislead; look for a trend across several years.
- **Check the comparison:** compare with the government's target, with the previous year and with similar
  economies.
- **Check the reliability:** GDP and unemployment figures are estimates and are often revised; the informal economy
  is not fully captured.
- **Weigh several indicators together:** strong growth with rising inflation and a widening current account deficit
  may signal overheating rather than success.

## Quick self-test

1. State the four main objectives of macroeconomic policy.
2. Give two other objectives a government may pursue.
3. A price index rises from 140 to 147. Calculate the rate of inflation.
4. Rebase the series 90, 100, 108 so that the third year is 100.
5. Nominal GDP rises 7% and the price level rises 3%. Roughly what is real growth?
6. Real GDP rises 2% and population rises 2.5%. What has happened to real GDP per capita?
7. The unemployment rate rises from 4.2% to 5.0%. Express the change in two ways.
8. List the four components of the current account.
9. Why is a current account balance often expressed as a percentage of GDP?
10. Give three limitations of real GDP per capita as a measure of living standards.
11. Why are PPP exchange rates used for international comparisons?
12. Why might a weighted price index rise even though most items' prices fell?

### Answers

1. Economic growth, price stability, minimising unemployment and a stable balance of payments on current
   account.
2. Balancing the budget; an equitable distribution of income.
3. (147 − 140) ÷ 140 × 100 = **5.0%**.
4. 90 ÷ 108 × 100 = **83.3**; 100 ÷ 108 × 100 = **92.6**; **100**.
5. About **4%**.
6. It has **fallen**, by roughly 0.5%.
7. A rise of **0.8 percentage points**; the rate itself is about **19% higher** (0.8 ÷ 4.2).
8. Trade in goods, trade in services, primary income and secondary income.
9. To allow comparison across years and between countries of different sizes.
10. Any three: it ignores distribution, informal and unpaid work, leisure, health and the environment, and
    changes in quality.
11. They adjust for differences in the cost of living, so incomes are compared by their purchasing power.
12. The items whose prices rose may carry much larger weights than those whose prices fell.
