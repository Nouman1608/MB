This guide covers the content External influences on marketing in the area of study Marketing within the external environment of the OCR A Level Business (H431) specification, Version 2.1 (January 2026), final assessment summer 2027. It is numbered 3.13 on this site; the specification does not number its content. The [marketing and marketing strategies study guide](/resources/ocr-a-level-business-marketing-and-marketing-strategies/) gives the overview of the whole topic, and the [external influences study guide](/resources/ocr-a-level-business-external-influences/) covers the wider course topic of external influences facing businesses. This page goes further: it sets out how each of the eight influences named in the specification affects the marketing mix, works three calculations (recession and income elasticity, exchange rate, and the cost of a new law), and shows how to rank influences by likelihood and impact. All businesses and figures are invented.

## What the specification asks for

The specification asks learners to evaluate the following influences on marketing: social, legal, ethical, environmental, economic, political, technological and international.

| Influence | Covered below |
|---|---|
| Social | Eight influences |
| Legal | Eight influences; Worked example 3 |
| Ethical | Eight influences |
| Environmental | Eight influences; Worked example 3 |
| Economic | Worked example 1 |
| Political | Eight influences |
| Technological | Eight influences |
| International | Worked example 2 |

"Evaluate" means weighing the size of the effect on the business, and on its mix, against what it can do in response.

## The eight influences

| Influence | What it covers | Possible effects on marketing |
|---|---|---|
| **Social** | Population size and age, lifestyles, tastes, attitudes, fashion | A rise in the number of older people opens a market for mobility products; a shift to plant-based eating changes products and promotion |
| **Legal** | Consumer protection, advertising rules, data protection, product safety, labelling | Limits on claims in advertising; consent needed to email customers; costs of safety testing; fines for breaking rules |
| **Ethical** | Moral standards: honesty in advertising, treatment of workers and suppliers, marketing to children | Customers may reject brands seen as unfair; ethical sourcing can become a selling point; but it can raise costs |
| **Environmental** | Climate, resource use, pollution, waste, packaging | Demand for recyclable packaging and low-carbon products; carbon-reduction costs; "greenwashing" risk |
| **Economic** | Growth, income, unemployment, inflation, interest rates, exchange rates | Recession cuts spending on luxuries; inflation raises costs; interest rates affect borrowing and demand for big purchases |
| **Political** | Government policy, taxes, regulation, trade policy, stability | Sugar tax changes drinks pricing; trade barriers affect exports; political change can alter demand for public-sector suppliers |
| **Technological** | New technology and how it is used | E-commerce, social media, data analytics, mobile payments; new distribution; faster product development; risk from rivals' innovation |
| **International** | Overseas markets, competition, trade agreements, cultures | Export opportunities, cheaper imports from overseas rivals, differences in language and law |

### Worked example 1: a recession and income elasticity

Cavell Home Goods sells premium and budget kitchenware. Next year consumers' incomes are forecast to fall by **3%**. Last year's sales were **£2.0 million** of premium goods and **£1.5 million** of budget goods. The income elasticity of demand (YED) is **+1.8** for premium goods and **−0.4** for budget goods.

1. Premium: % change in demand = YED × % change in income = 1.8 × (−3%) = **−5.4%**. New sales = £2.0m × (1 − 0.054) = **£1.892m**.
2. Budget: −0.4 × (−3%) = **+1.2%**. New sales = £1.5m × 1.012 = **£1.518m**.
3. Total = £1.892m + £1.518m = **£3.410m**, against £3.5m before, a fall of £90,000 (2.6%).

**Interpretation.** The recession hurts premium sales but the inferior good, budget kitchenware, partly offsets the fall. Cavell's marketing response could be to promote value, introduce a mid-priced range and offer instalment payment, while keeping the premium brand. The forecast depends on the size of the income fall and the accuracy of the YED, so the business should monitor sales monthly.

### Worked example 2: an exchange rate

Cavell exports a pan priced at **£50** to the United States. At an exchange rate of **$1.25 to the pound** it sells for $62.50. The pound strengthens to **$1.40**.

1. New dollar price = £50 × 1.40 = **$70.00**.
2. Increase = (70 − 62.50) ÷ 62.50 × 100 = **12%**.
3. If the price elasticity of demand for the pan in the US is −1.5, the change in quantity = −1.5 × 12% = **−18%**.

A stronger pound makes exports dearer for overseas buyers, and sales fall by about a fifth unless Cavell cuts its sterling price. It could absorb the change by reducing its price, hedge against exchange rate changes, or build a factory or partner in the US. Equally, a weaker pound would make Cavell's exports cheaper but would raise the cost of imported materials. The international and economic influences are therefore linked.

### Worked example 3: the cost of a new law

A law requires recyclable packaging. Teague Snacks sells **2,000,000** bags a year at **£4.00** each, with a variable cost of **£2.60** (a margin of 35%). The new packaging adds **£0.06** to each bag.

- Contribution before = 2,000,000 × (£4.00 − £2.60) = **£2,800,000**.
- **Option A: absorb the cost.** New cost £2.66; contribution per bag = £1.34; total = **£2,680,000**, a fall of £120,000.
- **Option B: raise the price to £4.06** (a rise of 1.5%). If price elasticity of demand is −1.2, sales fall by 1.8% to 1,964,000 bags. Contribution per bag = £4.06 − £2.66 = £1.40. Total = 1,964,000 × £1.40 = **£2,749,600**.

Passing on the cost is better (£2,749,600 against £2,680,000) while demand is not too elastic. If price elasticity were −3.0, the sales fall would be 4.5% (1,910,000 bags), giving a total of 1,910,000 × £1.40 = £2,674,000, slightly worse than absorbing. The best response depends on how customers react. Teague can also promote the change as a benefit ("now recyclable") to turn a legal duty into a marketing message, which links the legal and environmental influences.

## Ranking influences: likelihood and impact

Not all influences matter equally. A simple method scores each from 1 to 5 for **likelihood** and **impact** and multiplies. Suppose a snack business scores as follows.

| Influence | Likelihood | Impact | Score |
|---|---|---|---|
| Technological | 5 | 4 | **20** |
| Economic | 4 | 4 | **16** |
| Social | 4 | 3 | **12** |
| Legal | 3 | 4 | **12** |
| Environmental | 3 | 3 | **9** |
| Ethical | 2 | 4 | **8** |
| Political | 2 | 3 | **6** |
| International | 3 | 2 | **6** |

Technological change (20) and economic conditions (16) matter most for this business, so its marketing planning should concentrate there. The scores are judgements, but writing them down makes the reasoning clear. What matters in an exam is to say **which** influence matters most for **this** business and why.

## Evaluating the effects

When evaluating, consider:

- **Size:** how many customers or sales are affected?
- **Time:** is the effect immediate (a new law) or gradual (an ageing population)?
- **Control:** can the business influence it (an ethical policy) or only respond (a recession)?
- **Opportunity or threat:** the same change can be both, such as online shopping, which opens new markets and threatens shops.
- **Response:** the cost and speed of changing the mix.
- **Stakeholders:** customers, employees, suppliers, shareholders and communities may be affected differently.

A business that scans its environment regularly, using a framework such as PESTLE, is better prepared than one that reacts after the event.

## Responding to external change

A business cannot control the external environment, but it can prepare for it.

- **Scan regularly:** review the eight influences each quarter, using government statistics, trade reports and customer feedback.
- **Plan for scenarios:** for example, a forecast for a 3% fall in incomes and another for a 2% rise, so that the marketing budget can be adjusted quickly.
- **Build flexibility:** a business that can change a price, a pack size or an advertising message within weeks copes better than one tied to a long contract.
- **Turn threats into messages:** a new rule, a change in tastes or a rival's mistake can become a reason for customers to choose the business.
- **Keep the brand consistent:** a response that damages the brand, such as heavy discounting of a luxury product in a recession, can cause more harm than the original change.

Small businesses have fewer resources for scanning, but they are often closer to customers and quicker to adapt.

## Common mistakes

- Listing the eight influences without saying which matter most to the business in the case.
- Treating each influence as only a threat.
- Giving effects on the business in general instead of on the marketing mix.
- Ignoring links between influences, such as the exchange rate (economic) affecting exports (international).
- Assuming a business cannot respond; most can adapt the mix.

## Exam technique

Choose the influences that fit the case, explain the effect on one or more of the four Ps, use figures if given, and finish with a judgement about which matters most and why.
