This guide covers **Enforcement** in section 3.5 Human Rights of AQA A-level Law (7162). The
specification lists "Claims before the European Court of Human Rights; the role of domestic courts; the
effect of decisions on states and claimants" and "The process of judicial review". For the option as a
whole, see the [Human Rights study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-law-human-rights-study-guide/).

## Two levels of enforcement

A person whose Convention rights have been infringed now starts in the **domestic courts**, using the
Human Rights Act 1998. Only after those routes are exhausted can they apply to the **European Court of
Human Rights** in Strasbourg. This order reflects the principle of **subsidiarity**: states have the
primary responsibility to secure rights, and the Court is a supervisory safety net.

## Claims before the European Court of Human Rights

### The Court

The Court is an institution of the **Council of Europe**, not the European Union. Since Protocol 11 came
into force in 1998 it has been a single, full-time court; the former European Commission of Human Rights
was abolished. There is one judge for each state party. Cases are decided by a **single judge** (who can
only declare clearly inadmissible applications inadmissible), a **committee of three**, a **Chamber of
seven** or, for the most serious issues, a **Grand Chamber of seventeen**.

### Who may apply

- **Individual applications (Article 34)**: any person, non-governmental organisation or group of
  individuals claiming to be a **victim** of a violation by a state party. The person must be directly
  affected; there is no general right to challenge a law in the abstract.
- **Inter-state cases (Article 33)**: one state may bring a case against another, as in **Ireland v
  United Kingdom (1978)** about interrogation techniques in Northern Ireland. These are rare.

### Admissibility (Article 35)

Most applications fail at this stage. The main criteria are:

- **Exhaustion of domestic remedies**: the applicant must first use every effective remedy in national
  law, which in the UK now includes a Human Rights Act claim.
- **Time limit**: the application must be made within **four months** of the final domestic decision.
  Protocol 15 reduced the limit from six months; the new limit has applied since 2022.
- The application must not be anonymous or substantially the same as one already examined.
- It must not be **manifestly ill-founded** or an abuse of the right of application.
- Under Protocol 14, the applicant must have suffered a **significant disadvantage**, unless respect for
  human rights requires the case to be examined.

In urgent cases the Court can indicate **interim measures** under Rule 39 of its Rules of Court, for
example asking a state not to deport someone while the case is pending.

### Judgments

If the Court finds a violation, it may award **just satisfaction** under Article 41, which can include
compensation and costs. A Chamber judgment can be referred to the Grand Chamber within three months in
exceptional cases (Article 43). The Court cannot quash a national law or overturn a domestic decision.

## The effect of decisions on states and claimants

Under **Article 46** states undertake to abide by final judgments in cases to which they are parties.
The **Committee of Ministers** supervises execution. It checks two kinds of measure:

- **Individual measures** for the claimant, such as paying just satisfaction or reopening a case.
- **General measures** to prevent repetition, usually a change in law or practice.

The UK record shows how judgments change domestic law:

| Judgment | Response |
|---|---|
| Sunday Times v UK (1979) | Contempt of Court Act 1981 |
| Malone v UK (1984) | Interception of Communications Act 1985 |
| Goodwin v UK (2002) | Gender Recognition Act 2004 |
| S and Marper v UK (2008) | Protection of Freedoms Act 2012 |

Execution can be slow and political. After **Hirst v United Kingdom (No 2) (2005)** found the blanket ban
on prisoners voting incompatible with Article 3 of Protocol 1, the UK delayed for over a decade before
making limited administrative changes, which the Committee of Ministers accepted. Since Protocol 14, the
Committee may also bring **infringement proceedings** before the Court against a state that refuses to
comply.

For the claimant, a judgment gives a formal finding of violation and possibly compensation, but it comes
years after the events and cannot itself undo the domestic outcome.

## The role of domestic courts

The Human Rights Act 1998 makes the domestic courts the first and main forum.

- **Section 6**: it is unlawful for a **public authority** to act incompatibly with Convention rights.
  This covers **core** public authorities, such as the police and councils, and **hybrid** bodies when
  performing functions of a public nature. In **YL v Birmingham City Council (2007)** a private care home
  was held not to be performing a public function; Parliament reversed this for publicly arranged care in
  section 73 of the Care Act 2014.
- **Section 7**: only a **victim**, in the same sense as Article 34, may bring proceedings, normally
  within **one year** of the act complained of (section 7(5)). A victim can also rely on Convention
  rights in other proceedings, for example as a defence.
- **Section 8**: the court may grant any just and appropriate remedy within its powers. Damages are
  awarded only where necessary for just satisfaction; in **R (Greenfield) v Secretary of State for the
  Home Department (2005)** the House of Lords held that courts should be guided by Strasbourg practice,
  and often a finding of violation is enough.
- **Section 2**: courts must take into account Strasbourg case law. In **R (Ullah) v Special Adjudicator
  (2004)** Lord Bingham said domestic courts should keep pace with Strasbourg, "no more, but certainly no
  less". In **R v Horncastle (2009)** the Supreme Court declined to follow a Chamber decision on hearsay
  evidence, and the Grand Chamber later took account of the Supreme Court's reasoning, an example of
  judicial dialogue.
- **Sections 3 and 4**: interpretation and declarations of incompatibility, covered in the
  [Human Rights study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-law-human-rights-study-guide/).

## The process of judicial review

Judicial review is the main domestic procedure for challenging the **lawfulness** of a decision by a
public body, including on human rights grounds. It looks at how a decision was made, not whether it was
the best decision.

1. **Pre-action letter**: the claimant normally writes to the public body under the pre-action protocol,
   setting out the challenge.
2. **Claim form**: filed in the **Administrative Court** under Part 54 of the Civil Procedure Rules,
   **promptly and in any event within three months** of the grounds arising.
3. **Standing**: the claimant must have a **sufficient interest** (Senior Courts Act 1981, section 31(3)).
   For a claim relying on the Human Rights Act, the claimant must be a **victim** (section 7(3)).
4. **Permission**: a judge decides on the papers whether there is an **arguable case**. Many claims end
   here.
5. **Full hearing**: the court considers the grounds. Lord Diplock in **Council of Civil Service Unions v
   Minister for the Civil Service (1985)** classified them as **illegality**, **irrationality** and
   **procedural impropriety**. Where Convention rights are engaged the court applies **proportionality**,
   which **R (Daly) v Secretary of State for the Home Department (2001)** held is a more intense standard
   of review than irrationality.
6. **Remedies**, which are discretionary: a **quashing order**, a **prohibiting order**, a **mandatory
   order**, a **declaration**, an **injunction** and, in limited cases, damages. The court must normally
   refuse relief if it is **highly likely** that the outcome would not have been substantially different
   without the conduct complained of (section 31(2A) of the Senior Courts Act 1981).

## Worked example

*Facts: A prison governor stops Jo, a prisoner, sending letters to a newspaper about her conviction. Jo
wants to challenge the decision.*

```
Forum:      Domestic first: judicial review in the Administrative Court,
            relying on Article 10 and the HRA.
Standing:   Jo is directly affected, so she is a victim (s7(3) HRA).
Time:       Pre-action letter, then claim form promptly and within three
            months; HRA one-year limit also met.
Grounds:    Illegality (s6 HRA: acting incompatibly) and proportionality
            (Daly), not just irrationality.
Remedy:     Quashing order and declaration; damages only if needed (s8,
            Greenfield).
If she loses at the final domestic level: application to Strasbourg within
four months.
```

## Common mistakes

- Sending a claimant straight to Strasbourg without exhausting domestic remedies.
- Using the old six-month time limit.
- Saying the European Court can strike down UK legislation or overturn a UK judgment.
- Confusing the one-year HRA limit with the three-month judicial review limit.
- Describing judicial review as an appeal on the merits.

## Exam technique

- **Explain**: set out each stage in order with the relevant section, article or time limit.
- **Advise**: identify the public authority, the victim, the forum, the time limits, the grounds and the
  remedy.
- **Discuss**: evaluate effectiveness, for example the Strasbourg backlog, slow execution in Hirst, the
  permission filter in judicial review and the limited damages under section 8.
