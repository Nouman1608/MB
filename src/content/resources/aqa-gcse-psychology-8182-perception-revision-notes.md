Condensed recall for **section 3.1.2 Perception** of Paper 1. For full explanations and worked answers, read the [Perception study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-psychology-8182-perception/) first; then test yourself with the [Perception practice questions](/resources/aqa-gcse-psychology-8182-perception-practice/).

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Sensation | Detection of physical energy (light, sound) by the sense organs |
| Perception | The brain's interpretation of sensory information so it has meaning |
| Monocular cue | Depth cue that works with one eye |
| Binocular cue | Depth cue that needs both eyes |
| Retinal disparity | The difference between the two eyes' images; larger for near objects |
| Convergence | The eyes turning inwards to focus on near objects |
| Motion parallax | When moving, near objects pass quickly and far objects slowly |
| Size constancy | Seeing an object as the same size even when its retinal image changes |
| Bottom-up | Perception driven by the sensory information itself (Gibson) |
| Top-down | Perception driven by knowledge and expectation (Gregory) |
| Perceptual set | A readiness to perceive things in a particular way |
| Fiction | Perceiving something that is not there, such as an edge |
| Ambiguity | One image can be interpreted in more than one way |

## Depth cues

- **Monocular (4):** height in plane, relative size, occlusion, linear perspective.
- **Binocular (2):** retinal disparity, convergence.

**Memory hook:** "bi" = two eyes, and both binocular cues compare or move the two eyes.

## Illusions: name it, explain it

| Illusion | Explanation |
|---|---|
| Rubin's vase | Ambiguity (vase or faces) |
| Necker cube | Ambiguity (front face flips) |
| Kanizsa triangle | Fiction (edges filled in) |
| Ponzo | Misinterpreted depth cues + size constancy |
| Müller-Lyer | Misinterpreted depth cues + size constancy (fins read as corners) |
| Ames Room | Misinterpreted depth cues (room assumed rectangular) |

## The two theories side by side

| | Gibson: direct theory | Gregory: constructivist theory |
|---|---|---|
| Direction | Bottom-up | Top-down |
| Nature or nurture | Nature (innate) | Nurture (learned) |
| Key idea | The optic array holds enough information; motion parallax gives distance | The brain makes a best guess from incomplete information using past experience |
| Strength | Explains fast, accurate perception in the real world | Explains illusions and perceptual set |
| Weakness | Cannot easily explain illusions | Does not explain why perception is usually so fast and accurate |

## Factors affecting perception

- **Expectation -- Bruner and Minturn:** an ambiguous B/13 figure was seen as **B** among letters and **13** among numbers. Shows context creates perceptual set.
- **Motivation -- Gilchrist and Nesberg:** participants deprived of food for longer judged food pictures as **brighter**. Shows need affects perception.
- **Emotion:** feelings such as anxiety can change what we notice.
- **Culture:** experience of the environment (for example, straight-edged buildings) can affect how strongly illusions such as Müller-Lyer work.

Both studies are controlled laboratory tasks: easy to replicate, but low in ecological validity.

## Exam traps

- Gibson = bottom-up and nature; Gregory = top-down and nurture.
- Retinal disparity and convergence are **binocular**.
- An illusion is a **perception** error, not a sensation error: the retinal image is accurate.
- Name the factor **and** the study that shows it.

## Self-test

1. Distinguish between sensation and perception.
2. Name **two** monocular depth cues.
3. Which illusion is explained by fiction?
4. Explain motion parallax using a car journey.
5. Why does Gibson's theory struggle with illusions?
6. What did Gilchrist and Nesberg's study show?
7. In Bruner and Minturn's study, what caused participants to see a B rather than 13?

**Answers:**
1. Sensation is detecting physical energy with the senses; perception is the brain's interpretation of that information.
2. Any two of height in plane, relative size, occlusion, linear perspective.
3. The Kanizsa triangle.
4. Lampposts at the roadside rush past, while distant mountains move slowly; the difference in speed tells the brain which is nearer.
5. If perception were direct and accurate, we should not be fooled; illusions suggest the brain is making inferences.
6. That motivation (hunger) affects perception: hungrier participants judged pictures of food to be brighter.
7. Seeing it among letters created an expectation (perceptual set) that it was a letter.

## Frequently asked questions

**What are the binocular depth cues in AQA GCSE Psychology?**
Retinal disparity, where each eye receives a slightly different image, and convergence, where the eyes turn inwards more to focus on near objects.

**Is Gregory's theory nature or nurture?**
Nurture. Gregory argued that perception uses inferences based on past experience, which is learned.
