These notes cover section 9.3 *The impact of the new media* of Cambridge International A Level
Sociology (9699) Paper 4. Full explanations are in the
[impact of the new media study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-sociology-9699-impact-of-the-new-media/);
exam-style questions are in the
[impact of the new media practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-sociology-9699-impact-of-the-new-media-practice/).

## The argument in one paragraph

New media speed up globalisation by carrying images, ideas and relationships across borders, but
access is unequal. They give activists and citizens new ways to challenge power, yet states and
platform companies use the same tools for surveillance and profit. Optimists and pessimists each
capture part of the picture; the new media are best seen as a contested space. Identities become
more performed and managed, and relationships more networked, with effects that depend on how
media are used and by whom.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Global village** | McLuhan's image of a world connected as closely as a village by electronic media |
| **Mediascapes** | Global flows of media images and stories (Appadurai, 1996) |
| **Digital divide** | Unequal access to and use of digital technology: global, social and democratic (Norris, 2001) |
| **Networked protest** | Movements coordinated through online networks (Castells, 2012) |
| **Digital labour** | Users' unpaid activity that generates platform profit (Fuchs, 2014) |
| **Slacktivism** | Low-effort online support replacing sustained action (Morozov) |
| **Echo chamber** | Space where people hear only like-minded views (Sunstein, 2001) |
| **Digital optimism / pessimism** | Belief that new media empower and democratise / that they harm, divide and control |
| **Presentation of self** | Managing impressions as a performance (Goffman, 1959) |
| **Context collapse** | Merging of separate audiences online (Marwick and boyd, 2011) |
| **Identity tourism** | Adopting another ethnic or gender identity online (Nakamura) |
| **Polymedia** | Choosing among many media to express different feelings (Madianou and Miller, 2012) |
| **Networked individualism** | Belonging to loose personal networks rather than bounded groups (Rainie and Wellman, 2012) |

## New media and globalisation

- Instant global communication: the global village.
- Mediascapes shape aspirations, tastes and politics.
- Global platforms carry global brands but also let local content travel: glocalisation.
- Diasporas maintain transnational ties.
- Cross-border campaigns link to global social movements (7.3).
- **Limit:** Norris's three divides mean globalisation through new media is uneven.

## Challenge to power?

| Challenge | Limit |
|---|---|
| WikiLeaks cables (2010); Snowden disclosures (2013) | States respond with prosecutions and more surveillance |
| Arab Spring, Occupy (Castells, 2012) | Many movements fail to build lasting organisations |
| #MeToo (2017) | Online pressure varies by country and by who is accused |
| Citizen journalism | Misinformation spreads as easily as evidence |
| Low-cost publishing for all | Platforms profit from users' digital labour (Fuchs) |

## Optimism versus pessimism

- **Optimists:** Shirky (2008) — easy group formation and collective action; wider access to
  information; marginalised voices heard.
- **Pessimists:** Sunstein (2001) — echo chambers; misinformation; surveillance by states and firms;
  digital divide; unequal participation.
- **Synthesis:** a contested space; outcomes depend on access, skills and power.

## Identities

- Goffman: online profiles as managed front stages.
- Context collapse makes managing multiple audiences harder.
- Postmodern view: experiment with and combine identities.
- Nakamura: identity tourism can reproduce stereotypes.
- Miller et al. (2016): use of social media varies by culture.

## Relationships

- Madianou and Miller (2012): polymedia sustains transnational families but can intensify conflict
  and monitoring.
- Rainie and Wellman (2012): networked individualism.
- Pessimists: shallower intimacy; cyberbullying follows people home.
- Evaluation: the same tool can deepen or replace relationships.

## Essay plan in five steps

*"The new media is a challenge to existing power structures." Evaluate.*

1. Name the power structures: states, corporations, traditional media, patriarchy.
2. For: networked protest (Castells, 2012); leaks (WikiLeaks, Snowden); #MeToo; citizen journalism.
3. Against: state surveillance and shutdowns; slacktivism (Morozov); short-lived movements.
4. Marxist twist: platforms profit from users' digital labour (Fuchs, 2014), so protest feeds
   corporate power.
5. Judge: new opportunities and new controls; outcomes depend on access and resources (Norris).

## Exam traps

- Social media helped coordinate the Arab Spring; do not claim they caused it.
- Always mention access: the digital divide qualifies every optimistic claim.
- Do not confuse 9.3 with 10.3 (media and behaviour).
- Avoid assuming identity effects are the same everywhere; cite comparative evidence.
- Name the specific power structure being challenged: states, corporations, traditional media,
  patriarchy.

## Self-test

1. What did McLuhan mean by the global village?
2. What are mediascapes?
3. Name Norris's three digital divides.
4. Give one example of new media exposing powerful institutions.
5. What did Castells (2012) argue about networked movements?
6. What is digital labour, and who used the term in this context?
7. Define slacktivism.
8. Give one optimist and one pessimist writer.
9. What is context collapse?
10. What is identity tourism?
11. What is polymedia?
12. Give one way new media may weaken interpersonal relationships.

### Answers

1. A world in which electronic media connect people as closely as members of a village.
2. Global flows of media images and stories that people use to imagine other lives (Appadurai).
3. Global, social and democratic.
4. Any one: WikiLeaks diplomatic cables (2010); Snowden's surveillance disclosures (2013);
   #MeToo (2017); citizen footage of abuses.
5. Online networks let people share outrage and coordinate protest outside traditional
   organisations.
6. Users' unpaid online activity, which generates data and attention that platforms sell; Fuchs
   (2014).
7. Low-effort online support, such as liking or sharing, that may replace sustained action.
8. Optimist: Shirky (2008). Pessimist: Sunstein (2001) or Morozov.
9. The merging online of audiences normally kept separate, making identity management harder.
10. Adopting another ethnic or gender identity online, which may reproduce stereotypes.
11. Choosing among many media to express different kinds of feeling in relationships.
12. Any one: constant connection replacing face-to-face contact; cyberbullying; monitoring and
    control by partners or parents.
