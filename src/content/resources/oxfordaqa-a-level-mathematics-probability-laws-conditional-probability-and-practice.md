## Questions

**1.** An integer is chosen at random from 1 to 40 inclusive. Event A is "the number is a multiple of 4" and event B is "the number is a square number".

**(a)** List the outcomes in A ∩ B and find P(A ∩ B). **[2]**
**(b)** Find P(A ∪ B). **[1]**
**(c)** Find P(A'). **[1]**

**2.** Events A and B are mutually exclusive, with P(A) = 0.52 and P(B) = 0.3.

**(a)** Find P(A ∪ B). **[1]**
**(b)** Find the probability that neither A nor B occurs. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain, with a calculation, why A and B are not independent. **[2]**

**3.** For events A and B, P(A) = 0.6, P(B) = 0.35 and P(A ∩ B) = 0.21.

**(a)** Show that A and B are independent. **[2]**
**(b)** Find P(A ∪ B). **[2]**

**4.** A school surveyed 200 pupils about how they travel to school.

| | Bus | Walk | Car |
|---|---|---|---|
| Year 10 | 38 | 29 | 36 |
| Year 11 | 42 | 21 | 34 |

A pupil is chosen at random from the 200.

**(a)** Find P(walks). **[1]**
**(b)** Find P(Year 11 and bus). **[1]**
**(c)** Find P(bus | Year 10). **[2]**
**(d)** Find P(Year 11 | car). **[2]**
**(e)** Determine whether "walks" and "Year 11" are independent. **[3]**

**5.** A and B are independent events with P(A) = 0.65 and P(A ∪ B) = 0.93. Find P(B). **[4]**

**6.** A drawer holds 6 black socks and 4 white socks. Three socks are taken at random without replacement.

**(a)** Find the probability that all three are black. **[2]**
**(b)** Find the probability that at least one is white. **[2]**
**(c)** Find the probability that the first two socks taken are the same colour. **[3]**

**7.** A parcel passes through three independent stages. The probabilities that each stage is on time are 0.9, 0.85 and 0.8 respectively.

**(a)** Find the probability that all three stages are on time. **[2]**
**(b)** Find the probability that at least one stage is late. **[1]**
**(c)** Find the probability that exactly one stage is late. **[3]**

**8.** For events A and B, P(A) = 0.45, P(B) = 0.5 and P(A | B) = 0.4.

**(a)** Find P(A ∩ B). **[1]**
**(b)** Find P(A ∪ B). **[2]**
**(c)** Find P(B | A), giving your answer as a fraction. **[2]**

**9.** A factory has two machines. Machine X makes 60% of the items and machine Y makes 40%. Of the items from X, 3% are defective. Of the items from Y, 5% are defective. One item is chosen at random.

**(a)** Find the probability that it comes from Y and is defective. **[1]**
**(b)** Find the probability that it is defective. **[2]**
**(c)** Given that the item is defective, find the probability that it came from X. **[3]**

**10.** P(A) = 0.3 and P(B) = 0.4.

**(a)** Find P(A ∪ B) if A and B are mutually exclusive. **[1]**
**(b)** Find P(A ∪ B) if A and B are independent. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why two events with non-zero probabilities cannot be both mutually exclusive and independent. **[2]**

**11.** In a group of 80 people at a leisure centre, 45 use the pool, 38 use the sauna and 15 use neither.

**(a)** Find how many people use both. **[2]**
**(b)** One person who uses the sauna is chosen at random. Find the probability that they also use the pool. **[2]**
**(c)** Determine whether using the pool and using the sauna are independent. **[3]**
**(d)** Two different people are chosen at random from the 80. Find the probability that neither uses the pool or the sauna. **[3]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** A ∩ B = {4, 16, 36} [1], so P(A ∩ B) = 3/40 [1]
**(b)** A has 10 outcomes and B = {1, 4, 9, 16, 25, 36} has 6, so P(A ∪ B) = (10 + 6 − 3)/40 = **13/40** [1]
**(c)** P(A') = 1 − 10/40 = **3/4** [1]
*Examiner insight:* Listing the outcomes in the overlap shows the count behind your fraction, which makes it much easier to see where a slip occurred.

**2. (a)** P(A ∪ B) = 0.52 + 0.3 = **0.82** [1]
**(b)** 1 − 0.82 = **0.18** [1]
**(c)** Mutually exclusive means P(A ∩ B) = 0 [1], but P(A) × P(B) = 0.52 × 0.3 = 0.156, which is not 0, so the events are not independent [1]
*Examiner insight:* An explanation that quotes the independence condition and evaluates both sides is stronger than the bare statement that exclusive events cannot be independent.

**3. (a)** P(A) × P(B) = 0.6 × 0.35 = 0.21 [1], which equals P(A ∩ B), so the events are independent [1]
**(b)** P(A ∪ B) = 0.6 + 0.35 − 0.21 [1] = **0.74** [1]
*Examiner insight:* On a "show that" question, state the comparison and the conclusion in words as well as showing the arithmetic.

**4. (a)** 29 + 21 = 50 walk, so P(walks) = 50/200 = **1/4** [1]
**(b)** P(Year 11 and bus) = 42/200 = **21/100** [1]
**(c)** There are 38 + 29 + 36 = 103 Year 10 pupils [1], so P(bus | Year 10) = **38/103** [1]
**(d)** There are 36 + 34 = 70 car users [1], so P(Year 11 | car) = 34/70 = **17/35** [1]
**(e)** P(Year 11) = 97/200 and P(walks) = 1/4, so the product is 97/800 = 0.12125 [1]. P(walks and Year 11) = 21/200 = 0.105 [1]. These are not equal, so the events are **not independent** [1]
*Examiner insight:* In part (c) and part (d), the denominator is the total for the condition, so state that total before dividing, because a wrong denominator is the most common slip.

**5.** Let P(B) = p. Independence gives P(A ∩ B) = 0.65p [1]. Then 0.93 = 0.65 + p − 0.65p [1], so 0.28 = 0.35p [1], giving P(B) = **0.8** [1]
*Examiner insight:* Setting up the equation with a named unknown shows method clearly; substituting back to check that 0.65 + 0.8 − 0.52 = 0.93 guards against arithmetic slips.

**6. (a)** 6/10 × 5/9 × 4/8 [1] = 120/720 = **1/6** [1]
**(b)** P(at least one white) = 1 − P(all black) [1] = 1 − 1/6 = **5/6** [1]
**(c)** Both black: 6/10 × 5/9 = 30/90 [1]. Both white: 4/10 × 3/9 = 12/90 [1]. Total = 42/90 = **7/15** [1]
*Examiner insight:* Without replacement, show both the reduced numerator and the reduced denominator on the second and third picks, so that your method is easy for a reader to follow.

**7. (a)** 0.9 × 0.85 × 0.8 [1] = **0.612** [1]
**(b)** 1 − 0.612 = **0.388** [1]
**(c)** Stage 1 late only: 0.1 × 0.85 × 0.8 = 0.068; stage 2 late only: 0.9 × 0.15 × 0.8 = 0.108; stage 3 late only: 0.9 × 0.85 × 0.2 = 0.153 [2]. Sum = **0.329** [1]
*Examiner insight:* For "exactly one", list the three separate cases before adding them; a single product with no cases shown is easy to get wrong and hard to check.

**8. (a)** P(A ∩ B) = P(B) × P(A | B) = 0.5 × 0.4 = **0.2** [1]
**(b)** P(A ∪ B) = 0.45 + 0.5 − 0.2 [1] = **0.75** [1]
**(c)** P(B | A) = 0.2 / 0.45 [1] = **4/9** [1]
*Examiner insight:* Part (c) needs the other conditional probability, so quote P(A ∩ B) / P(A) and check that you have divided by P(A), not P(B).

**9. (a)** 0.4 × 0.05 = **0.02** [1]
**(b)** P(X and defective) = 0.6 × 0.03 = 0.018 [1]. P(defective) = 0.018 + 0.02 = **0.038** [1]
**(c)** P(X | defective) = P(X and defective) / P(defective) [1] = 0.018 / 0.038 [1] = **9/19** (about 0.474) [1]
*Examiner insight:* Write the conditional probability as a fraction of two named probabilities before you substitute; the denominator is the total from part (b), not the whole population.

**10. (a)** P(A ∪ B) = 0.3 + 0.4 = **0.7** [1]
**(b)** P(A ∩ B) = 0.3 × 0.4 = 0.12 [1], so P(A ∪ B) = 0.3 + 0.4 − 0.12 = **0.58** [1]
**(c)** If they are mutually exclusive, P(A ∩ B) = 0 [1]. If they are also independent, P(A ∩ B) = P(A) × P(B), which is non-zero when both probabilities are non-zero, so the two statements contradict each other [1]
*Examiner insight:* A good explanation uses the two defining equations side by side; a general comment about "one affecting the other" is less precise.

**11. (a)** 80 − 15 = 65 use the pool or the sauna [1]. Using both: 45 + 38 − 65 = **18** [1]
**(b)** P(pool | sauna) = 18/38 [1] = **9/19** [1]
**(c)** P(pool) × P(sauna) = 45/80 × 38/80 = 0.2672 (4 d.p.) [1]. P(pool and sauna) = 18/80 = 0.225 [1]. These differ, so the events are **not independent** [1]
**(d)** P(neither) = 15/80 × 14/79 [1], with the second fraction reduced because the first person is not replaced [1]. This gives 210/6320 = **21/632** [1]
*Examiner insight:* For a multi-part problem, carry exact values from earlier parts into later ones, and say which earlier result you are using.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using the wrong total in a conditional probability, especially "given" questions on a table.
- Adding P(A) and P(B) for events that overlap, without subtracting P(A ∩ B).
- Declaring independence or dependence with no numerical comparison.
- Mixing up mutually exclusive with independent.
- Not reducing the denominator on later picks when there is no replacement.
- Missing cases in "exactly one" questions, or forgetting to add them.
- Calculating P(A | B) when the question asked for P(B | A).
- Rounding partway through, which can change the final answer.
- Stopping at the intersection or union without answering the actual question.
