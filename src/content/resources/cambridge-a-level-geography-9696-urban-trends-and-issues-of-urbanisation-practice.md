## Section A

**1.** Distinguish between urban growth and urbanisation. **[2]**

**2.** Define the term "re-urbanisation". **[2]**

**3.** Give **two** differences between a greenfield site and a brownfield site. **[2]**

**4.** The table shows population data for the fictional country of Talmara.

| Year | Total population (millions) | Urban population (millions) |
|---|---|---|
| 1990 | 25 | 5 |
| 2010 | 40 | 14 |
| 2030 (projected) | 55 | 27.5 |

**(a)** Calculate the percentage of the population that was urban in 1990 and in 2030. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the change in the rural population between 1990 and 2030. **[2]**

**(c)** Suggest **three** reasons for the change in Talmara's urban population. **[3]**

**5.** The table shows population change in three zones of the fictional city region of Brennam, a high income country.

| Zone | Change 1971-1991 (%) | Change 1991-2021 (%) |
|---|---|---|
| City centre | −22 | +65 |
| Suburbs | +8 | +4 |
| Small towns and villages beyond the city | +31 | +6 |

**(a)** Identify the process occurring in the city centre between 1991 and 2021. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain the changes shown for the small towns and villages. **[4]**

**6.** Explain **two** reasons why world cities attract the headquarters of transnational corporations. **[4]**

## Section B

**7.** Explain the consequences of rapid urbanisation for cities in low and middle income countries. **[6]**

**8.** Explain how green belt policies affect competition for land in and around cities. **[4]**

**9.** Explain how a hierarchy of world cities develops. **[5]**

**10.** "Urban renewal schemes benefit investors more than local residents." With reference to examples, how far do you agree? **[15]**

## Answers

**1.** Urban growth is an increase in the number of people living in towns and cities [1]. Urbanisation is an increase in the proportion of the total population living in urban areas [1]. **[2]**

**2.** The return of people, investment and economic activity [1] to city centres or inner areas after a period of decline [1]. **[2]**

**3.** A greenfield site has not been built on before, usually farmland on the urban edge, whereas a brownfield site has been built on before, often old industrial land within the city [1]. Greenfield sites are usually cheaper to develop, whereas brownfield sites may need demolition or decontamination but already have roads and services [1]. **[2]**

**4. (a)** 1990: 5 ÷ 25 × 100 = 20% [1]. 2030: 27.5 ÷ 55 × 100 = 50% [1].

**(b)** Rural population 1990 = 25 − 5 = 20 million; 2030 = 55 − 27.5 = 27.5 million [1]. Change = an increase of 7.5 million [1].

**(c)** Rural-urban migration in search of jobs and services [1]. Natural increase in the young urban population [1]. Reclassification of growing villages as towns, or extension of city boundaries [1]. **[7]**

*Examiner insight:* (b) shows that urbanisation can happen while the rural population still grows.

**5. (a)** Re-urbanisation [1].

**(b)** Between 1971 and 1991 they grew fastest (+31%) through counterurbanisation [1], as car ownership and better roads let people live further from work [1]. Cheaper housing and a perceived better environment attracted families and retirees [1]. Growth slowed to +6% after 1991 as people and investment returned to the city centre, and planning restricted building in the countryside [1]. **[5]**

**6.** Corporations want to be near producer services such as banks, lawyers and accountants [1], which cluster in world cities, so face-to-face contact is easy [1]. World cities have major airports and digital links [1], giving executives quick access to other markets and offices worldwide [1]. **[4]**

*Examiner insight:* also credit access to a large pool of skilled workers, or the prestige of a world city address, if developed.

**7.** Housing supply cannot keep pace, so informal settlements grow [1], often on hazardous land such as floodplains and steep slopes [1]. Many people work in the informal sector with low, insecure incomes [1]. Water supply, sanitation and waste collection are overloaded, raising disease risk [1]. Traffic congestion and air pollution increase [1]. On the positive side, concentration allows services and jobs to be provided more efficiently, and incomes are often higher than in rural areas [1]. **[6]**

**8.** Green belts restrict building on open land around cities [1]. This protects farmland and countryside from sprawl [1]. Land inside the boundary becomes scarcer, so land and house prices rise and brownfield sites are used more intensively [1]. Development may leapfrog beyond the green belt, lengthening commutes [1]. **[4]**

**9.** Some cities gain command functions such as TNC headquarters and stock exchanges [1]. Producer-service firms cluster there to serve global clients [1]. Success attracts skilled workers and investment, which reinforces growth (cumulative causation) [1]. Cities with fewer of these functions serve regional rather than global markets, placing them lower in the hierarchy [1]. The hierarchy changes as global investment shifts, for example with the rise of Asian cities [1]. **[5]**

**10.** The real exam marks extended answers with levels of response, not point by point. The points below are indicative creditworthy content.

*Introduction:* defines urban renewal [1] and identifies the groups involved: investors, local residents and newcomers [1].

*Agree:* property-led schemes raise land values, giving large returns to developers [1]. Luxury housing and offices may be unaffordable to existing residents [1]. Gentrification can displace lower-income households and small businesses [1]. Small traders displaced by the Cheonggyecheon project in Seoul show local losers [1].

*Disagree:* renewal can improve housing quality and public spaces for residents [1]. New jobs in construction, retail and services may go to local people [1]. Environmental improvements, such as Seoul's restored stream, benefit everyone [1]. Partnership schemes with community involvement can include affordable housing [1].

*Variation:* outcomes depend on the type of scheme [1] and on whether local people were consulted and protected [1].

*Use of examples:* at least one named scheme with specific detail [1].

*Judgement:* a reasoned view on the balance of benefits [1], which may vary between schemes [1]. **[15]**

*Examiner insight:* distinguish between residents who stay and benefit and those who are displaced; "local residents" are not one group.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calculating urban growth when the question asks for urbanisation, or the reverse.
- Forgetting to subtract urban from total population to find the rural figure.
- Naming a process without explaining its causes.
- Treating world cities as simply the largest cities.
- Essays on renewal with no named scheme.
