This guide covers section 12.1 of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Geography (9696) syllabus for examination in 2025 and 2026. The syllabus lists "renewable and non-renewable energy resources"; "factors at the national scale affecting demand for and supply of energy and the balance between different sources (including sustainability, levels of development, resource endowment, climate, income, technology, pollution, energy policy and energy security)"; "trends in the consumption of fossil fuels, nuclear power and renewables (hydroelectric power (HEP), wind, biofuels) in LICs, MICs and HICs"; and "the environmental impacts of energy production, transport and usage at local and global scales". It is part of Topic 12, Environmental management, in Paper 4.

The [Topic 12 study guide](/resources/a-level-geography-9696-environmental-management/) defines the key terms and lists each factor. This page adds the tools you need for data questions (capacity, generation and capacity factor), models for explaining change (the energy ladder and the energy trilemma), and a closer look at each source. Condense it with the [12.1 revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-sustainable-energy-supplies-revision-notes/) and test it with the [12.1 practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-sustainable-energy-supplies-practice/).

## What 12.1 asks you to know

| Syllabus wording | What it means in practice |
|---|---|
| Renewable and non-renewable resources | Classify sources and explain why some "renewables" can be used unsustainably |
| National-scale factors (nine named) | Explain why demand, supply and the energy mix differ between countries |
| Consumption trends by income group | Describe and explain changes in fossil fuels, nuclear, HEP, wind and biofuels |
| Environmental impacts at local and global scales | Effects of producing, moving and using energy |

## Classifying energy resources more precisely

- **Non-renewable (stock):** coal, oil, natural gas, uranium. Finite on human timescales.
- **Renewable flow resources:** wind, solar, HEP, tidal, wave, geothermal. They cannot be used up, though their availability varies.
- **Renewable but depletable:** fuelwood, charcoal and biofuels. They regrow, but only if harvesting does not exceed regrowth.
- **Primary energy** is the raw source (coal, wind); **secondary energy** is a converted form, especially electricity. A country's electricity mix is not the same as its total energy mix, because transport and heating often use oil and gas directly.

## Reading energy data

| Measure | Unit | What it shows |
|---|---|---|
| Installed capacity | MW or GW | The maximum power a plant can produce at one moment |
| Generation | MWh, GWh or TWh | The electricity actually produced over a period |
| Capacity factor | % | Generation ÷ (capacity × hours in the period) |
| Energy per person | GJ or kWh per person per year | Consumption standard; linked to development |
| Energy intensity | Energy per unit of GDP | How efficiently an economy uses energy |

### Worked example

*A fictional 2 000 MW wind farm generates 6 132 GWh in a year. A fictional 1 200 MW nuclear plant runs at a capacity factor of 90%.*

- Wind farm maximum possible output = 2 000 MW × 8 760 hours = 17 520 000 MWh (17 520 GWh).
- Capacity factor = 6 132 ÷ 17 520 × 100 = **35%**.
- Nuclear plant generation = 1 200 MW × 8 760 × 0.90 = 9 460 800 MWh, about **9.5 TWh**.

So the nuclear plant, with less capacity, produces more electricity. Intermittent sources need more installed capacity, storage or back-up to supply the same amount, which is a central issue in changing the energy mix.

## Explaining national differences

### Demand-side factors

- **Level of development and income:** demand per person rises with industrialisation, urbanisation, car ownership and appliances. In HICs, demand per person can level off or fall as economies shift to services and efficiency improves.
- **Climate:** heating demand in cold winters; cooling demand in hot, humid climates, which is growing fast in many tropical MICs.
- **Technology:** efficient lighting, insulation and motors reduce demand; new uses such as electric vehicles raise electricity demand.

### Supply-side factors

- **Resource endowment:** coal-rich, oil-rich or river-rich countries build systems around what they have.
- **Climate:** sets the potential for solar, wind and HEP, and the reliability of river flow.
- **Technology:** horizontal drilling and fracking opened new gas and oil supplies; falling costs of solar panels and turbines widened renewable options.

### Balancing factors

- **Energy policy:** taxes, subsidies, targets and bans shape investment.
- **Pollution:** urban smog and climate commitments push away from coal.
- **Energy security:** dependence on imports, particularly from one supplier, encourages diversification.
- **Sustainability:** whether the mix can be maintained without harming future supply or the environment.

### Two useful models

**The energy ladder.** As household incomes rise, families move from dung and crop waste, to fuelwood and charcoal, to kerosene and liquefied petroleum gas (LPG), and finally to electricity. Many households use several rungs at once ("fuel stacking"), because electricity may be unreliable or costly. The ladder explains why traditional biomass still dominates household energy in many LICs.

**The energy trilemma.** Governments try to balance three goals: **security** (reliable supply), **equity** (affordable access) and **environmental sustainability**. Improving one often worsens another: cheap domestic coal gives security and affordability but high emissions; rapid expansion of wind and solar cuts emissions but needs investment in grids and storage. Use the trilemma to structure "assess" answers.

## Trends by income group

| Source | HICs | MICs | LICs |
|---|---|---|---|
| Coal | Falling in many countries as plants close | Still central where it is abundant, such as China and India | Small share overall |
| Oil and gas | Oil steady or falling; gas often replacing coal | Rising with car ownership and industry | Oil for transport and generators; low per person |
| Nuclear | Mixed: some phasing out, some extending or building | Growing in a few, notably China | Very little or none |
| HEP | Most good sites already used | Large new schemes in Brazil, China and elsewhere | Main grid source in some, such as Ethiopia; much potential unused |
| Wind | Fast growth, including offshore | Fast growth, led by China | Small, but rising in some countries |
| Biofuels | Liquid biofuels blended into vehicle fuel | Major producers include Brazil (sugar-cane ethanol) and Indonesia (palm oil biodiesel) | Traditional biomass (wood, charcoal, dung) dominant in households |

When describing a trend, give direction, scale and an example, and remember that a falling share can hide a rising total if overall consumption grows.

## Environmental impacts by source

| Source | Local impacts | Global impacts |
|---|---|---|
| Coal | Mining scars and subsidence; particulates and sulphur dioxide; acid rain downwind | Highest carbon dioxide per unit of electricity |
| Oil | Spills at wells, pipelines and tankers; refinery pollution | Carbon dioxide from transport fuels |
| Gas | Leaks; fracking concerns over groundwater and small earthquakes | Lower carbon dioxide than coal, but methane leaks add warming |
| Nuclear | Radioactive waste storage; accident risk; cooling water heats rivers | Very low carbon emissions in operation |
| HEP | Flooded valleys, displaced people, trapped sediment, blocked fish migration | Low carbon, though decaying vegetation in tropical reservoirs releases methane |
| Wind | Noise, visual impact, bird and bat deaths | Very low carbon |
| Biofuels | Monoculture, water use, fertiliser run-off | Deforestation for crops such as palm oil can release more carbon than the fuel saves |
| Fuelwood | Deforestation and soil erosion near settlements; indoor smoke | Carbon release if forest does not regrow |

## Annotated model answer

*Question: Explain why biofuels are not always a sustainable energy source. [5]*

> Biofuels are renewable because the crops can be regrown (**classification**). However, growing crops for fuel uses land that could produce food, so it can raise food prices (**food versus fuel**). Where forest or peatland is cleared to plant crops such as oil palm, the carbon released can be greater than the emissions saved by replacing fossil fuel (**carbon balance**). Large monocultures need fertiliser and water, polluting rivers and lowering water tables (**local impact**). Fuelwood is sustainable only if harvesting does not exceed regrowth (**depletable renewable**).

## Common mistakes

- Confusing capacity (MW) with generation (MWh).
- Treating "renewable" and "sustainable" as the same.
- Describing a trend in share when the question asks about total consumption, or the reverse.
- Listing the nine factors without saying whether each affects demand, supply or the mix.
- Writing only about global impacts when the question also asks about the local scale.

## Exam technique

- **Describe** trends: direction, rate, figures, and an anomaly.
- **Explain** differences: separate demand from supply, then name the factor and its effect.
- **Assess** or **Evaluate**: use the trilemma to weigh security, affordability and sustainability, and conclude for a named country type.
