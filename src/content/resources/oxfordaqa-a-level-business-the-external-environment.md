## What the section asks you to do

Calculate and interpret market size (volume and value), growth or decline, market share and the four firm concentration ratio; explain five barriers to entry and the effect of high or low barriers; explain how market conditions affect demand, decisions and profits; and explain the effect of changes in incomes (normal and inferior goods, YED), interest rates, exchange rates, input costs and competition.

## 1. Market size, growth and share

**Market size** can be measured two ways. **Volume** counts units sold (loaves, bikes, tonnes). **Value** is sales revenue in the market, which is volume multiplied by average price.

**Market growth or decline** is the percentage change in size between two periods:

**growth (%) = (new size - old size) / old size × 100**

A negative answer is market decline. Volume and value can move differently, so say which you use.

**Market share** shows how large one business is within its market:

**market share (%) = business sales / total market sales × 100**

Use the same measure on top and bottom: sales value over market value, or units over market volume.

### Worked example 1: the bread market

The sourdough bread market in one city sells 6.0 million loaves in Year 1 at an average price of 2.50 currency units (cu), and 6.3 million loaves in Year 2 at an average price of 2.60 cu. Tavistry Bakehouse's Year 2 sales revenue is 2.457 million cu.

Step 1, Year 1 value: 6.0m × 2.50 = 15.0m cu.
Step 2, Year 2 value: 6.3m × 2.60 = 16.38m cu.
Step 3, volume growth: (6.3 - 6.0) / 6.0 × 100 = 5.0%.
Step 4, value growth: (16.38 - 15.0) / 15.0 × 100 = 9.2%.
Step 5, Tavistry's market share: 2.457 / 16.38 × 100 = 15.0%.

Interpretation: value grew faster than volume (9.2% against 5.0%) because the average price also rose, so volume alone understates the growth in sales revenue. A 15.0% share makes Tavistry a significant player that does not dominate.

## 2. Four firm concentration ratio

The **four firm concentration ratio** adds the market shares of the four largest firms.

### Worked example 2: the electric bike market

Maldene Cycles competes in a market worth 80.0m cu. The largest firms have sales of 20.0m, 14.4m, 9.6m and 7.2m cu. Maldene is the third largest.

Step 1, shares: 20.0/80 = 25.0%; 14.4/80 = 18.0%; 9.6/80 = 12.0%; 7.2/80 = 9.0%.
Step 2, ratio: 25.0 + 18.0 + 12.0 + 9.0 = 64.0%.
Step 3, all other firms share the remaining 36.0%.

Interpretation: 64% means a few firms hold most of the market, which may point to higher barriers or scope to influence price. It does not by itself show how fiercely they compete.

## 3. Barriers to entry

A **barrier to entry** is anything that makes it hard or costly for a new business to enter a market. The specification lists five.

- **Entry costs:** premises, machinery, stock and launch marketing. 
- **Legal requirements:** licences, permits, safety rules or professional registration that must be met before trading.
- **Specialist knowledge:** skills or expertise that take years to build.
- **Loyalty to existing businesses:** customers who stay with brands they know, so a newcomer must spend heavily to win them over.
- **Access to resources or distribution:** a new firm may be unable to secure scarce raw materials, or shelf space and delivery routes already tied up by established rivals.

### Effect of high or low barriers

| | High barriers | Low barriers |
|---|---|---|
| Existing businesses | Protected from new rivals; more stable share and profit; less pressure to cut price or innovate | Always exposed to new entrants; must keep prices down and keep improving; profit can be competed away |
| Customers | Less choice; prices may be higher; less pressure on quality | More choice; keener prices; businesses are pushed to improve quality and service |

## 4. Market conditions and their impact

Market conditions describe how competitive, crowded and fast changing a market is. They affect **demand**, **business decisions** (price, range, investment) and **profits**.

- **Saturated market:** most potential customers already buy the product, so total sales barely grow. A firm can grow only by taking share from rivals. Decisions lean toward differentiation, promotion or cost cutting, and profit is squeezed if rivals cut price.
- **Dynamic market:** it changes quickly, with new products, technology or customer tastes and often fast growth. Demand can rise rapidly, but so can the risk of falling behind. Decisions lean toward investing in innovation and capacity.

## 5. The external business environment

The **external business environment** is everything outside the firm that affects it. A change can affect four things: **costs** (what the firm pays), **demand** (what customers buy), **decisions** (what managers choose to do) and **profits** (the result of the first two).

### 5.1 Incomes: normal and inferior goods, and YED

- A **normal good** is one whose demand rises when incomes rise (and falls when incomes fall). Its YED is positive.
- An **inferior good** is one whose demand falls when incomes rise (and rises when incomes fall). Its YED is negative.

**Income elasticity of demand (YED)** measures how responsive quantity demanded is to a change in income:

**YED = % change in quantity demanded / % change in income**

A normal good with YED above 1 is income elastic: demand rises by a larger percentage than income. A normal good with YED between 0 and 1 is income inelastic.

To find the change in quantity demanded when YED is known, rearrange:

**% change in quantity demanded = YED × % change in income**

### Worked example 3: ready meals and budget noodles

Yarrowick Foods records that average household income in its main market rose from 24,000 cu to 25,200 cu, and sales of its premium ready meals rose from 40,000 to 43,000 packs.

Step 1, income change: (25,200 - 24,000) / 24,000 × 100 = 5.0%.
Step 2, quantity change: (43,000 - 40,000) / 40,000 × 100 = 7.5%.
Step 3, YED = 7.5 / 5.0 = +1.5. Positive, so a normal good, and above 1 so income elastic.

Forecast: Yarrowick expects incomes to rise 2.5% next year. Premium meals sell 80,000 packs a year at present, with the same YED of +1.5.

Step 4, % change in quantity = 1.5 × 2.5 = 3.75%.
Step 5, new quantity = 80,000 × 1.0375 = 83,000 packs.

Yarrowick's budget noodles have a YED of -0.6 and sell 50,000 packs.

Step 6, % change = -0.6 × 2.5 = -1.5%.
Step 7, new quantity = 50,000 × 0.985 = 49,250 packs.

If incomes fell 3%, premium meals would fall 4.5% and noodles rise 1.8%, so a mixed range is partly protected.

### 5.2 Interest rates

An interest rate is the price of borrowing (and the reward for saving). A rise has these effects.

- **Costs:** firms with variable rate loans pay more. Borrowing 300,000 cu at 5% costs 15,000 cu a year; at 6.5% it costs 19,500 cu, an extra 4,500 cu.
- **Demand:** consumers pay more on loans and mortgages, so they have less to spend. Demand for goods bought on credit, such as cars and furniture, tends to fall.
- **Decisions:** investment projects that looked profitable at 5% may not be at 6.5%, so managers may delay expansion.

A fall works in the opposite direction. 

### 5.3 Exchange rates

The **exchange rate** is the price of one currency in terms of another. When the home currency rises in value (strengthens), imports become cheaper in home currency terms and exports become dearer for overseas buyers. A weaker home currency does the reverse.

### Worked example 4: imported clay and exported tiles

Ravenhallow Tiles buys clay for 40,000 foreign currency units (FC) per order and exports tiles priced at 30 cu each.

Rate 1: 1 cu = 2.0 FC. The clay costs 40,000 / 2.0 = 20,000 cu. A tile costs an overseas buyer 30 × 2.0 = 60 FC.
Rate 2 (home currency weakens): 1 cu = 1.6 FC. The clay costs 40,000 / 1.6 = 25,000 cu, a rise of 25%. A tile now costs 30 × 1.6 = 48 FC abroad.

So the weaker currency raises Ravenhallow's input costs by 25% but makes its tiles 20% cheaper for overseas customers, which should lift export demand. Net profit depends on which effect is larger.

### 5.4 Costs of inputs

Inputs include raw materials, components, energy and labour. A rise lifts unit costs and cuts margins unless the business passes it on or saves elsewhere.

### Worked example 5: jackets

Nestlebrook Outdoor sells jackets at 80 cu with a unit cost of 52 cu, of which 30 cu is fabric. It sells 10,000 jackets a year.

Step 1, margin now: 80 - 52 = 28 cu per jacket, which is 28/80 = 35.0% of price; profit contribution 280,000 cu.
Step 2, fabric up 10%: 30 × 1.10 = 33 cu, so unit cost is 55 cu.
Step 3, new margin: 80 - 55 = 25 cu, or 31.25%; contribution 250,000 cu, a fall of 30,000 cu.

Options: absorb the fall; raise price 5% to 84 cu (margin 29 cu, 34.5%) and risk losing customers; or change supplier.

### 5.5 Competition

A new entrant takes share and can force a price response, cutting profit per sale. A rival leaving does the opposite. How easily rivals can enter depends on the barriers in section 3. Competition from abroad works the same way: overseas rivals selling in the home market add to the number of businesses, take share and press on price, and a weaker home currency can make their goods dearer and ease the pressure.

### 5.6 Government policies and the degree of competition

The 9625 text also lists government policies and the degree of competition (the number of businesses). Policies such as taxes, subsidies, regulation or trade rules change costs (a tax on inputs), demand (a subsidy to buyers) and decisions (a rule that forces a change of product). Many businesses in a market means strong competition and little power over price; few mean the opposite. Use the same chain: change, cost or demand, decision, profit.

## Common errors

- Mixing volume and value, or dividing a percentage change by the new figure.
- Treating the concentration ratio as a sum of sales rather than shares.
- Calling an inferior good low quality, or stopping at "costs go up" with no link to profit.
