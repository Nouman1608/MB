These questions cover section 8.1, *Globalisation, poverty and inequalities*, of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Sociology (9699) syllabus for 2024–2026, assessed in Paper 4 Section A. Paper 4 sets only extended essays; Section A builds the concepts those essays need, and Section B practises paragraphs and essays. All countries, organisations and data in the items are fictional and invented for practice.

Revise first with the [globalisation, poverty and inequalities study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-sociology-9699-globalisation-poverty-inequalities/) and the [globalisation, poverty and inequalities revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-sociology-9699-globalisation-poverty-inequalities-revision-notes/).

## Section A – Concept questions

**1.** What is meant by "life chances"? **[2]**

**2.** What is meant by "neo-colonialism"? **[2]**

**3.** Explain why income per head may be a misleading measure of life chances. **[3]**

**4.** Explain one way in which globalisation may improve health in developing countries and one way in which it may harm it. **[4]**

**5.** Read the item. The data are invented.

> After the fictional country of Tamaru abolished primary school fees, enrolment rose from 1.2 million to 1.8 million pupils within three years. The number of primary teachers rose from 30,000 to 33,000 over the same period.

**(a)** Calculate the pupil–teacher ratio before and after the change. **[2]**
**(b)** Using your answer, explain one limitation of judging the policy by enrolment alone. **[2]**

**6.** Explain two criticisms of the role of the IMF and World Bank in tackling global inequality. **[6]**

**7.** Explain how a feminist perspective explains global inequality. **[4]**

**8.** Distinguish between modernisation and dependency explanations of global poverty. **[4]**

## Section B – Paragraph and essay questions

**9.** Explain and evaluate the view that integration into the global economy reduces poverty. **[8]**

**10.** Explain and evaluate the claim that colonialism is the main cause of global inequality today. **[8]**

**11.** "Globalisation has improved life chances in developing countries." Evaluate this view with reference to education, income and health. **[35]**

**12.** Evaluate the view that transnational organisations have been successful in tackling global inequalities. **[35]**

## Answers

**1.** A person's opportunities to achieve the things valued in a society [1], such as education, income, good health and long life [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Continued economic, political or cultural control of a country by outside powers [1] after it has gained formal independence (Nkrumah, 1965) [1]. **[2]**

**3.** It is an average, so it hides how income is distributed [1]; inequality within a country can rise while the average grows [1]. It also ignores non-income aspects of life chances such as health and education, which the Human Development Index includes [1]. **[3]**

**4.** Improve: global partnerships such as Gavi or the Global Fund have expanded vaccination and treatment [1], raising life expectancy and reducing disease [1].
Harm: brain drain draws doctors and nurses to richer countries [1], weakening health systems that the poor rely on [1]. **[4]**

**5. (a)** Before: 1,200,000 ÷ 30,000 = 40 pupils per teacher [1]. After: 1,800,000 ÷ 33,000 = 54.5 pupils per teacher, to one decimal place [1].
**(b)** Class sizes rose by about 14.5 pupils per teacher, so quality of teaching may have fallen [1]; higher enrolment does not show that more children are learning, which is what life chances depend on [1]. **[4]**

**6.** Criticism 1: structural adjustment conditions [1]. Loans in the 1980s and 1990s came with requirements to cut public spending and introduce user fees [1], which critics argue reduced access to schooling and health care for the poor [1].
Criticism 2: whose interests they serve [1]. Voting power is weighted towards richer states, and Stiglitz (2002) criticised IMF policies [1]; Marxists argue conditions open markets for Western capital rather than reduce inequality [1]. **[6]**

**7.** Feminists argue global inequality is shaped by patriarchy as well as capitalism [1]. Boserup (1970) showed development projects ignored women's agricultural work and directed resources to men [1]. Pearce's feminisation of poverty describes women's over-representation among the poor [1], and Mies argues global capitalism depends on women's unpaid and low-paid labour [1]. **[4]**

**8.** Modernisation theory locates the causes of poverty inside poor countries [1], such as lack of investment or traditional values, and sees integration into the world economy as the route to development (Rostow) [1]. Dependency theory locates the cause in the global system [1]: rich countries develop by extracting surplus from poor ones, so integration on unequal terms produces underdevelopment (Frank) [1]. **[4]**

**9.** Explanation: neo-liberal and modernisation views argue trade and investment create jobs and growth [1]. China's large reduction in extreme poverty after its late-1970s reforms is often cited [1]. Remittances and export sectors raise household incomes [1], and Bhagwati argues trade has helped reduce poverty [1].
Evaluation: dependency theory argues integration on unequal terms keeps countries poor [1]. Export work can be low-paid and unsafe [1]. Collier's bottom billion remain trapped by conflict, resources, geography and governance [1]. So integration can reduce poverty where states manage it, but not automatically [1]. **[8]**

**10.** Explanation: Rodney (1972) argued colonial rule extracted resources and labour and reshaped economies around exports to Europe [1]. Borders, institutions and infrastructure built for extraction persist [1]. Nkrumah's neo-colonialism argues control continued after independence [1], so colonial history still shapes inequality [1].
Evaluation: some former colonies have grown rapidly, so colonialism alone cannot explain current gaps [1]. Collier stresses internal traps such as conflict and governance [1]. Feminists add patriarchy, and world-systems theory sees capitalism as the wider cause [1]. Colonialism is a major historical cause, but "main cause today" understates other factors [1]. **[8]**

**11.** Marked by levels of response; this is indicative content.

- **Education (AO1/AO2):** fee abolition in Malawi, Uganda and Kenya raised enrolment; global goals; but quality fell, structural adjustment linked to cuts and user fees, girls' access limited in some regions.
- **Income:** poverty reduction in fast-growing economies such as China; microfinance; remittances; but insecure export work, debt cycles, commodity dependence, Collier's traps.
- **Health:** smallpox eradication; Gavi and the Global Fund; but patent rules and medicine prices, brain drain, pollution.
- **Theory (AO3):** modernisation and neo-liberal optimism versus dependency, world-systems, post-colonial and feminist critiques; measurement issues (averages versus distribution, the Human Development Index).
- **Judgement:** for example, life chances have improved on several indicators, but unevenly between and within countries, and on terms set by richer states.

**Level guidance:** top answers address all three named areas with contrasting evidence and theory, and reach a judgement on "improved" for whom; middle answers cover some areas with limited evaluation; low answers make general claims about poverty.

**12.** Marked by levels of response; this is indicative content.

- **For success:** debt relief through the HIPC initiative; Jubilee 2000 pressure; global health partnerships; Sachs's case for aid; NGO projects in water, sanitation and schooling.
- **Against:** structural adjustment and Stiglitz's critique; Moyo's argument that aid fuels corruption and dependency; Marxist view that organisations serve Western capital; weighted voting; small scale of NGOs.
- **Evaluation:** distinguish types of organisation; ask which indicator, for whom and compared with what; consider whether globalisation itself creates the inequalities these bodies address.
- **Judgement:** for example, partial success in health and debt relief, limited success in changing the structures of global inequality.

**Level guidance:** top answers distinguish organisations and criteria of success and weigh evidence across perspectives; middle answers describe organisations with some evaluation; low answers treat all organisations as one.
