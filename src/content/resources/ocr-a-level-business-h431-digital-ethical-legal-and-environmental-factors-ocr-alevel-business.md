This guide goes deeper into the last content areas of **External influences** in OCR A Level Business (H431), Version 2.1 (January 2026), final assessment summer 2027: **the digital revolution, ethical factors, legal factors and environmental factors**, plus the final statement on ignoring external factors (2.6 on this site). The wider [PESTLE study guide](/resources/ocr-a-level-business-political-economic-social-and-technological-factors-and-related-topics/) gives the overview. Companions: [digital, ethical, legal and environmental factors revision notes](/resources/ocr-a-level-business-h431-digital-ethical-legal-and-environmental-factors-ocr-alevel-business-revision-notes/) and [digital, ethical, legal and environmental factors practice questions](/resources/ocr-a-level-business-h431-digital-ethical-legal-and-environmental-factors-ocr-alevel-business-practice/).

## What the specification asks

- **The digital revolution:** explain what it is; evaluate the impact of the information age; evaluate the opportunities and threats it creates; evaluate why some businesses are more affected than others; evaluate rapid technological change for a business and its stakeholders.
- **Ethical factors:** distinguish law from ethics; explain ethical issues and why some firms are more affected; evaluate ethical trading methods and working practices; recommend ways to improve an ethical profile; evaluate behaving ethically and being seen as ethical; evaluate and manage conflicts between ethical and other objectives.
- **Legal factors:** explain the purpose of legislation on employment, equality, company and partnership, health and safety, data protection, intellectual property, planning, environment, consumer protection, contract and financial conduct; evaluate compliance, its conflicts, its importance and the consequences of failure.
- **Environmental factors:** describe issues; explain why they matter; evaluate an environmental policy; recommend ways to address issues; evaluate being seen as environmentally friendly; manage conflicts with other objectives; explain and evaluate sustainability.
- **Ignoring external factors:** evaluate the consequences.

Most statements say **evaluate**, so expect extended answers that end in a supported judgement.

## The digital revolution

The digital revolution is the move from mechanical and analogue technology to digital technology, which created the **information age**: a period in which information can be collected, stored, analysed and shared almost instantly and at very low cost.

Four changes matter most for a business:

1. **Markets widen.** A small firm can sell nationally or globally through its own website or online marketplaces.
2. **Customers are better informed.** Price comparison and online reviews make it harder to charge more than rivals without a clear reason.
3. **Data becomes an asset.** Purchase histories allow targeted promotion and better stock decisions.
4. **Products and processes change.** Some products become digital (music, maps, tickets) and many processes become automated.

**Why some businesses are more affected.** Exposure is highest where the product can be delivered digitally, where customers already shop online, or where intermediaries can be cut out (travel agents, high-street banks, newspapers). It is lowest where the service needs physical presence and local trust, such as a plumber or a hairdresser, though even these now rely on online reviews and bookings.

**Rapid technological change** brings first-mover advantage and productivity gains, but also short product lives, constant reinvestment, staff who need retraining, and customers who may be excluded if they are not online.

## Ethical factors

**Law versus ethics.** Law sets the minimum a business must do, enforced by penalties. Ethics are moral principles about what is right, and a business chooses how far beyond the law to go. Paying the legal minimum wage is compliance; paying more because staff cannot live on it is an ethical choice.

**Why some businesses are more affected.** Firms with long overseas supply chains (clothing, cocoa, electronics), those selling to young or vulnerable customers, those with a high public profile, and those whose brand rests on values face more scrutiny.

**Ethical trading methods and working practices** include buying from certified fair-trade suppliers, auditing factories, refusing suppliers that use child or forced labour, paying fair wages, giving safe conditions, and being open about where products come from.

### Worked example 1: the cost of an ethical choice

Thornbury Chocolate (invented) sells a bar at £1.80 with a unit cost of £1.20, a margin of 0.60 ÷ 1.80 = **33.3%**. Switching to certified cocoa adds 6p per bar.

- If price is unchanged: margin = 0.54 ÷ 1.80 = **30.0%**.
- If price rises by 10p to £1.90: margin = 0.64 ÷ 1.90 = **33.7%**, slightly higher than before.

The figures show the conflict and one way to manage it. Whether the price rise works depends on whether customers value the certification enough to pay 10p more, and how rivals price.

**Managing conflicts** between ethical and other objectives: phase changes in, pass part of the cost to customers who value it, find savings elsewhere, and set ethical targets alongside financial ones so managers are judged on both.

**Being seen as ethical** matters because customers, staff and investors act on perception. But claims that cannot be supported can do more damage than saying nothing.

## Legal factors

The specification lists eleven areas. Examples of UK laws in several of them:

| Area | Purpose | UK example |
|---|---|---|
| Employment | Set rights such as pay, notice and fair dismissal | Employment Rights Act 1996 |
| Equality | Prevent discrimination on protected characteristics | Equality Act 2010 |
| Company and partnership | Rules for forming and running businesses | Companies Act 2006 |
| Health and safety | Protect workers and others from harm | Health and Safety at Work etc. Act 1974 |
| Data protection | Control how personal data is collected and used | Data Protection Act 2018 |
| Consumer protection | Goods must be as described and fit for purpose | Consumer Rights Act 2015 |

Intellectual property law protects inventions, designs, brands and creative work; planning law controls land use and building; environmental law limits pollution and waste; contract law makes agreements enforceable; financial conduct rules keep financial services honest.

**Complying** means written policies, training, a named responsible manager, regular audits, and professional advice where the law is complex.

**Conflicts.** Compliance costs money and time and can slow decisions, which may clash with cost-cutting or rapid growth objectives. A start-up may be tempted to cut corners on health and safety to save money.

**Consequences of failing to comply** include fines, compensation claims, court orders, loss of licences or contracts, damaged reputation and, for directors, possible personal liability. A data breach, for example, can bring a regulator's fine, claims from customers and lost trust that hits future sales.

## Environmental factors

Issues include climate change and carbon emissions, air and water pollution, waste and packaging, energy use, and the depletion of resources. A business must consider them because of legislation, customer and investor pressure, staff attitudes, rising energy and waste costs, and risks to its own supply chain.

An **environmental policy** is a written statement of a business's aims and targets for reducing its environmental impact, with the actions it will take. Its impact on stakeholders varies: customers and local residents may gain, shareholders may lose short-term profit but gain long-term value, and suppliers may need to change their own processes.

**Sustainability** means meeting present needs without reducing the ability of future generations to meet theirs. A business acts sustainably by using renewable energy, cutting and recycling waste, sourcing materials responsibly and designing products to last or be repaired.

### Worked example 2: a greener choice with a payback

Holloway Couriers (invented) needs a new van. An electric van costs £48,000 against £36,000 for diesel. Lower fuel and servicing costs save £3,000 a year.

- Extra cost = £12,000. Payback on the extra cost = 12,000 ÷ 3,000 = **4 years**.

Evaluation: if the van lasts eight years, the switch saves money over its life; it also supports a green image with business customers who report their own emissions. Against it: a higher upfront cost, charging time and range limits on long routes.

**Being seen as environmentally friendly** attracts customers, staff and investors, but claims not backed by evidence (greenwashing) risk complaints and lost trust.

## Ignoring external factors

A business that ignores external factors may be overtaken by digital rivals, prosecuted for breaking the law, boycotted for unethical practice or hit by rising environmental costs it did not plan for. The consequences range from lost sales and fines to failure. The best answers show how one ignored factor leads to another, for example an ethical scandal that triggers legal action and then falling sales.

## Common mistakes

- Treating ethics and law as the same thing.
- Describing the digital revolution only as "using computers", without the information age or its effect on competition.
- Giving only benefits of ethical or green behaviour, with no cost or conflict.
- Naming laws without explaining what they require of the business.
- Ending an evaluation without saying what the judgement depends on.

## Exam technique for OCR command words

- **Distinguish**: give both meanings and the difference, ideally with one example that is legal but unethical.
- **Explain**: cause, mechanism, effect on the named business.
- **Recommend and justify**: choose one course of action and give reasons tied to the case, including why it beats the alternative.
- **Evaluate**: weigh benefits against costs for more than one stakeholder, then judge which matters most and why.
