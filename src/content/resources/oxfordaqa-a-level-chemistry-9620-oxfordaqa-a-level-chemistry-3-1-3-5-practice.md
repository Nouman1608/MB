These questions cover **3.1.3.5 Shapes of simple molecules and ions** of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Chemistry (9620) specification, Version 5.3. It is International AS content, examined in Unit 1. Section A has short recall and application questions; Section B has longer, structured questions. If a point is unfamiliar, read the [shapes study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-1-3-5/) or the [shapes revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-1-3-5-revision-notes/) first.

## Questions

### Section A

**1.** Write the three types of electron-pair repulsion in order, strongest first. **[1]**

**2.** Name the shape of a silicon tetrachloride molecule, SiCl₄, and give its bond angle. **[2]**

**3.** Name the shape of a phosphorus pentafluoride molecule, PF₅, and give its two bond angles. **[2]**

**4.** For the IF₄⁺ ion, state the total number of electron pairs around iodine and the number of lone pairs. **[2]**

**5.** Gaseous beryllium chloride contains BeCl₂ molecules. Name the shape and give the bond angle. **[2]**

**6.** Ammonia has four electron pairs around nitrogen, arranged tetrahedrally, yet its shape is called pyramidal. Explain why. **[2]**

### Section B

**7.** This question is about the oxonium ion, H₃O⁺.

**(a)** Deduce the shape of H₃O⁺. Explain your answer and give the bond angle. **[4]**

**(b)** The H–N–H angle in NH₄⁺ is 109.5°. Explain why the H–O–H angle in H₃O⁺ is smaller. **[2]**

**8.** This question is about sulfur tetrafluoride, SF₄.

**(a)** Show how many bonding pairs and lone pairs surround the sulfur atom. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain why the lone pair occupies an equatorial position rather than an axial one. **[2]**

**(c)** Name the shape of SF₄. **[1]**

**9.** Potassium iodide solution dissolves iodine to form the triiodide ion, I₃⁻, in which a central iodine atom is bonded to two others.

**(a)** Deduce the shape of I₃⁻ and give its bond angle. Show your working. **[3]**

**(b)** Draw the shape of I₃⁻, showing the lone pairs on the central atom. **[2]**

**10.** Bromine pentafluoride, BrF₅, has five fluorine atoms bonded to bromine.

**(a)** Name the shape of BrF₅. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain why the F–Br–F bond angles are slightly less than 90°. **[2]**

**(c)** Draw the shape of BrF₅, using wedges and dashed bonds where needed. **[2]**

**11.** Copy and complete the table by giving the shape and bond angle of each species. **[8]**

| Species | Shape | Bond angle |

**12.** SF₄ and ClF₄⁻ both have four fluorine atoms bonded to the central atom, but they have different shapes. Explain why. **[4]**

## Answers

### Section A

**1.** Lone pair–lone pair > lone pair–bond pair > bond pair–bond pair [1] **[1]**

**2.** Tetrahedral [1]; 109.5° [1] **[2]**

**3.** Trigonal bipyramidal [1]; 90° and 120° [1] **[2]**

**4.** (7 + 4 − 1) ÷ 2 = **5** pairs [1]; 4 bonding, so **1** lone pair [1] **[2]**

*Examiner insight:* For a positive ion, subtract one electron per charge; adding instead gives 6 pairs and the wrong shape.

**5.** Linear [1]; 180° [1] **[2]**

**6.** One of the four pairs is a lone pair [1]; the shape is named from the positions of the atoms only, and the N and three H atoms form a pyramid [1] **[2]**

### Section B

**7. (a)** O has 6 outer electrons + 3 from H − 1 for the charge = 8, so 4 pairs [1]; 3 bonding pairs and 1 lone pair [1]; pairs repel and arrange as far apart as possible, and the lone pair repels more strongly than the bonding pairs [1]; **pyramidal**, about **107°** [1]

**(b)** NH₄⁺ has four bonding pairs and no lone pairs [1]; in H₃O⁺ the lone pair repels the bonding pairs more strongly, pushing them closer together [1] **[6]**

*Examiner insight:* "Because it has a lone pair" alone earns nothing in (b); the mark needs the stronger lone pair repulsion.

**8. (a)** S has 6 outer electrons + 4 from F = 10, so 5 pairs [1]; 4 bonding pairs and 1 lone pair [1]

**(b)** An equatorial position has only two neighbouring pairs at 90° (an axial one has three) [1]; so the stronger lone pair repulsion is minimised [1]

**(c)** See-saw [1] **[5]**

**9. (a)** Central I: 7 outer electrons + 2 from the bonded I atoms + 1 for the charge = 10, so 5 pairs [1]; 2 bonding and 3 lone pairs, with all three lone pairs equatorial [1]; **linear, 180°** [1]

**(b)** Three I atoms in a straight line [1]; three lone pairs drawn around the central atom in the equatorial plane [1] **[5]**

*Examiner insight:* Many answers call I₃⁻ "bent" because the central atom has lone pairs. The equatorial placement keeps the atoms in a straight line.

**10. (a)** Square pyramidal [1]

**(b)** Br has six electron pairs: five bonding and one lone pair [1]; the lone pair repels the bonding pairs more strongly, pushing the four F atoms of the square base slightly up towards the fifth F atom [1]

**(c)** Four F atoms in a square around Br with one F above [1]; correct use of wedges and dashed bonds, with the lone pair shown below Br [1] **[5]**

**11.** AlCl₄⁻: tetrahedral [1], 109.5° [1]; NH₂⁻: bent [1], 104.5° [1]; SiF₆²⁻: octahedral [1], 90° [1]; XeF₄: square planar [1], 90° [1] **[8]**

*Examiner insight:* Each shape and each angle is a separate mark; "bent" for NH₂⁻ with 109.5° gains only one of the two.

**12.** S in SF₄: (6 + 4) ÷ 2 = 5 pairs, one of them a lone pair [1]; Cl in ClF₄⁻: (7 + 4 + 1) ÷ 2 = 6 pairs, two of them lone pairs [1]; in SF₄ the lone pair is equatorial in a trigonal bipyramid, giving a see-saw [1]; in ClF₄⁻ the two lone pairs are opposite each other in an octahedral arrangement, giving a square planar shape [1] **[4]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Not adjusting the electron count for the charge on an ion.
- Naming the electron-pair arrangement rather than the shape of the atoms.
- Giving 109.5° for every four-pair species.
- Placing lone pairs axially in five-pair species.
- Explanations that mention lone pairs but not their stronger repulsion.
