Subtopic 13.2 of Cambridge International AS & A Level Physics 9702 (2025–2027) covers Newton's law of gravitation, the point-mass treatment of uniform spheres, circular orbits and geostationary satellites. These notes are condensed for recall. For full explanations and worked examples, read the [study guide for this subtopic](/resources/cambridge-a-level-physics-9702-a-gravitational-force-between-point-masses/); to apply the ideas, try the [practice questions for this subtopic](/resources/cambridge-a-level-physics-9702-a-gravitational-force-between-point-masses-practice/).

## Newton's law of gravitation

```
F = Gm₁m₂ / r²          G = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N m² kg⁻²
```

- Force ∝ product of the masses; force ∝ 1/(separation)².
- Always **attractive**, along the **line joining the centres**.
- Equal and opposite forces on the two bodies (Newton's third law pair).
- Written for **point masses**; outside a **uniform sphere**, treat the sphere as a point mass at its centre.
- r is **centre to centre**: for a satellite at height h, r = R + h.

## Inverse square ratios

| Change | Force becomes |
|---|---|
| r × 2 | F ÷ 4 |
| r × 3 | F ÷ 9 |
| r × ½ | F × 4 |
| m₁ × 2 and r × 2 | F ÷ 2 |
| from surface (r = R) to height 2R (r = 3R) | F ÷ 9 |

## Circular orbits

Gravity is the **only** force and provides the centripetal force:

```
GMm / r² = mv² / r     →   v = √(GM / r)
GMm / r² = mrω²        →   ω² = GM / r³
T² = (4π² / GM) r³
M = 4π²r³ / (GT²)       (mass of the central body)
```

| Feature | Consequence |
|---|---|
| m cancels | Orbit does not depend on the satellite's mass |
| v ∝ 1/√r | Higher orbit: slower |
| T ∝ r^(3/2) | Higher orbit: longer period; r × 4 gives T × 8 |
| Force points at centre of M | Every orbit is centred on the centre of the central body |

## Geostationary orbit

| Condition | Reason |
|---|---|
| Period 24 hours | Matches one rotation of the Earth |
| West to east | Same direction as the Earth's rotation |
| Directly above the Equator | Orbit must be centred on the Earth's centre; only the equatorial plane keeps it over one point |

Radius ≈ 4.22 × 10⁷ m (height ≈ 3.59 × 10⁷ m), speed ≈ 3.07 km s⁻¹. Use: communications and weather satellites, so ground dishes can stay fixed.

## Numbers worth recognising

| Quantity | Value |
|---|---|
| Earth–Moon force | ≈ 2.0 × 10²⁰ N |
| Orbit at 400 km height | v ≈ 7.7 km s⁻¹, T ≈ 92 min |
| Force between two people 0.80 m apart | ~10⁻⁷ N |
| Earth's mass from the Moon's orbit | ≈ 6.0 × 10²⁴ kg |

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Point mass | A mass whose size is negligible; a uniform sphere acts as one at its centre for outside points |
| Gravitational constant G | Constant in Newton's law, 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N m² kg⁻² |
| Orbital radius | Distance from the centre of the central body to the orbiting body |
| Orbital period | Time for one complete orbit |
| Geostationary orbit | Equatorial, west-to-east orbit with a 24-hour period, staying above one point |
| Free fall | Motion with gravity as the only force; true of all orbiting bodies |

## Exam traps

- Substituting the height above the surface for r.
- Forgetting that a satellite's mass cancels.
- Adding a separate centripetal force to gravity.
- Listing only "24 hours" for geostationary, or placing it over a pole.
- Leaving the period in hours or days.
- Saying there is no gravity in orbit.
- Confusing r³ and r² when rearranging T² = (4π²/GM)r³.

## Self-test

1. State Newton's law of gravitation in words.
2. Why can the Earth be treated as a point mass for a satellite above its surface?
3. The separation of two point masses is halved. What happens to the force?
4. Derive v² = GM/r for a circular orbit.
5. Explain why two satellites of different mass at the same orbital radius have the same period.
6. A satellite's orbital radius is increased by a factor of 9. By what factor does its period change?
7. By what factor does its orbital speed change for the same radius change?
8. State the three conditions for a geostationary orbit.
9. Why must a geostationary satellite be above the Equator?
10. A moon orbits a planet at r = 2.0 × 10⁸ m with period 4.0 days. Find the mass of the planet.
11. Calculate the gravitational force between two 10 kg spheres whose centres are 0.20 m apart.
12. The Earth pulls the Moon with a force of 2.0 × 10²⁰ N. What force does the Moon exert on the Earth?

**Answers:** 1. Two point masses attract each other with a force proportional to the product of their masses and inversely proportional to the square of their separation. 2. For points outside a uniform sphere, its mass acts as if concentrated at its centre. 3. It becomes four times larger. 4. Gravity provides the centripetal force: GMm/r² = mv²/r; cancel m and one r: v² = GM/r. 5. m cancels from GMm/r² = mrω², so ω and T depend only on M and r. 6. T ∝ r^(3/2): 9^(3/2) = 27. 7. v ∝ 1/√r: speed is divided by 3. 8. Period 24 hours; west to east; directly above the Equator. 9. The gravitational force points to the Earth's centre, so the orbit is centred there; only an orbit in the equatorial plane stays above a single point. 10. T = 3.46 × 10⁵ s; M = 4π²r³/(GT²) = 4.0 × 10²⁵ kg. 11. F = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ × 10 × 10 ÷ 0.040 = 1.7 × 10⁻⁷ N. 12. 2.0 × 10²⁰ N, towards the Moon.
