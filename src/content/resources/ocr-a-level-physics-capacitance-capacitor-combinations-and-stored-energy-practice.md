## Questions

**1.** **(a)** State what is meant by one farad. **[1]**
**(b)** A 47 µF capacitor holds 0.28 mC on its positive plate. Calculate the p.d. across it. **[2]**

**2.** An uncharged capacitor is connected through a resistor to a 9.0 V battery. Explain, in terms of electrons, how the capacitor becomes charged and why the current eventually stops. **[4]**

**3.** The battery in question 2 is removed and the two plates are joined through the resistor. Describe the movement of electrons and explain what happens to the current. **[3]**

**4.** A 12 µF capacitor and a 24 µF capacitor are connected in series across a 27 V supply.
**(a)** Calculate the total capacitance. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the charge on the 12 µF capacitor. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the p.d. across each capacitor. **[2]**

**5.** An 8.0 µF capacitor is connected in parallel with a combination of a 6.0 µF capacitor and a 12 µF capacitor in series. The network is connected across a 10 V supply.
**(a)** Calculate the total capacitance of the network. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate the total charge supplied by the source. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the charge on the 12 µF capacitor. **[2]**

**6.** A 9.0 V cell of negligible internal resistance is connected in series with a 1.5 kΩ resistor and a 3.3 kΩ resistor. A 220 µF capacitor is connected across the 3.3 kΩ resistor and has been connected for a long time.
**(a)** Calculate the p.d. across the capacitor. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate the charge on the capacitor. **[2]**
**(c)** State the p.d. across the 1.5 kΩ resistor. **[1]**

**7.** Readings of p.d. V and charge Q for a capacitor are shown.

| Q / µC | 0 | 20 | 40 | 60 | 80 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| V / V | 0 | 1.0 | 2.0 | 3.0 | 4.0 |

**(a)** Use a gradient to find the capacitance. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the energy stored at 4.0 V. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the extra energy needed to raise the p.d. from 2.0 V to 4.0 V. **[2]**

**8.** A 4.0 µF capacitor is charged to 9.0 V. It is then disconnected from the supply and connected in parallel with an uncharged 2.0 µF capacitor.
**(a)** Calculate the initial charge on the 4.0 µF capacitor. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the final p.d. across the pair. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the energy stored before and after the connection. **[2]**
**(d)** Calculate the energy difference and suggest where it goes. **[2]**

**9.** A student investigates capacitors using a reed switch of frequency 50 Hz that connects the capacitor alternately to a 5.0 V supply and to a microammeter.
**(a)** Explain how the capacitance of one capacitor can be found from the meter reading. **[3]**
**(b)** The student has a 2.2 µF and a 4.7 µF capacitor. Calculate the readings expected when the two are connected in series and then in parallel, to 2 significant figures. **[3]**
**(c)** State two precautions when using capacitors in this investigation. **[2]**

**10.** A 6.0 µF capacitor and a 3.0 µF capacitor are connected in series across a 12 V supply.
**(a)** Calculate the charge on each capacitor. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the p.d. across each capacitor. **[2]**
**(c)** Show that the energy stored in the 3.0 µF capacitor is twice that in the 6.0 µF capacitor, and that the total equals ½CV² for the combination. **[2]**

**11.** A camera flash unit uses a 0.90 mF capacitor to store 15 J.
**(a)** Calculate the p.d. to which it must be charged, to 2 significant figures. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain why a capacitor is suitable for this use. **[2]**
**(c)** The energy is released in 2.0 ms. Calculate the mean power. **[2]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** One farad is the capacitance when a charge of one coulomb is stored per volt of p.d. (1 F = 1 C V⁻¹) [1].
**(b)** V = Q/C = 0.28 × 10⁻³ / 47 × 10⁻⁶ [1] = **6.0 V** (5.96 V) [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Convert mC and µF to base units before dividing, and write the substituted line so your working is visible.

**2.** Electrons flow from the negative terminal of the battery onto the plate connected to it [1]. Electrons leave the other plate and flow towards the positive terminal, leaving that plate positive [1]. No electrons cross the insulating gap between the plates [1]. The current stops when the p.d. across the capacitor equals the battery e.m.f., so there is no net p.d. driving charge round the circuit [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Name the direction of the electrons and what happens on each plate. "Charge flows through the capacitor" is the typical error.

**3.** Electrons flow from the negative plate, through the resistor, to the positive plate [1]. This is the opposite direction to the charging current [1]. The current decreases to zero as the plates become neutral and the p.d. falls to zero [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* State both that the direction reverses and that the current decreases, since they are separate points.

**4. (a)** 1/C = 1/12 + 1/24 = 3/24 [1], so C = **8.0 µF** [1].
**(b)** Q = CV = 8.0 × 10⁻⁶ × 27 = **2.2 × 10⁻⁴ C** (216 µC) [1].
**(c)** The charge is the same on each: V = Q/C. For 12 µF: 216 / 12 = **18 V** [1]. For 24 µF: 216 / 24 = **9.0 V** [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Check that the p.d.s sum to the supply (18 + 9.0 = 27 V). The smaller capacitor takes the larger p.d.

**5. (a)** Series pair: 1/C = 1/6.0 + 1/12 = 3/12, so C = 4.0 µF [1]. Parallel with 8.0 µF: C = 8.0 + 4.0 [1] = **12 µF** [1].
**(b)** Q = CV = 12 × 10⁻⁶ × 10 = **1.2 × 10⁻⁴ C** [1].
**(c)** The series branch has C = 4.0 µF, so its charge is 4.0 × 10⁻⁶ × 10 = 4.0 × 10⁻⁵ C [1]. The same charge is on each capacitor in series, so the 12 µF capacitor holds **4.0 × 10⁻⁵ C** (40 µC) [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* In part (c) the 12 µF capacitor does not have 10 V across it. Reduce the branch first, then use "same charge in series".

**6. (a)** Total resistance = 1500 + 3300 = 4800 Ω [1]. Steady current I = 9.0 / 4800 = 1.875 × 10⁻³ A [1]. V = IR = 1.875 × 10⁻³ × 3300 = **6.2 V** (6.19 V) [1].
**(b)** Q = CV = 220 × 10⁻⁶ × 6.19 [1] = **1.4 × 10⁻³ C** [1].
**(c)** 9.0 − 6.19 = **2.8 V** [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* The capacitor charges to the p.d. across the resistor it is connected to, not the cell e.m.f. Say that no steady current enters its branch, so the resistors behave as a simple series pair.

**7. (a)** Gradient of V against Q = 4.0 / 80 µC = 0.050 V µC⁻¹ [1]. C = 1/gradient = **20 µF** [1].
**(b)** W = ½QV = 0.5 × 80 × 10⁻⁶ × 4.0 [1] = **1.6 × 10⁻⁴ J** (160 µJ) [1].
**(c)** The area between V = 2.0 V and 4.0 V is a trapezium: ½(2.0 + 4.0) × (80 − 40) × 10⁻⁶ [1] = **1.2 × 10⁻⁴ J** (120 µJ) [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* The gradient of a V-Q graph is 1/C, not C.

**8. (a)** Q = CV = 4.0 × 9.0 = **36 µC** [1].
**(b)** Total capacitance = 4.0 + 2.0 = 6.0 µF [1]. Charge is conserved, so V = 36 / 6.0 = **6.0 V** [1].
**(c)** Before: ½ × 4.0 × 10⁻⁶ × 9.0² = **1.6 × 10⁻⁴ J** (162 µJ) [1]. After: ½ × 6.0 × 10⁻⁶ × 6.0² = **1.1 × 10⁻⁴ J** (108 µJ) [1].
**(d)** Difference = 162 − 108 = **54 µJ** [1]. It is transferred as thermal energy in the connecting wires (and some is radiated) as charge flows between the capacitors [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* The stored energy falls, so say where the 54 µJ goes. Saying only that energy is conserved is incomplete.

**9. (a)** Each cycle the capacitor is charged to V and then discharged through the meter [1]. The meter reads the mean current I = fQ = fCV [1]. So C = I / (fV), using the known frequency and supply p.d. [1].
**(b)** Series: C = (2.2 × 4.7) / (2.2 + 4.7) = 1.5 µF [1]; I = fCV = 50 × 1.4986 × 10⁻⁶ × 5.0 = **3.7 × 10⁻⁴ A** [1]. Parallel: C = 6.9 µF, so I = 50 × 6.9 × 10⁻⁶ × 5.0 = **1.7 × 10⁻³ A** [1].
**(c)** Any two from: check the polarity of electrolytic capacitors; do not exceed the rated voltage; discharge capacitors before changing the circuit; use a high-resistance voltmeter across the capacitor [1][1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Part (a) asks you to explain, so link the meter reading to charge per second rather than only describing the apparatus.

**10. (a)** 1/C = 1/6.0 + 1/3.0 = 3/6.0, so C = 2.0 µF [1]. Q = CV = 2.0 × 10⁻⁶ × 12 = **2.4 × 10⁻⁵ C**, the same on each capacitor [1].
**(b)** 6.0 µF: V = Q/C = 24 / 6.0 = **4.0 V** [1]. 3.0 µF: 24 / 3.0 = **8.0 V** [1].
**(c)** W = ½QV: 6.0 µF: ½ × 24 × 4.0 = 48 µJ; 3.0 µF: ½ × 24 × 8.0 = 96 µJ, which is twice 48 µJ [1]. Total = 144 µJ, and ½CV² = 0.5 × 2.0 × 10⁻⁶ × 12² = 144 µJ [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* On a "show that" question, give both energies and the final comparison. Saying "the answers match" demonstrates nothing.

**11. (a)** W = ½V²C, so V = √(2W/C) [1] = √(2 × 15 / 0.90 × 10⁻³) [1] = **1.8 × 10² V** (183 V) [1].
**(b)** It can release its stored energy in a very short time [1], giving a high-power pulse that a small battery could not supply directly [1].
**(c)** P = W/t = 15 / 2.0 × 10⁻³ [1] = **7.5 × 10³ W** [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the energy equation containing W and C, and rearrange before substituting.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Leaving µF or mF values unconverted.
- Adding capacitances for a series pair, or using 1/C = C₁ + C₂.
- Quoting 1/C as the final answer.
- Using the supply p.d. across a capacitor that sits across only part of the circuit.
- Saying that charge crosses the dielectric.
- Using W = QV or forgetting the square in ½CV².
- Using the gradient of a V-Q graph as C instead of 1/C.
- Describing a practical method without saying how C is calculated.
