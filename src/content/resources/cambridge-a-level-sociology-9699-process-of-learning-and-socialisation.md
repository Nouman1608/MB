Section 1.1, *The process of learning and socialisation*, opens Paper 1 of Cambridge International AS & A Level Sociology (9699). It asks how people learn to be members of a society, and why that learning makes the social world feel natural. The syllabus links it to the key concepts *Socialisation, culture and identity* and *Power, control and resistance*.

## What the syllabus lists

The 9699 syllabus for 2024–2026 has three bullet points for 1.1:

- Culture, roles, norms, values, beliefs, customs, ideology, power and status as elements in the social construction of reality.
- The importance of socialisation in influencing human behaviour, including the nurture versus nature debate.
- Agencies of socialisation and social control, including family, education, peer group, media and religion.

Each bullet can be the focus of a short-answer question, a two-explanations question or an essay, so treat all three as equally examinable.

## Building blocks of social reality: how the elements connect

Definitions alone earn few marks. Examiners reward showing how the elements depend on each other.

- **Values** are broad ideas about what is desirable, such as honesty or family loyalty. **Norms** turn values into specific expectations, such as not lying to parents. **Customs** are norms that have become traditional over generations, such as wedding rituals; **beliefs** are the ideas, religious or secular, that justify them.
- **Status** is a social position. Ascribed status is fixed at birth (for example, being a first-born son in a society that gives that position authority); achieved status is earned (for example, becoming a surgeon). **Roles** are the expected behaviours attached to a status. One person holds many statuses, so roles can clash: a working mother may face *role conflict* when a child is ill on an important workday.
- **Culture** is the whole way of life that contains all of these, both material (tools, clothes, buildings) and non-material (language, ideas, norms).
- **Power** decides whose culture becomes "normal". Max Weber defined power as the chance of a person or group carrying out their will even against the resistance of others. **Ideology** is a set of ideas that presents one group's interests as natural or universal; Marx argued that the ruling ideas of each age are the ideas of its ruling class.

### Why "social construction" matters

To say reality is socially constructed is to say that categories people treat as natural are made and maintained by society. Three tests show it:

1. **Variation between cultures.** The age at which a person counts as an adult, what counts as polite eating, and what it means to be on time all differ from society to society.
2. **Change over time.** Ideas of childhood, marriage and acceptable dress have changed within single lifetimes.
3. **Contest.** Groups argue over definitions, for example over what counts as appropriate behaviour for men and women. If a category were simply natural, there would be nothing to argue about.

In an answer, add the power point: a construction feels natural partly because powerful groups promote it through ideology.

## Primary, secondary and resocialisation

- **Primary socialisation** happens in early childhood, mainly in the family. Children learn language, basic norms and a first sense of self.
- **Secondary socialisation** continues through life via school, peers, media, religion and work. It teaches universal rules that apply to strangers, not only to kin.
- **Resocialisation** replaces earlier learning. Erving Goffman's *Asylums* (1961) described "total institutions" such as psychiatric hospitals, prisons and military training, where a person's old identity is stripped away (the "mortification of the self") and a new one imposed.
- **Anticipatory socialisation** is learning the norms of a role before you take it on, such as a trainee copying senior staff.

## How important is socialisation? The nature versus nurture debate

**The nature position.** Some argue that behaviour is rooted in biology. E.O. Wilson's *Sociobiology* (1975) proposed that some social behaviour, including aspects of sex differences and altruism towards kin, evolved because it helped genes survive. Hormone research is also used to explain differences in aggression.

**The nurture position.** Sociologists argue that human behaviour is learned. Their case rests on four kinds of evidence:

- **Cross-cultural variation**: if behaviour were fixed by genes, it would be similar everywhere, yet gender roles, family forms and ideas of beauty vary widely.
- **Historical change**: behaviour changes faster than genes could, as with women's rapid entry into higher education and professional work in many societies.
- **Cases of extreme deprivation**: children raised with little human contact struggle to acquire language and social skills, which suggests these depend on socialisation at the right stage.
- **Evidence of learning**: studies of how parents and schools treat boys and girls differently show behaviour being shaped.

**Evaluation.** Most sociologists now argue for interaction: biology gives humans capacities (for language, for example), while socialisation decides how those capacities are used. Note too that sociobiology can be criticised for justifying inequality by calling it natural, which links back to ideology.

## How the five agencies work

| Agency | Main process | Theory link | Evaluation point |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Family** | Primary socialisation; imitation, praise and punishment | Talcott Parsons: the family shapes children's personalities around society's shared values | Family forms and parenting styles vary, so outcomes differ |
| **Education** | Formal and hidden curriculum | Samuel Bowles and Herbert Gintis, *Schooling in Capitalist America* (1976): school rules prepare pupils to accept hierarchy at work | Pupils can reject school values, as anti-school subcultures show |
| **Peer group** | Imitation and acceptance among equals, strongest in adolescence | Interactionists: identity is negotiated with others | Peers can reinforce or oppose family and school norms |
| **Media** | Role models, representations, repeated messages | Albert Bandura's Bobo doll experiments (1961) suggested children imitate behaviour they watch | Active audience views, such as Jay Blumler and Elihu Katz's uses and gratifications approach (1974), say people select and interpret media |
| **Religion** | Moral codes, ritual, sacred texts, community | Émile Durkheim: shared ritual strengthens the collective conscience | Influence depends on how religious a society is |

Each agency is also an agency of **social control**: the same rewards and punishments that teach norms also enforce them.

## Theories of socialisation

**Functionalist.** Socialisation passes on a shared value system, which individuals internalise so that they *want* to do what society needs. Dennis Wrong's article "The Oversocialized Conception of Man in Modern Sociology" (1961) criticised this for treating people as if they absorbed society's values completely and had no will of their own.

**Marxist and feminist.** Socialisation is not neutral: it passes on ideology that benefits the ruling class (Marxist) or men (feminist). The question becomes whose values are being taught.

**Interactionist.** The self is built through interaction. Charles Horton Cooley's "looking-glass self" (1902) proposed that we come to see ourselves as we imagine others see us. George Herbert Mead (*Mind, Self, and Society*, 1934) described children moving from the *play* stage, taking the role of one significant other, to the *game* stage, understanding the expectations of the "generalised other". Socialisation is therefore active and ongoing, not simply received.

## Worked example: a two-explanations answer

*Question (practice): Explain two ways in which the peer group acts as an agency of socialisation.*

> **Way 1: through acceptance and rejection.** Young people want the approval of friends, so they adopt the group's norms of dress, speech and attitudes to school. Those who break the norms may be teased or excluded, which is an informal negative sanction. So the peer group both teaches and enforces norms.
>
> **Way 2: through role models and identity.** Interactionists argue that identity is negotiated in everyday encounters. In a peer group a teenager tries out roles, sees how others react, and adjusts, in the way Cooley's looking-glass self describes. This can create norms that differ from those of the family, showing secondary socialisation working alongside, or against, primary socialisation.

Why it works: each way names a process, explains how it shapes behaviour, and links to a concept or theory (sanctions, looking-glass self).

## Common mistakes

- Defining norms and values without showing the link between them.
- Treating nature versus nurture as one-sided; the strongest answers argue for interaction and evaluate both.
- Describing agencies with no mention of social control, which the syllabus names alongside socialisation.
- Presenting socialisation as passive when interactionists stress negotiation.
- Using a study without saying what it shows about socialisation.
