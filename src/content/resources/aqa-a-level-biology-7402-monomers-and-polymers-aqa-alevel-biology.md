This guide goes deeper into **3.1.1 Monomers and polymers** from the AQA AS and A-level Biology specification (7401/7402), first teaching 2015. It is the opening sub-topic of 3.1 Biological molecules and is examined at AS and A-level. Short as it is, every later sub-topic on carbohydrates, proteins and nucleic acids uses its ideas, so marks on it turn up across whole papers.

The [monomers, polymers and carbohydrates study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-biology-monomers-polymers-and-carbohydrates/) defines the terms and works one chain-of-glucose formula. This page adds what that guide does not: where the water in a condensation reaction comes from, how to recognise the bond in an unfamiliar diagram, what changes for branched and circular molecules, and how hydrolysis links the topic to digestion and to cell metabolism. Condense it with the [3.1.1 revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-biology-7402-monomers-and-polymers-aqa-alevel-biology-revision-notes/) and test yourself with the [3.1.1 practice questions](/resources/aqa-a-level-biology-7402-monomers-and-polymers-aqa-alevel-biology-practice/).

## What the specification says

The specification makes five statements:

- Monomers are the smaller units from which larger molecules are made.
- Polymers are molecules made from a large number of monomers joined together.
- Monosaccharides, amino acids and nucleotides are examples of monomers.
- A condensation reaction joins two molecules together with the formation of a chemical bond and involves the elimination of a molecule of water.
- A hydrolysis reaction breaks a chemical bond between two molecules and involves the use of a water molecule.

It also opens the topic by noting that the biochemical basis of life is similar for all living things. That is why the same three monomer families and the same two reactions appear in bacteria, plants and animals.

## Three polymer families, one chemistry

| Monomer | Polymer | Bond formed by condensation | Later sub-topic |
|---|---|---|---|
| Monosaccharide | Polysaccharide | Glycosidic | 3.1.2 |
| Amino acid | Polypeptide | Peptide | 3.1.4 |
| Nucleotide | Polynucleotide | Phosphodiester | 3.1.5 |

Each polymer is built by repeating one kind of reaction between one family of monomers. The order of monomers can vary (as in proteins and DNA) or the monomer can be the same throughout (as in starch, built only from α-glucose), but the bond within a polymer is always the same type.

### Where the water comes from

In a condensation reaction, an **–OH group** on one molecule and an **–H atom** on the other are removed and leave as H₂O. The two molecules are then joined by the new bond.

- **Glycosidic bond:** –OH groups on two monosaccharides react; one loses –OH and the other loses –H, leaving a C–O–C link.
- **Peptide bond:** the –OH of the carboxyl group of one amino acid and an –H from the amine group of the next leave; carbon bonds to nitrogen.
- **Phosphodiester bond:** the phosphate group of one nucleotide reacts with an –OH on the sugar of the next.

In hydrolysis, a water molecule is split. Its –OH and –H go back on to the two separated molecules, restoring the original groups. This is why mass increases on hydrolysis and decreases on condensation.

## Not every large molecule is a polymer

**Triglycerides** are made by condensation, forming three ester bonds between glycerol and three fatty acids. But they are not polymers: there is no chain of many repeating monomers, only one glycerol and three fatty acids. Calling lipids polymers is one of the most common errors in this topic. The [3.1.3 lipids guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-biology-7402-lipids-aqa-alevel-biology/) covers them in detail.

Equally, **dipeptides** and **disaccharides** are made by condensation but are not polymers, because two units are not "a large number of monomers".

## Counting bonds in different shapes

The rule "n monomers, n − 1 bonds" holds for a single unbranched chain. It also holds for a branched molecule, because each extra monomer, wherever it attaches, adds exactly one bond. It changes in two cases.

| Shape | Bonds for n monomers | Water released when built |
|---|---|---|
| Single chain (branched or not) | n − 1 | n − 1 |
| Closed ring (circular molecule) | n | n |
| Two separate chains of m and k units | (m − 1) + (k − 1) | m + k − 2 |

A **circular** polynucleotide, such as the circular DNA found in prokaryotic cells (3.2.1.2), has its two ends joined, so one extra bond is formed. Read the question for the word "circular" before you subtract one.

## Worked example 1: mass change on hydrolysis

A plant polysaccharide is a single chain of 1,200 α-glucose units. Take Mr of glucose as 180 and of water as 18. Calculate the Mr of the polysaccharide and the percentage increase in mass when it is fully hydrolysed.

1. Bonds = 1,200 − 1 = 1,199, so 1,199 water molecules were removed.
2. Mr = (1,200 × 180) − (1,199 × 18) = 216,000 − 21,582 = **194,418**.
3. Full hydrolysis puts back 1,199 water molecules, giving 216,000.
4. Percentage increase = 21,582 ÷ 194,418 × 100 = **11.1%** (to 3 significant figures).

The mass of the products is greater than the mass of the polymer, because water has been added. A common error is to say "mass is conserved, so nothing changes"; mass is conserved only when the water is counted.

## Worked example 2: a circular molecule

A circular polynucleotide contains 5,000 nucleotides. How many phosphodiester bonds does it contain, and how many water molecules are used when it is completely hydrolysed to nucleotides?

1. The molecule is a closed ring, so bonds = n = **5,000**.
2. Each bond needs one water molecule to break: **5,000** water molecules.

If the same 5,000 nucleotides were in one linear chain, the answer would be 4,999 each time.

## Recognising the bond in an unfamiliar diagram

Exam questions often show a molecule you have not seen and ask you to name the bond or the monomers. Use these clues.

| What you see between units | Bond | Monomer family |
|---|---|---|
| Two sugar rings joined through a single oxygen (C–O–C) | Glycosidic | Monosaccharides |
| C=O next to N–H in the backbone | Peptide | Amino acids |
| A phosphate linking two sugars in a chain, with a base on each sugar | Phosphodiester | Nucleotides |
| C=O next to an O, joining a chain to glycerol | Ester | Not a polymer: lipid |

To find the monomers, "add water back" across each bond in the diagram: put –OH on one side and –H on the other, then read off the separate units.

## Hydrolysis in organisms

Hydrolysis is not just a test-tube reaction. Enzymes catalyse it in many places you meet later:

- **Digestion (3.3.3):** large food molecules such as starch and proteins are hydrolysed to monomers small enough to be absorbed.
- **Lysosomes (3.2.1.1):** hydrolytic enzymes break down worn-out organelles and engulfed pathogens.
- **Energy release (3.1.6):** ATP is hydrolysed to ADP and inorganic phosphate.
- **Storage:** glycogen in liver and muscle cells is hydrolysed to glucose when respiratory substrate is needed.

Condensation reactions in cells are equally widespread: protein synthesis at ribosomes, DNA replication and building storage polysaccharides all join monomers by condensation, and each reaction is catalysed rather than happening by chance.

## Exam technique for AQA command words

- **Define / Give:** use the specification's wording. For condensation, include both "forms a chemical bond" and "eliminates (releases) a molecule of water".
- **Describe:** say what happens in order, for example "water is used; the bond between the monomers breaks; two monomers are released".
- **Explain:** give the reason, for example why hydrolysis increases the mass of the products.
- **Calculate:** write the bond count first, then the masses, and keep units consistent. State any rounding.
- **Suggest:** apply the ideas to an unfamiliar molecule, such as a polymer of a monomer you have not met.

## Common mistakes

- Saying hydrolysis releases water. It uses water.
- Calling triglycerides, disaccharides or dipeptides polymers.
- Subtracting one bond for a circular molecule.
- Writing that condensation "breaks" bonds or that hydrolysis "joins" molecules.
- Naming the bond after the polymer, such as "a starch bond", instead of glycosidic.

## Frequently asked questions

**What are the three examples of monomers in AQA A-level Biology?**
Monosaccharides, amino acids and nucleotides. They form polysaccharides, polypeptides and polynucleotides respectively.

**Are lipids polymers?**
No. Triglycerides are formed by condensation of one glycerol and three fatty acids, but they are not made of many repeating monomers, so they are not polymers.

**How many water molecules are released when a polymer is made?**
For a single chain of n monomers, n − 1, whether or not it is branched. For a circular molecule of n monomers, n.

**Why does the mass of the products increase on hydrolysis?**
Each bond broken uses one water molecule, and its atoms become part of the products.
