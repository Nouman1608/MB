This guide covers section **3.1.6 Business planning** of OxfordAQA International GCSE Business (9225), part of Topic 1, Business in the real world. The specification asks you to understand why businesses create plans, including their importance in **setting up a new business, raising finance, setting objectives and detailing how the functions of a business will be organised**; to know the **main sections of a business plan**; to analyse the **benefits and drawbacks of business planning**; and to understand **variable costs, fixed costs, total costs, revenue, profit and loss**. You will not be asked to write a full business plan. All businesses and figures in this guide are invented.

For the whole topic, see the [Business in the Real World study guide](/resources/igcse-oxfordaqa-business-business-in-the-real-world/). Condensed notes are in the [revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-business-9225-business-planning-revision-notes/) and questions are in the [practice set](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-business-9225-business-planning-practice/).

## What is a business plan?

A **business plan** is a written document that sets out what a business intends to do, how it will do it and how much money it expects to make. It is written by the owner or owners, usually before the business starts, and updated as the business develops. It turns an idea into a set of decisions.

## Why do businesses plan?

| Reason | Explanation |
|---|---|
| Setting up a new business | Planning forces the owner to think through the product, customers, costs and risks before spending money |
| Raising finance | Banks and investors usually ask to see a plan before they lend. It shows that the business can repay and is worth backing |
| Setting objectives | The plan states what the business aims to achieve, giving targets against which to judge progress |
| Organising the functions of the business | It explains who will do what: how products will be made or bought, who will be hired and how customers will be reached |

A plan also helps the owner to spot problems early, for example that expected sales will not cover the costs.

## The main sections of a business plan

A business plan usually includes the following sections. Names vary, so concentrate on what each section does.

| Section | What it contains |
|---|---|
| Executive summary | A short overview of the whole plan: the idea, the market, the money needed and the expected return |
| Business description | What the business sells, its legal structure, its owner and its location |
| Aims and objectives | The short- and long-term targets the business wants to reach |
| Market research and target market | Who the customers are, how many there are, who the competitors are and what customers want |
| Marketing plan | The product, price, promotion and place, and how the business will reach customers |
| Operations plan | How goods or services will be produced, which premises, equipment and suppliers are needed |
| Human resources plan | How many staff are needed, what skills they require and how they will be recruited and paid |
| Financial plan | Start-up costs, sales forecasts, cash flow forecasts, expected costs and profit, and how the owner will fund the business |

The financial plan is often the most important part for a lender. It shows how much money is needed, what it will be used for and when it will be repaid.

## Benefits and drawbacks of business planning

| Benefits | Drawbacks |
|---|---|
| Helps the owner to think clearly about the idea and the risks | Takes time and effort, which an owner could spend on running the business |
| Makes it easier to raise finance from a bank or investor | Forecasts may be wrong, especially for a new business with no sales record |
| Sets clear targets to measure progress | A plan can become out of date quickly if the market changes |
| Helps to avoid costly mistakes, such as setting up without enough demand | Being too rigid can stop the owner taking up new opportunities |
| Helps to coordinate staff and departments | Writing a plan does not guarantee success |

A good plan is regularly reviewed, so that it can be changed when sales or costs turn out differently from the forecasts.

## Basic financial terms

The specification asks you to understand some basic terms. Know the definitions and be able to use them in calculations.

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue (sales revenue) | Money received from selling goods or services | 3,000 loaves at $3 = $9,000 |
| Fixed costs | Costs that do not change with the level of output, in the short term | Rent, insurance, salaries |
| Variable costs | Costs that change directly with output | Flour, packaging, hourly paid labour |
| Total costs | Fixed costs plus variable costs | $2,400 + $3,600 = $6,000 |
| Profit | Revenue minus total costs, when revenue is higher | $9,000 - $6,000 = $3,000 |
| Loss | Total costs minus revenue, when costs are higher | $3,600 - $3,000 = $600 loss |

### Formulae to remember

- Revenue = selling price x quantity sold
- Variable costs = variable cost per unit x quantity
- Total costs = fixed costs + variable costs
- Profit (or loss) = revenue - total costs

You will not be given formulae in the exam, so learn them.

**Worked example 1: a profitable month.** Naledi's bakery has fixed costs of $2,400 a month. Flour and packaging cost $1.20 a loaf. She sells 3,000 loaves at $3 each.

```
Revenue        = 3,000 x $3.00  = $9,000
Variable costs = 3,000 x $1.20  = $3,600
Total costs    = $2,400 + $3,600 = $6,000
Profit         = $9,000 - $6,000 = $3,000
```

**Worked example 2: a loss.** In a quiet month Naledi sells only 1,000 loaves.

```
Revenue        = 1,000 x $3.00  = $3,000
Variable costs = 1,000 x $1.20  = $1,200
Total costs    = $2,400 + $1,200 = $3,600
Loss           = $3,600 - $3,000 = $600
```

Fixed costs stay at $2,400 even in a quiet month, so the loss is $600. The more fixed costs a business has, the more risk it faces when sales are low.

**Worked example 3: how a forecast can mislead.** Naledi's plan forecasts 3,000 loaves a month. The actual figure is 2,200 loaves, a shortfall of 800 loaves, or 26.7% below plan (800 / 3,000). Revenue is 2,200 x $3 = $6,600, variable costs are 2,200 x $1.20 = $2,640 and total costs are $5,040, so profit is $1,560. This is $1,440 below the planned $3,000. A plan that assumed too many sales would have caused problems if she had borrowed money on the basis of the forecast. A good plan includes a cautious scenario as well as a hopeful one.

## Using the plan

Once a business has started, the plan is used to:

1. **compare actual results with the forecast** and find out why they differ
2. **decide what to change**, such as lowering costs or increasing marketing
3. **show lenders** that the business is performing as expected
4. **review objectives** as the business grows.

## Worked exam-style answer

**Question (6 marks):** Analyse the benefits and drawbacks of a start-up writing a business plan.

**Answer:** A plan makes the owner think through the product, customers and costs before spending money. A smoothie stall that needs $18,000 can show a bank forecast sales of 2,500 cups and a profit of $3,500 a month, which makes a loan more likely. The plan also sets targets, so the owner can see quickly if sales of only 1,200 cups, which give a profit of just $120, are below plan. The drawbacks are that writing the plan takes time and that forecasts for a new business are uncertain, so a plan based on over-optimistic sales may mislead the owner. Overall, a plan is worth writing, provided its forecasts are cautious and it is reviewed regularly.

## Common mistakes

- Writing that a business plan guarantees success. It only improves the chance of success.
- Mixing up revenue and profit. Revenue is the sales income, and profit is what is left after total costs.
- Treating rent as a variable cost. Rent is fixed in the short term.
- Forgetting that fixed costs must be paid even when no sales are made.
- Listing the sections of a plan without saying what each does.
- Saying only that a plan "helps get a loan" without saying why a lender wants it.

## Exam technique for this section

For **state** and **identify** questions, give the section or term. For **explain** questions, link a section to a purpose: "the financial plan shows the bank how much money is needed and when the loan can be repaid". For **calculate** questions, show each step: revenue, then costs, then profit. For **evaluate** questions, such as whether a new business should write a plan, weigh the time cost and the risk of inaccurate forecasts against the benefits for raising finance and avoiding mistakes, then decide on the evidence given.
