**HL only.** This is the second study in the Regional study for the Americas, examined on Paper 3 at Higher Level. Paper 3 questions ask you to evaluate an argument and reach a judgement. This study covers a very wide span, circa 1492 to 1830, and brings together European conquest, the exploitation of Indigenous labour, the system of transatlantic slavery, resistance and the first organised opposition to slavery. You can refer to several colonial powers, and you need examples from more than one.

## The six lines of inquiry

The guide names six inquiry topics: the initial impact of European exploration; early treatment of Indigenous peoples; the reasons for the development of transatlantic slavery; its impact; resistance; and early opposition. Questions often ask about **cause and consequence**, **continuity and change** and **perspectives**. See the [four concepts guide](/resources/ib-dp-history-four-concepts/) for how to use them.

## Key chronology

- 1492: Columbus reaches the Caribbean.
- 1494: Treaty of Tordesillas divides new lands between Spain and Portugal.
- 1512: Laws of Burgos regulate the encomienda.
- 1519 to 1521: Cortés and the fall of the Aztec capital.
- 1532 to 1533: Pizarro and the fall of the Inca state.
- 1545: silver found at Potosí, followed by Zacatecas in 1546.
- 1542: New Laws limit the encomienda.
- 1607: English settlement at Jamestown. Tobacco soon became its main crop.
- 1685: French Code Noir regulates slavery in French colonies.
- 1791 to 1804: revolution in Saint-Domingue, leading to Haitian independence.
- 1807: British Act ends British participation in the slave trade. 1833 Slavery Abolition Act.

## Initial impact of European exploration

**Conquest.** Small Spanish forces overcame the Aztec and Inca states. Their success was not simply due to weapons. Allies were vital: Tlaxcala and other peoples who resented Aztec tribute joined Cortés, and a civil war in the Inca state weakened resistance. Epidemic disease, especially smallpox, killed very large numbers and disrupted leadership and defence. This is a good example of **multiple, interrelated causes**.

**Resources.** Spain's colonies became centres of mining. Silver from Potosí (in modern Bolivia) and Zacatecas (Mexico) made up a large share of Spanish imperial wealth, and flowed through the Atlantic fleets, and across the Pacific, into global trade. Gold was important in the early Caribbean, then in Brazil from the 1690s.

**Other resources.** French traders in northern North America dealt in fur, working closely with Indigenous hunters and trappers. English colonies in the Chesapeake grew tobacco. Sugar, cultivated first by the Portuguese in Brazil and then across the Caribbean, was the most valuable crop for much of the period.

**The Columbian Exchange.** This term describes the movement of plants, animals, people and diseases between the hemispheres. Maize, potatoes, tomatoes, cacao and tobacco spread to Europe and beyond. Wheat, sugar cane, horses, cattle and pigs spread to the Americas. Disease spread too, and Indigenous populations fell sharply, in some regions by a large majority over about a century. Historians disagree about the exact figures, and about how far to blame disease as opposed to violence, forced labour and famine. The older "Black Legend" overstated Spanish cruelty as unique. Some later writers have overstated disease to minimise human responsibility. A good answer holds both in view.

## Early treatment of Indigenous peoples

**Labour systems.** The Spanish crown granted **encomiendas** to settlers: the right to collect tribute and labour from a community, in return for protection and instruction in Christianity. In practice, abuse was widespread. The Laws of Burgos (1512) and the New Laws (1542) tried to limit it, influenced by critics such as the friar Bartolomé de las Casas. In the Andes, the **mita** adapted the Inca labour draft for colonial mining, notably at Potosí, from the 1570s. **Yanaconaje** referred to dependent servants who were tied to a landholder or mine owner (in the Andes, the term came from the Inca era). In Mexico, **repartimiento** was a similar rotating draft system. Over time, many Indigenous workers were replaced or supplemented by wage workers, debt-bonded labourers and enslaved Africans.

**Social impact.** Colonial society was stratified by origin and mixture: Spanish-born officials (peninsulares), American-born Spaniards (criollos), mixed-descent groups (often called castas), Indigenous people, and enslaved or free people of African descent. Missions and religious orders, including Franciscans, Dominicans and Jesuits, worked to convert Indigenous people, and resettled many in towns. Assimilation was uneven. Indigenous communities kept languages, local authority and customs, and adapted Christianity in their own ways.

**Plantations.** Early plantations used Indigenous labour in the Caribbean, but the population collapse there led to a search for other workers.

**Indigenous women.** Women faced forced labour in workshops (**obrajes**) and households, and sexual exploitation. Some, such as Malinche, who served as Cortés's interpreter, held roles that historians still debate. Women also kept family and cultural traditions alive and went to court to defend land and rights.

## Reasons for the development of transatlantic slavery

**Economic factors.** Sugar and other plantation crops needed large, controllable labour forces. Indigenous populations had fallen, and European indentured servants were limited in number and could leave after a fixed term. The Portuguese were already trading in enslaved Africans along the African coast from the 15th century. Profit was the major driver. In his book *Capitalism and Slavery* (1944), Eric Williams argued that the economic needs of planters led to slavery and that racism followed it as a justification. Other historians put more weight on prejudice that already existed before the Atlantic trade.

**Role of ideas.** Religious arguments, including the claim that enslaved people were "heathens" or were descendants of a cursed line, and developing racial theories, justified slavery. By the 18th century, ideas about racial hierarchy were more fixed.

**Political factors.** States granted monopolies (the Spanish **asiento** contracts to supply enslaved people; the British Royal African Company) and protected trade with navies and laws. Colonial legislation defined enslaved people as property. In Virginia, laws passed in the 17th century made slavery hereditary. The French Code Noir (1685) regulated slavery and gave some limited rights alongside harsh punishments.

**Different colonial powers.** Portugal led the early trade and took Brazil's sugar economy to scale. Spain used Africans in Caribbean, Mexican and Andean economies, but mostly bought them from other carriers. The Dutch won ground in the 17th century, briefly in north-east Brazil and then in Suriname and the Caribbean. France developed Saint-Domingue into the richest sugar colony of the 18th century. Britain became the largest carrier in the 18th century, with Jamaica and Barbados as major colonies. In all, more than 10 million enslaved Africans arrived in the Americas, and many more died in capture and transit.

## Impact of the system

**Economic and political.** Slavery produced sugar, tobacco, coffee, cotton and gold. It enriched merchants, planters and ports in Europe and America, though historians debate how far it caused European industrialisation. The planter class held great political power, especially in British Caribbean assemblies and, later, in the southern United States.

**Social and cultural.** African cultures shaped language, religion, food, music and craft across the Americas. Examples include Candomblé in Brazil, Vodou in Haiti and Obeah in the British Caribbean. Free people of colour formed a distinct group in many colonies. For Indigenous societies, the expansion of plantations pushed them off land and into conflict.

**Conditions.** Work on sugar plantations was brutal. Mortality was high, and slave populations in the sugar colonies often did not sustain themselves without new imports. Punishments were severe. Housing, diet and medical care were poor.

**Women.** Enslaved women worked in the fields and in houses, and were vulnerable to sexual violence. In many colonies, a child's status followed the mother's, which made women's bodies a source of profit for owners. Women also formed networks, passed on cultural knowledge, and took part in resistance.

## Resistance by enslaved peoples

- **Day-to-day resistance**: slowing work, breaking tools, feigning illness, theft, running away for short periods.
- **Cultural resistance**: keeping African religions and languages, creating new Creole forms, music, storytelling and family networks.
- **Rebellions**: Tacky's War in Jamaica (1760), the Berbice uprising (1763), the Haitian Revolution (from 1791), and rebellions in Demerara (1823). Most were crushed, but their effect on slave owners' fear was lasting. Haiti was the only one to create a new state.
- **Escape and communities**: **maroon** communities in Jamaica and Suriname, and **quilombos** in Brazil, notably Palmares, which survived through much of the 17th century before being destroyed in 1694. Some maroons won treaties with colonial governments.

## Emergence and growth of early opposition

Quakers were among the earliest organised critics, with a petition from Germantown in 1688. Enlightenment thinkers questioned slavery on grounds of natural rights, though not all applied these principles consistently. In Britain, the legal case of James Somerset (1772) was understood to limit slavery in England. From the 1780s, committees and societies led by Quakers, evangelical Christians and others, including Thomas Clarkson and William Wilberforce, organised petitions and boycotts. **Formerly enslaved people** such as Olaudah Equiano, whose autobiography appeared in 1789, gave powerful testimony. **Technology**: the printing press spread pamphlets and images, such as the plan of the slave ship *Brookes* (1788), which showed how people were packed on board. Historians now give much more weight to the actions of enslaved people themselves, including the Haitian Revolution, in forcing the issue.

## Arguments for Paper 3

- Did disease or conquest destroy Indigenous societies? Most historians stress both, with disease as a major factor.
- Was slavery caused by economics or by racism? Williams versus those who see racism as prior.
- Did abolition result from moral change or economic change? Williams argued that slavery had become unprofitable by the early 19th century, and that abolition followed. Seymour Drescher argued that slavery was still profitable, and that abolitionist action and slave resistance were key.
- Who ended slavery: reformers, or the enslaved?

## Common mistakes

- Treating Spanish, Portuguese, French, Dutch and British systems as the same.
- Describing cruelty without analysing causes or impact.
- Ignoring Indigenous experience when the question covers both Indigenous peoples and Africans.
- Writing about the US Civil War or later emancipation. This study stops at about 1830.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need all colonial powers?**
No, but know at least two well, and one example of each type of labour system.

**How should I use historians?**
Use two or three clear positions, such as Williams and Drescher, and explain how they differ.
