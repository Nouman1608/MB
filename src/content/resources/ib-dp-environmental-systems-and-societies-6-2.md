## Forcing: what pushes climate to change

A **climate forcing** is a factor that changes the balance between energy coming into the Earth system and energy leaving it. A positive forcing causes warming; a negative one causes cooling. Feedbacks then amplify or dampen the response.

### Natural forcings

- **Orbital (Milankovitch) cycles.** Slow changes in the shape of Earth's orbit (eccentricity, about 100 000 years), the tilt of its axis (obliquity, about 41 000 years) and the wobble of the axis (precession, roughly 20 000 years) change how sunlight is distributed by season and latitude. They paced the ice ages, with feedbacks from CO₂ and ice amplifying their small direct effect.
- **Solar output.** The Sun's energy output varies slightly, including over an 11-year cycle.
- **Volcanic eruptions.** Large eruptions inject sulfur dioxide into the stratosphere, forming sulfate aerosols that reflect sunlight. The 1991 eruption of Mount Pinatubo in the Philippines cooled global temperatures temporarily. The effect lasts only a year or two because the aerosols settle out.

### Human forcings

- Greenhouse gas emissions (positive forcing, the largest).
- Aerosols from burning fuels (mostly negative, partly masking warming).
- Land-use change, such as replacing forest with brighter cropland (changes albedo) and releasing carbon.

## How we know current warming is human-caused

Several lines of evidence point to greenhouse gases rather than natural forcings:

- **Timing.** Orbital cycles act over many thousands of years; recent warming has happened over about 150 years, closely following the rise in CO₂.
- **Solar output.** Measured solar output has not risen since the mid-twentieth century, while temperatures have.
- **The stratosphere is cooling.** If the Sun were brighter, both the lower and upper atmosphere would warm. Instead, the troposphere is warming while the stratosphere cools, which is the pattern expected when greenhouse gases trap more heat lower down.
- **Isotopes.** Carbon from fossil fuels has a distinctive isotopic signature, and the changing mix of carbon isotopes in the air shows that the added CO₂ comes from fossil sources.
- **Models.** Climate models reproduce observed warming only when human forcings are included.

The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC), set up in 1988 by the World Meteorological Organization and the UN Environment Programme, assesses this evidence. Its Sixth Assessment Report concluded that it is unequivocal that human influence has warmed the atmosphere, ocean and land.

## Where emitted carbon goes

Only part of the CO₂ emitted stays in the atmosphere. Roughly half of human emissions are taken up each year by **sinks**: the oceans (CO₂ dissolves in seawater) and land ecosystems (extra plant growth). The rest accumulates in the air.

This matters for two reasons. First, the sinks are not guaranteed: warmer water dissolves less CO₂, and drought and fire can turn forests from sinks into sources. Second, the ocean sink has a cost: it causes acidification.

### Worked example: the rate of CO₂ rise

A fictional monitoring station recorded annual mean CO₂ concentrations.

| Year | 1960 | 1990 | 2020 |
|---|---|---|---|
| CO₂ / ppm | 317 | 354 | 413 |

```
1960–1990: (354 − 317) ÷ 30 years = 1.23 ppm per year
1990–2020: (413 − 354) ÷ 30 years = 1.97 ppm per year
rise since a pre-industrial value of 280 ppm:
(413 − 280) ÷ 280 × 100 = 47.5 %
```

**Interpretation.** The concentration is not only rising but rising faster, because emissions grew faster than the sinks' uptake. A "describe" answer should quote both rates with units and say that the trend is accelerating.

## Lifetimes and why they matter

Gases stay in the atmosphere for very different lengths of time:

- **Methane** is broken down by chemical reactions in the atmosphere, mostly within about a decade. Cutting methane emissions therefore slows warming quickly.
- **Carbon dioxide** has no single lifetime: much is absorbed by sinks over decades to centuries, but a significant fraction remains for thousands of years. Warming from CO₂ is therefore effectively permanent on human time scales, and depends on **cumulative** emissions.
- **Nitrous oxide** and many **halocarbons** persist for around a century or longer.

This is why global warming potential depends on the time horizon chosen: over 20 years methane looks far more powerful relative to CO₂ than over 100 years.

## Comparing countries fairly

### Worked example: total, per person and cumulative emissions

Three fictional countries have the following annual emissions.

| Country | Annual emissions / Mt CO₂ | Population / millions |
|---|---|---|
| Arlo | 5000 | 330 |
| Bexa | 10 000 | 1400 |
| Corin | 30 | 12 |

```
per person = annual emissions ÷ population (t per person, since Mt ÷ millions = t)
Arlo:  5000 ÷ 330   = 15.2 t per person
Bexa:  10 000 ÷ 1400 = 7.1 t per person
Corin: 30 ÷ 12      = 2.5 t per person
```

**Interpretation.** Bexa emits the most in total, but each person in Arlo emits more than twice as much as each person in Bexa, and about six times as much as each person in Corin. If Arlo industrialised a century earlier, its **cumulative** (historical) emissions are larger still. Which measure is used shapes arguments about responsibility: total emissions focus on present-day large emitters; per-person and cumulative emissions focus on wealthy countries that industrialised early.

## Tipping elements

A **tipping element** is a large part of the Earth system that could pass a threshold and shift to a new state, often irreversibly on human time scales. Examples identified by researchers include the Greenland and West Antarctic ice sheets, the Amazon rainforest, permafrost, the Atlantic overturning circulation and warm-water coral reefs. Crossing one can trigger others: melting Greenland ice freshens the North Atlantic and may weaken the overturning circulation, which in turn affects rainfall in the tropics. The uncertainty about where thresholds lie is an argument for precaution.

## Ocean acidification

When CO₂ dissolves in seawater it forms carbonic acid, which releases hydrogen ions and lowers pH:

```
CO₂ + H₂O → H₂CO₃ → H⁺ + HCO₃⁻
```

The extra hydrogen ions also combine with carbonate ions, reducing the carbonate that corals, molluscs and some plankton need to build shells and skeletons. Acidification is caused by CO₂ itself, not by warming, so measures that reflect sunlight without cutting CO₂ would not stop it.

## Impacts: uneven and interacting

Impacts are shaped by **exposure** (being in harm's way), **sensitivity** (how strongly a system is affected) and **adaptive capacity** (the ability to cope).

- **Low-lying small island states** face sea-level rise and storm surges, with little high ground to retreat to.
- **Arctic communities** face thawing permafrost under buildings and roads, and loss of sea ice used for travel and hunting.
- **Farming in drylands** faces more intense droughts and shifting rainy seasons.
- **Ecosystems** face timing mismatches: if plants flower or insects emerge earlier but migrating birds arrive at the same time, food is missing when chicks hatch.
- **Health** impacts include heat stress and changing ranges of disease-carrying insects.

Poorer countries and communities often have the lowest emissions per person and the least adaptive capacity, which is the basis of climate justice arguments. HL students can apply the environmental law, economics and ethics lenses to these questions.

## Model answer: explain

**Question.** Explain why cooling of the stratosphere is evidence that recent warming is caused by greenhouse gases rather than by the Sun. **[3]**

**Answer.** A brighter Sun would add energy to the whole atmosphere, so both the troposphere and stratosphere would warm [1]. More greenhouse gases instead absorb more outgoing infrared in the lower atmosphere, so less reaches the stratosphere, and greenhouse gases in the stratosphere emit more energy to space [1]. The observed pattern of a warming troposphere and cooling stratosphere matches the greenhouse explanation, not the solar one [1].

## Common mistakes

- Saying that climate "has always changed, so humans are not responsible", without addressing timing, solar data and fingerprints.
- Treating the sinks as fixed; they weaken with warming.
- Confusing ocean acidification with warming; acidification comes from dissolved CO₂.
- Comparing countries on total emissions only.
- Describing a tipping point as any large change; it is a threshold beyond which change becomes self-sustaining.
- Listing impacts without saying who is affected and why they are vulnerable.

## Exam technique

- **Describe** a CO₂ or temperature graph: quote values, rates and units; note acceleration.
- **Explain** evidence: say what the evidence is and what it rules out.
- **Calculate** per-person emissions and rates of change; check units.
- **Discuss** responsibility: use total, per-person and cumulative emissions and reach a judgement.
