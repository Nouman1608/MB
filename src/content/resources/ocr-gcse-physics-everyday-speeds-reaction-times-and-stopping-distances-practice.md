## Questions

**1.** Give a typical speed in m/s for (a) a person walking and (b) sound in air. **[2]**

**2.** Convert (a) 126 km/h to m/s and (b) 4.5 m/s to km/h. **[2]**

**3.** A van travels at 45 mph. Calculate its speed in m/s to 2 significant figures. 1 mile = 1609 m. **[3]**

**4.** A cyclist rides 15 km in 50 minutes. Calculate the average speed in m/s. **[3]**

**5.** A motorbike accelerates from rest to 18 m/s in 6.0 s.
**(a)** Calculate its acceleration. **[2]**
**(b)** Compare this with the acceleration of a freely falling object, about 10 m/s². **[1]**

**6.** Estimate the acceleration of a person who starts from rest and reaches a normal walking speed. State the values you use. **[3]**

**7.** A student measures reaction time by catching a dropped ruler. The distances caught are 13, 17, 15, 16 and 14 cm. Part of the conversion table is: 10 cm = 0.14 s, 15 cm = 0.18 s, 20 cm = 0.20 s.
**(a)** Calculate the mean distance. **[2]**
**(b)** Use the table to give the reaction time. **[1]**
**(c)** Suggest two ways to make the results more reliable. **[2]**

**8.** A computer test records the time between a symbol appearing and a mouse click.
**(a)** Describe what the computer measures. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain why this is better than one student timing another with a stopwatch. **[2]**

**9.** A student measures four reaction times without a phone: 0.20, 0.22, 0.24 and 0.22 s. With a phone call, the times are 0.30, 0.32, 0.29 and 0.33 s.
**(a)** Calculate the mean for each condition. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage increase in mean reaction time when using the phone. **[2]**
**(c)** A car moves at 20 m/s. Calculate the extra distance travelled during the increase in reaction time. **[2]**

**10.** For each change, state whether it affects thinking distance, braking distance or both. **(a)** a tired driver; **(b)** an icy road; **(c)** a lower speed. **[3]**

**11.** Explain why, for the same speed:
**(a)** the braking distance is greater on a wet road than a dry road. **[3]**
**(b)** a heavily loaded van has a greater braking distance than an empty van with the same brakes. **[3]**

**12.** A council plans to cut the speed limit outside a school from 30 mph to 20 mph. 1 mile = 1609 m.
**(a)** Convert both limits to m/s, to 2 significant figures. **[2]**
**(b)** A driver has a reaction time of 0.70 s. Calculate the thinking distance at each limit, using the speeds from part (a). **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why the braking distance falls by more than the speed does in proportion. **[2]**
**(d)** Suggest one other measure to reduce the risk to pedestrians, and state which distance it changes. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** (a) About 1.5 m/s, accept 1 to 2 m/s [1]. (b) About 340 m/s, accept 330 to 345 m/s [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Give the unit with each value. A walking speed quoted as 5 with no unit is ambiguous, as 5 km/h and 5 m/s are very different.

**2.** (a) 126 ÷ 3.6 = **35 m/s** [1]. (b) 4.5 × 3.6 = **16.2 km/h** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Check the direction: a speed in km/h should be the bigger number. If it is not, the wrong operation has been used.

**3.** 45 × 1609 = 72,405 m per hour [1]. 72,405 ÷ 3600 = 20.1 m/s [1]. **20 m/s** to 2 s.f. [1] **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Show both the distance and the time conversion. Giving an unrounded 20.1125 as well as the rounded answer is a safe way to show you followed the instruction on significant figures.

**4.** Distance = 15 × 1000 = 15,000 m [1]. Time = 50 × 60 = 3000 s [1]. Speed = 15,000 ÷ 3000 = **5.0 m/s** [1] **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Dividing 15 by 50 gives a number in km per minute. Convert both quantities before dividing.

**5.** (a) a = Δv ÷ t = 18 ÷ 6.0 [1] = **3.0 m/s²** [1]. (b) It is about 0.30 times, or less than a third of, the free-fall value [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Start from rest means the change in velocity is the final speed. A comparison is clearer with a number such as "0.30 times" than a bare "smaller".

**6.** Final speed about 1.5 m/s, change in speed from rest 1.5 m/s [1]. Time about 2 s [1]. a = 1.5 ÷ 2 = **about 0.75 m/s²**, so roughly 1 m/s² [1] (accept 0.5 to 1.5 m/s² from sensible values). **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* An estimate is judged on whether the inputs are sensible and the arithmetic is correct. Always state the values you assumed.

**7.** (a) (13 + 17 + 15 + 16 + 14) = 75 [1]; 75 ÷ 5 = **15 cm** [1]. (b) 15 cm corresponds to **0.18 s** [1]. (c) Repeat more times [1]; keep the starting position and the catching hand the same, or take the mean of a larger number of results [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Convert the mean distance to a time. Converting each result to a time and then averaging also works, but the table only has some values, so the mean first is simpler.

**8.** (a) The time between the stimulus appearing and the click (the response) [1]. (b) The timing is electronic [1], so it does not include the extra reaction time of the person with the stopwatch [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Good answers name the source of error. "More accurate" alone gives no reason.

**9.** (a) Without: 0.88 ÷ 4 = **0.22 s** [1]. With: 1.24 ÷ 4 = **0.31 s** [1]. (b) Increase = 0.31 − 0.22 = 0.09 s [1]; 0.09 ÷ 0.22 × 100 = **41%** [1]. (c) Distance = speed × time = 20 × 0.09 [1] = **1.8 m** [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Percentage change is divided by the original value, here 0.22 s, not the new one.

**10.** (a) Thinking distance [1]. (b) Braking distance [1]. (c) Both [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Tiredness changes the driver, so the reaction time; ice changes the road, so the friction. Decide which of the two is affected first.

**11.** (a) Less friction between the tyres and the wet road [1], so the maximum braking force is smaller [1], and the vehicle needs a greater distance to lose its kinetic energy [1]. (b) The loaded van has more mass, so more kinetic energy at the same speed [1]. With the same braking force, more work must be done to stop it [1], and work done = force × distance, so the distance is greater [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Link the cause to the force or the energy. A bare statement such as "it is heavier so it takes longer" does not show the physics.

**12.** (a) 30 mph = 30 × 1609 ÷ 3600 = 13.4, so **13 m/s** [1]. 20 mph = 8.94, so **8.9 m/s** [1]. (b) 13 × 0.70 = **9.1 m** [1]; 8.9 × 0.70 = **6.2 m** [1]. (c) Kinetic energy depends on the square of speed [1], so a smaller speed removes far more than a proportional share of the energy the brakes must remove, shortening the braking distance more than proportionally [1]. (d) Any sensible measure, such as a ban on phone use at the wheel or a crossing patrol [1], with the matching distance: phone use changes thinking distance; better tyres or road surface changes braking distance [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* For an explain question, state the physical principle and apply it to the numbers in the context. A bare statement that "lower speed is safer" is usually too vague to score.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using 60 instead of 3.6 to convert between km/h and m/s.
- Leaving minutes or hours in a speed calculation.
- Failing to give the unit with a value, or the wrong unit.
- Averaging ruler-drop distances but forgetting to convert to a time.
- Dividing a percentage increase by the new value instead of the original.
- Putting tiredness, alcohol, phones or distraction under braking distance.
- Putting wet roads, worn tyres or worn brakes under thinking distance.
- Saying "friction" without linking it to the braking force that can be applied.
- Giving an estimate with no stated values.
