**This guide covers content area 3.2.1.9 of OxfordAQA International GCSE Islamiat (9237), The battles fought by Hazrat Muhammad (pbuh).** The specification asks for "the events and importance of the Battles of Badr, Uhud, Trench, Khaibar, and Hunayn", "the significance of Aam ul wafood", "the details of the Treaty of Hudaibiyah", "the importance of Fath-e-Makkah (conquest of Makkah) and the purification of the Kaaba", and the Prophet's (pbuh) "final victory, including the farewell pilgrimage".

The earlier guide on [Badr, Uhud, the Trench, Khaibar and Hunayn](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-islamiyat-9237-battles-badr-uhud-trench-khaibar-hunayn/) tells each battle as a story. This guide follows the whole journey from Badr to the Farewell Pilgrimage as one argument, gives the treaty and the conquest of Makkah the space they need, and teaches you to compare events by cause, result and lesson.

## The big picture: from defence to forgiveness

The Muslims did not begin the conflict. The Quraysh had persecuted them in Makkah for thirteen years, seized their homes after the Hijrah and still pursued them to Madinah. Permission to fight came in Madinah with the rule that fighting must be defensive and limited: "Fight in the way of Allah those who fight you but do not transgress" (Qur'an 2:190).

Read the events in order and a pattern appears. At Badr the Muslims survive. At Uhud they learn discipline. At the Trench they defend the city by planning rather than by force. At Hudaibiyah they win recognition through patience. At Makkah they win everything and use it to forgive. The sequence matters because exam questions often ask what the events *show*, not only what happened.

| Event | Approximate date | Main side | Result | One-line lesson |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Badr | 17 Ramadhan, 2 AH (624 CE) | Muslims about 313; Quraysh about 1,000 | Muslim victory | Help comes from Allah, not numbers |
| Uhud | Shawwal, 3 AH (625 CE) | Muslims about 700; Quraysh about 3,000 | Heavy Muslim losses | Obedience and discipline matter |
| The Trench | Shawwal, 5 AH (627 CE) | Muslims about 3,000; allies about 10,000 | Siege fails | Consultation and planning protect a community |
| Hudaibiyah | Dhul-Qa'dah, 6 AH (628 CE) | About 1,400 Muslims on pilgrimage | Treaty, no battle | Patience can achieve more than force |
| Khaibar | Early 7 AH (628 CE) | Muslims against fortified Khaibar | Muslim victory | Removing a standing threat |
| Fath-e-Makkah | Ramadhan, 8 AH (630 CE) | About 10,000 Muslims | Makkah opened almost without fighting | Power used with mercy |
| Hunayn | Shawwal, 8 AH (630 CE) | About 12,000 Muslims | Early setback, then victory | Pride is a danger even in strength |
| Farewell Pilgrimage | Dhul-Hijjah, 10 AH (632 CE) | Tens of thousands of pilgrims | The message completed | The legacy of unity and justice |

Numbers and dates are traditional and approximate; learn the order and rough scale.

## Badr and Uhud: survival and discipline

**Badr.** The Muslims set out to meet a Makkan caravan, but it escaped, and a Makkan army marched out instead. The Prophet (pbuh) consulted his companions. The Ansar, who had pledged only to protect him inside Madinah, said they would follow him wherever he went. The Muslims won, and the Qur'an later called the day the *Furqan*, the "criterion" between truth and falsehood (Qur'an 8:41). The captives were treated with kindness, and the literate among them could win their release by teaching Muslim children to read.

**Uhud.** The Quraysh came to take revenge. The Prophet (pbuh) placed archers on a hill and told them to hold their position whatever happened. When the Muslims appeared to be winning, most of the archers left to collect the spoils, and Khalid ibn al-Walid's cavalry attacked from behind. Around seventy Muslims were martyred, including Hazrat Hamza (RA), and the Prophet (pbuh) was wounded. The Qur'an comforted the community: "do not weaken and do not grieve" (Qur'an 3:139).

The prescribed passage for this content area, **Qur'an 3:121-127**, belongs to this section. It recalls the morning at Uhud, when the Prophet (pbuh) posted the believers at their stations, and it looks back to Badr: "already had Allah given you victory at [the battle of] Badr while you were weak." It then promises reinforcement from angels, three thousand and then five thousand, if the believers are patient and conscious of Allah, and says the angels were sent as "good tidings" to reassure the hearts. It closes with the key idea: "victory is not except from Allah, the Exalted in Might, the Wise."

Use the passage to make two linked points in a long answer:

- **Cause and effect:** the promise is conditional on patience and taqwa, which explains why Badr was won and Uhud was not.
- **Source of victory:** angels, numbers and weapons are means; the result belongs to Allah.

## The Trench and Khaibar: defence and security

**The Trench (Ahzab).** Banu Nadir leaders encouraged the Quraysh and other tribes to form an alliance of about 10,000. Hazrat Salman al-Farsi (RA) suggested a trench across the open side of Madinah. The Prophet (pbuh) dug beside the companions and shared their hunger. The cavalry could not cross, the siege dragged on in cold weather, and the alliance broke up. The Quraysh never again attacked Madinah in strength.

**Khaibar.** Khaibar was a group of fortified farming settlements to the north of Madinah. Some of its leaders had helped to organise the alliance against Madinah and were still a danger. The Muslims besieged the fortresses and took them one by one, and Hazrat Ali (RA) is remembered for his part in the final assault on the main fortress. The people were allowed to stay and farm the land in return for a share of the produce. Its importance is security: the northern threat was removed.

## The Treaty of Hudaibiyah

In Dhul-Qa'dah 6 AH the Prophet (pbuh) set out for Makkah with about 1,400 companions, intending to perform Umrah. They wore pilgrim dress and carried only the swords travellers normally carried. The Quraysh would not let them enter. The Muslims halted at Hudaibiyah, just outside the sacred territory.

The Prophet (pbuh) sent Hazrat Uthman (RA) as his envoy. When a rumour spread that Uthman had been killed, the companions pledged loyalty under a tree. This is the **Pledge of Ridwan**, and the Qur'an says that Allah was pleased with the believers who pledged allegiance under the tree (Qur'an 48:18).

**The terms** negotiated with Suhayl ibn Amr for the Quraysh were:

1. A truce of ten years between the Muslims and the Quraysh.
2. The Muslims would turn back this year and return the next year for three days.
3. A man from the Quraysh who joined the Muslims without his guardian's permission would be returned; a Muslim who went to the Quraysh would not be sent back.
4. Other tribes were free to make an alliance with either side.

**Why it looked unfair.** Clause 3 favoured the Quraysh. Some companions, including Hazrat Umar (RA), were troubled, and the document was written without the title "Messenger of Allah" after the Prophet's (pbuh) name, because the Quraysh objected. The Prophet (pbuh) accepted this for the sake of peace.

**Why it mattered.** The Qur'an called the outcome a "clear conquest" (Qur'an 48:1). The Quraysh had at last recognised the Muslims as a power to negotiate with. Peace meant that people could meet Muslims, hear the message and see how they lived, and many accepted Islam in the following two years. The Prophet (pbuh) also used the calm to send letters to rulers beyond Arabia.

## Fath-e-Makkah and the purification of the Kaaba

The truce lasted about two years. Then the Banu Bakr, allies of the Quraysh, attacked the Khuza'a, allies of the Muslims, and some Makkans helped them. This broke the treaty. In Ramadhan 8 AH the Prophet (pbuh) marched on Makkah with about 10,000 men.

**What happened.** The army entered from several sides with orders not to fight unless attacked. Abu Sufyan, once the Quraysh's leader against Islam, accepted Islam, and the Prophet (pbuh) announced that those who stayed in their houses, in the sanctuary or in Abu Sufyan's house would be safe. Makkah was taken with very little fighting.

**The Kaaba.** The Prophet (pbuh) went to the Kaaba and removed the idols that surrounded it, repeating: "Truth has come, and falsehood has departed. Indeed is falsehood, [by nature], ever bound to depart" (Qur'an 17:81). The building had been raised by Prophet Ibraheem (AS) and Prophet Ismail (AS) for the worship of one God, and purification restored that purpose. Bilal (RA), once a persecuted slave, gave the call to prayer from its roof.

**Mercy.** The Makkans who had driven out and fought the Muslims for twenty years feared revenge. The Prophet (pbuh) granted a general amnesty. This is the best-known example of forgiveness from a position of power. It also explains why Makkah accepted Islam so quickly: people were won by character as well as by strength.

## Hunayn and the Year of Delegations

**Hunayn.** The Hawazin and Thaqif tribes gathered to attack the Muslims. About 12,000 Muslims, the largest army yet, marched against them. Some felt confident because of their numbers, and the Qur'an later referred to this: "your great number pleased you, but it did not avail you at all" (Qur'an 9:25). The enemy ambushed them in a narrow valley and many fled. The Prophet (pbuh) stayed in place and called the companions back, and they rallied and won. Lesson: arrogance is dangerous, and faith matters more than numbers.

**Aam ul Wufood.** The ninth year after the Hijrah is called the Year of Delegations. Representatives of tribes from across Arabia, including Yemen, came to Madinah to meet the Prophet (pbuh), accept Islam or make agreements. The significance is that Islam had become the main power and religion of Arabia, mostly through persuasion, treaties and example rather than war.

## The final victory and the Farewell Pilgrimage

In 10 AH the Prophet (pbuh) led his first and only Hajj as a Muslim leader, with a very large number of pilgrims. At Arafat he gave the Final Sermon, and the verse "This day I have perfected for you your religion" (Qur'an 5:3) came to him. This is the "final victory": Arabia united under one belief, the rites of Hajj restored to the way of Ibraheem (AS), and the message complete. A few months later, in 11 AH, he died in Madinah. The sermon is examined in the guide to the [legacy and lessons of Hazrat Muhammad (pbuh)](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-islamiyat-9237-legacy-and-lessons-of-hazrat-muhammad/).

## Rules of conduct in war

The Prophet's (pbuh) instructions to commanders set limits on every battle:

- Do not harm women, children, the elderly or those who worship in monasteries and churches.
- Do not destroy crops, trees or homes without need.
- Treat prisoners humanely and give them a way to earn freedom.
- Accept peace when it is offered (Qur'an 8:61).

Use these in answers about importance: they show that the battles were bound by a moral code.

## Worked example: a 6-mark answer

**Question:** Explain **two** reasons why the Treaty of Hudaibiyah was important. Use a source of authority in your answer. **[6]**

> The treaty gave a ten-year truce [1], so the Muslims could travel and meet people without fear of attack, and many tribes learned about Islam and joined it [1]. It also showed that the Quraysh now accepted the Muslims as an equal power [1], because they agreed terms with the Prophet (pbuh) instead of trying to destroy him [1]. The Qur'an calls the result a "clear conquest" (Qur'an 48:1), which shows that peace gained through patience brought a greater victory than fighting would have done [2].

Four marks are for two reasons, each stated and explained; two marks are for using the source and applying it to the question.

## Common mistakes

- **Writing a list of dates.** Explain what each event showed.
- **Calling Hudaibiyah a defeat.** The Qur'an calls it a clear conquest, and its results prove this.
- **Saying Makkah was taken by a great battle.** It was taken almost without fighting, and the amnesty is the main point.
- **Forgetting Aam ul wafood.** The specification names it, and it shows how Islam spread after Makkah.
- **Mixing up Uhud and Hunayn.** Uhud: archers left the hill. Hunayn: pride in numbers, then an ambush in a valley.
- **Ignoring the passage.** A 12-mark answer here should use Qur'an 3:121-127.
