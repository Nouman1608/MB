Section 3.4.1 of the OxfordAQA International GCSE Chemistry (9202) specification, Version 6.2, has five statements. A pure element or compound contains only one substance, and you should be able to "identify substances and assess their purity from melting point and boiling point information" (a). Measures of purity matter in everyday substances such as foodstuffs and drugs (b). A mixture contains substances not chemically combined, each keeping its chemical properties, and can be separated by physical methods including distillation, filtration and crystallisation (c). Paper chromatography analyses substances in a solution, such as food colourings and inks; you should describe how to carry it out, identify components using **Rf values**, and know that solvents other than water can be used (d). Chromatography involves a **stationary** and a **mobile phase**, separation depends on relative solubility, and you should be able to "suggest chromatographic methods for distinguishing pure from impure substances" (e). The qualification is untiered.

The existing [purity, chromatography and gas tests guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-chemistry-purity-chromatography-and-gas-tests/) covers this section with 3.4.2. This guide focuses on 3.4.1 and gives fresh worked examples on identifying substances from data, the two meanings of "pure", and planning chromatography to test purity.

## Two meanings of "pure"

| Everyday meaning | Chemical meaning |
|---|---|
| nothing has been added; natural | contains only one substance |
| "pure orange juice" | orange juice is a mixture of water, sugars, acids and many other substances |
| "pure spring water" | spring water contains dissolved minerals, so it is a mixture |

In an exam, "pure" always has the chemical meaning. A substance described as pure in an advertisement can still be a mixture.

## Why purity matters (statement b)

- **Drugs**: the dose depends on the amount of the active substance. An impurity reduces the amount present in each tablet and may itself be harmful.
- **Foods**: impurities could be harmful, or could change taste, colour or how long the food keeps.

Purity checks are therefore part of making medicines and food. Melting point tests and chromatography are two simple checks used at this level.

## Melting point and boiling point as evidence (statement a)

A pure substance melts and boils at **fixed, sharp temperatures**. A mixture usually melts over a **range** of temperatures, and its melting point is lower than that of the pure main substance; its boiling point is usually higher, and it boils over a range.

This gives two uses:

1. **Identifying a substance**: compare a sharp measured value with data for known substances.
2. **Assessing purity**: check whether the value is sharp and matches the data value.

### Worked example 1: identifying a liquid

*A colourless liquid, believed to be pure, boils sharply at 56 °C. Use the data to identify it.*

| Liquid | Boiling point / °C |
|---|---|
| Methanol | 65 |
| Propanone | 56 |
| Ethanol | 78 |
| Water | 100 |

The liquid boils at a single temperature, which suggests it is pure, and the value matches **propanone**. A boiling range or a value between the listed values would mean the data could not identify it.

### Worked example 2: judging two samples

*Pure aspirin melts at 136 °C (value rounded). Sample A melts between 128 °C and 133 °C. Sample B melts at 136 °C. Comment on each.*

- Sample A melts over a range and below 136 °C, so it is **impure** aspirin.
- Sample B melts sharply at the data value, so it is **pure** aspirin, or at least contains no impurity detectable by this test.

## Mixtures and physical separation (statement c)

In a mixture, the substances are not chemically combined, so each keeps its own chemical properties and they can be separated by physical methods:

| Method | Separates | Property used |
|---|---|---|
| Filtration | insoluble solid from a liquid | solid particles do not pass through the paper |
| Crystallisation | a dissolved solid from its solution | solid crystallises as the solution cools and loses solvent |
| Distillation | a liquid from a solution, or liquids with different boiling points | differences in boiling point |

A compound is different: its elements are chemically combined and can be separated only by a chemical reaction.

## Paper chromatography (statements d and e)

### How it works

- The **stationary phase** is the paper, which does not move.
- The **mobile phase** is the solvent, which moves up the paper.
- Each substance is distributed between the two phases. A substance that is more soluble in the mobile phase spends more time moving with it and travels further. Separation therefore depends on the **relative solubility** of the components.

### Carrying it out

1. Draw a pencil start line near the bottom of the paper.
2. Put a small spot of each sample on the line, with known reference substances alongside.
3. Stand the paper in a container of solvent, with the solvent level **below** the line, and cover it.
4. When the solvent is near the top, remove the paper and mark the solvent front.
5. Dry the paper, measure the distances and calculate Rf values.

**Other solvents**: water is often used, but a substance that does not dissolve in water will not move. A different solvent, such as ethanol, may dissolve it. Permanent ink, for example, is designed not to dissolve in water, so it is separated using another solvent.

### Rf values

```
Rf = distance moved by substance ÷ distance moved by solvent
```

Both distances are measured from the start line. Rf has no units and is less than 1. In the same solvent, a substance always has the same Rf, so Rf values identify components.

### Worked example 3: identifying the dyes in a food colouring

*A food colouring is run in water. The solvent front moves 9.0 cm. The colouring gives two spots, 2.7 cm and 6.3 cm from the start line. Known dyes have these Rf values in water: E 0.30, F 0.50, G 0.70.*

- Rf of first spot = 2.7 ÷ 9.0 = **0.30**, matching E.
- Rf of second spot = 6.3 ÷ 9.0 = **0.70**, matching G.

The colouring is a mixture of **E and G**.

## Suggesting methods to distinguish pure from impure

The specification asks you to suggest chromatographic methods. Good suggestions:

- **Count the spots**: a pure substance gives one spot; an impure substance gives two or more.
- **Run a known pure sample alongside**: a pure sample matches the reference with a single spot at the same height; extra spots show impurities.
- **Use a second solvent**: two substances may have the same Rf in one solvent, giving one spot. If the sample still gives one spot in a different solvent, it is much more likely to be pure.

## Common mistakes

- **Using the everyday meaning of "pure".**
- **Saying impurities raise the melting point.** They lower it and give a range.
- **Drawing the start line in ink**, or putting it below the solvent level.
- **Measuring Rf distances from the bottom of the paper.**
- **Calling the paper the mobile phase.** The paper is stationary; the solvent moves.
- **Saying separation depends on the size of the particles.** It depends on relative solubility.

## Exam technique

- **Describe** chromatography: start line in pencil, spots, solvent below the line, solvent front, Rf.
- **Identify** from data: quote the measured value and the matching data value.
- **Suggest** a method for purity: count spots, compare with a pure reference, or use a second solvent.
- **Calculate** Rf: show both distances and give the answer to two decimal places.
