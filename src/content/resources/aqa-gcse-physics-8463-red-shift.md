This guide goes deeper into section **4.8.2 Red-shift** of the AQA GCSE Physics (8463) specification, for first
teaching 2016. It is the final section of Topic 8, Space physics, and is examined on Paper 2 at **both tiers**.

The [Space physics study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-physics-8463-space-physics/) introduces red-shift alongside
the rest of the topic. This page works through the chain of evidence step by step: how red-shift is measured,
what the speed–distance pattern shows, why it supports the Big Bang, and what remains unexplained. For quick
recall, use the [Red-shift revision notes](/resources/aqa-gcse-physics-8463-red-shift-revision-notes/), and test
yourself with the [Red-shift practice questions](/resources/aqa-gcse-physics-8463-red-shift-practice/).

## What this subtopic covers

The specification asks you to explain:

- **qualitatively** the red-shift of light from galaxies that are receding
- that the change of each galaxy's speed with distance is **evidence of an expanding universe**
- how red-shift provides **evidence for the Big Bang model**
- how scientists are able to use **observations to arrive at theories** such as the Big Bang theory
- that there is still much about the universe that is **not understood**, for example **dark mass** and **dark
  energy**.

There is no equation to learn, but questions can give spectra or tables and ask you to compare wavelengths or spot a
pattern.

## What red-shift is

There is an **observed increase in the wavelength of light from most distant galaxies**. This is called
**red-shift**: in the visible spectrum, longer wavelengths are towards the red end, so features in the spectrum
appear shifted towards red.

Three linked statements make up the core idea:

1. The light from most distant galaxies has a **longer wavelength** than expected.
2. This shows the galaxies are **moving away** from us (receding).
3. **The further away** a galaxy is, **the faster it is moving away**, and **the bigger the observed increase in
   wavelength**.

### Why moving away stretches the wavelength

Picture a source sending out wave crests at a steady rate while it moves away from you. Each crest is emitted a
little further away than the one before, so it has further to travel. The crests reach you more spread out than
they left the source. A greater gap between crests is a **longer wavelength**. The faster the source recedes, the
more the crests are spread out.

### How astronomers know the light has shifted

Each element absorbs light at particular wavelengths, which appear as dark lines in a spectrum. Hydrogen, for
example, always produces the same pattern of lines in a laboratory. In light from a distant galaxy, astronomers see
the **same pattern**, so they can recognise the element, but every line is at a **longer wavelength**. Comparing
the observed wavelength with the laboratory wavelength gives the size of the red-shift.

Red-shift is not "light turning red". Every wavelength increases, including those that were already beyond the red
end and those in other parts of the electromagnetic spectrum.

## Worked example 1: comparing red-shift

These data are invented. A dark line in the laboratory spectrum of hydrogen is at 486 nm. In the light from galaxy C
the same line is at 510 nm; from galaxy D it is at 535 nm.

```
Galaxy C: increase = 510 − 486 = 24 nm   fractional increase = 24 / 486 = 0.049 (4.9 %)
Galaxy D: increase = 535 − 486 = 49 nm   fractional increase = 49 / 486 = 0.10 (10 %)
```

Galaxy D has roughly twice the red-shift of galaxy C, so D is receding faster and is further away. The conclusion
rests on the pattern "bigger red-shift → faster → further". Always compare against the **laboratory** wavelength.

## From red-shift to an expanding universe

When red-shift is measured for many galaxies at many distances, a clear pattern appears: **a galaxy's speed away
from us increases with its distance**. This change of speed with distance is the evidence that **space itself is
expanding**.

Why does expansion produce this pattern? Use the balloon model. Draw dots on a balloon and blow it up. Every dot
moves away from every other dot. A dot twice as far from you on the surface moves away twice as fast, because there
is twice as much stretching surface between you. No dot is at the centre of the surface.

The model shows three things examiners look for:

- Galaxies are not flying through space away from one central point; **space between them is stretching**.
- **Every** observer, in any galaxy, would see the same pattern.
- Speed increasing with distance is exactly what a uniformly expanding universe predicts.

## Worked example 2: reading a pattern

These data are invented.

| Galaxy | Distance (relative units) | Speed away (km/s) |
|---|---|---|
| P | 1.0 | 1 500 |
| Q | 2.0 | 3 000 |
| R | 4.0 | 6 000 |
| S | 5.0 | ? |

Speed ÷ distance is 1500 for P, Q and R, so speed is proportional to distance. For S: 1500 × 5.0 = **7500 km/s**.
S would also have the biggest red-shift of the four.

## How red-shift supports the Big Bang

The **Big Bang theory** suggests that the universe **began from a very small region that was extremely hot and
dense**.

The argument in order:

1. Light from most distant galaxies is red-shifted, so they are receding.
2. Speed increases with distance, so the universe (space) is expanding.
3. If it is expanding now, it must have been **smaller in the past**.
4. Tracing the expansion back in time, everything was once concentrated in a very small region.
5. This matches the Big Bang theory, so red-shift is evidence for it.

Red-shift **supports** the theory; it does not prove it. A good answer uses the word evidence.

## How observations lead to theories

The specification asks how scientists use **observations to arrive at theories**. Red-shift is the example:

- **Observation**: measurements of the spectra of many galaxies.
- **Pattern**: wavelength increase grows with distance.
- **Explanation**: the universe is expanding.
- **Theory**: the Big Bang, which explains the observations and makes testable predictions.
- **Testing and change**: new observations can support, change or replace a theory.

New observations do change ideas. **Since 1998 onwards, observations of supernovae suggest that distant galaxies
are receding ever faster**: the expansion appears to be speeding up. This was not predicted by earlier models.

## What is still not understood

There is still much about the universe that is not understood:

- **Dark mass** (often called dark matter): there appears to be far more mass in galaxies than we can see, judged by
  its gravitational effects, but nobody knows what it is.
- **Dark energy**: the name given to whatever is causing the expansion of the universe to accelerate. Its nature is
  unknown.

Exam answers only need you to name these as examples of what is not yet understood and say briefly why each is
needed.

## Common mistakes

- Writing "the light turns red" instead of "the observed wavelength increases".
- Saying nearer galaxies have bigger red-shift. Further galaxies have bigger red-shift.
- Describing galaxies moving away from a centre, rather than space expanding.
- Saying red-shift "proves" the Big Bang. It provides evidence that supports it.
- Comparing the wavelengths of two galaxies with each other instead of each with the laboratory value.
- Confusing dark mass with dark energy: dark mass is about extra gravity; dark energy is about accelerating
  expansion.

## Exam technique

**Explain** questions on this section are chains. Learn the five-step Big Bang chain and the three-step red-shift
chain, and write every step: missing a middle link, such as "it was smaller in the past", is the usual reason for a
lost mark. **Describe** a spectrum by naming the direction of the shift (to longer wavelength, towards red) and its
size. **Suggest** questions may give unfamiliar data: find the pattern first, then link it to speed and distance. In
six-mark answers, finish with what the evidence supports.
