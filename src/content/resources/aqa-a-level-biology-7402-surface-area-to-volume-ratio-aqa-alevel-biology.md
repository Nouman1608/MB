This guide covers **3.3.1 Surface area to volume ratio** from AQA AS and A-level Biology (7401/7402), the opening sub-topic of Topic 3, *Organisms exchange substances with their environment*. It is AS content, examined on both AS papers and on A-level Papers 1 and 3. The overview of the whole topic is in the [exchange with the environment guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-biology-exchange-with-environment/). Condensed recall is in the [surface area to volume ratio revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-biology-7402-surface-area-to-volume-ratio-aqa-alevel-biology-revision-notes/), and calculation practice is in the [surface area to volume ratio practice questions](/resources/aqa-a-level-biology-7402-surface-area-to-volume-ratio-aqa-alevel-biology-practice/).

## What the specification asks for

The specification lists three ideas in 3.3.1:

| Statement | What you must be able to do |
|---|---|
| The relationship between the size of an organism or structure and its surface area to volume ratio | Calculate the ratio for regular shapes and explain why it falls as size increases |
| Changes to body shape and the development of systems in larger organisms as adaptations that facilitate exchange as this ratio reduces | Explain flattened or elongated shapes, specialised exchange surfaces and mass transport systems |
| The relationship between surface area to volume ratio and metabolic rate | Explain why small animals have a higher metabolic rate per unit mass |

The calculation work draws on the specification's maths skills for calculating the circumferences, surface areas and volumes of regular shapes. Questions give you the formula for anything harder than a cube or cuboid, but you need to be fluent at using one.

## Why the ratio falls as size increases

Surface area depends on length squared; volume depends on length cubed. When an object doubles in length, its surface area increases four times but its volume increases eight times, so the ratio halves.

| Cube side / cm | Surface area / cm² | Volume / cm³ | SA:V |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 6 | 1 | 6 : 1 |
| 2 | 24 | 8 | 3 : 1 |
| 4 | 96 | 64 | 1.5 : 1 |
| 10 | 600 | 1000 | 0.6 : 1 |

For a cube, SA:V simplifies to 6 ÷ side. For a sphere it is 3 ÷ radius. Both show the same pattern: the bigger the object, the smaller the ratio.

Why it matters: the volume of a cell or organism sets how much oxygen it uses and how much carbon dioxide and waste it makes, but the surface sets how fast those substances can be exchanged. Diffusion is also only fast over very short distances. In a single-celled organism, the large surface relative to volume and the short distance to the centre mean diffusion across the body surface meets its needs. In a large organism, the surface is too small for its volume and most cells are too far from the surface.

## Worked example 1: a cylinder

A filament of cells is modelled as a cylinder of radius 1 mm and length 10 mm (invented dimensions). Surface area of a cylinder = 2πr² + 2πrh; volume = πr²h.

```
Surface area = 2π(1)² + 2π(1)(10) = 2π + 20π = 22π = 69.1 mm²
Volume       = π(1)²(10)          = 10π         = 31.4 mm³
SA:V         = 69.1 ÷ 31.4        = 2.2 : 1
```

Keep π in the working until the last step. Here it cancels exactly: 22π ÷ 10π = 2.2.

## Worked example 2: same volume, different shape

Three blocks each have a volume of 8 cm³.

| Shape | Dimensions / cm | Surface area / cm² | SA:V |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cube | 2 × 2 × 2 | 24 | 3 : 1 |
| Long cuboid | 1 × 1 × 8 | 34 | 4.25 : 1 |
| Flat sheet | 4 × 4 × 0.5 | 40 | 5 : 1 |

For the flat sheet: 2 × (4 × 4) + 4 × (4 × 0.5) = 32 + 8 = 40 cm². This is the core of the "body shape" statement: without changing volume, an elongated or flattened shape gives a larger surface area to volume ratio and a shorter distance from the centre to the surface. A flatworm is the standard example of a flattened body that allows diffusion across the body surface; a long thin worm-shaped body achieves a similar effect.

## Adaptations as the ratio reduces

As organisms get larger, two kinds of adaptation make exchange possible:

1. **Specialised exchange surfaces** with a very large total area and a thin barrier: alveoli in mammals, gill filaments and lamellae in fish, tracheoles in insects, mesophyll surfaces in leaves, and villi and microvilli in the ileum. Each of these is a way of adding surface without adding much volume.
2. **Mass transport systems**, such as blood circulation in animals and xylem and phloem in plants, which carry substances between the exchange surfaces and cells deep inside the body, keeping diffusion distances short at both ends.

The detail of each surface is in 3.3.2 to 3.3.4. Here you need the logic: falling SA:V creates the need; increased surface area, shorter distance and maintained gradients meet it.

## Surface area to volume ratio and metabolic rate

Mammals and birds keep their body temperature roughly constant. Heat is produced in the volume of the body by respiration and lost through its surface. A small mammal has a large surface relative to its volume, so it loses heat faster per unit mass. To stay warm it must release more heat, so it has a **higher rate of respiration per unit body mass** — a higher metabolic rate per gram — and must take in more food and oxygen per gram of body mass.

Be careful with the comparison. A large mammal has a higher *total* metabolic rate because it has more cells. The small mammal's rate is higher only *per unit mass*. Exam answers that say "small animals have a higher metabolic rate" without "per unit mass" are ambiguous and may not score.

Related ideas that questions build on:

- Small mammals in cold climates are rare; animals in cold places tend to be larger with smaller ears and limbs, reducing SA:V.
- Animals in hot places often have large ears or thin limbs, increasing surface for heat loss.
- A higher metabolic rate per gram needs a faster supply of oxygen per gram, so small mammals often have high breathing and heart rates.

## Investigating the idea with agar blocks

A common investigation cuts agar containing an indicator into cubes of different sizes and places them in acid or alkali. The colour change shows how far the solution has diffused. All cubes change colour at about the same distance from the surface in a given time, but the smallest cube changes completely first because its centre is closest to the surface. The data support two linked points: diffusion distance limits the rate at which the whole volume is supplied, and a higher SA:V gives a larger proportion of the volume changed per unit time. This is a useful illustration, not one of the required practicals for this course.

## Common mistakes

- Calculating SA:V and then writing it upside down (volume : surface area).
- Mixing units, for example area in mm² and volume in cm³.
- Saying "a larger organism has a smaller surface area"; it has a larger surface area but a smaller surface area *to volume ratio*.
- Writing "metabolism is faster in small animals" with no "per unit mass".
- Saying heat is "produced at the surface"; it is produced throughout the body volume.
- Listing adaptations without linking them to SA:V, distance or gradient.

## Exam technique for AQA command words

| Command word | What to do |
|---|---|
| Calculate | Show the area, the volume and the division; give the ratio as *n* : 1 or as a single number, matching the question |
| Explain | Chain: size up → SA:V down → exchange per unit volume too slow → adaptation |
| Suggest | Apply the chain to an unfamiliar organism, for example a long thin shape or large ears |
| Use the data | Quote values from the table or graph, with units, in your answer |
