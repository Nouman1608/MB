## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Opportunity cost | The value of the next best alternative given up when a choice is made |
| Trade-off | The relationship in which more of one thing means less of another |
| Accounting cost | Money actually paid out |
| Production possibility curve (PPC) | The maximum combinations of two goods an economy can produce with all resources fully and efficiently used, technology given |
| Productive efficiency (on a PPC) | Producing on the curve: more of one good requires less of the other |
| Increasing opportunity cost | Each extra unit of one good requires giving up more of the other |
| Capital goods | Goods used to produce other goods, such as machines |
| Consumer goods | Goods bought to satisfy wants directly |

## Condensed recall

**Opportunity cost**

- One alternative: the best one forgone, not the total of all rejected options.
- Includes non-money costs: time, leisure, the use of resources already owned.
- Applies to every agent: a household choosing a holiday, a firm choosing an investment, a government choosing between projects.

**Reading a PPC**

- On the curve: efficient. Inside: unemployed or wasted resources. Outside: unattainable now.
- Moving along: a choice; the opportunity cost is the fall in the other good.
- Concave (bowed out) shape: increasing opportunity cost, because resources are not equally suited to both goods.
- Straight line: constant opportunity cost, because resources are equally suited to both.

**Shifting a PPC**

- Outward: more or better resources, better technology.
- Inward: loss of resources through disaster, war or emigration.
- Pivot: technology that helps only one good.
- No shift: reducing unemployment, which moves the economy toward the curve.

**Over time**

- More capital goods now means fewer consumer goods now, but a larger PPC later.

**Usefulness**

- Makes hidden costs visible and improves decisions.
- Hard to identify and value the next best option, especially for non-market goods.
- The PPC simplifies to two goods and fixed technology.

## Worked reminders

- A farmer can grow wheat (£9,000 profit), rent the field (£7,500) or run a campsite (£6,000). Choosing wheat, the opportunity cost is £7,500.
- Country L's PPC: raising food from 30m to 40m tonnes cuts machines from 36 to 22 thousand, an opportunity cost of 14 thousand machines, or 1.4 thousand per million tonnes.
- A point at 25m tonnes of food and 30 thousand machines lies inside the curve if the curve allows 40 thousand machines at that food output.

## Exam traps

- Adding up all rejected options.
- Ignoring the value of the owner's time or own resources.
- Showing a fall in unemployment as an outward shift.
- Calling a point outside the curve "inefficient" rather than "unattainable".
- Drawing a PPC without labelling the axes with the two goods.
- Reading opportunity cost as the level of the other good rather than the change in it.
- Forgetting to divide by the size of the change when asked for opportunity cost per unit.

## Self-test

Use this invented PPC for Country M for questions 5 to 9.

| Point | Consumer goods (bn units) | Capital goods (bn units) |
|---|---|---|
| P | 0 | 40 |
| Q | 10 | 37 |
| R | 20 | 31 |
| S | 30 | 21 |
| T | 40 | 0 |

1. Define opportunity cost.
2. What is the difference between a trade-off and an opportunity cost?
3. A student turns down a £60 shift at work and a free concert ticket worth £40 to her, and revises instead. What is the opportunity cost?
4. Why is accounting cost not the same as opportunity cost?
5. What is the opportunity cost of moving from Q to R?
6. What is the opportunity cost of moving from S to T, per billion consumer goods?
7. Does Country M face increasing or constant opportunity cost? How do you know?
8. Is a combination of 20bn consumer goods and 25bn capital goods efficient?
9. Why might Country M choose point Q rather than point S?
10. Name two causes of an outward shift of a PPC.
11. Unemployment falls. What happens on the PPC diagram?
12. Give one limitation of opportunity cost as a tool for government decisions.

### Answers

1. The value of the next best alternative given up when a choice is made.
2. A trade-off is the relationship of having less of one thing to get more of another; opportunity cost is the value of the specific alternative given up.
3. The £60 shift, the next best alternative (assuming she valued it more than the concert).
4. Accounting cost counts only money paid out, while opportunity cost includes the value of owned resources and time.
5. 6bn capital goods (37 to 31).
6. 21bn capital goods for 10bn consumer goods, or 2.1bn per billion consumer goods.
7. Increasing: each extra 10bn consumer goods costs more capital goods (3, 6, 10, then 21bn).
8. No: it is inside the curve, since the curve allows 31bn capital goods at that output.
9. More capital goods now would shift the PPC outward in future, allowing more of both goods later.
10. Any two of: a larger workforce, more capital, discovery of natural resources, better technology, a more skilled workforce.
11. The economy moves from a point inside the curve toward the curve; the curve does not shift.
12. The next best alternative may be hard to identify and value, especially for non-market benefits such as the environment.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why is the PPC usually drawn bowed outward?**
Because resources are not equally suited to producing both goods, so opportunity cost rises as more of one good is produced.

**Can a PPC show economic growth?**
Yes. An outward shift shows a rise in productive capacity, which is potential growth.
