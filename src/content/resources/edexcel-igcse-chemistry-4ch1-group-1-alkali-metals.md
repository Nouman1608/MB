This guide teaches sub-topic 2(a) Group 1 (alkali metals) – lithium, sodium and potassium, from Topic 2, Inorganic chemistry, of the Pearson Edexcel International GCSE Chemistry (4CH1) specification, Issue 3 (September 2024). It covers points 2.1 to 2.4C. Points 2.1 to 2.3 have no C reference and can be examined on Paper 1 and Paper 2. Point 2.4C has a C reference, so it is examined on **Paper 2 only**.

## Explaining each observation with water

A question may ask you to **describe and explain** what is seen. Pair each observation with its cause.

| Observation | Explanation |
|---|---|
| The metal floats | It is less dense than water |
| Fizzing / bubbles | Hydrogen gas is produced |
| The metal moves across the surface | The hydrogen released pushes it around |
| The metal gets smaller and disappears | It is used up as it reacts to form a soluble hydroxide |
| Sodium and potassium melt into a ball | The reaction gives out heat, and these metals have low melting points |
| Potassium: lilac flame | The heat ignites the hydrogen; the flame is coloured lilac by potassium |
| Universal indicator turns purple | The hydroxide formed is a strong alkali (source of OH⁻ ions) |

Both products can be tested: the gas gives a squeaky pop with a lighted splint (hydrogen), and the solution turns red litmus blue (alkaline).

**Model answer.** A small piece of sodium is added to water containing universal indicator. Describe and explain what is seen. **[6]**

> The sodium floats because it is less dense than water [1]. It fizzes because hydrogen gas is produced [1]. It melts into a silvery ball because the reaction releases heat and sodium has a low melting point [1]. It moves around the surface, pushed by the hydrogen [1]. It gets smaller and disappears as it reacts [1]. The indicator turns purple because sodium hydroxide, an alkali, forms: 2Na + 2H₂O → 2NaOH + H₂ [1].

Practical precautions: small pieces only, cut with a knife on a tile and handled with tongs; a safety screen and eye protection; the metals are stored under oil to keep out air and water vapour.

## Similarities as evidence of a family (2.1)

The evidence is that all three metals:

- react with cold water in the same way,
- produce the same type of products, a metal hydroxide (MOH) and hydrogen,
- form ions with the same charge, 1+, so their compounds have the same type of formula (LiOH, NaOH, KOH; Li₂O, Na₂O, K₂O).

## Differences as evidence of the trend (2.2)

**Rate with water.** The faster the fizzing and the more violent the reaction, the more reactive the metal: lithium < sodium < potassium.

**Tarnishing in air.** A freshly cut surface is shiny but dulls as an oxide layer forms. The faster it dulls, the more reactive the metal.

**Worked example 1 — turning results into evidence.** A student times how long equal-sized pieces take to disappear in water. (These results are invented for practice.)

| Metal | Time to disappear / s |
|---|---|
| Lithium | 62 |
| Sodium | 14 |
| Potassium | 3 |

(a) What do the results show? (b) Explain why the pieces must be the same size.

```
(a) time decreases down the group, so the rate of reaction increases:
    reactivity increases from lithium to potassium
(b) a larger piece has more metal to react and a larger surface,
    so size must be controlled for a fair comparison
```

## Predicting other alkali metals (2.3)

Rubidium and caesium come below potassium. Continue each trend and keep the family pattern.

**Worked example 2.** Predict how caesium reacts with water and with air, and give the formulae of its hydroxide and oxide.

```
reactivity increases down the group -> caesium reacts even more violently
                                       than potassium (explosively)
same family pattern -> products are caesium hydroxide and hydrogen
                       2Cs(s) + 2H2O(l) -> 2CsOH(aq) + H2(g)
Cs+ ion (1+) -> hydroxide CsOH; oxide Cs2O
air -> tarnishes almost instantly, faster than potassium
solution -> strongly alkaline (pH about 14 with universal indicator)
```

Physical trends also continue: melting point decreases down the group, so caesium has a lower melting point than potassium. Predictions should follow the direction of the trend; the precise value is an estimate.

## Calculating amounts of product

Reactions of alkali metals are good contexts for the mole calculations of Topic 1.

**Worked example 3.** 0.46 g of sodium reacts completely with water. (Ar: Na = 23, O = 16, H = 1)

```
2Na + 2H2O -> 2NaOH + H2

moles Na   = 0.46 / 23 = 0.020 mol
moles NaOH = 0.020 mol (1 : 1)
mass NaOH  = 0.020 x 40 = 0.80 g

moles H2   = 0.020 / 2 = 0.010 mol
```

**Paper 2 only (uses point 1.35C).** Volume of hydrogen at rtp = 0.010 × 24 000 = **240 cm³**.

## Explaining the trend (2.4C) — Paper 2 only

An alkali metal reacts by losing its one outer electron to form a 1+ ion. The more easily the electron is lost, the more reactive the metal.

**Model answer.** Explain why rubidium is more reactive than potassium. **[4]**

> A rubidium atom has more occupied electron shells than a potassium atom (2,8,8,1), so its outer electron is further from the nucleus [1]. There are more inner shells shielding the outer electron from the nucleus [1]. So the attraction between the nucleus and the outer electron is weaker [1], and rubidium loses its outer electron more easily [1].

Rubidium's full configuration is beyond the first 20 elements, so describe it as "one more shell than potassium" rather than writing it out. The higher nuclear charge of rubidium does not outweigh the extra distance and shielding.

## Common mistakes

- Explaining the lilac flame as the potassium burning away: the heat ignites the hydrogen, and potassium colours the flame lilac.
- "The metal dissolves": it reacts. The hydroxide formed dissolves.
- Writing NaOH₂ or Na₂OH: Group 1 hydroxides are MOH.
- Saying reactivity increases "because the atoms get bigger" with no reference to the outer electron and the nucleus.
- Predicting caesium floats: densities rise overall down the group, so this is not safe.
- Giving one difference only when the question asks about both air and water.

## Exam technique

- **Describe**: give separate observations, each a mark; "it reacts" is not an observation.
- **Explain**: pair each observation with its cause.
- **Predict**: state the trend, then apply it to the new metal.
- **Explain** the trend (Paper 2): distance, shielding, weaker attraction, electron lost more easily.
