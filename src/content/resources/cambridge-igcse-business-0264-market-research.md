This guide goes deeper on section 3.2 (Market research) of the Cambridge IGCSE Business 0264 syllabus for examination in 2027, 2028 and 2029. The section has one content statement, 3.2.1 Methods of market research, which lists: why businesses use market research; primary research methods such as questionnaires or surveys, interviews, focus groups and observation; the concept of sampling and why it is useful; secondary methods such as competitor websites, government sources, market reports and trade magazines; the advantages and disadvantages of each type; factors influencing the accuracy of the data; and analysing simple market research data. The broader [marketing and market research guide](/resources/igcse-business-0264-marketing-and-the-market-and-market-research/) defines each method. This page focuses on the skills questions test: choosing a method for a situation, spotting why results may be inaccurate, and drawing conclusions from data. ## Research starts with the decision

Market research is collecting and analysing information about customers, competitors and the market. It is worth doing only if it helps a decision. Before choosing a method, a business should know what it needs to find out.

| Decision | Question research must answer | Sensible starting point |
|---|---|---|
| Whether to open a shop in a new town | How many potential customers live there? | Secondary: government population figures |
| What price to charge for a new product | What do competitors charge? | Secondary: competitor websites |
| Which of two package designs to use | Which do customers prefer, and why? | Primary: focus group |
| Whether shoppers notice a new display | What do customers actually do in store? | Primary: observation |
| How satisfied current customers are | What do many customers think, in numbers? | Primary: questionnaire to a sample |

A common pattern is secondary first, primary second. Secondary research is cheaper and gives a picture of the whole market. Primary research then answers the specific questions secondary data cannot.

## Primary research: designing a good questionnaire

Questionnaires are the most common primary method in case studies, and the quality of the questions affects the accuracy of the results.

- **Closed questions** give a fixed choice of answers, such as "yes/no" or a rating from 1 to 5. Results are easy to count and compare, but they do not explain *why* people feel as they do.
- **Open questions** let people answer in their own words, such as "What would make you visit more often?" They give detail and new ideas, but answers are slow to analyse.
- **Leading questions** push people towards an answer, such as "Don't you agree our new menu is better value?" They make results less accurate and should be avoided.
- **Vague questions** such as "Do you eat out often?" mean different things to different people. "How many times did you eat at a café last month?" is clearer.

### Worked example: fixing a flawed survey

Quenby Smoothies plans a juice bar near a college. The owner wrote this survey and handed it out at a gym one Saturday morning to 30 people.

1. "Don't you think fresh smoothies are healthier than fizzy drinks?"
2. "Would you buy smoothies?"
3. "How much would you pay?"

Problems and fixes:

| Problem | Effect on accuracy | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Question 1 is leading | Most people agree, which says nothing about whether they will buy | Ask "Which drink do you most often buy with lunch?" with a list of options |
| Question 2 is vague | "Yes" might mean once a year | Ask how often they would buy, with options such as "never", "once a month", "once a week", "more than once a week" |
| Question 3 is open with no context | Answers vary wildly and are hard to use | Give price bands, such as under $3, $3 to $4, over $4 |
| Gym members only | Gym users are more health-conscious than college students, so interest is overstated | Survey students near the college site |
| Saturday morning only | Leaves out people who visit on weekdays | Survey on different days and times |
| Sample of 30 | Too small to rely on | Survey more people, for example 150 to 200 |

The improved survey still has limits: people may say they would buy and then not do so. That is why a business should treat survey results as an estimate, not a forecast it can rely on.

## Sampling: why and how much

A **sample** is a smaller group chosen to represent the whole group the business wants to learn about. Asking everyone is usually impossible or too expensive, so sampling saves time and money while still giving a guide.

A useful sample is:

- **representative:** it contains the same kinds of people as the target market, in similar proportions
- **large enough:** small samples can be distorted by a few unusual answers
- **chosen fairly:** picking people in a way that favours one group (such as only asking friends, or only visiting one place) introduces bias

There is a trade-off. A bigger, better-chosen sample is more accurate but costs more. A small business may accept a smaller sample and treat the results with more caution.

## Secondary research: what each source is good for

| Source | Useful for | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| Competitor websites | Prices, product ranges, opening hours | Shows what rivals want customers to see, not their sales or costs |
| Government sources | Population size, age structure, incomes by area | Collected for other purposes; may be several years old |
| Market reports | Market size, growth and trends | Can be expensive to buy; may cover a whole country, not one town |
| Trade magazines | News and trends in a particular industry | Written for the industry; may lack detail on local customers |

### Worked example: estimating demand from secondary data

Quenby finds government figures showing 4,800 students at the college. Its improved survey of 180 students finds that 54 would buy a smoothie at least once a week.

```
Percentage who would buy weekly = 54 / 180 x 100 = 30%
Estimated weekly customers     = 30 / 100 x 4,800 = 1,440 students
```

This combines secondary data (the number of students) with primary data (the share who are interested). The estimate is only as good as both sources: if the government figure is out of date, or students overstate their intentions, the real number will differ.

## Analysing simple market research data

You may be given a table and asked to calculate, compare and conclude. Use the same steps every time.

1. Calculate percentages so that groups of different sizes can be compared.
2. Identify the largest and smallest values, or the clearest difference between groups.
3. State what this means for the business's decision.
4. Note one limit of the data.

### Worked example: comparing two designs

Corvane Snacks tested two crisp packet designs. Each design was rated from 1 (poor) to 5 (excellent) by a sample of customers.

| Rating | Design A | Design B |
|---|---|---|
| 5 | 24 | 30 |
| 4 | 36 | 18 |
| 3 | 20 | 22 |
| 2 | 12 | 20 |
| 1 | 8 | 30 |
| Total asked | 100 | 120 |

```
Design A rated 4 or 5 = (24 + 36) / 100 x 100 = 60%
Design B rated 4 or 5 = (30 + 18) / 120 x 100 = 40%
Design B rated 1 or 2 = (20 + 30) / 120 x 100 = 41.7%
Design A rated 1 or 2 = (12 + 8) / 100 x 100 = 20%
```

Design B has more top ratings (30 against 24), but that is because more people saw it. As a percentage, Design A is rated highly by more customers and disliked by fewer. Design B divides opinion. Corvane should choose Design A, unless it is targeting a small segment that loves Design B. A limit: the ratings show *what* people prefer but not *why*, so a short focus group could help before the final choice.

## Exam technique

The command words in the 0264 syllabus include **calculate**, **define**, **identify**, **state**, **outline**, **explain**, **consider** and **justify**.

- **Identify / state:** name one method or one factor; no explanation needed.
- **Calculate:** show the division and multiplication, and give the percentage.
- **Explain:** say how the factor affects the results for *this* business. "The survey was done at a gym, so the sample overstates how health-conscious the college students are."
- **Consider / justify:** compare two methods for the situation, weighing cost, time, detail and accuracy, then decide.

## Common errors

- Comparing raw numbers when the groups are different sizes. Use percentages.
- Saying primary research is "always accurate". A biased sample or leading questions make it inaccurate.
- Saying secondary research is "free". Market reports can be expensive.
- Choosing a method without linking it to what the business needs to find out.
- Treating what people say they will buy as certain future sales.
