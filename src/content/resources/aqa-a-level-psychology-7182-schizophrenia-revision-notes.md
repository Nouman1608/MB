## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Positive symptom | An experience added to normal functioning, such as hallucinations or delusions. |
| Negative symptom | A loss or reduction of normal functioning, such as speech poverty or avolition. |
| Hallucination | A sensory experience with no external stimulus, such as hearing a voice nobody else hears. |
| Delusion | A firmly held belief not supported by evidence and kept despite contrary evidence. |
| Speech poverty | Reduced amount and quality of speech; brief, empty replies. |
| Avolition | Reduced ability to start and keep up goal-directed activity. |
| Co-morbidity | Two or more conditions occurring in the same person at the same time. |
| Symptom overlap | Different conditions sharing the same symptoms, making them hard to tell apart. |
| Culture bias | Diagnosis distorted by the clinician's own cultural norms. |
| Gender bias | Diagnosis distorted by the person's gender. |
| Polygenic | Risk comes from many genes, each with a small effect. |
| Neural correlate | A brain structure or activity pattern linked to a symptom (correlation, not cause). |
| Hyperdopaminergia | Excess dopamine activity, in subcortical areas in the original hypothesis. |
| Hypodopaminergia | Too little dopamine activity, in the prefrontal cortex in the revised hypothesis. |
| Expressed emotion (EE) | A family's level of criticism, hostility and emotional over-involvement. |
| Double bind | Contradictory messages from a carer, with the child unable to respond safely to either. |
| Metarepresentation | The ability to reflect on your own thoughts and recognise them as yours. |
| Central control | The ability to suppress automatic responses during deliberate actions. |
| Typical antipsychotic | An older drug, such as chlorpromazine, that blocks dopamine D2 receptors. |
| Atypical antipsychotic | A later drug, such as clozapine, that acts on dopamine and other systems, such as serotonin. |
| Diathesis-stress model | Disorder develops when a vulnerability (diathesis) meets a trigger (stress). |

## Theories and studies at a glance

| Who | What | Finding | One evaluation point |
|---|---|---|---|
| Buckley et al. (2009) | Review of co-morbidity | About half of people with schizophrenia also had depression; nearly half a substance use disorder | Raises doubt that schizophrenia is one distinct condition |
| Luhrmann et al. (2015) | Interviews with voice-hearers in the USA, India and Ghana | US voices more often violent; India and Ghana voices more often familiar or positive | Culture shapes symptoms, so one set of criteria may not fit all |
| Fearon et al. (2006), AESOP | Rates of diagnosis by ethnic group in England | Higher rates among Black Caribbean and Black African groups | Contested: diagnostic bias or social adversity and stress |
| Gottesman (1991) | Summary of family and twin studies | Risk about 48% identical twin, 17% non-identical twin, 9% sibling, 1% general population | Identical twin concordance below 100%, so not purely genetic |
| Ripke et al. (2014) | Genome-wide comparison | 108 genetic locations linked to schizophrenia | Supports polygenic risk, but each gene adds only a little |
| Tienari et al. (2004) | Finnish adoption study | Genetic risk mattered mainly when the adoptive family was dysfunctional | Direct support for diathesis-stress |
| Davis et al. (1991) | Revised dopamine hypothesis | Hypodopaminergia in prefrontal cortex linked to negative symptoms | Explains negative symptoms the original hypothesis missed |
| Juckel et al. (2006) | Brain scanning during reward anticipation | Lower ventral striatum activity, linked to negative symptoms | Correlational: cannot show cause |
| Bateson et al. (1956) | Double-bind theory | Contradictory communication may lead to confused thinking | Based on clinical observation, little controlled evidence |
| Brown, Birley and Wing (1972) | Expressed emotion | More relapse in people returning to high-EE homes | Explains relapse better than onset |
| Frith (1992) | Dysfunctional thought processing | Faulty metarepresentation and central control | Describes how, not why |
| Stirling et al. (2006) | Stroop task | People with schizophrenia much slower than controls | Supports weak central control |
| Leucht et al. (2013) | Comparison of 15 antipsychotics | All beat placebo; clozapine ranked most effective | Side effect profiles differ widely |
| Jauhar et al. (2014) | Review of CBT trials | Small effect, smaller still in well-masked trials | Questions how effective CBT really is |
| Pharoah et al. (2010) | Cochrane review of family intervention | May reduce relapse and hospital admission and help people keep taking medication | Usually given with drugs, so effect is hard to isolate |
| Meehl (1962) | Early diathesis-stress model | Single "schizogene" as the diathesis | Now replaced by polygenic and trauma-based ideas |

## Must-know distinctions

- **Positive vs negative symptoms.** Positive means added (hallucinations, delusions). Negative means lost (speech poverty, avolition). Neither word means good or bad.
- **Hallucination vs delusion.** A hallucination is a perception. A delusion is a belief.
- **Co-morbidity vs symptom overlap.** Co-morbidity is two conditions present together. Symptom overlap is two different conditions sharing symptoms. Both can lead to misdiagnosis, but for different reasons.
- **Original vs revised dopamine hypothesis.** Original: too much subcortical dopamine, positive symptoms. Revised: also too little prefrontal dopamine, negative symptoms.
- **Typical vs atypical antipsychotics.** Typical: older, mainly block D2, more movement side effects. Atypical: later, act on dopamine and other systems, different side effects such as weight gain.
- **Onset vs relapse.** EE research mainly predicts relapse. Do not use it as proof of cause.
- **Explaining vs treating.** The interactionist approach applies to both: combined causes and combined treatment.

## Mini essay plan

*Question style: "Discuss the dopamine hypothesis as an explanation of schizophrenia." (extended response)*

1. **AO1, about a third of the answer.** Original hypothesis: hyperdopaminergia in subcortical areas, linked to positive symptoms. Revised hypothesis: hypodopaminergia in prefrontal cortex, linked to negative symptoms. Howes and Kapur's "final common pathway" idea.
2. **AO3 point 1.** Drug evidence: amphetamines can produce psychotic symptoms; antipsychotics that block D2 reduce positive symptoms. Then the counterpoint: treatment-aetiology reasoning.
3. **AO3 point 2.** Juckel et al. (2006) on ventral striatum and negative symptoms. Correlation, not cause.
4. **AO3 point 3.** Glutamate and non-responders: dopamine is not the full story.
5. **AO3 point 4, issues and debates.** Biological reductionism; the diathesis-stress model offers a fuller account by adding stressors such as family environment.
6. **Judgement.** Dopamine is a strong part of the explanation, especially for positive symptoms, but best seen as one level within an interactionist account.

## Quick self-test

1. Name two positive and two negative symptoms in the specification.
2. What is avolition?
3. Define co-morbidity.
4. Give one example of symptom overlap involving schizophrenia.
5. What did Luhrmann et al. (2015) find?
6. Why does a concordance rate below 100% for identical twins matter?
7. State the difference between the original and revised dopamine hypotheses.
8. What is expressed emotion, and what does it best predict?
9. What does a failure of metarepresentation produce, according to Frith?
10. Why do people taking clozapine need regular blood tests?
11. What did Jauhar et al. (2014) conclude about CBT?
12. What is the diathesis in Meehl's (1962) model?

### Answers

1. Positive: hallucinations, delusions. Negative: speech poverty, avolition.
2. A reduced ability to start and keep up goal-directed activity, such as work or self-care.
3. Two or more conditions occurring together in the same person.
4. Avolition and reduced speech also occur in depression; delusions and hallucinations also occur in bipolar disorder.
5. Voice-hearers in the USA more often described violent, hostile voices; those in India and Ghana more often described familiar or positive voices.
6. Identical twins share all their genes, so if genes were the whole cause, concordance would be 100%. Environment must also play a part.
7. Original: excess subcortical dopamine linked to positive symptoms. Revised: adds too little prefrontal dopamine linked to negative symptoms.
8. A family's level of criticism, hostility and emotional over-involvement. It best predicts relapse.
9. The person may not recognise their own thoughts or inner speech as their own, so they may experience hallucinations or delusions such as thought insertion.
10. Clozapine can cause a serious fall in white blood cells.
11. CBT has a small effect on symptoms, which shrinks further in trials where assessors are masked.
12. A single gene (the "schizogene").

## Where marks are usually lost

- Defining positive symptoms as "good" rather than added to normal experience.
- Naming a diagnosis issue without explaining how it reduces the accuracy of diagnosis.
- Quoting Gottesman's figures without saying what they show and what they do not show.
- Writing that antipsychotics "cure" schizophrenia. They reduce symptoms and relapse risk.
- Using EE as evidence for the cause of schizophrenia, when it mainly predicts relapse.
- Describing CBT in general terms (as for depression) with no mention of voices, delusions or reality testing.
- Giving evaluation as one-word labels ("reductionist") with no explanation of why it matters here.
- In application questions, ignoring the details of the scenario.
- Using stigmatising language such as "schizophrenics" or "split personality".
