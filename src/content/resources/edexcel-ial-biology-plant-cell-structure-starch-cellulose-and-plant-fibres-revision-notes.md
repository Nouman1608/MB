## 4.1 and 4.2: structures and what they do

| Structure | Key features | Function |
|---|---|---|
| Cell wall | Cellulose microfibrils in a pectin and hemicellulose matrix | Support, prevents bursting, allows turgor |
| Middle lamella | Pectin-rich layer between two cells | Cements neighbouring cells together |
| Pits | Thin areas of wall, no secondary thickening | Movement of water and solutes between cells |
| Plasmodesmata | Channels through walls lined by the plasma membrane, with cytoplasm | Communication and transport between cells |
| Vacuole | Large, filled with cell sap | Stores solutes, keeps cell turgid |
| Tonoplast | Single membrane around the vacuole | Controls movement into and out of the vacuole |
| Chloroplast | Double membrane, grana, stroma, starch grains | Photosynthesis |
| Amyloplast | Double membrane, starch grains, no chlorophyll | Stores starch |

**Plant cell versus animal cell.** Plant: wall, large vacuole, chloroplasts, starch, plasmodesmata. Animal: no wall, small vacuoles, glycogen, no chloroplasts.

**Shared:** nucleus, mitochondria, ribosomes, rough ER, Golgi, plasma membrane.

### Method: recognising organelles in an EM image

1. Look for the boundary: double membrane (chloroplast, amyloplast) or single (vacuole).
2. Look inside: stacked discs mean grana; large pale blobs mean starch grains.
3. Wall features: the middle lamella is the central line between two cell walls; gaps through the wall are plasmodesmata.
4. Use the scale bar to check the size.

### Magnification reminders

magnification = image size ÷ actual size. Put both sizes in one unit before dividing (1 mm is 1000 µm).

## 4.3: starch and cellulose

| | Starch | Cellulose |
|---|---|---|
| Monomer | α-glucose | β-glucose |
| Bonds | α-1,4 glycosidic (amylose); α-1,4 and α-1,6 (amylopectin) | β-1,4 glycosidic |
| Shape | Amylose coiled; amylopectin branched | Straight, unbranched |
| Holding structure | Hydrogen bonds stabilise the coil | Hydrogen bonds between parallel chains make microfibrils |
| Role | Energy store | Structural, cell wall strength |
| Why suited | Compact, insoluble, no effect on water potential, many ends for quick hydrolysis | Many hydrogen bonds give high tensile strength; insoluble |

Why cellulose chains are straight: every second β-glucose is turned upside down so the –OH groups line up for β-1,4 bonds.

Glycosidic bonds hold residues **in** a chain. Hydrogen bonds hold **different** chains together.

Number of water molecules released in forming a chain = number of residues − 1.

## 4.4 and 4.5: wall structure, xylem, sclerenchyma, phloem

- Primary wall: loose mesh of microfibrils, allows growth.
- Secondary wall: layers of microfibrils at different angles, and lignin. Stronger, waterproof, rigid.
- Lignin deposits are in rings, spirals or networks in xylem, which leaves gaps between the thickened parts, where water can move sideways, and keeps flexibility.
- Strength comes from cellulose microfibrils plus lignin: strong in tension and compression.

| Feature | Sclerenchyma fibre | Xylem vessel | Phloem sieve tube |
|---|---|---|---|
| Function | Support | Support plus water and mineral ion transport | Translocation of organic solutes |
| Living? | No | No | Yes |
| Wall | Thick, lignified | Lignified, with pits | Thin, unlignified |
| Lumen | Narrow, empty | Wide, empty, no end walls | Cytoplasm present, sieve plates |
| Position in stem | Outer part of vascular bundles and cortex | Inner side of vascular bundle | Outer side of vascular bundle |
| Helper cells | None | None | Companion cells |

Plant fibres (flax, hemp, jute) are sclerenchyma fibres. Their high tensile strength and flexibility let people spin them into rope, cloth and paper and use them in composites.

## 4.6 Core Practical 7

| Task | Rules |
|---|---|
| Plan diagram | Low power, outlines of tissues only, no cells, no shading, single sharp lines, correct proportions, title, magnification, ruled label lines |
| Cell drawing | High power, a few cells, clear wall outlines, label the features you can see |
| Identify | Lignin stains help find xylem and sclerenchyma; sieve plates are easier to see in longitudinal section |

Typical stem order, from the outside: epidermis, cortex (with sclerenchyma), vascular bundles in a ring (phloem outermost, xylem innermost), pith.

Typical root order: epidermis, cortex, endodermis, vascular tissue in the centre.

Typical leaf order: upper epidermis, palisade mesophyll, spongy mesophyll with vascular bundles, lower epidermis with stomata.

## 4.7: sustainability

| Aspect | Oil-based plastic | Plant-based alternative |
|---|---|---|
| Source | Non-renewable crude oil | Renewable crops |
| Carbon dioxide | Released from fossil carbon | Taken up by the crop as it grows |
| End of life | Persists for a long time | Can often be composted or biodegraded |
| Drawbacks | Pollution, waste | Land, water and fertiliser use; energy for processing; some need industrial composting |

Plant-based products: starch-based plastics, fibre composites, paper packaging, natural rope and textiles.

## Worked reminders

**Scale bar.** A bar of 24 mm on an image stands for 2 µm. Magnification = 24 000 µm ÷ 2 µm = ×12 000. A structure that measures 18 mm on the image is 18 000 ÷ 12 000 = 1.5 µm long.

**Plan diagram.** A root section is 2.5 mm across and your drawing is 75 mm wide. Magnification = 75 ÷ 2.5 = ×30. A tissue 6 mm wide on the drawing is 6 ÷ 30 = 0.2 mm in the section.

**Mass of a polymer.** A chain of 500 glucose residues (Mr of glucose 180, water 18): 500 × 180 − 499 × 18 = 90 000 − 8 982 = 81 018.

## Describing and explaining: sentence frames

- **Structure to function:** "Cellulose chains are straight, so many hydrogen bonds form between neighbouring chains, producing microfibrils with high tensile strength."
- **Xylem:** "Lignin in the thickened wall prevents the vessel collapsing under the tension in the transpiration stream, and the empty lumen with no end walls gives an unobstructed tube."
- **Phloem:** "Sieve plates let the solution flow between sieve tube elements, and the companion cells supply the living functions that the sieve tube elements lack."
- **Sclerenchyma:** "Thick lignified walls and tapering, overlapping ends make the fibres strong and flexible, giving support to the stem."
- **Starch:** "Amylopectin has many branches, so many enzymes can act at once and glucose is released quickly."

## Linking tissues to their job

| Question to ask | Xylem | Sclerenchyma | Phloem |
|---|---|---|---|
| Does it need to be hollow? | Yes, for water flow | Almost, narrow lumen only | No, cytoplasm carries the solutes |
| Does it need to be waterproof? | Yes, lignin | Lignin gives resistance to decay | No, solutes move through the cell |
| Does it need living contents? | No | No | Yes, with the companion cell |
| Does it need to be strong? | Yes, resists collapse | Yes, supports and resists pulling | Moderate |

## Comparing electron microscope features

| Organelle | What you see | Common confusion |
|---|---|---|
| Chloroplast | Double membrane, grana stacks, stroma, starch grains | Mitochondrion has cristae, not stacked discs |
| Amyloplast | Double membrane, large pale starch grains, no grana | Looks like a chloroplast but has no thylakoids |
| Vacuole | One boundary line, plain interior | Often wrongly labelled as a vesicle; it is far larger |
| Cell wall | Thick, fibrous band outside the plasma membrane | Plasma membrane sits inside the wall |
| Plasmodesma | Narrow channel crossing the wall | Pit is a thin area, not a channel |

## Quick self-test

1. A nucleus image measures 28 mm and its actual diameter is 2.0 µm. What is the magnification?
2. A drawing of a cell is 45 mm long at ×600. What is the actual length in µm?
3. How many water molecules are released when a cellulose chain of 250 glucose residues forms?
4. A starch-based film loses mass in compost, from 62 g to 40 g. What is the percentage decrease?
5. Name the membrane around the vacuole.
6. Which plant tissue transports sucrose and is living?
7. What is the function of the middle lamella?
8. State two ways cellulose differs from starch.
9. Why do xylem vessels not collapse under tension?
10. State two features that identify a plan diagram as well drawn.

### Answers

1. 28 000 µm ÷ 2.0 µm = ×14 000
2. 45 000 µm ÷ 600 = 75 µm
3. 250 − 1 = 249
4. (62 − 40) ÷ 62 × 100 = 35.5%
5. The tonoplast.
6. Phloem (sieve tubes).
7. It cements neighbouring cells together.
8. Cellulose is made of β-glucose and is straight with hydrogen bonds between chains; starch is made of α-glucose and is coiled or branched.
9. Their walls are thickened and strengthened with lignin.
10. Any two: no individual cells, no shading, sharp lines, correct proportions, labelled, title and magnification.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "cellulose is a branched polymer" or "starch is made of β-glucose".
- Saying hydrogen bonds hold glucose residues together in a chain.
- Failing to state that many hydrogen bonds together, not one, give strength.
- Mixing up the tonoplast (vacuole membrane) and the plasma membrane.
- Calling chloroplasts and amyloplasts the same, and forgetting amyloplasts store starch.
- Not converting mm and µm before working out magnification.
- Drawing cells or shading in a plan diagram.
- Putting phloem inside the xylem in a stem diagram.
- Writing "strong" without saying why (lignin, microfibrils, hydrogen bonds).
- Evaluating bioplastics one-sidedly and ignoring land and energy use.
