This guide covers sub-topic **7.1 Structure of transport tissues** of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Biology 9700 syllabus for examination in 2025, 2026 and 2027. It is AS Level content, so it is examined on Paper 1 and Paper 2, and the drawing outcomes feed directly into the microscope work on Paper 3.

## What 7.1 asks you to do

| Outcome | Skill | What it means in practice |
|---|---|---|
| 7.1.1 | Draw | Plan diagrams of transverse sections (TS) of stems, roots and leaves of herbaceous dicotyledonous plants |
| 7.1.2 | Describe | Where xylem and phloem lie in each of those sections |
| 7.1.3 | Draw and label | Xylem vessel elements, phloem sieve tube elements and companion cells, from slides, photomicrographs and electron micrographs |
| 7.1.4 | Relate | Link each structural feature of those cells to its function |

"Herbaceous" means non-woody, so the sections show only primary tissues. "Dicotyledonous" means the vascular tissue follows the patterns below; a grass or maize stem, with bundles scattered through it, would not.

## Reading an unfamiliar section

Examiners often show a section without saying which organ it is. Work from the outside in.

1. **Is there a cuticle and a flattened shape with mesophyll?** It is a leaf. Find the midrib, where the largest vascular bundle lies.
2. **Is the vascular tissue in separate bundles arranged in a ring, with soft tissue (pith) in the centre?** It is a stem.
3. **Is there one central core of vascular tissue, with a star or cross of xylem and no pith?** It is a root. Root hairs on the epidermis confirm it.

Then decide which way up a leaf is. In a vein the xylem faces the **upper** surface and the phloem faces the **lower** surface. The palisade mesophyll is also next to the upper epidermis, so the two clues should agree.

### Why the tissues are where they are

The syllabus asks you to describe distribution, but the reasons help you remember it and are useful in "suggest" questions.

- **Stem**: the bundles form a ring towards the outside. The stem must resist bending in the wind, and lignified xylem towards the edge resists bending better than a central rod would.
- **Root**: the vascular tissue forms a central core. A root is pulled rather than bent as the shoot moves, and a central core resists pulling. The xylem is also close to the endodermis, which controls what enters it.
- **Leaf**: veins branch through the whole lamina, so no mesophyll cell is far from xylem (water) or phloem (removal of sugars).

## Plan diagrams in proportion

A plan diagram shows the **outlines of tissues**, never individual cells. Marks are given for correct proportions, so measure before you draw.

### Worked example 1: keeping proportions

A root TS is viewed with an eyepiece graticule. The whole root is 64 eyepiece units (epu) across, the central vascular core is 16 epu across, and the cortex on one side is 20 epu thick. You will draw the root 96 mm across.

```
scale factor = 96 mm ÷ 64 epu = 1.5 mm per epu
vascular core = 16 × 1.5 = 24 mm across
cortex thickness = 20 × 1.5 = 30 mm on each side
check: 30 + 24 + 30 = 84 mm, leaving 6 mm on each side for the epidermis
```

The core is one quarter of the root diameter (16 ÷ 64). If your drawing shows it at half the diameter, you lose the proportion mark however neat the lines are.

### Checklist for a plan diagram

- Fill at least half of the space provided.
- Use clear, single, continuous lines; no shading or cross-hatching.
- Draw one line for each tissue boundary: epidermis, cortex, endodermis (root), each vascular bundle (stem), xylem and phloem regions.
- In a stem, show every bundle you can see, in the correct positions, each with xylem towards the centre and phloem towards the outside.
- In a leaf, draw only the part asked for, often the midrib and a short length of lamina on each side.
- Rule label lines, end them exactly on the tissue, and do not let them cross.

## Drawing the cells (outcome 7.1.3)

The same cell looks different depending on the section and the microscope.

| Cell | In TS (light microscope) | In LS (light microscope) | In an electron micrograph |
|---|---|---|---|
| Xylem vessel element | Large, empty, rounded or polygonal outline with a thick, often darkly stained wall | Long tube; lignin seen as rings, spirals or a network with gaps (pits); end walls absent | No cytoplasm at all; thick layered wall; pits as gaps in the thickening |
| Sieve tube element | Wide cell with a thin wall; a sieve plate is sometimes caught in the section and looks like a sieve | Long cells joined end to end by sieve plates | Thin layer of cytoplasm against the wall; no nucleus; sieve plate with pores; plasmodesmata to the companion cell |
| Companion cell | Small, dense cell beside a sieve tube element, often with a visible nucleus | Narrow cell running alongside the sieve tube element | Large nucleus, many mitochondria, many ribosomes, dense cytoplasm |

When you draw cells at high power, draw cell walls with **two lines** (the wall has thickness), draw only what is visible, and keep the companion cell much narrower than its sieve tube element.

### Worked example 2: actual size from a scale bar

An electron micrograph has a scale bar labelled 20 µm that measures 16 mm. A sieve tube element in the image is 90 mm long.

```
magnification = image ÷ actual = 16 mm ÷ 20 µm = 16 000 µm ÷ 20 µm = × 800
actual length = 90 mm ÷ 800 = 90 000 µm ÷ 800 = 112.5 µm ≈ 113 µm
```

Convert to the same unit before dividing. Writing "16 ÷ 20 = 0.8" is the most common error here.

## Relating structure to function (outcome 7.1.4)

"Relate" needs a **feature plus a consequence for the job**. A list of features alone scores little.

### Xylem vessel elements

Their job is to carry water and mineral ions up the plant in a continuous column, and to help support it.

- **No cytoplasm, no end walls**: the vessel is a hollow tube, so there is nothing to slow the water and the column is unbroken from root to leaf.
- **Lignin in the walls**: water in the xylem is under tension, which pulls the walls inwards; lignin is strong, so the vessels do not collapse. It also makes the wall waterproof and adds to the support of the stem.
- **Pits**: areas where lignin is absent. Water can move sideways into neighbouring vessels and out to surrounding cells, and if one vessel is blocked by an air bubble, water can bypass it.
- **Narrow lumen**: a large proportion of the water is in contact with the walls, so adhesion of water to the walls helps hold the column up.

### Sieve tube elements

Their job is to carry sucrose and amino acids in solution, from sources to sinks, by mass flow.

- **No nucleus, few organelles, cytoplasm only as a thin layer at the edge**: the lumen is almost clear, so the sap flows with little resistance.
- **Sieve plates**: end walls perforated by pores, so sap flows from one element to the next while the end walls still give strength.
- **Living cell membrane**: needed to keep sucrose inside and to hold a high hydrostatic pressure; a dead tube could not do this.

### Companion cells

Their job is to keep the sieve tube element alive and to load assimilates into it.

- **Nucleus and many ribosomes**: make proteins, including the membrane proteins, for both cells.
- **Many mitochondria**: supply ATP for active transport, specifically the proton pumps used in loading sucrose.
- **Many plasmodesmata into the sieve tube element**: sucrose and other substances pass straight into the sieve tube element.

### Annotated model answer

*Question: Relate the structure of a companion cell to its function.* **[3]**

> Companion cells contain many mitochondria **(feature)**, which provide ATP for the proton pumps that load sucrose **(function)**. They have many plasmodesmata linking them to the sieve tube element **(feature)**, so sucrose passes directly into it **(function)**. They have a nucleus and many ribosomes **(feature)** to make proteins needed by the sieve tube element, which has no nucleus **(function)**.

Each sentence pairs one feature with one function, which is how mark schemes award the marks.

## Common mistakes

- Drawing cells or stippling inside a plan diagram.
- Showing a stem with one central vascular core, or a root with a ring of bundles.
- Putting phloem on the upper side of a leaf vein.
- Describing xylem vessels as living cells or sieve tube elements as having a nucleus.
- Saying lignin makes the vessel "permeable"; it makes the wall waterproof, and water crosses at the pits.
- Calling the companion cell "the cell that pumps sucrose" without mentioning mitochondria or ATP.
- Mixing units in magnification calculations.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Draw**: produce the diagram; for a plan diagram the examiner is checking outlines, proportions and the absence of cells.
- **Describe** (distribution): say where each tissue is relative to a named landmark: "xylem forms a central star; phloem lies between its arms".
- **Identify**: name the tissue or cell, often from a letter on a micrograph; one word or phrase is enough.
- **Relate** or **explain**: feature + "so" or "which" + function, one pair per mark.
- **Suggest**: apply the same ideas to an unfamiliar plant or cell; any reasonable biological link scores.
