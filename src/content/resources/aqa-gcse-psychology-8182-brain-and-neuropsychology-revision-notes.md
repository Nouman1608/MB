## Nervous system map

- **CNS** = brain + spinal cord.
- **PNS** = everything else, split into:
  - **somatic** -- senses and voluntary movement of skeletal muscles;
  - **autonomic** -- involuntary functions; **sympathetic** (fight-or-flight) and
    **parasympathetic** (rest and digest).

## Fight-or-flight, in order

Threat perceived → sympathetic branch activated → adrenal glands release **adrenaline** → heart
rate and breathing up, pupils dilate, blood to muscles, digestion slows → threat passes →
parasympathetic branch restores rest.

**Why it is adaptive:** the changes prepare the body to fight or run from physical danger: more oxygen and glucose reach the muscles, and non-urgent processes such as digestion are put on hold. **Why it can be unhelpful today:** many modern threats, such as exams or arguments, cannot be fought or run from, so the arousal is not used up, and repeated activation over long periods may harm health. The **parasympathetic** branch reverses the changes: heart rate and breathing slow, and digestion resumes.

**Somatic or autonomic?** Deciding to pick up a pen is **somatic** (voluntary, skeletal muscle). Your pupils widening in the dark or your heart pounding before a race is **autonomic** (involuntary).

## James-Lange theory of emotion

Event → **bodily change** → brain interprets the change → **emotion**. "We feel afraid because our
heart races." *Against:* many emotions share the same arousal. Evidence from people with spinal
cord injuries is mixed: some studies found weaker emotions after injury (which would support the
theory), while others found emotions were still felt strongly (which would not).

*For:* the theory explains why emotions feel physical, and why noticing a racing heart can make anxiety grow. *Further against:* the same bodily arousal (a pounding heart) occurs in fear, anger and excitement, so arousal alone cannot tell us which emotion we feel; emotions can also be felt very quickly, before bodily changes have time to develop. In an exam, name the order (event, physiological arousal, interpretation, emotion) before evaluating.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Sensory neuron | Receptors → CNS |
| Relay neuron | Connects neurons within the CNS |
| Motor neuron | CNS → muscles and glands |
| Synapse | Gap between two neurons |
| Neurotransmitter | Chemical messenger released into the synapse |
| Excitation | Makes the next neuron more likely to fire |
| Inhibition | Makes the next neuron less likely to fire |
| Cell assembly | Group of neurons strengthened by firing together (Hebb) |
| Localisation of function | Particular brain areas do particular jobs |
| Cognitive neuroscience | Study of how brain structure and function relate to thought and behaviour |

## Synaptic transmission in four steps

1. Impulse reaches the axon terminal.
2. Neurotransmitter released into the synapse.
3. Neurotransmitter binds to receptors on the next neuron.
4. Next neuron excited or inhibited.
5. **Reuptake**: the neurotransmitter is taken back up into the presynaptic neuron, which ends its effect and lets it be reused.

**Neuron structure:** **dendrites** receive signals; the **cell body** contains the nucleus; the **axon** carries the electrical impulse, often insulated by a **myelin sheath** that speeds it up; **axon terminals** release neurotransmitter. Transmission is **electrical** within a neuron and **chemical** across the synapse, and it only travels one way, from the presynaptic to the postsynaptic neuron.

**Reflex example:** touching a hot pan: receptors in the skin send a signal along a **sensory** neuron to the spinal cord, a **relay** neuron passes it on, and a **motor** neuron makes the arm muscles pull away, before the brain has registered pain.

**Hebb (1949):** repeated co-activation strengthens connections → learning physically changes the
brain ("fire together, wire together").
Hebb's ideas help explain why practice improves skills and why revision spread over time strengthens memories: repeated use makes the pathways more efficient. *Evaluation:* the theory was influential and later research on brain plasticity supports the general idea, but Hebb proposed it before brain activity could be observed directly in this detail.

## Brain areas

| Area | Function |
|---|---|
| Frontal lobe | Thinking, planning; **motor area**; **Broca's area** (speech production) |
| Parietal lobe | **Somatosensory area** (touch, temperature, pain) |
| Occipital lobe | **Visual area** |
| Temporal lobe | **Auditory area**, memory; **Wernicke's area** (understanding language) |
| Cerebellum | Balance and co-ordination |

Each hemisphere controls the **opposite** side of the body.

**Language areas:** **Broca's area** (usually left frontal lobe) is linked to **producing** speech; damage gives slow, effortful speech with comprehension largely kept. **Wernicke's area** (usually left temporal lobe) is linked to **understanding** language; damage can give fluent speech that makes little sense, with poor understanding of what others say.

**Cognitive neuroscience** links these structures to mental processes: for example, memory to the temporal lobe and to frontal areas (Tulving), and planning and decision-making to the frontal lobe.

## Studies at a glance

**Penfield -- interpretive cortex.** Awake epilepsy patients; temporal lobes stimulated with weak
current; some reported vivid experiences or memories.
*Evaluation:* direct evidence in living people; but few patients reported this, all had epilepsy,
experiences may not be real memories.

**Tulving (1989) -- 'gold' memory study.** Radioactive gold injected; tiny sample including Tulving;
thinking about episodic memories linked to **frontal** activity, semantic to **back** of cortex.
*Evaluation:* biological support for different memory types; but tiny, non-naive sample,
inconsistent results.

## Scans

| Scan | Shows | Method |
|---|---|---|
| CT | Structure | X-rays |
| PET | Activity | Radioactive tracer |
| fMRI | Activity | Blood-oxygen changes; no radiation |

| Scan | Strength | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| CT | Quick; good for finding damage such as tumours or bleeding | Shows structure only, not function; uses radiation |
| PET | Shows which areas are active during a task | Radioactive tracer; expensive; less precise about exact location than fMRI |
| fMRI | Shows activity with no radiation; fairly precise about location | Expensive; the person must keep very still in a noisy scanner; activity is measured indirectly through blood flow |

**Neurological damage:** stroke or injury → effects depend on location; motor area damage → weakness
on the opposite side; Broca's area damage → difficulty producing speech; frontal damage → changes in
planning, self-control and personality.
The classic case is **Phineas Gage**, a nineteenth-century railway worker whose behaviour was described as changing after an iron rod passed through his frontal lobe.

## Applying it to a scenario

*Scenario: after a head injury to the back of her brain, Maya has trouble with balance and her movements are clumsy, but her speech is normal.*

Three-step chain: **name** the structure (the **cerebellum**, at the back of the brain), **state its function** (balance and co-ordination of movement), and **link** to the detail (so damage there explains her clumsiness, while her speech areas in the frontal and temporal lobes are unaffected).

## Exam traps

- Reuptake is part of synaptic transmission: neurotransmitter is reabsorbed by the presynaptic neuron.
- Sympathetic = arousal; parasympathetic = calming. Do not swap them.
- James-Lange: the bodily change comes **before** the emotion.
- CT shows **structure**; PET and fMRI show **activity**.
- Broca's = production; Wernicke's = understanding.
- Penfield's patients were stimulated in the **temporal** lobes, and all had epilepsy.

## Self-test

1. Which branch of the autonomic nervous system produces fight-or-flight?
2. State the James-Lange order of events.
3. Where are relay neurons found?
4. What is the difference between excitation and inhibition?
5. Which lobe contains the visual area?
6. Which scan shows structure rather than activity?
7. What did Tulving find about episodic memory?
8. Why might a stroke in the left hemisphere weaken the right arm?
9. What happens during reuptake?
10. Which part of the PNS controls voluntary movement?
11. Give one strength and one limitation of fMRI.
12. What effect would damage to Wernicke's area be likely to have?

**Answers:** 1. The sympathetic branch. 2. Event, then bodily change, then interpretation of that
change as an emotion. 3. Within the CNS. 4. Excitation makes the next neuron more likely to fire;
inhibition makes it less likely. 5. The occipital lobe. 6. CT. 7. Thinking about episodic memories
was associated with more activity in frontal areas. 8. Each hemisphere's motor area controls the
opposite side of the body. 9. Neurotransmitter is taken back into the presynaptic neuron, ending its effect. 10. The somatic nervous system. 11. Strength: shows brain activity without radiation. Limitation: expensive, or the person must stay very still, or activity is measured indirectly. 12. Difficulty understanding language, with speech that may be fluent but make little sense.
