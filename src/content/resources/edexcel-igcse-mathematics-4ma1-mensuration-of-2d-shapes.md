This study guide goes deeper into section 4.9, Mensuration of 2D shapes, of topic 4 (Geometry and trigonometry) in the Pearson Edexcel International GCSE Mathematics A (4MA1) specification, Issue 2 (November 2017). Statements A to E are Foundation Tier content, which both tiers need. Statement A of the Higher Tier content, perimeters and areas of sectors of circles, is **Higher tier only**. The specification notes that radian measure is excluded, so every angle on this page is in degrees.

The [Geometry and trigonometry study guide](/resources/edexcel-igcse-maths-4ma1-geometry-and-trigonometry/) lists the formulae. This page shows how to choose the right one, how to convert units safely and how to build a perimeter or area for a shape made from several parts. Follow up with the [Mensuration of 2D shapes revision notes](/resources/edexcel-igcse-mathematics-4ma1-mensuration-of-2d-shapes-revision-notes/) and the [Mensuration of 2D shapes practice questions](/resources/edexcel-igcse-mathematics-4ma1-mensuration-of-2d-shapes-practice/).

## Which formulae are given in the examination?

The Foundation Tier formulae sheet gives the area of a trapezium, ½(a + b)h. The Higher Tier sheet gives the same formula. Neither sheet gives the circumference or area of a circle, or the formulae for a triangle, a parallelogram or a sector. Learn those. A trapezium has two parallel sides, a and b, and h is the **perpendicular** distance between them.

## Converting units of length and area (A)

Length units are easy to convert because you multiply or divide by 10, 100, 1000 and so on. Area units are harder, because an area unit is a square.

Think of 1 m². It is a square 100 cm by 100 cm, so it holds 100 × 100 = 10 000 cm². In general, **square the length conversion factor**.

| Length | Area |
|---|---|
| 1 cm = 10 mm | 1 cm² = 100 mm² |
| 1 m = 100 cm | 1 m² = 10 000 cm² |
| 1 km = 1000 m | 1 km² = 1 000 000 m² |

**Worked example 1.** Convert (a) 3.6 m² to cm², (b) 45 000 mm² to cm², (c) 0.0075 km² to m².

(a) Going from a large unit to a small one, multiply: 3.6 × 10 000 = **36 000 cm²**.

(b) Going from a small unit to a large one, divide: 45 000 ÷ 100 = **450 cm²**.

(c) 0.0075 × 1 000 000 = **7500 m²**.

A quick sense check: the number gets bigger when the unit gets smaller. A common mistake is to use 100 for cm² to m². That would be correct for lengths only.

## Perimeter of shapes made from triangles and rectangles (B)

The perimeter is the total distance around the edge. For a compound shape, find every outside edge. Work out any missing lengths from the lengths you are given.

**Worked example 2.** A rectangle measures 12 cm by 9 cm. A rectangle 5 cm by 4 cm is cut out of one corner, leaving an L-shape. Find its perimeter and area.

The cut-out is at the corner, so the L-shape has the same perimeter as the full 12 by 9 rectangle. The step in the edge uses up exactly the lengths that were removed from the two sides.

Perimeter = 2 × (12 + 9) = **42 cm**.

Area = 12 × 9 − 5 × 4 = 108 − 20 = **88 cm²**.

This shortcut works only when the cut-out sits in a corner. If the notch is in the middle of a side, you must add the extra edges.

Triangles in a perimeter question often need Pythagoras' theorem for a missing slant side. For a right-angled triangle with shorter sides 6 cm and 8 cm, the slant side is √(36 + 64) = 10 cm.

## Area of rectangles and triangles (C)

The area of a rectangle is length × width. The area of a triangle is

**area = ½ × base × perpendicular height**

The height must be at right angles to the base you choose. It is not usually a side of the triangle, except in a right-angled triangle.

**Worked example 3.** A triangle has a base of 14 cm and a perpendicular height of 9.5 cm. Area = ½ × 14 × 9.5 = **66.5 cm²**.

**Worked example 4.** A right-angled triangle has shorter sides 6 cm and 8 cm. Find the perpendicular height from the right angle to the hypotenuse.

Using the two short sides as base and height: area = ½ × 6 × 8 = 24 cm². The hypotenuse is 10 cm. Using it as the base: ½ × 10 × h = 24, so 5h = 24 and h = **4.8 cm**.

This works because the same area can be found from any side taken as the base. It is a useful way to find a missing height.

## Parallelograms and trapezia (D)

A **parallelogram** has two pairs of parallel sides. Its area is

**area = base × perpendicular height**

The slanting side is not the height. If you cut a right-angled triangle from one end and move it to the other, the parallelogram becomes a rectangle with the same base and height. That is why the formula has no ½.

**Worked example 5.** A parallelogram has a base of 11 cm, a slanting side of 7 cm and a perpendicular height of 6 cm. Find its area and its perimeter.

Area = 11 × 6 = **66 cm²**. Perimeter = 2 × (11 + 7) = **36 cm**. The height 6 cm is used for the area only, and the slanting side 7 cm is used for the perimeter only.

A **trapezium** has one pair of parallel sides, a and b. Its area is

**area = ½ × (a + b) × h**

**Worked example 6.** A trapezium has parallel sides of 8 cm and 14 cm, and they are 5 cm apart. Area = ½ × (8 + 14) × 5 = ½ × 22 × 5 = **55 cm²**.

**Worked example 7.** The area of a trapezium is 63 cm². Its parallel sides are 7 cm and 11 cm. Find the distance between them.

63 = ½ × (7 + 11) × h = 9h, so h = **7 cm**.

When a question gives the area and asks for a length, write the formula first, substitute, then solve the equation.

## Circles: circumference and area (E)

Two formulae connect the radius r and the diameter d = 2r:

- **circumference C = πd = 2πr**
- **area A = πr²**

Remember that the area uses the radius, not the diameter. If you are given the diameter, halve it before squaring.

**Worked example 8.** A circle has a radius of 6.5 cm. Find its circumference and its area to 3 significant figures.

C = 2 × π × 6.5 = 40.84... = **40.8 cm**.

A = π × 6.5² = π × 42.25 = 132.73... = **133 cm²**.

**Worked example 9.** A circle has a diameter of 15 m. Then r = 7.5 m, so A = π × 7.5² = π × 56.25 = 176.71... = **177 m²**.

If a question says "give your answer in terms of π", do not use the π button. Write 56.25π, or 225π/4, and stop.

**Working backwards.** The circumference of a circular track is 50 m. Find its radius. From C = 2πr we get r = 50 ÷ (2π) = 7.957... = **7.96 m**.

## Semicircles (E)

A semicircle is half a circle, so its area is half the circle's area, ½πr². Its perimeter has two parts: the curved edge, half the circumference (πr), and the straight edge, the diameter (2r). **Do not forget the diameter.**

**Worked example 10.** A semicircle has a diameter of 12 cm. So r = 6 cm.

Area = ½ × π × 6² = 18π = 56.5486... = **56.5 cm²**.

Perimeter = π × 6 + 12 = 18.849... + 12 = 30.849... = **30.8 cm**.

## Compound shapes with circles

Break the shape into parts that you know. Add areas for parts that are joined, and subtract for holes. For the perimeter, include only the outside edges.

**Worked example 11.** A shape is a rectangle 10 cm by 6 cm with a semicircle attached to one 6 cm side, so the diameter of the semicircle is 6 cm.

Area = 10 × 6 + ½ × π × 3² = 60 + 14.137... = 74.137... = **74.1 cm²**.

Perimeter: the two long sides (10 + 10), the opposite short side (6), and the curve (π × 3 = 9.424...). The side joined to the semicircle is not part of the outside. Perimeter = 26 + 9.424... = 35.424... = **35.4 cm**.

A shape with a hole, such as a circular pond with a path around it, is handled by subtraction: area of path = π × (outer radius)² − π × (inner radius)².

## Sectors of circles (Higher tier only)

A **sector** is the region between two radii and an arc. If the angle at the centre is θ degrees, the sector is a fraction θ/360 of the whole circle.

- **arc length = θ/360 × 2πr**
- **sector area = θ/360 × πr²**
- **sector perimeter = arc length + 2r**

**Worked example 12.** A sector has radius 9 cm and angle 140°. Find the arc length, the area and the perimeter.

The fraction is 140/360 = 7/18.

Arc length = 7/18 × 2 × π × 9 = 21.99... = **22.0 cm**.

Area = 7/18 × π × 81 = 98.96... = **99.0 cm²**.

Perimeter = 21.99... + 2 × 9 = 39.99... = **40.0 cm**.

**Finding the angle.** An arc of length 20 cm is cut from a circle of radius 8 cm. Set up 20 = θ/360 × 2π × 8, so θ = 20 × 360 ÷ (16π) = 143.23... = **143.2°**. Notice that you rearrange the equation; there is no need for a new formula.

**Finding the radius.** A sector of angle 100° has area 40 cm². Then 40 = 100/360 × πr², so r² = 40 × 360 ÷ (100π) = 45.83... and r = **6.77 cm**.

## Exam technique

- **Write the formula** before you substitute. Method marks are given for correct substitution into a correct formula.
- **Keep full calculator values** until the last line, and round only the final answer. State units: cm for length and perimeter, cm² for area.
- **"Give your answer in terms of π"** needs an exact answer with π in it.
- **Check the size.** A circle of radius 6.5 cm fits inside a 13 cm square, which has an area of 169 cm², so an area of 133 cm² is reasonable and 266 cm² is not.
- **Name the fraction** for a sector. Writing θ/360 first helps you see whether you are working with arc length or area.

## Common mistakes

- Using 100 as the factor for cm² to m². The factor is 10 000.
- Using the slanting side of a parallelogram or trapezium as the height.
- Using the diameter in πr² without halving it.
- Forgetting the straight edge (the diameter) in the perimeter of a semicircle or sector.
- Forgetting the ½ in the area of a triangle or a trapezium.
- Adding the edge where two parts of a compound shape join to the perimeter.
