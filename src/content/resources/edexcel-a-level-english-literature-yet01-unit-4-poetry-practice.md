context and different interpretations (AO1, AO2, AO3, AO5). Section A of this page gives short

## Section A: short questions

### Metaphysical Poets

**1.** Donne's "The Flea" opens: "Mark but this flea, and mark in this, / How little that which thou
deny'st me is". Explain how the opening establishes the poem as an argument. **[3]**

**2.** Marvell's "To His Coy Mistress" begins "Had we but world enough and time". Explain how this
conditional opening sets up the poem's structure. **[2]**

**3.** Explain what Samuel Johnson meant by his complaint that in Metaphysical poetry "heterogeneous
ideas are yoked by violence together", and give one way a student could challenge it. **[3]**

### The Romantics

**4.** In Blake's "London", explain the effect of the phrase "mind-forg'd manacles". **[3]**

**5.** In Keats's "Ode to a Nightingale", the speaker says "Forlorn! the very word is like a bell / To
toll me back from thee to my sole self!" Explain how these lines mark a turn in the poem. **[3]**

**6.** Explain how Wordsworth's phrase "what man has made of man", in "Lines Written in Early Spring",
complicates the poem's celebration of nature. **[2]**

### The Victorians

**7.** In Browning's "My Last Duchess", the Duke says "I gave commands; / Then all smiles stopped
together." Explain how these lines show the dramatic monologue revealing more than the speaker intends.
**[3]**

**8.** Rossetti's "Remember" ends: "Better by far you should forget and smile / Than that you should
remember and be sad." Explain how this ending turns the poem. **[2]**

**9.** Hardy's "The Darkling Thrush" closes with the speaker's sense of "Some blessed Hope, whereof he
knew / And I was unaware." Give **two** different interpretations of this ending. **[2]**

## Section B: statement essays

Each essay is marked out of 25. In your answer you must consider relevant contextual factors and
different interpretations. Refer to at least three prescribed poems from your movement.

**10. Metaphysical Poets.** "Metaphysical poetry is more concerned with cleverness than with
sincerity." Explore this view. **[25]**

**11. The Romantics.** "The Romantic poets look to nature for answers it cannot give." Explore this
view. **[25]**

**12. The Victorians.** "Victorian poets write most powerfully about what they cannot have." Explore
this view. **[25]**

---

## Answers

### Section A

**1.** The imperative "Mark" addresses the listener directly, like a speaker beginning a case (1); the
repetition "mark... mark" and the demonstrative "this flea" present the insect as evidence (1); the
logic that her refusal is as "little" as the flea sets up a persuasive, witty argument that the rest of
the poem develops (1).

**2.** The conditional "Had we" imagines an ideal situation that does not exist (1), setting up the
three-part argument in which "But" introduces reality and "Now therefore" draws the conclusion (1).

**3.** Johnson meant that the poets forced together images and ideas from unrelated areas, such as
science and love, in a strained or unnatural way (1). A valid challenge: the yoking of unlike ideas is
what creates the poems' meaning (1), developed with an example, such as the flea becoming marriage bed
and temple in Donne's poem, or with T. S. Eliot's argument that these poets fused thought and feeling
(1).

**4.** The metaphor makes oppression mental as well as physical (1); "mind-forg'd" suggests that people's
own beliefs, or the ideas imposed by authority, imprison them (1); the hard consonants and the image of
chains give the phrase force, so the poem's protest reaches beyond poverty to the way people think (1).

**5.** The word "Forlorn", repeated from the end of the previous stanza, breaks the speaker's imaginative
flight (1); the simile of a tolling bell, associated with death, pulls him back (1); "my sole self"
stresses isolation, so the poem turns from escape towards an acceptance of mortal limits (1).

**6.** The speaker's joy in spring is interrupted by grief at human cruelty or injustice (1), so nature
becomes a standard by which humanity is judged and found wanting rather than a simple escape (1).

**7.** The Duke's calm, brief statement conceals what it implies: that he had his wife silenced or
killed (1); the enjambment and the euphemism "all smiles stopped" make violence sound like an act of
household management (1); the reader understands his menace while he believes he is displaying power
and taste (1).

**8.** The speaker moves from asking to be remembered to releasing the beloved from grief (1), so the
volta turns a request into an act of selfless love, preferring his happiness to her own memory (1).

**9.** Any two (1 each): the thrush's song as a sign of hope at the turn of the century that the
speaker cannot share; the ending as irony, the "Hope" existing only for a bird and not for humanity; the
lines as tentative faith, suggesting the speaker is open to a hope he cannot name.

### Section B: how to mark questions 10 to 12

Marked holistically on AO1, AO2, AO3 and AO5. The bands below are Marlbridge's illustrative guidance, not
Pearson's published mark scheme.

| Marks | Typical features |
|---|---|
| 21–25 | Sophisticated, evaluative response; perceptive analysis of form and language across several poems; literary and historical context integrated; interpretations weighed |
| 16–20 | Clear argument; detailed analysis; relevant context and critical views discussed |
| 11–15 | Some argument; explanation of methods; context and interpretations present but undeveloped |
| 6–10 | Mainly paraphrase with some comment; context or critics bolted on |
| 1–5 | Little analysis; largely description |

**10. Metaphysical: indicative content.**

| AO | Points |
|---|---|
| AO1/AO2 | The playful argument of "The Flea"; the carpe diem logic of "To His Coy Mistress"; the conceits of "A Valediction of Weeping" and "The Definition of Love"; the plainer married love of Bradstreet's "A Letter to her Husband"; the intensity of Donne's Holy Sonnets |
| AO3 | Seventeenth-century learning and new science as the source of conceits; manuscript circulation among witty coteries; religious controversy |
| AO5 | Johnson's view of strained cleverness against Eliot's view of thought fused with feeling; readings of wit as a vehicle for sincerity rather than its opposite |

**11. Romantics: indicative content.**

| AO | Points |
|---|---|
| AO1/AO2 | "Ode to a Nightingale": the bird offers escape but the speaker is returned to his "sole self"; "Tintern Abbey": nature as moral guide, though the poem's hopes are framed by uncertainty; "Lines Written in Early Spring": nature cannot answer "what man has made of man"; "London": the answers are sought in the city, not in nature |
| AO3 | Industrialisation and urban poverty; the aftermath of the French Revolution; Wordsworth's Preface and the Romantic idea of feeling and the imagination |
| AO5 | Romanticism as escapism versus political engagement; critical readings of what "Tintern Abbey" leaves out; whether the poems' uncertainty is a weakness or their point |

**12. Victorians: indicative content.**

| AO | Points |
|---|---|
| AO1/AO2 | *In Memoriam* VII: the friend who cannot return; Rossetti's "Echo" and "Remember": love that exists in dream or memory; "My Last Duchess": the Duke's desire to possess what he could not control; Hardy's "The Darkling Thrush": hope the speaker cannot share; "Drummer Hodge": a home the soldier will never see |
| AO3 | Religious doubt and mourning culture; constraints on women's lives and voices; the Boer War; the end of the century |
| AO5 | Loss as personal grief or as wider loss of faith; the Duke as villain or as a study in self-deception; readings of Hardy as pessimist or as reluctant believer |

**What separates stronger answers.** They test the statement against poems that support and complicate
it, analyse form and language closely in three or four poems, and use literary context and critical
views to explain their judgement rather than decorate it.
