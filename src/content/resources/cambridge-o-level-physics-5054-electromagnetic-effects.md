## Two ideas behind everything here

1. **A changing magnetic field (or a conductor cutting field lines) induces
   an e.m.f.** — induction, generators and transformers.
2. **A current produces a magnetic field, and a current in a magnetic
   field feels a force** — relays, loudspeakers and motors.

## 4.5.1 Electromagnetic induction

**Experiment.** Connect a coil of wire to a sensitive centre-zero meter
(galvanometer). Push a bar magnet into the coil: the needle flicks one way.
Hold the magnet still inside: the needle returns to zero. Pull it out: the
needle flicks the other way. The same happens if the magnet is held still
and the coil is moved. An e.m.f. is induced only while the field through
the coil is **changing**, or while a wire is **cutting** field lines.

The size of the induced e.m.f. increases with:

- the **rate of change of the magnetic field**, or the rate at which field
  lines are cut — move the magnet faster, or use a stronger magnet
- the **number of turns** on the coil

**Lenz's law.** The current produced by an induced e.m.f. **opposes the
change that produces it**. Push an N pole into a coil and the induced
current makes the near end of the coil an **N pole**, which repels the
magnet. Pull the N pole out and the near end becomes an **S pole**, which
attracts it back. Either way, work must be done to move the magnet, and
that work is the source of the electrical energy.

**Demonstrating Lenz's law.** Find which way the meter deflects for a
current of known direction. Then push an N pole into the coil, read the
direction of the induced current, and use the right-hand grip rule to find
the pole at the coil's near end. It is an N pole, opposing the magnet.

## 4.5.2 The a.c. generator

A **rotating-coil generator** has a coil turned between the poles of a
magnet. Each end of the coil is joined to a **slip ring**, a complete
ring that turns with the coil. Carbon **brushes** press on the rings and
connect the coil to the external circuit without the wires twisting. In a
**rotating-magnet** generator, the magnet spins inside a fixed coil; since
the coil does not move, no slip rings are needed.

**Graph of e.m.f. against time.** As the coil turns steadily, the e.m.f.
varies like a smooth wave, positive for half a turn and negative for the
next. Linking the graph to the coil:

| Coil position (field horizontal) | Coil sides moving | E.m.f. |
|---|---|---|
| Plane of coil **parallel** to the field (horizontal) | Across the field lines, cutting at the fastest rate | **Maximum** (peak or trough) |
| Plane of coil **perpendicular** to the field (vertical) | Along the field lines, not cutting them | **Zero** |

So one full turn gives: zero → peak → zero → trough → zero. A peak and the
next trough are half a turn apart, and the coil passes through the zero
position twice per turn.

Turning the coil **faster** gives a larger peak e.m.f. **and** a higher
frequency (more cycles per second), so on the graph the waves become both
taller and closer together.

## 4.5.3 The magnetic effect of a current

**Straight wire.** The field lines are **concentric circles** centred on
the wire, in planes at right angles to it, more widely spaced further
away. The **right-hand grip rule** gives the direction: grip the wire with
the thumb along the conventional current, and the fingers curl in the
direction of the field.

**Solenoid.** Outside, the field is like that of a bar magnet; inside, the
lines are parallel and closely spaced, so the field is strong and nearly
uniform. Grip the coil with the fingers curling in the direction of the
current, and the thumb points to the **N pole** end.

For both: **increasing the current** makes the field stronger (lines closer
together); **reversing the current** reverses the field direction.

**Relays.** A relay uses a small current in a coil to switch a separate
circuit. Current in the coil magnetises its soft-iron core, which attracts
an iron armature; the armature pivots and pushes a pair of contacts
together (or apart). Applications: a car's ignition switch uses a small
current to operate the large starter-motor current; a low-voltage control
or sensor circuit can safely switch a mains device.

**Loudspeakers.** A coil attached to a paper or plastic cone sits in the
field of a permanent magnet. An alternating current from the amplifier
flows in the coil, so the force on the coil changes direction with each
reversal of the current. The coil and cone vibrate at the frequency of the
current, producing sound waves. A larger current gives larger vibrations
and a louder sound.

## 4.5.4 Forces on a current-carrying conductor

**Experiment.** Lay a short, light conductor (for example an aluminium rod
on two horizontal brass rails) between the poles of a U-shaped magnet, and
pass a current through it from a d.c. supply. The rod rolls along the
rails. **Reverse the current**: it moves the opposite way. **Reverse the
magnet** (swap the poles): it moves the opposite way again. Reversing both
leaves the direction unchanged.

**Relative directions — Fleming's left-hand rule.** Hold the thumb, first
finger and second finger of the left hand at right angles to one another.

- **F**irst finger: **F**ield (N to S)
- se**C**ond finger: **C**urrent (conventional, + to −)
- Thu**M**b: **M**otion (force)

The force is greatest when current and field are at right angles, and
there is no force when they are parallel.

**Parallel conductors.** Each wire sits in the circular field of the
other. When the currents are in the **same direction**, the two fields
point in opposite directions in the region between the wires and partly
cancel, so the field there is weak; the wires are pushed together:
**attraction**. When the currents are **opposite**, the fields between the
wires point the same way and add, so the field there is strong; the wires
are pushed apart: **repulsion**.

## 4.5.5 The d.c. motor

A current-carrying coil in a magnetic field experiences forces in
opposite directions on its two sides, producing a **turning effect**. The
turning effect increases with:

- the **number of turns** on the coil
- the **current**
- the **strength of the magnetic field**

**The split-ring commutator.** Without it, the coil would rock to the
vertical position and stop, because after half a turn the forces would
turn it back. The commutator is a ring split into two halves, connected to
the coil ends and turning with it; fixed carbon **brushes** press on it.
Every half turn, as the coil passes the vertical, the gaps pass the brushes
and the connections swap, **reversing the current in the coil**. The force
on the side now nearer each pole is therefore always in the same direction,
and the coil keeps rotating the same way. Its momentum carries it past the
vertical position, where the turning effect is momentarily zero.

## 4.5.6 The transformer

**Structure.** Two coils, the **primary** and the **secondary**, wound on a
common **soft-iron core**.

**Principle.** An alternating current in the primary produces a magnetic
field in the core that is **continually changing**. The core links this
changing field to the secondary, where it **induces an alternating e.m.f.**
A steady direct current would produce a constant field, inducing nothing
once the current was established, so transformers work only with a.c.

```
Vp / Vs = Np / Ns        p = primary, s = secondary
```

A **step-up** transformer has more turns on the secondary (Vs > Vp); a
**step-down** transformer has fewer (Vs < Vp).

**Worked example 1.** A phone charger contains a transformer with 2000
primary turns, connected to 230 V. The output is 11.5 V.

```
Ns = Np × Vs / Vp = 2000 × 11.5 / 230 = 100 turns   (step-down)
```

**High-voltage transmission.** For a given power, P = I V, so a **higher
voltage means a smaller current** in the cables. The cables have
resistance; the energy transferred to heat in them each second is the
current multiplied by the p.d. across the cables (which is itself I × R).
A smaller current therefore means much smaller losses, and thinner,
cheaper cables can be used.

**Worked example 2.** 50 kW is sent through cables of total resistance
2.0 Ω.

```
at 1000 V:    I = 50 000 / 1000 = 50 A
              p.d. across cables = 50 × 2.0 = 100 V
              power lost = 50 × 100 = 5000 W
at 10 000 V:  I = 50 000 / 10 000 = 5.0 A
              p.d. across cables = 5.0 × 2.0 = 10 V
              power lost = 5.0 × 10 = 50 W
```

Ten times the voltage gives one-hundredth of the power loss. Step-up
transformers raise the voltage for transmission; step-down transformers
lower it for consumers.

## Common mistakes

- Saying an e.m.f. is induced when a magnet is held still in a coil. The
  field must be changing.
- Using the right hand for the motor effect. Fleming's rule for force is
  the **left**-hand rule.
- Confusing slip rings (a.c. generator) with the split-ring commutator
  (d.c. motor).
- Stating that the generator e.m.f. is maximum when the coil is vertical
  (perpendicular to the field). That is the zero position.
- Saying transformers work on d.c.
- Writing that high-voltage transmission reduces the power *sent*. It
  reduces the current and so the power *lost*.

## Exam technique for Cambridge command words

- **Describe an experiment**: name the apparatus, what is moved or
  reversed, and what is observed each time.
- **State and use Lenz's law**: give the law, then name the pole induced
  and say it opposes the motion.
- **Explain** the commutator or the transformer: give the sequence of
  cause and effect in order, using "changing" and "reverses" precisely.
- **Sketch** an e.m.f.–time graph: label axes, show a regular wave, and if
  asked, mark which coil position gives each peak and zero.
