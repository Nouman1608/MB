This guide covers subtopic **4.2 Geometrical constructions** in the Cambridge IGCSE Mathematics 0580 syllabus for 2025, 2026 and 2027. The Core (C4.2) and Extended (E4.2) content is identical and has three statements:

| Statement | Syllabus notes |
|---|---|
| 1 Measure and draw lines and angles. | A ruler should be used for all straight edges. Constructions of perpendicular bisectors and angle bisectors are not required. |
| 2 Construct a triangle, given the lengths of all sides, using a ruler and pair of compasses only. | e.g. construct a rhombus by drawing two triangles. Construction arcs must be shown. |
| 3 Draw, use and interpret nets. | Draw nets of cubes, cuboids, prisms and pyramids; use measurements from nets to calculate volumes and surface areas. |

Every candidate needs a pair of compasses, a protractor and a ruler for every paper. The basic three-side triangle construction, a rhombus from two triangles and two net calculations are in the [geometrical terms, constructions and scale drawings study guide](/resources/igcse-mathematics-0580-geometrical-terms-constructions-and-scale-drawings/). This page adds the skills around them: reflex angles, checking a triangle is possible, other quadrilaterals built from triangles, and reading nets. Afterwards, use the [geometrical constructions revision notes](/resources/cambridge-igcse-mathematics-0580-geometrical-constructions-cambridge-igcse-maths-revision-notes/) and the [geometrical constructions practice questions](/resources/cambridge-igcse-mathematics-0580-geometrical-constructions-cambridge-igcse-maths-practice/).

## Measuring and drawing lines

- Line the **zero mark** up with the start of the line, not the end of the ruler, which is often a few millimetres before zero.
- Measure to the nearest **millimetre**: 6.4 cm, not "about 6 cm".
- Use a sharp pencil; a thick line can hide a millimetre.

## Measuring and drawing angles

A protractor has two scales. Put its centre on the vertex and its zero line along one arm, then read the scale that starts at **0 on that arm**.

**Estimate first.** If the angle looks acute, your reading must be less than 90°. This one check removes the most common error, reading 110° instead of 70°.

### Reflex angles

A protractor only reaches 180°. To **measure** a reflex angle, measure the angle on the other side and subtract it from 360°. To **draw** one, work out 360° minus the reflex angle and draw that instead, on the correct side.

### Worked example 1: drawing a reflex angle

Draw angle PQR = 215°.

```
360 - 215 = 145
1. Draw QR with a ruler.
2. Measure 145 degrees at Q from QR, on the side that leaves
   the reflex angle where the question wants it.
3. Draw QP through your mark.
4. Mark the reflex angle with an arc on the outside, labelled 215.
```

## Constructing a triangle from three sides

Ruler and compasses only, arcs left visible. Before you start, check the triangle exists: **each side must be shorter than the sum of the other two**. Sides 3 cm, 4 cm and 8 cm cannot form a triangle, because 3 + 4 = 7 is less than 8; the arcs would not meet.

The triangle can be drawn above or below the base. Both are correct, because they are congruent. If the question shows a sketch, match its orientation.

### Worked example 2: construct, then measure

Construct triangle PQR with PQ = 8 cm, QR = 6.5 cm and PR = 5 cm. Measure angle QPR.

```
Check: 5 + 6.5 = 11.5 > 8, so the triangle exists.
1. Draw PQ = 8 cm.
2. Compasses set to 5 cm, point on P: draw an arc.
3. Compasses set to 6.5 cm, point on Q: draw an arc to cross the first.
4. Label the crossing R. Join PR and QR with a ruler.
5. Measure angle QPR: about 54 degrees.
```

The other angles measure about 39° at Q and 87° at R. Their sum is 180°, which is a quick check on all three readings. A common slip is to set the compasses to 5 cm from Q instead of from P: read which side joins which two letters.

## Quadrilaterals from two triangles

The syllabus example builds a rhombus from two triangles on a shared diagonal. The same idea constructs any quadrilateral whose sides and one diagonal are known.

| Shape | The two triangles |
|---|---|
| Rhombus | two congruent isosceles triangles on a diagonal |
| Kite | two different isosceles triangles on the same diagonal (the line of symmetry) |
| Parallelogram | two congruent triangles placed on a diagonal with the sides swapped |

### Worked example 3: a kite

Construct kite ABCD with diagonal AC = 9 cm, AB = AD = 4 cm and CB = CD = 7 cm. Measure the other diagonal BD.

```
Check: 4 + 7 = 11 > 9.
1. Draw AC = 9 cm.
2. Compasses 4 cm from A and 7 cm from C, above AC: arcs cross at B.
3. Same radii below AC: arcs cross at D.
4. Join AB, BC, CD and DA. Keep all four arcs.
5. Measure BD: about 6.0 cm.
```

### Worked example 4: a parallelogram

Construct parallelogram EFGH with EF = 7 cm, FG = 4 cm and diagonal EG = 9 cm.

```
Triangle EFG: draw EG = 9 cm; arcs 7 cm from E and 4 cm from G meet at F.
Triangle EGH: on the other side, arcs 4 cm from E and 7 cm from G meet at H
              (opposite sides of a parallelogram are equal: EH = FG, GH = EF).
Join the sides.
```

Swapping the radii for the second triangle is the step people miss. Using 7 cm from E again gives a kite, not a parallelogram.

## Nets

A net is a flat shape that folds into a solid. To draw one, sketch the solid, list its faces, then lay them edge to edge so that edges which meet in the solid are equal in the net.

**Interpreting a cube net.** In a row of four squares, the first and third are opposite faces, as are the second and fourth. The two squares attached on either side of the row are opposite each other. There are 11 different nets of a cube; a 2 × 3 block of six squares is not one of them.

### Worked example 5: a cuboid from its net

A net has a row of four rectangles, each 4 cm high, with widths 3 cm, 6 cm, 3 cm and 6 cm. Two rectangles 6 cm by 3 cm are attached above and below one of the 6 cm rectangles. Find the surface area and volume of the cuboid.

```
Dimensions: 6 cm by 3 cm by 4 cm
Surface area = total area of the net
             = 2(6 x 3) + 2(6 x 4) + 2(3 x 4)
             = 36 + 48 + 24 = 108 cm^2
Volume = 6 x 3 x 4 = 72 cm^3
```

### Worked example 6: an isosceles triangular prism

A net has two isosceles triangles with base 6 cm and equal sides 5 cm, and three rectangles of length 8 cm. Find the surface area and volume of the prism.

```
Triangle height: sqrt(5^2 - 3^2) = sqrt(16) = 4 cm
Triangle area = 1/2 x 6 x 4 = 12 cm^2
Rectangles: (5 + 5 + 6) x 8 = 128 cm^2
Surface area = 2 x 12 + 128 = 152 cm^2
Volume = cross-section x length = 12 x 8 = 96 cm^3
```

The prism and pyramid volume formulas are on the formula list printed in the paper.

## Common mistakes

- Rubbing out construction arcs: the syllabus says they must be shown.
- Drawing lines freehand: a ruler is needed for all straight edges.
- Reading the wrong protractor scale.
- Measuring a reflex angle as the smaller angle.
- Swapping which side length goes from which point.
- Drawing a "net" with a face missing, two faces overlapping, or edges that do not match.
- Using a sloping edge of a triangular face as the triangle's height.

## Exam technique

- **Construct**: an accurate drawing with ruler and compasses, arcs visible.
- **Measure**: give the reading with units, to the nearest millimetre or degree.
- **Draw** a net: use a ruler, label lengths if the question asks, and count the faces at the end.
- **Calculate** from a net: write which dimensions you are using before you multiply.
