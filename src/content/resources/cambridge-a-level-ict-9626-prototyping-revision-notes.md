## What a prototype is and why it helps

- A **prototype** is an early working model of all or part of a system.
- Users react to something real, so misunderstandings and design problems are found early, when they are cheap to fix.
- It lets risky ideas be tested and keeps users involved.

## The four types

| Type | What it is | Why needed |
|---|---|---|
| **Evolutionary** | Built, tested with users, improved again and again. The final version becomes the real system | Requirements are unclear or changing |
| **Incremental** | Separate prototypes for each component, then combined into the whole system | Large system that can be split into parts |
| **Throwaway** | Built to find out what is needed, then discarded; the real system is built afresh | Requirements are poorly understood; mistakes are costly |
| **Rapid** | Built as quickly as possible with fast tools; may be discarded or developed | Fast feedback is needed |

## Advantages and disadvantages

| Type | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|
| **Evolutionary** | User involved throughout; no wasted work; easy to include changes; working software early | No clear end date or cost; structure can become messy; documentation and testing neglected; feature creep |
| **Incremental** | Parts built at the same time; each tested alone; useful parts early; easier to manage | Components must fit, so overall design needed; integration problems late; whole system seen late |
| **Throwaway** | Clarifies requirements before the real build; quality not important; cuts risk of building the wrong thing; bad ideas dropped cheaply | Prototype effort wasted; users may think it is nearly finished; extra time and cost; pressure to keep it |
| **Rapid** | Quick user feedback; misunderstandings found early; cheap to change; encourages involvement | Low quality; may omit validation, security and error handling; false expectations of speed; needs tools and skills |

## Rapid vs throwaway

- **Rapid** = how it is built (very fast).
- **Throwaway** = what happens to it (discarded).
- A prototype may be both; a rapid prototype need not be thrown away.

## Incremental prototyping vs incremental development

- **Incremental prototyping:** trial versions of components are built, then combined.
- **Incremental development:** finished, working parts are delivered one after another.

## Worked example

A bakery chain wants an ordering app, but the owners cannot say how store managers want to enter orders.

- **Throwaway or rapid:** build clickable screens in two days, let managers try them, then discard them and write the real app.
- **Evolutionary:** build a basic version, let managers use it, and keep improving it until it is the finished app.
- **Incremental:** build the ordering, delivery and stock components as separate prototypes, then join them.

The best choice depends on how well the requirements are known, and how much change is expected.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Evolutionary** (kept, becomes the system) vs **throwaway** (discarded).
- **Incremental** (components combined) vs **evolutionary** (one prototype grows).
- **Rapid** (speed) vs **throwaway** (fate).
- **Prototype** (working model) vs **specification** (written description).

## Quick self-test

1. What is a prototype?
2. Which type becomes the final system?
3. Which type is built from components that are combined?
4. Give one reason for building a throwaway prototype.
5. Give one disadvantage of evolutionary prototyping.
6. Why might users expect the final system too soon after seeing a rapid prototype?
7. Give two reasons why prototypes are useful.
8. Which type is suitable when requirements are changing?
9. State one risk of incremental prototyping.
10. What happens to a throwaway prototype?

### Answers

1. An early, working model of all or part of a system.
2. Evolutionary prototyping.
3. Incremental prototyping.
4. Any one: to find out what users need; to test an idea cheaply; to avoid building the wrong system.
5. Any one: no clear end date or cost; the structure can become messy; feature creep; neglected documentation.
6. The prototype looks and behaves like the real thing, so users think most of the work has been done.
7. Any two: users react to something real; misunderstandings found early; designs tested; risks tested; users are involved.
8. Evolutionary prototyping.
9. The components may not fit together, so integration problems appear late.
10. It is discarded once it has been used to clarify the requirements, and the real system is built afresh.

## Exam technique

- State what each type is, and when it suits the project: unclear requirements, a large system split into parts, a need for fast feedback.
- Always say what happens to the prototype: kept and refined (evolutionary), combined (incremental) or discarded (throwaway).
- Pair every advantage with a disadvantage for the same type.
- Use the scenario: who the users are, how often requirements change, how much time there is.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing all prototypes as thrown away.
- Mixing up incremental prototyping with incremental development.
- Naming a type without saying why it is needed.
- Giving the same advantage for each type.
- Giving only advantages.
- Describing a prototype as a paper plan.

## Frequently asked questions

**Which prototype type becomes the final system?**
The evolutionary prototype, which is refined repeatedly until it is the finished system.

**Why might throwaway prototyping be worth the wasted effort?**
Because finding out the real requirements early is cheaper than building the wrong system and changing it later.

**What is the main risk of rapid prototyping?**
Speed can cause poor quality, and users may expect the final system to be delivered as quickly.
