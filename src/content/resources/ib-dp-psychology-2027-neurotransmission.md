The learning objective for neurotransmission is "the process of neurotransmission and how an understanding of the process of neurotransmission allows psychologists to improve health and wellness". That sentence has two parts: you must be able to describe the process, and you must be able to show how knowing it is useful. This guide teaches both. ## The process of neurotransmission

Neurons communicate by electrical signals within a cell and chemical signals between cells. **Neurotransmission** is the chemical stage. Learn it as a sequence.

1. **Action potential.** An electrical impulse travels along the axon of the **presynaptic neuron** to the axon terminal.
2. **Release.** The impulse causes **vesicles** containing neurotransmitter molecules to fuse with the membrane and release their contents into the **synaptic cleft**, the tiny gap between neurons.
3. **Binding.** Neurotransmitter molecules cross the cleft and bind to **receptors** on the **postsynaptic neuron**. A receptor works like a lock that only matching molecules (keys) fit.
4. **Effect.** Binding makes the postsynaptic neuron more likely to fire (an **excitatory** effect) or less likely to fire (an **inhibitory** effect). Whether it fires depends on the sum of excitatory and inhibitory inputs it receives.
5. **Ending the signal.** The neurotransmitter is cleared from the cleft by **reuptake** (taken back into the presynaptic neuron through transporter proteins) or by **enzymes** that break it down. This stops the signal and allows it to be repeated.

**Excitatory and inhibitory.** Glutamate is the main excitatory neurotransmitter in the brain, and GABA is the main inhibitory one. Others, such as serotonin, dopamine, noradrenaline and acetylcholine, have varied effects depending on the receptor type and the brain region.

| Neurotransmitter | Linked to (examples, not exhaustive) |
|---|---|
| Serotonin | Mood, sleep, appetite |
| Dopamine | Reward, motivation, movement |
| GABA | Calming, reducing neural activity, anxiety |
| Glutamate | Learning and memory, general excitation |
| Acetylcholine | Muscle control, attention, memory |
| Noradrenaline | Alertness, arousal, stress response |

**Be careful with wording.** A neurotransmitter is not "the chemical for" one emotion. Each system affects many behaviours, and each behaviour involves many systems. Revise each as a two-part claim: what the chemical does at the synapse, and what behaviour that mechanism is proposed to explain.

## How drugs and treatments change neurotransmission

Understanding the stages shows exactly where a substance can act.

| Type of action | What it does | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Agonist | Mimics or boosts a neurotransmitter's effect | Nicotine acts on acetylcholine receptors |
| Antagonist | Blocks a receptor so the neurotransmitter cannot act | Some antipsychotic drugs block dopamine receptors |
| Reuptake inhibitor | Blocks removal from the cleft so the neurotransmitter acts for longer | SSRIs (serotonin); cocaine blocks dopamine reuptake |
| Precursor | Supplies the raw material for making the neurotransmitter | L-DOPA is converted to dopamine in Parkinson's disease treatment |
| Enhancer of inhibition | Increases the effect of an inhibitory transmitter | Benzodiazepines enhance GABA's effect |

**Worked example (SSRIs).** Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors block the transporters that return serotonin to the presynaptic neuron. More serotonin remains in the synaptic cleft, so serotonin receptors on the postsynaptic neuron are stimulated more. The drugs are prescribed for depression and anxiety. A strong answer then adds evaluation: the drugs change serotonin availability within hours but mood effects take weeks, so the story cannot be as simple as "low serotonin causes low mood"; a large review (Moncrieff et al., 2022) concluded that there is no consistent evidence that depression is caused by low serotonin; and meta-analyses suggest antidepressants are more effective than placebo overall (Cipriani et al., 2018) though the average benefit is modest (Kirsch et al., 2008, found larger differences for severe depression than for mild or moderate). Placebo effects are strong, so controlled trials are essential.

**Worked example (Parkinson's disease).** Parkinson's disease involves loss of dopamine-producing neurons in a movement-related region. Giving the precursor L-DOPA allows the brain to make more dopamine and reduces movement symptoms. This shows how understanding the process improves health directly. A limitation is that the benefit often declines over years and side effects occur, and the treatment does not stop the loss of neurons.

**Worked example (reward and addiction).** Olds and Milner (1954) found that rats would press a lever to receive electrical stimulation of certain brain regions, which became associated with reward systems rich in dopamine. Many addictive drugs boost dopamine activity in reward pathways, for example cocaine by blocking reuptake. Understanding this helps explain why drugs are reinforcing and supports treatments that target reward systems or cravings. The animal evidence raises ethical and generalisation questions, and addiction also involves learning, stress and social factors.

## How understanding neurotransmission allows psychologists to improve health and wellness

This is the second half of the learning objective. Build your answer around three uses:

1. **Treatment.** Drugs that act on specific stages (SSRIs for depression, L-DOPA for Parkinson's, GABA-enhancing drugs for severe anxiety) are designed from knowledge of the synapse.
2. **Explanation and diagnosis.** Models of neurotransmitter involvement guide research into disorders and individual differences in response to drugs.
3. **Prevention and advice.** Knowing how substances affect the reward system supports public health advice about addiction risk, sleep and stress.

A balanced answer also states the cautions: neurotransmitters work in networks, so changing one affects others; drugs have side effects; drug effects do not prove the cause of a disorder; and a purely chemical account is reductionist, so psychological therapies and social support remain important.

## Evaluating the study of neurotransmission

- **Strengths:** a clear mechanism that can be tested; links directly to effective treatments; supported by animal experiments and by controlled drug trials.
- **Limitations:**
  - *Measurement:* in living humans, neurotransmitter activity is measured indirectly (through brain scans that track chemicals, drug effects or blood and spinal fluid samples), so findings are inferences.
  - *Animal research:* much of the detail comes from rats and primates, which raises ethical concerns and generalisation limits.
  - *Reverse inference:* showing that a drug changes a transmitter and behaviour does not show that the transmitter was the original cause.
  - *Reductionism:* it neglects thoughts, social context and culture.
  - *Gene-environment interaction:* the claim that variation in a serotonin transporter gene interacts with stress to affect depression (Caspi et al., 2003) generated great interest, but later large analyses were not consistent in replicating it, which illustrates why single studies need replication.
- **Concepts:** causality, measurement, perspective (reductionism) and responsibility (animal research, medication use).

## Neurotransmitters, hormones and the wider system

The guide groups neurotransmission with chemical messengers in general. Compare the two systems when you revise. Neurotransmitters act across a gap of a few nanometres, and the signal lasts milliseconds before reuptake or breakdown. Hormones are released by glands into the blood and act on distant targets over seconds to hours. Some chemicals do both jobs: noradrenaline is a neurotransmitter in the brain and, as adrenaline, a hormone released in the stress response. A good answer can use this overlap to show that the systems interact. For example, a stressful event triggers the HPA axis to release cortisol, and cortisol in turn changes the sensitivity of serotonin and dopamine systems. This makes it hard to point to a single chemical as the cause of a behaviour, and it supports the diathesis-stress model, in which biological vulnerability and environmental stress combine.

## Using neurotransmission in an exam answer

- *Section A:* describe the process in four or five steps with correct terms, and add one example of a drug or condition.
- *Section B:* apply the process to a scenario, such as a person taking an SSRI or a drug user; say which step is affected and what behaviour or symptom follows.
- *Section C:* use neurotransmission as part of a wider argument, weighing biological explanations against cognitive and sociocultural ones, with at least two concepts.

## Common mistakes

- Saying that a neurotransmitter "travels along the neuron". It crosses the synaptic cleft.
- Confusing hormones (carried in the blood) with neurotransmitters (released at synapses).
- Writing "serotonin is the happiness chemical".
- Forgetting the second half of the learning objective: how understanding improves health and wellness.
- Treating a drug effect as proof of the cause of a disorder.
