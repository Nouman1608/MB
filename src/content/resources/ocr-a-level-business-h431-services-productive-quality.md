## What a service is

A **service** is an activity that one party carries out for another, where the main benefit is the activity itself and not the ownership of a physical product. Examples include a haircut, a bank loan, a train journey, a repair, legal advice and a hotel stay.

Services differ from goods in four ways.

- **Intangible.** The customer cannot touch or inspect a service before buying it.
- **Inseparable.** The service is produced and consumed at the same time, often with the customer present.
- **Variable.** Quality depends on who delivers it and when, so two customers may receive different experiences.
- **Perishable.** A service cannot be stored. An empty seat on a flight or an unused appointment slot is revenue lost for ever.

These features explain why quality is harder to control in services. There is no finished product to inspect before it reaches the customer, so quality depends on the people, the system and the customer's experience throughout.

## How a service is provided

A useful way to describe the process is to follow the customer through **front-of-house** activities that the customer sees and **back-office** activities that they do not.

Take a car repair garage (invented: Penrith Motors).

1. **Booking.** The customer calls, books online or walks in. The staff member records the details of the car and the fault.
2. **Arrival and diagnosis.** A technician inspects the car, and a quote is agreed with the customer.
3. **Back-office work.** Parts are ordered, a mechanic does the repair, and the work is checked.
4. **Handover.** The customer is told what was done, pays and receives the car.
5. **Follow-up.** The garage records the job, sends a reminder for the next service and asks for feedback.

At each step the **inputs** are staff time and skills, premises and equipment, parts and information. The **process** includes the contact with the customer. The **output** is the outcome (a repaired car) and the experience (how the customer was treated). Customers judge both. A perfect repair after three days of poor communication may still be rated poorly.

A business that wants consistent quality designs the process: it writes standard steps, trains staff, uses booking and tracking systems, and sets the points where the customer should be updated.

## Services and the UK economy

Services are the largest part of the UK economy. In recent years they have accounted for around four-fifths of national output and a similar share of jobs, according to official statistics. They include finance, retail, health, education, tourism, transport, communications and professional services such as law and accountancy.

Their importance:

- **Employment.** Services employ most workers, with a wide range of skills and pay.
- **Output and tax.** They produce most of the country's income and generate tax revenue.
- **Exports.** The UK exports services such as finance, insurance, consulting and education, which earn income from overseas.
- **Support for other sectors.** Manufacturers rely on transport, finance, advertising and maintenance services.
- **Growth.** Many services can expand without large factories, and digital delivery lets them reach customers abroad.

Limits and risks: the economy depends on a few sectors, such as finance, and services are sensitive to consumer spending in a recession. Some service jobs are low paid or insecure, and the pattern of jobs varies across regions.

## Measuring service quality

Quality of service can be **monitored** continuously or **measured** at set times. Methods include:

- **Customer satisfaction surveys.** Questions after a visit or purchase. Simple to compare over time, but only some customers respond, and answers may reflect mood.
- **Complaints and compliments.** The number and type show where the service is failing. Many unhappy customers never complain, so a low figure may be false comfort.
- **Mystery shoppers.** Trained people pose as customers and score the service against a checklist. This gives detailed, objective evidence, but covers a small number of visits and is costly.
- **Repeat custom and retention.** The share of customers who return shows loyalty.
- **Online reviews and social media.** Quick and public, but unrepresentative and open to bias.
- **Operational measures.** Waiting times, call-answering speed, first-time fix rate and delivery times.
- **Staff feedback.** Employees often see problems before customers complain.

Strong businesses combine several methods and track them over time against a target or a competitor.

### Worked example 1: monitoring a hotel

Harbour Hotel (invented) had 4,800 guests last year and 5,400 this year. Guest complaints were 96 last year and 81 this year. Average wait to check in was 11 minutes against a target of 8.

Complaints per 1,000 guests: last year 96 ÷ 4,800 × 1,000 = **20**; this year 81 ÷ 5,400 × 1,000 = **15**.

The number of complaints fell by 15, but the rate fell by 25%, a more accurate comparison because the hotel was busier. The check-in wait is 3 minutes, or 37.5%, above target, which is a likely source of dissatisfaction.

Evaluation: the falling complaint rate is encouraging, but complaints measure only the unhappy guests who speak up. The hotel should add surveys, reviews and a mystery shopper visit, and ask why the check-in wait is long.

## Impact of poor customer service

For the business:

- lost customers and lost repeat custom;
- damage to reputation, spread by reviews and word of mouth;
- the cost of dealing with complaints and refunds;
- lower staff morale and higher staff turnover, because staff dislike dealing with angry customers;
- loss of market share to rivals.

For stakeholders:

| Stakeholder | Effect of poor service |
|---|---|
| Owners | Lower profit and weaker business value |
| Employees | Stress, lower pay or bonus, risk of job loss |
| Customers | Wasted time and money; dissatisfaction |
| Suppliers | Fewer orders as sales fall |
| Community | Lost jobs if the business declines |

### Worked example 2: the cost of poor service

Harbour Hotel loses 3% of its 4,800 guests each year because of poor service. The average guest spends £220 and the hotel makes a profit margin of 35% on that spend.

Guests lost = 3% × 4,800 = **144**. Revenue lost = 144 × £220 = **£31,680**. Profit lost = 35% × £31,680 = **£11,088**.

The hotel plans a £9,000 training programme and a £6,000 booking system, a total of £15,000. This is expected to cut losses to 1% of guests, or 48.

Guests kept = 144 − 48 = 96. Profit gained = 96 × £220 × 35% = **£7,392** a year.

Payback = £15,000 ÷ £7,392 = **2.0 years**.

Evaluation: the payback is acceptable, but the figures do not include the damage when unhappy guests post reviews, which suggests that the benefit is higher. They also assume that 96 of the lost guests would have returned, which is uncertain. The benefit lasts only if staff keep using what they learnt.

## Improving customer service

- **Training.** Staff learn product knowledge, listening, problem solving and handling complaints.
- **Empowerment.** Letting front-line staff solve problems without asking a manager, for example offering a refund up to a set amount.
- **Better systems.** Online booking, tracking, customer records and quick-response contact channels.
- **Recruitment and rewards.** Hiring for attitude and rewarding good service with bonuses or recognition.
- **Using feedback.** Acting on surveys, complaints and mystery shopper findings, and telling customers what has changed.
- **Standards and targets.** Clear targets such as answering calls within 20 seconds, with regular reporting.
- **Staffing levels.** Enough staff at busy times to avoid long waits.

Each has a cost and a risk. Training takes time and staff may leave. Empowerment may lead to inconsistency or cost. Systems can fail or feel impersonal.

## Evaluating the importance of customer service

Customer service matters most where products are similar and rivals are close, and where customers pay for personal attention, such as in hotels, banking and health care. It matters less where price is the main factor, as in a discount store. When you evaluate:

1. link the answer to the type of business and its customers;
2. use figures from the case where given;
3. consider several stakeholders;
4. compare the cost of improving service with the cost of losing customers;
5. give a judgement and say what it depends on.

## Common mistakes

- Treating a service as if it were a physical product that can be stored or inspected before sale.
- Using the number of complaints rather than a rate when the number of customers changes.
- Assuming a low number of complaints means customers are happy.
- Listing methods of measurement without evaluating them.
- Forgetting that poor service also harms employees.

## Exam technique

Make sure you use the features of services (intangible, inseparable, variable, perishable) when you explain why quality is difficult to control. For "evaluate ways", compare at least two methods and judge which is best for the business in the case. Quote figures to show the scale of the effect of poor service.
