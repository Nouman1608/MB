This guide goes deeper on one section of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Biology (9610) specification, Version 5.1: **3.4.6 Homeostasis and negative feedback**, in Unit 4, Control. Unit 4 is assessed at International A-level only.

## What homeostasis is

**Homeostasis** is the maintenance of a stable internal environment within restricted limits, in spite of changes outside the organism or in its activity. In mammals, physiological control systems keep the temperature, pH, blood glucose concentration and water potential of the blood and tissue fluid within narrow ranges. Two examples are named in this section: core temperature and blood pH.

### Why a stable internal environment matters

The reason is **enzyme activity**.

**Core temperature.** Enzymes have an optimum temperature. If the core temperature rises too far above the optimum, hydrogen bonds in the enzymes break, the tertiary structure changes and the active site no longer fits the substrate. The enzymes are denatured and metabolic reactions slow or stop. If the temperature falls too low, molecules have less kinetic energy, so there are fewer collisions between enzymes and substrates and the rate of reaction falls. A stable temperature keeps the rate of metabolic reactions steady and high enough to meet the body's needs.

**Blood pH.** Changes in pH alter the charges on the R-groups of amino acids. Ionic and hydrogen bonds change, so the tertiary structure of enzymes and other proteins, such as haemoglobin, changes. The active site no longer fits the substrate, and enzyme activity falls. The pH of blood is kept in a narrow range, close to 7.4.

**Worked example 1: pH is a logarithmic scale.** Blood pH falls from 7.4 to 7.1. How does the hydrogen ion concentration change?

1. pH = -log10 of the hydrogen ion concentration, so concentration = 10 to the power of minus pH.
2. At pH 7.4 the concentration is 10^-7.4 = 4.0 x 10^-8 mol per dm3.
3. At pH 7.1 it is 10^-7.1 = 7.9 x 10^-8 mol per dm3.
4. 10^(7.4 - 7.1) = 10^0.3, which is about 2.0.
5. A fall of only 0.3 pH units doubles the hydrogen ion concentration. This is why small pH changes matter to enzymes.

## Negative feedback

**Negative feedback** restores a system to its original level. A change from the set point is detected, and the response reverses the change, so the system returns towards the set point. This is the main mechanism of homeostasis.

A control system has these components:

1. A **set point**, the normal level (for example a core temperature of about 37 degrees Celsius).
2. A **receptor** that detects a deviation from the set point (for example thermoreceptors in the hypothalamus and skin).
3. A **coordinator** that compares the information with the set point and sends signals (for example the hypothalamus).
4. An **effector** that produces the response (for example sweat glands and muscles).
5. The response, which is a **correction** that reverses the deviation. The information returns to the receptor, so the cycle continues until the set point is reached.

### Separate mechanisms for departures in each direction

The specification states that separate mechanisms involving negative feedback, controlling departures in different directions from the original state, give a **greater degree of control**.

For temperature, one set of responses works against a rise and another set against a fall:

| If core temperature rises | If core temperature falls |
|---|---|
| Vasodilation: more blood flows near the skin surface and loses heat | Vasoconstriction: less blood flows near the surface |
| Sweating: evaporation of water removes heat | Shivering: muscle contraction releases heat |
| Hair erector muscles relax: hairs lie flat | Hair erector muscles contract: hairs rise, trapping air |

The advantage is that the system can reverse a change in either direction. A system with only one mechanism, for example heat loss, could correct a rise only slowly, by switching the mechanism off, and could not actively correct a fall. With two opposing mechanisms the control is faster and finer, and the variation around the set point is smaller.

**Worked example 2: reading a negative feedback graph.** After exercise began, the core temperature of an athlete was measured: 37.0 degrees Celsius at 0 min, 37.6 at 10 min, 38.2 at 20 min, 37.8 at 30 min, 37.4 at 40 min, 37.1 at 50 min and 37.0 at 60 min.

1. The rise from 0 to 20 min was 38.2 - 37.0 = 1.2 degrees Celsius, a rate of 1.2 / 20 = 0.06 degrees per minute.
2. After 20 min, the temperature fell by 1.2 degrees over 40 min, a rate of 0.03 degrees per minute.
3. The temperature returned to the set point of 37.0, so negative feedback was working.
4. The fall was slower than the rise because the response (sweating and vasodilation) was only gradually effective, and the body was still generating heat.
5. The curve flattens as it approaches 37.0 because the size of the correction depends on the size of the deviation.

## Positive feedback

**Positive feedback** results in greater departures from the original level. A change causes a response that increases the change, so the system moves away from the set point at an increasing rate.

The specification states that positive feedback is often associated with the breakdown of control systems, such as in the control of temperature. For example, in a severe fever or heat stroke, a rise in temperature increases the rate of metabolic reactions, which releases more heat and raises the temperature further. Eventually, enzymes are denatured, and the system fails. In the opposite direction, in severe hypothermia, a fall in temperature slows metabolic reactions, which release less heat, so the temperature falls further.

Positive feedback is not always harmful. It is used in the body when a rapid change is needed and there is a limit to the process. In the nerve, the entry of sodium ions during an action potential opens more sodium channels, which causes further entry. The process stops because the channels close and the potential is restored. Where the feedback has no limit, the effect is a loss of control.

**Worked example 3: recognising positive feedback.** A patient's temperature was 39.0 degrees Celsius at 0 min, 40.0 at 30 min, 41.5 at 60 min and 43.5 at 90 min.

1. The rises in each 30-minute period were 1.0, 1.5 and 2.0 degrees.
2. The rate of rise is increasing: 0.033, 0.050 and 0.067 degrees per minute.
3. The temperature is moving further from the set point, and faster, so this is positive feedback.
4. Explanation: higher temperature speeds up metabolism, releasing more heat. Above about 41 degrees Celsius some enzymes are denatured, and the control system fails.

## Comparing the two

| Feature | Negative feedback | Positive feedback |
|---|---|---|
| Effect of the response | Reverses the change | Increases the change |
| Result | Stability near a set point | Greater departure from the original level |
| Role in homeostasis | Maintains it | Breakdown of control |
| Graph shape | Oscillates around, or returns to, the set point | Moves away from the set point, often at an increasing rate |

## Common mistakes

- Defining homeostasis as "keeping things constant". The conditions vary within limits.
- Saying that enzymes "die" at high temperature. They are denatured.
- Saying that negative feedback means a harmful effect. "Negative" means the response opposes the change.
- Writing that positive feedback always returns the system to normal.
- Describing the response without naming the effector.
- Saying only that the body "cools down", with no mechanism such as vasodilation or sweating.
- Not using values from a graph or table when asked to interpret.

## Exam technique

- **Name each component**: receptor, coordinator, effector, response.
- **For "explain the advantage of separate mechanisms"**, state that each reverses a change in one direction, which gives faster and finer control.
- **For graphs**, say whether the response opposes the change or increases it, and quote values or rates.
- **Link the importance** of a stable temperature or pH back to the enzyme's tertiary structure and active site.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is the control of blood glucose in this section?**
Blood glucose is a separate section, 3.4.7. It is a good example of negative feedback with separate mechanisms for a rise and a fall, so you can use it as an extra example.

**Does the specification name hormones or parts of the brain here?**
Not in 3.4.6. You can use general terms such as receptor, coordinator and effector. The examples of temperature control are for illustration, and you should expect to interpret the information given.
