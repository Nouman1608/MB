| Point | Wording |

| a | Definition of productivity |
| b | Factors affecting productivity: land (use of fertiliser, drainage, irrigation, reclamation); labour (quality of labour, including improved human capital through education and training, and the impact of migration); capital (increased quantity and technological advances) |
| c | Definition of division of labour |
| d | Advantages and disadvantages of the division of labour to workers and businesses |

## Productivity (point a)

**Productivity** is the output produced per unit of input over a period of time.

```
productivity = output ÷ quantity of input
```

The input can be any factor:

- **labour productivity:** output per worker, or output per hour worked
- **land productivity:** output per hectare
- **capital productivity:** output per machine.

### Per worker or per hour?

*Invented case.* A garment workshop has 10 workers producing 3 000 shirts a week. It hires 2 part-time workers, and output rises to 3 360 shirts. Full-time staff work 40 hours; part-timers work 20.

```
Before: per worker = 3 000 ÷ 10 = 300 shirts
        per hour   = 3 000 ÷ (10 × 40) = 3 000 ÷ 400 = 7.5 shirts
After:  per worker = 3 360 ÷ 12 = 280 shirts
        per hour   = 3 360 ÷ (400 + 40) = 3 360 ÷ 440 = 7.6 shirts (1 d.p.)
```

Output per worker appears to fall, but output per hour has risen slightly, because the new workers work fewer hours. When workers' hours differ, output per hour is the fairer measure. This kind of interpretation is what data-response questions reward.

### Why productivity matters to a business

If wages stay the same, higher productivity means a lower **labour cost per unit**.

*Invented figures.* A worker is paid 18 an hour and makes 24 units an hour. After training she makes 30 units an hour.

```
Labour cost per unit before = 18 ÷ 24 = 0.75
Labour cost per unit after  = 18 ÷ 30 = 0.60
Fall = (0.75 − 0.60) ÷ 0.75 × 100 = 20%
```

Lower unit costs let the firm cut prices to compete, or keep prices and earn more profit. Higher productivity can also support higher wages.

## What affects productivity (point b)

### Land

| Method | How it raises productivity |
|---|---|
| Fertiliser | Adds nutrients to soil, so each hectare yields more crop |
| Drainage | Removes excess water from waterlogged land, so crops can grow and machinery can work the fields |
| Irrigation | Supplies water in dry periods, so yields are higher and more reliable, and more harvests may be possible each year |
| Reclamation | Turns unusable land, such as marsh, desert edge or land from the sea, into productive land |

*Invented example.* A farm of 60 hectares yields 2.5 tonnes of wheat per hectare: 150 tonnes. Adding fertiliser raises the yield by 20%, to 2.5 × 1.2 = 3.0 tonnes per hectare, or 180 tonnes in total. Evaluation: the extra 30 tonnes must be worth more than the cost of the fertiliser, and heavy use can pollute rivers, an external cost.

### Labour

- **Quality of labour.** Skills, experience, health and motivation all affect how much a worker produces.
- **Human capital through education and training.** Human capital is the knowledge and skills a worker has. Education gives general skills such as literacy and numeracy; training gives job-specific skills. Both raise output per hour. Training has costs: fees, lost output while staff train, and the risk that trained workers move to rivals.
- **The impact of migration.** Immigration of skilled workers can fill skill shortages and raise average productivity. Immigration of workers with fewer skills, or with skills that do not match local jobs, may lower the average at first, even though total output rises. For the country that loses skilled workers through emigration, productivity may fall.

### Capital

- **Increased quantity:** more machines or tools per worker let each worker produce more, as when a builder uses a cement mixer rather than mixing by hand.
- **Technological advances:** newer capital works faster, more accurately or with less waste, such as computer-controlled cutting or automated stock systems.

The capital chain: firm invests in new technology → each worker produces more per hour → unit costs fall → the firm can compete on price. The limits are the cost of the equipment, the training needed to use it and the possibility that machines replace some workers.

## Division of labour (points c and d)

The **division of labour** is when the production process is split into separate tasks, with each worker specialising in one task.

### Worked example: a sandwich kitchen

*Invented figures.* Four workers each work 8 hours: 32 hours a day. When each worker makes whole sandwiches alone, a sandwich takes 4 minutes of labour (fetching ingredients, slicing, filling, wrapping).

```
Output = (32 × 60) ÷ 4 = 1 920 ÷ 4 = 480 sandwiches a day
```

The kitchen reorganises so that one worker slices bread, one adds fillings, one assembles and one wraps. Because each repeats one task and stops moving between stations, the labour time falls to 2.5 minutes per sandwich.

```
Output = 1 920 ÷ 2.5 = 768 sandwiches a day
Increase = (768 − 480) ÷ 480 × 100 = 60%
Output per worker rises from 120 to 192 a day
```

### Advantages and disadvantages

| | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|
| Workers | Become skilled and quick at one task; less training needed to start; may earn more if productivity rises | Repetitive work causes boredom and lower motivation; narrow skills make it harder to find other jobs if the task is automated |
| Businesses | Higher output per worker and lower unit costs; consistent quality; quicker to train new staff | Bored staff may make errors, be absent or leave; if one worker is absent, the whole process can stop; less flexibility when demand changes |

### Evaluating the division of labour

The benefits are largest where:

- the product is **standardised** and made in large quantities, so tasks can be repeated
- the **market is big enough** to buy the extra output
- the firm can **reduce boredom**, for example by rotating workers between tasks.

They are smaller for made-to-order or creative products, and where absence or staff turnover is high. A judgement should name which of these conditions the case in the question meets.

## Common mistakes

- Confusing production (total output) with productivity (output per unit of input).
- Saying more workers means higher productivity. More workers raise output; productivity depends on output per worker.
- Giving land methods with no explanation of how yield rises.
- Saying migration always lowers productivity, or always raises it. It depends on the skills of the migrants.
- Writing that division of labour is "using machines". It is splitting tasks among workers.
- Listing advantages for workers only when the question asks about businesses, or the reverse.

## Exam technique

- **Define / What is meant by:** two parts, such as "output" and "per unit of input over a period".
- **Calculate:** write the formula; check whether the input is workers or hours.
- **Explain:** factor plus two developments in the case context.
- **Assess / Evaluate:** weigh advantages against disadvantages for the group named, and say what the outcome depends on.
