This guide covers **4.3.2 Use of amount of substance in relation to masses of pure substances** of the AQA GCSE
Chemistry (8462) specification, for teaching from September 2016 onwards (version 1.2). It is assessed on
Paper 1. Most of it is **Higher Tier only**: 4.3.2.1 Moles, 4.3.2.2 Amounts of substances in equations, 4.3.2.3
Using moles to balance equations and 4.3.2.4 Limiting reactants are all marked (HT only). In 4.3.2.5
Concentration of solutions, calculating the mass of solute in a given volume of solution is for both tiers, and
explaining how the mass of solute and the volume are related to concentration is (HT only). Sections marked
(HT only) below are Higher Tier only. There is no required practical in this subsection.

For quick recall use the [amount of substance and masses revision notes](/resources/aqa-gcse-chemistry-8462-amount-of-substance-and-masses-revision-notes/),
and test yourself with the [practice questions](/resources/aqa-gcse-chemistry-8462-amount-of-substance-and-masses-practice/).

## Moles (HT only)

Amounts of substance are measured in **moles** (symbol **mol**). One mole of a substance contains the same number
of particles as one mole of any other substance. That number is the **Avogadro constant**, **6.02 × 10²³ per
mole**. The particles may be atoms, molecules, ions or electrons.

The mass of one mole in grams is numerically equal to the relative formula mass (Ar for an element, Mr for a
compound).

```
number of moles = mass (g) / Mr          mass (g) = number of moles x Mr
```

### Worked example 1: mass to moles to particles

*Calculate the number of moles in 14.6 g of hydrogen chloride, HCl, and the number of HCl molecules.*

```
Mr of HCl = 1 + 35.5 = 36.5
moles = 14.6 / 36.5 = 0.40 mol
molecules = 0.40 x 6.02 x 10^23 = 2.4 x 10^23
```

### Worked example 2: moles to mass

*Calculate the mass of 0.25 mol of calcium carbonate, CaCO₃.*

```
Mr = 40 + 12 + 48 = 100
mass = 0.25 x 100 = 25 g
```

One mole of carbon atoms contains the same number of particles as one mole of carbon dioxide molecules. The
numbers of particles are equal, but the masses are different (12 g and 44 g).

## Amounts in equations (HT only)

The coefficients in a balanced equation give the ratio of moles. For Mg + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂, **one mole** of
magnesium reacts with **two moles** of hydrochloric acid to give **one mole** of magnesium chloride and **one mole**
of hydrogen.

### Method for reacting masses

1. Write the balanced equation.
2. Convert the given mass to moles (mass ÷ Mr).
3. Use the equation ratio to find the moles of the substance you want.
4. Convert to mass (moles × Mr).

### Worked example 3: reacting mass

*Calculate the mass of magnesium chloride made from 6.0 g of magnesium. Mg + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂*

```
moles Mg = 6.0 / 24 = 0.25 mol
Ratio Mg : MgCl2 = 1 : 1, so moles MgCl2 = 0.25 mol
Mr MgCl2 = 24 + 2 x 35.5 = 95
mass = 0.25 x 95 = 23.75 g = 23.8 g (3 s.f.)
```

## Using moles to balance equations (HT only)

If you know the masses of the reactants and products, convert each to moles, divide by the smallest number of
moles, and turn the result into the simplest whole-number ratio. These are the balancing numbers.

### Worked example 4: finding the equation

*5.4 g of aluminium reacts with 4.8 g of oxygen to make 10.2 g of aluminium oxide, Al₂O₃. Find the balanced
equation.*

```
Al:    5.4 / 27 = 0.20 mol
O2:    4.8 / 32 = 0.15 mol
Al2O3: 10.2 / 102 = 0.10 mol
Divide by 0.05 : 4 : 3 : 2
Equation: 4Al + 3O2 -> 2Al2O3
```

## Limiting reactants (HT only)

Often one reactant is used in excess so that the other is completely used up. The reactant that is completely
used up is the **limiting reactant**, because it limits the amount of product.

### Worked example 5: finding the limiting reactant

*6.0 g of magnesium is added to 7.3 g of hydrogen chloride. Mg + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂. Find the limiting reactant
and the mass of magnesium chloride formed.*

```
Mg:  6.0 / 24 = 0.25 mol          HCl: 7.3 / 36.5 = 0.20 mol
0.25 mol Mg needs 2 x 0.25 = 0.50 mol HCl, but only 0.20 mol is present.
HCl is the limiting reactant.
Mg reacting = 0.20 / 2 = 0.10 mol;  MgCl2 formed = 0.10 mol
mass = 0.10 x 95 = 9.5 g
```

## Concentration of solutions (g/dm³)

Many reactions happen in solution. The **concentration** can be measured as the mass of solute per unit volume,
such as grams per cubic decimetre (g/dm³). 1 dm³ = 1000 cm³.

```
concentration (g/dm3) = mass of solute (g) / volume (dm3)
volume in dm3 = volume in cm3 / 1000
```

### Worked example 6: concentration (both tiers)

*12 g of sodium hydroxide is dissolved to make 500 cm³ of solution. Calculate the concentration in g/dm³.*

```
volume = 500 / 1000 = 0.5 dm3
concentration = 12 / 0.5 = 24 g/dm3
```

### Worked example 7: mass of solute (both tiers)

*How much solute is in 250 cm³ of a solution with a concentration of 40 g/dm³?*

```
volume = 250 / 1000 = 0.25 dm3
mass = 40 x 0.25 = 10 g
```

### Relating mass, volume and concentration (HT only)

The concentration is **directly proportional** to the mass of solute dissolved in a fixed volume: doubling the
mass doubles the concentration. It is **inversely proportional** to the volume for a fixed mass: doubling the
volume halves the concentration.

*Example (HT only):* 10 g in 0.5 dm³ is 20 g/dm³. 20 g in 0.5 dm³ is 40 g/dm³ (twice the mass, twice the
concentration). 10 g in 1.0 dm³ is 10 g/dm³ (twice the volume, half the concentration).

## Moles of different kinds of particle (HT only)

The measurement of amounts in moles applies to atoms, molecules, ions, electrons, formulae and equations. One
mole of carbon atoms (C) contains the same number of particles as one mole of carbon dioxide molecules (CO₂),
but each CO₂ molecule contains three atoms.

### Worked example 8: atoms in a compound (HT only)

*How many oxygen atoms are in 0.50 mol of carbon dioxide, CO₂?*

```
Molecules = 0.50 x 6.02 x 10^23 = 3.01 x 10^23
Each molecule has 2 O atoms
Oxygen atoms = 2 x 3.01 x 10^23 = 6.02 x 10^23
```

### Worked example 9: mass of a gas made (HT only)

*4.8 g of magnesium reacts with excess hydrochloric acid: Mg + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂. Calculate the mass of
hydrogen made.*

```
moles Mg = 4.8 / 24 = 0.20 mol
ratio Mg : H2 = 1 : 1, so moles H2 = 0.20 mol
mass H2 = 0.20 x 2 = 0.40 g
```

## A layout for every moles question (HT only)

| Step | What to write |
|---|---|
| 1 | The balanced equation |
| 2 | Mr of each substance you need |
| 3 | Moles of the known substance = mass ÷ Mr |
| 4 | Moles of the wanted substance, using the ratio |
| 5 | Mass of the wanted substance = moles × Mr |
| 6 | Answer with units and suitable significant figures |

Following the same layout each time makes mistakes easier to spot. Give answers to the same number of
significant figures as the data, or to 3 significant figures if you are unsure.

## Common mistakes

- Using the mass of the wrong substance, or forgetting to use the ratio from the equation.
- Forgetting to multiply by the balancing number in an Mr sum.
- Not converting cm³ to dm³ before using g/dm³.
- Writing the limiting reactant as the one with the smaller mass. Compare moles using the equation ratio.
- Giving the Avogadro constant in the wrong form (6.02 × 10²³ per mole).
- Rounding too early. Keep working values until the final step.

## Exam technique

- **Show your working:** a correct method can earn marks even if the final answer is wrong.
- **Use the three-step plan:** moles of what you know, equation ratio, then mass of what you want.
- **Give units and appropriate significant figures.**
- **Tier awareness:** mass-of-solute calculations from g/dm³ are for both tiers; everything else here is Higher.
