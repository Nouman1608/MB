## 1.4.12 Characteristics of job costing

**Job costing** is used when each order is different and made to a customer's specification. Examples are a building contractor, a printer, a furniture maker or a repair workshop.

- Each job has a **job number** and its own **job cost sheet**.
- Costs are collected for that job only: direct materials from materials requisitions, direct labour from time sheets, and direct expenses (such as subcontracted work) from invoices.
- **Overheads** are added using an absorption rate, such as a rate per labour hour or machine hour.
- The total cost is compared with the price quoted or the estimate, so management can see whether the job made a profit.
- A job that is unfinished at the end of the accounting period is **work in progress** and is valued at the cost incurred so far.

**Advantages:** a precise cost for each job; helps to set prices and quotations; shows which jobs are profitable; supports control by comparing estimate with actual. **Disadvantages:** time-consuming recording; overhead absorption involves judgement; it works poorly for standardised, continuous production, where process costing or batch costing is better.

## The job cost sheet

| Cost element | Source | How it is charged |
|---|---|---|
| Direct materials | Materials requisition notes | Cost of materials issued to the job |
| Direct labour | Time sheets or job cards | Hours worked on the job × hourly rate |
| Direct expenses | Supplier invoices | Actual cost for the job |
| **Prime cost** | | Total of the above |
| Overheads | Absorption rate | Hours (or other base) × rate |
| **Total cost** | | Prime cost + overheads |
| Profit | Mark-up or margin | Added to give the selling price |

## Worked example: Ridgeway Joinery, job 214

Job 214 is a set of fitted bookshelves.

- Materials issued from stores: 480
- Direct labour: 36 hours at 14 an hour
- Subcontracted staining: 120
- Overhead absorbed at 9 per direct labour hour

| | $ |
|---|---:|
| Direct materials | 480 |
| Direct labour (36 × 14) | 504 |
| Direct expenses | 120 |
| **Prime cost** | **1,104** |
| Overheads (36 × 9) | 324 |
| **Total cost** | **1,428** |

**Pricing with a mark-up.** Ridgeway adds a mark-up of 30% on cost. Profit = 1,428 × 30% = 428.40, so the selling price = **1,856.40**.

**Pricing with a margin.** If Ridgeway wants a profit margin of 25% of the selling price, the cost is 75% of the price. Selling price = 1,428 ÷ 0.75 = **1,904**, and profit = 1,904 − 1,428 = 476.

Mark-up and margin are different. A 25% margin is the same as a 33.3% mark-up on cost (25 ÷ 75). Using 25% as a mark-up would give 1,785, which is lower and would give a margin of only 20%.

**Estimate against actual.** If the quote was based on an estimated cost of 1,400, the actual cost of 1,428 is 28 higher, which is 2.0% (28 ÷ 1,400). The job cost sheet shows where the difference arose. Perhaps materials wasted or the staining cost more than expected.

## Work in progress and job costs

At the year end, job 215 is half finished. Materials of 300 and 10 labour hours at 14 have been charged. Overhead absorbed is 10 hours × 9 = 90. Work in progress = 300 + 140 + 90 = **530**. This appears as inventory in current assets. Completed jobs that are not yet delivered are finished goods. Jobs delivered to the customer are charged to cost of sales.

## 1.4.13 Batch costing

In **batch production** a quantity of identical units is made together, such as 500 uniforms for a school. The batch is treated like one job, and then the cost is divided by the number of units to find the **cost per unit**.

Batch costs often include **set-up costs**, such as setting machines or cutting patterns. These are incurred once per batch, so a larger batch spreads them over more units.

**Example: Batch B31, 500 units.**

| | $ |
|---|---:|
| Materials (500 × 2.50) | 1,250 |
| Direct labour (60 hours × 12) | 720 |
| Set-up cost (direct expense) | 180 |
| **Prime cost** | **2,150** |
| Overheads (50 machine hours × 6) | 300 |
| **Batch cost** | **2,450** |
| Cost per unit (2,450 ÷ 500) | **4.90** |

At a mark-up of 40%, the selling price per unit is 4.90 × 1.40 = **6.86**. At a margin of 25% it would be 4.90 ÷ 0.75 = 6.53.

**Effect of batch size.** If the batch is doubled to 1,000 units, materials are 2,500, labour is 120 hours × 12 = 1,440, set-up cost is unchanged at 180, and overheads are 100 hours × 6 = 600. The batch cost is 2,500 + 1,440 + 180 + 600 = 4,720, so the cost per unit is **4.72**. The set-up cost per unit falls from 180 ÷ 500 = 0.36 to 180 ÷ 1,000 = 0.18, which is the main saving. In practice, larger batches also raise inventory-holding costs and risks if demand falls.

## Customer orders

A **customer order** is a job made for a particular customer, and the same method applies. Each order receives a number so that costs are recorded against it and the profit on that customer's order is known. Where an order is large and takes several periods, the costs incurred are held as work in progress and charged to cost of sales when the order is delivered.

## Using job costs in decisions

- **Quotations:** the cost sheet of similar past jobs helps estimate costs for new quotations.
- **Control:** comparing estimate and actual shows where costs overran.
- **Profitability:** costs by job show which kinds of work make the most profit.
- **Price negotiation:** the cost gives a floor, below which a job loses money. Pricing below total cost may still be sensible if spare capacity exists and the contribution is positive, but this is a decision beyond simple job costing.

## Common mistakes

- Leaving the overhead out of the job cost, so the price is too low.
- Using actual overhead instead of the absorption rate.
- Charging hours at the overhead rate rather than the labour rate for direct labour, or the reverse.
- Mixing up mark-up (on cost) and margin (on selling price).
- Dividing a batch's cost by the wrong number of units.
- Treating set-up costs as a cost per unit that stays the same when the batch size changes.
- Not including unfinished jobs as work in progress at the year end.
- Charging indirect labour (supervision, cleaning) to a job as direct labour.

## Exam technique

- Use a job cost sheet layout with prime cost and total cost as clear subtotals.
- Write the working for each line: 36 × 14, 36 × 9.
- For a price, state whether you are using mark-up or margin, and show the working.
- For **explain** questions about the benefits of job costing, link the reason to a use: "It shows the profit on each customer order, so the business can set better prices."
- For batch costs, divide by the number of units *at the end* and show the answer to two decimal places.
