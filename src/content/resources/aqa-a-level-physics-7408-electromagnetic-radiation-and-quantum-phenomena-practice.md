These questions cover section 3.2.2 Electromagnetic radiation and quantum phenomena (3.2.2.1 to 3.2.2.4) of the AQA AS and A-level Physics specification (7407/7408). Section A has short questions; Section B has structured questions. Learn the content first with the [quantum phenomena study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-physics-7408-electromagnetic-radiation-and-quantum-phenomena/) and the [quantum phenomena revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-physics-7408-electromagnetic-radiation-and-quantum-phenomena-revision-notes/).

Use h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J s, c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹, e = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C, mₑ = 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ kg and neutron mass 1.67 × 10⁻²⁷ kg.

## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** State what is meant by:

**(a)** the work function of a metal **[1]**
**(b)** the threshold frequency of a metal. **[1]**

**2.** Ultraviolet radiation of wavelength 220 nm falls on a metal of work function 4.10 eV.

**(a)** Calculate the photon energy in eV. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the maximum kinetic energy of the photoelectrons, in J. **[2]**
**(c)** State the stopping potential. **[1]**
**(d)** Calculate the threshold frequency of the metal. **[1]**

**3.** Explain why no electrons are emitted from a metal surface lit by light below the threshold frequency, however intense the light is. **[3]**

**4.** A 5.0 mW laser emits light of wavelength 650 nm. Calculate the number of photons it emits each second. **[2]**

**5.** An electron is accelerated from rest through a pd of 50 V.

**(a)** State its kinetic energy in eV and calculate it in J. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate its speed. **[2]**

### Section B: structured questions

**6.** A student measures the stopping potential Vs for different frequencies of light on one metal.

| f / 10¹⁴ Hz | 6.0 | 7.0 | 8.0 | 9.0 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Vs / V | 0.36 | 0.77 | 1.19 | 1.60 |

**(a)** Show that eVs = hf − ϕ means a graph of Vs against f is a straight line with gradient h/e. **[1]**
**(b)** Use the data to find a value for the Planck constant. **[3]**
**(c)** Find the threshold frequency and the work function in eV. **[3]**

**7.** A simplified atom has energy levels at −10.4 eV (ground state), −5.5 eV, −3.7 eV and −1.6 eV.

**(a)** State how many different photon energies can be emitted by atoms excited to the −1.6 eV level. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the shortest wavelength emitted. **[2]**
**(c)** A free electron with kinetic energy 6.0 eV collides with an atom in the ground state. State which excitation can occur and the kinetic energy the electron keeps. **[2]**
**(d)** A photon of energy 6.0 eV meets an atom in the ground state. Explain whether it is absorbed. **[2]**

**8.** Describe how a fluorescent tube produces visible light. **[5]**

**9.** Explain how line spectra provide evidence that electrons in atoms have discrete energy levels. **[2]**

**10.** Neutrons from a reactor are slowed to a speed of 2200 m s⁻¹.

**(a)** Calculate their de Broglie wavelength. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why a beam of these neutrons is diffracted by a crystal. **[1]**

**11.** In an electron diffraction tube, electrons accelerated through a pd V pass through graphite and form rings on a screen.

**(a)** State what the rings show about the nature of electrons. **[1]**
**(b)** Show that the de Broglie wavelength is proportional to 1/√V. **[2]**
**(c)** V is increased by a factor of 4. State and explain the effect on the rings. **[2]**

**12.** The intensity of light on a photocell is doubled while the frequency is kept constant above the threshold. State and explain the effect on:

**(a)** the maximum kinetic energy of the photoelectrons **[2]**
**(b)** the photoelectric current. **[2]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** The minimum energy needed to remove an electron from the surface of the metal [1].
**(b)** The minimum frequency of radiation that releases electrons from the metal surface [1].

**2. (a)** E = hc/λ = 1.989 × 10⁻²⁵ / 220 × 10⁻⁹ = 9.04 × 10⁻¹⁹ J [1] = **5.65 eV** [1].
**(b)** Ek(max) = 5.65 − 4.10 = 1.55 eV [1] = 1.55 × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ = **2.48 × 10⁻¹⁹ J** [1].
**(c)** **1.55 V** [1].
**(d)** f₀ = ϕ/h = (4.10 × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹)/(6.63 × 10⁻³⁴) = **9.89 × 10¹⁴ Hz** [1].

**3.** Light arrives as photons of energy hf, and each electron absorbs one photon [1]. Below f₀, hf is less than the work function, so no single photon gives an electron enough energy to escape [1]. Greater intensity only increases the number of photons, not the energy of each, so still no electrons are emitted [1].
*Examiner insight:* The one-photon-to-one-electron idea is the key mark. "The light does not have enough energy" is not enough.

**4.** Photon energy = hc/λ = 1.989 × 10⁻²⁵ / 650 × 10⁻⁹ = 3.06 × 10⁻¹⁹ J [1]. Number per second = 5.0 × 10⁻³ / 3.06 × 10⁻¹⁹ = **1.6 × 10¹⁶** [1].

**5. (a)** 50 eV = 50 × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ = **8.0 × 10⁻¹⁸ J** [1].
**(b)** v = √(2Ek/m) = √(2 × 8.0 × 10⁻¹⁸ / 9.11 × 10⁻³¹) [1] = **4.2 × 10⁶ m s⁻¹** [1].

**6. (a)** Dividing by e: Vs = (h/e)f − ϕ/e, which has the form y = mx + c with gradient h/e [1].
**(b)** Gradient = (1.60 − 0.36) / ((9.0 − 6.0) × 10¹⁴) [1] = 4.13 × 10⁻¹⁵ V s [1]. h = e × gradient = **6.6 × 10⁻³⁴ J s** [1].
**(c)** At Vs = 0: f₀ = 6.0 × 10¹⁴ − 0.36 / 4.13 × 10⁻¹⁵ [1] = **5.1 × 10¹⁴ Hz** [1]. ϕ = hf₀ = 3.39 × 10⁻¹⁹ J = **2.1 eV** [1].
*Examiner insight:* Use points far apart on a best-fit line (here the end points of a straight set of data) to reduce the uncertainty in the gradient.

**7. (a)** **6** [1].
**(b)** Largest gap = 10.4 − 1.6 = 8.8 eV = 1.41 × 10⁻¹⁸ J [1]. λ = hc/ΔE = **1.41 × 10⁻⁷ m** [1].
**(c)** Excitation to −5.5 eV (needs 4.9 eV) only; the −3.7 eV level needs 6.7 eV [1]. The electron keeps 6.0 − 4.9 = **1.1 eV** [1].
**(d)** It is not absorbed [1], because a photon must give all its energy and 6.0 eV matches no level difference (the gaps from the ground state are 4.9, 6.7 and 8.8 eV) [1].

**8.** A high pd accelerates free electrons through the tube [1]. They collide with mercury atoms, exciting them [1]. The mercury atoms de-excite, emitting ultraviolet photons [1]. The phosphor coating absorbs the UV and its atoms are excited [1]. The phosphor atoms de-excite in smaller steps, emitting visible light [1].

**9.** Each line has a single, definite wavelength, so the photon has a definite energy [1]. Photon energy equals the difference between two levels, so only fixed level differences, and therefore discrete levels, can exist [1].

**10. (a)** λ = h/mv = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ / (1.67 × 10⁻²⁷ × 2200) [1] = **1.8 × 10⁻¹⁰ m** [1].
**(b)** The wavelength is similar to the spacing between atoms in the crystal, so significant diffraction occurs [1].

**11. (a)** Electrons have a wave nature (they can be diffracted) [1].
**(b)** eV = ½mv², so mv = √(2meV) [1]; λ = h/mv = h/√(2meV), so λ ∝ 1/√V [1].
**(c)** λ halves [1], so the electrons are diffracted less and the rings become smaller (closer to the centre) [1].

**12. (a)** No change [1]: the energy of each photon depends only on frequency, and each electron absorbs one photon [1].
**(b)** The current doubles [1]: twice as many photons arrive per second, so twice as many electrons are emitted per second [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Saying that intensity changes the kinetic energy of photoelectrons.
- Subtracting a work function in eV from a photon energy in J.
- Reading the gradient of a Vs–f graph as h, without multiplying by e.
- Using a single level value instead of a level difference.
- Claiming a photon can give part of its energy to an atom.
- Saying faster electrons give larger diffraction rings.

## Frequently asked questions

**How do you find the Planck constant from a stopping potential graph?**
Plot stopping potential against frequency. The gradient is h/e, so multiply it by the electronic charge to get h. The intercept on the frequency axis is the threshold frequency.

**Why is a 6.0 eV electron able to excite an atom when a 6.0 eV photon cannot?**
A colliding electron can transfer part of its kinetic energy, so it can supply a 4.9 eV gap and keep the rest. A photon must be absorbed whole, so its energy must exactly match a gap.
