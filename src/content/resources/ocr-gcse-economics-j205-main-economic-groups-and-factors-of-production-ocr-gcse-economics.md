This guide covers section 1.1, Main economic groups and factors of production, of the OCR GCSE (9-1) Economics specification (J205), Version 2.0, for first assessment in 2019. It sits in component J205/01, Introduction to Economics. The specification is not tiered. Every business, place and figure below is invented for practice.

This page goes further on 1.1 alone: what each group decides, how a change spreads between groups, how to classify awkward inputs, and how to explain a combination of factors in context. ## What the specification asks

Learners should be able to:

- explain the role of the main economic groups: consumers, producers and the government, including their interdependence;
- explain the factors of production: land, labour, capital and enterprise, including how they might be combined.

Both points use the command word **explain**. The specification says this means stating a definition, explaining a concept or giving an example, and/or applying knowledge and understanding to a context. In practice, most marks above the first go to application: using the business, place or data in the question.

## The decisions each group makes

It helps to think of each group by the decisions it takes, not only by what it is.

| Group | Main decisions | What it wants | Its other roles |
|---|---|---|---|
| Consumers | What to buy, how much, from whom; whether to spend or save | Satisfaction of wants from limited income | Workers who supply labour; taxpayers; voters |
| Producers | What to make, how to make it, how much, at what price | Usually profit; sometimes survival, growth or a social aim | Employers; buyers of materials from other firms; taxpayers |
| Government | How much to tax and spend; what to provide; which rules to set | Wider aims such as jobs, fair incomes and stable prices | Producer of some services; a large buyer from firms; regulator |

Three points earn credit because students often miss them:

1. **One person can belong to more than one group.** A self-employed plumber is a producer when she fixes a boiler, a consumer when she buys groceries, and a taxpayer to the government.
2. **Consumer spending signals what to produce.** When consumers buy more of a product, firms earn more from it and tend to produce more. This is why consumers are said to influence the allocation of resources.
3. **The government is also a consumer and a producer.** It buys goods from firms (school computers, road building) and provides some services itself.

## Interdependence: tracing a change

Interdependence means each group relies on the others, so a change for one group affects the rest. Exam questions often give a single event and ask you to explain the effect on another group. Follow the money and the goods in each direction.

**Worked example: a factory closure (invented).** Brackley Components, the largest employer in a small town, closes its factory with the loss of 600 jobs.

- **Producers to consumers:** 600 households lose wage income, so they spend less in local shops and cafés.
- **Consumers to producers:** lower spending reduces the sales of local businesses, which may cut staff hours or close.
- **Both to government:** the government collects less income tax from the workers and less tax on the firm's profit, but it may pay out more in benefits.
- **Government back to both:** it may fund retraining for the workers or offer support to attract a new employer.

**Worked example: rising demand (invented).** Consumers in a region start buying many more electric bikes. Bike shops order more stock, so bike producers hire workers and buy more parts from other firms. Those workers earn wages they spend elsewhere, and the government receives more tax revenue. It might respond by spending on cycle lanes, which raises demand for bikes again.

Notice the chain in each case: an **event**, the **first group affected**, the **link** to the next group, then the **outcome**. That four-step chain is exactly what an "explain" answer needs.

## The four factors: classifying awkward cases

| Factor | Meaning | Reward |
|---|---|---|
| Land | Natural resources: the ground itself, and what comes from nature, such as minerals, fish stocks, forests and water | Rent |
| Labour | Human effort, physical and mental, used in production | Wages |
| Capital | Goods made by people and used to produce other goods and services | Interest |
| Enterprise | The organising of the other factors and the taking of risk | Profit |

The difficult marks come from items that look like one factor but are another.

| Item | Factor | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Fish in the sea | Land | A natural resource, not made by people |
| A fishing boat | Capital | Made by people, used to catch fish |
| A software developer's work | Labour | Human mental effort |
| The software the developer writes, used by the firm | Capital | A made resource used to produce other services |
| Money in the firm's bank account | None | Money is finance used to buy factors, not a factor itself |
| A franchise owner who risks savings opening a shop | Enterprise | Organises the factors and bears the risk |
| A salaried manager running the shop | Labour | Paid a wage; does not bear the business risk |

The last two rows matter. Enterprise is defined by **risk-taking** as well as organising. A manager who earns a salary whatever happens is supplying labour.

Factors also differ in **quality**, not just quantity. Fertile land produces more crops than poor land; trained, healthy workers produce more than untrained ones; newer machines are often more productive. An answer that says "the firm needs more labour" scores less than one that says "the firm needs workers with the skills to operate the new machines".

## How factors might be combined

Every good or service needs some of each factor. The choice is the **mix**. A firm uses more of a factor when it is cheap or productive relative to the others, and less when it becomes expensive. The entrepreneur makes this choice and carries the risk if it goes wrong.

**Worked example: Quayside Café (invented).** The café's weekly costs are:

| Factor | Payment | £ per week |
|---|---|---|
| Land | Rent for the premises | 800 |
| Labour | Wages for five staff | 2,400 |
| Capital | Lease of the coffee machines and ovens | 300 |
| Total | | 3,500 |

Labour is 2,400 ÷ 3,500 × 100 = **68.6%** of these costs, so the café is labour-intensive, like most services where staff serve customers in person.

The owner replaces one member of counter staff with two self-order screens. Wages fall by £480 to £1,920 and the lease rises by £200 to £500.

```
New total     = 800 + 1,920 + 500 = £3,220
Saving        = 3,500 − 3,220 = £280 a week
              = 280 ÷ 3,500 × 100 = 8.0%
Labour share  = 1,920 ÷ 3,220 × 100 = 59.6%
```

The combination has moved towards capital. Applying this in an answer: the switch makes sense if the screens are reliable and customers are happy to use them, but it removes a job, and some customers may value being served by a person. Those points show you understand that the best mix depends on the business and its customers, not only on cost.

## Common mistakes

- Listing the three groups without saying what each one does.
- Describing interdependence as "they all need each other" with no chain from an event to an outcome.
- Calling money, or a bank loan, capital.
- Treating any manager as enterprise; enterprise involves bearing risk.
- Giving a factor with no example from the context in the question.
- Saying a firm should "use more machines" with no reason linked to cost, productivity or the product.

## Exam technique

- **Short "state" or "identify" items:** one precise word or example is enough.
- **Two-mark "explain":** a point plus a developed reason, ideally using the context ("the café uses labour, such as baristas, because customers expect to be served").
- **Longer "explain" or "analyse" items:** link at least two groups in a chain. Use connectives that show cause and effect: *so*, *which means*, *as a result*.
- Use figures from the question when they are given; even a simple percentage shows application.
