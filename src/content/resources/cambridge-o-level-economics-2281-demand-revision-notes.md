These are condensed notes for section 2.3, *Demand* (2.3.1 to 2.3.4), of the Cambridge O Level Economics 2281 syllabus for examination in 2026. For full explanations and worked examples, use the [study guide on demand](/resources/cambridge-o-level-economics-2281-demand/). Then try the [practice questions on demand](/resources/cambridge-o-level-economics-2281-demand-practice/). All examples are invented.

## Key definitions

| Term | Definition to learn |
|---|---|
| Demand | The quantity consumers are willing and able to buy at each possible price over a given period |
| Demand schedule | A table of quantity demanded at each price |
| Demand curve | A graph of the demand schedule, with price on the vertical axis |
| Extension in demand | A rise in quantity demanded caused by a fall in the good's own price (movement along the curve) |
| Contraction in demand | A fall in quantity demanded caused by a rise in the good's own price (movement along the curve) |
| Increase in demand | More demanded at every price; the curve shifts right |
| Decrease in demand | Less demanded at every price; the curve shifts left |
| Individual demand | The demand of one consumer |
| Market demand | The total of all individual demands at each price (aggregation) |
| Substitute | A good that can be used instead of another |
| Complement | A good used together with another |

## Drawing checklist

- Price on the vertical axis; quantity demanded (with a time period) on the horizontal axis.
- Even scales from zero; points plotted accurately.
- Curve labelled D (D1 and D2 for a shift); arrow showing the direction of any shift.
- Dotted lines from the axes to the curve for the prices and quantities in the question.

## Why the curve slopes down

- Lower price → more affordable for consumers with limited income.
- Lower price → cheaper than substitutes, so some consumers switch to it.

## Movement or shift? The one question

**Has the good's own price changed?**

- Yes → movement along the curve → extension (price falls) or contraction (price rises).
- No, something else changed → shift → increase (right) or decrease (left).

## Worked reminders

**Reading the curve (invented): cinema tickets in Darrow, per week.**

| Price (cu) | 10 | 8 | 6 | 4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Quantity demanded | 300 | 450 | 600 | 750 |

- Price falls from 8 to 6 cu: extension of 150 tickets; 150 ÷ 450 × 100 = 33.3% rise in quantity.
- Price rises from 6 to 10 cu: contraction of 300 tickets.

**Aggregation.** Two groups of cinema-goers: at 8 cu, adults want 300 and students 150, so market demand is 450. Add quantities at the same price; never add prices.

**A shift.** A popular film is released: demand increases by 100 tickets at every price. At 8 cu, quantity demanded is now 550. New schedule: 400, 550, 700, 850.

**Both at once.** The film is released and the price rises from 6 to 8 cu. Before: 600 (old curve at 6 cu). After: 550 (new curve at 8 cu). Net fall of 50: an increase in demand combined with a contraction along the new curve.

## Conditions of demand

| Change | Effect | Shift |
|---|---|---|
| Income rises (most goods) | More able to buy | Right |
| Income tax rises | Less disposable income | Left |
| Price of a substitute rises | Switch to this good | Right |
| Price of a substitute falls | Switch away | Left |
| Price of a complement rises | Combined cost higher | Left |
| Price of a complement falls | Combined cost lower | Right |
| Tastes or fashion favour the good | More willing to buy | Right |
| Successful advertising | More willing to buy | Right |
| Population rises, or the buying age group grows | More consumers | Right |
| Season or weather favours the good | More willing to buy | Right |

**Chain to write.** Price of petrol rises → running a car costs more (petrol and cars are complements) → fewer consumers willing to buy cars at each price → demand for cars decreases → curve shifts left.

## Must-know distinctions

- Extension or contraction (own price, movement) versus increase or decrease (other causes, shift).
- Wanting versus willing and able to buy.
- Individual versus market demand.
- Substitutes (used instead) versus complements (used together).

## Exam traps

- "Demand falls because the price rose": say quantity demanded contracts.
- Drawing a new curve for an own-price change.
- Leaving out the time period or "willing and able".
- Adding prices when aggregating.
- Reversing the effect of a complement's price change.
- Unlabelled shifts: always D1, D2 and an arrow.

## Quick self-test

1. Define demand.
2. What causes an extension in demand?
3. What causes a decrease in demand? Give **one** example.
4. Which axis shows price on a demand diagram?
5. Using the cinema table, what is quantity demanded at 4 cu?
6. Price falls from 10 cu to 8 cu. Name the change and give its size.
7. Explain how market demand is found from individual demand.
8. The price of butter rises. What happens to demand for bread, if they are complements?
9. The price of tea rises. What happens to demand for coffee, if they are substitutes?
10. Give **two** conditions of demand other than income.
11. Incomes rise and the good's price rises at the same time. Why can quantity demanded rise or fall?
12. What does a shift of the demand curve to the right show?

### Answers

1. The quantity of a good or service consumers are willing and able to buy at each possible price over a given period.
2. A fall in the good's own price.
3. A change in a condition of demand that makes consumers less willing or able to buy, for example a fall in income, a rise in the price of a complement, or a change in fashion away from the good.
4. The vertical axis.
5. 750 tickets a week.
6. An extension in demand of 150 tickets (300 to 450).
7. Add the quantities demanded by all consumers at each price (aggregation).
8. Demand for bread decreases (shifts left).
9. Demand for coffee increases (shifts right).
10. Any two: prices of substitutes; prices of complements; tastes and fashion; advertising; population; income tax; weather or season.
11. The income rise shifts the curve right, but the price rise causes a contraction along the new curve; the net effect depends on which is larger.
12. An increase in demand: more is demanded at every price.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is "a rise in demand" the same as "an extension in demand"?**
No. A rise (increase) in demand is a shift of the curve caused by a change in a condition of demand. An extension is a movement along the curve caused by a fall in the good's own price.

**Do I need to learn a fixed list of conditions of demand?**
The syllabus asks for the causes of shifts but gives no fixed list. Learn the common ones: income, prices of substitutes and complements, tastes, advertising, population, and taxes on income.
