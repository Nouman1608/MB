## The task in brief

- Unit 3: 2 hours, 50 marks, 30% of the International A-level. Section A is worth 25 marks.
- **One question from a choice of two**, each with data as a starting point.
- Topic covers **early acquisition in young children** and **later development in teenagers**.
- Data: **speech, writing or multimodal texts** by **native or EAL speakers**.
- Show topic knowledge (theories and approaches), data analysis, and academic argument with evaluation.

## Early childhood: quick recall

- Pre-verbal, holophrastic, two-word, telegraphic, post-telegraphic.
- Overgeneralisation ("runned"), overextension, phonological simplification.
- Theories: behaviourist (Skinner), nativist (Chomsky), cognitive (Piaget), social interactionist (Bruner).
- Caregiver features: recasts, expansions, questions, repetition.

## Later development (5-18)

| Area | What develops | Evidence in data |
|---|---|---|
| Grammar | Passives, relative and embedded clauses, nominalisation | "The bridge, which was built in 1900, was repaired" |
| Cohesion | Beyond "and then" | "However", "as a result", "on the other hand" |
| Vocabulary | Abstract, academic and subject lexis; idiom; polysemy | "photosynthesis", "on the fence" |
| Pragmatics | Register shifts, indirect requests, irony and sarcasm, repair | "Would you mind if I...?" |
| Identity | Peer-group slang and discourse markers | "like", "innit", new coinages |

## Adolescent language

- Slang and new forms often spread among teenagers first.
- **Eckert (1989)**: language variation linked to adolescent social categories in a Detroit-area high school.
- **Multicultural London English** (Cheshire, Kerswill and colleagues): a new variety among young people in
  multilingual areas.
- Treat variation as social and systematic, not as decline.

## Writing development

**Kroll (1981)**

1. Preparation: letter formation, basic spelling.
2. Consolidation: writing like speech.
3. Differentiation: writing distinguished from speech; genre awareness.
4. Integration: a controlled personal voice across forms.

**Spelling stages (Gentry, 1982)**: pre-communicative, semi-phonetic, phonetic, transitional, correct.

**What to analyse**: spelling patterns, punctuation, sentence structure, cohesion, genre conventions, audience
awareness, influence of teaching and reading.

## EAL learners

| Concept | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Interlanguage (Selinker, 1972) | The learner's rule-governed developing system between first language and target |
| Transfer | First-language features carried into English; can help or produce non-target forms |
| Overgeneralisation | Applying a rule too widely ("goed", "more fast") |
| Comprehensible input (Krashen) | Input just beyond the learner's current level drives acquisition |
| Silent period | A phase of listening more than speaking |
| BICS and CALP (Cummins) | Conversational fluency develops faster than academic language proficiency |
| Code-switching | Moving between languages as a resource for identity, emphasis or precision |

## Multimodal data

- Abbreviations, emoji, expressive spelling ("sooo"), punctuation for tone.
- **Crystal (2008)**: texting has not damaged literacy; playing with spelling needs knowledge of the standard.
- Research by **Clare Wood and colleagues** found no evidence that textism use harmed children's spelling.

## Analysis method

1. **Context**: age, language background, mode, purpose, audience.
2. **Select** three to five features across levels.
3. **Name and quote** each precisely.
4. **Explain** what the learner can do and what is developing.
5. **Evaluate** a theory or model against the evidence.
6. **Judge** what the data most clearly show.

## Model analytical sentences

- **Child writing**: *"Sed" represents every sound in "said", showing phonetic spelling: the child has strong
  phonological awareness but has not yet learned that this common word is irregular.*
- **Teenage speech**: *The discourse marker "lowkey" hedges the claim "I failed", softening it in a peer-approved
  style and marking the speaker as part of a shared adolescent register.*
- **EAL speech**: *"Yesterday I go" marks time with the adverb but not the verb, a systematic interlanguage feature
  that may reflect first-language transfer rather than random error.*

## Choosing between the two questions

- Read **both** questions and **both** data sets before deciding.
- Prefer the data where you can find features across **several language levels**.
- Prefer the question whose **claim you can evaluate**, not just describe.
- Check you know at least **two concepts or theories** that fit the data.

## Exam traps

- Revising only toddler stages.
- Labelling EAL or teenage forms as errors.
- Ignoring writing or multimodal data.
- Stage models applied rigidly.
- Theories recited rather than tested.
- Choosing a question without reading both sets of data.

## Self-test

1. What two age ranges does the specification include?
2. What kinds of data may be provided?
3. How many questions do you answer in Section A?
4. Name Kroll's four stages.
5. What does a phonetic spelling such as "sed" show?
6. Define interlanguage.
7. Give one example of positive and one of negative transfer.
8. What is the difference between BICS and CALP?
9. What is a silent period?
10. Give two features of later grammatical development.
11. What did Eckert's high-school research link?
12. Why does Crystal argue texting does not damage literacy?

**Answers:** 1. Early acquisition in young children and later development in teenagers. 2. Speech, writing or
multimodal texts, from native or EAL speakers. 3. One, from a choice of two. 4. Preparation, consolidation,
differentiation, integration. 5. That the child represents each sound they hear, showing phonological awareness.
6. Selinker's term for a learner's developing, rule-governed system between the first language and the target.
7. Positive: a cognate word shared with the first language; negative: article omission carried over from a first
language without articles. 8. BICS are conversational skills, developed relatively quickly; CALP is academic language
proficiency, which takes longer. 9. A phase in which a new learner listens and understands more than they speak.
10. Any two: passives; relative and embedded clauses; nominalisation; wider cohesive devices. 11. Language variation
and adolescent social categories. 12. Because deliberate non-standard spellings depend on knowing the standard
forms, so texting can involve literacy skills rather than replace them.
