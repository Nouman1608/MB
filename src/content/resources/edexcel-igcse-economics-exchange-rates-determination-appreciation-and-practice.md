These questions cover section 2.2.3 (Exchange rates) of the Pearson Edexcel International GCSE Economics (4EC1) specification, Issue 3, February 2026, which has a single tier of entry. The countries, currencies and figures are invented. Country C uses the cor and Country D uses the dar. Read the [study guide](/resources/edexcel-igcse-economics-exchange-rates-determination-appreciation-and/) or the [revision notes](/resources/edexcel-igcse-economics-exchange-rates-determination-appreciation-and-revision-notes/) first if you need to. ## Questions

**1.** Define the term exchange rate. **[2]**

**2.** In this question 1 cor = 4.00 dar.

**(a)** Calculate the price in dar of an item that costs 25 cor. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the price in cor of an item that costs 60 dar. **[1]**
**(c)** State the value of 1 dar in cor. **[1]**

**3.** Explain two factors that could increase the demand for the cor. **[4]**

**4.** The table shows the market for the cor, with the price in dar per cor and quantities in millions of cor per day.

| Rate (dar per cor) | Demand for cor | Supply of cor |
|---|---|---|
| 1.0 | 90 | 30 |
| 1.5 | 75 | 45 |
| 2.0 | 60 | 60 |
| 2.5 | 45 | 75 |
| 3.0 | 30 | 90 |

**(a)** State the equilibrium exchange rate and the quantity of cor traded. **[1]**
**(b)** Demand for the cor rises by 30 million at every rate. Find the new equilibrium rate and quantity. **[2]**
**(c)** Find the percentage change in the cor's exchange rate and name this type of change. **[2]**

**5.**

**(a)** Distinguish between appreciation and revaluation of a currency. **[2]**
**(b)** State what is meant by devaluation. **[1]**

**6.** The cor depreciates from 1 cor = 2.0 dar to 1 cor = 1.6 dar.

**(a)** Calculate the dar price of a Country C good that costs 500 cor, before and after the change. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage change in this dar price. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the cor price of a Country D good that costs 600 dar, before and after the change. **[2]**

**7.** Explain how a depreciation of a country's currency may improve its current account. **[6]**

**8.** Country C exports machines priced at 400 cor and imports pumps priced at 100 dar. The rate moves from 1 cor = 2.0 dar to 1 cor = 2.5 dar. Before the change Country C exports 10,000 machines and imports 30,000 pumps. Price elasticity of demand for the machines is 1.2 and for the pumps is 0.5.

**(a)** Calculate the dar price of a machine before and after the change. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the quantity of machines exported after the change and the new export earnings in cor. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the cor price of a pump before and after the change. **[1]**
**(d)** Calculate the quantity of pumps imported after the change and the new import spending in cor. **[2]**
**(e)** Calculate the balance (exports minus imports) before and after the change. **[2]**

**9.** Explain why a depreciation might make a current account deficit larger. **[4]**

**10.** Explain how currency speculators could cause the cor to depreciate. **[4]**

**11.** Explain one benefit and one drawback of an appreciation of the cor for Country C. **[4]**

**12.** "A lower exchange rate is always good for a country with a current account deficit." Evaluate this statement. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** The amount of one currency [1] needed to buy a unit of a different currency [1], for example how many dar are needed to buy one cor. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A definition needs the idea of two currencies. "The value of money" alone is too vague to be credited.

**2. (a)** 25 × 4.00 = **100 dar** [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** 60 ÷ 4.00 = **15 cor** [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** 1 ÷ 4.00 = **0.25 cor** [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the operation you used, so that your method is visible even if a slip occurs in the final figure.

**3.** Two factors, each with a developed reason (a third acceptable factor is speculators expecting the cor to rise, so they buy it now).
- Higher interest rates in Country C [1]: foreign savers want the higher return, so they buy cor to deposit in Country C banks, raising demand [1].
- Higher foreign demand for Country C's exports [1]: foreign buyers must buy cor to pay the exporters, raising demand [1].
**[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Each factor needs a link to the currency market. Saying "exports increase" without saying that foreigners must buy the cor stops short of the explanation.

**4. (a)** The curves cross at **2.0 dar per cor and 60 million cor** [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** New demand: 120, 105, 90, 75, 60 at rates 1.0 to 3.0. Supply equals new demand at 2.5 [1], where both are **75 million cor** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** (2.5 − 2.0) ÷ 2.0 × 100 [1] = **25% appreciation** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* In a diagram or table question, give both the rate and the quantity. For the percentage, divide the change by the starting rate.

**5. (a)** An appreciation is a rise in the value of a currency caused by market forces (demand and supply) [1]. A revaluation is a rise chosen by the government or central bank when it changes a set exchange rate [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Devaluation is a deliberate fall in the value of a currency, set by the government or central bank [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* The two terms differ in cause, so the answer should state the cause for both. Repeating "a rise in value" for each does not separate them.

**6. (a)** Before: 500 × 2.0 = 1,000 dar [1]. After: 500 × 1.6 = 800 dar [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** (800 − 1,000) ÷ 1,000 × 100 = **−20%** [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** Before: 600 ÷ 2.0 = 300 cor [1]. After: 600 ÷ 1.6 = **375 cor** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A fall in the home currency makes exports cheaper abroad and imports dearer at home. Check the direction of your answers against that before moving on.

**7.** A model chain, one mark per developed link:
- A depreciation lowers the price of exports in foreign currency [1].
- Foreign buyers therefore demand a larger quantity of exports [1].
- Export earnings rise if demand is elastic [1].
- The price of imports in the home currency rises [1].
- Households and firms buy a smaller quantity of imports [1].
- Exports minus imports improves, so the current account moves towards surplus or the deficit shrinks, provided demand is elastic enough [1].
**[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Answers gain credit for a clear sequence from the exchange rate to prices, to quantities, to the balance. Stating the conclusion without the middle steps leaves the reasoning unexplained.

**8. (a)** Before: 400 × 2.0 = 800 dar. After: 400 × 2.5 = **1,000 dar** [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** Price rises 25%, so quantity falls 1.2 × 25% = 30% [1]. New quantity 7,000 machines; earnings 7,000 × 400 = **2,800,000 cor** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Before: 100 ÷ 2.0 = 50 cor. After: 100 ÷ 2.5 = **40 cor** [1]. **[1]**
**(d)** Price falls 20%, so quantity rises 0.5 × 20% = 10% [1]. New quantity 33,000 pumps; spending 33,000 × 40 = **1,320,000 cor** [1]. **[2]**
**(e)** Before: 10,000 × 400 − 30,000 × 50 = 4,000,000 − 1,500,000 = 2,500,000 cor [1]. After: 2,800,000 − 1,320,000 = **1,480,000 cor** [1]. The balance has fallen by 1,020,000 cor. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Work in one currency throughout. Convert the pump price into cor before you multiply, and label the unit on each answer.

**9.** A model answer:
- A depreciation raises the price of imports in the home currency [1].
- If demand for imports is inelastic, the quantity bought falls by a smaller percentage than the price rises [1].
- Spending on imports therefore rises [1].
- If demand for exports is also inelastic, export earnings rise by little, so the extra import spending can outweigh the gain and the deficit widens [1].
**[4]**
*Examiner insight:* The explanation turns on elasticity, so name it and say whether it is higher or lower than one. A claim that "imports cost more" alone does not explain a larger deficit.

**10.** A model answer:
- Speculators trade currencies to make a profit from changes in the rate [1].
- If they expect the cor to fall in value, they sell cor now [1].
- This raises the supply of cor on the currency market (the supply curve shifts right) [1].
- The equilibrium rate falls, which may make the expected fall happen [1].
**[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Name the curve that moves and the direction. If you say "demand falls" when speculators sell, the diagram will not match the explanation.

**11.** One benefit and one drawback, each developed.
- Benefit: imports become cheaper in cor [1], so consumers can buy more for the same money and firms that import materials have lower costs [1].
- Drawback: exports become dearer in dar [1], so foreign demand for them falls and exporters sell less, earn less and may cut jobs [1].
**[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Match each effect to the right group. Cheaper imports help consumers and importers; dearer exports hurt exporters.

**12.** Points for the statement (up to 4):
- A lower rate makes exports cheaper abroad [1] and imports dearer at home [1], so exports rise and imports fall [1].
- If demand is elastic, export earnings rise and import spending falls, so the deficit shrinks [1].
Points against (up to 3):
- If demand for imports and exports is inelastic, the import bill can rise more than export earnings, so the deficit widens [1].
- Firms that rely on imported materials face higher costs, which can cancel their gain [1].
- It can take time for buyers to change habits, so the effect may be slow [1].
A judgement that says what the outcome depends on, such as elasticity, the size of the fall and the time allowed [1]. Credit is for developed points, up to **[8]**.
*Examiner insight:* "Always" is a strong word. Answers gain credit when they show a case where the statement fails, with the reason, and then reach a judgement that depends on that case.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing a definition of an exchange rate that never mentions a second currency.
- Multiplying when the conversion needs division.
- Working out a percentage change from the new figure instead of the starting one.
- Moving the wrong curve in a currency diagram, or not labelling the new equilibrium.
- Describing the exports effect without saying the price changes in the foreign currency.
- Treating a fall in import quantity as a fall in import spending.
- Saying a depreciation always cures a deficit, with no mention of elasticity.
- Confusing depreciation (market) with devaluation (government decision).
