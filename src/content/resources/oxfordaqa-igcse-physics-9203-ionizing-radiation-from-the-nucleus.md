This guide covers one subtopic in depth: **3.7.2 Ionizing radiation from the nucleus**, statements a to j of the OxfordAQA International GCSE Physics (9203) specification, for exams May/June 2018 onwards. Statements h to j (half-life, contamination and irradiation, and hazards) are marked **P** in the specification, so they are assessed in the full Physics award only. The specification limits two things you might otherwise over-learn: nuclear equations are only for **single alpha and beta decays**, completing the atomic and mass numbers, and you do **not** need to identify the daughter element; and radiation properties are limited to **ionising power, penetration through materials and range in air**.

## Radioactive decay (statements a to c)

Some nuclei are **unstable**. An unstable nucleus emits particles or radiation and changes into the nucleus of a different element, becoming more stable. This is **radioactive decay**, and the energy emitted comes from changes in the nucleus. Unstable nuclei can emit:

- alpha particles;
- beta particles;
- neutrons;
- gamma rays (electromagnetic radiation), often alongside an alpha or beta emission.

Two features are tested often:

- **Random.** It is impossible to predict when a particular nucleus will decay, even though a large sample follows a pattern overall.
- **Unaffected by conditions.** Neither chemical processes (forming compounds) nor physical processes (heating, cooling, pressure) change the rate of decay, because they affect the electrons and the arrangement of atoms, not the nucleus.

## Background radiation (statement d)

Background radiation is around us all the time. The specification names three kinds of source: radioactive substances in the environment (for example in rocks, soil and the air), radiation from space, and devices such as hospital X-ray machines. Because it is always present, every count rate measured from a source must be **corrected** by subtracting the background count rate.

**Worked example 1.** A detector records 300 counts in 10 minutes with no source present. With a source in place it records 4500 counts in 5 minutes.

```
Background count rate = 300 ÷ 10 = 30 counts per minute
Count rate with source = 4500 ÷ 5 = 900 counts per minute
Corrected count rate  = 900 − 30 = 870 counts per minute
```

Change both to rates per minute before subtracting.

## Alpha, beta and gamma (statements e and g)

| | Alpha (α) | Beta (β) | Gamma (γ) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nature | 2 protons + 2 neutrons (a helium nucleus) | High-speed electron ejected from the nucleus as a neutron turns into a proton | Electromagnetic radiation from the nucleus |
| Relative ionising power | Strongest | Moderate | Weakest |
| Penetration | Stopped by a sheet of paper | Passes through paper; stopped by a few mm of aluminium | Passes through aluminium; reduced by thick lead or concrete |
| Range in air | A few cm | Tens of cm to a few m | Very long |

The link between the rows is energy: each ionisation takes energy from the radiation. Alpha particles ionise so strongly that they lose their energy within a few centimetres of air. Gamma rays ionise weakly, so they travel a long way before losing their energy.

## Nuclear equations (statement f)

In every nuclear equation, the **mass numbers** on each side add to the same total, and so do the **atomic numbers**. Use ⁴₂He for an alpha particle and ⁰₋₁e for a beta particle.

| Decay | Mass number | Atomic number |
|---|---|---|
| Alpha | Falls by 4 | Falls by 2 |
| Beta | Unchanged | Rises by 1 |

**Worked example 2 (alpha).** Radon-222 (atomic number 86) emits an alpha particle.

```
²²²₈₆Rn → ᴬ_Z X + ⁴₂He
A = 222 − 4 = 218        Z = 86 − 2 = 84
Product: ²¹⁸₈₄X          Check: 218 + 4 = 222 and 84 + 2 = 86
```

**Worked example 3 (beta).** Strontium-90 (atomic number 38) emits a beta particle.

```
⁹⁰₃₈Sr → ᴬ_Z X + ⁰₋₁e
A = 90 − 0 = 90          Z = 38 − (−1) = 39
Product: ⁹⁰₃₉X           Check: 90 + 0 = 90 and 39 + (−1) = 38
```

The atomic number **rises** in beta decay because a neutron has become a proton. You only need to complete the numbers; naming the new element is not required.

## Half-life (statement h, P)

Although single decays are random, a large number of nuclei decays predictably. The specification gives two definitions of **half-life**:

- the average time for the number of nuclei of the isotope in a sample to halve;
- the time for the count rate from a sample containing the isotope to fall to half its initial level.

**Worked example 4.** A sample has a corrected count rate of 960 counts per minute. Its half-life is 8 minutes. Find the count rate after 24 minutes.

```
Number of half-lives = 24 ÷ 8 = 3
960 → 480 → 240 → 120 counts per minute
```

From a graph of count rate against time, read the time for the count rate to halve from any starting value; it should come out the same each time.

## Contamination and irradiation (statement i, P)

**Contamination** is the unwanted presence of radioactive atoms on or in other materials. The hazard continues for as long as the contaminating atoms keep decaying, and it depends on the type of radiation. An alpha emitter outside the body is fairly harmless, because skin stops alpha particles, but swallowed or breathed in it is very dangerous, because its strong ionisation acts directly on nearby cells.

**Irradiation** is exposing an object to ionising radiation. The object **does not become radioactive**, so the hazard stops when the source is removed or shielded. Precautions include shielding, keeping a distance, limiting the time of exposure and handling sources with tongs.

## Half-life and hazard (statement j, P)

Half-lives range from fractions of a second to billions of years. The **most unstable** nuclei have the **shortest** half-lives: they emit a lot of radiation in a short time and then little remains. The **least unstable** have the **longest** half-lives: they emit little radiation each second but keep emitting it for a very long time. When evaluating a use of radiation, weigh both the type of radiation and the half-life: for example, a source used inside the body should have a short half-life so that it does not stay active for long, while a source in a long-term device needs a long half-life.

## Common mistakes

- Saying a beta particle comes from the electron shells. It is ejected from the nucleus.
- Writing that heating speeds up decay.
- Subtracting background counts instead of background count rates.
- Making the atomic number fall in beta decay.
- Saying lead stops gamma completely. It reduces it.
- Believing an irradiated object becomes radioactive.

## Exam technique

- **Identify** a radiation from absorber data: compare the corrected count rate before and after each absorber.
- **Complete** an equation: write the totals for both sides and check them.
- **Compare** contamination and irradiation: say which continues after the source is removed.
- **Evaluate** a choice of source: refer to type, penetration and half-life together.
