Condensed notes for AQA A-level Biology (7402) section **3.7.3 Evolution may lead to speciation**. Worked examples on selection graphs and drift, and a six-mark speciation plan, are in the [evolution and speciation study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-biology-7402-evolution-may-lead-to-speciation/). Test yourself with the [evolution and speciation practice questions](/resources/aqa-a-level-biology-7402-evolution-may-lead-to-speciation-practice/).

## Variation in phenotype

- Caused by **genetic factors** and **environmental factors**.
- **Mutation:** primary source of genetic variation; the only source of new alleles.
- **Meiosis:** crossing over and independent segregation → new allele combinations.
- **Random fertilisation:** any gamete with any gamete → more combinations.
- Environmental variation is not inherited.

## Natural selection chain

1. Variation (alleles from mutation).
2. Selection pressure: predation, disease, competition for the means of survival.
3. Individuals with an advantageous phenotype are more likely to survive.
4. They reproduce more (differential reproductive success).
5. They pass on the favourable alleles.
6. Frequency of those alleles increases in the gene pool over generations.

**Evolution** = a change in the allele frequencies in a population.

## Types of selection

| Type | Environment | Effect | Graph |
|---|---|---|---|
| Stabilising | Stable | Extremes selected against; mean unchanged | Narrower peak, same centre |
| Directional | Changing | One extreme favoured | Peak shifts sideways |
| Disruptive | Two or more niches | Both extremes favoured | Two peaks |

Classic stabilising example: human birth mass.

## Genetic drift

- Change in allele frequency by **chance**, not advantage.
- No direction.
- Important only in **small populations**: a few chance events are a large share of the gene pool.
- Can lead to an allele being lost or fixed.
- In large populations, chance effects average out.

## Speciation sequence

1. Reproductive separation of two populations → no gene flow.
2. Different mutations arise in each.
3. Different selection pressures (and drift in small populations).
4. Allele frequencies change differently; differences accumulate in the gene pools.
5. Members can no longer interbreed to produce fertile offspring → new species.

## Allopatric versus sympatric

| | Allopatric | Sympatric |
|---|---|---|
| Separation | Geographical barrier | Reproductive isolation in the same area |
| Examples of cause | Mountains, sea, river, landslide | Different breeding seasons, courtship, or structures that prevent mating |

## Diversity over long periods

- Speciation happens again and again.
- Each new species can split further.
- Over long periods → great diversity of species sharing common ancestors.

## Selection compared with drift

| | Natural selection | Genetic drift |
|---|---|---|
| Cause | Differential survival and reproduction linked to phenotype | Chance |
| Direction | Towards advantageous alleles | Random |
| Population size | Any | Important mainly in small populations |
| Depends on environment? | Yes | No |

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Selection pressure | Environmental factor that affects survival and reproduction, such as predation or disease |
| Differential reproductive success | Some phenotypes leave more offspring than others |
| Evolution | Change in allele frequencies in a population |
| Genetic drift | Chance change in allele frequency |
| Reproductive separation | Two populations no longer interbreeding |
| Allopatric speciation | Speciation after geographical separation |
| Sympatric speciation | Speciation in the same area through reproductive isolation |
| Gene flow | Transfer of alleles between populations by interbreeding |

## Exam traps

- Mutations are random; they are not caused by the need to adapt.
- Answers must reach "allele frequency increases".
- Evolution is defined by allele frequencies, not by appearance.
- Directional = one shifted peak; disruptive = two peaks.
- Drift is not caused by advantage, and matters mainly in small populations.
- Speciation answers must end with "cannot interbreed to produce fertile offspring".
- Sympatric answers need a named isolating mechanism.
- Only mutation creates new alleles; meiosis and fertilisation reshuffle them.

## Answer frames that score

- **Selection:** "Snails with darker shells were less visible to birds, so more survived and reproduced, passing on the allele for dark shells; the frequency of this allele increased."
- **Drift:** "In a small population, chance events such as the death of a few individuals have a large effect on allele frequencies, so an allele can be lost or fixed."
- **Speciation:** "With no gene flow, different mutations and selection pressures changed allele frequencies differently in each gene pool until the populations could no longer interbreed to produce fertile offspring."

## Quick self-test

1. What is the primary source of genetic variation?
2. Name two other sources of genetic variation.
3. Give three selection pressures named in the specification.
4. Define evolution.
5. Describe the effect of stabilising selection on a distribution.
6. In what kind of environment is disruptive selection likely?
7. What is genetic drift?
8. Why is drift important only in small populations?
9. What must happen for two populations to become separate species?
10. What separates populations in allopatric speciation?
11. Give one mechanism that could cause sympatric speciation.
12. How has evolution produced a great diversity of species?

### Answers

1. Mutation.
2. Meiosis (crossing over, independent segregation) and random fertilisation.
3. Predation, disease, competition for the means of survival.
4. A change in the allele frequencies in a population.
5. Extremes are selected against; the mean stays the same and the range narrows.
6. One with two or more distinct niches or conditions favouring both extremes.
7. A change in allele frequency due to chance.
8. Chance events affect a large proportion of a small gene pool, but average out in a large one.
9. Their gene pools must become so different that members can no longer interbreed to produce fertile offspring.
10. A geographical barrier.
11. Any one: different breeding seasons; different courtship behaviour; changes in reproductive structures that prevent mating.
12. Repeated speciation over long periods, each species able to give rise to further species.
