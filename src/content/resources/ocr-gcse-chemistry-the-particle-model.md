## C1.1a: The particle model and states of matter

In the particle model, everything is built from tiny particles, which may be atoms, molecules or ions. It explains the three states by how the particles are **arranged**, how they **move** and how **close** they are.

| | Solid | Liquid | Gas |
|---|---|---|---|
| Arrangement | Regular, fixed pattern | Random, irregular | Random |
| Spacing | Touching, closely packed | Mostly touching, close | Far apart, with empty space between |
| Movement | Vibrate on the spot | Slide past one another | Dart about fast and randomly |
| Particle energy | Least | Middle | Greatest |
| Shape and volume | Fixed shape, fixed volume | Takes the container's shape, fixed volume | Fills the container |
| Compressible? | No | Hardly | Yes, easily |

Two ideas trip people up. First, the space between gas particles is **empty**, not filled with air. Second, the particles themselves **do not change** when a substance is heated. They do not swell or melt. They gain energy and move faster. A metal bar expands because its particles vibrate more and take up more room, not because each particle gets bigger.

### Changes of state

| Process | Start state → end state | Energy |
|---|---|---|
| Melting | solid → liquid | taken in |
| Freezing | liquid → solid | given out |
| Boiling or evaporating | liquid → gas | taken in |
| Condensing | gas → liquid | given out |
| Subliming | solid → gas directly | taken in |

Evaporation happens at a liquid's surface at any temperature; boiling happens throughout the liquid at its boiling point.

Changes of state are **reversible**. Cool water vapour and you get liquid water; cool that and you get ice. The particles are the same water molecules throughout, and the mass does not change, because no particles are gained or lost.

### Worked example 1: deciding a state from data

*Use these values. At what state is each substance at 20 °C, and which one changes state if it is warmed to 60 °C? Describe what happens to its particles.*

| Substance | Melting point / °C | Boiling point / °C |
|---|---|---|
| P | −62 | 48 |
| Q | 115 | 445 |
| R | −218 | −183 |
| S | 770 | 1420 |

**Step 1.** Below the melting point a substance is solid; between the melting and boiling points it is liquid; above the boiling point it is gas.

**Step 2.** At 20 °C: P is a **liquid** (20 is between −62 and 48). Q is a **solid** (20 is below 115). R is a **gas** (20 is above −183). S is a **solid**.

**Step 3.** At 60 °C, P is above its boiling point, so **P boils and becomes a gas**. Q, R and S stay in the same state.

**Step 4.** For P, the particles take in energy, move faster and spread far apart, moving quickly and randomly. The particles themselves stay the same.

Watch the negative numbers: −183 is higher than −218.

## C1.1b: Physical changes and chemical changes

The particle model gives you a clear test. Ask what happens to the **particles themselves**.

- In a **physical change**, the particles stay the same. Only their arrangement, spacing or movement changes. No new substance forms. Changes of state and dissolving are physical changes. They are usually easy to reverse.
- In a **chemical change**, atoms are **rearranged** to make **new particles**. Bonds between atoms break and new bonds form, so at least one new substance forms with different properties. The atoms are not created or destroyed, so the total mass of everything in a closed system stays the same.

The particle model also explains what elements and compounds are. An element is made of only one type of atom. In a chemical change, atoms of different elements can join to form a compound, and a compound's atoms can separate and recombine into different substances.

Do not use "can it be reversed?" as your only test. Some chemical changes can be reversed. The deciding question is whether new particles have formed.

### Worked example 2: one candle, two kinds of change

*A candle is lit. The wax near the wick turns to a clear liquid, some liquid wax rises up the wick and becomes a vapour, and the vapour burns. When the candle is blown out, the clear liquid sets white again. Classify each change and explain using particles.*

**Melting of the wax (physical).** The wax particles gain energy, leave their fixed positions and move past each other. When the candle is blown out they lose energy and the wax sets. Same particles, so no new substance.

**Wax liquid becoming vapour (physical).** The wax particles gain more energy and move apart as a gas. They are still wax particles.

**Burning of the vapour (chemical).** The wax molecules react with oxygen from the air. Their atoms are rearranged into new particles: carbon dioxide molecules and water molecules. These are new substances, and the change gives out energy as heat and light. You cannot get the wax back by cooling the products.

### The practical suggestion: observing both kinds of change

The specification suggests observing changes of state and comparing them with chemical changes. A typical lesson setup:

1. Gently warm a small piece of wax in a test tube in a water bath. Watch it melt, then let it cool and set. Note that it looks and behaves the same afterwards.
2. Heat a small amount of green copper(II) carbonate in a test tube over a Bunsen burner. It turns black, and the gas given off turns limewater cloudy. On cooling, the black solid does not turn green again.

The wax shows a physical change: same substance before and after. The copper(II) carbonate shows a chemical change: a new black solid (copper(II) oxide) and a new gas (carbon dioxide) form. Wear eye protection, and take the delivery tube out of the limewater before the heating stops, so no suck-back happens.

## C1.1c: Limitations of the particle model (Higher Tier only)

The simple model draws particles as **inelastic spheres**, like bowling balls: hard, solid, all alike and bouncing without changing. This is useful for describing the states, but OCR names three things it does not take into account.

1. **Forces of attraction between particles.** Bowling balls do not pull on each other. Real particles do attract each other. The model therefore cannot explain why energy is needed to melt or boil a substance, or why different substances melt and boil at different temperatures. A substance with stronger attractions between its particles needs more energy to separate them.
2. **The size of the particles.** The model shows every particle as the same size. Real particles have different sizes. A molecule of wax is much larger than a molecule of water, and an ion is a different size from an atom.
3. **The space between particles.** Diagrams usually draw gas particles only two or three particle-widths apart, so that they fit on the page. In reality the gaps are far larger.

Nor does the model explain why different materials have different properties. That needs the idea that the particles, and how they are held together, differ between substances (Topic C2).

### Worked example 3: how far apart are gas particles?

*1 g of liquid water takes up about 1 cm³. As steam at 100 °C and normal atmospheric pressure, the same 1 g takes up about 1,700 cm³. A textbook diagram shows water particles as circles 4 mm across. Estimate how far apart the centres of steam particles should be on a to-scale diagram, and comment on the textbook diagram.*

**Step 1.** Volume ratio = 1700 ÷ 1 = 1,700.

**Step 2.** Volume depends on distance cubed, so the distance between particles grows by the cube root: ∛1700 ≈ 11.9, or about 12.

**Step 3.** In the liquid, particles are roughly touching, so their centres are about one particle-width apart. In the steam, centres are about 12 particle-widths apart: 12 × 4 mm = **48 mm**, nearly 5 cm.

**Step 4.** Across a 210 mm wide sheet of A4, only about four steam particles would fit in a row. A diagram with gas particles a few millimetres apart shows the spaces far too small. The particles themselves fill only about 1/1700 of the volume, roughly 0.06%.

## CM1.1i: Moving between three dimensions and two

Real substances are three-dimensional, but you draw them on flat paper. You need to read a 2D diagram as a 3D structure and the other way round.

### Worked example 4: a model crystal

*A model of a solid is made as a cube of balls, 4 balls along each edge. How many balls are there? How many does a 2D drawing of one face show? How many balls are completely hidden inside?*

**Step 1.** Total balls = 4 × 4 × 4 = **64**.

**Step 2.** One face is a 4 × 4 square, so a 2D drawing shows **16**. A 2D diagram of a solid shows only one layer.

**Step 3.** The hidden inner cube is 2 balls along each edge (remove one layer from each side): 2 × 2 × 2 = **8**. So 64 − 8 = 56 balls are on the surface.

The same skill applies to the allotropes of carbon named in CM1.1i. A flat drawing of graphite shows one layer of hexagons, but graphite is many stacked layers. A 2D sketch of diamond hides the fact that its carbon atoms are joined in three dimensions. You study these in Topic C2.

## How the model was developed

Evidence for particles built up over time (Working Scientifically 1.1a). In 1827 the botanist Robert Brown saw tiny particles from pollen grains jiggling randomly in water under a microscope. In 1905 Albert Einstein explained this as the particles being struck by moving water molecules, and Jean Perrin's experiments in 1908–1909 confirmed his predictions. New instruments gave new evidence, and the model gained support. The model is also used to make predictions (Working Scientifically 1.1b), such as which state a substance will be in at a given temperature.

## Common errors

- Saying particles "expand", "melt" or "get hotter" when a substance is heated. The particles gain energy and move more; the substance expands.
- Saying there is air between gas particles, or that liquid particles are "far apart".
- Calling dissolving a chemical change.
- Using reversibility alone to decide between physical and chemical change.
- For Higher Tier, listing limitations of the model without saying what the model leaves out: forces, sizes, spaces.
