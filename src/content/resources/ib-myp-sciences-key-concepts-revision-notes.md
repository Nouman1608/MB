These revision notes condense the key concepts of IB MYP Sciences for the final weeks before assessment. For full explanations and worked examples, read the [key concepts study guide](/resources/ib-myp-sciences-key-concepts/) first. The notes follow the IB *MYP Sciences guide* (for use from September 2014/January 2015; published May 2014, updated May 2019), and suit MYP years 4 and 5 (there is no SL/HL split).

MYP has no prescribed content list -- schools design their own units -- but the key and related concepts are prescribed, and teachers must plan with them. Test yourself afterwards with the [key concepts practice questions](/resources/ib-myp-sciences-key-concepts-practice/). Course hub: [/boards/ib/ib-myp/myp-sciences/](/boards/ib/ib-myp/myp-sciences/). Printable checklist: [/checklists/ib/ib-myp/myp-sciences/](/checklists/ib/ib-myp/myp-sciences/).

## Core definitions

- **Concept**: a lasting "big idea" whose importance reaches beyond one topic, place or time.
- **Key concept**: a broad concept that works within and across subjects. There are sixteen in the MYP.
- **Sciences key concepts**: **change**, **relationships** and **systems**.
- **Other key concepts the guide says can matter in sciences**: **development**, **time, place and space**, and **creativity**.
- **Related concept**: a narrower, discipline-based concept used to explore a key concept in more depth (for example energy, models, evidence).
- **Global context**: the real-world setting that shows why the inquiry matters. There are six.
- **Statement of inquiry**: one sentence that sets the conceptual understanding in a global context.
- **Inquiry questions**: factual, conceptual and debatable questions drawn from the statement of inquiry.

## The three sciences key concepts at a glance

| Key concept | What it means in sciences | Words to use |
|---|---|---|
| Change | The difference in a system's state when observed at different times | qualitative, quantitative, rate; reversible, irreversible, self-perpetuating; cause, process, consequence |
| Relationships | Connections among variables, found by observation or experiment and testable by experiment | variable, correlation, proportional, form and function, model |
| Systems | Sets of components that function because they are interdependent or complementary | component, interdependent, open, closed, static, dynamic, simple, complex |

### Change: two ways to classify

**By what changes**
- Qualitative: structure, behaviour or level changes. Example: a caterpillar becomes a butterfly.
- Quantitative: a number or rate changes. Example: the count rate from a radioactive source falls over time.

**By whether it can go back**
- Reversible: sugar dissolves in water and can be recovered by evaporating the water.
- Irreversible: a cake bakes; an iron nail rusts.
- Self-perpetuating: the change drives more of itself. A forest fire spreads because burning releases heat that ignites the next trees.

### Relationships: three things to say

1. **Name the variables** and describe the pattern (increases, decreases, proportional, levels off).
2. **Give a scientific reason** for the pattern.
3. **Say how you could test it** by experiment, changing one variable and controlling others.

Form and function is a special relationship: link a feature (form) to what it does (function). Examples:
- Biology: a fish's gills have many thin filaments -- a large surface for absorbing oxygen from water.
- Chemistry: powdered zinc reacts faster with acid than a lump -- more surface area for collisions.
- Physics: a heat sink has many thin metal fins -- a large surface for transferring energy to the air.

Modelling represents relationships that are too large, too slow or too data-heavy to observe directly: for example, how a disease spreads through a population, or how a star changes over millions of years.

### Systems: open or closed

| | Guide's meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Closed system | Resources are not removed or replaced | A sealed jar with a burning candle: no new oxygen enters, so the flame goes out |
| Open system | Necessary resources are renewed regularly | A river: water, sediment and organisms flow in and out |

Models often use closed systems to simplify or limit variables. Further examples of systems: the digestive system (organs depend on each other), the Earth--Moon system (gravity links the parts), a fuel cell (fuel, oxygen, electrodes and electrolyte interact).

Remember: in later physics and chemistry, "closed" usually means no matter crosses the boundary, though energy can, and "isolated" means neither crosses. Say which meaning you are using.

## The other three key concepts

| Key concept | Why it matters in science | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Development | Scientific knowledge grows through change | Models of the solar system moved from Earth-centred to Sun-centred as evidence built up |
| Time, place and space | Science defines and measures them | The metre is defined using the speed of light |
| Creativity | Scientists need imagination to extend understanding | Inventing a new way to measure a quantity you cannot observe directly |

## Related concepts: the short version

The guide lists twelve related concepts for each of biology, chemistry and physics (and a list for integrated or modular courses that matches the biology one).

| | Related concepts |
|---|---|
| In all three sciences | consequences, energy, evidence, form, function, interaction, models, movement, patterns |
| Biology adds | balance, environment, transformation |
| Chemistry adds | balance, conditions, transfer |
| Physics adds | development, environment, transformation |

Teachers may use others, including concepts from other subject groups.

## The six global contexts

1. Identities and relationships
2. Orientation in space and time
3. Personal and cultural expression
4. Scientific and technical innovation
5. Globalization and sustainability
6. Fairness and development

Many science units sit naturally in scientific and technical innovation, but courses should give you chances to explore all six over time.

## Method in steps: writing a statement of inquiry

1. Pick **one key concept** (for example relationships).
2. Add **one or two related concepts** (for example interaction, consequences).
3. Choose **one global context** (for example identities and relationships, exploring health).
4. Write one sentence that links them and **does not name the topic**.
5. Test it: would it still be true in a different unit?

Example: *Interactions between substances and the body have consequences for health that depend on dose and exposure.* This fits units on medicines, alcohol or air pollution.

## Method in steps: sorting inquiry questions

The guide's headings are remembering facts and topics (factual), analysing big ideas (conceptual), and evaluating perspectives (debatable). The tests below are a Marlbridge revision aid.

| Type | Test | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Factual | Has one right answer you can look up | What is the unit of electrical resistance? |
| Conceptual | Explains a big idea that transfers to other topics | How does structure determine the properties of a material? |
| Debatable | Reasonable people disagree; needs evidence and perspectives | Should petrol cars be banned in city centres? |

## Must-know distinctions

- **Key concept vs related concept**: change, relationships, systems are key; energy, models, evidence are related.
- **Qualitative vs quantitative change**: kind of difference vs measured difference.
- **Reversible vs self-perpetuating**: one can go back; the other feeds itself forward.
- **Correlation vs tested relationship**: two variables moving together is not enough; a relationship in science can be tested by experiment.
- **Open vs closed system**: resources renewed vs not removed or replaced.
- **Topic vs statement of inquiry**: "Photosynthesis" is a topic; a statement of inquiry is a broader idea set in a global context.

## Quick self-test

1. Name the three key concepts the guide gives for sciences.
2. Name the three other key concepts the guide says can also be important in sciences.
3. Is boiling an egg reversible, irreversible or self-perpetuating?
4. A cooling drink falls from 80 °C to 52 °C in 10 minutes. Calculate the mean rate of temperature change.
5. Is a mountain lake fed by streams an open or a closed system? Give a reason.
6. A cube-shaped model cell has side 3 cm. Calculate its surface area to volume ratio.
7. Is "energy" a key concept or a related concept in MYP sciences?
8. Name two related concepts listed for chemistry but not for physics.
9. Classify: "What is the chemical formula of methane?"
10. Classify: "Should governments fund space exploration over medical research?"
11. Why do scientific models often use closed systems?
12. What is wrong with the statement of inquiry "Magnets attract iron"?

### Answers

1. Change, relationships, systems.
2. Development; time, place and space; creativity.
3. Irreversible: the egg proteins change permanently.
4. Fall = 80 − 52 = 28 °C; 28 ÷ 10 = **2.8 °C per minute**.
5. Open: water flows in from streams and out again, so its resources are renewed.
6. SA = 6 × 3² = 54 cm²; V = 3³ = 27 cm³; SA:V = 54 : 27 = **2 : 1**.
7. Related concept.
8. Any two of balance, conditions and transfer.
9. Factual.
10. Debatable.
11. To simplify the situation or limit the number of variables.
12. It is a topic fact. It has no key or related concept, no global context, and does not transfer to other units.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing an everyday definition of change ("when something becomes different") instead of the science one: a difference in a system's state at two observed times.
- Classifying a change without a reason. "Irreversible" earns more when you add why: new substances form, or structure is permanently altered.
- Describing a relationship with no direction ("they are linked") rather than stating how one variable changes as the other changes.
- Giving a form without its function, or a function without the form that causes it. Both halves are needed.
- Listing the parts of a system without saying how they depend on each other.
- Calling a system closed when matter or resources clearly enter or leave it.
- Naming energy, models or evidence as key concepts.
- Writing a statement of inquiry that names the topic and would not transfer.
- Writing a "debatable" question that has a factual answer.
