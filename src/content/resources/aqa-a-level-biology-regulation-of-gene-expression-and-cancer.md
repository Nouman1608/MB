## Transcription factors and oestrogen

In eukaryotes a gene is not transcribed all the time. **Transcription factors** are proteins that bind to specific base sequences of DNA near a target gene. Some stimulate transcription, by helping RNA polymerase to bind. Others inhibit it. The specification describes factors that move from the cytoplasm into the nucleus, so a gene can be switched by a signal that arrives at the cell.

**Oestrogen** is a steroid hormone, so it is lipid-soluble and diffuses through the phospholipid bilayer of the cell-surface membrane. The sequence is:

1. Oestrogen diffuses into the cell and binds to a specific **receptor** in the cytoplasm. Only cells that have this receptor respond.
2. Binding changes the shape of the receptor, forming a hormone-receptor complex that now acts as a transcription factor.
3. The complex enters the nucleus through a nuclear pore.
4. It binds to a specific base sequence next to the target gene.
5. It stimulates transcription: RNA polymerase can bind and make mRNA, which is translated into a protein.

The same pattern can work in reverse. A different transcription factor can bind and stop RNA polymerase attaching, so the gene is inhibited. In your answer, always say which gene is switched, which molecule binds, and what happens to RNA polymerase.

### Worked example: reading an expression experiment

Cells with oestrogen receptors were grown with and without oestrogen, and the mRNA from one target gene was measured (arbitrary units).

| Condition | mRNA level |
|---|---|
| Receptor-containing cells, no oestrogen | 12 |
| Receptor-containing cells, oestrogen added | 138 |
| Cells lacking the receptor, no oestrogen | 11 |
| Cells lacking the receptor, oestrogen added | 13 |
| Receptor-containing cells, oestrogen plus receptor-blocking drug | 15 |

Percentage increase with oestrogen: (138 − 12) / 12 × 100 = 1050%. The level is 11.5 times higher.

Interpretation: oestrogen increases transcription of the gene (138 against 12). The cells without the receptor barely change (11 to 13), so the receptor is needed. The blocking drug keeps the level near the starting value, which supports the idea that oestrogen must bind the receptor. The data show the effect on mRNA, not directly on transcription rate, because mRNA level also depends on how fast mRNA breaks down.

## Epigenetics

**Epigenetics** involves heritable changes in gene function without changes to the base sequence of DNA. The changes are caused by environmental changes and they inhibit transcription. The specification names two routes.

**Increased DNA methylation.** Methyl groups are added to bases in DNA, most often cytosine. The methylated region is not transcribed, because transcription factors cannot bind as easily and the DNA packs more tightly.

**Decreased histone acetylation.** DNA wraps around histone proteins. Histones are positively charged, so they attract the negatively charged phosphate groups of DNA. Acetylation of histones reduces this attraction. When acetylation decreases, the histones keep a stronger hold, the DNA is tightly wound, and transcription factors and RNA polymerase cannot reach the gene. High acetylation gives looser DNA and transcription can occur.

| Change | Effect on DNA packing | Effect on transcription |
|---|---|---|
| Increased methylation | Tighter, less accessible | Inhibited |
| Decreased acetylation | Histones hold DNA tightly | Inhibited |
| Increased acetylation | Looser | Possible |

Because these marks are copied when cells divide, they are passed to daughter cells. This explains how a cell type keeps its identity. It also means that an environmental factor can have lasting effects. The base sequence is unchanged, so epigenetic change is not a mutation.

### Worked example: methylation data

Gene G was studied in normal and tumour tissue.

| Tissue | Promoter sites methylated (%) | mRNA from gene G (relative) |
|---|---|---|
| Normal | 8 | 100 |
| Tumour | 74 | 6 |

Methylation is 74 / 8 = 9.25 times higher in the tumour. The mRNA falls by (100 − 6) / 100 × 100 = 94%. The two changes go together, so the data are consistent with methylation silencing gene G. They show a correlation, not proof of cause. A stronger test would remove the methyl groups and see whether mRNA recovers.

## RNA interference

In eukaryotes and some prokaryotes, translation of mRNA can be inhibited by **RNA interference (RNAi)**. The steps for small interfering RNA (siRNA) are:

1. Double-stranded RNA is cut into short lengths of siRNA by an enzyme.
2. One strand of the siRNA joins a protein complex.
3. The strand pairs by **complementary base pairing** with an mRNA that has the matching sequence.
4. The complex breaks down (hydrolyses) that mRNA.
5. The mRNA is not translated, so the protein is not made.

The gene is still transcribed. The control is at translation, after transcription. RNAi is therefore different from methylation and acetylation, which act on DNA.

### Worked example: matching siRNA to a target

A stretch of mRNA reads AUG GCU CCG UUA GCA. An siRNA guide strand is designed to pair with the 12 bases after AUG. Write each base's partner (A with U, G with C):

mRNA bases 4 to 15: G C U C C G U U A G C A
siRNA guide strand: C G A G G C A A U C G U

The siRNA is CGAGGCAAUCGU. If a protein level was 240 units in control cells and 54 units after siRNA treatment, the fall is (240 − 54) / 240 × 100 = 77.5%.

## Benign and malignant tumours

A **tumour** is a mass of cells formed by uncontrolled cell division.

| Feature | Benign | Malignant |
|---|---|---|
| Growth | Usually slow | Usually rapid |
| Boundary | Often a distinct border or capsule | Irregular; invades surrounding tissue |
| Spread | Does not spread to other tissues | Cells break away and form secondary tumours (metastasis), often via blood or lymph |
| Cells | Usually resemble normal cells of the tissue | Often less specialised, with abnormal nuclei |
| Danger | Usually less serious, but can press on organs | Often life-threatening |

## Oncogenes and tumour suppressor genes

Normal cells have genes that stimulate division and genes that slow it down. Cancer arises when this balance fails.

- **Oncogenes** are mutated or over-expressed forms of normal genes (proto-oncogenes) that stimulate cell division. A change makes the gene permanently active, or increases its expression, so cells divide without the usual signals. One altered allele can be enough.
- **Tumour suppressor genes** slow cell division, help to repair DNA mistakes, or cause cell death when damage cannot be repaired. When a mutation inactivates them, division is not held in check.

**Abnormal methylation** gives the same outcomes without a base change:

| Gene type | Abnormal methylation | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Tumour suppressor gene | Hypermethylation (too much) | Gene silenced; no slowing of division |
| Oncogene (proto-oncogene) | Hypomethylation (too little) | Gene over-expressed; faster division |

**Oestrogen and breast cancer.** In some breast cancers, cells with oestrogen receptors respond to oestrogen by dividing. Higher oestrogen concentrations, or a longer time at higher concentrations, stimulate more cell divisions. More divisions give more chances for mutation during DNA replication.

### Using the knowledge for prevention, treatment and cure

You may be given information and asked to interpret it. Typical ideas:

- **Prevention:** genetic testing can show that a person carries a faulty tumour suppressor allele; they can then be screened sooner or take preventive steps.
- **Treatment:** a drug can block the oestrogen receptor, so oestrogen cannot stimulate division in receptor-positive cells; siRNA can be designed to lower the translation of an oncogene mRNA.
- **Cure:** treatments that reverse methylation or restore a working tumour suppressor gene aim to restore control of division.

## Evaluating evidence on genetic risk

### Worked example: carrier data

In a long-term study, 400 women with a variant allele and 800 without were followed to age 70. Cancer developed in 62 carriers and 41 non-carriers.

- Carriers: 62 / 400 × 100 = 15.5%.
- Non-carriers: 41 / 800 × 100 = 5.1% (5.125% before rounding).
- Relative risk: 15.5 / 5.125 = 3.0. Carriers are about three times as likely to be affected.

Evaluate: the group sizes are large, the groups were followed for the same time, and the effect is large. Still, 84.5% of carriers did not develop cancer, so the allele increases risk but does not decide the outcome. Check age, diet, smoking, family history and whether chance alone could explain the gap.

### Worked example: twin data

Among 100 pairs of identical twins in which one twin had a cancer, the other twin also had it in 27 pairs. Among 100 pairs of non-identical twins, it was 13. Identical twins share all alleles, non-identical share about half. The higher figure for identical twins suggests a genetic influence. It is only 27%, so 73 of the identical pairs differ, and environment, chance and epigenetic differences must contribute.

### Points for any evaluation

- Correlation does not prove cause.
- Check sample size, controls, age matching and confounding variables.
- State the size of the effect, not only its direction.
- Say what extra evidence would help.

## Common errors

- Saying oestrogen "enters the nucleus"; the hormone-receptor complex does.
- Saying methylation changes the base sequence.
- Saying acetylation increases the positive charge on histones; it reduces it.
- Placing RNAi at transcription; it prevents translation.
- Calling every tumour cancerous; benign tumours do not spread.
- Dividing a change by the final figure; the starting figure is the divisor.
