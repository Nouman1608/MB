These notes condense subtopic **5.2 Effects of temperature and pH** of Cambridge O Level Biology 5090, 2026–2028 series: following the progress of an enzyme-catalysed reaction by measuring reactants or products, investigating and describing the effects of temperature and pH, and explaining them in terms of kinetic energy, shape and fit, denaturation and the frequency of effective collisions. Full explanations and a worked data example are in the [5.2 study guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-biology-5090-effects-of-temperature-and-ph/). Exam-style questions are in the [5.2 practice questions](/resources/cambridge-o-level-biology-5090-effects-of-temperature-and-ph-practice/). The mechanism itself is in the [5.1 revision notes](/resources/cambridge-o-level-biology-5090-enzyme-action-revision-notes/).

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Rate of reaction | How quickly substrate is used up or product is formed; often 1 ÷ time |
| Optimum temperature | Temperature at which the enzyme's activity is highest |
| Optimum pH | pH at which the enzyme's activity is highest |
| Kinetic energy | Energy of movement of molecules; rises with temperature |
| Effective collision | A collision in which the substrate enters the active site and a complex forms |
| Denaturation | Permanent change in the shape of the enzyme's active site, so the substrate no longer fits |
| Buffer solution | A solution that keeps pH constant |
| Control | A set-up without active enzyme (for example boiled enzyme) to show the enzyme causes the change |

## Following a reaction

- **Substrate disappearing:** amylase + starch; drops tested with iodine on a spotting tile; end point when iodine stays orange-brown (no blue-black).
- **Product appearing:** Benedict's test for reducing sugar; blue → green → yellow → orange → brick-red as more sugar forms.
- **Gas product:** volume collected in a fixed time.

## Planning an investigation

| | Temperature investigation | pH investigation |
|---|---|---|
| Independent variable | Temperature (water baths) | pH (buffer solutions) |
| Dependent variable | Time for starch to disappear (→ rate) | Same |
| Key controlled variables | pH (buffer), enzyme and substrate volume and concentration | Temperature (water bath), enzyme and substrate volume and concentration |
| Control | Boiled enzyme | Boiled enzyme |

- Pre-warm enzyme and substrate separately before mixing.
- Repeat each condition at least three times; calculate a mean, leaving out anomalies.
- Use smaller intervals near the optimum to locate it more precisely.

## Calculating rate

```
rate = 1 / time      (or 1000 / time for easier numbers)
```

Example: 50 s → 1000 ÷ 50 = 20.0; 80 s → 12.5; 150 s → 6.7. A shorter time means a faster rate. Plot rate, not time, against temperature to get the familiar rise-and-fall curve.

## Explanation checklist: temperature

**Rising to the optimum:**
- more kinetic energy;
- molecules move faster and collide more often;
- more effective collisions per second;
- more enzyme-substrate complexes, faster rate.

**Above the optimum:**
- bonds holding the enzyme's shape break;
- active site changes shape; substrate no longer fits;
- enzyme denatured (permanent);
- fewer effective collisions; rate falls towards zero.

**Low temperature:** low kinetic energy; few effective collisions; not denatured, so activity returns on warming.

**Explanation checklist: pH**

- Each enzyme has an optimum pH.
- Either side of it, the shape of the active site changes; the substrate fits less well.
- Fewer effective collisions and fewer enzyme-substrate complexes; slower rate.
- Extreme pH denatures the enzyme.
- Proteases in the stomach and the duodenum have different optimum pH values, matching the acidic stomach and the alkaline conditions in the duodenum.

## Exam traps

- "Killed" is wrong; enzymes are denatured.
- The enzyme, not the substrate, is denatured.
- "More energy" alone is not enough; mention collisions.
- Rate ≠ time: a long time means a slow rate.
- Do not claim an exact optimum from widely spaced readings.
- Control the other factor: pH when varying temperature, temperature when varying pH.
- Low temperature does not denature.

## Self-test

1. Give two ways of following the progress of an enzyme-catalysed reaction.
2. What colour does iodine solution turn with starch, and with no starch?
3. Name the independent and dependent variables when investigating the effect of temperature on amylase.
4. Why is a buffer solution used in a temperature investigation?
5. Why are enzyme and substrate warmed separately before mixing?
6. What is the purpose of a tube containing boiled enzyme?
7. Calculate the rate (1000 ÷ time) for a time of 80 s.
8. Explain why the rate increases from 20 °C to 35 °C.
9. Explain why the rate falls sharply above the optimum temperature.
10. Why does an enzyme cooled from 5 °C to room temperature work again, but one cooled from 70 °C does not?
11. Explain how a pH away from the optimum reduces enzyme activity.
12. Why might two proteases in the gut have different optimum pH values?

### Answers

1. Measuring how fast the substrate disappears (iodine test for starch) and how fast product appears (Benedict's test for reducing sugar, or volume of gas).
2. Blue-black with starch; stays orange-brown when no starch is present.
3. Independent: temperature. Dependent: time for starch to disappear (or rate).
4. To keep pH constant so that only temperature affects the rate.
5. So the reaction starts at the temperature being tested.
6. It is a control: it shows the change is caused by active enzyme.
7. 1000 ÷ 80 = 12.5.
8. Molecules gain kinetic energy and collide more often, so there are more effective collisions and more enzyme-substrate complexes form per second.
9. The enzyme is denatured: its active site changes shape so the substrate no longer fits, and fewer effective collisions occur.
10. At 5 °C the enzyme was only slowed by low kinetic energy; its shape was unchanged. At 70 °C it was denatured, which is permanent.
11. The shape of the active site changes, so the substrate fits less well; fewer effective collisions and complexes form.
12. They work in different parts of the gut with different pH: the stomach is acidic and the duodenum is alkaline.
