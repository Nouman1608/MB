Section 3.2.3 of the OxfordAQA International GCSE Chemistry (9202) specification, Version 6.2, has five statements. Carbon can form four covalent bonds (a). In diamond each carbon atom forms four covalent bonds in a giant covalent structure, so diamond is very hard (b). In graphite each carbon atom bonds to three others in layers that can slide over each other, because there are no covalent bonds between the layers, so graphite is soft and slippery; you should explain this "in terms of weak forces between the layers" (c). In graphite one electron from each atom is delocalised, so graphite conducts heat and electricity and "is similar to metals" in this respect (d). Carbon also forms fullerenes, used for drug delivery, in lubricants, as catalysts and in nanotubes for reinforcing materials; you need only know that their structure is "based on hexagonal rings of carbon atoms" (e). The qualification is untiered.

The existing [structure and bonding of carbon and nanoparticles guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-chemistry-structure-and-bonding-of-carbon-and-nanoparticles/) covers this section alongside 3.2.4. This guide concentrates on 3.2.3 alone and on the exam skill it tests most: explaining a property from a structure, step by step.

## Why carbon forms four bonds

Carbon has atomic number 6 and the electronic structure 2,4. It has four outer electrons, and it shares each of them in a covalent bond to reach the stable arrangement of eight outer electrons. Each covalent bond is a shared pair of electrons, and covalent bonds between atoms are **strong**. Every property in this section follows from how many of the four outer electrons are used in bonds, and how the bonded atoms are arranged.

## The three forms side by side

| | Diamond | Graphite | Fullerenes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bonds per carbon atom | 4 | 3 | 3 |
| Type of structure | giant covalent | giant covalent, in layers | molecules (cages or tubes) |
| Shape of rings | — | hexagonal rings in flat layers | based on hexagonal rings |
| Free (delocalised) electrons | none | one per atom | — (not required) |
| Hardness | very hard | soft, slippery | — (not required) |
| Electrical conductivity | does not conduct | conducts | — (not required) |
| Melting point | very high | very high | — (not required) |

The dashes mark properties that the specification does not ask you to know for fullerenes.

## Building an explanation: the three-link chain

Examiners reward explanations that link **structure → bonding → property**. Practise writing all three links every time.

**Diamond is very hard.**
1. Structure: each carbon atom is joined to four others in a giant three-dimensional lattice.
2. Bonding: all the bonds are strong covalent bonds.
3. Property: a very large amount of energy is needed to break the many bonds holding any atom in place, so the structure resists scratching and deformation.

**Graphite is soft and slippery.**
1. Structure: carbon atoms form flat layers of hexagonal rings, with each atom bonded to three others.
2. Bonding: there are no covalent bonds between the layers, only weak forces.
3. Property: the layers can slide over each other easily.

**Graphite conducts electricity.**
1. Structure: each atom forms only three bonds, using three of its four outer electrons.
2. Bonding: the fourth electron is delocalised, free to move along the layers.
3. Property: the delocalised electrons can move through the structure and carry charge. This is why graphite is described as similar to metals, which conduct for the same reason.

**Diamond does not conduct electricity.** All four outer electrons of each atom are used in covalent bonds. There are no delocalised electrons and no ions, so there are no charged particles free to move.

## A common trap: soft but high melting point

Graphite is soft, yet it has a very high melting point. Students often think these contradict each other. They do not, because they depend on different forces:

- **Softness** depends on the weak forces *between* layers, which are easily overcome when layers slide.
- **Melting** requires breaking the strong covalent bonds *within* the layers, because the atoms must move apart from each other, and this takes a great deal of energy.

So both diamond and graphite have very high melting points, because melting either one means breaking strong covalent bonds throughout a giant structure.

## Comparison with silicon dioxide

Section 3.2.2 names diamond, graphite and silicon dioxide (silica) as giant covalent structures. Silicon dioxide resembles diamond: every atom is held in a three-dimensional network by strong covalent bonds, so it is hard with a very high melting point, and it has no delocalised electrons, so it does not conduct. Graphite is the odd one out among the three because of its layers and its delocalised electrons.

## Fullerenes

Fullerenes are forms of carbon made of molecules with different numbers of carbon atoms. Their structures are based on **hexagonal rings of carbon atoms**, joined into hollow shapes such as balls or cylinders. The best-known has 60 carbon atoms (C₆₀). Cylindrical fullerenes are called **nanotubes**.

| Use named in the specification | Link to structure (where questions may provide details) |
|---|---|
| Drug delivery into the body | hollow cage can carry other molecules |
| Lubricants | small, rounded molecules can roll and slide between surfaces |
| Catalysts | very large surface for reacting particles to contact |
| Nanotubes for reinforcing materials, for example tennis rackets | long tubes held together by strong covalent bonds are strong for their mass |

You are not required to learn more detail about fullerene properties; questions may give information for you to apply.

## Worked example 1: matching uses to properties

*Diamond is used in the tips of drills for cutting rock, and graphite is used in pencil "leads". Explain each use. [4]*

**Model answer.** Diamond is very hard [1] because each carbon atom is held by four strong covalent bonds in a giant structure, so the tip is not worn away by the rock [1]. Graphite is soft [1] because its layers, held only by weak forces, slide off onto the paper, leaving a mark [1].

## Worked example 2: an annotated "compare" answer

*Compare the structures and properties of diamond and graphite. [6]*

**Model answer.**

- Both are giant covalent structures of carbon atoms joined by strong covalent bonds, so both have very high melting points. *(similarity with reason)*
- In diamond each atom forms four bonds in a three-dimensional lattice; in graphite each atom forms three bonds in flat layers of hexagonal rings. *(structural difference)*
- Diamond is very hard because of its rigid network; graphite is soft and slippery because there are only weak forces between the layers, so they slide. *(property difference with reason)*
- Graphite conducts electricity because one electron per atom is delocalised; diamond does not conduct because all its outer electrons are in bonds. *(property difference with reason)*

*Why it scores:* every statement about one form is matched by a statement about the other. Writing two separate descriptions without comparison usually limits the marks.

## Common mistakes

- **"Graphite has weak covalent bonds between the layers."** There are no covalent bonds between layers; there are weak forces.
- **"Graphite conducts because electrons move between the layers."** The delocalised electrons move through the structure; do not invent a direction.
- **"Graphite has a low melting point because it is soft."**
- **"Diamond conducts because it is a giant structure."** It has no delocalised electrons.
- **Calling fullerenes giant structures.** They are molecules.
- **Calling intermolecular forces "bonds" when explaining softness.**

## Exam technique

- **Explain**: use the three-link chain: structure, bonding, property.
- **Compare**: give similarities and differences, pairing diamond with graphite in each sentence.
- **Suggest** a use from information: identify the property the use needs, then the structural reason.
