These questions cover section 1.3 (opportunity cost) of the Cambridge IGCSE Economics 0455 syllabus for examination in 2026. All people, firms and figures are invented. Revise first with the [opportunity cost study guide](/resources/cambridge-igcse-economics-0455-opportunity-cost-cambridge-igcse-economics/) and the [opportunity cost revision notes](/resources/cambridge-igcse-economics-0455-opportunity-cost-cambridge-igcse-economics-revision-notes/).

## Section A: Short-answer questions

**1.** Which of the following best describes opportunity cost? **[1]**

A: The money price paid for a product

B: The total of all the alternatives given up

C: The next best alternative forgone

D: The cost of producing one more unit

**2.** Karim has a free afternoon. He ranks his options: play football (first), work in his uncle's shop for 30 (second), visit a museum (third). He plays football. What is his opportunity cost? **[1]**

A: Nothing, because football is free

B: The 30 he could have earned

C: The museum visit

D: The shop work and the museum visit together

**3.** Explain why a free good has no opportunity cost. **[2]**

**4.** A factory's production line can make either 800 bottles or 200 jars in a shift.

**(a)** Calculate the opportunity cost of one jar. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the opportunity cost of one bottle. **[1]**

**5.** Lena has savings of 3,000 in a bank paying 2.5% interest a year. She uses all of it to buy a motorbike, and her next best alternative was to keep the money saved. Calculate the interest she forgoes in one year. **[2]**

**6.** Explain why the opportunity cost of a fee-free public library is not zero. **[2]**

## Section B: Structured questions

**7.** A carpenter can work 40 hours a week. Each hour she can make a stool earning 6 profit, or spend the hour on repairs earning 9.

**(a)** Calculate her weekly profit if she spends every hour on stools, and if she spends every hour on repairs. **[2]**

**(b)** State the opportunity cost of spending the whole week making stools. **[1]**

**(c)** Explain one reason why she might still make some stools. **[2]**

**8.** Explain how opportunity cost influences a worker's decision to leave a job for two years of full-time study. **[4]**

**9.** A government has 90 million to spend. Option X is a new airport terminal costing 54 million. Option Y is a set of rural roads costing 9 million each.

**(a)** Calculate how many rural roads the terminal's cost could fund. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain what is meant by the opportunity cost of the terminal in this case. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain one benefit of choosing the rural roads instead. **[2]**

**10.** A firm has already spent 20,000 on research for a new product. Launching it will cost a further 50,000 and is expected to earn 45,000. The firm's manager argues that it must launch, "or the 20,000 will have been wasted". Explain whether the manager is right. **[4]**

**11.** Explain how the opportunity cost of saving might influence a consumer's spending when interest rates rise. **[4]**

**12.** Discuss whether governments should use opportunity cost as the main basis for their spending decisions. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** **C** [1]. A is the money price, B adds all alternatives and D is a cost of production, not a forgone choice. **[1]**

**2.** **B** [1]. The shop work is ranked second, so it is the next best alternative; the museum is third. **[1]**

**3.** A free good is not scarce [1], so using more of it does not reduce the amount available for any other use, and nothing is given up [1]. **[2]**

**4. (a)** 800 ÷ 200 = **4 bottles per jar** [1].

**(b)** 200 ÷ 800 = **0.25 (a quarter of a) jar per bottle** [1]. **[2]**

**5.** Interest forgone = 3,000 × 0.025 [1] = **75** [1]. **[2]**

**6.** The books, staff and building use scarce resources [1], which could have produced other goods or services, such as a sports centre, so society gives up an alternative even though users pay nothing [1]. **[2]**

**7. (a)** Stools: 40 × 6 = **240** [1]; repairs: 40 × 9 = **360** [1].

**(b)** The 360 profit from repairs she gives up [1].

**(c)** Any one developed reason: repair work may not be available for all 40 hours [1], so stools fill the time that would otherwise earn nothing; or regular stool customers may bring future orders [1]. **[5]**

**8.** The opportunity cost of study is the earnings forgone for two years [1], plus any fees, as these could have been spent on other things [1]. The worker compares this with the expected benefit, such as higher pay after qualifying [1]. If the expected benefit exceeds the earnings forgone, the worker is more likely to study; a higher current wage makes study less likely because the cost rises [1]. **[4]**

**9. (a)** 54 ÷ 9 = **6 rural roads** [1].

**(b)** The opportunity cost is the next best use of the 54 million [1], which here is the 6 rural roads that cannot be built if the terminal is chosen [1].

**(c)** Rural roads may help farmers reach markets at lower transport cost [1], raising their incomes and reducing poverty in regions the terminal would not serve [1]. **[5]**

**10.** The manager is wrong [1]. The 20,000 is a sunk cost: it has been spent whatever the firm decides now, so it is not an opportunity cost of the launch [1]. The relevant comparison is the further cost of 50,000 against expected revenue of 45,000, a loss of 5,000 [1]. Unless other benefits are expected, the firm should use the 50,000 in its next best alternative instead [1]. **[4]**

**11.** The opportunity cost of spending includes the interest that the money would have earned if saved [1]. When interest rates rise, this interest forgone increases [1], so spending now becomes more costly in terms of future income given up [1]. Some consumers will therefore save more and spend less, especially on expensive items [1]. **[4]**

**12.** For: budgets are limited, so every project means another is forgone [1]; comparing a project with its next best alternative helps to direct funds to the use with the greatest benefit to society [1]; it makes the cost of a decision, such as 6 roads given up for a terminal, visible to voters [1]. Against: many benefits, such as health or a cleaner environment, have no price and are hard to compare [1]; benefits arrive in the future and are uncertain, so estimates may be wrong [1]; governments also have aims such as fairness or helping a poor region that a simple comparison may miss [1]; political pressure can lead to different choices [1]. Conclusion: opportunity cost should be an essential part of each decision, but it works best alongside judgements about fairness and long-term effects, especially where benefits cannot be measured in money [1]. **[8]**

*Examiner insight:* A "Discuss" answer earns its final marks for a conclusion that names what the judgement depends on, here how measurable the benefits are.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Choosing the most valuable or the most expensive option rather than the one ranked second.
- Giving a calculation answer without its unit.
- Treating a sunk cost as a reason to continue.
- Explaining what opportunity cost is but not how it changes the decision in the question.
- Giving one side only in a "Discuss" question.
