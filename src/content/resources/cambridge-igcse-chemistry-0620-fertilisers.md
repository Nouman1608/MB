This guide goes deeper into subtopic **10.2 Fertilisers** of the Cambridge IGCSE Chemistry 0620 syllabus for 2026–2028. The broader [Chemistry of the Environment](/resources/chemistry-of-the-environment/) guide covers fertilisers in a short paragraph. Questions on fertilisers, however, usually draw on other parts of the syllabus too: formulae, salt preparation, solubility, the reaction of ammonium salts with bases, and ion tests. This page shows how those links work in fertiliser questions. For quick recall use the [fertilisers revision notes](/resources/cambridge-igcse-chemistry-0620-fertilisers-revision-notes/), and practise with the [fertilisers practice questions](/resources/cambridge-igcse-chemistry-0620-fertilisers-practice/).

## What 10.2 asks you to do

Both outcomes in 10.2 are **Core**:

1. State that **ammonium salts** and **nitrates** are used as fertilisers.
2. Describe the use of **NPK fertilisers** to provide the elements **nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium** for improved plant growth.

There is no Supplement content in 10.2. One section below, on percentage composition, uses a Supplement calculation from subtopic 3.3 and is marked *(Extended)*.

## Why fertilisers are needed

Plants take the elements they need for growth from the soil, dissolved in water through their roots. Three elements are needed in relatively large amounts: **nitrogen (N)**, **phosphorus (P)** and **potassium (K)**. When crops are harvested, the elements they absorbed are carried away with them and the soil gradually runs short. A fertiliser puts these elements back, so the next crop grows better.

Notice the wording: an NPK fertiliser provides the **elements** N, P and K, but not as the pure elements. They are supplied in **compounds**, usually salts.

## Ammonium salts and nitrates

The two families named in the syllabus both supply **nitrogen**:

- **Ammonium salts** contain the ammonium ion, NH₄⁺.
- **Nitrates** contain the nitrate ion, NO₃⁻.

Some fertiliser salts supply a second element as well. You can tell which elements a salt provides by reading its formula.

| Fertiliser salt | Formula | Elements it provides (N, P, K) |
|---|---|---|
| Ammonium nitrate | NH₄NO₃ | N (from both ions) |
| Ammonium sulfate | (NH₄)₂SO₄ | N |
| Ammonium phosphate | (NH₄)₃PO₄ | N and P |
| Potassium nitrate | KNO₃ | K and N |
| Potassium chloride | KCl | K |

**Method:** look for N (in NH₄⁺ or NO₃⁻), P (in PO₄³⁻) and K (as K⁺). Ignore elements such as S, Cl, O and H, which are not the NPK elements.

### Why fertilisers must be soluble

A plant can only take in substances dissolved in soil water. The solubility rules from subtopic 7.3 explain why these salts work: **all ammonium salts, all potassium salts and all nitrates are soluble**. An insoluble compound containing nitrogen would be of little use to the plant.

## NPK fertilisers

An **NPK fertiliser** is a mixture of salts that provides all three elements in one product. A farmer can choose a mixture to match the needs of the soil and the crop.

**Worked example.** A farmer mixes ammonium phosphate with potassium chloride. Explain why the mixture can be described as an NPK fertiliser. [2]

> Ammonium phosphate provides nitrogen and phosphorus [1]; potassium chloride provides potassium, so all three elements are present [1].

## Making a fertiliser salt

Fertiliser salts are made by neutralisation. Ammonium sulfate is made from aqueous ammonia, an alkali, and sulfuric acid:

```
ammonia + sulfuric acid → ammonium sulfate
2NH3 + H2SO4 → (NH4)2SO4
```

Both reactants are soluble, so in the laboratory this is a **titration** preparation (subtopic 7.3): find the exact volumes that neutralise each other using an indicator, repeat without the indicator, then crystallise the salt from the solution. Ammonium nitrate is made in the same way from ammonia and nitric acid; potassium nitrate from potassium hydroxide and nitric acid.

## Do not mix ammonium fertilisers with bases

Farmers sometimes add a base, such as calcium hydroxide (slaked lime), to soil that is too acidic. It should not be added at the same time as an ammonium fertiliser. Subtopic 7.1 states that bases react with ammonium salts: the reaction releases **ammonia gas**, so nitrogen is lost to the air instead of staying in the soil.

```
ammonium sulfate + calcium hydroxide → calcium sulfate + ammonia + water
(NH4)2SO4 + Ca(OH)2 → CaSO4 + 2NH3 + 2H2O
```

This is a favourite application question: it joins 10.2 to the chemistry of bases.

## Identifying fertiliser ions

The ion tests in Topic 12 can show what a fertiliser contains:

- **Ammonium ions:** warm with aqueous sodium hydroxide; ammonia is given off, which turns **damp red litmus paper blue**.
- **Nitrate ions:** warm with aqueous sodium hydroxide and **aluminium foil**; ammonia is given off, which turns damp red litmus blue.
- **Sulfate ions:** acidify with dilute nitric acid, then add aqueous barium nitrate; a **white precipitate** forms.

For ammonium nitrate, both the ammonium and the nitrate tests are positive: the first test (no aluminium) gives ammonia from the ammonium ions alone.

## Percentage of nitrogen by mass *(Extended)*

Calculating percentage composition by mass is Supplement content (subtopic 3.3), but it is often set in a fertiliser context. (Ar: H = 1, N = 14, O = 16, S = 32)

**Question.** Which provides a greater percentage of nitrogen by mass, ammonium nitrate or ammonium sulfate? [4]

```
NH4NO3:   Mr = 14 + 4 + 14 + 48 = 80
          % N = (2 × 14) ÷ 80 × 100 = 35.0 %              [2]

(NH4)2SO4: Mr = (2 × 18) + 32 + 64 = 132
          % N = (2 × 14) ÷ 132 × 100 = 21.2 %             [1]

Ammonium nitrate provides the greater percentage of N.    [1]
```

*The common slip is counting only one nitrogen atom in ammonium nitrate. There is one in NH₄⁺ and one in NO₃⁻.*

## The other side of fertilisers

Fertiliser that is not taken up by plants can be washed into rivers and lakes. Subtopic 10.1 lists nitrates and phosphates from fertilisers among the potentially harmful substances in water, because they lead to deoxygenation of water and damage to aquatic life. A fertiliser question may ask you to connect the two subtopics.

## Common mistakes

- **Saying fertilisers provide "nitrogen gas" or "pure phosphorus".** They provide the elements in compounds, as ions in salts.
- **Thinking nitrates are the only nitrogen fertilisers.** Ammonium salts supply nitrogen too.
- **Missing a second element.** Potassium nitrate supplies both K and N; ammonium phosphate supplies both N and P.
- **Counting only one N in NH₄NO₃.**
- **Forgetting the brackets.** Ammonium sulfate is (NH₄)₂SO₄, not NH₄SO₄.
- **Suggesting lime and an ammonium fertiliser be added together.** Ammonia would be lost.

## Exam technique for 10.2 command words

- **State:** "ammonium salts and nitrates"; "nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium".
- **Describe** an NPK fertiliser: a fertiliser containing compounds that provide N, P and K, used to improve plant growth.
- **Identify:** read the formula, then name each NPK element present.
- **Explain:** link the chemistry, such as solubility or the reaction with bases, to the farmer's purpose.
- **Calculate** *(Extended)*: show Mr, count every nitrogen atom, then give the percentage to three significant figures.
