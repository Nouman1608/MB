This guide goes deeper into section **4.4.4 Nuclear fission and fusion** of the AQA GCSE Physics (8463)
specification. It is a **physics only** section, so it is in GCSE Physics but not Combined Science. It is
assessed on Paper 1 at Foundation and Higher Tier, and nothing in it is marked (HT only). It has two parts:
nuclear fission (4.4.4.1) and nuclear fusion (4.4.4.2).

| Spec point | What you must be able to do |
|---|---|
| 4.4.4.1 Nuclear fission | Describe fission of a large, unstable nucleus such as uranium or plutonium; explain the role of neutron absorption; describe the products; explain chain reactions, controlled in a reactor and uncontrolled in a weapon; draw and interpret diagrams of fission and chain reactions |
| 4.4.4.2 Nuclear fusion | Describe fusion as the joining of two light nuclei to form a heavier nucleus, with some mass converted into the energy of radiation |

## 4.4.4.1 Nuclear fission

**Nuclear fission** is the splitting of a large and unstable nucleus, for example a nucleus of uranium or
plutonium.

### The four steps

1. **Absorption.** Spontaneous fission is rare. Usually the unstable nucleus must first **absorb a
   neutron**. The nucleus that forms is even less stable.
2. **Splitting.** The nucleus splits into **two smaller nuclei, roughly equal in size**.
3. **Emission.** It emits **two or three neutrons** and **gamma rays**.
4. **Energy.** Energy is released. **All of the fission products have kinetic energy**: the two smaller
   nuclei and the neutrons fly apart at high speed.

In a reactor, the kinetic energy of the fission products is transferred to the thermal energy store of the
surrounding material as they slow down. Describing how a power station then generates electricity is not
part of this section.

### Reading a fission equation

Fission equations balance in the same way as alpha and beta decay equations: the mass numbers (top) must
balance, and so must the atomic numbers (bottom). A neutron is written ¹₀n.

```
²³⁵₉₂U + ¹₀n → ¹⁴¹₅₆Ba + ⁹²₃₆Kr + 3 ¹₀n
mass numbers:   235 + 1 = 236      141 + 92 + 3 = 236
atomic numbers: 92 + 0 = 92        56 + 36 + 0 = 92
```

**Worked example 1.** Uranium-235 absorbs a neutron and splits into xenon-140 (atomic number 54) and
strontium-94 (atomic number 38). How many neutrons are released?

```
²³⁵₉₂U + ¹₀n → ¹⁴⁰₅₄Xe + ⁹⁴₃₈Sr + x ¹₀n
mass numbers: 235 + 1 = 140 + 94 + x
              236 = 234 + x   →   x = 2 neutrons
check atomic numbers: 92 = 54 + 38 ✓
```

The two product nuclei (140 and 94) are of roughly equal size, as the specification describes, and two
neutrons fits "two or three neutrons". Remember the absorbed neutron on the left: leaving it out makes the
count wrong by one.

### Chain reactions

The neutrons released by one fission may be absorbed by other uranium or plutonium nuclei, which then split
and release more neutrons. This is a **chain reaction**.

Think of the reaction in **generations**. If each fission causes two more:

```
generation:  1   2   3   4    5    6
fissions:    1   2   4   8   16   32      (doubles each generation)
```

If each fission causes three more, the numbers go 1, 3, 9, 27, 81. Either way, the number of fissions, and
so the energy released each second, grows extremely quickly.

| | Controlled chain reaction | Uncontrolled chain reaction |
|---|---|---|
| Where | Nuclear reactor | Nuclear weapon |
| Fissions per generation | Kept steady: on average, one neutron from each fission goes on to cause another fission | Grows rapidly: more than one neutron from each fission causes another fission |
| Energy release | At a steady, controlled rate | Huge amount in a very short time: an explosion |

The specification's point is the purpose: the chain reaction is controlled in a reactor **to control the
energy released**. In a reactor the extra neutrons are absorbed so that they do not cause further fissions.

**Worked example 2.** In a reactor, each fission releases 3 neutrons. On average, how many of them must be
absorbed without causing fission for the reaction to stay steady?

```
steady means 1 neutron per fission goes on to cause another fission
neutrons absorbed or lost = 3 − 1 = 2 per fission
```

If only one were removed, two would cause fission each time, and the number of fissions would double every
generation.

### Drawing and interpreting fission diagrams

You must be able to draw and interpret diagrams of fission and of chain reactions. A clear fission diagram
shows, from left to right:

- a neutron (small circle, labelled n) moving towards a large nucleus labelled, for example, uranium-235;
- two smaller nuclei of similar size moving apart;
- two or three neutrons moving away from the split, each with an arrow;
- a wavy arrow labelled gamma rays.

A chain reaction diagram repeats the pattern: each released neutron arrow ends at a new large nucleus.
When interpreting one, count three things: the neutrons absorbed, the fissions in each generation, and the
neutrons that escape without causing fission. An arrow that leads off the diagram, or to a nucleus that does
not split, is a neutron that does not continue the chain.

## 4.4.4.2 Nuclear fusion

**Nuclear fusion** is the joining of two **light** nuclei to form a **heavier** nucleus. In this process
**some of the mass may be converted into the energy of radiation**, so the products have slightly less mass
than the nuclei that joined.

One example is two isotopes of hydrogen joining to form helium:

```
²₁H + ³₁H → ⁴₂He + ¹₀n
mass numbers:   2 + 3 = 5      4 + 1 = 5
atomic numbers: 1 + 1 = 2      2 + 0 = 2
```

Fusion is the energy source of stars: a main sequence star fuses hydrogen nuclei into helium. You meet this
again in the [Space physics study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-physics-8463-space-physics/).

## Fission and fusion compared

| | Fission | Fusion |
|---|---|---|
| What happens | A large nucleus splits | Two light nuclei join |
| Nuclei involved | Heavy, such as uranium or plutonium | Light, such as hydrogen |
| Usual trigger | The nucleus absorbs a neutron | The two nuclei join directly; no neutron is absorbed first |
| Products | Two smaller nuclei, two or three neutrons, gamma rays | One heavier nucleus (sometimes with a neutron) |
| Energy | Released; products have kinetic energy | Released; some mass converted into the energy of radiation |
| Chain reaction | Yes, through the released neutrons | Not a chain reaction in this sense |

### Annotated model answer

**Question (4 marks).** Compare nuclear fission and nuclear fusion.

> In fission a large nucleus splits into smaller nuclei, **whereas** in fusion two light nuclei join to
> form a heavier one **[process, both sides]**. Fission happens to heavy nuclei such as uranium, **but**
> fusion involves light nuclei such as hydrogen **[nuclei involved]**. Fission usually needs a neutron to be
> absorbed first, **while** fusion does not **[trigger]**. **Both** release energy **[similarity]**.

Each sentence covers both processes with a comparison word. Two separate descriptions, one of each process,
rarely earn full marks.

## Common mistakes

- Starting a fission description at "the nucleus splits". The first step is **absorbing a neutron**.
- Writing that fission produces "two atoms". It produces two smaller **nuclei**.
- Saying fission releases "one neutron". It releases **two or three**.
- Forgetting the gamma rays or the kinetic energy of the products.
- Saying a reactor's chain reaction is "stopped". It is **controlled**, so energy is released at a steady
  rate.
- Describing fusion as splitting, or as joining heavy nuclei.
- Leaving the absorbed neutron out when balancing a fission equation.

## Exam technique

- **Describe** the fission process: give the steps in order, using the specification's words.
- **Explain** a chain reaction: say what the released neutrons do and why the number of fissions grows.
- **Compare**: one point per sentence, both processes in each sentence.
- **Draw**: label every particle and use arrows for motion; include the gamma rays.
- **Calculate** from an equation: balance the top numbers first to find missing neutrons, then check the
  bottom numbers.
