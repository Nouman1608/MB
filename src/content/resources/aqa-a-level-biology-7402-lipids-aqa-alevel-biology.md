This guide goes deeper into **3.1.3 Lipids** from the AQA AS and A-level Biology specification (7401/7402), first teaching 2015, examined at AS and A-level. The specification names two groups of lipid, triglycerides and phospholipids, and asks you to recognise saturated and unsaturated fatty acids from diagrams, to explain the different properties of the two groups, and to use the emulsion test.

The [lipids and protein structure study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-biology-lipids-and-protein-structure/) already builds one triglyceride from its formula, lists the properties of each lipid group and describes the emulsion test. This page goes further: a formula rule for counting double bonds, triglycerides made of different fatty acids, how phospholipids arrange themselves in different settings, how to compare the two groups in a single answer, and how to run and interpret the emulsion test with controls. Condense it with the [3.1.3 revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-biology-7402-lipids-aqa-alevel-biology-revision-notes/) and practise with the [3.1.3 practice questions](/resources/aqa-a-level-biology-7402-lipids-aqa-alevel-biology-practice/).

## What the specification says

- Triglycerides and phospholipids are two groups of lipid.
- Triglycerides are formed by the condensation of one molecule of glycerol and three molecules of fatty acid.
- A condensation reaction between glycerol and a fatty acid (RCOOH) forms an ester bond.
- The R-group of a fatty acid may be saturated or unsaturated.
- In phospholipids, one of the fatty acids of a triglyceride is substituted by a phosphate-containing group.
- The different properties of triglycerides and phospholipids related to their different structures.
- The emulsion test for lipids.

## Recognising saturated and unsaturated fatty acids

### From a diagram

Look along the hydrocarbon chain (the R group).

- Every carbon joined to the next by a **single** bond, with two hydrogens on each carbon in the chain: **saturated**.
- One or more **C=C double bonds**, where each carbon in the double bond carries only one hydrogen: **unsaturated**. One double bond is monounsaturated; two or more is polyunsaturated.

The clue in a displayed formula is a pair of neighbouring carbons with one hydrogen each and a double line between them. In a skeletal diagram, it is a double line in the zig-zag.

### From a molecular formula

A saturated fatty acid written as RCOOH has the R group CₙH₂ₙ₊₁. Each C=C removes two hydrogen atoms. So:

Number of double bonds = (2n + 1 − number of H in the R group) ÷ 2

### Worked example 1: three fatty acids

| Fatty acid | R group | Saturated R would be | Missing H | Double bonds |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A: C₁₅H₃₁COOH | C₁₅H₃₁ | C₁₅H₃₁ | 0 | 0, saturated |
| B: C₁₇H₃₁COOH | C₁₇H₃₁ | C₁₇H₃₅ | 4 | 2, polyunsaturated |
| C: C₁₉H₂₉COOH | C₁₉H₂₉ | C₁₉H₃₉ | 10 | 5, polyunsaturated |

Do not decide saturation from the length of the chain. A short chain can be unsaturated and a long one saturated.

## Why double bonds change properties

A C=C double bond usually puts a bend (kink) in the chain. Kinked chains cannot pack closely, so the forces between neighbouring molecules are weaker and less energy is needed to separate them. Triglycerides rich in unsaturated fatty acids therefore have lower melting points and are usually liquid (oils) at room temperature.

The illustrative data below are invented to show the pattern, not measured values for named compounds.

| Fatty acid (same chain length) | Double bonds | Illustrative melting point (°C) |
|---|---|---|
| P | 0 | 70 |
| Q | 1 | 15 |
| R | 2 | −5 |

The trend is the point: more double bonds, more kinks, lower melting point. In a data question, describe the trend, then explain it through packing.

## Mixed triglycerides

The three fatty acids on one glycerol need not be the same. Natural fats and oils are mixtures of many triglycerides, each with its own combination. A fat is called "saturated" or "unsaturated" according to the proportion of each type of fatty acid it contains.

### Worked example 2: mass of a mixed triglyceride

A triglyceride contains one each of fatty acids with Mr 256, 282 and 280. Glycerol has Mr 92 and water 18.

1. Three ester bonds form, so three water molecules are released.
2. Mr = 92 + 256 + 282 + 280 − (3 × 18) = 910 − 54 = **856**.
3. Hydrolysis uses three water molecules and gives back glycerol and the three fatty acids, total mass 910.

A phospholipid has only **two** fatty acids joined to glycerol by ester bonds; the third position carries the phosphate-containing group.

## How phospholipids arrange themselves

A phospholipid has a hydrophilic phosphate head and two hydrophobic fatty acid tails. Where it sits depends on its surroundings.

| Setting | Arrangement | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Surface of water, with air above | A single layer: heads in the water, tails pointing into the air | Tails avoid water |
| Small numbers in water | A tiny sphere (micelle): heads outward, tails inside | Tails hide from water on all sides |
| Water on both sides, as in a cell | A bilayer: two layers, heads facing the water on each side, tails inside | Both surfaces face water |

The bilayer is the basis of cell-surface membranes and organelle membranes. Its hydrophobic core is a barrier to water-soluble molecules and ions, which is why cells need proteins to transport them (3.2.3).

## Comparing the two groups

The specification asks you to explain the different properties of triglycerides and phospholipids. A comparison answer should link each property to a structural difference.

| Structural feature | Triglyceride | Phospholipid | Property it explains |
|---|---|---|---|
| Polar phosphate head | Absent | Present | Phospholipids interact with water at one end; triglycerides do not |
| Hydrophobic tails | Three | Two | Both are insoluble in water; phospholipids still form layers |
| Many C–H bonds in tails | More (three chains) | Fewer (two chains) | Triglycerides are the better energy store |
| Overall polarity | Non-polar | Amphipathic (one end polar) | Triglycerides form droplets in cells; phospholipids form bilayers |

Triglyceride functions follow from being non-polar and rich in C–H bonds: an energy store with no osmotic effect, a source of metabolic water when oxidised, thermal insulation and protection of organs. Phospholipid functions follow from being amphipathic: forming membranes that separate watery compartments.

## The emulsion test, with controls

1. Add the sample to a clean, dry test tube with about 2 cm³ of ethanol and shake well. Lipids dissolve in ethanol.
2. Pour the ethanol into a tube of water (or add water) and shake.
3. A milky white emulsion is a positive result: the lipid comes out of solution as tiny droplets that scatter light. A clear liquid is negative.

Run a **negative control** with ethanol and water only, to show that cloudiness is not caused by the reagents or by a dirty tube. Run a **positive control** with a known oil to confirm the method works. Ethanol is flammable, so keep it away from flames. The test shows that lipid is present, not how much; it is qualitative.

If the sample is put into water first, a lipid does not dissolve and simply floats, so the test fails. The order of the steps is part of the method.

## Exam technique

- **Recognise:** state "C=C present, so unsaturated" with the evidence from the diagram.
- **Explain properties:** link structure to property to function.
- **Compare:** write in matched pairs: "triglycerides have three fatty acids, whereas phospholipids have two and a phosphate group".
- **Describe the emulsion test:** ethanol first, then water, and the result "milky white emulsion".

## Common mistakes

- Calling lipids polymers. They are not made of repeating monomers.
- Saying unsaturated fatty acids have "fewer carbons". They have fewer hydrogens.
- Writing that phospholipids have a hydrophobic head and hydrophilic tails.
- Describing the emulsion as "white precipitate" or "cloudy" with no colour.
- Forgetting that a phospholipid has two ester bonds to fatty acids, not three.

## Frequently asked questions

**How do you tell a saturated fatty acid from an unsaturated one?**
Look for C=C double bonds in the hydrocarbon chain. None means saturated; one or more means unsaturated.

**Why are oils liquid at room temperature?**
They are rich in unsaturated fatty acids, whose double bonds kink the chains so they cannot pack closely, lowering the melting point.

**What is the difference between a triglyceride and a phospholipid?**
A triglyceride has glycerol and three fatty acids. In a phospholipid one fatty acid is replaced by a phosphate-containing group, giving a hydrophilic head.

**What is a positive result in the emulsion test?**
A milky white emulsion forms when the ethanol solution is mixed with water.
