This guide goes deeper into **A4.2 Conservation of biodiversity**, the last sub-topic of Theme A (Unity and diversity) in the IB DP *Biology guide*, first assessment 2025. A4.2 asks what biodiversity is, how humans are reducing it, how we know, and how we can choose what to conserve. Its statements, A4.2.1 to A4.2.8, apply to both SL and HL.

The [evolution, speciation and conservation study guide](/resources/ib-dp-biology-evolution-speciation-and-conservation-of-biodiversity/) introduces each statement and its case studies. This page goes further on the reasoning: how to build a case-study answer, how to judge the quality of evidence for a crisis, and how to argue for one conservation approach over another. Condense it with the [A4.2 revision notes](/resources/ib-ib-dp-biology-dp-biology-ib-dp-biology-1-a4-2-revision-notes/) and test yourself with the [A4.2 practice questions](/resources/ib-ib-dp-biology-dp-biology-ib-dp-biology-1-a4-2-practice/).

## A4.2.1 Three levels, three kinds of loss

| Level | What it measures | What its loss looks like |
|---|---|---|
| Ecosystem diversity | Variety of habitats and communities in an area | A wetland drained and replaced by farmland |
| Species diversity | Number of species and their relative abundance | A species declining until one or two others dominate |
| Genetic diversity | Variety of alleles within a species | A population shrinking so that alleles are lost |

The levels are linked. Losing an ecosystem removes the species that depend on it, and a species reduced to a few individuals keeps only a fraction of its former alleles, which limits its ability to adapt.

## A4.2.2 Today compared with the past

The fossil record shows that biodiversity has risen over geological time but has been interrupted by **mass extinctions**, in which a large proportion of species disappeared in a geologically short period. Five are usually recognised, the most recent at the end of the Cretaceous. Past mass extinctions had natural causes, such as major volcanic activity or an asteroid impact. The concern today is that human activity is driving extinction rates far above the background rate.

**Nature of science.** Counting species depends on classification, and the same organisms can be grouped differently. Biologists who split groups finely recognise more species than those who lump them together, so species counts carry uncertainty.

## A4.2.3 Building a case-study answer on extinction

The guide asks for case studies of species driven extinct by humans. A strong answer has four parts: the species and where it lived, what made it vulnerable, the human cause, and the outcome.

| Species | Vulnerability | Human cause | Outcome |
|---|---|---|---|
| North Island giant moa (New Zealand) | Large, flightless, slow-breeding; no history of human predators | Hunting by the first human settlers; habitat change | Extinct within about two centuries of human arrival |
| Caribbean monk seal | Bred on beaches and reefs within easy reach | Killed for oil and meat; competition from overfishing | Last confirmed sighting in 1952; declared extinct in 2008 |

The guide also asks for a further case study, which can be a plant or a species from your own region. Use the same four headings.

### Worked example 1: annotated model answer

*Question (4 marks): Explain how human activity caused the extinction of the North Island giant moa.*

> The moa was a large flightless bird that had evolved without mammal predators, so it had no defensive behaviour against humans **[1]**. When people settled New Zealand, they hunted moa for food **[1]**. Moa bred slowly, so the population could not replace adults as fast as they were killed **[1]**. Forest clearance by burning also reduced its habitat, and the species was extinct within about two centuries **[1]**.

## A4.2.4 Ecosystem loss

Ecosystems are lost when the land or water is converted to another use. The guide's case study is the **mixed dipterocarp forest** of Southeast Asia, a tropical forest dominated by tall trees of the family Dipterocarpaceae. It has been lost to logging for timber, clearance for plantations such as oil palm, and fire. You also need a second case study of ecosystem loss, ideally from your own region.

## A4.2.5 Judging the evidence for a crisis

Evidence comes from repeated, reliable surveys of many habitats, and from international assessments that combine them. The IPBES global assessment of 2019 concluded that around one million species face extinction.

When evaluating evidence, ask:

- **Is the method the same each time?** A change in effort or area can create a false trend.
- **Is the sample large and widespread?** One site may not represent a region.
- **Who collected it?** Citizen science gives huge coverage but identification skill varies; professional surveys are fewer but more consistent.
- **Is it published and peer-reviewed?** This lets others check methods.

### Worked example 2: is the decline real?

Two invented surveys of a heathland recorded butterfly species.

| Survey | Year | Area searched / ha | Hours of searching | Species recorded |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 2004 | 40 | 60 | 31 |
| 2 | 2024 | 25 | 30 | 22 |

1. Raw change: (22 − 31) ÷ 31 × 100 = **−29%**.
2. But survey 2 covered 62.5% of the area for half the time, so rare species were less likely to be found.
3. Conclusion: the data suggest a decline but cannot show its size. A valid comparison needs the same area and effort.

## A4.2.6 Causes of the crisis

The overarching cause is **human population growth**, which drives the specific causes:

- overexploitation (hunting, fishing, logging);
- urbanisation and land clearance for agriculture, including monocultures;
- pollution;
- invasive alien species, pests and diseases spread by global travel and trade.

When asked to explain a cause, link it to the level it affects: drainage removes an ecosystem; overfishing reduces a species; small remnant populations lose genetic diversity.

## A4.2.7 Choosing a conservation approach

No single approach works for every case, which is why the guide stresses the need for several.

| Approach | Examples | Best when | Limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| In situ | Nature reserves, rewilding, restoring degraded habitat | The habitat can be protected and the species can live wild | Needs large areas and enforcement |
| Ex situ | Zoos, botanic gardens, captive breeding | Wild population is tiny or the habitat is gone | Small captive populations lose genetic diversity; animals may not survive release |
| Storage of germ plasm | Seed banks, frozen sperm, eggs or tissue | Insurance against extinction; plants with long-lived seeds | Does not protect the ecosystem or behaviour |

### Worked example 3: arguing for a mix

*A frog species has fewer than 200 individuals in one stream, where an introduced fungal disease is spreading.*

1. In situ alone is risky: the disease could kill the remaining frogs.
2. Ex situ captive breeding, in disease-free conditions, provides a back-up population.
3. In situ work is still needed so there is somewhere to release frogs: controlling the disease and protecting the stream.
4. Conclusion: combine both, which is the point of A4.2.7.

## A4.2.8 Prioritising with EDGE

The **EDGE of Existence** programme ranks species by two measures: how **evolutionarily distinct** they are (how few close relatives they have, so how much unique evolutionary history they carry) and how **globally endangered** they are. A species scoring highly on both is a priority, because losing it would remove a long branch of the tree of life.

Prioritisation is not purely scientific. Choosing species also involves cost, the chance of success, cultural value and the needs of local people, so it is open to debate.

## Common mistakes

- Defining biodiversity as "the number of species" only.
- Giving a case study with no cause or outcome.
- Treating a raw survey change as reliable without checking method.
- Recommending one approach for all species.
- Describing EDGE as protecting the most endangered species only.

## Exam technique for IB command terms

- **Outline**: give the case study's cause and outcome briefly.
- **Explain**: vulnerability, cause and consequence, as in worked example 1.
- **Evaluate**: comment on method, sample and source of evidence.
- **Discuss**: compare in situ and ex situ for a named case and reach a conclusion.
- **Justify**: give the reason a species should be prioritised, using both EDGE measures.
