This guide covers **3.1.3 Section C: Coastal systems and landscapes** of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Geography (9635) specification, Version 3.2. It is the alternative Unit 1 option to hot deserts, assessed in Section B of the Unit 1 exam (40 marks, all questions compulsory). The specification divides it into 3.1.3.1 coasts as natural systems, 3.1.3.2 systems and processes, 3.1.3.3 coastal landscape development, 3.1.3.4 coastal management, 3.1.3.5 quantitative and qualitative skills and 3.1.3.6 case studies. It also requires study of **landscapes from beyond the United Kingdom**, though UK examples may be added.

The [coastal systems revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-geography-9635-coastal-systems-and-landscapes-revision-notes/) list the processes, landforms and management options. This guide shows how to explain and apply them: how energy is distributed along a coast, how to calculate and interpret a sediment budget, how to write landform sequences that earn full marks, and how to evaluate management. Practise with the [coastal systems practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-geography-9635-unit-1b-coastal-systems-practice/).

## Energy along a coastline

Wave energy depends on wind speed, wind duration and **fetch** (the distance of open water over which the wind blows). Energy is not spread evenly along the shore. As waves approach an indented coast, the part of each wave front in shallower water off a headland slows first. This **wave refraction** bends the wave fronts so that they converge on headlands and diverge in bays. Headlands therefore receive concentrated energy and erode, while bays receive less and accumulate sediment. Refraction explains why erosional features cluster on headlands and beaches occupy bays -- an important link for "explain" questions.

**Tides** set the height at which waves attack. On a coast with a small tidal range, wave energy is focused on a narrow band of cliff, encouraging a notch; a large range spreads attack over a wider zone and helps form broad wave-cut platforms. **Currents**, including longshore and rip currents, move sediment once it has been released.

## Sediment cells and budgets in practice

A sediment budget compares gains with losses in one cell over a set period. A worked example with invented figures:

> A bay gains 12,000 m^3 of sediment a year from cliff erosion and 4,000 m^3 from a river. Longshore drift carries 9,000 m^3 out of the cell and storms move 2,500 m^3 offshore.
> Budget = (12,000 + 4,000) - (9,000 + 2,500) = **+4,500 m^3 a year**.

The beach is likely to widen. Now suppose a sea wall stops cliff erosion entirely: inputs fall to 4,000 m^3, the budget becomes 4,000 - 11,500 = **-7,500 m^3 a year**, and the beach narrows. This shows numerically why protecting a cliff can starve beaches within the same cell -- a strong evaluation point for management questions.

## Writing landform sequences

Landform answers earn marks for each linked process, so write them as an ordered chain.

**Cliff and wave-cut platform.** (1) Waves break against the cliff base between high and low tide. (2) Hydraulic action, wave quarrying and abrasion cut a **wave-cut notch**. (3) The overhang above becomes unstable and collapses through mass movement. (4) Waves remove the debris, and the cliff retreats inland. (5) A gently sloping **wave-cut platform** is left, its surface smoothed by abrasion. (6) As the platform widens, waves break further offshore and lose energy, so retreat slows -- negative feedback.

**Cliff profile factors.** Where rock beds dip seawards, blocks slide along the bedding planes and the cliff slopes at a similar angle to the dip. Where they dip landwards, or are horizontal, cliffs are steep. Strong, well-jointed rock forms high vertical faces with caves along joints; weak clays slump into rounded profiles. Sub-aerial weathering and runoff lower the upper face, while marine erosion steepens the base.

**Spits, simple and compound.** (1) Longshore drift moves sediment along the shore. (2) Where the coast changes direction, such as at a river mouth, deposition continues out into open water. (3) The spit grows in the direction of drift. (4) Changes in wind and wave direction curve the end landwards into a **recurved** tip. (5) Repeated shifts leave several recurved ridges, making a **compound spit**. (6) Sheltered water behind the spit becomes a zone of mudflat and saltmarsh.

**Barrier beaches and islands.** A spit or bar that grows across a bay closes it to form a **barrier beach** with a lagoon behind. Long, narrow sand **barrier islands**, such as those along much of the Atlantic coast of the USA, run parallel to the mainland and are separated from it by lagoons; they are often linked to sea level rise reworking sediment landwards.

**Sand dunes.** Dry sand on a wide beach is blown inland and trapped by obstacles and pioneer plants. Succession runs from embryo dunes to fore dunes, yellow dunes and grey dunes, with more organic matter and less exposure away from the sea.

## Sea level change and its landforms

The specification asks for **eustatic, isostatic and tectonic** change and major sea level changes in the **last 10,000 years**. As the last glacial period ended, meltwater from shrinking ice sheets returned to the oceans and global sea level rose substantially (eustatic). At the same time, land that had been depressed under ice sheets began to rise (isostatic rebound), so in some places the land rose faster than the sea.

- **Emergence** (land rising relative to sea) leaves **raised beaches** and **marine platforms** above the present shoreline, often backed by old cliffs.
- **Submergence** (sea rising relative to land) drowns valleys: **rias** where river valleys are flooded (Galicia, Spain); **fjords** where glacial troughs are flooded (Norway); and **Dalmatian coasts**, where the sea fills valleys running parallel to the shore so the ridges between them survive as long, narrow islands (Croatia).
- **Recent and predicted climate change:** expansion of warming ocean water and melting ice raise sea level, threatening low-lying deltas, atolls and barrier islands with flooding, erosion and saltwater intrusion.

## Evaluating coastal management

The specification separates **traditional approaches** (hard and soft engineering) from **sustainable approaches** (shoreline management and **integrated coastal zone management**). Evaluate each option against effectiveness, cost, environmental impact and effects on the rest of the sediment cell.

- **Hard engineering** -- sea walls, groynes, rip-rap and revetments -- gives immediate, visible protection but is costly to maintain, can reflect wave energy and lower beaches, and interrupts sediment supply.
- **Soft engineering** -- beach nourishment, dune stabilisation and managed realignment -- works with natural processes. The Netherlands' **Sand Motor**, completed in 2011, placed about 21.5 million cubic metres of sand on one stretch of shore so that waves and currents would spread it along the coast over many years.
- **Shoreline management plans** set policies for each stretch -- hold the line, advance the line, managed realignment or no active intervention -- based on the whole sediment cell.
- **Integrated coastal zone management** coordinates all users and agencies over a whole coastline so that decisions in one place do not damage another.

## Case studies (3.1.3.6)

You need a **local-scale coastal environment** showing processes, landscape outcomes and the challenges of sustainable management, and a **contrasting coastal landscape** showing risks and opportunities for human occupation and evaluating responses of resilience, mitigation and adaptation. At least one must engage with field data. Useful field measurements include beach profiles with ranging poles and a clinometer, pebble size and roundness along the beach to test for longshore drift, and wave counts per minute to classify wave type.

## Worked example: an annotated answer

*Question: Evaluate the view that hard engineering is no longer a sustainable approach to coastal management.* **[9]**

> Hard engineering can still be justified where the value of what is protected is very high, such as a port or a city centre, because sea walls give reliable, immediate protection **(argument against the view, with condition)**. However, by stopping erosion it cuts sediment supply to the rest of the cell, so beaches further along the drift direction can narrow **(process-based limitation)**. Maintenance costs rise as sea level rises and storms intensify **(link to climate change)**. Schemes such as the Sand Motor show how soft approaches can protect a coast while letting natural processes distribute sediment **(named alternative)**. The view is largely correct for undeveloped or low-value coasts, but on densely developed shores hard engineering remains part of a sustainable plan when it is set within a shoreline management plan **(qualified judgement)**.

## Exam technique

- **Explain the formation of:** an ordered chain of processes, each linked to the next.
- **Calculate:** show the budget equation with units.
- **Compare:** use direct comparative words such as "whereas" and "both".
- **Evaluate / assess:** weigh costs, benefits and knock-on effects across the sediment cell, then give a judgement.

## Common mistakes

- Treating wave energy as equal along the whole coast; refraction concentrates it on headlands.
- Describing landforms without the sequence of processes.
- Confusing eustatic and isostatic change.
- Evaluating management for one town without considering the rest of the cell.
- Using only UK examples when the specification requires landscapes from beyond the UK.
