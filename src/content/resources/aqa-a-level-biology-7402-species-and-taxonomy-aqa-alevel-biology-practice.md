## Section A: short questions

**1.** **(a)** Give the definition of a species used in the AQA specification. **[1]**

**(b)** Name the taxon that lies between class and family in the hierarchy. **[1]**

**2.** A student wrote the scientific name of humans as "homo Sapiens". Give the correct form and state the rule broken. **[1]**

**3.** A horse and a donkey can mate and produce a mule. Mules are infertile. Explain why horses and donkeys are classified as different species. **[2]**

**4.** The table shows part of the classification of four organisms.

| Organism | Order | Family | Genus |
|---|---|---|---|
| W | Order 1 | Family A | Genus i |
| X | Order 1 | Family A | Genus ii |
| Y | Order 1 | Family A | Genus i |
| Z | Order 1 | Family B | Genus iii |

**(a)** Which two organisms are most closely related? **[1]**

**(b)** Explain your answer. **[1]**

**5.** **(a)** What is meant by a *phylogenetic* classification? **[1]**

**(b)** What is meant by "no overlap" in a classification hierarchy? **[1]**

**6.** Explain why the binomial system is useful to scientists. **[2]**

## Section B: longer questions

**7.** Female crickets of species C were placed in an arena with two loudspeakers. One played the courtship call of male species C and the other the call of a closely related species D. In 20 trials, females moved to the species C call 18 times and to the species D call once; once they moved to neither.

**(a)** What conclusion can be drawn from these results? **[2]**

**(b)** Explain the advantage to a female of responding only to the call of her own species. **[2]**

**8.** Explain why courtship behaviour is described as a necessary precursor to successful mating. **[2]**

**9.** Antibodies were made against a blood protein from species P. They were mixed with the same blood protein from four other species, and the amount of precipitate was measured.

| Species | Precipitate (% of that with species P) |
|---|---|
| Q | 23 |
| R | 74 |
| S | 6 |
| T | 51 |

**(a)** Which species is most closely related to P? **[1]**

**(b)** Explain how the results show this. **[3]**

**(c)** Give one limitation of using this method to judge relationships. **[1]**

**10.** The percentage of bases that differ in the same gene was found for four species.

| | E | F | G | H |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| E | — | 12 | 3 | 13 |
| F | 12 | — | 11 | 4 |
| G | 3 | 11 | — | 12 |
| H | 13 | 4 | 12 | — |

**(a)** Use the data to identify two pairs of closely related species. **[2]**

**(b)** Suggest why comparing many genes gives a more reliable conclusion than comparing one. **[1]**

**11.** Two populations of birds look identical, but they live in different areas and have never been seen breeding together. Describe the evidence that would show they are separate species. **[3]**

**12.** Early classifications were based mainly on observable features. Explain how advances in genome sequencing and immunology have helped to clarify evolutionary relationships. **[4]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** Two organisms belong to the same species if they are able to produce fertile offspring [1]. **[1]**

**(b)** Order [1]. **[1]**

**2.** *Homo sapiens*: the genus has a capital letter and the species name is all lower case (and both are italicised or underlined) [1]. **[1]**

**3.** Members of a species can produce fertile offspring [1]. Mules are infertile, so horses and donkeys do not meet the definition of one species [1]. **[2]**

**4. (a)** W and Y [1]. **[1]**

**(b)** They share the same genus, the lowest taxon shared by any pair, so they share the most recent common ancestor [1]. **[1]**

**5. (a)** Classification based on evolutionary origins and relationships (shared ancestry) [1]. **[1]**

**(b)** Each group is contained within only one group at the next rank up [1]. **[1]**

**6.** It is universal, so scientists in all countries use the same name and avoid confusion between common names [1]. The genus name shows which species are closely related [1]. **[2]**

**7. (a)** Females responded almost only to the call of their own species (18 of 20 trials compared with 1) [1], so the call is used in species recognition [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** Mating with a male of another species would produce no offspring or infertile offspring [1], wasting energy, time and gametes / reducing her reproductive success [1]. **[2]**

**8.** Mating happens only after the courtship sequence is completed, because each behaviour triggers the partner's response [1]. Courtship ensures the partner is of the same species and ready to mate, so mating is likely to produce fertile offspring [1]. **[2]**

**9. (a)** R [1]. **[1]**

**(b)** The antibodies are complementary to the protein of species P [1]. R's protein binds the most antibodies, giving the most precipitate, so its shape is most similar to P's protein [1]. A similar protein shape means a similar amino acid sequence, and so similar DNA, which indicates a recent common ancestor [1]. **[3]**

**(c)** Only one protein is compared; or the amount of precipitate is hard to measure precisely [1]. **[1]**

**10. (a)** E and G (3% difference) [1]; F and H (4% difference) [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** One gene may have changed unusually quickly or slowly, so many genes give a better measure of overall genetic similarity [1]. **[1]**

**11.** Bring individuals from the two populations together (or test their behaviour) [1] and see whether they mate and produce offspring [1]. If offspring are produced and are themselves fertile, they are the same species; if not, they are separate species [1]. Accept: courtship calls or displays not recognised by the other population; large differences in DNA base sequence. **[3]**

**12.** Similar appearance can result from similar environments rather than shared ancestry, so it can mislead [1]. Genome sequencing compares DNA base sequences directly: fewer differences show a more recent common ancestor [1]. Sequencing provides large amounts of data across many genes [1]. Immunology compares proteins: more antibody binding (more precipitate) shows more similar proteins and closer relationship [1]. **[4]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Leaving "fertile" out of the species definition.
- Mixing up the order of taxa.
- Formatting binomials incorrectly.
- Saying less precipitate means closer relationship.
- Drawing strong conclusions from a single protein or gene.
