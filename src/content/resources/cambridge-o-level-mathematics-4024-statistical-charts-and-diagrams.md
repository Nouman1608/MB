This guide covers section 9.4, Statistical charts and diagrams, in the Statistics topic of the Cambridge O Level Mathematics (Syllabus D) 4024 syllabus for 2025, 2026 and 2027. Every candidate studies it. The broader page [Statistics: Classifying Data, Averages, Spread and Statistical Charts](/resources/cambridge-o-level-mathematics-statistics-classifying-data-averages-spread-and/) shows how to draw each chart once; this guide concentrates on reading them accurately, working backwards from them, and the details that cost marks when you draw them. Afterwards, use the [statistical charts and diagrams revision notes](/resources/cambridge-o-level-mathematics-4024-statistical-charts-and-diagrams-revision-notes/) and the [statistical charts and diagrams practice questions](/resources/cambridge-o-level-mathematics-4024-statistical-charts-and-diagrams-practice/).

## What section 9.4 asks for

You must **draw and interpret**:

- (a) bar charts, including composite (stacked) and dual (side-by-side) bar charts;
- (b) pie charts;
- (c) pictograms;
- (d) simple frequency distributions.

Histograms and cumulative frequency diagrams are separate sections (9.7 and 9.6).

## Bar charts

**Drawing rules.** Bars have equal widths and equal gaps. The frequency axis starts at zero and has an equal scale. Both axes are labelled, and each bar is labelled with its category. Bar charts suit categorical data and discrete numerical data.

**Dual bar charts** place the bars for two or more groups side by side within each category, with a key. They make it easy to compare the groups category by category.

**Composite bar charts** stack the parts of a total in one bar, with a key. The height of the whole bar shows the total; the height of each section shows a part.

### Worked example 1: reading a composite bar chart

A composite bar chart shows drinks sold at a kiosk in three months. Each bar has water at the bottom, juice in the middle and tea on top. The boundaries between sections, read from the vertical scale, are:

| Month | Top of water | Top of juice | Top of tea |
|---|---|---|---|
| March | 30 | 50 | 65 |
| April | 25 | 55 | 65 |
| May | 40 | 75 | 90 |

Find the number of each drink sold in April, and comment on the trend in total sales.

```
April water = 25
April juice = 55 − 25 = 30
April tea   = 65 − 55 = 10
```

The key skill: **only the bottom section can be read straight from the scale**. For every other section, subtract the reading at its bottom from the reading at its top.

Totals are 65, 65 and 90, so total sales were the same in March and April and rose sharply in May. In May, juice was 75 − 40 = 35, the most of any drink in any month.

**Composite or dual?** Choose a composite chart when the totals matter; choose a dual chart when comparing the parts directly matters, because in a composite chart the upper sections do not start at zero and are hard to compare by eye.

## Pie charts

A pie chart shows how a whole is shared. Each sector's angle is proportional to its frequency.

```
sector angle = (frequency ÷ total) × 360°
frequency    = (sector angle ÷ 360°) × total
```

### Worked example 2: drawing with an awkward total

40 people chose a holiday type: beach 14, city 10, mountains 9, cruise 7. Calculate the sector angles.

Each person is 360° ÷ 40 = 9°.

```
Beach      14 × 9 = 126°
City       10 × 9 =  90°
Mountains   9 × 9 =  81°
Cruise      7 × 9 =  63°
Total               360°
```

Draw a radius, measure each angle in turn from the previous line with a protractor, and label each sector (or use a key). Angles should be accurate to within about 2°.

### Worked example 3: working backwards

In a pie chart of how a group of students travel, the bus sector is 126° and represents 42 students. The walking sector is 54°. Find the total number of students, the number who walk, and the percentage who take the bus.

```
126° represents 42 students, so 1° represents 42 ÷ 126 = 1/3 of a student
Total = 360 × 1/3 = 120 students
Walkers = 54 × 1/3 = 18 students
Bus percentage = 126 ÷ 360 × 100 = 35%
```

The percentage comes straight from the angle; you do not need the total for it.

**Comparing pie charts.** Two pie charts can only be compared as proportions unless you know both totals. A bigger sector in one chart may represent fewer people than a smaller sector in a chart with a larger total.

## Pictograms

A pictogram uses repeated symbols, with a **key** showing what one symbol represents. Parts of a symbol represent fractions of the key value.

### Worked example 4

In a pictogram of books borrowed, one symbol represents 12 books.

- Monday shows 2½ symbols: 2.5 × 12 = **30 books**.
- Tuesday shows 1¾ symbols: 1.75 × 12 = **21 books**.
- Wednesday had 15 books borrowed: 15 ÷ 12 = 1¼, so draw **one and a quarter symbols**.

When you choose a key, pick a number that divides your frequencies into simple fractions. With a key of 12, values that are multiples of 3 can be drawn as quarters; a value such as 17 could not be shown exactly.

## Simple frequency distributions

A frequency distribution lists each value (or category) with its frequency. To draw one, put the values on the horizontal axis and frequency on the vertical axis, and draw a bar (or a vertical line) for each value. For discrete data, bars have gaps, or vertical lines are used, because there are no values in between.

### Worked example 5

The number of goals scored in 30 matches is shown.

| Goals | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Matches | 4 | 9 | 8 | 5 | 3 | 1 |

On the chart, the bar for 1 goal is the tallest (9), so the mode is 1. The heights fall away to the right, showing that high-scoring matches were rare. You can read further facts: 5 + 3 + 1 = 9 matches had three or more goals, which is 9 ÷ 30 = 30% of the matches.

## Choosing a diagram

| Purpose | Suitable diagram |
|---|---|
| Compare frequencies of categories | bar chart |
| Compare two groups category by category | dual bar chart |
| Show totals and how they are made up | composite bar chart |
| Show shares of one whole | pie chart |
| Simple visual display with a key | pictogram |
| Show a discrete frequency table | bar chart or vertical line graph of the distribution |

## Common mistakes

- Reading the upper section of a composite bar from the scale without subtracting.
- A frequency axis that does not start at zero, or an uneven scale.
- Pie chart angles that do not add to 360°, often from rounding each angle.
- Using the frequency instead of the angle when converting to a percentage, or the reverse.
- Pictogram partial symbols read as whole symbols.
- Missing labels or a missing key.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Draw:** use a ruler and protractor; label axes, sectors and keys.
- **Complete the chart:** match the style and scale already used.
- **Write down** a value from a chart: read carefully, checking the scale.
- **Compare** or **comment:** quote values from the chart and say what they show.
