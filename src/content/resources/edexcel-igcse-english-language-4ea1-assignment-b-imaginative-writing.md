Assignment B is the writing half of the coursework route in Pearson Edexcel International GCSE English
Language A (4EA1). Unlike the exam alternative, you have time to plan, draft and refine, so the
standard of control expected in the finished piece is high. This guide covers the kinds of task your
teacher may set, what the assessment grids reward, and how to use the drafting process well within the
rules. The [Component 3 overview](/resources/igcse-edexcel-english-language-component-3/) explains how
the component fits the qualification.

## What the specification requires

- **A piece of imaginative writing** based on a selected topic.
- A **teacher-devised** assignment that allows access to **AO4 (18 marks)** and **AO5 (12 marks)**:
  30 marks in total.
- The **purpose should be to narrate, describe or entertain**.
- An advisory length of **about 700 words**; there is no penalty for exceeding it.
- You may write about **real or imagined experiences**.

## Kinds of task

The specification gives two routes for setting the topic:

| Route | Examples of the type the specification gives |
|---|---|
| Suggested by an Anthology piece | A piece that begins or ends with a given line; the next episode in a character's story after reading a Part 2 prose text |
| Unrelated, perhaps from a set of images | A title such as "The visitor"; a frightening experience; an embarrassing moment |

If your task is linked to an Anthology text, use it as a **springboard**, not a template. A
continuation should match the original's voice and situation closely enough to be recognisable, but
the writing, events and images must be your own.

## What the grids reward

| Level | AO4 (18) key wording | AO5 (12) key wording |
|---|---|---|
| Level 3 | "Communicates clearly"; "appropriate use of form, tone and register" (8–11) | "Varied vocabulary"; "accurate and varied punctuation" (5–7) |
| Level 4 | "Communicates successfully"; "a secure realisation of the writing task"; "effective use of form, tone and register" (12–15) | "Wide, selective vocabulary"; "managing sentence structures for deliberate effect" (8–10) |
| Level 5 | "Communication is perceptive and subtle"; "sharply focused on purpose"; "sophisticated use of form, tone and register" (16–18) | "Manipulates complex ideas"; "extensive vocabulary strategically"; punctuation used "to aid emphasis and precision" (11–12) |

The words **deliberate**, **strategically** and **selectively** recur at the top levels. They describe
choices you can make visible because you have time to revise.

## Making 700 words count

Seven hundred words is roughly five or six paragraphs. That is enough for one situation, one change and
one well-crafted ending, and not much more.

- **One scene or a short sequence** rather than a whole life.
- **A single viewpoint**, held steadily.
- **A clear shape**: for example a zoom from setting to detail, a present scene with one memory, or a
  cyclical ending.
- **Every paragraph doing a job**: establishing, developing, turning, resolving.

## Using images or a title as a stimulus

When the task is unrelated to the Anthology, perhaps prompted by a set of images or a title such as
"The visitor", the risk is a generic piece. Narrow the stimulus quickly: who is the visitor, to whom,
and why does the visit matter now? Choose one image from a set and ask what happened five minutes
before it was taken, or what the person outside the frame can see. Specific answers to these questions
give you a situation, a viewpoint and a source of tension, which is what AO4's "sharply focused on
purpose" requires. Decide early whether the piece will mainly narrate or describe, and keep that
purpose clear through every draft.

## Working within the coursework rules

- You must **work independently** and sign the authentication sheet; your teacher signs it too.
- Your teacher **may suggest general improvements at a draft stage**; you must make the corrections and
  update the work yourself.
- The work **must not be rewritten after it has been marked**.
- **Acknowledge sources**. Teachers are told to be alert to work derived from the internet or AI.
- The finished piece shows your name, the completion date, the mark and a brief teacher comment.

Plan your drafting so that the improvements happen **before** marking: draft, receive general
feedback, revise, proofread, submit.

## A drafting routine

1. **First draft:** get the whole shape down, including the ending, without polishing.
2. **Structure pass:** does each paragraph earn its place? Cut or merge any that repeat.
3. **Voice pass:** read aloud; mark any sentence where the voice slips.
4. **Vocabulary pass:** replace vague words ("nice", "scary", "very") with precise ones; delete
   adjectives that a stronger noun or verb makes unnecessary.
5. **Sentence pass:** check variety and the placement of short sentences at moments of emphasis.
6. **Accuracy pass:** spelling, punctuation (especially dialogue), paragraphing, tense.

## Worked redraft

Task (original, Anthology-linked): *After reading "The Necklace", write the scene in which Madame
Forestier returns home after her final conversation with Mathilde. Your purpose is to narrate.*

**First-draft paragraph:**

> Madame Forestier went home and thought about the necklace. She felt quite sad and guilty because
> Mathilde had worked so hard for ten years. It was a long time ago but she still remembered the ball.
> She looked out of the window and it was raining.

**Redrafted paragraph:**

> The rain had found its way into the gutters again; she could hear it gargling somewhere above the
> window. The case was where it had always been, at the back of the drawer, black leather worn pale at
> the corners. She had not even opened it, that day. She opened it now. The stones looked exactly as
> they always had, which was the worst of it: ten years of someone else's life, lying quietly on velvet,
> and she had never noticed the difference.

What changed, and why it scores higher:

- **AO4:** close third person gives access to her thoughts without stating emotions; the unopened case
  is a telling detail that turns into guilt; the short sentences "She had not even opened it, that day.
  She opened it now." pivot the paragraph from past to present, and the final sentence keeps the
  original story's irony.
- **AO5:** a precise verb ("gargling"), a semicolon and a colon used deliberately, and varied sentence
  lengths that slow down to the final admission.
- **Faithful but original:** the scene follows on from the Anthology story's ending and its detail of
  the returned case, while inventing its own images, voice and structure.

If you write a continuation, check every detail you borrow against the original text so that nothing
contradicts it.

## Common mistakes

- **Writing an essay or argument**: the purpose must be to narrate, describe or entertain.
- **Copying the Anthology text's sentences** in a continuation.
- **Too much plot** for 700 words.
- **Assuming time to draft means length**: extra words do not raise the mark; control does.
- **Leaving accuracy to the end** and running out of drafting time.
- **Ignoring the teacher's general feedback** or, at the other extreme, expecting the teacher to correct
  the work.
