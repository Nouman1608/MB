In the exam you choose **one** of three tasks and write a complete composition. This set breaks that
skill into parts: Section A practises techniques, and Section B practises planning, openings, endings
and revision for each task type.

## Section A — Technique questions

**1.** Identify the task type (descriptive, imaginative, argumentative or discursive) of each prompt.
**[3]**
(i) "Write a story in which a promise is broken."
(ii) "'Young people today have more freedom than ever.' Discuss."
(iii) "Describe a crowded beach at the end of a summer's day."

**2.** Rewrite this sentence to make it more effective as description, then explain **one** change you
made: *"The old house was very scary and dark."* **[3]**

**3.** Correct the **five** errors in this original paragraph. **[5]**
*"Their was no one on the platform, the train had already left. Its strange how quiet a station can
be at night. I sat down on the bench and I start to wonder weather I would ever get home."*

**4.** Explain **two** differences between argumentative and discursive writing. **[4]**

**5.** Write **three** sentences about a storm, each beginning in a different way, and name the
structure you used for each. **[3]**

**6.** A story opens: *"The clock in the hallway had stopped at ten past four."* Write a final
sentence that creates a cyclical ending, and explain its effect. **[3]**

## Section B — Planning and writing questions

**7.** Task: *Describe a market as a storm approaches.*
**(a)** Plan a four-paragraph structure, giving the focus of each paragraph. **[4]**
**(b)** Write the opening paragraph (about 80–120 words). **[6]**

**8.** Task: *Write a story that begins with a phone ringing in an empty house.*
**(a)** Outline the opening, the development, the turning point and the ending. **[4]**
**(b)** Write the final paragraph of the story (about 80–120 words). **[6]**

**9.** Task: *"Mobile phones should be banned in all schools." Write an article arguing for or against
this view.*
**(a)** Plan three developed points and one rebuttal. **[6]**
**(b)** Write the opening paragraph. **[4]**

**10.** Task: *"Is it better to grow up in a city or in the countryside?" Discuss.*
**(a)** Plan a balanced discussion with points on both sides. **[6]**
**(b)** Write the concluding paragraph. **[4]**

**11.** Improve this weak original paragraph from a descriptive piece, then explain **four** changes
you made with reference to the writing objectives. **[8]**
*"The forest was nice. There was lots of trees and they was green. Birds were singing. It was very
peaceful and I liked it, it was the best place ever."*

## Answers

**How these are marked.** The bracketed credits show one way to reach each tariff. For writing tasks,
credit is given for the quality shown, so a response of the same standard that takes a different
approach earns the same marks.

**1.** (i) imaginative [1]; (ii) discursive [1]; (iii) descriptive [1].

**2.** Rewrite [1], for example: *"The house leaned over the lane, its windows black and its door hanging
open like a mouth that had forgotten how to close."* Explanation [2], for example: replacing the vague
"scary and dark" with a precise image (the door as a mouth) makes the house seem alive and threatening,
so the reader feels fear rather than being told about it.

**3.** One mark per correction: "Their" → "There" [1]; comma splice after "platform" → full stop or
semicolon [1]; "Its" → "It's" [1]; "start" → "started" to keep the past tense [1]; "weather" →
"whether" [1].

**4.** Argumentative writing takes a clear position and persuades, while discursive writing explores
several viewpoints in a balanced way [2]. In argument, counter-arguments are raised in order to rebut
them and the piece ends with a firm conclusion or call to action; in discussion, they are weighed
fairly and the ending is a considered, possibly qualified, judgement [2].

**5.** One mark per correctly named, different structure, for example: *"Without warning, the sky split
open."* (fronted adverbial) [1]; *"Although the rain was deafening, the children kept playing."*
(subordinate clause first) [1]; *"Thunder."* (minor sentence) [1].

**6.** Sentence [1], for example: *"Behind me, in the empty hallway, the clock began to tick again."*
Effect [2]: returning to the clock links the ending to the opening, giving a sense of completion, and
the change from stopped to ticking suggests that something has been resolved or time has restarted for
the character.

**7. (a)** One mark per purposeful paragraph focus: wide view of the busy market under a heavy sky [1];
close detail of one stall-holder hurrying to cover fruit [1]; the first drops and wind, with sounds and
movement [1]; the emptied market in the rain, echoing the opening [1].
**(b)** Credit up to 6: a focused opening on one moment [1]; precise sensory detail [1]; a fresh image
[1]; varied sentence structures [1]; mood that hints at the coming storm [1]; accurate spelling and
punctuation [1]. Example: *"At noon the sky over the market turned the colour of a bruise. Nobody looked
up. A woman weighed onions on a brass scale, her bangles chiming with every handful, while a boy
balanced a tower of oranges higher than his head. Smoke from the kebab grill hung low and refused to
rise. Then the canvas roofs began to breathe, in and out, in and out, as if the whole market had started
to worry."*

**8. (a)** One mark each for a clear opening (the phone rings in a house the narrator believes is empty)
[1]; development (the narrator searches room by room and the ringing moves) [1]; turning point (the
caller's voice is the narrator's own) [1]; and a deliberate ending (the narrator leaves a message for
someone who will arrive tomorrow) [1].
**(b)** Credit up to 6: resolves or deliberately leaves open the story's central problem [2]; links back
to the opening image of the phone [1]; controlled pace at the climax [1]; varied sentences [1]; accuracy
[1]. Example: *"I picked up the receiver one last time. The line was silent now, but it was not empty;
I could hear breathing, slow and careful, the way you breathe when you are trying not to wake anyone.
I said my name. Somewhere in the house above me, a phone began to ring."*

**9. (a)** For each of three points [2]: a point plus a realistic example or reason, for example
(against a ban) phones support learning through quick research; they help students contact parents in
an emergency; schools should teach responsible use rather than avoid it. Rebuttal credit is included in
the third point's development, for example answering the worry about distraction by suggesting clear
rules for when phones are put away.
**(b)** Credit up to 4: an engaging hook [1]; a clear position [1]; a register suited to an article for
readers who may include students and parents [1]; accuracy [1]. Example: *"Picture a classroom where
every student holds a library, a dictionary and a calculator in their pocket, and is told to leave it in
a locker. Banning mobile phones from schools is not a solution; it is a refusal to teach the most
important skill of the century: using technology wisely."*

**10. (a)** Credit up to 6: two developed points for the city, such as access to schools, transport and
cultural activities [2]; two developed points for the countryside, such as space, safety and a closer
connection to nature [2]; a planned judgement that weighs both, for example that the best environment
depends on the child [2].
**(b)** Credit up to 4: a balanced summary [1]; a considered judgement [1]; a reflective, measured tone
[1]; accuracy [1]. Example: *"In the end, neither city nor countryside guarantees a happy childhood.
The city offers opportunity; the countryside offers room to breathe. Perhaps what matters most is not
where a child grows up, but whether the adults around them make the most of what that place can give."*

**11.** Improved paragraph [4], for example: *"Sunlight fell through the forest in narrow gold strips.
Oaks and beeches crowded the path, their leaves a hundred different greens, and somewhere above me a
blackbird repeated the same bright phrase as if testing it. Nothing hurried. For the first time in
weeks, neither did I."* Explanation of four changes [4], one mark each: vague words ("nice", "lots")
replaced with precise nouns and images (W1); "There was lots of trees and they was green" corrected
for agreement (W4); varied sentence lengths, ending with short sentences for emphasis (W2); the comma
splice removed and the ideas ordered from light to sound to feeling, giving shape (W3/W4).

## Where marks are usually lost

- Plans that list ideas without an order or an ending.
- Model openings that try every technique at once.
- Corrections that miss the tense error or the comma splice.

## Frequently asked questions

**How long should a full composition be?**
The specification does not set a length. Aim for a complete, controlled piece you can finish and check
in the time available for Section B.

**Should I practise full compositions too?**
Yes. Use the tasks above as full timed pieces once you have practised the parts.
