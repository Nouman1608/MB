## Demand and supply: movements and shifts

**Demand** is the quantity of a good consumers are willing and able to buy at each price over a period. It slopes down: a lower price makes the good cheaper relative to substitutes and increases buyers' purchasing power.

**Supply** is the quantity producers are willing and able to sell at each price. It slopes up: a higher price makes production more profitable and covers the higher cost of producing extra units.

| Change | Demand | Supply |
|---|---|---|
| Good's own price changes | Movement along: extension (price falls) or contraction (price rises) | Movement along: extension (price rises) or contraction (price falls) |
| Anything else changes | Shift: income, prices of substitutes and complements, tastes, advertising, population | Shift: costs of production, technology, indirect taxes and subsidies, number of firms, weather |

The rule that saves most marks: **a change in the good's own price never shifts its own demand or supply curve.**

## Worked example: the market for strawberries

Weekly demand and supply for punnets of strawberries in a region are:

```
Qd = 900 - 60P      Qs = 120P - 180
```

P is the price in pounds per punnet; Q is in thousand punnets.

**1. Equilibrium.** Set Qd = Qs: 900 - 60P = 120P - 180, so 1,080 = 180P and **P = £6**. Then Q = 900 - 360 = **540 thousand punnets**.

**2. Surplus.** Demand meets the price axis at £15 (where Qd = 0) and supply at £1.50 (where Qs = 0).

- Consumer surplus = 0.5 x (15 - 6) x 540 = **£2,430 thousand**: the triangle below demand and above the price.
- Producer surplus = 0.5 x (6 - 1.50) x 540 = **£1,215 thousand**: the triangle above supply and below the price.

**3. A supply shock.** A wet summer raises growers' costs, and supply becomes Qs = 120P - 360. At the old price of £6, quantity supplied is only 360 thousand while 540 thousand are demanded: a **shortage of 180 thousand**. The shortage pushes up the price, which contracts demand and extends supply, until 900 - 60P = 120P - 360 gives **P = £7** and **Q = 480 thousand**.

**4. Who loses?** Consumer surplus falls to 0.5 x (15 - 7) x 480 = £1,920 thousand, a loss of £510 thousand. Producer surplus becomes 0.5 x (7 - 3) x 480 = £960 thousand, also lower: the higher price does not make up for the higher costs and smaller sales. The diagram shows supply shifting left, price rising from £6 to £7 and quantity falling from 540 to 480.

## Disequilibrium and how markets adjust

| Situation | Cause | Pressure |
|---|---|---|
| Excess demand (shortage) | Price below equilibrium | Price rises: demand contracts, supply extends |
| Excess supply (surplus) | Price above equilibrium | Price falls: demand extends, supply contracts |

Adjustment takes time. Strawberries are perishable, so a surplus must be cleared quickly with price cuts, while a shortage of houses can last years because supply responds slowly.

## The interaction of markets

Markets are linked, so a change in one spreads to others.

| Link | Meaning | Example (continuing strawberries) |
|---|---|---|
| Joint demand | Goods bought together (complements) | Demand for cream rises when strawberries become cheaper |
| Competitive demand | Goods that are substitutes | When strawberries rise to £7, demand for raspberries increases |
| Derived demand | Demand for a factor because of demand for what it makes | Demand for fruit pickers depends on demand for strawberries |
| Composite demand | One good wanted for several uses | Glasshouse land wanted for strawberries, tomatoes or flowers |
| Joint supply | One process produces two goods | Producing more beef also produces more leather |
| Competitive supply | Producers can switch resources between goods | A farmer who switches fields to strawberries grows fewer potatoes |

**Chain of effects (invented).** The strawberry price rises from £6 to £7. Buyers switch to raspberries (competitive demand), so raspberry demand shifts right and its price rises. Raspberry growers expand, drawing land from other crops (competitive supply), so supply of those crops shifts left. Demand for cream falls slightly as fewer strawberries are bought (joint demand). One shock has moved four markets.

## Annotated model answer

*Question: Explain, with the aid of a diagram, the effect of a rise in the price of raspberries on the market for strawberries. [4]*

> Raspberries and strawberries are substitutes. When the price of raspberries rises, some buyers switch to strawberries, so the demand curve for strawberries shifts right from D1 to D2. At the original price there is now excess demand, so the price of strawberries rises from P1 to P2, supply extends along the supply curve, and quantity rises from Q1 to Q2.
>
> *Diagram:* price on the vertical axis, quantity on the horizontal; one upward-sloping supply curve; demand shifting right from D1 to D2; old and new equilibria labelled, with P1, P2, Q1 and Q2 marked.

*Why it works:* it names the relationship, shifts the right curve (demand, not supply), describes the adjustment, and lists exactly what the diagram must show.

## Common mistakes

- Shifting the demand curve when the good's own price changes.
- Shifting both curves when only one has a cause.
- Confusing a shortage with a decrease in supply.
- Leaving diagram axes or curves unlabelled.
- Getting the triangle wrong for surplus: consumer surplus is above price, producer surplus below.
- Treating joint demand as joint supply.

## Exam technique

- **Section A:** multiple-choice items often show a shift and ask for the cause; check whether the cause affects buyers (demand) or sellers (supply).
- **Section B:** with equations, set Qd = Qs, solve for P, then substitute for Q. Use the intercepts for surplus triangles.
- **Section C:** for an essay on a related market, trace the chain, then evaluate: how close are the substitutes, how elastic is supply, and how long will the effects last?

## Frequently asked questions

**Can both curves shift at once?**
Yes. If both shift, the direction of one variable is certain and the other depends on the relative sizes of the shifts; say which is uncertain.

**Why does a surplus not always disappear quickly?**
If prices are slow to change, or the good cannot be stored, sellers may be left with unsold stock for some time.
