## Causes of variation (3.5.3 a)

| Cause | Example |
|---|---|
| Genetic | Inherited blood group |
| Environmental | Less light, so a shorter plant |
| Both | Height, body mass |

Clones and identical twins have the same genes. Differences between them are environmental.

## Genes, chromosomes, gametes (3.5.3 b, c)

- Genes are carried on chromosomes in the nucleus.
- Chromosomes normally come in pairs.
- Genes pass to offspring in the gametes.
- Each gamete receives just one chromosome of every pair, and fertilisation puts the pairs back.

## Sex determination (3.5.3 d)

Human body cells have 23 pairs of chromosomes. One pair decides sex: female XX, male XY.

| Parent | Gametes |
|---|---|
| Mother XX | all X |
| Father XY | half X, half Y |

Offspring: 1 XX (girl) : 1 XY (boy). Probability of a boy is 1/2 every time.


Worked reminder: a couple have four children. The probability that all four are boys is (1/2)⁴ = 1/16. The probability that the next child is a boy is still 1/2, whatever the earlier children were, because each fertilisation is independent.

## Key terms (3.5.3 e, f, g)

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Gene | A short section of DNA that codes for a protein |
| Allele | A different form of a gene |
| Homozygous | Same two alleles (RR or rr) |
| Heterozygous | Two different alleles (Rr) |
| Dominant | Shows in the phenotype when present on one chromosome only |
| Recessive | Shows only when no dominant allele is present |
| Genotype | The alleles present |
| Phenotype | The observable characteristic |

Key distinction: a **carrier** is a heterozygous person who has a recessive disorder allele but does not show the disorder.

## Method for a monohybrid cross

1. Choose a letter. State which allele is dominant (capital) and which is recessive (lower case).
2. Write the parents' genotypes.
3. Write each parent's gametes.
4. Fill in a Punnett square.
5. State genotypes, phenotypes, the ratio and the probability.

### The four crosses to know

| Cross | Offspring genotypes | Phenotypes |
|---|---|---|
| Rr × Rr | 1 RR : 2 Rr : 1 rr | 3 dominant : 1 recessive |
| Rr × rr | 1 Rr : 1 rr | 1 dominant : 1 recessive |
| RR × rr | all Rr | all dominant |
| RR × Rr | 1 RR : 1 Rr | all dominant |

### Worked reminder

In sweet peas a tall stem (R) is dominant to a short stem (r). Two heterozygous plants give 80 seedlings.

- Expected tall: 80 × 3/4 = 60. Expected short: 80 × 1/4 = 20.
- Predicted counts are not guaranteed. A result of 57 tall and 23 short is consistent with the model because fertilisation is random.

### Unknown genotype

Cross the dominant-looking individual with a homozygous recessive one (rr). A recessive offspring proves the unknown parent was Rr. If all offspring show the dominant phenotype, the parent was probably RR.

Genetic diagrams are **models**. They predict probabilities. They do not say what happens in one family.

## Mendel

- Used garden peas with clear either-or characteristics.
- Began with pure-breeding lines and studied one characteristic at a time.
- Controlled which plants were crossed.
- Counted large numbers of offspring.
- Found first-generation offspring all showed one form, and the next generation was about 3 : 1 (for seed shape, 5474 round and 1850 wrinkled).
- His "inherited factors" were linked with chromosomes later, by other scientists.

## Family trees

| Pattern | Conclusion |
|---|---|
| Neither parent shows it, a child does | Recessive; the parents must both be carriers |
| Both parents show it, a child does not | Dominant; the parents must both be Dd-type heterozygotes |

Method:

1. Find a family where parents share a phenotype and a child differs.
2. Decide dominant or recessive from the table above.
3. Give genotypes: recessive phenotype is homozygous recessive; dominant phenotype has at least one dominant allele.
4. Use a Punnett square for any prediction.


### Worked reminder: reading a family tree

Two parents are both unaffected by a recessive disorder. They have one affected daughter and one unaffected son.

- The parents show nothing but a child is affected, so the allele is recessive.
- The daughter is homozygous recessive (aa). She received one a from each parent, so both parents are Aa.
- The son is AA or Aa. Out of the three unaffected outcomes from Aa × Aa, two are Aa, so the probability that he is a carrier is 2/3.
- If the parents have another child, the probability it is affected is 1/4.

## DNA, genes and proteins (3.5.3 h to k)

| Fact | Detail |
|---|---|
| Chromosomes | Made of large DNA molecules |
| DNA | Holds the coded information for inherited characteristics |
| Gene | Small section of DNA coding for a particular combination of amino acids, to make a specific protein |
| Structure | Very long strands twisted into a double helix |
| Bases | Four different compounds in the strands (names not required) |
| Triplet | Three bases in a row are the code for one amino acid |
| Order | The base sequence decides the sequence in which amino acids are joined |

Calculation: number of bases = 3 × number of amino acids. A protein of 210 amino acids needs at least 630 bases in its coding sequence.

Reading a code: split into threes first. With the invented code XYW = amino acid 1 and WWZ = amino acid 2, the sequence XYWWWZ is two amino acids: 1 then 2.


### Worked reminder: from base sequence to protein

A gene fragment has 18 bases. 18 ÷ 3 = 6, so it codes for 6 amino acids. Change the order of the bases and you may change the order of the amino acids, so the protein can fold differently and work differently. This is why a change to one gene can lead to an inherited disorder such as sickle cell anaemia, in which the abnormal allele leads to abnormal haemoglobin.

Chromosome link: chromosomes contain many genes, and each gene is a different section of DNA.

## Genetic disorders (3.5.4)

| Disorder | Key facts | Allele |
|---|---|---|
| Polydactyly | Extra fingers or toes | Dominant |
| Cystic fibrosis | Thick sticky mucus affects lungs and digestion | Recessive |
| Sickle cell anaemia | Red blood cells sickle-shaped and can block vessels | Recessive |
| Down's syndrome | Extra chromosome: 47 in body cells, not alleles | Not allele-based |

Carrier × carrier: 1/4 affected, 1/2 carrier, 1/4 unaffected non-carrier. An unaffected child of two carriers has a 2/3 probability of being a carrier.

### Reading disorder data

1. State what is measured and the units.
2. Compare groups using numbers.
3. Compare with the prediction from the cross (for example 25%).
4. Comment on sample size.

Worked reminder: 12 children, 4 affected. 4 ÷ 12 × 100 = 33.3%. Prediction 25% means 3 of 12. The difference is small for a small sample.


### Down's syndrome in more detail

- Cause: an extra chromosome. It is a different type of cause from an abnormal allele.
- Counts: the usual number in a human body cell is 46 (23 pairs). In Down's syndrome it is 47.
- Where it comes from: a gamete with 24 chromosomes joins with a normal gamete with 23, giving 47 in the zygote.
- Exam use: you may be given a table of cases against age or country. Describe the trend with numbers, then calculate any rate or ratio asked for.

## Quick self-test

1. A plant is grown from a cutting in two different soils and ends up different heights. Name the cause of the height difference.
2. Which sex chromosomes does a human male have?
3. What is the probability that a baby is a girl?
4. Define heterozygous.
5. In Tt × tt, state the phenotype ratio if T is dominant.
6. A cross of two heterozygous parents gives 120 offspring. How many are expected to show the recessive phenotype?
7. Two parents with a dominant characteristic have a child without it. Which type of allele causes the characteristic?
8. How many bases code for 90 amino acids?
9. How many chromosomes are in a body cell of a person with Down's syndrome?
10. Which of polydactyly, cystic fibrosis and sickle cell anaemia is caused by a dominant allele?

### Answers

1. Environment (the soils), because cuttings from one plant are genetically identical.
2. X and Y.
3. 1/2.
4. It has two different alleles of the gene.
5. 1 dominant : 1 recessive.
6. 120 × 1/4 = 30.
7. A dominant allele.
8. 90 × 3 = 270 bases.
9. 47.
10. Polydactyly.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing capital and lower-case letters that look the same (C and c, S and s). Choose clear letters.
- Writing a ratio where a probability is wanted, or the other way round.
- Forgetting to write the gametes, so the Punnett square has no clear source.
- Saying the recessive allele has been lost in a carrier.
- Describing Down's syndrome as an allele problem.
- Stating "all of the DNA codes for protein". A gene is only a section of DNA.
- Predicting exact counts when the question asks what is expected.
- Using "the parents have the gene". Say which allele and whether it is dominant or recessive.
