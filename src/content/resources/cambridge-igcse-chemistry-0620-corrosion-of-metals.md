## Rusting: what it is and what it needs

**Corrosion** is the gradual destruction of a metal by reaction with substances around it. **Rusting** is the name used only for the corrosion of **iron and steel**. Other metals corrode, but they do not "rust".

Rusting needs **both**:

- **water**, and
- **oxygen** (from the air).

The product is **hydrated iron(III) oxide**, the orange-brown solid called rust.

```
iron + water + oxygen → hydrated iron(III) oxide
```

Iron gains oxygen and its atoms lose electrons to become iron(III) ions, so rusting is an **oxidation**. Rust is crumbly and flakes away from the surface, so fresh iron underneath is exposed and the corrosion keeps going. That is why rusting weakens iron structures over time, and why it is worth preventing.

## Showing that both conditions are needed

A standard investigation uses three test tubes, each with a clean iron nail, left for several days.

| Tube | Set-up | Why each detail is there | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | nail in tap water, open to the air | both water and oxygen present (the control) | rusts |
| B | nail in water that has been **boiled**, with a layer of **oil** on top | boiling removes dissolved air; the oil stops air dissolving again | no rust |
| C | nail in a **stoppered** tube with **anhydrous calcium chloride** | the calcium chloride absorbs water vapour; the stopper keeps moist air out | no rust |

**Conclusion:** the nail rusts only in tube A, where both water and oxygen are present. Remove either one and rusting does not happen.

**Model answer (4 marks):** "In tube B there is water but no oxygen, because boiling removed dissolved oxygen and the oil stops more dissolving [1]; the nail does not rust [1]. In tube C there is air but no water, because the calcium chloride is a drying agent [1]; the nail does not rust. Only tube A, with both, rusts, so both water and oxygen are needed [1]."

## Barrier methods

A **barrier method** puts a layer over the iron that **excludes oxygen or water** from its surface. Without contact with both, the iron cannot rust.

The syllabus names three barrier methods. The skill examiners test is choosing the right one for the job.

| Method | Suits | Why it is chosen there |
|---|---|---|
| Painting | car bodies, bridges, railings, gates | covers large surfaces cheaply and lasts well if the surface does not rub |
| Greasing (or oiling) | bicycle chains, hinges, moving machine parts | paint would crack or rub off moving parts; grease also lubricates |
| Coating with plastic | wire fences, dish racks, garden furniture, tool handles | a tough, flexible, waterproof layer that does not chip easily |

**The weakness of all barrier methods:** if the coating is scratched, chipped or worn away, oxygen and water reach the iron and it starts to rust at that point. Grease also has to be replaced because it wears off and collects dirt.

## Galvanising (Supplement)

**Galvanising** is coating iron or steel with a layer of **zinc**. It protects in two ways.

1. **As a barrier.** While the zinc coating is intact, it keeps oxygen and water away from the iron, just like paint.
2. **By sacrificial protection.** If the coating is scratched and the iron is exposed, the iron **still does not rust**. The zinc corrodes instead.

Galvanised steel is used for buckets, dustbins, roofing sheets and outdoor fittings, where scratches are likely.

## Sacrificial protection explained (Supplement)

The syllabus asks for the explanation in two ways, and full-mark answers use both.

**In terms of the reactivity series:** zinc is **more reactive than iron**, so zinc reacts with oxygen and water in preference to iron.

**In terms of electron loss:** a more reactive metal loses electrons more readily. Zinc atoms lose electrons to form zinc ions, so the zinc is oxidised instead of the iron:

```
Zn → Zn2+ + 2e−
```

Because the zinc is losing electrons, the iron does not lose electrons, so it is not oxidised and does not rust. The zinc is "sacrificed": it corrodes away, and the iron is protected for as long as some zinc remains.

**The same idea without a coating:** blocks of a more reactive metal, such as zinc or magnesium, can be attached to large steel structures like ship hulls or underground pipes. The blocks corrode instead of the steel and are replaced when they have worn away.

### Model answer, annotated

**Question.** Explain why a galvanised steel bucket does not rust even when it is deeply scratched. [3]

> Zinc is more reactive than iron [1]. Zinc loses electrons more readily than iron, forming zinc ions [1], so the zinc is oxidised instead of the iron and the iron does not rust [1].

*An answer that says only "the zinc forms a barrier" scores 0 here, because a scratch has broken the barrier. The question is testing sacrificial protection.*

## Worked example: deducing from results

*The results below are invented for practice.* Iron nails were wrapped in strips of different metals and left in water for a week.

| Nail wrapped in | Rust after one week? |
|---|---|
| magnesium | no |
| zinc | no |
| copper | yes |
| nothing | yes |

**Question.** Explain the results for magnesium and copper. [3]

**Answer.** Magnesium is more reactive than iron [1], so it loses electrons / corrodes instead of the iron, giving sacrificial protection [1]. Copper is less reactive than iron, so it cannot give sacrificial protection and the iron rusts [1].

## Common mistakes

- **Giving only one condition for rusting.** Both water and oxygen are needed.
- **Calling rust "iron oxide".** The syllabus name is **hydrated iron(III) oxide**.
- **Saying aluminium or copper "rusts".** Only iron and steel rust; other metals corrode.
- **Explaining barrier methods vaguely.** Say what they exclude: oxygen or water.
- **Treating galvanising as only a barrier.** Its key feature is that it still protects when scratched.
- **Explaining sacrificial protection with reactivity alone.** Add electron loss: zinc loses electrons in preference to iron.
- **Choosing a less reactive metal for sacrificial protection.** It must be more reactive than iron.

## Exam technique for 9.5 command words

- **State:** "water and oxygen"; "painting, greasing, coating with plastic".
- **Describe:** for barrier methods, say what the layer does: "excludes oxygen and water from the surface of the iron".
- **Explain** (Extended): reactivity comparison, electron loss, and the consequence for iron.
- **Suggest:** choose a barrier method for a named object and justify it from how the object is used.
