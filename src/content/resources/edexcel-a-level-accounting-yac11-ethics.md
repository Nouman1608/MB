This guide covers outcome **1.6.4 Ethics in accounting analysis and decision making**. It forms the second part of Topic 1.6, Social and ethical accounting, in Unit 1 (The Accounting System and Costing) of the Pearson Edexcel International Advanced Subsidiary/Advanced Level in Accounting (YAC11), Specification Issue 2, September 2018. This is International AS content. All amounts are in dollars and every business and person named is invented.

Use it with the [social and ethical accounting revision notes](/resources/edexcel-ial-accounting-social-and-ethical-accounting-revision-notes/) and the [ethics practice questions](/resources/edexcel-a-level-accounting-yac11-ethics-practice/). The wider social effects of decisions are in the [social accounting guide](/resources/edexcel-a-level-accounting-yac11-social-accounting/).

## What the specification asks for

| Outcome | Wording | What you must be able to do |
|---|---|---|
| 1.6.4 | Ethics in accounting analysis and decision making | Recognise an ethical issue, explain why it matters, and recommend and justify a course of action |

The specification gives a single statement, so exam questions can set any realistic accounting situation. The skills are the same in every case: spot the issue, name who is affected, and use accounting concepts and ethical principles to reach a reasoned judgement.

## What ethics means in accounting

**Ethics** is the set of moral principles that decide what is right and wrong. In accounting it covers how information is prepared, presented and used. Ethics is not the same as the law. An action can be legal but unethical, such as presenting figures in a misleading way that breaks no rule. An action can also be illegal, such as fraud, which is unethical as well.

Accountants are trusted by owners, lenders, employees and the public to give reliable information. If the information is distorted, people make poor decisions. The profession therefore expects fair and honest behaviour.

## Ethical principles used in accounting

These principles are widely accepted in professional codes of conduct for accountants. The specification does not list them, but they are the usual way to describe good ethical practice.

| Principle | Meaning | Example of a breach |
|---|---|---|
| **Integrity** | Being straightforward and honest | Knowingly including a false figure |
| **Objectivity** | Not letting bias or pressure affect judgement | Valuing inventory high because the owner asks |
| **Professional competence and care** | Having the skill and taking proper care | Preparing accounts without checking the figures |
| **Confidentiality** | Not disclosing private information without permission | Telling a rival about a client's profit |
| **Professional behaviour** | Following the law and avoiding actions that damage the profession | Helping a client avoid tax illegally |

## Common ethical situations

1. **Profit manipulation.** Reporting a higher profit by treating revenue expenditure as capital, overstating closing inventory, ignoring irrecoverable debts or delaying expenses.
2. **Understating profit** to pay less tax or to hide profit from partners.
3. **Pressure from an owner or manager** to change figures before the accounts are shown to a lender.
4. **Conflict of interest.** An accountant advising two clients whose interests are opposed, or having a personal interest in the decision.
5. **Confidentiality.** Being asked to reveal client information.
6. **Gifts and bribes.** Accepting a gift that could influence a decision.
7. **Disclosure.** Hiding a liability such as a legal claim or a loan.
8. **Window dressing.** Arranging transactions around the year end to improve the look of the statement of financial position.

## How these distort the accounts: worked examples

**Case A: capitalising repairs.** A business has an honest profit for the year of 26,000. The owner wants to show a larger profit to impress the bank, so he asks the bookkeeper to record 12,000 of repairs to the building as an addition to the building cost (capital expenditure). Depreciation at 10% is 1,200.

Reported profit = 26,000 + 12,000 − 1,200 = **36,800**.

That is 10,800 higher than the honest figure, an increase of 41.5% (10,800 ÷ 26,000). Non-current assets are overstated by 10,800 at the year end. The repairs were revenue expenditure, so treating them as capital breaks the accounting rule (see outcome 1.1.12) and misleads the bank. The effects: the bank may lend more than it would otherwise, and the owner may withdraw more profit than the business has earned.

**Case B: overvaluing inventory.** Closing inventory is 5,000 higher than its true value. Profit for the year is overstated by 5,000 (from 26,000 to 31,000, a rise of 19.2%). Current assets are 45,000 instead of 40,000, and current liabilities are 20,000. The current ratio is shown as 45,000 : 20,000 = 2.25 : 1 instead of the true 40,000 : 20,000 = 2.00 : 1. The business looks more liquid than it is. In the next year the opening inventory is too high, so profit that year is understated by the same 5,000. The error corrects itself over two years, but it misleads users in the first.

**Case C: concealing a liability.** If a legal claim against the business of 30,000 is likely to succeed and no provision is made, profit and net assets are both overstated by 30,000. The prudence concept requires the provision.

## Link to accounting concepts

Ethical behaviour is supported by the same concepts as other topics:

- **Prudence** stops profit being overstated.
- **Accruals** requires income and expenses to be matched to the right period.
- **Consistency** stops the policy being changed only to improve results.
- **Materiality** allows small items to be treated simply, but is not an excuse to ignore large ones.
- **Going concern** requires doubt about survival to be disclosed.

## A framework for evaluating an ethical issue

1. **Identify the facts.** What is the situation and what is being asked?
2. **Identify the issue.** Which principle is under threat: integrity, objectivity, confidentiality?
3. **List the stakeholders** and say how each is affected, now and later.
4. **Consider the options**, such as refusing, raising it with a senior person, getting advice, or resigning.
5. **Apply concepts and principles.** Does the proposal break accounting rules? Would it mislead anyone?
6. **Decide and justify** your course of action, saying what you would do and why. Consider the consequences of each option.

**Example.** Priya, the bookkeeper, is told by the owner to include 5,000 of inventory that does not exist, so that the accounts meet a bank condition. Priya should refuse to include it (integrity). She should explain that the figure would breach the rules for valuing inventory and could lead to prosecution for her and the owner. She should document the request and, if the owner persists, seek advice from a professional body or a senior adviser. The cost to Priya might be losing her job, but the cost of taking part could be loss of professional standing and legal liability. The bank, other creditors and the owner's own long-term interest all suffer from misleading information.

## Why ethical behaviour is also good for business

- Users trust the accounts, so the business can raise finance more easily.
- Reputation builds customer and supplier trust.
- The risk of penalties and legal claims falls.
- Honest figures give better information for decisions.

An ethical decision may cost money in the short term, for example turning down a contract won through a bribe. The long-term cost of unethical behaviour is usually greater.

## Common mistakes

- Treating ethics and legality as the same thing.
- Describing an ethical principle without applying it to the case.
- Writing only about the effect on the owner, without other stakeholders.
- Recommending "report it" without saying to whom or what the accountant should do first.
- Failing to link the issue to accounting concepts, such as prudence.
- Giving a one-sided answer that ignores the pressure on the accountant.
- Saying the decision is "obviously wrong" without justification.

## Exam technique

- Quote the case: name the person, the request and the figures.
- **Explain** (why a behaviour is unethical): state the principle, link it to the case, give a consequence.
- **Evaluate** or **recommend**: weigh at least two courses of action, name the stakeholders, give a decision and a reason.
- Use figures where possible. "Profit would be overstated by 10,800" is stronger than "profit would be too high".
- Write in a calm, professional tone and avoid accusing people without evidence. Say "this appears to".
