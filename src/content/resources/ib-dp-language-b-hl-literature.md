This study guide teaches the **HL literature strand** of IB Diploma Programme Language B: reading
the two literary works HL students study, discussing an extract, and linking a work to the
prescribed themes, with worked links to Identities, Social organization and Sharing the planet. It
follows the IB Language B subject brief for first assessment 2020, which remains the examined course
until the new syllabus is first assessed in May 2029, so it applies to the 2026, 2027 and 2028
sessions. This unit is **HL only**: the brief makes literary works a requirement at HL and not at SL.
It is written in English; apply each method to the works you read in your target language.

Use it with the [HL literature revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-language-b-hl-literature-revision-notes/)
and the [HL literature practice questions](/resources/ib-dp-language-b-hl-literature-practice/). For
the whole course, see the [Language B course hub](/boards/ib/ib-dp/language-b/) and the
[printable Language B checklist](/checklists/ib/ib-dp/language-b/).

## What the brief says

The brief gives a small number of facts about literature at HL. Learn them exactly:

- "At HL the study of two literary works originally written in the target language is required."
  The works are not translations into your target language.
- HL students "are expected to extend the range and complexity of the language they use and
  understand".
- The **individual oral** is internally assessed, worth 25% (30 marks). At SL its stimulus is a
  visual image. At HL "the stimulus ... is an excerpt from one of the two literary works studied".
- The course is organised around five prescribed themes. This page works through three of them.

The brief does not describe how the oral runs, how long it lasts or how it is marked. Your teacher
will share the format, timings and assessment criteria. The excerpt can come from either work, so
you need to know **both** well.

## Reading a whole work in a second language

A literary work is longer and denser than most texts you meet in class. Read it in passes.

1. **First pass, for the story.** Read quickly without looking up every word. After each chapter,
   write three target-language sentences: what happened, who changed, what is unclear.
2. **Second pass, for detail.** Re-read with a dictionary. Note recurring words and phrases that
   sound deliberate.
3. **Third pass, for key passages.** Mark 10 to 12 passages per work: openings, turning points,
   confrontations, endings, and moments that show a theme clearly.

Keep a **reading log** for each work. One row per chapter or section:

| Section | What happens | Characters and relationships | Key quotation (short) | Theme link |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ch. 1 | ... | ... | ... | ... |

Add two support pages: a **character map** (names, relationships, how each person changes) and a
**timeline** (events in order, with chapter numbers). These let you locate any excerpt in seconds.

Build vocabulary by function: feelings, places, conflict, and verbs of speaking (*whispers*,
*insists*, *admits*).

## Tools for analysing a literary text

These are standard features of literary texts. Each gives you something to say beyond "what
happens".

| Feature | Question to ask | Useful sentence frame |
|---|---|---|
| Narrator and point of view | Who tells the story? First person or third? How much do they know? | "Because the story is told by..., we only see..." |
| Characterisation | Is the character described directly, or shown through actions and speech? | "Her silence here reveals..." |
| Setting | Where and when? Does the place reflect a mood or a society? | "The unchanged kitchen contrasts with..." |
| Dialogue | Who speaks, who stays silent, who has the last word? | "The grandmother's reply shows who holds authority..." |
| Imagery | Which comparisons (simile, metaphor) or sense details stand out? | "The simile of the coat suggests..." |
| Symbol and motif | Does an object or image recur and carry meaning? | "The greeting becomes a symbol of..." |
| Structure | Where does the extract sit? Is it a turning point? | "Coming just after..., this moment..." |
| Tone | Is the voice ironic, tender, bitter, detached? | "The ironic tone makes the reader question..." |

Always use the pattern **feature, quotation, effect**. Naming a simile scores nothing on its own;
explaining what the simile makes the reader understand does.

## Discussing an extract: a five-step method

Use these steps whenever you meet an extract from one of your works.

1. **Locate.** Where does it come from? What has just happened, and what follows?
2. **Summarise briefly.** One or two sentences only.
3. **Analyse.** Choose two or three features. Quote a short phrase for each and explain its effect.
4. **Connect to the whole work.** Show how this moment fits a character's development or a
   recurring idea.
5. **Link to a theme.** Name the prescribed theme and use one of its possible questions as a lens.
   Add a cultural perspective where the work supports one.

Steps 3 to 5 are where you show understanding. Step 2 is the step to keep short.

### Worked example

Imagine this extract comes from the middle of a novel about a young woman's first year away at
university.

> *Original text written for Marlbridge*
>
> When Anya came back from the city, she said "hi" at the gate instead of the old greeting. Her
> grandmother put down the bucket and waited, as if the word had not yet arrived. "In this house,"
> she said at last, "we greet the door before we greet the people." Anya laughed, then stopped.
> Behind her grandmother, the kitchen was exactly as she had left it: the blue cups on their hooks,
> the radio that only found one station. She said the old words quietly, and they felt too big for
> her mouth, like a coat she had outgrown -- or had not yet grown into.

**Step 1, locate.** Anya has spent a year in the city. This is her first return home, so it is a
test of whether she has changed.

**Step 2, summarise.** Anya greets her grandmother in a casual, city way and is corrected. She then
uses the traditional greeting.

**Step 3, analyse.**

- **Figurative language:** the grandmother waits "as if the word had not yet arrived". The phrase
  treats "hi" as something foreign that cannot enter the house. It shows the gap between the two
  women without either of them saying so.
- **Dialogue and authority:** the grandmother's line is a rule: "In this house, we greet the door
  before we greet the people." She speaks for the household, not only for herself.
- **Setting as contrast:** the kitchen is "exactly as she had left it". The place has not changed,
  which makes Anya's change more visible.
- **Simile:** the old words feel "like a coat she had outgrown -- or had not yet grown into". The
  dash turns one idea into two. Either Anya has moved beyond her home, or she is not yet ready for
  its traditions. The ending leaves both open.

**Step 4, connect.** In the rest of the work you would track whether Anya keeps using the old words,
and whether her grandmother ever accepts the new ones.

**Step 5, link.** The clearest link is **Identities**, through the recommended topic *language and
identity* and the possible question "How do language and culture contribute to form our identity?"
The greeting is not only politeness; it marks who belongs. A second link is **Social organization**:
the household rule shows how a small community sets its own conventions.

Notice that only two short sentences of the answer are summary. The rest is analysis and linking.

## Linking literature to the three themes

The brief gives each theme a guiding principle, optional recommended topics and possible questions.
Use them as lenses on your works.

### Identities

- **Guiding principle:** "Explore the nature of the self and what it is to be human."
- **Topics:** lifestyles; health and well-being; beliefs and values; subcultures; language and
  identity.
- **Possible questions:** "What constitutes an identity?" and "How do language and culture
  contribute to form our identity?"
- **Look for:** characters torn between two groups, changes of name or language, conflicts of
  belief, and how a narrator describes themselves.

### Social organization

- **Guiding principle:** "Explore the ways in which groups of people organize themselves, or are
  organized, through common systems or interests."
- **Topics:** social relationships; community; social engagement; education; the working world;
  law and order.
- **Possible questions:** "What is the individual's role in the community?" and "What role do rules
  and regulations play in the formation of a society?"
- **Look for:** schools, workplaces, families and villages as systems; who makes the rules; who
  obeys and who resists.

### Sharing the planet

- **Guiding principle:** "Explore the challenges and opportunities faced by individuals and
  communities in the modern world."
- **Topics:** the environment; human rights; peace and conflict; equality; globalization; ethics;
  urban and rural environment.
- **Possible questions:** "What environmental and social issues present challenges to the world, and
  how can these challenges be overcome?" and "What challenges and benefits does globalization
  bring?"
- **Look for:** descriptions of land and nature, moves between town and country, injustice, war,
  and moral choices.

A work can link to more than one theme, including Experiences and Human ingenuity.

### Build a theme map for each work

| Theme | Key passages (chapter) | Possible question it answers | Target-language vocabulary |
|---|---|---|---|
| Identities | ... | ... | ... |
| Social organization | ... | ... | ... |
| Sharing the planet | ... | ... | ... |

Fill it in as you read, not in the final week. When you meet an unseen excerpt, the map tells you
which link to make.

## Range and complexity when you talk about literature

The brief expects HL students to extend range and complexity. When you discuss literature, that
means:

- **Precise verbs** instead of "says" or "shows": *suggests, implies, reveals, undermines,
  contrasts with, foreshadows*.
- **Complex sentences** with concession and cause: "Although Anya laughs, her silence afterwards
  suggests that..."
- **Hypothesis:** "If the grandmother had accepted the new greeting, the scene would lose its
  tension."
- **Hedging** for interpretations: "This could be read as...", "It seems that..."
- **Reference to the whole work:** "Later in the novel...", "Earlier, we saw..."

Learn the target-language equivalents of these frames and practise saying them aloud, since the
literary excerpt is the stimulus for your oral.

## Common errors

- **Retelling the plot.** Summary is step 2 of five, not the whole answer.
- **Naming a technique without its effect.** "There is a simile" earns nothing until you explain it.
- **Long quotations.** Quote a few words and comment on them; do not read out whole sentences.
- **Confusing narrator and author.** A first-person narrator's views are not automatically the
  writer's views.
- **Forcing a theme link.** Choose the theme the excerpt actually supports, and name a possible
  question.
- **Knowing only one work.** The excerpt can come from either work, so revise both.
- **Stereotyping a culture.** Base cultural comments on what the text shows, and hedge them.
