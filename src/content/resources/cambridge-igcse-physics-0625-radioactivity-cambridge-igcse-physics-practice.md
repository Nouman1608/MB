## Section A

**1.** Name **two** sources that make a significant contribution to background radiation. **[2]**

**2.** State the nature of gamma radiation. **[1]**

**3.** State which of alpha, beta and gamma has the strongest ionising effect. **[1]**

**4.** A counter records 1500 counts in 2.5 minutes.

**(a)** Calculate the count rate in counts/minute. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the count rate in counts/s. **[1]**

**5.** Radioactive decay is random. State what this means. **[1]**

## Section B

**6.** A student places absorbers between a radioactive source and a detector. Background radiation is small and can be ignored.

| Absorber | Count rate / counts per minute |
|---|---|
| none | 480 |
| sheet of paper | 470 |
| 3 mm of aluminium | 250 |
| 2 cm of lead | 60 |

**(a)** State which types of radiation the source emits, giving a reason for each type you include or rule out. **[3]**
**(b)** Suggest why the reading with paper is slightly lower than with no absorber. **[1]**

**7.** The table shows the count rate from a sample of one isotope. Background radiation can be ignored.

| Time / minutes | 0 | 2 | 4 | 6 | 8 | 10 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Count rate / counts per minute | 800 | 566 | 400 | 283 | 200 | 141 |

**(a)** Determine the half-life of the isotope. **[2]**
**(b)** Predict the count rate at 16 minutes. **[1]**
**(c)** State the fraction of the original undecayed nuclei that remain after 12 minutes. **[1]**

**8.** This question is about radioactive decay.

**(a)** State what is meant by radioactive decay. **[2]**
**(b)** State which type of emission does **not** change the nucleus into a different element. **[1]**

**9.** This question is about safety.

**(a)** State **two** effects of ionising nuclear radiation on living things. **[2]**
**(b)** Describe **two** ways in which a radioactive source in a school is stored safely. **[2]**

**10.** *(Extended)* Thorium-232, ²³²₉₀Th, decays by emitting an alpha particle to form an isotope of radium (Ra). That radium nucleus then emits a beta particle to form an isotope of actinium (Ac).

**(a)** Write the equation for the alpha decay. **[2]**
**(b)** Write the equation for the beta decay. **[2]**
**(c)** Suggest why thorium-232 is unstable. **[1]**
**(d)** State the effect of beta decay on the number of excess neutrons in the nucleus. **[1]**

**11.** *(Extended)* This question is about the properties of alpha and beta particles.

**(a)** A beam of alpha and beta particles passes, in a vacuum, between two parallel plates. The upper plate is positive and the lower plate is negative. Describe the paths of the two types of particle. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain, with reference to kinetic energy and charge, why alpha particles are more strongly ionising than beta particles. **[2]**

**12.** *(Extended)* The readings below include background radiation. The background count rate is 30 counts per minute.

| Time / days | 0 | 2.0 | 4.0 | 6.0 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Count rate / counts per minute | 990 | 510 | 270 | 150 |

**(a)** Calculate the corrected count rate at time 0. **[1]**
**(b)** Determine the half-life of the source. **[2]**
**(c)** Predict the reading the detector would show at 8.0 days. **[2]**

**13.** *(Extended)* This question is about using radioactive isotopes.

**(a)** Sealed plastic packs of syringes are sterilised with radiation. Explain why gamma radiation is used rather than alpha or beta. **[2]**
**(b)** A factory rolls aluminium foil. A source on one side of the foil and a detector on the other monitor its thickness. State and explain which type of radiation should be used, and state a suitable half-life. **[3]**
**(c)** A technician stands behind a thick lead screen when using a gamma source. State which of time, distance or shielding this precaution uses. **[1]**

## Answers

**1.** Any two: radon gas (in the air); rocks and buildings; food and drink; cosmic rays [1 each]. **[2]**

**2.** **Electromagnetic radiation** (an electromagnetic wave) [1]. **[1]**

**3.** **Alpha** [1]. **[1]**

**4. (a)** 1500 ÷ 2.5 = **600 counts/minute** [1].
**(b)** 600 ÷ 60 = **10 counts/s** [1]. **[2]**

**5.** It is impossible to predict which nucleus will decay next, or when [1]. **[1]**

**6. (a)** No **alpha**: the paper causes almost no drop [1]. **Beta** is present: the aluminium causes a large drop [1]. **Gamma** is present: a large count rate passes through the aluminium and is only reduced by the lead [1].
**(b)** Radioactive emission is random, so repeated counts vary slightly [1]. **[4]**

*Common mistake:* concluding that alpha is present because the paper reading is 10 lower. A drop that small is within random variation; alpha would cause a large drop.

**7. (a)** 800 → 400 at 4 minutes (or 400 → 200 from 4 to 8 minutes) [1]; half-life = **4.0 minutes** [1].
**(b)** 16 minutes is 4 half-lives: 800 → 400 → 200 → 100 → **50 counts/minute** [1].
**(c)** 12 minutes is 3 half-lives: **1/8** [1]. **[4]**

**8. (a)** A change in an unstable nucleus [1] that results in the emission of alpha particles, beta particles and/or gamma radiation, happening spontaneously and randomly [1].
**(b)** **Gamma** emission [1]. **[3]**

**9. (a)** Any two: cell death; mutations; cancer [1 each].
**(b)** Any two: in a lead-lined box (container); in a locked cupboard; with a warning label; away from where people work [1 each]. **[4]**

**10. (a)** ²³²₉₀Th → **²²⁸₈₈Ra** + ⁴₂He: correct alpha symbol [1]; correct radium nuclide [1].
**(b)** ²²⁸₈₈Ra → **²²⁸₈₉Ac** + ⁰₋₁e: correct beta symbol [1]; correct actinium nuclide [1].
**(c)** The nucleus is too heavy (and has excess neutrons) [1].
**(d)** It reduces the number of excess neutrons (a neutron becomes a proton) [1]. **[6]**

*Common mistake:* in (b), lowering Z by 1. In beta decay Z rises by 1 because a neutron becomes a proton.

**11. (a)** Alpha particles bend towards the negative (lower) plate [1]; beta particles bend towards the positive (upper) plate [1]; beta particles are deflected more than alpha particles [1].
**(b)** Alpha particles have a larger charge (+2 compared with −1) [1] and a large kinetic energy, so they interact more strongly with the atoms they pass and cause more ionisation along a shorter path [1]. **[5]**

**12. (a)** 990 − 30 = **960 counts/minute** [1].
**(b)** Corrected values: 960, 480, 240, 120 [1]; the corrected rate halves every 2.0 days, so half-life = **2.0 days** [1].
**(c)** Corrected rate at 8.0 days = 60 counts/minute [1]; reading = 60 + 30 = **90 counts/minute** [1]. **[5]**

*Common mistake:* in (c), giving 60. The detector still records the background, so add it back.

**13. (a)** Gamma radiation passes through the plastic packaging to reach the syringes [1]; alpha and beta would be absorbed by the packaging [1].
**(b)** **Beta** [1]; it is partly absorbed by thin aluminium, so the count rate changes when the thickness changes (alpha would be stopped completely; gamma would pass almost unaffected) [1]; a **long** half-life, so the count rate stays steady and the source does not need frequent replacing [1].
**(c)** **Shielding** [1]. **[6]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Treating a small drop in count rate as evidence of alpha.
- Halving the time instead of the count rate.
- Forgetting to subtract (and later add back) the background.
- Lowering the proton number in a beta-decay equation.
- Saying alpha is deflected more than beta.
- Choosing an isotope without a half-life reason.
