> **These are original questions written for Marlbridge** on the class practicals of IB DP Psychology (first assessment 2027). They are not past-paper questions. Question 8 is built on the pattern of the Paper 2 Section A parts (4, 4, 6 and 6 marks); the other tariffs are practice tariffs. The study summary is constructed for practice.

Related: [Class practicals study guide](/resources/ib-dp-psychology-2027-class-practicals/) and [revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-psychology-2027-class-practicals-revision-notes/).

## Section A

**1.** State the method that students must use in the class practical for the human development context. **[1]**

**2.** State the minimum number of participants for a student-led survey/questionnaire. **[1]**

**3.** Distinguish a true experiment from a quasi-experiment. **[3]**

**4.** Describe stratified sampling and give one advantage. **[3]**

**5.** Outline two ethical considerations for a naturalistic observation in a public place. **[4]**

## Section B

**6.** Read the summary (constructed for practice).

> A teacher wants to run a class practical on how students manage stress. She plans to ask a group of six students from her class to discuss, in front of a recorder, "What do you do when you feel under pressure?" One student, Ana, usually speaks first and the others often agree with her. The teacher plans to give a consent form to each student, and says that she will store the recording on her personal phone.

**(a)** Identify the research method the teacher is using. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain one strength and one limitation of the method for this topic. **[4]**
**(c)** Identify two problems with the teacher's plan for ethics or data protection and suggest an improvement for each. **[4]**

**7.** Explain how the concept of **bias** could apply to a naturalistic observation. **[3]**

## Section C

**8.** A class completed a health and well-being practical using a semi-structured interview.

**(a)** Describe the semi-structured interview as a research method, using correct psychological terminology. **[4]**
**(b)** Explain how the concept of **measurement** applied to the practical. **[4]**
**(c)** Compare and contrast a semi-structured interview with a survey/questionnaire. **[6]**
**(d)** Design a survey/questionnaire study to investigate whether first-year students sleep fewer hours than final-year students. Describe the procedure. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** Observation (naturalistic or controlled, overt or covert, participant or non-participant) **[1]**.

**2.** 10 participants **[1]**.

**3.** In a true experiment the researcher manipulates the independent variable **[1]** and allocates participants to conditions at random **[1]**. In a quasi-experiment the groups already exist (for example age groups or cultures) so allocation is not random and the independent variable is not manipulated, which makes causal claims weaker **[1]**.

**4.** The population is divided into subgroups (strata), such as year groups **[1]**, and participants are selected from each stratum in proportion to its size **[1]**. Advantage: the sample is more representative of the population than an opportunity or self-selected sample **[1]**.

**5.** Any two, two marks each: participants cannot give informed consent, so the observation should only take place where people expect to be seen by strangers, such as a street or shopping centre **[2]**; participants cannot be debriefed, so no identifying information should be recorded and individuals must not be followed or filmed **[2]**; confidentiality and anonymity of notes and data **[2]**; no observation that causes distress or involves private behaviour **[2]**.

**6.** (a) A focus group (interview) **[1]**.

(b) Strength, any one: the discussion can reveal how students talk about and influence each other, giving rich qualitative data **[1]**, which suits an everyday topic such as managing stress **[1]**. Limitation, any one: a dominant member (Ana) may lead and others may conform **[1]**, so answers may not reflect what each student really does **[1]**.

(c) Any two problems, two marks each (problem, improvement). Consent: giving only a form is not enough; explain the aim, the right to withdraw at any time and that the recording is used only for the class practical **[1]**; consent must be explicit and participants must not be pressured, so allow a student to decline without penalty **[1]**. Storage: a personal phone is not secure and may be shared; use a school device or a locked file and delete the recording when the work is complete **[2]**. Anonymity: use codes instead of names in the transcript **[2]**. Credit also the risk that students feel pressure to disclose; keep the question to coping strategies, not private problems.

**7.** Any three points: observer bias, because the observer may see what they expect and record behaviour in line with their hypothesis **[1]**; a behavioural checklist with clear categories reduces this **[1]**; having a second observer and calculating inter-rater reliability allows the researcher to check for bias **[1]**. Also creditable: participant behaviour is unaffected in a naturalistic covert setting, which reduces participant bias but increases ethical concern.

**8.** (a) A semi-structured interview has a prepared list of open questions **[1]** but the interviewer can ask follow-up questions **[1]**. It produces mainly qualitative data **[1]** which is usually recorded and analysed using thematic analysis **[1]**.

(b) Measurement: constructs must be operationalised, for example "stress management" as the strategies a student reports using in the last month **[1]**. The interview gives indirect, self-reported data **[1]**, which depends on honesty and memory **[1]**. Triangulation with a questionnaire would improve credibility **[1]**.

(c) Similarities, up to 2: both collect self-reported data; both are open to social desirability bias; both need informed consent and confidentiality. Differences, up to 3: an interview gives detailed qualitative data from few people while a survey gives mainly quantitative data from many; an interviewer can clarify and probe but may bias answers, while a survey is standardised but cannot correct misunderstanding; interview analysis is subjective and slower, survey data are easier to analyse statistically. Judgement or use of terminology, 1. Total **[6]**.

(d) A full-mark design includes the following, one mark each up to six. Hypothesis or aim: first-year students report fewer hours of sleep than final-year students **[1]**. The method (a survey) and a sampling technique, such as stratified sampling by year group, with a stated sample size of at least 10 **[1]**. The variables: year group (independent variable) and self-reported hours of sleep per night (dependent variable), operationalised as an average over the past week **[1]**. Materials and procedure: a short questionnaire with identical wording, the same instructions, administered in a form period **[1]**. Ethics: consent form, right to withdraw, anonymity, secure storage and a debrief; parental consent where participants are under 16 **[1]**. Analysis: compare means or medians and show a bar graph; note that the design is quasi-experimental and cannot show cause **[1]**.

## Where marks are usually lost

- **Question 3:** saying the quasi-experiment is "not an experiment" instead of explaining random allocation and manipulation.
- **Question 6(c):** noting that consent is needed without saying what the plan lacks.
- **Question 8(d):** describing the topic or the likely results instead of the steps, sampling and ethics.
