## Questions

### Section A

**1.** The division of labour means: **[1]**

A: A country producing only the goods it is best at.

B: Splitting a production process into separate tasks done by different workers.

C: Sharing out income between workers and owners.

D: Workers moving between firms to find better pay.

**2.** The main problem of a barter economy is the need for: **[1]**

A: A central bank.

B: A double coincidence of wants.

C: Specialist equipment.

D: A stable rate of inflation.

**3.** A shop displays the price of a jacket as £45. Which function of money does this show? **[1]**

A: Medium of exchange.

B: Store of value.

C: Measure of value.

D: Standard of deferred payment.

**4.** Which of the following is a disadvantage of the division of labour? **[1]**

A: Workers become quicker at their task.

B: Specialist machinery can be used.

C: Repetitive work can reduce motivation.

D: Training for each task is shorter.

**5.** Explain two reasons why the division of labour raises labour productivity. **[4]**

**6.** A car-valeting team of five workers each valet 6 cars a day. After the work is divided into separate tasks, the team valets 45 cars a day. Each worker is paid £90 a day. Calculate the change in output per worker and the change in labour cost per car. **[3]**

### Section B

**7.** In one day, Kofi can catch 12 fish or gather 6 coconuts. Lena can catch 4 fish or gather 8 coconuts.

**(a)** Calculate total output if each spends half the day on each good. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate total output if each specialises completely in the good they produce best. **[1]**

**(c)** Explain why the gains from specialisation depend on trade. **[3]**

**8.** Explain how money's functions as a medium of exchange and as a store of value each support specialisation. **[4]**

**9.** *Extract (invented).* The town of Ashby grew around a single car factory, which employs a third of local workers. Many local firms supply parts to it. The factory's owners are considering moving production abroad.

Using the extract, explain one benefit and one risk of regional specialisation for Ashby. **[4]**

**10.** An economy has 8 goods and no money. Calculate the number of exchange rates needed and explain why money reduces the cost of trade. **[3]**

**11.** Explain why very high inflation could lead people to return to barter. **[3]**

**12.** Evaluate the view that specialisation and the division of labour are the most effective way to address the problem of scarcity. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** **B** [1]. Option A describes specialisation by a country. **[1]**

**2.** **B** [1]. **[1]**

**3.** **C** [1]. The price tag expresses the jacket's value in money units. **[1]**

**4.** **C** [1]. The other options are advantages. **[1]**

**5.** Workers repeat one task, so they build skill and speed through practice [1], which raises output per hour [1]. Less time is lost moving between tasks and tools [1], so more of each working hour is spent producing [1]. Other acceptable reasons: specialist equipment, matching skills to tasks. **[4]**

**6.** Output per worker rises from 6 to 45 / 5 = 9 cars a day, a rise of 50% [1]. Daily wage bill = 5 x 90 = £450 [1]. Labour cost per car falls from 450 / 30 = £15 to 450 / 45 = **£10** [1]. **[3]**

**7. (a)** Kofi: 6 fish and 3 coconuts; Lena: 2 fish and 4 coconuts [1]. Total = **8 fish and 7 coconuts** [1].

**(b)** Kofi catches 12 fish and Lena gathers 8 coconuts: **12 fish and 8 coconuts** [1].

**(c)** After specialising, total output is higher by 4 fish and 1 coconut [1], but Kofi has no coconuts and Lena has no fish [1]. Each can enjoy a mix of both goods, and share in the extra output, only if they exchange part of what they produce [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the totals from (a) and (b) to show the size of the gain before explaining why it needs trade.

**8.** As a medium of exchange, money is accepted in payment for anything [1], so a specialist can sell their output to one person and buy what they need from others, without a double coincidence of wants [1]. As a store of value, money keeps purchasing power over time [1], so a specialist can sell output when it is ready and spend the proceeds later, which matters when production is seasonal [1]. **[4]**

**9.** Benefit: workers and local firms build specialist skills and knowledge around car production [1], so productivity is high and suppliers gain from being close to their main customer [1]. Risk: a third of jobs depend on one factory [1], so if production moves abroad, unemployment would rise sharply and the parts suppliers would lose sales too [1]. **[4]**

**10.** 8 x 7 / 2 = **28** exchange rates [1]. With money, each good needs only one price, 8 in total [1]. Fewer prices to learn and compare, and no need to find a double coincidence of wants, make trade quicker and cheaper [1]. **[3]**

**11.** High inflation erodes the purchasing power of money [1], so it no longer works well as a store of value [1]. Sellers may refuse to accept money that loses value quickly, so people swap goods directly instead [1]. **[3]**

**12.** For: specialisation raises output per worker, as in the valeting example where productivity rose 50% [1], so more wants can be met from the same scarce resources [1]. It allows the use of specialist capital and lowers cost per unit [1]. With money and trade, specialisation spreads across firms, regions and countries, multiplying the gains [1]. Against: specialisation does not add to the quantity of resources, so it eases rather than solves scarcity [1]. It brings risks: dependence on one industry, as in Ashby [1], and monotony that can reduce motivation [1]. Other approaches matter, such as new technology, investment in capital and education, and using unemployed resources [1]. Judgement: specialisation is among the most powerful ways to get more from scarce resources [1], but its benefits depend on trade, stable money and avoiding over-dependence [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* Linking back to earlier figures and the extract shows application, and a judgement that names conditions earns the final marks.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Confusing specialisation by country with the division of labour.
- Choosing "store of value" for a price tag.
- Forgetting to divide the wage bill by output for labour cost per car.
- Not using the figures from part (a) and (b) in question 7(c).
- Describing barter problems without saying how money solves them.
- Ending the essay without a judgement.
