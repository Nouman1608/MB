## Profit and cash flow are different measures

**Cash flow** is the money that actually enters and leaves a business during a period. **Profit** is sales revenue minus costs for the period, recorded when the sale or cost is earned or incurred, not when money moves. The guide says a profit and loss account is also called an income statement, and that assessment uses the term "statement of profit or loss". For the balance sheet, assessment uses "statement of financial position".

Profit and cash flow differ for four main reasons.

1. **Credit sales.** The sale is revenue now. The cash arrives later.
2. **Credit purchases.** The cost is in profit now. The cash leaves later.
3. **Non-current asset purchases.** Buying equipment takes cash out at once. Profit only falls by depreciation each year.
4. **Non-cash costs.** Depreciation lowers profit but no money leaves. Loan repayments and some stock purchases move cash without being an expense of the period.

### Worked example 1: one month at Ferris Hollow Tiles

Ferris Hollow Tiles fits floor tiles for homes. Figures for March are in dollars. Sales of 48,000 were all on credit, and customers pay after about two months. Materials used cost 20,000, wages 12,000 and rent and utilities 4,000, all paid in March. Depreciation was 1,500. A tile-cutting machine was bought for 9,000 cash. Customers paid 30,000 in March for jobs done in January.

Profit for March:

**48,000 - (20,000 + 12,000 + 4,000 + 1,500) = 10,500**

Net cash flow for March:

| Item | Cash in | Cash out |
|---|---|---|
| Receipts from customers | 30,000 | |
| Materials | | 20,000 |
| Wages | | 12,000 |
| Rent and utilities | | 4,000 |
| Machine | | 9,000 |
| Total | 30,000 | 45,000 |

Net cash flow = 30,000 - 45,000 = **-15,000**.

So a profit of 10,500 sits beside a cash outflow of 15,000. A profit-to-cash bridge (a teaching extension, going a little beyond the guide's wording) shows why: start with profit 10,500, add back depreciation 1,500 (no cash left), take off 18,000 because receivables rose (48,000 earned but only 30,000 received), and take off the 9,000 machine. That gives -15,000. The business is profitable and still short of cash, which is what the guide's inquiry statement on profitable businesses facing insolvency points to.

## Working capital

**Working capital** is the pool of funds that keeps day-to-day trading going. The standard form is:

**working capital = current assets - current liabilities**

The guide's formulae appendix, as read for this page, gives the current ratio and acid test ratio but no separate working capital formula, so treat this as the standard form. Current assets are items expected to turn into cash within a year (stock, trade receivables, cash). Current liabilities are debts due within a year (trade payables, overdraft, tax owed).

Positive working capital means short-term assets cover short-term debts. Too little risks missing payments. Too much is also a problem, because cash and stock sitting idle earn nothing and could have funded investment or been returned to owners.

### Worked example 2: Ferris Hollow Tiles at 31 March

Current assets: stock 26,000; trade receivables 54,000; cash 3,000. Current liabilities: trade payables 31,000; overdraft 12,000; tax owed 8,000.

- Current assets = 83,000 and current liabilities = 51,000.
- Working capital = 83,000 - 51,000 = **32,000**.

Working capital is a cash-style cushion on paper, but 54,000 of it is money owed by customers, not money in the bank.

## Liquidity position

**Liquidity** is how readily a business can find the cash to settle debts when they come due within the year. A business's **liquidity position** is how well placed it is to do that. Three measures help you judge it. Two come from 3.5, where the guide gives the formulae:

**current ratio = current assets / current liabilities**

The second ratio removes stock, the slowest current asset to turn into cash:

**acid test (quick) ratio = (current assets - stock) / current liabilities**

Continuing example 2:

- Current ratio = 83,000 / 51,000 = **1.63**.
- Acid test ratio = (83,000 - 26,000) / 51,000 = 57,000 / 51,000 = **1.12**.

The third measure is the cash balance itself and the forecast. Ferris Hollow has only 3,000 cash against 12,000 overdraft and 31,000 owed to suppliers. The ratios look healthy because receivables are large. If customers pay late, liquidity is weaker than the ratios suggest. A fair judgement says the ratios are acceptable, but the position depends on collecting 54,000 on time.

## Cash flow forecasts

A **cash flow forecast** predicts cash inflows and outflows for future periods, usually month by month. The guide includes a cash flow forecast layout in its presentation of financial statements. The layout is not reproduced in the text used for this page, so the rows below are the standard form. Check them against the figure in your own copy of the guide.

| Row | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Opening balance | Cash at the start of the month (equals last month's closing balance) |
| Cash inflows | Receipts: cash sales, payments from customers, loans received, assets sold |
| Cash outflows | Payments made: staff pay, suppliers, premises costs, machinery, loan instalments |
| Net cash flow | Inflows less outflows for the month |
| Closing balance | Opening balance with that month's net cash flow added |

Only cash movements go in. Depreciation never appears. A credit sale is entered only when the customer settles.

### Worked example 3: Mistral Bay Kayaks

Mistral Bay Kayaks hires kayaks on a coastline. Figures are in dollars. The owner begins March with 6,000 cash.

| | Mar | Apr | May | Jun |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opening balance | 6,000 | 1,300 | -11,800 | -3,700 |
| Cash hire receipts | 3,000 | 9,000 | 15,000 | 22,000 |
| Group booking receipts | 2,000 | 1,500 | 4,000 | 6,000 |
| Total inflows | 5,000 | 10,500 | 19,000 | 28,000 |
| Wages | 3,500 | 5,000 | 7,500 | 9,000 |
| New kayaks | | 16,000 | | |
| Insurance | 4,200 | | | |
| Repairs and fuel | 1,200 | 1,800 | 2,600 | 3,100 |
| Loan repayment | 800 | 800 | 800 | 800 |
| Total outflows | 9,700 | 23,600 | 10,900 | 12,900 |
| Net cash flow | -4,700 | -13,100 | 8,100 | 15,100 |
| Closing balance | 1,300 | -11,800 | -3,700 | 11,400 |

Reading it: the business is overdrawn at the end of April and May, by 11,800 and 3,700. The cause is timing. The kayaks are bought before the busy season produces receipts. Recovery in June is large because summer receipts arrive.

Uses of a forecast: it shows when borrowing is needed, supports a loan application, and lets managers test a change before they make it. Its limits: it rests on assumptions about sales and payment dates, and weather, strikes or a late-paying customer can make it wrong. A forecast is a plan, not a record.

## Investment, profit and cash flow

**Investment** here means spending on assets (equipment, premises, stock) to earn returns later. The three measures move at different times.

- **Cash flow** falls when the money is paid, often all at once.
- **Profit** is hit only by the yearly depreciation charge, so the effect is spread over the asset's life. The guide covers how depreciation is calculated in 3.4, where the methods are HL only. Here you only need to know that the charge is non-cash.
- Later, if the investment works, it raises sales revenue, profit and cash inflows.

A business can therefore show rising profit and still be short of cash in the year it invests. In example 3, buying the kayaks cost 16,000 of cash in April. If they last four years and the yearly depreciation is 4,000, the profit effect in the year is 4,000, not 16,000. Investment in stock also ties up working capital before anything is sold.

## Strategies for dealing with cash flow problems

Choose the strategy that fits the cause. This is the AO3 skill. The guide names the point but does not list strategies, so the table below is standard teaching content, not a list taken from the guide. Overdrafts and leasing also appear in 3.2 as sources of finance.

| Strategy | How it helps | Drawback |
|---|---|---|
| Bank overdraft | Covers a short gap quickly | Interest can be high; the bank can withdraw it |
| Short-term loan | Gives a lump of cash with a fixed repayment plan | Interest, and repayments become outflows |
| Delay payments to suppliers | Keeps cash longer | Suppliers may refuse credit or raise prices |
| Chase receivables or shorten credit terms | Brings cash in sooner | Customers may buy elsewhere |
| Discount for early payment | Speeds receipts | Lowers revenue per sale |
| Debt factoring | Sells invoices for immediate cash | Fee; the business receives less than the invoice value |
| Sell and lease back an asset | Releases cash tied up in the asset | Leasing costs continue; ownership is lost |
| Lease instead of buy | Avoids a large up-front payment | Total cost can be higher |
| Reduce stock | Frees cash | Risk of stock-outs |
| Cut costs or delay investment | Lowers outflows | May harm quality or growth |

For Mistral Bay, leasing the kayaks at 1,100 a month from April would change the closing balances to 1,300, 3,100, 10,100 and 24,100, so the business is never overdrawn. Over many years the leasing could cost more than buying, and that trade-off is the evaluation point.

If the cause is losses rather than timing, short-term fixes only delay the problem. Say so.

## Common errors

- Putting depreciation in a cash flow forecast.
- Entering a credit sale in the month of sale rather than the month of payment.
- Starting a month with the wrong opening balance. It must equal last month's closing balance.
- Labelling a negative closing balance as a loss, when it signals a shortfall of cash.
- Recommending a long-term loan for a short gap, or an overdraft for a permanent loss.
- Treating a high current ratio as proof of good liquidity when most of it is stock or late-paying receivables.
