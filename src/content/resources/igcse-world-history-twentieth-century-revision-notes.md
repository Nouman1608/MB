Condensed recall notes for **Core Content Option B: The Twentieth
Century, 1919–1989**, Cambridge IGCSE World History 0470, 2024–2026
series. Use these alongside the full study guide on Option B for
explanation and evidence — these notes are for fast recall of the six
key questions in the final stretch of revision.

## Key question 1: Was the Treaty of Versailles fair?

- Three main peacemakers, three different aims: **Wilson** (USA) —
  Fourteen Points, self-determination; **Clemenceau** (France) —
  weaken Germany permanently for French security; **Lloyd George**
  (Britain) — firm enough for public opinion, not so harsh it
  destabilises Europe.
- Know the Treaty's key **terms**: territorial losses, military
  restrictions, reparations, the War Guilt Clause.
- Know its **social, economic and political impact on Germany to the
  end of 1923** specifically — this is the exact window the syllabus
  sets.
- Be ready to weigh **contemporary opinions** on the Treaty, not just
  modern hindsight.

## Key question 2: To what extent was the League of Nations a success?

- **Structure, aims, membership** — including its key structural
  weakness: no standing army, and not all major powers were members.
- **1920s peacekeeping successes**: Vilna, the Aaland Islands, Corfu,
  Bulgaria.
- **Humanitarian work**: refugees, health, working conditions,
  slavery.
- **1930s failures**: the Manchurian crisis (Japan) and the Abyssinian
  crisis (Italy) — both exposed the League's weaknesses once the
  Depression-era climate made collective security harder to enforce.

## Key question 3: How far was Hitler's foreign policy to blame for war in 1939?

- Chronological chain to memorise: **rearmament** (breach of
  Versailles) → **Saar plebiscite** → **remilitarisation of the
  Rhineland** (1936) → **Rome–Berlin Axis** and **Anti-Comintern Pact**
  → **Spanish Civil War** involvement → **Anschluss** with Austria
  (1938) → **Sudeten crisis** and **Munich Agreement** → **Polish
  crisis** (triggers British/French declarations of war, Sept 1939).
- Know **appeasement** as a deliberate British and French policy
  choice — exam questions expect this to be discussed, not just
  Hitler's actions narrated alone.

## Key question 4: Who was to blame for the Cold War?

- **1945 summit conferences** and the breakdown of the US–Soviet
  wartime alliance.
- **Soviet expansion into Eastern Europe to 1948**.
- **Occupation of Germany** and the **Berlin Blockade** — the
  flashpoint that hardened the division of Europe.
- Formation of **NATO** and the **Warsaw Pact** as the resulting
  military blocs.
- Exam questions expect both US and Soviet actions weighed, not
  one-sided blame.

## Key question 5: How effectively did the USA contain communism?

Three case studies, each with its own reasons for involvement, tactics
used, and reasons for the outcome:

- **Korea (1950–53)** — triggered by North Korea's invasion of the
  South.
- **Cuba (1959–62)** — the Cuban Revolution and the Missile Crisis.
- **Vietnam (1955–75)** — reasons for US involvement, strategy and
  tactics used, and reasons for eventual US withdrawal.

## Key question 6: How secure was Soviet control over Eastern Europe, 1948–c.1989?

- **Hungary (1956)** and **Czechoslovakia (1968)** uprisings and the
  Soviet response to each.
- The **Berlin Wall** (built 1961).
- **Solidarity** in Poland — a sign of weakening Soviet control.
- **Gorbachev's** personal role in the collapse of Soviet control by
  the late 1980s.

## Command word reminders

Option B answers are marked on how well a judgement is supported, not
on volume of narrative. When a question asks "how far," "to what
extent" or "who was to blame," structure the answer around at least two
competing factors and reach an explicit conclusion in the final
sentence — examiners reward a clear, evidenced verdict over a balanced
list that never actually decides anything. When a question instead asks
candidates to "describe" or "explain," specified content (the named
events, treaties and figures above) should be used as precise evidence
rather than generalised background.

## Quick recall checklist

- Can I name Wilson, Clemenceau and Lloyd George's Versailles aims
      without mixing them up?
- Can I explain both a League of Nations success and a League of
      Nations failure with a named example for each?
- Can I list Hitler's foreign policy steps 1933–39 in the correct
      order?
- Do I understand appeasement as a policy choice, not just a
      description of what Britain and France failed to do?
- Can I compare Korea, Cuba and Vietnam as three case studies of
      one containment policy, rather than three separate stories?
- Can I explain why Soviet control weakened between 1956 and 1989
      using at least three named events?

## How to approach it

Since every key question in Option B is a judgement question, not a
narrate-the-facts question, pair each bullet point above with one
sentence of your own that reaches a conclusion — for example, "the
League succeeded at small-scale peacekeeping in the 1920s but failed
where major powers had strong interests, as Manchuria and Abyssinia
show." Revising this way builds exam-ready judgements directly, rather
than leaving the analysis step to be improvised under timed conditions.
