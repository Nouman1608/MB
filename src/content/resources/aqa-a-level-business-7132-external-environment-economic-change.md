This guide goes deeper into section **3.7.5 Analysing the external environment to assess opportunities and threats: economic change** of the AQA AS and A-level Business specification (7132, for first teaching from September 2023). Section 3.7 is for the A-level only. The specification's content is:

- **the impact of changes in the UK and the global economic environment on strategic and functional decision making**
- the economic factors **GDP, taxation, exchange rates, inflation, fiscal and monetary policy, and more open trade versus protectionism**
- the ability to **understand economic data, interpret changes in economic data for the UK and globally, and consider the implications of such changes for business**.

All businesses and figures are invented.

## How to approach economic change

An economic change is only useful to a business when it is turned into a **consequence for a decision**. The chain of reasoning is:

1. What has changed, and by how much?
2. How does it affect **demand** (revenue), **costs**, or **finance**?
3. How does that affect the business's **objectives** and the **decisions** it must make?
4. How serious is it, and how long will it last?

Always ask whether the business sells necessities or luxuries, whether it sells at home or abroad, and whether it borrows heavily.

## Gross domestic product (GDP)

**GDP** is the total value of goods and services produced in a country in a period. **Economic growth** is the percentage rise in real GDP (GDP adjusted for inflation). A fall in GDP for two consecutive quarters is commonly called a **recession**.

| Phase | Typical effect on businesses |
|---|---|
| Growth or boom | Rising incomes and spending; sales, profit and employment rise; businesses invest; but shortages of labour and rising costs can appear |
| Slowdown or recession | Falling demand; unsold stock; price cuts; redundancies; business failures; lower investment; but some firms benefit, such as discount retailers |

**Income elasticity of demand (YED)** explains why firms are affected differently. **Normal goods** (cars, holidays) have positive YED, with luxuries above 1, so demand rises faster than income in a boom and falls faster in a recession. **Necessities** (basic food) have YED between 0 and 1, so are stable. **Inferior goods** (own-brand basics, second-hand goods) have negative YED, so demand can rise in a recession.

**Global GDP** matters for exporters. Slow growth in a major export market reduces demand. Fast growth in emerging economies can create opportunities. Interpret data carefully: a growth rate of 0.5% is low in a developing economy but not unusual in a mature one.

## Taxation

- **Corporation tax** on profit affects after-tax return, and so investment decisions and where companies locate.
- **Income tax** and employee contributions affect take-home pay, and so spending power and wage demands.
- **VAT** and other spending taxes raise prices to the consumer, so can reduce demand, especially for price-elastic goods.
- **Other business taxes** such as business rates and fuel duty affect costs.

Tax changes can be strategic (corporation tax rates may influence where to base the head office) and functional (VAT affects pricing; payroll taxes affect the cost of employing more staff).

## Exchange rates

An **exchange rate** is the price of one currency in terms of another. A **stronger pound** buys more foreign currency; a **weaker pound** buys less.

| | Stronger pound | Weaker pound |
|---|---|---|
| UK exports (priced in pounds) | Dearer abroad, so demand falls | Cheaper abroad, so demand rises |
| UK exports (priced in foreign currency) | Revenue converts into fewer pounds | Revenue converts into more pounds |
| Imports into the UK | Cheaper in pounds | Dearer in pounds |
| Overseas holidays and foreign investment | More attractive for UK residents | Less attractive |
| Inbound tourism | Less attractive | More attractive |

### Worked example: Tarrant Engineering

Tarrant Engineering sells 40 machines a year to the US at £50,000 each and buys components costing $20,000 per machine.

- At £1 = $1.30, the US price is £50,000 × 1.30 = **$65,000**, and a component costs $20,000 ÷ 1.30 = **£15,385**.
- The pound falls to £1 = $1.15. If Tarrant keeps the sterling price, the US price falls to £50,000 × 1.15 = **$57,500**, an 11.5% cut, which should raise US demand.
- If Tarrant keeps the dollar price of $65,000, its sterling revenue per machine becomes $65,000 ÷ 1.15 = **£56,522**, a rise of 13%.
- But the component now costs $20,000 ÷ 1.15 = **£17,391**, an increase of 13.0%.

**Analysis.** A weaker pound helps Tarrant's competitiveness in the US but raises the cost of imported components. The net effect depends on how much of the costs are imported and how price-sensitive US customers are. **Judgement.** Because Tarrant's components are a small part of its costs and its machines are priced in sterling, the fall is probably a net opportunity. It should still plan for volatility, for example by buying dollars in advance or finding dollar costs to match its dollar revenues (a "natural hedge").

## Inflation

**Inflation** is a sustained rise in the general price level. It is measured by indices such as the consumer price index.

**Causes:** *demand-pull* (spending rising faster than output) and *cost-push* (rising costs of labour, energy or materials).

**Effects on businesses:**

- Higher input costs; pressure to raise prices; risk of losing customers if rivals do not.
- Wage demands; if pay rises less than inflation, real pay falls and morale may suffer. For example, a 3% pay rise when prices rise 5% is a real fall of about 1.9% (1.03 ÷ 1.05 − 1).
- Uncertainty makes planning harder; a business may hold back investment.
- Borrowers gain if inflation erodes the real value of debt, but interest rates tend to rise.
- Cost advantages shift: businesses with strong brands can raise prices more easily.

Very low inflation or deflation can also be damaging, because customers delay purchases and the real burden of debt rises.

## Fiscal policy

**Fiscal policy** is the government's use of taxation and spending to influence the economy.

- **Expansionary:** lower taxes or higher spending raise demand, helping businesses such as construction and retailing. It may lead to higher inflation or government borrowing.
- **Contractionary:** higher taxes or lower spending reduce demand, hitting sectors dependent on government contracts or consumer spending.

Government spending priorities matter: a plan for new hospitals benefits builders and equipment makers.

## Monetary policy

**Monetary policy** is the use of interest rates and the money supply, usually by the central bank, to control inflation and support growth.

- **Higher interest rates:** more expensive borrowing for businesses and households, so lower investment and spending, especially on credit-financed goods such as cars and housing; savers gain; the currency often strengthens.
- **Lower interest rates:** cheaper borrowing, encouraging investment and spending, but may weaken the currency and raise inflation.

A business with variable-rate debt sees costs change at once. A highly geared firm is most exposed.

## More open trade versus protectionism

- **Open trade:** low tariffs and quotas, and trade agreements, increase export markets and give access to cheaper inputs and more suppliers, but also bring more foreign competition.
- **Protectionism:** tariffs, quotas, subsidies to domestic industry and tough standards protect domestic producers from imports but raise costs for businesses that import inputs, invite retaliation, and shrink export markets.

Global supply chains make firms sensitive to trade policy. A sudden tariff can make an imported component uneconomic and force a change of supplier.

## Which businesses are most exposed?

The same change hits businesses differently. Use this checklist to judge exposure:

| Feature of the business | More exposed to | Less exposed to |
|---|---|---|
| Sells luxuries or big-ticket goods | Recession, rising interest rates | Growth benefits it most |
| Sells necessities or budget goods | Little change in a recession | Weak gains in a boom |
| Exports a large share of output | Strong pound; slow growth abroad | Weak pound |
| Imports many inputs | Weak pound; tariffs | Strong pound |
| Highly geared with variable-rate debt | Interest rate rises | Rate cuts help it most |
| Labour-intensive | Wage and minimum pay rises | Automation lowers exposure |

A good answer picks the two or three features that matter most for the business in the case and builds the argument on them. Where a firm has both costs and revenues in a foreign currency, the effect of an exchange rate move partly cancels out.

## Interpreting economic data

When given a table of figures:

- State the direction and size of the change (percentage points or percentages).
- Distinguish **real** (adjusted for inflation) and **nominal** figures.
- Compare the UK with other countries, if given.
- Think about which of the business's markets and costs are affected.
- Note the limits: forecasts may be wrong, averages hide differences between regions or sectors, and effects take time.

## Exam technique

- **Analyse:** explain what changes, why it matters to demand, costs or finance, and what the business will decide.
- **Evaluate:** consider the size, duration and nature of the business (necessities or luxuries, exporter or home seller, borrower or not) and reach a judgement.

## Common mistakes

- Stating economic definitions with no link to a business decision.
- Assuming a weaker pound is always good, or always bad.
- Forgetting that businesses differ: a recession is an opportunity for some.
- Confusing fiscal and monetary policy.
- Quoting percentage and percentage-point changes interchangeably.
