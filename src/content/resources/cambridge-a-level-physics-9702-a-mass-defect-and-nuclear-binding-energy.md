This guide covers subtopic **23.1 Mass defect and nuclear binding energy** of Cambridge International AS & A Level Physics 9702, 2025–2027 series (A Level). The broader [Nuclear Physics study guide](/resources/a-physics-nuclear-physics/) introduces both parts of Topic 23; this page goes further into calculating mass defect and binding energy from data in unified atomic mass units, sketching and reading the binding energy per nucleon curve, and finding the energy released or needed in a reaction. For quick recall use the [Mass defect and binding energy revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-physics-9702-a-mass-defect-and-nuclear-binding-energy-revision-notes/), and for exam-style questions the [Mass defect and binding energy practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-physics-9702-a-mass-defect-and-nuclear-binding-energy-practice/).

## What 23.1 asks you to do

The syllabus lists seven outcomes. Candidates should be able to:

- understand the equivalence between energy and mass as represented by **E = mc²**, and recall and use this equation
- represent simple nuclear reactions by **nuclear equations** of the form ¹⁴₇N + ⁴₂He → ¹⁷₈O + ¹₁H
- define and use the terms **mass defect** and **binding energy**
- **sketch** the variation of binding energy per nucleon with nucleon number
- explain what is meant by **nuclear fusion** and **nuclear fission**
- explain the relevance of binding energy per nucleon to nuclear reactions, including fusion and fission
- calculate the energy released in nuclear reactions using **E = c²Δm**

## Mass-energy equivalence

E = mc² says that mass and energy are two measures of the same thing. Whenever a system gains energy its mass increases, and whenever it loses energy its mass decreases, by Δm = ΔE/c². Because c² = 9.00 × 10¹⁶ m² s⁻², a tiny mass corresponds to a huge energy: 1.0 g is equivalent to 1.0 × 10⁻³ × 9.00 × 10¹⁶ = 9.0 × 10¹³ J.

In chemical reactions the mass changes are far too small to measure. In nuclear reactions they are a measurable fraction of the masses involved, which is why nuclear masses must be known to six or more significant figures.

**Units.** Nuclear masses are usually given in **unified atomic mass units**, u, with 1 u = 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷ kg (from the data list). Convert a mass difference to kilograms before multiplying by c², or note that, with the data-list values, 1 u is equivalent to 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷ × 9.00 × 10¹⁶ = 1.49 × 10⁻¹⁰ J.

## Nuclear equations

In nuclide notation ᴬ_Z X, A is the **nucleon number** and Z is the **proton number**. In every nuclear reaction:

- the total nucleon number is the same on both sides
- the total proton number (charge) is the same on both sides

The syllabus example is ¹⁴₇N + ⁴₂He → ¹⁷₈O + ¹₁H. Check: nucleon numbers 14 + 4 = 17 + 1 = 18; proton numbers 7 + 2 = 8 + 1 = 9.

Symbols you need: proton ¹₁p (or ¹₁H), neutron ¹₀n, alpha particle ⁴₂He, electron (β⁻) ⁰₋₁e, positron (β⁺) ⁰₊₁e.

Mass itself is **not** conserved separately; the small mass difference between the two sides is what appears as energy.

## Mass defect and binding energy

The two definitions to learn:

- **Mass defect**: the difference between the total mass of the separate nucleons and the mass of the nucleus.
- **Binding energy**: the minimum energy needed to separate a nucleus completely into its individual protons and neutrons.

They are linked by E = mc²: binding energy = mass defect × c². A nucleus has **less** mass than its separated nucleons because energy would have to be **supplied** to pull them apart; equivalently, energy is released when the nucleons come together.

For a nucleus with Z protons and N = A − Z neutrons:

```
Δm = Z mₚ + N mₙ − m_nucleus
binding energy = Δm c²
```

## Worked example 1: helium-4

Data: mₚ = 1.007276 u, mₙ = 1.008665 u, mass of a helium-4 nucleus = 4.001506 u. Calculate the binding energy per nucleon in MeV.

```
mass of separate nucleons = 2(1.007276) + 2(1.008665) = 4.031882 u
Δm = 4.031882 − 4.001506 = 0.030376 u
   = 0.030376 × 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷ = 5.04 × 10⁻²⁹ kg
binding energy = Δm c² = 5.04 × 10⁻²⁹ × (3.00 × 10⁸)² = 4.54 × 10⁻¹² J
               = 4.54 × 10⁻¹² / 1.60 × 10⁻¹³ = 28.4 MeV
per nucleon   = 28.4 / 4 = 7.09 MeV
```

*Annotation.* Keep all six decimal places until the subtraction: rounding the masses first can wipe out the mass defect entirely. If **atomic** masses are given instead of nuclear masses, the electrons must be dealt with consistently, for example by using the mass of a hydrogen atom in place of the proton mass so that the electron masses cancel.

## The binding energy per nucleon curve

Binding energy per nucleon (total binding energy ÷ nucleon number) measures how tightly each nucleon is held, and so how **stable** a nucleus is. The sketch must show:

| Feature | Detail to include |
|---|---|
| Axes | Binding energy per nucleon (MeV) against nucleon number A (0 to about 240) |
| Light nuclei | Steep rise from a low value for ²₁H (about 1 MeV) |
| Helium-4 | A point well above its neighbours (about 7 MeV) |
| Maximum | About 8.8 MeV near A ≈ 56 (iron); the most stable nuclei |
| Heavy nuclei | Gradual fall to about 7.6 MeV for uranium |

The shape is the key to the energy from nuclear reactions: **any reaction whose products are closer to the peak** than the reactants releases energy.

## Fusion and fission

- **Nuclear fusion**: two light nuclei join to form a heavier nucleus. For example ²₁H + ³₁H → ⁴₂He + ¹₀n.
- **Nuclear fission**: a heavy nucleus splits into two lighter nuclei of similar mass, usually after absorbing a neutron, releasing further neutrons. For example ²³⁵₉₂U + ¹₀n → ¹⁴¹₅₆Ba + ⁹²₃₆Kr + 3¹₀n.

### Why both release energy

Fusion of light nuclei moves up the steep left-hand side of the curve; fission of a heavy nucleus moves up the gentler right-hand side. In each case the products have a **higher binding energy per nucleon** than the reactants. The total binding energy increases, so the products have less total mass, and the mass lost appears as kinetic energy of the products (and gamma radiation):

```
energy released = (total binding energy of products) − (total binding energy of reactants)
                = c² Δm,   where Δm = mass of reactants − mass of products
```

Fusion of very heavy nuclei, or fission of very light ones, would move **down** the curve and would need energy to be supplied. Nuclei at the peak, around iron-56, cannot release energy by either process.

## Worked example 2: energy from fusion

Data: ²₁H nucleus 2.013553 u, ³₁H nucleus 3.015501 u, ⁴₂He nucleus 4.001506 u, neutron 1.008665 u. Calculate the energy released when one deuterium and one tritium nucleus fuse.

```
mass before = 2.013553 + 3.015501 = 5.029054 u
mass after  = 4.001506 + 1.008665 = 5.010171 u
Δm = 0.018883 u = 3.13 × 10⁻²⁹ kg
E = c² Δm = (3.00 × 10⁸)² × 3.13 × 10⁻²⁹ = 2.82 × 10⁻¹² J (17.6 MeV)
```

*Annotation.* The answer is positive, so energy is released. Now try the syllabus reaction ¹⁴₇N + ⁴₂He → ¹⁷₈O + ¹₁H with atomic masses 14.003074 u, 4.002603 u, 16.999132 u and 1.007825 u. The mass before is 18.005677 u and after is 18.006957 u: the products are **heavier** by 0.001280 u, so this reaction needs about 1.91 × 10⁻¹³ J (1.2 MeV) to be supplied, from the kinetic energy of the alpha particle. A negative answer is not an error; it tells you the reaction is not energy-releasing.

## Common mistakes

- **Defining binding energy as "the energy holding the nucleus together"**. Give the precise definition: the minimum energy needed to separate it into individual nucleons.
- **Writing mass defect the wrong way round** (nucleus minus nucleons gives a negative answer).
- **Rounding nuclear masses** before subtracting.
- **Mixing atomic and nuclear masses** in one calculation.
- **Saying fission releases energy "because the nucleus breaks"**. Breaking a nucleus needs energy; energy is released because the products are more tightly bound.
- **Confusing total binding energy with binding energy per nucleon.** Uranium has a far larger total binding energy than iron but a smaller value per nucleon.
- **Forgetting to balance proton numbers** in an equation where a neutron or electron is emitted.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Define** (mass defect, binding energy): learn the wording above exactly.
- **Sketch** (the binding energy per nucleon curve): label axes with units, show the steep rise, the peak near A = 56 with an approximate value, and the gentle fall.
- **Explain** (why fission releases energy): products have higher binding energy per nucleon, so total binding energy increases, mass decreases, and the mass difference is released as energy.
- **Calculate** (energy released): list masses before and after, subtract, convert u to kg, multiply by c², and convert to MeV only if asked.
- **Complete** a nuclear equation: balance A and Z separately, and identify the particle from the missing values.
