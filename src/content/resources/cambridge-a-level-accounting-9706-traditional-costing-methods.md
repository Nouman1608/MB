This guide covers **2.2 Traditional costing methods** of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Accounting 9706 syllabus for 2026, 2027 and 2028: 2.2.1 Costing applications, 2.2.2 Absorption costing, 2.2.3 Marginal costing and 2.2.4 Cost–volume–profit analysis. ## What 2.2 asks you to do

The syllabus asks you to account for costs using **absorption** and **marginal** costing, explore the impact of changes in activity, and evaluate the benefits and limitations of each approach for pricing and management decisions. Every sub-section ends with **non-financial factors and their significance**, so a decision answer that stops at the numbers is incomplete. The syllabus also notes that you will interpret a break-even chart but will not be asked to prepare one.

## 1. Job costing in a service business

Section 2.2.1 requires unit, job and batch costing "in both manufacturing and service businesses as applicable". Service jobs are where students hesitate, because there are no materials to trace. The method is the same: collect every direct cost for the job, add overheads at a predetermined rate, then add profit.

**Worked example.** Halden Design prepares a brochure for a client (Job 214): senior designer 12 hours at 45.00, junior designer 20 hours at 25.00, specialist printing 180 and travel 60, both bought for this job only. Overheads are absorbed at 30.00 per direct labour hour. Halden adds a mark-up of 40% on cost.

| Job 214 | $ |
|---|---|
| Direct labour: senior 540, junior 500 | 1,040 |
| Direct expenses: printing 180, travel 60 | 240 |
| Overheads absorbed: 32 hours × 30.00 | 960 |
| **Total cost** | **2,240** |
| Mark-up 40% | 896 |
| **Price** | **3,136** |

Mark-up is a percentage of **cost**. Had Halden wanted a 40% **margin** (on selling price), the price would be 2,240 ÷ 0.60 = 3,733.33. Read which one the question gives.

Batch costing applies the same build-up to a group of identical items made together, such as 2,500 printed leaflets, then divides the total by the number of items. One-off set-up costs make the cost per item fall as the group gets larger.

## 2. Absorption costing: choosing the basis

An **overhead absorption rate (OAR)** is budgeted overhead ÷ budgeted activity. The syllabus asks for "an appropriate basis", and the choice changes product costs.

**Worked example.** Moorside Castings' machining department has budgeted overheads of 96,000, 12,000 budgeted machine hours and 4,000 budgeted direct labour hours. Product P needs 3 machine hours and 0.5 labour hours.

- Machine hour rate 96,000 ÷ 12,000 = 8.00, so P absorbs 3 × 8.00 = **24.00**
- Labour hour rate 96,000 ÷ 4,000 = 24.00, so P absorbs 0.5 × 24.00 = **12.00**

The department is machine-intensive (three times as many machine hours as labour hours), so overheads such as power, depreciation and maintenance are driven by machine time. The machine hour rate is appropriate; the labour hour rate would undercost P by 12.00 and could lead to a price that fails to cover its overheads.

**Under absorption and the profit statement.** Moorside actually worked 11,400 machine hours and spent 93,500. Absorbed = 11,400 × 8.00 = 91,200, which is 2,300 less than actual: **under-absorbed**. Two causes combine. Activity was 600 hours below budget, so 4,800 less was absorbed than planned; spending was 2,500 below budget. Net: 4,800 − 2,500 = 2,300 under-absorbed. Under-absorption is added to cost of sales, reducing profit; over-absorption is deducted from cost of sales, increasing it.

## 3. Absorption and marginal profit statements with opening inventory

**Worked example.** Kelsall Boards sells at 60.00. Variable production cost is 22.00 a unit and variable selling cost 3.00 a unit sold. Fixed production overhead is 180,000, absorbed on normal output of 12,000 units (15.00 a unit). Fixed non-production costs are 70,000. Opening inventory was 1,500 units; 11,000 were made and 11,800 sold, leaving 700.

| Absorption costing | $ | Marginal costing | $ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sales (11,800 × 60) | 708,000 | Sales | 708,000 |
| Opening inventory (1,500 × 37) | 55,500 | Variable cost of sales (11,800 × 22) | 259,600 |
| Production (11,000 × 37) | 407,000 | Variable selling cost | 35,400 |
| Closing inventory (700 × 37) | (25,900) | **Contribution** | **413,000** |
| Under-absorbed (1,000 × 15) | 15,000 | Fixed production overhead | 180,000 |
| Cost of sales | 451,600 | Fixed non-production costs | 70,000 |
| Gross profit | 256,400 | | |
| Variable selling and fixed non-production | 105,400 | | |
| **Profit** | **151,000** | **Profit** | **163,000** |

Production was 1,000 units below normal output, so absorption costing needs an under-absorption adjustment; marginal costing never does, because it charges actual fixed overhead in full.

**Reconciliation statement:**

| | $ |
|---|---|
| Marginal costing profit | 163,000 |
| Add fixed overhead in closing inventory (700 × 15) | 10,500 |
| Less fixed overhead in opening inventory (1,500 × 15) | (22,500) |
| **Absorption costing profit** | **151,000** |

Inventory fell by 800 units, so absorption profit is 800 × 15 = 12,000 lower: fixed overhead from last year, carried in the opening inventory, is released into this year's cost of sales.

## 4. Cost–volume–profit analysis and price changes

Brantwood Kettles sells at 30.00 with variable cost 18.00 and fixed costs 96,000; budgeted sales are 10,000 units. Contribution is 12.00, the C/S ratio 40%, break-even 8,000 units, margin of safety 2,000 units (20%) and profit 24,000.

The sales director proposes cutting the price to 27.00, expecting sales of 13,000 units.

- New contribution 9.00; C/S ratio 33.3%
- Break-even = 96,000 ÷ 9.00 = 10,666.7, so **10,667 units**
- Profit = 13,000 × 9.00 − 96,000 = **21,000**
- Margin of safety = 13,000 − 10,667 = 2,333 units, **17.9%** of sales
- Sales needed to keep profit at 24,000: 120,000 ÷ 9.00 = **13,334 units**

The cut lowers profit and raises risk, since the business must sell more just to break even. On a **break-even chart**, a price cut makes the sales revenue line less steep, so it meets the total cost line further to the right; the gap between the lines at any output shows profit or loss, and the margin of safety is the horizontal distance between break-even and the planned output.

CVP assumes a constant selling price and variable cost per unit, fixed costs that stay fixed, and sales equal to production. The 13,000 forecast itself is uncertain, which is the main limitation to raise when evaluating this proposal.

## 5. Special orders and closure

**Special order with spare capacity.** Brantwood can make 12,000 kettles and expects to sell 10,000. A retailer offers to buy 1,500 at 21.00 each, needing special packaging at 0.50 a unit. Fixed costs do not change. Contribution per kettle = 21.00 − 18.00 − 0.50 = 2.50, so profit rises by **3,750**: accept on financial grounds.

**When capacity is full.** If the order were for 3,000 kettles, 1,000 normal sales at 12.00 contribution would be lost. Order contribution 7,500 − lost contribution 12,000 = profit falls by **4,500**: reject unless the price rises.

**Closure of a business unit.** Glenmoor Stores' garden department has revenue 140,000, variable costs 98,000 and apportioned fixed costs 50,000, showing a "loss" of 8,000. Only 15,000 of those fixed costs (the department manager's salary) would be saved by closure. Closing it loses 42,000 contribution and saves 15,000, so profit falls by **27,000**. The department should stay open, because the rest of the apportioned costs would continue and be borne by other departments.

**Non-financial factors** for these decisions include: whether existing customers will demand the lower special price; the reliability of the new customer; staff morale and redundancies on closure; the reputation effect of dropping a product range; and whether a garden department draws customers who also buy elsewhere in the store.

## Common mistakes

- Confusing mark-up (on cost) with margin (on selling price).
- Choosing a labour hour rate for a machine-intensive department without justification.
- Forgetting the under- or over-absorption adjustment in an absorption costing statement when output differs from normal.
- Reconciling profits with the change in units only, forgetting opening inventory carries last year's fixed overhead.
- Rounding break-even units down: 10,666.7 units does not break even.
- Accepting a special order on contribution alone when capacity is full.
- Including unavoidable apportioned fixed costs in a closure decision.

## Exam technique for the 9706 command words

- **Calculate** and **Prepare**: label each figure (for example "closing inventory 700 × 37") so method marks are available.
- **Reconcile**: the syllabus defines it as processing two sets of figures to confirm their agreement; start from one profit and adjust only for fixed overhead in inventory.
- **Explain**: give the reason a result occurs, such as why inventory changes move profit.
- **Evaluate**, **Assess** and **Advise**: state the financial result, weigh points for and against including non-financial factors and the assumptions of the method, then make a clear recommendation.
- **Allocate**, **Apportion** and **Reapportion** are syllabus command words with defined meanings: allocate charges overheads attributable to one cost centre, apportion shares overheads between centres on an appropriate basis, and reapportion recharges service centre overheads.
