All products, firms and figures are invented. ## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Explain what is meant by demand. **[2]**

**2.** The table shows the weekly demand of three consumers for smoothies.

| Price (£) | X | Y | Z |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | 10 | 6 | 9 |
| 3 | 8 | 5 | 6 |
| 4 | 5 | 3 | 4 |

Calculate market demand at each price. **[3]**

**3.** For each event, state whether there is a movement along or a shift of the demand curve for restaurant meals, and its direction.

**(a)** Restaurant meal prices fall. **[1]**
**(b)** Average incomes rise. **[1]**

**4.** Explain the likely effect of a large rise in petrol prices on the demand for bicycles. **[2]**

**5.** The price of a sandwich rises from £1.20 to £1.50 and weekly sales fall from 800 to 700. Calculate the price elasticity of demand. **[2]**

**6.** Explain why the demand for table salt is likely to be price inelastic. **[2]**

### Section B: longer and structured questions

**7.** A gym cuts its monthly membership price from £30 to £24. Membership rises from 1,500 to 2,100.

**(a)** Calculate the price elasticity of demand. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the gym's monthly revenue before and after the price cut. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain whether the price cut was a good decision for the gym. **[2]**

**8.** Describe how to draw a relatively elastic and a relatively inelastic demand curve on the same diagram, and explain what each shows when price rises by the same amount. **[4]**

**9.** The price of tablet computers falls sharply. Analyse the consequences for consumers and producers of e-books. **[6]**

**10.** The table shows the daily market for ice lollies at a beach.

| Price (£) | Demand | Supply |
|---|---|---|
| 0.50 | 500 | 100 |
| 0.75 | 400 | 200 |
| 1.00 | 300 | 300 |
| 1.25 | 200 | 400 |

A heatwave increases demand by 200 at every price.

**(a)** State the original equilibrium price and quantity. **[1]**
**(b)** State the new equilibrium price and quantity. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the shortage at the original price after the heatwave begins. **[1]**
**(d)** Calculate the percentage change in sellers' total revenue. **[2]**

**11.** Explain why the demand for one brand of petrol is likely to be more price elastic than the demand for petrol as a whole. **[3]**

**12.** Evaluate the importance of price elasticity of demand for consumers and producers. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** The quantity of a good or service that consumers are willing and able to buy [1] at each price over a given period of time [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* "Willing and able" is the key phrase; wanting a good is not demand.

**2.** £2: 10 + 6 + 9 = **25** [1]. £3: 8 + 5 + 6 = **19** [1]. £4: 5 + 3 + 4 = **12** [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Add quantities at each price. Adding the prices is a common slip.

**3. (a)** Movement along the curve: an extension (more demanded) [1].
**(b)** Shift to the right: an increase in demand [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Only a change in the good's own price causes a movement.

**4.** Bicycles are a substitute for car travel, so dearer petrol makes driving more expensive [1]. Demand for bicycles increases, shifting the curve to the right [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Name the relationship (substitute) and the direction of the shift.

**5.** % change in price = +25% and % change in quantity = −12.5% [1]. PED = −12.5 ÷ 25 = **−0.5** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Quantity goes on top. Inverting the formula gives −2, which loses both marks.

**6.** Salt has few close substitutes in cooking [1] and takes a tiny share of income, so a price rise barely affects how much people buy [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* "It is a necessity" is acceptable as one reason, but it should be developed.

**7. (a)** % change in price = −20%, % change in quantity = +40% [1]. PED = 40 ÷ −20 = **−2** [1].
**(b)** Before: 30 × 1,500 = **£45,000** [1]. After: 24 × 2,100 = **£50,400** [1].
**(c)** Demand is elastic, so the price cut raised revenue by £5,400 [1]; however, more members may mean extra costs such as staff and equipment, so profit may not rise by as much [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Revenue is not profit. Considering costs gives the second mark in (c).

**8.** Draw price on the vertical axis and quantity on the horizontal axis, with both curves on the same scale [1]. The relatively elastic curve is shallow and the relatively inelastic curve is steep [1]. For the same price rise, the elastic curve shows a large fall in quantity demanded [1], while the inelastic curve shows a small fall [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Marking the same price change with dotted lines to both axes makes the contrast clear.

**9.** Tablets and e-books are complements [1], so cheaper tablets mean more people own them [1] and demand for e-books shifts to the right [1]. At the old price there is a shortage, so the price of e-books may rise and the quantity sold increases [1]. Producers of e-books gain higher revenue [1]. Consumers buy more e-books but may pay a higher price, although digital books can be supplied easily, so any price rise may be small [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* The chain starts with the relationship between the goods. Without "complement", the shift is unexplained.

**10. (a)** £1.00 and 300 lollies [1].
**(b)** £1.25 and 400 lollies [1].
**(c)** New demand at £1.00 is 500 against supply of 300, so the shortage is **200** [1].
**(d)** Revenue rises from 1.00 × 300 = £300 to 1.25 × 400 = £500 [1]; (500 − 300) ÷ 300 × 100 = **+66.7%** [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* The new demand at £1.25 is 200 + 200 = 400, matching supply.

**11.** A single brand has many close substitutes: other brands of petrol at nearby stations [1]. If one brand raises its price, drivers can switch easily [1], whereas petrol as a whole has few substitutes for car owners, so total demand changes little [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* The comparison is the point: substitutes for the brand versus substitutes for the product.

**12.** For producers, PED shows how a price change affects revenue [1]: with inelastic demand a price rise increases revenue, while with elastic demand a price cut does [1]. This helps firms set prices and plan output [1]. For consumers, inelastic demand for necessities such as heating fuel means price rises cannot easily be avoided [1], which hits low-income households hardest [1]. However, PED estimates are based on past data and may change [1], and other factors such as income may change at the same time [1]. Judgement: PED is important for pricing decisions and for understanding who bears price rises, but firms should treat estimates as a guide, not a certainty [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Cover both consumers and producers, then question the reliability of PED before judging.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Turning the PED formula upside down.
- Calling a negative PED "elastic" because of its sign.
- Shifting the curve for a change in the good's own price.
- Treating revenue as profit.
- Analysis chains that skip the shortage or surplus step.
- Evaluations that discuss producers only.
