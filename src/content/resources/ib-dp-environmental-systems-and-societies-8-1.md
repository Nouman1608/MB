## A population as a system

A national population is an open system. Its inputs are **births** and **immigration**; its outputs are **deaths** and **emigration**; the storage is the number of people, divided by age and sex.

```
Population change = (births − deaths) + (immigration − emigration)
                  = natural change + net migration
```

The natural increase rate leaves migration out, so it describes the global population well but can mislead for a single country. A country with low fertility can still grow if net migration is positive.

### Worked example 1: natural change and net migration

A fictional country has 40.0 million people at the start of a year. During the year there are 880,000 births, 360,000 deaths, 150,000 immigrants and 70,000 emigrants.

```
CBR = 880,000 ÷ 40,000,000 × 1,000 = 22 per 1,000
CDR = 360,000 ÷ 40,000,000 × 1,000 =  9 per 1,000
NIR = (22 − 9) ÷ 10 = 1.3%

Net migration = 150,000 − 70,000 = +80,000
Total change  = 520,000 + 80,000 = +600,000
Growth rate   = 600,000 ÷ 40,000,000 × 100 = 1.5%
```

Here migration adds a fifth of a percentage point to growth. If you projected the population from the NIR alone, you would underestimate it.

### Projecting forward

At a constant growth rate, a population grows exponentially:

```
Future population = present population × (1 + r)ⁿ
```

At 1.5% a year, 40.6 million becomes 40.6 × 1.015³⁰ ≈ 63.5 million after 30 years. The rule of 70 gives a doubling time of 70 ÷ 1.5 ≈ 47 years. Both assume the rate stays constant, which real rates rarely do; state this whenever you project.

## Measures beyond the crude rates

- **Infant mortality rate (IMR):** deaths of children under one year old per 1,000 live births in a year. It is a sensitive indicator of healthcare, sanitation, nutrition and the education of mothers. Note the denominator: live births, not total population.
- **Life expectancy:** the average number of years a newborn would live if current death rates stayed the same. It rises as infant and child deaths fall.
- **Youth and old-age dependency ratios:** the dependency ratio can be split to show which group dominates.

### Worked example 2: infant mortality and dependency

The same country records 26,400 deaths of infants under one year old. Its age structure is 12.6 million aged 0–14, 24.0 million aged 15–64 and 3.4 million aged 65 and over.

```
IMR = 26,400 ÷ 880,000 × 1,000 = 30 per 1,000 live births

Total dependency ratio = (12.6 + 3.4) ÷ 24.0 × 100 = 66.7
Youth dependency       = 12.6 ÷ 24.0 × 100 = 52.5
Old-age dependency     =  3.4 ÷ 24.0 × 100 = 14.2
```

The total ratio means about 67 dependants for every 100 people of working age, and most of them are children. The environmental and planning implications follow: demand for schools and future jobs now, and strong population momentum as these children reach child-bearing age. A country with the same total ratio made up mainly of older people would face different pressures: pensions, healthcare and a shrinking workforce.

## Why fertility falls

Use these groups of factors to explain a change in the total fertility rate in a case study.

| Factor type | Lowers fertility when... |
|---|---|
| Health | Infant and child mortality fall, so families no longer need many births to ensure some children survive |
| Education | Girls stay longer in school, marry later and have more choice over family size |
| Economic | Children cost more (schooling, housing) and contribute less labour, especially in cities; pensions reduce reliance on children in old age |
| Social and cultural | Norms shift towards smaller families; women's paid employment rises |
| Access to contraception | Family planning services are available and affordable |
| Political | Governments promote smaller families through incentives or campaigns |

Factors that keep fertility high include religious or cultural value placed on large families, the need for children's labour in farming, and limited access to contraception.

## Population policies and their consequences

Pro-natalist and anti-natalist policies are introduced in the Topic 8 guide. Examiners reward named examples with outcomes.

### China

China's one-child policy, introduced around 1980, limited most urban couples to one child, with exceptions in some rural areas and for some minorities. Fertility fell sharply, which slowed growth and reduced pressure on resources. The consequences included rapid population ageing, a smaller future workforce and a sex ratio at birth skewed towards boys. The policy was replaced by a two-child policy from 2016, and a three-child limit was announced in 2021 as the government tried to raise births. Use it to show that a policy can achieve its aim and still create new problems, and that reversing a low-fertility trend is much harder than creating it.

### Singapore

In the 1970s Singapore ran an anti-natalist "Stop at Two" campaign with incentives and penalties linked to family size. Fertility fell below replacement level. From 1987 the government reversed course with a pro-natalist approach that encouraged larger families among those who could afford them, later adding cash payments and parental leave. Fertility has remained low. This shows the lag and limited power of incentives once social norms change.

### Development without coercion

The Indian state of Kerala is often cited as a region where fertility fell to around replacement level largely through high female literacy and good basic healthcare rather than strict limits. It supports the argument that education and health are effective, ethically less contested routes to lower fertility.

## Population and environmental impact

Population size is one driver of impact, multiplied by consumption. The ecological footprint shows this clearly.

### Worked example 3: comparing total footprints

Two fictional countries:

```
Country P: 10 million people × 6.0 gha per person =  60 million gha
Country Q: 80 million people × 1.2 gha per person =  96 million gha
```

Country Q has eight times the population but only 1.6 times the total footprint. If Country Q's footprint per person rose to 3.0 gha as incomes grew, its total would become 240 million gha. Growth in consumption can raise impact as much as growth in numbers. Neo-Malthusian views, such as those in the 1972 report *The Limits to Growth*, emphasise numbers and finite resources; others stress consumption, technology and distribution.

## Common mistakes

- Projecting a country's population from the NIR when migration is significant.
- Using total population as the denominator for the infant mortality rate.
- Quoting a dependency ratio without saying whether the young or the old dominate.
- Listing fertility factors without linking them to the case in the question.
- Treating the one-child policy as wholly successful or wholly failed; examiners want both outcomes.
- Equating a large population with a large impact without considering consumption per person.

## Exam technique

- **Calculate:** write the formula, use the correct denominator, and give rates per 1,000 or as percentages as asked.
- **Explain** a change in birth or death rate: give the cause, the mechanism and the result.
- **Evaluate** a policy: aim, evidence of effect, unintended consequences, ethical issues, then a judgement.
- **Discuss** population and environment: weigh numbers against consumption and technology, using footprint data.
