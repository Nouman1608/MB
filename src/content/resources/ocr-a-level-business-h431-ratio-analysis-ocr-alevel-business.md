This guide covers the **Ratio analysis** content in the Area of Study **Financial accounting** of the OCR A Level Business (H431) specification, Version 2.1 (January 2026), final assessment summer 2027. It is numbered 6.12 on this site; the specification does not number its content. The [accounting and financial considerations study guide](/resources/ocr-a-level-business-accounting-and-financial-considerations/) covers the whole topic. This page goes further on this one heading: all five groups of ratio the specification names, each worked on two years of invented accounts, how to interpret each result, how to evaluate a business's financial position and how to judge the usefulness of ratio analysis. Where the specification gives no formula, the standard form is shown and labelled; use the form given in your question. All businesses and figures are invented.

Use it with the [ratio analysis revision notes](/resources/ocr-a-level-business-h431-ratio-analysis-ocr-alevel-business-revision-notes/) and the [ratio analysis practice questions](/resources/ocr-a-level-business-h431-ratio-analysis-ocr-alevel-business-practice/). The course hub is [OCR A Level Business](/boards/ocr/a-level/business/).

## What the specification asks for

| Learners should be able to | Covered below |
|---|---|
| calculate and interpret liquidity ratios: current ratio and acid test | Liquidity |
| calculate and interpret solvency ratios: gearing and interest cover | Solvency |
| calculate and interpret efficiency ratios: creditor turnover, debtor turnover, non-current assets turnover and stock turnover | Efficiency |
| calculate and interpret profitability ratios: gross profit margin, net profit margin, ROCE and return on equity | Profitability |
| calculate and interpret shareholder ratios: dividend per share, dividend yield, earnings per share and price/earnings ratio | Shareholder ratios |
| evaluate the financial position of a business | Evaluating the financial position |
| evaluate the usefulness of ratio analysis | Usefulness |

## The data

Marsden Textiles plc has 500,000 shares in issue. Its accounts for two years are below. (Tax is 20% of profit before tax.)

| Income statement | Year 1 | Year 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue | £2,150,000 | £2,400,000 |
| Cost of sales | £1,376,000 | £1,560,000 |
| Gross profit | £774,000 | £840,000 |
| Operating expenses | £516,000 | £540,000 |
| Operating profit | £258,000 | £300,000 |
| Interest | £36,000 | £40,000 |
| Profit before tax | £222,000 | £260,000 |
| Tax | £44,400 | £52,000 |
| Profit for the year | £177,600 | £208,000 |
| Dividends | £70,000 | £80,000 |

| Statement of financial position | Year 1 | Year 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Non-current assets | £1,100,000 | £1,238,000 |
| Inventory | £215,000 | £270,000 |
| Receivables | £290,000 | £360,000 |
| Cash | £70,000 | £30,000 |
| **Current assets** | £575,000 | £660,000 |
| Payables | £235,000 | £290,000 |
| Overdraft | £0 | £40,000 |
| **Current liabilities** | £235,000 | £330,000 |
| Non-current liabilities (loan) | £450,000 | £450,000 |
| **Net assets = total shareholders' equity** | £990,000 | £1,118,000 |
| **Capital employed** (equity + non-current liabilities) | £1,440,000 | £1,568,000 |
| Share price | £4.60 | £5.20 |

## Liquidity: can the business pay its short-term debts?

| Ratio | Standard formula | Year 1 | Year 2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Current ratio | Current assets ÷ current liabilities | 575 ÷ 235 = **2.45** | 660 ÷ 330 = **2.00** |
| Acid test | (Current assets − inventory) ÷ current liabilities | 360 ÷ 235 = **1.53** | 390 ÷ 330 = **1.18** |

(Figures in £000.) Interpretation: both ratios fell, as an overdraft appeared and cash halved. Year 2's acid test of 1.18 still shows enough liquid assets to cover short-term debts, but the trend is downwards, and about 41% of current assets (270 ÷ 660) is inventory, which cannot be turned into cash quickly. A "good" level depends on the industry.

## Solvency: can the business meet its long-term obligations?

| Ratio | Standard formula | Year 1 | Year 2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gearing | Non-current liabilities ÷ capital employed × 100 | 450 ÷ 1,440 = **31.3%** | 450 ÷ 1,568 = **28.7%** |
| Interest cover | Operating profit ÷ interest | 258 ÷ 36 = **7.2 times** | 300 ÷ 40 = **7.5 times** |

Interpretation: gearing fell because equity grew while the loan stayed the same, and interest is covered 7.5 times, so the business is not heavily dependent on debt. It has room to borrow more if it needs to.

## Efficiency: how well does it use its resources?

| Ratio | Standard formula | Year 1 | Year 2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Debtor turnover (days) | Receivables ÷ revenue × 365 | **49.2 days** | **54.8 days** |
| Creditor turnover (days) | Payables ÷ cost of sales × 365 | **62.3 days** | **67.9 days** |
| Non-current assets turnover | Revenue ÷ non-current assets | **1.95** | **1.94** |
| Stock turnover (times) | Cost of sales ÷ inventory | **6.40** | **5.78** |

Working for Year 2: 360,000 ÷ 2,400,000 × 365 = 54.75; 290,000 ÷ 1,560,000 × 365 = 67.85; 2,400,000 ÷ 1,238,000 = 1.94; 1,560,000 ÷ 270,000 = 5.78. Stock turnover can also be shown in days: 365 ÷ 5.78 = 63 days (Year 1: 57 days).

Interpretation: customers are taking nearly six days longer to pay, inventory is turning over more slowly, and Marsden is paying its own suppliers about six days later too. Each change ties up cash: this fits the fall in the cash balance. The non-current assets turnover is flat at 1.94, so each £1 of non-current assets generates about the same revenue as before; the investment in new assets (£138,000) has not yet raised sales per pound invested.

## Profitability: how well does it earn profit?

| Ratio | Standard formula | Year 1 | Year 2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gross profit margin | Gross profit ÷ revenue × 100 | **36.0%** | **35.0%** |
| Net profit margin | Profit for the year ÷ revenue × 100 | **8.3%** | **8.7%** |
| ROCE | Operating profit ÷ capital employed × 100 | 258 ÷ 1,440 = **17.9%** | 300 ÷ 1,568 = **19.1%** |
| Return on equity | Profit for the year ÷ total shareholders' equity × 100 | 177.6 ÷ 990 = **17.9%** | 208 ÷ 1,118 = **18.6%** |

Interpretation: the gross margin slipped by one percentage point (cost of sales rose faster than revenue), but control of operating expenses lifted the net margin. ROCE and return on equity both improved, so owners and lenders are getting a better return on the money invested. A comparison with the interest rate on the loan or with the best alternative use of funds shows whether a ROCE of 19.1% is attractive.

## Shareholder ratios

| Ratio | Standard formula | Year 1 | Year 2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Earnings per share (EPS) | Profit for the year ÷ number of shares | 177,600 ÷ 500,000 = **35.5p** | 208,000 ÷ 500,000 = **41.6p** |
| Dividend per share (DPS) | Dividends ÷ number of shares | **14.0p** | **16.0p** |
| Dividend yield | DPS ÷ share price × 100 | 14 ÷ 460 = **3.0%** | 16 ÷ 520 = **3.1%** |
| Price/earnings (P/E) | Share price ÷ EPS | 460 ÷ 35.52 = **13.0** | 520 ÷ 41.6 = **12.5** |

Interpretation: EPS rose by 17% (6.1p ÷ 35.5p) and the dividend by 14%. The yield is 3.1%, which is the cash return from dividends alone. The P/E of 12.5 means investors pay £12.50 for every £1 of current earnings; it fell slightly, so the share price rose less than earnings. Compare P/E with similar companies: a higher figure suggests that investors expect faster growth.

## Evaluating the financial position

Combine the results into a balanced picture and a judgement.

**Strengths.** Profitability improved (ROCE 17.9% to 19.1%; net margin 8.3% to 8.7%); gearing is moderate and falling; interest cover is good; shareholders gain (EPS +17%, dividends up).

**Weaknesses.** Liquidity has fallen (current ratio 2.45 to 2.00; acid test 1.53 to 1.18) and an overdraft has appeared; debtor days and inventory days are lengthening, so cash is tied up; gross margin has slipped.

**Judgement.** Marsden is profitable and not over-borrowed, but its cash position is weakening because of slower collection from customers and slower stock turnover. The most urgent action is to improve working capital: chase customers, reduce slow-moving stock and watch the overdraft. The conclusion would change if the rise in receivables is due to a few large, reliable customers, or if the stock was built up for a known contract.

## Usefulness of ratio analysis

**Strengths.** Turns accounts into comparable percentages; reveals trends; highlights strengths and weaknesses; allows comparison with competitors and targets; used by lenders, investors, managers and suppliers.

**Limitations.**

- It uses historic figures; the future may differ.
- It depends on accounting policies (depreciation, inventory valuation) that differ between businesses.
- A year-end snapshot may not be typical (seasonal inventory).
- Ratios need a benchmark: a figure alone says little.
- Financial ratios ignore non-financial matters such as staff, brand and the economic climate.
- Different formulas exist (for example, average or closing inventory), so compare like with like.
- It identifies *what* has changed, not *why*.

## Common mistakes

- Using the wrong base: debtor days use revenue; creditor days and stock turnover use cost of sales.
- Mixing up gearing (non-current liabilities) with a ratio including current liabilities.
- Forgetting to multiply by 100 or by 365.
- Calling a figure "good" or "bad" with no comparison.
- Listing ratios without a judgement.

## Exam technique

Write each formula, substitute and give the unit (%, times, days, pence). Comment on the direction and meaning of each change, give a cause, and finish with a judgement. In evaluation, group strengths and weaknesses by liquidity, solvency, efficiency, profitability and returns to shareholders.
