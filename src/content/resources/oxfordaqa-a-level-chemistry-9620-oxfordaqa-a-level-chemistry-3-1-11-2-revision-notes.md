## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Initial rate | Rate at t = 0; gradient of the tangent to the concentration–time curve at the start |
| Tangent | Straight line touching a curve at one point; its gradient is the rate at that time |
| Clock reaction | A reaction timed to a sudden colour change after a fixed small amount of reaction; initial rate ∝ 1/t |
| Continuous monitoring | Following one reaction mixture over time (colour, gas volume, mass, titration of samples) |
| Rate-determining step | The slowest step in a mechanism; it controls the overall rate |
| Intermediate | A species formed in one step and used up in a later step |

## The big idea

The rate equation is **experimentally determined**. Orders depend on the mechanism, not on the balancing numbers. Reactants can be zero order, and catalysts can appear in the rate equation.

## Reading graphs

**Concentration–time graph for a reactant**
- Straight line sloping down: constant rate, **zero order** in that reactant.
- Curve getting less steep: first or second order; use tangents to find rates at different concentrations.
- Rate at time t = −(gradient of tangent at t).

**Rate–concentration graph**

| Shape | Order |
|---|---|
| Horizontal line | 0 |
| Straight line through origin | 1 |
| Curve through origin, steepening (straight when plotted against [X]²) | 2 |

## Orders from an initial-rate table

1. Pick two experiments in which only one concentration changes.
2. Concentration factor *c* and rate factor *r*.
3. *r* = 1 → order 0; *r* = *c* → order 1; *r* = *c*² → order 2.
4. If no clean pair exists, divide out the effect of the reactant whose order you already know, then compare what is left.

**Factor check list:** × 1.5 gives × 2.25 for order 2; × 2.5 gives × 6.25; × 3 gives × 9; × 0.5 gives × 0.25.

## Building the rate equation

- Include only reactants (and catalysts) with non-zero order.
- Overall order = sum of orders.
- k = rate ÷ concentration terms, from any one experiment; check with a second.
- Units of k follow from the overall order (order 2: mol⁻¹ dm³ s⁻¹; order 3: mol⁻² dm⁶ s⁻¹).

## Orders and mechanisms

- Species in the rate equation take part in the rate-determining step or a step before it.
- Order = number of particles of that species involved up to and including the rate-determining step.
- Zero-order species react **after** the rate-determining step.
- A valid mechanism: slow step matches the rate equation **and** steps add up to the overall equation.

**Standard examples**
- Propanone + iodine (acid-catalysed): Rate = k[CH₃COCH₃][H⁺]; iodine reacts after the slow step.
- Bromoethane + OH⁻: Rate = k[CH₃CH₂Br][OH⁻]; one step involving both.
- 2-bromo-2-methylpropane + OH⁻: Rate = k[(CH₃)₃CBr]; C–Br breaks in the slow step, OH⁻ reacts later.

## Required practical 8

| Method | What you do | Rate measure |
|---|---|---|
| Initial rate | Several runs; vary one concentration; keep total volume and temperature fixed | 1/t (clock) or early gradient |
| Continuous monitoring | One run; measure concentration (or a property linked to it) at intervals | tangents to the concentration–time curve |

Quenching samples: for acid-catalysed iodination, add sodium hydrogencarbonate to remove the acid catalyst, then titrate iodine with sodium thiosulfate.

## Exam traps

- Assigning the full rate change to one reactant when two concentrations changed.
- Writing a zero-order reactant into the rate equation as [X]⁰ without simplifying.
- Leaving a catalyst out of the rate equation when the data show it has an order.
- Claiming a zero-order reactant is "not needed" in the reaction: it is still used up, just after the slow step.
- Varying total volume in an initial-rate series, so concentrations are no longer proportional to volumes.

## Quick self-test

1. Why must a rate equation be found by experiment?
2. Describe the concentration–time graph for a reactant that is zero order.
3. How do you find the rate at a particular time from a curved concentration–time graph?
4. In a clock reaction, how is the time measured related to the initial rate?
5. Describe the rate–concentration graph for a first-order reactant.
6. The concentration of X, [X], is tripled and the rate increases nine times. State the order with respect to X.
7. A is first order. In two experiments [A] doubles and [B] triples, and the rate increases six times. State the order with respect to B.
8. Rate = k[A]². What does this show about the rate-determining step?
9. A reactant is zero order. What does this tell you about when it reacts?
10. A mechanism has a slow step A + B → X and a fast step X + B → C. Write the rate equation it predicts.
11. Give two ways to follow a reaction by continuous monitoring.
12. Why is the total volume kept constant in an initial rate series?

### Answers

1. Orders depend on the mechanism (the rate-determining step), which cannot be read from the overall balanced equation.
2. A straight line with a negative gradient (constant rate).
3. Draw a tangent at that time and calculate its gradient; the rate is the magnitude of the gradient.
4. Initial rate is proportional to 1/t.
5. A straight line through the origin.
6. 3² = 9, so **second order**.
7. Doubling [A] accounts for × 2; the remaining × 3 matches [B] × 3, so **first order** in B.
8. The rate-determining step involves **two** particles of A (and no other reactant before or in it).
9. It reacts in a step **after** the rate-determining step.
10. **Rate = k[A][B]**.
11. Any two: measuring colour with a colorimeter; collecting gas volume; recording loss of mass; removing, quenching and titrating samples.
12. So that the concentration of each reactant is proportional to the volume of its solution used.
