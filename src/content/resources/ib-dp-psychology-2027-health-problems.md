The health and well-being context of IB Psychology (first assessment 2027) asks you to understand **one or more health problems** from this list: obesity, drug misuse or abuse, or social media addiction. The guide gives three areas of study: the **prevalence** of health problems, the role of **social learning**, and the role of **stress**. This guide teaches each area using obesity as the main thread and shows how to switch to drug misuse or social media addiction. It does not repeat the general account of determinants of health and the health belief model, which the health psychology study guide covers.

Related: [health psychology study guide](/resources/ib-dp-psychology-health-psychology-study-guide/), [risk and protective factors](/resources/ib-dp-psychology-risk-and-protective-factors/), [revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-psychology-2027-health-problems-revision-notes/) and [practice set](/resources/ib-dp-psychology-2027-health-problems-practice/).

## Why these three areas

Health problems are not only individual. They are patterns in populations, learned in social settings, and shaped by stress. The three areas give three lenses: **where and when** a problem occurs (prevalence), **how it is acquired** (social learning) and **what makes it worse** (stress). A strong answer uses the lenses together and keeps asking what the evidence shows.

## Defining the health problems

- **Obesity** is excess body fat that harms health. It is commonly defined for adults as a body mass index (BMI) of 30 or more. BMI is a convenient **measurement** but a limited one: it does not separate muscle from fat or show where fat is stored.
- **Drug misuse or abuse** is use of a drug in ways that harm the user or others, such as nicotine, alcohol or illegal drugs. Definitions of misuse differ by legal and cultural context.
- **Social media addiction** is a contested label for compulsive, harmful use of social media. It is not classified as an official disorder in the same way as substance addiction, and researchers use scales that measure features such as preoccupation, loss of control and conflict with other activities.

Remember that a definition is a choice about measurement, and the choice changes the prevalence figures.

## 1. Prevalence of health problems

The guide has two learning objectives: **factors that may explain changes in prevalence in a population over time** and **factors that may explain differences in prevalence between populations**. The word "may" signals that you should present explanations as plausible and tested, not proven.

### Changes over time

For obesity, the rise in many countries over recent decades is too fast for genes to be the explanation, since the gene pool changes slowly. Factors that may explain it include:
- **food environment:** cheaper, energy-dense and highly processed foods, larger portions and constant availability;
- **activity:** more sedentary jobs, screen time and car travel;
- **marketing:** advertising of food to children and young people;
- **economic and urban change:** work patterns, access to safe places to play and to fresh food.

For social media addiction, prevalence may rise as smartphone access spreads and platforms use design features that encourage frequent checking. For drug misuse, changes may follow price, availability, law, and shifts in social norms.

### Differences between populations

Differences between countries, income groups or cultures may reflect:
- **income and education,** which affect access to healthy food, health information and healthcare;
- **culture,** which shapes norms about food, body size, alcohol or drugs;
- **policy,** such as taxes, advertising rules and school meal standards;
- **measurement and sampling,** since a difference in recorded rates can come from different definitions, survey methods or reporting honesty.

**Worked example.** A country reports that obesity among children has doubled in 20 years, while a neighbouring country with lower income has a lower rate. A strong answer explains two plausible factors for the change over time (for example growth in processed food and screen time) and two for the difference (for example lower exposure to food marketing, or more physical work), then notes that the data may not be comparable and that the poorest groups in each country may not be represented in surveys.

**Evaluation.** Population data show real patterns, but they are mostly correlational, so we cannot be sure which factor is causal. Natural experiments, such as the introduction of a sugar tax, give stronger evidence. The risk of the **ecological fallacy**, assuming a population pattern applies to each individual, is also worth stating.

**Concepts:** change (trends and barriers to change), measurement (definitions and BMI), causality (multiple interacting factors), bias (sampling).

## 2. Social learning

**Social learning theory** (Bandura) says behaviour is learned by observing and imitating models, especially when a model is rewarded. The four conditions are attention, retention, reproduction and motivation.

**Obesity and eating behaviour.** Children copy the eating patterns they see at home and among friends. Birch and colleagues (1980) found that children's expressed preferences for vegetables could shift after they saw peers choosing them, which suggests modelling influences food choices. Marketing uses models too: Harris, Bargh and Brownell (2009) reported that children ate more snacks after viewing programmes containing food adverts than after programmes without them, showing how observation of appealing models can influence eating. The links to causality are strong in these experiments, but the effects were measured in short periods and in controlled settings.

**Drug misuse.** Adolescents are more likely to start smoking or vaping if friends or family do, and if they see these behaviours linked to status or enjoyment. Vicarious reinforcement matters: seeing a model gain social rewards makes imitation more attractive.

**Social media addiction.** Users observe peers receiving likes and attention, which models the rewards of constant posting and checking. Influencers act as role models.

**Using social learning to change behaviour.** The same theory supports prevention: positive role models, peer-led programmes, family meals with healthy eating, and campaigns that show non-use as normal.

**Evaluation.**
- Strength: explains why behaviour clusters in families and peer groups, and it supports practical interventions.
- Limitation: it underplays biology (such as appetite regulation and addiction pathways), and early evidence was from short-term experiments with children.
- Selection effect: people choose friends who behave like them, so similarity does not always mean influence.
- **Concepts:** causality, change, responsibility (advertising to children).

## 3. Stress and health

The guide asks for **the role of stress in one or more health problems**. Stress is a response to demands that exceed perceived resources. Selye's general adaptation syndrome describes alarm, resistance and exhaustion. In the short term the stress response is adaptive, but chronic stress keeps cortisol and other stress hormones elevated.

There are two routes by which stress affects health problems:
- **Direct (physiological) route.** Chronic cortisol can affect appetite, fat storage and the immune system. Epel and colleagues (2000) reported that women with more abdominal fat produced more cortisol in response to laboratory stressors, which is consistent with a link between stress responses and fat distribution, though it cannot show which came first. Kiecolt-Glaser and colleagues (1984) found weaker immune function in medical students during exams than before, which shows stress can affect the body.
- **Indirect (behavioural) route.** People under stress may eat more energy-dense food, sleep less, smoke or drink more, or use social media to escape. These coping behaviours are the pathway to obesity, drug misuse and compulsive use.

**Worked example.** A student under exam pressure sleeps four hours a night, eats snacks to keep going and scrolls on a phone to relax. By the direct route, short sleep and high cortisol can increase appetite. By the indirect route, the coping habits become a pattern. A good answer states both routes and a limitation: stress is usually measured by self-report, so people who are already unwell may report more stress.

**Evaluation.**
- Strength: physiological measures such as cortisol give objective data, and the model explains why a health problem can start or worsen during hard times.
- Limitation: not everyone under stress develops the problem, because individual differences and social support (the buffering effect) matter. Studies are often correlational, so the direction of cause is unclear (does obesity cause stress through stigma?).
- **Concepts:** causality (bidirectional ambiguity), measurement, change (stress management as a prevention strategy).

## Evidence about change

Although prevention and treatment have a separate area in the guide, evidence on change links to all three lenses. For example, Hunt and colleagues (2018) asked university students to limit social media to a short daily time for three weeks and reported reductions in loneliness and depression compared with a control group. The sample was small and self-reported, and the study did not test lasting change, but it shows how to test cause and effect with an experiment.

## Putting it together in a Section C response

A 15-mark response should combine at least two lenses and two concepts. A possible line for obesity: prevalence data show a rapid rise that genes cannot explain, pointing to environmental change. Social learning explains how eating patterns spread through families, peers and media, and stress explains why behaviour changes under pressure. The evidence is mostly correlational, so causality is uncertain, and measurement choices such as BMI shape the numbers. A strong conclusion weighs these, for example by saying that environmental and social explanations are best supported at population level, but individual differences remain.

## Common mistakes

- Describing the problem and its harm without answering the area of study.
- Presenting prevalence rates with no comment on how they were measured.
- Describing Bandura's Bobo doll study and not applying social learning to the health problem.
- Treating stress as a single cause. Stress interacts with biology, behaviour and social support.
- Using stigmatising language about weight or addiction.
