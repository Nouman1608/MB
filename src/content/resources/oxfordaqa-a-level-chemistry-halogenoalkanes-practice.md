The ten questions below test section **3.3.3 Halogenoalkanes**, points 3.3.3.1 (nucleophilic
substitution) and 3.3.3.2 (elimination), as set out in the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level
Chemistry (9620) specification, Version 5.3, used for exams from May/June 2020. This is International AS
content. Expect it on the Unit 2 paper, Organic 1 and Physical 1; the Unit 5 practical and synoptic paper
may revisit it.

If the mechanisms are new to you, start with the [halogenoalkanes study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-halogenoalkanes/)
or the shorter [halogenoalkanes notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-halogenoalkanes-revision-notes/).
For planning, use the [OxfordAQA 9620 hub](/boards/oxfordaqa/a-level/chemistry/), the
[9620 topic checklist](/checklists/oxfordaqa/a-level/chemistry/) and our free
[diagnostic quizzes](/diagnostics/). Relative atomic masses to use: Br = 79.9,
C = 12.0, Cl = 35.5, H = 1.0, K = 39.1, N = 14.0, O = 16.0.

## Questions

**1.** Here are five species: NH₄⁺, CN⁻, CH₄, NH₃, H⁺.

**(a)** Define the term nucleophile. **[1]**
**(b)** Identify the two species in the list that act as nucleophiles towards halogenoalkanes, and give the
feature they share. **[2]**

**2.** Nucleophiles react with a bromoalkane at one particular carbon atom. Say which one, and account for
this in terms of how the bonding electrons are shared. **[2]**

**3.** 1-Bromo-2,3-dimethylbutane, BrCH₂CH(CH₃)CH(CH₃)CH₃, is heated under reflux with potassium
cyanide in aqueous ethanol.

**(a)** Name the organic product. **[1]**
**(b)** Using curly arrows, outline how this substitution takes place. **[3]**
**(c)** A 4.947 g sample of 1-bromo-2,3-dimethylbutane gives 2.53 g of the product. Calculate the
percentage yield. **[3]**

**4.** Chlorocyclohexane, C₆H₁₁Cl, reacts with potassium hydroxide.

**(a)** Outline the mechanism for the reaction in which hydroxide ions act as a nucleophile. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage atom economy for forming cyclohexanol, C₆H₁₁OH, by substitution and
for forming cyclohexene, C₆H₁₀, by elimination. Both reactions use one KOH per C₆H₁₁Cl; the inorganic
products are KCl, and KCl + H₂O respectively. **[3]**
**(c)** State the conditions that would increase the proportion of cyclohexene. **[1]**

**5.** 3-Iodopentane, CH₃CH₂CHICH₂CH₃, is sealed in a tube with an ethanolic solution of ammonia, in excess, and heated.

**(a)** Outline the mechanism for the formation of the primary amine. **[4]**
**(b)** Write an overall equation for the reaction. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain why ammonia is used in excess. **[1]**

**6.** Data: mean bond enthalpy of C–Cl = 339 kJ mol⁻¹; of C–I = 240 kJ mol⁻¹.

A student states: "Chlorocyclopentane will react with hydroxide ions faster than iodocyclopentane,
because the C–Cl bond is more polar." Evaluate this statement. **[3]**

**7.** A student describes the mechanism for 1-bromo-3,3-dimethylbutane reacting with hydroxide ions:

"An arrow goes from the hydrogen atom of OH⁻ to the carbon bonded to bromine. A second arrow goes from
the bromine atom to that carbon. The products are 3,3-dimethylbutan-1-ol and Br⁻."

Identify **three** errors or omissions in the account of the mechanism. **[3]**

**8.** 2-Bromo-2-methylbutane, (CH₃)₂CBrCH₂CH₃, is heated with potassium hydroxide dissolved in ethanol.

**(a)** Explain the role of the hydroxide ion when an alkene forms, and how this differs from its role
when an alcohol forms. **[2]**
**(b)** Name the two alkenes that can form. **[2]**
**(c)** Outline the mechanism for the formation of **one** of these alkenes. **[3]**
**(d)** 12.07 g of 2-bromo-2-methylbutane gives 3.36 g of the mixture of alkenes, C₅H₁₀. Calculate the
percentage yield of alkenes. **[3]**
**(e)** Suggest one reason, other than mechanical losses, why the yield of alkenes is below 100%. **[1]**

**9.** 3-Chloro-3-methylpentane, CH₃CH₂C(CH₃)ClCH₂CH₃, reacts with potassium hydroxide. Substitution and
elimination happen concurrently.

**(a)** Write an equation for the substitution reaction and name the organic product. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why the same reagent can give two different types of product. **[2]**
**(c)** Draw or write the structures of every alkene that can form, counting stereoisomers separately,
and state how many there are. **[3]**
**(d)** Explain why one of the alkenes exists as E and Z isomers. **[1]**

**10.** For each equation, name the type of reaction and give the role of the inorganic reagent.

**(a)** C₆H₁₁Cl + OH⁻ → C₆H₁₀ + H₂O + Cl⁻ **[1]**
**(b)** C₅H₉I + CN⁻ → C₅H₉CN + I⁻ **[1]**
**(c)** CH₃CH₂CHICH₂CH₃ + 2NH₃ → CH₃CH₂CH(NH₂)CH₂CH₃ + NH₄I **[1]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** An electron pair donor (a species that donates a lone pair to form a covalent bond) [1]
**(b)** **CN⁻ and NH₃** [1]; both have a lone pair of electrons to donate [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* "Negative ion" is not the shared feature, since NH₃ is neutral; answers built
around the lone pair are the ones that earn credit.

**2.** The carbon joined to bromine: the C–Br bonding pair is drawn towards bromine, the more electronegative atom, leaving that carbon δ+ [1]. The
electron-deficient carbon attracts the lone pair of a nucleophile [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Say which atom is δ+ and why; "the bond is polar" alone does not explain the
attack.

**3. (a)** **3,4-Dimethylpentanenitrile**, NCCH₂CH(CH₃)CH(CH₃)CH₃ [1]
**(b)** δ+ on the CH₂ carbon and δ− on Br [1]; curly arrow leaving the lone pair on cyanide's **carbon**
and ending at the CH₂ carbon [1]; C–Br electrons moving onto Br, with Br⁻ shown as a product [1].
**(c)** Mr(C₆H₁₃Br) = 164.9; n = 4.947 ÷ 164.9 = 0.0300 mol [1]; theoretical mass of C₇H₁₃N
(Mr 111.0) = 0.0300 × 111.0 = 3.33 g [1]; yield = 2.53 ÷ 3.33 × 100 = **76.0%** [1] **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* An arrow drawn from the N of cyanide would join nitrogen to the chain, which does not
make this nitrile, so it is unlikely to be credited even if the rest is right.

**4. (a)** Dipole on C–Cl (Cδ+, Clδ−) [1]; arrow from a lone pair on O of :OH⁻ to the carbon bonded to Cl
[1]; arrow from the C–Cl bond to Cl, giving cyclohexanol and Cl⁻ [1].
**(b)** Total Mr of reactants = 118.5 + 56.1 = 174.6 [1];
substitution: 100.0 ÷ 174.6 × 100 = **57.3%** [1];
elimination: 82.0 ÷ 174.6 × 100 = **47.0%** [1]
**(c)** KOH dissolved in **ethanol**, heated (rather than aqueous and warm) [1] **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* Atom economy uses the total mass of all reactants in the balanced equation; dividing
by the Mr of chlorocyclohexane alone would give 84.4% for cyclohexanol, which is too high.

**5. (a)** Nitrogen's lone pair attacks carbon 3 while the C–I electrons move onto iodine (two arrows)
[1]; intermediate CH₃CH₂CH(NH₃⁺)CH₂CH₃ with I⁻ [1]; another NH₃ uses its lone pair to take a proton
from N⁺ [1]; the N–H electrons return to nitrogen, giving the amine and NH₄⁺ [1].
**(b)** **CH₃CH₂CHICH₂CH₃ + 2NH₃ → CH₃CH₂CH(NH₂)CH₂CH₃ + NH₄I** [1]
**(c)** The amine product also has a lone pair and could attack more halogenoalkane; excess ammonia
makes reaction with NH₃ much more likely [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* The positive charge must be on nitrogen in the intermediate; leaving it off or
placing it on carbon tends to cost the intermediate mark.

**6.** The statement is wrong: iodocyclopentane reacts faster [1]. The C–I bond enthalpy (240 kJ mol⁻¹)
is lower than C–Cl (339 kJ mol⁻¹), so less energy is needed to break it [1]. Rate depends on breaking the
C–X bond, so bond enthalpy, not polarity, is the deciding factor [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* "Evaluate" needs a verdict as well as the reasoning; quote the data to support it.

**7.** The first arrow should start from a lone pair on the **oxygen**, not from hydrogen [1]. The second
arrow should start from the C–Br **bond** and point to Br, not from Br to carbon [1]. The dipole (Cδ+ and
Brδ−) is missing [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* The products are correct; spotting a "mistake" that is not one gains nothing, so
check each claim before you criticise it.

**8. (a)** In elimination OH⁻ acts as a **base**: it removes H⁺ from a carbon neighbouring the one
bonded to bromine [1]; in substitution it acts as a **nucleophile**, donating its lone pair to the δ+ carbon [1].
**(b)** **2-Methylbut-1-ene** [1]; **2-methylbut-2-ene** [1].
**(c)** For 2-methylbut-2-ene: hydroxide's oxygen lone pair attacks a hydrogen of the CH₂ group [1];
the electrons of that C–H bond shift into the gap between C3 and C2, making the double bond [1]; the
C–Br pair goes to bromine, and the products are the alkene, H₂O and Br⁻ [1]. (For 2-methylbut-1-ene, the H comes from a CH₃ carbon.)
**(d)** n(C₅H₁₁Br) = 12.07 ÷ 150.9 = 0.0800 mol [1]; theoretical mass of C₅H₁₀ = 0.0800 × 70.0 = 5.60 g
[1]; yield = 3.36 ÷ 5.60 × 100 = **60.0%** [1]
**(e)** Substitution happens at the same time, so some of the halogenoalkane becomes
2-methylbutan-2-ol instead [1]. **[11]**
*Examiner insight:* In (c) the hydroxide arrow must end on a hydrogen; an arrow to carbon describes
substitution, so the elimination mechanism is unlikely to gain that mark.

**9. (a)** CH₃CH₂C(CH₃)ClCH₂CH₃ + OH⁻ → CH₃CH₂C(CH₃)(OH)CH₂CH₃ + Cl⁻ [1]; **3-methylpentan-3-ol** [1]
**(b)** OH⁻ has a lone pair, so it can donate it to the δ+ carbon as a nucleophile (substitution) [1], or
use it to accept a proton from an adjacent carbon as a base (elimination) [1].
**(c)** CH₂=C(CH₂CH₃)₂, 2-ethylbut-1-ene [1]; CH₃CH=C(CH₃)CH₂CH₃, 3-methylpent-2-ene, as **E and Z**
forms [1]; **three** alkenes in total [1].
**(d)** Rotation about C=C is restricted, and in 3-methylpent-2-ene neither alkene carbon has two identical
substituents (one has H and CH₃, the other CH₃ and C₂H₅) [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Taking H from either ethyl group leads to one and the same alkene, because the ethyl groups match;
listing it twice suggests you have not checked for duplicates.

**10. (a)** Elimination; OH⁻ acts as a **base** [1]
**(b)** Nucleophilic substitution; CN⁻ acts as a **nucleophile** [1]
**(c)** Nucleophilic substitution; NH₃ acts as a **nucleophile** (the second NH₃ acts as a base) [1] **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Each mark needs both parts, the reaction type and the role; "substitution" without
"nucleophilic" is weaker than the full name.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Arrows starting from a hydrogen atom or from a negative charge sign rather than from a lone pair.
- Using the nitrogen end of cyanide as the nucleophile.
- Missing the second ammonia molecule and the NH₄⁺ product.
- Dividing by the halogenoalkane's Mr alone in atom economy calculations.
- Explaining the reactivity order with polarity instead of C–X bond enthalpy.
- Removing hydrogen from the carbon that carries the halogen in an elimination.
- Counting equivalent alkenes twice, or missing E and Z forms.
- Rounding moles too early, which shifts a percentage yield in the last figure.
