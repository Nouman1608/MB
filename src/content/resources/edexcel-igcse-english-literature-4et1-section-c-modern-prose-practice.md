> **These are original questions written for Marlbridge**, for revision and practice on this
> content. They work with any novel on the 4ET1 modern prose list and do not quote the set texts.
> They are **not** reproduced past-paper questions; examination boards hold copyright in their own
> papers. Use these alongside the official past papers available free from your board.

Related: [Section C Modern Prose study guide](/resources/edexcel-igcse-english-literature-4et1-section-c-modern-prose/)
and [revision notes](/resources/edexcel-igcse-english-literature-4et1-section-c-modern-prose-revision-notes/).

In the real exam you answer one 40-mark question from a choice of two on your novel, closed book,
marked on AO1 and AO4, in about 45 minutes. The set list changes when the specification is revised,
so check that your novel is on the list for your series.

## Section A: planning and writing drills

Answer these about the novel you have studied.

**1.** For the question "How does the writer present hope in the novel?", write a two-sentence
overview that answers the question and indicates how the presentation changes. **[3]**

**2.** Here is a sentence from a student's answer: "Later on in the book, the main character goes
back to the place where it all started and things are very different." Explain two reasons why it
earns little credit, and rewrite it for your own novel. **[4]**

**3.** Choose a relationship in your novel. Identify four moments you would use to track it and
give a one-line reason for each choice. **[4]**

**4.** Write one sentence that links a specific moment in your novel to a specific social,
historical or cultural context. **[3]**

**5.** List five short quotations from your novel, from memory, and next to each name two themes or
characters it could support. **[5]**

**6.** In the exam you are offered two questions on your novel. Give two criteria you would use to
choose between them. **[2]**

## Section B: essay questions

Answer each in about 45 minutes, without the text.

**7.** Explore how the writer presents the relationship between a parent, or parent figure, and a
child. **[40]**

**8.** How does the writer present a character who is treated as an outsider? **[40]**

**9.** Explore the importance of one place or setting in the novel. **[40]**

**10.** How does the writer present the effects of change on a family or community? **[40]**

---

## Answers

### Section A

**1.** 1 mark for a direct answer to the question, 1 for indicating development across the novel, 1
for a link to context or the writer's purpose. For example (*Of Mice and Men*): "Steinbeck presents
hope through George and Lennie's dream of owning land, which briefly seems possible when others join
it. By the ending the dream has collapsed, suggesting that for migrant workers in the Depression hope
was necessary but almost impossible to achieve."

**2.** Reasons (1 mark each, up to 2): it is vague ("the main character", "it all started"), so it
shows little close knowledge; it narrates rather than makes a claim; it gives no textual reference;
it does not say what the change means. Rewrite (up to 2): names the character and the moment, makes
a claim about development and, ideally, links it to context.

**3.** 1 mark per moment with a valid reason, up to 4. Strong answers include the opening, a turning
point, a later development and the ending, so that the plan covers the whole novel.

**4.** 1 for a specific moment, 1 for a specific named context, 1 for explaining how the context
illuminates the moment. For example (*Things Fall Apart*): "When Nwoye is drawn to the missionaries'
church, Achebe shows how the arrival of Christianity under colonial rule divided Igbo families, as
well as challenging Okonkwo's idea of strength."

**5.** 1 mark for each accurate, short quotation with two plausible links, up to 5. Check accuracy
against your text afterwards.

**6.** Any two (1 each): which question lets me range across the whole novel; which one I can support
with the quotations I know best; which focus links most naturally to context; which I can answer
directly in my first sentence.

### Section B: how to mark questions 7 to 10

Essays are marked holistically across AO1 and AO4. The bands below are Marlbridge's illustrative
guidance, not Pearson's published mark scheme.

| Marks | Typical features |
|---|---|
| 33-40 | Assured, perceptive argument across the whole novel; precise, well-chosen references; context integrated to explain the writer's choices |
| 25-32 | Sustained, well-supported argument; relevant context linked to the text throughout |
| 17-24 | Clear response with relevant references; context present but sometimes general |
| 9-16 | Some understanding; narrative tendency; context limited or separate |
| 1-8 | Simple, mainly narrative response; little reference or context |

**7. Indicative content (examples from several set novels; credit any valid text):**

- *To Kill a Mockingbird*: Atticus as a parent who teaches empathy and integrity by example, tested
  when he defends Tom Robinson; Scout's retrospective narration lets the reader see what she learns.
  Context: segregation and racial injustice in 1930s Alabama, and the novel's publication during the
  civil rights era.
- *The Joy Luck Club*: mothers and daughters separated by culture and language; for example,
  Jing-mei's conflict with her mother over piano lessons and expectations. Context: Chinese
  immigration to the United States and the gap between generations.
- *The Whale Rider*: Kahu's relationship with her great-grandfather Koro Apirana, who resists her
  because she is a girl. Context: Māori tradition and leadership in a changing New Zealand.
- Strong answers track how the relationship changes and what the writer suggests about duty, love and
  inheritance.

**8. Indicative content:**

- *Of Mice and Men*: Crooks, isolated by race; Curley's wife, unnamed and without companionship;
  Candy, fearful of being discarded. Context: segregation, women's limited roles, and insecure migrant
  labour during the Depression.
- *To Kill a Mockingbird*: Boo Radley, feared through rumour then revealed as protective; Tom
  Robinson, condemned by prejudice. Context: the racial and social hierarchy of the 1930s American
  South.
- *Things Fall Apart*: Okonkwo's exile to his mother's village after accidentally killing a man, and
  the outcasts drawn to the new church. Context: Igbo law and custom, and the effects of colonial
  missionary activity.
- *Klara and the Sun*: Klara as an artificial "friend" whose status in the family is uncertain.
  Context: contemporary debates about technology and what it means to be human.

**9. Indicative content:**

- Track the setting at two or three points, each tied to a claim about character or theme. For
  example, the clearing by the river that opens and closes *Of Mice and Men* moves from safety and
  hope to the end of the dream; Umuofia in *Things Fall Apart* moves from a confident, ordered society
  to one divided by colonial change; Maycomb in *To Kill a Mockingbird* is both familiar and exposed as
  unjust.
- Context explains why the setting matters: rural poverty, colonial rule, segregation, or the
  community the novel describes.

**10. Indicative content:**

- *Things Fall Apart* is the clearest example: the arrival of missionaries and colonial government
  breaks the community's unity, and Okonkwo's response shows the cost of resisting change alone.
- *The Whale Rider*: the community's survival depends on accepting a new kind of leader, so change is
  presented as renewal.
- *The Joy Luck Club*: migration changes families, and daughters' assimilation creates distance
  from their mothers' pasts.
- Strong answers compare the beginning and end states of the family or community and use context to
  explain the writer's attitude to change.
