This set tests section 3.2 **Households** of the Cambridge O Level Economics 2281 syllabus for examination in 2026: the influences on spending, saving and borrowing, between different households and over time. All households and figures are invented. Teaching is in the [households study guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-economics-2281-households/) and the [households revision notes](/resources/cambridge-o-level-economics-2281-households-revision-notes/).

## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Define saving. **[1]**

**2.** Which change is most likely to increase household saving?

A: A fall in the rate of interest

B: A rise in consumer confidence

C: Fears of rising unemployment

D: Banks making loans easier to obtain

**[1]**

**3.** Which household is most likely to be dissaving?

A: A young worker buying a first home with a mortgage

B: A retired couple living partly on their savings

C: A middle-aged worker paying into a pension

D: A student saving part of a holiday job wage

**[1]**

**4.** The table shows two households' monthly figures.

| Household | Disposable income ($) | Saving ($) |
|---|---|---|
| X | 1,200 | 60 |
| Y | 3,600 | 720 |

**(a)** Calculate the share of income saved by each household. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain one reason for the difference. **[2]**

**5.** A household with a monthly disposable income of $2,400 owes $45,000 on a variable-rate loan, paying interest only. The interest rate rises from 3% to 5.5% a year.

**(a)** Calculate the monthly interest before and after the rise. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate interest as a percentage of monthly income after the rise. **[1]**

**6.** State two influences on household borrowing other than the rate of interest. **[2]**

### Section B: structured questions

**7.** Explain why a rise in interest rates may reduce spending by some households but increase spending by others. **[4]**

**8.** Explain how a fall in consumer confidence affects household spending, saving and borrowing. **[4]**

**9.** Explain how a person's spending, saving and borrowing are likely to change over their lifetime. **[4]**

**10.** House prices in Country H rise strongly, although incomes do not change. Explain why household spending may still rise. **[3]**

**11.** Analyse why a high-income household usually saves a larger share of its income than a low-income household. **[3]**

**12.** Discuss whether a cut in interest rates will increase household spending. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** Disposable income that is not spent on goods and services [1]. **[1]**

**2.** C [1]. Fear of job losses raises precautionary saving. A, B and D all tend to reduce saving. **[1]**

**3.** B [1]. Dissaving means spending more than current income by drawing on past savings, which is typical in retirement. **[1]**

**4. (a)** X: 60 ÷ 1,200 × 100 = 5% [1]. Y: 720 ÷ 3,600 × 100 = 20% [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** Household X must spend most of its income on essentials such as food and rent [1], so little is left to save, while Y has covered its needs and can save a larger share [1]. **[2]**

**5. (a)** Before: 45,000 × 0.03 ÷ 12 = $112.50 a month [1]. After: 45,000 × 0.055 ÷ 12 = $206.25 a month [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** 206.25 ÷ 2,400 × 100 = 8.6% [1]. **[1]**

**6.** Any two: income; confidence; availability of credit (banks' lending rules); expected price changes; stage of life; attitudes to debt [1 each]. **[2]**

**7.** Households with variable-rate loans or mortgages must pay more interest [1], so their disposable income falls and they cut spending [1]. Households with large savings earn more interest [1], so their income rises and they may spend more of it [1]. **[4]**

**8.** Lower confidence means households fear job losses or lower future income [1]. They increase precautionary saving [1], delay buying expensive items such as cars and furniture, so spending falls [1], and avoid taking on new debt, so borrowing falls [1]. **[4]**

**9.** When young, income is low but expected to rise, so people borrow for education, a car or a home and save little [1]. In middle age income is near its peak, so they repay debts and save more [1], for example for children's education or retirement [1]. In retirement income falls, so they run down savings (dissave) to finance spending [1]. **[4]**

**10.** Households' wealth rises because their homes are worth more [1], so they feel better off and save less of their income [1]. They can also borrow more using the house as security, which allows extra spending [1]. **[3]**

**11.** A low-income household must spend almost all its income on essentials [1]. A high-income household can cover essentials with part of its income [1], so it has more left after spending and can save a larger share, and is also less likely to need to borrow for emergencies [1]. **[3]**

**12.** Yes: borrowing becomes cheaper, so households take out more loans for cars, furniture and homes [1]; variable-rate borrowers pay less interest and have more disposable income to spend [1]; saving earns less, so some households save less and spend more [1]. No: if confidence is low, households may keep saving as a precaution whatever the interest rate [1]; households with heavy debts may use the saving to repay debt, and savers lose interest income and may spend less [1]. Judgement: a cut is most likely to raise spending when confidence is reasonable and many households have variable-rate debts; in a deep recession its effect may be small [1]. **[6]**

*Examiner insight:* Show that the same cut affects borrowers and savers differently. That is what turns a one-sided answer into a discussion.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Dividing saving by spending instead of by income when calculating the share saved.
- Forgetting to divide annual interest by 12 for a monthly figure.
- Saying higher interest rates cut everyone's spending.
- Confusing income (a flow) with wealth (a stock).
- Describing life stages without saying why behaviour changes at each stage.
- Ending a discussion without a judgement about when the effect is large or small.
