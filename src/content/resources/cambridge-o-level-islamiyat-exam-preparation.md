Cambridge O Level Islamiyat 2058 is assessed by two compulsory, externally assessed papers of equal
weight. Each lasts 1 hour 30 minutes, carries 50 marks and contributes 50% of the qualification.
Crucially, each paper prints **five questions, of which Question 1 and Question 2 are compulsory and
two further questions are chosen** from the remaining three. Paper 1 covers the Qur'an, the Prophet
Muhammad (pbuh) and the first Islamic community; Paper 2 covers the Hadiths, the Rightly Guided
Caliphs, and the Articles of Faith and Pillars of Islam. All answers are written in English. These
notes complement the site's content guides to
[Paper 1](/resources/o-level-islamiyat-paper-1/) and
[Paper 2](/resources/o-level-islamiyat-paper-2/).

## Two questions are compulsory -- so two areas of the syllabus cannot be dropped

The four-from-five structure is often misread as "you can leave out most of the syllabus". It cannot
be. Questions 1 and 2 must be answered whatever they happen to be about, and only the remaining two
are chosen. **Exam-preparation priority**: revise for full coverage of each paper's content, and treat
the choice as insurance against a difficult question rather than as permission to omit topics. A
candidate who has revised selectively and meets a compulsory question on an omitted area loses a
quarter of that paper before the choice is even reached.

## Four questions in ninety minutes is 22 minutes each -- including planning

Fifty marks are spread across four answered questions, and 90 minutes divided four ways leaves roughly
22 minutes per question, or a little less once the choice itself has been made. **Exam-preparation
priority**: practise complete answers to a 20-minute limit rather than writing untimed practice
essays. The commonest way to lose marks on this paper is not ignorance -- it is a superb first answer
followed by a fourth answer that is two paragraphs long because the clock ran out.

## Choosing the optional questions well is worth several marks

Choosing between the three optional questions deserves a deliberate two or three minutes. **Exam-
preparation priority**: read all three in full and choose on the basis of the *specific detail you can
supply*, not on which topic feels most familiar. Familiarity with a topic and the ability to produce
named, dated, specific evidence about it are different things, and it is the second that earns marks.
A slightly less comfortable topic on which you can name three specific incidents beats a comfortable
one on which you can only generalise.

## Answer the question set, not the topic named in it

A question naming a caliph, a pillar or a passage is not an invitation to write everything known about
it. **Exam-preparation priority**: before writing, underline the actual demand -- importance,
qualities, consequences, teaching -- and check every paragraph against it. Comprehensive but
undirected answers score consistently below shorter answers that address the demand throughout, and
this is the single most common structural weakness at this level.

## Specific evidence beats general reference

Answers improve sharply when a general statement is anchored to something specific: a named person, a
named place, a particular incident, a particular passage or Hadith. **Exam-preparation priority**:
when revising, convert each theme into a short list of two or three concrete items you could cite
under pressure. Revision that produces understanding but no retrievable specifics tends to produce
answers that are true, relevant and unmemorable to a marker.

## Worked structure: a four-paragraph answer under a 20-minute limit

The structure below is an original planning model written for this resource, not a reproduction of any
official mark scheme, past paper or examiner report.

```
Minutes 0-2: plan
Underline the demand word. Note three specific items you will cite
(person / place / incident / passage). Decide the order.

Minutes 2-7: paragraph 1 - the most substantial point
State the point. Give the specific evidence. Say what it shows.

Minutes 7-12: paragraph 2 - the second point
Same three-part shape. Choose a point that adds a different angle,
not a variation of the first.

Minutes 12-17: paragraph 3 - the third point
Same shape again.

Minutes 17-20: conclusion
Two or three sentences that answer the demand directly, referring
back to the points made rather than introducing new material.
```

Each paragraph follows point-evidence-significance. The significance sentence is the one candidates
omit under time pressure, and it is the one that converts an accurate paragraph into a
question-answering one.

## Before/during exam checklist

- **Before the exams**: revise both papers for full coverage -- the choice does not license omitting
  topics; build a short list of two or three specific citable items for each theme; practise complete
  answers to a 20-minute limit rather than untimed ones.
- **At the start of each paper**: spend two or three minutes reading all three optional questions in
  full, and choose on the specific evidence you can supply rather than on topic familiarity.
- **During each answer**: underline the demand word first, and check each paragraph against it.
- **In every paragraph**: state the point, give specific evidence, then say what it shows -- do not
  let the third element drop when time is tight.

## Self-test

1. Why does the four-from-five structure not allow selective revision?
2. Roughly how much time does each answered question get, and what does that imply for practice?
3. On what basis should the two optional questions be chosen?
4. Which element of a paragraph is most often dropped under time pressure, and why does it matter?

**Answers:** 1. Because Questions 1 and 2 are compulsory whatever their subject matter, so an omitted
topic can appear in a question that must be answered; only the remaining two questions are chosen.
2. About 22 minutes, or a little less once the choice has been made -- which is why practice should be
timed to roughly 20 minutes per complete answer rather than untimed. 3. On the specific, citable
evidence you can supply about each -- named people, places, incidents, passages -- rather than on
which topic feels most familiar. 4. The significance sentence: it is what connects the evidence to the
demand the question actually made, and without it a paragraph is accurate description rather than an
answer.
