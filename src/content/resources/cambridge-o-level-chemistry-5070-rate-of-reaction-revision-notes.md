Condensed recall for subtopic 6.2 of Cambridge O Level Chemistry 5070. For full explanations and two worked data examples, use the [rate of reaction study guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-chemistry-5070-rate-of-reaction/); to practise, try the [rate of reaction practice questions](/resources/cambridge-o-level-chemistry-5070-rate-of-reaction-practice/).

## Collision theory: the four syllabus ideas

- **Particles per unit volume**: concentration (solutions) or pressure (gases).
- **Frequency of collisions**: collisions per second.
- **Kinetic energy**: faster particles collide more often and harder.
- **Activation energy, Ea**: the minimum energy colliding particles must have to react; only collisions at or above Ea succeed.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Rate of reaction | Change in amount of reactant or product per unit time |
| Successful collision | A collision with energy ≥ Ea, leading to reaction |
| Collision frequency | Number of collisions per second |
| Catalyst | Increases rate, decreases Ea, unchanged at the end of the reaction |
| Enzyme | A biological catalyst |
| Limiting reactant | Reactant used up first; fixes the total amount of product |
| Mean rate | Total change ÷ time taken over an interval |
| Gradient | Steepness of a graph line; equals the rate at that point |

## Factor explanations, ready to write

- **Concentration up:** more particles per unit volume → more frequent collisions → faster.
- **Pressure up (gases):** more gas particles per unit volume → more frequent collisions → faster.
- **Surface area up (smaller pieces):** more particles exposed → more frequent collisions → faster.
- **Temperature up:** particles have more kinetic energy → move faster → more frequent collisions **and** a greater proportion of collisions have energy ≥ Ea → faster.
- **Catalyst added:** Ea lowered → a greater proportion of collisions are successful → faster. Removing it reverses this.

## Practical methods

| Method | How | Good for | Limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Change in mass | Flask on balance, cotton wool plug, read mass at intervals | Dense gases such as CO₂ | Useless for H₂: mass change too small |
| Gas syringe | Collect gas, read volume at intervals | Any gas | Syringe can stick; joints must be gas-tight |
| Collection over water | Upturned measuring cylinder of water | Insoluble gases such as H₂ | Soluble gases (CO₂ slightly) give low readings |

Control variables: volume and concentration of solutions, mass and size of solid pieces, temperature, catalyst presence. Change only one.

## Graph rules

- Steepest at the start: rate is greatest when reactants are most concentrated.
- Curve gets less steep: reactants are used up, so collision frequency falls.
- Horizontal line: the limiting reactant is used up; reaction has stopped.
- Faster reaction (higher temperature, concentration, surface area, catalyst): steeper start.
- Same amount of limiting reactant: same final level.
- Half the amount of limiting reactant: half the final level.
- Mass-loss graphs fall and level off; gas-volume graphs rise and level off.

## Calculating mean rate

Mean rate = change in volume (or mass) ÷ time interval. Units: cm³/s or g/s. Example: 36 cm³ collected between 20 s and 50 s gives 36 ÷ 30 = 1.2 cm³/s.

## Exam traps

- Write "more **frequent** collisions", not just "more collisions".
- Temperature needs **both** parts: more frequent collisions and more collisions with energy ≥ Ea.
- Concentration does not make particles faster.
- A catalyst does not increase the yield or the final volume.
- A catalyst is unchanged in **mass and chemical composition** at the end.
- Surface area applies to solids; pressure applies to gases.
- Do not raise the final level of a sketched curve when only the rate changes.
- Cotton wool plug: stops liquid escaping, lets gas out.

## Self-test

1. Define activation energy.
2. Explain why increasing the concentration of acid increases the rate of its reaction with zinc.
3. Explain, using collision theory, why raising the temperature increases the rate.
4. State **three** things that are true about a catalyst.
5. What is an enzyme?
6. Why is powdered calcium carbonate faster to react than lumps of the same mass?
7. Which method would you use to follow the rate of reaction of magnesium with acid, and why not mass loss?
8. Why is a cotton wool plug used in the mass-loss method?
9. 90 cm³ of gas is collected in 30 s. Calculate the mean rate.
10. On a gas-volume graph, what does a horizontal line show?
11. A reaction is repeated with the same amount of limiting reactant but at a higher temperature. How does the new curve compare?
12. Why does increasing the pressure speed up a reaction between gases?

**Answers**

1. The minimum energy that colliding particles must have to react.
2. More acid particles per unit volume, so more frequent collisions with zinc.
3. Particles gain kinetic energy and move faster, so they collide more frequently, and a greater proportion of collisions have energy equal to or greater than Ea.
4. It increases the rate; it decreases the activation energy; it is unchanged at the end of the reaction.
5. A biological catalyst.
6. The powder has a larger surface area, so more particles are exposed and collisions are more frequent.
7. A gas syringe: hydrogen has such a small mass that the mass loss is too small to measure accurately.
8. It lets gas escape but stops acid spray escaping, which would add to the mass lost.
9. 90 ÷ 30 = 3.0 cm³/s.
10. The reaction has stopped because the limiting reactant is used up.
11. Steeper at the start, levelling off sooner, at the same final volume.
12. The same gas particles occupy a smaller volume, so there are more per unit volume and collisions are more frequent.
