This guide covers subtopic **8.5 Noble gases** of Cambridge O Level Chemistry 5070 (2026–2028 series). It is a short outcome, but it supplies the reference point for almost all of the bonding in Topic 2: ions and covalent molecules are explained by atoms reaching a noble gas configuration. The topic overview in [Periodic Table: Groups and Trends](/resources/periodic-table-groups-and-trends/) gives the headline; this guide builds the full explanation and shows how examiners test it.

## What the syllabus asks

There is a single outcome:

> Describe the Group VIII noble gases as unreactive, monatomic gases and explain this in terms of electronic configuration.

Three descriptive words (**unreactive**, **monatomic**, **gases**) and one explanation (**electronic configuration**). Questions can also ask you to identify trends from data about the group, using the general skill from subtopic 8.1.

## The members of Group VIII

The noble gases are helium, neon, argon, krypton, xenon and radon. They form the last column of the Periodic Table, at the end of every period. Some tables label this column Group 0; the 5070 syllabus calls it **Group VIII**.

| Noble gas | Proton number | Electronic configuration | Outer electrons |
|---|---|---|---|
| Helium | 2 | 2 | 2 |
| Neon | 10 | 2,8 | 8 |
| Argon | 18 | 2,8,8 | 8 |

Helium is the exception to "eight outer electrons": its only shell holds a maximum of two, so two electrons fill it. In every noble gas the outer shell is **full**.

Helium's two outer electrons might suggest Group II, but it is placed in Group VIII because its chemistry matches the noble gases, not the reactive Group II metals: it is an unreactive, monatomic gas with a full shell. Group membership reflects chemical behaviour, which comes from having a full or partly filled outer shell, not simply from counting outer electrons.

## Why they are unreactive

Atoms react by losing, gaining or sharing electrons. They do this to obtain a full outer shell, which is the most stable arrangement. A noble gas atom **already has a full outer shell**, so it has no tendency to lose, gain or share electrons, and it does not react with other substances under normal conditions.

The evidence is in what other elements become when they react. Each forms an ion with the same configuration as a noble gas:

| Particle | Electrons | Configuration | Same as |
|---|---|---|---|
| O²⁻ | 10 | 2,8 | Neon |
| F⁻ | 10 | 2,8 | Neon |
| Na⁺ | 10 | 2,8 | Neon |
| Mg²⁺ | 10 | 2,8 | Neon |
| Cl⁻ | 18 | 2,8,8 | Argon |
| K⁺ | 18 | 2,8,8 | Argon |

Sodium (2,8,1) loses one electron to reach neon's configuration; chlorine (2,8,7) gains one to reach argon's. Covalent bonding achieves the same result by sharing: each chlorine atom in Cl₂ has eight outer electrons once the pair is shared. Elements react to become like the noble gases; the noble gases have nothing to gain by reacting.

### Worked example: a full "explain" answer

*Explain why argon is unreactive. [2]*

Model answer: "Argon has the electronic configuration 2,8,8, so its outer shell is full [1]. It therefore has no tendency to lose, gain or share electrons [1]."

Annotation: the first mark needs a reference to the **full outer shell** (quoting the configuration helps). The second needs the consequence for **electrons**. "Argon is stable" alone earns nothing: stable is the conclusion, not the reason.

A note on wording: chemists describe the noble gases as **unreactive** rather than completely inert, because a few compounds of the heavier noble gases can be made under extreme conditions. Use the syllabus word, "unreactive".

## Why they are monatomic

**Monatomic** means existing as **single atoms**. Most gaseous elements are diatomic: hydrogen (H₂), nitrogen (N₂), oxygen (O₂) and chlorine (Cl₂). Their atoms share electrons to reach a full outer shell, so they pair up. A noble gas atom already has a full outer shell, so there is no reason for two atoms to bond: each atom stays separate.

This is the same explanation as unreactivity, applied to atoms of the same element. That is why the syllabus links both words to electronic configuration. In equations and formulae, a noble gas is written as a single symbol: He, Ne, Ar. Writing Ar₂ is wrong. A consequence for calculations is that the relative formula mass of argon gas equals its relative atomic mass (40), whereas for chlorine gas it is twice the relative atomic mass (71).

## Why they are gases

The separate atoms are attracted to each other only by very weak forces. Little energy is needed to overcome them, so the noble gases have very low melting and boiling points and are all gases at r.t.p. This matches the pattern for simple molecular substances in subtopic 2.5: weak forces between particles mean low boiling points. (You do not need to name the type of force.)

## Trends down the group from given data

You are not required to learn noble gas data, but you can be given it.

| Noble gas | Boiling point / °C |
|---|---|
| Helium | −269 |
| Neon | −246 |
| Argon | −186 |
| Krypton | −153 |
| Xenon | −108 |

### Worked example: predicting radon

*Radon is below xenon. Describe the trend in the table and predict the boiling point of radon. State whether radon is a gas at r.t.p.*

- **Trend:** boiling point **increases** down the group.
- **Prediction:** above −108 °C; any value between about −100 °C and −40 °C is reasonable given the size of the steps (the real value is about −62 °C).
- **State:** a gas at r.t.p., because its predicted boiling point is still far below room temperature.
- Radon is also unreactive and monatomic, because it too has a full outer shell. Predictions about reactivity do **not** change down this group.

This last point is a deliberate trap: a candidate who applies the Group I idea ("reactivity increases down the group") to Group VIII loses the mark.

## Common mistakes

- **"Noble gases have eight outer electrons."** Helium has two. Say "a full outer shell".
- **"They are stable."** Give the reason: full outer shell, so no tendency to lose, gain or share electrons.
- **"They have no electrons to react with."** They have electrons; the outer shell is simply full.
- **Writing diatomic formulae** such as Ne₂ or He₂.
- **Confusing monatomic with "one type of atom".** Every element has one type of atom; monatomic means **not bonded** to other atoms.
- **Predicting a reactivity trend** down Group VIII.

## Exam technique

- **Describe**: use the three syllabus words, unreactive, monatomic, gases.
- **Explain**: two linked points, full outer shell, then no tendency to lose, gain or share electrons.
- **State** the configuration of a named noble gas when it helps: it makes the "full outer shell" point concrete.
- For data questions, state the direction of the trend and keep predictions consistent with the shared full outer shell.
