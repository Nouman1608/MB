All of this content is Stage 1. Systematic, stratified, cluster and quota sampling appear only in critique questions, because the specification does not require you to carry them out. Read the [study guide](/resources/ocr-a-level-mathematics-h240-statistical-sampling/) first, and use the [revision notes](/resources/ocr-a-level-mathematics-h240-statistical-sampling-revision-notes/) for recall. ## Questions

**1.** A water company wants to know the mean daily water use of the 24 000 households it supplies. It records the use of 300 households.

**(a)** State the population. **[1]**

**(b)** Suggest a suitable sampling frame. **[1]**

**2.** A manufacturer tests the lifetime of light bulbs by running them until they fail. Explain why a sample, not a census, must be used. **[1]**

**3.** A random sample of 80 students from a college of 1500 finds that 26 travel by train.

**(a)** Estimate the number of students at the college who travel by train. **[1]**

**(b)** A second random sample of 80 finds that 31 travel by train. Explain why the two samples give different results, and state whether this means either sample was badly chosen. **[2]**

**4.** A sports club has 640 members on a numbered list, 001 to 640. A random number generator gives: 512, 077, 903, 640, 077, 218, 000, 355.

**(a)** List the members selected for a simple random sample of 5, using the numbers in order. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain why 903 and the second 077 were not used. **[1]**

**5.** State the defining property of a simple random sample. **[1]**

**6.** A researcher asks the first 50 people leaving a cinema on Friday evening how many films they watch per month, to estimate the mean for adults in the town.

**(a)** Name the type of sampling. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain why the estimate is likely to be biased, and state the likely direction of the bias. **[2]**

**7.** A student says: "My sample was biased, so I will take a bigger sample using the same method to fix it." Comment on the student's plan. **[2]**

**8.** A quality inspector checks every 25th item from a production line, starting at a randomly chosen item from the first 25. The machine is reset after every 50 items, and items made just after a reset are more likely to be faulty.

**(a)** Name this method of sampling. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain why the method may give a misleading estimate of the proportion of faulty items. **[2]**

**9.** A council wants the views of residents in a large district on a new recycling scheme. It chooses 6 of the district's 60 streets at random and surveys every household in those streets.

**(a)** Name this method of sampling. **[1]**

**(b)** Give one advantage and one disadvantage of the method in this context. **[2]**

**10.** A market researcher is told to interview 30 men and 30 women in a shopping centre about their spending.

**(a)** Name this method of sampling. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain why it might not give a representative sample of shoppers. **[2]**

**11.** A university wants to estimate the mean number of hours of paid work done by its students. It has a full list of students and their year of study, and it expects first-year and final-year students to work very different hours. Recommend a sampling method and justify your choice. **[3]**

**12.** A town's newspaper invites readers to phone in and say whether they support a new bypass. Of 420 callers, 340 support it. The editor writes: "81% of the town supports the bypass."

**(a)** Verify the percentage. **[1]**

**(b)** Criticise the editor's conclusion. **[3]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** All 24 000 households supplied by the company [1]

**(b)** The company's list of customer accounts or addresses [1]. **[2]**

*Examiner insight:* "All households" without saying whose households (the company's 24 000) is too vague to identify the population.

**2.** Testing destroys the bulbs, so a census would leave none to sell [1]. **[1]**

**3. (a)** 26/80 × 1500 = 487.5, so **about 490** students [1]

**(b)** Each random sample contains different students, so results differ by chance (sampling variability) [1]; this does not mean either sample was badly chosen [1]. **[3]**

**4. (a)** Reject 903 (out of range), the second 077 (a repeat) and 000 (not on the list). The sample is **512, 077, 640** [1], **218, 355** [1].

**(b)** 903 is outside the range 001 to 640, and the second 077 is a repeat, which is not allowed when sampling without replacement [1]. **[3]**

*Examiner insight:* 640 is the last member on the list, so it is valid; 000 is not on the list.

**5.** Every possible sample of the chosen size has the same probability of being selected [1]. **[1]**

**6. (a)** Opportunity (convenience) sampling [1]

**(b)** Everyone in the sample has just been to the cinema, so frequent film-goers are over-represented and adults who rarely watch films are missed [1]; the estimate of the mean is likely to be too high [1]. **[3]**

**7.** A larger sample reduces sampling variability, so the estimate would be more consistent [1], but the bias comes from the method, so it would remain; the student should change the method, for example to a simple random sample [1]. **[2]**

**8. (a)** Systematic sampling [1]

**(b)** Because 50 is a multiple of 25, the items sampled are always the same positions after each reset [1]; they are either always just after a reset (overestimating faults) or never (underestimating), so the sample follows the pattern rather than representing all items [1]. **[3]**

**9. (a)** Cluster sampling [1]

**(b)** Advantage: cheaper and quicker, as interviewers visit only 6 streets [1]. Disadvantage: households in a street may be similar (for example in house type or bin space), so 6 streets may not represent the district [1]. **[3]**

**10. (a)** Quota sampling [1]

**(b)** The researcher chooses who to interview, so the sample is not random [1]; they may approach people who look approachable or are in one part of the centre at one time, so some types of shopper are under-represented [1]. **[3]**

**11.** Stratified sampling by year of study [1]. The population divides into groups (years) whose hours are expected to differ, so stratifying makes sure each year is represented in proportion [1]; it is possible because the list records every student's year [1]. **[3]**

*Examiner insight:* A recommendation needs two kinds of justification: why the method suits this population, and why it is feasible with the information available.

**12. (a)** 340/420 × 100 = **81.0%** [1]

**(b)** The callers chose themselves, so the sample is not random [1]; people with strong views, especially supporters, are more likely to phone, and only newspaper readers could take part [1]; so 81% of callers cannot be taken as an estimate for the whole town, and a simple random sample of residents would be better [1]. **[4]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Populations named vaguely, without the context.
- Repeats or out-of-range random numbers kept.
- "Biased" with no explanation tied to the situation.
- Claiming a larger sample removes bias.
- Missing the direction of bias when it can be deduced.
- Recommending a method without saying why it is feasible.
