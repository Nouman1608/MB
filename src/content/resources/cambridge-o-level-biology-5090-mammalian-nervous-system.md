This guide covers subtopic **14.1 Mammalian nervous system** of Cambridge O Level Biology 5090, 2026-2028 series. The syllabus asks you to state that the nervous system coordinates and regulates body functions; to describe the central and peripheral nervous systems; to identify sensory, relay and motor neurones; to state that electrical impulses travel along neurones; to describe a simple reflex arc and a reflex action; and to describe the structure and action of a synapse, including why impulses travel in one direction only.

## What the nervous system does

The nervous system, made of the brain, spinal cord and nerves, **coordinates and regulates body functions**. It detects changes inside and outside the body, decides on a response and sends instructions to the parts that carry out the response. It works by sending **electrical impulses** along cells called neurones. Impulses travel quickly, so the nervous system is the fast control system of the body.

## Two parts of the system

| Part | Made of | Role |
|---|---|---|
| Central nervous system (CNS) | The brain and the spinal cord | Receives information, processes it and sends out instructions |
| Peripheral nervous system (PNS) | The nerves outside the brain and spinal cord | Carries impulses between the CNS and the rest of the body |

A **nerve** is a bundle of neurones. Some carry impulses towards the CNS and some carry them away from it. The spinal cord runs inside the backbone and connects the brain to the nerves of the body.

## The three types of neurone

A neurone is a nerve cell that is specialised to carry electrical impulses. There are three types, and you must be able to identify each on a diagram.

| Neurone | Carries impulses | Where it is found |
|---|---|---|
| Sensory neurone | From a receptor towards the CNS | Mostly in the PNS |
| Relay neurone | Between sensory and motor neurones | Inside the CNS (brain or spinal cord) |
| Motor neurone | From the CNS to an effector | Mostly in the PNS |

To identify them on a diagram, follow the direction of the impulse. The neurone that brings the impulse in from a receptor is sensory. The neurone in the middle, completely inside the spinal cord, is relay. The neurone that takes the impulse out to a muscle or gland is motor. A motor neurone has a long axon that leaves the spinal cord. A sensory neurone has its cell body off to one side of the main route, in a swelling near the spinal cord, but you do not need to remember this detail to answer the usual diagram question.

## Receptors and effectors

A **receptor** is a cell or group of cells that detects a **stimulus**, which is a change in the environment. An **effector** is a part of the body that responds. The syllabus names two kinds of effector: **muscles**, which contract, and **glands**, which secrete a substance such as a hormone or enzyme.

## The reflex arc

A **reflex action** is a **rapid and automatic response to a stimulus**. "Automatic" means that it happens without having to think. The path that the impulse follows is the **reflex arc**. The syllabus gives the sequence:

1. **Receptor** detects the stimulus.
2. **Sensory neurone** carries the impulse to the CNS.
3. **Relay neurone** passes the impulse across the CNS.
4. **Motor neurone** carries the impulse away.
5. **Effector** (a muscle or a gland) produces the response.

Take a worked example. You touch a hot pan.

- The receptors in the skin of your finger detect the heat.
- A sensory neurone carries impulses along your arm to the spinal cord.
- A relay neurone in the spinal cord passes the impulses to a motor neurone.
- The motor neurone carries the impulses to the muscle in your arm.
- The muscle contracts, and your hand pulls away.

The reflex is quick because the path is short and has only a few neurones and synapses. The brain does not have to decide first. You become aware of the pain afterwards, because a branch of the information also reaches the brain. A reflex protects the body from harm, which is its purpose in this example.

A second example is the pupil of the eye getting smaller in bright light. The receptors are in the retina and the effector is a muscle in the iris. This is dealt with in the sense organs subtopic.

## Synapses

Neurones do not touch. Between the end of one neurone and the start of the next there is a tiny gap, and the junction is a **synapse**.

### Structure of a synapse

- The end of the first neurone contains **vesicles** that hold molecules of **neurotransmitter**.
- The **synaptic gap** separates the two neurones.
- The membrane of the next neurone has **receptor proteins** that fit the neurotransmitter molecules.

### Events at a synapse

1. An impulse arrives at the end of the first neurone and stimulates the vesicles to release neurotransmitter molecules into the synaptic gap.
2. The neurotransmitter molecules **diffuse** across the gap and **bind** with the receptor proteins on the next neurone.
3. This binding stimulates a new impulse in the next neurone.

The impulse is an electrical event, but at the synapse the message is carried by a chemical. This is why synapses make the pathway slightly slower than a single neurone alone.

### One-way direction

Only the first neurone contains vesicles of neurotransmitter, and only the second has the receptor proteins. Neurotransmitter can only be released from one side and detected on the other, so **synapses ensure that impulses travel in one direction only**. In a reflex arc this keeps the message moving from receptor to effector and never backwards.

## Worked example: describing a pathway

*Describe how the nervous system produces a reflex response when a person steps on a sharp object.*

A good answer follows the arc and names each part:

- The pain receptors in the skin detect the stimulus.
- Impulses pass along a sensory neurone to the spinal cord.
- At the synapse, neurotransmitter is released, diffuses across the gap and stimulates a relay neurone.
- A further synapse passes the impulse to a motor neurone.
- The motor neurone carries impulses to the muscle in the leg, the effector.
- The muscle contracts and the foot is lifted away.

Each of these bullet points is worth a mark, and a question like this often awards marks for naming the neurone and the effector, not for repeating the word "impulse".

## Worked example: why a short pathway is faster

*A sensory neurone carries an impulse 0.9 m from the toe to the spinal cord and a motor neurone carries it 0.9 m back to a leg muscle. Impulses travel at 90 m/s along these neurones. Calculate the time spent travelling along the neurones.*

1. Total distance = 0.9 + 0.9 = 1.8 m.
2. Time = distance divided by speed = 1.8 divided by 90 = 0.02 s.

The time at the synapses is extra, because the chemical step takes a short time at each one. A reflex arc has only a few synapses, so the response is quick. If the message had to go all the way to the brain and back, with many more neurones and synapses, the response would be slower. This is the main reason why a reflex is useful when the body is in danger.

## Investigating a nervous response

The syllabus lists nervous responses among the experimental contexts. A simple method measures reaction time with a ruler.

1. One student holds a 30 cm ruler vertically, with the zero end level with the top of the thumb and first finger of a second student, who is ready to catch it.
2. The first student drops the ruler without warning.
3. The second student catches it as quickly as possible and the distance fallen is read from the ruler.
4. The test is repeated several times and a mean is found.

A smaller distance means a faster response. To make the test fair, use the same hand, the same starting position and the same ruler each time. Because this response involves the brain deciding to catch, it is a voluntary response, not a reflex. A reflex such as the knee jerk is not controlled in this way.

## Reflex or voluntary?

| Feature | Reflex action | Voluntary action |
|---|---|---|
| Speed | Rapid | Slower |
| Thought needed | No, automatic | Yes, decided by the brain |
| Example | Pulling the hand from a hot object | Choosing to pick up a pen |

At this level, a reflex action is defined as a rapid and automatic response. Do not add details beyond this.

## Common mistakes

- Saying that impulses travel in the blood. Impulses travel along neurones. Hormones travel in the blood.
- Saying that neurones touch each other. They are separated by a synaptic gap.
- Describing the neurotransmitter as an electrical signal. It is a chemical that diffuses across the gap.
- Mixing up sensory and motor neurones. Sensory goes from the receptor towards the CNS.
- Writing "the brain" as the coordinator in a spinal reflex. The CNS is the coordinator.
- Forgetting the effector. Name it as a muscle or a gland.
- Saying that the brain is part of the PNS.

## Command words

- **State**: give a fact, such as the parts of the CNS.
- **Identify**: name the neurone or part on a diagram.
- **Describe**: give the path of the impulse in order.
- **Explain**: give a reason, for example why a synapse works in one direction.
