This guide teaches sub-topic 3(c) Reversible reactions and equilibria from Topic 3, Physical chemistry, of the Pearson Edexcel International GCSE Chemistry (4CH1) specification, Issue 3 (September 2024). It covers points 3.17 to 3.22. The qualification is untiered. Points 3.17 and 3.18 (reversible reactions and their examples) can be examined on Paper 1 and Paper 2. Points 3.19C to 3.22C carry a C reference (printed in bold in the specification), so dynamic equilibrium and the effects of catalyst, temperature and pressure are assessed on **Paper 2 only**. This guide labels them.

## Reversible reactions (3.17 and 3.18)

In most reactions the reactants change into products and that is the end. In a **reversible reaction**, the products can react to re-form the reactants. We show this with the symbol **⇌** instead of →. The reaction in one direction is the **forward** reaction, and the other is the **reverse** reaction. If the forward reaction is exothermic, the reverse is endothermic, with the same size of energy change.

### Hydrated copper(II) sulfate

Blue hydrated copper(II) sulfate crystals contain water of crystallisation. Heating drives the water off, leaving white anhydrous copper(II) sulfate:

```
CuSO4.5H2O (blue)  ⇌  CuSO4 (white) + 5H2O
```

The forward direction is endothermic (heating supplies energy). Adding water to the white solid reverses the change, giving the blue crystals again and getting hot, which is exothermic. This is also the test for water: anhydrous copper(II) sulfate turns from white to blue.

**Worked example 1.** 5.00 g of hydrated copper(II) sulfate (Mr = 249.5) is heated until it is fully white. What mass of anhydrous solid remains?

- Mass of 5H₂O in one formula unit = 5 x 18 = 90, so the fraction of water = 90 / 249.5 = 0.361 (36.1%).
- Mass of water lost = 0.361 x 5.00 = **1.80 g**.
- Mass of anhydrous solid = 5.00 - 1.80 = **3.20 g**.

### Ammonium chloride

When white ammonium chloride is heated in a test tube, it breaks down into two colourless gases, ammonia and hydrogen chloride:

```
NH4Cl (s)  ⇌  NH3 (g) + HCl (g)
```

The gases move up the cooler part of the tube and recombine, forming a white solid again on the cool glass. It looks as if the solid has moved, though it has really decomposed and re-formed. Damp litmus paper in the tube can show the two gases: the ammonia turns red litmus blue and the hydrogen chloride turns blue litmus red. Because ammonia travels faster than hydrogen chloride, the blue colour appears first.

## Dynamic equilibrium (3.19C and 3.20C, Paper 2 only)

If a reversible reaction takes place in a **sealed (closed) container**, so that nothing can escape, the system eventually reaches **dynamic equilibrium**. At the start, the forward reaction is fast and the reverse is slow (or does not occur), because there are no products. As reactants are used, the forward rate falls and, as products form, the reverse rate rises. Eventually the two rates are equal.

The two **characteristics** of dynamic equilibrium that you must know:

1. The **forward and reverse reactions occur at the same rate**.
2. The **concentrations of reactants and products remain constant** (not equal, constant).

"Dynamic" means that the reactions have not stopped; both directions continue, but there is no net change. A common error is to say that the concentrations of reactants and products are "equal". They are usually not. The **position of equilibrium** describes the balance: if it lies to the right, there is more product than reactant. If it lies to the left, there is more reactant.

An open container cannot reach equilibrium, because gases escape. Dynamic equilibrium needs a closed system.

## Catalysts and equilibrium (3.21C, Paper 2 only)

A catalyst speeds up the forward and the reverse reactions by the **same amount**, by lowering the activation energy for both. Because both rates rise equally, the equilibrium position does not change: the same proportion of reactants and products is present at equilibrium. The catalyst only helps the system to **reach** equilibrium **sooner**.

This is why industry uses a catalyst: it makes the reaction fast enough to be economic at a moderate temperature, but it does not increase the percentage yield at equilibrium.

## Changing temperature (3.22C, Paper 2 only)

**Rule:** an increase in temperature moves the position of equilibrium in the direction of the **endothermic** reaction. A decrease in temperature moves it in the direction of the **exothermic** reaction.

**Worked example 2.** The Haber process: N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) ⇌ 2NH₃(g), ΔH = -92 kJ/mol (forward exothermic).

- The forward reaction is exothermic, so the reverse reaction is endothermic.
- **Raising the temperature:** the position moves in the endothermic (reverse) direction, so the yield of ammonia **falls**.
- **Lowering the temperature:** the position moves in the exothermic (forward) direction, so the yield of ammonia **rises**.

The trade-off is that a low temperature slows the reaction. Industry uses a compromise: a moderate temperature with a catalyst, giving an acceptable yield at an acceptable speed.

**Worked example 3.** N₂O₄(g) ⇌ 2NO₂(g), ΔH = +57 kJ/mol. N₂O₄ is colourless and NO₂ is brown.

- The forward reaction is endothermic.
- **Heating** moves the position to the right (endothermic direction), so more brown NO₂ forms and the mixture darkens.
- **Cooling** moves it to the left, so the mixture becomes paler.

## Changing pressure (3.22C, Paper 2 only)

**Rule:** an increase in pressure moves the position of equilibrium in the direction that produces **fewer moles of gas**. A decrease in pressure moves it in the direction that produces more moles of gas. Pressure affects only equilibria with gases, and only if the number of moles of gas differs on the two sides.

**Method.**

1. Count the moles of gas on each side of the equation (use the balancing numbers; ignore solids and liquids).
2. If the pressure rises, the equilibrium shifts towards the side with the **smaller** number.
3. If the numbers are equal, pressure has no effect on the position.

| Reaction | Moles of gas (left : right) | Effect of higher pressure |
|---|---|---|
| N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃ | 4 : 2 | Shifts right, more ammonia |
| N₂O₄ ⇌ 2NO₂ | 1 : 2 | Shifts left, more N₂O₄ |
| H₂ + I₂ ⇌ 2HI | 2 : 2 | No effect on the position |
| 2SO₂ + O₂ ⇌ 2SO₃ | 3 : 2 | Shifts right, more sulfur trioxide |

For the Haber process, a high pressure increases the yield, but very high pressures need expensive equipment and cost a lot of energy, so a moderately high pressure is used.

## Summary table for predictions

| Change | Effect on position of equilibrium | Effect on rate |
|---|---|---|
| Add a catalyst | None | Reaches equilibrium faster |
| Raise temperature | Towards the endothermic direction | Faster |
| Lower temperature | Towards the exothermic direction | Slower |
| Raise pressure (gases only) | Towards fewer moles of gas | Faster |
| Lower pressure (gases only) | Towards more moles of gas | Slower |

## Common mistakes

- Saying that equilibrium means "the reaction has stopped". It has not; the rates are equal.
- Writing that concentrations are equal at equilibrium. They are constant, not equal.
- Claiming that a catalyst increases the yield at equilibrium.
- Mixing up the direction: "an increase in temperature favours the exothermic reaction". It favours the **endothermic** one.
- Counting solids or liquids when counting moles of gas.
- Applying a pressure rule to an equilibrium with equal moles of gas on both sides.
- Ignoring the closed system: an open container cannot be at equilibrium.
- Forgetting that these predictions about position are Paper 2 only, though reversible reactions (3.17, 3.18) can be examined on either paper.

## Exam technique

Always identify the exothermic and endothermic directions from ΔH before answering a temperature question. For a pressure question, write down the number of moles of gas on each side and state which side has fewer. When asked to "explain" a shift, give the rule and its result: "the forward reaction is exothermic, so increasing the temperature moves the position to the left, which lowers the yield". For colour changes, link the colour to the species. Label Paper 2-only answers carefully in your revision, since they cannot appear on Paper 1.
