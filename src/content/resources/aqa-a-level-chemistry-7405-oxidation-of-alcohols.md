This guide teaches section **3.3.5.2 Oxidation of alcohols** of the AQA AS and A-level Chemistry specification (7404/7405), for teaching from September 2015. It is AS content, examined on Paper 2 at AS and A-level, and A-level Paper 3 can test it with practical contexts. The [Alcohols study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-alcohols/) introduces classification, the dichromate colour change and the two aldehyde tests. This page goes further: classifying branched and cyclic alcohols, a reliable way to balance [O] equations (including molecules with two –OH groups), what each piece of apparatus does in distillation and reflux, what the test reagents actually do, and how to solve "identify the unknown" problems. Recall is in the [Oxidation of alcohols revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-oxidation-of-alcohols-revision-notes/), and questions are in the [Oxidation of alcohols practice questions](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-oxidation-of-alcohols-practice/).

## What the specification asks

| Content | What you must be able to do |
|---|---|
| Alcohols are classified as primary, secondary and tertiary | Classify any alcohol from its structure |
| Primary alcohols are oxidised to aldehydes, then to carboxylic acids; secondary alcohols to ketones; tertiary alcohols are not easily oxidised | Write equations for these reactions; equations using [O] are acceptable |
| Acidified potassium dichromate(VI) is a suitable oxidising agent | Explain how the method used decides whether an aldehyde or a carboxylic acid is obtained |
| | Use chemical tests, including Fehling's solution and Tollens' reagent, to distinguish aldehydes from ketones |

The skills column suggests preparing an aldehyde, and a carboxylic acid, by oxidising a primary alcohol.

## Classifying alcohols, including awkward cases

Look only at the carbon atom bonded to the –OH group and count how many carbon atoms are attached to it.

| Alcohol | Carbons on the C–OH carbon | Class |
|---|---|---|
| Methanol, CH₃OH | 0 | Treated as primary (gives methanal) |
| 3-methylbutan-1-ol | 1 | Primary, despite the branch |
| Cyclohexanol | 2 (both ring carbons) | Secondary |
| Pentan-3-ol | 2 | Secondary |
| 2-methylbutan-2-ol | 3 | Tertiary |

A quick cross-check: primary C–OH carbons carry two H atoms, secondary carry one, tertiary carry none. Oxidation removes one of these H atoms, which is why a tertiary alcohol is not easily oxidised.

## The oxidising agent and what you see

Potassium dichromate(VI) acidified with dilute sulfuric acid is orange because of the Cr₂O₇²⁻ ion. When it oxidises an alcohol, it is reduced to Cr³⁺, which is green. As a half-equation (a link to redox in 3.1.7):

```
Cr₂O₇²⁻ + 14H⁺ + 6e⁻ → 2Cr³⁺ + 7H₂O
```

The specification states that equations showing [O] as the oxidant are acceptable, so you do not need to combine half-equations for alcohol oxidations.

## Balancing [O] equations reliably

Each oxidation step does one of two things:

- **Alcohol → carbonyl** (aldehyde or ketone): one [O] removes two H atoms as H₂O.
- **Aldehyde → carboxylic acid**: one [O] is inserted into the C–H bond; no water forms.

```
primary   R–CH₂OH + [O]  → R–CHO + H₂O
          R–CHO   + [O]  → R–COOH
overall   R–CH₂OH + 2[O] → R–COOH + H₂O
secondary R–CH(OH)–R' + [O] → R–CO–R' + H₂O
```

### Worked example 1: a branched primary alcohol

2-methylpropan-1-ol, (CH₃)₂CHCH₂OH.

```
distil:  (CH₃)₂CHCH₂OH + [O]  → (CH₃)₂CHCHO + H₂O     2-methylpropanal
reflux:  (CH₃)₂CHCH₂OH + 2[O] → (CH₃)₂CHCOOH + H₂O    2-methylpropanoic acid
```

The carbon chain and the branch are unchanged; only the C–OH end changes. Check by counting atoms on each side.

### Worked example 2: two –OH groups

Ethane-1,2-diol, HOCH₂CH₂OH, has two primary –OH groups. Refluxed with excess oxidising agent, both become –COOH:

```
HOCH₂CH₂OH + 4[O] → HOOC–COOH + 2H₂O
```

Two [O] per primary group, and one H₂O per primary group. The same counting works for any number of –OH groups.

## Choosing the method: aldehyde or carboxylic acid?

The method decides the product because an aldehyde is itself easily oxidised.

### To obtain the aldehyde: distillation as it forms

- Use the alcohol **in excess** and add the acidified dichromate **slowly**, so there is never spare oxidising agent to attack the aldehyde.
- Warm gently and **distil the aldehyde off as it forms**.
- The aldehyde has the **lowest boiling point** in the mixture because its molecules cannot form hydrogen bonds with each other (they have no O–H bond), whereas the alcohol and the acid can. It leaves the flask before it can be oxidised further.
- Cool the collecting vessel, for example in ice, because the aldehyde is volatile.

### To obtain the carboxylic acid: heating under reflux

- Use **excess** acidified dichromate.
- Heat under **reflux**: a vertical condenser returns any vapour (including aldehyde) to the flask, so it stays in contact with the oxidising agent until fully oxidised.
- Separate the acid afterwards by distillation.

| Apparatus feature | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Anti-bumping granules | Smooth boiling, no violent bumps |
| Thermometer bulb at the side arm (distillation) | Measures the temperature of vapour leaving, to collect the right fraction |
| Condenser water in at the bottom | Keeps the condenser full of cold water |
| Open top of reflux condenser | Never heat a sealed system |
| Electric heater or water bath | Organic vapours are flammable |

## What the test reagents do

Both tests rely on an aldehyde being easily oxidised (to a carboxylic acid) while a ketone is not. Warm each in a water bath, not over a flame.

| Reagent | Active species | Aldehyde | Ketone |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tollens' reagent | [Ag(NH₃)₂]⁺, silver(I) | Silver mirror: Ag⁺ reduced to Ag | No change |
| Fehling's solution | Copper(II) complex, blue | Red precipitate (copper(I) oxide) | Stays blue |

In each positive test, the aldehyde is the reducing agent: RCHO + [O] → RCOOH. Acidified dichromate can also be used as a test: no colour change shows a tertiary alcohol, while green shows primary or secondary.

## Worked example 3: identifying three isomers of C₅H₁₂O

Alcohols X, Y and Z are each warmed with acidified potassium dichromate(VI).

- X turns the solution green; the product, distilled off as it forms, gives a silver mirror with Tollens' reagent.
- Y turns the solution green; the product gives no change with Fehling's solution.
- Z leaves the solution orange.

**Reasoning.** X gives an aldehyde, so X is primary, for example **pentan-1-ol**. Y gives a ketone, so Y is secondary, for example **pentan-3-ol**. Z is not oxidised, so Z is tertiary: the only tertiary C₅H₁₂O alcohol is **2-methylbutan-2-ol**. For X and Y, further data (such as the alkene formed on elimination) would be needed to pin down a single isomer; state the class, then give a correct example.

## Worked example 4: yield of a ketone

6.00 g of propan-2-ol (Mᵣ 60.0) gives 4.35 g of propanone (Mᵣ 58.0).

```
n(propan-2-ol) = 6.00 ÷ 60.0 = 0.100 mol
n(propanone)   = 4.35 ÷ 58.0 = 0.0750 mol
yield          = 0.0750 ÷ 0.100 × 100 = 75.0 %
```

## Common mistakes

- Classifying by the total number of carbons or branches rather than the carbons on the C–OH carbon.
- Writing an aldehyde as –COH; it is –CHO.
- Adding H₂O in the aldehyde → acid step.
- "Reflux" for an aldehyde, or "distil" for an acid.
- Explaining the low boiling point of the aldehyde as "it is smaller" rather than by the absence of hydrogen bonding between its molecules.
- Giving only the observation for the aldehyde in a test question, not for the ketone.
- Heating Tollens' reagent directly with a flame.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Write an equation**: use structural formulae, show [O], and balance H₂O.
- **Explain how the method determines the product**: name the method, the reagent amounts and why the aldehyde escapes or is retained.
- **Describe a test**: reagent, condition (warm in a water bath) and the observation for **both** compounds.
- **Identify**: give the class first, then a name that fits the formula, then the evidence.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why is a tertiary alcohol not easily oxidised?**
The carbon bonded to –OH has no hydrogen atom, so the C–H bond needed to form C=O cannot be broken without breaking C–C bonds.

**Why does the aldehyde distil off before the alcohol?**
Aldehyde molecules have no O–H bond, so they cannot hydrogen bond to each other. Their intermolecular forces are weaker than the alcohol's, so they boil at a lower temperature.

**Can acidified dichromate tell an aldehyde from a ketone?**
It turns green with an aldehyde but not with a ketone, so it can, but the tests the specification names are Fehling's solution and Tollens' reagent, so use one of those when asked to distinguish them.
