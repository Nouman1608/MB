## Questions

**1.** State what is meant by the balance of trade. **[1]**

**2.** Country C's figures (billion units) are: exports of goods 85, imports of goods 97, exports of services 41, imports of services 22, net income from abroad +2.
**(a)** Calculate the balance of trade. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the current account balance and state whether it is a deficit or a surplus. **[2]**

**3.** Country D's current account moved from a deficit of 14 to a deficit of 9 (billion units). Calculate the percentage change in the size of the deficit. **[2]**

**4.** Explain **two** reasons why a country may have a current account deficit. **[4]**

**5.** Explain why a large and persistent current account deficit may be a problem for a country. **[3]**

**6.** Explain how a supply-side policy could reduce a current account deficit in the long run. **[3]**

**7.** Distinguish between income and wealth, giving an example of each. **[4]**

**8.** Explain **two** causes of income inequality. **[4]**

**9.** Country E taxes the first 10,000 of income at 0% and income above that at 30%. Calculate the tax paid, as a percentage of income, by someone earning 15,000 and by someone earning 50,000. State whether the tax is progressive. **[4]**

**10.** Explain how government spending on education can redistribute income over time. **[3]**

**11.** "Raising the top rate of income tax is the best way to reduce inequality." Evaluate this view. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** The value of exports minus the value of imports of goods and services [1]. **[1]**

**2. (a)** Goods: 85 − 97 = −12; services: 41 − 22 = +19 [1]; balance of trade = **+7** [1].
**(b)** Current account = 7 + 2 = **+9** [1], a **surplus** [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A trade deficit in goods does not mean a current account deficit; services and income can outweigh it.

**3.** (9 − 14) ÷ 14 × 100 [1] = **−35.7%**, so the deficit shrank by about 36% [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Any two developed: domestic goods are less competitive [1], so consumers buy imports and foreigners buy fewer exports [1]; strong consumer spending [1] increases demand for imports [1]; a strong exchange rate [1] makes exports more expensive abroad and imports cheaper at home [1]. **[4]**

**5.** Money spent on imports supports jobs abroad rather than at home [1]; the deficit must be financed by borrowing from abroad or selling assets [1], and it may show that domestic firms are uncompetitive, which harms growth [1]. **[3]**

**6.** For example, training or investment incentives [1] raise productivity and quality, so domestic firms become more competitive [1]; exports rise and consumers switch from imports, narrowing the deficit [1]. **[3]**

**7.** Income is a flow of money received over a period [1], e.g. wages [1]. Wealth is a stock of assets owned at a point in time [1], e.g. a house or savings [1]. **[4]**

**8.** Any two developed: differences in skills [1] mean skilled workers command higher wages [1]; ownership of wealth [1] gives some people extra income from rent, interest or dividends [1]; unemployment [1] leaves people relying on lower benefit incomes [1]. **[4]**

**9.** Earner on 15,000: tax = 0.3 × 5,000 = 1,500, which is **10%** [1]. Earner on 50,000: tax = 0.3 × 40,000 = 12,000, which is **24%** [1] [1]. The percentage paid rises with income, so the tax is **progressive** [1]. **[4]**

**10.** Free education is used by low-income families who could not afford it otherwise [1]; it raises their skills and qualifications [1], so they can earn higher wages later, narrowing income gaps [1]. **[3]**

**11.** *For:*
- A higher top rate takes a larger share of the highest incomes [1], narrowing after-tax inequality [1].
- The revenue could fund benefits or services for low-income households [1].

*Against:*
- High earners may work less, move abroad or avoid tax, so revenue may not rise much [1] [1].
- Income tax does not tackle wealth inequality, which is larger [1].
- Spending on education and training tackles a cause of low income rather than the outcome [1].

*Judgement:* For example, a higher top rate helps but is unlikely to be the best single policy, because combining progressive taxes with spending on skills tackles both the outcome and the cause [1] [1]. **[9]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Leaving out "deficit" or "surplus" after a calculation.
- Treating a trade deficit in goods as a current account deficit.
- Mixing up income and wealth.
- Calling a tax progressive because rich people pay more money in total, rather than a higher percentage.
- Giving a 9-mark answer with no judgement.

## Frequently asked questions

**What should I write after a current account calculation?**
State whether the answer is a deficit or a surplus. Leaving out the word deficit or surplus after the number is a common way to lose a mark in AQA GCSE Economics.

**Is a trade deficit in goods the same as a current account deficit?**
No. The current account also includes trade in services and primary and secondary income, so a deficit in goods alone does not tell you the current account balance.

**How do I show a tax is progressive?**
Show that it takes a higher percentage of income from richer people, not just more money in total.
