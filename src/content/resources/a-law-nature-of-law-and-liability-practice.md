Section 1.1 of Pearson Edexcel International Advanced Level Law (Paper 1, YLA1) asks what law is and why it matters: how law differs from other rules, how liability works in civil and criminal law, why offenders are punished, and how legal theory and morality shape the law. These questions cover every content area from 1.1.1 to 1.1.14 of the Issue 4 (November 2021) specification. The companion [law making practice page](/resources/a-law-underlying-principles-practice/) covers section 1.2.

## Section A

**1.** Explain **three** ways in which a legal rule differs from a non-legal rule, such as a rule of etiquette or a school rule. **[6]**

**2.** Explain what is meant by legal personality, and give **one** example of a natural person and **one** example of a legal (artificial) person. **[4]**

**3.** For a civil case and a criminal case, state:

**(a)** who brings the case and what the parties are called **[2]**
**(b)** who bears the burden of proof and what the standard of proof is **[4]**
**(c)** **two** possible outcomes for a defendant who loses. **[2]**

## Section B

**4.** Explain how civil and criminal law sanctions differ in purpose, and how a single incident can lead to both kinds of proceedings. **[6]**

**5.** Explain **four** theories of punishment and link each to a type of sentence available to the courts. **[8]**

**6.** Compare positivism and natural law as theories of law, naming **one** theorist for each, and explain the utilitarian view. **[9]**

**7.** "The law should not concern itself with private morality." Evaluate this statement with reference to the Hart/Devlin debate, the Wolfenden Report and decided cases. **[15]**

## Answers

**1.** Any three, 2 marks each. **Certainty** — a legal rule is fixed in a recognised source (an Act, delegated legislation or a binding case), so people can find out what it requires; a rule of etiquette is vague and varies between groups [1] [1]. **Enforcement** — legal rules are enforced through the courts and, in criminal matters, the police and prosecuting authorities; non-legal rules are enforced only by social pressure or the body that made them [1] [1]. **Sanctioned by the state** — breach of a legal rule attracts a state sanction such as a fine, imprisonment or an award of damages; breaking a social rule might bring disapproval or, for a school rule, a detention, but no state sanction [1] [1].

**2.** Legal personality means being recognised by the law as able to hold **rights and duties** — to own property, make contracts, sue and be sued [1] [1]. A **natural person** is a human being [1]. A **legal person** is an entity the law treats as separate from its members, such as a registered company, which has a separate legal identity from its shareholders (*Salomon v A Salomon & Co Ltd* [1897]) [1].

**3. (a)** Civil: the **claimant** sues the **defendant** [1]. Criminal: the state prosecutes (in England and Wales usually through the **Crown Prosecution Service**), the case is cited as *R v* the defendant, and the person charged is the **defendant** [1].
**(b)** Civil: the burden is on the **claimant** [1], on the **balance of probabilities** [1]. Criminal: the burden is on the **prosecution** (*Woolmington v DPP* [1935]) [1], to prove guilt **beyond reasonable doubt** [1].
**(c)** Civil: found **liable**, and ordered to pay **damages** or made subject to an **injunction** [1]. Criminal: found **guilty** and given a sentence such as a **fine, community order or imprisonment** [1].

**4.** Criminal sanctions are imposed **by the state in the public interest**, to punish the offender, protect the public and deter offending [1] [1]. Civil remedies aim to **put the claimant back in the position they were in** before the wrong, usually by compensation, or to stop a continuing wrong by injunction [1] [1]. The same incident can involve both: a driver who injures a pedestrian through dangerous driving may be **prosecuted** for a driving offence and also **sued in negligence** by the victim for damages — each case has its own parties, court and standard of proof, so an acquittal does not prevent a civil claim succeeding [1] [1].

**5.** Any four, 2 marks each (1 for the theory, 1 for a correct link). **Retribution** — the offender is punished because they deserve it, in proportion to the seriousness of the offence; linked to custodial sentences scaled to seriousness, e.g. a mandatory life sentence for murder [1] [1]. **Deterrence** — individual deterrence discourages this offender from reoffending; general deterrence discourages others by example; linked to custodial sentences and exemplary fines [1] [1]. **Rehabilitation** — reforming the offender's behaviour; linked to community orders with requirements such as drug rehabilitation or unpaid work [1] [1]. **Reparation** — the offender makes amends to the victim or society; linked to compensation orders and unpaid work [1] [1]. **Denunciation** — the sentence expresses society's disapproval of the conduct [1] [1]. **Revenge** and **restoration** (restorative justice, bringing offender and victim together) are also credited. A strong answer notes that the **Criminal Justice Act 2003** lists the purposes of sentencing for adults in section 142: punishment, reduction of crime (including deterrence), reform and rehabilitation, protection of the public, and reparation.

**6. Positivism** — law is valid because it was made by the recognised authority through the proper procedure, **whatever its moral content**; law and morality are separate questions [1] [1]. Theorists: **Jeremy Bentham** and **John Austin** (law as the command of a sovereign backed by a sanction), and **H.L.A. Hart** (law as a system of primary and secondary rules identified by a rule of recognition) [1]. **Natural law** — there is a higher moral law, discoverable by reason, against which human laws are measured; an unjust law is not truly law [1] [1]. Theorist: **St Thomas Aquinas** [1]. Comparison: positivism gives **certainty** about what the law is, but can treat an immoral law as valid; natural law justifies refusing to obey unjust laws, but disagreement about morality makes it **uncertain** [1]. **Utilitarianism** (Bentham, **John Stuart Mill**) judges a law by its consequences: the right law produces **the greatest happiness of the greatest number**; Mill's harm principle limits state interference to preventing harm to others [1] [1].

**7.** Levels-based answer. Credit is given for:

- **Definitions** — public morality is the shared moral code of society; private morality concerns conduct between consenting adults that does not harm others.
- **Wolfenden Report (1957)** — recommended that homosexual acts in private between consenting adults should no longer be criminal, because there is a realm of private morality that is "not the law's business"; its recommendation was implemented by the **Sexual Offences Act 1967**.
- **Devlin** (Maccabaean Lecture, 1959, later *The Enforcement of Morals*) — society is held together by a shared morality; conduct that the "reasonable man" regards with intolerance, indignation and disgust threatens that cohesion, so the law may enforce morality even in private.
- **Hart** (*Law, Liberty and Morality*, 1963), building on Mill — the law should intervene only to prevent harm to others; enforcing morality for its own sake is an unjustified restriction of liberty.
- **Cases enforcing morality** — *Shaw v DPP* [1962] (conspiracy to corrupt public morals: publishing a directory of prostitutes), *Knuller v DPP* [1973], *R v Brown* [1993] (consent no defence to actual bodily harm inflicted during private sadomasochistic activity — a Devlin-style outcome).
- **Cases where law moved with changing morality** — *R v R* [1991] (a husband can be guilty of raping his wife), *Gillick v West Norfolk and Wisbech AHA* [1985] (contraceptive advice to a competent under-16), *Airedale NHS Trust v Bland* [1993] (withdrawing treatment from a patient in a persistent vegetative state).
- **Evaluation and conclusion** — the trend since 1967 broadly follows Hart, but *Brown* shows the courts still enforce morality where they see risk of harm; the line between private conduct and harm to others is itself contested.

Top-level answers reach a reasoned conclusion and apply the theory to the cases rather than describing each separately.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the difference between law and morality?**
Law is a set of rules made by a recognised authority and enforced by the state through the courts. Morality is a code of right and wrong enforced by conscience and social pressure. They overlap — murder is both unlawful and immoral — but some immoral acts are lawful and some unlawful acts, such as minor regulatory offences, carry no moral blame.

**What are the theories of punishment in Edexcel IAL Law?**
The YLA1 specification (1.1.8) lists revenge, retribution, rehabilitation, reparation, restoration, denunciation and deterrence. You must give examples of each and link them to the sentences available under the Criminal Justice Act 2003.

**What was the Hart/Devlin debate?**
It followed the 1957 Wolfenden Report. Lord Devlin argued that the law may enforce a society's shared morality to protect social cohesion; Professor Hart argued, following Mill, that the law should only prevent harm to others.

**What is the standard of proof in civil and criminal cases?**
In a civil case the claimant must prove the claim on the balance of probabilities. In a criminal case the prosecution must prove guilt beyond reasonable doubt.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calling the claimant a "prosecutor" or the criminal defendant "liable".
- Listing theories of punishment with no link to an actual sentence.
- Naming legal theories without a theorist, which the specification requires.
- Telling the Hart/Devlin story without applying it to cases such as *R v Brown* or *Shaw v DPP*.
