This guide covers one route through Unit 4 of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level English Language (9670) specification, Version 5.2: **3.4.2 Non-exam assessment (NEA)**, also called Unit 4b. The other route, a written exam, is covered in the [written unit guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-english-language-9670-written-unit/). Unit 4 is assessed at International A-level only, and a student takes one route or the other.

The NEA is a piece of independent research. You choose the topic, you collect your own data and you write a report. The specification says the skills and knowledge rewarded are the same as in the written route, so the NEA is a chance to demonstrate the same abilities with a topic of your choice. Condense this guide with the [NEA revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-english-language-9670-non-exam-assessment-nea-revision-notes/) and try the [practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-english-language-9670-non-exam-assessment-nea-practice/).

## What the specification says about the NEA

| Feature | What the specification states |
|---|---|
| Task | A language investigation: research an aspect of language use |
| Steps | Devise a research question, collect your own data and analyse it |
| Output | A report of approximately 2,500 words, excluding the data |
| Overview table | Describes the investigation as 2,500 to 3,000 words |
| Marks | 50 marks; 30% of the International A-level |
| Marking | Assessed by teachers and externally moderated |
| Topic | Any topic is acceptable provided that its central focus is on an aspect of language |
| Responsibility | The head of the school or college is responsible for making sure the NEA is conducted in line with OxfordAQA's instructions |

The two word counts in the specification differ. Section 3.4.2 says approximately 2,500 words, while the overview in section 2.3 gives 2,500 to 3,000. Ask your teacher which figure applies at your centre, and check the NEA guidance on the OxfordAQA website, which the specification directs centres to use for the administration of the NEA.

## Choosing a topic

The specification says students should be encouraged to pursue an area of **individual interest**, and it is not limited to areas that are formally taught. The reason given is that the basis of the investigation is the value of student-led enquiry supported by open learning. Any area that your supervising teacher sees as yielding interesting questions about language in use may be chosen.

The specification lists examples:

- representations of different individuals, social groups or nationalities;
- local varieties of English;
- social varieties, for example special interest groups;
- language and power, for example speeches and interviews;
- the language of new communication technologies;
- children's language use;
- norms and variations in usages of different kinds;
- the language of the media;
- the language of performance and play, for example drama and comedy;
- language puzzles and games;
- language in the environment, for example signage and shop names;
- literary language, for example narrative techniques in fiction;
- language rituals and routines, for example ceremonies, greetings and farewells;
- code switching and mixing between English and other languages;
- the language of different occupations;
- historical changes in English over time.

## Four kinds of question

The specification gives four kinds of question that can start an investigation.

| Type | Question | Example of a focus |
|---|---|---|
| Genre-based | What are the distinctive features of this type of language use? | Greetings in voice messages |
| Function or use-based | What is the language used to do? | How a coach uses language to motivate a team |
| Attitudes-based | How do people feel about this language? | Views on mixing languages in conversation |
| User-based | Who uses this type of language? | Which age groups use a particular slang term |

A strong investigation can combine types, but it needs one clear aim.

## Collecting data

You decide what kind of data to collect. The specification lists:

- spoken language;
- written language;
- multimodal language;
- word lists, such as lists of new words;
- attitudes to language;
- uses of language;
- views about language.

Practical points to plan early (these are Marlbridge suggestions, not requirements in the specification):

- **Amount.** Enough data to find patterns, but not so much that you cannot analyse it in detail. A few well-chosen extracts usually give more than a large unsorted collection.
- **Permission.** If you record or survey people, ask for consent, explain what the data will be used for and keep names out of your report. Follow your centre's rules.
- **Records.** Keep a note of when, where and how each piece of data was collected, because the Method section needs it.
- **Transcription.** If you record speech, decide on conventions (for example, how to show pauses and overlaps) and state them.

## The report structure

The specification says the investigation should contain six sections.

1. **Aim(s):** a statement about the aim or aims of the investigation.
2. **Method:** an explanation of the nature of the data, how it was collected and how it is to be analysed.
3. **Analysis:** a presentation of findings, categorised as appropriate to the topic and explored in detail.
4. **Conclusion:** conclusions drawn from the analysis and connected with the aims.
5. **References.**
6. **Appendix: Data.**

The word count of approximately 2,500 excludes the data. A possible split for 2,500 words is below. It is a suggestion only.

| Section | Words (suggested) |
|---|---|
| Aim(s) | 150 |
| Method | 350 |
| Analysis | 1,600 |
| Conclusion | 400 |

## How the work is marked

The 50 marks are split between the three assessment objectives, with the marks given in the specification's criteria: AO1 out of 20, AO2 out of 20 and AO3 out of 10.

| Level | AO1 and AO2 marks | AO3 marks |
|---|---|---|
| 5 | 17 to 20 | 9 to 10 |
| 4 | 13 to 16 | 7 to 8 |
| 3 | 9 to 12 | 5 to 6 |
| 2 | 5 to 8 | 3 to 4 |
| 1 | 1 to 4 | 1 to 2 |

What distinguishes the levels, in the words of the specification's criteria, is summarised here.

**AO1: understanding of methods, terminology, concepts and issues.**

- Level 5 students demonstrate an overview of issues and explore different views, approaches and interpretations. They articulate aims, scope and focus at the outset and with precision, explore theoretical frameworks and match them with texts and data, and use accurate terminology.
- Level 4 students identify different views and approaches. Their aims are explained, with scope and focus emerging as analysis proceeds, and they use relevant frameworks and mostly accurate terminology.
- Level 3 students show knowledge of relevant concepts and issues. Their aims are straightforward with little discussion of scope, the frameworks are partly relevant, and terminology is partly accurate.
- At Levels 2 and 1, aims may be unworkable, frameworks may be named without being applied, and terminology is limited or absent.

**AO2: analysis of how meanings are shaped.**

- Level 5: evaluating use of language and representations according to context, and exploring analysis within wider social and cultural contexts. This includes recognising complexity of patterning, exploring meanings as negotiated and using detailed examples to explore ideas of language as a social and cultural phenomenon.
- Level 4: analysing how language choices create meanings and representations, and how aspects of context work together. Students trace patterns and offer good examples.
- Level 3: interpreting the significance of specific language choices and linking them to an aspect of context, with some evidence.
- Levels 2 and 1: identifying features or describing content, with limited evidence.

**AO3: using English in different ways.**

- Level 5: form used expertly, register sustained and effective for the context, outstanding technical control, clear signposting and effective connections between points.
- Level 4: form used convincingly, skilful technical control and a clear line of argument.
- Level 3: competent form, mainly effective register and general clarity and accuracy.
- Levels 2 and 1: an attempt at an academic format with lapses in clarity and accuracy.

## A worked plan

Here is a plan for an attitudes-based investigation. It is an example for learning, not a model to copy.

**Topic:** attitudes towards switching between English and another language in conversation among students at one school.

**Aim:** to investigate how students feel about code switching and mixing, and whether those attitudes differ by how often students do it themselves.

**Data:** a short questionnaire completed by 30 students (anonymous, with consent), with two open questions, and two five-minute recordings of friends talking, transcribed.

**Method (key points):** describe the sample and why students were chosen; give the questions and the reasoning for them; explain consent and anonymity; say how responses will be counted and grouped; name the transcription conventions; name the frameworks, for example attitudes to language and code switching as a language strategy.

**Analysis (categories):** (1) the patterns in the questionnaire counts, (2) the reasons students give, grouped by theme, (3) examples from the transcripts of when and why switching happens, such as for emphasis, quotation or closeness, and (4) a comparison of attitudes and behaviour.

**Conclusion:** state what the data show about attitudes, whether they link to behaviour, and the limits of a small sample at one school.

The plan satisfies the criteria for a precise aim and scope (AO1), analysis of how language choices create meanings in context (AO2), and a structured, accurate report (AO3).

## Common mistakes

- A topic that is about a subject, not about language, such as an investigation of fashion rather than the language of fashion advertising.
- A question that is too broad, for example "How has English changed?" with no data or time frame.
- Too little data or data collected without a note of the method.
- Describing the data without using any frameworks or terminology.
- Analysis that is organised by participant or text with no categories.
- An appendix that is missing or data that are not referred to in the analysis.
- Counting the appendix in the word total, or forgetting that references and appendix are required sections.
- Leaving the conclusion until the last minute.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can I choose any topic?**
Any topic is acceptable provided that its central focus is on an aspect of language. Your supervising teacher decides whether a topic is likely to yield interesting questions.

**Who marks the NEA?**
The specification says teachers assess it and it is externally moderated.

**Does the report need references?**
Yes. References and an appendix of data are two of the six sections the specification lists.
