Every business needs money to start, to run day by day and to grow. Sub-topic 5.4 of Cambridge O Level Commerce (7100) has three parts: **ways in which entrepreneurs and commercial enterprises raise finance** (5.4.1), **commercial calculations** (5.4.2) and **improving commercial performance** (5.4.3).

## 5.4.1 Raising finance

An **entrepreneur** is a person who takes the risk of starting and running a business, hoping to make a profit. Entrepreneurs need **finance**, which can be short term (repaid within about a year) or long term (more than a year).

### Sources of short-term finance

| Source | Meaning | Advantage | Disadvantage |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Trade credit** | The supplier lets the business pay 30 or 60 days after delivery | Free for a time; helps cash flow | Lost cash discounts; suppliers may stop supply if payment is late |
| **Debt factoring** | The business sells its unpaid invoices to a factor, who pays most of the money at once and collects the debts | Quick cash; no need to chase debtors | The factor charges a fee, so profit falls |
| **Hire purchase** | Deposit plus instalments for equipment; owned after the last payment | Can use equipment before paying in full | Costs more than the cash price |
| **Leasing** | Rent equipment for a regular fee; the owner is the leasing company | No large payment; maintenance often included | Never owns the asset; long-term cost is high |
| **Sale and leaseback** | The business sells an asset such as a building and rents it back | Large sum of cash; business keeps using the asset | Pays rent; loses ownership and any rise in value |
| **Overdraft** | Spending beyond the bank balance, up to a limit | Flexible; interest only on the amount used | High interest; bank can ask for repayment |
| **Selling assets** | Selling unused equipment or property | No interest or repayments | One-off; asset gone; may sell below value |
| **Owner's savings** | The owner puts own money in | No interest; no need to repay | Limited amount; the owner can lose it |
| **Retained profit** | Profit kept in the business rather than paid out | No interest; no outside control | Only available once profit has been made; fewer withdrawals by owners |

### Sources of long-term finance

| Source | Meaning | Advantage | Disadvantage |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Ordinary shares** | Money from shareholders who own part of a company; dividends depend on profit | No repayment; no fixed payment | Owners share control and profit |
| **Preference shares** | Shareholders receive a fixed dividend before ordinary shareholders but usually have no vote | Fixed, known cost; control not shared | Dividends must be paid first |
| **Loans** | Borrowed from a bank for a long period at interest | Known repayments; owners keep control | Interest must be paid even in bad times; may need security |
| **Debentures** | Long-term loans to a company at a fixed rate of interest, often secured on assets | Fixed cost; no loss of control | Interest must be paid; large debts |
| **Venture capital** | Investors put money into new, risky businesses in return for a share | Large sums; advice | Investors want a say and a share of profit |
| **Crowdfunding** | Many people give small sums through a website | Reaches many people; tests demand | Needs a good idea and publicity; may fail to reach target |

### Choosing appropriate finance

| Situation | Suitable source | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Temporary cash shortage while customers have not yet paid | Overdraft or debt factoring | Short, flexible |
| New delivery van | Hire purchase or leasing | Spread payments |
| A new company wants a large sum for a factory | Ordinary shares, loan or debentures | Long-term |
| A start-up with a new invention and little security | Crowdfunding or venture capital | Takes the risk |
| An established, profitable business expands | Retained profit | Cheapest, no outside control |

### Financial terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Sales / revenue (turnover)** | Money received from selling goods or services |
| **Profit** | Revenue minus costs |
| **Working capital** | Current assets minus current liabilities: money available for day-to-day running |
| **Assets** | Things the business owns (premises, stock, cash) |
| **Liabilities** | Money the business owes (loans, trade creditors) |
| **Interest** | Payment for the use of borrowed money, or the return on savings |
| **Capital** | Money put into the business by the owners |
| **Cash discount** | A reduction for paying promptly |
| **Trade discount** | A reduction given by a seller to another business, regardless of when it pays |

*Worked examples.* Cash discount: an invoice of $800 with a 2.5% cash discount for payment within 14 days. Discount = 2.5% × 800 = $20, so the buyer pays $780. Trade discount: a list price of $400 with 25% trade discount. Discount = 25% × 400 = $100, so the price is $300.

*Worked example: debt factoring.* A business is owed $20,000 in invoices. The factor pays 90% now and charges a 3% fee on the invoice total.

1. Cash now = 90% × 20,000 = $18,000.
2. Fee = 3% × 20,000 = $600.
3. Total the business receives = 18,000 − 600 + the remaining 10% (2,000 when debtors pay) = **$19,400**.

The business pays $600 for quick access to $18,000.

## 5.4.2 Commercial calculations

The syllabus gives six formulas.

| Quantity | Formula |
|---|---|
| **Revenue** | Selling price per unit × number of units sold |
| **Gross profit** | Revenue − cost of sales (the cost of purchasing inventory) |
| **Gross profit margin (%)** | Gross profit ÷ revenue × 100 |
| **Net profit (or net loss) for the year** | Gross profit − expenses |
| **Working capital** | Current assets − current liabilities |
| **Mark-up percentage** | (Average selling price − unit cost) ÷ unit cost × 100 |

### Worked example 1: profit calculations

A shop sells 1,200 units at $25 each. The cost of sales is $18,000 and expenses are $7,500.

1. Revenue = 25 × 1,200 = **$30,000**.
2. Gross profit = 30,000 − 18,000 = **$12,000**.
3. Gross profit margin = 12,000 ÷ 30,000 × 100 = **40%**.
4. Net profit = 12,000 − 7,500 = **$4,500**.

### Worked example 2: working capital

Current assets: stock $9,000, debtors $3,500 and cash $1,500 = $14,000. Current liabilities: creditors $7,000 and overdraft $2,500 = $9,500.

Working capital = 14,000 − 9,500 = **$4,500**. Positive working capital means the business can pay its short-term debts.

### Worked example 3: mark-up

A trader buys a unit for $15 and sells it for $24.

Mark-up = (24 − 15) ÷ 15 × 100 = 9 ÷ 15 × 100 = **60%**.

Note the difference from margin: the margin on that unit is 9 ÷ 24 × 100 = 37.5%. Mark-up is based on cost; margin is based on selling price.

### Working backwards

If a trader wants a 40% mark-up on a unit costing $50, the selling price is 50 + (40% × 50) = 50 + 20 = **$70**.

## 5.4.3 Improving commercial performance

| Aim | Methods |
|---|---|
| Increase sales (turnover) | Improve advertising; sales promotion; introduce new products; open new outlets or sell online; improve customer service |
| Increase profit | Reduce expenses; raise prices (if customers will still buy); reduce cost of sales by finding cheaper suppliers; sell more |
| Increase mark-up | Increase selling price; buy at lower cost |
| Improve working capital | Collect debts faster; sell old stock; delay paying suppliers (within agreed terms); use trade credit; reduce expenses |

Each method has a risk. A higher price can reduce sales. Cutting expenses on advertising can lower turnover. Cheaper suppliers may have lower quality.

*Worked example.* The shop in worked example 1 raises the price from $25 to $26 and sells the same 1,200 units, with unchanged costs. New revenue = 26 × 1,200 = $31,200. Gross profit = 31,200 − 18,000 = $13,200. Net profit = 13,200 − 7,500 = **$5,700**, an increase of $1,200. If the higher price reduces sales to 1,100 units, revenue = 26 × 1,100 = $28,600, and with the same cost per unit of $15 (18,000 ÷ 1,200), cost of sales = 15 × 1,100 = $16,500. Gross profit = 28,600 − 16,500 = $12,100, and net profit = 12,100 − 7,500 = $4,600, only $100 more than before.

## Annotated model answer

**Question:** A new business needs $60,000 to buy equipment. Recommend a source of finance and justify your choice. [4]

> I would recommend a bank loan [1]. The repayments are fixed and known, which helps the owner plan [1], and the owner keeps full control of the business, unlike with shares or venture capital [1]. A disadvantage is that interest must be paid even in a bad year, but the equipment will help earn revenue to cover this [1].

**Why it scores 4:** a source is chosen, advantages are linked to the business, and a disadvantage is weighed.

## Common mistakes

- Using short-term finance for a long-term purchase.
- Taking the percentage of the wrong figure. Margin uses revenue; mark-up uses cost.
- Forgetting to multiply by 100.
- Confusing gross profit and net profit.
- Adding liabilities to assets for working capital.
- Treating retained profit as something that must be repaid.

## Exam technique

- **Show the formula, substitution, answer and units.**
- **Choose** a source of finance by linking it to the time, amount and risk in the case.
- **Improve performance**: say which figure improves and how, and mention the risk.
