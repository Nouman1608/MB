Condensed notes for the section 3.4 row **Essential requirements of contract**: "Offer and acceptance", "Consideration (including privity of contract)" and "Intention to create legal relations". For explanation and a worked formation problem, use the [essential requirements of contract study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-law-7162-essential-requirements-of-contract/).

## Formation checklist

```
OFFER  ->  still open?  ->  ACCEPTANCE (mirror image, communicated)
   +  CONSIDERATION from each side
   +  INTENTION to create legal relations
   =  CONTRACT
```

## Offer

| Point | Authority |
|---|---|
| "May be prepared to sell" not an offer | *Gibson v Manchester CC* (1979) |
| Statement of lowest price is information | *Harvey v Facey* (1893) |
| Goods displayed: invitation to treat | *Pharmaceutical Society v Boots* (1953); *Fisher v Bell* (1961) |
| Advertisements usually invitations | *Partridge v Crittenden* (1968) |
| Unilateral offer to the world | *Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co* (1893) |
| Auctions: bid is offer; hammer is acceptance | *Payne v Cave* (1789) |

## Termination of offer

| Method | Rule | Authority |
|---|---|---|
| Revocation | Must be communicated; not on posting | *Byrne v Van Tienhoven* (1880) |
| Via third party | Valid if reliable | *Dickinson v Dodds* (1876) |
| Counter-offer | Ends offer | *Hyde v Wrench* (1840) |
| Request for information | Does not end offer | *Stevenson v McLean* (1880) |
| Lapse | After stated or reasonable time | *Ramsgate Victoria Hotel v Montefiore* (1866) |
| Unilateral offer | Cannot revoke once performance begun | *Errington v Errington* (1952) |

**Promise to keep an offer open:** not binding without consideration.

<figure class="topic-figure">
  <img src="https://img.marlbridge.com/aqa-a-level-law-7162-essential-requirements-of-contract--02-how-an-offer-ends.webp" width="900" height="428" alt="Two columns. Things that end an offer: revocation communicated to the offeree (Byrne v Van Tienhoven), revocation through a reliable third party (Dickinson v Dodds), rejection or counter-offer (Hyde v Wrench), lapse of time (Ramsgate Victoria Hotel v Montefiore) and possibly death. Things that do not: a posted revocation not yet received, a request for information (Stevenson v McLean), and revoking a unilateral offer once performance has begun (Errington v Errington)." loading="lazy">
  <figcaption>Revocation must reach the offeree; a counter-offer kills the offer, an inquiry does not.</figcaption>
</figure>

## Acceptance

- Final, unqualified, mirror image: new terms = counter-offer.
- Battle of the forms: *Butler Machine Tool v Ex-Cell-O* (1979).
- By conduct: *Brogden v Metropolitan Railway* (1877).
- Silence is not acceptance: *Felthouse v Bindley* (1862).

| Method | Effective | Authority |
|---|---|---|
| Post | On posting (if reasonable to use) | *Adams v Lindsell* (1818); *Henthorn v Fraser* (1892) |
| Instant | On receipt | *Entores* (1955) |
| Telex/similar to business | When received or would be read in office hours | *The Brimnes* (1975); *Brinkibon* (1983) |
| Unilateral | By performance | *Carlill* (1893) |

## Consideration

**Definition:** *Currie v Misa* (1875): benefit to one party or detriment to the other.

| Rule | Authority |
|---|---|
| Must move from promisee | *Tweddle v Atkinson* (1861) |
| Need not be adequate | *Thomas v Thomas* (1842); *Chappell v Nestlé* (1960) |
| Must have some value | *White v Bluett* (1853) |
| Past consideration not good | *Re McArdle* (1951) |
| Exception: requested, payment expected | *Lampleigh v Braithwait* (1615); *Pao On v Lau Yiu Long* (1980) |
| Existing public duty not enough | *Collins v Godefroy* (1831) |
| Exceeding public duty is | *Glasbrook Bros v Glamorgan CC* (1925) |
| Existing contractual duty not enough | *Stilk v Myrick* (1809) |
| Exceeding contractual duty is | *Hartley v Ponsonby* (1857) |
| Practical benefit, no duress | *Williams v Roffey Bros* (1991) |
| Part payment of debt not enough | *Pinnel's Case* (1602); *Foakes v Beer* (1884) |
| Promissory estoppel | *Central London Property Trust v High Trees* (1947) |

<figure class="topic-figure">
  <img src="https://img.marlbridge.com/aqa-a-level-law-7162-essential-requirements-of-contract--04-consideration-rules.webp" width="900" height="746" alt="The Currie v Misa definition, then a table of rules. Consideration must move from the promisee (Tweddle v Atkinson); it need not be adequate (Thomas v Thomas; Chappell v Nestlé) but must be real (White v Bluett); it must not be past (Re McArdle) unless requested and understood to be paid (Lampleigh; Pao On); an existing public duty (Collins v Godefroy) or contractual duty (Stilk v Myrick) is not enough unless exceeded (Glasbrook; Hartley v Ponsonby) or a practical benefit arises (Williams v Roffey); part payment is not consideration (Pinnel; Foakes v Beer) unless something different is given, and promissory estoppel may apply (High Trees)." loading="lazy">
  <figcaption>Each side must give something real; doing only what you already must is not enough.</figcaption>
</figure>

## Privity

- Only parties can sue or be sued: *Dunlop v Selfridge* (1915).
- **1999 Act s1:** third party may enforce if expressly allowed, or term purports to benefit them (unless contrary intention); identified by name, class or description (s1(3)).
- Agency; *Jackson v Horizon Holidays* (1975); *Beswick v Beswick* (1968).

## Intention to create legal relations

| Context | Presumption | For | Rebutted |
|---|---|---|---|
| Social / domestic | Not binding | *Balfour v Balfour* (1919); *Jones v Padavatton* (1969) | *Merritt v Merritt* (1970); *Simpkins v Pays* (1955) |
| Commercial | Binding | *Edwards v Skyways* (1964) | *Rose & Frank v Crompton* (1925); *Kleinwort Benson v Malaysia Mining* (1989) |

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Offer | Statement of terms with intention to be bound on acceptance |
| Invitation to treat | Invitation to others to make offers |
| Counter-offer | Reply with new terms, ending the original offer |
| Revocation | Withdrawal of an offer before acceptance |
| Postal rule | Acceptance effective on posting |
| Consideration | Something of value given in return for a promise |
| Past consideration | Act done before the promise was made |
| Promissory estoppel | Equitable doctrine stopping a party going back on a promise relied on |
| Privity | Only parties to a contract can enforce it |
| Honour clause | Term showing the parties do not intend legal relations |

## Exam traps

- Revocation is never effective on posting.
- A request for information does not kill the offer; a counter-offer does.
- Adequacy is irrelevant; sufficiency is required.
- Part payment of a debt: *Foakes v Beer* still applies; *Williams v Roffey* does not extend to it.
- Social presumptions can be rebutted by writing or by separation (*Merritt*).

## Self-test

1. Why was there no contract in *Gibson*? *"May be prepared to sell" was not an offer.*
2. When is a revocation effective? *When communicated to the offeree (Byrne v Van Tienhoven).*
3. What did *Dickinson v Dodds* decide? *Revocation via a reliable third party is effective.*
4. Does a request for information end an offer? *No (Stevenson v McLean).*
5. Can a unilateral offer be revoked once performance has started? *No (Errington v Errington).*
6. Whose terms won in *Butler v Ex-Cell-O*? *The buyer's: the seller accepted the counter-offer by returning the slip.*
7. When is a posted acceptance effective? *On posting, where post is a reasonable method.*
8. Give the *Currie v Misa* definition of consideration. *A benefit to one party or a detriment to the other.*
9. When can past consideration be good? *Where the act was requested, payment was expected and would have been enforceable (Pao On).*
10. Why was there consideration in *Glasbrook*? *The police provided more protection than their public duty required.*
11. What did *High Trees* decide? *A promise to accept less rent, relied on, was binding for the wartime period.*
12. What does s1 of the 1999 Act allow? *A third party to enforce a term expressly allowing it or purporting to benefit them.*
