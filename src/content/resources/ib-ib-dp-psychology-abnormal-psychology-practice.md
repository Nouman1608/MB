IB DP Psychology Paper 2 (first assessment 2019) sets three 22-mark essays on each option. The six questions below span the option's three topics -- diagnosis, etiology and treatment -- with a model plan for each. Allow one hour per essay, including planning.

## How the essays are marked

|---|---|---|
| A Focus on the question | 2 | Explains the problem or issue posed by the question |
| B Knowledge and understanding | 6 | Accurate, detailed knowledge; well-explained terminology |

## Questions

**1.** Evaluate the validity and reliability of diagnosis. **[22]**

**2.** Discuss the role of clinical biases in diagnosis. **[22]**

**3.** Contrast two explanations for one disorder. **[22]**

**4.** Discuss prevalence rates of one disorder. **[22]**

**5.** Evaluate the effectiveness of one biological treatment of one disorder. **[22]**

**6.** Discuss the role of culture in the treatment of disorders. **[22]**

## Model essay plans

**1. Validity and reliability of diagnosis.**
- *A/B:* define validity (diagnosis identifies a real, distinct disorder) and reliability (consistency between clinicians and over time); classification systems DSM-5 and ICD-11.
- *C:* Rosenhan (1973) -- pseudopatients admitted and diagnosed, challenging validity; Cooper et al. (1972) -- different diagnoses by US and UK psychiatrists from the same interviews, challenging reliability; Regier et al. (2013) -- low kappa (about 0.28) for major depressive disorder in DSM-5 field trials.
- *D:* Rosenhan's study is dated, involved deception of hospital staff and tested the admission of patients who lied about symptoms; later revisions of the DSM added explicit criteria to improve reliability; field-trial conditions differ from real clinics; cultural expression of symptoms may lower both validity and reliability.

**2. Clinical biases.**
- *B:* confirmation bias, cultural bias, gender bias, and patient-side bias such as under-reporting or somatisation.
- *C:* Cooper et al. (1972) on national differences; Kleinman (1982) on neurasthenia and somatic presentation of depression in China; research on differences in diagnosis rates between men and women.
- *D:* difficulty separating bias from genuine differences in prevalence; structured interviews reduce bias; cultural concepts of distress in DSM-5 respond to criticism.

**3. Two explanations for depression.**
- *B/C:* biological -- Kendler et al. (2006) twin study, heritability about 38%; Caspi et al. (2003) gene-environment interaction. Cognitive -- Beck's cognitive triad and negative schemas; Alloy et al. (1999) negative cognitive style predicting later episodes.
- *D (contrast):* biological explanations stress inherited vulnerability and neurochemistry, cognitive ones stress learned thinking patterns; biological evidence is largely correlational or genetic-epidemiological, cognitive evidence relies on self-report; the diathesis-stress view combines both; implications for treatment (medication versus CBT).

**4. Prevalence rates.**
- *B:* prevalence -- the proportion of a population with a disorder at a time or over a lifetime; factors include gender, culture, socio-economic status and diagnostic practice.
- *C:* women diagnosed with depression roughly twice as often as men in many countries; Brown and Harris (1978) social vulnerability factors; Kleinman (1982) on cultural presentation affecting recorded rates.
- *D:* differences may reflect help-seeking and diagnosis rather than true rates; problems comparing rates across cultures with different classification and stigma.

**5. Biological treatment.**
- *B:* SSRIs increase serotonin availability by blocking reuptake.
- *C:* Kirsch et al. (2008) meta-analysis showing small advantage over placebo except in severe depression; Elkin et al. (1989) showing imipramine effective, especially for more severe depression; DeRubeis et al. (2005) comparing medication with CBT.
- *D:* methodological issues (publication bias, placebo response, short trials); side effects and relapse; biological treatment may not address cognitive or social causes; combined treatment as an alternative.

**6. Culture in treatment.**
- *B:* culture affects how distress is expressed, whether help is sought and which treatments are acceptable; culturally adapted therapy and therapist-client matching.
- *C:* research on culturally adapted CBT; Kleinman's work on cultural explanations of illness affecting treatment expectations.
- *D:* small and varied studies; "culture" is broad; risk of stereotyping clients; ethical importance of respecting clients' explanatory models.

## Worked example: one fully developed paragraph (Question 1)

> One challenge to the validity of diagnosis comes from Rosenhan (1973). Eight mentally healthy
> pseudopatients sought admission to twelve psychiatric hospitals in the USA, each reporting that they
> heard a voice saying words such as "empty" and "thud". All were admitted, and most were diagnosed with
> schizophrenia; once on the ward they behaved normally, yet they were discharged with schizophrenia
> "in remission". This suggests that clinicians could not distinguish the sane from the insane, so the
> diagnosis did not identify a real disorder. However, the study has limitations as evidence about
> diagnosis today. The pseudopatients lied about a symptom, and a psychiatrist may reasonably act on a
> reported hallucination; the hospitals were deceived, raising ethical concerns; and diagnostic criteria
> have since become far more explicit. Rosenhan's study therefore shows how labels can persist once
> applied, but it is weaker evidence that modern diagnosis lacks validity.

This paragraph earns credit in four criteria at once: knowledge (B), research described accurately (C), evaluation of method and ethics (D) and a final sentence that returns to the question (A).

## Frequently asked questions

**Must I name a disorder in every essay?**
Whenever the question says "one disorder" or asks about etiology, prevalence or treatment, yes. Diagnosis questions can draw on several disorders.

**How many studies should an essay use?**
Usually two or three, each explained and evaluated, rather than a long list.

**Is "contrast" the same as "compare"?**
No. In IB command terms, contrast means giving an account of the differences between the two explanations, referring to both throughout; a compare question also expects similarities.
