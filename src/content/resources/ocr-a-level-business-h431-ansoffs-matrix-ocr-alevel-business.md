## Nature and purpose

Ansoff's matrix is a planning model for growth. It places each growth option on two dimensions: whether the **product** is existing or new, and whether the **market** is existing or new. The four combinations are market penetration, market development, product development and diversification. Its purpose is to set out the range of growth options and to show that risk rises as a business moves away from what it already knows.

## Classifying hard cases

Exam questions often include options that are not obvious. Two tests help.

1. **Is the customer group new?** A market is defined by who buys, not by where the product is sold. Selling to the same customers through a new channel, such as an app, is still the existing market. Selling to a different group, such as businesses rather than households, is a new market, even in the same town.
2. **Is the product meaningfully new?** A minor change, such as new packaging or a new size, is still the existing product. A product that needs new skills, new equipment or a new supplier base is new.

| Option for a garden-furniture maker | Classification | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| A loyalty discount for past customers | Market penetration | Same product, same buyers |
| Selling the same benches to hotels and pubs | Market development | New customer group |
| A new range of garden sheds for existing customers | Product development | New product needing new skills |
| Opening a garden-design consultancy for commercial clients | Diversification | New service, new customer group |
| A larger version of the best-selling bench | Market penetration | Minor change; same customers |

When an option is borderline, say so and justify your classification: credit comes from the reasoning.

## Strategies and diversification types

Each quadrant includes a range of strategies.

- **Market penetration:** price promotions, more advertising, loyalty schemes, better distribution, taking over a direct rival.
- **Market development:** new regions or countries, new customer segments, new uses for the product.
- **Product development:** new models, improved versions, extensions to a range, complementary services.
- **Diversification**, which comes in kinds that carry different risk:
  - **Related diversification**, where the new activity shares skills, technology or customers with the old. This includes moving into a supplier's stage of production (**backward vertical**) or a customer's stage (**forward vertical**), such as a brewery buying pubs.
  - **Unrelated diversification**, where there is no link at all, such as a brewery opening a software business.

Related diversification is usually less risky, because some knowledge transfers.

## Where the risk comes from

Risk in Ansoff's matrix comes from **gaps in knowledge**. Each quadrant has a different gap, and so a different way of reducing it.

| Quadrant | Main source of risk | Ways to reduce it |
|---|---|---|
| Market penetration | Limited room to grow; rivals retaliate; price cuts squeeze margins | Check market share and saturation first; compete on service rather than price |
| Market development | Not knowing the new customers, rivals, laws or culture | Market research; use a local agent or partner; trial in one region |
| Product development | Technical failure; existing customers may not want the product; development cost | Prototypes and customer testing; launch to a sample of loyal customers |
| Diversification | Both gaps at once; management stretched | Related rather than unrelated; acquire a firm that already has the expertise; joint venture |

**Penetration is not always low risk.** If a business already holds a large share of a saturated market, the only way to grow is to take customers from rivals, which may provoke a price war. A low-risk label can hide a low-reward reality.

### Risk to stakeholders by quadrant

- **Penetration:** employees and suppliers face little change; shareholders face limited upside; a price war hurts margins and so dividends.
- **Market development:** employees may need to travel or relocate; lenders may be asked to fund premises in unfamiliar places.
- **Product development:** staff need new skills; existing customers may be disappointed if quality falls.
- **Diversification:** the widest exposure. Shareholders could gain or lose heavily; employees in the core business may lose investment or attention; lenders face the greatest uncertainty about repayment.

## Worked example: a recommendation using figures

**Case.** Halden Ice Cream makes premium ice cream sold through 300 independent shops in northern England. Sales revenue is £4.5 million and the market share in its region is about 30%. The owners want to raise annual profit by £150,000 within three years and have £400,000 to invest.

| Option | Quadrant | Cost | Forecast extra annual profit | Payback |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A: national advertising to sell more in existing shops | Market penetration | £250,000 | £60,000 | 4.2 years |
| B: supply ice cream to restaurants and hotels in the region | Market development | £180,000 | £75,000 | 2.4 years |
| C: a frozen-yoghurt range for existing shops | Product development | £220,000 | £80,000 | 2.75 years |
| D: open three ice-cream parlours | Diversification (related, forward vertical) | £400,000 | £110,000 | 3.6 years |

Payback = cost ÷ forecast extra annual profit. For B: 180,000 ÷ 75,000 = 2.4 years.

**Analysis.** Penetration (A) has the longest payback and the lowest profit, because with a 30% regional share and only 300 shops, room to grow is limited. Market development (B) has the quickest payback and uses Halden's existing product, so the main risk is learning what restaurants want. Product development (C) uses existing customers who already trust the brand, but frozen yoghurt needs new recipes and equipment. The parlours (D) offer most profit but use all £400,000 and require retail skills Halden lacks.

**Recommendation.** Halden should choose **B and C together**: combined cost £400,000, combined extra profit £155,000 a year, which meets the £150,000 target. B pays back fastest and C builds on existing customers, and the two risks are different, so a problem in one does not affect the other. This is preferable to D, which offers less profit for the same money, puts all the funds into one unfamiliar activity and leaves nothing in reserve.

**Condition.** The recommendation depends on the forecasts. If restaurant demand is weaker than expected, B could be scaled back and C would still be supported by existing shop customers. Halden should trial B with a small number of restaurants first.

## Usefulness

**Strengths.** The matrix is simple and quick. It sets out the full range of growth options. It links each option to a type and source of risk, which helps managers ask the right questions. It works well as a first step before detailed analysis.

**Limitations.** It gives no figures, so it cannot say which option is profitable or affordable; in the Halden example, the payback figures did more to decide the choice than the matrix did. "New" and "existing" are matters of degree. It ignores competitors' reactions and changes in the market. The risk ranking is a generalisation: a well-researched market development can be safer than a poorly judged penetration strategy in a saturated market.

**Judgement.** The matrix is most useful at the start of a strategic decision, to identify and classify options and the risks to investigate. It is least useful as a basis for the final choice, which needs financial data, market research and an assessment of the business's resources.

## Common mistakes

- Defining the market by place rather than by customer group.
- Treating any change to a product as product development.
- Saying penetration is always low risk without checking market share and saturation.
- Recommending a quadrant without using the case figures.

## Exam technique

"Distinguish" needs both quadrants and the difference. "Evaluate the level of risk" means explaining where the risk comes from, how big it is for this business and who bears it. "Recommend and justify" requires a choice, use of the figures, a rejected alternative and a condition.
