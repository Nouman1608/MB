This guide covers **Topic 1 What Is Business?**, the first of six
subject-content sections in AQA AS Business (7131), for first teaching
from September 2023 and current for cohorts taking exams through summer
2026. AQA runs AS as a separate, stand-alone qualification that shares
a combined specification document with A-level Business (7132) — the AS
content is the first six of the ten A-level sections, and this topic's
content is identical to Topic 1 of the A-level specification.

## Where this fits in 7131

As the opening topic of the AS course, What Is Business? introduces why
businesses exist, the range of legal structures they can take, and the
external factors that shape their decisions before those factors are
studied purely as a static list — most later AS topics (managers and
decision making, marketing, operations, finance, human resources) build
directly on the foundational vocabulary and concepts introduced here.

## Syllabus coverage

**AQA AS BUSINESS (7131) — TOPIC 1 WHAT IS BUSINESS?**

- 3.1.1 Understanding the nature and purpose of business — why
businesses exist and what they aim to achieve
- 3.1.2 Understanding different business forms — the range of legal and
organisational structures a business can take
- 3.1.3 Understanding that businesses operate within an external
environment — how factors outside a business's direct control,
including economic conditions and costs and demand, affect its
decisions

## How to approach it

Because AS Business is examined as a stand-alone qualification with its
own assessment, do not assume this content matters less than the same
material studied at full A-level — AS candidates are examined on
exactly this sub-topic set, and its concepts recur throughout the rest
of the AS course. Start with **added value**: a business converts
inputs into outputs that satisfy customer needs, and added value is the
difference between the cost of those inputs and the price a customer is
willing to pay. Added value can be increased through branding, quality,
service, convenience and design — not only by raising price — and
questions frequently ask candidates to suggest a specific, context-
appropriate way a named business could increase it.

For business forms (3.1.2), revise by comparing structures directly
against each other on liability, access to capital, and control, rather
than learning each form's definition in isolation: a sole trader has
unlimited liability and full control but limited access to capital; a
private limited company has limited liability and more capital access,
at the cost of some control and a requirement to publish accounts. This
comparative approach matches how exam questions typically ask you to
recommend or evaluate a form of ownership for a specific business
scenario.

For the external environment (3.1.3), the specification highlights
economic conditions and costs and demand specifically — practise
applying these to a real or plausible business scenario, since exam
questions typically ask you to analyse or evaluate the impact of a
named external change on a named business, not to list external
factors in the abstract.

## Worked example: added value in practice

A small coffee shop competes with a larger chain by sourcing specialty
beans and training staff in barista skills, charging a higher price
than the chain.

```
Input cost:      broadly similar cost of coffee beans and staff time to
                 the larger chain
Added value:     specialty sourcing (quality) and trained staff service
                 (service) justify a higher price than input cost alone
                 would suggest
Outcome:         the coffee shop earns a larger margin between input
                 cost and selling price than a business competing on
                 price alone
```

This shows added value increasing through quality and service, not
through raising price on an otherwise identical product — the
distinction the specification specifically tests.

## Common mistakes

Confusing added value with profit, when added value is calculated
before overheads are deducted. Describing a business form's features
without comparing it to the alternatives on liability, capital access
and control — the dimensions exam questions typically ask about.
Treating external environment factors as a fixed list to recite, rather
than applying a named factor's effect to the specific business scenario
given. Assuming AS content is less rigorously examined than the
equivalent A-level topic, when AS Business is assessed as its own
complete qualification.

## SMART objectives, applied to this topic

Although objective-setting is developed further later in the AS course,
Topic 1's introduction to why businesses exist is best paired with the
idea that whatever a business's overall aim, its objectives should be
**SMART** — specific, measurable, achievable, realistic and time-bound.
A business that says it wants to "grow" has stated a purpose but not an
objective; a business that says it wants to "increase market share by
5% within two years" has stated something specific and measurable
enough to actually judge success against later in the course.

## Quick revision checklist

- Define added value precisely and name at least three ways a business
can increase it besides raising price.
- Compare forms of ownership on liability, access to capital, and
control — not as isolated definitions.
- Apply the external environment's named factors (economic conditions,
costs and demand) to a specific business scenario rather than listing
them abstractly.
- Remember that AS Business (7131) is a stand-alone qualification with
its own full assessment on this content.
