These questions cover key question 1.1 of Cambridge O Level History 2147 (2024–2026): were the
revolutions of 1848 important? Section A practises single *describe* and *explain* parts; Section B gives
full three-part structured questions, one for each country named in the specified content and one on the
revolutions as a whole. Key question 1.1 is not a prescribed Paper 2 topic for 2024–2026, so there is no
source question here. The mark splits used are illustrative.

## Section A

**1.** Describe how Metternich tried to prevent revolution in Europe before 1848. **[4]**

**2.** Describe what happened in Hungary in 1848–49. **[4]**

**3.** Describe the work of the Frankfurt Parliament in 1848–49. **[4]**

**4.** Describe the events in Milan and Venice in March 1848. **[4]**

**5.** Explain why the June Days were important for the revolution in France. **[6]**

**6.** Explain why Louis Napoleon won the presidential election of December 1848. **[6]**

**7.** Explain why the abolition of serfdom in 1848 helped the Austrian government. **[6]**

## Section B

**8.** The Austrian Empire.
(a) Describe the events in Vienna in March 1848. **[4]**
(b) Explain why nationalism weakened the revolutions in the Austrian Empire. **[6]**
(c) "Military force was the main reason the revolutions in the Austrian Empire failed." How far do you
agree? Explain your answer. **[10]**

**9.** Italy.
(a) Describe the role of Pope Pius IX in 1848–49. **[4]**
(b) Explain why Piedmont failed to defeat Austria in 1848–49. **[6]**
(c) "The Italian revolutions of 1848–49 achieved nothing." How far do you agree? Explain your answer.
**[10]**

**10.** Germany.
(a) Describe the events in Berlin in March 1848. **[4]**
(b) Explain why the Frankfurt Parliament failed to unite Germany. **[6]**
(c) "Frederick William IV was to blame for the failure of the revolution in Germany." How far do you
agree? Explain your answer. **[10]**

**11.** The revolutions as a whole.
(a) Describe the economic problems in Europe in 1845–47. **[4]**
(b) Explain why so many revolutions broke out in the same year. **[6]**
(c) "The revolutions of 1848 had more differences than similarities." How far do you agree? Explain your
answer. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** One mark per relevant point, up to four: he played a leading part at the Congress of Vienna (1815),
which restored monarchies **[1]**; he used censorship of newspapers, books and universities, for example
through the Carlsbad Decrees in the German Confederation (1819) **[1]**; he used police and informers to
watch suspected revolutionaries **[1]**; Austrian troops were sent to crush risings in Italy, for example in
1821 and 1831 **[1]**.

**2.** One mark per point, up to four: led by Kossuth, the Hungarian Diet passed the April Laws, giving
Hungary its own government and ending serfdom **[1]**; the Croats under Jelačić, fearing Hungarian rule,
invaded Hungary for the emperor **[1]**; Hungary declared independence in April 1849 **[1]**; Austria, with a
large Russian army sent by Nicholas I, defeated Hungary in August 1849 **[1]**.

**3.** One mark per point, up to four: it was elected and first met in May 1848 to create a united Germany
**[1]**; it debated whether to include Austria (*Grossdeutschland*) or not (*Kleindeutschland*) **[1]**; it drew
up Fundamental Rights and a constitution **[1]**; in 1849 it offered the crown to Frederick William IV of
Prussia, who refused, and it broke up **[1]**.

**4.** One mark per point, up to four: in the Five Days of Milan the people rose against Austrian rule
**[1]** and forced Radetzky's army to withdraw **[1]**; Venice declared a republic under Daniele Manin **[1]**;
Charles Albert of Piedmont then declared war on Austria to support them **[1]**.

**5.** Credit explained reasons. The closure of the National Workshops led Paris workers to rise in June
1848, and Cavaignac crushed them with thousands killed **[2]**. This split the revolution: the middle classes
and peasants now feared radical workers more than the old order **[2]**. As a result France moved to the
right, which helped Louis Napoleon win the presidency in December **[2]**.

**6.** Credit explained reasons. His name recalled the glory of Napoleon I, which appealed especially to
peasants who now had the vote **[2]**. After the June Days many voters wanted order and saw him as a strong
leader, while workers would not vote for Cavaignac, who had crushed them **[2]**. He was little known
politically, so he could appeal to many groups, and some conservative politicians thought they could
control him **[2]**.

**7.** Credit explained reasons. Peasants had been the largest group with grievances, and the abolition of
serfdom in September 1848 removed their main complaint **[2]**. Once satisfied, they had little reason to
support urban liberals or nationalists, so the revolutions lost mass support in the countryside **[2]**.
Peasant soldiers in the army stayed loyal, which helped the government use force against Vienna and
Hungary **[2]**.

**8.** (a) One mark per point, up to four: students and workers rose in Vienna in March 1848 **[1]**;
Metternich resigned and fled **[1]**; Emperor Ferdinand promised a constitution and press freedom **[1]**;
an elected assembly was later called **[1]**. **[4]**
(b) Explained points: each nationality wanted different things, so there was no single revolution **[2]**;
Croats under Jelačić fought for the emperor against the Hungarians, and Czechs feared German or Hungarian
domination **[2]**; Vienna could use one nationality against another and defeat them one by one **[2]**.
**[6]**
(c) **Agree:** Windischgrätz bombarded Prague (June 1848) and retook Vienna (October 1848); Radetzky won in
Italy at Custoza and Novara; Hungary was only defeated by Austrian and Russian armies in 1849. **Disagree:**
divisions between nationalities, the abolition of serfdom winning over the peasants, and middle-class fear
of radicals all weakened the revolutions before the armies acted; the new emperor, Franz Joseph, gave the
government fresh leadership. **Judgement:** force was the decisive factor, but it succeeded because
divisions and peasant satisfaction had already isolated the revolutionaries. Higher marks go to explained
evidence on both sides and a supported judgement. **[10]**

**9.** (a) One mark per point, up to four: he was seen as a liberal and granted reforms and a constitution
in the Papal States **[1]**; in April 1848 he refused to support war against Catholic Austria **[1]**; he fled
Rome in late 1848 and a Roman Republic was declared in February 1849 **[1]**; he was restored when French
troops ended the Republic in 1849 **[1]**. **[4]**
(b) Explained reasons: Austria's army under Radetzky was stronger and better led, winning at Custoza
(1848) and Novara (1849) **[2]**; other Italian rulers and the Pope withdrew support, so Piedmont fought
largely alone **[2]**; Piedmont had no foreign ally, and many Italians distrusted Charles Albert's aims **[2]**.
**[6]**
(c) **Agree:** Austria recovered Lombardy and Venetia; rulers in Naples, Tuscany and the Papal States
withdrew their constitutions; the Roman Republic was crushed by France; Italy was no closer to unity in
1849. **Disagree:** Piedmont kept the Statuto, becoming the natural leader of Italian nationalism; Victor
Emmanuel II became king; Garibaldi and Mazzini became heroes; the failure showed that Italy needed foreign
help to defeat Austria. **Judgement:** they failed in their immediate aims but created the conditions for
later unification led by Piedmont. **[10]**

**10.** (a) One mark per point, up to four: crowds demanded reform and fighting broke out on 18 March
1848 **[1]**; many people were killed in street fighting with the army **[1]**; Frederick William IV withdrew
his troops from the city **[1]**; he promised a constitution and appeared to support German unity **[1]**.
**[4]**
(b) Explained reasons: it had no army, so it depended on Prussia and Austria, as Malmö showed **[2]**;
members spent months in debate, including over whether to include Austria, while rulers recovered
**[2]**; it needed the support of a major ruler, and when Frederick William IV refused the crown in 1849 it
had no means of making its constitution work **[2]**. **[6]**
(c) **Agree:** he refused the crown in April 1849, ending the plan for a united Germany; he dissolved the
Prussian assembly and imposed his own constitution in December 1848; he used the army once he felt
secure. **Disagree:** the Parliament was already weak, with no army, long debates and middle-class fear of
radicals; Austria opposed a united Germany that excluded or weakened it; many German rulers recovered
power on their own. **Judgement:** his refusal was the final blow, but the Parliament's weakness and
Austrian opposition meant failure was likely. **[10]**

**11.** (a) One mark per point, up to four: potato blight caused food shortages **[1]**; poor grain harvests
raised bread prices **[1]**; an industrial and financial slump caused unemployment in the towns **[1]**;
hunger and unemployment led to food riots and discontent **[1]**. **[4]**
(b) Explained reasons: the economic crisis affected many countries at once, creating widespread hunger and
anger **[2]**; liberal and nationalist grievances had built up across Europe since 1815 **[2]**; news of the
February revolution in Paris spread quickly and encouraged risings elsewhere, while rulers who gave way
encouraged others **[2]**. **[6]**
(c) **Agree:** aims differed: in France the form of government and workers' demands; in Italy and Germany
unity; in the Austrian Empire rights for nationalities, which clashed with each other; outcomes differed,
as France changed regime while elsewhere old rulers returned. **Disagree:** most shared liberal demands for
constitutions, parliaments and a free press; most were led by the middle classes and students with
workers' support; most were affected by the economic crisis; most failed for similar reasons, such as
divisions and loyal armies. **Judgement:** the revolutions shared causes and methods but differed in their
national aims, which helps explain why they could not support each other. **[10]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing events in part (b) instead of explaining why.
- Using Cavour and Bismarck as evidence in a question about 1848–49.
- Giving one side only in part (c), or ending with no judgement.
