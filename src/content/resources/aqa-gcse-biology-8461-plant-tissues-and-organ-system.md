A plant's roots, stem and leaves form an **organ system that transports substances around the plant**: water and
mineral ions move up in the xylem, and dissolved sugars move in the phloem. The AQA GCSE Biology (8461)
specification expects you to explain how each leaf tissue's structure suits its function, describe transpiration
and translocation, explain how four environmental factors change the rate of transpiration, and calculate a rate
of transpiration from experimental data.

This guide teaches **4.2.3.1 Plant tissues** and **4.2.3.2 Plant organ system** of Topic 4.2 Organisation
(specification Version 1.0, for teaching from September 2016 and exams from 2018). Both are on Paper 1 at
Foundation and Higher Tier, and none of the content is (HT only) or (biology only). The specification links three
practical activities to these sections: drawing a transverse section of a leaf, measuring the rate of transpiration
by the uptake of water, and investigating the distribution of stomata and guard cells.

## Why is the leaf called an organ?

An organ is a group of tissues working together to do a particular job. The leaf contains several tissues that
together carry out photosynthesis and gas exchange. AQA limits the knowledge of leaf structure to the **epidermis**,
**palisade mesophyll**, **spongy mesophyll**, **xylem**, **phloem**, and **guard cells surrounding stomata**.

## How is each plant tissue adapted to its function?

| Tissue | Where | Structure related to function |
|---|---|---|
| Upper epidermis | Top surface of the leaf | Cells are thin and transparent, letting light through to the cells below; covered by a waxy cuticle that reduces water loss |
| Palisade mesophyll | Just below the upper epidermis | Tall, tightly packed cells with many chloroplasts, near the top where light is brightest, for photosynthesis |
| Spongy mesophyll | Below the palisade layer | Loosely packed cells with large air spaces, so carbon dioxide and oxygen can diffuse through the leaf |
| Lower epidermis | Bottom surface | Contains most of the stomata, each surrounded by two guard cells |
| Xylem | In the veins (vascular bundles) | Hollow tubes strengthened by lignin; carry water and mineral ions up from the roots |
| Phloem | In the veins, next to the xylem | Tubes of elongated cells with pores in the end walls; carry dissolved sugars |
| Meristem | Growing tips of shoots and roots | Cells divide and can differentiate into many types of plant cell |

**Meristem tissue** is the plant's source of new cells. It links to the stem cells content in Topic 1: meristem
cells can differentiate into any type of plant cell throughout the plant's life.

### How do you draw a transverse section of a leaf?

Use a prepared slide under a light microscope at low power first. Draw with a sharp pencil, using clear continuous
lines and no shading. Draw tissue layers in the correct order and proportion, and label upper epidermis, palisade
mesophyll, spongy mesophyll, a vein (xylem and phloem), lower epidermis and a stoma with its guard cells. Give the
magnification used.

## How are root hair cells adapted?

Root hair cells have a long, thin extension that gives a **large surface area** for absorption. They take up:

- **water by osmosis**, because the soil water is more dilute than the cell sap;
- **mineral ions by active transport**, because the ions are usually more concentrated inside the root hair cell
  than in the soil. Active transport needs energy from respiration, so these cells contain many mitochondria.

## How do xylem and phloem compare?

| | Xylem | Phloem |
|---|---|---|
| Transports | Water and mineral ions | Dissolved sugars |
| Direction | From the roots to the stems and leaves (upwards) | From the leaves to the rest of the plant, for immediate use or storage (both directions) |
| Structure | Hollow tubes strengthened by lignin | Tubes of elongated cells; cell sap moves from one cell to the next through pores in the end walls |
| Name of the process | Transpiration stream | Translocation |

AQA states that the detailed structure of phloem and the mechanism of transport are **not required**. Stick to the
description in the table.

## What is transpiration?

**Transpiration** is the loss of water vapour from the leaves. Water evaporates from the mesophyll cells into the
air spaces and diffuses out through the stomata. This loss draws more water up the xylem from the roots, forming
the **transpiration stream**, which also carries mineral ions.

### What do stomata and guard cells do?

Stomata are pores, mostly in the lower epidermis. Each is surrounded by two **guard cells**, which change shape to
open or close the pore. The role of stomata and guard cells is to **control gas exchange and water loss**: open
stomata let carbon dioxide in for photosynthesis and oxygen out, but they also let water vapour escape. Having most
stomata on the shaded lower surface reduces water loss.

## What affects the rate of transpiration?

| Factor | Change | Effect on rate | Reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| Temperature | Increase | Increases | Water molecules have more kinetic energy, so evaporation and diffusion are faster |
| Humidity | Increase | Decreases | The air outside holds more water vapour, so the concentration gradient from leaf to air is smaller |
| Air movement | Increase | Increases | Moving air carries water vapour away from the leaf surface, keeping the gradient steep |
| Light intensity | Increase | Increases | Stomata open wider in light for photosynthesis, so more water vapour can diffuse out |

In a 6-mark answer, give the direction of the effect **and** the reason for each factor.

## How do you measure the rate of transpiration?

A **potometer** measures the rate of water **uptake** by a leafy shoot. Most of the water taken up is lost by
transpiration, so uptake is used as an estimate of the rate of transpiration.

1. Cut the shoot under water and fit it into the potometer under water, so no air enters the xylem.
2. Seal the joints so the apparatus is airtight and watertight.
3. Introduce an air bubble into the capillary tube.
4. Record the distance the bubble moves in a measured time.
5. Reset the bubble with the reservoir and repeat; calculate a mean.
6. Change one factor (for example air movement, using a fan at set distances) while keeping the others the same.

**Worked example 1.** The bubble moves 36 mm in 6 minutes.

- Rate = 36 / 6 = **6 mm per minute**.

**Worked example 2 (volume, MS 5c).** The capillary tube has a radius of 0.5 mm. Volume of water taken up =
pi x r squared x length = 3.14 x 0.5 x 0.5 x 36 = **28.3 mm cubed** (to 3 significant figures). Rate = 28.3 / 6 =
**4.71 mm cubed per minute**, about 4.7 mm cubed per minute.

## How do you investigate the distribution of stomata?

Paint clear nail varnish on the leaf surface, let it dry, lift it with clear tape and stick it onto a slide. Count
the stomata in the field of view at high power. Count several fields of view on each surface (sampling) and
calculate the mean.

**Worked example 3.** Five fields of view on a lower surface contain 14, 17, 12, 15 and 16 stomata. Mean =
74 / 5 = **14.8 stomata per field of view**. If the field of view has an area of 0.2 mm squared, the density is
14.8 / 0.2 = **74 stomata per mm squared**. The upper surface of the same leaf usually gives a much lower count.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why does a potometer not measure transpiration exactly?**
It measures water uptake. A small amount of the water taken up is used by the plant, for example in
photosynthesis, so uptake is only an estimate of the water lost.

**Is translocation the same as transpiration?**
No. Transpiration is the loss of water vapour from leaves, which pulls water up the xylem. Translocation is the
movement of dissolved sugars (food molecules) through the phloem.

**Why do root hair cells need mitochondria?**
They absorb mineral ions by active transport, against a concentration gradient. That needs energy from
respiration, which happens in mitochondria.

**Which tissue makes new cells in a plant?**
Meristem tissue at the growing tips of shoots and roots. Its cells divide and can differentiate into other types of
plant cell.

## Common mistakes

- Saying water enters root hair cells by active transport, or that mineral ions enter by osmosis.
- Saying phloem carries water, or that xylem carries sugars.
- Saying higher humidity increases the rate of transpiration (it decreases it).
- Explaining the light effect with "more photosynthesis uses water" instead of stomata opening.
- Dividing by the wrong quantity in a rate calculation, or leaving out the unit.
- Forgetting that transpiration is loss of water **vapour** by evaporation and diffusion.
