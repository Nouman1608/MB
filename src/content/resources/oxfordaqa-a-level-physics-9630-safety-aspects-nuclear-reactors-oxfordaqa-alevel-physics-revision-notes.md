## Condensed recall

**Fuel**
- Fresh fuel: enriched uranium (as uranium dioxide pellets in sealed tubes). Weakly radioactive, mainly α, stopped by the sealed tubes.
- Spent fuel: contains fission fragments (β⁻ and γ emitters) and heavy nuclei such as plutonium-239 (long-lived α emitters). Intensely radioactive and hot.

**Remote handling**
- Spent fuel is moved by machines operated from behind shielding, kept under water in cooling ponds.
- Water absorbs radiation and removes decay heat.
- γ intensity from a point source falls as 1/r², so distance greatly reduces exposure.

**Shielding**
- Steel pressure vessel plus thick concrete shield around the core.
- Main targets: neutrons and γ rays, the penetrating radiations.
- α and β are stopped near their source, but α emitters are dangerous if inhaled or swallowed.

**Emergency shut-down**
- Control rods fully inserted, falling under gravity if power fails; a neutron absorber can also be added to the coolant.
- Fission stops in seconds; decay heat does not. Cooling must continue for days.

**Waste**
- Produced as fission fragments, as heavy nuclei from neutron capture by uranium-238, and by neutron activation of structural materials.
- Low-level: drums, shallow burial. Intermediate-level: shielded, set in concrete. High-level: cooled in ponds for years, sealed in glass and steel, stored shielded; deep geological disposal planned.
- Short half-life waste decays quickly; long half-life waste remains hazardous for thousands of years.

**Risk and benefit**
- Risk combines probability and consequence.
- A judgement compares nuclear power with alternatives in context and reaches a conclusion.

## Building an extended answer

Six-mark questions on this subtopic reward linked points, not lists. For each safety measure, use the chain **hazard → measure → physics reason**:

| Hazard | Measure | Physics reason |
|---|---|---|
| Intense γ from spent fuel | Remote handling at a distance | γ intensity ∝ 1/r² |
| Neutrons and γ from the core | Steel vessel and concrete shield | Dense material absorbs γ; light nuclei slow neutrons for absorption |
| Runaway chain reaction | Rods that drop under gravity | Absorbing neutrons makes fewer than one per fission cause another |
| Decay heat after shut-down | Continued cooling | Fission products keep decaying |
| Long-lived waste | Deep, isolated storage | Activity falls only over many half-lives |

For a risk-benefit question, end with a sentence that starts "On balance…" and gives a reason.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Spent fuel | Fuel removed from a reactor after use, containing fission products |
| Remote handling | Moving radioactive material with machines controlled from a safe, shielded position |
| Shielding | Material placed between a source and people to absorb radiation |
| Emergency shut-down | Rapid full insertion of control rods (and absorber) to stop the chain reaction |
| Decay heat | Energy released by radioactive decay of fission products after fission has stopped |
| Neutron activation | Making a stable nucleus radioactive by neutron absorption |
| Vitrification | Sealing high-level waste in glass |
| Risk | The combination of the likelihood of harm and its severity |

## Exam traps

- Control rods "cool" the reactor: no, they absorb neutrons.
- Reactor safe once fission stops: no, decay heat continues.
- Fresh fuel described as highly radioactive.
- Shielding described as stopping α particles from the core.
- Advantages and disadvantages listed with no judgement.
- Inverse-square law applied to α or β particles.

## Quick self-test

1. Why is spent fuel more hazardous than fresh fuel?
2. Why is spent fuel stored under water?
3. What does the thick concrete around a reactor core absorb?
4. How is an emergency shut-down achieved?
5. Why must cooling continue after shut-down?
6. Give three ways in which a reactor produces radioactive waste.
7. Give an example of low-level waste and how it is disposed of.
8. The γ intensity from a source is 8000 units at 0.40 m. Find the intensity at 2.0 m.
9. A waste sample has activity 2.0 × 10¹⁶ Bq and deposits 0.70 MeV per decay. Find the heating power.
10. Strontium-90 has a half-life of 28.8 years. What fraction remains after 57.6 years?
11. Define risk.
12. Give two benefits and two risks of nuclear power.

### Answers

1. It contains fission fragments and heavy nuclei that emit intense β and γ radiation and generate heat; fresh fuel is a weak α emitter.
2. Water absorbs the radiation, shielding workers, and carries away the decay heat.
3. Neutrons and γ rays.
4. Control rods are inserted fully (falling under gravity if power fails), and a neutron absorber can be added to the coolant.
5. Fission products keep decaying and releasing energy, which could damage the fuel if not removed.
6. Fission fragments; heavy nuclei such as plutonium-239 from neutron capture by uranium-238; neutron activation of the structure and coolant.
7. Protective clothing or tools; sealed in drums and buried at shallow depth.
8. 8000 × (0.40/2.0)² = 320 units.
9. 2.0 × 10¹⁶ × 0.70 × 1.60 × 10⁻¹³ = 2.2 × 10³ W.
10. Two half-lives, so ¼.
11. The likelihood of a harmful event combined with how severe its consequences would be.
12. Benefits: large energy from small fuel mass; no carbon dioxide during operation; steady base-load output. Risks: rare severe accidents; long-lived waste; high decommissioning cost.
