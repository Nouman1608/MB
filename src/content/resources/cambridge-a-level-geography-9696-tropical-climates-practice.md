## Section A

**1.** Define the term "intertropical convergence zone". **[2]**

**2.** Explain why the trade winds in the northern hemisphere blow from the north-east. **[2]**

**3.** Outline how subtropical anticyclones form. **[3]**

**4.** The table shows monthly rainfall at the fictional station of Rimba, at 8°S.

| Month | J | F | M | A | M | J | J | A | S | O | N | D |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Rainfall (mm) | 280 | 250 | 220 | 120 | 40 | 10 | 5 | 10 | 30 | 80 | 160 | 240 |

**(a)** Calculate the percentage of the annual rainfall that falls in the three wettest months. Show your working. **[3]**

**(b)** Describe the seasonal pattern of rainfall at Rimba. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain the pattern you have described. **[3]**

**5.** The table shows mean daily temperatures at the fictional savanna station of Kalo in two seasons.

| Season | Mean daily maximum (°C) | Mean daily minimum (°C) |
|---|---|---|
| Dry season | 36 | 17 |
| Wet season | 30 | 22 |

**(a)** Calculate the mean diurnal range in each season. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain the difference between the two diurnal ranges. **[3]**

**6.** Suggest why the ITCZ lies slightly north of the equator when averaged over a year. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** Explain how the South Asian monsoon circulation differs between summer and winter, including the roles of the jet stream and relief. **[6]**

**8.** Explain the global distribution of humid tropical and seasonally humid tropical climates. **[5]**

**9.** Compare the annual and diurnal variations in temperature in humid tropical and seasonally humid tropical environments. **[6]**

**10.** "The movement of the ITCZ is the main cause of seasonal rainfall in tropical environments." How far do you agree? **[20]**

## Answers

**1.** A low-pressure belt near the equator [1] where the north-east and south-east trade winds converge and air is forced to rise, giving convective rain [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Air flows from the subtropical high towards the low pressure of the ITCZ, so it moves towards the equator from the north [1]. The Coriolis effect deflects it to the right in the northern hemisphere, so it arrives from the north-east [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Air rising at the ITCZ flows polewards at high altitude [1]. It cools by radiation and converges, sinking near 30°N and 30°S [1]. Sinking air warms adiabatically and dries, so high pressure and clear skies form at the surface [1]. **[3]**

**4. (a)** Annual total = 1 445 mm [1]. Three wettest months: January 280 + February 250 + December 240 = 770 mm [1]. 770 ÷ 1 445 × 100 = 53.3% [1].

**(b)** A single wet season from about November to March, peaking in January at 280 mm [1], and a dry season from May to September, with a minimum of 5 mm in July [1].

**(c)** Rimba is in the southern hemisphere, so the overhead sun and the ITCZ reach it in the southern summer, December to February [1]. Rising air and convection at the ITCZ give heavy rain [1]. In the southern winter the ITCZ moves north, and the subtropical high extends over Rimba, so sinking air brings a dry season [1]. **[8]**

**5. (a)** Dry season: 36 − 17 = 19 °C [1]. Wet season: 30 − 22 = 8 °C [1].

**(b)** In the dry season, clear skies allow strong daytime heating, giving high maxima [1]. At night, clear skies and dry air allow rapid loss of longwave radiation, so minima are low [1]. In the wet season, cloud reduces daytime heating and traps heat at night, and moist ground uses energy for evaporation, so the range is smaller [1]. **[5]**

**6.** The northern hemisphere has more land than the southern [1], and land heats more strongly than ocean, so the zone of greatest heating, and the ITCZ, is pulled northwards on average [1]. **[2]**

**7.** In summer the land, and the air above the Tibetan Plateau, heat strongly, forming low pressure over northern India [1]. The ITCZ is pulled far north, and moist south-westerly winds blow onshore from the Indian Ocean [1]. The subtropical westerly jet stream moves north of the Himalayas, allowing the monsoon circulation to develop [1]. Relief forces moist air to rise over the Western Ghats and Himalayas, giving heavy orographic rain [1]. In winter the land is cooler than the sea, high pressure forms over central Asia [1], and dry north-easterly winds blow offshore, while the jet returns south of the Himalayas [1]. **[6]**

**8.** Humid tropical climates lie within about 5-10° of the equator [1], where the ITCZ is present for most of the year, so rain falls in every month [1]. Examples include the Amazon and Congo basins and Indonesia [1]. Seasonally humid climates lie further from the equator, roughly 5-20° north and south [1], where the ITCZ arrives only in the high-sun season and the subtropical high dominates the rest of the year [1]. **[5]**

**9.** Both have high temperatures all year, with no month below 18 °C [1]. The annual range is very small in humid tropical areas, often 1-3 °C, whereas it is larger in seasonally humid areas [1], where the hottest month comes just before the rains [1]. In humid tropical areas the diurnal range is larger than the annual range [1], whereas in seasonally humid areas the diurnal range itself varies by season [1], being greatest in the clear dry season and smallest in the cloudy wet season [1]. **[6]**

**10.** The real exam marks this essay with levels of response, not point by point. The points below are indicative creditworthy content.

*Introduction:* defines the ITCZ [1] and seasonal rainfall as rainfall concentrated in part of the year [1].

*Agree:* the ITCZ follows the overhead sun, so a place receives rain when the ITCZ is over it [1]. Savanna areas have a wet season under the ITCZ [1] and a dry season under the subtropical high [1]. Near the equator, two passes of the ITCZ give two rainfall peaks [1]. The length of the wet season shortens with distance from the equator, matching the ITCZ's time overhead [1].

*Disagree:* monsoons depend on land-sea heating contrasts [1] and on changes in the jet stream, not the ITCZ alone [1]. Relief controls the amount of rain, for example orographic rain on the Western Ghats [1]. Ocean currents affect rainfall: cold currents and upwelling make some tropical coasts dry [1]. Subtropical highs, not the ITCZ, explain the length and dryness of the dry season [1]. Local convection gives rain in humid tropical areas whatever the ITCZ position [1].

*Interaction:* the monsoon can be seen as an intensified, land-driven shift of the ITCZ [1], so the factors are linked rather than separate [1]. Year-to-year variation in the ITCZ's movement explains droughts and late onsets in savanna areas [1].

*Use of examples:* named regions, such as South Asia, West Africa or northern Australia [1].

*Judgement:* the ITCZ is the main framework for seasonal rainfall [1], but monsoons, relief and currents modify it strongly [1], with a final view on "main cause" [1]. **[20]**

*Examiner insight:* top answers treat the monsoon as both evidence for and a qualification of the ITCZ explanation, and vary their judgement by region.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Forgetting that the southern hemisphere's wet season is December to February.
- Adding up the wrong three months, or not finding the annual total first.
- Explaining diurnal range by temperature alone, without cloud, humidity and radiation.
- Monsoon answers without the ITCZ shift or the wind reversal.
- Essays that describe the ITCZ without weighing other factors.
