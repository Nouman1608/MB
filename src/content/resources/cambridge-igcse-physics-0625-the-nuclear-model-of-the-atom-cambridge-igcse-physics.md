## The structure of the atom

An atom has a tiny, **positively charged nucleus** at its centre, containing almost all of its mass, with **negatively charged electrons** in orbit around it. Most of the atom is empty space.

| Particle | Where | Relative charge | Relative mass |
|---|---|---|---|
| Proton | Nucleus | +1 | about 1 |
| Neutron | Nucleus | 0 | about 1 |
| Electron | Orbiting the nucleus | −1 | very small (about 1/2000) |

The syllabus requires the relative **charges**. The relative masses explain why A, which counts only protons and neutrons, gives the mass of the nucleus.

A neutral atom has **equal numbers of protons and electrons**, so their charges cancel.

## Ions

Atoms become ions by **losing or gaining electrons**; the nucleus never changes when an ion forms.

- Losing electrons leaves more protons than electrons: a **positive ion**. Losing 2 electrons gives a charge of +2.
- Gaining electrons gives more electrons than protons: a **negative ion**.

### Worked example 1

State the numbers of protons, neutrons and electrons in (a) a neutral atom of strontium-90, ⁹⁰₃₈Sr, and (b) the ion of sulfur ³²₁₆S that has a charge of −2.

```
(a) protons = Z = 38
    neutrons = A − Z = 90 − 38 = 52
    electrons = 38 (neutral atom)
(b) protons = 16;  neutrons = 32 − 16 = 16
    charge −2 means 2 extra electrons: 16 + 2 = 18 electrons
```

## Nuclide notation

A nucleus is written as ᴬ_Z X, printed here with a superscript and a subscript, for example ²³₁₁Na:

- **A, the nucleon number (mass number)**, at the top: the number of protons **plus** neutrons.
- **Z, the proton number (atomic number)**, at the bottom: the number of protons.
- **X**, the chemical symbol. Z alone fixes the element: every nucleus with Z = 11 is sodium.
- Number of neutrons = **A − Z**.

**Extended only.** The **relative charge** of a nucleus equals **Z** (each proton contributes +1, neutrons nothing). The **relative mass** of a nucleus equals **A** (each nucleon has a relative mass of about 1). So the nucleus ⁵⁶₂₆Fe has a relative charge of +26 and a relative mass of 56.

## Isotopes

**Isotopes** are atoms of the same element (same proton number) with **different numbers of neutrons** (different nucleon numbers). An element may have more than one isotope. Hydrogen has three: ¹₁H (no neutrons), ²₁H (one neutron) and ³₁H (two neutrons). Carbon-12, ¹²₆C, and carbon-14, ¹⁴₆C, are isotopes of carbon with 6 and 8 neutrons.

Isotopes of one element have the same number of protons and, as neutral atoms, the same number of electrons; only the neutron number differs. A question that gives two nuclides with **different Z** is describing two different elements, never isotopes.

## Alpha-particle scattering (Extended only)

In the scattering experiment, a narrow beam of alpha particles (positively charged) is fired at a very thin sheet of metal foil, such as gold, in a vacuum. A detector is moved around the foil to count the particles scattered at each angle. Each observation supports one part of the nuclear model:

| Observation | What it shows |
|---|---|
| Almost all alpha particles pass straight through, or are deflected by only a small angle | The atom is **mostly empty space**, with a **very small** nucleus |
| A small number are deflected through large angles | The nucleus is **positively charged**: it repels the positive alpha particles that pass close to it |
| A very few bounce back towards the source | The nucleus contains **most of the mass** of the atom (and its charge is concentrated): only a massive, concentrated nucleus can turn a fast alpha particle back |

The marks are for linking **each observation to its own conclusion**. "The alpha particles were scattered, which shows there is a nucleus" earns far less than three separate links. Note why the foil must be very thin and the experiment done in a vacuum: so that alpha particles are not absorbed by the foil or stopped by air before being detected.

### Annotated model answer (Extended)

*Explain how the alpha-scattering experiment provides evidence that the nucleus is positively charged. [2]*

> "Alpha particles are positively charged. Some were deflected through large angles as they passed near a nucleus, which means they were pushed away by a strong repulsive force, so the nucleus must carry a positive charge like the alpha particle."

**Why this scores:** it states the alpha particle's charge, gives the observation (large-angle deflection) and links repulsion to like charges. Saying only "the nucleus repels alpha particles" loses the first point.

## Fission and fusion (Extended only)

**Nuclear fission** is the **splitting** of a large nucleus into two smaller nuclei, usually after it absorbs a neutron; two or three neutrons are released as well. **Nuclear fusion** is the **joining** of two light nuclei to form a heavier nucleus.

In both, the total mass of the products is slightly **less** than the total mass before. This lost mass is released as **energy**, mostly as kinetic energy of the products. The syllabus asks for this qualitatively, with no numerical values.

### Worked example 2 (Extended)

Complete and check these equations.

```
Fission:  ²³⁵₉₂U + ¹₀n → ¹⁴⁰₅₄Xe + ⁹⁴₃₈Sr + 2 ¹₀n
  top:    235 + 1 = 236      140 + 94 + 2 = 236
  bottom:  92 + 0 = 92        54 + 38 + 0 = 92

Fusion:   ²₁H + ³₁H → ⁴₂He + ¹₀n
  top:    2 + 3 = 5          4 + 1 = 5
  bottom: 1 + 1 = 2          2 + 0 = 2
```

If a question asks how many neutrons are released, subtract the product nucleon numbers from the total on the left: 236 − 140 − 94 = 2.

## Common mistakes

- Putting Z on top and A underneath.
- Giving the neutron number as A, or the electron number as A − Z.
- Saying positive ions gain protons.
- Defining isotopes by different numbers of protons or electrons.
- Linking all three alpha-scattering conclusions to one observation.
- Saying mass "increases" in fission or fusion, or giving it in numbers.

## Exam technique

- **Define:** use the syllabus terms, for example "nucleon number: the number of protons and neutrons in a nucleus".
- **Calculate** the number of neutrons: show A − Z.
- **State** the composition of a nucleus: give numbers for both protons and neutrons.
- **Describe** (Extended): for alpha scattering, write each observation followed by "which shows...".
- **Complete an equation** (Extended): balance the top row and the bottom row separately and show the check.
