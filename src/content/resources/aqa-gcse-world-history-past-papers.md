This page is a guide to AQA GCSE History (8145) past papers for the June 2026, June 2025 and June 2024 series. AQA's own title for this qualification is simply History; we list it here under World History. The page gives grade thresholds taken from AQA's grade-boundary documents, and a short account of what examiners reported, written by us from AQA's Reports on the Examination. We do not host any papers. Every paper, mark scheme and report is on AQA's own site, linked below.

## Where to get the official papers

- [AQA GCSE History (8145) specification](https://www.aqa.org.uk/subjects/history/gcse/history-8145/specification)
- [AQA GCSE History (8145) assessment resources](https://www.aqa.org.uk/subjects/history/gcse/history-8145/assessment-resources)
- [AQA grade boundaries](https://www.aqa.org.uk/exams-administration/results-days/grade-boundaries) and the [grade boundaries archive](https://www.aqa.org.uk/exams-administration/results-days/grade-boundaries/archive)

The assessment resources page lists question papers, mark schemes and reports on the examination for each series. For History, AQA publishes a separate report for each section of each paper, with comments by option. Use the three documents together. Sit a paper first, under timed conditions. Mark it strictly with the mark scheme, which shows what each mark needs. Then read the report for that section: it explains why answers that looked reasonable still lost marks. The notes for each series below are a quick way into those reports.

Check which specification and which options a paper was set on. 8145 is a linear, untiered qualification, first taught from September 2016 and first examined in June 2018. It has two written papers, each 2 hours, 84 marks (including 4 marks for spelling, punctuation and grammar) and 50% of the GCSE, 168 marks in all. Paper 1 (8145/1) Understanding the modern world has Section A, a period study, and Section B, a wider world depth study. Paper 2 (8145/2) Shaping the nation has Section A, a thematic study, and Section B, a British depth study that includes the historic environment. Candidates study one option in each section, declared at entry, so you should practise the papers for the options your school teaches. Every series on this page was set on this specification.

## How the thresholds are shown

Because candidates enter different option combinations, AQA publishes a separate subject boundary for each combination, identified by a code such as 8145AA, and gives no single boundary for the whole qualification. AQA's document lists 153 combinations for June 2026, 153 for June 2025 and 156 for June 2024. Reproducing every row would not be readable, so each table below gives the lowest and the highest boundary found anywhere in AQA's list for each grade. Your own boundary is one row of the document, so always check the exact row for your combination in AQA's PDF. Every row is out of 168 marks.

## June 2025

The four reports for June 2025 describe a strong group of candidates in every section, with good contextual knowledge. The same weaknesses came back across the options: answers that were too long for the marks available, narrative where explanation was needed, and confusion between what different question types ask for. The reports comment option by option; the points below are the ones that applied across sections.

**Where marks were lost**

- Paper 1 Section A, period studies: on the three interpretation questions, copying quotations from the interpretations instead of using them; stating differences rather than explaining why they exist; discussing provenance where the question asked for something else; and arguing for only one interpretation, which limits the mark. Many candidates worked line by line rather than judging each interpretation's overall argument. Weaker long answers narrated events with no line of argument.
- Paper 1 Section B, wider world depth studies: explanations that drifted into narrative after the focus of the question, mistakes in chronology, and cause questions answered by listing many factors superficially instead of developing the one named. In the source questions, provenance was often generalised, and answers did not address the second half of the question, such as how an event affected relations between the superpowers. Candidates should deal with the named event and one other in detail.
- Paper 2 Section A, thematic studies: the significance of a named individual described without placing it in a wider development; confusion between public health, disease, treatment and surgery, so that accurate material did not answer the question; and factors such as religion or communication mentioned in a sentence rather than explained over time. Famous names were told as stories instead of having their impact weighed.
- Paper 2 Section B, British depth studies: candidates saying they agreed with an interpretation "from my own knowledge" without supplying the knowledge, marking an interpretation down for what it left out, and some uncertainty over chronology or between similarly named figures. A higher number than before did not attempt one of the questions in one option. The strongest answers explained why an event mattered in its broader context rather than describing it.

## June 2024

The June 2024 reports repeat much of the advice for 2025. The interpretation questions in Paper 1 Section A again showed over-reliance on quotation and confusion over the three different tasks. Provenance in the source questions of Section B was thin, and Paper 2 reports point to the difference between content and provenance when judging usefulness.

**Where marks were lost**

- Paper 1 Section A, period studies: copying from interpretations; focusing on how they differ instead of why; a line-by-line approach; and, for the problems question, answers that did not keep to the two problems asked for or that mixed up groups of settlers. Long answers on how settlers survived were descriptive, with technology and legislation covered thinly and no clear argument.
- Paper 1 Section B, wider world depth studies: Level 3 provenance often missing, so source answers could not reach the top level; narratives of a campaign replacing an answer to the question set; League of Nations answers resting on unfocused facts; the impact of the peace terms on Germany limited to the armed forces; and chronology of the Cuban crisis confused. Provenance of a source was often reduced to a general comment about propaganda, with no link to the effect on the Cold War.
- Paper 2 Section A, thematic studies: candidates leaning on what a source says about welfare reform rather than on who wrote it and when; confusing the cause of a disease with how it spreads; surgery or treatments discussed where disease causation was asked about; and Level 1 answers that mentioned supernatural explanations or science in general without chronological understanding.
- Paper 2 Section B, British depth studies: interpretations asserted to be right without connecting them to specific knowledge; some confusion between archbishops and between battles; statutes listed instead of analysed until the higher levels; and a tendency to write British or Britain where England was meant.

## Frequently asked questions

**Where can I find AQA GCSE History 8145 past papers?**
AQA lists question papers, mark schemes and reports on the examination on the 8145 assessment resources page, with a separate report for each section of each paper. Grade boundaries are on its grade boundaries page and archive.

**Why is there no single grade boundary for 8145?**
AQA publishes a boundary for each combination of options, so the boundary that applies to you depends on the period study, depth studies and thematic study you took. The tables above show only the lowest and highest.

**Do the three interpretation questions ask for the same thing?**
No. The reports say candidates confuse them. One asks what is different, one asks why the interpretations differ, and one asks which is more convincing, which needs contextual knowledge and a view on both interpretations.

**Should I write more to earn more marks?**
The reports say answers that are too long for the marks available are a recurring problem. Focus on the question, develop the point asked for and move on.

**Can grade thresholds tell me what mark I need next time?**
No. Thresholds change every series and should only be used as a rough guide for the series they belong to, never to predict future ones.
