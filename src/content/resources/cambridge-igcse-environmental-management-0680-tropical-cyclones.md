Sub-topic 6.2 of Cambridge IGCSE Environmental Management (0680, 2027-2029) has four statements: the location and conditions needed for a tropical cyclone to form (6.2.1), its impacts on people and the environment (6.2.2), strategies for managing those impacts before, during and after the event (6.2.3), and a discussion of their benefits and limitations (6.2.4). The formation conditions are given as exact numbers in the syllabus, so they are easy marks if you learn them precisely. For a comparison with the other hazards, see the [Natural hazards study guide](/resources/cambridge-igcse-environmental-management-natural-hazards-study-guide/).

## One storm, three names (6.2.1 a)

The syllabus says "tropical cyclones: storms, hurricanes and typhoons". These are the same kind of weather system with different regional names. **Hurricane** is used over the North Atlantic and the north-east Pacific, **typhoon** over the north-west Pacific, and **cyclone** over the Indian Ocean and South Pacific. Weaker systems are called tropical storms. If a question uses any of these names, the same formation conditions and management strategies apply.

## Where and why they form (6.2.1 b to d)

Learn the three numerical conditions exactly as the syllabus gives them:

| Condition | Syllabus value | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Latitude | Between 5° and 30° north or south of the Equator | Closer than 5° the Earth's rotation is too weak to make the air spin; beyond about 30° the sea is usually too cool |
| Ocean temperature | At least 27 °C | Warm water evaporates rapidly, supplying the heat and moisture that power the storm |
| Ocean depth | At least 60 m | A deep layer of warm water stops the storm stirring up cold water from below and cutting off its own energy supply |

**How the storm grows.** Warm, moist air rises from the sea surface, creating very low pressure. As the air rises it cools and the water vapour condenses into towering clouds, releasing heat that warms the air and makes it rise faster. More air is drawn in across the sea surface, and the Earth's rotation makes it spiral. A calm, cloud-free **eye** forms at the centre, surrounded by a wall of the strongest winds and heaviest rain.

**How the storm dies.** Once the cyclone moves over land or cooler water, it loses its supply of warm, moist air. Friction with the land also slows the winds. This explains why coastal areas suffer most and why damage falls inland, though heavy rain can still cause floods far from the coast.

## Impacts on people and the environment (6.2.2)

The syllabus lists ten impacts. The three physical processes behind them are **strong winds**, **heavy rainfall** and a **storm surge** (a rise in sea level as wind pushes water onshore and low pressure lets the sea surface bulge upward).

- **Damage to buildings and infrastructure**: winds tear off roofs and bring down power lines and trees; the surge washes away coastal roads and homes.
- **Loss of crops**: wind flattens fields; flooding by sea water ruins harvests and leaves salt in the soil.
- **Livestock and habitats**: animals drown; mangroves, coral reefs and coastal forest are damaged.
- **Evacuation of people and livestock**: large numbers move inland or to shelters.
- **Flooding**: from heavy rain on land and the storm surge at the coast.
- **Landslides**: saturated soil on steep slopes gives way.
- **Contamination of drinking-water supplies**: sea water and sewage enter wells and reservoirs.
- **Water-related disease**: dirty water spreads cholera and diarrhoea; standing water lets mosquitoes breed.
- **Financial losses**: rebuilding, lost harvests, closed businesses and lost tourism.
- **Human health**: injuries from flying debris, drowning, disease and stress.

Make the chains clear. For example: storm surge → sea water floods farmland → salt left in the soil → crops fail for more than one season → food shortages and lost income.

## Managing the impacts (6.2.3)

The syllabus lists seven strategies. Note that, unlike the earthquake and flood lists, **land use zoning is not one of them** for cyclones.

**Before the cyclone**
- **Monitoring and warning**: satellites, weather radar and aircraft track the storm, and forecasters predict its path, so warnings can be issued days ahead through radio, phone messages and sirens.
- **Structure of buildings**: roofs strapped to walls, shutters over windows, raised floors above surge level, and stronger frames.
- **Disaster preparation**: emergency plans, drills, stores of food, water and medicine, and trained rescue teams.

**During the cyclone**
- **Evacuation**: moving people and livestock inland or to higher ground before the storm arrives.
- **Shelters**: strong, raised buildings where people can stay during the storm. Bangladesh, for example, has built many raised cyclone shelters along its low-lying coast.

**After the cyclone**
- **Rebuilding of damaged areas**, ideally to stronger standards.
- **International aid**: money, food, temporary housing, water purification and medical teams from other countries and organisations.

## Discussing the strategies (6.2.4)

| Strategy | Benefit | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Monitoring and warning | Days of warning; lives saved if people act | Path and strength can change; warnings may not reach remote areas; people may ignore them after false alarms |
| Structure of buildings | Fewer homes destroyed; fewer injuries | Costly for poor households; older buildings remain weak |
| Disaster preparation | Fast, organised response | Needs funding and regular drills to stay effective |
| Evacuation | Removes people from surge and wind | Traffic jams; people may stay to guard property or livestock; the poorest may have no transport |
| Shelters | Safe refuge near homes | Construction cost; overcrowding and poor sanitation |
| Rebuilding | Chance to build back more strongly | Slow; expensive; may repeat old weaknesses if rushed |
| International aid | Resources beyond local capacity | Can arrive late, miss remote areas or create dependency |

A strong conclusion notes that strategies depend on each other: a warning only saves lives if people have a plan, transport and a shelter to go to.

## Worked example 1: can a cyclone form here?

The data are invented. Which ocean location could produce a tropical cyclone?

| Location | Latitude | Sea temperature | Depth of warm water |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | 2° N | 29 °C | 80 m |
| B | 15° S | 28 °C | 90 m |
| C | 22° N | 25 °C | 70 m |
| D | 18° N | 28 °C | 40 m |

- A: too close to the Equator (less than 5°), so the air will not spin. No.
- B: between 5° and 30°, at least 27 °C and at least 60 m. **Yes.**
- C: sea temperature below 27 °C. No.
- D: warm layer shallower than 60 m. No.

Only location B meets all three conditions. In the exam, state the condition each location fails, using the syllabus number.

## Worked example 2: annotated "discuss" answer

**Question:** Discuss the benefits and limitations of evacuation as a strategy for managing the impacts of a tropical cyclone. [4]

**Model answer:** Evacuation moves people away from the coast before the storm surge arrives, which is often the greatest threat to life [1]. Because cyclones are tracked days ahead, there is usually time to move people and livestock [1]. However, poorer families may have no transport, and roads can become jammed [1]. Some people refuse to leave because they fear their homes will be looted or their animals lost [1]. Evacuation works best when shelters and transport are planned in advance.

## Common mistakes

- Giving 26.5 °C or "warm water" instead of the syllabus value, at least 27 °C.
- Forgetting the 60 m depth condition.
- Saying cyclones form on the Equator.
- Learning the cyclone strategies as a copy of the earthquake list; land use zoning is named for earthquakes and floods but not for cyclones, so spend your revision time on the seven cyclone strategies.
- Writing "wind damage" for every impact instead of linking each to wind, rain or storm surge.

## Exam technique

- **State** the formation conditions with the syllabus numbers and units: 5° to 30°, at least 27 °C, at least 60 m.
- **Explain** an impact: link it to wind, rain or storm surge, then to people or the environment.
- **Discuss**: give a benefit and a limitation for each strategy and show how they depend on each other.
- **Using the source**: quote figures such as wind speed or distance flooded.
