## What cracking is

**Cracking** is the breaking of C–C bonds in long-chain alkanes to form shorter-chain alkanes and alkenes. It is a **chemical** change, unlike fractional distillation, which is physical. The products are always a mixture of an alkane and at least one alkene, because the original molecule is saturated and has to lose hydrogen from somewhere: the hydrogen atoms end up distributed so that one product has a C=C double bond.

A general pattern:

```
long-chain alkane → shorter alkane + alkene (+ possibly more alkene)
```

### Writing and checking cracking equations

An equation must balance for carbon and for hydrogen.

```
C₁₀H₂₂ → C₈H₁₈ + C₂H₄
C₁₆H₃₄ → C₈H₁₈ + 2C₄H₈
C₁₄H₃₀ → C₁₀H₂₂ + 2C₂H₄
```

To check: carbons on the left equal carbons on the right (10 = 8 + 2; 16 = 8 + 8; 14 = 10 + 4). For hydrogen: 22 = 18 + 4; 34 = 18 + 16; 30 = 22 + 8.

### Worked example 1: find the missing product

Dodecane, C₁₂H₂₆, is cracked to give C₄H₁₀ and one other product. Deduce the formula of the other product.

- Carbon: 12 – 4 = 8.
- Hydrogen: 26 – 10 = 16.
- The other product is C₈H₁₆, an alkene (CₙH₂ₙ).

### Worked example 2: balance with a coefficient

Hexadecane is cracked to give one molecule of octane (C₈H₁₈) and propene (C₃H₆) and another alkene. Write the equation.

- Carbons left after octane: 16 – 8 = 8. Propene uses 3, leaving 5. Hydrogen left: 34 – 18 = 16, minus 6 for propene = 10.
- The remaining product is C₅H₁₀ (pentene).
- Equation: C₁₆H₃₄ → C₈H₁₈ + C₃H₆ + C₅H₁₀.

## Thermal cracking

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| Conditions | High pressure (about 7000 kPa) and high temperature (about 700 to 1200 K) |
| Catalyst | None |
| Main products | A high percentage of alkenes, especially short-chain alkenes such as ethene |
| Uses of products | Alkenes are the feedstock for polymers (poly(ethene)) and many other chemicals |

The mechanism is not required. At a high temperature, C–C bonds break and the large molecules fall apart into a mixture, rich in alkenes. The high pressure is used to keep the hydrocarbons in the reactor as a dense gas or liquid.

## Catalytic cracking

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| Conditions | Slight pressure, high temperature (about 720 K, or 450 °C) |
| Catalyst | A zeolite (a crystalline aluminosilicate with a large surface area) |
| Main products | Branched and cyclic alkanes, and aromatic hydrocarbons (such as benzene), plus alkenes |
| Uses of products | Motor fuels (petrol) and aromatic hydrocarbons for the chemical industry |

The catalyst allows the reaction to run at a lower temperature and a much lower pressure than thermal cracking. This saves energy. Carbon (coke) builds up on the catalyst surface, so it is burned off to regenerate it.

### Comparison

| | Thermal cracking | Catalytic cracking |
|---|---|---|
| Temperature | Very high | High (lower than thermal) |
| Pressure | High | Slight |
| Catalyst | None | Zeolite |
| Main products | High % alkenes | Motor fuels and aromatics |
| Energy cost | Higher | Lower |

## The economic reasons for cracking

The specification asks you to explain the economic reasons, so give the reasoning clearly.

1. **Supply and demand.** Crude oil contains a larger proportion of long-chain hydrocarbons than the market needs, and there is more demand for short-chain hydrocarbons such as petrol. Cracking turns the surplus, less valuable fractions into smaller, more valuable molecules.
2. **Alkenes are valuable.** The alkenes produced are important raw materials (feedstock) for making plastics and other chemicals. Fractional distillation of crude oil does not provide many alkenes.
3. **Better fuel.** Catalytic cracking forms branched and aromatic hydrocarbons that make better motor fuels.
4. **Lower costs with a catalyst.** Catalytic cracking uses a lower temperature and pressure than thermal cracking, so the energy costs and plant costs are lower.

## Calculations

### Worked example 3: mass and volume of a product

100 g of decane (Mᵣ = 142.0) is cracked completely according to C₁₀H₂₂ → C₈H₁₈ + C₂H₄. Calculate the mass and the volume of ethene formed (molar gas volume 24.0 dm³ mol⁻¹ at room temperature and pressure).

```
n(C₁₀H₂₂) = 100 ÷ 142.0 = 0.704 mol
n(C₂H₄) = 0.704 mol (1 : 1)
mass = 0.704 × 28.0 = 19.7 g
volume = 0.704 × 24.0 = 16.9 dm³
```

### Worked example 4: atom economy

Calculate the atom economy for making ethene in the reaction C₁₀H₂₂ → C₈H₁₈ + C₂H₄.

```
atom economy = Mᵣ of ethene ÷ total Mᵣ of products × 100 = 28.0 ÷ 142.0 × 100 = 19.7 %
```

The atom economy is low because octane is also formed, but octane is itself a useful product (a component of petrol), so in practice little is wasted.

## What happens to the products

After cracking, the mixture of products is separated, usually by fractional distillation, into gases and liquids. The alkenes are used to make polymers: ethene gives poly(ethene), and propene gives poly(propene). The branched alkanes and aromatic hydrocarbons are blended into petrol. The short-chain alkanes can also be used as fuels or as a feedstock. Because cracking converts a cheap, surplus material into several valuable products, it is one of the most important processes in an oil refinery.

### Worked example 5: the number of molecules

In the equation C₁₆H₃₄ → C₈H₁₈ + 2C₄H₈, one molecule becomes three. The number of molecules increases, so the entropy increases. This is one reason why cracking is favoured at high temperature, as the entropy term −TΔS becomes more important. The reaction is also endothermic, because C–C bonds are broken and the C=C π bond that forms is not as strong as the bonds lost in total.

## Common mistakes

- Giving cracking as a physical process. It is a chemical reaction that breaks C–C bonds.
- Writing equations that do not balance, or that give two alkanes and no alkene.
- Mixing up thermal and catalytic conditions (pressure in particular).
- Saying catalytic cracking needs high pressure.
- Not naming the catalyst as a zeolite.
- Saying cracking produces only alkenes. It produces an alkane and an alkene.
- Giving "to make longer molecules" as the reason. The purpose is to make shorter, more useful molecules.
- Forgetting the economic reason: supply and demand.
- Forgetting that the mechanism is not required, and writing long mechanisms.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Write an equation for cracking**: check carbon and hydrogen balance and make sure one product is an alkene.
- **State the conditions**: pressure, temperature and catalyst for each type.
- **Explain the economic reasons**: demand for short chains, value of alkenes, lower energy with a catalyst.
- **Calculate**: use mole ratios as in any equation.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why does cracking always produce an alkene?**
The molecule being cracked is saturated. When it splits into smaller pieces, the hydrogen atoms cannot all be accommodated in saturated products, so one product has a double bond.

**Is cracking the reverse of polymerisation?**
In a sense, but it is not the exact reverse. Cracking breaks alkanes into smaller alkanes and alkenes. Polymerisation joins alkenes into long chains.

**Why use a zeolite?**
It has a large surface area and a structure that lets the reaction run at lower temperature and pressure, and it favours the formation of branched and aromatic products used in fuels.
