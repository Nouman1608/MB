**These notes condense the methodological concepts in section 3.1.2 of Cambridge International AS & A Level Psychology (9990), syllabus for 2024 to 2026.** These concepts are tested in the abstract, in relation to the core studies, and in the scenario and planning questions on Paper 2. The [methodological concepts study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-psychology-9990-methodological-concepts-as/) explains them with examples; test yourself with the [methodological concepts practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-psychology-9990-methodological-concepts-as-practice/).

## Hypotheses: templates

| Type | Template |
|---|---|
| Null | There will be no difference in [DV] between [condition 1] and [condition 2]. |
| Directional (one-tailed) | [Condition 1] will produce more/less [DV] than [condition 2]. |
| Non-directional (two-tailed) | There will be a difference in [DV] between [condition 1] and [condition 2]. |
| Correlational (directional) | There will be a positive/negative correlation between [co-variable 1] and [co-variable 2]. |

Always fill the brackets with **operationalised** variables.

## Variables and controls

- **IV**: manipulated by the researcher. **DV**: measured.
- **Operational definition**: exactly how a variable is manipulated or measured.
- **Participant variables**: differences between people (age, ability, mood).
- **Situational variables**: differences in the setting (noise, light, time of day).
- **Uncontrolled variable**: any variable other than the IV that might affect the DV.
- **Standardisation**: same instructions, materials, timing and setting for everyone.

## Types of data

| | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|
| Quantitative | Easy to compare and summarise | Loses detail and reasons |
| Qualitative | Rich, explains why | Hard to compare; open to interpretation |
| Objective | Free from bias | May miss personal experience |
| Subjective | Captures experience | Open to bias |

## Sampling

| Technique | How | Strength | Weakness | Core-study example |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opportunity | Whoever is available | Quick, cheap | Unrepresentative | Piliavin et al. (passengers present) |
| Random | Equal chance from a full list | Most likely to be representative | Needs a full list; refusals | (Rare in the core studies) |
| Volunteer | Respond to an advert | Reaches many willing people | Volunteer bias | Milgram (newspaper advert) |

## Ethics: two lists

**Humans**: minimising harm (maximising benefit); valid and informed consent; right to withdraw; lack of deception; confidentiality; privacy; debriefing.

**Animals**: minimising harm (maximising benefit); replacement; species; numbers; procedures (pain, suffering and distress; housing; reward, deprivation and aversive stimuli).

## Validity and reliability

| Concept | Question it answers | Types or factors |
|---|---|---|
| Validity | Does it measure what it claims? | Ecological validity; subjectivity or objectivity; demand characteristics; generalisability |
| Reliability | Is it consistent? | Inter-rater; inter-observer; test-retest |
| Replicability | Can it be repeated exactly? | Standardised, fully described procedure |

## Data analysis

- **Mode**: most frequent score. **Median**: middle score in order. **Mean**: total divided by number of scores.
- **Range**: highest minus lowest. **Standard deviation**: average spread around the mean; larger means more variation.
- **Bar chart**: separate categories; gaps between bars. **Histogram**: continuous data in intervals; no gaps. **Scatter graph**: two co-variables.
- Candidates will not be required to carry out calculations.

Best average to choose: the mean uses every score but is distorted by extreme scores; the median is not affected by extremes; the mode is the only one for categories.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Aim | Statement of what the study investigates |
| Population | The whole group the researcher wants to generalise to |
| Sample | The people who take part |
| Generalisation | Applying findings beyond the sample |
| Demand characteristics | Cues that reveal the aim and change behaviour |
| Ecological validity | How far findings apply to real-life settings |
| Test-retest reliability | Same people, same test, similar results later |
| Inter-rater reliability | Agreement between people scoring the same data |
| Confounding variable | An uncontrolled variable that changes with the IV |
| Replacement | Using alternatives to animals where possible |

## Exam traps

- "No difference" hypotheses are null, even if they mention the IV and DV.
- A directional hypothesis is one-tailed, not "two-sided".
- Test-retest is about the *same* people taking the *same* test again.
- Random sampling needs the whole population list; picking "randomly" from people in a canteen is opportunity sampling.
- The median does not need calculation, but you must know how to find it.
- An ethics answer needs the guideline *and* the feature of the study.

## Self-test

1. Write a non-directional hypothesis for a study of whether music affects reading speed.
2. What is the difference between a participant variable and a situational variable?
3. Define standardisation.
4. Give one strength of qualitative data.
5. Why might a volunteer sample be biased?
6. Name the three "procedures" in the animal ethics guidelines.
7. What are demand characteristics?
8. How would you check inter-observer reliability?
9. Which measure of central tendency is not affected by one extreme score?
10. What does a larger standard deviation tell you?
11. Which graph shows the relationship between two co-variables?
12. What is the difference between a bar chart and a histogram?

**Answers:** (1) "There will be a difference in the number of words read per minute with and without background music." (2) participant variables are differences between people; situational variables are features of the setting. (3) keeping the procedure the same for every participant. (4) rich detail that can explain reasons for behaviour. (5) volunteers may be more motivated, curious or confident than the population. (6) pain, suffering and distress; housing; reward, deprivation and aversive stimuli. (7) cues that reveal the aim so participants change their behaviour. (8) two observers record the same behaviour independently using the same categories, then compare their records for agreement. (9) the median (or mode). (10) scores are more spread out around the mean. (11) a scatter graph. (12) a bar chart shows separate categories with gaps; a histogram shows continuous data with no gaps.
