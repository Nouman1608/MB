These questions cover section 4.2 of the OCR GCSE (9-1) Economics specification (J205, Version 2.0, June 2026), Balance of payments. All countries and figures are invented. Revise first with the [balance of payments study guide](/resources/ocr-gcse-economics-j205-balance-of-payments-ocr-gcse-economics/) and the [balance of payments revision notes](/resources/ocr-gcse-economics-j205-balance-of-payments-ocr-gcse-economics-revision-notes/).

## Section A: short-answer questions

**1.** State what is meant by a current account surplus. **[1]**

**2.** Identify the part of the UK current account in which each transaction appears, and whether it is a credit or a debit.

**(a)** A UK student pays tuition fees to a university in Australia. **[1]**
**(b)** The UK government gives aid to another country. **[1]**

**3.** A country exports goods worth £340bn and services worth £210bn. It imports goods worth £395bn and services worth £140bn. Calculate its balance of trade in goods and services. **[2]**

**4.** Which one of the following is a credit on the UK current account? **[1]**

- A. A UK firm imports steel from India.
- B. A German family takes a holiday in Cornwall.
- C. The UK government pays overseas aid.
- D. A UK family buys a car made in Japan.

**5.** Explain one reason why higher inflation than in trading partners may lead to a current account deficit. **[2]**

**6.** A country's current account deficit is £52bn and its GDP is £2,600bn. Express the deficit as a percentage of GDP. **[1]**

**7.** Explain why a country with a current account deficit must borrow from abroad or sell assets. **[2]**

## Section B: structured and extended questions

**8.** The table shows Country V's current account for one year, in £bn. The overall current account balance is −£20bn.

| Part | Credits | Debits |
|---|---|---|
| Goods | 520 | 610 |
| Services | 290 | 200 |
| Primary income | 120 | ? |
| Secondary income | 20 | 45 |

**(a)** Calculate the balance on trade in goods. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the missing value for primary income debits. **[2]**
**(c)** State whether trade in goods and services together is in surplus, deficit or balance. **[1]**
**(d)** Explain why the current account is in deficit. **[1]**

**9.** The table shows Country W's exports and imports of goods and services, in £bn.

| Year | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Exports | 210 | 225 | 240 | 228 | 250 |
| Imports | 230 | 250 | 275 | 255 | 262 |

**(a)** Calculate the trade balance for each year. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage change in exports and in imports between Year 1 and Year 5, to one decimal place. **[2]**
**(c)** Analyse a possible reason for the changes between Year 3 and Year 4. **[2]**

**10.** Analyse how a rise in the value of the pound could affect the UK's current account. **[3]**

**11.** Evaluate the importance of the current account balance to the UK economy. **[8]**

**12.** Evaluate whether low productivity is the main cause of a persistent current account deficit. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** When the value of money flowing in on the current account (credits) is greater than the value flowing out (debits) [1]. **[1]**

**2. (a)** Trade in services, debit [1]. **(b)** Secondary income, debit [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Exports = 340 + 210 = £550bn; imports = 395 + 140 = £535bn [1]. Balance = **+£15bn**, a surplus [1]. **[2]**

**4.** **B** [1]. Spending by foreign tourists in the UK is an export of services, so money flows in. **[1]**

**5.** If UK prices rise faster than prices abroad, UK goods become dearer compared with foreign goods [1]. Foreign buyers buy fewer UK exports and UK consumers switch to cheaper imports, so the current account worsens [1]. **[2]**

**6.** 52 ÷ 2,600 × 100 = **2.0%** [1]. **[1]**

**7.** A deficit means more money is paid out for imports and income than is received [1]. The difference has to be paid for, so the country borrows from foreign lenders or sells assets such as firms, property or shares to foreign buyers [1]. **[2]**

**8. (a)** 520 − 610 = **−£90bn** [1].
**(b)** Total credits = 520 + 290 + 120 + 20 = £950bn, so total debits = 950 + 20 = £970bn [1]. Primary debits = 970 − (610 + 200 + 45) = **£115bn** [1].
**(c)** Goods −90 + services +90 = 0, so trade is **in balance** [1].
**(d)** Net income flows are negative: primary income +£5bn, but secondary income −£25bn, giving −£20bn overall [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* A current account can be in deficit even when trade balances, because income flows count too.

**9. (a)** −20, −25, −35, −27, −12 (£bn) (three years correct [1]; all five correct [1]).
**(b)** Exports: (250 − 210) ÷ 210 × 100 = **+19.0%** [1]. Imports: (262 − 230) ÷ 230 × 100 = **+13.9%** [1].
**(c)** Both exports and imports fell in Year 4, by £12bn and £20bn [1]. This suggests a downturn at home, which cut spending on imports, and weaker demand abroad, which cut exports; imports fell more, so the deficit narrowed [1]. **[6]**

**10.** A stronger pound means foreign buyers need more of their own currency to buy UK goods, so UK exports become dearer abroad [1]. Imports become cheaper for UK buyers, so they buy more of them [1]. Export credits fall and import debits rise, so the current account worsens: a surplus shrinks or a deficit widens [1]. **[3]**

**11.** Reasons it is important: the UK is an open economy where trade supports many jobs and much income [1]. A persistent deficit must be financed by borrowing or selling assets to foreign owners, so more profit and interest flow abroad in future [1]. A large deficit may weaken the pound, raising import prices and inflation [1]. A deficit may show weak competitiveness in exporting industries [1]. Reasons it may matter less: the UK's strong services exports partly offset its goods deficit [1], and a deficit funded by foreign investment in UK businesses can raise future output [1]. Other objectives, such as inflation or unemployment, may be more pressing [1]. Judgement: the current account matters most if the deficit is large and long-lasting and reflects weak competitiveness; a moderate deficit financed by long-term investment is less of a concern [1]. **[8]**

**12.** For: low productivity raises the cost per unit of UK-made goods, so they are less price competitive abroad [1], and firms may also invest less in quality and design, losing sales on non-price factors [1]. Because productivity changes slowly, it can explain a deficit that persists [1]. Against: a strong exchange rate can make exports dear even if productivity is reasonable [1], and fast growth in domestic spending raises imports regardless of productivity [1]. Judgement: low productivity is likely to be a main cause of a deficit that lasts for many years, while exchange rates and the economic cycle explain shorter swings, so the cause depends on the time period studied [1]. **[6]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Putting services such as tourism or education into goods.
- Dropping minus signs in calculations.
- Forgetting income flows when finding the current account balance.
- Describing data without quoting figures.
- Confusing the current account with the government budget.
