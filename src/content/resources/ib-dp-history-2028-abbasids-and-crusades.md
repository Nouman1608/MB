**HL only.** This guide covers Regional study 1, "Transformation in North Africa and the Middle East (750 to 1291)", in the Africa and the Middle East option of IB DP History (first assessment 2028). Regional studies are examined in Paper 3, which is taken at Higher Level only. In Paper 3 you answer two essay questions, each from a different regional study, and each is marked out of 15. The skill the paper rewards is **evaluating historical arguments**: you must understand the claim in the question, weigh different perspectives on it, and reach a supported judgement. This guide therefore teaches the six inquiry topics of the study as a set of arguments, not as a story to be retold.

The guide lists six inquiry topics for this study: the rise of the Abbasid Caliphate, the impact of Abbasid rule, the reasons for and regional impact of the collapse of Abbasid rule, the reasons for the emergence of the Crusades, the reasons for their outcome, and their impact in the Middle East. Only people and events named in the guide can be named in exam questions, so the names below are the ones to know well.

## Rise of the Abbasid Caliphate

The Umayyad caliphs ruled from Damascus. By the 740s their rule was under strain. Rivalry between Arab tribal groups divided the army. Many non-Arab converts, called *mawali*, resented a system in which Arab status still counted for more than faith. Shi'a supporters of the family of Ali believed the caliphate had been taken from its rightful holders. Revolts, including the Berber revolt in the west, stretched Umayyad forces, and a civil war from 744 left the last caliph, Marwan II, short of reliable support.

The Abbasids were descended from the Prophet's uncle al-Abbas. They built a secret movement, with its centre in Khurasan in the far east of the empire, where Arab settlers and Persian converts shared grievances. Abu Muslim organised the rising under black banners from 747. The decisive victory over Marwan II came at the Zab in 750. Abu al-Abbas, known as al-Saffah, was proclaimed caliph, and the Umayyad family was hunted down. His successor al-Mansur secured the dynasty, removed rivals (including Abu Muslim) and moved the centre of gravity eastwards by founding Baghdad in 762.

Arguments to weigh:

- **The Abbasid revolution was a revolution of the non-Arab Muslims.** Support: the Khurasani base and the end of Arab privilege. Challenge: the leaders were Arab and Hashimite, and Arabs still held high office.
- **Umayyad weakness was the main cause.** Support: tribal division and financial grievance. Challenge: weakness alone does not explain why a new dynasty, rather than local rulers, took over; the organisation and propaganda of the Abbasid movement mattered.
- **Religion was decisive.** The Abbasids claimed legitimacy through descent from the Prophet's family and promised a rule closer to Islamic ideals. Some historians stress this religious appeal; others see a political coalition that used religious language.

## Impact of Abbasid rule

Under the Abbasids the caliphate became more Persian in its administration and court ritual. The caliph ruled through a vizier (the Barmakid family served Harun al-Rashid until their fall in 803) and a growing bureaucracy of secretaries. Revenue came from land tax and from trade. Baghdad, on the Tigris, was a hub for routes linking the Mediterranean, Central Asia and the Indian Ocean. Irrigated farming in Iraq, new crops and a money economy supported a large urban population. Paper, which spread westward in the eighth century, made record keeping and book production cheaper.

The cultural record is the best known. Scholars translated Greek, Persian and Indian works into Arabic. The House of Wisdom in Baghdad, associated with al-Ma'mun, was a centre of this movement. Al-Khwarizmi's work gave algebra its name, al-Razi advanced medicine, and astronomers built observatories. Law schools took shape, and writers produced history, poetry and theology.

Harun al-Rashid is remembered for the splendour of his court, but also for dividing the empire between his sons al-Amin and al-Ma'mun. Al-Ma'mun won the civil war of 811 to 813, supported intellectual life, and later pressed scholars to accept a doctrine about the Qur'an in the *mihna* (inquisition) from 833, an example of a caliph using state power over belief.

Arguments to weigh:

- **"Golden Age of Islam" is a fair description.** Support: the scale of translation, science and trade. Challenge: the label can hide uneven prosperity, the heavy tax burden on peasants, slavery in the economy, and the fact that much of the learning was paid for by courts and patrons, not state policy.
- **Abbasid government was centralised.** Support: the bureaucracy and the postal and intelligence system. Challenge: distance and local governors meant control weakened quickly.

## Reasons for, and regional impact of, the collapse of Abbasid rule

The caliphate did not fall in a day. Real power slipped away over about two centuries, and the Mongol sack of 1258 ended an institution that had already lost its authority.

- **Political factors.** The civil war between al-Amin and al-Ma'mun showed that the succession was fragile. Provincial governors founded their own dynasties in practice: the Tahirids and Saffarids in the east, the Tulunids in Egypt.
- **Military factors.** The caliphs came to rely on Turkic slave soldiers. Al-Mu'tasim moved the capital to Samarra in 836 to separate them from the people of Baghdad, but commanders came to make and unmake caliphs. In 945 the Buyids, a Shi'a dynasty from Iran, took Baghdad. In 1055 the Seljuks, Sunni Turks, entered the city and took the title of sultan. The Seljuks also defeated the Byzantines at Manzikert in 1071. Finally the Mongols under Hulagu took Baghdad in 1258 and killed the last caliph, al-Musta'sim.
- **Economic factors.** Costs of army and court rose while revenue fell as provincial rulers kept tax. The Zanj revolt of enslaved labourers in southern Iraq (869 to 883) damaged the rich agricultural region.
- **Religious factors.** The Fatimids, an Isma'ili Shi'a dynasty, claimed the caliphate for themselves, set up in Ifriqiya in 909 and conquered Egypt in 969, founding Cairo. Their rival caliphate broke the unity of the Islamic world. Christian powers, especially the Byzantines, also pressed on the frontier.

Regional impact: Egypt and Syria became centres of power separate from Baghdad; Turkic and Persian elites took the lead in much of the Muslim world; and the Seljuk advance in Anatolia, together with Byzantine appeals for help, fed into the origins of the Crusades.

## Reasons for the emergence of the Crusades

Pope Urban II called for armed pilgrimage at Clermont in 1095, after Emperor Alexios I asked for help against the Seljuks. The guide asks you to consider religious, political, economic and territorial factors.

- **Religious:** ideas of holy war, pilgrimage and penance; the promise of spiritual reward; devotion to Jerusalem.
- **Political:** the papacy's wish to lead Christendom and to ease the conflict among Western knights; the appeal from Byzantium; the weakness of the divided Muslim states in Syria after the death of Seljuk sultan Malik-Shah in 1092.
- **Economic:** the hope of land and wealth, and growing Italian trade interest in the eastern Mediterranean. Historians now give less weight to the older idea that landless younger sons drove the movement, because many crusaders spent heavily on the journey.
- **Territorial:** the opportunity to carve out lordships in Syria and Palestine.

## Reasons for the outcome of the Crusades

The First Crusade succeeded because Muslim Syria was divided between rival rulers in Damascus, Aleppo and elsewhere, and because the crusaders, led by figures such as Godfrey de Bouillon, kept together long enough to take Antioch (1098) and Jerusalem (1099). The Crusader states of Edessa, Antioch, Tripoli and Jerusalem depended on castles, military orders such as the Templars and Hospitallers, and sea supply from Italian cities.

The tide turned as Muslim leaders unified the region. Imad ad-Din Zengi of Mosul took Edessa in 1144. His son Nur ad-Din united Syria and promoted jihad. Salah ad-Din, who served Nur ad-Din, took power in Egypt in 1171, ended the Fatimid caliphate, then brought Syria under his rule. He destroyed the Frankish field army at Hattin in 1187 and took Jerusalem. The Third Crusade, with Richard I of England, recovered Acre and the coast and won at Arsuf, but could not retake Jerusalem; a truce in 1192 left the Franks a coastal strip. Later the Mamluk sultan Baybars took Antioch (1268) and his successors finished the task, ending with Acre in 1291.

Arguments to weigh: Muslim unity under strong leaders was the main reason for the outcome (support: the sequence from Zengi to Salah ad-Din; challenge: Salah ad-Din's empire fractured after 1193 and the Franks survived another century). Western weaknesses mattered too: crusades were launched sporadically, leaders quarrelled, and the Fourth Crusade (1204) attacked Constantinople instead.

## Impact of the Crusades in the Middle East

- **Political:** the Crusader states, then the rise of the Ayyubid and later Mamluk regimes, which owed part of their legitimacy to leading holy war.
- **Economic:** Italian merchants gained trading privileges; ports like Acre grew; long-distance trade continued despite war.
- **Social:** destruction, massacres and displacement (the sack of Jerusalem in 1099 is recalled as a massacre); Eastern Christians in mixed communities sometimes suffered suspicion; everyday contact also occurred between Franks and local people.
- **Cultural:** hardened memory of religious conflict, along with exchanges of ideas, building styles and goods.

Some historians argue the Crusades were a limited episode for the Islamic world compared with the Mongol invasions, which destroyed Baghdad and killed far more people. Others stress the long memory of the Crusades in later political speech.

## Worked example: planning a Paper 3 essay

*Question:* "The collapse of Abbasid rule was caused mainly by military factors." To what extent do you agree?

1. **Understand the argument.** The claim ranks military factors above others. You must test it against political, economic and religious factors.
2. **Evidence for the claim.** The reliance on Turkic soldiers, the Buyid seizure of Baghdad in 945, the Seljuk entry in 1055 and the Mongol conquest in 1258.
3. **Perspectives.** One view treats the soldiers as a symptom: the caliphs hired them because provincial governors had stopped sending revenue. A second view stresses religious division, with the Fatimid challenge breaking the unity of the caliphate. A third view highlights the economy.
4. **Judgement.** For example: military power was the immediate mechanism of collapse, but the underlying cause was the loss of fiscal and political control, so "mainly" is only partly justified.

## Common mistakes

- Telling the story of the Crusades without making an argument about outcome or impact.
- Treating "the Muslim world" as one actor; it was divided throughout.
- Blaming the Mongols alone for the end of the caliphate, ignoring the 300 years of earlier decline.
- Using dates loosely: the First Crusade took Jerusalem in 1099, Hattin was 1187, Acre fell in 1291.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need to learn all the Crusades?** Learn the First, Second and Third in detail, and know the main later events (Fourth Crusade, Frederick II's treaty of 1229, and the Mamluk conquests), because the guide asks about the whole outcome.

**Is historiography compulsory?** No. The guide states that Paper 3 does not require references to historiography for the highest markband, but you must evaluate different perspectives on the argument.

**Which leaders can be named in a question?** Those in the guide: Harun al-Rashid, al-Ma'mun, Godfrey de Bouillon, Richard I of England, Imad ad-Din Zengi, Nur ad-Din, Salah ad-Din and Baybars.
