These questions cover section 4.1 Market research (points 4.1.1 to 4.1.3) of the Pearson Edexcel International GCSE in Business (4BS1) specification, Issue 2, February 2026. Revise with the [market research study guide](/resources/edexcel-igcse-business-4bs1-market-research/) and the [market research revision notes](/resources/edexcel-igcse-business-4bs1-market-research-revision-notes/) first if needed. Every business is invented and amounts are in dollars. Give percentages to 1 decimal place where needed.

## Questions

### Section A: shorter questions

**1.** Define secondary research. **[2]**

**2.** State whether each of these is primary or secondary research.

**(a)** Counting the cars that pass a proposed shop site. **[1]**
**(b)** Reading a government report on household incomes. **[1]**
**(c)** Holding a focus group to discuss a new snack. **[1]**

**3.** Give two methods of primary research. **[2]**

**4.** Orchard Pet Supplies asked 320 people about a new dog-grooming service. 112 of them own a dog. Of the dog owners, 70 said they would use the service. **(Calculation)**

**(a)** Calculate the percentage of people asked who own a dog. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage of dog owners who would use the service. **[2]**
**(c)** The 70 people said they would spend a total of 1,890 a month on grooming. Calculate the average monthly spend per person. **[1]**

**5.** Outline one difference between qualitative and quantitative data. **[2]**

**6.** Explain one benefit of test marketing. **[3]**

**7.** Explain one reason why market research data may be unreliable. **[3]**

### Section B: longer questions

**8.** Seo-yeon plans to open a Korean restaurant in a town that has none. She will need a large bank loan to fit out the premises. Analyse how market research could help Seo-yeon reduce the risk of her plan. **[4]**

**9.** Velo Threads sells cycling clothing online. It ran a poll on its social media page, which has 2,400 followers. 600 followers voted, and 420 of them said they wanted a women's range.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage of voters who wanted a women's range. **[1]**
**(b)** Analyse one limitation of using this poll to decide whether to launch a women's range. **[4]**

**10.** Granary Lane Bakery has 9 employees. The owner wants to know whether customers would buy a new lunchtime sandwich range.

- **Option A:** a questionnaire handed to 200 customers at the counter, costing about 60 to print and taking one week.
- **Option B:** test marketing the sandwiches for two weeks, costing about 900 in ingredients and packaging, some of which would be covered by sales.

Justify which option Granary Lane Bakery should choose. **[6]**

**11.** Norland Foods plc is a large food manufacturer about to launch a new breakfast cereal nationally. It is test marketing the cereal in two regions. So far, 46 shops have sold 18,400 boxes over 8 weeks. A competitor is rumoured to be launching a similar cereal soon.

**(a)** Calculate the average weekly sales per shop. **[2]**
**(b)** Evaluate whether Norland Foods should complete its full test-marketing period before the national launch. **[9]**

**12.** **(a)** Give one example of information a business could find in a government report. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain why a business might carry out secondary research before primary research. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** Research using information that already exists [1], collected by someone else for a different purpose, such as market reports or government reports [1]. **[2]**

**2. (a)** Primary (observation) [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** Secondary [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** Primary [1]. **[1]**

**3.** Any two of: survey, questionnaire, focus group, observation, test marketing [1 each]. **[2]**

**4. (a)** 112 ÷ 320 × 100 = **35.0%** [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** 70 ÷ 112 [1] × 100 = **62.5%** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** 1,890 ÷ 70 = **27.00** [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Part (b) asks for a percentage of dog owners, so the base is 112, not 320. Read which group the question refers to.

**5.** Quantitative data is numerical, such as the percentage of people who would buy [1], while qualitative data is descriptive and records opinions or reasons, such as why people like a product [1]. **[2]**

**6.** Customers spend real money on the product in the test area [1], so the business sees actual demand rather than what people say they would buy [1], which gives stronger evidence for deciding how much to produce at the full launch [1]. **[3]**

**7.** The sample may not represent the target market [1], for example if only existing customers are asked [1], so the results may overstate demand and lead to a poor decision such as producing too much [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Other valid reasons include a small sample, leading questions, out-of-date data or a biased source. Develop one fully.

**8.** Seo-yeon could use a questionnaire to find out how many local people would eat Korean food and how much they would spend [1]. This tells her whether the gap in the market exists because there is no demand, or because no one has yet met it [1]. If the results show enough interest, she can borrow with more confidence; if not, she can change the plan before committing to a large loan [1], which lowers the chance of being unable to repay it [1]. **[4]**

**9. (a)** 420 ÷ 600 × 100 = **70.0%** [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** Only 600 of 2,400 followers voted, and followers already like the brand [1], so they may not represent women cyclists who do not follow Velo Threads [1]. Clicking a poll costs nothing, so people who vote yes may not actually buy [1]. If Velo Threads launches a full range based on 70% support, it could be left with unsold stock and wasted money [1]. **[4]**

**10.** For Option A: it is cheap at about 60 and quick, which suits a small business [1], and it reaches actual customers at the counter [1]. Against Option A: people may say they would buy sandwiches but not do so, so it measures intention, not real demand [1]. For Option B: customers spend real money, so it shows true demand and which fillings sell [1]. Against Option B: it costs about 900, a lot for a 9-person bakery, and any unsold sandwiches are wasted [1]. Judgement: for example, Option A first, because it is cheap and fast, then a small test only if the questionnaire shows strong interest [1]. **[6]**

**11. (a)** 18,400 ÷ 46 = 400 boxes per shop [1]; 400 ÷ 8 = **50** boxes per shop per week [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** For completing the test: early sales may include curious first-time buyers, so a longer test shows whether people buy again [1]. A national launch for a large business involves large costs for production and promotion [1], so better evidence lowers the risk of an expensive failure [1]. The two test regions may not represent the whole country, and more time allows results to be checked [1]. Against: the competitor may launch first, taking customers and shelf space [1], and the longer the test, the more time rivals have to study Norland's cereal [1]. Sales of 50 boxes per shop per week are already firm evidence of demand [1]. Judgement: for example, end the test early and launch nationally, because the evidence is already positive and being first matters [1], but keep tracking repeat sales so production can be adjusted [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* Weigh reducing risk against the risk of being beaten by a rival. Both are real; the judgement must say which matters more here and why.

**12. (a)** Any one of: population size or age structure, average incomes, household spending patterns, employment levels [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** Secondary research is usually cheaper and quicker [1], so it can give a general picture of the market first, such as its size or the main competitors [1]. This helps the business decide whether primary research is worth paying for and what questions it needs to ask [1]. **[3]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calling government or market reports primary research.
- Using the wrong base for a percentage when the question refers to a subgroup.
- Treating what people say they will buy as certain demand.
- Ignoring the size and budget of the business when choosing a method.
- Writing about social media in general without discussing the reliability of the data it gives.
