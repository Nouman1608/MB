## Section B: Paper 4 style questions

**6.** Nadia has felt low almost every day for the past month. She has lost interest in her hobbies, sleeps badly and feels worthless.

**(a)** Explain whether Nadia's symptoms meet the ICD-11 description of a depressive episode. **[2]**

**(b)** Her doctor gives her the Beck Depression Inventory. Explain **one** strength and **one** weakness of using it to assess Nadia. **[4]**

**7. (a)** Describe the sample used by Oruč et al. (1997). **[2]**

**8.** A therapist using REBT hears a client say: "I failed my driving test, so I am a complete failure and I must never try anything difficult again." Using the ABC model, identify the A, B and C, and give **one** example of a disputing question. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** 1 mark each for any two: euphoric, irritable or expansive mood; increased activity or energy; rapid or pressured speech; racing thoughts or flight of ideas; reduced need for sleep; distractibility; impulsive or reckless behaviour; inflated self-esteem or grandiosity; lasting at least one week.

**2.** Up to 2 marks. Bipolar type I requires at least one manic or mixed episode (1). Bipolar type II requires at least one hypomanic episode and at least one depressive episode, but no full manic episode (1).

**3.** Up to 3 marks. MAOIs inhibit the enzyme monoamine oxidase (1), which normally breaks down monoamines such as serotonin and noradrenaline, so more remain available in the synapse (1). Disadvantage: patients must avoid tyramine-rich foods such as some cheeses, which can cause a dangerous rise in blood pressure (1).

**4.** Up to 3 marks. Internal: the person blames themselves rather than circumstances (1). Stable: they believe the cause will last (1). Global: they believe it affects every area of life (1). Example: after being rejected for a job, "I'm useless (internal), I'll never get a job (stable), and I mess up everything (global)." Up to 3 marks in total; credit the example within the marking points.

**5. (a)** Up to 6 marks. Depression arises from faulty thinking (1). Negative schemas develop in childhood, for example through criticism or loss (1), and are triggered by later stressful events (1). They produce automatic negative thoughts (1) and the negative triad: negative views of the self, the world and the future (1). Cognitive errors such as overgeneralisation, selective abstraction, magnification and personalisation maintain the negative views (1).

**5. (b)** Level-based: 9–10 marks for a detailed, balanced evaluation with determinism developed and a conclusion. Indicative content:

- *Support*: cognitive therapy that targets negative thoughts reduces depression, which suggests thinking plays a causal role.
- *Cause or effect*: negative thinking might be a symptom of depression rather than its cause; correlational evidence cannot separate these.
- *Determinism versus free will*: schemas from childhood imply soft determinism, but the theory assumes people can learn to change their thinking, which gives them some control and hope.
- *Individual versus situational*: the theory focuses on the individual and may ignore real situational causes such as poverty or abuse, risking blame.
- *Reductionism*: it reduces depression to cognition and does not account for biochemical or genetic evidence.
- *Conclusion*: a useful and treatable account of how depression is maintained, best combined with biological and social explanations.

**6.** **(a)** Up to 2 marks. Yes: she has had depressed mood almost every day for longer than two weeks (1), with loss of interest plus other symptoms such as poor sleep and worthlessness (1).

**(b)** 2 marks each. Strength: standardised items scored 0–3 give a quantitative total (1) that can be compared with later scores to see whether treatment helps Nadia (1). Weakness: it relies on Nadia's self-report (1); she might under-report feelings out of embarrassment, or her mood on the day might distort the score (1).

**7. (a)** Up to 2 marks. 42 patients with bipolar I disorder (1) and 40 healthy controls (1).

**7. (b)** Up to 3 marks. No significant association was found in the whole sample, only trends in women (1). The patients had bipolar I disorder, not unipolar depression (1). An association, even if found, would be correlational and could show only a small increase in susceptibility, not a cause (1).

**8.** 1 mark each. A (activating event): failing the driving test (1). B (belief): "I am a complete failure and I must never try anything difficult again" (1). C (consequence): low mood and avoidance of challenges (1). Disputing question, for example: "Where is the evidence that failing one test makes you a complete failure?" (empirical) or "Does believing this help you pass next time?" (pragmatic) (1).

**9.** **(a)** Up to 2 marks. Random allocation gives every participant an equal chance of each treatment (1), so participant variables such as initial severity are likely to be spread evenly, and differences can be attributed to the treatment (1).

**(b)** Up to 2 marks. For example: BDI scores at the start and at six months (1), because it is standardised and quantitative, so change can be compared between the two groups (1).

**10.** Up to 2 marks. The change in style and the reduction in depression happened together, so the finding is correlational (1); improving mood could have made explanations more optimistic, rather than the other way round (1).

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need the BDI-II score bands?**
They are useful for applying the BDI to a scenario. Knowing that it has 21 items scored 0–3 is the essential part.

**Can I write about lithium for bipolar disorder?**
The syllabus lists antidepressants; you can mention other drugs as extra context, but your answer should focus on tricyclics, MAOIs and SSRIs.
