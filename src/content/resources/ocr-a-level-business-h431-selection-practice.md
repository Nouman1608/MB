## Questions

### Section A: short-answer questions

**1.** Explain what is meant by selection. **[2]**

**2.** Explain the purpose of a reference in the selection process. **[2]**

**3.** Explain **one** weakness of using interviews as the only method of selection. **[2]**

**4.** Distinguish between a CV and an application form. **[3]**

**5.** Which **one** of the following is a work trial?

A: A candidate does part of the job for a day under observation.

B: A candidate completes a numeracy test.

C: A candidate sends a letter explaining why they want the job.

D: A former employer writes about the candidate.

**[1]**

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**6.** Norland Garage is selecting a mechanic. It received 90 applications.

Method X: screen the applications, then interview six candidates. Screening takes 8 minutes per application at £24 an hour. Each interview takes 1 hour with two interviewers, each valued at £28 an hour.

Method Y: Method X plus a paid work trial for two candidates, costing £110 each.

A poor appointment costs about £6,000. Norland estimates a 30% chance of a poor appointment with Method X and 10% with Method Y.

**(a)** Calculate the cost of screening and interviews. **[3]**

**(b)** Calculate the expected cost of each method, including the expected cost of a poor appointment. **[4]**

**(c)** Evaluate which method Norland Garage should use. **[8]**

**7.** Explain **two** reasons why a business might ask for references as part of the selection process, and explain **one** limitation. **[6]**

**8.** Evaluate the importance of selection to a care home and its stakeholders. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** Selection is the process of choosing the most suitable person for a vacancy [1] from the applicants, by comparing them with the person specification [1]. **[2]**

**2.** A reference is a statement from a previous employer or teacher about the candidate [1], and is used to check facts and provide an independent view of the candidate's reliability and performance [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Interviews are subjective [1], so interviewers may favour candidates like themselves or be swayed by first impressions, and a candidate who interviews well may not perform well in the job [1]. Accept: costly in management time. **[2]**

**4.** A CV is a summary of education and experience prepared by the applicant in a format of their choice [1], whereas an application form is set by the business and asks every applicant for the same information [1]. A form makes comparison easier and fairer [1]. **[3]**

**5.** **A** [1]. **[1]**

**6. (a)** Screening = 90 × (8 ÷ 60) hour × £24 = 12 hours × £24 = **£288** [1]. Interviews = 6 × 1 hour × 2 interviewers × £28 = **£336** [1]. Total = **£624** [1]. **[3]**

**(b)** Method X: £624 + (30% × £6,000 = £1,800) = **£2,424** [1] [1]. Method Y: £624 + 2 × £110 = £844; plus 10% × £6,000 = £600; total = **£1,444** [1] [1]. **[4]**

**(c)** On the estimates, Method Y has a lower expected total cost, by £980 (£2,424 − £1,444) [1], although it costs £220 more to run [1]. A work trial is the best test of a mechanic's real skills [1], and lets the candidate see the garage.
The estimates of 30% and 10% are guesses. If the trial reduced the risk only to 25%, Method Y would cost £844 + £1,500 = £2,344, still slightly cheaper than X (£2,424) [1]. The advantage holds under a wide range of assumptions.
Costs and limits: the trial needs supervision, may disrupt work and must be paid and lawful [1]. A candidate in another job may be unable to attend [1].
Judgement: Norland should use Method Y, because the trial costs only £220 and the risk it reduces is expensive, with a more costly mistake in a skilled job; it should hold the trial only for the two best interviewees to limit the cost [1]. **[8]**

**7.** Reason 1: to check the facts on the application, such as dates of employment and duties [1], which protects the business from hiring someone who has exaggerated [1]. Reason 2: to get an independent view of reliability, attendance and performance [1], which an interview cannot show [1]. Limitation: referees are often reluctant to say anything negative, so references tend to be positive [1], and employers may only confirm dates, so they tell the business little about the candidate [1]. **[6]**

**8.** Importance to the business: staff in a care home look after vulnerable residents, so the wrong appointment can cause harm and breach of standards [1]. Checks on honesty and attitude, such as references and background checks, are essential [1]. Good selection reduces turnover, which is high in care, and so saves recruitment cost and keeps experienced staff [1].
Stakeholders: residents and families depend on safe, caring staff [1]; colleagues carry extra work when a poor appointment leaves or is moved out [1]; owners face inspection results, reputation and costs [1]; the regulator checks that appointment procedures are followed.
Methods: a work trial or shadow shift shows how the candidate treats residents [1], while an interview tests values and motivation. Tests of aptitude are less important than attitude [1].
Evaluation and judgement: selection is of very high importance in care because the consequence of error is serious, so the home should use several methods and spend more time per appointment than in a low-risk job [1]. **[9]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Confusing selection with recruitment.
- Describing a method without a strength or weakness for the post.
- Saying interviews are always the best method.
- Ignoring the cost of a poor appointment when comparing methods.
- Not matching the method to the job.
