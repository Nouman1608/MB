## Net force, F = ma and the newton (3.2.1(a), (b))

The acceleration of an object is proportional to the **net force** (the vector sum of all forces) and inversely proportional to its mass:

net force = mass × acceleration, F = ma

Acceleration has the same direction as the net force. If the net force is zero, the velocity is constant.

The **newton (N)** is the net force that produces an acceleration of 1 m s⁻² in a 1 kg mass. So 1 N = 1 kg m s⁻².

**Worked example 1.** A van of mass 1200 kg has a driving force of 3100 N and resistive forces totalling 900 N.

Net force = 3100 − 900 = 2200 N
a = F/m = 2200/1200 = 1.8 m s⁻² (2 significant figures), in the direction of the driving force.

## Weight, W = mg (3.2.1(c))

**Weight** is the gravitational force on an object. Mass (kg) does not change with place. Weight (N) depends on the gravitational field strength g, in N kg⁻¹ (numerically the same as m s⁻²). Near Earth's surface g = 9.81 N kg⁻¹. Weight acts vertically downwards.

**Worked example 2.** A rover has mass 180 kg. Take g = 9.81 N kg⁻¹ on Earth and 1.62 N kg⁻¹ on the Moon.

Earth: W = 180 × 9.81 = 1766 N (1.77 kN)
Moon: W = 180 × 1.62 = 292 N
The mass is 180 kg in both places.

## The four contact forces (3.2.1(d))

| Force | Meaning | Direction |
|---|---|---|
| Tension | Force in a stretched rope, string or cable | Along the rope, pulling on whatever it is attached to |
| Normal contact force | Force from a surface on an object pressing on it | Perpendicular to the surface |
| Upthrust | Upward force from a fluid on an object in it | Vertically upward |
| Friction | Force between surfaces in contact that opposes sliding, or the tendency to slide | Parallel to the surface, against the relative motion |

Upthrust is covered in 3.2.4; here treat it as one force in the balance. "Normal" means at right angles.

## Free-body diagrams (3.2.1(e))

A free-body diagram isolates **one object**, shown as a dot or box, with an arrow for every force acting **on** it.

1. Isolate the object. Do not draw the objects exerting the forces.
2. Draw each force from the object, as an arrow in the direction of the force, labelled with its name (weight, tension, normal contact force and so on).
3. Make arrow lengths roughly proportional to magnitudes. Equal and opposite forces get equal lengths.
4. Include only real forces. A "force of motion" is not a force on the object.
5. Show the net force separately, or state it, once you have resolved.

| Situation | Forces on the object |
|---|---|
| Book at rest on a table | Weight down, normal contact force up (equal) |
| Lift accelerating upward | Weight down, tension up (tension larger) |
| Box sliding down a rough slope | Weight (vertically down), normal contact force (perpendicular to slope), friction (up the slope) |
| Ball bearing falling in oil | Weight down, upthrust up, drag up |

**Worked example 3.** A lift of mass 640 kg hangs from a cable. Find the tension when it (i) accelerates upward at 1.2 m s⁻², (ii) moves at constant speed, (iii) decelerates at 1.2 m s⁻² while still moving upward.

Take upward as positive: T − mg = ma, so T = m(g + a).
(i) T = 640 × (9.81 + 1.2) = 7046 N, about 7.0 kN
(ii) a = 0: T = 640 × 9.81 = 6278 N, about 6.3 kN
(iii) the acceleration is downward, a = −1.2: T = 640 × (9.81 − 1.2) = 5510 N, about 5.5 kN

## Motion under constant force in two dimensions (3.2.1(f))

A constant net force gives a constant acceleration. Use F = ma with the equations of constant acceleration. In two dimensions, resolve into perpendicular components using F cos θ and F sin θ, and treat each direction separately. The components are independent: a force in one direction does not change the motion in the perpendicular direction.

### Motion on a slope

For a slope at angle θ to the horizontal, resolve the weight:

- Component down the slope: mg sin θ
- Component perpendicular to the slope: mg cos θ, balanced by the normal contact force if the object stays on the surface

**Worked example 4.** A block of mass 12 kg slides down a slope at 25° to the horizontal. Friction of 18 N acts up the slope.

Down-slope component = 12 × 9.81 × sin 25° = 49.8 N
Net force down the slope = 49.8 − 18 = 31.8 N
a = 31.8/12 = 2.6 m s⁻²
Normal contact force = 12 × 9.81 × cos 25° = 107 N

### Projectiles

For a projectile with only weight acting (no drag), the net force is vertical. So the horizontal acceleration is zero (constant horizontal velocity) and the vertical acceleration is g.

**Worked example 5.** A stone is thrown horizontally at 12.0 m s⁻¹ from a cliff 35.0 m high. Ignore drag.

Vertical: s = ½gt², so t = √(2 × 35.0/9.81) = 2.67 s
Horizontal distance = 12.0 × 2.67 = 32.1 m
Vertical speed on landing = gt = 9.81 × 2.67 = 26.2 m s⁻¹
Speed = √(12.0² + 26.2²) = 28.8 m s⁻¹, at tan⁻¹(26.2/12.0) = 65.4° below the horizontal.

## Drag and the factors affecting it (3.2.2(a), (b))

**Drag** is the frictional force on an object moving through a fluid (a liquid or a gas). It acts opposite to the object's velocity relative to the fluid. Air resistance is drag in air.

For an object travelling through air, drag is greater when:

- **speed is greater.** At low speed drag rises roughly in proportion to speed, and at higher speed it rises faster than that.
- **cross-sectional area is larger**, because more air must be pushed aside.
- **the shape is less streamlined.** A pointed or smooth profile gives less drag than a flat face at the same area.
- **the air is denser.** Drag is lower at altitude than at sea level.

The specification asks for the factors, not a drag equation. Describe each as an increase or decrease, and say what is kept the same.

## Falling with drag (3.2.2(c))

Take downward as positive for a falling object. Weight acts down, drag acts up:

net force = mg − D, so a = (mg − D)/m

**Worked example 6.** A ball of mass 0.25 kg falls and, at one instant, drag is 0.80 N.

Weight = 0.25 × 9.81 = 2.45 N
a = (2.45 − 0.80)/0.25 = 6.6 m s⁻² downward (less than g).

The motion in a uniform gravitational field has three stages:

1. **Release:** speed is zero, so drag is (almost) zero and a = g.
2. **Speeding up:** as speed rises, drag rises, net force falls, and acceleration falls. The object still speeds up, but more slowly. The velocity–time graph is a curve with a decreasing gradient.
3. **Terminal velocity:** drag equals weight, net force is zero, a = 0.

## Terminal velocity (3.2.2(d)(i))

**Terminal velocity** is the constant velocity reached by an object falling through a fluid when the resultant force on it is zero. In air, weight = drag. In a liquid, weight = drag + upthrust.

If conditions change, a new terminal velocity follows. If a parachute opens, drag suddenly exceeds weight. The net force is upward, so the parachutist decelerates. Drag then falls with the speed until it equals weight again, at a smaller terminal velocity.

**Worked example 7.** A steel ball bearing of mass 0.88 g falls through oil. The upthrust is 1.1 mN.

Weight = 0.88 × 10⁻³ × 9.81 = 8.6 mN
At release (no drag): net force = 8.6 − 1.1 = 7.5 mN, so a = 7.5 × 10⁻³/0.88 × 10⁻³ = 8.6 m s⁻²
At terminal velocity: drag = weight − upthrust = 8.6 − 1.1 = 7.5 mN

## Determining terminal velocity (3.2.2(d)(ii))

OCR links this to practical activity group 1 (PAG1), with a ball bearing in a viscous liquid or cones in air as examples.

### Ball bearing in a viscous liquid

1. Fill a tall transparent cylinder with glycerol or oil. Mark equal intervals (for example every 0.10 m) well below the surface.
2. Measure the ball's diameter with a micrometer at several orientations, then drop it just under the surface so it enters centrally.
3. Time the fall through each interval with a stopwatch or, better, a video with a clock in the frame. Repeat and average.
4. Calculate speed = interval/time. Terminal velocity has been reached when the speed stops changing.

**Worked example 8.** Interval times for successive 0.10 m sections were 0.62, 0.45, 0.38, 0.36, 0.36 and 0.36 s.

Speeds: 0.16, 0.22, 0.26, 0.28, 0.28, 0.28 m s⁻¹
The speed is constant for the last three intervals, so the terminal velocity is 0.28 m s⁻¹. A timing uncertainty of ±0.02 s on 0.36 s is 5.6%, so the result is about 0.28 ± 0.02 m s⁻¹.

### Cones in air

Use paper cake cases or paper cones. Drop each from a fixed height of at least 2 m, measure the time over the last stretch with light gates or video, and compute speed. Stacking cases changes the weight while keeping the shape, so you can investigate how terminal velocity depends on weight. Release from rest at the same height each time and avoid draughts.

### Reducing error

- Reaction time is large compared with 0.36 s, so use video or light gates.
- A ball near the cylinder wall falls more slowly, so drop it along the axis.
- Temperature changes the viscosity of oil, so keep it steady.

## Common errors

- Writing "weight" for mass, or giving weight in kg.
- Drawing the normal contact force vertical on a slope.
- Adding a "force of motion" arrow to a free-body diagram.
- Saying a terminal-velocity object has "no forces". It has balanced forces.
- Forgetting upthrust in a liquid.
- Saying drag stops the motion at terminal velocity: it ends the acceleration, not the movement.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the definition of a newton?**
One newton is the net force that gives a mass of 1 kg an acceleration of 1 m s⁻², so 1 N = 1 kg m s⁻².

**What factors affect drag on an object moving through air?**
Drag increases with greater speed, a larger cross-sectional area, a less streamlined shape and denser air. The OCR H556 specification asks for these factors, not a drag equation.

**What is terminal velocity?**
Terminal velocity is the constant velocity reached by an object falling through a fluid when the resultant force on it is zero. In air, weight equals drag; in a liquid, weight equals drag plus upthrust.

**Does an object at terminal velocity have no forces acting on it?**
No. Forces still act on it, but they are balanced, so the net force and the acceleration are zero while the object keeps moving at constant velocity.

**How do you resolve weight on a slope?**
For a slope at angle θ to the horizontal, the component of weight down the slope is mg sin θ and the component perpendicular to the slope is mg cos θ. The normal contact force acts perpendicular to the slope, not vertically.
