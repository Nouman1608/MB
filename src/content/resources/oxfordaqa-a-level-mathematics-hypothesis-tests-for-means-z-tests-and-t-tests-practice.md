## Questions

**1.** A population is normal with standard deviation 4. A random sample of 25 values is taken. Under the null hypothesis the population mean is 50. State the distribution of the sample mean X̄ under this hypothesis. **[3]**

**2.** A tram timetable states that a journey takes 14.0 minutes on average. Journey times are normally distributed with standard deviation 2.1 minutes. A passenger group believes the journey takes longer. They time 20 journeys and find a mean of 15.1 minutes. Test the belief at the 1% significance level. The critical value for a one-tailed 1% z-test is 2.326. **[5]**

**3.** Rice is sold in bags labelled 500 g. The mass of a bag is normally distributed with standard deviation 12 g. An inspector tests, at the 5% significance level, whether the mean mass has changed from 500 g, using a random sample of 9 bags.

**(a)** Find the critical region for the sample mean x̄. The two-tailed 5% critical values of z are ±1.960. **[4]**
**(b)** The sample mean is 506.5 g. State the conclusion of the test. **[2]**

**4.** Lamp lifetimes are positively skewed and the population standard deviation is unknown. A sample of 100 lamps is tested.

**(a)** Explain why a z-test for the mean can still be used. **[2]**
**(b)** State what is used in place of the population standard deviation, and why this is acceptable here. **[2]**

**5.** A kiosk owner believes that the mean spend per customer is 33.00. A random sample of 120 transactions gives Σx = 4032 and Σx² = 136 550, where x is the spend in the local currency. Test, at the 5% significance level, whether the mean spend differs from 33.00. The two-tailed 5% critical values of z are ±1.960. **[7]**

**6.** The curing time of a glue is claimed to have mean 5.0 hours. A technician suspects a new batch takes longer. Six randomly chosen batches take 4.8, 5.6, 5.1, 6.0, 5.4 and 5.9 hours. Assume the population is normal. Test the suspicion at the 5% significance level. The critical value of t with 5 degrees of freedom for a one-tailed 5% test is 2.015. **[6]**

**7.** State the test statistic for a test of the population mean in each case, giving any degrees of freedom.

**(i)** 10 observations from a normal population, variance unknown. **[1]**
**(ii)** 15 observations from a normal population with known standard deviation 4. **[1]**
**(iii)** 200 observations from a skewed population, variance unknown. **[1]**

**8.** A bakery says its loaves have a mean mass of 61 g above a baseline. For a random sample of 18 loaves, the differences x (in grams) from the baseline give Σx = 1047.6 and Σx² = 61 400. Assuming the population is normal, test at the 5% level whether the mean differs from 61. The two-tailed 5% critical values of t with 17 degrees of freedom are ±2.110. **[6]**

**9.** A test of H₀: μ = 80 against H₁: μ > 80 uses a random sample of 16 from a normal population with standard deviation 5. H₀ is rejected if x̄ > 81.8.

**(a)** Find the probability of a Type I error. **[3]**
**(b)** Describe in words what a Type II error would mean here. **[1]**

**10.** A random sample of 10 values from a normal population has mean 60.3 and standard deviation s = 4.2. Test at the 5% level H₀: μ = 58 against H₁: μ > 58. Critical values for a one-tailed 5% test: t with 9 degrees of freedom is 1.833, and z is 1.645.

**(a)** Calculate the standard error estimate and the value of the test statistic. **[3]**
**(b)** State the conclusion of the correct test. **[2]**
**(c)** A student compares the same statistic with 1.645. Say what the student would conclude and explain why this is not appropriate. **[3]**

**11.** Component masses are normal with standard deviation 6 g. Test H₀: μ = 50 against H₁: μ > 50 at the 5% level, with one-tailed critical value 1.645.

**(a)** A random sample of 16 components has mean 52.0 g. Carry out the test. **[5]**
**(b)** Find the smallest sample size for which a sample mean of 52.0 g would lead to H₀ being rejected. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** X̄ is normally distributed [1] with mean 50 [1] and variance 4²/25 = 0.64 (standard deviation 0.8) [1]. So **X̄ ~ N(50, 0.64)**. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* State whether the second parameter is a variance or a standard deviation.

**2.** H₀: μ = 14.0, H₁: μ > 14.0, where μ is the mean journey time [1].
Standard error = 2.1/√20 = 0.4696 [1].
z = (15.1 − 14.0)/0.4696 = 2.343 [1].
2.343 > 2.326 (p-value 0.0096 < 0.01) [1], so reject H₀. There is evidence at the 1% level that the mean journey time exceeds 14.0 minutes [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* The result is close to the critical value, so avoid rounding the statistic early.

**3. (a)** Standard error = 12/√9 = 4 [1].
Cut-off distance = 1.960 × 4 = 7.84 [1].
Cut-offs are 500 − 7.84 = 492.16 and 500 + 7.84 = 507.84 [1].
Critical region: **x̄ ≤ 492.16 or x̄ ≥ 507.84** [1].
**(b)** 506.5 is not in the critical region (z = 6.5/4 = 1.625, inside ±1.960) [1]. Do not reject H₀: insufficient evidence at the 5% level that the mean mass has changed [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Write both tails of the critical region as inequalities in x̄, not in z.

**4. (a)** The sample is large [1], so by the Central Limit Theorem the sample mean is approximately normally distributed whatever the shape of the population [1].
**(b)** The sample standard deviation s is used as an estimate of σ [1]. With n = 100, s is a reliable estimate, so the statistic is close to N(0, 1) [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Refer to the sampling distribution of the mean, not to the population being normal.

**5.** H₀: μ = 33, H₁: μ ≠ 33, where μ is the mean spend [1].
x̄ = 4032/120 = 33.6 [1].
s² = [136 550 − 4032²/120]/119 = 9.032 [1], so s = 3.005.
Standard error estimate = 3.005/√120 = 0.2743 [1].
z = (33.6 − 33)/0.2743 = 2.19 [1].
2.19 > 1.960 (two-tailed p-value 0.029), so reject H₀ [1].
Using the normal approximation, there is evidence at the 5% level that the mean spend differs from 33.00 [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the s² line explicitly, and say that the conclusion is approximate.

**6.** H₀: μ = 5.0, H₁: μ > 5.0, assuming a normal population [1].
Σx = 32.8, so x̄ = 5.467 [1].
Σx² = 180.38, so s² = (180.38 − 32.8²/6)/5 = 0.2147 [1], and s = 0.4633.
Standard error estimate = 0.4633/√6 = 0.1892 [1].
t = (5.467 − 5.0)/0.1892 = 2.47 with 5 degrees of freedom [1].
2.47 > 2.015, so reject H₀. There is evidence at the 5% level that the new batch cures for longer than 5.0 hours [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Quote the degrees of freedom alongside the t value.

**7. (i)** **t statistic with 9 degrees of freedom** (normal, σ unknown, small sample) [1].
**(ii)** **z statistic**, (x̄ − μ₀)/(4/√15), compared with N(0, 1) [1].
**(iii)** **z statistic using s in place of σ**, approximate by the Central Limit Theorem because n is large [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Give a reason with each choice, such as "σ unknown and n small".

**8.** H₀: μ = 61, H₁: μ ≠ 61, assuming a normal population [1].
x̄ = 1047.6/18 = 58.2 [1].
s² = (61 400 − 1047.6²/18)/17 = 25.28 [1], so s = 5.027 and the standard error estimate is 5.027/√18 = 1.185.
t = (58.2 − 61)/1.185 = −2.36 [1].
−2.36 < −2.110, so it is in the critical region [1]: reject H₀. There is evidence at the 5% level that the mean differs from 61 g (the p-value is about 0.030) [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* For a negative statistic, state which tail it is in.

**9. (a)** Under H₀, X̄ ~ N(80, 5²/16), standard error 1.25 [1].
P(Type I error) = P(X̄ > 81.8 | μ = 80) = P(Z > 1.8/1.25) = P(Z > 1.44) [1].
= **0.0749** [1].
**(b)** Failing to reject H₀ (concluding the mean is not above 80) when the mean really is above 80 [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A Type I error probability is found assuming H₀ is true, so use μ = 80.

**10. (a)** Standard error estimate = 4.2/√10 = 1.328 [1].
t = (60.3 − 58)/1.328 = 1.73 [1], with 9 degrees of freedom [1].
**(b)** The population is normal and σ is unknown with n small, so use t [1]. 1.73 < 1.833, so do not reject H₀: insufficient evidence at the 5% level that the mean exceeds 58 [1].
**(c)** 1.73 > 1.645, so the student would reject H₀ [1]. This is inappropriate because s from 10 values is an unreliable estimate of σ [1], so the statistic follows a t-distribution with heavier tails, not N(0, 1) [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* When asked to explain, mention both that σ is estimated and that the sample is small.

**11. (a)** H₀: μ = 50, H₁: μ > 50 [1].
Standard error = 6/√16 = 1.5 [1].
z = (52.0 − 50)/1.5 = 1.333 [1].
1.333 < 1.645 (p-value 0.091) [1], so do not reject H₀: insufficient evidence at the 5% level that the mean mass exceeds 50 g [1].
**(b)** Require (52.0 − 50)/(6/√n) > 1.645 [1].
√n > 1.645 × 3 = 4.935 [1], so n > 24.35 [1].
Check: n = 24 gives z = 1.633 and n = 25 gives z = 1.667, so **n = 25** [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* With an inequality in n, round up to the next whole number and confirm with the neighbouring value.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using σ in place of the standard error σ/√n in the denominator.
- Calculating s² with divisor n instead of n − 1 from Σx and Σx².
- Taking the degrees of freedom as n rather than n − 1, or using z for a small sample with unknown variance.
- Giving a critical region in terms of z when the question asks for x̄.
- Comparing with a positive critical value in a lower-tail test.
- Concluding with "H₀ is true" or "the claim is proved", or omitting the context.
- Not stating the normality assumption for t, or the approximation for z with s.
