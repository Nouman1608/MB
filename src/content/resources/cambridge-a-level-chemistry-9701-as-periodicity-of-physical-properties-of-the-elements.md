This guide covers subtopic **9.1, Periodicity of physical properties of the elements in Period 3**, from Topic 9, The Periodic Table: chemical periodicity, of Cambridge International AS & A Level Chemistry 9701, 2025–2027 series. It is AS Level content.

The [Periodicity Across Period 3](/resources/as-periodicity-across-period-3/) guide covers all of Topic 9 together. This page stays with the four physical properties in 9.1 and goes further: the ionic radius pattern explained through isoelectronic ions, the electrical conductivity pattern in detail, how to show that the trends are *periodic* by comparing Period 2, and how to write the melting point explanation so that each structural change is credited. Condensed notes are in the [9.1 revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-chemistry-9701-as-periodicity-of-physical-properties-of-the-elements-revision-notes/), and the [9.1 practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-chemistry-9701-as-periodicity-of-physical-properties-of-the-elements-practice/) test it.

## What 9.1 asks you to do

1. Describe qualitatively (and indicate the periodicity in) the variations in **atomic radius**, **ionic radius**, **melting point** and **electrical conductivity** of the elements.
2. Explain the variation in melting point and electrical conductivity in terms of the **structure and bonding** of the elements.

"Qualitatively" means you describe the direction and shape of each trend and explain it. You are not expected to recall numerical radii, although you may be given data to interpret.

## Atomic radius

Atomic radius **decreases** from sodium to chlorine. Across the period:

- The **nuclear charge** increases by one proton per element.
- The outer electrons are added to the **same shell** (the third shell).
- **Shielding** by the inner shells stays roughly the same, because the number of inner electrons (ten) does not change.

So the outer electrons feel a steadily stronger attraction and are pulled closer to the nucleus. Argon is usually left out of the comparison. It forms no bonds, so its radius cannot be measured in the same way as the bonded radii of the other elements.

## Ionic radius: two isoelectronic series

Ionic radius does **not** follow one smooth trend. There are two separate groups of ions, with a large jump between them.

| Ions | Electron configuration | Protons | Pattern |
|---|---|---|---|
| Na⁺, Mg²⁺, Al³⁺ | 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ (10 electrons, **two** shells) | 11, 12, 13 | Radius decreases |
| P³⁻, S²⁻, Cl⁻ | 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ (18 electrons, **three** shells) | 15, 16, 17 | Radius decreases |

Within each series the ions are **isoelectronic**: they have the same number of electrons. The only difference is the nuclear charge. More protons pull the same electron cloud in more tightly, so the radius falls: Na⁺ > Mg²⁺ > Al³⁺, and P³⁻ > S²⁻ > Cl⁻.

The jump between the two series happens because the positive ions have **lost** their third shell entirely, while the negative ions have **gained** electrons into the third shell. Every anion has one more occupied shell than every cation, so the smallest anion, Cl⁻, is still much larger than the largest cation, Na⁺.

Silicon does not readily form a simple ion, so it is usually left out of ionic radius comparisons.

**Worked example.** *Explain why the sulfide ion, S²⁻, is larger than the chloride ion, Cl⁻, but the sodium ion, Na⁺, is larger than the magnesium ion, Mg²⁺.*

> "S²⁻ and Cl⁻ both have 18 electrons in three shells [1]. Chlorine has one more proton (17) than sulfur (16), so the nuclear attraction on the same number of electrons is greater and Cl⁻ is smaller [1]. Na⁺ and Mg²⁺ both have 10 electrons in two shells, and Mg²⁺ has the greater nuclear charge (12 compared with 11), so Mg²⁺ is smaller [1]."

The same argument (same electrons, more protons, smaller ion) answers both halves.

## Melting point: four structure types

The melting point rises from sodium to a maximum at silicon, then falls sharply to phosphorus and stays low to argon. The shape is explained by the change in structure, so the answer must name the structure **and** the particles, forces or bonds that must be overcome.

| Element(s) | Structure | What melting must overcome | Melting point |
|---|---|---|---|
| Na, Mg, Al | Giant metallic lattice | Metallic bonding: attraction between cations and delocalised electrons | Rises Na → Al |
| Si | Giant covalent (macromolecular) lattice | Many strong covalent bonds | Highest in the period |
| P₄, S₈, Cl₂ | Simple molecular | Weak id-id forces between molecules | Low |
| Ar | Monatomic | Weak id-id forces between atoms | Lowest |

**Why the melting point rises from Na to Al.** The cation charge increases (+1, +2, +3), the cation becomes smaller, and the number of delocalised electrons per atom increases (1, 2, 3). Each change strengthens the attraction between the cations and the sea of delocalised electrons.

**Why silicon is highest.** Each silicon atom is covalently bonded to four others in a three-dimensional lattice. Melting requires many strong covalent bonds to be broken.

**Why the drop after silicon is so large.** Phosphorus, sulfur and chlorine also contain strong covalent bonds, but these are **within** molecules. Melting separates whole molecules from each other, so only the weak forces **between** molecules are overcome. The covalent bonds are not broken.

**The order within the simple molecular elements** follows the number of electrons per particle: S₈ has 128, P₄ has 60, Cl₂ has 34 and Ar has 18. More electrons give a larger, more polarisable electron cloud, so stronger instantaneous dipole–induced dipole forces. The melting point order is therefore S₈ > P₄ > Cl₂ > Ar.

## Electrical conductivity

| Element(s) | Conductivity | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Na, Mg, Al | Good, increasing Na → Al | Delocalised electrons are free to move through the lattice; the number per atom rises from 1 to 3 |
| Si | Low: a **semiconductor** | No delocalised electrons in the lattice, but a small number of electrons can gain enough energy to move; conductivity increases as temperature rises |
| P, S, Cl, Ar | Effectively none (insulators) | All electrons are held in covalent bonds or in individual atoms; there are no mobile charge carriers |

Notice the contrast for silicon. A metal conducts **less** well as it gets hotter, but a semiconductor such as silicon conducts **better**, because more electrons gain enough energy to become mobile.

## Showing the periodicity

"Periodicity" means the pattern repeats in each period. Period 2 shows the same shape of melting point trend:

- Lithium and beryllium are metals (giant metallic lattices).
- Boron and carbon form giant covalent lattices, and carbon has the highest melting point in the period.
- Nitrogen, oxygen and fluorine are simple molecular (N₂, O₂, F₂) and neon is monatomic, all with very low melting points.

So in both periods the melting point rises across the metals, peaks at the Group 14 giant covalent element, and falls sharply for the simple molecular and monatomic elements. Atomic radius likewise decreases across each period and then jumps up at the start of the next.

**Worked example: an annotated model answer.** *Describe and explain the variation in melting point of the elements from sodium to argon.* Each bracketed [1] marks a creditworthy point.

> "From Na to Al the melting point increases because these metals have giant metallic lattices [1] and the metallic bonding gets stronger as the ions get a larger charge and the number of delocalised electrons per atom increases [1]. Silicon has the highest melting point because it has a giant covalent structure [1] in which many strong covalent bonds must be broken [1]. Phosphorus, sulfur, chlorine and argon have low melting points because they are simple molecular (argon is monatomic) and only weak id-id forces between the particles are overcome [1]. Sulfur is highest of these because S₈ has the most electrons, giving the strongest id-id forces [1]."

## Common mistakes

- **"The melting point falls after silicon because the covalent bonds get weaker."** The bonds are not broken. The structure changes to simple molecular.
- **"Phosphorus has a low melting point because it has weak covalent bonds."** P–P bonds are strong; it is the forces between P₄ molecules that are weak.
- **Treating ionic radius as one trend from Na⁺ to Cl⁻.** It is two isoelectronic series with a jump between them.
- **Explaining the ionic radius jump by nuclear charge.** The jump is caused by the extra occupied shell in the anions.
- **Saying silicon conducts because it is "a metalloid".** Explain in terms of electrons that gain enough energy to move.
- **Forgetting "between molecules"** when naming id-id forces.

## Exam technique: command words in 9.1

- **Describe**: state the direction of each trend and where it changes ("increases from Na to Al, maximum at Si, falls sharply to P").
- **Explain**: name the structure, the particles, and the bonds or forces overcome. Compare strength.
- **Suggest**: apply the same reasoning to an unfamiliar element or period, such as Period 2.
- **Interpret** (data): quote values from the data and link each change to a structural reason.
