## 5.2.2(a): Entropy in one paragraph

**Entropy, S**: a measure of the dispersal of energy in a system. The more disordered the system, the greater its entropy. Units: **J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹**. Standard entropies are quoted at 298 K and are positive for pure substances. Elements have non-zero S values, unlike their ∆fH values, which are zero.

## 5.2.2(b): Which has more entropy?

**(i) States.** For one substance: **solid < liquid < gas**.

| State | Particle picture | Entropy |
|---|---|---|
| Solid | Vibrate about fixed lattice positions | Low |
| Liquid | Move past each other, no fixed positions | Higher |
| Gas | Fast, random motion through the whole volume | Much higher |

**(ii) Gas moles.** Count gaseous molecules on each side.

- Products have more gas molecules: ∆S **positive**.
- Products have fewer gas molecules: ∆S **negative**.
- Equal numbers: ∆S small; use data to decide.

Ignore solids and liquids in the count, but only after you have checked every state symbol.

## 5.2.2(c): Calculating ∆S

**∆S = ΣS(products) − ΣS(reactants)**

Method in steps:

1. Write each S value next to its species in the equation.
2. Multiply by the balancing number.
3. Add the products; add the reactants.
4. Subtract: products minus reactants.
5. Give a sign and units, J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹.

**Related quantities.** If ∆S is given and one S value is missing, write the full expression with the unknown as a letter, then rearrange. Divide by its balancing number at the end.

## 5.2.2(d) and (e): Feasibility and ∆G

**∆G = ∆H − T∆S**. A process is **feasible when ∆G is negative**.

| Symbol | Meaning | Usual units |
|---|---|---|
| ∆G | Free energy change | kJ mol⁻¹ |
| ∆H | Enthalpy change of the system | kJ mol⁻¹ |
| T | Temperature | K (never °C) |
| ∆S | Entropy change of the system | J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹, so divide by 1000 to use with kJ |
| T at ∆G = 0 | Threshold temperature | T = ∆H ÷ ∆S, both in kJ units |

Feasibility depends on two terms: ∆H, and T∆S. A negative ∆H helps. A positive ∆S helps more and more as T rises.

| ∆H | ∆S | ∆G against T | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| − | + | always below zero | Feasible at all T |
| + | − | always above zero | Never feasible |
| − | − | rises as T rises | Feasible below the threshold T |
| + | + | falls as T rises | Feasible above the threshold T |

**Graph reminder.** ∆G against T is a straight line (if ∆H and ∆S stay roughly constant): intercept ∆H, gradient −∆S.

Method in steps for ∆G:

1. Find ∆H (given, or from ∆fH data with products minus reactants).
2. Find ∆S and divide by 1000.
3. Convert T to kelvin.
4. Substitute: ∆G = ∆H − T∆S. Watch the sign when ∆S is negative.
5. State the conclusion: negative means feasible.

Method for a threshold temperature:

1. Set ∆G = 0, so T = ∆H ÷ ∆S.
2. Use the four-case table to decide whether the process is feasible above or below this T.

### Worked reminder: steam reforming of methane

CH₄(g) + H₂O(g) → CO(g) + 3H₂(g), ∆H = +206 kJ mol⁻¹.
Use S values (J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹): CH₄(g) 186.3, H₂O(g) 188.8, CO(g) 197.7, H₂(g) 130.7.

- ∆S = [197.7 + 3(130.7)] − [186.3 + 188.8] = 589.8 − 375.1 = **+214.7 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹** (2 mol gas become 4 mol)
- ∆G at 298 K = 206 − 298 × 0.2147 = **+142.0 kJ mol⁻¹**: not feasible
- Threshold T = 206 ÷ 0.2147 = **959 K**
- At 1100 K, ∆G = 206 − 236.2 = **−30.2 kJ mol⁻¹**: feasible

Both ∆H and ∆S are positive, so the reaction is feasible only above about 959 K.

## 5.2.2(f): Kinetic limits

- ∆G tells you whether a reaction **can** happen, not how fast.
- A reaction with negative ∆G but a large activation energy can be too slow to observe. Examples: diamond to graphite; fuels such as methane sitting in air until ignited.
- A catalyst changes the rate only. ∆H, ∆S and ∆G stay the same.
- When estimating an outcome, give the ∆G verdict, then comment on rate.

## Must-know distinctions

- **S versus ∆S**: S belongs to one substance; ∆S belongs to a reaction.
- **Feasible versus fast**: thermodynamics versus kinetics.
- **J versus kJ**: entropy data are in J; enthalpy data are in kJ.
- **Threshold temperature versus "reaction starts"**: at the threshold, ∆G = 0. Feasibility begins on one side of it.
- **∆fH(element) = 0, S(element) ≠ 0**: elements count in entropy sums.

## Quick self-test

1. Which has the greater entropy, 1 mol of liquid water or 1 mol of steam at the same temperature? Explain.
2. Predict the sign of ∆S for 3C₂H₂(g) → C₆H₆(l), with a reason.
3. Predict the sign of ∆S for 2KNO₃(s) → 2KNO₂(s) + O₂(g), with a reason.
4. Calculate ∆S for N₂(g) + O₂(g) → 2NO(g). S values: N₂ 191.6, O₂ 205.2, NO 210.8 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹.
5. Calculate ∆S for 2O₃(g) → 3O₂(g). S values: O₃ 238.9, O₂ 205.2 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹.
6. A reaction has ∆H = −96 kJ mol⁻¹ and ∆S = −204 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹. Find ∆G at 298 K and the temperature range over which it is feasible.
7. A reaction has ∆H = +66 kJ mol⁻¹ and ∆S = +172 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹. Is it feasible at 298 K? Find the minimum temperature for feasibility.
8. A process has ∆G = 0 at 520 K and ∆S = +135 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹. Find ∆H.
9. Which sign combination of ∆H and ∆S is never feasible?
10. Hydrogen and oxygen can be stored mixed at room temperature without reacting, although ∆G for forming water is negative. Explain.
11. Does adding a catalyst change ∆G? Explain.

### Answers

1. Steam. Gas particles move randomly through the whole volume, so energy is far more dispersed than in the liquid.
2. Negative: 3 mol of gas become a liquid, so disorder falls.
3. Positive: 1 mol of gas forms from solids only.
4. ∆S = 2(210.8) − (191.6 + 205.2) = 421.6 − 396.8 = **+24.8 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹**
5. ∆S = 3(205.2) − 2(238.9) = 615.6 − 477.8 = **+137.8 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹**
6. ∆G = −96 − 298 × (−0.204) = −96 + 60.8 = **−35.2 kJ mol⁻¹** (feasible). T = −96 ÷ −0.204 = 471 K, so feasible **below about 471 K**.
7. ∆G = 66 − 298 × 0.172 = **+14.7 kJ mol⁻¹**, so not feasible. Minimum T = 66 ÷ 0.172 = **384 K**.
8. ∆H = T∆S = 520 × 0.135 = **+70.2 kJ mol⁻¹**
9. ∆H positive and ∆S negative: both terms make ∆G positive at every temperature.
10. The activation energy is high, so the rate at room temperature is negligible. A spark supplies the energy needed to start the reaction.
11. No. A catalyst lowers the activation energy and changes the rate, but ∆H, ∆S and so ∆G are unchanged.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Substituting ∆S in J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ alongside ∆H in kJ mol⁻¹.
- Using 25 instead of 298 for T.
- Missing O₂(g), H₂(g) or another element from the entropy sum.
- Not multiplying S values by balancing numbers.
- Writing reactants minus products for ∆S.
- Dropping the sign of ∆S, so −T∆S has the wrong sign.
- Saying "feasible above" when ∆H and ∆S are both negative.
- Stating that a negative ∆G means a reaction is fast.
- Explaining entropy as "chaos" with no reference to how energy or particles are dispersed.
