This guide covers subtopic **23.2, Enthalpies of solution and hydration**, from Topic 23,
Chemical energetics, of Cambridge International AS & A Level Chemistry 9701, 2025–2027
series. It is A Level content. The broader
[Lattice Energy, Entropy and Gibbs Free Energy guide](/resources/a-chemical-energetics-lattice-energy-entropy-and-gibbs-free-energy/)
introduces the solution cycle with sodium chloride; this page goes further, with 2+ ions,
rearranging the cycle for each unknown, and the reasoning examiners expect when you compare
ions.

## What the syllabus asks for

The four learning outcomes for 23.2 are:

1. define and use the terms enthalpy change of hydration, ΔH<sub>hyd</sub>, and enthalpy
   change of solution, ΔH<sub>sol</sub>;
2. construct and use an energy cycle involving ΔH<sub>sol</sub>, lattice energy and
   ΔH<sub>hyd</sub>;
3. carry out calculations involving that cycle;
4. explain, in qualitative terms, the effect of ionic charge and ionic radius on the
   numerical magnitude of ΔH<sub>hyd</sub>.

Lattice energy itself belongs to 23.1, but you cannot use the solution cycle without it, so
its definition is repeated where needed below.

## The two definitions, word by word

**Enthalpy change of hydration, ΔH<sub>hyd</sub>:** the enthalpy change when **one mole of
gaseous ions** dissolves in **sufficient water** to give an **infinitely dilute solution**.
For example, Na⁺(g) → Na⁺(aq). The equation must start from the gaseous ion; writing
Na⁺(s) or NaCl(s) on the left loses the mark.

**Enthalpy change of solution, ΔH<sub>sol</sub>:** the enthalpy change when **one mole of an
ionic solid** dissolves in **sufficient water** to give an **infinitely dilute solution**.
For example, MgCl₂(s) → Mg²⁺(aq) + 2Cl⁻(aq).

"Infinitely dilute" means that adding more water causes no further enthalpy change, so the
ions are far enough apart not to interact with each other. Both values are quoted per mole
of the species named: ΔH<sub>hyd</sub> per mole of ions, ΔH<sub>sol</sub> per mole of the
compound.

**Why hydration is always exothermic.** Water is a polar molecule. Around a cation, water
molecules turn so that their partially negative oxygen atoms face the ion; around an anion,
they turn so that their partially positive hydrogen atoms face it. These ion–dipole
attractions form where there were none in the gas, so energy is released. There is no bond
breaking to offset it, which is why every ΔH<sub>hyd</sub> is negative.

**Why ΔH<sub>sol</sub> can be either sign.** Dissolving a solid involves an endothermic step
(separating the lattice into ions) and an exothermic step (hydrating those ions). The sign
of ΔH<sub>sol</sub> depends on which is larger.

## Building the energy cycle

Draw three levels:

- top: the separated gaseous ions, e.g. Mg²⁺(g) + 2Cl⁻(g);
- one side: the ionic solid, MgCl₂(s);
- other side: the aqueous ions, Mg²⁺(aq) + 2Cl⁻(aq).

The arrow from gaseous ions down to the solid is the **lattice energy**, ΔH<sub>latt</sub>
(exothermic). The arrow from gaseous ions down to the aqueous ions is the **sum of the
hydration enthalpies** of every ion in the formula. The direct arrow from solid to aqueous
ions is **ΔH<sub>sol</sub>**.

By Hess's law, going solid → gaseous ions → aqueous ions must give the same total as going
directly. Solid → gaseous ions is the reverse of lattice energy, so its sign is changed:

ΔH<sub>sol</sub> = −ΔH<sub>latt</sub> + ΣΔH<sub>hyd</sub>(ions)

The sum must count each ion as many times as it appears in the formula: for MgCl₂,
ΣΔH<sub>hyd</sub> = ΔH<sub>hyd</sub>(Mg²⁺) + **2** × ΔH<sub>hyd</sub>(Cl⁻).

Rearranged for the other two unknowns:

- ΔH<sub>latt</sub> = ΣΔH<sub>hyd</sub> − ΔH<sub>sol</sub>
- ΔH<sub>hyd</sub>(one ion) = ΔH<sub>sol</sub> + ΔH<sub>latt</sub> − ΔH<sub>hyd</sub>(the other ions)

Rather than memorising the rearrangements, write the cycle equation first and substitute,
keeping every sign in brackets.

## Worked example 1: a 1:1 salt that dissolves exothermically

*Data supplied for this example (values differ slightly between data sources):*
ΔH<sub>latt</sub>(KF) = −821 kJ mol⁻¹; ΔH<sub>hyd</sub>(K⁺) = −322 kJ mol⁻¹;
ΔH<sub>hyd</sub>(F⁻) = −506 kJ mol⁻¹. Calculate ΔH<sub>sol</sub>(KF).

ΔH<sub>sol</sub> = −(−821) + (−322) + (−506)

ΔH<sub>sol</sub> = +821 − 828 = **−7 kJ mol⁻¹**

The fluoride ion is small, so its hydration enthalpy is large enough to outweigh the energy
needed to break the lattice. Compare sodium chloride, where the same two terms leave a small
positive value: in both cases the answer is a small difference between two large numbers,
which is why ΔH<sub>sol</sub> values for 1:1 salts cluster close to zero.

## Worked example 2: finding a hydration enthalpy for a 2+ ion

*Data supplied:* ΔH<sub>sol</sub>(MgCl₂) = −155 kJ mol⁻¹;
ΔH<sub>latt</sub>(MgCl₂) = −2526 kJ mol⁻¹; ΔH<sub>hyd</sub>(Cl⁻) = −364 kJ mol⁻¹.
Calculate ΔH<sub>hyd</sub>(Mg²⁺).

Write the cycle equation:

ΔH<sub>sol</sub> = −ΔH<sub>latt</sub> + ΔH<sub>hyd</sub>(Mg²⁺) + 2ΔH<sub>hyd</sub>(Cl⁻)

Substitute:

−155 = +2526 + ΔH<sub>hyd</sub>(Mg²⁺) + 2(−364)

−155 = +2526 − 728 + ΔH<sub>hyd</sub>(Mg²⁺)

ΔH<sub>hyd</sub>(Mg²⁺) = −155 − 1798 = **−1953 kJ mol⁻¹**

Three checks an examiner would make: the chloride term was doubled; the lattice energy sign
was reversed; and the answer is negative and much larger than any 1+ ion's value, which is
what charge and radius arguments predict.

## Charge and radius: explaining the size of ΔH<sub>hyd</sub>

The magnitude of ΔH<sub>hyd</sub> depends on how strongly the ion attracts the water dipoles
around it. That attraction is stronger when:

- **the ionic charge is larger**, because a greater charge attracts each dipole more strongly;
- **the ionic radius is smaller**, because water molecules can approach the centre of the
  ion more closely.

The two effects combine in the idea of **charge density** (charge relative to size). A
complete exam answer names the factor, says which way it changes the attraction, and links
that to energy released. For example:

> Mg²⁺ has a more exothermic hydration enthalpy than Na⁺. Mg²⁺ has a greater charge **and**
> a smaller ionic radius, so it has a higher charge density. It attracts the δ⁻ oxygen atoms
> of water molecules more strongly, so more energy is released when it is hydrated.

Patterns worth knowing, all following from this reasoning:

| Comparison | More exothermic ΔH<sub>hyd</sub> | Main reason |
|---|---|---|
| Li⁺, Na⁺, K⁺ (down Group 1) | Li⁺ | same charge, smallest radius |
| Mg²⁺, Ca²⁺, Ba²⁺ (down Group 2) | Mg²⁺ | same charge, smallest radius |
| Na⁺, Mg²⁺, Al³⁺ (across Period 3) | Al³⁺ | greater charge and smaller radius |
| F⁻, Cl⁻, Br⁻, I⁻ (down Group 17) | F⁻ | same charge, smallest radius |

When charge and size work in opposite directions, the syllabus expects only qualitative
reasoning, so state both effects rather than claiming one wins unless data are given.

## Using the cycle to discuss trends

Lattice energy and hydration enthalpy respond to charge and radius in the same direction.
That is why they often partly cancel in the solution cycle. When an examiner asks why
ΔH<sub>sol</sub> changes down a group, compare **how fast** each term changes: if the
lattice energy becomes less exothermic more slowly than the hydration enthalpies do,
ΔH<sub>sol</sub> becomes more endothermic, and vice versa. This is the argument used in
Topic 27 for the solubility of Group 2 hydroxides and sulfates; see
[Group 2: Quantitative Trends](/resources/a-group-2-quantitative-trends/) for that
application.

A large positive ΔH<sub>sol</sub> usually goes with low solubility, but ΔH<sub>sol</sub>
alone does not decide whether a solid dissolves: the entropy change and ΔG (subtopics 23.3
and 23.4) also matter. Avoid writing that an endothermic ΔH<sub>sol</sub> means a salt is
insoluble.

## Common mistakes

- **Starting the hydration equation from the solid.** ΔH<sub>hyd</sub> starts from gaseous
  ions; only ΔH<sub>sol</sub> starts from the solid.
- **Forgetting to multiply.** In CaCl₂, Al₂(SO₄)₃ or any formula with more than one of an
  ion, the hydration term must be multiplied by the number of those ions.
- **Not reversing the lattice energy.** Lattice energy is defined from gaseous ions to solid,
  so the dissolving route uses −ΔH<sub>latt</sub>.
- **Saying hydration releases energy because "bonds are broken".** Hydration forms ion–dipole
  attractions; nothing is broken.
- **Describing water orientation the wrong way round.** Oxygen (δ⁻) faces a cation; hydrogen
  (δ⁺) faces an anion.
- **Using "size" vaguely.** Say "ionic radius" and specify the ion.

## Exam technique

- **Define**: give the full definition with "one mole", "gaseous ions" (for hydration) and
  "infinitely dilute solution". A state-symbol equation alongside earns credit if your words
  slip.
- **Construct** or **draw** an energy cycle: label every species with state symbols and every
  arrow with its enthalpy term; the arrow direction must match the definition.
- **Calculate**: show the cycle equation before substituting, give the sign and units, and
  round to the precision of the data.
- **Explain**: link charge or radius → strength of attraction to water dipoles → energy
  released. Two linked steps usually carry two marks.
- **Suggest**: apply the same reasoning to an unfamiliar ion and say which factor you are
  using.
