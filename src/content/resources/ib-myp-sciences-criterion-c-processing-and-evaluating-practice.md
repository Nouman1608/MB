## The investigation

A student asked: *How does the temperature of water affect the time a sugar cube takes to dissolve?* The hypothesis was: "If the water temperature is increased, then the time for a sugar cube to dissolve will decrease, because the water particles have more kinetic energy and collide with the cube more often." The student used 150 cm³ of water, one sugar cube from the same box each time, the same beaker and no stirring. Each temperature was tested three times. The time was measured with a stopwatch to the nearest second.

| Water temperature (°C) | Trial 1 (s) | Trial 2 (s) | Trial 3 (s) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 15 | 212 | 208 | 215 |
| 25 | 131 | 128 | 134 |
| 35 | 79 | 83 | 81 |
| 45 | 52 | 98 | 50 |
| 55 | 30 | 33 | 31 |

## Questions

**1.** (Criterion C) State what is meant by an *anomaly* in a set of results. **[1]**

**2.** (Criterion C) Identify the anomalous result in the table and explain how you know. **[2]**

**3.** (Criterion C) Calculate the mean time for each temperature, leaving out the anomaly. Give each mean to three significant figures. **[5]**

**4.** (Criterion C) Calculate the percentage decrease in the mean time between 15 °C and 55 °C. Use your answers to Question 3. **[2]**

**5.** (Criterion C) Calculate the rate of dissolving at 25 °C, using rate = 1000 ÷ mean time. Give the answer in units of 10⁻³ s⁻¹. **[2]**

**6.** (Criterion C) Describe how the student should present the mean results on a graph. **[3]**

**7.** (Criterion C) Interpret the results and explain them using scientific reasoning. **[4]**

**8.** (Criterion C) Evaluate the validity of the hypothesis, using the data. **[4]**

**9.** (Criterion C) Evaluate the validity of the method. Give three limitations and, for each one, state the effect on the results. **[6]**

**10.** (Criterion C) Explain two improvements the student could make to the method. **[4]**

**11.** (Criterion C) Suggest one extension to this investigation and explain why it follows from the results. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** An anomaly is a result that does not fit the pattern of the other results (it is much further from the other repeats than they are from each other) [1]. **[1]**

**2.** The 98 s result at 45 °C (trial 2) [1]. The other two trials at 45 °C are 52 s and 50 s, and the repeats at every other temperature differ by 7 s or less, so 98 s is far outside the spread [1]. **[2]**

**3.** 15 °C: (212 + 208 + 215) ÷ 3 = 211.67, so **212 s** [1].
25 °C: (131 + 128 + 134) ÷ 3 = **131 s** [1].
35 °C: (79 + 83 + 81) ÷ 3 = **81.0 s** [1].
45 °C: (52 + 50) ÷ 2 = **51.0 s**, with 98 s left out [1].
55 °C: (30 + 33 + 31) ÷ 3 = 31.33, so **31.3 s** [1]. **[5]**
*Marking note:* The 45 °C mean is only correct if the anomaly is excluded. Including it gives 66.7 s.

**4.** Decrease = 212 − 31.3 = 180.7 s [1]. Percentage decrease = 180.7 ÷ 212 × 100 = **85.2%** [1]. **[2]**
*Marking note:* Use of the unrounded means (211.67 and 31.33) also gives 85.2%.

**5.** Rate = 1000 ÷ 131 [1] = **7.63** (×10⁻³ s⁻¹) [1]. **[2]**

**6.** Plot the independent variable (temperature in °C) on the horizontal axis and the mean time (s) on the vertical axis, both labelled with units [1]. Use even scales that fill more than half of the grid, and plot the five mean values accurately [1]. Draw a smooth best-fit curve through the points (not a series of ruled lines), and give the graph a title [1]. **[3]**

**7.** As the temperature rises from 15 °C to 55 °C, the mean time to dissolve falls from 212 s to 31.3 s [1]. The time falls by 81 s from 15 °C to 35 °C and by 49.7 s from 35 °C to 55 °C, so the decrease is steep at first and then smaller [1]. At higher temperatures the water particles have more kinetic energy and move faster [1]. They collide with the sugar cube more often and with more energy, so molecules of sugar leave the cube faster and the cube dissolves in a shorter time [1]. **[4]**
*Marking note:* The difference 212 − 81.0 is 131 s... see the calculation below: from 15 °C to 35 °C the fall is 212 − 81.0 = 131 s, and from 35 °C to 55 °C it is 81.0 − 31.3 = 49.7 s. Any consistent correct comparison of the size of the decreases earns the second point.

**8.** The data support the hypothesis, because the mean time decreased at every increase in temperature [1]. Evidence: 212 s at 15 °C against 31.3 s at 55 °C, about 85% less [1]. The reasoning (more kinetic energy, more collisions) is consistent with the pattern [1]. Limits: the conclusion applies only between 15 °C and 55 °C, and the anomaly at 45 °C and the small number of values mean the exact shape of the relationship is uncertain [1]. **[4]**

**9.** Any three limitations, each with an effect (1 + 1 for each pair).
- Human timing and judging when the cube has gone: the reaction time of 0.2 to 0.5 s is a larger percentage of 31 s than of 212 s, so the fastest results are less accurate [1] [1].
- Water temperature changed during the run, since water at 15 °C warms and at 55 °C cools: the actual temperature was not the one recorded, so the points are slightly misplaced [1] [1].
- The sugar cubes may differ in size or mass: part of the spread in repeats may be due to the cubes and not to temperature, as shown by the 98 s anomaly [1] [1].
- Only five temperatures were tested: the shape of the curve between points is not known [1] [1].
- No stirring and the cube may sit against the side of the beaker, so the dissolving is uneven: this adds random variation to the results [1] [1].
**[6]**

**10.** Any two improvements, each with what, how and why (2 marks each).
- Weigh each sugar cube and use ones of the same mass [1], because mass affects the time, so the cubes would be a fair comparison [1].
- Use a thermometer at the start and end of each run and an insulated beaker [1], so that the temperature drift is small and the temperature is known [1].
- Repeat the 45 °C measurement three more times [1], because one anomaly left only two usable results there [1].
- Have a second person watch the end point or use a video recording [1], to reduce the effect of judging when the cube has gone [1].
**[4]**

**11.** A suitable extension, for example: does crushing the cube into granules change the time at each temperature? [1] It follows because the results suggest that collisions with the surface control the rate [1], and crushing increases the surface area available for collisions, so the idea can be tested [1]. **[3]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Including the anomaly in the mean without comment.
- Rounding means to different precisions.
- Joining plotted points with straight lines.
- Describing the pattern without numbers or without a scientific reason.
- Writing "human error" without saying what it was or what effect it had.
- Saying the results "prove" the hypothesis.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why leave out the anomaly?**
It does not fit the pattern and would distort the mean. State that you left it out and give a possible reason.

**How many decimal places should the means have?**
Be consistent. Three significant figures is the usual convention, and the same number of decimal places down a column makes a table easier to read.

**How do I get full marks for an evaluation?**
Give a verdict, evidence from the data, and a limit. For the method, link each limitation to its effect on the results.
