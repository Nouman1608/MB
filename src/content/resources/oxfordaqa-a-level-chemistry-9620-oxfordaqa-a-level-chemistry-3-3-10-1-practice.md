This set practises **3.3.10.1 Bonding** in benzene from the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Chemistry (9620) specification, Version 5.3. It is International A2 content, assessed in Unit 4 and in the synoptic Unit 5. If you get stuck, go back to the [bonding in benzene study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-3-10-1/) or the [bonding in benzene revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-3-10-1-revision-notes/). Use Ar values H = 1.0 and C = 12.0, and R = 8.31 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹.

## Section A: short questions

**1.** Which describes the shape of a benzene molecule?

A: planar, with C–C–C bond angles of 109.5°

B: planar, with C–C–C bond angles of 120°

C: non-planar, with C–C–C bond angles of 120°

D: planar, with C–C–C bond angles of 90°

**[1]**

**2.** State the number of atoms that lie in the same plane in a molecule of benzene. **[1]**

**3.** Compare the length of a carbon–carbon bond in benzene with the length of the C=C bond in cyclohexene, and explain the difference. **[2]**

**4.** Describe how the p orbitals on the carbon atoms of benzene give rise to delocalised electrons. **[3]**

**5.** A few drops of bromine water are shaken separately with cyclohexene and with benzene at room temperature. State what is seen in each case. **[2]**

## Section B: structured questions

**6.** Data: cyclohexene + H₂ → cyclohexane, ΔH = −120 kJ mol⁻¹; benzene + 3H₂ → cyclohexane, ΔH = −208 kJ mol⁻¹.

**(a)** Write an equation, using molecular formulae, for the hydrogenation of benzene. **[1]**

**(b)** Predict ΔH for the hydrogenation of cyclohexa-1,3,5-triene. **[1]**

**(c)** Use your answer to (b) to deduce how much more stable benzene is than cyclohexa-1,3,5-triene, and state the cause. **[2]**

**(d)** Describe an energy level diagram that shows these data. Give the positions of the levels and the labels. **[2]**

**7.** Mean bond enthalpies (kJ mol⁻¹): C=C 612, C–C 348, H–H 436, C–H 412.

**(a)** Calculate ΔH for hydrogenating one C=C bond. **[2]**

**(b)** Use your answer to predict ΔH for the hydrogenation of cyclohexa-1,3,5-triene. **[1]**

**(c)** The measured value for benzene is −208 kJ mol⁻¹. Calculate the stabilisation of benzene using this method, and explain why it differs from your answer to question 6(c). **[2]**

**8.** Data: cyclohexa-1,3-diene + 2H₂ → cyclohexane, ΔH = −232 kJ mol⁻¹. Use this together with the data in question 6.

**(a)** Calculate ΔH for cyclohexa-1,3-diene + H₂ → cyclohexene. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate ΔH for benzene + H₂ → cyclohexa-1,3-diene. **[1]**

**(c)** Explain how your answer to (b) helps to account for benzene reacting by substitution rather than addition. **[2]**

**9.** A sample of 3.12 g of benzene is completely hydrogenated to cyclohexane.

**(a)** Calculate the volume of hydrogen, in dm³, needed at 298 K and 100 kPa. **[2]**

**(b)** Use ΔH = −208 kJ mol⁻¹ to calculate the heat released. **[2]**

**10.** Give **two** pieces of evidence that benzene is not cyclohexa-1,3,5-triene. For each, say what the triene model would predict and what is actually found. **[4]**

**11.** A student writes: "Benzene is so stable that it does not react with anything." Evaluate this statement. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** **B** [1]. Each carbon has three bonding pairs, which repel to 120° in a plane.

**2.** 12 (six C and six H) [1]

**3.** The bond in benzene is longer than C=C (but shorter than C–C) [1]; the six delocalised electrons are shared between six bonds, so each bond has less electron density between the carbons than a localised double bond [1]

**4.** Each carbon has one electron in a p orbital at right angles to the plane of the ring [1]; each p orbital overlaps sideways with the p orbitals on both neighbouring carbons [1]; this gives a ring of six delocalised electrons above and below the plane [1]

**5.** Cyclohexene: orange (or yellow-brown) to colourless [1]; benzene: stays orange [1]

*Examiner insight:* "no reaction" is not an observation; say the colour stays.

**6. (a)** C₆H₆ + 3H₂ → C₆H₁₂ [1]

**(b)** 3 × (−120) = −360 kJ mol⁻¹ [1]

**(c)** 360 − 208 = 152 kJ mol⁻¹ [1]; benzene is more stable because of delocalisation of its p electrons [1]

**(d)** Cyclohexane at the bottom; benzene + 3H₂ 208 kJ mol⁻¹ above it, with the triene + 3H₂ 360 kJ mol⁻¹ above it [1]; downward arrows labelled with each ΔH and the 152 kJ mol⁻¹ gap labelled as the extra stability [1]

**7. (a)** Broken: 612 + 436 = 1048; made: 348 + 2(412) = 1172 [1]; ΔH = 1048 − 1172 = **−124 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]

**(b)** 3 × (−124) = **−372 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]

**(c)** 372 − 208 = 164 kJ mol⁻¹ [1]; mean bond enthalpies are averages over many compounds, not values for these particular bonds [1]

**8. (a)** −232 − (−120) = **−112 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]

**(b)** −208 − (−232) = **+24 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]

**(c)** Adding H₂ across one bond of benzene is endothermic, while adding it to the diene or to an alkene is exothermic [1]; this is because addition destroys the delocalised ring and loses its extra stability, so benzene avoids addition and takes part in substitution, which keeps the ring intact [1]

*Examiner insight:* in (a) and (b), set up the Hess cycle with cyclohexane as the common product; subtracting the wrong way round gives the right size with the wrong sign, and the sign is the point of (c).

**9. (a)** n(benzene) = 3.12 ÷ 78.0 = 0.0400 mol; n(H₂) = 0.120 mol [1]; V = nRT ÷ p = 0.120 × 8.31 × 298 ÷ 100 000 = 2.97 × 10⁻³ m³ = **2.97 dm³** [1]

**(b)** 0.0400 mol [1] × 208 kJ mol⁻¹ = **8.32 kJ** released [1]

**10.** Any two pieces of evidence, each with prediction [1] and finding [1]:

- Bond lengths: the triene would have two bond lengths (about 134 pm and 154 pm) and an irregular hexagon; benzene has six equal bonds of about 139 pm, a regular hexagon.
- Hydrogenation: the triene would release about three times the value for cyclohexene; benzene releases much less, so it is more stable.
- Reactivity: the triene would add bromine readily like an alkene; benzene does not decolourise bromine water and reacts mainly by substitution.

**11.** The statement is incorrect: benzene does react, by electrophilic substitution, with strong electrophiles such as NO₂⁺ [1]; "stable" means lower in energy than cyclohexa-1,3,5-triene, by about 150 kJ mol⁻¹, because of delocalisation [1]; this stability explains why benzene reacts in ways that keep the delocalised ring (substitution) and resists addition, not that it is completely unreactive [1]

*Examiner insight:* an evaluation needs a judgement and reasons; correcting the claim and then explaining what "stable" means scores more than listing facts about benzene.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Saying benzene "releases more energy" and is therefore more stable.
- Quoting a stability value without naming the more stable molecule.
- Placing delocalised electrons in the plane of the ring.
- Writing "no reaction" instead of an observation in the bromine water test.
- Sign errors in Hess's law steps.
