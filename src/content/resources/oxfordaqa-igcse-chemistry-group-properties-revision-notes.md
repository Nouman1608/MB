## The one-line summary

Group 1 metals lose one electron and get **more** reactive going down. Group 7 non-metals gain one electron and get **less** reactive going down. Both trends come from the outer electrons sitting in a higher energy level further down a group.

## Group 1, the alkali metals (3.7.1 a and b)

| Property | What to remember |
|---|---|
| Outer electrons | One |
| Density | Low for metals; Li, Na and K are less dense than water and float |
| Ion formed | M⁺ (charge +1) |
| Compounds with non-metals | Ionic; white solids; dissolve to give colourless solutions |
| With water | Release hydrogen; form the metal hydroxide |
| Hydroxide solution | Alkaline (pH above 7), because it contains OH⁻ ions |
| Trend down the group | More reactive |

**Equations to be able to write**

```
2M + 2H₂O → 2MOH + H₂          any Group 1 metal with water
2M + X₂  → 2MX                 with a halogen (X = Cl, Br, I)
4M + O₂  → 2M₂O                with oxygen
```

**Water reaction, side by side**

| Lithium | Sodium | Potassium |
|---|---|---|
| Floats, steady fizzing | Floats, melts into a ball, moves about quickly | Floats, hydrogen ignites with a lilac flame |

Further down means faster and more violent, but the same products.

**Test for the gas:** a lighted splint placed near hydrogen gives a pop.

### Method in steps: formula of a Group 1 compound

1. Write the ions: M⁺ and the non-metal ion (Cl⁻, Br⁻, O²⁻, S²⁻ ...).
2. Balance charges to zero: one M⁺ per 1− ion, two per 2− ion.
3. Write the formula: RbCl, Na₂O, K₂S.
4. In an equation, write the non-metal element as a molecule where it is diatomic (Cl₂, O₂).

**Worked reminder.** 2.3 g of sodium burns in chlorine. Moles of Na = 2.3 ÷ 23 = 0.10 mol. 2Na + Cl₂ → 2NaCl, so 0.10 mol NaCl. Mr NaCl = 58.5, so mass = **5.85 g**.

## Group 7, the halogens (3.7.1 c and d)

| Property | What to remember |
|---|---|
| Outer electrons | Seven |
| Form of the element | Diatomic molecules: F₂, Cl₂, Br₂, I₂ |
| With metals | Ionic compounds containing halide ions with charge 1− |
| Ion names | fluoride, chloride, bromide, iodide |
| Reactivity down the group | Decreases |
| Melting and boiling points down the group | Increase |

**States at room temperature**

| Fluorine | Chlorine | Bromine | Iodine |
|---|---|---|---|
| pale yellow gas | pale yellow-green gas | red-brown liquid | grey-black solid |

Gas to liquid to solid matches rising boiling points. Bigger molecules lower in the group have stronger forces between molecules.

**Metal halide formulae.** Balance the metal ion's charge with enough 1− halide ions:

```
Mg²⁺ + Br⁻ → MgBr₂        Al³⁺ + I⁻ → AlI₃        Li⁺ + F⁻ → LiF
```

Equation pattern for a 2+ metal: Mg + Cl₂ → MgCl₂.

## Displacement (3.7.1 e)

A halogen **higher** in Group 7 pushes a **lower** one out of a dissolved salt of that lower halogen.

```
Reactivity:  Cl₂ > Br₂ > I₂   (fluorine above all, but it reacts with water)
```

| Mix | Result | Colour change |
|---|---|---|
| Chlorine + bromide solution | Bromine formed | colourless to yellow-orange |
| Chlorine + iodide solution | Iodine formed | colourless to brown |
| Bromine + iodide solution | Iodine formed | turns brown |
| Bromine + chloride solution | No reaction | stays the colour of the added bromine water |
| Iodine + chloride or bromide | No reaction | stays the colour of the added iodine solution |

### Method in steps: writing a displacement equation

1. Compare positions: the halogen being added must sit above the halide already in the salt; otherwise the answer is "no reaction".
2. Exchange partners: the added halogen ends up as halide ions in the new salt, and the original halide is released as the element.
3. Keep the salt's formula balanced (a 2+ metal needs two halide ions).
4. Ionic version: leave out the metal ion. Example: Cl₂ + 2Br⁻ → 2Cl⁻ + Br₂.

**Worked reminder.** Chlorine water + lithium bromide solution:
Cl₂ + 2LiBr → 2LiCl + Br₂. The solution turns yellow-orange.

## Explaining reactivity (3.7.1 f)

Specification idea: the higher the energy level of the outer electrons, the more easily electrons are lost and the less easily they are gained. You may answer in terms of energy levels or shells.

**Chain for Group 1 (use every link):**
more energy levels → outer electron further from nucleus → more shielding → weaker attraction → electron lost more easily → more reactive.

**Chain for Group 7 (use every link):**
more energy levels → outer level further from nucleus → more shielding → weaker attraction for an incoming electron → electron gained less easily → less reactive.

## Predicting an unfamiliar element

Questions often name an element you have not studied in detail, such as francium or astatine. Use the group pattern, not memory.

| If the element is ... | Predict |
|---|---|
| Lower in Group 1 than potassium | Same products with water (hydroxide + hydrogen), faster and more violent reaction, 1+ ion, white compounds |
| Lower in Group 7 than iodine | Higher melting and boiling points than iodine, so a solid; less reactive than iodine; forms a 1− ion |
| A halogen above another | It displaces the lower one from its salt solution |

Always give the reason: "because it is further down the group" earns more than the prediction alone.

## Calculations that appear with this unit

Mole questions often borrow these reactions. Keep three habits:

1. Write the balanced equation first and read the mole ratio from it.
2. Use the diatomic Mr for a halogen element (Cl₂ = 71, Br₂ = 160).
3. Gas volume at room temperature and pressure = moles × 24 dm³ (or × 24 000 cm³).

**Worked reminder.** 0.30 mol of lithium is added to water. Equation: 2Li + 2H₂O → 2LiOH + H₂, so 0.15 mol of hydrogen, which is 0.15 × 24 000 = **3600 cm³**. The 0.30 mol of LiOH left in solution makes it alkaline.

## Must-know distinctions

| Easily confused | The difference |
|---|---|
| Chlorine vs chloride | Cl₂ molecules make up the element chlorine; Cl⁻ ions in a compound are called chloride |
| Lose vs gain | Group 1 atoms lose one electron; Group 7 atoms gain one |
| Reacts vs dissolves | Sodium reacts with water; sodium hydroxide dissolves in it |
| More reactive down vs less reactive down | Group 1 up the scale, Group 7 down the scale |
| Hydrogen vs hydroxide | Hydrogen is the gas given off; hydroxide is the OH⁻ in the solution |

## Quick self-test

1. State the charge on the ion formed by any alkali metal.
2. Name the gas released when caesium reacts with water.
3. Write the formula of rubidium sulfide.
4. Which three alkali metals float on water?
5. A Group 1 hydroxide solution is tested with universal indicator. Give the colour and a possible pH.
6. Write the formula of aluminium iodide.
7. Does bromine water react with potassium chloride solution? Give a reason.
8. Give the ionic equation when chlorine meets iodide ions.
9. Astatine is below iodine. Predict its state at room temperature.
10. What volume of hydrogen, measured at RTP, forms when 0.10 mol of potassium reacts with water? (Molar gas volume 24 dm³)
11. Give two reasons why fluorine is more reactive than chlorine, using the energy-level idea.
12. Describe the appearance of a solution of a Group 1 chloride.

### Answers

1. +1 (1+).
2. Hydrogen.
3. Rb₂S.
4. Lithium, sodium and potassium.
5. Blue or purple; any pH above 7, for example 13.
6. AlI₃.
7. No. Chlorine is higher in the group and more reactive, so bromine cannot replace it.
8. Cl₂ + 2I⁻ → 2Cl⁻ + I₂.
9. Solid (melting point rises down Group 7).
10. 2K + 2H₂O → 2KOH + H₂, so 0.050 mol H₂. Volume = 0.050 × 24 = **1.2 dm³**.
11. Fluorine's outer level is closer to the nucleus (lower energy level) and less shielded, so the attraction for an extra electron is stronger and it gains an electron more easily.
12. Colourless.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing halogens as single atoms (Cl instead of Cl₂) and then failing to balance the equation.
- Stating that alkali metals dissolve in water rather than react with it.
- Naming oxygen instead of hydrogen as the gas from Group 1 and water.
- Giving the Group 7 reactivity explanation in terms of losing electrons.
- Stopping the explanation at "the atom is bigger" without distance, shielding and weaker attraction.
- Saying a less reactive halogen displaces a more reactive one, or writing an equation for a reaction that does not happen.
- Describing displacement colour changes vaguely ("it changes colour") when the question asks what is seen.
- Mixing up the trends: melting and boiling points rise down Group 7, while reactivity falls.
- Giving a coloured solution for a Group 1 compound.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why do Group 1 compounds have the formula they do?**
An alkali metal always forms a 1+ ion, so the formula depends only on the charge of the non-metal ion: one M⁺ for each Cl⁻, and two M⁺ for each O²⁻ or S²⁻.

**How do you predict the properties of an unfamiliar element such as francium or astatine?**
Use the group pattern and give the reason. Francium, at the bottom of Group 1, should be the most reactive alkali metal, while astatine, at the bottom of Group 7, should be the least reactive halogen with the highest melting point.

**What colour change shows a halogen displacement reaction?**
When chlorine water is added to a bromide solution, the colourless solution turns yellow-orange as bromine forms. Describe the specific colour change rather than just saying "it changes colour".

**Why is fluorine not used in displacement tests in water?**
Fluorine reacts with water itself, so displacement questions in aqueous solution normally use chlorine, bromine and iodine.
