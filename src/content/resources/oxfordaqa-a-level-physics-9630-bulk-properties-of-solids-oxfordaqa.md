This guide goes deeper into **3.2.9 Bulk properties of solids** in Section 3.2 Mechanics and materials of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Physics (9630) specification. It is International AS content, assessed in Unit 1. It leads directly into 3.2.10 The Young modulus and Required practical 2, so the definitions here have to be exact.

## What the specification asks for

- density, ρ = m/V
- Hooke's law and the elastic limit; F = kΔL, with k as the stiffness or spring constant
- tensile stress and tensile strain
- elastic strain energy and breaking stress
- energy stored = ½FΔL = area under the force–extension graph
- description of plastic behaviour, fracture and brittle behaviour, linked to force–extension graphs
- energy conservation involving elastic strain energy and energy to deform
- elastic potential energy in a spring transformed to kinetic and gravitational potential energy
- interpretation of simple stress–strain curves.

## Density

**ρ = m/V**, in kg m⁻³. Density is a property of the material, not of the object: cutting a block in half halves both m and V and leaves ρ unchanged.

**Worked example 1.** A 250 cm³ sample of oil has a mass of 0.198 kg. V = 250 × 10⁻⁶ m³ = 2.50 × 10⁻⁴ m³, so ρ = 0.198 ÷ 2.50 × 10⁻⁴ = **792 kg m⁻³**. The oil floats on water, which has a density of 1000 kg m⁻³.

## Hooke's law and its limits

**Hooke's law:** the extension of an object is proportional to the force applied, F = kΔL, up to the **limit of proportionality**. k is the **stiffness** (spring constant), in N m⁻¹: a stiffer object has a larger k and extends less for the same force.

Two limits are easy to confuse:

| Point | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Limit of proportionality | Beyond it, F is no longer proportional to ΔL: the graph stops being a straight line |
| Elastic limit | Beyond it, the object does not return to its original length when the force is removed |

Below the elastic limit, deformation is **elastic**: fully reversible. Beyond it, deformation is **plastic**: there is a permanent extension.

### Worked example 2: a spring

**Question.** A spring extends by 24 mm under a load of 6.0 N, within its limit of proportionality. Find k, the extension under 9.0 N, and the energy stored at 24 mm.

**Method.**

1. k = F ÷ ΔL = 6.0 ÷ 0.024 = **250 N m⁻¹**.
2. Extension under 9.0 N = 9.0 ÷ 250 = 0.036 m = **36 mm** (assuming the limit is not passed).
3. Energy stored = ½FΔL = ½ × 6.0 × 0.024 = **0.072 J**.

## Stress and strain

Force and extension depend on the size of the sample. Stress and strain describe the **material**:

- **Tensile stress** = force ÷ cross-sectional area, σ = F/A, in Pa (N m⁻²).
- **Tensile strain** = extension ÷ original length, ε = ΔL/L, no unit.
- **Breaking stress** is the stress at which the material breaks. It is a property of the material, so a thicker wire of the same material needs a larger force to break it, in proportion to its area.

### Worked example 3: a steel wire

**Question.** A steel wire of diameter 0.50 mm and length 2.00 m carries a tension of 60 N and extends by 1.5 mm. Find the stress, the strain and the elastic strain energy.

**Method.**

1. A = π(0.25 × 10⁻³)² = 1.96 × 10⁻⁷ m².
2. Stress = 60 ÷ 1.96 × 10⁻⁷ = **3.1 × 10⁸ Pa**.
3. Strain = 1.5 × 10⁻³ ÷ 2.00 = **7.5 × 10⁻⁴**.
4. Energy = ½ × 60 × 1.5 × 10⁻³ = **0.045 J** (if the wire obeys Hooke's law up to this load).

### Worked example 4: maximum load

**Question.** A fishing line of diameter 0.40 mm is made from a material with a breaking stress of 5.0 × 10⁸ Pa (a value chosen for this example). Find the largest tension it can take.

**Method.** A = π(0.20 × 10⁻³)² = 1.26 × 10⁻⁷ m². F = σA = 5.0 × 10⁸ × 1.26 × 10⁻⁷ = **63 N**.

## Elastic strain energy

When a force stretches an object, work is done on it. If the deformation is elastic, the work is stored as **elastic strain energy** and can be recovered.

**Energy stored = ½FΔL = area under the force–extension graph.**

- For the straight-line (Hooke's law) region, the area is a triangle, so ½FΔL. Substituting F = kΔL gives ½k(ΔL)².
- If the graph curves, the area must be found by counting squares or splitting into shapes; ½FΔL no longer applies.

## Plastic behaviour, fracture and brittleness

Force–extension graphs show the type of behaviour:

| Behaviour | What the graph shows | Example materials |
|---|---|---|
| Elastic, Hooke's law | Straight line through the origin; unloading retraces the same line | Steel wire at small loads; a spring |
| Plastic (ductile) | Beyond the elastic limit, large extension for little extra force; on unloading the line is roughly parallel to the loading line but does not return to the origin | Copper wire |
| Brittle | Straight line up to fracture, with little or no plastic region | Glass, cast iron |

**Fracture** is the material breaking. A **brittle** material fractures without significant plastic deformation, so the broken pieces fit back together.

### Energy to deform

When a ductile material is loaded beyond its elastic limit and then unloaded:

- the area under the **loading** curve is the total work done on it
- the area under the **unloading** line is the elastic strain energy recovered
- the area **between** the two curves is the energy that went into permanent (plastic) deformation, mostly ending as internal energy of the material.

**Worked example 5.** A metal wire is loaded to 50 N, reaching an extension of 4.0 mm; the area under the loading curve is 0.14 J. When unloaded, it is left with a permanent extension of 1.5 mm, and the unloading line is straight. Energy recovered = ½ × 50 × (4.0 − 1.5) × 10⁻³ = 0.0625 J. Energy to deform permanently = 0.14 − 0.0625 = **0.078 J**.

This is the energy idea behind car crumple zones: metal deformed plastically absorbs kinetic energy that is not returned as a rebound.

## Spring energy to kinetic and gravitational energy

**Worked example 6.** A trolley of mass 0.10 kg is pushed against a spring of spring constant 800 N m⁻¹, compressing it by 0.025 m, and released on a level, frictionless track. Energy stored = ½ × 800 × 0.025² = 0.25 J. All becomes kinetic energy: ½ × 0.10 × v² = 0.25, so v = **2.2 m s⁻¹**. If the trolley then runs up a ramp, it rises 0.25 ÷ (0.10 × 9.81) = **0.25 m** before stopping.

## Interpreting stress–strain curves

A stress–strain graph removes the effect of sample size, so different materials can be compared directly.

- **Straight initial section**: Hooke's law region. A steeper gradient means a stiffer material (the gradient is the Young modulus, 3.2.10).
- **End of the straight section**: limit of proportionality; the elastic limit is close to it.
- **Yield point** (for metals such as steel): the material suddenly stretches a lot for little or no increase in stress.
- **Highest point**: the maximum stress the material can withstand.
- **End of the curve**: fracture; the stress there is the breaking stress.
- **Short curve ending abruptly** in a straight line: brittle. **Long curve with a large plastic region**: ductile.

## Common mistakes

- Treating the limit of proportionality and the elastic limit as the same point.
- Using the diameter instead of the radius in A = πr², or forgetting to convert mm² to m².
- Giving strain a unit, or calculating it with the new length instead of the original length.
- Using ½FΔL for a curved part of the graph.
- Calling a material "strong" when "stiff" is meant: stiffness is about extension for a given force; strength is about the stress at breaking.
- Describing a brittle material as one that "breaks easily": it breaks with little plastic deformation.

## Exam technique for OxfordAQA command words

- **Define** stress, strain, limit of proportionality and elastic limit in full sentences with the quantities named.
- **Calculate**: convert areas to m² and extensions to m before substituting.
- **Describe** behaviour from a graph: name each region (elastic, plastic, fracture) and say what happens on unloading.
- **Explain** why the area between loading and unloading curves is energy to deform: work done on loading minus energy recovered on unloading.
- **Compare** two materials from stress–strain curves: refer to gradient (stiffness), breaking stress and the size of the plastic region.
