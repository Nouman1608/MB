## Section A: Paper 3 style questions

**1.** Outline **two** symptoms of generalised anxiety disorder, other than worry. **[2]**

**2.** Explain how agoraphobia differs from a specific phobia. **[2]**

**3.** Describe how Watson and Rayner (1920) conditioned a fear in Little Albert. **[4]**

**4.** Explain how two-process theory accounts for the maintenance of a phobia. **[3]**

**5. (a)** Describe the psychodynamic explanation of fear-related disorders, using the case of Little Hans. **[6]**

## Section B: Paper 4 style questions

**6.** Sam scored 16 on the GAD-7.

**(a)** State what this score suggests about Sam's anxiety. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain **one** reason why a clinician should not rely on the GAD-7 alone to diagnose Sam. **[2]**

**7. (a)** Explain why applied tension rather than relaxation was used in the treatment of T by Chapman and DeLapp (2013). **[3]**

**7. (b)** Describe how T's progress was measured. **[3]**

**7. (c)** Explain **one** reason why the findings of Chapman and DeLapp may not generalise. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** 1 mark each for any two: muscle tension; restlessness; sympathetic nervous system overactivity such as racing heart or sweating; nervousness; difficulty concentrating; irritability; disturbed sleep.

**2.** Up to 2 marks. Agoraphobia is fear of several situations where escape might be difficult or help unavailable, such as crowds and public transport (1), whereas a specific phobia is fear of one particular object or situation, such as blood or injections (1).

**3.** Up to 4 marks. At about nine months Albert showed no fear of a white rat but was frightened by a loud noise (1). At about 11 months the rat (neutral stimulus) was presented and a steel bar was struck behind his head (unconditioned stimulus) (1). This pairing happened twice and then five more times a week later (1). Albert then showed fear (conditioned response) to the rat alone (conditioned stimulus), and later to similar objects (1).

**4.** Up to 3 marks. The fear is first acquired through classical conditioning (1). The person then avoids the feared object, which reduces anxiety (1). The reduction is negatively reinforcing, so avoidance is repeated and the person never learns the object is safe, keeping the fear in place (1).

**5. (a)** Up to 6 marks. Phobias arise from unconscious conflict, and fear is displaced onto a safer object (1). Hans, aged five, feared horses would bite him or fall down (1), beginning after he saw a horse collapse in the street (1). Freud, using letters from Hans's father (1), interpreted the fear as displaced anxiety about his father during the Oedipus complex (1); features such as the black around horses' mouths and blinkers were linked to the father's moustache and glasses, and the phobia faded as Hans's fantasies resolved the complex (1).

**5. (b)** Level-based: 9–10 marks for a detailed, balanced evaluation with nature versus nurture developed and a conclusion. Indicative content:

- *Genetic*: Öst (1992) suggests BII phobia runs in families, supporting nature, but families share environments, so modelling (nurture) could explain the pattern.
- *Classical conditioning*: Little Albert supports nurture; but it is one case, poorly controlled and unethical, and many people with phobias recall no conditioning event.
- *Psychodynamic*: Little Hans suggests unconscious causes; but interpretations are subjective, the data came from a biased father, and a conditioning account fits the horse accident.
- *Interaction*: an inherited tendency (such as the vasovagal response or general anxiety) may make conditioning more likely, so a diathesis-stress view combines nature and nurture.
- *Methodology*: all three rely heavily on case studies, which are idiographic and hard to generalise.
- *Conclusion*: behavioural and genetic explanations have more scientific support and lead to effective treatments; an interactionist account is most complete.

**6.** **(a)** 1 mark: severe anxiety (a score of 15 or above).

**(b)** Up to 2 marks. It is a self-report screening tool (1), so a diagnosis needs a clinical interview to check symptoms, duration and other possible causes such as a medical condition (1).

**7. (a)** Up to 3 marks. T fainted during blood tests because of a vasovagal response, a sudden drop in blood pressure (1). Relaxation lowers blood pressure further, making fainting more likely (1). Applied tension raises blood pressure by tensing large muscles, so he could stay conscious during exposure (1).

**7. (b)** Up to 3 marks. Questionnaires including the Beck Anxiety Inventory before and after treatment and at follow-up (1). SUDS ratings (0–100) of distress during each exposure step, within and across sessions (1). Progress up the fear hierarchy, ending with having his own blood drawn (1).

**7. (c)** Up to 2 marks. It is a case study of one man (1), whose motivation (for example wanting to support his child's medical care) may not be shared by other patients, so the treatment may not work as well for everyone (1).

**8.** Up to 2 marks. It shows whether improvements lasted after therapy ended (1); T's low BAI score at follow-up suggests the treatment produced a lasting change rather than a short-term effect (1).

**9.** **(a)** Up to 3 marks. IV: whether patients receive CBT with applied tension or CBT without it (1). DV: whether the patient faints during a standard blood test after treatment (1), recorded by a nurse using a yes or no checklist, or a SUDS rating during the test (1).

**(b)** Up to 3 marks. Protection from harm: patients in the CBT-alone group may faint and injure themselves during the blood test (1). The test should be done lying down with medical staff present (1), and patients should be told about the risk and allowed to withdraw at any time (1).

**10.** Up to 2 marks. People with BII phobia differ in how their fear shows: some mainly have frightening thoughts, some mainly physical reactions such as faintness, and some mainly avoidance (1). Measuring each system separately gives a fuller profile and helps choose the right treatment, such as applied tension for strong physiological responses (1).

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need the GAD-7 thresholds?**
They help you interpret scores in scenario questions. Knowing that it has seven items scored 0 to 3 and a maximum of 21 is the core.

**Can I use Chapman and DeLapp for systematic desensitisation questions?**
Use it to show why relaxation-based methods are adapted for BII phobia, which is a strong evaluation point.
