## How addition polymerisation works

A **monomer** is a small molecule that can join to many others. In addition polymerisation, the C=C bonds of many monomer molecules open up, and the molecules join in a long chain. No other product forms, so all the atoms of the monomers end up in the polymer.

```
n CH₂=CH₂  →  –(CH₂–CH₂)ₙ–
```

Features:

- Each monomer contributes **two carbon atoms** to the chain's backbone (the two carbons of its C=C).
- The backbone is made entirely of single carbon–carbon bonds, so the polymer is **saturated**.
- Any substituent (CH₃, Cl, C₆H₅ and so on) hangs off the backbone as a side group.
- Atom economy of the polymerisation is 100 % because nothing is lost.

The polymer is drawn as its **repeating unit** inside brackets, with bonds extending through the brackets and a subscript n. The n is a very large number, often thousands.

## Drawing the repeating unit from a monomer

Method for a substituted alkene CH₂=CHX (X = CH₃, Cl, C₆H₅):

1. Draw the monomer with the C=C in the centre and the two carbons as the backbone.
2. Change the double bond to a single bond.
3. Extend a bond out of each carbon to the left and right.
4. Put brackets around the two carbons and their groups, with n.

| Monomer | Repeating unit | Polymer name |
|---|---|---|
| CH₂=CH₂ (ethene) | –[CH₂–CH₂]ₙ– | Poly(ethene) |
| CH₂=CHCH₃ (propene) | –[CH₂–CH(CH₃)]ₙ– | Poly(propene) |
| CH₂=CHCl (chloroethene) | –[CH₂–CHCl]ₙ– | Poly(chloroethene), PVC |
| CH₂=CHC₆H₅ (phenylethene) | –[CH₂–CH(C₆H₅)]ₙ– | Poly(phenylethene) |
| CF₂=CF₂ (tetrafluoroethene) | –[CF₂–CF₂]ₙ– | Poly(tetrafluoroethene) |

## IUPAC naming

Name the polymer as **poly(** name of the monomer **)**, using the IUPAC name of the monomer in brackets. For CH₂=CHCl, the IUPAC name of the monomer is chloroethene, so the polymer is poly(chloroethene). The common name PVC (polyvinyl chloride) is also acceptable but the specification names the IUPAC form. Do not write "polythene" in an answer that asks for an IUPAC name: use poly(ethene).

## Drawing the repeating unit from a section of chain

A section of chain shows several repeating units. Find the shortest pattern that repeats along the backbone.

```
–CH₂–CH(CH₃)–CH₂–CH(CH₃)–CH₂–CH(CH₃)–
```

The pattern is –CH₂–CH(CH₃)–, so the repeating unit is that, with the extended bonds. Take care: a repeating unit contains **two backbone carbons** for a polymer made from a single alkene. Do not choose a unit that starts in the middle of the pattern.

## Drawing the monomer from a section of polymer

Take the two backbone carbons of one repeating unit, then:

1. Remove the extended bonds.
2. Put the C=C double bond back between the two backbone carbons.
3. Keep the side groups on the carbons they were on.

From the chain –CH₂–CH(C₆H₅)–CH₂–CH(C₆H₅)–, the monomer is CH₂=CH(C₆H₅), phenylethene.

## Why addition polymers are unreactive

Poly(alkenes) are **saturated** and made of C–C and C–H bonds.

- C–C and C–H bonds are **strong**: breaking them needs a lot of energy.
- They are **non-polar** (or only slightly polar), so they do not attract nucleophiles or electrophiles.
- There is no C=C bond and no lone pairs available for reaction.

For these reasons, polyalkenes resist attack by acids, alkalis, water and most reagents. This property makes them useful as containers and pipes, but it also makes them non-biodegradable (see the Biodegradability and disposal of polymers sub-topic).

## Intermolecular forces between polyalkene chains

In poly(ethene) and poly(propene) the only forces between chains are **London forces** (instantaneous dipole–induced dipole). They are weak per atom but chains are very long, so the total attraction is large. Longer chains and chains packed closely together mean stronger forces, a higher melting point and a stronger material.

In PVC, the C–Cl bonds are polar, so there are **permanent dipole–dipole forces** as well as London forces between chains. The stronger forces make PVC rigid.

## PVC and plasticisers

Unplasticised PVC is hard and rigid, used for window frames, drainpipes and guttering. Adding a **plasticiser** (small molecules that sit between the polymer chains) changes its properties.

- The small molecules push the chains apart.
- Intermolecular forces between chains become weaker.
- The chains can slide past each other, making the material **flexible** and softer.

Plasticised PVC is used for electrical cable insulation, flexible tubing, flooring and waterproof clothing.

## Development over time

Understanding of polymers has changed as methods improved. Poly(ethene) was first made in the 1930s at high pressure and was discovered in an experiment that was not planned to make it. Later, catalysts were developed that allowed the polymerisation at lower pressure with better control of chain structure. Chemists learned that the arrangement of chains changes properties such as density and strength. You should appreciate that production methods and knowledge have been refined through research.

## Worked example 1: monomer to repeating unit

**Question.** Draw the repeating unit of the polymer formed from but-1-ene, CH₂=CH–CH₂CH₃.

The backbone carbons are the two carbons of the C=C. The ethyl group is a side group on the second carbon. The repeating unit is –[CH₂–CH(CH₂CH₃)]ₙ–, and the polymer is poly(but-1-ene).

## Worked example 2: a trickier monomer

**Question.** Give the repeating unit for the polymer from but-2-ene, CH₃–CH=CH–CH₃.

The backbone carbons are C2 and C3, each carrying a methyl group. The repeating unit is –[CH(CH₃)–CH(CH₃)]ₙ–. The backbone has only two carbons per unit even though the monomer has four. This is the usual mistake: counting all four carbons into the backbone.

## Worked example 3: monomer from a section of chain

**Question.** Draw the monomer from this section of polymer:

```
–CH₂–CHCl–CH₂–CHCl–CH₂–CHCl–
```

The repeating unit is –CH₂–CHCl–. Replace the extended bonds with C=C: **CH₂=CHCl**, chloroethene.

## Worked example 4: using relative formula masses

PVC has an average relative molecular mass of 62 500. Calculate the average value of n. Use Aᵣ values C = 12.0, H = 1.0, Cl = 35.5.

```
Mr of repeating unit, C₂H₃Cl = 2(12.0) + 3(1.0) + 35.5 = 62.5
n = 62 500 ÷ 62.5 = 1000
```

Because all atoms of the monomer end up in the polymer, 1.00 tonne of chloroethene gives at most 1.00 tonne of PVC.

## Common mistakes

- Drawing a repeating unit with a double bond in it. The polymer has none.
- Using all the monomer carbons in the backbone (for example but-2-ene).
- Leaving off the extended bonds and the n, or writing the n inside the bracket.
- Naming the polymer from the repeating unit rather than from the monomer.
- Saying addition polymers are unreactive because they are "stable". The reason is the strong, non-polar saturated backbone.
- Describing the forces in PVC as only London forces.
- Saying a plasticiser makes PVC stronger. It makes it more flexible by weakening forces between chains.
- Writing "polythene" when the question asks for IUPAC names.

## Exam technique

- **Draw the repeating unit**: show two backbone carbons, side groups, extended bonds and n.
- **Draw the monomer**: restore the double bond and keep the side groups.
- **Explain unreactivity**: strong bonds, non-polar, saturated.
- **Explain properties**: link chain length, packing and intermolecular forces to the property.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why is n so large?**
Each monomer addition happens many times. Real polymer chains contain thousands of repeating units, so n is not a fixed number but an average.

**Is the double bond completely used up?**
Yes. Every C=C in the monomer becomes a single bond in the chain. A small number of end groups from the initiator may remain at the ends, but they are ignored in the repeating unit.

**What is the repeating unit of poly(tetrafluoroethene)?**
–[CF₂–CF₂]ₙ–. Its very strong C–F bonds make it extremely unreactive.
