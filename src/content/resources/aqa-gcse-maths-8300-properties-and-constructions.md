This guide teaches section **3.4.1 Properties and constructions** of the AQA GCSE Mathematics (8300) specification (content references G1 to G13). It goes deeper than the topic-level [geometry and measures study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-maths-8300-geometry-and-measures/) on this one section. Use it with the [revision notes](/resources/aqa-gcse-maths-8300-properties-and-constructions-revision-notes/) and the [practice questions](/resources/aqa-gcse-maths-8300-properties-and-constructions-practice/).

Geometry appears on all three papers. Constructions need a ruler and compasses, so practise with real equipment. On Paper 1 you have no calculator, so know the angle facts by heart.

## Terms and notation (G1)

- A **vertex** is a corner; an **edge** joins two vertices; a **face** is a flat surface.
- **Parallel** lines never meet and stay the same distance apart; **perpendicular** lines meet at a right angle.
- A **polygon** is a closed shape with straight sides; a **regular** polygon has equal sides and equal angles.
- In triangle ABC, the side opposite angle A is called a. Angle ABC means the angle at B, between BA and BC.
- Mark equal sides with matching dashes and parallel sides with matching arrows.

## Constructions and loci (G2)

Never rub out your arcs. They are your evidence.

**Perpendicular bisector of AB**

1. Set the compasses to more than half of AB.
2. Draw an arc centred on A above and below the line.
3. Without changing the width, draw arcs centred on B to cross the first two.
4. Join the crossing points. The line cuts AB in half at 90°.

**Bisector of angle ABC**

1. Centre B: draw an arc crossing both arms.
2. Centre each crossing point in turn, with the same width, and draw arcs that cross inside the angle.
3. Join B to the crossing point.

**Perpendicular from a point P to a line**: draw an arc from P cutting the line twice, then construct the perpendicular bisector of the two cutting points. **Perpendicular at a point P on a line**: mark equal distances either side of P, then bisect between them.

**Angle of 60°**: draw a line, centre one end with any radius to mark an arc on it, then from that mark with the same radius cross the first arc. Joining the end to the crossing makes an equilateral triangle with 60° angles. Bisecting gives 30°.

**The shortest distance from a point to a line** is the perpendicular distance.

### Loci

A **locus** is the set of points that obey a rule.

| Rule | Locus |
|---|---|
| A fixed distance from a point | A circle |
| Equidistant from two points | The perpendicular bisector |
| Equidistant from two lines | The angle bisector |
| A fixed distance from a line | A pair of parallel lines with rounded ends |

For region questions, draw each boundary, then shade the region where all conditions hold, and keep construction arcs.

## Angle facts (G3)

- Angles at a point add to **360°**; on a straight line to **180°**.
- **Vertically opposite** angles are equal.
- With parallel lines, **alternate** angles are equal, **corresponding** angles are equal and **co-interior** angles add to 180°. Use these words, not "Z angles" or "F angles".
- Angles in a triangle add to **180°**; in a quadrilateral to **360°**.
- Interior angles of an n-sided polygon add to **(n − 2) × 180°**.
- Exterior angles of any polygon add to 360°. A regular polygon has exterior angle 360° ÷ n, and interior angle 180° − exterior angle.

**Example 1.** A regular polygon has an interior angle of 162°. How many sides has it?
Exterior angle = 180° − 162° = 18°. Number of sides = 360° ÷ 18° = **20**.

**Example 2.** Find the sum of the interior angles of a hexagon, and the interior angle of a regular octagon.
Hexagon: (6 − 2) × 180° = **720°**. Regular octagon: (8 − 2) × 180° ÷ 8 = 1080° ÷ 8 = **135°**.

## Properties of triangles and quadrilaterals (G4)

| Shape | Key properties |
|---|---|
| Isosceles triangle | Two equal sides and two equal base angles |
| Equilateral triangle | Three equal sides; all angles 60° |
| Scalene triangle | No equal sides or angles |
| Square | Four equal sides, four right angles, diagonals equal and bisect at 90° |
| Rectangle | Opposite sides equal, four right angles, diagonals equal |
| Parallelogram | Opposite sides equal and parallel; opposite angles equal; diagonals bisect each other |
| Rhombus | Four equal sides; diagonals bisect at 90° |
| Kite | Two pairs of adjacent equal sides; one diagonal bisects the other at 90° |
| Trapezium | One pair of parallel sides |

Know the names pentagon (5), hexagon (6), octagon (8) and decagon (10).

## Congruence and proof (G5, G6)

Shapes are **congruent** if they have the same size and shape. Two triangles are congruent if they satisfy one of:

- **SSS**: three sides equal
- **SAS**: two sides and the **included** angle equal
- **ASA**: two angles and a corresponding side equal
- **RHS**: right angle, hypotenuse and one other side equal.

"SSA" and "AAA" do not prove congruence.

**Example 3.** Prove that the base angles of an isosceles triangle ABC (AB = AC) are equal.
Let M be the midpoint of BC. In triangles ABM and ACM: AB = AC (given), BM = CM (M is the midpoint), AM is common. So the triangles are congruent (SSS). Therefore angle ABM = angle ACM. ∎

In proofs, state each fact with a reason, and finish with the result.

## Similarity and transformations (G7, G8)

Similar shapes have equal angles and sides in the same ratio. An enlargement with scale factor k multiplies lengths by k from a centre. A **fractional** scale factor (0 < k < 1) makes the shape smaller (both tiers).

**Example 4.** Enlarge the triangle with vertices (2, 2), (6, 2), (6, 4) by scale factor 1/2, centre the origin. Multiply each coordinate by 1/2: **(1, 1), (3, 1), (3, 2)**.

**Higher tier only: negative scale factors.** With scale factor −2 and centre the origin, each point goes to the opposite side of the centre at twice the distance: (x, y) becomes (−2x, −2y). The image is inverted.

**Higher tier only: combined transformations.** Reflecting in the y-axis and then in the x-axis sends (x, y) to (−x, y) then to (−x, −y): this equals a rotation of **180° about the origin**. When describing a single transformation, state its type and all details (angle, direction and centre for a rotation; the line for a reflection; the vector for a translation).

## Circles (G9, G10)

Know: centre, radius, diameter, circumference, chord, **tangent** (touches at one point), **arc**, **sector** (between two radii and an arc) and **segment** (between a chord and an arc).

**Higher tier only: circle theorems**

- The angle at the centre is twice the angle at the circumference on the same arc.
- The angle in a semicircle is 90°.
- Angles in the same segment are equal.
- Opposite angles of a cyclic quadrilateral add to 180°.
- A tangent is perpendicular to the radius at the point of contact.
- Tangents from an external point are equal in length.
- The perpendicular from the centre to a chord bisects the chord.
- The angle between a tangent and a chord equals the angle in the alternate segment.

**Example 5 (Higher tier only).** A tangent from T touches a circle, centre O, at A. OA = 5 cm and OT = 13 cm. Find TA.
Angle OAT = 90° (tangent and radius). TA² = 13² − 5² = 144, so TA = **12 cm**.

## Coordinates and 3D shapes (G11 to G13)

**Example 6.** A(1, 1), B(5, 2) and C(6, 5) are three vertices of a parallelogram ABCD. Find D. In a parallelogram, D = A + C − B = (1 + 6 − 5, 1 + 5 − 2) = **(2, 4)**.

For a solid, **Euler's check**: faces + vertices − edges = 2. A triangular prism has 5 faces, 6 vertices and 9 edges: 5 + 6 − 9 = 2.

A **plan** is the view from above; the **front elevation** is the view from the front and a **side elevation** from the side. For a square-based pyramid the plan is a square with lines from each corner to the centre. The front elevation is a triangle.

## Common errors

- Using "Z angles" and "F angles" instead of alternate and corresponding angles.
- Forgetting to subtract 2 in (n − 2) × 180°, or mixing up interior and exterior angles.
- Rubbing out construction arcs.
- Using SSA as a congruence rule.
- Giving a transformation without full details.
- Applying circle theorems on a Foundation paper; they are not assessed there.
