These notes cover the topic **Change in culture and communities** from the Cambridge IGCSE Global Perspectives (0457) syllabus for examination in 2025, 2026 and 2027, Version 2 (published April 2025). They draw on section 2 (assessment objectives), section 3 (topic list and key terms) and section 4 (details of the assessment and command words). The topic can be used in Component 1 Written Exam, Component 2 Individual Report and Component 3 Team Project. Knowledge of content is not assessed: these notes show how to use the topic to practise the assessed skills.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning (syllabus wording where printed) |
|---|---|
| Issue | "An important subject or problem for discussion" |
| Perspective | "A viewpoint on an issue based on evidence and reasoning" |
| Research | "The systematic study of a global issue to find evidence and develop understanding" |
| Evidence | Information about an issue that helps develop understanding or prove something true or false |
| Argument | A series of statements with reasons and evidence supporting a claim |
| Claim | A statement put forward as true, which may or may not be supported |
| Fact | A statement that can be checked against evidence |
| Opinion | A personal view that cannot be proved true or false |

| Prediction | A statement about what will happen in the future |

| Culture | Shared ways of living: language, customs, beliefs, arts, food, celebrations |

## Issues by level at a glance

- **Global:** smaller languages declining; global media making cultures more alike or more mixed; protecting traditional knowledge.
- **National:** language of schooling; public funding for heritage, arts or festivals; how history is marked in public places.
- **Local:** a market, meeting place or club closing; young people leaving; rising rents changing who lives in a neighbourhood; a festival becoming commercial.

|---|---|---|---|

## Must-know distinctions

- **Topic vs issue.** "Change in culture and communities" is a topic. "Should minority languages be taught in every school?" is an issue.
- **Perspective vs opinion.** A perspective has evidence and reasoning; an opinion may have neither.
- **Fact vs claim.** A fact can be checked. A claim is put forward as true but may be unsupported.
- **Prediction vs evidence.** "The dialect will vanish" is a prediction. Falling numbers of speakers is evidence.
- **Cause vs consequence.** Rising rents (cause) → families move away (consequence) → fewer pupils at the local language class (further consequence).
- **Global issue vs local issue.** The Individual Report needs a global issue with local and/or national and global perspectives. The Team Project needs a local issue.
- **Describing a change vs judging it.** Marks come from analysing who gains and who loses, not from saying change is good or bad.
- **Emotive language vs evidence.** "The heart of our town is being destroyed" persuades but proves nothing.

## Source checks in four questions

1. Who wrote it, and what do they gain? (vested interest, bias)
2. How was the evidence gathered? (sample size, who, where, when)
3. Does the conclusion follow, or does it generalise or predict too far?
4. What does the language do? (emotive words, loaded questions)

## Mini answer plan: assessing actions

*Typical task (in the style of Component 1, Question 4):* "A town's traditional festival is losing visitors. Using the sources, assess two possible actions and recommend one."

1. **Name the issue and its main cause** from the sources (for example, ticket prices, fewer young volunteers, competing events).
2. **Action 1: lower prices with council funding.** Evidence for: tackles a cost barrier shown in a source. Evidence against: costs public money; may not change interest. Who gains, who loses.
3. **Action 2: invite young people to help design part of the festival.** Evidence for: tackles lack of involvement; builds ownership. Against: older organisers may object to changes; may alter the tradition.
4. **Compare** on effectiveness, feasibility, cost and sustainability.
5. **Judgement:** recommend one, say why it beats the other, and state one condition for success.

Keep every point tied to a source. In Component 1 you are not credited for outside knowledge.

## Quick self-test

3. Name the eight terms the syllabus says candidates benefit from understanding for Component 1.
4. "Within ten years this village will lose its dialect." What kind of statement is this?
5. A developer pays for a survey showing residents want flats instead of a heritage hall. Which term describes the problem?

7. Give one global and one local issue within this topic.
8. What word limit applies to the Individual Report?

12. What is a generalisation? Give an example from this topic.

### Answers

1. No. The syllabus states that knowledge of content is not assessed; topics are contexts for skills.
2. "A viewpoint on an issue based on evidence and reasoning."
3. Bias, claim, fact, generalisation, opinion, prediction, value, vested interest.
4. A prediction (also a claim).
5. Vested interest; the funder gains from one answer, so the evidence may be biased.
6. AO2 Reflection and AO3 Communication and collaboration; Component 1 is 100% AO1.
7. Global: smaller languages declining. Local: a traditional market closing. (Other valid answers accepted.)
8. 1500–2000 words, not counting the bibliography or reference list.
9. Two to five members.
10. It is a broad topic, not a question; it shows no issue that divides opinion and no perspectives.
11. Any two: effectiveness, feasibility, cost, who benefits, unintended consequences, sustainability.
12. A claim about a whole group based on a few members, e.g. "Teenagers don't care about local traditions."

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing background knowledge about a culture in Component 1 instead of using the sources.
- Naming a perspective ("the elders' view") without giving its reasons and evidence.
- Saying a source is biased without naming the interest or quoting the loaded words.
- Accepting a small or narrow survey as proof of what a whole generation or country thinks.
- Mixing up a prediction or target with evidence of what has happened.
- Listing causes and consequences instead of linking them in a chain.
- Proposing a course of action without weighing its drawbacks or who might lose out.
- Ending an extended answer without a clear judgement.
- Picking an Individual Report issue with no global dimension, or a Team Project issue that is not local.
- Presenting your own view on a contested cultural or religious practice as fact.
