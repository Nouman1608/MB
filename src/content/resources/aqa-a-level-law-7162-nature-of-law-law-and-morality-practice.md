## Section A

**1.** Give **one** example of conduct that is both unlawful and immoral, and **one** of conduct that is immoral but lawful. **[2]**

**2.** State what is meant by a "pluralist society". **[2]**

**3.** Identify the theory of law associated with Thomas Aquinas, and state its view of an unjust law. **[2]**

**4.** Distinguish legal moralism from legal paternalism. **[2]**

## Section B

**5.** Explain the positivist view of the relationship between law and morality. **[4]**

**6.** Outline **four** of Lon Fuller's requirements for the "inner morality" of law. **[4]**

**7.** Explain why the relationship between law and morality is important, giving **two** reasons. **[4]**

**8.** Explain how Parliament has responded to moral diversity over abortion and assisted suicide. **[4]**

**9.** Compare the decisions in *R v Brown* (1993) and *R v Wilson* (1996), and explain what the comparison suggests about the enforcement of morality by judges. **[6]**

**10.** Explain how the offences in *Knuller v DPP* (1972) and *R v Gibson* (1990) show the courts protecting public morals. **[4]**

**11.** A new Act makes it an offence to take part in a lawful but dangerous extreme sport without a licence. Using the harm principle, paternalism and moralism, explain how this law could be justified or criticised. **[6]**

**12.** "In a pluralist society, the law should not enforce moral values." Discuss. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** Unlawful and immoral: murder, theft or assault [1]. Immoral but lawful: lying to a friend, breaking a non-contractual promise, or failing to rescue a stranger [1].

**2.** A society in which people of different faiths, cultures and outlooks live together [1] and hold different moral views, so there is no single shared moral code [1].

**3.** Natural law [1]; an unjust law, in conflict with natural law, is not true law but a corruption of law [1].

**4.** Legal moralism is the use of law to enforce morality for its own sake, even where no one is harmed [1]; legal paternalism is the use of law to protect people from harming themselves [1].

**5.** Positivists such as Austin and Hart argue that a rule is law because it was made by the recognised process, such as an Act of Parliament [1]. Its validity does not depend on its moral content [1], so an immoral law can still be valid law [1]. Hart accepted that law and morality often overlap in practice, but denied that there is a necessary connection between them [1].

**6.** Any four, one mark each: rules must be general; publicly promulgated; prospective rather than retrospective; clear; not contradictory; possible to obey; reasonably stable over time; applied by officials as announced [1] [1] [1] [1].

**7.** Any two developed reasons, two marks each: law that reflects widely shared moral views gains legitimacy and is more readily obeyed [1], whereas law out of step with morality is resented and may be ignored [1]; judges draw on moral ideas to develop the common law [1], as Lord Atkin did in building the neighbour principle in *Donoghue v Stevenson* (1932) [1]. (Also accept: law can shape morality over time; conflicts between law and morality drive reform.)

**8.** On abortion, Parliament has permitted conduct on which views differ, but only under conditions [1]: the Abortion Act 1967 allows abortion where specified medical grounds are certified [1]. On assisted suicide, Parliament kept the offence in section 2 of the Suicide Act 1961 while abolishing the crime of suicide itself [1], and prosecution requires the consent of the Director of Public Prosecutions, allowing discretion in individual cases [1].

**9.** In *Brown* the House of Lords held that consent was no defence to actual bodily harm inflicted during consensual sadomasochistic activity in private [1], which reflects a moralist approach protecting society's standards even without an unwilling victim [1]. In *Wilson* a husband who branded his initials on his wife's buttocks at her request was not liable [1]; the Court of Appeal likened it to tattooing and said the law should not intrude into a married couple's private life [1]. The comparison suggests judges' enforcement of morality is inconsistent [1] and may reflect moral views about the type of relationship involved rather than a principled distinction based on harm [1].

**10.** In *Knuller*, publishers of a magazine carrying contact advertisements for gay men were convicted of conspiracy to corrupt public morals, and the House of Lords upheld the conviction [1], confirming the offence recognised in *Shaw v DPP* (1962) [1]. In *Gibson*, exhibiting earrings made from freeze-dried human foetuses was held to be outraging public decency [1]. Both offences protect shared standards of morality and decency rather than any identifiable victim, which is the enforcement of morality for its own sake [1].

**11.** Harm principle: the law restricts liberty where the risk falls mainly on the participant, so Mill would oppose it unless others are harmed [1], for example rescue workers put at risk or the cost to the health service [1]. Paternalism: the law could be justified as protecting participants from their own dangerous choices [1], an approach Hart accepted in some cases [1]. Moralism: if the law were based on disapproval of the sport rather than danger, it would be criticised as enforcing morality without harm [1]. A reasoned judgement on which justification is most convincing [1].

**12.** For the statement: in a pluralist society there is no shared morality, so enforcing one view imposes it on others [1]; Mill's harm principle and Hart's argument support limiting law to preventing harm [1]; the trend in Parliament has been to withdraw criminal law from private morality, as in the Sexual Offences Act 1967 [1]; judges' moral enforcement is inconsistent, as *Brown* and *Wilson* show [1]. Against: much of the criminal law is moral in content, such as murder and theft, so law cannot avoid enforcing some moral values [1]; Devlin argued shared morality is needed for social cohesion [1]; some moral protection, such as outraging public decency in *Gibson*, reflects values most people share [1]; courts have deferred contested moral questions to Parliament, as in *Nicklinson* (2014), which provides democratic legitimacy [1]. Conclusion: the law should enforce a minimum core of widely shared values linked to harm, but avoid enforcing contested private morality [1] [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Naming natural law or positivism without saying how each sees the relationship.
- Mixing up paternalism and moralism in scenario questions.
- Describing *Brown* and *Wilson* without drawing a conclusion from the comparison.
- In the 10-mark essay, failing to address pluralism, which the question names.
