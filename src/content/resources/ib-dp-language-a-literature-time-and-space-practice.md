## Questions

### Passage A -- "The Weighing House" (original text written for Marlbridge)

> The Weighing House still had its clock, though the hands had stopped at twenty past four some years
> before Ruth was born. When she was a girl, farmers had queued here with carts of wool, and her
> grandfather had stood at the great iron scale in his brown coat, calling out numbers that settled the
> price of a year's work. Now the arched doors were glass. A sign offered "Luxury Studio Apartments --
> Heritage Features". Through the window Ruth could see a white sofa where the scale had been, and a
> young man tapping at a laptop beneath the old beams. She put her hand on the cold stone of the
> doorway, where the carts had worn a groove, and found it was the only thing still the right shape.

### Passage B -- William Blake, "London", first stanza (*Songs of Experience*, 1794; public domain)

> I wander thro' each charter'd street,
> Near where the charter'd Thames does flow,
> And mark in every face I meet,
> Marks of weakness, marks of woe.

### Passage C -- a translator's note (original text written for Marlbridge; the novel is invented)

> **Translator's note to *The House in the Old Quarter*, translated from the Portuguese**
>
> The hardest word in this book is *saudade*. It appears whenever Amélia thinks of the harbour her
> father sailed from. English has no single word for it: it is a longing that knows the thing it misses
> may never return. I tried "homesickness", but Amélia is at home. I tried "nostalgia", but that sounded
> like a fondness for old furniture. In the end I kept *saudade* in italics the first time and wrote
> "the old ache" after that. Readers who know Portuguese will miss the music of the original word. Readers who do not will, I
> hope, feel its weight.

**1.** According to the subject brief, name the three areas of exploration of the course and state the
recommended teaching hours for Time and space at SL and at HL. **[3]**

**2.** Distinguish between the context of production and the context of reception of a literary work.
Give an example of each. **[4]**

**3.** Passage A: identify two details that show the passing of time and explain the effect of each.
Support each with a quotation. **[4]**

**4.** Passage A: explain how the contrast between the scale and the sofa presents a change in the
building's role in the town. **[3]**

**5.** Passage B was published in 1794. Explain how the word "charter'd" gains meaning from its context
of production. **[4]**

**6.** Passage B: explain the effect of "mark" in line 3 followed by "Marks" twice in line 4. **[3]**

**7.** Passage C: identify two choices the translator made and explain what each gains and loses for an
English-language reader. **[4]**

**8.** Chekhov's story of 1899 appears in English both as *The Lady with the Dog* and as *The Lady with
the Little Dog*; the Russian title uses a diminutive. Explain what this shows about how you should write
about a work you study in translation. **[3]**

**9.** For an early German production of *A Doll's House* (1879), Ibsen reluctantly wrote an alternative
ending in which Nora stays. Explain what this shows about the play's context of reception, and how a
reader today might respond differently. **[4]**

**10.** Paper 1-style task. Guiding question: *How does the writer present the relationship between a
place and memory in Passage A?* Write a thesis and a paragraph plan for a full answer. **[8]**

**11.** Individual oral plan. Using Kate Chopin's short fiction, including "The Story of an Hour" (1894,
written in English), and Henrik Ibsen's *A Doll's House* (studied in English translation), plan an
individual oral on a common global issue. Show how the context of each work shapes its treatment of the
issue. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** Readers, writers and texts; Time and space; Intertextuality: connecting texts [1]. Time and space:
50 hours at SL [1] and 80 hours at HL [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Recall questions reward exact figures; "about 50 hours" or mixing up SL and HL loses
the mark.

**2.** Context of production: the time, place and culture in which a work was written [1] -- for
example, Blake writing "London" in the city in 1794 [1]. Context of reception: the time, place and
culture in which it is read or watched [1] -- for example, a modern audience finding Nora's exit expected,
not scandalous [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* An example that is really about setting shows the terms learned but not
understood.

**3.** The stopped clock, "the hands had stopped at twenty past four" [1]: the building is frozen in its
past while the town moves on [1]. The worn stone, "where the carts had worn a groove" [1]: years of work
leave a physical trace that outlasts its purpose [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* The effect is where the mark is earned; a quotation followed by "this shows time
passing" repeats the question instead of answering it.

**4.** The scale made the building a place of shared work, where numbers "settled the price of a year's
work" [1]. Now "a white sofa" and a "laptop" make it private and domestic [1]. The spot has moved from
the town's common life to one person's comfort, so little is "still the right shape" for Ruth [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Strong answers name what the building meant to the town, not only what objects
were swapped.

**5.** "Charter'd" means granted privileges by a legal charter, so owned or controlled [1]. Applied to
the Thames as well as the streets, it suggests even nature has been owned [1]. In the 1790s, a decade of
fierce argument about rights and liberty, a charter suggests freedoms granted to a few rather than rights
shared by all [1]. So the speaker's "wander" is not free: every step is through someone's property [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Context earns credit only when it changes how the word reads.

**6.** "mark" as a verb means to notice [1]. "Marks" as a noun means visible signs or scars [1]. The
repetition binds looking and damage: every face noticed is stamped with suffering, with no escape [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Spotting the wordplay is the start; the mark for effect comes from saying what the
double meaning does.

**7.** Choice 1: keeping *saudade* in italics the first time [1]. It keeps the word's cultural
specificity, but may puzzle readers who do not know it [1]. Choice 2: writing "the old ache" afterwards
[1]. It carries the feeling in plain English, but loses "the music of the original word" [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* "Gains and loses" is a two-sided demand; answers that praise a choice without its
cost reach only half the marks.

**8.** The longer title keeps the diminutive's fond tone; the shorter one loses it [1]. Both are
defensible choices, so a single English word may reflect the translator rather than Chekhov [1]. Name
your translation and build main claims on what survives it: events, structure, images [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Precise phrasing such as "in this translation" shows awareness of the translator
and protects an argument from overreach.

**9.** Some theatres and audiences of the time found a mother leaving her children unacceptable [1].
So the ending challenged beliefs about marriage and motherhood: the play tried to shape its culture, not
only reflect it [1]. A reader today in many cultures may see Nora's choice as understandable [1]. The
words have not changed; the meaning readers draw from them has, and it still varies by culture [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A fact of reception is evidence, not an answer; link it to what the play does.

**10.** Model thesis (original writing for Marlbridge): "The Weighing House stores memory in objects
and stone; the present turns that memory into decoration, and only touch still links Ruth to her past."

Indicative points:
- A thesis that answers "place and memory" directly [1].
- The stopped clock as a symbol of time frozen in the building [1].
- The grandfather at the scale: family memory and a working community [1].
- The sign, "Heritage Features": irony, as the past becomes a selling point [1].
- Contrast: "iron scale" and "brown coat" against "white sofa" and "laptop" [1].
- Touch: "cold stone" and the "groove" keep memory in the building [1].
- The last line, "the only thing still the right shape", closes on Ruth's loss [1].
- Structure: past, present, then touch -- memory, loss, a small survival [1]. **[8]**

The real exam is marked holistically against the IB assessment criteria, not point by point; your
teacher will share the criteria. These points show what a strong plan covers.
*Examiner insight:* Plans that follow the passage line by line drift into summary; plans built on the
guiding question's two terms stay analytical.

**11.** Model plan (original writing for Marlbridge).

- **Global issue:** the limits placed on a married woman's freedom to choose her own life [1].
- **Chopin (in English):** Louise Mallard glimpses freedom alone in her room; the doctors' verdict, "the
  joy that kills", shows no one can read what she felt [1].
- **Chopin's context:** the 1890s, when a married woman's legal and social life was bound closely to her
  husband's [1].
- **Ibsen (in translation):** Nora's secret loan, Torvald's pet names ("lark", "squirrel"), her final
  exit [1].
- **Ibsen's context and reception:** first staged in 1879; the alternative German ending shows how hard
  some first audiences found Nora's choice [1].
- **Translation:** name the translator; the title itself varies, so rest the analysis on action and
  structure [1].
- **Connection:** Chopin keeps freedom inside one mind and one hour; Ibsen makes it a public act [1].
- **Structure:** move between the works point by point, not one after the other [1].
- **Reception now:** a reader today may read both as critiques; state your own position [1].
- **Conclusion:** freedom is inward and fatal in the story, outward and costly in the play [1]. **[10]**

The real individual oral is marked holistically against the IB assessment criteria, not point by point.
*Examiner insight:* Strong orals keep the global issue in every point; weak ones meet it only at the
start.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Background history given with no link back to a quotation or a writer's choice.
- Setting treated as context: "the story reflects the 1850s" when it was written much later.
- Only one side of a "gains and loses" or "production and reception" question answered.
- Claims about a single English word in a translated work with no mention of the translation.
- Moral judgement of a character from today's values with no analysis of the work's own culture.
- Inventing a precise date or country an unseen passage does not support.
- Oral plans that treat each work in its own half and meet the global issue only in the introduction.
