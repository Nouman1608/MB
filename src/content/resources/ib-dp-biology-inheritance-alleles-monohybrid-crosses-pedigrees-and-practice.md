## Questions

**1.** Tomato plants have 2n = 24. (a) State the number of chromosomes in a pollen nucleus and in a zygote. **[2]** (b) Explain why a tomato zygote has two copies of each autosomal gene. **[1]**

**2.** Describe how you would make a controlled cross between a pure-breeding red-fruited tomato plant and a pure-breeding yellow-fruited one, given that each flower can self-pollinate. **[4]**

**3.** Seed colour in a plant is controlled by a gene with alleles G (green, dominant) and g (yellow). **(a)** Define the term homozygous. **[1]** **(b)** Explain why plants of genotype GG and Gg have the same phenotype. **[3]**

**4.** **(a)** For each trait, state whether it is due to genotype only, environment only, or both: ABO blood group; the number of scars on a person's knee; body mass. **[3]** **(b)** Two genetically identical hydrangea cuttings are grown in soils of different acidity and produce different flower colours. Explain this using phenotypic plasticity. **[2]**

**5.** Two unaffected parents have a child with phenylketonuria (PKU). Use A and a for the alleles.

**(a)** State the genotype of both parents. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the probability that their next child has PKU. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why phenylalanine builds up in a child with PKU. **[2]**
**(d)** Explain why a low-phenylalanine diet changes the phenotype but not the genotype. **[1]**
**(e)** Explain why marriage between close relatives is prohibited in many societies. **[2]**

**6.** **(a)** Define single-nucleotide polymorphism. **[1]** **(b)** A gene has five alleles in a population. State how many alleles one person has, and why. **[2]** **(c)** Calculate the number of possible genotypes. **[2]**

**7.** A woman of blood group A and a man of group B have a child of group O.

**(a)** Deduce the genotypes of both parents, using Iᴬ, Iᴮ and i. **[2]**
**(b)** Find the probability that their next child is group AB. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why group AB shows codominance. **[2]**

**8.** In *Mirabilis jalapa* (marvel of Peru), red flowers (CᴿCᴿ) crossed with white (CᵂCᵂ) give pink flowers. Two pink plants are crossed and produce 320 seedlings.

**(a)** Predict the expected number of seedlings of each colour. **[3]**
**(b)** Distinguish between incomplete dominance and codominance. **[2]**

**9.** A man with haemophilia (XʰY) and a carrier woman (XᴴXʰ) have children.

**(a)** Draw a Punnett grid and state the probability that a daughter has haemophilia. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain why X-linked recessive disorders are more common in males. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why the man cannot pass his haemophilia allele to a son. **[1]**
**(d)** Explain how sperm determine the sex of the zygote. **[2]**

**10.** In a family, I-1 (male) and I-2 (female) are both affected by a condition. Their children are II-1 (male, affected), II-2 (female, unaffected) and II-3 (female, affected). II-3 and an unaffected man II-4, from outside the family, have two children: III-1 (male, unaffected) and III-2 (female, affected).

**(a)** Deduce that the condition is autosomal dominant. **[3]**
**(b)** Using D and d, state the genotypes of I-1 and II-3. **[2]**
**(c)** II-3 and II-4 have a third child. Calculate the probability it is affected. **[2]**
**(d)** Identify which step above used inductive reasoning and which used deductive reasoning. **[2]**

**11.** Shoot heights (cm) of 12 seedlings: 23, 27, 27, 27, 29, 30, 31, 33, 34, 35, 36, 52.

**(a)** Find the median, first quartile, third quartile and interquartile range. **[3]**
**(b)** Determine whether 52 is an outlier. **[2]**
**(c)** State the values at which the whiskers end. **[1]**
**(d)** Calculate the mean and state the mode. **[2]**
**(e)** Explain why shoot height shows continuous variation, unlike ABO blood group. **[2]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** Pollen nucleus 12 [1]; zygote 24 [1]. **(b)** One copy of each chromosome comes from each parent, so the zygote has homologous pairs [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* the pollen nucleus is haploid and the zygote diploid; give both numbers and do not swap them.

**2.** Remove the anthers from the red-fruited flower before they release pollen [1]. Cover the flower to keep out foreign pollen [1]. Transfer pollen from the yellow-fruited plant to the stigma [1]. Collect the seeds and grow them to score the F1 [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* name the part removed and say why; "stop it self-pollinating" without emasculation is incomplete.

**3. (a)** Having two identical alleles of a gene [1]. **(b)** Gg is green [1]. G codes for a functional polypeptide, such as an enzyme [1]. One copy makes enough product, so Gg matches GG [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* "dominant masks recessive" is a restatement of the term; an explanation needs the working polypeptide.

**4. (a)** ABO: genotype only [1]; scars: environment only [1]; body mass: both [1]. **(b)** The genotype is identical, so the difference is caused by the environment altering gene expression [1]. Plasticity lets the plant develop a form matched to its conditions, with no change to the DNA [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* "a mutation occurred" is a tempting wrong answer; state that the genotype is unchanged.

**5. (a)** Both parents are Aa [1]. **(b)** Gametes A or a from each parent [1]; the child is aa with probability 1/4 [1]. **(c)** The enzyme that converts phenylalanine to tyrosine is non-functional [1], so phenylalanine accumulates [1]. **(d)** The diet reduces phenylalanine intake, an environmental change, while the child's alleles stay aa [1]. **(e)** Close relatives share recent ancestors [1], so both partners are more likely to carry the same rare recessive allele [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* in "calculate", show the gametes or grid before the fraction.

**6. (a)** A position in the genome where individuals differ by a single base [1]. **(b)** Two [1]; a diploid person inherits one allele from each parent [1]. **(c)** Genotypes = 5 × 6 ÷ 2 [1] = **15** [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* show the method, and remember that homozygous genotypes count as well as heterozygous ones.

**7. (a)** Child ii, so each parent gave i [1]. Mother Iᴬi, father Iᴮi [1]. **(b)** Gametes Iᴬ or i; Iᴮ or i [1]. IᴬIᴮ is one of four equally likely outcomes, so **1/4** [1]. **(c)** The heterozygote has both alleles [1] and both are expressed, giving a dual phenotype [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* write genotypes with the superscripts; a plain "A" and "B" does not show the alleles.

**8. (a)** Cross CᴿCᵂ × CᴿCᵂ gives 1 red : 2 pink : 1 white [1]. Red = 80 and white = 80 [1], pink = 160 [1]. **(b)** In incomplete dominance the heterozygote has an intermediate phenotype [1]; in codominance it shows both alleles' phenotypes at once [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* give expected counts, not just the ratio, when the question supplies a total.

**9. (a)** Gametes: father Xʰ or Y; mother Xᴴ or Xʰ [1]. Daughters are XᴴXʰ and XʰXʰ [1]. Half of daughters are affected: **1/2** [1]. **(b)** Males have one X [1], so one recessive allele gives the disorder; a female needs two [1]. **(c)** He gives his son the Y, not an X [1]. **(d)** Eggs always carry X [1]; a sperm carrying X gives a girl, and one carrying Y gives male-typical characteristics [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* "males are affected more" needs the reason that males have a single X.

**10. (a)** Affected parents I-1 and I-2 have an unaffected daughter [1], so the condition is dominant and both parents carry a recessive allele [1]. Affected father I-1 has an unaffected daughter, so it is not X-linked dominant, as all his daughters would be affected: autosomal [1]. **(b)** I-1 is Dd, since his daughter II-2 is dd and received d from him [1]. II-3 is Dd, since III-1 is dd and received d from her [1]. **(c)** Dd × dd: gametes D or d; d [1]. Probability = **1/2** [1]. **(d)** Concluding autosomal dominant from this family's pattern is inductive [1]; using that rule to assign II-3's genotype is deductive [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* ruling out the alternatives is rewarded; naming the pattern alone is less convincing.

**11. (a)** Median = (30 + 31) ÷ 2 = **30.5** [1]. First quartile = median of the lower six = (27 + 27) ÷ 2 = **27**; third quartile = median of the upper six = (34 + 35) ÷ 2 = **34.5** [1]. IQR = 34.5 − 27 = **7.5** [1]. **(b)** Upper limit = 34.5 + 1.5 × 7.5 = 45.75 [1]; 52 exceeds it, so 52 is an outlier [1]. **(c)** 23 and 36 [1]. **(d)** Mean = 384 ÷ 12 = **32.0** cm [1]; mode = **27** cm [1]. **(e)** Height can take any value in a range, while ABO falls into four categories [1]. Many genes and the environment, such as nutrition, contribute [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* show the limit calculation; stating "52 is far from the others" is not a test.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Saying a dominant allele "overpowers" the recessive instead of describing the polypeptide.
- Writing genotypes such as H and h without the X.
- Treating plasticity as a mutation or a change of genotype.
- Giving a ratio when a probability, a count or a percentage is asked for.
- Forgetting that a male passes his Y, not an X, to his sons.
- Naming an outlier without calculating the 1.5 × IQR limits.
- Saying a pedigree is "recessive" without ruling out other patterns.
- Confusing codominance with incomplete dominance.
