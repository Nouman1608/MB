## Questions

**1.** Define investment appraisal and explain one reason why a business needs to use it. **[3]**

**2.** Orrin Hill Vineyard is considering a press costing £96,000. Expected net cash inflows are £24,000, £30,000, £30,000, £24,000 and £18,000 in Years 1 to 5. Calculate the payback period in years and months, assuming inflows arrive evenly through each year. **[3]**

**3.** Kelsingham Outdoor will buy a tent-making machine for £180,000. It is expected to last 5 years, with net cash inflows of £50,000, £60,000, £70,000, £60,000 and £40,000, and then be sold for £20,000.
**(a)** Calculate the accounting rate of return (ARR). **[4]**
**(b)** The company's target ARR is 20%. State whether the machine meets it. **[1]**

**4.** Wilmerby Freight can buy a van for £60,000. Expected net cash inflows are £22,000, £24,000 and £26,000 in Years 1 to 3. Discount factors at 6% are 0.943, 0.890 and 0.840. At 10% they are 0.909, 0.826 and 0.751.
**(a)** Calculate the net present value (NPV) at 6%. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate the NPV at 10% and state how the decision changes. **[3]**

**5.** Haverdene Bakery plans to replace its ovens. Explain two qualitative factors it should consider before deciding. **[4]**

**6.** Explain two limitations of using ratio analysis of published accounts to judge a business's performance. **[4]**

**7.** Tressock Tools had profit for the year of £450,000, an annual dividend of £150,000 and 1.5 million shares in issue. Its market price per share is 360p.
**(a)** Calculate dividend cover. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate dividend yield. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the price/earnings ratio. **[2]**

**8.** Explain two ways in which the annual report of a company could be useful to a bank deciding whether to lend to it. **[4]**

**9.** Fordhampton Textiles has issued shares of £5.0m, reserves of £3.0m and non-current liabilities of £2.0m (interest 6% a year). Profit from operations is £1.2m, tax is 20% of profit before tax and the annual dividend is £0.40m. It needs £2.5m for new looms that will add £0.5m to profit from operations. It can issue shares or borrow at 8%.
**(a)** Calculate gearing now and after each funding option. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate dividend cover after each option, assuming the dividend is unchanged. **[3]**
**(c)** Analyse why ROCE is the same under both options. **[2]**

**10.** Tressock Tools (question 7) cuts its dividend to £75,000.
**(a)** Calculate the new dividend cover and dividend yield. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain one effect of the cut on its shareholders. **[2]**

**11.** Orrin Hill Vineyard must choose between a bottling plant (Project X) and a tasting room (Project Y), each costing £120,000 with no residual value. Its target ARR is 20% and it wants payback within 3 years. Results are below, with NPV at a 10% discount rate. Project X would need three new staff; Project Y depends on summer tourism.

| | Project X | Project Y |
|---|---|---|
| Payback | 3 years | 2 years 4 months |
| ARR | 23.3% | 16.7% |
| NPV | £23,810 | £18,240 |

Evaluate which project the vineyard should choose. **[10]**

**12.** A cafe chain's revenue rises by 30% after it opens four new branches. Explain two ratios that might worsen in the short term. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** Investment appraisal is the process of assessing whether a proposed capital project is worth its cost [1]. It is needed because capital spending is large and hard to reverse [1], so the business must check the likely return before committing funds [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* a definition saying only "looking at an investment" is thin; credit tends to follow when the answer says what is judged (return against cost) and then develops a reason.

**2.** Cumulative inflows: £24,000, £54,000, £84,000 [1]. £12,000 is still needed after Year 3, and Year 4 brings £24,000, so 12,000 / 24,000 = 0.5 of a year [1]. Payback is **3 years 6 months** [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* the cumulative line shows the method even if the final step slips; a decimal answer of 3.5 without converting may lose credit when the question asks for years and months.

**3. (a)** Total inflows = £280,000, depreciation = 180,000 − 20,000 = £160,000, so total profit = £120,000 [1]. Average profit = 120,000 / 5 = £24,000 [1]. Average investment = (180,000 + 20,000) / 2 = £100,000 [1]. ARR = 24,000 / 100,000 × 100 = **24%** [1].
**(b)** Yes, 24% is above 20% [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* writing the formula and the definition of average investment lets a reader follow your method; the final judgement should refer to the 20% target, not just state the percentage.

**4. (a)** Present values: 22,000 × 0.943 = £20,746; 24,000 × 0.890 = £21,360; 26,000 × 0.840 = £21,840 [1]. Total = £63,946 [1]. NPV = 63,946 − 60,000 = **£3,946** [1].
**(b)** Present values at 10%: £19,998; £19,824; £19,526, total £59,348 [1]. NPV = **−£652** [1]. At 6% the van is worthwhile, but at 10% the NPV is negative, so it would be rejected [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* the initial cost is paid now and is not discounted; a negative result should be linked to a decision, since the number alone does not answer "how the decision changes".

**5.** Two factors, each identified and developed. Employees [1]: new ovens may need retraining or change shift patterns, which affects morale and output [1]. Reputation and product quality [1]: more consistent baking could protect the brand with customers, while disruption during installation could harm it [1]. (Environmental impact is another valid factor.) **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* answers tend to score better when the factor is tied to a bakery (ovens, bread quality, energy) rather than listed in the abstract.

**6.** Two limitations. Accounts are historical [1], so ratios may not predict future performance when conditions change [1]. Firms may use different accounting policies [1], which weakens comparison with a competitor [1]. (Ignoring non-financial factors and window dressing are other valid limitations.) **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* a bare list ("historical, not comparable") tends to earn less than a limitation that says what goes wrong for the person using the ratio.

**7. (a)** Dividend cover = profit for the year / annual dividend = 450,000 / 150,000 [1] = **3.0** [1].
**(b)** Dividend per share = 150,000 / 1,500,000 = 10p [1]. Yield = 10 / 360 × 100 = **2.8%** [1].
**(c)** EPS = 450,000 / 1,500,000 = 30p [1]. P/E = 360 / 30 = **12.0** [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* dividend yield and dividend cover use different bases (market price and profit for the year); stating the formula before substituting protects the method.

**8.** Two ways. The statement of financial position shows existing non-current liabilities [1], so the bank can judge gearing and the risk of adding more debt [1]. The statement of profit or loss shows profit from operations and interest [1], which helps judge whether repayments are affordable [1]. (The directors' report on plans and risks, or the auditor's report, are other valid links.) **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* name the stakeholder's decision (lend or not) and link each item of the report to it; a general description of the report earns little.

**9. (a)** Now: capital employed = 5.0 + 3.0 + 2.0 = £10.0m, gearing = 2.0 / 10.0 × 100 = **20%** [1]. Shares: capital employed £12.5m, gearing = 2.0 / 12.5 = **16%** [1]. Loan: non-current liabilities £4.5m, gearing = 4.5 / 12.5 = **36%** [1].
**(b)** Operating profit after the project = £1.7m. Shares: interest £0.12m, profit before tax £1.58m, profit for the year £1.264m [1], cover = 1.264 / 0.40 = **3.16** [1]. Loan: interest 0.12 + 0.20 = £0.32m, profit for the year (1.7 − 0.32) × 0.8 = £1.104m, cover = **2.76** [1].
**(c)** ROCE uses profit from operations, which is before interest [1], and both options give the same £1.7m and the same capital employed of £12.5m, so ROCE is 13.6% either way [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* analysis improves when you say which ratio the funding choice moves (gearing, cover) and which it does not (ROCE), then link to risk.

**10. (a)** New cover = 450,000 / 75,000 = **6.0** [1]. New dividend per share = 5p [1]. Yield = 5 / 360 × 100 = **1.4%** [1].
**(b)** Income-seeking shareholders receive half the cash they expected [1], so some may sell, and the share price could fall [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* effects on shareholders gain credit when they follow from the numbers (halved yield) rather than a general claim that shareholders will be unhappy.

**11.** Quantitative: X meets the 20% target and the 3-year payback limit, but only just on payback; Y misses the ARR target [1], though it pays back faster [1]. X has the higher NPV by £5,570 [1], so on value X looks better [1]. Qualitative: X needs three new staff, which adds recurring cost and recruitment risk, but also creates skills and jobs [1]. Y depends on summer tourism, so its cash flows are less reliable, and weather could cut inflows [1]. Y's early cash helps liquidity [1]. Judgement: choose X, because it meets both criteria and has the higher NPV [1], provided the staff cost is already in the forecast [1]. The decision would change if the vineyard faced a cash shortage or if the discount rate were much higher, since early cash then matters more [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* evaluation answers tend to score higher when they weigh both sets of evidence, say what would change the decision, and finish on a clear choice; repeating the table without comment stays at description.

**12.** Two ratios. Operating profit margin may fall [1], because new branches add rent and wages before they reach full sales [1]. Rate of inventory turnover may fall [1], because stock is held in every new branch before sales build up [1]. (The current ratio or acid test is another valid choice.) **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* tie each ratio to a cause from the case (new branches), and avoid asserting a ratio worsens without saying why.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Discounting the initial cost or forgetting to subtract it in NPV.
- Not stating how average investment was found in ARR.
- Leaving payback as a decimal when the answer should be in years and months.
- Stating a number with no link to the decision or target rate.
- Treating qualitative factors as a list rather than developing one or two in context.
- Mixing up ROCE (before interest) with profit for the year ratios.
- Judging gearing as good or bad without risk and context.
- Evaluating with one side only or no final judgement.
