Cambridge International AS & A Level Biology (9700) is a two-stage course
for students who want to study living systems in depth, from the
structure of a single cell to the conservation of whole ecosystems. The
syllabus edition covered here is for examination in 2025, 2026 and 2027.
The full A Level is assessed through five externally assessed papers:
three at AS Level (Papers 1 to 3, worth 15.5%, 23% and 11.5% of the full
A Level) and two at A Level (Papers 4 and 5, worth 38.5% and 11.5%).

## How the course is organised

The syllabus has 19 numbered topics. Topics 1 to 11 form the AS content
and Topics 12 to 19 form the additional A Level content.

**AS Level: the cell and the body (Topics 1 to 11).** The AS topics move
outward from the cell. Topics 1 to 6 build the molecular and cellular
foundation: cell structure (including the microscope in cell studies),
biological molecules, enzymes, cell membranes and transport, the mitotic
cell cycle, and nucleic acids and protein synthesis. Topics 7 to 9 apply
that foundation to whole-organism systems: transport in plants, transport
in mammals (the circulatory system, oxygen and carbon dioxide transport,
and the heart) and gas exchange. Topics 10 and 11 close the AS stage with
infectious diseases and immunity, including antibiotics, antibodies and
vaccination.

**A Level: energy, control, inheritance and populations (Topics 12 to
19).** The A Level topics fall into three groups. Energy and respiration
and Photosynthesis (Topics 12 and 13) deal with how organisms transfer
energy. Homeostasis and Control and coordination (Topics 14 and 15) cover
regulation in both mammals and plants. Inheritance, Selection and
evolution, Classification, biodiversity and conservation, and Genetic
technology (Topics 16 to 19) carry the course from genes and phenotype up
to variation, evolution, conservation and the applied use of genetic
technology in medicine and agriculture.

## How it's assessed

| Paper | Title | Duration | Marks | Weighting (full A Level) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Paper 1 | Multiple Choice | 75 minutes | 40 | 15.5% |
| Paper 2 | AS Level Structured Questions | 75 minutes | 60 | 23% |
| Paper 3 | Advanced Practical Skills | 120 minutes | 40 | 11.5% |
| Paper 4 | A Level Structured Questions | 120 minutes | 100 | 38.5% |
| Paper 5 | Planning, Analysis and Evaluation | 75 minutes | 30 | 11.5% |

All five papers are externally assessed. Paper 3 is a practical paper;
the other four are written examinations.

The course is staged. Papers 1 to 3 can be certificated on their own as a
standalone Cambridge International AS Level, in which case they are
weighted 31%, 46% and 23% of the AS Level. Alternatively, a candidate
continues to Papers 4 and 5 for the full A Level, and the weightings in
the table above apply. Your school decides which route you are entered
for, so check this early, because it changes how much each AS paper
counts.

## How the parts fit together

The AS topics are not a separate course that is finished and forgotten.
Later A Level topics rely on them directly. Energy and respiration and
Photosynthesis depend on what you learned about enzymes, membranes and
cell structure. Inheritance and Genetic technology build on nucleic acids
and protein synthesis and on the mitotic cell cycle. Homeostasis and
Control and coordination extend the transport and gas exchange topics
into questions about regulation. Because of this, it pays to keep the AS
topics fresh while studying Topics 12 to 19, rather than treating the A
Level content in isolation.

Practical and data skills form the second thread. Paper 3 tests practical
skills directly, and Paper 5 asks you to plan investigations and analyse
and evaluate data in writing. Topics such as Testing for biological
molecules, Factors that affect enzyme action and Investigation of limiting
factors in photosynthesis are natural places to practise this way of
thinking, since each one is built around variables, measurements and
conclusions.

## Planning your study

A sensible order follows the syllabus numbering, because each block
prepares the next:

- **First stage:** Topics 1 to 6, building cell and molecular biology,
  with regular short practical write-ups alongside.
- **Second stage:** Topics 7 to 11, applying that knowledge to transport,
  gas exchange, disease and immunity, then consolidating all AS content
  before any AS papers.
- **Third stage:** Topics 12 to 15, energy transfer and control, revisiting
  enzymes and membranes as you go.
- **Fourth stage:** Topics 16 to 19, inheritance through to genetic
  technology, followed by whole-course revision.

Start using past papers once a block of topics is complete rather than
waiting until the end of the course. Multiple-choice and structured
questions on finished topics are a useful check on understanding. Leave
full timed papers and paper-specific technique to the final revision
period, and use the exam preparation page for that stage.

## Frequently asked questions

**How many papers are there in Cambridge A Level Biology 9700?**
There are five papers. Papers 1, 2 and 3 make up the AS Level, and Papers 4 and 5 complete the full A Level. All five are externally assessed.

**Can I take Cambridge AS Level Biology on its own?**
Yes. Papers 1 to 3 can be certificated as a standalone Cambridge International AS Level, weighted 31%, 46% and 23% of the AS Level. A candidate can instead continue to Papers 4 and 5 for the full A Level.

**Which paper carries the most weight in 9700?**
Paper 4, A Level Structured Questions, is worth 38.5% of the full A Level. It lasts 120 minutes and carries 100 marks.

**Is there a practical exam in A Level Biology 9700?**
Paper 3, Advanced Practical Skills, is a practical paper lasting 120 minutes and worth 11.5% of the full A Level. Paper 5, Planning, Analysis and Evaluation, is a written paper that also assesses practical and data-handling skills.

**Which topics are A Level only?**
Topics 12 to 19 are A Level content: Energy and respiration, Photosynthesis, Homeostasis, Control and coordination, Inheritance, Selection and evolution, Classification, biodiversity and conservation, and Genetic technology.
