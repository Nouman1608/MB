The specification flags three misconceptions: that small rises in carbon dioxide cannot matter in so large an atmosphere, that the ozone hole causes global warming, and that humans alone cause the greenhouse effect. Each is corrected below.

## How the first atmosphere formed (C6.3a)

The Earth formed about 4.5 billion years ago. Its early atmosphere is thought to have come from gases released by **volcanoes**, and to have been mostly **carbon dioxide** and **water vapour**, with some **nitrogen**, possibly small amounts of methane and ammonia, and little or no **oxygen**.

As the surface cooled below 100 °C, water vapour **condensed** and fell as rain, forming the oceans. Carbon dioxide then fell steadily because:

1. it **dissolved in the oceans** and ended up in **carbonate** rocks such as limestone;
2. **photosynthesis** by early organisms turned it into carbon compounds;
3. carbon from living things was locked away in **fossil fuels** and sedimentary rocks.

Unreactive nitrogen built up slowly and was not removed. Today dry air is about 78% nitrogen, 21% oxygen, just under 1% argon and roughly 0.04% carbon dioxide.

**Why "it is thought".** Nobody measured the early atmosphere. The evidence is indirect: gases from volcanoes today, the chemistry of very old rocks, and models of a cooling planet. Use cautious words such as "suggests".

**Worked example 1 (orders of magnitude, CM6.3ii).** One model of the early atmosphere (an illustrative figure) gives carbon dioxide as 70% of the gas. The air at Mauna Loa in May 2025 averaged about 430 parts per million (ppm) of carbon dioxide. Compare the two.

- 1% = 10 000 ppm, so 430 ppm = 430 ÷ 10 000 = 0.043%
- ratio = 70 ÷ 0.043 ≈ 1600
- 1600 is between 10³ and 10⁴, closest to 10³

The early proportion was roughly **three orders of magnitude** (about a thousand times) larger.

## How oxygen built up (C6.3b)

Early oceans contained simple organisms. When **cyanobacteria** and later algae evolved, they began **photosynthesis**:

6CO₂ + 6H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂

At first the oxygen reacted with **dissolved iron** in seawater, so little reached the air. Iron oxides settled out as **banded iron formations**, layered rocks that are part of the evidence. Once the iron was used up, oxygen collected in the atmosphere. The first large rise, called the Great Oxidation Event, is dated to about 2.4 billion years ago.

Oxygen then rose slowly to levels that could support animals. On time-scales (M2h): the oxygen build-up took **billions** of years, while the recent rise in carbon dioxide took about **two hundred** years, roughly seven orders of magnitude shorter. Ideas about this history have changed as new evidence, such as rock dating, appeared (Working Scientifically 1.1a).

## The greenhouse effect (C6.3c)

The greenhouse effect is about how **radiation interacts with molecules** in the atmosphere.

1. The Sun emits mostly **short-wavelength** radiation (visible light and some ultraviolet). Most of it passes through the atmosphere and is absorbed by the Earth's surface, which warms up.
2. The warm surface emits **longer-wavelength infrared** radiation.
3. **Greenhouse gases**, such as water vapour, carbon dioxide and methane, **absorb** some of this infrared radiation. Their molecules then **re-emit** the energy, sending part of it down to the ground again.
4. Less energy escapes to space, so the lower atmosphere and the surface are warmer than they would otherwise be.

Nitrogen and oxygen, the main gases in air, absorb almost none of this infrared radiation.

Two corrections:

- The greenhouse effect is **natural**. Without it the average surface temperature would be around −18 °C instead of about 15 °C. Humans are **enhancing** it.
- The **ozone hole** lets more ultraviolet reach the ground. It does not cause global warming.

## Evidence for human-caused climate change (C6.3d)

The key evidence in the specification is the **correlation** between carbon dioxide in the atmosphere and the burning of **fossil fuels**. Carbon dioxide was about 280 ppm before the Industrial Revolution; its monthly average passed 430 ppm in 2025. Over the same period, fossil fuel use rose steeply.

Other evidence supports the link: average temperatures have risen alongside carbon dioxide, and air bubbles in ice cores show past carbon dioxide levels and temperatures moving together.

**Worked example 2 (reading a table, CM6.3i).** The table gives rounded annual mean carbon dioxide levels at one monitoring station.

| Year | Carbon dioxide / ppm |
|---|---|
| 1970 | 326 |
| 1990 | 354 |
| 2010 | 390 |
| 2020 | 414 |

Calculate the average yearly rise in each interval and describe the trend.

- 1970–1990: (354 − 326) ÷ 20 = 28 ÷ 20 = 1.4 ppm per year
- 1990–2010: (390 − 354) ÷ 20 = 36 ÷ 20 = 1.8 ppm per year
- 2010–2020: (414 − 390) ÷ 10 = 24 ÷ 10 = 2.4 ppm per year

The level rises in every interval and the **rate of rise is increasing**. Overall, the 1970–2020 increase is 88 ppm, about 27% of the 1970 value.

**Uncertainties you must be able to describe:**

- **Correlation is not proof of cause.** Two quantities can rise together for other reasons. The case is strong because there is also a mechanism (C6.3c).
- **Natural factors** also change climate: variation in the Sun's output, large volcanic eruptions and ocean cycles.
- **Older data are indirect.** Ice cores, tree rings and sediments are proxies, and early thermometer records covered fewer places.
- **Climate models** are simplifications. Clouds and feedbacks, such as melting ice reflecting less sunlight, are hard to model precisely.

## Effects and mitigation (C6.3e)

Carbon dioxide and methane are both greenhouse gases. Methane comes from cattle, rice paddies, landfill and gas leaks. Its level has more than doubled since pre-industrial times, and each molecule warms more than a carbon dioxide molecule.

**Potential effects of increased levels:**

- rising average temperatures;
- **sea-level rise**, from water expanding as it warms and from melting land ice, flooding low-lying coasts;
- more frequent or more severe **extreme weather**, such as heatwaves, droughts and storms;
- changes in where and how much rain falls, affecting **food production** and water supplies;
- changes to habitats, so some species move or decline.

**Mitigation:**

| Approach | How it helps | A limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Renewable or nuclear electricity | Less fossil fuel burned | Cost, intermittency, public concern |
| Energy efficiency (insulation, efficient vehicles) | Less energy needed | Needs investment by households and firms |
| Carbon capture and storage | Carbon dioxide stored underground instead of released | Expensive; not yet used widely |
| Planting trees | Photosynthesis removes carbon dioxide | Needs large areas of land; slow |
| Cutting methane (capturing landfill gas, fixing gas leaks) | Methane has a strong warming effect per molecule | Hard to control emissions from farming |
| Carbon taxes and emission limits | Make low-carbon choices cheaper | Political disagreement; need international agreement |

Weigh **scale, risk and environmental implications** (Working Scientifically 1.1f, Working Scientifically 1.1h): one country acting alone changes little; some effects are unlikely but very serious; some fixes, such as large dams, bring problems of their own.

## Air pollutants (C6.3f)

| Pollutant | Main source | Problems caused |
|---|---|---|
| **Carbon monoxide**, CO | Incomplete combustion of carbon-containing fuels, e.g. engines, faulty boilers | Toxic: combines with haemoglobin so blood carries less oxygen; colourless and odourless |
| **Sulfur dioxide**, SO₂ | Sulfur impurities in fossil fuels, especially coal and some fuel oils, burning: S + O₂ → SO₂ | Causes **acid rain**, which damages buildings, trees and lakes; irritates the lungs |
| **Oxides of nitrogen**, NO and NO₂ | At engine temperatures, atmospheric nitrogen combines with oxygen: N₂ + O₂ → 2NO, then 2NO + O₂ → 2NO₂ | Acid rain; breathing problems; photochemical smog |
| **Particulates** (soot) | Incomplete combustion, especially in diesel engines | Lung and heart disease; darken buildings; can reduce sunlight reaching the ground (global dimming) |

Oxides of nitrogen do **not** come from nitrogen in the fuel. They form from the air.

**Worked example 3.** Pentane, C₅H₁₂, burns in a poorly ventilated heater. Write equations for burning in plenty of air, in limited air forming carbon monoxide, and in very limited air forming carbon.

- plenty of air: C₅H₁₂ + 8O₂ → 5CO₂ + 6H₂O
- limited air: 2C₅H₁₂ + 11O₂ → 10CO + 12H₂O
- very limited: C₅H₁₂ + 3O₂ → 5C + 6H₂O

Check the oxygen atoms each time: 16 = 10 + 6; 22 = 10 + 12; 6 = 6.

**Worked example 4.** A school boiler burns 1200 kg of heating oil containing 0.80% sulfur by mass. All the sulfur becomes sulfur dioxide. Relative atomic masses: S = 32, O = 16. Calculate the mass of sulfur dioxide.

- mass of sulfur = 1200 × 0.80 ÷ 100 = 9.6 kg
- Mr of SO₂ = 32 + 16 + 16 = 64, so 32 kg of S gives 64 kg of SO₂
- mass of SO₂ = 9.6 × 64 ÷ 32 = 19.2 kg

## Potable water (C6.3g)

**Potable water** is water that is safe to drink. It is not pure: it still contains low levels of dissolved salts. The method depends on the source.

**Ground water and fresh surface water** (aquifers, rivers, reservoirs):

1. **Sedimentation**: water stands so that larger solid particles settle.
2. **Filtration** through beds of sand and gravel removes smaller insoluble particles.
3. **Sterilisation** with chlorine, ozone or ultraviolet light kills microbes.

**Waste water** needs extra stages first: screening out large objects and grit, settling, and bacteria breaking down organic matter.

**Salt water** needs **desalination**:

- **distillation**: boil the water and condense the steam, leaving the salts behind;
- **reverse osmosis**: force water at high pressure through a membrane that lets water molecules through but holds back salt ions.

**Ease of treatment.** Ground water is usually easiest, as rock has already filtered it. Waste water needs more stages. Salt water is hardest because desalination needs a lot of energy. Distillation and filtration are the same separation techniques you met in C2.1; see [the C2.1 separation methods page](/resources/ocr-gcse-chemistry-purity-and-separating-mixtures/).

**Worked example 5.** A coastal town needs 20 000 m³ of water a day. Use these values: reverse osmosis uses 3 kWh per m³ and distillation uses 30 kWh per m³. Compare the daily energy.

- reverse osmosis: 20 000 × 3 = 60 000 kWh
- distillation: 20 000 × 30 = 600 000 kWh
- ratio = 30 ÷ 3 = 10

Distillation needs about ten times as much energy: one order of magnitude more.

## Common errors

- Writing that greenhouse gases "reflect" heat. They **absorb** and **re-emit** infrared radiation.
- Linking global warming to the ozone hole.
- Saying oxides of nitrogen come from nitrogen in the fuel.
- Calling potable water "pure".
