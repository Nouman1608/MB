## Section A

**1.** A phone charger supplies a steady current of 1.8 A for 45 minutes. Calculate the charge that flows. **[2]**

**2.** A neutral metal sphere loses 2.5 × 10¹² electrons. State the sign of its charge and calculate its magnitude. **[2]**

**3.** State, with a reason, whether a net charge of 1.12 × 10⁻¹⁸ C is possible on an object. **[2]**

**4.** In an electrolyte, positive ions move to the left and negative ions move to the right. Explain why both contribute to a current in the same direction. **[2]**

**5.** The current in a circuit falls uniformly from 0.80 A to zero in 6.0 s. Calculate the charge that flows in this time. **[2]**

## Section B

**6.** The current in a component is 0.20 A from t = 0 to t = 3.0 s. It then rises uniformly to 0.50 A at t = 7.0 s, and then falls uniformly to zero at t = 9.0 s.

**(a)** Calculate the total charge that flows. **[3]**

**(b)** Calculate the mean current over the 9.0 s. **[1]**

**7.** In a solution, zinc ions Zn²⁺ carry a current of 0.48 A towards the cathode.

**(a)** Calculate the number of zinc ions reaching the cathode each second. **[2]**

**(b)** At the same time, 2.0 × 10¹⁸ chloride ions Cl⁻ move towards the anode each second. Calculate the total current in the solution. **[2]**

**8.** A battery supplies 4.0 A to junction X. From X, three parallel branches leave: lamp L1 carries current I₁, lamp L2 carries 1.5 A and a resistor carries 0.6 A. The branches rejoin at junction Y, from which a single wire returns to the battery.

**(a)** Calculate I₁. **[1]**

**(b)** State the current in the wire from Y to the battery. **[1]**

**(c)** Explain your answer to part (b) in terms of charge. **[1]**

**9.** Silver has density 10 500 kg m⁻³ and molar mass 0.108 kg mol⁻¹. Assume each atom provides one free electron.

**(a)** Show that the number density of free electrons in silver is about 6 × 10²⁸ m⁻³. **[2]**

**(b)** A silver wire of diameter 0.80 mm carries 3.0 A. Calculate the mean drift velocity of the electrons. **[3]**

**10.** A copper wire (n = 8.5 × 10²⁸ m⁻³, cross-sectional area 1.0 mm²) is joined in series with an alloy wire (n = 3.4 × 10²⁸ m⁻³, cross-sectional area 0.50 mm²). Calculate the ratio of the drift velocity in the alloy to the drift velocity in the copper. **[3]**

**11.** The number density of charge carriers in a semiconductor sample rises from 2.0 × 10¹⁹ m⁻³ to 6.0 × 10¹⁹ m⁻³ when it is warmed.

**(a)** For the same current, state the factor by which the drift velocity changes. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain, in terms of n, why semiconductors are placed between conductors and insulators. **[2]**

**12.** The mean drift velocity of electrons in a lamp circuit is less than 1 mm s⁻¹, yet the lamp lights as soon as the switch is closed. Explain why. **[2]**

**13.** An electron beam in a vacuum tube carries a current of 50 μA.

**(a)** Calculate the number of electrons arriving at the screen each second. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the charge arriving in 2.0 minutes. **[1]**

## Answers

**1.** t = 45 × 60 = 2700 s [1]. Q = It = 1.8 × 2700 = **4.9 × 10³ C** (4860 C) [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Losing electrons leaves the sphere **positive** [1]. Q = 2.5 × 10¹² × 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ = **4.0 × 10⁻⁷ C** [1]. **[2]**

**3.** 1.12 × 10⁻¹⁸ ÷ 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 7.0 [1]. This is a whole number of elementary charges, so the charge **is possible** (7 electrons lost) [1]. **[2]**

*Examiner insight:* Show the division by e; the conclusion depends on the result being a whole number.

**4.** Current is the rate of flow of charge, and its direction is defined as the direction of positive charge flow [1]. Negative charge moving right has the same effect on the net charge on each side as positive charge moving left, so both contributions are to the left and they add [1]. **[2]**

**5.** Charge = area under the I-t graph = ½ × 6.0 × 0.80 [1] = **2.4 C** [1]. **[2]**

**6. (a)** 0 to 3.0 s: 0.20 × 3.0 = 0.60 C [1]. 3.0 to 7.0 s: trapezium ½(0.20 + 0.50) × 4.0 = 1.40 C; 7.0 to 9.0 s: triangle ½ × 2.0 × 0.50 = 0.50 C [1]. Total = **2.5 C** [1].

**(b)** Mean current = 2.5 ÷ 9.0 = **0.28 A** [1]. **[4]**

*Examiner insight:* Split the graph into rectangles, triangles and trapezia and write each area separately so a single slip does not cost every mark.

**7. (a)** Charge per ion = 2e = 3.2 × 10⁻¹⁹ C [1]. Ions per second = 0.48 ÷ 3.2 × 10⁻¹⁹ = **1.5 × 10¹⁸** [1].

**(b)** Chloride current = 2.0 × 10¹⁸ × 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 0.32 A [1]. Total = 0.48 + 0.32 = **0.80 A** [1]. **[4]**

*Examiner insight:* The two ion currents add, because the ions have opposite charges and move in opposite directions.

**8. (a)** 4.0 = I₁ + 1.5 + 0.6, so **I₁ = 1.9 A** [1].

**(b)** **4.0 A** [1].

**(c)** Charge is conserved and does not build up at Y, so the total charge per second leaving Y equals the total arriving, 1.9 + 1.5 + 0.6 = 4.0 A [1]. **[3]**

**9. (a)** Atoms per m³ = (ρ ÷ M) × N_A = (10 500 ÷ 0.108) × 6.02 × 10²³ [1] = 5.9 × 10²⁸ m⁻³, which is about **6 × 10²⁸ m⁻³** [1].

**(b)** A = π(0.40 × 10⁻³)² = 5.03 × 10⁻⁷ m² [1]. v = I ÷ (Ane) = 3.0 ÷ (5.03 × 10⁻⁷ × 5.85 × 10²⁸ × 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹) [1] = **6.4 × 10⁻⁴ m s⁻¹** [1]. **[5]**

*Examiner insight:* In a "show that", give the unrounded value (5.85 × 10²⁸ m⁻³) before comparing it with the value stated.

**10.** The current is the same in both, so v ∝ 1 ÷ (nA) [1]. v(alloy) ÷ v(copper) = (n_Cu × A_Cu) ÷ (n_alloy × A_alloy) = (8.5 × 10²⁸ × 1.0) ÷ (3.4 × 10²⁸ × 0.50) [1] = **5.0** [1]. **[3]**

*Examiner insight:* The areas appear as a ratio, so they need not be converted to m²; say so, or convert both.

**11. (a)** n is three times larger, so v becomes **one third** of its value [1].

**(b)** Metals have very large n, so they conduct well with small drift velocities; insulators have extremely small n, so almost no current flows [1]. Semiconductors have n far smaller than metals but much larger than insulators, so their ability to conduct lies in between (and increases as n rises) [1]. **[3]**

**12.** When the switch closes, the electric field is set up round the whole circuit almost instantly (close to the speed of light) [1]. Free electrons everywhere in the circuit, including in the lamp filament, begin drifting at the same time, so current flows in the lamp at once [1]. **[2]**

**13. (a)** Electrons per second = I ÷ e = 50 × 10⁻⁶ ÷ 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ [1] = **3.1 × 10¹⁴ s⁻¹** [1].

**(b)** Q = It = 50 × 10⁻⁶ × 120 = **6.0 × 10⁻³ C** [1]. **[3]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Leaving time in minutes, or μA not converted to A.
- Using the height of an I-t graph as the charge.
- Using e rather than 2e for a doubly charged ion.
- Converting a diameter to an area incorrectly, or using the diameter where the radius belongs.
- Explaining Kirchhoff's first law without mentioning conservation of charge.
- Comparing materials by "how good a conductor" they are, instead of by n.

## Frequently asked questions

**How do I find the charge when the current is not steady?**
Find the area under the current-time graph. Split it into simple shapes, or count squares for a curve, and multiply by the value of one square.

**Do I need to know how to find n from density?**
The specification asks you to use I = Anev and to compare materials by n. Finding n from density, molar mass and the Avogadro constant is a way of combining it with other parts of the course, so it can appear with the data given.

**Why is the drift velocity larger in the thinner wire?**
The same current flows through both, so the same charge passes per second. With a smaller area, the carriers must drift faster to deliver that charge.
