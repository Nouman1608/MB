## Questions

**1.** Explain two reasons why the Partition of Bengal (1905) provoked opposition. **[4]**

**2.** Read the extract, which is *constructed for practice* and is not a real quotation.

> "A pinch of salt cost the villager nothing to pick up from the shore, yet the law called it a crime. In breaking it, an old man with a staff made every poor household feel it was part of the struggle." (A historian's summary, constructed for practice)

**(a)** Explain what the extract suggests about why the Salt March was effective. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain one limitation of the view that the Salt March alone forced Britain to change its policy. **[3]**

**3.** Analyse the impact of the Second World War on the movement for Indian independence. **[6]**

**4.** To what extent was Gandhi the most important leader in the achievement of Indian independence? **[15]**

**5.** Evaluate the reasons for the Partition of India in 1947. **[15]**

**6.** Explain how the princely states of Hyderabad and Kashmir were integrated into India. **[6]**

**7.** Explain two ways in which the Government of India Act (1935) affected Indian politics. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** Any two, each developed (1 mark for the reason, 1 for explanation). Bengalis saw the division as an attempt to split the Hindu and Muslim communities and weaken a centre of nationalist politics; Curzon's administrative argument was not accepted because it was done without consulting Indians; the new western province placed Bengali speakers in a minority, which caused fear of lost influence; it triggered the Swadeshi boycott, which showed the strength of feeling. **[4]**

**2.** **(a)** The extract suggests that salt was a simple, universal necessity (1), so an act of breaking the law was easy to understand and share (1). It suggests that Gandhi's personal example turned the poor into participants in the national movement (1). **[3]**
**(b)** Award up to 3. The Salt March was followed by years of civil disobedience, and the Gandhi–Irwin Pact (1931) brought only limited concessions (1); change in British policy also came from the 1935 Act, the financial cost of empire and the Second World War (1); Muslim League and other groups pursued separate aims, so the march did not unite all of India (1). **[3]**

**3.** Marking points (up to 6): Britain was weakened economically and militarily, and needed Indian co-operation and soldiers (up to 2); the Congress ministries resigned in 1939 and the Quit India movement of 1942 showed mass unrest, although it was repressed (up to 2); the war raised Jinnah's and the League's position as Congress leaders were jailed (up to 1); Bose's Indian National Army and the trials of 1945 to 1946 raised nationalist feeling and doubts about army loyalty (up to 1). **[6]**

**4.** Marking points (up to 15):
- A thesis and criteria for "most important" (up to 2).
- Gandhi: return in 1915, Non-Cooperation, Salt March, Quit India, the use of satyagraha and the appeal to the poor (up to 4).
- Other leaders: Nehru as Congress leader and negotiator, Jinnah in shaping the final settlement, Bose as a radical alternative, and Patel (up to 3).
- Other factors: the Second World War, British decisions and economic weakness, the Congress organisation (up to 3).
- Perspectives: nationalist accounts against the Cambridge school and Subaltern Studies (up to 1).
- A judgement consistent with the analysis (up to 2). **[15]**

**5.** Marking points (up to 15):
- A thesis that ranks causes (up to 2).
- Muslim separatism: the League, the Two-Nation theory, the Lahore Resolution of 1940, the 1937 elections (up to 3).
- British actions: separate electorates, the 1935 Act, the Cabinet Mission, haste under Mountbatten (up to 4).
- Congress policy and Hindu–Muslim relations, including the breakdown in 1937 to 1939 (up to 2).
- Violence: Direct Action Day and communal killing in 1946 (up to 2).
- A judgement, with Jalal's thesis or another view (up to 2). **[15]**

**6.** Marking points (up to 6): the princes had to accede to India or Pakistan under pressure from Patel and Menon (up to 1); Hyderabad's Nizam sought independence, India applied economic pressure and then used a police action in September 1948 (up to 2); in Kashmir the Hindu ruler hesitated, tribal forces from Pakistan invaded in October 1947, he signed accession to India, and war followed, ended by a United Nations ceasefire in 1949 (up to 2); credit a comment on the role of force and on the population mix in each state (up to 1). **[6]**

**7.** Any two, each developed (1 mark for the effect, 1 for explanation). It gave the provinces elected ministries with real powers, and in the 1937 elections Congress won majorities in most provinces, which gave it experience of government and prestige; it widened the electorate to a much larger share of the population, which encouraged mass party organisation; it kept separate electorates and planned a federation including the princes, which the League and Congress both distrusted, so the federation never came into being; the poor League result in 1937 and Congress's refusal to share power in some provinces fed Jinnah's claim that Muslims needed separate safeguards. **[4]**

## Frequently asked questions

**How much detail on Jallianwala Bagh is needed?**
Date, place, the role of General Dyer, the effect on opinion and a careful statement about casualty figures.

**Do I need to know about women in Partition?**
Yes. The guide names the experience of women and of different ethnic groups, so include at least one example.
