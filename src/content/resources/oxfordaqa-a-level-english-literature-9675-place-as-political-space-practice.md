## Section A: knowledge and concepts

**1.** Explain what makes a fictional place a "political space", giving two features you would look
for. **[3]**

**2.** Explain how a threshold such as a door, gate or border can carry political meaning in a novel.
**[2]**

**3.** Explain why a narrator's viewpoint matters when analysing the politics of a place. **[3]**

**4.** The specification also lists "how people are placed in society". Explain how this idea
connects to place as a political space. **[2]**

**5.** Explain how one Section A novel uses a small space to stand for a larger political order.
**[4]**

## Section B: an original passage and essay skills

**Passage (original).** Rania has returned to the city where she grew up, after ten years abroad.

> The office had moved since her last visit, from the old stone building by the square to a glass
> tower near the motorway. Inside, the queue for residence papers began at the door and folded back on
> itself four times between metal posts. A screen above the counter showed a waving flag and a
> number that did not change. Rania held her folder against her chest. In the city of her childhood
> she needed a document to prove she was allowed to wait. Behind her, a man explained to his son, in
> the language their family had spoken for three generations, why they must not speak it here.

**6.** Analyse how the writer presents the office as a political space. **[6]**

**7.** Explain how the passage connects place to identity and belonging. **[4]**

**8.** Explain how the final sentence changes the significance of the passage. **[2]**

**9.** A student wants to use this kind of passage to support an essay on *Americanah*. Explain two
links the student could make to the novel. **[4]**

**10.** For your own set novel, plan an essay on the question: "Explore the significance of power in
the places of the novel." List five elements of the plan. **[5]**

**11.** Read this student paragraph on *The Remains of the Day*: "Darlington Hall is a big house in
England. Lord Darlington had political meetings there in the 1930s. This was a bad time in history."
Explain two weaknesses and write an improved topic sentence. **[4]**

**12.** "In these novels, places belong to those with money, not those who live in them." Give two
arguments for and one argument against this view, each linked to a novel. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** Definition [1]: a place shaped by power, control and exclusion, where the text shows who
decides and who is affected. Two features, [1] each: ownership; visible institutions or authority;
borders, doors or queues controlling entry; naming or mapping; a small space standing for a national
or imperial order. **[3]**

**2.** A threshold marks who is allowed in and who is kept out [1], so a scene at a door or border
shows power deciding a character's place [1]. **[2]**

**3.** A narrator may belong to the powerful group or depend on it [1], so the account may hide or
excuse the politics of the place [1]. The reader's ironic distance from the narrator then becomes part
of the meaning, as with Stevens or Marlow [1]. **[3]**

**4.** Being "placed in society" means being given a position in a hierarchy of class, race or status
[1]; political spaces enforce those positions physically, by deciding where people may live, work or
enter [1]. **[2]**

**5.** Space identified [1]; what it stands for [1]; methods and explanation [2]. For example:
Vishram Society in *Last Man in Tower* [1] works as a small state [1]; meetings, petitions and
factions copy political processes [1], and the residents' final violence shows money corrupting a
community's idea of collective will [1]. Credit Darlington Hall as a site of international politics,
Queenie's house as a test of Britain's attitude to migrants, or the river stations as the colonial
system. **[4]**

**6.** Any six, [1] each:

- The move from a "stone building by the square" to a "glass tower near the motorway" shifts
  authority from the civic centre to an impersonal edge of the city.
- The queue that "folded back on itself four times" makes waiting a physical experience of control.
- "Metal posts" turn the crowd into a regulated line, with power built into the furniture.
- The waving flag on the screen is a national symbol placed over the counter, so the state watches
  the queue.
- The number that "did not change" suggests indifference and endless delay.
- Rania holding her folder "against her chest" shows documents as the thing that protects her.
- The paradox that she needs "a document to prove she was allowed to wait" exposes the absurdity of
  the system.
- The ban on the family's language extends control from bodies to speech.

**7.** Any four, [1] each: "the city of her childhood" sets personal memory against official
status; her ten years abroad mean she must prove a belonging she once took for granted; the folder
reduces identity to papers; the man's family language "for three generations" shows a long
belonging the place now refuses; the scene suggests that home can become foreign through law rather
than distance. **[4]**

**8.** It extends the politics from Rania to a whole community [1], showing that the state controls
language and inheritance as well as residence, so the place becomes a site of cultural loss [1].
**[2]**

**9.** Two links, each identified [1] and explained [1]. For example: Obinze's life in London depends
on papers he does not have [1], and his deportation shows a place defined by documents, as in the
passage [1]; Ifemelu's return to Lagos after years in America [1] raises the same question of whether
someone who left can belong again [1]. Credit links on race as an identity imposed by place. **[4]**

**10.** Any five, [1] each: a one-sentence argument about what power does in the novel's places;
two or three named locations with contrasting levels of power; specific passages chosen in advance;
methods to analyse (viewpoint, juxtaposition, institutional detail, thresholds); one relevant context
linked to a passage; an alternative reading to weigh; a conclusion on what the novel suggests about
place and power. **[5]**

**11.** Weakness one [1]: it describes the house and its history without analysing how the novel
presents it. Weakness two [1]: "a bad time in history" is vague context that does not explain any
part of the text. Improved topic sentence [2] for an argued, specific claim, [1] for a partial
improvement. Example: "Through Stevens's careful account of serving the dinners, Darlington Hall
becomes a place where political decisions pass unexamined through a servant who will not judge
them." **[4]**

**12.** Two for, [1] each: the developer's offer in *Last Man in Tower* turns homes into assets and
the residents against Masterji; the Company in *Heart of Darkness* claims land through trade; Lord
Darlington's wealth gives his house a political role while Stevens has none; East Egg's old money
protects its position in *The Great Gatsby*. One against [1]: in *Small Island*, Queenie keeps her
house and chooses her tenants against her neighbours' prejudice; or the villagers at Moscombe insist
on their own political voice. **[3]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Background history that does not explain how the place is written.
- Assuming "political" means only elections and governments.
- Forgetting that the narrator may not see the politics the reader sees.
- Quoting long open-book passages instead of analysing short ones closely.
