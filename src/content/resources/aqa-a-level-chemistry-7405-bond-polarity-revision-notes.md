These notes condense section **3.1.3.6 Bond polarity** of the AQA AS and A-level Chemistry specification (7404/7405), for teaching from September 2015. It is AS content, examined on Papers 1 and 2 (and Paper 3). The [Bond polarity study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-bond-polarity/) explains each idea with worked examples, and the [Bond polarity practice questions](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-bond-polarity-practice/) give exam-style practice.

## What the specification asks

- **Electronegativity** is the power of an atom to attract the pair of electrons in a covalent bond.
- Unsymmetrical electron distribution in a bond gives a **polar covalent bond** and may give the molecule a **permanent dipole**.
- **Use partial charges** to show that a bond is polar.
- **Explain why some molecules with polar bonds do not have a permanent dipole.**

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Electronegativity | The power of an atom to attract the pair of electrons in a covalent bond |
| Polar bond | A covalent bond in which the electron pair is shared unequally |
| Partial charge (δ+ or δ−) | A small charge on an atom from unequal sharing |
| Permanent dipole | An unsymmetrical charge distribution that is always present in a molecule |
| Symmetrical molecule | One in which the bond dipoles cancel by geometry |

## Electronegativity values and trends

| Element | H | C | N | O | F | Cl | Br | I | S | P |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pauling value | 2.1 | 2.5 | 3.0 | 3.5 | 4.0 | 3.0 | 2.8 | 2.5 | 2.5 | 2.1 |

- **Increases across a period**: more protons, smaller radius, similar shielding.
- **Decreases down a group**: larger radius, more shielding.
- Fluorine is the most electronegative element; oxygen, nitrogen and chlorine follow.

## Bond polarity

| Case | Result |
|---|---|
| Same atoms (Cl–Cl) | Equal sharing; non-polar |
| Small electronegativity difference | Slightly polar |
| Large difference (O–H, H–F) | Strongly polar |
| Very large difference (Na and Cl) | Electron transfer; ionic |

Show the polarity with partial charges: the more electronegative atom is **δ−**, the other **δ+**.

```
δ+ H – F δ−          C δ+ – Cl δ−          O δ− – H δ+
```

The C–H bond is treated as essentially non-polar.

## From bonds to molecules: two-step test

1. Are the bonds polar?
2. Is the molecule symmetrical, so that the pulls cancel?

| Molecule | Shape | Polar bonds? | Permanent dipole? | Reason |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| H₂O | Bent | Yes | Yes | Pulls do not cancel |
| NH₃ | Pyramidal | Yes | Yes | Pulls do not cancel |
| HCl | Diatomic | Yes | Yes | Only one polar bond |
| CHCl₃ | Tetrahedral, uneven | Yes | Yes | Three C–Cl pulls and no opposing pull |
| CO₂ | Linear | Yes | No | Equal pulls opposite each other |
| CCl₄ | Tetrahedral | Yes | No | Symmetrical, four equal pulls |
| BF₃ | Trigonal planar | Yes | No | Three equal pulls at 120° |
| SF₆ | Octahedral | Yes | No | Opposite pairs cancel |
| PCl₅ | Trigonal bipyramidal | Yes | No | Axial pair cancel, equatorial three cancel |
| trans-C₂H₂Cl₂ | Planar | Yes | No | C–Cl pulls oppose |
| cis-C₂H₂Cl₂ | Planar | Yes | Yes | C–Cl pulls on the same side |

## Answer structure

**Why is a bond polar?** The atoms differ in electronegativity, so the bonding pair is pulled towards the more electronegative atom. That atom has a partial negative charge and the other has a partial positive charge.

**Why does CO₂ have no permanent dipole?** Each C=O bond is polar, but the molecule is linear and symmetrical, so the two dipoles are equal and opposite and cancel.

**Why does H₂O have a permanent dipole?** The O–H bonds are polar, and the molecule is bent, so the dipoles do not cancel.

## Worked reminders

**Compare the polarity of the H–F and H–Cl bonds.** Fluorine is more electronegative than chlorine (4.0 against 3.0), so the H–F bond is more polar.

**Why is CH₄ non-polar?** Carbon and hydrogen have almost the same electronegativity, so the C–H bonds are essentially non-polar. The molecule is also symmetrical.

**Why is CH₃Cl polar although CH₄ is not?** The C–Cl bond is polar, and the molecule is not symmetrical because only one hydrogen has been replaced by chlorine. The pulls do not cancel.

**Why is the electronegativity of oxygen higher than that of sulfur?** Oxygen is smaller with less shielding, so the nucleus attracts the bonding pair more strongly.

## Common mistakes

- Thinking that any molecule with polar bonds is polar.
- Putting δ+ and δ− on the wrong atoms.
- Calling CO₂ "non-polar bonds".
- Not stating that a molecule is symmetrical when explaining cancellation.
- Missing the pair of electrons in the definition of electronegativity.
- Confusing electronegativity (an atom) with polarity (a bond or molecule).

## Self-test

1. Define electronegativity.
2. Explain why electronegativity increases across a period.
3. Explain why electronegativity decreases down Group 17.
4. Mark the partial charges on the H–Br bond.
5. Explain why the Cl–Cl bond is non-polar.
6. Explain why the molecule BF₃ has no permanent dipole although the B–F bonds are polar.
7. Explain why NH₃ has a permanent dipole.
8. State and explain whether CCl₄ is polar.
9. State and explain whether SO₂ (bent) has a permanent dipole.
10. Explain why trans-1,2-dichloroethene is non-polar but the cis isomer is polar.
11. Which bond is more polar, C–O or C–N?
12. Why does a thin stream of a polar liquid bend towards a charged rod but a non-polar liquid does not?

### Answers

1. The power of an atom to attract the pair of electrons in a covalent bond.
2. The nuclear charge increases, the atomic radius decreases and the shielding is similar, so the nucleus attracts the bonding pair more strongly.
3. The atoms get larger and have more inner shells, so the bonding pair is further from the nucleus and the attraction is weaker.
4. H δ+ and Br δ− (bromine is more electronegative than hydrogen).
5. Both atoms are the same, so they attract the pair equally.
6. The B–F bonds are arranged symmetrically in a trigonal planar shape; the three equal pulls cancel.
7. The N–H bonds are polar and the molecule is pyramidal, so the pulls do not cancel.
8. Non-polar: the C–Cl bonds are polar but the tetrahedral shape is symmetrical, so the dipoles cancel.
9. Polar. The S=O bonds are polar and the bent shape means that the dipoles do not cancel.
10. In the trans isomer the two C–Cl dipoles point in opposite directions and cancel; in the cis isomer they point the same way and combine.
11. C–O. Oxygen is more electronegative than nitrogen, so the electronegativity difference is larger.
12. A polar molecule has δ+ and δ− ends, which are attracted to the charged rod. A non-polar molecule has no permanent charge separation.
