These notes condense **4.6.1 Rate of reaction** of the AQA GCSE Chemistry (8462) specification (for teaching
from September 2016). Paper 2 content. Higher tier only: rates in mol/s and the gradient of a tangent. For
explanations use the [rate of reaction study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-chemistry-8462-rate-of-reaction/); to
test yourself use the [rate of reaction practice questions](/resources/aqa-gcse-chemistry-8462-rate-of-reaction-practice/).

## Core facts

- **Mean rate = quantity of reactant used ÷ time** or **quantity of product formed ÷ time**.
- Units: **g/s** or **cm³/s**. Higher tier also: **mol/s**.
- Rate is **fastest at the start** and falls as reactants are used up.
- A graph goes **flat** when a reactant has run out.
- A **steeper** graph line means a **faster** reaction.
- Factors: **concentration, pressure (gases), surface area, temperature, catalyst**.

## Calculation methods

| Question | Method | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Mean rate from volume | volume ÷ time | 54 cm³ in 90 s: 0.60 cm³/s |
| Mean rate from mass lost | mass ÷ time | 1.50 g in 250 s: 0.0060 g/s |
| Rate over an interval | change in quantity ÷ change in time | 30 cm³ in 20 s: 1.5 cm³/s |
| Rate at a point | gradient of a tangent | steeper tangent = faster rate |

**Higher tier only: mol/s.** Convert mass to moles (mass ÷ Ar or Mr), then divide by time. 0.12 g Mg (Ar 24)
used in 50 s: 0.0050 mol ÷ 50 s = **1.0 × 10⁻⁴ mol/s**.

**Higher tier only: tangent gradient.** Draw a tangent that touches the curve at the chosen time. Pick two
well-separated points on the tangent. Gradient = change in y ÷ change in x. Example: (10 s, 25 cm³) and
(50 s, 49 cm³) gives 24 ÷ 40 = **0.60 cm³/s**.

## Measuring methods

| Method | Used for | Weakness |
|---|---|---|
| Gas syringe | Gas volume against time | Leaks, sticking plunger |
| Mass loss on a balance | Gas that escapes | Light gases (hydrogen) give small changes |
| Turbidity (disappearing cross) | Precipitate forming | Judging when the cross vanishes |
| Colour change | Time for colour to change | Judging the end point |

## Required practical 5: concentration

- Form a **hypothesis** first.
- Two methods: **gas volume** (magnesium with hydrochloric acid) and **turbidity** (sodium thiosulfate with
  hydrochloric acid).
- **Change** concentration (dilute with water). **Keep** temperature, volume, mass and surface area the same.
- Repeat and calculate a **mean**.
- Plot the results and look for a pattern, such as doubling the concentration roughly halving the time.

## Collision theory

Reactions need particles to **collide with enough energy**. The minimum is the **activation energy**.

| Factor increased | Why the rate increases |
|---|---|
| Concentration | More particles per unit volume, so more **frequent** collisions |
| Pressure (gases) | Particles closer together, so more frequent collisions |
| Surface area | More exposed particles, so more frequent collisions |
| Temperature | Particles move faster: more **frequent** and more **energetic** collisions |

Proportionality: doubling the concentration doubles the particles in a given volume, so about doubles the
collision frequency and the rate.

## Surface area to volume ratio

| Cube side | Surface area | Volume | Ratio |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 cm | 6 cm² | 1 cm³ | 6 : 1 |
| 2 cm | 24 cm² | 8 cm³ | 3 : 1 |

Smaller pieces → larger ratio → faster reaction. Powders react faster than lumps. Cutting a cube into smaller
cubes keeps the volume but raises the surface area.

## Catalysts

- Change the rate and are **not used up**.
- Provide an **alternative pathway** with a **lower activation energy**.
- Not in the **chemical equation**.
- On a reaction profile, the catalysed curve has a **lower peak**; reactants, products and overall energy change
  stay the same.
- **Enzymes** are biological catalysts.
- Different reactions need different catalysts.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Rate of reaction | How fast reactants are used up or products formed |
| Collision theory | Particles must collide with sufficient energy to react |
| Activation energy | Minimum energy particles need to react |
| Catalyst | Substance that speeds up a reaction and is not used up |
| Surface area to volume ratio | Surface area divided by volume of a solid |
| Tangent | Straight line touching a curve at one point |

## Exam traps

- **Missing units.** Rate needs g/s, cm³/s or mol/s.
- **Only "more collisions".** For temperature, also say collisions are more energetic.
- **"More energy" for concentration.** Concentration changes the number of particles, not their energy.
- **Catalyst "used up" or "in the equation".** It is neither.
- **Wrong profile change.** A catalyst lowers the peak only.
- **Rate vs amount.** A faster reaction gives the same final volume if the amounts are unchanged.
- **Tangent crossing the curve.** It should touch at one point.

## Quick self-test

1. State the equation for the mean rate of reaction.
2. A reaction makes 36 cm³ of gas in 45 s. Calculate the mean rate.
3. Name two ways of measuring the rate of a reaction that produces a gas.
4. Why does the rate fall during a reaction?
5. What does a flat section of a rate graph show?
6. Explain, using collision theory, the effect of increasing concentration.
7. Give two reasons why a higher temperature increases the rate.
8. What does a catalyst do to the activation energy?
9. How can you tell from an equation that a substance is a catalyst?
10. (Higher tier only) 0.030 mol of a reactant is used up in 60 s. Calculate the mean rate in mol/s.
11. (Higher tier only) A tangent passes through (20 s, 10 cm³) and (60 s, 34 cm³). Calculate the rate.
12. (Higher tier only) The tangent gradient is 0.60 cm³/s at 20 s and 0.25 cm³/s at 80 s. At which time is the reaction faster, and why?

### Answers

1. Quantity of reactant used (or product formed) ÷ time taken.
2. 36 ÷ 45 = 0.80 cm³/s.
3. Collecting the gas in a gas syringe; measuring the loss in mass on a balance (also inverted measuring cylinder).
4. The reactants are used up, so their concentrations fall and collisions become less frequent.
5. The reaction has stopped because a reactant has been used up.
6. More particles in the same volume, so collisions are more frequent and the rate increases.
7. Particles move faster so collide more frequently, and a greater proportion of collisions has at least the activation energy.
8. It lowers it, by providing an alternative pathway.
9. It is not included in the equation, because it is not used up.
10. 0.030 ÷ 60 = 5.0 × 10⁻⁴ mol/s (0.00050 mol/s).
11. (34 − 10) ÷ (60 − 20) = 24 ÷ 40 = 0.60 cm³/s.
12. At 20 s, because a larger gradient means a faster rate; the reactants have a higher concentration early in the reaction, so collisions are more frequent.
