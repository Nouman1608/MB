## What a buffer is

A buffer solution resists changes in pH when small amounts of acid or base are added, or when it is diluted. It is **not** a solution that cannot change pH at all. If a large amount of acid or base is added, the buffer is overwhelmed and the pH changes sharply.

A buffer needs two things in the same solution:

- A reservoir of a species that can **remove added H⁺ ions**. This is the conjugate base (the anion of the salt).
- A reservoir of a species that can **remove added OH⁻ ions**. This is the weak acid.

Both must be present in reasonable amounts. A weak acid alone has too little of its conjugate base. A salt alone has too little of the acid.

## Acidic buffers

An acidic buffer contains a weak acid HA and one of its salts, usually the sodium or potassium salt. The classic example is ethanoic acid and sodium ethanoate.

The weak acid is only slightly dissociated, but the salt is fully dissociated:

```
CH₃COOH ⇌ CH₃COO⁻ + H⁺        (small amount of dissociation)
CH₃COONa → CH₃COO⁻ + Na⁺      (complete dissociation)
```

So the solution contains plenty of both CH₃COOH and CH₃COO⁻.

### How it resists added acid

When a small amount of H⁺ is added, it reacts with the ethanoate ion, the base:

```
CH₃COO⁻ + H⁺ → CH₃COOH
```

The equilibrium CH₃COOH ⇌ CH₃COO⁻ + H⁺ shifts to the left, removing most of the added H⁺. [CH₃COOH] rises slightly and [CH₃COO⁻] falls slightly, but the ratio [CH₃COOH] ÷ [CH₃COO⁻] changes very little, so [H⁺] and pH change very little.

### How it resists added alkali

When a small amount of OH⁻ is added, it reacts with H⁺ to make water. The equilibrium then moves to the right, as more CH₃COOH dissociates to replace the H⁺:

```
CH₃COOH + OH⁻ → CH₃COO⁻ + H₂O
```

Again, the ratio of acid to anion is almost unchanged.

### How it resists dilution

Diluting a buffer reduces the concentrations of both the acid and its salt by the same factor. The ratio is the same, so the pH is almost unchanged.

## Basic buffers

A basic buffer contains a weak base and the salt of that weak base, for example ammonia and ammonium chloride:

```
NH₃ + H₂O ⇌ NH₄⁺ + OH⁻
NH₄Cl → NH₄⁺ + Cl⁻
```

- Added **acid**: H⁺ reacts with OH⁻, shifting the equilibrium to the right, converting NH₃ into NH₄⁺. The base in the buffer (NH₃) removes the acid.
- Added **alkali**: OH⁻ reacts with NH₄⁺ to give NH₃ and water, shifting the equilibrium to the left. The salt in the buffer (NH₄⁺) removes the alkali.

The specification asks for a qualitative description of basic buffers and a calculation only for acidic buffers.

## Calculating the pH of an acidic buffer

Start from the Ka expression for the weak acid HA:

```
Ka = [H⁺][A⁻] / [HA]     so     [H⁺] = Ka × [HA] / [A⁻]
```

In a buffer, [HA] is taken as the concentration of the acid added, because its dissociation is suppressed by the salt. [A⁻] is taken as the concentration of the salt added, because the salt is fully dissociated and contributes almost all the A⁻. These are the approximations (compare MS 0.4 in the specification, which refers to mathematical approximations in buffer calculations).

If the volume is the same for both, you can use moles instead of concentrations in the ratio, because the volume cancels.

### Worked example 1: equal amounts

A buffer contains 0.100 mol dm⁻³ ethanoic acid and 0.100 mol dm⁻³ sodium ethanoate. Ka = 1.75 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³.

```
[H⁺] = 1.75 × 10⁻⁵ × 0.100 ÷ 0.100 = 1.75 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³
pH = 4.76
```

When [HA] = [A⁻], pH = pKa. This is the pH at which the buffer is most effective against both acid and alkali.

### Worked example 2: unequal amounts

A buffer contains 0.200 mol dm⁻³ ethanoic acid and 0.0500 mol dm⁻³ sodium ethanoate.

```
[H⁺] = 1.75 × 10⁻⁵ × 0.200 ÷ 0.0500 = 7.00 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³
pH = 4.15
```

More acid than salt gives a lower pH than pKa.

### Worked example 3: making a buffer by partial neutralisation

50.0 cm³ of 0.200 mol dm⁻³ ethanoic acid is mixed with 25.0 cm³ of 0.100 mol dm⁻³ sodium hydroxide. Calculate the pH.

```
n(CH₃COOH) = 0.200 × 0.0500 = 1.00 × 10⁻² mol
n(NaOH) = 0.100 × 0.0250 = 2.50 × 10⁻³ mol
NaOH reacts: CH₃COOH + OH⁻ → CH₃COO⁻ + H₂O
n(CH₃COO⁻) formed = 2.50 × 10⁻³ mol
n(CH₃COOH) left = 1.00 × 10⁻² – 2.50 × 10⁻³ = 7.50 × 10⁻³ mol
[H⁺] = Ka × n(HA) ÷ n(A⁻) = 1.75 × 10⁻⁵ × 7.50 × 10⁻³ ÷ 2.50 × 10⁻³ = 5.25 × 10⁻⁵
pH = 4.28
```

A buffer can be made by partially neutralising a weak acid with a strong base, leaving excess weak acid. The volume cancels in the ratio, so there is no need to calculate concentrations.

### Worked example 4: the effect of adding acid

A buffer in 1.00 dm³ contains 0.200 mol ethanoic acid and 0.200 mol ethanoate (pH 4.76). 0.0100 mol of HCl is added. Assume the volume does not change.

```
H⁺ reacts with CH₃COO⁻:  n(CH₃COO⁻) = 0.200 – 0.0100 = 0.190 mol
                         n(CH₃COOH) = 0.200 + 0.0100 = 0.210 mol
[H⁺] = 1.75 × 10⁻⁵ × 0.210 ÷ 0.190 = 1.93 × 10⁻⁵
pH = 4.71
```

The pH fell by only 0.05. If 0.0100 mol of HCl were added to 1.00 dm³ of water, [H⁺] = 0.0100 and the pH would be 2.00, a fall of 5 units.

### Worked example 5: the effect of adding alkali

The same buffer with 0.0100 mol NaOH added:

```
n(CH₃COOH) = 0.190 mol, n(CH₃COO⁻) = 0.210 mol
[H⁺] = 1.75 × 10⁻⁵ × 0.190 ÷ 0.210 = 1.58 × 10⁻⁵
pH = 4.80
```

## Choosing and preparing a buffer

- Choose a weak acid whose pKa is close to the pH you need. The buffer works best when pH is within about one unit of pKa.
- Adjust the ratio of acid to salt to set the exact pH. Use [H⁺] = Ka × [HA] ÷ [A⁻] backwards: for pH 4.50, [H⁺] = 3.16 × 10⁻⁵, so [HA] ÷ [A⁻] = 3.16 × 10⁻⁵ ÷ 1.75 × 10⁻⁵ = 1.81.
- Buffer capacity increases with the concentrations of the acid and salt. A more concentrated buffer can absorb more added acid or alkali.

## Applications

| Application | Buffer system or purpose |
|---|---|
| Blood | Carbonic acid and hydrogencarbonate ions keep blood pH at about 7.4. Small changes can be dangerous. |
| Food and drink | Buffers control acidity for taste and to limit microbial growth |
| Shampoos and skin products | Maintain a pH close to that of skin to avoid irritation |
| Biological and medical research | Enzymes work in a narrow pH range, so media for experiments are buffered |
| Calibrating pH meters | Solutions of known, stable pH |

## Common mistakes

- Saying a buffer "keeps the pH constant" instead of "approximately constant".
- Forgetting to mention both the equilibrium and the reaction with added acid or alkali.
- Using the concentration of the whole solution volume without noticing that the volume cancels in the ratio.
- Putting salt over acid in the [H⁺] formula (upside down).
- Using the Ka of the wrong acid, or the pH of the acid alone.
- Treating a mixture of a weak acid and a strong acid as a buffer.
- Forgetting that a buffer made by partial neutralisation needs the weak acid in excess.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Explain how a buffer resists change**: name the two components, write the equilibrium, state what happens on adding acid and on adding alkali, and say that the ratio hardly changes.
- **Calculate the pH**: write the Ka expression, rearrange for [H⁺], substitute, then convert.
- **Suggest how to prepare a buffer of a given pH**: select the acid, compare its pKa to the target pH, then find the ratio.

## Frequently asked questions

**Does a buffer have a pH of 7?**
No. A buffer can be acidic, neutral or alkaline. The pH depends on the acid or base used and the ratio of the components.

**Why is an equimolar mixture of acid and salt best?**
It has the same capacity to remove added acid and added alkali, so the pH is most stable.

**Is a buffer made from a strong acid and its salt?**
No. A strong acid is fully dissociated, so there is no reservoir of undissociated acid to release H⁺ when alkali is added.
