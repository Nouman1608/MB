This guide covers section **3.6.1 Sources of finance** of OxfordAQA International GCSE Business (9225), part of Topic 6, Finance. The specification asks you to understand the main **internal and external sources of finance**, including **family and friends, retained profit, a new share issue, a loan or mortgage, selling unwanted assets, overdrafts, trade credit, crowdfunding and government and charitable grants or loans**; to **analyse the advantages and disadvantages** of each for a given situation; and to **evaluate the suitability** of sources of finance for **new and established businesses**. All businesses and figures in this guide are invented.

For the whole topic, see the [Finance study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-business-finance/). Condensed notes are in the [revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-business-9225-sources-of-finance-revision-notes/) and questions are in the [practice set](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-business-9225-sources-of-finance-practice/).

## Why do businesses need finance?

A business needs money to start up, to run day to day and to grow.

| Purpose | Examples |
|---|---|
| Start-up capital | Buying equipment, a first stock of goods, paying a deposit on premises |
| Working capital | Paying wages, suppliers and rent while waiting for customers to pay |
| Expansion | Opening new shops, buying new machines, developing new products |
| Cash flow problems | Covering a temporary shortage of cash |
| Emergencies | Replacing a broken machine |

### Short term and long term finance

- **Short-term finance** is needed for less than about a year, and is used for working capital, such as paying bills until customers pay. Examples: overdrafts and trade credit.
- **Long-term finance** is needed for several years and is used for large purchases or expansion. Examples: loans, mortgages, share issues and retained profit.

A common rule is to match the source to the purpose: use short-term finance for short-term needs and long-term finance for large, lasting investments.

## Internal and external sources

**Internal sources** come from within the business or its owners. **External sources** come from outside.

| Internal | External |
|---|---|
| Owner's savings and family and friends* | Bank loans and mortgages |
| Retained profit | Overdrafts |
| Selling unwanted assets | New share issue |
| | Trade credit |
| | Crowdfunding |
| | Government and charitable grants or loans |

*The specification lists "family and friends" among the sources of finance. They are outside the business itself, but they are close to the owner, so they are often treated like an internal source.

## The sources of finance

### Family and friends

Money lent or given to the owner by relatives or friends, often to help a new business start.

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Quick and easy to arrange | Amount is limited |
| Interest may be low or nothing | Strains relationships if the business struggles |
| Less formal than a bank | They may want a say in how the business is run |
| Useful when banks will not lend to a new business | Terms may be unclear unless written down |

Suited to: new, small businesses.

### Retained profit

Profit that the owners leave in the business instead of taking it out or paying it as dividends.

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| No interest and no repayment | Only available if the business is profitable |
| No outside control | Owners or shareholders get less income now |
| Quick to use | Amount may be too small for large projects |

Suited to: established, profitable businesses. A new business has no retained profit.

**Worked example 1: retained profit.** A company makes a profit of $60,000 and decides to keep 70% in the business.

```
Retained profit = 70% x $60,000 = $42,000
Paid out        = $60,000 - $42,000 = $18,000
```

The $42,000 can pay for new equipment without borrowing, but shareholders receive less.

### Selling unwanted assets

Selling assets the business no longer needs, such as an old van, spare land or surplus equipment, raises cash quickly.

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| No interest or repayment | Only possible if there are assets to spare |
| Reduces the cost of looking after unused assets | May be sold below value if cash is needed quickly |
| Quick | A one-off source; cannot be repeated |

Suited to: established businesses with spare assets. A new business usually has none.

### New share issue

A limited company raises money by selling new shares to investors.

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Can raise large sums | Takes time and costs money to arrange |
| No repayment or interest | New shareholders share the profit through dividends |
| Reduces reliance on borrowing | Existing owners' control is diluted |

**Worked example 2: dilution.** A company with 100,000 shares sells 40,000 new shares at $3 each.

```
Money raised = 40,000 x $3 = $120,000
Owners' stake before = 100% of 100,000 shares
Owners' stake after  = 100,000 / 140,000 x 100 = 71.4%
New shareholders     = 40,000 / 140,000 x 100 = 28.6%
```

The company raises $120,000 with no interest to pay, but the original owners now hold 71.4% of the votes and profits.

Suited to: limited companies, especially established ones, which want to expand. It does not suit sole traders or partnerships, which cannot issue shares.

### Loans and mortgages

A **loan** is borrowing a fixed sum from a bank, repaid with interest over an agreed period. A **mortgage** is a long-term loan, usually to buy land or buildings, which the lender can claim if the borrower fails to repay.

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Large amounts available | Interest increases the total cost |
| Repayments are fixed and can be planned | The lender may require security (an asset it can claim) |
| Owners keep control | A new business with no track record may be refused |
| Interest is lower than an overdraft | Repayments must be made even if sales are poor |

**Worked example 3: cost of a loan.** A business borrows $30,000 at 9% simple interest a year over 3 years.

```
Interest per year = 9% x $30,000 = $2,700
Total interest    = 3 x $2,700   = $8,100
Total to repay    = $30,000 + $8,100 = $38,100
Monthly repayment = $38,100 / 36 = $1,058.33 (to the nearest cent)
```

The business must find $1,058.33 each month, so the loan is only suitable if cash flow can support it.

Suited to: businesses with steady income that need money for long-term purposes, such as equipment or buildings. Mortgages suit property purchases.

### Overdraft

An **overdraft** lets a business spend more than it has in its bank account, up to a limit.

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Flexible: use it only when needed | High interest, charged on the amount overdrawn |
| Interest only on the amount used | Bank can ask for it back at short notice |
| Quick to arrange for existing customers | Fees can be added |
| Good for short-term cash flow gaps | Not suitable for long-term needs |

**Worked example 4: overdraft interest.** A business is overdrawn by an average of $4,000 for 2 months at 15% a year.

```
Interest = $4,000 x 15% x 2/12 = $100
```

Suited to: short-term cash shortages, for example until customers pay. Not suited to buying a machine.

### Trade credit

Trade credit means getting goods or services from suppliers now and paying later, often after 30 or 60 days.

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Free if paid on time | Lose discounts for early payment |
| Improves cash flow | Late payment may damage the relationship and credit terms |
| Easy to arrange with regular suppliers | Suppliers may refuse credit to new businesses |

Suited to: businesses buying stock regularly, new or established.

### Crowdfunding

Raising small amounts of money from a large number of people, usually through a website, often in return for rewards, products or a share in the business.

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Access to money from many people without a bank | May not reach the target; many schemes are all or nothing |
| Tests interest in a product and gives publicity | Takes time and effort to run the campaign |
| Often no interest | Platform fees; rewards or products cost money |
| Backers become loyal customers | The idea is made public, so rivals can copy it |

**Worked example 5: a crowdfunding target.** A start-up needs $25,000 and expects backers to give an average of $50.

```
Backers needed = $25,000 / $50 = 500
```

If only 320 backers join, the total of 320 x $50 = $16,000 may mean that the project does not go ahead.

Suited to: new, creative or popular products with a wide appeal.

### Government and charitable grants and loans

Governments and charities support some businesses, for example new businesses, businesses in poorer regions or those with environmental or social aims.

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Grants do not need to be repaid | Competition for funds, and strict conditions |
| Loans may have low interest | Applications are slow and involve paperwork |
| Can support a good cause | The money must be used only for the stated purpose |

Suited to: start-ups and businesses meeting the scheme's conditions.

## Choosing the right source

The specification asks you to **evaluate suitability** for new and established businesses.

| Business | Likely suitable | Less suitable |
|---|---|---|
| New start-up | Owner's savings, family and friends, crowdfunding, grants, trade credit | Retained profit (none yet), selling assets, new share issue |
| Small established business | Retained profit, bank loan, overdraft, trade credit | Share issue if a sole trader |
| Large company expanding | Share issue, long-term loans, retained profit | Overdraft for long-term projects |

Factors to consider:

1. **How much** is needed and **for how long**.
2. **Cost:** interest and fees.
3. **Risk:** does the business have to repay even if sales are poor?
4. **Control:** will owners give up any control?
5. **Speed:** how quickly is the money needed?
6. **Type of business:** sole traders cannot issue shares.
7. **Security:** what assets can be used as security for a loan?

## Common mistakes

- Using an overdraft to pay for a long-term asset, such as a building.
- Suggesting a new share issue for a sole trader or partnership.
- Saying that retained profit is free. It reduces payments to owners and could have been used elsewhere.
- Listing sources without matching them to the amount, time and type of business.
- Forgetting that loans carry interest and must be repaid even if sales fall.
- Treating all external finance as expensive. Trade credit can be free.

## Exam technique for this section

For **state** questions, name the source. For **explain** questions, give an advantage or drawback with a reason that fits the business. For **calculate** questions, show interest and totals. For **evaluate** questions, compare at least two sources against the amount, time, cost, risk and control, then recommend one and explain why the other is less suitable.
