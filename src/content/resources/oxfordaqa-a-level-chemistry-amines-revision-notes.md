## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Amine | Compound based on ammonia with one or more H atoms replaced by alkyl or aryl groups |
| Primary / secondary / tertiary | One / two / three carbon atoms bonded directly to N |
| Quaternary ammonium ion | R₄N⁺: four carbons on N, no lone pair, no N–H |
| Aliphatic amine | N bonded to a chain or non-aromatic ring carbon |
| Aromatic amine | N bonded directly to a benzene ring |
| Base | Proton (H⁺) acceptor |
| Nucleophile | Electron-pair donor that attacks an electron-deficient carbon |
| Cationic surfactant | Surfactant whose water-attracting head is positively charged |

The **lone pair on N** explains the whole section. It accepts H⁺ (base) and attacks δ+ carbon (nucleophile).

## 3.3.11.1 Preparation at a glance

| Product | Starting material | Reagent and conditions | Equation pattern |
|---|---|---|---|
| Primary aliphatic amine | Halogenoalkane RX | Excess NH₃ in ethanol, heated in a sealed vessel | RX + 2NH₃ → RNH₂ + NH₄X |
| Primary aliphatic amine | Nitrile RCN | Reduce: H₂ with Ni catalyst, or LiAlH₄ in dry ether (no reagent named in the specification) | RCN + 4[H] → RCH₂NH₂ |
| Aromatic amine | Nitroarene ArNO₂ | Tin and concentrated HCl, heat under reflux; then NaOH to free the amine | ArNO₂ + 6[H] → ArNH₂ + 2H₂O |

- The ammonia route gives a **mixture** (the amine attacks more RX). Excess NH₃ limits this.
- The nitrile route gives **only** the primary amine and adds **one carbon** (RX → RCN → RCH₂NH₂).
- Aromatic amines made this way are used to **manufacture dyes**.

### Method in steps: counting [H]

1. Write the starting group and the product group.
2. Each O removed leaves as H₂O, using 2[H].
3. Add the H atoms that end up on N or C.
4. For C≡N → CH₂–NH₂: 0 O removed, 4 H added, so **4[H]**.
5. For NO₂ → NH₂: 2 O removed (4[H]), 2 H added on N, so **6[H]** and **2H₂O**.
6. Multiply for molecules with two groups.

**Worked reminder.** Make heptan-1-amine, CH₃(CH₂)₆NH₂, from 1-bromohexane. Six carbons become seven, so a C–C bond must form. Step 1: KCN in ethanol and water, heat under reflux, giving heptanenitrile, CH₃(CH₂)₅CN. Step 2: reduce with H₂/Ni: CH₃(CH₂)₅CN + 4[H] → CH₃(CH₂)₆NH₂.

## 3.3.11.2 Base properties

In water, an amine is a **weak base**: only a small fraction is protonated, so use ⇌.

```
RNH2 + H2O ⇌ RNH3+ + OH−          (solution is alkaline)
RNH2 + HCl → RNH3+Cl−             (salt: alkylammonium chloride)
RNH3+ + OH− → RNH2 + H2O          (NaOH frees the amine)
```

**Order of base strength:** primary aliphatic amine **>** ammonia **>** primary aromatic amine.

### Method in steps: explaining base strength

1. Say that the **lone pair on N** is what accepts H⁺.
2. Aliphatic: alkyl group is **electron-releasing**, so electron density on N rises; lone pair **more available**.
3. Ammonia: no alkyl or aryl group on N; reference point.
4. Aromatic: the ring **delocalises the lone pair**, lowering the electron density on N; lone pair **less available**.
5. Conclude: greater lone-pair availability means a stronger base.

## 3.3.11.3 Nucleophilic properties

### Repeated substitution by halogenoalkanes

| Nucleophile | + RX gives | Class |
|---|---|---|
| NH₃ | RNH₂ | primary amine |
| RNH₂ | R₂NH | secondary amine |
| R₂NH | R₃N | tertiary amine |
| R₃N | R₄N⁺X⁻ | quaternary ammonium salt (end of the ladder) |

- Excess **ammonia** → mainly primary amine.
- Excess **halogenoalkane** → mainly quaternary salt.
- R₄N⁺ has **no lone pair**, so it is neither a base nor a nucleophile.

### Method in steps: substitution mechanism

1. Show δ+ on the carbon bonded to the halogen, δ− on the halogen.
2. Arrow from the **N lone pair** to the δ+ carbon.
3. Arrow from the C–X bond to X; X⁻ leaves.
4. Draw the intermediate with **+ on N**.
5. Arrow from the lone pair of a second NH₃ or amine molecule to an H on N⁺; arrow from that N–H bond to N⁺.
6. Products: the new amine and an ammonium salt.

Exception: a tertiary amine forming a quaternary salt stops at step 4. There is no H on N⁺ to remove.

**Worked reminder.** Propan-2-amine with a large excess of bromomethane ends at **(CH₃)₂CHN⁺(CH₃)₃ Br⁻**, after passing through (CH₃)₂CHNHCH₃ and (CH₃)₂CHN(CH₃)₂.

### Cationic surfactants

Give a quaternary ammonium salt a **single long hydrocarbon chain** and it has a positive N⁺ head that interacts with water and a non-polar tail that mixes with grease and oils. Because the head is positive, it is a **cationic surfactant**. Such salts are used in fabric softeners and hair conditioners.

### Addition–elimination: acyl chlorides and acid anhydrides

| | Acyl chloride, RCOCl | Acid anhydride, (RCO)₂O |
|---|---|---|
| With NH₃ | RCONH₂ + NH₄Cl (2 mol NH₃) | RCONH₂ + RCOOH (or RCOONH₄ with excess NH₃) |
| With primary amine R′NH₂ | RCONHR′ + R′NH₃⁺Cl⁻ (2 mol amine) | RCONHR′ + RCOOH |
| Rate | fast, at room temperature | slower |
| Mechanism needed? | yes | no (products only) |

The product from a primary amine is an **N-substituted amide**; the N- shows that the group is on nitrogen.

### Method in steps: addition–elimination mechanism (acyl chloride)

1. δ+ on the carbonyl carbon (O and Cl both draw electrons away).
2. Arrow from the **N lone pair** to the carbonyl carbon; arrow from C=O to O, giving **O⁻**.
3. Tetrahedral intermediate: O⁻, Cl, R and N⁺ on the same carbon.
4. Arrow from O⁻ lone pair to re-form C=O; arrow from C–Cl to Cl; **Cl⁻ leaves**.
5. Cl⁻ or a second NH₃/amine molecule removes **H⁺ from N⁺**.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Class of amine vs class of alcohol.** Amines count carbons on N; alcohols count carbons on the C–OH carbon. (CH₃)₃CNH₂ is a primary amine, but (CH₃)₃COH is a tertiary alcohol.
- **Aromatic vs aliphatic.** C₆H₅NH₂ is aromatic; C₆H₅CH(CH₃)NH₂ is aliphatic because N is on a chain carbon.
- **Nitrile route vs ammonia route.** Pure primary amine with one extra carbon vs a mixture with the same carbon count.
- **Base vs nucleophile.** Same lone pair; a base gives it to H⁺, a nucleophile gives it to a carbon.
- **Amine vs amide.** An amide has C=O bonded to N (–CONH–); an amine has no C=O on N.
- **Acyl chloride by-product vs anhydride by-product.** HCl (taken up as a chloride salt) vs a carboxylic acid.

## Quick self-test

1. Is (CH₃CH₂)₂NCH₃ primary, secondary or tertiary?
2. Is C₆H₅CH(CH₃)NH₂ an aromatic or an aliphatic amine? Give a reason.
3. How many [H] are needed to reduce butanedinitrile, NCCH₂CH₂CN, to butane-1,4-diamine?
4. Give the reagents used to reduce 1-ethyl-4-nitrobenzene, and name the amine formed.
5. Put in order of increasing base strength: butan-1-amine, phenylamine, ammonia.
6. Explain why the tetramethylammonium ion cannot act as a nucleophile.
7. Write an equation for hexan-1-amine reacting with nitric acid and name the salt.
8. Calculate the volume of 0.200 mol dm⁻³ hydrochloric acid that exactly reacts with 0.606 g of hexan-1-amine, C₆H₁₅N (Mr = 101.0).
9. Name the organic product when heptanoyl chloride reacts with excess methylamine, and name the other product.
10. Name the by-product when ethanoic anhydride, not ethanoyl chloride, reacts with ammonia (1 : 1).
11. State the two structural features that let a quaternary ammonium salt act as a cationic surfactant.
12. Name the mechanism for (a) an amine with a halogenoalkane and (b) an amine with an acyl chloride.

### Answers

1. **Tertiary**: three carbon groups on N, no N–H.
2. **Aliphatic.** N is bonded to a chain carbon, not to the ring.
3. **8[H].** Each C≡N needs 4[H]: NCCH₂CH₂CN + 8[H] → H₂N(CH₂)₄NH₂.
4. **Tin and concentrated hydrochloric acid** under reflux, then sodium hydroxide; **4-ethylphenylamine**, CH₃CH₂C₆H₄NH₂ (6[H] used).
5. **Phenylamine < ammonia < butan-1-amine.**
6. N has used its lone pair in four bonds, so it has **no lone pair** to donate.
7. CH₃(CH₂)₅NH₂ + HNO₃ → CH₃(CH₂)₅NH₃⁺NO₃⁻; **hexylammonium nitrate**.
8. n = 0.606 ÷ 101.0 = 6.00 × 10⁻³ mol; ratio 1 : 1; V = 6.00 × 10⁻³ ÷ 0.200 = 0.0300 dm³ = **30.0 cm³**.
9. **N-methylheptanamide**, CH₃(CH₂)₅CONHCH₃; by-product **methylammonium chloride**, CH₃NH₃⁺Cl⁻.
10. **Ethanoic acid**, CH₃COOH.
11. A **positively charged N⁺ head** and a **long hydrocarbon chain**.
12. (a) **Nucleophilic substitution**; (b) **nucleophilic addition–elimination**.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using the carbon next to N, not N itself, to classify an amine.
- Calling an amine aromatic because the molecule has a benzene ring somewhere.
- Equations with 2[H] for nitriles or 3[H] for nitro groups, or no water shown for nitro reduction.
- Leaving out the NaOH step after reduction with tin and hydrochloric acid.
- A full arrow, not ⇌, for an amine reacting with water.
- Base-strength answers that never mention the lone pair on N or accepting H⁺.
- "Phenylamine is weaker because benzene withdraws electrons" with no mention of delocalisation into the ring.
- Missing the + charge on N in either intermediate, or omitting the final H⁺ removal.
- Writing HCl as a product of an anhydride reaction.
