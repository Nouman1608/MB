## 3.3.3.8 Dynamics of competition

### Beyond price

Firms do not compete on price alone. They also try to improve products, cut costs and raise the quality of service.

**Short-run benefits** (the period before firms can change all inputs or new firms can arrive):
- lower prices, wider choice and better service as sellers fight for customers;

**Long-run benefits** (when entry and exit are possible and firms can invest):
- lower unit costs and new and better products from investment and new methods.

### Creative destruction

The specification expects you to understand the following chain. Firms with monopoly power and large profits attract attention. Over time that profit is an incentive for new firms to enter and to innovate their way past the existing barriers to entry. The new product or process damages the old one, and the old firm's profit falls. This is **creative destruction**, which the specification describes as a fundamental feature of how competition works in a market economy.

**Worked example (invented).** In Country A, Firm P is the only seller of a product. It sells 8,000 units a month at 50 with a unit cost of 30. Firm Q develops a cheaper process, with a unit cost of 22, and enters. Both firms then sell at 38, and each sells 5,000 units, so the market is 10,000 units.

- Firm P profit before: (50 - 30) x 8,000 = 160,000. After: (38 - 30) x 5,000 = 40,000, a fall of 75%.
- Firm Q profit: (38 - 22) x 5,000 = 80,000.
- Price falls by 12/50 = 24%. Output rises by 2,000/8,000 = 25%.
- Assume the demand curve is a straight line through the two points: P = 98 - 0.006Q. Consumer surplus rises from half x 8,000 x (98 - 50) = 192,000 to half x 10,000 x (98 - 38) = 300,000, a gain of 108,000 or 56.25%.

Firm P's profit drew Firm Q in. Consumers gain, and Firm P has a reason to cut its own costs. The "destruction" is the loss of profit and, possibly, of jobs and invested capital.

**Evaluation.** The process is slower where barriers to entry are high (patents, large sunk costs, control of supply). Entry can duplicate research, which wastes resources. A firm may buy a rival before it can compete. Whether the gains outweigh the disruption depends on how fast entry happens and who bears the cost of the destruction.

## 3.3.3.9 Efficiency

### Static versus dynamic

**Static efficiency** looks at one moment, with technology and capital taken as given. Are resources used at lowest cost (productive efficiency), and is the output the one consumers value most (allocative efficiency)?

**Dynamic efficiency** concerns change over time. It is influenced by research and development (R&D), by investment in human capital (skills) and non-human capital (machines, buildings), and by technological change. Together these improve products and cut costs.

### Productive efficiency

Productive efficiency means producing at the **minimum point of average total cost**. At that output MC = ATC, because the MC curve cuts the ATC curve at its lowest point.

**Worked example (invented).** A firm has total cost TC = 360 + 6Q + 0.1Q², so MC = 6 + 0.2Q.

| Q | TC | ATC | MC |
|---|---|---|---|
| 30 | 630 | 21.00 | 12 |
| 45 | 832.5 | 18.50 | 15 |
| 60 | 1,080 | 18.00 | 18 |
| 75 | 1,372.5 | 18.30 | 21 |
| 90 | 1,710 | 19.00 | 24 |

ATC is lowest at Q = 60, where MC = ATC = 18. Output of 45 is inefficient because ATC is above the minimum (18.50 against 18.00).

### Allocative efficiency

Allocative efficiency means **price = marginal cost** (P = MC). The price shows the value consumers put on the last unit and MC shows the cost of the resources used for it. If P is above MC, more output would raise welfare. If P is below MC, resources are overused.

Using the market in the next section (demand P = 60 - 2Q, MC = 12 + 2Q), a monopoly output of 8 has P = 44 and MC = 28. The gap of 16 per unit shows underproduction.

### X-inefficiency

**X-inefficiency** is the gap between the costs a firm actually has and the lowest costs it could reach, because of slack management, weak incentives or lack of competitive pressure.

Example: the firm above could make 60 units at an ATC of 18, but slack means its actual ATC is 22.50. The excess is 4.50 per unit, which is 25% above the minimum, and the waste is 4.50 x 60 = 270. Barriers to entry reduce the pressure to remove slack.

### Dynamic efficiency

Spending on R&D, training and new equipment can lower costs later. Suppose a firm with unit cost 18 spends on research and cuts unit cost 4% a year: 18.00, 17.28, 16.59, 15.93. That is a fall of 11.5% over three years, and a rival with no investment stays at 18.

### Comparing market structures

The specification asks you to use **conduct** (how firms behave: pricing, advertising, R&D spending) and **performance** (the results: profit, prices, costs, productivity, quality) indicators to compare structures.

| Indicator (invented) | Market X (CR4 = 85%) | Market Y (CR4 = 30%) |
|---|---|---|
| Price | 42 | 30 |
| Marginal cost | 30 | 26 |
| Price minus MC, as % of price | 28.6% | 13.3% |
| R&D as % of sales | 1.5% | 4.5% |

Market X has high concentration, a wide price-cost gap and little R&D, so it looks weaker statically and dynamically. But a few numbers prove little: Market X might have large economies of scale. Ask what an indicator leaves out.

## 3.3.3.10 Consumer and producer surplus

**Consumer surplus (CS)** is how much more buyers would have paid than they did pay, shown by the region between the demand curve and the price line. **Producer surplus (PS)** is how much more sellers receive than the least they would accept, shown between the price line and the supply (MC) curve. **Deadweight loss** is the surplus lost to everyone when output differs from the efficient level.

### Worked example: competition and monopoly

Demand: P = 60 - 2Q. Marginal cost (the competitive supply curve): P = 12 + 2Q. Q is in thousands of units and prices are in currency units, so surplus is in thousands.

**Competition.** 60 - 2Q = 12 + 2Q, so Q = 12 and P = 36.
- CS = half x 12 x (60 - 36) = 144.
- PS = half x 12 x (36 - 12) = 144.
- Total surplus = 288.

**Monopoly.** MR = 60 - 4Q. Set MR = MC: 60 - 4Q = 12 + 2Q, so Q = 8. Price from demand: 60 - 16 = 44. MC at 8 is 28.
- CS = half x 8 x (60 - 44) = 64.
- PS = 8 x (44 - 28) + half x 8 x (28 - 12) = 128 + 64 = 192.
- Deadweight loss = half x (12 - 8) x (44 - 28) = 32.
- Check: 64 + 192 + 32 = 288.

Sketch in words: the demand line falls from 60 on the price axis and the MC line rises from 12. They cross at X (Q = 12, P = 36). The monopoly point is on demand at Q = 8, P = 44 (MC there is 28). The deadweight loss is the triangle between the monopoly point, the MC curve at Q = 8 and X.

Consumers lose 80, the firm gains 48, and the remaining 32 is lost to everyone.

### Price discrimination

**Perfect (first-degree) discrimination** charges every buyer the most that buyer is prepared to pay. In the market above, output rises to 12, where the last buyer pays 36 = MC. CS = 0, PS = half x 12 x (60 - 12) = 288 and deadweight loss = 0. It is allocatively efficient, but all the surplus goes to the firm.

**Third-degree discrimination** charges different groups different prices. Take constant MC = 10 and two groups: A with P = 50 - 2Q and B with P = 30 - 2Q.
- Discriminating: set MR = MC in each group. Group A: 50 - 4Q = 10, so Q = 10, P = 30. Group B: 30 - 4Q = 10, so Q = 5, P = 20. Profit = 200 + 50 = 250. CS = 100 + 25 = 125. Total surplus = 375.
- One price: market demand is P = 40 - Q for prices up to 30, so MR = 40 - 2Q. MR = MC gives Q = 15 and P = 25. Profit = 225. CS = 162.5. Total surplus = 387.5.

Profit rises by 25 under discrimination, consumers lose 37.5, and total surplus falls by 12.5. Total output is 15 either way, but it is shared between groups at different marginal values (30 against 20). Welfare can rise if discrimination serves buyers whom a single price would shut out.

### Tariffs

A tariff is a tax on imports. Country A is a small importer. Domestic demand: P = 60 - 2Q. Domestic supply: P = 6 + 2Q. The world price is 18 (the country cannot affect it).

| | Free trade (P = 18) | Tariff of 6 (P = 24) |
|---|---|---|
| Domestic demand | 21 | 18 |
| Domestic supply | 6 | 9 |
| Imports | 15 | 9 |

- Loss of consumer surplus = half x (21 + 18) x 6 = 117.
- Gain in producer surplus = half x (6 + 9) x 6 = 45.
- Government revenue = 6 x 9 = 54.
- Deadweight loss = half x 3 x 6 + half x 3 x 6 = 18 (extra high-cost domestic output, and consumption foregone).
- Check: 45 + 54 + 18 = 117.

Evaluation: the tariff moves surplus from consumers to producers and the government. A young industry may need time to grow, though protected firms may lose the pressure to cut costs.

## Evaluating surplus and efficiency

- Surplus uses willingness to pay, which is hard to measure and depends on income.
- It treats each unit of currency as equal, so it says nothing about who gains.
- It assumes no externalities, so MC is the full social cost.
- A monopoly can be dynamically efficient, so static deadweight loss is not the whole story. Suppose in the market above innovation cuts MC to 6 + 2Q. A monopoly then sets Q = 9 and P = 42, CS = 81, PS = 243 and total surplus = 324, above the old competitive 288. A competitive market on the new costs would still reach 364.5.

## Common errors

- Writing "price = MR" instead of P = MC for allocative efficiency.
- Putting productive efficiency at the lowest MC rather than the lowest ATC.
- Drawing the deadweight loss without a corner at the monopoly point.
- Forgetting that perfect discrimination removes the deadweight loss.
- Counting tariff revenue as a loss to the country.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why is a monopoly allocatively inefficient?**
A profit-maximising monopoly produces where MR = MC and sets price from the demand curve, so price is above marginal cost. Consumers value extra units more than they cost to make, so too little is produced and a deadweight loss appears.

**What drives dynamic efficiency?**
Dynamic efficiency is influenced by research and development, investment in human capital (skills) and non-human capital (machines and buildings), and technological change. Together these improve products and lower costs over time.

**Does third-degree price discrimination raise or lower welfare?**
The firm's profit rises and consumer surplus usually falls, but total welfare can rise or fall. It depends on whether total output rises, for example by serving buyers a single price would shut out, and on how output is shared between groups.

**What are conduct and performance indicators in market structure analysis?**
Conduct indicators describe how firms behave, such as pricing, advertising and R&D spending. Performance indicators describe results, such as profit, prices, unit costs, productivity and quality, and the OxfordAQA 9640 specification asks you to use both to compare market structures.

**What are the limitations of using consumer and producer surplus to judge welfare?**
Willingness to pay is hard to measure and depends on income, surplus treats each unit of currency as equal so it ignores who gains, and it assumes no externalities, so marginal cost may not be the full social cost.
