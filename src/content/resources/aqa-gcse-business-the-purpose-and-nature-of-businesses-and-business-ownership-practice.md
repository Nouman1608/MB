Revise first with the [study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-business-the-purpose-and-nature-of-businesses-and-business-ownership/) or the [revision notes](/resources/aqa-gcse-business-the-purpose-and-nature-of-businesses-and-business-ownership-revision-notes/). ## Questions

**1.** State the main reason for starting a business shown in each example. **(a)** A firm buys apples from growers and sells them to cafés. **[1]** **(b)** A retired teacher runs a free reading club for children. **[1]** **(c)** A joiner builds and sells bookcases. **[1]** **(d)** A new resident sees that the village has no taxi service and starts one. **[1]**

**2.** Distinguish between a need and a want. Give one example of each. **[3]**

**3.** Identify the sector (primary, secondary or tertiary) for each: **(a)** a slate quarry **[1]**; **(b)** a cabinet maker **[1]**; **(c)** a dental practice **[1]**.

**4.** Alvanley Fabrications makes metal gates. State which factor of production each item is: **(a)** the welding robot **[1]**; **(b)** the welders **[1]**; **(c)** the owner's decision to set up the firm **[1]**; **(d)** the yard where the gates are made **[1]**.

**5.** (Calculation) On Saturday, the owner of Lynmouth Surf School can teach 6 lessons earning £45 profit each, hire out boards for three hours earning £70 an hour, or work an 8-hour shift at a café for £11 an hour. She chooses to teach. Calculate the opportunity cost. **[3]**

**6.** Explain **two** characteristics of an entrepreneur. **[4]**

**7.** Wintergreen Garden Centre has sold plants and peat-based compost from its shop for years. Online orders are growing, and customers increasingly ask for peat-free compost. Analyse **two** ways the changing business environment could affect Wintergreen. **[6]**

**8.** Alvanley Fabrications closes owing £85,000. Selling its assets raises £31,000. **(a)** Calculate the shortfall. **[2]** **(b)** Explain how the owner's position differs if Alvanley is a sole trader, compared with a private limited company in which the owner invested £12,000. **[4]**

**9.** Kelsingra Physio Clinic is a partnership. The partners agreed to share profit 5:3. This year's profit is £56,000. **(a)** Calculate each partner's share. **[3]** **(b)** Explain **one** drawback of a partnership compared with being a sole trader. **[3]**

**10.** Usherwood Hospice is a not-for-profit organisation. This year it received £240,000 in donations, grants and shop sales and spent £232,000. **(a)** Calculate the surplus. **[1]** **(b)** Explain how the use of this surplus is likely to differ from the use of profit in a private limited company. **[3]**

**11.** Hari owns Brockhurst Farm Shop as a sole trader. He wants to open two more shops, which he estimates will cost £150,000. He has £40,000 of savings and lives in a house he owns. He wants to keep control of the business. Evaluate whether Hari should convert to a private limited company. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** **(a)** Distributing products [1]. **(b)** Providing a service to benefit others [1]. **(c)** Producing goods [1]. **(d)** Fulfilling a business opportunity [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Answers tend to gain credit when they use the specification's reasons and link each one to the clue in the stem, such as "no taxi service" pointing to a gap.

**2.** A need is something people must have to live, such as food or shelter [1]. A want is something people would like but could live without, such as a new phone [1]. One correct example of each, for example food as a need and a phone as a want [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* A definition and an example are separate points, so an example with no distinction usually earns little. Beware of examples that could be either, such as "a coat" with no detail.

**3.** **(a)** Primary [1]. **(b)** Secondary [1]. **(c)** Tertiary [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Sector questions reward the activity the business carries out (extracting, making, serving), not the product name. A quarry sells stone but its main activity is extraction.

**4.** **(a)** Capital [1]. **(b)** Labour [1]. **(c)** Enterprise [1]. **(d)** Land [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A common slip is to call the robot "land" because it sits in a yard, or to call the owner's savings capital. Learn that capital means man-made resources used in production.

**5.** The three alternatives are valued: teaching 6 × £45 = £270, board hire 3 × £70 = £210 and café 8 × £11 = £88 [1]. Teaching, the highest, is chosen [1]. The opportunity cost is the next best alternative given up, which is board hire at **£210** [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Giving £88 or £298 (210 + 88) shows the definition is not secure. The usual route to full credit is to value every option, then name the single next best.

**6.** Any two, each with a characteristic and an explanation [2 × 2]. Example: willing to take a risk [1], because the entrepreneur may lose their savings if customers do not buy [1]. Innovative [1], because new ideas help the business stand out from rivals [1]. Hard working, organised or other creditworthy characteristics are acceptable. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Naming a characteristic alone usually earns the first mark only. The second mark needs a reason that explains why the trait helps the business.

**7.** Technology: growing online orders [1], so Wintergreen may need a website and delivery arrangements [1], which changes its operations and adds costs, although it may reach more customers [1]. Environmental expectations: customers want peat-free compost [1], so Wintergreen may need to change its product range and suppliers [1], and if it does not, it may lose sales to rivals that do [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Strong analysis follows a chain from the change to an effect on the business and then to a consequence. Answers that only repeat the stem ("customers want peat-free") tend to stay at the first step.

**8.** **(a)** £85,000 − £31,000 [1] = **£54,000** [1]. **(b)** A sole trader has unlimited liability [1], so the £54,000 shortfall could be recovered from the owner's personal assets such as savings or home [1]. In a private limited company the owner has limited liability [1], so the owner loses the £12,000 invested but is not personally responsible for the rest of the debt [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Linking liability to personal assets and to the figures in the stem is usually what separates a developed answer from "limited liability is safer". Use the numbers given.

**9.** **(a)** 5/8 × £56,000 [1] = **£35,000** [1]; 3/8 × £56,000 = **£21,000** [1]. **(b)** Profit must be shared [1], so each partner receives less than a sole trader would keep [1], and with a clinic earning £56,000 the owner of a sole-trader clinic would keep all of it [1]. Other acceptable drawbacks include disagreements over decisions or unlimited liability, each developed in context. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* A drawback "compared with a sole trader" requires a comparison. Saying only "partners may argue" does not compare unless it contrasts with the sole trader making decisions alone.

**10.** **(a)** £240,000 − £232,000 = **£8,000** [1]. **(b)** A not-for-profit organisation normally reinvests the surplus in its cause [1], for example improving care [1], while a private limited company can pay profit to its shareholders as dividends in proportion to shares held [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Answers tend to do better when they contrast who benefits from the money (the cause versus the owners) rather than saying only that one organisation "does not make a profit".

**11.** Benefit: limited liability [1] means Hari's house would not be at risk if the larger business failed, which matters with £150,000 of investment [1]. Benefit: selling shares can raise the £110,000 gap beyond his £40,000 savings [1], without relying only on loans [1]. Drawback: Hari may have to sell shares to others to raise the money [1], which would dilute his control, something he wants to keep [1]. Other drawbacks are extra paperwork and less privacy about accounts. Alternative: staying a sole trader would keep full control but leave his home exposed and capital limited [1]. Judgement: converting is probably sensible, since the scale of the investment makes the protection and extra capital outweigh the loss of some control [1], especially if he sells only a minority of shares so he keeps the majority [1]. If investors insisted on more than half the shares, the answer might change. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* Evaluation answers generally gain more when they weigh both sides using the numbers in the case, then reach a clear judgement with a reason and a condition. A list of ltd features without a decision rarely reaches the top.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Giving the whole of the choice as the opportunity cost instead of the single next best alternative.
- Confusing sector with product, such as calling a quarry secondary.
- Writing a characteristic of an entrepreneur with no explanation.
- Describing a change in the environment without saying how the business is affected.
- Saying limited liability means the business cannot fail or that the owner has no debts.
- Forgetting to use figures from the case study when comparing structures.
- Treating "public limited company" as meaning state-owned.
- Finishing an evaluation with no judgement, or a judgement with no reason.
