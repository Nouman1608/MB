These questions cover **3.2.3 Economic performance** in AQA AS Economics (7135), specification version 1.3, parts 3.2.3.1 to 3.2.3.5. All countries and figures are invented. Revise first with the [3.2.3 study guide](/resources/aqa-as-level-economics-7135-economic-performance-aqa-aslevel-economics/) and the [3.2.3 revision notes](/resources/aqa-as-level-economics-7135-economic-performance-aqa-aslevel-economics-revision-notes/). Give percentages to one decimal place.

## Section A: short questions

**1.** Distinguish between a positive output gap and a negative output gap. **[2]**

**2.** Over a year, the rate of inflation in a country falls from 6% to 2%. Which one of the following describes this change? **[1]**

A: Deflation

B: Disinflation

C: Cost-push inflation

D: A fall in the price level

**3.** A country has 1.5 million people unemployed and a labour force of 32 million. Calculate its unemployment rate. **[1]**

**4.** State two reasons why the claimant count and the Labour Force Survey measure of unemployment may give different figures. **[2]**

**5.** A country's current account shows: trade in goods -52 bn, trade in services +38 bn, primary income -6 bn, secondary income -12 bn. Its GDP is 1,600 bn.

**(a)** Calculate the current account balance. **[1]**

**(b)** Express the balance as a percentage of GDP. **[1]**

**6.** Explain the difference between demand-pull and cost-push inflation. **[2]**

## Section B: structured questions

**7.** Data for Country E over four years:

| Year | Real GDP growth (%) | Unemployment (%) | CPI inflation (%) | Investment growth (%) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 3.8 | 4.0 | 4.1 | 6 |
| 2 | 1.5 | 4.3 | 3.2 | -2 |
| 3 | -0.9 | 5.6 | 1.4 | -8 |
| 4 | 1.2 | 6.0 | 0.9 | 3 |

**(a)** Identify the phase of the economic cycle in year 3, using two indicators. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain why unemployment rose in year 4 even though real GDP grew. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain why investment changes by more than real GDP across the cycle. **[2]**

**8.** Country F's potential output is 1,000 bn in year 1 and grows by 2% a year. Actual real GDP is 980 bn in year 1, 1,010 bn in year 2 and 1,060 bn in year 3.

**(a)** Calculate the output gap as a percentage of potential output in each year. **[3]**

**(b)** Explain the likely change in inflationary pressure over the three years. **[2]**

**9.** Explain two ways in which a recession in a country's main export markets could affect its unemployment and inflation. **[4]**

**10.** Explain how a large rise in world oil prices could cause cost-push inflation and a fall in real output. **[4]**

**11.** Explain two factors that could cause a country's current account deficit to widen. **[4]**

**12.** Country G has an unemployment rate of 7%, but employers report many unfilled vacancies, especially for engineers and care workers. Evaluate whether policies to raise aggregate demand are the best way to reduce unemployment in Country G. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** A positive output gap exists when real GDP is above the economy's productive potential [1]. A negative output gap exists when real GDP is below its productive potential, so there is spare capacity [1]. *Total: 2.*

**2.** **B** [1]. Prices are still rising, but at a slower rate. *Total: 1.*

**3.** 1.5 / 32 x 100 = **4.7%** [1]. *Total: 1.*

**4.** Any two [1 each]: some unemployed people are not entitled to, or choose not to claim, benefits, so they appear in the LFS but not the claimant count; benefit rules can change who is counted in the claimant count without any change in joblessness; the LFS is a survey, so it is an estimate subject to sampling error. *Total: 2.*

**5. (a)** -52 + 38 - 6 - 12 = **-32 bn**, a deficit [1].

**(b)** -32 / 1,600 x 100 = **-2.0% of GDP** [1]. *Total: 2.*

**6.** Demand-pull inflation is caused by aggregate demand growing faster than the economy's capacity to supply [1]. Cost-push inflation is caused by rising production costs, such as wages or imported materials, which shift SRAS to the left [1]. *Total: 2.*

**7. (a)** Recession [1]: real GDP fell by 0.9% and unemployment rose sharply from 4.3% to 5.6% (investment fell 8% and inflation eased) [1].

**(b)** Growth of 1.2% is below the trend rate, so output was still well below potential and firms had spare staff [1]. Firms often wait for recovery to be confirmed before hiring, so unemployment lags behind output [1].

**(c)** Through the accelerator, investment depends on the rate of change of demand for output [1]. A small slowdown in GDP growth means firms need far fewer extra machines, so investment swings from +6% to -8% while GDP growth moves within a narrower range [1]. *Total: 6.*

**8. (a)** Potential: 1,000, 1,020, 1,040.4. Year 1: -20 / 1,000 = **-2.0%** [1]. Year 2: -10 / 1,020 = **-1.0%** [1]. Year 3: +19.6 / 1,040.4 = **+1.9%** [1].

**(b)** In years 1 and 2 the negative gap means spare capacity, so inflationary pressure is weak [1]. By year 3 output is above potential, so labour and materials become scarce and inflationary pressure rises [1]. *Total: 5.*

**9.** Unemployment: lower demand abroad cuts exports, so AD falls [1] and firms in exporting industries cut output and jobs, with the multiplier spreading job losses to other sectors [1]. Inflation: the fall in AD creates or widens a negative output gap, reducing demand-pull pressure [1]; world commodity prices may also fall in a global recession, lowering import costs and inflation further [1]. *Total: 4.*

**10.** Oil is an input for transport, energy and many materials, so its higher price raises firms' production costs [1]. SRAS shifts to the left [1]. Firms pass higher costs on, so the general price level rises: cost-push inflation [1]. At the new equilibrium real output is lower, because higher costs make some production unprofitable [1]. *Total: 4.*

**11.** A rise in the exchange rate makes exports dearer abroad and imports cheaper at home [1], so export volumes fall and import volumes rise, widening the deficit [1]. Higher inflation than in trading partners makes domestic goods less price competitive [1], so consumers switch to imports and foreign buyers buy fewer exports [1]. Other acceptable factors: slower productivity growth than rivals; faster growth at home than abroad; a recession in export markets. *Total: 4.*

**12.** Raising AD, for example through lower interest rates or tax cuts, reduces cyclical unemployment by raising output and the demand for labour [1]. If part of the 7% is cyclical, this would help [1]. But the many unfilled vacancies suggest much of the unemployment is structural: the jobless lack the skills, or live in the wrong places, for the vacancies [1]. Extra AD would raise demand for engineers and care workers who are already scarce, pushing up wages and prices [1] while doing little for workers whose skills do not match [1]. Supply-side measures such as training in engineering and care, or help with moving to jobs, tackle the mismatch directly [1], although they take time and cost public money [1]. Judgement: raising AD is not the best main policy here; targeted supply-side measures are better, with demand support only if the data also show a negative output gap [1]. *Total: 8.*

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calling falling inflation "deflation".
- Dividing the output gap by actual output instead of potential output.
- Naming a cycle phase without quoting the indicators.
- Adding the four current account parts with the wrong signs.
- Recommending one policy for unemployment without identifying its cause.
