## 1.1.4(a) and (d): precision, accuracy and error

- **Precision** is about the spread of repeated results. Results that are close together are precise. The range of repeats is a quick measure of it.
- **Accuracy** is about closeness to the true or accepted value. It is judged with the percentage error.

```
percentage error = (experimental value − accepted value) ÷ accepted value × 100
percentage uncertainty = absolute uncertainty ÷ measured value × 100
```

A set of results can be precise but inaccurate (close together, all too low) or accurate on average but imprecise (widely spread around the true value).

### The key comparison

Compare the percentage error with the total percentage uncertainty from the apparatus.

| Finding | What it suggests |
|---|---|
| Percentage error ≤ total percentage uncertainty | The difference can be explained by apparatus uncertainty; the result agrees with the accepted value within its margin of error |
| Percentage error > total percentage uncertainty | Apparatus uncertainty cannot explain it; look for a systematic error from a limitation in the procedure |

The **margin of error** of a result is its absolute uncertainty. A result of 0.0898 ± 0.0007 mol dm⁻³ lies somewhere between 0.0891 and 0.0905 mol dm⁻³.

## 1.1.4(b): anomalies

An anomaly is a measurement that does not fit the pattern of the others.

**Worked example 1.** Times for a cross to disappear at 40 °C are 31 s, 33 s, 46 s and 32 s.

- 46 s is anomalous: it is far from the other three, which agree within 2 s.
- Leave it out of the mean: (31 + 33 + 32) ÷ 3 = **32 s**.
- Repeat the measurement if possible.
- Suggest a cause that fits a time that is too long: for example, the stopwatch was started before the acid was added, or the mixture had cooled below 40 °C.

On a graph, an anomalous point lies well off the trend of the others. Do not draw the best-fit line through it; circle it and say why it was ignored.

## 1.1.4(c) and (e): limitations and improvements

A limitation is a specific weakness of the procedure that affects the result. Every improvement you suggest must fix a limitation you have named. "Use better equipment" or "be more careful" earns nothing; say which equipment and why.

| Experiment type | Limitation | Effect on result | Improvement |
|---|---|---|---|
| Calorimetry (solution) | Heat lost to surroundings | ΔT too small; ΔH less exothermic | Insulated cup with lid; plot temperature against time and extrapolate |
| Combustion with a spirit burner | Heat lost; incomplete combustion | ΔH much less exothermic than data | Shield the flame; put the beaker closer; use a calorimeter designed for combustion |
| Gas collection | Gas escapes while the bung is replaced | Volume too low | Put the solid in a separate small tube inside the flask, then tip it over after sealing |
| Gas over water | Gas partly dissolves | Volume too low | Use a gas syringe |
| Timing a visual end point | Judging when a cross disappears is subjective | Random scatter in times | Use a light sensor or colorimeter |
| Heating a hydrated salt | Not all water removed | Too little water; x too small | Heat to constant mass |
| Heating a hydrated salt | Strong heating decomposes the anhydrous salt | Extra mass lost; x too large | Heat gently and steadily |
| Titration | End point overshot | Titre too large | Add dropwise near the end point, swirling |

## 1.1.4(d): uncertainties in apparatus

- If a question gives an apparatus uncertainty (often printed on glassware), use that value.
- Otherwise, take the uncertainty of a reading as **half the smallest division** for a scale you read, or **± 1 in the last digit** shown by a digital instrument, as the question tells you.
- A quantity found from **two readings** (titre, temperature change, mass by difference) has **twice** the uncertainty of one reading.
- For a result found by multiplying or dividing measurements, **add the percentage uncertainties**.

**Worked example 2: a titration's total uncertainty.** A 25.0 cm³ pipette (±0.06 cm³), a 250.0 cm³ volumetric flask (±0.3 cm³) and a burette (±0.05 cm³ per reading) give a mean titre of 22.50 cm³.

```
pipette:          0.06 ÷ 25.0  × 100 = 0.24%
volumetric flask: 0.3  ÷ 250.0 × 100 = 0.12%
titre:            (2 × 0.05) ÷ 22.50 × 100 = 0.44%
total percentage uncertainty ≈ 0.80%
```

The titre is the largest contribution. It could be reduced by using a more dilute titrant, giving a larger titre, or a larger pipetted volume. A larger titre with the same ±0.10 cm³ gives a smaller percentage.

## Full evaluation: water of crystallisation

**Worked example 3.** A student heats hydrated copper(II) sulfate, CuSO₄·xH₂O, in a crucible once, for five minutes, and finds:

| Reading | Mass / g |
|---|---|
| Empty crucible | 15.214 |
| Crucible + hydrated salt | 17.711 |
| Crucible + residue after heating | 16.900 |

Use Mr(CuSO₄) = 159.6 and Mr(H₂O) = 18.0. The balance reads to ±0.001 g. The accepted value of x is 5.

**Result.**

```
mass of residue (taken as CuSO₄) = 16.900 − 15.214 = 1.686 g → 1.686 ÷ 159.6 = 0.01056 mol
mass of water lost               = 17.711 − 16.900 = 0.811 g → 0.811 ÷ 18.0 = 0.04506 mol
x = 0.04506 ÷ 0.01056 = 4.27
```

**Percentage error.** (4.27 − 5) ÷ 5 × 100 = **−14.6%**.

**Percentage uncertainty.** Each mass by difference is ±0.002 g. Water: 0.002 ÷ 0.811 × 100 = 0.25%. Residue: 0.002 ÷ 1.686 × 100 = 0.12%. Total ≈ **0.37%**.

**Evaluation.** The error (about 15%) is far larger than the uncertainty (about 0.4%), so the balance cannot explain the difference. The value of x is too low, which fits not all the water being removed: the residue still contained water, so its mass was too high and the water lost too low. The student heated only once. **Improvement:** heat, cool and reweigh repeatedly until two successive masses agree (heating to constant mass). This tests directly whether all the water has gone.

Notice the structure: result, error, uncertainty, comparison, the limitation that explains the direction of the error, and an improvement that fixes that limitation.

## Common mistakes

- Calling results "accurate" because they are close together; that is precision.
- Using one reading's uncertainty for a quantity found from two readings.
- Adding absolute uncertainties of measurements in different units instead of percentage uncertainties.
- Naming a limitation that would change the result in the opposite direction to the error found.
- Suggesting improvements that do not fix a named limitation, such as "repeat the experiment" for a systematic error.
- Leaving an anomaly in a mean or a best-fit line without comment.

## Exam technique

- **"Evaluate"**: give the comparison of error with uncertainty, a judgement, and the main limitation.
- **"Suggest an improvement"**: name the change, and say which limitation it fixes and how the result would change.
- **"Explain whether the result is accurate"**: quote the percentage error and compare it with the uncertainty.
- **Direction matters**: say whether a limitation makes the result too high or too low, and check that this matches the data.
