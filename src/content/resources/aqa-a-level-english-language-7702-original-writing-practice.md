These tasks rehearse the skills of 3.3.2 Original writing in AQA A-level English Language (7702). Your NEA uses a
published style model chosen with your teacher; the two short extracts below are **original**, written for this page
to practise the same analysis. Mill Lane is a fictional street.

**Text M**: the opening of a short story (Storytelling).

> *"The bus was late again. Nadia counted the cracks in the shelter's glass: seven, the same as yesterday. Behind
> her, someone laughed into a phone. She didn't turn round. She already knew who it was."*

**Text N**: the opening of a local history piece (Information).

> *"Walk down Mill Lane today and you'll see a car park, a nail bar and a bus stop. Stand still for a moment, though,
> and imagine the noise. A century ago, this was where the town's looms never stopped."*

---

## Section A

**1.** Name the three areas for NEA original writing, and suggest a suitable form for each. **[3]**

**2.** Explain the difference between borrowing technique and borrowing content from a style model. **[2]**

**3.** Improve this audience statement so it can guide writing choices: *"My story is for adults."* **[2]**

**4.** Identify **three** features of Text M from different language levels. **[3]**

**5.** Explain how sentence structure controls pace and tension in Text M. **[3]**

**6.** Identify **two** features of Text N and explain how each engages a non-specialist reader. **[4]**

## Section B

**7.** Write the next 60 to 80 words of a story in the style of Text M, using **two** of its techniques. Name the
techniques you used. **[5]**

**8.** Plan a persuasive piece. State the form, audience and purpose, and name **three** techniques you would take
from a style model, explaining how you would adapt **one** of them. **[6]**

**9.** Rewrite this commentary sentence so that it analyses rather than narrates: *"First I read lots of travel
articles, then I picked one and wrote my piece about Lisbon."* **[4]**

**10.** A commentary discusses only the student's own piece and never mentions the style model. Explain **two**
weaknesses and how to fix them. **[4]**

**11.** Redraft this sentence from a persuasive leaflet and explain the linguistic reason for your change: *"It has
been decided that the youth club will be closed by the people in charge."* **[4]**

**12.** Write a commentary paragraph of about 120 words for a local history piece that used Text N as its style model.
**[6]**

## Answers

**1.** One mark per area with a suitable form [1] [1] [1]: The Power of Persuasion, for example a speech on a
controversial topic or a letter to an MP; The Power of Storytelling, for example a short story or a dramatic
monologue; The Power of Information, for example travel journalism or a piece of local history.

**2.** Borrowing technique means adopting how the style model writes, such as its structure, sentence patterns or
mode of address [1]; borrowing content means taking its subject matter, ideas or phrases, which should be avoided
because the piece must be original [1].

**3.** Example: *"My story is for readers of a literary magazine aged 18 to 30 who enjoy realistic stories about city
life."* [1] It specifies age, reading context and expectations, which guides lexis, themes and length [1].

**4.** Any three from different levels, 1 mark each [1] [1] [1]: grammar, short simple declaratives ("The bus was
late again."); lexis, concrete detail ("cracks", "shelter's glass"); discourse, a withheld identity ("who it was")
creating a narrative hook; pragmatics, the adverb "again" implying a repeated routine; punctuation and graphology,
the colon introducing a precise number.

**5.** Short simple sentences slow the reader to Nadia's watchful pace [1]; the colon and precise count ("seven, the
same as yesterday") show obsessive attention, implying anxiety [1]; the final two short declaratives refuse
explanation, so tension rests on what is withheld [1].

**6.** Two features, each 1 mark for identification and 1 for explanation. Second-person imperatives ("Walk down",
"Stand still") [1] place the reader physically in the street, making history immediate [1]. The contrast between a
present-day list ("a car park, a nail bar and a bus stop") and the past ("the looms never stopped") [1] creates a
before-and-after structure that makes change concrete for a reader with no specialist knowledge [1].

**7.** Credit, 1 mark each: a continuation in third person past tense consistent with Text M [1]; use of short simple
sentences to control pace [1]; use of withheld information or implication [1]; concrete, precise detail [1]; two
techniques correctly named [1]. Model: *"The phone call ended. Footsteps. Nadia watched the road, not the
reflection. A coat brushed her sleeve. 'Still getting this one?' he said. She nodded at the timetable, as if it
might answer for her. Seven cracks. Six minutes. One bus she could not miss."* (Techniques: minor and simple
sentences for pace; implication without explanation.)

**8.** Credit, 1 mark each: a form suited to persuasion, such as a speech [1]; a precise audience, such as a town
council meeting [1]; a clear purpose, such as keeping a library open [1]; three relevant techniques, such as opening
anecdote, triadic structure and a pronoun shift from "I" to "we" [1] [1]; an explained adaptation, for example
replacing a rally speech's emotive hyperbole with hedged, evidence-led claims for councillors [1].

**9.** Example: *"My style model, a feature article on a coastal town, opened in the present tense with second-person
address ('You step off the tram'), placing the reader in the scene; I adopted this for my Lisbon piece, but shortened
the sentences because my audience reads on a phone."* Credit, 1 mark each: names the style model and a feature [1];
uses terminology [1]; explains a choice through audience or purpose [1]; replaces narration of steps with analysis
[1].

**10.** Weaknesses, 1 mark each: the commentary cannot show what was learned from the style model or explain choices
as adaptations [1]; it misses connections between the two texts, which the commentary is assessed on [1]. Fixes, 1
mark each: begin with an analysis of the style model's key techniques [1]; compare each of your choices with the
style model throughout, explaining adoption and adaptation [1].

**11.** Example: *"The council has decided to close the youth club."* or *"The council is closing our youth club."* [1]
[1] Reason: the original's agentless passive "It has been decided" and delayed agent hide responsibility [1]; the
active clause with "The council" as subject makes the decision-maker explicit, which suits a persuasive leaflet
asking readers to act [1].

**12.** Credit, 1 mark each: names the style model and its key technique (second-person imperatives) [1]; explains
its effect on the reader [1]; states how the student adopted it [1]; explains an adaptation for their own audience
[1]; refers to a redrafting decision with a linguistic reason [1]; evaluates effectiveness honestly [1]. Model:
*"Like my style model, I opened with imperatives ('Look up at the chapel roof') to make readers stand in the place.
The style model used a present-day list to frame the past; I kept this structure but replaced its shop names with
sounds, since my piece is for a podcast. In redrafting I removed two dates from the opening, because they slowed the
hook. The opening now engages listeners quickly, although the middle section relies more heavily on dates than the
style model did."*

## Where marks are usually lost

- Naming techniques without showing them in your own writing.
- Audience statements too vague to guide choices.
- Commentary that tells the story of the writing process.
- Redrafts that change words without explaining the linguistic reason.
