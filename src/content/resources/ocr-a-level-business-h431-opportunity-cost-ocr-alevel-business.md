The list is short, but the idea runs through the whole course: every investment appraisal, every choice between strategies and every use of staff time has an opportunity cost.

## Meaning

**Opportunity cost** is the benefit of the next best alternative that is given up when a choice is made. It exists because resources are scarce: money, time, space and people can each be used only once at a time.

Two points cause most errors:

- It is the **next best** alternative only, not the total of all the alternatives rejected.
- It is a **benefit given up**, not money spent. A business can have a large opportunity cost even when it spends nothing, for example by leaving cash idle.

## Four scarce resources

| Resource | Example decision | Opportunity cost |
|---|---|---|
| Money | Spend £50,000 on a new van | The return from the next best use, such as repaying a loan and saving interest |
| Time | The owner spends two days a week on bookkeeping | The sales or new clients she could have won in that time |
| Capacity | Use a machine for a low-margin order | The contribution from the higher-margin work the machine could have done |
| People | Assign the best engineer to an internal project | The customer work, and income, that engineer would have delivered |

Money is the easiest to value. Time and management attention are often larger but harder to measure, which is why they are overlooked.

## Worked example 1: an order at full capacity

Thornfield Joinery makes oak staircases. Its workshop has 2,400 machine hours a year, and all are used. Each staircase takes 60 hours, so it makes 40 a year. Each staircase sells for £6,500 and has variable costs of £3,800.

A hotel offers an order for 120 oak doors at £350 each. Each door has variable costs of £190 and takes 4 machine hours.

**Without opportunity cost**, the order looks profitable:

- Contribution per door = 350 − 190 = £160.
- Total contribution = 120 × 160 = **£19,200**.

**With opportunity cost:** the workshop is full, so the doors would replace staircases.

- Hours needed for the doors = 120 × 4 = 480 hours.
- Staircases given up = 480 ÷ 60 = 8.
- Contribution per staircase = 6,500 − 3,800 = £2,700.
- Opportunity cost = 8 × 2,700 = **£21,600**.

The order earns £19,200 but costs £21,600 of contribution forgone, so accepting it would **reduce** profit by **£2,400**. Fixed costs are unchanged either way, so they do not affect the comparison.

**Interpreting.** On these figures Thornfield should decline, unless something not in the numbers changes the balance: the hotel might bring regular future work, there might be a gap in staircase orders that leaves hours unused, or Thornfield could rent extra capacity. If the workshop had 480 spare hours, the opportunity cost of those hours would be close to zero, and the order would add £19,200 of contribution. **The same order can be a good or a bad decision depending on the opportunity cost of the resources it uses.**

## Worked example 2: the owner's forgone income

Ruth owns Dalby Garden Centre. Its accounts show a profit of £40,000 for the year. Ruth has £200,000 of her own money invested in the business and works full time in it, taking no salary beyond the profit.

- If she worked as a garden centre manager elsewhere, she estimates she would earn **£35,000** a year.
- If her £200,000 were in a savings account at 4%, it would earn **£8,000** a year.

Profit after these opportunity costs = 40,000 − 35,000 − 8,000 = **−£3,000**.

On the accounts, the business looks profitable. Once Ruth's forgone salary and interest are counted, she is £3,000 a year worse off than if she sold up, invested the money and took a job.

**Interpreting.** This does not mean Ruth should close. Opportunity cost here is an estimate, and it leaves out non-financial benefits: independence, the satisfaction of running her own business, and the chance that profit will grow. It also assumes the job and the 4% return are actually available. But it changes the question she should ask: not "am I making a profit?" but "is this the best use of my time and money?" A potential buyer of the business would ask the same.

## Impact on decisions

Opportunity cost changes decision making in several ways.

- **It forces comparison.** A project is judged against the alternatives, not alone. A project that earns 6% looks fine until a safer option earns 8%.
- **It reveals hidden costs.** Idle cash, spare space and staff time have value; using them for one purpose means not using them for another.
- **It depends on circumstances.** At full capacity, the opportunity cost of an extra order is high; with spare capacity, it is low. The same decision can therefore have different answers at different times.
- **It separates opportunity cost from sunk cost.** Money already spent and unrecoverable is a sunk cost. It has no opportunity cost now, because it cannot be used for anything else. Decisions should look only at future costs and benefits.
- **It links to risk.** The next best option may be less risky, so the true cost of a risky project includes the safety given up.
- **It links to strategy.** Choosing one strategy can close off others. Committing all cash to new shops may mean being unable to respond to an online rival, a cost that is hard to put in figures.

### Limitations

- The next best alternative is often uncertain, so its value is an estimate.
- Non-financial benefits are hard to compare with financial ones.
- In large businesses there may be many alternatives, and the next best is not obvious.
- Managers may underestimate the opportunity cost of their own time.

## Common mistakes

- Adding up all the rejected options.
- Saying "the opportunity cost is the money spent".
- Treating a sunk cost as part of the decision.
- Naming the alternative without giving its value when figures are available.
- Assuming an extra order is profitable without checking whether capacity is spare.

## Exam technique

To **identify** an opportunity cost: name the next best alternative and give its value. To **analyse** its impact: explain how including it changes the decision and why, then link to the case, such as capacity, cash or the owner's time. Strong answers end with what would change the conclusion.
