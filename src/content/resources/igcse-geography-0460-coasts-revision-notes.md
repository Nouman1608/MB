## Waves

| | Constructive | Destructive |
|---|---|---|
| Height | Low | High |
| Swash and backwash | Swash stronger | Backwash stronger |
| Effect | Builds beaches | Erodes beaches |

**What controls wave size:** wind speed, how long the wind blows and the **fetch** (the distance of
open water the wind blows over). A long fetch and strong, steady winds give large, powerful waves.
Destructive waves are also more frequent (several a minute close together), so each backwash drags
material away before the next swash can push it back.

- **Swash** -- water rushing up the beach after a wave breaks.
- **Backwash** -- water flowing back down the beach under gravity.

## Processes

- **Erosion:** hydraulic action, abrasion (corrasion), attrition, solution (corrosion).
- **Transport:** traction, saltation, suspension, solution.
- **Weathering** breaks rock where it stands; erosion removes it. Do not treat them as the same.
- **Longshore drift:** waves arrive at an angle, swash carries material up the beach at that angle,
  backwash returns straight down, so sediment zigzags along the coast.

| Erosion process | How it works |
|---|---|
| **Hydraulic action** | Waves force water and air into cracks; pressure widens them |
| **Abrasion (corrasion)** | Waves hurl sand and pebbles against the cliff, scraping it |
| **Attrition** | Pebbles knock into each other, becoming smaller and rounder |
| **Solution (corrosion)** | Seawater dissolves soluble rock such as limestone or chalk |

**Weathering on cliffs:** freeze-thaw where water in cracks freezes and expands; salt crystals
growing in cracks; chemical weathering of limestone by rainwater. Weathering loosens rock so that
waves and gravity can remove it more easily.

**Deposition** happens when waves lose energy: in sheltered bays, where the coast changes direction,
or behind obstacles.

## Erosional landforms

- **Wave-cut platform:** waves undercut a cliff to form a notch; the cliff collapses and retreats,
  leaving a gently sloping rock platform.
- **Cave → arch → stack → stump:** waves exploit a weakness in a headland; the cave is cut through to
  an arch; the roof collapses leaving a stack; erosion lowers it to a stump.
- **Bays and headlands:** soft rock erodes into bays; hard rock remains as headlands.

**Annotated sequence for a stack (model answer):** hydraulic action and abrasion attack a **joint
or fault** in a headland, widening it into a **cave**. Caves on either side of the headland erode
back to back until they break through, forming an **arch**. Waves keep eroding the base while
weathering weakens the roof, until the roof **collapses** under its own weight, leaving an isolated
**stack**. Continued erosion at the base reduces it to a **stump**, covered at high tide.

**Why bays and headlands form:** the coast is made of alternating bands of rock that meet the sea.
Less resistant rock such as clay erodes quickly into bays; resistant rock such as chalk or
granite is left jutting out as headlands. Once headlands stick out, waves bend (**refract**) around
them, concentrating energy on the headlands and spreading it out in the bays, where beaches form.

## Depositional landforms

- **Beach:** sand or shingle left where wave energy is too low to remove it.
- **Spit:** longshore drift deposits sediment where the coast changes direction, building a ridge out
  into the sea.
- **Coastal sand dunes:** wind deposits dry sand inland of the beach, often stabilised by vegetation.

**Spit detail:** the end of a spit often curves (a **recurved end**) because of changing wind
direction and waves bending around the tip. The sheltered water behind it is calm, so mud settles and
**salt marsh** can form.

**Dune formation:** at low tide, wind dries the sand and blows it inland. It is trapped by obstacles
such as driftwood, building small embryo dunes. Plants with long roots such as marram grass bind the
sand, so the dunes grow higher and more stable further from the sea.

## Coral reefs and mangroves

| | Conditions |
|---|---|
| **Coral reef** | Warm, clear, shallow, typically nutrient-poor water with plenty of sunlight (the algae in coral need light) |
| **Mangrove swamp** | Warm, sheltered, low-energy coasts such as estuaries and lagoons where fine sediment settles |

Mangrove roots trap sediment and reduce wave energy, protecting coasts; they are also nursery areas
for fish.

**More detail on reefs:** corals also need **salt water** of normal salinity and water free of
sediment, so reefs are rare near the mouths of large rivers, where fresh water and silt reduce light
and salinity. **Types:** fringing reefs (close to the shore), barrier reefs (separated from the
coast by a lagoon, as with the **Great Barrier Reef** off Australia) and atolls (rings of coral round
a lagoon).

**Threats:** coral bleaching when sea temperatures rise; pollution and sediment from farming;
overfishing; tourism and coral collecting. Mangroves are cleared for shrimp farms, tourism and
building, which removes natural coastal protection.

## Hazards, opportunities, management

- **Hazards:** coastal erosion and tropical storms.
- **Hard engineering:** sea walls, groynes, rock armour.
- **Soft engineering:** beach nourishment, dune stabilisation, managed retreat.

**Opportunities:** coasts provide ports and trade, fishing, tourism and recreation, flat land for
farming and building, and oil and gas landing points.

| Method | How it works | Evaluation |
|---|---|---|
| **Sea wall** | Concrete wall reflects wave energy | Strong protection, but very costly and can scour the beach in front |
| **Groynes** | Fences across the beach trap sediment moving by longshore drift | Builds a wider beach, but starves beaches further along |
| **Rock armour (rip-rap)** | Large boulders absorb wave energy | Cheaper than a wall, but can look out of place and be hard to walk over |
| **Beach nourishment** | Sand or shingle added to the beach | Looks natural, but must be repeated |
| **Dune stabilisation** | Planting marram grass, fencing off paths | Cheap and natural, slow to work |
| **Managed retreat** | Allow the sea to flood low-value land | Cheap and creates habitats, but people lose land and homes |

## Case study

The syllabus requires **opportunities, hazards and management of an area of coastline**. Prepare a
named area with its opportunities, hazards and the specific management used.

**Example card: the Holderness coast, eastern England.** Low cliffs of soft **boulder clay**
(glacial till) are eroded by powerful North Sea waves, making this one of the fastest-eroding
coastlines in Europe. Longshore drift carries sediment south to build **Spurn Head**, a spit across
the mouth of the Humber estuary.

- **Opportunities:** seaside tourism at resorts such as Hornsea and Withernsea; farmland on the cliff
  tops; a major gas terminal at Easington.
- **Hazards:** rapid cliff retreat destroys farmland, roads and homes; storm surges threaten
  low-lying land.
- **Management:** groynes and rock armour at **Mappleton** in the early 1990s protected the village
  and its coast road; but sediment was trapped there, so beaches to the south became narrower and
  cliff erosion increased -- the classic knock-on effect of groynes. Much of the rest of the coast is
  left undefended because the cost would be greater than the value of the land protected.

## Exam traps

- Mixing up river and coastal landforms -- state that the agent is the sea or wind.
- Listing cave, arch, stack and stump without the process linking them.
- Swapping constructive and destructive waves.
- Giving reef or mangrove conditions as a list of words with no reason.
- Naming management methods but not saying how they work.
- Forgetting that defending one stretch of coast can increase erosion further along.

## Self-test

1. Which wave has the stronger backwash?
2. Describe longshore drift.
3. Put these in order: stack, cave, stump, arch.
4. Why must coral reef water be clear and shallow?
5. Give one hard and one soft coastal management method.
6. What is fetch and how does it affect waves?
7. Explain how hydraulic action erodes a cliff.
8. Why do headlands receive more wave energy than bays?
9. How does marram grass help sand dunes grow?
10. Why are coral reefs rare near large river mouths?
11. Why did groynes at Mappleton increase erosion further south?

**Answers:** 1. Destructive. 2. Waves arrive at an angle, swash carries sediment up the beach at that
angle and backwash returns it straight down, moving it along the coast. 3. Cave, arch, stack, stump.
4. Coral algae need sunlight. 5. Hard: sea wall or groynes; soft: beach nourishment or managed
retreat. 6. The distance of open water the wind blows over; a longer fetch gives bigger, more
powerful waves. 7. Waves force water and air into cracks, and the pressure widens them until rock
breaks away. 8. Waves refract (bend) around headlands, concentrating energy on them and spreading it
out in the bays. 9. Its long roots bind the sand, trapping more and stabilising the dune. 10. Fresh
water lowers salinity and sediment reduces the light corals need. 11. They trapped sediment moving
south by longshore drift, so beaches further south were starved of sand and cliffs were less
protected.
