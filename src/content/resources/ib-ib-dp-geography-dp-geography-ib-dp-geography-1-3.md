This guide goes deeper into sub-topic 1.3 of Option A, Freshwater, in IB Diploma Programme Geography (first assessments 2019): water scarcity and water quality. The [1.3 revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-geography-freshwater-water-scarcity-and-water-quality-revision-notes/) already define physical and economic scarcity, water stress, water footprint and virtual water; give the Falkenmark indicator; explain salinisation, groundwater depletion, eutrophication and saline intrusion; and summarise the Aral Sea and the Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam. This page adds a second way of measuring stress, a closer look at who suffers from scarcity, how water quality is actually managed, and a further shared-river case, the Indus. Practise with the [1.3 practice questions](/resources/ib-ib-dp-geography-dp-geography-ib-dp-geography-1-3-practice/); the [Freshwater option study guide](/resources/ib-ib-dp-geography-freshwater-study-guide/) gives the overview.

## What this sub-topic asks you to do

- Distinguish physical and economic water scarcity and explain their causes and consequences.
- Explain how agriculture and other activities affect water quality.
- Evaluate ways of managing water quality and access.
- Explain why internationally shared water resources can cause tension, and how cooperation is attempted.

## Two ways to measure stress

The Falkenmark indicator compares **supply with population**. A second family of measures compares **use with supply**: the withdrawal-to-availability ratio.

**withdrawal ratio = annual water withdrawn / annual renewable water available**

Many assessments treat a ratio above about 0.4 (40 per cent) as high stress, because at that level users compete strongly and ecosystems lose much of their flow. Thresholds differ between organisations, so name the one you use.

**Worked example (invented countries).**

| | Country P | Country Q |
|---|---|---|
| Renewable water (million cubic metres per year) | 8,000 | 30,000 |
| Population (millions) | 4 | 25 |
| Water per person (cubic metres per year) | 8,000 / 4 = 2,000 | 30,000 / 25 = 1,200 |
| Water withdrawn (million cubic metres per year) | 4,400 | 6,000 |
| Withdrawal ratio | 4,400 / 8,000 = 0.55 | 6,000 / 30,000 = 0.20 |

On the Falkenmark indicator, Country Q (1,200) looks worse off than Country P (2,000). On the withdrawal ratio, Country P is under high stress (0.55) while Country Q is not (0.20). The two measures tell different stories because Country P uses its water intensively, perhaps for irrigated export crops, while Country Q has less water per person but withdraws little of it. Neither measure shows **access**: Country Q could still suffer **economic scarcity** if most of its people lack piped supply or treatment.

The lesson for exams: whenever you are given one indicator, comment on what it leaves out. National averages also hide regional contrasts, seasonal shortages and differences between rich and poor households.

## Who bears scarcity

Scarcity does not fall evenly.

- **Gender.** Where water must be carried from a distant source, the task falls mostly on women and girls, reducing time for school and paid work.
- **Income.** Households without a piped connection may buy water from tankers or vendors at a higher price per litre than connected, wealthier households pay. Poor people can pay more for less.
- **Rural and urban.** Rapidly growing informal settlements often lie outside piped networks; rural areas may depend on a single well that fails in drought.
- **Health.** Turning to unsafe sources spreads water-borne diseases such as cholera and diarrhoeal infections, which hit young children hardest.
- **Livelihoods.** Farmers who lose irrigation water may lose income and migrate.

Use this list to move from "people suffer" to "these people suffer, for these reasons".

## Managing water quality

Quality problems come from **point sources** (sewage outfalls, factory pipes) and **non-point sources** (runoff from many fields, streets and roofs). Strategies match the source.

**Treating wastewater** is the main response to point sources:

1. **Primary treatment** screens out solids and lets sediment settle.
2. **Secondary treatment** uses bacteria to break down dissolved organic matter, lowering biochemical oxygen demand.
3. **Tertiary treatment** removes remaining nutrients such as phosphates and nitrates, or disinfects the water.

Each stage costs more, which is why many lower-income cities release sewage with little treatment.

**Reducing agricultural runoff** targets non-point sources:

- **Buffer strips** of grass or trees along rivers trap sediment and absorb nutrients before they reach the channel.
- **Precision fertiliser use** and timing applications away from heavy rain reduce what washes off.
- **Constructed wetlands** filter runoff through reed beds where plants and microbes take up nutrients.
- **Regulation**: in the European Union, the 1991 Nitrates Directive requires member states to identify areas where water is polluted by farm nitrates and to set rules on fertiliser and manure use there.

**Evaluation.** Treatment works are effective but costly to build and run. Non-point measures are cheap per farm but depend on thousands of individual decisions, so they need incentives, advice or enforcement. Lags matter too: nitrate already in groundwater may take years or decades to flush through, so improvements appear slowly.

## Shared rivers: the Indus Waters Treaty

When a river crosses borders, upstream use affects downstream users, and water becomes political. The **Indus Waters Treaty**, signed by India and Pakistan in 1960 with the World Bank as a signatory and facilitator, divided the Indus system: the three eastern rivers (Ravi, Beas and Sutlej) were allocated to India and the three western rivers (Indus, Jhelum and Chenab) mainly to Pakistan, with India allowed limited uses such as run-of-river hydroelectricity on the western rivers.

Why it matters as an example:

- It **survived wars** between the two countries for decades, which is often used as evidence that water agreements can outlast political conflict.
- It also shows the **limits** of fixed allocation. Disputes over the design of Indian hydroelectric projects on western rivers went to the treaty's dispute procedures, and in April 2025, after a deadly attack in Indian-administered Kashmir, India announced it was holding the treaty "in abeyance", a step Pakistan rejected.
- Pakistan's farming depends heavily on Indus irrigation, so any threat to flow is seen as a threat to food security.

Compare it with the Blue Nile case in the revision notes: both show that upstream dam building raises downstream fears, and that agreements need trust and data sharing as well as allocation rules.

## Annotated model answer

*Distinguish between physical and economic water scarcity, with reference to examples.* **(sample answer)**

"Physical scarcity occurs when demand exceeds the water naturally available, as in Central Asia's Aral Sea basin, where irrigation withdrew so much river flow that the sea shrank **[definition with a located example]**. Economic scarcity occurs where water exists but people cannot access it because of a lack of money, infrastructure or effective governance, a situation widespread in parts of sub-Saharan Africa where rivers and aquifers are under-used but piped supply is limited **[contrast made explicit]**. The distinction matters because the responses differ: physical scarcity calls for demand management or new sources, while economic scarcity calls for investment in pipes, treatment and institutions **[implication shows understanding]**."

## Common mistakes

- Using one indicator as if it gave the full picture of scarcity.
- Treating scarcity as a problem of quantity only, ignoring access and quality.
- Saying "people" suffer without saying which groups, and why.
- Writing about water treatment without separating point and non-point sources.
- Presenting a transboundary treaty as permanently solving conflict.

## Exam technique

- **Distinguish** requires both terms defined and the difference stated, ideally with an example of each.
- **Explain** a consequence by giving a chain: cause, mechanism, effect on a named group.
- **Evaluate** a quality strategy with criteria: effectiveness, cost, who pays, time lag and enforcement.
- **Suggest** reasons for a pattern on a water stress map: think climate, population, irrigation, wealth and infrastructure.
