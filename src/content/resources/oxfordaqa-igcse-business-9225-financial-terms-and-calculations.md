This guide covers section **3.6.3 Financial terms and calculations** of OxfordAQA International GCSE Business (9225), part of Topic 6, Finance. The specification asks you to understand the difference between **variable, fixed and total costs**; the concepts of **revenue, costs, profit and loss**; the main **investment projects** (new machinery, buildings and vehicles) and how to **calculate the payback period**; the meaning of **break-even output**, how to **interpret break-even charts** and **identify the break-even level of output and margin of safety** from a chart; and how to **evaluate the value of break-even analysis**. You will not be asked to draw break-even charts or to use the break-even formula. All businesses and figures in this guide are invented.

For the whole topic, see the [Finance study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-business-finance/). Condensed notes are in the [revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-business-9225-financial-terms-and-calculations-revision-notes/) and questions are in the [practice set](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-business-9225-financial-terms-and-calculations-practice/).

## Costs, revenue, profit and loss

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Fixed costs | Costs that stay the same whatever the output (in the short term) | Rent, insurance, managers' salaries |
| Variable costs | Costs that change with output | Materials, packaging, hourly wages for production |
| Total costs | Fixed costs plus variable costs | $18,000 + $12,000 = $30,000 |
| Revenue | Money from sales = price x quantity sold | 1,500 x $20 = $30,000 |
| Profit | Revenue minus total costs, if positive | $40,000 - $34,000 = $6,000 |
| Loss | Total costs minus revenue, if costs are greater | $22,000 - $10,000 = $12,000 |

Formulae to learn:

- Variable costs = variable cost per unit x output
- Total costs = fixed costs + variable costs
- Revenue = selling price x quantity sold
- Profit (or loss) = revenue - total costs

Formulae are not given in the exam. Fixed costs do not rise with output, so average cost per unit falls as output rises.

## Break-even

**Break-even output** is the level of output at which revenue exactly equals total costs, so the business makes neither a profit nor a loss. Below it the business makes a loss; above it, a profit.

### Break-even from a table

**Worked example 1: finding break-even.** A business sells a product at $20. Fixed costs are $18,000 and variable costs are $8 per unit.

| Output (units) | Revenue | Variable costs | Total costs | Profit or loss |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 0 | 0 | 18,000 | -18,000 |
| 500 | 10,000 | 4,000 | 22,000 | -12,000 |
| 1,000 | 20,000 | 8,000 | 26,000 | -6,000 |
| 1,500 | 30,000 | 12,000 | 30,000 | 0 |
| 2,000 | 40,000 | 16,000 | 34,000 | 6,000 |
| 2,500 | 50,000 | 20,000 | 38,000 | 12,000 |

At 1,500 units revenue and total costs are both $30,000, so the **break-even output is 1,500 units**. Each extra unit above this earns $20 - $8 = $12 profit.

(You will not be expected to use the break-even formula. As a check only: fixed costs divided by the profit per unit, $18,000 / $12 = 1,500 units.)

### Interpreting a break-even chart

A break-even chart shows output (units) on the horizontal axis and money (revenue or costs) on the vertical axis. You will be given the chart. It normally has these lines:

- **Fixed costs:** a horizontal line (here at $18,000).
- **Total costs:** a line that starts at the fixed cost level (here $18,000 at zero output) and slopes upwards.
- **Revenue:** a line that starts at zero and slopes upwards more steeply than total costs.

The point where the revenue line crosses the total costs line is the **break-even point**. Read straight down to the horizontal axis to find the break-even output (1,500 units), and across to the vertical axis for the revenue or cost at that point ($30,000).

To identify profit or loss at any output, find the vertical gap between the revenue and total costs lines. To the left of break-even, total costs are above revenue (loss); to the right, revenue is above total costs (profit).

### Margin of safety

The **margin of safety** is the amount by which actual (or planned) output is above the break-even output. It shows how far sales can fall before the business starts to make a loss.

```
Margin of safety = actual output - break-even output
```

**Worked example 2: margin of safety.** The business above expects to sell 2,200 units.

```
Margin of safety = 2,200 - 1,500 = 700 units
As a percentage of expected sales = 700 / 2,200 x 100 = 31.8%
Profit at 2,200 units = 700 x $12 = $8,400
```

(Check: revenue 2,200 x $20 = $44,000; total costs $18,000 + 2,200 x $8 = $35,600; profit $44,000 - $35,600 = $8,400.)

Sales could fall by 700 units, or about 32%, before the business starts making a loss. A larger margin of safety means a lower risk.

### Changes that move break-even

| Change | Effect on break-even | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Higher selling price | Falls | More profit on each unit |
| Higher variable costs | Rises | Less profit on each unit |
| Higher fixed costs | Rises | More to recover before profit |
| Lower fixed costs | Falls | Less to recover |

**Worked example 3: effect of a price rise.** If the price rises to $22, profit per unit is $22 - $8 = $14 and break-even is $18,000 / $14 = 1,286 units (rounded up). If variable costs rise to $9 per unit instead, profit per unit is $20 - $9 = $11 and break-even is $18,000 / $11 = 1,637 units (rounded up). A price rise helps, but customers may buy fewer units.

### Evaluating the value of break-even analysis

| Advantages | Limitations |
|---|---|
| Shows how much must be sold to avoid a loss | Assumes all output is sold, which may not be true |
| Helps decide the price and the target sales | Assumes costs and the price stay the same at all levels of output |
| Shows the margin of safety and the risk | Only as good as the cost and price estimates |
| Useful in a business plan and for loan applications | Hard for businesses with many products |
| Easy to update to show "what if" changes | Does not take account of competitors or changing demand |

Break-even analysis is a useful planning tool, especially for new products and start-ups, but it should be used with market research, not on its own.

## Investment projects and payback period

Businesses invest in **projects** to increase future income. The main types named in the specification are:

- **new machinery** (to raise output or reduce costs)
- **buildings** (a bigger factory or new shops)
- **vehicles** (for delivery or sales).

A business needs to know how long it will take to recover the cost of the investment. The **payback period** is the time it takes for the net cash flows from an investment to equal the initial cost.

**Worked example 4: payback on a machine.** A machine costs $60,000. Extra net cash flow from using it is expected to be $12,000 in year 1, $18,000 in year 2, $24,000 in year 3 and $24,000 in year 4.

| Year | Net cash flow | Cumulative cash flow |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 12,000 | 12,000 |
| 2 | 18,000 | 30,000 |
| 3 | 24,000 | 54,000 |
| 4 | 24,000 | 78,000 |

After 3 years $54,000 has been recovered, and $6,000 remains. In year 4 the machine earns $24,000, so the remaining $6,000 takes 6,000 / 24,000 = 0.25 of the year.

```
Payback period = 3 years + 0.25 of a year = 3 years 3 months
```

**Worked example 5: even cash flows.** A delivery van costs $40,000 and brings in an extra $10,000 each year.

```
Payback period = $40,000 / $10,000 = 4 years
```

### Using the payback period

| Advantages | Limitations |
|---|---|
| Simple to calculate and understand | Ignores profit after the payback date |
| Useful when the business needs cash back quickly or the future is uncertain | Ignores the timing of cash flows within the payback period |
| Useful for comparing projects | Depends on forecasts, which may be wrong |

A shorter payback period is usually better, because the business recovers the money sooner and is less exposed to risk. A project that has a longer payback may still be better overall if it earns more profit later. The machine above pays back in 3.25 years and earns $78,000 over four years; the van takes 4 years to pay back.

## Common mistakes

- Confusing fixed costs (do not change with output) with variable costs (change with output).
- Starting the total cost line at zero. It starts at the level of the fixed costs.
- Reading the break-even point from the wrong axis. Output is on the horizontal axis.
- Defining margin of safety as profit. It is a number of units (or a percentage) above break-even.
- Forgetting that the payback is in years and months, and that part-years may be needed.
- Treating break-even as a prediction. It is a guide that relies on assumptions.

## Exam technique for this section

For **define** questions, give the meaning with an example. For **calculate** questions, show each step: costs, revenue, then profit; or cumulative cash flow, then the part-year. For **interpret a chart** questions, say which lines cross and read the value on the correct axis. For **evaluate** questions, such as whether break-even analysis helps a business, give advantages and limitations with figures from the case, and conclude with a judgement.
