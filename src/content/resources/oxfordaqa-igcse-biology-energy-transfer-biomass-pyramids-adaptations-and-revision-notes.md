## Energy flow

- Light from the Sun is trapped by plants and algae. About **1%** of the incident energy is stored in cells.
- Each trophic level passes on about **10%** of its biomass.
- Two reasons for the 90% not passed on:
  - waste materials (faeces, urine) carry materials and energy away;
  - respiration supplies the energy for all life processes, including movement, and much ends up as heat in the surroundings.
- A further useful point: some parts are not eaten (bones, roots, shells).
- Energy is transferred to the surroundings, not destroyed.

**Method: percentage transferred**

1. Take the biomass of the higher level.
2. Divide by the biomass of the level below.
3. Multiply by 100.

Reminder: producers 8 000 kJ, primary consumers 800 kJ. Transfer = 800 ÷ 8 000 × 100 = 10%. Predicted secondary consumers at 10% = 80 kJ.

**Method: 1% then 10%**

Light 3.2 × 10⁶ kJ per m². Producers store 1%: 3.2 × 10⁴ kJ. Primary consumers at 10%: 3.2 × 10³ kJ. Secondary consumers: 320 kJ.

## Pyramids of biomass

| Feature | Rule |
|---|---|
| Base | Producers |
| Bar length | Proportional to biomass (use a scale) |
| Bar height | Equal for every bar |
| Position | Bars centred on a vertical line |
| Labels | Each trophic level named; units and scale stated |
| Measure | Usually dry mass per unit area |

**Method: constructing a pyramid**

1. Pick a scale so the largest bar fits on the page.
2. Divide each value by the scale.
3. Draw and label the bars.

Reminder: 640 kg, 72 kg, 8 kg with 1 mm = 10 kg gives 64 mm, 7.2 mm, 0.8 mm.

Interpreting: the narrowing of the bars shows biomass lost at each transfer. If the second bar is 12% of the first, then 12% was transferred.

## Efficiency of food production (B)

- Every extra stage loses about 90% of the biomass.
- Fewer stages means more of the producers' biomass reaches humans.
- Plant crop eaten directly is more efficient than the same crop fed to livestock.
- Reminder: 7 500 MJ of crop. Directly eaten: 7 500 MJ. Through cattle (10%): 750 MJ. That is a tenfold difference.
- Balanced point: grazing can use land that cannot grow crops, and meat provides protein.

## Needs and competition

| Group | Needs from surroundings | Needs from other organisms | Competes for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Plants | Light, carbon dioxide, water, mineral ions, space | Pollination, seed dispersal | Light, space, water, mineral ions |
| Animals | Oxygen, water, shelter, space | Food, mates | Food, mates, territory |

- Competition happens when a resource is in short supply.
- Interdependence: organisms rely on each other, for example predators on prey and flowers on pollinators.
- Crowding (high density) reduces mean growth per individual because each gets a smaller share.
- Removing one species can affect others that depend on it.

## Reading competition data

**Method**

1. Find the trend in mean mass (or number) as density or numbers change.
2. Quote two values from the table, and a percentage change if asked: (old − new) ÷ old × 100.
3. Link the trend to a resource in short supply.

Reminder: mean mass per seedling falls from 1.50 g to 0.30 g as density rises. Decrease = (1.50 − 0.30) ÷ 1.50 × 100 = 80%. Crowded seedlings share the same light, water and mineral ions, so each grows less.

## Adaptations

| Type | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Structural | Shape, size, colour | White fur in snow; flattened flea; thick waxy leaf cuticle |
| Behavioural | What the organism does | Migration; huddling; burrowing |
| Functional | Reproduction, metabolism and other processes | Concentrated urine; many eggs; hibernation lowers metabolic rate |

**Method: suggesting an adaptation from information**

1. Problem the habitat creates (cold, dry, dark, predators).
2. The feature given in the information.
3. How it helps survival or reproduction.

Reminder: a seabird has dense waterproof feathers and a thick fat layer under the skin. Problem: cold water. Features: feathers trap an insulating layer of air; fat insulates. Benefit: less body heat lost, so less energy from respiration is needed to stay warm.

### Extremophiles

- Live where conditions are very extreme: **high salt, high temperature, high pressure**.
- Examples: archaea in salt lakes, *Thermus aquaticus* in hot springs, microorganisms in the deep ocean.
- They are adapted: their cell chemistry works in conditions that would harm most organisms.

## Parasite adaptations (B)

| Parasite | Lives | Key adaptations |
|---|---|---|
| Flea | Among mammal hair | Flattened sideways; claws; piercing mouthparts; jumping legs |
| Tapeworm | Mammal intestine | Hooks and suckers; thick covering resists enzymes; flat and long for absorbing digested food; many eggs |
| Malaria parasite | Single-celled; inside human liver and red blood cells | Tiny; rapid reproduction; spread by mosquito vector |

Always write: feature, then function. For example, hooks on a tapeworm's head (feature) hold it against the gut wall so it is not carried out with the food (function). A feature without a function earns little credit.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Energy transferred vs energy lost**: lost from the food chain, but still exists as heat.
- **Biomass vs number**: pyramids here use biomass; pyramids of numbers are not required.
- **Competition vs interdependence**: competition is for scarce resources; interdependence is reliance on others.
- **Adaptation vs habitat**: the habitat sets the problem (cold, dry, salty); the adaptation is the feature of the organism that solves it.
- **Parasite vs predator**: a parasite usually lives on or in a host and feeds on it without killing it quickly.

## Quick self-test

1. What percentage of incident light energy do plants and algae transfer?
2. Give two reasons why only about 10% of biomass passes to the next trophic level.
3. Producers hold 2 500 kg and primary consumers 275 kg. Calculate the percentage transferred.
4. Scale 1 mm = 25 kg. What bar length shows 600 kg?
5. Where on a pyramid of biomass are the producers?
6. Why is eating wheat more efficient than eating beef from cattle fed on wheat?
7. Name two things plants compete for and two things animals compete for.
8. What is an extremophile? Give one condition they can tolerate.
9. Classify as structural, behavioural or functional: (a) huddling, (b) camouflage colouring, (c) producing thousands of eggs.
10. State two adaptations of a tapeworm for life in the gut.
11. Why can a flea move easily through fur?

### Answers

1. About 1%.
2. Any two: materials and energy lost in waste; energy from respiration used for life processes, including movement; heat transferred to the surroundings.
3. 275 ÷ 2 500 × 100 = **11%**.
4. 600 ÷ 25 = **24 mm**.
5. At the base (bottom bar).
6. Fewer trophic levels, so less biomass and energy are lost in waste and respiration.
7. Plants: any two from light, space, water, mineral ions. Animals: any two from food, mates, territory.
8. An organism that lives in very extreme conditions, such as high salt, high temperature or high pressure.
9. (a) behavioural; (b) structural; (c) functional.
10. Any two: hooks or suckers; thick covering resisting digestive enzymes; long, flat body with large surface area; many eggs.
11. Its body is flattened from side to side, so it slides between hairs.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing that energy is "used up" instead of transferred to the surroundings as heat.
- Giving only one reason for the 90% when two are asked for.
- Forgetting units, the scale or labels on a pyramid.
- Making bars different heights, or not centring them.
- Dividing the wrong way round when calculating percentage transfer (always higher level over lower level).
- Mixing up resources: plants and mates, animals and light.
- Naming an adaptation without saying how it helps the organism survive.
- Stating "it is adapted to the cold" with no feature named.
- Describing a parasite feature but not linking it to attachment, feeding or spreading.
