Subtopic 6.1 of Cambridge O Level Chemistry 5070 has a single learning outcome: "Identify physical and chemical changes, and describe the differences between them". It is short, but it underpins every practical in the course, because preparing a salt, extracting a metal or analysing an unknown always mixes physical steps with chemical ones.

The general [Physical and Chemical Changes guide](/resources/physical-and-chemical-changes/) introduces the basic idea and the usual evidence. This guide goes further: it explains the difference at the level of particles and bonds, works through pairs of changes that look alike, and shows how to prove a new substance has formed. For quick recall use the [physical and chemical changes revision notes](/resources/cambridge-o-level-chemistry-5070-physical-and-chemical-changes-revision-notes/), and test yourself with the [physical and chemical changes practice questions](/resources/cambridge-o-level-chemistry-5070-physical-and-chemical-changes-practice/).

## The single test

Ask one question: **has a new substance, with a different chemical formula, been formed?**

- If **no**, the change is **physical**. The substance may now be a different state, shape, size or mixture, but its particles are the same.
- If **yes**, the change is **chemical**. Atoms have been rearranged into new substances, which can be shown by a chemical equation with different formulae on each side.

Every other clue (a gas, a colour change, a temperature change, how easy it is to reverse) is evidence that helps you answer that one question. None of them is the test on its own.

## Differences you can describe

The outcome asks you to **describe the differences**. These are the points that earn credit:

| | Physical change | Chemical change |
|---|---|---|
| New substance? | No | Yes, at least one |
| Particles | Same particles; only their arrangement, spacing or movement changes | Atoms rearranged; new particles with new formulae |
| Bonds within particles | Not broken (for molecular substances) | Broken and new ones made |
| Equation | Only state symbols change, e.g. H₂O(l) → H₂O(g) | Different formulae, e.g. Fe + S → FeS |
| Reversing | Usually by a physical process such as cooling or evaporating | Usually needs another chemical reaction |
| Mass | Conserved | Conserved |

The last row is a trap in reverse: both kinds of change conserve mass. If a mass seems to change, a gas has escaped or been gained from the air.

## The particle picture

When ice melts or water boils, the **forces between** water molecules are overcome so the molecules can move more freely, but the covalent bonds **inside** each H₂O molecule stay intact. The molecules after the change are identical to those before it.

When water is **electrolysed**, the bonds inside the molecules are broken and new H–H and O=O bonds form, giving hydrogen and oxygen. Those are new substances, so electrolysis is a chemical change.

### A deeper case: melting an ionic compound

When sodium chloride melts, the ionic lattice breaks down and the ions become free to move. Strong ionic bonds are being overcome, yet this is a **physical** change: molten sodium chloride is still sodium chloride, made of the same Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions, and it solidifies back into the same crystals on cooling. Electrolysing that molten salt, however, turns the ions into sodium metal and chlorine gas, which is chemical.

This case shows why "bonds are broken" is not a safe definition of a chemical change. The test remains whether a new substance forms.

## A mixture versus a compound: iron and sulfur

This classic sequence shows both kinds of change one after the other.

1. **Mixing** iron filings with yellow sulfur powder is a **physical** change. The iron is still magnetic and can be pulled out with a magnet; the sulfur is still yellow. Each keeps its own properties, and the proportions can be anything.
2. **Heating** the mixture starts a **chemical** change. The mixture glows, and the glow continues after the heat is removed because the reaction is exothermic. The product, iron(II) sulfide, FeS, is a black solid that is **not** attracted to a magnet: it has properties different from both elements.

Fe(s) + S(s) → FeS(s)

The magnet test is powerful evidence because it shows that a property of the starting material has gone.

## Look-alike pairs

Many questions describe two changes that seem similar. Decide each one with the single test.

| Physical | Chemical | What separates them |
|---|---|---|
| Boiling water | Electrolysing water | Steam is still H₂O; electrolysis forms H₂ and O₂ |
| Sugar dissolving in water | Magnesium "disappearing" in hydrochloric acid | Evaporating the sugar solution gives sugar back; evaporating the acid solution gives magnesium chloride, not magnesium, and hydrogen bubbles were given off |
| Heating iodine crystals (sublimation) | Heating blue hydrated copper(II) sulfate | Iodine vapour condenses back to iodine; the copper(II) sulfate turns white as water of crystallisation is driven off, forming a different substance |
| Candle wax melting | Candle wax vapour burning | Burning forms carbon dioxide and water |
| Crushing marble chips | Heating marble strongly | Powder is still CaCO₃; strong heating forms CaO and CO₂ |
| Mixing ethanol and water | Mixing silver nitrate and sodium chloride solutions | No new substance in the first; a white precipitate of silver chloride forms in the second |

The copper(II) sulfate row deserves care. Adding water to the white solid turns it blue again, so the change is reversible, but it is still chemical: the hydrated and anhydrous forms have different formulae, CuSO₄·5H₂O and CuSO₄. Subtopic 6.3 treats this as a reversible reaction.

A burning candle shows both kinds of change at once: the wax melts (physical) and the wax vapour reacts with oxygen (chemical).

## Evidence that can mislead

- **Gas:** bubbles form when water boils, but no new substance is made.
- **Colour:** diluting a coloured solution makes it paler, which is physical.
- **Temperature:** dissolving some solids, such as ammonium nitrate, makes the water colder, yet dissolving is physical.
- **Disappearance:** a solid that "disappears" may have dissolved (physical) or reacted (chemical).

So when you use evidence, link it to a **new substance**: "bubbles of a gas that turns limewater milky, so carbon dioxide has been formed".

## Proving that a new substance formed

The strongest evidence is a test showing a substance that was not there before:

- a gas test: carbon dioxide turns limewater milky; hydrogen gives a 'pop' with a lighted splint; oxygen relights a glowing splint
- a property test: the iron(II) sulfide magnet test; a precipitate that was not present in either starting solution
- a melting point that differs from the starting material's

## Worked model answers

**Question 1.** Magnesium powder is added to blue aqueous copper(II) sulfate. The blue colour fades, a pink-brown solid forms and the temperature rises. Identify the type of change and explain your answer. **[3]**

**Answer.** Chemical change [1]. A new substance, copper (the pink-brown solid), is formed [1]; the fading blue colour shows copper(II) ions are being used up, and the temperature rise shows energy is released by the reaction [1].

**Question 2.** A student makes copper(II) sulfate crystals by adding copper(II) oxide to warm dilute sulfuric acid, filtering, evaporating and crystallising. Identify each step as physical or chemical. **[4]**

**Answer.** Reacting the oxide with the acid: chemical, since copper(II) sulfate and water are formed. Filtering: physical, since it only separates the excess solid. Evaporating: physical, since only water leaves as vapour. Crystallising: physical, since the dissolved salt simply comes out of solution as a solid.

## Common mistakes

- **Using one clue as the definition**, such as "gas was given off, so it is chemical".
- **Calling a reversible change physical.** Hydrated copper(II) sulfate losing water is reversible and chemical.
- **Saying mass changes in a chemical reaction.** Mass is conserved in both kinds of change.
- **Calling melting an ionic solid chemical** because ionic bonds are broken.
- **Writing "a reaction happened"** as the explanation. Name the new substance or its evidence.

## Exam technique

- **Identify:** name the type of change. One word, physical or chemical, is often enough for the first mark.
- **Describe the differences:** compare both kinds of change in the same sentence, such as "a chemical change forms a new substance, whereas a physical change does not".
- **Explain:** give the reason, linked to a new substance or the lack of one, and quote evidence from the question.
