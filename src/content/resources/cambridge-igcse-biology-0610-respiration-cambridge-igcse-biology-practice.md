## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Which of these is **not** one of the uses of energy named in the syllabus?

A: active transport

B: breathing

C: cell division

D: protein synthesis

**[1]**

**2.** State **three** uses of energy in a human muscle cell. **[3]**

**3.** A bean seedling is growing in soil. State **two** uses of energy in the cells of its roots. **[2]**

**4.** Explain why maintenance of a constant body temperature is a use of energy in a rabbit but not in a tree. **[2]**

**5.** Name the gas released by yeast that is used to measure its rate of respiration. **[1]**

### Section B: structured questions

**6.** A student investigates the effect of temperature on respiration in yeast. She counts the bubbles released from a delivery tube in one minute.

**(a)** State the independent variable and the dependent variable. **[2]**

**(b)** State **three** variables she must keep the same. **[3]**

**(c)** Explain why she leaves each tube in the water bath for 10 minutes before she starts counting. **[1]**

**(d)** Suggest **one** way she could measure the rate of respiration more accurately. **[1]**

**7.** The table shows the volume of gas collected from a yeast and glucose mixture in 4 minutes at different temperatures.

| Temperature / °C | 15 | 25 | 35 | 45 | 55 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Volume of gas in 4 minutes / cm³ | 2.0 | 6.0 | 10.4 | 4.8 | 0.4 |

**(a)** Calculate the rate of gas production at 35 °C in cm³ per minute. **[1]**

**(b)** Describe the effect of temperature on the volume of gas collected. Use data from the table. **[3]**

**(c)** Explain the result at 55 °C. **[2]**

**(d)** State the range within which the optimum temperature lies, and suggest how the student could find it more precisely. **[2]**

**8.** A second student uses hydrogencarbonate indicator to compare respiration in yeast at 20 °C and 35 °C. The indicator is placed above the yeast mixture.

**(a)** State the colour change of the indicator as carbon dioxide is released. **[2]**

**(b)** The indicator changes colour in 240 s at 20 °C and in 80 s at 35 °C. Calculate how many times faster the rate is at 35 °C. **[2]**

**9.** A student sets up a tube containing glucose solution but no yeast, alongside the tubes containing yeast.

**(a)** State the purpose of this tube. **[1]**

**(b)** State what result you would expect from this tube. **[1]**

**10.** Respiration releases energy in all living cells.

**(a)** A student writes: "Respiration produces energy, which is used for movement and keeping warm." Rewrite the sentence using the correct scientific terms. **[3]**

**(b)** Nerve cells in the spinal cord respire continuously. State the use of the energy that is particular to these cells. **[1]**

**11.** Yeast cells are added to a dough that is left in a warm place to rise before baking.

**(a)** Suggest why the dough rises more quickly at 30 °C than at 15 °C. **[2]**

**(b)** Suggest why the dough stops rising when it is put in a hot oven. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** **B** [1].

*Examiner insight:* Breathing movements use energy through muscle contraction, but "breathing" itself is not on the syllabus list.

**2.** Any three of: muscle contraction; protein synthesis; cell division; active transport; growth; maintenance of a constant body temperature [1] [1] [1].

*Examiner insight:* "Movement" alone is usually not accepted for muscle contraction.

**3.** Any two of: active transport (of ions into root hair cells); protein synthesis; cell division; growth [1] [1].

**4.** A rabbit is a mammal that keeps its body temperature constant, using heat released by respiration [1]; a tree does not keep a constant body temperature, so it does not use energy for this [1].

**5.** Carbon dioxide [1].

**6. (a)** Independent: temperature [1]. Dependent: number of bubbles per minute / rate of respiration [1].

**(b)** Any three of: volume of glucose solution; concentration of glucose solution; mass or volume of yeast; time left to warm up; time over which bubbles are counted; type of yeast [1] [1] [1].

**(c)** So that the mixture reaches the temperature of the water bath before measurements start [1].

**(d)** Collect the gas in a gas syringe (or measuring cylinder over water) and measure its volume [1].

*Examiner insight:* "Repeat it" improves reliability, not accuracy; the question asks for a more accurate measurement method.

**7. (a)** 10.4 ÷ 4 = **2.6 cm³ per minute** [1].

**(b)** As temperature increases from 15 °C to 35 °C, the volume of gas increases [1]; the highest volume is 10.4 cm³ at 35 °C [1]; above 35 °C the volume decreases, to 0.4 cm³ at 55 °C [1].

**(c)** Respiration is controlled by enzymes [1]; at 55 °C the enzymes are (mostly) denatured, so very little respiration takes place [1].

**(d)** Between 25 °C and 45 °C [1]; repeat using smaller temperature intervals, for example every 2 °C between 25 °C and 45 °C [1].

*Examiner insight:* In (b), quote values with units. In (c), "the yeast is killed by heat" is not the explanation credited; name the enzymes.

**8. (a)** From red [1] to yellow [1].

**(b)** 240 ÷ 80 [1] = **3 times** faster [1].

*Examiner insight:* A shorter time means a faster rate, so divide the longer time by the shorter one.

**9. (a)** It is a control: to show that the gas is produced by the yeast / that glucose alone does not release gas [1].

**(b)** No gas is produced / no bubbles / the indicator stays red [1].

**10. (a)** Respiration **releases** energy [1], which is used for **muscle contraction** [1] and the **maintenance of a constant body temperature** [1].

**(b)** The passage of nerve impulses [1].

**11. (a)** Yeast respires faster at 30 °C because the enzymes controlling respiration work faster at the higher temperature [1]; more carbon dioxide is released, making the dough rise faster [1].

**(b)** The high temperature denatures the yeast's enzymes [1], so respiration stops and no more carbon dioxide is released [1].

*Examiner insight:* This is an unfamiliar context, so marks go to linking temperature to enzymes and then to carbon dioxide release, the same reasoning as the laboratory investigation.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using everyday words instead of the syllabus list of uses of energy.
- Including uses that need muscles, neurones or a constant body temperature for plants.
- Confusing accuracy (better measurement method) with reliability (repeats).
- Describing a trend without quoting data and units.
- Saying the yeast, rather than its enzymes, is denatured.
- Dividing times the wrong way round when comparing rates.
