## Key definitions

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Market | Wherever buyers meet sellers to swap goods, services or resources for payment |
| Primary sector | Extracts or grows natural resources |
| Secondary sector | Manufactures and builds |
| Tertiary sector | Provides services |
| Good / service | Physical, storable item / intangible activity produced and consumed together |
| Factor market | Where land, labour, capital and enterprise are bought and sold |
| Product market | Where completed goods and services change hands |
| Derived demand | Demand for a factor that depends on demand for what it produces |
| Specialisation | Focusing a worker, firm, region or country on a narrow set of jobs or products |
| Demand | How much buyers will purchase, and can afford, at each price over a stated period |
| Market demand | Every buyer's quantity added together at each price |
| Extension / contraction | Movement down / up the curve because the good's own price changed |
| Increase / decrease in demand | Shift right / left caused by a non-price factor |
| PED | Responsiveness of quantity demanded to a change in price |
| Total revenue | Price × quantity sold |

## 2.1 in short

- **Sectors.** Primary and secondary make goods; tertiary supplies services. As an economy develops, the tertiary share of jobs usually rises. Check numbers: in an invented Country A, 4.5 million tertiary jobs become 8.4 million: (8.4 − 4.5) ÷ 4.5 = +86.7%.
- **Factor and product markets are linked.** Households sell labour (factor market) and earn wages; they spend wages in product markets. Firms sell output there and use the receipts to buy factors. Fall in product demand leads to fall in factor demand.
- **Specialisation and exchange.**

| Group | Benefit | Cost or risk |
|---|---|---|
| Producers | Higher output, lower unit cost | Dependence on one product; breakdown stops all stages |
| Workers | Skill, higher productivity and pay | Boredom; narrow skills; unemployment |
| Regions | Skills, suppliers, jobs gather together | One industry's decline hits the whole area |
| Countries | More output and choice via trade | Import dependence; exposure to world prices |

**Worked reminder.** Five potters each make 6 mugs a day alone (30). With separate stages the team makes 42. Output +40%. Daily wages £300: cost per mug £300 ÷ 30 = £10.00 falls to £300 ÷ 42 = £7.14 (−28.6%).

**Evaluation pointers.** Gains depend on demand staying strong and on workers retraining easily. Diversified regions and countries cope better. Always end with a judgement: usually the gains are larger, provided risk is managed.

## 2.2: demand

**Method: building a market demand schedule**

1. List prices in order.
2. Add every individual's quantity at the same price.
3. Plot price (vertical) against quantity (horizontal) and join the points; label the curve D.

**Method: deciding movement or shift**

1. Did the good's own price change? If so, movement along the curve.
2. If something else changed (income, tastes, related prices, population, expectations, advertising, season), the whole curve shifts.
3. State the direction and say increase or decrease in demand.

| Cause | Result |
|---|---|
| Income up, normal good | Shift right |
| Income up, inferior good | Shift left |
| Rival substitute dearer | Shift right |
| Complement dearer | Shift left |
| Fashion or advert favourable | Shift right |
| Price expected to rise | Shift right now |

**Consequences.** Shift right with unchanged supply: price pressure upward, higher revenue and perhaps more hiring for producers; consumers pay more or find shortages. Shift left: lower sales, discounts, possible cuts in output and jobs. Movement: price cut raises quantity; whether revenue rises depends on PED.

## PED

**PED = quantity change (%) ÷ price change (%)**, where % change = change ÷ original × 100.

| PED size | Type | Curve | Price rise: revenue |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | Perfectly inelastic | Vertical | Rises |
| 0 to 1 | Inelastic | Steep | Rises |
| 1 | Unitary | Between | Roughly unchanged |
| Over 1 | Elastic | Shallow | Falls |
| Infinity | Perfectly elastic | Horizontal | Falls to zero |

**Determinants:** substitutes, necessity or luxury, share of income, time, habit, narrowness of definition.

**Worked reminder.** Price £50 to £55 (+10%); quantity 2,000 to 1,700 (−15%). PED = −15 ÷ 10 = **−1.5**, elastic. Revenue £100,000 to £93,500: it fell, as expected.

**Importance.** Producers: pricing, forecasting revenue, setting promotions. Consumers: inelastic goods take a larger bite when prices rise. Evaluation: revenue is not profit, PED is an estimate that changes over time, rivals may react, and costs rise with extra output.

## Method: calculating PED in steps

1. Price change as a percentage: (new − old) ÷ old × 100.
2. Quantity change as a percentage, found the same way.
3. Divide the quantity result by the price result.
4. Say whether the size is below 1 (inelastic), equal to 1 (unitary) or above 1 (elastic).
5. If the question asks about revenue, calculate price × quantity before and after, and compare.

Do not stop at the number: add the interpretation in words, since a bare figure leaves the meaning unstated.

## Must-know distinctions

| Pair | Difference |
|---|---|
| Movement and shift | Own price change versus any other factor |
| Extension and increase in demand | Extension is a movement down the curve; an increase is a shift right |
| Individual and market demand | One buyer versus all buyers added together at each price |
| Factor market and product market | Inputs are traded versus outputs are traded |
| Slope and elasticity | Slope is steepness; elasticity is a percentage comparison and varies along a straight line |
| Revenue and profit | Revenue is price × quantity; profit is revenue minus costs |

## Drawing checklist

- Mark Price on the upright axis and Quantity on the flat one, with a unit or period if given.
- Draw demand as a downward-sloping line labelled D; draw a second line D1 for a shift.
- For an extension or contraction, mark two points on the same curve and show an arrow along it.
- For perfectly inelastic demand, draw a vertical line; for perfectly elastic demand, a horizontal line; for inelastic, a steep curve; for elastic, a shallow curve.
- Use data from the question when it is given: plot the points, then join them.

## Evaluation chains to rehearse

- **Specialisation:** more output per worker leads to lower unit costs and lower prices or higher profit; but a single-product region is exposed to a fall in demand; so the judgement depends on how easily workers retrain and how varied the economy is.
- **Rise in demand:** more demand leads to higher price or more sales; producers earn more and may expand; but consumers pay more, and supply may not be able to respond quickly.
- **Price cut on an elastic good:** quantity rises by a larger percentage than price falls, so revenue rises; but extra output costs more to make, so profit may not rise, and rivals may cut prices too.
- **Price rise on an inelastic good:** quantity falls by a smaller percentage, so revenue rises; but over time buyers may find substitutes, and consumers on low incomes lose most.

## Quick self-test

1. Define a market.
2. Which sector does a fishing business belong to, and does it make goods or services?
3. Give one way factor and product markets are interdependent.
4. Give one cost of specialisation for workers.
5. Price of a snack rises from £4 to £5 and demand falls from 800 to 680. Calculate PED.
6. PED = −0.3. Price rises 20%. What happens to quantity demanded?
7. Is the answer to question 5 elastic or inelastic? Does revenue rise or fall?
8. Incomes rise and demand for bus travel falls. What type of good is it?
9. Price of tea rises. Does the demand for coffee shift left or right?
10. On a diagram, which curve shape shows perfectly inelastic demand?
11. In a market, price of a drink falls from £3 to £2.40 and quantity rises from 500 to 650. Calculate PED.

### Answers

1. Wherever buyers meet sellers to exchange goods, services or resources.
2. Primary; it produces goods.
3. Wages earned in factor markets are spent in product markets (or demand for labour is derived from demand for the product).
4. Repetitive work, narrow skills or risk of unemployment (any one).
5. Price +25%; quantity (680 − 800) ÷ 800 = −15%; PED = −15 ÷ 25 = **−0.6**.
6. Falls by 0.3 × 20 = **6%**.
7. Inelastic (0.6 is below 1). Revenue before 3,200; after 5 × 680 = 3,400, so it **rises**.
8. An inferior good.
9. Right: coffee is a substitute, so demand for it increases.
10. A vertical line.
11. Price −20%; quantity +30%; PED = **−1.5** (elastic).

## Where marks are usually lost

- Saying demand rose after the good's own price fell; that is an extension, a rise in quantity demanded.
- Dividing by the new figure instead of the original.
- Leaving out units, or stating PED without saying elastic or inelastic.
- Claiming revenue always rises with a price cut.
- Describing specialisation benefits only, with no cost or judgement.
- Mixing up primary and secondary (processing is secondary).
- Naming a good as a service, or a service as a good.
- Giving a list of factors that shift demand but no consequence for producers or consumers.
