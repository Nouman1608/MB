Condensed recall for **2.1 Diurnal energy budgets** (the energy balance of a surface over 24 hours). For full explanations, read the [Core Physical Geography study guide](/resources/a-level-cambridge-geography-core-physical-geography/).

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Insolation | Incoming solar radiation (shortwave) |
| Albedo | Proportion of incoming radiation reflected by a surface |
| Longwave radiation | Heat radiated by the Earth's surface and atmosphere |
| Sensible heat | Heat transferred by conduction and convection; changes air temperature |
| Latent heat | Heat absorbed or released when water changes state |
| Ground (subsurface) heat flux | Heat conducted into or out of the ground |
| Net radiation | Incoming minus outgoing radiation at the surface |
| Diurnal | Over a 24-hour day-night cycle |
| Temperature inversion | Air temperature rising with height near the ground, usually after a clear night |
| Radiation fog | Fog formed when surface cooling chills moist air to its dew point |

## Daytime budget

Inputs: shortwave solar radiation (plus longwave from the atmosphere). Losses and uses:

- reflected shortwave (set by albedo);
- energy absorbed into the surface and ground;
- sensible heat transferred to the air;
- latent heat used in evaporation (from soil, water and plants);
- longwave radiation emitted.

Net radiation is positive, so the surface warms and temperature rises towards early afternoon.

**The daytime budget as an equation** (the six-component model often used, after T. R. Oke):

> Energy available at the surface = incoming solar radiation - reflected solar radiation - surface absorption (into the ground) - sensible heat transfer - longwave radiation - latent heat transfer (evaporation)

The surface is in balance when inputs equal all the uses and losses. Learn each term, which way it goes, and what controls its size.

**Why the warmest time is after midday:** insolation peaks around noon, but net radiation stays positive into the early afternoon, so the surface and air keep gaining energy until incoming and outgoing flows balance.

## Night-time budget

There is no solar input. The surface loses energy by longwave radiation, and cools. Offsetting gains: heat conducted up from the subsurface, sensible heat from the air, and latent heat released if dew or fog forms. Net radiation is negative.

**The night-time budget as an equation:**

> Energy available at the surface = - longwave radiation + subsurface supply + sensible heat transfer + latent heat transfer (condensation)

The coldest time is usually just before dawn, after a whole night of longwave loss.

## Albedo values (approximate)

| Surface | Typical albedo |
|---|---|
| Fresh snow | Very high, often 80 to 90 per cent |
| Desert sand | Fairly high, about 30 to 40 per cent |
| Grass and crops | About 20 to 25 per cent |
| Forest | Low, about 10 to 20 per cent |
| Asphalt | Very low, under 10 per cent |
| Water | Low when the sun is high; much higher at low sun angles |

These are typical ranges; use the figures given in a question if they differ.

## What changes the budget

| Factor | Effect |
|---|---|
| Surface type | Dark, dry surfaces have low albedo and heat quickly; snow reflects most radiation |
| Moisture | Wet surfaces use energy for evaporation, so heat less |
| Cloud | By day reflects insolation; by night traps longwave, so nights are warmer |
| Vegetation | Shades the ground, transpires, alters albedo |
| Urban areas | Dark materials store heat and release it at night |
| Wind | Mixes air, spreading sensible heat and limiting both daytime heating and night cooling |
| Season and latitude | Sun angle and day length control the size of the solar input |

## Contrasting two surfaces

| | Hot desert | Tropical rainforest |
|---|---|---|
| Daytime | Little moisture, so almost no latent heat; most energy heats the ground and air; very high surface temperatures | Much energy used in evaporation and transpiration; temperatures high but moderated |
| Night | Clear skies and dry air allow strong longwave loss; rapid cooling | Cloud and humid air trap longwave; small night cooling |
| Diurnal range | Very large | Small |

## Night-time phenomena

- **Dew:** on a clear, calm night, surfaces cool by longwave radiation; air in contact cools to its dew point and water vapour condenses on grass and other surfaces, releasing latent heat. Below 0 degrees C, frost forms instead.
- **Radiation fog:** if the air is moist and there is a light breeze, cooling spreads through a shallow layer and droplets form in the air.
- **Temperature inversion:** air near the cold ground becomes colder than the air above it. Pollution and fog are trapped beneath the inversion until the morning sun warms the surface.
- **Frost hollows:** cold, dense air drains downhill at night and collects in valley floors, which are colder than the slopes above.

## Annotated model paragraph

*"Explain why the diurnal temperature range is greater on clear days than cloudy ones."*

> By day, clear skies let more insolation reach the surface, so it heats strongly (**daytime input**). At night, no cloud absorbs and returns longwave radiation, so heat escapes and the surface cools sharply (**night loss**). Cloud reflects insolation by day and traps longwave by night (**contrast**). So the range is larger under clear skies (**conclusion**).

## Exam traps

- Albedo is the **reflected share**, not the absorbed amount.
- Cloud acts in both directions: cooler days, warmer nights.
- Latent heat is not a temperature change you can feel; sensible heat is.
- Describe both day and night if a question says "diurnal".
- Insolation is shortwave; terrestrial radiation is longwave. Do not mix them up.
- The ground stores heat by day and returns it at night: the subsurface flux changes direction.
