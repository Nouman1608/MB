## 4.4.1 Defences: condensed recall

### Volenti non fit injuria (complete defence)

**Requirements:** full knowledge and understanding of the risk, and free agreement to accept it.
Knowledge alone is not enough (Smith v Baker, 1891).

| Context | Rule | Authority |
|---|---|---|
| Employment | Rarely succeeds; employees lack free choice | Bowater v Rowley Regis (1944); exception ICI v Shatwell (1965) |
| Medical treatment | Consent to a procedure, not to negligence | General principle |
| Sport: players | Inherent risks only, not reckless play | Condon v Basi (1985) |
| Sport: spectators | Errors of judgement, not reckless disregard | Wooldridge v Sumner (1963) |
| Rescuers | Not volunteers | Haynes v Harwood (1935); Baker v TE Hopkins (1959) |
| Passengers in cars | Defence excluded | s149 Road Traffic Act 1988 |
| Drunk pilot | Defence succeeded | Morris v Murray (1991) |

### Contributory negligence (partial defence)

- **Statute:** Law Reform (Contributory Negligence) Act 1945, s1(1); damages reduced as just and
  equitable having regard to the claimant's share in responsibility.
- **Fault contributes to damage, not necessarily the accident:** Froom v Butcher (1976), 25 per cent if
  a seat belt would have prevented injury, 15 per cent if it would have reduced it; O'Connell v
  Jackson (1972), no crash helmet.
- **Objective test:** Jones v Livox Quarries (1952). **Children:** Gough v Thorne (1966).
- **Drunk driver:** Owens v Brimmell (1977).
- **No 100 per cent reduction:** Pitts v Hunt (1991).

### Other general defences

| Defence | Meaning | Authority |
|---|---|---|
| Inevitable accident | Harm not avoidable by reasonable care | Stanley v Powell (1891) |
| Act of God | Natural event no foresight could guard against | Nichols v Marsland (1876); contrast Greenock Corporation v Caledonian Railway (1917) |
| Statutory authority | Inevitable result of an authorised activity | Allen v Gulf Oil Refining (1981) |
| Illegality | Claim arising from the claimant's serious wrongdoing | Pitts v Hunt (1991); Gray v Thames Trains (2009); Patel v Mirza (2016); contrast Revill v Newbery (1996) |
| Necessity | Action to prevent greater harm | Cope v Sharpe (1912); Rigby v Chief Constable of Northamptonshire (1985) |

## 4.4.2 Remedies: condensed recall

**Purpose of damages:** restore the claimant to the position before the tort (Livingstone v Rawyards
Coal, 1880).

| Type | Content |
|---|---|
| Special damages | Quantified losses to trial: medical bills, repairs, lost earnings |
| General damages | Pain, suffering, loss of amenity; future losses |
| Nominal | Small sum for infringement without loss |
| Contemptuous | Tiny sum for a technically successful but unmeritorious claim (Grobbelaar, 2002) |
| Aggravated | Compensation for extra injury to feelings from the manner of the tort |
| Exemplary | Punishment; only in Rookes v Barnard (1964) categories |

**Rookes v Barnard categories:** oppressive, arbitrary or unconstitutional action by government
servants; conduct calculated to make a profit exceeding compensation; authorised by statute.

**Personal injury:**

- Pain, suffering and loss of amenity even if unaware (H West and Son v Shephard, 1964).
- Future loss: multiplicand (annual loss) × multiplier (period, adjusted for discount rate and
  actuarial tables).
- Lump sum versus periodical payment orders (Damages Act 1996); provisional damages for risk of
  serious deterioration.

**Death:** estate claims survive (Law Reform (Miscellaneous Provisions) Act 1934); dependants' loss of
support and bereavement award (Fatal Accidents Act 1976).

**Policy issues:** guesswork in lump sums; cost and delay; fault-based inequality between similar
injuries; burden on insurers and public bodies. **Reforms:** more periodical payments, no-fault schemes,
tariffs for minor claims.

**Injunctions:**

| Type | Purpose | Authority |
|---|---|---|
| Prohibitory | Stop a continuing tort | Kennaway v Thompson (1981), limited racing |
| Mandatory | Require positive action; strict conditions | Redland Bricks v Morris (1970) |
| Interlocutory | Preserve position before trial | American Cyanamid v Ethicon (1975) |
| Damages in lieu | Money instead of an injunction | Shelfer (1895); Coventry v Lawrence (2014); Miller v Jackson (1977); Dennis v MoD (2003) |

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Volenti non fit injuria | No harm is done to one who consents to the risk |
| Contributory negligence | Claimant's own fault contributing to the damage |
| Apportionment | Division of responsibility between claimant and defendant |
| Act of God | Natural event beyond human foresight |
| Ex turpi causa | No action arises from a base cause; the illegality defence |
| Special damages | Precisely calculable past losses |
| General damages | Losses not precisely calculable, including future loss |
| Exemplary damages | Damages intended to punish |
| Multiplier | Figure reflecting the duration of future loss |
| Interlocutory injunction | Temporary order before trial |
| Damages in lieu | Damages awarded instead of an injunction |

## Exam traps

- **Knowledge is not consent:** apply Smith v Baker before accepting volenti.
- **Passengers:** volenti is excluded by s149 Road Traffic Act 1988; use contributory negligence.
- **Apply percentages correctly:** reduce the total damages, then state the final award.
- **Aggravated versus exemplary:** aggravated compensate; exemplary punish.
- **Injunctions are discretionary:** explain why the court might award damages in lieu.
- **Statutory authority** does not cover negligent performance of the authorised activity.

## Self-test

1. Why did volenti fail in Smith v Baker?
2. Why did volenti succeed in ICI v Shatwell?
3. What does s149 Road Traffic Act 1988 do?
4. What guideline reductions did Froom v Butcher give?
5. Why can a reduction for contributory negligence not be 100 per cent?
6. What is the difference between inevitable accident and Act of God?
7. What did Revill v Newbery show about illegality?
8. What happened in Rigby v Chief Constable of Northamptonshire?
9. Distinguish special and general damages.
10. Name the three Rookes v Barnard categories for exemplary damages.
11. What did Kennaway v Thompson show about injunctions?
12. How did Coventry v Lawrence affect the Shelfer test?

**Answers:**

1. Knowing of the risk of stones swung overhead was not agreeing to accept it.
2. Experienced shotfirers freely and jointly broke safety rules, accepting the risk.
3. It prevents a driver relying on volenti against a passenger.
4. 25 per cent if a seat belt would have prevented the injury; 15 per cent if it would have reduced it.
5. The claimant would recover nothing, which the 1945 Act does not contemplate (Pitts v Hunt).
6. Inevitable accident is harm reasonable care could not avoid; Act of God is a natural event beyond
   human foresight.
7. Illegality is not automatic; the burglar recovered damages, reduced for contributory negligence.
8. CS gas fired to flush out a gunman was justified by necessity, but the police were negligent in
   having no fire-fighting equipment ready.
9. Special damages are calculable losses to trial; general damages cover non-pecuniary and future losses.
10. Oppressive, arbitrary or unconstitutional government action; conduct calculated to make a profit;
    statutory authorisation.
11. An injunction can be tailored to limit, rather than ban, an activity.
12. The Shelfer criteria should not be applied mechanically; public interest can support damages in lieu.
