## 7.1 Uptake and transport of water and ions

### Root hair cells

A **root hair cell** is a cell on the outer surface of a young root with a long, thin extension that grows out between soil particles.

| Feature | How it helps uptake |
|---|---|
| Long, thin hair extension | Gives a large surface area, so more water enters by osmosis and more ions by active transport |
| Thin cell wall | Short distance for water to cross |
| Cell sap with a lower water potential than soil water | Water moves into the cell by osmosis |
| Many mitochondria | Release energy by respiration for active transport of mineral ions |

Water enters by **osmosis**. Mineral ions are usually less concentrated in the soil than in the cell, so they enter by **active transport**.

### The pathway of water

The syllabus limits the pathway to four stops:

```
soil water → root hair cells → root cortex cells → xylem
           (root, then stem, then leaf vein) → mesophyll cells
```

Water crosses the root cortex cell to cell by osmosis to the xylem in the centre of the root. Xylem vessels carry it up the stem into the leaf veins, and it passes into the mesophyll cells. Mineral ions travel dissolved in the same water.

### Practical: tracing water in a cut stem

**Worked example: planning the stain investigation**

*Question:* Describe how you would show the pathway of water through a stem.

*Answer, step by step:*
1. Cut the base of a leafy stem, such as a celery stalk, under water so no air enters the xylem.
2. Stand it in a beaker of coloured stain, such as eosin or a water-soluble dye.
3. Leave it in a warm, bright place for a few hours so water is pulled up quickly.
4. Remove it and cut thin transverse sections at several heights with a sharp blade. Cut away from your fingers on a tile.
5. Look at the sections with a hand lens or microscope.
6. Expected result: the stain appears only in the vascular bundles, in the xylem. Leaf veins also become coloured.
7. Conclusion: water travels up the stem in the xylem.

## 7.2 Transpiration and translocation

### What transpiration is

**Transpiration is the loss of water vapour from leaves.**

It happens in two steps:
1. Water **evaporates** from the surfaces of the mesophyll cells into the **air spaces** inside the leaf.
2. The water vapour then **diffuses** out of the leaf through the **stomata**, from a higher concentration of water vapour inside the leaf to a lower concentration outside.

Most stomata are on the lower epidermis, so most water vapour leaves from the underside of the leaf.

### Factors that affect transpiration rate

| Factor | Change | Effect on rate | Explanation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wind speed | Increases | Increases | Wind blows away water vapour from around the leaf, keeping the concentration gradient between inside and outside steep |
| Temperature | Increases | Increases | Water molecules have more kinetic energy, so they evaporate from mesophyll surfaces and diffuse faster |
| Humidity | Increases | Decreases | Air outside already holds more water vapour, so the concentration gradient is smaller and diffusion slower |
| Light intensity | Increases | Increases | Stomata open wider in light (for photosynthesis), so more water vapour can diffuse out |

### How wilting occurs

**Wilting** happens when a plant loses water by transpiration faster than it takes water up from the soil. The cells lose water, become **flaccid** and lose **turgor pressure**. Turgid cells normally press against each other and hold up the stem and leaves, so without turgor the leaves and soft stems droop.

Wilting is most likely on a hot, dry, windy day, or when the soil is dry.

### Practical: investigating transpiration rate

**Method 1: the potometer.** A leafy shoot is fitted, under water, into a tube connected to a capillary tube. As the shoot takes up water, an air bubble moves along the capillary. The distance moved per minute gives the rate of water uptake. Almost all water taken up is lost by transpiration, so this is used as a measure of transpiration rate.

To change one factor at a time:
- **Wind speed:** a fan at a fixed distance, on different settings, or off.
- **Light intensity:** a lamp at different distances, with a heat filter (a beaker of water) between lamp and shoot.
- **Temperature:** the whole apparatus in rooms or cabinets at different temperatures.

Keep the same shoot, let it settle after each change, and repeat each reading.

**Method 2: weighing.** Seal the pot and soil of a potted plant in a plastic bag so only the shoot loses water, and weigh it before and after a set time.

**Worked example: potometer calculations**

A student ran a potometer for 5 minutes in each condition. The capillary has a cross-sectional area of 0.5 mm².

| Condition | Bubble distance in 5 min / mm |
|---|---|
| Still air | 15 |
| Fan on | 40 |

*Rate in still air:* 15 ÷ 5 = **3.0 mm per minute**.
*Rate with fan:* 40 ÷ 5 = **8.0 mm per minute**.
*Volume taken up with fan:* volume = distance × cross-sectional area = 40 × 0.5 = **20 mm³** in 5 minutes, which is 20 ÷ 5 = **4.0 mm³ per minute**.
*Percentage increase from still air to fan:* (8.0 − 3.0) ÷ 3.0 × 100 = **167%** (to 3 significant figures).

*Explanation:* moving air removes water vapour from around the leaf, keeping the concentration gradient steep.

**Worked example: weighing method**

A sealed potted plant fell from 412.0 g to 398.5 g in 3 hours. Mass lost = 412.0 − 398.5 = **13.5 g**. Rate = 13.5 ÷ 3 = **4.5 g per hour**.

### How water moves up the xylem

The syllabus explains this as a **transpiration pull**:

1. Water evaporates from mesophyll cells and diffuses out of the stomata.
2. Mesophyll cells replace this water from the xylem in the leaf veins.
3. This removal of water from the top of the xylem creates a **pull** on the water below.
4. Water molecules are held together by **forces of attraction between water molecules** (sometimes called cohesion).
5. So the whole **column of water** in the xylem is drawn upwards, from the roots, through the stem, to the leaves, in an unbroken column.

**Worked example: explaining a result**

*Question:* A student removes all the leaves from a shoot fitted to a potometer. The bubble almost stops. Explain why.

*Answer:* With no leaves, there are no mesophyll surfaces to evaporate from and no stomata for water vapour to leave by. Transpiration almost stops, so there is no transpiration pull to draw the column of water up the xylem, and little water is taken up.

### Translocation

**Translocation is the movement of sucrose and amino acids in the phloem**, from parts of the plant that produce or release them (**sources**) to parts that use or store them (**sinks**).

| Source (produces or releases) | Sink (uses or stores) |
|---|---|
| Photosynthesising leaves make sucrose | Growing shoot tips and young leaves use it for growth and respiration |
| A storage organ, such as a potato tuber, breaking down its starch when new shoots grow | Roots use it for respiration |
| | Developing fruits and seeds, and storage organs while they fill, store it |

The same organ can be a sink at one time and a source at another. A potato tuber is a sink in summer, when it fills with stored starch, and a source in spring, when stored food is released to the new shoots. Because of this, phloem can carry substances up or down the plant. Xylem carries water only upwards.

**Worked example: sources and sinks**

*Question:* In early spring a tulip bulb sends up leaves before they can photosynthesise much. Identify the source and sink.

*Answer:* The bulb is the **source**: it releases stored food. The growing leaves and shoot are the **sink**: they use the sucrose for growth and respiration. Later the roles reverse.

### Xylem, phloem and cortex in transverse sections

You need to identify three tissues in sections of **non-woody dicotyledonous** roots and stems.

| | Root (transverse section) | Stem (transverse section) |
|---|---|---|
| Xylem | In the centre, often in a star or cross shape | In each vascular bundle, on the side towards the centre |
| Phloem | In patches between the arms of the xylem | In each vascular bundle, on the side towards the outside |
| Cortex | A wide band of cells between the outer surface and the central xylem and phloem | The band of cells between the outer surface and the ring of vascular bundles |

In the stem, the vascular bundles form a **ring** near the outside: phloem faces out, xylem faces in.

**Worked example: identifying a section**

*Question:* A section shows a central star-shaped tissue with patches of a second tissue between its arms, surrounded by a wide band of cells. Is it a root or a stem? Name the three tissues.

*Answer:* It is a **root**, because the vascular tissue is in the centre rather than in a ring of bundles. The star-shaped tissue is **xylem**, the patches between its arms are **phloem**, and the wide band is **cortex**.

### Xylem: functions and structure

**Functions of xylem:**
- transport of **water** and **mineral ions**, from the roots to the leaves
- **support** for the plant

**Structure related to function:**

| Feature of xylem vessels | Link to function |
|---|---|
| Thick walls containing **lignin** | Strong and rigid, so they support the plant and do not collapse as water is pulled up |
| **No cell contents** | Nothing blocks the flow of water |
| Cells joined **end-to-end with no cross walls**, forming a long continuous tube | Water moves in an unbroken column from root to leaf |

Details of how lignin is laid down are not required.

## Common errors

- Writing that transpiration is "loss of water". It is loss of **water vapour**, and from **leaves**.
- Saying water "evaporates out of the stomata". It evaporates from mesophyll cell surfaces into air spaces, then the vapour **diffuses** out through stomata.
- Explaining humidity backwards. Higher humidity **lowers** the rate.
- Saying light increases transpiration "because it is hotter". Link light to **stomata opening**.
- Saying xylem carries sucrose. Xylem carries water and mineral ions; phloem carries sucrose and amino acids.
- Calling the transpiration pull "suction from the roots". The pull comes from water loss at the leaves.
- Putting phloem on the inside of a stem vascular bundle.
