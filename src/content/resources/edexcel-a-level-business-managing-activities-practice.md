## Section A (30 marks)

> **Source: Bright Leaf Stationery**
>
> Bright Leaf Stationery is a UK-based sole trader business, founded
> three years ago, selling handmade greetings cards and stationery
> through a single shop and a growing website. The owner, wanting to
> open a second shop, is preparing a business plan to approach a bank
> for a loan and wants to retain full control of the business rather
> than bring in outside investors. Sales have grown steadily, but the
> owner has noticed that in some months the business has struggled to
> pay suppliers on time despite the year-end accounts showing a profit
> for the year.
>
> Questions 1–6 refer to Bright Leaf Stationery.

**1.** Define the term "internal source of finance", using one example relevant to Bright Leaf Stationery. **[2]**

**2.** Calculate the break-even point and the margin of safety for Bright Leaf Stationery's planned second shop, given fixed costs of $60,000, a selling price of $30 per unit and a variable cost of $18 per unit, if the shop is expected to sell 6,000 units. **[4]**

**3.** Construct a cash-flow forecast for the second shop's first three months, starting from an opening balance of $2,000, given cash inflows of $10,000, $12,000 and $15,000 and cash outflows of $9,000, $11,000 and $13,000, showing the closing balance for each month. **[4]**

**4.** Explain, using the source, one reason why Bright Leaf Stationery can show a profit for the year and still struggle to pay its suppliers on time. **[4]**

**5.** Analyse the difference between historical-figures-based budgeting and zero-based budgeting, and use your analysis to explain which would better help Bright Leaf Stationery identify the cash-flow problem described in the source. **[6]**

**6.** Assess the most suitable source of finance for Bright Leaf Stationery to open its second shop, given the owner's wish in the source to retain full control of the business. **[10]**

## Section B (30 marks)

> **Source: Kestrel Precision Ltd**
>
> Kestrel Precision Ltd is a UK manufacturer of precision-machined
> metal components, supplying the automotive industry. Over the past
> year the company has taken on several large new contracts and
> expanded its output rapidly, and inventory levels have risen sharply
> as a result. Kestrel relies heavily on a bank loan at a variable
> interest rate, at a time when interest rates are expected to rise
> further, and its finance director is now deciding whether to accept
> another large new contract.
>
> Questions 7–12 refer to Kestrel Precision Ltd.

**7.** Define the term "capacity utilisation". **[2]**

**8.** Explain, using the source, one way in which Kestrel Precision Ltd's rising inventory levels could affect its liquidity. **[4]**

**9.** Calculate the current ratio and the acid test ratio for Kestrel Precision Ltd, given current assets of $120,000, including $50,000 of inventory, and current liabilities of $60,000. **[4]**

**10.** Analyse the causes of a business such as Kestrel Precision Ltd failing as a result of overtrading. **[6]**

**11.** Analyse how the interest rate rise described in the source could affect Kestrel Precision Ltd. **[6]**

**12.** Discuss whether break-even analysis is a reliable tool for Kestrel Precision Ltd to use when deciding whether to accept the new large contract described in the source. **[8]**

## Section C (20 marks)

**13.** Using Kestrel Precision Ltd, evaluate the extent to which effective resource management — production methods, capacity utilisation, inventory control and quality management — determines a manufacturing business's ability to compete on cost and quality. **[20]**

## Answers

**1.** An internal source of finance is money raised **from within the business itself** [1], such as Bright Leaf Stationery's retained profit from its first shop, the owner's own capital, or the sale of unused assets [1].

**2.** Contribution per unit = 30 − 18 = **$12** [1]. Break-even point = 60,000 ÷ 12 = **5,000 units** [1]. Margin of safety at 6,000 units sold = 6,000 − 5,000 = **1,000 units** [1] [1] (method and correct figure).

**3.**

| Month | Opening balance | Inflow | Outflow | Net cash flow | Closing balance |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | $2,000 | $10,000 | $9,000 | +$1,000 | **$3,000** |
| 2 | $3,000 | $12,000 | $11,000 | +$1,000 | **$4,000** |
| 3 | $4,000 | $15,000 | $13,000 | +$2,000 | **$6,000** |

[1 mark for each correct closing balance, 1 mark for a correctly structured forecast — 4 marks total]

**4.** As the source describes, **customers paying late while suppliers require quick payment** [1] creates a **timing gap between cash received and cash owed** [1], so even a business that is profitable on paper, like Bright Leaf Stationery, can run short of cash to pay a supplier on time [1], which is a cash-flow problem rather than a profitability problem [1].

**5.** **Historical-figures-based budgeting** starts from the previous period's actual figures and adjusts them, which is quick but can **carry forward past inefficiencies or problems unnoticed** [1] [1]. **Zero-based budgeting** starts each period from nothing and requires **every cost to be justified afresh** [1] [1]. For Bright Leaf Stationery, zero-based budgeting would better surface the cash-flow problem the source describes, because it forces the owner to justify the timing of supplier payments each period rather than simply carrying forward the previous year's pattern, which is exactly what could be masking the problem [2].

**6.** Considering the options against the owner's stated wish to retain full control: **share capital or venture capital** would raise the funds but **dilute ownership and control**, which the source says the owner wants to avoid [1] [1]; a **bank loan or leasing** would preserve ownership but brings **interest payments and repayment obligations**, and leasing in particular avoids a large upfront outlay while spreading the cost of shop fittings over their useful life [1] [1]; **retained profit or owner's capital**, if enough is available, avoids interest, dilution and repayment altogether, but is only usable if the business already has sufficient accessible funds [1] [1]. **Recommendation:** retained profit is the most suitable if available, since it is an **internal source with no interest, no dilution of ownership and no loss of control**, directly meeting the owner's stated priority [1] [1]; if insufficient retained profit is available, a **bank loan** is the next best option, since — unlike share or venture capital — it does not dilute the owner's control, even though it does add fixed repayment obligations that the cash-flow problem in the source makes riskier than usual [1] [1].

**7.** Capacity utilisation measures a business's current output as a proportion of its maximum possible output [1], calculated as (current output ÷ maximum possible output) × 100 [1].

**8.** Kestrel's rising inventory levels tie up cash in unsold stock rather than in the bank [1] [1], which reduces the cash available to pay its current liabilities as they fall due [1], worsening its liquidity as measured by both the current ratio and, more directly, the acid test ratio, which excludes inventory from current assets altogether [1].

**9.** Current ratio = 120,000 ÷ 60,000 = **2.0** [1] [1] (method and figure). Acid test ratio = (120,000 − 50,000) ÷ 60,000 = 70,000 ÷ 60,000 = **1.17** [1] [1] (method and figure).

**10.** **Overtrading** happens when a business grows its sales faster than its cash flow can support [1], so it commits cash to fulfil larger orders — buying stock, paying staff, extending credit to new customers — before it has been paid for previous orders [1]. This is worsened by **poor working capital management**, such as holding too much cash in slow-moving inventory or allowing customers too long to pay [1], which leaves too little available cash to cover liabilities as they fall due [1]. The underlying cause is a **mismatch between the timing of cash inflows and outflows** rather than a lack of profit [1], which is why a business can be growing and profitable on paper — as Kestrel's new contracts suggest it is — while still failing from a cash shortage [1].

**11.** A rise in interest rates **increases the cost of servicing Kestrel's existing variable-rate bank loan**, since its repayments rise directly [1], reducing the cash available for other spending [1]. It also makes **any new borrowing more expensive**, so Kestrel may need to reconsider financing further growth through debt [1]. Higher rates tend to **reduce consumer spending** as mortgage and loan repayments rise for the consumers who ultimately buy the cars Kestrel's components go into [1], which can reduce demand for Kestrel's products at the same time as its own costs rise [1]. The combined effect is a **squeeze on both costs and revenue**, which is more severe the more heavily a business depends on debt rather than internal or equity finance, as the source suggests Kestrel does [1].

**12. Arguments that it is reliable:** break-even analysis gives Kestrel a **clear, calculable minimum sales target** it must hit on the new contract before it starts making a profit, based on known fixed and variable costs [1]; it allows Kestrel to test **different pricing scenarios** for the contract and see the effect on the break-even point before committing to it [1]; the **margin of safety** gives a concrete sense of how much room for error Kestrel has if the contract's sales fall short of the forecast [1]. **Arguments that it is unreliable:** it assumes **all output is sold** and that costs and selling price stay constant, which rarely holds exactly in practice, especially for a new contract with no sales history [1] [1]; it depends entirely on the accuracy of the **sales forecast** feeding into it, and a new large contract is exactly the case where demand and costs are hardest to predict [1] [1]; it says nothing about **cash-flow timing** — the contract could be forecast to break even over a year while still causing the kind of overtrading crisis the source already shows Kestrel is at risk of in its early months [1].

**13.** This question is marked against a **levels-based scheme**, not discrete points, matching the real paper's extended-writing questions:

- **Level 1 (1–5 marks):** isolated or generic points about production, capacity, inventory or quality, with little or no application to Kestrel Precision Ltd.
- **Level 2 (6–10 marks):** relevant knowledge of resource management is applied to Kestrel's context, but analysis of *how* it affects competitiveness is one-sided or incomplete.
- **Level 3 (11–15 marks):** developed analysis of how resource management affects **both** cost (productivity, capacity utilisation, waste) **and** quality (quality assurance, TQM, Kaizen) at a business such as Kestrel, with clear application throughout.
- **Level 4 (16–20 marks):** a sustained, well-evidenced argument reaches a supported judgement on the *extent* to which resource management determines Kestrel's competitiveness relative to other factors (such as marketing, finance or the external environment), rather than treating it as the only determinant.

**Indicative content:** efficient production methods and high capacity utilisation lower Kestrel's average cost, supporting competitive pricing for its automotive customers; just-in-time and lean production would reduce the waste and tied-up cash that its rising inventory levels, described in the source, already show is a problem; quality assurance, TQM and Kaizen reduce defects and returns, supporting a reputation for reliability that matters as much as price to automotive customers with exacting tolerances. Against this, resource management alone cannot compensate for the financing pressure the source also describes — a rise in interest rates squeezing a business that depends heavily on debt — so a balanced judgement should note that resource management is a significant but not sole determinant of Kestrel's competitiveness, and that its overtrading and interest-rate exposure could undermine even excellent resource management if left unaddressed.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Dividing fixed costs by selling price instead of contribution per unit when calculating break-even.
- Confusing the current ratio with the acid test ratio, or forgetting to subtract inventory for the acid test.
- Recommending a source of finance without matching it to the scenario's stated priorities (such as retaining control).
- Blending internal and external causes of business failure into one undifferentiated list.
- Treating break-even analysis as fully reliable without questioning the sales forecast it depends on.
- Describing resource management tools without linking them to cost or quality, or reaching a judgement on extent.
- On Section A and Section B, writing an answer that could apply to any business rather than referring explicitly to the named company and figures each source gives.

## Approaching managing business activities questions

Treat every calculation question as a two-step process: first identify and state the correct formula, then substitute the given figures and show the working explicitly, since Edexcel's mark scheme awards method marks for a correctly identified formula even when a later arithmetic step goes wrong. For scenario-based questions on sources of finance, causes of failure, or external influences, always tie the answer back to the specific details given in the source — the business's size, its need for control, or the timing of its cash-flow problem — rather than stating textbook definitions in isolation, since application marks on this unit are earned through that specific link, not general knowledge. For the twenty-mark evaluation, spend proportionate time planning both sides of the argument before writing, since a supported judgement on *extent* is what separates a Level 4 answer from a well-informed but one-sided Level 2 or 3 answer.
