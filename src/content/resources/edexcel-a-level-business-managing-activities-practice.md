## Part One — Short-answer and calculation questions

**1.** Define the term "internal source of finance", using one example. **[2]**

**2.** Calculate the break-even point and the margin of safety for a business with fixed costs of $60,000, a selling price of $30 per unit and a variable cost of $18 per unit, given that it sells 6,000 units. **[4]**

## Part Two — Structured and extended-writing questions

**3.** Construct a cash-flow forecast for a business's first three months, starting from an opening balance of $2,000, given cash inflows of $10,000, $12,000 and $15,000 and cash outflows of $9,000, $11,000 and $13,000, showing the closing balance for each month. **[4]**

**4.** Explain **one** reason why a profitable business can still fail due to cash-flow problems. **[4]**

**5.** Explain the difference between historical-figures-based budgeting and zero-based budgeting. **[4]**

**6.** Calculate the current ratio and the acid test ratio for a business with current assets of $120,000, including $50,000 of inventory, and current liabilities of $60,000. **[4]**

**7.** Analyse the causes of a business failing as a result of overtrading. **[6]**

**8.** Analyse how a rise in interest rates could affect a business that relies heavily on bank loans. **[6]**

**9.** Discuss whether break-even analysis is a reliable tool for a new business deciding whether to launch a product. **[8]**

**10.** Assess the most suitable source of finance for a small, family-owned business that wants to raise $20,000 to buy new equipment without giving up any control or ownership. **[10]**

**11.** Evaluate the extent to which effective resource management -- production methods, capacity utilisation, inventory control and quality management -- determines a manufacturing business's ability to compete on cost and quality. **[20]**

## Answers

**1.** An internal source of finance is money raised **from within the business itself** [1], such as retained profit, owner's capital, or the sale of unused assets [1].

**2.** Contribution per unit = 30 − 18 = **$12** [1]. Break-even point = 60,000 ÷ 12 = **5,000 units** [1]. Margin of safety at 6,000 units sold = 6,000 − 5,000 = **1,000 units** [1] [1] (method and correct figure).

**3.**

| Month | Opening balance | Inflow | Outflow | Net cash flow | Closing balance |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | $2,000 | $10,000 | $9,000 | +$1,000 | **$3,000** |
| 2 | $3,000 | $12,000 | $11,000 | +$1,000 | **$4,000** |
| 3 | $4,000 | $15,000 | $13,000 | +$2,000 | **$6,000** |

[1 mark for each correct closing balance, 1 mark for a correctly structured forecast — 4 marks total]

**4.** Any one reason, developed, e.g. **customers paying late while suppliers require quick payment** [1] creates a **timing gap between cash received and cash owed** [1], so even a business that is profitable on paper can run short of cash to pay a supplier or wage bill on time [1], which can force it to seek emergency short-term borrowing or, in the worst case, become insolvent despite trading profitably [1].

**5.** **Historical-figures-based budgeting** starts from the previous period's actual figures and adjusts them, which is quick but can **carry forward past inefficiencies** [1] [1]. **Zero-based budgeting** starts each period from nothing and requires **every cost to be justified afresh**, which takes longer but can **eliminate unnecessary spending** that a historical approach would simply repeat [1] [1].

**6.** Current ratio = 120,000 ÷ 60,000 = **2.0** [1] [1] (method and figure). Acid test ratio = (120,000 − 50,000) ÷ 60,000 = 70,000 ÷ 60,000 = **1.17** [1] [1] (method and figure).

**7.** **Overtrading** happens when a business grows its sales faster than its cash flow can support [1], so it commits cash to fulfil larger orders — buying stock, paying staff, extending credit to new customers — before it has been paid for previous orders [1]. This is worsened by **poor working capital management**, such as holding too much cash in slow-moving inventory or allowing customers too long to pay [1], which leaves too little available cash to cover liabilities as they fall due [1]. The underlying cause is a **mismatch between the timing of cash inflows and outflows** rather than a lack of profit [1], which is why a business can be growing and profitable on paper while still failing from a cash shortage [1].

**8.** A rise in interest rates **increases the cost of servicing existing bank loans**, since variable-rate repayments rise directly [1], reducing the cash available for other spending [1]. It also makes **new borrowing more expensive**, so the business may delay or cancel planned investment funded by debt [1]. Higher rates tend to **reduce consumer spending** as mortgage and loan repayments rise for the business's own customers [1], which can reduce demand for the business's products at the same time as its own costs rise [1]. The combined effect is a **squeeze on both costs and revenue**, which is more severe the more heavily the business depends on debt rather than internal or equity finance [1].

**9. Arguments that it is reliable:** break-even analysis gives a **clear, calculable minimum sales target** the business must hit before it starts making a profit, based on known fixed and variable costs [1]; it allows the business to test **different pricing scenarios** and see the effect on the break-even point before committing to a launch [1]; the **margin of safety** gives a concrete sense of how much room for error the business has if sales fall short of the forecast [1]. **Arguments that it is unreliable:** it assumes **all output is sold** and that costs and selling price stay constant, which rarely holds exactly in practice, especially for a genuinely new product with no sales history [1] [1]; it depends entirely on the accuracy of the **sales forecast** feeding into it, and new products are exactly the case where consumer trends, economic variables and competitor actions are hardest to predict [1] [1]; it says nothing about **cash-flow timing** — a product could be forecast to break even over a year while still causing a cash-flow crisis in its early months [1].

**10.** Considering the options: **share capital or venture capital** would raise the funds but **dilute ownership and control**, which the business has explicitly ruled out [1] [1]; a **bank loan or leasing** would preserve ownership but brings **interest payments and repayment obligations**, and leasing in particular avoids a large upfront outlay while spreading the cost of the equipment over its useful life [1] [1]; **retained profit or owner's capital**, if available in sufficient amount, avoids interest, dilution and repayment altogether, but is only usable if the business already has $20,000 in accessible funds [1] [1]. **Recommendation:** retained profit (or owner's capital) is the most suitable if available, since it is an **internal source with no interest, no dilution of ownership and no loss of control**, directly meeting the business's stated priorities [1] [1]; if insufficient funds are available internally, **leasing** is the next best option, since it avoids diluting ownership while spreading the cost of the equipment rather than requiring the full $20,000 upfront [1] [1].

**11.** This question is marked against a **levels-based scheme**, not discrete points, matching the real paper's extended-writing questions:

- **Level 1 (1–5 marks):** isolated or generic points about production, capacity, inventory or quality, with little or no application to a manufacturing business.
- **Level 2 (6–10 marks):** relevant knowledge of resource management is applied to a manufacturing context, but analysis of *how* it affects competitiveness is one-sided or incomplete.
- **Level 3 (11–15 marks):** developed analysis of how resource management affects **both** cost (productivity, capacity utilisation, waste) **and** quality (quality assurance, TQM, Kaizen), with clear application throughout.
- **Level 4 (16–20 marks):** a sustained, well-evidenced argument reaches a supported judgement on the *extent* to which resource management determines competitiveness relative to other factors (such as marketing, finance or the external environment), rather than treating it as the only determinant.

**Indicative content:** efficient production methods and high capacity utilisation lower average cost, supporting competitive pricing; just-in-time and lean production reduce waste and tied-up cash; quality assurance, TQM and Kaizen reduce defects and returns, supporting a reputation for reliability, which can matter as much as price in many markets. Against this, resource management alone cannot compensate for weak marketing, an unsuitable price, or an unfavourable external environment (e.g. a recession or new competition), so a balanced judgement should note that resource management is a significant but not sole determinant of competitiveness.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Dividing fixed costs by selling price instead of contribution per unit when calculating break-even.
- Confusing the current ratio with the acid test ratio, or forgetting to subtract inventory for the acid test.
- Recommending a source of finance without matching it to the scenario's stated priorities (such as retaining control).
- Blending internal and external causes of business failure into one undifferentiated list.
- Treating break-even analysis as fully reliable without questioning the sales forecast it depends on.
- Describing resource management tools without linking them to cost or quality, or reaching a judgement on extent.

## Approaching managing business activities questions

Treat every calculation question as a two-step process: first identify and state the correct formula, then substitute the given figures and show the working explicitly, since Edexcel's mark scheme awards method marks for a correctly identified formula even when a later arithmetic step goes wrong. For scenario-based questions on sources of finance, causes of failure, or external influences, always tie the answer back to the specific details given in the question — the business's size, its need for control, or the timing of its cash-flow problem — rather than stating textbook definitions in isolation, since application marks on this unit are earned through that specific link, not general knowledge. For the twenty-mark evaluation, spend proportionate time planning both sides of the argument before writing, since a supported judgement on *extent* is what separates a Level 4 answer from a well-informed but one-sided Level 2 or 3 answer.
