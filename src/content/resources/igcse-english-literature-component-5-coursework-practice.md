Component 5 of Cambridge IGCSE Literature in English (0475) has no question paper: you submit two
assignments of 600–1200 words on two different texts, each marked out of 25 against the eight-level
Component 5 criteria. These practice tasks therefore do two jobs. Section A checks that you know the
rules that can cost marks before a word is written. Section B gives full coursework titles on
out-of-copyright texts, each with indicative content mapped to AO1–AO4 and a note on what separates the
levels, so you can draft, self-assess and redraft.

## Section A: Rules and task-setting

**1.** State how many assignments the portfolio contains, the word guidance for each, and whether
quotations count towards that guidance. **[3]**

**2.** A student plans one assignment on a Paper 1 set novel and the other on a Paper 3 set play.
Explain whether this is allowed. **[2]**

**3.** A student wants to write an empathic response in the voice of the speaker of a poem. Explain
whether this is allowed and suggest an alternative. **[2]**

**4.** A student submits an assignment that analyses one short story only. Explain the problem and how
to fix it. **[2]**

**5.** Rewrite each title so that it allows all four assessment objectives to be assessed. **[6]**
(a) "The witches in *Macbeth*"
(b) "Describe what happens in chapter 1 of *Great Expectations*"
(c) "Christina Rossetti's poems"

**6.** A student uses a website that underlines weak sentences and suggests replacement words. Explain
why this breaks the coursework rules. **[2]**

## Section B: Coursework titles (each marked out of 25)

**7. Critical assignment (drama).** How does Shakespeare use the supernatural to shape the audience's
judgement of Macbeth? Refer to the whole play. **[25]**

**8. Critical assignment (prose).** Explore how Dickens presents Pip's sense of shame in *Great
Expectations*. **[25]**

**9. Critical assignment (poetry).** How do two poems by Christina Rossetti that you have studied
present longing? You need not compare them. **[25]**

**10. Empathic assignment (prose).** You are Elizabeth Bennet in *Pride and Prejudice*, on the evening
after you have read Mr Darcy's letter. Write your thoughts. **[25]**

## Answers

**1.** Two assignments [1], each 600–1200 words [1]. Quotations **do** count; references and the
bibliography do not [1].

**2.** Not allowed. The syllabus permits **one** of the two assignments to be on a text set for Papers
1, 2 and 3 [1]; the other must be on a different text of equivalent scope and demand, originally
written in English [1].

**3.** Not allowed: the empathic option is only for a prose or drama text [1]. The student could write
a critical assignment on two or more poems, or an empathic piece on a novel or play [1].

**4.** Where short stories (or poems) are used, the assignment must cover at least **two** stories [1].
Add a second story; the two do not need to be compared [1].

**5.** Award 2 marks per title: 1 for turning it into a question, 1 for focusing on the writer's
methods. Model answers:
(a) "How does Shakespeare use the witches to create uncertainty about Macbeth's responsibility for his
actions?"
(b) "How does Dickens make the opening of *Great Expectations* both frightening and comic?" (the
assignment must still show study of the whole novel)
(c) "How does Rossetti present the conflict between earthly love and religious faith in two poems you
have studied?"

**6.** The syllabus forbids online tools that act as writing assistants [1]: tools that identify
specific sections where a correction should be made and propose the words to use instead [1].

### Question 7: indicative content

| AO | What a strong answer does |
|---|---|
| AO1 | Ranges across the play: the witches' first meeting, the prophecies in 1.3, the dagger soliloquy, Banquo's ghost, the apparitions in 4.1, and Macbeth's final reliance on the prophecies |
| AO2 | Argues a position on responsibility: the prophecies tempt but do not compel; Banquo hears similar words and does not act; Macbeth chooses to visit the witches in Act 4 |
| AO3 | Analyses methods: the trochaic, rhyming chant that separates the witches' speech from the blank verse of the court; equivocation ("Fair is foul, and foul is fair"); dramatic irony in the apparitions; the dagger as a staged or imagined object |
| AO4 | Reaches a personal, evaluated judgement, for example that the supernatural exposes ambition rather than creating it, and sustains it to the conclusion |

Context (AO2) can be used briefly where it explains a choice: for example, early modern beliefs about
witchcraft help explain why an audience might fear the witches, but the paragraph must return to the
play.

**Level guidance.** A Level 4 answer (11–13) tells the story of the prophecies with some quotation. A
Level 6 answer (17–19) explains how the witches' language works and shows "some of its deeper
implications". Level 8 (23–25) needs an individual argument held across the essay, with references
integrated "skilfully and with flair".

**Model paragraph (Level 7–8 quality).** "The witches never tell Macbeth to kill; they only name what he
will become. Shakespeare makes this gap the centre of the play's moral judgement, because Macbeth's
startled reaction to 'king hereafter' shows the thought was already present. Banquo, hearing a
prophecy of equal weight, warns that 'instruments of darkness tell us truths' to win us to harm, and
the contrast leaves the audience unable to blame the supernatural alone."

### Question 8: indicative content

| AO | What a strong answer does |
|---|---|
| AO1 | Refers to the Satis House visits and Estella's contempt for his "coarse hands", Pip's embarrassment at Joe's visit to London, and his later recognition of Magwitch as his benefactor |
| AO2 | Traces the development of shame from class-consciousness to moral self-knowledge; links shame to Victorian ideas of the "gentleman" where this clarifies Dickens's criticism |
| AO3 | Analyses the double perspective of the older narrator judging his younger self; irony; the symbolism of hands and of the forge versus Satis House; the structure of the three "stages" |
| AO4 | Evaluates whether Pip earns the reader's sympathy, and supports a clear personal view |

**Level guidance.** The key discriminator is AO3: answers at Level 5 "make some response to the way
the writer uses language", while Level 7 responds "sensitively and in detail" to how Dickens achieves
his effects, especially through the retrospective narrator's irony.

### Question 9: indicative content

| AO | What a strong answer does |
|---|---|
| AO1 | Covers at least two poems (for example "Remember" and "Song: When I am dead, my dearest"), with brief, accurate quotation |
| AO2 | Shows how longing is directed at a lover, at release from earthly feeling, or at God, and how the poems question whether longing should be indulged |
| AO3 | Analyses form (the Petrarchan sonnet's volta in "Remember"; the song's regular stanzas), repetition, imperative voice and the imagery of memory and forgetting |
| AO4 | Offers a personal reading, for example that the speakers find a kind of freedom in renouncing longing |

**Level guidance.** Comparison is not required, so do not lose words to forced links. An answer that
discusses only one poem cannot meet the rule for poetry assignments, whatever its quality.

### Question 10: indicative content (empathic)

| AO (empathic wording) | What a strong answer does |
|---|---|
| AO1 | Weaves in accurate detail from the letter and earlier events: Wickham's story, Darcy's remarks at the Meryton ball, Jane and Bingley, the Hunsford proposal |
| AO2 | Shows Elizabeth's realisation that her prejudice has misled her, matching the novel's turning point |
| AO3 | Sustains a convincing voice: articulate, self-critical, witty even in distress, in a register close to Austen's |
| AO4 | Selects and shapes the material so that the piece has its own direction, ending at the edge of a changed judgement |

**Level guidance.** Level 8 "sustains an entirely convincing voice" and "seamlessly uses well-selected
textual detail". A piece that slips into modern slang, or that summarises the letter instead of
reacting to it, stays in the lower levels however long it is.

## A note on technique

Write more than two assignments during the course: the syllabus advises this so that you and your
teacher can choose the strongest two. Plan each with a one-sentence argument, check every quotation
against the text, read the piece aloud, and keep to 600–1200 words.

## Frequently asked questions

**How long should I spend drafting?**
The syllabus sets no time; assignments can be completed at any point in the course. What matters is that
plans and drafts are your own, since teachers can give only brief summative comments.

**Can the empathic task be my stronger piece?**
Yes. One assignment may be empathic, and it is marked against the same eight levels, with the AO1 and AO3
descriptors adapted to voice and textual detail.

**Should I compare texts within an assignment?**
Not unless your title asks for it. Each assignment is on one text, and poetry or story assignments need
two or more pieces but no comparison.
