This guide covers the Technology content in the area of study Productive efficiency, part of the Operational strategy topic of the OCR A Level Business (H431) specification, Version 2.1 (January 2026), final assessment summer 2027. It is numbered 4.12 on this site; the specification does not number its content headings. The [operational strategy study guide](/resources/ocr-a-level-business-operational-strategy/) gives the short version. This page goes further: it sets out how technology is applied at each stage of operations, costs an automation decision and weighs the effect on each stakeholder group. All businesses and figures are invented.

Use it with the [technology revision notes](/resources/ocr-a-level-business-h431-technology-revision-notes/) and the [technology practice questions](/resources/ocr-a-level-business-h431-technology-practice/). The course hub is [OCR A Level Business](/boards/ocr/a-level/business/).

## How technology is applied

The specification does not list particular technologies, so you may use any that fit the case. These are the most common.

**Design.** Computer-aided design (CAD) lets designers draw and test products on screen, change a measurement in seconds and share drawings with suppliers. It shortens development time and reduces the cost of building physical prototypes. Three-dimensional printing can produce a prototype or a small batch from a digital design without special tooling.

**Production.** Computer-aided manufacture (CAM) uses a computer to control machines directly from the design file. **Automation** and **robots** carry out repetitive or dangerous tasks quickly, accurately and without breaks. Benefits are higher output, consistent quality and less waste. Drawbacks are the high cost of buying and maintaining the equipment and the loss of flexibility, because machines have to be reprogrammed to make something different.

**Stock and supply.** Barcode scanners, radio-frequency tags and stock-management software record stock in real time, trigger reorders and support just-in-time systems. Electronic links to suppliers reduce errors and delays.

**Quality.** Sensors and cameras can inspect every item on a line, and data from machines can warn of faults before a breakdown. This supports quality control and quality assurance.

**Selling and service.** E-commerce lets a business sell through a website, take payments online and track orders. Customer relationship software records customers' preferences and complaints. Automated chat and booking systems provide service outside normal hours.

**Management information.** Data from sales and machines helps the business to forecast demand, plan capacity and spot waste. Technology can also support flexible working and communication between sites.

In a service business the same ideas apply differently: online booking, self-service tills, mobile apps for staff and digital records, rather than robots on a line.

## Benefits and costs

| Benefit | Explanation |
|---|---|
| Higher productivity | More output per worker or per hour |
| Lower unit cost | Less labour and waste per unit once the equipment is paid for |
| Consistent quality | Machines repeat tasks to the same standard |
| Speed | Quicker design, production and delivery |
| Flexibility in some cases | Computer control can switch between product designs faster than manual set-up |
| Better information | Real-time data for decisions |

| Cost or risk | Explanation |
|---|---|
| Capital cost | Equipment and software are expensive to buy and install |
| Running cost | Maintenance, energy, licences and updates |
| Training | Staff need new skills |
| Job losses | Fewer workers needed for routine tasks |
| System failure | Breakdowns, software faults or cyber-attacks can halt operations |
| Inflexibility | Specialised equipment may suit only one product |
| Obsolescence | Equipment can be out of date within a few years |

## Worked example: should Barrow Metalworks buy a robot?

Barrow Metalworks (invented) makes 120,000 metal parts a year with 10 production workers. Each worker costs £28,000 a year plus 20% on-costs, a total of £33,600. At present 5% of parts are scrapped, and each part's materials cost £4.

A welding robot costs £240,000 and is expected to last 8 years. It would replace 4 of the 10 workers, so 6 would remain. Maintenance and software would cost £30,000 a year. The scrap rate would fall to 2%. Redundancy costs for the four workers would be £9,000 each.

**Productivity.** Output per worker rises from 120,000 ÷ 10 = 12,000 parts to 120,000 ÷ 6 = 20,000 parts. This is an increase of (20,000 − 12,000) ÷ 12,000 × 100 = **66.7%**.

**Labour saving.** 4 × £33,600 = £134,400 a year.

**Scrap saving.** Before: 5% × 120,000 = 6,000 parts. After: 2% × 120,000 = 2,400 parts. Fewer scrapped parts = 3,600 × £4 = £14,400 a year.

**Annual cash benefit** = £134,400 + £14,400 − £30,000 maintenance = **£118,800**.

**Payback.** Without redundancy costs: £240,000 ÷ £118,800 = 2.0 years. With redundancy costs of 4 × £9,000 = £36,000: (£240,000 + £36,000) ÷ £118,800 = **2.3 years**.

**Unit cost (labour, scrap and robot only).**

- Before: (10 × £33,600 + 6,000 × £4) ÷ 120,000 = (£336,000 + £24,000) ÷ 120,000 = **£3.00** a part.
- After: (6 × £33,600 + 2,400 × £4 + £30,000 maintenance + £30,000 depreciation) ÷ 120,000 = (£201,600 + £9,600 + £60,000) ÷ 120,000 = **£2.26** a part. Depreciation of £30,000 is the £240,000 cost spread over 8 years.

**Evaluation.**

- A payback of about two to three years on an eight-year machine is attractive, if demand stays at 120,000 parts.
- The result depends on assumptions: if demand fell by a third, the same machine would be underused and the saving would be smaller.
- There is a risk of breakdown. With only six workers left and an automated line, a fault could stop most output.
- Redundancy damages morale and the remaining staff may be anxious. Training to run and maintain the robot is a cost not shown in the figures.
- Customers may gain from more consistent quality and lower prices, but only if the business passes on some of the saving.

**Judgement.** Barrow should buy the robot if it has at least three years of confirmed orders and can finance £276,000 without cash-flow problems, and if it retrains some of the four workers rather than making them redundant. If orders are uncertain, leasing the robot or phasing in the change would reduce the risk.

## A smaller decision: the 3D printer

Barrow also considers a 3D printer for prototypes. It costs £36,000 and would replace outside prototype fees of £15,000 a year.

Payback = £36,000 ÷ £15,000 = **2.4 years**. The annual saving is 15,000 ÷ 36,000 × 100 = 41.7% of the cost, before maintenance and materials.

This case is weaker than the robot on its own figures, because the saving is only the fees avoided and it depends on how many prototypes Barrow needs. Non-financial benefits count too: designers can test an idea in a day instead of a fortnight, so new products reach customers sooner, and designs stay private rather than being shared with an outside firm. A business should weigh these benefits against the payback, and ask whether the printer would be busy enough to justify its cost.

## Stakeholders

| Stakeholder | Possible positive impact | Possible negative impact |
|---|---|---|
| Owners | Lower costs, higher profit, competitive edge | Large investment; risk of failure |
| Employees | Safer and less repetitive work; higher-skilled jobs | Redundancy; need to retrain; stress |
| Customers | Consistent quality, faster delivery, online ordering | Less personal service; data security worries |
| Suppliers | Electronic ordering; larger and steadier orders | Pressure to meet system standards |
| Community | Higher-skilled employment; less waste | Fewer local jobs; energy use |
| Government | More tax if the business grows | Cost of unemployment support |

## Evaluating the impact and importance of technology

For a long answer consider the following.

1. **Is technology essential or optional in this industry?** In some sectors, such as online retail, rivals set the standard and a business that does not invest loses customers. In others, it is a choice.
2. **Size of business.** Small firms may find the capital cost too high but can use cheaper tools such as cloud software and online selling.
3. **Cost against benefit.** Use payback or unit cost, and say what the figures assume.
4. **Dependence and risk.** Failure of one system can stop the business. Back-up and security need spending.
5. **People.** Training, retraining and consultation affect whether technology delivers its benefits.
6. **Competitive position.** Technology may give an advantage only until rivals buy the same equipment.
7. **Judgement.** Which technology, in what order and under what conditions.

## Common mistakes

- Listing technologies with no link to operations such as design, production, stock, quality or selling.
- Assuming that technology always reduces cost, without considering capital and running costs.
- Writing only about job losses and ignoring benefits for employees such as safer work.
- Ignoring system failure and data security.
- Treating the same technology as suitable for every business whatever its size.

## Exam technique

For "explain how", name the technology, the operations stage where it is used and the result. For "evaluate", weigh benefits against costs for a stated stakeholder, give a judgement and say what it depends on. If figures are given, calculate payback or unit cost and interpret them.
