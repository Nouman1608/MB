## The industrial system

- **Inputs**: raw materials or components, energy, labour, capital,
  land, research.
- **Processes**: refining, smelting, assembling, testing, packaging.
- **Outputs**: products, by-products, **waste** (gases, heat, waste
  water, scrap), profit.
- **Feedback**: profit reinvested.
- Waste links 3.3 to 3.7 (pollution).

## Types of industry

| Type | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Processing | Raw material made more useful | Oil refining, sugar refining, smelting, flour milling |
| Manufacturing | Making goods from materials | Steel products, textiles, furniture |
| Assembly | Components put together | Cars, televisions, mobile phones |
| High technology | Research-based advanced products | Microchips, software, pharmaceuticals, aerospace |

## Seven location factors: what to say

| Factor | Key idea |
|---|---|
| Land | Flat, cheap, room to expand; greenfield v brownfield |
| Labour | Cheap and plentiful (assembly, clothing) or highly skilled (high tech) |
| Raw materials | Heavy, bulky, weight-losing or perishable → locate at source |
| Fuel and power | Once coalfields; now national grid → footloose; aluminium seeks cheap HEP |
| Transport | Ports for imports (break of bulk), motorways, rail, airports for light high-value goods |
| Markets | Weight-gaining, fragile, perishable or customer-facing → near market |
| Political | Grants, tax breaks, export processing zones, special economic zones; trade barriers |

Plus **agglomeration**: firms cluster near suppliers, skilled workers
and related firms.

## Matching factor to industry

- Iron and steel, sugar refining → raw materials.
- Soft-drink bottling, bakery → market.
- Clothing, electronics assembly → cheap labour.
- Microchips, software → skilled labour, universities, capital.
- Aluminium smelting → cheap electricity.

## Case study outline: Silicon Valley, California (USA)

- Southern San Francisco Bay Area: Palo Alto, Mountain View,
  Cupertino, Santa Clara, San Jose.
- High technology: Intel (Santa Clara), Apple (Cupertino), Google
  (Mountain View); Hewlett-Packard founded in Palo Alto in 1939.
- Factors: Stanford University (graduates and research); venture
  capital; agglomeration; airports at San Francisco and San Jose;
  pleasant climate attracts workers.
- Problems: very high housing costs, congestion, inequality.
- Alternative: Bengaluru, India (Infosys and Wipro headquarters,
  Electronic City, Indian Institute of Science).

## Pakistan examples of location factors

- **Textiles, Faisalabad**: close to the cotton-growing districts of Punjab (raw material), a large
  supply of semi-skilled labour, a long-established cluster of spinning, weaving and finishing
  firms (agglomeration), and road and rail links to Karachi for export.
- **Steel at Port Qasim, Karachi**: Pakistan Steel Mills was built on the coast because much of
  its iron ore and coking coal was imported; a coastal site at a **break-of-bulk point** avoids
  moving heavy, bulky materials inland. (The plant has not produced steel since 2015, but its
  location is still a clear example of this factor.)
- **Export processing zones**, such as the one at Karachi, offer tax and customs advantages to
  firms that make goods for export, showing the **political** factor at work.

## How location factors have changed

- **Then**: heavy industry tied to coalfields and raw materials, because transport was slow and
  costly.
- **Now**: electricity from a national grid, cheaper bulk shipping and lorries make many firms
  **footloose**; access to markets, skilled labour and motorways matters more.
- **Globally**: assembly and clothing have shifted to countries with lower labour costs, while
  research and design often stay in high-income countries.

## Worked example: choosing a site

*A map shows two possible sites for a new electronics assembly factory. Choose one and justify
your choice.* Model answer: "Site X is better. It is beside the motorway junction, so components
can arrive quickly and finished goods can reach the airport and markets. It is on flat land on
the edge of the town, which is cheaper and leaves room to expand. A large town nearby gives a
supply of labour. Site Y is in the town centre, where land is expensive, roads are congested and
there is no space for car parking or expansion."

Choose clearly, use **map evidence**, link each factor to the needs of that industry, and say why
the other site is worse.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Footloose industry | Industry not tied to raw materials or power, free to locate widely |
| Greenfield site | Undeveloped land, often on the edge of a town |
| Brownfield site | Previously developed land, often old industrial land |
| Agglomeration | Clustering of related firms that benefit from being close |
| Break-of-bulk point | Place where goods are transferred between transport modes, such as a port |
| Export processing zone | Area offering tax and customs advantages to firms that make goods for export |
| Science park | Planned site for high-technology firms, often linked to a university |
| Weight-losing industry | Industry whose product is lighter than its raw materials, e.g. steel |
| Weight-gaining industry | Industry whose product is heavier or bulkier than its inputs, e.g. bottled drinks |
| Industrial zone | An area planned or used mainly for factories and related firms |
| Venture capital | Money invested in new, risky businesses in return for a share of profits |

## Exam traps

- Forgetting waste as an output of the industrial system.
- "Near raw materials" given for every industry; match the factor to the industry type.
- Describing a factor without explaining how it lowers costs or raises profits.
- Ignoring **political** factors such as grants, tax breaks and export processing zones.
- A case study with no named firms, towns or institutions.

## Self-test

1. What output of an industrial system is often forgotten?
2. Classify: (a) a car plant using parts from many suppliers, (b) an
   oil refinery.
3. Why do iron and steel works often locate near raw materials?
4. What is a footloose industry?
5. Give two reasons for the growth of Silicon Valley.
6. Why might a government create an export processing zone?
7. Why is Faisalabad a good location for textile manufacturing?
8. Explain why Pakistan Steel Mills was built on the coast.
9. Why have many industries become footloose?
10. Give one disadvantage of a city-centre site for a new factory.
11. Classify a soft-drink bottling plant as weight-losing or weight-gaining, and say where it is
    likely to locate.

**Answers:** 1. Waste. 2. (a) Assembly. (b) Processing. 3. The raw
materials are heavy and bulky and lose weight in processing, so
locating near them cuts transport costs. 4. An industry that can
locate almost anywhere because it is not tied to raw materials or a
power source. 5. Any two: Stanford University providing graduates and
research; venture capital; clustering of related firms; good airports;
attractive climate. 6. To attract foreign investment, creating jobs
and export earnings. 7. Any two: nearby cotton-growing areas supply raw material; plentiful
labour; a cluster of related textile firms; transport links to Karachi for export. 8. Much of its
iron ore and coal was imported by sea, so a coastal break-of-bulk site avoids the cost of moving
heavy, bulky materials inland. 9. Electricity from a national grid and cheaper transport mean they
are no longer tied to raw materials or a power source. 10. Any one: expensive land; congestion; no
space to expand; nearby housing objects to noise. 11. Weight-gaining (water is added); near the
market to cut the cost of transporting heavy bottles.
