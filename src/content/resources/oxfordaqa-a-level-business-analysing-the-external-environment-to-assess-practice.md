This set tests section 3.3.4, Analysing the external environment to assess opportunities and threats, from the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Business (9725) specification, Version 1.1 (International AS exams from May/June 2027, International A-level exams from May/June 2028). Every business here is invented, and the exchange rates are illustrative.

## Questions

**1.** Identify **two** areas, other than competition, in which the law within a country can affect a business. **[2]**

**2.** Explain **one** limitation of PESTLE analysis for a business planning its strategy. **[3]**

**3.** Saltreach Furnishings imports sofas from Italy at €900 each. The exchange rate moves from £1 = €1.25 to £1 = €1.20. Calculate the percentage increase in the cost of one sofa in pounds. **[4]**

**4.** Pellinore Optics sells a lens to Japanese buyers for £250. The exchange rate moves from £1 = ¥190 to £1 = ¥180.

**(a)** Calculate the yen price before and after the change if Pellinore keeps its pound price. **[2]**
**(b)** Pellinore could instead hold the yen price at ¥47,500. Calculate its revenue per lens in pounds and explain **one** reason it might choose this option. **[3]**

**5.** The price index for a manufacturer's main raw material rose from 118.0 to 129.8 in a year. The manufacturer raised its selling prices by 6%. Calculate the percentage increase in raw material prices and comment on what it means for the manufacturer. **[3]**

**6.** Wendlebury Brewing has £2,600,000 outstanding on a variable-rate loan. Each year it repays £400,000 of the loan plus interest on the opening balance. The interest rate rises from 4.5% to 6%. Calculate the percentage increase in this year's total payment. **[4]**

**7.** Dunmarrow Ceramics exports tableware. Its largest export market introduces an import quota on ceramics. Analyse how this could affect Dunmarrow's marketing and operations. **[6]**

**8.** Explain the difference between the stakeholder concept and the shareholder concept, using a business decision as an example. **[4]**

**9.** Fenwyck Pharmacies runs a chain of pharmacies. Its country's population is ageing and inward migration is rising. Analyse how these two social changes could affect Fenwyck. **[6]**

**10.** Amberlow Insurance plans to use artificial intelligence to assess claims and answer customer queries. Analyse the possible impact on Amberlow's functional areas and how it could manage the change. **[9]**

**11.** Strathdene Batteries is choosing a country for a new factory. Country P offers a large government grant but has poor roads and slow permit approval. Country R offers no grant but has reliable infrastructure and a trade agreement with Strathdene's main export markets. Analyse the external factors Strathdene should weigh. **[6]**

**12.** Hollinsworth Garden Machinery makes petrol lawnmowers. Its revenue rose from £6.0 million to £6.24 million last year, while the national price index rose from 120.0 to 127.2. The government plans stricter emission limits on small petrol engines, a recession is forecast, and a rival has launched app-controlled robotic mowers. Customers increasingly ask about firms' environmental record.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage change in Hollinsworth's revenue in real terms. **[4]**
**(b)** Evaluate whether Hollinsworth should respond by switching its range to battery-powered and robotic mowers. **[16]**

## Answers

**1.** Any two: consumer protection [1]; the labour market, including employee rights [1]; environmental issues; health and safety. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* "Identify" needs only the area named; a long description of one area earns no more than a short correct name.

**2.** PESTLE is a snapshot of conditions at one time [1]. Factors such as exchange rates or interest rates can change quickly [1], so a strategy built on it may rest on out-of-date assumptions unless the analysis is reviewed regularly [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* An "explain" answer needs the consequence for the business, not just the limitation named.

**3.** Cost before = 900 ÷ 1.25 = £720 [1]. Cost after = 900 ÷ 1.20 = £750 [1]. Increase = (750 − 720) ÷ 720 × 100 [1] = **4.17%** [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Converting euros into pounds means dividing by the euros-per-pound rate; multiplying gives a figure that should look obviously wrong.

**4. (a)** 250 × 190 = **¥47,500** [1]; 250 × 180 = **¥45,000** [1].
**(b)** 47,500 ÷ 180 = **£263.89** [1], about 5.6% more per lens than before [1]. If Japanese demand for its lenses is price inelastic, a lower yen price would win few extra sales, so taking the higher margin earns more [1].
*Examiner insight:* Linking the pricing choice to price elasticity is the step that turns a calculation into an explanation.

**5.** (129.8 − 118.0) ÷ 118.0 × 100 [1] = **10%** [1]. Input prices are rising faster than selling prices, so the profit margin per unit is squeezed unless costs are cut elsewhere or prices rise further [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Dividing the 11.8-point rise by 100 gives 11.8%, a common slip; always divide by the earlier index value.

**6.** Before: 2,600,000 × 0.045 = £117,000, so total payment = £517,000 [1]. After: 2,600,000 × 0.06 = £156,000, so total = £556,000 [1]. Increase = £39,000 [1]. Percentage increase = 39,000 ÷ 517,000 × 100 = **7.54%** [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* The question asks about the total payment, so capital must be included; the interest alone rises by 33%, which answers a different question.

**7.** A quota caps the quantity of ceramics allowed in, so Dunmarrow's export volume may be limited however good its products are [1]. Marketing may focus on higher-value ranges so each permitted unit earns more [1], or redirect promotion to other export markets, which needs new research and spending [1]. Operations face spare capacity if exports fall [1], raising unit costs as fixed costs are spread over less output [1]. It could cut shifts or switch output to the home market; the impact depends on how tight the quota is and how much output goes to that market [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Develop each function into a consequence; describing a quota as a tax gains little.

**8.** The shareholder concept says a business exists mainly to maximise returns to its owners [1]. The stakeholder concept says it should balance the interests of all groups it affects, such as employees, customers and the community [1]. For example, when deciding whether to close a factory, a shareholder view would focus on the effect on profit and dividends [1], while a stakeholder view would also weigh job losses and the local community [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Credit for the example depends on showing how the two concepts lead to different priorities in the same decision.

**9.** An older population raises demand for prescriptions, mobility aids and health advice [1], so Fenwyck could extend its range and services, increasing sales per store [1]. It may need staff trained to advise older customers, adding training costs [1]. Rising migration enlarges the labour pool, which may ease recruitment of qualified staff [1]. Migrants may also bring demand for different products and languages [1], so Fenwyck may adapt stock and signage in some areas; how much each change matters depends on where its stores are [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Answers that apply each change to a pharmacy's products, staff or locations score above general points about population.

**10.** AI could assess straightforward claims faster than staff [1], cutting unit costs in operations and improving customer service speed [1]. Finance must fund software and data systems up front [1], with savings arriving later, so the payback is uncertain [1]. For HR, fewer claims-handling roles may be needed, which could mean redundancies or redeployment [1], and fear of job losses could lower morale [1]. Marketing could promote faster claims, but errors or unfair decisions by AI could damage trust [1]. Amberlow could consult staff early and retrain them for complex claims [1], and keep human review of disputed claims to protect accuracy and reputation [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* Linked chains of reasoning across several functions earn more credit than a list of AI uses.

**11.** The grant in Country P lowers the start-up cost [1], but it may come with conditions and could be withdrawn later [1]. Poor roads raise transport costs and the risk of late deliveries [1], and slow permits could delay the opening and the return on the investment [1]. Country R's trade agreement means fewer barriers when exporting to Strathdene's main markets [1], which may save more each year than a one-off grant [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Weighing a one-off benefit against ongoing costs lifts this above a description of each country.

**12. (a)** Nominal growth = (6.24 − 6.0) ÷ 6.0 × 100 = 4% [1]. Inflation = (127.2 − 120.0) ÷ 120.0 × 100 = 6% [1]. Real revenue = 6.24 ÷ 1.06 = £5.89 million in last year's prices [1]. Real change = **−1.9%** (to 1 d.p.) [1].
**(b)** The real fall in revenue suggests the petrol range is already losing ground [1]. Stricter emission limits are a legal threat that may raise the cost of meeting standards or end some petrol models [1], so switching protects Hollinsworth from future compliance costs [1]. Customers' growing interest in environmental records means a low-emission range could support the brand [1] and could fit a CSR message that wins loyalty [1]. The rival's robotic mowers are a disruptive technology [1]; staying with petrol risks losing customers to the new model [1]. However, switching needs heavy investment in designs, batteries and software skills [1], and a recession will cut demand for income-elastic garden machinery [1], so revenue may fall just when spending peaks [1]. Borrowing for the change could be costly if interest rates rise [1]. Hollinsworth also lacks experience in app-controlled products, so it may be weaker than the rival [1]. A phased switch could balance these, starting with battery mowers that use existing skills [1] and partnering with a technology firm for robotics [1]. On balance, the legal and technological pressures make the switch necessary, because petrol sales look set to shrink whatever happens [1]; the timing and pace should depend on how deep the recession is and how quickly the emission rules take effect [1].
*Examiner insight:* Evaluation earns more when the judgement uses the real fall calculated in (a) and weighs the changes against each other.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Multiplying by the exchange rate when the foreign price should be divided, or the reverse.
- Treating a change in index points as the percentage change.
- Leaving capital repayments out of a total-payment calculation, or using the original loan instead of the outstanding balance.
- Describing a quota as a tax, or a tariff as a quantity limit.
- Naming an external change without tracing its effect to a functional area.
- Treating CSR or AI as automatically good, without weighing costs.
- Ending an evaluation with no clear, supported judgement.
