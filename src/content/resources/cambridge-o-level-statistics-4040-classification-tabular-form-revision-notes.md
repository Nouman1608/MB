These notes condense section 2.1 of the Cambridge O Level Statistics (4040) syllabus for examination in 2025, 2026 and 2027: "Classification and representation in tabular form", including two-way tables. The full method with worked examples is in the [classification and tables study guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-statistics-4040-classification-tabular-form/); the [classification and tables practice questions](/resources/cambridge-o-level-statistics-4040-classification-tabular-form-practice/) give exam-style drill. For all of topic 2 at a glance, see the [summary representation revision notes](/resources/o-level-statistics-summary-representation-revision-notes/).

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Classification | Sorting each observation into exactly one category or class |
| Mutually exclusive classes | No observation can belong to more than one class |
| Exhaustive classes | Every possible observation belongs to some class |
| Open class | A class with no upper (or lower) limit, such as "60 and over" |
| Frequency table | A table of categories or classes with the number of observations in each |
| Two-way table | A table classifying observations by two variables, one on rows and one on columns |
| Row total, column total | The sum of the cells across a row, or down a column |
| Grand total | The total number of observations; equal to the sum of the row totals and of the column totals |
| Row percentage | A cell as a percentage of its row total |

## Classification checklist

- Do any classes overlap at an edge? (10–20 and 20–30 overlap at 20.)
- Is there a class for the largest and smallest values? Add an open class or "Other" if not.
- Would two people sort the same observation into the same class?
- Do the classes separate the groups the question is about?

## Table design checklist

- Title stating what, where and when.
- Clear headings for rows and columns.
- Units in the headings (minutes, kg, $).
- Totals, with a check that they agree.
- Categories in a sensible order.
- Source stated for secondary data.

## Completing a two-way table

1. Draw the empty table with a Total row and column.
2. Enter every given figure in the correct cell.
3. Find any row or column with only one gap; subtract to fill it.
4. Repeat until full.
5. Check that the row totals and the column totals both give the grand total.

**Worked reminder.** 60 members of a club are classified by sex and preferred sport.

| | Swim | Run | Cycle | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Male | 9 | ? | 6 | 28 |
| Female | ? | ? | ? | ? |
| Total | 20 | ? | 15 | 60 |

```
Male run      = 28 − 9 − 6 = 13
Female total  = 60 − 28 = 32
Female swim   = 20 − 9 = 11
Female cycle  = 15 − 6 = 9
Female run    = 32 − 11 − 9 = 12
Run total     = 13 + 12 = 25
Check: 20 + 25 + 15 = 60, and 28 + 32 = 60
```

The last line checks both directions. If the two do not agree, recheck each subtraction and each figure copied from the question before going further.

**With algebra.** If written information gives a relationship ("twice as many walk as cycle"), call the smaller number x, write the row or column total as an equation, and solve.

## Percentage bases

| Wording in the question | Base |
|---|---|
| "What percentage of the girls ..." | The girls' row (or column) total |
| "What percentage of those who walk are girls?" | The walkers' column (or row) total |
| "What percentage of all pupils are girls who walk?" | The grand total |

The group after the word "of" is the base. When comparing groups of different sizes, convert each group to percentages of its own total before comparing.

## Exam traps

- Leaving one class open at the bottom but not at the top (or the reverse).
- Writing classes such as 0–5, 5–10 for whole-number data; use 0–4, 5–9, or inequalities for continuous data.
- Forgetting the units in a heading: "Time" alone is incomplete.
- Taking the percentage of the grand total for a question that names a subgroup.
- Comparing counts for unequal groups.
- Not using the final check of totals, so a slip in one cell goes unnoticed.
- Rounded row percentages may add to 99.9% or 100.1%; state that this is due to rounding.

## Quick self-test

1. What does "mutually exclusive" mean for classes?
2. Classes for the number of goals scored are 0–2, 2–4, 4–6. State the fault.
3. Ages of adults are grouped 18–29, 30–49, 50–64. What is missing?
4. Name four features of a well-designed table.
5. A two-way table has row totals 35 and 45. What is the grand total?
6. In a row with total 50, two cells are 18 and 21. Find the third cell.
7. 24 of 80 boys walk to school. What percentage of the boys walk?
8. 24 of the 60 pupils who walk are boys. What percentage of walkers are boys?
9. Group A has 200 people, 50 of whom smoke. Group B has 120, 36 of whom smoke. Which group has the higher proportion of smokers?
10. In a table, the walking total is twice the cycling total, and together they make 54. Find each.
11. Why is it better to tally raw records in a single pass through the list?
12. Why should row percentages be used to compare two groups of different sizes?

### Answers

1. No observation can fall into more than one class.
2. The classes overlap: 2 and 4 each fit two classes.
3. A class for adults aged 65 and over (the classes are not exhaustive).
4. Any four: title, headings, units, totals, sensible order, source.
5. 80.
6. 50 − 18 − 21 = 11.
7. 24/80 × 100 = 30%.
8. 24/60 × 100 = 40%.
9. A: 50/200 = 25%. B: 36/120 = 30%. Group B.
10. 3x = 54, so cycling 18 and walking 36.
11. Each record is used exactly once, so none is missed or counted twice.
12. Counts depend on group size; percentages put both groups on the same scale.

## Frequently asked questions

**Are inequalities or written limits better for continuous classes?**
Inequalities such as 10 ≤ t < 20 make the edges completely clear, so they are safer for continuous data. Written limits such as 10–19 suit whole-number data.

**What should I do if a two-way table will not balance?**
Recheck each subtraction and each figure copied from the question. Totals must agree in both directions.
