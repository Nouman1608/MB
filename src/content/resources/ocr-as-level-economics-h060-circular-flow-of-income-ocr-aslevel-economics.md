This guide covers the **Circular flow of income**, topic 1.1 in Component 02, Macroeconomics, of the OCR *AS Level Economics (H060)* specification, Version 3.0 (June 2026). The specification asks you to explain "the circular flow of income, with injections and leakages" and "the methods of measuring national income, output and expenditure". It is the starting point for the whole of area 1, Aggregate demand and aggregate supply, because it shows why spending, income and output are three views of the same activity. ## 1. The simplest model: households and firms

Start with an economy that has only two sectors, households and firms, no government and no foreign trade, in which households spend all their income.

- **Real flows.** Households supply the factors of production (labour, land, capital and enterprise) to firms. Firms use them to produce goods and services, which flow back to households.
- **Money flows.** Firms pay factor incomes to households: wages for labour, rent for land, interest for capital and profit for enterprise. Households spend that income on firms' output, which becomes firms' revenue.

The money flow runs in the opposite direction to the real flow. In this closed loop, every pound spent becomes someone's income, and every pound of income pays for output. So:

**national output = national income = national expenditure**

This identity is the foundation of national income accounting. It holds by definition because the value of output is shared out as income to those who produced it.

## 2. Leakages and injections

Real economies have more sectors, and money can leave or enter the loop.

| Leakages (withdrawals), W | Injections, J |
|---|---|
| **Saving (S):** income not spent by households | **Investment (I):** spending by firms on capital goods |
| **Taxation (T):** income taken by the government | **Government spending (G):** spending on goods, services and public sector pay |
| **Imports (M):** spending on goods made abroad | **Exports (X):** foreign spending on domestic output |

Each leakage pairs naturally with an injection from the same sector: saving with investment through the financial sector, taxes with government spending, imports with exports through the foreign sector. The pairs do **not** have to balance individually. A government can run a deficit (G > T) while households save more than firms invest (S > I).

Transfer payments, such as pensions and unemployment benefits, are not part of G as an injection. They move income from taxpayers to recipients without any new output being bought, so they are best treated as negative taxes.

## 3. Equilibrium national income

National income is in **equilibrium** when total planned injections equal total planned withdrawals:

**S + T + M = I + G + X**

- If **J > W**, more money enters the flow than leaves it. Spending exceeds the current level of output, firms see stocks run down, and they raise output and employment. National income rises.
- If **J < W**, more leaves than enters. Firms find unsold stock building up, cut output and employment, and national income falls.

Why does income stop changing? Because withdrawals depend on income. As income rises, households save more, pay more tax and buy more imports, so W rises until it matches J. Injections, by contrast, are driven mostly by other things: business expectations and interest rates for I, government decisions for G, and foreign incomes and the exchange rate for X.

**Worked example 1: is income rising or falling?** In Country A, planned S = 60, T = 90 and M = 70 (billion currency units), so W = 220. Planned I = 50, G = 100 and X = 60, so J = 210. Withdrawals exceed injections by 10 billion, so national income will **fall** until withdrawals drop to 210. Notice that G (100) exceeds T (90): the government is injecting more than it withdraws, but not by enough to offset saving and imports.

**Worked example 2: finding the new equilibrium.** In Country B, withdrawals are 40% of national income (W = 0.4Y) and injections are fixed at 240 billion.

- Equilibrium: 0.4Y = 240, so Y = **600 billion**.
- Exports rise by 20 billion, so J = 260. New equilibrium: 0.4Y = 260, so Y = **650 billion**.

National income rises by 50 billion, more than the 20 billion increase in exports, because each round of extra income is partly re-spent before it leaks out. At A Level this is developed as the multiplier. For AS, the key point is the direction and the reason: an injection raises income until enough extra leaks out to match it.

## 4. Measuring national income: three methods

Because output, income and expenditure are equal in principle, statisticians can measure the economy's activity in three ways. Gross Domestic Product (GDP) is the usual headline measure.

### The output method

Add the value of everything produced, but count only **value added** at each stage: the value of a firm's output minus the cost of the inputs it bought from other firms. Adding total sales would count the same inputs several times.

**Worked example 3: value added.**

| Stage | Sales | Bought-in inputs | Value added |
|---|---|---|---|
| Farmer grows wheat | 200 | 0 | 200 |
| Miller makes flour | 350 | 200 | 150 |
| Baker makes bread | 600 | 350 | 250 |
| Total | 1,150 | | **600** |

The bread sold to consumers is worth 600, and total value added is also 600. Adding sales (1,150) would count the wheat three times and the flour twice.

### The income method

Add the factor incomes earned in producing output: wages and salaries, rent, interest and profit. Exclude **transfer payments**, because no output is produced in return. Pensions or student grants paid by the state are not income for producing anything.

**Worked example 4.** Wages 400, rent 50, interest 30 and profits 120 give national income of **600**. State pensions of 80 are excluded.

### The expenditure method

Add spending on final output: consumption by households (C), investment (I), government spending on goods and services (G) and exports (X), then subtract imports (M), because spending on imports does not buy domestic output.

**Worked example 5.** C = 420, I = 90, G = 110, X = 150, M = 170.
Expenditure = 420 + 90 + 110 + 150 - 170 = **600**.

All three methods give 600, as they should.

## 5. Why the three measures differ in practice

In real data the three totals rarely match exactly, and statisticians publish a balancing item called the statistical discrepancy.

- **The hidden economy.** Cash-in-hand work goes unrecorded in income data but may show up in spending.
- **Different sources.** Output data come from business surveys, income data partly from tax records and spending data from household surveys, each with its own errors.
- **Timing.** Spending, production and the receipt of income may fall in different periods.
- **Stocks.** Output produced but not yet sold must be counted as investment in inventories for the identity to hold.

## 6. Evaluating the circular flow model

The model is useful because it shows clearly how a change in one sector spreads through the economy: a fall in exports means lower incomes for exporters' workers, who then spend less at home. It also explains why governments watch total spending when they are worried about recession.

But it is a simplification:

- It treats each leakage and injection as one number, hiding how they interact. Higher saving might lower interest rates and raise investment.
- It shows flows but not **capacity**. Rising injections raise *nominal* income; whether real output rises or prices rise depends on spare capacity, which is why the course moves on to aggregate supply.
- It ignores **stocks** of wealth and debt, which can change spending even when income is unchanged.
- Equilibrium income need not be the full-employment level. Income can settle where W = J with many people out of work.

## Common mistakes

- Saying the economy is in equilibrium only when S = I, T = G and X = M. Only the totals need to balance.
- Counting transfer payments as government spending in the expenditure method or as income in the income method.
- Adding sales at every stage instead of value added.
- Forgetting to subtract imports in the expenditure method.
- Describing a leakage as "money that disappears". Saving and taxes go somewhere; they just leave the flow of spending on domestic output.
- Assuming a rise in injections always raises real output.

## Exam technique

- **Explain** the flow as a chain: an injection rises, firms' revenues rise, they pay out more income, households spend part of it, and income rises until withdrawals match.
- When asked to **calculate**, write the formula first, such as C + I + G + (X - M), and show each substitution.
- For data-response questions, compare total J with total W and state the direction in which national income will move, quoting the figures.
- To **evaluate** the model, weigh what it reveals (linkages between sectors) against what it omits (capacity, prices and wealth).
