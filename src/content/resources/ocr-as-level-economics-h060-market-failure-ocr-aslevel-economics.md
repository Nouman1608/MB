## 1. What market failure is

**Market failure** occurs when the price mechanism, left to itself, allocates resources inefficiently, so that the outcome does not maximise society's welfare. Too much or too little of a good is produced and consumed compared with the level society would choose if all costs and benefits were counted.

The benchmark is **allocative efficiency**: output where the value to society of the last unit equals its cost to society. A free market reaches that point only if the people making decisions bear all the costs and receive all the benefits of their choices. When they do not, the market sends the wrong signals.

Market failure is about **efficiency**, not about whether an outcome is fair. A market can be efficient and still leave some households unable to afford a good; that is a question of equity, which the market failure framework does not settle on its own.

## 2. Complete and partial market failure

- **Partial market failure:** a market exists but produces the wrong quantity or charges the wrong price. A factory's output of cement causes dust that harms neighbours, but cement is still bought and sold; the market just produces too much.
- **Complete market failure:** no market exists at all for a good or service that society values. This is a **missing market**. There is no market in which households can buy cleaner air from a polluter, because no one owns the air and its use cannot be priced.

## 3. Private, external and social: the six terms

Every decision has costs and benefits to the person making it and, sometimes, to others.

| Term | Meaning | Usually shown by |
|---|---|---|
| Marginal private cost (MPC) | Cost to the producer of one more unit | Supply curve |
| Marginal external cost (MEC) | Cost to third parties of one more unit (pollution, noise, congestion) | Vertical gap between MSC and MPC |
| Marginal social cost (MSC) | Full cost to society of one more unit | MPC + MEC |
| Marginal private benefit (MPB) | Benefit to the consumer of one more unit | Demand curve |
| Marginal external benefit (MEB) | Benefit to third parties of one more unit | Vertical gap between MSB and MPB |
| Marginal social benefit (MSB) | Full benefit to society of one more unit | MPB + MEB |

The two identities to learn:

**MSC = MPC + MEC** and **MSB = MPB + MEB**

A **third party** is anyone outside the transaction: neither the buyer nor the seller. If there are no external effects, MEC and MEB are zero, so private and social values coincide and the market can be efficient.

## 4. Market output, social optimum and welfare loss

- The **market** produces where **MPB = MPC**, because buyers and sellers weigh only their own costs and benefits.
- The **social optimum** is where **MSB = MSC**.
- If the two outputs differ, there is a **welfare loss** (deadweight loss) on the units between them.

**Worked example 1 (invented market for ready-mixed concrete).** Q is in thousand cubic metres a month and prices are in £.

- MPB = 48 − 2Q (demand)
- MPC = 12 + 2Q (supply)
- MEC = £8 per unit (dust and lorry noise), so MSC = 20 + 2Q
- No external benefit, so MSB = MPB.

| Q | MPB = MSB | MPC | MSC | Compare MSB with MSC |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 6 | 36 | 24 | 32 | MSB > MSC: produce more |
| 7 | 34 | 26 | 34 | Equal: social optimum |
| 8 | 32 | 28 | 36 | MSC > MSB: too much |
| 9 | 30 | 30 | 38 | Market output (MPB = MPC) |
| 10 | 28 | 32 | 40 | |

- Market: 48 − 2Q = 12 + 2Q, so Q = **9** and P = **£30**.
- Optimum: 48 − 2Q = 20 + 2Q, so Q = **7**, where buyers would pay **£34**.
- Overproduction: 2 thousand cubic metres.
- Welfare loss = ½ × (9 − 7) × (38 − 30) = ½ × 2 × 8 = **8**, that is £8 thousand a month.

The welfare loss is the triangle between MSC and MSB from the optimum to the market output. For each of those units, society's cost exceeds the benefit, and the sum of those excesses is the loss.

On the diagram, draw MPB = MSB sloping down, MPC sloping up, and MSC parallel above MPC by the MEC. Mark Q market (9) and Q optimum (7), and shade the triangle pointing back towards the optimum.

## 5. Causes of market failure

| Cause | Why the market goes wrong | AS links |
|---|---|---|
| Negative externalities | Decision makers ignore costs imposed on third parties, so MSC > MPC or MSB < MPB: overproduction or overconsumption | Taxation, regulation, tradable pollution permits |
| Positive externalities | Decision makers ignore benefits to third parties: underproduction or underconsumption | Subsidies, government expenditure |
| Missing markets | Without property rights, some resources cannot be priced at all | Legislation, permits that create a market |
| Poor information | Buyers misjudge the private benefit or cost of a good, so demand is too high or too low | Information provision, regulation |
| Market power | A dominant firm restricts output to raise price | Competition policy |
| Price instability | Volatile prices in markets with inelastic supply, such as farm goods, can cause hardship and under-investment | Buffer stock systems, price controls |

Notice that the government methods listed in the AS specification line up with these causes. When you explain an intervention, start from the cause it is designed to fix.

## 6. Does market failure justify intervention?

Market failure shows that the market outcome can be improved, not that government action will improve it.

- **Measurement:** the MEC of £8 in the example is easy to use on paper but hard to estimate in reality. If the government guesses wrongly, a tax may be too high or too low.
- **Size:** a small welfare loss may cost less than the administration needed to correct it.
- **Who are the third parties?** External costs often fall on people who cannot easily be identified or compensated.
- **Government failure:** intervention can create new distortions, so the comparison is between an imperfect market and an imperfect government.
- **Time:** some external costs appear only years later, so present choices are hard to correct.

A strong judgement says when intervention is most justified: when the welfare loss is large, the external effect can be measured with reasonable confidence and a low-cost tool exists.

## Annotated model answer

*Question:* "Explain why the free market leads to a misallocation of resources when production causes pollution."

> Firms decide output by comparing price with their marginal private cost and ignore the marginal external cost of pollution on local residents *[cause]*. So marginal social cost is above marginal private cost *[terms]*. The market produces where MPB = MPC, at 9 thousand cubic metres in the concrete example, but the social optimum is where MSB = MSC at 7 thousand *[application with data]*. The last 2 thousand units cost society more than they are worth, a welfare loss of £8 thousand, so too many resources are used in this market *[consequence]*.

## Common mistakes

- Defining market failure as "a market that makes a loss" or "a market that collapses".
- Writing MSC = MPC − MEC.
- Putting the welfare-loss triangle on the wrong side of the optimum.
- Assuming market failure always justifies government action.
- Treating unfair outcomes as market failure without explaining the efficiency problem.

## Exam technique

- **Explain:** name the cause, use the marginal terms, and finish with over or under allocation of resources.
- **Explain, with the aid of a diagram:** label all curves (MPC, MSC, MPB, MSB), both outputs and the welfare loss.
- **Calculate:** set MPB = MPC for the market and MSB = MSC for the optimum, then find the triangle.
- **Evaluate:** weigh the case for intervention against measurement problems and government failure, and reach a conditional judgement.
