## Questions

**1.** A firm sells 30 units a week at a price of 85. It cuts the price to 80 and sells 33 units a week.
**(a)** Work out total revenue at 85 and at 80. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the marginal revenue per unit of the extra 3 units. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain why this marginal revenue is lower than the new price of 80. **[2]**

**2.** A firm faces this demand schedule.

| Quantity | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Price | 60 | 52 | 44 | 36 | 28 | 20 |

**(a)** Find TR and MR for each quantity in the table. **[2]**
**(b)** State the output at which total revenue is greatest and use marginal revenue to explain your answer. **[2]**

**3.** Use the schedule in question 2.
**(a)** Calculate price elasticity of demand between 2 and 3 units. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain how your answer relates to the change in total revenue and to marginal revenue. **[2]**

**4.** A firm can sell as much as it wants at a market price of 12. Calculate total revenue, average revenue and marginal revenue at 500 units. **[3]**

**5.** A firm faces the demand curve P = 150 − 10Q.
**(a)** Write expressions for total revenue and marginal revenue in terms of Q. **[2]**
**(b)** Find the output that maximises total revenue and the maximum total revenue. **[2]**
**(c)** State the value of price elasticity of demand at this output, with a reason. **[2]**

**6.** A small printer has revenue of 192,000 and pays 161,000 for materials, rent and wages. The owner could earn 24,000 in another job.
**(a)** Find the firm's accounting profit. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate abnormal profit. **[2]**
**(c)** Revenue falls to 183,000. Explain what is likely to happen to the firm in the long run. **[2]**

**7.** Explain two reasons why normal profit differs from one industry to another. **[4]**

**8.** Explain how abnormal profit in a market can lead to a reallocation of resources, and state one limitation of this process. **[6]**

**9.** A firm can make a product by hand (fixed cost 18,000, variable cost 5 a unit) or with a machine (fixed cost 54,000, variable cost 2 a unit).
**(a)** Calculate average cost for each method at 15,000 units. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate the output at which the two methods have the same average cost. **[2]**
**(c)** Which method suits a firm expecting 8,000 units? Give a reason. **[1]**

**10.** A public university in Country B develops a cheap fibre. Two years later a clothing firm sells the first shirts made from it. After buying new cutting machines, the firm's 8 workers make 7,280 shirts a week instead of 5,200.
**(a)** Identify the invention and the innovation. **[2]**
**(b)** By what percentage did output per worker rise? **[2]**
**(c)** Explain one link between this and creative destruction. **[2]**

**11.** Analyse two arguments for, and two against, protecting inventions with patents. **[8]**

**12.** Evaluate whether governments should do more to promote technological change. **[12]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** 85 × 30 = 2,550 [1]; 80 × 33 = 2,640 [1].
**(b)** (2,640 − 2,550) ÷ 3 = **30** [1].
**(c)** The 3 extra units sell for 80 each, adding 240 [1], but the price of the original 30 units falls by 5 each, a loss of 150, so net gain 90 and MR 30 [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the lost revenue on the earlier units; stating "MR is less than price" without the 150 usually explains nothing.

**2. (a)** TR: 60, 104, 132, 144, 140, 120 [1]. MR: 60, 44, 28, 12, −4, −20 [1].
**(b)** TR is greatest at **4 units** (144) [1]. MR is still positive (12) for the 4th unit and negative (−4) for the 5th, so extra output lowers revenue after 4 [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Keep the minus signs on negative marginal revenue; they are the evidence for your explanation.

**3. (a)** %ΔQ = 1 ÷ 2 = +50% [1]; %ΔP = −8 ÷ 52 = −15.4%; PED = 50 ÷ −15.4 = **−3.25** [1].
**(b)** Demand is elastic, so the price cut raises TR from 104 to 132 [1], which matches positive MR of 28 for the third unit [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the starting price and quantity as the base for each percentage, and say "elastic" when you interpret a figure beyond 1 in size.

**4.** TR = 12 × 500 = **6,000** [1]; AR = 6,000 ÷ 500 = **12** [1]; MR = **12**, since each extra unit sells at the same price [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Giving a reason why MR equals price shows you can tell a price taker from a firm with market power.

**5. (a)** TR = P × Q = 150Q − 10Q² [1]; MR = 150 − 20Q [1].
**(b)** MR = 0, so Q = **7.5** [1]; TR = 150 × 7.5 − 10 × 7.5² = **562.5** [1].
**(c)** PED is **−1** (unit elastic) in size [1], because TR is at its maximum where MR = 0 [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the MR expression before solving; a bare value for Q gives the marker nothing to follow if it is wrong.

**6. (a)** 192,000 − 161,000 = **31,000** [1].
**(b)** Abnormal profit is accounting profit less the owner's opportunity cost [1]: 31,000 − 24,000 = **7,000** [1].
**(c)** Accounting profit is 22,000, which is 2,000 below normal profit [1]. The owner earns less than in the next best use, so is likely to leave in the long run [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Compare profit with the 24,000 alternative explicitly; "the firm makes a loss" would be wrong because accounting profit is positive.

**7.** Risk: industries with a high chance of failure need a bigger expected reward to keep owners [1], so normal profit is higher, as in remote drilling compared with a stationery shop [1]. Opportunity cost: if the skills or capital needed could earn a lot elsewhere [1], the reward needed to stay is higher [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Say why the required reward changes for each reason; naming risk alone is a label, not an explanation.

**8.** Abnormal profit signals that consumers value the good highly relative to cost [1]. Firms outside the market are attracted to enter [1], so supply rises and price falls [1]. Resources move to this market until abnormal profit falls to normal [1]. Limitation: barriers to entry may block new firms [1], so abnormal profit stays and resources are not reallocated [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Write the chain in order, link by link; jumping from profit to lower price omits the entry step that does the work.

**9. (a)** Hand: (18,000 + 5 × 15,000) ÷ 15,000 = 93,000 ÷ 15,000 = **6.20** [1]. Machine: (54,000 + 2 × 15,000) ÷ 15,000 = 84,000 ÷ 15,000 = **5.60** [1]. Machine is lower at this output [1].
**(b)** 18,000 + 5Q = 54,000 + 2Q [1], so 3Q = 36,000 and Q = **12,000** [1].
**(c)** Hand: 8,000 is below 12,000, where hand-made average cost is 7.25 and the machine's is 8.75 [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Total cost divided by output gives average cost; dividing only variable cost leaves out the fixed cost that drives the answer.

**10. (a)** The cheap fibre is the invention [1]; the first sale of shirts made from it is the innovation [1].
**(b)** Before: 5,200 ÷ 8 = 650. After: 7,280 ÷ 8 = 910 [1]. Rise: (910 − 650) ÷ 650 = **40%** [1].
**(c)** Creative destruction is the replacement of old products and methods by new ones [1]. The new fibre could cut demand for older fabrics, destroying firms and jobs in that market while creating new ones [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Apply the term to the fibre; a definition on its own does not use the context.

**11.** For 1: A patent gives sole rights for a period, so the inventor can charge prices above cost [1] and recover research costs, which encourages invention [1]. For 2: The patent requires the idea to be disclosed, so other firms can learn from it [1], and later innovation can build on it [1]. Against 1: The holder can charge monopoly prices [1], so consumers pay more and fewer buy [1]. Against 2: Rivals are blocked from improving the idea during the patent [1], so follow-on innovation may be delayed and legal costs rise [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Pair each argument with its consequence for firms or consumers; a list of labels with no chain stays at description.

**12.** For: Private firms under-invest in research because knowledge spreads to rivals, a positive externality [1]. Government funding of research or tax relief can close this gap [1]. Innovation can raise productivity and lower costs, helping long-run growth [1]. Basic research may be too risky or long-term for private firms [1]. Better education, training and infrastructure support firms that adopt new methods [1]. Patent law helps the innovator recover costs [1]. Against: Governments may back the wrong projects [1]. Funds have an opportunity cost, for example less health spending [1]. Subsidies may replace private spending that would have happened anyway [1]. Patents and protection can create monopoly power [1]. Evaluation: The case is stronger where spillovers are large and the market lacks private funding, and weaker for projects firms already fund [1]. Overall, targeted support for basic research and skills is more defensible than picking winning firms [1]. **[12]**
*Examiner insight:* Finish with the circumstances in which support works and where it does not, rather than repeating one side.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calculating MR from one price change without dividing by the change in quantity.
- Writing MR = price for a firm with a downward-sloping demand curve.
- Basing percentage changes on the new value instead of the starting value.
- Leaving out minus signs on marginal revenue or elasticity.
- Calling accounting profit abnormal profit without deducting normal profit.
- Describing profit as a signal without the entry step.
- Saying technology cuts costs with no mention of output.
- Listing patent points with no consequence or judgement.
