This guide covers section 2.4, Linear equations, in topic 2 (Equations, formulae and identities) of the Pearson Edexcel International GCSE Mathematics A (4MA1) specification, Issue 2. It is Foundation Tier content; the Higher Tier says "See Foundation Tier" and adds a harder example with two fractions, so both tiers study the same statements and Higher papers simply set harder versions.

The topic-level [Expressions, formulae and linear equations study guide](/resources/edexcel-igcse-maths-4ma1-expressions-formulae-and-linear-equations/) covers 2.3 and 2.4 together. This page goes further into 2.4 alone: a single method that works for every type, new worked examples, and a structure for word problems. Afterwards, use the [linear equations revision notes](/resources/edexcel-igcse-mathematics-4ma1-linear-equations-edexcel-igcse-maths-revision-notes/) and the [linear equations practice questions](/resources/edexcel-igcse-mathematics-4ma1-linear-equations-edexcel-igcse-maths-practice/).

## What the specification says

| Statement | Students should be taught to | Tier |
|---|---|---|
| 2.4 A | solve linear equations, with integer or fractional coefficients, in one unknown in which the unknown appears on either side or both sides of the equation | Both |
| 2.4 B | set up simple linear equations from given data | Both |

The specification's examples range from 5x + 8 = 12 and 7(x + 3) = 5x − 8 to (4x + 5)/2 = 3, and, in the Higher Tier list, an equation with three fractions such as (2x − 3)/6 + (x + 2)/3 = 5/2. A linear equation has no x² or higher powers, so it has exactly one solution (unless it simplifies to something always or never true).

## One method for every linear equation

1. **Clear fractions**: multiply every term on both sides by the lowest common multiple of the denominators.
2. **Expand brackets**, taking care with a minus sign in front of a bracket.
3. **Collect**: move the unknown terms to the side with the larger coefficient, and the numbers to the other side.
4. **Divide** by the coefficient of the unknown.
5. **Check** by substituting into the original equation.

Each step keeps the equation balanced: whatever you do to one side, you do to the whole of the other side.

## Worked examples: brackets and unknowns on both sides

**Worked example 1.** Solve 3(2x − 1) − 2(x + 4) = 9.

```
6x − 3 − 2x − 8 = 9         expand: −2 × +4 = −8
4x − 11 = 9                 collect like terms
4x = 20
x = 5
Check: 3(9) − 2(9) = 27 − 18 = 9 ✓
```

**Worked example 2.** Solve 7 − 3x = 2x + 22.

```
7 = 5x + 22                 add 3x to both sides (keeps the x term positive)
−15 = 5x
x = −3
Check: 7 − 3(−3) = 16 and 2(−3) + 22 = 16 ✓
```

Moving the x terms to the side where they stay positive avoids dividing by a negative number. Negative and fractional solutions are common; do not assume an answer is wrong because it is not a positive whole number.

## Worked examples: fractional coefficients

**Worked example 3.** Solve x/4 + x/6 = 5.

```
LCM of 4 and 6 is 12; multiply every term by 12
3x + 2x = 60
5x = 60
x = 12
```

**Worked example 4.** Solve (3x − 1)/5 − (x + 2)/2 = 1.

```
Multiply every term by 10:
2(3x − 1) − 5(x + 2) = 10
6x − 2 − 5x − 10 = 10        −5 × +2 = −10
x − 12 = 10
x = 22
Check: 65/5 − 24/2 = 13 − 12 = 1 ✓
```

Put a bracket round each numerator as soon as you clear the fractions. The fraction line acts as a bracket, so the minus sign in front of (x + 2)/2 applies to both x and 2.

**Worked example 5 (Higher style).** Solve (x − 4)/3 + (2x + 1)/4 = 7/6.

```
LCM of 3, 4 and 6 is 12
4(x − 4) + 3(2x + 1) = 14     the right-hand side: 12 × 7/6 = 14
4x − 16 + 6x + 3 = 14
10x − 13 = 14
10x = 27
x = 2.7  (or 27/10)
```

Multiply the right-hand side too. A common slip is to multiply only the fractions on the left.

## The unknown in a denominator

Some equations become linear once you multiply by the denominators.

**Worked example 6.** Solve 12/(2x − 1) = 4/(x + 2).

```
12(x + 2) = 4(2x − 1)       multiply both sides by (2x − 1)(x + 2)
12x + 24 = 8x − 4
4x = −28
x = −7
Check: 12/(−15) = −0.8 and 4/(−5) = −0.8 ✓
```

## Setting up equations from data (2.4 B)

Word problems follow a fixed structure:

1. **Define** the unknown in words: "Let the width be w cm."
2. **Express** every other quantity in terms of it.
3. **Form** the equation from the fact the question gives (a total, a perimeter, an angle sum, "the same as").
4. **Solve** it.
5. **Answer** the question asked, in context and with units.

**Worked example 7 (angles).** A triangle has angles x°, x° and (2x − 20)°. Find the angles.

```
Angle sum of a triangle: x + x + 2x − 20 = 180
4x = 200,  x = 50
Angles: 50°, 50° and 80°
```

**Worked example 8 (perimeter).** The length of a rectangle is 5 cm less than three times its width. Its perimeter is 62 cm. Find its length and width.

```
Let the width be w cm; length = (3w − 5) cm
2(w + 3w − 5) = 62
8w − 10 = 62
8w = 72,  w = 9
Width 9 cm, length 22 cm
```

**Worked example 9 (money).** Amir has twice as much money as Ben. Amir gives Ben £15, and they now have the same amount. How much did each have at the start?

```
Let Ben have £b; Amir has £2b
After the gift: Amir 2b − 15, Ben b + 15
2b − 15 = b + 15
b = 30
Ben had £30 and Amir had £60
```

Check in the story: Amir £45 and Ben £45 after the gift. ✓

## Common mistakes

- Multiplying only some terms when clearing fractions, especially the right-hand side or a term with no fraction.
- Losing the sign: −2(x + 4) = −2x − 8, and −(x + 2)/2 becomes −5(x + 2) after × 10.
- "Moving" a term without changing its sign.
- Dividing the wrong way: 4x = 20 gives x = 5, not x = 1/5; −15 = 5x gives x = −3.
- In word problems, stopping at x = 50 when the question asks for the angles.
- Not defining the letter, so the examiner cannot follow the equation.

## Exam technique

- **"Solve"**: show each line of working. Guessing and checking does not show an algebraic method, and a wrong answer with no working leaves nothing to give credit for.
- **"Show clear algebraic working"**: every step must be visible; the answer alone does not earn full marks.
- **"Form an equation"**: the equation itself earns marks; write it before solving.
- **"Show that x = ..."**: substituting the given value in can confirm it, but forming and solving the equation is usually what is wanted.
- Leave answers as exact fractions or terminating decimals (27/10 or 2.7); do not round unless asked.
