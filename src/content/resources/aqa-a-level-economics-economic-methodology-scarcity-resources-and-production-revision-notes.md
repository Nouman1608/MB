## 3.1.1.1 Economic methodology

| Idea | Recall |
|---|---|
| Social science | Studies human choice under scarcity using models, hypotheses and evidence |
| Like natural science | Models, assumptions, testable hypotheses, ceteris paribus |
| Unlike natural science | People react to incentives and to theories; experiments are rare and data are messy; assumptions such as rationality are approximate; results are tendencies; value judgements are closer to the surface |
| Positive statement | Can be tested against evidence; can be true or false |
| Normative statement | Contains a value judgement about what ought to be; evidence cannot settle it alone |
| Value judgement | A personal view of good or bad, fair or unfair |

**Method: classify a statement**
1. Underline words such as "should", "ought", "unfair", "too high", "better".
2. If present, the statement is normative, or mixed if it also contains a testable claim.
3. If absent, ask whether data could in principle prove it wrong. If yes, it is positive.

**Why people disagree on the best option**
- They predict different positive consequences (a different model or different evidence).
- They hold different moral and political judgements (fairness, freedom, the role of the state), so they weigh the same consequences differently.
- Value judgements influence which objectives a government chooses and which groups it protects.

## 3.1.1.2 Purpose of economic activity

- Purpose: produce goods and services to satisfy **needs** (what is required to survive) and **wants** (what is desired beyond that).
- The line between the two depends on circumstances and is partly a judgement.
- Three key decisions:

| Question | Meaning |
|---|---|
| What? | Which goods and services, and how many |
| How? | Which production methods; the mix of labour and capital |
| For whom? | How output is shared among households |

**Worked reminder: how.** A print shop makes 1,000 leaflets a day. Method X uses 5 workers and 1 printer; method Y uses 2 workers and 2 printers. With a wage of 90 and printer cost of 200 a day, X costs 450 + 200 = 650 (0.65 per leaflet) and Y costs 180 + 400 = 580 (0.58). If the wage rises to 140, X costs 900 and Y costs 680. A higher wage makes the capital-intensive method relatively more attractive.

## 3.1.1.3 Economic resources

| Factor of production | Definition | Reward |
|---|---|---|
| Land | Natural resources provided by nature | Rent |
| Labour | Physical and mental effort of workers | Wages |
| Capital | Man-made goods used to make other goods | Interest |
| Enterprise | Organising factors and bearing risk | Profit |

- The **environment is a scarce resource**: limited supply of clean air, water, forests and waste-absorbing capacity.
- Non-renewable resources cannot be replaced once used; renewable resources can run down if used faster than they regenerate.
- Over-use happens when the environment is treated as free. Use now has an opportunity cost for future generations.

## 3.1.1.4 Scarcity, choice and opportunity cost

- **Scarcity** = limited resources against unlimited wants. It is the fundamental economic problem, and every economy faces it.
- Scarcity forces **choices** about how resources are allocated between uses.
- **Opportunity cost** = the benefit of the second-best option that is given up.

**Method: opportunity cost**
1. List the options.
2. Choose the best one.
3. The opportunity cost is the second best only, not the sum of everything rejected.

**Worked reminder.** A hectare gives 900 from wheat, 700 from barley and 650 from sheep. Wheat is chosen. Opportunity cost = 700 per hectare.

## 3.1.1.5 Production possibility diagrams

**Axes:** two goods or categories (for example capital goods and consumer goods). **Boundary:** maximum combinations with all resources fully and efficiently used.

| Position of a point | Meaning |
|---|---|
| On the boundary | Attainable, resources fully used; productively efficient |
| Inside the boundary | Attainable, but resources unemployed or inefficiently used |
| Outside the boundary | Unattainable with current resources and technology |

**Method: opportunity cost between two boundary points**
1. Find the change in the good gained.
2. Find the change in the good lost.
3. Opportunity cost of one unit gained = units lost ÷ units gained.

Example: a move along the boundary from (20, 90) to (50, 60) gains 30 of K and loses 30 of C, so each extra K costs 1 C.

**Shapes**
- Bowed outward: increasing opportunity cost, because resources fit the two uses unequally.
- Straight line: constant opportunity cost, because resources are equally suited.

**Movements and shifts**
- Along the boundary: reallocation of resources (a trade-off).
- Inside to boundary: use idle resources, which is actual growth at no opportunity cost.
- Outward shift: potential growth, from more or better resources or technology.
- A shift in one axis only (technology in one good) moves one intercept.

**Growth percentage:** (new − old) ÷ old × 100. If the maximum of a good rises from 80 to 92, growth is 12 ÷ 80 = 15%.

### Efficiency

| Term | Definition | On a PPD |
|---|---|---|
| Productive efficiency | Resources fully used at lowest cost; cannot make more of one good without making less of another | Every boundary point |
| Allocative efficiency | Output matches what society values most; marginal benefit equals marginal cost | Only one boundary point; the diagram cannot show which |

## Must-know distinctions

- Need versus want.
- Positive versus normative (testable versus "ought").
- Movement along the boundary (trade-off) versus shift of the boundary (growth).
- Actual growth (moving to the boundary) versus potential growth (shifting it).
- Productive efficiency (technical, all boundary points) versus allocative efficiency (what society values).
- Capital (physical) versus money.

## Quick self-test

1. Classify each as positive or normative. (a) Rent controls in Country C reduced the supply of rental homes by 4%. (b) Landlords should accept lower rents.
2. Define scarcity.
3. Name the factor of production for each: a delivery van, a forest, a baker's skill, an entrepreneur's risk-taking.
4. A field yields either 40 tonnes of wheat or 25 tonnes of beans. What is the opportunity cost of one tonne of wheat in beans?
5. A boundary passes through (20, 90) and (50, 60) in thousands of units. What is the opportunity cost of one extra unit of the horizontal good between them?
6. An economy is at (30, 50), inside its boundary. The boundary passes through (30, 70). What is the largest gain in the vertical good with no loss of the horizontal good?
7. The maximum output of one good rises from 80 to 92 after a technology gain. State the percentage rise.
8. Why does a PPD usually bow outward?
9. True or false: every point on the boundary is allocatively efficient.
10. Give one positive consequence and one moral or political judgement that could explain why two people disagree about a tax on a harmful product.

### Answers

1. (a) Positive: testable. (b) Normative: contains "should".
2. Scarcity is the problem that resources are limited while wants are unlimited, so choices must be made.
3. Capital; land (the environment); labour; enterprise.
4. 25 ÷ 40 = 0.625 tonnes of beans.
5. 30 ÷ 30 = 1 unit of the vertical good.
6. 70 − 50 = 20 thousand units.
7. (92 − 80) ÷ 80 × 100 = 15%.
8. Resources fit the two goods unequally, so each extra unit of one good costs more of the other.
9. False. All are productively efficient, but only one combination is allocatively efficient.
10. Positive consequence: consumption of the product falls. Judgement: the tax is unfair to low-income households, or the state should not interfere in choices (accept any well-explained version).

## Where marks are usually lost

- Labelling a claim positive just because it quotes a figure, although it also says "should".
- Describing a positive statement as "true". It only needs to be testable, and may well be false.
- Treating a PPD as a forecast. It shows what is possible, not what will happen.
- Counting every rejected option as the cost, rather than only the runner-up.
- Defining capital as money, or leaving out enterprise.
- Confusing a movement along the boundary with a shift of the boundary.
- Showing a point inside the boundary and calling it efficient.
- Saying that every boundary point is allocatively efficient.
- Using the PPD to claim which point is best without naming the value judgement behind the choice.
- Dividing the wrong way round in opportunity cost (gain ÷ loss rather than loss ÷ gain).
