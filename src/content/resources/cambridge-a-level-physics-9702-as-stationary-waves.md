- show an understanding of experiments that demonstrate stationary waves using

negligible; knowledge of the concept of end corrections is not required)

- understand how wavelength may be determined from the positions of nodes or

## The principle of superposition

> When two or more waves meet at a point, the resultant displacement is the
> sum of the individual displacements at that point.

Displacements have direction, so the sum takes signs into account. If one
wave gives +3.0 mm and the other −5.0 mm at the same point and instant, the
resultant is −2.0 mm. After passing through each other, the waves continue
unchanged. Superposition is the single idea behind all of Topic 8: stationary
waves, interference and the diffraction grating.

## Forming a stationary wave: the graphical method

A **stationary wave** forms when two progressive waves of the **same
frequency** (and, for complete cancellation at the nodes, the same
amplitude) travel in **opposite directions** through the same region and
superpose. In practice the second wave is usually a reflection of the first.

The syllabus asks you to explain the formation **graphically**. Draw both
waves at a series of instants, a quarter of a period apart, and add them
point by point:

- **t = 0.** Arrange the two waves to be exactly in step. Adding them gives a
wave of twice the amplitude, with crests where both have crests.
- **t = T/4.** Each wave has moved a quarter-wavelength, one to the right and
one to the left. They are now in antiphase everywhere, so the resultant is
**zero along the whole length**.
- **t = T/2.** The waves are in step again, but the resultant is inverted:
former crests are now troughs.
- **t = 3T/4.** Zero everywhere again.

Looking across all four diagrams, some points have zero resultant at **every**
instant. These are **nodes**. Midway between them are points where the
resultant swings between the largest positive and negative values. These are
**antinodes**.

```
node to adjacent node          = lambda / 2
antinode to adjacent antinode  = lambda / 2
node to adjacent antinode      = lambda / 4
```

Here λ is the wavelength of the two progressive waves that formed the pattern.

## Amplitude and phase in a stationary wave

Two properties separate a stationary wave from a progressive one and are
often examined:

- **Amplitude varies with position**: zero at a node, maximum at an antinode,
and in between at other points. In a progressive wave (with no energy loss)
every point has the same amplitude.
- **Phase**: all points between two adjacent nodes oscillate **in phase**;
points in neighbouring segments are in **antiphase** (180°). In a progressive
wave, phase changes steadily along the wave.

A stationary wave does not transfer energy along its length: the two
component waves carry equal energy in opposite directions.

## Experiment 1: microwaves

A microwave transmitter faces a flat metal plate, which reflects the
microwaves back along their path. The incident and reflected waves form a
stationary wave between transmitter and plate. A small probe receiver is moved
slowly along the line between them; its reading rises and falls, with minima
at nodes and maxima at antinodes.

**Determining the wavelength.** Measure the distance across several node
intervals, rather than one, to reduce the percentage uncertainty.

**Worked example.** The probe detects a minimum, and moving it 7.0 cm passes
through four further minima to a fifth. Find the wavelength and frequency.

```
7.0 cm covers 5 node-to-node intervals:  lambda / 2 = 7.0 / 5 = 1.4 cm
lambda = 2.8 cm = 0.028 m
f = c / lambda = 3.00 x 10^8 / 0.028 = 1.1 x 10^10 Hz
```

A common slip is to divide by 6 (the number of minima) instead of 5 (the
number of gaps between them).

## Experiment 2: a stretched string

A string is attached to a vibration generator driven by a signal generator,
passes over a pulley and is kept taut by hanging masses. Waves travel along
the string, reflect at the fixed end and superpose with the incoming waves.
At certain frequencies a large-amplitude stationary wave appears.

Both ends are fixed, so **both ends are nodes** (the vibrator's amplitude is
small enough to treat as a node). The allowed patterns fit a whole number of
half-wavelengths into the length L:

```
L = n lambda / 2       n = 1, 2, 3 ...
```

Measuring the distance between nodes with a metre rule gives λ; with the
generator's frequency, v = fλ gives the wave speed on the string.

## Experiment 3: air columns

A loudspeaker or tuning fork at the open end of a tube sends sound waves down
it; they reflect at the far end and a stationary wave forms in the air. In
terms of the **displacement** of air particles:

- a **closed end** is a **node** (air cannot move into the wall)
- an **open end** is an **antinode** (air moves freely)

With end corrections neglected, as the syllabus specifies:

| Tube | Allowed lengths | Lowest-frequency pattern |
|---|---|---|
| Closed at one end | L = λ/4, 3λ/4, 5λ/4 … | L = λ/4 |
| Open at both ends | L = λ/2, λ, 3λ/2 … | L = λ/2 |

**Worked example — closed tube.** A tube 0.32 m long is closed at one end.
The speed of sound is 340 m s⁻¹. Find the lowest resonant frequency and the
next one.

```
lowest: L = lambda / 4, so lambda = 4 x 0.32 = 1.28 m
f1 = v / lambda = 340 / 1.28 = 266 Hz
next:   L = 3 lambda / 4, so lambda = 0.427 m, f = 797 Hz (three times f1)
```

**Worked example — measuring the speed of sound.** A tube closed at one end
by a movable piston is held near a 512 Hz source. Loud resonances occur at
lengths 0.165 m and 0.497 m.

```
successive resonances are lambda / 2 apart:
lambda / 2 = 0.497 - 0.165 = 0.332 m,   lambda = 0.664 m
v = f lambda = 512 x 0.664 = 340 m/s
```

Using the *difference* between two resonance lengths is the natural method,
because both ends of that interval are at the same kind of point in the
pattern.

## Exam technique

- **Explain how a stationary wave forms**: name the two waves (same
frequency, opposite directions, usually incident and reflected), state that
they superpose, and say that nodes are where they always cancel.
- **Graphical method**: draw the component waves at a stated instant, then
the resultant; repeat a quarter-period later.
- **Determine λ**: show which gaps you counted and remember the factor of 2.
- **Air columns**: state that you are describing particle displacement when
you place nodes and antinodes.

## Common mistakes

- Taking the node-to-node distance as λ instead of λ/2.
- Counting nodes instead of the gaps between them.
- Putting an antinode at the closed end of a tube.
- Saying all points in a stationary wave have the same amplitude, or that
phase changes steadily along it.
- Saying a stationary wave transfers energy along its length.

## Quick revision checklist

- Principle of superposition: resultant displacement is the sum
- Stationary wave from two waves of equal frequency in opposite directions
- Graphical method at quarter-period intervals; nodes and antinodes
- Node–node = antinode–antinode = λ/2; node–antinode = λ/4
- Microwaves with a reflector and probe; strings fixed at both ends; air
columns with closed (node) and open (antinode) ends
- Phase: in phase within a segment, antiphase across a node

## Frequently asked questions

**Why is a closed end of an air column a node?**
The air particles at a solid surface cannot move along the tube, so their
displacement is always zero.

**How far apart are adjacent nodes?**
Half a wavelength. Adjacent antinodes are also half a wavelength apart.

**Why measure across several nodes?**
A longer distance has a smaller percentage uncertainty; dividing by the
number of intervals then gives λ/2 more precisely.
