## Periods, groups and blocks (3.1.1)

Rows are **periods**; columns are **groups**, numbered 1 to 18. The four **blocks** are named after the sublevel the highest-energy electron enters:

- **s-block**: groups 1 and 2 (plus helium)
- **p-block**: groups 13 to 18
- **d-block**: groups 3 to 12
- **f-block**: the lanthanoids and actinoids, usually printed below the main table

Metals fill the left and centre. Non-metals sit upper right, plus hydrogen. Between them runs a staircase of **metalloids**, usually boron, silicon, germanium, arsenic, antimony and tellurium, with properties between the two. The guide notes that the data booklet includes a periodic table.

## Position and electron configuration (3.1.2)

The **period number** is the outer occupied main energy level. Every element in a **group** has the same number of **valence electrons**. In groups 1 and 2 this equals the group number; in groups 13 to 18 take 10 away from the group number (helium, with 2, is the exception).

Know these names: **alkali metals** (group 1, not hydrogen), **halogens** (group 17), **noble gases** (group 18) and **transition elements** (in the d-block; see 3.1.8).

### Worked example 1: from position to configuration

Germanium is in period 4, group 14, in the p-block.

```
Period 4 -> outer level is n = 4
Group 14 -> 14 - 10 = 4 valence electrons: 4s² 4p²
Filling order up to it: 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ 4s² 3d¹⁰ 4p²
Condensed: [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s² 4p²   (check: 18 + 10 + 2 + 2 = 32 = Z)
```

### Worked example 2: from configuration to position

An atom has the configuration [Ne] 3s² 3p³.

```
Highest level occupied: n = 3 -> period 3
Last electron enters 3p -> p-block
Valence electrons: 2 + 3 = 5 -> group 15
Z = 10 + 5 = 15 -> phosphorus
```

Learn the two exceptions up to Z = 36: chromium [Ar] 3d⁵ 4s¹ and copper [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s¹.

## Periodicity: trends across and down (3.1.3)

**Periodicity** means trends in properties across a period and down a group. Two ideas explain them:

- **Across a period**, nuclear charge rises by one each step, but new electrons enter the same main level, so shielding barely changes. Outer electrons feel a stronger pull.
- **Down a group**, each element has one more occupied main level. Outer electrons are further away and more shielded, which outweighs the extra nuclear charge.

| Property | Across a period | Down a group |
|---|---|---|
| Atomic radius | Decreases | Increases |
| First ionization energy | Increases (with dips) | Decreases |
| Electron affinity | Generally more exothermic | Generally less exothermic |
| Electronegativity | Increases | Decreases |

**Electron affinity** is the energy change, per mole, when isolated gaseous atoms each gain an electron and become gaseous 1− ions. **Electronegativity** is an atom's ability to attract the shared pair in a covalent bond; fluorine's is highest.

**Ionic radius:**

- A positive ion is **smaller** than its atom: it has lost its outer main level.
- A negative ion is **larger** than its atom: extra electrons add repulsion with no extra nuclear charge.
- In an **isoelectronic** series (same electron count), the ion with the highest nuclear charge is smallest.

### Worked example 3: ranking ions

Rank S²⁻, Cl⁻, K⁺ and Ca²⁺ by radius. Each has 18 electrons; nuclear charges are 16, 17, 19 and 20. More protons pull the same electrons closer: **S²⁻ > Cl⁻ > K⁺ > Ca²⁺**.

## Group 1 and group 17 (3.1.4)

Metallic character **increases** down group 1. Non-metallic character **decreases** down group 17.

**Group 1 with water.** Every alkali metal reacts to give a hydroxide and hydrogen:

2M(s) + 2H₂O(l) → 2MOH(aq) + H₂(g)

The solution turns alkaline. Lithium fizzes steadily; sodium melts into a moving ball; potassium ignites the hydrogen with a lilac flame. Reactivity rises down the group: with each extra main level the outer electron sits further out and has more inner electrons between it and the nucleus, so it is lost more easily.

**Group 17 with halide ions.** A halogen takes electrons from the halide ion of any halogen **below** it, displacing it:

Cl₂(aq) + 2I⁻(aq) → 2Cl⁻(aq) + I₂(aq)

In water, chlorine is pale green, bromine orange and iodine brown. Reactivity falls down the group: larger atoms attract an incoming electron less strongly, because it is further from the nucleus and more shielded. So iodine gives no reaction with bromide ions.

## Oxides: from basic to acidic (3.1.5)

Properties form a **continuum** across a period: **basic** metal oxides, then **amphoteric** oxides such as Al₂O₃ (reacting with acids and bases), then **acidic** non-metal oxides.

| Oxide | Equation with water | Product |
|---|---|---|
| Na₂O (group 1) | Na₂O(s) + H₂O(l) → 2NaOH(aq) | Alkaline solution |
| CaO (group 2) | CaO(s) + H₂O(l) → Ca(OH)₂(aq) | Alkaline solution |
| CO₂ | CO₂(g) + H₂O(l) ⇌ H₂CO₃(aq) | Weakly acidic |
| SO₂ | SO₂(g) + H₂O(l) → H₂SO₃(aq) | Acidic |
| SO₃ | SO₃(g) + H₂O(l) → H₂SO₄(aq) | Strongly acidic |

**Acid rain.** Gaseous non-metal oxides from burning fuels dissolve in rain. SO₂ gives sulfurous acid, or is oxidized to SO₃, giving sulfuric acid. Nitrogen dioxide: 2NO₂(g) + H₂O(l) → HNO₃(aq) + HNO₂(aq).

**Ocean acidification.** As atmospheric CO₂ rises, more dissolves in seawater. Carbonic acid releases hydrogen ions, H₂CO₃(aq) ⇌ H⁺(aq) + HCO₃⁻(aq), so the pH falls. The extra H⁺ also reacts with carbonate ions, H⁺(aq) + CO₃²⁻(aq) → HCO₃⁻(aq), leaving less carbonate for shell-building organisms.

### Worked example 4

Strontium oxide is a group 2 oxide, so basic: SrO(s) + H₂O(l) → Sr(OH)₂(aq). Potassium oxide is basic too: K₂O(s) + H₂O(l) → 2KOH(aq).

## Oxidation states (3.1.6)

Imagine every bond in a compound were fully ionic: the charge an atom would then carry is its **oxidation state**. Write the sign first: +2, −1. "Oxidation number" is an accepted alternative term.

Rules, highest priority first:

1. An uncombined element is **0**: its atoms bond only to identical atoms, so no electrons transfer.
2. Oxidation states sum to 0 in a compound, or to the charge in an ion.
3. Group 1 is +1 and group 2 is +2. Fluorine is always −1.
4. Hydrogen is +1, but **−1** when bonded to a metal in a **metal hydride** (NaH, for example).
5. Oxygen is −2, except in **peroxides** such as H₂O₂, where it is **−1**.

**Naming oxyanions.** Roman numerals give the central atom's oxidation state: ClO₃⁻ is chlorate(V). Generic names are also accepted: NO₃⁻ nitrate, NO₂⁻ nitrite, SO₄²⁻ sulfate, SO₃²⁻ sulfite.

### Worked example 5

```
Fe in FeO₄²⁻:  x + 4(−2) = −2  -> x = +6   (ferrate(VI))
Al in LiAlH₄:  (+1) + x + 4(−1) = 0 -> x = +3   (H is −1, a metal hydride)
```

## HL only: discontinuities in first ionization energy (3.1.7)

First ionization energy rises across period 3 but dips twice. The dips are evidence for **sublevels**. Explain them by the **energy of the electron removed**, not "special stability".

- **Magnesium to aluminium.** Aluminium loses a 3p electron, which is higher in energy than magnesium's 3s electron, so less energy is needed.
- **Phosphorus to sulfur.** In sulfur two 3p electrons share an orbital. Their repulsion raises the energy of one, so it is removed more easily than any of phosphorus's unpaired 3p electrons.

If every electron in a main level had equal energy, the rise would be smooth.

## HL only: transition elements (3.1.8)

An **incomplete d-sublevel** gives transition elements these properties:

- **variable oxidation states**
- **high melting points** (both 3d and 4s electrons contribute to metallic bonding)
- **magnetic properties**, from unpaired d electrons (the types of magnetism are not assessed)
- **catalytic properties**, for example iron in ammonia synthesis
- **coloured compounds**
- **complex ions**, in which **ligands** donate lone pairs to the central ion

Zinc is not a transition element: the atom and Zn²⁺ both have a full 3d sublevel. Scandium is debated, because Sc³⁺ has no d electrons.

## HL only: variable oxidation states and ion configurations (3.1.9)

The 4s and 3d sublevels are close in energy, so successive ionization energies rise **gradually**, and several different numbers of electrons can be lost.

### Worked example 6: iron and strontium

Successive ionization energies in kJ mol⁻¹ (use these values):

```
Fe: 762, 1561, 2957, 5290
Sr: 549, 1064, 4138
```

Iron's increases (799, 1396, 2333) show no huge jump, so Fe²⁺ and Fe³⁺ both form. Strontium's third value is almost four times its second, as that electron comes from an inner main level: only Sr²⁺ forms.

**Ion configurations.** Remove the **4s electrons first**, then 3d.

```
Fe³⁺: Fe is [Ar] 3d⁶ 4s² -> remove 4s², then one 3d -> [Ar] 3d⁵
V³⁺:  V is [Ar] 3d³ 4s²  -> remove 4s², then one 3d -> [Ar] 3d²
Cu⁺:  Cu is [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s¹ -> remove 4s¹ -> [Ar] 3d¹⁰
```

## HL only: why complexes are coloured (3.1.10)

Ligands **split** the d-sublevel into two sets of orbitals. A d electron absorbs a photon matching the gap and is **promoted** to the higher set, removing that frequency from white light. The colour seen is **complementary** to the colour absorbed, opposite it on the colour wheel. Splitting patterns are not required. The guide says the data booklet supplies both the colour wheel and c = λf.

A simple colour wheel (approximate ranges; use the one you are given):

| Colour | Wavelength / nm | Complementary colour |
|---|---|---|
| Violet | 400–450 | Yellow |
| Blue | 450–490 | Orange |
| Green | 490–560 | Red |
| Yellow | 560–590 | Violet |
| Orange | 590–630 | Blue |
| Red | 630–700 | Green |

### Worked example 7

A complex ion absorbs most strongly at 505 nm. Deduce the colour seen and the frequency absorbed (c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹).

```
505 nm lies in the green range -> colour seen is red
f = c / λ = (3.00 × 10⁸) / (505 × 10⁻⁹) = 5.94 × 10¹⁴ s⁻¹
```

A d¹⁰ ion (Zn²⁺) or d⁰ ion (Sc³⁺) cannot undergo this promotion, so its compounds are usually colourless or white.

## Common errors

- Counting valence electrons for groups 13 to 18 as the group number, not the group number minus 10.
- Writing CO₂ + H₂O → H₂CO₃ as a complete, one-way reaction; it is an equilibrium.
- Giving oxygen −2 in a peroxide, or hydrogen +1 in a metal hydride.
- (HL) Removing 3d electrons before 4s when writing ion configurations.
- (HL) Giving the absorbed colour as the colour seen.
