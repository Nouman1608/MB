These questions test the section 3.3 row on private nuisance and the rule in *Rylands v Fletcher*.

---

## Section A

**1.** Define private nuisance. **[2]**

**2.** Explain who may bring a claim in private nuisance, with an authority. **[2]**

**3.** Explain why locality was not relevant in *St Helen's Smelting v Tipping* (1865). **[2]**

**4.** Explain the effect of malice on whether an interference is a nuisance, with **one** case. **[3]**

**5.** State the test for non-natural use given in *Transco v Stockport MBC* (2003). **[2]**

**6.** Explain why the claim failed in *Read v J Lyons* (1947). **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** Explain when an occupier is liable for a nuisance created by a trespasser or arising naturally on their land, using **two** cases. **[5]**

**8.** Explain how planning permission may affect a claim in private nuisance. **[4]**

**9.** Explain the decision in *Cambridge Water v Eastern Counties Leather* (1994) and its importance for the rule in *Rylands v Fletcher*. **[4]**

**10.** Rowan owns a house in a quiet residential street. His neighbour, Pip, sets up a dog-grooming business in her garage. Dogs bark from 7am to 7pm, six days a week. Rowan's partner, Sol, who lives with him but has no interest in the house, suffers headaches. Pip says she was given planning permission and that the business is useful to the community. Advise Rowan and Sol. **[8]**

**11.** Delta Ltd stores large tanks of liquid fertiliser on its farm. A tank bursts after a valve fails, and the fertiliser flows into a neighbour's fishpond, killing the fish. The neighbour, Fen, also slips on the fertiliser while trying to rescue the fish and breaks her wrist. Advise Fen under the rule in *Rylands v Fletcher*. **[7]**

**12.** Discuss whether the rule in *Rylands v Fletcher* still has a useful role in modern tort law. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** An unlawful interference with a person's use or enjoyment of land [1], or some right over or in connection with it [1].

**2.** Only a person with a proprietary interest in the land affected, such as an owner or tenant, may sue [1]: *Hunter v Canary Wharf* (1997) [1].

**3.** Fumes from a copper smelter damaged the claimant's trees and shrubs [1]. Where a nuisance causes physical damage to property, the character of the neighbourhood is irrelevant; locality matters only for interference with comfort and enjoyment [1].

**4.** If the defendant acts deliberately to harm the claimant, the interference is more likely to be unreasonable [1], even if the same activity done innocently would not be [1]. In *Christie v Davey* (1893) a neighbour who banged on the wall and shouted to disrupt music lessons was liable [1]. (Credit *Hollywood Silver Fox Farm*.)

**5.** An extraordinary and unusual use of land [1] that creates an exceptionally high risk of danger or mischief if there is an escape [1].

**6.** A shell exploded inside a munitions factory and injured an inspector [1]. There was no escape of the thing from the defendant's premises to outside, so *Rylands* did not apply [1].

**7.** An occupier who did not create a nuisance can be liable if they know or ought to know of it and fail to take reasonable steps to end it [1]. In *Sedleigh-Denfield v O'Callaghan* (1940) a trespasser laid a pipe on the defendant's land [1]; the defendant knew of it and used it, so it adopted and continued the nuisance when the pipe blocked and flooded the neighbour [1]. In *Leakey v National Trust* (1980) soil and rocks from a natural mound fell onto neighbouring houses [1]; the occupier knew of the risk and was liable for failing to take reasonable steps [1].

**8.** Planning permission is not a defence to private nuisance [1]. However, it may change the character of the locality, which affects what is reasonable [1]: in *Gillingham BC v Medway Dock* (1993) conversion of a dockyard into a commercial port changed the locality, so lorry noise was not a nuisance [1]. In *Wheeler v JJ Saunders* (1996) permission for pig units did not change the locality, and in *Coventry v Lawrence* (2014) the Supreme Court said planning permission is generally of limited relevance to liability [1].

**9.** A tannery used a solvent that seeped through the ground and contaminated the claimant's borehole over a mile away [1]. Storage of chemicals was a classic case of non-natural use [1], but the claim failed because damage of that type was not reasonably foreseeable at the time [1]. The case established that foreseeability of the type of damage is required, linking *Rylands* closely to nuisance [1].

**10.** **Rowan:** he owns the house, so he has a proprietary interest and can sue [1]. Barking from 7am to 7pm, six days a week, is a continuing interference [1]. In a quiet residential street, the locality factor favours Rowan (*Sturges v Bridgman*) [1], although daytime hours are less serious than night-time noise (*Halsey*) [1]. Planning permission is not a defence, and a small grooming business is unlikely to change the character of the street (*Wheeler*) [1]. Public benefit is not a defence to liability, though it may affect the remedy (*Miller v Jackson*) [1]. Conclusion: Rowan has a good claim [1]. **Sol:** no interest in the land, so cannot sue in private nuisance (*Hunter*) [1].

**11.** Delta brought the fertiliser onto its land and accumulated it in tanks [1]. Large quantities of liquid fertiliser are likely to do mischief if they escape [1]. Bulk storage of chemicals is arguably an extraordinary use creating an exceptionally high risk, even on a farm (*Cambridge Water*; *Transco*) [1]. The fertiliser escaped onto Fen's land [1], and killing fish in a pond is a foreseeable type of damage [1]. Delta is liable without proof of fault, and the valve failure is no defence [1]. Fen cannot recover for her broken wrist under *Rylands*, since personal injury is not recoverable (*Transco*), and would need to sue in negligence [1].

**12.** *Rylands* imposes strict liability for escapes of dangerous things [1], which protects neighbours who cannot prove fault [1]. However, *Transco* (2003) narrowed it: the use must be extraordinary and create exceptionally high risk, so few cases succeed [1]. *Cambridge Water* required foreseeability, bringing it close to nuisance and negligence [1]. Personal injury is excluded, and much hazardous activity is now regulated by statute [1]. Conclusion: the rule has a small but real role for one-off escapes from high-risk activities, which is why the House of Lords in *Transco* chose to keep it rather than abolish it [1].

---

## Where marks are usually lost

- Allowing someone with no interest in the land to sue.
- Treating planning permission or public benefit as a defence.
- Including personal injury in a *Rylands* claim.
- Skipping the escape element.
