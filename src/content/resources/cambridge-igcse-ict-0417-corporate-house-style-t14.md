Topic 14 of Cambridge IGCSE ICT 0417 lists one statement under "know and understand": the **purpose and uses of a corporate house style**. It sits beside the practical skill of creating, editing and applying styles. In the theory paper you may be asked to explain why an organisation has a house style or where it would be used; in the practical papers you are often handed a short set of house-style rules and must follow every one. This guide goes deeper on that single statement. For styles in general, read the [Styles study guide](/resources/cambridge-igcse-ict-styles-study-guide/) first.

## What a house style is

A **corporate house style** is the agreed set of presentation rules that an organisation applies to everything it produces, so that every item is recognisably from the same source. The rules are usually written down in a **style guide** (sometimes called brand guidelines) and then built into files that staff use every day.

The rules cover appearance and also wording. A house style might state that the company name is always written in full in the first mention, that dates are written as 3 March 2027 and never as 03/03/27, or that every letter ends with the same sign-off. Wording rules matter because inconsistent spelling of the organisation's own name looks careless.

## The purpose of a corporate house style

Each purpose below is a separate point in an exam answer. Learn the reason behind each one, not just the label.

- **Consistency across people and time.** A large organisation has many staff writing documents. Without shared rules, each person chooses their own fonts and layouts. A house style means a report written by one department looks the same as a leaflet from another, this year and next year.
- **Recognition (corporate identity).** Repeating the same logo, colours and fonts makes the organisation instantly identifiable. A customer who sees the colour scheme on a website, an invoice and a delivery note knows they all come from the same company.
- **Professional image and trust.** Consistent, well-designed documents suggest an organisation that is organised and reliable. Mixed fonts and logos of different sizes suggest the opposite.
- **Saving time.** Staff do not have to make design decisions. They open a template that already has the styles, logo and layout, and simply add content.
- **Reducing errors.** Standard wording, such as the registered address or a legal notice in the footer, is stored once in the template, so it is not retyped (and mistyped) on every document.
- **Readers find information quickly.** If the reference number is always in the same place on every letter, a customer or a member of staff knows exactly where to look.
- **Distinguishing the organisation from competitors**, because its documents do not look like anyone else's.

## Where a house style is used

The syllabus asks for the **uses** as well as the purpose. A house style applies to anything the organisation produces, printed or on screen:

- letterheads, letters, compliment slips and envelopes;
- invoices, receipts, order forms and delivery notes;
- business cards and staff identity badges;
- reports, newsletters, leaflets, posters and adverts;
- presentations shown to customers or staff;
- the website, emails and email signatures;
- social media posts and online adverts.

In ICT terms, each type of software has its own way of storing the rules, so the house style is set up once and reused.

| Document type | Where the house style is stored | What it holds |
|---|---|---|
| Word-processed letter or report | A template with named styles | Fonts, sizes, colours, spacing, margins, logo in the header, standard footer |
| Presentation | The master slide | Background colour, logo position, heading and bullet formats, slide footer and numbering |
| Web page | An external stylesheet | Fonts, colours, spacing and layout for every page of the site |
| Spreadsheet or chart | A template workbook | Fonts, cell styles and the colours used for chart series |
| Database report | A saved report layout | Title format, header and footer content, fonts and alignment |

The same idea runs through Topics 14, 19 and 21: define the formatting once, store it centrally and apply it everywhere.

## What the rules usually specify

| Element | Example rule (from an invented style guide) |
|---|---|
| Logo | Top right of page 1 only, 3 cm wide, never stretched or recoloured |
| Colours | Navy for headings, grey for body text, orange only for highlights |
| Fonts | One sans-serif font for headings and one for body text, with set point sizes |
| Layout | Margins of 2.5 cm, body text left aligned, 6 pt after each paragraph |
| Header and footer | Company name in the header; page number centred in the footer; registered address on the left of the footer |
| Wording | Organisation name in full at first use; dates written as day, month in words, year |
| Images and charts | Photographs with a thin navy border; chart bars in the company colours |

## Worked example: auditing a draft against a house style

*An invented company, Brookfield Garden Supplies, has this house style for letters:* logo top right; headings Verdana 14 pt bold, navy; body text Verdana 11 pt, left aligned, single line spacing with 6 pt after paragraphs; footer containing the registered address, left aligned, in 8 pt; dates written in full, for example 12 April 2027.

A new member of staff produces a draft letter. Checking it against the rules, line by line:

1. The logo is top left. *Departure: move it to the top right.*
2. The heading is Verdana 14 pt bold, but black. *Departure: change the colour to navy, preferably by editing the heading style so that every heading matches.*
3. The body text is Verdana 11 pt but fully justified. *Departure: change to left aligned.*
4. The date reads 12/04/27. *Departure: rewrite as 12 April 2027.*
5. The address in the footer has been typed in 10 pt. *Departure: 8 pt is required. Better still, start from the company template, where the footer is already correct.*

Five departures were found. The last point shows the real lesson: most of the errors would not have happened if the template had been used, which is why organisations store the house style in templates rather than relying on staff to remember the rules.

## Annotated model answer

*Question: Explain why a company uses a corporate house style. [4]*

> All of the company's documents look the same whoever produces them, **(point)** so customers recognise the company's letters, website and leaflets as coming from one organisation. **(developed with recognition)** A consistent appearance makes the company look professional, which helps customers trust it. **(point and reason)** Staff save time because the styles and logo are already set up in a template, so they do not have to design each document. **(point and reason)**

*Why it scores:* it makes several different points and gives a reason for each one. A weaker answer would list "consistency, professional, saves time, recognition" with no reasons, which may limit the marks on an *explain* question.

## Drawbacks

A house style also has costs, which you may need for a *discuss* or *evaluate* question:

- Designing the style and building the templates takes time and money at the start.
- Staff must be trained to use the templates and to follow the rules.
- It limits individual creativity: a designer cannot change the look of a leaflet to suit one event.
- When the organisation changes its logo or colours, every template, master slide, stylesheet and stock of printed stationery must be updated.

## Common mistakes

- Confusing a house style with a **style** in the software. A style is one saved set of formatting; a house style is a whole set of organisation-wide rules, carried out using styles, templates, master slides and stylesheets.
- Giving "it looks nice" as a purpose. Link the point to recognition, professionalism, time or accuracy.
- Writing only about printed documents. Websites, presentations and emails are uses too.
- In practical tasks, following most of the rules but missing one small detail, such as the size of the footer text or the logo position.
- Formatting by hand to imitate the house style when the task provides a template or styles to apply.

## Exam technique

**State** or **identify** needs a brief fact, for example "logo". **Describe** needs what the feature is or does, for example "the logo appears in the same position on every document". **Explain** needs a reason, usually introduced by *so that* or *because*. If the question names an organisation, such as a bank or a school, refer to it and to its documents in every point. In the practical papers, read the house-style instructions before you start, and check them off one at a time when you finish.

## Check your understanding

1. What is a style guide? *The written document that sets out an organisation's house-style rules.*
2. Give two purposes of a corporate house style. *Recognition of the organisation, and a consistent, professional image (also saving time, fewer errors).*
3. Where is a house style stored for a presentation? *On the master slide.*
4. Where is it stored for a website? *In an external stylesheet.*
5. Why does using a template reduce errors? *Standard content such as the address and legal notice is stored once and not retyped.*
6. Give one drawback of a house style. *It takes time and money to set up, or it limits creativity, or it must all be updated if the brand changes.*
