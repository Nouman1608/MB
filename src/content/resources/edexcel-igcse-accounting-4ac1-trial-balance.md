## Purpose

A trial balance is a list of the balances on every ledger account at one date, with debit balances in one column and credit balances in the other. Its purposes are:

- **Checking arithmetical accuracy.** Every transaction has equal debits and credits, so the totals should agree. If they do not, at least one error has been made.
- **A basis for the financial statements.** The income statement and statement of financial position are prepared from its figures.
- **Helping to locate errors.** The amount of any difference gives clues about what went wrong.

## Step 1: balancing the ledger accounts

Before a trial balance can be prepared, each account is balanced: add both sides, find the difference, carry it down on the smaller side and bring it down on the larger side. The brought-down balance is the figure that goes into the trial balance.

### Worked example 1: three accounts

| Account | Debit entries | Credit entries | Balance for the trial balance |
|---|---|---|---|
| Office equipment | Bank 3,400; Kerr Supplies 1,250 | none | Debit 4,650 |
| Wages | Bank 2,100; 2,250; 2,180 | none | Debit 6,530 |
| L. Grey (supplier) | Bank 1,800; Purchases returns 120 | Purchases 1,860; Purchases 940 | Credit 880 |

L. Grey: credits 1,860 + 940 = 2,800; debits 1,800 + 120 = 1,920; balance 2,800 − 1,920 = 880 on the credit side, because the business still owes the supplier.

## Step 2: placing each balance

The mnemonic **DEAD CLIC** helps: **D**ebits are **E**xpenses, **A**ssets and **D**rawings; **C**redits are **L**iabilities, **I**ncome and **C**apital.

Items that need care:

| Balance | Column | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Opening inventory | Debit | An asset at the start of the year; closing inventory is not in the trial balance unless stated |
| Sales returns (returns inwards) | Debit | Reduces income |
| Purchases returns (returns outwards) | Credit | Reduces an expense |
| Carriage inwards and carriage outwards | Debit | Both are expenses |
| Discount allowed | Debit | Expense |
| Discount received | Credit | Income |
| Provision for depreciation | Credit | Reduces an asset; held as a credit balance |
| Provision for irrecoverable debts | Credit | Reduces trade receivables |
| Bank overdraft | Credit | Owed to the bank |
| Drawings | Debit | Reduces capital, but the account has a debit balance |

The debit balances total 219,800. The credit balances other than capital total 160,925. Capital is the figure that makes the columns agree: 219,800 − 160,925 = **58,875**.

Two balances are often misplaced here. Irrecoverable debts (the expense, 410) is a debit; the provision for irrecoverable debts (395) is a credit. They are different accounts and both appear.

## Limitations

A trial balance that agrees proves only that total debits equal total credits. It does **not** prove that the entries are correct. Errors that leave the totals equal include:

- **Omission**: a transaction left out entirely.
- **Commission**: correct amount, correct type of account, wrong account (two customers mixed up).
- **Principle**: correct amount, wrong class of account (a non-current asset charged as an expense).
- **Original entry**: the wrong amount used on both sides from the start.
- **Complete reversal**: the right accounts, but each on the wrong side.
- **Compensating errors**: two errors of equal size that cancel out.

The 4AC1 specification does not name these types, so in an answer describe what happened as well as giving the name.

Other limitations to understand:

- It shows balances at one date only, not the movements during the period.
- It does not check that amounts are realistic, such as whether a provision is adequate.
- Adjustments made at the year end, such as other payables, may not yet be included.

## Using the difference to locate errors

When the totals disagree, the difference itself is a clue.

| Test | What it suggests | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Is there a single balance equal to the difference? | A balance has been left out of the trial balance | Difference 1,860: insurance omitted |
| Is there a balance equal to half the difference? | A balance has been put in the wrong column | Difference 960: a 480 debit entered as a credit |
| Is the difference divisible by 9? | Two digits may have been transposed | 1,290 written as 1,920: difference 630 = 9 × 70 |

These are search tools, not proofs. Once the error is found, it is corrected through the journal, and a suspense account may hold the difference meanwhile.

### Worked example 3: a trial balance that does not agree

Two trainees prepare the Hartwell Books trial balance and each gets totals that disagree.

- **Trainee A**: debits 219,320, credits 219,800. The debit column is 480 short. Carriage outwards is a debit balance of exactly 480, and it is missing from the list. Adding it gives 219,800 in both columns.
- **Trainee B**: debits 219,070, credits 220,530. The difference is 1,460, and half of it is 730, the discount allowed balance. It has been entered in the credit column. Moving it reduces the credits by 730 and raises the debits by 730, giving 219,800 in both columns.

The lesson is method: test the difference first, then trace the account it points to, rather than re-adding every figure.

## Exam technique

- **Explain the purpose**: give at least two purposes with a reason each.
- **Prepare**: give a heading with the business name and date ("Trial balance at 31 December 2027"), list every account once, put each in one column, and total both columns on the same line.
- **Limitations**: name the error, describe it with a short example and say why the totals still agree.

## Common errors

- Putting closing inventory in a trial balance that should show opening inventory.
- Placing drawings or provisions in the wrong column.
- Treating carriage inwards as income.
- Listing the same account twice, or showing both the individual entries and the balance.
- Writing "the trial balance proves the books are correct".
- Leaving out the date in the heading: a trial balance is at a date, not for a year.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why is a trial balance headed "at" a date rather than "for the year"?**
It lists balances as they stand on one day, so it is a snapshot at a date. The income statement, by contrast, covers a period.

**Where do provisions go in a trial balance?**
Both the provision for depreciation and the provision for irrecoverable debts have credit balances, so they go in the credit column.

**Does an agreed trial balance mean there are no errors?**
No. Errors such as omission, commission, principle, original entry, complete reversal and compensating errors leave the totals equal.
