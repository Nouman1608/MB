This guide covers **bias**, one of the ideas the IB DP Psychology course (first assessment 2019) uses to judge the quality of research, alongside validity, credibility and generalisation. At HL, the third question on Paper 3 asks you to discuss one of three issues for an unseen study, and one of them is how the researcher could **avoid bias**. The [approaches to researching behaviour study guide](/resources/ib-ib-dp-psychology-approaches-to-researching-behaviour-study-guide/) introduces researcher and participant bias in a sentence each; this page names the main types, explains where each comes from and how each is reduced, and shows how to build a Paper 3 answer. The course described here has its last examination session in November 2026.

## What bias means

**Bias** is any systematic influence that pushes results away from what is actually true about the participants or the behaviour. "Systematic" is the important word: a random error makes results less precise, while a bias makes them consistently wrong in one direction. Bias can come from the participants, from the researcher, or from the way the study is designed, and it threatens validity in quantitative research and credibility in qualitative research.

A Paper 3 answer must apply this to the study in front of you. A list of every bias you know, with no link to the study, is the most common reason for a low mark.

## Participant bias

| Type | What happens | How to reduce it |
|---|---|---|
| Demand characteristics | participants guess the aim and change their behaviour to fit (or spoil) it | a cover story or mild deception, approved by an ethics committee; independent samples so each person sees one condition; a debrief afterwards |
| Social desirability | participants present themselves in a favourable light, especially on sensitive topics | anonymity and confidentiality, stated clearly; indirect or neutral questions; building rapport before sensitive topics |
| Acquiescence | a tendency to agree with statements or questions, whatever they say | balanced questions that do not all point the same way; open questions rather than yes/no |
| Dominant respondent | in a focus group, one confident person steers the discussion and others follow | a trained moderator who invites quieter members; smaller groups; individual interviews as well |
| Sensitivity bias | participants are reluctant to discuss a topic they find embarrassing or painful | rapport, a skilled interviewer, the right to skip questions, a private setting |
| Placebo effect | expectations of a treatment produce a change in themselves | a placebo control group and single- or double-blind procedures |

## Researcher bias

| Type | What happens | How to reduce it |
|---|---|---|
| Confirmation bias | the researcher notices, records or interprets data in ways that fit the hypothesis | reflexivity; a second researcher analysing the data independently (researcher triangulation); recording and transcribing interviews |
| Expectancy effects | the researcher's expectations subtly change how participants behave | double-blind procedures; standardised instructions read from a script |
| Leading questions | the wording of a question suggests the answer | neutral, open-ended questions; piloting the interview schedule |
| Question order | early questions shape answers to later ones | moving from general to specific questions; varying the order |
| Sampling bias | the way participants are chosen makes the sample unrepresentative | random or stratified sampling where possible; a clear rationale for purposive sampling |
| Biased reporting | some findings are reported and others left out | reporting all themes, including those that contradict expectations; supporting themes with quotations |
| Cultural bias | the researcher interprets behaviour through the norms of their own culture | an emic approach; involving researchers from the participants' culture; reflexivity |

## Reflexivity: the key qualitative tool

**Reflexivity** is the researcher's deliberate reflection on how they themselves may influence the research. Two kinds are often distinguished:

- **Personal reflexivity**: how the researcher's own background, beliefs and experiences may shape what they ask and how they interpret answers. A researcher who was once a nurse, studying nurses' stress, might over-identify with participants.
- **Epistemological reflexivity**: how the design itself, such as the choice of method or the wording of questions, may have shaped the findings. Would a focus group have produced different themes from individual interviews?

Reflexivity is often recorded in a research diary. It does not remove bias, but it makes possible bias visible so that others can judge the findings.

## Quantitative and qualitative contrasts

In **experiments**, bias is mostly controlled through the design: random allocation, standardised procedures, blind conditions and control groups. Because data are numerical and procedures fixed, bias is easier to detect and limit.

In **qualitative research**, the researcher is the instrument: they ask the questions, choose follow-ups, select quotations and identify themes. Bias cannot be designed out, so the strategies are about transparency and checking: reflexivity, triangulation, recording, rich description and careful wording. A strong Paper 3 answer recognises which kind of study it is dealing with and chooses strategies to match.

## Worked example: an HL Paper 3 answer

The study summary below is original, written for this guide.

> A researcher wanted to explore how teachers experience stress during school inspections. She
> recruited eight secondary teachers through a teachers' online forum. She was herself a former teacher.
> Each teacher took part in a 45-minute semi-structured interview in the school staff room during the
> week of an inspection. Interviews were recorded and transcribed, and the researcher carried out a
> thematic analysis. Three themes emerged: loss of control, pressure from leadership and support from
> colleagues.

**Question:** Discuss how the researcher in the study could avoid bias. **[9]**

**Plan and model answer points:**

1. **Identify the risk of participant bias.** Interviews took place in the staff room during the inspection week, where colleagues or managers might overhear. Teachers may have given socially desirable answers, playing down criticism of leaders. *Strategy:* interview off site or in a private room, and stress confidentiality and anonymity at the start. *Weigh it:* a private setting reduces social desirability but cannot remove a teacher's wish to appear professional.
2. **Identify sensitivity bias.** Stress can be a painful subject. *Strategy:* begin with general questions, build rapport and remind participants of the right to skip questions. *Weigh it:* this may produce fuller answers, but it lengthens interviews.
3. **Identify researcher bias from her background.** As a former teacher, she may have expected stress to come from leadership and asked follow-up questions that led towards that theme (confirmation bias). *Strategy:* keep a reflexive diary recording her expectations; pilot the interview schedule; have a second researcher, without a teaching background, code the transcripts independently and compare themes.
4. **Identify bias from sampling.** Teachers who join an online forum and volunteer may hold stronger views than other teachers. *Strategy:* recruit through several schools as well, using purposive sampling to include teachers of different experience. Note that this partly concerns generalisation; tie it back to bias by explaining that a self-selected sample may push the themes in one direction.
5. **Conclude** with a judgement: the most serious risk is researcher bias, because she alone analysed the data, so researcher triangulation and reflexivity are the most important steps.

Answers in the top band discuss several relevant biases, apply each to the study, propose a realistic strategy and comment on how far the strategy would work.

## Common mistakes

- Writing about generalisation or ethics when the question asks about bias.
- Listing biases without saying where in this study they could arise.
- Suggesting double-blind procedures for an interview study, where they make little sense.
- Treating reflexivity as a way to remove bias rather than to make it visible.
- Proposing changes that would turn a qualitative study into an experiment, which changes the aim.

## Exam technique

**Discuss** in Paper 3 needs a range of points, each linked to the study, with some weighing of how effective each strategy would be. Read the summary twice and annotate where participants, the researcher and the design could each introduce bias. Use the study's details: the setting, the recruitment method, the researcher's background and the analysis. Quote or refer to them in every paragraph.
