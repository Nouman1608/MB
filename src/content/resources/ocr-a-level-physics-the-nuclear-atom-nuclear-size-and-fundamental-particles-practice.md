## Questions

**1.** A neutral atom of argon-36 is written ³⁶₁₈Ar.
**(a)** Give the numbers of protons, neutrons and electrons in this atom. **[3]**
**(b)** Argon-38 is an isotope of argon-36. State what is the same and what is different about the two nuclei. **[2]**

**2.** An atom has a radius of 1.3 × 10⁻¹⁰ m and its nucleus has a radius of 4.2 × 10⁻¹⁵ m.
**(a)** Calculate the ratio of the atomic radius to the nuclear radius. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the fraction of the volume of the atom occupied by the nucleus. **[2]**
**(c)** State what your answer suggests about the structure of an atom. **[1]**

**3.** Describe the alpha-particle scattering experiment and explain how its results provide evidence for a small, charged nucleus. **[6]**

**4.** The strong nuclear force acts between nucleons.
**(a)** State the separations over which it is attractive and the separations over which it is repulsive. **[2]**
**(b)** State why the repulsive part of the force is needed. **[1]**
**(c)** Protons in a nucleus repel one another electrostatically. Explain why the nucleus does not fly apart. **[1]**
**(d)** Explain why a nucleon at one edge of a large nucleus is hardly affected by a nucleon at the opposite edge. **[1]**

**5.** Take r₀ = 1.25 fm, where 1 fm = 10⁻¹⁵ m.
**(a)** Calculate the radius of a gold-197 nucleus. **[2]**
**(b)** A nucleus has a radius of 5.5 fm. Calculate its nucleon number. **[2]**
**(c)** Radii R of several nuclei are plotted against A^(1/3). State the shape of the graph and what its gradient represents. **[2]**

**6.** Use r₀ = 1.25 fm, u = 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷ kg, and for a hydrogen atom a mass of 1.67 × 10⁻²⁷ kg and a radius of 5.3 × 10⁻¹¹ m.
**(a)** Show that the density of nuclear matter does not depend on the nucleon number A. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate the density of nuclear matter. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the ratio of this density to the mean density of the hydrogen atom. **[2]**

**7.** **(a)** State the antiparticle of the electron, giving one way in which it is the same and one way in which it is different. **[3]**
**(b)** A neutron and an antineutron are both uncharged. State, in terms of quarks, how they differ. **[1]**

**8.** The particles listed are an electron, a proton, a neutrino and a neutron.
**(a)** State which are hadrons. **[1]**
**(b)** State which are leptons. **[1]**
**(c)** State the force that hadrons feel but leptons do not. **[1]**
**(d)** State a force felt by both hadrons and leptons. **[1]**

**9.** The charge on an up quark is +2/3 e and on a down quark −1/3 e. A strange quark has the same charge as a down quark.
**(a)** State the quark composition of the antiproton and show that its charge is −e. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the charge of a particle made of a down quark and an anti-up quark. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the charge of a particle made of an up quark and an anti-strange quark. **[1]**
**(d)** Explain why a particle made of two up quarks and an anti-down quark would not be a possible hadron. **[1]**

**10.** **(a)** Write down the quark-level equation for beta-minus decay and show that charge is conserved. **[3]**
**(b)** Write down the quark-level equation for beta-plus decay. **[1]**
**(c)** State the change in the proton number of a nucleus in beta-plus decay and name the fundamental force responsible. **[2]**

**11.** The sigma-minus particle has quark composition dds. One of its decay modes produces a neutron (udd), an electron and an antineutrino.
**(a)** Calculate the charge of the sigma-minus particle in units of e. **[1]**
**(b)** State which quark changes and write the quark transformation. **[2]**
**(c)** Show that charge is conserved in the decay. **[2]**
**(d)** State why the neutral particle emitted is an antineutrino and not a neutrino. **[1]**
**(e)** Name the force responsible. **[1]**
**(f)** State what this decay shows about which forces the sigma-minus particle and the electron feel. **[1]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** Protons = 18 [1]. Neutrons = 36 − 18 = 18 [1]. Electrons = 18, because the atom is neutral [1] **[3]**
**(b)** Same number of protons (18) [1]. Argon-38 has 20 neutrons against 18, so a different nucleon number [1] **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* "Same element" is weaker than "same number of protons"; say the number, and give the neutron numbers to show the difference.

**2. (a)** 1.3 × 10⁻¹⁰ / 4.2 × 10⁻¹⁵ = **3.1 × 10⁴** [1] **[1]**
**(b)** Volume fraction = (4.2 × 10⁻¹⁵ / 1.3 × 10⁻¹⁰)³ [1] = **3.4 × 10⁻¹⁴** [1] **[2]**
**(c)** The atom is mostly empty space [1] **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Volume goes as radius cubed, so cube the ratio. Squaring it, or quoting the radius ratio, answers a different question.

**3.** A narrow beam of alpha particles is fired at a thin metal foil, such as gold, in a vacuum [1]. A detector is moved round to count particles at different angles [1]. Most particles go straight on, so the atom is largely empty space [1]. A small fraction turn through large angles, because the positive alpha particles are repelled by positive charge concentrated in one place [1]. A very few rebound by more than 90°, so the charge is in a very small region that is also massive [1]. Positive charge spread through the whole atom could only give small deflections, so there must be a small, charged nucleus [1] **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Pair each observation with its own conclusion. A list of observations followed by one general sentence leaves the reasoning for the examiner to supply.

**4. (a)** Attractive from about 0.5 fm to about 3 fm [1]. Repulsive below about 0.5 fm [1] **[2]**
**(b)** It stops the nucleons being pulled together into a single point (the nucleus would collapse) [1] **[1]**
**(c)** At these separations the attractive strong force is larger than the electrostatic repulsion [1] **[1]**
**(d)** The strong force has a short range, so it is negligible beyond a few femtometres [1] **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Quote the numbers with the unit, fm. "Short range" alone does not say how short.

**5. (a)** R = r₀A^(1/3) = 1.25 × 197^(1/3) [1] = 1.25 × 5.82 = **7.3 fm** [1] **[2]**
**(b)** A^(1/3) = 5.5 / 1.25 = 4.4 [1]. A = 4.4³ = 85.2, so **A = 85** [1] **[2]**
**(c)** A straight line through the origin [1]. The gradient is r₀ [1] **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the cube root step. A common slip in (b) is to divide R by r₀ and stop, forgetting to cube.

**6. (a)** Mass of nucleus ≈ A × u [1]. Volume = (4/3)πR³ = (4/3)π r₀³A [1]. A cancels in mass/volume, giving 3u / (4π r₀³), which is constant [1] **[3]**
**(b)** ρ = 3 × 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷ / (4π × (1.25 × 10⁻¹⁵)³) [1] = **2.0 × 10¹⁷ kg m⁻³** [1] **[2]**
**(c)** Hydrogen atom density = 1.67 × 10⁻²⁷ / ((4/3)π(5.3 × 10⁻¹¹)³) = 2.7 × 10³ kg m⁻³ [1]. Ratio = 2.0 × 10¹⁷ / 2.7 × 10³ = **7.6 × 10¹³** [1] **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A "show that" answer needs the cancellation written out. Stating that density is constant without the algebra is a conclusion rather than a demonstration.

**7. (a)** The positron [1]. Same mass [1]. Opposite charge, +e rather than −e [1] **[3]**
**(b)** Neutron: udd. Antineutron: ū d̄ d̄ (three antiquarks) [1] **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* "Opposite charge" cannot be the answer for a neutral pair; the difference is in the antiquarks.

**8. (a)** Proton and neutron [1] **[1]**
**(b)** Electron and neutrino [1] **[1]**
**(c)** The strong nuclear force [1] **[1]**
**(d)** The weak nuclear force [1] **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* If asked for a force felt by leptons, name the weak force. The electromagnetic force is also felt by charged particles but is not what the specification classification is based on.

**9. (a)** Antiproton = ū ū d̄ [1]. Charge = −2/3 − 2/3 + 1/3 = **−1 e** [1] **[2]**
**(b)** d ū: −1/3 − 2/3 = **−1 e** [1] **[1]**
**(c)** u s̄: +2/3 + 1/3 = **+1 e** [1] **[1]**
**(d)** u u d̄ has charge 2/3 + 2/3 + 1/3 = 5/3 e, which is not a whole number of e [1] **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Write each quark's charge before adding. The usual error is giving an antiquark the same sign as its quark.

**10. (a)** d → u + e⁻ + ν̄ [1]. Left: −1/3 [1]. Right: +2/3 − 1 + 0 = −1/3, so charge is conserved [1] **[3]**
**(b)** u → d + e⁺ + ν [1] **[1]**
**(c)** The proton number decreases by 1 [1]. The weak nuclear force [1] **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Beta-minus emits an antineutrino and beta-plus emits a neutrino. Learn the pair, since charge balance cannot tell them apart.

**11. (a)** Charge = −1/3 − 1/3 − 1/3 = **−1 e** [1] **[1]**
**(b)** The strange quark changes to an up quark [1]. dds → udd + e⁻ + ν̄ (that is, s → u + e⁻ + ν̄) [1] **[2]**
**(c)** Before: −1 e [1]. After: neutron 0, electron −1, antineutrino 0, total −1 e, so charge is conserved [1] **[2]**
**(d)** An electron is emitted, as in beta-minus decay, and beta-minus decay emits an antineutrino [1] **[1]**
**(e)** The weak nuclear force [1] **[1]**
**(f)** The sigma-minus particle is a hadron, so it feels the strong and weak forces, while the electron is a lepton and feels the weak force but not the strong force [1] **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* In (b) name the quark that changes and give its equation, and leave the other two quarks out of the change. Quoting only the whole-particle equation does not show which quark is responsible.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing alpha scattering without saying why a vacuum or a thin foil is used, or without tying each observation to a conclusion.
- Saying "the nucleus is small" but not "charged" or "massive".
- Using R = r₀A instead of the cube root, or reading R off in metres when fm is asked for.
- Squaring instead of cubing the radius ratio when comparing volumes.
- Claiming the strong force is attractive at every separation, or leaving out the numbers 0.5 fm and 3 fm.
- Stating that nuclear density increases with A.
- Giving an antiquark the same sign of charge as its quark.
- Putting a neutrino in beta-minus decay and an antineutrino in beta-plus decay.
- Adding charges without writing the left and right totals side by side.
