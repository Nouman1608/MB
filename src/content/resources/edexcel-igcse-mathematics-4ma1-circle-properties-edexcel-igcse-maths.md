This study guide goes deeper into section 4.6, Circle properties, of topic 4 (Geometry and trigonometry) in the Pearson Edexcel International GCSE Mathematics A (4MA1) specification, Issue 2 (November 2017). Section 4.6 has Foundation Tier content, which both tiers need, and further content marked **Higher tier only** below. The specification states that formal proof of the circle theorems is not required: you must know them, use them and name them as reasons.

The [Geometry and trigonometry study guide](/resources/edexcel-igcse-maths-4ma1-geometry-and-trigonometry/) lists the facts. This page shows how to spot which fact a diagram needs, works through multi-step examples, and covers the cases that trip people up, such as reflex angles at the centre. Follow up with the [Circle properties revision notes](/resources/edexcel-igcse-mathematics-4ma1-circle-properties-edexcel-igcse-maths-revision-notes/) and the [Circle properties practice questions](/resources/edexcel-igcse-mathematics-4ma1-circle-properties-edexcel-igcse-maths-practice/).

## What the specification asks

| Statement | Content | Tier |
|---|---|---|
| Foundation 4.6 A | recognise the terms centre, radius, chord, diameter, circumference, tangent, arc, sector and segment | Both tiers |
| Foundation 4.6 B | understand chord and tangent properties of circles | Both tiers |
| Higher 4.6 A | understand and use the internal and external intersecting chord properties | Higher tier only |
| Higher 4.6 B | recognise the term "cyclic quadrilateral" | Higher tier only |
| Higher 4.6 C | understand and use angle properties of the circle (five theorems) | Higher tier only |

## Vocabulary (both tiers)

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Radius | A line from the centre to the circumference |
| Diameter | A chord through the centre; twice the radius |
| Chord | A line segment joining two points on the circumference |
| Circumference | The perimeter of the circle |
| Tangent | A straight line that touches the circle at exactly one point |
| Arc | Part of the circumference |
| Sector | The region between two radii and an arc ("pizza slice") |
| Segment | The region between a chord and an arc |

A chord that is not a diameter splits the circle into a **minor** (smaller) and a **major** (larger) segment. Sector and segment are the two terms most often confused.

## Chord and tangent properties (both tiers)

The specification gives three facts:

1. Tangents are perpendicular to the radius at the point of contact.
2. Two tangents from a point to a circle are equal in length.
3. The line from the centre of a circle which is perpendicular to a chord bisects the chord, and the converse: the line from the centre to the midpoint of a chord is perpendicular to it.

A fourth idea is not a separate statement but is used constantly: **any two radii are equal**, so a triangle with two radii as sides is isosceles.

### Spotting right angles

Facts 1 and 3 each create a right angle, which means Pythagoras' theorem or trigonometry (section 4.8) usually follows.

**Worked example 1.** A tangent from P touches a circle with centre O at T. The radius is 5 cm and OP = 13 cm. Find PT.

```
angle OTP = 90° (tangent perpendicular to radius)
PT² = OP² − OT² = 169 − 25 = 144
PT = 12 cm
```

**Worked example 2.** A chord is 2.5 cm from the centre of a circle of radius 6.5 cm. Find the length of the chord.

```
The perpendicular from the centre bisects the chord.
half-chord = √(6.5² − 2.5²) = √(42.25 − 6.25) = √36 = 6 cm
chord = 12 cm
```

### Two tangents make a kite

**Worked example 3.** Tangents from P touch a circle, centre O, at A and B. Angle APB = 40°. Find angle AOB and angle AOP.

```
angle OAP = angle OBP = 90° (tangent perpendicular to radius)
quadrilateral OAPB: angle AOB = 360° − 90° − 90° − 40° = 140°
```

OAPB is a kite (OA = OB as radii, PA = PB as equal tangents), and OP is its line of symmetry. So angle AOP = 140° ÷ 2 = **70°** and angle APO = 20°.

### Isosceles triangles from radii

**Worked example 4.** AB is a chord of a circle with centre O. Angle OAB = 34°. Find angle AOB.

OA = OB (radii), so triangle OAB is isosceles and angle OBA = 34°. Angle AOB = 180° − 68° = **112°**.

## Intersecting chords (Higher tier only)

**Internal.** If chords AB and CD meet at X inside the circle, then AX × XB = CX × XD.

**External.** If a line through an outside point P meets the circle at A and B, and another meets it at C and D, then PA × PB = PC × PD. Each length is measured **from P**, so PB is the whole line to the far point, not the chord AB.

**Worked example 5.** Chords AB and CD cross at X. AX = 4 cm, XB = 9 cm and CX = 6 cm. Find CD.

```
4 × 9 = 6 × XD, so XD = 6 cm
CD = 6 + 6 = 12 cm
```

**Worked example 6.** From P, a line meets the circle at A then B, with PA = 3 cm and AB = 9 cm. Another line meets the circle at C then D, with PC = 4 cm. Find CD.

```
PB = 3 + 9 = 12 cm
3 × 12 = 4 × PD, so PD = 9 cm
CD = 9 − 4 = 5 cm
```

The usual error is writing 3 × 9 = 4 × CD, using the chord instead of the whole distance from P.

## The five angle theorems (Higher tier only)

A **cyclic quadrilateral** has all four vertices on a circle. The theorems are listed in the specification as:

1. The angle subtended by an arc at the centre is twice the angle subtended at any point on the remaining part of the circumference.
2. The angle subtended at the circumference by a diameter is a right angle.
3. Angles in the same segment are equal.
4. The sum of the opposite angles of a cyclic quadrilateral is 180°.
5. The alternate segment theorem: the angle between a tangent and a chord is equal to the angle in the alternate segment.

### How to spot each one

- Two lines from the ends of an arc meeting at the **centre** and at the **circumference**: theorem 1. Look for an "arrowhead" or "bow-tie" through O.
- A **diameter** with a triangle standing on it: theorem 2.
- Two angles at the circumference standing on the **same chord**, on the same side, forming a bow-tie: theorem 3.
- Four points on the circle joined in order: theorem 4.
- A **tangent** meeting a chord at the point of contact: theorem 5.

**Worked example 7 (centre and cyclic quadrilateral).** A, B, C and D lie on a circle with centre O. C is on the major arc AB and D is on the minor arc AB. Angle AOB = 136°. Find angle ACB and angle ADB.

```
angle ACB = 136° ÷ 2 = 68°   (angle at centre is twice angle at circumference)
ACBD is a cyclic quadrilateral, so
angle ADB = 180° − 68° = 112°   (opposite angles of a cyclic quadrilateral add to 180°)
```

Equivalently, the reflex angle AOB is 360° − 136° = 224°, and D stands on that major arc, so angle ADB = 224° ÷ 2 = 112°. Both routes agree, which is a good check.

**Worked example 8 (semicircle).** AB is a diameter and C is on the circle. Angle CAB = 27°. Angle ACB = 90° (angle in a semicircle), so angle ABC = 180° − 90° − 27° = **63°**.

**Worked example 9 (cyclic quadrilateral with algebra).** Opposite angles of a cyclic quadrilateral are (2x + 10)° and (3x − 5)°.

```
2x + 10 + 3x − 5 = 180
5x + 5 = 180, so x = 35
angles: 80° and 100°
```

**Worked example 10 (tangent, chord and centre).** A tangent touches a circle with centre O at A. The chord AB makes an angle of 64° with the tangent. C is a point on the circle in the alternate segment. Find angle ACB, angle AOB and angle OAB.

```
angle ACB = 64°            (alternate segment theorem)
angle AOB = 2 × 64° = 128° (angle at centre is twice angle at circumference)
angle OAB = 90° − 64° = 26° (tangent perpendicular to radius)
```

Check with the isosceles triangle OAB: (180° − 128°) ÷ 2 = 26°. ✓

## Exam technique

- **"Give reasons for your answer"**: write the theorem in words next to each step. "Angle at centre is twice angle at circumference" scores; "circle theorem" does not. See the [Geometrical reasoning study guide](/resources/edexcel-igcse-mathematics-4ma1-geometrical-reasoning-edexcel-igcse-maths/) for the full list of accepted wordings.
- **Mark angles on the diagram** as you find them. A chain of three or four facts is normal on Higher papers.
- **"Diagram not accurately drawn"** means you must not measure; calculate every angle.

## Common mistakes

- Halving the angle at the centre when the circumference point is on the wrong arc (the reflex case in worked example 7).
- Using "angles in the same segment" for two angles on opposite sides of the chord; those add to 180° instead.
- Applying the cyclic quadrilateral rule when one vertex is the centre, which is not on the circle.
- Using the chord length instead of the full distance from P in the external chord rule.
- Forgetting that two radii make an isosceles triangle.
