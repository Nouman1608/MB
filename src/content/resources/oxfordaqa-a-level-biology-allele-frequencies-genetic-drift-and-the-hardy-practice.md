## Questions

**1.** Define each of the following terms: population, gene pool and allele frequency. **[3]**

**2.** A gene controlling throat colour in a lizard has two codominant alleles, S and T. A sample of 160 lizards contains 56 SS, 72 ST and 32 TT.

**(a)** Calculate the frequency of allele S. **[2]**
**(b)** If the population were in Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium, calculate the number of ST lizards expected in the sample. **[2]**

**3.** An allele has a frequency of 0.10 in population A, which has 10 individuals, and in population B, which has 500 individuals.

**(a)** Calculate the number of copies of the allele in each population. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why genetic drift is more likely to remove this allele from population A than from population B. **[2]**

**4.** A population of 6000 salamanders contains a rare allele at a frequency of 0.01. A landslide destroys most of the habitat and leaves 12 survivors.

**(a)** Calculate the copies of the allele in the original population. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the number of copies of this gene in the survivors, and the number of copies of the rare allele you would expect among them if they were a random sample. **[2]**
**(c)** The probability that the rare allele is absent from the survivors is 0.99 raised to the power of the number of gene copies in the survivors. Calculate this probability. **[1]**
**(d)** Suggest why the allele frequency in the survivors may differ from 0.01. **[1]**

**5.** Dairy farmers have bred cattle for high milk yield for many generations.

**(a)** Explain how repeated selection of the highest-yielding cows and bulls as parents changes the gene pool of the breed. **[3]**
**(b)** Suggest two disadvantages of having a gene pool with little variety. **[2]**

**6.** **(a)** Give three conditions required for a population to be in Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium. **[3]**
**(b)** A population of plants is grazed by animals that eat only plants with one particular phenotype. Explain why the Hardy-Weinberg equation would not correctly predict the genotype frequencies in the next generation. **[2]**

**7.** A recessive disorder affects 1 in 625 individuals in a large, randomly mating population with no selection, mutation or migration.

**(a)** Work out the frequency of the recessive allele. **[2]**
**(b)** Work out the frequency of the dominant allele. **[1]**
**(c)** Work out the frequency of carriers. **[1]**
**(d)** Work out how many carriers are expected in a population of 50 000. **[1]**

**8.** In a meadow of 2500 plants, 94.24% have hairy leaves. Hairy (H) is dominant to smooth (h). Hardy-Weinberg conditions hold.

**(a)** Find the frequencies of alleles H and h. **[3]**
**(b)** Find how many plants are heterozygous. **[2]**
**(c)** Find how many plants have smooth leaves. **[1]**

**9.** A population of 300 beetles contains 135 AA, 120 Aa and 45 aa individuals.

**(a)** Calculate the frequency of allele a. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the numbers of AA, Aa and aa beetles expected if the population were in Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium. **[3]**
**(c)** Compare the observed and expected numbers and suggest one reason for the difference. **[2]**

**10.** A population of 4000 tree frogs has a recessive allele d at a frequency of 0.30. A disease outbreak leaves 20 survivors: 6 DD, 10 Dd and 4 dd. The disease affects all genotypes equally.

**(a)** Calculate the frequency of allele d among the survivors. **[2]**
**(b)** Name the type of event the outbreak represents for this population. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain why the change in the frequency of d is an example of genetic drift and not natural selection. **[2]**
**(d)** The survivors breed and produce a generation of 400 frogs. Assuming Hardy-Weinberg conditions from now on, calculate the number of dd frogs expected. **[2]**
**(e)** Suggest one reason why Hardy-Weinberg conditions are unlikely to hold in a population this small. **[1]**

**11.** The table shows illustrative data for wheat.

| | Wild relatives | Modern high-yield variety |
|---|---|---|
| Mean number of alleles per gene studied | 7.4 | 2.9 |
| Mean grain yield (tonnes per hectare) | 3.1 | 8.4 |

**(a)** Calculate the percentage decrease in the mean number of alleles per gene. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage increase in mean grain yield. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain how selection for high yield could produce these results, and describe one risk of growing the modern variety over a large area. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** Population: organisms of one species that could interbreed, living in the same place at the same time [1]. Gene pool: every allele of a gene present in one population [1]. Allele frequency: the share of the gene copies in the population that are a chosen allele [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Include the qualifiers: one species, same place and time, every allele, a proportion of all copies.

**2. (a)** Copies of S = (2 × 56) + 72 = 184 [1]; total copies = 2 × 160 = 320, so frequency = 184 ÷ 320 = **0.575** [1].
**(b)** q = 1 − 0.575 = 0.425, so 2pq = 2 × 0.575 × 0.425 = 0.48875 [1]; 0.48875 × 160 = 78.2, so about **78 lizards** [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Show 2N as the denominator. Dividing 184 by 160 gives a value above 1: a sign of mixing up alleles and individuals.

**3. (a)** Population A: 2 × 10 × 0.10 = **2 copies** [1]. Population B: 2 × 500 × 0.10 = **100 copies** [1].
**(b)** In A there are few copies, so a chance event removes a large fraction of all copies [1]. In B there are many copies, so chance events affect a small fraction and the effects average out, so the allele is much less likely to be lost [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Tie your explanation to the number of copies: each chance event is a larger proportion of them.

**4. (a)** 2 × 6000 × 0.01 = **120 copies** [1].
**(b)** Copies of the gene in the survivors = 2 × 12 = **24** [1]; expected copies of the rare allele = 24 × 0.01 = **0.24** [1].
**(c)** 0.99²⁴ = **0.79** (to 2 significant figures) [1].
**(d)** The survivors form a small random sample, so the allele may by chance be over- or under-represented [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* In (d), say the survivors are a chance sample, not that the allele was "weaker".

**5. (a)** Cows and bulls with a high milk yield are chosen to breed, and the rest are not [1]. Alleles for high yield are passed on more often, so their frequency rises [1]. Other alleles become less frequent or are lost, so the gene pool becomes smaller and less varied [1].
**(b)** Any two from: little variation for selection to act on if conditions change [1]; more individuals susceptible to a new disease at the same time [1]; breeding between relatives makes harmful homozygous recessive genotypes more likely. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Link the farmer's choice to a change in allele frequency, not just to "better cows".

**6. (a)** Any three from: a large population [1]; mating at random [1]; mutation absent [1]; no gene flow into or out of the population; no selection.
**(b)** The condition of no selection is broken [1]. The plants with the eaten phenotype are less likely to survive and reproduce, so the allele frequencies change and the genotype frequencies do not match p² + 2pq + q² for the original frequencies [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Name the condition broken, then say what it does to allele frequency.

**7. (a)** q² = 1 ÷ 625 = 0.0016 [1]; q = √0.0016 = **0.04** [1].
**(b)** p = 1 − 0.04 = **0.96** [1].
**(c)** 2pq = 2 × 0.96 × 0.04 = **0.0768** [1].
**(d)** 0.0768 × 50 000 = **3840 carriers** [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* "1 in 625" is q², not q. Write q² = 1/625 before taking the square root.

**8. (a)** The smooth phenotype frequency is 1 − 0.9424 = 0.0576, so q² = 0.0576 [1]; q = **0.24** [1]; p = **0.76** [1].
**(b)** 2pq = 2 × 0.76 × 0.24 = 0.3648 [1]; 0.3648 × 2500 = **912 plants** [1].
**(c)** 0.0576 × 2500 = **144 plants** [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* The 94.24% is the dominant phenotype, which is p² + 2pq, not p². Subtract it from 1 to get q² first.

**9. (a)** Copies of a = (2 × 45) + 120 = 210 [1]; frequency = 210 ÷ 600 = **0.35** [1].
**(b)** p = 0.65 [1]. AA = 0.65² × 300 = **126.75**  [1]; Aa = 2 × 0.65 × 0.35 × 300 = **136.5** and aa = 0.35² × 300 = **36.75**  [1].
**(c)** There are fewer heterozygotes than expected (120 against 136.5) and more of both homozygotes (135 against 126.75; 45 against 36.75), so the population is not in equilibrium [1]. A possible reason is non-random mating, such as mating between relatives, or selection against the heterozygotes [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* Quote observed and expected numbers and say how they differ.

**10. (a)** Copies of d = (2 × 4) + 10 = 18 [1]; total copies = 2 × 20 = 40, so frequency = 18 ÷ 40 = **0.45** [1].
**(b)** A genetic bottleneck [1].
**(c)** The frequency rose from 0.30 to 0.45, but the disease affected all genotypes equally, so d gave no survival advantage [1]. The change arose because the 40 surviving gene copies were a small random draw from the alleles present, which is genetic drift [1].
**(d)** q² = 0.45² = 0.2025 [1]; 0.2025 × 400 = **81 frogs** [1].
**(e)** In a very small population drift is large (or mating between relatives is likely), so the large-population and random-mating conditions are not met [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* In (c), "the disease did not select" is not enough alone. Say what did cause it: a small random sample. In (d), use the new q of 0.45.

**11. (a)** Decrease = 7.4 − 2.9 = 4.5 [1]; (4.5 ÷ 7.4) × 100 = **60.8%** [1].
**(b)** Increase = 8.4 − 3.1 = 5.3 [1]; (5.3 ÷ 3.1) × 100 = **171%** [1].
**(c)** Breeders bred from the highest-yielding plants, so alleles for high yield increased in frequency [1] while other alleles were lost, so the gene pool became less varied [1]. Risk: with little variation, a new disease or pest could affect most of the crop at once [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* For percentage change, divide by the starting value (the wild relatives for (a), the lower yield for (b)).

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using a recessive phenotype frequency (such as 1 in 625) as q rather than q².
- Dividing by the number of individuals instead of 2N when finding allele frequency.
- Missing the 2 in 2pq.
- Describing drift as "survival of the fittest".
- Giving a vague Hardy-Weinberg condition instead of a named one.
- Using the wrong base value for a percentage change.
