## Key definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Polar molecule | Has regions of small positive (δ+) and negative (δ−) charge |
| Hydrogen bond | Weak electrostatic attraction linking a δ+ hydrogen on one molecule to a δ− oxygen or nitrogen on another |
| Monomer | Small unit that joins with others to make a polymer |
| Polymer | Long chain of many monomers |
| Condensation | Joins two molecules, releases water |
| Hydrolysis | Breaks a bond using water |
| Glycosidic bond | Covalent bond between two monosaccharides |
| Ester bond | Covalent bond between glycerol and a fatty acid |
| Hydrophilic | Attracted to water |
| Hydrophobic | Repelled by water |

## 2.1.2(a) Water

Method in steps for any water question: name the property, give the cause (hydrogen bonds), then the role.

| Role | Property | Link |
|---|---|---|
| Solvent | Polar | Ions and polar molecules dissolve, so reactions occur in solution |
| Transport medium | Cohesion, solvent | Continuous column in xylem; plasma carries dissolved substances |
| Coolant | High latent heat of vaporisation | Evaporation removes much energy: sweat, transpiration |
| Habitat | High specific heat capacity; ice less dense than water; surface tension | Stable temperature; ice floats and insulates; surface supports small animals |

Prokaryote examples: bacterial cytoplasm is a solvent; aquatic bacteria live in a thermally stable habitat. Eukaryote examples: sweating in mammals, xylem in plants, ponds as habitats.

Reminder: 144 kJ removed by 60 g of sweat at 2.4 kJ per gram.

## 2.1.2(d) Monosaccharides

- Glucose C6H12O6: hexose, ring of 5 C plus 1 O, CH2OH outside the ring.
- Carbon 1 –OH: down in α-glucose, up in β-glucose.
- Ribose C5H10O5: pentose, ring of 4 C plus 1 O.
- Hexose has six carbons; pentose has five.

## 2.1.2(e) Disaccharides

| Disaccharide | Made from |
|---|---|
| Maltose | two glucose units |
| Sucrose | glucose and fructose |
| Lactose | glucose and galactose |

All are C12H22O11, Mr 342. Condensation forms the glycosidic bond; hydrolysis breaks it.

## 2.1.2(f), (g) Polysaccharides

| | Amylose | Amylopectin | Glycogen | Cellulose |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Unit | α-glucose | α-glucose | α-glucose | β-glucose |
| Links | α-1,4 | α-1,4 plus α-1,6 branches | α-1,4 plus α-1,6 branches | β-1,4 |
| Shape | Helix | Branched | Very branched | Straight chains |
| Role | Plant energy store | Plant energy store | Animal and fungal energy store | Cell wall |

Function in a sentence each:

- **Glucose**: soluble, so transported; substrate for respiration.
- **Starch**: insoluble, so no osmotic effect; compact; hydrolysed to glucose when needed.
- **Glycogen**: many ends, so fast hydrolysis; compact; found in liver and muscle, also in some bacteria.
- **Cellulose**: hydrogen bonds between straight chains form microfibrils; strong wall resists bursting.

Must-know distinctions: α against β (carbon 1 –OH, and β chains alternate in orientation); amylose against amylopectin (branching); starch against glycogen (branching frequency, and plant against animal).

## 2.1.2(h), (i), (j) Lipids

| Molecule | Structure | Key property | Function |
|---|---|---|---|
| Triglyceride | Glycerol + 3 fatty acids, 3 ester bonds | Hydrophobic, high energy per gram | Energy store, insulation, protection |
| Phospholipid | Glycerol + 2 fatty acids + phosphate | Hydrophilic head, hydrophobic tails | Bilayer of membranes |
| Cholesterol | Four-ring steroid with –OH | Small, mostly hydrophobic | Regulates fluidity and stability of eukaryotic membranes |

- Saturated: no C=C; straight chain. Unsaturated: one or more C=C; kinked chain.
- Condensation to make a triglyceride releases 3 water molecules.
- About 38 kJ g⁻¹ for lipid and 17 kJ g⁻¹ for carbohydrate (give as data when asked).
- Tristearin: 92 + 3 × 284 − 54 = 890.

## Method boxes

**Counting bonds and water in a polymer**

1. Count the monomers, n.
2. Bonds formed = n − 1, and water released = n − 1.
3. Mr of polymer = (n × Mr of monomer) − ((n − 1) × 18).
4. Check: complete hydrolysis uses the same n − 1 water molecules.

**Counting for a triglyceride**

1. Mr = glycerol (92) + 3 × fatty acid − 3 × 18.
2. Each of the three ester bonds comes with one water given off.

**Comparing two molecules**

1. State one similarity (for example, both are glucose polymers).
2. Give the difference in bond type, then in shape, then in the property that follows.
3. Finish with the function that the property allows.

**Percentage change**

1. (new − old) ÷ old × 100.
2. State whether it is an increase or a decrease.

## Worked reminders

Lactose, 1.71 g, hydrolysed completely: 1.71 ÷ 342 = 0.00500 mol; two monosaccharides per lactose gives 0.0100 mol; 0.0100 × 180 = 1.80 g. The extra 0.09 g is the water used. (Galactose also has Mr 180.)

A glycogen molecule of 2 400 residues with a branch every 12 residues has 200 branches and 201 free ends. Amylopectin of the same size with a branch every 30 has 80 branches and 81 free ends. The ratio is about 2.5.

A vesicle membrane leaks dye at 10 units per minute without cholesterol and 6 units with it: (10 − 6) ÷ 10 × 100 = 40% decrease.

Energy: 4 g of lipid at 38 kJ g⁻¹ gives 152 kJ. The mass of carbohydrate at 17 kJ g⁻¹ needed for 152 kJ is 152 ÷ 17 = 8.9 g.

## Linking ideas together

- Hydrogen bonds matter in three places: between water molecules, between cellulose chains, and (in other units) inside proteins and between DNA strands.
- Condensation always releases water and hydrolysis always uses it, whether the product is a glycosidic bond or an ester bond.
- Hydrophobic means tails or chains that avoid water: fatty acids and cholesterol rings. Hydrophilic means groups that attract water: the phosphate head, and the –OH groups of sugars.
- Insoluble stores (starch, glycogen, triglyceride) do not alter water potential; soluble transport molecules (glucose, sucrose) can.

## Quick self-test

1. State which atom of H2O is δ− and which are δ+.
2. Name the property that makes sweating an effective coolant.
3. A chain is built from 250 glucose residues. State the water released and calculate its Mr.
4. State the structural difference between α-glucose and β-glucose.
5. What is the molecular formula of lactose, and which monosaccharides make it?
6. Why is glycogen hydrolysed faster than amylose?
7. Give two reasons why cellulose is suited to plant cell walls.
8. How many ester bonds are in one phospholipid, and how many in one triglyceride?
9. How much energy is stored in 5 g of lipid and 5 g of carbohydrate, using 38 and 17 kJ g⁻¹?
10. Name the type of cell whose membranes contain cholesterol, and state its effect on membrane fluidity.
11. Name the hydrophilic part of a phospholipid.
12. Explain the lower density of ice compared with liquid water.

### Answers

1. Oxygen is δ−; the two hydrogens are δ+.
2. High latent heat of vaporisation: evaporation takes away a large amount of energy for a small mass of water.
3. 249 water molecules; Mr = 250 × 180 − 249 × 18 = 45 000 − 4 482 = 40 518.
4. Carbon 1 has its –OH down (α) or up (β), relative to the ring.
5. C12H22O11; glucose and galactose.
6. Glycogen is much more branched, so it has more free ends where enzymes can act at once.
7. Straight β-1,4 chains form hydrogen bonds with neighbouring chains, making strong microfibrils; the wall resists the pressure of the cell contents.
8. Two ester bonds in a phospholipid; three in a triglyceride.
9. Lipid 5 × 38 = 190 kJ; carbohydrate 5 × 17 = 85 kJ.
10. Eukaryote (especially animal) cells; it regulates fluidity, restricting tail movement when warm and preventing close packing when cold.
11. The phosphate head.
12. In ice, hydrogen bonds hold the molecules in an open, fixed lattice, so they are further apart than in liquid water.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Naming a property (such as "high specific heat capacity") without saying what causes it or what it does for the organism.
- Stating that hydrogen bonds are "between atoms in the molecule".
- Giving a role for water with only a human example when a prokaryote or plant is asked.
- Confusing condensation and hydrolysis, or forgetting which one uses water.
- Counting n bonds and n water molecules for a polymer of n monomers.
- Describing α and β glucose as different formulae. Both are C6H12O6.
- Saying starch is "branched" without separating amylose from amylopectin.
- Describing cellulose function without hydrogen bonding between chains.
- Explaining the bilayer without naming which region faces water.
- Calling cholesterol a phospholipid or a triglyceride.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the difference between alpha and beta glucose?**
Alpha and beta glucose have the same formula, C₆H₁₂O₆, but differ in the position of the hydroxyl group on carbon 1. Describing them as having different formulae is a common error.

**Where are hydrogen bonds in water found?**
Hydrogen bonds form between neighbouring water molecules, from the δ+ hydrogen of one molecule to the δ− oxygen of another. They are not between the atoms within one molecule, which are joined by covalent bonds.

**What is the difference between condensation and hydrolysis?**
Condensation joins monomers and releases water, while hydrolysis breaks a bond by using water. Confusing which one uses water loses marks.

**Why is cellulose strong?**
Hydrogen bonds form between neighbouring cellulose chains, holding them together in strong fibres. Describing the function of cellulose without this hydrogen bonding is incomplete.
