These questions cover section **3.1.3.5 Shapes of simple molecules and ions** of the AQA AS and A-level Chemistry specification (7404/7405), for teaching from September 2015. It is AS content, examined on Papers 1 and 2 (and Paper 3). Learn the methods in the [Shapes of simple molecules and ions study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-shapes-of-simple-molecules-and-ions/) and the [revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-shapes-of-simple-molecules-and-ions-revision-notes/).

## Questions

**1.** State the order of repulsion between pairs of electrons in the outer shell of a central atom. **[1]**

**2.** Methane, CH₄, ammonia, NH₃, and water, H₂O, all have four electron pairs around the central atom.

**(a)** State the H–C–H bond angle in methane. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain why the H–N–H angle in ammonia is smaller than the angle in methane. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain why the H–O–H angle in water is smaller than the angle in ammonia. **[2]**

**3.** Boron trifluoride, BF₃, is a gas.

**(a)** Deduce the number of electron pairs around the boron atom. **[1]**

**(b)** Name the shape of the molecule and state the F–B–F bond angle. **[2]**

**4.** Deduce the shape and bond angle of each species. Show how you counted the electron pairs.

**(a)** Phosphorus trichloride, PCl₃ **[3]**

**(b)** The ammonium ion, NH₄⁺ **[3]**

**(c)** Beryllium chloride, BeCl₂ (beryllium has 2 outer electrons) **[2]**

**5.** Phosphorus pentachloride, PCl₅, and sulfur hexafluoride, SF₆, both have a central atom with more than eight electrons in its outer shell.

**(a)** Name the shape and give the bond angles in PCl₅. **[3]**

**(b)** Name the shape and give the bond angle in SF₆. **[2]**

**6.** The tetrafluoroborate ion, BF₄⁻, and the phosphorus tetrachloride ion, PCl₄⁺, have the same shape.

**(a)** Work out the number of electron pairs on the central atom of BF₄⁻ (B has 3 outer electrons). **[2]**

**(b)** Name the shape and give the bond angle. **[2]**

**7.** Deduce the shape of the tetrachloroiodate ion, ICl₄⁻ (iodine has 7 outer electrons). Explain your answer, stating the number of lone pairs. **[4]**

**8.** Draw the shape of a water molecule using wedge and dash bonds if you wish, show the lone pairs, and label the bond angle. **[3]**

**9.** Explain why carbon dioxide is linear but sulfur dioxide is bent. **[4]**

**10.** The carbonate ion, CO₃²⁻, has three identical bonds.

**(a)** State the shape and bond angle. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain why a double bond is treated as one charge cloud when predicting this shape. **[1]**

**11.** Chlorine trifluoride, ClF₃, has a T-shaped molecule.

**(a)** Use the number of electron pairs to show that the central atom has two lone pairs. **[2]**

**(b)** Suggest why the F–Cl–F angle is less than 90°. **[1]**

## Answers

**1.** Lone pair–lone pair repulsion is greater than lone pair–bond pair, which is greater than bond pair–bond pair [1]. **[1]**

**2. (a)** **109.5°** [1]

**(b)** Ammonia has one lone pair and three bond pairs [1]; the lone pair repels the bond pairs more than bond pairs repel one another, so the angle is reduced (107°) [1]

**(c)** Water has two lone pairs [1]; the extra lone pair repulsion reduces the angle further (104.5°) [1]. **[5]**

**3. (a)** B: 3 + 3 = 6 outer electrons, which is **3 pairs** [1]

**(b)** Trigonal planar [1]; **120°** [1]. **[3]**

**4. (a)** P: 5 + 3 = 8, so 4 pairs, 3 bonding and 1 lone [1]; shape **pyramidal** [1]; angle **less than 109.5°** (accept any value from about 100° to 107°) [1]

**(b)** N: 5 + 4 − 1 = 8, so 4 pairs, all bonding [1]; **tetrahedral** [1]; **109.5°** [1]

**(c)** Be: 2 + 2 = 4, so 2 pairs, both bonding [1]; **linear**, 180° [1]. **[8]**

**5. (a)** P: 5 + 5 = 10, so 5 bonding pairs [1]; **trigonal bipyramidal** [1]; angles of **90° and 120°** [1]

**(b)** S: 6 + 6 = 12, so 6 bonding pairs; **octahedral** [1]; **90°** [1]. **[5]**

**6. (a)** B: 3 + 4 + 1 = 8, so 4 pairs [1]; all 4 are bonding pairs, so no lone pairs [1]

**(b)** **Tetrahedral**, **109.5°** [1 + 1]. **[4]**

**7.** I: 7 + 4 + 1 = 12 electrons, so 6 pairs [1]; 4 bonding pairs and 2 lone pairs [1]; the six pairs form an octahedral arrangement with the two lone pairs opposite one another, as far apart as possible, to minimise repulsion [1]; the shape is **square planar**, with 90° angles [1]. **[4]**

**8.** Oxygen at the centre with two hydrogens drawn as a bent (V) shape [1]; two lone pairs on oxygen shown (as pairs of dots, in the plane or above and below) [1]; angle labelled as 104.5° (accept about 104 to 105) [1]. **[3]**

**9.** Carbon in CO₂ has 4 outer electrons, giving 2 pairs, which are the two double bonds (2 charge clouds) and no lone pairs [1]; the two clouds repel as far apart as possible, giving a linear molecule [1]. Sulfur in SO₂ has 6 outer electrons, giving 3 pairs: two bonding clouds and one lone pair [1]; the lone pair repels the two bonds, so the shape is bent (angle just under 120°) [1]. **[4]**

**10. (a)** **Trigonal planar**, **120°** [1 + 1]

**(b)** The electrons of a double bond are all located in the same region between the two atoms and repel the other bonds as a single cloud [1]. **[3]**

**11. (a)** Cl: 7 + 3 = 10 electrons, so 5 pairs [1]; 3 bonding pairs, so 2 lone pairs [1]

**(b)** The two lone pairs repel the bond pairs more strongly than bond pairs repel each other, so the angle is squeezed below 90° [1]. **[3]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Naming the arrangement of all pairs instead of the shape of the atoms.
- Giving 109.5° for ammonia or water.
- Forgetting to adjust the electron count for the charge on an ion.
- Giving only one angle for PCl₅.
- Failing to state that the lone pair repulsion is greater, so the angle is smaller.
- Omitting the lone pairs from a drawing when asked to show them.
