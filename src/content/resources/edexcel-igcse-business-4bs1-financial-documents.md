This guide goes deeper into section 3.4 Financial documents of the Pearson Edexcel International GCSE in Business (4BS1) specification, Issue 2, February 2026. Point 3.4.1 is "The purpose of statements of comprehensive income": the main features (sales, cost of sales, gross profit, expenses, operating profit), their use in decision making, and the nature of profit and its importance. Point 3.4.2 is "The purpose of statements of financial position": the main features (current and non-current assets, current and non-current liabilities, capital employed) and interpreting one. The specification states that you will **not** be required to construct either statement; you read, interpret and use them.

This page concentrates on reading the documents themselves: the vocabulary, the logic of each line, and what the figures say about decisions. ## The vocabulary the exam uses

The specification includes a glossary and says the assessments use International Accounting Standards (IAS) terminology only. Older textbooks may use different words, so learn the exam versions.

| Older term you may meet | Term used in the exam |
|---|---|
| Sales | Revenue |
| Cost of goods sold | Cost of sales |
| Stock | Inventory |
| Sundry expenses | Other operating expenses |
| Balance sheet | Statement of financial position |
| Fixed assets | Non-current assets |
| Debtors | Trade receivables |
| Creditors | Trade payables |
| Long-term liabilities | Non-current liabilities |

The specification's own content heading uses "sales" and the formulae use "revenue"; treat them as the same figure.

## The statement of comprehensive income, line by line

Its purpose is to show how much revenue a business earned over a period, usually a year, and how much profit was left after costs.

| Line | What it shows | Question it answers |
|---|---|---|
| Sales (revenue) | Value of goods or services sold | How much did customers buy? |
| Cost of sales | Direct cost of the goods sold, such as materials or goods bought for resale | What did the products themselves cost? |
| Gross profit | Sales − cost of sales | Is the business making money on its products before running costs? |
| Expenses (other operating expenses) | Running costs such as wages of office staff, rent, advertising, utilities | What does it cost to run the business? |
| Operating profit | Gross profit − other operating expenses | Is the business profitable from its normal trading? |

Notice the order: each profit figure is what is left after one more layer of cost. A business can have a healthy gross profit and still make an operating loss if its expenses are too high.

### Worked example 1: reading two years side by side

Merrow Pharmacy

| | Year 1 | Year 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Sales | 620,000 | 700,000 |
| Cost of sales | 372,000 | 434,000 |
| Gross profit | 248,000 | 266,000 |
| Other operating expenses | 171,000 | 189,000 |
| Operating profit | 77,000 | 77,000 |

What the statement tells a manager:

- Sales rose by 80,000, an increase of 80,000 ÷ 620,000 × 100 = **12.9%**.
- Cost of sales rose by 62,000, which is 62,000 ÷ 372,000 × 100 = **16.7%**. Product costs grew faster than sales, so each sale earned a little less gross profit.
- Expenses rose by 18,000 (10.5%).
- The result: 80,000 more in sales produced **no** extra operating profit.

Possible decisions: renegotiate prices with suppliers, review selling prices, or check whether extra expenses (for example longer opening hours) brought in enough extra sales to justify them.

## Using the statement in decision making

The specification names "the use of statements of comprehensive income in decision making". Typical uses:

- **Pricing.** If cost of sales is rising, the business may need to raise prices to protect gross profit.
- **Cost control.** Comparing each expense with last year shows where spending has grown.
- **Product choices.** If separate figures exist for product lines, the business can see which earns most gross profit. Merrow sells beauty products (sales 240,000, cost of sales 132,000, gross profit **108,000**) and gifts (sales 160,000, cost of sales 120,000, gross profit **40,000**). Shelf space might be moved towards beauty products.
- **Expansion and finance.** Owners judge whether profit is high enough to fund growth; lenders judge whether profit can cover interest and repayments.

Limitation: the statement records the past. It does not show cash, and next year's market may be different.

## The nature of profit and why it matters

Profit is the surplus of revenue over costs in a period. It matters because:

- it rewards owners for the risk of investing;
- it is a source of finance, as retained profit, for new equipment or expansion;
- it attracts investors and reassures lenders;
- it measures how well the business is run compared with past years and rivals.

Profit is **not** the same as cash. Revenue counts when the sale is made, even if the customer pays later.

## The statement of financial position

Its purpose is to show, on one date, what a business owns (assets), what it owes (liabilities), and how it is financed. It is a snapshot, whereas the statement of comprehensive income covers a whole period.

| Feature | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Non-current assets | Owned and used for more than a year | Property, vehicles, machinery |
| Current assets | Expected to become cash within a year | Inventory, trade receivables, cash |
| Current liabilities | Must be paid within a year | Trade payables, bank overdraft |
| Non-current liabilities | Due after more than a year | Bank loans repayable after 12 months |
| Capital employed | The long-term finance used in the business | Equity plus non-current liabilities |

The specification lists capital employed without a formula. The standard calculation is **non-current assets + current assets − current liabilities**, which equals **equity + non-current liabilities**.

### Worked example 2: Tovey Builders at 31 March

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Property | 250,000 |
| Vehicles and equipment | 130,000 |
| **Non-current assets** | **380,000** |
| Inventory | 46,000 |
| Trade receivables | 28,000 |
| Cash | 9,000 |
| **Current assets** | **83,000** |
| Trade payables | 31,000 |
| Bank overdraft | 12,000 |
| **Current liabilities** | **43,000** |
| Bank loan (repayable in 8 years) | 150,000 |
| **Equity** | **270,000** |

Capital employed = 380,000 + 83,000 − 43,000 = **420,000**. Check: equity 270,000 + loan 150,000 = 420,000.

Interpretation:

- Most of the money is in property and equipment. That suits a builder, but those assets cannot quickly be turned into cash.
- Cash is only 9,000, while the business already uses a 12,000 overdraft. More than half of current assets (46,000 of 83,000) is inventory, which must be used in jobs and paid for by customers before it becomes cash.
- The long-term loan of 150,000 is over a third of capital employed (150,000 ÷ 420,000 = 35.7%), so a lender has a large stake and interest is a regular cost.

A decision this supports: before taking on a large new contract, Tovey should check it can pay for extra materials, because its current cash is low.

## Annotated model answer

*Analyse how the statement of comprehensive income could help Merrow Pharmacy decide whether to open longer hours next year.*

> The statement shows other operating expenses rose by 18,000 in Year 2 while operating profit stayed at 77,000. If the longer hours were the cause of those extra costs, they did not raise profit, because the extra sales were mostly absorbed by the higher cost of sales. Merrow could compare the sales made in the extra hours with the wages and electricity they cost. If the extra hours only cover their own costs, opening longer again would add work without adding operating profit, so the owner may decide to cut back to the original hours.

Why it works: it uses figures from the statement, follows a chain from the evidence to the decision and stays within reading, not constructing.

## Exam technique

- **Define** and **Give** questions often test the IAS terms: learn them exactly.
- **Calculate** questions may ask for a missing line, such as gross profit from sales and cost of sales. Show the subtraction.
- **Identify** questions ask you to read a value from the table.
- **Analyse** and **evaluate** questions want you to say what the figures mean for a decision. Quote the numbers.

## Common mistakes

- Calling the statement of financial position a record of a year. It shows one date.
- Putting a bank overdraft under non-current liabilities. It is current.
- Treating inventory as a non-current asset.
- Saying higher sales must mean higher profit.
- Confusing gross profit with operating profit.
