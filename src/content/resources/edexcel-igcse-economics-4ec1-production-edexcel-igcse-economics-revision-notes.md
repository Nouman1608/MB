These notes condense section 1.2.1, Production, of the Pearson Edexcel International GCSE Economics (4EC1) specification, Issue 3, February 2026, examined in Paper 1 with a single tier of entry. All figures are invented. For explanations and worked cases, read the [production study guide](/resources/edexcel-igcse-economics-4ec1-production-edexcel-igcse-economics/); to practise, use the [production practice questions](/resources/edexcel-igcse-economics-4ec1-production-edexcel-igcse-economics-practice/).

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Factors of production | Resources used to produce goods and services: land, labour, capital, enterprise |
| Land | All natural resources, including soil, seas, minerals, forests and fish stocks |
| Labour | The physical and mental effort of people in production |
| Capital | Man-made goods used to produce other goods and services |
| Enterprise | Organising the other factors, making decisions and taking risks |
| Primary sector | Extracting or growing natural resources |
| Secondary sector | Manufacturing and construction: turning raw materials into goods |
| Tertiary sector | Providing services |
| Developing economy | An economy with lower incomes, often with a large primary sector |
| Developed economy | An economy with high incomes, usually with a large tertiary sector |

## Factor checklist

| If the resource is… | It is… |
|---|---|
| Found in nature (oil, fish, timber, farmland, water) | Land |
| Human effort, paid by wages or salary | Labour |
| Made by people and used to make other things (machine, van, computer, factory) | Capital |
| The risk-taking and organising of the owner or entrepreneur | Enterprise |
| Money | Not a factor in this classification; it buys capital |

## Sector checklist

| Activity | Sector |
|---|---|
| Mining copper, drilling for oil, fishing, growing tea | Primary |
| Refining oil, making cars, processing food, building houses | Secondary |
| Transport, retail, banking, insurance, tourism, teaching, healthcare | Tertiary |

**Rule:** classify by the activity, not the product. One product can pass through all three sectors (cocoa farm → chocolate factory → shop).

## Structural change: the usual pattern

- **Developing economies:** large primary share of employment and output; secondary grows with industrialisation; tertiary grows as incomes rise.
- **Developed economies:** small primary share; secondary share often falling; tertiary provides most jobs and output.

## Four reasons for the pattern

1. **Rising incomes:** demand shifts from food to manufactured goods to services.
2. **Mechanisation of farming:** fewer farm workers needed, labour released to other sectors.
3. **Industrialisation:** factories grow, often producing for export.
4. **Manufacturing relocates or automates:** in developed economies, some production moves to lower-cost countries and factories use more machines, so secondary employment falls.

**Chain to learn:** incomes rise → demand for services rises → firms in services expand → more jobs and output in the tertiary sector.

## Employment versus output

- A sector's share of **employment** can fall while its **output** rises, if each worker produces more.
- A sector's **share** can fall while the **number** of workers rises, if total employment grows.

*Drill (invented):* total employment rises from 20 million to 25 million; the primary share falls from 50% to 44%. Primary workers: 10 million → 11 million. The share fell by **6 percentage points**, but the number rose by **10%**.

## Percentage points or per cent?

| Change | Correct description |
|---|---|
| Tertiary share 60% → 66% | Up 6 percentage points |
| Tertiary workers 12 million → 15 million | Up 25% |

## Exceptions

- Resource-rich economies may keep a large primary share of output.
- Some economies grow services, such as tourism, without a large manufacturing stage.
- Use "tends to" or "usually" rather than "always".

## Exam traps

- Money is not capital; buildings are capital, not land.
- A salaried manager supplies labour; the risk-taker supplies enterprise.
- Construction is secondary; transport is tertiary.
- Do not mix percentage points with per cent.
- Read whether the data are for employment or output.
- Developed economies still have a primary sector; it is just small.

## Quick self-test

1. Name the four factors of production.
2. Is a delivery van land, labour, capital or enterprise?
3. Is a bank loan a factor of production?
4. Which factor includes fish stocks in the sea?
5. Which sector does oil refining belong to?
6. Which sector does a hotel belong to?
7. Describe the usual change in the primary sector's share of employment as an economy develops.
8. Give one reason why the tertiary sector grows as incomes rise.
9. Why might farm output rise while farm employment falls?
10. The secondary share of employment falls from 30% to 24%. Describe the change correctly.
11. Total employment is 40 million and the tertiary share is 55%. How many tertiary workers are there?
12. Give one exception to the usual pattern of structural change.

### Answers

1. Land, labour, capital, enterprise.
2. Capital.
3. No: money is used to buy capital but is not itself a factor in this classification.
4. Land.
5. Secondary.
6. Tertiary.
7. It falls.
8. Richer households spend a larger share of income on services such as travel, leisure and healthcare.
9. Mechanisation and better methods raise output per worker.
10. A fall of 6 percentage points (a 20% fall in the share).
11. 0.55 × 40 = **22 million**.
12. A resource-rich country may keep a large primary share of output (or: a country may grow services such as tourism without a large manufacturing stage).

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calling money capital.
- Classifying by product rather than activity.
- Confusing employment share with output share.
- Writing "per cent" for a change in a share.
- Stating structural change as a fixed law.
