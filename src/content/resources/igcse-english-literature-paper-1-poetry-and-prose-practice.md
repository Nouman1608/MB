## Section A

**1.** State the total length and total marks of Paper 1, and how the
marks are divided between its two sections. **[3]**

**2.** Name the four assessment objectives tested on every Paper 1
question. **[4]**

**3.** State Cambridge's own definition of the command word "Explore." **[1]**

## Section B

**4.** Explain why Paper 1 is described as rewarding "close, textual
analysis over recall," given how the passages are presented on the
exam paper. **[3]**

**5.** A student's answer to an "Explore the ways in which…" question
briefly mentions six different quotations from the extract, spending
only one sentence on each. Explain why this approach is unlikely to
score highly, and suggest a better strategy. **[4]**

**6.** A paragraph states a point about a character, quotes a short
phrase describing them, explains what a specific word in that phrase
suggests, and closes by linking this to the writer's likely intention.

**(a)** Identify which assessment objectives this paragraph is
addressing, and explain how. **[4]**
**(b)** Explain why this paragraph structure tends to satisfy AO4
without needing a separate "I think…" sentence. **[2]**

**7.** Explain the difference between the two question styles
Cambridge sets on this paper — a direct personal-response question and
an implied one — and explain how recognising the difference should
change how explicitly personal response appears in the answer. **[3]**

**8.** A candidate spends 55 minutes on their first answer because it
"feels finished," leaving only 35 minutes for their second 25-mark
answer.

**(a)** Explain why this timing approach is likely to cost the
candidate marks overall. **[2]**
**(b)** Suggest a better approach to dividing the 90 minutes between
the two answers. **[2]**

**9.** Explain why misquoting the printed text, even in a small way,
undermines an analysis that focuses on a specific word choice. **[2]**

**10.** Explain why "narrating the plot of the extract" earns only
limited credit on Paper 1, even if the narration is entirely accurate. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** Paper 1 is 1 hour 30 minutes long and worth 50 marks in total
[1], divided into Section A (Poetry, 25 marks) and Section B (Prose,
25 marks) [2].

**2.** AO1 (knowledge of the content of the text, shown through
reference to detail and quotation), AO2 (understanding of characters,
relationships, situations and themes), AO3 (understanding of the
writer's intentions and methods, including response to their use of
language), and AO4 (personal response) [4].

**3.** "Write in detail about particular aspects" [1].

**4.** Because the relevant passage or poem is printed on the question
paper itself, candidates do not need to memorise the text word for
word [1]; this means revision time is better spent practising how to
analyse an unfamiliar or partly familiar extract in detail — moving
through what happens, what it reveals, how the writer achieves the
effect, and a personal response — rather than rote-learning quotations
[2].

**5.** Six briefly mentioned quotations are unlikely to score highly
because "Explore" specifically asks for sustained, detailed engagement
with a small number of well-chosen moments, not broad coverage of the
whole extract [2]. A better strategy is to select two or three
quotations and unpack each fully for language, structure and effect,
since this depth of analysis is what the command word is asking for
and what the mark scheme rewards over breadth [2].

**6. (a)** AO1 is addressed by referring to specific textual detail
(the quoted phrase) [1]; AO3 is addressed by analysing the specific
word choice within that quotation [1]; AO2 is addressed if the point
relates to character, relationship or theme [1]; and AO4 is addressed
by connecting the analysis to the writer's likely intention or effect
on the reader [1].
**(b)** Because personal response is being demonstrated through the
quality and direction of the analysis itself — what the writer's
choice is shown to achieve and why it matters — rather than requiring
a separate, explicitly stated opinion sentence, which examiners
identify as a weaker, more mechanical way of meeting AO4 [2].

**7.** A direct personal-response question ("What do you think about…")
asks candidates to state and support their own view explicitly from
the outset [1]. An implied question ("Explore the ways in which…")
asks candidates to build toward personal response through detailed
analysis, rather than stating an opinion upfront [1]. Recognising
which style is set changes whether personal response should be stated
directly and early, or allowed to emerge naturally through the
analysis across the answer [1].

**8. (a)** Spending 55 minutes on the first answer leaves only 35
minutes for the second 25-mark answer, which is likely to result in a
rushed, underdeveloped second response that cannot access the same
range of marks as a properly planned answer, even if the first answer
scores very highly [2].
**(b)** A roughly even split between the two answers, for example
around 5 minutes reading and planning and 35–38 minutes writing for
each, protects the overall mark total more reliably than writing until
the first answer "feels finished" [2].

**9.** Misquoting the text undermines the analysis because the
candidate is claiming to analyse the effect of a specific word or
phrase choice; if the quotation is inaccurate, the analysis is no
longer describing what the writer actually wrote, which examiners
notice and which weakens the credibility of the AO3 analysis being
offered [2].

**10.** Narrating the plot only demonstrates baseline AO1 knowledge of
what happens in the text, without engaging with AO2 (understanding of
meaning), AO3 (analysis of the writer's methods) or AO4 (personal
response) [2]. Since all four assessment objectives are marked on
every question, an accurate but purely descriptive answer cannot
access the marks reserved for analysis and personal engagement, no
matter how accurate the plot summary is [1].

## A note on exam technique for this topic

Because Paper 1's passages are printed fresh on the exam paper, the
skill genuinely being tested is analysis under exam conditions, not
memorisation — the most productive practice is annotating unfamiliar
or partly familiar extracts cold and building a timed answer that
folds all four assessment objectives into each paragraph, rather than
writing them as four separate sections. Keep a close eye on timing
discipline in practice sessions specifically, since a strong first
answer that overruns is one of the most common ways marks are lost on
this paper — not through poor textual knowledge, but through leaving
too little time for the second response to be developed properly.
