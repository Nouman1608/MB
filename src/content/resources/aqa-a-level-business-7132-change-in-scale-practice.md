These questions cover section 3.9.1 assessing a change in scale of the AQA A-level Business (7132) specification. All businesses and figures are invented. Revise first with the [study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-change-in-scale/) and the [revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-change-in-scale-revision-notes/).

## Section A: Short-answer questions

**1.** A furniture maker buys one of its timber suppliers. What type of integration is this? **[1]**

A: Horizontal

B: Forward vertical

C: Backward vertical

D: Conglomerate

**2.** Which **one** of the following is a diseconomy of scale? **[1]**

A: Buying materials at a bulk discount

B: Slower decisions caused by many layers of management

C: Spreading advertising costs over more units

D: Using larger, more efficient machinery

**3.** Define "overtrading". **[2]**

**4.** A franchisee earns revenue of £600,000 a year and pays the franchisor 5% of revenue. Calculate the annual payment. **[2]**

**5.** Explain **one** reason why a business might choose organic rather than external growth. **[3]**

## Section B: Structured questions

Calloway Ltd manufactures kitchen worktops. Its fixed costs are £250,000 a year and variable costs are £4 per unit. When output reaches 200,000 units, total costs rise to £1,450,000 because a second factory and extra managers are needed.

**6.** Calculate Calloway's average cost per unit at outputs of 50,000 and 150,000 units. **[2]**

**7.** Calculate the average cost at 200,000 units, and explain what this suggests about the firm's scale. **[3]**

**8.** Two firms, each with administrative costs of £200,000, plan to merge. Combined administration is expected to cost £320,000. Calculate the saving and explain what this is an example of. **[3]**

**9.** Calloway's revenue grows from £1.8m to £2.7m. It allows customers 40 days' credit. Calculate the extra money tied up in receivables, and explain why this might put the business at risk. **[4]**

**10.** Explain **two** possible effects on the human resources function of Calloway's decision to open a second factory. **[4]**

**11.** Calloway is considering buying a retailer that sells worktops direct to homeowners. Analyse **two** advantages and **one** risk of this strategy. **[6]**

**12.** A listed retailer has been losing money for two years and plans to close a third of its stores. Assess whether retrenchment is the right decision. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** **C** [1]. The supplier is earlier in the supply chain, so this is backward vertical integration. **[1]**

**2.** **B** [1]. Slower decisions with many layers of management raise costs in large firms. The others are economies of scale. **[1]**

**3.** Expanding sales faster than the business's finances can support [1], so that it runs short of cash, even though it may be profitable, because money is tied up in inventory and receivables [1]. **[2]**

**4.** 5% × £600,000 [1] = **£30,000** [1]. **[2]**

**5.** Organic growth is slower but usually lower risk [1], because it is funded from profit or loans in stages and the firm learns as it grows [1]. It also keeps control and culture within the business, avoiding the clashes that can follow a takeover [1]. **[3]**

**6.** At 50,000 units: (250,000 + 4 × 50,000) ÷ 50,000 = £450,000 ÷ 50,000 = **£9.00** [1]. At 150,000 units: (250,000 + 600,000) ÷ 150,000 = **£5.67** [1]. **[2]**

**7.** £1,450,000 ÷ 200,000 = **£7.25** [1]. This is higher than at 150,000 units (£5.67) [1], so Calloway is experiencing diseconomies of scale beyond about 150,000 units, caused by the extra factory and management [1]. **[3]**

**8.** Combined costs fall from £400,000 to £320,000, a saving of **£80,000** (20%) [1]. This is an example of synergy [1], because the combined business costs less to run than the two separate ones [1]. **[3]**

**9.** Extra revenue = £0.9m; extra receivables = £900,000 × 40 ÷ 365 [1] = **£98,630** [1]. This money is owed by customers and not yet in the bank [1], so Calloway needs extra cash to pay for materials and wages in the meantime; if it cannot find it, it may be unable to pay its bills (overtrading) even though sales are rising [1]. **[4]**

**10.** Effect 1: recruitment and training [1]. HR must recruit and train staff and supervisors for the new site, costing time and money [1]. Effect 2: communication and culture [1]. Staff at two sites may feel remote from each other and from management, so HR must work to keep morale and standards consistent [1]. **[4]**

**11.** Advantage 1: forward vertical integration lets Calloway capture the retailer's profit margin [1], increasing profit per unit sold [1]. Advantage 2: it gains direct contact with customers and control over how its products are sold [1], giving market information and a guaranteed outlet [1]. Risk: it has no retail experience [1], so it may manage the stores badly, and other retailers who buy from Calloway may stop buying from a competitor [1]. **[6]**

**12.** For: closing loss-making stores stops the losses and releases cash [1], which can reduce debt or fund better stores [1]. A smaller, focused business may have lower costs and a clearer strategy [1]. Against: closures cost money in redundancy and lease exit payments [1], and lower morale and reputation [1]. A smaller business may lose economies of scale and buying power [1]. If the problem is a weak offer rather than too many stores, closures will not fix it [1]. Judgement: retrenchment is likely to be right if the stores closed are the loss-makers and the remaining business is viable [1], but it should be part of a wider plan to improve the offer [1]. **[9]**

*Examiner insight:* The best answers weigh growth or retrenchment against the business's finances, objectives and the likely effect on each function.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Treating growth as always beneficial.
- Confusing overtrading with overspending.
- Mixing up forward and backward integration.
- Using total cost as if it were cost per unit.
- Ignoring the costs of retrenchment.
