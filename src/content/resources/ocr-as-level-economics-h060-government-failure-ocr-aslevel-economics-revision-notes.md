These notes condense the specification's requirement to explain "Government failure" and evaluate its "Causes and consequences". The content is part of topic 2.8 Government intervention, in Component 01, Microeconomics, of the OCR *AS Level Economics (H060)* specification, Version 3.0 (June 2026). For the full worked examples, read the [government failure study guide](/resources/ocr-as-level-economics-h060-government-failure-ocr-aslevel-economics/). ## Core recall

- **Government failure:** intervention leads to a less efficient allocation of resources, or a net welfare loss, compared with what could have been achieved.
- **Complete:** the outcome is worse than the free market.
- **Partial:** the outcome improves on the market but by less than it could, or at excessive cost.
- Market failure is a case for considering intervention; government failure is the risk that intervention makes things worse.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Unintended consequence | An effect of a policy that its designers did not expect or want |
| Black market | Illegal trade at prices above a legal maximum, or in banned goods |
| Distortion of price signals | Controls stop prices from rationing and signalling |
| Administrative cost | Cost of running, monitoring and enforcing a policy |
| Regulatory capture | A regulator acts in the interests of the regulated firms instead of the public |
| Short-termism | Choosing policies for quick or visible results, for example before an election |
| Conflicting objectives | Achieving one goal worsens another |
| Time lag | Delay between a problem, the policy decision and its effect |

## Causes: the checklist

1. Inadequate information (wrong tax, subsidy or limit).
2. Unintended consequences (black markets, smuggling, avoidance).
3. Distorted price signals (shortages, surpluses).
4. Administrative costs above the welfare gain.
5. Conflicting objectives.
6. Political self-interest and short-termism.
7. Regulatory capture.
8. Time lags.

## Consequences

- New or larger welfare losses from too much or too little output.
- Wasted public money and its opportunity cost.
- Shortages or higher black-market prices.
- Weakened incentives to cut costs or innovate.
- Unfair burdens on unintended groups.
- Loss of trust in future policy.

## Worked reminders

Market: Qd = 200 − 4P and Qs = 4P − 40, so P = £30 and Q = 80. MEC = £4.

- **Correct £4 tax:** Qs = 4P − 56, so P = £32 and Q = **72**. Loss with no tax = ½ × 8 × 4 = **16**.
- **Excessive £10 tax:** Qs = 4P − 80, so P = £35 and Q = **60**. At Q = 60 buyers value the last unit at £35 and the social cost is £25 + £4 = £29, a gap of £6. Loss = ½ × 12 × 6 = **36**, more than twice the loss with no tax: complete government failure.
- **Maximum price of £25:** Qd = 100 and Qs = 60, a shortage of 40. Buyers would pay up to (200 − 60) ÷ 4 = **£35** for the 60 units supplied, so a black market may charge more than the free-market price of £30.
- **Administration:** a scheme that removes a loss of 16 but costs 20 to run leaves society 4 worse off.

## Which tools carry most risk?

| Tool | Main government failure risk |
|---|---|
| Price controls | Shortages, surpluses, black markets |
| Taxes | Wrong rate; smuggling; regressive effects |
| Subsidies | Wrong rate; dependency; cost to taxpayers |
| Regulation | Enforcement cost; regulatory capture |
| Buffer stocks | Band set too high; stockpiles; running out of money |
| Government provision | Weak incentives to keep costs down |

## Evaluation points

- The real choice is between an imperfect market and imperfect intervention.
- Partial government failure may still improve on the market.
- Failure is more likely with poor information, price controls and weak enforcement.
- Pilot schemes, gradual changes, review and independent bodies lower the risk.
- Market-based tools keep price signals working; direct controls switch them off.
- A failing policy can be adjusted over time.

## Exam traps

- Government failure is about resource allocation, not just "a policy that does not work".
- Explain how each cause worsens allocation; a list of causes earns little.
- Do not conclude that government failure means never intervene.
- Include administrative costs in any judgement.
- Use figures from the question to show the welfare effect.

## Self-test

1. Define government failure.
2. Distinguish complete from partial government failure.
3. Give two causes of government failure.
4. Explain how a maximum price can lead to a black market.
5. What is regulatory capture?
6. Why does inadequate information cause government failure with a pollution tax?
7. A scheme removes a welfare loss of £50 million but costs £65 million to run. What is the net effect?
8. Qd = 100 − 2P and Qs = 3P − 50. Find the free-market equilibrium.
9. A maximum price of £25 is set in question 8. Find the shortage.
10. In question 9, what is the highest price buyers would pay for the quantity supplied?
11. Give one way of reducing the risk of government failure.
12. Why are price controls more likely than taxes to cause government failure?

### Answers

1. When government intervention leads to a less efficient allocation of resources or a net welfare loss.
2. Complete: worse than the free market. Partial: better than the market but less than possible, or at excessive cost.
3. Any two of: inadequate information, unintended consequences, distorted price signals, administrative costs, conflicting objectives, political self-interest, regulatory capture, time lags.
4. The ceiling creates a shortage, so buyers who cannot get the good legally are willing to pay more, and some sellers resell illegally at a higher price.
5. A regulator comes to act in the interests of the firms it regulates rather than consumers.
6. The government cannot measure the external cost exactly, so the tax may be set too high or too low and output does not reach the social optimum.
7. Society is **£15 million** worse off.
8. 100 − 2P = 3P − 50, so **P = £30** and **Q = 40**.
9. Qd = 50 and Qs = 25: shortage of **25**.
10. 100 − 2P = 25 gives **P = £37.50**.
11. Any one of: pilot schemes, gradual introduction, regular review, independent bodies, market-based tools.
12. They stop prices from rationing and signalling, creating shortages or surpluses and black markets, while taxes keep the price mechanism working.
