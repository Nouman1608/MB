## What the syllabus asks

The syllabus asks you to understand and use ratio and proportion to:

- give ratios in their simplest form
- divide a quantity in a given ratio
- use proportional reasoning and ratios in context, for example adapting recipes, using map scales and determining best value.

These questions appear on both papers and often form part of a longer problem.

## What a ratio means

A ratio such as 3 : 5 says that for every 3 parts of the first quantity there are 5 parts of the second. The order matters: 3 : 5 is not the same as 5 : 3. A ratio has no units, so both quantities must be in the same unit before you compare them.

## Simplest form

Divide every part of the ratio by the highest common factor. The simplified ratio has whole numbers with no common factor.

```
36 : 48 : 60       divide by 12     →   3 : 4 : 5
```

### Ratios with different units

Convert to the same unit first.

```
1.2 m : 40 cm      →   120 cm : 40 cm   →   3 : 1
45 min : 1 h 30 min →   45 min : 90 min  →   1 : 2
```

### Ratios with decimals

Multiply by a power of 10 to make every part a whole number, then simplify.

```
2.5 : 4     multiply by 10   →   25 : 40   →   5 : 8
```

### Ratios with fractions

Multiply by the lowest common multiple of the denominators.

```
3/4 : 5/6        LCM of 4 and 6 is 12
                 3/4 x 12 = 9,   5/6 x 12 = 10
                 →   9 : 10
```

## Equivalent ratios and finding a missing value

Multiplying or dividing every part of a ratio by the same number gives an equivalent ratio.

**Worked example.** Boys and girls in a school are in the ratio 7 : 5. There are 35 girls. How many boys are there, and how many students altogether?

```
girls: 5 parts = 35, so 1 part = 7
boys:  7 parts = 7 x 7 = 49
total: 49 + 35 = 84
```

**Worked example.** Two people have money in the ratio 5 : 3. The first has 24 more than the second. Find how much each has.

```
difference in parts: 5 - 3 = 2 parts
2 parts = 24, so 1 part = 12
first:  5 x 12 = 60
second: 3 x 12 = 36
check: 60 - 36 = 24 ✓
```

Notice that when you are told a difference, you use the difference in parts.

## Dividing a quantity in a given ratio

1. Add the parts to find the total number of parts.
2. Divide the quantity by the total number of parts to find one part.
3. Multiply by each ratio number.
4. Check that the shares add up to the original quantity.

**Worked example.** Share $360 in the ratio 5 : 3 : 4.

```
parts: 5 + 3 + 4 = 12
one part: 360 ÷ 12 = 30
shares: 5 x 30 = 150,   3 x 30 = 90,   4 x 30 = 120
check: 150 + 90 + 120 = 360 ✓
```

**Worked example.** A 7.2 m rope is cut in the ratio 5 : 4. Find the lengths.

```
9 parts; one part = 7.2 ÷ 9 = 0.8 m
5 x 0.8 = 4 m   and   4 x 0.8 = 3.2 m
```

**Worked example.** The angles of a triangle are in the ratio 2 : 3 : 7. Find the angles.

```
12 parts = 180°, so one part = 15°
angles: 30°, 45°, 105°
```

## Combining ratios

When two ratios share a quantity, make that quantity the same number in both.

**Worked example.** a : b = 2 : 3 and b : c = 4 : 5. Find a : b : c.

```
The common term is b. Make b equal in both ratios.
LCM of 3 and 4 is 12.
a : b = 2 : 3 = 8 : 12
b : c = 4 : 5 = 12 : 15
a : b : c = 8 : 12 : 15
```

## Changing a ratio after adding to a quantity

**Worked example.** A bag has red and blue counters in the ratio 3 : 5. There are 40 counters. Eight more red counters are added. Find the new ratio of red to blue.

```
8 parts = 40, so one part = 5
red = 15,  blue = 25
new red = 15 + 8 = 23
new ratio 23 : 25
```

The ratio 23 : 25 is already in simplest form because 23 is prime and does not divide 25.

## Proportional reasoning: the unitary method

When two quantities are in direct proportion, you can find the value for one unit first. Then scale up or down.

**Worked example.** A recipe for 4 people needs 250 g of flour and 3 eggs. How much is needed for 10 people?

```
for 1 person: 250 ÷ 4 = 62.5 g,   3 ÷ 4 = 0.75 egg
for 10 people: 62.5 x 10 = 625 g,   0.75 x 10 = 7.5 eggs
```

An answer of 7.5 eggs means the recipe needs rounding in practice, so use 8 eggs or change the number of people.

An alternative is to use a scale factor: 10 ÷ 4 = 2.5, so every quantity is multiplied by 2.5.

## Map scales

A map scale of 1 : 25 000 means that 1 cm on the map represents 25 000 cm in real life.

**Worked example.** On a map with scale 1 : 25 000, two villages are 8.4 cm apart. Find the real distance in kilometres.

```
real distance = 8.4 x 25 000 = 210 000 cm
210 000 cm = 2100 m = 2.1 km
```

**Worked example.** The real distance from a hotel to a beach is 3.5 km. How far apart are they on the same map?

```
3.5 km = 350 000 cm
map distance = 350 000 ÷ 25 000 = 14 cm
```

To change from cm to km, divide by 100 000. To change from km to cm, multiply by 100 000.

## Best value

To compare prices, find the cost per unit (per gram, per litre or per item), or the quantity per unit of money, and compare. Use the same unit throughout.

**Worked example.** Which is the best value: 750 g for $2.85, 1.2 kg for $4.32, or 500 g for $1.95?

```
cost per 100 g:
750 g:  2.85 ÷ 7.5 = $0.38
1200 g: 4.32 ÷ 12  = $0.36
500 g:  1.95 ÷ 5   = $0.39
```

The 1.2 kg pack is the best value at $0.36 per 100 g.

## Exam technique

- Show the number of parts and the value of one part. They carry the method marks.
- Check that shares add up to the whole quantity.
- Give ratios in simplest form, using whole numbers. A ratio of 2.5 : 1 should be written as 5 : 2.
- Convert units before simplifying.
- Read the question carefully for the order of the ratio (first to second).
- When given a difference, use the difference in parts, not the total.

## Common mistakes

- Dividing by the sum of the ratio numbers and then using that as one of the shares, rather than multiplying by each ratio number.
- Dividing the quantity by one of the ratio numbers, such as 360 ÷ 5.
- Reversing the order of the ratio.
- Simplifying ratios with units, such as 1.2 m : 40 cm, without converting.
- Using the total instead of the difference in parts.
- Comparing prices without using the same quantity or unit.
- Leaving a decimal in a ratio when the question asks for simplest form.

## Where this leads

Ratio is the foundation for rates, percentages, proportion, similar shapes, scale drawings and probability. The same unitary method appears in exchange rates, speeds and densities.
