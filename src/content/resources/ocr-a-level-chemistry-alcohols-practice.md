## Questions

**1.** State whether each alcohol is primary, secondary or tertiary.

**(a)** CH₃CH₂CH(CH₃)CH₂OH **[1]**
**(b)** CH₃CH(OH)CH₂CH₂CH₂CH₃ **[1]**
**(c)** CH₃CH₂C(CH₃)₂OH **[1]**

**2.** Use these values: propan-1-ol, Mᵣ 60.0, boiling point 97 °C; butane, Mᵣ 58.0, boiling point −1 °C.

**(a)** Explain the difference in boiling points. **[3]**
**(b)** Propan-1-ol mixes with water in all proportions, but heptan-1-ol is only slightly soluble. Explain this difference. **[2]**

**3.** Butan-1-ol burns completely in oxygen.

**(a)** Write the balanced equation. **[1]**
**(b)** A sample of 1.48 g of butan-1-ol is burned. Calculate the mass of carbon dioxide formed and the mass of oxygen used. **[3]**

**4.** A spirit burner of propan-1-ol is used to heat 150 g of water. Burning 0.528 g of the alcohol raises the water temperature by 21.0 K. The specific heat capacity of water is 4.18 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹.

**(a)** Calculate the enthalpy change of combustion of propan-1-ol, in kJ mol⁻¹, to 3 significant figures. **[3]**
**(b)** Suggest one reason why this value is less exothermic than the accepted value. **[1]**

**5.** Hexan-1-ol is oxidised by acidified potassium dichromate.

**(a)** Name the two organic products that can form. **[2]**
**(b)** Describe and explain the conditions you would use to obtain the first product rather than the second. **[3]**
**(c)** Write an equation, using [O], for the oxidation of hexan-1-ol to the second product. **[1]**

**6.** Samples of butan-2-ol, 2-methylpropan-2-ol and butanone are each warmed with acidified potassium dichromate.

**(a)** Predict what is observed for each compound. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain the result for 2-methylpropan-2-ol. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why this test cannot tell butanone apart from 2-methylpropan-2-ol. **[1]**
**(d)** Write an equation, using [O], for the reaction of butan-2-ol. **[1]**

**7.** 4-methylpentan-2-ol, CH₃CH(OH)CH₂CH(CH₃)₂, is converted into alkenes.

**(a)** State the reagent and the condition needed. **[2]**
**(b)** State the type of reaction. **[1]**
**(c)** Name the two structural isomers of the alkene that can form. **[2]**
**(d)** One of these alkenes exists as E and Z isomers. Identify it and explain why it shows E/Z isomerism. **[2]**

**8.** 2-bromopropane is made from propan-2-ol.

**(a)** State the reagents and the condition. **[2]**
**(b)** Write two equations: one for forming the hydrogen halide and one for the reaction with propan-2-ol. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why this is a substitution reaction. **[1]**

**9.** A student oxidises 4.64 g of heptan-1-ol, C₇H₁₅OH, with excess acidified potassium dichromate and collects 3.38 g of heptanoic acid, C₆H₁₃COOH.

**(a)** State the colour change and give the reason for using reflux. **[2]**
**(b)** Write an equation, using [O], for the reaction. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the percentage yield. **[3]**

**10.** Alcohol P belongs to the homologous series CₙH₂ₙ₊₁OH and has Mᵣ = 88.0. When P is warmed with acidified potassium dichromate, the solution stays orange.

**(a)** Show that P has the molecular formula C₅H₁₁OH. **[2]**
**(b)** Deduce the structure of P. Explain your reasoning. **[3]**
**(c)** P is heated with concentrated phosphoric acid. Name the two alkenes that form. **[2]**
**(d)** Name the organic product when P is heated with sodium bromide and sulfuric acid. **[1]**
**(e)** Q is an isomer of P. Q is a secondary alcohol with a branched carbon chain. Identify Q and name the product of its oxidation by acidified dichromate. **[2]**

**11.** 3-methylbutan-1-ol, (CH₃)₂CHCH₂CH₂OH, is used to make three different compounds.

**(a)** Classify 3-methylbutan-1-ol. **[1]**
**(b)** For each conversion, state the reagent(s) and conditions, and give the structural formula of the organic product.

**(i)** to a carboxylic acid **[2]**
**(ii)** to an alkene **[2]**
**(iii)** to a bromoalkane **[2]**

**(c)** Explain why only one structural isomer of the alkene forms in (b)(ii). **[1]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** **Primary**: one carbon is attached to the carbon carrying the OH [1]
**(b)** **Secondary**: the C–OH carbon is C2, bonded to C1 and C3 [1]
**(c)** **Tertiary**: three carbons are attached to the carbon carrying the OH [1]
*Examiner insight:* In (c), drawing out the condensed formula shows the C–OH carbon has no hydrogen and three carbon neighbours.

**2. (a)** Both have similar numbers of electrons, so similar London forces [1]. Propan-1-ol also has hydrogen bonds between –OH groups of neighbouring molecules [1]. Hydrogen bonds are the stronger force, so separating the molecules takes more energy, giving the **higher boiling point** [1]
**(b)** Propan-1-ol's –OH forms hydrogen bonds with water molecules [1]. Heptan-1-ol has a much longer non-polar chain that cannot hydrogen-bond, so it disrupts the hydrogen bonding in water and **solubility is low** [1]
*Examiner insight:* "Alcohols have hydrogen bonds" earns little alone; place the hydrogen bonds between molecules and compare their strength with London forces.

**3. (a)** **C₄H₉OH + 6O₂ → 4CO₂ + 5H₂O** [1]
**(b)** n(C₄H₉OH) = 1.48 ÷ 74.0 = 0.0200 mol [1]; n(CO₂) = 4 × 0.0200 = 0.0800 mol, mass = 0.0800 × 44.0 = **3.52 g** [1]; n(O₂) = 6 × 0.0200 = 0.120 mol, mass = 0.120 × 32.0 = **3.84 g** [1]
*Examiner insight:* Doubling to 2C₄H₉OH + 12O₂ is equally correct, but forgetting the alcohol's own oxygen gives 6.5O₂ per molecule and makes every later mole ratio wrong.

**4. (a)** q = 150 × 4.18 × 21.0 = 13 167 J = 13.2 kJ [1]; n(propan-1-ol) = 0.528 ÷ 60.0 = 0.00880 mol [1]; ΔcH = −13.167 ÷ 0.00880 = **−1.50 × 10³ kJ mol⁻¹** [1]
**(b)** Heat is lost to the surroundings instead of reaching the water (or combustion is incomplete) [1]
*Examiner insight:* In q = mcΔT, m is the water's mass, not the alcohol's; give the negative sign; a positive value describes an endothermic change, which combustion is not.

**5. (a)** **Hexanal** [1] and **hexanoic acid** [1]
**(b)** Excess hexan-1-ol (limited oxidising agent), gentle heat [1]. Distil the hexanal off as it forms [1]. Hexanal molecules do not hydrogen-bond to each other, so it boils lower than hexan-1-ol and leaves before further oxidation [1]
**(c)** **CH₃(CH₂)₄CH₂OH + 2[O] → CH₃(CH₂)₄COOH + H₂O** [1]
*Examiner insight:* Link distilling the aldehyde off as it forms to stopping further oxidation; "distil" alone explains nothing.

**6. (a)** 2-methylpropan-2-ol and butanone: **stay orange** [1]; butan-2-ol: **orange to green** [1]
**(b)** It is a tertiary alcohol [1]; there is no hydrogen atom on the carbon bonded to the –OH, so it cannot be oxidised [1]
**(c)** Neither compound is oxidised by acidified dichromate, so both give the same result (no colour change) [1]
**(d)** **CH₃CH(OH)CH₂CH₃ + [O] → CH₃COCH₂CH₃ + H₂O** [1]
*Examiner insight:* Describe the colour change from start to finish; "turns green" alone is incomplete.

**7. (a)** Concentrated phosphoric acid (or concentrated sulfuric acid) as catalyst [1]; heat [1]
**(b)** **Elimination** (dehydration) [1]
**(c)** **4-methylpent-1-ene** [1] and **4-methylpent-2-ene** [1]
**(d)** **4-methylpent-2-ene** [1]. Both C=C carbons carry two different groups (C2: H and CH₃; C3: H and CH(CH₃)₂), and rotation about the C=C is restricted [1]
*Examiner insight:* In (c), listing the E and Z forms as two structural isomers confuses structural isomerism with stereoisomerism.

**8. (a)** Sodium bromide [1] and sulfuric acid, heated [1]
**(b)** NaBr + H₂SO₄ → NaHSO₄ + HBr [1]; **CH₃CH(OH)CH₃ + HBr → CH₃CHBrCH₃ + H₂O** [1]
**(c)** The –OH group is replaced by a bromine atom [1]
*Examiner insight:* Bromine is the reagent for alkenes, not for this conversion; naming Br₂ here gains no credit.

**9. (a)** Orange to green [1]. The condenser sends the volatile aldehyde intermediate back into the flask, and there it is oxidised to the acid [1]
**(b)** **CH₃(CH₂)₅CH₂OH + 2[O] → CH₃(CH₂)₅COOH + H₂O** [1]
**(c)** n(heptan-1-ol) = 4.64 ÷ 116.0 = 0.0400 mol [1]; theoretical mass of acid = 0.0400 × 130.0 = 5.20 g [1]; yield = 3.38 ÷ 5.20 × 100 = **65.0%** [1]
*Examiner insight:* Dividing 3.38 g by 4.64 g compares two different substances and gives a meaningless 72.8%; find the theoretical mass first.

**10. (a)** Mᵣ of CₙH₂ₙ₊₁OH = 12n + 2n + 1 + 16 + 1 = 14n + 18 [1]; 14n + 18 = 88.0, so n = 5 and P is **C₅H₁₁OH** [1]
**(b)** Not oxidised, so P is tertiary [1]. The C–OH carbon carries three groups made from four carbons: CH₃, CH₃ and C₂H₅ [1]. P is **2-methylbutan-2-ol, CH₃CH₂C(CH₃)₂OH** [1]
**(c)** **2-methylbut-1-ene**, CH₂=C(CH₃)CH₂CH₃ [1], and **2-methylbut-2-ene**, (CH₃)₂C=CHCH₃ [1]
**(d)** **2-bromo-2-methylbutane** [1]
**(e)** Q is **3-methylbutan-2-ol**, CH₃CH(OH)CH(CH₃)₂ [1]; it is oxidised to **3-methylbutan-2-one**, CH₃COCH(CH₃)₂ [1]
*Examiner insight:* In (b), link the colour test to "tertiary, so not oxidised"; a correct name without that reasoning is only part of the answer.

**11. (a)** **Primary** [1]
**(b)(i)** Excess acidified potassium dichromate, heat under reflux [1]; **(CH₃)₂CHCH₂COOH** [1]
**(ii)** Concentrated H₃PO₄ (or H₂SO₄), heat [1]; **(CH₃)₂CHCH=CH₂** [1]
**(iii)** NaBr and H₂SO₄, heat [1]; **(CH₃)₂CHCH₂CH₂Br** [1]
**(c)** The –OH is on C1, so the only neighbouring carbon is C2; the C=C can only form between C1 and C2 [1]
*Examiner insight:* Be specific: "acid" alone is too vague for (b)(ii), and (b)(i) needs both "excess" and "reflux".

## Where marks are usually lost

- Comparing an alcohol with an alkane of very different Mᵣ, so the argument about London forces fails.
- Forgetting the oxygen in the alcohol when balancing combustion equations.
- Leaving out water, or writing [O] where 2[O] is needed, in oxidation equations.
- Using reflux for the aldehyde.
- Giving "no reaction" for a tertiary alcohol without the observation (stays orange).
- Missing the second alkene from an alcohol whose C–OH carbon has two unlike H-bearing neighbours.
- Naming bromine, not NaBr and H₂SO₄, for making a bromoalkane.
