A **warehouse** is a building used to store goods until they are needed. In the supply chain it sits between production and sale, holding stock so that businesses can match what they have to what customers want. Section 4.2 of the Cambridge IGCSE Commerce (0715) syllabus asks you to know four things: the **functions of warehousing** in the supply chain, the **different types of warehouse**, the **uses of automation** in warehousing, and the **benefits of warehouse automation**. This guide covers each one, with a worked cost example and an annotated answer.

## What are the functions of warehousing in the supply chain?

The syllabus names five functions. Each one answers the question "why not just sell the goods straight away?"

### Links with seasonal production

Some goods are made or grown at one time of year but sold all year. Wheat is harvested once a year, yet people buy bread every day. A warehouse holds the harvest so that supply continues between harvests. The same idea works the other way round: a toy maker may produce steadily all year and store toys to sell in the weeks before a festival.

### Links with demand

Demand may be seasonal even when production is steady. Umbrellas, heaters and school uniforms sell in bursts. Warehouses let a business build up stock before the busy period and release it when customers arrive, so it does not run out of stock and lose sales.

### Protecting supply

Stock held in a warehouse acts as a **buffer**. If a supplier is late, a ship is delayed or a factory stops, the business can keep serving customers from the stored goods. This protects the supply chain from disruption.

### Price stability

If everything harvested were sold at once, prices would fall sharply at harvest and rise sharply later. By storing goods when supply is high and releasing them when supply is low, warehouses help to even out price changes. This benefits both producers and consumers.

### Logistics postponement

**Logistics postponement** means delaying the final steps, such as packing, labelling or assembling, until an order is known. A warehouse holds goods in a plain or unfinished state and completes them when a customer asks. For example, a firm may store plain boxes of phone chargers and add the right plug type for each country only when an order arrives. This avoids holding large stocks of every finished version and reduces waste.

| Function | One-line meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Seasonal production | Store goods made at one time of year to sell all year | Harvested rice kept for twelve months |
| Demand | Build up stock before a busy period | Winter coats stored in summer |
| Protecting supply | Stock acts as a buffer against delays | A shop keeps spare stock if a ship is late |
| Price stability | Store when supply is high, release when low | Fruit stored at harvest |
| Logistics postponement | Delay final packing or labelling until the order is known | Plug type added when the order arrives |

## What types of warehouse does the syllabus list?

There are four types to know.

| Type | What it is | Typical goods |
|---|---|---|
| **Bonded warehouse** | A secure warehouse under customs control, where imported goods are stored without paying import duty until they are released | Imported wine, spirits, tobacco, electronics |
| **Cold storage** | A warehouse kept at low temperature, using refrigeration or freezing | Meat, fish, dairy, fruit, vegetables, medicines |
| **Cash and carry** | A self-service wholesale warehouse where trade customers pay at once and take goods away | Groceries, drinks, catering supplies |
| **Regional distribution centre (RDC)** | A large warehouse that receives goods in bulk and sends them on to shops in a region | Supermarket stock for all stores in an area |

### Bonded warehouse in more detail

When goods arrive from another country, the importer usually pays **import duty** (a tax) at the border. If the goods are put in a bonded warehouse, the duty is **delayed** until the goods are taken out for sale. The importer may also re-export the goods without ever paying duty. This helps cash flow, because the importer does not pay tax on goods that have not yet been sold.

### Regional distribution centres (RDCs)

A supermarket chain does not usually send goods from suppliers straight to each shop. Suppliers deliver large loads to an RDC, which sorts goods and sends mixed loads to the shops. This means shops receive one delivery instead of many, and suppliers make fewer trips.

## What is warehouse automation, and where is it used?

**Automation** means using machines and computer systems to do work that people once did by hand. The syllabus lists three uses.

- **Robotics.** Robots move goods around the warehouse, lift pallets, stack shelves or carry items to packing stations. They can work through the night without breaks.
- **Automated sorting.** Conveyor belts and scanners read barcodes or codes on parcels and send each one to the right chute or lorry bay without staff deciding the route.
- **Voice picking.** Staff wear a headset. The system tells them by voice which item to pick and from which shelf, and they confirm by speaking. Their hands and eyes are free to handle the goods.

## What are the benefits of warehouse automation?

| Benefit | How automation helps |
|---|---|
| Lower labour costs | Fewer staff are needed for repetitive tasks, and robots do not need breaks |
| Less human error | Machines follow the same steps every time; fewer wrong or damaged items |
| Improved picking accuracy | Barcodes and voice systems check that the right item and quantity are chosen |
| Improved inventory control | The system records every item moved, so the stock record is always current |
| Improved customer service | Orders are packed correctly and sent faster, so customers receive the right goods on time |

Automation also has drawbacks: robots, scanners and software cost a great deal to buy and maintain, staff need training, and a power or system failure can stop the whole warehouse. These are not listed in the syllabus, but they help in evaluation questions.

## Worked example: is automation worth it? (invented figures)

*A warehouse packs customer orders by hand. It uses 6 pickers, each paid $90 per day. A new scanning system would need only 2 pickers, but it costs $140 per day to run (including its purchase cost spread over its life).*

1. Current labour cost per day: 6 x 90 = **$540**.
2. With the system, labour: 2 x 90 = $180.
3. Add the daily cost of the system: 180 + 140 = **$320**.
4. Saving per day: 540 - 320 = **$220**.
5. Saving over a 250-day working year: 220 x 250 = **$55,000**.

**Judgement:** the system saves $220 a day on these figures. It would also reduce picking errors, which gives extra benefits that the calculation does not show, such as fewer returned orders. The firm should still check whether it can afford the purchase and whether orders are high enough to keep the system busy.

## Annotated model answer

*Question: Explain two ways in which a regional distribution centre helps a supermarket chain.* **[4]**

> An RDC receives **large deliveries** from suppliers and holds the goods centrally [1], so the supermarket chain does not need every shop to take lots of separate deliveries from suppliers, which saves time and transport costs [1]. The RDC can also **send mixed loads** to each shop, according to what that shop has sold [1], so shelves are refilled quickly and the shop needs less storage space at the back [1].

Why it scores: each point states what the RDC does, then explains the benefit to the chain.

## What are the common mistakes in 4.2?

- Saying warehouses only "store goods" without naming a function such as price stability or protecting supply.
- Confusing a **bonded warehouse** (customs control, delayed duty) with any warehouse that stores imported goods.
- Describing **cash and carry** as delivering goods to retailers. It does not: customers collect.
- Writing "robots" for every example of automation. Voice picking and automated sorting are different uses.
- Giving benefits of automation without saying **how** the machine produces them (for example, naming "accuracy" but not barcodes or voice checks).

## Exam technique

- **Identify** or **state**: give the name only (for example "cold storage").
- **Describe** a type of warehouse: say what it is and what it stores or does.
- **Explain** a function: link the warehouse to the business problem it solves (for example seasonal supply).
- **Assess** automation: give benefits and a limit, then decide for the business in the case.
- In calculations, show every step and keep the same unit (per day, per year).
