This guide covers **Question 3a, the Summary task**, in Section B of Cambridge O Level English Language
1123 Paper 1 Reading. The syllabus says: "Candidates answer a summary task in response to Text B.
Candidates write a summary as continuous writing of no more than 150 words." Text B is "approximately
550–600 words long". The task tests reading objectives **R1** and **R5** (10 marks) and writing
objectives **W2** and **W3** (10 marks). For the paper as a whole see the
[Paper 1 Reading study guide](/resources/o-level-english-language-paper-1-reading/). This page is about
the process of writing a summary that earns both sets of marks.

## Two halves of one answer

| Half | Objectives | What earns the marks |
|---|---|---|
| Reading (10) | R1 explicit meanings; R5 select and use information for specific purposes | choosing every relevant point, and only relevant points |
| Writing (10) | W2 organise and structure ideas for deliberate effect; W3 a range of vocabulary and sentence structures appropriate to context | grouping and linking the points into clear, concise prose in your own words |

The syllabus defines **Summarise** as "select and present the main points, without detail". Both
halves are in that definition: *select* is the reading half; *present* is the writing half.

## Step 1: find the focus

The question never asks you to summarise the whole text. It names a focus, for example *the benefits
of*, *the problems faced by*, *the reasons why*, *what X did to...*. Underline it. Then decide what
does **not** count: if the focus is benefits, drawbacks are excluded however interesting; if it is
what volunteers did, what the government did is excluded.

## Step 2: select the points

Read Text B once for its overall argument. On the second reading, mark each sentence or phrase that
answers the focus. Then:

- **Strip the detail.** Examples, statistics and anecdotes usually illustrate a point; the point is
  what you keep. *"Last year Maria, a nurse from Lisbon, saved enough on fuel to pay for a holiday"*
  illustrates *cycling saves money*.
- **Merge repeats.** Writers restate key ideas; count each idea once.
- **List in note form** in the margin or on the planning page: eight to ten short notes is typical.

## Step 3: group and order

A list of points in the order they appear produces a list-like summary. Grouping produces structure
(W2). Common groupings: benefits to individuals / to society; causes / effects; short-term /
long-term; what is done / why it works. Decide two or three groups and assign each note to one.

## Step 4: write in your own words

You may keep technical terms or words with no simple equivalent, but phrase the sentences yourself.
Methods:

1. **Generalise**: *"buses, trains and trams"* becomes *"public transport"*.
2. **Compress**: *"people who live in the area and the businesses that operate there"* becomes
   *"local residents and businesses"*.
3. **Re-structure**: turn a list of separate sentences into one sentence with a shared subject.

Write continuous prose. Bullet points, headings and numbered lists are not "continuous writing".

## Step 5: check the limit

The limit is a ceiling: "no more than 150 words". Count roughly by lines as you write (find your
average words per line beforehand), then check exactly at the end. A summary well under the limit has
probably missed points; one over it has spent words on detail.

## Worked example

Shortened Text B (original, written for this guide):

> Across many cities, schools are turning their concrete playgrounds into gardens, and the results are
> striking. Teachers report that children who spend breaktime among plants return to lessons calmer
> and better able to concentrate. One head teacher in a busy inner-city school said the number of
> playground arguments had fallen noticeably since the garden opened.
>
> The gardens are also classrooms in their own right. Pupils measure plant growth in mathematics,
> study insects in science and write about the changing seasons. For children who struggle in a
> traditional classroom, practical work outdoors can be the first time they feel successful at school.
>
> Not everyone is convinced. Some parents worry about mud, insects and allergies, and gardens need
> regular care during the long summer holiday. But supporters argue that the benefits reach beyond the
> school gate: a green space cools the surrounding streets on hot days, provides a home for birds and
> bees, and, when it is opened at weekends, gives local families somewhere to meet.

Task: *Summarise the benefits of school gardens, as described in the text.*

Plan (notes, grouped):

- *Pupils' well-being*: calmer after break; better concentration; fewer arguments.
- *Learning*: used in maths, science and writing; practical success for those who struggle.
- *Community and environment*: cooler streets; habitat for wildlife; meeting place for families.

Excluded: parents' worries; holiday care (drawbacks); the head teacher's identity (detail).

Model summary (74 words, for a shortened text):

> School gardens improve pupils' behaviour and focus: children return from break calmer, concentrate
> better and argue less. The gardens also become places of learning, supporting several subjects
> through hands-on activities and giving pupils who find ordinary lessons difficult a chance to
> succeed. Their value extends to the wider area too, since green spaces lower the temperature of
> nearby streets, shelter wildlife and, when opened at weekends, offer local families a place to come
> together.

*Commentary:* all eight points are present (R1, R5); none of the drawbacks appears. Three groups give
three sentences, joined by signposts ("also", "too"), which is the deliberate structure W2 rewards.
Phrasing is the candidate's own ("lower the temperature of nearby streets" for "cools the surrounding
streets"), with varied sentence forms (W3). A real Text B is about twice as long and would give more
points, so a full answer would approach, but not exceed, 150 words.

## When the focus has two parts

Some tasks combine two aspects, such as *the problems faced by volunteers and how they overcame
them*. Treat each aspect as a group in your plan, check you have points for both, and keep them in
separate sentences so the examiner can see that both have been answered. A summary that covers one
aspect fully and the other in a single phrase usually misses reading marks it could have had.

## Common mistakes

- **Summarising the whole text** instead of the focus.
- **Keeping examples and figures** that illustrate points.
- **Copying sentences** from Text B, which limits the writing marks.
- **A list in sentence form**: "Firstly... Secondly... Thirdly..." through every point, with no grouping.
- **Adding opinion or comment**: "This is a wonderful idea" is not in the text.
- **Exceeding the limit**: points made after the limit is reached are not credited.

## Exam technique

- Plan before writing: points first, then groups, then prose.
- Aim for one sentence per group, sometimes two.
- Use linking words that show relationships (*as a result*, *in addition*, *however*), not just
  sequence.
- Question 3a is the heaviest question on the paper (20 marks): give it a fair share of Section B's
  time, leaving room for the 5-mark Question 3b short response on the same text.
