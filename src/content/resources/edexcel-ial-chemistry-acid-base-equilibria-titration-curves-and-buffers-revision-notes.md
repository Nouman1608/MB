## Key definitions

- **Equivalence point**: the volume at which acid and base have reacted exactly in the ratio of the equation.
- **End point**: the volume at which the indicator changes colour. A good indicator makes the end point match the equivalence point.
- **Buffer solution (14.17)**: a solution whose pH changes very little when a small amount of acid or alkali is added to it.
- **Acidic buffer**: weak acid + its conjugate base (salt), e.g. propanoic acid + sodium propanoate.
- **Basic buffer**: weak base + its conjugate acid (salt), e.g. NH₃ + NH₄Cl.
- **Half-neutralisation point**: half the equivalence volume; here [HA] = [A⁻] and pH = pKa.

## Titration curves at a glance (14.15)

Base added to 25 cm³ of acid of similar concentration:

| Pair | Start pH | Equivalence pH | Steep section |
|---|---|---|---|
| Strong acid, strong base | ~1 | 7 | ~3 to ~11 |
| Weak acid, strong base | ~3 | >7 | ~7 to ~11 |
| Strong acid, weak base | ~1 | <7 | ~3 to ~7 |
| Weak acid, weak base | ~3 | ~7 | none sharp |

Why the equivalence pH moves:

- Weak acid + strong base gives a salt whose anion (A⁻) accepts protons from water, so the solution is alkaline.
- Strong acid + weak base gives a salt whose cation (e.g. NH₄⁺) donates protons, so the solution is acidic.

**Acid added to base**: same shapes, reflected; the pH starts high and falls.

**Sketch checklist**: label both axes (pH; volume added / cm³), mark the equivalence volume, the start pH, the half-way pH (weak species only) and a final pH that levels off near the burette solution's pH.

### Diprotic acids and bases

- Diprotic weak acid H₂X with NaOH: two steep sections if pKa₁ and pKa₂ are well apart. Second equivalence volume = 2 × first.
- pH at half the first equivalence volume = pKa₁; pH midway between the two equivalence volumes = pKa₂.
- Sulfuric acid with NaOH: one steep section; twice the moles of NaOH needed compared with HCl.
- Sodium carbonate with HCl (diprotic base): CO₃²⁻ + H⁺ → HCO₃⁻, then HCO₃⁻ + H⁺ → H₂O + CO₂. Two steep sections, the second at twice the first volume.

## Indicators (14.16)

**Rule**: the indicator's whole colour-change range must lie inside the steep section of the curve.

| Titration | Indicator |
|---|---|
| Strong acid, strong base | almost any (methyl orange, phenolphthalein, bromothymol blue) |
| Weak acid, strong base | phenolphthalein (range about 8.2 to 10.0) |
| Strong acid, weak base | methyl orange (range about 3.1 to 4.4) |
| Weak acid, weak base | none: use a pH meter |
| Na₂CO₃ with HCl | phenolphthalein first, then methyl orange |

Colours: methyl orange red in acid, yellow in alkali; phenolphthalein colourless in acid, pink in alkali. In an exam, use the ranges you are given.

## How a buffer works (14.18)

Acidic buffer equilibrium: HA ⇌ H⁺ + A⁻. The solution holds big reservoirs of HA and of A⁻.

| Added | What removes it | Equation |
|---|---|---|
| H⁺ | conjugate base A⁻ | H⁺ + A⁻ → HA |
| OH⁻ | weak acid HA | OH⁻ + HA → A⁻ + H₂O |

For NH₃/NH₄⁺: H⁺ + NH₃ → NH₄⁺ and OH⁻ + NH₄⁺ → NH₃ + H₂O.

Key phrase for full answers: the **ratio** [HA]/[A⁻] changes only slightly because both are present in **large** amounts compared with the acid or alkali added, so [H⁺] changes only slightly.

## Formulas

| Use | Formula |
|---|---|
| Buffer pH (14.19) | [H⁺] = Ka × [HA]/[A⁻] |
| Same, log form | pH = pKa − log([HA]/[A⁻]) |
| Buffer recipe (14.20) | [A⁻] = Ka × [HA]/[H⁺] |
| Half-neutralisation (14.21) | pH = pKa, so Ka = 10^-pH |
| Start of a weak acid curve | [H⁺] = √(Ka × [HA]) |
| Excess strong base | [H⁺] = Kw/[OH⁻], Kw = 1.00 × 10⁻¹⁴ mol² dm⁻⁶ at 298 K |

Assumptions for buffer pH: the weak acid's own dissociation is negligible, and all A⁻ comes from the salt (or from neutralisation).

## Method boxes

**Buffer pH by partial neutralisation**

1. Moles of weak acid at the start.
2. Moles of OH⁻ added.
3. HA left = step 1 − step 2; A⁻ formed = step 2.
4. [H⁺] = Ka × (HA left)/(A⁻ formed). Moles are fine: the volume cancels.
5. pH = −log[H⁺].

**New buffer pH after adding a little strong acid**: add the moles of H⁺ to HA and subtract them from A⁻, then repeat step 4. For strong alkali, do the reverse.

**Making a buffer of a chosen pH**

1. [H⁺] = 10^-pH.
2. [A⁻] = Ka × [HA]/[H⁺].
3. Moles of salt = [A⁻] × volume in dm³; mass = moles × Mr.
4. State the assumption that the volume does not change when the solid dissolves.

**Ka from a titration curve**

1. Find the equivalence volume at the middle of the steep section.
2. Halve it.
3. Read the pH at that volume: this is pKa. Ka = 10^-pKa.

## Buffers in living things and food (14.22)

- Blood pH stays close to 7.4. Main buffer: H₂CO₃ ⇌ H⁺ + HCO₃⁻.
- Extra acid from cells (e.g. lactic acid): H⁺ + HCO₃⁻ → H₂CO₃, removed as CO₂ through the lungs.
- Cells: phosphate and protein buffers keep enzymes working in their narrow pH range.
- Foods: bacteria and fungi can change pH as they grow; a buffer resists that change and slows deterioration.

## Core Practical 11: Ka for a weak acid (14.23)

- Calibrate the pH meter with buffers of known pH.
- Titrate a portion of the weak acid with NaOH (phenolphthalein) to find the end-point volume.
- Make a half-neutralised solution: a second identical portion of acid plus half that volume of NaOH (or add an equal portion of acid to the neutralised flask).
- pH of this solution = pKa. No concentrations are needed.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Equivalence point vs end point**: one is chemistry, the other is the indicator.
- **Equivalence point vs half-neutralisation point**: pH = pKa only at the half-way volume.
- **Buffer region vs steep section**: flat part = buffering; steep part = buffer used up.
- **Diluting a buffer vs adding acid**: dilution leaves the [HA]/[A⁻] ratio and pH almost unchanged; adding acid shifts the ratio.

## Quick self-test

1. Define a buffer solution.
2. Methyl orange changes over pH 3.1 to 4.4 and phenolphthalein over 8.2 to 10.0. Which suits hydrochloric acid titrated against aqueous ammonia?
3. A buffer is 0.30 mol dm⁻³ HA and 0.20 mol dm⁻³ NaA. Ka = 6.0 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³. Find the pH.
4. A weak acid's curve has its equivalence point at 26.0 cm³; at 13.0 cm³ the pH is 4.40. Find Ka.
5. What ratio [A⁻]/[HA] gives a pH 0.30 above the pKa?
6. Why do titres for a weak acid against a weak base vary so much when an indicator is used?
7. In a sodium carbonate titration with HCl, the first end point is at 9.0 cm³. Where is the second?
8. Write the equation for how blood removes extra H⁺.
9. A buffer is diluted to twice its volume with water. What happens to its pH?
10. A solution contains 0.0200 mol HA (pKa = 4.35) and 0.0050 mol NaOH. Find the pH.
11. A diprotic acid's curve has equivalence points at 11.0 and 22.0 cm³. At which volumes does the pH equal pKa₁ and pKa₂?

### Answers

1. A solution whose pH changes very little when a small amount of acid or alkali is added.
2. Methyl orange: strong acid with weak base has its steep section below pH 7.
3. [H⁺] = 6.0 × 10⁻⁵ × 0.30/0.20 = 9.0 × 10⁻⁵; pH = **4.05**.
4. pKa = 4.40; Ka = 10^-4.40 = **4.0 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³**.
5. log([A⁻]/[HA]) = 0.30, so the ratio is **2.0**.
6. The pH rises gradually near equivalence with no steep section, so the colour change is spread over several cm³; use a pH meter instead.
7. **18.0 cm³** (twice the first).
8. H⁺ + HCO₃⁻ → H₂CO₃.
9. Almost **no change**: both concentrations fall by the same factor, so the ratio is unchanged.
10. HA = 0.0150 mol, A⁻ = 0.0050 mol; pH = 4.35 − log 3.0 = **3.87**.
11. pKa₁ at **5.5 cm³**; pKa₂ at **16.5 cm³**.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Putting every equivalence point at pH 7.
- Choosing an indicator because its range touches the steep section, rather than lying wholly inside it.
- Using starting moles in the buffer ratio instead of moles left after neutralisation.
- Inverting the ratio, writing Ka × [A⁻]/[HA].
- Reading the pH at the equivalence volume and calling it pKa.
- Explaining buffer action without an equation, or without saying that HA and A⁻ are present in large amounts.
- Forgetting that the second end point for a diprotic species is at twice the first volume.
- Describing blood buffering without naming HCO₃⁻ and H₂CO₃.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the difference between the equivalence point and the end point?**
The equivalence point is the volume at which acid and base have reacted exactly in the ratio of the equation. The end point is where the indicator changes colour; a good indicator makes the two match.

**What is a basic buffer?**
A basic buffer contains a weak base and its conjugate acid, for example ammonia with ammonium chloride. An acidic buffer contains a weak acid and its conjugate base.

**Where is the second end point for a diprotic species?**
For a diprotic acid or base, the second end point comes at twice the volume of the first. Forgetting this two-step stoichiometry is a common error in Edexcel IAL Chemistry questions.

**Which species buffer blood?**
Blood is buffered by the carbonic acid–hydrogencarbonate system, H₂CO₃ and HCO₃⁻. Describing blood buffering without naming these species loses marks.
