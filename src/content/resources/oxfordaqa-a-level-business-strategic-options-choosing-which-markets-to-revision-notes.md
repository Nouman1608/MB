## Key definitions

- **Strategy**: a long-term plan for how a business will achieve its objectives, including which markets it competes in and what products it offers.
- **Tactic**: a short-term action that helps carry out a strategy.
- **Strategic decision**: a decision that is difficult to reverse, high risk, involves significant resources and is usually one-off.
- **SWOT analysis**: strengths and weaknesses (internal, present) plus opportunities and threats (external, future).
- **Functional decisions**: choices made within marketing, operations, HR and finance to deliver the strategy.
- **Ansoff matrix**: a grid of four growth strategies defined by existing or new products and existing or new markets.
- **Market penetration**: more sales of existing products in existing markets.
- **Market development**: existing products in new markets (new places or new segments).
- **Product development**: new products for existing markets.
- **Diversification**: new products in new markets.

## Significance of a strategy (four quick points)

1. Gives a common direction for every function.
2. Guides allocation of scarce resources.
3. Provides a benchmark for judging later decisions.
4. Builds on strengths and opportunities, raising the chance of meeting objectives.

## The Ansoff matrix

| | Existing products | New products |
|---|---|---|
| **Existing markets** | Market penetration (lowest risk) | Product development |
| **New markets** | Market development | Diversification (highest risk) |

**Value of each, in one line each side:**

| Strategy | For | Against |
|---|---|---|
| Market penetration | Cheap, uses known products and customers | Limited by market size; rivals retaliate |
| Market development | New sales for proven products; spreads market risk | Unfamiliar customers, laws and competitors |
| Product development | Uses brand and customer knowledge | R&D cost; new products often fail; may cannibalise sales |
| Diversification | Spreads risk; access to new growth | No product or market knowledge; biggest outlay |

## Assessing value: what the right choice depends on

An "assess the value" answer weighs the option against the case, not against the textbook. Check:

- **Objectives**: rapid growth may justify more risk; survival or stable profit favours penetration.
- **Market conditions**: a saturated or shrinking home market weakens the case for penetration.
- **Resources**: spare capacity, cash, low gearing and relevant skills make development options realistic.
- **Competitor response**: strong rivals may match price cuts or copy new products quickly.
- **Return against risk**: a bigger expected gain can justify a riskier box if failure would not sink the business.

**Worked reminder.** A regional ice-cream maker has a loyal customer base, little cash and a stable market. Product development (new flavours for existing stockists) uses its brand at modest cost; diversification would need finance it does not have.

## Method in steps: using SWOT to choose a strategy

1. **Calculate** the data given (margins, capacity utilisation, market share and growth, gearing, productivity, labour turnover).
2. **Compare** each figure with a benchmark (industry average, last year, target).
3. **Classify** each as a strength, weakness, opportunity or threat. Internal figures are S or W; market and environment trends are O or T.
4. **Match**: favour strategies that use strengths to take opportunities; reject ones that expose weaknesses to threats.
5. **Judge**: name the strategy the evidence supports and the main limitation (snapshot data, unweighted factors, missing information).

**Worked reminder.** A firm runs at 64% capacity utilisation, has gearing of 22% and faces a shrinking home market. Spare capacity plus borrowing headroom against a falling home market points away from penetration and towards market development, if a growing market elsewhere can be found.

## Method in steps: functional implications

For any named strategy, take each function in turn and write **action → consequence**:

- Marketing: research, promotion, pricing, distribution → cost and time before sales arrive.
- Operations: capacity, new processes, suppliers, quality → disruption, investment, unit cost changes.
- HR: recruitment, training, new skills, workforce planning → cost, resistance, time to become productive.
- Finance: funding, budgets, cash flow, currency → borrowing raises gearing; returns arrive later than costs.

## Strategy and functions: the chain

```
Mission → corporate objectives → strategy → functional objectives → functional decisions
```

Two-way link: functions deliver the strategy, but their limits (cash, skills, capacity) decide which strategies are realistic.

## Reasons for, and challenges of, changing strategy

| Reasons | Challenges |
|---|---|
| New competitors or technology | Cost and time before results |
| Falling demand or a saturated market | Staff resistance and fear of change |
| New leaders or new objectives | Contracts, assets and stock tied to the old plan |
| Poor results under the current strategy | Culture and skills may not fit |
| Changes in the economy or law | Loss of confidence among investors, lenders, customers |
| New finance or resources available | The new strategy is itself uncertain |

## Must-know distinctions

- **Strategy vs tactic**: long-term direction vs short-term action.
- **Strategic vs tactical decision**: hard to reverse, high risk, large resources, one-off vs the reverse.
- **Market development vs product development**: the thing that is new is the market in one, the product in the other.
- **Product development vs diversification**: diversification also involves a new market.
- **New segment vs new customers**: selling to a new segment is market development; winning more customers in the same segment is penetration.
- **Strengths/weaknesses vs opportunities/threats**: internal and controllable vs external and outside the firm's control.

## Quick self-test

1. State the four features of a strategic decision.
2. A fruit-juice maker starts selling its current range in a neighbouring country. Which Ansoff strategy is this?
3. A mobile network offers home broadband to its existing mobile customers. Which strategy?
4. A coach company opens a hotel. Which strategy?
5. Hawksmere Bakery produces 4,600 trays a week; its capacity is 5,750 trays. Calculate capacity utilisation.
6. A penetration campaign would add 690 trays a week at Hawksmere. Calculate the new capacity utilisation.
7. Halvane Foods has sales of £2.73 million in a market worth £42 million. Calculate its market share.
8. A firm has non-current liabilities of £2.7 million and total equity of £8.1 million. Calculate gearing.
9. A market grows from £92 million to £96.6 million. Calculate the growth rate.
10. Distinguish between a strategy and a tactic.
11. Give two reasons why a business might change its strategy.
12. Explain why diversification is usually the highest-risk Ansoff strategy.

### Answers

1. Difficult to reverse; high risk; involves significant resources; usually one-off.
2. Market development.
3. Product development.
4. Diversification.
5. 4,600 ÷ 5,750 × 100 = **80%**.
6. (4,600 + 690) ÷ 5,750 × 100 = **92%**.
7. 2.73 ÷ 42 × 100 = **6.5%**.
8. 2.7 ÷ (2.7 + 8.1) × 100 = 2.7 ÷ 10.8 × 100 = **25%**.
9. (96.6 − 92) ÷ 92 × 100 = **5%**.
10. A strategy is a long-term plan to reach objectives; a tactic is a short-term action that helps carry it out.
11. Any two: new competition; falling demand or saturated market; new technology; new leadership or objectives; poor results; change in economy or law.
12. The business has no experience of either the product or the market, so it lacks knowledge, skills and customers, and usually needs the largest investment.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calling a price promotion or advert a "strategy" when it is a tactic.
- Placing a new customer segment in the same country under penetration instead of market development.
- Describing all four Ansoff boxes when the question asks you to assess one.
- Stating that market penetration is "risk-free": rivals can still retaliate and the market may be saturated.
- Using SWOT labels with no figure, no comparison and no link to a strategy.
- Classifying a falling market as a weakness when it is an external threat.
- Writing functional implications that would apply to any strategy rather than the one named.
- Omitting non-current liabilities from capital employed when calculating gearing.
- Listing reasons for changing strategy without saying why they apply to the case business.
- Ending an evaluation without saying which strategy is best and what it depends on.

## Frequently asked questions

**What are the four features of a strategic decision?**
A strategic decision is difficult to reverse, high risk, involves significant resources and is usually one-off. A tactical decision is the opposite on each count.

**What are the four options in the Ansoff matrix?**
Market penetration sells existing products in existing markets, market development sells existing products in new markets, product development sells new products in existing markets, and diversification sells new products in new markets.

**Is a price promotion a strategy or a tactic?**
A price promotion or an advert is a tactic, a short-term action within a strategy. A strategy is the long-term direction, so calling a promotion a strategy is a common error.

**How do you use SWOT to choose a strategy?**
Calculate the data given, such as margins, capacity utilisation, market share and gearing, compare each with a benchmark and classify it as a strength, weakness, opportunity or threat. Then favour strategies that use strengths to take opportunities, and name the main limitation of the data in your judgement.
