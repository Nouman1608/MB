## Key definitions

| Term | Meaning to learn |
|---|---|
| Sovereignty | A state's independence, control over its territory and ability to govern itself (the guide's starting point; the concept is contestable) |
| Westphalian sovereignty | Territorial, supreme authority with no outside actor in domestic affairs; states legally equal |
| Popular sovereignty | Final authority rests with the people; the state rules by their consent |
| Pooled sovereignty | States choose to exercise some powers jointly through shared institutions |
| Sovereignty as responsibility | The right to non-intervention depends on protecting your own people (basis of R2P) |
| Supranationality | A body above states that makes rules binding on members, even against their will |
| Internal sovereignty | Supreme authority inside the borders |
| External sovereignty | Independence from outside control; recognition as an equal |
| De jure sovereignty | The legal right to rule |
| De facto sovereignty | Actual control on the ground |

## Traditional versus modern notions

| | Traditional | Modern |
|---|---|---|
| Nature | Absolute, indivisible | Divisible, can be shared or conditional |
| Holder | The ruler or the state | The people; sometimes shared with institutions |
| Borders | Fixed and closed to interference | Porous to flows; some intervention accepted |
| Key idea | Non-intervention (UN Charter Art. 2(7)) | Responsibility (R2P, 2005 World Summit) |
| Classic thinkers | Bodin (1576), Hobbes (1651) | Krasner (1999): four meanings of sovereignty |

**Krasner's four meanings** -- learn them as a checklist for any case: domestic (control at home), interdependence (control of cross-border flows), international legal (recognition), Westphalian (no outside authority inside).

## Sources of sovereignty

| Source (guide's list) | How it works | Weak point | Example to use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Possession and use of force | Monopoly of legitimate force (Weber) lets the state enforce law | Armed groups break the monopoly | Haiti's gangs; UN-authorised Kenya-led mission (2023) |
| International law and norms | Territorial integrity and non-intervention protect states; Montevideo criteria define statehood | Enforcement depends on powerful states | UNGA resolution 68/262 (2014) on Ukraine's territorial integrity |
| Recognition by other states | Gives diplomatic status, treaties, UN seat | Driven by economics and the balance of power | Nicaragua (2021) and Honduras (2023) switch from Taipei to Beijing |
| Consent of the governed | Participation gives authority a popular base | Low turnout, protest, secession votes | Scotland's agreed 2014 referendum |

**Montevideo Convention (1933)** criteria for statehood: permanent population, defined territory, government, capacity to enter relations with other states.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Sovereignty vs legitimacy.** Sovereignty is about authority and control; legitimacy is about acceptance. A state can be sovereign but its government seen as illegitimate.
- **Internal vs external.** Rebels threaten internal sovereignty; an invasion or binding outside ruling threatens external sovereignty. Some cases hit both.
- **De jure vs de facto.** Kosovo: declared independence 2008, many recognitions, no UN seat. Taiwan: strong de facto control, few formal recognitions.
- **Declaratory vs constitutive recognition.** Declaratory: a state exists once it meets the criteria. Constitutive: it exists as a state only when others recognise it.
- **Supranational vs intergovernmental.** Supranational bodies bind members (EU law has primacy, *Costa v ENEL*, 1964). Intergovernmental bodies need each state's agreement.
- **Losing sovereignty vs pooling it.** Pooling is a choice and can be reversed: the UK left the EU in January 2020 after its 2016 referendum.
- **Humanitarian intervention vs interference.** Intervention claims a protective aim and, ideally, UN authorisation (Libya, resolution 1973, 2011). Interference serves the intervening state's own goals.

## Challenges: quick map

| Challenge | Notion most threatened | Counter-argument | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Global interdependence | Interdependence sovereignty | States pool power to regain control | 15% global minimum tax (2021) |
| Interference by other states | Westphalian, territorial | Collective condemnation reasserts the norm | Crimea (2014); invasion of Ukraine (2022) |
| Supranationality | Traditional, absolute | Pooling is chosen and reversible | EU; UK exit (2020) |
| Humanitarian intervention | Non-intervention | Sovereignty as responsibility | Libya (2011); vetoes on Syria |
| Indigenous claims | State as sole source of authority | Self-government inside the state | UNDRIP (2007); Treaty of Waitangi |
| Transnational cultural groups | Fixed borders | Recognition withheld | Kurdish referendum in Iraq (2017) |
| Nationalist movements | Territorial integrity | Consent tested by agreed votes | Scotland (2014) vs Catalonia (2017) |
| Violent non-state actors | Monopoly of force | States reclaim territory | Islamic State (2014--2019) |
| TNCs | Regulatory control | States can win disputes and cooperate | Philip Morris v Uruguay (2016) |

## Sovereignty across the thematic studies

- **Rights and justice:** does non-intervention shield a state that abuses rights? Use R2P.
- **Development and sustainability:** loan conditions (Greece's bailouts from 2010) and control of natural resources.
- **Peace and conflict:** secession, territorial disputes, armed groups and host-state consent to peacekeepers.

## Method in steps: a Paper 1 evaluation question on sovereignty

Paper 1 lasts 1 hour 15 minutes and has four compulsory questions worth 3, 4, 6 and 12 marks. The 12-mark question asks you to evaluate the sources and combine them with your own knowledge.

```
1. Define the notion of sovereignty you are testing (e.g. internal,
   de facto; or Westphalian).
2. Take the claim apart: which sources support it, which challenge it?
3. Quote or cite each source briefly and say what it shows.
4. Add own knowledge: one or two named, contemporary examples,
   explained, not just named.
5. Weigh: which side is stronger, and under which notion?
6. Conclude with a judgement that answers the exact question.
```

Worked reminder: the claim "Sovereignty is being eroded by TNCs" is strong under interdependence sovereignty (firms move capital freely) but weaker under international legal sovereignty (only states sign treaties and win cases, as Uruguay did).

## Quick self-test

1. Give the four features of the Westphalian notion of sovereignty.
2. Distinguish internal from external sovereignty.
3. Name the four Montevideo criteria.
4. Which UN Charter article protects domestic jurisdiction, and what is the exception?
5. When was R2P endorsed by UN members, and at which meeting?
6. What does "pooled sovereignty" mean? Give an example.
7. Name the four sources of sovereignty in the guide's list (after the Westphalian conception).
8. State the difference between declaratory and constitutive theories of recognition.
9. Give one example of recognition shaped by economics.
10. Which of Krasner's four meanings is most affected by an armed group seizing territory?
11. Why is Scotland 2014 a useful contrast with Catalonia 2017?
12. Give one reason a supranational body may not reduce sovereignty.

### Answers

1. Territoriality; supreme authority inside the borders; non-intervention; legal equality of states.
2. Internal: supreme authority over people and territory within borders. External: independence from outside control and recognition by other states.
3. Permanent population; defined territory; government; capacity to enter relations with other states.
4. Article 2(7); enforcement measures under Chapter VII.
5. 2005, at the UN World Summit.
6. States choose to exercise some powers jointly, e.g. EU members negotiating trade as one bloc.
7. Possession and use of force; international law and norms; recognition by other states; consent of the governed through participation.
8. Declaratory: statehood exists once the criteria are met. Constitutive: statehood depends on recognition by others.
9. Nicaragua (2021) or Honduras (2023) switching recognition from Taipei to Beijing.
10. Domestic sovereignty (effective control at home).
11. Scotland's referendum was agreed with the central government; Catalonia's was ruled illegal and followed by direct rule. Same challenge, opposite responses.
12. Membership is chosen by treaty and can be ended (UK, 2020); pooling can give a state more influence than it has alone.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using "sovereignty" without saying which notion or dimension you mean, so the judgement has no anchor.
- Treating sovereignty and legitimacy as one idea.
- Claiming the EU "abolished" member sovereignty; members keep international legal sovereignty and can leave.
- Naming Libya 2011 without explaining the Security Council authorisation or the regime-change criticism.
- Describing Paper 1 sources in turn instead of weighing them against each other on the 12-mark question.
- Using only Westphalia and other historical cases instead of contemporary examples.
- Forgetting non-state challenges (Indigenous claims, TNCs) and writing only about states.
- Giving one side only on "to what extent" or "evaluate" questions.
- Confusing de jure recognition with de facto control when describing cases such as Taiwan or Kosovo.
