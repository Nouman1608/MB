Condensed recall for **8.1 Coastal processes**, the first section of Topic 8, Coastal environments, in Paper 3 Advanced Physical Geography Options. The syllabus covers wave generation and characteristics (fetch, energy, refraction, breaking waves, high and low energy waves, swash and backwash), marine erosion, sub-aerial processes, and marine transportation and deposition (sediment sources and characteristics, sediment cells and longshore drift). For full explanations and worked calculations, read the [8.1 study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-coastal-processes/), then try the [8.1 practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-coastal-processes-practice/).

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Fetch | Distance of open water over which the wind blows |
| Swell | Long, regular waves that have left their area of generation |
| Wave height | Trough to crest |
| Wavelength | Crest to crest |
| Wave period | Time between successive crests passing a point |
| Wave steepness | Height ÷ wavelength |
| Wave base | Depth, about half a wavelength, below which orbital motion is negligible |
| Shoaling | Slowing, shortening and heightening of waves in shallow water |
| Refraction | Bending of wave crests as they slow in shallow water |
| Orthogonal | Line at right angles to wave crests, showing the direction of energy |
| Swash / backwash | Uprush of water up the beach / return flow down it |
| High-energy (destructive) wave | High, steep wave whose backwash exceeds its swash |
| Low-energy (constructive) wave | Low, long wave whose swash exceeds its backwash |
| Sub-aerial processes | Weathering and mass movement acting on the land above the waves |
| Sediment cell | Stretch of coast within which sediment moves between sources, transfers and sinks, with little exchange beyond |
| Sediment budget | Balance of sediment inputs and outputs in a cell |
| Longshore drift | Movement of sediment along the coast by oblique swash and straight backwash |
| Sorting | How similar in size the particles in a sediment are |

## Formulae and numbers

- Wave energy is proportional to height squared: double the height, four times the energy; triple it, nine times.
- Deep-water wavelength L ≈ 1.56 × T² (metres; T in seconds).
- Deep-water speed C ≈ 1.56 × T (metres per second).
- Steepness = H ÷ L.
- Waves break when the depth is roughly 1.3 times the wave height.
- Sediment budget = total inputs − total outputs.

## Wave sequence from generation to beach

1. Wind transfers energy to the sea surface; size depends on wind speed, duration and fetch.
2. Waves travel as swell, with water moving in orbits, not forwards.
3. In water shallower than half the wavelength, friction slows the wave; it shortens and grows higher.
4. The wave steepens and breaks: spilling on gentle beaches, plunging on moderate, surging on steep.
5. Swash runs up the beach and backwash returns; their balance decides whether sediment is gained or lost.

## High and low energy waves

| | High-energy | Low-energy |
|---|---|---|
| Shape | High and steep | Low and flat |
| Dominant flow | Backwash | Swash |
| Beach effect | Lowered and flattened; sediment moved offshore to bars | Built up into berms; steepened |
| Usual cause | Storms, strong local winds | Distant swell, calm weather |

Feedback: steep beaches encourage plunging waves and strong backwash, which flatten them again.

## Refraction

- Crests slow first off headlands, so they bend.
- Orthogonals converge on headlands: energy concentrated, erosion.
- Orthogonals diverge in bays: energy spread, deposition.
- Long-term result: indented coasts tend to become straighter.

## Marine erosion

| Process | How it works | Most effective on |
|---|---|---|
| Hydraulic action | Water force and compressed air in joints | Well-jointed rock |
| Cavitation | Collapsing bubbles send shock waves into rock | Rock surfaces hit by turbulent breaking waves |
| Corrasion/abrasion | Sediment thrown at or dragged over rock | Any rock where waves carry sand and shingle |
| Solution | Chemical dissolving | Limestone and chalk |
| Attrition | Particles wear each other down | Acts on sediment, not cliffs |

**Controls on erosion rate:** wave energy, rock type, rock structure, beach width and human activity.

## Sub-aerial processes

- **Weathering:** salt crystallisation, wetting and drying of clays, biological boring and roots, freeze-thaw in cold climates, solution of limestone.
- **Mass movement:** rockfall, sliding on seaward-dipping beds, rotational slumping in saturated clay or till, mudflows.
- **Balance:** marine-dominated cliffs are steep and fast-retreating; sub-aerially dominated cliffs are lower-angled and slumped.

## Transport, deposition and sediment

- Transport by traction, saltation, suspension and solution, both onshore-offshore and alongshore.
- Sources: rivers (largest globally), cliffs, sea bed, shells and coral, wind-blown sand, beach nourishment.
- Describe sediment by size, shape (roundness) and sorting.
- Rounding increases with distance travelled because of attrition.
- Deposition where energy falls: bays, changes of coastline direction, behind obstacles.
- England and Wales: 11 major sediment cells for shoreline planning, each split into sub-cells.

## Exam traps

- Energy rises with the **square** of height.
- The wave form moves forward; the water mostly moves in orbits.
- Attrition does not erode cliffs.
- Cavitation is about collapsing bubbles; do not describe it as "air in cracks".
- Give the direction of longshore drift from the wave approach stated in the question.
- Show working and units in every calculation.
- Link sub-aerial processes to marine removal at the cliff foot.

## Self-test

1. Name the three factors that control wave size.
2. A wave's height rises from 1.5 m to 4.5 m. By how many times does its energy increase?
3. Calculate the deep-water wavelength of a wave with a period of 8 s.
4. What is the steepness of a wave 2.5 m high with a wavelength of 50 m?
5. What happens to wavelength and height as a wave enters shallow water?
6. Which breaker type is typical of steep beaches?
7. Explain why headlands receive more wave energy than bays.
8. Distinguish hydraulic action from cavitation.
9. Name two sub-aerial weathering processes on cliffs.
10. Why do slumped cliffs form in glacial till?
11. A cell has inputs of 220 and outputs of 260 thousand m³ per year. What is its budget, and what might happen to its beaches?
12. Why do pebbles become rounder along the direction of drift?

### Answers

1. Wind speed, wind duration and fetch.
2. (4.5 ÷ 1.5)² = 3² = 9 times.
3. 1.56 × 8² = 1.56 × 64 = 99.8 m.
4. 2.5 ÷ 50 = 0.05.
5. Wavelength decreases and wave height increases.
6. Surging.
7. Refraction makes orthogonals converge on headlands and diverge in bays.
8. Hydraulic action is the force of water and compressed air in joints; cavitation is the collapse of bubbles releasing shock waves.
9. Any two of salt crystallisation, wetting and drying, biological weathering, freeze-thaw, solution.
10. Till is weak and becomes saturated after heavy rain, so it fails along curved slip surfaces.
11. 220 − 260 = −40 thousand m³ per year; a deficit, so beaches may narrow and erosion increase.
12. Attrition during transport knocks off corners and edges.
