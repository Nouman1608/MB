This guide goes deeper into **3.3.1.1 Nomenclature**, the first subtopic of section 3.3.1 Introduction to organic chemistry in the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Chemistry (9620) specification, Version 5.3. It is International AS content, assessed in Unit 2 (Organic 1 and Physical 1) and available again in the synoptic Unit 5. ## What the specification asks for

- Organic compounds can be represented by empirical, molecular, general, structural, displayed and skeletal formulas.
- The characteristics of a homologous series, a series of compounds containing the same functional group.
- IUPAC rules for nomenclature.
- Draw structural, displayed and skeletal formulas for given organic compounds.
- Apply IUPAC rules to name organic compounds, and to draw a compound from its name, limited to chains and rings with up to six carbon atoms each.

## Six formulas for one compound

Take 3-methylbutanal, an aldehyde with five carbon atoms.

| Formula | What it shows | 3-methylbutanal |
|---|---|---|
| Molecular | Actual number of each type of atom | C₅H₁₀O |
| Empirical | Simplest whole-number ratio of atoms | C₅H₁₀O (no common factor, so the same as the molecular formula) |
| General | Formula for any member of the series | CₙH₂ₙO (aldehydes and ketones) |
| Structural | Arrangement of atoms, carbon by carbon, without drawing every bond | (CH₃)₂CHCH₂CHO |
| Displayed | Every atom and every bond | below |
| Skeletal | Carbon skeleton as lines; C and the H atoms on C omitted | below |

```
          H
          |
      H - C - H
          |
      H   |   H   O
      |   |   |   ‖
  H - C - C - C - C
      |   |   |   |
      H   H   H   H
```

In the displayed formula, the right-hand carbon is C1: it carries the double bond to O and one H. The methyl branch sits on C3. Count the hydrogens: 3 + 1 + 2 + 1 + 3 = 10, matching C₅H₁₀O. The skeletal formula is a zig-zag of three lines for the four-carbon chain, a short branch line at C3 for the methyl group, and a double line from the end carbon to an O. The aldehyde H is not written, because it is bonded to carbon.

**Writing structural formulas clearly.** Write an aldehyde group as –CHO (never –COH, which looks like an alcohol), a carboxylic acid as –COOH, and a ketone carbonyl as –CO– within the chain. Put branches in brackets after the carbon they are attached to: CH₃CH(CH₃)CH₂CH₃.

## Homologous series

A homologous series is a series of compounds containing the same functional group. Its members:

- fit the same **general formula**
- differ from the next member by **CH₂** (a difference in Mr of 14.0)
- have **similar chemical properties**, because the functional group decides how they react
- show a **gradual trend in physical properties**: boiling point rises as the chain lengthens, because van der Waals forces between molecules increase.

| Series | Functional group | General formula | First member |
|---|---|---|---|
| Alkanes | none (C–C, C–H only) | CₙH₂ₙ₊₂ | methane |
| Cycloalkanes | ring | CₙH₂ₙ | cyclopropane |
| Alkenes | C=C | CₙH₂ₙ | ethene |
| Halogenoalkanes | C–X | CₙH₂ₙ₊₁X | chloromethane (for X = Cl) |
| Alcohols | –OH | CₙH₂ₙ₊₁OH | methanol |
| Aldehydes | –CHO | CₙH₂ₙO | methanal |
| Ketones | C=O in the chain | CₙH₂ₙO | propanone |
| Carboxylic acids | –COOH | CₙH₂ₙ₊₁COOH | methanoic acid (n = 0) |

Two series can share a general formula (alkenes and cycloalkanes; aldehydes and ketones). The general formula alone does not identify the series.

## The IUPAC method

1. **Find the functional group** and so the suffix or prefix. Halogens are always prefixes (chloro-, bromo-). –OH is the suffix -ol. C=C changes -an- to -en- in the stem.
2. **Find the longest carbon chain that contains the functional group** (and the C=C, if there is one). This gives the stem: meth-, eth-, prop-, but-, pent-, hex-. For a ring, add cyclo-.
3. **Number the chain** from the end that gives the suffix group the lowest number. If the suffix group does not decide it, give the C=C the lowest number, then the full set of substituent locants.
4. **Name the substituents**, with di- or tri- for repeats, in **alphabetical order** (ignoring di- and tri-).
5. **Punctuate**: commas between numbers, hyphens between numbers and letters.

Points that cost marks:

- In aldehydes, carboxylic acids and nitriles, the functional carbon is **C1** and needs no locant: butanal, not butan-1-al.
- A nitrile's –C≡N carbon counts in the chain: CH₃CH₂CH₂CN is **butanenitrile**.
- Ketones need a locant from five carbons upwards: propanone and butanone have only one possible position, but pentan-2-one and pentan-3-one are different.
- The final "e" of the alkane name is dropped before a suffix that starts with a vowel (pentan-2-ol, propanal, butanone) but kept before one that starts with a consonant (butanenitrile, ethane-1,2-diol).
- A molecule with two C=C bonds is a **diene**, and an "a" is added to the stem: CH₂=CHCH=CH₂ is **buta-1,3-diene**.
- In a cycloalkene the C=C carbons are C1 and C2, so cyclohexene needs no locant.

### When there are two functional groups

Only one group can be the suffix. In these compounds the order of preference is carboxylic acid > aldehyde > ketone > alcohol; the other group becomes a prefix (–OH becomes hydroxy-). For example, CH₃CH(OH)COOH is **2-hydroxypropanoic acid**. An alkene and an alcohol together keep both: the C=C stays in the stem and –OH stays as the suffix, as in pent-3-en-2-ol, CH₃CH=CHCH(OH)CH₃.

## Worked example 1: naming

Name each compound.

**(a) CH₃CH(Cl)CH₂CH(OH)CH₃**

- Five-carbon chain with –OH: pentan-…-ol.
- Number from the right so –OH is on C2. Cl is then on C4.
- Name: **4-chloropentan-2-ol**.

**(b) CH₃CH₂C(CH₃)₂CHO**

- Aldehyde: the CHO carbon is C1. Chain C1 to C4: butanal.
- Two methyl groups on C2.
- Name: **2,2-dimethylbutanal**.

**(c) CH₃COCH₂CH(CH₃)₂**

- Ketone; longest chain is five carbons: pentanone.
- Numbering from the left puts C=O on C2 (from the right it would be C4).
- Methyl on C4. Name: **4-methylpentan-2-one**.

**(d) CH₂=CHCH(CH₃)CH₂OH**

- Four-carbon chain containing both –OH and C=C: but-…-en-…-ol.
- The suffix –OH gets the lowest number: C1 is the CH₂OH carbon. C=C is then between C3 and C4; methyl on C2.
- Name: **2-methylbut-3-en-1-ol**.

## Worked example 2: drawing from names

**(a) 3-bromo-2-methylpentane**: a five-carbon chain, CH₃ on C2, Br on C3.

```
CH₃CH(CH₃)CHBrCH₂CH₃
```

**(b) pent-3-en-2-one**: five carbons, C=O on C2, C=C between C3 and C4.

```
CH₃COCH=CHCH₃
```

**(c) 2-methylpropanenitrile**: three-carbon chain including the CN carbon, CH₃ on C2.

```
(CH₃)₂CHCN        molecular formula C₄H₇N
```

Always count back the carbons in your structure and check that the name you would give it matches the name in the question.

## Worked example 3: correcting wrong names

| Name given | Problem | Correct name |
|---|---|---|
| 2-ethylpropane | Longest chain missed; it is four carbons | 2-methylbutane |
| pent-4-ene | Numbered from the wrong end | pent-1-ene |
| 3-chloro-2-bromobutane | Prefixes not alphabetical | 2-bromo-3-chlorobutane |
| 2,2-dimethylbutan-4-ol | –OH should take the lowest number | 3,3-dimethylbutan-1-ol |

Redraw the structure from the wrong name first; then name it from scratch.

## Common mistakes

- Choosing the chain as written left to right, rather than the longest chain.
- Numbering to give the substituent the lowest number when the suffix group should decide.
- Writing –COH for an aldehyde, or putting a locant on an aldehyde or acid.
- Forgetting to count the nitrile carbon.
- Missing hydrogens on heteroatoms in skeletal formulas (write OH, not just O, on an alcohol).
- Ordering prefixes as "dimethyl" under d rather than m.
- Confusing empirical and molecular formulas when they happen to be the same.

## Exam technique for OxfordAQA command words

- **Name** or **give the IUPAC name**: the full name with correct punctuation; a nearly correct name usually scores zero.
- **Draw** (displayed formula): every atom and every bond, including O–H. (Skeletal formula): heteroatoms and their H atoms shown; C–H omitted.
- **Give** (structural formula): unambiguous, with branches in brackets.
- **Deduce** (formula from data): show the working, for example from Mr and a general formula.
