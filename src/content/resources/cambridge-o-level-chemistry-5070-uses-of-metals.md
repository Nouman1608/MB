This guide covers subtopic **9.2 Uses of metals** of Cambridge O Level Chemistry 5070 (2026–2028 series). The table in [Alloys and Extraction of Metals](/resources/alloys-and-extraction-of-metals/) lists the four named uses; this guide explains *why* each property suits each use, how to compare aluminium with copper and iron, and how to handle "suggest" questions that apply the same reasoning to unfamiliar uses.

## What the syllabus asks

One outcome: describe the uses of metals **in terms of their physical properties**, including:

- (a) aluminium in the manufacture of **aircraft** because of its **low density**
- (b) aluminium in the manufacture of **overhead electrical cables** because of its **low density and good electrical conductivity**
- (c) aluminium in **food containers** because of its **resistance to corrosion**
- (d) copper in **electrical wiring** because of its **good electrical conductivity and ductility**

The word "including" means these four must be known exactly; examiners can also give you data about other metals and ask you to suggest a use. The key phrase is "in terms of their physical properties": every use must be justified by a property, not by cost or tradition.

## The answer pattern

Every mark in this subtopic follows one pattern:

> **[Metal]** is used for **[use]** because it has **[property]**, which means **[benefit in that use]**.

For example: "Aluminium is used to make aircraft because it has a low density, so the aircraft is lighter and needs less fuel to fly." The syllabus requires the first three parts; the "which means" clause is what turns a recalled fact into an explanation, and it is what earns credit in "explain" and "suggest" questions.

## (a) Aluminium for aircraft: low density

Density is mass per unit volume. A low-density metal gives a light structure for a given size.

| Metal | Density / g cm⁻³ |
|---|---|
| Aluminium | 2.70 |
| Iron | 7.87 |
| Copper | 8.96 |

An aircraft body made of aluminium is roughly a third of the mass of the same shape in iron (2.70 ÷ 7.87 ≈ 0.34). A lighter aircraft needs less fuel and can carry more passengers or cargo. In practice aircraft use aluminium **alloys**, because pure aluminium is relatively soft; alloying makes it stronger while keeping the low density (see [Alloys and their properties](/resources/cambridge-o-level-chemistry-5070-alloys-and-their-properties/)).

Write "low density", not "light". "Light" describes the object; density is the property of the material, and it is the syllabus term.

## (b) Aluminium for overhead cables: low density and good conductivity

Overhead cables hang between pylons over long distances. Two properties are needed together:

- **Good electrical conductivity**, so that current flows with little energy wasted. Aluminium conducts well because, like all metals, it has delocalised electrons that move through the structure.
- **Low density**, so that long lengths of cable are light enough to be supported between pylons without sagging too much or needing pylons very close together.

Copper is a better conductor than aluminium, so why not use copper overhead? Because of density.

### Worked example: comparing cable masses

*A length of overhead cable contains 50 000 cm³ of metal. Calculate the mass of metal if the cable is made of (a) aluminium, (b) copper. Use the densities in the table above. Explain which is more suitable.*

- (a) mass = density × volume = 2.70 × 50 000 = 135 000 g = **135 kg**
- (b) mass = 8.96 × 50 000 = 448 000 g = **448 kg**
- Copper cable would be over three times heavier (448 ÷ 135 ≈ 3.3). Aluminium is more suitable: it still conducts electricity well, and its much lower density means the pylons support far less mass.

A two-property use needs **both** properties in the answer. "Aluminium is used for cables because it conducts electricity" scores only half, because copper also conducts, and better.

## (c) Aluminium for food containers: resistance to corrosion

Aluminium is high in the reactivity series, above zinc and iron, so its resistance to corrosion surprises many candidates. The explanation is its **oxide layer**: aluminium's surface is covered by a thin, tightly bonded layer of aluminium oxide that forms naturally in air. This layer stops air and water reaching the metal underneath, so the container does not corrode and the food is not contaminated with corrosion products. Subtopic 9.4 asks you to explain aluminium's apparent unreactivity in exactly these terms.

Food containers made from aluminium include cans, trays and foil. Its malleability lets it be rolled into thin foil and pressed into trays, and its low density keeps packaging light, but the property the syllabus pairs with this use is **resistance to corrosion**.

## (d) Copper for electrical wiring: good conductivity and ductility

- **Good electrical conductivity:** copper is one of the best electrical conductors among common metals, so little energy is wasted as heat in the wire.
- **Ductility:** copper can be drawn out into long, thin wires without breaking, because its layers of ions slide over one another while the delocalised electrons hold the structure together.

Inside buildings, a wire's mass is unimportant, so copper's high density is not a problem, and its better conductivity makes it the preferred choice. Contrast this with use (b), where mass matters a great deal.

## "Suggest" questions: unfamiliar uses

Examiners test the same skill with metals or uses not named in the syllabus. Choose the property that matters most for the use, then match it to the data.

### Worked example

*A saucepan base must transfer heat quickly from the cooker to the food. Using the properties of metals, suggest why copper is suitable.*

- Copper is a good **thermal conductor**, so heat passes quickly through the base to the food.
- It has a high melting point, so it does not melt on the cooker.

Both properties come from subtopic 9.1; you are applying them to a new use. If the question gives a table of data, choose the column that matches the requirement (here, thermal conductivity), quote the value or description for the metal you choose, and say why the other metals in the table are less suitable. A "suggest" answer is marked on sensible reasoning, so any relevant physical property linked to the use is creditworthy.

## Common mistakes

- **Giving a use without a property**, or a property without a use.
- **"Aluminium is light."** Write **low density**.
- **"Aluminium does not corrode because it is unreactive."** It is reactive; its protective **oxide layer** prevents corrosion.
- **Giving only one property** for overhead cables or electrical wiring; both uses need two.
- **"Copper is used in wiring because it is a good conductor of heat."** The property is **electrical** conductivity.
- **"Because it is cheap."** Cost is not a physical property and does not answer this outcome.

## Exam technique

- **Describe** a use: metal, use, property in one sentence.
- **Explain** a use: add what the property achieves in that use.
- **Suggest** a metal for an unfamiliar use: identify the key requirement, then quote the property from the data provided.
- When comparing two metals for one use, mention the property in which they differ (density for aluminium versus copper).
