> These are original practice questions written in the style of Cambridge O Level
> Geography (2217) assessment objectives. They are not taken from any past paper
> and are not endorsed by Cambridge International. The River Selva, the town of
> Corvel and the Kaluma district are fictional, and their data is illustrative.
> Where a question asks for a named example, use your own learned case study.

Use these questions alongside the [Environmental Risks study guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-geography-environmental-risks-economic-development/)
and the [Environmental Risks revision notes](/resources/cambridge-o-level-geography-environmental-risks-economic-development-revision-notes/).

## Section A

**1.** Define the term *desertification*. **[1]**

**2.** State two types of pollution, other than water pollution, that
may be caused by a large quarry. **[2]**

**3.** Name two greenhouse gases released by economic activities. **[2]**

**4.** Explain the difference between a local and a global environmental
threat. Give an example of each. **[4]**

**5.** Define *sustainable development*. **[2]**

## Section B

**6.** Table 1 shows the amount of dissolved nitrate measured at sites
along the fictional River Selva. A large fertiliser factory discharges
waste water at 4 km, and the river then flows through farmland.

| Distance downstream (km) | 0 | 2 | 4 | 6 | 8 | 10 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Nitrate (mg per litre) | 2 | 3 | 18 | 14 | 12 | 11 |

**(a)** Describe the changes in nitrate levels along the river. Use
figures. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage fall in nitrate between 4 km and 10 km,
to the nearest whole number. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain how high nitrate levels can lead to the death of fish. **[3]**
**(d)** Suggest two ways of reducing nitrate pollution in the river. **[2]**

**7.** In the fictional semi-arid Kaluma district, the number of cattle
grazing on common land rose from 12,000 to 30,000 over 20 years, while
rainfall became less reliable.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage increase in cattle numbers. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain how these changes could lead to desertification. **[4]**
**(c)** Suggest two management strategies that could reduce the risk. **[2]**

**8.** The town of Corvel is planning a new coal-fired power station.
Explain the environmental risks this could create at local and global
scales. **[5]**

**9.** Evaluate the importance of resource conservation in reducing the
environmental risks of economic development. **[6]**

**10.** For a named area where economic development is causing
environmental risk, describe the risks and evaluate how successfully
they are being managed. **[7]**

## Answers

**1.** The process by which land in dry areas becomes more desert-like,
mainly because of human misuse such as overgrazing, often made worse by
drought [1].

**2.** Any two of: noise pollution from blasting and machinery; air
pollution from dust; visual pollution from scarring of the landscape
[1 each].

**3.** Any two of: carbon dioxide, methane, nitrous oxide [1 each].

**4.** A local threat affects the area close to its source [1], for
example noise or dust from a quarry affecting nearby homes [1]. A global
threat affects the whole planet, whatever its source [1], for example
enhanced global warming from carbon dioxide emissions [1].

**5.** Development that meets the needs of people today [1] without
reducing the ability of future generations to meet their own needs [1].

**6. (a)** Nitrate is low upstream, 2 to 3 mg per litre between 0 and
2 km [1]. It rises sharply to a peak of 18 mg per litre at 4 km, where
the factory discharges waste water [1]. Further downstream it falls
gradually to 11 mg per litre at 10 km, but stays well above the upstream
level [1].
**(b)** (18 - 11) / 18 x 100 [1] = **39%** fall [1].
**(c)** Nitrate acts as a nutrient, causing a rapid growth of algae (an
algal bloom) [1]. When the algae die, bacteria decompose them and use up
the oxygen in the water [1]. Fish cannot get enough oxygen and die [1].
**(d)** Any two of: require the factory to treat waste water before
discharge; fine or regulate the factory; reduce fertiliser use on
farms; leave buffer strips of vegetation beside the river to absorb
run-off [1 each].

**7. (a)** (30,000 - 12,000) / 12,000 x 100 = **150%** [1].
**(b)** Up to 4 marks for a linked explanation, for example: more cattle
eat more vegetation than can regrow, so the land is overgrazed [1];
bare soil is exposed to wind and occasional heavy rain [1], so topsoil
is eroded [1]; less reliable rainfall means vegetation recovers more
slowly, so the land becomes increasingly barren and desert-like [1].
**(c)** Any two of: limit herd sizes or rotate grazing areas; plant
trees and shrubs as windbreaks; build small stone lines (bunds) to trap
water and soil; provide alternative incomes or fuels [1 each].

**8.** Up to 5 marks, with both scales needed for full marks:
- Local: air pollution from smoke and dust causing breathing problems
  [1]; noise and visual pollution from the plant, chimneys and coal
  deliveries [1]; water pollution if waste or hot cooling water enters a
  river [1].
- Regional: sulphur dioxide and nitrogen oxides causing acid rain that
  can fall far from the plant, damaging forests and lakes [1].
- Global: carbon dioxide from burning coal adds to the enhanced
  greenhouse effect and global warming [1].

**9.** Levels-style marking.
- Importance [up to 3]: recycling and reuse reduce the need to mine and
  log, cutting habitat loss and pollution; efficiency lowers fossil
  fuel use and emissions; managing forests, fish and soils so they are
  not overused keeps them productive; finite resources last longer for
  future generations.
- Limits [up to 3]: conservation alone cannot stop risks where demand
  keeps rising with population and development; it needs laws,
  enforcement and cleaner technology; poorer countries may lack the
  money for recycling systems or renewable energy.
- Judgement [required for full marks]: for example, "conservation is
  essential but works best alongside regulation and new technology".
  No judgement: maximum 4.

**10.** Answers depend on the case study. Credit:
- Named area and activities [up to 2].
- Risks to environment and people with specific detail [up to 2].
- Management described [up to 1].
- Evaluation of success with a supported judgement [up to 2].

Example using the Amazon in Brazil: cattle ranching, soya, logging and
mining (for example gold mining using mercury) clear forest; risks
include soil erosion and leaching, carbon release, mercury in rivers and
fish, biodiversity loss and loss of indigenous land. Management:
protected areas, the Forest Code, INPE satellite monitoring and the Soy
Moratorium. Evaluation: clearance fell sharply in the late 2000s when
enforcement was strong but rose again in some later years, so success
has depended on political will and enforcement.
