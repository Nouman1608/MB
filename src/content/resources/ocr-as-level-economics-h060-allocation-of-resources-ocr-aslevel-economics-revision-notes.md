## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Resource allocation | The way scarce resources are divided between competing uses |
| Price mechanism | The way changes in prices, driven by demand and supply, allocate resources |
| Signalling function | Price changes carry information about scarcity to buyers and sellers |
| Rationing function | A higher price limits quantity demanded to the quantity available |
| Incentive function | A higher price rewards producers for supplying more |
| Incentive | A reward or penalty that changes the pay-off of a choice |
| Shortage (excess demand) | Quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied at the current price |
| Surplus (excess supply) | Quantity supplied exceeds quantity demanded at the current price |
| Non-price rationing | Allocating goods by queues, ballots, permits or waiting lists instead of price |
| Market, planned and mixed economies | Allocation mainly by prices, by a central authority, or by both |

## The three functions in one chain

Demand rises, so price rises (signal), so some buyers buy less (rationing) and firms supply more (incentive), until quantity demanded equals quantity supplied at a new equilibrium. When demand falls, the chain runs in reverse and resources move to other uses.

## Price held below equilibrium

- Venue example: 4,000 seats, Qd = 9,000 - 100P. Market price £50.
- Cap at £30: Qd = 6,000, shortage = 2,000.
- Consequences: queues, ballots, favouritism, resale at higher prices, weaker incentive to supply more.
- Lesson: if price does not ration, something else does, with its own costs.

## Incentives: what makes them work

| Factor | Effect on effectiveness |
|---|---|
| Size of reward or penalty | Larger relative to the cost of changing, more effective |
| Information | People must know and understand the incentive |
| Alternatives | Easy substitutes make behaviour more responsive |
| Unintended effects | Rewarding one measure can distort others |
| Habit and motivation | Can weaken or override financial incentives |

Deposit example: a 20p deposit lifts bottle returns from 45% to 85%, a rise of 40 percentage points.

## Comparing economic systems

**Market:** quick response, uses dispersed information, strong incentives, choice; but misses public goods, ignores externalities, allocates by ability to pay, unstable.

**Planned:** can target priorities and basic needs and reduce inequality; but planners lack information, so shortages and surpluses occur; weak incentives; little choice; queues ration goods.

**Mixed:** markets for most goods, government for public goods, merit goods, redistribution and regulation; the balance is debated because of the risk of government failure.

## Exam traps

- Naming the functions without explaining them.
- Mixing up signalling (information) and incentive (reward).
- Claiming a price cap increases the quantity available.
- Ignoring non-price rationing in planned systems.
- Treating "fair" or "unfair" as positive statements.
- Calling 45% to 85% a rise of 40%; it is 40 percentage points (an 89% rise in the return rate).
- Evaluating systems without a judgement that depends on the type of good.

## Self-test

1. Define resource allocation.
2. Which function of price tells producers that consumers want more of a good?
3. Which function limits quantity demanded to the amount available?
4. Explain the incentive function in one sentence.
5. Demand is Qd = 500 - 10P and supply is fixed at 300. Find the market-clearing price.
6. In question 5 the price is capped at 15. Find the shortage.
7. Give two ways a shortage may be allocated without price.
8. State two factors that affect how well an incentive works.
9. Give one advantage of allocation by a planned economy.
10. Give one disadvantage of allocation by a planned economy.
11. Why do most economies choose to be mixed?
12. A bonus for hospitals that cut waiting times leads some to delay recording new patients. What does this show about incentives?

### Answers

1. The way scarce resources are divided between competing uses.
2. The signalling function.
3. The rationing function.
4. A higher price raises the reward for producing, so firms supply more.
5. 500 - 10P = 300, so P = 20.
6. Qd = 500 - 150 = 350; shortage = 350 - 300 = 50.
7. Any two: queues, ballots, permits, waiting lists, first come first served, favouritism.
8. Any two: size of the reward, information, availability of alternatives, unintended effects, habit.
9. Resources can be directed to priorities or basic needs, or inequality can be reduced.
10. Planners lack information about wants, so shortages and surpluses appear; or weak incentives for efficiency.
11. To combine the responsiveness and incentives of markets with government action where markets fail.
12. Incentives can have unintended effects when they reward one measure that people can manipulate.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is the price mechanism the same as the market economy?**
The price mechanism is the process by which prices allocate resources; a market economy is a system in which that process allocates most resources.

**Why do shortages persist under a price cap?**
Because the price cannot rise to reduce quantity demanded or raise quantity supplied, so excess demand remains until the cap is removed or supply is increased.
