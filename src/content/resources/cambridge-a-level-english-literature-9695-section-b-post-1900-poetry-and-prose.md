Section B of Paper 4 asks for one essay on a post-1900 set text, poetry or prose, whichever form you did
not choose in Section A. It is worth 25 of the paper's 50 marks and is assessed on **AO1 to AO5**, each
20%. This guide focuses on the methods typical of modern and contemporary writing, and on building AO5
from the critical approaches these texts invite. For the paper as a whole, see the
[Paper 4 study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-english-literature-paper-4-pre-and-post-1900-poetry-and-prose-study-guide/).

## What the section asks of you

On each set text you choose an **(a) question**, an essay on the whole text, or a **(b) question** on a
passage or poem printed on the paper. Texts are not allowed in the exam room. Remember the form rule:
across Paper 4, one essay on poetry and one on prose.

For 2027, Elizabeth Bishop's poems, Richard Powers's *The Overstory* and Ocean Vuong's *Night Sky with Exit
Wounds* replace Glück, *Beloved* and *Native Guard*. Check the list for your examination year.

## The key idea: modern texts make the reader work

Much post-1900 writing withholds what older texts supplied: a reliable narrator, chronological order, a
settled meaning, a single voice. The reader has to assemble the meaning. Strong answers describe **how a
text makes the reader do this work** and why that matters to its meaning.

## Post-1900 prose methods

### Narrative voice and tense

- **First-person present tense** places the reader inside a narrator's moment without the hindsight of
  retrospective narration. Coetzee's Magistrate narrates largely in the present, so his understanding
  grows, and fails, alongside ours.
- **Interior focalisation** and **free indirect discourse** are central to Mansfield's stories, which
  follow a character's shifting perceptions closely, often within a single day or event.
- **Multiple voices**: *Beloved* moves between characters' perspectives and includes passages in which
  voices merge, so no single account is complete.

### Fragmented and non-linear structure

*Beloved* reveals its central event gradually, circling back to it through memory, so the reader
experiences the past as something that returns rather than something that is simply told. Ask why a
writer delays or fragments information, and what the reader feels at the moment of revelation.

### Allegory and unnamed settings

*Waiting for the Barbarians* is set in an unnamed Empire and a frontier town at no specified date. The
lack of names allows the novel to work as **allegory**: its concerns with power, torture and the
construction of an "enemy" are not tied to one regime. Analyse the effect of this deliberate vagueness,
and weigh it in AO5: does allegory universalise, or does it risk abstracting real suffering?

### Short-story method

Mansfield's stories compress meaning into moments: a party, a journey, a conversation. Endings are often
open, or turn on a small realisation. For an (a) essay on the selection, choose stories that let you
compare methods, and follow the question's instruction about how many to discuss.

## Post-1900 poetry methods

### Sequences and voices

Several set collections are built as **sequences**, so poems speak to one another. *The Wild Iris* gives
voices to flowers, to a gardener-speaker, and to a god, and the poems answer one another across the
book. *Native Guard* weaves personal elegy for the poet's mother with the history of a regiment of Black
Union soldiers, including a sequence of linked sonnets in the voice of a soldier. In a (b) answer, place
the poem in its sequence; in an (a) answer, use the sequence's structure as evidence.

### Traditional forms used in new ways

Modern poets often use received forms (sonnet, villanelle, ghazal, pantoum) while changing their
meaning. A form associated with order or celebration used for loss or violence creates tension. Trethewey,
for example, uses strict repeating forms in poems about memory, so the form enacts return. Ask what a
form brings with it and how the poem uses that inheritance.

### Free verse and the line

Where there is no fixed metre, the **line break** does the shaping work. Analyse where lines end, what
word is held at the edge, and how white space on the page creates pause or isolation.

### Voice between traditions

Okara's poetry is often read as working between African and European traditions of language, image and
belief. Analyse how imagery, rhythm or syntax carries that tension, rather than describing it in general
terms.

## Context and AO5 for modern texts

Relevant contexts include colonialism and its aftermath, slavery and its legacy, war, the modernist
break with Victorian conventions, gender and family, and environmental change. Use them to explain
choices: why a narrative fragments, why a poem gives voice to the silenced, why a setting is unnamed.

Modern texts invite clearly defined **critical approaches**, which give AO5 a framework:

- **Postcolonial** readings: power, empire, the construction of the "other".
- **Feminist** readings: voice, the body, domestic life, who is silenced.
- **Psychoanalytic** readings: trauma, memory, repression.
- **Ecocritical** readings: the relationship between people and the natural world.

### Annotated model paragraph

> Coetzee's Magistrate narrates in the present tense, so the reader shares his partial understanding as
> events unfold. A sympathetic reading treats him as the novel's conscience: the official who refuses
> the Empire's cruelty and suffers for it. A more sceptical postcolonial reading notes that his interest
> in the barbarian girl repeats, in a gentler form, the Empire's desire to read and possess the bodies of
> others. The present-tense narration supports the second reading more strongly, because it denies him
> the hindsight to judge himself: we watch him fail to see his own complicity. The novel is most
> powerful, I would argue, as a portrait of a liberal conscience that cannot escape the system it
> opposes.

The paragraph uses **narrative method** (AO2), presents and tests **two readings** (AO5), and ends with a
**judgement** (AO3).

## Answer shapes

**(b) passage or poem**: locate in the text or sequence → thesis → close analysis of voice, structure,
form and imagery → link outward → evaluated AO5.

**(a) whole text**: positioned thesis → moments or poems from across the text → method in every
paragraph → AO5 woven through.

## Common mistakes

- **Filling the gaps**: inventing explanations a fragmented text deliberately withholds, instead of
  analysing the withholding.
- **Biography as reading**: assuming a poem's "I" is simply the poet.
- **Ignoring the sequence**: treating a poem from a collection as if it stood alone.
- **Approach without evidence**: naming a postcolonial or feminist reading without testing it on the
  text.
- **Breaking the form rule** across Paper 4.
