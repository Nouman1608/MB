This guide covers subtopic **34.4, Amino acids**, from Topic 34, Nitrogen compounds, of Cambridge
International AS & A Level Chemistry 9701, 2025–2027 series. It is A Level content. The combined
[Amides and Amino Acids study guide](/resources/a-amides-and-amino-acids/) introduces zwitterions and
electrophoresis; this page goes further, with side chains that carry their own charge, a method for
drawing the species at any pH, sequence counting for peptides, and electrophoresis predictions worked
through with data.

## What the syllabus asks for

Subtopic 34.4 has three learning outcomes:

1. describe the acid / base properties of amino acids and the formation of zwitterions, "to include
   the isoelectric point"
2. describe the formation of amide (peptide) bonds between amino acids to give di- and tripeptides
3. "interpret and predict the results of electrophoresis on mixtures of amino acids and dipeptides at
   varying pHs (the assembling of the apparatus will not be tested)"

## Structure of an amino acid

The amino acids in this subtopic have the general formula **H₂NCH(R)COOH**: an amine group and a
carboxylic acid group on the same carbon. Only the side chain R differs.

| Amino acid | R group | Feature of R |
|---|---|---|
| glycine | –H | no side-chain group; not chiral |
| alanine | –CH₃ | non-polar |
| serine | –CH₂OH | polar, uncharged |
| aspartic acid | –CH₂COOH | contains a second –COOH |
| lysine | –(CH₂)₄NH₂ | contains a second –NH₂ |

Every amino acid except glycine has four different groups on the central carbon, so it is chiral.

## Acid and base properties

An amino acid is **amphoteric**: the –NH₂ group is basic and the –COOH group is acidic.

- With an acid: H₂NCH(R)COOH + HCl → ⁺H₃NCH(R)COOH Cl⁻
- With an alkali: H₂NCH(R)COOH + NaOH → H₂NCH(R)COO⁻Na⁺ + H₂O

**Zwitterion.** Within one molecule, the –COOH group donates H⁺ to the –NH₂ group:

    H₂NCH(R)COOH → ⁺H₃NCH(R)COO⁻

The zwitterion has a positive and a negative charge, but **no overall charge**. In the solid state
amino acids exist as zwitterions, which is why they are crystalline solids with high melting points.

## Species at different pH: a three-step method

Start from the zwitterion and ask what extra H⁺ or OH⁻ will do.

1. **Low pH (excess H⁺):** –COO⁻ gains H⁺ → ⁺H₃NCH(R)COOH, overall **+1**.
2. **Isoelectric point:** the zwitterion dominates, overall **0**.
3. **High pH (excess OH⁻):** –NH₃⁺ loses H⁺ → H₂NCH(R)COO⁻, overall **−1**.

    ⁺H₃NCH(R)COOH  ⇌  ⁺H₃NCH(R)COO⁻  ⇌  H₂NCH(R)COO⁻
       (low pH)          (isoelectric)       (high pH)

**The isoelectric point** is the pH at which the amino acid exists mainly as the zwitterion and has
no overall charge. It is a property of each amino acid, set by its R group.

**Side chains that carry charge.** Apply the same rules to the extra group:

- **Aspartic acid** has a second –COOH. Its isoelectric point is low (acidic), because extra acid must
  be present to stop the side chain ionising. Near pH 7 the side chain is –CH₂COO⁻, so the overall
  charge is **−1**.
- **Lysine** has a second –NH₂. Its isoelectric point is high (alkaline). Near pH 7 the side chain is
  –(CH₂)₄NH₃⁺, so the overall charge is **+1**.

To find the charge at any pH: compare the pH with the isoelectric point. **pH below the isoelectric
point → positive. pH above → negative. Equal → zero.**

## Peptide bonds, dipeptides and tripeptides

The –COOH of one amino acid condenses with the –NH₂ of another, losing H₂O and forming a **peptide
(amide) bond**, –CONH–.

    H₂NCH₂COOH + H₂NCH(CH₃)COOH → H₂NCH₂CONHCH(CH₃)COOH + H₂O

**Sequence matters.** Glycine and alanine can join in two ways:

- glycine's –COOH to alanine's –NH₂: H₂NCH₂CONHCH(CH₃)COOH (Gly–Ala)
- alanine's –COOH to glycine's –NH₂: H₂NCH(CH₃)CONHCH₂COOH (Ala–Gly)

These are **different compounds** (structural isomers). In a mixture of glycine and alanine, Gly–Gly
and Ala–Ala can also form, giving **four** possible dipeptides.

**Counting sequences.** For a tripeptide:

- three different amino acids, each used once: 3 × 2 × 1 = **6** sequences;
- two amino acids, each position free to be either: 2 × 2 × 2 = **8** sequences.

**Hydrolysis.** Heating a peptide under reflux with aqueous acid breaks each peptide bond, giving
back the amino acids (as their cations in acid). This is the reverse of condensation and uses the
same chemistry as amide hydrolysis.

## Electrophoresis: interpreting and predicting

A mixture is placed in the centre of a strip of buffered gel or paper and a voltage is applied.

- **Direction:** positive species move to the **cathode (−)**; negative species move to the **anode
  (+)**; a species with no overall charge stays at the start.
- **Distance:** a **larger charge** moves further; a **larger, heavier** species moves more slowly
  than a smaller one with the same charge.

So for a dipeptide and an amino acid with the same overall charge, the amino acid (smaller) usually
travels further.

## Worked example 1: predicting an electrophoresis result

**Question.** A mixture of glycine, lysine and aspartic acid is separated by electrophoresis at
pH 6.0. Their isoelectric points are 6.0, 9.7 and 2.8 respectively. Predict the result.

**Answer.**

| Amino acid | Isoelectric point | pH 6.0 is... | Charge | Movement |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| glycine | 6.0 | equal | 0 | stays at the start |
| lysine | 9.7 | below | positive | towards the cathode (−) |
| aspartic acid | 2.8 | above | negative | towards the anode (+) |

Three separate spots: one at the origin, one on each side.

*Examiner's note:* state the charge, then the electrode, for each species. "Lysine moves to the
negative" without saying it is positive will often lose the mark.

## Worked example 2: choosing a pH to separate two amino acids

**Question.** Glycine (isoelectric point 6.0) and alanine (isoelectric point 6.0) cannot be separated
at pH 6.0. Explain why, and suggest why separation is also poor at pH 9.

**Answer.** At pH 6.0 both are zwitterions with no overall charge, so neither moves. At pH 9 both are
above their isoelectric points and both carry a −1 charge, so both move towards the anode. Their
masses are similar (alanine has one extra CH₂ unit), so they travel similar distances and the spots
overlap. Electrophoresis separates best when the species differ in **charge**, not just in mass.

## Common mistakes

- Drawing H₂NCH(R)COOH as the form in the solid or at the isoelectric point; it is the zwitterion.
- Sending positive ions to the anode. **Positive → cathode (−).**
- Assuming every amino acid has an isoelectric point near 7; side chains change it.
- Counting Gly–Ala and Ala–Gly as the same dipeptide.
- Forgetting that size affects distance as well as charge.
- Describing how to set up the apparatus: not tested.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Describe** acid/base properties: give the zwitterion and the species formed with H⁺ and OH⁻,
  with charges.
- **Describe** peptide formation: name the bond, the small molecule lost, and give the structure.
- **Predict** electrophoresis: compare each pH with each isoelectric point, give the charge, then the
  direction and relative distance.
- **Interpret** an electrophoresis result: work backwards from distance and direction to charge, then
  to pH relative to the isoelectric point.
