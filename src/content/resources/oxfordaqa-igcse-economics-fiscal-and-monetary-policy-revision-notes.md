## Key definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Fiscal policy | Government decisions on what to spend and what to tax, aimed at steering the economy |
| Government budget | Planned revenue and spending for a year |
| Balanced budget | Spending = revenue |
| Budget deficit | Spending > revenue; paid for by borrowing |
| Budget surplus | Revenue > spending |
| National debt | Total the government owes from past borrowing |
| Monetary policy | Changing interest rates to steer the economy |
| Expansionary | Aims to raise total spending |
| Contractionary | Aims to reduce total spending |

## Fiscal policy at a glance

| Tool | Expansionary | Contractionary |
|---|---|---|
| Government spending | Up | Down |
| Income tax | Down | Up |
| Corporation tax | Down | Up |
| Effect on demand | Up | Down |

**Chain for a tax cut:** lower income tax, higher disposable income, more consumption, more demand, firms raise output and hire, income rises.

**Chain for spending:** the government buys goods or builds projects, firms and workers gain income, they spend part of it, and income rises by more than the first injection.

### Calculation reminders

**Balance and size of deficit**
1. Add up revenue.
2. Add up spending.
3. Balance = revenue − spending. Negative means deficit.
4. Percentage of GDP = balance ÷ GDP × 100.

*Example (Country B, bn units).* Revenue 62, spending 58. Balance = +4, a surplus. With GDP of 250, that is 4 ÷ 250 × 100 = **1.6% of GDP**. Debt of 90 falls to 86, which is 36.0% of GDP falling to 34.4%.

**Knock-on effect of extra spending**
1. Find the share of extra income spent again in the country (call it s).
2. Total change = first injection ÷ (1 − s).

*Example.* Spending rises by 2.5 and s = 0.6. Total = 2.5 ÷ 0.4 = **6.25**. The knock-on factor is 1 ÷ 0.4 = 2.5.

*Tax cut of 4 with s = 0.6.* First-round extra spending = 0.6 × 4 = 2.4. Total = 2.4 ÷ 0.4 = **6.0**, less than the 10 that 4 of direct spending would give (4 ÷ 0.4 = 10).

The specification does not name the multiplier. Treat this as the knock-on effect of spending.

## Fiscal policy and objectives

| Objective | Action | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| Growth | Spending on roads, schools; lower business taxes | Demand and investment rise; productive capacity rises |
| Employment | Spending up or taxes down | Demand rises, so labour demand rises |
| Price stability | Spending down or taxes up | Demand falls, so price pressure eases |
| Balance of payments | Taxes up | Lower incomes, fewer imports |
| Reducing inequality | Taxes on high incomes up; benefits up | Disposable incomes move closer together; limit: weaker incentives |

Conflicts to mention: growth and jobs may raise inflation and imports; cutting inflation may raise unemployment.

## Deficit versus surplus

| | Deficit | Surplus |
|---|---|---|
| Debt | Rises | Can be repaid |
| Interest | Rises | Falls |
| Demand | Supported | Weakened |
| Future | Burden on later taxpayers | More room for tax cuts or spending |
| Danger | Less money for services; inflation near full capacity | Less demand; may come from harmful cuts |

A balanced budget avoids debt growth but leaves little room to support a recession.

## Monetary policy at a glance

**Rate rise:** borrowing dearer, saving rewarded, consumption and investment fall, demand falls, inflation slows.
**Rate cut:** the reverse; demand, output and jobs tend to rise, but prices may rise faster.

| Objective | Rate move | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Control inflation | Up | Less demand, less demand-pull pressure |
| Growth | Down | Cheaper borrowing, more spending and investment |
| Employment | Down | Higher demand, more hiring |
| Balance of payments | Down (usually) | Weaker currency, cheaper exports, dearer imports |

### Calculation reminders

- Interest on a loan = amount × rate. *Loan of 20,000 at 5% = 1,000; at 3.5% = 700; saving of 300.*
- Saver: 5,000 at 2% earns 100; at 3.5% earns 175.
- Inflation = (new index − old index) ÷ old index × 100. *Index 120.0 to 123.0: 3.0 ÷ 120 × 100 = **2.5%**.*

### Must-know distinctions

- **Deficit vs debt:** one year's gap vs the running total.
- **Fiscal vs monetary:** taxes and spending vs interest rates.
- **Demand-pull vs cost-push:** demand tools work on the first; on the second they mainly cut output.
- **Expansionary vs contractionary:** direction of the change in demand.

### Evaluation checklist

- Time lags
- Spare capacity
- Leakages into saving and imports
- Confidence
- Who gains and loses (borrowers, savers, taxpayers)
- Conflict with another objective
- Short run versus long run

### Exchange rate reminder (Country B, illustrative)

A lower interest rate tends to lower the exchange rate. Suppose 1 unit of Country B's money = 1.50 zed (the neighbour's currency), and it falls to 1.30 zed.
- An export priced at 30 units: foreign price = 30 × 1.50 = 45 zed, then 30 × 1.30 = 39 zed. It is **13.3% cheaper** abroad, so export demand tends to rise.
- An import priced at 39 zed: home price = 39 ÷ 1.50 = 26 units, then 39 ÷ 1.30 = 30 units. It is **15.4% dearer**, so import demand tends to fall.

Both effects point the same way, towards a smaller current account deficit. How large the change is depends on how strongly buyers respond to price.

### Building a full-chain answer

Use this order for any "explain how policy X achieves objective Y" question.
1. Name the policy change and its direction.
2. State the immediate effect (disposable income, borrowing cost, firms' costs).
3. State the effect on spending (consumption, investment, government spending, net exports).
4. State the effect on total demand and output, then on prices or jobs.
5. Link back to the objective in the question.
6. Add one limit: a time lag, spare capacity, a leakage or a conflict.

*Short example.* Income tax is cut. Households keep more of each pay cheque. They spend part of it, so consumption rises and firms sell more. Output rises and firms hire, so unemployment falls. The gain may be small if households save most of the cut, and demand may pull up prices.

### Choosing between fiscal and monetary policy

| Situation | Likely tool | Reason to prefer it | Limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Deep recession, firms not borrowing | Fiscal expansion | Government spending adds demand directly | Raises the deficit and debt |
| Demand-pull inflation, public finances sound | Rate rise | Quick to announce, no new taxes | Borrowers lose, jobs may fall |
| Need to protect public services | Rate move | Avoids cutting spending | Slow to take effect |
| Low confidence | Fiscal expansion | Does not depend on borrowers | Takes time to plan |

## Quick self-test

1. Define a budget deficit.
2. Country B takes in revenue of 71 and spends 68. Work out the balance and name it.
3. Calculate the balance in question 2 as a percentage of GDP of 300.
4. State two expansionary fiscal actions.
5. Spending rises by 3 and s = 0.5. Calculate the total change.
6. Explain one cost of running a budget surplus.
7. A price index rises from 150.0 to 153.0. Calculate inflation.
8. Explain how a rate rise reduces inflation.
9. A loan of 40,000 has its rate cut from 6% to 4.5%. Calculate the fall in annual interest.
10. Explain one way a lower interest rate could help the balance of payments.

### Answers

1. Spending greater than revenue in a year.
2. 71 − 68 = **+3**, a surplus.
3. 3 ÷ 300 × 100 = **1%**.
4. Any two: raise government spending; cut income tax; cut corporation tax.
5. 3 ÷ (1 − 0.5) = **6**.
6. Taxing more than is spent removes money from the economy, so demand and growth may slow; or services may be cut to produce the surplus.
7. (153 − 150) ÷ 150 × 100 = **2%**.
8. Borrowing costs more and saving pays more, so households and firms spend and invest less, demand falls and price pressure eases.
9. Interest falls from 2,400 (6%) to 1,800 (4.5%), a fall of **600**.
10. A lower rate makes the country less attractive to foreign savers, so demand for its currency falls and the exchange rate falls. Exports become cheaper abroad and imports dearer, so net exports tend to rise.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calling a deficit the national debt.
- Giving a policy but no chain of reasoning to the objective.
- Saying a surplus is good without a cost.
- Reversing the direction of a rate change.
- Forgetting that borrowers and savers are affected in opposite ways.
- Using demand-side tools for cost-push inflation without a limit.
- Giving a calculation answer without units or working.
- Listing evaluation points without saying which matters most.
