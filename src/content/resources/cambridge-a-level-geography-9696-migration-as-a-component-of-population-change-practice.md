## Section A

**1.** Define the term "migration" as it is used in the 9696 syllabus. **[2]**

**2.** Distinguish between gross migration and net migration. **[2]**

**3.** Explain how chain migration operates. **[3]**

**4.** Outline **two** different types of barrier that can prevent people from migrating. **[4]**

**5.** The table shows migration data for four districts of the fictional country of Dravia in one year.

| District | Description | Mid-year population | In-migrants | Out-migrants |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | Suburban district next to the capital | 120 000 | 4 200 | 2 400 |
| B | Remote upland farming district | 45 000 | 600 | 1 500 |
| C | Capital city with three universities | 300 000 | 18 000 | 17 100 |
| D | Small market town | 60 000 | 2 100 | 2 400 |

**(a)** Calculate the net migration rate for district B. Show your working. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the migration effectiveness for district C and state what it shows. **[3]**

**(c)** Suggest reasons for the contrast in net migration between districts A and B. **[4]**

**6.** The table shows the percentage of each age group in Dravia that changed district of residence during one year.

| Age group | 0-14 | 15-19 | 20-24 | 25-29 | 30-44 | 45-59 | 60-69 | 70+ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Moved (%) | 6 | 9 | 18 | 15 | 8 | 4 | 5 | 3 |

**(a)** Describe the pattern shown in the table. **[3]**

**(b)** Explain the peak at ages 20-29 and the small rise at ages 60-69. **[4]**

## Section B

**7.** Explain how migration can change the age-sex structure of **both** a source area and a destination area. **[6]**

**8.** Explain why the number of migrants usually decreases as distance increases, and why some migration flows do not follow this pattern. **[5]**

**9.** Explain why commuting and most seasonal movements are not counted as migration, and why they still matter to the study of migration. **[4]**

**10.** "Push factors are more important than pull factors in causing migration." With reference to examples, how far do you agree? **[15]**

## Answers

**1.** A change of a person's usual place of residence [1] that lasts for at least one year; movements of less than a year are excluded [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Gross migration is the total of in-migrants plus out-migrants, showing the amount of movement [1]. Net migration is in-migrants minus out-migrants, showing the overall gain or loss [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Early (pioneer) migrants settle in a destination [1]. They send back information, money for travel or offers of housing and work to relatives and neighbours [1]. This lowers the cost and risk for later migrants, who follow to the same places, so streams grow and cluster [1]. **[3]**

**4.** Any two types, 2 marks each: an economic barrier such as the cost of travel and agents' fees [1], which the poorest households cannot afford [1]; a political barrier such as visas or border controls [1], which limit who can legally enter [1]; a social barrier such as family responsibilities or language [1], which ties some people to the origin [1]; a physical barrier such as mountains or sea [1], which raises the time and cost of a journey [1]. **[4]**

**5. (a)** Net migration = 600 − 1 500 = −900 [1]; −900 ÷ 45 000 × 1000 = −20.0 per 1000 [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** Net migration 18 000 − 17 100 = 900; gross migration 18 000 + 17 100 = 35 100 [1]; 900 ÷ 35 100 × 100 = 2.6% [1]. Flows in and out almost cancel: the city has a very high turnover but little net gain [1]. **[3]**

**(c)** District A gains (+1 800) because it is next to the capital, so families can move to larger, cheaper homes while still commuting to city jobs [1]. New housing estates on the city edge attract movers [1]. District B loses (−900) because remote farming offers few jobs and low wages, pushing young adults out [1]. Distance from the capital and poor services make it unattractive to in-migrants [1]. **[4]**

*Examiner insight:* quote the net figures for both districts; "suggest" answers must fit the descriptions in the table.

**6. (a)** Mobility is low among children (6%) and rises to a peak at 20-24 (18%) [1]. It then falls steadily to a low of 4% at 45-59 [1]. There is a small rise to 5% at 60-69 before the lowest figure, 3%, at 70+ [1]. **[3]**

**(b)** People aged 20-29 leave education, start work and form partnerships [1], and have few ties such as owned homes or school-age children [1]. They have a long working life in which to gain from a move [1]. At 60-69 some people move on retirement, for example to cheaper or quieter areas or nearer to family [1]. **[4]**

*Examiner insight:* also credit the point that the 0-14 figure (6%) is close to the 30-44 figure (8%) because children move with their parents, in place of any point in (b).

**7.** In the source area, out-migration of young adults narrows the pyramid at ages 20-39 [1]. Fewer births follow because people of child-bearing age have left, so the base narrows too [1]. The population ages and the old-age dependency ratio rises [1]. In the destination, the pyramid bulges at 20-39 [1] and later at 0-9 as migrants have children [1]. If the stream is dominated by one sex, for example men moving for construction work, the sex ratio becomes unbalanced in both places [1]. **[6]**

**8.** Longer moves cost more in travel and resettlement [1]. People have less information about distant places [1]. More barriers, such as borders, language and culture, are crossed [1]. Intervening opportunities nearer the origin capture some migrants [1]. Exceptions include skilled workers in a national or global job market, or refugees whose destination is set by safety, so they may travel far [1]. **[5]**

**9.** The syllabus defines migration as a change of residence lasting a year or more [1], and commuters and seasonal workers keep their usual home [1]. They still matter because circulation often comes before a permanent move [1], and it spreads information and contacts that start chain migration [1]. **[4]**

**10.** The real exam marks extended answers with levels of response, not point by point. The points below are indicative creditworthy content.

*Introduction:* push factors are features of the origin that encourage people to leave [1]; pull factors are attractions of a destination [1].

*Agree:* forced migration is push-dominated: refugees flee conflict or persecution with little regard for the destination [1]. Environmental push factors, such as repeated drought, can force rural people to leave even when urban prospects are poor [1]. Rural poverty and land shortage drive rural-urban migration in many LICs [1].

*Disagree:* many voluntary moves are pull-dominated, such as skilled workers recruited by employers abroad [1]. The choice of destination is explained by pull factors and networks, not by the origin [1]. Chain migration shows that information about a destination decides who moves and where [1]. People in the same push conditions often do not move, so push factors alone cannot explain migration [1].

*Interaction and other factors:* Lee's model shows both sets of factors are filtered by intervening obstacles [1] and personal factors such as age [1]. Barriers such as cost mean the poorest, who face the strongest push, often cannot move far [1].

*Use of examples:* a named forced flow supports the agree side [1]; a named labour stream supports the disagree side [1].

*Judgement:* the balance varies by type of migration, push for forced and pull for most voluntary moves, with a reasoned final view [1]. **[15]**

*Examiner insight:* the best answers vary their judgement by type of migration and show that barriers decide whether push or pull factors are acted on at all.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Leaving out the one-year rule when defining migration.
- Dropping the minus sign in net migration calculations, or using the wrong population as the denominator.
- Describing age patterns without quoting figures from the table.
- Explaining pyramid changes for only one of the two areas asked about.
- Listing push and pull factors without weighing them.
- Essays with no named, real migration stream.
