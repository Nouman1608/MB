These questions cover topic 1.3 Population structure in Theme 1 (Population and settlement) of the Cambridge IGCSE Geography (0460) syllabus for examination in 2025 and 2026. All countries and towns in the data questions are fictional and the data are invented. Pyramid data are given as tables; sketch the pyramid first if that helps you.

Revise first with the [Population Structure study guide](/resources/cambridge-igcse-geography-0460-population-structure/) and the [Population Structure revision notes](/resources/igcse-geography-0460-population-structure-revision-notes/).

## Section A

**1.** Define the term "dependency ratio". **[2]**

**2.** State the two age groups that are normally counted as the dependent population. **[2]**

**3.** Give two features of the population pyramid of a country with a youthful population. **[2]**

**4.** Suggest why the top bars of most population pyramids are wider on the female side. **[1]**

**5.** State two implications of an ageing population for a country. **[2]**

## Section B

**6.** The table shows the population structure of the fictional Country R, as percentages of the total population.

| Age group | Males (%) | Females (%) |
|---|---|---|
| 0-14 | 22 | 21 |
| 15-29 | 13 | 13 |
| 30-44 | 7 | 7 |
| 45-64 | 5 | 6 |
| 65 and over | 3 | 3 |

**(a)** Calculate the percentage of Country R's population aged 0-14. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate Country R's dependency ratio. Give your answer to one decimal place. **[2]**
**(c)** Describe the population structure of Country R. **[3]**
**(d)** Suggest the level of economic development of Country R, giving reasons from the data. **[2]**

**7.** In the fictional coastal town of Seabrook, 34% of residents are aged 65 and over and 9% are aged 0-14. Suggest reasons for this structure and its implications for services in the town. **[4]**

**8.** In 2025, the population pyramid of a fictional country shows a notch in the 35-39 age group for both males and females, and a bulge in the 0-4 and 5-9 age groups. Suggest reasons for these two features. **[4]**

**9.** The table shows data for two fictional countries.

| | 0-14 (%) | 15-64 (%) | 65 and over (%) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Country X | 18 | 60 | 22 |
| Country Y | 38 | 57 | 5 |

**(a)** Calculate the dependency ratio of each country, to one decimal place. **[2]**
**(b)** Country X has the lower dependency ratio. Explain why its government may still be more worried about the future cost of its dependent population. **[3]**

**10.** For a named country with a high dependent population, describe its population structure and explain the problems this structure causes. **[7]**

**11.** Explain how international migration can change the population structure of both the area that migrants leave and the area they move to. **[5]**

**12.** "A youthful population causes more problems for a country than an ageing population." To what extent do you agree? Use examples. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** The ratio of the dependent population (aged 0-14 and 65 and over) to the working population (aged 15-64) [1], usually multiplied by 100 [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Give the age groups; "the number of people who depend on workers" alone is too vague for both marks.

**2.** 0-14 (young dependants) [1]. 65 and over (elderly dependants) [1]. **[2]**

**3.** A wide base, showing a high birth rate [1]. Sides that narrow quickly and a small top, showing a short life expectancy [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Women have a longer life expectancy than men, so more women survive to old age [1]. **[1]**

**5.** Rising spending on pensions and healthcare [1]. A shrinking workforce, causing labour shortages and fewer taxpayers [1]. Also credit closure of schools or the need to raise the retirement age. **[2]**

**6. (a)** 22 + 21 = 43% [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** (43 + 6) ÷ (26 + 14 + 11) × 100 = 49 ÷ 51 × 100 [1] = 96.1 [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** A very wide base: 43% of the population is under 15 [1]. Numbers fall quickly with age, to only 11% at 45-64 [1]. Only 6% are 65 and over, and males and females are almost equal in every group, with slightly more women at 45-64 [1]. **[3]**
**(d)** A low level of development [1], because the large share under 15 suggests a high birth rate and the small share over 65 suggests a short life expectancy [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Descriptions must quote data from the table; a general description of a youthful pyramid with no figures gains little.

**7.** Older people move to the town after retiring, attracted by the coast and a quieter lifestyle [1]. Young adults may leave for jobs or university elsewhere, so few children are born [1]. Implication: high demand for healthcare, care homes and accessible transport [1]. Implication: falling demand for schools, which may close or merge [1]. **[4]**

**8.** Notch: people aged 35-39 in 2025 were born in about 1986 to 1990, so births may have fallen then because of war, famine, economic crisis or a policy restricting births [1]. Alternatively, people of this age may have been killed in a conflict or epidemic, or emigrated in large numbers [1]. Bulge: births have risen in the last ten years, for example because of a pro-natalist policy [1], or the arrival of young immigrant families [1]. **[4]**

**9. (a)** Country X: (18 + 22) ÷ 60 × 100 = 66.7 [1]. Country Y: (38 + 5) ÷ 57 × 100 = 75.4 [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Most of Country X's dependants are elderly (22%), and with only 18% under 15 the number of future workers is shrinking [1]. Elderly dependants need pensions and expensive healthcare for many years, often more costly per person than educating a child [1]. Country Y's young dependants will soon become workers and taxpayers, whereas Country X's elderly dependants will increase as the large working-age groups grow older [1]. **[3]**

**10.** The real exam marks extended answers with levels of response, not point by point. Indicative creditworthy content, for example for Uganda:

Named country and structure: close to half the population is under 15 [1]; a wide base and a small top, showing high fertility and a short life expectancy [1]. Reason: early marriage and limited family planning in rural areas [1]. Problem: pressure on schools; when primary fees were removed in 1997, enrolment rose and classes became very large [1]. Problem: too few teachers and classrooms [1]. Problem: far more young people reach working age each year than there are formal jobs [1]. Problem: high demand for maternal and child health services [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* Top-level answers link each problem to the structure; a list of problems found in any low-income country is not enough.

**11.** In the destination, immigration widens the young working-age groups, especially 20-39 [1], and often more on the male side where labour migrants are mostly men [1]. Later, migrants' children widen the base [1]. In the origin, the 20-39 age groups narrow as young adults leave [1], leaving a higher proportion of children and elderly people, and sometimes more women than men [1]. **[5]**

**12.** Indicative content:

Youthful problems: pressure on schools and child healthcare [1]; too few jobs for young people reaching working age, leading to unemployment and migration [1]; population momentum means continued growth, as in Niger or Uganda [1]. Ageing problems: rising pension and healthcare costs [1]; labour shortages and a shrinking tax base, as in Japan [1]. Counter-argument: a youthful population can become a large workforce that drives growth if jobs are created [1]. Counter-argument: wealthier ageing countries usually have more money to pay for their dependants [1]. Judgement: a youthful population is harder for a low-income country because it has fewer resources, but in the long term an ageing population is harder to reverse [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Use one named country for each type, and make the judgement depend on something specific, such as wealth or time scale.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Putting the working population on the top of the dependency ratio.
- Describing pyramid shapes with no figures from the resource.
- Explaining a notch without linking it to the date when that age group was born.
- Giving pension costs as a problem of a youthful population.
- Case study answers with no named country.
