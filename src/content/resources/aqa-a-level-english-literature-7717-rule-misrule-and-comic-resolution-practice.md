## Section A: structural concepts

**1.** Explain how comic structure reverses AQA's description of tragic structure, and why this
allows an audience to laugh at characters' discomfort. **[3]**

**2.** Explain what "misrule" means and how the idea applies to one comedy you have studied. **[3]**

**3.** AQA refers to "how competition between characters is set up and resolved". Identify one
competition in a studied text and the device that resolves it. **[2]**

**4.** Explain the structural function of the Hastings and Constance plot in *She Stoops to Conquer*.
**[3]**

**5.** Explain why the resolution of *The Importance of Being Earnest* can be read as both using and
mocking the conventions of comic plotting. **[3]**

## Section B: an original scene and essay skills

**Extract (original drama).** It is the night of a village fair. A strict magistrate, Mr Pyke, has
banned dancing in the square. His nephew Ned and the innkeeper's daughter Sal have organised a dance
anyway, and have dressed the town drunk, Old Barnaby, in the magistrate's spare robes.

> *(Music. Villagers dance. OLD BARNABY sits on a barrel, robed, holding a ladle like a sceptre.)*
> **BARNABY:** Silence in court! I sentence the fiddler to play faster.
> *(Laughter. The fiddler obeys.)*
> **SAL:** And what is the sentence for Mr Pyke, my lord?
> **BARNABY:** To dance. Twice. Backwards.
> *(Enter MR PYKE, in his nightshirt, carrying a candle. The music stops.)*
> **PYKE:** Who has stolen my robes?
> **NED:** No one, Uncle. They were borrowed by the law.
> **PYKE:** I am the law.
> **BARNABY:** *(rising, with dignity)* Not tonight, sir. Tonight you are the accused.

**6.** Analyse how the extract presents misrule. **[6]**

**7.** Explain how Pyke's entrance works as a structural turning point in the scene. **[3]**

**8.** Suggest two ways the play's ending could resolve the conflict between Pyke and the villagers,
and explain which would better fit AQA's idea that "all will end well". **[4]**

**9.** Explain how a festive scene like the extract could also contain the seeds of discomfort that
complicate a comic resolution, with reference to a similar moment in a studied text. **[4]**

**10.** Plan a Section B answer on your Shakespeare play for the question: "The ending of the play
restores order, but the audience remembers the misrule." List four elements of your plan. **[4]**

**11.** Explain how the alternating structure of *Small Island* contributes to its comic and serious
effects. **[3]**

**12.** In a Section C answer linking two texts, explain how comparing their resolution devices could
meet AO4, and give an example from two texts on the Option 1B list. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** Tragedy moves from order to disorder, while comedy moves from disorder to order [1]. Because the
structure promises a new order at the end [1], the audience trusts that discomfort is temporary and
can laugh at it rather than fear it [1].

**2.** Misrule is a period in which normal authority is suspended or inverted, borrowed from festive
tradition [1]. Application, [2] for a developed example: for instance, in *Twelfth Night* Sir Toby's
late-night revelry overturns the household's order until Malvolio, the steward, tries to silence it,
so rule and misrule are set against each other; or the one night of mistakes in *She Stoops to
Conquer*, when a gentleman's house is treated as an inn.

**3.** A named competition [1] and its resolving device [1]: for example, the husbands' rivalry over
their wives' obedience in *The Taming of the Shrew*, resolved by the wager; or the fox and Chauntecleer
in "The Nun's Priest's Tale", resolved by a counter-trick.

**4.** It provides a parallel love plot beside Marlow and Kate [1]; it adds complications through the
jewels and Mrs Hardcastle's plans [1]; it is resolved when Tony is found to be of age and can refuse
Constance, so the sub-plot's knot is untied in the final act alongside the main plot [1].

**5.** The handbag and Miss Prism's confession untie the plot neatly, as comic resolutions conventionally
do [1]; but the coincidence that Jack's name really is Ernest is so extreme [1] that Wilde exposes the
artificiality of such endings, so the play draws attention to itself as comedy [1].

**6.** Any six, [1] each:

- Barnaby, the town drunk, is dressed in the magistrate's robes, a direct inversion of authority.
- The ladle "like a sceptre" turns a kitchen object into a symbol of rule, mocking its dignity.
- "Silence in court!" parodies legal language for a sentence that rewards rather than punishes.
- The sentence on the absent Pyke, "To dance. Twice. Backwards", turns the man who banned dancing
  into its victim.
- The villagers' laughter and the fiddler's obedience show the community sharing the inversion.
- Ned's "borrowed by the law" is wordplay that justifies the misrule.
- Barnaby's "Not tonight, sir" marks the misrule as temporary, a single festive night.
- "Tonight you are the accused" completes the reversal of judge and judged.

**7.** The music stopping marks a sudden shift from festivity to confrontation [1]; Pyke's nightshirt
and candle contrast with the stolen robes, so the real authority appears less dignified than the
mock one [1]; his claim "I am the law" sets up the competition the rest of the play must resolve [1].

**8.** Two plausible resolutions [1 each]: for example, Pyke joins the dance and lifts the ban; or Pyke
reasserts the ban and punishes the villagers. A judgement that the first better fits "all will end
well" because rule and misrule are reconciled rather than one defeating the other [1], with a reason
linked to comic structure, such as opposites contrasted and reconciled [1].

**9.** The extract's humiliation of Pyke could leave him resentful [1], so a festive trick might turn
into a grievance that disturbs the ending [1]. Comparison [2]: for example, in *Twelfth Night* the
revel against Malvolio grows into the trick that ends in his confinement, and his final exit
threatening revenge shows that misrule has a cost.

**10.** Any four, [1] each: a thesis that takes a position on whether misrule outweighs order; a
paragraph analysing the main period of misrule and its placement; a paragraph analysing the final
scene's restoration of order; a paragraph on who remains outside the resolution; reference to
context such as festive tradition; an alternative interpretation weighed.

**11.** The movement between 1948 and "Before" lets comic misunderstandings in the present sit beside
wartime loss and past hardship [1]; the four narrators give competing versions of the same events,
which creates comedy of misunderstanding [1]; and the shifting viewpoint also exposes prejudice from
the inside, so the structure keeps serious effects in view [1].

**12.** Comparing devices links the texts through a shared aspect of comedy [1], provided both texts are
discussed within each point [1]. Example [1]: *The Importance of Being Earnest* is resolved by a
sudden revelation of identity, whereas *Emma* is resolved more gradually through revelations that
correct Emma's misreadings; both end with marriages, but one mocks its contrivance and the other
grounds it in moral learning.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Retelling the confusions instead of analysing how they are arranged.
- Assuming every opposite is reconciled in the final scene.
- Ignoring sub-plots, or treating them as unrelated stories.
- In Section C, writing about each text's ending separately.

For full practice, choose a scene of misrule from your Shakespeare play and write a 25-mark Section A
response in about 50 minutes, opening with one sentence that places the passage in the comic
structure.
