These notes cover the topic "Climate change, energy and resources" from the Cambridge IGCSE Global Perspectives (0457) syllabus for examination in 2025, 2026 and 2027, Version 2 (published April 2025). The topic is on the topic list in section 3, which the syllabus says is for all components: Component 1 Written Exam, Component 2 Individual Report and Component 3 Team Project. The skills come from section 2 (assessment objectives) and section 4 (details of the assessment).

**Topic knowledge is not assessed.** The syllabus says "Knowledge of content is not assessed in this syllabus." These notes show how to use this topic to practise the assessed skills. Do not revise climate facts for their own sake.

## Real examples at a glance

You will not be marked for knowing these. Use them as context for research and to test your reasoning.

| Example | What it is | What it shows | One evaluation point |
|---|---|---|---|
| Paris Agreement (2015) | Global deal: hold warming well below 2°C and pursue 1.5°C | Global course of action | Countries set their own targets, so success depends on them keeping their promises |
| UN Framework Convention on Climate Change (1992) | Treaty with "common but differentiated responsibilities" | Why developing and developed countries disagree | The principle is agreed, but how to share costs is still disputed |
| IPCC (set up 1988) | Body that assesses published climate research | A source with high credibility | Projections are ranges that depend on future emissions |
| COP27 (2022) loss and damage fund | Agreement to set up a fund for vulnerable countries | A fairness-based course of action | A fund only helps if enough money is paid into it |
| Germany's nuclear shutdown (15 April 2023) | Last three nuclear plants closed | Perspectives within one country | Safety concerns on one side, low-carbon supply on the other |
| Alliance of Small Island States | Group of island and low-lying coastal states | A strong global perspective on urgency | They have a vested interest, but that alone does not weaken their evidence |

## Must-know distinctions

- **Fact vs claim.** A fact can be checked. A claim needs evidence before you accept it. A number in a source is only as good as its method.
- **Opinion vs perspective.** An opinion can stand alone. A perspective is based on evidence and reasoning.
- **Prediction vs evidence.** "Bills will fall" is a prediction. It can be reasonable, but it is not yet evidence.
- **Vested interest vs bias.** A vested interest is a reason a source might be biased. Bias is shown in the content (one-sided, missing evidence). Check the content before you decide.
- **Cause vs consequence.** In a feedback loop, one can become the other (heat → air conditioning → more emissions → more heat).
- **Mitigation vs adaptation.** Cutting emissions vs coping with effects. A course of action can do one without the other.
- **Global, national, local.** Component 2 needs local and/or national and global perspectives. Component 3 starts from a local issue.
- **Several courses of action vs one.** In Component 2, consider several, then propose a single course of action and explain how it could be implemented and what its impact would be.

## Mini essay plan

**Typical extended task:** evaluate which of three courses of action would best reduce a country's emissions from electricity (in the Written Exam, the actions and evidence come from the source insert).

1. **Set the criteria.** Effectiveness, cost and feasibility, fairness, and side effects. Say which matters most and why.
2. **Action 1, e.g. carbon tax.** Use source evidence: it may cut emissions by making fossil fuels dearer. Limitation: it can hit poorer households hardest unless money is returned to them.
3. **Action 2, e.g. renewables subsidy.** Evidence of uptake from the source. Limitation: uptake may be uneven if only wealthier people can afford the rest of the cost.
4. **Action 3, e.g. community energy schemes.** Strong on fairness and local support. Limitation: small scale, slow to grow.
5. **Compare directly.** Do not just describe each. Say which is better on each criterion, using source evidence.
6. **Judgement.** Choose one (or a justified combination). Explain why it beats the others, and what would need to be true for it to work.

Each paragraph should follow: point from the source → apply it → analyse the effect → evaluate with a limitation.

## Coursework ideas at a glance

**Individual Report (Component 2).** The issue must divide opinion, and your title is a global research question. You need local and/or national and global perspectives, then one chosen course of action.

| Possible question | Perspectives to research |
|---|---|
| Should rich countries pay poorer countries to stop using coal? | A coal-mining town; a national government balancing jobs and targets; international donors and campaign groups |
| Should new oil and gas fields still be opened? | Local workers; a national treasury that relies on fuel income; countries most at risk from warming |
| Should cities ban petrol cars from their centres? | Local shop owners and commuters; national transport policy; global health and climate views |

**Team Project (Component 3).** Start from a local issue the team can act on and measure.

| Local issue | Possible action | How to measure success |
|---|---|---|
| Lights and fans left on in empty classrooms | Switch-off campaign with student monitors | Count rooms left on before and after |
| Families unsure how to cut energy bills | Short guide shared with parents | Short survey before and after reading it |
| Most pupils driven to school | Walk-to-school week | Count cars at the gate on matched days |

## Quick self-test

1. What does the syllabus say about knowledge of content in 0457?
2. Give the syllabus definition of a perspective.
3. Name the eight terms Component 1 says you will benefit from understanding.
4. "Electric cars will replace petrol cars within 20 years." Which term best describes this statement?
5. Why does a vested interest not prove that a source is wrong?
6. Give one global, one national and one local issue within this topic.
7. What is the difference between mitigation and adaptation?
8. Why is "Climate change" a poor Individual Report title?
9. Which assessment objective makes up all of Component 1?
10. What must a Team Project plan say about the action, besides what it is?
11. Give one limitation of using IPCC projections as evidence.

### Answers

1. Knowledge of content is not assessed. The topics are a context for building skills.
2. A viewpoint on an issue based on evidence and reasoning.
3. Bias, claim, fact, generalisation, opinion, prediction, value and vested interest.
4. A prediction. It is about the future and cannot yet be checked.
5. It is a reason to check the evidence closely. The source may still be accurate and well reasoned.
6. For example: global, sharing the cost of cutting emissions; national, choosing how to generate electricity; local, energy waste in a school.
7. Mitigation reduces the causes of climate change. Adaptation helps people cope with its effects.
8. It is a topic, not a question. It does not name an issue that divides opinion or set up different perspectives.
9. AO1 Research, analysis and evaluation.
10. How the action will be evidenced and how its success will be measured, plus team roles and responsibilities.
11. They are ranges that depend on future emissions, so no single figure is certain.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Adding outside facts about climate science in Component 1 instead of using the sources. Prior knowledge is not assessed.
- Identifying a term (for example "this is an opinion") without explaining why, using words from the source.
- Calling a source unreliable only because of who wrote it, without looking at its evidence and reasoning.
- Accepting survey numbers without asking who was asked, how many, and by whom.
- Describing each course of action separately and never comparing them or reaching a judgement.
- Giving perspectives that are only "for" and "against" with no reasons, evidence or group named.
- In the Individual Report, discussing courses of action but never choosing one and explaining how it could be implemented.
- In the Team Project, choosing "awareness" actions with no way to measure success.
- In the Reflective Paper, describing what happened instead of reflecting on what you learned and how you could improve.
