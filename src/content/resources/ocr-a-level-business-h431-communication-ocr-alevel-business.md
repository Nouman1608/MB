## Formal and informal communication

**Formal communication** follows the official channels of the business, usually up, down or across the structure, and is often recorded. Examples are a written report to a manager, minutes of a meeting, a company newsletter, an appraisal interview and a contract.

**Informal communication** happens outside official channels, through conversation, social messaging and the "grapevine". Examples are a chat in the canteen or a message between colleagues.

| | Formal | Informal |
|---|---|---|
| Channel | Official, through the chain of command | Personal and social |
| Record | Usually kept | Often none |
| Speed | Can be slow | Fast |
| Accuracy | High if checked | Can distort; rumours |
| Use | Policies, instructions, legal matters | Building relationships, quick queries, early warning |

Informal communication can build trust and spot problems early, but spreads rumours, especially during redundancies or takeovers. A business needs both: formal channels for the essential messages and a culture that allows open informal exchange.

## Groups, norms and objectives

A **group** is two or more people who interact and share some purpose. Groups may be formal (a department or project team created by management) or informal (friends who eat lunch together).

**Group norms** are the unwritten rules and expectations about how members behave, such as how hard to work, how to treat customers, or whether it is acceptable to arrive late. They are enforced by approval and pressure from other members.

**Group objectives** are the aims of the group, which may differ from the **individual objectives** of its members. An individual may want promotion or higher pay; the group may want to protect jobs or maintain a comfortable work pace.

Effects on communication:

- A group with positive norms, such as sharing information openly, communicates well and solves problems quickly.
- Strong informal groups may filter or block messages from management, for example by ignoring a new target they dislike.
- A group's norms may not fit the aims of the business; a norm of "don't work faster than the slowest" slows output.
- Group objectives that conflict with individual objectives can cause tension, and individuals may withhold information.
- Managers can use groups, for instance team briefings, to send messages more effectively than one-to-one emails, if they win the group's trust.

## Verbal and non-verbal communication

**Verbal communication** uses words, spoken or written. **Non-verbal communication** does not use words: body language, facial expression, tone of voice, gestures, dress, silence and visual signs such as charts and logos.

Most face-to-face messages combine both. If words and body language conflict, receivers usually trust the non-verbal signal. A manager who says "my door is always open" but looks at a screen while someone speaks sends a mixed message.

Factors affecting the choice of method:

- **Urgency.** Speech or a call for urgent matters.
- **Complexity.** Written documents and diagrams for detailed instructions.
- **Need for a record.** Written methods for legal or safety matters.
- **Need for feedback.** Face-to-face for sensitive issues where reaction matters.
- **Cost.** Time spent in meetings against the cost of electronic messages.
- **Number of people and their location.** Electronic methods for large or dispersed groups.
- **Confidentiality.** A private conversation rather than a noticeboard.
- **Skills and preferences of the receiver.** Language, literacy and access to technology.

## Communication media

| Medium | Examples | Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|---|---|
| Written | Letters, reports, memos, notices | Permanent record; detailed | Slow; no immediate feedback; can be unread |
| Face-to-face | Meetings, interviews, briefings | Immediate feedback; body language; builds relations | Time-consuming; no record unless minuted; hard for dispersed staff |
| Oral | Phone calls, voice messages, announcements | Quick; tone of voice | No record; misheard; no body language on phone |
| Social | Social networks, messaging apps | Fast, informal, reaches customers | Hard to control; privacy and reputation risk |
| Electronic | Email, intranet, video calls, text | Fast, cheap per message, stored | Overload; misread tone; needs technology |
| Mass | TV, radio, press, online advertising | Reaches large audiences | Costly; little feedback; not targeted |

Factors affecting the choice of medium are the same as for methods, with the addition of the audience (staff, customers, the public), the image the business wants to project and the cost.

### Worked example 1: choosing between an email and team briefings

Kestrel Distribution (invented) must tell 250 staff about a change to the rota. Staff time is valued at £16 an hour and managers' time at £28 an hour.

**Option A: email.** Each person takes 6 minutes (0.1 hour) to read it. Staff time = 250 × 0.1 = 25 hours × £16 = **£400**. Past experience suggests 8% of staff misread a rota email, and each misunderstanding costs £90 in cover and lost output. Cost of errors = 8% × 250 = 20 people × £90 = **£1,800**. Total = £400 + £1,800 = **£2,200**.

**Option B: ten team briefings of 25 staff, each 30 minutes.** Staff time = 10 × 25 × 0.5 = 125 hours × £16 = **£2,000**. Manager time = 10 × 0.5 × £28 = **£140**. With feedback in the room, the misunderstanding rate is 2%: 5 people × £90 = **£450**. Total = £2,000 + £140 + £450 = **£2,590**.

Email is cheaper by £2,590 − £2,200 = **£390**.

Evaluation:

- Email is the cheaper option on these figures, but the saving is small and relies on the 8% misreading rate. If the rate were 14%, the email option would cost 35 × £90 = £3,150 in errors, and the total of £3,550 would exceed the briefing cost.
- Briefings allow questions, show that managers take the change seriously and may reduce resistance. Email gives a written record that staff can check.
- A hybrid is possible: send an email with the rota, then hold short briefings only for staff whose shifts change.
- The choice depends on the importance of the change. For a major change to jobs or pay, face-to-face communication has much greater value than the cost of time.

## The communication model

A common model describes communication as a process with six elements.

1. **Source (sender).** The person or business that starts the message.
2. **Message.** The information, ideas or instruction, put into words, images or signals (encoding).
3. **Media.** The channel used to send it: speech, email, notice, video.
4. **Barriers.** Anything that blocks or distorts the message, sometimes called noise.
5. **Receiver.** The person who receives and interprets (decodes) the message.
6. **Feedback.** The receiver's response, which tells the source whether the message was understood.

Barriers include: jargon or language differences; poor choice of medium; information overload; physical noise or distance; technology failure; differences in status that make receivers afraid to ask; lack of trust; different assumptions or culture; and a message that is too long or unclear.

**Usefulness of the model.** It helps managers to diagnose where communication fails (was the message unclear, the medium wrong or was there no feedback?). It shows why feedback matters. However, it simplifies, because real communication is often two-way and continuous, and a model cannot show the emotional side or the effect of group norms.

## Impact of poor communication

- Mistakes, rework and wasted time.
- Lower motivation and trust; staff feel uninformed.
- Conflict between departments.
- Poor service and lost customers.
- Slow decisions and missed opportunities.
- Higher absence and labour turnover.
- Damage to reputation.

### Worked example 2: the cost of unclear instructions

Kestrel Distribution handles 5,000 orders a month. Because instructions between sales and the warehouse are unclear, 4% of orders are wrong. Each wrong order costs £35 to put right.

Wrong orders = 4% × 5,000 = 200 a month. Cost = 200 × £35 = **£7,000** a month, or £84,000 a year.

A shared order system and a checklist cost £30,000 and are expected to cut the error rate to 1.5%.

New wrong orders = 1.5% × 5,000 = 75. Cost = 75 × £35 = £2,625 a month. Saving = £7,000 − £2,625 = **£4,375** a month = £52,500 a year.

Payback = £30,000 ÷ £52,500 = 0.57 years, about **6.9 months** (£30,000 ÷ £4,375).

The figures do not include lost customers and damaged reputation, so the real benefit is probably higher. They do depend on the assumption that the system really changes how staff communicate.

**Stakeholders.** Customers receive the right goods; warehouse staff face less blame and frustration; owners save cost; managers spend less time on complaints.

## Evaluating effectiveness

Communication is effective if the message is received, understood and acted on as intended, at an acceptable cost. A strong evaluation:

1. names the objective of the communication;
2. judges the medium against urgency, complexity, cost and audience;
3. considers barriers and feedback;
4. weighs the effect on stakeholders (employees, customers, suppliers);
5. reaches a judgement about the best approach.

## Common mistakes

- Treating formal as "good" and informal as "bad". Both are needed.
- Confusing verbal with oral. Verbal includes written words.
- Listing media without choosing one for the situation.
- Forgetting feedback, which completes the process.
- Forgetting to say how a group's norms affect messages.
- Assuming electronic communication is always best.

## Exam technique

For "distinguish" questions give the difference in one sentence. For "evaluate a medium", compare at least two with the situation in the case (size, urgency, complexity, cost) and make a recommendation. Use figures where they are given.
