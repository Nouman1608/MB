## Section A

**1.** Place these utterances in order of likely development and name the stage of each: (a) "Where is the cat going?", (b) "bababa", (c) "cat go", (d) "juice", (e) "cat go outside garden". **[5]**

**2.** Identify the phonological process in each: (a) "tummy" pronounced "tutty", (b) "nana" for "banana", (c) "boon" for "spoon". **[3]**

**3.** Name the Halliday function that best fits each utterance: (a) "Let's be pirates!", (b) "What's that for?", (c) "Daddy, look, a bus!", (d) "No touch!" **[4]**

**4.** Explain what the "wug" test showed about how children acquire grammar, and which theory it supports. **[3]**

**5.** Explain the difference between an expansion and a recast in child-directed speech, giving an example of each. **[4]**

## Section B

**Transcript 1** (original, invented). Leo is about 2 years 3 months. He and his mother are looking at a picture book.

```
MUM:  look (.) who's that ↗
LEO:  /dɒdi/ (.) doggy
MUM:  yes (.) it's a doggy (.) what's the doggy doing
LEO:  doggy eat (2) doggy eat bone
MUM:  he's eating a bone (.) is it a big bone
LEO:  big (.) more (.) more book
MUM:  more book (.) okay (.) turn the page then
```

**6.** Using Transcript 1, analyse **three** features of Leo's language that indicate his stage of development. **[6]**

**7.** Using Transcript 1, analyse how the mother's language supports Leo's development, linking to **one** theory. **[6]**

**Transcript 2** (original, invented). Maya is about 6 years old, telling her older brother about a school trip.

```
MAYA:   and then we goed to the (.) the big room with all the bones
SAM:    the museum
MAYA:   yeah the museum (.) and there was a dinosaur and it was SO big (.) bigger than
        our house
SAM:    no way
MAYA:   yes way (.) and Mr Okafor said if we touched it it might fall down (.) so nobody
        touched it (.) except Jamie
```

**8.** Analyse **three** features of Maya's language that show development beyond the telegraphic stage. **[6]**

**9.** Analyse how Maya uses narrative and pragmatic skills to engage her listener. **[6]**

**10.** Using both transcripts, evaluate how far the evidence supports Chomsky's idea of an innate language acquisition device. **[8]**

**11.** Explain how transcript conventions can provide evidence for analysis, using **two** examples from the transcripts above. **[4]**

**12.** "Children learn language because adults teach it to them." Discuss this view, referring to **two** theories. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** Order: (b), (d), (c), (e), (a) [1]. (b) "bababa" is **babbling** [1]; (d) "juice" is **holophrastic** [1]; (c) "cat go" is **two-word** and (e) "cat go outside garden" is **telegraphic** [1]; (a) is **post-telegraphic**, with an auxiliary and inversion [1].

**2.** (a) **Assimilation**: the /m/ changes to match the earlier /t/ [1]. (b) **Unstressed syllable deletion** [1]. (c) **Consonant cluster reduction** [1].

**3.** (a) **Imaginative** [1]. (b) **Heuristic** [1]. (c) **Representational** (also interactional, drawing attention) [1]. (d) **Regulatory** [1].

**4.** Jean Berko showed children an invented creature called a "wug", then two of them, and children supplied "wugs" [1]. Because they could not have heard the word before, they were **applying a plural rule** rather than imitating [1], which supports **nativist theory / Chomsky's LAD** [1].

**5.** An **expansion** adds information to the child's utterance [1]: child "car!" / adult "yes, a fast red car" [1]. A **recast** returns the child's utterance in its correct adult form without explicit correction [1]: child "he runned" / adult "he ran, didn't he?" [1].

**6.** Any three, two marks each. **"/dɒdi/" for "doggy"**: substitution of /d/ for /g/, a common simplification, although he then produces "doggy", showing the target is emerging [1] [1]. **"doggy eat bone"**: a three-word telegraphic utterance with no auxiliary, "-ing" inflection or article [1] [1]. **"more book"**: an instrumental two-word utterance; "more" shows he understands recurrence [1] [1]. Alternatively, "big" repeated from the mother's question shows imitation of a single salient word.

**7.** The mother uses **"wh-" and yes/no questions** ("who's that", "is it a big bone") to hand Leo the turn and prompt vocabulary [1] [1]. She **expands and recasts** his telegraphic "doggy eat bone" into "he's eating a bone", modelling the auxiliary and "-ing" form [1] [1]. These features fit **Bruner's LASS**, as book reading is a scaffolded routine in which the adult structures the interaction [1]; a valid evaluative point is that Leo does not repeat the recast, so its effect is not shown in this short extract [1].

**8.** Any three, two marks each. **Subordination**: "if we touched it it might fall down" uses a conditional clause [1] [1]. **Comparative structure**: "bigger than our house" shows control of comparative morphology and syntax [1] [1]. **Reported speech**: "Mr Okafor said if..." embeds another speaker's words in her narrative [1] [1]. Alternatively, "goed" is an overgeneralisation, but it still shows the regular past-tense rule in use.

**9.** Narrative: **sequencing with "and then"** and **"so"** shows cause and order [1] [1]; she builds to a **climax with a delayed twist**, "except Jamie", placed at the end for effect [1]. Pragmatics: **emphatic stress "SO big"** and **hyperbole** engage the listener [1]; she **accepts her brother's repair** "the museum", showing conversational cooperation [1]; she **responds playfully to "no way" with "yes way"**, sustaining the interaction [1].

**10.** Support: Maya's **"goed"** is an overgeneralisation not heard from adults, suggesting rule application [1] [1]; Leo's **consistent word order** ("doggy eat bone") suggests underlying grammatical structure even with limited input [1]. Against or qualifying: Leo's mother **models and scaffolds** language constantly, so interaction may explain much of his progress [1] [1]; Maya's **vocabulary and reported speech** reflect school and social experience, not an innate device [1]. Judgement: the transcripts offer **some evidence of rule-based learning**, but **social input is clearly present**, so they best support a combined view [1] [1].

**11.** Conventions are evidence, not decoration [1]. **Phonemic transcription /dɒdi/** shows Leo's actual pronunciation, revealing a substitution that ordinary spelling would hide [1]. **Capitals in "SO"** mark stress, showing Maya's emphasis as a deliberate narrative choice [1]. Pauses such as **"(2)"** before "doggy eat bone" may suggest planning time before a longer utterance [1].

**12.** For: **imitation and reinforcement theory** explains vocabulary, accent and routines learned from adults [1] [1]; **Bruner's LASS** shows caregivers scaffolding through routines and child-directed speech [1] [1]. Against: **overgeneralisations** such as "goed" are not taught by adults [1]; **Chomsky's LAD** proposes an innate capacity for grammar, explaining speed of acquisition and rules children infer [1] [1]; children **resist explicit correction** and acquire language where child-directed speech is limited [1]. Conclusion: adults **provide input and interaction** but do not directly teach grammar; acquisition involves both social support and the child's own rule-forming [1] [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Naming a stage without quoting the evidence for it.
- Labelling "goed" an error without explaining the rule it shows.
- Ignoring adult or older-child speakers in the transcript.
- Presenting a theory without evaluating it against the data.
- Treating approximate ages as fixed milestones.
