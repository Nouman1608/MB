This guide covers **3.2.3 Research methods 1** of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Psychology (9685) specification, Version 3.1. It is Section C of the Unit 2 exam: 30 marks of compulsory questions within a 1 hour 30 minute, 90-mark paper. The specification says research methods, practical research skills and mathematical skills "will be assessed in Unit 2", and lists three groups of content: **methods** (experiments, observation, self-report and correlations), **scientific processes** (aims, hypotheses, sampling, piloting, designs, variables, demand characteristics, behavioural categories, question types and ethics) and **data handling and analysis** (types of data, descriptive statistics, presentation and distributions). Research methods 2 at A-level builds on all of it.

The [Unit 2 study guide](/resources/a-level-oxfordaqa-psychology-biopsychology-development-research-methods/) lists the content briefly. This guide explains each idea with an example and a strength or limitation, then works through the design and analysis of one study from start to finish. Condense it with the [research methods 1 revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-psychology-9685-research-methods-1-revision-notes/) and test yourself with the [research methods 1 practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-psychology-9685-research-methods-1-practice/).

## Methods

**Experiments** manipulate an independent variable (IV) and measure its effect on a dependent variable (DV).

| Type | Description | Strength | Limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Laboratory** | Controlled setting; researcher manipulates the IV | High control, so cause and effect can be inferred; easy to replicate | Artificial; may lack ecological validity; demand characteristics |
| **Field** | Natural setting; researcher still manipulates the IV | More natural behaviour | Less control of extraneous variables; consent may be difficult |
| **Natural** | The IV varies naturally (for example, a new school policy); researcher measures the DV | Allows study of variables that could not ethically be manipulated | No random allocation, so groups may differ in other ways |

**Observation techniques** record behaviour as it happens.

- **Naturalistic** observation takes place in the normal setting; **controlled** observation sets up the situation, as in a laboratory playroom.
- **Covert** observers are hidden, reducing participant reactivity but raising consent issues; **overt** observers are known to participants.
- **Participant** observers join the group being studied, gaining insight but risking loss of objectivity; **non-participant** observers stay apart.

**Self-report techniques** ask people directly.

- **Questionnaires** collect data from many people quickly, but answers may be affected by **social desirability bias**.
- **Interviews** are face to face. **Structured** interviews use fixed questions in a fixed order, so answers are easy to compare; **unstructured** interviews follow the conversation, giving richer data but making comparison harder.

**Correlations** analyse the relationship between two **co-variables** measured, not manipulated. A **positive** correlation means both increase together; a **negative** correlation means one increases as the other decreases; a **zero** correlation means no relationship. Correlations cannot show cause and effect, because a third variable may explain both.

## Scientific processes

**Aims and hypotheses.** An aim is a general statement of what the study investigates. A hypothesis is a precise, testable prediction. A **directional** hypothesis states which way the difference or relationship will go; a **non-directional** hypothesis predicts a difference or relationship without stating its direction. Researchers choose a directional hypothesis when previous research suggests the likely outcome.

**Sampling.** The **population** is the group the researcher wants to study; the **sample** is the people who actually take part.

- **Random sampling:** every member of the population has an equal chance of selection, for example by drawing names from a list. It avoids researcher bias but needs a full list of the population, and the sample may still be unrepresentative by chance.
- **Opportunity sampling:** using whoever is available at the time. It is quick and easy, but likely to be biased towards particular types of people, so findings may not **generalise** to the population.

**Pilot studies** are small trial runs that check procedures, instructions and timings before the main study, saving time and money if problems are found.

**Experimental designs.**

- **Independent groups:** different participants in each condition. No order effects, but **participant variables** may differ between groups. **Random allocation** to conditions reduces this risk.
- **Repeated measures:** the same participants do every condition. Participant variables are controlled, but **order effects** (practice, fatigue) may occur. **Counterbalancing** -- for example, half do A then B, half do B then A -- spreads order effects across conditions.
- **Matched pairs:** participants are matched on a relevant variable, such as IQ, and one of each pair goes into each condition. This reduces participant variables without order effects, but matching is time-consuming and never perfect.

**Variables.** The IV is manipulated; the DV is measured. **Extraneous variables** are any other variables that might affect the DV and must be **controlled**, for example through standardised instructions and the same room and time of day. **Operationalisation** means defining variables in measurable terms: "memory" becomes "the number of words correctly recalled from a list of 20 within two minutes".

**Demand characteristics** are cues that let participants guess the aim, so they may change their behaviour to help or spoil the study. Keeping the aim from participants until the debrief, and using an independent groups design so each person sees only one condition, reduces them.

**Behavioural categories** turn a target behaviour into observable, measurable units. "Aggression" might become hitting, pushing and shouting. Categories should be clearly defined, observable and not overlap.

**Questionnaire questions.** **Closed questions** have fixed answers (yes/no, rating scales), producing quantitative data that are easy to analyse. **Open questions** let people answer freely, producing qualitative data that are richer but harder to compare.

## Ethics

The specification names seven issues. Know what each means and how researchers deal with it:

| Issue | Meaning | How it is dealt with |
|---|---|---|
| Consent | Participants agree, knowing what the study involves | Consent form; parental consent for children |
| Deception | Misleading participants about the aim | Only when necessary; full debrief |
| Debrief | Explaining the true aim at the end | Reassure participants; offer to withdraw data |
| Right to withdraw | Leaving at any time, without penalty | Stated clearly at the start |
| Protection from harm | No greater risk than in everyday life | Stop if distress occurs; offer support |
| Confidentiality | Personal data kept secure and not identifiable | Use numbers instead of names |
| Privacy | Not observing people where they expect privacy | Observe only in public places |

## Data handling and analysis

- **Quantitative** data are numerical; **qualitative** data are descriptive, such as interview quotations. Quantitative data are easy to analyse but may lack depth; qualitative data give depth but are harder to compare.
- **Primary** data are collected by the researcher; **secondary** data were collected by others. A **meta-analysis** combines the results of many studies on the same topic, increasing sample size but depending on the quality of the studies included.
- **Mean** (total divided by number of scores) uses all the data but is distorted by extreme scores. **Median** (middle value when ordered) is unaffected by extreme scores. **Mode** (most frequent value) suits categorical data but may not be unique.
- **Range** (highest minus lowest) is quick but distorted by extreme scores. **Standard deviation** measures the average spread around the mean: a larger value means scores are more spread out.
- **Graphs:** tables summarise data; **bar charts** compare categories, with gaps between bars; **line graphs** show change across a continuous variable; **scattergrams** show correlations.
- **Normal distribution:** symmetrical, bell-shaped, with mean, median and mode equal. **Positive skew:** a tail of high scores pulls the mean above the median. **Negative skew:** a tail of low scores pulls the mean below the median.

## Worked example: designing and analysing a study

*Aim:* to investigate whether background music affects recall of a word list.

- **Directional hypothesis:** participants who learn a list of 20 words in silence will recall more words than participants who learn them with background music.
- **IV:** music or silence. **DV:** number of words correctly recalled in two minutes (operationalised).
- **Design:** independent groups, with random allocation, so no participant learns the list twice.
- **Controls:** same list, same volume of music, same room and standardised instructions.
- **Ethics:** informed consent, right to withdraw and a debrief.

Invented results:

| | Scores | Mean | Median | Mode | Range |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Music | 7, 8, 9, 9, 10, 11, 12, 14 | 80 / 8 = 10 | (9 + 10) / 2 = 9.5 | 9 | 14 - 7 = 7 |
| Silence | 11, 12, 13, 13, 14, 15, 16, 18 | 112 / 8 = 14 | (13 + 14) / 2 = 13.5 | 13 | 18 - 11 = 7 |

The silence group's mean is 4 words higher, a difference of 40% relative to the music group (4 / 10 x 100), with identical ranges, so the spread of scores was similar. The results are consistent with the hypothesis, although only an inferential test, met at A-level, could show whether the difference is significant. A bar chart of the two means would present this clearly.

## Common mistakes

- Calling a correlation an experiment.
- Writing hypotheses without operationalised variables.
- Naming a sampling technique without an implication for bias or generalisation.
- Confusing random sampling with random allocation.
- Drawing a histogram-style bar chart with no gaps for categorical data.
- Saying a positive skew has its tail on the left.
