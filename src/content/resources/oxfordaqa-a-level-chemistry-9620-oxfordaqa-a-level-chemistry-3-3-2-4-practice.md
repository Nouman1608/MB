These questions cover **3.3.2.4 Chlorination of alkanes** of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Chemistry (9620) specification, Version 5.3. It is International AS content, assessed in Unit 2 and able to reappear in Unit 5. Section A has short recall and application questions; Section B has longer, structured questions. If a point is unfamiliar, read the [chlorination of alkanes study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-3-2-4/) or the [chlorination of alkanes revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-3-2-4-revision-notes/) first. Take Ar values as H = 1.0, C = 12.0 and Cl = 35.5.

## Questions

### Section A

**1.** Which equation is a termination step?

A: Cl₂ → 2Cl•

B: •CH₃ + Cl₂ → CH₃Cl + Cl•

C: CH₃Cl + Cl• → •CH₂Cl + HCl

D: •CH₂Cl + Cl• → CH₂Cl₂ **[1]**

**2.** State the role of ultraviolet light in the reaction of methane with chlorine. **[1]**

**3.** Write the equation for the initiation step. **[1]**

**4.** Explain why the propagation steps make this a chain reaction. **[2]**

**5.** Give the structural formula of the organic product formed when •CH₃ and •CH₂Cl combine. **[1]**

**6.** State what is meant by a radical. **[1]**

### Section B

**7.** When excess chlorine is used, tetrachloromethane is the main product.

**(a)** Write the two propagation steps that convert trichloromethane into tetrachloromethane. **[2]**

**(b)** Two •CCl₃ radicals combine. Write the equation and give the molecular formula of the product. **[1]**

**(c)** Write the overall equation for the formation of tetrachloromethane from methane. **[1]**

**(d)** State how the proportions of the reactants would be changed to make mainly chloromethane instead. **[1]**

**8.** 8.0 g of methane reacts with excess chlorine in ultraviolet light to form tetrachloromethane.

**(a)** Calculate the maximum mass of tetrachloromethane. **[2]**

**(b)** 46.2 g of tetrachloromethane is collected. Calculate the percentage yield. **[1]**

**(c)** Calculate the percentage atom economy for making tetrachloromethane by CH₄ + 4Cl₂ → CCl₄ + 4HCl. **[1]**

**(d)** Suggest one reason why the percentage yield is less than 100 %. **[1]**

**9.** The Cl–Cl bond enthalpy is about 243 kJ mol⁻¹; the mean C–H bond enthalpy is about 413 kJ mol⁻¹. Use these values to explain which bond breaks in the initiation step, and state how it breaks. **[2]**

**10.** A sample made by chlorinating methane contains a little ethane and a little chloroethane. Explain, with equations, how each forms, and why their presence supports the free-radical mechanism. **[3]**

**11.** The chlorinated products of methane have these approximate boiling points.

| Compound | CH₃Cl | CH₂Cl₂ | CHCl₃ | CCl₄ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Boiling point / °C | −24 | 40 | 61 | 77 |

**(a)** Explain why the mixture can be separated by fractional distillation. **[1]**

**(b)** State which compound would distil off first. **[1]**

**(c)** Explain why CCl₄ has the highest boiling point although its molecules are non-polar. **[2]**

**12.** Explain how methane reacts with chlorine in ultraviolet light to form chloromethane, and why the product is usually a mixture. Include equations for the steps. **[6]**

## Answers

### Section A

**1.** D [1]

**2.** It provides the energy to break the Cl–Cl bond homolytically, forming chlorine radicals (initiation) [1]

**3.** Cl₂ → 2Cl• [1]

**4.** Each propagation step uses up one radical and produces another [1]; the Cl• produced in the second step reacts with another CH₄ molecule, so the cycle repeats many times [1]

**Total: 2**

**5.** CH₃CH₂Cl [1]

**6.** A species with an unpaired electron [1]

### Section B

**7.**

**(a)** CHCl₃ + Cl• → •CCl₃ + HCl [1]; •CCl₃ + Cl₂ → CCl₄ + Cl• [1]

**(b)** 2•CCl₃ → C₂Cl₆ (hexachloroethane, Cl₃C–CCl₃) [1]

**(c)** CH₄ + 4Cl₂ → CCl₄ + 4HCl [1]

**(d)** Use excess methane (a high ratio of methane to chlorine) [1]

**Total: 5**

**8.**

**(a)** n(CH₄) = 8.0 ÷ 16.0 = 0.50 mol [1]; mass of CCl₄ = 0.50 × 154.0 = 77 g [1]

**(b)** 46.2 ÷ 77.0 × 100 = 60.0 % [1]

**(c)** 154.0 ÷ (16.0 + 4 × 71.0) × 100 = 154.0 ÷ 300.0 × 100 = 51.3 % [1]

**(d)** Any one: other chlorinated products (CH₃Cl, CH₂Cl₂, CHCl₃) also form; termination products form; product lost during separation [1]

**Total: 5**

*Examiner insight:* Percentage yield and atom economy measure different things. Yield compares actual and theoretical product; atom economy compares the desired product with all products, and HCl makes it low here.

**9.** The Cl–Cl bond is the weaker bond, so UV light provides enough energy to break it and not the C–H bond [1]; it breaks homolytically, with one electron going to each chlorine atom [1]

**Total: 2**

**10.** Ethane: two methyl radicals combine in termination, 2•CH₃ → C₂H₆ [1]; chloroethane: a methyl radical and a chloromethyl radical combine, •CH₃ + •CH₂Cl → CH₃CH₂Cl [1]; both contain a C–C bond that can only form when two carbon radicals meet, so their presence shows carbon radicals were present [1]

**Total: 3**

**11.**

**(a)** The compounds have different boiling points [1]

**(b)** CH₃Cl (chloromethane) [1]

**(c)** CCl₄ has the most electrons (and the largest molecules) [1]; so its van der Waals forces are the strongest, outweighing the permanent dipole–dipole forces in the polar molecules [1]

**Total: 4**

**12.** Indicative content; award one mark per valid point, up to 6 [6]

- Mechanism named as free-radical substitution: a Cl atom replaces an H atom.
- Initiation: UV light breaks Cl–Cl homolytically, Cl₂ → 2Cl•.
- Propagation: CH₄ + Cl• → •CH₃ + HCl.
- Propagation: •CH₃ + Cl₂ → CH₃Cl + Cl•; the Cl• regenerated continues the chain.
- Termination: two radicals combine, for example Cl• + •CH₃ → CH₃Cl or 2•CH₃ → C₂H₆.
- Mixture: Cl• can attack C–H bonds in CH₃Cl and later products, giving CH₂Cl₂, CHCl₃ and CCl₄; termination between carbon radicals gives two-carbon products.
- Using excess methane gives mainly chloromethane.

**Total: 6**

*Examiner insight:* Curly arrows on these steps are not required; equations with correctly placed dots score the marks.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing H• instead of HCl in the first propagation step.
- Dots on the wrong atom, or missing altogether.
- Treating UV light as a catalyst.
- Confusing percentage yield with atom economy.
- Explaining boiling points of the chloromethanes by polarity alone.
