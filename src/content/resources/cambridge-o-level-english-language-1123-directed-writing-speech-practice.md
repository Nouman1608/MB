## Section A

**1.** Write a suitable opening sentence for a speech (a) to your year group at assembly and (b) to residents at a town meeting. **[2]**

**2.** Rewrite this sentence so that it sounds spoken and suits an audience of classmates: "It is evident that the provision of additional recreational facilities would be beneficial." **[2]**

**3.** Identify two features that make this sentence suitable for a speech: "So I ask you, all of you: are we really going to let this happen?" **[2]**

**4.** Write a signposting sentence that introduces a counter-argument. **[1]**

**5.** Explain why this closing line is unsuitable for a speech: "I look forward to hearing from you. Yours faithfully." **[2]**

**6.** Write a three-part recap for a speech arguing that the school should plant more trees. **[1]**

## Section B

Read the two texts (original, written for this practice set).

> **Text 1: from a statement by the town council**
>
> The town's swimming pool is over forty years old. Repairs to the roof and heating last year cost
> more than the pool earns in a year, and visitor numbers have fallen. The council proposes to close
> the pool next summer and use the savings to improve parks and footpaths, which are used by far more
> residents. Swimmers would be able to use the new leisure centre in the neighbouring town, twenty
> minutes away by bus.
>
> **Text 2: from a letter by a swimming club coach**
>
> Our club teaches three hundred children to swim every year. Many of their families do not have cars,
> and a bus journey of twenty minutes each way, with an hour's lesson, would take most of an evening.
> Visitor numbers fell because the pool was closed for repairs for four months. Swimming is not a
> luxury: it is a skill that saves lives. Once the pool is gone, it will never come back.

**7.** List two points from the texts you would use, one you would develop and one you would evaluate in a speech opposing the closure. **[4]**

**8.** Write the opening (about 50 words) of a speech to residents at a town meeting, opposing the closure. **[3]**

**9.** Write a body section (about 70 words) that evaluates the council's claim that visitor numbers have fallen. **[3]**

**10.** Write the counter-argument section (about 60 words) of the same speech. **[3]**

**11.** The task changes: *you are a member of the youth council and must give a speech that considers both sides of the proposal before recommending a decision.* Explain two ways your speech would differ from the persuasive version. **[2]**

**12.** Write a speech to be given at a town meeting, persuading residents to oppose or support the closure of the pool. Use and evaluate ideas from both texts. Write about 250–350 words. **[25]**

## Answers

**1.** For example: (a) "Good morning, everyone." **[1]** (b) "Good evening, and thank you all for coming to tonight's meeting." **[1]**

**2.** For example: "More places to relax and play would make this school a better place for all of us." **[2]** One mark for natural spoken wording, one for keeping the meaning.

**3.** Direct address ("I ask you, all of you") **[1]**; a rhetorical question ("are we really going to let this happen?") **[1]**. Also creditable: inclusive "we"; repetition.

**4.** For example: "Now, some of you will say that the money would be better spent elsewhere, and that deserves an answer." **[1]**

**5.** It uses letter conventions **[1]**: a speaker does not sign off "Yours faithfully" or wait for a written reply, so the register and form are wrong for an audience listening at a meeting **[1]**.

**6.** For example: "Cleaner air, cooler playgrounds and a greener future." **[1]**

**7.** One mark each:
- Use: the club teaches three hundred children to swim every year (Text 2) **[1]**.
- Use: the roof and heating repairs were costly (Text 1) **[1]**.
- Develop: the bus journey to the neighbouring town would take most of an evening for families without cars, so children from poorer families are most likely to stop swimming **[1]**.
- Evaluate: the council's claim that visitor numbers have fallen ignores the four-month closure for repairs, which Text 2 says caused the fall **[1]**.

**8.** Model:

> Good evening, everyone. Every year, three hundred children in this town learn to swim in a pool that
> the council now wants to close. Tonight I want to persuade you that this is not a saving at all. It
> is a cost, and our children will be the ones who pay it.

Marking: greeting and register suited to residents **[1]**; a hook drawn from the sources **[1]**; a clear position **[1]**.

**9.** Model:

> The council tells us that fewer people are using the pool. That sounds convincing, until you
> remember why. For four months last year, the pool was closed for repairs. Of course numbers fell:
> you cannot swim in a pool that is shut. Before we close a pool because nobody uses it, let us at
> least count the visitors in a year when it is open.

Marking: identifies the council's evidence **[1]**; evaluates it using Text 2 **[1]**; spoken register sustained **[1]**.

**10.** Model:

> I understand the council's position. Repairs are expensive, and our parks and footpaths do need
> money. But a footpath cannot teach a child to swim. If the pool closes, the bus fare, the time and
> the cost will fall on the families who can least afford them.

Marking: presents the other side fairly **[1]**; answers it with a reason **[1]**; links to the audience's interests **[1]**.

**11.** For example: the discursive speech would give balanced space to both sides, setting out the benefits for parks and footpaths as fully as the case for the pool **[1]**; its tone would be calmer and more even-handed, ending with a reasoned recommendation rather than a passionate call to action **[1]**.

**12.** Marked out of 25: reading (R3, R5) and writing (W1–W5).
- **Reading:** uses points from both texts; develops them (travel time and cost for families without cars, the effect on children from poorer families); evaluates them (the four-month closure behind the fall in numbers, the council's comparison of users, the coach's interest as a club member, the question of whether repairs could be funded another way) **[10]**.
- **Writing:** speech conventions throughout (greeting, signposts, direct address, controlled rhetorical devices, a call to action); a clear persuasive structure with a counter-argument; register suited to residents at a meeting; varied vocabulary and sentence structures; accurate spelling, punctuation and grammar **[15]**.

Either side can score fully if it is argued from the sources. Lower-level responses retell the texts in order, use only one text, or read as an essay or letter rather than a speech.

## Where marks are usually lost

- **Question 7:** four "use" points and no development or evaluation.
- **Question 9:** dismissing the council ("they are lying") instead of weighing its evidence.
- **Question 12:** writing to "fellow students" when the audience is residents at a town meeting.
- **Question 12:** exceeding 350 words by retelling both texts before starting the argument.
