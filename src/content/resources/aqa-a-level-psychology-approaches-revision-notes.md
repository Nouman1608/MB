## Six approaches at a glance

| Approach | Core idea | Key figure(s) | Free will or determinism |
|---|---|---|---|
| Behaviourist | All behaviour is learned from the environment | Pavlov, Skinner | Determinist |
| Social learning theory | Behaviour learned by observing and imitating others | Bandura | Soft determinist |
| Cognitive | Behaviour explained by internal mental processes | — | Soft determinist |
| Biological | Behaviour explained by genes, neurochemistry, evolution | — | Determinist |
| Psychodynamic | Behaviour driven by unconscious conflict | Freud | Determinist |
| Humanistic | Behaviour reflects free will and self-actualisation | Maslow, Rogers | Free will |

Learn the "free will vs determinism" column cold — comparison questions ask for it directly and it is
the fastest mark-scheme point to lose through vague wording.

## Learning approaches (i): behaviourism

Classical conditioning (Pavlov) — learning through **association**: an unconditioned stimulus paired
repeatedly with a neutral stimulus produces a conditioned response to the previously neutral stimulus.
Operant conditioning (Skinner) — learning through **consequences**: positive reinforcement, negative
reinforcement, and punishment shape future behaviour. Only observable behaviour matters; the mind is
a "black box" not worth studying scientifically.

## Learning approaches (ii): social learning theory

Adds cognitive processes behaviourism ignores. Learning happens by **observing** a model, then
**imitating** if the model's behaviour is reinforced (vicarious reinforcement) — no personal
reinforcement is required first, unlike operant conditioning. **Identification** makes imitation more
likely when the model is similar, high-status, or attractive. Four **mediational processes** sit
between observing and imitating: attention, retention, motor reproduction, motivation.

## Cognitive approach

Studies internal mental processes (memory, perception, language, thinking) using the computer as a
model of the mind — input, processing, output, with schemas as mental "shortcuts" built from
experience. Introduced the theoretical and computer models now standard across the discipline, and
gave rise to cognitive neuroscience, which studies the neural basis of cognition directly.

## Biological approach

Behaviour explained through genes (genotype vs phenotype), neurochemistry (neurotransmitters,
hormones), and evolution (natural selection favouring adaptive behaviours). Genetically identical
twins (monozygotic) are compared to non-identical twins (dizygotic) to estimate genetic influence.

## Psychodynamic approach

Freud's structure of personality: **Id** (pleasure principle, present at birth), **Ego** (reality
principle, mediates), **Superego** (morality principle, develops around age 5). Anxiety triggers
**defence mechanisms** — repression (pushing a memory out of conscious awareness entirely), denial
(refusing to accept reality), displacement (redirecting an emotion onto a safer target). Development
proceeds through fixed **psychosexual stages**; unresolved conflict at any stage causes fixation.

## Humanistic psychology

Rejects both determinism and the scientific, lab-based methods of the other approaches. Centres on
**free will** and **self-actualisation** — the innermost layer of Maslow's hierarchy of needs, reached
only once lower needs (physiological, safety, love/belonging, esteem) are met. Rogers added
**congruence** (match between self-image and ideal self) and **conditions of worth** (conditional
acceptance in childhood that can block congruence in adulthood).

## Worked example

*Question style: "A teenager starts smoking after watching an older sibling get praised by friends for
it. Explain this using social learning theory. [4 marks]"*

```
Model:                the older sibling
Identification:       likely, since a sibling is similar/relatable
Vicarious reinforcement: friends' praise reinforces the sibling's
                       behaviour, observed (not personally
                       experienced) by the teenager
Mediational process:  motivation -- the teenager expects similar
                       social reward, making imitation more likely
```

Naming the specific mediational process involved, not just "SLT explains this," is what separates a
4-mark answer from a 1-mark answer on this style of question.

## Comparison of approaches

Every approach can be placed on: (1) free will vs determinism, (2) nature vs nurture, (3) reductionist
vs holistic, (4) scientific/nomothetic vs idiographic. Behaviourism and the biological approach sit at
the determinist, reductionist, nomothetic end; humanistic psychology sits at the opposite, free-will,
holistic, idiographic end; cognitive, SLT, and psychodynamic approaches fall between the two extremes
in different ways. An extended-writing question asking you to "compare two approaches" wants you to
select two or three of these dimensions explicitly rather than describing each approach separately and
leaving the comparison implicit.

## Why this comparison matters for the exam

Paper 2 regularly asks you to apply two or more approaches to the *same* behaviour, rather than
describe them one at a time. A useful habit: for any behaviour in a scenario, ask what the
behaviourist, cognitive, and biological approaches would each point to as the cause, then note where
they would disagree. Behaviourism looks only at the environmental history of reinforcement; cognition
looks at the mental processing between stimulus and response; biology looks at genes or brain
chemistry. Practising this three-way split on ordinary scenarios (a phobia, an addiction, a habit)
builds the flexibility scenario-based questions actually test, rather than memorising each approach
in isolation.

## Exam traps

- Describing behaviourism and SLT as identical — SLT adds cognitive/mediational processes and does
  not require personal reinforcement.
- Confusing the Id/Ego/Superego with the psychosexual stages — they are separate parts of the theory.
- Naming a defence mechanism without describing what it does.
- Forgetting that self-actualisation sits at the top of Maslow's hierarchy and requires lower needs
  to be met first.

## Self-test

1. What is the key difference between classical and operant conditioning?
2. Name the four mediational processes in social learning theory.
3. What are the three parts of personality in the psychodynamic approach, and which develops last?
4. What does "self-actualisation" mean in the humanistic approach?
5. Which two approaches sit at the determinist, reductionist end of the comparison dimensions?

**Answers:** 1. Classical conditioning is learning through association (pairing stimuli); operant
conditioning is learning through consequences (reinforcement and punishment). 2. Attention, retention,
motor reproduction, motivation. 3. Id, Ego, Superego — the Superego develops last, around age 5.
4. Reaching one's full potential, the innermost layer of Maslow's hierarchy, achievable only once
lower needs are met. 5. Behaviourist and biological.
