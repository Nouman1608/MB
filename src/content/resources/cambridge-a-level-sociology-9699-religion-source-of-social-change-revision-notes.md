## The argument in one paragraph

Weber argued that the Calvinist ethic, through salvation anxiety, the calling and asceticism, had an
elective affinity with the spirit of capitalism and helped modern capitalism emerge. Critics argue
that economic change came first (Tawney, Kautsky) or that religion was not sufficient (Marshall).
Liberation theology shows religion opposing an unjust order, and religious movements in the USA and
Iran show religion shaping political struggles, from gradual legal change to revolution. Religion
can drive change, but usually alongside economic and political conditions.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Predestination** | God has already decided who will be saved |
| **The elect** | Those chosen for salvation |
| **Salvation anxiety** | Psychological strain of not knowing whether one is saved |
| **The calling** | Worldly work as a duty to God |
| **Asceticism** | Self-discipline and rejection of luxury |
| **Spirit of capitalism** | Rational, disciplined pursuit of profit for reinvestment |
| **Elective affinity** | A fit between ideas and interests that reinforce each other (Weber) |
| **Verstehen** | Interpretive understanding of actors' meanings |
| **Iron cage** | Rational economic order that traps modern people |
| **Liberation theology** | Catholic movement linking faith to the struggle against poverty and oppression |
| **Preferential option for the poor** | Liberation theology's commitment to put the poor first |
| **Base ecclesial communities** | Small groups of poor Catholics relating the Bible to their conditions |
| **Velayat-e faqih** | Guardianship of the Islamic jurist: clerical political authority (Khomeini) |

## Weber in six steps

1. Predestination.
2. Salvation anxiety.
3. The calling: success as a possible sign of election.
4. Asceticism: profits reinvested, not spent.
5. Spirit of capitalism: Franklin's "time is money".
6. Elective affinity with economic conditions, not sole cause.

**Comparative studies:** China and India had resources but lacked an ethic of rational worldly
activity.

**Iron cage:** capitalism no longer needs its religious roots, linking to secularisation.

## Alternative views

| View | Argument |
|---|---|
| Tawney (1926) | Economic change came first; Protestantism adapted |
| Kautsky (Marxist) | Capitalism predated Calvinism; capitalists were drawn to it |
| Counter-examples | Early capitalism in some Catholic regions; slow development in some Calvinist areas |
| Marshall (1980) | Scotland had the ethic but lacked capital and skilled labour |
| Martin (1990) | Latin American Pentecostalism encourages discipline and saving, a modern parallel |

## Liberation theology

- **Origins:** Vatican II (1962–65); Medellín (1968); base ecclesial communities; Gutiérrez (1971).
- **Significance:** religion opposing injustice; challenges Marx; Löwy (1996) on a movement combining
  Christian and Marxist ideas; Maduro's relative autonomy.
- **Limits:** Vatican criticism from the 1980s; conservative appointments; democratisation; growth of
  Pentecostalism.

## Case studies

**Evangelicals in US politics**
- Moral Majority (1979); Christian Coalition (Pat Robertson, 1989).
- Issues: abortion, school prayer, family values.
- *Roe v. Wade* (1973) overturned by *Dobbs* (2022) after decades of campaigning.
- Bruce (2003): limited success in a pluralist system — *Dobbs* invites re-evaluation.

**Iran**
- White Revolution (1963): rapid modernisation and Westernisation.
- Khomeini's velayat-e faqih.
- Protests 1978; Shah leaves January 1979; Khomeini returns February 1979; referendum creates the
  Islamic Republic in 1979.
- Religion supplied leadership, mosque networks and a language of justice.

**Also usable:** the US civil rights movement and the Southern Christian Leadership Conference
(1957); the Catholic Church and Solidarity in Poland in the 1980s.

## Essay plan in five steps

*"Religion is a conservative force rather than a source of change." Evaluate.*

1. Define both sides: conservative (functionalism, Marxism, 11.2) versus change.
2. Weber: Calvinist ethic and the spirit of capitalism; note elective affinity.
3. Alternatives: Tawney, Kautsky, Marshall weaken the causal claim.
4. Change in action: liberation theology; Iran 1979; US Evangelicals and *Dobbs*.
5. Judge: religion can cause change when economic and political conditions favour it, and is
   conservative when tied to ruling institutions.

## Exam traps

- Weber did not say Calvinism was the only cause.
- Include alternative views for balance.
- Say why liberation theology matters for theory, not just what it was.
- The syllabus says "such as": other case studies are acceptable, but know at least one in depth.
- Get the Iranian dates in order.

## Self-test

1. What is predestination, and why did it cause salvation anxiety?
2. How did the idea of the calling encourage capitalist behaviour?
3. What did Weber mean by elective affinity?
4. What did Weber's studies of China and India suggest?
5. What did Tawney argue?
6. What was Kautsky's criticism of Weber?
7. What did Marshall's study of Scotland find?
8. Name two events that shaped the origins of liberation theology.
9. Why does liberation theology challenge Marxist theory?
10. Give two reasons for the decline of liberation theology.
11. How does the *Dobbs* decision (2022) relate to Bruce's view of the Evangelical movement?
12. What was velayat-e faqih?

### Answers

1. The belief that God has already decided who will be saved; believers could not know if they were
   chosen, which created anxiety.
2. Worldly work became a duty to God and success a possible sign of election, encouraging
   disciplined, methodical work.
3. A fit between Calvinist ideas and the spirit of capitalism that reinforced each other, without
   religion being the sole cause.
4. That they had the economic resources for capitalism but lacked a religious ethic of rational
   worldly activity.
5. That economic change came first and Protestant ideas adapted to justify it.
6. That capitalism predated Calvinism, and capitalists were attracted to Calvinism because it suited
   their interests.
7. A Calvinist work ethic existed, but shortages of capital and skilled labour slowed capitalist
   development.
8. Any two: the Second Vatican Council (1962–65); the Medellín conference (1968); Gutiérrez's book
   (1971); base ecclesial communities.
9. It shows religion opposing an unjust order instead of legitimating it.
10. Any two: Vatican criticism; conservative bishops appointed; democratisation; growth of
    Pentecostalism.
11. Bruce argued the movement had limited policy success; *Dobbs* shows a major long-term legal
    change, so his judgement can be questioned.
12. Khomeini's doctrine that a senior Islamic jurist should hold political authority.
