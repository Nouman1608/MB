This guide covers **Topic 2: External Influences Facing Businesses**,
the second Area of Study in OCR A Level Business (H431). It follows
Topic 1 (Business Objectives and Strategic Decisions) and shifts the
focus from decisions a business makes internally to the market and
wider environmental forces it must respond to but cannot fully
control.

## Syllabus coverage

**OCR A LEVEL BUSINESS H431 -- TOPIC 2 EXTERNAL INFLUENCES FACING BUSINESSES**

- **Markets** -- the market, market size and growth (explaining what a
  market, competition, market size and market growth mean, and
  evaluating how a business could increase market share); the
  features of monopoly, oligopoly and monopolistic competition, and
  the relationship between market structure and a firm's
  decision-making power; demand and supply, including how changes in
  their determinants affect price and output, and constructing and
  interpreting demand-and-supply diagrams to show excesses, shortages
  and equilibrium.
- **Market forces and competition** -- evaluating the impact of market
  forces on a business and its stakeholders, and how a business should
  respond; the distinction between physical and non-physical (online,
  digital) markets and why firms choose to operate in each; how the
  strength of competition affects a business, and why firms enter or
  exit a market; and market dominance -- how mergers, acquisitions and
  organic growth can create dominant firms, and how the UK regulates
  and restricts market dominance.
- **The global context** -- globalisation and the factors that
  facilitate it (the internet, communication technologies,
  e-commerce, trade liberalisation, transport infrastructure and
  multinationals); the role of multinationals and global branding;
  international trade and free trade, including barriers to trade,
  exchange rates and currency conversion, trading blocs, and the
  factors a business must consider when trading internationally
  (language, culture, logistics, currency); the influence of the
  European Union, including the advantages and disadvantages to a UK
  business of EU or Eurozone membership; and emerging markets and
  changes in the global environment.
- **PESTLE-style factors** -- political factors (political stability
  and uncertainty, and the effect of government policy at local,
  national, EU and international level); economic factors (key
  economic indicators, taxation, government expenditure, subsidies,
  monetary/fiscal/supply-side policy, GDP, and the business cycle);
  social factors; technological factors and the digital revolution
  (the information age, and the opportunities and threats of rapid
  technological change); ethical factors (distinguishing law from
  ethics, and the trade-offs of ethical trading); legal factors
  (legislation covering employment, equality, company and partnership
  structure, health and safety, data protection, intellectual
  property, planning, environment, consumer protection, contract and
  financial conduct); and environmental factors.

## Why this topic is examined as a connected whole, not a checklist

It is tempting to treat Topic 2 as a long list of factors to memorise,
but OCR's own assessment objectives weight evaluation and analysis as
heavily as knowledge, so exam questions consistently ask you to judge
which external influence matters *most* in a given business scenario,
and why, rather than simply list every possible factor. A strong
answer on, for example, a small UK manufacturer facing rising import
costs will explain the specific link between exchange rate movements
(global context) and input costs (economic factors), rather than
separately describing "economic factors" and "the global context" as
unconnected checklist items. This is precisely why the specification
groups markets, competition and global/PESTLE factors together under
one topic -- they are meant to be argued together.

## Market structure as a spectrum, not four fixed boxes

Perfect competition, monopolistic competition, oligopoly and monopoly
are often taught as four separate categories, but the specification's
own framing -- market dominance, decision-making power, and barriers
to entry and exit -- treats them as points on a spectrum of
competitive intensity. A stronger evaluative answer identifies where a
real business sits on that spectrum and how much pricing and strategic
freedom that position gives it, rather than simply naming the
"correct" category from a textbook definition.

## Common mistakes

- **Listing PESTLE factors without applying them to the specific
  business in the question.** A generic explanation of "political
  factors affect businesses" earns far fewer marks than one that names
  the specific policy and traces its effect on the named business's
  costs, revenue or strategy.
- **Treating globalisation as purely positive.** The specification
  explicitly requires evaluation of both opportunities and threats,
  including the risk that a business is more exposed to competition,
  currency movements and international market shocks the more global
  it becomes.
- **Confusing market dominance with simply being a large firm.**
  Dominance is about decision-making power over price and output
  relative to competitors, not size alone -- a large firm in a highly
  contestable market may have little real dominance.
- **Discussing ethical and legal factors as identical.** The
  specification explicitly requires distinguishing law (compulsory,
  enforceable) from ethics (a business choice, not a legal
  requirement), and exam questions test this distinction directly.
- **Ignoring the digital revolution as a distinct factor** separate
  from "technological factors" generally -- the specification treats
  the information age and rapid technological change as having their
  own specific opportunities and threats worth analysing separately.

## How to approach it

Build a bank of real, named business examples for each external
influence as you revise -- a genuine case of a company responding to
exchange rate change, a real merger creating market dominance, an
actual example of ethical or environmental pressure changing a
business's practice -- because Topic 2 questions consistently reward
specific, current evidence over generic theory. Practise explicitly
weighing conflicting influences against each other (for example, cost
pressure from economic factors against reputational pressure from
ethical factors) since the specification's higher-mark questions
require exactly this kind of trade-off judgement. Revise market
structures and market forces together with the global/PESTLE factors,
rather than as separate blocks, since real exam scenarios typically
combine a market-structure question with an external-influence
context in the same case study.
