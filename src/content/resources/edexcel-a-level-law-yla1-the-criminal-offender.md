This guide covers **section 2.2, The criminal offender**, the second of the three sections of Paper 2 (The Law in Action) in Pearson Edexcel International Advanced Level Law, Issue 4. The [Law in Action guide](/resources/a-level-edexcel-law-the-law-in-action/) lists each offence and its section. This page adds the cases that define the elements and shows how to work through a criminal scenario.

## What section 2.2 covers

- **The nature of criminal liability (2.2.1 to 2.2.3)**: *actus reus* (causation, omissions), *mens rea* (intention including transferred malice, subjective recklessness) and strict liability. The specification notes that ss 18, 20 and 47 of the **Offences Against the Person Act 1861** may be used to illustrate these ideas.
- **Property offences (2.2.4 to 2.2.7)**: theft, robbery, burglary and blackmail (**Theft Act 1968**); fraud by false representation and obtaining services dishonestly (**Fraud Act 2006**); making off without payment (**Theft Act 1978**); criminal damage and lawful excuse (**Criminal Damage Act 1971**).
- **General defences (2.2.8 to 2.2.9)**: duress of threats and of circumstances; voluntary and involuntary intoxication.
- **Sentencing (2.2.10)**: custodial sentences, community orders, fines and discharges under the **Criminal Justice Act 2003**.

## Actus reus: causation

**Factual causation** asks whether the result would have happened "but for" the defendant's act. In *R v White* [1910] the victim died of a heart attack before the poison could work, so White was not the cause of death (he was guilty of attempted murder). **Legal causation** asks whether the act was an **operating and substantial cause** (*R v Smith* [1959]). The chain can be broken by an intervening act, but:

- the defendant must take the victim as they find them (the **thin skull rule**: *R v Blaue* [1975]);
- poor medical treatment rarely breaks the chain unless it is "palpably wrong" (*R v Jordan* [1956]) or so independent that the original wound is insignificant (*R v Cheshire* [1991]);
- a victim's reasonably foreseeable reaction in escaping does not break it (*R v Roberts* [1971]).

## Actus reus: omissions

There is no general duty to act, but a duty arises from:

| Source | Case |
|---|---|
| Contract | *R v Pittwood* [1902]: a level-crossing keeper who left the gate open |
| Relationship | *R v Gibbins and Proctor* [1918]: a father who starved his child |
| Voluntary assumption of care | *R v Stone and Dobinson* [1977] |
| Public office | *R v Dytham* [1979]: a police officer who did nothing during an assault |
| Creating a dangerous situation | *R v Miller* [1983]: failing to deal with a fire he had started |
| Statute | For example, failing to provide a breath specimen |

## Mens rea

- **Direct intention**: the defendant's aim or purpose (*R v Mohan* [1975]).
- **Oblique intention**: the jury may find intention where the result was a **virtual certainty** and the defendant appreciated that (*R v Woollin* [1998], refining *R v Nedrick* [1986]).
- **Subjective recklessness**: the defendant personally foresaw a risk and unreasonably took it (*R v Cunningham* [1957]; for criminal damage, *R v G* [2003]).
- **Transferred malice**: *mens rea* towards one victim transfers to another (*R v Latimer* [1886]), but not between different kinds of offence (*R v Pembliton* [1874]: intending to hit people, he broke a window).
- **Coincidence**: *actus reus* and *mens rea* must coincide, though a continuing act can satisfy this (*Fagan v Metropolitan Police Commissioner* [1969]).

**Using the OAPA 1861**: s 47 (assault occasioning actual bodily harm) needs only *mens rea* for the assault; s 20 (unlawful wounding or inflicting grievous bodily harm) needs foresight of some harm; s 18 (wounding or causing grievous bodily harm with intent) needs **intention**. These show how *mens rea* separates offences with similar *actus reus*.

## Strict liability

Some offences need no *mens rea* for at least one element, mostly regulatory offences protecting the public. The presumption is that *mens rea* is required (*Sweet v Parsley* [1970]), displaced only where the statute and its purpose show otherwise (*Gammon v Attorney General of Hong Kong* [1985]). Examples: selling unfit meat despite taking care (*Callow v Tillstone* [1900]) and selling a lottery ticket to an under-age buyer (*Harrow LBC v Shah* [1999]). For: efficient enforcement and higher standards. Against: an offender who took every care can be convicted.

## Property offences

**Theft (Theft Act 1968, ss 1 to 6)**: dishonestly appropriating property belonging to another with the intention of permanently depriving the other of it.

| Element | Section | Key point |
|---|---|---|
| Dishonesty | s 2 | Not dishonest if D believes they have a legal right, the owner would consent, or the owner cannot be found by reasonable steps. Otherwise the test in *Ivey v Genting Casinos* [2017]: D's actual belief about the facts, then the standards of ordinary decent people |
| Appropriation | s 3 | Any assumption of the rights of an owner (*R v Morris* [1983]), even with consent (*R v Gomez* [1993]) |
| Property | s 4 | Money and all other property, real or personal, including things in action |
| Belonging to another | s 5 | Includes possession or control; property got by another's mistake (s 5(4)) |
| Intention permanently to deprive | s 6 | Includes treating the thing as one's own to dispose of; borrowing is enough only if the "goodness" is gone (*R v Lloyd* [1985]) |

The specification states candidates are not examined on a change in the law until two years after it, giving *Ivey* as its own example, so it is now part of the law you should know.

**Robbery (s 8)**: stealing, and immediately before or at the time of doing so, using force or putting someone in fear of force, in order to steal. Little force is needed (*R v Dawson and James* [1976]); appropriation can be continuing (*R v Hale* [1978]); no theft means no robbery (*R v Robinson* [1977]).

**Burglary (s 9)**: s 9(1)(a) entering a building or part of a building as a trespasser with intent to steal, inflict grievous bodily harm or do unlawful damage; s 9(1)(b) having entered as a trespasser, stealing or inflicting grievous bodily harm, or attempting to. Entry must be "effective and substantial" (*R v Collins* [1972]), later put simply as "effective" (*R v Brown* [1985]), and can be partial (*R v Ryan* [1996]); "part of a building" covers a restricted area such as behind a shop counter (*R v Walkington* [1979]).

**Blackmail (s 21; s 34 defines gain and loss)**: an unwarranted demand with menaces, with a view to gain or intent to cause loss, gain and loss being in money or other property.

**Fraud Act 2006**: s 2 fraud by false representation (dishonestly making a false representation, intending to make a gain or cause a loss); s 5 defines gain and loss; s 11 obtaining services dishonestly, knowing payment is required and intending not to pay.

**Making off without payment (Theft Act 1978, s 3)**: knowing that payment on the spot is required, dishonestly making off without paying, intending to avoid payment permanently (*R v Allen* [1985]).

**Criminal damage (1971 Act, s 1)**: destroying or damaging property belonging to another, intending or being reckless as to that. **Lawful excuse (s 5)**: an honest belief that the owner would consent, or that the property needed protecting from immediate danger; the belief need only be honest (*Jaggard v Dickinson* [1981]).

## General defences

**Duress of threats**: a threat of death or serious injury; the *R v Graham* [1982] two-stage test (was D compelled by a reasonable belief in the threat, and would a sober person of reasonable firmness have responded the same way?); not available to murder (*R v Howe* [1987]) or attempted murder (*R v Gotts* [1992]); lost where D voluntarily associated with criminals (*R v Hasan* [2005]). **Duress of circumstances** applies the same test to danger from a situation (*R v Willer* [1986]; *R v Martin* [1989]).

**Intoxication**: voluntary intoxication is no defence to basic-intent offences (*DPP v Majewski* [1977]) but can negate specific intent; "Dutch courage" defeats it (*Attorney General for Northern Ireland v Gallagher* [1963]). Involuntary intoxication helps only if D did not form *mens rea*: an intoxicated intent is still intent (*R v Kingston* [1994]); taking a sedative that has an unexpected effect may count (*R v Hardie* [1985]).

## Sentencing

The **Criminal Justice Act 2003** framework, now consolidated in the Sentencing Act 2020, sets four main types:

- **Custody**: only where the offence is so serious that neither a fine nor a community sentence can be justified; may be suspended.
- **Community orders**: one or more requirements, such as unpaid work, curfew or rehabilitation activity.
- **Fines**: set by the seriousness of the offence and the offender's means.
- **Discharges**: conditional or absolute.

Courts consider seriousness (culpability and harm), aggravating factors (previous convictions; hostility based on race or religion), mitigating factors, and a reduction of up to one third for an early guilty plea.

## Worked example: a burglary scenario

*Owen enters a neighbour's garage through an unlocked door, meaning to take a bicycle. He finds the owner inside and pushes her over to get away, without taking anything.*

1. **Trespasser?** He had no permission: yes.
2. **Building or part of one?** A garage is a building.
3. **Intent on entry?** He intended to steal: s 9(1)(a) burglary is complete on entry.
4. **Robbery?** No theft occurred, so no robbery (*Robinson*); consider an assault offence for the push.

## Common mistakes

- Using "but for" without legal causation.
- Calling borrowing theft without applying s 6 and *Lloyd*.
- Mixing up s 9(1)(a) (intent at entry) with s 9(1)(b) (an offence after entry).
- Allowing duress as a defence to murder.
- Naming a sentence without explaining what it involves.

## Exam technique

For scenarios, deal with **one offence at a time**: *actus reus*, *mens rea*, defences, then conclusion. **Explain** needs the rule plus a case; **evaluate** or **assess** needs strengths, weaknesses and a judgement.
