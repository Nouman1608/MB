This guide covers sub-topic **1.2 Economic methodology** of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Economics 9708 syllabus for examination in 2026, 2027 and 2028. It is AS Level content. The syllabus lists four points: economics as a social science (1.2.1); positive and normative statements, the distinction between facts and value judgements (1.2.2); the meaning of the term ceteris paribus (1.2.3); and the importance of the time period: short run, long run and very long run (1.2.4). These ideas rarely earn many marks on their own, but they are used in almost every later answer: each time you hold other things constant, separate a fact from an opinion, or say "in the long run", you are using 1.2. All examples are invented.

## 1.2.1 Economics as a social science

A **social science** studies human behaviour and society using systematic methods: observing, building explanations, and testing them against evidence. Economics studies how people, firms and governments make choices about scarce resources.

Economists work in a recognisable sequence.

1. **Observe** a pattern, such as house prices rising faster in one city than in others.
2. **Build a model**, a simplified representation of reality based on assumptions, such as the demand and supply model.
3. **Derive a prediction** (a hypothesis), such as "if more homes are built, price growth will slow".
4. **Test** it against data.
5. **Accept, revise or reject** the model.

**Why economics is a science, but a social one.**

| Like a natural science | Unlike a natural science |
|---|---|
| Uses models, hypotheses and data | Controlled laboratory experiments are rarely possible; many things change at once |
| Aims for predictions that can be tested | People learn and change their behaviour, sometimes because of the prediction itself |
| Revises theories when evidence contradicts them | Data are collected after the event and can be revised or imprecise |
| | Values influence which questions are studied and how results are used |

**Why models use assumptions.** A model that tried to include everything would be as complex as the economy itself and would explain nothing. The demand and supply model assumes, for example, that many buyers and sellers act independently. An assumption is judged by whether the model's predictions are useful, not by whether the assumption is perfectly realistic. In evaluation, though, an unrealistic assumption is a good reason to doubt a prediction in a particular case.

## 1.2.2 Positive and normative statements

A **positive statement** is a statement of fact, or a prediction, that can be tested against evidence. A **normative statement** contains a value judgement: an opinion about what ought to be, which evidence alone cannot settle.

The test is **testability, not truth**. "Bus fares in the city fell last year" is positive even if fares actually rose, because data can check it.

**Tricky cases.**

| Statement | Type | Why |
|---|---|---|
| A 10% rise in bus fares would reduce passenger numbers by 4% | Positive | A testable prediction, with numbers that can be checked |
| Bus fares are too high for students | Normative | "Too high" is a judgement about fairness |
| Most economists believe a fare rise would reduce passenger numbers | Positive | Whether most economists believe it can be checked by surveying them |
| The government should cut bus fares because pollution has risen by 6% | Normative | It contains a fact (pollution rose) but its conclusion ("should") is a value judgement |
| Unemployment is a more serious problem than inflation | Normative | "More serious" depends on whose interests are weighted |
| Cutting fares would increase inequality | Positive | A testable claim about outcomes, even though inequality is a topic people feel strongly about |

Two rules follow. First, a statement is normative if it contains any value judgement, even when it also contains facts. Second, a topic being controversial does not make a statement normative; only the presence of a value judgement does.

**Why the distinction matters.** Two economists can agree on every positive fact about a policy, for example that a fare cut would raise passenger numbers and cost 30 million cu a year, and still disagree about whether it should happen, because they weigh the benefits to passengers and the cost to taxpayers differently. Positive economics narrows disagreements; it cannot remove those that rest on values.

## 1.2.3 Ceteris paribus

**Ceteris paribus** means "other things being equal": in analysing the effect of one change, all other influences are assumed to stay constant.

The real world rarely co-operates, which is exactly why the assumption is needed.

**Worked example 1: separating two changes.** In an invented city, over one year, bus fares rise by 10% and average incomes also rise by 5%. Passenger journeys fall by only 1%. Does this mean fares hardly affect journeys?

Suppose separate evidence shows that, ceteris paribus:

- a 10% fare rise on its own reduces journeys by 4%;
- a 5% income rise on its own increases journeys by 3%, because people travel more for leisure and work.

Combined, journeys change by roughly −4 + 3 = **−1%**, matching the data. Without ceteris paribus, a reader of the raw data would wrongly conclude that fares have very little effect. Holding incomes constant reveals the true effect of the fare rise: a fall of 4%.

**Using it in answers.** Diagrams rely on it. When you shift a demand curve because incomes rise, you are assuming the product's price, the prices of other goods, tastes and every other influence on demand are unchanged. In evaluation you can relax the assumption: "ceteris paribus the fare rise cuts journeys, but if incomes are rising at the same time the observed fall will be smaller".

## 1.2.4 The importance of the time period

Economists distinguish three time periods by what can change, not by a fixed number of months.

| Period | What can change | Example: an invented electricity company |
|---|---|---|
| Short run | At least one factor of production is fixed; output changes by varying other factors | Burn more fuel and run existing power stations for longer hours |
| Long run | All factors of production can be changed, but technology is given | Build an additional power station of the existing design |
| Very long run | Technology and the quality of factors can also change | Develop and adopt a new generating technology |

The length of each period differs between industries. A street food stall can change all its inputs in a few weeks, so its long run is short; a power company may need ten years to build a new station.

**Why time matters for analysis.**

- **Responses grow over time.** When the price of petrol rises, drivers can change little at first; given longer, they buy more fuel-efficient cars or move closer to work. Effects measured in the short run can understate long-run effects.
- **Costs and benefits arrive at different times.** A policy that is costly now, such as investment in training, may bring benefits many years later.
- **Judgements can reverse.** A firm may make a loss in the short run on a new product and still expect profit in the long run.

**Worked example 2: petrol demand over time.** In an invented country, after a 20% rise in petrol prices, petrol use falls 3% in the first year and 12% after five years, as people replace their cars. A minister who judged a petrol tax only on the first year would conclude it barely changes behaviour; over five years it cuts use four times as much.

## Common mistakes

- Judging whether a statement is positive by whether it is true.
- Treating a statement with a fact in it as positive even though it ends with "should".
- Calling a controversial topic normative when the statement itself is testable.
- Using ceteris paribus as a phrase without saying what is being held constant.
- Defining the short and long run by months or years instead of by which factors can change.
- Forgetting the very long run, when technology itself changes.

## Exam technique

- **Identify** or **State**: classify each statement and give the reason in a few words (testable, or contains a value judgement).
- **Explain:** say what ceteris paribus holds constant in the case given, or which factors are fixed in each time period.
- **Assess** or **Evaluate:** use 1.2 as an evaluation tool in other topics: relax a ceteris paribus assumption, or contrast short-run and long-run effects.
