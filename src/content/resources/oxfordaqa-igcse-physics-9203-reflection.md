This guide goes deeper into one subtopic: **3.3.4 Reflection**, statements a to c of the OxfordAQA International GCSE Physics (9203) specification, for exams May/June 2018 onwards. The qualification is untiered, so every candidate needs all of it. The specification also says you will be expected to **construct ray diagrams** to show the changing path of reflected rays, so a large part of this page is about drawing and reasoning with angles.

For the wider unit that also covers refraction, see the [reflection, refraction and total internal reflection guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-physics-reflection-refraction-refractive-index-and-total/). When you have finished here, use the [reflection revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-physics-9203-reflection-revision-notes/) and the [reflection practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-physics-9203-reflection-practice/).

## What the specification asks for

| Statement | What you must be able to do |
|---|---|
| 3.3.4 a | State that when waves are reflected, the angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection, and use it |
| 3.3.4 b | Define the normal as a construction line perpendicular to the reflecting surface at the point of incidence |
| 3.3.4 c | Describe the image in a plane mirror as virtual, upright and laterally inverted |
| Guidance | Construct ray diagrams that show how reflected rays change direction |

Notice the wording of statement a: "when **waves** are reflected". The law is not only about light. Water waves in a ripple tank meeting a straight barrier, sound bouncing off a cliff and microwaves reflecting from a metal plate all obey it. An exam question may set the law in any of these contexts.

## The law of reflection, measured properly

The angle of incidence, *i*, is the angle between the incoming ray and the normal. The angle of reflection, *r*, is the angle between the reflected ray and the normal. The law says *i* = *r*. Three further facts follow from the geometry and are useful in calculations:

- The incident ray, the reflected ray and the normal all lie in the same flat plane.
- A ray that arrives along the normal (*i* = 0°) goes straight back along its own path.
- The angle a ray makes with the **surface** is 90° minus its angle with the normal. Questions often give the surface angle on purpose to see whether you convert it.

For a wavefront picture, the same law means a straight wavefront striking a straight barrier at a slant leaves at the same slant on the other side of the normal. The wavelength, frequency and speed are all unchanged by reflection, because the wave stays in the same medium.

## Why the normal matters

The normal is not a real line in the apparatus. It is drawn so that both angles are measured from the same reference. At a flat mirror every normal is parallel, which is why a parallel beam stays parallel after reflection. A rough surface is made of many tiny surfaces facing in different directions, so each part of a parallel beam meets a normal in a different direction. Each ray still obeys *i* = *r*, but the reflected rays leave in many directions. That is why paper reflects light without giving an image, while a polished mirror gives a clear one.

## The image in a plane mirror

Statement c uses three precise words. Learn what each one means, because "describe the image" questions are marked word by word.

- **Virtual**: the reflected rays only appear to come from the image. No light actually passes through the point behind the mirror, so the image cannot be caught on a screen.
- **Upright**: the image is the same way up as the object.
- **Laterally inverted**: left and right are swapped. Your raised right hand appears as the image's left hand. Writing seen in a mirror reads backwards.

Two further properties come straight out of a correct ray diagram: the image is the **same size** as the object, and it is the **same distance behind** the mirror as the object is in front, on a line through the object perpendicular to the mirror.

## Constructing a reflection ray diagram

When you are asked to construct a diagram to find where an image is, or to show the path of a ray between two mirrors, work in this order:

1. Draw the mirror as a straight line, and mark the point where the ray strikes it.
2. Draw the normal there with a ruler and protractor, as a dashed line at 90° to the mirror.
3. Measure the angle of incidence from the normal.
4. Draw the reflected ray on the other side of the normal at the same angle, with an arrow showing direction.
5. To locate an image, repeat with a second ray from the same object point and extend both reflected rays backwards with dashed lines. Where the dashed lines cross is the image.

Real rays are solid lines with arrows; extensions behind the mirror are dashed, because no light travels there. Many marks are lost by drawing the extensions as solid lines or by leaving out arrows.

## Worked example 1: the periscope

A simple periscope has two plane mirrors fixed parallel to each other, each at 45° to the vertical tube. Light from a distant object travels horizontally into the top of the tube.

The mirror makes 45° with the horizontal ray, so the ray makes 45° with the mirror surface. Its angle with the normal is therefore 90° − 45° = **45°**, so the angle of reflection is also 45°. The ray is turned through 45° + 45° = 90° and travels straight down the tube. At the lower mirror the same thing happens in reverse: the vertical ray meets the mirror at 45° to the normal and leaves horizontally, travelling in its original direction but lower down. The image seen at the bottom is upright, and it is not laterally inverted, because the second reflection undoes the reversal made by the first.

## Worked example 2: two mirrors at right angles

Two plane mirrors meet at 90°. A ray hits the first mirror with an angle of incidence of 25°. Find the angle of incidence at the second mirror and the final direction of the ray.

```
At mirror 1:  i = 25°, so r = 25°
              angle between reflected ray and mirror 1 surface = 90° − 25° = 65°
Mirrors meet at 90°, so in the triangle formed by the ray and the two mirrors:
              angle between ray and mirror 2 surface = 180° − 90° − 65° = 25°
At mirror 2:  i = 90° − 25° = 65°, so r = 65°
Turning:      mirror 1 turns the ray through 180° − 2 × 25° = 130°
              mirror 2 turns it through 180° − 2 × 65° = 50°
              total turn = 130° + 50° = 180°
```

The ray leaves travelling back parallel to its original direction. This works for any starting angle, which is why reflectors made of mirrors at right angles send light back towards its source.

## Worked example 3: turning the mirror

A ray strikes a plane mirror with an angle of incidence of 30°. The mirror is then rotated by 10° so that the angle of incidence becomes 40°. By how much does the reflected ray turn?

Before: angle between incident and reflected rays = 30° + 30° = 60°. After: 40° + 40° = 80°. The incident ray has not moved, so the reflected ray has turned through 80° − 60° = **20°**, twice the rotation of the mirror. You should be able to reach this answer from the law of reflection alone, by adding the two angles each time.

## Common mistakes

- Measuring the angle of incidence from the mirror surface instead of from the normal.
- Writing "the image is real" or "the image is upside down". A plane mirror image is virtual and upright.
- Saying "laterally inverted" means upside down. It means left and right are reversed.
- Placing the image on the mirror surface rather than the same distance behind it.
- Drawing the normal by eye. In a construction question, use a protractor to make it 90° to the surface.
- Thinking a rough surface breaks the law of reflection. Each ray still obeys it; the normals simply point in different directions.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **State** the law: "the angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection". No explanation is needed, but naming both angles is.
- **Define** the normal: include all three ideas, a construction line, perpendicular to the surface, at the point of incidence.
- **Describe** the image: use the three specification words, virtual, upright and laterally inverted. If the question has more marks, add same size and same distance behind the mirror.
- **Draw** or **construct**: use a ruler, a dashed normal, arrows on rays and dashed extensions behind the mirror. Angles should be accurate to within a couple of degrees.
- **Calculate** an angle: write each step, converting a surface angle to a normal angle first, and give the unit (degrees).
- **Explain** why a mirror image is virtual: say that the reflected rays diverge and only appear to come from a point behind the mirror, so no light reaches that point.
