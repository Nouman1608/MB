## Section A

**1.** Define the term *delayering*. **[2]**

**2.** Explain the difference between authority and responsibility. **[2]**

**3.** Identify **two** features of a formal organisational structure. **[2]**

**4.** Explain **one** reason why a business might organise itself by product rather than by function. **[2]**

**5.** Explain why a manager who delegates a task remains accountable for it. **[2]**

**6.** Brightmoor Bank has a structure of 5 levels. Every manager has a span of control of 4. Calculate the number of people at the lowest level, assuming one person at the top. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** Hollins Retail plans to remove its layer of 15 area managers, each paid $52 000 a year. Its 75 store managers will each receive an extra $3 500 a year for taking on more responsibility.

**(a)** Calculate the net annual saving.

**(b)** Analyse **two** risks to Hollins of this change. **[7]**

**8.** Explain how a matrix structure could help an engineering firm develop a new product. **[4]**

**9.** Analyse the conflict between control and trust that might arise when the owner of a growing bakery delegates purchasing decisions to a new supervisor. **[4]**

**10.** Explain **two** processes a business could use to hold managers accountable. **[4]**

**11.** Garnett Builders' HR department requires every vacancy to be advertised for four weeks. Site managers say this delays projects. Explain why this conflict arises between line and staff functions and **one** way it could be reduced. **[4]**

**12.** Selborne Hotels owns 25 hotels in different countries. All decisions on prices, menus and staffing are taken at head office. Evaluate whether Selborne should decentralise decision-making. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** Removing one or more levels of hierarchy from an organisation [1], usually to cut costs and shorten the chain of command [1].

**2.** Responsibility is the duty to carry out a task [1]; authority is the power to make the decisions and give the instructions needed to carry it out [1].

**3.** Any two: levels of hierarchy [1]; chain of command [1]; span of control; delegation; accountability; centralisation or decentralisation.

**4.** If the business makes distinct product ranges that need different expertise or serve different markets [1], each product division can focus on its own customers and respond faster to their needs [1].

**5.** Delegation passes authority to carry out the task, not the duty to answer for its result [1]; the manager chose to delegate and still answers to their own superior for the outcome [1].

**6.** People at level 5 = 4 × 4 × 4 × 4 [1] = 256 [1].

**7.** **(a)** Saving = 15 × 52 000 = $780 000 [1]. Extra cost = 75 × 3 500 = $262 500 [1]. Net saving = $517 500 [1].

**(b)** Any two, analysed. Store managers may become overloaded with extra duties [1], so customer service or stock control in stores could suffer, reducing sales [1]. Senior managers will each have far more store managers reporting to them [1], so they may be unable to monitor performance closely, and problems may go unnoticed [1]. Allow: loss of promotion steps lowering motivation, or loss of area managers' experience.

**8.** A matrix brings together staff from different departments, such as design, production and marketing, into one project team [1]. Designers can check with production staff that the product can be made efficiently [1], and marketing staff can make sure it meets customer needs [1], so development is faster and fewer changes are needed later [1].

**9.** The owner remains accountable for purchasing, so may want to check every order [1]; but checking too closely suggests a lack of trust, which may demotivate the new supervisor [1]. If the owner checks too little, an inexperienced supervisor might overbuy and waste stock [1]. The owner could set clear limits, such as approval only for orders above a set value, and reduce checks as the supervisor gains experience [1].

**10.** Any two, explained. Setting targets and reviewing results against them [1], so managers know what they must achieve and can be judged on it [1]. Regular appraisal or performance reviews [1], in which managers must explain their results and agree actions [1]. Allow reports, audits or meetings.

**11.** HR is a staff function that wants fair, consistent recruitment [1], while site managers are line managers who need workers quickly to meet project deadlines [1]. Each judges the process by different aims, so they clash [1]. It could be reduced by agreeing a faster process for urgent site roles, designed with site managers' input [1].

**12.** For decentralisation: hotels in different countries face different customers, costs and laws, so local managers understand local prices and tastes better [1]; decisions on menus and staffing could be made faster [1], improving customer satisfaction and occupancy [1]; local managers given authority may be more motivated and develop as future senior managers [1]; head office would be freed to focus on strategy [1].
Against: local decisions could weaken brand consistency, which matters to international guests who expect the same standard [1]; head office may lose the benefits of bulk buying or consistent pricing [1]; some local managers may lack the skills to make good pricing decisions [1].
Judgement: Selborne should partly decentralise [1], giving local managers authority over menus, staffing and some pricing [1], while keeping brand standards, major investment and purchasing at head office [1]. The success depends on the quality of local managers and on accountability processes such as monthly performance reports [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Forgetting to subtract the extra cost when calculating a delayering saving.
- Saying accountability can be delegated.
- Describing a matrix structure without applying it to the business's project.
- Recommending complete centralisation or decentralisation without considering which decisions suit each.
- Explaining line and staff functions without explaining why they conflict.
