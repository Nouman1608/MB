Subtopic 14.1 of Cambridge International AS & A Level Physics 9702 (2025–2027) covers the direction of thermal energy transfer and the meaning of thermal equilibrium. These notes are condensed for recall. For full explanations and worked examples, read the [Thermal equilibrium study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-physics-9702-a-thermal-equilibrium/); to apply the ideas, try the [Thermal equilibrium practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-physics-9702-a-thermal-equilibrium-practice/).

## The two syllabus statements

1. Thermal energy is transferred from a region of **higher temperature** to a region of **lower temperature**.
2. Regions of **equal temperature** are in **thermal equilibrium**.

## Core ideas

- **Direction** of net transfer depends on **temperature only**, not on mass, material or total internal energy.
- **Amount** transferred depends on mass, specific heat capacity and temperature change (Q = mcΔθ, from 14.3).
- **Thermal equilibrium**: no **net** transfer of thermal energy between regions, which happens when they are at the same temperature.
- Equilibrium is **dynamic**: energy still crosses the boundary in both directions, at equal rates.
- Molecular picture: the hotter region has the greater mean kinetic energy per molecule; collisions pass energy on, on average, from higher to lower mean kinetic energy.

## Thermal equilibrium does not mean

| Misconception | Correction |
|---|---|
| Equal internal energy | A large and a small body at the same temperature have very different internal energies |
| No energy moving at all | Energy moves both ways at equal rates |
| Feels equally hot to the touch | Good conductors feel colder at the same temperature because they draw energy from the skin faster |
| The bodies have the same specific heat capacity | Any two materials can be in equilibrium |

## Thermometers

- A thermometer shows **its own** temperature.
- It is valid only once it is in **thermal equilibrium** with the object.
- If A and B are each in equilibrium with the same thermometer at the same reading, A and B are at the same temperature (sometimes called the zeroth law of thermodynamics).
- The thermometer absorbs or releases energy while reaching equilibrium, so it can change the temperature of a **small** sample. Use a sample much larger than the thermometer, or a thermometer that needs little energy per kelvin.

## Mixing calculations

```
energy lost by hotter body = energy gained by cooler body
m₁c₁(θ₁ – θ) = m₂c₂(θ – θ₂)
```

- Bracket the differences so both sides are positive.
- Check: θ must lie **between** θ₁ and θ₂.
- The final temperature is closer to the starting temperature of the body with the larger **mc** (energy needed per kelvin).
- Stated assumptions: no energy exchanged with the surroundings; container's heat capacity negligible.
- Same material mixed (e.g. water with water): c cancels, so θ = (m₁θ₁ + m₂θ₂) / (m₁ + m₂).

## Worked figures to recognise

| Situation | Final temperature |
|---|---|
| 0.20 kg copper (c = 385) at 95 °C into 0.30 kg water at 20 °C | 24 °C |
| 10 g glass (c = 840) at 20 °C into 5.0 g water at 60 °C | 49 °C |
| Same glass into 200 g water at 60 °C | 59.6 °C |
| 0.30 kg water at 70 °C mixed with 0.10 kg water at 10 °C | 55 °C |

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Temperature | The property that decides the direction of net thermal energy transfer |
| Thermal energy transfer | Energy transferred because of a temperature difference |
| Thermal equilibrium | No net thermal energy transfer between regions; equal temperatures |
| Internal energy | Total kinetic and potential energy of the molecules (Topic 16) |
| Thermal contact | Arrangement in which energy can be transferred between bodies by thermal processes |

## Exam traps

- "No energy transfer" instead of "no **net** energy transfer".
- Saying energy flows from the body with **more energy**.
- Using temperature and thermal energy as if they meant the same thing.
- Reading a thermometer before it reaches equilibrium.
- Answers outside the range of starting temperatures in mixing calculations.
- Forgetting to state that losses to the surroundings were ignored.

## Self-test

1. State the direction of net thermal energy transfer between two regions.
2. Define thermal equilibrium.
3. A small bead at 300 °C is dropped into a bucket of water at 20 °C. Which way does net energy flow?
4. Why is thermal equilibrium described as dynamic?
5. A metal rail feels colder than a wooden fence post in the same field. Are they at different temperatures? Explain.
6. Why can a thermometer give a misleading reading for a very small volume of liquid?
7. Body P is in equilibrium with a thermometer reading 37 °C. Body Q gives the same reading. What can you say about P and Q?
8. Mix 0.20 kg of water at 80 °C with 0.60 kg of water at 20 °C. Find the final temperature (no losses).
9. In a mixing calculation you obtain a final temperature higher than both starting temperatures. What does that tell you?
10. Give two assumptions made in a simple mixing calculation.
11. Two bodies are at the same temperature. Must they have the same internal energy?
12. In terms of molecules, why does energy flow from a hotter region to a cooler one?

**Answers:** 1. From the region at higher temperature to the region at lower temperature. 2. A state in which there is no net transfer of thermal energy between regions, because they are at the same temperature. 3. From the bead to the water, because the bead is at the higher temperature. 4. Energy is still transferred in both directions, but at equal rates, so the net transfer is zero. 5. No; they are at the same temperature. The metal conducts energy away from the hand faster, so it feels colder. 6. It absorbs energy from the liquid while reaching equilibrium, lowering the liquid's temperature. 7. They are at the same temperature and would be in thermal equilibrium with each other. 8. θ = (0.20 × 80 + 0.60 × 20) ÷ 0.80 = 35 °C. 9. A sign error; the final temperature must lie between the starting temperatures. 10. No energy exchanged with the surroundings; negligible energy taken by the container (or thermometer). 11. No; internal energy also depends on the amount and type of material. 12. Molecules in the hotter region have greater mean kinetic energy, so in collisions at the boundary they give, on average, more energy than they receive.
