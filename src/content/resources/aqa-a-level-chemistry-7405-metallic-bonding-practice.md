## Questions

**1.** Describe the bonding in a piece of solid magnesium. **[3]**

**2.** The table gives melting points for three Group 1 metals.

| Metal | Melting point / °C |
|---|---|
| Lithium | 181 |
| Sodium | 98 |
| Potassium | 63 |

**(a)** State the trend in melting point down the group. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain the trend. **[3]**

**3.** The melting points of sodium, magnesium and aluminium are 98 °C, 650 °C and 660 °C.

**(a)** Explain why magnesium has a higher melting point than sodium. **[3]**

**(b)** Predict, with a reason, whether aluminium would have more or fewer delocalised electrons per unit volume than magnesium. **[1]**

**4.** Explain why:

**(a)** solid copper conducts electricity **[2]**

**(b)** solid sodium chloride does not conduct electricity **[2]**

**(c)** molten sodium chloride conducts electricity **[1]**

**5.** A piece of metal is hammered into a flat sheet.

**(a)** Explain why the metal does not shatter. **[3]**

**(b)** State why a crystal of an ionic compound would shatter under the same treatment. **[2]**

**6.** Explain why metals are good conductors of heat. **[2]**

**7.** A student writes: "The metallic bond is the attraction between the nucleus of one atom and the electrons of the next." Identify two errors in this statement. **[2]**

**8.** Calcium and potassium are in the same period. Calcium has a melting point of 842 °C and potassium has a melting point of 63 °C.

**(a)** Compare the number of delocalised electrons provided by each atom. **[1]**

**(b)** Use your answer to explain the difference in melting point. **[2]**

**9.** Draw a labelled diagram to show the arrangement of particles in solid sodium, showing at least three rows and indicating the charge on the particles. **[3]**

**10.** Metals are insoluble in water. Explain why a lump of magnesium does not dissolve in water in the way that sodium chloride does. **[2]**

**11.** Aluminium wire is used in overhead power cables. Use the structure of aluminium to explain why it is:

**(a)** a good electrical conductor **[1]**

**(b)** ductile **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** A lattice of positive ions (Mg²⁺) [1]; delocalised electrons that are free to move through the structure [1]; electrostatic attraction between the positive ions and the delocalised electrons holds the lattice together [1]. **[3]**

**2. (a)** The melting point decreases down the group [1]

**(b)** The ions all have the same charge (1+) and each atom provides one delocalised electron [1]; the ions get larger down the group, so the delocalised electrons are further from the positive nuclei [1]; the electrostatic attraction is weaker, so less energy is needed to overcome it [1]. **[4]**

**3. (a)** Mg²⁺ has a larger charge than Na⁺, and magnesium provides two delocalised electrons per atom, not one [1]; Mg²⁺ is smaller than Na⁺, so the delocalised electrons are closer to the nuclei [1]; the electrostatic attraction is stronger, so more energy is needed to overcome it [1]

**(b)** More. Each aluminium atom provides three delocalised electrons and each magnesium atom provides two, and the aluminium ions are smaller [1]. **[4]**

**4. (a)** Copper has delocalised electrons [1]; that are free to move and carry charge through the solid [1]

**(b)** The ions are held in fixed positions in the lattice [1]; so there are no mobile charge carriers [1]

**(c)** The lattice breaks down when molten and the ions are free to move and carry charge [1]. **[5]**

**5. (a)** Layers of positive ions can slide over one another [1]; the delocalised electrons move with them and remain between the ions [1]; so the electrostatic attraction continues in the new positions and the structure does not break [1]

**(b)** A shift of one layer brings ions of the same charge next to each other [1]; the repulsion between them splits the crystal [1]. **[5]**

**6.** Delocalised electrons are free to move, so they carry kinetic energy quickly through the lattice [1]; the positive ions also vibrate and pass energy to their neighbours [1]. **[2]**

**7.** Any two of: the bond is between positive ions and delocalised electrons, not between atoms or nuclei [1]; the attraction is not just to one neighbouring atom but acts throughout the whole lattice [1]; the electrons are delocalised, not held by one particular atom [1]. **[2]**

**8. (a)** Calcium provides two delocalised electrons per atom; potassium provides one [1]

**(b)** Ca²⁺ has a larger charge and is smaller than K⁺ [1]; so there is a stronger electrostatic attraction between the ions and the delocalised electrons and more energy is needed to melt calcium [1]. **[3]**

**9.** At least three rows of regularly arranged circles [1]; labelled Na⁺ or with the charge 1+ shown on each [1]; delocalised electrons shown between the ions (and labelled) [1]. **[3]**

**10.** Water molecules cannot attract the positive ions strongly enough to pull them from the lattice [1]; the metallic bonding between the ions and the delocalised electrons is stronger than any attraction to water. (Sodium chloride dissolves because water attracts its separate Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions.) [1]. **[2]**

**11. (a)** Aluminium has delocalised electrons that are free to move through the structure and carry charge [1]

**(b)** Layers of Al³⁺ ions slide when the metal is drawn out [1]; the delocalised electrons keep the ions bonded in the new arrangement [1]. **[3]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "atoms" for the particles in the lattice, instead of positive ions.
- Saying that ions, not electrons, move to carry the current in a metal.
- Forgetting the size of the ion in a comparison down a group.
- Describing metallic bonding as a bond between two atoms.
- Using "free electrons" without the word delocalised, or leaving out electrostatic attraction.
