## Section A

**1.** State what is meant by the elastic limit of a material. **[1]**

**2.** Explain why the area under a force–extension graph represents the work done in stretching an object. **[2]**

**3.** A spring of spring constant 120 N m⁻¹ stores 0.60 J of elastic potential energy. It obeys Hooke's law. Calculate its extension. **[2]**

**4.** A wire is loaded beyond its limit of proportionality but not beyond its elastic limit. Describe what happens when the load is removed, and explain why Eₚ = ½kx² cannot be used to find the energy stored at the maximum load. **[2]**

**5.** Use F = kx to show that ½Fx and ½kx² are equivalent expressions, and show that ½kx² has the unit of energy. **[2]**

## Section B

**6.** A spring of spring constant 300 N m⁻¹ obeys Hooke's law. It is stretched from an extension of 2.0 cm to an extension of 6.0 cm.

**(a)** Calculate the energy stored at an extension of 2.0 cm. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the additional energy stored as the extension increases from 2.0 cm to 6.0 cm. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain why the answer to **(b)** is not equal to ½k(0.040 m)². **[1]**

**7.** A metal wire is loaded. The force–extension graph is a straight line from the origin to 60 N at an extension of 1.2 mm, which is the limit of proportionality. Further loading to 75 N takes the extension to 3.0 mm; this section may be treated as a straight line. The load is then removed, and the wire unloads along a straight line parallel to the first section.

**(a)** Calculate the elastic potential energy stored at 60 N. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the total work done on the wire as the load increases from 0 to 75 N. **[2]**

**(c)** Calculate the permanent extension of the wire after unloading. **[2]**

**(d)** Calculate the energy dissipated in the wire during the loading and unloading cycle. **[2]**

**8.** A student stretches a rubber band and plots a curved force–extension graph.

**(a)** She estimates that the area under the loading curve is 48 small squares. Each small square represents 0.20 N on the force axis and 2.0 mm on the extension axis. Calculate the work done in stretching the band. **[2]**

**(b)** The unloading curve lies below the loading curve but returns to the origin. State what type of deformation the band has undergone, and what the area between the two curves represents. **[2]**

**9.** A toy launcher contains a spring of spring constant 450 N m⁻¹. The spring is compressed by 0.060 m and released, launching a ball of mass 0.030 kg vertically upwards. Assume all the elastic potential energy is transferred to the ball, and ignore the small change in height while the ball is in contact with the spring.

**(a)** Calculate the elastic potential energy stored in the compressed spring. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the maximum height the ball would reach above the release point if there were no air resistance. **[2]**

**(c)** The ball actually rises 2.2 m. Calculate the energy dissipated by air resistance. **[2]**

**10.** The same force F is applied to two springs, A and B, both obeying Hooke's law. The spring constant of B is twice that of A. Determine the ratio of the energy stored in A to the energy stored in B. **[2]**

**11.** A steel wire of original length 1.5 m and cross-sectional area 2.0 × 10⁻⁷ m² is stretched by 3.0 mm, within its limit of proportionality. The Young modulus of steel is 2.0 × 10¹¹ Pa.

**(a)** Calculate the force needed to produce this extension. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the elastic potential energy stored in the wire. **[1]**

**12.** The front of a car is designed to deform plastically, rather than elastically, in a collision. Suggest, in terms of energy, why plastic deformation is preferred. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** The point (maximum load or extension) beyond which the material no longer returns to its original shape when the load is removed: deformation becomes **plastic** [1].

**2.** For a small increase in extension ∆x the force is approximately constant, so the work done is **F∆x**, the area of a thin strip under the graph [1]. Adding all the strips gives the **total area**, which is the total work done [1].

**3.** ½kx² = 0.60, so x² = 2 × 0.60 ÷ 120 = 0.010 [1]; x = **0.10 m** [1].

**4.** The wire **returns to its original length** because the elastic limit was not exceeded [1]. The graph is **curved** beyond the limit of proportionality, so k is not constant and the area is not a triangle; the area under the curve must be found instead [1].

**5.** Substituting F = kx: ½Fx = ½(kx)x = ½kx² [1]. Unit: N m⁻¹ × m² = N m = **J** [1].

**6. (a)** ½ × 300 × 0.020² = **0.060 J** [1].

**(b)** ½ × 300 × (0.060² − 0.020²) [1] = 150 × 0.0032 = **0.48 J** [1].

**(c)** The spring is already stretched, so the force over the extra 4.0 cm is **larger** than if it started from zero; the area is a trapezium, not a triangle starting at the origin [1].

**7. (a)** Eₚ = ½Fx = ½ × 60 × 1.2 × 10⁻³ [1] = **0.036 J** [1].

**(b)** Second section = ½ × (60 + 75) × 1.8 × 10⁻³ = 0.1215 J [1]; total = 0.036 + 0.1215 = **0.16 J** (0.158 J) [1].

**(c)** Gradient of first section = 60 ÷ 1.2 × 10⁻³ = 5.0 × 10⁴ N m⁻¹; extension recovered = 75 ÷ 5.0 × 10⁴ = 1.5 mm [1]. Permanent extension = 3.0 − 1.5 = **1.5 mm** [1].

**(d)** Energy recovered = ½ × 75 × 1.5 × 10⁻³ = 0.056 J [1]. Energy dissipated = 0.158 − 0.056 = **0.10 J** [1].

**8. (a)** One square = 0.20 × 2.0 × 10⁻³ = 4.0 × 10⁻⁴ J [1]; work done = 48 × 4.0 × 10⁻⁴ = **0.019 J** [1].

**(b)** **Elastic** deformation, since the band returns to its original length (zero extension) [1]. The area between the curves is the **energy dissipated** (as internal energy) during the cycle and not recovered [1].

**9. (a)** Eₚ = ½kx² = ½ × 450 × 0.060² [1] = **0.81 J** [1].

**(b)** mgh = 0.81, so h = 0.81 ÷ (0.030 × 9.81) [1] = **2.8 m** [1].

**(c)** GPE gained = 0.030 × 9.81 × 2.2 = 0.65 J [1]; energy dissipated = 0.81 − 0.65 = **0.16 J** [1].

**10.** x = F/k, so Eₚ = ½Fx = F²/2k [1]. With the same F, Eₚ ∝ 1/k, so A : B = **2 : 1** [1].

**11. (a)** F = EAx/L [1] = 2.0 × 10¹¹ × 2.0 × 10⁻⁷ × 3.0 × 10⁻³ ÷ 1.5 = **80 N** [1].

**(b)** Eₚ = ½Fx = ½ × 80 × 3.0 × 10⁻³ = **0.12 J** [1].

**12.** In plastic deformation, the work done is largely **dissipated** (as internal energy) rather than stored [1]. Elastic deformation would store the kinetic energy and return it, making the car **rebound** and increasing the forces on the occupants [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using ½k(x₂ − x₁)² for energy added between two extensions (Question 6).
- Treating the whole area under a plastic loading curve as recoverable energy (Question 7).
- Forgetting to convert the energy value of one square into joules (Question 8).
- Assuming the stiffer spring stores more energy under the same force: it extends less, so it stores less (Question 10).
- Leaving N × mm uncorrected, which gives answers a thousand times too large.
- Describing elastic or plastic deformation without saying what happens when the load is removed.
