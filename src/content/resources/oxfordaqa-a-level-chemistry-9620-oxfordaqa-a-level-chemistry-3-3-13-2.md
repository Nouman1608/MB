This guide goes deeper into **3.3.13.2 Proteins**, the second subtopic of section 3.3.13 Amino acids and proteins in the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Chemistry (9620) specification, Version 5.3. It is International A2 content, assessed in Unit 4 (Organic 2 and Physical 2) and in the synoptic Unit 5. This guide takes each skill in turn with new examples.

## What the specification asks for

- Proteins are sequences of amino acids joined by **peptide links**.
- The importance of **hydrogen bonding** and **sulfur–sulfur bonds** in proteins.
- The **primary**, **secondary** (α-helix and β-pleated sheets) and **tertiary** structure of proteins.
- **Hydrolysis** of the peptide link produces the constituent amino acids.
- Amino acids can be separated and identified by **thin-layer chromatography**, located with **ninhydrin** or **ultraviolet light**, and identified by their **Rf values**.
- Skills: draw a peptide of up to three amino acids; draw the amino acids from hydrolysis; identify primary, secondary and tertiary structures in diagrams; explain how they are maintained by hydrogen bonds and S–S bonds; calculate Rf values.

## Skill 1: drawing peptides

A peptide link, –CO–NH–, forms when the –COOH of one amino acid condenses with the –NH₂ of another and water is lost. The order matters: by convention the amino acid written first has the free –NH₂ group.

Two different amino acids can therefore give **two** dipeptides. Glycine (Gly, R = H) and alanine (Ala, R = CH₃) give:

- Gly–Ala: H₂N–CH₂–CO–NH–CH(CH₃)–COOH
- Ala–Gly: H₂N–CH(CH₃)–CO–NH–CH₂–COOH

Three different amino acids, each used once, can give 3 × 2 × 1 = **6** tripeptides.

### Worked example 1: a tripeptide

Draw Gly–Ala–Ser and calculate its Mr. Serine has R = –CH₂OH. Mr: glycine 75.0, alanine 89.0, serine 105.0.

1. Write the backbone three times: H₂N–CH(R)–CO– ... with glycine, then alanine, then serine.
2. Join glycine's C=O to alanine's N, and alanine's C=O to serine's N.

`H₂N–CH₂–CO–NH–CH(CH₃)–CO–NH–CH(CH₂OH)–COOH`

3. Check: one free –NH₂ at the left, one free –COOH at the right, two peptide links, side chains unchanged.
4. Two links means two water molecules lost: Mr = 75.0 + 89.0 + 105.0 − 36.0 = **233.0** (C₈H₁₅N₃O₅).

When drawing displayed formulae, show the peptide link with C=O and N–H explicitly. Linking C to C or O to N is the most common error.

## Skill 2: hydrolysis products

Hydrolysis adds water across each C–N bond of a peptide link: the carbon gets –OH back and the nitrogen gets –H back. In the laboratory the peptide is heated under reflux with concentrated hydrochloric acid, or with aqueous sodium hydroxide. The conditions decide which form of each amino acid you draw.

### Worked example 2: one dipeptide, two conditions

The dipeptide Ala–Ser, H₂N–CH(CH₃)–CO–NH–CH(CH₂OH)–COOH, is completely hydrolysed (a) with excess hydrochloric acid and (b) with excess sodium hydroxide. Draw the products.

| Condition | From alanine | From serine |
|---|---|---|
| Excess acid | ⁺H₃N–CH(CH₃)–COOH | ⁺H₃N–CH(CH₂OH)–COOH |
| Excess alkali | H₂N–CH(CH₃)–COO⁻ | H₂N–CH(CH₂OH)–COO⁻ |

Acid protonates every amino group; alkali deprotonates every carboxyl group, just as for free amino acids. The side-chain –OH is unchanged.

## Skill 3: levels of structure

| Level | What it is | Bonds that maintain it |
|---|---|---|
| Primary | The sequence of amino acids in the chain | Covalent peptide links |
| Secondary | Regular folding of the backbone into an **α-helix** or a **β-pleated sheet** | Hydrogen bonds between C=O and N–H groups of peptide links |
| Tertiary | The overall three-dimensional folding of the whole chain | Interactions between side chains, including hydrogen bonds and **S–S bonds** |

**Recognising diagrams.** A list of amino acids or three-letter codes is primary structure. A coil or spiral with dashed lines running roughly along its axis is an α-helix; parallel strands or arrows linked by dashed lines across them are a β-pleated sheet. A whole chain folded into a compact shape, often with S–S bridges labelled between distant points, is tertiary structure.

## Skill 4: hydrogen bonds and S–S bonds

**Hydrogen bonds.** In a peptide link, nitrogen is more electronegative than hydrogen, so the N–H hydrogen is δ+. The carbonyl oxygen is δ− and has lone pairs. A hydrogen bond forms between the δ+ H of an N–H group and a lone pair on the δ− O of a C=O group in another peptide link.

- In an **α-helix**, the C=O of one peptide link bonds to an N–H further along the same chain, a few residues away, holding the coil in shape.
- In a **β-pleated sheet**, C=O groups on one section of chain bond to N–H groups on a neighbouring section lying alongside it.

**S–S bonds.** The amino acid cysteine has the side chain –CH₂SH. When two cysteine residues come close in the folded chain, their –SH groups can be oxidised to form a **disulfide bridge**:

`–CH₂–SH + HS–CH₂– + [O] → –CH₂–S–S–CH₂– + H₂O`

This is a covalent bond, much stronger than a hydrogen bond, so it holds distant parts of the chain together firmly and helps fix the tertiary structure.

## Skill 5: thin-layer chromatography

After hydrolysis, the amino acids in a protein can be separated and identified by TLC.

1. Draw a baseline in **pencil**, which does not dissolve in the solvent. Spot the hydrolysate and reference amino acids on it.
2. Handle the plate by its edges or wear gloves: fingerprints contain amino acids, which would give extra spots.
3. Stand the plate in a tank with the solvent **below the baseline**, so the spots do not dissolve into the solvent pool. Cover the tank so the air inside is saturated with solvent vapour.
4. Remove the plate before the solvent reaches the top and **mark the solvent front** immediately, before it evaporates.
5. Locate the colourless spots: spray with **ninhydrin** and warm, giving coloured (usually purple) spots; or use a plate containing a fluorescent indicator and **ultraviolet light**, where spots of compounds that absorb UV appear dark.
6. Measure the distances and calculate Rf.

**Rf = distance moved by the spot ÷ distance moved by the solvent front**, both measured from the baseline.

### Worked example 3: identifying amino acids

The hydrolysate of a peptide is run alongside three standards on the same plate. The solvent front is 8.0 cm from the baseline. The hydrolysate gives spots at 2.0 cm and 4.8 cm. On this plate the standards give Rf values of 0.25 (amino acid P), 0.60 (amino acid Q) and 0.80 (amino acid S). Identify the amino acids in the peptide.

1. Spot 1: 2.0 ÷ 8.0 = **0.25**, matching P.
2. Spot 2: 4.8 ÷ 8.0 = **0.60**, matching Q.
3. The peptide contains P and Q but not S.

TLC identifies which amino acids are present; it does not give their order in the chain. Rf values depend on the solvent and the plate, so compare only with standards run under the same conditions, ideally on the same plate.

## Common mistakes

- Linking C to C or N to O when drawing a peptide.
- Subtracting one water for a tripeptide; two links means two waters.
- Drawing neutral amino acids as hydrolysis products when the conditions are acidic or alkaline.
- Saying secondary structure is held by S–S bonds; secondary structure is held by hydrogen bonds between peptide links.
- Saying hydrogen bonds form "between N and O"; the H on N is attracted to a lone pair on O.
- Drawing the baseline in ink, or measuring from the bottom of the plate.
- Giving an Rf value greater than 1, or with units.

## Exam technique

- **Draw** a peptide or its products: count the links, check each end, and apply the conditions to every amino and carboxyl group.
- **Identify** a level of structure: say what feature of the diagram shows it.
- **Explain** how structure is maintained: name the bond, say which groups form it and, for hydrogen bonds, give the partial charges and the lone pair.
- **Calculate** Rf: show both distances, give the answer to two significant figures, and compare with the standards.
