## The city as a system

- **Inputs:** people, energy, food, water, capital.
- **Processes:** the activities of the city that transform inputs.
- **Outputs:** waste, goods, emissions.

Cities change through demographic, economic and political processes.

## Processes of change

| Process | Key idea |
|---|---|
| Rural-urban migration | Push factors (lack of jobs) and pull factors (perceived opportunity), plus natural increase |
| Deindustrialisation | Decline of older industry; growth of services; regeneration and, in some districts, gentrification |
| Suburbanisation | Growth at the edge of the city, with consequences for transport and land use |
| Counter-urbanisation | Movement of people out of cities to smaller settlements |
| Urban sprawl | Unplanned outward spread of built-up land |
| Globalisation | Cities compete for investment and connect through trade, finance and migration |
| Technology | Digital connectivity and the move toward smart cities |

**Gentrification:** wealthier residents move into a formerly lower-income area, raising property
prices and sometimes displacing existing residents.

## Contrasting change

- A city in a **lower-income country** may absorb migrants faster than services and housing expand.
- A **post-industrial city** may face population loss and the need to reinvent its economy.

Consider planning, government policy and how different groups are affected differently.

## Growth calculations

Illustrative city: 2.4 million people, growing at 3.5 per cent a year.

- After 10 years: 2.4 x (1.035)^10, about 2.4 x 1.4106 = 3.385 million.
- Rule of 70: doubling time = 70 / 3.5 = 20 years, so about 4.8 million after 20 years.
- Treat this as a projection, not a forecast, because real growth rates change.

## Exam traps

- Presenting one process as if it affected every city in the same way.
- Forgetting that natural increase, not only migration, drives urban growth.
- Quoting a projection as a certain future.

## Self-test

1. Name two inputs and two outputs of an urban system.
2. Give one push and one pull factor in rural-urban migration.
3. Define gentrification.
4. A city of 1.5 million grows 2 per cent a year. Approximate doubling time?
5. Why might a post-industrial city differ from a fast-growing one?

**Answers:** 1. Inputs such as people and energy; outputs such as waste and emissions. 2. Push: lack of
jobs; pull: perceived opportunity. 3. Wealthier residents moving into a lower-income area, raising
prices and sometimes displacing residents. 4. 70 / 2 = 35 years. 5. It may be losing population and
reinventing its economy, whereas a fast-growing city struggles to expand services.
