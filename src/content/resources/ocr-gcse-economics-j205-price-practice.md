## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Explain what is meant by equilibrium price. **[2]**

**2.** At a price of £12, the quantity demanded is 250 and the quantity supplied is 400. Name and calculate the imbalance in the market. **[2]**

**3.** Explain what is likely to happen to the price in question 2. **[2]**

**4.** State the **three** functions of the price mechanism. **[3]**

**5.** Explain why price is described as a reflection of worth. **[2]**

**6.** State the effect on equilibrium price and equilibrium quantity of a decrease in supply. **[2]**

### Section B: longer and structured questions

**7.** The table shows the weekly market for cycle helmets in an invented city.

| Price (£) | Demand | Supply |
|---|---|---|
| 20 | 700 | 300 |
| 25 | 600 | 400 |
| 30 | 500 | 500 |
| 35 | 400 | 600 |
| 40 | 300 | 700 |

**(a)** State the equilibrium price and quantity. **[1]**
**(b)** State the size of the shortage or surplus at £25. **[1]**
**(c)** A new safety campaign increases demand by 200 at every price. State the new equilibrium price and quantity. **[1]**
**(d)** Calculate the percentage change in the equilibrium price and in the equilibrium quantity. **[2]**

**8.** Better technology increases the supply of laptops while rising incomes increase the demand for them. Analyse the effect on the equilibrium price and quantity of laptops. **[4]**

**9.** Consumers switch from petrol cars to electric cars. Analyse how the price mechanism reallocates resources between the two markets. **[6]**

**10.** Describe the diagram you would draw to show the effect of a fall in the cost of producing solar panels, and state the effect on equilibrium. **[4]**

**11.** Explain **one** reason why allocating a scarce essential good by price may be seen as unfair. **[3]**

**12.** Evaluate whether markets are the best way to determine prices and allocate resources. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** The price at which the quantity demanded equals the quantity supplied [1], so the market clears with no shortage or surplus [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* "Where demand meets supply" is acceptable for one mark; the second needs the idea of no shortage or surplus.

**2.** A surplus (excess supply) [1] of 400 − 250 = **150** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Name the imbalance and give its size; the number alone gains one mark.

**3.** Sellers have unsold stock, so they cut the price [1]. As the price falls, demand extends and supply contracts until the two are equal at equilibrium [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Use "extends" and "contracts", not "demand increases", for movements along the curves.

**4.** Signalling [1]; incentive [1]; rationing [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Learn the three names exactly; descriptions without the names may not score.

**5.** The price shows how much buyers are willing to pay for a good [1], given how much is available, so a higher price indicates buyers value the good highly relative to its supply [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Link value to scarcity, as in the comparison between tap water and a rare painting.

**6.** Equilibrium price rises [1]; equilibrium quantity falls [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Each mark needs the variable and its direction.

**7. (a)** £30 and 500 helmets [1].
**(b)** A shortage of 600 − 400 = **200** [1].
**(c)** £35 and 600 helmets [1].
**(d)** Price: (35 − 30) ÷ 30 × 100 = **+16.7%**; quantity: (600 − 500) ÷ 500 × 100 = **+20%**. One mark for each correct percentage [1] [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* In (c), new demand at £35 is 400 + 200 = 600, which matches supply.

**8.** Supply shifts right, which on its own would lower the price and raise quantity [1]. Demand shifts right, which on its own would raise the price and raise quantity [1]. So the equilibrium quantity of laptops definitely rises [1], but the price could rise, fall or stay the same, depending on whether the increase in demand or in supply is larger [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Never state a definite price change for a double shift unless the question gives figures to decide it.

**9.** Demand for electric cars shifts right, so at the old price there is a shortage and their price rises [1]. The higher price signals that consumers want more electric cars [1] and gives producers an incentive to expand output, so firms invest in battery factories and hire workers [1]. Demand for petrol cars shifts left, so their price falls and producers' profits fall [1]. Firms cut production of petrol cars, releasing workers, factory space and capital [1]. These resources move to producing electric cars, so resources follow consumers' choices without central planning [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Cover both markets and use the function names: signal, incentive and, where relevant, rationing.

**10.** Axes labelled Price and Quantity, with demand D and supply S1 crossing at P1, Q1 [1]. Lower costs shift supply to the right, to S2, shown with an arrow [1]. The new equilibrium is at P2 and Q2 [1]. Price falls and quantity rises [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* The demand curve does not move here; only supply shifts, because costs are a condition of supply.

**11.** Under the price mechanism, goods go to those willing and able to pay [1]. People on low incomes may be unable to afford the good even if they need it most [1], so an essential such as medicine could go to those who value it less but can pay more [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Develop the point with a chain: price, income, who gets the good.

**12.** Markets use prices to signal what consumers want [1] and give firms an incentive to supply it, so resources move quickly to valued uses [1]. Competition between sellers pushes prices down and encourages efficiency [1]. However, rationing by price means people on low incomes may go without essentials [1], and some goods, such as street lighting, may not be provided at all by markets [1]. Adjustment can be slow and painful for workers in declining industries [1]. Judgement: markets are the most effective way to allocate most goods [1], but government action is needed where fairness matters, such as for health care, or where markets fail to provide a good [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* A mixed judgement is strong if it says exactly which kinds of goods suit markets and which do not.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using "demand increases" to describe a movement along the curve.
- Stating a price change for a double shift without evidence.
- Forgetting to give the size of a shortage or surplus.
- Diagram descriptions with no labels or new equilibrium.
- One-sided evaluations of markets.
