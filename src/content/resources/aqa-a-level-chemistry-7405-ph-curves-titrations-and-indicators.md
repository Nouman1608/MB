## What a pH curve shows

A pH curve plots the pH of the flask against the volume of solution added from the burette. Three features matter.

- **The starting pH** depends on whether the solution in the flask is a strong or weak acid or base.
- **The vertical section** is the rapid change in pH near the equivalence point, where the amounts of acid and base are in the ratio in the equation. A small addition of titrant changes the pH by several units.
- **The pH at the equivalence point** depends on the salt formed. Strong acid with strong base gives pH 7 at 298 K. Weak acid with strong base gives a salt that raises pH above 7, and strong acid with weak base gives a salt that lowers it below 7.

## The four curves

The examples below start with 25.0 cm³ of a 0.100 mol dm⁻³ solution in the flask and add a 0.100 mol dm⁻³ solution from the burette, so the equivalence point is at 25.0 cm³ in each case.

### 1. Strong acid and strong base (HCl and NaOH)

- Start: pH 1.00.
- pH rises slowly at first, as the acid is neutralised.
- Vertical section: from about pH 3.7 (24.9 cm³) to about pH 10.3 (25.1 cm³), centred on pH 7.
- After equivalence, pH levels off near the pH of the alkali (13.00 for 0.100 mol dm⁻³ NaOH).

The pH of the solution just before and after equivalence follows from the excess:

```
At 24.9 cm³: excess H⁺ = 0.100 × 0.0001 = 1.0 × 10⁻⁵ mol in 0.0499 dm³
             [H⁺] = 2.0 × 10⁻⁴ mol dm⁻³, pH = 3.70
At 25.1 cm³: excess OH⁻ = 1.0 × 10⁻⁵ mol in 0.0501 dm³
             [OH⁻] = 2.0 × 10⁻⁴ mol dm⁻³, [H⁺] = 5.0 × 10⁻¹¹, pH = 10.30
```

### 2. Weak acid and strong base (ethanoic acid and NaOH)

- Start: pH 2.88 (higher than for a strong acid of the same concentration, because ethanoic acid is only partly dissociated).
- Rises quickly at first, then flattens in the **buffer region**, where the flask holds both CH₃COOH and CH₃COO⁻.
- At half the equivalence volume (12.5 cm³), [HA] = [A⁻], so pH = pKa = 4.76.
- Vertical section: from about pH 7 to about pH 10. It is shorter than for strong–strong.
- Equivalence point: pH about 8.7, above 7, because the ethanoate ion is a base: CH₃COO⁻ + H₂O ⇌ CH₃COOH + OH⁻.
- After equivalence, the pH is controlled by excess NaOH and the curve joins that of strong–strong.

### 3. Strong acid and weak base (HCl added to ammonia)

- Start: pH about 11.1 (lower than for a strong base).
- Falls slowly in a buffer region (NH₃ and NH₄⁺), where pH = pKa of NH₄⁺ (9.25) at half-equivalence.
- Vertical section: from about pH 7 to about pH 4.
- Equivalence point: pH about 5.3, below 7, because NH₄⁺ is a weak acid: NH₄⁺ ⇌ NH₃ + H⁺.
- After equivalence, the pH is controlled by excess HCl.

### 4. Weak acid and weak base (ethanoic acid and ammonia)

- There is **no sharp vertical section**. The curve rises gradually over a wide range of volume around the equivalence point.
- The pH at the equivalence point is close to 7, since both ions hydrolyse to similar extents.
- No simple indicator gives a sharp colour change, so a pH meter is used.

| Combination | Start pH | Vertical section (approx.) | pH at equivalence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Strong acid + strong base | 1 | 3.7 to 10.3 | 7 |
| Weak acid + strong base | 2.9 | 7 to 10 | about 8.7 |
| Strong acid + weak base | 11 | 7 to 4 | about 5.3 |
| Weak acid + weak base | 3 | none | about 7 |

The specification does not ask you to calculate the pH at equivalence for weak–strong pairs, but it does expect you to say whether it is above, below or about 7 and to explain why.

## Indicators

A pH indicator is a weak acid, HIn, whose conjugate base In⁻ has a different colour. It changes colour over a range of about two pH units.

| Indicator | Colour change | pH range |
|---|---|---|
| Methyl orange | Red to yellow | 3.1 to 4.4 |
| Phenolphthalein | Colourless to pink | 8.3 to 10.0 |

**Rule: the indicator's pH range must lie within the vertical section of the curve.** Then a single drop of titrant causes the sharp colour change at the endpoint.

| Titration | Suitable indicator | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Strong acid + strong base | Either | The vertical section (3.7 to 10.3) covers both ranges |
| Weak acid + strong base | Phenolphthalein | Vertical section is above pH 7; methyl orange would change colour in the buffer region, well before equivalence |
| Strong acid + weak base | Methyl orange | Vertical section is below pH 7; phenolphthalein would change colour in the buffer region |
| Weak acid + weak base | None | No vertical section |

## Titration calculations

Use n = c × V (with V in dm³), then the ratio in the balanced equation.

### Worked example 1: unknown alkali

25.0 cm³ of NaOH solution requires a mean titre of 22.40 cm³ of 0.100 mol dm⁻³ HCl for neutralisation.

```
n(HCl) = 0.100 × 0.02240 = 2.24 × 10⁻³ mol
n(NaOH) = 2.24 × 10⁻³ mol   (1:1)
c(NaOH) = 2.24 × 10⁻³ ÷ 0.0250 = 0.0896 mol dm⁻³
```

### Worked example 2: vinegar

25.0 cm³ of vinegar is diluted to 250 cm³. A 25.0 cm³ portion of the diluted solution needs 21.30 cm³ of 0.100 mol dm⁻³ NaOH. Calculate the concentration of the original vinegar and the mass of ethanoic acid (Mᵣ = 60.0) per dm³.

```
n(NaOH) = 0.100 × 0.02130 = 2.13 × 10⁻³ mol
n(CH₃COOH) in 25.0 cm³ portion = 2.13 × 10⁻³ mol (1:1)
c(diluted) = 2.13 × 10⁻³ ÷ 0.0250 = 0.0852 mol dm⁻³
c(original) = 0.0852 × 10 = 0.852 mol dm⁻³
mass per dm³ = 0.852 × 60.0 = 51.1 g dm⁻³
```

### Worked example 3: finding Ka from the curve

In a titration of 25.0 cm³ of a weak acid with 0.100 mol dm⁻³ NaOH, the equivalence point is at 25.0 cm³. The pH after adding 12.5 cm³ is 4.35. At this half-equivalence point pH = pKa, so Ka = 10^(–4.35) = 4.47 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³.

## Required practical 9: pH changes in titrations

1. Calibrate the pH meter with buffer solutions and rinse the electrode between measurements.
2. Place a measured volume (for example 25.0 cm³) of acid in a beaker, with the electrode in it. Stir.
3. Add the alkali from a burette in 1 cm³ portions at first, and in drops of 0.1 cm³ or 0.2 cm³ near the equivalence point where pH changes rapidly.
4. Record pH after each addition and plot the curve.
5. Find the equivalence point from the middle of the vertical section.

Take extra readings near the vertical section because it is the most informative part. Note the total volume of solution changes after each addition, which is why concentrations are recalculated if you work out the pH.

## Common mistakes

- Drawing the vertical section centred on pH 7 for every titration.
- Choosing an indicator by its colour rather than by its pH range.
- Saying methyl orange is suitable for a weak acid–strong base titration.
- Forgetting the buffer region of a weak acid or weak base curve.
- Using the burette volume only, rather than total volume, in pH calculations during a titration.
- Reading the equivalence point from the top or bottom of the vertical section instead of its midpoint.
- Suggesting an indicator for a weak acid and weak base titration.
- Labelling the starting pH of a weak acid curve as 1.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Sketch a curve**: label both axes, mark the starting pH, the vertical section and its approximate pH values, and the final pH.
- **Explain the shape**: refer to the strengths of the acid and base, the buffer region and the pH of the salt at equivalence.
- **Select an indicator**: state the pH range of the vertical section and show that the indicator's range falls within it.
- **Calculate a concentration**: moles of titrant, mole ratio, moles in the sample, concentration.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why does pH change so quickly at equivalence?**
Near equivalence, a very small amount of acid or base is left, and one drop of titrant changes the concentration of H⁺ or OH⁻ by a large factor. Because pH is logarithmic, this gives a change of several units.

**Why is pH at equivalence not always 7?**
The salt formed can react with water. A salt of a weak acid gives a basic solution, and a salt of a weak base gives an acidic one.

**Can phenolphthalein be used for any titration with sodium hydroxide?**
No. It is suitable when the vertical section covers pH 8.3 to 10.0, so it works for strong acid–strong base and weak acid–strong base, but not when the acid is weak and the base is weak.
