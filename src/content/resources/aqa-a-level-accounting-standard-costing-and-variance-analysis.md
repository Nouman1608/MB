## 1. Standard costing: purpose and terms

A **standard cost** is a predetermined cost of one unit of output, set in advance from expected
quantities and prices under planned working conditions. It is built up on a **standard cost card**:
the standard quantity of material at a standard price, plus the standard hours of labour at a
standard rate.

**Variance analysis** compares actual results with standards, measures each difference, splits it
into a price element and a quantity element, and explains the causes so that managers can act.

- **Favourable (F):** the difference increases profit (cost below standard, revenue above budget).
- **Adverse (A):** the difference reduces profit.

The **purpose** of standard costing is control. Managers set targets, measure performance against
them, and investigate only the significant differences. This is **management by exception**.

### Advantages

- **Control and responsibility.** Each variance can be traced to a manager: the buyer for materials
  price, the production supervisor for usage and efficiency, the personnel function for pay rates,
  the sales manager for price and volume.
- **Management by exception.** Managers spend time only on significant variances.
- **Planning.** Standards provide the unit costs for budgets and help with pricing and quotations.
- **Motivation.** Agreed, attainable standards give staff a clear target.
- **Inventory valuation.** Inventory can be valued at standard cost, which simplifies record keeping.
- **Cost awareness.** Setting standards makes managers examine methods and find waste.

### Disadvantages

- **Cost and time** to set and update standards, and to calculate and investigate variances.
- **Out-of-date standards.** Prices and methods change, so old standards produce misleading variances.
- **Unsuitable for some businesses.** Standards suit repetitive production of identical units, not
  one-off jobs or many services.
- **Demotivation.** Standards that are too tight produce constant adverse variances; standards
  imposed without consultation are resented.
- **Wrong behaviour.** A buyer chasing a favourable price variance may buy poor material that
  causes adverse usage and efficiency variances elsewhere.
- **Timing.** Variances are reported after the period, so problems may already have cost money.
- **Non-financial factors** such as quality, delivery and customer satisfaction are not measured.

## 2. Calculating variances

Use one business throughout. **Brackwell Joinery Ltd** makes garden benches.

| Standard cost card, per bench | £ |
|---|---|
| Timber: 12 kg at £2.50 per kg | 30.00 |
| Labour: 3 hours at £14.00 per hour | 42.00 |
| Standard variable cost | 72.00 |
| Standard selling price | 150.00 |
| Standard contribution | 78.00 |

Budget: 800 benches; fixed overheads £40 000. Budgeted profit = 800 × £78 − £40 000 = **£22 400**.

Actual: 850 benches made and sold at £146 each. Timber used 10 400 kg, costing £27 040. Labour 2 450
hours, costing £35 525. Fixed overheads £41 200.

Work out actual unit prices first: timber £27 040 ÷ 10 400 = £2.60 per kg; labour £35 525 ÷ 2 450 =
£14.50 per hour.

### Materials variances

```
Price variance = (standard price − actual price) × actual quantity
               = (2.50 − 2.60) × 10 400 = £1 040 A

Usage variance = (standard quantity for actual output − actual quantity) × standard price
  Standard quantity = 850 × 12 = 10 200 kg
               = (10 200 − 10 400) × 2.50 = £500 A

Total materials variance = standard cost of actual output − actual cost
               = (850 × 30) − 27 040 = 25 500 − 27 040 = £1 540 A   (1 040 A + 500 A)
```

The price variance uses **actual** quantity. The usage variance uses **standard** price, so it
measures the quantity effect alone. Standard quantity is always for the **actual** output, not the
budgeted output.

### Labour variances

```
Rate variance       = (standard rate − actual rate) × actual hours
                    = (14.00 − 14.50) × 2 450 = £1 225 A

Efficiency variance = (standard hours for actual output − actual hours) × standard rate
  Standard hours    = 850 × 3 = 2 550 hours
                    = (2 550 − 2 450) × 14.00 = £1 400 F

Total labour variance = (850 × 42) − 35 525 = 35 700 − 35 525 = £175 F   (1 225 A + 1 400 F)
```

The total labour variance is small, but it hides two large variances pulling in opposite directions.
That is why the total is split.

### Sales variances

```
Sales price variance  = (actual price − standard price) × actual units sold
                      = (146 − 150) × 850 = £3 400 A

Sales volume variance = (actual units sold − budgeted units) × standard contribution per unit
                      = (850 − 800) × 78 = £3 900 F
```

The volume variance is valued at standard contribution, not selling price, because each extra bench
also carries £72 of standard variable cost. This page keeps fixed overheads as a separate line, so
standard contribution makes the profit reconciliation add up. If a question gives a standard profit
per unit and asks you to use it, follow the question.

### Interpreting the variances

- **Materials price £1 040 A:** timber cost 10p a kg more. Possible causes: a supplier price rise,
  a better grade of timber, or an urgent purchase at short notice. A general price rise is outside
  the buyer's control.
- **Materials usage £500 A:** 200 kg more timber than standard. Possible causes: a warped batch,
  careless cutting, or untrained staff.
- **Labour rate £1 225 A:** 50p an hour more. Possible causes: a pay award not built into the
  standard, overtime premiums, or more skilled staff.
- **Labour efficiency £1 400 F:** 100 hours saved. Possible causes: new jigs, experienced staff,
  or good-quality timber that was easy to work.
- **Sales price £3 400 A and sales volume £3 900 F:** a £4 price cut may have won the extra 50
  sales. Together they add £500 to profit, so the cut paid off, just.

## 3. The interrelationship between variances

Variances are not independent. One decision often shows up in several places, which is why you should
look at the pattern before blaming a manager.

| Cause | Likely variances |
|---|---|
| Cheaper, lower-grade materials | Materials price F; materials usage A (more waste); labour efficiency A (harder to work) |
| Higher-grade materials | Materials price A; materials usage F; labour efficiency F |
| Less skilled, lower-paid staff | Labour rate F; labour efficiency A; materials usage A (more mistakes) |
| More skilled, higher-paid staff | Labour rate A; labour efficiency F; materials usage F |
| Selling price cut | Sales price A; sales volume F (if demand responds) |
| Selling price rise | Sales price F; sales volume A |
| Bulk buying for a discount | Materials price F; may raise storage costs and wastage |

At Brackwell, labour rate A with efficiency F fits better-paid, more skilled staff. The adverse usage
does not fit that story, so it needs a separate investigation. The general rule: a favourable
variance is not automatically good news, and an adverse one is not automatically a failure. Judge
the variances together, and against their effect on profit.

## 4. Reconciliation statements

### Budgeted cost and actual cost

To reconcile cost, start from the **standard (budgeted) cost of the actual output**. Comparing the
original budget for 800 benches with actual cost for 850 would mix volume with efficiency.

| Brackwell Joinery Ltd: cost reconciliation | £ | £ |
|---|---|---|
| Standard cost of actual output: 850 × £72 + £40 000 fixed overheads | | 101 200 |
| Materials price variance | 1 040 A | |
| Materials usage variance | 500 A | |
| Labour rate variance | 1 225 A | |
| Labour efficiency variance | 1 400 F | |
| Fixed overhead expenditure (£41 200 − £40 000) | 1 200 A | |
| Net adverse cost variances | | 2 565 A |
| **Actual cost** (27 040 + 35 525 + 41 200) | | **103 765** |

Adverse cost variances are **added** to standard cost; favourable ones are **deducted**.

### Budgeted profit and actual profit

Actual profit = £124 100 revenue − £27 040 − £35 525 − £41 200 = **£20 335**.

| Brackwell Joinery Ltd: profit reconciliation | £ F | £ A | £ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Budgeted profit | | | 22 400 |
| Sales volume variance | 3 900 | | |
| Sales price variance | | 3 400 | |
| Materials price variance | | 1 040 | |
| Materials usage variance | | 500 | |
| Labour rate variance | | 1 225 | |
| Labour efficiency variance | 1 400 | | |
| Fixed overhead expenditure | | 1 200 | |
| Totals | 5 300 | 7 365 | 2 065 A |
| **Actual profit** | | | **20 335** |

In a profit reconciliation, favourable variances are **added** and adverse ones **deducted**: the
opposite way round from a cost reconciliation. Always check that your final line equals the actual
profit you calculated separately. If it does not, a variance is wrong.

Interpretation for the directors: profit fell £2 065 short of budget even though volume beat budget.
The main problems were timber (£1 540 A in total) and the labour pay rate. The efficient workforce
recovered most of the extra pay. Managers should check whether the timber price rise will last; if
it will, the standard should be updated.

## Common errors

- Using budgeted output instead of actual output for standard quantity or standard hours.
- Mixing prices: the usage and efficiency variances use **standard** price or rate.
- Valuing the sales volume variance at selling price instead of standard contribution (or the
  standard profit the question gives).
- Using budgeted units in the sales price variance; it uses actual units sold.
- Leaving out F or A, or labelling a cost saving "adverse".
- Adding adverse variances to budgeted profit in a profit reconciliation.
- Interpreting each variance alone and missing the link between, for example, cheap materials and
  poor efficiency.
- Listing causes without a judgement when a question asks you to evaluate.
