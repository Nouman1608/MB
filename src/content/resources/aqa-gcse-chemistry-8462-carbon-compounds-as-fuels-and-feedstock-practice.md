These questions cover section **4.7.1 Carbon compounds as fuels and feedstock** of the AQA GCSE Chemistry (8462)
specification, for teaching from September 2016. The content is assessed on Paper 2 at both Foundation and
Higher Tier. The specification marks nothing in this section (HT only), so no question is labelled Higher tier
only.

[carbon compounds as fuels and feedstock study guide](/resources/aqa-gcse-chemistry-8462-carbon-compounds-as-fuels-and-feedstock/)

## Questions

**1.** Alkanes form a homologous series with the general formula CₙH₂ₙ₊₂.

**(a)** Write the molecular formula of the alkane with seven carbon atoms. **[1]**
**(b)** A hydrocarbon has the formula C₅H₁₀. Explain why it is not an alkane. **[2]**
**(c)** Name the first four members of the alkane series. **[2]**

**2.** Crude oil is a mixture of hydrocarbons.

**(a)** State what is meant by a hydrocarbon. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain what is meant by saying crude oil is a mixture. **[2]**
**(c)** Describe how crude oil was formed. **[2]**

**3.** Crude oil is separated into fractions in a fractionating column.

**(a)** State the name of this process. **[1]**
**(b)** Describe and explain how the process separates the hydrocarbons, using the words evaporates, condenses and boiling point. **[4]**
**(c)** State the position in the column where the fraction with the longest molecules is collected. **[1]**

**4.** Fraction X contains hydrocarbons with 6 to 8 carbon atoms. Fraction Y contains hydrocarbons with 20 to 30 carbon atoms.

Compare the properties of the two fractions by completing the sentences, using "higher" or "lower", "more" or "less". **[3]**

Fraction Y has a ___ boiling point than X. It is ___ viscous than X. It is ___ flammable than X.

**5.** Write a balanced equation for the complete combustion of each hydrocarbon.

**(a)** Propane, C₃H₈ **[2]**
**(b)** Pentane, C₅H₁₂ **[2]**
**(c)** Hexane, C₆H₁₄ (use whole numbers) **[3]**

**6.** Dodecane, C₁₂H₂₆, can be cracked to make octane and one other product.

**(a)** Work out the molecular formula of the other product and state the type of hydrocarbon it is. **[2]**
**(b)** Write the balanced equation for this cracking reaction. **[1]**
**(c)** Describe the conditions used for catalytic cracking. **[2]**
**(d)** Give **two** reasons why cracking is useful. **[2]**

**7.** A student has two unlabelled gas jars. One contains ethane and the other contains ethene.

**(a)** Describe a test to tell them apart. **[1]**
**(b)** State the result for each gas. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why ethene reacts but ethane does not. **[1]**

**8.** Petrol is mainly made of molecules with 5 to 10 carbon atoms. Bitumen is made of molecules with more than 50 carbon atoms.

**(a)** Explain why petrol is a better fuel for a car engine than bitumen. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain, in terms of molecule size, why bitumen is found at the bottom of the fractionating column. **[2]**

**9.** The petrochemical industry uses fractions from crude oil as feedstock.

**(a)** State what is meant by feedstock. **[1]**
**(b)** Give **two** types of useful product made from crude oil other than fuels. **[2]**

**10.** A student cracks tetradecane: C₁₄H₃₀ → C₁₀H₂₂ + 2C₂H₄.

**(a)** Show that the equation is balanced by counting atoms. **[2]**
**(b)** Name the alkene formed. **[1]**
**(c)** Describe what you would see when the alkene is shaken with bromine water. **[1]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** C₇H₁₆ [1] (2 × 7 + 2 = 16)
**(b)** An alkane with 5 carbons would have 2 × 5 + 2 = **12** hydrogens [1]; C₅H₁₀ has **two fewer** hydrogens, so it has a C=C double bond / is an alkene [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** **Methane, ethane, propane, butane** [2 marks for all four; 1 for two or three]. **[2]**

**2. (a)** A compound made of **hydrogen and carbon atoms only** [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** It contains **many different compounds** [1] that are **not chemically joined** together [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** From the remains of **ancient plankton** (biomass) [1] that was **buried in mud** and changed over millions of years [1]. **[2]**

**3. (a)** **Fractional distillation** [1]
**(b)** The oil is **heated** and **evaporates** [1]; the vapour rises up a column that is **hotter at the bottom and cooler at the top** [1]; each hydrocarbon **condenses** where the temperature is below its **boiling point** [1]; molecules with **similar boiling points** are collected together as a fraction [1]. **[4]**
**(c)** At the **bottom** of the column [1]. **[1]**

**4.** **Higher** boiling point [1]; **more** viscous [1]; **less** flammable [1]. **[3]**

**5. (a)** C₃H₈ + 5O₂ → 3CO₂ + 4H₂O [correct formulae 1; balanced 1] **[2]**
**(b)** C₅H₁₂ + 8O₂ → 5CO₂ + 6H₂O [formulae 1; balanced 1] **[2]**
**(c)** 2C₆H₁₄ + 19O₂ → 12CO₂ + 14H₂O [formulae 1; carbon and hydrogen balanced 1; oxygen balanced 1] **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Balance carbon first, then hydrogen, then oxygen. For 6 carbons and 14 hydrogens, oxygen atoms needed are 12 + 7 = 19 for one molecule, which is 9.5 O₂, so double everything.

**6. (a)** Carbon: 12 − 8 = 4; hydrogen: 26 − 18 = 8, so **C₄H₈** [1]; an **alkene** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** C₁₂H₂₆ → C₈H₁₈ + C₄H₈ [1]
**(c)** The vaporised hydrocarbon is passed over a hot **catalyst** [1] at a **high temperature** [1]. **[2]**
**(d)** Any two [1] each: makes smaller molecules for fuels such as petrol, which are in high demand; makes alkenes for polymers; matches supply to demand. **[2]**

**7. (a)** Shake each gas with **bromine water** [1].
**(b)** Ethene: bromine water changes from **orange to colourless** [1]; ethane: it **stays orange** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Ethene is **unsaturated**, with a C=C double bond, so it is more reactive than ethane (which has only single bonds) [1]. **[1]**

**8. (a)** Petrol has **smaller molecules**, so it is **more flammable** / easier to ignite and burns more easily [1]; bitumen is very **viscous**, with a very high boiling point, so it does not vaporise and burn in an engine [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Bitumen has **very large molecules** with a **very high boiling point** [1], so it **condenses first**, at the bottom where it is hottest [1]. **[2]**

**9. (a)** A raw material used to make other chemicals [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** Any two [1] each: polymers (plastics); solvents; lubricants; detergents. **[2]**

**10. (a)** Left: C = 14, H = 30 [1]. Right: C = 10 + 2 × 2 = 14, H = 22 + 2 × 4 = 30 [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** **Ethene** [1]
**(c)** The bromine water changes from **orange to colourless** [1]

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing the trend without the direction, for example "boiling point changes".
- Saying large molecules "evaporate" at the top of the column.
- Leaving oxygen unbalanced or leaving a fractional coefficient in a combustion equation.
- Writing that bromine water "goes clear".
- Confusing crude oil (a mixture) with a single compound.
