This study guide goes deeper into section 4.5, Construction, of topic 4 (Geometry and trigonometry) in the Pearson Edexcel International GCSE Mathematics A (4MA1) specification, Issue 2 (November 2017). Section 4.5 is Foundation Tier content, and the Higher Tier entry says "See Foundation Tier", so both tiers need it. Bring a sharp pencil, a ruler marked in millimetres, a protractor and a pair of compasses that hold their setting.

The [Geometry and trigonometry study guide](/resources/edexcel-igcse-maths-4ma1-geometry-and-trigonometry/) lists the bisector steps briefly. This page explains why each construction works, how to choose the right method for a triangle, and how to run a scale drawing problem from start to finish. Pair it with the [Construction revision notes](/resources/edexcel-igcse-mathematics-4ma1-construction-edexcel-igcse-maths-revision-notes/) and the [Construction practice questions](/resources/edexcel-igcse-mathematics-4ma1-construction-edexcel-igcse-maths-practice/).

## What the specification asks

| Statement | Students should be taught to |
|---|---|
| 4.5 A | measure and draw lines to the nearest millimetre |
| 4.5 B | construct triangles and other two-dimensional shapes using a combination of a ruler, a protractor and compasses |
| 4.5 C | solve problems using scale drawings |
| 4.5 D | use straight edge and compasses to (i) construct the perpendicular bisector of a line segment and (ii) construct the bisector of an angle |

Statement D says **straight edge and compasses**. A protractor must not be used for those two constructions, and the arcs are the evidence that you used the right method.

## 4.5 A: Accurate lengths

A length "to the nearest millimetre" is given in centimetres with one decimal place, such as 6.4 cm, or in millimetres, such as 64 mm. Start measuring from the 0 mark, not from the end of the ruler, which is often a few millimetres before 0. When you draw a line of a given length, mark both ends with a short cross line so the endpoints are clear.

## 4.5 B: Constructing triangles

A triangle is fixed by three pieces of information. Which tools you use depends on what you are given.

| Given | Name | Tools |
|---|---|---|
| Three sides | SSS | Ruler and compasses |
| Two sides and the angle between them | SAS | Ruler and protractor |
| Two angles and the side between them | ASA | Ruler and protractor |

### Three sides (SSS)

**Worked example 1.** Construct triangle ABC with AB = 7.5 cm, BC = 6.2 cm and AC = 5.0 cm.

1. Draw AB = 7.5 cm with a ruler. Choose the longest side as the base; it keeps the drawing on the page.
2. Set the compasses to 5.0 cm (the length of AC). With the point on A, draw an arc above AB.
3. Set the compasses to 6.2 cm (the length of BC). With the point on B, draw an arc crossing the first.
4. The crossing point is C. Join AC and BC with a ruler. Leave the arcs.

Check: measure AC and BC. Then measure angle ACB; for these lengths it should be about 83°. If you are far out, one of the arcs was drawn from the wrong end.

**Can every set of three lengths make a triangle?** No. The two shorter sides must add to more than the longest side. With 3 cm, 4 cm and 8 cm, the arcs of radius 3 cm and 4 cm drawn from the ends of an 8 cm line never meet, because 3 + 4 < 8.

### Two sides and the included angle (SAS)

**Worked example 2.** Construct triangle PQR with PQ = 7 cm, angle QPR = 50° and PR = 5.5 cm.

1. Draw PQ = 7 cm.
2. Put the protractor on P, measure 50° from PQ and mark the point.
3. Draw a line from P through the mark and measure 5.5 cm along it to fix R.
4. Join QR. It should measure about 5.5 cm.

### Two angles and the included side (ASA)

**Worked example 3.** Construct triangle XYZ with XY = 8 cm, angle YXZ = 40° and angle XYZ = 65°.

Draw XY. Measure 40° at X and 65° at Y, both on the same side of XY, and draw long lines. Where they cross is Z. The third angle must be 180° − 40° − 65° = 75°, so measuring angle XZY is a quick check. XZ, which is opposite the 65° angle, should be about 7.5 cm, and YZ about 5.3 cm.

### Other shapes

The same tools build quadrilaterals and polygons.

- **Equilateral triangle and a 60° angle.** Draw a side, then arcs of the same radius from both ends. Each angle of the triangle is 60°, so this gives a 60° angle without a protractor.
- **Rhombus with side 5 cm and an angle of 60°.** Draw a 60° angle with arms 5 cm long, then from the end of each arm draw an arc of radius 5 cm; they cross at the fourth vertex. The short diagonal is also 5 cm (the rhombus splits into two equilateral triangles) and the long diagonal measures about 8.7 cm.
- **Rectangle or square.** Use the protractor for right angles, or construct them with a perpendicular bisector.

## 4.5 D: The two straight-edge-and-compasses constructions

### Perpendicular bisector of a line segment AB

1. Open the compasses to more than half of AB.
2. With the point on A, draw an arc above and below the line.
3. Without changing the setting, repeat from B.
4. Join the two crossing points with a ruler, extending the line beyond them.

**Why it works.** Each crossing point is the same distance from A as from B, because both arcs had the same radius. A, the upper point, B and the lower point form a rhombus, and the diagonals of a rhombus bisect each other at right angles. So the line meets AB at its midpoint, at 90°. Every point on the perpendicular bisector is the same distance from A as from B.

### Bisector of angle ABC

1. With the point on B, draw an arc that crosses both arms. Call the crossing points P and Q.
2. With the point on P, draw an arc inside the angle. With the same setting, draw an arc from Q that crosses it.
3. Join B to the crossing point.

**Why it works.** BP = BQ, and the two inner arcs are equal, so B, P, the crossing point and Q form a kite. The diagonal from B of a kite is a line of symmetry, so it splits angle ABC into two equal parts.

**Combining constructions.** Bisect a 60° angle (from an equilateral triangle) to get 30°. Bisect a straight angle of 180°, which is the same as constructing a perpendicular, to get 90°, then bisect again to get 45°.

## 4.5 C: Scale drawings

A scale is written as a ratio (1 : 200) or in words (1 cm represents 2 m). Both mean that real lengths are 200 times the lengths on the drawing. Angles are **not** scaled: a 35° angle on a plan is 35° in real life.

**Worked example 4.** A garden is a rectangle 18 m by 12 m. Draw it to a scale of 1 : 200.

```
18 m = 1800 cm   1800 ÷ 200 = 9 cm
12 m = 1200 cm   1200 ÷ 200 = 6 cm
```

Draw a 9 cm by 6 cm rectangle. A tree 7 m from one corner along the long side goes 700 ÷ 200 = 3.5 cm from that corner.

**Worked example 5 (map).** On a map with scale 1 : 20 000, two towers are 4.6 cm apart. The real distance is 4.6 × 20 000 = 92 000 cm = 920 m. In the other direction, at a scale of 1 : 40 000, a real distance of 3.3 km is 330 000 cm ÷ 40 000 = 8.25 cm on the map.

Convert to the same unit before dividing. Changing km to cm means multiplying by 100 000.

**Worked example 6 (bearings by scale drawing).** A boat sails 6 km from harbour H on a bearing of 060° to a buoy B. It then sails 4 km on a bearing of 150° to a point C. Using a scale of 1 cm to 1 km, find the distance and bearing of C from H.

1. Mark H and draw a north line. Measure 60° clockwise from north and draw HB = 6 cm.
2. At B, draw a new north line parallel to the first. Measure 150° clockwise and draw BC = 4 cm.
3. Join HC and measure it: about 7.2 cm, so about **7.2 km**.
4. Measure the angle clockwise from the north line at H to HC: about **094°**.

You can check the drawing: the two bearings differ by 90°, so angle HBC is a right angle and HC = √(6² + 4²) ≈ 7.21 km. A measured answer within a millimetre or a degree of this shows an accurate drawing.

## Exam technique

- **"Use ruler and compasses only"** or **"show all your construction lines"**: a bisector with no arcs scores nothing, however accurate it looks.
- **"Make an accurate drawing"**: measure carefully, and leave arcs and protractor marks visible.
- **"Use the scale drawing to find"**: give the measured length converted to real units, with the unit.
- Draw lightly but clearly, and use a sharp pencil; a thick line can hide a 1 mm error.

## Common mistakes

- Using a protractor to "bisect" an angle when the question asks for compasses.
- Opening the compasses to less than half of AB, so the arcs do not meet.
- Changing the compass setting between the two ends of a perpendicular bisector.
- Drawing an arc for an SSS triangle with the radius of the wrong side.
- Scaling an angle as well as the lengths.
- Measuring a bearing from the second point's north line when the question asks for the bearing from the start.
