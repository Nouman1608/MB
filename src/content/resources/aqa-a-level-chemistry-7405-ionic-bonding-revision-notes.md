These notes condense section **3.1.3.1 Ionic bonding** of the AQA AS and A-level Chemistry specification (7404/7405), for teaching from September 2015. It is AS content, examined on Papers 1 and 2 (and Paper 3). The [Ionic bonding study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-ionic-bonding/) explains each idea with worked examples, and the [Ionic bonding practice questions](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-ionic-bonding-practice/) give exam-style practice.

## What the specification asks

- Ionic bonding is **electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions in a lattice**.
- Know the **formulas of compound ions**: sulfate, hydroxide, nitrate, carbonate and ammonium.
- **Predict the charge** on a simple ion from the element's position in the Periodic Table.
- **Construct formulas** for ionic compounds.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Ion | An atom or group of atoms with an overall electric charge |
| Cation / anion | Positive ion / negative ion |
| Ionic bond | Electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions |
| Lattice | Regular three-dimensional arrangement of ions |
| Compound ion | Group of covalently bonded atoms carrying an overall charge |
| Formula unit | Simplest ratio of ions in an ionic compound |
| Isoelectronic | Having the same number and arrangement of electrons |

## Simple ion charges

| Group | 1 | 2 | 13 | 15 | 16 | 17 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ion charge | 1+ | 2+ | 3+ | 3− | 2− | 1− |
| Example | Li⁺ | Ca²⁺ | Al³⁺ | N³⁻ | S²⁻ | Br⁻ |

- Metals lose electrons to form positive ions with the same configuration as a noble gas.
- Non-metals gain electrons to form negative ions.
- Transition metals give more than one ion; the Roman numeral gives the charge (copper(II) is Cu²⁺).

## Compound ions

| Name | Formula |
|---|---|
| Hydroxide | OH⁻ |
| Nitrate | NO₃⁻ |
| Carbonate | CO₃²⁻ |
| Sulfate | SO₄²⁻ |
| Ammonium | NH₄⁺ |

## Writing a formula

1. Write each ion and its charge.
2. Find the smallest numbers that make positive charge equal negative charge.
3. Brackets only round a compound ion that is needed more than once.

| Compound | Ions | Formula |
|---|---|---|
| Calcium nitrate | Ca²⁺, NO₃⁻ | Ca(NO₃)₂ |
| Aluminium hydroxide | Al³⁺, OH⁻ | Al(OH)₃ |
| Ammonium sulfate | NH₄⁺, SO₄²⁻ | (NH₄)₂SO₄ |
| Sodium carbonate | Na⁺, CO₃²⁻ | Na₂CO₃ |
| Magnesium nitride | Mg²⁺, N³⁻ | Mg₃N₂ |

## Properties and explanations

| Property | Explanation |
|---|---|
| High melting point | Strong attractions in all directions in a giant lattice; much energy needed |
| Brittle | Layers shift and like charges meet, so they repel and the crystal splits |
| Insulator as a solid | Ions fixed in the lattice |
| Conductor when molten or dissolved | Ions free to move |
| Often soluble in water | Polar water molecules attract the ions |

## Strength of ionic bonding

The attraction is greater when:

- the ionic **charges are larger**, and
- the **ionic radii are smaller**.

| Compound | Ions | Relative melting point | Reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| NaCl | 1+, 1− | Lower (801 °C) | Smaller charges, larger ions |
| MgO | 2+, 2− | Higher (about 2850 °C) | Larger charges, smaller ions |

Answer structure: name the **ions**, the **electrostatic attraction**, the **factor** (charge or radius), and the **energy needed**.

## Worked reminders

**Formula.** Iron(III) oxide: Fe³⁺ and O²⁻. Lowest common multiple of 3 and 2 is 6, so 2 Fe³⁺ and 3 O²⁻: **Fe₂O₃**.

**Charge from formula.** In Cr₂(SO₄)₃ three sulfates give −6, so each of two chromium ions is +3.

**Explanation.** Why does MgCl₂ conduct only when molten? In the solid the Mg²⁺ and Cl⁻ ions are held in the lattice and cannot move; when molten they are free to move and carry charge.

## Lattice and structure detail

- In sodium chloride each Na⁺ is surrounded by six Cl⁻ and each Cl⁻ by six Na⁺ (6 : 6 co-ordination). The bonding is non-directional, so it acts equally in all directions.
- A formula such as NaCl is a **formula unit**, the simplest ratio. There is no separate "NaCl molecule".
- Ionic radius: a positive ion is smaller than its parent atom (it has lost its outer shell). A negative ion is larger than its parent atom (more electron repulsion with the same nuclear charge).
- Within a group the ions get larger going down (Li⁺ < Na⁺ < K⁺), so lattice attractions weaken.
- Isoelectronic ions (N³⁻, O²⁻, F⁻, Na⁺, Mg²⁺, Al³⁺) all have 1s² 2s² 2p⁶. Among them the one with the most protons is the smallest, because the same ten electrons are pulled in by a larger nuclear charge.

## Dot-and-cross checklist

1. Draw the outer shell of each atom before transfer, using dots for one atom and crosses for the other.
2. Show the transfer, then draw each ion in square brackets.
3. Put the charge at the top right outside the bracket.
4. Show the right number of each ion (for example, two [Na]⁺ for Na₂O).
5. Draw only the outer shell of each ion, which is full after transfer.

## Why ionic compounds dissolve

Water is polar. The δ⁻ oxygen atoms are attracted to cations and the δ⁺ hydrogen atoms to anions. If these attractions outweigh the lattice attractions, the compound dissolves. Compounds with very large ionic charges (for example MgO) are generally insoluble because the lattice is too strong.

## Common mistakes

- Unbalanced charges, such as MgCl or AlO.
- Brackets round a compound ion needed only once, or missing when two are needed.
- Saying ionic compounds contain "molecules".
- Saying "electrons are free to move" for molten ionic compounds.
- Comparing lattice strength without mentioning both charge and size.
- Confusing "brittle" with "weak".

## Self-test

1. Give the charge on an ion of (a) Group 2, (b) Group 16.
2. State the formula of the sulfate ion and the ammonium ion.
3. Write the formula of aluminium sulfate.
4. Write the formula of potassium nitrate.
5. Write the formula of calcium hydroxide.
6. Deduce the charge on iron in FeCO₃.
7. Define ionic bonding.
8. Explain why solid sodium chloride does not conduct electricity.
9. Explain why magnesium oxide has a higher melting point than sodium chloride.
10. Explain why ionic crystals are brittle.
11. Which ions have the electron configuration 1s² 2s² 2p⁶: K⁺, Mg²⁺, O²⁻, Cl⁻?
12. Name the ions present in (NH₄)₃PO₄, using the ammonium ion and the phosphate ion PO₄³⁻. Write the ratio of ions.

### Answers

1. (a) 2+; (b) 2−.
2. SO₄²⁻ and NH₄⁺.
3. Al₂(SO₄)₃.
4. KNO₃.
5. Ca(OH)₂.
6. Carbonate is 2−, so iron is **2+** (iron(II)).
7. Electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions in a lattice.
8. The ions are held in fixed positions in the lattice, so there are no mobile charged particles.
9. Mg²⁺ and O²⁻ have larger charges and smaller radii than Na⁺ and Cl⁻, so the electrostatic attraction is stronger and more energy is needed to separate the ions.
10. A blow moves a layer so ions of the same charge become neighbours; they repel and the crystal splits.
11. **Mg²⁺ and O²⁻**. (K⁺ and Cl⁻ have the argon configuration.)
12. NH₄⁺ and PO₄³⁻ in the ratio **3 : 1**.
