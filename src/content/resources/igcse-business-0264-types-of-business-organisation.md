## Ownership and liability: the key ideas

Each type of organisation answers three questions. Who owns it? Who is responsible for its debts? How can it raise money?

**Liability** means legal responsibility for the debts of the business. With **unlimited liability** the owner can be forced to pay business debts from personal assets such as savings or a house. With **limited liability** an owner can lose only the money they put into the business. Creditors cannot claim the owner's other property.

A company is treated in law as separate from its owners. A sole trader or partnership is not.

## Sole traders

A **sole trader** is a business owned and usually controlled by one person. The owner may employ staff.

**Advantages**
- Easy and cheap to set up, with few legal formalities.
- The owner keeps all the profit, takes decisions quickly and has complete control.

**Disadvantages**
- Unlimited liability.
- Finance is limited to personal savings, profit kept in the business and loans, so growth is slow.
- The owner may work long hours, and illness can stop the business.

### Worked example: unlimited versus limited liability

Yarrowmead Bakery is run by Rosa Danvers as a sole trader. The business fails owing 42,000 to suppliers and the bank. Selling its ovens and stock raises 30,000.

1. Shortfall = 42,000 - 30,000 = 12,000.
2. Rosa has unlimited liability, so creditors can claim 12,000 from her personal assets. If she has 25,000 of savings, she is left with 13,000.
3. If Yarrowmead had been a limited company with the same figures, the shareholders would lose the value of their shares, but creditors could not claim the shareholders' personal savings. The creditors would bear the unpaid 12,000.

The comparison shows why limited liability encourages people to invest, and why lenders may be cautious about lending to a limited company.

## Partnerships

A **partnership** is a business owned by two or more people who agree to share the risks, work and profit. Partners normally sign a written partnership agreement covering the sharing of profit, the money each puts in, and how decisions and disputes are handled. Treat partners as having unlimited liability unless the question says otherwise.

**Advantages**
- More capital than a sole trader, because each partner contributes.
- Skills and workload are shared.
- More than one person to take decisions and cover absence.

**Disadvantages**
- Unlimited liability, and each partner can be affected by decisions made by the others.
- Profits must be shared.
- Disagreements can slow decisions or end the partnership.

### Worked example: sharing profit

Ivanova and Kapoor Joinery made a profit of 84,000. The agreement shares profit in the ratio 5:3, because Ivanova invested more capital.

1. Total shares = 5 + 3 = 8.
2. Ivanova = 5/8 x 84,000 = 52,500.
3. Kapoor = 3/8 x 84,000 = 31,500.
4. Check: 52,500 + 31,500 = 84,000.

## Private limited companies

A **private limited company** (Ltd) is owned by shareholders, who hold shares in it. Its shares cannot be offered to the general public. They are normally sold privately, often to family, friends or existing owners, and usually only with the agreement of the other shareholders. Shareholders have limited liability. Profit paid out to shareholders is called a **dividend**.

**Advantages**
- Limited liability.
- Can raise more capital than a partnership by selling shares.
- The company continues when a shareholder leaves.

**Disadvantages**
- More legal paperwork and cost to set up and run.
- Shares cannot be sold to the public, so there is a ceiling on funds raised.
- Selling shares to new investors can reduce the founders' share of control.
- Some financial information usually has to be made available, so there is less privacy than for a sole trader.

### Worked example: control and new shares

Rowanleigh Textiles Ltd has 100,000 shares. The founder holds 60,000.

1. Founder's share = 60,000/100,000 = 60%, so she has a majority.
2. The company sells 25,000 new shares to an outside investor to raise capital.
3. Total shares = 125,000. Founder's share = 60,000/125,000 = 48%.
4. She no longer holds a majority. Raising capital has cost her some control.

## Public limited companies

A **public limited company** (plc) can sell shares to the general public, usually through a stock exchange. Shareholders have limited liability, and a plc is often large, though size alone does not define it.

**Advantages**
- Can raise very large sums by selling shares to many investors, and has limited liability.
- Easier to borrow from banks.
- Shares can be bought and sold freely, which attracts investors.

**Disadvantages**
- Setting up and running costs are high, and accounts are widely available to competitors.
- Ownership is spread out, which can make a takeover possible.
- Managers may focus on short-term results to please shareholders.

### Worked example: dividends and control

Kittiwake Freight plc has 4,000,000 shares. Profit after tax is 1,600,000 and the directors decide to pay out half as dividends.

1. Total dividend = 50% x 1,600,000 = 800,000.
2. Dividend per share = 800,000/4,000,000 = 0.20 (20 cents).
3. An investor with 25,000 shares receives 25,000 x 0.20 = 5,000, and owns 25,000/4,000,000 = 0.625% of the company.
4. A buyer needs more than 2,000,000 shares to hold a majority. Holding 2,100,000 shares would give 52.5%.

## Comparing the four main types

| Feature | Sole trader | Partnership | Private limited company | Public limited company |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Owners | One person | Two or more | Shareholders | Shareholders |
| Liability | Unlimited | Unlimited (usual position) | Limited | Limited |
| Share sale | Not applicable | Not applicable | Not to the public | To the public |
| Ability to raise capital | Low | Low to medium | Medium | High |
| Control | One person | Shared | Shareholders, often founders | Shareholders, often widely spread |
| Setup and running | Simple | Fairly simple | More formal | Most formal |

## Franchises

A **franchise** is an agreement where a business with a successful brand and product (the **franchisor**) gives another business or person (the **franchisee**) the right to use its name, products and methods in return for payment. Payment is usually an initial fee plus a regular **royalty**, often a percentage of sales. The franchisee runs the outlet as a separate business.

**For the franchisor**
- Advantages: expansion without investing its own capital, since the franchisee supplies most of the money; a motivated local owner-manager; income from fees and royalties.
- Disadvantages: it gives up some of the profit to the franchisee; the brand can be damaged if one franchisee offers poor quality; it has less direct control than with its own outlets.

**For the franchisee**
- Advantages: an established brand and product with proven demand; training and support; lower risk of failure than a new brand.
- Disadvantages: the franchisee pays the initial fee and continuing royalties; must follow the franchisor's rules and cannot easily change products; poor performance by other franchisees may harm the brand.

### Worked example: a franchise

Pennywhistle Pizza charges an initial fee of 20,000 and a royalty of 6% of sales. One outlet sells 250,000 in its first year and earns 52,000 operating profit before paying the franchisor.

1. Royalty = 6% x 250,000 = 15,000.
2. Profit after royalty = 52,000 - 15,000 = 37,000.
3. After also paying the initial fee, the franchisee is left with 37,000 - 20,000 = 17,000 in year one.
4. If eight franchisees are identical, the franchisor receives 8 x 20,000 + 8 x 15,000 = 160,000 + 120,000 = 280,000 in year one.

## Joint ventures

A **joint venture** is when two or more businesses cooperate on a project or in a market, sharing costs, risks and rewards, while staying separate businesses.

**Advantages**
- Costs and risks are shared.
- Each partner brings skills, contacts or local knowledge.
- Can open a new market, especially abroad, faster than going alone.

**Disadvantages**
- Profits must be shared.
- The businesses may disagree on aims, management or how to divide work.
- Decisions may be slower because both sides must agree.

### Worked example: sharing a joint venture

Ebbsfleet Motors and Wildmoor Cycles set up a joint venture to make electric bikes. Costs are 800,000, shared 60:40, and the first-year profit is 150,000, shared in the same ratio.

1. Ebbsfleet pays 60% x 800,000 = 480,000 and receives 60% x 150,000 = 90,000.
2. Wildmoor pays 40% x 800,000 = 320,000 and receives 40% x 150,000 = 60,000.

## Social enterprises

A **social enterprise** is a business that trades to achieve social or environmental aims. Any profit is mainly reinvested in those aims rather than paid out to owners. It still has to cover its costs, so it needs sales revenue.

Greenbench Repairs earns 96,000 from repair services, has costs of 81,000, and uses 80% of the surplus to fund free training for unemployed people.

1. Surplus = 96,000 - 81,000 = 15,000.
2. Training fund = 80% x 15,000 = 12,000.
3. Retained = 15,000 - 12,000 = 3,000.

Benefits include customer goodwill and a purpose that can motivate staff. Drawbacks include less money for growth, and difficulty raising finance from investors who want a return.

## Recommend and justify a suitable type

Use these steps on case-study questions.

1. Pull out the facts: size, finance needed, risk, who wants control, growth plans.
2. Match each fact to a type. A need for large capital points toward a company. A wish to keep full control and keep things simple points to a sole trader.
3. Make your recommendation clear.
4. Justify it with two or three reasons from the case.
5. Say why another type is less suitable, then give a short judgement.

Example: three friends plan a small design studio with modest start-up costs and a wish to share the work. A partnership or private limited company fits. If they fear personal debt, the private limited company is better. If speed and low cost matter most, a partnership with a written agreement may be preferable.

## Common errors

- Writing that a limited company "cannot fail". It can; the owners lose only their investment.
- Confusing private and public limited companies by saying private companies are owned by the government.
- Listing generic points with no reference to the business in the case.
- Treating a franchisee as an employee of the franchisor, when it owns its outlet.
- Giving only one party's view when the question asks about both franchisor and franchisee.
