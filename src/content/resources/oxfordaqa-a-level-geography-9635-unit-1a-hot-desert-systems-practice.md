These questions cover section 3.1.2 Section B: Hot desert systems and landscapes of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Geography (9635) specification, Version 3.2, including 3.1.2.1 to 3.1.2.6. The specification assesses this option in Section B of Unit 1: Physical geography 1. Revise first with the [hot desert systems study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-geography-9635-unit-1a-hot-desert-systems/) and the [hot desert systems revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-geography-9635-hot-desert-systems-and-landscapes-revision-notes/).

The stations Qasr Tal and Hadir, Dunmere Bay and all data about them are fictional.

## Questions

### Section A: short-answer questions

**1.** Distinguish between exogenous and endorheic sources of water in hot deserts. **[2]**

**2.** Outline what is meant by dynamic equilibrium, using an example from a hot desert system. **[2]**

**3.** Explain how thermal fracture breaks down rock in hot deserts. **[3]**

**4.** Qasr Tal receives 85 mm of precipitation a year and has a potential evapotranspiration of 2,100 mm a year.

**(a)** Calculate the aridity index (P / PET) for Qasr Tal and classify it, using the bands hyper-arid below 0.05, arid 0.05 to 0.20 and semi-arid 0.20 to 0.50. **[2]**
**(b)** Hadir receives more rainfall than Qasr Tal but has a lower aridity index. Explain how this is possible. **[2]**

**5.** A dune field gains 18,000 tonnes of sand a year from a dry riverbed and 3,500 tonnes from weathering. Wind removes 15,000 tonnes a year.

**(a)** Calculate the sediment budget. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain what this budget suggests about the future of the dune field. **[2]**

**6.** Explain the formation of a seif dune. **[3]**

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**7.** Students collected sand samples at three positions across the windward slope of a dune at Dunmere Bay, a fictional coastal dune system used to study aeolian processes.

| Position | Mean grain size (mm) |
|---|---|
| Windward base | 0.42 |
| Mid-slope | 0.31 |
| Crest | 0.24 |

**(a)** Describe the pattern shown, using figures. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain the pattern with reference to wind transport processes. **[3]**
**(c)** Suggest **one** way the students could improve the reliability of their data. **[2]**

**8.** Explain how wadis, bahadas and playas form part of a single mountain-front landscape. **[6]**

**9.** Explain how wind abrasion produces yardangs and zeugen. **[4]**

**10.** Assess the relative importance of past and present processes in shaping the landscapes of hot deserts. **[9]**

**11.** With reference to a local-scale area where desertification has occurred, evaluate the effectiveness of human responses of resilience, mitigation and adaptation. **[9]**

**12.** "The future for populations living at the margins of hot deserts is one of inevitable decline." To what extent do you agree with this view? **[12]**

## Answers

Questions 10 to 12 are extended responses. The real exam marks these with levels of response, not point by point; the [1] points show the kind of content that earns credit.

**1.** Exogenous water comes from outside the desert, such as a river fed by rain in wetter mountains, like the Nile [1]. Endorheic drainage flows inland into a basin with no outlet to the sea, ending in a lake or playa [1]. **[2]**

**2.** A system adjusts to changes in inputs but keeps a broadly stable average form over time [1], for example a barchan that changes shape slightly with each wind event but keeps its crescent form as it migrates [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Rock is heated strongly by day and cools rapidly at night under clear skies [1]. Repeated expansion and contraction set up stresses [1], and because rock conducts heat poorly, the outer layers are stressed more than the interior, so the rock cracks and fragments [1]. **[3]**

**4. (a)** 85 / 2,100 = 0.040 [1], so Qasr Tal is hyper-arid [1].
**(b)** The aridity index depends on potential evapotranspiration as well as rainfall [1]. If Hadir is hotter, sunnier or windier, its PET may be so much higher that P / PET is lower despite the extra rain [1]. **[4]**

**5. (a)** (18,000 + 3,500) - 15,000 = +6,500 tonnes a year [1].
**(b)** The budget is positive, so more sand is stored each year [1], meaning dunes are likely to grow or the field to expand, unless inputs fall, for example if the riverbed's sediment supply is reduced [1]. **[3]**

**6.** Seifs form where winds blow from two main directions at an angle to each other [1]. Each wind builds a slip face on one side, so sand accumulates along a line between them [1], producing a long, sharp-crested ridge that extends in the average direction of the two winds [1]. **[3]**

**7. (a)** Grain size falls from 0.42 mm at the base to 0.24 mm at the crest [1], a decrease of 0.18 mm, or about 43% [1].
**(b)** Larger grains need more wind energy to move [1], so they tend to roll by surface creep and stay near the base [1]. Finer grains are lifted by saltation and carried further up the slope, so the crest is finer and better sorted [1].
**(c)** Take several samples at each position, or repeat on more dunes, and use mean values [1], so that one unusual sample does not distort the pattern [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* in (c), the second mark is for saying *why* the change improves reliability.

**8.** Rare intense storms produce flash floods that cut steep-sided wadis into the mountains [1]. Where a wadi emerges onto the plain, the flow spreads and loses energy [1], depositing an alluvial fan with the coarsest material near the apex [1]. Neighbouring fans merge into a continuous bahada along the mountain front [1]. The finest sediment and water travel furthest into the basin, where water collects in a temporary lake [1]. Evaporation leaves a flat, salt-crusted playa, completing a source-to-sink sediment cascade [1]. **[6]**

**9.** Saltating sand travels close to the ground, so abrasion is greatest near the surface [1]. Yardangs form where ridges of rock aligned parallel to the prevailing wind are streamlined by abrasion of softer rock between them [1]. Zeugen form where a resistant cap rock lies over softer rock [1]; abrasion along joints cuts down into the soft layer, leaving tabular blocks [1]. **[4]**

**10.** *Indicative content (levels marking applies).* Present processes include wind erosion, transport and deposition, and episodic flash floods [1]. Dunes, ventifacts and desert pavements are being shaped by present-day winds [1]. Many wadis are larger than present floods could cut, suggesting formation in wetter past climates [1]. The early to middle Holocene Sahara had lakes and grassland, so some landforms are inherited [1]. Pediments and inselbergs need very long periods, possibly spanning many climatic phases [1]. Present flash floods still modify wadis and fans, so the forms are being reworked [1]. Weathering continues to supply sediment today [1]. Importance varies by landform: aeolian forms reflect present processes, large water-cut forms reflect the past [1]. Judgement: landscapes are palimpsests of past and present processes, with the past more important for large-scale form [1]. **[9]**

**11.** *Indicative content (levels marking applies).* Locate the area, for example the Central Plateau of Burkina Faso in the Sahel [1]. Causes: drought in the 1970s and 1980s and pressure on land [1]. Adaptation: zai pits concentrate water and manure around crops [1]. Mitigation: stone lines along contours slow runoff and trap soil [1]. Resilience: these methods are cheap and farmer-led, so they spread between villages [1]. Evidence of success: yields and tree cover improved on treated land [1]. Limits: building pits and lines is labour-intensive [1]. Large-scale schemes such as the Great Green Wall have progressed more slowly than planned [1]. Judgement: local, community-led responses have been more effective than top-down schemes [1]. **[9]**

**12.** *Indicative content (levels marking applies).* Define the desert margins and the populations at risk [1]. For: predicted higher temperatures and more variable rainfall may increase drought risk [1]. Population growth adds pressure on marginal land [1]. Land degradation reduces yields, causing food insecurity [1]. Out-migration to cities may leave rural areas depopulated [1]. Against: farmer-managed natural regeneration in Niger has re-greened large areas [1]. Water harvesting methods such as zai pits raise yields [1]. Drought-resistant crops and improved grazing management increase resilience [1]. Migration and remittances can support those who remain [1]. Outcomes depend on governance, investment and land rights [1]. Desert margins have advanced and retreated naturally before, so decline is not fixed [1]. Judgement: decline is likely without action but not inevitable, because human responses shape the outcome [1]. **[12]**
*Examiner insight:* "alternative futures" means more than one possible outcome; an answer describing only decline cannot reach the top level.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Aridity index answers with no working or no classification.
- Describing data without quoting figures, or quoting figures without a trend.
- Landform answers that name the feature but give no process.
- Treating desertification as purely human or purely natural.
- Extended answers with no named area, dates or final judgement.
