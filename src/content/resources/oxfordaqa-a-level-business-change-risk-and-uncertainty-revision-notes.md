## Key definitions

- **Change:** any alteration to what a business does or how it does it: its structure, products, markets, processes or people.
- **Driving forces:** pressures pushing a business towards a change.
- **Restraining forces:** pressures holding a change back.
- **Risk:** the possibility that results differ from the plan, where outcomes can be identified and roughly estimated.
- **Uncertainty:** a situation where future outcomes cannot be predicted or measured with confidence.
- **Contingency plan:** a prepared response to a specific possible event.
- **Crisis management:** how a business responds while an unexpected, damaging event is unfolding.
- **Agile business:** one that senses change early and responds quickly.

## Reasons for change

| Internal | External |
|---|---|
| New leader or owner | New technology |
| New objectives or strategy | Competitors' actions |
| Poor performance (profit, costs, labour turnover) | Economic change: wages, interest or exchange rates |
| Growth beyond current structure or systems | New laws or regulations |
| Decision to adopt new methods | Shifts in customer tastes |

### Change and stakeholders

| Stakeholder | Can gain | Can lose |
|---|---|---|
| Employees | New skills, more varied work, a more secure employer | Jobs, overtime, familiar routines and teams |
| Managers | Chance to lead and show results | Workload, blame if it fails |
| Shareholders | Higher long-term profit | Short-term dividends while costs are paid |
| Customers | Better products, prices or service | Disruption during the switch |
| Suppliers | Larger orders if the firm grows | Contracts if the firm switches or in-sources |
| Community | Investment, skilled jobs | Redundancies, site closures |

For each group in a case, give one gain and one loss, then say whose interest is likely to win and why.

## Barriers to change

General barriers: cost, skills gaps, culture, management time.

**Kotter and Schlesinger's four reasons for resistance**

| Reason | Signal in a case study |
|---|---|
| Self-interest | Staff stand to lose pay, status, overtime or jobs |
| Misunderstanding and lack of trust | Rumours, poor communication, past broken promises |
| Inertia | "We've always done it this way"; staff keep old habits |
| Different assessments of the situation | Staff genuinely think the change is a mistake |

## Managing change

### Method in steps: force field analysis

1. List the driving forces and the restraining forces.
2. Score each force (e.g. 1 = weak to 5 = strong).
3. Total each side and compare.
4. Plan to weaken the strongest restraining forces (and, where possible, strengthen driving forces).
5. Judge: the scores are subjective, so treat a narrow margin with caution.

**Mini example.** **Larkspire Travel** wants to close its high-street branches and sell only online. Driving: falling branch visits 2, lower costs online 5 → total **7**. Restraining: older customers' loyalty 3, staff opposition 3, lease penalties 2 → total **8**. Restraining forces lead by 1, so the change is unlikely to succeed until something shifts the balance.

### Kotter and Schlesinger's six ways to overcome resistance

| Method | Use when | Main drawback |
|---|---|---|
| Education | Staff misunderstand | Slow |
| Participation | Staff have knowledge or power to block | Slow; may water down the change |
| Facilitation | Fear or a skills gap | Costly |
| Negotiation | A powerful group clearly loses | Costly; sets a precedent |
| Manipulation | Time and money are short | Trust collapses if exposed |
| Coercion | Speed matters and managers hold power | Resentment, demotivation, staff leave |

The methods run roughly from slow and consensual to fast and forceful. Match each method to the reason for resistance.

### Method in steps: a "how should managers overcome resistance" answer

1. Identify the reason for resistance from the case evidence (which of the four?).
2. Choose the method that fits that reason, e.g. misunderstanding → education; skills fear → facilitation.
3. Weigh its drawback against the case constraints: time, cost, managers' power.
4. Judge: often a combination works, with forceful methods held back as a last resort.

## Risk

**Why assess and plan:** protects cash and profit, keeps output flowing, avoids fines, reassures lenders and customers, speeds the response. But excessive caution means missed opportunities.

| Type of risk | Examples |
|---|---|
| Financial | Heavy borrowing, falling profit, liquidity shortages |
| Strategic | Entering new markets, launching new products |
| Operational | Production breakdowns, supply-chain disruption |
| Compliance | Breaking laws or regulations |
| Reputational | Negative publicity, dissatisfied customers |
| Cybersecurity | Hacking, ransomware, data theft |

### Methods of managing risk

| Method | For | Against |
|---|---|---|
| Market research | Tests demand first | Cost, time, possible bias |
| Contingency/crisis plans | Faster, calmer response | May not fit the actual event |
| Insurance | Transfers insurable losses | Premiums; not all risks insurable |
| Diversifying products, markets, suppliers | Spreads exposure | Lost economies of scale; less focus |
| Training | Fewer errors and breaches | Cost; trained staff may leave |
| Forecasting | Early warning | Unreliable when conditions shift |
| Cybersecurity measures | Fewer, smaller attacks | Ongoing cost; never complete |

### Method in steps: recommending a way to manage a risk

1. Name the type of risk and the event causing it.
2. Ask how likely it is and how damaging it would be.
3. Pick a method that reduces either the likelihood or the impact.
4. Weigh the method's cost against the damage avoided, then judge.

**Mini reminder.** Larkspire's online-only plan raises **strategic** risk (will customers book online?) and **cybersecurity** risk (card data on its site). Market research tests the first; firewalls, backups and staff training reduce the second.

## Uncertainty

| Approach | For | Against |
|---|---|---|
| Scenario planning | Prepares responses to several futures | Time-consuming; reality may match no scenario |
| Agile business | Adapts to any change; seizes opportunities | Constant change can unsettle staff; less tightly efficient |

**Four ways to develop agility:** flat organisational structure (faster decisions), empowerment (decisions made close to the problem), transformational leadership (a vision that makes staff welcome change), cross-functional teams (ideas tested by every function at once).

**Selbrooke Foods** shows the chain: a flat structure and empowered store managers let it switch to a new local supplier within days when a drought hit its main supplier's region.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Risk vs uncertainty:** risk can be estimated; uncertainty cannot. Market research and forecasting suit risk; scenario planning and agility suit uncertainty.
- **Contingency plan vs crisis management:** the plan is written in advance; crisis management is the live response.
- **Reasons for resistance vs ways to overcome it:** four reasons, six methods. Do not mix the lists.
- **Driving vs restraining forces:** driving forces push towards the change; restraining forces hold it back.
- **Strategic vs operational risk:** strategic risk comes from big choices; operational risk comes from day-to-day disruption.

## Evaluation hooks

Judgements on this topic usually turn on a few case facts. Use them to say "it depends on...":

- **Time available:** a tight deadline favours faster methods (negotiation, coercion); more time allows education and participation.
- **Size and finances:** a small firm may not afford insurance for every risk, full scenario planning or a dedicated cybersecurity team.
- **Likelihood and impact:** spend most on risks that are both likely and damaging.
- **Culture and leadership:** an agile structure fails without leaders who trust staff to decide.
- **Speed of change in the market:** the faster the market moves, the more valuable agility is compared with detailed plans.

## Quick self-test

1. Give one internal and one external reason for change.
2. Name Kotter and Schlesinger's four reasons for resistance.
3. Which method suits resistance caused by misunderstanding?
4. Driving forces score 3 and 4; restraining forces score 2, 2 and 2. Which side is stronger, and by how much?
5. Name the type of risk: a firm fails a hygiene inspection.
6. Name the type of risk: a bank cuts a firm's overdraft limit.
7. Why can't a firm insure against a failed product launch?
8. State one disadvantage of diversifying suppliers.
9. What is scenario planning?
10. Explain how empowerment makes a business more agile.
11. Which of Kotter and Schlesinger's methods involves offering incentives such as early-retirement terms?
12. State one advantage of a contingency plan.

### Answers

1. Internal: e.g. a new chief executive. External: e.g. a competitor cuts prices.
2. Self-interest; misunderstanding and lack of trust; inertia; different assessments of the situation.
3. Education.
4. Driving 3 + 4 = 7; restraining 2 + 2 + 2 = 6. Driving forces are stronger by 1.
5. Compliance risk.
6. Financial risk (liquidity).
7. Its outcome depends on the firm's own decisions and the probability cannot be calculated, so insurers will not cover it.
8. Smaller orders with each supplier can lose bulk discounts (or quality becomes harder to control).
9. Describing several plausible futures and planning a response to each.
10. Staff can act without waiting for approval, so the response to change is faster.
11. Negotiation.
12. A response is ready in advance, so damage is limited and recovery is faster.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Swapping the four reasons for resistance with the six methods.
- Recommending coercion without weighing resentment and staff loss.
- Totalling force field scores but not saying what management should do next.
- Calling an unpredictable event a "risk" and suggesting insurance for it.
- Naming the type of risk without linking it to the case business.
- Listing methods of risk management without advantages and disadvantages.
- Describing agility in general terms without one of the four named ways.
- Giving stakeholder effects for "employees" only and ignoring other groups.
