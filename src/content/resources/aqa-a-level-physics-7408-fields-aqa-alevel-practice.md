These questions cover sub-section 3.7.1 Fields of the AQA AS and A-level Physics specification (7407/7408, AS and A-level exams June 2016 onwards): the concept of a force field and the similarities and differences between gravitational and electrostatic forces. They also use the field equations from 3.7.2 and 3.7.3 that the comparison depends on. It is A-level only and assessed in Paper 2.

Revise from the [fields study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-physics-7408-fields-aqa-alevel/) and [fields revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-physics-7408-fields-aqa-alevel-revision-notes/) first. Mixed questions on section 3.7 are in the [Fields and their consequences practice questions](/resources/aqa-a-level-physics-fields-and-their-consequences-practice/).

Data: G = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N m² kg⁻²; 1/4πε₀ = 8.99 × 10⁹ N m² C⁻²; e = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C; proton mass 1.67 × 10⁻²⁷ kg; g at Earth's surface = 9.81 N kg⁻¹; Moon mass 7.35 × 10²² kg; Earth–Moon distance 3.84 × 10⁸ m.

## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Define a force field, and name one force that is not a field force. **[2]**

**2.** Explain what is meant by the direction of a field being "determined by inspection", using the electric field near a negative point charge as your example. **[2]**

**3.** State one similarity and one difference between gravitational and electrostatic forces. **[2]**

**4.** Explain why equipotential surfaces must be perpendicular to field lines. **[2]**

**5.** Two point charges are a distance d apart. Both charges are halved and the separation is halved. State the factor by which the force between them changes, showing your reasoning. **[2]**

### Section B: structured questions

**6.** A planet of mass 6.4 × 10²³ kg has a moon of mass 1.0 × 10²² kg. Their centres are 9.0 × 10⁷ m apart.

**(a)** Explain why there is a point between them where the resultant gravitational field strength is zero. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the distance of this point from the centre of the planet. **[3]**
**(c)** Calculate the gravitational potential at this point. **[2]**
**(d)** Explain why the potential is not zero although the field strength is. **[1]**

**7.** Point charges of +5.0 nC at A and −5.0 nC at B are 0.20 m apart in air. M is the midpoint of AB.

**(a)** Calculate the electric field strength at M and state its direction. **[3]**
**(b)** State the electric potential at M. **[1]**
**(c)** P lies on the line AB extended, 0.10 m beyond B. Calculate the resultant field strength at P and state its direction. **[3]**

**8.** Two protons are separated by a distance r.

**(a)** Calculate the ratio of the electrostatic force to the gravitational force between them. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why this ratio does not depend on r. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain why gravitational forces, not electrostatic forces, govern the orbits of planets. **[1]**

**9.** Two parallel plates 0.050 m apart have a potential difference of 500 V; the lower plate is at 0 V.

**(a)** Describe the equipotentials at 100 V intervals between the plates and state their spacing. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the work done moving a +3.0 nC charge from the 100 V equipotential to the 400 V equipotential. **[1]**
**(c)** State the work done moving the charge along the 400 V equipotential. **[1]**
**(d)** Calculate the spacing of gravitational equipotentials 10 J kg⁻¹ apart near Earth's surface. **[2]**

**10.** Describe the field lines and the equipotentials for:

**(a)** an isolated point mass; **[2]**
**(b)** the region between two oppositely charged parallel plates, away from the edges. **[2]**

**11.** The Moon exerts a gravitational field at the Earth.

**(a)** Calculate the Moon's gravitational field strength at the Earth's centre. **[2]**
**(b)** Compare this with the Earth's own field at its surface. **[1]**

**12.** Compare the graph of potential against distance r outside an isolated point mass with that outside an isolated positive point charge. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** A region in which a body experiences a non-contact force [1]; any contact force, such as friction, tension or the normal contact force [1].

**2.** The direction is the direction of the force on a positive test charge placed at that point [1]; a positive charge is attracted to a negative charge, so the field points radially inwards towards it [1].

**3.** Similarity: both follow inverse-square laws (or both use field lines, potential, equipotentials) [1]. Difference: masses always attract, but charges can attract or repel [1].

**4.** If the field had a component along an equipotential, work would be done moving a test object along it [1]; but no work is done along an equipotential, so the field must have no component along it and is perpendicular [1].

**5.** F ∝ Q₁Q₂/r²: the charges contribute ½ × ½ = ¼ and the separation contributes 1/(½)² = 4 [1]; the force is **unchanged** (factor 1) [1].

**6. (a)** Between the bodies the two fields point in opposite directions, each towards its own body, so they can cancel [1].
**(b)** GM_P/x² = GM_m/(d − x)² [1]; x/(d − x) = √(6.4 × 10²³/1.0 × 10²²) = 8 [1]; x = 8d/9 = **8.0 × 10⁷ m** [1]
**(c)** V = −G(M_P/x + M_m/(d − x)) = −6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ × (8.0 × 10¹⁵ + 1.0 × 10¹⁵) [1] = **−6.0 × 10⁵ J kg⁻¹** [1]
**(d)** Potential is a scalar and both contributions are negative, so they add rather than cancel [1].
*Examiner insight:* Field strengths cancel as vectors; potentials never cancel for masses because they are all negative.

**7. (a)** Each field = 8.99 × 10⁹ × 5.0 × 10⁻⁹/0.10² = 4.5 × 10³ N C⁻¹ [1]; both point from A towards B, so they add [1]; E = **9.0 × 10³ N C⁻¹ towards B** [1]
**(b)** **0 V** (equal and opposite contributions) [1]
**(c)** From A: 8.99 × 10⁹ × 5.0 × 10⁻⁹/0.30² = 499 N C⁻¹ away from A [1]; from B: 8.99 × 10⁹ × 5.0 × 10⁻⁹/0.10² = 4495 N C⁻¹ towards B [1]; resultant = **4.0 × 10³ N C⁻¹ towards B (towards A)** [1]
*Examiner insight:* In (a), V = 0 at M but E is not zero; a zero potential does not mean a zero field.

**8. (a)** F_E/F_G = e²/(4πε₀Gm²) = 8.99 × 10⁹ × (1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹)²/(6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ × (1.67 × 10⁻²⁷)²) [1] = **1.2 × 10³⁶** [1]
**(b)** Both forces are inverse-square, so r² cancels [1].
**(c)** Planets are electrically neutral overall, so the net electrostatic force is negligible, while their masses are enormous [1].

**9. (a)** Horizontal planes parallel to the plates, perpendicular to the field [1]; equally spaced, 0.050/5 = **0.010 m** apart [1]
**(b)** W = QΔV = 3.0 × 10⁻⁹ × 300 = **9.0 × 10⁻⁷ J** [1]
**(c)** **Zero** [1]
**(d)** ΔV = gΔh, so Δh = 10/9.81 [1] = **1.0 m** [1]

**10. (a)** Field lines radial, pointing inwards towards the mass, closer together nearer the mass [1]; equipotentials are concentric spheres (circles in a diagram) whose spacing increases outwards for equal steps of potential [1].
**(b)** Field lines straight, parallel, equally spaced, from the positive to the negative plate [1]; equipotentials are planes parallel to the plates, equally spaced [1].

**11. (a)** g = GM/r² = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ × 7.35 × 10²²/(3.84 × 10⁸)² [1] = **3.3 × 10⁻⁵ N kg⁻¹** [1]
**(b)** It is about 3 × 10⁻⁶ of (about three millionths of) the Earth's surface field [1].

**12.** Both have magnitude proportional to 1/r, approaching zero at infinity [1]; the gravitational potential is negative and rises towards zero as r increases, while the electric potential is positive and falls towards zero [1]; in both cases the gradient gives the field strength (g = −ΔV/Δr; E = ΔV/Δr in magnitude) [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Adding field strengths without stating directions.
- Treating potential as a vector.
- Searching for a neutral point between unlike charges.
- Forgetting to square-root the mass or charge ratio.
- Giving "both inverse-square" as a difference.
