> These are original practice questions written in the style of Cambridge O Level
> Geography (2217) assessment objectives. They are not taken from any past paper
> and are not endorsed by Cambridge International. Scenarios are deliberately
> generic — substitute your own learned case study's real names, places and
> figures wherever the question asks for one.

Use these questions alongside the [Economic Development study
guide](/resources/o-level-geography-economic-development/) and
[revision
notes](/resources/o-level-geography-economic-development-revision-notes/).

## Section A

**1.** State one economic indicator, one social indicator, and one
composite indicator of development, giving an example of each. **[3]**

**2.** Name the four sectors of production, and state which two grow
in relative importance as a country develops. **[3]**

**3.** State the three inputs of a farming or industrial system. **[3]**

**4.** Name the four environmental risks specified in Theme 3.7. **[2]**

**5.** State two natural causes and two economic or political causes
of food shortages. **[4]**

## Section B

**6.** A country has a high GNP per capita but a comparatively low
life expectancy and literacy rate.

**(a)** Explain why using GNP per capita alone would give a misleading
picture of this country's development. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why a composite index such as the Human Development
Index gives a more balanced picture. **[2]**

**7.** Using a named transnational corporation you have studied,
describe its global links and explain one positive and one negative
impact of its operations on the country where it has invested. **[6]**

**8.** A government is deciding whether to expand nuclear power or
invest further in renewable energy.

**(a)** State one advantage and one disadvantage of nuclear power. **[2]**
**(b)** State one advantage and one disadvantage of renewable energy. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why a balanced conclusion on this decision should
depend on the country's specific circumstances rather than favouring
one source absolutely. **[2]**

**9.** Using a named industrial zone or factory you have studied,
explain how three location factors (from land, labour, raw materials,
fuel and power, transport, and markets) influenced its location. **[6]**

**10.** A coastal area has seen rapid growth in tourism over the past
decade.

**(a)** Suggest one physical attraction and one human attraction that
might explain this growth. **[2]**
**(b)** State one benefit and one disadvantage of this tourism growth
for the receiving area. **[2]**
**(c)** Suggest one measure that could make tourism in this area more
sustainable. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** Economic: GNP per capita [1]. Social: literacy rate or life
expectancy [1]. Composite: the Human Development Index (HDI), which
combines several measures into one score [1].

**2.** Primary, secondary, tertiary and quaternary [1–2]. Secondary
and tertiary/quaternary grow in relative importance as a country
develops, as employment shifts away from primary activity [1].

**3.** Inputs, processes and outputs [3, one mark each; for an
industrial system, outputs additionally include waste].

**4.** Any four of: soil erosion, desertification, enhanced global
warming, pollution (water, air, noise or visual) [2, allow 1 mark for
any two correct].

**5.** Natural causes: any two of drought, floods, tropical storms,
pests [2]. Economic/political causes: any two of low capital
investment, poor transport and distribution, conflict [2].

**6. (a)** GNP per capita is only an economic measure and does not
reflect social wellbeing, so a country could appear developed by this
measure alone while its population experiences poor health and
education outcomes, as shown by the low life expectancy and literacy
rate here [2].
**(b)** A composite index combines economic and social measures into
one score, avoiding the distortion of relying on a single indicator,
so it would more accurately reflect this country's mixed picture of
high income alongside lower social outcomes [2].

**7.** Answers will vary depending on the transnational corporation
studied, but should include: a description of its global links (for
example, where it is headquartered, where it operates or sources
materials, and the scale of its investment or workforce in the
country studied) [1–2]; one positive impact, such as job creation,
skills transfer, or infrastructure investment [1–2]; and one negative
impact, such as loss of local control over resources, profit
repatriation, or environmental cost [1–2]. Full marks require
concrete, place-specific detail (named locations, sectors, or
figures) rather than generic statements.

**8. (a)** Advantage: nuclear power is a reliable, high-output source
with low ongoing carbon emissions once built [1]. Disadvantage: very
high upfront construction cost and the unresolved long-term problem of
radioactive waste disposal [1].
**(b)** Advantage: renewable sources produce little to no carbon
emissions during operation and use naturally replenished resources
[1]. Disadvantage: output can be unreliable or weather-dependent (for
example, wind or solar), and some renewable infrastructure requires
significant land area [1].
**(c)** A balanced conclusion should depend on context because factors
such as a country's available capital, existing infrastructure,
climate and geography, and capacity to manage risk or waste safely
will make one source more suitable than the other in different
countries, rather than either source being universally the better
choice [2].

**9.** Answers will vary depending on the industrial zone or factory
studied, but should explain three named location factors with
place-specific detail — for example: proximity to raw materials
reducing transport costs [1–2]; access to a large or skilled labour
force in the area [1–2]; and good transport links (road, rail, port
or air) enabling the movement of inputs and finished goods to markets
[1–2]. Full marks require the explanation to be tied to the specific
named location studied, not stated as a generic list of factors.

**10. (a)** Physical attraction: for example, a beach, coastline or
favourable climate [1]. Human attraction: for example, historic sites,
hotels and resorts, or accessible transport links [1].
**(b)** Benefit: income and employment for the local population [1].
Disadvantage: environmental damage (for example, to coastal
ecosystems) or cultural disruption to the local community [1].
**(c)** Any reasonable sustainable-management suggestion, for example:
limiting visitor numbers at sensitive sites, investing tourism revenue
in environmental protection, or promoting off-season tourism to
spread demand more evenly [2].

## A note on case-study preparation

Six of Theme 3's seven subtopics require a named, specific case study,
and questions 7 and 9 above are written to be answered with whichever
real example you have studied — substitute your own transnational
corporation or industrial zone into the same question structure rather
than treating the generic prompt as the full answer. Because a single
well-learned case study can often support more than one subtopic (a
transnational corporation can supply detail for both development's
global links and industry's location factors, for instance), prioritise
depth over breadth: learn three or four examples with genuine
place-specific facts you can write under exam conditions, rather than
seven shallow ones you can only describe in general terms.
