**This guide covers the opening statement of 3.2.2 Social influence in OxfordAQA International GCSE
Psychology (9218), Version 1.1: "Identification and explanation of how social factors (group size,
anonymity and task difficulty) and dispositional factors (personality, expertise) affect conformity to
majority influence. Conformity as investigated by Asch."** The
[Conformity and Obedience study guide](/resources/igcse-oxfordaqa-psychology-conformity-and-obedience/)
describes Asch's line task and its headline results, and gives each factor a sentence. This page
explains every factor in depth, with the variations Asch ran to test them and the two kinds of
social influence that explain why each factor works.

## Why people conform: two kinds of influence

Every factor in this statement works by changing one or both of two pressures. Using them makes your
explanations precise.

- **Normative social influence:** conforming to **fit in** and be accepted, and to avoid ridicule or
  rejection. The person may privately disagree but goes along publicly (**compliance**).
- **Informational social influence:** conforming because the person believes the group is **right**,
  usually when they are unsure. The person may genuinely change their view (**internalisation**).

In Asch's task the correct answer was obvious, so most conformity was **normative**: after the study,
many conformers said they knew the group was wrong but did not want to stand out.

## Asch's procedure in brief

Male American college students took part in what they were told was a study of visual perception.
Each real participant sat with a group of confederates and, on each of 18 trials, said aloud which of
three comparison lines matched a standard line. On 12 **critical trials** the confederates all gave
the same wrong answer before the participant answered. When participants judged the lines alone, they
made almost no errors, so mistakes in the group condition could be put down to group pressure.
Across critical trials, participants conformed about **37%** of the time; about **75%** conformed at
least once and about **25%** never conformed.

Asch then changed the situation to test what increased or decreased conformity. These variations are
your evidence for the social factors.

## Social factor 1: group size

**Finding.** Asch varied the number of confederates giving the wrong answer.

| Number of confederates in the majority | Approximate conformity |
|---|---|
| 1 | 3% |
| 2 | 13% |
| 3 | 32% |
| More than 3 | Little further increase |

**Explanation.** A larger unanimous majority increases **normative** pressure: disagreeing with three
or more people is more uncomfortable than disagreeing with one. Beyond three or four, extra members add
little, perhaps because people begin to suspect the group is not judging independently.

**Related point: unanimity.** When one confederate gave the right answer, breaking the group's
agreement, conformity dropped sharply. A large majority only has its full effect when it is
**united**.

## Social factor 2: anonymity

**Finding.** In one variation, the confederates still answered aloud, but the real participant
**wrote** their answer privately. Conformity fell substantially, to around an eighth of critical trials.

**Explanation.** Conformity in the line task was mainly **normative**: people agreed publicly to avoid
standing out. When their answer was **anonymous**, no one could see them disagree, so the pressure to
fit in disappeared. Being identifiable increases conformity; being anonymous reduces it.

**Application.** Secret ballots, anonymous online polls and private written feedback all reduce
normative conformity, because people's answers cannot be seen by the group.

## Social factor 3: task difficulty

**Finding.** When Asch made the comparison lines closer in length to the standard line, so the task was
harder, conformity **increased**.

**Explanation.** A difficult or ambiguous task makes people less confident in their own judgement, so
they look to others for the right answer: **informational** social influence. The harder the task, the
more reasonable it seems to assume the majority knows better.

**Application.** In an unfamiliar situation, such as a first day at a new school or a complex question
in class, people are more likely to follow the group than when the answer is obvious.

## Dispositional factor 1: personality

Some people are consistently more likely to conform than others, whatever the situation. Traits
linked to conformity include:

- **low self-esteem or low self-confidence:** less trust in one's own judgement, so more reliance on
  the group;
- **a high need for social approval or to belong:** more sensitivity to normative pressure;
- an **external locus of control:** a belief that events are controlled by outside forces, making
  people more likely to go along with others.

Asch's own results show that personality matters: in the same situation, a quarter of participants
never conformed while others conformed on most critical trials.

## Dispositional factor 2: expertise

**Expertise** is knowledge or skill in the area of the task. An expert is **less** likely to conform
on a task in their own field, because:

- they are **confident** in their own judgement, which reduces informational influence;
- they may feel their expertise gives them the right to disagree, which reduces normative pressure.

Expertise can also work the other way round: a person with **less** expertise than the group is more
likely to conform, especially on a hard task. In a British replication of Asch's study, Perrin and
Spencer (1980) found almost no conformity among science and engineering students, who may have felt
especially confident about judging measurements; the researchers also pointed to cultural change since
the 1950s, so this is not evidence about expertise alone.

## How the factors combine

| Factor | Main influence affected | Increases conformity when... |
|---|---|---|
| Group size | Normative | Majority is three or more and unanimous |
| Anonymity | Normative | Answers are public and identifiable |
| Task difficulty | Informational | Task is hard or ambiguous |
| Personality | Normative (and informational) | Low self-esteem, high need for approval |
| Expertise | Informational | The person lacks expertise in the task |

## Worked example: applying the factors

**Question.** In a cooking competition, five judges vote aloud one after another. The first four
judges praise a dish. The fifth, Ravi, a professional chef, thinks the dish is undercooked but also
praises it. Use your knowledge of factors affecting conformity to explain Ravi's behaviour, and
suggest how the competition could reduce conformity among judges. [6]

> **Group size and unanimity:** Ravi faces a unanimous majority of four, which creates strong
> **normative** pressure to agree so that he does not stand out **[1] [1]**.
>
> **Anonymity:** votes are given aloud, so Ravi's disagreement would be public, increasing the
> pressure to conform **[1]**.
>
> **Expertise:** as a professional chef Ravi should be confident in his own judgement, so his
> conformity is likely to be normative (public compliance) rather than informational; he probably
> still privately believes the dish is undercooked **[1]**.
>
> **Reducing conformity:** judges could write their scores privately before any are revealed,
> making answers anonymous **[1]**, which removes the normative pressure that a public majority creates
> **[1]**.

## Worked example: a percentage calculation

Research methods questions can use conformity data. In an invented replication, 20 participants each
completed 12 critical trials, and 72 conforming answers were recorded in total.

```
Total critical trials = 20 × 12 = 240
Percentage conforming = 72 ÷ 240 × 100 = 30%
```

A question might then ask you to compare this with Asch's figure of about 37% and suggest reasons for
the difference, such as a different culture, time period or sample.

## Evaluation

- **Strength:** Asch's variations are controlled laboratory evidence: one factor changes while the
  task stays the same, so cause and effect can be judged.
- **Limitation:** the line task is trivial, with no real consequences, so it may not reflect conformity
  on important issues; on the other hand, it shows conformity even when the answer is obvious.
- **Limitation:** the sample was male American students in the 1950s, a period and culture that may
  have valued conformity strongly.
- **Ethics:** participants were deceived and some were visibly uncomfortable.
- **Social and dispositional together:** the variations show the situation matters; the fact that some
  participants never conformed shows that personality and confidence matter too.

## Common mistakes

- **Saying anonymity increases conformity.** It reduces it, because normative pressure needs an
  audience.
- **Saying conformity keeps rising with group size.** It levels off beyond about three.
- **Classing expertise as a social factor.** The specification lists it as dispositional.
- **Missing the mechanism.** Name normative or informational influence when you explain a factor.

## Exam technique

- **Identify** a factor using the specification's words.
- **Explain** it: what changes, which influence it affects, and why.
- **Apply:** link each factor to a scenario detail (how many people, public or private, how hard the
  task, the person's traits or knowledge).
- **Evaluate:** support with Asch's variations; limitations of the line task and sample.
