## Section A

**1.** State what is meant by a gravitational field, and define gravitational field strength. **[2]**

**2.** Show that the unit N kg⁻¹ is equivalent to m s⁻². **[2]**

**3.** On the Moon, a rock sample of mass 0.250 kg hangs at rest from a newton-meter reading 0.405 N.

**(a)** Calculate the gravitational field strength on the Moon's surface. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the weight of the same sample on the Earth's surface. **[1]**

**4.** Explain why the mass used to measure a gravitational field should be small. **[1]**

**5.** State **two** differences between a gravitational field and an electric field. **[2]**

**6.** At a point on the line joining two stars, the field due to one star is 6.0 × 10⁻³ N kg⁻¹ towards it and the field due to the other is 2.5 × 10⁻³ N kg⁻¹ in the opposite direction. Calculate the resultant field strength and state its direction. **[2]**

---

## Section B

**7.** An isolated uniform sphere has a gravitational field around it.

**(a)** Sketch, in words or with a labelled description, the field lines around the sphere. State **three** features your sketch must show. **[3]**

**(b)** Explain how the field lines show that the field strength decreases with distance from the sphere. **[1]**

**8.** Over a small region close to the Earth's surface, the gravitational field is described as uniform.

**(a)** Describe the field lines in this region. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain why the field may be treated as uniform here even though the Earth's field is radial. **[2]**

**(c)** State **one** AS Level equation that depends on this assumption. **[1]**

**9.** A space probe of mass 15 kg is at point P. The gravitational field due to body A at P is 0.80 N kg⁻¹ due west, and the field due to body B is 0.60 N kg⁻¹ due south. No other masses have a significant effect.

**(a)** Calculate the magnitude and direction of the resultant field at P. **[3]**

**(b)** Calculate the resultant gravitational force on the probe. **[1]**

**(c)** The probe's engines are off. State its acceleration. **[1]**

**10.** A planet has a moon whose mass is much smaller than the planet's mass.

**(a)** Describe the pattern of field lines around the planet and moon together. **[3]**

**(b)** Explain why the point on the line between them where the resultant field is zero lies closer to the moon. **[2]**

**11.** A student says: "A stone lying on the ground has an acceleration of 9.81 m s⁻², because it is in a gravitational field of 9.81 N kg⁻¹."

**(a)** Explain why the student is wrong. **[2]**

**(b)** The same stone, of mass 0.20 kg, is now falling through air, with an air resistance force of 0.30 N. Calculate its acceleration. **[2]**

**12.** A student hangs a 0.500 kg mass from a newton-meter in a lift that is accelerating upwards at 1.2 m s⁻². The newton-meter reads 5.5 N. The student divides 5.5 N by 0.500 kg and claims the gravitational field strength in the lift is 11 N kg⁻¹.

**(a)** Show that the reading of 5.5 N is consistent with g = 9.81 N kg⁻¹. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain the error in the student's method. **[1]**

**13.** A diagram of the field around a single planet shows two field lines that cross just above the surface, and one line with an arrow pointing away from the planet. Explain why each of these features is incorrect. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** A region in which a mass experiences a force [1]. Gravitational field strength is the force per unit mass on a small mass placed at that point [1].

**2.** 1 N = 1 kg m s⁻² (from F = ma) [1]; so N kg⁻¹ = kg m s⁻² ÷ kg = m s⁻² [1].

**3. (a)** g = F/m = 0.405 ÷ 0.250 = 1.62 N kg⁻¹ [1].

**(b)** W = mg = 0.250 × 9.81 = 2.45 N [1].

**4.** So that its own gravitational field does not significantly change the field being measured (or move the source) [1].

**5.** Any two [1 each]: gravitational fields act on mass, electric fields act on charge; gravitational forces are always attractive, electric forces can be attractive or repulsive; gravitational field strength is force per unit mass, electric field strength is force per unit positive charge.

**6.** The fields are in opposite directions, so subtract: 6.0 × 10⁻³ − 2.5 × 10⁻³ = 3.5 × 10⁻³ N kg⁻¹ [1], towards the first star (the one producing the larger field) [1].

**7. (a)** Any three [1 each]: straight radial lines that would meet at the centre of the sphere; arrows pointing towards the sphere; lines evenly spaced around the sphere; lines meeting the surface at right angles; lines not crossing.

**(b)** The lines get further apart with distance from the sphere, showing the field becomes weaker [1].

**8. (a)** Parallel lines [1], equally spaced, perpendicular to the surface and pointing downwards [1].

**(b)** The radius of the Earth is very large compared with the size of the region [1], so the radial lines are almost parallel and their spacing is almost unchanged over that region: the field has nearly the same strength and direction [1].

**(c)** ΔE_P = mgΔh (or W = mg with g constant, or the equations of uniformly accelerated motion with a = g) [1].

**9. (a)** Magnitude = √(0.80² + 0.60²) = 1.0 N kg⁻¹ [1]. Angle = tan⁻¹(0.60 ÷ 0.80) = 37° [1], south of west [1].

**(b)** F = mg = 15 × 1.0 = 15 N (in the same direction as the field) [1].

**(c)** 1.0 m s⁻², in the direction of the resultant field [1].

**10. (a)** Lines curve inwards towards both the planet and the moon [1]; close to each body the lines are nearly radial, with many more lines ending on the planet than on the moon [1]; there is a region between them, nearer the moon, where no lines pass (the point of zero field) [1].

**(b)** Field strength depends on the mass of the body and decreases with distance [1]; the moon has the smaller mass, so its field equals the planet's field only at a point much closer to the moon [1].

**11. (a)** The field strength gives the force per unit mass due to gravity, not the acceleration [1]; the stone also experiences a normal contact force from the ground equal to its weight, so the resultant force and the acceleration are zero [1].

**(b)** Resultant force = 0.20 × 9.81 − 0.30 = 1.66 N [1]; a = 1.66 ÷ 0.20 = 8.3 m s⁻² [1].

**12. (a)** Resultant upward force = ma, so reading − mg = ma [1]; reading = 0.500 × (9.81 + 1.2) = 5.5 N [1].

**(b)** The reading equals the gravitational force only when the mass has no acceleration; here it also includes the force needed to accelerate the mass, so F/m from the reading is not the field strength [1].

**13.** Lines cannot cross because the force on a mass at any point has only one direction [1]; the arrow must point towards the planet because gravitational forces are always attractive [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Defining field strength without "per unit mass".
- Adding perpendicular field strengths as plain numbers.
- Forgetting to state the direction of a resultant field.
- Field-line sketches with outward arrows, uneven spacing or curved lines around an isolated sphere.
- Treating field strength as the acceleration of an object that has other forces acting on it.
- Using a reading taken in an accelerating frame as if it measured gravity alone.
