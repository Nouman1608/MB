This guide goes deep on sub-topic **3(b) Properties of waves**,
statements 3.2 to 3.9 of the Pearson Edexcel International GCSE in
Physics (4PH1), Issue 4. None of the statements carries a "P", so all can
be assessed on both papers. For Topic 3 as a whole, see the
[Waves study guide](/resources/edexcel-igcse-physics-waves/).

## What the specification asks

- **3.2** explain the difference between longitudinal and transverse waves
- **3.3** know the definitions of amplitude, wavefront, frequency,
  wavelength and period of a wave
- **3.4** know that waves transfer energy and information without
  transferring matter
- **3.5** know and use wave speed = frequency × wavelength (v = f × λ)
- **3.6** use frequency = 1 ÷ time period (f = 1 ÷ T)
- **3.7** use these relationships in different contexts, including sound
  waves and electromagnetic waves
- **3.8** explain why there is a change in the observed frequency and
  wavelength of a wave when its source is moving relative to an observer,
  and that this is known as the Doppler effect
- **3.9** explain that all waves can be reflected and refracted

## Transverse and longitudinal waves (3.2)

A wave is a travelling disturbance. The particles (or the field) oscillate
about fixed positions while the wave moves on.

| | Transverse | Longitudinal |
|---|---|---|
| direction of oscillation | at right angles (perpendicular) to the direction the wave travels | along (parallel to) the direction the wave travels |
| features | crests (peaks) and troughs | compressions (particles close together) and rarefactions (particles spread out) |
| examples | light and all electromagnetic waves, water waves, waves on a rope or string | sound waves, push-pull waves on a slinky spring |

A slinky spring shows both. Moving one end from side to side sends a
transverse wave along it. Pushing and pulling the end along the slinky's
length sends a longitudinal wave of compressions and rarefactions.

When asked to **explain** the difference, compare the direction of
oscillation with the direction of energy transfer for **both** types in
one answer.

## Definitions (3.3)

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| amplitude | the maximum displacement of a point on the wave from its rest (undisturbed) position |
| wavelength (λ) | the distance from one point on a wave to the same point on the next wave, for example crest to crest |
| frequency (f) | the number of waves passing a point each second (or produced by the source each second), in Hz |
| period (T) | the time taken for one complete wave to pass a point, in s |
| wavefront | a line joining points on a wave that are at the same point in their cycle, such as a line along a crest |

Two precise points matter. Amplitude is measured from the rest position
to a crest, not from crest to trough (that is twice the amplitude).
Wavelength is a distance along the direction of travel, not a height.

For a longitudinal wave, the wavelength is the distance from the centre
of one compression to the centre of the next.

## Energy and information, not matter (3.4)

A cork floating on a pond bobs up and down as ripples pass but does not
travel across the pond with them. Air molecules in a sound wave vibrate
backwards and forwards about fixed positions and do not travel from the
loudspeaker to your ear. What travels is energy (the cork gains kinetic
energy; your eardrum is made to vibrate) and information (speech, music,
television and radio signals, data in optical fibres).

## The wave equations (3.5–3.7)

```
v = f × λ           wave speed (m/s) = frequency (Hz) × wavelength (m)
f = 1 / T           frequency (Hz) = 1 / time period (s)
```

v = f × λ follows from speed = distance ÷ time: in one period T, the wave
moves forward one wavelength λ, so v = λ ÷ T = f × λ.

The relationships apply to all waves. Speeds for context: sound in air is
about 340 m/s (faster in liquids and solids); electromagnetic waves travel
at 3.0 × 10⁸ m/s in free space; water waves in a ripple tank travel at
centimetres per second.

**Worked example 1: a ripple tank.** A dipper makes 12 waves in 4.0 s. The
distance across 5 complete wavelengths is 7.5 cm.

```
f = 12 / 4.0 = 3.0 Hz
λ = 7.5 cm / 5 = 1.5 cm = 0.015 m
v = f × λ = 3.0 × 0.015 = 0.045 m/s
T = 1 / f = 1 / 3.0 = 0.33 s
```

*Commentary.* Measuring across several wavelengths and dividing gives a
more accurate λ than measuring one. Converting cm to m gives the speed in
m/s.

**Worked example 2: sound.** A tuning fork vibrates at 256 Hz. Sound
travels at 340 m/s in air.

```
λ = v / f = 340 / 256 = 1.33 m ≈ 1.3 m
```

## Reflection and refraction (3.9)

All waves can be **reflected** (bounce back from a surface) and
**refracted** (change speed, and usually direction, when they pass into a
different medium).

- Water waves reflect from the wall of a ripple tank; sound reflects from
  a cliff as an echo; light reflects from a mirror.
- Water waves travel more slowly in shallow water. Where the waves cross
  from deep to shallow water, their speed and wavelength decrease, and if
  they meet the boundary at an angle, they change direction.
- Light slows down when it enters glass from air and bends towards the
  normal. Sound refracts too, for example when it passes between air
  layers at different temperatures.

In refraction the **frequency stays the same**: the source sets the
frequency, and waves cannot be created or lost at the boundary. Since
v = f × λ, a lower speed means a shorter wavelength.

**Worked example 3.** Water waves of frequency 5.0 Hz travel at 0.30 m/s
in deep water and 0.20 m/s in shallow water.

```
deep:    λ = 0.30 / 5.0 = 0.060 m
shallow: λ = 0.20 / 5.0 = 0.040 m
```

## The Doppler effect (3.8)

When a source of waves moves towards an observer, each wave is emitted a
little closer to the observer than the previous one. The wavefronts are
squashed together in front of the source, so the observed **wavelength
is shorter** and the observed **frequency is higher**. When the source
moves away, the wavefronts are spread out behind it: longer wavelength,
lower frequency.

The source itself emits the same frequency throughout. Only the observed
values change, and only while there is relative motion between source and
observer. Examples: the pitch of a passing siren falls as it goes past;
light from galaxies moving away from us is shifted to longer wavelengths
(red-shifted), which is used in Topic 8.

## Common mistakes

- Defining amplitude as the height from trough to crest.
- Saying sound is transverse, or that "particles travel with the wave".
- Saying frequency changes in refraction. Speed and wavelength change;
  frequency does not.
- Forgetting to convert cm or kHz before using v = f × λ.
- Describing the Doppler effect as the source "changing its frequency".
  The observed frequency changes because of relative motion.

## Exam technique with command words

- **Explain** the difference between wave types: one sentence for each
  type, both comparing oscillation direction with travel direction.
- **State** a definition: use the precise wording, with "from the rest
  position" for amplitude and "each second" for frequency.
- **Calculate**: equation, converted values, answer with unit.
- **Explain** the Doppler effect: describe the wavefronts bunching (or
  spreading), then the effect on wavelength, then on frequency.
