## 1. Elasticity as one idea

Every elasticity has the same structure:

**Elasticity = percentage change in the responding variable ÷ percentage change in the causing variable**

Percentages remove units: a fall of 200 tickets means little until you know whether the cinema sells 400 or 40,000. The result is a pure number that can be compared across goods and markets.

Read every coefficient in two ways. The **sign** tells you the direction of the relationship. The **size** (ignoring the sign) tells you the strength: above 1 is elastic (proportionately more responsive), below 1 is inelastic (proportionately less responsive).

## 2. Price elasticity of demand

PED = % change in quantity demanded ÷ % change in the good's own price.

Because demand curves slope downwards, PED is negative for almost every good. Writing the minus sign shows you understand the inverse relationship.

**Worked example 1 (invented Palace Cinema).** The price of an evening ticket rises from £8 to £10. Weekly attendance falls from 2,000 to 1,700.

- % change in price = (10 − 8) ÷ 8 × 100 = **+25%**
- % change in quantity = (1,700 − 2,000) ÷ 2,000 × 100 = **−15%**
- PED = −15 ÷ 25 = **−0.6**, so demand is price inelastic.

Interpretation: a 1% rise in price cuts attendance by about 0.6%. The likely reason is the lack of close substitutes for a night out at that cinema in that town.

| Value of PED (size) | Name | Diagram to draw |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | Perfectly inelastic | Vertical demand curve |
| Between 0 and 1 | Inelastic | Steep curve |
| 1 | Unitary | Rectangular hyperbola (every price × quantity rectangle has the same area) |
| Greater than 1 | Elastic | Shallow curve |
| Infinity | Perfectly elastic | Horizontal demand curve |

Slope and elasticity are not the same thing: along a straight-line demand curve the slope is constant but PED falls from elastic at high prices to inelastic at low prices. When comparing an elastic and an inelastic curve, make them cross at the starting price.

## 3. PED and total revenue

Total revenue (TR) = price × quantity. A price change pulls TR in two directions: each unit earns more (or less), but fewer (or more) units are sold. PED tells you which effect wins.

| Demand is | Price rises | Price falls |
|---|---|---|
| Inelastic | TR rises | TR falls |
| Unitary | TR unchanged | TR unchanged |
| Elastic | TR falls | TR rises |

Check with Palace Cinema: TR was 8 × 2,000 = £16,000 and becomes 10 × 1,700 = **£17,000**. Inelastic demand plus a price rise gives higher revenue.

**Worked example 2 (invented snack brand).** A supermarket cuts the price of a branded snack from £1.20 to £1.08 (−10%) and weekly sales rise from 5,000 to 6,000 (+20%). PED = 20 ÷ −10 = **−2.0** (elastic). TR rises from £6,000 to **£6,480**.

On a diagram, TR is the rectangle under the price. After a price rise, the area gained is "extra price × new quantity" and the area lost is "old price × units no longer sold"; state which is larger.

A rise in revenue is not the same as a rise in profit. If extra units cost money to produce, the snack brand's profit could fall even though TR rose. Say so in an evaluation.

## 4. Income and cross elasticity of demand

**YED** = % change in quantity demanded ÷ % change in real income.

| YED | Type of good | Example reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| Negative | Inferior | Value-brand pasta: buyers trade up when incomes rise |
| 0 to 1 | Normal, income inelastic | Bread: demand rises a little as incomes rise |
| Above 1 | Normal, income elastic (luxury) | Holidays abroad: spending rises faster than income |

Worked figures: incomes rise 4%. Holidays abroad +10% gives YED = **+2.5**. Bread +1% gives **+0.25**. Value-brand pasta −3% gives **−0.75**.

**XED** = % change in quantity demanded of good A ÷ % change in the price of good B.

- **Positive** XED: substitutes. Bus fares rise 10% and train journeys rise 3%, so XED = **+0.3** (weak substitutes).
- **Negative** XED: complements. Petrol rises 12% and sales of large cars fall 6%, so XED = **−0.5**.
- **Zero** XED: unrelated goods.

The size of XED shows how close the relationship is. A firm whose rival's price cut produces an XED of +2.0 faces fierce competition.

## 5. Price elasticity of supply

PES = % change in quantity supplied ÷ % change in price. It is positive because supply curves slope upwards.

**Worked example 3 (invented strawberry growers).** The market price rises 20%. The next day growers can only pick a little more fruit that was already ripe: quantity supplied rises 2%, so PES = **0.1**. After a full season, with more land planted, quantity rises 16%: PES = **0.8**. Supply becomes more elastic as time passes.

| PES | Diagram |
|---|---|
| 0 | Vertical supply curve |
| Between 0 and 1 (inelastic) | Straight line that starts on the quantity axis |
| 1 (unitary) | Any straight line through the origin |
| Greater than 1 (elastic) | Straight line that starts on the price axis |
| Infinity | Horizontal supply curve |

## 6. Evaluating the determinants

| Elasticity | Main determinants | Evaluation point |
|---|---|---|
| PED | Number and closeness of substitutes; share of income; necessity or habit; time period; how broadly the market is defined | "Petrol" is inelastic, but one brand of petrol on a road with four stations is elastic. Definition of the market often matters most |
| YED | Whether the good is a necessity or luxury; current income level | The same good can be a luxury in a low-income country and a necessity in a high-income one |
| XED | How closely goods substitute or complement in use | Brand loyalty weakens substitution even between similar products |
| PES | Spare capacity; stocks; ease of switching factors; production time; time period | A firm with idle machines and full warehouses can respond fast; farming and housing cannot |

## 7. Usefulness and significance

**For firms.** PED guides pricing: raise price where demand is inelastic, cut it where elastic. YED helps firms plan for booms and recessions; a firm selling income-elastic goods should expect sharp falls in sales in a downturn. XED shows which rivals matter and which complementary products to price together (cheap printers, dear ink).

**For government.** An indirect tax raises most revenue, and changes consumption least, when PED is low. That is why goods with inelastic demand are attractive to tax, but also why such taxes do little to cut consumption of a harmful good. Low PES explains why prices of farm goods and housing swing sharply when demand changes.

**Limits.** Estimates use past data, while tastes and substitutes change. Elasticity varies along a curve, so a value calculated for a small change may not hold for a large one. The ceteris paribus assumption rarely holds in real data. Firms seldom know their exact PED and may face rivals who respond to a price change.

## Annotated model answer

*Question (data response style):* "Using the information on Palace Cinema, evaluate whether the cinema should raise its ticket price again." 

> PED was −0.6, so demand was inelastic and the rise from £8 to £10 increased revenue from £16,000 to £17,000 *[uses the data]*. Since most costs of a cinema are fixed, the 300 lost customers save little cost, so profit probably rose too *[analysis beyond revenue]*. However, PED is likely to become more elastic at higher prices and over time, as customers try streaming or a cinema in a nearby town *[determinants applied]*. Fewer visitors also means less spent on food and drink, which is a complement with negative XED *[links two elasticities]*. A further rise is sensible only if the cinema is confident demand stays inelastic, so a small trial rise would be the safer choice *[judgement with a condition]*.

## Common mistakes

- Dividing the percentages the wrong way up: the responding variable goes on top.
- Using absolute changes instead of percentage changes.
- Calling a steep curve "inelastic" everywhere, or confusing slope with elasticity.
- Saying a price rise always increases revenue.

## Exam technique for OCR command words

- **Calculate:** show the two percentage changes, the division and the answer to two decimal places; state the sign.
- **Explain:** give the definition, apply it to the case and link the chain to an outcome, for example revenue.
- **Explain, with the aid of a diagram:** draw the curves crossing at the starting point, label axes (price, quantity), shade the revenue rectangles and refer to the diagram in your writing.
- **Evaluate:** weigh determinants and limits of the estimate, then reach a judgement that depends on a stated condition, such as time period or the closeness of substitutes.
