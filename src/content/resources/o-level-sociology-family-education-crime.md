This guide covers **Paper 2 Family, Education and Crime**, for
Cambridge O Level Sociology 2251, Version 3, 2025–2027 series. Paper 2
covers three further named topics, and candidates answer questions on
2 of the 3 — unlike Paper 1, where Question 1 (Research Methods) is
compulsory alongside a choice of one more.

## Where this fits in 2251

Paper 2 turns from the foundational concepts and methods covered in
Paper 1 to three specific social institutions and phenomena — the
family, the education system, and crime and deviance — each examined
through the lens of the research methods and sociological concepts
Paper 1 introduces. Because candidates choose 2 of the 3 topics on
this paper, it is possible to specialise, but the syllabus still
expects candidates to be able to apply Paper 1's research methods
content to whichever two topics they choose, so Research Methods
remains relevant preparation for this paper too, not just Paper 1.

## Syllabus coverage

**CAMBRIDGE O LEVEL SOCIOLOGY 2251 — PAPER 2 FAMILY, EDUCATION AND
CRIME**

- 2.1 Family — the different forms family takes across societies, the
functions the family performs, and how family structures and roles
are changing
- 2.2 Education — the role and functions of education in society, and
factors affecting educational achievement
- 2.3 Crime, Deviance and Social Control — how crime and deviance are
defined and measured, explanations for criminal and deviant
behaviour, and the formal and informal methods societies use to
control it

## How to approach it

Because candidates answer on only 2 of these 3 topics, a practical
revision strategy is to prepare all three to a reasonable standard
early, then commit to genuinely deep revision on two closer to the
exam — abandoning a topic too early risks being caught out if the
exam's specific questions on the other two are less comfortable than
expected. For Family (2.1), strong answers move beyond describing the
nuclear family as a fixed norm and can discuss real variation — 
extended families, single-parent families, and changing patterns such
as smaller family size or delayed marriage — and can explain a
function of the family (such as primary socialisation or economic
support) with a specific example rather than naming the function
alone.

Education (2.2) rewards being able to discuss more than one
explanation for why some students achieve more than others — factors
such as social class, parental attitudes, school resources and peer
groups are all legitimate angles, and the strongest answers weigh more
than one factor against each other rather than presenting a single
cause as the whole explanation. A common error is treating educational
achievement as explained entirely by one factor (usually the student's
own effort or ability) without engaging with the sociological factors
the syllabus specifically asks about.

Crime, Deviance and Social Control (2.3) is often the most engaging
topic to students but also where vague, common-sense answers replace
sociological explanation most often — being able to name and briefly
explain a specific sociological theory of why crime occurs (rather
than simply describing what crime is), and to distinguish formal
social control (police, courts, laws) from informal social control
(family disapproval, peer pressure, community norms), is what this
sub-topic actually tests. Measuring crime is also worth revising
directly: official crime statistics have known limitations (such as
under-reporting and differences in how offences are recorded), and
being able to explain why the "true" crime rate may differ from
recorded statistics is a frequently tested idea.

Across all three topics, the strongest preparation links back to
Paper 1: practise suggesting which research method would suit
studying a specific family, education or crime-related question, and
why, since examiners reward candidates who treat the syllabus as one
connected body of knowledge rather than three separate topics with no
relationship to the methods content examined elsewhere.

## Worked example: explaining educational achievement

A typical exam question asks candidates to explain why some students
achieve more highly than others. A weak answer states "some students
work harder than others" and stops there. A stronger answer identifies
a specific sociological factor — for instance, that children from
homes with more books, more parental involvement in homework, and more
familiarity with the vocabulary and expectations schools use may enter
education with an advantage sometimes described as cultural capital —
and explains, in the candidate's own words, the mechanism by which
that factor plausibly affects achievement, rather than simply naming
the factor. Where two factors are given in a question (for example,
home background and school resources), the strongest answers weigh
them against each other and reach a reasoned judgement about which
matters more in a given case, rather than describing each in
isolation.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- Treating "the family" as a single fixed type rather than
  acknowledging genuine variation in family structure across societies
  and over time.
- Explaining educational achievement using only common-sense
  assumptions about effort or intelligence, without engaging any of
  the sociological factors the syllabus specifies.
- Describing what a crime is without explaining why it occurs — a
  definition is not, by itself, an explanation.
- Confusing formal social control (laws, police, courts) with informal
  social control (family, peers, community norms), or treating them as
  interchangeable rather than distinct mechanisms.
- Forgetting that official crime statistics have known limitations,
  and presenting recorded crime figures as if they were a complete and
  accurate picture of all crime that actually occurs.
