This guide covers **Topic 3: Globalisation of trade** in Cambridge O Level Commerce (7100), following the
site's existing guides to [Commerce and Production](/resources/o-level-cambridge-commerce-commerce-and-production/)
and [Commercial Operations](/resources/o-level-cambridge-commerce-commercial-operations/). Where those two
topics establish what commerce is and how commercial operations function, Topic 3 widens the lens to
international trade and the forces reshaping it.

## Where this fits in 7100

Globalisation of trade is the third of six topics in 7100 and marks the point where the syllabus moves
from domestic commercial activity to the international dimension of trade -- multinational companies,
exchange rates, and the single global market these forces are creating.

## Syllabus coverage

**CAMBRIDGE O LEVEL COMMERCE (7100) -- TOPIC 3: GLOBALISATION OF TRADE**

- Globalisation: the development of a single market for the whole world, and the reasons driving it,
  including growth in multinational companies, improved technology enabling faster and cheaper
  transactions, lower transport costs, and changing consumer tastes
- Multinational companies: their characteristics, the reasons they expand into other countries, and the
  opportunities and conflicting interests they create for the countries in which they locate
- Exchange rates: the price of one currency in terms of another, and how the demand and supply of
  currencies determines that price
- International trade: the reduction of trade barriers and the movement of raw materials, goods and
  services across borders

## How to approach it

Globalisation questions frequently ask candidates to evaluate rather than simply describe -- for example,
weighing the benefits a multinational brings to a host country (jobs, investment, technology transfer)
against the potential drawbacks (profits leaving the country, competition for local firms, reduced
control over the local economy). Preparing both sides of this evaluation in advance, rather than only the
positive case, pays off across this topic.

## Why multinationals expand abroad

Multinational companies expand internationally for several overlapping reasons: access to new markets and
customers, lower production costs (such as cheaper labour or raw materials), avoiding trade barriers by
producing inside the target market rather than exporting into it, and spreading business risk across
several economies rather than depending on one. For a host country, this can bring real benefits --
employment, infrastructure investment, and the transfer of new technology and skills -- but also real
costs, including profits repatriated back to the multinational's home country rather than reinvested
locally, and the risk that a large multinational can out-compete smaller domestic firms.

## Exchange rates: the basics

An exchange rate expresses the value of one currency in terms of another -- for example, how many US
dollars a British pound will buy. Like any price in a market, an exchange rate is determined by the
interaction of demand and supply for each currency: if more people want to buy a currency than sell it,
its value (price) tends to rise, and vice versa. A weaker currency makes a country's exports cheaper for
foreign buyers (potentially boosting export sales) but makes imports more expensive for domestic buyers --
a trade-off commonly tested by asking candidates to explain the effect of a currency movement on a
country's importers or exporters.

## Trade barriers and their reduction

A trade barrier is any restriction that makes it harder or more expensive to trade across borders --
tariffs (taxes on imported goods), quotas (limits on the quantity that can be imported), and administrative
barriers (complex customs procedures or product standards a foreign firm must meet) are the most common
examples. Globalisation has been accompanied by a general trend toward reducing these barriers, on the
argument that freer trade allows countries to specialise in producing what they are relatively efficient
at and to access a wider range of goods and services at lower cost. This trend is not without critics,
however, since reduced barriers can also expose domestic producers to competition they struggle to match,
which is why trade policy remains a genuinely debated area rather than one with a single agreed answer.

## Worked example: evaluating a multinational's arrival

The routine below is an original model written for this resource, not a reproduction of any official past
paper or mark scheme.

```
Question style: "Discuss the impact on [Country X] of a large
multinational company opening a new factory there."

Step 1 - identify a specific benefit:
e.g. the factory creates several hundred new jobs, raising local
household incomes.

Step 2 - identify a specific drawback:
e.g. profits generated may be sent back to the multinational's
home country rather than reinvested locally.

Step 3 - weigh the two against each other, with reasoning:
e.g. argue that the employment benefit is likely to outweigh the
profit-repatriation drawback in the short term, but the balance
may shift if the multinational later relocates production
elsewhere.

Step 4 - conclude with a judgement, not just a list of points:
a strong answer commits to a reasoned overall view rather than
simply listing pros and cons without a conclusion.
```

## Common mistakes

Describing globalisation only in terms of "the world becoming more connected" without naming specific
drivers (multinational growth, technology, transport costs, consumer tastes). Discussing multinational
companies only positively, without addressing the genuine conflicts of interest they can create for a host
country. Explaining exchange rate movements without linking demand and supply of the currency to the price
change described. Failing to reach a conclusion in evaluate-style questions, leaving a list of points
unweighed against each other.

## Quick revision checklist

- Be able to name several specific drivers of globalisation, not just describe the general trend.
- Prepare both a benefit and a drawback of multinational companies locating in a host country.
- Explain how demand and supply for a currency determines its exchange rate.
- Practise "discuss" and "evaluate" style questions with a structure that reaches a reasoned conclusion.
- Link exchange rate changes to their specific effect on importers or exporters, rather than describing
  the change in isolation.
