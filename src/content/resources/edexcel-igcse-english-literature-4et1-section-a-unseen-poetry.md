This guide goes deeper on one part of Component 1 of Pearson Edexcel International GCSE English
Literature (4ET1): **Section A, Unseen Poetry**. It is the first of the three sections of the
two-hour, closed-book Paper 1. For the paper as a whole, including how Section A sits beside the
anthology and prose sections, start with the
[Poetry and Modern Prose study guide](/resources/igcse-edexcel-english-literature-poetry-and-modern-prose/).

## What Section A covers

You are given a poem you have not studied, printed on the question paper, and you write **one
essay** about it. The section is worth **20 marks** (about 22% of Paper 1) and is assessed on
**AO2** alone: analysing the language, form and structure used by a writer to create meanings and
effects. The paper recommends **35 minutes** for this section, proportionally more time than its
marks suggest, because part of that time is for reading a poem cold.

There is nothing to memorise. The poem could be from any period or place, and you are not expected
to know who wrote it or when. What the examiner is looking for is a reader who can work out what
the poem is doing and explain, precisely, **how** the poet makes it do that.

## What AO2 means for an unseen poem

AO2 asks about three things. It helps to keep them separate in your head, then let them overlap in
your writing.

- **Language**: individual word choices, imagery (simile, metaphor, personification), sound
  (alliteration, assonance, sibilance, rhyme), tone and register, and the grammar of the poem
  (questions, commands, pronouns, tense).
- **Form**: the shape the poem takes as a kind of poem: stanza lengths, line lengths, rhyme scheme
  or its absence, regular or broken rhythm, and any recognisable form such as a sonnet or a ballad.
- **Structure**: how the poem moves from beginning to end. Where does it turn? What changes
  between the first and last stanza? Which line is isolated or held back? Where do sentences run
  across line or stanza breaks (enjambment) and where are they stopped mid-line (caesura)?

"Meanings and effects" is the part many answers forget. Naming a technique earns very little on its
own; the credit is for explaining what that choice does to the reader's understanding or feeling.

## A reading method for 35 minutes

1. **First read, for sense (3 minutes).** Who is speaking, to whom, about what situation? What is
   the mood at the start and at the end? Read the title as part of the poem.
2. **Find the turn.** Most poems shift somewhere: a "but" or "yet", a change of tense, a new
   stanza that looks different, a final line set apart. That shift is often the key to your
   argument.
3. **Second read, annotating (5 minutes).** Mark six to eight short details: one or two words,
   rarely more than a line. Next to each, write the effect in a few words, not just the technique.
4. **Decide your overview.** One sentence that answers the question: "The poet presents X as Y,
   and the poem moves from A to B."
5. **Plan three or four paragraphs** that follow the poem's movement, each mixing language, form
   and structure. Write for about 22 minutes and keep two or three minutes to reread.

## Worked example 1: an original unseen poem

The poem below was written for this guide; it is not a set text.

> **Night Shift**
>
> My mother comes home as the street wakes up,  
> her coat still cold with the hours she has kept,  
> and puts the kettle on as if the day  
> were something she could boil and pour and drink.
>
> She does not ask about my homework. I  
> do not ask about the ward. We share  
> the kitchen like two clocks set to different towns,  
> both right, both ticking, neither of us there.
>
> Then she is gone upstairs. The curtains close  
> on morning. I eat my breakfast in her dark.

**Question:** Explore how the poet presents the relationship between the speaker and her mother. [20]

**Overview:** The poet presents a loving but disconnected relationship, in which mother and child
live side by side on different timetables; the poem moves from close observation to an ending in
which the child adapts to the mother's world.

**Sample paragraph:**

> The poet shows that the two are close in space but apart in time. The simile "like two clocks
> set to different towns" suggests both are working properly ("both right, both ticking") yet
> cannot meet, so the distance is no one's fault. The structure reinforces this: the sentence "I /
> do not ask" is split across a line break, leaving "I" isolated at the end of the line, as if the
> speaker is left on her own while her mother is present. The matching negatives, "She does not
> ask" and "do not ask", balance the two silences, so the reader sees a shared habit rather than
> blame. Even the final word of the stanza, "there", answers the rhyme set up by "share", but
> its meaning undercuts the sharing: neither of them is really present.

**Why it works:** each quotation is a few words long; each feature is tied to an effect; language
(the simile), structure (the line break) and form (rhyme) are woven together in the service of one
idea; and the paragraph keeps answering the question about the relationship.

A final paragraph would look at the shortened last stanza. Two lines instead of four make the ending
feel abrupt, matching the mother's sudden exit, and the paradox "The curtains close / on morning"
shows the child adjusting to a reversed day. "I eat my breakfast in her dark" is tender as well as
lonely: the speaker chooses to share the mother's night.

## Worked example 2: from feature-spotting to analysis

The same detail, written three ways:

| Level of response | Example |
|---|---|
| Feature-spotting | "The poet uses enjambment in stanza 2." |
| Explained | "Enjambment after 'I' makes the reader pause on the speaker." |
| Analysed, linked to the question | "Breaking 'I / do not ask' across the line leaves the speaker stranded at the line's edge, so the form enacts how isolated she feels even in her mother's company." |

Only the third connects the method, its effect and the focus of the question. Aim for that in every
paragraph.

## Key ideas to carry into any unseen poem

- **Tentative language is a strength.** "Perhaps", "this could suggest" and "a reader might feel"
  show you are interpreting, and they allow you to offer a second reading of an ambiguous line.
- **Structure is the easiest marks to miss.** Ask what changes from start to finish and why the
  poem ends where it does.
- **Form only counts when it means something.** "The rhyme scheme is ABCB" is description; "the
  only full rhyme in the stanza makes the absence of togetherness ring louder" is analysis.
- **Follow the poem's order.** Working through the poem chronologically, while grouping points by
  idea, stops you missing the turn and the ending.

## Common mistakes

- **Paraphrasing.** Retelling the poem line by line shows understanding but not analysis.
- **Long quotations.** Copying out whole lines hides which word you are analysing. Pick the word.
- **Inventing a biography.** Guessing at the poet's life or the date of the poem earns nothing in
  an AO2-only section, and can lead you away from the text.
- **Technique lists.** A paragraph on "language", then "form", then "structure" tends to become a
  checklist. Organise by idea instead.
- **Ignoring the ending.** The last lines often change how the whole poem reads; save time for them.
- **One-word effects.** "This creates imagery" or "this makes the reader want to read on" could be
  said of any poem.

## Exam technique and command words

Section A questions typically ask you to **explore how** the poet presents a feeling, a person, an
experience or an idea. "Explore" invites you to look at several possibilities and develop them, not to
give one fixed answer; "how" tells you the marks are for methods and their effects. If the question
offers prompts, use them as a checklist for coverage, but keep your own argument in control.

Because Section A comes first, it is tempting to overrun. Keep to about 35 minutes: Section C is worth
twice as much and needs its full time. A focused answer of four well-developed paragraphs usually
scores better than a long one that covers everything thinly.
