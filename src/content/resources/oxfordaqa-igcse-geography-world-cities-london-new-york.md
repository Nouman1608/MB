This guide covers **3.2.1.2 World cities – London and New York**, part
of Section A Urban Issues and Challenges in Topic 2 Challenges in the
Human Environment, OxfordAQA International GCSE Geography (9230),
Version 4.3. The [Urban Issues and Challenges study guide](/resources/igcse-oxfordaqa-geography-urban-issues-and-challenges/)
sets out the whole of Section A; this page gives the place-specific
detail you need for the world-city questions.

The specification uses London or New York as the example world city,
so you need one in depth. This guide covers both, so you can use
whichever your class studies and add a comparison if you learn both.

## What makes a world city

A **world city** (or global city) has influence far beyond its own
country. It is a centre of finance and business, has headquarters of
transnational corporations, is a hub for international transport, and
has global cultural and political importance. World cities are mostly
found in North America, Europe and East Asia, with London, New York and
Tokyo usually ranked at the top. A growing number are in newly-emerging
economies, such as Shanghai, Mumbai and São Paulo, as economic power
spreads.

## London

**Location.** London is the capital of the United Kingdom, in south-east
England on the River Thames. Its population was about 8.8 million at the
2021 census. Globally, it lies on the Greenwich Meridian (0° longitude),
so its working day overlaps with Asian markets in the morning and North
American markets in the afternoon. Heathrow is one of the world's busiest
international airports, and Eurostar trains link London to mainland
Europe through the Channel Tunnel.

**Migration.**

- *International*: London has attracted migrants for centuries. After
  1948, people arrived from the Caribbean (the Windrush generation),
  followed by large numbers from South Asia, Africa and, after the
  European Union expanded in 2004, Eastern Europe. Around four in ten
  Londoners were born outside the UK.
- *National*: many young adults move to London from the rest of the UK
  for university and work, while some older people and families move
  out to towns in the South East.
- *Character*: London is one of the world's most culturally diverse
  cities, with hundreds of languages spoken, distinctive neighbourhoods
  such as Brick Lane (Bangladeshi community) and events such as the
  Notting Hill Carnival, which grew from the Caribbean community.

**Importance.**

- *Finance and business*: the City of London (the "Square Mile") and
  Canary Wharf hold the Bank of England, the London Stock Exchange and
  the offices of many global banks, insurers and law firms.
- *Trade*: international airports and the port facilities on the
  Thames estuary.
- *Culture*: West End theatres, the British Museum, Tate Modern,
  international sport and global media.
- *Politics*: the seat of UK government at Westminster, and the base of
  international bodies such as the Commonwealth Secretariat and the
  International Maritime Organization.

## New York

**Location.** New York is the largest city in the USA, on the Atlantic
coast of the north-east at the mouth of the Hudson River, with a large
natural harbour. It has five boroughs: Manhattan, Brooklyn, Queens, the
Bronx and Staten Island, with a population of about 8.8 million at the
2020 census. The wider metropolitan area spreads into New Jersey, Long
Island and beyond.

**Migration.**

- *International*: between 1892 and 1954, millions of European migrants
  entered the USA through Ellis Island in New York harbour. Later
  arrivals came from the Caribbean, Latin America and Asia. More than a
  third of New Yorkers today were born abroad.
- *National*: during the 20th century, many African Americans moved
  from the southern states to northern cities in the Great Migration,
  and Harlem became a centre of African American culture.
- *Character*: neighbourhoods such as Chinatown and Little Italy in
  Manhattan reflect waves of migration, and the city's food, music and
  festivals show this diversity.

**Importance.**

- *Finance and business*: Wall Street, the New York Stock Exchange and
  Nasdaq; headquarters of many global companies.
- *Trade*: the Port of New York and New Jersey, and JFK International
  Airport.
- *Culture*: Broadway theatres, the Metropolitan Museum of Art, global
  media, fashion and advertising.
- *Politics*: the headquarters of the United Nations is in Manhattan,
  even though New York is not the national capital.

## Opportunities

| Type | London | New York |
|---|---|---|
| Economic | Jobs in finance, tech, creative industries and tourism | Jobs in finance, media, tourism and services |
| Social | World-class universities, hospitals, museums (many national museums free to enter) | Universities, museums, diverse culture and entertainment |
| Environmental (urban greening) | Royal Parks; Queen Elizabeth Olympic Park; London named the world's first National Park City in 2019 | Central Park; the High Line on a disused elevated railway; Freshkills Park on a former landfill site |

## Challenges

- **Deprivation and inequality**: in London, boroughs such as Tower
  Hamlets and Newham have high rates of child poverty close to the
  wealth of Canary Wharf; in New York, parts of the Bronx are among the
  poorest areas in the city, while much of Manhattan is very wealthy.
  Inequality shows in housing, education, health and employment.
- **Housing**: very high house prices and rents in both cities;
  overcrowding; gentrification in areas such as Hackney in London or
  Harlem in New York can push out poorer residents.
- **Dereliction**: former docks, factories and rail yards left empty as
  industry declined.
- **Brownfield and greenfield**: London's Metropolitan Green Belt limits
  building on greenfield land, so more housing must go on brownfield
  sites, which are often costly to clean up.
- **Urban sprawl**: New York's suburbs spread across a large region,
  increasing car commuting.
- **Food supply**: both cities import food from around the world, sold
  through major wholesale markets such as New Covent Garden in London
  and Hunts Point in the Bronx.
- **Traffic congestion**: London introduced a Congestion Charge in 2003
  and an Ultra Low Emission Zone in 2019; New York began congestion
  pricing for vehicles entering Manhattan south of 60th Street in
  January 2025.
- **Waste, water and energy**: London's Victorian sewers overflow into
  the Thames after heavy rain, so the Thames Tideway Tunnel was built to
  capture this sewage. New York draws most of its water from reservoirs
  in the Catskill and Delaware watersheds upstate and exports much of its
  rubbish to other states since the Fresh Kills landfill closed in 2001.
  Hurricane Sandy (2012) flooded parts of New York, cutting power to
  lower Manhattan.

## Flagship regeneration: London Docklands

**Why it was needed.** London's docks east of the city were once among
the busiest in the world, but from the 1960s ships grew too large for
them and container trade moved downstream to Tilbury. The docks closed
between the late 1960s and 1981, leaving derelict land, high
unemployment and poor housing.

**Main features.** The London Docklands Development Corporation
(1981–1998) attracted private investment. Canary Wharf became a second
financial district; the Docklands Light Railway (1987), London City
Airport (1987) and the Jubilee line extension (1999) improved transport;
new housing, shops and the ExCeL exhibition centre followed.

**Impacts.**

- *Economic*: many thousands of new office jobs, but many were taken by
  commuters with skills that local former dock workers lacked.
- *Social*: better transport and services, but much new housing was too
  expensive for existing residents, causing resentment.
- *Environmental*: derelict land and docks were cleaned up, and
  waterside areas improved, though the area is densely built up.

**Judgement**: the project transformed the area economically, but the
benefits were unevenly shared with the original community.

A New York alternative is the **High Line**, a disused elevated freight
railway on Manhattan's West Side reopened as a park from 2009. It
brought visitors and investment to a run-down area, but raised property
values and rents nearby.

## Worked example

**Question:** Explain how international migration has affected the
character of a world city you have studied. **[4]**

```
Point:        London has received migrants from the Caribbean after 1948,
              South Asia and, after 2004, Eastern Europe
Development:  around four in ten Londoners were born outside the UK,
              so the city is highly multicultural
Effect:       distinct neighbourhoods, such as Brick Lane's Bangladeshi
              community, and events like the Notting Hill Carnival,
              which grew from the Caribbean community
Link:         migration has shaped London's food, culture and labour
              force, making it more attractive as a global city
```

## Common mistakes

- Writing about "big cities" with no named places, dates or schemes.
- Mixing up national and international migration.
- Listing challenges without saying who is affected or why.
- Describing a regeneration project without its reasons and judged
  impacts.
