These notes condense section 6.3, *Ethnicity and educational attainment*, of Cambridge International A Level Sociology (9699), assessed on Paper 3 – Education. Four syllabus strands: racism in schools; cultural explanations for patterns; ethnicity and subcultures; and the relationship between ethnicity, social class and gender.

Full explanations: [ethnicity and educational attainment study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-sociology-9699-ethnicity-educational-attainment/).

## Core argument

There is no single "minority gap": some minority groups achieve above average and others below. Explanations must account for this variation. School processes (racism, labelling, setting) and cultural factors (aspirations, community networks, responses to minority status) interact with class and gender, so single-factor answers fall short.

## The pattern

- In England, groups such as Chinese and Indian pupils have long achieved above average; Black Caribbean, Gypsy/Roma and Irish Traveller pupils below it.
- White British pupils eligible for free school meals are among the lower-attaining groups.
- Class profiles differ between groups, so compare like with like.
- Patterns differ by country: describe your own society's evidence equally carefully.

## Racism in schools

| Study | Finding | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Wright, *Race Relations in the Primary School* (1992) | Teachers assumed Asian pupils had poor English and excluded them from discussion; Black Caribbean boys disciplined more often and harshly | Racism through routine judgements |
| Foster (1990) | Little evidence of teacher racism in one multi-ethnic comprehensive; earlier studies may over-interpret | Sceptical counter-evidence |
| Coard (1971); Gillborn (1990, 2008); Gillborn and Youdell (2000) | Labelling, institutional racism, triage | Overview studies |

**Institutional racism** works through routines (setting, exclusions, tiers, curriculum), not just attitudes.

## Cultural explanations

1. **Deficit:** Bereiter and Engelmann (1966) — poor Black American children's language called deficient; criticised by Labov.
2. **Supportive culture:** Modood (2004) — **ethnic capital**: aspirations and networks help children succeed despite modest class position.
3. **Responses to minority status:**
   - Ogbu — **voluntary** minorities (chose to migrate; see education as a route up) versus **involuntary** minorities (incorporated by slavery or conquest; may distrust institutions);
   - Fordham and Ogbu (1986) — "the burden of acting white".
- **Swann Report, *Education for All* (1985):** rejected IQ explanations; gave weight to socio-economic factors; diversity-aware education for all schools.

**Evaluation:** cultural accounts explain variation between groups but can treat each group as having one culture; Ogbu underplays variation within groups.

## Ethnicity and subcultures

| Study | Group | Finding |
|---|---|---|
| Fuller (1984) | Black girls, London comprehensive | Pro-education, anti-school; used success to reject labels |
| Mac an Ghaill, *Young, Gifted and Black* (1988) | Black and Asian students | "Rasta Heads" and "Warriors" resisted school; "Black Sisters" pro-education, anti-school |
| Sewell (1997) | African-Caribbean boys | Conformists, innovators, retreators, rebels |

**Point:** the same pressures produce different responses depending on gender, peers and aims.

## Ethnicity, class and gender

Three tests to apply:
1. Does the gap remain with class held constant?
2. Does it differ for boys and girls?
3. Does class work the same way for every group?

**Policy debate:** multicultural education (add minority cultures; raise self-esteem) versus anti-racist education (tackle institutional practice). Stone (1981): Black pupils did not have low self-esteem; multicultural lessons could distract from core skills.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Ethnicity | Shared cultural identity, such as language, religion or heritage |
| Racism | Unfair treatment based on supposed race or ethnicity |
| Institutional racism | Discrimination built into routines and procedures |
| Ethnocentrism | Viewing one's own culture as the norm |
| Ethnic capital | Aspirations and networks within an ethnic group that support success |
| Voluntary minority | Group that migrated by choice |
| Involuntary minority | Group incorporated by slavery, conquest or colonisation |
| "Acting white" | Peer view that academic effort betrays a Black identity |
| Pro-education, anti-school | Valuing qualifications while rejecting school's authority |
| Intersectionality | Class, ethnicity and gender combining |
| Multicultural education | Including minority cultures in the curriculum |
| Anti-racist education | Challenging institutional racism in schools |

## Exam traps

- Writing "ethnic minorities underachieve" — some groups achieve above average.
- Assuming racism requires racist individuals.
- Using Ogbu as fact rather than a debated model.
- Ignoring class differences between groups.
- Treating anti-school subcultures as anti-education; Fuller shows otherwise.

## Self-test

1. Why is "the minority gap" a misleading phrase?
2. What did Wright (1992) find about Asian pupils?
3. What was Foster's (1990) argument?
4. Define institutional racism in your own words.
5. What is ethnic capital, and who used the idea?
6. Distinguish voluntary from involuntary minorities.
7. What did Fordham and Ogbu mean by "the burden of acting white"?
8. Describe Fuller's Black girls in four words.
9. Name the three groups in Mac an Ghaill (1988).
10. What did the Swann Report conclude about IQ?
11. Give Stone's criticism of multicultural education.

### Answers

1. Some minority groups achieve above average and others below, so there is no single gap.
2. Teachers assumed they had poor English, used simplified language and left them out of discussions.
3. He found little evidence of teacher racism in the comprehensive he studied and argued earlier studies over-interpreted interactions.
4. Discrimination built into an organisation's routines, such as setting and exclusion procedures, whatever individuals intend.
5. High aspirations and community networks within some minority groups that help children succeed; Modood (2004).
6. Voluntary minorities migrated by choice and tend to see education as a route upward; involuntary minorities were incorporated through slavery or conquest and may distrust institutions.
7. Some Black American students' peers saw academic effort as abandoning Black identity.
8. Pro-education but anti-school.
9. The Rasta Heads, the Warriors and the Black Sisters.
10. IQ did not explain ethnic differences in attainment; socio-economic factors were given weight instead.
11. Black pupils did not suffer from low self-esteem, and multicultural lessons could distract from teaching core skills.
