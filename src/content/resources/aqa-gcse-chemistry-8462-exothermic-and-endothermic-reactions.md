## 4.5.1.1 Energy transfer in reactions

**Energy is conserved** in chemical reactions. The energy in the universe at the end of a reaction is the same
as before it. If a reaction transfers energy to the surroundings, the product molecules must have **less
energy** than the reactants, by the amount transferred.

| | Exothermic | Endothermic |
|---|---|---|
| Energy flow | Transfers energy **to** the surroundings | Takes in energy **from** the surroundings |
| Temperature of surroundings | **Rises** | **Falls** |
| Products compared with reactants | Lower energy | Higher energy |
| Examples | Combustion, many oxidation reactions, neutralisation | Thermal decomposition, citric acid with sodium hydrogencarbonate |

Remember **exo** means "out" and **endo** means "in", from the point of view of energy leaving or entering the
reacting mixture.

### Everyday uses

- **Self-heating cans** (for drinks or food) and **hand warmers** use exothermic reactions.
- **Sports injury packs** (instant cold packs) are based on endothermic reactions.

When the specification asks you to **evaluate** uses, you compare points such as: how easy it is to use, whether
it can be reused, the maximum temperature reached, safety, cost and the waste produced. For example, a
reusable hand warmer based on crystallisation can be reset by heating in boiling water, but a hand warmer based
on iron oxidising is single use. A cold pack has the advantage of working immediately without a fridge, but
it cannot be refrozen and it lasts for only a short time.

### Measuring temperature change

The specification limits you to **measurement of temperature change**. Calculating energy in joules or ΔH
from a practical is not required. You must be able to say a reaction is exothermic or endothermic **from the
temperature change of the surroundings**.

#### Required practical 4

You investigate the variables that affect the temperature change in reacting solutions, such as acid with
metals, acid with carbonates, neutralisations or displacement of metals. A typical method:

1. Measure a fixed volume of solution (for example 25 cm³) into a polystyrene cup with a lid, inside a
   beaker for stability.
2. Record the starting temperature.
3. Add the other reactant (a measured mass of metal, or a measured volume of a second solution).
4. Stir and record the highest (or lowest) temperature reached.
5. Calculate the **temperature change** = final temperature − start temperature.

Polystyrene and a lid reduce heat loss. A thermometer reads to 0.5 or 1 °C; a digital probe is more precise.
Variables you can change include concentration, mass or volume of reactant, and which metal is used. Control
all the others. A table such as the one below lets you see the pattern:

| Metal (same mass of powder, 25 cm³ of the same copper sulfate solution) | Start / °C | Highest / °C | Temperature change / °C |
|---|---|---|---|
| Magnesium | 21.0 | 52.5 | +31.5 |
| Zinc | 21.0 | 33.0 | +12.0 |
| Iron | 21.0 | 26.5 | +5.5 |

Larger temperature rises go with more reactive metals displacing copper, so all three are exothermic, with
magnesium transferring the most energy to the solution.

## 4.5.1.2 Reaction profiles

Reactions occur only when particles **collide** with **enough energy**. The minimum energy that colliding
particles must have in order to react is the **activation energy**. A **reaction profile** (energy level
diagram) shows the relative energies of reactants and products, the activation energy and the overall energy
change.

### Drawing a reaction profile

1. Draw axes. **Vertical axis: energy.** Horizontal axis: progress of reaction.
2. Draw a short horizontal line at the energy of the **reactants**, on the left.
3. Draw a short horizontal line at the energy of the **products**, on the right.
   - **Exothermic:** products lower than reactants.
   - **Endothermic:** products higher than reactants.
4. Join them with a **smooth curved line** that rises to a peak and comes down again.
5. Mark the **activation energy** with an arrow from the reactants' level **up to the top of the curve**.
6. Mark the **overall energy change** with an arrow between the reactant level and the product level, pointing
   in the direction of the change (down for exothermic, up for endothermic).

### Reading a profile

- **Products lower than reactants → exothermic.** Energy is released, so the surroundings get warmer.
- **Products higher than reactants → endothermic.**
- The **height of the peak above the reactants** is the activation energy.
- A reaction with a **high** activation energy needs more energy before it starts, so it may need heating,
  even if it is exothermic overall. A match must be struck to get the reaction going, then the energy released
  keeps it going.

Explain activation energy in one sentence for the exam: "the minimum energy particles must have when they
collide for a reaction to occur".

## 4.5.1.3 Energy change from bond energies (Higher tier only)

During a reaction:

- **Energy must be supplied to break bonds** in the reactants (this is endothermic).
- **Energy is released when bonds form** in the products (this is exothermic).

The **overall energy change** = energy needed to break bonds − energy released when bonds form. Bond energies
are given in **kJ/mol** and are supplied in the question.

- If the energy released on forming bonds is **greater** than the energy needed to break bonds, the reaction is
  **exothermic** (negative overall change).
- If the energy needed to break bonds is **greater**, the reaction is **endothermic** (positive overall change).

### Worked example 1: hydrogen and chlorine

*Bond energies in kJ/mol: H–H 436, Cl–Cl 243, H–Cl 432. Calculate the energy change for H₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl.*

```
Bonds broken:  1 x H-H + 1 x Cl-Cl = 436 + 243 = 679 kJ/mol
Bonds formed:  2 x H-Cl            = 2 x 432   = 864 kJ/mol
Energy change = 679 - 864 = -185 kJ/mol
```

The negative sign shows that the reaction is **exothermic**: more energy is released making bonds than is
needed to break them.

### Worked example 2: methane burning

*Bond energies in kJ/mol: C–H 413, O=O 498, C=O 805, O–H 464. Calculate the energy change for
CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O.*

```
Bonds broken:  4 x C-H + 2 x O=O = 4 x 413 + 2 x 498 = 1652 + 996 = 2648
Bonds formed:  2 x C=O + 4 x O-H = 2 x 805 + 4 x 464 = 1610 + 1856 = 3466
Energy change = 2648 - 3466 = -818 kJ/mol  (exothermic)
```

Count bonds carefully. Methane has four C–H bonds. Water has two O–H bonds each, so two molecules have four.

### Worked example 3: an endothermic reaction

*Bond energies in kJ/mol: N≡N 945, O=O 498, N=O 630. Calculate the energy change for N₂ + O₂ → 2NO.*

```
Bonds broken:  945 + 498 = 1443
Bonds formed:  2 x 630   = 1260
Energy change = 1443 - 1260 = +183 kJ/mol  (endothermic)
```

Here, more energy is needed to break bonds than is released on forming them, so the surroundings get colder.

## Exam technique

- Link everything to the temperature of the **surroundings**: it rises for exothermic and falls for endothermic.
- On a profile, label the **axes**, **reactants**, **products**, **activation energy** and **overall energy
  change**. Arrows must go the right way.
- In bond energy calculations, write out the number of each bond and show broken, formed and the difference.
  Give the answer with a sign and units.
- For evaluation questions, give a use, a benefit and a drawback; then reach a judgement.

## Common mistakes

- Saying exothermic reactions "make energy". Energy is transferred, not created.
- Drawing the activation energy arrow from the reactants to the products, or from the axis.
- Drawing the profile with straight lines instead of a curve.
- Reversing the sign in bond energy calculations (formed minus broken).
- Counting only one bond when the molecule has several, for example one C–H instead of four.
- Saying a reaction needing heating to start must be endothermic. The activation energy is a separate idea.
