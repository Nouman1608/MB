These questions cover sections **2.1 Use of symbols** and **2.2 Algebraic manipulation** of Topic 2, Equations, formulae and identities, in the Pearson Edexcel International GCSE Mathematics A (4MA1) specification, Issue 2 (November 2017), for the January and June series examined on it. Questions 1–4 use content on both tiers. Questions 5–12 are **Higher tier only** and are marked (Higher). A calculator may be used on every 4MA1 paper, but no calculator will do the algebra for you, so show every line: most marks here are for method.

Learn the content first in the [study guide](/resources/edexcel-igcse-maths-4ma1-use-of-symbols-and-algebraic-manipulation/) and the [revision notes](/resources/edexcel-igcse-maths-4ma1-use-of-symbols-and-algebraic-manipulation-revision-notes/). ## Questions

**1.** (Both tiers) Simplify

**(a)** t⁴ × t⁵ **[1]**
**(b)** w¹⁰ ÷ w⁻³ **[1]**
**(c)** (h³)⁴ **[1]**
**(d)** Write 1/y⁶ as a single power of y. **[1]**

**2.** (Both tiers) p = 3, q = −2 and r = 0.5.

**(a)** Work out the value of 4p − 5q. **[2]**
**(b)** Work out the value of p²q − 6r. **[2]**

**3.** (Both tiers)

**(a)** Simplify 7a + 3b − 2a − 8b. **[1]**
**(b)** Expand and simplify 3(2x − 5) − 4(x − 2). **[2]**
**(c)** Factorise fully 15m²n − 20mn². **[2]**

**4.** (Both tiers)

**(a)** Expand and simplify (y + 6)(y − 9). **[2]**
**(b)** Factorise x² + 3x − 40. **[2]**
**(c)** Factorise x² − 13x + 36. **[2]**

**5.** (Higher)

**(a)** Work out 81^(3/4). **[1]**
**(b)** Work out 125^(−1/3). **[1]**
**(c)** Work out (4/9)^(−3/2), giving your answer as a fraction. **[2]**
**(d)** Simplify fully (27x⁶y⁻³)^(2/3), giving your answer with positive powers only. **[2]**

**6.** (Higher) Expand and simplify (3x + 2)(x − 1)(2x + 5). **[3]**

**7.** (Higher) Factorise fully

**(a)** 8x² + 14x − 15 **[2]**
**(b)** 3x² − 75 **[2]**
**(c)** 25a² − 16b² **[1]**

**8.** (Higher)

**(a)** Simplify fully (2x² − 7x − 4)/(x² − 16). **[3]**
**(b)** Write 5/(x − 2) − 3/(x + 1) as a single fraction in its simplest form. **[3]**

**9.** (Higher)

**(a)** Write x² + 10x + 31 in the form (x + p)² + q. **[2]**
**(b)** Hence explain why x² + 10x + 31 is positive for all values of x. **[1]**
**(c)** Write 2x² − 12x + 7 in the form a(x + b)² + c. **[3]**

**10.** (Higher) Prove that the sum of the squares of any two consecutive integers is always odd. **[3]**

**11.** (Higher) A rectangle has length (2x + 3) cm and width (x − 1) cm. A square has sides of length (x + 2) cm.

**(a)** Show that the area of the rectangle is (2x² + x − 3) cm². **[2]**
**(b)** Show that the area of the rectangle minus the area of the square is (x² − 3x − 7) cm². **[2]**
**(c)** Write x² − 3x − 7 in the form (x + p)² + q. **[2]**
**(d)** Explain why x must be greater than 1. **[1]**

**12.** (Higher)

**(a)** Express (x + 3)/4 − (2x − 1)/6 as a single fraction in its simplest form. **[3]**
**(b)** Simplify fully (x² + 5x)/(x² + 2x − 15) ÷ 2x/(x + 1). **[4]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** t⁴⁺⁵ = **t⁹** [1]
**(b)** w¹⁰⁻⁽⁻³⁾ = **w¹³** [1]
**(c)** h³ˣ⁴ = **h¹²** [1]
**(d)** **y⁻⁶** [1]
*Examiner insight:* Each part is a single accuracy mark, so w⁷ in (b), from subtracting 3 instead of −3, scores nothing; there is no method mark to fall back on.

**2. (a)** 4(3) − 5(−2) [1] = 12 + 10 = **22** [1]
**(b)** (3)²(−2) − 6(0.5) [1] = −18 − 3 = **−21** [1]
*Examiner insight:* The method mark needs the correct substitution seen; writing 12 − 10 = 2 in (a) shows the sign of q was lost, so neither mark is earned.

**3. (a)** **5a − 5b** [1]
**(b)** 6x − 15 − 4x + 8 [1] = **2x − 7** [1]
**(c)** **5mn(3m − 4n)** [1] [1]
*Examiner insight:* In (c) a correct but partial factorisation, such as 5m(3mn − 4n²) or mn(15m − 20n), usually earns 1 of the 2 marks; "fully" needs both the 5 and mn outside.

**4. (a)** y² − 9y + 6y − 54 (at least three of the four terms correct) [1] = **y² − 3y − 54** [1]
**(b)** Two numbers with product −40 and sum +3: 8 and −5 [1]; **(x + 8)(x − 5)** [1]
**(c)** Product +36, sum −13: −4 and −9 [1]; **(x − 4)(x − 9)** [1]
*Examiner insight:* For factorising, the method mark is typically given for brackets that expand to give two of the three terms correctly, such as (x − 8)(x + 5); expand your answer to catch this.

**5. (a)** Fourth root of 81 is 3, 3³ = **27** [1]
**(b)** 1/125^(1/3) = **1/5** [1]
**(c)** (9/4)^(3/2) [1] = (3/2)³ = **27/8** [1]
**(d)** 27^(2/3) = 9 and x⁶ˣ⁽²ᐟ³⁾ = x⁴ (at least two of the three parts correct) [1]; y⁻³ˣ⁽²ᐟ³⁾ = y⁻², so **9x⁴/y²** [1]
*Examiner insight:* In (d) the answer 9x⁴y⁻² gets the method mark but not the accuracy mark, because the question asks for positive powers only.

**6.** (3x + 2)(x − 1) = 3x² − x − 2 [1]; (3x² − x − 2)(2x + 5) = 6x³ + 15x² − 2x² − 5x − 4x − 10 (all six terms, at most one error) [1] = **6x³ + 13x² − 9x − 10** [1]
*Examiner insight:* Marks are usually given for the first pair of brackets expanded correctly, then for the six-term expansion; an answer with no working that is wrong gets nothing.

**7. (a)** ac = −120, numbers 20 and −6: 8x² + 20x − 6x − 15 = 4x(2x + 5) − 3(2x + 5) [1]; **(4x − 3)(2x + 5)** [1]
**(b)** 3(x² − 25) [1] = **3(x − 5)(x + 5)** [1]
**(c)** **(5a − 4b)(5a + 4b)** [1]
*Examiner insight:* In (b), 3(x² − 25) or (3x − 15)(x + 5) gets only the method mark; "fully" means taking out the 3 and then using the difference of two squares.

**8. (a)** Numerator (2x + 1)(x − 4) [1]; denominator (x − 4)(x + 4) [1]; **(2x + 1)/(x + 4)** [1]
**(b)** [5(x + 1) − 3(x − 2)] / [(x − 2)(x + 1)] [1]; numerator 5x + 5 − 3x + 6 = 2x + 11 [1]; **(2x + 11)/[(x − 2)(x + 1)]** [1]
*Examiner insight:* In (b) the common-denominator mark can still be earned with a sign error, but 5x + 5 − 3x − 6 loses both the numerator mark and the final answer.

**9. (a)** (x + 5)² − 25 + 31 [1] = **(x + 5)² + 6** [1]
**(b)** (x + 5)² ≥ 0 for all x, so **(x + 5)² + 6 ≥ 6 > 0** [1]
**(c)** 2(x² − 6x) + 7 [1]; 2[(x − 3)² − 9] + 7 [1] = **2(x − 3)² − 11** [1]
*Examiner insight:* "Hence" in (b) means the explanation must use your completed-square form; testing a few values of x scores 0.

**10.** Let the integers be n and n + 1 [1]. n² + (n + 1)² = n² + n² + 2n + 1 = 2n² + 2n + 1 [1]. 2n² + 2n + 1 = 2(n² + n) + 1, which is one more than a multiple of 2, so **the sum is always odd** [1]
*Examiner insight:* The last mark needs the expression written as 2(…) + 1 with a statement; stopping at 2n² + 2n + 1 does not show it is odd.

**11. (a)** (2x + 3)(x − 1) = 2x² − 2x + 3x − 3 [1] = **2x² + x − 3** [1]
**(b)** Square: (x + 2)² = x² + 4x + 4 [1]; 2x² + x − 3 − (x² + 4x + 4) = **x² − 3x − 7** [1]
**(c)** (x − 1.5)² − 2.25 − 7 [1] = **(x − 1.5)² − 9.25** [1]
**(d)** The width x − 1 must be positive, so **x > 1** [1]
*Examiner insight:* On a "show that", every line must be written; jumping from (2x + 3)(x − 1) straight to the given answer earns no marks because the result is printed in the question.

**12. (a)** Common denominator 12: [3(x + 3) − 2(2x − 1)] / 12 [1]; 3x + 9 − 4x + 2 [1] = **(11 − x)/12** [1]
**(b)** x² + 5x = x(x + 5) and x² + 2x − 15 = (x + 5)(x − 3) [1]; multiply by the reciprocal (x + 1)/(2x) [1]; cancel x and (x + 5) [1] = **(x + 1)/[2(x − 3)]** [1]
*Examiner insight:* A common denominator of 24 in (a) is acceptable, but the answer must then be cancelled to (11 − x)/12 for the final mark, as the question asks for simplest form.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Multiplying indices when they should be added: t⁴ × t⁵ written as t²⁰.
- Substituting negatives without brackets, so (−2) becomes −2 and signs go wrong.
- Leaving out the HCF of the letters when factorising fully, or leaving out the number.
- Missing a common factor before factorising a quadratic, as in 3x² − 75.
- Losing a term when expanding three brackets: there must be six products before collecting.
- Putting a sign wrong in the second numerator when subtracting algebraic fractions.
- Cancelling terms that are not whole factors, such as the x² in (2x² − 7x − 4)/(x² − 16).
- Not multiplying the subtracted square by a when completing the square with a ≠ 1.
- On "show that" questions, jumping to the printed answer instead of showing each line.
- In proofs, giving numerical examples instead of general algebra, or omitting the final statement.
