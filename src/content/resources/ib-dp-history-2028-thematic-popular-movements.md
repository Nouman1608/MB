The thematic study **Popular movements (from 1750 CE onwards)** is one of four options in IB DP History (first assessment 2028). A class studies one thematic study, assessed in paper 2. The guide sets four inquiry questions, each with four lines of inquiry, and **prescribes no examples**. You and your teacher choose examples, and each line of inquiry must be explored with at least two examples from more than one region. This guide explains the framework, suggests an example bank, and shows how to build paper 2 answers. It is the same for SL and HL.

## What the guide means by popular movements

The guide defines popular movements as the collective efforts of a group of people to bring about a change. It lists movements for political freedoms and equality (civil rights, women, youth and labour movements), movements that advocate for an idea or issue (peace and environmental movements), movements for Indigenous rights, and independence and anti-colonial movements. You may organise your examples around one type or range widely.

## How the study is assessed

Paper 2 lasts 1 hour 45 minutes and is worth 25 marks. Section A (6 marks) asks you to analyse a specified concept using an example from your thematic study. In section B you answer one question: part (a) is a short response (4 marks) and part (b) is an essay (15 marks) using examples from at least two regions (Africa and the Middle East, the Americas, Asia and Oceania, Europe). The top essay band needs consistent analysis, a reasoned judgement and well-developed connections between examples.

## Inquiry question 1: Why did popular movements emerge?

**Lines of inquiry:** political factors, economic factors, role of ideas, social factors.

- **Political.** In India, exclusion from political power under British rule, the Rowlatt Acts of 1919 and the Amritsar massacre of that year increased support for the Indian National Congress. In the American South, black citizens were denied voting rights by state laws and intimidation.
- **Economic.** Polish workers protested against food price rises in 1980, which led to strikes at the Gdańsk shipyard. In the anti-apartheid struggle, low wages and restrictions on black workers' mobility were grievances.
- **Ideas.** Gandhi's ideas of non-violent resistance (satyagraha), Christian beliefs among civil rights leaders, and the ideas of equality and democracy shaped movements. For Aboriginal activists, the idea of land rights became central.
- **Social.** Discrimination, segregation and the exclusion of women from public life created common grievances. Education, newspapers and church networks helped people to organise.

## Inquiry question 2: How did popular movements create change?

**Lines of inquiry:** political participation, non-violent methods, cultural influence, violent methods.

- **Political participation.** Suffrage campaigners lobbied Parliament and held petitions and meetings. In South Africa, black voters formed the majority in the 1994 election, the first in which all adults could vote.
- **Non-violent methods.** Gandhi's Salt March (March to April 1930) was a protest against the salt tax. The Montgomery bus boycott (1955 to 1956) and the March on Washington (1963) used boycotts, marches and speeches. The Defiance Campaign in South Africa in 1952 used peaceful disobedience of unjust laws.
- **Cultural influence.** Music, art, newspapers and film spread movements' messages. Freedom songs in the American civil rights movement and in the South African struggle gave unity and spirit.
- **Violent methods.** The Women's Social and Political Union (the suffragettes) in Britain used window-smashing and arson in the years before 1914. After the Sharpeville killings in 1960, the African National Congress formed Umkhonto we Sizwe in 1961 and began a campaign of sabotage. Historians debate whether violent methods helped or harmed the movement's goals.

## Inquiry question 3: How were popular movements challenged?

**Lines of inquiry:** political opposition, divisions within the movement, violent opposition, resilience of traditional ideas.

- **Political opposition.** Governments passed laws and imprisoned leaders. In Poland, martial law was declared in December 1981 and Solidarity was banned. In Britain, the "Cat and Mouse Act" of 1913 allowed the release and re-arrest of hunger-striking suffragettes.
- **Divisions.** Suffragists and suffragettes disagreed on methods. In the Indian movement, disputes between the Congress and the Muslim League grew in the 1930s and 1940s. In the United States, activists argued over the pace of change and the role of non-violence, as in the rise of Black Power in the later 1960s.
- **Violent opposition.** Civil rights marchers were attacked in Selma, Alabama, in March 1965. In South Africa, police shot protesters at Sharpeville in 1960 and at Soweto in 1976.
- **Resilience of traditional ideas.** Beliefs in the separate roles of men and women, racial hierarchies, or the legitimacy of colonial rule persisted. Many Australians resisted Aboriginal land claims, and the High Court's Mabo decision of 1992 faced political backlash.

## Inquiry question 4: What was the impact of popular movements?

**Lines of inquiry:** political change, social change, experiences of women, experiences of marginalised groups.

- **Political.** The Civil Rights Act of 1964 and the Voting Rights Act of 1965 changed US law. The Representation of the People Act of 1918 gave the vote to women over 30 in Britain, and an act of 1928 gave equal voting rights to men and women. India became independent in 1947, though it was partitioned.
- **Social.** The ending of legal segregation changed public life in the American South, though economic inequality remained. In South Africa, the 1994 election was a political settlement, while social and economic inequalities persisted.
- **Women.** Women were central to many movements: Rosa Parks and the Women's Political Council in Montgomery, women in the Salt March and the Indian movement, and women in the suffrage campaign. Their leadership was often under-recognised.
- **Marginalised groups.** The 1967 referendum in Australia, passed by about 90 per cent of voters, enabled the federal government to make laws for Aboriginal people and to count them in the census. Later land-rights decisions, including Mabo in 1992, recognised native title.

## Making connections between examples

- **Similarity:** most movements combined mass participation with leadership and a powerful symbol.
- **Difference:** methods ranged from strict non-violence to sabotage, and the context (colony or democracy) shaped what was possible.
- **Pattern:** movements with a broad coalition and international attention often did better.
- **Exception:** Solidarity's success depended on wider changes in the Soviet bloc, not on its own strength alone.

## Common mistakes

- Telling the story of one movement and not answering the question.
- Treating a movement as united.
- Overlooking the role of women and marginalised groups.
- Using two examples from the same region in the essay.

## Frequently asked questions

**Does a movement need to be successful?** No. Failures and partial successes are good examples, especially for challenge and impact.

**Can a movement be both violent and non-violent?** Yes. Many used both, and the relationship between methods is a good line of analysis.
