## Section A: short questions

**1.** State what is meant by an element. **[1]**

**2.** Classify each sample as an element, a compound or a mixture.

**(a)** Particles that are all CH₄ molecules **[1]**
**(b)** Particles that are all P₄ molecules **[1]**
**(c)** A sample of N₂ molecules and O₂ molecules **[1]**
**(d)** Steel, which contains iron with a small amount of carbon not chemically combined **[1]**

**3.** Give **two** differences between a compound and a mixture. **[2]**

**4.** A sample of a white solid melts sharply at 80 °C. A second sample of the same compound melts from 74 °C to 78 °C. Explain which sample is pure. **[2]**

**5.** Name the separation method for each task.

**(a)** Collecting ethanol from a mixture of ethanol and water **[1]**
**(b)** Removing chalk powder from water **[1]**
**(c)** Finding out whether a green food colouring contains more than one dye **[1]**

**6.** In a chromatography experiment the solvent front moves 6.0 cm from the baseline. A spot moves 1.5 cm. Calculate the Rf value of the spot. **[1]**

## Section B: structured questions

**7.** A student distils blue copper(II) sulfate solution using simple distillation.

**(a)** Name the piece of apparatus where the water vapour turns back into liquid. **[1]**
**(b)** State where the thermometer bulb should be placed and explain why. **[2]**
**(c)** State the colour of the distillate and explain your answer. **[2]**
**(d)** Explain why simple distillation could not be used to separate two liquids that mix and have boiling points of 65 °C and 78 °C. **[1]**

**8.** A mixture of three liquids that mix completely is separated by fractional distillation.

| Liquid | Boiling point / °C |
|---|---|
| X | 56 |
| Y | 82 |
| Z | 118 |

**(a)** State which liquid is collected first and explain why. **[2]**
**(b)** Describe how the student knows when to change the collecting container. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain the purpose of the fractionating column. **[2]**

**9.** A student has a mixture of sand and sodium chloride. Describe how she could obtain a pure, dry sample of each solid. **[6]**

**10.** A student makes copper(II) sulfate crystals from a solution.

**(a)** Describe how the student can tell when enough water has been evaporated. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain why the solution is left to cool rather than heated until all the water has gone. **[2]**
**(c)** The crystals are washed with a little cold distilled water. Explain why the water must be cold and only a little is used. **[1]**

**11.** Four food colourings, A, B, C and D, and a sweet coating S are tested by paper chromatography in water. The solvent front is 10.0 cm above the baseline.

| Sample | Distances of spots from the baseline / cm |
|---|---|
| A | 2.0 |
| B | 4.5 |
| C | 6.5 |
| D | 4.5 and 8.0 |
| S | 2.0, 4.5 and 0 |

**(a)** Explain why the baseline is drawn in pencil and not in ink. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the Rf value of colouring C. **[1]**
**(c)** Identify the colourings that are present in S. Give a reason. **[2]**
**(d)** State what the spot at 0 cm tells you about S. **[1]**
**(e)** A student says S cannot contain D because only one of D's spots appears in S. Explain whether the student is right. **[2]**

**12.** Describe how to carry out a paper chromatography experiment to compare the dyes in three inks. Include how you would make sure the results are reliable and how you would calculate the Rf values. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** A substance made of only one type of atom [1].

**2. (a)** Compound [1].
**(b)** Element [1].
**(c)** Mixture [1].
**(d)** Mixture [1].
*Marking note:* In (b), P₄ has four atoms per molecule but only one type of atom, so it is an element.

**3.** Any two [1 each]: compounds are chemically combined but mixtures are not; compounds have a fixed composition but mixtures can vary; compounds have different properties from their elements but the parts of a mixture keep their properties; mixtures can be separated by physical methods but compounds need a chemical reaction.

**4.** The first sample is pure [1], because it has a sharp, fixed melting point; the second melts over a range (and lower), showing an impurity is present [1].

**5. (a)** Fractional distillation [1].
**(b)** Filtration [1].
**(c)** Paper chromatography [1].

**6.** 1.5 ÷ 6.0 = 0.25 [1].

**7. (a)** The condenser [1].
**(b)** Level with the side arm (at the opening to the condenser) [1], so it measures the temperature of the vapour leaving the flask [1].
**(c)** Colourless [1]. Only water boils and passes into the condenser; the copper(II) sulfate stays in the flask [1].
**(d)** Both liquids would boil and distil over together because their boiling points are close, so they would not be separated [1].

**8. (a)** X [1], because it has the lowest boiling point, so it reaches the top of the column first [1].
**(b)** When the thermometer reading starts to rise above the steady temperature [1].
**(c)** Vapour condenses and re-boils many times on the surfaces in the column [1], so only the liquid with the lowest boiling point reaches the top while the others run back into the flask [1].

**9.** One mark per point, up to 6:
- Add water to the mixture and stir (warm if needed) so that the sodium chloride dissolves [1].
- Filter the mixture [1].
- Sand stays in the filter paper as the residue; sodium chloride solution is the filtrate [1].
- Wash the sand with distilled water and dry it in a warm oven or between filter papers [1].
- Heat the filtrate to evaporate some of the water until crystals form on a cold glass rod, then leave it to cool [1].
- Filter off the crystals and dry them between filter papers [1].
*Marking note:* Each solid needs its own route to a dry sample; an answer that stops at "evaporate the filtrate" misses the crystal steps.

**10. (a)** A drop on a cold glass rod forms crystals [1].
**(b)** Heating to dryness can make the crystals lose their water of crystallisation / decompose [1], and soluble impurities would stay in the solid instead of the remaining solution [1].
**(c)** So that only a little of the crystals dissolves (solubility is lower in cold water) [1].

**11. (a)** Ink would dissolve in the solvent and move up the paper, mixing with the spots [1].
**(b)** 6.5 ÷ 10.0 = 0.65 [1].
**(c)** A and B [1]: S has spots at the same heights as A (2.0 cm) and B (4.5 cm) [1].
**(d)** S contains a substance that is insoluble in water [1].
**(e)** The student is right [1]: if S contained D, both of D's spots (4.5 cm and 8.0 cm) would appear; there is no spot at 8.0 cm [1].

**12.** One mark per point, up to 6:
- Draw a baseline in pencil about 1–2 cm above the bottom of the paper [1].
- Put a small, concentrated spot of each ink on the baseline, spaced apart and labelled in pencil [1].
- Stand the paper in a container of solvent with the solvent level below the baseline [1].
- Put a lid on the container and leave it undisturbed until the solvent has nearly reached the top [1].
- Remove the paper, mark the solvent front in pencil straight away, and let it dry [1].
- Measure from the baseline to the centre of each spot and to the solvent front; Rf = distance moved by spot ÷ distance moved by solvent front; repeat and compare Rf values [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calling a mixture of elements a compound.
- Giving the distillate the colour of the solution.
- Forgetting the cooling step in crystallisation.
- Measuring Rf distances from the paper's edge, or forgetting to mark the solvent front.
- Treating the number of spots as the exact number of substances.
