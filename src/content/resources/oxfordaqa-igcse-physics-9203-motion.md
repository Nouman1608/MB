This guide covers **3.1.2 Motion** (statements a to d) of the OxfordAQA International GCSE Physics (9203) specification, for exams May/June 2018 onwards. The section is short, but its graph skills appear across the whole paper. The broader [forces, Hooke's law and distance-time guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-physics-forces-hookes-law-vectors-and-distance-time-motion/) introduces the ideas; this page goes further into drawing graphs from data, tangents, journeys that turn back, and unit conversions. For quick recall use the [Motion revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-physics-9203-motion-revision-notes/), and for exam-style questions the [Motion practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-physics-9203-motion-practice/).

## What the specification asks for

| Statement | Content |
|---|---|
| a | A straight-line journey can be shown as a distance-time graph, giving the distance from a certain point |
| b | Speed is the gradient of a distance-time graph |
| c | Velocity is speed in a given direction: v = s ÷ t, with s the displacement |
| d | The same equation gives the average speed for non-uniform motion |

Acceleration, velocity-time graphs and areas under graphs are in section 3.1.3 Resultant forces, so they are not part of this guide. Always check the y-axis label before you read a graph: a line that is flat on a distance-time graph means "stopped", but on a velocity-time graph it means "moving at constant velocity".

## Distance from a point

Statement a describes the graph as showing the object's **distance from a certain point**. That wording matters. If a runner sets off from a start line, runs 300 m along a straight track and then jogs back 100 m, the graph rises to 300 m and then **falls** to 200 m, because the runner is getting closer to the start line again. A falling line does not mean negative speed in the scalar sense; it means motion back towards the reference point. The size of the gradient still gives the speed.

| Shape of the line | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Horizontal | Stationary: distance from the point is not changing |
| Straight, rising | Moving away at constant speed |
| Straight, falling | Moving back towards the point at constant speed |
| Steeper straight line | Faster constant speed |
| Curve getting steeper | Speeding up |
| Curve getting less steep | Slowing down |

## Plotting a distance-time graph from data

Questions often give a table and ask you to plot it. Examiners look for:

1. Time on the x-axis and distance on the y-axis, both labelled with units.
2. Scales that are linear (equal steps) and use more than half of the grid.
3. Points plotted accurately, as small crosses.
4. A line of best fit: a ruled straight line where the points lie on one, or a smooth curve where they do not. Do not join the dots with short straight segments when the motion is clearly smooth.

## Gradient means speed

For a straight section:

```
speed = gradient = change in distance / change in time
```

Use a large triangle, at least half the length of the line, so that reading errors are small compared with the values. Write the coordinates you read so that the method is visible.

**Worked example 1: a delivery robot.** A robot moves along a straight corridor. Its distance from the charging point is recorded.

| Time (s) | 0 | 10 | 20 | 30 | 40 | 50 | 60 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Distance (m) | 0 | 8 | 16 | 16 | 16 | 10 | 4 |

- 0 to 20 s: gradient = (16 − 0) ÷ (20 − 0) = 0.80 m/s, moving away.
- 20 to 40 s: horizontal, so stationary for 20 s.
- 40 to 60 s: distance falls from 16 m to 4 m. Speed = (16 − 4) ÷ 20 = 0.60 m/s, moving back towards the charger.
- Total distance travelled = 16 + 12 = 28 m. Displacement at 60 s = 4 m from the charger, in the original direction.
- Average speed = 28 ÷ 60 = 0.47 m/s. Average velocity = 4 ÷ 60 = 0.067 m/s away from the charger.

The robot's average speed and average velocity differ because it turned back. Speed uses total distance; velocity uses displacement.

## Speed at an instant: the tangent

When the line is curved the speed is changing, and the gradient is different at every point. To find the speed at one time:

1. Mark the point on the curve at that time.
2. Using a ruler, draw a straight line that just touches the curve at that point and has the same slope as the curve there. This is the **tangent**.
3. Extend the tangent and draw a large triangle on it.
4. Gradient of the tangent = speed at that instant.

**Worked example 2: a car pulling away.** A tangent drawn at t = 5 s on a curved distance-time graph passes through (2 s, 0 m) and (8 s, 30 m).

```
speed = (30 - 0) / (8 - 2) = 30 / 6 = 5.0 m/s
```

The tangent's coordinates are not points on the curve, apart from the touching point, so read them from the tangent itself.

## Velocity and the equation v = s ÷ t

Velocity is speed in a given direction. The equation uses **displacement**, s, which is a vector:

```
v = s / t      v in m/s, s in m, t in s
```

Rearranged, s = v × t and t = s ÷ v. A velocity answer needs a direction: "12 m/s north" or "12 m/s towards the station".

## Average speed for non-uniform motion

Statement d extends the equation to journeys where speed changes. Average speed is total distance divided by total time, **including stops**:

```
average speed = total distance / total time
```

**Worked example 3: a train between stations.** A train covers 1.8 km in 2.5 minutes, including time spent speeding up and slowing down.

```
distance = 1.8 km = 1800 m
time = 2.5 x 60 = 150 s
average speed = 1800 / 150 = 12 m/s
```

At times the train moves faster than 12 m/s and at times slower; the average describes the whole journey.

**Worked example 4: two-part journey.** A cyclist rides 6.0 km at 5.0 m/s, then 4.0 km at 8.0 m/s.

```
time 1 = 6000 / 5.0 = 1200 s
time 2 = 4000 / 8.0 = 500 s
average speed = 10 000 / 1700 = 5.9 m/s
```

The mean of 5.0 and 8.0 is 6.5 m/s, which is wrong: more time was spent at the lower speed.

## Converting units

| Conversion | Method | Example |
|---|---|---|
| km to m | multiply by 1000 | 2.4 km = 2400 m |
| minutes to s | multiply by 60 | 3.5 min = 210 s |
| hours to s | multiply by 3600 | 0.25 h = 900 s |
| km/h to m/s | divide by 3.6 | 72 km/h = 20 m/s |
| m/s to km/h | multiply by 3.6 | 15 m/s = 54 km/h |

The factor 3.6 comes from 1000 m ÷ 3600 s. Convert before substituting into v = s ÷ t, not after.

## Common mistakes

- Reading a falling distance-time line as "slowing down". It shows motion back towards the reference point at a steady speed.
- Taking the gradient from a small triangle or from two plotted points that are not on the line.
- Using coordinates of the curve, not of the tangent, when finding speed at an instant.
- Averaging speeds instead of dividing total distance by total time.
- Giving a velocity with no direction.
- Leaving out the time spent stationary when finding average speed.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Describe** the motion shown by a graph: say what happens in each section and give values, for example "stationary for 20 s, then returns at 0.60 m/s".
- **Calculate**: show the gradient triangle values or the equation with substituted numbers.
- **Determine**: use data from the graph or table; show which values you read.
- **Compare** two journeys: make a comparative statement for each feature, such as "A is faster than B in the first 10 s".
- **Plot** and **draw**: follow the graph checklist above; a line of best fit should be one smooth line.
