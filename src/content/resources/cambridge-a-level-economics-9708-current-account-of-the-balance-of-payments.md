This guide covers **section 6.3 Current account of the balance of payments** of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Economics 9708 syllabus for examination in 2026, 2027 and 2028. It is AS Level content. The syllabus lists the "components of the current account", the "definition of balance and imbalances (deficit and surplus)", the "calculation of" four balances (trade in goods, trade in services, trade in goods and services, and the current account balance, CAB), the "causes of imbalances" and their "consequences ... for the domestic and external economy".

The broader [current account and policies study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-economics-current-account-of-the-balance-of-payments-and/) introduces the components and a full calculation. This page goes deeper on the skills that cost marks: placing a transaction in the right component with the right sign, working backwards to a missing figure, judging the size of an imbalance, and writing causes and consequences as chains. All countries and figures are invented.

## 6.3.1 Classifying transactions

The current account records flows of money for goods, services, income and transfers between residents of one country and the rest of the world over a period, usually a year. Ask two questions about any transaction:

1. **Which component?** Goods (physical items), services (intangible items), primary income (income earned from work or from owning assets abroad), or secondary income (transfers with nothing given in return).
2. **Credit or debit?** Money coming **into** the country is a credit; money going **out** is a debit. It helps to follow the money, not the item.

| Transaction, seen from Country A | Component | Sign |
|---|---|---|
| A Country A airline sells tickets to foreign tourists | Trade in services | Credit |
| Country A residents holiday abroad | Trade in services | Debit |
| A Country A farm sells rice to a foreign importer | Trade in goods | Credit |
| A foreign-owned factory in Country A sends its profit to its parent company | Primary income | Debit |
| A Country A citizen working abroad for a few months is paid by a foreign employer | Primary income | Credit |
| Migrants from Country A who now live abroad send money to their families at home | Secondary income | Credit |
| Country A's government gives aid in cash to another country | Secondary income | Debit |
| Country A pays interest on government bonds held by foreigners | Primary income | Debit |
| A Country A bank charges a foreign firm for financial advice | Trade in services | Credit |

Two classic traps sit in this table. **Tourism is a service**, and its sign follows the money: foreign tourists spending in Country A is an export of services, even though they consume it inside Country A. **Profit, interest and dividends are primary income**, not trade, because they are rewards for owning capital. Buying the factory or the bonds in the first place is not in the current account at all; at AS you only need to know that such purchases of assets are recorded elsewhere in the balance of payments.

**Balance and imbalance.** The balance on any line is credits minus debits. A positive result is a surplus and a negative result is a deficit. An **imbalance** in the current account means a deficit or a surplus; balance means credits equal debits.

## 6.3.2 Calculations, including working backwards

The four required results build on each other:

```
balance of trade in goods           = exports of goods - imports of goods
balance of trade in services        = exports of services - imports of services
balance of trade in goods and services = goods balance + services balance
current account balance (CAB)       = goods and services balance
                                      + primary income balance
                                      + secondary income balance
```

Examiners often give the CAB and ask for a missing figure. Rearrange the identity rather than guessing.

**Worked example 1: a missing figure.** Country B, one year, billion:

| Item | Value |
|---|---|
| Exports of goods | 84 |
| Imports of goods | 97 |
| Exports of services | 41 |
| Imports of services | ? |
| Primary income balance | +6 |
| Secondary income balance | −3 |
| Current account balance | −2 |

- Goods balance = 84 − 97 = −13.
- CAB = goods balance + services balance + primary + secondary, so −2 = −13 + services balance + 6 − 3.
- Services balance = −2 + 13 − 6 + 3 = +8.
- Imports of services = 41 − 8 = **33 billion**.
- Check: goods and services balance = −13 + 8 = −5; CAB = −5 + 6 − 3 = −2. Correct.

**Worked example 2: judging size.** Country C's CAB is −18 billion and its GDP is 450 billion, so the deficit is 18 ÷ 450 × 100 = **4% of GDP**. Country D's CAB is −30 billion with GDP of 1,500 billion, a deficit of **2% of GDP**. Country D's deficit is larger in money but smaller relative to the size of its economy, so it is less likely to be hard to finance. Always ask for, or calculate, the ratio to GDP before judging a deficit as large.

## 6.3.3 Causes of imbalances: sort by time scale

A useful way to organise causes is to ask whether they are likely to be **temporary** or **long-lasting**.

| Likely temporary | Likely long-lasting |
|---|---|
| A boom at home raising import spending | Low productivity making exports uncompetitive in price |
| A recession in major export markets | Poor quality, design or after-sales service (non-price competitiveness) |
| A one-off fall in the world price of a main export | Reliance on a narrow range of exports with falling demand |
| A temporary appreciation of the currency | Persistently higher inflation than trading partners |
| A poor harvest cutting farm exports | Large interest and profit payments to foreign owners of assets |

The distinction matters for evaluation: a deficit caused by a temporary boom may correct itself as the economy slows, while one caused by weak competitiveness will persist until productivity or quality improves. Causes of a **surplus** are broadly the reverse, for example strong competitiveness, high domestic saving that holds down imports, or a high world price for a main export.

## 6.3.4 Consequences for the domestic and the external economy

Write consequences as chains, and say which economy is affected.

**Domestic economy (deficit):**

- Net exports are a component of AD, so a larger deficit reduces AD: output and employment are lower in export industries and in firms competing with imports.
- A deficit means the country is paying out more foreign currency than it earns, which may push the exchange rate down. A lower currency raises import prices and can add to inflation.
- A deficit lets residents consume and invest more than the country currently produces, which can raise living standards now. The cost is that the deficit must be financed by borrowing from abroad or selling assets to foreigners, which creates future interest and profit outflows on the primary income line.

**External economy:**

- One country's deficit is matched by surpluses elsewhere, so trading partners gain export sales and jobs.
- If a large economy cuts its imports sharply, its partners' exports and growth fall.
- Large, lasting imbalances can lead to pressure for protection, harming trade for all.

**Surplus:** the reverse chains apply. A surplus adds to AD, which helps output and jobs but may add to inflation near full capacity; it may push the currency up; and it means residents consume less than the country produces.

**Annotated model answer.** *Question:* Explain why a current account deficit may not be a problem.

> A deficit is less worrying if it is small relative to GDP, because it is easy to finance. **[Size.]** If the deficit arises because firms are importing machinery, it raises future productive capacity, which may increase exports later and close the deficit. **[Cause: investment, not consumption.]** If it results from a temporary boom, imports will fall as growth slows. **[Time scale.]** It becomes a problem when it is large, persistent and financed by short-term borrowing. **[Condition.]**

## Common mistakes

- Treating tourism as trade in goods, or giving foreign tourists' spending a debit sign.
- Putting profit and interest flows in trade in services instead of primary income.
- Calling the balance of trade in goods and services the current account balance.
- Writing a deficit as a positive number.
- Describing a deficit as debt. A deficit is a flow over one year; debt is a stock built up over time.
- Judging the size of an imbalance without relating it to GDP.

## Exam technique for 9708 command words

- **Calculate:** set out each balance on its own line, keep signs, and state units.
- **Explain:** link a cause to a specific line of the account, then to the balance.
- **Assess:** consider size relative to GDP, cause, duration and how the deficit is financed, and reach a judgement.
