This guide covers **Theme 2 Local, National, International and Global
Areas of Interest**, the second of three themes in OxfordAQA
International GCSE Urdu (9264), for International GCSE exams May/June
2026 onwards. Where [Theme 1 Identity and Culture](/resources/igcse-oxfordaqa-urdu-identity-and-culture/)
covers personal and family life, Theme 2 moves outward to the
candidate's surroundings and to wider social and global concerns,
requiring a noticeably broader and more abstract vocabulary range.

## Where this fits in 9264

As with every theme in this specification, Theme 2 is examined across
both Paper 1 (Reading, Grammar and Meaning) and Paper 2 (Writing),
each 1 hour 45 minutes and worth 70 marks (50% of the GCSE). There is
no separate paper for this theme -- the same two skills papers draw on
content from all three themes.

## Syllabus coverage

**OXFORDAQA INTERNATIONAL GCSE URDU (9264) — THEME 2 LOCAL, NATIONAL, INTERNATIONAL AND GLOBAL AREAS OF INTEREST**

- 2.1 Home, Town, Neighbourhood and Region — describing where you live,
local amenities and facilities, and giving directions
- 2.2 Social Issues — vocabulary and structures for discussing issues
affecting society
- 2.3 Global Issues — vocabulary and structures for discussing issues
affecting the wider world, such as the environment
- 2.4 Travel and Tourism — vocabulary and structures for discussing
holidays, transport and travel experiences

## Vocabulary you need in place

This theme demands a wider and more opinion-heavy vocabulary than
Theme 1. Build reliable, active vocabulary in these fields:

- **Home and locality** — types of housing, rooms and facilities, local
amenities (shops, parks, transport links), giving and following
directions.
- **Social issues** — poverty, inequality, homelessness, and the
vocabulary needed to describe problems and propose solutions.
- **Global issues** — the environment, climate, pollution, and
recycling, alongside the vocabulary of cause and effect.
- **Travel and tourism** — modes of transport, booking and planning a
trip, describing a holiday experience (past tense), and comparing
destinations.

Because Theme 2 leans more heavily on opinion and issue-based language
than Theme 1's personal vocabulary, treat "problem and solution"
language -- the issue is..., a possible solution would be..., this
would help because... -- as core vocabulary in its own right, not an
optional extra.

## Structures that raise the grade

The structures that mattered for Theme 1 remain essential, but Theme 2
rewards a wider range applied to more abstract subject matter:

- **Conditional structures** — if we recycled more, the environment
would improve.
- **Passive-style and impersonal constructions** — used for describing
general social or global problems rather than personal experience.
- **Comparatives and superlatives** — applied to places (this town is
quieter than the city) and to travel experiences (the best holiday I
have had).
- **Cause and effect connectives** — because of this, as a result,
this leads to.
- **Past tense narrative** — for recounting a specific travel
experience, alongside present tense for describing ongoing local or
global issues.

Because this theme mixes description (home, travel) with argument
(social and global issues), practise switching registers within a
single piece of writing -- describing a place factually, then
evaluating a problem affecting it opinion-led.

## Approaching the tasks

For **reading and listening**, questions on social and global issues
often test whether a text is describing a problem, proposing a
solution, or evaluating how effective a solution has been --
distinguish these before answering rather than treating the whole
passage as one block of information. For **writing and speaking**,
travel and tourism tasks frequently ask for a past-tense recount
alongside a present-tense evaluation (what you did, and what you think
of it now), so practise moving between tenses within the same
response rather than defaulting to one throughout.

## Worked example

Task: *Write about an environmental problem in your area and suggest
what could be done about it.*

```
Weak:   there is a lot of pollution
        (states a problem, no detail, no solution, one tense)

Strong: describes the specific problem (present tense) -- traffic
          pollution near local schools
        gives a cause -- because of this, more cars drive through
          residential streets
        proposes a solution (conditional) -- if the town introduced a
          bicycle scheme, this would reduce
        gives a personal opinion with a reason -- I believe this
          matters because children's health is affected
```

Naming a specific local problem, giving its cause, proposing a
conditional solution, and closing with a justified opinion is a
reliable structure for the strongest responses in this theme.

## Common mistakes

Treating "social issues" and "global issues" as interchangeable, when
strong answers distinguish problems affecting a local community from
those affecting the wider world. Describing a problem without
proposing any solution, missing an easy opportunity to use conditional
structures. Writing about travel purely as a narrative without any
evaluative comment, missing the chance to demonstrate opinion-giving
language. Losing gender agreement when adjectives modify unfamiliar,
more abstract nouns (an issue, a solution) rather than the familiar
personal nouns practised in Theme 1.

## Quick revision checklist

- Build active vocabulary across all four sub-topics: home/locality,
social issues, global issues, and travel/tourism.
- Practise conditional structures for proposing solutions to problems.
- Rehearse switching between past-tense narrative and present-tense
evaluation within a single travel-themed response.
- Use cause-and-effect connectives when discussing social or global
issues.
- Distinguish "describing a problem" questions from "evaluating a
solution" questions in reading and listening tasks.
