These questions cover section 1.1, Main economic groups and factors of production, of the OCR GCSE (9-1) Economics specification (J205), Version 2.0, for first assessment in 2019. The specification is not tiered. All businesses and figures are invented. Revise first with the [1.1 study guide](/resources/ocr-gcse-economics-j205-main-economic-groups-and-factors-of-production/) or the [1.1 revision notes](/resources/ocr-gcse-economics-j205-main-economic-groups-and-factors-of-production-revision-notes/). ## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** State **two** roles of the government in the economy. **[2]**

**2.** Identify the factor of production in each case.

**(a)** oil under the seabed **[1]**
**(b)** a delivery van **[1]**
**(c)** a hairdresser **[1]**
**(d)** a person who risks her savings to open a gym **[1]**

**3.** State the reward to capital and the reward to enterprise. **[2]**

**4.** Explain why money held in a firm's bank account is not counted as capital. **[2]**

**5.** Explain **one** way producers depend on the government. **[2]**

**6.** A recording studio's weekly costs are rent £900, wages £2,100 and equipment hire £1,500. Calculate the percentage of these costs paid to labour and the percentage paid to capital. **[2]**

### Section B: longer and structured questions

**7.** Using an example, explain how one person can belong to more than one economic group. **[3]**

**8.** Read the extract.

> Hilltop Farm (invented) has 30 hectares of apple orchards and its own spring for water. Each autumn the owner, Priya, hires 15 pickers. She leases a tractor and a fruit-sorting machine. Priya put her own savings into the farm and keeps the profit, or bears the loss, each year.

**(a)** Identify **one** example of each factor of production from the extract. **[4]**
**(b)** The wages of pickers rise sharply. Explain how Priya might change the combination of factors she uses. **[2]**

**9.** A laundry business (invented) can use one of two methods.

| | Method A | Method B |
|---|---|---|
| Workers (paid £100 a day each) | 8 | 3 |
| Machine costs per day | £150 | £550 |
| Items cleaned per day | 400 | 450 |

**(a)** Calculate the cost per item for each method, to the nearest penny. **[4]**
**(b)** Explain which method a profit-seeking owner would choose, and give **one** reason the owner might still choose the other. **[2]**

**10.** Explain how a rise in consumer spending on holidays within the country could affect producers and the government. **[4]**

**11.** The government cuts the rate of income tax. Analyse the likely effects on consumers and producers. **[6]**

**12.** A supermarket chain is deciding whether to replace most checkout staff with self-service machines. Evaluate whether it should make this change. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** Any two of: collecting taxes [1]; providing services such as education or roads [1]; making laws and regulations; redistributing income through benefits; buying goods and services from firms. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* "Running the country" is too vague to score. Name an economic role.

**2. (a)** Land [1]. **(b)** Capital [1]. **(c)** Labour [1]. **(d)** Enterprise [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* For (d), the risk-taking is what makes it enterprise. A paid gym manager would be labour.

**3.** Capital: interest [1]. Enterprise: profit [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Rent goes to land and wages to labour; mixing these up is the usual error.

**4.** Capital is a man-made good used to produce other goods or services, such as a machine [1]. Money only buys factors; it does not produce anything itself, so it is finance [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* The second mark needs the reason, not just "money is not capital".

**5.** For example, producers rely on the government for infrastructure such as roads [1], which lets them deliver goods to customers and receive supplies [1]. Alternatively, government buys goods from firms, providing them with revenue. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* One way, developed, is better than two undeveloped ways.

**6.** Total = 900 + 2,100 + 1,500 = £4,500. Labour = 2,100 ÷ 4,500 × 100 = **46.7%** [1]. Capital = 1,500 ÷ 4,500 × 100 = **33.3%** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Rent is the reward to land, so it is not part of either answer.

**7.** One person can be a worker supplying labour to a firm [1] and a consumer who spends their wages on goods [1], as well as a taxpayer who pays income tax to the government [1]. For example, a bus driver earns wages, buys groceries and pays tax. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Three distinct roles, each linked to a different group, earn the three marks.

**8. (a)** Land: the orchards or the spring water [1]. Labour: the 15 pickers [1]. Capital: the tractor or the fruit-sorting machine [1]. Enterprise: Priya, who invested her savings and bears the risk [1].
**(b)** Labour has become more expensive relative to capital [1], so Priya may lease a harvesting machine and hire fewer pickers, making production more capital-intensive [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* In (a), quote the extract. "Workers" scores, but "money" for capital does not.

**9. (a)** Method A: (8 × 100) + 150 = £950 [1]; 950 ÷ 400 = **£2.38** per item [1]. Method B: (3 × 100) + 550 = £850 [1]; 850 ÷ 450 = **£1.89** per item [1].
**(b)** Method B, because its cost per item is lower, so profit per item is higher [1]. The owner might choose A because B's machines may break down or cost money up front, or because A keeps more jobs and suits small or varied orders [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Compare cost per item, not total daily cost: B also produces more.

**10.** More spending on domestic holidays raises the revenue of hotels, transport and tourist businesses [1], so they may hire more workers and buy more supplies from other firms [1]. The government receives more tax from the extra profits, wages and spending [1], which it could spend on services or infrastructure such as transport links [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Two marks for producers and two for the government. Each needs a link, not just "producers benefit".

**11.** A lower income tax rate raises consumers' net income [1], so many will spend more on goods and services [1]. Producers then sell more, so their revenue and possibly profit rise [1], and they may employ more workers to meet demand [1], which raises incomes further [1]. However, some consumers may save the extra income, so the rise in spending, and the gain for producers, may be smaller [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* "Analyse" rewards a chain. Each step should follow from the one before, linked with words such as *so* and *which means*.

**12.** Benefits: machines may lower labour costs over time [1], so cost per item falls and profit or price competitiveness improves [1]; machines can operate many tills at once with fewer staff, cutting queues [1]. Costs: the machines need money up front and maintenance [1]; checkout jobs are lost, a cost to workers and possibly to the local community [1]; some customers, such as those who prefer personal service, may shop elsewhere [1]. Judgement: the change is likely to be worthwhile in large, busy stores where savings are greatest [1], provided some staffed tills remain for customers who need them [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Without a judgement that weighs the points, the answer stays at analysis level. Say what your decision depends on.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calling money or loans capital.
- Writing "manager" for enterprise when the person bears no risk.
- Interdependence answers that never link one group to another.
- Ignoring the context in extract questions.
- Comparing total cost instead of cost per item.
- One-sided "evaluate" answers with no judgement.
