## Definitions to know

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Productive efficiency | Producing at the lowest cost per unit; for a firm, output at minimum AC, where MC = AC; for the economy, on the production possibility curve |
| Allocative efficiency | Resources go to the goods consumers value most; P = MC (MSB = MSC when externalities exist) |
| Pareto optimality | No one can be made better off without making someone else worse off |
| Pareto improvement | At least one person gains and nobody loses |
| Dynamic efficiency | Efficiency improving over time through investment and innovation |
| Market failure | The free market leaves resources allocated inefficiently, so welfare could be higher |
| Externality | A cost or benefit that falls on a third party outside the transaction |
| Asymmetric information | One party to a transaction knows more than the other |
| Moral hazard | Behaviour changes because someone else bears the cost of risk |

## Formulas and relationships

| Item | Relationship |
|---|---|
| Social cost | SC = PC + EC |
| Marginal social cost | MSC = MPC + MEC |
| Social benefit | SB = PB + EB |
| Marginal social benefit | MSB = MPB + MEB |
| Free market | MPB = MPC |
| Social optimum | MSB = MSC |
| Net social benefit | SB − SC |
| Welfare loss triangle | ½ × gap between curves at the market quantity × quantity difference |
| Total external cost or benefit | Area under MEC or MEB (add up unit by unit if a schedule) |

For a firm with TC = a + bQ², AC = a/Q + bQ and MC = 2bQ. Minimum AC is where they meet, at Q = √(a/b).

## Productive and allocative efficiency (7.3.1, 7.3.2)

- Productive efficiency is about cost per unit. Allocative efficiency is about what is produced.
- A firm can meet one and miss the other. A profit-maximising monopoly sets MR = MC, so P is above MC.
- Perfect competition in the long run gives P = MC = minimum AC, meeting both conditions.
- Economy-wide, a point inside the production possibility curve is productively inefficient. A point on the curve can still be allocatively inefficient if the mix is not what people want.

**Method for a firm.**
1. Write AC and MC from TC.
2. Set MC = AC (or find the minimum AC) for productive efficiency.
3. Set P = MC for allocative efficiency.
4. Compare the actual output with each.

**Small reminder.** For TC = 100 + Q², AC = 100/Q + Q. MC = 2Q. They meet where Q² = 100, so Q = 10 and AC = 20 = MC.

## Pareto optimality and dynamic efficiency (7.3.3, 7.3.4)

- Test a move: does anyone lose? If anyone loses, it is not a Pareto improvement.
- Many Pareto optimal points exist, some very unequal. Pareto optimality says nothing about fairness.
- Dynamic efficiency is judged over time. Lower costs and better products from research count, even if prices are above MC now.
- Trade-off: market power can cause static loss but may fund investment. Whether it does depends on how profit is used and on threat of entry.

## Market failure and its reasons (7.3.5, 7.3.6)

- Complete failure: no market exists, as with public goods.
- Partial failure: a market exists with the wrong quantity or price.
- Reasons: externalities, public goods, merit and demerit goods (imperfect information), asymmetric information, market power, immobile factors, unequal incomes.
- Evaluate by size. A small externality in a minor market may not justify action, and intervention itself can fail.

## Four types of externality (7.4.3, 7.4.4)

| | Negative | Positive |
|---|---|---|
| Production | MSC above MPC: over-production | MSC below MPC: under-production |
| Consumption | MSB below MPB: over-consumption | MSB above MPB: under-consumption |

Rule: external costs add to cost or subtract from benefit, so they lift MSC or lower MSB. External benefits do the opposite.

**Method for a calculation.**
1. Write all curves as equations.
2. Market quantity: set MPB = MPC.
3. Social quantity: add MEC or MEB to the correct side, then set MSB = MSC.
4. Find the gap between MSB and MSC at the market quantity.
5. Welfare loss = ½ × gap × difference in quantity.

### Worked reminder: a negative externality of consumption

A fictional city centre. Q is in thousand car trips, values in CU.

- MPB = 96 − 4Q
- MPC = 12 + 2Q
- Marginal external cost of consumption = Q, so MSB = 96 − 5Q

1. Market: 96 − 4Q = 12 + 2Q gives 84 = 6Q, so Q = 14 and P = 40.
2. Social optimum: 96 − 5Q = 12 + 2Q gives 84 = 7Q, so Q = 12 and MSB = MSC = 36.
3. Over-consumption = 14 − 12 = 2 thousand trips.
4. At Q = 14, MSB = 96 − 70 = 26 and MC = 12 + 28 = 40, a gap of 14.
5. Welfare loss = ½ × 14 × 2 = 14 thousand CU.

At the social optimum the marginal external cost is 12 CU per trip, which is the size of the gap a corrective tax would aim to close.

## Deadweight welfare loss (7.4.5)

- It arises because the market produces a quantity where MSB and MSC differ.
- Negative externality: the loss sits to the right of the social optimum.
- Positive externality: the loss sits to the left of the social optimum.
- On a diagram, shade only the triangle between MSB and MSC. Label the market quantity and the social quantity.

## Asymmetric information and moral hazard (7.4.6)

- Asymmetric information: sellers often know more than buyers (used goods, financial products, medical care). Poor decisions follow, and better-quality goods may leave the market.
- Moral hazard: after insurance or a guarantee, the protected party takes more risk.
- Insurer's example: loss 5,000 CU, chance of a claim 4% before cover and 6% after. Expected claim rises from 200 to 300 CU. A premium of 200 loses 100 CU per policy.
- Responses: excesses, monitoring, sharing information, regulation. Each has a cost.

## Costs and benefits in decisions (7.4.7)

**Method for a cost-benefit decision.**
1. List private and external costs, and private and external benefits.
2. Give each a money value.
3. Add up SB and SC.
4. Net social benefit = SB − SC. Positive means go ahead.
5. Test sensitivity: which values are least certain?

Net present value is not required. A decision with positive net social benefit but large uncertain values deserves caution.

## Quick self-test

1. State the condition for productive efficiency for a firm.
2. State the condition for allocative efficiency when externalities exist.
3. At the 40th unit, MPC = 22 and MEC = 7. Find MSC.
4. PB = 340 and EB = 90. Find SB.
5. A change gives person A two more units of utility and leaves B unchanged. Is it a Pareto improvement?
6. For TC = 100 + Q², find the output and AC at which the firm is productively efficient.
7. MPB = 80 − 4Q, MPC = 20 + 2Q, and MEC is a constant 6. Find the market quantity, the social optimum and the welfare loss.
8. Give an example of a positive externality of production.
9. Why is moral hazard a problem for insurers?
10. SB = 48 and SC = 51 (CU million). Should the project go ahead on these figures?
11. Why can a monopoly be dynamically efficient but statically inefficient?

### Answers

1. Output at the minimum of AC, where MC = AC.
2. MSB = MSC.
3. 22 + 7 = 29.
4. 340 + 90 = 430.
5. Yes. A gains and nobody loses.
6. AC = 100/Q + Q and MC = 2Q. Q² = 100, so Q = 10 and AC = 20.
7. Market: 80 − 4Q = 20 + 2Q gives Q = 10, P = 40. Social: MSC = 26 + 2Q, so 54 = 6Q and Q = 9, with MSB = MSC = 44. At Q = 10, MSC = 46 and MPB = 40, a gap of 6, so welfare loss = ½ × 6 × 1 = 3.
8. A firm that trains workers who later move to other firms; or a firm whose research is used by others.
9. Cover makes the insured take more risk, so claims exceed the level the premium assumed.
10. No. Net social benefit = 48 − 51 = −3, but check how uncertain the values are.
11. It may price above MC (static loss) but use the profit to cut costs or create products over time.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "P = MC" for productive efficiency, or "MC = AC" for allocative.
- Stating a diagram result without naming the curves, the market quantity or the social optimum.
- Putting MEC on the benefit curve or MEB on the cost curve.
- Shading a large area as welfare loss when only the triangle between MSB and MSC counts.
- Using the wrong direction: a negative externality gives over-production, a positive one under-production.
- Calling any gain a Pareto improvement even though someone loses.
- Mixing up asymmetric information (before the deal) with moral hazard (after the deal).
- Concluding a cost-benefit result without commenting on uncertainty, intangibles or who is affected.
- Ending a calculation without a unit or a plain-English conclusion.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the difference between productive efficiency and allocative efficiency?**
Productive efficiency is about cost per unit and occurs where MC = AC, at minimum average cost. Allocative efficiency is about what is produced and occurs where P = MC, or MSB = MSC when externalities exist.

**What is a Pareto improvement?**
A move is a Pareto improvement only if someone gains and nobody loses. Pareto optimality says nothing about fairness, because many Pareto optimal points exist and some are very unequal.

**What is the difference between asymmetric information and moral hazard?**
Asymmetric information is a problem before the deal, when one side, often the seller, knows more than the other. Moral hazard arises after the deal, when a party protected by insurance or a guarantee takes more risk.

**How do you calculate deadweight welfare loss from an externality?**
Find the market quantity where MPB = MPC and the social optimum where MSB = MSC. Welfare loss is half the gap between MSB and MSC at the market quantity, multiplied by the difference in quantity.

**Is net present value required for cost-benefit analysis in Cambridge 9708?**
No. Net present value is not required; you need to list and value private and external costs and benefits, find net social benefit and consider which values are least certain.
