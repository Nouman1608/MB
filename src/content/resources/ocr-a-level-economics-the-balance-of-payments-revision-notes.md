## Definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Balance of payments | The account of every payment crossing a country's border, in either direction, in a stated time span |
| Credit and debit | Credit: money flowing in. Debit: money flowing out |
| Balance | Credits minus debits. Positive is a surplus, negative is a deficit |
| Current account | Trade in goods, trade in services, income, current transfers |
| Capital account | Small capital transfers and non-produced, non-financial assets |
| Financial account | Direct investment, portfolio investment, other investment (loans, deposits), reserves |
| Errors and omissions | The balancing item that makes the total zero |
| Imbalance | A persistent surplus or deficit, rather than a position near zero |
| Sustainable position | One that can be maintained without painful adjustment |

Accounting identity: current account + capital account + financial account + errors and omissions = 0. A deficit on the current account is matched by net inflows elsewhere. In this page a net inflow on the financial account is positive.

## The current account at a glance

| Component | Credit | Debit |
|---|---|---|
| Goods | Exports of products | Imports of products |
| Services | Tourism spending by visitors, insurance sold abroad | Residents' overseas travel |
| Income | Profit and interest received from abroad | Profit and interest paid abroad |
| Current transfers | Migrants' remittances received | Aid given, payments to international bodies |

## Method: calculating balances

1. Write credits and debits for each component.
2. Subtract: balance = credits − debits. Keep the sign.
3. Goods and services balance = goods balance + services balance.
4. Current account balance = goods + services + income + current transfers.
5. Percentage of GDP = balance ÷ GDP × 100.
6. To find the financial account: financial = −(current + capital + errors and omissions).

**Worked reminder.** Country N, Year 1 (illustrative), cu bn. GDP 900.

| Component | Credits | Debits | Balance |
|---|---|---|---|
| Goods | 120 | 150 | −30 |
| Services | 60 | 35 | +25 |
| Income | 18 | 30 | −12 |
| Current transfers | 5 | 11 | −6 |

Current account: −30 + 25 − 12 − 6 = **−23**, which is 23 ÷ 900 × 100 = **2.6% of GDP**. Capital account +1, errors and omissions −2. Financial account = −(−23 + 1 − 2) = **+24**.

Check: −23 + 1 + 24 − 2 = 0.

## Why a sustainable position matters

- A deficit financed by borrowing adds to foreign liabilities, and the interest and profit paid abroad worsen the income balance.
- Large deficits put downward pressure on the currency, raising import prices.
- Lenders may lose confidence, and short-term inflows can leave quickly.
- The government may be forced into policies that conflict with growth and employment.
- A persistent surplus can cause overheating at home and retaliation abroad.

## Causes of imbalances

| Type | Cause | Direction |
|---|---|---|
| Cyclical | Domestic boom raises import demand | Deficit |
| Cyclical | Recession abroad cuts export demand | Deficit |
| Structural | High unit labour costs, low productivity | Deficit |
| Structural | Overvalued currency | Deficit |
| Structural | Poor quality, non-price weakness | Deficit |
| Structural | Dependence on one or two exports whose prices fall | Deficit |
| Structural | Low savings, high consumption | Deficit |
| Structural | High foreign ownership of firms, so large profit outflows | Income deficit |
| Structural | Strong competitiveness, high saving, undervalued currency | Surplus |

**Formula for the effect of growth on trade.** Percentage change in imports = YED of imports × percentage change in domestic income. Percentage change in exports = YED of exports × percentage change in foreign income.

**Worked reminder.** Country N's imports of goods are 150, YED of imports 1.5, domestic income grows 4%. Imports rise 1.5 × 4 = 6%, which is 150 × 0.06 = 9. New imports 159.

## Consequences

- **Deficit:** rising foreign liabilities; income outflows; currency weakness and import-price inflation; pressure to cut demand; leakage of demand from domestic producers.
- **Surplus:** growing overseas assets; upward pressure on the currency; export-led jobs; risk of inflation and trade disputes; weak domestic consumption.

## Policies to correct a deficit

| Group | Examples | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| Expenditure-reducing | Higher taxes, lower spending, higher interest rates | Act fairly quickly on imports | Cost output and jobs; blunt |
| Expenditure-switching | Lower exchange rate, tariffs, quotas | Redirect spending to home goods | Depend on price responsiveness; retaliation; raise inflation |
| Supply-side | Training, infrastructure, research and development | Lasting competitiveness gains | Slow, costly, uncertain |

**Method for the multiplier route.**

1. Find the multiplier as the reciprocal of the sum of the three leakage rates (saving, tax, imports).
2. Multiply the spending change by it to get the income change.
3. Multiply that income change by the import rate to get the change in imports.

**Worked reminder.** MPS 0.2, MPT 0.2, MPM 0.2. Multiplier = 1 ÷ 0.6 = 1.67. Spending cut of 9 gives income down 15 and imports down 0.2 × 15 = **3**.

**Unit labour cost** = wage per hour ÷ output per hour. If a wage of 30 is paired with output of 20, ULC is 1.50. Wage +3% and output +9%: 30.9 ÷ 21.8 = 1.42, a fall of 5.5%.

## Chains of reasoning to rehearse

Write each chain as a sequence, then add a judgement.

**Deficit from a domestic boom.** Income rises, so spending on imports rises, so imports exceed exports and the goods balance worsens. If the boom is temporary, the deficit closes when growth slows. Judgement: little need for action unless financing becomes difficult.

**Deficit from weak competitiveness.** Unit labour costs are high, so export prices are high and imports are relatively cheap, so the trade balance worsens, so the deficit persists. Judgement: a structural problem that needs supply-side action, because demand cuts only treat the symptom.

**Deficit leading to inflation.** A large deficit means heavy supply of the domestic currency, so the currency weakens, so imports cost more, so cost-push inflation appears, so real incomes fall. Judgement: depends on how much of the economy's spending is on imports.

**Deficit financed by short-term inflows.** Portfolio investors supply funds while confidence lasts. If confidence goes, funds leave, the currency falls and interest rates may have to rise. Judgement: riskier than a deficit financed by long-term direct investment.

**Surplus.** Strong exports and high saving give a surplus, so the currency tends to rise, so exporters lose price competitiveness, so the surplus narrows. Judgement: a surplus is self-correcting only if the currency can move freely.

## Reading data questions

- Identify which account and which component each figure belongs to before calculating.
- Check whether the data show a balance or the credits and debits behind it.
- Compare years using percentages of GDP, not raw values, if GDP changes.
- Note any sign convention stated in the data and follow it.
- When a question gives two years, calculate the change in each component, then say which component drove the change in the total.

**Worked reminder.** Country N, Year 2 (illustrative): goods balance −34, services +27, income −10, current transfers −6. Current account = −34 + 27 − 10 − 6 = **−23**. The total is unchanged from Year 1 (−23) although the parts moved: goods worsened by 4, services improved by 2, income improved by 2 and transfers did not change (−4 + 2 + 2 + 0 = 0).

## Must-know distinctions

- **Current account versus the whole balance of payments.** The whole always sums to zero.
- **Cyclical versus structural.** Cyclical imbalances close as the cycle turns. Structural ones need policy.
- **Expenditure-reducing versus expenditure-switching.** The first lowers total demand, the second shifts it from foreign to home goods.
- **Deficit versus a deficit as a percentage of GDP.** Always scale.

## Evaluation toolkit

Ask: how big, how long, how financed, what caused it, how fast the policy works, what it costs elsewhere, and what other countries do in response.

## Quick self-test

1. State the three accounts of the balance of payments.
2. Goods credits 80, debits 95. Find the goods balance.
3. Services balance +14, income −9, current transfers −4, goods −11. Find the current account balance.
4. A current account deficit of 18 on GDP of 600: what is it as a percentage of GDP?
5. With current account −18, capital account +2, errors and omissions +1, find the financial account balance.
6. Give one example of a current transfer.
7. Imports of 200 with a YED of 2 and income growth of 3%. By how much do imports rise?
8. MPS 0.1, MPT 0.3, MPM 0.1. Find the multiplier.
9. Name one cyclical and one structural cause of a deficit.
10. Give one weakness of using higher interest rates to correct a deficit.

### Answers

1. Current, capital, financial.
2. 80 − 95 = −15.
3. −11 + 14 − 9 − 4 = −10.
4. 18 ÷ 600 × 100 = 3.0%.
5. −(−18 + 2 + 1) = +15.
6. Remittances sent home by migrant workers, or foreign aid.
7. 2 × 3 = 6%, so 200 × 0.06 = 12.
8. 1 ÷ (0.1 + 0.3 + 0.1) = 1 ÷ 0.5 = 2.
9. Cyclical: a domestic boom. Structural: high unit labour costs.
10. It slows growth and raises unemployment, and may attract short-term capital that raises the exchange rate and hurts exporters.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calling the current account deficit "the balance of payments deficit".
- Subtracting the wrong way round and losing the sign.
- Leaving out income or current transfers from the current account balance.
- Quoting a balance without scaling it by GDP when asked about seriousness.
- Listing policies without saying which cause each addresses.
- Treating all deficits as harmful without considering financing and cause.
- Giving a policy and no counterweight: cost to growth, jobs or inflation.
- Ignoring time: supply-side effects are slow, demand-side effects faster.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the balance of payments accounting identity?**
The current account plus the capital account plus the financial account plus errors and omissions equals zero. A current account deficit is therefore matched by net inflows elsewhere.

**What is the difference between expenditure-reducing and expenditure-switching policies?**
Expenditure-reducing policies lower total demand, so spending on imports falls. Expenditure-switching policies change relative prices, for example through a lower exchange rate or tariffs, so spending moves from foreign to domestic goods.

**What are the consequences of a current account surplus?**
A persistent surplus builds overseas assets and supports export-led jobs, but it puts upward pressure on the currency and can bring a risk of inflation, trade disputes and weak domestic consumption.

**What is the difference between a cyclical and a structural deficit on the current account?**
A cyclical imbalance, such as one caused by a domestic boom, tends to close as the economic cycle turns. A structural imbalance, such as one caused by high unit labour costs, needs policy action, usually on the supply side.
