## The unit in one sentence

Works reflect and shape the time and place they were written in, and they are read differently in other
times and places; your job is to link a fact of context to a choice in the text and to its effect.

## What the brief says (use these phrases accurately)

- Assessment objective 1 includes knowing and interpreting "contexts in which texts are written and/or
  received".
- The course studies literary texts "from different times and places".
- The course asks how interpretations "are shaped by cultural belief systems".
- The individual oral pairs "one work originally written in the language studied and one work studied
  in translation", approached through a common global issue.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning | Quick example |
|---|---|---|
| Context of production | Time, place, culture and conditions in which a work was written | Ibsen writing *A Doll's House* in 1879 for a theatre audience of the time |
| Context of reception | Time, place and culture in which a work is read or watched | A modern audience seeing Nora's exit as expected rather than scandalous |
| Setting | Time and place inside the story | The desert in "Ozymandias" |
| Cultural perspective | The beliefs and values a work shows, questions or assumes | Marriage as duty versus marriage as partnership |
| Reception history | How readings of a work have changed over time | *The Tempest* read later in the light of colonial history |
| Work in translation | A work studied in a language other than the one it was written in | Chekhov's story read in English |
| Translator's choice | A decision about wording where the original allows several options | *The Lady with the Dog* or *The Lady with the Little Dog* |
| Anachronistic reading | Judging a work only by the values of the reader's own time | Calling a character "backward" and stopping there |

## Method in steps: context to effect

> **Step 1.** Quote or reference a precise moment in the text.
> **Step 2.** Name the writer's choice (image, structure, voice, form, ending).
> **Step 3.** State the effect on meaning or on the reader.
> **Step 4.** Add the context that explains why the choice was made, or why it lands as it does.
> **Step 5.** If useful, show how a reader in another time or place might read it differently.

Always start from the text. Context comes in at Step 4 to explain, never at Step 1 to fill space.

## Method in steps: working with a translated work

> **Step 1.** Name the translator and edition.
> **Step 2.** Base your main claims on what survives translation: events, structure, images, point of
> view.
> **Step 3.** Make claims about single words as claims about *this translation*.
> **Step 4.** Where you can, compare two translations of a key line, or read the translator's note.
> **Step 5.** Say what the work shows about its source culture, and how you, as a reader in another
> culture, respond.

## Small worked reminders

**Kate Chopin, "The Story of an Hour" (1894).** The story ends: "When the doctors came they said she had
died of heart disease—of the joy that kills."

- *Choice:* an ironic final diagnosis. The doctors think she died of joy at seeing her husband alive.
- *Effect:* the reader knows the opposite: her shock came from losing a freedom she had just imagined.
- *Context:* written when a married woman's legal and social life was tied closely to her husband's.
  The doctors' certainty shows how easily her real feelings could be misread.
- *Reception:* many readers now see the ending as a sharp critique; a reader who shares the doctors'
  assumptions would miss the irony entirely.

**Shelley, "Ozymandias" (1818).** "Nothing beside remains." Three plain words after a king's boast. Time
itself answers the tyrant. For Shelley's radical first readers, the target could be any living ruler.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Setting vs context.** Setting is inside the story; context is around the work. A novel set in the
  past was still written in its own present.
- **Production vs reception.** Production asks "why was it written like this then?" Reception asks "how
  is it read now, or elsewhere?"
- **Reflect vs shape.** A work can mirror its culture's beliefs, or push against them and help change
  them. Many do both.
- **Original vs translation.** In a translated work, sound and single words belong partly to the
  translator. Structure and events belong more securely to the writer.
- **Context as evidence vs context as decoration.** Context earns credit when it explains a choice. A
  list of dates does not.
- **Understanding vs approving.** You can explain a past belief without sharing it, and you can
  criticise it after you have explained it.

## Quick self-test

1. What are the recommended teaching hours for Time and space at SL and at HL?
2. Define "context of reception" in one sentence.
3. A novel written in 1950 is set in 1850. What is its setting and what is its context of production?
4. Name two features of a work that usually survive translation well.
5. Name two features that often change in translation.
6. What does the individual oral require about the language of its two works?
7. Why is *The Lady with the Dog* / *The Lady with the Little Dog* a useful example?
8. In "Ozymandias", what is the effect of "Nothing beside remains." following the king's boast?
9. What does Ibsen's alternative ending for *A Doll's House* show about reception?
10. What is an anachronistic reading, and why is it weak on its own?
11. In Paper 1 you may not know where an unseen passage comes from. What should you do about context?
12. In "The Story of an Hour", what is ironic about "the joy that kills"?

### Answers

1. 50 hours at SL and 80 hours at HL.
2. The time, place and culture in which a work is read or watched, which shapes how its meaning is
   understood.
3. Setting: 1850. Context of production: 1950.
4. Any two of: plot and order of events, structure, major images and symbols, point of view, characters'
   actions.
5. Any two of: sound (rhyme, rhythm, puns), register and dialect, connotations of single words,
   culture-specific words.
6. One work must be originally written in the language studied and one must be a work studied in
   translation.
7. It shows two translators making different, defensible choices about one word, so a claim based on a
   single English word may be a claim about the translator.
8. The boast is undercut at once: time has destroyed the works the king said would inspire despair, so
   the "Mighty" should despair for a different reason.
9. Some theatres and audiences of the time found Nora leaving her children unacceptable, so the same play
   was received differently from how it is often received today.
10. Judging a work only by the values of your own time. It is weak alone because it skips what the work
    shows about its own culture; it can be a final step after that explanation.
11. Infer time and place from clues in the text (vocabulary, objects, social rules) and present them as
    inferences, without inventing precise dates or countries.
12. The doctors think she died of joy at her husband's return; the reader knows it was the shock of
    losing the freedom she had just begun to imagine.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Opening a paragraph with background history and never linking it to a quotation or choice.
- Treating setting as context: "this novel reflects the 1850s" when it was written a century later.
- Speaking of "people back then" as one group instead of naming whose beliefs a work shows or questions.
- Moral judgement of a character with no analysis of what the work itself suggests.
- Building an argument on the sound or connotation of one word in a translated work without naming the
  translation.
- Forgetting that reception includes you: never stating how a reader now might respond.
- In Paper 1, stating an exact period or country for an unseen passage with no textual support.
- In the individual oral, discussing each work's context separately instead of linking both to the
  global issue.
