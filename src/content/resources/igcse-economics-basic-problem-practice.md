## Section A

**1.** Define scarcity and explain why it forces choices to be made. **[3]**

**2.** State the three fundamental economic questions every economy must answer. **[3]**

## Section B

**3.** Explain opportunity cost, giving an example for a consumer, a firm and a government. **[6]**

**4.** A production possibility curve shows capital goods on one axis and consumer goods on the other.

**(a)** Explain what a point **inside** the curve indicates. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain what a point **outside** the curve indicates. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why the curve is usually concave to the origin. **[3]**
**(d)** Explain **two** causes of an outward shift of the curve. **[4]**

**5.** Explain the four factors of production and the reward earned by each. **[8]**

**6.** Explain why an economy that devotes more resources to capital goods today may grow faster in future. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** Scarcity means that **resources are limited while human wants are unlimited** [1] [1]. Since not all wants can be satisfied, **choices must be made about how to allocate the limited resources**, and every choice involves giving something up [1].

**2.** **What to produce**, **how to produce it**, and **for whom to produce** [1] [1] [1].

**3.** Opportunity cost is the **benefit of the next best alternative forgone** when a choice is made [1] [1]. A **consumer** who spends $50 on a concert ticket gives up the meal out they could have bought instead [1]. A **firm** that uses a factory to make chairs gives up the profit from making tables there [1] [1]. A **government** that builds a hospital gives up the school it could have built with the same funds [1].

**4. (a)** The economy is **not using all its resources fully or efficiently** [1], for example because there is unemployment or idle capacity [1].
**(b)** It is **currently unattainable** with the existing resources and technology [1]; it could only be reached after **economic growth shifts the curve outwards** [1].
**(c)** Resources are **not equally suited to producing both goods** [1]; as more of one good is produced, **increasingly unsuitable resources have to be switched to it** [1], so the opportunity cost of each extra unit rises, making the curve steeper [1].
**(d)** Any two, 2 marks each: an **increase in the quantity of resources**, e.g. a larger workforce through immigration or the discovery of new raw materials [1] [1]; an **improvement in the quality of resources**, e.g. better education and training raising labour productivity [1] [1]; **advances in technology** allowing more output from the same inputs [1] [1].

**5. Land** — all **natural resources**, including minerals, forests and the sea; the reward is **rent** [1] [1]. **Labour** — the **human physical and mental effort** used in production; the reward is **wages** [1] [1]. **Capital** — the **man-made goods used to produce other goods**, such as machinery and factories; the reward is **interest** [1] [1]. **Enterprise** — the **willingness to take risks and organise the other factors**; the reward is **profit** [1] [1].

**6.** Capital goods are **used to produce other goods in the future** [1], so building more of them **increases the economy's productive capacity** [1]. This **shifts the production possibility curve outwards**, allowing more of everything to be produced later [1]. The cost is that **fewer consumer goods are available now**, so living standards are lower in the short run — the opportunity cost of faster future growth [1].

---

## Where marks are usually lost

- Defining opportunity cost as "the money spent".
- Saying a point inside the PPC is unattainable.
- Explaining the concave shape by "diminishing returns" without mentioning unsuitable resources.
- Giving the reward for enterprise as wages.
