This guide teaches section **3.1.3.5 Shapes of simple molecules and ions** of the AQA AS and A-level Chemistry specification (7404/7405), for teaching from September 2015. It is AS content, examined on Papers 1 and 2 (and Paper 3). The specification asks you to explain the shapes of, and bond angles in, simple molecules and ions with **up to six electron pairs** around the central atom, using electron pair repulsion. This page gives a counting method that works for unfamiliar species, then the full range of shapes with worked examples. The [Bonding study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-bonding/) has a shorter treatment. Recall is in the [Shapes of simple molecules and ions revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-shapes-of-simple-molecules-and-ions-revision-notes/), and questions are in the [Shapes of simple molecules and ions practice questions](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-shapes-of-simple-molecules-and-ions-practice/).

## What the specification asks

| Content and skills | What you must be able to do |
|---|---|
| Bonding pairs and lone pairs of electrons as charge clouds that repel each other | Use the idea of charge clouds |
| Pairs in the outer shell arrange themselves as far apart as possible to minimise repulsion | Predict the arrangement of pairs |
| Lone pair–lone pair repulsion is greater than lone pair–bond pair, which is greater than bond pair–bond pair | Order the repulsions and use them to explain angles |
| The effect of electron pair repulsion on bond angles | Give bond angles |
| Explain the shapes of, and bond angles in, simple molecules and ions with up to six electron pairs | Work out an unfamiliar shape |

## Step 1: count the electron pairs

1. Take the number of outer electrons on the central atom.
2. Add one electron for each hydrogen or halogen atom bonded to the central atom. A terminal oxygen atom adds none, because it completes its own shell by taking two electrons from the central atom.
3. For an ion, add one for each negative charge and subtract one for each positive charge.
4. Divide by two to give the total number of electron pairs.
5. The number of atoms bonded to the central atom gives the number of bonding clouds. Subtract these from the total number of pairs to find the lone pairs.

A **double or triple bond is one charge cloud**, so it repels like a single bond pair in the shape rule.

### Worked example 1: ammonia, NH₃

Nitrogen has 5 outer electrons; three hydrogens add 3. Total 8, so 4 pairs. Three are bonding pairs and one is a lone pair.

### Worked example 2: the ammonium ion, NH₄⁺

Nitrogen 5, four hydrogens add 4, less 1 for the positive charge: 8, so 4 pairs. All four are bonding pairs, none is a lone pair.

### Worked example 3: sulfur hexafluoride, SF₆

Sulfur has 6, six fluorines add 6: 12, so 6 pairs, all bonding, none a lone pair.

## Step 2: choose the arrangement

Pairs spread out as far as possible. The arrangement of charge clouds depends only on the **total** number of pairs.

| Pairs | Arrangement | Angle between pairs |
|---|---|---|
| 2 | Linear | 180° |
| 3 | Trigonal planar | 120° |
| 4 | Tetrahedral | 109.5° |
| 5 | Trigonal bipyramidal | 90° and 120° |
| 6 | Octahedral | 90° |

## Step 3: name the shape from the atoms only

The shape describes the positions of the **atoms**, not the lone pairs. Lone pairs are included in the count, but not in the name.

| Bonding pairs | Lone pairs | Shape | Bond angle | Example |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | 0 | Linear | 180° | BeCl₂, CO₂ |
| 3 | 0 | Trigonal planar | 120° | BF₃ |
| 2 | 1 | Bent (V-shaped) | just under 120° | SO₂ |
| 4 | 0 | Tetrahedral | 109.5° | CH₄, NH₄⁺ |
| 3 | 1 | Pyramidal (trigonal pyramidal) | 107° | NH₃ |
| 2 | 2 | Bent (non-linear) | 104.5° | H₂O |
| 5 | 0 | Trigonal bipyramidal | 90° and 120° | PCl₅ |
| 4 | 1 | See-saw | about 90° and 120°, reduced | SF₄ |
| 3 | 2 | T-shaped | about 90°, reduced | ClF₃ |
| 2 | 3 | Linear | 180° | XeF₂ |
| 6 | 0 | Octahedral | 90° | SF₆ |
| 5 | 1 | Square pyramidal | about 90°, reduced | BrF₅ |
| 4 | 2 | Square planar | 90° | XeF₄ |

For the species with lone pairs, AQA expects you to give the shape and explain the reduction in bond angle; you do not need to recall the exact reduced values except for the common ones (107° in NH₃, 104.5° in H₂O).

## Step 4: explain the bond angles

**Lone pair–lone pair repulsion is greater than lone pair–bond pair repulsion, which is greater than bond pair–bond pair repulsion.** A lone pair is held by only one nucleus, so it spreads out more and repels more strongly.

| Species | Pairs | Angle | Explanation |
|---|---|---|---|
| CH₄ | 4 bonding | 109.5° | Equal repulsion between four bond pairs |
| NH₃ | 3 bonding, 1 lone | 107° | The lone pair repels the bond pairs more strongly and squeezes them together |
| H₂O | 2 bonding, 2 lone | 104.5° | Two lone pairs squeeze the bond pairs more |

Each extra lone pair reduces the angle by about 2.5°.

### Worked example 4: explain the shape and angle in water

Oxygen has 6 outer electrons and two hydrogens add 2: 8 electrons, so 4 pairs, of which 2 are bonding and 2 are lone. The four pairs repel to a tetrahedral arrangement, but the shape of the atoms is bent (non-linear). Lone pair–lone pair and lone pair–bond pair repulsions are greater than bond pair–bond pair, so the H–O–H angle is reduced from 109.5° to about 104.5°.

### Worked example 5: boron trifluoride, BF₃

Boron has 3 outer electrons and three fluorines add 3: 6, so 3 pairs, all bonding. Three clouds spread out in one plane. The shape is trigonal planar with F–B–F angles of 120°.

### Worked example 6: phosphorus pentachloride, PCl₅

Phosphorus has 5 and five chlorines add 5: 10, so 5 pairs, all bonding. The shape is trigonal bipyramidal with 120° in the plane of three chlorines and 90° between these and the two chlorines above and below. This is an example of an atom with more than eight electrons in its outer shell.

## Ions and multiple bonds

### Worked example 7: the carbonate ion, CO₃²⁻

Carbon has 4 outer electrons, the oxygens add none, and the 2− charge adds 2: 6, so 3 pairs. There is one C=O double bond and two C–O⁻ bonds, giving three bonding clouds around carbon and no lone pairs, so the shape is trigonal planar with 120° angles. (The negative charge is spread across all three oxygens.)

### Worked example 8: carbon dioxide, CO₂

Carbon has 4 outer electrons and the oxygens add none: 4, so 2 pairs. Carbon has two C=O double bonds. Each double bond is one charge cloud, so there are two clouds and no lone pairs: linear, 180°.

### Worked example 9: the tetrachloroiodate ion, ICl₄⁻

Iodine has 7 outer electrons; four chlorines add 4, and the negative charge adds 1: 12, so 6 pairs. Four are bonding and two are lone. The pairs form an octahedron, the two lone pairs sit opposite each other to minimise repulsion, and the shape of the atoms is **square planar** with 90° angles.

### Worked example 10: the sulfate ion, SO₄²⁻

Sulfur has 6 outer electrons, the oxygens add none, and the 2− charge adds 2: 8, so 4 pairs. There are four S–O bonding clouds and no lone pairs: tetrahedral, 109.5°.

## Drawing shapes

Use wedge and dash notation to give a three-dimensional impression. A solid wedge shows a bond coming towards you, a dashed line a bond going away, and a plain line a bond in the plane of the paper. Draw lone pairs as pairs of dots when asked, and label the bond angle.

## Common mistakes

- Giving the shape of the electron pair arrangement instead of the atoms (calling NH₃ "tetrahedral" rather than pyramidal).
- Using 109.5° for NH₃ and H₂O.
- Forgetting to add or subtract electrons for the charge on an ion.
- Counting a double bond as two separate clouds.
- Saying "lone pairs repel more" without comparing with bond pairs.
- Writing "bond angles are reduced because of repulsion" with no mention of lone pairs.
- Writing a bond angle for 5 or 6 pairs without both angles, such as 90° and 120° for PCl₅.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Explain** the shape: state the number of bonding pairs and lone pairs, say the pairs repel to get as far apart as possible, then name the shape.
- **Explain** the angle: compare the repulsions in order, lone pair–lone pair > lone pair–bond pair > bond pair–bond pair.
- **Deduce**: show the pair-counting arithmetic.
- **Draw**: show wedge and dash bonds, the lone pairs and the bond angle.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do double bonds change the shape?**
A double bond counts as one charge cloud for the shape, so it does not alter the arrangement, but it does repel slightly more than a single bond.

**Why is a lone pair more repelling?**
It is attracted by only one nucleus, so its charge cloud is wider and closer to the central atom.

**Do I need to know the exact angles of every species?**
You need 180°, 120°, 109.5°, 90°, 107° (NH₃) and 104.5° (H₂O). For other lone-pair examples, explain the direction of the change.
