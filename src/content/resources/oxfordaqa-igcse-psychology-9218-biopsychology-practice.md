## Section A — Short questions

**1.** Which **one** of the following is part of the central nervous system?
A Somatic nervous system
B Spinal cord
C Adrenal gland
D Motor neuron in the arm **[1]**

**2.** Outline the difference between the somatic and the autonomic nervous systems. **[2]**

**3.** Identify the lobe of the brain in which **(a)** Broca's area **(b)** the visual area is found. **[2]**

**4.** State **one** difference between how hormones and neurotransmitters carry messages. **[2]**

**5.** Explain what is meant by inhibition at a synapse. **[2]**

**6.** Identify which type of scan would be most suitable for a doctor who only needs to check for a structural injury after a fall, and give **one** reason. **[2]**

## Section B — Longer questions

**7.** Describe the process of synaptic transmission. **[4]**

**8.** Mira hears a loud bang outside her window at night. Her heart pounds and her hands shake. Explain Mira's reaction using the fight or flight response. **[4]**

**9.** Using the example of Mira, explain how the James-Lange theory would describe the order in which she experiences fear, and give **one** criticism of the theory. **[3]**

**10.** Explain Hebb's theory of learning and neuronal growth, using the example of a student learning to play a piece on the piano. **[4]**

**11.** Describe Penfield's study of the interpretive cortex. **[3]**

**12.** Mr Okafor has had a stroke affecting the left side of his brain. He understands what people say but speaks slowly with great difficulty, and his right hand is weak. Explain his symptoms using your knowledge of localisation of function. **[4]**

**13.** Describe **and** evaluate Tulving's 'gold' memory study. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** **B** [1].

**2.** The somatic nervous system controls **voluntary movement** and carries sensation from skin and muscles [1], whereas the autonomic nervous system controls **involuntary functions** of internal organs and glands, such as heart rate [1].

**3.** **(a)** Frontal lobe [1]. **(b)** Occipital lobe [1].

**4.** Hormones travel in the **bloodstream** to target organs [1], whereas neurotransmitters cross the **synapse** between neurons (accept: hormones are slower and longer-lasting; neurotransmitters are faster and short-lived) [1].

**5.** The neurotransmitter **makes the next neuron less likely to fire** [1], so the message is reduced or stopped from being passed on [1].

**6.** A **CT scan** [1]; it shows **brain structure** clearly and quickly, and activity does not need to be measured (accept: cheaper or more widely available than PET or fMRI) [1].

**7.** An electrical impulse reaches the **axon terminal**, where **vesicles** release **neurotransmitter** into the synaptic gap [1]. The neurotransmitter crosses the gap and **binds to receptors** on the next neuron [1]. This has an **excitatory or inhibitory** effect on whether the next neuron fires [1]. Leftover neurotransmitter is taken back into the sending neuron by **reuptake**, ending the signal [1].

**8.** The bang is perceived as a **threat** [1]. Her **autonomic nervous system** is triggered, and **adrenaline** is released from her adrenal glands [1]. This **raises heart rate**, explaining her pounding heart [1], and **tenses the muscles** ready for action, explaining the shaking hands [1].

**9.** First the bang causes **physiological arousal** (pounding heart, shaking) [1]; Mira then **interprets** this arousal, and that interpretation is the feeling of fear [1]. Criticism, any one: bodily changes happen **too slowly** to explain how quickly emotion is felt; the **same arousal** occurs in different emotions, so arousal alone cannot tell her which emotion she feels [1].

**10.** Hebb proposed that when neurons **repeatedly fire together**, the connection between them **strengthens** [1]. Each time the student practises the piece, the same neurons are activated together [1]. They form a **cell assembly** that represents the piece [1], so with practice the pathway fires more quickly and easily and the student plays more fluently — learning has physically changed the brain [1].

**11.** Penfield operated on patients with **epilepsy** who were **awake** during brain surgery [1]. He **stimulated areas of the cortex with a weak electric current** and recorded what patients reported [1]. Stimulating parts of the **temporal lobe** sometimes produced vivid memories or sensations, such as hearing music [1].

**12.** The left hemisphere usually contains **Broca's area** in the frontal lobe, which controls **speech production** [1]; damage here explains his slow, effortful speech [1]. **Wernicke's area**, which controls understanding, appears undamaged, so he still understands speech [1]. Each motor area controls the **opposite side** of the body, so damage to the left motor area explains his weak right hand [1].

**13.** *Description (up to 3):* Tulving and a few other volunteers received an injection of **radioactive gold**, which allowed blood flow to active brain regions to be tracked [1]. They thought about **episodic** memories (personal events) or **semantic** memories (facts) [1]. Episodic memories were linked to more activity towards the **front** of the cortex and semantic memories towards the **back** [1].
*Evaluation (up to 3):* a strength is that it provides **physical evidence** that episodic and semantic memory are separate and localised [1]. A limitation is the **very small sample**, so the results may not generalise [1]; in addition, the difference was **not clear in every participant**, which weakens the conclusion [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Putting Broca's area in the temporal lobe or Wernicke's area in the frontal lobe.
- Describing synaptic transmission without mentioning receptors or reuptake.
- Explaining fight or flight in general without linking each change to the person in the scenario.
- Reversing the order of the James-Lange theory.
- Forgetting that the motor area controls the opposite side of the body.

## Frequently asked questions

**Which studies are named for biopsychology in 9218?**
Penfield's study of the interpretive cortex and Tulving's 'gold' memory study. Know what each did, what it found and what it shows about localisation.

**Do I need to know the names of neurotransmitters?**
The specification focuses on the process of release, binding, excitation, inhibition and reuptake rather than on individual neurotransmitters.

**How should I answer a brain damage scenario?**
Match each symptom to the area responsible, say what that area normally does, and remember that motor control is on the opposite side of the body.
