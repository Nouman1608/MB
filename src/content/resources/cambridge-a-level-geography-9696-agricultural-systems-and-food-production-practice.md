## Section A

**1.** Give the meaning of the term "agricultural productivity". **[2]**

**2.** Distinguish between land use and farming practices. **[2]**

**3.** Outline **two** physical factors that limit arable farming in upland areas. **[4]**

**4.** The table shows data for two fictional rice farms in the same country.

| Farm | Area (ha) | Workers | Output per year (t) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lanto (hand-cultivated, canal irrigation) | 1.5 | 4 | 6.3 |
| Merrow (mechanised, pumped irrigation) | 900 | 5 | 2 700 |

**(a)** Calculate the yield per hectare for each farm. Show your working. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the output per worker for each farm. **[2]**

**(c)** Using your answers, explain which farm is more intensive in its use of land and labour. **[3]**

**5.** The table shows energy data for two fictional farms.

| Farm | Energy input (MJ per ha) | Food energy output (MJ per ha) |
|---|---|---|
| R (oxen, hand labour, manure) | 8 000 | 36 000 |
| S (tractors, fertiliser, pesticides) | 45 000 | 72 000 |

**(a)** Calculate the energy ratio for each farm. **[2]**

**(b)** Suggest why farm S may still be more profitable than farm R. **[3]**

**6.** Explain how distance from markets can influence both land use and farming practices. **[4]**

**7.** For **one** pastoral system you have studied, identify two inputs, one throughput, one subsystem link and two outputs. **[6]**

## Section B

**8.** Explain how irrigation can lead to the intensification of agriculture **and** to environmental problems. **[6]**

**9.** The table shows changes in the fictional Kanvo district, a semi-arid farming area.

| Indicator | 1990 | 2020 |
|---|---|---|
| Population | 40 000 | 95 000 |
| Share of land cultivated (%) | 30 | 65 |
| Average fallow period (years) | 8 | 2 |
| Average maize yield (t per ha) | 1.6 | 1.1 |
| Dry-season grazing land (km²) | 220 | 80 |

**(a)** Calculate the percentage change in maize yield from 1990 to 2020. **[2]**

**(b)** Describe how farming in Kanvo has changed between 1990 and 2020. **[3]**

**(c)** Suggest reasons why yields fell while the area cultivated increased. **[5]**

**10.** Explain how land tenure can influence the use of agricultural technology. **[5]**

**11.** "Physical factors are now less important than economic and political factors in influencing agricultural land use." With reference to examples, how far do you agree? **[20]**

## Answers

**1.** The output of a farm [1] per unit of an input, such as per hectare or per worker [1]. **[2]**

*Examiner insight:* a bare "how much a farm produces" is output, not productivity; the "per unit of input" idea earns the second mark.

**2.** Land use is what the land is used for, such as rice or grazing [1]; farming practices are the methods used, such as hand weeding, terracing or rotational grazing [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Lower temperatures at height shorten the growing season [1], so some crops may not ripen [1]. Steep slopes make machinery hard or dangerous to use [1] and increase soil erosion when ploughed [1]. **[4]**

*Examiner insight:* also credit thin, leached or waterlogged soils, or heavy rainfall that hinders harvesting, in place of either factor, with 1 mark for the factor and 1 for its effect.

**4. (a)** Lanto: 6.3 ÷ 1.5 = 4.2 t per ha [1]; Merrow: 2 700 ÷ 900 = 3.0 t per ha [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** Lanto: 6.3 ÷ 4 = 1.6 t per worker (1.575) [1]; Merrow: 2 700 ÷ 5 = 540 t per worker [1]. **[2]**

**(c)** Lanto is more intensive in its use of land, with 4.2 t per ha against 3.0 t per ha [1], because many workers are applied to a small area [1]. Merrow substitutes machinery and capital for labour, so it achieves far higher output per worker (540 t) with low labour inputs per hectare [1]. **[3]**

*Examiner insight:* keep the units and use your figures from (a) and (b); intensity is judged per hectare, not by the size of the farm.

**5. (a)** R: 36 000 ÷ 8 000 = 4.5 [1]; S: 72 000 ÷ 45 000 = 1.6 [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** Farm S produces twice as much food energy per hectare, so it has more to sell [1]. It needs far fewer workers, so labour costs per tonne are lower [1]. Energy inputs such as fuel may be cheap or subsidised, so a low energy ratio need not mean a low profit [1]. **[3]**

**6.** Bulky or perishable products such as vegetables, milk or flowers lose value quickly in transport [1], so they are grown close to the market [1]. Farms far from markets favour durable or low-bulk products, or livestock that can be walked to market [1]. Practices change too: farms near markets use land intensively with high inputs because land is valuable, while distant farms use extensive methods [1]. **[4]**

*Examiner insight:* the question says "both", so one point about practices is needed to reach full marks.

**7.** Credit any real pastoral system, such as nomadic pastoralism in East African drylands or commercial dairying. Two inputs, such as natural pasture, water, herds, family labour or knowledge of routes [1] [1]. One throughput, such as moving herds between seasonal pastures, watering or milking [1]. One subsystem link, such as the grassland subsystem feeding the herd subsystem, with movement allowing grass to recover [1]. Two outputs, such as milk, meat, hides, animals for sale or manure [1] [1]. **[6]**

**8.** Irrigation removes the dependence on rainfall [1], so two or three crops can be grown each year on the same land [1]. Reliable water makes it worth buying high-yielding seed and fertiliser, raising inputs per hectare [1]. Environmental problems: where water evaporates and drainage is poor, salts build up in the soil (salinisation) [1]; over-pumping lowers water tables faster than recharge [1]; waterlogging and fertiliser run-off can pollute rivers and cause eutrophication [1]. **[6]**

**9. (a)** (1.1 − 1.6) ÷ 1.6 × 100 [1] = −31.3%, a fall of about 31% [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** Cultivated land more than doubled, from 30% to 65% [1]. Fallow periods were cut from 8 years to 2 [1]. Dry-season grazing fell from 220 km² to 80 km², while population more than doubled from 40 000 to 95 000 [1]. **[3]**

**(c)** Shorter fallows give soil less time to recover nutrients and organic matter [1]. Newly cultivated land is likely to be more marginal, with thinner or drier soils [1]. Continuous cropping on semi-arid land exposes soil to wind and water erosion [1]. Farmers may lack money for fertiliser to replace nutrients [1]. Less grazing land means fewer animals and less manure for the fields [1]. **[5]**

*Examiner insight:* each reason should connect to a figure in the table; generic "climate change" earns nothing unless linked to the data.

**10.** Owner-occupiers with secure title can invest in technology that pays back slowly, such as tubewells or orchards [1]. Secure land can be used as collateral, so owners can borrow to buy machinery [1]. Sharecroppers keep only part of extra output, so new seed or fertiliser brings them less return [1]. Short tenancies discourage investment in fixed equipment that stays with the land [1]. Fragmented holdings, often caused by inheritance, are too small and scattered for tractors or pivot irrigation [1]. **[5]**

**11.** The real exam marks extended answers with levels of response, not point by point. The points below are indicative creditworthy content.

*Introduction:* physical factors include climate, relief, soils and water [1]; economic factors include demand, prices, costs and market access, and political factors include subsidies, price guarantees and land reform [1].

*Agree:* irrigation, greenhouses and drainage stretch physical limits, so farmers can choose crops the climate alone would not allow [1]. Refrigeration and air freight reduce the effect of distance, so land use follows demand in distant markets [1]. Guaranteed prices for particular crops shape what is grown, as in the Topic 11 India case study [1]. Subsidies or tariffs can keep farming in places that would otherwise be uneconomic [1]. Rising incomes change demand towards meat and dairy, changing land use over wide areas [1]. Land reform or tenure law decides who farms and at what scale [1].

*Disagree:* physical factors still set absolute limits, such as the length of the growing season at high latitude [1]. Rain-fed farming, still common in many LICs, depends directly on rainfall reliability [1]. Steep relief and thin soils still keep many uplands under grazing or forest [1]. Stretching limits costs money, so poorer farmers remain controlled by physical conditions [1]. Climate change is making physical factors more important again in drought-prone areas [1].

*Analysis:* economic and political factors operate within physical limits rather than replacing them [1]. Their importance varies with the level of development: greater on capital-rich commercial farms than on subsistence holdings [1]. Technology itself is an economic factor, because it is bought [1].

*Use of examples:* a named commercial region where policy or demand decides land use [1]; a named rain-fed or upland area where physical limits dominate [1].

*Judgement:* a reasoned conclusion that varies by farm type and wealth [1], for example agreeing for commercial farming but not for subsistence farming [1]. **[20]**

*Examiner insight:* top answers do not treat factor groups separately; they show how money and policy decide whether a physical limit is overcome.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calculations without working or units.
- Mixing up yield and output per worker.
- System answers with no throughput or subsystem.
- Describing a table without figures from both years.
- "Suggest" answers that ignore the resource.
- Essays that discuss physical and human factors separately and never compare them.
