## What the specification asks for

- Define **internal energy** and explain that heating either raises the temperature or produces a change of state.
- State what the temperature rise depends on, and apply **ΔE = m c Δθ** (given on the Physics equation sheet).
- Define **specific heat capacity** and **specific latent heat**, including fusion and vaporisation.
- Apply **E = m L** (given on the equation sheet).
- Interpret **heating and cooling graphs** that include changes of state.
- **Distinguish** between specific heat capacity and specific latent heat.
- The specification also suggests an experiment to measure the **specific latent heat of fusion of water**.

## Internal energy

The particles (atoms and molecules) of a system store energy. **Internal energy is the total kinetic energy and
potential energy of all the particles that make up a system.**

- **Kinetic energy** comes from the particles' motion: vibrating in a solid, moving past each other in a liquid,
  moving freely in a gas.
- **Potential energy** comes from the forces between particles and depends on their separation.

Heating increases the energy of the particles, and this does **one of two things**:

1. It **raises the temperature**, by increasing the particles' kinetic energy, or
2. It produces a **change of state**, by increasing the particles' potential energy as the forces between them
   are overcome, while the temperature stays the same.

## Temperature change and specific heat capacity

When a system's temperature rises, the size of the rise depends on three things:

- the **mass** of the substance heated
- the **type of material** (its specific heat capacity)
- the **energy input** to the system.

The **specific heat capacity** of a substance is the amount of energy required to raise the temperature of one
kilogram of the substance by one degree Celsius.

```
change in thermal energy = mass × specific heat capacity × temperature change
ΔE = m c Δθ
ΔE in J, m in kg, c in J/kg °C, Δθ in °C
```

A material with a **high** specific heat capacity needs **more** energy for each degree of temperature rise, and
releases more energy for each degree it cools. Water's high value (about 4200 J/kg °C) is why it heats up and cools
down slowly.

**Worked example 1 (finding c).** An electric heater transfers 9000 J to a 1.0 kg metal block, and the block's
temperature rises from 20 °C to 30 °C. Calculate the specific heat capacity of the metal.

```
Δθ = 30 − 20 = 10 °C
c = ΔE ÷ (m Δθ) = 9000 ÷ (1.0 × 10)
c = 900 J/kg °C
```

**Worked example 2 (finding the temperature change).** 21 000 J is transferred to 0.25 kg of water
(c = 4200 J/kg °C). Calculate the rise in temperature.

```
Δθ = ΔE ÷ (m c) = 21 000 ÷ (0.25 × 4200) = 21 000 ÷ 1050
Δθ = 20 °C
```

## Changes of state and specific latent heat

The energy needed for a substance to change state is called **latent heat**. During a change of state, the energy
supplied changes the internal energy **but not the temperature**.

The **specific latent heat** of a substance is the amount of energy required to change the state of one kilogram
of the substance **with no change in temperature**.

```
energy for a change of state = mass × specific latent heat
E = m L
E in J, m in kg, L in J/kg
```

- **Specific latent heat of fusion**: change of state from solid to liquid.
- **Specific latent heat of vaporisation**: change of state from liquid to vapour.

The same amount of energy per kilogram is released when the change happens in reverse (freezing or condensing).

**Worked example 3.** The specific latent heat of vaporisation of water is 2 260 000 J/kg. Calculate the energy
needed to turn 0.050 kg of boiling water into steam.

```
E = m L = 0.050 × 2 260 000
E = 113 000 J  (113 kJ)
```

## Measuring the specific latent heat of fusion of water

1. Set up two funnels of crushed ice over two beakers. Place an immersion heater in one funnel; the other is a
   control with no heater.
2. Switch on the heater for a measured time and record the energy supplied (with a joulemeter, or from
   power × time).
3. Measure the mass of meltwater collected in each beaker.
4. Subtract the control's mass from the heated funnel's mass: this removes ice melted by the warm room.
5. Calculate L = E ÷ m.

**Worked example 4.** A 60 W heater runs for 5.0 minutes. The heated funnel collects 0.060 kg of water and the
control collects 0.006 kg. Calculate the specific latent heat of fusion.

```
E = P t = 60 × 300 = 18 000 J          (power × time from the Electricity topic)
m = 0.060 − 0.006 = 0.054 kg
L = E ÷ m = 18 000 ÷ 0.054
L = 333 000 J/kg  (3.3 × 10⁵ J/kg to 2 s.f.)
```

If some heater energy warms the surroundings instead of melting ice, less ice melts than expected and the value of
L calculated would be too high.

## Heating and cooling graphs

On a graph of temperature against time for a substance heated at a steady rate:

| Part of graph | What is happening | Particle energy |
|---|---|---|
| Sloping, before the first flat part | Solid warming | Kinetic energy rising |
| First flat part | Melting, at the melting point | Potential energy rising; temperature constant |
| Sloping, between flat parts | Liquid warming | Kinetic energy rising |
| Second flat part | Boiling, at the boiling point | Potential energy rising; temperature constant |
| Final slope | Gas warming | Kinetic energy rising |

- The **second flat part is usually much longer** than the first for water, because the specific latent heat of
  vaporisation is much larger than that of fusion.
- A **steeper slope** means a smaller specific heat capacity for that state, if the heating rate is the same.
- A **cooling graph** has the same features in reverse: flat parts at the condensing and freezing points, where
  energy is transferred to the surroundings while the temperature stays constant.

## Specific heat capacity versus specific latent heat

| | Specific heat capacity, c | Specific latent heat, L |
|---|---|---|
| What changes | Temperature | State |
| What stays the same | State | Temperature |
| Equation | ΔE = m c Δθ | E = m L |
| Unit | J/kg °C | J/kg |
| Graph | Sloping parts | Flat parts |

## Common mistakes

- Defining internal energy as only kinetic, or only "heat".
- Using a final temperature instead of the temperature change in ΔE = m c Δθ.
- Using ΔE = m c Δθ for a flat part of a heating graph.
- Saying the temperature rises during melting because energy is being supplied.
- Giving L in J/kg °C.
- Forgetting the control funnel, so meltwater from room warming is counted.

## Exam technique

- **Explain** a flat part of a graph: energy is still being supplied, it increases the internal energy (potential
  energy of the particles), but not the temperature, because the substance is changing state.
- **Calculate** in two stages when a substance warms and then changes state: ΔE = m c Δθ for the warming, then
  E = m L for the change of state, then add.
- **Distinguish** questions: one sentence for c, one for L, each saying what changes and what does not.
- **Evaluate** a latent heat experiment: say how energy losses affect the result and whether the value is too high
  or too low.
