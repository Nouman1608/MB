## Questions

**1.** Classify each activity in Country H as primary, secondary or tertiary: a dairy farm, a bakery making bread, and a private tutoring centre. **[3]**

**2.** Country H's nominal GDP is 850 billion units in year 1 and 918 billion in year 2. Its price index stands at 100 in year 1 and 108 in year 2.

**(a)** Calculate nominal growth and real growth between the two years. **[3]**
**(b)** Comment on what the figures show. **[1]**

**3.** In year 1 Country J has real GDP of 1,500 billion units and a population of 25.0 million. In year 2 real GDP is 1,545 billion units and the population is 25.5 million.

**(a)** Calculate real GDP per capita in each year. **[2]**
**(b)** Work out the percentage change in real GDP per capita. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain why this is lower than the growth in real GDP. **[1]**

**4.** Real GDP in Country K, as an index over five quarters, is 250.0, 248.5, 247.2, 247.9 and 250.6.

**(a)** Calculate the growth rate in quarter 2 and in quarter 3. **[2]**
**(b)** State whether Country K was in recession, giving a reason. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate how far output in quarter 4 is below its starting level. **[1]**
**(d)** State the first quarter in which output was back above its starting level. **[1]**

**5.** Explain the difference between short-run and long-run growth, using a PPF/C and an AD/AS diagram in your answer. **[6]**

**6.** Explain two difficulties in measuring GDP as an indicator of economic growth. **[6]**

**7.** Evaluate the view that investment in education is the most important cause of long-run growth in an economy. **[8]**

**8.** Evaluate the consequences of a recession for an economy. **[8]**

**9.** Country L's real GDP grows by 5% every year for ten years. Its population grows by 1% a year and its carbon dioxide emissions grow by 4% a year.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage increase in real GDP over the ten years. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage increase in real GDP per capita over the ten years. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the percentage increase in emissions, and the percentage change in emissions per unit of real GDP. **[2]**
**(d)** Evaluate whether this growth is worth its costs. **[4]**

**10.** Country M's nominal GDP over four years is 600, 642, 689 and 727 billion units. The price index is 100, 104, 109 and 112.

**(a)** Calculate real GDP in years 2, 3 and 4. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate cumulative nominal growth and cumulative real growth from year 1 to year 4. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why the two figures differ. **[2]**

**11.** Country N's real GDP is 900 billion units in 1990 and 1,200 billion in 2024. Sector shares were: primary 18%, secondary 30%, tertiary 52% in 1990; primary 7%, secondary 25%, tertiary 68% in 2024.

**(a)** Calculate the value of primary output in each year and its percentage change. **[3]**
**(b)** Evaluate the relationship between growth, this change of structure and sustainable development. **[5]**

## Answers

**1.** Dairy farm: primary [1]. Bakery making bread: secondary [1]. Tutoring centre: tertiary [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Name the sector for each activity and link it briefly to what the sector does (extracting, making, serving).

**2. (a)** Nominal growth = (918 - 850) / 850 x 100 = **8.0%** [1]. Real GDP in year 2 = 918 / 108 x 100 = 850 [1]. Real growth = (850 - 850) / 850 = **0%** [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** The whole rise in nominal GDP reflects higher prices; no more was produced [1].
*Examiner insight:* Show the deflating step as well as the final figure, and use the word "real" in the conclusion; answers that stop at 8% have not removed the effect of prices.

**3. (a)** Year 1: 1,500 billion / 25.0 million = **60,000** [1]. Year 2: 1,545 billion / 25.5 million = **60,588.24** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** (60,588.24 - 60,000) / 60,000 x 100 = **0.98%** [1].
**(c)** Real GDP grew by 3.0% but population grew by 2.0%, so output per head grew by much less [1].
*Examiner insight:* Show population in the same units as GDP before dividing; mixing billions and millions is a common slip that wrecks every later figure.

**4. (a)** Quarter 2: (248.5 - 250.0) / 250.0 = **-0.6%** [1]. Quarter 3: (247.2 - 248.5) / 248.5 = **-0.52%** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Yes: two consecutive quarters of negative growth (quarters 2 and 3) [1].
**(c)** (247.9 - 250.0) / 250.0 x 100 = **-0.84%** [1].
**(d)** Quarter 5 (250.6 is above 250.0) [1].
*Examiner insight:* A recession verdict is stronger when it cites the rule used and the quarters that meet it; "output fell" alone is thin.

**5.** Short-run growth is a rise in actual output using spare capacity [1]. On a PPF/C it is a move from a point inside the curve towards the curve [1]. On AD/AS it is a rightward shift of AD along SRAS, raising real output (and the price level, more so near capacity) [1]. Long-run growth is a rise in potential output [1]. On a PPF/C the whole curve shifts outwards [1]. On AD/AS the LRAS curve (and usually SRAS) shifts right, raising full-capacity output [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Say where each curve sits and which way it moves (price level, real output, LRAS, PPF/C); a description that never locates the shift stays thin.

**6.** First difficulty, for example the informal economy: cash-in-hand work and unreported output go unrecorded [1], so GDP understates output and comparisons are distorted where informal activity differs in size [1], which could make growth look different from reality [1]. Second difficulty, for example externalities: spending to repair pollution or storm damage counts as output [1], so GDP may overstate the rise in welfare [1], because resources used to repair damage give no net gain in living standards [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* State the direction of bias and why it matters for growth or comparisons; a list of four problems with one line each rarely does this.

**7.** Education raises human capital and productivity [1]. This raises potential output, shifting LRAS or the PPF/C outwards [1]. Other causes also raise capacity: investment in capital equipment, technology, resources and better institutions [1]. Education also raises the ability to adopt new technology [1]. Evaluation: education takes many years to pay off, and graduates need jobs and capital to work with [1]. Poor institutions or low investment may limit returns more than skills [1]. Where unemployment is high the short-run constraint may be demand, not skills [1]. Judgement: education is among the major long-run causes, but its importance depends on whether it is combined with investment, technology and sound institutions [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Strong evaluation compares education with a rival cause and reaches a conclusion, instead of saying only that "other factors matter".

**8.** Falling output reduces incomes and spending [1]. Firms cut jobs, so unemployment rises, with possible long-term unemployment and skills loss [1]. Profits and investment fall, which reduces future capacity [1]. Tax revenue falls and welfare spending rises, widening the budget deficit [1]. Evaluation: severity depends on depth and length; a short, shallow fall differs from a long deep one [1]. Depends on cause: a demand collapse differs from a supply shock [1]. Lower demand cuts imports and inflation, and weaker firms may exit, which can help efficiency in the long run [1]. Judgement: effects are usually negative and larger when unemployment persists, but policy response and starting position affect how serious it is [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Chains such as "output falls, so jobs fall, so spending falls" show analysis; a view on which consequence matters most supports a judgement.

**9. (a)** 1.05^10 = 1.6289 [1], so an increase of **62.9%** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Real GDP per capita factor = 1.6289 / 1.01^10 = 1.6289 / 1.1046 = 1.4746 [1], an increase of **47.5%** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Emissions factor = 1.04^10 = 1.4802, an increase of **48.0%** [1]. Emissions per unit of real GDP: 1.4802 / 1.6289 = 0.9087, a fall of **9.1%** [1]. **[2]**
**(d)** Benefits: real output per head up by almost half, so higher living standards and more tax revenue [1]. Cost: total emissions rise by 48%, even though they fall per unit of output, so environmental harm grows [1]. Judgement depends on the harm done by emissions and whether output is cleaner each year [1]. Conclusion supported by data: growth that is 9.1% cleaner per unit of output is not yet sustainable if total emissions continue to rise [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Bring your own results into the judgement (a 48.0% rise set against a 9.1% fall per unit); evaluation that stays general ignores the data supplied.

**10. (a)** Real GDP = nominal / index x 100 [1]: year 2 = 617.31, year 3 = 632.11, year 4 = 649.11 [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Nominal growth = (727 - 600) / 600 = **21.2%** [1]. Real growth = (649.11 - 600) / 600 = **8.2%** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Nominal GDP is valued at each year's prices, so it includes price rises [1]. The price index rose by 12%, which accounts for most of the gap between 21.2% and 8.2% [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Work with the base year consistently (year 1 as 100) and say which year's prices real GDP is measured in; mixing years is the usual error.

**11. (a)** 1990: 18% x 900 = 162 [1]. 2024: 7% x 1,200 = 84 [1]. Change = (84 - 162) / 162 x 100 = **-48.1%** [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** Real GDP rose by one third while primary output fell sharply, and tertiary rose from 468 to 816 [1]. Structural change shifts resources to services, which usually use fewer natural resources, so growth may be more compatible with sustainable development [1]. Higher income can fund investment in clean technology, education and infrastructure for future generations [1]. But a falling primary sector may mean job losses in some regions, and services depend on energy and imports, so impacts may be moved abroad [1]. Judgement: the relationship depends on what drives the growth and how the gains are used; structural change helps only if resources are protected and gains are shared [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Link the structural data to sustainable development explicitly (resource use, future generations); describing the shift without that link leaves the question unanswered.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Stating money-value growth when the question is about real output.
- Dividing by the index but forgetting to multiply by 100 (or the reverse).
- Mixing billions and millions when calculating GDP per capita.
- Describing a diagram in vague terms instead of naming axes and shifting curves.
- Listing difficulties of measuring GDP without saying whether it overstates or understates.
- Giving a recession verdict without citing the quarters or rule used.
- Evaluating in general terms rather than using the figures in the question.
