The [assessment revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-environmental-systems-and-societies-assessment-revision-notes/)
set out how ESS's written papers and fieldwork investigation are weighted. These notes turn that
into an exam-day plan -- specifically how to answer questions that deliberately combine scientific
and societal reasoning -- with a worked scenario and a checklist, alongside the
[full syllabus guide](/resources/ib-dp-environmental-systems-and-societies-syllabus-guide/) already
on the site.

## The core exam-preparation skill: answering across both strands

Because ESS can count as a sciences subject, an individuals-and-societies subject, or both, its
written papers are deliberately built to combine short-answer, data-based and extended-response
questions drawing on scientific methodology **and** societal analysis side by side, rather than
separating the two into different papers. The single most valuable exam-preparation habit is
therefore **checking every "evaluate" or "discuss" answer for both dimensions** before finishing it
-- a strong answer on an environmental policy, for instance, weighs both its scientific
effectiveness (does it actually reduce the measured environmental impact) and its social/economic
feasibility (can it realistically be implemented, who bears the cost), not just one.

## Command terms and what each expects

Lower-tier terms (**state**, **identify**, **outline**) need precise, short factual answers. Middle
terms (**explain**, **compare**, **suggest**) need a reasoned chain of cause and effect. Top-tier
terms (**evaluate**, **discuss**, **to what extent**) need a judgement that weighs multiple
perspectives or pieces of evidence -- and in ESS specifically, that weighing should draw on both the
scientific and societal strands wherever the question allows it, since this dual-strand reasoning is
precisely what distinguishes a strong ESS answer from a good but single-strand one.

## Worked practice scenario: evaluating an environmental policy

A question asks: "Evaluate the effectiveness of a government ban on single-use plastic bags as a
response to plastic pollution."

```
Scientific dimension:   A ban directly reduces the input of one specific plastic
                         source into the environment, but plastic bags are only
                         one contributor among many (packaging, fishing gear,
                         microplastics from other sources), so the ban's
                         measurable impact on overall plastic pollution may be
                         smaller than its symbolic impact suggests.

Societal dimension:      Effectiveness depends on enforcement, the availability
                         and cost of alternatives for consumers and businesses,
                         and public compliance -- a ban with weak enforcement or
                         no accessible substitute may simply push consumers
                         toward another single-use material with its own
                         environmental cost, rather than reducing overall waste.

Reasoned judgement:      The ban can be a genuinely effective first step for the
                         specific source it targets, but its overall effectiveness
                         against plastic pollution as a whole depends heavily on
                         enforcement, availability of alternatives, and whether
                         it is paired with wider measures addressing other
                         plastic sources -- a narrow scientific view (does it
                         reduce this one source) and a narrow societal view (is
                         it politically popular) each miss half the picture.
```

Explicitly labelling the scientific and societal dimensions before writing the judgement, as shown
here, is a reliable way to make sure both strands are genuinely present in the answer rather than
one being addressed only in passing.

## Before/during exam checklist

- **Before the exam**: practise identifying, for a range of environmental issues, both a scientific
  and a societal angle before attempting to answer; practise full "evaluate"/"discuss" answers that
  explicitly address both dimensions rather than defaulting to whichever strand feels more familiar.
- **During the exam**: for any question using a top-tier command term, check your answer plan
  includes both a scientific and a societal consideration before you start writing; use data
  precisely where a data-based question supplies it, rather than answering only in general terms.
- **On every paper**: match answer length and depth to the command term and mark allocation, and
  make sure extended responses end with an explicit judgement.

## Using terminology precisely across both strands

ESS draws vocabulary from both scientific and societal fields, and precise terminology matters in
both directions -- a scientific term used loosely (calling any environmental change "unsustainable"
without defining the specific system or timescale) loses marks just as readily as a societal term
used loosely (calling a policy "effective" without stating effective at what, and according to whom).
Building a short glossary of precisely defined terms from both strands -- carrying capacity, resilience
and tipping point on the scientific side; environmental value system, sustainability and equity on
the societal side -- and practising using each correctly in a full sentence is a fast way to close
this specific gap before the exam.

## Self-test

1. Why are ESS's written papers deliberately built to combine scientific and societal content in
   the same questions?
2. What should you check before finishing an "evaluate" or "discuss" answer in this subject
   specifically?
3. In the worked scenario, what is one scientific limitation and one societal factor affecting the
   plastic-bag ban's effectiveness?
4. What distinguishes a strong ESS top-tier answer from a good but incomplete one?

**Answers:** 1. Because ESS can be counted toward either the sciences group or the
individuals-and-societies group (or both), so its assessment is built to be defensible as either a
science or a humanities subject, combining both strands within the same papers rather than
separating them. 2. Whether the answer weighs both the scientific and the societal dimension of the
issue, not just one. 3. Scientific limitation: the ban only addresses one source among many
contributors to plastic pollution; societal factor: effectiveness depends on enforcement and
whether accessible alternatives exist for consumers and businesses. 4. A strong answer explicitly
weighs both the scientific and societal dimensions of the issue before reaching a judgement; an
incomplete answer addresses only one dimension, even if that single dimension is well argued.
