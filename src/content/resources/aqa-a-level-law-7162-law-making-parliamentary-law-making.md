This guide covers the **Law making: parliamentary law making** row of AQA A-level Law (7162) section 3.1. The specification lists five things: **Green and White papers**; **the formal legislative process**; **the influences on parliament**; **the doctrine of parliamentary supremacy and limitations on it**; and **the advantages and disadvantages of influences on parliamentary law making**. The [Rule of Law, Parliament, Morality and Justice study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-law-rule-of-law-parliament-morality-and-justice-study-guide/) gives the outline: types of Bill, the five stages, the Parliament Acts, a table of influences and the main limits on supremacy. This page goes into the detail that separates a middle-band answer from a top-band one.

## Before a Bill: Green and White Papers

Most government Bills begin as policy inside a government department. Consultation is not legally required for every Bill, but it is normal for significant reform.

- A **Green Paper** sets out options and invites comment from anyone interested: professional bodies, pressure groups, businesses and the public. It is genuinely open.
- A **White Paper** sets out the government's settled proposals, usually after consultation, and is often followed directly by a Bill.
- A **draft Bill** may also be published for **pre-legislative scrutiny** by a parliamentary committee before formal introduction, allowing technical problems to be fixed early.

**Evaluation:** consultation improves quality and legitimacy, but government decides whether to consult, how long to allow and whether to take any notice of the responses.

## Kinds of Bill in more detail

| Type | Introduced by | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Public Bill (government) | A minister | Most major legislation |
| Private members' Bill | A backbench MP or peer | Murder (Abolition of Death Penalty) Act 1965, introduced by Sydney Silverman |
| Private Bill | A promoter such as a local authority or company, affecting a particular body or place | Bills to give a local authority specific powers |
| Hybrid Bill | Government; a public Bill that also affects particular private interests | High Speed Rail (London - West Midlands) Act 2017 |

Private members' Bills enter through three routes in the Commons: the **ballot** at the start of each session, which gives the most debating time; the **Ten Minute Rule**, which allows a short speech and is mainly a way of raising an issue; and **presentation**, with no speech. Few succeed without government support, because government controls most parliamentary time. The Voyeurism (Offences) Act 2019, which made "upskirting" an offence, began as a private member's Bill that was blocked by a single objection, after which the government adopted the measure as its own Bill.

## The formal legislative process: the detail

| Stage | Commons | Lords |
|---|---|---|
| First reading | Formal: title read; no debate | Formal |
| Second reading | Main debate on principle; vote | Main debate on principle; Lords rarely vote against a government Bill at this stage |
| Committee stage | Usually a **Public Bill Committee** of about 16 to 50 MPs reflecting party strength; some Bills, such as major constitutional Bills, go to a **Committee of the whole House** | Usually a committee of the whole House or a Grand Committee open to all peers |
| Report stage | The amended Bill is considered; further amendments | Further amendments |
| Third reading | Final debate; in the Commons no substantive amendments | Amendments still possible |

**Ping-pong:** if the second House amends a Bill, it returns to the first, and may pass back and forth until both agree on the text.

**Royal Assent:** under the Royal Assent Act 1967 the monarch's assent is normally notified to each House rather than given in person. Assent has not been refused since 1708 and is now a formality by convention.

**Commencement:** an Act may come into force on Royal Assent, on a date stated in the Act, or on a date set later by a minister through a **commencement order**, which is a statutory instrument. Some provisions are never brought into force.

### When the Lords and Commons disagree

The **Parliament Act 1911** removed the Lords' power to veto most public Bills: money Bills could be delayed for only one month and other Bills for two years. The **Parliament Act 1949** reduced the delay for non-money Bills to one year. The Acts have been used rarely; besides the War Crimes Act 1991 and the Hunting Act 2004, they were used for the European Parliamentary Elections Act 1999 and the Sexual Offences (Amendment) Act 2000. The **Salisbury Convention** means the Lords do not usually block, at second reading, a government Bill delivering a commitment in its election manifesto.

## Influences on Parliament: new examples and evaluation

The specification requires the influences **and** their advantages and disadvantages. Use different examples for each, and evaluate each one.

### Pressure groups

**Insider groups** have regular access to government and are consulted (the British Medical Association, the Law Society). **Outsider groups** lack access and rely on publicity and protest. Campaigns by individuals can also work: Gina Martin's campaign contributed to the Voyeurism (Offences) Act 2019, and a campaign after a police dog, Finn, was stabbed contributed to the Animal Welfare (Service Animals) Act 2019, known as "Finn's Law".

- *Advantages:* expertise; give a voice to people who would otherwise be unheard; raise issues parties neglect.
- *Disadvantages:* influence depends on money and contacts; a narrow interest may be presented as public opinion; law made in reaction to one case may be too narrow.

### The media

Newspapers, broadcasters and social media publicise issues and can pressure the government into acting quickly.

- *Advantages:* fast, reach a large audience, can expose problems (media investigations put pressure on government after high-profile failures).
- *Disadvantages:* media owners have their own agendas; pressure for speed can produce badly drafted law; coverage reflects what sells, not what matters most.

### Political parties and manifestos

The governing party's **manifesto** sets much of the legislative programme, and the **King's Speech** at the start of each session announces it.

- *Advantages:* democratic mandate from an election; voters know what to expect.
- *Disadvantages:* a large majority lets the government pass laws with little effective opposition; manifestos bundle many policies, so a vote is not approval of each one.

### Law Commission and expert bodies

Law Commission reports, often with draft Bills, can lead to legislation, such as the Fraud Act 2006.

- *Advantages:* independence and legal expertise; careful consultation.
- *Disadvantages:* reports depend on government finding time; many are delayed or not implemented.

### Public opinion

Elected MPs respond to constituents, petitions and polls.

- *Advantages:* keeps law in line with social attitudes.
- *Disadvantages:* opinion can be volatile, poorly informed or hostile to minorities.

## Parliamentary supremacy and its limits

### The doctrine

1. **Parliament can make or unmake any law.**
2. **No Parliament can bind its successors.** If a later Act conflicts with an earlier one, the later Act impliedly repeals it, as in *Vauxhall Estates v Liverpool Corporation* (1932) and *Ellen Street Estates v Minister of Health* (1934).
3. **No court or other body can set aside an Act.** In *British Railways Board v Pickin* (1974) the courts refused to examine whether Parliament had been misled during a private Bill's passage.

### The limits

- **Constitutional statutes.** In *Thoburn v Sunderland City Council* (2002), the "metric martyrs" case, Laws LJ said some statutes, such as the European Communities Act 1972 and the Human Rights Act 1998, are "constitutional" and cannot be impliedly repealed; only express words will do.
- **Human Rights Act 1998.** Courts read legislation compatibly with Convention rights where possible (section 3) and can make a declaration of incompatibility (section 4). A minister must make a **statement of compatibility** on a Bill under section 19, or state that he cannot but wishes to proceed. In practice Parliament usually responds to declarations.
- **Devolution.** The Scotland Act 1998, Government of Wales Act 1998 and Northern Ireland Act 1998 gave law-making powers to devolved bodies. The **Sewel Convention**, that Westminster will not normally legislate on devolved matters without consent, is now written into statute, but in *R (Miller) v Secretary of State for Exiting the European Union* (2017) the Supreme Court held it is a political convention that courts cannot enforce.
- **European Union membership** (historical): *Factortame* allowed courts to disapply Acts that conflicted with EU law while the UK was a member. Withdrawal removed this limit.
- **Practical and political limits:** the electorate, public opinion and international obligations restrain what Parliament will actually do.

## Worked example: evaluation of one influence

**Question:** Discuss the advantages and disadvantages of the media as an influence on Parliament. (Plan)

```
Define: newspapers, broadcasting, social media
Advantage 1: speed and reach -> issue reaches government quickly
Advantage 2: scrutiny of failures -> accountability
Disadvantage 1: agenda of owners and editors -> distorted priorities
Disadvantage 2: reaction to one case -> rushed, poorly drafted law
Weigh: media works best as a trigger for consultation, worst as a substitute for it
Conclude
```

## Common mistakes

- Saying a Green Paper contains firm proposals.
- Describing the stages without the role of each (what actually happens at committee).
- Stating that the Lords can veto any Bill, or that the Parliament Acts apply to every Bill.
- Listing influences without advantages and disadvantages, which the specification names separately.
- Treating the Human Rights Act as allowing courts to strike down Acts.

## Exam technique

**Outline** the process with each stage's function, not a list of names. **Explain** an influence by saying how it works and giving an example. **Discuss** or **evaluate** influences or supremacy by weighing both sides and concluding.
