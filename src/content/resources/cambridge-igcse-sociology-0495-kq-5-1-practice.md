These questions practise key question 5.1 of Cambridge IGCSE Sociology (0495), *What is education and schooling?* Section A uses the short-answer styles of parts (a) and (b); Section B uses the longer "explain", "discuss" and "evaluate" styles.

## Section A

**1.** Define the term *formal education*. **[2]**

**2.** Define the term *vocational learning*. **[2]**

**3.** Give **two** features of the official curriculum. **[2]**

**4.** Give **two** alternative approaches to education other than homeschooling. **[2]**

**5.** Define the term *faith school*. **[2]**

**6.** Give **two** limitations of homeschooling. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** Explain **three** differences between formal and informal education. **[6]**

**8.** Explain **three** strengths of online learning. **[6]**

**9.** Explain **three** limitations of selective schools. **[6]**

**10.** Discuss the strengths and limitations of progressive schooling.

Your answer should include:
- at least three developed points with evidence. **[8]**

**11.** Discuss how effective faith schools are for their students and for society.

Your answer should include:
- at least three developed points with evidence. **[8]**

**12.** Evaluate the view that private schools are more effective than state schools.

Your answer should include:
- at least three arguments for the view and three arguments against the view
- a conclusion. **[14]**

## Answers

### Section A

**1. [2]** Planned, structured learning that takes place in institutions such as schools and colleges (1), taught by trained teachers following a curriculum and usually assessed for qualifications (1).

**2. [2]** Education or training designed to prepare people for a particular job or trade (1), for example an apprenticeship in plumbing or a course in hospitality (1).

**3. [2]** Any two, 1 mark each: the subjects taught; the syllabus content within subjects; testing and examinations; a timetable of lessons; qualifications at the end.

**4. [2]** Any two, 1 mark each: online learning; unschooling; vocational learning; progressive schooling.

**5. [2]** A school with a religious character, often linked to a particular faith organisation (1), which teaches the normal curriculum alongside the beliefs, worship and values of that religion (1).

**6. [2]** Any two, 1 mark each: depends on parents' time and subject knowledge; costly in lost earnings and materials; fewer opportunities to mix with peers; harder for authorities to check quality; limited access to specialist facilities such as laboratories.

### Section B

**7. [6]** Three differences, 1 for identifying and 1 for developing each:
- **Setting**: formal education takes place in schools and colleges; informal education happens anywhere in daily life, such as the home or online.
- **Planning**: formal education follows a set curriculum and timetable; informal learning is unplanned and often unconscious.
- **Who teaches**: trained, paid teachers in formal education; parents, peers and media in informal education.
- **Assessment and outcomes**: formal education is tested and leads to qualifications; informal learning shows in skills and behaviour without certificates.

**8. [6]** Three strengths, 1 + 1 each:
- **Flexibility**: learners can study at their own pace and time, fitting around work or caring responsibilities.
- **Access for remote learners**: students in rural areas or without a local specialist teacher can take courses.
- **Wide choice**: a greater range of subjects and courses than one school could offer.
- **Continuity in emergencies**: learning can continue when schools close, for example during illness or disasters.
- **Digital skills**: learners develop skills valued in modern workplaces.

**9. [6]** Three limitations, 1 + 1 each:
- **Favours advantaged children**: entry tests reward tutoring and cultural capital, so middle-class pupils are over-represented.
- **Labelling**: children who fail to gain entry may see themselves as failures, lowering motivation (self-fulfilling prophecy).
- **Early judgement**: ability is assessed at a young age, ignoring late development.
- **Reduces social mixing**: pupils of different backgrounds are separated, weakening social cohesion.
- **Results reflect intake**: high exam results may say more about who is admitted than about teaching quality.

**10. [8]** Levels: 7–8 good knowledge, three developed points with evidence, terms used well; 4–6 some knowledge, most points supported, some undeveloped; 1–3 limited, descriptive.
Indicative content:
- Progressive schools are child-centred: pupils have choice in what and how they learn. Summerhill (A. S. Neill, 1921) makes lessons optional and runs through school meetings.
- **Strengths**: intrinsic motivation; confidence and independence; democratic and decision-making skills; less pressure from constant testing.
- **Limitations**: pupils may avoid difficult subjects and leave with fewer qualifications; outcomes are hard to compare with mainstream schools; often fee-paying, so limited to some families; may not suit children who need structure.
- Evaluation: success may depend on the individual child and the family's support.

**11. [8]** Levels as Question 10. Indicative content:
- **Effective for students**: a strong shared ethos and sense of community; clear expectations of behaviour; some faith schools achieve good results.
- **Critical points**: results may reflect selective admissions or more supportive families rather than the school itself; pupils may have less contact with other religions and cultures.
- **Effective for society?** Supporters argue faith schools protect religious freedom and community identity; critics argue they can divide communities along religious and ethnic lines and weaken social cohesion.
- Evaluation: effectiveness depends on how it is measured and on admissions policies.

**12. [14]** Levels: 12–14 excellent knowledge, three developed and evidenced points on each side, balanced, with a conclusion; 8–11 good knowledge, developed points on both sides but less balance; 4–7 some knowledge, simple two-sided or one-sided answer; 1–3 limited and descriptive. A one-sided answer cannot score more than 6.
*For the view:*
- Smaller class sizes allow more individual attention.
- More resources: facilities, technology, extracurricular activities.
- Strong exam results and university entry in many private schools.
- Networks and contacts that help with careers (social capital).
- Can attract and keep experienced teachers.
*Against the view:*
- Intake: private schools admit pupils whose families already have money and cultural capital, so results may not reflect school effectiveness; value added may be similar.
- Many state schools achieve excellent results and progress with fewer resources.
- Effectiveness for society: Marxists argue private schools reproduce class inequality and undermine meritocracy.
- State schools may prepare pupils better for a socially mixed society.
- Quality varies widely within both sectors.
*Conclusion:* a supported judgement, for example that private schools often have higher raw results, but once intake is considered the difference is smaller, and they may be less effective for equality of opportunity.
