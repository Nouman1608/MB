This guide covers sub-topic **2.1 Elements, compounds and mixtures** of the Cambridge IGCSE Chemistry 0620 syllabus for 2026, 2027 and 2028. The sub-topic has one Core outcome, and there is no Supplement content:

> 1 Describe the differences between elements, compounds and mixtures

The [elements, atomic structure and isotopes guide](/resources/igcse-chemistry-0620-elements-atomic-structure-and-isotopes/) introduces the three definitions with iron and sulfur. This page goes further: it compares the three across five features, shows how to read particle diagrams described in words, and works through classification problems with everyday substances. Recap with the [elements, compounds and mixtures revision notes](/resources/cambridge-igcse-chemistry-0620-elements-compounds-and-mixtures-revision-notes/) and practise with the [elements, compounds and mixtures practice questions](/resources/cambridge-igcse-chemistry-0620-elements-compounds-and-mixtures-practice/).

## The three definitions

- An **element** is a substance made of only one type of atom. It cannot be broken down into anything simpler by chemical reactions.
- A **compound** is a substance made of two or more elements chemically combined in a fixed proportion.
- A **mixture** is two or more substances (elements or compounds) that are together but not chemically combined.

Elements and compounds are both **pure substances**. A mixture is not a single substance.

## Five differences

"Describe the differences" questions reward specific comparisons. These five cover almost every question.

| Feature | Element | Compound | Mixture |
|---|---|---|---|
| Particles | One type of atom; may exist as single atoms or as molecules of identical atoms | Atoms of different elements bonded together in a fixed ratio | Different substances whose particles are not bonded to each other |
| Composition | Fixed | Fixed: always the same proportions, shown by a formula | Variable: proportions can be changed |
| Properties | Its own | Different from the elements it is made from | Each component keeps its own properties |
| Separation | Cannot be split into simpler substances | Only by a chemical reaction | By physical methods such as filtration, distillation or using a magnet |
| Melting and boiling | Sharp, fixed points | Sharp, fixed points | Usually over a range of temperatures |
| Formation | — | A chemical reaction, usually with an energy change | No reaction; just mixing |

### Properties change when a compound forms

The clearest evidence that a compound is a new substance is that its properties are completely different from those of its elements.

| Elements | Compound |
|---|---|
| Sodium: a soft, very reactive metal. Chlorine: a toxic, green-yellow gas | Sodium chloride: a white, unreactive solid used as table salt |
| Hydrogen: a flammable gas. Oxygen: a gas that supports burning | Water: a liquid at room temperature, used to put out many fires |
| Magnesium: a shiny grey metal. Oxygen: a colourless gas | Magnesium oxide: a white powder |

In a mixture of hydrogen and oxygen, each gas keeps its properties: the mixture would burn explosively if lit, because the hydrogen is still flammable. Once they have reacted to form water, that property is gone.

## Elements made of molecules

Students often assume an element must be made of single atoms. Many are not. Oxygen exists as O₂ molecules, nitrogen as N₂ and chlorine as Cl₂. Each molecule contains two atoms, but both atoms are of the same element, so the substance is still an element. The test is the **type** of atom, not how many atoms are joined.

| Substance | Formula | Element or compound? | Reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nitrogen | N₂ | Element | Both atoms are nitrogen |
| Carbon monoxide | CO | Compound | Atoms of two different elements |
| Ozone | O₃ | Element | Three atoms, all oxygen |
| Hydrogen chloride | HCl | Compound | Hydrogen and chlorine bonded |

The formula helps: count the **different element symbols**. One symbol means an element; two or more means a compound. Watch capital letters: Co (cobalt) is an element, but CO (carbon monoxide) is a compound.

## Reading particle diagrams

Exams often show boxes of particles and ask you to identify each one. When diagrams are described in words, apply three questions:

1. Are all the particles the same?
2. Within each particle, are the atoms the same or different?
3. Are there two or more kinds of particle?

### Worked example 1: diagrams described in words

- **Box A** contains identical particles, each made of two atoms of the same kind joined together. → Every particle is the same, and each is made of one type of atom: an **element** made of molecules, like O₂.
- **Box B** contains identical particles, each made of one large atom joined to two smaller, different atoms. → Every particle is the same, but each contains two types of atom bonded: a **compound**, like H₂O.
- **Box C** contains some single atoms and some pairs of identical joined atoms of a different kind. → Two kinds of particle not bonded to each other: a **mixture of two elements**.
- **Box D** contains some particles like those in Box A and some like those in Box B. → A **mixture of an element and a compound**.

Notice that Box D is still a mixture, even though one of its components is a compound. Being made of molecules does not make a substance a compound, and containing a compound does not make a sample a single compound.

## Worked example 2: everyday substances

Classify each substance and give the reason.

| Substance | Classification | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Air | Mixture | Nitrogen, oxygen, argon, carbon dioxide and water vapour, not chemically combined; its composition varies |
| Distilled water | Compound | Hydrogen and oxygen chemically combined in a fixed ratio, H₂O |
| Sea water | Mixture | Water with dissolved salts; the amount of salt varies from place to place |
| Copper | Element | Only copper atoms |
| Carbon dioxide | Compound | Carbon and oxygen combined, CO₂ |
| Brass | Mixture | An alloy of copper and zinc, which are not chemically combined |
| Sodium chloride | Compound | Sodium and chlorine combined, NaCl |

Alloys catch many students out. An alloy is a mixture of a metal with other elements, so brass and steel are mixtures, not compounds.

## Worked example 3: deciding from evidence

A student heats a grey powder, Y, in air. It glows brightly and forms a white powder with a greater mass. The white powder cannot be split into anything simpler by heating alone.

- The grey powder reacted with oxygen from the air: the bright glow is an energy change and the product has new properties.
- The mass increased because oxygen atoms combined with Y.
- So the white powder is a **compound** of Y and oxygen, and Y may be an element (a metal such as magnesium behaves this way).

The reasoning, not just the answer, earns the marks: energy change, new properties, gain in mass from combining with another element.

## Common mistakes

- Saying an element "contains one atom". It contains one **type** of atom.
- Thinking O₂ or N₂ is a compound because it has two atoms.
- Calling a mixture a compound because it contains more than one element.
- Classifying alloys or air as compounds.
- Writing that a compound "has the properties of its elements".
- Saying a compound can be separated by filtration or distillation into its elements.

## Exam technique

- **Define**: one precise sentence; use "chemically combined" for compounds and "not chemically combined" for mixtures.
- **Describe the differences**: make paired comparisons, such as "a compound has a fixed composition, but a mixture's composition can vary".
- **Identify from a diagram**: say what you see in the particles, then name the type.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is pure water an element or a compound?**
A compound. It is made of hydrogen and oxygen atoms chemically combined in a fixed ratio of 2 : 1.

**Can a mixture contain compounds?**
Yes. Sea water is a mixture of water, a compound, and dissolved salts, which are also compounds.
