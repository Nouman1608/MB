This guide covers **section 3.1.3 Research methods** in OxfordAQA International GCSE Sociology (9292). It is the one part of the specification examined in **both papers**, so this page serves Paper 1 and Paper 2. The [Introducing Sociology guide](/resources/igcse-oxfordaqa-sociology-introducing-sociology/) gives a one-table summary of methods; this page goes through every content area in depth.

## How research methods is examined in each paper

The content is the same; the question style differs.

| | Paper 1 Introducing Sociology | Paper 2 Exploring Sociology |
|---|---|---|
| Section | Section C, 16 marks | Section C, 16 marks |
| Question style | Short questions and a **structured question based on data about an unseen issue of global sociological significance** | Short and extended response questions |
| What to expect | Reading a table, chart or extract you have not seen before, then explaining its patterns, strengths and limitations | Applying and evaluating methods in more depth, often linked to Paper 2 topics such as inequality, socialisation or crime |

So for Paper 1, practise **interpreting unfamiliar data** quickly and accurately; for Paper 2, practise **extended evaluation** of a method for a specific research topic. Both need the same vocabulary.

## What section 3.1.3 covers

- **3.1.3.1 Approaches**: quantitative and qualitative data; primary and secondary sources; positivist and interpretivist approaches.
- **3.1.3.2 Methods**: surveys and questionnaires (postal, telephone, online, self-completion; types of question); interviews (structured, unstructured, group); ethnography and participant observation; covert, overt and non-participant observation; laboratory and field experiments; triangulation; longitudinal studies; qualitative and quantitative secondary sources.
- **3.1.3.3 Issues**: operationalisation; populations and sampling frames; random, systematic, quota, multistage, stratified random and snowball samples; validity and reliability; researcher and interviewer bias, the Hawthorne effect, social desirability; time and funding; ethics; causation and correlation; representativeness and generalisability.
- **3.1.3.4 Interpreting data**: tables, charts and graphs; extracts from field notes and unstructured interviews.

## Approaches to research

**Positivists** model sociology on the natural sciences. They prefer **quantitative** data from large samples, using methods such as questionnaires and official statistics, to find patterns and causes. Durkheim's study of suicide, based on official statistics, is the classic example.

**Interpretivists** want to understand the **meanings** people give to their actions. They prefer **qualitative** data from methods such as unstructured interviews and participant observation, which give depth and **validity**.

**Primary** data is collected by the researcher; **secondary** data already exists.

## The main methods

**Surveys and questionnaires.** Postal and online surveys reach large, scattered samples cheaply but often have low response rates; telephone surveys get quicker replies but can feel intrusive. Question types:

- **Closed** questions with fixed answers, easy to count.
- **Open** questions, letting respondents answer in their own words.
- **Scaled** questions, such as rating agreement from 1 to 5.

**Interviews.** **Structured** interviews follow a fixed schedule, so they are reliable and comparable. **Unstructured** interviews are like guided conversations, giving rich, valid data but taking longer and being hard to repeat. Oakley's research on housework used interviews with housewives. **Group interviews** can draw out shared views but louder members may dominate.

**Observation and ethnography.** **Ethnography** studies a group's way of life in its natural setting, usually by **participant observation**, where the researcher joins in. **Overt** observation means the group knows they are being studied; **covert** observation means they do not, which avoids the Hawthorne effect but raises ethical problems of deception. In **non-participant** observation the researcher watches without joining in. Willis combined observation and interviews in his study of working-class lads at school.

**Experiments.** **Laboratory** experiments give control over variables but are artificial; **field** experiments take place in real settings, as in Rosenthal and Jacobson's study of teacher expectations, but give less control and raise questions of consent.

**Triangulation** combines methods or data sources to check findings and gain both breadth and depth. **Longitudinal studies** follow the same group over time to show change, but are expensive and people drop out.

**Secondary sources.** **Qualitative**: diaries, letters and personal documents, which give insight into feelings but may be unrepresentative. **Quantitative**: official statistics, which are cheap and cover whole populations but are collected for official purposes and may not measure what the sociologist wants.

## Issues in research

- **Operationalisation**: turning a concept into something measurable. To study "poverty", a researcher must decide whether to measure income, possessions or both.
- **Population and sampling frame**: the population is the whole group being studied; the sampling frame is the list from which the sample is drawn, such as a school register.

| Sample | How it works |
|---|---|
| Random | Everyone has an equal chance of selection |
| Systematic | Every *n*th name on the list |
| Stratified random | The population is divided into groups (such as by gender) and a random sample is taken from each in proportion |
| Quota | The researcher fills set numbers from each category, choosing who to ask |
| Multistage | Sampling in stages, such as choosing regions, then schools, then pupils |
| Snowball | Each participant introduces others; used for hard-to-reach groups |

- **Validity**: a true picture of what is studied. **Reliability**: the same results when repeated.
- **Researcher or interviewer bias**: the researcher's views or presence influence results. **Hawthorne effect**: people behave differently because they know they are being studied. **Social desirability**: people give answers that make them look good.
- **Practical issues**: time, funding and access.
- **Ethics**: **informed consent**, **confidentiality**, **anonymity** and **participant wellbeing**.
- **Causation and correlation**: two things rising together (correlation) does not prove one causes the other.
- **Representativeness and generalisability**: whether a sample reflects the population, so findings can be applied more widely.

## Interpreting data

For a **table, chart or graph**: read the title, units and source first; state the highest and lowest figures; describe the overall **trend or pattern**; **quantify** differences; note what the data does **not** show, such as causes or how the data was collected. For a **qualitative extract**, such as field notes, identify what it reveals about **meanings**, quote a short phrase as evidence, and consider whether the observer's presence may have shaped what happened.

## Worked example: choosing a method for a Paper 2 topic

*A sociologist wants to study how young people experience racism in their town. Which method would you choose?*

```
Questionnaire     anonymous, so may reduce social desirability;
                  large sample shows how common racism is;
                  BUT closed questions lose the meaning of experiences
Unstructured      rich, valid accounts of how racism feels;
interview         BUT small sample; interviewer's ethnicity may affect
                  what is said (interviewer bias)
Ethics            sensitive topic: informed consent, anonymity,
                  wellbeing and support afterwards
Judgement         triangulation: questionnaire for extent, then
                  unstructured interviews for meaning
```

## Common mistakes

- Confusing reliability with validity.
- Calling any sample chosen "at random" a random sample; it needs a sampling frame.
- Treating a correlation as proof of cause.
- Evaluating a method in general rather than for the topic in the question.
- Describing data without quoting figures from it.
