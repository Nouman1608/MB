These questions cover section **3.3.11 Amines** of the AQA AS and A-level Chemistry specification
(7404/7405), version 1.1, 1 December 2015, for exams from June 2016 onwards: 3.3.11.1 Preparation, 3.3.11.2
Base properties and 3.3.11.3 Nucleophilic properties. All of it is **A-level only**. Organic chemistry is
placed on A-level Paper 2, and Paper 3 can test any content.

Learn the ideas first in the [amines study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-amines/) and the
[amines revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-amines-revision-notes/). The course hub is
[AQA A-Level Chemistry](/boards/aqa/a-level/chemistry/), and the
[printable checklist](/checklists/aqa/a-level/chemistry/) lists every specification point. For the first
substitution step, see the [halogenoalkanes practice questions](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-halogenoalkanes-practice/).
Use Ar values H = 1.0, C = 12.0, N = 14.0, O = 16.0, Cl = 35.5, Br = 79.9 wherever they are needed.

## Questions

**1.** Classify each amine as primary, secondary or tertiary.
(i) CH₃CH(NH₂)CH₂CH₃ (ii) CH₃NHCH₂CH₂CH₃ (iii) (CH₃CH₂)₃N **[3]**

**2.** Propanenitrile, CH₃CH₂CN, can be reduced to a primary amine. Suggest a suitable reducing agent and
write an equation for the reaction, using [H] for the reducing agent. **[3]**

**3.** Phenylamine is made from nitrobenzene.

**(a)** State the reagents used to reduce nitrobenzene, and the substance added afterwards to release free
phenylamine. **[2]**
**(b)** Write an equation for the reduction, using [H]. **[1]**
**(c)** 18.45 g of nitrobenzene gives 10.32 g of phenylamine. Calculate the percentage yield. **[3]**
**(d)** State one use of aromatic amines given in the specification. **[1]**

**4.** Methylamine, ammonia and phenylamine all act as bases.

**(a)** Write the three in order of decreasing base strength. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain the order. **[4]**

**5.** Butylamine, CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂NH₂, dissolves in water to give an alkaline solution.

**(a)** Write an equation for butylamine reacting with water, and explain why butylamine is described as
a weak base. **[2]**
**(b)** Write an equation for butylamine reacting with hydrochloric acid and name the salt formed. **[2]**
**(c)** State how butylamine can be recovered from this salt. **[1]**

**6.** Propylamine reacts with bromoethane to form a secondary amine.

**(a)** Outline the mechanism. Describe each curly arrow and the intermediate. **[4]**
**(b)** Name the type of mechanism. **[1]**

**7.** Ammonia reacts with excess chloromethane.

**(a)** Give the formulae of the three amines formed in sequence, and of the final product. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why the sequence stops at the final product. **[2]**
**(c)** Trimethylamine reacts with 1-bromohexadecane, CH₃(CH₂)₁₅Br. Give the formula of the product and
explain why it can act as a cationic surfactant. **[3]**

**8.** Propanoyl chloride, CH₃CH₂COCl, reacts with excess ethylamine.

**(a)** Write an equation for the reaction. **[2]**
**(b)** Name the organic product. **[1]**
**(c)** Outline the mechanism. Describe each curly arrow. **[4]**

**9.** Phenylamine reacts with ethanoic anhydride.

**(a)** Write an equation for the reaction (1 : 1). **[2]**
**(b)** Name the organic product containing nitrogen. **[1]**
**(c)** State one difference between this reaction and the reaction of phenylamine with ethanoyl chloride.
**[1]**

**10.** 1.18 g of a primary aliphatic amine was dissolved in water and made up to 250 cm³. A 25.0 cm³
portion needed 20.0 cm³ of 0.100 mol dm⁻³ hydrochloric acid for neutralisation. One mole of amine reacts
with one mole of HCl.

**(a)** Calculate the Mr of the amine. **[3]**
**(b)** Deduce the molecular formula. **[1]**
**(c)** Draw or write the structures, and give the names, of the two primary amines with this formula.
**[2]**

**11.** A chemist wants to make hexylamine, CH₃(CH₂)₅NH₂, from 1-bromopentane.

**(a)** Give a two-step route. For each step state the reagent and conditions, and name the intermediate.
**[4]**
**(b)** Step 1 has an 85% yield and step 2 a 60% yield. Calculate the overall percentage yield. **[1]**
**(c)** A second chemist heats 1-bromopentane with excess ammonia instead. Name the main organic product
and calculate the percentage atom economy for its formation. **[3]**
**(d)** Explain why the product mixture in (c) is harder to purify than the product of the nitrile route.
**[2]**

## Answers

**1.** (i) **primary** [1] (one carbon on N); (ii) **secondary** [1]; (iii) **tertiary** [1] **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* For (i) the carbon bearing N is secondary, but the amine is primary; count carbons on N,
not on the carbon next to it.

**2.** LiAlH₄ (in dry ether), or H₂ with a nickel catalyst [1];
CH₃CH₂CN + 4[H] → **CH₃CH₂CH₂NH₂**: correct amine [1]; 4[H] to balance [1] **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* An equation with 2[H] is unbalanced and loses the balancing mark, even with the correct
product.

**3. (a)** Tin and concentrated hydrochloric acid [1]; then sodium hydroxide [1].
**(b)** C₆H₅NO₂ + 6[H] → C₆H₅NH₂ + 2H₂O [1]
**(c)** n(C₆H₅NO₂) = 18.45 ÷ 123.0 = 0.150 mol [1]; theoretical mass = 0.150 × 93.0 = 13.95 g [1];
yield = 10.32 ÷ 13.95 × 100 = **74.0%** [1]
**(d)** Manufacture of **dyes** [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* An error in an Mr in (c) can usually be carried forward into the later steps, so show
each value.

**4. (a)** **methylamine > ammonia > phenylamine** [1]
**(b)** Each base accepts H⁺ using the lone pair on N [1]. The methyl group is electron-releasing and
increases electron density on N [1]. In phenylamine the N lone pair is delocalised into the benzene ring,
decreasing electron density on N [1]. So lone-pair availability, and the ability to accept H⁺, is greatest
in methylamine and least in phenylamine [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* "Benzene is electron-withdrawing" without saying the lone pair is delocalised into the
ring is too vague for the phenylamine mark.

**5. (a)** CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂NH₂ + H₂O ⇌ CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂NH₃⁺ + OH⁻ [1]; it is only partly protonated / the
equilibrium lies to the left [1].
**(b)** CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂NH₂ + HCl → CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂NH₃⁺Cl⁻ [1]; **butylammonium chloride** [1].
**(c)** Add a strong base such as sodium hydroxide [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* The equilibrium sign is needed in (a); a full arrow there contradicts "weak base".

**6. (a)** Arrow from the lone pair on N of propylamine to the δ+ carbon of C–Br [1]; arrow from the C–Br
bond to Br, Br⁻ leaves [1]; intermediate CH₃CH₂CH₂N⁺H₂CH₂CH₃ [1]; a second propylamine removes H⁺, with an
arrow from its lone pair to H and from the N–H bond to N⁺, giving CH₃CH₂CH₂NHCH₂CH₃ [1].
**(b)** **Nucleophilic substitution** [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* An arrow starting from the N atom rather than from its lone pair is a common way to lose
the first mark.

**7. (a)** CH₃NH₂, (CH₃)₂NH, (CH₃)₃N [1]; **(CH₃)₄N⁺Cl⁻** [1]
**(b)** In (CH₃)₄N⁺ the N has no lone pair [1], so it cannot act as a nucleophile and attack another
chloromethane [1].
**(c)** **CH₃(CH₂)₁₅N⁺(CH₃)₃ Br⁻** [1]; it has a positively charged ionic head that is attracted to water
[1] and a long hydrocarbon chain that is not [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* In (c) the word "cationic" needs the positive head to be named; a description of the
chain alone earns only one mark.

**8. (a)** CH₃CH₂COCl + 2CH₃CH₂NH₂ → CH₃CH₂CONHCH₂CH₃ [1] + CH₃CH₂NH₃⁺Cl⁻ [1]
**(b)** **N-ethylpropanamide** [1]
**(c)** Arrow from N lone pair to the δ+ carbonyl carbon [1]; arrow from C=O to O, giving O⁻ [1]; arrow from
O⁻ reforming C=O with the C–Cl bond breaking and Cl⁻ leaving [1]; Cl⁻ or a second ethylamine removes H⁺
from N⁺ [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* A one-step substitution with no O⁻ intermediate is not an addition–elimination
mechanism and loses the second and third marks.

**9. (a)** (CH₃CO)₂O + C₆H₅NH₂ → CH₃CONHC₆H₅ [1] + CH₃COOH [1]
**(b)** **N-phenylethanamide** [1]
**(c)** The by-product is ethanoic acid rather than HCl, or the reaction is slower [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Writing HCl as a by-product of an anhydride reaction is wrong chemistry and loses the
second mark in (a).

**10. (a)** n(HCl) = 0.0200 × 0.100 = 2.00 × 10⁻³ mol [1]; n(amine) in 250 cm³ = 2.00 × 10⁻³ × 10 =
0.0200 mol [1]; Mr = 1.18 ÷ 0.0200 = **59.0** [1]
**(b)** **C₃H₉N** (C₃H₇NH₂) [1]
**(c)** CH₃CH₂CH₂NH₂, propylamine (propan-1-amine) [1]; CH₃CH(NH₂)CH₃, 1-methylethylamine (propan-2-amine)
[1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Forgetting the ×10 scale-up from 25.0 to 250 cm³ loses one mark; the Mr can still earn
credit as follow-through.

**11. (a)** Step 1: KCN in aqueous ethanol, heat under reflux [1]; intermediate **hexanenitrile**,
CH₃(CH₂)₄CN [1]. Step 2: reduce with LiAlH₄ in dry ether, or H₂ with a Ni catalyst [1]; CH₃(CH₂)₄CN + 4[H]
→ CH₃(CH₂)₅NH₂ [1].
**(b)** 0.85 × 0.60 × 100 = **51%** [1]
**(c)** **Pentylamine**, CH₃(CH₂)₄NH₂ [1]; Mr of reactants C₅H₁₁Br + 2NH₃ = 150.9 + 34.0 = 184.9 [1];
atom economy = 87.0 ÷ 184.9 × 100 = **47.1%** [1]
**(d)** Pentylamine has a lone pair on N, so it reacts further with 1-bromopentane to give secondary and
tertiary amines and a quaternary ammonium salt [1]; the nitrile route gives only the primary amine [1].
**[10]**
*Examiner insight:* Naming the intermediate pentanenitrile in (a) is the usual slip; the C≡N carbon is the
sixth carbon in the chain.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Classifying amines by the carbon next to N instead of by the number of carbons on N.
- Using 2[H] for nitrile reduction or 4[H] for nitro reduction.
- Naming the nitrile without counting the C≡N carbon.
- Base-strength explanations with no mention of the lone pair or of accepting H⁺.
- Saying phenylamine's ring "withdraws electrons" without saying the N lone pair is delocalised into it.
- A full arrow instead of ⇌ for an amine reacting with water.
- Leaving out the final deprotonation step in either mechanism.
- Drawing addition–elimination as one step, with no O⁻ intermediate.
- Writing HCl as the by-product of an acid anhydride reaction.
- Forgetting to scale titration moles from the portion to the whole solution.
