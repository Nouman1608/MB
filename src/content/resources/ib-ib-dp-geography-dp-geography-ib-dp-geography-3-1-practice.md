## Section A

**1.** Define the term wet-bulb temperature. **[1]**

**2.** Outline why each breath contains less oxygen at high altitude. **[2]**

**3.** Using the approximation that air pressure falls by a factor of e for every 8,000 m, calculate the fraction of sea-level pressure at 4,000 m, to two decimal places. **[1]**

**4.** State two reasons why the resident population of a mining region may be much smaller than its workforce. **[2]**

**5.** Outline why Antarctica has no permanent inhabitants but a population that changes through the year. **[2]**

## Section B

**6.** The table gives invented climate data.

| | Station P | Station Q |
|---|---|---|
| Warmest month mean (degrees Celsius) | 12 | 31 |
| Coldest month mean (degrees Celsius) | -22 | 11 |
| Annual precipitation (mm) | 180 | 150 |
| Annual PET (mm) | 250 | 1,800 |

**(a)** Calculate the annual temperature range and the aridity index for each station. **[4]**

**(b)** Using the UNEP scale (hyper-arid below 0.05; arid 0.05 to 0.20; semi-arid 0.20 to 0.50; dry sub-humid 0.50 to 0.65), classify each station's aridity. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain why Station P is not classed as arid despite its low precipitation. **[2]**

**7.** Explain two ways in which seasonality affects access to cold environments. **[4]**

**8.** Explain why heat stress is more dangerous in humid conditions than in dry ones at the same air temperature. **[3]**

**9.** Describe the typical population distribution within a hot desert, and explain it. **[3]**

## Section C

**10.** Examine the factors that make some environments extreme for human habitation. **[10]**

**11.** "Inaccessibility is the main reason why extreme environments have low population densities." Discuss this statement. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** A temperature measure that combines air temperature and humidity, showing how well evaporation, including of sweat, can cool a surface [1]. **[1]**

**2.** The proportion of oxygen in the air stays about the same, but air pressure falls with height [1], so each breath of the same volume contains fewer oxygen molecules [1]. **[2]**

**3.** e to the power (-4,000 / 8,000) = e to the power (-0.5) = **0.61** [1]. **[1]**

**4.** Any two [1] each: fly-in fly-out workers live elsewhere and stay only for shifts; workers are housed in temporary camps; families do not move because of the lack of services; jobs are short-term contracts. **[2]**

**5.** Harsh cold, darkness and isolation, and the Antarctic Treaty's focus on science, mean only research staff live there [1]; numbers rise in the southern summer when access and fieldwork are possible and fall in winter, when stations run with small crews [1]. **[2]**

**6. (a)** Station P: range = 12 - (-22) = **34 degrees** [1]; aridity index = 180 / 250 = **0.72** [1]. Station Q: range = 31 - 11 = **20 degrees** [1]; aridity index = 150 / 1,800 = **0.083** [1].

**(b)** Station P: above 0.65, so **not dryland** (humid) on this scale [1]. Station Q: **arid** (0.05 to 0.20) [1].

**(c)** Cold temperatures mean potential evapotranspiration is very low [1], so the small precipitation total is still large compared with the atmosphere's demand for water, and the ground is not dry; its extremeness comes from cold rather than aridity [1]. **[8]**

**7.** Frozen ground and lakes allow winter ice roads [1], so heavy supplies can only be moved in a short winter window, which warmer winters shorten [1]. In summer, thawing of the active layer makes ground waterlogged [1], so overland travel becomes difficult and communities depend on air or sea transport [1]. (Accept: sea ice blocking or opening shipping routes; polar darkness limiting flights.) **[4]**

**8.** The body cools mainly through the evaporation of sweat [1]. In humid air, the air is already close to saturation, so sweat evaporates slowly [1], and the body cannot shed heat, so core temperature rises; in dry air, sweat evaporates freely, though dehydration becomes the main risk [1]. **[3]**

**9.** People are concentrated in small clusters, with large areas almost empty [1]. Settlements lie where water is available: oases fed by groundwater, river valleys such as the Nile, or wells [1], because water is the limiting factor for drinking, farming and livestock [1]. **[3]**

**10.** Indicative content:

- **Temperature:** very low temperatures cause hypothermia and frostbite and limit growing seasons; very high temperatures with humidity exceed the body's ability to cool [1] [1].
- **Water:** low and unreliable precipitation in deserts limits drinking water and farming [1].
- **Altitude:** falling air pressure reduces oxygen per breath, causing altitude sickness and limiting work [1].
- **Relief and terrain:** steep slopes, ice and dunes make building and transport difficult [1].
- **Soils and productivity:** thin, saline or frozen soils and low productivity limit food supply [1].
- **Inaccessibility:** distance, seasonality and weather raise costs [1].
- **Examination:** factors combine; Antarctica is both cold and dry, and high mountains combine cold with low oxygen [1]; technology and indigenous knowledge reduce the effect of some factors [1].
- **Conclusion:** extremeness is relative to people and technology, not fixed by the environment alone [1]. **[10]**

**11.** Indicative content:

- **For the statement:** distance and terrain raise the cost of supplies and services [1]; seasonal access limits year-round settlement [1]; isolation discourages families and service provision [1].
- **Against:** physical limits matter more in many places: lack of water in deserts, low oxygen at altitude and cold in polar regions [1] [1]; low productivity of soils and vegetation limits food [1]; some accessible extreme places, such as desert cities with desalinated water, have high densities, showing other factors can be overcome [1].
- **Interaction:** inaccessibility is partly a result of the physical conditions, such as ice and steep slopes [1].
- **Change over time:** technology and climate change alter accessibility [1].
- **Conclusion:** inaccessibility is important but secondary to physical limits on water, oxygen and warmth; the two reinforce each other [1]. **[10]**

*Examiner insight:* In "discuss" answers, use examples from more than one type of extreme environment. A point that holds for deserts may not hold for high mountains.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calculating an aridity index the wrong way up (PET divided by precipitation).
- Subtracting negative temperatures incorrectly.
- Describing desert population as "low" without describing the clustered pattern.
- Treating extremeness as fixed rather than relative to technology and people.
