These questions cover the bystander-factors strand of **3.2.2 Social influence** from Paper 2 of
OxfordAQA International GCSE Psychology (9218): presence of others, cost of helping, similarity to
victim and expertise.

---

## Section A — Short questions

**1.** Which **one** of the following is a dispositional factor affecting bystander intervention in
the specification?
A Presence of others
B Cost of helping
C Expertise
D Ambiguity of the situation **[1]**

**2.** Define the term *bystander effect*. **[1]**

**3.** Explain the difference between diffusion of responsibility and pluralistic ignorance. **[2]**

**4.** Identify **two** costs of helping a stranger who has collapsed in the street. **[2]**

**5.** Outline how similarity to the victim affects bystander intervention. **[2]**

**6.** Priya sees smoke coming from under a door in a busy office. She looks around and sees that her
colleagues are carrying on working, so she decides it is probably nothing. Identify the process
affecting Priya and explain your answer. **[3]**

## Section B — Longer questions

**7.** Outline the five stages of Latané and Darley's decision model of bystander intervention. **[5]**

**8.** Explain how the cost of helping affects bystander intervention. Refer to Piliavin's arousal:
cost-reward model in your answer. **[4]**

**9.** A man collapses on a crowded beach. A lifeguard and several holidaymakers are nearby. Use your
knowledge of expertise to explain why the lifeguard is more likely to help than the holidaymakers.
**[4]**

**10.** Explain why someone who needs help in a crowded place is advised to point at one person and
ask that person directly. **[2]**

**11.** Ana and her friend are travelling home after a football match. A man wearing the shirt of
Ana's team trips on the station steps and drops his bag. The station is very crowded. Use your
knowledge of social and dispositional factors to explain whether Ana is likely to help. **[6]**

**12.** Evaluate the claim that the presence of others always reduces bystander intervention. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** **C** [1].

**2.** The finding that people are less likely to help (or slower to help) in an emergency when other
people are present [1].

**3.** Diffusion of responsibility is when responsibility for helping is shared among bystanders, so
each feels less obliged to act [1]; pluralistic ignorance is when bystanders use each other's
inaction to judge the situation and wrongly decide it is not an emergency [1].

**4.** Any two, [1] each: risk of physical harm or infection; time or delay; effort; embarrassment if
help is not needed or is done badly; unpleasantness. Maximum 2.

**5.** People are more likely to help a victim they see as similar to themselves or in their own group
[1], because shared identity increases empathy and the cost of not helping someone who is "one of
us" [1].

**6.** **Pluralistic ignorance** [1]. Priya uses her colleagues' calm behaviour to interpret an
ambiguous situation [1], so she wrongly concludes there is no emergency, because each colleague may be
doing the same [1].

**7.** One mark per stage [5]: the bystander must **notice** the event [1]; **interpret** it as an
emergency [1]; **accept responsibility** for helping [1]; **know how** to help [1]; **decide** to help
and act [1].

**8.** One mark per point, up to 4:
- Seeing someone in distress produces unpleasant arousal, which the bystander is motivated to reduce
  [1].
- The bystander weighs the costs of helping (such as danger, effort, embarrassment) against the costs
  of not helping (such as guilt and blame) [1].
- When the cost of helping is low, direct help is likely [1].
- When the cost of helping is high, for example a victim who seems drunk, help is less likely or may
  be indirect, such as fetching someone else [1].
Maximum 4.

**9.** One mark per point, up to 4:
- The lifeguard is trained, so is more likely to **recognise** that the man's collapse is an emergency
  [1].
- The lifeguard's role and training mean they feel **responsibility** falls on them, reducing
  diffusion of responsibility [1].
- The lifeguard **knows how to help**, for example how to give first aid [1].
- This lowers the cost of helping for them (less risk of harm or embarrassment), whereas the
  holidaymakers may fear doing the wrong thing [1].
- The holidaymakers may step back because they see an expert is present [1].
Maximum 4.

**10.** Asking one named person removes **diffusion of responsibility**, because responsibility is
placed on that individual [1], and it removes ambiguity, so the person cannot assume it is not an
emergency (pluralistic ignorance) [1].

**11.** Award up to 6. Credit points that identify a factor, explain its mechanism and link it to the
scenario:
- **Presence of others (social):** the station is very crowded, so responsibility may be diffused and
  Ana may assume someone else will help [1] [1].
- **Similarity to victim (dispositional):** the man wears Ana's team's shirt, so she is likely to see
  him as part of her group, increasing empathy and the cost of not helping [1] [1].
- **Cost of helping (social):** the cost is low (picking up a bag, checking he is all right), which
  makes helping more likely [1].
- **Judgement:** for example, the low cost and similarity are likely to outweigh the crowd, so Ana is
  likely to help [1].
Maximum 6.

**12.** Award up to 6:
- Support: Latané and Darley's smoke study found that participants alone reported smoke far more
  often than those with others present [1], explained by pluralistic ignorance [1].
- Diffusion of responsibility explains why help becomes less likely as numbers increase [1].
- Challenge: Piliavin's subway study found that larger groups of passengers did not reduce helping [1],
  probably because the emergency was clear and everyone could see each other [1].
- Other factors, such as low cost, similarity to the victim and expertise, can override the effect of
  numbers [1].
- Conclusion: the presence of others often reduces intervention, especially in ambiguous
  emergencies, but not always [1].
Maximum 6.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calling expertise or similarity a social factor.
- Writing "there were lots of people" without naming diffusion of responsibility or pluralistic
  ignorance.
- Listing factors in a scenario question without referring to the people and details given.
- Evaluating with no study evidence, or describing studies without linking them to the claim.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need to name the decision model?**
The specification names the four factors, not the model, but the model is a clear way to explain how
each factor works.

**Which studies go with these factors?**
Latané and Darley's smoke study, Piliavin's subway study and Levine's football supporter study, all
named in 3.2.2.
