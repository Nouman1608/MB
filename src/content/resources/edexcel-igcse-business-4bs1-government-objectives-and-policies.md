This guide covers section 1.6, Government objectives and policies, in Topic 1 of the Pearson Edexcel International GCSE in Business (4BS1) specification, Issue 2, February 2026. The specification lists:

- 1.6.1 government spending: to provide public service; taxation and constraints on public spending
- 1.6.2 how governments can affect business activity: infrastructure provision; legislation; trade policy, membership of trading blocs, tariffs
- 1.6.3 the effect of interest rates on businesses and on consumer spending.

Questions rarely ask you to describe a policy for its own sake. They ask what a policy does to a named business: its costs, its revenue, its prices or its plans. Paper 1 businesses are small and often borrow from banks; Paper 2 businesses are large and often trade across borders, so tariffs and trading blocs feature there. The first overview, with section 1.7, is in the [government objectives and policies and external factors guide](/resources/edexcel-igcse-business-government-objectives-and-policies-and-external-factors/). Companion pages: the [government objectives and policies revision notes](/resources/edexcel-igcse-business-4bs1-government-objectives-and-policies-revision-notes/) and the [government objectives and policies practice questions](/resources/edexcel-igcse-business-4bs1-government-objectives-and-policies-practice/). Businesses and figures are invented; amounts are in dollars. The specification lists these points without defining them, so the definitions here are standard ones.

## 1. Government spending and the business that supplies it (1.6.1)

Governments spend to provide public services such as health care, schools, policing and roads. Many businesses are suppliers to that spending: builders, caterers, cleaning firms, software companies. When public spending rises or falls, their order books follow.

### Worked example 1: dependence on public contracts

Marlow Construction has revenue of 4.0 million, of which 2.6 million comes from building schools for the government.

- Share from public contracts: 2.6 ÷ 4.0 × 100 = **65%**.
- If school spending is cut and Marlow loses a third of that work: 2.6 × 1/3 = about 0.87 million lost, which is about 22% of total revenue.

A business this dependent should look for private customers to spread its risk. The answer is stronger for saying so.

## 2. Taxation and constraints on public spending (1.6.1)

Taxes pay for most public spending. In general terms, three kinds reach a business in different ways.

| Tax on | Effect on the business |
|---|---|
| Business profits | Less profit is left to retain for growth or pay to owners |
| Workers' incomes | Customers have less to spend, so demand may fall |
| Spending (sales taxes) | Prices to customers rise, so fewer units may be sold |

### Worked example 2: a tax on profit

Fenwick Ltd makes a profit of 300,000. A profit tax of 20% is charged.

- Tax: 20% × 300,000 = 60,000. Profit after tax: 240,000.
- The directors pay half as dividends, so 120,000 is retained.
- If the rate rose to 25%: tax 75,000, profit after tax 225,000, retained 112,500. Fenwick has 7,500 less each year to invest.

**Constraints on public spending.** A government cannot spend without limit. Tax revenue depends on how well the economy is doing; higher taxes are unpopular with voters and businesses; borrowing must be repaid with interest; and money spent on one service cannot be spent on another. When the economy weakens, tax receipts fall just as calls on public services rise, so governments often cut or delay projects. That is why a business relying on public contracts faces political risk.

## 3. Infrastructure provision (1.6.2)

Infrastructure is the roads, railways, ports, airports, energy, water and communications networks that businesses rely on. Better infrastructure cuts costs and widens markets.

### Worked example 3: a new road

Hillside Dairy makes 1,500 delivery trips a year. A new road cuts each trip by 40 minutes and 18 km. Driver time costs 15 an hour, and vehicle running costs are 0.60 per km.

- Time saved: 1,500 × 40 ÷ 60 = 1,000 hours, worth 1,000 × 15 = 15,000.
- Distance saved: 1,500 × 18 = 27,000 km, worth 27,000 × 0.60 = 16,200.
- Total yearly saving: **31,200**.

The dairy can also reach shops further away within the time its milk stays fresh, so the market grows as well as costs falling.

## 4. Legislation (1.6.2)

Laws protect consumers, employees and the environment, and set rules for competition. For a business the usual effects are compliance costs, changes to products or processes, and penalties for breaking the rules. Laws also give benefits: fair competition and customers who trust products that meet standards.

### Worked example 4: compliance cost per unit

A new labelling rule costs any producer 30,000 a year to meet, whatever its size.

| | Small producer | Large producer |
|---|---:|---:|
| Units a year | 10,000 | 1,000,000 |
| Cost per unit | 3.00 | 0.03 |

The same law is a much heavier burden on the small producer, which may have to raise prices or accept lower profit. Describe laws in general terms; the specification names no particular laws.

## 5. Trade policy: trading blocs and tariffs (1.6.2)

A **tariff** is a tax on imports. A **trading bloc** is a group of countries that trade with each other on easier terms, usually without tariffs between members.

Tariffs help some businesses and hurt others.

- **Domestic producers competing with imports** gain: imported rivals become more expensive.
- **Businesses that import materials or parts** lose: their costs rise.
- **Exporters** may lose if other countries respond with tariffs of their own.

Membership of a trading bloc gives a business tariff-free access to a larger market, but also exposes it to more competition from businesses in other member countries.

### Worked example 5: one tariff, two businesses

The government puts a 10% tariff on imported steel.

- Corran Steel, a domestic producer, sells at 600 a tonne. Imported steel was 580 a tonne and is now 638, so Corran becomes the cheaper supplier and may win orders.
- Ridley Gates imports 500 tonnes a year at 580. Its cost rises by 500 × 58 = **29,000**, unless it switches to Corran at 600, which costs 500 × 20 = **10,000** more than before.

## 6. Interest rates (1.6.3)

An interest rate is the cost of borrowing and the reward for saving.

**Effects on businesses.** Higher rates raise interest on loans and overdrafts, which reduces profit and cash. They also make investment less attractive: if a project is expected to return 7% a year and the business must borrow at 9%, it is unlikely to go ahead. Lower rates work the other way.

**Effects on consumer spending.** Higher rates make credit dearer, reward saving and leave borrowers with variable-rate debts less to spend.

### Worked example 6: a household mortgage

A household has a mortgage of 150,000. Interest rises from 4% to 6% a year.

- Interest at 4%: 6,000. At 6%: 9,000.
- The household has **3,000** a year less to spend on other things.

Businesses selling goods often bought on credit, such as cars, furniture and holidays, notice these changes most. Sellers of everyday food notice least.

## 7. Annotated model answers

**Analyse the effect of a rise in interest rates on a furniture retailer with a large overdraft** (Paper 1 style).
*Higher rates raise the interest on the overdraft, so costs rise and profit falls. At the same time, customers who buy furniture on credit find it dearer, so sales may fall. The two effects together squeeze cash flow, so the retailer may cut stock orders or delay plans to open a second shop.*

**Evaluate whether joining a trading bloc would benefit a large food manufacturer** (Paper 2 style). For: tariff-free access to more customers, and cheaper imported ingredients from members. Against: competitors from member countries can sell freely in its home market. Judgement: it benefits a manufacturer that is efficient enough to compete, especially one already exporting; a high-cost producer protected by tariffs could lose.

## Common mistakes

- Calling a tariff a tax on exports.
- Saying a tariff hurts every domestic business; producers competing with imports may gain.
- Describing a policy with no effect on costs, revenue, prices or demand.
- Assuming higher interest rates affect all businesses equally.
- Forgetting that government is also a customer of many businesses.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why do new laws often hurt small businesses more?**
Compliance costs are often similar for any size of business, so a small business spreads them over far fewer sales.

**How do interest rates affect consumer spending?**
Higher rates make borrowing dearer and saving more rewarding, so spending, especially on credit, tends to fall.
