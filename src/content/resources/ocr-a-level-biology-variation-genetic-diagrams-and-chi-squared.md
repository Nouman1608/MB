## Genetic and environmental variation, 6.1.2(a)

Phenotype, the observable characteristics of an organism, depends on genotype (the alleles it carries) and on the environment in which it develops. Most characteristics are affected by both.

**Genetic contribution.** Alleles are inherited, so differences in alleles cause differences in phenotype. New alleles arise by mutation. Sexual reproduction then shuffles existing alleles in three ways:

- **Crossing over** in prophase I of meiosis exchanges sections of non-sister chromatids within a bivalent, so chromatids carry new allele combinations.
- **Independent assortment**: each homologous pair lines up at the equator in metaphase I independently of the others, so maternal and paternal chromosomes are distributed randomly into the gametes.
- **Random fusion of gametes** at fertilisation: which sperm reaches which egg is a matter of chance.

**Worked example.** A garden pea has 2n = 14, so n = 7 pairs of homologous chromosomes. Ignoring crossing over, one plant makes 2⁷ = 128 different gametes, and two parents give 128 × 128 = 16 384 combinations at fertilisation.

**Environmental contribution.** Two examples are specified.

- *Diet in animals.* Genetically similar animals on a richer diet reach a greater body mass than those on a poor diet.
- *Etiolation in plants.* A seedling grown in the dark has a long, thin, pale stem, small unexpanded leaves and a yellow colour, because chlorophyll formation needs light. The extra stem growth carries the shoot tip towards light. Cuttings from one plant are genetically identical, so differences between light and dark grown cuttings are environmental.

Genes and environment can interact. In Himalayan rabbits the allele codes for an enzyme that works only at lower temperatures, so dark pigment forms only on the cooler ears, paws, nose and tail.

To separate the causes, compare genetically identical individuals (clones, identical twins) in different environments.

## Genetic diagrams, 6.1.2(b)(i)

Terms: an **allele** is a version of a gene; a **locus** is a gene's fixed place on a chromosome. **Homozygous** means two identical alleles, **heterozygous** two different alleles. A **dominant** allele is expressed in the heterozygote; a **recessive** allele only when no dominant allele is present. **Codominant** alleles are both expressed in the heterozygote. A **carrier** has one copy of a recessive allele and does not show the condition.

A full genetic diagram gives: a key defining the symbols, parental phenotypes, parental genotypes, gametes (circled or listed), offspring genotypes (a table is clearest), offspring phenotypes and the ratio.

### Monogenic inheritance

A monogenic cross follows one gene. In mice, allele C gives pigmented fur and c gives albino. Two pigmented mice have an albino pup, so both parents must be Cc.

| | C | c |
|---|---|---|
| **C** | CC | Cc |
| **c** | Cc | cc |

Phenotypic ratio 3 pigmented : 1 albino. A **test cross** mates an unknown-genotype individual showing the dominant phenotype with a homozygous recessive partner. Recessive offspring show the unknown parent was heterozygous (ratio 1 : 1); all dominant offspring suggest homozygous.

### Codominance

In some cattle, red coat (Cᴿ) and white coat (Cᵂ) are codominant, and the heterozygote is roan with both red and white hairs. Roan × roan gives CᴿCᴿ : CᴿCᵂ : CᵂCᵂ in the ratio 1 : 2 : 1, which is 1 red : 2 roan : 1 white. Genotype ratio and phenotype ratio match, a clue to codominance.

### Multiple alleles

Multiple alleles means three or more versions of one gene exist in a population; each diploid individual still carries just two. In rabbit coat colour, take four alleles in the dominance order Cᶠ (full colour) > Cᶜ (chinchilla) > Cʰ (Himalayan) > Cᵃ (albino). These labels are for this example only.

Cross a full-colour rabbit CᶠCʰ with a chinchilla rabbit CᶜCᵃ. Gametes: Cᶠ or Cʰ; Cᶜ or Cᵃ. Offspring: CᶠCᶜ, CᶠCᵃ (both full colour), CᶜCʰ (chinchilla), CʰCᵃ (Himalayan). Ratio 2 full colour : 1 chinchilla : 1 Himalayan.

### Dihybrid inheritance

A dihybrid cross tracks two genes on separate chromosomes that assort independently. In maize, purple kernels (P) are dominant to yellow (p), and smooth (S) is dominant to wrinkled (s). Cross two PpSs plants. Each makes four gametes in equal numbers: PS, Ps, pS, ps. The 16 boxes give:

| Phenotype | Genotypes | Boxes |
|---|---|---|
| purple smooth | P_S_ | 9 |
| purple wrinkled | P_ss | 3 |
| yellow smooth | ppS_ | 3 |
| yellow wrinkled | ppss | 1 |

From 640 kernels you would expect 360 : 120 : 120 : 40. Using probability, purple wrinkled is (3/4) × (1/4), which is 3/16. Test crossing PpSs with ppss gives purple smooth : purple wrinkled : yellow smooth : yellow wrinkled in the ratio 1 : 1 : 1 : 1 when the genes assort independently.

### Sex linkage

A sex-linked gene lies on the X chromosome, where the Y has no matching locus. A male (XY) has one allele, so a recessive allele is always expressed. Red-green colour blindness is X-linked recessive: Xᴺ is normal vision, Xⁿ is colour blindness.

A carrier woman (XᴺXⁿ) has children with a man with normal vision (XᴺY).

| | Xᴺ | Xⁿ |
|---|---|---|
| **Xᴺ** | XᴺXᴺ | XᴺXⁿ |
| **Y** | XᴺY | XⁿY |

Daughters all have normal vision (half are carriers). Half the sons are colour-blind. The probability that a child is colour-blind is 1/4, but for a son it is 1/2. A colour-blind father passes Xⁿ to every daughter and never to a son.

## Linkage and epistasis from ratios, 6.1.2(b)(ii)

An unexpected ratio is information. The method is to compare the observed ratio with the expected ratio, then explain the difference.

**Autosomal linkage.** Two genes on one autosome are usually inherited as a unit. Alleles on one chromosome are written together, for example PL/pl. In a dihybrid test cross, independent assortment gives 1 : 1 : 1 : 1. With linkage, the two parental phenotype classes are much commoner than the two recombinant classes. Recombinants arise only when crossing over happens between the two loci.

**Worked example.** A sweet pea plant PL/pl (purple flowers and long pollen both dominant) is test-crossed with ppll. Of 480 offspring: 214 purple long, 22 purple round, 24 red long, 220 red round. The parental classes (purple long, red round) total 434; recombinants total 46, which is 46/480 = 9.6 per cent of offspring. Expected under independent assortment is 120 in each class, giving χ² = 313.8 (see below), so the genes are linked.

**Sex linkage.** Suspect it when the ratio differs between males and females, or when reciprocal crosses differ. For an X-linked recessive allele, an affected male × homozygous normal female gives no affected children, while an affected female × normal male gives affected sons and carrier daughters.

**Epistasis.** The allele at one locus masks or modifies the expression of alleles at a second locus. Two heterozygous parents give a modified version of 9 : 3 : 3 : 1, with 16 as the total.

**Worked example.** In Labrador retrievers, B (black) is dominant to b (brown), and gene E/e controls whether pigment is deposited in the coat: ee dogs are yellow whatever their B/b alleles. BbEe × BbEe gives 9 B_E_ (black), 3 bbE_ (brown), and 3 B_ee plus 1 bbee (4 yellow). The ratio is 9 black : 3 brown : 4 yellow, with the last two classes merged because ee masks B/b. This is recessive epistasis.

| Ratio from two heterozygotes | Interpretation |
|---|---|
| 9 : 3 : 3 : 1 | two genes, independent assortment, no interaction |
| 9 : 3 : 4 | recessive epistasis, as above |
| 12 : 3 : 1 | dominant epistasis: one dominant allele hides the other gene |
| 9 : 7 | both dominant alleles needed for the phenotype |
| 15 : 1 | either dominant allele is enough for the phenotype |

## The chi-squared test, 6.1.2(c)

Observed counts rarely match the expected ratio exactly, because fertilisation is random. The chi-squared test judges whether the difference between observed and expected results is significant. The formula will be provided in assessments:

χ² = Σ (fo − fe)² / fe

where fo is the observed frequency and fe the expected frequency in each category.

1. State the **null hypothesis**: there is no significant difference between the observed and expected results.
2. Work out expected values: share the total number of offspring in the expected ratio.
3. Calculate (fo − fe)² / fe for each category and add them.
4. Degrees of freedom = number of categories − 1.
5. Read the critical value at p = 0.05 for those degrees of freedom: 3.84 for 1, 5.99 for 2, 7.81 for 3.
6. A χ² below the critical value means no significant difference: chance may explain it, so do not reject the null hypothesis. If χ² is equal to or greater than it, the difference is significant, so reject the null hypothesis and look for a cause, such as linkage or epistasis.

Use raw counts, not percentages or proportions, and only for categories (discontinuous data).

**Worked example.** Maize, PpSs × PpSs, expected 9 : 3 : 3 : 1. Observed from 560 kernels: 322, 118, 86, 34.

| Class | fo | fe | (fo − fe)² / fe |
|---|---|---|---|
| purple smooth | 322 | 315 | 49/315 = 0.156 |
| purple wrinkled | 118 | 105 | 169/105 = 1.610 |
| yellow smooth | 86 | 105 | 361/105 = 3.438 |
| yellow wrinkled | 34 | 35 | 1/35 = 0.029 |

χ² = 5.23. With 3 degrees of freedom the critical value is 7.81. As 5.23 < 7.81, the difference is not significant, so the results are consistent with 9 : 3 : 3 : 1.

**Worked example (epistasis).** Labradors, expected 9 : 3 : 4 from 280 puppies. Observed 150 black, 62 brown, 68 yellow. Expected 157.5, 52.5, 70. χ² = 0.357 + 1.719 + 0.057 = 2.13. With 2 degrees of freedom (critical 5.99) the difference is not significant, so the data fit recessive epistasis.

## Continuous and discontinuous variation, 6.1.2(d)

| | Discontinuous | Continuous |
|---|---|---|
| Phenotypes | distinct categories, no intermediates | a range, with all values between the extremes |
| Genes | one gene, or a small number of genes | many genes, each with a small additive effect (polygenic) |
| Environment | little or no effect | often a large effect |
| Graph | bar chart | histogram; often a bell-shaped (normal) curve |
| Examples | ABO blood group, pea seed shape | height, body mass, leaf length |

**Why many genes give a smooth curve.** Suppose two genes each have an allele that adds one unit to a trait (A, B) and an allele that adds nothing (a, b). Cross AaBb × AaBb: count the capital letters in each of the 16 offspring. The numbers with 0, 1, 2, 3 and 4 capital letters are in the ratio 1 : 4 : 6 : 4 : 1, giving five classes with most offspring in the middle. With three such genes there are seven classes (1 : 6 : 15 : 20 : 15 : 6 : 1). In general, n genes give 2n + 1 classes. With many genes, and environmental effects, the steps blur into a bell-shaped curve.

## Common errors

- Writing gametes with two alleles of the same gene. Each gamete has one.
- Treating Xⁿ as inherited from the father by a son.
- Using percentages in chi-squared, or forgetting to subtract 1 for degrees of freedom.
- Claiming that a low χ² proves the expected ratio.
