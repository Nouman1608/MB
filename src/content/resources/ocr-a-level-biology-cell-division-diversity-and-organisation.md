Section 2.1.6 of OCR A Level Biology A (H420) explains how cells divide and how they become different. You need the
cell cycle and its checkpoints, the stages of mitosis and meiosis and why each matters in life cycles, how eight
named cell types are specialised, how cells are organised into tissues, organs and organ systems, and how stem cells
differentiate and could be used in research and medicine. Practical work (PAG1) includes examining stained root tip
squashes and drawing the stages you see.

## What happens in the cell cycle? (a)

| Phase | What happens |
|---|---|
| G1 (first growth phase) | The cell grows, makes proteins and organelles, and carries out its normal functions |
| S (synthesis) | DNA replicates by semi-conservative replication, so each chromosome becomes two sister chromatids |
| G2 (second growth phase) | The cell grows further, makes the proteins needed for division, and DNA is checked |
| Mitosis | The nucleus divides, giving two genetically identical nuclei |
| Cytokinesis | The cytoplasm divides, forming two genetically identical daughter cells |

G1, S and G2 together make up **interphase**, which is usually the longest part of the cycle.

## How is the cell cycle regulated? (b)

**Checkpoints** are control points where the cell checks that conditions are right before it moves on.

- **G1 checkpoint** (end of G1): checks cell size, nutrients, growth factors and DNA damage before DNA replication.
  Cells that do not pass may enter a resting state (G0).
- **G2 checkpoint** (end of G2): checks that DNA has replicated correctly and is undamaged before mitosis.
- **Spindle assembly checkpoint** (in metaphase): checks that every chromosome is attached to spindle fibres
  before anaphase begins.

If damage cannot be repaired, the cell may be destroyed. Failure of these controls can lead to uncontrolled
division and tumours.

## What are the stages of mitosis? (c)

| Stage | Key events |
|---|---|
| Prophase | Chromosomes condense and become visible as two sister chromatids joined at a **centromere**; the nuclear envelope breaks down; in animal cells **centrioles** move to opposite poles and **spindle fibres** form |
| Metaphase | Chromosomes line up on the equator (metaphase plate); spindle fibres attach to the centromeres |
| Anaphase | Centromeres divide; spindle fibres shorten, pulling sister chromatids to opposite poles |
| Telophase | Chromatids (now chromosomes) reach the poles and uncoil; a nuclear envelope reforms around each set |
| Cytokinesis | In animal cells the **cell membrane** is pulled inwards (a cleavage furrow) until the cell divides; in plant cells a cell plate forms, which becomes the new cell wall |

### How do you examine root tip squashes? (d) and PAG1

Root tips (for example garlic) contain meristem tissue that is dividing. Soak the tip in warm hydrochloric acid to
separate the cells, stain (for example with toluidine blue), squash gently under a cover slip so cells form a single
layer, and view at low then high power. Draw labelled diagrams of the stages you see with sharp, clear lines and no
shading.

**Worked example 1.** A student counts 160 cells, of which 24 are in a stage of mitosis. Mitotic index = 24 / 160 =
**0.15**. If the cycle takes 20 hours, mitosis lasts about 0.15 x 20 = **3 hours**.

## Why do organisms need mitosis and meiosis? (e) and (f)

**Mitosis** produces genetically identical cells, which is needed for:

- **growth** of multicellular organisms;
- **tissue repair**, replacing damaged or worn-out cells (for example skin cells);
- **asexual reproduction** in plants (for example runners), animals (for example budding in Hydra) and fungi (for
  example yeast budding).

**Meiosis** produces **haploid** cells (with one set of chromosomes), so that when two gametes fuse at fertilisation
the diploid number is restored. It also produces **genetic variation** by:

- **crossing over** in prophase I, when non-sister chromatids of homologous chromosomes exchange sections of DNA;
- **independent assortment** in metaphase I (and of chromatids in metaphase II), when homologous pairs line up
  randomly, so many combinations of maternal and paternal chromosomes are possible.

## What are the stages of meiosis? (g)

Meiosis follows interphase and involves two divisions. **Homologous chromosomes** are pairs of chromosomes, one from
each parent, with the same genes at the same loci.

| Stage | Key events |
|---|---|
| Prophase I | Chromosomes condense; homologous chromosomes pair up (forming bivalents); crossing over occurs; nuclear envelope breaks down; spindle forms |
| Metaphase I | Homologous pairs line up on the equator; independent assortment |
| Anaphase I | Homologous chromosomes are pulled to opposite poles; chromatids stay joined |
| Telophase I | Nuclear envelopes may reform; cytokinesis gives two haploid cells |
| Prophase II | Chromosomes condense again; new spindles form |
| Metaphase II | Chromosomes line up individually on the equator; chromatids assort independently |
| Anaphase II | Centromeres divide; sister chromatids are pulled to opposite poles |
| Telophase II | Nuclear envelopes reform; cytokinesis gives four genetically different haploid cells |

OCR does not require the names of the stages within prophase I.

## How are cells specialised? (h)

| Cell | Specialisation |
|---|---|
| Erythrocyte (red blood cell) | Biconcave disc gives a large surface area for diffusion of oxygen; no nucleus, leaving more room for haemoglobin; flexible, to squeeze through capillaries |
| Neutrophil | Multi-lobed nucleus helps it squeeze through gaps to reach infection; many lysosomes containing enzymes to digest pathogens engulfed by phagocytosis |
| Squamous epithelial cell | Very thin and flat, giving a short diffusion distance (for example lining the alveoli) |
| Ciliated epithelial cell | Cilia on the surface beat to move mucus (for example in the trachea); many mitochondria to supply ATP |
| Sperm cell | Flagellum for swimming; many mitochondria for ATP; acrosome with digestive enzymes to penetrate the egg; haploid nucleus |
| Palisade cell | Many chloroplasts to absorb light for photosynthesis; tall, closely packed shape |
| Root hair cell | Long extension gives a large surface area for uptake of water and mineral ions; carrier proteins for active transport |
| Guard cell | Pair surrounds a stoma; when turgid they bend and open the stoma, because their inner walls are thicker and less flexible than their outer walls |

## How are cells organised into tissues, organs and organ systems? (i)

A **tissue** is a group of similar cells (and often the material they secrete) working together for a function. An
**organ** is a group of tissues working together; an **organ system** is a group of organs.

| Tissue | Description |
|---|---|
| Squamous epithelium | A single layer of flat cells on a basement membrane; lines alveoli |
| Ciliated epithelium | Ciliated cells with goblet cells that secrete mucus; lines the trachea and bronchi |
| Cartilage | Cells called chondrocytes in a firm matrix containing collagen and elastin fibres; for example the C-shaped rings in the trachea |
| Muscle | Cells that contract to move the body or organs |
| Xylem | Dead, hollow vessels strengthened with lignin, carrying water and mineral ions |
| Phloem | Sieve tube elements and companion cells, carrying assimilates such as sucrose |

Example: the lungs are an organ made of epithelium, cartilage, muscle and blood; they form part of the gas exchange
system.

## What are stem cells and how do they differentiate? (j) to (l)

**Stem cells** are undifferentiated cells that can divide repeatedly by mitosis (they are a **renewing source**) and
can differentiate into specialised cells. Their **potency** describes how many cell types they can become:
totipotent (any cell type, including placental cells), pluripotent (any body cell type), multipotent (a limited
range) and unipotent (one type).

- **(k) Bone marrow.** Multipotent stem cells in bone marrow produce both **erythrocytes** and **neutrophils**.
  As erythrocytes develop they lose their nucleus and most organelles and fill with haemoglobin; neutrophils develop
  a lobed nucleus and many lysosomes. Two distinct cell types come from a common stem cell.
- **(l) Meristems.** Meristem cells in plants (including the vascular cambium) divide and differentiate into **xylem
  vessels**, which lay down lignin, lose their contents and die to form hollow tubes, and **phloem sieve tubes**,
  which lose most organelles and develop sieve plates, supported by companion cells.

## How could stem cells be used? (m)

- **Repairing damaged tissues**, for example growing new cells to replace tissue damaged by injury or disease.
- **Treating neurological conditions**, by replacing nerve cells lost in degenerative conditions. OCR does not
  require you to recall specific conditions.
- **Research into developmental biology**, studying how cells differentiate and how organisms develop, which can
  explain developmental disorders.

Discussion points include the source of the cells (embryonic stem cells raise ethical concerns because the embryo
is destroyed), the risk of rejection, the risk that cells divide uncontrollably, and the cost of treatment.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the difference between mitosis and meiosis?**
Mitosis is one division producing two genetically identical diploid cells. Meiosis is two divisions producing four
genetically different haploid cells.

**What happens at a cell cycle checkpoint?**
The cell checks for conditions such as correct size, undamaged DNA, or spindle attachment. It continues only if the
checks are passed, otherwise it pauses, repairs damage or is destroyed.

**Why do erythrocytes have no nucleus?**
Losing the nucleus leaves more space for haemoglobin, so each cell carries more oxygen.

**What does "stem cells are a renewing source" mean?**
They can keep dividing to make more stem cells, as well as differentiating into specialised cells, so the supply is
not used up.

## Common mistakes

- Saying DNA replicates in G1 or G2; it replicates in the **S phase**.
- Confusing anaphase I (homologous chromosomes separate) with anaphase II (sister chromatids separate).
- Saying crossing over happens between sister chromatids; it is between **non-sister** chromatids of homologous
  chromosomes.
- Describing a tissue as "a group of organs".
- Saying xylem vessels are living cells.
- Leaving out a link between a cell's feature and its function.
