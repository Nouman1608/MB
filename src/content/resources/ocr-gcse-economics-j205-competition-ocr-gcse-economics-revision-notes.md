These notes condense section 2.5, Competition, of the OCR GCSE (9-1) Economics specification (J205), Version 2.0, for first assessment in 2019 (component J205/01). The specification is not tiered. The worked price-cutting example and the concentration-ratio table are in the [2.5 study guide](/resources/ocr-gcse-economics-j205-competition-ocr-gcse-economics/); then try the [2.5 practice questions](/resources/ocr-gcse-economics-j205-competition-ocr-gcse-economics-practice/). All figures are invented.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Competition | Rivalry between producers for the same customers |
| Market economy | An economy where most resources are allocated by markets and private firms |
| Price competition | Winning customers by lower prices or discounts |
| Non-price competition | Winning customers through quality, branding, service, design or location |
| Market share | A firm's sales as a percentage of total market sales |
| Barrier to entry | Anything that makes it hard for a new firm to enter a market |
| Monopoly | One producer, or one dominant producer facing little effective competition |
| Oligopoly | A market dominated by a few large firms |
| Concentration ratio | The combined market share of the largest few firms |
| Interdependence | Each firm's decisions affect, and depend on, its rivals' decisions |

## Why producers compete

- Profit, market share, survival, growth.
- In a market economy customers can choose, so poor value loses sales.

## How competition affects price

```
Undercutting:  one firm cuts price → customers switch
               → rivals match to protect sales → market price falls

Entry:         profits attract new firms → supply shifts right
               → surplus at old price → price falls

Long run:      pressure to cut costs → new methods → lower prices sustained
```

**Worked reminder.** Two cafés each sell 300 coffees a day at £3.00 with a cost of £1.80. Both cut to £2.70 and still sell 300 each. Consumers save 0.30 × 600 = **£180 a day**; each café's daily profit falls from 1.20 × 300 = £360 to 0.90 × 300 = **£270**.

## Price versus non-price competition

| Price | Non-price |
|---|---|
| Discounts, offers, lower everyday prices | Quality, branding, advertising, service, design, location, delivery |
| Effective when products are similar | Effective when products can be differentiated |
| Risk: price wars cut everyone's profit | Aim: loyalty, so demand becomes less price elastic |

## Barriers to entry

- Patents and licences.
- Very high set-up costs.
- Control of a key resource.
- Brand loyalty and advertising budgets.
- Economies of scale of existing firms.

## Market structures compared

| | Competitive | Oligopoly | Monopoly |
|---|---|---|---|
| Firms | Many | Few dominate | One |
| Price control | Little | Some, watching rivals | Most |
| Entry | Easy | Hard | Very hard |
| Competition | Mainly price | Mainly non-price | Little |

**Concentration ratio method:** add the largest firms' sales, divide by total market sales, multiply by 100.

**Worked reminder.** Top four firms sell £45m, £30m, £15m and £10m in a £125m market. Four-firm ratio = 100 ÷ 125 × 100 = **80%**.

## Impact of competition

| | Benefits | Costs |
|---|---|---|
| Consumers | Lower prices, choice, quality, innovation | Cost-cutting may lower quality; prices may rise if rivals are driven out |
| Producers | Pressure to be efficient; best firms grow | Lower margins, less to invest, weaker firms close |

**Judgement depends on:** ease of switching; whether costs can fall without quality falling; how many firms survive; height of barriers to entry.

## Monopoly: two sides

- Against: higher prices, less choice, little pressure to improve.
- For: possible economies of scale; profits can fund research and investment.

## Evaluation chain to learn

Competition lowers prices and widens choice → consumers gain, especially where switching is easy → but producers' margins shrink and weaker firms leave → if too few firms remain, the market can become an oligopoly → prices may then rise again. Judgement: competition benefits consumers most when barriers to entry stay low, because the threat of new rivals keeps surviving firms' prices down.

## Exam traps

- Calling a few-firm market a monopoly.
- Competition answers that mention only price cuts.
- Ignoring producers in an "impact on producers and consumers" question.
- Treating monopoly as always bad.
- Concentration ratios that divide by the wrong total.

## Quick self-test

1. Give three reasons why producers compete.
2. Give two methods of non-price competition.
3. Explain how a price cut by one firm can lower the market price.
4. State two barriers to entry.
5. Define oligopoly.
6. The top three firms sell £60m, £50m and £40m in a £200m market. Calculate the three-firm concentration ratio.
7. Explain what interdependence means in an oligopoly.
8. Give one way monopoly can harm consumers.
9. Give one way a monopoly could benefit consumers.
10. Give one cost of competition for producers.
11. State one factor that decides how much consumers gain from competition.

### Answers

1. Profit, market share, survival, growth (any three).
2. Advertising, branding, quality, customer service, design, location, delivery speed (any two).
3. Customers switch to the cheaper firm, so rivals cut their prices too to keep sales, and the market price falls.
4. Patents, high set-up costs, control of a key resource, brand loyalty, economies of scale (any two).
5. A market dominated by a few large firms.
6. (60 + 50 + 40) ÷ 200 × 100 = **75%**.
7. Each firm's decisions, such as on price, affect its rivals, so each must consider how rivals will react.
8. Higher prices, less choice or lower quality, because customers cannot switch.
9. Lower average costs from economies of scale, or profits used for research into new products.
10. Lower profit margins, less money for investment, or closure of weaker firms.
11. How easily customers can compare and switch between firms (or how many firms remain).
