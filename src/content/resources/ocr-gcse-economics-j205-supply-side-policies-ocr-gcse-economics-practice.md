These questions cover section 3.7 of the OCR GCSE (9-1) Economics specification (J205, Version 2.0, June 2026), Supply side policies. All countries, regions and figures are invented. Revise first with the [supply side policies study guide](/resources/ocr-gcse-economics-j205-supply-side-policies-ocr-gcse-economics/) and the [supply side policies revision notes](/resources/ocr-gcse-economics-j205-supply-side-policies-ocr-gcse-economics-revision-notes/).

## Section A: short-answer questions

**1.** State what is meant by supply side policy. **[1]**

**2.** Give two examples of supply side policies. **[2]**

**3.** Explain how improving a country's infrastructure can increase its productive capacity. **[2]**

**4.** Which one of the following is a supply side policy? **[1]**

- A. A cut in interest rates
- B. An increase in unemployment benefits
- C. Funding for adult retraining courses
- D. A tax on pollution

**5.** Explain why supply side policies may take a long time to have an effect. **[2]**

**6.** A government spends £1.8bn on apprenticeships. As a result, 60,000 people each add £7,500 a year to output. Calculate the extra output per year and how many years of extra output it takes to equal the cost. **[2]**

**7.** Explain one way in which a cut in income tax may increase the supply of labour. **[2]**

## Section B: structured and extended questions

**8.** In the region of Eastmoor, unemployment has reached 11% since its shipyards closed. Former shipyard workers lack the skills needed by the region's growing engineering and care sectors. The government is choosing between spending £900m on retraining and help to move for work in Eastmoor, or a £900m national cut in income tax.

**(a)** Identify the main type of unemployment in Eastmoor. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain why the retraining option is better targeted at this problem. **[2]**
**(c)** Retraining helps 30,000 people into jobs paying £27,000 a year. Each pays 20% of earnings in tax and no longer receives £6,000 a year in benefits. Calculate the yearly gain to the government's finances. **[2]**
**(d)** Calculate how many years it takes for this gain to cover the £900m cost, to one decimal place. **[1]**

**9.** Country G cuts corporation tax from 28% to 22%. Company profits are £150bn.

**(a)** Calculate tax revenue before and after the cut, assuming profits do not change. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the profits needed for revenue to remain at its original level, to the nearest £bn. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain why the government might cut the tax even though revenue may fall. **[2]**

**10.** Analyse how supply side policies can help a government achieve price stability. **[3]**

**11.** Evaluate the costs and benefits of government spending on infrastructure as a supply side policy. **[8]**

**12.** Evaluate whether supply side policies are more effective than expansionary fiscal policy in reducing unemployment. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** Government measures intended to increase the productive capacity of the economy [1]. **[1]**

**2.** Any two: education and training; apprenticeships; infrastructure spending; grants or tax relief for research; cuts in income or corporation tax; reducing unnecessary regulation; childcare support (one mark each) [1] [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Better roads, rail or broadband reduce firms' transport and communication costs and journey times [1]. Firms can produce and deliver more with the same resources, so the maximum output of the economy rises [1]. **[2]**

**4.** **C** [1]. Retraining improves the quality of labour. A is monetary policy, B supports incomes, and D corrects an externality. **[1]**

**5.** Policies such as education and infrastructure take years to plan, build or complete [1], and their benefits, such as more productive workers, arrive only once the training or project is finished [1]. **[2]**

**6.** Extra output = 60,000 × £7,500 = **£450m** a year [1]. Years = 1,800 ÷ 450 = **4 years** [1]. **[2]**

**7.** A lower income tax rate raises take-home pay from each hour worked [1], so working, or working more hours, becomes more rewarding and some people who were inactive may take jobs [1]. **[2]**

**8. (a)** Structural unemployment [1].
**(b)** The problem is a mismatch between workers' skills and the jobs available [1]. Retraining gives workers the skills engineering and care firms want, whereas a national tax cut raises demand everywhere but does not fix the skills gap in Eastmoor [1].
**(c)** Tax = 30,000 × £27,000 × 0.2 = £162m; benefits saved = 30,000 × £6,000 = £180m [1]. Total = **£342m** a year [1].
**(d)** 900 ÷ 342 = **2.6 years** [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Part (b) needs the link to the case study: the skills mismatch in Eastmoor. A general point about training earns less.

**9. (a)** Before: 28% × £150bn = **£42bn** [1]. After: 22% × £150bn = **£33bn** [1].
**(b)** 42 ÷ 0.22 = **£191bn** [1].
**(c)** Lower tax on profits leaves firms more to reinvest and makes the country more attractive to foreign firms [1], so investment, output and jobs may rise over time, and higher profits and employment would recover some of the lost revenue [1]. **[5]**

**10.** Policies such as training and investment in technology raise productivity [1], so firms' cost per unit falls [1]. Lower costs and higher capacity mean output can rise to meet demand without firms raising prices, easing cost-push and demand-pull pressure [1]. **[3]**

**11.** Benefits: lower transport and communication costs make firms more competitive and able to expand [1]. Better connections can attract firms to poorer regions, reducing regional unemployment [1]. Building creates jobs in construction in the short run [1]. The gain in capacity lasts for decades [1]. Costs: projects are very expensive, with an opportunity cost in health, education or tax cuts [1]. They take years to plan and build, and often run over budget [1]. Construction can damage the environment and local communities [1]. Judgement: infrastructure is worthwhile when it removes a clear bottleneck that raises firms' costs, but the case is weaker for very expensive projects with uncertain demand, where the opportunity cost may be greater than the benefit [1]. **[8]**

**12.** Supply side policy: training and help to move tackle structural unemployment directly, by matching workers' skills and location to available jobs [1], and the effect lasts because capacity has risen [1]. However, it is slow and may not help when unemployment is caused by a recession [1]. Fiscal policy: tax cuts or extra spending raise demand quickly, so firms hire more and cyclical unemployment falls [1], but it can raise inflation and the deficit, and does not fix skill mismatches [1]. Judgement: supply side policy is more effective for long-term structural unemployment, while fiscal policy is more effective for cyclical unemployment, so the best choice depends on the type of unemployment the economy faces [1]. **[6]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing supply side policy as a short-term boost to demand.
- Forgetting to name an opportunity cost.
- Ignoring the type of unemployment when choosing a policy.
- Generic points with no link to the case study.
- Evaluating without a final judgement.
