A limited company has a legal identity separate from its owners, the shareholders. Directors run the company, and shareholders are not the same people. This separation of ownership from control is why published accounts follow strict standards and are checked by an independent auditor.

## 2.1.1 The three statements

### Statement of comprehensive income

This statement shows revenue, profit for the year and **other comprehensive income** (gains and losses recognised directly in equity, such as a revaluation gain on property). **Total comprehensive income** is the sum of the two. A typical layout in function format is:

Revenue, cost of sales, gross profit, distribution costs, administrative expenses, profit from operations, finance costs, profit before tax, income tax, profit for the year, other comprehensive income, total comprehensive income.

### Statement of changes in equity

This reconciles the opening and closing balance of every component of equity: share capital, share premium, revaluation reserve and retained earnings. It shows total comprehensive income, dividends paid and any new share issues.

### Statement of financial position

This lists non-current assets, current assets, equity, non-current liabilities and current liabilities. Equity is the shareholders' stake: share capital plus reserves.

## 2.1.2 The auditor and the reports

### The role of the auditor

The auditor is an independent professional who examines the financial statements and the records behind them. Directors are responsible for preparing the accounts. The auditor's job is to give an **opinion** on whether they give a **true and fair view** and have been prepared in accordance with the relevant standards and law. The auditor does not prepare the accounts and does not guarantee that there is no fraud or error.

### The Auditors' Report

Typical content: the **opinion** on the financial statements; the basis for the opinion (the work done); the responsibilities of directors and auditors; and any matters that the auditor wants to bring to attention. The opinion may be:

- **Unmodified (clean):** the statements give a true and fair view.
- **Qualified:** the statements are fairly presented except for a specific matter.
- **Adverse:** the statements are materially misstated.
- **Disclaimer:** the auditor could not obtain enough evidence to form an opinion.

Its importance: shareholders, lenders and others who do not run the company can rely on the figures with greater confidence. A qualified report is a warning sign.

### The Directors' Report

Prepared by the directors, it gives information not found in the financial statements. Typical content: a review of the business and its performance; principal risks and uncertainties; proposed dividends; the names of directors; important events after the year end; employment policies; and, where relevant, research and development. It helps shareholders understand results and see how the directors are managing the company. The auditor checks that it is consistent with the accounts.

### Auditors and corporate governance

**Corporate governance** is the system by which a company is directed and controlled. Because directors act for shareholders, there is a risk that they will favour themselves or present performance too favourably. The auditor supports good governance by providing an independent check on the directors' accounts, by reporting to shareholders (not to the directors), and by raising concerns about internal control and misstatement. An audit committee of independent directors usually oversees this relationship. An auditor needs independence: if the auditor depends financially on the company or provides too many other services, objectivity may be at risk.

## 2.1.3 Continuing and discontinued activities, and exceptional items

**Discontinued operations** are parts of the business that have been sold, closed or classified as held for sale. Their results are shown separately from **continuing operations**. In the example, the loss of 12,000 appears on its own line, so users can see that the continuing business earned a profit of 176,000.

Why it matters: past profit that came from a discontinued division will not recur, so continuing results are a better guide to the future. Without separate disclosure, a company could hide losses in a closed division or boast about profit from a business it no longer owns.

**Exceptional items** are items of income or expense that are material because of their size or nature and are not expected to occur regularly. Examples are restructuring costs, large losses on disposal of assets, or settlement of a legal claim. In the example, 25,000 of the administrative expenses is exceptional. Disclosing it allows users to see that the underlying administrative costs were 125,000, and to judge whether profit from operations of 240,000 is typical. Profit excluding the exceptional item would be 265,000 (240,000 + 25,000). Directors should not use the label to hide recurring costs, which would be misleading.

## Common mistakes

- Putting other comprehensive income in the statement of changes in equity as retained earnings rather than in the revaluation reserve.
- Deducting dividends in the statement of comprehensive income. Dividends are shown in the statement of changes in equity.
- Treating finance costs as an operating expense above profit from operations.
- Showing the revaluation gain as part of profit for the year.
- Confusing share premium with retained earnings.
- Saying the auditor "prepares" or "guarantees" the accounts.
- Describing exceptional items as errors or as always bad news.
- Forgetting that tax payable is a current liability.

## Exam technique

- Follow the IAS 1 headings exactly and show subtotals for gross profit, profit from operations, profit before tax, profit for the year and total comprehensive income.
- Reconcile each column of the statement of changes in equity, and check that the closing equity equals the equity in the statement of financial position.
- **Explain** questions on the auditor need a reason and a consequence: "The auditor is independent, so shareholders can trust the accounts more."
- For **evaluate**, consider benefits and limits of audit (cost, sampling, dependence on information) and reach a judgement.
- Use the exact word "true and fair" when describing an auditor's opinion.
