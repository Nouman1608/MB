## How external influences reach operations

Operations management turns inputs (labour, materials, energy, equipment, information) into outputs (goods and services). External influences change the cost, availability or acceptability of inputs, the methods allowed, and the demand for outputs. They arise outside the business and are outside its control, so the aim is to **anticipate** and **respond**: use forecasts, flexible capacity, a choice of suppliers and contingency plans.

## The eight influences

### Social

Changes in population, attitudes and lifestyles. An ageing population changes who can be hired and what customers want. Customers who want fast delivery and personalisation push operations to be more flexible. Changing attitudes to work, such as demand for flexible hours and remote working, affect staffing and shift patterns. Local attitudes affect planning: a community may object to noise or lorry traffic.

### Legal

Laws set minimum standards for operations. Examples are the minimum wage, working-time and health and safety rules, product safety and consumer protection laws, planning rules, and rules on waste. Legal compliance adds cost but also reduces the risk of fines and legal claims. A change in law forces change: new product-safety rules can require new testing equipment.

### Ethical

Ethical influences concern what is considered right, beyond the law. They include fair treatment of workers in the supply chain, avoiding child labour, honest labelling and the treatment of animals. Customers, pressure groups and the media can act on these concerns. Following ethical practices may raise costs in sourcing and monitoring, but protects reputation and can attract customers.

### Environmental

Pressure to cut waste, energy use, emissions and pollution. Laws, taxes and customer expectations now push operations to recycle, redesign packaging, use renewable energy and measure their carbon footprint. Weather and climate also affect operations: floods can disrupt transport; heat can affect crops and energy demand. Environmental action can lower costs (less energy and waste) as well as add cost.

### Economic

The state of the economy: growth, unemployment, inflation, interest rates and exchange rates. In a boom, demand rises and capacity may be stretched; labour is harder to hire; materials cost more. In a recession demand falls, spare capacity rises and the business may cut costs. Interest rates change the cost of borrowing for machines. Exchange rates change the cost of imported materials and the price of exports.

### Political

Government policy and stability: taxes, subsidies, trade policy, regulation and the risk of political change. A government grant for investing in a region can alter the choice of site. Tariffs, trade deals and sanctions change supply routes and costs. Political instability in a supplier's country is a supply risk.

### Technological

New equipment and systems change what can be produced and how: automation, 3D printing, data analytics and digital supply chains. The influence is external when rivals or suppliers adopt a technology and the business must keep up. It affects cost, quality, speed and the skills needed. The risk is of falling behind, or of buying a technology that soon becomes out of date.

### International

Globalisation gives access to overseas suppliers, markets and labour. It brings competition from overseas, supply chains which are long and exposed to disruption, and exchange-rate and tariff risks. International standards and customer expectations can set the level of quality needed to export.

## Worked example 1: a legal change (minimum wage)

Hobbs Plastics (invented) makes 220,000 containers a year. It employs 30 operatives on the statutory minimum wage of £22,000 a year each, a total of £660,000. The law raises the minimum wage by 6%.

Extra cost = 6% × £660,000 = **£39,600** a year. Per container: £39,600 ÷ 220,000 = **£0.18**.

Evaluation of the influence:

- If the selling price is £9.50, the increase is 1.9% of the price (0.18 ÷ 9.50 × 100). Hobbs may pass part of this on, depending on how much competitors do.
- Hobbs could offset the cost by raising productivity. If output per operative rose by 6%, the same output would need fewer hours.
- The change affects other stakeholders: employees gain income, but jobs may be cut to save cost; customers may pay more; suppliers' prices may also rise because they face the same law.
- The change applies to all UK competitors, so Hobbs is not at a disadvantage relative to them. It might be relative to overseas rivals.

## Worked example 2: an economic change (exchange rate)

Hobbs buys 400 tonnes of resin a year from a European supplier at €1,100 a tonne, a total of €440,000. The cost in pounds depends on the exchange rate.

| Exchange rate | Cost in pounds |
|---|---|
| £1 = €1.30 | £338,462 |
| £1 = €1.20 | £366,667 |
| £1 = €1.10 | £400,000 |

A fall in the pound from €1.20 to €1.10 raises the cost by £400,000 − £366,667 = **£33,333**, an increase of 9.1%.

Evaluation:

- Hobbs's base profit is £150,000. After the wage rise (£39,600) and the exchange-rate fall (£33,333) profit would be £150,000 − £39,600 − £33,333 = **£77,067**, a fall of nearly half. Two external changes together matter more than either alone.
- Responses: agree a fixed price in pounds with the supplier, buy forward, find a UK supplier, or hold more stock when the pound is strong. Each has a cost.
- The influence is unpredictable, so planning should use more than one scenario.

## Worked example 3: an environmental change

A new rule requires Hobbs to treat its waste water. This costs £45,000 to install and £6,000 a year to run. Hobbs could also spend £60,000 on energy-saving equipment that saves £14,000 a year.

Energy-saving payback = £60,000 ÷ £14,000 = **4.3 years**.

Over five years the waste-water rule costs £45,000 + 5 × £6,000 = **£75,000**.

Evaluation:

- The waste-water cost is compulsory; the energy equipment is a choice.
- The energy payback is long, but if energy prices rise it shortens. A green reputation may win contracts with customers who care about the environment.
- Hobbs must also consider the risk of fines and loss of licence if it does not comply.

## Evaluating an influence

Ask these questions in turn.

1. **How likely is it?** A law already passed is certain; a change in tastes is not.
2. **How big is the effect?** Use figures where given.
3. **How quickly does it arrive?** Slow social changes can be planned for; a sudden political event cannot.
4. **Does it affect all rivals equally?** If it does, the effect on competitiveness is smaller.
5. **Can the business respond?** Through flexible capacity, supplier choice, technology or price.
6. **What do stakeholders gain and lose?**
7. **Judgement.** Which influence matters most to this business and why.

## Common mistakes

- Listing influences with no link to operations (cost, capacity, quality, suppliers, location).
- Describing an effect on sales only. This heading is about operations, so refer to production, staffing, stock, quality or logistics.
- Confusing legal with ethical factors. Legal factors are required; ethical ones are about what is right beyond the law.
- Writing about each influence in the same words for every case.
- Treating influences separately when two together have a larger effect.

## Exam technique

Choose the influence that matters most for the case, explain its route to operations, quantify it if you can and then evaluate. Use "however" and "this depends on" to show the limits, and finish with a judgement.
