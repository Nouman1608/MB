This study guide teaches section 3.2.9, Interpretation, analysis and communication of accounting information, from the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Accounting (9615) specification, Version 1.2, for International AS exams May/June 2025 onwards and International A-level exams May/June 2026 onwards. It is **International A-level only** content. The specification lists 3.2.9 in what is assessed by Paper 3: Financial Accounting and by Paper 4: Accounting for analysis and decision making. Every company and number below is made up; money is shown in dollars, with share prices quoted in cents.

The International AS ratios and their general limitations are taught in [analysis and evaluation of financial information](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-accounting-analysis-and-evaluation-of-financial-information/) and not repeated here. ## What section 3.2.9 covers

| Specification content (3.2.9) | What you must be able to do |
|---|---|
| How accounting techniques, measures and ratios are used to analyse and interpret accounting information (both financial and management), and the limitations | Use the 3.1.8 ratios plus five investors' ratios; read management reports |
| How performance is evaluated internally and across accounting periods, and externally in comparison to competitors | Compare years and rivals under profitability, liquidity, efficiency and capital structure |
| Difference between cash and profits; effect of transactions on profitability and liquidity | Trace company transactions through profit, cash and ratios |
| The interests of stakeholders and effective communication | Match each listed stakeholder to the information it needs; value of published accounts |
| The impact, advantages and disadvantages of systems of recording data | Describe what accounting software does; compare computerised with manual records, and single with double entry |
| The critical assessment of recommendations | Judge effects on stakeholders, on the economy locally and nationally, and on the environment |

## Investors' ratios

OxfordAQA's guide *Formulae for accounting ratios* states that you are required to know the formulae. The five added at A-level:

| Ratio | Express as | Formula |
|---|---|---|
| Earnings per share | cents per share | Profit after tax / Number of issued ordinary shares |
| Dividend yield | per cent | Dividend per share / Market price per share × 100 |
| Interest cover | times | Profit before interest and tax / Interest payable |
| Price earnings | times | Market price per share / Earnings per share |
| Dividend cover | times | Profit after interest and tax / Ordinary share dividends paid |

Dividend per share is total ordinary dividends shared across every issued ordinary share. Work EPS and dividend per share in cents when the share price is in cents. For a company, profit before interest and tax is profit from operations.

### Worked example: Ardleigh Pumps plc

Ardleigh has 8,000,000 issued ordinary shares. Years ended 30 June:

| Item | 2026 | 2025 |
|---|---:|---:|
| Revenue | 12,400,000 | 11,000,000 |
| Gross profit | 4,712,000 | 4,290,000 |
| Profit from operations | 1,488,000 | 1,380,000 |
| Finance costs (interest payable) | 188,000 | 130,000 |
| Profit before tax | 1,300,000 | 1,250,000 |
| Tax | 260,000 | 250,000 |
| Profit after tax | 1,040,000 | 1,000,000 |
| Ordinary dividends paid | 416,000 | 400,000 |
| Share price at year end (cents) | 208 | 225 |

2026 working:

```
EPS                = 104,000,000 cents / 8,000,000 = 13.0 cents
Dividend per share =  41,600,000 cents / 8,000,000 = 5.2 cents
Price earnings     = 208 / 13.0                   = 16.0 times
Dividend yield     = 5.2 / 208 × 100              = 2.50%
Dividend cover     = 1,040,000 / 416,000          = 2.50 times
Interest cover     = 1,488,000 / 188,000          = 7.91 times
```

**Reading them.** EPS rose by half a cent, yet the share price fell, so price earnings dropped from 18.0 to 16.0: investors are less confident about growth. Dividends grew in line with profit, so cover held at 2.50. Interest cover fell because finance costs rose by 58,000 while operating profit rose by only 108,000.

## Interpreting management information

The specification includes management information. A month's variance report from Ardleigh's foundry:

| Variance | Dollars |
|---|---:|
| Material price | 3,200 F |
| Material usage | 5,100 A |
| Labour rate | 1,800 A |
| Labour efficiency | 2,400 F |
| Fixed overhead expenditure | 700 A |
| Net | 2,000 A |

Look past the total. A favourable price variance beside an adverse usage variance suggests cheaper, lower-grade metal that produced more scrap. A higher labour rate with favourable efficiency suggests more skilled staff working faster. The methods behind these figures are in [standard costing and variance analysis](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-accounting-standard-costing-and-variance-analysis/) and [budgeting](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-accounting-budgeting/).

## Evaluating performance across periods and against competitors

**Internal comparison** sets this year against earlier years, a budget or a target. **External comparison** sets the business against a competitor or the industry average. Organise both under profitability, liquidity, efficiency and capital structure.

Merrowdale Pumps plc is a rival. Ardleigh's equity was 7,400,000 in 2025 and 8,024,000 in 2026; its debentures were 1,600,000 and 2,600,000.

| Measure | Ardleigh 2025 | Ardleigh 2026 | Merrowdale 2026 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Gross profit margin | 39.00% | 38.00% | 41.00% |
| Profit from operations to revenue | 12.55% | 12.00% | 14.00% |
| Return on capital employed | 15.33% | 14.01% | 16.80% |
| Current ratio | 2.00 : 1 | 1.80 : 1 | 1.30 : 1 |
| Liquid capital ratio | 1.24 : 1 | 1.00 : 1 | 0.70 : 1 |
| Capital gearing | 17.78% | 24.47% | 52.00% |
| Interest cover | 10.62 times | 7.91 times | 3.00 times |
| EPS (cents) | 12.5 | 13.0 | 16.8 |
| Dividend cover | 2.50 times | 2.50 times | 1.25 times |
| Dividend yield | 2.22% | 2.50% | 8.00% |
| Price earnings | 18.0 times | 16.0 times | 10.0 times |

ROCE for 2026 is 1,488,000 / (8,024,000 + 2,600,000) × 100 = 14.01%.

- **Profitability.** Revenue grew 12.73%, but both margins slipped. Merrowdale converts more of each sale into profit and earns more on its capital.
- **Liquidity.** Ardleigh's ratios fell but remain sound. Merrowdale relies on selling inventory to pay current liabilities.
- **Capital structure.** Merrowdale finances over half its long-term capital with debt; interest cover of 3.00 times leaves little room if profit falls.
- **Investors.** Merrowdale pays out 80% of profit after tax, giving a high yield, but cover of 1.25 times makes the dividend fragile. Its low price earnings shows the market prices in that risk. Its higher EPS proves nothing on its own: it has half as many shares.

**Judgement.** Merrowdale is the more profitable business but the riskier one; Ardleigh suits a cautious investor, provided it reverses the slide in margins.

### Limitations when comparing

- Rivals may use different depreciation methods and year ends.
- Share-price ratios depend on the date the price is taken.
- Dividend policy is a choice: a low yield may mean reinvestment, not weakness.
- Ratios ignore non-financial factors such as staff morale, product quality and reputation.

## Cash, profit and company transactions

Profit follows the accruals concept; cash moves only on receipt or payment. Each line below starts from Ardleigh's 2026 position (current assets 3,150,000; current liabilities 1,750,000) and is taken separately.

| Transaction | Profit | Cash | Effect on ratios |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rights issue of 1 for 4 at 160 cents (2,000,000 shares) | None | Up 3,200,000 | Current ratio 3.63 : 1; EPS on the same profit falls to 10.4 cents; gearing falls to 18.81% |
| Land revalued upwards by 900,000 | None (revaluation reserve) | None | Gearing falls to 22.56%; ROCE falls to 12.91% |
| Machine with carrying amount 300,000 sold for 240,000 | Falls by 60,000 (loss on disposal) | Up 240,000 | Current ratio 1.94 : 1 |
| Debentures of 600,000 redeemed from the bank | None now; future interest saved | Down 600,000 | Current ratio 1.46 : 1; gearing 19.95% |

The disposal moves profit and cash in opposite directions; the revaluation improves gearing with no cash moving.

## Stakeholders and communication

| Stakeholder | Type | Main interest and useful information |
|---|---|---|
| Employees | Internal | Job security and pay: profit trend, liquidity |
| Management | Internal | Meeting targets: budgets, variances, all ratios |
| Owners/shareholders | Internal | Return and risk: EPS, dividend yield and cover, price earnings, ROCE |
| Customers | External | Continued supply: liquidity, survival |
| Suppliers | External | Being paid: liquidity ratios, trade payable days |
| Government | External | Tax and jobs: profit, employee numbers |
| Lenders | External | Repayment: interest cover, gearing, security |
| Local community | External | Jobs and pollution: expansion or closure plans |

**Effective communication.** The specification asks you to report to stakeholders with logical arguments and sound judgements. Answer the reader's question first, use only the figures they need, explain ratios in plain words and state the limitations.

**Value of published accounts.** A standard format allows comparison across years and companies, and they are the main source for outsiders. But they are historic, summarised, published months after the year end, and omit non-financial performance and plans.

## Systems of recording data

**Accounting software** posts both sides of an entry from one input, keeps sales and purchase ledgers, raises invoices, reconciles the bank, runs payroll, tracks inventory and produces statements and reports on demand.

**Computerised compared with manual.** Advantages: speed, accurate arithmetic, up-to-date balances, instant reports. Disadvantages: set-up and training cost; wrong inputs still pass through; cyber attack and data loss; work stops if the system fails.

**Single entry compared with double entry.** Single entry is cheap and simple, but gives no trial balance and makes errors and fraud harder to detect. Double entry gives a complete, self-checking record from which statements are drawn directly, at the cost of time and skill. [Accounting for organisations with incomplete records](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-accounting-accounting-for-organisations-with-incomplete/) shows how statements are rebuilt from single entry records.

## Critical assessment of recommendations

Work through the financial case, each stakeholder, the local and national economy, the environment, then judge.

### Worked example: funding a new plant

Ardleigh's directors recommend building a 4,000,000 plant in a coastal town, financed by 9% debentures rather than a rights issue at 160 cents. They expect profit from operations to rise by 720,000. Tax is 20% of profit before tax.

```
New profit from operations       = 1,488,000 + 720,000 = 2,208,000

Debentures: interest = 188,000 + 360,000 = 548,000
  Profit after tax = (2,208,000 − 548,000) × 0.8 = 1,328,000
  EPS = 132,800,000 / 8,000,000  = 16.6 cents
  Interest cover = 2,208,000 / 548,000 = 4.03 times
  Gearing = 6,600,000 / 14,624,000 × 100 = 45.13%

Rights issue: 2,500,000 new shares
  Profit after tax = (2,208,000 − 188,000) × 0.8 = 1,616,000
  EPS = 161,600,000 / 10,500,000 = 15.39 cents
  Interest cover = 2,208,000 / 188,000 = 11.74 times
  Gearing = 2,600,000 / 14,624,000 × 100 = 17.78%
```

- **Shareholders:** debt gives the higher EPS, but gearing nearly doubles.
- **Lenders:** cover falls to about four times.
- **Employees and local economy:** new jobs, wages spent locally, contracts for local suppliers.
- **National economy:** more output and tax revenue.
- **Environment:** building on coastal land, extra energy use and transport emissions.

**Judgement.** The plant earns 18% on its cost, above the 9% interest rate, so debt raises EPS. But the forecast is an estimate. Borrow only if it is reliable; otherwise the rights issue is safer.

## Common errors

- Dividing a share price in cents by EPS in dollars.
- Comparing EPS between companies with different share numbers.
- Putting profit before tax into dividend cover, or deducting interest before working out interest cover.
- Praising a high dividend yield without checking dividend cover.
- Listing stakeholder impacts without reaching a judgement.
