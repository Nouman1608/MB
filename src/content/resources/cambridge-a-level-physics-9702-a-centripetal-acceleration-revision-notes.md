Subtopic 12.2 of Cambridge International AS & A Level Physics 9702 (2025–2027) covers what causes centripetal acceleration and the equations a = rω², a = v²/r, F = mrω² and F = mv²/r. These notes are condensed for recall. For full explanations and worked examples, read the [Centripetal acceleration study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-physics-9702-a-centripetal-acceleration/); to apply the ideas, try the [Centripetal acceleration practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-physics-9702-a-centripetal-acceleration-practice/).

## Cause and effect in one chain

1. A force acts that is **always perpendicular** to the velocity.
2. It has **no component along the velocity**, so the speed does not change and no work is done.
3. It changes the **direction** of the velocity, so there is an acceleration.
4. If the force also has **constant magnitude**, the velocity turns through equal angles in equal times: **constant angular speed**, and the path is a circle.
5. The acceleration points along the radius **towards the centre**: centripetal acceleration.

## The equations

```
a = v² / r        a = rω²
F = mv² / r       F = mrω²
v = rω            ω = 2π / T = 2πf
```

- ω in **rad s⁻¹**; r in **m**; F is the **resultant** force towards the centre.
- From rev min⁻¹: divide by 60, then multiply by 2π.
- Derivation outline: in time Δt the velocity turns through Δθ, so Δv = vΔθ and Δt = rΔθ/v, giving a = Δv/Δt = v²/r.

## Which proportionality applies

| Held constant | Equation to reason with | Result |
|---|---|---|
| ω (same rigid rotating body) | a = rω² | a ∝ r |
| v (same speed, different bends) | a = v²/r | a ∝ 1/r |
| r, speed changed by factor k | a = v²/r | a changes by k² |
| r, period changed by factor k | a = rω² | a changes by 1/k² |

## What supplies the force

| Situation | Real force towards the centre |
|---|---|
| Object on a string, horizontal frictionless surface | Tension |
| Car on a flat bend | Friction from the road |
| Satellite in circular orbit | Gravity |
| Rider in a spinning rotor | Normal contact force from the wall |
| Charge moving perpendicular to a uniform magnetic field | Magnetic force |
| Conical pendulum | Horizontal component of tension (T sin θ) |

## Conical pendulum in four lines

```
vertical:     T cos θ = mg
horizontal:   T sin θ = mrω²
radius:       r = L sin θ
combined:     ω² = g / (L cos θ)     (mass cancels)
```

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Centripetal acceleration | Acceleration of a body in circular motion, directed towards the centre, of size v²/r |
| Centripetal force | The resultant force towards the centre needed for circular motion; supplied by real forces |
| Angular speed ω | Angle swept out by the radius per unit time, in rad s⁻¹ |
| Uniform circular motion | Motion in a circle at constant speed and constant angular speed |
| Tangential velocity | The velocity at any instant, along the tangent to the circle |
| Period T | Time for one complete revolution |

## Numbers worth recognising

- A point on the Equator: a ≈ 0.034 m s⁻² (about 0.3% of g).
- A spin dryer drum of radius 0.25 m at 1200 rev min⁻¹: a ≈ 3.9 × 10³ m s⁻².
- If a result for a ≫ g appears in a fairground or car question, check the units of ω.

## Exam traps

- Drawing "centripetal force" as an extra arrow on a free-body diagram.
- Writing that the acceleration is "outwards" or that the object is "flung outwards"; when the force stops, the object moves along the tangent.
- Leaving ω in rev s⁻¹ or degrees s⁻¹.
- Using the string length instead of L sin θ as the radius of a conical pendulum.
- Using the whole tension, rather than its horizontal component, as the centripetal force.
- Stating that constant speed means zero resultant force.
- Claiming the centripetal force does work; it is perpendicular to the motion, so it does none.

## Self-test

1. Why does a force that is always perpendicular to the velocity not change the speed?
2. What extra condition on the force makes the path a circle with constant angular speed?
3. State the direction of centripetal acceleration.
4. Convert 900 rev min⁻¹ to rad s⁻¹.
5. Two points on a rotating disc are at radii 4.0 cm and 12.0 cm. Find the ratio of their centripetal accelerations (outer : inner).
6. A car takes a bend at the same speed as before but the radius is halved. By what factor does the required friction force change?
7. The speed of a ball on a string is tripled at the same radius. By what factor does the tension change (horizontal frictionless surface)?
8. A 2.0 kg mass moves at 3.0 m s⁻¹ in a circle of radius 1.5 m. Calculate the resultant force on it.
9. What supplies the centripetal force for a rider pressed against the wall of a spinning rotor?
10. In a conical pendulum, which component of the tension balances the weight?
11. A body moves in a circle of radius 0.50 m with period 2.0 s. Calculate a.
12. A string breaks while a stone is moving in a horizontal circle. Describe the stone's motion just afterwards (seen from above).

**Answers:** 1. It has no component along the velocity, so it does no work and the kinetic energy is unchanged. 2. Constant magnitude. 3. Towards the centre of the circle. 4. 900 ÷ 60 = 15 rev s⁻¹; ω = 2π × 15 = 94 rad s⁻¹. 5. Same ω, a ∝ r: 3 : 1. 6. a = v²/r, so it doubles. 7. a = v²/r, so it increases by a factor of 9. 8. F = mv²/r = 2.0 × 9.0 ÷ 1.5 = 12 N. 9. The normal contact force from the wall. 10. The vertical component, T cos θ. 11. ω = 2π ÷ 2.0 = 3.14 rad s⁻¹; a = rω² = 0.50 × 9.87 = 4.9 m s⁻². 12. It moves in a straight line along the tangent at the point where the string broke (and falls under gravity).
