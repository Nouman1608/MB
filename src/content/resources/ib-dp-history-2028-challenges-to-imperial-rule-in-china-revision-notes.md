These revision notes are for **higher level (HL) only**. Challenges to imperial rule in China (1736 to 1911) is a regional study examined on Paper 3, which standard level students do not sit. They condense the IB *History guide*, first assessment 2028. Start with the [study guide](/resources/ib-dp-history-2028-challenges-to-imperial-rule-in-china/) for the full account, then use the [practice set](/resources/ib-dp-history-2028-challenges-to-imperial-rule-in-china-practice/).

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Qing | The Manchu dynasty ruling China from 1644 to 1912 |
| Mandate of Heaven | The belief that Heaven grants the right to rule and withdraws it from bad rulers |
| Tributary system | China's model of foreign relations: others offered respect and gifts in return for recognition |
| Kowtow | Ritual bow showing submission to the emperor |
| Canton system | Restriction of European trade to Canton from 1757, through the Co-hong merchants |
| Unequal treaties | Treaties forced on China by war: indemnities, treaty ports, extraterritoriality |
| Extraterritoriality | The right of foreigners to be tried under their own country's law |
| Treaty port | A Chinese city opened to foreign residence and trade |
| Self-Strengthening | Late nineteenth-century effort to adopt Western military and industrial techniques |
| Tongmenghui | Revolutionary alliance founded in 1905 in Tokyo |

## Qianlong and the first challenges

- **Strengths:** large expanded empire; Grand Council; wealth.
- **Weaknesses:** Heshen's corruption, population pressure, military decline.
- **Rebellions:** White Lotus (poor settlers, sect), Miao (land taken by Han settlers).

## The opening of China: a causal chain

1. Britain wanted tea but had little to sell, so exported Indian opium.
2. Silver flowed out of China; addiction grew.
3. Lin Zexu destroyed the opium in 1839.
4. Britain used force: the First Opium War.
5. Nanjing (1842) set the model of unequal treaties.
6. Further wars (1856 to 1860) extended foreign rights.

## Treaties compared

| Treaty | Main terms |
|---|---|
| Nanjing 1842 | Hong Kong ceded; five treaty ports; indemnity; end of the Co-hong |
| Tianjin 1858 | More ports; foreign envoys in Beijing; missionary freedom; opium legalised under a tariff |
| Beijing 1860 | Kowloon ceded; more indemnity; Tianjin opened |
| Shimonoseki 1895 | Taiwan and the Pescadores ceded; Korean independence; large indemnity |
| Boxer Protocol 1901 | 450 million taels; legation guards; punishments |

## Taiping: a quick analysis

- **Causes:** Guangxi poverty, floods, ethnic conflict, weakened Qing after the war, Hong's religion.
- **Policies:** ban on opium and foot-binding; promise of land equality; strict religion.
- **Why defeated:** divisions among leaders after 1856, regional armies under Zeng and Li, foreign help, loss of Confucian support.
- **Result:** huge death toll, regional power, damage to the dynasty.

## Reform and its failure

- **Cixi and Prince Gong:** the 1861 coup; Zongli Yamen.
- **Self-Strengthening:** arsenals, shipyards, steamship company; limited by provincial funding and conservatism.
- **1895 defeat:** showed the effort had failed.
- **Hundred Days:** radical, short, ended by a coup.
- **New Policies (after 1901):** exam abolition, army, assemblies; too late and too slow.

## The road to 1911

- Boxer failure and indemnity deepened the dynasty's crisis.
- New army and assemblies created new power centres.
- Sun's Three Principles gave a programme; the Tongmenghui gave an organisation.
- Railway nationalisation made investors angry; the Wuchang uprising spread; the court turned to Yuan Shikai; the emperor abdicated.

## Perspectives

- **Impact-response (Fairbank):** the West drove change in China.
- **China-centred (Cohen):** look first at Chinese society.
- **New Qing History:** the Qing as a Manchu, Inner Asian empire.
- **Nationalist narrative:** "century of humiliation".

## Worked example: a plan

**Question:** "Evaluate the reasons for the failure of reform in the Qing dynasty between 1861 and 1911."

1. Thesis: reform failed mainly because it was limited, provincial and led by a court that feared losing power.
2. Self-Strengthening: technology without institutional change.
3. Leadership: Cixi's balance between reformers and conservatives.
4. External pressure: defeat in 1895 and the concessions of 1898.
5. Late reforms: genuine but too late; they created a new army and assemblies that turned against the dynasty.
6. Judgement with a view of historians.

## Common mistakes

- Treating the Taiping as only a religious rebellion; it was also social and political.
- Forgetting the Miao and White Lotus risings.
- Mixing up Tianjin 1858 and Tianjin 1860 outcomes.
- Dating the fall of the Qing to 1911 only; the abdication was in February 1912.

## Self-test

1. What was the Canton system?
2. Which commissioner destroyed opium in 1839?
3. Name the five treaty ports of 1842.
4. Who led the Taiping Rebellion?
5. Which two men raised regional armies to fight the Taiping?
6. What was the result of the First Sino-Japanese War?
7. Who ended the Hundred Days' Reform?
8. What were the Three Principles of the People?

**Answers:** 1. Restriction of European trade to Canton through the Co-hong from 1757. 2. Lin Zexu. 3. Canton, Amoy, Fuzhou, Ningbo and Shanghai. 4. Hong Xiuquan. 5. Zeng Guofan and Li Hongzhang. 6. Treaty of Shimonoseki: Taiwan ceded, Korean independence and an indemnity. 7. Cixi and conservative officials. 8. Nationalism, democracy and people's livelihood.

## Frequently asked questions

**How many treaties should I memorise?**
Know Nanjing, Tianjin, the Convention of Beijing, Shimonoseki and the Boxer Protocol, with two terms each.

**Is the Taiping Rebellion more important than the Opium Wars?**
It is a good question to argue. The Taiping killed far more people, but the wars changed China's position in the world. Argue it with criteria.
