## What a product is

A **product** is anything a business offers to meet a need, whether a physical good or a service. It includes the core benefit (a phone makes calls), the features and design, the quality, the packaging, the brand name and the support that comes with it, such as a warranty and customer service. Customers buy a package of benefits, not just an object.

Product is the starting element of the mix: price, place and promotion all depend on what is being sold. A poor product cannot be rescued by promotion for long.

## Product strategy tools

Evaluating a product strategy means judging whether a business has the right products, in the right balance, for its objectives. The specification names five tools.

1. **The product life cycle** shows the stage of each product, and so when to launch, support or withdraw (see the [product life cycle study guide](/resources/ocr-a-level-business-h431-product-life-cycle/)).
2. **Value analysis** compares the cost of each feature with the value customers place on it.
3. **The Boston matrix** classifies products by market growth and relative market share.
4. **Ansoff's matrix** compares growth options: market penetration, product development, market development and diversification.
5. **Product portfolio analysis** examines the whole range to check that it is balanced between products that earn cash now and products that will earn in future.

### Worked example 1: a Boston matrix for Kestrel Foods

The Boston matrix plots **market growth** (high or low) against **relative market share** (share divided by the share of the biggest rival). A relative share above 1.0 means the product leads its market. In this example, high growth is taken as 8% or more.

| Product | Market growth | Own share | Largest rival's share | Relative share | Sales (£m) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Oat Crunch | 12% | 18% | 9% | 18 ÷ 9 = **2.0** | 4.5 |
| Classic Muesli | 2% | 30% | 15% | 30 ÷ 15 = **2.0** | 6.0 |
| Fruit Bites | 9% | 4% | 20% | 4 ÷ 20 = **0.2** | 1.2 |
| Rice Cakes | −1% | 3% | 25% | 3 ÷ 25 = **0.12** | 0.8 |

**Classification.**

- **Oat Crunch: star.** High growth and a strong share. It needs investment to keep its lead.
- **Classic Muesli: cash cow.** Low growth, strong share. It earns steady cash with little investment.
- **Fruit Bites: question mark (problem child).** High growth but a weak share. It could become a star with investment, or fail.
- **Rice Cakes: dog.** Low growth and weak share. It earns little and may be sold or withdrawn.

Total sales are 4.5 + 6.0 + 1.2 + 0.8 = £12.5 million. Classic Muesli provides 48% of sales (6.0 ÷ 12.5) and Oat Crunch 36%, so 84% of sales come from two products. That is a risk: if the cash cow declines, Kestrel has few other sources of cash.

**Strategy.** Use the cash from Classic Muesli to fund Oat Crunch and test Fruit Bites in a few more stores, but set a limit: if its share has not reached, say, 8% within two years, withdraw it. Review Rice Cakes: unless it supports other sales in shops, drop it.

**Limits of the matrix.** It reduces a product to two measures; market definition changes the result (a narrow market makes share look larger); it ignores links between products and does not show how to improve a product. Use it with other tools.

### Worked example 2: value analysis

Value analysis (and value engineering) examines each feature of a product to see whether its cost is justified by what customers value. Aria sells an electric kettle for £50. The unit cost is £28. A survey of 500 customers asked which features they considered important.

| Feature | Cost per unit | Customers who rate it important |
|---|---|---|
| Body and heating element | £9 | 95% |
| Rapid boil | £4 | 82% |
| Temperature display | £5 | 40% |
| App connection | £6 | 12% |
| Decorative trim | £4 | 20% |
| **Total cost** | **£28** | |

The costs add up to £28. The app connection costs £6 but only 12% of customers value it, so it is the weakest value for money. Removing it:

1. New unit cost = £28 − £6 = **£22**.
2. Contribution per kettle rises from £50 − £28 = £22 to £50 − £22 = **£28**.
3. Margin rises from 22 ÷ 50 = **44%** to 28 ÷ 50 = **56%**.

Suppose sales are 20,000 a year and removing the app loses 3% of sales (600 kettles).

- Before: 20,000 × £22 = **£440,000** total contribution.
- After: 19,400 × £28 = **£543,200**.
- Gain: £543,200 − £440,000 = **£103,200**.

**Evaluation.** Value analysis can cut cost without losing customers, or let a business put the money where customers value it. But survey answers do not always match behaviour, and a feature that few customers value may still win a loyal minority or support a premium image. The business should test before dropping a feature, and watch its competitors.

## USP

A **unique selling point (USP)** is a feature or benefit that sets a product apart so that customers have a reason to choose it. It might be a design, a benefit, a service or a production method. Kestrel Foods' USP might be "the only breakfast cereal made from oats grown within 30 miles".

To **identify** a USP, ask what customers say the business does better than rivals, and whether rivals can copy it. A USP is **used** in promotion, packaging and the brand message, because it gives customers a reason to pay a premium and helps the business to stand out. It lasts only while rivals cannot copy it, so the business must keep protecting and renewing it, for example by patents or by constant innovation.

## Brands

A **brand** is a name, symbol, design or reputation that identifies a product and distinguishes it from rivals. **Branding** is the process of building that identity through name, logo, packaging, quality and promotion.

Importance of a brand:

- allows a **premium price**: if a branded bar sells at £3.20 and the own-label equivalent at £2.40, the brand earns a 33% premium (3.20 ÷ 2.40 − 1);
- builds **loyalty and repeat purchase**, reducing the cost of winning customers;
- makes it easier to **launch new products** under a known name;
- gives **protection** from price competition;
- adds **value for shareholders**, since a strong brand is an asset.

Limits: building a brand is slow and costly; one scandal or product failure damages every product under the name; brand loyalty can fall if quality drops or rivals offer better value. For stakeholders: employees may take pride in the brand, but a damaged brand may threaten jobs; retailers benefit from a brand that sells itself.

## Differentiation

**Product differentiation** makes a product different in the eyes of customers through quality, design, features, service or brand image. It reduces direct price competition and supports a higher price. The cost is that differentiation must be real or well communicated, and it can be copied. In a market where all products are almost the same (such as basic flour), differentiation is very hard.

## Product mix

The **product mix** is the full range of products a business sells. Its **width** is the number of product lines; its **depth** is the number of variants in each line. Kestrel has four lines (width 4). If they have 5, 3, 6 and 2 variants, the depth totals 16 products.

To **recommend and justify** a mix, link it to the objectives and the tools above. For Kestrel: keep the cash cow and star, give Fruit Bites a time-limited trial, and cut Rice Cakes. That narrows the range to three lines, cuts costs and focuses money on growth, but it reduces choice for retailers, so Kestrel should check that Rice Cakes does not bring customers who also buy other products.

## Common mistakes

- Describing the Boston matrix without calculating relative share.
- Saying a dog must always be dropped. It may still generate cash or support other sales.
- Treating a USP as permanent.
- Confusing branding with advertising. Branding is the whole identity.
- Recommending a product mix without stating the objective it serves.

## Exam technique

State the tool, apply it with figures from the case, and judge the strategy. For "recommend and justify", name the products to keep, develop or drop, explain the reasons and give a risk.
