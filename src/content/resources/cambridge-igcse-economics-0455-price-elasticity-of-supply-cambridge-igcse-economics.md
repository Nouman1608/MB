## 2.8.1 Definition

**Price elasticity of supply (PES)** measures the responsiveness of quantity supplied to a change in the price of the product.

```
PES = percentage change in quantity supplied / percentage change in price
```

## 2.8.2 Calculation and interpretation

Price and quantity supplied move in the same direction, so PES is **positive**.

| PES | Description | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | Perfectly inelastic | Quantity supplied cannot change |
| Between 0 and 1 | Inelastic | Quantity supplied changes by a smaller percentage than price |
| 1 | Unitary | Equal percentage changes |
| Greater than 1 | Elastic | Quantity supplied changes by a larger percentage than price |
| Infinite | Perfectly elastic | Producers will supply any quantity at the current price |

**Worked example 1: calculating PES.** The price of tomatoes rises from 1.60 to 2.00 per kg. A grower's weekly supply rises from 5,000 kg to 5,500 kg.

1. % change in price = 0.40 ÷ 1.60 × 100 = **+25%**.
2. % change in quantity supplied = 500 ÷ 5,000 × 100 = **+10%**.
3. PES = 10 ÷ 25 = **0.4**: inelastic. The grower cannot quickly plant and harvest more.

**Worked example 2: predicting output.** A furniture factory has PES of 1.5. The price of its chairs rises by 8%.

1. % change in quantity supplied = 1.5 × 8 = **+12%**.
2. If it supplied 2,500 chairs a month, it now supplies 2,500 × 1.12 = **2,800**.

### Supply diagrams showing different PES

Draw with price on the vertical axis and quantity on the horizontal axis.

- **Perfectly inelastic (PES = 0):** a vertical supply curve, such as the seats in a stadium on match day.
- **Perfectly elastic (PES infinite):** a horizontal supply curve.
- For **straight-line supply curves**, where the line meets the axes tells you the elasticity:

| Straight supply line | PES |
|---|---|
| Passes through the origin | Unitary (1) at every point |
| Meets the price axis first (cuts the vertical axis above zero) | Elastic (greater than 1) |
| Meets the quantity axis first (cuts the horizontal axis to the right of zero) | Inelastic (less than 1) |

**Check with numbers.** A line through the origin, such as quantity = 50 × price: price 4 to 5 is +25%, quantity 200 to 250 is +25%, so PES = 1. A line that meets the quantity axis, such as quantity = 100 + 25 × price: price 4 to 5 (+25%), quantity 200 to 225 (+12.5%), so PES = 0.5.

## 2.8.3 Determinants of PES

| Determinant | More elastic when | More inelastic when |
|---|---|---|
| Spare capacity | Idle machines and workers can be used | Factories already at full capacity |
| Stocks | Large stocks can be released for sale | Few or no stocks |
| Ease of storage | Product keeps well | Product is perishable |
| Time period | Long enough to hire workers and build capacity | Very short period |
| Production time | Quick to produce (printed T-shirts) | Slow to produce (crops, trees, buildings) |
| Ease of switching resources | Factors can move easily between uses | Factors are specialised and immobile |

### Time and PES

- **A very short period (days):** supply is fixed at what is already available; PES is close to 0.
- **Short run:** firms can use more of their variable factors (overtime, extra shifts), so supply becomes more elastic.
- **Long run:** firms can add factories and new firms can enter, so supply is most elastic.

**Worked example 3: the same price rise in two markets.** After a rise in demand, the price rises by 20% in both markets.

| Market | PES | % change in quantity supplied |
|---|---|---|
| Fresh flowers in the week before a festival | 0.2 | 0.2 × 20 = **+4%** |
| Printed greeting cards | 2.5 | 2.5 × 20 = **+50%** |

Flowers take months to grow and cannot be stored long, so extra demand mostly raises the price. Cards can be printed quickly from stocks of paper, so extra demand is mostly met by more output.

## 2.8.4 Significance of PES

### Consumers

- When supply is inelastic, a rise in demand pushes the price up sharply, so consumers pay much more. Housing in a growing city is a common example: new homes take years to build.
- When supply is elastic, extra demand is met mostly by extra output, so prices rise little.
- Inelastic supply also makes prices unstable when supply itself is hit, as with farm products after bad weather.

### Producers

- Firms with elastic supply can take advantage of a rise in demand by expanding output quickly, gaining extra revenue.
- Firms with inelastic supply gain mainly through a higher price, but risk losing customers to rivals who can supply more.
- Firms can make supply more elastic by keeping stocks, holding spare capacity, training staff to do several jobs and using flexible machinery. Each of these has a cost, such as storage or idle equipment.

### Government

- **Subsidies:** a subsidy to producers raises output a lot when supply is elastic, but mainly lowers prices when supply is inelastic and output cannot respond.
- **Policies to raise PES:** training that improves the mobility of workers, quicker approval for new buildings, and support for storage can make supply more responsive, so price rises are smaller when demand grows.
- **Planning for shortages:** where supply is inelastic, such as hospital places or school buildings, governments need to plan years ahead.

## Common mistakes

- Writing PES as a negative number.
- Putting price on top of the formula.
- Thinking that a steep supply curve is always perfectly inelastic. Only a vertical one is.
- Judging a straight supply line by its steepness alone: check where it meets the axes.
- Listing determinants without linking them to the product, for example "crops take a season to grow, so supply cannot respond in a month".
- Confusing PES determinants (about producers) with PED determinants (about buyers).

## Exam technique for the 0455 command words

- **Define:** "the responsiveness of quantity supplied to a change in price".
- **Calculate:** show both percentage changes, divide, then say elastic or inelastic with a reason.
- **Explain:** link a determinant to the firm's ability to change output: "spare capacity, so idle machines can be switched on, so output rises quickly".
- **Analyse:** compare how price and quantity respond to the same demand shift in markets with different PES.
- **Discuss:** judge whether a policy such as a subsidy will raise output, using PES and other factors such as cost.
