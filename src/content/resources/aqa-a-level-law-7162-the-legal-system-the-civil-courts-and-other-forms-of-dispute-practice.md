## Section A

**1.** Identify the **three** divisions of the High Court. **[3]**

**2.** State the normal upper limit of the small claims track. **[1]**

**3.** Name the Act that created the First-tier Tribunal and the Upper Tribunal. **[1]**

**4.** State **one** difference between mediation and negotiation. **[2]**

## Section B

**5.** Explain what happens before a civil claim is issued and why it matters. **[4]**

**6.** Explain how a defended claim is allocated to a track, including **two** factors other than value. **[4]**

**7.** Explain the appeal routes in civil cases and when permission is needed. **[5]**

**8.** Outline the structure of the tribunal system and the role of tribunals. **[5]**

**9.** Explain how the courts' approach to mediation has developed, referring to **three** cases. **[6]**

**10.** Explain how Part 36 offers encourage negotiation. **[3]**

**11.** Owen bought a used car for £18,000 from a dealer. It broke down within a month, and the dealer refuses to repair it or refund him. Separately, Owen has been refused a social security benefit. Advise Owen on how each dispute could be resolved. **[8]**

**12.** "Tribunals and mediation provide better justice than the civil courts." Discuss. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** King's Bench Division [1]; Chancery Division [1]; Family Division [1].

**2.** £10,000 [1].

**3.** The Tribunals, Courts and Enforcement Act 2007 [1].

**4.** In mediation a neutral third party, the mediator, helps the parties reach an agreement [1], whereas in negotiation the parties or their lawyers deal with each other directly with no neutral helper [1].

**5.** The parties are expected to follow the relevant pre-action protocol or practice direction [1], which requires them to exchange information about the claim, such as a letter before claim and a response [1], and to try to settle or consider alternative dispute resolution [1]. It matters because it can avoid litigation altogether, and a party who ignores it may face costs penalties [1].

**6.** If a claim is defended, the court considers the information provided by the parties and allocates it to the small claims, fast, intermediate or multi-track [1], with value the starting point [1]. Other factors, any two: complexity of the facts and law; number of parties; amount of oral evidence; importance of the claim to people who are not parties; the parties' views [1] [1].

**7.** Appeals from a district judge go to a circuit judge, and from a circuit judge to a High Court judge [1]. Appeals from the High Court go to the Court of Appeal (Civil Division), and then to the Supreme Court on points of law of general public importance [1]. Permission is needed for almost all appeals, from the lower court or the appeal court [1], and is granted only if the appeal has a real prospect of success or there is another compelling reason; a second appeal needs an important point of principle or practice or some other compelling reason [1]. In limited cases a leapfrog appeal can go directly from the High Court to the Supreme Court [1].

**8.** Tribunals decide disputes in specialist areas, often between individuals and the state, such as social security, immigration and tax, and between employees and employers [1]. The First-tier Tribunal hears cases at first instance and is organised into chambers by subject, such as the Social Entitlement Chamber [1]. Appeals on points of law go to the Upper Tribunal, which also has chambers, such as the Administrative Appeals Chamber [1], and then to the Court of Appeal with permission [1]. Employment Tribunals and the Employment Appeal Tribunal sit outside this structure [1].

**9.** In *Dunnett v Railtrack plc* (2002) the successful party was denied its costs because it had refused to consider mediation [1] [1]. In *Halsey v Milton Keynes General NHS Trust* (2004) the Court of Appeal held that courts could not compel parties to mediate, but could penalise an unreasonable refusal through costs [1] [1]. In *Churchill v Merthyr Tydfil County Borough Council* (2023) the Court of Appeal held that courts can order parties to engage in non-court dispute resolution, provided it is proportionate and does not impair the right to a fair trial [1] [1].

**10.** A Part 36 offer is a formal offer to settle made under the Civil Procedure Rules [1]. If the other party rejects it and then fails to obtain a better result at trial, it faces costs consequences [1], so parties have a strong incentive to consider reasonable offers seriously and settle by negotiation [1].

**11.** Car: Owen should first negotiate with the dealer, for example through a letter before claim, as expected by pre-action conduct [1]. If that fails, he can issue a claim in the County Court, because the value is well below the High Court threshold [1]. A claim for £18,000 would normally be allocated to the fast track [1], with a trial of no more than one day, limited expert evidence and fixed recoverable costs [1]. Mediation may also be suitable, as courts encourage it and since *Churchill* (2023) can order it [1]. Benefit: Owen's dispute is with the state, so it would be heard by the First-tier Tribunal in its Social Entitlement Chamber [1], which is cheap, informal and specialist [1]. An appeal on a point of law would go to the Upper Tribunal, with permission [1].

**12.** For the statement: tribunals are cheaper and quicker than courts, and their judges and members are specialists [1]; their informality makes them more accessible to people without lawyers [1]; mediation lets parties control the outcome and preserve relationships, which can give a fairer result for both sides [1]; mediation is private and avoids the stress of a hearing [1]. Against: legal aid is rarely available in tribunals, so an unrepresented individual may face a represented public body [1]; mediation depends on willingness to compromise and a weaker party may be pressured into a poor settlement [1]; mediation produces no binding decision unless agreed and creates no precedent, so the law does not develop [1]; the courts provide public, reasoned and enforceable judgments and an appeal system that protects legal rights [1]. Conclusion: tribunals and mediation provide better justice for many specialist and lower-value disputes, but the courts remain necessary for complex claims, setting precedent and protecting weaker parties [1] [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- In advice questions, naming the right track but not explaining its features.
- Sending a dispute with the state to the County Court instead of a tribunal.
- Describing mediation cases without the development from *Halsey* to *Churchill*.
- In essays, failing to compare the methods against the courts, as the question requires.
