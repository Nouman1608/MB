## Questions

**1.** A section of bases is removed from one gene and joined into a different chromosome.
**(a)** State the name of this type of gene mutation. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain how it could change the polypeptide coded for by the gene it was removed from. **[2]**

**2.** A gene is transcribed into this mRNA: AUG GCU UCC GAA GGG UGG UAA. Codon data: AUG Met; GCU and GCA Ala; UCC Ser; GAA and GAG Glu; GGG Gly; UGG Trp; UAA stop.
**(a)** Give the amino acid sequence the mRNA codes for. **[1]**
**(b)** The codon GAA changes to GAG. Explain the effect on the polypeptide. **[2]**
**(c)** Instead, GAA changes to GCA. State the effect. **[1]**
**(d)** Instead, GAA changes to UAA. Describe and explain the effect. **[2]**

**3.** Use the mRNA in question 2. Further codon data: AUC Ile; CGA and AGG Arg; GUG and GUA Val.
**(a)** One A is inserted after the sixth base (after GCU). Write the new mRNA as triplets and give the amino acids it codes for. **[2]**
**(b)** Instead, the three bases UCC are deleted. Give the amino acid sequence. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain why the mutation in (a) has a greater effect than the one in (b). **[2]**

**4.** The template strand of a gene reads, from 3' to 5': TAC GAA TTT CCC GGT ATT. Codon data: AUG Met; CUU Leu; AAA and AAG Lys; GGG Gly; CCA Pro; UAA stop.
**(a)** Write the mRNA transcribed from this template. **[2]**
**(b)** The template triplet TTT changes to TTC. Explain why the polypeptide does not change. **[2]**

**5.** The coding strand of a gene reads ATG GAA TCC AAA GTC TGG TAA. Three mutant versions are shown. Codon data: AUG Met; GAA Glu; UCC Ser; AAA Lys; GUC Val; UGG Trp; GAC Asp; UUU Phe; AGU Ser; CUG Leu; GUA Val.
Mutant 1: ATG GAA TCC AAA GTC GTC TGG TAA
Mutant 2: ATG GAA TCC GAC TTT TGG TAA
Mutant 3: ATG GAA TCC AAA AGT CTG GTA A
For each mutant, name the type of mutation and describe its effect on the polypeptide. **[6]**

**6.** A polypeptide has 312 amino acids. A mutation produces a stop codon at what was codon 46.
**(a)** Calculate the number of bases in the coding region of the original gene, including its stop codon. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage of the original amino acids missing from the mutant polypeptide. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why the mutant protein is unlikely to function. **[1]**

**7.** Totipotent cells occur in early mammalian embryos.
**(a)** State what is meant by a totipotent cell. **[1]**
**(b)** Name the type of stem cell described in each case: (i) found in embryos and able to divide in unlimited numbers; (ii) found in mature mammals and forms a limited number of cell types; (iii) found in mature mammals and forms only cardiomyocytes. **[3]**
**(c)** All cells in an embryo have the same DNA. Explain how they can become different specialised cells. **[2]**

**8.** A student prepared 40 explants from cauliflower tissue for tissue culture. Five became contaminated. Of the rest, 31 formed callus.
**(a)** Calculate the percentage of uncontaminated explants that formed callus. **[2]**
**(b)** Describe two precautions to reduce contamination. **[2]**
**(c)** Suggest what the formation of callus shows about the cells of an explant. **[1]**

**9.** **(a)** Describe how induced pluripotent stem (iPS) cells can be produced from adult skin cells. **[3]**
**(b)** State one advantage of iPS cells compared with embryonic stem cells. **[1]**

**10.** In a trial, 45 patients with a heart disorder received a therapy using cells derived from pluripotent stem cells, and 45 received standard care. After 12 months, 27 treated patients and 15 control patients had improved. Tumours were found in 3 treated patients and in none of the control patients.
**(a)** Calculate, for each group, the percentage of patients showing improvement. **[2]**
**(b)** Evaluate the use of this therapy. **[4]**

**11.** A rare disorder is caused by a deletion of one base in the gene for an enzyme.
**(a)** Explain how this deletion can stop the enzyme working. **[4]**
**(b)** State one advantage of using iPS cells rather than embryonic stem cells to treat the patient. **[1]**
**(c)** Skin cells from this patient are used to make iPS cells. Explain why the iPS cells would not by themselves cure the disorder. **[2]**
**(d)** Give two concerns about using pluripotent stem cells in treatment. **[2]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** Translocation. [1]
**(b)** The removed bases are lost from the gene, as in a deletion. [1] This can change the amino acids coded for, and if the number of bases lost is not a multiple of three it causes a frame shift. [1]
*Examiner insight:* Linking a translocation to what happens at the old site, in terms of deletions you already know, is a safer route than recalling a separate rule.

**2. (a)** Met-Ala-Ser-Glu-Gly-Trp, then stop. [1]
**(b)** GAG and GAA both code for Glu. [1] The polypeptide is unchanged because the genetic code is degenerate. [1]
**(c)** Glu is replaced by Ala, so one amino acid is different. [1]
**(d)** UAA is a stop codon, so translation ends at this point. [1] The polypeptide is shorter: Met-Ala-Ser only. [1]
*Examiner insight:* Naming the triplets and amino acids on each side of the change gains credit; "the protein changes" gives little to mark.

**3. (a)** The new mRNA reads AUG GCU AUC CGA AGG GUG GUA A. [1] The amino acids are Met-Ala-Ile-Arg-Arg-Val-Val. [1]
**(b)** Met-Ala-Glu-Gly-Trp, then stop. [1]
**(c)** In (a) one base is added, so the reading frame shifts. [1] Every triplet after the insertion changes, so many amino acids differ, whereas in (b) three bases are lost, the frame is kept and only Ser is missing. [1]
*Examiner insight:* An explain-why question needs a comparison; mention both the frame and the number of amino acids affected.

**4. (a)** The mRNA is complementary to the template, with U in place of T. [1] AUG CUU AAA GGG CCA UAA. [1]
**(b)** The template change TTT to TTC alters the mRNA codon from AAA to AAG. [1] Both code for Lys, because the genetic code is degenerate, so the polypeptide is unchanged. [1]
*Examiner insight:* Check which strand you are given. The codon you compare must come from the mRNA, not from the template.

**5.** Mutant 1: a duplication of GTC. [1] Three bases are added, so the frame is kept, and the polypeptide has one extra Val: Met-Glu-Ser-Lys-Val-Val-Trp. [1]
Mutant 2: an inversion of AAAGTC. [1] Lys-Val is replaced by Asp-Phe, so two amino acids change and the length stays the same. [1]
Mutant 3: an addition of one base (A). [1] This is a frame shift: from the fifth triplet every amino acid changes (Met-Glu-Ser-Lys-Ser-Leu-Val) and the stop codon is no longer read. [1]
*Examiner insight:* Name the mutation and state its effect separately. Noting whether the length has changed separates the types quickly.

**6. (a)** 312 amino acids plus one stop codon is 313 triplets, and 313 × 3 = 939 bases. [1]
**(b)** The mutant has 45 amino acids, so 312 − 45 = 267 are missing. [1] 267 ÷ 312 × 100 = **85.6%**. [1]
**(c)** Most of the primary structure is missing, so the tertiary structure and any active site or binding site cannot form. [1]
*Examiner insight:* Show the triplet count and the stop codon explicitly, so it is clear the stop codon was considered.

**7. (a)** A cell that can divide and produce any type of body cell. [1]
**(b)** (i) Pluripotent. [1] (ii) Multipotent. [1] (iii) Unipotent. [1]
**(c)** All the cells have the same DNA (genes) because they arise from the zygote by mitosis. [1] Each type of specialised cell translates only part of its DNA, so it makes different proteins. [1]
*Examiner insight:* Say that cells translate only part of their DNA; saying specialised cells "lose" genes contradicts the idea being tested.

**8. (a)** 40 − 5 = 35 uncontaminated explants. [1] 31 ÷ 35 × 100 = **88.6%**. [1]
**(b)** Any two of: sterilise the explants; sterilise instruments and work near a flame; use sterile agar; seal the dish. [2]
**(c)** The cells can divide and give rise to new cells (callus), as totipotent cells do. [1]
*Examiner insight:* Decide the denominator before calculating; the question asks about the uncontaminated explants, not all 40.

**9. (a)** Take adult somatic cells, such as skin cells. [1] Treat them with appropriate protein transcription factors. [1] These switch on genes so that the cells become pluripotent. [1]
**(b)** No embryo is destroyed, or the cells come from the patient so there is less risk of rejection. [1]
*Examiner insight:* Mention adult somatic cells and transcription factors; "taken from embryos" does not describe iPS cells.

**10. (a)** Treated: 27 ÷ 45 × 100 = **60.0%**. [1] Control: 15 ÷ 45 × 100 = **33.3%**. [1]
**(b)** More of the treated group improved, by 26.7 percentage points. [1] Tumours occurred in 3 treated patients and none in the control group, so there is a safety concern. [1] The groups are small and followed for only 12 months, so long-term benefit and risk are unknown. [1] Judgement: promising, but more and larger trials with longer follow-up are needed before the benefit outweighs the risk. [1]
*Examiner insight:* An evaluation needs both sides, a limitation of the evidence and a conclusion; quoting figures from the data gains more than general statements.

**11. (a)** Deleting one base shifts the reading frame, because the number of bases lost is not a multiple of three. [1] Every triplet downstream of the deletion changes. [1] Many amino acids are different, or a stop codon appears early. [1] The primary structure and so the tertiary structure change, so the active site no longer has the shape to bind the substrate. [1]
**(b)** No embryo is destroyed, or less risk of rejection because the cells come from the patient. [1]
**(c)** The iPS cells come from the patient's own cells, so they carry the same deletion. [1] Cells made from them would still make the faulty enzyme unless the gene is corrected. [1]
**(d)** Any two of: pluripotent cells may form tumours; the process is inefficient; long-term safety is unknown; ethical objections if embryonic cells are used. [2]
*Examiner insight:* Follow the chain: mutation, frame shift, amino acids, protein shape, function. Skipping a link leaves the argument incomplete.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Forgetting that a degenerate code can leave the polypeptide unchanged.
- Calling a three-base deletion a frame shift.
- Describing a frame shift as affecting one amino acid only.
- Using the wrong strand when transcribing from a template.
- Dividing by the wrong total in a percentage calculation.
- Describing iPS cells as embryonic.
- Giving one side only in an evaluation, with no judgement.
