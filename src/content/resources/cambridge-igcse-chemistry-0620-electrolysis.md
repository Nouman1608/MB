This guide covers subtopic **4.1 Electrolysis** in Topic 4, Electrochemistry, for Cambridge
IGCSE Chemistry 0620 (2026–2028 series). The overview in
[Electrolysis and Fuel Cells](/resources/electrolysis-and-fuel-cells/) sets out the rules;
this page concentrates on the **observations** examiners ask you to describe, a decision
method for **any** halide solution, and how to build half-equations rather than memorise
them.

## What the syllabus asks for

**Core**

- Define **electrolysis** as the decomposition of an ionic compound, when molten or in
  aqueous solution, by the passage of an electric current.
- Identify the **anode** (positive electrode), **cathode** (negative electrode) and
  **electrolyte** (the molten or aqueous substance that undergoes electrolysis).
- Identify the products and describe the observations for **molten lead(II) bromide**,
  **concentrated aqueous sodium chloride** and **dilute sulfuric acid**, using inert
  platinum or carbon/graphite electrodes.
- State that **metals or hydrogen** form at the cathode and **non-metals (other than
  hydrogen)** at the anode.
- Predict the products for a **binary compound in the molten state**.
- State that metal objects are **electroplated** to improve appearance and resistance to
  corrosion, and describe how.

**Supplement** (Extended candidates only)

- Describe the **transfer of charge**: electrons in the external circuit, loss or gain of
  electrons at the electrodes, and movement of ions in the electrolyte.
- Identify products and observations for **aqueous copper(II) sulfate** with inert
  carbon/graphite electrodes and with **copper electrodes**.
- Predict products for a **halide compound in dilute or concentrated aqueous solution**.
- Construct **ionic half-equations** at the anode (oxidation) and cathode (reduction).

## The cell and the charge transfer

The electrolyte must contain **ions that are free to move**, so the compound must be molten
or dissolved. Solid ionic compounds do not undergo electrolysis.

*(Supplement)* Follow the charge round the circuit:

1. The power supply pushes **electrons** through the wire to the **cathode**, making it
   negative.
2. **Positive ions move** through the electrolyte to the cathode and **gain electrons**
   there (reduction).
3. **Negative ions move** to the anode and **lose electrons** there (oxidation).
4. Those electrons travel from the **anode** back through the **external circuit** to the
   power supply.

Electrons never travel through the electrolyte; ions carry the charge there.

## Products and observations (Core)

Observations are what you would **see**, not the names of products. Learn both.

| Electrolyte (inert electrodes) | Cathode product and observation | Anode product and observation |
|---|---|---|
| Molten lead(II) bromide | **Lead**: silvery-grey liquid metal collects | **Bromine**: red-brown fumes |
| Concentrated aqueous sodium chloride | **Hydrogen**: bubbles of colourless gas | **Chlorine**: bubbles of pale yellow-green gas |
| Dilute sulfuric acid | **Hydrogen**: bubbles of colourless gas | **Oxygen**: bubbles of colourless gas |

Extra points that earn credit:

- Lead(II) bromide must be **heated until molten** before a current flows; the solid does
  not conduct.
- In concentrated sodium chloride, sodium ions stay in solution, so the solution left
  contains **sodium hydroxide**.
- In dilute sulfuric acid, about **twice the volume** of gas collects at the cathode as at
  the anode.

## Predicting molten binary products (Core)

A **binary** compound contains just two elements. When molten, the only ions are the metal
cation and the non-metal anion, so: **metal at the cathode, non-metal at the anode**.

- Molten zinc chloride → zinc (cathode) and chlorine (anode).
- Molten potassium iodide → potassium (cathode) and iodine (anode).
- Molten calcium bromide → calcium (cathode) and bromine (anode).

## Predicting aqueous halide products (Supplement)

Water provides H⁺ and OH⁻ ions in addition to the compound's own ions. Use two decisions.

**Cathode:** Is the metal **less reactive than hydrogen** (copper, silver)?
Yes → the **metal** forms. No → **hydrogen** forms.

**Anode:** Is the halide solution **concentrated**?
Yes → the **halogen** forms. No (dilute) → **oxygen** forms.

| Solution | Cathode | Anode |
|---|---|---|
| Concentrated potassium bromide | hydrogen | bromine |
| Dilute sodium chloride | hydrogen | oxygen |
| Concentrated copper(II) chloride | copper | chlorine |
| Dilute copper(II) chloride | copper | oxygen |

**Worked example 1.** *Predict the products of electrolysing concentrated aqueous
copper(II) chloride with carbon electrodes, and describe what you would see.*

- Ions: Cu²⁺, Cl⁻, H⁺, OH⁻.
- Cathode: copper is less reactive than hydrogen → **copper**: a pink-brown coating on
  the electrode.
- Anode: concentrated chloride → **chlorine**: bubbles of pale yellow-green gas that
  bleaches damp litmus paper.

## Copper(II) sulfate (Supplement)

| | Carbon electrodes | Copper electrodes |
|---|---|---|
| Cathode | Copper deposits (pink-brown coating) | Copper deposits; cathode gains mass |
| Anode | Oxygen bubbles | Copper anode dissolves; anode loses mass |
| Solution | Blue colour fades as Cu²⁺ is removed | Blue colour stays the same |

With copper electrodes, every Cu²⁺ ion deposited at the cathode is replaced by one entering
from the anode, so the concentration of Cu²⁺ does not change.

## Building half-equations (Supplement)

1. Write the ion on one side and the element (in its usual form) on the other.
2. Balance the atoms (2Cl⁻ for one Cl₂).
3. Add electrons so the total charge is the same on both sides.
4. Electrons appear on the **left at the cathode** (gained: reduction) and on the **right
   at the anode** (lost: oxidation).

```
cathode:  Pb²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Pb          Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu       2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H2
anode:    2Br⁻ → Br2 + 2e⁻         2Cl⁻ → Cl2 + 2e⁻
          4OH⁻ → O2 + 2H2O + 4e⁻   (oxygen from dilute solutions)
          Cu → Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻          (copper anode dissolving)
```

**Worked example 2.** *Silver ions are reduced at a cathode. Write the half-equation.*
Ag⁺ has charge 1+, so one electron is needed: **Ag⁺ + e⁻ → Ag**. Check: left 1+ − 1 = 0;
right 0.

## Electroplating (Core)

**Why:** to improve **appearance** and **resistance to corrosion**.

**How**, for example copper-plating a steel key:

- **Cathode:** the key (the object to be plated), cleaned first.
- **Anode:** a piece of **pure copper** (the plating metal).
- **Electrolyte:** a solution containing **copper ions**, such as copper(II) sulfate.
- Pass a direct current: copper ions are deposited on the key while the copper anode
  dissolves and replaces them.

The pattern always matches: object = cathode; plating metal = anode; electrolyte contains
ions of the plating metal.

## Common mistakes

- **"Sodium forms at the cathode"** from aqueous sodium chloride.
- **Products without observations**, or observations that are just product names.
- **Electrons moving through the electrolyte** in charge-transfer answers.
- **The object as the anode** in electroplating: it would dissolve.
- **Half-equations with charge not balanced**, or electrons on the wrong side.
- **Chlorine from dilute sodium chloride** — dilute halide solutions give oxygen.

## Exam technique: command words

- **Define**: include *ionic compound*, *molten or aqueous* and *electric current*.
- **Identify**: name the product at the named electrode — one word each.
- **Describe the observations**: colours, bubbles, coatings, colour changes in solution.
- **Predict**: apply the two decisions above and say which ion is discharged.
- **Construct**: balanced half-equations with electrons on the correct side.
