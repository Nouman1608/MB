**This page is for HL only.** Regional study 7, The Russian Revolution (1855 to 1924), is one of twelve studies in the Europe option examined on HL Paper 3, where you write two essays from different regional studies. The guide has six inquiry topics: the policies of Alexander II, Alexander III and Nicholas II; the causes and impact of the 1905 Revolution; repression and reform from 1905 to 1917; the reasons for the October/November Revolution and the Bolshevik rise to power; the survival and consolidation of Bolshevik rule; and the impact of Bolshevik rule to 1924. Russian dates before February 1918 follow the old calendar, which ran thirteen days behind the western one; this guide gives the western date where it helps.

## Policies of the last three tsars (1855 to 1917)

*Alexander II (1855 to 1881).* Defeat in the Crimean War (1853 to 1856) showed that Russia was backward. Alexander introduced the "Great Reforms".
- *Social reform.* The **Emancipation of the serfs** in 1861 freed millions of peasants. But they had to pay redemption payments over decades, and the village commune (*mir*) kept control over land use, so many remained poor and tied to the village. New local councils (**zemstva**, 1864), judicial reform (1864) with independent courts and juries, and military reform (1874) with shorter service also followed.
- *Economic modernization.* Railways and foreign investment grew, but industry remained small.
- *Repression.* After a Polish revolt in 1863 and attempts on his life, he became more cautious. The Third Section (secret police) continued to operate.
- *Opposition.* Radical students ("going to the people", 1874) failed to rouse the peasants; the terrorist group **People's Will** assassinated Alexander on 1 March 1881.

*Alexander III (1881 to 1894).* He rejected reform. The Statute of State Security (1881) gave police extra powers; land captains (1889) gave nobles control over peasants; the zemstvo franchise was narrowed; non-Russian peoples faced Russification, and Jews faced restrictions and pogroms. **Sergei Witte**, finance minister from 1892, pushed rapid industrialization with foreign loans, the gold standard and the Trans-Siberian Railway. Growth created a new working class in cities, which later fed unrest.

*Nicholas II (1894 to 1917).* He believed firmly in autocracy. Opposition grew: Marxists formed the Russian Social Democratic Labour Party (1898), which split in 1903 into Lenin's **Bolsheviks** and the **Mensheviks**; the Socialist Revolutionaries (SRs) appealed to the peasants; liberals demanded a constitution. A famine in 1891 to 1892 and industrial hardship had already raised discontent.

## Causes and impact of the 1905 Revolution

*Role of ideas.* Marxism, populism and liberalism all challenged the regime. They gave leaders a language, but their effect on workers and peasants in 1905 should not be overstated.

*Social and economic factors.* Workers faced low pay, long hours and overcrowded housing. Peasants faced land hunger. A poor harvest and a recession in 1900 to 1903 added to the pressure.

*Significance of the Russo-Japanese War.* The war of 1904 to 1905 ended in defeat, with the fall of Port Arthur and the destruction of the Baltic fleet at Tsushima. It exposed the weakness of the regime and removed troops from the cities, so it was a trigger rather than the only cause.

*Course.* On **Bloody Sunday** (22 January 1905 by the old calendar; 9 January by the western one) troops fired on a peaceful workers' march led by Father Gapon in St Petersburg. This broke the image of the Tsar as protector. Strikes, peasant revolts and mutinies (such as the battleship *Potemkin*) followed, and a St Petersburg Soviet of workers' deputies formed.

*Political factors.* The October Manifesto (October 1905), drafted by Witte, promised civil liberties and an elected Duma with real law-making power. It split the opposition: liberals were satisfied, while radicals were not. The army remained loyal and crushed the Moscow uprising in December. The **Fundamental Laws** of 1906 kept the Tsar's autocratic powers: he appointed ministers, controlled foreign policy and the army, could dissolve the Duma and could rule by decree when it was not sitting.

## Repression and reform (1905 to 1917)

*Dumas.* The first two Dumas (1906, 1907) were radical and were dissolved. The Tsar then changed the electoral law in June 1907 to favour landowners, producing a more conservative Third Duma that lasted its full term. The Duma showed that a constitutional element survived, but it had little control.

*Role of Stolypin.* **Pyotr Stolypin**, prime minister from 1906 to 1911, combined repression and reform. He used field courts-martial against revolutionaries (the "Stolypin necktie"). His agrarian reforms ended redemption payments, helped peasants leave the commune and set up private farms, and encouraged settlement in Siberia, hoping to create a class of prosperous farmers loyal to the regime. Results were mixed: a minority of peasants benefited, while most stayed in communes. He was assassinated in 1911.

*Industrial reform and unrest.* Industry grew fast from 1909, but workers' conditions stayed poor, and the Lena goldfields shooting in 1912 led to a new wave of strikes.

*Use of violence.* The **Okhrana**, the secret police, infiltrated revolutionary groups and arrested leaders, which kept the Bolsheviks weak inside Russia before 1917. Historians debate whether Russia was heading for gradual reform before 1914 (the optimist view) or for a revolution (the pessimist view). Evidence for each is useful in an essay.

## Reasons for the October/November Revolution and the Bolshevik rise

*Impact of the First World War.* Russia entered the war in 1914. Early defeats at Tannenberg and the Masurian Lakes, then the great retreat of 1915, brought heavy casualties. In September 1915 Nicholas took personal command of the army, which tied him to defeat and left the government in the hands of the Tsarina Alexandra and Rasputin, whose influence discredited the court. Inflation, food shortages and failures of transport caused misery in cities.

*February/March Revolution and Dual Power.* In February 1917 (old style), International Women's Day protests turned into strikes in Petrograd. Soldiers refused to fire and joined the crowds. The Tsar abdicated on 2 March (15 March western date). Two bodies shared authority: the **Provisional Government**, drawn from Duma politicians, and the **Petrograd Soviet** of workers and soldiers. This was **Dual Power**. The Soviet's Order No. 1 gave soldiers' committees power over orders, which weakened the army's discipline.

*Failures of the Provisional Government.* The Government decided to carry on the war, and the June offensive failed. It put off land reform and elections to a Constituent Assembly. Prices kept rising. The July Days revealed the Bolsheviks' strength, but Lenin fled. In the Kornilov Affair (late August), General Kornilov's attempted march on Petrograd was stopped with Bolshevik help, which restored their credit and left the Government's authority gone.

*Roles of Lenin and Trotsky.* **Lenin** returned in April 1917 and issued the April Theses, demanding "Peace, Land and Bread" and "All power to the Soviets". He pushed his party to take power. **Trotsky**, chairman of the Petrograd Soviet from September and head of its Military Revolutionary Committee, organised the seizure of the key points. On 25 October (7 November), the Bolsheviks took the Winter Palace almost without resistance. Historians argue between a "coup" view (Pipes, and others) and a "mass movement" view (such as Rabinowitch and later social historians), and between stress on Lenin's leadership and stress on circumstances.

## Survival and consolidation of Bolshevik rule

*Treaty of Brest-Litovsk (March 1918).* Lenin accepted harsh terms to end the war: Russia lost Poland, the Baltic lands, Ukraine and Finland, a large share of its population (about a third by some counts) and much of its industry. It was a gamble to save the revolution; opponents inside the party and the SRs bitterly objected.

*Terror and coercion.* The Cheka (secret police) was set up in December 1917. Elections to the Constituent Assembly in November gave the SRs most seats and the Bolsheviks around a quarter; the Assembly met once, in January 1918, and was dissolved. After attempts on Lenin's life in August 1918, the Red Terror began. The royal family was shot in July 1918.

*Russian Civil War (1917 to 1922).* The Reds faced the Whites (various anti-Bolshevik forces under leaders such as Kolchak, Denikin and Wrangel), as well as Allied intervention. The Reds won due to central position and control of railways and factories, the leadership of Trotsky and a disciplined Red Army, the Whites' disunity, and the fact that many peasants feared a White return of landowners.

*War Communism and the NEP.* War Communism (1918 to 1921) meant nationalised industry, forced grain requisitioning and rationing. It fed the army but caused famine and revolt, including the Kronstadt rising in March 1921. Lenin replaced it with the **New Economic Policy (NEP)**: a tax in kind and freedom for small-scale trade and private farming, while the state kept the "commanding heights". Within the party, factions were banned.

## Impact of Bolshevik rule to 1924

*Social policies and religion.* The Church was separated from the state (1918), Church property was seized and clergy were persecuted. Education and literacy campaigns were promoted.

*Women.* The new regime gave legal equality, easier divorce and civil marriage, and (in 1920) legal abortion. The Zhenotdel (women's section) promoted women's involvement. In practice, women faced hardship and the party leadership remained male.

*Marginalized groups.* Anti-Semitism was officially condemned after the civil war, when Jews had suffered pogroms, especially in Ukraine, mainly by White and nationalist forces. National minorities were promised cultural rights within a new Union (1922), but Moscow kept control. Former nobles and "bourgeois" people lost rights and property.

*Different classes.* Peasants gained land but suffered requisitioning, and the NEP brought some recovery. Workers were told they ruled, but unions and strikes were controlled. The NEP produced a "Nepman" class of traders. Lenin died in January 1924 after a series of strokes, leaving a succession struggle.
