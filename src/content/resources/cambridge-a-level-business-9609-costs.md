## Classifying costs: the awkward cases

The four types (fixed, variable, direct, indirect) do not always fit neatly.

- **Semi-variable costs** have a fixed part and a variable part, such as an electricity bill with a standing charge plus a charge per unit used. For analysis, split them into their two parts.
- **Stepped fixed costs** stay fixed only within a range of output. One supervisor can oversee up to 3 000 units a month; above that, a second is needed and fixed costs jump.
- **Labour** may be fixed (salaried staff kept whatever the output) or variable (piece-rate workers, or agency staff hired for busy periods).

These cases explain why cost information is never perfectly accurate, and why decisions based on it carry some risk.

## Why accurate cost information matters

- **Pricing**: if costs are understated, prices may not cover them.
- **Product decisions**: a product may be dropped because it looks unprofitable when in fact it helps cover overheads.
- **Performance**: managers cannot be judged fairly if their departments are charged for costs they do not control.
- **Planning**: budgets and break-even targets built on wrong costs give false confidence.

## Full costing: how the allocation basis changes the answer

Full costing shares indirect costs (overheads) between products. The result depends on the basis chosen.

**Worked example.** Torrin Kitchenware makes two products. Overheads are $24 000 a month.

| | Alpha pans | Beta trays |
|---|---|---|
| Units a month | 2 000 | 1 000 |
| Price | $25 | $30 |
| Variable cost per unit | $14 | $22 |
| Labour hours per unit | 0.5 | 0.5 |
| Share of factory floor space | 40% | 60% |
| Total contribution | 2 000 × 11 = $22 000 | 1 000 × 8 = $8 000 |

**Basis 1: labour hours.** Total hours = 1 000 + 500 = 1 500, so $16 per hour, giving Alpha $16 000 and Beta $8 000 of overheads.
- Alpha profit = 22 000 − 16 000 = $6 000. Beta profit = 8 000 − 8 000 = $0.

**Basis 2: floor space.** Alpha gets 40% = $9 600; Beta gets 60% = $14 400.
- Alpha profit = $12 400. Beta profit = 8 000 − 14 400 = **−$6 400**.

Under basis 2, Beta appears to lose money. But total profit is $6 000 either way: 22 000 + 8 000 − 24 000.

**What if Torrin drops Beta?** The overheads do not disappear: rent and heating for the factory are still $24 000. Profit becomes 22 000 − 24 000 = **−$2 000**. Dropping a product with a positive contribution reduces profit. This is the central limitation of full costing and the main reason for using contribution costing in such decisions.

Full costing still has uses: it reminds managers that every product must, in the long run, help cover all costs; it supports cost-plus pricing; and it gives a complete figure for each product's cost.

## Contribution and profit

- **Contribution per unit** = price − variable cost per unit.
- **Total contribution** = contribution per unit × units sold.
- **Profit** = total contribution − fixed costs.

Contribution is the amount each unit adds towards paying fixed costs and then towards profit. A product can have a positive contribution but the business can still make a loss if total contribution is below fixed costs.

### When contribution costing is and is not used

| Used | Not used on its own |
|---|---|
| Deciding whether to keep or drop a product | Setting long-term prices that must cover all costs |
| One-off special orders with spare capacity | When fixed costs would change because of the decision |
| Choosing which products to promote | When there is no spare capacity |

## Average, marginal and total cost

**Worked example.** Fixed costs are $20 000 a month and variable cost is $5 a unit.

| Output | Total cost | Average cost |
|---|---|---|
| 1 000 | $25 000 | $25.00 |
| 2 000 | $30 000 | $15.00 |
| 4 000 | $40 000 | $10.00 |

Marginal cost, the cost of one more unit, stays at $5 while fixed costs do not change. Average cost falls because fixed costs are spread over more units. Two decisions follow: a business should not price below its marginal cost on any sale, and its long-run average price must exceed average cost if it is to make a profit.

## Costs and pricing

- **Cost-plus pricing** adds a mark-up to unit cost. It is simple and aims to cover costs, but ignores what customers will pay and what rivals charge.
- **Contribution pricing** sets a price above variable cost to win extra sales, accepting that the item adds less to fixed costs. It suits spare capacity, off-peak periods and one-off orders.

## Special orders when capacity is full

The standard special-order rule (accept if price exceeds variable cost) assumes **spare capacity**. When capacity is limited, extra work may push out full-price sales.

**Worked example.** Penhallow Pumps can make 5 000 pumps a month and sells 4 600 at $50. Variable cost is $30. A customer offers $38 each for 800 pumps.

- Spare capacity is only 400 pumps, so 400 regular sales would be lost.
- Contribution gained from the order = 800 × (38 − 30) = $6 400.
- Contribution lost from regular sales = 400 × (50 − 30) = $8 000.
- Net effect on profit = 6 400 − 8 000 = **−$1 600**.

On financial grounds Penhallow should reject the order, or accept only 400 pumps. Non-financial factors could still change the decision: the customer might become a regular buyer, or regular customers might be willing to wait.

## Break-even: interpreting changes

**Worked example.** Brisa Lamps sells at $40. Variable cost is $16 and fixed costs are $36 000 a month. Monthly sales are 2 000.

- Contribution = 40 − 16 = $24.
- Break-even = 36 000 ÷ 24 = **1 500 lamps**.
- Margin of safety = 2 000 − 1 500 = 500 lamps, which is 25% of sales and 500 × 40 = $20 000 of revenue.
- Profit = 2 000 × 24 − 36 000 = **$12 000**.

**What if Brisa cuts the price to $36 to win sales?**

- Contribution falls to $20, so break-even rises to 36 000 ÷ 20 = **1 800 lamps**.
- To keep profit at $12 000, sales must be (36 000 + 12 000) ÷ 20 = **2 400 lamps**, a 20% rise.

On a break-even chart, a lower price makes the revenue line flatter, so it crosses total costs further to the right. Higher fixed costs raise the total cost line in parallel; a higher variable cost makes it steeper. Both move the break-even point to the right.

### Uses and limitations in context

Break-even is useful for a start-up applying for a loan, for testing "what if" changes and for setting sales targets. Its assumptions are its limitations: it treats price and variable cost per unit as constant, assumes all output is sold, and works best for a single product. In the Brisa example, the 20% sales rise needed after a price cut depends on how customers respond, which break-even cannot predict.

## Common mistakes

- Dropping a product because full costing shows a loss, without checking its contribution.
- Treating contribution as profit.
- Accepting a special order at full capacity without counting lost regular sales.
- Rounding break-even output down instead of up.
- Saying a lower price lowers break-even output.

## Exam technique: command words

- **Calculate**: show the formula and units; round break-even output up to whole units.
- **Explain**: say why a cost is fixed or variable, or why a method suits the decision.
- **Analyse**: trace how a price or cost change affects contribution, break-even and profit.
- **Evaluate** or **Assess**: weigh the financial result against non-financial factors and the reliability of the cost data.
- **Advise** or **Justify**: recommend accepting, rejecting or changing an order or product, using the figures from the case.
