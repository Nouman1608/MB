Enthalpy values are in kJ mol⁻¹ and absolute entropies in J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ throughout.

## Questions

**1.** This question is about the terms used in Born–Haber cycles.

**(a)** Define the first ionisation energy of an element. **[2]**
**(b)** Define the lattice enthalpy of formation of an ionic compound. **[2]**
**(c)** Write an equation, with state symbols, for the first electron affinity of sulfur. **[1]**

**2.** Use these data for lithium oxide, Li₂O.
ΔfH⦵(Li₂O) –598; ΔatH⦵(Li) +159; first IE(Li) +520; O=O bond enthalpy +498; first EA(O) –141;
second EA(O) +798.

**(a)** Calculate the lattice enthalpy of formation of Li₂O. **[4]**
**(b)** Explain why only half the O=O bond enthalpy is used in the cycle. **[1]**

**3.** For rubidium bromide: ΔfH⦵(RbBr) –395; ΔatH⦵(Rb) +81; first IE(Rb) +403; ΔatH⦵(Br) +112;
lattice enthalpy of formation of RbBr –660. Calculate the first electron affinity of bromine. **[3]**

**4.** The table gives lattice enthalpies of formation.

| Compound | Born–Haber cycle | Perfect ionic model |
|---|---|---|
| KF | –821 | –817 |
| CuCl | –996 | –921 |

**(a)** State two assumptions of the perfect ionic model. **[2]**
**(b)** Which compound has the greater degree of covalent bonding? Justify your choice from the data. **[3]**
**(c)** Predict whether LiF or LiI shows the larger difference between the two values. Explain
your answer. **[2]**

**5.** This question is about strontium bromide.

**(a)** The lattice enthalpy of dissociation of SrBr₂ is +2075. ΔhydH(Sr²⁺) = –1445 and
ΔhydH(Br⁻) = –335. Calculate the enthalpy of solution of SrBr₂. **[3]**
**(b)** ΔhydH(F⁻) = –506. Explain why the hydration enthalpy of Br⁻ is less exothermic than that of
F⁻. **[2]**

**6.** For potassium iodide, ΔsolH = +20, ΔhydH(K⁺) = –322 and ΔhydH(I⁻) = –293. Calculate the
lattice enthalpy of formation of KI. **[3]**

**7.** Entropy and feasibility.

**(a)** State and explain the sign of ΔS for each change.
(i) I₂(s) → I₂(g)
(ii) 2H₂O₂(l) → 2H₂O(l) + O₂(g)
(iii) 2Mg(s) + O₂(g) → 2MgO(s) **[3]**
**(b)** Explain why the sign of ΔH alone cannot tell you whether a change is feasible. Use an example
in your answer. **[2]**

**8.** Mercury(II) oxide decomposes on heating: 2HgO(s) → 2Hg(l) + O₂(g), ΔH⦵ = +182.
Absolute entropies: HgO(s) 70; Hg(l) 76; O₂(g) 205.

**(a)** Calculate ΔS for the reaction. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate ΔG at 298 K. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the lowest temperature at which the decomposition is feasible. **[2]**

**9.** Methanol can form from carbon monoxide and hydrogen: CO(g) + 2H₂(g) → CH₃OH(g), ΔH⦵ = –91.
Absolute entropies: CO(g) 198; H₂(g) 131; CH₃OH(g) 240.

**(a)** Calculate ΔS for the reaction. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate ΔG at 298 K and state whether the reaction is feasible. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain how ΔG changes as the temperature is raised. **[2]**
**(d)** Calculate the temperature above which the reaction is no longer feasible. **[2]**

**10.** Tin is extracted from tin(IV) oxide by heating with carbon.
Reaction A: SnO₂(s) + 2C(s) → Sn(s) + 2CO(g)
Reaction B: SnO₂(s) + C(s) → Sn(s) + CO₂(g)
ΔfH⦵: SnO₂(s) –581; CO(g) –111; CO₂(g) –394.
Absolute entropies: SnO₂(s) 49; C(s) 6; Sn(s) 52; CO(g) 198; CO₂(g) 214.

**(a)** Calculate ΔH for reaction A. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate ΔS for reaction A. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the lowest temperature at which reaction A is feasible. **[2]**
**(d)** Calculate the lowest temperature at which reaction B is feasible, and state which reaction
becomes feasible first as the mixture is heated. **[3]**
**(e)** Suggest why a reaction with a negative ΔG may still not be observed. **[1]**

**11.** Potassium bromide dissolves in water and the solution cools.

**(a)** The lattice enthalpy of dissociation of KBr is +679. Use ΔhydH(K⁺) and ΔhydH(Br⁻) from
questions 5 and 6 to calculate ΔsolH(KBr). **[2]**
**(b)** Absolute entropies: KBr(s) 96; K⁺(aq) 103; Br⁻(aq) 82. Calculate ΔS for
KBr(s) → K⁺(aq) + Br⁻(aq). **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate ΔG at 298 K. **[2]**
**(d)** Explain what your answers show about why KBr dissolves. **[1]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** The enthalpy change to remove 1 mole of electrons from 1 mole of gaseous atoms [1] to form
1 mole of gaseous 1+ ions [1].
**(b)** The enthalpy change when 1 mole of an ionic solid is formed [1] from its gaseous ions [1].
**(c)** S(g) + e⁻ → S⁻(g) [1]
*Examiner insight:* "gaseous" is part of both definitions; an answer that leaves out the state of
the atoms or ions describes a different process and loses the point.

**2. (a)** Two Li atoms: 2 × 159 = 318 and 2 × 520 = 1040 [1]
One O atom: ½ × 498 = 249; sum = 318 + 1040 + 249 – 141 + 798 = +2264 [1]
Lattice enthalpy of formation = –598 – 2264 [1]
= **–2862 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]
**(b)** Li₂O contains one O atom, and breaking one O=O bond gives two atoms [1].
*Examiner insight:* show each multiplier next to its term; a final answer with no visible working
gives no way to recover credit if one step was doubled wrongly.

**3.** –395 = 81 + 403 + 112 + EA – 660 [1]
EA = –395 – 81 – 403 – 112 + 660 [1]
= **–331 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]
*Examiner insight:* bromine's atomisation value is given directly because Br₂ is a liquid; halving a
bond enthalpy here would be wrong.

**4. (a)** The ions are perfect spheres with evenly spread charge [1]. The only attraction is
electrostatic, with no covalent bonding [1].
**(b)** **CuCl** [1]. Its Born–Haber value is 75 kJ mol⁻¹ more exothermic than the model, compared
with 4 kJ mol⁻¹ for KF [1]. Cu⁺ polarises the Cl⁻ electron cloud, adding attraction the model
ignores [1].
**(c)** **LiI** [1]. I⁻ is much larger than F⁻, so its electron cloud is more easily distorted
(polarised) by the small Li⁺ ion [1].
*Examiner insight:* say that the experimental value is *more exothermic*; quoting the size of the gap
without its direction does not show why the extra attraction exists.

**5. (a)** ΔsolH = lattice dissociation + ΔhydH(Sr²⁺) + 2 × ΔhydH(Br⁻) [1]
= 2075 – 1445 – 670 [1]
= **–40 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]
**(b)** Br⁻ is larger than F⁻ [1], so its charge is less concentrated and it attracts the δ+
hydrogen atoms of water less strongly [1].
*Examiner insight:* in (b), water's δ+ hydrogens face an anion; describing δ– oxygen attracted to Br⁻
reverses the interaction.

**6.** ΔsolH = lattice dissociation + ΔhydH(K⁺) + ΔhydH(I⁻), so
lattice dissociation = 20 + 322 + 293 = +635 [1]
Lattice enthalpy of formation is the reverse [1]
= **–635 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]
*Examiner insight:* the question asks for formation, so +635 alone answers a different question;
check which definition is wanted before giving the sign.

**7. (a)** (i) Positive: a solid becomes a gas, so disorder increases [1].
(ii) Positive: a gas forms from liquids [1].
(iii) Negative: a gas is used up to form a solid [1].
**(b)** Some endothermic changes are feasible, so a positive ΔH does not rule a change out [1]. For
example, ice melts above 273 K because the entropy increase outweighs the positive ΔH [1].
*Examiner insight:* a sign of ΔS with no reason is incomplete; refer to states or moles of gas each
time.

**8. (a)** ΔS = [2(76) + 205] – 2(70) [1] = **+217 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹** [1]
**(b)** ΔG = 182 – 298 × 0.217 [1] = **+117 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]
**(c)** T = ΔH/ΔS = 182 / 0.217 [1] = **839 K** [1]
*Examiner insight:* both ΔH and ΔS are positive here, so the reaction is feasible at 839 K and
above; say "lowest" or "above" to show you know the direction.

**9. (a)** ΔS = 240 – [198 + 2(131)] [1] = **–220 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹** [1]
**(b)** ΔG = –91 – 298 × (–0.220) = **–25 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]; negative, so feasible [1].
**(c)** ΔS is negative, so –TΔS is positive [1]. As T rises, this term grows and ΔG becomes less
negative, then positive [1].
**(d)** T = –91 / –0.220 [1] = **414 K** [1]
*Examiner insight:* subtracting a negative TΔS is where sign errors creep in; write ΔG = –91 + 0.220T
before substituting.

**10. (a)** ΔH = 2(–111) – (–581) [1] = **+359 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]
**(b)** ΔS = [52 + 2(198)] – [49 + 2(6)] [1] = **+387 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹** [1]
**(c)** T = 359 / 0.387 [1] = **928 K** [1]
**(d)** Reaction B: ΔH = –394 + 581 = +187 and ΔS = (52 + 214) – (49 + 6) = +211 [1]
T = 187 / 0.211 = **886 K** [1]
**Reaction B** becomes feasible first (at the lower temperature) [1].
**(e)** The activation energy may be so high that the rate is too slow to observe [1].
*Examiner insight:* elements in their standard states have ΔfH⦵ = 0 but non-zero absolute
entropies; setting S(C) or S(Sn) to zero changes ΔS.

**11. (a)** ΔsolH = 679 – 322 – 335 [1] = **+22 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]
**(b)** ΔS = (103 + 82) – 96 [1] = **+89 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹** [1]
**(c)** ΔG = 22 – 298 × 0.089 [1] = **–4.5 kJ mol⁻¹** [1]
**(d)** Dissolving is endothermic but ΔG is negative, so the entropy increase makes it feasible [1].
*Examiner insight:* (d) needs a link between the two results; stating only that ΔG is negative does
not explain why an endothermic change happens.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Leaving "gaseous" out of ionisation energy, electron affinity or lattice enthalpy definitions.
- Doubling only some steps when a formula contains two of a particle (Li₂O, SrBr₂).
- Halving the bond enthalpy of an element whose standard state is not a gas.
- Giving a lattice enthalpy of dissociation when formation was asked for, or the reverse.
- Explaining covalent character without saying the experimental value is more exothermic.
- Counting a hydration enthalpy once for an ion that appears twice.
- Mixing J and kJ in ΔG = ΔH – TΔS, or using °C.
- Stating a temperature without saying whether the reaction is feasible above or below it.
- Setting the absolute entropy of an element to zero.
