Key question 2.1 of Cambridge IGCSE Sociology (0495) asks *How do we learn our identity?* The
sociological answer is that identity is not simply inborn: it is learned through **socialisation**,
the lifelong process by which people learn the culture of their society. To answer exam questions you
need the building-block concepts of culture, the nature–nurture debate, the agencies and processes of
socialisation, and the competing functionalist, Marxist and feminist interpretations of what
socialisation does.

For a short overview of all of Paper 1, see the
[research methods, identity and inequality guide](/resources/igcse-sociology-research-methods-identity-and-inequality/).

## Syllabus coverage

Cambridge IGCSE Sociology 0495, syllabus for 2025, 2026 and 2027, **2.1 How do we learn our
identity?**

- **2.1.1** (a) Society as a product of social construction: culture, norms, values, customs, roles,
  status (achieved/ascribed), stereotypes; (b) how these concepts influence individual behaviour and
  social identity.
- **2.1.2** The social construction of our identity: the nature–nurture debate; the relativity of
  culture; socialisation; norms and values.
- **2.1.3** Primary and secondary socialisation: key agencies (family, education, peer group, media,
  religion, workplace); the effectiveness of each agency.
- **2.1.4** Processes used by agencies, e.g. canalisation, hidden curriculum, media representations,
  peer pressure, religious teachings, role modelling, workplace training.
- **2.1.5** The debate between perspectives: consensus and conflict; functionalist ideas (inadequate
  socialisation, social cohesion/value consensus); Marxist ideas (socialisation into capitalist
  ideology, reproduction of social class); feminist ideas (gender role socialisation into
  masculinity/femininity).

## The building blocks of culture (2.1.1)

**Social construction** means that something is created by people in society rather than fixed by
nature. Seven concepts describe what is constructed.

| Concept | Meaning | Example of influence on behaviour and identity |
|---|---|---|
| **Culture** | The whole way of life of a group: language, beliefs, knowledge, customs, food, dress | Gives people a sense of who "we" are |
| **Values** | Broad beliefs about what is good and important | A value placed on education leads families to invest in tutoring |
| **Norms** | Specific rules of expected behaviour, usually based on values | Queuing; removing shoes before entering a home |
| **Customs** | Traditional, long-established practices passed between generations | Wedding rituals; festival foods |
| **Roles** | Expected patterns of behaviour attached to a position | A "good student" is expected to arrive on time and listen |
| **Status** | A position in society and the prestige attached to it — **ascribed** (given at birth) or **achieved** (earned) | A royal title is ascribed; a doctor's status is achieved |
| **Stereotypes** | Fixed, oversimplified generalisations about a group | Assuming boys are naturally bad at caring roles can steer career choices |

The link between them matters: values underpin norms; norms define roles; roles attach to statuses;
and stereotypes can push people into roles regardless of their own abilities. Together they shape
how people behave and how they see themselves.

## Nature or nurture? (2.1.2)

The **nature** view says behaviour is mainly inborn — genes, instincts, hormones. The **nurture** view,
which sociologists emphasise, says behaviour is mainly learned through socialisation. Three kinds of
evidence support nurture.

- **Feral and isolated children.** "Genie", discovered in Los Angeles in 1970 at the age of 13 after
  being kept in severe isolation, could not speak and had great difficulty learning language and
  social behaviour afterwards. Such cases suggest that without early socialisation humans do not
  develop normal language or social skills. However, cases are rare, and some children may have had
  disabilities from birth, so the evidence must be treated with care.
- **The relativity of culture.** Norms and values differ between societies and over time, which would
  not happen if behaviour were fixed by biology. Margaret Mead's *Sex and Temperament in Three
  Primitive Societies* (1935) described three groups in New Guinea — the Arapesh, Mundugumor and
  Tchambuli — with very different expectations of how men and women should behave. Mead's fieldwork
  has since been questioned, but the wider point that gender norms vary is well supported.
- **Change within one society.** Expectations about women's paid work or men's role in childcare have
  changed within a few generations, far too fast for genetic change.

A balanced answer accepts that biology sets some limits (for example, physical maturation) but
argues that the meaning attached to biology is learned.

## Primary and secondary socialisation (2.1.3)

**Primary socialisation** takes place in early childhood, mainly within the **family**, and teaches
language, basic norms, emotional ties and early gender identity. **Secondary socialisation** continues
through later childhood and adult life through **education**, the **peer group**, the **media**,
**religion** and the **workplace**.

### How effective is each agency?

| Agency | Why it can be effective | Limits on its effectiveness |
|---|---|---|
| **Family** | Earliest and most emotionally powerful; constant contact | Families differ; some fail to socialise children adequately; influence weakens in adolescence |
| **Education** | Many years of daily attendance; formal and hidden curricula | Anti-school subcultures reject school values (Willis, 1977) |
| **Peer group** | Strong in adolescence; acceptance matters greatly | Peer groups vary; individuals belong to several |
| **Media** | Constant exposure; reaches across the world; role models and representations | Audiences interpret and reject messages actively |
| **Religion** | Moral rules backed by belief and community | Influence varies greatly between and within societies |
| **Workplace** | Teaches occupational norms, time discipline, professional identity | Only affects adults in work; people may comply without accepting values |

Effectiveness is always relative: an agency may be strong for one group or at one life stage and weak
for another.

## How do agencies socialise us? (2.1.4)

The syllabus gives examples of processes. Learn to match each process to an agency and explain how it
works.

- **Canalisation** (family) — Ann Oakley (*Sex, Gender and Society*, 1972) described how parents
  channel children's interests towards toys and activities seen as suitable for their sex, such as
  dolls for girls and construction toys for boys. Oakley also identified **manipulation** — encouraging
  behaviour seen as appropriate for the child's sex and discouraging other behaviour.
- **Hidden curriculum** (education) — the lessons taught by the way school is organised rather than
  by the timetable: punctuality, obedience, accepting hierarchy, competition.
- **Media representations** (media) — repeated images of groups, such as particular body types in
  advertising, shape what audiences see as normal or desirable.
- **Peer pressure** (peer group) — the pressure to conform to friends' norms, backed by acceptance or
  ridicule.
- **Religious teachings** (religion) — sacred texts, sermons and rituals teach moral codes, for
  example about honesty or family duty.
- **Role modelling** (all agencies) — learning by copying people we admire, such as parents,
  teachers, celebrities or older siblings.
- **Workplace training** (workplace) — induction, rules and supervision teach the norms of a job,
  from dress codes to customer manners.

## Theories of socialisation (2.1.5)

**Consensus** theories see socialisation as creating shared values that benefit everyone; **conflict**
theories see it as serving powerful groups.

**Functionalism (consensus).** Talcott Parsons argued that the family's basic functions include the
primary socialisation of children, so that they internalise society's values. Émile Durkheim saw
education as passing on shared values and creating **social cohesion** (solidarity). Functionalists
explain deviance partly through **inadequate socialisation** — if families or schools fail to pass on
values, individuals may not conform.

**Marxism (conflict).** Marxists argue socialisation transmits **capitalist ideology** — ideas that
make inequality seem fair, such as the belief that success depends only on effort. Louis Althusser
(1971) called institutions such as schools, religion and the media "ideological state apparatuses".
Samuel Bowles and Herbert Gintis (*Schooling in Capitalist America*, 1976) argued that the hidden
curriculum prepares working-class students to be obedient workers, so schooling **reproduces social
class** rather than overcoming it.

**Feminism (conflict).** Feminists argue that **gender role socialisation** teaches girls femininity
(caring, appearance, domestic roles) and boys masculinity (toughness, competition), maintaining
patriarchy. Oakley's canalisation and manipulation are examples. Sue Sharpe's study *Just Like a Girl*
(1976) found that girls' priorities were love, marriage, husbands and children; when she repeated the
research in the early 1990s, girls' priorities had shifted towards jobs, careers and independence —
evidence that gender socialisation changes over time.

**Evaluation points.** Functionalism assumes everyone shares the same values and underplays conflict.
Marxism can make people seem passive — Willis's "lads" rejected school values rather than absorbing
them. Feminism has shown that gender socialisation changes, as Sharpe's repeat study suggests, and
some critics say it underplays individual choice.

## Worked example: a Question 2(e)-style "discuss"

*Discuss the role of the media as an agency of socialisation.*

> The media is an important agency of secondary socialisation because people of all ages spend many
> hours each day watching, reading and scrolling. It works through **media representations**: when
> advertisements repeatedly show slim, young bodies as attractive, viewers may come to see this as the
> norm and judge themselves against it. It also provides **role models**, such as athletes or
> influencers whose lifestyles young people copy.
>
> Marxists argue the media spreads capitalist ideology, for example by encouraging consumerism. Feminists
> point to stereotyped representations of women.
>
> However, audiences are not passive. People interpret messages differently according to their family,
> religion and peer group, and many young people now choose the content they consume and challenge
> stereotypes online. The media's influence is therefore strong but filtered through other agencies.

**Why it works:** it names processes from the syllabus, applies two perspectives, and evaluates.

## Common mistakes

- Defining norms and values the same way. Values are broad ideals; norms are specific rules.
- Saying ascribed status is "what others think of you". It is status given at birth.
- Listing agencies without explaining **processes** or **effectiveness**.
- Treating feral children as proof that biology plays no role.
- Forgetting that Marxists and feminists are both **conflict** theories.

## Exam technique

- **Define** (precise meaning) — one sentence plus a short example.
- **Explain** (reasons, how or why, with evidence) — process, how it works, an example.
- **Discuss** — several developed points, ideally including perspectives, in paragraphs.
- **Evaluate** — for example "Evaluate the view that the family is the most important agency of
  socialisation": arguments for, arguments against (other agencies, adolescence, media), and a
  judgement.
