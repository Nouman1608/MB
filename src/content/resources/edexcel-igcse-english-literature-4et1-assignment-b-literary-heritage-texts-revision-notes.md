Assignment B in Pearson Edexcel International GCSE English Literature (4ET1) Component 3 is **one
coursework essay of about 650–800 words on a literary heritage set text, marked out of 30 (AO1 10, AO2
10, AO4 10)**. It is the only Component 3 essay that assesses context. For the full explanation, use
the [Assignment B literary heritage study guide](/resources/edexcel-igcse-english-literature-4et1-assignment-b-literary-heritage-texts/).

## At a glance

| Detail | Assignment B |
|---|---|
| Text | One literary heritage set text |
| Marks | 30: AO1 10, AO2 10, AO4 10 |
| Task wording | Asks for language, form and structure **and** reference to context |
| Length | 650–800 words advisory |
| Marking | By the centre, moderated by Pearson |

## What each objective asks for

| AO | Marks | In practice |
|---|---|---|
| AO1 | 10 | A clear, informed argument that answers the task, with precise reference across the whole text |
| AO2 | 10 | Analysis of language, form and structure and their effects: imagery, dialogue, narrative voice, scene and chapter structure |
| AO4 | 10 | How the contexts of writing and reception shape what the text means, linked to specific moments |

Because the three objectives carry equal marks, a paragraph that does only two of them leaves a third
of its potential unused.

## Key moments to build an essay around

These are reference points to locate in your own edition; chapter numbering can vary slightly
between editions of the novels.

| Text | Beginning | Turning point | Ending |
|---|---|---|---|
| *Romeo and Juliet* | The Prologue's sonnet announces the lovers' deaths; the meeting at the Capulet feast (Act 1 Scene 5) | The deaths of Mercutio and Tybalt and Romeo's banishment (Act 3 Scene 1); Capulet's fury when Juliet refuses Paris (Act 3 Scene 5) | The tomb and the reconciliation of the fathers (Act 5 Scene 3) |
| *Macbeth* | The witches' prophecies (Act 1 Scene 3); Lady Macbeth's persuasion (Act 1 Scene 7) | The aftermath of Duncan's murder (Act 2 Scene 2); Banquo's ghost at the banquet (Act 3 Scene 4) | The sleepwalking scene (Act 5 Scene 1); Macbeth's response to his wife's death (Act 5 Scene 5) |
| *The Merchant of Venice* | The bond agreed between Shylock and Antonio (Act 1 Scene 3) | Shylock's speech on shared humanity and revenge (Act 3 Scene 1) | The trial, with its forced conversion (Act 4 Scene 1); the return to Belmont (Act 5) |
| *Pride and Prejudice* | Darcy's slight at the Meryton assembly (Chapter 3); Mr Collins's proposal (Chapter 19) | Darcy's first proposal and his letter (Chapters 34 and 35); the visit to Pemberley (Chapter 43) | Lady Catherine's confrontation (Chapter 56); the second proposal (Chapter 58) |
| *Great Expectations* | Pip meets the convict in the churchyard (Chapter 1); Satis House and Estella's contempt (Chapter 8) | Magwitch revealed as Pip's benefactor (Chapter 39) | Magwitch's death and Pip's changed loyalties; the final meeting with Estella |
| *The Scarlet Letter* | Hester on the scaffold in the market-place (Chapter 2) | Dimmesdale's midnight vigil on the scaffold (Chapter 12); the forest meeting (Chapters 17 and 18) | Dimmesdale's public confession on the scaffold (Chapter 23) |

The scaffold in *The Scarlet Letter* is a useful example of structure: it returns at the beginning,
middle and end, and each return changes who stands on it and who is watching.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| AO4 | The relationship between a text and the contexts of its writing and reception |
| Integrated context | Context that explains a specific moment or method in the same paragraph |
| Social and cultural context | Attitudes and beliefs of the time, such as views on marriage, class or religion |
| Literary context | Genre or tradition: tragedy, comedy of manners, the novel of growing up |
| Reception | How readers and audiences at different times respond to the text |
| Writer's situation | Details of the writer's life, relevant only when they shed light on the text |

## The five kinds of context, with an example for each

| Kind | Example |
|---|---|
| Writer's situation | Hawthorne's family link to the Puritan past he writes about |
| Historical setting | Seventeenth-century Boston in *The Scarlet Letter* |
| Social and cultural attitudes | A father's authority over his daughter's marriage in *Romeo and Juliet* |
| Literary context | *Great Expectations* published in weekly parts, shaping its cliffhangers |
| Reception | Modern audiences' response to the treatment of Shylock |

Aim to use at least two kinds in one essay.

## Coursework versus exam

In the Component 2 exam the challenge is remembering context under time pressure. In Assignment B
you can research, so the challenge is **selection**: choose the two or three contexts that most
change the meaning of your chosen moments, and cut the rest.

## 800-word plan

Introduction 70 words; three paragraphs of about 200 (moment, method, context, link); conclusion
70. Use the beginning, a turning point and the ending.

## Paragraph test

- Does the context explain *why* the writer made this choice, or how a reader responds?
- Would the paragraph be weaker without the context sentence? If not, cut it.
- Is there still a precise AO2 point about language, form or structure?

## Annotated model paragraph

Task: *Explore how Shakespeare presents the relationship between parents and children in* Romeo and
Juliet*. Refer to language, form and structure and to context.*

> Shakespeare presents Capulet's love for Juliet as conditional on her obedience, and Act 3 Scene 5
> exposes that condition. **[AO1: argument that answers the task]** When Juliet refuses to marry
> Paris, Capulet's language collapses from fatherly concern into a torrent of insults and threats,
> ending with the curt instruction that she may "hang, beg, starve" if she will not obey.
> **[AO2: language and its effect, short quotation]** The harsh list of verbs leaves no option
> between submission and ruin, which shows how quickly affection turns into ownership. **[AO2:
> developed analysis]** For an Elizabethan audience, a father's right to choose his daughter's
> husband was widely accepted, so Juliet's refusal would have seemed shocking, yet Shakespeare
> makes Capulet's rage so extreme that the audience's sympathy moves to the daughter. **[AO4:
> social context and audience response, integrated]** Placed just after the lovers' parting at
> dawn in the same scene, the confrontation isolates Juliet completely, turning a family quarrel into a step towards the
> tragedy the Prologue foretold. **[AO2 structure and AO4 literary context: tragic form]**

## Process rules

- Work independently; sign the authentication statement.
- Teachers may give general guidance at draft stage, not corrections.
- No rewriting after marking.
- Acknowledge sources of ideas and facts.

## Common mistakes

- A history or biography paragraph before the analysis begins.
- One kind of context repeated in every paragraph.
- So much context that AO2 analysis disappears.
- Modern judgements presented as facts rather than as a reading.
- Copying researched facts without acknowledging the source.
- Choosing moments from only one part of the text.
- Generalising about "people back then" instead of naming a specific attitude and its effect.

## Self-test

1. How are Assignment B's 30 marks divided?
2. Name the five kinds of context the specification lists.
3. Why is selection the main challenge in coursework context?
4. Give an example of literary context for *Pride and Prejudice*.
5. When does the writer's own life count as context?
6. Write a word budget for an 800-word essay with three main paragraphs.
7. What makes context "integrated"?
8. Which scene of *Romeo and Juliet* shows Capulet's fury at Juliet's refusal to marry Paris?
9. At which point in *Great Expectations* is Magwitch revealed as Pip's benefactor?
10. Why is the scaffold in *The Scarlet Letter* a useful structural focus?
11. In the model paragraph, which sentence earns AO4, and why is it integrated?
12. Name three moments from *Pride and Prejudice* that span beginning, turning point and ending.

**Answers:** 1. AO1 10, AO2 10, AO4 10. 2. The writer's situation (where relevant), historical setting,
social and cultural attitudes, literary context, and reception by different audiences. 3. Research
time means students can collect far more than 800 words can hold, so they must choose contexts that
explain their chosen moments. 4. It is a comedy of manners about courtship, with an ironic narrator.
5. Only when it sheds light on the text. 6. Introduction 70, three paragraphs of about 200,
conclusion 70. 7. It explains a specific moment or method in the text within the same paragraph,
rather than sitting beside it. 8. Act 3 Scene 5. 9. Chapter 39. 10. It appears at the beginning,
middle and end, and each return changes who stands on it and who watches, tracing the movement from
public shame to public confession. 11. The sentence on an Elizabethan father's right to choose his
daughter's husband; it explains why Juliet's refusal is shocking and how Shakespeare shifts the
audience's sympathy in this scene. 12. For example: Darcy's slight at the Meryton assembly (Chapter
3), his first proposal and letter (Chapters 34 and 35), and the second proposal (Chapter 58).
