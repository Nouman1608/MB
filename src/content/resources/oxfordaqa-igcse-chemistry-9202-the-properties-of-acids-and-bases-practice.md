This set practises section 3.5.1 The properties of acids and bases (statements a to g) of the OxfordAQA International GCSE Chemistry (9202) specification, Version 6.2, for exams May/June 2018 onwards. Section A is short recall; Section B asks for equations, formulae and explanations in terms of ions. The course is untiered.

A: zinc oxide

B: iron(II) hydroxide

C: lithium hydroxide

D: copper(II) oxide

**[1]**

**2.** Name the ion present in all acidic solutions. **[1]**

**(a)** calcium nitrate **[1]**

**(b)** aluminium chloride **[1]**

**(c)** ammonium sulfate **[1]**

## Section B

**7.** Potassium hydroxide solution reacts with dilute sulfuric acid.

**(a)** Write a balanced symbol equation, with state symbols, for the reaction. **[3]**

**(b)** Rewrite the equation showing all the aqueous compounds as separate ions. **[2]**

**(c)** Name the spectator ions and give the ionic equation for the reaction. **[2]**

**8.** Write balanced equations for these neutralisation reactions.

**(a)** magnesium oxide + hydrochloric acid **[2]**

**(b)** aluminium oxide + nitric acid **[2]**

**(c)** lithium hydroxide + sulfuric acid **[2]**

**9.** Ammonia gas dissolves in water.

**(a)** Write an equation to show why the solution is alkaline. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain why ammonia is described as forming an alkaline solution even though it is not a metal hydroxide. **[1]**

**(c)** Ammonia solution reacts with hydrochloric acid. Write the ionic equation for the change that happens to the ammonia, and explain why no water is formed. **[2]**

**10.** A student has a solution of nitric acid with a pH of 1.

**(a)** 10 cm³ of this acid is mixed with 990 cm³ of pure water. Predict whether the pH of the new solution is higher or lower than 1, and explain your answer in terms of ions. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain why diluting the acid can never give a pH above 7. **[1]**

**11.** Four solutions are tested with universal indicator.

| Solution | Colour with universal indicator |
|---|---|
| W | green |
| X | purple |
| Y | orange |
| Z | red |

**(a)** Place the solutions in order of increasing pH. **[1]**

**(b)** Which solution has the greatest concentration of hydroxide ions? **[1]**

**(c)** Solutions X and Z are mixed in amounts that react exactly. Predict the colour of the mixture and explain your answer using an ionic equation. **[3]**

**12.** Carbon dioxide is bubbled into limewater.

**(a)** Name the alkali in limewater. **[1]**

**(b)** Write a balanced equation for the reaction. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain why the limewater becomes cloudy. **[1]**

**(d)** A few drops of universal indicator are added to the limewater before the carbon dioxide is bubbled in. Suggest how the colour changes as carbon dioxide is added, and explain why. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** C [1].

**2.** Hydrogen ion, H⁺ (aq) [1].

**3. (a)** Ca(NO₃)₂ [1]

**(b)** AlCl₃ [1]

**(c)** (NH₄)₂SO₄ [1]

*Examiner insight:* Brackets are needed whenever more than one group ion is present; CaNO₃₂ is not accepted.

**4.** pH 7 [1]; pure water (or a named neutral salt solution such as sodium chloride solution) [1].

**5.** Iron(III) sulfate [1].

**6.** Ammonium nitrate [1].

**7. (a)** 2KOH(aq) + H₂SO₄(aq) → K₂SO₄(aq) + 2H₂O(l): correct formulae [1]; balanced [1]; state symbols [1].

**(b)** 2K⁺(aq) + 2OH⁻(aq) + 2H⁺(aq) + SO₄²⁻(aq) → 2K⁺(aq) + SO₄²⁻(aq) + 2H₂O(l): all aqueous compounds split correctly [1]; water left as H₂O(l) [1].

**(c)** K⁺ and SO₄²⁻ [1]; H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l) [1].

*Examiner insight:* 2H⁺ + 2OH⁻ → 2H₂O is acceptable, but simplifying to the smallest whole numbers is expected.

**8. (a)** MgO + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂O: formulae [1]; balanced [1].

**(b)** Al₂O₃ + 6HNO₃ → 2Al(NO₃)₃ + 3H₂O: formulae [1]; balanced [1].

**(c)** 2LiOH + H₂SO₄ → Li₂SO₄ + 2H₂O: formulae [1]; balanced [1].

**9. (a)** NH₃ + H₂O ⇌ NH₄⁺ + OH⁻: correct species [1]; reversible arrow (or equation balanced with OH⁻ shown as product) [1].

**(b)** It reacts with water to produce hydroxide ions, and hydroxide ions make solutions alkaline [1].

**(c)** NH₃ + H⁺ → NH₄⁺ [1]. The ammonia molecule simply gains the hydrogen ion; there is no OH⁻ or O²⁻ in the base for the H⁺ to combine with, so no water forms [1].

**10. (a)** Higher [1]. The same number of H⁺ ions is spread through a much larger volume, so the concentration of H⁺ ions is lower [1].

**(b)** Water adds no OH⁻ in excess, so the solution can only approach neutral (pH 7), never become alkaline [1].

**11. (a)** Z, Y, W, X [1].

**(b)** X [1].

**(c)** Green [1]. The H⁺ ions from Z react with the OH⁻ ions from X [1]: H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O, leaving a neutral solution [1].

**12. (a)** Calcium hydroxide [1].

**(b)** Ca(OH)₂ + CO₂ → CaCO₃ + H₂O: formulae [1]; balanced [1].

**(c)** Calcium carbonate is insoluble, so it forms as a fine white solid (precipitate) [1].

**(d)** The colour moves from blue/purple towards green [1], because hydroxide ions are used up as calcium hydroxide reacts with carbon dioxide [1].

*Examiner insight:* Part (d) asks you to apply 3.5.1 f to a new context. Credit is for linking the colour change to OH⁻ being removed.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Missing brackets in formulae such as Ca(NO₃)₂ and (NH₄)₂SO₄.
- Balancing by changing subscripts inside a formula instead of the numbers in front.
- Keeping spectator ions in the ionic equation, or splitting water into ions.
- Adding water to the products of ammonia and an acid.
- Explaining pH changes without mentioning H⁺ or OH⁻ ions.
