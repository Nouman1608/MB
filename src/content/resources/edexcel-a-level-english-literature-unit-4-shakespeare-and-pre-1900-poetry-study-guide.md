Unit 4 completes the International A Level by pairing the most famous dramatist in English with poetry written before 1900. The two halves ask for slightly different habits of mind: for Shakespeare you must think as a reader and as a theatre audience; for pre-1900 poetry you must be comfortable with older forms and vocabulary and able to say why they matter. This guide offers methods that work for any set play or poet, but your own specification and past papers decide the texts and the question format.

## Where this fits in the specification

Edexcel International A Level English Literature (YET01) has four units. Unit 4 is a two-hour examination of 50 marks, one of the units that completes the full International A Level after the two units of the International Advanced Subsidiary. Two sections of equal size are typical of this unit, one on Shakespeare and one on poetry, as the guide to [pacing across the four units](/resources/edexcel-ial-english-literature-exam-preparation/) explains. As Unit 2 concentrates on drama more broadly, the guide to [Unit 2 Drama](/resources/a-level-edexcel-english-literature-drama/) is useful background for reading plays as performance.

## Shakespeare

### Drama, not just text

A play is written to be performed. Ask in every scene what an audience sees and hears: who is on stage, who is silent, what is staged, where the characters stand in relation to each other, and how the pace changes. Stage directions in Shakespeare are often implied in the dialogue, so an exit, a pause or a sudden change of rhythm can carry meaning.

### Language and verse

Much of Shakespeare is in blank verse, unrhymed lines of ten syllables with a regular stress pattern. Departures from that pattern are meaningful. Prose often marks lower-status speech, comic scenes or loss of control; rhyming couplets often close a scene or give a sense of finality. Consider imagery clusters, soliloquies and asides, wordplay, repetition and shifts in register.

Short example: Hamlet's line "To be, or not to be, that is the question" is a soliloquy, in which the character thinks aloud, and the audience is placed in the position of hearing his private thoughts. A paragraph about it might discuss how the balance of "to be" and "not to be" sets up an either-or structure that the rest of the speech then complicates, and how the form lets us watch thought in progress, not a conclusion.

### Themes and characters

Think about how the play develops ideas such as power, ambition, justice, love, appearance and reality, and how characters change or fail to change. Watch for **foils**, characters who contrast with the protagonist, and for how subplots comment on the main plot. If the play is a tragedy, consider the hero's flaw, the part played by fate and choice, and the audience's response at the end; if a comedy, consider disguise, disorder and restoration of order.

### Context and interpretation

Show awareness of the world in which the play was written, including attitudes to kingship, order, gender and religion, and of the way different audiences and productions have read the play. Use this only where it clarifies a choice in the text. Different interpretations, such as seeing a character as heroic or as culpable, deserve consideration; weigh them against evidence rather than listing them.

### Writing about an extract

1. State where the extract falls in the play and what has just happened.
2. Make a claim about its dramatic purpose.
3. Work through the language, verse form, stagecraft and imagery, linking each point to effect.
4. Connect the extract to the play as a whole, particularly to developing themes and later events.

An extract is a part of the play, so an answer that stays only in the extract misses the play's design.

## Pre-1900 poetry

### Understanding older poems

Older poems may use unfamiliar diction, inverted word order or classical references. Read slowly, aloud if possible, and paraphrase difficult lines before you analyse them. Notice who the speaker is and what the poem is arguing. Common forms include sonnets, odes, ballads, lyrics and dramatic monologues, each with its own conventions: a sonnet usually develops an argument and turns; a ballad tells a story in a simple rhythm; an ode addresses and praises a subject.

Short examples: Wordsworth's opening line "I wandered lonely as a cloud" uses a simile that places the speaker in nature and sets up a mood of solitude, which the poem later changes by recalling the daffodils. Blake's line "I wander thro' each charter'd street" uses the repeated, heavy rhythm and the word "charter'd", with its implication of ownership and control, to present the city as restricted and oppressive.

### Method

Use a sequence for every poem: situation, form, language, movement and effect. Then consider the relationship of the poem to its period. For example, Romantic poets often respond to nature, imagination and social change, and Victorian poets to doubt, industry and morality. Be careful not to force a poem to fit a general label; instead, show how a specific feature reflects a wider concern.

### Comparison and wider reading

If your question asks you to consider more than one poem, organise by point of comparison. Where the specification permits wider reading, briefly use a second poem or poet by the same writer to support a claim, but keep your focus on the one named in the question.

## Planning and timing

Fifty marks in two hours is a generous allowance, so use planning time to decide your thesis and your three or four key moments in the text. Allocate time in proportion to marks, leave a few minutes to read through, and avoid spending so long on the first answer that the second is thin.

## Common mistakes

- Narrating the plot of the play instead of analysing dramatic method.
- Treating Shakespeare as a novel and ignoring staging and audience.
- Saying a character "feels" something without evidence from the language.
- Paraphrasing a poem instead of explaining its methods.
- Using context in a separate paragraph that does not return to the text.
- Assuming one correct interpretation of a character or poem.

## Exam technique

Underline the question's key terms and answer those, not a prepared essay. In extract answers, keep moving between the passage and the whole play. In poetry answers, select short quotations and give each its own analysis. Write a brief conclusion that makes a final judgement.

## Frequently asked questions

**How long is YET01 Unit 4?**
2 hours for 50 marks, open book, and 25% of the International A Level.

**What are the sections?**
Section A Shakespeare and Section B Pre-1900 Poetry, each worth 25 marks and assessing AO1, AO2, AO3 and AO5.

**Is Unit 4 open book?**
Yes. All four YET01 units are open-book examinations.


## Check your understanding

1. Why should you think about staging when writing about Shakespeare? (The play is written for performance, so what the audience sees and hears creates meaning.)
2. What is blank verse? (Unrhymed lines of ten syllables with a regular stress pattern.)
3. What can a switch from verse to prose suggest? (Lower status, comedy, or loss of control or formality.)
4. What is a soliloquy, and what effect does it have? (A character speaking alone; it gives the audience access to private thought.)
5. What is a foil? (A character who contrasts with another, highlighting their qualities.)
6. How should you open an answer on a Shakespeare extract? (Place it in the play, then make a claim about its dramatic purpose.)
7. What should you do before analysing a difficult older poem? (Read slowly and paraphrase the sense to be sure of the meaning.)
8. How should context be used? (To explain a particular choice or effect, then link back to the text.)
