## 1. Scale and long-run average cost

**Scale** is the size of a firm's whole operation: plant, workforce and capital together. It can change only in the long run, when all factors are variable. **Economies of scale** are the falls in long-run average cost that come from increasing scale. **Diseconomies of scale** are rises in long-run average cost when a firm grows beyond a certain size.

A simple test: if total cost rises by a **smaller** percentage than output, average cost falls and the firm is experiencing economies of scale. If total cost rises by a **larger** percentage than output, average cost rises: diseconomies.

**Worked example 1 (invented Northfield Bottling).** Each row is a different plant size, with all factors adjusted.

| Output (bottles a day) | Total cost (£) | LRAC (£ per bottle) |
|---|---|---|
| 1,000 | 20,000 | 20.00 |
| 2,000 | 32,000 | 16.00 |
| 4,000 | 48,000 | 12.00 |
| 8,000 | 88,000 | 11.00 |
| 16,000 | 208,000 | 13.00 |

- From 1,000 to 2,000 bottles output rises 100% but cost rises only 60%, so LRAC falls: economies of scale.
- From 8,000 to 16,000 output rises 100% but cost rises 136%, so LRAC rises: diseconomies of scale.
- LRAC is lowest, £11, at 8,000 bottles in this table.

The LRAC curve drawn through such points is usually U-shaped or L-shaped: falling while economies dominate, flat when they are exhausted, rising if diseconomies take over.

Do not confuse this with the short-run U-shape. Short-run average cost rises because a fixed factor becomes crowded (diminishing returns); long-run average cost rises because the whole organisation becomes too large to run well.

## 2. Internal economies of scale

Internal economies arise from the growth of the firm itself.

| Type | How it lowers average cost |
|---|---|
| Technical | Larger, more efficient machines; specialisation of workers and capital; the link between volume and surface area |
| Purchasing (bulk buying) | Discounts and stronger bargaining power with suppliers |
| Marketing | Advertising and distribution costs spread over more units |
| Financial | Lower interest rates and easier access to loans and share issues |
| Managerial | Specialist managers (finance, marketing, logistics) each working on what they do best |
| Risk-bearing | Selling many products or in many markets cushions a fall in any one |

**Worked example 2: a technical economy (containers).** A cube-shaped tank with sides of 1 metre has a surface area of 6 m² and holds 1 m³. Double the sides to 2 metres: surface area becomes 24 m² (four times as much) but capacity becomes 8 m³ (eight times). Material per cubic metre of capacity falls from 6 m² to 3 m², so the cost of building storage per unit halves. This is why tankers, warehouses and blast furnaces are built large.

**Worked example 3: indivisibility in transport.** A small van costs £400 a trip and carries 10 tonnes: £40 a tonne. A large lorry costs £700 a trip and carries 40 tonnes: **£17.50** a tonne. A firm needs enough volume to fill the lorry before it can use it, which is why large firms enjoy the lower cost.

**Worked example 4: a financial economy.** A small firm borrows £2 million at 9% interest (£180,000 a year). A large firm, judged less risky, borrows the same sum at 6% (£120,000). The large firm saves **£60,000** a year.

Specialisation and the division of labour, which the AS course asks you to explain, are a source of technical and managerial economies: a large firm can split work into narrow tasks and employ specialists for each.

## 3. Internal diseconomies of scale

| Type | Why average cost rises |
|---|---|
| Communication | More layers of management slow decisions and distort messages |
| Co-ordination and control | It is harder to monitor many sites and departments, so waste goes unnoticed |
| Motivation | Workers who feel remote from decisions may be less committed, raising absence and staff turnover |
| Bureaucracy | More rules, meetings and paperwork add cost without adding output |

Diseconomies are mostly managerial and human. They can be reduced by giving local managers more authority, but that has its own costs.

## 4. External economies and diseconomies

External economies and diseconomies arise from the growth of the **industry** or the area, not of the individual firm. They shift the firm's whole LRAC curve rather than moving it along the curve.

- **External economies:** a pool of trained workers, specialist suppliers and repair firms nearby, shared research, local colleges running relevant courses, better transport links built for the industry.
- **External diseconomies:** congestion that raises delivery times, competition for land that raises rents, competition for skilled labour that raises wages.

**Worked example 5.** Several bottling plants cluster in one town. A local college starts a technician course, so Northfield's training cost falls from £4,000 to £2,500 per new worker. With 40 recruits a year, it saves **£60,000**. The saving does not depend on Northfield's own size, so it is an external economy.

## 5. Minimum efficient scale and significance

**Minimum efficient scale (MES)** is the lowest output at which LRAC reaches its minimum. If MES is large compared with the size of the market, only a few firms can produce at lowest cost, so the industry tends to be dominated by a few large firms (car manufacturing). If MES is small, many firms can compete (hairdressing, plumbing).

**Evaluating the significance of economies of scale:**

- **Consumers** may gain lower prices, but only if competition forces firms to pass on savings; a large firm may use its size to raise prices instead.
- **Firms** gain lower costs and higher profit, and can compete internationally. But growth can bring diseconomies, and size reduces flexibility.
- **Small firms survive** because many markets are small or local, consumers value personal service, and MES in many services is low.
- **Efficiency:** economies of scale allow lower average cost, supporting productive efficiency, but a firm that is too large for its market may never reach MES or may move into diseconomies.

## Annotated model answer

*Question:* "Explain one economy of scale a large supermarket chain might gain."

> A purchasing economy is a fall in average cost from buying in bulk *[definition]*. A supermarket chain buying millions of units of a product can negotiate a lower price per unit from suppliers than a single shop can, because suppliers depend on its orders *[mechanism]*. Lower input prices reduce average cost as the chain grows, so it can cut its prices or earn more profit per unit *[consequence]*.

## Common mistakes

- Calling any fall in cost an economy of scale. It must result from a change in scale.
- Confusing a fall in total cost with a fall in average cost; economies of scale are about cost per unit.
- Describing internal economies when the cause is the growth of the industry.
- Explaining short-run rising costs with diseconomies of scale instead of diminishing returns.
- Assuming large firms always charge lower prices.

## Exam technique

- **Calculate:** compare the percentage change in total cost with the percentage change in output, or compute LRAC row by row.
- **Explain:** name the economy, explain the mechanism, and finish with its effect on average cost.
- **Explain, with the aid of a diagram:** draw LRAC falling then rising, mark MES, and show an external economy as a downward shift of the whole curve.
- **Evaluate:** weigh benefits to consumers and firms against diseconomies and market power, and make the judgement depend on the size of MES relative to the market and the strength of competition.
