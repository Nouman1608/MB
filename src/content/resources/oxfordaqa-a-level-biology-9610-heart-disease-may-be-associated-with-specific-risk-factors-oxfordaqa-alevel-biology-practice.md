## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Define atheroma and state where in an artery it forms. **[2]**

**2.** Which statement describes a thrombus?

A: Lipid-containing material in an artery wall

B: A bulge in a weakened artery wall

C: A blood clot formed inside a blood vessel

D: Death of part of the heart muscle **[1]**

**3.** Name the four risk factors for coronary heart disease given in the specification. **[2]**

**4.** Explain why a thrombus near the start of a main coronary artery usually causes more damage than one in a small branch. **[2]**

**5.** Explain how a diet high in salt may increase the risk of coronary heart disease. **[2]**

**6.** Explain how atheroma can lead to a rise in blood pressure. **[2]**

### Section B: longer questions

**7.** A ten-year study recorded new cases of CHD in groups with different blood pressures.

| Systolic blood pressure | Number of people | New cases of CHD |
|---|---|---|
| Under 120 | 25 000 | 50 |
| 120 to 139 | 30 000 | 105 |
| 140 to 159 | 15 000 | 90 |
| 160 or over | 5 000 | 55 |

**(a)** Calculate the incidence of CHD per 1000 people for each group. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the relative risk for the highest group compared with the lowest. **[1]**

**(c)** Describe the relationship shown. **[1]**

**8.** Two groups of 200 adults were studied. The mean incidence of CHD per 1000 people per year was 7.2 (range 4.7 to 9.7) in a group with high saturated fat intake and 5.9 (range 3.5 to 8.3) in a group with low intake. Evaluate the conclusion that a high saturated fat intake increases the risk of CHD. **[3]**

**9.** A study found that people who drank more than five cups of coffee a day had a higher incidence of CHD than people who drank none. The coffee drinkers were also more likely to smoke. Explain why the study does not show that coffee increases the risk of CHD. **[3]**

**10.** Describe and explain how cigarette smoking can lead to a myocardial infarction. **[6]**

**11.** Explain why stopping smoking may reduce a person's risk of CHD even though atheroma already present does not disappear. **[3]**

**12.** Researchers want to find out whether blood cholesterol concentration is linked to CHD. Suggest four features of a study design that would make the results more valid. **[4]**

**13.** Explain why an aneurysm in the aorta is dangerous. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** Lipid-containing material [1] within the wall of the artery (beneath the endothelium) [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* "In the lumen" is wrong. Atheroma forms in the wall and bulges into the lumen.

**2.** **C** [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* A is atheroma, B an aneurysm, D a myocardial infarction. Learn all four as a set.

**3.** Any two of diet, blood cholesterol, cigarette smoking and high blood pressure [1]; the other two [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* These are the four the specification names. Lack of exercise and age are risk factors too, but they are not the ones asked for here.

**4.** The start of a main coronary artery supplies a large region of heart muscle through its branches [1], so a blockage there cuts off oxygen to more cardiac muscle and more muscle dies [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Link the position of the blockage to the amount of muscle supplied.

**5.** A high salt intake is associated with raised blood pressure [1]. High blood pressure damages the endothelium of arteries, making atheroma more likely [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Two links are needed: salt to blood pressure, then blood pressure to arterial damage.

**6.** Atheroma narrows the lumen of the artery [1], increasing resistance to blood flow, so the heart must pump at a higher pressure to maintain flow (and the wall is less elastic) [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* This is why atheroma can worsen over time: higher pressure causes further damage.

**7. (a)** Under 120: 2.0; 120 to 139: 3.5 [1]; 140 to 159: 6.0; 160 or over: 11.0 per 1000 [1].

**(b)** 11.0 ÷ 2.0 = **5.5** [1].

**(c)** As blood pressure increases, the incidence of CHD increases (a positive correlation) [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* The 120 to 139 group has the most cases (105) but not the highest rate. That is why rates are calculated.

**8.** The mean incidence is higher in the high-fat group, which is consistent with the conclusion [1]. However, the ranges overlap widely, so the difference may be due to chance [1]. The groups are small and other variables, such as smoking or blood pressure, may not have been controlled, so the conclusion is not well supported by these data [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Evaluation needs a point for the conclusion and points against it, using the figures given.

**9.** The study shows only a correlation between coffee drinking and CHD [1]. Smoking is a confounding variable: it is a known risk factor and is more common among the coffee drinkers [1], so smoking, not coffee, could explain some or all of the higher incidence [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Name the confounding variable from the question and explain why it matters.

**10.** Any six:
- Chemicals in smoke damage the endothelium of arteries [1].
- Lipids such as cholesterol and white blood cells build up in the damaged wall, forming atheroma [1].
- Nicotine raises blood pressure, causing further damage [1].
- Atheroma damages or breaks the endothelium, triggering clotting [1].
- Nicotine makes platelets more likely to stick together, so a thrombus forms more easily [1].
- A thrombus blocks a coronary artery, interrupting blood flow to cardiac muscle [1].
- The muscle receives no oxygen, cannot respire aerobically and dies (myocardial infarction).
- Carbon monoxide reduces the oxygen carried by haemoglobin, so the heart muscle is already short of oxygen. **[6]**

*Examiner insight:* The best answers form a chain from smoke to dead muscle. Isolated facts about smoking score fewer marks than a linked sequence.

**11.** Smoking increases the risk in ways that continue only while the person smokes: nicotine raises blood pressure and makes platelets stickier, and carbon monoxide reduces oxygen transport [1]. Stopping removes these effects, so a thrombus is less likely to form on existing atheroma [1]. Further damage to the endothelium, and so new atheroma, is also less likely [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* This links to section 3.2.1.2: changes in lifestyle may be associated with a reduced risk.

**12.** Any four: a large sample [1]; follow participants over a long period [1]; groups matched (or results adjusted) for age, sex, smoking, blood pressure and other risk factors [1]; cholesterol measured by a blood test rather than self-reported [1]; diagnosis of CHD made by the same standard method for all; repeat the study in other populations. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Each feature should make the comparison fairer or the data more reliable. "Make it a fair test" is not specific enough.

**13.** The aorta carries blood at high pressure from the left ventricle [1], so if the aneurysm bursts there is rapid, severe internal bleeding and a large fall in blood pressure [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* The danger comes from bursting under high pressure. Say so.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Confusing atheroma, aneurysm, thrombus and infarction.
- Placing atheroma in the lumen.
- Comparing raw case numbers instead of rates.
- Ignoring overlapping ranges or error bars.
- Writing "correlation is not causation" without naming a confounding variable.
- Listing smoking effects without linking them to atheroma, thrombosis and heart muscle.

**Are these OxfordAQA past paper questions?**
No. They are original questions for section 3.2.7 of OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Biology (9610). Use them alongside the official past papers.

**Why are rates used instead of numbers of cases?**
Groups in a study are often different sizes. A rate per 1000 or per 100 000 people allows a fair comparison.
