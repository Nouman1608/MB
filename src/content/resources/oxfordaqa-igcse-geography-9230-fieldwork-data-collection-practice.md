These questions cover strand 2 of geographical enquiry, "Selecting, measuring and recording data appropriate to the chosen enquiry", in section 3.3.2 of the OxfordAQA International GCSE Geography (9230) specification, Version 4.3, with the numerical skill to "design fieldwork data collection sheets and collect data with an understanding of accuracy, sample size and procedures, control groups and reliability". For the whole unit, see the [Geographical and fieldwork skills study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-geography-9230-geographical-and-fieldwork-skills/).

## Section A

**1.** Define quantitative data and qualitative data. **[2]**

**2.** State **two** pieces of equipment used to measure a beach profile. **[2]**

**3.** Explain the difference between random sampling and systematic sampling. **[2]**

## Section B

At one site on the fictional Wenning Brook, students recorded a width of 4.0 m and these depths (cm) at ten equal intervals across the channel: 12, 18, 25, 31, 34, 29, 22, 15, 9, 5.

**4.** Calculate the mean depth in metres and the cross-sectional area of the channel. Show your working. **[3]**

At the same site, a float took 25 s, 24 s and 41 s to travel 10 m.

**5.**

**(a)** Identify the anomalous reading and suggest **one** reason for it. **[2]**
**(b)** Using the two other readings, calculate the velocity in metres per second, to two decimal places. **[2]**
**(c)** Using your answers, calculate the discharge in cubic metres per second, to two decimal places. **[1]**

**6.** A fictional dune system is 20% embryo dunes, 50% yellow dunes and 30% grey dunes by area. Students have time for 20 quadrats. Using stratified sampling, state how many quadrats should be placed in each dune type, and justify the method. **[3]**

**7.** Design the column headings for a recording sheet for an environmental quality survey at ten sites along a route. **[4]**

**8.** Using the measurement of pebble size as an example, explain the difference between accuracy and reliability. **[4]**

**9.** A student investigating views on a new shopping centre writes: "We asked ten of our friends what they thought." Evaluate this as a sample. **[4]**

**10.** Describe and justify a method for collecting land-use data along a road leading out of a town centre. **[4]**

**11.** A student copies a finished table of river data from a textbook and calls it her data collection. Explain why this would not meet the specification's requirement for alternative data collection, and suggest an acceptable alternative. **[3]**

**12.** Give the title of your individual fieldwork enquiry. Describe and justify one primary data collection method you used, and evaluate how far it gave reliable data. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** Quantitative data is numerical, such as a pedestrian count [1]. Qualitative data is descriptive, such as interview comments or photographs [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Any two: ranging poles [1]; clinometer [1]; tape measure [1]. **[2]**

**3.** In random sampling every point has an equal chance of being chosen, for example using random numbers [1]; in systematic sampling sites are chosen at regular intervals, such as every 50 m [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Total depth = 200 cm; mean = 200 ÷ 10 = 20 cm = **0.20 m** [1]. Cross-sectional area = width × mean depth = 4.0 × 0.20 [1] = **0.80 m²** [1]. **[3]**

**5. (a)** 41 s [1]. Any one reason: the float caught on a rock or vegetation; it drifted into slower water at the edge; wind blew it back; the stopwatch was started late or stopped late [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Mean time = (25 + 24) ÷ 2 = 24.5 s [1]; velocity = 10 ÷ 24.5 = **0.41 m/s** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Discharge = 0.80 × 0.408 = **0.33 m³/s** (accept 0.80 × 0.41 = 0.33) [1]. **[1]**

**6.** Embryo dunes: 4 quadrats; yellow dunes: 10; grey dunes: 6 [1] [1]. Justification: each dune type is sampled in proportion to its area, so every stage of the dune system is represented fairly [1]. **[3]**

**7.** One mark each for any four appropriate headings: site number [1]; location or distance from the start of the route [1]; date and time [1]; scores for each criterion, such as litter, noise, greenery and building condition, on a stated scale [1]; total score [1]; notes [1]. **[4]**

**8.** Accuracy is how close a measurement is to the true size of the pebble [1], for example measuring the longest axis with callipers rather than estimating by eye [1]. Reliability is whether repeating the measurement gives similar results [1], for example two students measuring the same pebbles and getting similar values, or measuring a larger sample at each site [1]. **[4]**

**9.** The sample is small, so a few unusual views could distort the results [1]. Friends are likely to be similar in age and interests, so the sample is not representative of shoppers or residents [1]. They were chosen for convenience, which introduces bias [1]. Judgement: unreliable for the enquiry; a larger sample of shoppers chosen systematically, such as every tenth person at the entrance, would be better [1]. **[4]**

**10.** Description: walk the road from the centre outwards and record the ground-floor use of each building, using set categories such as shops, offices, housing, services and open space, marked on a base map [1] [1]. Justification: recording every building, or every building at regular intervals, shows how land use changes with distance [1]; using fixed categories means different students classify buildings in the same way, making results more reliable [1]. **[4]**

**11.** The specification says alternative methods "must go beyond the students being presented with pre-processed data that is ready for analysis" [1], and there must be some element of planning and selecting [1]. Acceptable alternative, for example: searching for and selecting river-flow data from an official website, or working with raw, unprocessed data supplied by the teacher [1]. **[3]**

**12.** Levels-style indicative content:
- States the title of the enquiry [1].
- Describes the method precisely: equipment, sites, timing and number of readings [1] [1].
- Justifies the method by linking it to the hypothesis [1].
- Justifies a sampling choice and explains how it was carried out [1].
- Identifies a strength for reliability, such as repeat readings or a consistent procedure [1].
- Identifies a weakness, such as a small sample, one day only or equipment error, with its effect on the data [1].
- Uses details or figures from the student's own data [1].
- Reaches a judgement on how reliable the data were, such as "largely reliable" with the main reason [1]. **[9]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calculating area with depth still in centimetres.
- Including an obvious anomaly in a mean without comment.
- Placing stratified samples equally instead of in proportion.
- Recording-sheet designs with no units or scale.
- Treating "reliable" and "accurate" as the same thing.
- Section C answers that describe fieldwork in general instead of the candidate's own titled enquiry.
