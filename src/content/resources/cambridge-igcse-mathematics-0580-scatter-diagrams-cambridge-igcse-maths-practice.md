These questions cover section 9.5, Scatter diagrams, in the Cambridge IGCSE Mathematics 0580 syllabus for 2025, 2026 and 2027. C9.5 and E9.5 are identical, so every question suits Core and Extended candidates. Each question is labelled (non-calculator) or (calculator). Where a question asks you to draw a scatter diagram and a line of best fit, plot the table on graph paper; answers read from your own line are accepted within the ranges given. Section A has short questions; Section B has longer, structured ones.

Revise first with the [scatter diagrams study guide](/resources/cambridge-igcse-mathematics-0580-scatter-diagrams-cambridge-igcse-maths/) and the [scatter diagrams revision notes](/resources/cambridge-igcse-mathematics-0580-scatter-diagrams-cambridge-igcse-maths-revision-notes/).

## Questions

### Section A

**1.** (non-calculator) State the type of correlation you would expect between each pair of variables.

**(a)** The outside temperature and the cost of heating a house. **[1]**

**(b)** The height and the mass of adults. **[1]**

**(c)** The last digit of a person's phone number and their height. **[1]**

**2.** (non-calculator) Which one of these describes a line of best fit?

A: a line that joins every point in order

B: a single ruled straight line with the points roughly evenly spread either side of it

C: a straight line that must pass through the origin

D: a smooth curve through as many points as possible

**[1]**

**3.** (non-calculator) A scatter diagram shows negative correlation between the number of days a student was absent and their exam mark. Describe what this shows, in context. **[1]**

**4.** (non-calculator) A line of best fit passes through the points (5, 20) and (25, 70).

**(a)** Use the line to estimate y when x = 13. **[1]**

**(b)** Use the line to estimate x when y = 55. **[1]**

**5.** (non-calculator) Here are six pairs of values.

```
x   2   3   4   5   6   7
y   5   7   9   4  13  15
```

Write down the pair that does not follow the pattern of the others. **[1]**

**6.** (non-calculator) For children aged 5 to 11, shoe size and reading age show positive correlation. Explain why this does not mean that bigger feet make children better readers. **[1]**

### Section B

**7.** (calculator) A café recorded the midday temperature and the number of hot drinks sold on ten days.

```
Temperature (°C)   8   10   12   13   15   17   18   20   22   25
Hot drinks sold   82   78   70   71   62   55   54   47   40   31
```

**(a)** Draw a scatter diagram and describe the correlation. **[1]**

**(b)** Interpret the correlation in the context of the café. **[1]**

**(c)** Draw a line of best fit. Use it to estimate the number of hot drinks sold on a day when the temperature is 16 °C. **[2]**

**(d)** Use your line to estimate the temperature on a day when 60 hot drinks were sold. **[1]**

**(e)** Explain why your line should not be used to estimate sales on a day when the temperature is 35 °C. **[1]**

**8.** (calculator) The heights and arm spans, in centimetres, of ten students are shown.

```
Height     148  152  155  158  160  163  165  170  174  178
Arm span   146  151  156  157  161  138  166  171  172  179
```

**(a)** Describe the correlation. **[1]**

**(b)** One student's values do not fit the pattern. Write down this student's height and arm span, and give a possible reason. **[2]**

**(c)** Draw a line of best fit, ignoring that student. Use it to estimate the arm span of a student who is 168 cm tall. **[2]**

**9.** (non-calculator) Sam draws a scatter diagram. His vertical axis is labelled 0, 5, 10, 20, 40. He joins each point to the next with a straight line. He then draws a line from the origin to the top right-hand corner of the grid as his line of best fit. Write down three mistakes Sam has made. **[3]**

**10.** (non-calculator) The heights of a child were measured once a year from age 2 to age 10. A line of best fit for the data passes through (2, 86) and (10, 138), where age is in years and height is in centimetres.

**(a)** Use the line to estimate the child's height at age 7. **[1]**

**(b)** Extending the line to age 40 gives a height of 333 cm. Show how this value is obtained. **[1]**

**(c)** Explain what this shows about using the line outside the data. **[1]**

**11.** (non-calculator) Diagram P shows strong positive correlation. Diagram Q shows weak positive correlation. A line of best fit is drawn on each.

**(a)** Which line will give more reliable estimates? Give a reason. **[2]**

**(b)** Diagram R shows zero correlation. Should a line of best fit be drawn on it? Give a reason. **[1]**

**12.** (non-calculator) A museum recorded the hours of sunshine and the number of visitors on several days. A line of best fit passes through (2, 410) and (10, 170), where the first value is the hours of sunshine.

**(a)** What type of correlation does the line show? **[1]**

**(b)** Estimate the number of visitors on a day with 7 hours of sunshine. **[2]**

**(c)** Estimate the hours of sunshine on a day with 320 visitors. **[1]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** **Negative** [1]

**(b)** **Positive** [1]

**(c)** **Zero** (no correlation) [1] **[3]**

**2.** **B** [1]

**3.** Students who were absent on more days **tended to** get lower exam marks [1]

**4. (a)** Gradient = 50 ÷ 20 = 2.5; y = 20 + 2.5 × 8 = **40** [1]

**(b)** (55 − 20) ÷ 2.5 = 14; x = 5 + 14 = **19** [1] **[2]**

**5.** **(5, 4)** [1]

**6.** Older children have bigger feet **and** read better; age is a third variable that explains the link, so correlation does not show cause [1]

**7. (a)** Points plotted as crosses; **negative** correlation (strong) [1]

**(b)** On warmer days the café **tends to** sell fewer hot drinks [1]

**(c)** A single ruled line across the data from about 8 °C to 25 °C, with points balanced either side [1]; estimate about **59 drinks** (accept 56 to 62, consistent with your line) [1]

**(d)** About **15.7 °C** (accept 15 °C to 16.5 °C, consistent with your line) [1]

**(e)** 35 °C is **outside the range** of the data (8 °C to 25 °C); the pattern may not continue, so the estimate is unreliable [1] **[6]**

*Examiner insight:* reading lines drawn on the graph show the examiner where your estimate came from; an estimate that does not match your own line loses the mark.

**8. (a)** **Positive** correlation [1]

**(b)** Height **163 cm**, arm span **138 cm** [1]; for example, the arm span was measured or recorded wrongly [1]

**(c)** Line drawn through the main pattern, not pulled towards (163, 138) [1]; estimate about **168 cm** (accept 165 to 171 cm, consistent with your line) [1] **[5]**

**9.** Any three [1] [1] [1]:

- the vertical scale is **not even** (the steps 5, 5, 10, 20 are not equal);
- the points **should not be joined**;
- the line of best fit **should not be forced through the origin** or drawn across the whole grid; it should extend across the data only, with points balanced either side.

**10. (a)** Gradient = 52 ÷ 8 = 6.5 cm per year; 86 + 6.5 × 5 = **118.5 cm** [1]

**(b)** 86 + 6.5 × (40 − 2) = 86 + 247 = **333 cm** [1]

**(c)** Extrapolating far beyond the data (ages 2 to 10) gives an **impossible** height; children stop growing, so the pattern does not continue [1] **[3]**

**11. (a)** **Diagram P's line** [1]; the points are close to the line, so values read from it are close to the real data [1]

**(b)** **No**: with zero correlation there is no linear trend for a line to show [1] **[3]**

**12. (a)** **Negative** [1]

**(b)** Gradient = (170 − 410) ÷ 8 = −30 visitors per hour [1]; 410 − 30 × 5 = **260 visitors** [1]

**(c)** (410 − 320) ÷ 30 = 3; 2 + 3 = **5 hours** [1] **[4]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Joining points or drawing a curve instead of one ruled line.
- Lines forced through the origin or stretched across the whole grid.
- Estimates that do not match the candidate's own line.
- "Positive" or "negative" with no context when asked to interpret.
- Treating an extrapolated value as reliable, or claiming that correlation proves cause.
