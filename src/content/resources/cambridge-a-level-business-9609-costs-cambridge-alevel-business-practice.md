## Section A

**1.** Define the term *marginal cost*. **[2]**

**2.** Explain the difference between a direct cost and an indirect cost, giving an example of each for a school uniform maker. **[4]**

**3.** Kielder Candles has fixed costs of $9 000 a month and a variable cost of $2.50 per candle. It makes 4 000 candles a month. Calculate the average cost per candle. **[3]**

**4.** Explain the difference between contribution and profit. **[2]**

**5.** Hamble Bags sells bags at $45. Variable cost is $27 per bag and fixed costs are $25 200 a month. Calculate the break-even output. **[3]**

**6.** Explain **one** limitation of full costing. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** Using the data in question 5, Hamble Bags sells 1 800 bags a month.

**(a)** Calculate the margin of safety in units and as a percentage of sales.

**(b)** Calculate the monthly profit.

**(c)** Calculate the number of bags Hamble must sell to make a profit of $9 000. **[7]**

**8.** Grisdale Garden Tools makes two products. Monthly overheads are $30 000.

| | Spades | Rakes |
|---|---|---|
| Units sold | 3 000 | 2 000 |
| Price | $20 | $16 |
| Variable cost per unit | $11 | $12 |
| Overheads allocated | $18 000 | $12 000 |

**(a)** Calculate the profit or loss on rakes using full costing.

**(b)** Calculate the total contribution from rakes.

**(c)** Explain whether Grisdale should stop making rakes. **[6]**

**9.** Ashby Print has spare capacity. Its usual price for a box of leaflets is $60, full cost is $48 and variable cost is $35. A charity offers $40 a box for 300 boxes.

**(a)** Calculate the effect of the order on profit.

**(b)** Explain **one** non-financial factor Ashby should consider. **[4]**

**10.** Explain how a rise in fixed costs would affect a break-even chart and the break-even output. **[3]**

**11.** Verrin Chairs can make 3 000 chairs a month and currently sells 2 800 at $90 each. Variable cost is $55 per chair. A hotel offers $70 per chair for 500 chairs. Calculate whether accepting the order would increase or reduce profit. **[5]**

**12.** Callow Coffee is thinking of cutting the price of its coffee bags from $12 to $10 to increase sales. Variable cost is $6 a bag, fixed costs are $24 000 a month and current sales are 8 000 bags. Evaluate whether Callow should cut the price, using break-even analysis. **[10]**

---

## Answers

**1.** The extra cost of producing one more unit of output [1]; usually equal to the variable cost per unit if fixed costs do not change [1].

**2.** A direct cost can be traced to a particular product [1], such as the fabric used in a blazer [1]. An indirect cost cannot be traced to one product and is shared [1], such as rent for the factory [1].

**3.** Total cost = 9 000 + 4 000 × 2.50 = $19 000 [1]. Average cost = 19 000 ÷ 4 000 [1] = $4.75 [1].

**4.** Contribution is revenue minus variable costs, the amount available to pay fixed costs [1]; profit is what remains after fixed costs have also been paid [1].

**5.** Contribution = 45 − 27 = $18 [1]. Break-even = 25 200 ÷ 18 [1] = 1 400 bags [1].

**6.** Any one, explained. Overheads must be shared using a chosen basis, which is a judgement [1], so a different basis gives a different cost per product and could make a product wrongly appear unprofitable [1].

**7.** **(a)** Margin of safety = 1 800 − 1 400 = 400 bags [1]; 400 ÷ 1 800 × 100 = 22.2% [1].

**(b)** Profit = 1 800 × 18 − 25 200 [1] = $7 200 [1].

**(c)** (25 200 + 9 000) ÷ 18 [1] = 34 200 ÷ 18 [1] = 1 900 bags [1].

**8.** **(a)** Revenue = 2 000 × 16 = $32 000; variable costs = 2 000 × 12 = $24 000 [1]. Loss = 32 000 − 24 000 − 12 000 = −$4 000 [1].

**(b)** Contribution = 2 000 × (16 − 12) = $8 000 [1].

**(c)** Rakes make a positive contribution of $8 000 [1]. The $12 000 of overheads allocated to rakes would remain if rakes were dropped [1], so total profit would fall by $8 000; Grisdale should keep making rakes unless it can use the capacity for something more profitable [1].

**9.** **(a)** Contribution per box = 40 − 35 = $5 [1]; extra profit = 300 × 5 = $1 500 [1].

**(b)** Any one, explained. Regular customers may learn of the $40 price and ask for the same discount [1], reducing profit on future orders [1]. Allow good publicity from helping a charity, or possible repeat orders.

**10.** The fixed cost line and the total cost line both shift upwards by the amount of the rise [1]. Total cost now crosses total revenue at a higher output [1], so break-even output rises [1].

**11.** Spare capacity = 3 000 − 2 800 = 200 chairs, so 300 regular sales would be lost [1]. Contribution gained = 500 × (70 − 55) = $7 500 [1]. Contribution lost = 300 × (90 − 55) = $10 500 [1]. Net effect = 7 500 − 10 500 = −$3 000 [1], so accepting the order would reduce profit [1].

**12.** Current position: contribution = 12 − 6 = $6; break-even = 24 000 ÷ 6 = 4 000 bags [1]; profit = 8 000 × 6 − 24 000 = $24 000 [1].
After the cut: contribution = 10 − 6 = $4; break-even = 24 000 ÷ 4 = 6 000 bags [1]. To keep profit at $24 000, sales must be (24 000 + 24 000) ÷ 4 = 12 000 bags, a 50% rise [1].
For the cut: a lower price may attract many new customers and win market share from rivals [1]; higher sales could bring bulk-buying discounts, lowering variable cost [1].
Against: sales must rise by 50% just to keep profit the same, which is a large increase [1]; the margin of safety would shrink, so the business becomes riskier [1]; rivals may match the price cut, so the extra sales may not happen [1].
Judgement: Callow should not cut the price unless market research shows demand will rise by more than 50%; break-even analysis assumes constant costs and that all output is sold, so it should be combined with evidence on how customers respond [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Dividing fixed costs by price instead of by contribution.
- Rounding break-even output down.
- Treating allocated overheads as if they would disappear when a product is dropped.
- Ignoring displaced regular sales when capacity is full.
- Evaluating a price cut without calculating the new break-even point or the sales needed.
