## Questions

**1.** Two cubes of agar have sides of 3 mm and 6 mm.
**(a)** Calculate the surface area to volume ratio of each. **[2]**
**(b)** A spherical single-celled organism has a diameter of 40 µm. Its surface area to volume ratio is 6 divided by the diameter. Calculate the ratio and explain why the organism needs no specialised gas exchange organ. **[3]**

**2.** Animal A has a mass of 20 g and uses 36 cm³ of oxygen per hour. Animal B has a mass of 400 g and uses 120 cm³ per hour.
**(a)** Calculate the oxygen used per gram per hour by each animal. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain the difference using surface area to volume ratio. **[2]**
**(c)** Describe two adaptations, each a change to body shape or a body system, that help a large organism exchange substances. **[2]**

**3.** **(a)** Describe how oxygen reaches the muscle cells of a flying insect. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain the compromise between gas exchange and water loss in insects. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain how the stomata and mesophyll of a dicotyledonous leaf allow gas exchange. **[2]**
**(d)** Explain how one feature of a xerophyte reduces water loss and state the cost to gas exchange. **[2]**

**4.** In a fish gill, water passes over the lamellae while blood flows through them in the opposite direction.
**(a)** Name this arrangement and explain how it helps oxygen uptake. **[2]**
**(b)** If blood and water flowed in the same direction, the two would reach about 50% saturation and then stop exchanging. Explain why. **[1]**
**(c)** In a counter-current gill, water entering at 100% saturation leaves at 10% saturation. Calculate the percentage of oxygen removed and how many percentage points more this is than the parallel-flow value. **[2]**

**5.** **(a)** A resting person has a tidal volume of 0.50 dm³ and breathes 12 times per minute. Calculate the pulmonary ventilation rate. **[1]**
**(b)** During exercise the rate is 45 dm³ per minute and the tidal volume 2.5 dm³. Calculate the breathing rate. **[2]**
**(c)** Describe three features of the alveolar epithelium that favour gas exchange. **[3]**

**6.** **(a)** Explain how the intercostal muscles and diaphragm cause inhalation. **[4]**
**(b)** In pulmonary fibrosis lung tissue is thickened and scarred. Explain how this reduces gas exchange. **[2]**

**7.** A study followed adults for 10 years. The numbers of lung disease cases per 1000 people were: non-smokers 12; 1 to 10 cigarettes per day 31; 11 to 20 per day 58; over 20 per day 94.
**(a)** Calculate how many times greater the incidence is in the 11 to 20 group than in non-smokers. **[1]**
**(b)** Evaluate whether the data show that smoking causes lung disease. **[4]**

**8.** **(a)** Describe how proteins are digested to amino acids. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain how amino acids are absorbed by co-transport. **[3]**
**(c)** Explain the roles of bile salts and micelles in the digestion and absorption of lipids. **[3]**

**9.** The table shows percentage saturation of haemoglobin from two animals at different partial pressures of oxygen (pO2).

| pO2 (kPa) | 2 | 4 | 6 | 8 | 10 | 13 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Adult human (%) | 20 | 53 | 80 | 91 | 95 | 97 |
| Animal X (%) | 40 | 75 | 92 | 96 | 98 | 98 |

**(a)** Calculate the difference in saturation at 4 kPa. **[1]**
**(b)** Suggest an advantage to animal X of its haemoglobin. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain the S-shape of the adult curve. **[2]**
**(d)** Explain how raised carbon dioxide at a muscle alters oxygen release. **[2]**

**10.** Pressures in the left side of a heart (kPa) at four times are: 0.05 s, atrium 0.8, ventricle 0.3, aorta 10.5; 0.15 s, atrium 0.6, ventricle 4.0, aorta 10.0; 0.25 s, atrium 0.4, ventricle 15.0, aorta 14.0; 0.45 s, atrium 0.5, ventricle 1.0, aorta 11.0.
**(a)** State the times at which the atrioventricular valve is closed and explain your choice. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why the semilunar valve is open at 0.25 s. **[1]**
**(c)** Stroke volume is 70 cm³ and heart rate 72 beats per minute. Calculate cardiac output in dm³ per minute. **[2]**
**(d)** Cardiac output rises to 7.2 dm³ per minute at a stroke volume of 80 cm³. Calculate the heart rate. **[2]**

**11.** At the arteriole end of a capillary, hydrostatic pressure is 4.3 kPa; at the venule end it is 1.9 kPa. The pull of the plasma proteins is 3.3 kPa throughout. Ignore pressure outside the capillary.
**(a)** Calculate the net pressure at each end, stating the direction of fluid movement. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain the formation and return of tissue fluid. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain how the structure of an artery and a vein suits each function. **[3]**

**12.** **(a)** Explain how water rises in the xylem. **[3]**
**(b)** A ring of bark and phloem is removed from a stem. Describe and explain the expected result. **[2]**
**(c)** A tracer moves 36 cm along a stem in 30 minutes. Calculate the speed in mm per second. **[1]**
**(d)** Give one piece of evidence for and one against the mass flow hypothesis. **[2]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** 3 mm cube: surface area 54 mm², volume 27 mm³, ratio **2 : 1** [1]. 6 mm cube: 216 mm² and 216 mm³, ratio **1 : 1** [1].
**(b)** Ratio = 6 / 40 = **0.15 per µm** [1]. The ratio is large, so oxygen diffuses across the surface fast enough [1] to supply the small volume of cytoplasm [1].
*Examiner insight:* Show surface area and volume before dividing.

**2. (a)** A: 36 / 20 = **1.8 cm³ per g per hour** [1]. B: 120 / 400 = **0.3 cm³ per g per hour** [1].
**(b)** A is smaller, so it has the larger surface area to volume ratio and loses heat faster [1]; it needs a higher metabolic rate per gram, using six times as much oxygen [1].
**(c)** Any two: flattened body shape gives a short diffusion distance [1]; a circulatory system carries substances around the body [1].
*Examiner insight:* Compare oxygen per gram, not total, to show the ratio link.

**3. (a)** Air enters through spiracles and passes along tracheae [1] to tracheoles reaching the muscle cells, where oxygen diffuses in [1].
**(b)** Open spiracles let oxygen in but water vapour out [1]; closing them reduces water loss but also restricts gas exchange [1].
**(c)** Stomata open so gases diffuse into air spaces [1]; the mesophyll cells' large wet surface lets gases dissolve and exchange [1].
**(d)** Sunken stomata or hairs trap humid air, lowering the water vapour gradient [1]; the cost is a longer diffusion path for CO2 [1].
*Examiner insight:* A compromise answer states both the benefit and the cost.

**4. (a)** Counter-current flow [1]; blood always meets water with a higher oxygen concentration, so a gradient remains along the whole lamella [1].
**(b)** Concentrations equalise partway along, so there is no gradient for further diffusion [1].
**(c)** 100 - 10 = **90%** removed [1]; 90 - 50 = **40 percentage points** more [1].
*Examiner insight:* Say "opposite directions" explicitly.

**5. (a)** 0.50 × 12 = **6.0 dm³ per minute** [1].
**(b)** Rate = 45 / 2.5 [1] = **18 per minute** [1].
**(c)** Any three: one cell thick, so a short diffusion distance [1]; close to a dense capillary network, keeping a gradient [1]; very many alveoli give a large area [1].
*Examiner insight:* Pair each feature with its effect.

**6. (a)** External intercostals contract, raising the ribs [1]; the diaphragm contracts and flattens [1]; thoracic volume increases [1]; pressure falls below atmospheric so air flows in [1].
**(b)** The thicker tissue lengthens the diffusion path [1], so less oxygen crosses per unit time [1].
*Examiner insight:* Name the pressure change; "air is sucked in" does not explain why.

**7. (a)** 58 / 12 = **4.8 times** [1].
**(b)** Incidence rises with cigarettes smoked, a trend that supports a link [1]. Correlation alone does not prove cause [1]; age or pollution might differ between groups [1]. A known mechanism, such as smoke damaging lung tissue, strengthens the case [1].
*Examiner insight:* A balanced evaluation ends with a judgement.

**8. (a)** Endopeptidases hydrolyse peptide bonds inside the chain [1]; exopeptidases remove terminal amino acids [1]; membrane-bound dipeptidases split dipeptides into amino acids [1].
**(b)** Sodium ions are actively transported out of the epithelial cell, lowering the internal concentration [1]; sodium enters down its gradient through a carrier that brings in an amino acid too [1]; the amino acid then leaves by facilitated diffusion into blood [1].
**(c)** Bile salts emulsify lipids, giving droplets with more surface for lipase [1]; lipase produces monoglycerides and fatty acids [1]; these join micelles, which carry them to the membrane where they diffuse in [1].
*Examiner insight:* Name sodium ions; co-transport with no ion gradient described is incomplete.

**9. (a)** 75 - 53 = **22 percentage points** [1].
**(b)** It is more saturated at low pO2, so it loads oxygen where oxygen is scarce, for example at a placenta [1]; X is 75% saturated at 4 kPa against 53% for the adult [1].
**(c)** The first oxygen changes the shape of haemoglobin [1], making the next ones bind more easily until saturation nears [1].
**(d)** The curve moves right (Bohr effect) [1], so haemoglobin releases more oxygen at the same pO2 [1].
*Examiner insight:* State the direction of the shift and its effect separately.

**10. (a)** **0.15, 0.25 and 0.45 s** [1], because ventricular pressure exceeds atrial pressure at those times [1].
**(b)** Ventricular pressure (15.0) exceeds aortic (14.0), forcing the valve open [1].
**(c)** 70 × 72 = 5040 cm³ = **5.04 dm³ per minute** [1]; unit conversion [1].
**(d)** 7200 / 80 = **90 beats per minute** [1]; conversion from dm³ shown [1].
*Examiner insight:* Quote the two pressures as figures when comparing them.

**11. (a)** Arteriole end: 4.3 - 3.3 = **1.0 kPa outward** [1]. Venule end: 1.9 - 3.3 = **-1.4 kPa, so inward** [1].
**(b)** Net outward pressure forms tissue fluid [1]; plasma proteins lower water potential, so water returns by osmosis at the venule end [1].
**(c)** Artery: thick elastic and muscular wall withstands and smooths high pressure [1]. Vein: thin wall and wide lumen suit low pressure [1]; valves stop backflow [1].
*Examiner insight:* The sign of each net value shows the direction.

**12. (a)** Transpiration at the leaf lowers pressure, so water is pulled up under tension [1]; hydrogen bonds make water molecules cohere [1] and adhere to the walls, keeping the column continuous [1].
**(b)** Sugars accumulate above the ring [1] because phloem transport is interrupted, while the xylem keeps working [1].
**(c)** 360 mm / 1800 s = **0.2 mm per second** [1].
**(d)** For: sap exudes from cut phloem under pressure, or a tracer moves faster than diffusion alone allows [1]. Against: sieve plates would be expected to slow bulk flow, or translocation stops when respiration is inhibited, which pressure flow alone does not explain [1].
*Examiner insight:* Convert units before dividing; a speed in the wrong unit can lose the final mark.

## Where marks are usually lost
- Leaving out units or conversions in ventilation and cardiac output.
- Describing co-transport without sodium ions.
- Judging risk factors by correlation alone.
- Confusing xylem (tension) with phloem (pressure flow).
