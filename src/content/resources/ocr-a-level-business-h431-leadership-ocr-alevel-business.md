## What leadership is

**Leadership** is the ability to influence other people so that they work willingly towards a goal. A leader sets direction, inspires effort and shapes the culture of a team. **Management** is wider: it includes planning, organising and controlling resources. A manager can be a poor leader (they run the budget well but nobody follows their lead), and a leader need not be a manager (a respected senior nurse may lead a ward without being its manager).

Leadership matters because the same team, with the same pay and equipment, can perform very differently under different leaders. That is why the theories below try to explain what makes leadership effective.

## The four families of theory

### Trait theory

Trait theory says leaders are **born, not made**. Carlyle (the "great man" idea) and Galton argued that leadership comes from inherited qualities such as intelligence, confidence, decisiveness and presence. If the theory is right, a business should select leaders by looking for those traits rather than training people.

**McGregor's Theory X and Theory Y** are often placed here because they describe what a manager believes about people:

- **Theory X:** most people dislike work, avoid responsibility and need to be controlled, directed and threatened. The leader therefore supervises closely and decides alone.
- **Theory Y:** people like work, accept responsibility and are self-motivated. The leader therefore delegates, consults and gives freedom.

McGregor's point is that the manager's belief shapes the style. A Theory X manager creates tight rules and so produces the passive behaviour they expected.

### Behavioural theory: Blake and Mouton's managerial grid

Behavioural theory looks at what leaders do, not what they are, so leadership can be learned. The **managerial grid** (the specification prints the name as Moulton; the authors are Blake and Mouton) scores a leader from 1 to 9 on two axes: **concern for production** (results, targets, efficiency) and **concern for people** (welfare, relationships, development).

| Grid position (production, people) | Name | What it looks like |
|---|---|---|
| 9, 1 | Authority-compliance | Results come first; people are treated as tools |
| 1, 9 | Country club | A friendly team; targets are neglected |
| 1, 1 | Impoverished | Minimum effort on both |
| 5, 5 | Middle of the road | A compromise that delivers moderate results |
| 9, 9 | Team management | High commitment and high output together |

The grid suggests that 9, 9 is the best position, whatever the situation. That is its main strength (a clear target for training) and its main weakness (it ignores context).

### Contingency theory: Lewin, and Tannenbaum and Schmidt

Contingency theory says there is **no single best style**; the right one depends on the situation, the task, the people and the time available.

- **Lewin** identified three styles: autocratic, democratic and laissez-faire. His research suggested that the effect of each on a group differs, so the leader should adapt.
- **Tannenbaum and Schmidt's behavioural continuum** is a line from **boss-centred** leadership (the manager decides and announces) through stages where the manager sells the decision, asks for views or lets the team decide within limits, to **subordinate-centred** leadership (the team decides). The leader chooses a point on the line by looking at three forces: the manager (values, confidence in staff), the subordinates (skills, wish for independence) and the situation (urgency, type of task).

### Functional theory: Adair's Three Circles

Adair's model, called **action-centred leadership**, says that effective leaders balance three overlapping needs:

- **Task:** what has to be achieved and by when.
- **Team:** keeping the group together and working well.
- **Individual:** the needs of each person, including development and recognition.

If the leader attends to one circle only, the other two suffer. A leader obsessed with the task loses the team; one obsessed with the team may miss deadlines.

## Leadership styles

| Style | How decisions are made | Suits | Risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Autocratic | The leader decides and gives orders | Crises, unskilled staff, safety-critical work | Low commitment, high turnover, no ideas from staff |
| Democratic | The leader consults and often follows the group view | Skilled staff, creative work, change that needs buy-in | Slow decisions; may be hard to reach agreement |
| Laissez-faire | Staff decide; the leader steps back | Experts and self-motivated teams | Drift, confusion, no direction |
| Paternalistic | The leader decides, but explains and acts in what they see as the staff's interests | Businesses valuing loyalty, a family culture | Staff may feel they are treated as children |

## Worked example and evaluation

Ferncliffe Care Group runs three care homes, each with 20 care staff. Each is led by a manager with a different style. Last year's figures are below. The cost of replacing one care worker who leaves (advertising, agency cover and training) is £3,200.

| Home | Leadership style | Leavers | Staff turnover | Replacement cost |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| North | Autocratic | 9 | 45% | £28,800 |
| Central | Democratic | 4 | 20% | £12,800 |
| South | Laissez-faire | 6 | 30% | £19,200 |

Working: turnover = leavers ÷ staff × 100. North: 9 ÷ 20 × 100 = 45%. Cost: 9 × £3,200 = £28,800. The gap between North and Central is £28,800 − £12,800 = **£16,000** a year.

**Using the theories.**

- *Trait theory* is weak here. The three managers were all chosen for confidence and experience, yet results differ, which suggests style matters more than inherited traits.
- *Blake and Mouton* would place North near 9, 1 and Central near 9, 9 (or 5, 9). The grid explains why North loses staff but gives no advice about when firm control is still right.
- *Tannenbaum and Schmidt* would say North's manager sits too far towards boss-centred for skilled care workers who expect a say.
- *Adair* would ask which circle North neglects. If the manager gives orders but never discusses development, it is the individual circle, and the 45% turnover would be the likely result.

**Judgement.** On this evidence the democratic style gives the best staff retention. But the figures cover one year and three managers, so other causes (location, building quality, local job markets) may play a part. Turnover is also only one measure; North may have had the best inspection scores. A fair conclusion is that style probably explains part of the gap, and the business should check results on care quality before changing North's manager.

## Evaluating theories and styles

Evaluating **usefulness** means saying what a theory helps a business do, and where it fails.

| Theory | Useful because | Limited because |
|---|---|---|
| Trait | Simple guide for selecting leaders | Little evidence leaders are born; ignores training |
| Theory X and Y | Shows how beliefs shape behaviour | Two extremes; most people are mixed |
| Managerial grid | Clear, trainable target | Assumes 9, 9 is always best |
| Contingency | Realistic; adapts to the situation | Needs a flexible leader and good judgement |
| Adair | Practical checklist of three needs | Does not say how to weigh the circles |

The stakeholders affected also differ. Staff value being consulted. Shareholders value results. Customers value consistent service. A style that pleases one group may disappoint another, so a good evaluation names the stakeholder.

## Impact and importance

Leadership affects:

- **Motivation and retention:** as the Ferncliffe figures show, style changes turnover and so cost.
- **Productivity and quality:** clear direction reduces wasted effort.
- **Culture:** leaders set what is acceptable; this spreads across the business.
- **Change:** leaders must gain support if new methods are to work.
- **Stakeholders:** owners gain from performance; staff from fair treatment; customers from service.

Its importance is greatest when the business is changing, when staff are skilled and mobile, and when competitors can hire away unhappy employees. It is less decisive where rules are fixed and tasks are routine, because the system itself guides behaviour.

## Recommending a style

To **recommend and justify**, choose a style, give two or three reasons linked to the case, state a risk and say what would change your mind.

*Example.* A software firm with 40 skilled developers is launching a new product in six weeks. Recommend a democratic style, moving towards autocratic in the final two weeks. Reasons: skilled staff expect to contribute and may leave if over-controlled; but a hard deadline needs firm decisions near launch. Risk: switching style may confuse staff, so the leader should explain the change in advance. This would change if a key client demanded a rapid decision in week one.

## Common mistakes

- Treating the four styles as a theory. Styles are descriptions; contingency theory is the explanation of when to use each.
- Writing that the democratic style is "best" without the situation.
- Using Theory X and Y as the names of styles, rather than as beliefs about workers.
- Describing a theory without applying it to the case.
- Forgetting a judgement in "evaluate" and "recommend" answers.

## Exam technique

"Explain" needs the meaning and one linked example. "Distinguish" needs both items and the difference. "Evaluate" needs two sides, a judgement and what it depends on. "Recommend and justify" needs a clear choice, reasons from the case and a risk.
