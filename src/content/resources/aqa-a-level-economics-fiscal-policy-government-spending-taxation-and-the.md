## The tools of fiscal policy

**Fiscal policy** means deliberately adjusting what the government spends, what it raises in tax and the resulting budget balance. The government can change how much it spends, what it spends on, how much it raises in tax, which taxes it uses and who pays them. Because spending and tax revenue together fix the budget balance, any change in either alters it. Whether the resulting deficit or surplus is wise is a separate question, covered elsewhere.

Fiscal policy has two kinds of function:

- **Macroeconomic:** influencing aggregate demand, the economy's growth, employment, inflation and the balance of payments. Cutting taxes in a downturn is a macro use.
- **Microeconomic:** changing outcomes in particular markets or for particular groups. A tax on a harmful product, a subsidy for a merit good or a benefit for low-income households is a micro use.

One measure often does both. A rise in tax on tobacco corrects an externality in one market and also raises revenue.

## Influencing aggregate demand

Aggregate demand is C + I + G + (X − M). Fiscal policy acts on it in two ways.

- **Government spending (G)** is a direct component. A rise shifts AD to the right.
- **Taxation** works indirectly. Lower income tax raises disposable income and so consumption. Lower corporation tax can raise investment. Higher taxes do the reverse.

A change in G or in tax causes a bigger change in income through the **multiplier**. The multiplier is 1 / (1 − MPC), where MPC is the marginal propensity to consume: the share of each extra unit of income that is spent on domestic output and so passed on. The rest leaks out as saving, tax and imports. AQA requires the multiplier to be calculated from the MPC only; calculations from the marginal propensities to withdraw are not expected.

**Worked example (Country A, illustrative).** MPC = 0.50.

1. 1 − MPC = 1 − 0.50 = 0.50.
2. Multiplier = 1 / 0.50 = 2.
3. Government spending rises by 6bn. Change in income = 6 × 2 = 12bn.

Now compare a tax cut of 6bn. Households save or spend on imports 30% of it and spend 70% at home, so the first-round injection is 6 × 0.7 = 4.2bn. Income rises by 4.2 × 2 = 8.4bn. Spending gives a bigger boost than an equal tax cut because the whole sum enters the circular flow at once.

**Chain of reasoning:** government spending rises → AD shifts right → firms with spare capacity raise output and hire → incomes rise → further spending (multiplier) → real GDP and employment rise. If the economy is near full capacity, the same shift mainly raises the price level.

## Influencing aggregate supply

Fiscal policy can also change productive capacity, mostly over the longer run.

- **Capital spending** on roads, rail or digital networks lowers firms' costs and raises productivity.
- **Spending on education, training and health** improves the quality of labour.
- **Tax incentives** can raise the supply of labour (lower income tax on extra earnings), investment (lower corporation tax or allowances for equipment) or research.

A successful policy shifts long-run aggregate supply right, raising potential output and lowering inflationary pressure at any given growth rate. Evaluate with time lags, uncertainty over how far people respond to incentives, and the fact that a project may be badly chosen.

## The pattern of economic activity

Spending and tax change **what is produced, where and by whom**.

- Spending on a new rail line moves jobs and investment to the regions it serves.
- A tax on fuel raises the relative cost of road transport, shifting demand to rail and encouraging fuel-efficient vehicles.
- Higher public spending raises the public sector's share of output. Heavy borrowing to fund it may raise interest rates and reduce private investment (**crowding out**), shifting activity from private to public use.
- Subsidies shift resources towards the favoured industry at the expense of others.

## Types of and reasons for public expenditure

Public expenditure falls into three types:

- **Current spending** on the day-to-day running of services, such as teachers' and nurses' pay.
- **Capital spending** on assets that last, such as hospitals and roads.
- **Transfer payments** such as pensions and unemployment benefit, which move income without buying output.

**Reasons:** provide public goods (private markets will not); provide or subsidise merit goods such as education and health; redistribute income; correct externalities; stabilise the economy; and invest in capacity for growth.

**Country A's spending (illustrative, billions; GDP = 1,000).**

| Category | Amount | Share of total | Type |

| Health | 100 | 25.0% | Current |
| Education | 60 | 15.0% | Current |
| Defence | 30 | 7.5% | Current |
| Transport infrastructure | 40 | 10.0% | Capital |
| Public order and other | 30 | 7.5% | Current |
| **Total** | **400** | 100% | 40.0% of GDP |

## Why governments levy taxes

- **Raise revenue** to pay for public expenditure.
- **Redistribute** income and wealth through progressive taxes and benefits.
- **Change behaviour and correct market failure**, for example taxing a good with negative externalities.
- **Manage aggregate demand** by raising or cutting tax.
- **Influence the pattern of activity**, for example by protecting a sector or encouraging saving.

## Direct and indirect taxes

A **direct tax** is levied on income, wealth or profit and borne by the person or firm taxed (income tax, corporation tax). An **indirect tax** is levied on spending and collected by a seller who passes it on in the price, so the burden can fall on someone else (a sales tax or an excise duty).

Country A's revenue (billions): income tax 130, social contributions 80, corporation tax 40, property tax 15, sales tax 90, excise duties 25. The total is 380, or 38% of GDP. Direct taxes are 265 (69.7%) and indirect 115 (30.3%). Direct taxes are easier to make progressive. Indirect taxes are harder to avoid but tend to take a larger share of low incomes.

## Progressive, proportional and regressive taxes

Compare the tax as a share of income (the **average rate**) at different incomes. The **marginal rate** is the rate on the next unit of income.

- **Progressive:** the average rate rises with income.
- **Proportional:** the average rate is constant.
- **Regressive:** the average rate falls as income rises.

**Worked example.** Country A's income tax has a tax-free allowance of 7,500, 16% on income from 7,500 to 32,500 and 36% above 32,500.

| Income | Working | Tax | Average rate |
|---|---|---|---|
| 6,000 | below allowance | 0 | 0% |
| 24,000 | (24,000 − 7,500) × 0.16 | 2,640 | 11.0% |
| 50,000 | 25,000 × 0.16 + 17,500 × 0.36 | 10,300 | 20.6% |
| 100,000 | 4,000 + 67,500 × 0.36 | 28,300 | 28.3% |

The average rate rises, so the tax is progressive. A flat 15% on all income would be proportional.

**An indirect tax can be regressive.** Suppose a household pays an effective 10% on its spending. A household on 20,000 spends 95% of its income (19,000) and pays 1,900, or 9.5% of income. A household on 100,000 spends 55% (55,000) and pays 5,500, or 5.5%. The richer household pays more in total but a smaller share of income, so the tax is regressive. A flat licence fee is the extreme case.

## Principles of taxation

A good tax meets agreed criteria:

- **Equity (fairness):** *vertical equity* asks that those with greater ability to pay contribute more; *horizontal equity* asks that people in similar circumstances pay similar amounts. The *benefit principle* says payment should match benefit received.
- **Efficiency:** the tax should distort decisions as little as possible, apart from where distortion is the aim.
- **Certainty and simplicity:** people should know what they owe and when.
- **Low administrative and compliance costs.**
- **Flexibility:** revenue should be able to adjust to changing needs.

Principles conflict. A highly progressive system scores well on vertical equity but may weaken the incentive to work, and complex reliefs that make a tax fairer also make it less simple.

## The role and merits of UK taxes

| Tax | Role | Merits | Weaknesses |
|---|---|---|---|
| Income tax | Large direct tax on earnings | Progressive, raises substantial revenue | Can weaken work incentives; avoidance |
| National Insurance | Contributions on earnings | Reliable revenue | Adds to labour costs |
| Corporation tax | Tax on company profits | Taxes ability to pay of firms | Can deter investment; profits can be shifted |
| VAT | Broad tax on spending | Hard to avoid, wide base | Regressive |
| Excise duties | Tax on fuel, alcohol, tobacco | Corrects externalities, reliable | Regressive, black-market risk |
| Council tax | Local tax on property | Simple to collect | Linked to property bands, not current income |

Judge each tax against the principles above, and say who bears it.

## Assessing level and distribution

You must **assess the economic significance** of changes in the level and distribution of spending and tax.

- **Level:** a higher overall level of spending or tax shifts AD. The effect depends on spare capacity, the multiplier, how it is financed, confidence and time lags. Country A spends 40% of GDP, so a 1% cut in its spending would remove 4bn, and with a multiplier of 2 a fall in income of up to 8bn.
- **Distribution:** who gets the spending and who pays the tax matters. Poorer households spend a larger share of extra income, so transfers to them raise AD more than the same sum going to rich households. A shift from direct to indirect taxes can make the system less progressive.

**Worked example.** Using the income tax schedule above, a 24,000 earner keeps 21,360 and a 100,000 earner keeps 71,700. The gross income ratio is 4.17 to 1, but after tax it is 3.36 to 1, so the tax narrows the gap. Add the indirect taxes and the gap narrows by less.

A strong evaluation weighs short-run against long-run effects, equity against efficiency, and whether the economy is in a recession or near full capacity.

## Common errors

- Saying a tax is progressive because the *amount* paid rises with income. Compare the average rate.
- Using a marginal rate where the average rate is asked for.
- Writing that transfers are part of G in AD. Transfers affect AD only when recipients spend them.
- Assuming that a tax cut and a spending rise of equal size have equal effects.
- Listing taxes without judging them against a principle.

## Frequently asked questions

**What are the macroeconomic and microeconomic functions of fiscal policy?**
Macroeconomic functions influence aggregate demand, growth, employment, inflation and the balance of payments. Microeconomic functions change outcomes in particular markets or for particular groups, such as taxing a harmful good.

**What are the three types of public expenditure?**
Current spending on running services, capital spending on lasting assets such as hospitals and roads, and transfer payments such as pensions and benefits.

**What are the principles of a good tax?**
Equity, efficiency, certainty and simplicity, low administrative and compliance costs, and flexibility. These principles often conflict.

**Can an indirect tax be regressive?**
Yes. Poorer households spend a larger share of their income, so a tax on spending such as VAT can take a larger share of a poor household's income than a rich one's.

**How can fiscal policy affect aggregate supply?**
Capital spending on infrastructure, spending on education, training and health, and tax incentives for work and investment can raise productive capacity and shift LRAS right.
