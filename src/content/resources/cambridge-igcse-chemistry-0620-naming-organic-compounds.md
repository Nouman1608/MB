This guide goes deeper into subtopic **11.2 Naming organic compounds** of the Cambridge IGCSE Chemistry 0620 syllabus for 2026–2028. The broader [Organic Chemistry: Formulae and Naming](/resources/organic-chemistry-formulae-and-naming/) guide gives the endings and the idea of locant numbers. This page sets out a step-by-step naming method, lists every compound the syllabus expects you to name and draw, explains the lowest-number rule, and shows how to split an ester formula into its alcohol and acid parts. For quick recall use the [naming revision notes](/resources/cambridge-igcse-chemistry-0620-naming-organic-compounds-revision-notes/), and practise with the [naming practice questions](/resources/cambridge-igcse-chemistry-0620-naming-organic-compounds-practice/).

## What 11.2 asks you to do

| Outcome | Tier | What you must be able to do |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Core | Name and draw the displayed formulae of methane, ethane, ethene, ethanol and ethanoic acid, and of the products of the reactions in subtopics 11.4–11.7 |
| 2 | Core | State the type of compound present, given a name ending in -ane, -ene, -ol or -oic acid, or a molecular or displayed formula |
| 3 | Supplement | Name and draw the structural and displayed formulae of unbranched alkanes, alkenes (including but-1-ene and but-2-ene), alcohols (including propan-1-ol, propan-2-ol, butan-1-ol and butan-2-ol) and carboxylic acids, with up to four carbon atoms |
| 4 | Supplement | Name and draw the displayed formulae of the unbranched esters that can be made from unbranched alcohols and carboxylic acids, each with up to four carbon atoms |

## The naming method

Every name in 11.2 is built from two parts.

**1. The stem gives the number of carbon atoms in the longest chain.**

| Stem | meth- | eth- | prop- | but- |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Carbon atoms | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 |

**2. The ending gives the homologous series.**

| Ending | Series | Functional group |
|---|---|---|
| -ane | alkane | none (all C–C single) |
| -ene | alkene | C=C |
| -ol | alcohol | –OH |
| -oic acid | carboxylic acid | –COOH |

So **propanoic acid** is a carboxylic acid with three carbon atoms, and **butene** is an alkene with four. For a carboxylic acid, the carbon of the –COOH group **is counted** in the stem: ethanoic acid, CH₃COOH, has two carbons.

## Core compounds to name and draw

You must be able to draw the displayed formula of each of these.

```
methane          ethane              ethene
    H                H   H           H       H
    |                |   |            \     /
H — C — H        H — C — C — H         C = C
    |                |   |            /     \
    H                H   H           H       H
```

Ethanol and ethanoic acid are drawn in the [formulae and terminology study guide](/resources/cambridge-igcse-chemistry-0620-formulae-functional-groups-and-terminology/).

**Products of reactions in 11.4–11.7.** Outcome 1 also covers the organic products of the reactions in those subtopics. Each product sits at the same tier as the reaction that makes it. The main ones are:

| Reaction | Organic product |
|---|---|
| methane + chlorine in ultraviolet light (monosubstitution) | chloromethane, CH₃Cl |
| ethene + bromine | 1,2-dibromoethane, CH₂BrCH₂Br |
| ethene + hydrogen (nickel catalyst) | ethane |
| ethene + steam (acid catalyst) | ethanol |
| fermentation of glucose | ethanol |
| oxidation of ethanol | ethanoic acid |
| ethanoic acid + metal, base or carbonate | an ethanoate salt, e.g. sodium ethanoate, CH₃COONa |
| carboxylic acid + alcohol (acid catalyst) | an ester, e.g. ethyl ethanoate |

## Identifying the type of compound (Core outcome 2)

You may be given a name, a molecular formula or a displayed formula.

- **From a name:** read the ending.
- **From a molecular formula:** compare with the general formulae. C₃H₈ fits CₙH₂ₙ₊₂ (alkane); C₃H₆ fits CₙH₂ₙ (alkene); a formula ending in OH is an alcohol; one ending in COOH is a carboxylic acid.
- **From a displayed formula:** look for the functional group: C=C, O–H on a carbon with no C=O, or a carbon with both C=O and O–H.

That last point matters: ethanoic acid contains an O–H, but it is not an alcohol, because the same carbon also has C=O. Together they make –COOH.

## Unbranched compounds up to four carbons (Supplement)

| Carbons | Alkane | Alkene | Alcohol | Carboxylic acid |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | methane CH₄ | – | methanol CH₃OH | methanoic acid HCOOH |
| 2 | ethane CH₃CH₃ | ethene CH₂=CH₂ | ethanol CH₃CH₂OH | ethanoic acid CH₃COOH |
| 3 | propane CH₃CH₂CH₃ | propene CH₂=CHCH₃ | propan-1-ol CH₃CH₂CH₂OH; propan-2-ol CH₃CH(OH)CH₃ | propanoic acid CH₃CH₂COOH |
| 4 | butane CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₃ | but-1-ene CH₂=CHCH₂CH₃; but-2-ene CH₃CH=CHCH₃ | butan-1-ol CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂OH; butan-2-ol CH₃CH(OH)CH₂CH₃ | butanoic acid CH₃CH₂CH₂COOH |

### Locant numbers and the lowest-number rule

A **locant** is the number that shows which carbon atom carries the double bond or the –OH group. Number the chain from the end that gives the **lowest** possible number.

- CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂OH: counting from the –OH end, the –OH is on carbon 1, so **butan-1-ol**. Counting from the other end would give "butan-4-ol", which is never used.
- CH₃CH₂CH(OH)CH₃: from the right, –OH is on carbon 2, so **butan-2-ol**, not butan-3-ol.
- CH₃CH₂CH=CH₂: the double bond starts at carbon 1 from the right, so **but-1-ene**, not but-3-ene.

**When no locant is needed:** methanol, ethanol, ethene and propene have only one possible position, so no number is written. Carboxylic acids never need one, because the –COOH carbon is always carbon 1.

### Worked example: drawing butan-2-ol

**Question.** Draw the displayed formula of butan-2-ol. [2]

```
    H   H   H   H
    |   |   |   |
H — C — C — C — C — H
    |   |   |   |
    H   O   H   H
        |
        H
```

*One mark for a four-carbon chain with the –OH on the second carbon; one mark for every bond, including O–H, and the correct number of hydrogens (ten in total).*

## Naming esters (Supplement)

An ester is made from a carboxylic acid and an alcohol. Its name has two words:

1. **first word** from the **alcohol**, ending in **-yl** (methyl, ethyl, propyl, butyl);
2. **second word** from the **acid**, ending in **-oate** (methanoate, ethanoate, propanoate, butanoate).

Ethanol + propanoic acid gives **ethyl propanoate**. The syllabus limits esters to unbranched ones from alcohols and acids with up to four carbon atoms each; in practice the alcohol part comes from methanol, ethanol, propan-1-ol or butan-1-ol.

### Splitting an ester formula

In a structural formula the **acid part is usually written first**, which is the opposite of the name. Find the –COO– group:

- the carbon chain attached to the **C** of COO, **counting that C**, is the acid part;
- the chain attached to the single-bonded **O** is the alcohol part.

**Worked example.** Name CH₃CH₂COOCH₃. [2]

```
CH3CH2CO– | –O–CH3
acid part: 3 carbons → propanoate     [1]
alcohol part: 1 carbon → methyl
name: methyl propanoate                [1]
```

The common wrong answer, "propyl methanoate", comes from reading the formula left to right.

## Common mistakes

- **Not counting the –COOH carbon in the stem.** CH₃COOH is ethanoic acid (two carbons), not methanoic acid.
- **Using the higher locant.** Butan-3-ol and but-3-ene are always wrong.
- **Adding unnecessary numbers.** Ethanol has only one possible position for –OH, so no locant is needed; write ethanol, not "ethan-1-ol".
- **Calling ethanoic acid an alcohol** because it contains O–H.
- **Reversing the ester name.** The alcohol part comes first.
- **Writing "ethanate" or "ethylate".** The correct forms are ethanoate and ethyl.

## Exam technique for 11.2 command words

- **Name:** stem + ending, with a locant only where needed.
- **Draw:** displayed formulae with every bond; check every carbon has four.
- **State** the type of compound: give the series name and the evidence.
- **Deduce** (esters): split at –COO–, name the alcohol part first.
