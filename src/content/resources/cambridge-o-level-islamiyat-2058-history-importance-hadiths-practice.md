**How to use this set.** In Paper 2 this subtopic is Question 2, which every candidate answers: an
essay question worth 14 marks, normally a knowledge part (a) worth 10 and an understanding part (b)
worth 4. Section A builds recall of terms and names; Section B gives full exam-style questions.

## Section A — Short questions

**1.** Define **isnad** and **matn**. **[2]**

**2.** Give **three** ways in which the Companions learned the Sunna during the Prophet's lifetime.
**[3]**

**3.** Name **two** early written collections (sahifas) and the person associated with each. **[4]**

**4.** What did Umar ibn Abd al-Aziz order, and why? **[3]**

**5.** Explain the difference between a musnad and a musannaf collection, naming **one** example of
each. **[4]**

**6.** Name the **four** Shi'a collections of Hadith. **[4]**

**7.** Explain the meaning of the grades **sahih** and **mawdu'**. **[2]**

## Section B — Exam-style questions

**8. (a)** Describe how the Hadiths were preserved and collected from the Prophet's lifetime to the
time of Umar ibn Abd al-Aziz. **[10]**
**(b)** Explain why the early written collections were important for later compilers. **[4]**

**9. (a)** Write about the main features of the four Sunan collections in the six Sunni books. **[10]**
**(b)** Why do Sunni Muslims give the two Sahih collections a higher status than the Sunan? **[4]**

**10. (a)** Describe the four Shi'a collections of Hadith and their compilers. **[10]**
**(b)** Explain why Shi'a scholars give special weight to Hadiths narrated through the Prophet's
family and the Imams. **[4]**

**11. (a)** Describe the methods scholars used to test the isnad and the matn of a Hadith. **[10]**
**(b)** Explain why it was necessary to develop these methods. **[4]**

## Answers

**How Section B is marked.** Levels of response: part (a) Level 4 = 8–10, Level 3 = 5–7,
Level 2 = 3–4, Level 1 = 1–2; part (b) Level 2 = 3–4, Level 1 = 1–2. Each developed point below earns
[2]; credit the best five in (a) (maximum 10) and the best two in (b) (maximum 4). Other accurate
points of the same quality earn the same credit.

**1.** The isnad is the chain of narrators through whom a Hadith was passed down [1]; the matn is the
text, the actual words or description of the action [1].

**2.** Hearing his words and memorising them [1]; watching his actions, as in prayer and hajj [1];
noting what he saw or heard and approved without objection [1].

**3.** Any two, [2] each: al-Sahifa al-Sadiqa, written by Abdullah ibn Amr ibn al-As with the
Prophet's permission; the sahifa of Hammam ibn Munabbih, from his teacher Abu Hurayra; the sheet kept
by Ali with rulings such as those on blood money and captives. Maximum 4.

**4.** He ordered governors and scholars to write down the Hadiths [1], because many who had learned
them were dying and he feared the knowledge would be lost [1]; scholars such as Ibn Shihab al-Zuhri
carried out the work [1].

**5.** A musnad is arranged by the Companion who narrated each Hadith [1], for example the Musnad of
Ahmad ibn Hanbal [1]; a musannaf is arranged by subject, such as prayer or trade [1], for example
al-Muwatta of Imam Malik [1].

**6.** Al-Kafi [1]; Man la yahduruhu al-faqih [1]; Tahdhib al-ahkam [1]; al-Istibsar [1].

**7.** Sahih means sound: an unbroken chain of reliable narrators and a text free of defects [1].
Mawdu' means fabricated: falsely attributed to the Prophet (pbuh) and rejected [1].

**8. (a)** Credit the best five, [2] each:
- In the Prophet's lifetime Companions memorised his words and copied his actions; he said "Pray as
  you have seen me pray" [2].
- Some wrote with permission, such as Abdullah ibn Amr's al-Sahifa al-Sadiqa; writing was limited at
  first, reportedly so it would not be confused with the Qur'an [2].
- After 632 Companions taught in new centres such as Kufa, Basra and Damascus; Abu Hurayra, Aisha,
  Ibn Umar, Anas and Ibn Abbas narrated many Hadiths [2].
- Abu Bakr and Umar were cautious, and Umar asked for a witness to a Hadith to show reports must be
  checked [2].
- The tabi'un travelled to collect Hadiths, and sahifas such as that of Hammam ibn Munabbih
  circulated [2].
- Umar ibn Abd al-Aziz (717–720) ordered official written collection, with scholars including al-Zuhri
  [2].
**(b)** Credit the best two, [2] each:
- They were written close to the source, so later compilers could compare them with oral reports and
  check wording [2].
- They showed that Hadiths had been written down from the first generation, answering claims that
  everything relied only on memory [2].
- Their material was absorbed into later works, such as the Musnad of Ahmad ibn Hanbal, which
  includes Hammam's narrations [2].

**9. (a)** Credit the best five, [2] each:
- A sunan collection concentrates on Hadiths with legal rulings, arranged by subject from
  purification to trade and inheritance [2].
- Abu Dawud's Sunan gathers Hadiths used by jurists, and he noted where a Hadith had a weakness [2].
- Al-Tirmidhi's Jami' comments on the grade of many Hadiths and records which scholars acted on them,
  so it is valued for comparing legal opinions [2].
- Al-Nasa'i's Sunan is known for strict judgement of narrators, and is often placed closest to the two
  Sahihs [2].
- Ibn Majah's Sunan includes many Hadiths found in none of the other five, which made it valuable,
  though it contains more weak reports [2].
- Unlike the two Sahihs, the Sunan include some hasan and da'if Hadiths, often with the compiler's
  note on their status [2].
**(b)** Credit the best two, [2] each:
- Al-Bukhari and Muslim accepted only Hadiths meeting the strictest conditions of narrator
  reliability and continuity, while the Sunan include some weaker reports [2].
- Later scholars examined the two Sahihs in great detail and their agreement on most contents
  strengthened confidence; they are called the most reliable books after the Qur'an [2].

**10. (a)** Credit the best five, [2] each:
- Al-Kafi was compiled by al-Kulayni and is the largest and most important; it is divided into
  sections on belief (usul) and law (furu') [2].
- Al-Kafi includes reports from the Imams as well as the Prophet, since Shi'a Muslims accept the
  Imams' words as authoritative [2].
- Man la yahduruhu al-faqih, "for one who has no jurist present", was compiled by Ibn Babawayh, known
  as al-Shaykh al-Saduq, as a guide to law for ordinary believers [2].
- Tahdhib al-ahkam was compiled by al-Tusi, explaining the reports behind legal rulings [2].
- Al-Istibsar, also by al-Tusi, deals with reports that seem to conflict and shows how they can be
  reconciled [2].
- Together these four are called al-Kutub al-Arba'a, the main source of Shi'a law after the Qur'an
  [2].
**(b)** Credit the best two, [2] each:
- Shi'a Muslims believe the Imams were divinely guided and protected from error, so their reports
  preserve the Sunna faithfully [2].
- The Prophet's family lived closest to him and knew his practice in private life best; the Hadith
  of the two weighty things links the Qur'an with his family [2].

**11. (a)** Credit the best five, [2] each:
- Each narrator in the isnad had to be of upright character and practice, known for honesty [2].
- Each narrator had to have a reliable memory or accurate written record [2].
- The chain had to be continuous back to the Prophet, each narrator able to have met the one before;
  al-Bukhari required proof that they had actually met [2].
- Scholars compiled biographical dictionaries of narrators ('ilm al-rijal) giving teachers, students,
  dates and reputation, to check these conditions [2].
- The matn had to agree with the Qur'an and established Sunna, and not contradict reason or known
  facts [2].
- Its language had to fit the Prophet's style; exaggerated promises of huge rewards for small acts
  were warning signs [2].
- Hadiths were then graded sahih, hasan, da'if or mawdu' [2].
**(b)** Credit the best two, [2] each:
- After the Prophet's death some people invented Hadiths for political, sectarian or personal reasons,
  so false reports had to be separated from true ones [2].
- Hadiths are a source of law, so a false Hadith could lead to wrong rulings on worship or rights [2].
- Testing protected the Prophet's words from distortion, as he warned against lying about him [2].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing only about al-Bukhari when the question asks about the Sunan or the Shi'a books.
- Swapping the meanings of musnad and musannaf.
- Naming a test without explaining it, such as "the narrator must be reliable" with no detail.

## Frequently asked questions

**How much do I need to know about each compiler?**
Their name, their collection and its main features: arrangement, strictness and purpose.

**Is the importance of the Hadiths asked in part (a) as well?**
It can be. If part (a) asks you to describe their use in law, give examples of how a Hadith details a
Qur'anic command.
