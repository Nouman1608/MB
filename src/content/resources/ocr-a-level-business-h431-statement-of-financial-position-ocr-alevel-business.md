## What it shows

The **statement of financial position** (formerly the balance sheet) is a snapshot of what a business owns, owes and is worth **on a particular date**. It rests on the equation:

**Net assets = Equity**, that is, **Assets − Liabilities = Shareholders' equity**.

| Section | Items |
|---|---|
| **Non-current assets** | Assets kept for more than a year: buildings, machinery, vehicles, intangibles such as patents and goodwill, long-term investments |
| **Current assets** | Inventory, receivables, cash |
| **Current liabilities** | Payables, overdraft, tax due: due within a year |
| **Working capital** | Current assets − current liabilities |
| **Non-current liabilities** | Debts due after more than a year: long-term bank loans, debentures |
| **Net assets** | Non-current assets + working capital − non-current liabilities |
| **Equity** | Share capital + retained earnings (and other reserves) |

**Retained earnings** are the profits kept in the business over its life. **Total shareholders' equity** is the owners' stake: what would be left for them if all assets were sold at their book values and all liabilities paid.

### Worked example 1: completing a statement

Alder Engineering plc has these balances at 31 March.

- Non-current assets at cost £640,000; accumulated depreciation £190,000.
- Inventory £85,000; receivables (after a provision for doubtful debts) £112,000; cash £18,000.
- Payables £74,000; bank overdraft £16,000.
- Bank loan repayable in five years £180,000.
- Share capital £150,000.

| | £ |
|---|---|
| Non-current assets (cost £640,000 less depreciation £190,000) | 450,000 |
| Inventory | 85,000 |
| Receivables | 112,000 |
| Cash | 18,000 |
| **Current assets** | **215,000** |
| Payables | (74,000) |
| Overdraft | (16,000) |
| **Current liabilities** | **(90,000)** |
| **Working capital** | **125,000** |
| **Total assets less current liabilities** | **575,000** |
| Non-current liabilities: bank loan | (180,000) |
| **Net assets** | **395,000** |
| Share capital | 150,000 |
| Retained earnings (balancing figure) | 245,000 |
| **Total shareholders' equity** | **395,000** |

Working: 450,000 + 125,000 = 575,000; 575,000 − 180,000 = 395,000; retained earnings = 395,000 − 150,000 = 245,000, so that net assets equal equity.

Analysis: working capital is positive (£125,000), the current ratio is 215,000 ÷ 90,000 = 2.4, and the business is funded mainly by equity (£395,000) with a loan of £180,000, so gearing (non-current liabilities ÷ capital employed of 575,000) is 31.3%. Retained earnings are 62% of equity (245,000 ÷ 395,000), which shows that most of the shareholders' stake comes from profits kept in the business, not from new shares.

## Valuing assets

Assets appear at the amount at which they are valued, which is not always what they would sell for. Different assets raise different problems.

| Asset | How valued | Issues |
|---|---|---|
| Non-current assets | Cost less accumulated depreciation (net book value) | May differ from market value; property usually rises in value, equipment falls faster than the depreciation charge |
| Investments | Cost, or market value | Market value is more up to date but changes daily |
| Patents | Cost, written off (amortised) over the legal life | Hard to value: the real value depends on future earnings |
| Goodwill | Only recorded when a business is bought: price paid minus fair value of net assets | Intangible; can be overstated; may need to be written down if the acquired business does badly |
| Receivables | Amount owed less a provision for doubtful debts | A provision is an estimate; a **bad debt** is a debt that will not be paid and is written off |
| Inventories | Lower of cost and net realisable value | Obsolete or damaged stock must be written down |

**Examples (invented).**

- Investments: Alder bought shares for £25,000 that are now worth £31,000. At cost they appear at £25,000; at market value, £31,000, a difference of £6,000.
- Goodwill: Alder buys a small firm for £300,000 when its net assets are fairly valued at £240,000. Goodwill is £300,000 − £240,000 = **£60,000**.
- Patents: a patent bought for £40,000 with a legal life of ten years is written off at £4,000 a year.
- Receivables: gross receivables were £118,500 and the provision for doubtful debts is £6,500, so they are shown at **£112,000**.

The principle of **prudence** means assets are not overstated: if value falls below the recorded amount, the asset is written down at once.

## Depreciation

**Depreciation** is the reduction in the value of a non-current asset over its life, through wear, age or obsolescence. **Providing for depreciation** means charging part of the cost of the asset to the income statement each year.

**Why it is needed.**

- It follows the **accruals (matching)** convention: the cost of an asset is spread over the years that benefit from it.
- It avoids overstating profit (if the cost were ignored) and overstating assets.
- It records the fall in value on the statement of financial position.
- It helps the business plan to replace the asset, though it does not itself set aside cash.

### Worked example 2: two methods

Alder buys a machine for £90,000. It expects to use it for five years and then sell it for £10,000 (the residual value).

**Straight line:** the same charge every year = (cost − residual value) ÷ life = (90,000 − 10,000) ÷ 5 = **£16,000** a year.

**Reducing balance at 30%:** the rate is applied to the net book value (NBV) at the start of each year.

| Year | Straight line charge | NBV (SL) | Reducing balance charge | NBV (RB) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | £16,000 | £74,000 | £27,000 | £63,000 |
| 2 | £16,000 | £58,000 | £18,900 | £44,100 |
| 3 | £16,000 | £42,000 | £13,230 | £30,870 |

Working: reducing balance year 1 = 30% × 90,000 = 27,000; NBV = 63,000. Year 2 = 30% × 63,000 = 18,900; NBV = 44,100. Year 3 = 30% × 44,100 = 13,230; NBV = 30,870.

**Interpretation.** Reducing balance charges £11,000 more in year 1 (27,000 − 16,000) and so reports lower profit and lower net assets early on, but less in later years. It suits assets that lose value quickly, such as vehicles or computers, or whose repair costs rise with age (so total cost of use is spread evenly). Straight line is simple and suits assets that give even service. Whichever method is chosen, the **consistency** convention requires the same method each year.

## Impact of depreciation

- **Profit:** lowers profit each year by the charge. A higher charge in year 1 under reducing balance reduces year 1 profit by £11,000 relative to straight line.
- **Net assets and equity:** lower by the accumulated depreciation.
- **Cash:** unchanged. Depreciation is not a cash payment; the cash left when the asset was bought. Lower reported profit may mean lower dividends, which leaves more cash in the business.
- **Ratios:** lower profit reduces margins and ROCE; lower net assets can increase gearing.
- **Stakeholders:** shareholders see lower profit and dividends; lenders may look at profit before depreciation to judge cash generation; managers may be tempted to change method or life to flatter profit, which is why consistency matters.

## Evaluating usefulness

**Uses.** Shows what the business owns and owes, its funding and the strength of its position (liquidity, gearing); allows comparison between years; used by lenders, investors, suppliers, managers.

**Limitations.** It is a snapshot, so can be changed by timing (such as a delayed supplier payment before the year end); assets are at cost less depreciation, not what they would fetch; intangible assets and goodwill are hard to value; it omits assets with no record, such as a skilled workforce or brand; and it says nothing about future trading.

A balanced judgement says that the statement is essential for judging financial strength, but it needs to be read with the income statement and cash-flow information.

## Common mistakes

- Putting dividends or tax due in the wrong section.
- Forgetting that net assets must equal equity.
- Using the wrong base for reducing balance (apply the rate to NBV, not cost).
- Saying depreciation reduces cash.
- Treating goodwill as an asset for any business, rather than one recorded on purchase.

## Exam technique

Set the statement out in order with sub-totals. Check that net assets equal equity. For depreciation, show the formula, the rate and each year's NBV. In evaluation, name the user (lender, shareholder) and the limitation.
