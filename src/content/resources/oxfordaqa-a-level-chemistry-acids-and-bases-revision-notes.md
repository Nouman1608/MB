## Definitions (3.1.10.1 to 3.1.10.4)

- **Acid**: proton donor. **Base**: proton acceptor.
- **Acid–base equilibrium**: one in which protons (H⁺) are transferred. Check both directions: the species that loses H⁺ in the reverse reaction is also an acid.
- **Strong acid or base**: completely dissociated in water.
- **Weak acid or base**: dissociates only slightly in water.
- **pH** = −log₁₀[H⁺].
- **Kw** = [H⁺][OH⁻], the ionic product of water; it comes from Kc for water ionising.
- **Ka**: dissociation constant for a weak acid; **pKa** = −log₁₀ Ka.
- **Neutral**: [H⁺] = [OH⁻]. This is pH 7 only at 25 °C.

## Equations at a glance

| Situation | Relationship | Units of the constant or answer |
|---|---|---|
| Any solution | pH = −log₁₀[H⁺], so [H⁺] = 10^(−pH) | pH has none; [H⁺] in mol dm⁻³ |
| Water equilibrium | Kw = [H⁺][OH⁻] | Kw in mol² dm⁻⁶ |
| Pure water | [H⁺] = √Kw = [OH⁻] | mol dm⁻³ |
| Fully dissociated base | [H⁺] = Kw ÷ [OH⁻] | mol dm⁻³ |
| Weak acid equilibrium | Ka = [H⁺][A⁻] ÷ [HA] | Ka in mol dm⁻³ |
| Weak acid on its own | [H⁺] = √(Ka[HA]) | mol dm⁻³ |
| Strength scale | pKa = −log₁₀ Ka and Ka = 10^(−pKa) | pKa has none |
| Buffer (acidic) | [H⁺] = Ka[HA] ÷ [A⁻] | mol dm⁻³ |
| Half-way to equivalence | pH = pKa | none |

Kw = 1.00 × 10⁻¹⁴ mol² dm⁻⁶ at 25 °C. Use the value a question gives at any other temperature.

## Why Kw = [H⁺][OH⁻]

```
H₂O ⇌ H⁺ + OH⁻        Kc = [H⁺][OH⁻] / [H₂O]
```

[H₂O] is so large compared with the ions that it is effectively constant, so Kc × [H₂O] = Kw. The dissociation is **endothermic**, so raising the temperature moves the equilibrium right and **Kw increases**. Pure water then has pH below 7 but stays neutral.

## Method boxes

**pH of a strong acid (monoprotic)**
1. [H⁺] = concentration of the acid.
2. pH = −log₁₀[H⁺].

**pH of a strong base**
1. [OH⁻] = concentration × number of OH⁻ per formula unit.
2. [H⁺] = Kw / [OH⁻].
3. pH = −log₁₀[H⁺].

**pH of a weak acid from Ka**
1. Assume [H⁺] = [A⁻] and [HA] ≈ starting concentration.
2. [H⁺] = √(Ka × [HA]).
3. pH = −log₁₀[H⁺].

**Ka from the pH of a weak acid**
1. [H⁺] = 10^(−pH).
2. Ka = [H⁺]² / [HA].
3. pKa = −log₁₀ Ka if asked.

**Strong acid mixed with strong base**
1. Work out n(H⁺) and n(OH⁻) from concentration × volume in dm³.
2. Take the smaller from the larger; whichever is left over decides the pH.
3. Convert that leftover to a concentration using the **combined** volume.
4. Leftover H⁺ gives pH at once; leftover OH⁻ needs Kw to reach [H⁺].

**Acidic buffer pH**
1. Get the final amounts of HA and A⁻, allowing for any base or acid that has reacted.
2. [H⁺] = Ka × n(HA) / n(A⁻).
3. pH = −log₁₀[H⁺].

### Small worked reminders

- 0.0055 mol dm⁻³ HNO₃: pH = −log₁₀(0.0055) = 2.26.
- A weak acid with Ka = 2.0 × 10⁻⁶ mol dm⁻³ at 0.500 mol dm⁻³: [H⁺] = √(1.0 × 10⁻⁶) = 1.0 × 10⁻³, so pH = 3.00.
- Buffer with n(HA) = n(A⁻): [H⁺] = Ka, so pH = pKa.

## Titrations, pH curves and indicators (3.1.10.5)

**Titration results**: use only concordant titres (usually within 0.10 cm³) for the mean, and ignore the rough titre.

| Acid + base | Start pH (acid in flask) | Vertical section | Equivalence pH | Indicator |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Strong + strong | about 1 | about 3 to 11 | 7 | methyl orange or phenolphthalein |
| Weak + strong | higher | about 7 to 11 | above 7 | phenolphthalein |
| Strong + weak | about 1 | about 3 to 7 | below 7 | methyl orange |
| Weak + weak | higher | none | hard to find | none: use a pH meter |

Indicator ranges to know: methyl orange 3.1–4.4 (red to yellow); phenolphthalein 8.3–10.0 (colourless to pink).

**Rule**: an indicator is suitable if its entire colour-change range sits inside the vertical section.

**Features to explain on a sketch**
- Weak acid starts higher: only slightly dissociated.
- Buffer region early in a weak acid–strong base titration: HA and A⁻ both present.
- pH = pKa at half the equivalence volume, because [HA] = [A⁻].
- Equivalence above 7 with a weak acid and strong base: A⁻ removes H⁺ from water, leaving OH⁻.
- Equivalence below 7 with a strong acid and weak base: the cation of the salt donates H⁺ to water.

## Required practical 7

**Aim**: record pH while a strong base is run into a weak acid, then while a strong acid is run into a weak base.

- Calibrate the pH meter with buffers of known pH first.
- Measure the solution in the beaker with a pipette; add the other from a burette.
- Stir, wait for a steady reading, then record pH and total volume.
- Use smaller additions near the end point to define the steep section.
- Plot pH (y-axis) against volume added (x-axis). Equivalence volume is at the middle of the steep section.
- For the weak acid run, read pH at half the equivalence volume to get pKa.

## Buffers (3.1.10.6)

**Buffer**: a solution whose pH stays nearly constant when it is diluted or when a little acid or base is added.

| Type | Contains | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Acidic | weak acid + its salt | HA with NaA, or HA partly neutralised by NaOH |
| Basic | weak base + its salt | methylamine with methylammonium chloride |

**Acidic buffer, qualitative**
- Add H⁺: H⁺ + A⁻ → HA. Equilibrium HA ⇌ H⁺ + A⁻ shifts left.
- Add OH⁻: OH⁻ + H⁺ → H₂O; HA dissociates to replace H⁺. Equilibrium shifts right.
- Large reservoirs of HA and A⁻ mean the ratio [HA]/[A⁻] hardly changes.

**Basic buffer, qualitative**
- Add H⁺: base + H⁺ → conjugate acid (CH₃NH₂ + H⁺ → CH₃NH₃⁺).
- Add OH⁻: conjugate acid + OH⁻ → base + H₂O.

**Applications**: calibrating pH meters; controlling the pH of blood; controlling acidity in foods and drinks; any process where enzymes or reactions need a steady pH.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Strong vs concentrated**: "strong" describes the extent of dissociation (complete); "concentrated" describes how many moles are in each dm³.
- **Weak acid vs buffer**: for a weak acid alone [H⁺] = [A⁻]; in a buffer [A⁻] is much larger, so [H⁺] = Ka[HA]/[A⁻].
- **End point vs equivalence point**: the end point is where the indicator changes; the equivalence point is where reacting amounts match.
- **Ka vs pKa**: larger Ka means stronger; larger pKa means weaker.

## Quick self-test

1. In HCO₃⁻ + OH⁻ → CO₃²⁻ + H₂O, which species is the Brønsted–Lowry acid?
2. Calculate the pH of 0.0072 mol dm⁻³ HI.
3. Calculate [H⁺] in a solution of pH 10.46.
4. Calculate the pH of 0.0028 mol dm⁻³ NaOH at 25 °C.
5. Ka = 4.9 × 10⁻¹⁰ mol dm⁻³. Calculate pKa.
6. pKa = 2.95. Calculate Ka.
7. A weak acid has Ka = 7.2 × 10⁻⁶ mol dm⁻³. Calculate the pH of a 0.0350 mol dm⁻³ solution.
8. Which of methyl orange and phenolphthalein suits a strong acid–weak base titration?
9. In a pH curve, the pH at half the equivalence volume is 5.13. State pKa.
10. A buffer contains 0.024 mol HA and 0.016 mol A⁻ (Ka = 3.0 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³). Calculate its pH.
11. At 0 °C, Kw is about 1.12 × 10⁻¹⁵ mol² dm⁻⁶. Calculate the pH of pure water and say whether it is neutral.

### Answers

1. HCO₃⁻ (it donates a proton to OH⁻).
2. pH = −log₁₀(0.0072) = **2.14**.
3. [H⁺] = 10^(−10.46) = **3.47 × 10⁻¹¹ mol dm⁻³**.
4. [H⁺] = 1.00 × 10⁻¹⁴ / 0.0028 = 3.57 × 10⁻¹², so pH = **11.45**.
5. pKa = **9.31**.
6. Ka = **1.12 × 10⁻³ mol dm⁻³**.
7. [H⁺] = √(7.2 × 10⁻⁶ × 0.0350) = 5.02 × 10⁻⁴, so pH = **3.30**.
8. **Methyl orange**: its range lies in the acidic vertical section.
9. **pKa = 5.13**.
10. [H⁺] = 3.0 × 10⁻⁵ × 0.024 / 0.016 = 4.5 × 10⁻⁵, so pH = **4.35**.
11. [H⁺] = √(1.12 × 10⁻¹⁵) = 3.35 × 10⁻⁸, so pH = **7.48**; still **neutral** because [H⁺] = [OH⁻].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Leaving H₂O in the Kw expression, or giving Kw units of mol dm⁻³ instead of mol² dm⁻⁶.
- Forgetting that a hydroxide with two OH⁻ per formula unit doubles [OH⁻].
- Using √(Ka × [HA]) in a buffer calculation.
- Using only one solution's volume, rather than the combined volume, for the leftover reagent.
- Describing an indicator as suitable when only part of its range lies in the vertical section.
- Stating the pH of neutral water is always 7.
- Writing the buffer explanation without naming which equilibrium shifts and in which direction.
- Missing the half-equivalence trick (pH = pKa) when a question gives a pH curve.
