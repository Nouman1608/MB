## Key definitions

| Term | Meaning (learn the wording) |
|---|---|
| Respiration (10.1.1) | The chemical reactions in all living cells that release energy from glucose |
| Aerobic respiration (10.2.1) | The release of a relatively large amount of energy by the breakdown of glucose in the presence of oxygen |
| Anaerobic respiration (10.3.1) | The release of a relatively small amount of energy by the breakdown of glucose without using oxygen |
| Oxygen debt / EPOC (10.3.4) | The extra oxygen needed after vigorous exercise to break down the lactic acid that built up; EPOC stands for Excess Post-exercise Oxygen Consumption |

Three words to keep apart:

- **Respiration**: chemical reactions in cells.
- **Breathing**: muscle movements that move air in and out of the lungs.
- **Gas exchange**: diffusion of oxygen and carbon dioxide across the alveoli.

## Equations to learn (10.2.2, 10.3.2, 10.3.3)

| Type | Equation |
|---|---|
| Aerobic, word | glucose + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water |
| Aerobic, balanced | C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O |
| Anaerobic in humans, word | glucose → lactic acid |
| Anaerobic in yeast, word | glucose → ethanol + carbon dioxide |

Only aerobic respiration needs a balanced chemical equation in this syllabus. The anaerobic ones are word equations.

**Memory check for the balanced equation.** Count atoms. Left: C 6, H 12, O 6 + 12 = 18. Right: C 6, H 12, O 12 + 6 = 18. If your oxygen count does not reach 18 on both sides, a 6 is missing.

**Ratio reminder.** 1 glucose : 6 oxygen : 6 carbon dioxide : 6 water. So 4 glucose molecules need 24 oxygen molecules and give 24 carbon dioxide molecules.

## Uses of energy (10.1.2)

The syllabus lists seven. Learn them as a list, then practise matching each one to a situation:

1. Muscle contraction
2. Protein synthesis
3. Cell division
4. Active transport
5. Growth
6. The passage of electrical impulses along neurones
7. The maintenance of a constant body temperature

| If the question describes... | The use is... |
|---|---|
| Ions taken up against a concentration gradient | Active transport |
| Amino acids joined to make an enzyme | Protein synthesis |
| A nerve carrying a message | Passage of electrical impulses along neurones |
| A bird staying warm in winter | Maintenance of a constant body temperature |
| New cells forming at a root tip | Cell division (and growth) |

## Aerobic vs anaerobic: must-know distinctions

| | Aerobic | Anaerobic (humans) | Anaerobic (yeast) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Oxygen | Used | Not used | Not used |
| Energy released per glucose | Relatively large | Relatively small | Relatively small |
| Products | Carbon dioxide + water | Lactic acid | Ethanol + carbon dioxide |
| Carbon dioxide made? | Yes | **No** | Yes |

Both humans and yeast can respire either way. Your muscles switch to extra anaerobic respiration only when oxygen cannot reach them fast enough.

## The yeast and temperature practical (10.1.3)

**Method in steps**

1. Yeast + glucose solution in tubes; same mass of yeast, same volume and concentration of glucose in each.
2. Each tube in a water-bath at a different temperature; check with a thermometer.
3. Leave time for the mixture to reach the water-bath temperature.
4. Measure carbon dioxide release for a fixed time: volume collected over water, bubbles per minute, or time for limewater to turn milky / hydrogencarbonate indicator to turn yellow.
5. Repeat and find a mean; add a control of boiled yeast.

**Rate** = volume (or number of bubbles) ÷ time. For a time-to-change method, a shorter time means a faster rate.

**Expected pattern and explanation**

- Rate rises as temperature rises: more kinetic energy, more collisions between enzymes and substrate.
- Rate peaks at the optimum.
- Rate falls above the optimum: enzymes denatured, active site changes shape, substrate no longer fits.

**Worked reminder.** 42 bubbles in 3 minutes = 42 ÷ 3 = 14 bubbles per minute.

**Describing a results table or graph, in steps**

1. State the overall trend: the rate increases, then decreases.
2. Name the temperature with the highest rate, and quote that rate with its unit.
3. Quote at least one more pair of values, for example the lowest rate.
4. Say what happens at the highest temperature tested.

Describe first, then explain. "Describe" wants the pattern with data; "explain" wants the reasons (kinetic energy, collisions, denaturation).

**Improving the investigation**

- Use smaller temperature steps near the highest rate to find the optimum more precisely.
- Collect gas volume rather than counting bubbles, because bubbles vary in size.
- Repeat each temperature at least three times and find a mean; leave out any clear anomaly.
- Keep the tubes in the water-bath during collection so the temperature stays the same.

## Where respiration fits in the course

- **Cells**: aerobic respiration happens in the mitochondria; muscle cells have many of them. See [Cell structure and organisation](/resources/cell-structure-and-organisation-revision-notes/).
- **Enzymes**: every step of respiration is enzyme-controlled, which is why temperature affects the rate.
- **Gas exchange**: the oxygen used in aerobic respiration enters the blood at the alveoli, and the carbon dioxide made leaves there. Expired air has more carbon dioxide because of respiration.
- **Plants**: plant cells respire all the time, in the light and the dark, while photosynthesis needs light. Never write that plants "photosynthesise instead of respiring". See [Plant nutrition](/resources/o-level-biology-5090-plant-nutrition-revision-notes/).

## Lactic acid and the oxygen debt (10.3.4, 10.3.5)

**Why lactic acid builds up, in steps**

1. Vigorous exercise: muscles need energy very fast.
2. Oxygen cannot be supplied fast enough for aerobic respiration alone.
3. Muscles also respire anaerobically.
4. Lactic acid is made and builds up in the muscles and blood.
5. Oxygen is needed later to break it down: the oxygen debt (EPOC).

**How the debt is removed (the only two points the syllabus asks for)**

| What continues after exercise | Why |
|---|---|
| Fast heart rate | Transports lactic acid in the blood from the muscles to the liver |
| Deeper and faster breathing | Supplies oxygen for the breakdown of lactic acid in the liver |

**Measuring the debt.** Subtract the resting oxygen uptake from each value after exercise, then add the differences. The total is the extra oxygen used, which is the size of the debt.

## Quick self-test

1. Describe respiration.
2. State the word equation for aerobic respiration.
3. State the balanced chemical equation for aerobic respiration.
4. State the word equation for anaerobic respiration in yeast.
5. State the word equation for anaerobic respiration in humans.
6. Which type of respiration releases more energy from each glucose molecule?
7. State four uses of energy in living organisms.
8. How many oxygen molecules are needed to respire 3 glucose molecules aerobically?
9. Yeast released 42 bubbles in 3 minutes. Calculate the rate.
10. Resting oxygen uptake is 0.25 dm³ per minute. For four minutes after exercise, uptake is 1.25, 0.75, 0.45 and 0.25 dm³ per minute. Calculate the oxygen debt.
11. Why does the heart rate stay high after vigorous exercise?
12. Why does yeast release almost no gas at 60 °C?

### Answers

1. The chemical reactions in all living cells that release energy from glucose.
2. glucose + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water
3. C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O
4. glucose → ethanol + carbon dioxide
5. glucose → lactic acid
6. Aerobic respiration.
7. Any four: muscle contraction; protein synthesis; cell division; active transport; growth; passage of electrical impulses along neurones; maintenance of a constant body temperature.
8. 3 × 6 = **18** oxygen molecules.
9. 42 ÷ 3 = **14 bubbles per minute**.
10. Differences: 1.00 + 0.50 + 0.20 + 0.00 = **1.70 dm³**.
11. To transport lactic acid in the blood from the muscles to the liver.
12. The enzymes that control respiration are denatured, so the reactions stop.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing that respiration "produces" or "makes" energy instead of releasing it.
- Using "respiration" to mean breathing.
- Adding carbon dioxide to the human anaerobic equation, or lactic acid to the yeast one.
- Writing "alcohol" with no gas, or "oxygen" as a product of anaerobic respiration in yeast.
- Missing the 6 in front of O₂, CO₂ or H₂O in the balanced equation.
- Giving a vague use of energy such as "moving" or "working" instead of a named use from the list.
- Saying lactic acid is broken down in the muscles rather than the liver.
- Saying breathing stays fast after exercise "for more energy" instead of "to supply oxygen to break down lactic acid in the liver".
- Explaining the fall in yeast respiration above the optimum without saying enzymes are denatured.
- Forgetting that a shorter time for a colour change means a faster rate.
