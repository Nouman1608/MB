These questions are for **section 1.2 Quality of information** (Topic 1, AS Level) of Cambridge International AS & A Level Information Technology (9626), 2025–2027. All organisations are invented.

---

## Section A

**1.** Identify **three** factors that affect the quality of information. **[3]**

**2.** Describe what is meant by the accuracy of information. **[2]**

**3.** Explain why information can be accurate but still of poor quality. **[2]**

**4.** Give **one** example of information that is too detailed for its purpose and explain the problem it causes. **[2]**

**5.** State **two** ways of improving the completeness of information collected on an online form. **[2]**

## Section B

**6.** A taxi company stores the pick-up times of journeys. The manager reads a report that says "Most journeys were late last week".

**(a)** Explain why this statement has a poor level of detail. **[2]**

**(b)** Describe **two** pieces of extra detail that would make the report more useful. **[2]**

**7.** Oakfield Library prints a leaflet of opening hours. Three months later the hours change, but the leaflet is still given out.

**(a)** Identify the factor of information quality that is now poor. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain **two** effects on library users. **[4]**

**8.** A vet stores records of animals. Some records do not contain the animal's weight and others hold the weight of a different animal.

**(a)** Identify the factor of quality affected by the missing weights. **[1]**

**(b)** Identify the factor of quality affected by the wrong weights. **[1]**

**(c)** Explain the harm that could result from each problem. **[4]**

**9.** Calderwick Travel sends customers an email listing holiday offers. Most customers receive offers for countries they have no interest in. Explain which quality factor is poor and suggest how the company could improve it. **[4]**

**10.** A manager says: "Newer information is always better quality." Discuss this statement. **[4]**

**11.** Wren Electronics bases its decision to launch a new phone on a survey of 30 customers carried out four years ago in one town.

Evaluate the quality of the information the company is using. **[6]**

**12.** Explain how validation when entering data can improve the quality of the information a business holds. **[3]**

---

## Answers

**1.** Any three from: accuracy [1]; relevance [1]; age [1]; level of detail [1]; completeness [1]. **[3]**

**2.** Accurate information is correct [1] and free from errors, so it matches the real situation [1]. **[2]**

**3.** The information may be correct but not relevant to the purpose [1], or it may be out of date, incomplete or have the wrong level of detail, so the decision-maker cannot use it well [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Example: a printout of every single transaction when a manager needs only the monthly total [1]. The important trend is hidden, and it takes a long time to find what is needed [1]. **[2]**

**5.** Any two from: make important fields required (a presence check) [1]; use a checklist or summary page for the user to review before submitting [1]; show messages for any blank field [1]; use drop-down menus so users cannot skip options [1]. **[2]**

**6. (a)** "Most" and "late" are vague [1]; the manager cannot tell how many journeys, how late, or which drivers or areas were affected, so cannot act [1].

**(b)** Any two from: the number or percentage of journeys that were late [1]; how many minutes late each was [1]; the drivers, times of day or areas involved [1]; the causes of lateness [1]. **[4]**

**7. (a)** Age (the information is out of date) [1].

**(b)** Any two effects, each with a development: users may arrive when the library is closed [1], wasting their journey [1]; users may think the library closes earlier and miss a chance to use it [1], so the library receives fewer visitors [1]; the library may receive complaints [1], harming its reputation [1]. **[5]**

**8. (a)** Completeness [1].

**(b)** Accuracy [1].

**(c)** Missing weights: the vet cannot work out the correct dose of medicine, or may guess [1], which could harm the animal or delay treatment [1]. Wrong weights: the vet may give an overdose or underdose [1], which may make the animal ill or leave the illness untreated [1]. **[6]**

**9.** Relevance is poor [1] because the email is not tailored to each customer's interests [1]. Improvement: store each customer's preferred destinations or past bookings [1] and send only offers that match them [1]. **[4]**

**10.** For: new information reflects current prices, laws and conditions, so decisions are more reliable [1]; out-of-date information can mislead [1]. Against: age depends on the purpose, since historical research needs old sources [1]; newer information may be less accurate if it has not been checked, or less detailed, so other factors matter too [1]. **[4]**

**11.** Age: four years old, so customer opinions and the phone market have probably changed [1]. Sample size: only 30 customers is small and may not represent the market [1]. Relevance: one town may not represent the countries where the phone will be sold [1]. Completeness: the question topics are not stated, so price, features or competitors may be missing [1]. Accuracy: the way the survey was carried out is not known, so it cannot be checked [1]. Conclusion: the information is of poor quality for a launch decision, so the company should collect current, larger and wider data first [1]. **[6]**

**12.** Validation checks that data is sensible and in the correct format as it is entered [1], so impossible or missing values are rejected [1], which improves accuracy and completeness of stored information [1]. **[3]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "accurate" or "up to date" without saying what is wrong in the scenario.
- Mixing up accuracy (is it correct?) and completeness (is it all there?).
- Describing the cause but not the effect on the user.
- Treating all old information as poor quality without linking age to purpose.
- Missing the conclusion in an evaluate question.
