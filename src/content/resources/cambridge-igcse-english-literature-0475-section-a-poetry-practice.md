**How to use this set.** Every poem and line on this page is original, written for practice, so you
can try the skills of Section A without relying on notes about a set text. Section A builds
individual skills; Section B uses three short original poems, printed below, for longer analysis.

**Poem 1: Drought** *(original poem)*

> The river has forgotten its own name.
> Stones lie open-mouthed along its bed,
> and the heron stands where the water was,
> patient as a question no one asks.
> In the village, buckets wait in rows,
> tin faces lifted to an empty sky.

**Poem 2: The Old Piano** *(original poem)*

> No one plays it now. It keeps
> its yellowed teeth behind a lid,
> and when the lorries pass, it weeps
> one low note, as it always did
> when Grandad's heavy fingers found
> the tune he never named. The sound
> hangs in the hall a moment, then
> the house is quiet again.

**Poem 3: Leaving Day** *(original poem)*

> All summer I counted the days like coins,
> spending them slowly, one by one,
> dreaming of the city and its trains.
>
> This morning my mother folded my shirts
> the way she folds the prayer mat, carefully,
> smoothing each crease as if it were a word.
>
> Now the car is packed. The gate is open.
> I had not known a gate could look like that:
> both arms held wide, and nothing in them.

## Section A

**1.** For each original line, name the main method and explain its effect.
**(a)** "The fog fingered every window." **[2]**
**(b)** "Silence. Then the door." **[2]**

**2.** Rewrite this feature-spotting sentence as analysis, using the quotation in a sentence of your
own: "In 'The Old Piano' the poet uses personification: 'it weeps'." **[3]**

**3.** A question reads: "How does the poet vividly convey the speaker's fear?" Identify the **two**
key focuses of the question and explain what each requires of your answer. **[2]**

**4.** Give **two** different interpretations of the original line "the town wore its lights like
medals". **[2]**

**5.** Explain why the title of a poem is worth discussing, using "Drought" as your example. **[2]**

**6.** Identify the turn in "Leaving Day" and explain how the poet signals it. **[3]**

## Section B

**7.** Read "Drought".
**(a)** How does the poet make the river's dryness vivid in lines 1–4? **[6]**
**(b)** Explore the effect of the final two lines. **[4]**

**8.** Read "The Old Piano".
**(a)** Explore how the poet uses personification to present the piano. **[6]**
**(b)** Explain how rhyme and line length shape the ending (lines 6–8). **[4]**

**9.** Write the opening paragraph of an answer to: "How does the poet movingly convey memory in 'The
Old Piano'?" **[5]**

**10.** Read "Leaving Day". Explore how the speaker's feelings about leaving change through the
poem. **[8]**

**11.** In "Leaving Day", explain the effect of the simile in lines 5–6, "the way she folds the prayer
mat, carefully, / smoothing each crease as if it were a word". **[4]**

**12.** Write a five-point plan for the question: "Explore the ways in which the poet creates a sense
of waiting in 'Drought'." **[5]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** Personification: the fog "fingered" the windows [1]; the verb makes it seem like a hand
feeling for a way in, creating an uneasy, intrusive atmosphere [1].
**(b)** Minor sentences and caesura (the full stop after "Silence") [1]; the abrupt pause makes the
reader wait, so the arrival of "the door" feels sudden and tense [1].

**2.** A point linked to the poem's meaning [1], the quotation embedded [1], and the effect explained
[1]. For example: "The piano is made almost human when it 'weeps / one low note', as if it grieves
for Grandad; the verb turns a mechanical vibration into an act of mourning, so the house itself seems
to miss him."

**3.** "Vividly" [1]: the answer must explore how language, imagery and sound make the fear intense
and real for the reader, not just say that fear is present. "The speaker's fear" [1]: every point must
stay on the speaker's feelings of fear, not drift to other themes.

**4.** Any two reasonable readings, [1] each: the town is proud, showing off its lights as a soldier
displays medals; the lights are rewards for hard work or survival; the image is ironic, suggesting the
town's display is empty show; the medals suggest war or past suffering behind the brightness.
Maximum 2.

**5.** The title frames the reader's expectations before the poem begins [1]; "Drought" names the
cause that every image depends on, so even the "patient" heron and waiting buckets are read as
suffering from the lack of rain [1].

**6.** The turn comes in the final stanza with "Now" [1], which shifts from past dreaming and the
morning's preparations to the present moment of departure [1]; short, end-stopped sentences ("Now the
car is packed. The gate is open.") slow the pace and signal the change of feeling [1].

**7. (a)** Any three developed points, [2] each:
- "The river has forgotten its own name": personification suggests the river has lost its identity,
  so dryness is shown as a kind of death or loss of memory [2].
- "open-mouthed" stones: they seem to gape like thirsty creatures, making the riverbed look as if it
  is gasping for water [2].
- "where the water was": the past tense marks absence; the soft repeated "w" sounds create emptiness [2].
- "patient as a question no one asks": the simile makes the heron's waiting seem endless and
  unanswered, adding a sense of hopelessness [2].
Maximum 6.
**(b)** Any two developed points, [2] each:
- The scene moves from nature to people, so the drought is shown to affect human lives [2].
- "buckets wait in rows": the buckets are personified as patient, orderly queues, suggesting
  rationing or hope that rain will come [2].
- "tin faces lifted to an empty sky": the buckets look upward like people praying; "empty" ends the
  poem on disappointment [2].
Maximum 4.

**8. (a)** Any three developed points, [2] each:
- "It keeps / its yellowed teeth behind a lid": the keys become teeth and the lid a closed mouth, so
  the piano seems to have fallen silent by choice [2].
- "yellowed" suggests age and neglect, like an old person's teeth [2].
- "it weeps / one low note": the passing lorries make it vibrate, but the verb presents this as grief [2].
- "as it always did": the piano is given a memory, linking it to Grandad and the past [2].
Maximum 6.
**(b)** Any two developed points, [2] each:
- The rhymes "found / sound" link Grandad's playing with the sound that remains, so the music survives
  him [2].
- The last two lines end on only a half-rhyme ("then / again") and the final line is shorter, so the
  ending feels unresolved, like the silence after loss [2].
- Enjambment ("then / the house is quiet") makes the reader hang on "then" before the quiet arrives [2].
Maximum 4.

**9.** A direct answer to the question [1]; a focus on "movingly" [1]; reference to at least one
precise quotation [1] with a brief comment on method [1]; a sense of the line of argument to follow
[1]. For example: "The poet conveys memory movingly by making the piano itself remember. Though 'no
one plays it now', it still 'weeps / one low note, as it always did', so an object holds onto
Grandad when the family can only listen. I will show how personification, the piano's single
unnamed tune and the closing silence make memory feel both precious and painful."

**10.** Any four developed points, [2] each, that follow the change:
- Stanza 1: excitement and impatience; days counted "like coins" suggests they are precious, and he
  spends them "slowly", savouring the wait [2].
- "dreaming of the city and its trains": the trains suggest movement and possibility [2].
- Stanza 2: the focus moves to the mother; the care in her folding makes the speaker notice what he
  is leaving [2].
- Stanza 3: "Now" and the short sentences bring the moment of leaving into the present [2].
- "I had not known": the speaker admits a feeling he did not expect [2].
- The gate's "arms held wide, and nothing in them" is an image of an embrace with no one to hold,
  suggesting the home he leaves behind and his sudden sense of loss [2].
Maximum 8.

**11.** The comparison links the mother's packing to an act of worship [1], suggesting the love and
devotion in an ordinary task [1]. "Smoothing each crease as if it were a word" suggests she puts into
her actions what she cannot say [1], so her silence becomes a message the speaker only now
understands [1].

**12.** One mark for each of five relevant, sequenced points [5], for example:
1. Introduction: waiting is created by stillness and the absence of rain.
2. The river "has forgotten its own name": time has passed and memory has faded.
3. The heron "patient as a question no one asks": endless, unanswered waiting.
4. The buckets "wait in rows": human waiting, orderly and hopeful.
5. The ending, "tin faces lifted to an empty sky": hope directed upwards but unmet; conclusion on
   how the poem leaves the waiting unresolved.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Naming methods with no comment on their effect.
- Paraphrasing each line rather than choosing the most revealing details.
- Losing sight of the question's key word, such as "vividly" or "movingly".
- Ignoring the ending and the poem's structure.
