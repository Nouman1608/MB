Section 5.2 of Cambridge International A Level Sociology (9699) asks whether education gives everyone a fair chance to rise. The syllabus lists four strands: equal opportunity and the idea of meritocracy; the extent to which education systems are meritocratic today; the importance of education in influencing life chances, and the consequences of educational under-achievement for the individual and for society; and evidence and arguments about the links between education and social mobility. The section feeds the key concepts of Inequality and opportunity and Structure and human agency, and is examined on Paper 3 – Education.

The [education, society and the curriculum overview](/resources/a-level-sociology-9699-education-society-and-the-curriculum/) introduces Young's meritocracy, formal and substantive equality, Goldthorpe's mobility study and Dore's diploma disease. This page adds the explanations that connect education to mobility, the debate about whether today's systems are meritocratic, and the exam skills the section demands.

## Three meanings of "equality" in education

Arguments in 5.2 often go wrong because writers switch between different ideas of equality.

| Idea | What it requires | Who tends to support it |
|---|---|---|
| **Equality of access** | Everyone may enter the same schools and sit the same exams | Liberal and functionalist thinkers |
| **Equality of opportunity** | Everyone has a genuinely equal chance of success, so hidden barriers must also be removed | Social democrats |
| **Equality of outcome** | Results, or rewards, are broadly equal between groups | Some Marxist and radical critics |

A system can offer equal access and still fail equal opportunity: an open exam favours pupils who can pay for tutoring. In an essay, define which meaning a quotation assumes before judging it.

## Contest and sponsored mobility

Ralph Turner's article "Sponsored and Contest Mobility and the School System" (1960) gives a useful framework for comparing systems.

- **Contest mobility** treats success as an open race. Selection is delayed and many routes stay open, so late developers can catch up. Turner associated this with the United States.
- **Sponsored mobility** selects an elite early, and those chosen are groomed for high status. Turner associated this with England's selective system of the time.

The concepts let you apply 5.2 to any country: ask when selection happens, how reversible it is, and who does the choosing. Early, irreversible selection tends to magnify the effect of family background.

## How far are education systems meritocratic today?

**Arguments that they largely are**

- **Peter Saunders**, in *Unequal but Fair?* (1996), used data from a British birth cohort to argue that ability and effort predicted occupational outcomes better than class background. On this view, much of the class gap reflects differences in ability, not unfairness.
- Mass secondary and higher education mean far more people gain qualifications than in earlier generations.
- Anti-discrimination law, published criteria and anonymous marking reduce open bias.

**Arguments that they are not**

- **Halsey, Heath and Ridge**, in *Origins and Destinations* (1980), studied men in England and Wales and found boys from service-class backgrounds far more likely to reach university than working-class boys, even after the expansion of secondary education.
- **Phillip Brown** (1990) argued that systems are moving from meritocracy to **parentocracy**: success depends on the wealth and wishes of parents. Private schooling, tutoring, house moves into catchment areas and unpaid internships all buy advantage.
- **Randall Collins**, in *The Credential Society* (1979), argued that employers use qualifications as a filter for status culture rather than as proof of job skill, so credentials reward the right background as much as competence.

**Evaluating Saunders.** Critics reply that "ability" measured in childhood is itself shaped by class, so controlling for it may hide class influence rather than remove it. See [intelligence and educational attainment](/resources/cambridge-a-level-sociology-9699-intelligence-educational-attainment/) for the measurement debate.

## Why does background affect educational outcomes? Primary and secondary effects

Raymond Boudon, in *Education, Opportunity and Social Inequality* (1974), separated two ways class shapes education.

- **Primary effects** are differences in attainment itself: children from advantaged homes tend to perform better at each stage.
- **Secondary effects** are differences in *choices* among pupils who perform equally well. A working-class pupil with good grades may weigh the costs of staying on, and the social distance from friends and family, differently from a middle-class pupil with the same grades.

Richard Breen and John Goldthorpe (1997) developed this into **relative risk aversion**: families aim above all to avoid their children falling below the parents' class. For a middle-class family, university is needed simply to stay level; for a working-class family, a secure skilled job already maintains position, so the costs and risks of higher education weigh more heavily.

This is a strong analytical point for essays: even if schools became perfectly fair in marking, secondary effects would still produce class differences in who stays in education.

## Education and life chances

Life chances are the opportunities a person has for good things such as income, health, secure work and long life. Education matters because qualifications act as entry tickets to the labour market and because schooling shapes confidence, networks and health knowledge.

**Positional goods.** Fred Hirsch, in *Social Limits to Growth* (1976), described goods whose value depends on how many others have them. A degree is partly positional: its value in the job market falls when most competitors also hold one. This explains why expanding education can raise everyone's qualifications without changing anyone's relative position.

**Consequences of under-achievement**

| For the individual | For society |
|---|---|
| Restricted job options and lower lifetime earnings | Wasted talent and lower productivity |
| Greater risk of unemployment and insecure work | Higher spending on welfare and support |
| Reduced confidence and civic participation | Weaker social cohesion if groups feel shut out |
| Fewer resources to support one's own children's schooling | Inequality reproduced across generations |

Functionalists and social democrats treat under-achievement as a waste that policy should fix. Marxists argue it is functional for capitalism, which needs people to fill low-paid jobs and to believe they deserve them.

## Evidence and arguments on education and mobility

Three positions are worth setting against each other.

1. **Education as the engine of mobility.** Functionalists argue that schooling allows talent to rise, and expansion has coincided with large absolute upward mobility as professional and managerial jobs grew.
2. **Education as a reproducer.** Marxists and Bourdieu argue education converts class advantage into credentials, so relative chances barely change. Boudon's secondary effects and Brown's parentocracy explain how.
3. **Education as necessary but not sufficient.** Mobility depends on the occupational structure as much as on schools. If few good jobs are created, more qualifications produce credential inflation rather than mobility. This middle view lets you reach a balanced judgement.

## Worked example: Question 2 style

*Explain two reasons why educational expansion may not increase social mobility.*

> **Reason 1: positional competition.** Hirsch argues that qualifications are partly positional goods. If every group gains more degrees, employers raise their entry requirements, so the advantaged keep their lead. Expansion lifts the whole distribution without changing relative positions, which is why Goldthorpe found relative mobility broadly stable while absolute mobility rose.
>
> **Reason 2: secondary effects.** Boudon shows that, among pupils with similar grades, working-class pupils are less likely to choose to continue. Breen and Goldthorpe explain this through relative risk aversion: for a working-class family, the costs and risks of university may outweigh the gain, because a skilled job already maintains their position. More places do not remove these different calculations.

Each reason names the mechanism, supports it with a sociologist, and explains *why* it blocks mobility. That is the AO1 plus AO2 combination the syllabus attaches to Question 2.

## Worked essay plan

*"Education is the key to social mobility in modern societies." Evaluate this view.*

1. Define social mobility (absolute, relative, intergenerational) and meritocracy.
2. For: functionalist role allocation; expansion and rising absolute mobility; Saunders's meritocracy argument.
3. Against: Halsey, Heath and Ridge on class differences in university entry; Boudon's secondary effects; Breen and Goldthorpe's risk aversion; Brown's parentocracy; Collins on credentialism.
4. Alternative factor: changes in the occupational structure drive much absolute mobility.
5. Comparative application: contest versus sponsored systems.
6. Judgement: education is a necessary route for individuals but does not by itself equalise relative chances.

## Common mistakes

- Using "meritocracy" and "equality of opportunity" as if they meant the same thing; meritocracy is about how rewards are allocated, equal opportunity about the chances to compete.
- Treating rising qualification levels as proof of rising mobility.
- Confusing absolute and relative mobility in evaluation.
- Discussing consequences of under-achievement only for the individual when the syllabus also asks about society.

## Exam technique

- In Question 3, an argument against "education is meritocratic" must show *how* background shapes outcomes, not merely state that it does.
- Use one comparative example from a society you know to show application.
- In Question 4 essays, a judgement that distinguishes absolute from relative mobility usually shows stronger evaluation than a simple yes or no.
