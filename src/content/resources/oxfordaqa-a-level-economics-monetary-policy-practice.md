## Questions

**1.** Define monetary policy and state its usual prime objective. **[3]**

**2.** Country C has a target of 2% inflation with a tolerance of one percentage point either side. Its consumer price index is 112.0 in Year 1, 117.0 in Year 2 and 119.5 in Year 3.

**(a)** Calculate the inflation rate in Year 2 and in Year 3, giving each to 1 decimal place. **[2]**
**(b)** State whether each year is inside the target range. **[2]**

**3.** In Year 2 the nominal interest rate in Country C is 3.5% and inflation is 4.5%. Calculate the approximate real interest rate and explain one effect on spending. **[3]**

**4.** Give two reasons why higher interest rates tend to lower aggregate demand, and explain each. **[4]**

**5.** A firm in Country C has a variable-rate loan of 250,000 and annual operating profit of 50,000. The rate rises from 4.5% to 6.0%.

**(a)** Find the extra annual interest and express it as a percentage of the firm's profit. **[3]**
**(b)** Across the economy, variable-rate household debt is 36bn. Households spend 50% of lost income and investment falls by 0.18bn. The multiplier is 1.8 and GDP is 270bn. Find the fall in real GDP, in bn and relative to GDP. **[3]**

**6.** The exchange rate moves from 1 C-unit = 3.00 D-units to 1 C-unit = 2.55 D-units. Find the percentage fall in the C-unit's value. A machine is imported for 1,530 D-units. Work out its cost in C-units before and after the move, and the percentage rise. **[4]**

**7.** Explain how a depreciation of the currency may increase inflation. **[4]**

**8.** A government bond pays a fixed 3.6 a year on a face value of 150.

**(a)** Calculate the yield when the price rises from 150 to 180. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain how quantitative easing can increase aggregate demand. **[5]**

**9.** Analyse two reasons why a cut in interest rates might do little to raise aggregate demand. **[6]**

**10.** Inflation in Country C is 4.5%, mostly from higher import prices after a depreciation. The output gap is -1.5%. Evaluate whether raising the interest rate is the best response. **[12]**

**11.** Evaluate the likely effects of a depreciation on the macroeconomic objectives of Country C. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** Central bank action to influence interest rates, money and credit, or the exchange rate [1], to meet its objectives, with the bank rather than the government doing the acting [1]. Prime objective: **control of inflation** [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* A good definition names the central bank and at least one tool, not just "managing the economy".

**2. (a)** Year 2: (117.0 - 112.0) / 112.0 x 100 = **4.5%** [1]. Year 3: (119.5 - 117.0) / 117.0 x 100 = **2.1%** [1].
**(b)** The range is 1% to 3%. Year 2 is above the range [1]. Year 3 is inside it [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Each year's rate is measured against the year before it; dividing every change by Year 1 gives wrong figures.

**3.** Real rate = 3.5 - 4.5 = **-1.0%** [1]. Savers earn less than inflation, so saving is discouraged [1]. Borrowing is cheap in real terms, so spending is encouraged [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* A negative result needs its sign, and the point is stronger when linked to what households do with their money.

**4.** Any two. Loan costs rise, so borrowers on variable rates are left with less to spend [1] and consumption falls [1]. Borrowing is dearer for firms [1] so investment falls [1]. Alternatives: asset prices fall, reducing wealth; the currency may appreciate, reducing net exports. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Naming a channel and then its effect on a component of AD usually earns more than naming channels alone.

**5. (a)** 250,000 x 0.015 = **3,750** a year [1] [1]. 3,750 / 50,000 x 100 = **7.5%** of profit [1].
**(b)** Lost income = 36 x 0.015 = 0.54bn, so consumption falls 0.5 x 0.54 = 0.27bn and the first-round fall in AD is 0.27 + 0.18 = 0.45bn [1]. GDP falls 0.45 x 1.8 = **0.81bn** [1]. 0.81 / 270 x 100 = **0.3%** [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Setting out each stage of a multi-step calculation makes your method clear and helps you spot slips.

**6.** (2.55 - 3.00) / 3.00 x 100 = **-15%** [1]. Before: 1,530 / 3.00 = **510** C-units [1]. After: 1,530 / 2.55 = **600** C-units [1]. Rise: (600 - 510) / 510 x 100 = **17.6%** [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Divide D-units by the exchange rate to get C-units; a sense-check that the import has become dearer catches a flipped calculation.

**7.** Imported inputs cost more in home currency [1], so firms' costs rise, SRAS shifts left and the price level rises [1]. Cheaper exports abroad lift net exports and AD [1], which pulls prices up when firms have few idle resources [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Answers do well when they name both routes, cost-push and demand-pull, and say when each matters.

**8. (a)** Yield = 3.6 / 180 x 100 [1] = **2.0%** [1].
**(b)** The central bank pays for large purchases of government bonds with new electronic money [1]. Sellers' bank balances rise, so the money supply increases [1]. Bond prices rise and yields fall [1], so borrowing is cheaper and investors buy other assets, raising their prices [1]. Consumption and investment rise, so the AD curve moves right [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* Mentioning both the lower interest rate and the larger money supply shows the full chain; either alone is a partial answer.

**9.** First reason: confidence is low [1], so households and firms do not borrow even when credit is cheap [1], and AD barely moves [1]. Second reason: many households have fixed-rate loans, or savings that earn less after a cut [1], so disposable income does not rise much [1], and the effect on consumption is small [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* For each reason, say why it happens and what follows for AD; a bare assertion leaves the point undeveloped.

**10.** A higher rate cuts consumption and investment, moving AD left [1] and easing demand-pull pressure [1]. The currency may rise, lowering import prices [1]. However, inflation here is mostly cost-push from import prices [1], so lower AD does not remove the cause [1]. An output gap of -1.5% means spare capacity [1], so reducing demand adds to unemployment [1] and slows growth [1]. The delay before the effect arrives may mean import prices have settled by then [1]. The effect depends on variable-rate debt [1]. A flexible target tolerates a temporary overshoot [1]. Judgement: the rate rise is probably not the best response, unless inflation expectations are rising [1]. **[12]**
*Examiner insight:* Strong answers separate the cause of the inflation from the tool and end with a supported judgement.

**11.** Net exports rise as exports become cheaper and imports dearer [1], so AD shifts right [1] and growth rises [1]. Unemployment falls as output rises [1]. Inflation rises through dearer imports [1] and demand-pull [1]. The current account improves only if elasticities are high enough [1] and takes time [1]. Judgement: the gains are likely if spare capacity exists, but living standards fall for import buyers [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* Ending on a judgement that depends on elasticity, spare capacity and timing is stronger than a plain list of effects.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using Year 1 as the base for every year's inflation rate.
- Mixing up percentages and percentage points.
- Multiplying instead of dividing when converting currency.
- Stating that the government sets interest rates.
- Ending the chain at "spending falls" without reaching prices or output.
- Treating a depreciation as always good for the current account.
- Recommending a rate rise for cost-push inflation while ignoring the lost output.
- Evaluating with no judgement or no mention of the output gap.
