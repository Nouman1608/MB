## Section A

Questions 1 to 6 refer to Source 1.

**1.** Define the term "gearing". **[2]**

**2.** Calculate Fenwick's gross profit margin and profit for the year margin in each year. **[4]**

**3.** Calculate Fenwick's current ratio and acid test ratio in each year. **[4]**

**4.** Calculate Fenwick's gearing and return on capital employed (ROCE) in Year 2. **[4]**

**5.** Explain **one** reason why Fenwick's gross profit margin fell in Year 2. **[4]**

**6.** Analyse the change in Fenwick's liquidity between Year 1 and Year 2. **[6]**

## Section B

Questions 7 to 12 refer to Source 2 (with Source 1 where needed).

**7.** Define the term "labour turnover". **[2]**

**8.** Calculate revenue per employee in each year and the percentage change. **[4]**

**9.** Calculate Fenwick's labour turnover and absenteeism in Year 2. **[4]**

**10.** Analyse the likely effects of Fenwick's labour turnover on its competitiveness. **[6]**

**11.** Discuss whether Fenwick should use empowerment or financial rewards to reduce labour turnover. **[8]**

**12.** Assess whether Fenwick is more competitive in Year 2 than in Year 1. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** The proportion of a business's capital employed [1] that is financed by long-term borrowing (non-current liabilities) [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Year 1: gross profit = 2,000 - 1,300 = 700, so margin = 700 / 2,000 x 100 = **35.0%** [1]; operating profit = 700 - 400 = 300, profit for the year = 300 - 30 = 270, so margin = 270 / 2,000 x 100 = **13.5%** [1]. Year 2: gross profit = 2,300 - 1,610 = 690, so margin = **30.0%** [1]; operating profit = 690 - 460 = 230, profit for the year = 230 - 70 = 160, so margin = 160 / 2,300 x 100 = **7.0%** [1]. **[4]**

**3.** Year 1: current assets = 250 + 200 + 50 = 500; current ratio = 500 / 300 = **1.67**; acid test = (500 - 250) / 300 = **0.83** [2]. Year 2: current assets = 400 + 330 + 20 = 750; current ratio = 750 / 600 = **1.25**; acid test = (750 - 400) / 600 = **0.58** [2]. **[4]**

**4.** Capital employed = non-current liabilities + equity = 750 + 1,100 = $1,850,000 [1]. Gearing = 750 / 1,850 x 100 [1] = **40.5%** [1]. ROCE = operating profit / capital employed = 230 / 1,850 x 100 = **12.4%** [1]. **[4]**

**5.** Fenwick paid more for steel [1], which raised cost of sales from 65% of revenue to 70% (1,610 / 2,300) [1]. Unless it raised prices to match, each sale left less gross profit [1], so the margin fell from 35.0% to 30.0%, meaning revenue growth of 15% was not matched by a rise in gross profit (which fell from 700 to 690) [1]. **[4]**

**6.** The current ratio fell from 1.67 to 1.25 and the acid test from 0.83 to 0.58 [1]. Current liabilities doubled from 300 to 600, while current assets grew by only 50% [1]. Cash fell from $50,000 to $20,000, and most current assets are tied up in inventory (up 60%) and receivables (up 65%) [1]. An acid test of 0.58 means that Fenwick has only 58 cents of liquid assets for each $1 of short-term debt [1], so it may struggle to pay suppliers or the overdraft on time [1]. Fenwick could improve liquidity by collecting debts sooner, holding less inventory, or refinancing some short-term debt as long-term [1]. **[6]**

**7.** The number of employees who leave in a period [1] as a percentage of the average number employed [1]. **[2]**

**8.** Year 1 = 2,000,000 / 100 = **$20,000** [1]. Year 2 = 2,300,000 / 120 = **$19,167** [1]. Change = (19,167 - 20,000) / 20,000 x 100 [1] = **-4.2%** [1]. **[4]**

**9.** Labour turnover = 18 / 120 x 100 [1] = **15%** [1]. Absenteeism = 2,400 / (120 x 220) x 100 [1] = 2,400 / 26,400 x 100 = **9.1%** [1]. **[4]**

**10.** A turnover of 15% means Fenwick must recruit and train many replacements, which raises costs [1]. New staff are less productive, which helps explain why revenue per employee fell by 4.2% [1]. Only 80% of the staff at the start of the year (88 of 110) remained, so skills and experience are being lost [1]. Lower productivity and higher cost per unit make it harder for Fenwick to compete on price [1]. Quality and delivery could also suffer while teams are short-staffed or inexperienced [1], and lower customer satisfaction would reduce competitiveness further [1]. **[6]**

**11.** **Empowerment:** exit interviews show that leavers felt they had little say [1]; giving staff more authority could raise motivation and satisfaction without high direct cost [1]; faster decisions could improve service [1].
**Financial rewards:** leavers were also unhappy with pay, so higher pay or bonuses address a direct cause [1]; they are quick to introduce and easy to understand [1]; but they raise costs at a time when profit for the year margin has fallen to 7.0% [1].
**Judgement:** Fenwick should start with empowerment, because it is cheaper and addresses the second complaint, while it cannot easily afford large pay rises when its liquidity is weak [1]. It may need some pay review as well if its wages are below rivals', and the answer depends on how far pay is below the market rate [1]. **[8]**

**12.** **Evidence of weaker competitiveness:** gross margin fell from 35.0% to 30.0% and profit for the year margin from 13.5% to 7.0% [1]; ROCE fell from 21.4% to 12.4% (Year 1: 300 / 1,400) [1]; liquidity weakened, with an acid test of 0.58 [1]; gearing rose from 28.6% to 40.5%, increasing risk and interest [1]; revenue per employee fell and labour turnover is 15% with absenteeism at 9.1% [1].
**Evidence of strengths:** revenue grew 15%, showing that demand is rising [1]; new machinery could improve efficiency and capacity in future [1]; gearing of 40.5% is still below 50%, so borrowing is not extreme [1].
**Limitations:** the ratios cover only two years, and there is no rival data to compare with [1]; heavy investment in Year 2 may depress ratios temporarily before benefits arrive [1].
**Judgement:** Fenwick is less competitive in Year 2 on current evidence, because profitability, liquidity and productivity have all weakened [1]. The decisive issue is whether the new machinery restores margins; if it does, the decline may be temporary, but if the liquidity problem leads to late payments it could become serious [1]. **[12]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using profit for the year instead of operating profit in ROCE.
- Adding current liabilities to the capital employed.
- Including inventory in the acid test.
- Calculating absenteeism from employee numbers, without multiplying by days available.
- Describing ratios without a comparison or a cause.
- Judging competitiveness from one ratio only.
