## Section A

**1.** Explain how the reliability of a Hadith is tested, referring to both the chain of transmitters
and the text itself. **[8]**

**2.** Give **four** of the Six Articles of Faith. **[4]**

## Section B

**3.** Describe the main achievements of Abu Bakr as the first Caliph. **[10]**

**4.** Explain the significance of Umar's period of rule for the expansion and administration of the
Muslim state. **[10]**

**5.** Explain the importance of prayer (salat) as one of the Five Pillars of Islam, referring to its
preparation and performance. **[10]**

**6.** Explain **two** meanings of jihad and why the concept covers more than armed struggle. **[8]**

---

## Answers

**1.** Reliability is tested through **isnad** — the chain of transmitters connecting a Hadith back to
the Prophet — by checking the character, memory and reliability of every narrator in the chain and
whether the chain is complete without gaps [1] [1]. Reliability is also tested through **matn** —
examination of the text itself — checking it does not contradict the Qur'an, established Hadiths or
sound reason, and that its content is consistent with what is otherwise known of the Prophet's
teaching [1] [1]. Scholars used these two tests together to grade Hadiths by strength, from sound
(sahih) down through weak categories [1] [1], and the six Sunni and four Shi'a collections reflect
different scholarly applications of this method [1] [1].

**2.** Any four, 1 mark each: **belief in God** (tawhid) [1]; **belief in angels**, their nature and
duties [1]; **belief in the divine books** and their contents and purpose [1]; **belief in the
prophets**, their character and function [1]; **belief in God's predestination and decree** (qadr)
[1]; **belief in the Resurrection and the Last Day** [1].

**3.** Abu Bakr was chosen at **Saqifah** immediately after the Prophet's death, at a moment of crisis
for the young Muslim community [1]. He **despatched the expedition of Usama** as the Prophet had
already ordered, despite advice to keep the army at home for the community's defence — showing his
determination to fulfil the Prophet's own instructions exactly [1] [1]. He **suppressed the false
prophets**, including Musaylimah at the Battle of Yamama, and **compelled the tribes who had refused
zakat to return to obedience** during the Ridda (apostasy) wars [1] [1]. He **initiated the collection
of the Qur'an** into a single written text after a significant number of huffaz (those who had
memorised the Qur'an) were killed at Yamama, entrusting the work to Zayd ibn Thabit [1] [1].

**4.** Umar's rule saw the **rapid expansion of the Muslim state** through the conquests of large
parts of the Byzantine and Sasanian empires, including Syria, Egypt, Iraq and Persia [1] [1]. He is
credited with major **administrative innovations**: establishing the diwan (a register for
distributing state revenue), founding garrison towns (amsar) to house Muslim armies away from
conquered populations, and introducing the Hijri calendar dating from the Hijrah [1] [1]. He set
precedents for **just governance of non-Muslim subjects**, including terms of protection (dhimmi
status) that allowed conquered peoples to retain their religion in exchange for tax [1] [1]. His
combination of military success with lasting administrative structures is why he is widely regarded
as having shaped the Muslim state's institutions, not just its territory [1] [1].

**5.** Salat is performed **five times daily at fixed times**, preceded by wudu (ritual washing) to
achieve the state of purity required for prayer [1] [1]. It follows a **fixed physical sequence** —
standing, bowing and prostration — accompanied by set recitations including verses from the Qur'an
[1] [1]. It may be performed **individually or in congregation**, with congregational prayer,
particularly Friday (Jumu'ah) prayer, carrying particular importance for community cohesion [1] [1].
Its significance lies in maintaining **regular, structured remembrance of God** throughout the day,
reinforcing discipline and submission, and reminding worshippers of their equality before God
regardless of social status when praying together in rows [1] [1].

**6.** Jihad covers a **range of meanings beyond armed conflict**: physical jihad refers to defensive
struggle to protect the Muslim community when under threat [1] [1]; but the concept equally covers
**mental and spiritual struggle** — the effort to live according to Islamic teaching, resist
temptation and improve one's own character, sometimes called the "greater jihad" in contrast to the
"lesser jihad" of physical struggle [1] [1]. Understanding jihad only as warfare therefore misses that
its everyday, most common application for most Muslims is personal moral and spiritual effort [1] [1].

## A note on structuring Paper 2 answers

Because candidates must answer Question 1 and Question 2 plus two more of their choosing, it pays to
decide in the exam room's first few minutes which three of the four topics you will actually answer
on, rather than drifting between them mid-paper. A well-prepared candidate can answer confidently on at
least three of the four topics, since relying on only two leaves no room if one of those two is
examined in a way that does not suit that year's preparation.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Explaining isnad without also covering matn (the text-based check), or vice versa — the question
  asks for both.
- Listing Abu Bakr's achievements without the Ridda wars, which the specification treats as central to
  his significance.
- Describing salat's physical actions without linking them to its significance for community and
  discipline.
- Defining jihad only as armed struggle, missing the syllabus's explicit range of meanings.
