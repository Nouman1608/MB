Data: G = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N m² kg⁻²; g = 9.81 m s⁻²; ε₀ = 8.85 × 10⁻¹² F m⁻¹; e = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C; electron mass 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ kg; proton mass 1.67 × 10⁻²⁷ kg. Give two or three significant figures.

## Questions

**1.** State Coulomb's law for point charges, and explain why the same equation can be used for charges in air. **[3]**

**2.** Two small spheres carry charges of +2.4 μC and −0.90 μC. Their centres are 0.35 m apart in air.

**(a)** Calculate the magnitude of the force on each sphere and state whether it is attractive or repulsive. **[3]**
**(b)** The separation is doubled. State the new force. **[1]**

**3.** A conducting sphere of radius 3.0 cm carries a charge of +40 nC. A small sphere carrying +1.5 nC is held 5.0 cm from the surface of the large sphere. Calculate the force on the small sphere and state its direction. **[4]**

**4.** An electron and a second electron are 1.0 nm apart.

**(a)** Calculate the electrostatic force between them. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the gravitational attraction between the two electrons. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the ratio of the electrostatic force to the gravitational force, and state how this ratio changes if the separation is doubled. **[2]**

**5.** **(a)** Describe the electric field lines around an isolated negative point charge. **[2]**
**(b)** Describe the field lines between two oppositely charged parallel plates, and say what they show about field strength. **[2]**

**6.** **(a)** Define electric field strength. **[1]**
**(b)** A test charge of +3.5 nC feels a force of 2.8 × 10⁻⁵ N at a point in a field. Calculate the field strength at that point. **[2]**
**(c)** State the direction of the force on a −3.5 nC charge placed at the same point, relative to the field. **[1]**

**7.** A small sphere carries +12 nC.

**(a)** Calculate the electric field strength at 0.25 m from its centre. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the field strength at 0.50 m from its centre. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the charge on a sphere that would produce a field strength of 2.0 × 10⁴ N C⁻¹ at 0.50 m from its centre. **[2]**

**8.** Two parallel plates are separated by a distance d and have a potential difference ΔV. Starting from the work done in moving a charge Q from one plate to the other, show that the field strength between the plates is E = ΔV/d. **[3]**

**9.** Parallel plates 1.8 cm apart have a p.d. of 540 V across them. An electron starts from rest next to the negative plate and crosses to the positive plate.

**(a)** Calculate the field strength in the gap. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the force on the electron. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the acceleration of the electron. **[1]**
**(d)** Calculate the speed of the electron when it reaches the positive plate. **[3]**

**10.** A helium nucleus (mass 6.64 × 10⁻²⁷ kg, charge +2e) enters the region between two horizontal parallel plates at 4.0 × 10⁵ m s⁻¹, parallel to the plates. The plates are 6.0 cm long and 2.0 cm apart, with a p.d. of 150 V.

**(a)** Calculate the electric field strength in the gap. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the force on the nucleus. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the acceleration of the nucleus across the plates. **[1]**
**(d)** Calculate the time the nucleus spends between the plates. **[1]**
**(e)** Calculate the sideways displacement of the nucleus when it leaves the plates. **[2]**
**(f)** Describe the shape of the path between the plates and explain why it has this shape. **[2]**
**(g)** A proton enters in the same way at the same speed. Calculate its sideways displacement on leaving. **[1]**

**11.** A tiny oil droplet of mass 2.4 × 10⁻¹⁵ kg is held stationary between horizontal plates 1.2 cm apart with a p.d. of 350 V. The droplet is negatively charged.

**(a)** Calculate the field strength in the gap. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the charge on the droplet. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate how many electrons' worth of charge this is. **[1]**
**(d)** State which plate is positive. **[1]**
**(e)** The p.d. is halved. Explain why the droplet now accelerates, and calculate the size and direction of its acceleration. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** The force varies directly with the product of the two charges [1] and inversely with the square of the distance between them [1]. Air changes the force by a negligible amount, so it can be treated as a vacuum and ε₀ used [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* "Proportional to the charges and the distance" misses both the product and the inverse square.

**2. (a)** F = Q₁Q₂/(4πε₀r²) = 2.4 × 10⁻⁶ × 0.90 × 10⁻⁶ / (4π × 8.85 × 10⁻¹² × 0.35²) [1] = **0.16 N** [1]. The charges are unlike, so the force is **attractive** [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** r doubles, so F falls to a quarter: 0.1585 / 4 = **0.040 N** [1] **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Convert μC to C before substituting. Say "attractive" or "repulsive", not just a number.

**3.** The charge acts as if at the centre, so r = 3.0 + 5.0 = 8.0 cm = 0.080 m [1]. F = 40 × 10⁻⁹ × 1.5 × 10⁻⁹ / (4π × 8.85 × 10⁻¹² × 0.080²) [1] = **8.4 × 10⁻⁵ N** [1], repulsive, so directed away from the large sphere (like charges) [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Using 0.050 m gives 2.2 × 10⁻⁴ N. Show the line r = 0.080 m so your reasoning is visible.

**4. (a)** Fₑ = e²/(4πε₀r²) = (1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹)² / (4π × 8.85 × 10⁻¹² × (1.0 × 10⁻⁹)²) [1] = **2.3 × 10⁻¹⁰ N** [1] **[2]**
**(b)** F_g = Gm²/r² = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ × (9.11 × 10⁻³¹)² / (1.0 × 10⁻⁹)² [1] = **5.5 × 10⁻⁵³ N** [1] **[2]**
**(c)** Ratio = 2.30 × 10⁻¹⁰ / 5.54 × 10⁻⁵³ = **4.2 × 10⁴²** [1]. It is unchanged, because both forces are proportional to 1/r² [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A comparison needs the ratio calculated, not just two numbers. Saying that r cancels shows why the ratio is fixed.

**5. (a)** Straight lines spreading out from the charge [1], with the arrows pointing in towards it [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** The lines are parallel, equally spaced and at right angles to the plates, pointing from the positive plate to the negative plate [1]. Equal spacing shows the field strength is the same everywhere between the plates (uniform) [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Include arrowheads, and link spacing to field strength in words.

**6. (a)** The force per unit positive charge [1] at a point. **[1]**
**(b)** E = F/Q = 2.8 × 10⁻⁵ / 3.5 × 10⁻⁹ [1] = **8.0 × 10³ N C⁻¹** [1] **[2]**
**(c)** **Opposite to the field direction** [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* "Per unit charge" alone is incomplete: say positive charge. The unit N C⁻¹ should be stated with the answer.

**7. (a)** E = Q/(4πε₀r²) = 12 × 10⁻⁹ / (4π × 8.85 × 10⁻¹² × 0.25²) [1] = **1.7 × 10³ N C⁻¹** [1] **[2]**
**(b)** r doubles, so E falls to a quarter: **4.3 × 10² N C⁻¹** [1] **[1]**
**(c)** Q = E × 4πε₀r² = 2.0 × 10⁴ × 4π × 8.85 × 10⁻¹² × 0.50² [1] = **5.6 × 10⁻⁷ C** [1] **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Rearrange the equation before substituting. In part (b), a clearly stated ratio argument is quicker than recalculating.

**8.** Work done moving charge Q across the gap against force F is W = Fd [1]. The work done per unit charge is the p.d., so W = QΔV [1]. Equating and using F = QE: QEd = QΔV, so cancelling Q gives **E = ΔV/d** [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* A "show that" needs every step. Starting from E = V/d proves nothing.

**9. (a)** E = V/d = 540 / 0.018 = **3.0 × 10⁴ V m⁻¹** [1] **[1]**
**(b)** F = eE = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 3.0 × 10⁴ = **4.8 × 10⁻¹⁵ N** [1] **[1]**
**(c)** a = F/m = 4.8 × 10⁻¹⁵ / 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ = **5.3 × 10¹⁵ m s⁻²** [1] **[1]**
**(d)** v² = 2ad [1] = 2 × 5.27 × 10¹⁵ × 0.018 [1], so v = **1.4 × 10⁷ m s⁻¹** [1] **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Carry unrounded values forward. The energy route, eV = ½mv², gives the same speed.

**10. (a)** E = 150 / 0.020 = **7.5 × 10³ V m⁻¹** [1] **[1]**
**(b)** F = QE = 2 × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 7.5 × 10³ = **2.4 × 10⁻¹⁵ N** [1] **[1]**
**(c)** a = F/m = 2.4 × 10⁻¹⁵ / 6.64 × 10⁻²⁷ = **3.6 × 10¹¹ m s⁻²** [1] **[1]**
**(d)** t = L/v = 0.060 / 4.0 × 10⁵ = **1.5 × 10⁻⁷ s** [1] **[1]**
**(e)** y = ½at² [1] = ½ × 3.61 × 10¹¹ × (1.5 × 10⁻⁷)² = **4.1 mm** [1] **[2]**
**(f)** A **parabola** [1]. The speed along the plates is constant, and there is a constant acceleration at right angles to it [1]. **[2]**
**(g)** The proton has half the charge and a quarter of the mass, so a is twice as large. The time is the same, so y = **8.1 mm** [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the charge 2e in part (b). In part (f), "constant force at right angles to a constant velocity" explains the parabola; "it curves" does not.

**11. (a)** E = 350 / 0.012 = **2.9 × 10⁴ V m⁻¹** [1] **[1]**
**(b)** QE = mg [1], so Q = 2.4 × 10⁻¹⁵ × 9.81 / 2.92 × 10⁴ = **8.1 × 10⁻¹⁹ C** [1] **[2]**
**(c)** 8.07 × 10⁻¹⁹ / 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 5.04, so **5** electrons [1] **[1]**
**(d)** The force on the droplet is upwards, so the field points downwards and the **upper plate is positive** [1] **[1]**
**(e)** Halving the p.d. halves E, so the upward force falls to mg/2 [1]. The net force is mg/2 downwards [1], so a = g/2 = **4.9 m s⁻², downwards** [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* State the equilibrium condition (electric force equals weight) before substituting. In part (e), give a direction.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Taking r to the surface of a sphere rather than to its centre.
- Forgetting to convert nC, μC and cm to C and m.
- Giving no direction when the question asks for one.
- Using the charge e instead of 2e for a helium nucleus.
- Writing "it curves" instead of "parabola", with a reason.
- Starting a "show that" from the result.
- Defining E as "force per charge" without saying positive charge.
