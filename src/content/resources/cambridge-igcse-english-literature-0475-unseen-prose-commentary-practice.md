**How to use this set.** Paper 4's prose question is "based on a passage of literary prose, such as an
extract from a novel or short story". Both passages below are original, written for this page, so
you can treat them as genuinely unseen.

**Passage A** *(original passage)*. *In this extract, a boy waits outside the head teacher's office.*

> The corridor outside Mr Adeyemi's office was the longest in the school, although Kofi had never
> noticed until today. There were eleven tiles between the bench and the door. He counted them twice.
> Somewhere a class was chanting the seven times table, and the numbers floated down the corridor,
> calm and orderly, as if nothing in the world could ever go wrong.
>
> The secretary typed without looking at him. Each time the phone rang, Kofi's stomach rose and
> fell with it.
>
> Then the door opened.
>
> "Kofi," said Mr Adeyemi, and smiled, which was worse than anything.

**Passage B** *(original passage)*. *In this extract, an old woman returns to the village where she
grew up.*

> The bus left her at the crossroads, where the signpost still leaned towards a village that seemed
> to have shrunk to fit it. Mrs Lowe stood for a while with her small case, letting the dust settle.
> The well was gone. In its place was a car park, very neat, with white lines and no cars.
>
> She walked the way her feet remembered. Past the chapel, now a shop that sold candles. Past the
> school, where children in bright coats were running in circles, shouting a game she did not know.
> At the end of the lane the house was still there, and someone had painted the door blue, a
> confident, cheerful blue, and she found that she could not forgive them.

## Section A

**1.** Read Passage A. State the narrative voice and explain **one** effect it has. **[2]**

**2.** In Passage A, explain the effect of the precise detail "There were eleven tiles between the
bench and the door. He counted them twice." **[3]**

**3.** In Passage A, explain the effect of the one-line paragraph "Then the door opened." **[2]**

**4.** In Passage B, identify **one** example of ironic contrast and explain its effect. **[2]**

**5.** In Passage B, what is suggested by "the way her feet remembered"? **[2]**

**6.** Divide Passage B into **three** sections and give each a heading describing Mrs Lowe's
changing feelings. **[3]**

## Section B

**7.** Read Passage A.
**(a)** How does the writer create tension in this passage? **[8]**
**(b)** Explore the effect of the final sentence. **[3]**

**8.** Read Passage B. Explore how the writer presents the changes to the village. **[6]**

**9.** Explore how the writer conveys Mrs Lowe's feelings in the final sentence of Passage B. **[4]**

**10.** Write the introduction (three to four sentences) to a commentary on: "How does the writer make
Passage B so moving?" **[4]**

**11.** A candidate writes about Passage B: "Mrs Lowe left the village because of a family argument
many years ago, and now she regrets it." Explain why this would not earn credit and rewrite it as a
valid inference. **[3]**

**12.** A candidate has read both passages in the planning time. Give **two** sensible reasons for
choosing Passage A over Passage B, and **one** reason for the opposite choice, based on what each
passage offers for analysis. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** Third-person narration closely following Kofi's perceptions [1]; it lets the reader share his
anxiety and notice what he notices, such as the tiles and the phone [1].

**2.** The exact number suggests Kofi is fixing his attention on something small to avoid thinking
about what awaits him [1]. Counting "twice" shows how slowly time is passing [1]. The short, flat
sentences create a nervous, stilled rhythm [1].

**3.** Isolating the sentence makes the moment feel sudden and important [1], breaking the waiting and
marking the turning point of the passage [1].

**4.** Either, [2]: the "calm and orderly" times table drifting down the corridor while Kofi is
anxious [1], which sharpens his fear by contrast [1]; or the "confident, cheerful blue" door set
against Mrs Lowe's grief [1], which makes her sense of loss more painful [1].

**5.** She walks from habit or bodily memory rather than conscious thought [1], suggesting how deeply
the village is part of her even after long absence [1].

**6.** One mark for each sensible heading [3], for example: 1. Disorientation (arrival, the shrunken
village, the vanished well); 2. Quiet loss (the chapel and school changed, a game she does not know);
3. Resentment (the house and the blue door she "could not forgive").

**7. (a)** Any four developed points, [2] each:
- The opening claim that the corridor was "the longest in the school" shows how fear stretches space
  and time [2].
- Counting the tiles, twice, shows anxious distraction and slows the pace [2].
- The calm chanting contrasts with Kofi's unease, implying the ordinary world is indifferent [2].
- The secretary who "typed without looking at him" isolates him [2].
- "Kofi's stomach rose and fell" with each phone call physically conveys dread [2].
- The one-line paragraph "Then the door opened." releases the waiting suddenly [2].
- The writer never says why Kofi is there, so the reader's uncertainty matches his [2].
Maximum 8.
**(b)** Up to [3]: the smile is unexpected, overturning the expected anger [1]; "which was worse than
anything" shows Kofi reads it as a sign of something serious [1], so the passage ends on heightened,
unresolved tension rather than relief [1].

**8.** Any three developed points, [2] each:
- The village "seemed to have shrunk", suggesting it is diminished in her eyes, or that she remembers
  it as larger [2].
- The well replaced by a car park "very neat, with white lines and no cars" contrasts a living centre
  with an empty, purposeless order [2].
- The chapel "now a shop that sold candles" shows sacred places becoming commercial, presented with
  quiet irony [2].
- Children "shouting a game she did not know" show the village belongs to a new generation [2].
- The sentence fragments ("Past the chapel… Past the school…") list changes as she walks, so the
  reader experiences them one after another [2].
Maximum 6.

**9.** Any two developed points, [2] each:
- The repetition of "blue" with "confident, cheerful" makes the colour seem almost insulting to her
  [2].
- The anonymous "someone" shows strangers now own her past [2].
- "she found that she could not forgive them" is a surprising, strong reaction to a small change,
  suggesting the door stands for everything she has lost [2].
- The sentence builds slowly with commas and ends on "forgive them", so the emotional weight falls
  at the very end [2].
Maximum 4.

**10.** Narrative situation [1]; a direct answer to "moving" [1]; how the passage develops [1]; a sense
of the methods to be analysed [1]. For example: "In this extract an old woman returns alone to the
village of her childhood and finds it changed. The passage is moving because her quiet observations
reveal a grief she never states directly. It moves from bewilderment at the crossroads to a sudden
resentment at the end, and I will explore how ironic contrast, the list of changed places and the
final image of the blue door create this effect."

**11.** It invents back-story that the passage does not give [1], so it cannot be supported by
evidence [1]. A valid inference: "Her long absence is implied by how much has changed and by her
walking 'the way her feet remembered', which suggests she knew the village intimately once" [1].

**12.** Two reasons for Passage A, [1] each: a clear build-up of tension to analyse; a sharp turning
point and final line; distinctive structural features such as the one-line paragraph. One reason for
Passage B [1]: rich imagery and ironic contrast; a strong emotional ending; a clear sequence of
changes that structures a commentary. Maximum 3.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Retelling the passage instead of analysing selected details.
- Inventing back-story or explanations.
- Ignoring structure: paragraphing, sentence length and the final line.
- Losing focus on the question's key word.

## Frequently asked questions

**How much of the passage should I cover?**
Enough to show its development from beginning to end, analysing selected moments closely rather than
every sentence.

**Can I organise the commentary by technique instead of by section?**
You can, if each paragraph still answers the question, but following the passage's sections usually
gives a clearer argument.
