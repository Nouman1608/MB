## 4.2.4(a): What infrared radiation does to bonds

Atoms joined by a covalent bond are always vibrating. The bond stretches and bends, a little like two balls joined by a spring. Each bond vibrates naturally at particular frequencies. These depend on the masses of the two atoms and on the strength of the bond.

When infrared radiation of exactly the right frequency meets a bond, the bond absorbs that energy and vibrates more. An IR spectrometer passes a range of IR frequencies through a sample and records how much of each gets through. Frequencies the bonds absorb show up as dips in transmittance.

The horizontal axis of an IR spectrum is **wavenumber**, in cm⁻¹. It runs from about 4000 cm⁻¹ on the left to about 500 cm⁻¹ on the right. The vertical axis is percentage transmittance, so an absorption is a downward peak.

## 4.2.4(b): Greenhouse gases and global warming

The Earth's surface is warmed by sunlight and then gives out energy as infrared radiation. Some atmospheric gases contain bonds that absorb this IR:

- carbon dioxide, CO₂, through its C=O bonds
- water vapour, H₂O, through its O–H bonds
- methane, CH₄, through its C–H bonds.

The bonds absorb IR and vibrate more. The energy is then given out again in all directions, some of it back towards the surface. Less energy escapes to space, so the lower atmosphere stays warmer than it otherwise would.

Higher concentrations of these gases, mainly CO₂ from burning fossil fuels and CH₄ from farming and landfill, are linked to global warming. Scientists treat this link as strongly supported by evidence, and the specification calls it a suspected link. Accepting the evidence has pushed governments towards policies that change how energy is supplied and used:

- more electricity from renewable sources such as wind, solar and hydroelectric power
- less burning of coal, oil and gas
- better energy efficiency in buildings, vehicles and industry.

In an answer, name the bond in each gas and say that it absorbs IR. "Greenhouse gases trap heat" on its own does not use the chemistry.

## 4.2.4(c): Identifying alcohols, carbonyls and acids

Each type of bond absorbs in its own range of wavenumbers. In examinations, the specification says IR absorption data will be provided on the Data Sheet. For practice on this page, use these typical values:

| Bond | Where it is found | Wavenumber / cm⁻¹ | Appearance |
|---|---|---|---|
| O–H | alcohols | 3200–3600 | broad |
| O–H | carboxylic acids | 2500–3300 | very broad |
| C–H | almost all organic compounds | 2850–3100 | sharp, medium |
| C=O | aldehydes, ketones, carboxylic acids | 1630–1820 | strong, sharp |
| C=C | alkenes | 1620–1680 | medium |
| C–O | alcohols, carboxylic acids | 1000–1300 | strong |

The specification reminds you that most organic compounds give a peak near 3000 cm⁻¹ because of C–H bonds. That peak does not identify anything, so do not use it as evidence for a functional group.

The three patterns you must recognise:

1. **Alcohol:** broad O–H absorption at 3200–3600 cm⁻¹, and **no** C=O peak.
2. **Aldehyde or ketone:** strong C=O absorption at about 1700 cm⁻¹, and **no** broad O–H.
3. **Carboxylic acid:** strong C=O at about 1700 cm⁻¹ **and** a very broad O–H spreading across 2500–3300 cm⁻¹, often overlapping the C–H peaks.

### Worked example 1: three unlabelled liquids

Three bottles hold pentan-1-ol, cyclohexanone and 2-methylbutanoic acid, but the labels have fallen off. The main absorptions are:

- Liquid A: 2870–2960 cm⁻¹ and 1715 cm⁻¹ only.
- Liquid B: a very broad band from 2500 to 3300 cm⁻¹, and 1710 cm⁻¹.
- Liquid C: a broad band at 3330 cm⁻¹, 2930 cm⁻¹ and 1060 cm⁻¹.

**Step 1.** Ignore 2850–3100 cm⁻¹. All three compounds have C–H bonds.

**Step 2.** Look for C=O near 1700 cm⁻¹. A and B have it; C does not.

**Step 3.** Look for O–H. B has the very broad acid O–H; C has the alcohol O–H; A has none.

**Conclusion.** A is **cyclohexanone** (C=O, no O–H). B is **2-methylbutanoic acid** (C=O plus very broad O–H). C is **pentan-1-ol** (O–H, with C–O at 1060 cm⁻¹, and no C=O).

## 4.2.4(d): Interpreting and predicting unfamiliar spectra

The same table lets you work forwards (structure to spectrum) or backwards (spectrum to functional group). For a prediction:

1. Draw the structure and list every bond type except plain C–C.
2. Match each bond to a range from the data supplied.
3. Say which peaks you expect and which you do **not** expect. The absence of a peak is evidence too.

### Worked example 2: predicting a spectrum

Predict the main IR absorptions of prop-2-en-1-ol, CH₂=CHCH₂OH.

- O–H in an alcohol: broad absorption at 3200–3600 cm⁻¹.
- C=C in an alkene: absorption at 1620–1680 cm⁻¹.
- C–O: absorption at 1000–1300 cm⁻¹.
- C–H: absorption at 2850–3100 cm⁻¹ (present but not diagnostic).
- No C=O, so nothing strong at 1630–1820 cm⁻¹ other than the weaker C=C peak.

The C=C and C=O ranges overlap slightly. A C=O peak is usually strong. A C=C peak is usually weaker. Use the rest of the spectrum and any other data to decide.

## 4.2.4(e): Pollution monitoring and breathalysers

Because each gas absorbs IR at its own wavenumbers, an IR instrument can pick out one gas in a mixture. The more of that gas there is, the more IR it absorbs at its wavenumber. So the amount absorbed is a measure of concentration.

- **Air pollution.** Roadside and exhaust monitors use IR absorption to measure gases such as carbon monoxide, CO, and nitrogen monoxide, NO, from car engines. Each gas has absorptions the other gases do not share.
- **Breathalysers.** Modern evidential breathalysers pass IR through a breath sample and measure absorption in the region where C–H bonds in ethanol absorb, close to 2900 cm⁻¹. The absorption gives the ethanol concentration in the breath. Some instruments compare two nearby wavelengths so that other compounds in breath, such as propanone, are not mistaken for ethanol.

The readings from these instruments can be used as evidence in court, which is the HSW12 point in the specification.

## 4.2.4(f): The molecular ion and molecular mass

In a mass spectrometer, organic molecules are ionised. A molecule M loses one electron to give the **molecular ion**, M⁺. On this course you only meet singly charged ions, so m/z (mass ÷ charge) equals the mass of the ion.

The **molecular ion peak** is the peak with the highest m/z, apart from a very small peak just after it. Its m/z value equals the relative molecular mass.

That small peak is the **M+1 peak**. About 1.1% of carbon atoms are carbon-13, so a few molecules are 1 unit heavier than the rest. Do not mistake it for the molecular ion.

The specification states that you will not be expected to interpret mass spectra of organic halogen compounds.

## 4.2.4(g): Fragmentation

Many molecular ions break apart inside the spectrometer. Each break gives one positive ion and one neutral radical:

M⁺ → X⁺ + Y•

Only the positive ion is detected, so only X⁺ gives a peak. The radical Y• is uncharged and leaves no trace on the spectrum. Common fragment ions:

| m/z | Possible fragment ion |
|---|---|
| 15 | CH₃⁺ |
| 29 | C₂H₅⁺ or CHO⁺ |
| 31 | CH₂OH⁺ |
| 43 | C₃H₇⁺ or CH₃CO⁺ |
| 45 | COOH⁺ |
| 57 | C₄H₉⁺ or C₂H₅CO⁺ |

Two different fragments can share the same m/z. Use the rest of the evidence to decide between them. When you write a fragment ion, include the positive charge. When you write the lost piece, show it as a radical with a dot.

### Worked example 3: interpreting peaks

A carbonyl compound gives peaks at m/z 15, 43, 71, 86 and a very small peak at 87.

- **Molecular ion:** m/z 86, so Mr = 86. The peak at 87 is the M+1 peak from carbon-13.
- **m/z 71:** 86 − 71 = 15, so a CH₃ group has been lost. The ion is C₄H₇O⁺.
- **m/z 43:** could be CH₃CO⁺ or C₃H₇⁺. Both have mass 43.
- **m/z 15:** CH₃⁺.

For 3-methylbutan-2-one, CH₃COCH(CH₃)₂, one fragmentation that gives the m/z 43 peak is:

[CH₃COCH(CH₃)₂]⁺ → CH₃CO⁺ + •CH(CH₃)₂

Breaking on the other side of the carbonyl group, the same molecule can lose •CH₃ and give (CH₃)₂CHCO⁺ at m/z 71.

## 4.2.4(h): Combining analytical data

No single technique gives the whole answer. Use them in this order:

1. **Elemental analysis** gives the empirical formula (method from 2.1.3(c)).
2. **Mass spectrum** gives Mr from the molecular ion. Divide Mr by the empirical formula mass to get the molecular formula.
3. **IR spectrum** gives the functional group(s).
4. **Fragmentation peaks** narrow down how the atoms are arranged.
5. **Check** that your structure fits every piece of data.

### Worked example 4: from percentages to structure

Compound Z contains carbon, hydrogen and oxygen only. By mass it is 69.8% C, 11.6% H and 18.6% O. Its mass spectrum is the one in Worked example 3. Its IR spectrum shows a strong peak at 1715 cm⁻¹, C–H peaks near 2900 cm⁻¹, and no broad absorption above 2500 cm⁻¹. Use Ar values C = 12.0, H = 1.0, O = 16.0.

**Empirical formula.** Assume 100 g.

```
C: 69.8 ÷ 12.0 = 5.82 mol
H: 11.6 ÷ 1.0 = 11.6 mol
O: 18.6 ÷ 16.0 = 1.16 mol
Divide by 1.16:  C 5.01   H 10.0   O 1.00
Empirical formula C5H10O, mass 86.0
```

**Molecular formula.** Mr = 86 from the molecular ion. 86 ÷ 86.0 = 1, so the molecular formula is **C₅H₁₀O**.

**Functional group.** C=O at 1715 cm⁻¹ with no O–H, so Z is an aldehyde or a ketone.

**Fragments.**

- The peak at m/z 43 fits CH₃CO⁺. The peak at m/z 71 shows loss of CH₃. Together these point to a methyl ketone, CH₃CO–R, with R = C₃H₇.
- An aldehyde would usually show CHO⁺ at m/z 29. Pentan-3-one would give C₂H₅CO⁺ at m/z 57 and C₂H₅⁺ at m/z 29. There are no peaks at 29 or 57, so neither fits.

**Conclusion.** Z is a methyl ketone C₃H₇COCH₃: **pentan-2-one or 3-methylbutan-2-one**. Both fit every piece of data given. To tell them apart you would need NMR spectroscopy (6.3.2), which you meet in Module 6. Saying honestly that two structures fit is better than forcing a single answer the data do not support.

## Common errors

- Treating the C–H peak near 3000 cm⁻¹ as evidence for an alcohol or acid.
- Calling any absorption in 2500–3600 cm⁻¹ "O–H" without saying whether it is the alcohol or the acid range and shape.
- Naming a carboxylic acid from the C=O peak alone, without the very broad O–H.
- Saying greenhouse gases "reflect" IR, rather than that their bonds absorb it and vibrate more.
- Reading the M+1 peak as the molecular ion and giving Mr one unit too high.
- Writing fragment ions without a positive charge, or the lost radical without a dot.
- Giving a single structure when the data fit two or more isomers.
