These questions cover section 7.1, *Perspectives on globalisation*, of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Sociology (9699) syllabus for 2024–2026, assessed in Paper 4 Section A. Paper 4 itself sets only extended essays; Section A below uses short concept questions to build the knowledge those essays need, and Section B practises paragraph and essay writing. All places and organisations in the items are fictional.

Revise first with the [perspectives on globalisation study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-sociology-9699-perspectives-on-globalisation/) and the [perspectives on globalisation revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-sociology-9699-perspectives-on-globalisation-revision-notes/).

## Section A – Concept questions

**1.** Define "glocalisation", with an example. **[2]**

**2.** Identify Held et al.'s four features of globalisation. **[2]**

**3.** Explain one problem in defining globalisation. **[3]**

**4.** Explain what Appadurai meant by describing global culture as disjunctive. **[4]**

**5.** Distinguish between the sceptic and transformationalist perspectives on globalisation. **[4]**

**6.** Explain how the Marxist concept of a transnational capitalist class answers the question of who benefits from globalisation. **[4]**

**7.** Read the item.

> Velantra, a fictional clothing company, designs garments in one country, has them sewn by mainly female workers on short contracts in a second country, and sells them online worldwide. When wages rise in the second country, it moves production to a third.

Using the item, explain how feminist and Marxist perspectives would interpret who benefits. **[6]**

**8.** Explain Bauman's distinction between "tourists" and "vagabonds" and what it suggests about who benefits from globalisation. **[4]**

## Section B – Paragraph and essay questions

**9.** Explain and evaluate the globalist claim that globalisation benefits most people. **[8]**

**10.** Explain and evaluate the sceptic view that globalisation has been exaggerated. **[8]**

**11.** "Globalisation mainly benefits a transnational capitalist class." Evaluate this view. **[35]**

**12.** Evaluate the view that globalisation is too vague a concept to be useful in sociology. **[35]**

## Answers

**1.** The adaptation of global products, ideas or brands to local cultures (Robertson) [1], for example a global fast-food chain changing its menu to fit local religious or dietary rules [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Extensity and intensity [1]; velocity and impact [1]. **[2]**

**3.** It is unclear whether globalisation is new [1]: trade empires and colonialism linked continents centuries ago [1], so sceptics argue today's interconnection is a difference of degree, although Held et al. argue intensity and velocity are now of a different order [1]. **[3]**

**4.** Appadurai described global culture as made of five flows, or scapes, of people, media, technology, finance and ideas [1]. These flows move at different speeds and in different directions [1], for example capital moving freely while people face border controls [1], so global culture does not become one uniform culture but is uneven and locally varied [1]. **[4]**

**5.** Sceptics argue globalisation is exaggerated [1]: trade is concentrated in a Triad of regions and states remain powerful (Hirst and Thompson) [1]. Transformationalists accept globalisation is real [1] but see it as uneven and open-ended, changing the role of states rather than abolishing them (Held et al.) [1]. **[4]**

**6.** Sklair argues globalisation is driven by a transnational capitalist class whose interests are global rather than national [1]. It includes corporate, state, technical and consumerist fractions [1]. They gain from open markets that let capital move to where labour is cheapest [1], while workers, who cannot move freely, bear the costs of competition [1]. **[4]**

**7.** Feminist: the company relies on mainly female workers on short contracts [1], illustrating Standing's global feminisation of flexible, low-paid labour [1]; women gain income but on insecure terms, while consumers in richer countries benefit from cheap clothing [1].
Marxist: moving production when wages rise shows capital's mobility [1], giving owners and executives profits from cheap labour [1]; workers in each country lose jobs and bargaining power, so the transnational capitalist class benefits most [1]. **[6]**

**8.** Tourists are people who can move freely across borders by choice [1], such as wealthy professionals; vagabonds are those stuck in place or forced to move [1], such as poor workers or refugees. It suggests globalisation benefits the mobile [1] and brings insecurity to those without the resources to choose where they live or work [1]. **[4]**

**9.** Explanation: globalists argue globalisation is a real, powerful process [1] in which open trade and investment spread growth (Ohmae; Bhagwati, 2004) [1]. Consumers gain cheaper goods and workers in export sectors gain jobs [1], and countries that integrate can reduce poverty [1].
Evaluation: Marxists argue the gains are concentrated in a transnational capitalist class (Sklair) [1]. Feminists show many new jobs are insecure and low-paid (Standing) [1]. Bauman's vagabonds face insecurity rather than benefit [1]. So the claim fits some countries and groups but not "most people" everywhere [1]. **[8]**

**10.** Explanation: Hirst and Thompson argued that trade and investment are concentrated in a Triad of Europe, North America and Japan [1], and that the economy before 1914 was in some respects similarly open [1]. States still control borders, taxes and law [1], so globalisation is less new and less global than claimed [1].
Evaluation: Held et al. argue that the intensity and velocity of flows are now of a different order [1]. Digital media and finance connect more people more quickly than in earlier periods [1]. Appadurai's scapes show cultural flows sceptics underplay [1]. Sceptics may be right about the persistence of states but understate change [1]. **[8]**

**11.** Marked by levels of response; this is indicative content.

- **For the view (AO1/AO2):** Sklair's transnational capitalist class and its four fractions; Harvey on neo-liberalism restoring elite power; capital mobility and relocation of production; tax avoidance across borders; Bauman's tourists.
- **Against the view:** globalist arguments that trade has reduced poverty (Bhagwati); consumers gain cheaper goods; feminists show women gain income and some independence; transformationalists stress variation between states; sceptics argue national policies still shape who gains.
- **Evaluation (AO3):** distinguish economic from cultural and political dimensions; consider middle classes in emerging economies; Marxist analysis can understate agency of states and workers; apply to contrasting societies.
- **Judgement:** for example, the capitalist class gains disproportionately, but "mainly" understates gains for some workers and consumers.

**Level guidance:** top answers build a sustained argument using at least three perspectives in depth, with contrasting evidence and a judgement on "mainly"; middle answers describe perspectives with limited weighing; low answers list views or rely on assertion.

**12.** Marked by levels of response; this is indicative content.

- **For the view:** problems of definition — is it new, one process or several, global at all, ideological; the term can describe almost any change, so it risks explaining nothing.
- **Against the view:** Waters's three-part definition and Held et al.'s four features make it measurable; Appadurai's scapes give it analytical structure; transformationalism shows it can be used precisely to compare societies.
- **Evaluation:** vagueness may reflect a complex reality rather than a weak concept; precision depends on the dimension studied; the ideological use of the word is itself sociologically interesting.
- **Judgement:** for example, the concept is useful when broken into dimensions and features, but weak when used as a single cause.

**Level guidance:** top answers test the concept against specific definitions and uses; middle answers describe definitional problems; low answers assert that globalisation is "everywhere".
