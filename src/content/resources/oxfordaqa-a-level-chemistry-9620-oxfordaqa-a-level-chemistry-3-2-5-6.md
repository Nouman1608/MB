This guide goes deeper into **3.2.5.6 Catalysts**, the last subtopic of section 3.2.5 Transition metals in the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Chemistry (9620) specification, Version 5.3. It is International A2 content, assessed in Unit 3 (Inorganic 2 and Physical 2) and available again in the synoptic Unit 5. You need to describe how both kinds of transition metal catalyst work, explain why variable oxidation states matter, and write the equations for three named examples.

## What the specification asks for

- Transition metals and their compounds can act as heterogeneous and homogeneous catalysts.
- A heterogeneous catalyst is in a different phase from the reactants, and the reaction occurs at active sites on the surface.
- A support medium is used to maximise the surface area of a heterogeneous catalyst and minimise the cost.
- V₂O₅ acts as a heterogeneous catalyst in the Contact process; Fe is used as a heterogeneous catalyst in the Haber process.
- Heterogeneous catalysts can become poisoned by impurities that block the active sites, reducing efficiency; this has a cost implication.
- A homogeneous catalyst is in the same phase as the reactants; the reaction then proceeds through an intermediate species.
- Explain the importance of variable oxidation states in catalysis, and explain with equations how V₂O₅ acts in the Contact process, how Fe²⁺ catalyses the reaction between I⁻ and S₂O₈²⁻, and how Mn²⁺ autocatalyses the reaction between C₂O₄²⁻ and MnO₄⁻.

A catalyst provides an alternative reaction route with a lower activation energy, and is not used up overall. The general idea is in the [kinetics study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-kinetics/); this subtopic is about how transition metals achieve it.

## Heterogeneous catalysis

"Phase" here means solid, liquid, gas or aqueous solution. In industry, heterogeneous catalysts are usually solids with gaseous reactants.

### What happens at an active site

1. **Adsorption.** Reactant molecules form weak bonds to atoms at active sites on the catalyst surface.
2. **Reaction.** Bonds within the adsorbed reactants are weakened, and the reactants are held close together, so they react with a lower activation energy.
3. **Desorption.** The products leave the surface, freeing the active sites for more reactant.

Because reaction happens only at the surface, the amount of catalyst that matters is its **surface area**, not its mass.

### Support media

Catalysts such as platinum and rhodium are expensive. Spreading a very thin layer of catalyst over an inert **support medium** with a large surface area, such as a ceramic honeycomb, gives a very large catalyst surface from a small mass of metal. That is how catalytic converters in car exhausts are built: the support **maximises surface area** and **minimises the cost**.

### Two named industrial examples

| Process | Overall reaction | Catalyst | Type |
|---|---|---|---|
| Contact process | 2SO₂(g) + O₂(g) ⇌ 2SO₃(g) | V₂O₅(s) | heterogeneous |
| Haber process | N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) ⇌ 2NH₃(g) | Fe(s) | heterogeneous |

### How V₂O₅ works

Vanadium changes oxidation state, which lets the oxide pass oxygen from O₂ to SO₂:

```
V₂O₅ + SO₂ → V₂O₄ + SO₃          V: +5 → +4   (V₂O₅ oxidises SO₂)
V₂O₄ + ½O₂ → V₂O₅                V: +4 → +5   (catalyst regenerated)
Sum:  SO₂ + ½O₂ → SO₃
```

The two steps add up to the overall reaction, and V₂O₅ appears on both sides, so it is not used up.

### Poisoning

An impurity that adsorbs strongly onto active sites and does not desorb **blocks** them. Fewer sites remain for the reactants, so the catalyst becomes less efficient. Well-known examples: sulfur compounds in the feed gas poison the iron catalyst in the Haber process, and lead compounds poison catalytic converters, which is why cars fitted with them need unleaded fuel.

Poisoning costs money in several ways:

- the reaction slows, so output per hour falls
- the catalyst has to be cleaned or replaced more often
- the feedstock has to be purified first to remove the poison.

## Homogeneous catalysis

A homogeneous catalyst is in the **same phase** as the reactants; for transition metals this usually means aqueous ions. The catalyst reacts with one reactant to form an **intermediate species**, which then reacts to give the products and regenerate the catalyst. Each step has a lower activation energy than the uncatalysed reaction, so an enthalpy profile shows two smaller humps in place of one large one.

### Why variable oxidation states matter

A transition metal ion can switch between oxidation states easily. It can accept electrons from one reactant and pass them to another, and end up back where it started. That is exactly what the two homogeneous examples in the specification rely on.

### Fe²⁺ with peroxodisulfate and iodide

```
Uncatalysed:  S₂O₈²⁻ + 2I⁻ → 2SO₄²⁻ + I₂      (slow)
Step 1:       S₂O₈²⁻ + 2Fe²⁺ → 2SO₄²⁻ + 2Fe³⁺
Step 2:       2Fe³⁺ + 2I⁻ → 2Fe²⁺ + I₂
```

The uncatalysed reaction is slow because both reactants are **negative ions**, which repel each other, so the activation energy is high. In each catalysed step, the ions that collide have **opposite charges** and attract. The Fe³⁺ formed in step 1 is the intermediate. Fe³⁺ catalyses the reaction equally well, because step 2 can happen first.

### Worked example 1: checking a catalyst with E⦵ values

Use these values to show that Fe²⁺ can catalyse the reaction.

| Half-equation | E⦵ / V |
|---|---|
| S₂O₈²⁻ + 2e⁻ ⇌ 2SO₄²⁻ | +2.01 |
| Fe³⁺ + e⁻ ⇌ Fe²⁺ | +0.77 |
| I₂ + 2e⁻ ⇌ 2I⁻ | +0.54 |

```
Step 1: S₂O₈²⁻ oxidises Fe²⁺   E⦵cell = 2.01 − 0.77 = +1.24 V   feasible
Step 2: Fe³⁺ oxidises I⁻        E⦵cell = 0.77 − 0.54 = +0.23 V   feasible
```

Both steps are feasible because the E⦵ of the catalyst's couple lies **between** those of the two reactant couples. Any ion pair with E⦵ outside that range would fail at one step. E⦵cell shows only feasibility, not rate; the catalysed route is faster because its steps involve oppositely charged ions.

### Autocatalysis by Mn²⁺

In an **autocatalysed** reaction, a product catalyses the reaction. Manganate(VII) and ethanedioate react slowly at first (again two negative ions), but the Mn²⁺ produced is a catalyst:

```
Overall:  2MnO₄⁻ + 16H⁺ + 5C₂O₄²⁻ → 2Mn²⁺ + 10CO₂ + 8H₂O
Step 1:   MnO₄⁻ + 4Mn²⁺ + 8H⁺ → 5Mn³⁺ + 4H₂O
Step 2:   2Mn³⁺ + C₂O₄²⁻ → 2Mn²⁺ + 2CO₂
```

Mn³⁺ is the intermediate. Because Mn²⁺ builds up as the reaction goes on, the rate rises after a slow start.

### Worked example 2: reading an autocatalysis curve

A colorimeter follows [MnO₄⁻] (purple) at constant temperature with excess ethanedioate. Results:

| Time / s | 0 | 30 | 60 | 90 | 120 | 150 | 180 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| [MnO₄⁻] / 10⁻³ mol dm⁻³ | 2.00 | 1.95 | 1.80 | 1.30 | 0.60 | 0.20 | 0.05 |

*Describe and explain the shape of the curve.*

- **0 to 60 s:** the concentration falls slowly. Little Mn²⁺ is present, so the reaction is mainly uncatalysed and slow.
- **60 to 120 s:** the gradient is steepest (a fall of 0.70 × 10⁻³ mol dm⁻³ in 30 s between 90 and 120 s). Mn²⁺ has built up and catalyses the reaction.
- **After 120 s:** the gradient decreases again because the MnO₄⁻ is being used up.

The result is an S-shaped decay curve, unlike the steadily flattening curve of an ordinary reaction. If some MnSO₄ were added at the start, there would be no slow initial stage.

## Common mistakes

- Defining a heterogeneous catalyst as "a solid catalyst". The definition is about a **different phase** from the reactants.
- Saying a support medium "increases the rate" without the reasons the specification gives: maximum surface area and minimum cost.
- Saying a poison "reacts with" or "uses up" the catalyst. It **blocks active sites**.
- Writing the V₂O₅ steps without oxidation states, or forgetting that V₂O₄ is reoxidised.
- Explaining the Fe²⁺ catalysis with E⦵ only. The reason it is faster is the attraction between oppositely charged ions.
- Calling Fe in the Haber process homogeneous, or Fe²⁺(aq) in the iodide reaction heterogeneous.
- Describing autocatalysis as "the catalyst is used up and remade". The point is that the catalyst is a **product**.

## Exam technique for OxfordAQA command words

- **Explain, with the aid of equations**: give each step as a balanced equation, label oxidation-state changes, and show that the catalyst is regenerated.
- **Explain** (why a catalyst works): lower activation energy route, plus the specific reason (oppositely charged ions; change of oxidation state).
- **Describe and explain** (graphs): describe each section of the curve with data, then give the cause.
- **Suggest** (costs): link a chemical fact (blocked sites, expensive metal) to an economic effect.
