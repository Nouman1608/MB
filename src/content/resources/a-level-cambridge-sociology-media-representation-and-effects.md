Media representation is how the media portray social groups, and media effects are the influence
those portrayals have on audiences. Syllabus heading 10 of Cambridge International A Level
Sociology (9699), *Media representation and effects*, is the second half of Paper 4's Media
section. It asks how class, gender, ethnicity and age groups are represented (10.1), which models
best explain media influence (10.2), and whether the media change behaviour, from violence to
crime and positive action (10.3). Strong 35-mark essays weigh direct-effects claims against
active-audience evidence.

Ownership and theories of content (heading 9) are covered in the
[media ownership and control guide](/resources/a-level-cambridge-sociology-media-ownership-and-control/).

## Syllabus coverage

Cambridge International AS & A Level Sociology 9699 (2024–2026), Paper 4 Section B, heading 10:

- **10.1 Media representations of class, gender, ethnicity, and age groups** — how different groups
  are represented; ways the media contributes to gender socialisation; moral panics around class,
  gender, ethnicity and age groups; the relationship between media and popular culture.
- **10.2 Different models of media effects** — direct effects, including the hypodermic syringe
  model; indirect effects, including uses and gratification, two-step flow, normative and cultural
  effects models; strengths and limitations of each; arguments and evidence on how far behaviour
  is influenced by the media.
- **10.3 The impact of the media on behaviour** — whether violent media leads to violent behaviour;
  the impact on crime, including deviance amplification and moral panics; positive impacts on
  behaviour; effects of sensationalism and stereotyping.

## How are social groups represented in the media?

| Group | Typical findings | Key sources |
|---|---|---|
| **Gender** | Women under-represented or confined to domestic and sexualised roles; men shown as active and powerful | Gaye Tuchman's "symbolic annihilation" of women (1978); Laura Mulvey's "male gaze" (1975); Marjorie Ferguson's *Forever Feminine* (1983) on women's magazines |
| **Ethnicity** | Minorities linked to crime, conflict or exoticism; under-representation in positions of authority | Edward Said's *Orientalism* (1978); Teun van Dijk's *Racism and the Press* (1991) |
| **Class** | Upper and middle classes normalised; working class shown as comic, deviant or "undeserving" | Owen Jones, *Chavs: The Demonization of the Working Class* (2011) |
| **Age** | Youth linked to deviance and risk; older people under-represented or stereotyped as frail | Stanley Cohen's research on youth (1972) |

## How does the media contribute to gender socialisation?

- **Role models and norms:** representations show "appropriate" masculinity and femininity.
- **Beauty ideals:** Naomi Wolf's *The Beauty Myth* (1990) argued that unattainable ideals control
  women.
- **Teenage magazines:** Angela McRobbie's analysis of *Jackie* (late 1970s) showed girls taught that
  romance was their main goal.
- **Change and choice:** David Gauntlett (*Media, Gender and Identity*, 2002) argued modern media
  offer more diverse images, which audiences use to build their own identities.

## What are moral panics?

Stanley Cohen's *Folk Devils and Moral Panics* (1972) studied the reaction to Mods and Rockers in
English seaside towns in 1964. Exaggerated, sensational reporting turned a group into **folk
devils**, provoking public concern and harsher control. Stuart Hall and colleagues' *Policing the
Crisis* (1978) linked a moral panic about "mugging" to the racialisation of crime and a crisis of
hegemony. Moral panics can target class (welfare claimants), gender (single mothers), ethnicity
(migrants) and age (youth subcultures, online gaming).

## How do media and popular culture relate?

Popular culture is the culture consumed by the majority — music, film, sport, social media.
Marxists of the Frankfurt School criticised a **mass culture** "industry" that pacifies audiences;
postmodernists such as Dominic Strinati (1995) argue the line between "high" and "popular" culture
has blurred, and that audiences use popular culture creatively.

## Which models of media effects are there?

| Model | Claim | Evidence | Limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Hypodermic syringe** (direct) | Media "inject" messages into a passive audience, producing immediate effects | Albert Bandura's Bobo doll experiments (1961, 1963): children copied aggression they had watched | Laboratory artificiality; ignores social context and interpretation |
| **Two-step flow** | Messages pass through opinion leaders who interpret them for others | Elihu Katz and Paul Lazarsfeld, *Personal Influence* (1955) | Social media blur who is an "opinion leader" |
| **Uses and gratifications** | Audiences actively use media to satisfy needs (diversion, relationships, identity, surveillance) | Jay Blumler and Denis McQuail (1968); McQuail's typology | Underplays media power to shape needs themselves |
| **Cultural effects** | Repeated messages slowly shape attitudes and "common sense" over time | George Gerbner's cultivation research (1970s); Glasgow University Media Group | Long-term effects are hard to measure |
| **Normative** | Media influence behaviour by shaping norms of what is acceptable, especially where other socialising agencies are weak | Used to explain imitation among those with weak social bonds | Difficult to separate media from other influences |

Stuart Hall's **encoding/decoding** model (1973) and David Morley's *The Nationwide Audience*
(1980) showed audiences reading the same programme in dominant, negotiated or oppositional ways,
supporting active-audience views.

## Does violent media cause violent behaviour?

**Arguments for:** Bandura's experiments; the Newson Report (1994), written after the murder of
James Bulger, argued that violent videos could harm children; repeated exposure may desensitise.

**Arguments against:**
- Tony Charlton's study of St Helena, where television arrived in 1995, found no increase in
  children's anti-social behaviour in the years that followed.
- David Gauntlett's "Ten things wrong with the media effects model" (1998) criticised effects
  research for treating children as inadequate, using artificial methods and ignoring meaning.
- David Buckingham (1993) found children were media-literate and distinguished fiction from
  reality.

## How does the media affect crime?

- **Deviance amplification** — Leslie Wilkins (1964) and Jock Young's *The Drugtakers* (1971):
  media attention and police reaction increase the deviance they report.
- **Moral panics** — Cohen (1972); Hall et al. (1978).
- **Copycat crime** and the glamorising of offending.
- **Fear of crime** — Gerbner found heavy viewers overestimated their risk of victimisation.

## Can the media have a positive impact?

Charity appeals and public-health campaigns, pro-social children's programmes, representation of
marginalised groups and campaigns against discrimination can change attitudes and behaviour.
Social media can mobilise support for causes and help people find communities.

## How do sensationalism and stereotyping affect people?

Sensational reporting heightens fear and prejudice; stereotypes can produce a **self-fulfilling
prophecy** for labelled groups, shape how police and employers respond, and affect self-esteem —
linking to labelling theory.

## Frequently asked questions

**Which effects model is strongest?** No single model is accepted. Many sociologists combine active
audience ideas (uses and gratifications, encoding/decoding) with long-term cultural effects.

**Is Bandura enough evidence for a direct-effects essay?** Use it, but evaluate its laboratory
setting and short-term measures, and set it against Charlton and Gauntlett.

**What is the difference between a moral panic and deviance amplification?** A moral panic is the
exaggerated public reaction; deviance amplification is the spiral in which that reaction increases
the deviance itself.
