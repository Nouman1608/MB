## Reflection: using i = r in calculations

Every angle in this subtopic is measured from the **normal**, the line at
90° to the surface at the point where the ray strikes. Questions often give
the angle to the mirror surface instead, so convert first: angle of
incidence = 90° − angle to the surface.

The plane-mirror image is **virtual**, **upright**, the **same size** as the
object and the **same distance behind** the mirror as the object is in
front. That last property is the source of most distance questions.

**Worked example 1.** A girl stands 2.0 m in front of a plane mirror and
walks 0.5 m towards it. How far is she from her image now, and by how much
has that distance changed?

```
new object distance  = 2.0 − 0.5 = 1.5 m
image distance       = 1.5 m behind the mirror
girl to image        = 1.5 + 1.5 = 3.0 m   (was 4.0 m)
change               = 1.0 m — twice the distance she walked
```

The image always moves the same distance as the object, so the gap between
them changes by double.

## Refraction and refractive index

Light slows down when it enters glass or water from air, and the side of
the beam that enters first slows first, so the ray turns **towards the
normal**. Leaving the block, it speeds up and turns **away from the
normal**. A ray along the normal (i = 0°) changes speed but not direction.

The refractive index is defined with the angle in air as i:

```
n = sin i / sin r        (i in air, r in the material)
```

**Worked example 2 (from a block experiment).** A student traces a ray
into a glass block and measures i = 50° and r = 30.7°.

```
n = sin 50° / sin 30.7° = 0.766 / 0.511 = 1.50
```

Repeating for several angles and averaging gives a more reliable value,
because each pencil-and-protractor reading carries an error of about a
degree.

**Light going from glass into air.** Rearrange the same relationship so
that the angle in air is still on top: sin (angle in air) = n × sin (angle
in glass). For a ray inside glass of n = 1.50 at 35° to the normal:

```
sin (angle in air) = 1.50 × sin 35° = 1.50 × 0.574 = 0.860
angle in air = 59.4°
```

The ray bends away from the normal, as expected.

**Blocks of different shapes.** A rectangular block gives an emergent ray
parallel to the incident ray but displaced sideways. A triangular prism
deviates the ray towards its base. A semicircular block lets a ray aimed at
the centre of the flat face enter the curved face along a normal, so it
does not bend there; this is why it is used for the internal reflection
experiment.

## Critical angle and total internal reflection

When light travels from glass towards air, increasing the angle of
incidence makes the refracted ray bend further from the normal. At the
**critical angle** c the refracted ray travels along the boundary (angle of
refraction 90°). Above c, no light leaves: all of it is reflected inside,
which is **total internal reflection**. Below c there is a refracted ray
and a weak internally reflected ray together.

```
n = 1 / sin c
```

**Worked example 3.** Glass has n = 1.50. Find c, then state what happens
to rays meeting the glass–air boundary from inside at 40° and at 45°.

```
sin c = 1 / 1.50 = 0.667     c = 41.8°
40° < c : refracts out; sin (angle in air) = 1.50 × sin 40° = 0.964 → 74.6°
45° > c : total internal reflection, reflected at 45°
```

Working backwards works too: a plastic with c = 37° has n = 1 / sin 37° =
1.66. A larger refractive index means a smaller critical angle.

**Optical fibres.** Light enters one end and meets the fibre wall at angles
greater than c, so it is totally internally reflected again and again until
it emerges at the far end. In telecommunications, pulses of light carry
telephone, internet and television signals. Advantages to state: a fibre
carries far more information per second than a copper cable of similar
size, the signal is not affected by electrical interference, and fibres are
thinner and lighter. When a question names a context, tie each advantage
to it rather than listing them in general.

## Thin lenses and ray diagrams

A thin **converging** lens brings a parallel beam to a point, the
**principal focus** F. A thin **diverging** lens spreads a parallel beam so
that it appears to come from a principal focus on the incoming side. The
**principal axis** runs through the centre of the lens at right angles to
it, and the **focal length** is the distance from the lens centre to F.

Three rays are easy to draw from the top of the object:

1. Parallel to the principal axis, then through F on the far side (for a
   diverging lens, away from the axis as if from F on the near side).
2. Through the centre of the lens, undeviated.
3. Through F on the near side, then parallel to the axis.

Two are enough; the third is a check. Where the rays actually cross after
the lens, the image is **real** (formed by converging rays, and it can be
caught on a screen). If the rays spread out after the lens, extend them
backwards as dashed lines: where the dashed lines meet is a **virtual**
image, formed by diverging rays.

**Worked example 4 (scale drawing).** An object 3.0 cm tall stands 25 cm
from a converging lens of focal length 10 cm. Drawn to scale, the rays
cross about 16.7 cm beyond the lens, giving an image 2.0 cm tall that is
inverted.

```
linear magnification = image length / object length = 2.0 / 3.0 = 0.67
```

The object is beyond 2F (20 cm), so the image is real, inverted and
diminished, between F and 2F on the far side. Magnification has no unit.

**The magnifying glass.** Put the object closer to the lens than F. The
rays leave diverging, so the eye sees a virtual, upright, enlarged image on
the same side as the object. With f = 6.0 cm and a 1.0 cm object placed
4.0 cm from the lens, a scale drawing gives the image 12 cm from the lens
and 3.0 cm tall: magnification 3.0.

**The eye.** The cornea and eye lens act together as a converging system
that focuses a real, inverted image on the retina. In a **short-sighted**
eye, light from a distant object focuses in front of the retina; a
**diverging** lens spreads the rays slightly first so the focus moves back
onto the retina. In a **long-sighted** eye, light from a near object
focuses behind the retina; a **converging** lens adds convergence so the
focus moves forward onto the retina. In eye diagrams, show the rays
crossing at the correct place relative to the retina, both before and after
correction.

## Dispersion

White light is a mixture of colours. Each colour has a slightly different
speed in glass, so a prism refracts each by a different amount and white
light spreads into a spectrum. Violet is refracted most and red least.

| Order | Red → Violet |
|---|---|
| Frequency | increases |
| Wavelength | decreases |

So in order of increasing frequency: red, orange, yellow, green, blue,
indigo, violet. In order of increasing wavelength, reverse the list.

## Common mistakes

- Measuring angles from the surface instead of the normal.
- Putting the angle in glass on top in n = sin i / sin r, which gives n
  less than 1. A refractive index for glass or water is always greater
  than 1.
- Stating total internal reflection for light going from air into glass.
  It needs light travelling from the denser medium, and i greater than c.
- Drawing rays without arrows, or drawing virtual-image extensions as solid
  lines.
- Correcting short sight with a converging lens. Short sight needs a
  diverging lens; long sight needs a converging lens.
- Saying the frequency of light changes in glass. Speed and wavelength
  change; frequency does not.

## Exam technique for Cambridge command words

- **Define** (refractive index, critical angle, linear magnification):
  give the formal statement or equation in words; the symbol equation on
  its own may not earn the mark unless the symbols are named.
- **Describe an experiment**: name the apparatus (ray box, block, protractor,
  pins), say what is measured and how, and say what is plotted or compared.
- **Draw a ray diagram**: use a ruler, arrows on every ray, mark F on both
  sides, and label the image real or virtual.
- **Calculate**: write the equation, substitute with units, and give the
  angle to a sensible number of significant figures (three is usual).
- **Explain** (for example why a fibre carries light round a bend): link
  the angle at the wall to the critical angle, then name total internal
  reflection.
