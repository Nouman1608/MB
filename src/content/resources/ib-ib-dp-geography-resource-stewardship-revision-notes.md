Condensed recall for **10.3 Resource stewardship** (Unit 3: Global resource consumption and
security). For the full explanation, read the
[Global resource consumption study guide](/resources/ib-ib-dp-geography-global-resource-consumption-study-guide/).

## Definition

**Stewardship** is responsible management of resources so that they are available for the future. It
operates from individual to global scale and involves governments, businesses, international
organisations, non-governmental groups, communities and consumers.

## Perspectives on population and resources

| Perspective | Core idea | Evaluation |
|---|---|---|
| Malthus (1798) | Population tends to grow faster than food supply, so growth is checked by famine, disease or war | Did not foresee the farming and technological gains of the 19th and 20th centuries |
| Boserup (1965) | Population pressure stimulates new farming methods ("necessity is the mother of invention") | The Green Revolution supports her, but intensification can damage soils and water |
| Neo-Malthusian: *The Limits to Growth* (Club of Rome, 1972) | Continued exponential growth in population, industry and pollution would exceed the Earth's limits | Criticised for underestimating technology, but revived by concern over climate and biodiversity |
| Resource optimists (for example Julian Simon) | Human ingenuity and markets will find substitutes as resources become scarce | Price signals work poorly for unpriced resources such as the atmosphere |

**The Green Revolution** (from the 1960s), with high-yielding varieties of wheat and rice, fertiliser
and irrigation, greatly raised yields in Asia and Latin America. It is evidence for Boserup, but in
places such as Punjab it also depleted groundwater and increased fertiliser and pesticide use.

## Approaches

| Approach | Examples |
|---|---|
| Efficiency and conservation | Insulation, efficient appliances, irrigation efficiency, reducing food waste |
| Substitution and technology | Renewable energy, recycled materials, improved crop varieties |
| Circular economy | Designs out waste by reusing, repairing, remanufacturing and recycling |
| Certification and labelling | Forestry and fisheries certification |
| Regulation, pricing and incentives | Taxes, quotas, standards, subsidies, carbon pricing |
| Agreements and cooperation | International agreements on shared resources; local community management |

The **linear** model is take-make-dispose; the circular economy contrasts with it.

## Stewardship in practice

- **Sustainable Development Goals (2015):** the 17 goals include clean water and sanitation (Goal 6),
  affordable and clean energy (Goal 7) and responsible consumption and production (Goal 12), giving a
  shared framework for national targets.
- **Water efficiency:** drip irrigation, developed commercially in Israel from the 1960s, delivers
  water to plant roots and cuts losses to evaporation. Singapore recycles treated wastewater as
  NEWater.
- **Waste and the circular economy:** the European Union has adopted circular economy action plans
  (2015 and 2020); Germany's deposit return scheme for drinks containers achieves very high return
  rates; England's charge on single-use plastic bags (introduced in 2015) sharply reduced their use.
- **Food waste:** the UN Food and Agriculture Organization has estimated that around a third of food
  produced is lost or wasted, so cutting waste is one of the cheapest ways to raise food security.
- **Fisheries:** the collapse of the Grand Banks cod fishery off Newfoundland, which led Canada to
  impose a moratorium in 1992, shows what happens without effective stewardship. Certification by
  the Marine Stewardship Council rewards sustainably managed fisheries.
- **Community management:** Elinor Ostrom (Nobel economics prize, 2009) showed that local
  communities can manage shared resources such as forests, pastures and irrigation systems well,
  challenging Garrett Hardin's "tragedy of the commons" (1968) argument that shared resources are
  always overused.

## Evaluating stewardship

Ask whether the strategy is **effective, affordable, fair and enforceable**.

- **Rebound effect:** efficiency gains reduce cost and lead to more consumption, offsetting savings.
- **Equity:** lower-income countries argue that their right to develop must be respected, while
  higher-income countries are responsible for most past consumption.
- Balance competing views and use clear criteria.

The rebound effect was first described by William Stanley Jevons in 1865, who observed that more
efficient steam engines increased total coal use in Britain (the **Jevons paradox**).

## Worked example: the rebound effect (invented figures)

A household buys a car that uses 25 per cent less fuel per kilometre. Because driving is now cheaper,
it drives 10 per cent further.

- New fuel use = 0.75 x 1.10 = 0.825 of the original.
- Actual saving = 17.5 per cent, not the 25 per cent expected.
- Rebound = (25 - 17.5) / 25 = 30 per cent of the expected saving was lost.
- If driving rose by more than a third (above 1.333 times), fuel use would exceed the original:
  "backfire".

## Model paragraph (evaluate)

*"Germany's deposit return scheme is effective because the deposit gives consumers a direct incentive
to return containers, and it is enforceable because retailers must accept returns. It is less
transferable to lower-income countries without the same retail infrastructure, and it tackles drinks
packaging rather than wider overconsumption. It is therefore a strong but narrow stewardship
strategy."*

## Exam traps

- Offering stewardship ideas with no evaluation or no named stakeholders.
- Assuming efficiency always reduces total use (the rebound effect).
- Ignoring the fairness dispute between countries.
- Listing places without explaining a pattern.
- Presenting Malthus or Boserup as simply right or wrong instead of weighing evidence.
- Treating recycling as the whole circular economy; reuse and repair come higher in the hierarchy.

## Self-test

1. Define resource stewardship.
2. Contrast the circular and linear economy.
3. What is the rebound effect?
4. Give four criteria for evaluating a stewardship strategy.
5. Why is stewardship contested between countries?
6. Summarise Malthus's argument.
7. How does the Green Revolution support Boserup?
8. What did Ostrom show about common resources?
9. A household replaces its fridge with one using 40 per cent less electricity, then adds a second
   identical efficient fridge. Has total use fallen?
10. What happened to the Grand Banks cod fishery?
11. Name one Sustainable Development Goal linked to resource stewardship.

**Answers:** 1. Responsible management so resources remain available for the future. 2. Circular designs
out waste through reuse, repair and recycling; linear is take-make-dispose. 3. Efficiency lowers costs
and encourages more consumption. 4. Effective, affordable, fair and enforceable. 5. States disagree
about historic responsibility, rights to develop and who bears the costs. 6. Population grows faster
than food supply, so growth is checked by famine, disease or war. 7. Population pressure was met by
new seeds, fertiliser and irrigation that raised yields. 8. Local communities can manage shared
resources sustainably with suitable rules. 9. No: two fridges at 0.6 each use 1.2 times the original
electricity. 10. It collapsed from overfishing, and Canada imposed a moratorium in 1992. 11. For
example Goal 12, responsible consumption and production.
