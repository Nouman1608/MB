These questions cover **Aggregate demand and supply**, part of topic 1.4 The interaction of aggregate demand and supply in Component 02, Macroeconomics, of the OCR *AS Level Economics (H060)* specification, Version 3.0 (June 2026): the assumptions underlying the AD and AS models and the analysis of changes in one or both curves. All countries and data are invented. Revise first with the [aggregate demand and supply study guide](/resources/ocr-as-level-economics-h060-aggregate-demand-and-supply-ocr-aslevel-economics/) and the [aggregate demand and supply revision notes](/resources/ocr-as-level-economics-h060-aggregate-demand-and-supply-ocr-aslevel-economics-revision-notes/).

## Section A: Short questions

**1.** A rise in world energy prices shifts the SRAS curve. Which **one** of the following is the most likely outcome? **[1]**

A: The price level falls and real output rises

B: The price level rises and real output rises

C: The price level rises and real output falls

D: The price level falls and real output falls

**2.** AD and SRAS both shift to the right in the same period. Which **one** of the following must happen? **[1]**

A: The price level rises

B: The price level falls

C: Real output rises

D: Real output falls

**3.** State **two** assumptions that underlie the AD and AS models. **[2]**

**4.** Explain why a large rise in money wages may break the assumption that the AD and AS curves are independent. **[2]**

**5.** In Country Z inflation rose from 1.5% to 4.0% in a year while real GDP fell by 0.8%. Identify the curve shift that best explains this data, and give a reason. **[2]**

**6.** Explain why the same rise in AD may raise real output by more in an economy with high unemployment than in one with very low unemployment. **[3]**

## Section B: Structured and extended questions

**7.** The table shows invented schedules for Country A (real output in billions; price level as an index).

| Price level | AD1 | SRAS1 | AD2 | SRAS2 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 90 | 520 | 480 | 560 | 440 |
| 100 | 500 | 500 | 540 | 460 |
| 110 | 480 | 520 | 520 | 480 |
| 120 | 460 | 540 | 500 | 500 |

**(a)** State the initial equilibrium price level and real output, using AD1 and SRAS1. **[1]**

**(b)** AD shifts to AD2. State the new equilibrium and calculate the percentage changes in the price level and real output. **[2]**

**(c)** SRAS then shifts to SRAS2. State the final equilibrium and compare it with the initial one. **[2]**

**8.** Explain, with the aid of a diagram, the effects on the price level and real output of a large depreciation of a country's currency, considering both AD and SRAS. **[4]**

**9.** Explain **two** assumptions of the AD/AS model and why each may not hold in practice. **[4]**

**10.** A new technology raises productivity across the economy, and at the same time raises business confidence. Explain the likely effects on the price level and real output. **[4]**

**11.** Two invented economies receive the same rise in government spending.

| | Economy B | Economy C |
|---|---|---|
| Unemployment rate (%) | 9.0 | 3.5 |
| Firms reporting capacity shortages (%) | 12 | 61 |

Explain how the effects of the rise in spending are likely to differ between the two economies. **[4]**

**12.** Evaluate the usefulness of the AD/AS model for predicting the effects of a large rise in business investment. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** C [1]. SRAS shifts left, so prices rise and output falls. **[1]**

**2.** C [1]. Both shifts raise output; the price effect depends on their relative sizes. **[1]**

**3.** Any two from: aggregation into one price level and output [1]; ceteris paribus [1]; independence of the curves; sticky short-run costs; flexible long-run prices; given expectations; known capacity. **[2]**

**4.** Higher money wages raise firms' costs, shifting SRAS left [1], but they also raise households' incomes and consumption, shifting AD right, so one change moves both curves [1]. **[2]**

**5.** A leftward shift of SRAS [1]: the price level rose while output fell, and opposite movements in P and Y point to a supply-side cause [1]. **[2]**

**6.** With high unemployment there is spare capacity, so the economy is on the flat part of the AS curve [1]. Firms can raise output without bidding up costs, so most of the rise in AD goes into real output [1]. With very low unemployment the economy is near the vertical part, so most of the rise goes into higher prices [1]. **[3]**

**7. (a)** P = **100**, Y = **500** billion [1].

**(b)** AD2 = SRAS1 at P = **110**, Y = **520** [1]. The price level rises by 10% and real output by 4% [1].

**(c)** AD2 = SRAS2 at P = **120**, Y = **500** [1]. Real output is back to its initial level, but the price level is 20% higher [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* A demand rise followed by a cost rise can leave output unchanged and prices much higher; quote both equilibria.

**8.** Exports become cheaper abroad and imports dearer at home, so net exports rise and AD shifts right [1]. Imported raw materials and components cost more, so SRAS shifts left [1]. The diagram shows AD1 to AD2 and SRAS1 to SRAS2, with the price level certainly higher [1]. The effect on real output is uncertain: it rises if the AD shift is larger than the SRAS shift [1]. **[4]**

**9.** Assumption 1: aggregation into a single price level and output [1]; in practice sectors and regions differ, so an average can hide a slump in one area [1]. Assumption 2: the curves are independent [1]; in practice investment raises AD and also adds to capacity, so both curves shift [1]. Other valid assumptions: flexible long-run wages (wages may resist cuts); given expectations (confidence changes suddenly). **[4]**

**10.** Higher productivity lowers unit costs, shifting SRAS (and LRAS) right [1]. Higher business confidence raises investment, shifting AD right [1]. Real output certainly rises because both shifts increase it [1]. The price level may rise or fall, depending on whether the AD shift or the SRAS shift is larger [1]. **[4]**

**11.** Economy B has 9.0% unemployment and only 12% of firms report capacity shortages, so it has spare capacity [1]; the rise in AD is likely to raise output and employment with little inflation [1]. Economy C has 3.5% unemployment and 61% of firms short of capacity [1]; the same rise in AD is likely to go mostly into a higher price level, with a small rise in output [1]. **[4]**

**12.** Award up to 10 marks across knowledge, application, analysis and evaluation.
- Investment is a component of AD, so a rise shifts AD right, raising output and the price level in the short run [1].
- With spare capacity, most of the effect is on output; near capacity, more is on prices [1].
- Investment also adds to the capital stock, shifting LRAS right over time [1], which can lower inflationary pressure later [1].
- The model can show both effects, but its independence assumption treats them as separate shifts [1].
- It is comparative statics, so it does not show how long capacity takes to build [1].
- Aggregation hides where investment happens: a boom in one sector may create bottlenecks there [1].
- Expectations matter: if the boom is driven by optimism, it may reverse quickly [1].
- The model gives the direction of change but not reliable magnitudes, because slopes are hard to measure [1].
- Judgement: useful for showing the short-run demand effect and long-run supply effect, but its predictions depend heavily on spare capacity and timing, so it should be used alongside data [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* Recognising that investment shifts both curves at different speeds is the key evaluative step.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Shifting only AD for a depreciation or a wage rise.
- Giving a definite price effect when AD and SRAS both shift right.
- Reading the wrong pair of columns from a schedule.
- Listing assumptions without saying when they fail.
- Ignoring the data on spare capacity when comparing economies.
