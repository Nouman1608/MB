These notes condense **neurotransmission** from the biological approach (SL and HL, first assessment 2027). The learning objective has two parts: the process of neurotransmission, and how an understanding of it allows psychologists to improve health and wellness. Read the [study guide](/resources/ib-dp-psychology-2027-neurotransmission/) for detail, then try the [practice set](/resources/ib-dp-psychology-2027-neurotransmission-practice/).

## The process in five steps

1. **Action potential** reaches the axon terminal of the presynaptic neuron.
2. **Release:** vesicles release neurotransmitter into the synaptic cleft.
3. **Binding:** neurotransmitter binds to receptors on the postsynaptic neuron (lock and key).
4. **Effect:** excitatory (more likely to fire) or inhibitory (less likely to fire).
5. **Ending:** reuptake into the presynaptic neuron, or breakdown by enzymes.

## Key definitions

- **Neurotransmitter:** a chemical released by a neuron that crosses a synapse to affect another cell.
- **Synapse and synaptic cleft:** the junction between neurons and the gap across it.
- **Receptor:** a protein on the postsynaptic membrane to which a neurotransmitter binds.
- **Excitatory and inhibitory:** increasing or decreasing the chance that the next neuron fires.
- **Reuptake:** return of neurotransmitter to the presynaptic neuron.
- **Agonist:** a substance that mimics or boosts a neurotransmitter's effect.
- **Antagonist:** a substance that blocks a receptor.
- **SSRI:** selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor.
- **Precursor:** a substance from which a neurotransmitter is made.
- **Hormone:** a chemical messenger carried in the blood (different from a neurotransmitter).

## Main neurotransmitters

| Neurotransmitter | Linked to | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Serotonin | Mood, sleep, appetite | Target of SSRIs |
| Dopamine | Reward, motivation, movement | Loss in Parkinson's; boosted by many addictive drugs |
| GABA | Reduces neural activity | Main inhibitory transmitter |
| Glutamate | Learning and memory | Main excitatory transmitter |
| Acetylcholine | Muscles, attention, memory | Nicotine acts on its receptors |
| Noradrenaline | Alertness and arousal | Stress response |

Do not write "the happiness chemical". Each system has many roles.

## How substances act

| Action | Effect | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Agonist | Boosts or mimics | Nicotine |
| Antagonist | Blocks a receptor | Some antipsychotic drugs |
| Reuptake inhibitor | Keeps the transmitter in the cleft longer | SSRIs; cocaine (dopamine) |
| Precursor | Raises production | L-DOPA to dopamine |
| Enhancer of inhibition | Strengthens GABA's effect | Benzodiazepines |

## Improving health and wellness: three uses

1. **Treatment:** drugs designed to act on a specific step.
2. **Explanation:** guides research on disorders and individual differences in drug response.
3. **Prevention and advice:** how substances affect the reward system.

### Evidence to know

- **SSRIs and depression:** more serotonin in the cleft quickly, mood change in weeks. A large review (Moncrieff et al., 2022) found no consistent evidence for low serotonin as the cause. Meta-analyses (Cipriani et al., 2018) found antidepressants more effective than placebo; the average benefit is modest, and larger for severe depression (Kirsch et al., 2008).
- **Parkinson's disease:** dopamine neuron loss; L-DOPA raises dopamine and reduces movement symptoms; benefits can decline and side effects occur.
- **Reward (Olds and Milner, 1954):** rats pressed a lever for brain stimulation, linked to reward areas rich in dopamine; many addictive drugs raise dopamine activity. Animal evidence raises ethical and generalisation issues.

## Evaluation

- **Strengths:** a testable mechanism; links to effective treatments; controlled drug trials.
- **Limitations:** indirect measurement in living humans; animal research; reverse inference (a drug working does not prove the cause); reductionism; networks of interacting transmitters; side effects.
- **Gene-environment example:** Caspi et al. (2003) proposed an interaction between a serotonin transporter gene and stress; later large analyses did not consistently replicate it, which shows why replication matters.

## Concept links to prepare

- **Causality:** experiments and trials versus correlation; reverse inference.
- **Measurement:** indirect measures of neurotransmitter activity.
- **Perspective:** biological reductionism versus cognitive and sociocultural accounts.
- **Responsibility:** animal research, medication use, informed consent.
- **Change:** drug treatments and how long benefits last.
- **Bias:** publication bias and placebo effects in drug trials.

## A model Section A answer

**Question:** Describe the process of neurotransmission. (4 marks, written for practice.)

**Strong answer:** When an electrical impulse reaches the end of a presynaptic neuron, vesicles release neurotransmitter into the synaptic cleft. The molecules cross the gap and bind to matching receptors on the postsynaptic neuron, which makes that neuron more or less likely to fire, depending on whether the effect is excitatory or inhibitory. The signal ends when the neurotransmitter is taken back into the presynaptic neuron (reuptake) or broken down by enzymes. For example, SSRIs block reuptake of serotonin, so more remains in the cleft.

## A model Section B approach

Scenario: a patient takes a medicine that blocks dopamine reuptake and reports higher energy and restlessness.
1. Name the step: reuptake blocked, so dopamine stays longer in the cleft.
2. Apply: more stimulation of postsynaptic dopamine receptors, matching increased energy.
3. Add a qualification: effects depend on dose, and other systems are affected.

## Common mistakes

- Saying that neurotransmitters travel along neurons rather than across the cleft.
- Mixing up hormones and neurotransmitters.
- Treating a drug effect as proof of cause.
- Describing the process and omitting the health and wellness application.
- Naming a chemical as "the" cause of a behaviour.

## Self-test

1. Put these in order: binding, release, action potential, reuptake.
2. What is the difference between an agonist and an antagonist?
3. How do SSRIs act?
4. Why does L-DOPA help in Parkinson's disease?
5. Why does a drug's effect not prove that a transmitter caused a disorder?
6. Give one ethical issue in neurotransmitter research.
7. Why must neurotransmission be studied indirectly in living humans?

**Answers:** 1. Action potential, release, binding, reuptake. 2. An agonist mimics or boosts a transmitter's effect; an antagonist blocks the receptor. 3. They block serotonin reuptake, so more serotonin stays in the cleft. 4. It is a precursor converted to dopamine, which makes up for the loss of dopamine neurons. 5. A drug may work through other routes (or placebo effects), so the original cause could be different. 6. Any one: animal welfare in experiments; informed consent and side effects for patients. 7. Sampling the brain directly is invasive, so researchers use scans, drug effects or fluid samples as indirect measures.
