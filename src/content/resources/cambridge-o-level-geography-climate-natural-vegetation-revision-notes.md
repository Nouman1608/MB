## Two climates side by side

| | Equatorial | Hot desert |
|---|---|---|
| Location | Within about 5–10° of the Equator (Amazon, Congo Basin) | About 15–30° N and S, near the Tropics (Sahara, Namib, Atacama) |
| Temperature | 26–28 °C every month | Very hot summers, cooler winters |
| Annual range | Small, about 1–3 °C | Larger |
| Diurnal range | Small | Large: hot days, cold nights |
| Rainfall | Over 2,000 mm, every month | Under 250 mm, unreliable |
| Pressure | Low: air rises | High: air sinks |
| Main rain type | Convection, afternoon storms | Rare, short storms |

## Factors: one line each

- **Latitude**: high Sun angle at the Equator all year → intense heating.
- **Pressure**: rising air at the Equator (low) → cloud and rain;
  sinking air at the Tropics (high) → clear, dry skies.
- **Winds**: trade winds crossing land reach deserts dry.
- **Distance from the sea**: continental interiors lack moisture.
- **Ocean currents**: cold currents (Benguela by the Namib, Humboldt by
  the Atacama) cool the air → fog, not rain.
- **Altitude**: roughly 6 °C cooler per 1,000 m.
- **Relief**: moist air forced up → relief rain on windward slopes;
  rain shadow on the leeward side.

## Worked example: describing a climate graph

*A climate graph for an equatorial station shows monthly means between 26 °C and 28 °C and rain
in every month, from 120 mm to 300 mm. Describe the climate.* Model answer: "Temperatures are high
all year, between 26 °C and 28 °C, so the annual range is small, only 2 °C. Rainfall is heavy in
every month, from 120 mm in the driest month to 300 mm in the wettest, so there is no dry season."

- Describe **temperature** (maximum, minimum, range) and **rainfall** (total, wettest and driest
  months, any dry season) separately.
- Quote figures with units; do the arithmetic for the range.
- Leave out reasons unless the question says "explain".

## Model explanation: why hot deserts are dry

"Hot deserts lie near the Tropics, where air that rose at the Equator sinks. Sinking air warms
and creates high pressure, so clouds cannot form and skies are clear. The trade winds blow from
the land, so they carry little moisture. On some west coasts, cold ocean currents cool the air
from below, so it forms fog rather than rain."

## Ecosystems

**Tropical rainforest**

- Layers: emergents → canopy → understorey → forest floor.
- Adaptations: drip tips, buttress roots, lianas, epiphytes, thin bark.
- Soil: deep, red, nutrient-poor; leaching; nutrients held in plants;
  rapid decomposition and recycling.
- Animals: very high biodiversity, most life in the canopy.

**Hot desert**

- Adaptations: long tap roots or wide shallow roots; small waxy leaves
  or spines; water-storing stems; ephemerals with short life cycles.
- Soil: thin, sandy or stony, low organic matter, salts near surface.
- Animals: nocturnal, burrowing, heat-losing ears (fennec fox), camel.

## Deforestation (Amazon)

**Causes**: cattle ranching (largest), soya farming, logging, mining
(Carajás), roads (Trans-Amazonian Highway), HEP dams (Belo Monte).

**Effects chain**: canopy removed → rain hits bare soil → erosion and
leaching → infertile soil → river silting and flooding; plus habitat
and species loss, less transpiration, carbon dioxide released,
indigenous land lost. Positives to weigh: jobs, exports, roads.

**Pakistan link**: the **Thar** (Sindh) and **Cholistan** (Punjab) deserts lie on the edge of
the hot desert belt. Summers are extremely hot (Jacobabad is one of the hottest towns in Asia),
and rain is low and unreliable, falling mainly in short monsoon bursts. Plants such as acacias
have deep roots and small leaves; people and their livestock depend on wells and ponds that
collect monsoon rain.

## Why climate and vegetation are linked

- High temperatures and rain all year allow continuous growth in the rainforest, giving tall,
  dense, evergreen forest with many layers and species.
- In the desert, lack of water is the main limit, so plants are spaced out, low and adapted to
  store or find water.
- Soils reflect both: heavy rain leaches rainforest soils; low rain and high evaporation draw
  salts up in desert soils.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Annual temperature range | Hottest month mean minus coolest month mean |
| Diurnal range | Difference between day and night temperatures |
| Convection rainfall | Heated air rises, cools and condenses into rain |
| Relief rainfall | Air forced over high ground cools and condenses |
| Leaching | Nutrients washed down through the soil by rain |
| Nutrient cycling | Movement of nutrients between soil, plants and litter |
| Xerophyte | A plant adapted to dry conditions |
| Ephemeral | A plant that completes its life cycle quickly after rain |
| Emergent | Very tall rainforest tree that rises above the canopy |
| Canopy | Continuous upper layer of tree crowns in a forest |
| Drip tip | Pointed leaf end that sheds heavy rain quickly |
| Epiphyte | Plant that grows on another plant to reach light, without harming it |
| Biodiversity | The variety of plant and animal species in an area |

## Exam traps

- Giving desert temperatures as "hot all year" without noting cooler winters and cold nights.
- Describing an adaptation without explaining how it helps the plant survive.
- Explaining climate when the question asks you to describe a graph.
- Deforestation effects listed with no chain from cause to consequence.
- Forgetting to give units (°C, mm) with figures from a climate graph.

## Self-test

1. Name the two climates and the two ecosystems in 2.5.
2. Why is the annual temperature range small at the Equator?
3. Name the cold current off the Namib Desert.
4. Explain how buttress roots help rainforest trees.
5. Why are rainforest soils infertile once the trees are cleared?
6. Give two reasons why hot deserts have a large diurnal range.
7. Name the largest single cause of deforestation in the Brazilian
   Amazon.
8. A station's hottest month is 34 °C and its coolest 14 °C. Calculate
   the annual range.
9. Explain how a drip tip helps a rainforest plant.
10. Why does sinking air lead to dry conditions?
11. Give one adaptation of a desert plant and explain how it helps.

**Answers:** 1. Equatorial and hot desert climates; tropical rainforest
and hot desert ecosystems. 2. The Sun is high in the sky all year and
day length hardly changes, so heating is similar in every month.
3. The Benguela Current. 4. They support very tall trees whose roots
are shallow. 5. Most nutrients were stored in the vegetation; once it
is removed, heavy rain erodes and leaches the soil and no new leaf
litter replaces the nutrients. 6. Clear skies allow strong heating by
day and rapid heat loss at night; there is little moisture or cloud
to trap heat. 7. Cattle ranching. 8. 34 − 14 = **20 °C**. 9. It sheds
heavy rain quickly, so the leaf does not stay wet and heavy, which stops
fungi and algae growing on it and rotting the leaf. 10. Sinking air warms and
creates high pressure, so clouds and rain cannot form. 11. For example,
long tap roots reach deep groundwater; small waxy leaves or spines
reduce water loss by transpiration; fleshy stems store water.
