**How to use this set.** In Paper 1 this subtopic is Question 2, which every candidate answers: an
essay question worth 14 marks, normally a knowledge part (a) worth 10 and an understanding part (b)
worth 4. Section A builds the factual recall you need; Section B gives full exam-style questions.

## Section A — Short questions

**1.** State **three** facts about the first revelation of the Qur'an. **[3]**

**2.** Explain **two** reasons why the Qur'an was revealed gradually rather than all at once. **[4]**

**3.** Give **two** differences between the suras revealed in Makka and those revealed in Madina.
**[4]**

**4.** Describe **two** ways the Qur'an was preserved during the lifetime of the Prophet (pbuh). **[4]**

**5.** Why did Zayd ibn Thabit (RA) find the task of compiling the Qur'an so difficult, and how did he make
sure he included only genuine material? **[3]**

**6.** Explain the difference between the work of Abu Bakr (RA) and the work of Uthman (RA) on the Qur'an.
**[4]**

**7.** Using **one** example, explain how qiyas depends on the Qur'an. **[2]**

## Section B — Exam-style questions

**8. (a)** Describe the main events of the first revelation and the way revelation continued until
632. **[10]**
**(b)** Explain why the Qur'an's description of itself as guidance is important for Muslims today.
**[4]**

**9. (a)** Write an account of the compilation of the Qur'an during the caliphate of Abu Bakr (RA). **[10]**
**(b)** "The Qur'an would have been lost without Abu Bakr (RA)." Do you agree? Give reasons. **[4]**

**10. (a)** Describe how the Qur'an is used, alongside the Hadith, ijma' and qiyas, in Islamic legal
thinking. **[10]**
**(b)** Explain why the Qur'an must always come first among the sources of law. **[4]**

**11. (a)** Explain why the Qur'an is regarded as the basis of all thought and action in Islam, giving
examples from worship and daily life. **[10]**
**(b)** How can a young Muslim today make the Qur'an part of daily life? **[4]**

## Answers

**How Section B is marked.** Cambridge marks by levels of response: part (a) Level 4 = 8–10,
Level 3 = 5–7, Level 2 = 3–4, Level 1 = 1–2; part (b) Level 2 = 3–4, Level 1 = 1–2. Below, each
developed point in (a) earns [2] up to the tariff of 10, and each reasoned point in (b) earns [2] up
to 4. Other accurate points of the same quality earn the same credit.

**1.** Any three: it came in 610 [1]; in the month of Ramadan [1]; in the Cave of Hira on Mount Nur
[1]; brought by the angel Jibril [1]; the first words were Sura 96.1–5, "Read! in the name of your
Lord" [1]. Maximum 3.

**2.** It strengthened the Prophet's (pbuh) heart through constant contact with God during years of
opposition, as Sura 25.32 says [2]. It allowed the Companions to memorise, understand and act on each
passage, and allowed laws such as the ban on wine to be introduced step by step [2].

**3.** Makkan suras are generally shorter, Madinan suras generally longer [2]. Makkan suras mainly call
people to belief in one God, prophethood and the Day of Judgement, while Madinan suras give detailed
laws for the community, such as rules of inheritance, family life and war [2].

**4.** Companions memorised each passage as it was revealed, and many became huffaz, reciting it in
prayer so it was constantly repeated [2]. Scribes such as Zayd ibn Thabit (RA) wrote it down on materials
like palm stalks, leather and bones, and the Prophet (pbuh) told them where each passage belonged [2].

**5.** It was a great responsibility, since an error would affect the word of God; he said moving a
mountain would have been easier [1]. He checked every written piece against the memories of huffaz
[1], and accepted written material only when witnesses confirmed it had been written in the Prophet's (pbuh)
presence [1].

**6.** Abu Bakr (RA) had the scattered written and memorised Qur'an **collected into one copy** after the
huffaz were killed at Yamama [2]. Uthman (RA) had that copy **reproduced as standard copies** sent to the
main cities, ending disputes over recitation, and other versions were destroyed [2].

**7.** Qiyas can only extend a ruling the Qur'an (or Sunna) already gives: wine is forbidden in Sura
5.90 because it intoxicates [1], so scholars apply the same ruling to other intoxicating drugs that
share that cause [1].

**8. (a)** Credit the best five, [2] each:
- The Prophet (pbuh) used to retreat to the Cave of Hira to reflect; in Ramadan 610, at about forty,
  Jibril appeared and commanded him to read [2].
- He said he could not read; the angel pressed him three times and recited Sura 96.1–5 [2].
- Shaken, he returned to Khadija (RA), who comforted him and took him to Waraqa ibn Nawfal, who recognised
  the angel as the one sent to Musa [2].
- After a pause, revelation resumed with Sura 74, "arise and warn", beginning his public mission [2].
- Revelation then came for about twenty-three years in Makka and Madina, in different ways: like the
  ringing of a bell, Jibril in human form, true dreams [2].
- It was gradual, "to strengthen your heart" (Sura 25.32), and Jibril reviewed it with him each
  Ramadan, twice in the final year [2].
**(b)** Credit the best two, [2] each:
- The Qur'an calls itself "guidance sure, without doubt" (Sura 2.2), so Muslims turn to it for
  decisions about honesty, family and work rather than following fashion or pressure [2].
- Because it is God's unchanged word, its guidance does not go out of date, so Muslims can apply its
  principles to new issues such as online behaviour or the environment [2].
- Its guidance gives certainty and comfort in hardship, since believers trust it comes from God who
  knows all things [2].

**9. (a)** Credit the best five, [2] each:
- After the Prophet's (pbuh) death, Abu Bakr (RA) fought the Ridda wars; at the Battle of Yamama against
  Musaylima many huffaz were killed [2].
- Umar (RA) feared that if more huffaz died, parts of the Qur'an would be lost, and urged Abu Bakr (RA) to
  collect it [2].
- Abu Bakr (RA) at first hesitated to do what the Prophet (pbuh) had not done, but became convinced it was
  necessary [2].
- He chose Zayd ibn Thabit (RA), a young and trusted hafiz who had written revelation for the Prophet (pbuh) [2].
- Zayd gathered the writings on palm stalks, leather and stones and checked them against memory,
  accepting writing only with witnesses that it was written before the Prophet (pbuh) [2].
- The last verses of Sura 9 were found in writing only with Abu Khuzayma al-Ansari; the completed
  sheets were kept by Abu Bakr (RA), then Umar (RA), then Hafsa (RA) [2].
**(b)** A judgement [1] supported by reasons [3]. For example: I partly agree [1]. Without the
collection, the deaths of more huffaz could have created gaps, so Abu Bakr's (RA) decision protected the
text [1]. However, the Qur'an was still memorised by many Companions and recited daily [1], and
Muslims believe God promised to guard it (Sura 15.9), so Abu Bakr (RA) was the means God used rather than
the only reason it survived [1]. An equally reasoned answer reaching a different judgement earns the
same credit.

**10. (a)** Credit the best five, [2] each:
- The Qur'an is the first and highest source; every ruling must agree with it [2].
- It states many laws directly, for example prayer, zakat, fasting and inheritance shares [2].
- The Hadith explain and detail its commands, such as the number of units in each prayer and the
  rates of zakat, and cover matters it does not mention in detail [2].
- Ijma' is the agreement of qualified scholars on a ruling based on the Qur'an and Sunna, such as the
  Companions' agreement to compile the Qur'an [2].
- Qiyas applies an existing ruling to a new case with the same cause, such as extending the ban on
  wine to intoxicating drugs [2].
- The Hadith of Mu'adh ibn Jabal shows the order: the Book of God, then the Sunna, then reasoning [2].
**(b)** Credit the best two, [2] each:
- Muslims believe it is God's own word, so no human opinion or consensus can override it [2].
- The other sources depend on it: the Sunna explains it, and ijma' and qiyas must rest on it, so
  putting it first keeps the law consistent [2].
- It protects the law from change by rulers or fashion, since its text is fixed and preserved [2].

**11. (a)** Credit the best five, [2] each:
- Muslims believe the Qur'an is God's unchanged word, protected by Him (Sura 15.9), so it has final
  authority over belief and action [2].
- It teaches the core beliefs: tawhid, angels, books, prophets, the Day of Judgement [2].
- It commands worship; Sura 1 is recited in every unit of prayer, and many recite the whole Qur'an
  during Ramadan [2].
- It gives rules for family life, such as marriage, divorce and inheritance [2].
- It sets moral and economic standards, such as honesty in trade and the ban on interest [2].
- It is the first source of law, so scholars judge new issues by it [2].
**(b)** Credit the best two, [2] each:
- Reading or listening to a short passage each day with its meaning, and reflecting on how it applies
  to the day's choices [2].
- Memorising suras to recite in prayer, which builds a lasting personal link with the text [2].
- Acting on its teachings, for example honesty in exams and kindness to parents, so it shapes
  behaviour, not just knowledge [2].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Answering Question 9 with Uthman's (RA) standardisation as well, which earns nothing for that question.
- Writing about "the Qur'an's importance" in part (a) of a narrative question.
- Giving four one-line reasons in part (b) instead of two explained ones.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is Question 2 always on the same part of this topic?**
No. It can be set on any area in the syllabus list: revelation, compilation, themes, legal use or
significance, so prepare all of them.

**Can I quote the Qur'an in English?**
Yes. Quoting a short phrase with its sura and verse number supports your point.
