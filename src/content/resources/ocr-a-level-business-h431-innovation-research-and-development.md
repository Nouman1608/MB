## Key terms

- **Invention** is the creation of a new idea or device. **Innovation** is the commercial use of a new idea: bringing a new or improved product, service or process to market. Many inventions never become innovations because no one finds a way to sell or make them profitably.
- **Research** is the search for new knowledge. **Pure research** pursues knowledge with no product in mind; **applied research** aims at a specific commercial use.
- **Development** turns research findings into a working product or process, through designs, prototypes and tests.
- **Research and development (R&D)** is the combined activity. It is often measured by **R&D intensity**: R&D spending as a percentage of sales. If a firm spends £0.6 million a year on R&D and has sales of £12 million, its intensity is 0.6 ÷ 12 × 100 = **5%**.

## Why businesses innovate

Within a business, innovation can:

- create a **competitive advantage** (a better or cheaper product that rivals cannot yet copy);
- **replace products** that are in decline, extending the business's product portfolio;
- **cut costs** through better processes;
- meet **changing customer needs** or fashion;
- respond to **laws or customer concern**, such as lower emissions;
- support a **premium price** and brand.

Within an industry, firms innovate because competitors do. In technology industries, standing still means falling behind. Innovation may also be needed to meet industry standards or to remain compatible with new technology. In some industries, such as pharmaceuticals, the cost of failure is so high that firms rely on patents to protect their returns for a period.

## Product and process innovation

- **Product innovation** is a new or improved product or service: a lighter laptop, a new medicine, a mobile banking app.
- **Process innovation** is a new or improved way of making or delivering a product: robots on an assembly line, a new recipe that cuts waste, a self-service checkout.

The two are linked. A new product often needs a new process, and a new process may allow a better product. They affect different parts of the business: product innovation changes **what** the customer buys and the revenue the business earns; process innovation changes **how** it is made and mainly affects costs, quality and speed.

## The R&D cycle

The process from idea to market follows broad stages, although real projects loop back.

1. **Idea generation:** from customers, staff, competitors, suppliers or universities.
2. **Research and screening:** testing whether the idea is technically possible and wanted by customers; weak ideas are dropped.
3. **Development:** designing and building prototypes.
4. **Testing:** laboratory and market tests (see trial marketing in the [identifying customer needs study guide](/resources/ocr-a-level-business-h431-identifying-customer-needs/)).
5. **Protection:** patents, copyrights or trademarks to prevent copying.
6. **Commercialisation:** production and launch.
7. **Review:** monitoring sales and feedback, which feeds into the next cycle.

Each stage costs money, and fewer ideas survive at each stage. This explains why R&D is risky.

### Worked example 1: valuing a risky R&D project

Cobalt Devices plans to develop a new fitness tracker. R&D will cost **£1.2 million** over two years. Managers estimate a **40%** chance of success. If it succeeds, the product will earn a contribution of **£900,000** a year for **5** years. If it fails, the income is zero.

1. Benefit if successful = £900,000 × 5 = **£4.5 million**.
2. Expected benefit = 0.40 × £4.5m = **£1.8 million**.
3. Expected net value = £1.8m − £1.2m = **+£0.6 million**.
4. Payback if successful = £1.2m ÷ £0.9m = **1.33 years** after launch.

**Sensitivity.** The result depends on the probability. The break-even probability is £1.2m ÷ £4.5m = **26.7%**. If the chance of success is only 25%, the expected benefit is 0.25 × £4.5m = £1.125m, and the expected net value is £1.125m − £1.2m = **−£0.075m**. A small fall in confidence turns a good project into a loss.

**Evaluation.** The expected value is positive but hides the fact that there is a 60% chance of losing the whole £1.2 million. If Cobalt has strong cash reserves it can afford the risk; if £1.2 million is a large part of its capital, a failure could endanger the business. Cobalt could reduce risk by testing the idea early, sharing the cost with a partner, or developing in stages with a review at each. The estimates of probability and sales are uncertain, so the decision also needs judgement about the market and the firm's capacity.

### Worked example 2: a process innovation

Cobalt considers a new assembly robot that costs **£250,000**. It makes 50,000 devices a year.

- Labour cost per device falls from **£6.00** to **£4.20**, a saving of £1.80 each: 50,000 × £1.80 = **£90,000** a year.
- The defect rate falls from 4% to 1.5%. A defective device costs £12 to scrap or rework. Fewer defects = 50,000 × 0.025 = 1,250 devices, saving 1,250 × £12 = **£15,000** a year.
- Total yearly saving = £90,000 + £15,000 = **£105,000**.
- Payback = £250,000 ÷ £105,000 = **2.38 years**.

Payback is under two and a half years, which is attractive if the robot lasts five years or more. Against this: the robot reduces flexibility if the product changes, staff may fear job losses, and the saving depends on volume (at 25,000 devices a year the saving would be half, and payback nearly five years). Process innovation is therefore most valuable where volumes are high and stable.

## Advantages and disadvantages

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Competitive advantage and possible first-mover benefits | High cost, and results are uncertain |
| Premium prices and higher margins on new products | Long time before any return; cash outflow first |
| Lower costs and higher quality from process innovation | Failure wastes money; rivals may copy quickly |
| Extends the product portfolio and replaces declining products | Needs specialist staff and equipment |
| Strengthens the brand and attracts talent | Possible disruption to staff and existing production |
| Patents can protect profits | Patent protection is limited in time and expensive to enforce |

## Importance

- **To the business:** it supports growth, survival in technology-led markets and profit. A business that does not innovate risks decline as products age.
- **To consumers:** better, safer, cheaper or more convenient products, and more choice. But new products may initially cost more, and some marketing may exaggerate benefits.
- **To other stakeholders:** employees gain skills and secure jobs (or lose jobs when processes are automated); shareholders gain if innovation raises profit but bear the risk of failure; suppliers may receive new orders or need to change specifications; communities may benefit from skilled jobs and cleaner processes.
- **To the economy:** innovation raises productivity and living standards, creates new industries and exports, and can attract investment. It can also cause structural unemployment where old industries decline.

## Common mistakes

- Treating invention and innovation as the same.
- Describing R&D only as "making new products", forgetting process innovation.
- Listing advantages without mentioning risk and cost.
- Forgetting that R&D results are uncertain: use probability and payback where figures are given.
- Ignoring the effect of innovation on employees.

## Exam technique

For "explain", define and give an example. For "evaluate", compare the cost and risk with the return, use figures if given, consider the size and financial strength of the business, and give a judgement.
