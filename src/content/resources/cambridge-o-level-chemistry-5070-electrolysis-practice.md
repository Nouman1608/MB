## Section A

**1.** Define electrolysis. **[2]**

**2.** A student passes a current through molten zinc bromide. Graphite rod X is connected to the positive terminal of the power supply and graphite rod Y to the negative terminal. Identify the anode, the cathode and the electrolyte. **[3]**

**3.** Name the product formed at each electrode when molten magnesium chloride is electrolysed. **[2]**

**4.** Explain why solid lead(II) bromide must be melted before it can be electrolysed. **[1]**

**5.** Construct the half-equations for the reactions at the cathode and at the anode when molten sodium iodide is electrolysed. **[2]**

**6.** State **two** reasons why metal objects are electroplated. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** Describe the transfer of charge during the electrolysis of molten lead(II) bromide. Refer to the external circuit, the electrolyte and the electrodes. **[4]**

**8.** Concentrated aqueous sodium chloride is electrolysed using inert electrodes.

**(a)** Name the product at the cathode and the product at the anode.

**(b)** Describe a test for the gas formed at the anode, giving the result.

**(c)** The solution becomes alkaline during the electrolysis. Explain why. **[6]**

**9.** Dilute sulfuric acid is electrolysed using platinum electrodes.

**(a)** Name the gas formed at each electrode.

**(b)** Construct the half-equation for the reaction at each electrode.

**(c)** The volume of gas collected at the cathode is twice the volume collected at the anode. Use your half-equations to explain why. **[6]**

**10.** Aqueous copper(II) sulfate is electrolysed twice: first with carbon electrodes, then with copper electrodes. Compare what is observed in the two experiments. **[5]**

**11.** Predict the product at the cathode and at the anode for the electrolysis of each of these aqueous solutions, using inert electrodes.

**(a)** concentrated potassium bromide

**(b)** silver nitrate

**(c)** sodium sulfate **[6]**

**12.** An iron nail is to be electroplated with copper. Describe how this is done, and construct the half-equation for the reaction at the nail. **[4]**

**13.** During the electrolysis of molten potassium iodide, the half-equation at the anode is 2I⁻ → I₂ + 2e⁻.

**(a)** Explain why this reaction is described as oxidation.

**(b)** State the half-equation at the cathode and explain why it is reduction. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** The decomposition of an ionic compound [1] when molten or in aqueous solution by the passage of an electric current [1].

**2.** Anode: rod **X** (positive) [1]. Cathode: rod **Y** (negative) [1]. Electrolyte: **molten zinc bromide** [1].

**3.** Cathode: **magnesium** [1]. Anode: **chlorine** [1].

**4.** In the solid the ions are in fixed positions and cannot move; melting lets the ions move and carry the current [1].

**5.** Cathode: Na⁺ + e⁻ → Na [1]. Anode: 2I⁻ → I₂ + 2e⁻ [1].

**6.** To improve appearance [1]; to improve resistance to corrosion [1].

**7.** Electrons flow through the external circuit (wires) from the anode to the cathode [1]. In the electrolyte, Pb²⁺ ions move towards the cathode and Br⁻ ions move towards the anode [1]. At the cathode, Pb²⁺ ions gain electrons to form lead [1]. At the anode, Br⁻ ions lose electrons to form bromine [1].

**8. (a)** Cathode: **hydrogen** [1]. Anode: **chlorine** [1].

**(b)** Damp (blue) litmus paper [1] is bleached / turns white [1].

**(c)** H⁺ ions (from water) are discharged at the cathode as hydrogen, while Na⁺ ions stay in solution [1]; OH⁻ ions are left behind with the Na⁺ ions, forming sodium hydroxide [1].

**9. (a)** Cathode: **hydrogen** [1]. Anode: **oxygen** [1].

**(b)** Cathode: 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂ [1]. Anode: 4OH⁻ → O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻ [1].

**(c)** The same number of electrons passes through each electrode; 4 electrons form one O₂ molecule at the anode but two H₂ molecules at the cathode [1]. Equal amounts of gas occupy equal volumes, so the volume of hydrogen is twice that of oxygen [1].

**10.** Carbon electrodes: pink-brown copper forms on the cathode [1]; bubbles of gas (oxygen) form at the anode [1]; the blue colour of the solution fades [1]. Copper electrodes: copper still forms on the cathode, but the anode dissolves / gets thinner and no gas is seen [1]; the blue colour stays the same [1].

**11. (a)** Cathode: **hydrogen** [1]. Anode: **bromine** [1].

**(b)** Cathode: **silver** [1]. Anode: **oxygen** [1].

**(c)** Cathode: **hydrogen** [1]. Anode: **oxygen** [1].

**12.** The nail is made the cathode (connected to the negative terminal) [1]. The anode is a piece of pure copper [1]. The electrolyte is an aqueous copper salt such as copper(II) sulfate [1]. At the nail: Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu [1].

**13. (a)** Iodide ions lose electrons, and loss of electrons is oxidation [1].

**(b)** K⁺ + e⁻ → K [1]; potassium ions gain electrons, and gain of electrons is reduction [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Question 1: leaving out "ionic compound" or "molten or in aqueous solution". Both are part of the definition.
- Question 4: saying "the electrons cannot move". In ionic compounds the charge carriers are ions.
- Question 7: describing electrons moving through the molten electrolyte. This contradicts the mechanism and loses the mark.
- Question 8(b): writing "turns litmus red". Chlorine bleaches damp litmus; it may turn red briefly first, but the bleaching is the result credited.
- Question 9(c): stating "water is H₂O, so there are twice as many hydrogen atoms". The explanation must use electrons from the half-equations.
- Question 10: giving products without saying what is seen. A comparison needs both experiments described.
- Question 11(a): writing potassium at the cathode. Potassium is more reactive than hydrogen, so it stays in solution as K⁺.
- Question 12: making the nail the anode. Metal ions gain electrons at the cathode, so the object must be the cathode.
- Question 13: writing "iodine loses electrons". It is the iodide **ions** that lose electrons; the iodine molecule is the product.
