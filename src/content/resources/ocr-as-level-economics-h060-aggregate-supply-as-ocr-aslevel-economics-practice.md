Revise first with the [aggregate supply study guide](/resources/ocr-as-level-economics-h060-aggregate-supply-as-ocr-aslevel-economics/) and the [aggregate supply revision notes](/resources/ocr-as-level-economics-h060-aggregate-supply-as-ocr-aslevel-economics-revision-notes/).

## Section A: Short questions

**1.** Which **one** of the following would shift the short-run aggregate supply curve to the right? **[1]**

A: A rise in VAT

B: A fall in world commodity prices

C: A rise in money wages with no change in productivity

D: A depreciation of the exchange rate

**2.** Which **one** of the following is most likely to shift the long-run aggregate supply curve to the right? **[1]**

A: A rise in consumer confidence

B: A cut in interest rates that raises consumption

C: A rise in net investment in machinery

D: A rise in the general price level

**3.** Hourly wages in Country W rise by 2.5% and output per hour rises by 3.5%. Calculate the percentage change in unit labour costs. **[2]**

**4.** Explain why the SRAS curve slopes upwards. **[3]**

**5.** State **two** reasons why the neo-classical LRAS curve is vertical. **[2]**

**6.** Explain why the Keynesian LRAS curve is horizontal at low levels of output. **[2]**

## Section B: Structured and extended questions

**7.** The table shows invented data for Country X.

| Year | Hourly wage (cu) | Output per hour (units) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 20.00 | 40 |
| 2 | 21.20 | 41 |

**(a)** Calculate unit labour cost in each year. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the percentage change in unit labour cost and state the likely direction of the shift in SRAS. **[2]**

**8.** A firm's costs per unit are labour 45, imported components 35 and energy 20. The domestic currency depreciates, raising the price of imported components by 10%, and energy prices fall by 15%.

**(a)** Calculate the net percentage change in the firm's cost per unit. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain what would happen to SRAS if all firms had a similar cost structure. **[2]**

**9.** Explain, with the aid of a diagram, how an increase in the number of people of working age who join the labour force affects long-run aggregate supply. **[4]**

**10.** Explain **two** ways in which improvements in education and training could shift LRAS to the right. **[4]**

**11.** Explain why a permanent improvement in technology is likely to shift both SRAS and LRAS, while a temporary rise in oil prices shifts only SRAS. **[4]**

**12.** Evaluate whether the Keynesian or the neo-classical view of long-run aggregate supply is more useful for analysing an economy in recession. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** B [1]. Cheaper commodities lower firms' costs. **[1]**

**2.** C [1]. Net investment adds to the capital stock and productive capacity. **[1]**

**3.** 1.025 / 1.035 - 1 [1] = **-0.97%** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Wages rose, but unit labour costs fell because productivity grew faster.

**4.** In the short run, money wages and some other costs are fixed by contracts [1]. A higher price level raises the revenue from each unit relative to its cost, so expanding output becomes profitable [1]. Costs per unit also rise as firms approach capacity, so more is supplied only at a higher price level [1]. **[3]**

**5.** Wages and prices are fully flexible in the long run [1]; output is determined by the quantity and quality of factors of production and technology, not by the price level [1]. **[2]**

**6.** At low output there is widespread spare capacity, with unemployed workers and idle machines [1], so firms can raise output without bidding up wages or input prices, and the price level does not rise [1]. **[2]**

**7. (a)** Year 1: 20.00 / 40 = **0.50** [1]. Year 2: 21.20 / 41 = **0.517** [1].

**(b)** 0.517 / 0.50 - 1 = **+3.4%** [1]. Unit labour costs rise, so SRAS shifts **left** [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Wages rose 6% and productivity 2.5%; the rise in unit labour cost is roughly the gap between them.

**8. (a)** Components: 35 x 0.10 = +3.5 [1]. Energy: 20 x 0.15 = -3.0. Net change = +0.5 on 100, a rise of **0.5%** [1].

**(b)** Costs rise slightly, so SRAS shifts slightly to the left [1]; the effect is small because the energy price fall offsets most of the import cost rise [1]. **[4]**

**9.** More people in the labour force raises the quantity of labour available [1], increasing the economy's productive capacity [1]. The diagram shows LRAS shifting right from LRAS1 to LRAS2, with price level and real output axes [1]. The effect depends on whether the new workers have the skills employers need and whether there are jobs for them [1]. **[4]**

**10.** Way 1: better skills raise output per worker [1], so the same labour force can produce more, increasing potential output [1]. Way 2: training helps workers move into growing industries [1], reducing structural unemployment and raising the number of people in productive work [1]. Other valid ways: a more skilled workforce supports innovation and new technology. **[4]**

**11.** A permanent improvement in technology lowers costs per unit at existing output, shifting SRAS right [1], and raises the maximum the economy can produce, shifting LRAS right [1]. A temporary rise in oil prices raises firms' costs, shifting SRAS left [1], but the economy's labour, capital and technology are unchanged, so capacity and LRAS do not change [1]. **[4]**

**12.** Award up to 10 marks across knowledge, application, analysis and evaluation.
- The neo-classical LRAS is vertical: flexible wages and prices return the economy to potential output [1].
- On this view a recession is temporary, and falling wages and prices restore output without demand policy [1].
- The Keynesian LRAS has a horizontal section where there is spare capacity [1].
- In a recession, output is on the flat section, so a rise in AD raises output with little inflation [1].
- Keynesians argue wages are sticky downwards, so unemployment can persist for years [1].
- Evidence of long periods of high unemployment after deep recessions supports the Keynesian view [1].
- But the neo-classical view warns that demand expansion near capacity only raises prices [1] and focuses attention on long-run capacity [1].
- The more useful model depends on how much spare capacity exists and how quickly wages adjust [1].
- Judgement: in a deep recession with high unemployment the Keynesian view is usually more useful; in a mild downturn close to capacity the neo-classical view gives a better warning about inflation [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* Tie the choice of model to evidence of spare capacity rather than declaring one model "correct".

## Where marks are usually lost

- Shifting SRAS because the price level changed.
- Ignoring productivity when wages rise.
- Forgetting that depreciation raises import costs.
- Shifting LRAS for a temporary cost shock.
- Drawing the Keynesian curve without labelling its sections.
- Claiming one LRAS model is always right.
