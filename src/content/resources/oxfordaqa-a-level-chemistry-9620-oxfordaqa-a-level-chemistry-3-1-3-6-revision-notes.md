These notes condense **3.1.3.6 Bond polarity** from the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Chemistry (9620) specification, Version 5.3. It is International AS content, examined in Unit 1. For explanations and worked examples, read the [bond polarity study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-1-3-6/); for exam-style questions, use the [bond polarity practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-1-3-6-practice/).

## Core ideas

- **Electronegativity**: the power of an atom to attract the pair of electrons in a covalent bond.
- Different electronegativities → **unsymmetrical** electron distribution → **polar covalent bond**.
- The more electronegative atom is **δ−**; the other is **δ+**.
- A polar bond **may** give a molecule a **permanent dipole**: only if the bond dipoles do not cancel.
- Dipoles cancel in symmetrical shapes with identical outer atoms.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Electronegativity | Power of an atom to attract the pair of electrons in a covalent bond |
| Polar bond | A covalent bond with an unsymmetrical electron distribution between atoms of different electronegativity |
| Partial charge (δ+ or δ−) | The small charge on each atom of a polar bond |
| Bond dipole | The separation of charge along one polar bond; it has a direction |
| Permanent dipole | An overall, lasting separation of charge across a whole molecule |
| Non-polar molecule | A molecule with no permanent dipole, either because its bonds are non-polar or because its bond dipoles cancel |

## Electronegativity values and patterns

H 2.1, C 2.5, N 3.0, O 3.5, F 4.0, Cl 3.0, Br 2.8, I 2.5, S 2.5 (Pauling scale; no units; values are given in questions).

- Increases **across a period**: greater nuclear charge, similar shielding, smaller atoms.
- Decreases **down a group**: bonding pair further from the nucleus and more shielded.
- Fluorine is the most electronegative element.
- Bigger difference → more polar bond; a very large difference → ionic bonding.

## Partial charges in common bonds

| Bond | δ+ atom | δ− atom |
|---|---|---|
| O–H | H | O |
| N–H | H | N |
| N–F | N | F |
| C–Cl | C | Cl |
| C=O | C | O |
| H–Cl | H | Cl |
| C–H | Only slightly polar (difference 0.4); usually treated as non-polar | |

## Does the molecule have a permanent dipole?

1. Are any bonds polar? If not → **non-polar**.
2. Work out the shape from electron pairs.
3. Are all the outer atoms the same **and** is the shape one of: linear, trigonal planar, tetrahedral, trigonal bipyramidal, octahedral, square planar? If yes → dipoles **cancel**, **non-polar**.
4. Otherwise → dipoles do **not** cancel → **permanent dipole**.

| Non-polar (dipoles cancel) | Polar (permanent dipole) |
|---|---|
| CO₂, BF₃, CCl₄, PCl₅, SF₆, XeF₄, XeF₂ | H₂O, NH₃, NF₃, SF₄, ClF₃, BrF₅, CHCl₃, CH₂Cl₂, OCS, HCl |

## Three quick decisions

- **BF₃ against NF₃.** Both have three polar bonds to fluorine. BF₃ has no lone pair on boron, so it is trigonal planar and the dipoles cancel: non-polar. NF₃ has a lone pair on nitrogen, so it is pyramidal and the dipoles do not cancel: polar.
- **SF₆ against BrF₅.** SF₆ is octahedral with six identical bonds: non-polar. BrF₅ has one lone pair, giving a square pyramid with no atom opposite the apical fluorine: polar.
- **CO₂ against OCS.** Both are linear. In CO₂ the two C=O dipoles are equal and opposite: non-polar. In OCS the C=O dipole is larger than the C=S dipole, so they do not cancel: polar, with the oxygen end δ−.

## Why polarity matters later

A permanent dipole gives permanent dipole–dipole forces between molecules, which raise boiling points (3.1.3.7). Polar bonds also explain the reactivity of many organic compounds, where a δ+ carbon is attacked by nucleophiles.

## Evidence

A charged rod deflects a thin stream of a polar liquid (water, trichloromethane) but not a non-polar one (hexane, tetrachloromethane).

## Exam traps

- A definition that leaves out "the pair of electrons in a covalent bond".
- "Polar bonds, so polar molecule": shape must be checked.
- "Lone pairs, so polar": XeF₄ and XeF₂ have lone pairs and are non-polar.
- Ignoring different outer atoms: CHCl₃ is polar though CCl₄ is not.
- Partial charges reversed, especially on nitrogen (δ− in N–H, δ+ in N–F).
- "Dipoles cancel" with no shape given.

## Self-test

1. Define electronegativity.
2. Why does electronegativity decrease down Group 7?
3. Add partial charges to the O–H bond.
4. Which is more polar, C–Br or C–F? Explain using electronegativity values.
5. Which atom is δ+ in an N–F bond?
6. Why is the Br–Br bond non-polar?
7. Explain why CO₂ has no permanent dipole.
8. Does BrF₅ have a permanent dipole? Give its shape.
9. Explain why CH₂Cl₂ is polar even though it is tetrahedral.
10. XeF₂ has three lone pairs on xenon. Is it polar? Explain.
11. Describe a simple experiment that shows trichloromethane molecules have a permanent dipole.

### Answers

1. The power of an atom to attract the pair of electrons in a covalent bond.
2. The atoms get larger, so the bonding pair is further from the nucleus and more shielded, and it is attracted less strongly.
3. **O δ−**, **H δ+**.
4. **C–F**: difference 4.0 − 2.5 = 1.5, compared with 2.8 − 2.5 = 0.3 for C–Br.
5. **N** (fluorine is more electronegative).
6. Both atoms have the same electronegativity, so the pair is shared equally.
7. It is linear; the two equal C=O dipoles point in opposite directions and cancel.
8. **Yes**: it is square pyramidal, so the five Br–F dipoles do not cancel.
9. The C–H and C–Cl dipoles are not equal, so even in a tetrahedral arrangement they do not cancel.
10. **No**: the lone pairs are equatorial, so the molecule is linear and the two equal Xe–F dipoles cancel.
11. Run a thin stream of the liquid from a burette past a charged rod; the stream is deflected towards the rod.
