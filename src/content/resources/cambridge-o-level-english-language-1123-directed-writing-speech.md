This guide covers one of the five forms named for **Section A, Directed Writing**, of Cambridge O Level English Language 1123 Paper 2. The syllabus says candidates "use, develop and evaluate the information in the text(s) to create a discursive/argumentative/persuasive **speech**, email, report, letter or article", writing "about 250–350 words". The [directed writing study guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-english-language-1123-directed-writing/) explains how to use, develop and evaluate the sources and gives each form a single row in a table. This page is about the speech alone: what makes writing sound spoken, how to organise it for listeners, which devices help and which hurt, and how to turn source material into something an audience would want to hear.

## Why the form matters for marks

Directed writing is marked for writing (W1–W5, 15 marks) and reading (R3 and R5, 10 marks). Two writing objectives depend directly on the form:

- **W2**, "organise and structure ideas and opinions for deliberate effect": a speech is organised for listeners, who cannot re-read.
- **W4**, "use register appropriate to context": the register must suit the audience and occasion the question names.

A good argument written as an essay with "Good morning" stuck on the front does not meet either objective fully.

## What makes writing sound spoken

A listener hears each sentence once. Speeches therefore use features that help people follow and remember:

| Feature | Purpose | Example (original) |
|---|---|---|
| Direct address | keeps the audience involved | "Parents, you have every right to ask..." |
| Inclusive pronouns | builds a shared position | "We all want the same thing." |
| Signposting | shows where the speech is going | "My first point is simple." / "Finally..." |
| Short sentences after long ones | creates emphasis | "Then the funding stopped. Just like that." |
| Rhetorical questions, used sparingly | invites the audience to agree | "Is that really a saving?" |
| Repetition and patterns of three | makes ideas memorable | "Safer streets, cleaner air, calmer mornings." |
| Brief anecdote | makes a point concrete | "Last week I watched a Year 7 student..." |
| Recap before the close | reminds listeners of the argument | "So: it costs less, it helps more, and it can start now." |

**Control is what earns credit.** A speech that asks a rhetorical question in every paragraph sounds forced. Choose two or three devices and place them where they matter: the opening, the turn to the counter-argument and the close.

## Audience and register

The question will name an audience and often an occasion. Let both shape your vocabulary and tone.

| Audience and occasion | Register | Typical opening |
|---|---|---|
| Your class or year group, at assembly | lively, direct, inclusive, never slang | "Good morning, everyone." |
| Parents at a school meeting | respectful, clear, reasoned | "Good evening, and thank you for coming." |
| A town or community meeting | formal but warm; aware that views differ | "Good evening, ladies and gentlemen." |
| A debate or speaking competition | confident, structured, persuasive | "Madam Chair, members of the audience..." |

Avoid letter features: no "Dear...", no "Yours sincerely". A speech can end with "Thank you for listening."

## Purpose: persuasive or discursive

- A **persuasive** or **argumentative** speech takes a side and moves the audience to agree or act. It still answers the strongest opposing point; ignoring it makes the speaker sound unaware.
- A **discursive** speech weighs both sides for an audience that has to decide, then gives a reasoned recommendation. The tone is calmer and more even-handed.

Read the task wording for clues: "persuade", "convince" and "argue" point one way; "consider the views" or "give your views on both sides" point the other.

## A structure for listeners

1. **Opening (40–50 words).** Greet the audience, hook them with a question, image or surprising fact from the sources, and state your position.
2. **Body (three or four sections, 50–70 words each).** One main point per section, each beginning with a clear signpost. In each, **use** a source point, **develop** it for this audience and, where possible, **evaluate** it.
3. **Counter-argument (40–60 words).** Present the other side fairly, then answer it.
4. **Close (30–40 words).** Recap, then a call to action or a line that returns to the opening hook.

This adds up to roughly 300 words, inside the syllabus range of "about 250–350 words".

## From source sentence to spoken argument

Source material must be reworked, not copied. Compare:

- **Source (original):** *"The council estimates that closing the library on Saturdays would save money for road repairs."*
- **Copied into a speech:** "The council estimates that closing the library on Saturdays would save money for road repairs."
- **Used, developed and evaluated:** "The council says Saturday closing would pay for road repairs. But ask yourselves who uses the library on Saturdays: working parents, students revising for exams, people who cannot come in the week. And notice that the council gives us an estimate, not a figure. Are we really going to close a library on a guess?"

The second version keeps the source point, applies it to the audience, questions the evidence and sounds like someone speaking.

## Worked example

Sources (original, shortened):

> **Text 1, from the head teacher's newsletter:** From next term, the Friday afternoon sports session
> for Year 11 will be replaced by extra examination lessons. Results in two subjects fell last year,
> and parents have asked us to do more to prepare students.
>
> **Text 2, from a PE teacher's blog:** For many Year 11 students, Friday sport is the only exercise
> they get all week. Students under exam pressure need a break more than ever. Research shows that
> physical activity can improve concentration and mood.

**Task:** *You are a Year 11 student. Write a speech to be given at a meeting of parents and teachers, arguing that the sports session should be kept.*

**Model opening:**

> Good evening, and thank you for letting a student speak tonight. I want to begin with a confession:
> I am not good at football. But every Friday afternoon, for one hour, I stop thinking about
> examinations, and I come back on Monday ready to work. Tonight I want to persuade you that removing
> that hour will cost us more than it gives.

**Model body section (counter-argument and evaluation):**

> Now, the school's concern is a fair one. Results in two subjects did fall last year, and parents are
> right to want action. But look closely at the proposal. Two subjects fell, yet every Year 11 student
> loses their sport. Would it not be better to offer extra help in those two subjects, at lunchtime or
> after school, to the students who need it? We can protect results and protect our health. We do not
> have to choose.

**Model close:**

> So I ask you to keep Friday sport: for our fitness, for our concentration and for our sanity in the
> hardest year of school. Give us that one hour, and we will give you our best work. Thank you.

**Commentary.** The opening greets the occasion, uses a personal anecdote and states the position. The body section concedes fairly, then **evaluates** the proposal (two subjects but every student) and **develops** an alternative neither source offered. The close uses a recap in three parts and a balanced final sentence. Register is respectful to adults but recognisably a student's voice.

## Common mistakes

- **Essay with a greeting:** formal paragraphs that no one would say aloud.
- **Device overload:** five rhetorical questions in a row, or exclamation marks on every line.
- **Wrong audience:** speaking to "fellow students" when the task names parents.
- **Slang** in an attempt to sound spoken.
- **Copying the sources** instead of reworking them.
- **No counter-argument**, so the speaker seems unaware of the other side.
- **Letter conventions** at the start or end.

## Exam technique

Underline the audience, the occasion and the purpose in the question before you read the sources. While reading, mark each source point **U**, **D** or **E** as the directed writing guide suggests, and note one fact or image to use as your hook. Write your opening and close in your plan first, so the speech has a clear destination. Leave a few minutes to check spelling, punctuation and grammar (W5): a speech still has to be written accurately.
