## Key definitions

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Empirical formula | Simplest whole-number ratio of atoms of each element in a compound |
| Molecular formula | Actual number of atoms of each element in a molecule |
| General formula | Algebraic formula for all members of a homologous series, e.g. CₙH₂ₙ₊₂ |
| Structural formula | Arrangement of atoms carbon by carbon, e.g. CH₃CH(OH)CH₃ |
| Displayed formula | Shows every atom and every bond |
| Skeletal formula | Lines for the carbon skeleton, H on carbon omitted, functional groups drawn |
| Homologous series | A series of compounds containing the same functional group |
| Functional group | Atom or group of atoms responsible for the typical reactions of a compound |
| Radical | Species with an unpaired electron, shown by a dot |
| Structural isomers | Same molecular formula, different structural formula |
| Stereoisomers | Same structural formula, different arrangement of atoms in space |

## 3.3.1.1 Formulas and homologous series

**Features of a homologous series**

- same general formula
- successive members differ by CH₂
- same functional group, so similar chemical properties
- gradual trend in physical properties (e.g. boiling point rises with chain length).

**General formulas to know**

| Family | General formula |
|---|---|
| Alkanes | CₙH₂ₙ₊₂ |
| Alkenes (one C=C) and cycloalkanes | CₙH₂ₙ |
| Alcohols | CₙH₂ₙ₊₁OH |

**Skeletal formula checklist**

- Every corner and every line end is a carbon.
- Count H on each carbon as 4 minus the bonds drawn.
- Draw –OH, =O, –Cl and similar groups in full, including H on O.

## 3.3.1.1 IUPAC naming

**Stems:** meth (1), eth (2), prop (3), but (4), pent (5), hex (6). You need chains and rings of up to six
carbons each.

| Group | Suffix or prefix | Carbon 1 fixed? |
|---|---|---|
| C=C | -ene | No — give the lowest number |
| –OH | -ol | No — give the lowest number |
| C=O in chain | -one | No — give the lowest number |
| –CHO | -al | Yes |
| –COOH | -oic acid | Yes |
| –C≡N | -nitrile (CN carbon is counted) | Yes |
| –F, –Cl, –Br, –I | fluoro-, chloro-, bromo-, iodo- | Prefix |
| –CH₃, –C₂H₅ | methyl-, ethyl- | Prefix |
| Ring | cyclo- | — |

**Method in steps**

1. Longest chain containing the functional group → stem.
2. Number from the end that gives the main functional group the lowest number.
3. Prefixes in alphabetical order (chloro before methyl), each with its locant.
4. di-, tri- for repeats: 2,3-dichloro…
5. Commas between numbers, hyphens between numbers and letters.

**Small reminders**

- CH₃CH(Cl)CH₂CH₃ → 2-chlorobutane (not 3-chlorobutane).
- CH₃CH₂CH₂CHO → butanal (no number).
- CH₃COCH₂CH₂CH₃ → pentan-2-one.
- CH₂=CHCH(OH)CH₃ → but-3-en-2-ol: –OH takes the suffix and the lower number.

## 3.3.1.2 Mechanism conventions

| | Free-radical mechanisms | Other mechanisms |
|---|---|---|
| What you draw | Balanced equation for each step | Structures plus curly arrows |
| Symbol | Dot for the unpaired electron (Br•, C₂H₅•) | Curly arrow = movement of an electron pair |
| Curly arrows? | Not required | Required |

**Curly-arrow rules**

- Bond formed: arrow starts at a **lone pair** or at a **covalent bond**, and ends where the new bond forms.
- Bond broken: arrow starts at the **bond**, and ends at the atom that keeps the pair.
- Show lone pairs and δ+/δ− where they explain the arrows.

**Free-radical step check**

- Atoms balance on both sides.
- A step with one radical in has one radical out; two radicals combining give no radical.
- The names initiation, propagation and termination are taught with the chlorination of methane in 3.3.2.4.

## 3.3.1.3 Isomerism

| Type | Same | Different | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Chain | Molecular formula, functional group | Carbon skeleton | butane / methylpropane |
| Position | Molecular formula, skeleton, functional group | Position of the group | 1-chloropropane / 2-chloropropane |
| Functional group | Molecular formula | Functional group | hex-1-ene / cyclohexane |
| E–Z (stereo) | Structural formula | Arrangement in space about C=C | (E)- and (Z)-pent-2-ene |

**E–Z: method in steps**

1. Find the C=C.
2. Check that **each** carbon of the C=C has two different groups. If either has two identical groups, there
   are no E–Z isomers.
3. On each carbon, rank the two atoms directly attached by atomic number. Higher atomic number = higher
   priority.
4. If they tie, compare the atoms one bond further out; stop at the first difference.
5. Higher-priority groups on the same side = **Z**; on opposite sides = **E**.

**Why E–Z isomers exist:** restricted rotation about the planar C=C double bond.

**Worked reminder:** in BrCH=CHCl, one carbon has Br and H, the other Cl and H. Br beats H and Cl beats H.
If Br and Cl are on the same side of the C=C, the isomer is (Z)-1-bromo-2-chloroethene; if they are on
opposite sides, it is the E isomer.

```
    Br      Cl              Br      H
      \    /                  \    /
       C=C                     C=C
      /    \                  /    \
     H      H                H      Cl
       (Z)                     (E)
```

**Common priority comparisons**

| Higher | Lower | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| –Br (35) | –Cl (17) | Atomic number |
| –Cl (17) | –CH₃ (C = 6) | Atomic number |
| –CH₂CH₃ | –CH₃ | Next atoms (C,H,H) beat (H,H,H) |
| –OH (8) | –CH₃ (6) | Atomic number |

## Must-know distinctions

- **Empirical vs molecular formula**: ethene's molecular formula is C₂H₄; its empirical formula is CH₂.
- **Structural vs displayed formula**: CH₃CH₂OH is structural; showing every C–H, C–C, C–O and O–H line is
  displayed.
- **Structural isomers vs stereoisomers**: structural isomers differ in which atoms are joined to which;
  stereoisomers have the same bonds but a different arrangement in space.
- **E/Z vs cis/trans**: the specification uses E and Z with CIP priorities. Z is not always the "both H on one side"
  form — apply the rules each time.
- **Lone pair vs negative charge**: a curly arrow starts at a lone pair, not at the minus sign.

## Quick self-test

1. Give the empirical formula of hexane.
2. State the general formula of the alkanes and give the molecular formula of the alkane with 8 carbons.
3. How many carbon atoms are in pentanenitrile? Give its structural formula.
4. Name CH₃CH₂CH₂OH.
5. Name (CH₃)₂C=CHCH₃ and state whether it has E–Z isomers.
6. Give the name of a functional group isomer of butanal.
7. 1-chloropropane and 2-chloropropane are isomers. What type?
8. Which has higher CIP priority: –CH₂OH or –CH₂CH₃?
9. What does the dot in Br• represent?
10. State the two places a curly arrow that forms a new bond can start.
11. Define the term stereoisomers.
12. Explain why the C=C double bond gives rise to stereoisomers.

### Answers

1. C₃H₇ (C₆H₁₄ divided by 2).
2. CₙH₂ₙ₊₂; C₈H₁₈.
3. Five; CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂CN (the CN carbon is counted).
4. Propan-1-ol.
5. 2-methylbut-2-ene. No E–Z isomers: carbon 2 carries two identical CH₃ groups.
6. Butanone (C₄H₈O).
7. Position isomers.
8. –CH₂OH: the next atoms are (O, H, H) against (C, H, H), and O has the higher atomic number.
9. An unpaired electron.
10. From a lone pair of electrons, or from a covalent bond.
11. Compounds with the same structural formula but a different arrangement of atoms in space.
12. There is restricted rotation about the planar C=C, so groups on each carbon are fixed on one side.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing the molecular formula when asked for the empirical formula, or not simplifying (C₂H₄ for ethene's
  empirical formula).
- Numbering from the wrong end, so the functional group gets a higher locant than it should.
- Prefixes not in alphabetical order (methylchloro… instead of chloro…methyl…).
- Forgetting that the CN carbon counts in a nitrile name.
- Skeletal formulas with H drawn on carbon, or with the end carbons missed in the count.
- Curly arrows in a free-radical mechanism, or a radical with no dot.
- A curly arrow starting from an atom, or from a charge, instead of from a lone pair or bond.
- E–Z assigned from "H on the same side" rather than from CIP priorities.
- A definition of stereoisomers that says "same molecular formula" only — the structural formula must be the
  same.
- Missing "restricted rotation" when explaining why E–Z isomers exist.
