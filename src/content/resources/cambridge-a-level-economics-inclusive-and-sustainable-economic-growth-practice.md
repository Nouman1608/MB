## Questions

**1.** **(a)** Define inclusive economic growth. **[2]**
**(b)** Distinguish between equity and equality. **[3]**

**2.** In Country Gamma, average income per household in the poorest fifth rises from 5.0 to 5.2 (thousand) and in the richest fifth from 30.0 to 33.0.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage growth of each group's income. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the absolute gain of each group. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the ratio of richest to poorest income in each year. **[2]**
**(d)** Comment on whether this growth is inclusive. **[2]**

**3.** Explain two policies a government could use to promote inclusive growth. **[4]**

**4.** **(a)** Define sustainable economic growth. **[2]**
**(b)** Distinguish between a renewable and a non-renewable resource. **[2]**

**5.** A fishing area holds 1,200 tonnes of fish. The stock regenerates by 8% of its size each year. Fishers take 120 tonnes a year.

**(a)** State the sustainable yield in the first year. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the stock after two years. **[3]**
**(c)** Explain why this level of fishing is unsustainable. **[2]**

**6.** Country Delta has 450 million tonnes of a mineral and extracts 15 million tonnes a year.

**(a)** Calculate how long the reserve lasts at constant extraction. **[1]**
**(b)** Extraction now grows by 5% a year. Show that the reserve runs out during year 19. **[3]**
**(c)** Explain one reason why the reserve might last longer than your answer to part (b). **[2]**

**7.** A country emits 80 million tonnes of greenhouse gases. GDP rises 4% and emissions intensity (emissions per unit of GDP) falls 5%.

**(a)** Calculate the new level of emissions. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate emissions if intensity had fallen only 2%. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain why a fall in emissions intensity does not guarantee a fall in emissions. **[2]**

**8.** In a market, the demand function is Qd = 800 − 10P and the supply function is Qs = −100 + 10P. Each unit causes 0.4 tonne of emissions. A tax of 10 per unit is placed on producers.

**(a)** Find the price and quantity at which the market clears before the tax. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the equilibrium price and quantity after the tax. **[3]**
**(c)** Calculate the change in emissions. **[2]**
**(d)** Calculate the tax revenue and state who bears more of the tax. **[2]**

**9.** Suppose a carbon tax of this kind raises the price of the goods by 10%. A low-income household with income 9,000 spends 400 on these goods. A high-income household with income 48,000 spends 1,200 on them.

**(a)** Calculate the extra cost as a percentage of income for each household. **[3]**
**(b)** Suggest one way the government could offset the effect on the low-income household. **[2]**

**10.** Firms X and Y each emit 90 tonnes. A cap allows 120 tonnes in total. Reducing emissions costs X 12 per tonne and Y 36 per tonne.

**(a)** Calculate the total cost if each firm cuts emissions by the same amount. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the total cost if X makes the whole cut. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain how tradable permits could achieve the second outcome. **[3]**

**11.** Evaluate the view that policies to reduce environmental damage from growth will always make growth less inclusive. **[10]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** Growth in which the benefits are shared widely across society [1], with opportunities open to poorer households, regions and groups [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Equality is an even spread of income or wealth, which can be measured [1]. Equity is fairness of the distribution [1], a normative judgement that depends on values, so an equal outcome may not be equitable [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* a one-line contrast scores less than a pair of clear definitions. State what each term means, then say which is measurable.

**2. (a)** Poorest: (5.2 − 5.0) ÷ 5.0 × 100 = **4.0%** [1]. Richest: (33.0 − 30.0) ÷ 30.0 × 100 = **10.0%** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Poorest gain **0.2**, richest gain **3.0** (thousand) [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** Year 1: 30 ÷ 5 = **6.0** [1]. Year 2: 33 ÷ 5.2 = **6.3** [1]. **[2]**
**(d)** The poorest gain, but their growth is well below the richest group's [1]. The gap widens in absolute and relative terms, so the growth is not inclusive [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* show the working for each group separately and then compare. A comment that cites both the percentages and the ratio is stronger than one that quotes only the mean.

**3.** Policy one, for example education and training: it raises the skills of lower-income workers [1], so they can earn more and gain from growth [1]. Policy two, for example progressive taxes and transfers: they take a higher share from high earners [1] and pay benefits to low-income households, which narrows gaps [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* for each policy, link the mechanism to the poorest group. Naming a policy without that link gains little.

**4. (a)** Growth that meets today's needs [1] while leaving future generations able to meet theirs [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** A renewable resource regenerates, so it can be used indefinitely if use does not exceed regeneration [1]. A non-renewable resource is finite, so any use reduces what remains [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* include the future-generations idea in the definition. A definition that only says protecting the environment is incomplete.

**5. (a)** 8% of 1,200 = **96 tonnes** [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** Year 1: 1,200 + 96 − 120 = 1,176 [1]. Year 2 regeneration: 0.08 × 1,176 = 94.08 [1]. Stock = 1,176 + 94.08 − 120 = **1,150.08, about 1,150 tonnes** [1]. **[3]**
**(c)** Harvest (120) exceeds regeneration (96) [1], so the stock falls each year, which cuts future regeneration and future catches [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* regeneration is a percentage of the current stock, so recalculate it each year. Using the original 96 in year 2 is a common slip.

**6. (a)** 450 ÷ 15 = **30 years** [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** Cumulative extraction after n years = 15 × (1.05ⁿ − 1) ÷ 0.05 [1]. After 18 years: 15 × (1.05¹⁸ − 1) ÷ 0.05 = 422.0, which is below 450 [1]. After 19 years: 458.1, which is above 450, so the reserve runs out during year 19 [1]. **[3]**
**(c)** New discoveries add to reserves [1]. Alternatively, a rising price may slow extraction, or recycling and substitutes may reduce demand [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* in a show-that question, give the figure on each side of 450 so the conclusion follows from your working.

**7. (a)** Emissions change factor = 1.04 × 0.95 [1] = 0.988 [1]. New emissions = 80 × 0.988 = **79.04 million tonnes**, a fall of 1.2% [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** 80 × 1.04 × 0.98 = **81.5 million tonnes** [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** If GDP grows faster than intensity falls, total emissions rise [1], and the stock of gases in the atmosphere keeps building even when emissions grow more slowly than GDP [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* multiply the growth factors rather than subtracting the percentages. Subtracting gives a different answer.

**8. (a)** 800 − 10P = −100 + 10P gives 900 = 20P [1]. **P = 45 and Q = 350** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** New supply: Qs = −100 + 10(P − 10) = −200 + 10P [1]. 800 − 10P = −200 + 10P gives P = 50 [1]. **Q = 300** [1]. **[3]**
**(c)** Before: 350 × 0.4 = 140 tonnes [1]. After: 300 × 0.4 = 120 tonnes, a **fall of 20 tonnes** [1]. **[2]**
**(d)** Revenue = 10 × 300 = **3,000** [1]. Consumers pay 5 more (50 − 45) and producers receive 5 less (40 instead of 45), so the burden is shared equally [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* a tax on producers shifts the supply curve up by the amount of the tax. Describe the shift in words if you are asked for a diagram.

**9. (a)** Extra cost: low-income 10% of 400 = 40, so 40 ÷ 9,000 = **0.44%** of income [1]. High-income 10% of 1,200 = 120, so 120 ÷ 48,000 = **0.25%** [1]. The low-income household pays a larger share, so the tax is regressive [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** Return some of the tax revenue as a rebate to low-income households [1], for example a payment of at least 40 a year to the household in the question, which would cover its extra cost [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* compare shares of income. Comparing the money amounts, 40 and 120, points the other way and misses the question.

**10. (a)** Each cuts 30 tonnes (60 in total). X: 30 × 12 = 360 [1]. Y: 30 × 36 = 1,080. Total = **1,440** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** X cuts 60 tonnes: 60 × 12 = **720** [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** Give each firm 60 permits, so each may emit 60 tonnes [1]. X cuts its emissions to 30 tonnes and sells 30 spare permits to Y [1]. Y buys at any price between 12 and 36, for example 24, which costs it 720, less than its abatement cost of 1,080. X receives 720, which covers its cost of cutting, so both gain [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* state the price range in which both firms gain. Trade only happens between the two abatement costs.

**11.** A strong answer covers these points:

- Analysis of the claim: a carbon tax or higher energy prices take a larger share of low incomes, as shown by a regressive effect [1]. Regulation can raise costs and prices, and job losses in polluting industries can fall on workers with few other options [1].
- Analysis against: pollution often harms poorer groups most, for example through poor air quality or climate shocks, so reducing damage can be inclusive [1]. Green investment can create jobs in lagging regions [1].
- Evaluation: recycling revenue as rebates or training can offset the cost to low-income households [1]. The effect depends on the policy chosen, the elasticity of demand for the taxed good, how revenue is used and the country's income level [1]. Timing matters, as costs may come first and benefits later [1]. Compensation needs funding [1].
- Judgement: a supported conclusion, for example that the claim is too strong because "always" ignores design, but that unmitigated taxes can hurt the poor [1]. A view on which factor matters most [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* the word "always" invites you to find one case where the claim fails and to explain what makes the difference. Finish with a judgement tied to those conditions.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Defining inclusive growth as faster growth.
- Treating equity and equality as the same.
- Quoting the mean growth rate when the question asks about the poorest group.
- Comparing money amounts instead of percentages of income.
- Using the original stock each year instead of recalculating regeneration.
- Subtracting percentages instead of multiplying growth factors.
- Shifting demand instead of supply when a tax falls on producers.
- Listing policies without a judgement about which works best and why.
