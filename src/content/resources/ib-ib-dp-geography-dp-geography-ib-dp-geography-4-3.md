The [4.3 revision notes](/resources/ib-dp-geography-geophysical-hazards-hazard-risk-and-vulnerability-revision-notes/) already define vulnerability and disaster, list the types of vulnerability, explain the pressure and release model, compare the 2010 Haiti and Chile earthquakes, and outline the disaster management cycle and Park's model. ## What this sub-topic asks you to do

- Explain why vulnerability to geophysical hazards varies between places and between groups of people.
- Analyse the impacts of geophysical hazard events on different people and places, in the short and long term.
- Compare hazard events with contrasting impacts and explain the differences.

## Vulnerability inside a community

Vulnerability is often compared between countries, but large differences exist **within** a single place. Several factors combine for each person.

- **Gender.** In the 2004 Indian Ocean tsunami, Oxfam found that in many affected villages in Indonesia, India and Sri Lanka, considerably more women than men died. Explanations included that many women were at home or on the shore with children when the waves arrived while many men were at sea or in fields, that fewer women had learned to swim or climb trees, and that women often tried to save children and older relatives.
- **Age.** Young children and older people are less able to move quickly, and depend on others to evacuate.
- **Disability and health.** People with limited mobility, or who rely on medicine and electricity, face greater danger and slower recovery.
- **Time and place of activity.** The 2005 Kashmir earthquake struck in the morning while many schools were in session; the collapse of school buildings killed thousands of children. Where people are at the moment of the event shapes who dies.
- **Tenure and income.** Renters and informal settlers may receive less help, have no insurance and struggle to prove ownership when reconstruction aid is allocated.
- **Language and information.** Migrants or minorities who do not understand official warnings may not act on them.

These factors **intersect**: an older, low-income woman with limited mobility in informal housing faces far higher vulnerability than an average figure for her town would suggest.

## Measuring vulnerability

Vulnerability is multi-dimensional, so it is often measured with a **composite index** that combines several indicators. International examples include the INFORM Risk Index, which combines hazard and exposure, vulnerability and lack of coping capacity, and the WorldRiskIndex.

**Worked example: a simple composite index (invented districts).** Each indicator is scored from 0 (least vulnerable) to 10 (most vulnerable), then averaged.

| Indicator | District X | District Y |
|---|---|---|
| Share of homes of unreinforced construction | 8 | 3 |
| Poverty rate | 7 | 4 |
| Share of population over 70 | 3 | 6 |
| Distance to nearest hospital | 9 | 2 |
| Literacy (inverted: low literacy = high score) | 6 | 2 |
| **Mean score** | (8 + 7 + 3 + 9 + 6) / 5 = **6.6** | (3 + 4 + 6 + 2 + 2) / 5 = **3.4** |

District X is about twice as vulnerable on this index. Notice, though, that District Y scores higher on age. An index summarises, but it can hide specific weaknesses and depends on which indicators are chosen and how they are weighted. Equal weights here assume a hospital's distance matters as much as housing, which is a judgement, not a fact.

## Losses relative to the economy

Rich countries usually suffer larger absolute economic losses because their assets are worth more. But the **impact** on the economy depends on loss relative to its size.

**Worked example (invented countries).**

```
Country A: loss 30 billion, GDP 5,000 billion
           loss as share of GDP = 30 / 5,000 x 100 = 0.6%

Country B: loss 8 billion, GDP 20 billion
           loss as share of GDP = 8 / 20 x 100 = 40%
```

Country A's loss is almost four times larger in money, yet Country B has lost two-fifths of a year's output. Recovery in Country B will be slow and dependent on outside aid; in Country A, insurance and government budgets can absorb the cost. When you compare events, quote losses relative to GDP as well as absolute figures, and quote deaths and people affected as well as money.

## Two contrasting eruptions in 2010

### Eyjafjallajokull, Iceland (April 2010)

- **Local impacts:** meltwater floods from the ice-capped volcano and ash fall on farmland; several hundred local residents were evacuated as a precaution. No deaths were directly caused by the eruption.
- **Distant impacts:** fine ash carried south-east by the wind led to the closure of much of European airspace for several days, cancelling a very large number of flights, stranding travellers and disrupting trade, including air-freighted fresh produce from countries such as Kenya.
- **Why low local vulnerability:** sparse population near the volcano, good monitoring, effective civil protection and a wealthy, well-governed state.

### Merapi, Indonesia (October to November 2010)

- **Impacts:** pyroclastic flows travelled further down the slopes than in recent eruptions; more than 300 people died and hundreds of thousands were evacuated as the exclusion zone was widened.
- **Why higher vulnerability:** very dense rural population on the fertile slopes; livelihoods tied to farming and livestock, which made some people reluctant to leave or eager to return; strong cultural ties to the volcano. Mbah Maridjan, the volcano's traditional spiritual guardian, refused to leave his village and died there.
- **What reduced losses:** monitoring and the decision to widen the exclusion zone are credited with saving many lives, showing that warnings worked even where vulnerability was high.

**The contrast.** Eyjafjallajokull caused few local impacts but large economic disruption far away; Merapi caused heavy local loss of life. The difference lay less in the eruptions than in **exposure**, **livelihoods**, **perception** and the **reach of the hazard** through global networks.

## Annotated model answer

*Examine why vulnerability to a geophysical hazard differs between groups within the same place.* **(sample paragraph)**

"Within one community, vulnerability depends on who people are and where they are when the hazard strikes **[framing]**. In the 2004 tsunami, more women than men died in many villages, partly because women were at home or on the shore with children while many men were at sea **[located evidence on gender]**. Age and disability slow evacuation, and renters or informal settlers may be excluded from reconstruction aid because they cannot prove ownership **[further factors]**. These factors overlap, so the most vulnerable are often those facing several at once **[intersection]**, which is why average figures for a town can hide the people most at risk **[evaluative point]**."

## Common mistakes

- Comparing only countries and ignoring differences within communities.
- Treating a composite index as an objective measurement.
- Comparing economic losses in absolute terms only.
- Writing about an event's impacts without separating local and distant, or short- and long-term.
- Using uncertain death tolls; give a rounded figure you are sure of, or describe the scale.

## Exam technique

- **Compare** two events by setting their impacts side by side and explaining each difference with a factor.
- **Analyse** impacts by group (age, gender, income), by type (social, economic, environmental) and by time.
- **Examine** vulnerability by showing how factors intersect, not just listing them.
- Quote figures relative to population or GDP where possible.
