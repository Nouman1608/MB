## Questions

### Section A: short-answer questions

**1.** Distinguish between product innovation and process innovation. **[3]**

**2.** Explain **one** reason why firms in an industry may be forced to innovate. **[3]**

**3.** Explain the purpose of the testing stage in the R&D cycle. **[2]**

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**4.** Mistral Toys spends £0.9 million a year on R&D and has annual sales of £15 million. Calculate its R&D spending as a percentage of sales. **[1]**

**5.** Mistral is considering a new robot toy. R&D would cost £800,000. Managers estimate a 35% chance of success. If it succeeds, the toy would earn a contribution of £600,000 a year for four years. If it fails, there is no income.

**(a)** Calculate the contribution if the project succeeds. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the expected benefit. **[2]**

**(c)** Calculate the expected net value. **[1]**

**(d)** Calculate the break-even probability of success, to one decimal place. **[2]**

**(e)** Evaluate whether Mistral Toys should go ahead with the R&D project. **[8]**

**6.** Mistral also considers a new moulding machine that costs £180,000. Materials cost £400,000 a year, of which 8% is currently wasted. The machine would cut waste to 3%. It would also save £1 of labour on each of 30,000 toys a year.

**(a)** Calculate the yearly saving in materials. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the total yearly saving and the payback period in years. **[3]**

**(c)** Evaluate the importance of process innovation to Mistral Toys and its stakeholders. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** Product innovation is the introduction of a new or improved good or service [1]. Process innovation is a new or improved way of making or delivering it [1]. Product innovation mainly changes revenue and what customers buy, while process innovation mainly changes costs, quality or speed [1]. **[3]**

**2.** If rivals bring out new or improved products, a firm that does not respond will lose customers [1], because customers compare products and switch to the newest or best [1], so firms in fast-changing industries such as technology must keep innovating to stay competitive [1]. **[3]**

**3.** To check whether a prototype works and whether customers want it [1], so that faults can be corrected or weak ideas dropped before the cost of full launch [1]. **[2]**

**4.** 0.9 ÷ 15 × 100 = **6%** [1]. **[1]**

**5. (a)** 600,000 × 4 = **£2,400,000** [1]. **[1]**

**(b)** 0.35 × 2,400,000 [1] = **£840,000** [1]. **[2]**

**(c)** 840,000 − 800,000 = **+£40,000** [1]. **[1]**

**(d)** 800,000 ÷ 2,400,000 [1] = **33.3%** [1]. **[2]**

**(e)** For: the expected net value is positive (+£40,000) [1]; if successful, the project returns three times the R&D cost (£2.4m against £0.8m) and pays back in 1.33 years of sales [1]; a successful toy could strengthen the brand and replace ageing products [1].
Against: the expected gain is small compared with the £800,000 at risk [1]; there is a 65% chance of losing everything [1]; the probability of 35% is only slightly above the 33.3% break-even, so a small drop in confidence would make it a loss [1]; sales of toys can be fashion-driven and rivals may copy [1].
Judgement: the project is marginal, so Mistral should proceed only if it can afford the loss, for example because the R&D is a small share of its funds, or reduce the risk by staging the development and testing early with children and parents [1]. **[8]**

**6. (a)** Current waste = 400,000 × 0.08 = £32,000 [1]. New waste = 400,000 × 0.03 = £12,000. Saving = **£20,000** [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** Labour saving = 30,000 × £1 = £30,000 [1]. Total = £20,000 + £30,000 = **£50,000** [1]. Payback = 180,000 ÷ 50,000 = **3.6 years** [1]. **[3]**

**(c)** Benefits for Mistral: lower costs raise profit or allow lower prices [1]; less waste supports environmental objectives and brand image, and more consistent moulding may improve quality [1].
Stakeholders: customers may benefit from lower prices or better quality [1]; shareholders benefit from higher profit if the saving is achieved [1]; employees may lose hours if labour is saved, or gain skills in operating the machine [1]; the local community benefits from less waste [1].
Limits: payback of 3.6 years is long if toy designs change quickly and the machine is not flexible [1]; the saving depends on keeping 30,000 toys a year of output [1].
Judgement: process innovation is important because the savings are certain compared with the uncertain return of a new product, but its value depends on output and on the machine's life of at least five years [1]. **[9]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Confusing invention with innovation.
- Forgetting to multiply the benefit by the probability.
- Ignoring the 65% chance of failure.
- Evaluating process innovation without the effect on employees.
