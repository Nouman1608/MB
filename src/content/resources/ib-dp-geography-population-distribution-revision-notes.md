## Why populations are unevenly distributed

The core starting point is that population is not spread evenly across the Earth's surface, and this
theme asks why. Revise the main physical and human factors that pull settlement toward some areas and
away from others: physical factors include climate, relief (terrain), soil fertility, availability of
fresh water and resource access; human factors include economic opportunity, historical patterns of
settlement, political stability, and infrastructure. A strong answer names specific factors relevant
to a specific named place, rather than listing generic categories.

## Key vocabulary

- **Population density** -- the number of people per unit area, usually expressed per square
  kilometre. Distinguish this from population distribution, which describes the pattern (where people
  are) rather than a single density figure.
- **Carrying capacity** -- the maximum population size an environment can sustainably support given
  its available resources.
- **Population change** -- driven by natural change (births minus deaths) and net migration
  (immigration minus emigration); revise being able to state both components separately rather than
  only quoting an overall change figure.
- **Demographic transition** -- the model describing how birth rates and death rates change as a
  country develops, moving through stages from high birth/death rates to low birth/death rates, and
  the resulting effect on population growth rate at each stage.

## Reading population data

This sub-theme is frequently examined through data-response questions -- population pyramids,
choropleth density maps, and demographic transition model diagrams. Revise how to read a population
pyramid specifically: a wide base indicates high birth rates, a narrowing top indicates lower life
expectancy or an ageing population, and an unusually narrow or wide band at a specific age range can
indicate a historical event (war, baby boom, mass migration) affecting that cohort. Practising reading
unfamiliar pyramids and maps under timed conditions is more valuable revision than only memorising
labelled examples, since Paper 2 typically presents new, unseen data.

## Case studies to build

Because Paper 2 rewards named, specific examples over generalised description (the same expectation
that applies across every DP Geography theme), build a small case-study bank specifically for
population distribution: one example of a densely populated area and the factors explaining its
density, one example of a sparsely populated area and the factors explaining its sparsity, and one
example of significant population change (through migration or natural change) with real, named
data -- dates, figures, and specific policy or event context, not a generic account.

## How this sub-theme connects to the wider core

Population distribution sits alongside Global climate: vulnerability and resilience, and Global
resource consumption and security in the compulsory core -- and the three are not meant to be revised
as unconnected blocks. A region's population distribution directly shapes its vulnerability to
climate impacts (dense coastal populations facing sea-level rise, for instance) and its resource
consumption patterns (population size and distribution driving demand). Revising these connections
explicitly, rather than treating each core sub-theme in isolation, prepares you for Paper 2 questions
that draw on more than one sub-theme within a single answer.

## How to approach it

Because this content is examined identically for SL and HL students, do not deprioritise it relative
to your chosen options -- Paper 2 tests this core with the same weight as Paper 1 tests your options.
Practise the specific skill of moving between quantitative data reading (extracting a figure or trend
from a pyramid, map or graph) and qualitative explanation (why that pattern exists, using named
factors) within the same answer, since this combination is exactly what data-response questions on
this theme typically require.

## Self-test

1. Name two physical and two human factors that influence where population settles.
2. What is the difference between population density and population distribution?
3. What are the two components of population change, and how is each defined?
4. What does a wide base on a population pyramid typically indicate?
5. Why is it important to build named, specific case studies for this sub-theme rather than relying on
   generic description?
6. Name the other two sub-themes in the compulsory DP Geography core, alongside population
   distribution.

**Answers:** 1. Physical: any two of climate, relief, soil fertility, water availability, resource
access. Human: any two of economic opportunity, historical settlement patterns, political stability,
infrastructure. 2. Population density is a single figure (people per unit area); population
distribution describes the overall pattern of where people are located. 3. Natural change (births
minus deaths) and net migration (immigration minus emigration). 4. High birth rates. 5. Because Paper
2 rewards named, specific examples with real data over generalised description, and a case-study bank
lets you support an answer with concrete evidence rather than only abstract explanation. 6. Global
climate: vulnerability and resilience, and Global resource consumption and security.
