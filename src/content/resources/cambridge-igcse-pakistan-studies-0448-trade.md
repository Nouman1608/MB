**Trade is Section 6 of Paper 2, The environment of Pakistan, in Cambridge IGCSE Pakistan Studies
0448.** It has two parts: (a) major exports and imports, and (b) Pakistan's trading partners. You
need to name what Pakistan buys and sells, explain how changes in trade affect the economy, and
explain why trade is difficult for a developing country. This guide goes into more depth than the

## Syllabus coverage

**CAMBRIDGE IGCSE PAKISTAN STUDIES (0448), 2026 — PAPER 2, SECTION 6: TRADE**

- **6(a) Major exports and imports:** name the main exports and imports; describe changes in the
  types, amounts and value of goods exported and imported in recent years; know the meaning of GNP
  and GDP and the difference between them; explain the effect of changing trends in exports and
  imports on Pakistan's balance of trade and economy.
- **6(b) Pakistan's trading partners:** name and locate the main trading partners and the goods
  traded with them; understand the factors that may promote or hinder trade, and why it is difficult
  for Pakistan as a developing country to maintain or increase its share of trade; understand the
  factors that may promote and limit trade, including trading blocs, trade barriers and currency
  exchange rates.

## What does Pakistan export, and where are the goods made?

| Export | Where it comes from | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Textiles: cotton yarn, cloth, knitwear, garments, bed linen, towels | Faisalabad, Karachi, Lahore and other Punjab cities | The largest group of exports, built on home-grown cotton |
| Rice, including basmati | The rice belt of northern Punjab; Sindh | A major food export |
| Leather and leather goods | Karachi, Lahore, Kasur, Sialkot | Jackets, gloves and footwear from livestock hides |
| Sports goods | Sialkot | Footballs and other equipment |
| Surgical instruments | Sialkot | High-value metal goods made by skilled workers |
| Fish and seafood | Karachi and the coast | Sold to East Asia and the Middle East |
| Fruit and vegetables | Punjab, Sindh, Balochistan | For example mangoes and kinnow |

## What does Pakistan import?

- **Petroleum and petroleum products, and liquefied natural gas**, because home production does not
  meet demand for transport, power and industry.
- **Machinery and transport equipment**, for factories, power stations and infrastructure.
- **Chemicals, fertilisers and medicines.**
- **Edible oil**, especially palm oil from Malaysia and Indonesia.
- **Iron and steel, vehicles, electronics and tea.**

## How has trade changed?

The syllabus asks you to describe changes in the **types, amounts and value** of goods.

- **Types:** a shift from exporting raw materials, such as raw cotton, towards processed and
  manufactured goods, such as yarn, cloth and finished garments. Each stage adds value, so the same
  cotton earns more as a shirt than as a bale.
- **Amounts and value:** the value of both exports and imports has grown, but imports have usually
  grown faster, driven by fuel, machinery and food.
- **Concentration:** textiles still make up a large share of exports, so the country depends on one
  industry.

When you answer from a graph or table in the exam, describe the overall trend first, then quote the
highest and lowest values with their years, and finally note any change in direction.

## GDP, GNP and the balance of trade

- **GDP (gross domestic product):** the total value of goods and services produced within the
  country in a year.
- **GNP (gross national product):** GDP plus income earned from abroad by the country's people and
  businesses, minus income paid to foreign owners.
- **Balance of trade:** the value of exports minus the value of imports. A **deficit** means imports
  cost more than exports earn; a **surplus** means the opposite.

### Worked example (invented figures)

These figures are invented for practice and do not describe Pakistan.

```
Country X
GDP                                         $300 bn
Income earned abroad by Country X residents  $25 bn
Income paid to foreign owners                 $7 bn
GNP = 300 + 25 - 7                          = $318 bn

Exports                                      $30 bn
Imports                                      $55 bn
Balance of trade = 30 - 55                   = -$25 bn (a deficit)
```

**Effect of a persistent deficit on the economy:** the country must pay for imports with borrowing
or with money sent home by workers abroad; foreign debt grows; pressure on the currency increases;
less money is left for development such as schools and hospitals. If exports grow faster than
imports, the deficit narrows, foreign exchange reserves can recover and industries that export
create jobs.

## Factors that promote and limit trade

**Promoting factors**
- **Trading blocs and agreements**, which lower tariffs between members: the South Asian
  Association for Regional Cooperation (SAARC) and its free-trade area; the Economic Cooperation
  Organisation (ECO); a free-trade agreement with China; the European Union's GSP+ scheme, which
  removes or reduces tariffs on many Pakistani exports.
- Good transport links, such as Karachi's ports, and developing routes such as those linked to
  Gwadar.
- Competitive costs in labour-intensive industries.

**Limiting factors**
- **Trade barriers:** tariffs (taxes on imports), quotas (limits on quantity) and strict quality,
  safety and labour standards in richer markets.
- **Currency exchange rates** (see below).
- Political tension with neighbours, which can close borders.

## Exchange rates: a worked example

The figures below are invented to show the principle.

```
Exchange rate falls from Rs 160 to Rs 200 per US dollar

Pakistani shirt priced at Rs 1,600
  cost to a US buyer before: 1,600 / 160 = $10
  cost to a US buyer after:  1,600 / 200 = $8     -> exports become cheaper abroad

Imported oil at $80 a barrel
  cost in rupees before: 80 x 160 = Rs 12,800
  cost in rupees after:  80 x 200 = Rs 16,000    -> imports become dearer at home
```

A weaker rupee can help exporters win orders, but it raises the cost of fuel and machinery, which
are essential imports. Many exporters also import inputs such as dyes and machinery, so part of the
benefit is lost. This is why a falling rupee often makes the trade deficit worse in the short term.

## Why is trade difficult for a developing country?

1. **Narrow export base:** dependence on textiles means a fall in world prices or demand hits
   earnings hard.
2. **Low value added:** semi-processed goods, such as yarn, earn less than branded finished goods.
3. **Competition** from other low-cost producers of textiles and rice.
4. **Energy shortages and high costs** raise production costs and delay orders.
5. **Standards and barriers** in richer markets need investment to meet.
6. **Dependence on imported fuel and machinery**, whose prices Pakistan cannot control.
7. **Image and security concerns**, which can discourage buyers and investors.

## Linking to the Paper 2 themes

The syllabus asks you to apply recurring themes across Paper 2: problems of a developing country,
environmental effects and conservation, sustainable development, and feasibility. For trade,
examples are the problems listed above; the pollution caused by tanneries and textile dyeing; the
sustainability of relying on one crop; and the feasibility of developing new exports, such as
information technology services.

## Exam technique

Paper 2 lasts 1 hour 30 minutes: you answer three questions from a choice of five, each worth 25
marks with parts (a) to (d) built around resources. Part (d) specifically assesses analysis.

- **Name** or **identify**: one word or phrase each; no explanation.
- **Describe** a trend from a graph: overall pattern, figures with years, any change.
- **Explain**: give reasons, each developed.
- **Evaluate** or **give your views**: consider evidence for and against and reach a judgement.

## Common mistakes

- Confusing GDP and GNP, or the balance of trade with the balance of payments.
- Saying a falling rupee is simply good for trade.
- Listing problems for developing countries without linking them to Pakistan's exports.
- Naming partners without the goods traded.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need current trade statistics?**
No exact figures are required from memory, but you must interpret any figures in the question.

**Is the balance of trade the same as the balance of payments?**
No. The balance of trade covers goods only; the wider balance of payments also includes services,
income and transfers such as remittances.
