Condensed notes on section **3.7.2 Analysing the existing internal position of a business to assess strengths and weaknesses: financial ratio analysis** of AQA A-level Business (7132, for first teaching from September 2023). Section 3.7 is A-level only. The full worked comparison of Ardent Joinery is in the [study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-internal-position-financial-ratio-analysis/); questions are in the [practice set](/resources/aqa-a-level-business-7132-internal-position-financial-ratio-analysis-practice/).

## Specification content

- How to assess the financial performance of a business using balance sheets, income statements and financial ratios.
- Ratios to include: profitability (return on capital employed), liquidity (current ratio), gearing, and efficiency ratios (payables days, receivables days, inventory turnover).
- The value of financial ratios when assessing performance; data may be analysed over time or in comparison with other businesses.

## Formulas

| Ratio | Formula | Unit |
|---|---|---|
| ROCE | Operating profit ÷ capital employed × 100 | % |
| Current ratio | Current assets ÷ current liabilities | x : 1 |
| Gearing | Non-current liabilities ÷ capital employed × 100 | % |
| Receivables days | Receivables ÷ revenue × 365 | days |
| Payables days | Payables ÷ cost of sales × 365 | days |
| Inventory turnover | Cost of sales ÷ inventory | times a year |

**Capital employed** = total assets − current liabilities = equity + non-current liabilities.

## What each ratio tells you

| Ratio | Higher means | Warning signs |
|---|---|---|
| ROCE | More profit per £100 of long-term funds | Falling over time; below the cost of borrowing or competitors |
| Current ratio | More cover for short-term debts | Below 1 (cash risk); far above 2 (idle assets) |
| Gearing | More reliance on borrowing | Above about 50%; interest not covered by profit |
| Receivables days | Customers pay more slowly | Rising days; bad debt risk |
| Payables days | Business pays suppliers more slowly | Very high; suppliers may refuse credit |
| Inventory turnover | Stock sold and replaced more often | Falling (stock piling up); extreme highs (stock-outs) |

## Worked mini-example

Operating profit £240,000; capital employed £1,600,000; current assets £420,000; current liabilities £300,000; non-current liabilities £480,000; receivables £150,000; revenue £1,825,000.

- ROCE = 240,000 ÷ 1,600,000 × 100 = **15%**
- Current ratio = 420,000 ÷ 300,000 = **1.4 : 1**
- Gearing = 480,000 ÷ 1,600,000 × 100 = **30%**
- Receivables days = 150,000 ÷ 1,825,000 × 365 = **30 days**

## Gearing: balance of views

- **High gearing:** interest must be paid whatever the profit; vulnerable to rising interest rates; lenders may impose conditions. But borrowing can finance growth cheaply and increase returns to shareholders when profit is good.
- **Low gearing:** safer, but may miss growth opportunities.

## Over time or against others

| Comparison | Gives you | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| Over time | Trends and the effect of past decisions | One-off events; seasonal timing |
| Against competitors | Relative strengths and weaknesses | Different accounting policies, size, business model |
| Against industry average | Whether the business is typical | Averages hide different strategies |

## Value and limits of ratios

- **Value:** make figures comparable; show trends; help lenders, investors and managers decide where to act; cheap to produce.
- **Limits:** historic; one date on the balance sheet; accounting policies differ; no reasons given; ignore non-financial factors; industries differ; one ratio can mislead.

## Writing a good analysis

1. State the ratio and the two figures with units.
2. Say whether it is better or worse and by how much.
3. Give a reasoned cause linked to the case.
4. Say what it means for cash, risk or return.
5. Qualify: what else you would need to know.

## Model analysis paragraph

"ROCE fell from 16.9% to 13.1%. Operating profit fell 6.7% while capital employed rose 21%, so the new investment is not yet earning a return. This weakens the business's position because it earns less on each £100 of long-term funds. It may be a temporary effect of expansion, but if the trend continues the owners may find that borrowing costs approach the return on investment. More information on when the new assets will contribute would help."

## Command words

- **Calculate:** formula, substitution, answer with units.
- **Analyse / assess:** explain what the change means, its likely cause and its effect on cash, risk or return.

## Exam traps

- ROCE uses **operating profit**, not revenue.
- Payables days use **cost of sales**; receivables days use **revenue**.
- Gearing uses **non-current** liabilities, not all liabilities.
- A high current ratio is not always good; a low one is not always bad.
- State units: %, days, times, or a ratio.

## Self-test

1. Write the formula for ROCE.
2. A business has operating profit of £90,000 and capital employed of £600,000. Calculate ROCE.
3. Current assets are £360,000 and current liabilities £240,000. Calculate the current ratio.
4. Non-current liabilities are £320,000 and capital employed is £800,000. Calculate gearing.
5. Receivables are £84,000 and revenue is £730,000. Calculate receivables days.
6. Payables are £60,000 and cost of sales is £438,000. Calculate payables days.
7. Cost of sales is £540,000 and inventory is £90,000. Calculate inventory turnover.
8. Why might a rise in payables days help a business but also create a risk?
9. Give two reasons a high current ratio might not be a strength.
10. Give two limitations of using ratios from a single year's balance sheet.
11. Why can a highly geared business suffer when interest rates rise?
12. Give one reason why it is useful to compare ratios with a competitor's.

### Answers

1. Operating profit ÷ capital employed × 100.
2. 90,000 ÷ 600,000 × 100 = **15%**.
3. 360,000 ÷ 240,000 = **1.5 : 1**.
4. 320,000 ÷ 800,000 × 100 = **40%**.
5. 84,000 ÷ 730,000 × 365 = **42 days**.
6. 60,000 ÷ 438,000 × 365 = **50 days**.
7. 540,000 ÷ 90,000 = **6 times**.
8. It keeps cash in the business for longer, but suppliers may withdraw credit, raise prices or refuse to supply.
9. Any two: cash or inventory may be sitting idle; the business may not be investing; customers may be slow to pay (high receivables); inventory may be unsold.
10. Any two: it is one date and may not be typical; seasonal effects; it does not show trends; the figures may be out of date.
11. Its interest payments rise, taking more of its profit or cash, and it cannot avoid them.
12. It shows whether the business is stronger or weaker than its rivals, and which areas need action.
