## Formulas to know

| Measure | Formula | Stage |
|---|---|---|
| TC | FC + VC | AS and A2 |
| AFC, AVC | FC ÷ output, VC ÷ output | AS and A2 |
| AC | TC ÷ output = AFC + AVC | AS and A2 |
| MC | change in TC ÷ change in output | A2 |
| TR | price × quantity | AS and A2 |
| AR | TR ÷ quantity (= price at one price) | AS and A2 |
| MR | change in TR ÷ change in output | A2 |
| Profit | TR − TC | AS and A2 |
| Profit per unit | AR − AC | AS and A2 |
| PED | %ΔQ demanded ÷ %Δ price | AS and A2 |
| YED | %ΔQ demanded ÷ %Δ income | AS and A2 |
| XED | %ΔQ demanded of A ÷ %Δ price of B | AS and A2 |
| PES | %ΔQ supplied ÷ %Δ price | AS and A2 |
| MR and PED | MR = P × (1 + 1/PED), PED negative | A2 |

## Cost, revenue and profit: method in steps

1. Write the formula and the units (per day, per month).
2. Split total cost into FC and VC if the question gives only one of them.
3. Total first, then average: divide by output last.
4. Marginal values need two rows: subtract, then divide by the change in output.
5. Check: AFC + AVC should equal AC, and TR − TC should equal (AR − AC) × output.

**Worked reminder.** A laundrette has FC of 500 a week and a constant 3 variable cost per wash. At 400 washes: VC = 3 × 400 = 1,200; TC = 500 + 1,200 = 1,700; AC = 1,700 ÷ 400 = 4.25. If TC rises to 1,790 at 410 washes, MC = (1,790 − 1,700) ÷ 10 = 9. MC (9) is above AC (4.25), so AC will rise.

**Must-know distinctions**

- AC is TC ÷ output; MC is the cost of the extra unit.
- AVC ignores FC; AC does not.
- Revenue is not profit. Profit needs costs.
- Normal and abnormal profit: normal profit is the minimum return needed to keep the firm in the industry and it varies between industries (3.3.2.5). A profit calculation of TR − TC compares revenue with the costs you are given, so say what the costs include.

## Elasticity: method in steps

1. Percentage change = (new − old) ÷ old × 100.
2. Divide the quantity change by the cause.
3. Keep the sign. Write the value to 2 decimal places or as the question asks.
4. Classify: above 1 elastic; below 1 inelastic; 1 unit elastic; 0 perfectly inelastic.
5. Interpret in context and, if asked, link to revenue.

| Result | Meaning |
|---|---|
| PED between 0 and −1 | Inelastic: a price rise lifts TR |
| PED below −1 | Elastic: a price rise cuts TR |
| YED positive | Normal good (above 1: income elastic) |
| YED negative | Inferior good |
| XED positive | Substitutes |
| XED negative | Complements |
| PES below 1 | Inelastic supply (slow or costly to expand) |

**Worked reminder.** A snack bar cuts the price of a wrap from 5.00 to 4.50 (−10%) and demand is inelastic with PED −0.4, so quantity rises 4%. With 1,000 sold, TR goes from 5.00 × 1,000 = 5,000 to 4.50 × 1,040 = 4,680, a fall of 6.4%. An inelastic demand makes a price cut a poor revenue move.

**Elasticity on a straight-line demand curve.** The slope is constant but elasticity is not: demand is more elastic at high prices and low quantities, and less elastic at low prices and high quantities. TR peaks where PED is −1.

**Determinants to quote.** PED: availability of substitutes, necessity or luxury, share of income, time. YED: whether the good is a necessity or a luxury. XED: how close the goods are. PES: spare capacity, stock levels, time to produce, how mobile the factors of production are.

## PED and revenue at a glance

| PED (ignoring sign) | Price rises | Price falls |
|---|---|---|
| Below 1 | TR rises | TR falls |
| 1 | TR unchanged | TR unchanged |
| Above 1 | TR falls | TR rises |

Sketch of a straight-line demand curve (vertical axis price, horizontal axis quantity):

```
price
 |\   elastic (PED below -1): TR rises as price falls
 | \
 |  \--- midpoint: PED = -1, TR at its maximum, MR = 0
 |   \
 |    \  inelastic (PED between 0 and -1): TR falls as price falls
 +------\---- quantity
```

**A2: marginal revenue and PED.** Positive MR means elastic demand and TR rising as output rises. Zero MR means unit elasticity. Negative MR means inelastic demand and TR falling as output rises. Worked reminder: at a price of 24 with PED −3, MR = 24 × (1 − 1/3) = 16. A profit-maximising firm sets MR equal to MC, and MC is positive, so it operates where MR is positive, which means on the elastic part of its demand curve.

## Interpretation chains and evaluation

Use a chain of three links: the number, what it means, then the decision or effect.

- PED −0.6 on tram fares: a 10% rise in fares cuts passengers by about 6%, so TR rises. The operator may raise fares. Evaluation: the estimate comes from past data, and over a longer period people may find alternatives, so demand becomes more elastic.
- YED +2.5 for takeaway meals: demand grows faster than income, so a firm in this market gains most in a boom and loses most in a recession. Evaluation: the value may differ for lower-income and higher-income households.
- XED −1.5 between printers and ink: a printer maker that cuts the printer price can expect higher ink sales. Evaluation: a single value does not show brand loyalty or compatibility rules.
- PES 0.5 for a crop: a price rise brings only a small rise in output in the short run, so prices stay high until planting adjusts. Evaluation: storage and imports can raise short-run supply.

General evaluation points: other things are rarely equal; elasticity differs along a demand curve; short-run and long-run values differ; and a higher TR is not automatically a higher profit.

## Quick self-test

1. A cafe has FC of 500 and VC of 1,200 at 400 coffees. State TC.
2. State AC at that output.
3. TC rises from 1,700 to 1,790 when output rises from 400 to 410. State MC (A2).
4. Price 15, sales 80 units, TC 950. State TR and profit.
5. Price rises 5% and quantity demanded falls 8%. State PED and classify it.
6. Income rises 4% and demand falls 2%. State YED and classify the good.
7. The price of good B rises 12% and demand for good A falls 6%. State XED and the relationship.
8. Price rises from 40 to 44 and quantity supplied from 250 to 270. State PES.
9. Price 18, PED −2. State MR (A2).
10. Why can a firm with inelastic demand raise TR by raising price, yet still see profit fall?

### Answers

1. TC = 500 + 1,200 = **1,700**.
2. AC = 1,700 ÷ 400 = **4.25**.
3. MC = 90 ÷ 10 = **9**.
4. TR = 15 × 80 = **1,200**; profit = 1,200 − 950 = **250**.
5. PED = −8 ÷ 5 = **−1.6**, elastic.
6. YED = −2 ÷ 4 = **−0.5**, an inferior good.
7. XED = −6 ÷ 12 = **−0.5**, complements.
8. %ΔP = 10%, %ΔQ = 8%, PES = **0.8**, inelastic.
9. MR = 18 × (1 − 0.5) = **9**.
10. TR rises, but costs may rise by more, for example if a higher price needs costly changes, or if the fall in output does not cut fixed costs. Profit is TR − TC, so check both.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using the wrong starting value in a percentage change.
- Leaving out the minus sign, so a complement looks like a substitute.
- Saying "elastic" because the answer is large in absolute terms, without comparing it with 1.
- Not stating a conclusion in context after a correct calculation.
- Mixing up AC and MC when explaining where AC is lowest.
- Treating a rise in TR as a rise in profit.
- Calling a change in price a shift of the demand curve.
- Dividing total cost by the wrong output.
- Giving a numerical answer with no unit or no working when the question says "calculate".

## Frequently asked questions

**How do you calculate price elasticity of demand?**
Divide the percentage change in quantity demanded by the percentage change in price, finding each percentage change as (new minus old) divided by old, times 100. Keep the sign, then classify the value: above 1 (ignoring the sign) is elastic and below 1 is inelastic.

**Why does elasticity change along a straight-line demand curve?**
The slope is constant, but the same unit change is a bigger percentage at low quantities. Demand is more elastic at high prices and low quantities, less elastic at low prices and high quantities, and total revenue peaks where PED is -1.

**What is the link between marginal revenue and price elasticity of demand?**
For A2, MR = P x (1 + 1/PED), with PED negative. Positive MR means elastic demand, zero MR means unit elasticity and negative MR means inelastic demand, so a profit-maximising firm operates on the elastic part of its demand curve.

**What is the difference between average cost and marginal cost?**
Average cost is total cost divided by output, while marginal cost is the change in total cost from producing one more unit. When marginal cost is above average cost, average cost rises.
