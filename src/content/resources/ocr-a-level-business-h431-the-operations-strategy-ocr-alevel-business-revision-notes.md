This is the strategy heading in the operations area, not those in other areas of study. ## What the specification lists

- Evaluate the impact and importance of an operations strategy to a business and its stakeholders.
- Recommend and justify an operations strategy for a business.

## Core recall

### Definition

- **Operations strategy:** a long-term plan for how operations will use resources to support the objectives of the whole business.
- It sits below corporate objectives and alongside marketing, finance and human resources strategies; all must be consistent.

### What it contains

| Decision area | Typical choices |
|---|---|
| Production method | Job, batch, flow; lean methods |
| Capacity | Amount; how to change it; utilisation target |
| Location and logistics | Site; in-house or external delivery; offshoring or re-shoring |
| Technology | What to automate; digital systems |
| Stock control | JIT or buffer stock |
| Quality | Control, assurance, TQM, external standards, benchmarking |
| Productivity and scale | Targets, training, economies of scale |
| People | Skills and flexibility |
| Suppliers | Choice and contracts |

### Performance objectives (a common framework; not named in the specification)

| Objective | Meaning | Tends to conflict with |
|---|---|---|
| Cost | Low unit cost | Quality, flexibility |
| Quality | Fit for purpose | Cost |
| Speed | Short time to deliver | Cost |
| Dependability | Delivering as promised | Flexibility |
| Flexibility | Changing product, volume or timing | Cost |

A business chooses a priority; it cannot be best at all five.

### Why a strategy matters

- Direction and consistency of decisions.
- Competitive advantage.
- Focus of investment.
- Preparation for external change.
- Targets that can be monitored.

### Limits

- Forecasts may be wrong.
- Can be rigid in a fast-changing market.
- Depends on people to implement.
- Takes time and money to prepare.

### Stakeholders

| Stakeholder | Gain | Cost or risk |
|---|---|---|
| Owners | Profit, market position | Investment; failure risk |
| Employees | Training, secure jobs | Redundancy, relocation, pressure |
| Customers | Quality, speed, price, choice | Higher prices or less choice |
| Suppliers | Bigger or long-term contracts | Pressure on price and standards |
| Community | Local jobs, less waste | Closures, traffic, pollution |

## Worked reminder

Westbrook Furniture (invented), five-year comparison.

- Strategy A (cost leadership): invest £400,000; full success £130,000 a year (0.6), partial £50,000 (0.4). Expected annual benefit **£98,000**; five-year net **£90,000**; expected payback 4.1 years.
- Strategy B (quality and flexibility): invest £150,000; full £60,000 a year (0.8), partial £25,000 (0.2). Expected annual benefit **£53,000**; five-year net **£115,000**; expected payback 2.8 years.
- A has the better best case (£250,000 against £150,000) but a worst case of −£150,000. B is preferred on expected value and risk.

## Monitoring against targets

| Measure | How to calculate |
|---|---|
| Cost gap | (actual − target) ÷ target × 100 |
| On-time delivery gap | Target % − actual % (percentage points) |
| Defect rate gap | Actual % − target % (percentage points) |

Example: unit cost £44.10 against a target of £42.00 is 2.10 ÷ 42.00 × 100 = **5.0%** above target. Find the causes before changing the strategy; review it when external conditions change.

## Recommending a strategy: eight steps

1. Start from the business's objectives.
2. Identify the priority performance objective.
3. Choose consistent decisions (method, capacity, technology, location, quality).
4. Count cost and risk (payback or expected value).
5. Consider external influences.
6. Consider people.
7. Set measures and review dates.
8. Justify with evidence and say what the recommendation depends on.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Operations strategy | Long-term plan for the operations function |
| Corporate objective | A goal for the whole business |
| Functional strategy | Plan for one function, such as operations |
| Competitive advantage | A reason customers choose this business over rivals |
| Expected value | Probability-weighted average outcome |
| KPI | Key performance indicator used to monitor progress |
| Trade-off | Gaining in one area by accepting a loss in another |

## Exam traps

- Link the strategy to the objectives of the business, not to operations in general.
- Do not recommend cost cutting for a luxury brand, or the reverse.
- Strategy is more than cost; consider quality, speed, dependability and flexibility.
- Show the risk and cost of implementation.
- People carry out the strategy; mention training and consultation.
- State what the recommendation depends on.

## Self-test

1. Define an operations strategy.
2. Name the five performance objectives.
3. Why might cost and flexibility conflict?
4. Strategy X costs £100,000. It brings £50,000 a year (probability 0.5) or £20,000 a year (probability 0.5). Calculate the expected annual benefit.
5. Calculate the expected payback period for Strategy X.
6. Unit cost is £30.00 against a target of £28.00. Calculate the percentage gap.
7. Give two reasons why a strategy may fail.
8. Name two stakeholders likely to be affected by offshoring and say how.
9. Why must operations, marketing and finance strategies be consistent?
10. When should a business review its operations strategy?

### Answers

1. A long-term plan for how operations will use resources to support the objectives of the whole business.
2. Cost, quality, speed, dependability and flexibility.
3. Low cost usually comes from standard methods and high volume, whereas flexibility requires spare capacity and varied methods, which raise cost.
4. (0.5 × £50,000) + (0.5 × £20,000) = £25,000 + £10,000 = **£35,000**.
5. £100,000 ÷ £35,000 = **2.9 years**.
6. (30 − 28) ÷ 28 × 100 = **7.1%**.
7. Any two: forecasts were wrong; the market changed; staff did not support it; implementation was costly or delayed.
8. UK employees (job losses) and customers (cheaper prices but possibly longer delivery times).
9. A strategy in one function can undermine another, for example a growth plan without the finance to fund the capacity.
10. When external conditions change (law, costs, competitors), or when monitoring shows targets are not met.
