## Questions

**1.** State two conditions necessary for wage discrimination. **[2]**

**2.** In Country B's market for kitchen workers, the demand for labour is L = 1,400 − 60w and the supply is L = 40w + 400, with L counting workers.

**(a)** Find the wage and number of workers when the market clears. **[2]**
**(b)** A legal floor of cr 12 is set. Find how many are employed and how many more want work than can find it. **[2]**
**(c)** Work out the percentage change in the total wage bill, and say what it suggests about how responsive demand for labour is. **[2]**

**3.** An employer in a monopsony labour market faces supply w = 3 + 0.005L, where L is the number of workers. The marginal revenue product of labour is MRP = 15 − 0.005L.

**(a)** Calculate employment and the wage in the absence of regulation. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate the effect of a minimum wage of cr 9. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate employment and unemployment if the minimum wage is cr 12. **[2]**

**4.** A restaurant has weekly costs of cr 6,000 and weekly revenue of cr 6,600. Staff paid the minimum wage cost cr 1,600 a week and staff just above it cost cr 800. The minimum wage rises by 5% and the second group receives 2%.

**(a)** Calculate the extra weekly cost. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the price rise needed to protect profit, assuming the same sales volume. **[2]**

**5.** Explain two reasons why employment may not fall after a national minimum wage is introduced. **[4]**

**6.** Evaluate the case for a national minimum wage. **[10]**

**7.** In a firm, average hourly pay is cr 25.00 for men and cr 21.50 for women. Women in the same grade and working the same hours earn an average of cr 24.00.

**(a)** Express the gap between men's and women's pay as a percentage of men's pay. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the like-for-like gap. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the share of the gap that is explained by grade and hours. **[1]**
**(d)** Explain why the unexplained gap does not prove discrimination. **[1]**

**8.** An employer pays two groups, P and Q, and each worker adds cr 28 to revenue per hour, whichever group they belong to. Group P supply is w = 8 + 0.1L. Group Q supply is w = 2 + 0.2L.

**(a)** Calculate the employment and wage for each group when the employer maximises profit. **[4]**
**(b)** Calculate the pay gap and use elasticities of supply to explain why it arises. **[2]**
**(c)** Calculate the employer's extra revenue over wage cost from paying less than the value of output. **[2]**

**9.** Explain how discrimination may affect the types of job that a group can obtain. **[4]**

**10.** Assess the advantages and disadvantages of wage discrimination for workers, employers and the economy. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** Any two: the employer has market power over wages (monopsony) [1]; the groups can be identified and kept apart, with different elasticities of labour supply or limited mobility [1] **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* name the condition and give the reason it matters; "prejudice" alone is thin because in a competitive market discrimination would be competed away.

**2. (a)** 1,400 − 60w = 40w + 400, so 100w = 1,000 [1], w = cr 10 and L = 800 [1] **[2]**
**(b)** Employment is the demand figure: 1,400 − 720 = 680 [1]. Supply is 40 × 12 + 400 = 880, so excess supply is 200 [1] **[2]**
**(c)** Bill goes from 10 × 800 = 8,000 to 12 × 680 = 8,160, up 2% [1]. Employment falls 15% for a 20% rise in the wage, so demand is inelastic (−0.75) [1] **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* show both equations being used so your method is visible, and give the elasticity as a ratio with its sign.

**3. (a)** Marginal cost of labour is 3 + 0.01L [1]. Setting it equal to MRP gives 12 = 0.015L, so L = 800 [1]. Wage from supply is 3 + 4 = cr 7 [1] **[3]**
**(b)** Cr 9 is the competitive wage (MRP = supply gives L = 1,200 at cr 9) [1], so employment rises from 800 to 1,200 [1] **[2]**
**(c)** MRP = 12 gives L = 600 [1]; at cr 12 supply is 1,800, so unemployment is 1,200 [1] **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* the wage must be read from the supply curve, not from MRP; quoting the MRP as the wage paid is a common way to lose credit.

**4. (a)** 1,600 × 5% = 80 and 800 × 2% = 16 [1], total cr 96 [1] **[2]**
**(b)** 96 ÷ 6,600 [1] = 1.45% [1] **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* choose the base for a percentage carefully (revenue for price, costs for cost share) and say which you used.

**5.** First, monopsony: the employer pays below MRP, so a floor between the monopsony wage and the competitive wage raises employment [1] because the wage is no longer rising with each hire [1]. Second, productivity: a higher wage raises motivation and lowers turnover [1], so output per worker rises, which offsets the cost and keeps demand for labour [1] **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* each reason needs a mechanism, so a list of three bare labels earns less than two developed chains.

**6.** Competitive model: a floor above equilibrium creates excess supply, so jobs fall [1]; higher costs lead to substitution towards capital [1]. Under monopsony, employment can rise if the floor is below the competitive wage [1]. Gains: higher incomes for the lowest paid [1], better work incentives and lower in-work benefit costs [1], possible productivity gains [1]. Costs: cost-push price rises [1]. Poverty is measured by household and the NMW may miss poor households and benefit some who are not poor [1]. Judgement: depends on elasticity of demand for labour, the level of the floor relative to typical pay and the time period [1], with a supported conclusion, for example that a modest floor is likely to help more than harm [1] **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* a strong evaluation says what the conclusion depends on and then commits to a judgement; a list of pros and cons followed by "overall it depends" does not.

**7. (a)** (25 − 21.5) ÷ 25 = 14% [1]
**(b)** (25 − 24) ÷ 25 = 4% [1]
**(c)** (24 − 21.5) ÷ (25 − 21.5) = 2.5 ÷ 3.5 = 71.4% [1]
**(d)** Other unmeasured factors such as career breaks, qualifications or negotiation may explain the rest [1] **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* write the formula with the figures substituted, and say what the percentage is of.

**8. (a)** P: marginal cost 8 + 0.2L = 28, so L = 100 [1] and w = 8 + 10 = cr 18 [1]. Q: 2 + 0.4L = 28, so L = 65 [1] and w = 2 + 13 = cr 15 [1] **[4]**
**(b)** Gap = (18 − 15) ÷ 18 = 16.7% [1]. Elasticity of supply is 18 ÷ (0.1 × 100) = 1.8 for P and 15 ÷ (0.2 × 65) = 1.15 for Q, so Q's less elastic supply allows a lower wage [1] **[2]**
**(c)** P: (28 − 18) × 100 = 1,000 and Q: (28 − 15) × 65 = 845 [1], total cr 1,845 per hour [1] **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* set marginal cost, not the supply curve, equal to MRP, then read the wage off supply; mixing these up is an easy slip on this model.

**9.** Occupational segregation: a group is steered into lower-paid sectors or roles, for example through stereotypes [1], so their earnings and options are lower than for equally able others [1]. Glass ceiling or statistical discrimination: employers judge people by group averages and overlook them for promotion [1], leading to part-time, insecure or overqualified employment [1] **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* name the type of employment affected, not just that pay is lower.

**10.** Workers: the favoured group gains higher pay [1], while the disadvantaged group loses pay, jobs, training and motivation [1]. Employers: lower costs and higher profit [1], but only with market power over wages [1]; risks include legal action, reputation damage and lost talent [1]. Economy: talent is misallocated and output falls below potential [1]; inequality widens and tax receipts and demand are lower [1]. A possible advantage is that perfect discrimination can hire the competitive number of workers, though the employer takes the surplus [1]. Evaluation: employer gains are short-run and depend on weak enforcement [1]; overall the disadvantages outweigh the advantages, because gains go to a few while the losses are spread widely [1] **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* assess for each of the three groups named in the question, and give a final judgement that follows from your points.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using the competitive diagram when the question describes a monopsony.
- Reading the wage from the MRP curve instead of the supply curve.
- Calling every pay difference discrimination.
- Leaving out who gains and who loses from a policy.
- Listing effects of the NMW with no chain from wage to cost to outcome.
- Calculating a percentage change from the wrong base.
- Writing "it depends" without saying on what.
- Forgetting that wage discrimination needs market power.
