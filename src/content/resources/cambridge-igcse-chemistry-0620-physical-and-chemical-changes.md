This guide covers subtopic **6.1 Physical and chemical changes** in Topic 6, Chemical
reactions, for Cambridge IGCSE Chemistry 0620 (2026–2028 series). The broader page
[Physical and Chemical Changes](/resources/physical-and-chemical-changes/) sets out the
evidence for a chemical change with everyday examples. This page goes further: it explains
the difference **at particle level**, applies it to processes you meet elsewhere in the 0620
course, and deals with the tricky cases where both kinds of change happen together.

## What the syllabus asks for

**Core** (there is no Supplement content in 6.1)

- **Identify** physical and chemical changes, and **describe the differences** between
  them.

Two skills are tested: deciding which type of change a described process is (with a
reason), and stating how the two types differ. Questions often sit inside other topics —
a question on crude oil or on salt preparation may ask which step is a physical change.

## The core difference

- In a **physical change**, **no new substance is formed**. The substance may change state,
  shape or how it is spread out (for example by dissolving), but its chemical identity is
  the same before and after.
- In a **chemical change** (a chemical reaction), **one or more new substances are formed**,
  with different properties from the starting substances.

## What is happening to the particles

The particle picture explains *why* the two kinds of change behave differently.

**Physical change.** The particles themselves are unchanged. What changes is their
**arrangement, spacing or movement**:

- melting: particles in a regular arrangement start to move past each other;
- boiling: particles separate completely;
- dissolving: solute particles spread out between solvent particles.

No bonds *within* the particles are broken. When water boils, H₂O molecules move apart, but
each one is still H₂O.

**Chemical change.** **Bonds are broken and new bonds are made**. The atoms are
**rearranged** into new combinations, so different particles exist at the end. When
methane burns, C–H and O=O bonds break and C=O and O–H bonds form, giving new molecules of
carbon dioxide and water.

In **both** kinds of change atoms are neither created nor destroyed, so **total mass is
conserved** in a closed system.

## The differences, side by side

| Feature | Physical change | Chemical change |
|---|---|---|
| New substance formed? | No | Yes |
| Particles | Same particles, rearranged or spread out | Atoms rearranged into new particles |
| Bonds within particles | Not broken | Broken and new bonds made |
| Chemical properties | Unchanged | New substance has different properties |
| Reversal | Usually reversed by a simple physical method (cooling, evaporating) | Usually needs another chemical reaction to reverse |
| Equation | Same formula on both sides, e.g. H₂O(l) → H₂O(g) | Different formulae on each side |
| Mass in a closed system | Conserved | Conserved |

The equation row is a fast test: if the formula is the same on both sides with only a state
symbol changing, the change is physical.

## Applying it across the course

Many 0620 processes can be classified with this test.

| Process | Type | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Filtration of sand from water | Physical | Separates substances that are already there |
| Simple distillation of sea water | Physical | Water evaporates and condenses; still H₂O |
| Fractional distillation of crude oil | Physical | Separates existing hydrocarbons by boiling point |
| Chromatography of food dyes | Physical | Dyes separate unchanged |
| Crystallisation of a salt from solution | Physical | Dissolved salt comes out of solution unchanged |
| Cracking of long-chain alkanes | Chemical | New, shorter alkanes and alkenes form |
| Fermentation of glucose | Chemical | Ethanol and carbon dioxide form |
| Electrolysis of molten lead(II) bromide | Chemical | Lead and bromine form from the compound |
| Heating copper(II) carbonate | Chemical | Green solid turns black (copper(II) oxide) and CO₂ is given off |
| Neutralisation of an acid | Chemical | Salt and water form |

Notice that all the **separation techniques** in Topic 12 are physical changes: they only
separate substances that were already present. That is why they can be used to obtain a
pure substance without changing it.

## Worked example 1: iron and sulfur

*Iron filings and powdered sulfur are mixed. Part of the mixture is then heated strongly
and glows red, leaving a black solid that is not attracted to a magnet.*

- **Mixing:** physical. Both substances are still present; the iron can be removed with a
  magnet and the mixture has the properties of its parts.
- **Heating:** chemical. A **new substance**, iron(II) sulfide, forms. It has **different
  properties** (not magnetic), the glow shows energy is given out, and iron can no longer
  be removed with a magnet.

## Worked example 2: cases where both changes happen

**A burning candle.** The wax **melts** near the flame (physical: solid wax becomes liquid
wax). Wax vapour then **burns** (chemical: carbon dioxide and water form). A full answer
names both.

**Heating hydrated copper(II) sulfate.** Blue crystals turn to a white powder and water is
given off. This is a **chemical change**: the water of crystallisation is removed and a
different substance, anhydrous copper(II) sulfate, forms. Adding water turns it blue again
and the mixture gets warm — another chemical change. This example shows that a chemical
change **can** be reversed; what makes it chemical is the new substance, not how hard it is
to undo.

## Annotated model answer

*Question: Describe two differences between a physical change and a chemical change.* **[2]**

> In a chemical change a **new substance is formed**, but in a physical change no new
> substance is formed ✓. Physical changes can usually be **reversed by simple physical
> methods** such as cooling, whereas chemical changes usually cannot ✓.

Each point is a **comparison**: it says what happens in each type of change. Writing only
"a chemical change makes a new substance" describes one type and may earn nothing for a
"differences" question.

## Common mistakes

- **Classifying by appearance alone.** Bubbles appear in boiling water (physical) and in
  acid–carbonate reactions (chemical). Ask whether a new substance forms.
- **Calling a colour change automatically chemical.** It is strong evidence, but check what
  causes it.
- **Calling dissolving chemical** because the solid disappears.
- **Saying mass changes in a chemical reaction.** In a closed system it is conserved; an
  apparent loss means a gas escaped.
- **Ignoring one stage** of a two-stage process.
- **One-sided "difference" answers** that describe only one type of change.

## Exam technique: command words

- **Identify**: name the type of change, then give **one reason** linked to the description
  ("chemical — a black solid that is a new substance forms").
- **Describe the differences**: make **comparative** statements covering both types.
- **Suggest**: for an unfamiliar process, apply the new-substance test and quote the
  evidence in the question.

## Quick checklist

- Definition of each type of change
- Particle-level difference: arrangement versus rearrangement of atoms
- Classifying separation techniques (physical) and reactions (chemical)
- Two-stage processes such as a burning candle
