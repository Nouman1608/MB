## Section A

**1.** Define ethics in the context of accounting. **[2]**

**2.** State **two** ethical principles that an accountant should follow. **[2]**

**3.** Explain why an action can be legal but still unethical. **[2]**

**4.** A business has a profit of 22,000 before the following. The owner asks the bookkeeper to record repairs of 8,000 as an addition to the cost of a machine. The machine is depreciated at 20% of cost. Calculate the profit if the request is followed and by how much it is overstated. **[4]**

**5.** Closing inventory is overvalued by 3,000. Current assets with the error are 33,000 and current liabilities are 15,000. Calculate the current ratio with and without the error, and state the effect of the error on next year's profit. **[4]**

## Section B

**6.** Explain what is meant by a conflict of interest and give an example for an accountant. **[3]**

**7.** Dev Rao is the accountant of Sunbeam Foods. The owner is about to apply for a bank loan. A customer has made a legal claim of 30,000 against the business that Dev believes is likely to succeed. The owner tells Dev not to mention it in the accounts. Without the claim, profit is 26,000 and net assets are 140,000.

**(a)** Calculate the profit and net assets if a provision is made. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain **two** ethical principles that are at risk if Dev follows the owner's instruction. **[4]**

**(c)** Recommend what Dev should do, giving reasons. **[6]**

**8.** Explain what is meant by window dressing and why it is unethical. **[3]**

**9.** Evaluate the statement: "An accountant should always follow the instructions of the owner, because the owner pays the accountant." **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** Ethics are the moral principles that decide what is right and wrong [1]; in accounting they guide how information is prepared, presented and used honestly and fairly [1].

**2.** Any two: integrity [1]; objectivity [1]; professional competence and care [1]; confidentiality [1]; professional behaviour [1].

**3.** The law sets minimum rules, but some actions that break no law are still dishonest or unfair [1]; for example, presenting figures in a misleading way may be allowed by law but would breach honesty and integrity [1].

**4.** Depreciation 8,000 × 20% = 1,600 [1]; profit if the request is followed 22,000 + 8,000 − 1,600 [1] = **28,400** [1]; overstated by 28,400 − 22,000 = **6,400** [1].

**5.** Current ratio with the error 33,000 : 15,000 = **2.2 : 1** [1]; without the error current assets are 30,000, so 30,000 : 15,000 = **2.0 : 1** [1]; the error makes the business look more liquid than it is [1]; next year's opening inventory is too high, so next year's profit is understated by 3,000 [1].

**6.** A conflict of interest arises when a person's own interests, or duties to another party, could affect or appear to affect their judgement [1]. Example: an accountant advising two clients who are competing for the same contract [1], or valuing a business that the accountant wants to buy [1]. (Any suitable example, 2 marks for the definition and 1 for the example.)

**7. (a)** Profit 26,000 − 30,000 = **−4,000** (a loss of 4,000) [1]; net assets 140,000 − 30,000 = **110,000** [1].

**(b)** Integrity: leaving out the claim would be dishonest, because it hides a known liability [1]; it would mislead the bank [1]. Objectivity: Dev would be letting pressure from the owner override his own judgement [1], so the accounts would no longer be fair [1]. (Professional competence and the prudence concept are also acceptable.)

**(c)** Recommendation: Dev should refuse to leave out the claim and make a provision of 30,000 under the prudence concept [1]. He should explain to the owner that omitting it would overstate profit by 30,000 and net assets by 30,000 and could mislead the bank into lending money that is at risk [1]. If the owner persists, Dev should record his objection in writing and take advice from his professional body or a senior colleague [1], and consider whether he can continue to act [1]. The risks of following the owner are legal action, loss of professional standing and a loss to the bank [1]. The cost of refusing may be losing the client, but this is outweighed by the long-term cost of dishonest accounts, and the owner could discuss the claim openly with the bank to seek the loan on honest terms [1].

**8.** Window dressing is arranging transactions around the year end so that the statement of financial position looks better than it normally is [1], for example by delaying payments to improve the cash balance [1]. It is unethical because users are misled about the true position [1].

**9.** For following the owner: the owner is the client and has a right to make decisions about the business [1]; an accountant who refuses may lose the client [1]. Against: accountants have a duty of integrity and objectivity that cannot be overridden by the client [1]; the accounts are used by others, such as banks and tax authorities, who rely on honest figures [1]; following an unethical instruction could lead to legal action against the accountant [1]. Judgement: the accountant should follow lawful instructions about the business but must refuse any instruction that requires dishonest figures [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing that "it is illegal" without saying what is wrong with the figures (Question 7).
- Naming a principle without linking it to the case (Question 7(b)).
- Forgetting that the error in inventory reverses in the next year (Question 5).
- Listing actions without a reason, such as "tell the bank" with no explanation.
- Ignoring the pressure on the accountant when recommending a course of action.

## Approaching these questions

Identify the facts, the principle at risk and the people affected. Use figures from the case. Weigh at least two courses of action, choose one and justify it, and say what the accountant should do first if the first step does not work.
