**This guide covers one optional kind of coursework assignment in Component 5.** The syllabus states:
"One of the assignments may be an empathic response to a prose or drama text." The portfolio
contains two assignments of 600–1200 words, each on a different text, and each title "must be
phrased to allow scope for the assessment of all four assessment objectives". The
[Component 5 coursework study guide](/resources/cambridge-igcse-english-literature-component-5-coursework-study-guide/)
covers the whole portfolio, its rules and the teacher's role; this page explains how to write the
empathic piece well. Because coursework texts are chosen by your centre, the worked example uses an
invented novel.

## What an empathic response is

An empathic response is written in the voice of a character from a novel, short story or play, at a
particular moment in the text. A common form of task is: "You are [character] at [moment]. Write your
thoughts." It is still literary criticism, but carried out from inside the text: your understanding of
the character, the situation and the writer's methods is shown through what the character thinks,
notices, remembers and fears.

Note the limits the syllabus sets:

- **Only one** of the two assignments may be empathic.
- It must be on a **prose or drama** text, not poetry.
- The text must be one the candidate has studied in full, equivalent in scope and demand to the set
  texts.

## How the levels describe an empathic task

The Component 5 assessment criteria adjust the wording of AO1 and AO3 for empathic work. At Level 8
(23–25 marks), the candidate "seamlessly uses well-selected textual detail in an empathic task" (AO1)
and "sustains an entirely convincing voice for the character in an empathic task" (AO3). At Level 6
the voice is "generally authentic"; at Level 4 the candidate "begins to assume a voice for the
character". AO2 (critical understanding of the text) and AO4 (personal and evaluative engagement)
apply as for any assignment.

So two things carry the marks: **voice** and **detail**, with understanding and engagement shown
through how you use them.

## Step 1: choose the moment

The best moments are those where the character has something urgent to think about and the text
leaves room for reflection:

- just after a discovery, a quarrel, a decision or a loss;
- at a turning point where the character's view of another person is changing;
- at a gap in the text, such as a night that the narrative passes over.

Avoid moments where nothing is at stake, and moments so late that the character would simply
summarise the plot. Decide exactly what the character knows at that point and what they do not.
Hindsight is the commonest error: a character cannot refer to events that have not yet happened.

## Step 2: build the voice

A convincing voice comes from close study of how the character speaks in the text:

- **Vocabulary:** formal or colloquial; the words and phrases they use repeatedly.
- **Sentence patterns:** long and controlled, or short and broken.
- **Attitude:** proud, anxious, sarcastic, devout, self-deceiving.
- **Period and place:** a character from an older text should not use modern idioms.
- **Blind spots:** what this character fails to see about themselves; letting those show is a sign
  of critical understanding.

Make a short voice sheet before drafting: ten words or phrases the character uses, three typical
sentence shapes, and two things they would never say.

## Step 3: weave in textual detail

Detail must be integrated, not listed. The character remembers specific events, images and words
from the text naturally, as part of their thinking. Echo key phrases where the character would
plausibly recall them, but do not quote long passages; a character does not think in quotations.

## Step 4: give the piece a shape

A strong empathic piece moves somewhere. A useful structure:

1. **Immediate reaction** to the moment.
2. **Memory:** the character returns to earlier events that the moment changes.
3. **Struggle:** conflicting feelings or interpretations.
4. **Where the character ends:** a decision, a fear, a resolution or an unresolved question,
   consistent with what happens next in the text.

## Worked example: an invented novel

*The Salt Road* is an invented novel used for this example. Its central character, Leila, has
always believed her father to be a heroic sea captain lost in a storm. Halfway through the novel she
finds letters in his trunk revealing that he survived and chose not to come home. The next chapter
skips forward a week.

**Task:** You are Leila on the night you find the letters. Write your thoughts.

**Voice sheet notes:** Leila is seventeen, proud, fond of the sea words her father taught her
("steady as she goes", "tide"); in the novel she speaks in quick, confident sentences that falter when
she is hurt; she idolises her father and dismisses her mother's caution.

**Extract from a strong response:**

> Steady as she goes. That is what he used to say, his hand over mine on the tiller, and I believed
> that steadiness was something he had and would give me. Eleven letters. Eleven, tied with the same
> blue cord he used to mend nets. The storm was in March, Mother always said, and I always hated how
> she said it, flat, as though she were reading the tide tables. Now I know why. She was not grieving.
> She was waiting for me to stop believing. Stop. I will not cry over him, not over a man who sends a
> letter from a harbour three days' sail away and does not sail.

**Why it scores highly:**
- *Voice:* the father's phrase "Steady as she goes", the sea vocabulary and the confident rhythm
  breaking into "Stop." show the character in the text, under strain.
- *Detail:* the tiller, the net cord and the mother's flat way of speaking would all be details from
  the novel, used as memories rather than listed.
- *Understanding (AO2):* her sudden re-reading of her mother shows the novel's turning point from
  inside the character.
- *No hindsight:* she does not refer to anything she learns in the following chapters.

## The commentary-free rule

An empathic piece should not step outside the voice to explain itself ("This shows Leila feels
betrayed"). Everything is shown through the character. If your centre asks for a brief rationale or
plan, follow its instructions, but keep the piece itself in role.

## Common mistakes

- **Retelling the plot** in the first person.
- **Modern slang** in a character from an older or very formal text.
- **Hindsight**: referring to later events the character could not know.
- **Inventing major events** that contradict the text.
- **A generic voice** that could belong to any character.
- **Listing quotations** instead of weaving in detail.
- **Exceeding or falling far short of** the 600–1200 word guidance, which the syllabus says can
  disadvantage candidates.

## Drafting and authenticity

The syllabus says candidates "can draft and redraft work", while teachers "should only give brief
summative comments on progress during this drafting phase". It also states that candidates "must not
use online tools which act as writing assistants". The voice must be your own creation from your own
reading.
