This study guide teaches section **4.2.2.7 Cancer** of the AQA GCSE Biology (8461) specification, for teaching from September 2016 and exams from 2018 onwards. It is Paper 1 content at Foundation and Higher Tier, and nothing in 4.2.2.7 is marked (HT only).

The [cancer revision notes](/resources/aqa-gcse-biology-8461-cancer-revision-notes/) give the definitions and the benign-malignant comparison in brief. This guide teaches the ideas behind them: how cancer connects to the cell cycle, why uncontrolled division produces a tumour so quickly, exactly how a malignant tumour spreads, and how lifestyle and genetic risk factors combine. Then test yourself with the [cancer practice questions](/resources/aqa-gcse-biology-8461-cancer-practice/). Course hub: [AQA GCSE Biology](/boards/aqa/gcse/biology/).

## What the specification asks for

Section 4.2.2.7 states:

- Students should be able to "describe cancer as the result of changes in cells that lead to uncontrolled growth and division."
- "Benign tumours are growths of abnormal cells which are contained in one area, usually within a membrane. They do not invade other parts of the body."
- "Malignant tumour cells are cancers. They invade neighbouring tissues and spread to different parts of the body in the blood where they form secondary tumours."
- "Scientists have identified lifestyle risk factors for various types of cancer. There are also genetic risk factors for some cancers."

Two other sections feed into this one: 4.2.2.5 (viruses living in cells can trigger cancers) and 4.2.2.6 (carcinogens, including ionising radiation, as risk factors in cancer; smoking and lung cancer).

## Cancer and the cell cycle

In Topic 1 you met the cell cycle: a cell grows, copies its DNA, then divides by mitosis into two identical cells. In healthy tissue this is **controlled**: cells divide when new cells are needed for growth or repair, and stop when they are not.

Cancer begins when **changes in a cell** (changes to its DNA) remove that control. The cell divides again and again, and so do all the cells made from it. The result is a mass of abnormal cells, a **tumour**.

### Worked example 1: how fast uncontrolled division adds up

Suppose (invented figures) one abnormal cell divides every 3 days and every new cell keeps dividing.

```
Number of divisions in 30 days = 30 / 3 = 10
Number of cells = 2^10 = 1024
```

After 60 days there would be 2^20, over a million cells. Real tumours grow more irregularly, but the calculation shows why uncontrolled division matters: the number doubles each time, not increases by a fixed amount. A common error is to calculate 2 × 10 = 20 instead of 2¹⁰.

## Benign and malignant tumours compared

| Feature | Benign tumour | Malignant tumour |
|---|---|---|
| Cells | Abnormal cells | Cancer cells |
| Location | Contained in one area | Invade neighbouring tissues |
| Boundary | Usually within a membrane | Not contained |
| Spread | Does not invade other parts of the body | Spreads in the blood to other parts of the body |
| Further tumours | None | Secondary tumours |
| Is it cancer? | No | Yes |

### Why a benign tumour can still be harmful

"Benign" does not mean harmless. A benign tumour keeps growing in its one area, so it can **press on nearby organs or blood vessels**. A benign tumour in the brain, for example, can press on brain tissue in the confined space of the skull. The key difference is that it does not invade or spread.

### How a malignant tumour spreads, step by step

1. Cells in the malignant tumour divide uncontrollably.
2. They **invade neighbouring tissues**, growing into them.
3. Some cells break away and enter the **blood**.
4. The blood carries them to other parts of the body.
5. They settle in a new tissue or organ and keep dividing, forming a **secondary tumour**.

A secondary tumour is made of the same kind of cancer cells as the original tumour, now growing in a new place. Because they can appear in vital organs, secondary tumours make malignant cancers especially dangerous.

### Annotated model answer

*Question (4 marks): A person has a malignant tumour in the bowel. Some months later, a tumour is found in the liver. Explain how this happened.*

> Cells from the malignant bowel tumour invaded neighbouring tissue (**invasion**). Some cancer cells broke away and entered the blood (**route**). The blood carried them to the liver (**transport**), where they divided uncontrollably to form a secondary tumour (**outcome, using the key term**).

Four marking points, four steps. "The cancer spread to the liver" restates the question and scores little.

## Risk factors for cancer

A risk factor raises the chance of developing a cancer; it does not make cancer certain.

**Lifestyle risk factors** (identified by scientists for various cancers):

- smoking, linked to lung cancer, because tar contains carcinogens;
- exposure to carcinogens, including ionising radiation, which can damage DNA.

**Genetic risk factors** (for some cancers):

- some people inherit gene variants that raise their risk; for example, inherited faulty BRCA1 or BRCA2 genes raise the risk of breast cancer.

**Viruses** (from 4.2.2.5): viruses living in cells can trigger cancers, for example some types of human papillomavirus (HPV) and cervical cancer.

Because many diseases are caused by an interaction of factors, a person with a genetic risk factor may never develop cancer, and a person without one may.

### Worked example 2: comparing two groups

A study (invented data) followed 30 000 workers exposed to a chemical at work and 30 000 similar workers who were not exposed. Over 20 years, 45 exposed workers and 9 unexposed workers developed a particular cancer.

```
Exposed:   45 / 30 000 x 100 000 = 150 per 100 000
Unexposed:  9 / 30 000 x 100 000 =  30 per 100 000
150 / 30 = 5, so exposed workers were 5 times as likely to develop the cancer
```

To decide whether the chemical is a carcinogen, scientists would also look for a mechanism (does it damage DNA?), a dose-response pattern, and other studies with the same result. The groups should be similar in age and smoking habits, so those factors do not explain the difference.

## Exam technique

- **Describe** cancer: "changes in cells that lead to uncontrolled growth and division". Keep the specification's words.
- **Compare** tumours: matched points ("benign tumours are contained in one area, whereas malignant tumours invade neighbouring tissues").
- **Explain** spread: invade → blood → secondary tumour.
- **Give** risk factors: name the type (lifestyle or genetic) and an example.
- **Calculate**: rates per 100 000, or cell numbers using powers of 2.

## Common errors

- Writing that cancer is "caused by a virus" in all cases, or that it is communicable.
- Saying benign tumours are cancer, or that they are always harmless.
- Saying malignant cells spread "through the body" without naming the blood.
- Calling the new growth a "second cancer" instead of a secondary tumour.
- Treating a risk factor as a certain cause.
- Using 2 × n instead of 2ⁿ for repeated division.
