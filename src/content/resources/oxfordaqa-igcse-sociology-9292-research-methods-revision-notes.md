## One topic, two papers

Research methods is **Section C (16 marks) of both papers**. **Paper 1**: short questions plus a structured question on **data about an unseen issue of global sociological significance**. **Paper 2**: short and extended response questions, usually applying methods to Paper 2 topics. Same content; Paper 1 tests reading data, Paper 2 tests extended evaluation.

## Approaches (3.1.3.1)

| Positivist | Interpretivist |
|---|---|
| Sociology as science | Understanding meanings |
| Quantitative data | Qualitative data |
| Questionnaires, structured interviews, official statistics | Unstructured interviews, participant observation, personal documents |
| Strong on reliability | Strong on validity |

**Primary** = collected by the researcher. **Secondary** = already exists.

## Methods (3.1.3.2)

| Method | Strength | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Postal or online survey | Large, cheap, wide reach | Low response rate |
| Telephone survey | Quick replies | Intrusive; people hang up |
| Structured interview | Reliable, comparable | Little depth; interviewer bias |
| Unstructured interview | Valid, detailed | Slow; hard to repeat |
| Group interview | Shared views emerge | Some voices dominate |
| Overt participant observation | Real behaviour in context | Hawthorne effect |
| Covert participant observation | Avoids Hawthorne effect | Deception; risky; hard to record |
| Non-participant observation | Researcher stays detached | Misses meanings |
| Laboratory experiment | Control of variables | Artificial |
| Field experiment | Natural setting | Less control; consent issues |
| Longitudinal study | Shows change over time | Costly; people drop out |
| Personal documents | Insight into feelings | Unrepresentative; may be selective |
| Official statistics | Cheap; whole population | Collected for other purposes |

**Triangulation**: combine methods to check findings. **Question types**: closed, open, scaled.

### Methods in named studies

Linking a method to a study you already know from other topics gives you a ready-made example.

| Study | Method | What it shows about the method |
|---|---|---|
| Durkheim, suicide | Official statistics (secondary, quantitative) | The positivist search for patterns and causes across whole populations |
| Oakley, housework | Interviews with housewives | Interviews give access to experiences that are hard to observe directly |
| Willis, "the lads" | Observation combined with interviews | Ethnography reveals a group's own meanings, but from a small, unrepresentative group |
| Rosenthal and Jacobson, teacher expectations | Field experiment in a school | Real setting raises validity, but teachers were misled, which raises ethical questions |

### The three angles for evaluating any method

- **Practical**: time, cost, access to the group, and whether the researcher has the skills (for example, could they join a group to observe it?).
- **Ethical**: informed consent, deception, confidentiality, anonymity, possible harm, and extra care with children and vulnerable people.
- **Theoretical**: does it give **valid** data (a true picture) or **reliable** data (repeatable)? Is the sample **representative** enough to **generalise**? Would a positivist or an interpretivist prefer it?

Always apply the angle to the topic in the question: a postal questionnaire about domestic violence raises different problems from one about favourite school subjects.

## Issues (3.1.3.3)

- **Operationalisation**: making a concept measurable.
- **Population** (whole group) and **sampling frame** (list used to sample).
- **Samples**: random, systematic, stratified random, quota, multistage, snowball.
- **Validity** (true picture) v **reliability** (repeatable).
- **Bias**: researcher or interviewer bias; Hawthorne effect; social desirability.
- **Practical**: time, funding, access.
- **Ethics**: informed consent, confidentiality, anonymity, wellbeing; with children, parental consent as well.
- **Correlation ≠ causation**.
- **Representativeness** → **generalisability**.

### Choosing a sample: worked example

*A researcher wants 60 pupils from a school of 600 to study attitudes to homework.*

- **Random**: put every name on the register (the sampling frame) into a computer draw and pick 60. Fair, but by chance it could include few younger pupils.
- **Systematic**: take every 10th name on the register (600 divided by 60). Simple, but any repeating pattern in how the list is ordered could bias it.
- **Stratified random**: if half the pupils are girls, randomly choose 30 girls and 30 boys, so the sample matches the school on gender.
- **Quota**: the researcher stops pupils in the corridor until 30 girls and 30 boys have answered. Quick, but the researcher's choice of who to ask can introduce bias.
- **Snowball** would not be needed here, because the population is easy to reach; it suits hidden groups such as people living without fixed addresses.

## Interpreting data (3.1.3.4)

Tables, charts and graphs: title, units, highest and lowest, trend, quantified difference, what is missing. Qualitative extracts: meanings, a short quoted phrase, observer effect.

**A routine for data questions:**

1. Read the title, the units (percentages, numbers, thousands) and the source before looking at any figure.
2. **Identify** exactly what the question asks for: a highest value, a difference, a trend over time.
3. **Quote** the figures you use, with units, and calculate differences rather than writing "much higher".
4. **Describe** the pattern in one sentence ("rose steadily", "fell, then levelled off").
5. **Limitations**: the data shows what, not why; consider who collected it, when, from which sample, and whether definitions changed over time.

For an unseen global issue on Paper 1, link the pattern to concepts you know (for example poverty, gender inequality in access, or the digital divide), but do not claim the data proves a cause.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Sampling frame | The list from which a sample is chosen |
| Snowball sample | Participants recruit further participants |
| Hawthorne effect | Behaviour changes because people know they are studied |
| Social desirability | Giving answers that show oneself in a good light |
| Generalisability | Applying findings beyond the sample |
| Operationalisation | Defining a concept so it can be measured |
| Validity | Data giving a true picture of what is studied |
| Reliability | Getting the same results if the research is repeated |
| Triangulation | Combining methods or sources to check findings |
| Informed consent | Agreeing to take part after being told what the research involves |
| Ethnography | Studying a group's way of life in its natural setting |
| Longitudinal study | Following the same group over a period of time |
| Interviewer bias | Results shaped by the interviewer's presence, tone or characteristics |

## Exam traps

- Reliability and validity reversed: reliable means repeatable; valid means true to life.
- Calling overt observation "unethical" without explaining why; the main ethical issues belong to covert observation.
- Saying official statistics are "always accurate": they reflect how they were defined and collected.
- Evaluating with only "it is expensive" or "it takes time" and no link to the topic or to validity.
- Describing a sample as random when the researcher chose whom to ask; that is closer to a quota sample.
- Copying out data from an item without saying what pattern it shows.
