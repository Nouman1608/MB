These notes condense section 3.2.2 (Digestion and absorption) of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Biology (9610) specification, Version 5.1, in Unit 2, which counts for both the International AS and the International A-level. For worked examples and data analysis, use the [digestion and absorption study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-biology-9610-digestion-and-absorption-oxfordaqa-alevel-biology/), then try the [digestion and absorption practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-biology-9610-digestion-and-absorption-oxfordaqa-alevel-biology-practice/).

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Digestion | Hydrolysis of large molecules by enzymes to produce smaller molecules |
| Hydrolysis | Breaking a chemical bond using a molecule of water |
| Absorption | Movement of the products of digestion across the gut epithelium into the body |
| Assimilation | Use of absorbed molecules to build the body's own cells and molecules |
| Egestion | Removal of undigested food as faeces |
| Emulsification | Splitting large lipid droplets into many small ones, increasing surface area |
| Micelle | Small structure of bile salts with monoglycerides and fatty acids that carries lipid products to the epithelium |
| Chylomicron | Triglycerides packaged with protein inside epithelial cells, released into the lacteal |
| Co-transport | Movement of one substance down its gradient carrying a second substance against its gradient, through the same protein |

## Structure in order (3.2.2.1)

Oesophagus → stomach → duodenum → ileum → colon → rectum. Glands: salivary glands (into the mouth) and pancreas (into the duodenum). Bile salts also reach the duodenum.

## Enzymes, bonds and products (3.2.2.2)

| Food | Enzymes | Bond | End products |
|---|---|---|---|
| Starch | Amylase, then membrane-bound maltase | Glycosidic | Glucose |
| Sucrose | Membrane-bound sucrase | Glycosidic | Glucose and fructose |
| Lactose | Membrane-bound lactase | Glycosidic | Glucose and galactose |
| Triglycerides | Lipase | Ester | Monoglycerides and fatty acids |
| Proteins | Endopeptidases, exopeptidases, membrane-bound dipeptidases | Peptide | Amino acids |

**Protein enzymes work as a team:** endopeptidases cut inside chains and create more ends; exopeptidases remove amino acids from the ends; dipeptidases on the epithelial membrane split dipeptides.

**Bile salts:** emulsify lipids (not enzymes, no bonds broken), raising the surface area for lipase; later form micelles.

## Absorption routes (3.2.2.3)

| Product | Route |
|---|---|
| Glucose, galactose | Co-transport with sodium ions, driven by the sodium gradient; then facilitated diffusion into blood |
| Fructose | Facilitated diffusion |
| Amino acids | Co-transport with sodium ions; then facilitated diffusion into blood |
| Monoglycerides, fatty acids | Micelles carry them to the membrane; they diffuse through the bilayer |

**Co-transport sequence:**

1. Sodium-potassium pump in the basal membrane uses ATP to move sodium ions out of the epithelial cell into the blood.
2. Sodium concentration inside the cell is now lower than in the lumen.
3. Sodium ions diffuse in through a co-transporter protein, bringing glucose or an amino acid with them against its gradient.
4. Glucose leaves the cell into the blood by facilitated diffusion.

**Lipid sequence:** micelles → diffusion into cell → triglycerides reformed (endoplasmic reticulum) → packaged with protein into chylomicrons (Golgi) → exocytosis → lacteal → lymph → blood.

## Ileum adaptations by principle

| Principle | Features |
|---|---|
| Large surface area | Folds, villi, microvilli |
| Short diffusion distance | Single layer of epithelial cells; capillaries close beneath |
| Steep gradient | Blood flow in capillaries; lacteal removes chylomicrons |
| Energy for active processes | Many mitochondria in epithelial cells |
| Uptake and final digestion | Carrier proteins, co-transporters and membrane-bound enzymes |

## Quick calculations

- Bonds in a chain of n monomers = n − 1; water molecules for complete hydrolysis = n − 1.
- Lipase giving a monoglyceride: 2 water molecules per triglyceride.
- Emulsifying a droplet to 1/k of its diameter: k³ droplets, total surface area × k.

## Exam traps

- "Amylase breaks down carbohydrate." Name starch → maltose.
- "Disaccharidases are secreted into the lumen." They are membrane-bound.
- "Bile digests fat." Bile salts emulsify it.
- "Glucose is absorbed by diffusion." Mainly co-transport with sodium ions, especially when the lumen concentration is low.
- "The co-transporter uses ATP." ATP is used by the sodium-potassium pump.
- "Fatty acids are actively transported." They diffuse, being lipid-soluble.
- "Chylomicrons enter the capillaries." They enter the lacteals.
- "Egestion is excretion." Egestion removes undigested food, which never entered cells.

## Quick self-test

1. Define digestion as the specification does.
2. Name the bond hydrolysed by lipase and the products formed.
3. Where are disaccharidases found, and why is this an advantage?
4. Explain how endopeptidases increase the rate of protein digestion.
5. A polypeptide has 120 amino acids. How many water molecules are needed to hydrolyse it completely?
6. Explain why bile salts speed up lipid digestion even though they are not enzymes.
7. A droplet's diameter is reduced to one-fifth by emulsification. By what factor does total surface area increase?
8. Why does glucose absorption fall when a respiratory inhibitor is added?
9. Why can fatty acids cross the epithelial membrane without a carrier protein?
10. Describe what happens to monoglycerides and fatty acids after they enter an epithelial cell.
11. Give two features of a villus that maintain a steep concentration gradient.
12. Distinguish absorption from assimilation.

### Answers

1. The process in which large molecules are hydrolysed by enzymes to produce smaller molecules.
2. Ester bonds; monoglycerides and fatty acids.
3. Bound to the cell-surface membrane of ileum epithelial cells; the products are made beside the transport proteins that absorb them.
4. They hydrolyse peptide bonds within chains, creating more chain ends for exopeptidases to act on.
5. 120 − 1 = **119**.
6. They emulsify lipids into smaller droplets, giving a larger total surface area on which lipase can act.
7. **5 times** (125 droplets, each with 1/25 of the area).
8. No ATP is made, so the sodium-potassium pump stops; the sodium gradient is lost, so co-transport of glucose stops.
9. They are lipid-soluble, so they diffuse through the phospholipid bilayer.
10. They are recombined into triglycerides, packaged with protein into chylomicrons and released by exocytosis into the lacteal.
11. Blood flowing through the capillaries removes absorbed molecules; the lacteal carries away chylomicrons.
12. Absorption is the movement of digested products into the body's cells and blood; assimilation is their use to build the body's own cells and molecules.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the difference between an endopeptidase and an exopeptidase?**
An endopeptidase hydrolyses peptide bonds within a polypeptide chain. An exopeptidase hydrolyses the bonds at the ends, removing single amino acids or dipeptides.

**Why is co-transport called indirect active transport?**
ATP is used by the sodium-potassium pump to make the sodium gradient, not by the co-transporter. The co-transporter uses that gradient to move glucose or amino acids against their own gradient.
