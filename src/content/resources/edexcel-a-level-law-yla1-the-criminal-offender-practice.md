## Section A

**1.** State the meaning of the "thin skull" rule and name **one** case that illustrates it. **[2]**

**2.** Distinguish between direct and oblique intention. **[3]**

**3.** Explain what is meant by a strict liability offence, giving **one** example. **[3]**

**4.** Identify the section of the Theft Act 1968 that defines each of the following:

**(a)** appropriation **[1]**
**(b)** intention permanently to deprive. **[1]**

**5.** State **two** situations in which, under section 2 of the Theft Act 1968, a person is not to be regarded as dishonest. **[2]**

## Section B

**6.** Maya stabs Joel in a fight. At hospital, Joel is given an antibiotic to which he is allergic, despite his notes recording the allergy. He dies of the reaction while his wound is healing well.

Explain whether Maya is the legal cause of Joel's death. **[6]**

**7.** Ben eats a three-course meal at a restaurant. When the bill arrives he tells the waiter he will fetch his wallet from his car, then drives away without paying.

**(a)** Explain whether Ben has committed making off without payment. **[4]**
**(b)** Explain whether Ben could alternatively be liable for fraud by false representation. **[4]**

**8.** Carla is told by a gang member that her brother will be badly beaten unless she smashes the windows of a rival's shop that night. She does so.

Explain whether Carla can rely on the defence of duress. **[6]**

**9.** After drinking heavily at a party, Sam picks up a stranger's expensive coat, believing it to be his own, and takes it home. He also punches a guest, causing a bruise.

Explain how voluntary intoxication affects Sam's liability for theft of the coat and for assault occasioning actual bodily harm. **[8]**

**10.** Explain the factors a court takes into account when choosing between a community order and a custodial sentence under the Criminal Justice Act 2003 framework. **[6]**

**11.** Evaluate whether strict liability offences are justified in English criminal law. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** The defendant must **take the victim as they find them**, so a victim's unusual physical condition or beliefs do not break the chain of causation [1]. Case: *R v Blaue* [1975], where the victim's refusal of a blood transfusion on religious grounds did not break the chain [1].

**2.** **Direct intention** is where the result is the defendant's **aim or purpose** (*R v Mohan* [1975]) [1]. **Oblique intention** is where the result is not D's aim, but it is a **virtually certain consequence** of D's actions and D appreciates that [1]; the jury **may** then find intention (*R v Woollin* [1998]) [1].

**3.** An offence where **no *mens rea* is required for at least one element** of the *actus reus* [1], so D can be guilty even if they took care [1]. Example: *Callow v Tillstone* [1900] (selling unfit meat after a vet had passed it) or *Harrow LBC v Shah* [1999] (selling a lottery ticket to an under-age buyer) [1].

**4. (a)** Section 3 [1]. **(b)** Section 6 [1].

**5.** Any two [1] each: D believes they have a **legal right** to deprive the other of it; D believes the owner **would consent** if they knew of the appropriation and its circumstances; D believes the owner **cannot be discovered by taking reasonable steps** (except where the property came to D as trustee or personal representative).

**6.** **Factual causation**: but for the stabbing, Joel would not have been in hospital or given the drug, so it is satisfied [1]. **Legal causation**: Maya's act must be an **operating and substantial cause** (*R v Smith* [1959]) [1]. Medical treatment does not usually break the chain (*R v Cheshire* [1991]) [1], but it can where treatment is **"palpably wrong"** and the original wound is healing, as in *R v Jordan* [1956], where a patient was given a drug to which he was known to be intolerant [1]. Joel's facts are very close to *Jordan* [1]. **Conclusion**: the treatment is likely to be a *novus actus interveniens*, so Maya is probably not the legal cause of death, though she remains liable for the stabbing itself [1].

**7. (a)** Under the **Theft Act 1978, s 3**, D must, **knowing that payment on the spot is required** [1], **dishonestly make off** without paying [1], **intending to avoid payment permanently** (*R v Allen* [1985]) [1]. Ben knew payment was due, left the premises and drove away with no intention of returning: all elements appear satisfied [1].
**(b)** Under the **Fraud Act 2006, s 2**, D must **dishonestly make a false representation** [1], knowing it is or might be untrue or misleading [1], intending to make a gain or cause a loss (s 5) [1]. Ben's statement that he was fetching his wallet was false and made to escape payment, so he gained by keeping money he owed: s 2 is likely satisfied [1].

**8.** Duress requires a **threat of death or serious injury** [1], which can be to a family member, so a threat that her brother will be "badly beaten" can qualify if it means serious injury [1]. Applying *R v Graham* [1982]: was Carla compelled to act because she **reasonably believed** the threat would be carried out [1], and would a **sober person of reasonable firmness** sharing her characteristics have responded in the same way? [1]. The threat must be **imminent** enough that she had no safe avenue of escape, such as going to the police; a threat for "that night" may leave time to do so (*R v Hasan* [2005]) [1]. If she had **voluntarily associated** with the gang, knowing she might be subject to compulsion, the defence would fail [1].

**9.** **Theft** is a **specific-intent** offence [1]. Sam must be dishonest and intend permanently to deprive another of the coat [1]. Because he honestly believed it was his own, even though the belief was formed while drunk, he may lack dishonesty (s 2(1)(a), a belief in a legal right) and lacks intention to deprive another [1] [1]. Voluntary intoxication can therefore help him on theft. **Assault occasioning ABH (OAPA 1861, s 47)** is a **basic-intent** offence [1]. Under *DPP v Majewski* [1977], voluntary intoxication is **no defence** [1], because becoming drunk is itself reckless [1]. Sam is likely guilty of the s 47 offence if the bruise amounts to actual bodily harm, otherwise of common assault [1].

**10.** **Seriousness**: custody only where the offence is **so serious that neither a fine nor a community sentence can be justified** [1]; a community order where it is **serious enough** to warrant one [1]. **Culpability and harm** caused or intended [1]. **Aggravating factors**, such as previous convictions or hostility based on race or religion [1]; **mitigating factors**, such as remorse or good character [1]. **Guilty plea** reduction and the **purposes of sentencing**, such as rehabilitation, which may point towards a community order with requirements [1].

**11.** Levels-based answer. Credit:

- **AO1**: definition; presumption of *mens rea* (*Sweet v Parsley*); the *Gammon* factors; examples (*Callow v Tillstone*, *Harrow LBC v Shah*).
- **For**: protects the public in areas such as food safety and the environment; easier to enforce, saving court time; encourages high standards among businesses; penalties are usually small.
- **Against**: convicts people who took all reasonable care, which conflicts with the idea that punishment should follow fault; little evidence that it improves standards where care was already taken; a conviction still carries stigma; inconsistency in deciding which offences are strict.
- **Evaluation**: consider due diligence defences as a middle way, and reach a justified conclusion, for example that strict liability is acceptable for regulatory offences with low penalties but not for offences carrying real stigma.

Top-level answers apply cases and reach a reasoned judgement.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Concluding causation from "but for" alone.
- Forgetting that a drunken belief in ownership can still negate dishonesty for theft.
- Applying *Majewski* to a specific-intent offence.
- Treating any threat as enough for duress, without the *Graham* test or the escape point.
- Mixing up the 1978 Theft Act (making off) with the 1968 Act.
