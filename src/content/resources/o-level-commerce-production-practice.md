## Section A

**1.** Explain the difference between trade and aids to trade, giving two examples of each. **[4]**

**2.** State the four sectors of production and give one example of an industry at each. **[4]**

## Section B

**3.** A forestry operation fells and extracts timber, a furniture maker buys the timber, builds tables, and sells them through a retailer in another city.

**(a)** Identify which stage of production each business is engaged in. **[3]**
**(b)** State which utility is created when the furniture is transported from the workshop to the retailer's city. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain why the furniture maker's activity is described as creating form utility. **[2]**

**4.** Explain **three** advantages and **two** disadvantages of specialisation. **[10]**

**5.** "Commerce is the same thing as production." Discuss whether this statement is correct. **[4]**

**6.** A country imports electronic components, stores and repackages them without manufacturing them further, then re-exports them to other countries.

**(a)** Identify this type of foreign trade. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain how it differs from a simple import followed by domestic sale. **[2]**

**7.** Specialisation's advantages and disadvantages are required for three specific contexts.

**(a)** State the three contexts in which the syllabus requires the advantages and disadvantages of specialisation, giving an example of each. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain why specialisation in any of these contexts makes commerce necessary rather than optional. **[3]**

## Answers

**1. Trade** is the buying and selling of goods, split into home trade (wholesale and retail) and foreign trade (import, export, entrepot) [1] [1]. **Aids to trade** are the services that make trade possible, such as banking, insurance, transport, warehousing, communication and advertising [1] [1].

**2. Primary (extractive)** — extraction of raw materials, e.g. mining or farming [1]. **Secondary** — turning raw materials into finished or semi-finished goods, e.g. manufacturing [1]. **Tertiary** — providing services that support the other two, e.g. retailing or banking [1]. **Quaternary** — digital technology, e.g. an e-commerce platform [1].

**3. (a)** The forestry operation is **primary** (felling and extracting timber only, with no processing) [1]; the furniture maker is **secondary** (manufacturing tables) [1]; the retailer is **tertiary** (selling to the final consumer) [1].
**(b)** **Place utility** [1].
**(c)** Form utility is created because the furniture maker **changes the timber into a more useful form** — a table — that better satisfies a consumer's want than the raw material did [1] [1].

**4. Advantages** (any three, e.g.): **higher output per worker**, since repeating one task builds skill and speed [1] [1]; **permits mechanisation and economies of scale**, lowering unit costs [1] [1]; **improved quality**, since a worker/firm becomes expert in a narrow task [1] [1]. **Disadvantages** (any two): work becomes **repetitive and demotivating** [1] [1]; specialisation creates **total interdependence**, so a breakdown anywhere in the chain of exchange disrupts everyone relying on it [1] [1].

**5.** The statement is **not precisely correct** [1]. Production is the **creation of goods and services** through the primary, secondary, tertiary and quaternary sectors [1]. Commerce is narrower: it means **distribution** — trade and aids to trade — not the creation of goods [1]; commercial services sit within the tertiary sector of production, so commerce and production are not two unrelated categories, but commerce specifically is not itself the creation of goods [1].

**6. (a)** **Entrepot trade** (background term — not itself named in the syllabus, though the underlying idea of foreign trade is) [1].
**(b)** In a simple import, the goods are brought in **for sale within the domestic market** [1]; in entrepot trade, the goods are imported **specifically in order to be re-exported**, often after storage or minor processing (short of manufacture), rather than being sold to domestic consumers [1].

**7. (a)** **Individual** — a person specialising in one task or trade, e.g. a single worker on a production line [1]. **Factory** — a factory specialising in producing one product or a narrow range, e.g. a car-parts factory [1]. **Country** — a nation specialising in producing goods suited to its climate or resources, e.g. a country with fertile land specialising in agricultural exports [1].
**(b)** In each of these contexts, specialisation means producing **far more of one thing than is needed and far less of everything else** [1], so goods and services that are not self-produced must be **obtained through exchange** [1]; commerce (trade and its aids) is the mechanism that makes this exchange possible, so a specialised individual, factory or country cannot function without it [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Saying commerce "includes production" — commerce means distribution (trade and aids to trade), not the creation of goods, though commercial services do sit within the tertiary sector.
- Naming a utility without explaining *why* that utility applies to the scenario given.
- Listing advantages of specialisation without linking them to output, cost or quality.
- Forgetting that specialisation's biggest disadvantage — interdependence — is precisely what makes commerce necessary.
- Confusing entrepot trade with a simple import — entrepot trade specifically involves re-exporting after storage or minor processing, not domestic sale and not full manufacture.
- Substituting "worker", "region" or "town" for the syllabus's actual three specialisation contexts — individual, factory and country.
- Forgetting the fourth (quaternary) sector when listing the sectors of production.

## Specialisation and commerce — the logical chain

Almost every extended question on this topic rewards being able to
state the same underlying chain of reasoning explicitly: specialisation
(for an individual, a factory or a country) increases output, quality or
efficiency, but it also means that individual, factory or country produces
far more of one thing and far less of everything else it needs — creating
**interdependence**. Interdependence, in turn, is exactly what makes
**exchange** necessary, and commerce (trade plus its aids) is the
mechanism that makes exchange possible across distance and time. A
strong answer traces this chain through explicitly — specialisation →
interdependence → the necessity of commerce — rather than treating
specialisation and commerce as two separate topics that happen to
appear in the same syllabus section. For the full treatment of trade,
production and utility, see the [Commerce and Production revision notes](/resources/o-level-commerce-production-revision-notes/).
