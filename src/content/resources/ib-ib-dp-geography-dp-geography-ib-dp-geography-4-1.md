## What this sub-topic asks you to do

- Explain plate tectonic processes and how they generate earthquakes and volcanic activity.
- Describe and explain the characteristics of different volcano types and their eruptions.
- Describe the characteristics of earthquakes.
- Describe types of mass movement and explain the physical factors that cause them.

## Mass movement: classifying the movement

Mass movement is the downslope movement of rock, soil and debris under gravity. Classify it by **how** the material moves and **how fast**.

| Type | How material moves | Speed | Typical setting |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fall | Blocks detach and drop freely through the air | Very rapid | Steep cliffs, jointed rock, freeze-thaw zones |
| Topple | Blocks rotate forward and tip over | Rapid | Steep faces with near-vertical joints |
| Rotational slide (slump) | A mass moves along a curved, spoon-shaped surface, leaving a back-tilted block | Slow to rapid | Clay or weak rock over a firmer base, often undercut |
| Translational slide | A mass slides along a flat surface such as a bedding plane | Rapid | Rock layers dipping parallel to the slope |
| Flow (mudflow, debris flow, earthflow) | Saturated material moves like a fluid | Slow to very rapid | Steep channels after heavy rain; volcanic slopes (lahars) |
| Creep | Particles move downslope a fraction at a time | Very slow (millimetres a year) | Soil-covered slopes everywhere |

The water content matters as much as the material: falls involve little water, slides some, and flows a lot.

## Why slopes fail: forces and the factor of safety

A slope stays in place while the forces resisting movement (**shear strength**: friction and cohesion) exceed the forces driving it (**shear stress**: the downslope pull of gravity). Engineers express this as the **factor of safety**:

**factor of safety = resisting forces / driving forces**

A value above 1 means the slope is stable; at 1 it is on the point of failure; below 1 it fails.

**Worked example (invented values for a slope section, in kilonewtons).**

```
Dry conditions:      resisting 260, driving 200
                     factor of safety = 260 / 200 = 1.30, stable

After days of rain:  water adds weight, so driving rises to 220
                     pore water pressure reduces friction, so resisting falls to 210
                     factor of safety = 210 / 220 = 0.95, failure
```

Rain affects both sides of the balance. Water adds weight, increasing stress, and water pressure in the pores between grains pushes them apart, reducing friction and therefore strength. This is why many landslides follow prolonged or intense rain, even on slopes that have been stable for years.

Other physical factors:

- **Slope angle**: steeper slopes have a larger downslope component of gravity.
- **Rock type and structure**: weak clays, deeply weathered rock, and bedding planes or joints parallel to the slope all reduce strength.
- **Undercutting** by rivers or waves removes support at the base.
- **Vegetation loss**: roots bind soil, and transpiration lowers soil moisture.
- **Earthquake shaking**: briefly increases stress and can liquefy saturated sediment, which is why large earthquakes in mountains trigger many landslides.

## Volcano types

| Type | Shape | Magma and eruption | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Shield volcano | Broad, gently sloping dome | Runny basaltic lava flows long distances; mostly effusive | Mauna Loa, Hawaii |
| Stratovolcano (composite) | Steep, symmetrical cone of alternating lava and ash layers | Viscous andesitic or rhyolitic magma; explosive eruptions | Mount Fuji, Japan; Mount St Helens, USA |
| Cinder cone | Small, steep cone of loose fragments | Gas-rich bursts throw out lava clots that fall around the vent | Paricutin, Mexico, which grew from a farm field in 1943 |
| Caldera | Large depression formed when a volcano collapses into its emptied magma chamber | Usually follows a very large explosive eruption | Yellowstone, USA; Crater Lake, USA |

## Eruption styles

Eruption styles form a sequence of increasing explosivity:

1. **Hawaiian**: fountains and flows of runny lava; low explosivity.
2. **Strombolian**: regular bursts of gas throwing out glowing lava fragments.
3. **Vulcanian**: short, violent explosions that clear a blocked vent, throwing out ash and blocks.
4. **Plinian**: sustained, very high eruption columns of gas and ash, with pyroclastic flows when the column collapses. Named after Pliny the Younger, who described the eruption of Vesuvius in AD 79.

The controlling factors are magma **viscosity** (set mainly by silica content and temperature) and **gas content**. Viscous, gas-rich magma traps gas until pressure is released violently.

## Earthquake depth

Earthquakes are classified by the depth of the focus:

| Class | Focus depth | Typical setting |
|---|---|---|
| Shallow | 0 to 70 km | All boundary types; most damaging |
| Intermediate | 70 to 300 km | Within subducting plates |
| Deep | 300 to about 700 km | Deep in subducting slabs |

Deep and intermediate earthquakes occur almost entirely at subduction zones, because only there is cold, brittle lithosphere carried to great depth. A map of foci that deepen in one direction away from a trench reveals the direction of subduction.

## Worked example: plate speed from a hotspot chain

A volcanic island chain forms as a plate moves over a stationary mantle plume. If you know an island's age and distance from the active hotspot, you can estimate plate speed. Figures here are illustrative.

```
Island A: 400 km from the active volcano, 5 million years old

Speed = distance / time
      = 400 km / 5,000,000 years
      = 40,000,000 cm / 5,000,000 years
      = 8 cm per year
```

Plates typically move a few centimetres a year, about the rate fingernails grow. Converting kilometres to centimetres (1 km = 100,000 cm) before dividing avoids errors.

## Annotated model answer

*Explain why landslides often occur after periods of heavy rainfall.* **(sample paragraph)**

"Slopes remain stable while shear strength exceeds shear stress **[principle]**. Prolonged rain saturates the soil and regolith, adding weight and so increasing the downslope stress **[first effect of water]**. At the same time, water pressure in the pores between particles pushes them apart, reducing friction and cohesion, so shear strength falls **[second effect, often missed]**. When the factor of safety drops below 1, the slope fails, typically as a slide or, if the material is very wet, as a flow **[outcome linked to type]**. Steep slopes, weak clays and removal of vegetation make this more likely **[conditioning factors]**."

## Common mistakes

- Using "landslide" for all mass movement instead of naming the type.
- Saying rain only "lubricates" a slope; explain pore water pressure and added weight.
- Describing a stratovolcano as made only of lava.
- Mixing eruption style with volcano type.
- Forgetting that deep earthquakes only occur at subduction zones.
