This guide covers **discipline options and eAssessment subjects** for
IB Middle Years Programme Individuals and Societies. The [full
syllabus
guide](/resources/ib-myp-individuals-and-societies-syllabus-guide/)
confirms this subject group's specific content varies considerably by
school, since schools choose which discipline to teach, alongside the
[Criteria in
Practice](/resources/ib-myp-individuals-and-societies-criteria-in-practice/)
guide already on the site.

## Where this fits in the subject

This subject group covers disciplines traditionally studied under
humanities and social sciences. Schools develop courses in integrated
humanities, history, economics, geography, philosophy,
sociology/anthropology, business management, psychology, or world
religions -- a genuine school-level choice. The subject group name and
four assessment criteria (Knowing and understanding, Investigating,
Communicating, Thinking critically) stay the same everywhere, but the
specific content studied can differ substantially between schools.

## Only three eAssessment subjects exist

Despite the wide range of disciplines schools may teach, the
on-screen eAssessment is available in only history, geography, or
integrated humanities. This matters directly for revision: confirming
which of these three your own course actually prepares you for is an
essential first step, since a school teaching, say, economics or
psychology as its individuals and societies discipline may still
direct students toward one of the three examinable subjects for the
eAssessment itself.

## The three eAssessment tasks

Investigating (criteria A and B, 26 marks) involves evaluating an
investigation and planning one of the student's own, demonstrating
knowledge from the course or from exam source material. Communicating
(criteria A and C, 18 marks) involves engaging creatively with a
topic, presenting information appropriately for audience and purpose.
Thinking critically (criteria A, C and D, 36 marks, the largest task)
involves discussing issues, arguments and perspectives through
structured questions, culminating in extended writing.

## Worked example: the source-evaluation skill

Official sample questions illustrate the investigating task's
specific demand. One sample question asks candidates to "explain one
value or one limitation of source B for a student researching
protests." Another asks candidates to "compare and contrast source C
and source D in terms of their usefulness in studying protest
movements." The skill both questions demonstrate is evaluating what a
source can and cannot tell you about a topic -- not simply summarising
its content -- applied both to a single source and comparatively
across two sources. Build the habit of asking what a source can and
cannot tell you every time you work with primary or secondary material
in class, since this is tested directly and is distinct from simply
extracting facts.

## Building for extended writing

Because the thinking-critically task carries the largest share of
marks (36 of 80) and ends in extended writing, a sample question such
as "To what extent do you agree that gaining support from different
groups in society is the most important factor in making sure a
protest movement is successful?" models the expected approach: take a
position, weigh it against alternatives, and reach a reasoned
judgement -- not simply describe events.

## Sample eAssessment topics

The official brief lists sample topics the on-screen examinations may
draw on: demographics and human movement, settlement and urban
morphology, superpowers and supra-national alliances, significant
individuals, warfare and peacekeeping, rights and social protest,
trade and economic exchange, and industrialization and technological
development. Because these span history, geography and broader
social-science themes, they illustrate why the three eAssessment
subjects can draw on genuinely overlapping content even though a
student's own taught discipline may have been narrower.

## How to approach it

Confirm which of the three eAssessment subjects -- history, geography,
integrated humanities -- your course actually prepares you for, rather
than assuming your school's taught discipline automatically determines
it. Practise stating one value and one limitation of a source for a
specific research purpose, not just summarising its content, and
practise comparing two sources' usefulness for the same research
question, since both single-source and comparative source-evaluation
questions appear. Build extended-writing practice around taking and
justifying a position on the largest-weighted task, thinking
critically, rather than only describing events, since a purely
narrative answer cannot access this task's top marks regardless of how
accurate or detailed it is.

## Why criteria transfer even when discipline changes

Because the same four assessment criteria and the same
level-and-band scoring structure apply across every MYP subject group,
a student moving between, say, individuals and societies and MYP
Sciences encounters a familiar assessment logic even though the
criterion names, content and specific skills tested differ. This
consistency is deliberate: it lets the IB apply the same underlying
standard of rigor across very different kinds of coursework.

## Common mistakes

Assuming the discipline your school teaches automatically determines your eAssessment subject, without
confirming which of the three -- history, geography, integrated humanities -- actually applies to your
own cohort. Summarising a source's content in the investigating task instead of evaluating its value or
limitation for a specific research purpose, which is a different skill from simply restating what a
source says. Writing a descriptive, narrative response to the thinking-critically task instead of a
structured, judgement-based argument, which cannot access this task's top marks regardless of how
accurate or detailed the description is. Neglecting the communicating task's audience-and-purpose
demand, since presenting information "appropriately for audience and purpose" is itself part of what
is assessed, not just the accuracy of the information presented.
