OxfordAQA International GCSE Psychology (9218) is a linear, untiered qualification with no coursework,
assessed by two equally weighted written papers. Paper 1 (Cognition and Behaviour) and Paper 2 (Social
Context and Behaviour) each run 2 hours, carry 100 marks and are worth 50%. **Individual components
may not be re-sat -- only the whole qualification can be retaken**, any number of times. This is a
recently launched specification: first teaching September 2023, first examined May/June 2025. These
notes complement the site's guides to
[Cognition and Behaviour](/resources/igcse-oxfordaqa-psychology-cognition-and-behaviour/) and
[Conformity and Obedience](/resources/igcse-oxfordaqa-psychology-conformity-and-obedience/).

## The no-individual-resit rule raises the cost of an off-day on either paper

Because a single weak paper cannot be retaken on its own, a strong Paper 1 cannot rescue a
disappointing Paper 2 within the same qualification attempt. **Exam-preparation priority**: prepare
both papers to the same standard, and treat each as equally consequential rather than favouring the one
that feels more comfortable. If a retake does become necessary, it means sitting both papers again,
which makes first-attempt preparation worth extra care.

## Named studies are the backbone of every answer

Psychology at GCSE level is assessed through named research: specific studies, their aims, methods,
findings and conclusions. A general description of a phenomenon without naming the study that
established it earns far less than one that does. **Exam-preparation priority**: for each key study,
memorise it in four parts -- who, what they did, what they found, and one limitation -- since this is
the shape most exam answers require.

## Two hours for 100 marks is 1.2 minutes per mark

That is a workable but not generous budget for a subject with extended-response questions.
**Exam-preparation priority**: practise complete papers to time, and specifically practise the
extended-response questions under a firm time limit, since they carry the largest single mark
allocations and are the easiest place to run over.

## Methodology questions test understanding of the research process itself

Both papers examine research methods -- sampling, ethics, reliability and validity -- not just as a
separate topic but as something applied to the named studies themselves. **Exam-preparation priority**:
practise applying methodological concepts to a specific study rather than defining them in the
abstract. A question asking about a study's sample, for instance, wants you to evaluate that specific
sample, not to recite a general definition of sampling.

## Evaluation needs both a strength and a limitation, applied specifically

Higher-tariff questions typically ask for evaluation of a study or a theory, and reward strengths and
limitations that are specific to the study in question rather than generic ones that could apply to
almost any research. **Exam-preparation priority**: practise writing evaluation points that reference
a detail of the specific study -- its sample, its method, its setting -- rather than a stock phrase like
"the sample was small" without saying why that matters for this particular research.

## Worked routine: evaluating a named study

The routine below is an original model written for this resource, not a reproduction of any official
past paper, named study or mark scheme.

```
Step 1 - name the study and state its aim in one sentence:
Precisely -- vague identification ("a study on memory") is not
creditable detail.

Step 2 - state the key finding:
The specific result, not the general topic area it relates to.

Step 3 - give a strength, tied to this study's method:
E.g. a controlled procedure that reduces confounding variables --
specific to what this study actually did.

Step 4 - give a limitation, tied to this study's specifics:
E.g. a sample drawn from one setting, limiting generalisability --
again specific, not generic.

Step 5 - state what the strength or limitation means for how far
the findings can be trusted or applied:
The consequence, not just the observation.
```

Step 5 is the step that converts a list of features into genuine evaluation -- stating what a strength
or limitation actually means for the conclusions that can be drawn.

## Before/during exam checklist

- **Before the exams**: prepare both papers to the same standard, since components cannot be resat
  individually; learn each key study in four parts (who, what, findings, one limitation); practise
  complete two-hour papers, with firm time limits on extended-response questions.
- **During either paper**: budget roughly 1.2 minutes per mark and protect time for the highest-tariff
  questions.
- **On methodology questions**: apply the concept to the specific study named in the question rather
  than defining it generically.
- **On evaluation questions**: give a strength and a limitation specific to the study, and state what
  each means for the conclusions drawn.

## Self-test

1. What happens if a candidate wants to improve just one paper's result?
2. What four elements should be memorised for each key study?
3. Why does a generic evaluation point ("the sample was small") score poorly?
4. What is the final step that turns an evaluation point into genuine evaluation?

**Answers:** 1. Nothing can be improved in isolation -- individual components cannot be resat, so the
whole qualification (both papers) must be retaken. 2. Who conducted it, what they did, what they
found, and one limitation. 3. Because it is not tied to the specific study's method, sample or
setting, and does not explain why that feature matters for this particular research. 4. Stating what
the strength or limitation means for how far the findings can be trusted or generalised, not just
naming the feature itself.
