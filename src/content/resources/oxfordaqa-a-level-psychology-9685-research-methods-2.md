## Content analysis

Content analysis is a way of studying behaviour indirectly, through things people have produced. These include adverts, social media posts, diaries, interview transcripts, films and children's drawings.

**Process.**
1. Choose a sample of material (for example, every post on a forum in one month).
2. Create **coding categories**. These work like behavioural categories in an observation.
3. Read or watch the material and tally each time a category appears. This gives **quantitative** data.
4. In **thematic analysis**, the researcher looks for themes that emerge from repeated reading. This gives **qualitative** data.

**Key terms**
- *Coding:* putting units of material into categories.
- *Coding unit:* what is counted, such as a word, an image or a whole post.

**Strengths.** The material already exists, so it is cheap and easy to access. Ethical issues are often small when the material is public. Findings can be checked, because another researcher can re-code the same material.

**Limitations.** Coders interpret meaning, so bias can creep in. The material may be out of context, as you do not know why a post was written. Tallies show how often something appears but not why.

**Reliability check.** Two coders code the same material separately. Their tallies are compared, which is inter-observer reliability applied to content analysis.

**Worked paragraph.** *A fictional researcher codes 200 food adverts for "health claims" and "taste claims".* Content analysis suits this aim because adverts are permanent and public, so no consent is needed (knowledge, application). Tallying claims converts adverts into frequencies that can be compared (analysis). However, deciding whether "light and fresh" is a health or taste claim needs judgement, so a second coder should code a sample, and agreement should be checked (evaluation).

## Case studies

A case study is an in-depth investigation of one person, group, institution or event. It usually uses several methods, such as interviews, observation and psychological tests. Many case studies are **longitudinal** and follow the case over time.

**Henry Molaison (HM).** In 1953 the surgeon William Scoville removed parts of HM's medial temporal lobes on both sides, including much of the hippocampus, to treat severe epilepsy. Scoville and Milner (1957) reported that HM was left with severe amnesia for events after the operation. Later testing found his immediate memory span was normal, and Milner showed he improved at a mirror-drawing task without remembering having practised it.

**Phineas Gage.** In 1848 an iron rod passed through Gage's skull in a railway construction accident. He survived. His physician, John Harlow, reported changes in his personality. Later historians have argued that many popular details of the case were exaggerated.

**Strengths.**
- Rich, detailed data, often both qualitative and quantitative.
- Allow study of rare cases that could never be created ethically, such as brain damage.
- A single case can challenge a theory. HM's normal short-term span alongside lost long-term learning supported separate memory stores.

**Limitations.**
- One case may not generalise to others.
- The researcher may become close to the participant, which risks bias in interpretation.
- Data about the past (retrospective data) rely on memory or old records, as in the Gage case.
- Cases cannot be repeated, so findings are hard to replicate.

**Worked paragraph.** HM's case supports the view that the hippocampus is needed to form new long-term memories (knowledge). The surgery was precisely recorded and he was tested over decades (application). However, HM had epilepsy before the operation, so his brain may not have been typical (analysis). This means findings from one person must be checked against other patients and brain imaging before being generalised (evaluation).

## Reliability

**Reliability** means consistency. A reliable measure gives the same result if the study or test is repeated in the same way.

**Test-retest.** Give the same test or questionnaire to the same people on two occasions, with a gap long enough that they cannot recall their answers but short enough that the trait has not changed. Correlate the two sets of scores.

**Inter-observer reliability.** Two or more observers record the same behaviour separately, using the same categories and time sampling. Their tallies are correlated.

**Interpreting the correlation.** A strong positive correlation shows good reliability. A rule of thumb used in many textbooks is +0.80 or above. The specification does not give a figure, so your teacher will confirm what is expected.

**Improving reliability.**
- Questionnaires: rewrite ambiguous items, or replace open questions with closed ones.
- Observations: operationalise categories more clearly and train observers together.
- Experiments: standardise procedures and instructions.
- Content analysis: tighten coding definitions and pilot them.

## Validity

**Validity** means accuracy. A valid measure measures what it claims to measure, and valid findings can be generalised beyond the study.

| Type | Meaning | How it is assessed |
|---|---|---|
| Face validity | The measure looks, on the surface, as if it measures what it should | An expert inspects the items |
| Concurrent validity | Scores agree with an established measure of the same thing | Give both measures; correlate the scores |
| Predictive validity | Scores predict a later behaviour or outcome | Correlate scores with the later outcome |
| Ecological validity | Findings generalise to real-life settings | Compare with field studies; judge how natural the task and setting were |

**Correlation in validity.** For concurrent and predictive validity, a strong positive correlation between the new measure and the comparison supports validity.

**Worked paragraph.** *A fictional company designs a 20-item test of nursing aptitude.* To check concurrent validity, applicants could also complete an established aptitude test, and the two sets of scores could be correlated (knowledge, application). To check predictive validity, scores could be correlated with supervisors' ratings after a year in post (application). A strong positive correlation in each case would suggest the test measures aptitude (analysis). A weak link with job ratings might reflect training received after selection rather than a poor test, so one correlation is not proof (evaluation).

## Designing and reporting investigations

Design choices come from Research methods 1: aim, hypothesis, sampling, design, variables, controls and ethics. Research methods 2 adds how the study is written up.

| Section | What it contains |
|---|---|
| Abstract | A short summary of the aim, hypothesis, method, results and conclusion, so a reader can decide whether to read on |
| Introduction | Background theory and past research, leading to the aim and hypotheses |
| Method | Design, participants and sampling, materials, procedure and ethics, in enough detail to replicate the study |
| Results | Descriptive statistics, graphs and tables, and the inferential test with its outcome |
| Discussion | What the results mean, links to past research, limitations, improvements and real-world applications |
| Referencing | Full details of every source cited |

A reference usually lists author(s), year, title, journal, volume and page numbers. For example: Scoville, W. B. and Milner, B. (1957). Loss of recent memory after bilateral hippocampal lesions. *Journal of Neurology, Neurosurgery and Psychiatry*, 20, 11-21. Your teacher will confirm the format they want.

## Probability, significance and inferential tests

**Purpose.** Descriptive statistics show a difference or correlation in the sample. An **inferential test** tells you how likely it is that this result happened by chance. That decides whether you can accept the alternative hypothesis.

**Null hypothesis.** This states there is no difference or no correlation. The test tells you whether to reject it.

**Probability (p).** Psychologists usually use **p ≤ 0.05**: there is 5% or less probability that the result is due to chance. A stricter level, **p ≤ 0.01**, is used when the risk of a wrong conclusion matters more, such as a study of a new treatment.

**Critical values.** Each test produces a **calculated value**. You compare it with a **critical value** in a statistical table. To find the right critical value you need:
- the significance level (usually 0.05);
- whether the hypothesis is directional (**one-tailed**) or non-directional (**two-tailed**);
- the number of participants (N) or degrees of freedom (df).

For most tests the calculated value must be **equal to or greater than** the critical value. For **Mann-Whitney, Wilcoxon and the Sign test** it must be **equal to or less than** the critical value. A memory aid: tests with an "r" in the name (Spearman's rho, Pearson's r, unrelated t-test, related t-test, Chi-squared) need a calculated value that is greater. Each table usually prints this rule underneath.

**Type I error.** Rejecting the null hypothesis when it is true: a false positive. More likely if the level is too lenient, such as 0.10.

**Type II error.** Accepting the null hypothesis when it is false: a false negative. More likely if the level is too strict, such as 0.01. Using 0.05 balances the two risks.

## Levels of measurement

- **Nominal:** data in separate categories; each person counts in one category only. Example: whether each driver stopped or did not stop at an amber light.
- **Ordinal:** data that can be put in order, but the gaps between points are not equal. Example: ratings of mood from 1 to 10. Ordinal data are converted to ranks for testing.
- **Interval:** data on a scale with equal, standard units. Example: time in seconds, or temperature.

Interval data are the most precise. They can be turned into ordinal data by ranking, but not the other way round.

## Choosing a statistical test

The specification names three factors: the research aim, the level of measurement and the experimental design.

| Aim and design | Nominal | Ordinal | Interval |
|---|---|---|---|
| Difference; unrelated (independent groups) | Chi-squared | Mann-Whitney | Unrelated t-test |
| Difference; related (repeated measures or matched pairs) | Sign test | Wilcoxon | Related t-test |
| Correlation or association | Chi-squared | Spearman's rho | Pearson's r |

Textbooks usually add that the t-tests and Pearson's r (parametric tests) need interval data that are roughly normally distributed, with similar spread in each condition. Your teacher will confirm how much of this the exam expects.

**Worked example.** *A fictional researcher tests whether people who revise in silence recall more than people who revise with music. Two separate groups of 10 rate their recall confidence on a 1-10 scale. Mann-Whitney gives U = 18.* Mann-Whitney is right: the aim is a difference, the design is independent groups, and ratings are ordinal. The hypothesis is directional, so the one-tailed critical value at p ≤ 0.05 for n1 = n2 = 10 is 27. U must be equal to or less than this. 18 ≤ 27, so the result is significant and the null hypothesis is rejected. The one-tailed critical value at p ≤ 0.01 is 19, so the result is also significant at the stricter level, which reduces the risk of a Type I error.

## Common errors

- Saying "the results are significant" without comparing calculated and critical values.
- Using the "greater than" rule for Wilcoxon, Mann-Whitney or the Sign test.
- Choosing a related test for independent groups. Matched pairs count as related.
- Calling rating scales interval data. Ratings are ordinal.
- Confusing reliability (consistency) with validity (accuracy).
- Describing concurrent validity with no correlation against an established measure.
- Writing that a Type I error is "a mistake in the method". It is a wrong decision about the null hypothesis.
- Treating a case study as useless because N = 1; say what it can and cannot show.
- Putting conclusions in the results section, or new data in the discussion.

## Frequently asked questions

**What does OxfordAQA 9685 Research methods 2 cover?**
Section 3.3.3 covers content analysis, case studies, reliability, validity, designing and reporting investigations, probability and significance, levels of measurement and choosing statistical tests.

**What are the three levels of measurement?**
Nominal data are separate categories, ordinal data can be put in order but have unequal gaps, and interval data use a scale with equal, standard units.

**Why are rating scales treated as ordinal data?**
The gaps between points on a rating scale are not equal, since one person's 6 may not mean the same as another's.

**What does p ≤ 0.05 mean?**
It means there is a 5% or less probability that the result is due to chance. A stricter level, p ≤ 0.01, is used when the risk of a wrong conclusion matters more, such as a study of a new treatment.
