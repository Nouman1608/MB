This study guide goes deeper into section 4.7, Geometrical reasoning, of topic 4 (Geometry and trigonometry) in the Pearson Edexcel International GCSE Mathematics A (4MA1) specification, Issue 2 (November 2017). Section 4.7 is about **how you write** angle answers: each numerical step must be supported by a geometrical reason. It has a Foundation Tier statement and a wider Higher Tier statement.

The [Geometry and trigonometry study guide](/resources/edexcel-igcse-maths-4ma1-geometry-and-trigonometry/) gives a short paragraph on reasons. This page sets out the accepted wordings, shows how to lay out a chain of reasoning, and annotates model answers. Use it with the [Geometrical reasoning revision notes](/resources/edexcel-igcse-mathematics-4ma1-geometrical-reasoning-edexcel-igcse-maths-revision-notes/) and the [Geometrical reasoning practice questions](/resources/edexcel-igcse-mathematics-4ma1-geometrical-reasoning-edexcel-igcse-maths-practice/).

## What the specification asks

| Tier | Statement |
|---|---|
| Foundation 4.7 A | give informal reasons, where required, when arriving at numerical solutions to geometrical problems. Note: reasons will only be required for geometrical calculations based on lines (including chords and tangents), triangles or polygons |
| Higher 4.7 A | provide reasons, using standard geometrical statements, to support numerical values for angles obtained in any geometrical context involving lines, polygons and circles |

So on the Foundation Tier you may be asked to justify angles found from lines, triangles and polygons, including simple chord and tangent facts. On the Higher Tier the reasons must be **standard geometrical statements**, and circle theorems are included.

## The reason bank

These are the standard statements. Write them in full; abbreviations such as "Z angles" or "F angles" are not standard statements.

### Lines

| Situation | Reason to write |
|---|---|
| Angles meeting on a straight line | Angles on a straight line add up to 180° |
| Angles all round a point | Angles at a point add up to 360° |
| Two straight lines crossing | Vertically opposite angles are equal |
| Parallel lines, Z shape | Alternate angles are equal |
| Parallel lines, F shape | Corresponding angles are equal |
| Parallel lines, C or U shape | Co-interior (allied) angles add up to 180° |

The specification uses the word "allied"; "co-interior" means the same thing, and either is a standard term.

### Triangles and polygons

| Situation | Reason to write |
|---|---|
| Three angles of a triangle | Angles in a triangle add up to 180° |
| Exterior angle of a triangle | The exterior angle of a triangle equals the sum of the two interior opposite angles |
| Isosceles triangle | Base angles of an isosceles triangle are equal |
| Equilateral triangle | Angles in an equilateral triangle are all 60° |
| Quadrilateral | Angles in a quadrilateral add up to 360° |
| Any polygon | The interior angles of an n-sided polygon add up to (n − 2) × 180° |
| Exterior angles | Exterior angles of a polygon add up to 360° |
| Interior and exterior | Interior angle + exterior angle = 180° (angles on a straight line) |
| Parallelogram | Opposite angles of a parallelogram are equal |

### Circles

| Situation | Reason to write | Tier |
|---|---|---|
| Two radii in a triangle | Radii are equal, so the triangle is isosceles | Both |
| Tangent and radius | A tangent is perpendicular to the radius at the point of contact | Both |
| Two tangents from a point | Tangents from an external point are equal in length | Both |
| Perpendicular from centre | The perpendicular from the centre to a chord bisects the chord | Both |
| Angle at centre | The angle at the centre is twice the angle at the circumference | Higher |
| Diameter | The angle in a semicircle is 90° | Higher |
| Bow-tie | Angles in the same segment are equal | Higher |
| Four points on a circle | Opposite angles of a cyclic quadrilateral add up to 180° | Higher |
| Tangent and chord | Alternate segment theorem | Higher |

## How to lay out a reasoned answer

1. Find one angle at a time.
2. Write it as a statement naming the angle with three letters: "angle ACB = 62°".
3. Write the reason on the same line, straight after the statement.
4. Repeat until you reach the angle asked for, then state the final answer clearly.

A mark scheme usually gives method marks for the calculation and separate marks for the reasons. A correct answer with no reasons, when reasons are asked for, loses the reasoning marks. One reason is needed for **each** step, not one reason at the end.

## Worked example 1 (Foundation): two routes to one angle

ABC is a triangle. The side BC is extended to D. Angle ACD = 118° and angle BAC = 47°. Work out angle ABC, giving reasons.

**Route 1 (one step):**

```
angle ABC = 118° − 47° = 71°
reason: the exterior angle of a triangle equals the sum of the two interior opposite angles
```

**Route 2 (two steps):**

```
angle ACB = 180° − 118° = 62°   (angles on a straight line add up to 180°)
angle ABC = 180° − 47° − 62° = 71°   (angles in a triangle add up to 180°)
```

Either route scores full marks if every step has its reason.

## Worked example 2 (Foundation): parallel lines with algebra

AB and CD are parallel lines. A straight line crosses AB at P and CD at Q. B and D are on the same side of PQ. Angle BPQ = (3x + 20)° and angle PQD = (2x − 5)°. Find x.

Angles BPQ and PQD lie between the parallel lines, on the same side of PQ: a C shape.

```
3x + 20 + 2x − 5 = 180   (co-interior angles add up to 180°)
5x + 15 = 180
x = 33
```

Check: the angles are 119° and 61°, which add to 180°.

## Worked example 3 (Foundation): showing lines are parallel

The angles of quadrilateral ABCD, taken in order, are A = (x + 15)°, B = 2x°, C = (x + 40)° and D = (x + 30)°. Show that AD is parallel to BC.

```
x + 15 + 2x + x + 40 + x + 30 = 360   (angles in a quadrilateral add up to 360°)
5x + 85 = 360, so x = 55
angle A = 70°, angle B = 110°
angle A + angle B = 180°
```

Angles A and B lie between AD and BC on the same side of AB. They add up to 180°, so they are co-interior angles and **AD is parallel to BC**. The final sentence is what turns a calculation into a reason.

## Worked example 4 (Foundation): inside a regular polygon

ABCDEF is a regular hexagon. Work out angle ACD, giving reasons.

```
exterior angle = 360° ÷ 6 = 60°   (exterior angles of a polygon add up to 360°)
interior angle = 180° − 60° = 120°   (interior + exterior = 180°)
AB = BC, so angle BCA = (180° − 120°) ÷ 2 = 30°   (base angles of an isosceles triangle are equal)
angle ACD = 120° − 30° = 90°
```

## Worked example 5 (Higher): a circle chain

A tangent touches a circle at A, and T is a point on the tangent. B and C are points on the circle, with C in the alternate segment. Angle TAB = 52° and CA = CB. Find angle CAB.

```
angle ACB = 52°   (alternate segment theorem)
angle CAB = (180° − 52°) ÷ 2 = 64°   (CA = CB, so base angles of an isosceles triangle are equal; angles in a triangle add up to 180°)
```

## Worked example 6 (Higher): centre and cyclic quadrilateral

A, B, C and D lie on a circle with centre O. A is on the major arc BD and C is on the minor arc BD. Angle BOD = 140°. Find angle BAD and angle BCD.

```
angle BAD = 140° ÷ 2 = 70°   (angle at the centre is twice the angle at the circumference)
angle BCD = 180° − 70° = 110°   (opposite angles of a cyclic quadrilateral add up to 180°)
```

## Exam technique

- **"Give a reason for your answer"** (singular) usually needs one statement; **"give reasons"** needs one per step.
- **"Show that angle x = 40°"**: you must reach 40° by working, with reasons. Starting from 40° and checking it is not acceptable.
- **"Diagram not accurately drawn"** means no measuring.
- Name angles with three letters, such as angle PQR, so the examiner knows which one you mean.

## Common mistakes

- "Z angles", "F angles" or "because of parallel lines" instead of "alternate angles are equal".
- Calling co-interior angles equal: they add up to 180°.
- Giving "angles in a triangle" when the triangle is isosceles and you used equal base angles: two facts need two reasons.
- Giving one reason at the end for a three-step calculation.
- Using a circle theorem in a Higher answer without its standard wording, such as "circle rule".
