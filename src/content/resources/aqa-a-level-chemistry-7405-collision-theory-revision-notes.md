These notes condense section **3.1.5.1 Collision theory** of the AQA AS and A-level Chemistry specification (7404/7405), for teaching from September 2015. It is AS content, examined on Paper 2 (and Paper 3). The [Collision theory study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-collision-theory/) explains each idea with worked examples, and the [Collision theory practice questions](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-collision-theory-practice/) give exam-style practice.

## What the specification asks

- Reactions can only occur when **collisions** take place between particles having **sufficient energy**.
- This energy is called the **activation energy**.
- **Define** activation energy.
- **Explain** why most collisions do not lead to a reaction.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Collision | Two particles meeting |
| Successful (effective) collision | A collision in which the particles have energy at least equal to Eₐ and react |
| Activation energy, Eₐ | The minimum energy that colliding particles must have for a reaction to occur |
| Collision frequency | Number of collisions per second |
| Rate of reaction | Change in amount or concentration of a substance per unit time |
| Reaction profile | Graph of enthalpy against progress of reaction |

## The core ideas

1. Particles must collide.
2. They must collide with energy **equal to or greater than** Eₐ.
3. Only successful collisions give products.
4. Rate depends on the **frequency of successful collisions**.

## Enthalpy profile

| Feature | What it shows |
|---|---|
| Reactants level | Starting enthalpy |
| Peak | Highest enthalpy, at the point of reaction |
| Eₐ | Height from reactants to peak |
| Products level | Final enthalpy |
| ΔH | Difference between products and reactants |

- Exothermic: products lower than reactants, ΔH negative.
- Endothermic: products higher than reactants, ΔH positive.
- Eₐ is always positive and is **not** the same as ΔH.

```
 enthalpy
   ▲          ____
   │ ______  /    \
   │ reactants     \______ products
   │   Eₐ ↑           ΔH ↓
   └──────────────────────► progress of reaction
```

## Why most collisions do not lead to reaction

- Particles in a gas or solution have a range of energies.
- Most particles have energy **less than Eₐ**.
- When these collide, they bounce apart without reacting.
- Only a small fraction have energy of at least Eₐ.
- The higher the Eₐ, the smaller this fraction, and the slower the reaction.

## Ways to increase rate (preview)

| Change | Collision frequency | Proportion successful |
|---|---|---|
| Higher concentration or pressure | Higher | Unchanged |
| Larger surface area | Higher | Unchanged |
| Higher temperature | Higher | Higher |
| Catalyst | Unchanged | Higher (lower Eₐ) |

## Worked reminders

**Define activation energy.** "The minimum energy that colliding particles must have for a reaction to occur."

**Why does a mixture of H₂ and O₂ not react at room temperature?** Very few collisions have energy of at least Eₐ, which is high because strong bonds must break. A spark provides the energy to start the reaction.

**Which reaction is faster at the same temperature, Eₐ = 50 or Eₐ = 90 kJ mol⁻¹?** The reaction with Eₐ = 50, because a larger fraction of collisions has enough energy.

**Can an exothermic reaction be slow?** Yes. ΔH is negative but Eₐ may be large.

**Why does collision frequency not decide the rate alone?** Most collisions are not successful. Rate depends on the frequency of successful collisions.

## Answer pattern for "explain why most collisions do not lead to reaction"

1. Only collisions with energy at least equal to the activation energy lead to reaction.
2. Most particles have energy less than this.
3. So most collisions are unsuccessful and the particles bounce apart.

## Reading numbers from a profile

If reactants are at 0, the peak at +85 and the products at −120 kJ mol⁻¹:

- Eₐ (forward) = 85 − 0 = **85 kJ mol⁻¹**.
- ΔH (forward) = −120 − 0 = **−120 kJ mol⁻¹**, so the reaction is exothermic.
- Eₐ (reverse) = 85 − (−120) = **205 kJ mol⁻¹**, so the reverse reaction is endothermic and much slower at the same temperature.

Always measure Eₐ from the starting level of the reaction you are discussing, up to the peak.

## Common mistakes

- Omitting "minimum" or "colliding particles" from the definition.
- Confusing Eₐ with ΔH.
- Saying "greater than" instead of "equal to or greater than".
- Saying that most particles do not collide.
- Describing activation energy as energy released.
- Saying a catalyst gives particles more energy.
- Drawing the Eₐ arrow from the products rather than the reactants.

## Self-test

1. Define activation energy.
2. State the two conditions for a successful collision.
3. Explain why most collisions in a gas at room temperature do not lead to reaction.
4. Sketch a reaction profile for an endothermic reaction and label Eₐ and ΔH.
5. A reaction has ΔH = −200 kJ mol⁻¹ but is very slow. Explain.
6. Which has the larger rate at the same temperature: a reaction with Eₐ = 30 kJ mol⁻¹ or one with Eₐ = 100 kJ mol⁻¹? Explain.
7. State two ways of increasing the frequency of collisions.
8. Why does a higher temperature increase the proportion of successful collisions?
9. Why is a spark needed to start the reaction of a methane–oxygen mixture?
10. What is the difference between a collision and a successful collision?
11. State the units of activation energy.
12. State the sign of ΔH and of Eₐ for an exothermic reaction.

### Answers

1. The minimum energy that colliding particles must have for a reaction to occur.
2. The particles must collide, and they must have energy equal to or greater than the activation energy.
3. Most particles have energy less than Eₐ, so most collisions are unsuccessful and the particles bounce apart.
4. Reactants low and products higher, a peak above the products; Eₐ from the reactants to the peak; ΔH as an upward arrow from reactants to products.
5. The reaction is strongly exothermic but has a high activation energy, so few collisions are successful.
6. The one with Eₐ = 30, because a larger proportion of collisions have energy of at least Eₐ.
7. Any two of: increase concentration, increase pressure of a gas, increase surface area of a solid, increase temperature.
8. Particles have more kinetic energy, so a greater proportion of collisions have energy of at least Eₐ.
9. The spark gives some molecules energy of at least Eₐ. The heat released then supplies more.
10. A collision is any meeting of particles; a successful collision has energy at least Eₐ and results in a reaction.
11. kJ mol⁻¹.
12. ΔH is negative and Eₐ is positive. They are different quantities.
