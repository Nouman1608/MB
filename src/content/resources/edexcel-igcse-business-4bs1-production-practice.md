These questions cover section 5.2 Production (points 5.2.1 to 5.2.3) of the Pearson Edexcel International GCSE in Business (4BS1) specification, Issue 2, February 2026. Revise with the [production study guide](/resources/edexcel-igcse-business-4bs1-production/) and the [production revision notes](/resources/edexcel-igcse-business-4bs1-production-revision-notes/) first if needed. Every business is invented and amounts are in dollars. Give answers to 2 decimal places and percentages to 1 decimal place where needed.

## Questions

### Section A: shorter questions

**1.** Define batch production. **[2]**

**2.** State the production process used in each case.

**(a)** A shipyard builds a cruise ship to one customer's design. **[1]**
**(b)** A factory fills two million cans of soft drink a week on a continuous line. **[1]**
**(c)** A bakery makes 200 white loaves, cleans the mixer, then makes 150 brown loaves. **[1]**

**3.** Give two principles of lean production. **[2]**

**4.** Westcombe Garments has 18 machinists who each work a 7.5-hour day. They make 1,620 shirts a day. **(Calculation)**

**(a)** Calculate productivity per worker per day. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate productivity per worker-hour. **[1]**
**(c)** After new sewing machines are installed, the same team makes 1,890 shirts a day. Calculate the percentage increase in productivity. **[2]**
**(d)** Machinists are paid 15 an hour. Calculate the labour cost per shirt before and after the new machines. **[2]**

**5.** Outline one impact of flow production on workers. **[2]**

**6.** Explain one benefit of Kaizen to a business. **[3]**

**7.** Explain one risk of just-in-time (JIT) for a car manufacturer. **[3]**

### Section B: longer questions

**8.** Copperfield Candles makes scented candles in runs of 400. Each candle costs 2.10 in materials and labour, and each run costs 360 to set up. It is considering a flow line costing 90,000 a year to run, with materials and labour of 1.20 per candle. It sells about 30,000 candles a year in 12 different scents.

**(a)** Calculate the cost per candle using its current method. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the cost per candle using the flow line at 30,000 and at 60,000 candles a year. **[2]**
**(c)** Analyse whether Copperfield Candles should switch to flow production. **[4]**

**9.** Analyse how using resources more effectively could benefit a busy restaurant. **[4]**

**10.** Hexley Logistics runs a warehouse. A robot picking system would cost 450,000. It would replace 8 pickers, each paid 26,000 a year, and cost 40,000 a year to maintain. Picking errors on 400,000 orders a year would fall from 2% to 0.4%. Each error costs 5 to put right.

**(a)** Calculate the yearly labour saving after maintenance costs. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the yearly saving from fewer errors. **[2]**
**(c)** Justify whether Hexley Logistics should buy the robot picking system. **[6]**

**11.** Stanmore Bakeries plc is a large bakery company. It holds an average of 120,000 of flour. Holding stock costs 18% of its value a year. Under JIT, average flour stock would fall to 20,000. Its flour supplier is 300 kilometres away, and snow sometimes closes roads in winter.

**(a)** Calculate the yearly saving in holding costs from JIT. **[2]**
**(b)** Evaluate whether Stanmore Bakeries should introduce JIT for flour. **[9]**

**12.** **(a)** State one likely effect on product variety of moving from job to flow production. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain why job production is often labour intensive. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** Making a set quantity of identical items together [1], then changing equipment to make a quantity of a different item [1]. **[2]**

**2. (a)** Job production [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** Flow production [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** Batch production [1]. **[1]**

**3.** Any two of: just-in-time (JIT); Kaizen; using resources effectively [1 each]. **[2]**

**4. (a)** 1,620 ÷ 18 = **90** shirts per worker per day [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** 90 ÷ 7.5 = **12** shirts per worker-hour [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** (1,890 − 1,620) ÷ 1,620 × 100 [1] = **16.7%** [1]. **[2]**
**(d)** Before: 15 ÷ 12 = **1.25** per shirt [1]. After: 1,890 ÷ 135 worker-hours = 14 shirts per worker-hour, so 15 ÷ 14 = **1.07** per shirt [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* The productivity rise lowers labour cost per shirt even though the wage rate is unchanged. Always state the unit, such as "shirts per worker-hour".

**5.** Workers repeat the same simple task many times [1], so the work can become boring, which may lower motivation and increase errors or absence [1]. **[2]**

**6.** Workers suggest many small improvements to how they do their jobs [1], so waste of time and materials is gradually removed [1], which lowers costs and can improve quality, and staff feel more involved [1]. **[3]**

**7.** A car maker needs thousands of parts to arrive exactly when needed [1], so if one supplier's delivery is late, there is no stock to fall back on [1], and the whole assembly line may stop, losing output and delaying orders to customers [1]. **[3]**

**8. (a)** 2.10 + 360 ÷ 400 [1] = 2.10 + 0.90 = **3.00** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** At 30,000: 1.20 + 90,000 ÷ 30,000 = **4.20** [1]. At 60,000: 1.20 + 90,000 ÷ 60,000 = **2.70** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** At its current sales of 30,000 candles, flow costs 4.20 per candle, which is 1.20 more than the current 3.00 [1]. Flow would only be cheaper at much higher sales, such as 60,000 [1], which is double what Copperfield sells. It also makes 12 scents, and a flow line suits continuous production of one standard product, so switching scents would waste time [1]. Copperfield should keep its current method unless it expects demand to double and can reduce the range of scents [1]. **[4]**

**9.** The restaurant could order fresh ingredients in quantities closer to daily demand [1], so less food is thrown away [1], which lowers the cost of each meal [1] and allows lower prices or higher profit, and less waste may also appeal to customers who care about the environment [1]. **[4]**

**10. (a)** 8 × 26,000 = 208,000 [1]; 208,000 − 40,000 = **168,000** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Before: 0.02 × 400,000 × 5 = 40,000; after: 0.004 × 400,000 × 5 = 8,000 [1]; saving = **32,000** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** For buying: total yearly savings are 168,000 + 32,000 = 200,000, so the 450,000 cost is recovered in about 2.25 years [1]. Fewer errors mean more orders are correct first time, which improves customer satisfaction [1]. Against: 450,000 is a large sum to find [1], and if the robots break down, picking stops for everyone at once [1]. Robots may be less flexible than people when the range of goods or order sizes changes, and 8 jobs are lost, which may lower morale among remaining staff [1]. Judgement: for example, buy the system, because the cost is recovered quickly and both productivity and quality improve, provided Hexley has a maintenance contract and retrains some pickers to supervise the robots [1]. **[6]**

**11. (a)** Now: 0.18 × 120,000 = 21,600; with JIT: 0.18 × 20,000 = 3,600 [1]; saving = **18,000** a year [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** For JIT: it saves 18,000 a year in holding costs [1] and releases 100,000 of cash tied up in flour, which could be used elsewhere in the business [1]. Less flour in store means less risk of it going stale or being damaged, so less waste [1]. Against JIT: the supplier is 300 kilometres away, so deliveries take time and are harder to speed up if demand rises [1]. Snow can close roads in winter, so a delivery could be held up [1], and a bakery cannot bake without flour, so production of bread for supermarkets could stop [1], leading to lost sales and damage to Stanmore's reputation with its customers [1]. Judgement: for example, Stanmore should not move to full JIT [1]; it could cut flour stock part of the way, keeping a safety stock in winter, or find a supplier closer to its bakeries [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* The case gives two clear risks (distance and winter roads). An evaluation that ignores them and just lists JIT benefits will not reach a supported judgement.

**12. (a)** Product variety falls, as flow produces identical items [1]. **[1]**
**(b)** Each job is different, so tasks cannot easily be done by specialised machines [1]. Skilled workers are needed to adapt to each customer's requirements [1], so labour makes up a large part of the cost of each item compared with machinery [1]. **[3]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calling job production the cheapest method.
- Forgetting to spread set-up or fixed costs across the number of units.
- Giving productivity without units, or dividing by cost instead of workers.
- Listing JIT benefits without the supplier risk shown in the case.
- Judging robots on cost alone.
