Condensed recall for subtopic **1.1 Business activity**. For full explanations and worked examples, use the [Business activity study guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-business-7115-business-activity/).

## The core chain

Unlimited wants + limited resources = **scarcity** → every choice has an **opportunity cost** → businesses **specialise** to use resources efficiently → they combine factors of production to make goods and services that satisfy needs and wants → the difference between what customers pay and what bought-in inputs cost is the **added value**.

## Needs, wants and scarcity

- A **need** is essential for survival: food, water, shelter, basic clothing.
- A **want** is desired but not essential: a new phone, a holiday, a branded drink.
- Wants are unlimited because people always desire more; resources are limited.
- **Scarcity** is the problem of limited resources against unlimited wants. It applies to rich people and countries too, not only to the poor.

## Opportunity cost

- The **next best alternative given up** when a choice is made.
- It is one named option, the second choice, not all the options rejected.
- It applies to money, time, land and workers: a field used for maize cannot also grow cotton.
- Businesses face it in every decision: spending on advertising instead of new equipment, or using staff time on one order instead of another.

## Factors of production

| Factor | Meaning | Example for a bakery |
|---|---|---|
| Land | Natural resources, including the site | Wheat, water, the shop's plot |
| Labour | Human effort | Bakers, counter staff |
| Capital | Man-made equipment and buildings | Ovens, mixers, delivery van |
| Enterprise | Organising the other factors and taking risk | The owner who starts the bakery |

## Specialisation

**Benefits:** higher output per worker, skills improve with repetition, less time lost switching tasks, specialist machinery can be used, easier recruitment and training.

**Limitations:** boredom and lower motivation, the whole process may stop if one stage fails, dependence on other businesses, workers with narrow skills may find it harder to change jobs.

The **division of labour** is specialisation by task inside one business. Specialisation makes **exchange** necessary, and money makes exchange easier.

## Purpose of business activity

Combine scarce factors of production → produce goods and services → satisfy needs and wants → provide jobs and incomes → (usually) earn profit for the owners as the reward for risk.

## Added value

```
added value = selling price − cost of bought-in materials and components
```

- Per unit unless you multiply by quantity sold.
- Pays for wages, rent and other overheads; profit is what is left.
- **Increase it** by raising what customers will pay (quality, design, brand, service, convenience, packaging) or by cutting bought-in costs (cheaper suppliers, bulk buying, less waste).
- Each method has a risk: higher-quality inputs cost more; cheaper inputs may hurt quality; higher prices may reduce sales.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Need | A good or service essential for survival |
| Want | A good or service people desire but can live without |
| Scarcity | Limited resources compared with unlimited wants |
| Opportunity cost | The next best alternative given up when a choice is made |
| Specialisation | Concentrating on a narrow range of tasks or products |
| Division of labour | Splitting production into separate tasks done by different workers |
| Factors of production | Land, labour, capital and enterprise |
| Added value | Selling price minus the cost of bought-in materials and components |

## Exam traps

- **Opportunity cost is not money spent.** It is the alternative given up; name it from the case.
- **Added value is not profit**, and it is not revenue.
- **"Increase the price" is not enough.** Explain why customers would accept it.
- **Specialisation answers must be applied**: say how it affects this business's output, costs or reliability.
- **Scarcity is not poverty.** Even a profitable business has limited funds and must choose.
- **Classifying a product as a need** needs the survival test: bread is a need, a particular brand of bread is a want.

## Self-test

1. Define opportunity cost.
2. A farmer can plant wheat, rice or vegetables on one field and ranks them in that order. What is the opportunity cost of planting wheat?
3. State the four factors of production.
4. A café buys ingredients costing $1.40 for a sandwich it sells for $4.50. Calculate the added value.
5. The café sells 300 sandwiches a week. Calculate the weekly added value.
6. Why is the answer to question 5 not the café's profit?
7. Give two ways the café could increase added value without changing its suppliers.
8. Explain one benefit of specialisation to a car manufacturer.
9. Explain one limitation of specialisation to its workers.
10. Why does specialisation make exchange necessary?
11. A business spends $5,000 on advertising instead of a staff party. State the opportunity cost.
12. What is the purpose of business activity?

## Answers

1. The next best alternative given up when a choice is made.
2. **Rice**, the second choice.
3. Land, labour, capital and enterprise.
4. $4.50 − $1.40 = **$3.10**.
5. 300 × $3.10 = **$930**.
6. Added value must still pay wages, rent and other overheads; profit is what remains after those.
7. Any two, for example: a better-known brand or attractive packaging that supports a higher price; table service or delivery that customers will pay more for; a more convenient opening time or location.
8. Workers repeating one task become faster and more accurate, so output per worker rises and the cost per car may fall.
9. Repeating one task can be boring, lowering motivation; narrow skills may make it harder to move to other jobs.
10. A specialist produces only a narrow range of goods, so it must trade its output for everything else it needs.
11. **The staff party** (the alternative given up).
12. To combine scarce factors of production to make goods and services that satisfy people's needs and wants, providing jobs and, for most private businesses, a profit.
