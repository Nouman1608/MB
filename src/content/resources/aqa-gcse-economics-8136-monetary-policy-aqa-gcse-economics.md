## What monetary policy is and who runs it

Monetary policy is the use of **interest rates** (and the supply of money) to influence the level of spending in the economy. In the UK it is carried out by the **Bank of England**, the central bank. The Bank's Monetary Policy Committee decides the **Bank Rate**, which feeds through to the rates that high-street banks charge on loans and mortgages and pay on savings.

The government sets the target, an inflation rate of 2% measured by the CPI, but the Bank decides how to meet it. Separating the two means interest-rate decisions are less likely to be shaped by short-term political pressures, such as a wish to cut rates before an election.

## How a rise in interest rates reduces inflation

A higher Bank Rate works through several channels at once. Strong answers pick one or two and follow them through fully.

**1. Borrowing and debt repayments.** Loans, credit cards and variable-rate mortgages cost more. Households with these debts have less left to spend, and fewer people take out new loans for cars or home improvements.

**2. Saving.** Saving becomes more rewarding, so some households save a larger share of their income and spend less.

**3. Business investment.** Firms often borrow to buy machinery or build premises. When borrowing costs more, fewer projects are worth doing, so investment spending falls.

**4. Confidence and house prices.** Dearer mortgages reduce demand for houses, so house prices may rise more slowly or fall. Homeowners who feel less wealthy tend to spend less.

**5. The exchange rate.** Higher UK interest rates attract savings from abroad, which raises demand for pounds. A stronger pound makes imports cheaper in pounds, lowering the prices of imported goods directly.

All five channels reduce **total demand**. With less spending, firms face less pressure to raise prices, so demand-pull inflation slows. A cut in interest rates works the other way.

### Worked example: a household and a firm (invented)

**A household.** A family has an interest-only mortgage of £180,000 on a variable rate. At 3% it pays 0.03 × 180,000 = **£5,400** a year in interest. At 5.5% it pays 0.055 × 180,000 = **£9,900**. The extra **£4,500** a year must come from other spending.

**A firm.** A bakery could borrow £200,000 for a new oven line that would earn an extra £12,000 a year, a return of 12,000 ÷ 200,000 × 100 = **6%**. At an interest rate of 4% the loan costs £8,000 a year, so the project adds £4,000 to profit and goes ahead. At 7% the loan costs £14,000 a year, more than the project earns, so the bakery does not invest.

These two cases show why higher rates reduce both consumer spending and investment.

## Who gains and who loses from higher interest rates

| Group | Effect of a rise |
|---|---|
| Borrowers on variable rates | Repayments rise at once; less to spend |
| Borrowers on fixed rates | No change until the fixed deal ends, then a possibly large rise |
| Savers | Higher returns on savings |
| Firms with large loans | Higher costs; less investment |
| Exporters | Hurt if the pound strengthens, as their goods become dearer abroad |
| Importers and consumers of imported goods | Gain from a stronger pound |
| Government | Higher interest on new borrowing |

Because many mortgages in the UK are on fixed rates for a few years, a rise in Bank Rate can take a long time to reach all households.

## Monetary policy and other objectives

| Objective | Policy | Chain | Conflict |
|---|---|---|---|
| Growth | Lower interest rates | Cheaper borrowing → more consumer spending and investment → more output | Faster demand may push inflation above target |
| Full employment | Lower interest rates | More demand → firms need more workers | Same conflict with inflation |
| Balance of payments | Higher interest rates | Less spending at home → fewer imports | Stronger pound makes exports dearer; slower growth |

The Bank of England's priority is the inflation target, so it will not usually cut rates to boost growth if that would push inflation well above 2%. In a deep downturn with low inflation, though, cutting rates helps several objectives at once. In March 2009, during the financial crisis, the Bank cut Bank Rate to 0.5%, and from 2009 it also used quantitative easing, creating money to buy bonds, when cutting rates further was not possible.

## Limits of monetary policy

- **Time lags:** changes take many months, often more than a year, to have their full effect, so the Bank must act on forecasts.
- **Low confidence:** in a recession, households and firms may not borrow even at very low rates.
- **One rate for the whole country:** a rate that suits a booming region may be too high for a struggling one.
- **Cost-push inflation:** higher rates cannot lower world energy or food prices; they reduce inflation only by reducing demand, which costs output and jobs.
- **Distribution:** borrowers and savers are affected in opposite directions.

## Annotated model answer

*Question (original):* Explain how a cut in the Bank Rate could reduce unemployment. **[4]**

*Model answer:* A cut in the Bank Rate leads banks to lower the interest rates on loans and mortgages [first step]. Borrowing becomes cheaper, so households spend more on goods such as cars and furniture, and firms invest in new equipment [second step: spending rises]. Firms selling more need to raise output [third step], so they hire more workers, which reduces cyclical unemployment [links to the objective and names the type].

## Common mistakes

- Saying "the government sets interest rates"; in the UK the Bank of England does.
- Calling interest rates fiscal policy.
- Stopping the chain at "people borrow less" without reaching total demand and prices.
- Claiming a rate cut always raises spending, ignoring confidence.
- Forgetting the exchange-rate channel when a question is about trade.

## Exam technique

- **Define** questions: "the use of interest rates and the money supply to influence the economy" plus who runs it.
- **Explain** questions: interest rate → cost of borrowing or reward for saving → spending → total demand → prices or output and jobs.
- **Evaluate** questions: weigh the target objective against a conflict, consider time lags and confidence, and use the type of inflation in the case.
