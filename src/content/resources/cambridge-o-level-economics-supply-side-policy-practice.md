## Questions

**1.** Define supply-side policy. **[2]**

**2.** Explain one difference between supply-side policy and policy that works through changing spending. **[3]**

**3.** A pottery workshop employs 8 workers. They make 400 mugs a week and the weekly wage bill is 4,800. After a government-funded training scheme the same workers make 460 mugs a week and the wage bill is unchanged.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage change in output per worker. **[3]**
**(b)** Work out the labour cost of one mug before and after the scheme, then the percentage change, to one decimal place. **[3]**

**4.** Explain two ways in which labour market reforms could reduce unemployment. **[4]**

**5.** A worker in Country R is offered an extra hour at 40. The tax rate on the extra pay is cut from 35% to 25%.

**(a)** Calculate the amount she keeps from the hour under each tax rate. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage rise in the amount kept, to one decimal place. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain one reason why the tax cut might not lead her to work more hours. **[2]**

**6.** A firm makes a profit of 150,000. Corporation tax is cut from 25% to 20%.

**(a)** Calculate the tax paid before and after, and the change in government revenue if profit does not change. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate the profit needed for the government to collect the same revenue as before at the new rate. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why a government might still cut the rate. **[2]**

**7.** A state-owned bus company is sold to private investors.

**(a)** Explain how privatisation could improve efficiency. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain one way privatisation could harm consumers. **[3]**

**8.** A government removes licence limits on taxi firms in a city. The average fare falls from 8 to 6.80.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage change in the fare. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why more firms in the market might lead to lower fares. **[3]**
**(c)** State and explain one drawback of removing the limits. **[2]**

**9.** Country D carried out supply-side reforms over three years. The figures are illustrative.

| | Before | After |
|---|---|---|
| Labour force (million) | 20.0 | 20.5 |
| Unemployed (million) | 1.6 | 1.025 |
| Real GDP (billion) | 300 | 321 |
| Price index | 100 | 106 |
| Exports (billion) | 80 | 92 |
| Imports (billion) | 90 | 96 |

**(a)** Calculate the unemployment rate before and after. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage growth of real GDP. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the percentage rise in the price index. **[1]**
**(d)** Calculate the balance of trade (exports minus imports) before and after. **[2]**
**(e)** Explain how supply-side policy could have contributed to the change in the balance of trade. **[3]**

**10.** Explain how supply-side policy could help a government achieve (a) stable prices and (b) balance of payments stability. **[6]**

**11.** Evaluate whether education and training is the most effective supply-side measure for reducing unemployment. **[8]**

**12.** Country E (invented) has taxable income of 200 billion and an income tax rate of 30%. The government cuts the rate to 25% to improve incentives to work.

**(a)** Calculate the revenue before and after the cut if taxable income stays at 200 billion. **[2]**
**(b)** The cut raises taxable income by 8%. Calculate the new taxable income and revenue. **[2]**
**(c)** Evaluate whether the cut is a good way to achieve economic growth and redistribution of income. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** Government action to increase the economy's ability to produce [1] by raising the productivity and efficiency of labour, capital and markets [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Include productive capacity or the ability to produce in the definition. "Policy that helps the supply side" repeats the question and gives little to reward.

**2.** Supply-side policy aims to raise productive capacity or potential output, for example through training [1]. Fiscal or monetary policy changes how much people and firms spend, for example through lower interest rates [1]. Supply-side effects are usually slower and lasting, while spending changes work faster but can push up prices [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Both policies must appear in a comparison. Writing about supply-side policy alone answers a different question.

**3. (a)** Before: 400 ÷ 8 = 50 mugs per worker [1]. After: 460 ÷ 8 = 57.5 [1]. Change = (57.5 − 50) ÷ 50 × 100 = **15%** [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** Before: 4,800 ÷ 400 = 12 [1]. After: 4,800 ÷ 460 = 10.43 [1]. Change = (10.43 − 12) ÷ 12 × 100 = **−13.0%** [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the division before the percentage. A clear method line lets a small slip stay small.

**4.** Easier hiring and dismissal, or less union blocking of change, lowers the risk of employing someone [1], so firms are more willing to take on workers and unemployment falls [1]. Better job information and retraining help jobseekers find vacancies and move between regions and occupations [1], so the time spent unemployed falls [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Two separate ways are needed, each with a link to lower unemployment. Two examples of the same idea earn credit once.

**5. (a)** Before: 40 × 0.65 = 26 [1]. After: 40 × 0.75 = 30 [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** (30 − 26) ÷ 26 × 100 = **15.4%** [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** She may reach her target income with fewer hours, so she works less [1], because higher take-home pay on each hour lets her earn the same total in less time [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A reason must be developed. "People might not respond" is a statement. Add why they might not.

**6. (a)** Before: 150,000 × 0.25 = 37,500 [1]. After: 150,000 × 0.20 = 30,000 [1]. Revenue falls by **7,500** [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** 37,500 ÷ 0.20 [1] = **187,500** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Firms keep more profit to reinvest, so investment and output may rise [1], and the extra activity may bring in tax from workers and spending, offsetting some lost revenue [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Part (b) needs revenue divided by the new rate. Using the old rate gives the original profit, which is a common slip.

**7. (a)** Private owners want profit, so they have a reason to cut waste and costs [1]. If rivals exist, competition pushes the firm to keep costs low and service good [1], so output per unit of input rises and unit costs fall [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** If the firm has no rivals on its routes, it may raise fares [1] because customers have no alternative [1]. Routes that lose money may be cut, leaving some areas with no service [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* A harm should be tied to the situation. The reason a private owner can raise prices (little competition) is what turns a statement into analysis.

**8. (a)** (6.80 − 8) ÷ 8 × 100 [1] = **−15%** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** More firms means more competition for passengers [1]. A firm that charges more loses customers to rivals [1], so firms cut fares and costs to attract passengers [1]. **[3]**
**(c)** Safety standards or driver conditions may be weaker [1], because firms cut costs and the rules may have been protecting passengers [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A drawback needs a reason. State what goes wrong and why removing the rule causes it.

**9. (a)** Before: 1.6 ÷ 20.0 × 100 = 8.0% [1]. After: 1.025 ÷ 20.5 × 100 = **5.0%** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** (321 − 300) ÷ 300 × 100 = **7%** [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** (106 − 100) ÷ 100 × 100 = **6%** [1]. **[1]**
**(d)** Before: 80 − 90 = −10 [1]. After: 92 − 96 = **−4** [1]. The deficit narrows. **[2]**
**(e)** Lower costs or better quality help firms sell more exports [1]. Exports rose by 12, more than imports at 6 [1]. Better home products may also reduce the need to import, so the deficit fell from 10 to 4 [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the figures given. A link to the data (exports rose by more than imports) is more convincing than general statements.

**10. (a)** Training, investment and competition raise output per worker and lower unit costs [1]. Firms can hold prices down or cut them [1], so inflation is lower, unless wages rise as fast as productivity [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** Lower costs and better quality make exports more competitive abroad [1]. Home goods become more attractive than imports [1], so exports rise and imports fall, which narrows a deficit [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Write a chain for each named aim.

**11.** Award credit for balanced points such as these:
- Training matches skills to vacancies, which reduces structural unemployment [1].
- Skilled workers are more productive, so firms may hire more as costs per unit fall [1].
- It is slow: courses and results take years [1].
- It costs the government money with an opportunity cost [1].
- Trained workers may leave, or skills may not match jobs [1].
- Labour market reform could act faster by easing hiring [1].
- Incentives or lower taxes might draw more people into work, but they do not teach skills [1].
- Judgement: training is likely best where unemployment comes from missing skills, but a package works better, because no single measure fixes every cause [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* The judgement should answer "most effective" and give a reason. A list of advantages and disadvantages with no decision leaves the question unanswered.

**12. (a)** Before: 200 × 0.30 = 60 billion [1]. After: 200 × 0.25 = 50 billion [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** New income = 200 × 1.08 = 216 billion [1]. Revenue = 216 × 0.25 = **54 billion** [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** Growth: more reward for work may increase labour supply and effort, raising output [1]. Revenue still falls by 6 billion (60 − 54), which may reduce spending on services such as schools [1]. Break-even income is 60 ÷ 0.25 = 240 billion, a rise of 20%, which is unlikely from a rate cut alone [1]. Redistribution: the cut gives most cash to high earners and may widen the gap [1]. Lower revenue could limit welfare help for the poor [1]. Judgement: the cut is weak for redistribution and may help growth only if the response is large and timely, so a targeted measure such as training for low earners may do better [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Use your own numbers from parts (a) and (b) in the evaluation. Analysis that uses the figures earns more than general comment.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Defining supply-side policy as any policy that affects the economy, without productive capacity.
- Describing a measure without a chain to the named macroeconomic aim.
- Forgetting the "× 100" in percentage changes, or using the new figure as the base instead of the old one.
- Calculating break-even revenue with the old tax rate.
- Saying a tax cut will increase tax revenue without any figures or reasoning.
- Giving only benefits when asked to evaluate, and ending without a judgement.
- Mixing up privatisation (ownership) with deregulation (rules).
- Ignoring the data in a table and writing general points.
