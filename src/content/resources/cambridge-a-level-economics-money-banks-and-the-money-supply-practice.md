These questions cover syllabus outcomes 9.4.1 to 9.4.5 (Money and banking) in the Cambridge International AS and A Level Economics 9708 syllabus for examination in 2026, 2027 and 2028. Topic 9 is A Level content. Country C uses the lira and Country D uses the tola. All figures are invented for illustration and are not real statistics.

Answers show each creditworthy step with a number in square brackets, and the bold figure at the end of each answer is the question total. Work each question on paper first. ## Questions

**1.** Define money and state two of its functions. **[3]**

**2.** A village uses blocks of salt as money. The blocks differ in size and break easily. Explain two characteristics of good money that the salt blocks lack. **[4]**

**3.** In Country C (million lira): notes and coins held by the public 55; demand deposits 185; savings deposits 240; cash held in bank vaults 30.

**(a)** Calculate narrow money, defined as notes and coins held by the public plus demand deposits. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate broad money, defined as narrow money plus savings deposits. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain why the 30 of vault cash is left out of both measures. **[2]**

**4.** In Country D, M = 250 million tola, V = 8 and T = 1,000 million transactions.

**(a)** Calculate the price level, P. **[2]**
**(b)** M rises to 300, with V and T unchanged. Calculate the new P and its percentage change. **[2]**
**(c)** With M at 300, V then falls to 6. Calculate P. **[2]**

**5.** Explain two reasons why MV = PT does not by itself show that an increase in the money supply causes inflation. **[4]**

**6.** Bank Z has cash and reserves of 81, securities of 219 and loans of 600 (million lira). It has deposits of 810 and equity of 90.

**(a)** Calculate its reserve ratio. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate its capital ratio, using total assets. **[2]**
**(c)** A regulator raises the required capital ratio to 12%. Calculate the maximum assets Bank Z can now hold and the fall in assets it needs if equity stays at 90. **[3]**
**(d)** Explain one conflict between liquidity and profitability for Bank Z. **[2]**

**7.** A new deposit of 2,000 million lira is paid into the banking system of Country C. The reserve ratio is 12.5%.

**(a)** Calculate the bank credit multiplier. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the maximum total deposits and the maximum credit created. **[3]**
**(c)** Give two reasons why the actual increase may be lower. **[2]**

**8.** The government of Country D has a budget deficit of 480 million tola. Explain how the effect on the money supply differs if the deficit is financed by selling bonds to (i) the non-bank public and (ii) the central bank, which assumes a reserve ratio of 8%. **[6]**

**9.** A bond pays 3 lira a year. Its price is 100 before a quantitative easing programme and 120 after it.

**(a)** Calculate the yield before and after. **[2]**
**(b)** Evaluate the effectiveness of quantitative easing as a way of raising the money supply. **[6]**

**10.** Country D fixes its exchange rate at 3.00 tola per unit of foreign currency and has a balance of payments surplus. Net inflows of foreign currency are 40 million units.

**(a)** Calculate the domestic currency the central bank pays to buy the foreign currency. **[1]**
**(b)** The reserve ratio is 10%. Calculate the maximum increase in deposits that this inflow could support. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain how the central bank could offset the effect on the money supply, and why the exchange rate system matters. **[4]**

**11.** Assess whether credit creation by commercial banks is the most important cause of changes in the money supply in an open economy. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** Money is anything generally accepted as payment for goods and services and as settlement of debt [1]. Medium of exchange [1]. Any one other function, such as store of value, unit of account or standard of deferred payment [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* A definition that names only one function, such as exchange, is incomplete; include acceptance as payment and for debts.

**2.** Uniformity: units must be equal in value, but blocks of different sizes are not equivalent, so prices are hard to state and compare [2]. Durability: money must last, but breaking blocks destroys value, so it fails as a store of value [2]. (Accept divisibility, with a reason linked to the context.) **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Naming a characteristic earns less than showing, in the context given, why its absence matters.

**3. (a)** 55 + 185 [1] = **240 million lira** [1].
**(b)** 240 + 240 = **480 million lira** [1].
**(c)** The money supply counts money held by the public [1]. Vault cash is the banks' own reserve, not circulating or spent by the public [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the addition and state the unit; a clear definition of each measure keeps (a) and (b) consistent.

**4. (a)** P = MV ÷ T = (250 × 8) ÷ 1,000 [1] = **2.00** [1].
**(b)** P = (300 × 8) ÷ 1,000 = **2.40** [1]; percentage change = (0.40 ÷ 2.00) × 100 = **20%** [1].
**(c)** P = (300 × 6) ÷ 1,000 [1] = **1.80** [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Write the rearranged formula first; showing the substitution lets a reader follow your method and see where a slip occurred.

**5.** MV = PT is an identity, so it holds by definition and does not show which variable causes another [2]. Velocity V may change, for example falling when people hoard money, so P need not rise with M; alternatively T may rise if there is spare capacity, so extra money raises output rather than prices [2]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* A reason gains most when it names the variable that offsets the rise in M and says which way it moves.

**6. (a)** Reserve ratio = cash and reserves ÷ deposits = 81 ÷ 810 [1] = **10%** [1].
**(b)** Capital ratio = 90 ÷ 900 [1] = **10%** [1].
**(c)** Maximum assets = 90 ÷ 0.12 [1] = 750 [1]. Assets must fall by 900 − 750 = **150 million lira** [1].
**(d)** Cash and reserves are perfectly liquid but earn no interest [1]. Moving funds into loans raises profit but makes it harder to meet sudden withdrawals [1]. **[9]**
*Examiner insight:* State which ratio you use in (a) and (b), and use total assets as the question says; mixing deposits and assets is a common slip.

**7. (a)** 1 ÷ 0.125 = **8** [1].
**(b)** Total deposits = 2,000 × 8 [1] = **16,000** [1]. Credit created = 16,000 − 2,000 = **14,000 million lira** [1].
**(c)** Any two: the public keeps some money as cash; banks hold more than the minimum reserve; weak demand for loans; capital requirements limit assets [2]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Remember to subtract the original deposit for credit created, and describe the figure in (b) as a maximum.

**8.** (i) Non-bank public: buyers pay with existing deposits, which transfer to the government [1]. When the government spends, the money returns to the public, so the money supply does not rise at the start [1]. (ii) Central bank: it creates new reserves to buy the bonds [1]. When the government spends 480, banks' reserves rise by 480 [1]. At 8% the multiplier is 1 ÷ 0.08 = 12.5 [1], so deposits could rise by up to 480 × 12.5 = **6,000 million tola**, far more than (i) [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* A clear comparison needs both cases, with the buyer of the bonds named each time.

**9. (a)** Before: 3 ÷ 100 = **3%** [1]. After: 3 ÷ 120 = **2.5%** [1].
**(b)** The central bank buys bonds with newly created reserves [1]. This raises bond prices and lowers yields, and sellers and banks gain deposits and reserves [1], so borrowing may become cheaper and lending rise [1]. It is more effective when banks are willing to lend and firms and households want to borrow [1]. It is weaker if banks hold the reserves or demand is low after a downturn, or if the extra money mostly raises asset prices [1]. Judgement: it raises the money supply directly through deposits, but its effect on lending depends on conditions [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* An evaluation point needs a condition (who lends, who borrows) and a judgement, not only a list of disadvantages.

**10. (a)** 40 × 3.00 = **120 million tola** [1].
**(b)** 120 ÷ 0.10 = **1,200 million tola** [1], a maximum, because the multiplier shows the greatest possible change [1].
**(c)** Sterilisation: the central bank sells 120 million tola of bonds [1], taking reserves out of the banks and cancelling the increase [1]. This is needed because the fixed rate obliges it to buy the foreign currency [1]. Under a floating rate the currency would appreciate and the central bank need not buy it, so the money supply would be less affected [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* A surplus raises the money supply only if domestic currency is created to buy the foreign currency; say how.

**11.** Credit creation: banks' lending creates deposits, and the multiplier (1 ÷ reserve ratio) makes this large; it works day to day through loans [1], and the actual size depends on loan demand and bank willingness [1]. Other causes: a central bank can add reserves by deficit financing or QE [1]; a payments surplus under a fixed rate raises reserves [1]; a deficit lowers them [1]. Analysis: these others often work through banks' reserves and credit creation, so credit creation may be the transmission channel [2]. Evaluation: in a crisis the central bank's actions or QE may dominate if banks do not lend [1]; in an open economy with a fixed rate, external flows could dominate [1]. Judgement: credit creation is usually the main everyday cause, but its importance depends on the exchange rate system and the central bank's actions [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* A reasoned conclusion that says which cause matters most, and under which conditions, is stronger than a list of causes.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Giving the money supply as a list of causes rather than the definition asked for in a definition question.
- Leaving out the unit (million lira or tola) from final answers.
- Using the credit multiplier without calling it a maximum, or forgetting to subtract the original deposit.
- Mixing up the reserve ratio and the capital ratio.
- Treating QE as "printing money" without a chain of reasoning through yields, banks and lending.
- Giving the deficit-financing effect without saying who buys the bonds.
- Stating that a payments surplus raises the money supply without mentioning the fixed rate or intervention.
- Making an evaluation point with no condition or conclusion.
