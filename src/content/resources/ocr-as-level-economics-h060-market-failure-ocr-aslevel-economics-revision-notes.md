These notes condense the specification requirement to explain "Market failure" and the six marginal terms (MSC, MEC, MPC, MSB, MEB and MPB) from topic 2.7 Market failure and externalities, Component 01, Microeconomics, of the OCR *AS Level Economics (H060)* specification, Version 3.0 (June 2026). They provide the toolkit that the externality and intervention topics build on. For the full concrete-market example, read the [market failure study guide](/resources/ocr-as-level-economics-h060-market-failure-ocr-aslevel-economics/). Then practise with the [market failure practice questions](/resources/ocr-as-level-economics-h060-market-failure-ocr-aslevel-economics-practice/). All figures are invented.

## Core recall

- **Market failure:** the free market allocates resources inefficiently, so society's welfare is not maximised.
- The benchmark is **allocative efficiency**, where **MSB = MSC**.
- The market produces where **MPB = MPC**. Failure occurs when the two outputs differ.
- **Partial** failure: the market exists but gives the wrong quantity or price. **Complete** failure: a missing market.
- Market failure is about efficiency, not fairness.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| MPC | Marginal private cost: cost to the producer of one more unit |
| MEC | Marginal external cost: cost to third parties of one more unit |
| MSC | Marginal social cost = MPC + MEC |
| MPB | Marginal private benefit: benefit to the consumer of one more unit |
| MEB | Marginal external benefit: benefit to third parties of one more unit |
| MSB | Marginal social benefit = MPB + MEB |
| Third party | Anyone affected who is neither the buyer nor the seller |
| Social optimum | Output where MSB = MSC |
| Welfare (deadweight) loss | Net loss to society from producing above or below the social optimum |
| Missing market | No market exists for something society values, usually because property rights cannot be assigned |

## Curve map

| Curve | Diagram role |
|---|---|
| MPB | Demand curve |
| MPC | Supply curve |
| MSB | Above MPB if there is an external benefit; the same curve if not |
| MSC | Above MPC if there is an external cost; the same curve if not |

## Method: market output, optimum and welfare loss

1. Set MPB = MPC to find market output.
2. Build MSC = MPC + MEC and MSB = MPB + MEB.
3. Set MSB = MSC to find the social optimum.
4. Welfare loss = ½ × (gap in output) × (gap between MSC and MSB at the market output).

**Worked reminder: external cost.** MPB = 60 − 3Q, MPC = 10 + 2Q, MEC = £10. Market: 60 − 3Q = 10 + 2Q, so Q = 10. Optimum: 60 − 3Q = 20 + 2Q, so Q = 8. At Q = 10, MSC = 40 and MSB = 30. Welfare loss = ½ × 2 × 10 = **10**.

**Worked reminder: external benefit.** MPB = 30 − Q, MEB = £6, so MSB = 36 − Q. MPC = MSC = 6 + Q. Market: Q = 12. Optimum: Q = 15. At Q = 12, MSB = 24 and MSC = 18. Welfare loss = ½ × 3 × 6 = **9**: underproduction.

## Causes and matching interventions

| Cause | Result | Typical intervention named in the specification |
|---|---|---|
| External cost | Overproduction or overconsumption | Taxation, regulation, tradable pollution permits |
| External benefit | Underproduction or underconsumption | Subsidies, government expenditure |
| Missing market | Resource used without being priced | Legislation; permits that create a market |
| Poor information | Wrong level of demand | Information provision, regulation |
| Market power | Output restricted, price raised | Competition policy |
| Price instability | Hardship for producers, under-investment | Buffer stocks, price controls |

## Evaluation points

- The MEC or MEB is hard to measure in money, so the "right" correction is uncertain.
- A small welfare loss may not be worth the cost of intervening.
- Intervention can cause government failure.
- External effects may cross borders or appear years later.
- The judgement depends on the size of the loss, how well it can be measured and whether a cheap tool exists.

## Exam traps

- MSC = MPC **plus** MEC, never minus.
- The welfare loss triangle lies between the optimum and the market output, between MSC and MSB.
- An external cost means the market output is **too high**; an external benefit means it is **too low**.
- Do not call any loss-making or collapsing market a market failure.
- Market failure is a reason to consider intervention, not proof that it will work.
- Label every curve on a diagram; unlabelled lines earn little.

## Self-test

1. Define market failure.
2. State the condition for the social optimum.
3. What is the difference between partial and complete market failure?
4. MPC is £14 and MEC is £5. Calculate MSC.
5. MSB is £40 and MPB is £32. Calculate MEB.
6. Who is a third party?
7. In a market with an external cost only, is output too high or too low? Why?
8. MPB = 50 − 2Q, MPC = 5 + Q, MEC = £6. Find the market output and the social optimum.
9. In question 8, calculate the welfare loss.
10. Give one example of a missing market.
11. Name one cause of market failure other than externalities.
12. Give two reasons why market failure may not justify intervention.

### Answers

1. When the free market allocates resources inefficiently, so the output of a good differs from the level that maximises society's welfare.
2. MSB = MSC.
3. Partial: the market exists but gives the wrong quantity or price. Complete: no market exists (a missing market).
4. 14 + 5 = **£19**.
5. 40 − 32 = **£8**.
6. Someone affected by a transaction who is neither the buyer nor the seller.
7. Too high, because producers ignore the external cost, so MSC is above MPC and the market produces beyond MSB = MSC.
8. Market: 50 − 2Q = 5 + Q, so **Q = 15**. Optimum: 50 − 2Q = 11 + Q, so **Q = 13**.
9. At Q = 15, MSC = 26 and MSB = 20, a gap of 6. Welfare loss = ½ × 2 × 6 = **6**.
10. For example, clean air or a quiet neighbourhood, which cannot be bought from a polluter because no one owns it.
11. Any one of: poor information, market power, price instability, missing markets.
12. Any two of: the external effect is hard to measure; the cost of intervening may exceed the loss; intervention may cause government failure; the effect may be small.
