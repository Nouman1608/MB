These questions cover section 3.1 of the OCR GCSE (9-1) Economics specification (J205, Version 2.0, June 2026), Economic growth. All countries and figures are invented. Revise first with the [economic growth study guide](/resources/ocr-gcse-economics-j205-economic-growth-ocr-gcse-economics/) and the [economic growth revision notes](/resources/ocr-gcse-economics-j205-economic-growth-ocr-gcse-economics-revision-notes/).

## Section A: short-answer questions

**1.** State what is meant by economic growth. **[1]**

**2.** Explain the difference between nominal GDP and real GDP. **[2]**

**3.** Country M has GDP of £2,250bn and a population of 75 million. Calculate its GDP per capita. **[1]**

**4.** Real GDP rises from £640bn to £656bn. Calculate the rate of economic growth. **[2]**

**5.** Country S's nominal GDP rises from £1,500bn to £1,590bn. Over the same year its price index rises from 100 to 104.

**(a)** Calculate the nominal growth rate. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate real GDP at the end of the year, at start-of-year prices, to the nearest £bn. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the real growth rate to one decimal place. **[1]**

**6.** Which one of the following is most likely to raise GDP per capita? **[1]**

- A. A rise in population with no change in output
- B. Investment in new technology that raises output per worker
- C. A rise in the general price level
- D. More workers retiring early

**7.** Explain one way in which education and training can lead to economic growth. **[2]**

## Section B: structured and extended questions

**8.** The table shows Country N's real GDP growth. Its population grows by 1.0% every year.

| Year | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Real GDP growth (%) | 2.8 | 2.1 | 0.9 | −1.6 | −0.4 | 1.7 |

**(a)** Identify the years in which output fell. **[1]**
**(b)** Describe the pattern of growth shown in the table. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why GDP per capita fell in Year 3 even though GDP grew. **[2]**
**(d)** Real GDP was £300bn at the start of Year 1. Calculate real GDP at the end of Year 2, to one decimal place. **[2]**

**9.** Country P has GDP of £180bn and a population of 12 million. Country Q has GDP of £45bn and a population of 1.5 million.

**(a)** Calculate GDP per capita for each country. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain which country is likely to have higher average living standards, and give one limitation of using GDP per capita to judge this. **[3]**

**10.** Analyse how an increase in investment by firms can affect economic growth. **[4]**

**11.** Country R has discovered large reserves of natural gas and plans to extract and export it. Evaluate whether the economic growth this creates is likely to be sustainable. **[8]**

**12.** Evaluate whether government policies are the most important determinant of economic growth. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** An increase in the real output of goods and services in an economy over time [1]. **[1]**

**2.** Nominal GDP is measured at current prices, so it rises when prices rise even if output does not [1]. Real GDP is adjusted for inflation, so it shows changes in the actual quantity of output [1]. **[2]**

**3.** £2,250bn ÷ 75 million = **£30,000** per person [1]. **[1]**

**4.** (656 − 640) ÷ 640 × 100 [1] = **2.5%** [1]. **[2]**

**5. (a)** (1,590 − 1,500) ÷ 1,500 × 100 = **6.0%** [1].
**(b)** 1,590 ÷ 104 × 100 = **£1,529bn** [1].
**(c)** (1,528.8 − 1,500) ÷ 1,500 × 100 = **1.9%** [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Nearly 70% of the nominal rise was inflation. Say so; it shows you understand why real figures matter.

**6.** **B** [1]. More output per worker raises output per person. A and D lower GDP per capita; C changes nominal, not real, GDP. **[1]**

**7.** Training raises workers' skills, so each worker produces more per hour [1]. Higher productivity raises the output the economy can produce, so real GDP grows [1]. **[2]**

**8. (a)** Years 4 and 5 [1].
**(b)** Growth slowed each year from 2.8% in Year 1 to 0.9% in Year 3 [1], became negative in Years 4 and 5 (a recession), then recovered to 1.7% in Year 6 [1].
**(c)** GDP grew by 0.9% but population grew by 1.0% [1], so output per person fell slightly, by about 0.1% [1].
**(d)** 300 × 1.028 × 1.021 [1] = **£314.9bn** [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* In (b), quote figures. "It went up and down" earns little.

**9. (a)** P: 180bn ÷ 12m = **£15,000** [1]. Q: 45bn ÷ 1.5m = **£30,000** [1].
**(b)** Q is likely to have higher average living standards, because output per person is double P's even though P's total GDP is four times larger [1]. Limitation: GDP per capita is an average and does not show how income is shared [1], so most people in Q could still be poor if a few receive most of the income (or: it ignores the environment, leisure time or prices in each country) [1]. **[5]**

**10.** Firms buy more capital goods such as machines and buildings [1]. Each worker has more and better equipment, so output per worker rises [1]. This increases the productive capacity of the economy, so real GDP can grow [1]. In the short run the spending on capital goods also adds to demand, raising output and jobs in the firms that make the machines [1]. **[4]**

**11.** For: extraction creates jobs and income in the short run, raising GDP [1]. Export revenue and tax receipts could be used to fund education and infrastructure, which would raise growth after the gas runs out [1]. Against economic sustainability: gas is a non-renewable resource, so the growth ends when reserves are used up [1], and the economy may come to depend on one industry, exposed to swings in world gas prices [1]. Environmental: burning gas releases carbon dioxide, and extraction can damage land and water [1]. Social: gains may go mainly to the extracting firms and one region rather than the whole population [1]. Evaluation: sustainability depends on how the revenue is used and how long reserves last [1]. Judgement: the growth is unlikely to be sustainable on its own, but it could be if revenue is invested in skills and other industries before the gas runs out [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* Cover economic, social and environmental sustainability. The best answers say what would make the growth sustainable, not only why it might not be.

**12.** Government policies matter because spending on transport, schools and research supports the other determinants, and stable laws give firms confidence to invest [1]. Policies such as tax relief for investment can directly raise capital spending [1]. However, investment decisions are mostly made by private firms responding to expected demand and profit [1], and changes in technology often come from firms' own research [1]. A country's natural resources and workforce size are largely outside short-term government control [1]. Judgement: government policy is important because it shapes the conditions for the other determinants, but investment and technology are the direct sources of higher output per worker, so policy is best seen as enabling rather than the most important factor [1]. **[6]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using nominal figures as evidence of growth.
- Describing slower growth as falling output.
- Forgetting population when comparing living standards.
- Adding growth rates instead of multiplying across years.
- Evaluation without a final judgement.
