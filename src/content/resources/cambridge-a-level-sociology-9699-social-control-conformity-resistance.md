Section 1.2, *Social control, conformity and resistance*, is the theoretical heart of Paper 1 of Cambridge International AS & A Level Sociology (9699). It asks how far individuals are shaped by society, why most people follow the rules, how order is kept, and why some people break the rules. The syllabus links it to the key concepts *Power, control and resistance* and *Structure and human agency*.

This page goes deeper than the [socialisation, social control and identity guide](/resources/a-level-cambridge-sociology-socialisation-social-control-and-identity/), which summarises all of section 1. Revise with the [1.2 revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-sociology-9699-social-control-conformity-resistance-revision-notes/) and practise with the [1.2 practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-sociology-9699-social-control-conformity-resistance-practice/).

## What the syllabus lists

- The role of structure and agency in shaping the relationship between the individual and society, including an awareness of the differences between structuralist and interactionist views.
- Factors explaining why individuals conform to social expectations, including sanctions, social pressure, self-interest and social exchange.
- The mechanisms through which order is maintained, including power, ideology, force and consensus.
- How sociologists explain deviance and non-conformity, including subcultures, under-socialisation, marginalisation, cultural deprivation and social resistance.

## Structure and agency

**Structure** means the patterned social arrangements, such as class systems, institutions and norms, that exist before any individual and limit what they can do. **Agency** means people's capacity to make choices and act on them.

| | Structuralist views | Interactionist views |
|---|---|---|
| Starting point | Society as a whole | Individuals in everyday interaction |
| Direction of influence | Society shapes the individual ("top down") | Individuals create society through meanings ("bottom up") |
| Key idea | Émile Durkheim, *The Rules of Sociological Method* (1895): social facts are external to individuals and constrain them | Herbert Blumer, *Symbolic Interactionism* (1969): people act on the meanings things have for them, and meanings arise in interaction |
| Examples of theories | Functionalism, Marxism, some feminism | Symbolic interactionism, labelling theory |
| Preferred methods | Large-scale, often quantitative | Small-scale, qualitative |
| Main criticism | Treats people as passive | Ignores the power and inequality that limit choice |

Max Weber took a middle route: sociology should explain social action by understanding the meanings people give it, while recognising large structures such as bureaucracy. Anthony Giddens' *The Constitution of Society* (1984) set out **structuration theory**: structures both limit action and are reproduced by it, a "duality of structure". For example, a language constrains how you can speak, but it exists only because people keep speaking it, and it changes as they use it differently. Structuration is a strong way to conclude an essay on structure and agency because it treats the two as connected rather than opposed.

## Social control: formal and informal

Social control is every process that encourages conformity. It can be sorted two ways.

| | Positive sanction (reward) | Negative sanction (punishment) |
|---|---|---|
| **Formal** (official, written rules, specialist agencies) | Promotion, a medal, an exam certificate | A fine, a prison sentence, school exclusion |
| **Informal** (everyday, unwritten, from people around us) | Praise, a smile, acceptance by friends | Gossip, ridicule, being ignored |

Formal control is exercised by the police, courts, armed forces and institutional rules. Informal control works through family, peers and community. Small communities with dense relationships often rely more on informal control.

## Why do people conform?

The syllabus names four reasons. Each fits a different picture of human beings.

- **Sanctions.** People conform to gain rewards and avoid punishments. This fits a behaviourist view: conformity is learned through consequences.
- **Social pressure.** People conform to belong or to obey. Besides peer pressure, Stanley Milgram's obedience experiments (published 1963) found that many participants were willing to give what they believed were dangerous electric shocks when instructed by an authority figure. Pressure can come from above as well as from equals.
- **Self-interest.** People calculate that conformity pays: a job, a reputation or a relationship would be put at risk by deviance. This is a rational-choice picture.
- **Social exchange.** Social life is a series of exchanges of approval, help and status. Peter Blau's *Exchange and Power in Social Life* (1964) argued that people who receive more than they can return become dependent, which gives others power over them. People conform to keep valued relationships going.

**Evaluation.** These factors work together, but functionalists would add that most conformity is not calculated at all: values have been internalised, so people *want* to conform. Interactionists reply that conformity is constantly negotiated in each situation.

## How is order maintained?

**Power.** Weber distinguished power based on legitimate **authority** from coercion. He identified three types of authority:

- **Traditional**: accepted because "it has always been so", as with hereditary rulers or elders.
- **Charismatic**: accepted because of a leader's exceptional personal qualities.
- **Rational-legal**: accepted because it follows agreed rules and procedures, as with elected governments and bureaucracies.

**Ideology.** Marxists argue that the ruling class keeps order by spreading ideas that make inequality seem fair or natural. Workers who accept these ideas are said to have *false consciousness*. Ideology produces order without the cost or resentment of force.

**Force.** Weber, in his lecture "Politics as a Vocation" (1919), defined the state as the body that successfully claims a monopoly of the legitimate use of physical force in a territory. Force (police, prisons, armies) is used when other mechanisms fail, but heavy reliance on force often signals that consent is weak.

**Consensus.** Functionalists argue that order rests mainly on agreement about values. Durkheim called the shared beliefs of a society its *collective conscience*.

**Surveillance.** Michel Foucault's *Discipline and Punish* (1975) used the image of the panopticon, a prison where inmates can always be watched but never know when, to argue that modern societies control people by making them watch and discipline themselves. This links power to schools, workplaces and digital monitoring.

A good evaluation asks which mechanism is dominant in a given society and points out that they usually combine: consent for most people, force at the margins.

## Explaining deviance and non-conformity

**Deviance** is behaviour that breaks a group's norms; it is not the same as crime, which breaks the law. **Non-conformity** includes deviance but also principled refusal to follow norms.

| Syllabus term | Explanation | Key sociologists |
|---|---|---|
| **Under-socialisation** | Inadequate socialisation, often blamed on family breakdown, leaves people without internalised norms | New Right writers on the "underclass" |
| **Cultural deprivation** | Some groups lack the norms, language or values that mainstream institutions reward | Walter Miller (1958): lower-class "focal concerns" such as toughness, excitement and "smartness" lead to deviance |
| **Subcultures** | Groups with values that reward deviance, often formed when legitimate routes are blocked | Robert Merton (1938): strain between cultural goals and legitimate means; Richard Cloward and Lloyd Ohlin (1960): criminal, conflict and retreatist subcultures depend on local opportunities |
| **Marginalisation** | Groups pushed to the edge of society, with no organisation or voice, may turn to deviance | John Lea and Jock Young (1984): relative deprivation, marginalisation and subculture together explain crime |
| **Social resistance** | Deviance as a meaningful response to domination, especially by youth | Stuart Hall and Tony Jefferson (eds), *Resistance Through Rituals* (1976): youth subcultures as symbolic resistance |

Merton's five adaptations to strain are useful for this whole section because they map conformity and non-conformity in one scheme: **conformity** (accept goals and means), **innovation** (accept goals, reject means, such as theft), **ritualism** (reject goals but follow the rules), **retreatism** (reject both, such as drop-outs) and **rebellion** (replace both with new goals and means).

**Interactionist challenge.** Howard Becker's *Outsiders* (1963) argued that deviance is not a quality of the act but the result of others applying a label. Once labelled, a person may accept a deviant identity. This shifts the question from "why do they deviate?" to "who has the power to define deviance?"

## Worked example: explaining and arguing against a view

*Quotation (practice): "Social order is mainly the result of shared values."*

**Explaining the view (AO1, AO2).** Functionalists argue that socialisation passes on a value consensus that people internalise. Durkheim's collective conscience binds people together, so most conformity is voluntary and order is stable without constant force. Sanctions reinforce values but are needed only for a minority.

**Arguing against (AO2, AO3).** First, Marxists argue that apparent agreement is ideology: the ruling class shapes the values people hold, so consensus is manufactured, and force is used when it breaks down. Second, Foucault suggests order rests on surveillance and self-discipline rather than shared values. Third, interactionists argue that order is negotiated in each encounter, so it is more fragile and varied than "shared values" implies.

Why it works: each counter-argument names a perspective, explains a different mechanism of order (ideology and force, surveillance, negotiation) and links back to the claim.

## Common mistakes

- Treating deviance and crime as the same thing.
- Describing structure and agency without naming structuralist and interactionist views, which the syllabus asks for directly.
- Listing reasons for conformity without showing how they differ.
- Writing about force and ideology as if a society uses only one.
- Explaining deviance with one theory when the question names several factors.
