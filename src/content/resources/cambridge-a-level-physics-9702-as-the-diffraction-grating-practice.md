## Section A

**1.** A diffraction grating has 250 lines per millimetre. Calculate the grating spacing d in metres. **[1]**

**2.** State **two** advantages of using a diffraction grating rather than a double slit to determine the wavelength of light. **[2]**

**3.** Light of wavelength 589 nm is incident normally on a grating. The first-order maximum is at 17.1° to the normal. Calculate the number of lines per millimetre on the grating. **[3]**

**4.** White light is incident normally on a grating. Explain why the zero-order maximum is white while the first-order maximum is a spectrum. **[2]**

**5.** Monochromatic light is incident normally on a grating whose spacing is 3.5 times the wavelength. Determine the total number of maxima produced. **[2]**

**6.** A grating is replaced by one with more lines per millimetre. State and explain the effect on the angle of the first-order maximum for the same light. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** A laser is directed normally at a grating with 300 lines per mm. On a screen 1.20 m away, the distance between the two first-order maxima is 0.42 m.

**(a)** Calculate the grating spacing. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the angle θ of the first-order maximum. **[2]**

**(c)** Calculate the wavelength of the laser light. **[2]**

**(d)** Explain why the student measures the distance between the two first-order maxima rather than from the centre to one of them. **[1]**

**(e)** Suggest why using the second-order maxima would give a more accurate value of the wavelength. **[2]**

**8.** White light containing wavelengths from 400 nm to 700 nm is incident normally on a grating with 600 lines per mm.

**(a)** Calculate the angular width of the first-order spectrum. **[3]**

**(b)** Show that the second-order and third-order spectra overlap. **[3]**

**(c)** State whether the red end of the third-order spectrum can be observed. Justify your answer. **[1]**

**(d)** State which colour in each spectrum is deviated most. **[1]**

**9.** A student shines light of a single wavelength normally on a grating with 500 lines per mm and measures the angles of three orders: 15.8° for n = 1, 33.1° for n = 2 and 55.0° for n = 3.

**(a)** Explain why a graph of sin θ against n should be a straight line through the origin. **[2]**

**(b)** Use the values for n = 1 and n = 3 to find the gradient of this line, and hence determine the wavelength. **[3]**

**(c)** Explain why no fourth-order maximum is observed. **[2]**

**10.** Describe how a diffraction grating, a laser and other simple laboratory apparatus could be used to determine the wavelength of the laser light. Include the measurements made and how the wavelength is calculated. A spectrometer is not available. **[5]**

**11.** A lamp emits two wavelengths. One is 640 nm. With a particular grating, the second-order maximum for 640 nm is at exactly the same angle as the third-order maximum for the other wavelength.

**(a)** Calculate the other wavelength. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain whether these two maxima would still coincide if a grating with a different number of lines per millimetre were used. **[2]**

**12.** Show that, for light incident normally on a grating, the highest order that can be observed is the whole-number part of d/λ, and explain what happens if d/λ is exactly a whole number. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** d = 1 ÷ (250 × 10³) = 4.0 × 10⁻⁶ m [1].

**2.** Any two [1 each]: the maxima are much sharper (narrower), so their positions can be located precisely; the maxima are brighter; the angles are larger, so the percentage uncertainty in measuring them is smaller; several orders can be measured and averaged.

**3.** d = λ ÷ sin θ = 589 × 10⁻⁹ ÷ sin 17.1° [1] = 2.00 × 10⁻⁶ m [1]; lines per mm = 1 ÷ (2.00 × 10⁻⁶) ÷ 1000 = 500 [1].

**4.** At the zero order θ = 0 for every wavelength, so all colours overlap and combine to give white [1]. For n = 1, sin θ = λ/d depends on wavelength, so each colour emerges at a different angle and the colours separate [1].

**5.** n ≤ d/λ = 3.5, so the highest order is 3 [1]; total = 3 on each side plus the zero order = 7 maxima [1].

**6.** The angle increases [1], because d is smaller and sin θ = λ/d, so sin θ is larger [1].

**7. (a)** d = 1 ÷ (300 × 10³) = 3.33 × 10⁻⁶ m [1].

**(b)** Distance from centre to one maximum = 0.42 ÷ 2 = 0.21 m [1]; tan θ = 0.21 ÷ 1.20, θ = 9.9° [1].

**(c)** λ = d sin θ = 3.33 × 10⁻⁶ × sin 9.9° [1] = 5.7 × 10⁻⁷ m [1].

**(d)** The centre of the zero-order spot is hard to locate precisely; measuring between two sharp maxima and halving avoids this and halves the uncertainty in each distance [1].

**(e)** The second-order maxima are further apart [1], so the same absolute uncertainty in measuring the distance gives a smaller percentage uncertainty in θ and hence in λ [1].

**8. (a)** d = 1.67 × 10⁻⁶ m. Violet: sin θ = 400 × 10⁻⁹ ÷ 1.67 × 10⁻⁶ = 0.24, θ = 13.9° [1]. Red: sin θ = 0.42, θ = 24.8° [1]. Angular width = 24.8° − 13.9° = 10.9° [1].

**(b)** Second-order red: sin θ = 2 × 700 × 10⁻⁹ ÷ 1.67 × 10⁻⁶ = 0.84, θ = 57.1° [1]. Third-order violet: sin θ = 0.72, θ = 46.1° [1]. Third-order violet is at a smaller angle than second-order red, so the spectra overlap (between 46.1° and 57.1°) [1].

**(c)** No: sin θ would be 3 × 700 × 10⁻⁹ ÷ 1.67 × 10⁻⁶ = 1.26, which is greater than 1 [1].

**(d)** Red (the longest wavelength) [1].

**9. (a)** sin θ = (λ/d) × n [1]; λ/d is constant, so sin θ is proportional to n: a straight line through the origin with gradient λ/d [1].

**(b)** sin 15.8° = 0.272 and sin 55.0° = 0.819 [1]; gradient = (0.819 − 0.272) ÷ (3 − 1) = 0.274 [1]; d = 2.00 × 10⁻⁶ m, so λ = 0.274 × 2.00 × 10⁻⁶ = 5.5 × 10⁻⁷ m [1].

**(c)** For n = 4, sin θ = 4 × 0.274 = 1.09 [1]; sin θ cannot exceed 1, so no fourth-order maximum exists [1].

**10.** Any five [1 each]: set up the laser so the beam is normal to the grating, with a screen beyond it; measure the grating-to-screen distance L with a metre rule or tape; mark the positions of the two maxima of the same order n on the screen and measure the distance between them, halving it to give y; calculate θ from tan θ = y/L; calculate d from the number of lines per millimetre (d = 1/N); calculate λ = d sin θ / n; repeat for other orders, or plot sin θ against n and use gradient = λ/d; use a large L to make y large and reduce the percentage uncertainty.

**11. (a)** 2 × 640 nm = 3λ [1], so λ = 427 nm [1].

**(b)** Yes, they still coincide [1]: the condition is 2 × 640 nm = 3λ, which does not involve d, so for any grating both maxima have the same sin θ (provided that order can be observed) [1].

**12.** sin θ = nλ/d and sin θ cannot be greater than 1 [1], so n ≤ d/λ, and since n must be a whole number the highest order is the whole-number part of d/λ [1]. If d/λ is exactly whole, that order would be at θ = 90°, travelling along the surface of the grating, so it cannot be observed and the highest observable order is one less [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using lines per mm as if it were d, or forgetting to convert millimetres to metres.
- Taking θ as the angle between the two first-order maxima instead of half of it.
- Assuming sin θ ≈ tan θ for large grating angles.
- Rounding d/λ up to find the highest order.
- Counting maxima on only one side of the zero order.
- Writing an experiment description with no measurements or no equation.
