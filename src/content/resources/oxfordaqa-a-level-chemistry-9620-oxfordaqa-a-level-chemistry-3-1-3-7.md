This guide covers **3.1.3.7 Forces between molecules** from the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Chemistry (9620) specification, Version 5.3. It is International AS content, examined in Unit 1, and it is used again throughout organic chemistry. The specification names three forces: **permanent dipole–dipole forces**, **induced dipole–dipole (van der Waals, dispersion, London) forces** and **hydrogen bonding**. It states that the melting and boiling points of molecular substances are influenced by the strength of these forces, and it names the importance of hydrogen bonding in **the low density of ice** and **the anomalous boiling points of compounds**. You must explain the existence of these forces between familiar and unfamiliar molecules, and explain how they influence melting and boiling points.

The [shapes, polarity and intermolecular forces overview](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-shapes-polarity-and-intermolecular-forces/) introduces the three forces. This page explains how each one arises, works through boiling-point comparisons with data, and shows how to structure answers. Afterwards use the [forces between molecules revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-1-3-7-revision-notes/) and the [forces between molecules practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-1-3-7-practice/).

## Forces between molecules, not bonds within them

When a molecular substance melts or boils, the molecules separate but stay intact. Only the **forces between molecules** are overcome. Each of the three forces below is much weaker than a covalent bond, which is why molecular substances have low melting and boiling points compared with giant structures.

## Induced dipole–dipole (van der Waals) forces

**How they arise.** The electrons in a molecule are always moving. At any instant they may be unevenly spread, giving a **temporary dipole**. This dipole repels or attracts the electrons of a neighbouring molecule, **inducing** a dipole in it. The two dipoles attract. The temporary dipoles keep disappearing and reforming, but at every moment attractions exist.

**Where they act.** Between **all** molecules (and between atoms of the noble gases), polar or not.

**What makes them stronger.**

- **More electrons** (usually a larger molecule): the electron cloud is more easily distorted, so larger dipoles form.
- **Greater surface contact**: long, unbranched molecules touch along more of their length than compact, branched ones.

| Noble gas | He | Ne | Ar | Kr | Xe |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Boiling point / °C | −269 | −246 | −186 | −153 | −108 |

The only forces between noble gas atoms are van der Waals forces, so the rise down the group shows the effect of more electrons on its own.

| Isomer of C₅H₁₂ | Pentane | 2-Methylbutane | 2,2-Dimethylpropane |
|---|---|---|---|
| Boiling point / °C | 36 | 28 | 10 |

The three isomers have the same number of electrons. Branching makes the molecule more compact, reducing the surface contact between molecules, so the van der Waals forces are weaker and the boiling point falls.

## Permanent dipole–dipole forces

**How they arise.** A polar molecule with a **permanent dipole** (see [bond polarity](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-9620-oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-3-1-3-6/)) lines up so that the δ+ end of one molecule is near the δ− end of the next. These permanent attractions act **in addition to** van der Waals forces.

**Example.** Propanone, CH₃COCH₃ (Mr 58), boils at 56 °C. Butane, C₄H₁₀ (Mr 58), boils at about −1 °C. Their molecules have similar numbers of electrons, so their van der Waals forces are similar. Propanone has a polar C=O bond and a permanent dipole, so it also has permanent dipole–dipole forces. More energy is needed to separate its molecules.

## Hydrogen bonding

**Conditions.** A hydrogen bond forms between:

- a hydrogen atom bonded directly to **nitrogen, oxygen or fluorine**, and
- a **lone pair** on a nitrogen, oxygen or fluorine atom of a neighbouring molecule.

**Why only N, O and F.** These atoms are very electronegative and small. The N–H, O–H or F–H bond is very polar, leaving the hydrogen strongly δ+. Hydrogen has no inner electrons, so its small, exposed nucleus can approach a lone pair on a neighbouring N, O or F very closely.

**Drawing one.** Show the δ+ on H and δ− on the N, O or F; draw the lone pair; join the H to the lone pair with a dashed line. The X–H···Y arrangement is close to linear (about 180°).

Hydrogen bonding is the strongest of the three forces, but it is still much weaker than a covalent bond.

## Worked example 1: three molecules with the same number of electrons

Ethane (CH₃CH₃), fluoromethane (CH₃F) and methanol (CH₃OH) each have 18 electrons. Their boiling points are about −89 °C, −78 °C and 65 °C. Explain the order.

**Annotated answer.**

- All three have similar van der Waals forces, because they have the same number of electrons [this removes size as the explanation].
- Ethane is non-polar, so it has only van der Waals forces: lowest boiling point.
- Fluoromethane has a polar C–F bond and a permanent dipole, so it also has permanent dipole–dipole forces: higher boiling point [the extra force is named].
- In fluoromethane every hydrogen is bonded to carbon, not to fluorine, so it **cannot** form hydrogen bonds [a common trap avoided].
- Methanol has an O–H bond, so it forms hydrogen bonds, the strongest intermolecular force: by far the highest boiling point, as most energy is needed to separate the molecules [link to energy].

## Anomalous boiling points

Down a group, boiling points of hydrides usually rise because the molecules have more electrons. The first member can break the trend:

| Group 15 hydride | NH₃ | PH₃ | AsH₃ | SbH₃ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Boiling point / °C | −33 | −88 | −62 | −17 |

From PH₃ to SbH₃, boiling points rise with increasing van der Waals forces. NH₃ boils far higher than the trend predicts because its molecules form **hydrogen bonds**, which need extra energy to overcome. The same pattern appears for H₂O in Group 16 and HF in Group 17. Group 14 hydrides (CH₄ to SnH₄) show no anomaly: carbon is not electronegative enough for hydrogen bonding.

## The low density of ice

In ice, each water molecule forms hydrogen bonds to **four** neighbours (two through its hydrogen atoms, two through its lone pairs). The hydrogen bonds hold the molecules in a rigid, **open** tetrahedral lattice with a lot of empty space. When ice melts, some hydrogen bonds break, the lattice collapses and the molecules move closer together. So liquid water is **denser** than ice, and ice floats.

## Worked example 2: comparing an unfamiliar pair

Hydrazine, H₂N–NH₂ (Mr 32), boils at about 114 °C. Oxygen, O₂ (Mr 32), boils at −183 °C. Explain the difference.

- Both have Mr 32 and similar numbers of electrons (18 in hydrazine, 16 in oxygen), so their van der Waals forces are similar in size.
- O₂ is non-polar and has only van der Waals forces.
- Hydrazine has N–H bonds and lone pairs on nitrogen, so its molecules form hydrogen bonds with each other.
- Much more energy is needed to overcome hydrogen bonds as well as van der Waals forces, so hydrazine boils much higher.

## Common mistakes

- Writing that covalent bonds break when a molecular substance boils.
- Leaving out van der Waals forces for polar molecules; they are always present.
- Claiming hydrogen bonding wherever a molecule contains H and O, N or F, even when the H is bonded to carbon (CH₃F, CH₃OCH₃).
- Drawing a hydrogen bond to an atom instead of to a lone pair, or with no partial charges.
- Explaining the density of ice by "hydrogen bonds are strong" rather than by the **open lattice** they create.
- Comparing boiling points using only "more forces" without saying which force and why it is stronger.

## Exam technique

For any boiling-point comparison:

1. Name **every** force present in each substance.
2. Identify the **difference** that matters (more electrons, more surface contact, a permanent dipole, hydrogen bonding).
3. Say which forces are **stronger** and that **more energy** is needed to overcome them.

**Explain** answers need all three steps for both substances. **Draw** answers need partial charges, a lone pair and a dashed line.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do polar molecules have van der Waals forces?**
Yes. Induced dipole–dipole forces act between all molecules. Polar molecules have permanent dipole–dipole forces in addition.

**Why does branching lower the boiling point of an alkane?**
Branched molecules are more compact, so there is less surface contact between neighbouring molecules. The van der Waals forces are weaker, and less energy is needed to separate the molecules.

**Why is ice less dense than water?**
Hydrogen bonds hold the molecules in ice in an open lattice with a lot of space. On melting, some hydrogen bonds break and the molecules pack more closely, so the liquid is denser.
