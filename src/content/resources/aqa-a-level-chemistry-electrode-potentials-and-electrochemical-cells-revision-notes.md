## 3.1.11.1 Electrode potentials and cells (A-level only)

### Key definitions

- **Electrochemical cell**: two half-cells joined by a wire and a salt bridge. Electrons pass from reducing agent to oxidising agent through the external circuit.
- **Half-cell**: an electrode in contact with a solution, such as Cu in Cu²⁺(aq), or platinum in a solution of Fe²⁺ and Fe³⁺.
- **Salt bridge**: usually filter paper soaked in saturated KNO₃(aq). Ions move through it to balance charge. It does not carry electrons.
- **Standard electrode potential, E⦵**: the EMF of a half-cell measured against the standard hydrogen electrode at 298 K, 100 kPa and 1.00 mol dm⁻³ solutions of ions.
- **Electrochemical series**: half-equations listed in order of E⦵.
- **EMF**: the potential difference of a cell when no current flows.

### The standard hydrogen electrode (SHE)

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| Electrode | Platinum |
| Gas | H₂ at 100 kPa |
| Solution | 1.00 mol dm⁻³ H⁺(aq), e.g. HCl(aq) |
| Temperature | 298 K |
| Half-equation | 2H⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ ⇌ H₂(g) |
| Potential | 0.00 V by definition |
| Cell notation | Pt(s) \| H₂(g) \| H⁺(aq) |

### Conventions to know

| Convention | Rule |
|---|---|
| IUPAC half-equation | Written as a reduction: electrons on the left, ⇌ arrow |
| Single line \| | Phase boundary |
| Double line \|\| | Salt bridge |
| Left of \|\| | Negative electrode, oxidation |
| Right of \|\| | Positive electrode, reduction |
| Electrode position | On the outside of each side |
| Same-phase species | Separated by a comma, with Pt on the outside |
| EMF | E⦵(right) − E⦵(left) = E⦵(positive) − E⦵(negative) |

### Method: writing a cell and its EMF

1. Find the two E⦵ values.
2. The more negative half-cell is the negative electrode. Put it on the left.
3. Write the left side so it reads as an oxidation: `Zn(s) | Zn²⁺(aq)`.
4. Write the right side so it reads as a reduction: `Ag⁺(aq) | Ag(s)`.
5. EMF = E⦵(right) − E⦵(left). The answer must be positive.
6. Overall equation: reverse the left half-equation, scale both so electrons match, add.

### Method: is a reaction feasible?

1. Find the half-equation containing the proposed oxidising agent on its **left**.
2. Find the one containing the proposed reducing agent on its **right**.
3. Feasible if the oxidising agent's E⦵ is **more positive**. Check: E⦵(oxidising agent) − E⦵(reducing agent) > 0.
4. Write the equation: the more positive half-equation forwards, the other backwards.

Memory aid: **the more positive half-equation goes forwards**.

### What E⦵ tells you, and what it does not

| E⦵ tells you | E⦵ does not tell you |
|---|---|
| Whether a reaction is feasible under standard conditions | How fast it goes |
| Relative strength of oxidising and reducing agents | What happens far from standard conditions |
| The EMF of a standard cell | Whether the activation energy is too high |

### Effect of conditions (no Nernst equation)

Each electrode is an equilibrium, so Le Chatelier's principle predicts the direction:

- More oxidised form (e.g. higher [Cu²⁺]) → equilibrium right → E **more positive**.
- Less oxidised form (lower [Cu²⁺]) → equilibrium left → E **less positive**.
- Temperature and gas pressure also change E. That is why E⦵ is defined at fixed conditions.

### Values used on these pages

| Half-equation | E⦵ / V |
|---|---|
| Mg²⁺ + 2e⁻ ⇌ Mg | −2.37 |
| Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻ ⇌ Zn | −0.76 |
| Fe²⁺ + 2e⁻ ⇌ Fe | −0.44 |
| Ni²⁺ + 2e⁻ ⇌ Ni | −0.25 |
| 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ ⇌ H₂ | 0.00 |
| Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ ⇌ Cu | +0.34 |
| I₂ + 2e⁻ ⇌ 2I⁻ | +0.54 |
| Fe³⁺ + e⁻ ⇌ Fe²⁺ | +0.77 |
| Ag⁺ + e⁻ ⇌ Ag | +0.80 |
| Br₂ + 2e⁻ ⇌ 2Br⁻ | +1.07 |
| Cl₂ + 2e⁻ ⇌ 2Cl⁻ | +1.36 |

Always use the data the question gives you.

- Strongest oxidising agent: left-hand species at the **most positive** end (Cl₂ here).
- Strongest reducing agent: right-hand species at the **most negative** end (Mg here).

### Required practical 8: measuring EMF

- Clean metal electrodes with emery paper to remove oxide.
- Use 1.00 mol dm⁻³ solutions for standard values.
- Salt bridge of filter paper soaked in saturated KNO₃. Avoid KCl with Ag⁺ (AgCl precipitates).
- Use a high-resistance voltmeter so negligible current flows and the reading is the EMF.
- Record which electrode is negative.
- Extension suggested in the specification: change concentration or temperature in Zn | Zn²⁺ || Cu²⁺ | Cu and observe the EMF.

## 3.1.11.2 Commercial applications of cells (A-level only)

### Types of cell

| Type | Key point |
|---|---|
| Non-rechargeable (irreversible) | Reactions cannot practically be reversed; discarded when used up |
| Rechargeable | An external supply reverses the electrode reactions |
| Fuel cell | Fuel and oxygen supplied continuously; no electrical recharging needed |

**Deducing reactions from given data (non-rechargeable or rechargeable cell):** the more negative half-equation runs backwards (oxidation) at the negative electrode; the more positive one runs forwards (reduction) at the positive electrode; EMF = E⦵(positive) − E⦵(negative). In a rechargeable cell, both reactions reverse on charging. Practice questions 4 (non-rechargeable) and 11 (rechargeable) use this method.

### Lithium cell (simplified, as in the specification)

```
Positive electrode:  Li⁺ + CoO₂ + e⁻ → Li⁺[CoO₂]⁻
Negative electrode:  Li → Li⁺ + e⁻
Overall (discharge): Li + CoO₂ → Li⁺[CoO₂]⁻
```

Cobalt goes from +4 to +3 (reduced). Lithium goes from 0 to +1 (oxidised). Recharging reverses both.

### Alkaline hydrogen–oxygen fuel cell

```
Positive electrode:  O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻ → 4OH⁻        (E⦵ = +0.40 V)
Negative electrode:  H₂ + 2OH⁻ → 2H₂O + 2e⁻        (from 2H₂O + 2e⁻ ⇌ H₂ + 2OH⁻, E⦵ = −0.83 V)
Overall:             2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O                EMF = +1.23 V
```

### How a cell generates a current

Oxidation at the negative electrode releases electrons. They flow through the external circuit to the positive electrode, where reduction takes them up. Ions move inside the cell to balance charge.

### Benefits and risks

- Fuel cells: only water at the point of use, but hydrogen production needs energy, often from fossil fuels; hydrogen is flammable and hard to store.
- Rechargeable cells: less waste than throwaway cells; lithium is reactive and damaged cells can catch fire.
- All cells: disposal can release toxic metal compounds, so recycling matters.

## Must-know distinctions

| This | Is not this |
|---|---|
| Negative electrode = oxidation | Negative electrode = reduction |
| E⦵ stays the same when you multiply a half-equation | E⦵ doubles |
| Feasible | Fast |
| Salt bridge moves ions | Salt bridge moves electrons |
| Fuel cell is not recharged electrically | Fuel cell is recharged like a battery |

## Quick self-test

Use the values in the table above.

1. State the three standard conditions for E⦵.
2. Write the IUPAC half-equation for the silver electrode.
3. Calculate the EMF of a copper–silver cell.
4. Write the conventional representation of the copper–silver cell.
5. Calculate the EMF of an iron (Fe²⁺/Fe)–copper cell.
6. Will chlorine oxidise bromide ions? Give the EMF.
7. Will iodine oxidise Fe²⁺ ions? Explain.
8. A zinc half-cell is connected to a SHE. What does the voltmeter read, and which electrode is negative?
9. Metal X is the negative electrode when connected to a standard silver half-cell. The EMF is 1.05 V. Find E⦵ for X²⁺/X.
10. State what happens to the EMF of a magnesium–zinc cell if the Zn²⁺ concentration is raised.
11. Give the oxidation state of cobalt in Li⁺[CoO₂]⁻.
12. Why does a fuel cell not need to be electrically recharged?

### Answers

1. 298 K; 100 kPa; 1.00 mol dm⁻³ solutions of ions.
2. Ag⁺(aq) + e⁻ ⇌ Ag(s)
3. 0.80 − 0.34 = **+0.46 V**
4. Cu(s) | Cu²⁺(aq) || Ag⁺(aq) | Ag(s)
5. 0.34 − (−0.44) = **+0.78 V**
6. Yes. Cl₂/Cl⁻ (+1.36) is more positive than Br₂/Br⁻ (+1.07). EMF = **+0.29 V**. Cl₂ + 2Br⁻ → 2Cl⁻ + Br₂.
7. No. I₂/I⁻ (+0.54) is less positive than Fe³⁺/Fe²⁺ (+0.77), so EMF would be −0.23 V.
8. **0.76 V**; zinc is the negative electrode.
9. 0.80 − 1.05 = **−0.25 V** (matches nickel).
10. Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻ ⇌ Zn shifts right, so the zinc (positive) electrode becomes more positive and the EMF **increases** above 1.61 V.
11. **+3**
12. Fuel and oxygen are supplied continuously, so the reactants never run out.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing electrode half-equations as oxidations instead of IUPAC reductions.
- Multiplying an E⦵ value when the half-equation is scaled up.
- Calculating left − right and getting a negative EMF, then changing the sign without explanation.
- Missing Pt from a half-cell where both species are ions, or from the SHE.
- Leaving state symbols out of a conventional representation.
- Quoting a pressure other than 100 kPa, or leaving out temperature, for standard conditions.
- Saying a salt bridge carries electrons.
- Claiming a feasible reaction must happen quickly.
- Getting the concentration effect backwards: more oxidised form makes E more positive.
- Writing fuel cell half-equations for acid conditions when the question asks for the alkaline cell.

## Frequently asked questions

**What are the standard conditions for measuring electrode potentials?**
Standard electrode potentials are measured against the standard hydrogen electrode at 298 K, 100 kPa and 1.00 mol dm⁻³ solutions of ions. Quoting a different pressure or leaving out the temperature loses the mark.

**What does the salt bridge do in an electrochemical cell?**
The salt bridge, usually filter paper soaked in saturated KNO₃(aq), lets ions move to balance charge and complete the circuit. It does not carry electrons.

**Which electrode is negative in an electrochemical cell?**
Oxidation happens at the negative electrode and reduction at the positive electrode. In the conventional cell representation, the negative electrode is written on the left.

**Does a fuel cell need to be recharged?**
No. In a fuel cell, fuel and oxygen are supplied continuously, so it generates a current without electrical recharging. A rechargeable cell needs an external supply to reverse its electrode reactions.

**How does concentration affect an electrode potential?**
Increasing the concentration of the oxidised form makes the electrode potential more positive. This topic is A-level only content in AQA Chemistry 7405 section 3.1.11.
