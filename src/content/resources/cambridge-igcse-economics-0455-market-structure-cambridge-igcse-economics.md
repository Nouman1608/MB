This guide covers section 3.8, Market structure, of the Cambridge IGCSE Economics 0455 syllabus for examination in 2026. The section has two parts: 3.8.1 competitive markets, which asks for "the effect of having a high number of firms on price, quality, choice, profit", and 3.8.2 monopoly markets, which asks for the "characteristics, advantages and disadvantages of monopoly". The syllabus notes that the theory of perfect and imperfect competition is not required, and that diagrams are not required for either part. All firms and figures below are invented.

## What market structure means

**Market structure** describes the features of a market that affect how firms behave: above all, how many firms sell the product and how easily new firms can enter. At one extreme are **competitive markets** with many firms; at the other is **monopoly**, with a single seller. Most real markets lie between.

A useful measure is **market share**: a firm's sales as a percentage of total market sales.

## 3.8.1 Competitive markets

A competitive market has a high number of firms, each with a small share, selling similar products, with buyers who can compare and switch easily, and with new firms able to enter.

### The four effects

| Effect | What happens | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Price | Tends to be low | A firm charging more than rivals loses customers to them |
| Quality | Tends to be maintained or improved | Firms compete for customers by offering better products and service |
| Choice | Wide | Many firms offer different varieties, locations and service levels |
| Profit | Tends to be low, especially over time | Low prices limit profit per unit; high profits attract new firms |

### How entry competes profits away

**Worked example 1 (invented).** In a town, 4 firms sell bottled juice. Each unit costs 7 to make and sells for 10. Total sales are 80,000 bottles a year, shared equally.

```
Sales per firm   = 80,000 / 4 = 20,000
Profit per unit  = 10 - 7 = 3
Profit per firm  = 3 x 20,000 = 60,000
```

The high profits attract 4 new firms. With 8 firms competing, the price falls to 8, and total sales rise to 100,000 because juice is now cheaper.

```
Sales per firm   = 100,000 / 8 = 12,500
Profit per unit  = 8 - 7 = 1
Profit per firm  = 1 x 12,500 = 12,500
```

| | Before entry | After entry |
|---|---|---|
| Price | 10 | 8 (20% lower) |
| Bottles bought | 80,000 | 100,000 (25% more) |
| Profit per firm | 60,000 | 12,500 (79% lower) |

Consumers gain a lower price and more product; each firm's profit falls sharply. This process is why the syllabus links a high number of firms to low profit. It works only if new firms can enter easily.

### Limits to the benefits of competition

- Many small firms may each be too small to gain economies of scale, so costs per unit can be higher than one large firm would achieve.
- Low profits leave little money for research and new products.
- Firms under pressure to cut prices may cut corners on quality, safety or workers' pay.
- Very wide choice can make it hard for consumers to compare products.

## 3.8.2 Monopoly

### Characteristics

A **pure monopoly** is a market with a single seller. In practice a firm with a very large market share may also be described as having monopoly power. Key characteristics:

- **One seller, or one dominant seller.**
- **No close substitutes** for the product.
- **Barriers to entry** that stop new firms competing.
- **Price maker:** the firm can set its price, although it cannot force consumers to buy at that price; if it raises price, it sells less.
- **High profits** that can last over time because rivals cannot enter.

### Barriers to entry

Barriers explain why monopoly profits are not competed away as in Worked example 1.

| Barrier | How it keeps rivals out | Example (invented) |
|---|---|---|
| Legal barriers | Patents, licences or government grant of sole right to supply | A drug company holds a patent on a new medicine |
| High start-up costs | A newcomer cannot raise the finance needed | Building a new national rail network |
| Economies of scale | The incumbent's large scale gives low costs; a small newcomer would have higher costs | A large cement producer |
| Control of resources | The monopolist owns the key raw material or outlets | One firm owns the only diamond mine |
| Brand loyalty | Consumers trust the established brand | A long-established soft drink |

### Natural monopoly

In some industries, one firm can supply the whole market more cheaply than two or more could, because the main cost is a single network.

**Worked example 2 (invented).** Supplying piped gas to a city of 50,000 homes needs a network costing 60 million to build, plus running costs of 100 per home per year. Spread the building cost over 20 years: 3 million a year.

```
One firm:  yearly cost = 3,000,000 + 50,000 x 100 = 8,000,000
           cost per home = 8,000,000 / 50,000 = 160
Two firms (two networks, 25,000 homes each):
           yearly cost = 2 x 3,000,000 + 50,000 x 100 = 11,000,000
           cost per home = 11,000,000 / 50,000 = 220
```

Duplicating the network raises the cost per home by 37.5%. A single supplier is cheaper, but it would also have the power to charge high prices, so such monopolies are often owned or regulated by the government.

### Advantages and disadvantages from each viewpoint

| Viewpoint | Possible advantages | Possible disadvantages |
|---|---|---|
| Consumers | Lower prices if economies of scale are passed on; stable supply; investment in new products | Higher prices; less choice; poorer quality and service because there is no rival |
| The monopoly firm | High, secure profits; can plan long term; funds for research | Little pressure to be efficient may lead to waste |
| Workers and suppliers | Secure employment in a stable firm | The firm may pay suppliers or workers less because they have few other buyers |
| The economy | A large firm may compete abroad and earn export revenue; no wasteful duplication in natural monopolies | Resources misallocated if output is restricted; high prices reduce consumers' real income |

**Worked example 3 (invented).** A monopoly ferry company carries 300,000 passengers a year at a fare of 25. Its cost per passenger is 15.

```
Profit = (25 - 15) x 300,000 = 3,000,000
```

A rival is licensed and the fare falls to 19. Total passengers rise to 360,000, of which the original firm keeps 240,000.

```
Original firm's profit = (19 - 15) x 240,000 = 960,000
Fall in its profit = 2,040,000, or 68%
Saving for each passenger = 6 a trip, a 24% lower fare
```

## Annotated model answer

**Question (invented):** Discuss whether a monopoly is always bad for consumers. [6]

> A monopoly faces no rivals, so it can charge a higher price than in a competitive market, reducing consumers' real income [disadvantage]. Consumers have no alternative supplier, so the firm has little reason to improve quality or service [second disadvantage, developed]. However, a monopoly may gain large economies of scale, and if lower costs are passed on, prices can be lower than many small firms could offer [advantage]. High profits may fund research into new products that benefit consumers [advantage]. A monopoly is not always bad: a regulated natural monopoly can supply more cheaply than competing firms, but an unregulated monopoly protected by barriers is likely to harm consumers [judgement with condition].

## Common mistakes

- Drawing diagrams of perfect competition or monopoly. The syllabus does not require them.
- Saying a monopoly can charge any price and still sell the same amount. Higher prices still reduce the quantity bought.
- Forgetting barriers to entry when explaining why monopoly profits last.
- Treating competition as always good for consumers, without considering lost economies of scale.
- Writing "price, quality, choice, profit" as a list with no explanation of why many firms produce each effect.
