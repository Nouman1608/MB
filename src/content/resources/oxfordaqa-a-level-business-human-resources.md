## 3.2.2.1 Human resource objectives and planning

The specification lists three HR objectives:

- **Number and skill level of employees** -- having enough people with the right skills, in the right place, at the right time.
- **Employee engagement** -- how committed employees are to the business and its goals.
- **Employee retention** -- keeping the employees the business wants.

**Human resource planning** works out the workforce needed and how to get it through **recruitment**, **training**, **redeployment** (moving staff to other roles), **retention** and **redundancies**. Its value: staffing matches the corporate plan, avoiding skill shortages or overstaffing. Its limit: plans rely on sales forecasts, and staff leave unexpectedly.

### Recruitment and selection

The recruitment process has three early stages, each with a purpose:

1. **Job analysis** -- study what the role involves. Without it, the advert describes the wrong job.
2. **Job description** -- sets out the tasks, duties and responsibilities of the job.
3. **Person specification** -- sets out the skills, qualifications and qualities the ideal person needs. It is the yardstick for shortlisting.

**Internal recruitment** is cheaper and quicker, the candidate knows the business, and promotion can motivate; but it brings no new ideas and leaves a vacancy behind. **External recruitment** widens the pool and brings fresh skills, but costs more, takes longer and is riskier because less is known about the candidate.

| Selection method | Advantage | Disadvantage |
|---|---|---|
| CV | Cheap; candidate shows what they choose | Hard to compare; may exaggerate |
| Application form | Same information from everyone | Takes time to design and complete |
| Interview | Tests communication and fit | Interviewer bias; nervous candidates under-perform |
| References | Independent view of past behaviour | Referees may be vague or chosen by the candidate |
| Testing | Measures specific skills objectively | Only tests part of the job |
| Assessment centre | Several tasks over a day give a rounded view | Expensive; suits senior or graduate roles |

**Employment contracts.** Full-time and permanent contracts build loyalty and skill but raise fixed costs. Part-time contracts cover peaks; temporary contracts suit seasonal work but bring less commitment. **Zero-hours** contracts give maximum flexibility and no cost when there is no work, but no guaranteed income for staff, which can hurt engagement and retention.

**Value of effective recruitment and selection:** the right person is productive sooner and stays longer; a poor hire means repeat recruitment costs and lost output.

**Training.** **Induction** introduces new staff to the business, its rules and colleagues. **On-the-job** training happens while working: cheap and relevant, but can pass on bad habits. **Off-the-job** training happens away from the workplace: wider expertise, but costlier and output is lost meanwhile. Trained staff may be poached.

## 3.2.2.2 Human resource data

The specification's Appendix: Formulae and Key data gives these formulas:

| Measure | Formula |
|---|---|
| Labour turnover (%) | number of employees leaving in a year ÷ average number of total employees × 100 |
| Employee retention rate (%) | number of employees at the end of a period ÷ average number of employees at the start of a period × 100 |
| Labour productivity | output in a period ÷ average number of employees during the period |
| Employee costs as % of turnover | total employee costs ÷ total revenue × 100 |
| Labour costs per unit | total labour costs ÷ units of output produced |
| Sales per employee | total sales revenue ÷ average number of employees |
| Profit per employee | total profit ÷ average number of employees |

The specification also names the percentage of employees still at the business one year after recruitment: recruits still there after a year ÷ number recruited × 100.

### Worked example: Tamarind Lane Ceramics

Tamarind Lane Ceramics had 180 employees at the start of the year and 200 at the end. 38 employees left during the year. Output was 76,000 items, revenue was £9.5m, employee (labour) costs were £2.66m and profit was £1.14m.

```
Average employees      = (180 + 200) ÷ 2 = 190
Labour turnover        = 38 ÷ 190 × 100 = 20%
Labour productivity    = 76,000 ÷ 190 = 400 items per employee
Employee costs % rev.  = 2.66 ÷ 9.5 × 100 = 28%
Labour cost per unit   = £2,660,000 ÷ 76,000 = £35
Sales per employee     = £9,500,000 ÷ 190 = £50,000
Profit per employee    = £1,140,000 ÷ 190 = £6,000
```

Of the 180 employees there at the start, 153 were still employed at the end: 153 ÷ 180 × 100 = **85%** retained. Of 40 people recruited in the previous year, 30 were still there one year after joining: 30 ÷ 40 × 100 = **75%**. Applied to raw headcounts, the appendix formula gives 200 ÷ 180 × 100 = 111%, because new joiners are counted; so check which staff the question wants counted.

**Interpreting.** 20% turnover means one in five staff left: higher recruitment and training costs and lost experience, though some turnover brings new ideas. The specification stresses that measures such as labour productivity **vary in different contexts**: a hand-finishing workshop makes fewer items per employee than an automated line. Compare like with like and look at trends.

## 3.2.2.3 Organisational design

- **Span of control** -- the number of people a manager directly supervises.
- **Levels of hierarchy** -- the number of layers of management between the top and the bottom.
- **Tall structures** (many levels, narrow spans) give close supervision and promotion steps, but slow communication and high management costs. **Flat structures** (few levels, wide spans) give faster communication and more responsibility, but managers can be overstretched.
- **Delegation** -- passing authority down the hierarchy. It develops staff and frees managers, but the manager stays accountable.
- **Centralisation** keeps decisions at the top (consistency, tight cost control); **decentralisation** pushes them to branches or lower levels (faster local responses, more motivated managers, but less consistency).
- **Employee representation rate** -- the proportion of the workforce represented by a trade union or other employee body. A high rate gives employees a stronger collective voice and can make consultation easier to organise.

Influences include the size of the business, staff skills, the nature of the work, culture and the cost of managers.

Pelican Freight has a chief executive, 3 regional directors, 15 depot managers (5 per region) and 180 drivers (12 per depot manager): 199 staff on 4 levels. Removing the regional directors (each paid £90,000) saves 3 × £90,000 = **£270,000** a year, but the chief executive's span rises from 3 to **15**.

**Restructuring and delayering** cut costs, speed up decisions and can empower staff. Drawbacks: fewer promotion opportunities, heavier workloads for remaining managers, lost experience and fear among those left.

## 3.2.2.4 Motivation

**Benefits of motivated employees:** higher productivity, better quality and service, lower labour turnover and absence.

**Theories.**

- **Taylor's scientific management**: workers are motivated by money. Break jobs into simple tasks, find the most efficient method, pay by output and supervise closely. It suits repetitive work but ignores social needs.
- **Maslow's hierarchy of needs**: basic (physiological), safety, social, esteem and self-actualisation. A need motivates until it is met; then the next level matters. Pay meets basic needs, secure contracts safety, teams social needs, recognition esteem, and challenging work self-actualisation.
- **Herzberg's two-factor theory**: **hygiene factors** (pay, working conditions, supervision, company policy) cause dissatisfaction if poor but do not motivate when improved. **Motivators** (achievement, recognition, responsibility, the work itself, advancement) give lasting motivation.

**Financial methods.**

| Method | Advantage | Disadvantage |
|---|---|---|
| Wages (hourly) | Easy to adjust hours | No reward for effort |
| Piece rate | Links pay to output | Quality may fall; income unpredictable |
| Commission | Rewards sales effort | Pressure selling; unstable pay |
| Salaries | Security; simple to run | No direct link to effort |
| Performance-related pay | Rewards targets met | Appraisal can feel unfair |
| Fringe benefits | Help retention | Cost; may not be valued by all |

For example, a worker paid £0.80 per item who makes 550 items in a week earns 550 × £0.80 = **£440**. A sales assistant on a £300 basic plus 4% commission who sells £12,500 earns £300 + £500 = **£800**.

**Non-financial methods of motivation and engagement.** **Job enlargement** adds more tasks at the same level (more variety, but can feel like more work for the same pay). **Job enrichment** adds responsibility and more challenging tasks (meets Herzberg's motivators, but needs training). **Job rotation** moves staff between tasks (variety and cover, but little extra challenge). **Empowerment** gives staff authority to make decisions (faster, more committed staff, but risk of mistakes). **Teamwork** meets social needs and shares ideas, but conflict and free riders are possible.

**Influences on choice and assessing effectiveness:** the nature of the work (output easy to measure suits piece rate), cost and cash available, management style, employee skills and expectations, and culture. Judge effectiveness with HR data: has turnover fallen or productivity risen?

## 3.2.2.5 Employer-employee relations

**Employee involvement** draws on staff knowledge, improves commitment and eases change. **Consultation** (asking views before deciding) is cheap, but staff feel ignored if nothing changes. **Trade unions** negotiate collectively, giving one clear channel, but can resist change. **Employee forums** (staff representatives meeting managers) give direct input but may lack power. Involvement depends on management style, business size, urgency, legislation and staff skills.

**Good relations** mean lower turnover and fewer disputes. **Conflict** arises over pay, conditions, job losses, changed working practices and poor communication.

**Actions employees might take:** withdraw cooperation, **work to rule** (follow every rule exactly so work slows), leave, **strike**, or impose an **overtime ban**.

**Resolving conflict:** **participation** (involve staff in finding solutions), **bargaining** (negotiation between managers and employee representatives) and **arbitration** (an independent third party hears both sides and recommends or decides a settlement). Relations improve through regular communication, fair pay, consultation before change and clear grievance procedures.

**Managing human resources.** Technology changes recruitment (online adverts, video interviews), the nature of work, the skills needed and ways of working such as **remote working**, which cuts office costs but makes supervision harder. Managing people well raises **competitiveness**. HR decisions link to other functions: recruitment depends on the finance budget and training affects operations quality. **Ethical issues** include unsafe working conditions and practices and low pay; acting ethically costs money now but protects reputation and retention.

## Common errors

- Using the end-of-year headcount instead of the average number of employees in labour turnover or productivity.
- Confusing job description (the job) with person specification (the person).
- Saying Herzberg treats pay as a motivator: it is a hygiene factor.
- Mixing up job enlargement (more tasks, same level) and job enrichment (more responsibility).

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the difference between job enlargement and job enrichment?**
Job enlargement adds more tasks at the same level, giving variety but possibly feeling like more work for the same pay. Job enrichment adds responsibility and more challenging tasks, which meets Herzberg's motivators but needs training.

**What is the difference between arbitration and bargaining?**
Bargaining is negotiation between managers and employee representatives. Arbitration brings in an independent third party who hears both sides and recommends or decides a settlement.

**Why does effective recruitment and selection matter?**
The right person becomes productive sooner and stays longer, while a poor hire means repeat recruitment costs and lost output.

**What are the benefits of motivated employees?**
Motivated employees bring higher productivity, better quality and service, and lower labour turnover and absence.

**How does technology affect the management of human resources?**
Technology changes how businesses recruit, for example through online adverts and video interviews, as well as the nature of work, the skills needed and ways of working. Remote working, for example, cuts office costs but makes supervision harder.
