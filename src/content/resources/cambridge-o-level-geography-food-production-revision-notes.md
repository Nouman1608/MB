## The system

**Inputs → processes → outputs**, with feedback.

- **Physical inputs**: relief, climate, soil. They set the limits.
- **Human inputs**: labour, capital, machinery, seed, fertiliser,
  irrigation, markets, transport, government policy, land tenure.
  They decide intensity and purpose.
- **Processes**: ploughing, sowing, transplanting, weeding, harvesting,
  rearing, milking.
- **Outputs**: crops, meat, milk, income, by-products.
- **Feedback**: profit or manure returned as inputs.

## Classification: always use three scales

| Scale | Options |
|---|---|
| Purpose | Commercial / subsistence |
| Product | Arable / pastoral / mixed |
| Intensity | Intensive / extensive |

Examples: wet rice in a delta = intensive subsistence arable; cattle
ranch = extensive commercial pastoral; glasshouse horticulture =
intensive commercial arable; nomadic herding = extensive subsistence
pastoral.

## Case study 1: wet rice, Ganges–Brahmaputra delta (Bangladesh)

- Flat delta, fertile alluvium, high temperatures, monsoon rain
  (about June to September) floods paddies; clay holds water.
- Small family plots, hand labour, draught animals or power tillers.
- Seedlings raised in nursery, transplanted into bunded flooded fields,
  weeded, harvested by hand.
- Outputs: rice for the family, surplus sold; straw. Second dry-season
  crop where irrigated.

## A second system: irrigated wheat and cotton, Punjab (Pakistan)

- **Physical inputs**: flat alluvial plains; hot summers and mild winters; low or unreliable
  rainfall in much of the province, so river water is essential.
- **Human inputs**: canal irrigation from the Indus and its tributaries, supplemented by tube
  wells; high-yielding seed, fertiliser, tractors on larger farms and family labour on small
  ones; government support prices for wheat.
- **Processes**: two seasons a year: **kharif** (summer) crops such as cotton and rice, and
  **rabi** (winter) crops such as wheat.
- **Outputs**: wheat for food (much of it sold), cotton for the textile industry, fodder for
  livestock.
- **Classification**: mostly commercial arable, intensive on irrigated land, though many small
  farms keep part of the harvest for the family.
- **Issues**: waterlogging and salinity on poorly drained irrigated land; falling water tables
  where too many tube wells pump groundwater.

## Worked example: inputs explained

*Explain how physical inputs make wet rice farming possible in the delta.* Model answer: "The flat
delta land can be divided into small bunded fields that hold water evenly. High temperatures
throughout the growing season and heavy monsoon rain provide the heat and water that rice
needs. The clay-rich alluvial soil is fertile and holds water, so the paddies stay flooded."

Each input is named **and** linked to what the crop needs.

## Food shortage causes

| Natural | Economic and political |
|---|---|
| Drought | Low capital investment |
| Floods, tropical storms | Poor transport and distribution |
| Pests (locusts) and plant disease | Conflict |
| | Population growth, land degradation, cash crops for export |

## Effects

Undernutrition and malnutrition → stunting, disease, less energy for
work → lower output → migration, aid dependence → famine in extreme
cases.

## Case study 2: South Sudan

- Independent 2011; civil war from late 2013; famine declared in parts
  of Unity State in February 2017.
- Causes: conflict (farmers displaced, markets closed, aid blocked),
  economic collapse and high food prices, poor roads, droughts and
  floods.
- Effects: severe malnutrition, mass displacement, reliance on food aid.

## Solutions

- **Short term**: food aid, therapeutic feeding, cash transfers,
  early-warning systems.
- **Long term**: irrigation, high-yielding and drought-resistant seed,
  fertiliser, appropriate technology (hand pumps, terraces, grain
  stores), roads and markets, pest control, peace.
- Evaluate: aid saves lives but can undercut local farmers; new seeds
  need water and fertiliser; appropriate technology is cheap but small
  scale.

## Evaluating the Green Revolution

- **For**: high-yielding wheat and rice varieties raised yields sharply in South Asia from the
  1960s, including in Pakistan's Punjab, helping food supply keep pace with population.
- **Against**: the seeds need reliable irrigation, fertiliser and pesticide, so richer farmers
  gained most; heavy water and chemical use contribute to falling water tables, salinity and
  pollution.
- **Judgement**: it increased output where water and money were available, but it is not a
  solution for poor farmers in dry, rain-fed areas without support.

Natural causes also strike in Pakistan: desert locust swarms damaged crops in 2019–2020, and the
2022 monsoon floods destroyed crops and livestock across large areas of Sindh. Both show how
hazards can turn a food-producing region into one needing food aid.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Subsistence farming | Producing food mainly for the farmer's own family |
| Intensive farming | High inputs and output per hectare |
| Extensive farming | Low inputs and output per hectare over a large area |
| Malnutrition | Poor health from a diet lacking the right balance of nutrients |
| Famine | Extreme, widespread food shortage causing many deaths |
| Green Revolution | Use of high-yielding seed with fertiliser and irrigation to raise output |
| Appropriate technology | Simple, affordable technology local people can maintain |
| Commercial farming | Producing crops or animals to sell for profit |
| Arable farming | Growing crops |
| Pastoral farming | Rearing animals |
| Mixed farming | Growing crops and rearing animals on the same farm |
| Irrigation | Artificial watering of farmland |
| Undernutrition | Not having enough food energy to stay healthy |
| Salinisation | Build-up of salt in soil, often from poorly drained irrigation |

## Exam traps

- Classifying a farm on only one scale; give purpose, product and intensity.
- Listing inputs without linking them to the crop or animal ("hot climate" with no "so...").
- Treating malnutrition and undernutrition as the same: malnutrition can mean the wrong balance
  of nutrients, even with enough food energy.
- Blaming food shortage only on drought when conflict or poverty is the main cause, as in South
  Sudan.
- Solutions given with no evaluation of who benefits and what they need.

2. Classify a large wheat farm using machinery on the Canadian prairies.
3. Why is wet rice farming described as intensive?
4. Give two economic or political causes of food shortages.
5. Why can food aid be a problem in the long term?
6. When was famine declared in parts of South Sudan?
7. Name the two cropping seasons in Pakistan's Punjab and one crop grown in each.
8. Why is irrigation an essential human input in much of Punjab?
9. Give one disadvantage of the Green Revolution.
10. Give one natural hazard that has caused crop losses in Pakistan.
11. Distinguish between malnutrition and undernutrition.
