These questions cover section **3.5.9 Collisions of electrons with atoms** of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Physics (9630) specification: ionisation and excitation, the fluorescent tube, the electronvolt, line spectra and hf = E1 – E2, and X-ray tubes, spectra and medical uses. Marks are shown for each part. The atoms in Questions 4 and 7 are invented for practice. Use h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J s, c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹ and e = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C.

Revise first with the [electron collisions study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-physics-9630-collisions-of-electrons-with-atoms-oxfordaqa/) and the [electron collisions revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-physics-9630-collisions-of-electrons-with-atoms-oxfordaqa-revision-notes/).

## Section A

**1.** State the difference between excitation and ionisation of an atom. **[2]**

**2.** An energy of 3.2 eV is equal to

A: 2.0 × 10⁻¹⁹ J

B: 5.1 × 10⁻¹⁹ J

C: 3.2 × 10⁻¹⁹ J

D: 2.0 × 10¹⁹ J **[1]**

**3.** Convert an energy of 2.88 × 10⁻¹⁹ J into electronvolts. **[1]**

**4.** An electron in an atom falls from a level of −1.6 eV to a level of −3.6 eV. Calculate the frequency of the photon emitted. **[2]**

**5.** The wavelengths of the characteristic lines in an X-ray spectrum depend on

A: the tube voltage

B: the filament current

C: the target metal

D: the temperature of the filament **[1]**

**6.** Explain why a free electron with kinetic energy 5.0 eV can excite an atom whose first energy gap is 4.2 eV, but a photon of energy 5.0 eV cannot. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** An atom has energy levels of −8.0 eV (ground state), −4.5 eV, −2.9 eV and −1.2 eV.

**(a)** Calculate the ionisation energy of the atom in joules. **[2]**

**(b)** An electron of kinetic energy 5.0 eV collides with the atom in its ground state. State the level to which the atom can be excited, and the kinetic energy of the electron afterwards. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain why a photon of energy 5.0 eV is not absorbed by the atom in its ground state. **[2]**

**(d)** Atoms are excited to the −1.2 eV level. State how many different photon energies can be emitted as they return to the ground state. **[1]**

**(e)** Calculate the longest wavelength these atoms can emit. **[3]**

**8.** Explain how a fluorescent tube produces visible light. Your answer should refer to excitation, ionisation and the coating on the tube. **[5]**

**9.** The energy levels of atomic hydrogen include −13.6 eV, −3.40 eV, −1.51 eV and −0.85 eV.

**(a)** Show that the transition from −0.85 eV to −3.40 eV gives a wavelength of about 490 nm. **[3]**

**(b)** Explain why the transition from −3.40 eV to −13.6 eV is not seen in the visible spectrum. **[2]**

**10.** An X-ray tube operates with a p.d. of 80 kV and a beam current of 5.0 mA.

**(a)** Calculate the maximum energy, in joules, of an X-ray photon. **[1]**

**(b)** Calculate the minimum wavelength of the X-rays. **[2]**

**(c)** Calculate the number of electrons striking the target each second. **[1]**

**(d)** Calculate the power delivered to the target and explain why the target must be cooled. **[2]**

**11.** Describe how the X-ray spectrum from a tube changes when:

**(a)** the tube voltage is increased, **[2]**

**(b)** the filament current is increased, with the voltage unchanged. **[2]**

**12.** Explain how emission and absorption line spectra provide evidence that the energy levels in atoms are discrete. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** Excitation moves an electron to a higher energy level within the atom, which stays neutral [1]; ionisation removes an electron from the atom completely, leaving a positive ion [1].

**2.** **B** [1]. 3.2 × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 5.12 × 10⁻¹⁹ J.

**3.** 2.88 × 10⁻¹⁹/1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ = **1.8 eV** [1]

**4.** ΔE = 3.6 − 1.6 = 2.0 eV = 3.2 × 10⁻¹⁹ J [1]; f = ΔE/h = 3.2 × 10⁻¹⁹/6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ = **4.8 × 10¹⁴ Hz** [1]

**5.** **C** [1]

**6.** The electron can give the atom 4.2 eV and keep the remaining 0.8 eV as kinetic energy [1]; a photon must be absorbed completely, so it can only excite the atom if its energy exactly matches a gap, and 5.0 eV does not [1].

**7.**

**(a)** Ionisation energy = 8.0 eV [1] = 8.0 × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ = **1.28 × 10⁻¹⁸ J** [1]

**(b)** To the **−4.5 eV** level (gap 3.5 eV; the next gap is 5.1 eV, too large) [1]; the electron keeps 5.0 − 3.5 = **1.5 eV** [1]

**(c)** The gaps from the ground state are 3.5 eV, 5.1 eV and 6.8 eV, and 8.0 eV to ionise [1]; a photon is absorbed only if its energy exactly equals one of these (or exceeds the ionisation energy), and 5.0 eV matches none [1].

**(d)** **6** [1]

**(e)** The longest wavelength comes from the smallest gap [1], which is −2.9 eV to −4.5 eV, 1.6 eV = 2.56 × 10⁻¹⁹ J [1]; λ = hc/ΔE = 1.989 × 10⁻²⁵/2.56 × 10⁻¹⁹ = **7.8 × 10⁻⁷ m** (777 nm) [1]

**8.** The p.d. across the tube accelerates free electrons [1]. They collide with mercury atoms in the vapour and excite them [1]; some collisions ionise atoms, releasing further electrons that keep the current flowing [1]. The excited atoms de-excite and emit photons, mainly ultraviolet [1]. The phosphor coating absorbs the ultraviolet photons and re-emits lower-energy photons of visible light [1].

**9.**

**(a)** ΔE = 3.40 − 0.85 = 2.55 eV [1] = 2.55 × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 4.08 × 10⁻¹⁹ J [1]; λ = 1.989 × 10⁻²⁵/4.08 × 10⁻¹⁹ = **4.88 × 10⁻⁷ m**, about 490 nm [1]

**(b)** The energy difference is 10.2 eV, much larger than visible photon energies [1], so λ = hc/ΔE is about 122 nm, which is in the ultraviolet [1].

**10.**

**(a)** E = eV = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 80 × 10³ = **1.28 × 10⁻¹⁴ J** [1]

**(b)** λ(min) = hc/E [1] = 1.989 × 10⁻²⁵/1.28 × 10⁻¹⁴ = **1.55 × 10⁻¹¹ m** [1]

**(c)** n = I/e = 5.0 × 10⁻³/1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ = **3.1 × 10¹⁶ s⁻¹** [1]

**(d)** P = IV = 5.0 × 10⁻³ × 80 × 10³ = **400 W** [1]; only a small fraction of this becomes X-rays, so most of it heats the target, which would otherwise melt or be damaged [1].

**11.**

**(a)** The minimum wavelength becomes shorter and the intensity increases at all wavelengths [1]; the characteristic lines stay at the same wavelengths [1].

**(b)** The intensity increases, because more electrons strike the target each second [1]; the shape of the spectrum, λ(min) and the line wavelengths are unchanged [1].

**12.** An excited gas emits only certain wavelengths, and a cool gas absorbs exactly the same wavelengths from a continuous spectrum [1]; each line corresponds to one photon energy hf = E1 – E2 [1]; since only certain photon energies occur, only certain energy differences, and so only certain energy levels, can exist [1].

## Where marks are usually lost

- Requiring the colliding electron to have exactly the gap energy (Questions 6 and 7).
- Using a single level's energy rather than a difference (Questions 4 and 7).
- Mixing eV and J in one calculation (Questions 7 and 9).
- Leaving out the phosphor's absorption step in the fluorescent tube (Question 8).
- Saying that a higher current shortens the minimum wavelength (Question 11).
- Answering an evidence question without linking photon energy to energy differences (Question 12).
