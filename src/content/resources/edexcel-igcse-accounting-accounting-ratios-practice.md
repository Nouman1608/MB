Use the [study guide](/resources/edexcel-igcse-accounting-accounting-ratios/) and [revision notes](/resources/edexcel-igcse-accounting-accounting-ratios-revision-notes/) first if you need them. Every business named here is invented, and amounts are in dollars. Give percentages to 2 decimal places where needed and liquidity ratios as x : 1 to 2 decimal places.

## Questions

**1.** Explain the difference between profitability and liquidity. **[3]**

**2.** Sotherby Pet Supplies had revenue of 96,000, cost of sales of 62,400 and profit for the year of 9,600.

**(a)** Calculate the gross profit percentage. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate profit for the year as a percentage of revenue. **[2]**

**3.** Kinlochard Print has non-current assets of 110,000, current assets of 38,000, current liabilities of 23,000 and non-current liabilities of 25,000. Profit for the year was 22,500.

**(a)** Calculate capital employed. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate return on capital employed. **[2]**
**(c)** State one comparison that would help the owner interpret the result. **[1]**

**4.** At the year end Dornfield Bikes had inventory of 27,000, trade receivables of 15,000, bank of 6,000, trade payables of 30,000 and other payables of 6,000.

**(a)** Calculate the current (working capital) ratio. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate the liquid (acid test) ratio. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why the acid test ratio is lower than the current ratio. **[1]**

**5.** A trader's gross profit percentage rose this year, but profit for the year as a percentage of revenue fell. Suggest two different expenses that may have increased. **[2]**

**6.** Ferrantine Bakery has a current ratio of 2.5 : 1 and current liabilities of 24,000. Its inventory is 18,000.

**(a)** Calculate current assets. **[1]**
**(b)** Calculate the liquid (acid test) ratio. **[3]**

**7.** Mertonby Frames has current assets of 40,000, including inventory of 16,000, and current liabilities of 25,000. It pays 8,000 to trade payables from the bank.

**(a)** Calculate the current ratio before and after the payment. **[4]**
**(b)** Calculate the liquid (acid test) ratio before and after, and comment on the result. **[3]**

**8.** Norcliffe Gifts had these results.

| | Year 1 | Year 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue | 150,000 | 180,000 |
| Gross profit | 60,000 | 64,800 |
| Profit for the year | 21,000 | 18,000 |

**(a)** Calculate the gross profit percentage and the profit for the year as a percentage of revenue for both years. **[4]**
**(b)** Revenue rose but profit for the year fell. Using your answers and the expenses in each year, explain why. **[4]**

**9.** Zelmont Cafe has these Year 2 figures: revenue 210,000; cost of sales 126,000; profit for the year 25,200; capital employed 168,000; inventory 9,000; trade receivables 2,000; bank 16,000; current liabilities 18,000. The Year 1 ratios were: gross profit 38%, profit for the year 13% of revenue, ROCE 17%, current ratio 1.4 : 1, acid test 0.9 : 1.

**(a)** Calculate the five Year 2 ratios. **[5]**
**(b)** Comment on profitability in Year 2 compared with Year 1. **[4]**
**(c)** Comment on liquidity in Year 2 compared with Year 1. **[3]**

**10.** A supplier must decide which of two customers to offer 60 days' credit. Both ask for the same amount of goods.

| Ratio | Wickersham | Brandvik |
|---|---|---|
| Gross profit percentage | 26% | 30% |
| Profit for the year as a percentage of revenue | 11% | 8% |
| ROCE | 19% | 12% |
| Current ratio | 2.1 : 1 | 1.1 : 1 |
| Acid test ratio | 1.2 : 1 | 0.4 : 1 |

Evaluate which customer is the safer choice for the supplier. **[8]**

**11.** Tosswick Rugs started the year with equity of 80,000. Profit for the year was 24,000, drawings were 14,000 and non-current liabilities were 30,000. Revenue was 200,000.

**(a)** Calculate closing equity. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate capital employed. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate ROCE. **[2]**
**(d)** Calculate profit for the year as a percentage of revenue. **[2]**

## Answers

**1.** Profitability is whether the business earns a profit, and how large it is compared with revenue or capital [1]. Liquidity is whether it can pay its short-term debts as they fall due, using cash and near-cash assets [1]. A business can be profitable yet short of cash, for example if its profit is tied up in inventory [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Define both terms separately and add the link between them; two definitions alone leave the question of how they differ unanswered.

**2. (a)** Gross profit = 96,000 − 62,400 = 33,600 [1]. 33,600 ÷ 96,000 × 100 = **35%** [1].
**(b)** 9,600 ÷ 96,000 × 100 [1] = **10%** [1].
*Examiner insight:* Gross profit has to be worked out first here; writing it down shows the method even if the final division slips.

**3. (a)** 110,000 + 38,000 − 23,000 [1] = **125,000** [1]. (Check: equity of 100,000 + 25,000.)
**(b)** 22,500 ÷ 125,000 × 100 [1] = **18%** [1].
**(c)** Compare with the previous year's ROCE, with a similar business, or with what the money could earn elsewhere [1].
*Examiner insight:* Naming a comparison alone is enough for (c); an answer that only repeats 18% adds nothing.

**4. (a)** Current assets = 27,000 + 15,000 + 6,000 = 48,000 [1]. Current liabilities = 30,000 + 6,000 = 36,000 [1]. 48,000 ÷ 36,000 = **1.33 : 1** [1].
**(b)** (48,000 − 27,000) = 21,000 [1]; 21,000 ÷ 36,000 = **0.58 : 1** [1].
**(c)** Inventory is excluded from the acid test and is over half of the current assets [1].
*Examiner insight:* Both liabilities must be added into the denominator; leaving out other payables is a common slip and changes both ratios.

**5.** Any two different expenses, such as wages [1] and rent, advertising, depreciation or interest [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Two named expenses are needed, so repeating "expenses went up" in different words earns nothing extra.

**6. (a)** 2.5 × 24,000 = **60,000** [1].
**(b)** 60,000 − 18,000 = 42,000 [1]; 42,000 ÷ 24,000 [1] = **1.75 : 1** [1].
*Examiner insight:* Work back from a ratio by multiplying by the denominator, then show each step clearly.

**7. (a)** Before: 40,000 ÷ 25,000 = **1.6 : 1** [1]. After: current assets 32,000 [1] and current liabilities 17,000 [1]; 32,000 ÷ 17,000 = **1.88 : 1** [1].
**(b)** Before: (40,000 − 16,000) ÷ 25,000 = **0.96 : 1** [1]. After: 16,000 ÷ 17,000 = **0.94 : 1** [1]. The current ratio improved but the acid test fell slightly, because the ratio is below 1 : 1 and the same amount comes off both parts [1].
*Examiner insight:* The cash and the liability both reduce by 8,000, so adjust both parts; changing only one is the usual error.

**8. (a)** Year 1: 60,000 ÷ 150,000 × 100 = **40%** [1] and 21,000 ÷ 150,000 × 100 = **14%** [1]. Year 2: 64,800 ÷ 180,000 × 100 = **36%** [1] and 18,000 ÷ 180,000 × 100 = **10%** [1].
**(b)** Expenses = gross profit − profit: 39,000 in Year 1 and 46,800 in Year 2 [1]. As a percentage of revenue both are 26% [1], so expenses did not grow faster than sales. The fall comes from gross profit percentage, down from 40% to 36% [1], which may be due to lower selling prices or higher supplier costs [1].
*Examiner insight:* Use figures from the question and give the cause the figures support; a generic "expenses rose" does not fit data in which expenses were steady.

**9. (a)** Gross profit percentage: 84,000 ÷ 210,000 × 100 = **40%** [1]. Profit percentage: 25,200 ÷ 210,000 × 100 = **12%** [1]. ROCE: 25,200 ÷ 168,000 × 100 = **15%** [1]. Current ratio: (9,000 + 2,000 + 16,000) = 27,000; 27,000 ÷ 18,000 = **1.5 : 1** [1]. Acid test: 18,000 ÷ 18,000 = **1.0 : 1** [1].
**(b)** Gross profit percentage improved from 38% to 40% [1], but profit as a percentage of revenue fell from 13% to 12% [1], so expenses took a larger share of revenue [1]. ROCE fell from 17% to 15%, so each dollar of capital earned less [1].
**(c)** Liquidity improved: current ratio from 1.4 to 1.5 and acid test from 0.9 to 1.0 [1]. Liquid assets now just cover current liabilities [1], mainly because of the 16,000 in the bank [1].
*Examiner insight:* A comment scores when it gives both figures and the direction of change; "profit is better" with no numbers is too vague.

**10.** Liquidity matters most to a supplier waiting for payment [1]. Wickersham's current ratio is 2.1 : 1 and acid test 1.2 : 1 [1], so it can cover its current liabilities without selling inventory [1]. Brandvik's acid test is 0.4 : 1 [1], so it would struggle to pay quickly and may pay late [1]. Wickersham also has better profit as a percentage of revenue (11% against 8%) and ROCE (19% against 12%) [1], suggesting good control of expenses even though its gross profit percentage is lower (26% against 30%) [1]. Conclusion: Wickersham is the safer choice; trends over several years would give more confidence [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* An evaluation needs a decision supported by the ratios that matter for the stated purpose; listing all ten figures without weighing them does not reach a conclusion.

**11. (a)** 80,000 + 24,000 − 14,000 [1] = **90,000** [1].
**(b)** 90,000 + 30,000 = **120,000** [1].
**(c)** 24,000 ÷ 120,000 × 100 [1] = **20%** [1].
**(d)** 24,000 ÷ 200,000 × 100 [1] = **12%** [1].
*Examiner insight:* Drawings reduce equity, so subtract them before finding capital employed, and say which capital employed formula you used.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using revenue instead of capital employed as the base for ROCE.
- Mixing up gross profit and profit for the year.
- Leaving out other payables when adding current liabilities.
- Leaving inventory in the acid test.
- Writing liquidity ratios as percentages.
- Forgetting drawings when finding closing equity.
- Commenting without figures from the question.
- Giving reasons that conflict with the data.
