Condensed recall for **section 4.5 Valuation of inventory**. Worked examples on
damaged and obsolete goods and on opening and closing errors are in the
[4.5 study guide](/resources/cambridge-igcse-accounting-0452-valuation-of-inventory/);
questions are in the
[4.5 practice set](/resources/cambridge-igcse-accounting-0452-valuation-of-inventory-practice/).
Amounts are in dollars ($).

## The three syllabus points

1. Valuation of inventory at the lower of cost and net realisable value.
2. How to calculate the value of inventory.
3. The effect of an incorrect valuation on gross profit, profit for the year,
   equity and asset valuation.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Inventory | Goods held for resale (and, for a manufacturer, raw materials and work in progress) |
| Cost | The price paid for the goods, given per unit in questions |
| Net realisable value (NRV) | Expected selling price less costs still to be paid to make the sale |
| Write-down | Units × (cost − NRV) when NRV is lower |
| Obsolete goods | Goods no longer wanted, such as an old model, often with a scrap value only |
| Prudence | Expected losses are recognised at once; expected gains are not |
| Realisation | Profit is recognised when goods are sold, not before |

## Method

1. Work item by item, or by groups of identical items.
2. NRV per unit = selling price − repair − packaging − commission − delivery.
3. Take the lower of cost and NRV.
4. Multiply by units and add up.
5. Write-down = total at cost − total at lower of cost and NRV.

**Signals in a question:** "damaged", "out of date", "last year's model",
"price has fallen", "needs repair", "will be sold at a discount", "sales agent
charges commission".

## Worked reminder

40 coats cost 55 each. They can be sold for 60 after alterations costing 9, and
delivery costs 2 each. NRV = 60 − 9 − 2 = 49, lower than cost.
Value = 40 × 49 = **1,960**; write-down = 40 × 6 = **240**.

## Where the write-down goes

The lower closing inventory goes into the cost of sales calculation:

```
Cost of sales = opening inventory + purchases − closing inventory
```

A lower closing inventory gives a higher cost of sales and a lower gross profit.
Do **not** also list the write-down as an expense.

## Error effects

| Error | Cost of sales | Gross profit and profit for the year | Inventory asset | Equity at year end |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Closing overstated | Down | Up | Up | Up |
| Closing understated | Up | Down | Down | Down |
| Opening overstated | Up | Down | No effect | No effect |
| Opening understated | Down | Up | No effect | No effect |

- An error in closing inventory becomes next year's opening inventory error,
  so profit moves the opposite way next year.
- Equity at any year end is wrong by exactly the closing inventory error.
- Opening and closing errors in the same year: add their effects on cost of
  sales, taking care with signs.

**Combined reminder.** Opening understated by 600 (profit overstated 600) and
closing overstated by 1,100 (profit overstated 1,100): profit overstated by
**1,700**; inventory asset and equity overstated by **1,100**.

## Effects on ratios

| Ratio | Closing inventory overstated |
|---|---|
| Gross profit margin and mark-up | Higher than they should be |
| Profit margin | Higher |
| Current ratio | Higher |
| Acid test ratio | No effect (inventory is excluded) |

## Exam traps

- **Totals hide losses.** Never compare total cost with total NRV.
- **NRV above cost?** Use cost. Inventory is never written up.
- **Selling costs come off the selling price**, not off cost.
- **Repair after sale price.** If the price quoted is after repair, deduct the
  repair cost to find NRV.
- **No double counting.** The write-down is inside cost of sales.
- **Opening errors** do not affect this year's statement of financial position
  inventory.
- **Amounts and directions.** "Overstated by 1,350" earns the mark; "affected" does not.

## Self-test

1. Define net realisable value.
2. 15 units cost 30 each, sell for 34, delivery 6 each. Calculate their value.
3. 50 units cost 8 and have an NRV of 11. Calculate their value.
4. Why is inventory never valued above cost?
5. Closing inventory includes a write-down of 600. Should 600 also be shown as an
   expense?
6. Opening inventory was overstated by 700. State the effect on this year's gross profit.
7. Does an opening inventory error affect this year's current assets?
8. Closing inventory is understated by 1,100. State the effect on equity.
9. Draft gross profit is 18,000. Closing inventory should be 400 lower. State the
   corrected gross profit.
10. Opening understated by 500, closing understated by 500. State the effect on
    this year's profit.
11. Closing inventory is overstated. What happens to the acid test ratio?
12. Give two reasons why NRV may fall below cost.

### Answers

1. Expected selling price less the costs still to be paid to sell the goods.
2. NRV = 34 − 6 = 28, lower than 30: 15 × 28 = **420**.
3. Cost is lower: 50 × 8 = **400**.
4. Realisation: profit is not recognised until the goods are sold. Prudence:
   assets are not overstated.
5. No. That would count the loss twice.
6. Cost of sales is overstated, so gross profit is **understated by 700**.
7. No. Only closing inventory is a current asset at the year end.
8. Equity is **understated by 1,100**.
9. 18,000 − 400 = **17,600**.
10. **No effect**: the opening error lowers cost of sales by 500 and the closing
    error raises it by 500.
11. **No effect**: inventory is excluded from the acid test.
12. Any two: damage, obsolescence, a fall in market selling price, high selling
    costs such as commission or delivery.
