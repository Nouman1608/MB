## Why financial statements matter

Financial statements are formal records of a business's financial activities. They let people inside and outside the business answer questions such as: Is it profitable? Is profit growing? Can it pay its debts? Should we lend, invest, supply or work for it?

- **Owners and shareholders** judge whether the business is earning a good return and decide whether to invest more.
- **Managers** spot problems, such as rising costs, and set targets.
- **Banks and lenders** judge whether loans will be repaid.
- **Suppliers** judge whether the business can pay for goods on credit.
- **Employees** look for job security and the possibility of pay rises.

## The two statements

### The income statement

The income statement shows revenue, costs and profit **over a period**, usually a year. It is built from the top down.

| Line | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Sales revenue | Income from selling goods or services |
| − Cost of sales | The direct cost of the goods sold, such as stock bought for resale or materials |
| = Gross profit | Profit before other running costs |
| − Expenses | Running costs not directly tied to each sale: rent, salaries, marketing, utilities |
| = Net profit | What remains after all costs |

### Worked example: building an income statement

Kelmoor Cycles, a bike shop, has these figures for the year.

| £ | This year | Last year |
|---|---|---|
| Sales revenue | 480,000 | 400,000 |
| Cost of sales | 288,000 | 220,000 |
| **Gross profit** | **192,000** | **180,000** |
| Expenses | 144,000 | 132,000 |
| **Net profit** | **48,000** | **48,000** |

Gross profit this year: £480,000 − £288,000 = £192,000. Net profit: £192,000 − £144,000 = £48,000.

### The statement of financial position

The statement of financial position shows what a business owns and owes **on one particular date**. It is a snapshot, not a period.

| Section | Contents |
|---|---|
| Non-current assets | Owned for more than a year: premises, equipment, vehicles |
| Current assets | Expected to turn into cash within a year: inventories (stock), receivables (money owed by customers), cash |
| Current liabilities | Due within a year: payables (money owed to suppliers), overdraft |
| Non-current liabilities | Due after more than a year: long-term bank loans, mortgages |
| Net assets | Total assets − total liabilities |
| Total equity | Owners' money in the business: share capital + retained profit, equal to net assets |

### Worked example: Kelmoor Cycles' statement of financial position

| Item | £ |
|---|---|
| Premises | 150,000 |
| Equipment | 30,000 |
| **Non-current assets** | **180,000** |
| Inventories | 40,000 |
| Receivables | 6,000 |
| Cash | 9,000 |
| **Current assets** | **55,000** |
| **Total assets** | **235,000** |
| Payables | 25,000 |
| Overdraft | 10,000 |
| **Current liabilities** | **35,000** |
| Bank loan (five years) | 80,000 |
| **Non-current liabilities** | **80,000** |
| **Total liabilities** | **115,000** |
| **Net assets** | **120,000** |
| Share capital | 50,000 |
| Retained profit | 70,000 |
| **Total equity** | **120,000** |

Net assets: £235,000 − £115,000 = £120,000, which matches total equity of £50,000 + £70,000 = £120,000.

## Assets and liabilities

An **asset** is something the business owns or is owed that has value: a building, stock, money owed by customers, cash. A **liability** is something the business owes to others: money owed to suppliers, an overdraft, a loan.

A common trap: **receivables** (customers owe the business) are an asset; **payables** (the business owes suppliers) are a liability.

From Kelmoor's statement, current assets of £55,000 are well above current liabilities of £35,000, so it should be able to meet its short-term debts. But £40,000 of the current assets is stock, which must be sold before it becomes cash. Cash itself is only £9,000.

## Profit margins

Gross profit margin = (gross profit ÷ sales revenue) × 100

Net profit margin = (net profit ÷ sales revenue) × 100

For Kelmoor Cycles:

| | This year | Last year |
|---|---|---|
| Gross profit margin | 192,000 ÷ 480,000 × 100 = **40%** | 180,000 ÷ 400,000 × 100 = **45%** |
| Net profit margin | 48,000 ÷ 480,000 × 100 = **10%** | 48,000 ÷ 400,000 × 100 = **12%** |

A margin shows how many pence of profit each £1 of sales produces. This year Kelmoor kept 40p of gross profit and 10p of net profit from each £1 of sales.

### What the margins reveal

Revenue rose by £80,000, which is 20%, yet net profit stayed at £48,000. Both margins fell.

- **Gross margin fell** from 45% to 40%: cost of sales grew faster than revenue. Perhaps suppliers raised prices, or Kelmoor cut its own prices to win sales.
- **Net margin fell** from 12% to 10%: as well as the lower gross margin, expenses rose by £12,000 (about 9.1%).

The owner sold more but kept less of each sale. Higher revenue is not the same as better performance.

## Judging performance

The specification names four ways to judge performance. A full answer uses more than one.

| Approach | Question to ask | Kelmoor Cycles |
|---|---|---|
| Current performance | Are this year's margins healthy for this kind of business? | 10% net margin is positive, but there is little cash |
| Against previous years | Is the trend up or down? | Revenue up 20%; both margins down; net profit flat |
| Against competitors | Is it doing better or worse than rivals? | A rival with a 42% gross and 11% net margin would be doing slightly better |
| Stakeholders' perspectives | What would each group think? | See below |

### Stakeholder perspectives

- **Owners:** pleased that sales grew, concerned that profit did not.
- **Bank:** the loan of £80,000 and overdraft of £10,000 against cash of £9,000 may cause concern, although net assets of £120,000 offer some security.
- **Suppliers:** payables of £25,000 against cash of £9,000; they may want reassurance that stock will sell.
- **Employees:** stable profit suggests jobs are secure, but pay rises may be limited.

## Common mistakes

- Dividing by gross profit, or by cost of sales, instead of by sales revenue.
- Saying a business is doing well because revenue rose, without checking margins and profit.
- Treating the statement of financial position as covering a year; it shows one date.
- Calling receivables a liability or payables an asset.
- Comparing one year's figures with nothing: performance needs a comparison.
- Giving a judgement without stating which stakeholder's view it represents.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Calculate:** show the division and × 100, and give the answer as a percentage.
- **Identify:** pick out a component, such as a current asset, from a statement.
- **Analyse:** explain what a change in a margin shows and suggest a cause.
- **Evaluate:** use at least two of the four comparisons and reach a judgement.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the difference between gross profit and net profit?**
Gross profit is sales revenue minus cost of sales. Net profit is gross profit minus expenses, so it is what remains after all costs.

**What is the difference between the income statement and the statement of financial position?**
The income statement shows revenue, costs and profit over a period. The statement of financial position shows assets, liabilities and equity on one date.

**How do you judge a business's financial performance?**
Look at current performance, compare with previous years and with competitors, and consider the views of different stakeholders.
