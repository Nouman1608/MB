## Questions

**1.** Define magnetic flux and state what is meant by one weber. **[2]**

**2.** A circular loop of radius 3.0 cm lies in a uniform magnetic field of flux density 0.37 T. The field lines make an angle of 25° with the plane of the loop. Calculate the magnetic flux through the loop. **[3]**

**3.** A coil has 600 turns and a cross-sectional area of 2.5 × 10⁻⁴ m². Its plane is perpendicular to a uniform field of flux density 0.060 T.

**(a)** Calculate the magnetic flux linkage of the coil. **[2]**
**(b)** The coil is pulled out of the field in 0.15 s. Calculate the mean induced e.m.f. **[2]**

**4.** The north pole of a bar magnet is dropped, pole first, into the top of a vertical coil that is part of a closed circuit.

**(a)** State the direction of the induced current as seen from above, and explain how you decided. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain, using energy, why the magnet falls with a smaller acceleration than it would without the coil. **[2]**

**5.** The flux linkage of a coil rises uniformly from zero to 6.0 mWb turns in 3.0 ms, stays constant for 4.0 ms, then falls uniformly to zero in 2.0 ms.

**(a)** Calculate the magnitude of the induced e.m.f. in the first and the last stage. **[2]**
**(b)** State the e.m.f. in the middle stage and give the reason. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain why the e.m.f. in the first and the last stage has opposite signs. **[2]**

**6.** A search coil has 250 turns and a cross-sectional area of 3.0 × 10⁻⁴ m². It is placed in the alternating field of a solenoid supplied at 100 Hz, with its normal along the axis. An oscilloscope shows a peak e.m.f. of 46 mV.

**(a)** Explain why the induced e.m.f. alternates at the same frequency as the field. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the peak flux density at the search coil. **[3]**

**7.** A simple a.c. generator has a rectangular coil of 80 turns measuring 5.0 cm by 8.0 cm. It rotates at 40 Hz in a uniform field of flux density 0.12 T.

**(a)** State the purpose of the slip rings and brushes. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the peak e.m.f. **[3]**
**(c)** The rotation frequency is increased to 60 Hz. Calculate the new peak e.m.f. and the new period of the output. **[2]**

**8.** An ideal transformer reduces a mains supply of 230 V to 6.0 V. The secondary coil has 90 turns.

**(a)** Calculate the number of turns on the primary coil. **[2]**
**(b)** The secondary supplies a current of 1.5 A. Calculate the current in the primary coil. **[2]**

**9.** Explain why a transformer needs an alternating supply, and why its iron core is laminated. **[4]**

**10.** A student investigates a transformer with a fixed primary of 300 turns connected to a 6.0 V a.c. supply. She changes the number of turns on the secondary coil and measures the secondary p.d.

| Secondary turns Ns | 75 | 150 | 300 | 450 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Secondary p.d. Vs / V | 1.42 | 2.85 | 5.70 | 8.55 |

**(a)** Describe how she should take readings, including one safety precaution. **[3]**
**(b)** State what graph she should plot and the gradient she expects for an ideal transformer, and comment on the data. **[3]**
**(c)** With 150 secondary turns, the primary takes 3.5 A and the secondary supplies 6.0 A. Calculate the efficiency of the transformer. **[2]**

**11.** The flux through each turn of a 50-turn generator coil varies as Φ = Φ₀ cos(2πft), where Φ₀ = 4.0 mWb and f = 50 Hz.

**(a)** Calculate the peak e.m.f. of the generator. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain why the e.m.f. is zero when the flux is at its maximum value. **[2]**
**(c)** The output is connected to the primary of an ideal transformer with 800 primary turns and 150 secondary turns. Calculate the peak p.d. across the secondary. **[2]**
**(d)** A resistor of 24 Ω is connected across the secondary. Calculate the peak current in the secondary and in the primary. **[3]**

## Answers

**1.** Magnetic flux is the product of the flux density and the area perpendicular to the field, Φ = BA cos θ [1]. One weber is the flux through an area of 1 m² that is perpendicular to a uniform field of flux density 1 T [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A definition of the weber is stronger when it names the perpendicular area and the field strength.

**2.** A = π × (0.030)² = 2.83 × 10⁻³ m² [1]. The angle to the normal is 90° − 25° = 65° [1]. Φ = 0.37 × 2.83 × 10⁻³ × cos 65° = **4.4 × 10⁻⁴ Wb** [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* The angle given is to the plane, so using 25° straight in the cosine is the usual slip. Show the 65° step.

**3. (a)** Φ = BA = 0.060 × 2.5 × 10⁻⁴ = 1.5 × 10⁻⁵ Wb [1]. NΦ = 600 × 1.5 × 10⁻⁵ = **9.0 × 10⁻³ Wb turns** [1].
**(b)** |E| = ΔNΦ/Δt = 9.0 × 10⁻³ / 0.15 [1] = **0.060 V** [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Taking the final flux linkage as zero is the key reading of "pulled out of the field". Give the unit Wb turns.

**4. (a)** The coil must repel the approaching north pole, so the top face of the coil is a north pole [1]. A north face means the current is anticlockwise as seen from above [1].
**(b)** The induced current opposes the motion, so there is an upward force on the magnet [1]. The magnet does work against it, so some gravitational potential energy becomes electrical energy instead of kinetic energy [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* State what is being opposed. "Opposes the motion of the magnet" or "opposes the increase in flux" is stronger than "opposes the magnet".

**5. (a)** First stage: |E| = 6.0 mWb turns / 3.0 ms = **2.0 V** [1]. Last stage: |E| = 6.0 mWb turns / 2.0 ms = **3.0 V** [1].
**(b)** The e.m.f. is **zero** because the flux linkage is not changing [1].
**(c)** The flux linkage is increasing in the first stage and decreasing in the last [1]. The induced e.m.f. opposes the change, so it acts in opposite directions (opposite signs, E = −gradient) [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* mWb turns divided by ms gives volts directly. Give a zero e.m.f. a reason that refers to the rate of change.

**6. (a)** The field varies sinusoidally with time, so the flux linkage of the coil varies in the same way [1]. The e.m.f. depends on how fast the flux linkage changes, which varies sinusoidally at the same frequency [1].
**(b)** ω = 2π × 100 = 628 rad s⁻¹ [1]. B₀ = E₀/(NAω) = 0.046 / (250 × 3.0 × 10⁻⁴ × 628) [1] = **9.8 × 10⁻⁴ T** [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Convert millivolts to volts before substituting.

**7. (a)** The slip rings rotate with the coil and let the current pass out while the coil turns continuously [1]. The fixed brushes press on the rings and connect them to the external circuit [1].
**(b)** A = 0.050 × 0.080 = 4.0 × 10⁻³ m² [1]. ω = 2π × 40 = 251 rad s⁻¹ [1]. E₀ = NBAω = 80 × 0.12 × 4.0 × 10⁻³ × 251 = **9.7 V** [1].
**(c)** E₀ is proportional to ω, so the new value is 9.65 × 60/40 = **14.5 V** [1] and the period is 1/60 = **0.017 s** [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* Using the ratio 60/40 in part (c) avoids repeating the full calculation.

**8. (a)** Np/Ns = Vp/Vs [1], so Np = 90 × 230/6.0 = **3450 turns** [1].
**(b)** Ip/Is = Ns/Np, so Ip = 1.5 × 90/3450 [1] = **0.039 A** [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Checking that power in equals power out (9.0 W each) shows the current ratio is the right way up.

**9.** An alternating p.d. gives an alternating current in the primary and a changing flux in the core [1]. The changing flux linkage in the secondary induces an e.m.f. [1]. A steady supply would give a constant flux and no induced e.m.f. The core is laminated because the changing flux induces eddy currents in the iron [1]. Thin insulated sheets restrict these currents, so less energy is transferred as heat [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Link each feature to its effect: lamination reduces eddy currents, which reduces heating.

**10. (a)** Connect the primary to a low-voltage a.c. supply and an a.c. voltmeter across the secondary [1]. Keep Np and Vp constant, change Ns in steps and record Vs each time [1]. Safety: use a low voltage and switch off before changing the coils [1].
**(b)** Plot Vs against Ns [1]. For an ideal transformer this is a straight line through the origin with gradient Vp/Np = 6.0/300 = 0.020 V per turn [1]. The data give 0.019 V per turn, about 5% below the ideal value, which suggests systematic losses such as heating in the windings or flux that does not link both coils [1].
**(c)** Output power = 2.85 × 6.0 = 17.1 W and input power = 6.0 × 3.5 = 21.0 W [1]. Efficiency = 17.1/21.0 = **81%** [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* In comments on data, quote a figure from the data (here the gradient or the percentage difference) rather than only saying "close to the ideal value".

**11. (a)** ω = 2π × 50 = 314 rad s⁻¹ [1]. E₀ = NΦ₀ω = 50 × 4.0 × 10⁻³ × 314 [1] = **63 V** [1].
**(b)** The e.m.f. depends on how fast the flux linkage changes [1]. At maximum flux the flux linkage is momentarily not changing (the graph is flat at its peak), so the gradient and the e.m.f. are zero [1].
**(c)** Vs = E₀ × Ns/Np = 62.8 × 150/800 [1] = **12 V** [1].
**(d)** Is = Vs/R = 11.8/24 = **0.49 A** [1]. Ip = Is × Ns/Np = 0.491 × 150/800 [1] = **0.092 A** [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* Carry unrounded values through the later parts.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using the angle to the plane instead of the angle to the normal in Φ = BA cos θ.
- Leaving out the number of turns when finding flux linkage or e.m.f.
- Not converting centimetres, millivolts or milliseconds before substituting.
- Saying Lenz's law "stops" the change, or not naming the change being opposed.
- Giving a reason for zero e.m.f. that refers to the flux being zero instead of its rate of change.
- Inverting the current ratio in the transformer equation.
- Describing a transformer investigation without stating what is kept constant.
