This guide goes deeper into **3.2.1.1 Structure of eukaryotic cells** from the AQA AS and A-level Biology specification (7401/7402), first teaching 2015, examined at AS and A-level. The specification restricts the content to a named list of structures, then asks you to apply that knowledge to explain the adaptations of eukaryotic cells and to understand how specialised cells are organised into tissues, organs and systems.

The [eukaryotic and prokaryotic cell structure study guide](/resources/a-level-aqa-biology-eukaryotic-and-prokaryotic-cell-structure/) pairs organelles by function and contrasts eukaryotes with prokaryotes, and the [cell structure revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-biology-cell-structure-revision-notes/) work the mitochondrion and the secretory pancreatic cell. This page goes organelle by organelle in more detail, adds the clues for identifying each one in an electron micrograph, sorts them by number of membranes, and applies them to four different specialised cells. Condense it with the [3.2.1.1 revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-biology-7402-structure-of-eukaryotic-cells-aqa-alevel-biology-revision-notes/). ## The specification's list

The structure of eukaryotic cells is restricted to the structure and function of:

- cell-surface membrane
- nucleus (containing chromosomes, consisting of protein-bound, linear DNA, and one or more nucleoli)
- mitochondria
- chloroplasts (in plants and algae)
- Golgi apparatus and Golgi vesicles
- lysosomes (a membrane-bound organelle that releases hydrolytic enzymes)
- ribosomes
- rough endoplasmic reticulum and smooth endoplasmic reticulum
- cell wall (in plants, algae and fungi)
- cell vacuole (in plants).

Learn exactly where each is found. A question that puts chloroplasts or a cell wall in an animal cell is testing those brackets.

## Organelle by organelle

| Structure | Structure in detail | Function | Identification clue in an electron micrograph |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cell-surface membrane | Phospholipid bilayer with proteins | Controls what enters and leaves the cell | Thin line around the cytoplasm |
| Nucleus | Double membrane (nuclear envelope) with pores; chromosomes of protein-bound linear DNA; one or more nucleoli | Holds genetic information; the nucleolus makes ribosomes | Largest structure; darker nucleolus inside; pores visible as gaps in the envelope |
| Mitochondrion | Double membrane; inner membrane folded into cristae; matrix | Site of aerobic respiration, producing ATP | Oval, with inner folds running across it |
| Chloroplast | Double membrane; stacks of thylakoids (grana) in a fluid stroma | Photosynthesis | Larger than mitochondria, with layered internal membranes |
| Golgi apparatus | Stack of flattened, curved membrane sacs | Modifies and packages proteins and lipids; forms lysosomes | Stack of curved sacs with vesicles budding from the edges |
| Golgi vesicle | Small membrane-bound sac | Transports products, for example to the cell-surface membrane for secretion | Small circles near the Golgi apparatus |
| Lysosome | Membrane-bound sac of hydrolytic enzymes | Hydrolyses pathogens, worn-out organelles and other material | Small dense sphere with a single membrane |
| Ribosome | Two subunits of RNA and protein; no membrane | Site of protein synthesis | Tiny dots, free or on membranes |
| Rough ER | Flattened membrane sacs studded with ribosomes | Synthesises and transports proteins | Parallel membranes dotted with ribosomes |
| Smooth ER | Membrane sacs without ribosomes | Synthesises and processes lipids | Smooth, often tubular membranes |
| Cell wall | Outside the membrane; in plants and algae made of cellulose microfibrils (3.1.2) | Strength and support; stops the cell bursting when water enters | Thick outer layer |
| Cell vacuole | Fluid-filled sac surrounded by a membrane (the tonoplast) | Stores cell sap; keeps the cell turgid | Large pale area filling much of a plant cell |

Note two details the table makes clear. Ribosomes are made of RNA and proteins, linking to 3.1.5. And the nucleolus is inside the nucleus, so "nucleolus" and "nucleus" are not interchangeable.

## Sorting by membranes

Questions often ask which structures are membrane-bound, or how many membranes surround a structure.

| Number of membranes | Structures |
|---|---|
| Two (an envelope) | Nucleus, mitochondrion, chloroplast |
| One | Golgi apparatus, Golgi vesicles, lysosomes, rough ER, smooth ER, vacuole (tonoplast) |
| None | Ribosomes, cell wall (which lies outside the cell-surface membrane) |

The cell-surface membrane is itself a single membrane around the whole cell. Being exact here also helps in 3.2.1.2, where the absence of membrane-bound organelles is one of the named differences of prokaryotic cells.

## How organelles work together

Organelles rarely act alone. Three sequences are worth knowing as chains:

- **Making and secreting a protein:** nucleus (genetic information) → ribosomes on rough ER (synthesis) → transport through the ER → vesicle to the Golgi apparatus (modification and packaging) → Golgi vesicle → cell-surface membrane (secretion).
- **Destroying a pathogen:** the cell engulfs the pathogen in a vesicle → lysosomes fuse with it → hydrolytic enzymes break it down. You will meet this in phagocytosis (3.2.4).
- **Powering the cell:** mitochondria resynthesise ATP in aerobic respiration (3.1.6), supplying energy for protein synthesis, active transport and movement.

## Applying the list to specialised cells

The specification asks you to explain adaptations. Use the same pattern each time: **feature → organelle's function → why the cell needs it**.

### Worked example: four specialised cells

| Cell | Feature | Explanation |
|---|---|---|
| Phagocyte (white blood cell) | Many lysosomes | Hydrolytic enzymes digest engulfed pathogens |
| Epithelial cell of the ileum | Many mitochondria | ATP for the active transport of ions involved in absorbing glucose and amino acids |
| Palisade mesophyll cell of a leaf | Many chloroplasts | More photosynthesis per cell, near the upper surface where light is strongest |
| Plasma cell (antibody-secreting cell) | Extensive rough ER and Golgi apparatus | Synthesis, packaging and secretion of large amounts of antibody protein |

Two cautions. Not all plant cells have chloroplasts: root cells lack them because they receive no light. And a feature only counts as an adaptation if you link it to the cell's job.

### Worked example: interpreting organelle counts

A student counts mitochondria in sections of three cell types. The data are invented for practice.

| Cell type | Mean mitochondria per cell section |
|---|---|
| A | 4 |
| B | 38 |
| C | 12 |

1. Cell B has 38 ÷ 4 = **9.5 times** as many mitochondria as cell A.
2. Suggest: B is likely to have the highest demand for ATP, for example a muscle cell or one carrying out much active transport.
3. Evaluate: a section shows only one slice through the cell, so counts depend on where the cut was made; more sections from more cells would give a more reliable mean.

## From cells to systems

In complex multicellular organisms, cells become specialised and are organised into larger units.

| Level | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Tissue | A group of similar cells working together for a function | Epithelium lining the ileum; xylem in a plant |
| Organ | Several tissues working together | The stomach; a leaf |
| Organ system | Several organs working together | The digestive system |

Use the specification's order in answers: cells into tissues, tissues into organs, organs into systems.

## Exam technique

- **Describe** an organelle's structure with its key features, such as "double membrane, inner membrane folded into cristae".
- **Explain** an adaptation using feature, function and need.
- **Identify** from a micrograph by naming the clue you used.
- **Compare** cell types in matched pairs, such as "a plant cell has a cellulose cell wall, whereas an animal cell has none".

## Common mistakes

- Saying mitochondria "make energy". They release energy in respiration and resynthesise ATP.
- Giving ribosomes a membrane.
- Writing that all plant cells have chloroplasts.
- Putting a cell wall or large vacuole in animal cells.
- Confusing the nucleolus with the nucleus.
- Saying the Golgi apparatus "makes proteins". Ribosomes make them; the Golgi modifies and packages them.

## Frequently asked questions

**Which organelles have a double membrane?**
The nucleus, mitochondria and chloroplasts.

**Do all plant cells contain chloroplasts?**
No. Chloroplasts are found in plant cells that photosynthesise, such as leaf cells. Root cells, for example, do not have them.

**What is the function of the Golgi apparatus?**
It modifies and packages proteins and lipids, forms Golgi vesicles that transport them, and forms lysosomes.

**Which eukaryotic cells have a cell wall?**
The specification names plants, algae and fungi. Animal cells have no cell wall.
