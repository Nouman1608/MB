This study guide teaches subtopic **5.5 Compound shapes and parts of shapes** from Topic 5 Mensuration of the Cambridge IGCSE Mathematics 0580 syllabus for 2025, 2026 and 2027. It brings together everything in 5.2 to 5.4: you split, combine or cut the shapes and solids you already know. The statements are the same for both tiers; the example the syllabus gives differs. Core (C5.5) gives "find the volume of half of a sphere", while Extended (E5.5) gives "find the surface area and volume of a **frustum**".

Consolidate with the [compound shapes revision notes](/resources/cambridge-igcse-mathematics-0580-compound-shapes-and-parts-of-shapes-cambridge-igcse-maths-revision-notes/) and test yourself with the [compound shapes practice questions](/resources/cambridge-igcse-mathematics-0580-compound-shapes-and-parts-of-shapes-cambridge-igcse-maths-practice/).

## What this subtopic covers

| Statement | What you must do |
|---|---|
| 5.5.1 | Carry out calculations and solve problems involving perimeters and areas of compound shapes and parts of shapes |
| 5.5.2 | Carry out calculations and solve problems involving surface areas and volumes of compound solids and parts of solids |

Answers may be asked for in terms of π. The building blocks are the rectangle, triangle, parallelogram and trapezium (5.2), circles, arcs and sectors (5.3) and the six solids of 5.4.

## Two strategies for area and volume

- **Add:** split the shape into pieces you know and add their areas or volumes.
- **Subtract:** take a larger simple shape and remove what is missing.

Choose whichever needs fewer unknown lengths. Mark every length you work out on your sketch, because compound questions hide lengths that are not labelled.

## Perimeter is different

The perimeter of a compound shape is the length of its **outside edge only**. Lines you draw to split the shape are not part of it, and an edge where two pieces join disappears. Never add the perimeters of the pieces.

### Worked example 1 (non-calculator): rectangle with a semicircular end

A shape is a rectangle 12 cm by 8 cm with a semicircle on one of its 8 cm sides. Find its area and perimeter in terms of π.

```
semicircle radius = 8 ÷ 2 = 4 cm
area      = 12 × 8 + ½ × π × 4² = (96 + 8π) cm²
perimeter = 12 + 12 + 8 + ½ × 2π × 4
          = (32 + 4π) cm
```

The 8 cm side under the semicircle is inside the shape, so it is not in the perimeter.

### Worked example 2 (non-calculator): an L-shape

An L-shape is a 10 cm by 7 cm rectangle with a 4 cm by 3 cm rectangle cut from one corner. Find its area and perimeter.

```
area = 10 × 7 − 4 × 3 = 70 − 12 = 58 cm²
perimeter: the two cut edges (4 cm and 3 cm) replace the two
           missing parts of the outer edge, which are also 4 cm and 3 cm
perimeter = 2 × 10 + 2 × 7 = 34 cm
```

Check by listing all six edges: 10, 7 − 3 = 4, 4, 3, 10 − 4 = 6 and 7 gives 10 + 4 + 4 + 3 + 6 + 7 = 34 cm.

## Parts of shapes

These parts come up most often:

| Part | Area | Perimeter |
|---|---|---|
| Semicircle, radius r | ½πr² | πr + 2r |
| Quarter circle | ¼πr² | ½πr + 2r |
| Annulus (ring), radii R and r | πR² − πr² | 2πR + 2πr |
| Segment (between chord and arc) | sector area − triangle area | arc + chord |

### Worked example 3 (calculator): shaded regions

(a) A circle of radius 5 cm fits exactly inside a square of side 10 cm. Find the area of the square that is outside the circle.
(b) A quarter circle has radius 6 cm. Find the area of the segment between its arc and the chord joining the ends of the arc.

```
(a) 10² − π × 5² = 100 − 25π = 21.5 cm²  (3 s.f.)

(b) sector    = ¼ × π × 36 = 9π
    triangle  = ½ × 6 × 6 = 18   (the two radii meet at 90°)
    segment   = 9π − 18 = 10.3 cm²  (3 s.f.)
```

For segments with other angles, the triangle needs the Extended formula ½ab sin C from subtopic 6.5.

## Compound solids

The volume of a compound solid is the sum of its parts. The **surface area** needs more care: only faces on the outside count, so the faces where two solids are joined are left out.

### Worked example 4 (calculator): a grain silo

A silo is a cylinder of radius 3 m and height 10 m with a hemisphere on top. It stands on the ground. Find its volume and the area of its outside surface, not including the base.

```
volume:
  cylinder   π × 3² × 10 = 90π
  hemisphere ½ × (4/3)π × 3³ = 18π
  total      108π = 339 m³  (3 s.f.)
outside surface (no base, and no join between the parts):
  cylinder curved surface   2π × 3 × 10 = 60π
  hemisphere curved surface ½ × 4π × 3² = 18π
  total                     78π = 245 m²  (3 s.f.)
```

## Parts of solids

### Worked example 5 (non-calculator): a solid hemisphere

A solid hemisphere has radius 4 cm. Find its volume and total surface area in terms of π.

```
volume = ½ × (4/3)π × 4³ = (2/3)π × 64 = 128π/3 cm³
curved surface = ½ × 4π × 4² = 32π
flat face      = π × 4² = 16π
total surface area = 48π cm²
```

The flat circular face is the part most often forgotten. A solid hemisphere has total surface area 3πr².

### Worked example 6 (calculator): a hollow pipe

A metal pipe is 20 cm long. Its outer radius is 5 cm and its inner radius is 4 cm. Find the volume of metal.

```
cross-section = π × 5² − π × 4² = 9π  (an annulus)
volume = 9π × 20 = 180π = 565 cm³  (3 s.f.)
```

The pipe is a prism whose cross-section is a ring.

## The frustum (Extended)

A frustum is what is left when the top of a cone (or pyramid) is cut off by a cut parallel to the base. The removed top is a smaller cone **similar** to the original, so its dimensions are in the same ratio.

### Worked example 7 (Extended, calculator)

A cone has base radius 9 cm and height 15 cm. A cone of height 5 cm is removed from the top, leaving a frustum. Find the volume and total surface area of the frustum.

```
scale factor of small cone = 5/15 = 1/3, so its radius = 3 cm
volume = (1/3)π × 9² × 15 − (1/3)π × 3² × 5
       = 405π − 15π = 390π = 1230 cm³  (3 s.f.)
large slant height L = √(9² + 15²) = √306 = 17.49... cm
small slant height = L ÷ 3
curved surface = π × 9 × L − π × 3 × (L/3) = 8πL = 439.6... cm²
two circular faces = π × 9² + π × 3² = 90π = 282.7... cm²
total surface area = 722 cm²  (3 s.f.)
```

Find the small cone's radius from similarity, not by measuring or guessing. Keep √306 unrounded to the end.

## Exam technique

- Start by **sketching and naming** the pieces: "rectangle + semicircle", "cylinder + hemisphere − cone".
- Write each piece's area or volume on a separate line, then combine. Method marks are given piece by piece.
- For "in terms of π", collect the π terms and the number terms separately, for example (96 + 8π) cm².
- For surface area, list the faces before adding, and cross out any hidden joins.

## Common mistakes

- Adding the perimeters of the pieces, so internal edges are counted.
- Using the diameter of a semicircle as its radius.
- Forgetting the flat face of a hemisphere or the cut faces of part of a solid.
- Including the joined faces in the surface area of a compound solid.
- In a frustum, assuming the small radius is half the large one without checking the heights.
- Rounding an intermediate slant height or radius.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is the frustum on the Core paper for 0580?**
The frustum is the example given in the Extended notes (E5.5). The Core notes give the volume of half a sphere as their example.

**How do I find the perimeter of a compound shape?**
Trace round the outside edge only, adding each length once. Lines inside the shape and edges where pieces join are not included.
