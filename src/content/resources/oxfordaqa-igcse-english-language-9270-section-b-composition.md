**Section B of Paper 1 in OxfordAQA International GCSE English Language (9270) is the composition
task.** It carries 40 of the paper's 80 marks. There are three tasks, and you choose **one**: the
specification lists the types as descriptive, imaginative, and argumentative or discursive. This guide
explains what each type demands, how the writing objectives are applied, and how to plan and control
a piece under exam conditions.

## What the specification says

- The writing section consists of **three tasks from which students choose one**.
- The task types are **descriptive, imaginative, argumentative and/or discursive**.
- Writing is assessed against **AO2**, worth 30% of the qualification through Paper 1:

| Objective | Specification wording | In practice |
|---|---|---|
| W1 | Communicate clearly, effectively and imaginatively; use and adapt forms and select vocabulary appropriate to task and purpose in ways that engage the reader | Content, voice, vocabulary and engagement |
| W2 | Use a range of sentence structures for clarity, purpose and effect | Varied, controlled sentences chosen for effect |
| W3 | Organise information and ideas into sentences, paragraphs and whole texts; use a variety of linguistic and structural features to support cohesion and overall coherence | Shape, paragraphing, links, a planned whole |
| W4 | Demonstrate technical accuracy in grammar, punctuation and spelling | Correct, secure written English |

## Choosing your task

Spend the first minutes deciding, not writing. Ask:

1. **Which task can I plan an ending for right now?** If you cannot see the end, you will drift.
2. **Which suits my strengths?** Precise vocabulary favours description; a strong sense of shape
   favours narrative; clear opinions and evidence favour argument.
3. **What exactly does the task ask?** "Describe a moment when…" is not "Write a story about…". A task
   asking you to "discuss" wants a balanced exploration; one asking you to "argue" wants a position.

## Descriptive writing

Description builds a picture and a mood without needing a plot.

- **Narrow the focus.** One place at one moment, or one person in one action. A whole day becomes a
  list.
- **Move the camera.** Structure description by changing viewpoint: wide view → detail → wider again,
  or outside → inside, or the same place at two times.
- **Use all the senses**, but select: one precise sound beats five vague ones.
- **Choose exact nouns and verbs** before adjectives: "the shutters rattled" is stronger than "the very
  loud windows".
- **Let the mood develop.** A piece that begins calm and ends tense, or the reverse, has shape.

## Imaginative writing

Imaginative writing usually means a narrative, but the same control applies.

- **Keep it small.** One main character, one setting, one problem, a short time span.
- **Start late.** Begin close to the moment of change rather than with waking up.
- **Plan the ending first.** A twist, a realisation, a circular return to the opening image or an open
  ending all work if they are deliberate.
- **Show, don't summarise.** Reveal feelings through action, detail and brief dialogue.
- **Control time.** Slow down at the key moment; summarise less important time in a sentence.

## Argumentative and discursive writing

These are different tasks, and the difference matters:

| Argumentative | Discursive |
|---|---|
| Takes a clear position and persuades the reader | Explores several viewpoints in a balanced way |
| Counter-arguments raised in order to rebut them | Counter-arguments weighed fairly |
| Confident, committed tone | Measured, reflective tone |
| Ends with a call to action or firm conclusion | Ends with a considered judgement, which may be qualified |

For both:

- **Plan three or four developed points**, each with evidence or an example and an explanation.
- **Open with a hook**: an anecdote, a striking fact you know to be true, a question or a bold claim.
- **Link paragraphs** with discourse markers that show logic: *however, consequently, admittedly, in
  contrast, above all*.
- **Use rhetoric purposefully**: rhetorical questions, direct address, the rule of three, emotive
  language, but never in every sentence.
- **Avoid invented statistics.** A vivid, realistic example is more convincing than a made-up number.

## Sentence control (W2)

- A **short sentence** after longer ones creates emphasis.
- A **long, multi-clause sentence** can build a list of details or show rising tension.
- **Fronted adverbials** ("Without warning, …") and **subordinate clauses first** ("Although the
  door was open, …") vary openings.
- **Minor sentences** ("Silence.") are effective once or twice, not throughout.
- Vary for a reason: every choice should do a job.

## Organisation and cohesion (W3)

- Use **paragraphs** to mark a change of time, place, speaker, focus or argument.
- Build **cohesion** with repeated motifs or images, pronouns, synonyms and linking words.
- Aim for **coherence across the whole piece**: the ending should answer the opening.

## Accuracy (W4)

Check in the final minutes for: sentence boundaries (no comma splices), apostrophes, homophones
(there/their/they're, your/you're), consistent tense, capital letters and the punctuation of speech.

## Worked example: a descriptive opening with annotation

Task (original): *Describe a railway station late at night.*

> *The last train has gone. Under the station clock, a single pigeon walks in careful circles, as if
> it has lost something small. The vending machine hums to itself. Somewhere above the empty platform
> a loose sign creaks, settles, creaks again — the only conversation left in the building.*

- **Short opening sentence** establishes the moment and the mood of absence (W2).
- **Precise, unusual detail**: the pigeon "walks in careful circles", a simile suggesting searching
  and loneliness (W1).
- **Personification** of the machine ("hums to itself") and the sign ("conversation") makes the
  station feel alive but deserted.
- **Dash** isolates the final phrase for emphasis, and the image could return at the end of the piece
  for a cyclical structure (W3).

## Worked example: argument vs discussion

Task (original): *"Teenagers should be paid for doing well at school."*

- **Argumentative opening:** "Imagine working for five years with no wages, no promotion and a
  report card as your only reward. That is school. Yet paying students for grades would be one of the
  worst ideas a government could adopt."
- **Discursive opening:** "Few questions divide parents more quickly than whether children should be
  rewarded for success. Supporters see money as motivation; critics fear it teaches students to value
  learning only for what it pays."

Both are effective; they simply do different jobs. Decide which the task wants before you begin.

## Planning in five minutes

```
1. Task type and form, in one line
2. Ending: what is the final image, realisation or conclusion?
3. Four or five paragraph headings in order
4. Three ambitious but accurate words or images to use
5. One structural device: cyclical ending, time shift, change of focus
```

## Common mistakes

- **Choosing quickly and changing mind** half way through.
- **Writing a story when asked to describe**, or a list of opinions when asked to discuss.
- **Over-writing**: piling up adjectives and similes until meaning is lost.
- **Weak endings**: "and then I woke up", or an argument that simply stops.
- **Uncontrolled ambition**: a misused word costs more than a simple word used well.
- **No time to check**, so avoidable errors remain.
