> These are original practice questions written in the style of Cambridge A Level
> Psychology (9990) assessment objectives. They are not taken from any past
> paper and are not endorsed by Cambridge International. Questions are written
> to be adaptable to whichever two specialist options your own course teaches
> — confirm with your teacher which options apply to you.

Use these questions alongside the [Specialist Options study
guide](/resources/a-level-psychology-specialist-options/) and
[revision
notes](/resources/a-level-psychology-specialist-options-revision-notes/).

## Section A

**1.** Name the four specialist options available on this syllabus. **[4]**

**2.** State how many of the four options a typical course studies,
and via which two papers. **[2]**

**3.** Name the four core approaches from AS Level that each
specialist option applies to its specific context. **[4]**

## Section B

**4.** A candidate assumes their course has taught all four specialist
options and revises accordingly. Explain why this assumption is risky,
and what the candidate should have done instead. **[2]**

**5.** A candidate answering a Clinical Psychology question presents a
single classification system as though it were the only accepted way
to diagnose a disorder, with no discussion of any alternative or
limitation.

**(a)** Explain why this answer is unlikely to reach the top mark
band. **[2]**
**(b)** Suggest what the candidate should have included instead. **[2]**

**6.** A question asks a candidate to explain why an advertising
campaign successfully increased sales, using a named psychological
concept.

**(a)** Explain why an answer discussing "consumer behaviour in
general" would be weaker than one applying a specific concept to the
campaign described. **[2]**
**(b)** Suggest one type of psychological concept (from persuasion or
decision-making) that could be applied to such a scenario. **[1]**

**7.** A patient does not follow medical advice given by their doctor.
A candidate explains this using a cognitive approach, focused on how
the patient perceives their own risk of illness.

**(a)** Explain why this is a stronger answer than simply stating "the
patient didn't care." **[2]**
**(b)** Suggest one limitation of relying only on this cognitive
explanation. **[2]**

**8.** A company introduces a bonus scheme, but productivity does not
increase. A candidate applies Herzberg's two-factor theory to explain
this.

**(a)** Explain how Herzberg's theory accounts for why a purely
financial incentive might fail to increase productivity. **[2]**
**(b)** Suggest one limitation of applying Herzberg's theory to this
scenario. **[2]**

**9.** Explain why a specialist-option question might ask a candidate
to suggest and evaluate a research method for studying a claim within
that option, rather than only testing theoretical knowledge. **[2]**

**10.** Explain why two candidates at different schools, both sitting
Cambridge A Level Psychology (9990), might revise completely different
specialist-option content despite taking the same qualification. **[3]**

---

## Answers

**1.** Clinical Psychology, Consumer Psychology, Health Psychology,
and Organisational Psychology [4].

**2.** Two of the four options, studied via Paper 3 and Paper 4 (one
option per paper) [2].

**3.** Biological, Cognitive, Learning and Social [4].

**4.** This assumption is risky because a school studies only two of
the four options, and which two is a curriculum decision made locally
rather than fixed by the syllabus [1]; the candidate should confirm
with their teacher exactly which two options their own course covers
before revising [1].

**5. (a)** This answer is unlikely to reach the top mark band because
strong Clinical Psychology answers discuss more than one
classification or treatment approach and weigh their relative
strengths, rather than presenting a single model as uncontested [2].
**(b)** The candidate should have discussed at least one alternative
classification system or treatment approach and evaluated the
strengths and limitations of each relative to the other [2].

**6. (a)** A general discussion of consumer behaviour does not
demonstrate the ability to apply a specific psychological concept to
the actual scenario given, whereas applying a named concept (such as a
specific persuasion technique or decision-making bias) directly to the
campaign shows the applied understanding the question is testing [2].
**(b)** For example, a persuasion technique (such as social proof) or
a decision-making bias (such as anchoring) [1].

**7. (a)** This is a stronger answer because it applies a specific
psychological framework — how the patient's own risk perception shapes
their behaviour — directly to the scenario, rather than offering an
unexplained, common-sense statement with no psychological basis [2].
**(b)** For example, a purely cognitive explanation may understate the
role of social or economic barriers (such as family attitudes or cost)
that also influence whether the patient follows medical advice [2].

**8. (a)** Herzberg's theory classifies pay as a hygiene factor, which
can prevent dissatisfaction but does not, on its own, generate genuine
motivation; genuine motivation instead requires motivators such as
recognition or job enrichment, which explains why a purely financial
incentive like a bonus scheme might fail to increase productivity [2].
**(b)** Herzberg's theory was developed from research in specific
occupational contexts, so its findings may not generalise fully to all
types of workplace or culture [2].

**9.** Because each specialist option is assessed alongside Research
Methods applied to that specific context, so the syllabus expects
candidates to be able to suggest and evaluate how a claim within the
option could actually be studied, not just recall the option's
theoretical content in isolation [2].

**10.** Because any of the four specialist options can, in principle,
be studied for either Paper 3 or Paper 4, and which two options a
school chooses to teach is a local curriculum decision, not fixed by
the syllabus itself; two schools could therefore reasonably choose two
entirely different pairs of options while both preparing candidates
for the same qualification [3].

## A note on exam technique for this topic

Across all four specialist options, the same underlying pattern
separates strong answers from weak ones: apply a specific named
approach, concept or theory directly to the scenario given, then
evaluate it by naming a genuine limitation, rather than describing the
applied context in general terms or presenting a single explanation as
complete on its own. Because each option is also assessed alongside
Research Methods, revision should not treat theoretical content and
methodology as separate topics — be ready to suggest and evaluate how
a claim within your chosen option could actually be investigated, since
this is tested as consistently here as in the core AS Level papers.
Before doing any past-paper practice, confirm which two of the four
options your own course actually teaches, since practising the wrong
option wastes revision time regardless of how well-written the
questions are.
