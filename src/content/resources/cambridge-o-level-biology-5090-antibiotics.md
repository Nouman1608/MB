This guide covers subtopic **12.2 Antibiotics** of Cambridge O Level Biology 5090, 2026-2028 series. The syllabus asks you to describe a drug as any substance taken into the body that modifies or affects chemical reactions in the body, to describe the use of antibiotics for the treatment of bacterial infection, to state that antibiotics kill bacteria but do not affect viruses, and to explain how the development of antibiotic-resistant bacteria, including MRSA, can be minimised by using antibiotics only when essential.

## Drugs

A **drug** is **any substance taken into the body that modifies or affects chemical reactions in the body.**

This definition is wide. It covers medicines such as painkillers and antibiotics, and also substances such as alcohol, nicotine and caffeine. A drug may be helpful (a medicine) or harmful (an addictive or poisonous drug). The key phrase is *modifies or affects chemical reactions in the body*, which a good answer should always include.

## Antibiotics

**Antibiotics** are drugs used to treat **bacterial infections**. They are made by or based on substances produced by fungi or bacteria. Penicillin was the first.

**Use**: a doctor prescribes an antibiotic to a patient with an infection caused by bacteria, such as some types of throat infection, ear infections or tuberculosis. The antibiotic is taken into the body, and it **kills the bacteria** (or stops them from growing) so that the person recovers.

### Antibiotics do not affect viruses

**Antibiotics kill bacteria but do not affect viruses.** This is a key fact.

- Bacteria are living cells with structures, such as a cell wall, that antibiotics attack.
- Viruses are not cells. They have no cell wall or cytoplasm, and they replicate inside the host's cells, where an antibiotic cannot reach them without harming the host.

Antibiotics therefore do **not** work on colds, influenza or HIV, which are caused by viruses. Taking an antibiotic for a viral infection will not make the person better, and it also contributes to the problem of resistance.

| | Bacteria | Viruses |
|---|---|---|
| What they are | Single living cells | Protein coat with genetic material, not cells |
| Treated by antibiotics? | Yes | No |
| Examples of diseases | Cholera, tuberculosis | Influenza, HIV, the common cold |

## Antibiotic resistance

Bacteria can become **resistant** to an antibiotic, which means the antibiotic no longer kills them. The development of resistance is an example of natural selection.

### How resistance develops

1. In a large population of bacteria, **mutations** occur by chance. A mutation may make one bacterium resistant to an antibiotic.
2. When the antibiotic is used, it kills the non-resistant bacteria.
3. The resistant bacterium **survives**. There is now less competition, so it reproduces.
4. It passes the gene for resistance to its offspring. Bacteria can divide very quickly (as often as every 20 minutes), so the resistant population grows fast.
5. The infection can no longer be treated with that antibiotic.

### Worked example: how fast resistance spreads

*A population of 1 000 000 bacteria contains 1 in 100 000 resistant bacteria. An antibiotic kills all the non-resistant bacteria. The survivors divide every 20 minutes. How many resistant bacteria are there after 4 hours?*

1. Number of resistant bacteria at the start = 1 000 000 divided by 100 000 = **10**.
2. In 4 hours there are 4 x 60 = 240 minutes, which is 240 divided by 20 = **12 divisions**.
3. The population doubles in each division, so it is multiplied by 2 twelve times: 2 to the power 12 = **4096**.
4. Number after 4 hours = 10 x 4096 = **40 960**.

Within a few hours, the population of resistant bacteria has grown from 10 to over 40 000, and the antibiotic is useless against them. This is why resistance can spread so fast in a hospital or a community.

### MRSA

**MRSA** (methicillin-resistant *Staphylococcus aureus*) is a strain of bacteria resistant to many antibiotics. It is a problem in hospitals, where patients with weak immune systems or open wounds are exposed to it, and infections are hard to treat.

## Minimising resistance

The syllabus requires you to explain that resistance can be reduced by **using antibiotics only when essential**. The reasons are:

- The more often an antibiotic is used, the more chance there is for resistant bacteria to be selected and to increase.
- Using antibiotics only when they are really needed gives bacteria fewer opportunities to evolve resistance.

Ways of doing this:

| Measure | Reason |
|---|---|
| Do not use antibiotics for viral infections | They have no effect on viruses, and it only gives bacteria a chance to become resistant |
| Use them only when essential, as prescribed by a doctor | Fewer bacteria are exposed, so fewer resistant bacteria are selected |
| Complete the whole course | Prevents the survivors, which are likely to be the most resistant, from multiplying |
| Do not overuse antibiotics in farming | Reduces the exposure of bacteria to antibiotics outside human medicine |
| Good hygiene in hospitals | Prevents resistant bacteria such as MRSA from spreading |
| Develop new antibiotics | Replace those that no longer work |

## Investigating antibiotic effects: inhibition zones

In a school or laboratory practical, bacteria are spread on an agar plate, and small paper discs soaked in different antibiotics are placed on it. After incubation, a clear ring called an **inhibition zone** appears around a disc where the bacteria could not grow.

### Worked example

*Three discs gave clear zones of diameter 24 mm (A), 12 mm (B) and 0 mm (C).*

1. **Which antibiotic is most effective?** A, because it has the largest clear zone.
2. **Which does not work?** C, as there is no zone, so the bacteria grew right up to the disc. These bacteria are resistant to C.
3. **Compare the areas**: area = pi x radius squared. For A, radius 12 mm: 3.14 x 12 x 12 = **452 mm squared**. For B, radius 6 mm: 3.14 x 6 x 6 = **113 mm squared**. The area for A is 452 divided by 113 = **4 times** that for B, although the diameter is only twice as big.
4. **Safety and fairness**: use sterile apparatus, keep the plate taped (not sealed) and at a temperature below 30 degrees Celsius so that harmful bacteria are not encouraged, and compare discs of the same size on the same plate.

## Linking antibiotics to other topics

- **Cells and classification (topics 1 and 2)**: bacteria are prokaryotes with a cell wall and no nucleus. Viruses are not cells. This is why one treatment works on one group and not the other.
- **Inheritance and variation**: the resistance gene arises by mutation, a change in the genetic material. The process by which the resistant bacteria survive and the others die is selection.
- **Disease (subtopic 12.1)**: cholera and tuberculosis are bacterial diseases that antibiotics can treat. HIV and influenza are viral and cannot be treated this way. Malaria is caused by a protoctist, so it needs a different kind of drug.
- **Immunity (subtopic 12.3)**: vaccination prevents many diseases, which means that fewer antibiotics need to be used. Preventing infection is a second way to slow the spread of resistance.

## Why resistance is a serious problem

If common antibiotics stop working, minor infections and routine operations could again become dangerous. Doctors would need to use stronger or more expensive drugs, which may have more side effects. New antibiotics take many years to develop, and bacteria can become resistant to them as well. For these reasons the careful use of the antibiotics we already have is important.

A short summary for a "discuss" question: antibiotics have saved many lives, but overuse selects for resistant bacteria such as MRSA. Reducing unnecessary use, finishing courses, and improving hygiene and vaccination can slow this process, although they cannot remove it entirely.

## Common mistakes

- Saying that antibiotics kill viruses, or that they cure colds and flu.
- Saying that **bacteria** become resistant because they "get used to" the antibiotic. The correct explanation is that resistant mutants survive and reproduce.
- Saying that **people** become resistant to antibiotics. It is the bacteria.
- Writing that taking antibiotics "weakens" the body's immune system.
- Saying that MRSA is a virus.
- Forgetting that a drug "modifies or affects chemical reactions in the body".
- Writing that resistance is caused by the antibiotic. It is selected by the antibiotic; the mutation arises by chance.

## Command words

- **Describe**: define a drug, or describe the use of antibiotics.
- **State**: give the fact that antibiotics do not affect viruses.
- **Explain**: give the stages of the development of resistance, linking mutation, survival and reproduction.
- **Suggest**: give a reason why using antibiotics only when essential reduces resistance.
