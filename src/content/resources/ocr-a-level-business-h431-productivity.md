## What productivity is

**Productivity** measures how efficiently a business turns inputs into outputs. It is output compared with the resources used to make it. Higher productivity means more output from the same inputs, or the same output from fewer.

The most common measure is **labour productivity**:

labour productivity = output ÷ number of employees (or output ÷ hours worked)

Other measures:

- **Capital productivity** = output ÷ capital employed (the value of machinery and equipment). It shows how well the business uses its assets.
- **Labour cost per unit** (unit labour cost) = total labour cost ÷ output. It links productivity to cost.
- **Output per hour** is useful when staff work different hours.

Productivity is not the same as production. Production is the amount made. A business can raise production by hiring more people without raising productivity.

## Worked example 1: three measures

Orchard Joinery makes kitchen units. All figures are invented.

| | Year 1 | Year 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Output (units) | 48,000 | 52,500 |
| Employees | 40 | 41 |
| Hours worked per employee | 1,600 | 1,600 |
| Wage per employee | £28,000 | £28,000 |

**Labour productivity (per employee).**

- Year 1: 48,000 ÷ 40 = **1,200 units**.
- Year 2: 52,500 ÷ 41 = **1,280.5 units** (to one decimal place).
- Change = (1,280.5 ÷ 1,200 − 1) × 100 = **+6.7%**.

Output rose by 9.4% (52,500 ÷ 48,000 = 1.09375) but employment rose by 2.5% (41 ÷ 40), so productivity rose by about 6.7%.

**Output per hour.**

- Year 1: 48,000 ÷ (40 × 1,600) = 48,000 ÷ 64,000 = **0.75 units**.
- Year 2: 52,500 ÷ (41 × 1,600) = 52,500 ÷ 65,600 = **0.80 units**.

**Labour cost per unit.**

- Year 1: (40 × £28,000) ÷ 48,000 = £1,120,000 ÷ 48,000 = **£23.33**.
- Year 2: (41 × £28,000) ÷ 52,500 = £1,148,000 ÷ 52,500 = **£21.87**.

Labour cost per unit fell by £1.46 (6.3%) even though pay per employee was unchanged. This is the link between productivity and competitiveness: higher productivity lowers the labour cost of each unit, so the business can cut prices or raise profit.

## Importance of productivity

| Stakeholder | Impact |
|---|---|
| Owners and shareholders | Lower unit cost and higher profit; a stronger business |
| Employees | Scope for higher pay and job security if the business competes well; but pressure, redundancy or intensification of work if productivity gains come from cutting staff |
| Customers | Lower prices or better quality |
| Suppliers | Higher volumes if the business grows |
| The economy | Productivity growth is the main source of rising living standards; low productivity weakens competitiveness |

Productivity is especially important in competitive markets and in labour-intensive industries where wages are a large share of cost. Limits of the measure: it ignores quality (a faster but poorer product), it is hard to compare when products differ, and it can rise because output rose for reasons other than efficiency, such as a boom.

## Ways to improve productivity

| Method | How it helps | Costs and risks |
|---|---|---|
| Training | Staff work faster and make fewer errors | Cost and time off work; staff may leave after training |
| New technology and machinery | Does more work per worker | High cost; job losses; maintenance; staff need new skills |
| Motivation and pay incentives | Bonuses, recognition and better conditions raise effort | Recurring cost; may encourage speed over quality |
| Job redesign and teamwork | Makes work more varied and flexible | Needs time and managers' support |
| Better layout and processes (lean methods) | Cuts waste and waiting | Disruption while changing |
| Better management and communication | Clear targets and fewer delays | Hard to measure |

### Worked example 2: comparing methods

Orchard's output is **52,000** units a year with **40** employees (labour productivity 1,300 units each) and **£400,000** of capital employed (capital productivity 52,000 ÷ 400,000 = **0.13** units per £ of capital). The contribution on each unit is **£8**. Three methods are proposed:

| Method | Cost | Expected rise in output |
|---|---|---|
| A: training programme | £30,000 one-off | +6% |
| B: new machinery | £120,000 one-off | +15% |
| C: annual bonus scheme | £18,000 each year | +4% |

**Extra output and extra contribution a year.**

- A: 52,000 × 6% = 3,120 units; 3,120 × £8 = **£24,960**.
- B: 52,000 × 15% = 7,800 units; 7,800 × £8 = **£62,400**.
- C: 52,000 × 4% = 2,080 units; 2,080 × £8 = **£16,640**.

**Payback and return.**

- A: £30,000 ÷ £24,960 = **1.20 years**.
- B: £120,000 ÷ £62,400 = **1.92 years**.
- C: the cost recurs. Each year the benefit (£16,640) is less than the cost (£18,000), a net loss of **£1,360** a year.

**Productivity after the change.**

- A: output 55,120; per employee 55,120 ÷ 40 = **1,378** (+6%).
- B: output 59,800; per employee 59,800 ÷ 40 = **1,495** (+15%). But capital employed rises to £520,000, so capital productivity = 59,800 ÷ 520,000 = **0.115**, down from 0.13.

**Interpretation.** Training pays back fastest among the one-off options and raises labour productivity at no extra capital. The machinery earns the largest yearly gain but takes longer to pay back and lowers capital productivity, so the business gets more output per worker but less from each pound invested. Over five years B would earn 5 × £62,400 − £120,000 = **£192,000**, against A's 5 × £24,960 − £30,000 = **£94,800**, so B is more profitable if the machine lasts and the extra output can be sold. The bonus loses money as designed and would need a lower cost or a higher gain. The extra output also has to be sold: if demand is flat, higher productivity means fewer staff are needed, which has consequences for employees.

## Recommending methods

A strong recommendation:

1. identifies the main cause of low productivity (skills, equipment, motivation, layout);
2. selects one or two methods with figures, as in worked example 2;
3. compares cost with benefit and payback;
4. considers effects on staff (redundancy, morale) and on quality;
5. states a risk and how success will be measured.

For Orchard, a sound recommendation is to **train staff first** (quick payback, low risk), then invest in machinery if orders rise, while keeping a check on quality. Reject the bonus in its present form.

## Common mistakes

- Confusing productivity (output per input) with production (total output).
- Calculating labour productivity from total output without dividing by employees or hours.
- Forgetting that a recurring cost, such as a bonus, must be compared with the yearly benefit.
- Assuming higher productivity always means a better business: quality and demand matter.
- Ignoring the effect on employees.

## Exam technique

Write the formula, substitute and give the unit. Interpret changes as percentages. For "recommend and justify", choose a method, give figures and justify with payback, then state a risk.
