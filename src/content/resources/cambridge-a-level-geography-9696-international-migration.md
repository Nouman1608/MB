This guide covers section 5.3 of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Geography (9696) syllabus for examination in 2025 and 2026: "Voluntary and forced (involuntary) movements" and "Causes and patterns of international migrations (including economic migration and refugee flows) and impacts on source areas and receiving/destination areas." It is part of Topic 5, Migration, in Paper 2 Core Human Geography.

The [5.3 revision notes](/resources/a-level-geography-9696-international-migration-revision-notes/) give key terms and a table of four streams. This guide explains why international flows take the shapes they do, adds further examples, and shows how to structure answers on impacts. Test yourself with the [5.3 practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-international-migration-practice/).

## Voluntary and forced movement

**Voluntary migrants** choose to move, usually for work, study or to join family. **Forced (involuntary) migrants** move because staying would put their lives or freedom at risk: war, persecution, or disaster.

The **1951 Refugee Convention** defines a refugee as a person outside their country of nationality who has a well-founded fear of persecution for reasons of race, religion, nationality, membership of a particular social group or political opinion. People fleeing general violence or disaster are often protected in practice, but the legal definition is narrower than everyday use.

In reality movement sits on a continuum. A household leaving after several failed harvests, or a family leaving a city where violence and unemployment are both rising, makes a choice under heavy pressure. Many routes carry **mixed migration**: refugees and economic migrants travelling together, using the same smugglers and arriving at the same borders. Strong answers place a flow on the continuum and justify it.

## Causes of economic migration

| Cause | How it works |
|---|---|
| Wage and income gaps | Earnings for the same work can be many times higher in the destination |
| Labour demand | Ageing HICs and oil-rich states need workers in care, construction, farming and services |
| Recruitment | Employers and agencies recruit abroad, sometimes through government agreements |
| Networks | Earlier migrants supply information, housing and jobs (chain migration) |
| Historical and language links | Former colonial ties, such as North Africa to France or South Asia to the UK, shape destinations |
| Policy | Visa rules, free movement areas and points systems open or close routes |

Note the interaction. A wage gap explains why people want to move; networks, history and policy explain **where** they go and **who** can go.

## Causes of refugee flows

Refugee flows follow conflict, persecution and the collapse of states. Their size and timing are set by events, and their direction by geography and policy.

- **Rohingya to Bangladesh.** After violence in Rakhine State, Myanmar, from August 2017, more than 700 000 Rohingya crossed into the Cox's Bazar district of Bangladesh within a few months. Kutupalong became one of the largest refugee settlements in the world.
- **Venezuela.** Economic collapse, hyperinflation and shortages from the mid-2010s led millions of Venezuelans to leave, mostly to other Latin American countries. Colombia, the largest host, announced a ten-year temporary protection status for Venezuelans in 2021.
- **Ukraine.** After the Russian invasion of February 2022, millions fled west. In March 2022 the European Union activated its Temporary Protection Directive for the first time, giving Ukrainians the right to live and work in member states without an asylum claim. Poland received very large numbers.

The Venezuelan case sits near the middle of the continuum: people left because of economic collapse rather than war, yet many had little real choice.

## Patterns

1. **Most refugees stay close to home.** Neighbouring countries host most refugees, because people flee on foot or by road and hope to return. Many hosts are themselves LICs or MICs.
2. **South-South flows are large.** Movement between developing countries, for example within West Africa or from South Asia to the Gulf states, is as important as movement to HICs.
3. **Corridors.** Flows concentrate along particular routes, such as Mexico to the United States, shaped by distance, networks and history.
4. **Distance decay is weaker than for internal moves** for skilled workers, whose job market is global, but stronger for the poorest, who cannot afford long journeys.
5. **Flows change over time.** Policy shifts, recessions and conflicts can start, stop or reverse a stream within a few years.

## Impacts on source areas

**Demographic.** Loss of young adults lowers the birth rate and ages the population. Sex ratios become unbalanced where one sex dominates emigration.

**Economic.** Remittances raise household incomes and bring in foreign currency. India receives more remittances than any other country. Emigration can relieve unemployment. Against this, the loss of skilled workers, such as doctors, nurses and engineers, weakens services (brain drain), and the state loses the return on the education it paid for.

**Social.** Families are separated, and children may grow up with one or both parents abroad. Returning migrants bring skills, savings and new ideas ("social remittances"), which can change attitudes to education or women's work.

**Political.** Large diasporas can lobby for their country of origin, but the departure of critics can also weaken pressure for reform at home.

## Impacts on receiving areas

**Demographic.** Migrants are mostly young adults, so they lower the average age, raise the birth rate and slow population ageing.

**Economic.** Labour gaps are filled in sectors such as health care, farming and construction. Migrants pay taxes and start businesses. Some studies find small downward pressure on wages for low-paid local workers, while others find little effect; answers should present this as debated.

**Social and cultural.** Diversity in food, music and religion; but also segregation, and tension when migration is rapid or politically contested.

**Services.** Short-term pressure on housing, schools and health care, especially where arrivals are concentrated.

**Refugee host areas.** In host districts of LICs and MICs the impacts are sharper. In Cox's Bazar, camps were built on forested hillsides, which were cleared for shelter and firewood, raising the risk of landslides in the monsoon. Local wages for casual work fell as refugees took informal jobs, while food prices rose. At the same time, aid agencies brought jobs and spending into the district.

## A framework for impacts answers

Examiners reward answers that recognise impacts are not the same for everyone. Use four filters:

- **Place:** source or destination, and which part (capital, border district, village).
- **Group:** employers, local workers, migrants themselves, governments, families left behind.
- **Time:** the first months of a refugee crisis differ from twenty years later.
- **Type:** economic, demographic, social, environmental, political.

## Worked example: interpreting data

*A table shows, for the fictional country of Velmar, that remittances rose from 4% to 11% of GDP over twenty years, while the number of doctors per 10 000 people fell from 6 to 4. Suggest what the data show about the impacts of emigration on Velmar.*

- Remittances nearly tripled as a share of the economy (4% to 11%), suggesting that emigration has grown and that migrants send much of their income home. This supports household incomes and the balance of payments.
- Doctors per 10 000 fell by a third (6 to 4), suggesting the emigration of skilled health workers, a brain drain that weakens health care.
- The data show a trade-off: financial gains at household level against losses in public services. Dependence on remittances also makes the economy vulnerable to recession in destination countries.

## Annotated model answer

*Question: Assess the impacts of a refugee flow on the receiving area. [8]*

> When more than 700 000 Rohingya arrived in Cox's Bazar from 2017, housing and food were the first needs (**scale and timing**). Camps on cleared hillsides removed forest and raised landslide risk (**environmental impact**). Casual wages fell as refugees competed for informal work, harming poorer local households (**economic impact on one group**), while food prices rose for everyone. However, aid agencies brought jobs and spending, which benefited some local businesses and workers (**positive impact, different group**). The impacts were therefore mainly negative for the poorest local people in the short term, but less so for those who gained work in the aid economy (**judgement by group and time**).

## Common mistakes

- Using "refugee", "asylum seeker" and "migrant" interchangeably.
- Assuming most international migration is from LICs to HICs.
- Writing about remittances without saying who benefits and what the risks are.
- Describing impacts without saying whether they fall on the source or the destination.
- Giving only economic impacts when the question asks for impacts generally.
- Confusing 5.3 with 5.4. Section 5.4 is the single case study of one stream in depth; 5.3 needs general understanding supported by examples.

## Exam technique

- **Outline** causes: give the essential reasons briefly, with no extended detail.
- **Explain** a pattern on a map: say where flows go, then why (distance, networks, policy, history).
- **Assess** or **Evaluate** impacts: weigh positive against negative, distinguish groups, and end with a judgement.
- **Discuss** questions: give different viewpoints, such as those of employers, workers and governments.
