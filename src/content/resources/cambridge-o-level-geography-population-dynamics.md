This guide goes deeper into **sub-topic 1.1 Population Dynamics** of Cambridge O Level
Geography (2217), 2026 (Version 3). The
[Population and Settlement study guide](/resources/o-level-geography-population-and-settlement/)
sets out the whole of Theme 1; this page concentrates on one sub-topic and the reasoning
examiners expect you to show in it.

## What 1.1 asks you to do

Population Dynamics is about **why the number of people changes**, at world scale and in
individual countries. In practice you need to be able to:

- describe and explain the rapid growth of the world's population over the last two centuries;
- understand **over-population** and **under-population**, with their causes and consequences;
- identify the three components of population change: **births, deaths and migration**;
- explain why rates of natural change differ so much between countries;
- describe and **evaluate** population policies.

Expect to support these with named examples: a country that can be described as
over-populated, one that can be described as under-populated, and a country with a population
policy. The skill being tested is cause and effect, so every fact you learn should come with a
"because".

## Key idea 1: the arithmetic of change

All population change comes from three flows. Births add people, deaths remove them and
migration can do either.

| Term | Meaning | Usual unit |
|---|---|---|
| Birth rate | Live births in a year for every 1,000 people | per 1,000 per year |
| Death rate | Deaths in a year for every 1,000 people | per 1,000 per year |
| Natural change | Birth rate minus death rate | per 1,000 per year, or % |
| Net migration | Immigrants minus emigrants | per 1,000 per year |
| Infant mortality rate | Deaths of babies under one year for every 1,000 live births | per 1,000 live births |
| Life expectancy | Average number of years a newborn can expect to live | years |

Note the trap in the last two rows: infant mortality is measured per 1,000 **live births**, not
per 1,000 people. Also remember that natural change can be negative (natural decrease), as it is
in several European and East Asian countries today.

## Key idea 2: why the world's population grew so fast

For most of human history the population grew very slowly because high birth rates were
cancelled out by high death rates. The world reached roughly one billion people around 1800, but
growth then accelerated sharply: the United Nations estimated that the total passed seven billion
in 2011 and eight billion in November 2022.

The cause was a **fall in death rates that was not matched by an equal fall in birth rates**.
Death rates fell first because of:

- improved food supply and storage, so famines became less frequent;
- clean piped water and sewage disposal, which cut deaths from cholera and typhoid;
- vaccination, antibiotics and wider access to clinics;
- better knowledge of hygiene and nutrition, especially for mothers and babies.

Birth rates fall later because they depend on decisions inside families, which are shaped by
culture, religion, education and economics and change over a generation rather than a few years.
The gap between the two rates is what produces rapid growth. Today most growth is in low-income
countries, especially in sub-Saharan Africa, while many high-income countries are growing slowly
or shrinking.

## Key idea 3: contrasting rates of natural change

An explain question on this sub-topic usually wants factors grouped clearly. Use categories:

| Factor group | Pushes the birth rate up | Pushes the birth rate down |
|---|---|---|
| Economic | Children work on farms and support parents in old age | Children are costly to educate and house in cities |
| Social | Early marriage; low status of women | Women in education and paid work; later marriage |
| Health | High infant mortality, so more births "replace" losses | Low infant mortality and access to contraception |
| Cultural and religious | Beliefs that value large families | More secular attitudes, smaller family norms |
| Political | Pro-natalist incentives | Anti-natalist campaigns and limits |

Death rates differ for reasons of **healthcare, diet, water and sanitation, conflict and
disease**. Diseases such as HIV/AIDS have had a large effect on death rates and life expectancy
in parts of southern Africa, mainly among young adults, which also affects the structure of the
population (see [1.3 Population Structure](/resources/cambridge-o-level-geography-population-structure/)).
A useful refinement: a high-income country with an ageing population can have a *higher* crude
death rate than a low-income country, simply because a larger share of its people are elderly.

## Key idea 4: over-population and under-population

These terms compare **people with resources**, not people with area.

- **Over-population**: there are too many people for the resources and technology available to
  give everyone a decent standard of living. Signs include food shortages, unemployment,
  overcrowded housing and pressure on water, schools and health services.
- **Under-population**: there are too few people to make full use of the resources available, so
  the standard of living could rise if the population grew. Signs include labour shortages,
  unused land or minerals and difficulty supporting services in remote areas.
- **Optimum population**: the number that allows the highest standard of living with the
  resources and technology available.

A densely populated country is not automatically over-populated: the Netherlands is densely
populated but wealthy enough to import food and resources. **Bangladesh** is often used as an
example of over-population: a large population on a low-lying delta, with pressure on farmland
and frequent flooding that limits what the land can support. **Australia** is often used as an
example of under-population: a very large country rich in minerals and farmland with a
relatively small population, which is one reason it has long run planned immigration
programmes to attract skilled workers.

## Key idea 5: population policies

A population policy is a set of government measures designed to change the birth rate, the death
rate or migration.

- **Anti-natalist policies** aim to lower the birth rate. **China's one-child policy**, introduced
  around 1979–80, restricted most urban couples to one child, using incentives for compliance and
  penalties for breaking the rules. It was relaxed to a two-child policy from 2016 and a
  three-child policy in 2021.
- **Pro-natalist policies** aim to raise it, for example through child allowances, tax relief,
  paid parental leave and subsidised childcare. **Singapore** moved from discouraging large families
  in the 1970s to encouraging them from the late 1980s, which shows that a policy can be reversed
  when its effects overshoot.

To **evaluate** a policy, judge it against its aims and then weigh its side effects:

1. Did it change the birth rate? China's growth slowed, although the birth rate was already falling
   before the policy began, so not all of the fall can be credited to it.
2. What were the unintended consequences? In China these include a sex ratio with more boys than
   girls, a rapidly ageing population and the "4-2-1" family, in which one adult child may have to
   support two parents and four grandparents.
3. Was it acceptable? Coercive measures raised serious human rights concerns.
4. Overall judgement: effective at slowing growth, but at a social and demographic cost that
   later forced the government to reverse it.

## Worked example: calculating change

A country has a birth rate of 32 per 1,000, a death rate of 9 per 1,000 and net migration of
−2 per 1,000.

- Natural change = 32 − 9 = **23 per 1,000**, which is **2.3%** per year (divide by 10).
- Total change = 23 + (−2) = **21 per 1,000**, or **2.1%** per year.
- With a population of 40 million, the yearly increase is 40,000,000 × 21 ÷ 1,000 = **840,000
  people**.

An answer that stops at the natural change has missed the migration step. Figures in this example
are illustrative.

## Common mistakes

- Writing "birth rate is the number of babies born" without "per 1,000 people per year".
- Treating high density as proof of over-population.
- Saying the world's population grew because birth rates rose. Death rates fell; birth rates
  stayed high for a time.
- Describing China's policy without evaluating it, or evaluating it only as a success.
- Listing factors without explaining how each one changes the rate.

## Exam technique

- **Define** and **state**: one precise sentence with the unit.
- **Describe** a graph of world population: give the trend, the date when it changed, and figures
  read from the graph.
- **Explain**: link factor to result, for example "high infant mortality, so parents have more
  children to make sure some survive to look after them".
- **Suggest**: apply your knowledge to an unfamiliar country or data set; reasonable ideas get
  credit even if they are not in your notes.
- **Evaluate**, or "how successful": give successes, problems and a judgement.
- For a case study, **name the country** and add specific detail such as dates, measures and
  outcomes.
