These questions cover the **Circular flow of income** in Component 02, Macroeconomics, of the OCR *AS Level Economics (H060)* specification. All countries and data are invented. Revise first with the [circular flow study guide](/resources/ocr-as-level-economics-h060-circular-flow-of-income-ocr-aslevel-economics/) and the [circular flow revision notes](/resources/ocr-as-level-economics-h060-circular-flow-of-income-ocr-aslevel-economics-revision-notes/).

## Section A: Short questions

**1.** Which **one** of the following is an injection into the circular flow of income? **[1]**

A: Spending by UK households on imported cars

B: Income tax paid by workers

C: Spending by foreign tourists in the UK

D: Interest received on household savings accounts

**2.** Which **one** of the following would be excluded when national income is measured by the income method? **[1]**

A: Profits of a supermarket chain

B: Rent paid to a landlord

C: Salaries of nurses

D: Unemployment benefit paid by the state

**3.** In Country J, S = 70, T = 120, M = 90, I = 80, G = 130 and X = 60 (billion currency units). State whether national income will rise, fall or stay the same, and give the gap between injections and withdrawals. **[2]**

**4.** Explain why national output, national income and national expenditure are equal in the circular flow model. **[2]**

**5.** Withdrawals in Country K are 30% of national income and injections are 210 billion currency units. Calculate equilibrium national income. **[2]**

**6.** Explain why the output method of measuring national income uses value added rather than the value of sales. **[2]**

## Section B: Structured and extended questions

**7.** A pair of jeans is produced in four stages (invented data).

| Stage | Value of sales |
|---|---|
| Cotton grower | 6 |
| Fabric maker | 15 |
| Jeans manufacturer | 32 |
| Retailer (sale to consumer) | 50 |

**(a)** Calculate the value added at each stage. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain why the total value added equals the price paid by the consumer. **[2]**

**8.** The table shows national accounts data for Country L (billion currency units).

| Item | Value |
|---|---|
| Household consumption | 640 |
| Investment | 150 |
| Government spending on goods and services | 210 |
| State pensions and benefits | 120 |
| Exports | 280 |
| Imports | 310 |

**(a)** Calculate GDP using the expenditure method. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain why one item in the table should be left out of your calculation. **[2]**

**9.** In Country M, withdrawals are 25% of national income. Injections are 300 billion currency units.

**(a)** Calculate equilibrium national income. **[1]**

**(b)** Investment falls by 20 billion. Calculate the new equilibrium and the change in national income. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain why national income falls by more than the fall in investment. **[2]**

**10.** Explain **two** reasons why the output, income and expenditure measures of national income may give different totals in practice. **[4]**

**11.** Explain how a fall in the incomes of a country's main trading partners could affect its national income, using the circular flow model. **[4]**

**12.** Evaluate the usefulness of the circular flow of income model for understanding changes in national income. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** C [1]. Spending by foreigners on domestic output is an export, an injection. **[1]**

**2.** D [1]. A benefit is a transfer payment: no output is produced in return. **[1]**

**3.** W = 70 + 120 + 90 = 280; J = 80 + 130 + 60 = 270 [1]. W exceeds J by 10 billion, so national income will **fall** [1]. **[2]**

**4.** The value of output is paid out as factor incomes to those who produced it [1], and that income is spent on the output, so the three are the same flow measured at different points [1]. **[2]**

**5.** 0.3Y = 210 [1], so Y = **700 billion** [1]. **[2]**

**6.** Adding sales would count the value of inputs each time they are sold on [1]; value added counts each stage's contribution once, avoiding double counting [1]. **[2]**

**7. (a)** Cotton 6; fabric 15 - 6 = 9; manufacturer 32 - 15 = 17; retailer 50 - 32 = 18 [1]. Total = **50** [1].

**(b)** Each stage's value added is its sales minus the inputs it bought [1], so when the stages are added the bought-in inputs cancel out, leaving only the value of the final good, 50 [1]. **[4]**

**8. (a)** 640 + 150 + 210 + 280 - 310 [1] = **970 billion** [1].

**(b)** State pensions and benefits (120) are transfer payments [1]; they redistribute income but buy no output, so including them would overstate national expenditure [1]. **[4]**

**9. (a)** 0.25Y = 300, so Y = **1,200 billion** [1].

**(b)** J = 280, so Y = 280 / 0.25 = **1,120 billion** [1]. National income falls by **80 billion** [1].

**(c)** The fall in investment cuts the incomes of firms and workers in the capital goods sector [1]. They spend less, cutting other people's incomes in turn, and income keeps falling until withdrawals have fallen by 20 billion to match the new injections [1]. **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Show why the chain continues: each round of lost income cuts spending again.

**10.** Reason 1: the hidden economy [1]; cash-in-hand income is not declared, but some of the spending may be recorded, so the totals diverge [1]. Reason 2: different data sources [1]; output figures come from business surveys and income from tax records, each with its own errors and timing [1]. Other valid reasons: changes in stocks, timing of payments. **[4]**

**11.** Lower foreign incomes reduce demand for the country's exports [1], so injections fall [1]. With J now below W, firms selling abroad cut output and pay less income [1], and national income falls until withdrawals have fallen to match the lower level of injections [1]. **[4]**

**12.** Award up to 10 marks across knowledge, application, analysis and evaluation.
- The model shows that one person's spending is another's income, so a change in one sector spreads through the economy [1].
- It identifies the injections and withdrawals that drive changes in national income [1] and the direction of the change when J and W differ [1].
- It underpins national income accounting: the three measures follow from it [1].
- It helps explain policy: raising G or cutting T can offset a fall in exports or investment [1].
- Limitation: it shows money flows, not capacity, so it cannot say whether higher spending raises real output or prices [1].
- Limitation: equilibrium income can be below full employment, so the model alone gives no target [1].
- Limitation: it treats flows as separate when they interact, for example saving and investment through interest rates [1].
- Limitation: it ignores wealth, debt and confidence, which drive spending in practice [1].
- Judgement: useful as a framework for the direction of changes in national income, but it needs aggregate supply to explain real output and prices [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* Evaluation earns credit when you say what the model can and cannot tell a policymaker, not when you list more features.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Requiring S = I, T = G and X = M for equilibrium.
- Counting transfer payments in G or in factor incomes.
- Adding sales instead of value added.
- Forgetting to subtract imports.
- Saying national income changes by exactly the change in an injection.
- Treating a rise in nominal income as a rise in real output.
