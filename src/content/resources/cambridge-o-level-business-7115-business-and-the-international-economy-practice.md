These questions cover section 6.3 Business and the international economy of the Cambridge O Level Business Studies 7115 syllabus for examination in 2026. Exchange rate calculations are not assessed, so the exchange rate questions ask for reasoning only. All businesses and figures are invented, and money is in dollars. Revise first with the [international economy study guide](/resources/cambridge-o-level-business-7115-business-and-the-international-economy/) and the [international economy revision notes](/resources/cambridge-o-level-business-7115-business-and-the-international-economy-revision-notes/).

## Section A: Short-answer questions

**1.** Define the term "multinational company". **[2]**

**2.** Identify **two** reasons for the growth of globalisation. **[2]**

**3.** State what is meant by the depreciation of a currency. **[2]**

**4.** Explain **one** threat that globalisation creates for a small local clothing manufacturer. **[3]**

**5.** Explain why a government might use an import tariff to protect a new industry. **[3]**

**6.** A multinational moves some of its production from its home country to a country with lower costs. Identify **two** of its stakeholders and state how each might be affected. **[4]**

## Section B: Structured questions

**7.** Solano Bicycles makes bicycles locally and sells them for $210. Imported bicycles sell for $180. The government introduces a 20% import tariff on bicycles, and the importers pass all of it on to customers.

**(a)** Calculate the new price of an imported bicycle. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain how the tariff could benefit Solano Bicycles. **[3]**

**(c)** Solano imports the gears for each bicycle at a cost of $30. The tariff also applies to imported gears. Explain how this part of the tariff could harm Solano. **[3]**

**8.** Velantra Motors, a large multinational, opens a car factory in Mirovia employing 2,000 local workers. It imports most of its components and exports 60% of the cars it makes. Mirovia's only local car maker says it is losing sales to Velantra.

**(a)** Identify **two** benefits to Mirovia of the Velantra factory. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain **two** drawbacks to Mirovia of the Velantra factory. **[4]**

**(c)** Consider whether Mirovia's government should have offered grants to attract Velantra. Justify your answer. **[6]**

**9.** Amberly Teas exports most of its tea. Its home currency depreciates.

**(a)** Explain how the depreciation affects the price competitiveness of Amberly's tea abroad. **[3]**

**(b)** Explain **two** ways Amberly could respond to the depreciation. **[4]**

**10.** Petra Electronics imports mobile phones and sells them in its home country. The home currency appreciates. Explain the likely effect on Petra's profitability. **[4]**

**11.** Hollowell Furniture buys 70% of its timber from abroad. It sells 20% of its chairs abroad and the rest at home. Its home currency depreciates. Consider whether Hollowell Furniture is likely to be better off or worse off. Justify your answer. **[6]**

**12.** Consider whether globalisation is more of an opportunity than a threat for a small local business. Justify your answer. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** A business that has operations, such as factories, offices or shops [1], in more than one country [1]. **[2]**

*Examiner insight:* Selling abroad is not enough; an exporter is not a multinational unless it operates in other countries.

**2.** Any two: cheaper and faster transport [1]; the internet and better communications (or fewer trade barriers, growth of multinationals, rising incomes) [1]. **[2]**

**3.** A fall in the value of a currency [1], so that it buys less of other currencies [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Cheaper imported clothes from countries with lower costs can enter the market [1]. Customers may switch to these lower-priced imports [1], so the local manufacturer loses sales and may have to cut its prices, reducing profit or forcing it to close [1]. **[3]**

**5.** A new industry has high costs at first because it is small [1], so it cannot match the prices of established foreign producers [1]. A tariff raises the price of imports, giving the new industry time to grow and lower its costs until it can compete without protection [1]. **[3]**

**6.** Any two stakeholders, each with an effect (1 + 1 each). Shareholders [1]: may gain higher profits and dividends from lower costs [1]. Employees in the home country [1]: may lose their jobs as production moves abroad [1]. (Also credit: customers, lower prices; local suppliers, lost orders; managers, harder to control the business.) **[4]**

**7. (a)** Tariff = 20% × $180 = $36 [1]; new price = 180 + 36 = **$216** [1].

**(b)** Imported bicycles now cost $216, more than Solano's $210 [1]. Customers who chose imports because they were cheaper may switch to Solano [1], so its sales and revenue rise and jobs at the business are more secure [1].

**(c)** The gears now cost 30 × 1.2 = $36, an extra $6 per bicycle [1]. Solano's costs rise [1], so it must either raise its price, losing some of its new advantage over imports, or accept a lower profit on each bicycle [1]. **[8]**

*Examiner insight:* A tariff can help and harm the same business; the case tells you which parts of its activity are affected.

**8. (a)** Any two: 2,000 jobs for local workers [1]; exports that earn foreign currency (or investment in the factory, increased choice of cars, new skills) [1].

**(b)** Drawback 1: the local car maker is losing sales [1], which could lead to job losses there and reduce the gain in employment [1]. Drawback 2: Velantra imports most of its components [1], so few orders go to Mirovian suppliers and much of the money spent on production leaves the country (also credit: repatriation of profits; the risk that Velantra leaves) [1].

**(c)** For: grants attracted 2,000 jobs, and the workers' spending supports other businesses [1]; exports earn foreign currency [1]. Against: the grant is paid from taxes that could have funded services [1]; the local car maker is harmed and few local suppliers benefit [1]; Velantra could leave later, perhaps when the grant ends [1]. Judgement: the grant is justified if the jobs and exports last for many years and the government can encourage Velantra to buy more local components; otherwise the benefits may not be worth the cost [1]. **[12]**

**9. (a)** After the depreciation, foreign buyers need less of their own currency to pay the same home-currency price [1], so Amberly's tea becomes cheaper abroad [1] and more price competitive against tea from other countries [1].

**(b)** Response 1: keep its home-currency price the same so the foreign price falls [1], aiming to win more customers and increase sales volume [1]. Response 2: raise its home-currency price so the foreign price stays the same [1], earning a higher profit margin on each sale without needing to sell more [1]. **[7]**

*Examiner insight:* Both responses are valid; which suits Amberly depends on how price-sensitive its foreign customers are.

**10.** The appreciation means Petra needs less home currency to buy the same phones [1], so its costs fall [1]. If it keeps its selling prices the same, its profit margin on each phone rises [1]; alternatively it can cut prices to win customers from rivals, which may raise sales and total profit [1]. **[4]**

**11.** Worse off: 70% of its timber is imported, so the depreciation raises a large part of its costs [1]; 80% of its chairs are sold at home, where it gains no price advantage [1], so it must raise prices at home or accept lower profit margins [1]. Better off: its exported chairs become cheaper abroad and more competitive [1], so export sales may rise [1]. Judgement: Hollowell is more likely to be worse off, because the rise in timber costs affects most of its output while the export gain affects only 20% of sales, unless it can find home suppliers of timber or grow its exports [1]. **[6]**

*Examiner insight:* Use the percentages in the case to decide which effect is larger.

**12.** Opportunity: the internet lets a small business sell to customers in other countries [1], and it may buy cheaper or better materials from abroad [1]. Threat: larger foreign firms with lower costs may enter its market [1], and imported goods may take its customers [1]. Some small businesses are protected because they offer local services or personal contact that imports cannot replace [1]. Judgement: it depends on the business; a small firm with a distinctive product or a local service may gain more than it loses, while one making standard goods that compete with cheap imports is more likely to be threatened [1]. **[6]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Confusing tariffs (a tax) and quotas (a quantity limit).
- Mixing up appreciation and depreciation.
- Stating that an exchange rate change is "good" without saying whether the business exports or imports.
- Giving only benefits, or only drawbacks, of a multinational for the host country.
- Ignoring figures in the case, such as the share of inputs imported.
- Stopping an explanation before the effect on sales or profit.
