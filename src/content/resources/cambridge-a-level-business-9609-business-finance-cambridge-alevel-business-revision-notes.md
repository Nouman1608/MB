Condensed notes for **5.1 Business finance** (Cambridge International AS & A Level Business 9609, 2026–2028). Worked examples on growth and cash, the working capital cycle and early-payment discounts are in the [Business finance study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-business-9609-business-finance-cambridge-alevel-business/); exam-style questions are in the [Business finance practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-business-9609-business-finance-cambridge-alevel-business-practice/).

## What 5.1 asks you to handle

- **5.1.1**: finance to start up, grow and survive; short-term and long-term need; cash against profit; failure through bankruptcy, liquidation and administration.
- **5.1.2**: working capital; managing trade receivables and trade payables; capital against revenue expenditure.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Cash | Money the business holds and can spend now |
| Profit | Revenue minus costs for a period |
| Insolvent | Unable to pay debts as they fall due |
| Bankruptcy | Legal process for an individual who cannot pay debts |
| Liquidation | Closing a business and selling its assets to pay creditors |
| Administration | An administrator runs the company to try to rescue it or sell it as a going concern |
| Working capital | Current assets − current liabilities |
| Trade receivables | Money owed by customers who bought on credit |
| Trade payables | Money owed to suppliers |
| Capital expenditure | Spending on non-current assets used for more than a year |
| Revenue expenditure | Spending on day-to-day running costs |

## Why finance is needed

- **Start up**: premises, equipment, first inventory, costs before revenue.
- **Grow**: new sites, machinery, larger inventory, takeovers.
- **Survive**: slow seasons, bad debts, cost rises.

**Matching rule**: long-term need → long-term finance; short-term need → short-term finance.

## Cash or profit? Quick classifier

| Transaction | Profit now? | Cash now? |
|---|---|---|
| Credit sale | Yes | No |
| Inventory bought, unsold | No | Yes (out) |
| Loan received | No | Yes (in) |
| Loan repaid | No (only interest) | Yes (out) |
| Machine bought | Spread over life | Yes (out, large) |
| Owner's capital in | No | Yes (in) |

## Why growing firms run short of cash

Costs of extra output are paid now; revenue from extra sales arrives after the credit period. Gap = extra monthly costs × months of credit. Profit rises while cash falls.

## Failure outcomes

- **Bankruptcy** → individuals (sole traders, partners); personal assets at risk.
- **Liquidation** → business ends; assets sold for creditors.
- **Administration** → rescue attempt; creditors held back.

**Warning signs**: rising overdraft, suppliers demanding cash, late wages or taxes, slow-paying large customers.

## Working capital cycle

Cash → inventory → sales on credit → trade receivables → cash.

Gap (days) = inventory days + production days + customer credit days − supplier credit days.
Finance needed ≈ gap × daily costs.

## Too little against too much

- Too little → missed payments, lost discounts, insolvency risk.
- Too much → cash idle in inventory or receivables, not earning a return.
- Right level depends on the business: cash retailers need little; construction firms need much.

## Managing receivables and payables: trade-offs

| Action | Benefit | Risk |
|---|---|---|
| Shorter customer credit | Cash sooner | Lost sales |
| Early-payment discount | Cash sooner | Lower revenue |
| Credit checks | Fewer bad debts | Some sales lost |
| Debt factoring | Immediate cash | Fee |
| Pay suppliers later | Cash kept longer | Lost goodwill, stricter terms |

Discount test: compare the discount given with the interest saved on borrowing for the same period.

## Capital against revenue expenditure

- Capital: van, extension, machinery. Revenue: fuel, wages, rent, repairs.
- Matters for **financing** (long-term against regular income) and **profit measurement** (capital cost spread over years).
- Repair = revenue; improvement that adds capacity = capital.

## Exam traps

- Cash ≠ profit.
- Bankruptcy is for **individuals**; companies go into liquidation or administration.
- Administration does **not** always close the business.
- Overdraft for a building, or a 10-year loan for a 2-month gap: mismatched finance.
- Working capital is a **difference**, not just the cash figure.
- Longer customer credit can raise sales but costs cash.

## Self-test

1. Give one reason a business needs finance to survive.
2. Explain why an overdraft is unsuitable for buying a factory.
3. A firm sells $15 000 of goods on credit and pays $9 000 of costs in cash this month. What is the effect on profit and on cash?
4. Distinguish between liquidation and administration.
5. Can a private limited company be made bankrupt? Explain.
6. Current assets are $64 000 and current liabilities $41 000. Calculate working capital.
7. A firm's cycle is 25 days of inventory, 40 days of customer credit and 30 days of supplier credit (no production time). How many days must it finance?
8. Give one risk of having too much working capital.
9. Give one way to reduce trade receivables and its risk.
10. Classify: (a) new delivery van; (b) servicing the van.
11. Why might a profitable firm become insolvent?
12. Give one warning sign of financial difficulty.

**Answers**

1. For example, to pay wages during a slow season, or to cover a large bad debt.
2. An overdraft can be withdrawn at short notice and is meant for short-term needs, while a factory is used for many years.
3. Profit rises by $6 000; cash falls by $9 000 (the customer has not yet paid).
4. Liquidation closes the business and sells its assets to pay creditors; administration puts an administrator in control to try to rescue it or sell it as a going concern.
5. No; bankruptcy applies to individuals. A company that cannot pay its debts may go into liquidation or administration.
6. 64 000 − 41 000 = $23 000.
7. 25 + 40 − 30 = 35 days.
8. Cash is tied up in idle inventory or receivables instead of earning a return elsewhere.
9. For example, shorter credit terms; risk: customers may buy from rivals.
10. (a) Capital expenditure; (b) revenue expenditure.
11. Revenue may be recorded before cash arrives, so it cannot pay debts as they fall due, especially when growing fast.
12. For example, a growing overdraft or suppliers demanding cash on delivery.
