These notes condense section **3.1.4.1 Enthalpy change** of the AQA AS and A-level Chemistry specification (7404/7405), for teaching from September 2015. It is AS content, examined on Papers 1 and 2 (and Paper 3). The [Enthalpy change study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-enthalpy-change/) explains each idea with worked examples, and the [Enthalpy change practice questions](/resources/aqa-a-level-chemistry-7405-enthalpy-change-practice/) give exam-style practice.

## What the specification asks

- Reactions can be **endothermic** or **exothermic**.
- Enthalpy change (ΔH) is the **heat energy change measured under conditions of constant pressure**.
- **Standard conditions**: 100 kPa and a stated temperature (for example ΔH₂₉₈⦵).
- **Define** standard enthalpy of **combustion** (ΔcH⦵) and of **formation** (ΔfH⦵).

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Enthalpy change, ΔH | Heat energy change at constant pressure |
| Exothermic | Releases heat to the surroundings; ΔH negative |
| Endothermic | Takes in heat from the surroundings; ΔH positive |
| Standard conditions | 100 kPa and a stated temperature, usually 298 K |
| Standard state | Physical state of a substance at 100 kPa and the stated temperature |
| ΔfH⦵ | Standard enthalpy of formation |
| ΔcH⦵ | Standard enthalpy of combustion |

## Exothermic and endothermic

| | Exothermic | Endothermic |
|---|---|---|
| Heat | Released | Absorbed |
| Surroundings | Warm up | Cool down |
| ΔH | Negative | Positive |
| Profile | Products lower than reactants | Products higher than reactants |
| Examples | Combustion, neutralisation | Thermal decomposition of calcium carbonate, photosynthesis |

- ΔH = H(products) − H(reactants).
- The unit is **kJ mol⁻¹**, meaning per mole of reaction as written.
- Doubling the equation doubles ΔH. Reversing the equation changes the sign of ΔH.
- On a profile diagram, ΔH is the difference in height between reactants and products. The activation energy is the height of the hump above the reactants.

## Standard states of common elements

C(s, graphite), O₂(g), H₂(g), N₂(g), Cl₂(g), Br₂(l), I₂(s), S(s), Na(s), Mg(s). The enthalpy of formation of an element in its standard state is **zero**.

## The two definitions

**ΔfH⦵: standard enthalpy of formation.** The enthalpy change when **one mole of a compound** is formed from its **elements**, with all reactants and products in their **standard states**, under **standard conditions**.

**ΔcH⦵: standard enthalpy of combustion.** The enthalpy change when **one mole of a substance** is **completely burned in oxygen**, with all reactants and products in their **standard states**, under **standard conditions**.

## Writing the equations

| Quantity | Rule | Example |
|---|---|---|
| ΔfH⦵ | One mole of compound on the right; only elements on the left | 2C(s) + 3H₂(g) + ½O₂(g) → C₂H₅OH(l) |
| ΔfH⦵ | Use fractions if needed | ½N₂(g) + 1½H₂(g) → NH₃(g) |
| ΔcH⦵ | One mole of substance on the left; O₂ in excess; products CO₂(g) and H₂O(l) | C₂H₅OH(l) + 3O₂(g) → 2CO₂(g) + 3H₂O(l) |
| ΔcH⦵ | Fractions of O₂ are allowed | H₂(g) + ½O₂(g) → H₂O(l) |

Some equations serve as both: C(s) + O₂(g) → CO₂(g) is ΔfH⦵ of CO₂ and ΔcH⦵ of carbon (graphite).

## Calculations

Energy change = n × ΔH, where n = mass ÷ Mᵣ.

**Energy released.** Burning 2.30 g of ethanol (ΔcH⦵ = −1367 kJ mol⁻¹, Mᵣ = 46.0):
n = 0.0500 mol; energy released = 0.0500 × 1367 = **68.4 kJ**.

**Mass needed.** To release 1000 kJ from methane (ΔcH⦵ = −890 kJ mol⁻¹, Mᵣ = 16.0):
n = 1000 ÷ 890 = 1.124 mol; mass = 1.124 × 16.0 = **18.0 g**.

**Scaling.** 2H₂(g) + O₂(g) → 2H₂O(l): ΔH = 2 × (−286) = **−572 kJ mol⁻¹**.

**Reversing.** H₂O(l) → H₂(g) + ½O₂(g): ΔH = **+286 kJ mol⁻¹**.

## State symbols matter

| Quantity | Value / kJ mol⁻¹ |
|---|---|
| ΔfH⦵ H₂O(l) | −286 |
| ΔfH⦵ H₂O(g) | −242 |
| ΔfH⦵ CO₂(g) | −394 |
| ΔfH⦵ NO(g) | +90 |

The 44 kJ mol⁻¹ difference between liquid and gaseous water is the energy to vaporise one mole of water. Formation reactions can be endothermic (NO).

## Common mistakes

- Missing or wrong state symbols.
- Writing two moles of product in a formation equation, or two moles burned in a combustion equation.
- A compound on the left side of a formation equation.
- Writing O instead of O₂.
- A positive ΔH for an exothermic reaction.
- Leaving out "standard states", "complete" or "one mole" in a definition.
- Using ΔH for the activation energy on a diagram.
- Quoting kJ rather than kJ mol⁻¹.

## Self-test

1. Define enthalpy change of reaction in terms of pressure.
2. State the standard conditions of pressure and temperature used in enthalpy data.
3. Define the standard enthalpy of formation of a compound.
4. Define the standard enthalpy of combustion of a substance.
5. Write the equation for the standard enthalpy of formation of ethane, C₂H₆(g).
6. Write the equation for the standard enthalpy of combustion of propan-1-ol, C₃H₇OH(l).
7. State the standard enthalpy of formation of O₂(g).
8. Calculate the energy released when 0.250 mol of methane is burned (ΔcH⦵ = −890 kJ mol⁻¹).
9. The ΔH for 2NO(g) → N₂(g) + O₂(g) is −180 kJ mol⁻¹. What is the ΔH for N₂(g) + O₂(g) → 2NO(g)?
10. Explain why the temperature of the surroundings falls in an endothermic reaction.
11. Which equation is both a formation and combustion equation: C(s) + O₂(g) → CO₂(g) or 2H₂(g) + O₂(g) → 2H₂O(l)?
12. Why is it important to write state symbols in a ΔfH⦵ equation?

### Answers

1. The heat energy change measured at constant pressure.
2. 100 kPa and a stated temperature, normally 298 K.
3. The enthalpy change when one mole of a compound is formed from its elements, with all reactants and products in their standard states, under standard conditions.
4. The enthalpy change when one mole of a substance is completely burned in oxygen, with all reactants and products in their standard states, under standard conditions.
5. 2C(s) + 3H₂(g) → C₂H₆(g).
6. C₃H₇OH(l) + 4½O₂(g) → 3CO₂(g) + 4H₂O(l).
7. Zero.
8. 0.250 × 890 = **222.5 kJ** released (about 223 kJ).
9. **+180 kJ mol⁻¹** (the reverse reaction).
10. The reaction takes in heat energy from the surroundings, so the surroundings lose heat energy.
11. C(s) + O₂(g) → CO₂(g). It forms one mole of CO₂ from its elements and burns one mole of carbon. The other equation makes two moles.
12. The enthalpy depends on the physical state; for example, the value for H₂O(l) differs from that for H₂O(g).
