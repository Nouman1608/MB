Cells are the basic units of living things. For Cambridge O Level Biology
5090 you must identify the structures in animal, plant and bacterial cells
and state what each does, prepare and draw stained cells from a microscope
slide, explain how specialised cells suit their functions, use the terms
cell, tissue, organ, organ system and organism, and calculate magnification.

This guide covers Topic 1, Cells, in full — subtopics **1.1 Cell structure
and function** and **1.2 Specialised cells, tissues and organs** — for
Cambridge O Level Biology 5090, 2026–2028 series. For quick recall use the
[cell structure revision notes](/resources/cell-structure-and-organisation-revision-notes/),
then test yourself with the [cell structure practice questions](/resources/cell-structure-and-organisation-practice/).

## Which structures are found in animal, plant and bacterial cells?

| Structure | Animal cell | Plant cell | Bacterial cell |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cell membrane | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ |
| Cytoplasm | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ |
| Ribosomes | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ |
| Mitochondria | ✓ | ✓ | — |
| Nucleus | ✓ | ✓ | — (circular DNA + plasmids instead) |
| Cell wall | — | ✓ (cellulose) | ✓ |
| Chloroplasts | — | ✓ | — |
| Sap vacuole | — | ✓ | — |

Each structure has a specific function: the **cell membrane** controls what
enters and leaves the cell; **cytoplasm** is where many chemical reactions
occur; **ribosomes** are the site of protein synthesis; **mitochondria**
release energy through respiration; the **nucleus** contains DNA and
controls the cell's activities; the **cell wall** (plant and bacterial)
gives structural support and shape; **chloroplasts** are the site of
photosynthesis; the **sap vacuole** stores cell sap and helps maintain the
cell's turgor.

Bacterial cells differ fundamentally from animal and plant cells: they have
no true nucleus (their DNA is circular and free in the cytoplasm, plus
separate small circles of DNA called plasmids) and no mitochondria or
chloroplasts.

## How do you prepare and draw cells from a microscope slide?

Syllabus points 1.1.1 and 1.1.2 expect you to have made and drawn your own
slides.

**Animal cells (for example cheek cells).** Gently scrape the inside of the
cheek with a clean cotton bud, smear it on a glass slide, add a drop of
**methylene blue** stain and lower a cover slip at an angle with a mounted
needle so that no air bubbles are trapped. Methylene blue stains the
nucleus dark blue so it stands out against the paler cytoplasm.

**Plant cells (for example onion epidermis).** Peel a thin layer of
epidermis from the inner surface of an onion scale leaf, lay it flat on a
slide in a drop of **iodine solution**, and add a cover slip. The layer is
only one cell thick, so light passes through it. Iodine stains the nucleus
and any starch, and the cell walls show clearly. Onion epidermis cells have
no chloroplasts because they grow underground.

View the slide at low power first, then high power. When you draw:

- use a sharp pencil and clear, single, unbroken lines, with no shading;
- draw only what you see, at a sensible size, keeping the proportions;
- label with ruled lines that touch the structure, and add a title and
  the magnification.

## How do you calculate magnification?

Magnification relates the size of an image to the actual size of the
object it represents:

```
magnification = image size / actual size
```

**Worked example.** A photomicrograph shows a cell 40 mm long. Its actual
length is 0.02 mm. Find the magnification.

```
magnification = image size / actual size = 40 / 0.02 = 2000×
```

Rearranging this same equation lets you find actual size or image size
instead, given the other two quantities — always keep the units of image
size and actual size consistent before dividing.

```
actual size = image size / magnification
image size  = actual size x magnification
```

**Worked example (with a unit conversion).** A drawing of a palisade cell
is 60 mm long and the magnification is ×400. What is the actual length in
micrometres?

```
actual size = 60 / 400 = 0.15 mm
1 mm = 1000 µm, so 0.15 mm = 0.15 x 1000 = 150 µm
```

Measure the image with a ruler in millimetres, convert to micrometres only
at the end if the question asks for them, and give magnification with a ×
sign and no unit.

## How are cells specialised for their functions?

Cells become **specialised** — developing a structure suited to a specific
function — rather than remaining generic. A red blood cell, for instance,
is packed with haemoglobin and has no nucleus, maximising its
oxygen-carrying capacity; a root hair cell has a long, thin extension,
maximising surface area for water uptake.

Examples you meet later in the 5090 syllabus include:

| Specialised cell | Feature | How it helps |
|---|---|---|
| Red blood cell | No nucleus; contains haemoglobin | More room to carry oxygen |
| Root hair cell | Long, thin extension | Large surface area to absorb water and mineral ions |
| Palisade mesophyll cell | Many chloroplasts | Absorbs more light for photosynthesis |
| Guard cell | Pair surrounds a stoma and changes shape | Opens and closes the stoma to control gas exchange and water loss |
| Xylem vessel | Hollow, with no cross walls; lignified walls | Water flows through easily; walls are strong |
| Sperm cell | Flagellum and many mitochondria | Swims to the egg, using energy from respiration |

In every answer, state the feature **and** link it to the function with
"so" or "which means".

## What are tissues, organs and organ systems?

This specialisation builds into a hierarchy of organisation:

```
cell → tissue → organ → organ system → organism
```

A **tissue** is a group of similar cells performing the same function; an
**organ** is a structure made of several tissues working together for a
particular function; an **organ system** is a group of organs working
together; the **organism** is the complete individual.

For example, muscle cells form muscle tissue; muscle tissue, together with
other tissues, forms the heart (an organ); the heart and blood vessels form
the circulatory system (an organ system), which is one of the systems that
make up a human (the organism). In a plant, the leaf is an organ made of
epidermis, mesophyll and vascular tissue.

## Frequently asked questions

**Which structures do bacterial cells lack?**
A bacterial cell has no nucleus, no mitochondria and no chloroplasts. Its
DNA is a single circular molecule in the cytoplasm, with small extra rings
called plasmids.

**Why is methylene blue or iodine added to a slide?**
Most cell structures are colourless. A temporary stain colours structures
such as the nucleus so they can be seen and drawn.

**What is the difference between a tissue and an organ?**
A tissue is a group of similar cells with the same function. An organ is
made of several different tissues working together, such as the heart or
a leaf.

**Does magnification have a unit?**
No. It is a ratio, written with a × sign, for example ×2000. Image size
and actual size must be in the same unit before you divide.

## Common mistakes

- **Listing chloroplasts or a cell wall for an animal cell**, or vice
versa listing a nucleus for a bacterial cell — the structural differences
between the three cell types are a common and specific exam focus.
- **Confusing the cell membrane with the cell wall**, or the sap vacuole
with the cytoplasm, on a labelled diagram — know exactly which structure
each named term refers to.
- **Mixing up image size and actual size** in the magnification formula,
producing a wildly wrong answer — actual size is always the smaller
number for a magnified image.
- **Not converting units before calculating magnification** — image size
and actual size must be in the same units before dividing.
- **Treating "specialised" as just "different-looking"** rather than
explaining *how* the structure relates to the cell's specific function.
