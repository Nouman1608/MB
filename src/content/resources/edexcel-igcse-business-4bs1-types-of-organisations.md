This guide covers section 1.2, Types of organisations, in Topic 1 of the Pearson Edexcel International GCSE in Business (4BS1) specification, Issue 2, February 2026. The specification lists three points:

- 1.2.1 the main types of business ownership: sole trader, partnerships, limited companies (private and public), public corporations
- 1.2.2 characteristics relating to size: risk, ownership and limited liability; public corporations (reasons for and against public ownership); ownership, control, sources of finance, use of profits, stakeholders and shareholders; appropriateness of different forms of ownership
- 1.2.3 different forms of business organisation: franchises, social enterprises, multinationals.

Paper 1 sets questions on a small business and Paper 2 on a large one, so expect to judge which form suits the business in front of you. The first overview is in the [business objectives and types of organisation guide](/resources/edexcel-igcse-business-business-objectives-and-types-of-organisation/). Companion pages: the [types of organisations revision notes](/resources/edexcel-igcse-business-4bs1-types-of-organisations-revision-notes/) and the [types of organisations practice questions](/resources/edexcel-igcse-business-4bs1-types-of-organisations-practice/). Businesses and figures are invented; amounts are in dollars. The specification names the forms without defining them, so the descriptions below are standard meanings.

## 1. One business, four forms

The clearest way to see the differences is to follow one business as it grows.

| Stage | Form | What changes |
|---|---|---|
| Nadia starts selling handmade soap | Sole trader | She owns it, controls it, keeps all profit, and has unlimited liability |
| She brings in her cousin, who has savings and sales skills | Partnership | Two owners share capital, decisions and profit; both still have unlimited liability |
| They need a workshop and want to protect their homes | Private limited company | Owners hold shares; liability is limited; shares are not sold to the public |
| Years later they need millions to build a factory | Public limited company | Shares can be bought by the general public; directors run it for many shareholders |

At each step the business gains access to more finance and lowers the personal risk of its owners, but the original owners give up some control and privacy.

## 2. Risk, ownership and limited liability

**Risk** is the chance of losing money. **Unlimited liability** means owners can be made to pay the business's debts from their personal assets. **Limited liability** means shareholders can lose only what they invested.

### Worked example 1: who pays the debts?

A business closes owing 180,000. Selling its assets raises 110,000, leaving 70,000 unpaid.

- **Partnership of two:** the partners can be made to pay the 70,000 from personal savings or property.
- **Private limited company:** the shareholders lose the money they put in, but the 70,000 is not taken from their personal assets.

Limited liability is why investors are more willing to buy shares, and why a growing business often becomes a company.

## 3. Ownership and control

In a sole trader business the owner is also the manager. In a company, shareholders **own** the business and elect directors who **control** it day to day. Votes usually follow shares, so whoever holds more than half the shares can usually control decisions.

### Worked example 2: selling shares and keeping control

The Okafor family owns 60,000 of the 100,000 shares in Okafor Tools Ltd, which is 60%. To raise finance, the company plans to issue 30,000 new shares to outside investors.

- New total: 100,000 + 30,000 = 130,000 shares.
- Family share: 60,000 ÷ 130,000 × 100 = **46.2%**.

The family would lose its majority. Issuing 20,000 new shares instead leaves it with 60,000 ÷ 120,000 = 50%, which is no longer more than half either, so to stay in control the family could issue at most 19,999. Questions on becoming a company often turn on this trade-off between finance and control.

## 4. Sources of finance and use of profits

| Form | Typical sources of finance | Use of profits |
|---|---|---|
| Sole trader | Owner's savings, retained profit, bank loans | All belongs to the owner |
| Partnership | Partners' capital, retained profit, loans | Shared as the partners agree |
| Private limited company | Shares sold to chosen people, retained profit, loans | Dividends to shareholders and retained profit |
| Public limited company | Shares sold to the public, retained profit, loans | Dividends and retained profit |
| Public corporation | Government funding, revenue from customers, borrowing | Kept for the service or returned to government |

**Stakeholders** are all groups with an interest in a business: owners, employees, customers, suppliers, lenders, the community and the government. **Shareholders** are the stakeholders who own shares. A sole trader has no shareholders but still has stakeholders.

## 5. Public corporations: for and against

A public corporation is owned by the state. The specification asks for reasons for and against public ownership; these are standard arguments.

| For public ownership | Against public ownership |
|---|---|
| Essential services can be provided where they would not make a profit, such as remote bus routes | Without owners seeking profit, there is less pressure to cut waste |
| Prices can be kept affordable | Losses may be paid for by taxpayers |
| Decisions can put the public interest first | Decisions may follow political aims rather than commercial ones |

Use the case: a service that is essential but unprofitable makes the "for" side stronger; a service that private firms already provide well makes the "against" side stronger.

## 6. Franchises, social enterprises and multinationals (1.2.3)

**Franchise.** A franchisor allows a franchisee to trade under its brand, using its methods, in return for fees.

### Worked example 3: the franchise from both sides

A franchisee pays a start-up fee of 25,000 and a yearly fee of 5% of revenue. The outlet expects revenue of 260,000 and other costs of 190,000.

- Yearly fee: 5% × 260,000 = 13,000.
- Franchisee's profit: 260,000 − 190,000 − 13,000 = **57,000** a year.
- The franchisor has 40 outlets of this size, so it receives 40 × 13,000 = **520,000** a year in fees, without paying for the outlets itself.

The franchisee gains a known brand and support but pays fees and follows rules. The franchisor grows quickly using franchisees' money, but its reputation depends on outlets it does not run.

**Social enterprise.** A business with social or environmental objectives that earns its income by trading. If one makes a profit of 30,000 and puts 80% into its purpose, 24,000 goes to the cause and 6,000 is kept for the business.

**Multinational.** A business with operations in more than one country. Section 1.5 covers why businesses become multinationals and the effects on host countries.

## 7. Choosing an appropriate form

Work from the facts of the case:

1. How much finance is needed? Small sums suit a sole trader; large sums suit a company.
2. How much risk will the owners take? Owners who want to protect personal assets need limited liability.
3. How much control do they want? Selling shares widely dilutes control.
4. How fast do they want to grow, and how? A franchise can grow without owning every outlet.
5. What is the purpose? A social purpose suggests a social enterprise.

### Annotated "justify" answer

*Nadia and her cousin should form a private limited company rather than stay a partnership.* (Decision first.) *They need 80,000 for a workshop, and limited liability would protect their homes if the business failed* (a fact from the case, linked to a feature). *They could sell shares to friends and family without losing control to the public* (finance and control). *A partnership would keep things simpler and private, but with a large loan the personal risk is too high* (why the other option is weaker).

## Common mistakes

- Confusing a public limited company (owned by shareholders) with a public corporation (owned by the state).
- Saying limited liability means a business cannot fail.
- Treating "stakeholder" and "shareholder" as the same.
- Saying partners have limited liability; in an ordinary partnership they do not.
- Recommending a form without using the case facts on finance, risk and control.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why does a business become a private limited company?**
Usually to give its owners limited liability and to raise more finance by selling shares to chosen people, while keeping shares off the public market.

**What is the difference between a franchisor and a franchisee?**
The franchisor owns the brand and methods; the franchisee pays fees to run an outlet using them.
