International trade is the buying and selling of goods and services between countries. Sub-topic 3.1 of Cambridge O Level Commerce (7100) has four sections: the **benefits of international trade to a country** (3.1.1), the **challenges to exporters and importers** (3.1.2), the **Balance of Trade and Balance of Payments** (3.1.3), and **free trade, trading blocs, trade restrictions and sources of support for exporters** (3.1.4). Exam questions often combine a short calculation with a developed explanation.

An **export** is a good or service sold to another country. An **import** is a good or service bought from another country.

## 3.1.1 Benefits of international trade to a country

| Benefit | Explanation | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Wider choice of goods | Consumers can buy goods that are not made at home or cannot be grown in the local climate | A cold country buying tropical fruit |
| Higher standard of living | People can enjoy more goods and services, and often better quality | Access to modern medical equipment |
| Lower cost of imports | Goods from countries that can make them cheaply cost less than home-made ones | Cheaper clothing from a country with low production costs |
| More investment | Foreign firms may build factories or invest in the country | A foreign car maker opens a plant |
| Creation of jobs | Exporting firms and foreign investment employ more workers | Dock workers, factory staff and drivers |

## 3.1.2 Challenges for exporters and importers

| Challenge | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Distance | Long journeys take time, cost more and goods may be damaged |
| Language | Misunderstandings in contracts, labels and instructions |
| Methods of payment | The seller wants to be sure of payment; the buyer wants to be sure of delivery |
| Documentation | Many documents are needed, and mistakes cause delays |
| Transport | Several types of transport may be needed, with cost and risk of loss |
| Customs duties | Taxes on goods entering a country raise their price |
| Non-payment | A foreign buyer may not pay, and it is harder to take legal action abroad |
| Currencies | Payments may need to be changed into another currency |
| Foreign exchange rates | The rate at which one currency is changed into another can move, altering the cost or the income |
| Competition | Foreign producers compete with local firms, and exporters meet local competitors abroad |
| Different legal systems | Laws on contracts, safety and labelling differ from country to country |

### Worked example: a change in the exchange rate

A UK importer must pay a foreign supplier 12,000 units of the supplier's currency. At the start, 1 pound buys 2.0 units, so the cost is 12,000 ÷ 2.0 = 6,000 pounds. Later the exchange rate falls to 1 pound buys 1.5 units. The cost is now 12,000 ÷ 1.5 = 8,000 pounds. The importer pays 2,000 pounds more for exactly the same goods. For an exporter being paid in a foreign currency the effect is the opposite way: a weaker pound would mean the exporter receives more pounds for each unit of foreign currency.

## 3.1.3 Balance of Trade and Balance of Payments

**Balance of Trade** is the difference between the value of a country's exports of **goods** and its imports of goods. It is called **visible trade**.

> Balance of Trade = value of exports of goods − value of imports of goods

**Balance of Payments** is a record of **all** the money flowing into and out of a country, including goods, services (such as tourism, banking and insurance, called **invisible trade**), and income and investment flows.

A **surplus** means more money flowing in than out. A **deficit** means more flowing out than in.

### Worked example ($ million)

| Item | Value |
|---|---|
| Exports of goods | 340 |
| Imports of goods | 395 |
| Exports of services | 210 |
| Imports of services | 150 |
| Net income and investment flowing in | 20 |

1. Balance of Trade = 340 − 395 = **−55**. This is a deficit.
2. Balance on services = 210 − 150 = **+60**.
3. Balance of Payments (on these items) = −55 + 60 + 20 = **+25**. This is a surplus.

**Interpretation:** the country buys more goods than it sells, but earnings from services such as tourism and the income from abroad more than make up for it.

### How commercial activities affect the balances

- A manufacturer winning export orders raises exports of goods, improving the Balance of Trade.
- A hotel chain attracting foreign tourists earns foreign currency, improving the Balance of Payments (invisible exports).
- A retailer importing more foreign goods increases imports and worsens the Balance of Trade.
- A bank or insurance firm selling services abroad improves the Balance of Payments.

## 3.1.4 Free trade, trading blocs and restrictions

### Free trade

**Free trade** is trade between countries without barriers such as tariffs, quotas or other restrictions.

### Trading blocs

A **trading bloc** is a group of countries that agree to trade with each other on special terms.

Features: member countries reduce or remove tariffs and quotas on trade between them; they often share common rules and sometimes put a common tariff on goods from outside the bloc.

| Advantages of joining | Disadvantages of joining |
|---|---|
| A larger market for exports with no or lower tariffs | Must follow the bloc's rules, so less freedom to make own trade decisions |
| Cheaper imports from members | Local firms may face more competition from members |
| Attracts foreign investment | May lose trade with countries outside the bloc because of the bloc's common tariff |
| Cooperation on rules and standards makes trade easier | Weaker members may be harmed by stronger ones |

### Trade restrictions

| Restriction | Meaning | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Tariff** | A tax on imported goods | Raises the price of imports; raises money for the government |
| **Quota** | A limit on the quantity of a good that can be imported | Reduces supply from abroad; may raise price |
| **Embargo** | A ban on trade with a country or in certain goods | Stops trade completely |

**Worked example of a tariff.** An imported phone costs the importer $400. The country puts a 20% tariff on imported phones. Tariff = 20% × 400 = $80, so the cost becomes $480. The retailer must either raise its price or accept less profit. The home producer of phones now has an advantage because the imported phone is dearer.

**Effects on commercial activities:** import costs rise, so retailers and wholesalers may raise prices; home producers may sell more; exporters may find that other countries respond with restrictions of their own; an embargo can stop an exporter's whole market.

### Sources of support for exporters

| Support | How it helps |
|---|---|
| **International trade fairs** | Exporters show products to buyers from many countries and meet possible customers |
| **Cheaper bank loans** | Lower interest helps exporters pay for materials and production before they are paid |
| **Insurance guarantees** | Protect the exporter if a foreign buyer does not pay |

## Worked example: a case from start to finish

*Invented example.* Sunrise Textiles is a small clothing manufacturer that wants to sell shirts abroad.

1. **Benefit to the country:** if Sunrise wins export orders, the country gains export earnings and Sunrise can employ more workers.
2. **Challenges:** the buyer speaks another language, so contracts and labels must be translated; the shirts travel a long way, so transport costs and delays are likely; the buyer pays in another currency, so a movement in the exchange rate may reduce Sunrise's income; and the buyer's country charges customs duties, which makes the shirts dearer.
3. **Support:** Sunrise can show its shirts at an international trade fair, borrow from the bank at a lower rate to pay for materials, and take out an insurance guarantee in case the buyer does not pay.
4. **Effect on the balance:** the shirts are visible exports, so each sale improves the Balance of Trade.
5. **Risk of restrictions:** if the buyer's government introduces a quota or an embargo, Sunrise's sales would fall or stop, so it is wise to sell to several countries.

## Annotated model answer

**Question:** Explain **two** challenges faced by an exporter selling goods to a foreign country. [4]

> One challenge is non-payment [1]. A foreign buyer may not pay, and it is harder and more costly to take legal action in another country [1]. A second challenge is foreign exchange rates [1]. If the buyer pays in a foreign currency and the exchange rate moves against the exporter, the exporter receives less money than expected [1].

**Why it scores 4:** each challenge is named and then developed with the reason it matters for an exporter.

## Common mistakes

- Defining the Balance of Trade as "all trade". It covers goods only.
- Getting the subtraction the wrong way round. Exports minus imports.
- Writing "a surplus is good and a deficit is bad" without interpretation.
- Confusing a tariff (a tax) with a quota (a limit).
- Saying a trading bloc has no rules.
- Listing challenges without saying why each matters.

## Exam technique

- **Calculate**: write the formula, put in the numbers, give the answer with the word surplus or deficit.
- **Explain**: name the point, then give the effect on the exporter, importer or country.
- **Discuss**: give one side, the other side, then a decision.
