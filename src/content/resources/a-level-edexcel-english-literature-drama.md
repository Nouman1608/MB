This guide covers **Unit 2: Drama**, the second of two units forming
the International Advanced Subsidiary (IAS) half of Pearson Edexcel
International A-Level English Literature (YET01). Where Unit 1 pairs
post-2000 poetry with a post-2000 prose text, Unit 2 moves into drama
and spans a much wider historical range, pairing one text written
before 1900 with one written after 1900.

## Where this fits in YET01

Unit 2 (unit code WET02/01) is an open-book written exam lasting 2
hours, worth 50 marks: 50% of the IAS grade, or 25% of the full
International Advanced Level once combined with the A2 units (Units 3
and 4). It is examined in January and June sessions and is available
as either a paper-based or onscreen assessment.

## Syllabus coverage

**PEARSON EDEXCEL INTERNATIONAL A-LEVEL ENGLISH LITERATURE (YET01) — UNIT 2: DRAMA**

**Section A: Pre-1900 Drama** — candidates study **one** text, chosen
by their centre from a list of five: Christopher Marlowe's *Doctor
Faustus* (the A text, as in the New Mermaids edition), William
Shakespeare's *Othello*, Aphra Behn's *The Rover*, Oliver Goldsmith's
*She Stoops to Conquer*, or William Shakespeare's *Twelfth Night*.
Candidates answer one essay question, chosen from a choice of two, on
their set text. This section is assessed against four assessment
objectives: AO1 (informed personal response, using appropriate
terminology), AO2 (analysing the ways in which meanings are shaped in
the text -- through language, form and structure among other methods,
not a fixed checklist), AO3 (understanding of context) and AO5
(exploring different interpretations), worth 25 marks.

**Section B: Post-1900 Drama** — candidates study **one** text, chosen
from a different list of five: Lorraine Hansberry's *A Raisin in the
Sun*, Tennessee Williams's *A Streetcar Named Desire*, Arthur Miller's
*Death of a Salesman*, Caryl Churchill's *Top Girls*, or Samuel
Beckett's *Waiting for Godot*. As with Section A, candidates answer one
essay question from a choice of two, assessed against the same four
objectives (AO1, AO2, AO3 and AO5), also worth 25 marks.

Because both sections are assessed identically -- one essay question
from a choice of two, against the same four assessment objectives -- the
skills developed for one section transfer directly to the other; the
main variable is the historical distance between the two texts and what
that means for contextual knowledge.

## How to approach it

Since the exam is open book, the value of memorising long quotations is
lower than knowing your text well enough to locate and deploy the right
evidence quickly. Because AO3 (context) and AO5 (interpretations) both
carry weight in every answer, build revision notes that go beyond plot
and character to cover the circumstances in which each play was written
and first performed, and how critical or theatrical interpretations of
it have shifted over time -- for the pre-1900 text especially, this
often means engaging with the conventions of its original genre (for
example, Restoration comedy -- specifically comedy of intrigue,
turning on carnival and misrule, disguise, costume and the discomfort
of its closing marriages -- for *The Rover*, or morality-play structure
for *Doctor Faustus*). For the post-1900 text, AO3 more often means
situating the play within its immediate social or political moment --
post-war America for *A Streetcar Named Desire* (1947) and *Death of a
Salesman* (1949), for instance, or 1980s Britain for *Top Girls*. The
McCarthy era (1950-1954) postdates both American plays, so it cannot be
their immediate context of writing, though the reception of both plays
during the McCarthy years is legitimate material if treated separately
as reception history rather than as the moment either play was written
into. Since drama
is written to be performed, practising analysis of staging, stage
directions and dramatic form (entrances, exits, asides, silence) tends
to strengthen AO2 responses more than treating the text purely as prose
to be read.

## The question format

Both sections give candidates a statement to respond to, rather than an
open "explore how..." prompt with no stem -- the specification states
this for both sections, and says explicitly that the statement given in
each question is the mechanism for assessing AO5: it is the invitation
to demonstrate that the text can be read in different ways. Each
question also instructs candidates to consider relevant contextual
factors, tying the statement format directly to AO3. Practising against
statement-based prompts, not generic explore-how questions, is what
prepares you for the actual mechanism the exam uses to credit AO5.

## Worked example: using AO5 on a single text

A common weakness in Unit 2 answers is naming AO5 without actually using
it. Take *Death of a Salesman* as an example: a response that argues
only "Willy Loman is a tragic figure destroyed by the American Dream"
answers AO1 and AO2 but leaves AO5 untouched. A stronger response
acknowledges that critics and directors have staged and read the play
differently -- some productions emphasise Willy as a sympathetic victim
of economic forces beyond his control, while others foreground his own
self-deception and complicity in his downfall, and post-war American
audiences may have responded to the play's critique of capitalism very
differently from audiences watching it today. Naming this range of
readings, and then arguing for your own position within it, is what
distinguishes an AO5-aware answer from one that simply asserts a single
fixed meaning. The same approach applies to the pre-1900 text: for
*Othello*, for instance, readings of Iago's motivation have shifted
across different centuries and critical schools, from morality-play
villain to psychologically complex character, and a strong essay can
draw on this range rather than presenting only one fixed interpretation
without acknowledgement.

## Common mistakes

Treating the pre-1900 and post-1900 texts as needing entirely different
essay-writing approaches, when both sections are assessed against the
same four objectives in the same format. Under-using AO5 by presenting
only one reading of the play rather than acknowledging how
interpretations have differed -- across productions, critics, or time
periods. Discussing a play only as if it were a novel, ignoring the
performance and staging choices that are central to how meaning is made
in drama. Bringing an annotated copy of the set text into the open-book
exam, which is not permitted.

## Frequently asked questions

**How long is YET01 Unit 2?**
2 hours for 50 marks, open book: 50% of the International AS and 25% of the International A Level.

**What are the two sections?**
Section A Pre-1900 Drama and Section B Post-1900 Drama, 25 marks each, both assessing AO1, AO2, AO3 and AO5.

**What is AO5?**
Exploring literary texts informed by different interpretations.


## Quick revision checklist

- Confirm which pre-1900 text and which post-1900 text your centre has
chosen for Unit 2.
- Build contextual notes for each text -- the pre-1900 text's original
genre conventions, and the post-1900 text's immediate social or
political context.
- Practise applying AO5 by identifying at least two differing
interpretations of your set text.
- Analyse staging and dramatic form (stage directions, entrances and
exits, silence) rather than treating the play purely as prose.
- Bring a clean, unannotated copy of your set text to the open-book
exam.
