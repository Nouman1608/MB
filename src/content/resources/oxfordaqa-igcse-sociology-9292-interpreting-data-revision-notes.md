**These notes cover 3.1.3.4 Interpretation of data** in OxfordAQA International GCSE Sociology
(9292): "How to interpret data from quantitative sources, including tables, charts and graphs" and
"How to interpret data from qualitative sources, including extracts from field notes and unstructured
interviews". They condense the [interpreting data study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-sociology-9292-interpreting-data/)
and expand the data routine in the [research methods revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-sociology-9292-research-methods-revision-notes/).
Data questions appear in Section C of both papers; Paper 1 Section C uses "data about an unseen issue
of global sociological significance".

## Before any calculation

- **Title:** what, where, when.
- **Units:** numbers, %, thousands, per 1,000.
- **Categories:** rows, columns, axes, key.
- **Source and date.**

## Key calculations

| Task | Method | Example (invented) |
|---|---|---|
| Difference | Larger − smaller, with units | 60% − 20% = 40 percentage points |
| Percentage change | Change ÷ starting value × 100 | (60 − 20) ÷ 20 × 100 = 200% |
| Rate per 1,000 | Number ÷ population × 1,000 | 900 ÷ 20,000 × 1,000 = 45 per 1,000 |
| Share of a total | Part ÷ total × 100 | 70 ÷ 200 × 100 = 35% |
| Number from a percentage | Percentage ÷ 100 × total | 35% of 200 = 70 people |
| "Times larger" | Larger ÷ smaller | 14,000 ÷ 2,800 = 5 times |

**Percentage points vs per cent:** a rise from 70% to 91% is **21 percentage points** but a **30%**
increase (21 ÷ 70 × 100).

## Describing data

- **Trend:** the overall direction ("rose steadily", "fell, then levelled off").
- **Quantify:** quote the start and end values with units.
- **Compare:** the gap between groups, and whether it widened or narrowed.
- **Exceptions:** a year or group that does not fit the pattern.

## Reading charts

| Chart | Best for | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| Table | Exact figures | Units in the heading |
| Bar chart | Comparing categories | Read against the scale |
| Line graph | Change over time | Axes not starting at zero |
| Pie chart | Shares of a whole | It does not show the total number |

## Limitations to name

- Does not show **causes** or **meanings**.
- **Sample** size and representativeness.
- **Definitions** and changes in measurement.
- **Source** and purpose; recording practices in official statistics.
- **Time span** too short.
- **Correlation is not causation.**

Always tie the limitation to the data in front of you.

## Qualitative extracts

| Step | What to do |
|---|---|
| Themes | Identify two or three ideas that recur |
| Evidence | Quote a short phrase for each |
| Meaning | Explain what it shows about people's experiences or understandings |
| Research process | Consider the observer's or interviewer's effect (Hawthorne effect, interviewer bias) |
| Limits | One setting, one group; the researcher's interpretation; missing voices |

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Quantitative data | Numerical data |
| Qualitative data | Data in words or images, showing meanings and experiences |
| Percentage point | The unit of a simple difference between two percentages |
| Rate | A number expressed relative to population size, such as per 1,000 |
| Trend | The overall direction of change over time |
| Correlation | A relationship in which two variables change together |
| Causation | One variable directly bringing about a change in another |
| Field notes | A researcher's written record of observation |

## Exam traps

- Percentage points and percentage change confused.
- Raw numbers compared across populations of different sizes.
- No figures or units in a description.
- Chart read by eye, not by scale.
- Generic limitation unrelated to the data.
- A single qualitative extract treated as representative.
- Correlation presented as proof.

## Self-test

1. What four things should you read before looking at any figure?
2. What is the difference between a percentage-point change and a percentage change?
3. A figure rises from 40% to 50%. State the change in percentage points and as a percentage.
4. Town C has 600 burglaries and 30,000 people. Calculate the rate per 1,000.
5. Why can raw numbers mislead when comparing places?
6. What does a pie chart not show?
7. Why should you check where the vertical axis of a graph starts?
8. Give three limitations that might apply to a table of official statistics.
9. What is the difference between correlation and causation?
10. Name four steps for interpreting a qualitative extract.
11. How might an observer's presence affect field notes?
12. In 200 survey responses, 15% chose "newspapers". How many people is that?

**Answers:** 1. The title, the units, the categories and the source and date. 2. A percentage-point
change is the simple difference between two percentages; a percentage change compares the change with
the starting value. 3. 10 percentage points; a 25% increase (10 ÷ 40 × 100). 4. 600 ÷ 30,000 × 1,000 =
20 per 1,000. 5. Because populations differ in size, so a larger place may have more incidents but a
lower rate. 6. The total number in each category. 7. A scale that does not start at zero can make
small differences look large. 8. Any three: it does not show causes; it depends on what is reported
and recorded; definitions may have changed; it covers a limited time span; it may exclude some groups.
9. Correlation means two variables change together; causation means one brings about the change in
the other. 10. Identify themes; quote evidence; interpret meanings; consider the research process and
limitations. 11. People may change their behaviour because they know they are being watched (the
Hawthorne effect). 12. 15 ÷ 100 × 200 = 30 people.
