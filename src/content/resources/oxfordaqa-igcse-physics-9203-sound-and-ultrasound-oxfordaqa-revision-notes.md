Condensed recall for **3.3.3 Sound and ultrasound** in OxfordAQA International GCSE Physics (9203), for exams May/June 2018 onwards. Statements d to h are P content. Explanations and worked examples are in the [Sound and ultrasound study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-physics-9203-sound-and-ultrasound-oxfordaqa/); exam-style questions are in the [Sound and ultrasound practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-igcse-physics-9203-sound-and-ultrasound-oxfordaqa-practice/).

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Sound wave | A longitudinal wave that causes vibrations in a medium, detected as sound |
| Medium | The material a wave travels through (solid, liquid or gas) |
| Compression | Region where the medium's particles are pushed together |
| Rarefaction | Region where the particles are spread apart |
| Range of human hearing | About 20 Hz to 20 000 Hz |
| Pitch | How high or low a sound is; set by the frequency of the source |
| Loudness | Related to the amplitude of the wave |
| Echo | A reflected sound wave |
| Ultrasound | Sound with a frequency above the human hearing range (above about 20 000 Hz) |
| Interface (boundary) | The surface between two different media |
| Partial reflection | Part of a wave is reflected at a boundary and the rest is transmitted |
| Time base | The oscilloscope setting giving the time represented by each horizontal division |

## Equations

```
s = v x t        distance (m) = speed (m/s) x time (s)
echo depth = v x (echo time) / 2
v = f x λ
f = 1 / T
```

## Condensed recall

**Sound (a).** Longitudinal; needs a medium; cannot travel through a vacuum. Hearing range about 20 Hz to 20 kHz.

**Pitch and loudness (b).**
- Higher frequency → higher pitch.
- Larger amplitude → louder.

**Reflection and diffraction (c).** Echoes are reflections. Sound diffracts round doorways and corners because its wavelengths (centimetres to metres) are comparable to these gaps.

**Ultrasound (d, e).** Frequency above 20 kHz; produced electronically by a transducer driven by an electrical signal.

**Ranging (f, g).**
1. Send a pulse.
2. Part is reflected at each boundary.
3. Time each echo.
4. Distance to boundary = speed × time ÷ 2.

**Medical uses (h).**
- Prenatal scanning: echoes from tissue boundaries build an image; non-ionising, so safe for the fetus.
- Kidney stones: high-energy ultrasound shatters the stone into small pieces that pass out naturally; no cut needed.

## Reading an oscilloscope trace

| Measurement | Method |
|---|---|
| Period | Divisions for one wave × time base |
| Frequency | 1 ÷ period |
| Echo time | Divisions from transmitted pulse to echo × time base |
| Relative loudness | Compare peak heights at the same vertical setting |

## Time unit conversions

| Unit | In seconds |
|---|---|
| 1 ms | 1 × 10⁻³ s |
| 1 μs | 1 × 10⁻⁶ s |

## Wavelengths of sound in air (340 m/s)

| Frequency | Wavelength |
|---|---|
| 20 Hz (lowest audible) | 17 m |
| 340 Hz | 1.0 m |
| 3400 Hz | 0.10 m |
| 20 000 Hz (highest audible) | 0.017 m |

These are comparable to doorways and rooms, which is why audible sound diffracts noticeably.

## Echo calculation steps

1. Read or calculate the total echo time (divisions × time base, for a trace).
2. Convert to seconds.
3. Total path = speed × time.
4. Distance to the boundary = total path ÷ 2.
5. Thickness of a layer = deeper boundary depth − shallower boundary depth.

## Comparing two traces

- Check the time base and vertical settings are the same for both traces.
- More waves across the screen means a shorter period, a higher frequency and a higher pitch.
- Taller peaks mean a larger amplitude and a louder sound.
- Write one comparative sentence for pitch and one for loudness, each with its reason.

## Exam traps

- **Halve the echo time** when finding the distance to a reflector.
- **Convert ms and μs** before multiplying by speed.
- **Pitch is frequency**, not amplitude.
- **Ultrasound is still sound.**
- **Partial, not total, reflection** at each tissue boundary.
- **Explain why ultrasound suits pregnancy:** non-ionising.
- **No ear structure** is needed for this specification; do not spend time on it.

## Self-test

**1.** State the type of wave sound is.

**2.** Give the range of human hearing.

**3.** Why can sound not travel through a vacuum?

**4.** Two notes have the same amplitude but different frequencies. How do they sound different?

**5.** An oscilloscope shows one wave across 4.0 divisions at 0.50 ms per division. Find the frequency.

**6.** Find the wavelength in air (340 m/s) of a 680 Hz sound.

**7.** Define ultrasound.

**8.** An echo from a building returns 0.60 s after a shout. Sound travels at 340 m/s. How far away is the building?

**9.** An ultrasound echo returns 50 μs after the pulse in tissue (1500 m/s). How deep is the boundary?

**10.** Why does a scanner receive more than one echo from a single pulse?

**11.** Why is ultrasound used for prenatal scans instead of X-rays?

**12.** How does ultrasound treat kidney stones?

## Self-test answers

**1.** Longitudinal.

**2.** About 20 Hz to 20 000 Hz.

**3.** Sound is a vibration of a medium; a vacuum has no particles to vibrate.

**4.** They have different pitches; the higher-frequency note sounds higher.

**5.** T = 4.0 × 0.50 = 2.0 ms = 0.0020 s; f = 1 ÷ 0.0020 = 500 Hz.

**6.** λ = 340 ÷ 680 = 0.50 m.

**7.** Sound with a frequency above the upper limit of human hearing (above about 20 000 Hz).

**8.** 340 × 0.60 ÷ 2 = 102 m, about 100 m.

**9.** 1500 × 50 × 10⁻⁶ ÷ 2 = 0.0375 m, about 3.8 cm.

**10.** The pulse is partially reflected at each boundary it reaches, so deeper boundaries send back later echoes.

**11.** Ultrasound is not ionising, so it does not harm the fetus's cells.

**12.** High-energy ultrasound is focused on the stone, breaking it into small pieces that pass out of the body naturally.
