These questions cover AQA A-level Biology (7402) section 3.7.2, *Populations*. All data are invented. Use p + q = 1 and p² + 2pq + q² = 1, where p is the frequency of the dominant allele and q the frequency of the recessive allele. Teaching is in the [populations study guide](/resources/aqa-a-level-biology-7402-populations-aqa-alevel-biology/) and the [populations revision notes](/resources/aqa-a-level-biology-7402-populations-aqa-alevel-biology-revision-notes/).

## Section A: short questions

**1.** **(a)** Define the term *gene pool*. **[1]**

**(b)** State what the Hardy–Weinberg principle predicts about allele frequencies. **[1]**

**2.** Snails of one species live on two islands 40 km apart. Explain why the snails on the two islands are two populations rather than one. **[2]**

**3.** Explain why the frequency of heterozygotes in the Hardy–Weinberg equation is 2pq and not pq. **[1]**

**4.** Give three conditions under which the Hardy–Weinberg principle applies. **[3]**

**5.** In a population of a flowering plant, 91% of plants have the dominant phenotype. Calculate the percentage of plants that are homozygous dominant. **[3]**

**6.** Explain why the frequency of the dominant allele cannot be found directly from the frequency of the dominant phenotype. **[2]**

## Section B: longer questions

**7.** A recessive condition affects 1 in 2500 people in a population of 50 000. Calculate the number of people expected to be heterozygous carriers. **[3]**

**8.** A population of 300 beetles was sampled. There were 108 BB, 144 Bb and 48 bb individuals.

**(a)** Calculate the frequency of allele B. **[2]**

**(b)** Use the Hardy–Weinberg equation to show that these genotype numbers are those expected if the population is in equilibrium. **[2]**

**9.** A population of 500 plants had 230 AA, 140 Aa and 130 aa.

**(a)** Calculate the number of heterozygotes expected if the Hardy–Weinberg principle applied. **[3]**

**(b)** Suggest one reason for the difference between the observed and expected numbers of heterozygotes. **[1]**

**10.** In a population of moths, the recessive phenotype made up 36% of the population in one year and 16% twenty generations later.

**(a)** Calculate the frequency of the recessive allele in each sample. **[2]**

**(b)** Suggest an explanation for the change. **[2]**

**11.** A population of 2000 snails contains 320 that are homozygous recessive for shell colour.

**(a)** Calculate the number of heterozygous snails expected. **[3]**

**(b)** Thrushes that feed on these snails find the recessive phenotype easier to see. Explain how this could affect the allele frequencies in later generations. **[2]**

**12.** Explain why the Hardy–Weinberg principle is less likely to apply to a very small population than to a large one. **[2]**

## Answers

**1. (a)** All the alleles of all the genes in a population at a given time [1]. **[1]**

**(b)** Allele frequencies will not change from one generation to the next [1]. **[1]**

**2.** A population occupies the same space at the same time [1]; snails on different islands cannot potentially interbreed with each other because they are separated [1]. **[2]**

**3.** There are two ways to be heterozygous (dominant allele from either parent), so the probability is pq + pq = 2pq [1]. **[1]**

**4.** Any three [1 each]: large population; random mating; no mutation; no migration into or out of the population; no selection. **[3]**

**5.** q² = 1 − 0.91 = 0.09, so q = 0.3 [1]; p = 1 − 0.3 = 0.7 [1]; p² = 0.49, so **49%** [1]. **[3]**

**6.** The dominant phenotype includes both homozygous dominant and heterozygous individuals (p² + 2pq) [1], so it is not equal to p or p²; the recessive phenotype (q²) must be used first [1]. **[2]**

**7.** q² = 1 ÷ 2500 = 0.0004, so q = 0.02 and p = 0.98 [1]. 2pq = 2 × 0.98 × 0.02 = 0.0392 [1]. 0.0392 × 50 000 = **1960** [1]. **[3]**

**8. (a)** B alleles = 2 × 108 + 144 = 360 of 600 [1]; p = **0.6** [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** q = 0.4. Expected BB = 0.36 × 300 = 108; Bb = 0.48 × 300 = 144 [1]; bb = 0.16 × 300 = 48, which match the observed numbers [1]. **[2]**

**9. (a)** A alleles = 2 × 230 + 140 = 600 of 1000, so p = 0.6 [1]; q = 0.4 [1]; expected heterozygotes = 2 × 0.6 × 0.4 × 500 = **240** [1]. **[3]**

**(b)** Any one: mating is not random, for example self-pollination or mating with close neighbours; selection against heterozygotes [1]. **[1]**

*Examiner insight:* 140 observed against 240 expected is a large deficit, so name a condition that reduces heterozygotes specifically.

**10. (a)** First sample: q = √0.36 = **0.6** [1]. Later sample: q = √0.16 = **0.4** [1]. **[2]**

**(b)** The recessive phenotype had lower survival or reproduction, for example because it was more visible to predators after an environmental change [1], so the recessive allele was passed on less often (directional selection) [1]. **[2]**

**11. (a)** q² = 320 ÷ 2000 = 0.16, so q = 0.4 [1]; p = 0.6 [1]; heterozygotes = 2 × 0.6 × 0.4 × 2000 = **960** [1]. **[3]**

**(b)** Snails with the recessive phenotype are more likely to be eaten, so fewer survive to reproduce [1]. Fewer recessive alleles are passed on, so the frequency of the recessive allele falls and the dominant allele frequency rises [1]. **[2]**

**12.** In a small population, chance events such as the death of a few individuals or which individuals happen to breed have a large effect [1], so allele frequencies change by genetic drift and are not stable [1]. **[2]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using the recessive phenotype frequency as q without taking the square root.
- Starting from the dominant phenotype.
- Writing pq for heterozygotes.
- Counting individuals instead of alleles.
- Forgetting to multiply by the population size when a number is asked for.
