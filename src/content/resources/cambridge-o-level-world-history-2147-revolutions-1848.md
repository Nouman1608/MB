**Key question 1.1 asks whether the revolutions that swept Europe in 1848 mattered.** Cambridge sets the content as the nature of the revolutions and the influence of liberalism and nationalism; the causes, events and results of the revolutions in France, Italy, Germany and the Austrian Empire; and the reasons for their failure. This guide goes country by country, then answers the focus points.

## Where this fits in 2147

Key question 1.1 is the first of six in Core Content Option A ("The nineteenth century: the development of modern nation states, 1848–1914") of the Cambridge O Level History 2147 syllabus for 2024–2026. It is examined on Paper 1 as a three-part structured question: Section A sets two questions from key questions 1–3 in each option, so 1.1 competes with the unification of Italy and of Germany. It is not one of the prescribed Paper 2 topics for 2024–2026, so you need essay knowledge rather than source practice. It also sets up key questions 1.2 and 1.3. The overview of the whole option is in the [Nineteenth Century study guide](/resources/o-level-world-history-nineteenth-century-1848-1914/).

## Syllabus coverage

The five official focus points are:

1. Why had liberalism and nationalism grown in influence by 1848?
2. Why were there so many revolutions in 1848?
3. Did the revolutions have anything in common?
4. Why did most of the revolutions fail?
5. Did the revolutions change anything?

## Liberalism, nationalism and the Vienna settlement

After Napoleon's defeat, the Congress of Vienna (1815) restored monarchies and drew borders with little regard for nationality. The Austrian Empire ruled a patchwork of Germans, Hungarians, Czechs, Poles, Croats and Italians; Italy was divided into separate states with Austria controlling Lombardy and Venetia; Germany was a loose German Confederation of 39 states under Austrian presidency. The Austrian chancellor **Metternich** used censorship, police and the threat of intervention to suppress change.

- **Liberalism** demanded written constitutions, elected parliaments, freedom of the press and of speech, and the rule of law. Its supporters were mainly the educated and propertied middle classes.
- **Nationalism** held that people sharing a language and culture should govern themselves in their own state. In Italy and Germany it meant unification; in the Austrian Empire it meant self-government for Hungarians, Czechs and others, which threatened the empire's survival.

Both grew between 1815 and 1848 because of spreading education and newspapers, the memory of the French Revolution, secret societies and movements such as Mazzini's Young Italy (1831), and economic links such as the Prussian-led customs union, the Zollverein (1834), which showed Germans the benefits of acting together.

## Why so many revolutions in 1848?

- **Economic crisis, 1845–47.** Potato blight and poor grain harvests pushed up food prices; an industrial and financial slump caused unemployment in the towns. Hungry, unemployed crowds made protest dangerous for rulers.
- **Long-term political grievances** of liberals and nationalists, unanswered since 1815.
- **The French example.** Revolution in Paris in February 1848 showed that a king could be overthrown, and news spread fast by newspaper and rail.
- **Weak or hesitant rulers.** Several governments gave way at the first sign of trouble, which encouraged risings elsewhere.

## France

The **July Monarchy** of King Louis-Philippe allowed only a small, wealthy minority to vote, and his chief minister Guizot refused reform. When the government banned a political banquet in Paris in February 1848, protests turned into street fighting. Guizot was dismissed, and on **24 February Louis-Philippe abdicated**. A provisional government proclaimed the **Second Republic** and introduced **universal male suffrage**. To help the unemployed it set up **National Workshops**.

Elections in April produced a moderate and conservative assembly, because most voters were rural. When it closed the National Workshops, Paris workers rose in the **June Days** (June 1848). General **Cavaignac** crushed them, with thousands killed. The middle classes now feared the workers more than the old order. In **December 1848 Louis Napoleon Bonaparte**, nephew of Napoleon I, was elected president by a large majority. In December 1851 he seized power in a coup, and in 1852 he became Emperor Napoleon III.

## The Austrian Empire

In **March 1848** risings broke out in **Vienna**; **Metternich** resigned and fled. Emperor Ferdinand promised a constitution. In **Hungary**, led by **Kossuth**, the Diet passed the **April Laws**, giving Hungary its own government and ending serfdom there. Czech nationalists in **Prague** demanded rights within the empire.

The empire's recovery began with the army. **Windischgrätz** bombarded Prague in June 1848 and recaptured Vienna in October. In **September 1848** the imperial assembly abolished **serfdom** across the empire, which satisfied many peasants. The Croats under **Jelačić**, who feared Hungarian rule, fought for the emperor against Hungary. In December 1848 Ferdinand abdicated in favour of the young **Franz Joseph**. Hungary declared independence in April 1849, but Austria, with the help of a large **Russian** army sent by Tsar Nicholas I, defeated the Hungarians in August 1849.

## Italy

The first rising of the year came in **Sicily** (Palermo, January 1848). Rulers in Naples, Tuscany, the Papal States and **Piedmont** granted constitutions; Piedmont's was the **Statuto** of March 1848. In the **Five Days of Milan** (March 1848) the people drove out the Austrian army under **Radetzky**, and **Venice** declared a republic under Daniele Manin. King **Charles Albert** of Piedmont declared war on Austria.

The Italian effort collapsed. **Pope Pius IX** refused to support war against Catholic Austria (April 1848). Radetzky defeated Piedmont at **Custoza** (July 1848). In Rome, a **Roman Republic** was declared in February 1849, led by Mazzini and defended by Garibaldi. Piedmont renewed the war and was defeated at **Novara** (March 1849); Charles Albert abdicated in favour of Victor Emmanuel II. **French troops**, sent by Louis Napoleon, ended the Roman Republic in July 1849, and Venice surrendered to Austria in August 1849.

## Germany

In March 1848 rulers across the German states granted reforms. In **Berlin**, street fighting on 18 March persuaded King **Frederick William IV of Prussia** to withdraw his troops and promise a constitution. In May 1848 an elected **Frankfurt Parliament** met to create a united Germany. Its members were mostly middle-class lawyers, officials and professors.

The Parliament debated for months. It argued over whether Germany should include Austria (*Grossdeutschland*) or exclude it (*Kleindeutschland*). It had no army of its own: when it opposed Denmark over Schleswig-Holstein, Prussia made peace (the armistice of Malmö, August 1848) without its consent. It drew up Fundamental Rights and, in 1849, a constitution, and offered the crown of a united Germany to Frederick William IV. In **April 1849 he refused** a crown offered by an elected assembly. The Parliament broke up in 1849. Meanwhile Frederick William had dissolved the Prussian assembly in December 1848 and imposed his own constitution, which kept power with the king.

## Answering the focus points

**Did the revolutions have anything in common?** Most shared liberal demands (constitutions, parliaments, a free press), a nationalist element (except in France), a role for the economic crisis, urban leadership by the middle classes and students with support from workers, and a similar timing, breaking out within weeks of Paris. But aims differed: in France the issue was the form of government and the workers' demands; in Italy and Germany unity; in the Austrian Empire independence or autonomy for nationalities, which divided them.

**Why did most of the revolutions fail?**

| Reason | Evidence |
|---|---|
| Divisions among revolutionaries | Liberals feared radicals and workers (June Days); nationalities fought each other (Croats against Hungarians) |
| Armies stayed loyal to rulers | Radetzky in Italy; Windischgrätz in Prague and Vienna; Prussian army |
| Rulers recovered | Concessions in March were withdrawn once armies regained control |
| Peasants satisfied | Abolition of serfdom in 1848 removed their main grievance |
| Foreign intervention | Russia in Hungary; France in Rome |
| No power behind parliaments | Frankfurt depended on Prussia and Austria |
| Economic recovery | As food supplies and trade recovered, popular unrest declined |

**Did the revolutions change anything?** Few aims were achieved, but some changes lasted: serfdom in the Austrian Empire was not restored; Piedmont kept the Statuto and became the leader of Italian nationalism; Prussia kept a parliament, if a limited one; France kept universal male suffrage; Metternich's era ended. Nationalists also learned that unification needed the power of a strong state and its army.

## Annotated model answer

**Question (c):** "The revolutions of 1848 failed mainly because the revolutionaries were divided." How far do you agree? Explain your answer. **[10]**

*Agree:* Divisions weakened every revolution. In France, middle-class republicans turned on the workers in the June Days, and fear of radicalism helped elect Louis Napoleon. In the Austrian Empire, Croats under Jelačić fought for the emperor against the Hungarians. In Frankfurt, arguments over *Grossdeutschland* and *Kleindeutschland* wasted months. **(Explained, with specific evidence.)**

*Disagree:* Military force decided the outcome. Radetzky won at Custoza and Novara, Windischgrätz retook Prague and Vienna, and Russian troops defeated Hungary. Foreign intervention also ended the Roman Republic. The Frankfurt Parliament failed because it had no army and Frederick William IV refused the crown. **(Explained alternative factors.)**

*Judgement:* Divisions made the revolutionaries vulnerable, but they failed mainly because rulers kept control of loyal armies and used them once the shock of March had passed. **(A supported conclusion that weighs the factors.)**

## Common mistakes

- Treating 1848 as one revolution; each country had different aims and outcomes.
- Writing about Italian unification after 1859 in a 1.1 answer; stay within 1848–49 unless the question asks about results.
- Forgetting that France's revolution was not nationalist in the same way as the others.
- Describing events without explaining why they led to failure.
- In "how far" questions, giving one side only or no final judgement.

## Exam technique

The 2147 command words are **describe** (state the main points or features) and **explain** (give reasons, say why or how, supported with evidence); you will also meet "How far do you agree...?". In a part (a) *describe* question, give precise, relevant facts. In a part (b) *explain* question, give two or three reasons, each linked to the question with "because" or "this meant". In a part (c) *how far* question, argue both sides with specific evidence and finish with a judgement that weighs them.
