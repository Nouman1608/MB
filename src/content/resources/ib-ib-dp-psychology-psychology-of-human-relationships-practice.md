IB DP Psychology Paper 2 (first assessment 2019) sets three 22-mark essays on the human relationships option. The nine questions below match the option's nine content points, one each, with a model plan organised by the ERQ criteria. Allow about an hour per essay.

## How the essays are marked

| Criterion | Marks | Top-band expectation |
|---|---|---|
| A Focus on the question | 2 | Explains the problem or issue posed by the question |
| B Knowledge and understanding | 6 | Accurate, detailed, well-explained terminology |
| C Use of research | 6 | Relevant research, explained and linked to the question |
| D Critical thinking | 6 | Well-developed evaluation and consideration of alternatives |
| E Clarity and organisation | 2 | Clear and coherent throughout |

## Questions

**1.** Evaluate research on the formation of personal relationships. **[22]**

**2.** Discuss the role of communication in personal relationships. **[22]**

**3.** Evaluate explanations for why relationships change or end. **[22]**

**4.** Discuss cooperation and competition in groups. **[22]**

**5.** Evaluate research on prejudice and discrimination. **[22]**

**6.** Discuss the origins of conflict and strategies for conflict resolution. **[22]**

**7.** Evaluate research on bystanderism. **[22]**

**8.** Contrast two explanations of prosocial behaviour. **[22]**

**9.** Discuss strategies for promoting prosocial behaviour. **[22]**

## Model plans

**1. Formation.**
- *C:* Festinger, Schachter and Back (1950) proximity; Buss (1989) cross-cultural mate preferences; Fisher et al. (2005) neural correlates of romantic love.
- *D:* Festinger's housing sample was homogeneous married students; Buss relied on self-reported preferences rather than actual choices, and his samples were not always representative; fMRI shows correlation and uses small samples. Different approaches explain different aspects -- environment, evolution, neurochemistry.

**2. Communication.**
- *B:* communication patterns, self-disclosure, attributions.
- *C:* Gottman's four horsemen and the Buehlman, Gottman and Katz (1992) oral-history interview; Bradbury and Fincham (1990) on attributions.
- *D:* observational and interview data from mainly American married couples; correlation between communication and outcomes does not show direction; prediction accuracy may be lower in replications.

**3. Change or end.**
- *B:* social exchange; Rusbult's investment model.
- *C:* studies testing the investment model with dating couples over time.
- *D:* the model predicts commitment well, but relies on self-report; it does not explain abusive relationships fully without considering fear and dependence; cultural differences in relationship dissolution.

**4. Cooperation and competition.**
- *B:* realistic conflict theory; superordinate goals.
- *C:* Sherif et al. (Robbers Cave): competition between two groups of boys produced hostility, and superordinate goals such as repairing the water supply reduced it.
- *D:* field experiment with high ecological validity but a small, culturally narrow sample; ethical issues of induced conflict and lack of consent; SIT suggests competition is not necessary for in-group bias (Tajfel, 1971).

**5. Prejudice and discrimination.**
- *B:* definitions (attitude versus behaviour); SIT; stereotypes.
- *C:* Tajfel et al. (1971); Bertrand and Mullainathan (2004) field experiment on hiring discrimination.
- *D:* minimal groups are artificial; the CV study shows behaviour in a real setting, though names may signal class as well as race; ethics of deceiving employers.

**6. Conflict and resolution.**
- *B:* origins -- competition, identity, perceived threat; resolution -- contact hypothesis, cooperation, superordinate goals.
- *C:* Sherif et al.; Allport's (1954) conditions for contact; Aronson's jigsaw classroom.
- *D:* contact works best under the stated conditions, which are rarely all present; jigsaw effects are often short-term and classroom-specific.

**7. Bystanderism.**
- *B:* diffusion of responsibility, pluralistic ignorance, decision model, cost-reward model.
- *C:* Darley and Latané (1968); Piliavin et al. (1969).
- *D:* lab study high control but low ecological validity and deception; Piliavin's field study showed higher helping rates than lab studies, possibly because the victim was visible and escape difficult; lack of informed consent.

**8. Two explanations of prosocial behaviour (contrast).**
- *B/C:* empathy-altruism (Batson et al., 1981) versus egoistic explanations such as negative-state relief or the cost-reward model; kin selection as a biological alternative.
- *D:* Batson's lab manipulations are artificial and involve deception; egoistic accounts fit everyday helping with low costs; motives are hard to observe directly.

**9. Promoting prosocial behaviour.**
- *B:* modelling, prosocial media, education about bystander effects, increasing personal responsibility.
- *C:* research on prosocial content in children's television; studies showing that learning about bystander research increases later helping.
- *D:* short-term effects and lab settings; cultural differences in norms of helping; difficulty measuring real-world behaviour.

## Worked paragraph: evaluating a field experiment (Question 7)

> Piliavin et al. (1969) staged collapses on New York subway trains, with a confederate who appeared
> either ill (carrying a cane) or drunk. The ill victim received help in nearly all trials, the drunk
> victim less often, and the size of the group did not reduce helping as Darley and Latané's findings
> predicted. This suggests that in face-to-face emergencies where escape is difficult, a cost-reward
> calculation explains behaviour better than diffusion of responsibility. The field setting gives the
> study high ecological validity, but passengers could not consent or be debriefed, and observers could
> not control who was in each carriage, so findings must be weighed against controlled lab research.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can I use the same study in two essays?**
Yes -- Tajfel's research fits both prejudice and conflict -- but link it to each question.

**How many approaches should I use?**
Using more than one approach (for example evolutionary and sociocultural) is a strong way to show critical thinking in criterion D.

**What is the most common error in relationship essays?**
Describing studies without evaluating them; criterion D carries six of the 22 marks.
