This guide covers **2.1 Diurnal energy budgets** in Topic 2, Atmosphere and weather, of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Geography (9696) syllabus for 2025 and 2026. The syllabus names the factors affecting the diurnal energy budget as: **incoming (shortwave) solar radiation, reflected solar radiation, energy absorbed into the surface and subsurface, albedo, sensible heat transfer, longwave radiation, latent heat transfer (evaporation), dew and absorbed energy returned to earth.**

The content is examined in Paper 1, often as a Section A question built around a diagram or table of energy flows, and in the Atmosphere and weather structured question in Section B. The [2.1 diurnal energy budgets revision notes](/resources/a-level-geography-9696-diurnal-energy-budgets-revision-notes/) summarise the day and night equations, typical albedo values and night-time phenomena; this guide explains how each component behaves and how to work with budget data. Test yourself with the [2.1 practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-geography-9696-diurnal-energy-budgets-practice/).

## What a diurnal energy budget is

An energy budget accounts for the energy arriving at and leaving a surface. "Diurnal" means over a 24-hour cycle. The surface concerned is the ground and the air layer just above it, so this is a **micro-scale** study: a field, a car park, a forest floor or a beach. Each term in the budget is a **flux**, measured in watts per square metre (W/m²), and the budget balances when energy gained equals energy used or lost. When it does not balance, the surface warms (net gain) or cools (net loss).

## The daytime components, one by one

- **Incoming shortwave solar radiation** is the main input. Its size depends on sun angle (latitude, season and time of day), cloud cover and the clarity of the air. It peaks around midday.
- **Reflected solar radiation** is the share bounced straight back without heating the surface. It is set by the surface's **albedo**, the proportion of incoming radiation reflected. Albedo is therefore a controlling factor on the size of a component rather than a flow in its own right.
- **Energy absorbed into the surface and subsurface** is the energy conducted downwards into the soil or material below. It depends on the material's ability to conduct heat. Wet soil conducts heat better than dry, loose soil, and concrete and rock conduct and store more heat than sand or vegetation.
- **Sensible heat transfer** is heat moved between the surface and the air by conduction and then convection. By day the surface is warmer than the air, so heat flows upwards. It is larger when the surface is much warmer than the air and when wind or rising thermals mix the warmed air away.
- **Longwave radiation** is the infrared radiation emitted by the warmed surface. The warmer the surface, the more it emits. Part is absorbed by water vapour, carbon dioxide and cloud and re-radiated back.
- **Latent heat transfer (evaporation)** is energy used to change liquid water into vapour. It cools the surface without changing the temperature of the air directly; the energy is carried upwards in the vapour and released later when it condenses. Its size depends on the water available, the dryness of the air and the wind.

The central idea is **partitioning**. After reflection, the energy a surface absorbs is shared between subsurface heating, sensible heat, latent heat and longwave emission. A wet surface spends a large share on evaporation, so it warms less. A dry surface has almost no latent heat use, so much more goes into sensible heat and the air above becomes hot.

## The night-time components

With no solar input, the budget changes direction:

- **Longwave radiation** from the surface continues, so the surface loses energy and cools. Loss is greatest under **clear skies** and in **dry air**, because there is little cloud or vapour to absorb and return the radiation.
- **Absorbed energy returned to earth** (the subsurface supply) is heat stored in the ground during the day and conducted back up to the cooling surface. Surfaces that stored a lot of heat by day, such as rock or concrete, return more.
- **Sensible heat transfer** now runs downwards, from the warmer air to the colder surface.
- **Dew** forms if the surface cools to the dew point of the air in contact with it. Condensation releases **latent heat**, which slows further cooling.

## Worked example 1: a daytime budget for two surfaces

*The table gives fictional midday fluxes (W/m²) for a grass field and an asphalt car park side by side. Both receive 800 W/m² of incoming solar radiation.*

| Component | Grass (albedo 0.25) | Asphalt (albedo 0.08) |
|---|---|---|
| Reflected solar | 200 | 64 |
| Absorbed solar (incoming minus reflected) | 600 | 736 |
| Longwave radiation (net loss) | 100 | 180 |
| Subsurface absorption | 60 | 150 |
| Sensible heat transfer | 160 | 386 |
| Latent heat transfer | 280 | 20 |

**Check the budget:** grass: 100 + 60 + 160 + 280 = 600, which equals the absorbed solar radiation, so the budget balances. Asphalt: 180 + 150 + 386 + 20 = 736, also balanced.

**Interpret:** the asphalt absorbs 136 W/m² more because of its lower albedo. With almost no water to evaporate, it uses only 20 W/m² as latent heat, against 280 W/m² for grass. On grass, latent heat takes 280 / 600 = 46.7% of absorbed energy; on asphalt only 20 / 736 = 2.7%. The asphalt therefore puts far more into sensible heat (386 against 160 W/m²), heating the air, and into the subsurface (150 against 60 W/m²), storing heat that will be returned at night.

## Worked example 2: a night-time budget under clear and cloudy skies

*Fictional fluxes (W/m²) for the same grass field on two nights:*

| Component | Clear, calm night | Cloudy night |
|---|---|---|
| Longwave radiation (net loss) | -70 | -25 |
| Absorbed energy returned from the subsurface | +35 | +15 |
| Sensible heat transfer from the air | +20 | +8 |
| Latent heat released by dew | +5 | 0 |
| **Net** | **-10** | **-2** |

On the clear night the net loss is five times larger, so the surface cools faster and is more likely to reach the dew point, which is why dew contributes only on that night. Under cloud, much of the longwave radiation is absorbed and re-radiated downwards, so the net loss falls from 70 to 25 W/m².

## Why the daily temperature cycle lags behind the sun

Incoming solar radiation peaks at about midday, but surface and air temperatures usually peak in the **early afternoon**. This is because the surface continues to gain more energy than it loses for some time after noon: as long as the budget is in surplus, temperature keeps rising. The minimum temperature comes **around dawn**, at the end of a whole night of net loss. Clouds by day reduce the maximum (less incoming radiation); clouds by night raise the minimum (less net longwave loss). Together these give a small diurnal temperature range on cloudy days and a large range under clear skies.

## Annotated model answer

*"Explain why a surface covered by wet soil warms less during the day than a surface of dry sand." [5]*

> A wet surface has water available for evaporation, so a large share of the absorbed solar energy is used as **latent heat** to change water to vapour **(key component named)**. This energy does not raise the surface temperature **(effect)**. Wet soil also conducts heat better, so more energy is **absorbed into the subsurface** rather than staying at the surface **(second component)**. Dry sand has no water to evaporate, so almost all absorbed energy goes into **sensible heat** and longwave emission, and it is a poor conductor, so heat stays concentrated at the surface **(contrast)**. The sand surface therefore becomes much hotter **(conclusion)**.

## Common mistakes

- Treating albedo as an energy flow rather than the proportion that controls reflection.
- Saying latent heat "heats the air" during the day; evaporation cools the surface.
- Forgetting that sensible heat transfer changes direction between day and night.
- Writing that cloud makes nights colder.
- Omitting dew, or describing dew formation without saying it releases latent heat.
- Describing components without saying whether each increases or decreases surface temperature.

## Exam technique

- **Describe** a budget diagram: name each flow, its direction and its size from the resource.
- **Explain** a contrast: identify which components differ and why, then give the effect on temperature.
- **Calculate**: check that the components balance, and state units (W/m²) or percentages clearly.
- **Suggest** for an unfamiliar surface (snow, forest, a lake): reason from albedo, water availability, conductivity and shelter.
- Use the syllabus terms exactly, especially "absorbed energy returned to earth" for the night-time subsurface supply.
