## Equilibrium and disequilibrium

**Equilibrium** is the price at which buyers want to buy exactly what sellers want to sell. Nothing pushes the price up or down.  Any other price is **disequilibrium**.

**Worked example 1 (Island Y, illustrative).** Weekly demand for mangoes is Qd = 480 − 30P and supply is Qs = −60 + 30P, with P in currency units (cu) per kg and Q in tonnes.

Set Qd = Qs: 480 − 30P = −60 + 30P, so 540 = 60P and **P = 9 cu**. Then Q = 480 − 270 = **210 tonnes**. Check with supply: −60 + 270 = 210.

| Price | Qd | Qs | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| 6 | 300 | 120 | Excess demand of 180 |
| 9 | 210 | 210 | Equilibrium |
| 12 | 120 | 300 | Excess supply of 180 |

**Why price moves.** At 12 sellers are left with 180 unsold tonnes. Unsold stock builds up and some fruit rots, so sellers mark prices down to move it. The lower price draws out extra purchases (an extension of demand) while some sellers cut back (a contraction of supply), and the gap narrows. At 6 the reverse happens: buyers cannot all be served, some offer more, and sellers see they can raise price. Price rises, quantity supplied extends and quantity demanded contracts. Always state the direction of both movements along the curves.

In a diagram, price runs up the vertical axis and quantity along the horizontal one. The downward-sloping demand curve meets the upward-sloping supply curve at the equilibrium point. A price above it shows excess supply as the horizontal gap between the curves; a price below it shows excess demand.

## Shifts and the size of price changes

A shift of either curve creates a new equilibrium. Name the cause, state which curve moves and in which direction, then take the new price and quantity from where the curves now cross.

**Worked example 2 (demand rises).** A health campaign adds 60 tonnes of demand at every price: Qd = 540 − 30P. Then 540 − 30P = −60 + 30P, so 600 = 60P and **P = 10, Q = 240**. Price is up 11.1% and quantity up 14.3%.

**Worked example 3 (supply falls).** Storm damage removes 90 tonnes of supply at every price: Qs = −150 + 30P. With the original demand, 480 − 30P = −150 + 30P, so 630 = 60P and **P = 10.50, Q = 165**. Price is up 16.7% and quantity down 21.4%.

**Elasticity decides the split between price and quantity.** Take the same demand rise but a market where supply responds less: Qs = 120 + 10P. The original equilibrium is still 9 and 210, because 480 − 270 = 120 + 90. After the rise, 540 − 30P = 120 + 10P, so 420 = 40P and **P = 10.50, Q = 225**.

| Supply | New price | Price change | New quantity | Quantity change |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Responsive (Qs rises 30 per 1 cu) | 10.00 | +11.1% | 240 | +14.3% |
| Less responsive (Qs rises 10 per 1 cu) | 10.50 | +16.7% | 225 | +7.1% |

When supply is steeper, producers cannot expand much, so more of the demand rise is absorbed by price. A steeper demand curve works the same way for a supply shift: price moves more and quantity less.

## Commodity prices and speculation

Commodity prices swing widely because supply depends on weather and harvests, short-run supply and demand are both unresponsive to price, and so a modest shift needs a large price move to restore equilibrium.

**Worked example 4 (Crop K, illustrative).** Demand is Qd = 400 − 20P. Supply in a normal harvest is Qs = 100 + 20P.

| Harvest | Supply | Price | Quantity | Farmers' revenue |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Normal | 100 + 20P | 7.5 | 250 | 1,875 |
| Poor (supply down 60) | 40 + 20P | 9.0 | 220 | 1,980 |
| Bumper (supply up 60) | 160 + 20P | 6.0 | 280 | 1,680 |

Prices swing by 20% either way. Farmers' revenue is higher after the poor harvest and lower after the bumper one, because demand is unresponsive: the price change outweighs the quantity change. That is why producers' incomes can be unstable even when output is high.

**Speculation** means buying or selling mainly to gain from an expected change in price. Suppose traders expect Crop K to rise and add 40 units of demand at every price: Qd = 440 − 20P. Then 440 − 20P = 100 + 20P, so **P = 8.5, Q = 270**. Price has risen 13.3% before any real shortage appeared, and the rise may confirm the traders' belief and draw in more buyers. This is a self-fulfilling prophecy and it can build a bubble. If the expectation proves wrong, the reverse sell-off brings a sharp fall. Evaluation: speculators can also stabilise prices. Buying when the harvest is good lifts a low price, and selling stocks when it is poor limits the rise. The effect depends on whether traders follow fundamentals or follow each other.

## Linked markets

Markets are connected, so a change in one has knock-on effects. Use the correct term.

| Term | Meaning | Example direction |
|---|---|---|
| Competitive demand | Goods that are substitutes | Price of tea up, demand for coffee up |
| Joint demand | Goods bought together (complements) | Price of printers down, demand for ink up |
| Composite demand | One good with several uses | Milk for drinking and for cheese |
| Derived demand | Demand for an input or product that comes from demand for what it helps to make | Demand for batteries from scooter sales |
| Joint supply | One process yields two or more goods | Lamb and wool |

**Chain of reasoning (competitive and joint demand).** A poor tea harvest in Country A raises the price of tea. Some tea drinkers move to coffee: coffee's demand curve moves right and its price rises. Fewer cups of tea mean less milk bought with them, so the demand curve for milk shifts left. Tea pickers are in derived demand, so the lower tea output reduces demand for their labour.

**Worked example 5 (composite demand).** Milk in Country A is used for drinking, Qd1 = 300 − 20P, and for cheese, Qd2 = 200 − 10P. Total demand is 500 − 30P. Supply is Qs = −100 + 30P, so 600 = 60P, **P = 10, Q = 200**, split 100 for drinking and 100 for cheese. A cheese boom adds 60 to cheese demand: Qd2 = 260 − 10P. Total demand is 560 − 30P, so 660 = 60P and **P = 11, Q = 230**. Drinking falls to 300 − 220 = 80 and cheese rises to 260 − 110 = 150. Drinkers lose out because cheese makers bid milk away.

**Worked example 6 (derived demand).** A firm fits 2 batteries to each scooter. Sales of 40,000 need 80,000 batteries. If scooter sales fall 15% to 34,000, battery demand falls to 68,000, also 15%. A supplier whose only customer is the scooter firm has no other source of demand, so it feels the whole fall.

**Worked example 7 (joint supply).** A farm region has 50,000 sheep, each giving 3 kg of wool, so wool output is 150 thousand kg. Wool demand is Qd = 300 − 10P (thousand kg, P in cu), so P = 15. Rising demand for lamb pushes the flock up 10% to 55,000, and wool output to 165 thousand kg (55 × 3). Wool is a by-product, so farmers do not raise the flock because of the wool price: supply simply shifts right to 165. The new price is (300 − 165)/10 = **13.50**, down 10%. A rise in demand for one joint product lowers the price of the other.

## How prices allocate resources

The **price mechanism** resolves the basic economic problem of what, how and for whom to produce without a central planner, by coordinating the separate decisions of buyers and sellers through price. It has three functions.

- **Rationing:** when supply is limited, a higher price allocates the good to those willing and able to pay. Example: 120 prints are made and demand is Qd = 300 − 10P. At 12, quantity demanded is 180, an excess of 60. Price rises to 18, where 300 − 180 = 120, and buyers who value a print at less than 18 drop out.
- **Incentive:** higher prices promise higher profits and so encourage firms to produce more, while workers move to better-paid jobs. Lower prices discourage production.
- **Signalling:** price changes tell producers and consumers about scarcity and tastes. A rising price for a good signals higher demand or lower supply and directs resources towards it.

**What, how and for whom.** What: demand for a good rises, its price and profit rise, and firms switch resources into it. How: a tile workshop can use method X (12 workers, 2 machines) or method Y (5 workers, 5 machines). With wages of 40 cu and machines at 120 cu a day, X costs 480 + 240 = 720 and Y costs 200 + 600 = 800, so X is chosen. If wages rise to 60, X costs 960 and Y 900, so firms switch to Y. For whom: goods go to those with the income and willingness to pay.

**Advantages.** It responds quickly, rewards efficiency, requires no central information gathering and offers consumer choice. **Disadvantages.** It ignores those without income, may under-provide goods like street lighting, ignores external costs, and can leave inequality and instability.

**Assessing "an impersonal method".** For: prices treat every buyer and seller by the same rule, regardless of who they are, so nobody's favouritism decides. Against: the result still depends on the unequal distribution of income, so those with more money have more votes. Judgement: impersonal does not mean neutral or fair.

**Extending markets into new areas.** Charging for places on a public clinic's waiting list, for example, might cut queues and raise funds. But it may be undesirable: access depends on income, and the activity changes. Volunteer helpers paid by the hour may feel their role is now a job and give less. Weigh efficiency gains against equity and the nature of the activity.

## Common errors

- Writing "demand increases" for a movement along the curve. A price change causes an extension or contraction.
- Calling any price above equilibrium a "surplus" without saying whose: say excess supply.
- Forgetting that after a shift price and quantity both change, in directions you must state.
- Listing the three functions without applying them to a scenario.
- Treating joint demand and joint supply as the same thing: one is about buying together, the other about producing together.
