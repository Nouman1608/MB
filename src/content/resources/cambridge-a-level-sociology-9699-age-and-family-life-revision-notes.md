Condensed notes for section 4.2, *Age and family life*, of Cambridge International AS & A Level Sociology (9699), Paper 2. Full explanations and a worked answer are in the [4.2 study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-sociology-9699-age-and-family-life/); test yourself with the [age and family life practice questions](/resources/a-level-cambridge-sociology-age-and-family-life-practice/).

## Syllabus checklist

- The social construction of childhood; changes in the role and social position of children in the family.
- The role and social position of grandparents, including cross-cultural comparison and the impact of changing life expectancy.
- Social class, gender and ethnicity as factors affecting children's experiences in the family.
- Changes in the concepts of motherhood and fatherhood.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Social construction of childhood | The idea that the meaning, length and treatment of childhood are set by society, not biology alone |
| Child-centred family | A family organised around children's needs and interests |
| March of progress | The view that children's position has steadily improved |
| Age patriarchy | Gittins (1985): adult domination over children |
| Dionysian and Apollonian child | Jenks (1996): the child as naturally wild versus naturally innocent |
| Beanpole family | Brannen (2003): many generations, few members in each |
| Pivot generation | Adults caring for both children and ageing parents |
| Life expectancy | The average number of years a person can expect to live |
| Concerted cultivation | Lareau (2003): middle-class parenting through organised activities and negotiation |
| Bedroom culture | McRobbie and Garber (1976): girls' home-based leisure |
| Izzat | Family honour, emphasised in Bhatti's (1999) study of Asian families |
| Intensive mothering | Hays (1996): an ideology of child-centred, time-consuming mothering |
| Intimate fatherhood | Dermott (2008): fatherhood based on emotional closeness |

## Childhood as a social construction

**History**
- Ariès (1962): medieval children treated as small adults after infancy.
- Shorter (1975): high infant mortality limited emotional investment.
- Pollock (1983): diaries and letters show parents did see children as different, challenging Ariès.
- Cunningham (2006): gradual change in ideas of childhood over a long period.

**Comparison**
- Punch (2001): children in rural Bolivia combine school with substantial work and negotiate autonomy.
- Legal ages for work, marriage and criminal responsibility vary between countries.

**Images of the child**: Jenks (1996), Dionysian (needs control) and Apollonian (needs protection).

## Children's changing position

| March of progress | Conflict view |
|---|---|
| Laws on child labour and compulsory schooling | Gittins (1985): age patriarchy |
| Child protection and the UN Convention on the Rights of the Child (1989) | Adults control time, space, bodies and money |
| Child-centred families; more say in decisions | Protection doubles as control |
| Lower infant mortality, smaller families, more investment per child | Abuse shows the danger of adult power |

Plus the toxic or disappearing childhood debate: media and consumer culture may blur childhood, but children can be active, competent users of technology.

## Grandparents

- **Life expectancy**: more and longer-lived grandparents; Brannen's beanpole family.
- **Roles**: childcare for working parents; financial help; emotional support.
- **Pivot generation**: middle-aged adults caring both ways.
- **Dependency**: longer life can mean longer periods needing care.
- **Cross-cultural**: in many South and East Asian societies grandparents traditionally live with a married son and hold authority; in many Western societies they live apart and involvement depends on choice and distance; migration and urbanisation are changing both patterns.

## Class, gender and ethnicity

| Factor | Effect on children's experience | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Class | Parenting style, resources, responsibilities | Lareau (2003): concerted cultivation versus natural growth |
| Gender | Boys allowed more freedom; girls more supervised and given more domestic tasks | Hillman et al. (1990); McRobbie and Garber (1976) |
| Ethnicity | Family honour, religion, expectations of obedience and kin | Bhatti (1999) |

Remember the factors interact; no group is uniform.

## Motherhood and fatherhood

- **Motherhood**: from full-time mother to working mother; Hays (1996) intensive mothering raises demands; the "good mother" ideal places responsibility on women.
- **Fatherhood**: from breadwinner and disciplinarian to involved "new father"; Dermott (2008) intimate fatherhood.
- **Ideal versus practice**: mothers still do most routine care; policy such as paternity leave shapes change.

## Exam traps

- **Ariès alone.** Pair him with Pollock.
- **Child-centred is not child power.** Investment does not mean control.
- **Grandparents without comparison.** The syllabus asks for cross-cultural comparison and life expectancy.
- **Class only.** Gender and ethnicity are also named.
- **Ideal versus practice.** "Concepts" of parenthood may change faster than behaviour.
- **One society as universal.** Childhood differs sharply between societies.

## Self-test

1. What does it mean to say childhood is socially constructed?
2. What did Ariès argue?
3. How did Pollock challenge Ariès?
4. What is age patriarchy?
5. Give two pieces of evidence for the march of progress view.
6. What is a beanpole family, and why has it developed?
7. What is the pivot generation?
8. Give one cross-cultural difference in the role of grandparents.
9. What did Lareau mean by concerted cultivation?
10. What did Hillman's research suggest about gender and childhood?
11. What is intensive mothering?
12. What did Dermott mean by intimate fatherhood?

### Answers

1. Its meaning, length and treatment are defined by society and vary between cultures and over time.
2. That in medieval Europe childhood as a separate stage barely existed; children joined adult life after infancy.
3. She used diaries and letters to show parents did see children as different and cared for them.
4. Gittins' term for adult domination over children's time, space, bodies and money.
5. Any two: child labour laws; compulsory schooling; child protection; children's rights under the UN Convention (1989); child-centred families.
6. A family with many generations but few members in each, because of rising life expectancy and smaller family size.
7. Middle-aged adults caring for both their children and their ageing parents.
8. In many South and East Asian societies grandparents traditionally live with a married son's family and hold authority, while in many Western societies they live separately.
9. Middle-class parenting that develops children's talents through organised activities and reasoning with adults.
10. Boys were allowed more independence than girls, for example to travel alone.
11. An ideology that good mothering is child-centred, expert-guided and very time-consuming.
12. Fatherhood defined by emotional closeness to children rather than breadwinning or time alone.
