## Section A: short questions

**1.** Read this original sentence: *"I believed, as children will, that the house had a temper of
its own."* Identify the type of narrator and explain one effect of this narrative voice. **[2]**

**2.** Define *free indirect style*. Then explain how this original sentence uses it: *"Mrs Bell set
down the letter. Ruined, then, and by her own sister. Well, she would not weep in front of the
servants."* **[3]**

**3.** In the original sentence *"The fog pressed against the windows of the court as though it, too,
wished to hear the verdict,"* identify the method and explain its effect. **[2]**

**4.** Identify the semantic field in this original sentence and explain what it suggests: *"The
factory swallowed the workers at six, digested them through the day, and spat them out at dusk."*
**[2]**

**5.** A student writes: *"The writer uses old-fashioned language which makes it hard to read."*
Explain why this comment earns little credit and write an improved version about a long sentence.
**[3]**

**6.** Explain what is meant by an *unreliable narrator* and give one way a writer can signal to the
reader that a narrator's account should not be fully trusted. **[2]**

## Section B: longer questions

Read the original extract, then answer Questions 7 and 8. It is narrated by Eliza, looking back on her
youth.

> *Original extract:* I was seventeen when my aunt first permitted me to sit in the front parlour, and
> I remember that I took the privilege for a kindness. The room was kept for visitors and for grief:
> its blinds were drawn by day, its clock was never wound, and the sofa still bore, beneath a holland
> cover, the shape of my uncle, who had died upon it. "You will sit very still, Eliza," said my aunt,
> "and you will be agreeable to Mr Pellow." I resolved to be agreeable. I did not then understand that
> Mr Pellow, who was fifty and owned three mills, had come to look at me as he looked at a horse he
> meant to buy; nor that my aunt, in drawing the blinds a little higher that afternoon, had arranged
> the light as carefully as any shopkeeper arranges his window.

**7.** How does the writer present Eliza's situation in this extract? Refer to narrative voice,
language and structure. **[10]**

**8.** How does the writer use the setting of the parlour in this extract? **[6]**

**9.** Explain how a context point could be woven into an answer on this extract. Write one sentence
of analysis that includes context, then explain why it works. **[3]**

**10.** Using your own studied novel, write a five-minute plan for this task: *Starting with an
extract of your choice, explore how the writer presents a central character's development.* Show your
argument, the extract details you would use and two further moments from the novel. **[6]**

**11.** Using your own studied novel: *Starting with a key extract of your choice, explore how the
writer presents a central character as changing over the course of the novel. Write about how the
writer presents this character in this extract, and how the writer presents this character in the
novel as a whole.* **[30]**

**12.** Using your own studied novel: *Starting with a key extract of your choice, explore how the
writer presents ideas about society or social class. Write about how the writer presents these ideas
in this extract, and how the writer presents them in the novel as a whole.* **[30]**

## Answers

**1.** A retrospective first-person narrator, an adult looking back on childhood [1]. Effect: the
phrase "as children will" shows the older narrator gently distancing herself from her younger
belief, creating a gap between what the child felt and what the adult now knows [1].

**2.** Free indirect style is third-person narration that takes on a character's own thoughts and
phrasing without quotation marks [1]. "Ruined, then, and by her own sister" is a fragment in Mrs
Bell's own voice rather than the narrator's [1]; "Well, she would not weep" captures her resolve in
her own idiom, so the reader shares her pride and shock directly while the third-person frame remains
[1].

**3.** Personification (the fog "wished to hear") combined with pathetic fallacy [1]. It makes the
setting seem tense and expectant, so the weather mirrors the anxious, oppressive atmosphere of the
courtroom and builds suspense before the verdict [1].

**4.** The semantic field is eating or digestion: "swallowed", "digested", "spat" [1]. It presents
the factory as a monster consuming the workers, suggesting that industrial labour uses people up and
treats them as material rather than human beings [1].

**5.** It names no specific method, explains no effect on meaning, and the judgement about difficulty
is not analysis [1]. Improved example: *"The long sentence piles clause upon clause, listing the
drawn blinds, the unwound clock and the covered sofa,"* [1] *"so the reader feels the parlour's
stillness accumulate until it seems airless."* [1]

**6.** A narrator whose account may be biased, limited or mistaken [1]. One signal: other characters'
words or events contradict the narrator, or the narrator admits they did not understand at the time
[1].

**7.** Award one mark per developed point, up to 10:

- The retrospective voice creates two Elizas: the girl who "took the privilege for a kindness" and the
  narrator who now knows better [1].
- "I did not then understand" signals dramatic irony; the reader is invited to see what the younger
  self missed [1].
- The aunt's commands, "You will sit very still... you will be agreeable", use the future tense as an
  order, showing Eliza's lack of choice [1].
- The short sentence "I resolved to be agreeable" shows her obedience; its brevity suggests how little
  room she has [1].
- The simile of a horse "he meant to buy" presents marriage as a purchase and Eliza as property [1].
- Mr Pellow's age and "three mills" are given together, showing that wealth, not affection, is his
  qualification [1].
- The shopkeeper simile presents the aunt as arranging Eliza for sale, so the closest family member
  becomes a seller [1].
- The structure saves these revelations for the final long sentence, so the extract moves from
  innocence to exposure [1].
- The tone is restrained and ironic rather than openly angry, which makes the criticism sharper [1].
- A plausible alternative reading: the aunt may be acting from economic necessity rather than cruelty,
  so the writer could be criticising the social system more than the individual [1].

**8.** Award one mark per developed point, up to 6:

- The parlour is "kept for visitors and for grief", linking it with display and death [1].
- Drawn blinds suggest darkness and enclosure, reflecting Eliza's confinement [1].
- The clock "never wound" suggests time has stopped, a household trapped in mourning [1].
- The covered shape of the dead uncle on the sofa is a disturbing image of absence and of a man who
  still dominates the room [1].
- The blinds being raised "a little higher" for Mr Pellow shows the room being staged, so setting
  becomes a tool of the aunt's plan [1].
- Overall, the setting presents the domestic space as a place of display and control rather than
  comfort [1].

**9.** Example sentence: *"By describing Mr Pellow's interest as that of a buyer at a horse sale, the
writer questions a society in which a young woman without fortune might depend on marriage for
security."* [1] It works because the context is attached to a specific image [1] and explains why the
writer presents the marriage this way, rather than giving general history [1].

**10.** Award marks as follows:

- A one-sentence argument that promises development across the novel [1].
- Three or four extract details linked to the character, including one on narrative voice [2].
- Two further moments, such as an earlier and a later point, each with a quotation or precise event
  [2].
- One context point linked to a specific choice [1].

**11.** AQA marks this holistically against level descriptors; the weighting is a guide only.

- **AO1 [12]:** a clear argument about how and why the character changes; references from the
  extract and from at least two other stages of the novel; a personal judgement, for example on
  whether the change is complete or convincing.
- **AO2 [12]:** analysis of narrative voice and how it shapes sympathy; language choices in the
  extract such as imagery, dialogue or sentence shape; structure, such as where change is triggered
  and how the ending reflects the beginning.
- **AO3 [6]:** context that explains the change, such as attitudes to class, gender, morality or
  religion relevant to the novel.

**12.** As for Question 11, marked holistically:

- **AO1 [12]:** an argument about the writer's view of society, developed across the novel, with
  precise references from the extract and elsewhere; recognition of more than one interpretation.
- **AO2 [12]:** analysis of how methods present class or society: contrasting settings and
  characters, dialogue that marks status, the narrator's irony or open comment, and structural
  patterns such as rises and falls in fortune.
- **AO3 [6]:** relevant context about class, wealth, poverty or social expectations in the period
  the novel was written, linked to specific moments.

## Using these questions well

Work through Questions 1 to 9 to sharpen narrative and language analysis on unfamiliar prose. Then
plan Question 10 in five minutes and write Question 11 or 12 in about 50 minutes without your text.
Afterwards, check whether each paragraph analysed a method and showed development; mark any paragraph
that only retold events and rewrite it.
