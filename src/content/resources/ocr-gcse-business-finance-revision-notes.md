## 5.1 The finance function

- **Purpose:** to provide financial information, and to support business planning and decision making.
- **Influence on business activity:** what finance reports affects what the other functions can do. Tight cash can limit marketing and recruitment; strong profit can allow expansion.
- Link every answer to a decision: finance information is useful because someone uses it to choose something.

## 5.2 Sources of finance

**Why finance is needed (the five reasons to learn):** establishing a new business; funding expansion; to run the business; recruitment; marketing.

| Source | One-line summary | Better suited to |
|---|---|---|
| Loan | Borrow a sum, repay with interest | Large, long-term needs; established firms or new ones with a plan |
| Overdraft | Spend beyond the balance up to a limit | Short-term gaps in cash |
| Trade credit | Pay suppliers later | Delaying cash outflow for stock |
| Retained profit | Profit kept in the business | Established businesses (new ones have none yet) |
| Sale of assets | Sell something owned | Raising cash without borrowing; asset is lost |
| Owners' capital | Owner's own money | New businesses and small top-ups |
| New partner | Another co-owner puts in money | Partnerships needing money and skills |
| Share issue | Sell shares to investors | Companies raising larger sums |
| Crowdfunding | Many small contributions, usually online | New ideas with public appeal |

**Matching method, in steps:**

1. Identify the need: how much, and for how long?
2. Identify the business: new (no profit history) or established (records, assets, profit)?
3. Pick the source that fits both.
4. Add a drawback, such as interest, lost control or a failed target.

## 5.3 Revenue, costs, profit and loss

| Term | Meaning or formula |
|---|---|
| Revenue | Selling price × quantity sold |
| Fixed costs | Do not change with output in the short term (rent) |
| Variable costs | Change with output (materials) |
| Total costs | Fixed costs + variable costs |
| Profit or loss | Revenue − total costs (a negative answer is a loss) |
| Gross profit | Revenue − cost of sales |
| Net profit | Gross profit − other business expenses |
| Gross profit margin | (gross profit ÷ revenue) × 100 |
| Net profit margin | (net profit ÷ revenue) × 100 |
| Average rate of return (ARR) | (average annual profit ÷ cost of investment) × 100 |

The specification gives the ratio names and ARR without formulas, and does not define cost of sales or contribution; the forms above are the standard ones.

**Quick reminder (margins).** Revenue £60,000; cost of sales £39,000; other expenses £15,000.

```
Gross profit = 60,000 - 39,000 = 21,000    margin = 21,000/60,000 x 100 = 35%
Net profit   = 21,000 - 15,000 =  6,000    margin =  6,000/60,000 x 100 = 10%
```

**Interpreting margins:**

- A higher margin means more profit from each pound of revenue.
- Compare with the previous year, a rival, or a target.
- If gross margin falls, look at cost of sales or pricing. If gross margin holds but net margin falls, look at other expenses.
- Percentage change = (change ÷ original) × 100.

**Quick reminder (ARR).** A £20,000 machine returns £32,500 in total over five years.

```
Total profit = 32,500 - 20,000 = 12,500
Average annual profit = 12,500 / 5 = 2,500
ARR = 2,500 / 20,000 x 100 = 12.5%
```

Interpretation: compare with other projects or with a target. Higher is better, but ARR does not allow for risk or for forecasts being wrong.

## 5.4 Break-even

- **Concept:** total costs = total revenue. No profit, no loss.
- **Contribution per unit** = selling price − variable cost per unit.
- **Break-even quantity** = fixed costs ÷ contribution per unit.
- Round up if the answer is not whole.

**Quick reminder.** Sedgeley Print Studio sells a pack for £6. Variable cost is £2.50. Fixed costs are £1,750 a month.

```
Contribution = 6 - 2.50 = 3.50
Break-even   = 1,750 / 3.50 = 500 packs
Sales above 500 earn £3.50 profit each; sales below 500 make a loss.
```

**Usefulness:** informs marketing and planning decisions (pricing, sales targets, whether a plan is realistic). **Limits:** assumes everything made is sold and that prices and costs stay constant.

## 5.5 Cash and cash flow

- **Importance of cash:** provides liquidity, so the business can meet short-term debts and expenses.
- **Cash versus profit:**

| Cash | Profit |
|---|---|
| Money actually received and paid | Revenue minus costs for a period |
| Changes when payment happens | Counted when the sale or cost occurs |
| Shortage stops bills being paid | Can look healthy even when cash is short |

- **Why a forecast is useful:** a planning tool; anticipates periods of cash shortage; enables remedies to be put in place; provides targets.

**Completing a forecast, in steps:**

1. Write the opening balance for the first month.
2. Total the cash in. Total the cash out.
3. Net cash flow = cash in − cash out.
4. Closing balance = opening balance + net cash flow.
5. Copy the closing balance to the next month's opening balance.
6. Look for negative balances, then suggest remedies.

**Quick reminder.** Opening £900, cash in £4,200, cash out £3,500: net cash flow = £700; closing balance = £1,600.

**Remedies for a shortage:** overdraft, longer trade credit from suppliers, chasing customers who owe money, delaying spending, selling an asset. Choose the one that fits the size and length of the gap.

## Command words and answer shape

- **State, identify, define:** a short answer, no explanation needed.
- **Calculate:** write the formula, substitute, give the answer with units or a percentage sign.
- **Explain:** make a point, then say why or what follows. Use the business named in the question.
- **Analyse:** break a situation into parts and link causes to effects, using the figures given.
- **Evaluate and justify:** give points on both sides, then reach a judgement and say why it wins. For a source of finance, weigh cost, speed, risk and control against the size and length of the need.
- **Recommend:** choose one option and support it with evidence from the case.

**Short reminder on a sources-of-finance answer.** A new nursery needs £6,000 for equipment. Owners' capital is quick and has no interest, but the owners may not have enough. A loan could cover the sum, but repayments start before many customers arrive. A judgement might favour a mix of owners' capital and a small loan, because the nursery has no retained profit yet.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Gross profit versus net profit:** net takes off other expenses as well as cost of sales.
- **Profit versus cash:** timing of payments separates them.
- **Fixed versus variable:** does the cost change when output changes?
- **Margin versus amount:** margin is a percentage of revenue, profit is a sum of money.
- **Contribution versus profit:** contribution is per unit before fixed costs.

## Quick self-test

1. State two purposes of the finance function.
2. Name three reasons a business may need finance.
3. Which source of finance best suits a temporary shortfall of cash?
4. A product sells for £12 with variable cost £7.50 per unit. Fixed costs are £2,700. Find the break-even quantity.
5. The business sells 700 units. Find its profit.
6. Revenue is £50,000 and gross profit £17,500. Find the gross profit margin.
7. Net profit is £4,000 on revenue of £50,000. Find the net profit margin.
8. An investment of £15,000 earns total profit of £6,750 over three years. Find ARR.
9. A business starts a month overdrawn by £300, receives £2,100 and pays out £1,500. What is the closing balance?
10. Explain why a profitable business can run short of cash.

### Answers

1. To provide financial information; to support planning and decision making.
2. Any three: establishing a new business, funding expansion, running the business, recruitment, marketing.
3. An overdraft, because it covers a short gap flexibly and is repaid as cash recovers. Trade credit is also a defensible answer, because it delays paying suppliers and so eases a temporary shortfall, but it only helps where suppliers agree and only for goods or services bought, whereas an overdraft can cover any bill. Either, with a reason, is fine.
4. Contribution = £4.50; 2,700 ÷ 4.50 = **600 units**.
5. Revenue £8,400; total costs £2,700 + (700 × £7.50) = £7,950; profit **£450**.
6. 17,500 ÷ 50,000 × 100 = **35%**.
7. 4,000 ÷ 50,000 × 100 = **8%**.
8. Average = 6,750 ÷ 3 = £2,250; 2,250 ÷ 15,000 × 100 = **15%**.
9. −300 + 2,100 − 1,500 = **£300**.
10. Sales on credit count as revenue now but bring cash later, while the business must pay wages and suppliers sooner. Profit can look healthy while cash is short.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using revenue instead of gross profit, or mixing up the two margins.
- Writing the break-even formula with selling price in place of contribution.
- No working shown for a calculation, so one slip loses everything.
- Leaving out the unit or the percentage sign.
- Not carrying the closing balance forward in a cash flow forecast.
- Naming a source of finance without saying why it suits this business.
- Describing cash and profit as the same thing.
- Calculating ARR with total profit instead of average annual profit.
- Giving the figure but no interpretation when the question says "interpret" or "analyse".
