This set covers section 3.3.7 (Optical isomerism, subsection 3.3.7.1) of OxfordAQA's International AS and A-level Chemistry specification (9620), Version 5.3, for International AS and A-level exams from May/June 2020. The whole section is International A2 content, so it is **International A-level only**. Unit 4 assesses sections 3.3.7 to 3.3.16, and the synoptic Unit 5 can draw on any content. Questions 6, 9 and 10 also use reactions from sections 3.3.4.2 and 3.3.8.1, and question 11 uses E–Z isomerism from 3.3.1.3.

Learn the ideas first in the [optical isomerism study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-optical-isomerism/) and the [revision notes](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-chemistry-optical-isomerism-revision-notes/). **Data for this set.** Relative atomic masses: H = 1.0, C = 12.0, N = 14.0, O = 16.0. 3D drawing key: plain line = bond flat on the page; ▼ = wedge, out of the page; ⋮ = hashed bond, into the page.

## Questions

**1.** The four dichloropropanes are 1,1-dichloropropane, 1,2-dichloropropane, 1,3-dichloropropane and 2,2-dichloropropane. Identify the one with an asymmetric carbon atom and give the four groups bonded to that carbon. **[2]**

**2.** **(a)** State what is meant by a racemic mixture. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain why a racemic mixture has no overall effect on plane-polarised light. **[2]**

**3.** Draw the structural formulas of the two enantiomers of 2-methylbutanoic acid, CH₃CH₂CH(CH₃)COOH, showing their 3D arrangement. **[2]**

**4.** Draw the displayed formulas of the two enantiomers of 2-chloropropan-1-ol, CH₃CHClCH₂OH. Show the 3D arrangement around the chiral carbon. **[3]**

**5.** The two enantiomers of a compound cannot be told apart by their melting points. Explain why, and state how they can be told apart. **[2]**

**6.** 2-methylbut-2-ene, (CH₃)₂C=CHCH₃, reacts with hydrogen bromide by electrophilic addition. Two products form.

**(a)** Name the major product and explain why it does not show optical isomerism. **[2]**
**(b)** The minor product is 2-bromo-3-methylbutane. Explain why it forms as a racemic mixture. **[3]**

**7.** Four carboxylic acids have the molecular formula C₅H₁₀O₂.

**(a)** Name the one that shows optical isomerism. **[1]**
**(b)** Give the total number of carboxylic acids with this formula, counting each enantiomer separately. Explain your answer. **[2]**

**8.** Three solutions of 2-methylbutanoic acid, P, R and S, have the same concentration. Under the same conditions, P rotates the plane of plane-polarised light by 8° clockwise, R rotates it by 8° anticlockwise and S does not rotate it.

**(a)** State the relationship between the compound in P and the compound in R. **[1]**
**(b)** Suggest what S contains and explain why it does not rotate the plane. **[2]**
**(c)** Equal volumes of P and R are mixed. Predict the effect of the mixture on plane-polarised light. **[1]**

**9.** A student adds an excess of KCN, followed by dilute acid, to 3.44 g of pentanal, CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂CHO.

**(a)** Give the name of the hydroxynitrile formed. **[1]**
**(b)** Explain why a racemic mixture of this product forms. **[3]**
**(c)** Calculate the maximum mass of product that could form. **[2]**
**(d)** The yield is 80.0%. Calculate the mass of each enantiomer obtained. **[1]**

**10.** Hexan-2-one, CH₃COCH₂CH₂CH₂CH₃, reacts with HCN to form a hydroxynitrile.

**(a)** Write an equation for this reaction, using structural formulas. **[1]**
**(b)** Name the hydroxynitrile formed. **[1]**
**(c)** Give the four groups bonded to the chiral centre in the product. **[1]**
**(d)** Explain how the reaction gives equal amounts of the two enantiomers. **[3]**
**(e)** A sample of the product made this way has no effect on plane-polarised light. A student concludes that the product has no chiral centre. Evaluate this conclusion. **[2]**

**11.** 3-chlorobut-1-ene, CH₂=CHCHClCH₃, and 1-chlorobut-2-ene, ClCH₂CH=CHCH₃, are structural isomers.

**(a)** Explain why 3-chlorobut-1-ene shows optical isomerism but not E–Z isomerism. **[2]**
**(b)** Explain why 1-chlorobut-2-ene shows E–Z isomerism but not optical isomerism. **[2]**
**(c)** State the total number of stereoisomers of the two compounds together. **[1]**

## Answers

**1.** **1,2-dichloropropane** [1]. C2 carries **H, Cl, CH₃ and CH₂Cl** [1] **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* 1,1-dichloropropane is the usual wrong pick; its C1 has two Cl atoms, so it cannot gain the first mark or the second.

**2. (a)** A mixture of **equal amounts** of the two enantiomers [1]
**(b)** The two enantiomers turn the plane of polarisation through equal angles but in **opposite senses** [1]. The amounts are equal, so the rotations **cancel** [1] **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* An answer to (b) that says the molecules "are not chiral" contradicts the definition in (a) and earns nothing.

**3.** C2 is the chiral centre (H, CH₃, C₂H₅, COOH). First enantiomer drawn in 3D, using two flat bonds plus a wedge and a hashed bond [1]; second enantiomer a true reflection of the first [1] **[2]**

```
             CH3          :          CH3
             ▼            :          ▼
    C2H5 --  C  --  COOH  : HOOC --  C  --  C2H5
             ⋮            :          ⋮
             H            :          H
```

*Examiner insight:* Any sensible 3D layout works, provided the second drawing is a true reflection; check that each group sits on the same type of bond in both.

**4.** C2 is the chiral centre (H, Cl, CH₃, CH₂OH) [1]. First enantiomer as a displayed formula with a wedge and a hashed bond at C2 [1]. Second enantiomer as its mirror image, also fully displayed [1] **[3]**

```
         H     Cl    H
         |     ▼     |
    H -- C --- C --- C -- O -- H
         |     ⋮     |
         H     H     H
```

```
              H     Cl    H
              |     ▼     |
    H -- O -- C --- C --- C -- H
              |     ⋮     |
              H     H     H
```

*Examiner insight:* Writing CH₃ or OH as a group anywhere in a displayed formula loses the drawing marks, even when the 3D arrangement is right.

**5.** The enantiomers have the same bonds and the same intermolecular forces, so they have identical melting points [1]. Plane-polarised light separates them: one turns its plane **clockwise** and the other **anticlockwise**, by equal angles [1] **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* "They react differently" is not a valid way to tell them apart here; the answer needs plane-polarised light.

**6. (a)** **2-bromo-2-methylbutane** [1]. Its C2 carries two CH₃ groups (with Br and C₂H₅), so it has no asymmetric carbon [1]
**(b)** The secondary carbocation (CH₃)₂CHC⁺HCH₃ has a flat (**planar**) arrangement around C⁺ [1]. Br⁻ may bond on the top face or the bottom face, and the two are **equally probable** [1]. The two enantiomers are therefore made in a **1:1 ratio** [1] **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* In (b), state where the plane is (the positive carbon); a vague "the molecule is flat" does not show which carbon becomes chiral.

**7. (a)** **2-methylbutanoic acid** [1]
**(b)** Pentanoic acid, 3-methylbutanoic acid and 2,2-dimethylpropanoic acid have no chiral centre; 2-methylbutanoic acid exists as two enantiomers [1]. Total = 4 + 1 = **5** [1] **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* The explanation earns its own mark here, so name the acids rather than writing a bare number.

**8. (a)** They are the two **enantiomers** of 2-methylbutanoic acid [1]
**(b)** S is a **racemic mixture** (equal amounts of both enantiomers) [1]. The equal and opposite rotations cancel [1]
**(c)** No overall rotation, because it contains equal amounts of the two enantiomers [1] **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* In (b), "S has no chiral centre" is wrong: all three solutions contain the same chiral compound.

**9. (a)** **2-hydroxyhexanenitrile**, CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂CH(OH)CN [1]
**(b)** In pentanal the C=O carbon and its three attached atoms lie in one **plane** [1]. The cyanide ion can add to this carbon from **above the plane or from below it** [1]. Neither route is favoured, so a **50:50 mixture** of the enantiomers results [1]
**(c)** Mr of pentanal = 86.0, so n = 3.44 / 86.0 = 0.0400 mol [1]. Mr of C₆H₁₁NO = 113.0, so maximum mass = 0.0400 × 113.0 = **4.52 g** [1]
**(d)** 0.800 × 4.52 = 3.616 g; each enantiomer = 3.616 / 2 = **1.81 g** [1] **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* Show both Mr values in (c); a clear line of working lets an examiner follow a slip, while a bare wrong answer earns nothing.

**10. (a)** CH₃COCH₂CH₂CH₂CH₃ + HCN → **CH₃C(OH)(CN)CH₂CH₂CH₂CH₃** [1]
**(b)** **2-hydroxy-2-methylhexanenitrile** [1]
**(c)** **OH, CN, CH₃ and CH₂CH₂CH₂CH₃** [1]
**(d)** The C=O carbon in hexan-2-one is trigonal **planar** [1]. CN⁻ approaches it from **one face or the other** [1]. Both faces are attacked **at the same rate**, giving equal quantities of each enantiomer [1]
**(e)** The conclusion is wrong: the product does have a chiral centre (C2) [1]. It forms as a racemate, so the equal and opposite rotations of the two enantiomers cancel [1] **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* For (e), an "evaluate" answer needs a judgement plus a reason; "the student is wrong" on its own earns nothing.

**11. (a)** C3 carries **H, Cl, CH₃ and CH=CH₂**, so it is a chiral centre [1]. the terminal CH₂= carbon has two identical H atoms, so E and Z forms are impossible [1]
**(b)** Rotation about the C=C is restricted and both alkene carbons have two unlike substituents (C2: H and CH₂Cl; C3: H and CH₃) [1]. No carbon has four different groups: C1 is CH₂Cl and C4 is CH₃ [1]
**(c)** 2 enantiomers + 2 E–Z isomers = **4** [1] **[5]**
*Examiner insight:* Deal with each type of stereoisomerism separately; one sentence covering both usually misses a reason for one of them.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Calling a carbon chiral when two of its groups are the same, such as C1 in 1,1-dichloropropane.
- Leaving "non-superimposable" out of the meaning of enantiomers.
- Saying a racemate "has no chiral molecules" instead of explaining that equal and opposite rotations cancel.
- Drawing structural formulas with every bond flat, so the two enantiomers look identical.
- Showing CH₃ or OH as groups in a "displayed formula".
- Explaining racemate formation without naming the planar carbon, the attack from either side and the equal chance of each.
- Treating the product of a symmetrical ketone or of methanal with KCN as chiral.
- Giving a bare total when counting isomers, with no list or reason.
- In yield calculations, halving the maximum mass but forgetting the percentage yield, or the other way round.
