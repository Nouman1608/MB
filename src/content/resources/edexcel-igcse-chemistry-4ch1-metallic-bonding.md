## The structure of a metal

In a solid metal, the atoms are packed closely together in a regular arrangement. Each atom's outer-shell electrons leave it and become **delocalised**: they are no longer attached to any one atom and can move throughout the whole structure. What remains of each atom is a **positive metal ion**. So a metal is a **giant lattice of positive ions surrounded by a "sea" of delocalised electrons**.

The number of delocalised electrons each atom gives equals its number of outer-shell electrons, which is the same as the charge on its ion:

| Metal | Outer electrons per atom | Ion in the lattice | Delocalised electrons per ion |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sodium (Group 1) | 1 | Na⁺ | 1 |
| Magnesium (Group 2) | 2 | Mg²⁺ | 2 |
| Aluminium (Group 3) | 3 | Al³⁺ | 3 |

The metal as a whole is neutral: the total negative charge of the delocalised electrons equals the total positive charge of the ions.

## Drawing the 2-D diagram (1.52C)

A good diagram has four features, and each is a common marking point.

1. **Positive ions in regular rows**, drawn as circles of equal size touching or nearly touching.
2. **A charge on each ion**, such as + for sodium or 2+ for magnesium.
3. **Electrons in the spaces between the ions**, shown as e⁻ or as small dots or crosses.
4. **Labels**: "positive metal ion" and "delocalised electron".

**Worked example 1.** Describe a 2-D diagram for aluminium that shows the correct ratio of electrons to ions.

```
a grid of 3 rows x 4 circles = 12 ions, each labelled 3+
between them: 12 x 3 = 36 electrons, labelled "delocalised electrons"
labels: "Al3+ ion" and "delocalised electron (e-)"
```

An exact electron count is not normally needed, but a diagram with many ions and only a few electrons, or electrons drawn inside the circles, misrepresents the structure. Never draw negative ions: there are none in a metal.

## Metallic bonding as electrostatic attraction (1.53C)

**Metallic bonding is the strong electrostatic attraction between the positive metal ions and the delocalised electrons.** The attraction acts in all directions throughout the lattice, so the bonding is not between particular pairs of atoms; it holds the whole structure together.

Compare the three types of bonding, which are all electrostatic:

| Bonding | Attraction between | Found in |
|---|---|---|
| Ionic | Positive ions and negative ions | Compounds of metals with non-metals |
| Covalent | A shared pair of electrons and the nuclei of two atoms | Non-metal elements and compounds |
| Metallic | Positive metal ions and delocalised electrons | Metals and alloys |

The strength of metallic bonding depends on the charge of the ions and the number of delocalised electrons. Magnesium (Mg²⁺, two delocalised electrons per ion) has stronger metallic bonding than sodium (Na⁺, one per ion), which is consistent with magnesium's much higher melting point.

## Explaining the properties of metals (1.54C)

### Electrical conductivity

**Model answer.** Explain why copper conducts electricity when solid. **[2]**

> Copper contains delocalised electrons [1]. These electrons are free to move through the structure and carry charge when a potential difference is applied [1].

The same explanation works for a molten metal, because the electrons are still delocalised. This is the key contrast with ionic compounds, which conduct only when molten or dissolved because there the charge carriers are ions.

### Malleability

**Malleable** means the metal can be hammered or bent into shape without breaking.

**Model answer.** Explain why metals are malleable. **[3]**

> The positive ions are arranged in layers [1]. When a force is applied, the layers can slide over each other [1]. The delocalised electrons still attract the ions in their new positions, so the metallic bonding is not broken and the metal does not shatter [1].

### Other typical properties

- **High melting and boiling points (most metals):** many strong electrostatic attractions between ions and delocalised electrons must be overcome, which needs a lot of energy.
- **Good thermal conductivity:** the delocalised electrons move through the metal and transfer energy quickly.

Sodium and potassium are exceptions with low melting points, consistent with having only one delocalised electron per ion.

### Linking to alloys

In an alloy, atoms of a different size disrupt the regular layers, so the layers cannot slide as easily and the alloy is harder than the pure metal. This is point 2.27C (also Paper 2 only), covered in the [extraction and uses of metals study guide](/resources/edexcel-igcse-chemistry-4ch1-extraction-and-uses-of-metals/).

## Identifying metallic substances from data

**Worked example 2.** Identify the type of bonding in each substance.

| Substance | Melting point / °C | Conducts as solid? | Conducts as liquid? |
|---|---|---|---|
| P | 1538 | Yes | Yes |
| Q | 801 | No | Yes |
| R | 114 | No | No |

```
P: conducts as a solid and a liquid, high mp -> metallic (delocalised electrons)
Q: conducts only when liquid, high mp        -> ionic (ions free to move when molten)
R: never conducts, low mp                    -> simple molecular (covalent)
```

The decisive clue for a metal is **conduction in the solid**, combined with evidence that it is not graphite (graphite is not malleable and is a non-metal).

## Common mistakes

- Drawing negative ions in a metallic lattice, or calling the electrons "free ions".
- Saying metals conduct because "ions move". In a metal it is the delocalised electrons.
- Writing "electrons are free" without saying they **move** and **carry charge**.
- Explaining malleability with "atoms slide" but leaving out why the metal does not break.
- Describing metallic bonding as "attraction between atoms" instead of between positive ions and delocalised electrons.

## Exam technique

- **Draw**: regular positive ions with charges, electrons between them, both labelled.
- **Explain** conductivity: delocalised electrons → move → carry charge.
- **Explain** malleability: layers → slide → electrons keep attracting ions → no fracture.
- **Compare** with another structure: give the charge carriers or forces in both substances.
