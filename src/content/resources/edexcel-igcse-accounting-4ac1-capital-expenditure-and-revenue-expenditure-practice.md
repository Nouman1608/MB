These questions cover section 2.4, Capital expenditure and revenue expenditure, of Topic 2 (Introduction to bookkeeping) in the Pearson Edexcel International GCSE in Accounting (4AC1) specification, Issue 1, October 2016. This is Paper 1 content. Businesses are invented; amounts are in dollars. Where depreciation is needed, a full year's charge is made in the year of purchase.

## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** State whether each item is capital expenditure or revenue expenditure.

**(a)** Fitting a security alarm to a newly bought shop before it opens. **[1]**
**(b)** Annual servicing of the delivery van. **[1]**
**(c)** Legal fees on the purchase of a warehouse. **[1]**
**(d)** Replacing a broken pane of glass in the shop window. **[1]**
**(e)** First year's insurance on a new machine. **[1]**
**(f)** Building a storeroom onto the existing premises. **[1]**

**2.** Explain the term revenue expenditure. **[2]**

**3.** Explain why the cost of delivering a new machine is treated as capital expenditure. **[2]**

**4.** A business buys a machine with a list price of 15,600, less 5% trade discount. Delivery costs 380, installation costs 520 and a one-year maintenance contract costs 640. Calculate the cost at which the machine is recorded. **[3]**

**5.** Distinguish between an improvement to a non-current asset and the maintenance of it, giving an example of each. **[2]**

### Section B: structured questions

**6.** Pinewood Joinery made these payments in the year.

| Item | Amount |
|---|---:|
| New lathe | 7,200 |
| Carriage on the lathe | 180 |
| Installation of the lathe | 420 |
| Servicing of existing machines | 650 |
| Extension to the workshop | 14,500 |
| Repainting the workshop | 1,300 |
| Legal fees on buying adjoining land | 900 |
| Wages of workshop staff | 22,000 |
| New office computer | 1,150 |
| Printer ink | 95 |

Calculate total capital expenditure and total revenue expenditure. **[6]**

**7.** Ridley Couriers reported a profit for the year of 41,000. A delivery van bought during the year for 18,000 was debited to motor expenses. Vans are depreciated at 20% a year on cost.

**(a)** Calculate the corrected profit for the year. **[3]**
**(b)** State the effect of the error on the statement of financial position at the year end. **[2]**
**(c)** State the effect on next year's profit if the error is not corrected. **[1]**

**8.** Talbot Print reported a profit of 33,500. A repair to a printing press costing 2,800 was debited to the machinery account, and depreciation of 10% on cost was charged on it along with the other machinery.

**(a)** Calculate the corrected profit. **[3]**
**(b)** State the effect of the error on non-current assets. **[2]**

**9.** Corbett Florists reported a profit of 27,600 before the following errors were found.

1. A refrigerated display cabinet costing 4,000 was debited to repairs. Display equipment is depreciated at 25% a year on cost.
2. Installation of the cabinet, 300, was debited to general expenses.
3. Repainting the shop, 1,200, was debited to the premises account. Premises are not depreciated.

**(a)** Calculate the corrected profit for the year. **[6]**
**(b)** Calculate the net effect of the corrections on the carrying value of non-current assets. **[2]**

**10.** Explain the importance of the correct treatment of capital expenditure and revenue expenditure. **[6]**

**11.** Elm Street Dental bought a computer system for 3,600 at the start of Year 1. It has a three-year life with no residual value and should be depreciated by the straight line method. The whole 3,600 was charged as an expense in Year 1.

**(a)** Calculate the effect of the error on profit in each of Years 1, 2 and 3. **[3]**
**(b)** The practice manager says: "The total profit over the three years is right, so the error does not matter." Explain why the manager is wrong. **[3]**

**12.** The owner of a small café suggests charging all spending, including new equipment, as an expense in the year it is paid, "because it is simpler and makes profit look lower for tax". Evaluate this suggestion. **[6]**

## Answers

**1.** (a) Capital: making the new shop ready for use [1]. (b) Revenue [1]. (c) Capital [1]. (d) Revenue: restores existing condition [1]. (e) Revenue [1]. (f) Capital [1]. **[6]**

**2.** Spending on the day-to-day running of the business, including keeping non-current assets in their existing condition [1]; it is charged in full as an expense in the income statement of the period in which it is incurred [1]. **[2]**

**3.** Delivery is a cost of bringing the machine to its place of use so that it can operate [1], so it forms part of the machine's cost and benefits all the periods in which the machine is used [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Price after trade discount = 15,600 × 95% = 14,820 [1]. Add delivery and installation: 14,820 + 380 + 520 [1] = **15,720** [1]. The maintenance contract is revenue expenditure. **[3]**

**5.** An improvement makes the asset better than its original condition, for example adding a more powerful engine to a van [1]; maintenance keeps it in its existing condition, for example replacing worn tyres [1]. **[2]**

**6.** Capital: lathe 7,200, carriage 180, installation 420 [1]; extension 14,500 [1]; legal fees 900 and computer 1,150 [1]. Total capital **24,350** [1]. Revenue: servicing 650, repainting 1,300, wages 22,000, printer ink 95 [1]. Total revenue **24,045** [1]. **[6]**

**7. (a)** Add back the van wrongly expensed: 41,000 + 18,000 [1]. Deduct depreciation 18,000 × 20% = 3,600 [1]. Corrected profit = **55,400** [1].
**(b)** Non-current assets understated by the van's carrying value of 18,000 − 3,600 = 14,400 [1]; capital is understated by the same 14,400 [1].
**(c)** Next year's profit would be overstated by 3,600, as no depreciation would be charged on the van [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Adding back 18,000 without charging depreciation is the most common slip.

**8. (a)** Expense omitted: deduct 2,800 [1]. Depreciation wrongly charged: 2,800 × 10% = 280, add back [1]. Corrected profit = 33,500 − 2,800 + 280 = **30,980** [1].
**(b)** Machinery is overstated at cost by 2,800 [1] and at carrying value by 2,800 − 280 = 2,520 [1]. **[5]**

**9. (a)** Cabinet cost = 4,000 + 300 = 4,300 [1]. Add back items wrongly expensed: 4,000 + 300 [1]. Depreciation = 4,300 × 25% = 1,075 [1]. Repainting is an expense: deduct 1,200 [1]. Corrected profit = 27,600 + 4,000 + 300 − 1,075 − 1,200 [1] = **29,625** [1].
**(b)** Cabinet added at carrying value 4,300 − 1,075 = 3,225; premises reduced by 1,200 [1]. Net increase = **2,025** [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* The installation must be added to the cabinet's cost before calculating depreciation.

**10.**
- If capital expenditure is treated as revenue, profit for the year and non-current assets are both understated [1].
- If revenue expenditure is treated as capital, profit and non-current assets are both overstated [1].
- Depreciation follows the asset, so later years' profits are also wrong [1].
- Capital (equity) is misstated because profit is added to it [1].
- Users are misled: the owner may draw too much, a lender may misjudge security, and the tax authority receives a wrong profit figure [1].
- The accruals concept requires costs to be matched with the periods that benefit, so year-on-year comparisons stay fair [1]. **[6]**

**11. (a)** Correct depreciation = 3,600 ÷ 3 = 1,200 a year. Year 1: profit understated by 3,600 − 1,200 = **2,400** [1]. Year 2: overstated by **1,200** [1]. Year 3: overstated by **1,200** [1].
**(b)** Each year's statements are used separately [1]; Year 1 profit appears lower and Years 2 and 3 higher than they really were, so trends are distorted [1], and the computer is missing from the statement of financial position in Years 1 and 2, understating assets [1]. **[6]**

**12.** Up to 6 marks:
- It is simpler: no asset records or depreciation calculations are needed [1].
- For small, low-value items, charging as an expense is acceptable on grounds of materiality [1].
- But for major items, profit in the year of purchase would be understated [1] and profits in later years overstated, because no depreciation would be charged [1].
- Non-current assets would be missing from the statement of financial position, misleading lenders and the owner [1].
- Conclusion: the suggestion should be rejected for material items. It breaks the accruals concept and gives a misleading profit to the tax authority and other users; only trivial items should be expensed [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Credit the one valid point (materiality for small items) before rejecting the idea. A one-sided answer rarely reaches the top marks.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Leaving delivery or installation out of an asset's cost, or adding insurance to it.
- Correcting a misclassified asset without charging depreciation.
- Forgetting to add back depreciation wrongly charged on a capitalised repair.
- Giving the effect on profit but not on assets or capital.
- Writing "the accounts are wrong" without saying which figure and in which direction.
- Evaluations with no conclusion.

## Frequently asked questions

**How do I correct profit when an asset was charged as an expense?**
Add back the cost that was wrongly expensed, then deduct the depreciation that should have been charged for the year.

**Can a small item of equipment be treated as revenue expenditure?**
Yes, if it is not material. Low-value items are often expensed for simplicity, provided the policy is applied consistently.
