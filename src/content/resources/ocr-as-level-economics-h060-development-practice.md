## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Define sustainable development. **[2]**

**2.** Which of the following is a tertiary sector activity?

A: Coal mining

B: Car assembly

C: Insurance

D: Fishing **[1]**

**3.** Country S employs 1.8 million workers in the primary sector, 2.7 million in the secondary sector and 4.5 million in the tertiary sector. Calculate the tertiary sector's share of employment. **[1]**

**4.** Which of the following is **not** one of the three dimensions of the Human Development Index?

A: Life expectancy

B: Years of schooling

C: Income per head

D: Income inequality **[1]**

**5.** Use this simplified method: each index = (actual − minimum) ÷ (maximum − minimum), with goalposts of 20 to 85 years for life expectancy, 0 to 18 for expected years of schooling and 0 to 50,000 for income per head, and the simplified HDI is the simple average of the three indices. Country T has a life expectancy of 66.8 years, 12.6 expected years of schooling and income per head of 14,000. Calculate its simplified HDI. **[3]**

**6.** State two limitations of GDP per capita as a measure of development. **[2]**

### Section B: structured and extended questions

**7.** The table shows the share of output (%) by sector in invented Country U.

| Year | Primary | Secondary | Tertiary |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2005 | 34 | 24 | 42 |
| 2015 | 26 | 31 | 43 |
| 2025 | 15 | 29 | 56 |

**(a)** Calculate the change in the tertiary sector's share of output between 2005 and 2025. **[1]**

**(b)** Describe how the structure of Country U's economy changed between 2005 and 2025. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain two reasons why the share of the tertiary sector tends to rise as incomes rise. **[4]**

**8.** Invented Country V has GDP per capita of 24,000 and an HDI of 0.71. Invented Country W has GDP per capita of 16,000 and an HDI of 0.78.

**(a)** Calculate how much higher GDP per capita is in Country V than in Country W, as a percentage. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain two reasons why Country W might have a higher HDI than Country V despite its lower GDP per capita. **[4]**

**9.** Explain how economic growth based on depleting natural resources might conflict with the objective of sustainable development. **[4]**

**10.** Evaluate the usefulness of the HDI as a measure of development. **[6]**

**11.** Evaluate whether economic growth is compatible with sustainable development. **[10]**

## Answers

**1.** Development that meets the needs of the present generation [1] without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs [1]. **[2]**

**2.** **C** [1]. A and D are primary activities and B is secondary. **[1]**

**3.** Total = 9.0 million, so 4.5 ÷ 9.0 × 100 = **50%** [1]. **[1]**

**4.** **D** [1]. The HDI uses average income per head; inequality is left out. **[1]**

**5.** Health index = 46.8 ÷ 65 = 0.720 and education index = 12.6 ÷ 18 = 0.700 [1]. Income index = 14,000 ÷ 50,000 = 0.280 [1]. Simplified HDI = (0.720 + 0.700 + 0.280) ÷ 3 = 1.700 ÷ 3 = **0.567** [1]. **[3]**

**6.** It is an average, so it hides inequality [1]. It leaves out unpaid work and the informal economy [1]. Also accept: it counts pollution and resource depletion as gains, or it ignores leisure, health and wellbeing. **[2]**

**7. (a)** 56 − 42 = **14 percentage points** [1].

**(b)** The primary share fell sharply, from 34% to 15% [1]. The secondary share rose to 31% by 2015 and then fell slightly, while the tertiary share rose to 56%, so the economy industrialised and then moved towards services [1].

**(c)** Rising productivity in farming and manufacturing means fewer workers are needed to produce food and goods [1], so labour is released to services [1]. As incomes rise, spending on food rises less than in proportion while many services, such as health, leisure and finance, are income elastic [1], so demand shifts towards the tertiary sector [1]. **[7]**
*Examiner insight:* A change in a share is in percentage points. Writing "14%" for part (a) loses the mark.

**8. (a)** (24,000 − 16,000) ÷ 16,000 × 100 = **50%** [1].

**(b)** Country W may spend a larger share of its income on public health care and schooling [1], so life expectancy and years of schooling are higher, raising two of the three HDI dimensions [1]. In Country V, income may be unequally distributed, for example if it comes from oil exports owned by a few [1], so many households have poor health and education despite the high average income [1]. **[5]**

**9.** Using up natural resources, such as clearing forest or over-fishing, raises output and GDP now [1]. But these are stocks of natural capital that regrow slowly or not at all [1], so future generations will have fewer resources to produce output and meet their needs [1]. Measured growth therefore overstates the lasting gain, and it may also bring pollution and lost biodiversity, which conflict with the environmental strand of sustainable development [1]. **[4]**

**10.** Award up to 6 marks.
- The HDI is broader than GDP per capita: it adds health and education, so it shows whether income is turned into better lives [1].
- It shows that countries with similar incomes can have very different development, which helps target policy [1].
- But it leaves out inequality, so a country where a few gain can score well [1].
- It ignores the environment, so it cannot show whether development is sustainable, and it leaves out freedom and political rights [1].
- Its equal weights are a choice, and years of schooling say nothing about the quality of education [1].
- Judgement: the HDI is a better single guide to development than GDP per capita, but it is most useful alongside indicators of inequality and the environment [1]. **[6]**

**11.** Award up to 10 marks across knowledge, application, analysis and evaluation.
- Economic growth is a rise in real GDP; sustainable development meets present needs without harming future generations' ability to meet theirs [1].
- Growth raises incomes and tax revenue, which fund health, education and clean water, the social strand [1].
- Growth reduces absolute poverty, which is central to development in low-income countries [1].
- Richer economies can afford cleaner technology and stricter regulation, so pollution may fall at higher incomes [1].
- But growth based on resource extraction runs down natural capital, so output today cuts output in the future [1].
- Production raises pollution and carbon emissions, which harm future generations [1].
- Relative decoupling, a fall in emissions per unit of output, still lets total emissions rise; only absolute decoupling cuts them [1].
- The gains from growth may be unequal, so social progress lags behind GDP [1].
- Judgement: growth and sustainable development are compatible only under some conditions [1], such as pricing pollution, protecting natural capital and sharing the gains widely, and for very poor countries growth may be the fastest route to better health and education [1]. **[10]**
*Examiner insight:* The best answers make the judgement depend on the type of growth and the policies that go with it, rather than saying growth is simply good or bad.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Treating growth and development as the same thing.
- Writing per cent for a change in a share instead of percentage points.
- Defining sustainable development only in environmental terms.
- Describing the HDI without its limitations.
- Ending an evaluation without saying what the answer depends on.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why can a country with higher GDP per capita have a lower HDI?**
The HDI also measures health and education. If income is unequally shared or little is spent on public services, life expectancy and schooling can be low even when average income is high.

**Is sustained growth the same as sustainable growth?**
No. Sustained growth continues over a long period. Sustainable growth does not run down the resources and environment that future generations need.
