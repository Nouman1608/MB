## The extract

> *(An original extract written for this page.)*
>
> The city announced itself first as a smell: diesel, bread, the sea somewhere behind the buildings.
> Nadia stood at the top of the station steps with her suitcase and her uncle's address written on the
> back of a bus ticket. She had practised the street name on the train until it sounded like a word she
> owned. Now, saying it to a man at the newspaper stand, she heard it come out wrong.
>
> "Again?" he said, not unkindly.
>
> She said it again. He pointed, and she thanked him twice, which was once too many; she could see that
> in his face. It would be weeks before she understood how much a single thank-you could weigh here. For
> now she picked up the case and walked into the smell, as if it were a door.

## Section A: short questions

**1.** Identify the narrative viewpoint of the extract and explain one effect of it. **[2]**

**2.** Explain how the first sentence uses grammatical structure to create an impression of the city.
**[2]**

**3.** Analyse the phrase "a word she owned". **[2]**

**4.** Identify an example of free indirect discourse and explain its effect. **[3]**

**5.** What does the sentence "It would be weeks before she understood how much a single thank-you could
weigh here" add to the narration? **[3]**

**6.** Comment on the function of the short line of dialogue, "Again?". **[2]**

**7.** Explain how the ending of the extract links back to its opening. **[2]**

**8.** A Section B statement reads: *"The novel is less interested in where its characters come from
than in who they become."* Write a one-sentence thesis responding to this statement for **any** novel
you have studied, defining one of its key terms. **[2]**

## Section B: statement essays

Each essay is marked out of 25 (AO1, AO2, AO3). In your answer you must consider relevant contextual
factors.

**9. *Brooklyn*.** "Eilis's life is shaped more by other people's decisions than by her own." Evaluate
this view. **[25]**

**10. *The Kite Runner*.** "The novel suggests that redemption is always possible." Evaluate this view.
**[25]**

**11. *Life of Pi*.** "In *Life of Pi*, the better story matters more than the true one." Evaluate this
view. **[25]**

**12. *Purple Hibiscus*.** "Silence is the most powerful force in the novel." Evaluate this view.
**[25]**

**13. *The White Tiger*.** "*The White Tiger* is a comic novel about a tragic society." Evaluate this
view. **[25]**

## Answers

### Section A

**1.** Third person, closely tied to Nadia's perception (close third person) (1). Effect, for example:
the reader shares her sensory impressions and embarrassment while the narrator can still comment from
a distance (1).

**2.** The city is the grammatical subject that "announced itself" (1), and the colon introduces a list
of smells, so the city seems to act on Nadia before she can act on it, overwhelming her senses (1).

**3.** The metaphor suggests she has tried to make the new place hers through language (1); "owned"
implies belonging and control, which the next sentence undercuts when the word "came out wrong" (1).

**4.** Example: "which was once too many; she could see that in his face" (1). The narration slips into
Nadia's own self-conscious judgement (1), so the reader experiences her embarrassment from the inside
while the sentence remains in the third person (1).

**5.** It is a prolepsis or flash-forward (1): the narrator knows more than Nadia does now (1), which
creates dramatic irony and hints at the social codes of the new place that she will have to learn (1).

**6.** Its brevity shows the gap between them (1); "not unkindly" stops the reader judging the man,
so the difficulty is presented as cultural misunderstanding rather than hostility (1).

**7.** The extract returns to "the smell" from the opening (1), and the simile "as if it were a door"
turns the overwhelming city into an entrance, marking a shift from intimidation to tentative courage (1).

**8.** One mark for a clear position on the statement (1); one for defining a key term ("where they come
from" as family, class or homeland; "who they become" as moral or social change) (1).

### Section B: how to mark questions 9 to 13

Marked holistically on AO1, AO2 and AO3. The bands below are Marlbridge's illustrative guidance, not
Pearson's published mark scheme.

| Marks | Typical features |
|---|---|
| 21–25 | Sustained, evaluative argument on the statement; perceptive analysis of narrative methods; context integrated to explain choices |
| 16–20 | Clear argument; detailed analysis; relevant context linked to the text |
| 11–15 | Some argument; explanation of methods; context present but sometimes general |
| 6–10 | Largely narrative, with some comment on methods; context bolted on |
| 1–5 | Mainly retelling |

**9. *Brooklyn*.**
- AO1: others arrange Eilis's emigration and her work; her choices often emerge as reactions; the
  ending can be read as her first real decision or as one forced on her.
- AO2: restrained close third person; much of her feeling is implied; the structure of departure,
  settlement and return places the key choice at the end.
- AO3: limited opportunities in 1950s Ireland; the Church's role in emigration; expectations of
  women's duty to family.

**10. *The Kite Runner*.**
- AO1: Amir's attempt to make amends through Sohrab; whether atonement is complete or only partial;
  what redemption costs others.
- AO2: retrospective first-person narration shaped by guilt; parallels and echoes between childhood
  and adult scenes; the final kite-flying scene as a tentative, not complete, recovery.
- AO3: Afghanistan's history from the 1970s to Taliban rule; the persecution of Hazaras; the novel's
  wide popular reception and debate about its coincidences as part of reception.

**11. *Life of Pi*.**
- AO1: the novel invites the reader to prefer the story with animals, but the second version
  questions what the first conceals.
- AO2: the frame narrator; Pi's first-person account; the three-part structure; the interview
  transcript and the question of which story is better.
- AO3: religious pluralism and faith; postmodern interest in storytelling and truth; reception
  including the novel's 2002 Booker Prize and its later film adaptation.

**12. *Purple Hibiscus*.**
- AO1: Kambili's silence under her father's control; speech, laughter and freedom in Aunty Ifeoma's
  home; Mama's silent endurance and its final, violent end.
- AO2: Kambili's constrained first-person voice; the non-chronological structure from Palm Sunday;
  the purple hibiscus as a symbol of freedom; contrasts between the two households.
- AO3: political instability and military rule in Nigeria; the suppression of the press; Catholicism
  and Igbo tradition.

**13. *The White Tiger*.**
- AO1: the humour and energy of Balram's voice set against poverty, servitude and murder; whether the
  comedy makes the society seem more or less tragic.
- AO2: epistolary form addressed to the Chinese Premier; satire and irony; the Rooster Coop metaphor;
  the contrast of "Darkness" and "Light".
- AO3: India's economic growth and inequality; class and servant culture; reception, including the
  2008 Booker Prize and debates over the novel's portrait of India.
