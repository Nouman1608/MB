This guide covers **Global Climate: Vulnerability and Resilience**,
part of the compulsory SL and HL core (Geographic perspectives: global
change) for IB Diploma Programme Geography, first assessment 2019. The
[full syllabus guide](/resources/ib-dp-geography-syllabus-guide/)
confirms this core is identical in content and hours for every
student, unlike the seven teacher-selected geographic-themes options.

## Where this fits in the syllabus

Because the geographic-themes options vary by school, revision
material on an unstudied option is wasted effort. The core is
different: every DP Geography student, regardless of school, studies
Global Climate as part of the shared 70-hour core, examined in Paper 2
for everyone without exception. This makes it one of the
highest-value topics to revise thoroughly, since unlike an option, it
applies universally.

## Syllabus coverage

**IB DP GEOGRAPHY — SL AND HL CORE: GLOBAL CLIMATE — VULNERABILITY AND
RESILIENCE**

- **Vulnerability** — the degree to which a place, community or system
is susceptible to harm from a climate hazard, shaped by three named
components: exposure, sensitivity and adaptive capacity
- **Resilience** — the capacity of a place or community to absorb,
recover from, and adapt to climate-related disturbance
- **Adaptation** — deliberate changes made in response to actual or
expected climate impacts (for example, flood defences, drought-
resistant crops)
- **Mitigation** — action taken to reduce the causes of climate change
itself, distinct from adaptation's focus on managing its effects

At HL, this core vocabulary connects directly to Global risks and
resilience, part of the HL-only extension examined in Paper 3, which
applies the same vulnerability-and-resilience concepts to
interconnected global systems more broadly rather than climate
specifically.

## How to approach it

Keep adaptation and mitigation clearly separate — a common error is
using the two terms interchangeably, when the specification treats
them as distinct types of response with different targets (mitigation
targets the cause, adaptation targets the effect). When analysing why
two places facing a similar climate hazard experience different
outcomes, always name all three components of vulnerability explicitly
(exposure, sensitivity, adaptive capacity) rather than simply
asserting that one place is "more vulnerable" — a strong Paper 2
answer treats vulnerability as the combination of these three factors,
not the size of the hazard alone. As with every DP Geography theme,
generic description scores lower than named, specific case studies
with real dates, locations and figures, so build a small bank of
specific vulnerability and resilience examples — a named flood event,
drought, or heatwave with its human and policy response — that can be
reused flexibly across different Paper 2 questions.

## Worked example: comparing vulnerability across two places

A question asks why two places facing a similar climate hazard (for
example, flooding) might experience very different outcomes.

```
Exposure:           both places face a similar physical hazard
                     (rising flood risk)

Sensitivity:         Place A has more people living in low-lying,
                     poorly built housing; Place B has stronger
                     building codes and better drainage infrastructure

Adaptive capacity:   Place A has fewer economic resources and weaker
                     institutions to respond; Place B has greater
                     financial and institutional capacity to invest in
                     defences and recovery

Conclusion:          vulnerability is not just about the size of the
                     hazard but the combination of exposure,
                     sensitivity and adaptive capacity -- two places
                     facing an identical hazard can have very
                     different outcomes
```

Naming all three components explicitly, rather than only describing
one place as "more vulnerable" than the other, is what distinguishes a
strong Paper 2 answer from a purely descriptive one.

## Connecting to the fieldwork report

Because the internally assessed fieldwork report can be based on any
suitable syllabus topic, Global Climate is a viable choice where local
conditions allow a genuinely testable question -- for example,
investigating a community's perceived vulnerability or adaptive
responses to a local climate hazard. Where fieldwork engages with this
core theme, use its specific vocabulary (exposure, sensitivity,
adaptive capacity) to frame both the investigation's question and its
analysis, since this demonstrates direct application of taught core
content rather than a generic geographic inquiry that happens to
mention climate.

## Common mistakes

Confusing adaptation (managing a hazard's effects) with mitigation
(reducing the hazard's underlying causes). Describing vulnerability in
general terms without naming its three components. Using vague,
unnamed examples such as "a developing country" instead of a specific,
named place and event with real figures. Assuming this theme is
optional, or blending it into a geographic-themes option answer
without being explicit about which paper's content is being used —
Paper 1 (options) and Paper 2 (core) are marked against different
prescribed content even where the underlying geography is related.

## Quick revision checklist

- Learn the three named components of vulnerability (exposure,
  sensitivity, adaptive capacity) and be able to apply all three to an
  unfamiliar scenario.
- Keep adaptation and mitigation precisely distinct.
- Build a bank of specific, named case studies with real dates,
  locations and figures.
- If your class studies Extreme environments or Geophysical hazards,
  practise distinguishing option-specific points from core Global
  Climate points.
- HL: connect this core vocabulary forward to the Global risks and
  resilience extension content.

## Why resilience is more than the absence of vulnerability

A frequent conceptual slip is treating resilience simply as "low
vulnerability" -- the specification treats them as related but
distinct ideas. A place can reduce its exposure or sensitivity to a
hazard (lowering vulnerability) while separately building the
institutional and economic capacity to recover quickly if a disaster
does occur (building resilience); strong Paper 2 answers can discuss
a place's response along both dimensions rather than assuming that
addressing one automatically addresses the other. This distinction
matters most in comparative questions, where a place might be less
vulnerable in the moment a hazard strikes but recover more slowly
afterwards than a place with different characteristics -- a nuance
that only becomes visible once resilience is treated as its own
concept rather than a mirror image of vulnerability.
