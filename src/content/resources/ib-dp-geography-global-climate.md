This guide covers **Global Climate: Vulnerability and Resilience**,
part of the compulsory SL and HL core (Geographic perspectives: global
change) for IB Diploma Programme Geography, first assessment 2019. The
[full syllabus guide](/resources/ib-dp-geography-syllabus-guide/)
confirms this core is identical in content and hours for every
student, unlike the seven teacher-selected geographic-themes options.

## Where this fits in the syllabus

Because the geographic-themes options vary by school, revision
material on an unstudied option is wasted effort. The core is
different: every DP Geography student, regardless of school, studies
Global Climate as part of the shared 70-hour core, examined in Paper 2
for everyone without exception. This makes it one of the
highest-value topics to revise thoroughly, since unlike an option, it
applies universally.

## Syllabus coverage

**IB DP GEOGRAPHY — SL AND HL CORE: GLOBAL CLIMATE — VULNERABILITY AND
RESILIENCE**

The IB Geography guide (first assessment 2019) divides this unit into
three sub-topics:

1. **Causes of global climate change** — how natural and human
processes affect the global energy balance. This covers the
atmospheric system, including the natural greenhouse effect and energy
balance (incoming shortwave radiation and outgoing longwave radiation),
and changes in the global energy balance, and the role of feedback
loops, resulting from factors that include solar radiation variations
(including global dimming due to volcanic eruptions) and terrestrial
albedo changes; the guide's list of factors continues beyond these.
In brief: greenhouse gases absorb some of the longwave radiation the
Earth's surface emits and re-emit it, warming the lower atmosphere;
human activity that raises greenhouse gas concentrations enhances this
effect; volcanic aerosols reflect incoming sunlight, causing temporary
cooling; and melting ice lowers albedo, so more energy is absorbed and
warming increases further — a positive feedback loop.
2. **Consequences of global climate change** — the effects of climate
change. Commonly studied effects include sea-level rise, changing
rainfall patterns, more frequent or intense extreme weather such as
heatwaves, and knock-on impacts on ecosystems, farming, water supply
and human health.
3. **Responding to global climate change** — possibilities for
responding to climate change and power over the decision-making
process, including disparities in exposure to climate change risk and
vulnerability (variations in people's location, wealth, social
differences such as age, gender and education, and risk perception),
detailed examples of two or more societies with contrasting
vulnerability, government-led adaptation and mitigation strategies
(global geopolitical efforts, recognising that the sources of
greenhouse gas emissions may be spatially distant from the countries
most impacted; carbon emissions offsetting and trading; and technology,
including geo-engineering), and civil society and corporate strategies
to address global climate change, with a case study of the response to
climate change in one country focusing on the actions of
non-governmental stakeholders.

Key vocabulary for the responding sub-topic:

- **Vulnerability** — the degree to which a place, community or system
is susceptible to harm from a climate hazard. This resource breaks it
into three parts — exposure, sensitivity and adaptive capacity — a
framework widely used in climate-change research (for example in IPCC
assessment reports). It is this resource's analytical tool, not the IB
guide's wording: the guide does not use the terms sensitivity or
adaptive capacity.
- **Resilience** — the capacity of a place or community to absorb,
recover from, and adapt to climate-related disturbance
- **Adaptation** — deliberate changes made in response to actual or
expected climate impacts (for example, flood defences, drought-
resistant crops)
- **Mitigation** — action taken to reduce the causes of climate change
itself, distinct from adaptation's focus on managing its effects

At HL, this core vocabulary connects directly to Global risks and
resilience, part of the HL-only extension examined in Paper 3, which
applies the same vulnerability-and-resilience concepts to
interconnected global systems more broadly rather than climate
specifically.

## How to approach it

Keep adaptation and mitigation clearly separate — a common error is
using the two terms interchangeably, when they are distinct types of
response with different targets (mitigation targets the cause,
adaptation targets the effect). When analysing why two places facing a
similar climate hazard experience different outcomes, break
vulnerability down rather than simply asserting that one place is
"more vulnerable": tie your points to the factors the guide names
(location, wealth, social differences such as age, gender and
education, and risk perception), and use a framework such as this
resource's exposure, sensitivity and adaptive capacity to organise
them, so that vulnerability is treated as more than the size of the
hazard alone. Do not skip the causes and consequences sub-topics: be
able to explain the energy balance, the natural greenhouse effect and
at least one feedback loop, not only responses. As with every DP Geography theme,
generic description scores lower than named, specific case studies
with real dates, locations and figures, so build a small bank of
specific vulnerability and resilience examples — a named flood event,
drought, or heatwave with its human and policy response — that can be
reused flexibly across different Paper 2 questions.

## Worked example: comparing vulnerability across two places

A question asks why two places facing a similar climate hazard (for
example, flooding) might experience very different outcomes.

```
Exposure:           both places face a similar physical hazard
                     (rising flood risk)

Sensitivity:         Place A has more people living in low-lying,
                     poorly built housing; Place B has stronger
                     building codes and better drainage infrastructure

Adaptive capacity:   Place A has fewer economic resources and weaker
                     institutions to respond; Place B has greater
                     financial and institutional capacity to invest in
                     defences and recovery

Conclusion:          vulnerability is not just about the size of the
                     hazard but the combination of exposure,
                     sensitivity and adaptive capacity -- two places
                     facing an identical hazard can have very
                     different outcomes
```

Working through a structure like this — and linking each part to the
guide's named factors, such as wealth or location — gives a more
analytical answer than only describing one place as "more vulnerable"
than the other. The framework is not the guide's own; the guide asks
for detailed examples of two or more societies with contrasting
vulnerability, so apply it to real, named places.

## Connecting to the fieldwork report

This unit is **not** a good basis for the internally assessed
fieldwork report. The IB guide lists "Unit 2: Global climate
vulnerability and resilience" among the topics "unlikely to be suitable
for fieldwork investigation", and states that global issues are
unsuitable for study unless they can be adapted to the local scale.
Choose a fieldwork topic that allows a genuinely testable question
with primary data collected locally, and check your choice with your
teacher.

## Common mistakes

Confusing adaptation (managing a hazard's effects) with mitigation
(reducing the hazard's underlying causes). Describing vulnerability in
general terms without breaking it down into the factors that make one
place or group more vulnerable than another. Presenting exposure,
sensitivity and adaptive capacity as IB-specified terms rather than as
an analytical framework. Revising only responses and neglecting the
causes (energy balance, greenhouse effect, feedback loops) and
consequences sub-topics. Using vague,
unnamed examples such as "a developing country" instead of a specific,
named place and event with real figures. Assuming this theme is
optional, or blending it into a geographic-themes option answer
without being explicit about which paper's content is being used —
Paper 1 (options) and Paper 2 (core) are marked against different
prescribed content even where the underlying geography is related.

## Quick revision checklist

- Revise all three sub-topics: causes (energy balance, natural
  greenhouse effect, feedback loops), consequences, and responding.
- Know the guide's factors behind disparities in exposure and
  vulnerability: location, wealth, social differences (age, gender,
  education) and risk perception.
- Prepare detailed examples of two or more societies with contrasting
  vulnerability.
- Prepare a case study of one country's response to climate change
  focusing on the actions of non-governmental stakeholders (civil society
  and corporate strategies).
- Use this resource's exposure, sensitivity and adaptive capacity
  framework to organise vulnerability analysis, remembering it is a
  research framework rather than the guide's own terms.
- Keep adaptation and mitigation precisely distinct.
- Build a bank of specific, named case studies with real dates,
  locations and figures.
- If your class studies Extreme environments or Geophysical hazards,
  practise distinguishing option-specific points from core Global
  Climate points.
- HL: connect this core vocabulary forward to the Global risks and
  resilience extension content.

## Why resilience is more than the absence of vulnerability

A frequent conceptual slip is treating resilience simply as "low
vulnerability" -- they are related but distinct ideas. A place can reduce its exposure or sensitivity to a
hazard (lowering vulnerability) while separately building the
institutional and economic capacity to recover quickly if a disaster
does occur (building resilience); strong Paper 2 answers can discuss
a place's response along both dimensions rather than assuming that
addressing one automatically addresses the other. This distinction
matters most in comparative questions, where a place might be less
vulnerable in the moment a hazard strikes but recover more slowly
afterwards than a place with different characteristics -- a nuance
that only becomes visible once resilience is treated as its own
concept rather than a mirror image of vulnerability.
