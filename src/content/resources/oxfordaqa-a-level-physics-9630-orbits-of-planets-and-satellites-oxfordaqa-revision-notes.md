These notes condense section **3.7.4 Orbits of planets and satellites** of the OxfordAQA International AS and A-level Physics (9630) specification: "orbital period and speed related to radius of circular orbit", "energy considerations for an orbiting satellite" and the "significance of a geosynchronous orbit". Section 3.7 is International A-level only.

For explanations and worked examples, read the [orbits of planets and satellites study guide](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-physics-9630-orbits-of-planets-and-satellites-oxfordaqa/). Test yourself with the [orbits practice questions](/resources/oxfordaqa-a-level-physics-9630-orbits-of-planets-and-satellites-oxfordaqa-practice/).

Data: G = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N m² kg⁻²; GM(Earth) = 3.98 × 10¹⁴ N m² kg⁻¹; Earth radius 6.37 × 10⁶ m.

## Core recall

- In a circular orbit, gravity provides the centripetal force: GMm/r² = mv²/r.
- The satellite's mass cancels: the orbit depends only on M and r.
- v = √(GM/r): larger orbits are slower.
- T² = (4π²/GM) r³: larger orbits take longer.
- Total energy E = −GMm/(2r) is negative; Ek = GMm/(2r); Ep = −GMm/r.
- Moving to a higher orbit needs energy, yet the satellite ends up slower.
- Geosynchronous: period equals the Earth's rotation period. Geostationary: also equatorial and in the direction of rotation, so it stays above one point.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Orbital radius r | Distance from the centre of the central body to the satellite |
| Orbital period T | Time for one complete orbit |
| Orbital speed v | Speed along the circular path, 2πr/T |
| Bound orbit | Orbit with negative total energy; the satellite cannot escape without extra energy |
| Geosynchronous orbit | Orbit with period equal to the Earth's rotation period |
| Geostationary orbit | Geosynchronous, circular, equatorial, same direction as the rotation |
| Low orbit | Orbit close to the surface: short period, high speed |

## Equations

| Equation | Use |
|---|---|
| v = √(GM/r) | Orbital speed |
| T = 2πr/v | Period from speed |
| T² = (4π²/GM) r³ | Period and radius |
| M = 4π²r³/(GT²) | Mass of the central body |
| Ek = GMm/(2r) | Kinetic energy in orbit |
| Ep = −GMm/r | Potential energy |
| E = −GMm/(2r) | Total energy |

## Ratios

| Radius change | Period | Speed | Total energy |
|---|---|---|---|
| r × 4 | × 8 | × 1/2 | × 1/4 (closer to zero) |
| r × 9 | × 27 | × 1/3 | × 1/9 |
| r × 2 | × 2.83 | × 0.707 | × 1/2 |

## Graphs

- T² against r³: straight line through the origin, gradient 4π²/GM.
- log T against log r: straight line, gradient 1.5.

## Energy in three statements

1. Ep = −2Ek and E = −Ek.
2. Higher orbit: E rises (less negative), Ep rises, Ek falls.
3. Circular orbit: speed constant, gravity does no work (force at 90° to velocity).

Launch energy (ignoring air and rotation): E(orbit) − E(surface) = −GMm/(2r) + GMm/R.

## Geosynchronous orbit: significance

- Radius about 4.2 × 10⁷ m from the Earth's centre (about 3.6 × 10⁷ m above the surface), the same for every satellite.
- Ground dishes point in a fixed direction; no tracking needed.
- Continuous coverage of one region: communications, broadcasting, weather.
- Limitations: signal delay of about 0.24 s up and down; poor coverage near the poles; too distant for detailed imaging.

## Quick reminders with numbers

- r = 7.0 × 10⁶ m: v = 7.54 km s⁻¹.
- Planet mass from a moon at 5.8 × 10⁸ m with T = 6.4 days: 3.8 × 10²⁶ kg.
- 500 kg launched to r = 7.0 × 10⁶ m: about 1.7 × 10¹⁰ J needed.

## Exam traps

- r is from the centre, not the altitude.
- Convert T to seconds.
- Satellite mass never affects v or T.
- Higher orbit: more total energy but less speed.
- Geosynchronous is not automatically geostationary.
- Do not say gravity does work in a circular orbit.
- "Period of one day" needs the link to the Earth's rotation.

## Self-test

1. Derive v = √(GM/r) for a circular orbit.
2. Find the orbital speed at r = 8.0 × 10⁶ m around the Earth.
3. The radius of an orbit is multiplied by 9. By what factor does the period change?
4. Why does a heavier satellite not need a different speed for the same orbit?
5. A moon orbits at 1.9 × 10⁹ m with period 16 days. Find the planet's mass.
6. Give the gradient of a log T against log r graph.
7. State the total energy of a 650 kg satellite at r = 1.2 × 10⁷ m around the Earth.
8. The satellite in question 7 moves to r = 2.4 × 10⁷ m. Find the energy that must be supplied.
9. What happens to its speed in question 8?
10. Define a geosynchronous orbit.
11. State two advantages of a geostationary satellite for communications.
12. Calculate the minimum time for a signal to travel from the ground to a geostationary satellite 3.58 × 10⁷ m up and back down.

### Answers

1. GMm/r² = mv²/r, so **v² = GM/r** and v = √(GM/r).
2. v = √(3.98 × 10¹⁴/8.0 × 10⁶) = **7.1 × 10³ m s⁻¹**.
3. 9^1.5 = **27**.
4. The satellite's mass **cancels** from both sides of GMm/r² = mv²/r.
5. M = 4π²(1.9 × 10⁹)³/(6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ × (1.38 × 10⁶)²) = **2.1 × 10²⁷ kg**.
6. **1.5**.
7. E = −3.98 × 10¹⁴ × 650/(2 × 1.2 × 10⁷) = **−1.08 × 10¹⁰ J**.
8. E(new) = −5.39 × 10⁹ J, so **5.4 × 10⁹ J** must be supplied.
9. It **falls**, from 5.76 to 4.07 km s⁻¹ (by a factor of √2).
10. An orbit whose **period equals the Earth's rotation period**.
11. **Fixed ground dishes**, no tracking; **continuous coverage** of the same region.
12. t = 2 × 3.58 × 10⁷/3.00 × 10⁸ = **0.24 s**.
