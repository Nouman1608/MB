## What moves in a progressive wave

In a **progressive wave**, energy is transferred from one place to another,
but the medium is not. Each particle oscillates about its own equilibrium
position, and each particle starts its oscillation slightly after the one
before it. A rope flicked at one end, a stretched spring pushed and pulled
along its length, and the water surface in a ripple tank all show this: a
ribbon tied to the rope, a marked coil of the spring or a floating cork moves
to and fro, while the disturbance travels along.

## The vocabulary, made precise

| Term | Meaning | Unit |
|---|---|---|
| Displacement | Distance of a particle from its equilibrium position, in a stated direction (can be negative) | m |
| Amplitude | Maximum displacement from the equilibrium position | m |
| Period T | Time for one complete oscillation of a particle | s |
| Frequency f | Number of complete oscillations per unit time; f = 1/T | Hz |
| Wavelength λ | Distance between two adjacent points oscillating in phase | m |
| Speed v | Distance travelled by the wave energy per unit time | m s⁻¹ |
| Phase difference | Fraction of a cycle by which one oscillation leads or lags another, as an angle | ° or rad |

Amplitude is measured from the equilibrium position, not from crest to
trough: the crest-to-trough distance is twice the amplitude.

## Phase difference

Phase difference compares two oscillations of the same frequency. One whole
cycle is 360° or 2π rad.

**Between two points on the same wave**, a distance ∆x apart along the
direction of travel:

```
phase difference = (dx / lambda) x 360 degrees = (dx / lambda) x 2 pi rad
```

**Between the same point at two times** ∆t apart:

```
phase difference = (dt / T) x 360 degrees
```

Points one wavelength apart are **in phase** (0° or 360°); points half a
wavelength apart are in **antiphase** (180°, π rad).

**Worked example.** A wave of frequency 50 Hz travels along a rope at
12 m s⁻¹. Find the phase difference between two points 0.18 m apart.

```
lambda = v / f = 12 / 50 = 0.24 m
dx / lambda = 0.18 / 0.24 = 0.75 of a cycle
phase difference = 0.75 x 360 = 270 degrees  (3 pi / 2 rad)
```

A phase difference of 270° is the same relationship as one oscillation
lagging the other by 90°. Either answer is correct if you state which point
leads.

## Using a cathode-ray oscilloscope

A CRO plots voltage (vertically) against time (horizontally).

- The **y-gain** (V per division) converts vertical divisions into voltage.
- The **time-base** (s per division) converts horizontal divisions into time.

To find **amplitude**, measure the peak height from the centre line, in
divisions, and multiply by the y-gain. Measuring peak-to-peak and halving is
more precise, because both edges of the trace are used. To find
**frequency**, measure several complete cycles across the screen, divide to
get the period in divisions, multiply by the time-base, and take f = 1/T.

**Worked example.** With the time-base at 0.20 ms div⁻¹, 2.5 complete cycles
fill 10.0 divisions. The y-gain is 5.0 mV div⁻¹ and the trace is 6.0 divisions
from peak to trough.

```
period = 10.0 / 2.5 = 4.0 div  ->  4.0 x 0.20 ms = 0.80 ms
f = 1 / 0.80 x 10^-3 = 1250 Hz
amplitude = (6.0 / 2) x 5.0 mV = 15 mV
```

**Two traces on one screen.** If two signals of the same frequency are shown
together, the horizontal offset between corresponding peaks gives the phase
difference. With a period of 6.0 divisions and peaks 1.5 divisions apart, the
phase difference is 1.5/6.0 × 360° = **90°**.

## Deriving v = fλ

The syllabus asks you to derive the wave equation **from the definitions of
speed, frequency and wavelength**:

```
1. In one period T, the source completes one oscillation and the wave
   advances by one wavelength lambda.
2. speed = distance / time = lambda / T
3. frequency f = 1 / T
4. so v = f lambda
```

The equation applies to every progressive wave, mechanical or
electromagnetic. The speed is set by the medium; the frequency is set by the
source. If the frequency of the source is doubled in the same medium, the
speed stays the same and the wavelength halves.

## Energy transfer and intensity

A progressive wave transfers energy, and the rate at which it arrives through
a surface is described by **intensity**:

```
intensity I = power / area        unit: W m^-2
I proportional to (amplitude)^2
```

Intensity is measured on an area **perpendicular to the direction of travel**.

**Worked example — a solar panel.** Sunlight of intensity 800 W m⁻² falls
normally on a panel of area 1.6 m². Power received = intensity × area =
800 × 1.6 = **1280 W**.

**Worked example — spreading from a point source.** A small loudspeaker emits
0.80 W of sound equally in all directions. At a distance r the power is spread
over a sphere of area 4πr².

```
at 5.0 m:  I = 0.80 / (4 pi x 5.0^2) = 2.5 x 10^-3 W m^-2
at 10 m:   I = 0.80 / (4 pi x 10^2)  = 6.4 x 10^-4 W m^-2  (a quarter)
```

Doubling the distance quarters the intensity. Because I ∝ A², the amplitude
at 10 m is **half** the amplitude at 5.0 m.

**Amplitude scaling.** If the amplitude rises from 2.0 mm to 3.0 mm, the
intensity is multiplied by (3.0/2.0)² = 2.25.

## Exam technique

- **Define wavelength** using "adjacent points in phase"; "the length of one
wave" is not credited.
- **Derive v = fλ**: state that the wave moves one wavelength in one period,
then use the definitions in order.
- **Determine from a CRO**: show divisions, the setting used and the unit
conversion on separate lines.
- **Calculate a phase difference**: give the unit (° or rad) and, when asked,
which oscillation leads.

## Common mistakes

- Measuring amplitude from crest to trough without halving.
- Reading the period from a displacement–distance graph: that axis gives the
wavelength.
- Forgetting to convert ms div⁻¹ into seconds before finding f.
- Taking intensity as proportional to amplitude rather than amplitude squared.
- Saying the medium moves along with the wave.

## Quick revision checklist

- Particles oscillate about fixed positions; energy, not matter, is
transferred
- Definitions of displacement, amplitude, period, frequency, wavelength,
speed and phase difference
- Phase difference = (∆x/λ) × 360° or (∆t/T) × 360°
- CRO: y-gain for amplitude, time-base for period and frequency
- Derivation of v = fλ
- Intensity = power/area and intensity ∝ amplitude²; a point source spreads its power over 4πr²

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the difference between displacement and amplitude?**
Displacement is the distance from equilibrium at any instant and can be
positive or negative. Amplitude is the maximum displacement.

**Does a wave's speed change if the frequency increases?**
Not in the same medium. The speed depends on the medium, so the wavelength
decreases instead.

**How does intensity change if the amplitude doubles?**
It is multiplied by four, because intensity is proportional to amplitude
squared.
