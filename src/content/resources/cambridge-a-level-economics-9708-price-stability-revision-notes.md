These notes condense section **4.6 Price stability** of the Cambridge International AS & A Level Economics 9708 syllabus (examination in 2026, 2027 and 2028), AS content in Topic 4. For explanations and worked examples, use the [price stability study guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-economics-9708-price-stability/); for exam-style questions, use the [price stability practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-economics-9708-price-stability-practice/).

## Definitions (4.6.1)

- **Inflation:** a sustained rise in the general price level.
- **Deflation:** a sustained fall in the general price level (negative inflation rate).
- **Disinflation:** a fall in the inflation rate while it stays positive; prices still rise, more slowly.

A fall in inflation from 5% to 3% is a fall of **2 percentage points**; prices are still rising.

## The CPI (4.6.2)

1. Household expenditure survey finds a representative basket.
2. Categories are **weighted** by their share of spending.
3. Prices collected; category indices calculated.
4. CPI = weighted average; base year = 100.

```
Weighted % change = sum of (weight x % price change of category)
Inflation rate    = (new CPI - old CPI) / old CPI x 100
```

**Quick check (invented).** Weights 0.5, 0.3, 0.2; price changes +8%, +2%, -5%. Inflation = 4.0 + 0.6 - 1.0 = 3.6%.

**Difficulties:** no household is typical; weights go out of date; substitution to cheaper goods; quality changes; new products; informal markets and regional differences.

**Reading a series (invented CPI).** 100 -> 103 -> 105 -> 104. Inflation: 3.0%, then (105 - 103) / 103 x 100 = 1.9% (disinflation), then (104 - 105) / 105 x 100 = -1.0% (deflation). Only the last year shows falling prices.

## Nominal and real (4.6.3)

```
Real value = nominal value / price index x 100
Real growth is approximately nominal growth - inflation
Real interest rate is approximately nominal interest rate - inflation
```

- Nominal: in current prices. Real: adjusted for price changes, so it reflects quantities or purchasing power.
- Nominal wage up 5% with inflation 7%: real wage down about 2%.
- Nominal GDP 880 with a price index of 110: real GDP = 880 / 110 x 100 = 800 in base-year prices.

## Causes (4.6.4)

| | Demand-pull | Cost-push |
|---|---|---|
| Cause | AD grows faster than capacity | Production costs rise |
| Curve | AD right | SRAS left |
| Output | Rises | Falls |
| Examples | Tax cuts, credit boom, export boom, more government spending | Wages above productivity, dearer energy or materials, weaker currency, higher indirect taxes |

**Diagnosis:** prices up with output and employment up -> demand-pull. Prices up with output down -> cost-push.

**Imported cost-push chain:** the currency falls -> imported fuel, food and components cost more in domestic currency -> firms' costs rise -> SRAS shifts left -> higher price level and lower real output. Workers may then seek higher wages to protect real pay, which raises costs again.

## Consequences (4.6.5)

- **Losers:** fixed-income earners, savers with interest below inflation, lenders at fixed rates.
- **Gainers:** borrowers with fixed repayments (real debt falls).
- **Firms:** menu costs; uncertainty that can delay investment.
- **Households:** shoe-leather costs of managing money.
- **Trade:** exports less price competitive if inflation exceeds that of trading partners.

**Evaluation:** harm depends on whether inflation is **anticipated**, its **rate** and its **stability**. Low, stable inflation does least damage.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| General price level | The average level of prices across the economy |
| Consumer price index | A weighted index of the prices of a basket of goods and services bought by households |
| Weight | A category's share of household spending in the index |
| Base year | The year in which the index is set to 100 |
| Nominal value | A value measured in the prices of the time |
| Real value | A value adjusted for changes in the price level |
| Demand-pull inflation | Inflation caused by aggregate demand rising faster than capacity |
| Cost-push inflation | Inflation caused by rising costs of production |
| Menu costs | Costs to firms of changing prices |
| Shoe-leather costs | Time and effort spent managing money when inflation is high |

## Exam traps

- "Inflation fell, so prices fell": only true if inflation is negative.
- Confusing an index level with an inflation rate.
- Using the new index as the base in a percentage change.
- Treating nominal increases as real increases.
- Showing cost-push as an AD shift.
- Saying inflation harms everyone; borrowers can gain.

## Self-test

1. Define inflation.
2. Inflation falls from 3.2% to 1.1%. Name this and state what happens to prices.
3. CPI rises from 120 to 126. Calculate inflation.
4. CPI then rises from 126 to 129.15. Calculate inflation and name the change from Question 3.
5. Why are categories weighted in the CPI?
6. Give two difficulties in measuring inflation with a CPI.
7. Nominal GDP is 660; the price index is 110. Calculate real GDP.
8. A savings account pays 3% and inflation is 5%. Give the approximate real interest rate.
9. A doubling of world oil prices raises prices and cuts output. Which type of inflation?
10. Rising consumer confidence leads to faster spending and price rises. Which curve shifts?
11. Who gains from unanticipated inflation?
12. Why is unanticipated inflation more harmful than anticipated inflation?

**Answers**

1. A sustained rise in the general price level.
2. Disinflation; prices are still rising, but more slowly.
3. (126 - 120) / 120 x 100 = 5.0%.
4. (129.15 - 126) / 126 x 100 = 2.5%; disinflation.
5. So that each category affects the index in proportion to its share of household spending.
6. Any two: no typical household; weights go out of date; substitution; quality changes; new products; informal markets.
7. 660 / 110 x 100 = 600.
8. About -2%.
9. Cost-push.
10. AD shifts right (demand-pull).
11. Borrowers with fixed repayments (and others whose income rises faster than prices).
12. Contracts, wages and interest rates are set on wrong expectations, so purchasing power is redistributed unexpectedly and planning becomes harder.
