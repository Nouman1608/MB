This guide covers **Introduction to microeconomics**, the first content section of Component 01, Microeconomics, in the OCR *A Level Economics (H460)* specification, Version 3.0 (June 2026), for first assessment in 2021. The section has three topics: 1.1 The economic problem, 1.2 The allocation of resources and 1.3 Opportunity cost. Most students can define its terms; marks are lost on its **evaluate** outcomes, which ask you to judge ideas rather than describe them. This guide concentrates on those judgements, using invented examples throughout.

## 1. Evaluating scarcity and the requirement to make choices

Scarcity exists because wants are unlimited while the resources to satisfy them are limited, so every agent must choose. To evaluate, ask **how binding** the constraint is and **what kind** of constraint it is.

- **Scarcity is relative, not absolute.** A wealthy household still faces scarcity of time; a poor household faces scarcity of basic needs. The problem is universal, but its consequences differ sharply, which is why "for whom" matters.
- **Scarcity changes over time.** Technology can ease it (more output from the same inputs), while depletion and pollution can tighten it. A free good, such as clean air in an uncrowded place, can become an economic good once demand for it outstrips what is available without sacrifice.
- **Choices are forced at every level,** but the quality of the choice depends on information. A government choosing between two projects needs reliable estimates of their benefits; without them, scarcity is managed badly rather than escaped.

**Worked example 1: a town council (invented).** A council has 6.0 million currency units (cu) of capital spending. A new library costs 4.0 million, a flood barrier 3.5 million and a sports hall 2.0 million. It cannot afford all three (9.5 million). It can afford the library and the sports hall (6.0 million), or the flood barrier and the sports hall (5.5 million, leaving 0.5 million unused), but not the library and the barrier together (7.5 million). Choosing the library and sports hall means the barrier is forgone. The evaluation question is not whether to choose but on what basis: expected benefit per cu, who benefits, and the risk the barrier would have reduced.

## 2. Evaluating rationality

The specification asks you to evaluate **rationality as a way of understanding the behaviour of economic agents**. The assumption is that agents weigh costs and benefits and choose the option that best meets their objective: households maximise utility, firms maximise profit, and governments maximise social welfare.

**Strengths of the assumption**

- It generates **testable predictions**. If the price of a product rises, a rational consumer buys less of it, other things equal. Most market data support this.
- It gives a **benchmark**. Even if individuals err, the model shows the best available choice, against which actual outcomes can be compared.
- In aggregate, random individual errors may cancel out, so the model can predict market behaviour well even when some individuals behave oddly.

**Limitations**

- **Information is imperfect.** A consumer comparing 40 mobile phone tariffs (invented) with different allowances, contract lengths and charges may not be able to identify the cheapest one.
- **Calculation takes time.** Agents often use rules of thumb, such as "buy the brand I bought last time".
- **Habit and emotion** influence many purchases.
- **Objectives may not be self-interested.** People give to charity and act on values.

**Judgement.** Rationality is most useful for predicting the *direction* of responses to price changes in markets with frequent, simple purchases. It is least reliable for rare, complex decisions, such as choosing a pension, where information problems are greatest.

## 3. Evaluating the different objectives of economic agents

| Agent | Usual objective in the model | Why it may differ in practice |
|---|---|---|
| Households | Maximise utility | Limited information; concern for others |
| Firms | Maximise profit | Managers may pursue growth or sales; social enterprises aim at welfare |
| Government | Maximise social welfare | Different groups gain and lose; political pressures; short time horizons |

Objectives **conflict**. A firm maximising profit may pollute a river that households value; a government raising taxes to fund public services lowers households' disposable income. Evaluation means asking whose objective dominates in a given market and what limits it, such as competition, regulation or public opinion. Firms' objectives are developed further in the Business objectives section of the specification.

## 4. Evaluating the effectiveness of incentives

An **incentive** is something that motivates an agent to act in a particular way, such as a price, a wage, a tax, a subsidy or a fine.

**Worked example 2: a bottle deposit (invented).** A city adds a 0.20 cu refundable deposit to each plastic drinks bottle. Before the scheme, 55% of bottles were returned for recycling; a year later the figure is 80%.

- Rise in the return rate: 80 − 55 = 25 percentage points.
- For 10 million bottles a year, returns rise from 5.5 million to 8.0 million, an extra 2.5 million.
- Unreturned deposits fall from 4.5 million × 0.20 = 0.9 million cu to 2.0 million × 0.20 = 0.4 million cu.

The incentive worked because the reward was clear, immediate and large relative to the effort of returning a bottle. Effectiveness generally depends on:

- the **size** of the incentive relative to the cost of changing behaviour;
- **information**: agents must know about it and understand it;
- **time**: some behaviour, such as commuting habits, changes slowly;
- **unintended consequences**: a reward per item returned can encourage fraud if bottles from outside the scheme can be claimed;
- the **availability of alternatives**: people respond to a tax on a product only if substitutes exist.

## 5. Efficiency and evaluating allocation in different systems

**Productive efficiency** means producing at the lowest possible cost, so the economy is on its PPC. **Allocative efficiency** means producing the combination of goods that best matches what society wants: no reallocation could make someone better off without making someone else worse off. A point on the PPC can be productively efficient yet allocatively inefficient if it produces too much of a good people value little.

When evaluating systems, judge each against both kinds of efficiency and against equity.

- **Market systems** use prices as signals and profit as an incentive, which pushes firms towards productive efficiency. Allocation reflects willingness *and ability* to pay, so it can neglect people on low incomes and under-provide some goods.
- **Planned systems** can direct resources to priorities and share output more equally, but planners lack the price signals that reveal consumers' wants, and weak incentives can reduce productive efficiency.
- **Mixed systems** combine the two. The live debate in any real economy is the *degree* of state involvement in particular markets, such as health or energy, not the choice between pure systems.

## 6. Opportunity cost and its usefulness

**Opportunity cost** is the value of the next best alternative forgone; a **trade-off** is the need to give up some of one thing to get more of another. On a PPC, a movement along the curve shows the opportunity cost of reallocating resources; a shift shows a change in productive capacity.

**Worked example 3: a firm's own land (invented).** A firm owns a plot it uses as a staff car park. It could rent the plot to a developer for 50,000 cu a year. Accounting records show no cost for the car park, but its opportunity cost is 50,000 cu a year. If the staff value the parking at less than that, the firm would gain by renting the land and paying staff a travel allowance instead.

**Why the concept is useful**

- It reveals **hidden costs** that accounts miss, such as the owner's own time or the earnings from assets already owned.
- It frames every choice as a comparison, which is the core of economic reasoning.
- It explains specialisation and the shape of the PPC.

**Its limitations**

- The next best alternative can be hard to identify and harder to value, especially for non-market items such as leisure or environmental quality.
- Valuations are subjective; two agents may disagree on the same opportunity cost.
- Agents often ignore it in practice, which links back to the limits of rationality.

## Common mistakes

- Defining opportunity cost as "all the alternatives given up", rather than the next best one.
- Treating rationality as either fully right or useless, instead of judging where it predicts well.
- Saying incentives "always work" without the conditions that make them effective.
- Confusing productive efficiency (lowest cost) with allocative efficiency (the right mix of goods).
- Comparing pure market and planned systems without reference to real mixed economies.

## Exam technique

- **State, Identify, Describe:** short, precise definitions. Use the specification's wording.
- **Explain:** give the mechanism, for example how a price signal changes behaviour.
- **Compare** and **Distinguish between:** cover both items in the same sentence or paragraph.
- **Evaluate:** apply at least two "it depends" factors, such as information, time or the size of an incentive, and reach a reasoned judgement about the case in the question.
