## Key definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Pure monopoly | A single seller facing the whole market demand curve |
| Price maker | A firm that chooses its price because its demand curve slopes down |
| Monopoly power | The ability to hold price above marginal cost; a matter of degree |
| Barrier to entry | A cost or rule that blocks or discourages new firms from entering |
| Supernormal profit | Profit above the minimum needed to keep the firm in the market (price above AC) |
| Price discrimination | Charging different prices for the same product where the difference is not due to cost |
| Natural monopoly | A market where one firm's average cost falls over the whole range of demand |

## The monopoly diagram: method

1. Draw downward-sloping **AR** (demand). Draw **MR** below it, twice as steep if demand is a straight line.
2. Draw **MC** (and AC). Mark output where **MR = MC**.
3. Go up to AR to read **price**. Go to AC to read **average cost**.
4. **Supernormal profit** = (price − AC) × output.
5. Label each axis and curve, and name the equilibrium output and price.

Key facts:
- MR = 0 at the output where total revenue is highest and PED = 1.
- Profit-maximising output is always on the elastic part of demand.
- A linear demand curve P = a − bQ gives MR = a − 2bQ.

### Worked reminder (illustrative)

Demand P = 80 − 4Q, so MR = 80 − 8Q. MC = £16, fixed costs £100.
- 80 − 8Q = 16, so **Q = 8**; P = 80 − 32 = **£48**.
- AC = 16 + 100/8 = £28.50; profit = (48 − 28.50) × 8 = **£156**.
- PED = (1/4)(48/8) = **1.5** (elastic, as expected).

### Second worked reminder: revenue and elasticity (illustrative)

Demand P = 96 − 6Q, so MR = 96 − 12Q.

| Q | P (£) | TR (£) | MR between rows (£) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | 84 | 168 | |
| 4 | 72 | 288 | 60 |
| 6 | 60 | 360 | 36 |
| 8 | 48 | 384 | 12 |
| 10 | 36 | 360 | −12 |

- TR rises while MR is positive and peaks at Q = 8 (MR = 0, PED = 1). Beyond that, MR is negative.
- With MC = £24, MR = MC gives 96 − 12Q = 24, so Q = 6 and P = £60.
- PED at Q = 6 is (1/6)(60/6) = 1.67, so demand is elastic where the monopolist operates.
- Quick test: if the stated MR at an output is below MC, cut output; if above, expand.

## Factors influencing monopoly power

| Factor | Effect on power |
|---|---|
| Barriers to entry (legal, scale economies, sunk costs, control of inputs, brands) | Stop rivals entering, so supernormal profit lasts |
| Number of competitors | Fewer rivals mean fewer substitutes and less elastic demand |
| Advertising | Builds loyalty and lowers elasticity; can be a sunk-cost barrier |
| Product differentiation | Fewer close substitutes and a steeper demand curve |

**Chain of reasoning for a barrier (use in 4-6 mark answers):** high sunk costs → rivals fear unrecoverable losses → no entry → the firm keeps its market share and its supernormal profit → it can hold price above marginal cost.

**Chain for differentiation:** distinctive product → few close substitutes → less elastic demand → a price rise loses fewer sales → larger mark-up.

## Price makers in monopolistic competition and oligopoly

- Both face downward-sloping demand, so both choose price using MR = MC.
- The degree of power differs: close substitutes (many rivals) or rivals' reactions (few rivals) limit the mark-up.
- Rule: **the more elastic the demand, the smaller the gap between price and MC.**

### Worked reminder: two price makers (illustrative)

Both firms have MC = £6. Firm S faces P = 30 − 2Q (MR = 30 − 4Q); Firm T faces P = 18 − Q (MR = 18 − 2Q).
- S: 30 − 4Q = 6, Q = 6, price = £18, mark-up £12 (66.7% of price), PED = (1/2)(18/6) = 1.5.
- T: 18 − 2Q = 6, Q = 6, price = £12, mark-up £6 (50% of price), PED = (1)(12/6) = 2.0.
- Firm S has more monopoly power because its demand is less elastic.
- Oligopolists and monopolistic competitors are price makers, but rivals limit how high the mark-up can go.

## Monopoly: advantages and disadvantages

| Disadvantages | Advantages |
|---|---|
| Higher price, lower output than a competitive market with the same costs | Economies of scale can lower costs enough to beat the competitive price |
| Price above MC (allocative inefficiency) | Supernormal profit can fund research and investment (dynamic efficiency) |
| Possible X-inefficiency | Natural monopoly avoids wasteful duplication |
| Income moves from consumers to owners | Patent reward encourages invention |
| Less choice or slower improvement | Scale may help international competitiveness |

**Cost-saving test (illustrative).** With demand P = 50 − 2Q and a competitive MC of £30, competition gives P = £30. The monopoly price is (50 + MC)/2, so it only matches £30 if the monopolist's MC is £10. Above that MC, consumers pay more.

**Evaluation hooks:** size of the cost saving; reinvestment of profit; contestability; regulation; whether the product is a necessity.

**Writing an evaluation of monopoly:** state a gain and a loss, then say what decides between them: the size of the cost saving, whether profit is reinvested, how easy entry is, and whether regulation limits price.

## Price discrimination

**Four conditions:** price-setting power; separable markets; different PEDs; no resale.

**Two-market rule:** produce in each market where MR = MC. Charge the **higher price where demand is less elastic**.

**Method:**
1. Find MR in each market.
2. Set each MR equal to MC to get output in each.
3. Read each price from that market's demand curve.
4. Add profits, then compare with a single price if asked.

### Worked reminder (illustrative)

MC = £10. Market X: P = 70 − 2Q, MR = 70 − 4Q. Market Y: P = 40 − Q, MR = 40 − 2Q.
- X: 70 − 4Q = 10, Q = 15, P = **£40**. PED = (1/2)(40/15) = 1.33.
- Y: 40 − 2Q = 10, Q = 15, P = **£25**. PED = 25/15 = 1.67.
- Profit = 30 × 15 + 15 × 15 = £450 + £225 = **£675**.

**Perfect price discrimination:** each customer pays their own maximum; output rises to where P = MC; consumer surplus is zero; the firm takes all the gain.

| | Producers | Consumers |
|---|---|---|
| Gains | Higher profit; spare capacity filled | Lower price for the elastic group; access for low-income groups; more output in some cases |
| Losses | Cost of separating markets, stopping resale | Higher price for the inelastic group; less consumer surplus; fairness concerns |

Real-world examples: peak and off-peak travel, student and child discounts, booking-date airfares, cinema matinees.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Price maker vs monopolist:** all monopolists are price makers, but price makers are not all monopolists.
- **Price discrimination vs cost differences:** a premium for a cost-related reason is not price discrimination.
- **Supernormal profit vs profit per unit:** multiply by output.

## Quick self-test

1. State the condition for profit maximisation for a monopolist.
2. For P = 90 − 6Q, write the MR function.
3. At Q = 5 with P = 60 − 4Q, what are P and MR?
4. A monopolist faces demand P = 80 − 4Q and MC = £16. Find output and price.
5. Find PED at that point.
6. List the four conditions for price discrimination.
7. Which market is charged more: elasticity 1.2 or 2.5?
8. Give one advantage and one disadvantage of monopoly.
9. Why would a monopolist never produce where MR is negative?
10. Perfect price discrimination: demand P = 70 − 5Q, MC = £20. Find output and profit (no fixed cost).
11. Why must resale be prevented?

### Answers

1. MR = MC.
2. MR = 90 − 12Q.
3. P = 60 − 20 = £40; MR = 60 − 40 = £20 (with MR = 60 − 8Q).
4. 80 − 8Q = 16, **Q = 8**, **P = £48**.
5. PED = (1/4)(48/8) = **1.5**.
6. Price-setting power; separable markets; different PEDs; no resale.
7. The market with elasticity **1.2**.
8. Advantage: economies of scale or funds for research. Disadvantage: higher price and lower output than competition.
9. A rise in output would lower revenue and raise cost, so profit falls.
10. Q = (70 − 20)/5 = **10**. Revenue = (70 + 20)/2 × 10 = 450. Cost = 200. Profit = **£250**.
11. Low-price buyers would sell to high-price buyers, undercutting the firm and removing the price difference.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Taking the monopoly price off the MR curve when it belongs on the AR curve.
- Using MR = AR for a monopolist.
- Writing "high barriers to entry" without linking them to lasting supernormal profit.
- Describing monopoly purely as bad, with no evaluation.
- Citing the discrimination conditions in a list without applying them to the scenario.
- Saying price discrimination always raises output.
- Confusing a cost-based price difference with price discrimination.
- Omitting units and the word "per" when quoting prices and profits.
