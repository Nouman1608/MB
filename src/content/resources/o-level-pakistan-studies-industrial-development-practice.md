**These two Paper 2 style questions cover Section 5 Industrial development, each worth 25 marks in
parts (a) to (d).** Question 1 is about manufacturing location and the formal and informal
sectors; Question 2 about government promotion, call centres and tourism. Resource figures are
illustrative.

## Question 1: Where industry locates

**Fig. 1.1** (described): a map of Pakistan marking cement plants near Dera Ghazi Khan, Daudkhel,
Pezu and Thatta; fertiliser plants near Daharki and Sadiqabad; and a steel mill at Port Qasim.

**(a) (i)** Using Fig. 1.1, name the industry located at Port Qasim. **[1]**

**(ii)** Define the term *raw material*. **[1]**

**(iii)** Using Fig. 1.1, describe the distribution of cement plants. **[3]**

**(b) (i)** Explain why fertiliser plants are located near Daharki and Sadiqabad. **[3]**

**(ii)** Explain why the steel mill was built on the coast rather than inland. **[3]**

**(c)** A Sialkot football is stitched by hand in a village home, then packed and exported by a
registered factory.

**(i)** State whether village stitching is part of the formal or informal sector, and give a
reason. **[2]**

**(ii)** Explain the advantages to Pakistan of the informal sector. **[4]**

**(d)** "The informal sector does more harm than good to Pakistan's development." Do you agree?
Give reasons for your answer. **[8]**

## Mark scheme: Question 1

**(a) (i)** Iron and steel [1].

**(ii)** A natural substance from which a product is made, for example limestone for cement [1].

**(iii)** Any three: scattered rather than clustered [1]; most in northern and western Punjab and
Khyber Pakhtunkhwa (D.G. Khan, Daudkhel, Pezu) [1]; one in southern Sindh near Thatta [1]; close to
hill ranges (Sulaiman, Salt Range) rather than the central plains [1]; none shown in Balochistan's
interior [1].

**(b) (i)** Natural gas is the raw material (feedstock) for urea [1] and the fuel for the plant
[1]; Daharki lies near gas fields in upper Sindh and Sadiqabad is close by, linked by pipeline [1];
both are in farming areas that buy the fertiliser [1]. Maximum 3.

**(ii)** Iron ore and coking coal are mostly imported [1] and are bulky and heavy [1], so a site
at the port avoids costly inland transport [1]; the coast also provided flat land and water for
cooling [1]; Karachi supplies labour and a market [1]. Maximum 3.

**(c) (i)** Informal [1], because the home worker is unregistered, has no contract and pays no tax
[1].

**(ii)** Any four: jobs for people without formal qualifications, especially rural women and
migrants [1]; little capital needed to start [1]; cheap goods and services for poor households [1];
recycling of scrap and waste [1]; flexible, low-cost subcontracting that helps exporters stay
competitive [1]; a training ground where people learn skills [1].

**(d)** Levels: Level 3 (6–8) balanced, developed, judgement; Level 2 (3–5); Level 1 (1–2).

**Harm:** low productivity and pay keep households poor; no tax revenue for schools and hospitals;
child and bonded labour, for example in some brick kilns; unsafe workshops; firms cannot get bank
credit so stay small. **Good:** employs millions who would otherwise be unemployed; supplies
formal exporters such as Sialkot's sports-goods firms; provides cheap transport, repairs and food;
recycles materials. A judgement might be that it does more good than harm in the short term, but
the long-term aim should be to formalise it.

## Mark scheme: Question 2

**(a) (i)** 18,000 workers [1].

**(ii)** Karachi, Sialkot, Risalpur, Gujranwala, Gwadar, Saindak or Duddar [1].

**(iii)** Any three: fenced, secure site [1]; ready-built factory sheds and serviced plots [1];
reliable power, water and gas [1]; duty-free import of machinery and raw materials [1]; tax
concessions [1]; most output exported [1]; quick customs clearance on site [1].

**(b)** Any four developed points: to attract foreign investment [1] and technology [1]; to earn
foreign exchange through exports and reduce the trade deficit [1]; to create jobs [1]; to spread
industry away from Karachi and Lahore to areas such as Khyber Pakhtunkhwa and Balochistan [1]; to
give small firms serviced plots they could not build themselves [1].

**(c) (i)** Two marks each: name [1] and brief description [1]. Natural: for example Fairy Meadows,
a high grassland with views of Nanga Parbat; Deosai Plains, a high plateau with wildflowers and
wildlife. Cultural: for example the Makli necropolis near Thatta, with carved tombs; Takht-i-Bahi, a
Buddhist monastery near Mardan; Rohtas Fort near Jhelum.

**(ii)** Fibre-optic and internet links let calls and data travel cheaply to customers abroad [1];
firms in Lahore, Karachi or Islamabad can answer calls or process data for overseas companies in
real time [1]; English-speaking graduates and lower wages give a cost advantage [1].

**(d)** Levels: Level 3 (7–9); Level 2 (4–6); Level 1 (1–3).

**Feasible because:** world-class scenery (Karakoram peaks, Hunza, Deosai, Fairy Meadows); domestic
tourism has grown with better roads such as the Karakoram Highway and the motorway to the Kaghan
area; jobs for guides, porters, drivers, hotel and restaurant staff, craft sellers; foreign
mountaineers spend in foreign currency, which adds to GNP. **Less feasible because:** heavy snow and
landslides close roads in winter, so jobs are seasonal; limited hotels, power and water; security
perceptions deter some foreign visitors; litter, sewage and unplanned building harm fragile valleys;
much of the money may go to outside companies. A judgement such as "feasible if access,
infrastructure and environmental controls improve" earns the top level.

## Frequently asked questions

**What does "describe the distribution" require?**
According to the 2059 glossary, refer to where the features are in relation to others shown, such
as provinces and rivers, and to their density in particular areas.

**How do I get the reason mark in a formal/informal question?**
Say what makes the work informal: no registration, no contract, no tax, no legal protection.

**Should tourism answers include foreign and domestic visitors?**
Yes. Domestic tourists make up most visitors to the northern areas, while foreign visitors bring
foreign exchange, so both affect jobs and GDP.
