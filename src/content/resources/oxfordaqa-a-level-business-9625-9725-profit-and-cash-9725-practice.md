## Section A

**1.** Distinguish between gross profit and operating profit. **[2]**

**2.** Oakhanger Outdoor had revenue of £250,000, cost of sales of £150,000, operating expenses of £70,000, interest of £4,000 and taxes of £6,000.

**(a)** Calculate the gross profit margin. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the operating profit margin. **[2]**

**(c)** Calculate the net profit margin. **[2]**

**3.** Pellow Hardware made a profit of £48,000 on revenue of £600,000. Treloar Tools made a profit of £30,000 on revenue of £200,000. Using these figures, explain the difference between profit and profitability. **[3]**

**4.** Explain why a profitable business may run out of cash. **[3]**

**5.** Complete the variance column for Quenby Crafts, stating whether each variance is favourable (F) or adverse (A).

| Materials | 21,600 | 19,800 |
| Wages | 18,000 | 18,900 |
| Overheads | 9,000 | 9,000 |

**6.** Quenby Crafts (question 5) originally budgeted to sell 4,000 items at £15 each, with materials costing £4 an item. It then wins an order that raises planned sales to 4,400 items. Amend the budgeted revenue and materials figures. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** Complete the cash flow forecast for Rillaton Cleaning. Its opening balance for month 1 is £3,000.

| £ | Month 1 | Month 2 | Month 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Total inflows | 12,500 | 11,000 | 16,200 |
| Total outflows | 14,000 | 13,800 | 12,900 |

Calculate the net cash flow and the opening and closing balances for each month. **[5]**

**8.** Rillaton (question 7) now expects a customer who owes £4,000 to pay in month 3 instead of month 2. Amend the forecast and state the new closing balances for months 2 and 3. **[3]**

**9.** Saltash Marine has revenue of £584,000, trade receivables of £64,000, cost of sales of £292,000 and trade payables of £20,000.

**(a)** Calculate receivables days. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate payables days. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain what your answers mean for Saltash's cash flow. **[2]**

**10.** Explain **one** advantage and **one** disadvantage to Saltash Marine of using debt factoring. **[4]**

**11.** Tregony Coffee House has seen its gross margin fall from 68% to 61% over a year while its operating expenses stayed at the same share of revenue. Analyse **two** ways Tregony could improve its profitability. **[6]**

**12.** Venn Valley Joinery makes fitted kitchens for house builders. Last year it made a net profit of £64,000, but its bank balance has been overdrawn for four of the last six months. House builders pay 75 days after a kitchen is fitted, while Venn Valley pays its timber suppliers in 30 days. The owner is choosing between debt factoring and asking the house builders to pay within 45 days. The largest house builder provides 40% of Venn Valley's orders.

Evaluate which of the two methods Venn Valley should use to improve its cash flow. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** Gross profit is revenue minus cost of sales, the direct costs of the goods sold [1]; operating profit is gross profit minus operating expenses (overheads such as rent and marketing) [1]. **[2]**

**2. (a)** Gross profit = 250,000 − 150,000 = £100,000 [1]; 100,000 ÷ 250,000 × 100 = **40%** [1].

**(b)** Operating profit = 100,000 − 70,000 = £30,000 [1]; 30,000 ÷ 250,000 × 100 = **12%** [1].

**(c)** Net profit = 30,000 − 4,000 − 6,000 = £20,000 [1]; 20,000 ÷ 250,000 × 100 = **8%** [1]. **[6]**

**3.** Profit is an absolute amount: Pellow makes more profit, £48,000 against £30,000 [1]. Profitability measures profit relative to revenue: Pellow's margin is 8% and Treloar's is 15% [1]. So Treloar is more profitable although it makes less profit, because it keeps more of each pound of sales [1]. **[3]**

**4.** Profit counts sales when they are made, but customers buying on credit may not pay for weeks [1]. Meanwhile the business must pay wages, suppliers and loan repayments in cash, or may spend cash on assets or inventory [1]. If cash outflows come before inflows, the bank balance can run out even though the business is making a profit over the period [1]. **[3]**

**5.** Revenue **3,600 A** [1]; materials **1,800 F** [1]; wages **900 A** and overheads **0** [1]; profit **2,700 A** [1]. **[4]**

**6.** Revenue = 4,400 × 15 = **£66,000** [1]; materials = 4,400 × 4 = **£17,600** [1]. **[2]**

**7.**

| £ | Month 1 | Month 2 | Month 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Net cash flow | (1,500) | (2,800) | 3,300 |
| Opening balance | 3,000 | 1,500 | (1,300) |
| Closing balance | 1,500 | (1,300) | 2,000 |

Net cash flows correct [2] (one mark if two are correct); opening balances carried forward correctly [1]; closing balances correct [2] (one mark if two are correct). **[5]**

**8.** Month 2 inflows fall to £7,000, so net cash flow is (6,800) and the closing balance is **(£5,300)** [1]. Month 3 inflows rise to £20,200, so net cash flow is 7,300 [1] and the closing balance is **£2,000**, unchanged, because only the timing has moved [1]. **[3]**

**9. (a)** 64,000 ÷ 584,000 × 365 [1] = **40 days** [1].

**(b)** 20,000 ÷ 292,000 × 365 [1] = **25 days** [1].

**(c)** Saltash pays its suppliers about 15 days before its customers pay it [1], so it must fund that gap from its bank balance or an overdraft, which puts pressure on cash flow [1]. **[6]**

**10.** Advantage: the factor pays most of the value of Saltash's invoices straight away [1], so Saltash no longer waits 40 days and can pay its suppliers on time [1]. Disadvantage: the factor keeps a fee [1], which reduces Saltash's profit on every sale it factors [1]. **[4]**

**11.** Tregony could raise its prices [1]. Coffee-shop customers may be loyal to a local café, so a small rise may cut sales only slightly [1], raising the gross margin and therefore profitability, because each sale earns more over its direct cost [1]. Alternatively it could find cheaper suppliers of coffee, milk and food [1]. Because the problem is in the gross margin, not overheads, lowering direct costs tackles the cause [1]; however, cheaper ingredients could lower quality and drive regular customers away, so Tregony should test changes carefully [1]. **[6]**

**12.** One mark per point, up to 12.

- Venn Valley's problem is timing: builders take 75 days to pay while timber is paid for in 30 days, a 45-day gap [1], which explains the overdrafts despite £64,000 of profit [1].
- Factoring would bring in most of each invoice's value as soon as the kitchen is fitted [1], removing the gap almost at once and avoiding overdraft interest [1].
- However, the factor's fee reduces profit on every kitchen [1], and on a long-term basis this is a permanent cost to solve a timing problem [1].
- Asking builders to pay in 45 days would cut the gap to 15 days without a fee [1], so profit is protected [1].
- But builders are powerful customers, and the largest provides 40% of orders [1]; if it refuses or moves to a rival joinery, Venn Valley could lose a large share of its revenue [1].
- Judgement: factoring is the safer choice in the short term because it does not risk the key customer relationship [1]; Venn Valley could then negotiate shorter terms gradually, perhaps starting with smaller builders, and stop factoring once the gap has narrowed [1]. **[12]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Using operating profit in the net margin, or forgetting × 100.
- Treating a cost above budget as favourable because the difference is a positive number.
- Not carrying each closing balance forward when amending a forecast.
- Treating a timing change as if it changed the total cash over the period.
- Giving payables or receivables days without saying what the gap means for cash.
- Recommending a cash flow method without considering its effect on profit or on relationships with customers and suppliers.
