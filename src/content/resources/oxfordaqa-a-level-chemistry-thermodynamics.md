## 3.1.8.1 The enthalpy terms

A Born–Haber cycle is a Hess's law cycle linking an ionic solid to its elements through gaseous
atoms and ions. Each term refers to **1 mole**; learn its equation with state symbols.

| Term | Definition | Example equation |
|---|---|---|
| Enthalpy of formation, ΔfH⦵ | Enthalpy change when 1 mole of a compound forms from its elements in their standard states | Cs(s) + ½F₂(g) → CsF(s) |
| Enthalpy of atomisation, ΔatH⦵ | Enthalpy change when 1 mole of gaseous atoms forms from the element in its standard state | Ba(s) → Ba(g) |
| Bond enthalpy | Enthalpy change to break 1 mole of a covalent bond, with all species gaseous | F₂(g) → 2F(g) |
| First ionisation energy | Enthalpy change to remove 1 mole of electrons from 1 mole of gaseous atoms, forming 1 mole of gaseous 1+ ions | Cs(g) → Cs⁺(g) + e⁻ |
| Second ionisation energy | Enthalpy change to remove 1 mole of electrons from 1 mole of gaseous 1+ ions, forming gaseous 2+ ions | Ba⁺(g) → Ba²⁺(g) + e⁻ |
| First electron affinity | Enthalpy change when each atom in 1 mole of gaseous atoms gains one electron, forming 1 mole of gaseous 1– ions | F(g) + e⁻ → F⁻(g) |
| Lattice enthalpy of dissociation | Enthalpy change when 1 mole of an ionic solid separates into its gaseous ions | CsF(s) → Cs⁺(g) + F⁻(g) |
| Lattice enthalpy of formation | Enthalpy change when 1 mole of an ionic solid forms from its gaseous ions | Cs⁺(g) + F⁻(g) → CsF(s) |

Key points:

- **The two lattice enthalpies have the same size and opposite signs.** Always name the one you use.
- **Atomisation is per mole of atoms; bond enthalpy is per mole of bonds.** For a gaseous diatomic
  element, ΔatH⦵ = ½ × bond enthalpy. Iodine is a solid, so ½I₂(s) → I(g) also includes turning the
  solid into a gas; its value is given separately.
- **Ionisation energies are always endothermic.** A 2+ ion needs the first *and* second values.
- **First electron affinities of halogens and oxygen are exothermic.** The second electron affinity
  of oxygen, O⁻(g) + e⁻ → O²⁻(g), is endothermic: the negative ion repels the incoming electron.

## Constructing a Born–Haber cycle

Draw an energy level diagram. The elements in their standard states sit at zero. Endothermic steps
are arrows pointing up; exothermic steps point down. Hess's law says both routes from the elements to
the solid have the same total:

```
ΔfH = ΔatH(metal) + ΔatH(non-metal) + IE(s) + EA(s) + lattice enthalpy of formation
```

Multiply every step by the number of times it happens in the formula.

### Worked example 1: lattice enthalpy of caesium fluoride

Data (kJ mol⁻¹): ΔfH⦵(CsF) –554; ΔatH⦵(Cs) +76; first IE(Cs) +376; F–F bond enthalpy +158;
first EA(F) –328. ΔatH(F) = ½ × 158 = +79.

Build the diagram level by level (energies relative to the elements):

| Level | Step to reach it | Energy (kJ mol⁻¹) |
|---|---|---|
| Cs(s) + ½F₂(g) | start | 0 |
| Cs(g) + ½F₂(g) | ΔatH(Cs), up | +76 |
| Cs(g) + F(g) | ΔatH(F), up | +155 |
| Cs⁺(g) + e⁻ + F(g) | first IE(Cs), up | +531 |
| Cs⁺(g) + F⁻(g) | first EA(F), down | +203 |
| CsF(s) | lattice enthalpy of formation, down | –554 |

```
Lattice enthalpy of formation = ΔfH – (level of gaseous ions)
                              = –554 – 203 = –757 kJ mol⁻¹
```

So the lattice enthalpy of dissociation of CsF is **+757 kJ mol⁻¹**.

### Worked example 2: finding a different term (atomisation of barium)

The same cycle can be solved for any one unknown. For BaCl₂, barium loses two electrons and two
chlorine atoms each gain one.

Data (kJ mol⁻¹): ΔfH⦵(BaCl₂) –859; first IE(Ba) +503; second IE(Ba) +965; Cl–Cl bond enthalpy +242;
first EA(Cl) –349; lattice enthalpy of formation of BaCl₂ –2051.

```
Two Cl atoms need one whole Cl–Cl bond broken: +242
Two electron affinities: 2 × (–349) = –698
–859 = ΔatH(Ba) + 503 + 965 + 242 – 698 – 2051
ΔatH(Ba) = –859 – 503 – 965 – 242 + 698 + 2051 = +180 kJ mol⁻¹
```

The answer is positive, as every atomisation must be; a negative answer signals a sign or multiplier
error.

## Born–Haber values and the perfect ionic model

Theory gives a **calculated** value from a **perfect ionic model**: perfectly spherical
ions with evenly spread charge, held only by electrostatic attraction, with no covalent character.
A Born–Haber value uses measured data, so it includes the actual bonding.

- **Values almost equal:** the bonding is very close to purely ionic.
- **Born–Haber value clearly more exothermic:** the real lattice is held more strongly than pure
  electrostatics predicts: **evidence for covalent character**.

Covalent character comes from **polarisation**: the cation distorts the anion's electron cloud,
pulling electron density between the ions. A small or highly charged cation and a large anion give
the most polarisation.

### Worked example 3: silver iodide

Data (kJ mol⁻¹): ΔfH⦵(AgI) –62; ΔatH⦵(Ag) +285; first IE(Ag) +731; ΔatH⦵(I) +107; first EA(I) –295.
A perfect ionic model value of –808 kJ mol⁻¹ is used for this example.

```
Steps to gaseous ions = 285 + 731 + 107 + (–295) = +828
Born–Haber lattice enthalpy of formation = –62 – 828 = –890 kJ mol⁻¹
Difference from model = –890 – (–808) = –82 kJ mol⁻¹ (about 10% more exothermic)
```

The experimental value is 82 kJ mol⁻¹ more exothermic than the model predicts, so AgI has
significant **covalent character**. The large, easily distorted iodide ion is polarised by Ag⁺.

## Enthalpy of hydration and enthalpy of solution

**Enthalpy of hydration, ΔhydH⦵**: the enthalpy change when 1 mole of gaseous ions dissolves in water
to form 1 mole of aqueous ions, for example F⁻(g) → F⁻(aq). It is always exothermic: the δ– oxygen
atoms of water are attracted to a cation and the δ+ hydrogen atoms to an anion. Smaller or more highly
charged ions have more exothermic values.

**Enthalpy of solution, ΔsolH⦵**: the enthalpy change when 1 mole of an ionic solid dissolves in water,
for example RbF(s) → Rb⁺(aq) + F⁻(aq).

The cycle has two routes from solid to aqueous ions: dissolve directly, or break the lattice into
gaseous ions and then hydrate them:

```
ΔsolH = lattice enthalpy of dissociation + Σ ΔhydH(each ion)
```

### Worked example 4: enthalpy of solution of rubidium fluoride

Data (kJ mol⁻¹): lattice enthalpy of dissociation of RbF +785; ΔhydH(Rb⁺) –296; ΔhydH(F⁻) –506.

```
ΔsolH = +785 + (–296) + (–506) = –17 kJ mol⁻¹
```

Hydration releases slightly more energy than breaking the lattice needs, so dissolving is exothermic.

### Worked example 5: lattice enthalpy from an enthalpy of solution

Data (kJ mol⁻¹): ΔsolH(SrCl₂) –52; ΔhydH(Sr²⁺) –1445; ΔhydH(Cl⁻) –381.

```
–52 = lattice dissociation + (–1445) + 2 × (–381)
lattice dissociation = –52 + 1445 + 762 = +2155 kJ mol⁻¹
```

ΔhydH(Cl⁻) counts twice because SrCl₂ contains two Cl⁻ ions.

## 3.1.8.2 Entropy and feasibility

### Why ΔH is not enough

Ice melts on a warm day although melting is endothermic, so ΔH alone cannot decide feasibility.
The missing factor is **entropy, S**, a measure of disorder, in **J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹**.

Entropy increases (ΔS positive) in both kinds of change:

- **Physical changes:** solid → liquid → gas. Gas particles move freely and are the most disordered.
- **Chemical changes:** a reaction that makes more moles of gas than it uses, or turns a solid into
  a gas.

### Calculating ΔS from absolute entropies

```
ΔS = ΣS(products) – ΣS(reactants)
```

Scale each entropy by its equation coefficient; elements have non-zero absolute entropies.

### The Gibbs free-energy change

The specification gives the relationship

```
ΔG = ΔH – TΔS        (derivation not required)
```

with T in kelvin. **At constant temperature and pressure, ΔG < 0 gives a forward thermodynamic driving force; ΔG = 0 is the equilibrium boundary with no net driving force.** Standard-state data give ΔG⦵, while the direction in a non-standard mixture also depends on composition. Convert ΔS from J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹
to kJ K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ (÷ 1000) to match ΔH.

Melting ice shows the balance. For H₂O(s) → H₂O(l), take ΔH = +6.01 kJ mol⁻¹ and
ΔS = +22.0 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹:

```
At 263 K: ΔG = 6.01 – 263 × 0.0220 = +0.22 kJ mol⁻¹   (not feasible)
At 283 K: ΔG = 6.01 – 283 × 0.0220 = –0.22 kJ mol⁻¹   (feasible)
ΔG = 0 at T = 6.01 / 0.0220 = 273 K
```

### How ΔG varies with temperature

Treat ΔH and ΔS as fixed. ΔG then changes in a straight line with T, and –TΔS sets the direction.

| ΔH | ΔS | As T rises | Feasible |
|---|---|---|---|
| – | + | ΔG stays negative | At all temperatures |
| + | – | ΔG stays positive | At no temperature |
| + | + | ΔG becomes more negative | Above T = ΔH/ΔS |
| – | – | ΔG becomes less negative | Below T = ΔH/ΔS |

To find the temperature at which a reaction becomes feasible, set ΔG = 0:

```
T = ΔH / ΔS        (ΔS in kJ K⁻¹ mol⁻¹)
```

"Feasible" says nothing about rate: a feasible reaction can be very slow if its activation energy
is high.

### Worked example 6: ΔH and ΔS both positive

N₂O₄(g) → 2NO₂(g), ΔH⦵ = +57 kJ mol⁻¹.
Absolute entropies (J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹): N₂O₄(g) 304; NO₂(g) 240.

```
ΔS = (2 × 240) – 304 = +176 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ = +0.176 kJ K⁻¹ mol⁻¹
ΔG at 298 K = 57 – (298 × 0.176) = +4.6 kJ mol⁻¹   → not feasible
T = 57 / 0.176 = 324 K
```

ΔS is positive because 1 mole of gas becomes 2. In this fixed-property standard-state model, ΔG⦵ crosses zero near 324 K and is negative above that temperature; the actual mixture composition must also be considered.

### Worked example 7: ΔH and ΔS both negative

PCl₃(g) + Cl₂(g) → PCl₅(g), ΔH⦵ = –88 kJ mol⁻¹.
Absolute entropies (J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹): PCl₃(g) 312; Cl₂(g) 223; PCl₅(g) 365.

```
ΔS = 365 – (312 + 223) = –170 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ = –0.170 kJ K⁻¹ mol⁻¹
ΔG = –88 – T(–0.170) = –88 + 0.170T
At 300 K: ΔG = –37 kJ mol⁻¹   At 500 K: ΔG = –3 kJ mol⁻¹   At 700 K: ΔG = +31 kJ mol⁻¹
T = –88 / –0.170 = 518 K
```

ΔG rises as T rises, so the reaction is feasible **below** 518 K and not above it.

## Common errors

- Using one ionisation energy for a 2+ ion, or one hydration enthalpy for an ion that appears twice.
- Writing Cl₂(g) → 2Cl(g) as an atomisation: it makes 2 moles of atoms.
- Halving a bond enthalpy for iodine or bromine, whose standard states are not gases.
- Treating a *less* exothermic Born–Haber value as evidence for covalent character.
- Mixing J and kJ in ΔG = ΔH – TΔS, using T in °C, or not saying "above" or "below" with T = ΔH/ΔS.
