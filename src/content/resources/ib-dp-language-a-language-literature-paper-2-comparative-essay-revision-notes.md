## Paper 2 at a glance

| Fact (from the subject brief) | SL | HL |
|---|---|---|
| Task | comparative essay based on two literary works | same |
| Choice | one out of four questions | same |
| Time | 1.75 hours (105 minutes) | 1.75 hours (105 minutes) |
| Weighting | 35% | 25% |

- Literary works only. Non-literary texts are for Paper 1 and the individual oral.
- Expect questions you have not seen; your teacher will show you specimen questions.
- Plan to write from memory. Your teacher confirms the exam rules and which works you may use.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning for Paper 2 |
|---|---|
| Comparative thesis | One arguable claim that answers the question for *both* works and names how they differ |
| Method | *How* a writer creates meaning: form, voice, structure, imagery, setting, dialogue |
| Effect | What the method does to meaning or to the reader or audience |
| Point-by-point structure | Every body paragraph discusses both works on one point |
| Block structure | Half the essay on Work A, half on Work B; comparison left to the end |
| Remembered evidence | Short quotations and precise references you recall without the text |
| Context of production | When, where and for whom a work was written |
| Context of reception | When, where and by whom it is being read now |
| Convention | An expectation of a form or genre, which a work may follow or break |

## Method in steps

### Choosing the question (first 5 minutes)

```
1. Read all four questions.
2. Underline the key term in each.
3. For each promising question, name one moment from each of two works.
4. Need three comparable points? Can't find them -> change question or pair.
5. Commit. Don't switch after you start writing.
```

### Writing the thesis

```
Both [A] and [B] present [key term] as [shared claim],
but [Writer A] uses [method] to [effect],
whereas [Writer B] uses [method] to [effect],
because of [form or context].
```

### Writing a body paragraph

```
1. Comparative topic sentence (both works, one point)
2. Evidence from A + analysis of method
3. Connective + evidence from B + analysis of method
4. What the difference (or likeness) shows about the question
5. Link to thesis
```

## Thesis patterns: from weak to strong

The pairs below are public-domain works: Mary Shelley, *Frankenstein* (1818); Wilfred Owen, *Poems*
(1920), including "Dulce et Decorum Est"; Kate Chopin, *The Awakening* (1899); Henrik Ibsen, *A Doll's
House* (1879).

| Question key term | Weak | Strong |
|---|---|---|
| The cost of an ideal | "Both works show that ideals are dangerous." | "Both *Frankenstein* and Owen's 'Dulce et Decorum Est' expose the human cost of a glorious ideal, but Shelley lets Victor narrate his own ruin in a long confession, while Owen compresses one soldier's agony into a single remembered scene and turns directly on the reader." |
| Freedom | "Both women want freedom." | "Chopin and Ibsen both link a woman's freedom to her leaving a household, but Chopin traces Edna's change through images of the sea and swimming, while Ibsen stages Nora's change as an argument that ends offstage." |

What makes the strong column strong: the key term is in it, both works are named, and each work's
*method* is named.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Method vs content.** "Both are about war" is content. "One uses a frame narrative, the other a
  single remembered scene" is method. Paper 2 comparisons should rest on method.
- **Similarity vs difference.** Most strong essays find a shared concern and then a difference in
  method or effect. All-similarity essays tend to flatten both works.
- **Point-by-point vs block.** Use point by point. Block structures push the comparison to the end.
- **Quotation vs precise reference.** Both are evidence. A precise reference ("in the final chapter,
  Victor dies on Walton's ship in the Arctic") is better than a misquotation.
- **Context that explains vs context as background.** Context earns its place when it explains a
  difference between the works. "Owen wrote as an officer who had fought at the front" explains why the poem addresses a
  civilian reader with such anger. A paragraph of dates does not.
- **Following vs breaking a convention.** Intertextual points often come from how each work uses its
  form: a novel's frame, a poem's closing turn, a play's final stage direction.

## Introduction and final paragraph in brief

- **Introduction (3–4 sentences):** name both works, writers and forms; define the question's key term
  in your own words; state the thesis. No biography, no plot summary.
- **Final paragraph:** do not repeat the thesis word for word. Say what the comparison has shown that
  studying either work alone would not show, for example that the same concern looks like argument on
  stage and like confinement in a first-person narrative.
- **Balance check:** before you finish, count the pieces of evidence for each work. If one work has
  twice as many, add a precise reference to the other in the weakest paragraph.

## Worked reminder: turning a block into a weave

*Block (two separate claims):*
"Victor wants to be famous for his science. Later in the novel he regrets it. Owen's poem is about a
gas attack. The poem ends by calling the Latin motto a lie."

*Woven (one comparative claim), original writing for Marlbridge:*
"Both works deliver a warning addressed to someone who still believes in the ideal. Victor tells
Walton to learn from his example 'how dangerous is the acquirement of knowledge', while Owen's speaker
turns on 'My friend' and calls the Latin motto 'The old Lie'. Shelley frames the warning inside a
story told to one listener, so it feels like a confession; Owen aims it outwards at the reader, so it
feels like an accusation."

The woven version keeps one point in view, uses one short quotation from each work, and ends by
comparing *effect*.

## Evidence bank: one page per work

| Heading | How many | Tip |
|---|---|---|
| Key moments | 8–10 | Note where each falls: opening, turning point, ending |
| Short quotations | 5–6 | Keep them under about ten words so you can recall them exactly |
| Form and structure | 3–4 notes | Voice, frame, genre conventions, how it ends |
| Context | 2 notes | One on production, one on how a reader today may differ |
| Human concerns | 4–6 | Single words or short phrases: power, guilt, freedom |

## Quick self-test

1. What does the subject brief say Paper 2 is based on?
2. How many questions are you choosing from?
3. How long is Paper 2 at SL, and at HL?
4. What is Paper 2 worth at SL, and at HL?
5. Name the two areas of exploration these notes link to Paper 2.
6. Give the three things a strong comparative thesis contains.
7. Why is a point-by-point structure preferred to a block structure?
8. You cannot remember the exact words of a line. What should you do?
9. When does a context sentence earn its place?
10. Name two comparative connectives that set up a difference.
11. In the thesis pattern, what goes after "because"?
12. What should you have ready for each work in your pool, apart from quotations?

### Answers

1. Two literary works (a comparative essay based on two literary works).
2. Four; you answer one.
3. 1.75 hours (1 hour 45 minutes) at both levels.
4. 35% at SL; 25% at HL.
5. Intertextuality: connecting texts; Time and space.
6. Both works named; one claim that uses the question's key term; the difference in method or effect.
7. It keeps both works in every paragraph, so the comparison runs through the essay instead of arriving
   only at the end.
8. Paraphrase precisely, naming the moment and where it falls, rather than misquoting.
9. When it explains a difference in the works' methods or effects.
10. Any two of: whereas, while, by contrast, unlike, where A ..., B ....
11. A reason from form or context that explains why the works differ.
12. Any of: key moments, form and structure notes, context notes, human concerns.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Discussing each work in its own half of the essay, with one comparative sentence at the end.
- A thesis that does not contain the question's key term, so the essay drifts away from the question.
- Answering only half the question: *how* without *to what effect*, or likeness without difference.
- Retelling plot instead of analysing method.
- Uneven evidence: detailed quotation for one work, vague summary for the other.
- Misquoting. Accurate paraphrase is safer than invented inverted commas.
- Context paragraphs that never return to the works.
- Choosing a favourite pair of works when another pair fits the question better.
- Running out of time for the final paragraph because planning was skipped.
