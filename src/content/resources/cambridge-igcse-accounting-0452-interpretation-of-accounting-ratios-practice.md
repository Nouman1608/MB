## Questions

### Section A: short questions

**1.** Which change, on its own, would increase a business's gross profit margin?

A: selling more units at the same price and cost

B: a supplier raising its prices

C: raising the selling price while costs stay the same

D: understating closing inventory

**[1]**

**2.** Which change would make the bank balance increase by less than profit for
the year?

A: charging depreciation

B: an increase in trade receivables

C: an increase in trade payables

D: a decrease in inventory

**[1]**

**3.** State two ways a business could improve its acid test (liquid) ratio. **[2]**

**4.** Explain how gross profit can increase while the gross profit margin stays the
same. **[2]**

**5.** A business's gross profit margin was unchanged, but its profit margin fell.
State two possible reasons. **[2]**

**6.** Explain how an overstatement of closing inventory could mislead someone
comparing gross profit margins. **[2]**

### Section B: structured questions

**7.** Kenton Stationers provides these summaries.

| | Year 1 | Year 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue | 180,000 | 198,000 |
| Cost of sales | 117,000 | 123,750 |
| Gross profit | 63,000 | 74,250 |
| Other income | 1,500 | 1,500 |
| Expenses | 41,400 | 49,500 |
| Profit for the year | 23,100 | 26,250 |

In Year 2 the owner moved to a cheaper supplier and opened a second shop.

**(a)** Calculate the percentage change from Year 1 to Year 2 in revenue, gross
profit, expenses and profit for the year. **[4]**

**(b)** Calculate the gross profit margin and profit margin for each year. **[4]**

**(c)** Comment on the business's profitability. **[4]**

**8.** Duffield Hardware's liquidity ratios were:

| | Year 1 | Year 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Current ratio | 1.90 : 1 | 2.80 : 1 |
| Acid test ratio | 1.10 : 1 | 0.80 : 1 |
| Inventory turnover (days) | 40 | 75 |

**(a)** Comment on the change in liquidity. **[4]**

**(b)** Suggest two ways to improve liquidity. **[2]**

**9.** A business's trade receivables turnover rose from 35 to 52 days, and its trade
payables turnover fell from 45 to 28 days. Explain the effect on the business's cash
and suggest two improvements. **[4]**

**10.** Nadim's profit for the year was 28,000, but his bank balance fell by 5,000.
During the year inventory rose by 9,000, trade receivables rose by 6,000, trade
payables fell by 3,000, a van was bought for 15,000, depreciation of 4,000 was
charged and drawings were 4,000.

**(a)** Show how profit for the year links to the change in the bank balance. **[4]**

**(b)** Explain why depreciation is added back in your answer to (a). **[1]**

**11.** A business sold 4,000 units at $30 each in Year 1 and 4,400 units at $29 each
in Year 2. Each unit cost $18 in both years.

**(a)** Calculate gross profit for each year. **[2]**

**(b)** Explain why gross profit rose while the gross profit margin fell. **[2]**

**12.** Last year a company took out a large loan to build a new warehouse. Its profit
for the year increased, but its return on capital employed fell from 18.00% to 14.00%.
A director says: "Profit is up, so the business is performing better." Discuss
whether you agree. **[4]**

## Answers

**1.** C [1]. Each sale earns more gross profit per dollar of revenue. **[1]**

**2.** B [1]. Sales are recorded but the cash has not been received. **[1]**

**3.** Any two from: collect money from customers faster; offer cash discounts for
prompt payment; raise long-term finance and hold the cash; reduce current
liabilities by converting an overdraft into a long-term loan; sell surplus
non-current assets for cash [1] [1]. **[2]**

**4.** If the business sells more units at the same selling price and cost per unit
[1], gross profit rises in proportion to revenue, so gross profit as a percentage of
revenue does not change [1]. **[2]**

**5.** Any two from: expenses increased faster than revenue; other income fell; a new
expense such as loan interest or depreciation on new assets; an increase in the
allowance for irrecoverable debts [1] [1]. **[2]**

**6.** Overstated closing inventory lowers cost of sales, so gross profit and the
gross profit margin are too high [1]. The business would appear to have improved its
trading when it has not, and next year's margin will look worse because opening
inventory is too high [1]. **[2]**

**7. (a)** Revenue +18,000 ÷ 180,000 = **+10.00%** [1]. Gross profit +11,250 ÷ 63,000
= **+17.86%** [1]. Expenses +8,100 ÷ 41,400 = **+19.57%** [1]. Profit +3,150 ÷ 23,100
= **+13.64%** [1].

**(b)** Gross profit margin: Year 1 63,000 ÷ 180,000 = **35.00%** [1]; Year 2 74,250 ÷
198,000 = **37.50%** [1]. Profit margin: Year 1 23,100 ÷ 180,000 = **12.83%** [1];
Year 2 26,250 ÷ 198,000 = **13.26%** [1].

**(c)** The gross profit margin improved from 35.00% to 37.50% because the cheaper
supplier reduced the cost of each item sold [1]. The profit margin improved only
slightly, from 12.83% to 13.26% [1], because expenses rose by 19.57%, faster than
revenue, probably from running the second shop [1]. Overall profitability has
improved, but the business should control the second shop's expenses to benefit
fully from its better buying [1]. **[12]**

**8. (a)** The current ratio rose from 1.90 : 1 to 2.80 : 1, which looks stronger [1],
but the acid test fell from 1.10 : 1 to 0.80 : 1, so liquid assets no longer cover
current liabilities [1]. The difference is inventory: it is now held for 75 days
instead of 40 [1], so much of the current assets are tied up in goods that sell
slowly, and the business may struggle to pay suppliers on time [1].

**(b)** Any two from: reduce purchases until inventory falls; sell slow-moving
goods at a discount; collect receivables faster; obtain long-term finance [1] [1]. **[6]**

**9.** Customers now take 17 days longer to pay, while the business pays suppliers
17 days sooner, so cash flows out faster than it comes in and the bank balance will
fall or an overdraft may be needed [1] [1]. Improvements, any two: offer cash
discounts for prompt payment or chase overdue debts; negotiate longer credit with
suppliers; reduce credit terms for new customers [1] [1]. **[4]**

**10. (a)**

```
Profit for the year                         28,000
Add depreciation (no cash paid)              4,000
Less increase in inventory                  (9,000)
Less increase in trade receivables          (6,000)
Less decrease in trade payables             (3,000)
Less purchase of van                       (15,000)
Less drawings                               (4,000)
Change in bank balance                      (5,000)
```

Depreciation added [1]; working capital changes with correct signs [1]; van and
drawings deducted [1]; result of a fall of 5,000 [1].

**(b)** Depreciation reduces profit but involves no payment of cash in the year [1]. **[5]**

**11. (a)** Year 1: 4,000 × (30 − 18) = **48,000** [1]. Year 2: 4,400 × (29 − 18)
= **48,400** [1].

**(b)** Gross profit rose because 400 more units were sold [1], but each unit earned
$11 instead of $12 because of the price cut, so the margin fell from 40.00% to
37.93% [1]. **[4]**

**12.** Profit rising is good [1], but ROCE shows that each $100 of long-term finance
now earns 14.00 instead of 18.00, because the loan increased capital employed more
than profit increased [1]. The warehouse may take time to raise profit, so the fall
could be temporary [1]. I partly agree: profit is up, but the business is using its
finance less efficiently at present, so "performing better" is not yet proven [1]. **[4]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing a change without explaining its cause from the scenario.
- Saying more sales volume improves the gross profit margin.
- Judging liquidity from the current ratio alone when inventory has grown.
- Suggesting improvements that do not address the problem found.
- Treating profit and cash as the same thing.
