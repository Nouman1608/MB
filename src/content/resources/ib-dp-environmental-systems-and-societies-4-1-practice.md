## Section A

**1.** Distinguish between blue water and green water. **[2]**

**2.** State the water budget equation for a drainage basin. **[1]**

**3.** Define *lag time* on a storm hydrograph. **[1]**

**4.** Outline how sea ice formation in the North Atlantic helps drive the thermohaline circulation. **[3]**

**5.** Explain why the hypolimnion of a stratified lake can become low in dissolved oxygen in late summer. **[3]**

**6.** State why the melting of floating sea ice does not directly raise sea level, while the melting of an ice sheet on land does. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** (Data response) The table shows annual figures for a fictional 1200 km² drainage basin.

| Year | Precipitation / mm | Evapotranspiration / mm | Runoff / mm |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2021 | 980 | 560 | 360 |
| 2022 | 610 | 480 | 230 |

**(a)** Calculate ΔS for each year. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the change in storage in 2022 as a volume in m³. **[2]**
**(c)** Explain why runoff in 2022 was greater than zero even though storage fell. **[2]**

**8.** (Data response) Two neighbouring fictional catchments of similar size received the same storm, with peak rainfall at 08:00.

| | Catchment F (forested) | Catchment U (urban) |
|---|---|---|
| Time of peak discharge | 18:00 | 11:00 |
| Peak discharge / m³ s⁻¹ | 22 | 55 |
| Baseflow before the storm / m³ s⁻¹ | 6 | 4 |

**(a)** Calculate the lag time for each catchment. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate how many times greater the peak discharge of U is than that of F. **[1]**
**(c)** Explain the differences in lag time and peak discharge between the catchments. **[4]**
**(d)** Suggest why baseflow is lower in Catchment U. **[1]**

**9.** Explain how wind-driven surface currents transfer heat from the tropics towards the poles. **[3]**

**10.** Explain how an El Niño event can affect a coastal fishery in Peru. **[4]**

**11.** (Data response) Temperature and dissolved oxygen were measured at depth in a fictional temperate lake in August.

| Depth / m | 0 | 2 | 4 | 6 | 8 | 10 | 14 | 18 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Temperature / °C | 22 | 22 | 21 | 15 | 9 | 7 | 6 | 6 |
| Dissolved oxygen / mg L⁻¹ | 9.0 | 9.0 | 8.8 | 6.1 | 3.2 | 2.0 | 1.2 | 0.8 |

**(a)** Identify the depth range of the thermocline. **[1]**
**(b)** Describe the relationship between depth and dissolved oxygen. **[2]**
**(c)** Predict how the profiles will change after autumn turnover, giving reasons. **[3]**

**12.** Discuss how human activities in a drainage basin can alter the flows and storages of the hydrological cycle, and the consequences for people downstream. **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** Blue water is liquid freshwater in rivers, lakes and aquifers that can be pumped or diverted [1]; green water is water held in the soil and used by plants, returning to the air by evapotranspiration [1]. **[2]**

**2.** P = Q + E ± ΔS (precipitation = runoff + evapotranspiration ± change in storage) [1]. **[1]**

**3.** The time between peak rainfall and peak discharge [1]. **[1]**

**4.** When sea ice forms, salt is left behind in the surrounding water, increasing its salinity [1]. Cold temperatures also increase density [1]. The cold, salty, dense water sinks, drawing surface water in behind it and driving deep circulation [1]. **[3]**

**5.** The thermocline prevents mixing between the surface and bottom layers [1], so oxygen from the air and from photosynthesis cannot reach the hypolimnion [1]. Decomposers in the dark bottom water continue to respire and use up the oxygen that is there [1]. **[3]**

**6.** Floating sea ice already displaces its own mass of water, so melting it barely changes the volume of the ocean [1]; ice on land adds new water to the oceans when it melts, raising sea level [1]. **[2]**

**7. (a)** 2021: 980 − 560 − 360 = **+60 mm** [1]; 2022: 610 − 480 − 230 = **−100 mm** [1].
**(b)** 0.100 m × 1200 × 10⁶ m² [1] = **1.2 × 10⁸ m³** lost from storage [1].
**(c)** Groundwater continued to drain into the river as baseflow [1], so the stores built up in wetter years, such as 2021, supplied runoff even in a dry year [1]. **[6]**

**8. (a)** F: 18:00 − 08:00 = **10 hours** [1]; U: 11:00 − 08:00 = **3 hours** [1].
**(b)** 55 ÷ 22 = **2.5 times** [1].
**(c)** In U, impermeable roofs and roads prevent infiltration [1], and drains carry water quickly to the river, so lag time is short [1]. More of the rain becomes surface runoff and arrives together, giving a higher peak [1]. In F, trees intercept rain and soils allow infiltration, so water reaches the river slowly through throughflow and groundwater, giving a longer lag and lower peak [1].
**(d)** Less water infiltrates in U, so groundwater stores are recharged less and supply less baseflow [1]. **[8]**

**9.** Winds blowing over the ocean surface drag water along, setting up currents [1]. The Coriolis effect and the continents deflect currents into large gyres [1], so warm water flows poleward along the western sides of ocean basins and releases heat to the air at higher latitudes, while cold currents return water towards the equator [1]. **[3]**

**10.** During El Niño, the Pacific trade winds weaken [1], so warm surface water spreads east towards South America [1]. Upwelling of cold, nutrient-rich water off Peru weakens [1], so phytoplankton productivity falls and fish such as anchoveta decline or move, reducing catches and incomes [1]. **[4]**

**11. (a)** About 4 m to 10 m (temperature falls from 21 °C to 7 °C) [1].
**(b)** Dissolved oxygen falls with depth, from 9.0 mg L⁻¹ at the surface to 0.8 mg L⁻¹ at 18 m [1]; it stays almost constant in the top 4 m and falls most steeply through the thermocline [1].
**(c)** As the surface cools, the temperature becomes nearly uniform with depth [1], because the whole lake reaches a similar density and wind mixes it [1]; dissolved oxygen rises at depth and becomes more even throughout, because oxygenated surface water is mixed down [1]. **[6]**

**12.** Indicative content. Extended answers in the real exam are marked holistically against the IB's criteria, so treat these points as a guide:
- Urbanisation replaces permeable ground with impermeable surfaces, reducing infiltration and groundwater recharge [1].
- Runoff increases and reaches rivers faster, shortening lag time and raising flood peaks downstream [1].
- Deforestation reduces interception and transpiration, increasing runoff and soil erosion, which adds sediment to rivers [1].
- Dams create large surface stores, increase evaporation and reduce peak flows downstream [1].
- Dams also trap sediment, so downstream channels, deltas and floodplains lose fertile deposits [1].
- Irrigation transfers water from rivers and aquifers to fields, increasing evapotranspiration and reducing downstream discharge [1].
- Groundwater abstraction faster than recharge lowers the water table and reduces baseflow in dry seasons [1].
- Consequences differ: downstream users may gain flood protection from a dam but lose water and sediment, creating conflict between upstream and downstream communities [1].
- Judgement: the size of the impact depends on how much of the basin is changed and whether measures such as sustainable drainage or managed releases are used [1]. **[9]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Arithmetic sign errors in ΔS.
- Not converting km² and mm before calculating a volume.
- Quoting lag time without units.
- Describing table data without figures and units.
- Saying El Niño brings "warm water" without linking it to upwelling and productivity.
