## 3.1.5 The ten general accounting concepts

Learn the specification's names exactly. Each line gives the rule and the "so what" for the figures.

| Concept | Rule in one line | Effect you should be able to state |
|---|---|---|
| Money measurement | Only items with a reliable money value are recorded | Skills, reputation and location never appear as assets |
| Duality | Two equal and opposite effects per transaction | Debits = credits; the accounting equation balances |
| Historic cost | Assets at the price originally paid | No upward revaluation for rising market prices |
| Going concern | Business assumed to continue | Assets at cost less depreciation, not break-up value |
| Accruals | Income and expenses in the period they relate to | Accruals, prepayments, income due, income received in advance, depreciation |
| Consistency | Same methods every period | Results comparable year on year |
| Prudence | Do not overstate assets or profit | Foreseeable losses now; gains only when realised |
| Materiality | Small items treated in the simplest way | Low-cost durable items expensed |
| Realisation | Revenue when earned (goods or service pass to the customer) | Not on order, not on deposit, not on cash receipt |
| Business entity | Business separate from owner | Private spending = drawings; owner's money in = capital |

Memory hook: three concepts deal with **what** is recorded (money measurement, business entity, materiality),
three with **value** (historic cost, going concern, prudence), three with **timing** (accruals, realisation,
consistency over time), and duality with **how** it is recorded.

## 3.1.5 Concepts applied to the named situations

| Situation in the specification | Main concepts | Typical exam task |
|---|---|---|
| Preparation of financial statements | Accruals, realisation, business entity, prudence | Correct a draft profit |
| Asset valuation | Historic cost, going concern, prudence, money measurement | Explain why an asset is not at market value |
| Depreciation of non-current assets | Accruals, consistency, prudence | Calculate a charge; judge a change of method |
| Inventories (cost or NRV) | Prudence, historic cost, realisation | Value inventory item by item |
| Recording the purchase of non-current assets | Historic cost, accruals, materiality | Split capital and revenue expenditure |
| Recording transactions in ledger accounts | Duality, business entity, money measurement | Write the double entry and name the concept |
| Goods sold on a sale or return basis | Realisation, prudence | Remove unaccepted goods from sales; add to inventory at cost |

## Method in steps

### Inventory: lower of cost and NRV

```
1. NRV per unit = selling price - costs to complete and sell
2. Compare NRV with cost for each line separately
3. Value per line = lower figure x units
4. Add the lines
5. Write-down = total cost - total value used (reduces profit)
```

Small reminder: 40 scarves cost $11 each; they will sell for
$14 but need
$4 of re-labelling and postage.
NRV = 14 - 4 = 10, below cost, so value = 40 × 10 = 400 dollars, a write-down of 40.

### Sale or return at the year end

```
1. Find goods still on approval (not accepted, return date not passed)
2. Revenue:            reduce by their selling price
3. Trade receivables:  reduce by their selling price
4. Closing inventory:  add their cost (NRV if lower)
5. Profit falls by:    selling price - cost
Cost from a mark-up on cost of m%:  cost = selling price / (1 + m/100)
```

Goods the customer has already accepted stay in revenue.

### Capital or revenue expenditure

```
Capital (debit the asset account): purchase price, delivery, installation,
  modifications needed before first use, legal fees on buying property
Revenue (income statement): running costs, servicing, repairs,
  fuel, insurance, replacement consumables
```

### Ledger entries: which concept decides?

```
Business pays owner's home electricity, 210      Dr Drawings 210         Cr Bank 210
Owner transfers private car to the business      Dr Motor vehicles 7,800 Cr Capital 7,800
  at an agreed value of 7,800
Owner's chef is "worth a fortune" to the cafe    No entry
```

The first two are business entity; the third is money measurement. Every recorded entry has equal debits and
credits, which is duality, so the trial balance still agrees.

### Correcting a draft profit

```
Draft profit
+ expenses wrongly charged (private spending, prepaid amounts, capital items)
- income wrongly included (deposits, unaccepted sale or return goods)
- write-downs (inventory to NRV) and missing charges (depreciation, accruals)
= Corrected profit
```

Always write the concept next to each line.

## Must-know distinctions

- **Realisation vs accruals.** Realisation decides *whether* revenue has been earned; accruals decides *which
  period* income and expenses belong to.
- **Prudence vs pessimism.** Prudence means caution with estimates. It does not allow deliberate understating
  of profit.
- **Historic cost vs NRV.** Historic cost is the normal basis; NRV replaces it only when lower (inventory).
- **Going concern vs closure.** If the business is closing, use expected sale values instead.
- **Capital vs drawings.** Owner's money or assets in = capital (credit). Owner's private use = drawings
  (debit).
- **Materiality is relative.** The same amount may be material for one business and not another.
- **Consistency vs never changing.** A change is allowed for a fairer view, with disclosure, not to manage
  profit.

## Quick self-test

1. The business bank account pays for the owner's family wedding. Name the concept and the correct entry.
2. An item cost $48. It will sell for
   $55 after repairs costing
   $11. At what value per unit is it included in
   inventory?
3. 60 units cost $15 each and have an NRV of
   $16.50 each. Value the inventory.
4. Goods with a selling price of $2,800 were sent on sale or return and recorded as sales. Mark-up is 40% on
   cost. They were not accepted by the year end. By how much is profit overstated?
5. A $120 waste bin will last six years. How should it be recorded, and which concept allows this?
6. A van costs $21,000. Shelving fitted before use costs
   $1,400 and the first tank of fuel costs
   $90. What
   amount is debited to the vehicles account?
7. Year end 30 June. Rent of $9,600 for twelve months was paid on 1 March. How much is prepaid at the year
   end?
8. Commission of $650 was earned in March and received in April. The year ends on 31 March. In which year is
   it recorded, and how is it shown?
9. A business shows its machinery at what it would raise at auction next week, although it plans to keep
   trading. Which concept is broken?
10. A machine cost $18,400, will be sold for
    $1,600 at the end of a 7-year life, and is depreciated on the
    straight-line basis. Calculate the annual charge and name the concept behind it.
11. Which concept explains why the trial balance totals agree?

### Answers

1. Business entity. Debit drawings, credit bank: it is not a business expense.
2. NRV = 55 - 11 = **44 dollars**, lower than cost of 48, so 44 per unit.
3. NRV is above cost, so use cost: 60 × 15 = **900 dollars**.
4. Cost = 2,800 / 1.40 = 2,000. Profit overstated by 2,800 - 2,000 = **800 dollars** (realisation).
5. As an expense in the income statement; **materiality**.
6. 21,000 + 1,400 = **22,400 dollars** (historic cost). The fuel is revenue expenditure.
7. March to June is 4 months used; 8 months prepaid: 8/12 × 9,600 = **6,400 dollars** (accruals).
8. The year to 31 March; shown as income due, an other receivable (**accruals**).
9. **Going concern** (assets should be at cost less depreciation).
10. (18,400 - 1,600) / 7 = **2,400 dollars per year**; accruals (cost matched to the years that benefit).
11. **Duality**.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Naming a concept but not explaining its effect on profit, assets or liabilities.
- Using "cost" loosely when a question is about net realisable value, or mixing up the two.
- Comparing total cost with total NRV for inventory instead of each line.
- Taking sale or return goods out of revenue but forgetting to add their cost to closing inventory.
- Adding sale or return goods back at selling price, or removing goods the customer has already accepted.
- Working out cost from a mark-up by multiplying by (1 - m%) instead of dividing by (1 + m%).
- Recording a customer deposit as revenue before the goods or service have been supplied.
- Treating the owner's private bills as business expenses, or crediting drawings.
- Describing prudence as "understating profit to be safe".
- Defending a change of depreciation method only because it raises profit.
