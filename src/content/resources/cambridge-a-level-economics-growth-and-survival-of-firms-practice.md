## Questions

**1.** State two reasons why a small firm may choose to remain small. **[2]**

**2.** A tailoring workshop has these long-run total costs: 300 suits cost 18,000 cu, 1,200 suits cost 48,000 cu and 3,000 suits cost 150,000 cu.

**(a)** Calculate the average cost at each output. **[3]**
**(b)** State the output with the lowest average cost and what happens to average cost beyond it. **[2]**

**3.** Identify the type of integration in each case.

**(i)** A coffee roaster buys a chain of cafes. **[1]**
**(ii)** A toy maker merges with another toy maker. **[1]**
**(iii)** A bus operator takes over a software company. **[1]**

**4.** Market shares in Country C are 30%, 25%, 15%, 10%, 8%, 7% and 5%.

**(a)** Calculate the three-firm and four-firm concentration ratios. **[2]**
**(b)** The firms with 15% and 10% merge. Recalculate both ratios. **[2]**

**5.** A furniture maker buys 80,000 kg of timber a year at 2.50 cu a kg. It considers building a sawmill that would produce timber at 2.15 cu a kg, with extra fixed costs of 20,000 cu a year.

**(a)** Calculate the annual saving on timber. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the net gain after the fixed cost. **[1]**
**(c)** Calculate the minimum annual timber use at which the sawmill breaks even. **[2]**

**6.** A firm's quarterly profits (thousand cu) from product X are 40, −10, 30, −20. Product Y, in an unrelated industry, gives −5, 35, −10, 30.

**(a)** Calculate the annual profit and the highest-minus-lowest quarter for X, Y and the two combined. **[3]**
**(b)** Explain why a conglomerate firm may have steadier profit. **[2]**

**7.** Explain three conditions that make a cartel effective. **[6]**

**8.** Market demand is P = 78 − Q, where Q is thousands of units and P is in cu. Marginal cost is a constant 30 cu. Three firms form a cartel that acts as a monopolist and shares output equally.

**(a)** Calculate the cartel's output, price, total profit and each firm's profit. **[4]**
**(b)** One firm raises output to 16 while the other two keep to their quotas. Calculate the new price and each firm's profit. **[3]**
**(c)** Explain what (b) shows about the stability of cartels. **[3]**

**9.** Explain why the principal-agent problem arises in a company and outline two ways to reduce it. **[6]**

**10.** A firm faces P = 120 − Q (Q in thousands, P in cu) with constant marginal cost of 40 cu.

**(a)** Calculate the profit-maximising output, price and profit (thousand cu). **[3]**
**(b)** Managers choose to maximise revenue. Calculate output, price, revenue and profit. **[3]**
**(c)** Managers are paid 2% of revenue. Calculate the managers' gain and the shareholders' loss from the change in (b). **[2]**

**11.** Evaluate whether a horizontal merger between two of the largest firms in a market benefits consumers. **[12]**

## Answers

**1.** Any two from: the market is small or local; the product is niche or personal; the owner wants control; finance is hard to raise; diseconomies set in early. One mark for each of two reasons [1] [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Give two distinct reasons. Restating one reason in different words wastes space that a second reason would use.

**2. (a)** 18,000 ÷ 300 = **60 cu** [1]; 48,000 ÷ 1,200 = **40 cu** [1]; 150,000 ÷ 3,000 = **50 cu** [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** Lowest at **1,200 suits** (40 cu) [1]. Beyond it average cost rises (50 cu at 3,000), so diseconomies of scale operate [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the division for each output, and name diseconomies of scale instead of just saying "costs rise".

**3.** (i) **Forward vertical** [1]. (ii) **Horizontal** [1]. (iii) **Conglomerate** [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Say forward or backward for vertical integration. "Vertical" alone is incomplete.

**4. (a)** Three-firm: 30 + 25 + 15 = **70%** [1]. Four-firm: 70 + 10 = **80%** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** The merged firm has 25%. Shares are 30, 25, 25, 8, 7, 5. Three-firm = **80%**; four-firm = 30 + 25 + 25 + 8 = **88%** [1] [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Re-rank the shares after the merger, because the largest firms may change.

**5. (a)** Current cost 80,000 × 2.50 = 200,000 cu [1]. Own sawmill 80,000 × 2.15 = 172,000, so saving **28,000 cu** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** 28,000 − 20,000 = **8,000 cu** a year [1]. **[1]**
**(c)** Saving per kg = 0.35 cu [1]. Break-even volume = 20,000 ÷ 0.35 = **about 57,143 kg** [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A fixed cost must be set against the saving, so say whether the answer is a gain or a loss.

**6. (a)** X: total **40**, range 40 − (−20) = **60** [1]. Y: total **50**, range 35 − (−10) = **45** [1]. Combined 35, 25, 20, 10, total **90**, range 35 − 10 = **25** [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** The two products have profits that peak in different quarters [1], so a bad quarter in one is offset by the other and total profit is steadier [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Refer to the actual figures, and note that the argument depends on the products not facing the same shocks.

**7.** Any three conditions, each with a reason. Few firms [1], so agreement and monitoring are easier [1]. High barriers to entry [1], so new rivals cannot undercut the high price [1].  Easy detection of cheating [1], so members stay within quotas [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Link each condition to why it makes agreement or policing easier. A bare list of conditions is thin.

**8. (a)** MR = 78 − 2Q = 30, so **Q = 24** [1]. P = 78 − 24 = **54** [1]. Profit = (54 − 30) × 24 = **576** thousand cu [1]. Each firm: 8 units, **192** [1]. **[4]**
**(b)** Total Q = 16 + 8 + 8 = 32, so **P = 46** [1]. Cheater: (46 − 30) × 16 = **256** [1]. Each other firm: (46 − 30) × 8 = **128** [1]. **[3]**
**(c)** The cheater gains (256 against 192) while the others lose (128 against 192) [1]. Every member has the same incentive [1], so if all cheat the price falls towards the competitive level and the cartel breaks down [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Use the figures from (b) to support the point, and state the incentive for every member, not just one.

**9.** Shareholders (principals) own the firm but managers (agents) run it [1]. Their objectives differ: shareholders want profit and share price, managers may want salary, status or security [1]. Owners cannot observe everything managers do (asymmetric information) [1]. Solution one: pay linked to profit or share options [1], which makes managers' reward depend on shareholder outcomes [1]. Solution two: audits, independent directors or a takeover threat [1], which monitor or discipline managers. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Identify who is principal and who is agent, and explain how each solution aligns the two sets of objectives.

**10. (a)** MR = 120 − 2Q = 40, so **Q = 40** [1]. P = 80 [1]. Profit = (80 − 40) × 40 = **1,600** [1]. **[3]**
**(b)** MR = 0, so **Q = 60** and **P = 60** [1]. Revenue = **3,600** [1]. Profit = (60 − 40) × 60 = **1,200** [1]. **[3]**
**(c)** Managers gain 2% × (3,600 − 3,200) = **8** thousand cu [1]. Shareholders lose 1,600 − 1,200 = **400** thousand cu [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* Original revenue is 80 × 40 = 3,200, so use it as the base for the change in revenue.

**11.** Analysis: a merger can cut average cost through economies of scale, such as shared head office or bulk buying [1]. If savings are passed on, price falls [1]. Greater scale could fund research, which may improve quality [1]. A merger also removes a rival, so market share and concentration rise [1], which gives more power to raise price [1]. Less competition may reduce choice and cut incentives to control costs [1]. Evaluation: the outcome depends on whether the savings exceed the loss of competition [1]. It depends on barriers to entry, because a contestable market may still discipline the merged firm [1]. It also depends on the size of the merged share [1], on whether regulators intervene [1], and on whether the merger works in practice, since diseconomies of scale and culture clashes can raise costs [1]. Judgement: consumers are likely to gain where entry is easy and costs fall, and lose where the merged firm dominates a market with high barriers to entry [1]. **[12]**
*Examiner insight:* Reach a reasoned judgement tied to conditions, and consider more than one affected group instead of listing points on one side only.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Writing "vertical" without saying forward or backward.
- Forgetting to re-rank market shares after a merger.
- Ignoring fixed costs when judging integration.
- Describing cartel conditions without linking them to effectiveness.
- Treating a cartel as stable and missing the incentive to cheat.
- Mixing up principal and agent.
- Saying a merger always lowers costs or always raises prices.
- Ending an evaluation answer by repeating points without choosing a side.
