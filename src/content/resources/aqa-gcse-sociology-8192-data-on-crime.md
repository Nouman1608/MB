## Why data on crime is a sociological problem

Crime is partly hidden by its nature: offenders try not to be caught, and some victims never realise or never say they were victims. Every source of data therefore captures only part of the picture. The exam question is rarely "what does the data say?" but "how useful is it, and what does it miss?"

## The three main sources

### 1. Police recorded crime (official crime statistics)

**How it is collected:** for a crime to appear, it must be **reported** to the police (or discovered by them) and then **recorded** by them. Police forces in England and Wales record crimes under Home Office counting rules, and the figures are published by the Office for National Statistics. They are **secondary data** for sociologists.

**Usefulness:**

- National coverage, collected regularly, so trends can be tracked over time.
- Good for crimes that are almost always reported, such as homicide and vehicle theft (insurance claims require a police report).
- Show how the police and criminal justice system are working: what they record, charge and prosecute.

**Limitations:**

- Many crimes are never **reported**, because victims think them too trivial, private, or something the police cannot help with, or because they fear reprisal or do not realise a crime happened.
- Reported crimes are not always **recorded**: police use **discretion**, may judge there is not enough evidence, or the victim may withdraw.
- Changes in **counting rules**, recording practice, police priorities and the law can change the figures without any change in behaviour. In 2014 the UK Statistics Authority withdrew the National Statistics designation from police recorded crime because of concerns about the quality of recording.

### 2. Victim surveys: the Crime Survey for England and Wales

**How it is collected:** a large sample of households is interviewed about crimes they have experienced in the past twelve months, whether or not they reported them. The survey began in 1982 as the British Crime Survey and was renamed the Crime Survey for England and Wales (CSEW) in 2012. It is **quantitative** and uses **random sampling**.

**Usefulness:**

- Captures crime that is **not reported** to the police, so it reveals part of the dark figure.
- Unaffected by changes in police recording, so it is often seen as a better guide to long-term trends for the crimes it covers.
- Asks about reasons for not reporting, fear of crime and attitudes to the police.

**Limitations:**

- Cannot record crimes without a direct individual victim, such as many **corporate crimes**, or crimes where the victim is unaware, such as some fraud.
- Does not cover homicide (the victim cannot be interviewed) or crimes against businesses.
- Relies on **memory**; respondents may forget, misremember dates or exaggerate.
- Sensitive crimes, such as domestic and sexual violence, may still be under-reported to interviewers.

### 3. Self-report studies

**How it is collected:** people, often young people, are asked in anonymous questionnaires or interviews which offences they have committed, whether or not they were caught.

**Usefulness:**

- Reveal **hidden offending** and the characteristics of offenders who are never caught.
- Test whether official statistics accurately show **who** commits crime, for example by class or ethnicity.

**Limitations:**

- Respondents may **lie**, forget, or exaggerate to impress.
- Usually limited to minor offences; few people admit serious crimes.
- Samples are often of young people, so they say little about white collar crime.

## The dark figure of crime

The **dark figure** is crime that is **unreported** or **unrecorded**, so it never appears in official statistics. Think of it as two filters between an offence and the official figures:

1. **Offence committed → reported?** Lost if the victim does not know, does not care, is afraid or does not trust the police.
2. **Reported → recorded?** Lost if police discretion, lack of evidence or recording rules mean it is not counted.

Victim surveys reach some crime lost at the first filter. Self-report studies reach some offending never detected at all. No source measures the whole dark figure.

## Patterns and trends

The specification asks you to describe patterns and trends "using relevant statistical data". In the exam you will usually be given a table or graph, so the skill is reading it accurately. Key long-term trends in England and Wales that are worth knowing in outline:

- CSEW estimates of crime peaked in the mid-1990s and then fell substantially over the following two decades for many types of crime, such as burglary and vehicle crime.
- Police recorded crime rose in the later 2010s, which the Office for National Statistics linked partly to improvements in recording practice and greater willingness to report some offences, rather than simply more offending.
- Since the mid-2010s, the CSEW has included questions on fraud and computer misuse, which showed these to be among the most commonly experienced crimes.
- Recorded offending is concentrated among young people and men (see 3.5.3).

**How to describe data in the exam:** state the overall direction (rise, fall, stable), quote the start and end figures, note any exception or turning point, and, if asked, calculate a difference or percentage change. Then ask whether a change reflects behaviour or recording.

## Perspectives on the use of data on crime

| Perspective | View of crime statistics |
|---|---|
| **Functionalist** | Tends to treat official statistics as a broadly accurate picture of who commits crime, which explanations such as Merton's and Albert Cohen's then seek to explain (for example, working-class over-representation) |
| **Marxist** | Official statistics are a **social construct** that reflects who is policed and prosecuted. Working-class crime is targeted, while white collar and corporate crimes are under-detected and often handled outside the criminal courts, so the statistics serve the interests of the ruling class |
| **Feminist** | Crimes against women, especially domestic and sexual violence, are heavily under-reported and under-recorded, so statistics understate the harm women suffer. Some feminists also question whether low female offending figures reflect chivalry or harsher treatment of women who break gender norms |

Interactionists are not listed for this sub-section, but labelling ideas from 3.5.1 help explain why recorded crime reflects police decisions.

## Worked example: an annotated 4-mark answer

**Question (practice):** Identify and explain one reason why victim surveys may give a more accurate picture of crime than police recorded crime. **[4]**

> Victim surveys include crimes that were never reported to the police **[identifies: 1]**. Many victims do not report crimes because they think them too trivial or that the police cannot help **[explains why crimes go unreported: 1]**. The Crime Survey for England and Wales asks a large random sample about crimes they experienced, whether or not they reported them **[describes how the survey captures them: 1]**. So it uncovers part of the dark figure that police statistics miss, although it still cannot record crimes without a direct victim **[conclusion with qualification: 1]**.

## Common mistakes

- Defining the dark figure as "unsolved crime". It is unreported and unrecorded crime.
- Saying victim surveys capture "all crime". They miss victimless, corporate and some sensitive crimes.
- Treating a rise in recorded crime as proof that offending has increased.
- Confusing victim surveys (people as victims) with self-report studies (people as offenders).
- Describing a table without quoting any figures.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Identify:** name a source or limitation.
- **Describe:** say how a source is collected, or what a trend shows, with figures.
- **Explain:** show *why* a source is useful or limited, step by step.
- **Discuss how far:** weigh the usefulness of official statistics against the dark figure and alternative sources, using functionalist, Marxist and feminist views, and reach a judgement.
