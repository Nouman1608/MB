**The People and Economy of Pakistan is topic 3.2.2 of OxfordAQA International GCSE Pakistan
Studies 9236, a compulsory topic examined in Paper 2, Section B (28 marks).** It covers population
structure, growth and migration; how the economy has shifted between the primary, secondary and
tertiary sectors; trade and indicators of development; and urbanisation, especially the megacities
of Karachi and Lahore. Expect data-response items alongside extended answers that ask you to explain
and evaluate.

## How has Pakistan's population changed?

Pakistan's 2023 census counted about 241 million people, making it one of the most populous
countries in the world. Its **population structure** is youthful: a wide-based pyramid with a large
share of children and young adults and a small share over 65.

Growth has been rapid because the **death rate** fell sharply after independence, thanks to
vaccination, antibiotics, cleaner water and better food supply, while the **birth rate** fell much
more slowly. **Infant mortality** has fallen but remains high by international standards, and
**life expectancy** has risen. The gap between births and deaths, the **natural increase**, has
narrowed in recent decades as more girls stay in school, marriage age rises and family planning
spreads, but it remains high.

**Distribution and density:** most people live on the fertile, irrigated **Indus Plain** of Punjab
and Sindh and in large cities, where flat land, water, farming, industry and services support dense
settlement. Mountains in the north, the Balochistan plateau and deserts such as the Thar and Kharan
are sparsely populated because of steep land, aridity, extreme temperatures and limited jobs and
services.

## Why do people migrate within and from Pakistan?

**Internal migration** is mainly rural to urban: push factors include low farm incomes, small
holdings, mechanisation, floods and poor services; pull factors include jobs in factories,
construction and services, and better schools and hospitals. **International migration** includes
millions of Pakistanis working in the Gulf states, especially Saudi Arabia and the UAE, and
communities in the UK, Europe and North America. Pakistan has also received large numbers of
Afghan refugees since 1979.

**Impacts:** emigrants send **remittances** that support families and the balance of payments;
diaspora communities spread Pakistani culture abroad; but the country loses skilled workers such as
doctors and engineers ("brain drain"). Internal migration fuels urban growth and informal
settlements, while villages lose young workers.

## How has the economy changed over the past 100 years?

At independence, Pakistan's economy was overwhelmingly agricultural, with little industry. Since
then:

- **Primary sector:** agriculture's share of GDP has fallen steadily, but it still employs more than a third of workers, more than any other single sector. Farming is concentrated on the irrigated plains of Punjab and Sindh, where flat land, fertile alluvial soil, canal water and a long growing season support wheat, cotton, rice and sugar cane.
- **Secondary sector:** manufacturing grew from the 1950s, led by cotton textiles, then cement, fertiliser, sugar, steel, cars and pharmaceuticals. Factories cluster in **Karachi** (port, labour, market), **Lahore**, **Faisalabad** (textiles, close to cotton) and **Sialkot** (sports goods and surgical instruments). Textiles remain the largest manufacturing employer and exporter.
- **Tertiary and quaternary sectors:** services now produce the largest share of GDP. They include retail, transport, banking, telecommunications, health, education and government. Quaternary activities, such as software development, IT outsourcing and research, are growing in Lahore, Karachi and Islamabad, where skilled graduates, fast internet and investment are concentrated.

## How is economic development measured?

| Indicator | What it shows | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| GDP | Total value of goods and services produced in a year | Ignores inequality and the informal economy |
| GDP per capita | Average output per person | Hides gaps between rich and poor, and urban and rural |
| HDI | Combines life expectancy, education and income | Does not show regional differences |
| FDI | Investment by foreign companies, e.g. through CPEC | Can create debt or send profits abroad |
| Trade | Value of exports and imports | Swings with global prices |

Pakistan is classed by the UN Development Programme among countries with lower human development,
which reflects low adult literacy and high child mortality as well as income.

## What does Pakistan trade, and with whom?

**Exports:** textiles and clothing (the largest group), rice, leather goods, sports goods, surgical
instruments, fruit and fish. **Imports:** petroleum and LNG, machinery, chemicals, palm oil, iron and
steel and vehicles. Pakistan usually has a **trade deficit**, which **remittances** from overseas
workers help to cover. **Key partners:** China (largest source of imports), the USA (major export
market), the UAE and Saudi Arabia (oil and jobs), and the UK and EU. **Factors affecting trade:**
world prices, energy costs, exchange rates, quality standards and political relations.

**Trade blocs and institutions:** the **EU's GSP+ scheme** gives many Pakistani goods tariff-free
access to the EU; Pakistan has a free-trade agreement with China and belongs to the Economic
Cooperation Organization and SAARC. As a member of the **World Trade Organization**, it follows global
trade rules and can bring disputes to the WTO.

## What are the opportunities and challenges of urbanisation?

Around two-fifths of Pakistanis now live in urban areas, and the share is rising. **Karachi**, the
country's largest city and main port, and **Lahore**, Punjab's capital, are both megacities.
Growth is driven by rural–urban migration and natural increase, helped by flat coastal or plain
sites and transport links.

**Opportunities:** jobs in industry and services; universities and schools; innovation hubs and
start-ups; investment and infrastructure such as Lahore's Orange Line metro.
**Challenges:** not enough formal jobs, so many work informally; shortage of affordable housing and
growth of *katchi abadis*; traffic congestion; air pollution and winter smog; untreated sewage and
waste.

**Strategies:** low-cost housing schemes; upgrading informal settlements with water and sanitation,
as the community-led Orangi Pilot Project in Karachi showed; mass transit such as bus rapid transit
lines; tree planting and controls on brick kilns and crop burning to reduce smog.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why is Pakistan's population so young?** Birth rates have stayed high while death rates fell, so
each generation of children has been larger than the last.

**What are remittances?** Money sent home by Pakistanis working abroad. They are a major source of
foreign exchange and help pay for imports.

**What is the quaternary sector?** Knowledge-based work such as IT, research and consultancy, which
is growing in Pakistan's large cities.
