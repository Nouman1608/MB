This guide covers subtopic **13.1 Gravitational field** of Cambridge International AS & A Level Physics 9702, 2025–2027 series (A Level). The broader [Gravitational Fields study guide](/resources/a-physics-gravitational-fields/) introduces all four parts of Topic 13; this page goes further into the field idea itself: what makes something a field of force, why field strength is defined per unit mass, how field strengths combine, and the rules for drawing field lines that earn marks. For quick recall use the [Gravitational field revision notes](/resources/cambridge-a-level-physics-9702-a-gravitational-field-revision-notes/), and for exam-style questions the [Gravitational field practice questions](/resources/cambridge-a-level-physics-9702-a-gravitational-field-practice/).

## What 13.1 asks you to do

The syllabus lists two outcomes. Candidates should be able to:

- understand that a gravitational field is an example of a **field of force** and define gravitational field as **force per unit mass**
- represent a gravitational field by means of **field lines**

Everything else in Topic 13 builds on this: Newton's law (13.2) gives the size of the force, 13.3 turns that into a formula for field strength, and 13.4 describes the same field in energy terms.

## What a field of force is

A **field of force** is a region of space in which an object with a particular property experiences a force, without contact with the source. For a gravitational field, the property is **mass**: any mass placed in the field experiences a force towards the mass that creates the field.

Three features matter in explanations:

- **The field belongs to the source.** The Earth's field exists at a point whether or not anything is placed there. A test mass is only a way of detecting and measuring it.
- **The force acts at a distance.** No medium or contact is needed; the Moon is held in orbit across empty space.
- **Gravitational forces are always attractive.** There is no negative mass, so a gravitational field always points towards the masses that cause it. This is a key difference from electric fields, where like charges repel.

The 9702 course meets three fields of force, and the same pattern of definition runs through all of them:

| Field | What experiences the force | Field strength defined as | Unit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gravitational (Topic 13) | Any mass | Force per unit mass | N kg⁻¹ |
| Electric (Topic 18) | Any charge | Force per unit positive charge | N C⁻¹ |
| Magnetic (Topic 20) | A moving charge or a current-carrying conductor | Defined through the force on a current (flux density) | T |

## Defining gravitational field strength

The syllabus definition, which you should be able to write word for word, is that the **gravitational field strength** at a point is the **force per unit mass** acting on a mass placed at that point:

```
g = F / m
```

- g is a **vector**. Its direction is the direction of the force on the mass, towards the source.
- Its unit is **N kg⁻¹**. Since 1 N = 1 kg m s⁻², this is the same as **m s⁻²**, which is why a falling object in the field has acceleration g if no other force acts.
- The mass used to probe the field should be **small**, so that its own field does not noticeably change the field being measured or move the source.

The definition is "per unit mass" so that g describes the field, not the object. A 2 kg mass and a 20 kg mass at the same point feel forces in the ratio 1 : 10, but F/m is the same for both. Weight follows directly: an object of mass m at a point where the field strength is g experiences a gravitational force **W = mg**.

**Field strength and acceleration of free fall.** Near the Earth's surface both have the value 9.81 used in the data list. They describe different things: g as field strength is force per unit mass at a point; acceleration of free fall is what happens to an object if gravity is the only force acting on it. They are numerically equal only in that case. A book resting on a table sits in a field of 9.81 N kg⁻¹ but has zero acceleration.

## Worked example 1: measuring an unknown field

On the surface of a planet, a 0.500 kg mass hangs from a newton-meter that reads 1.86 N. The mass is at rest. Calculate the gravitational field strength, and the weight of a 12.0 kg instrument there.

```
g = F / m = 1.86 / 0.500 = 3.72 N kg⁻¹
W = mg = 12.0 × 3.72 = 44.6 N
```

*Annotation.* The newton-meter reading equals the gravitational force only because the mass is in equilibrium. Note that the answer is the same whatever test mass is used, which is the point of the definition. If the planet were rotating quickly, part of the gravitational force would be needed for circular motion and the reading would be slightly less than the full gravitational force, as in the [Centripetal acceleration guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-physics-9702-a-centripetal-acceleration/).

## Field strengths add as vectors

Because g is force per unit mass, and forces add as vectors, the field strengths from two or more masses at the same point also add as vectors.

## Worked example 2: two fields at right angles

At point P, the field due to mass A is 3.0 N kg⁻¹ directed due east, and the field due to mass B is 4.0 N kg⁻¹ directed due north. Calculate the resultant field strength at P and the force on a 2.0 kg mass placed there.

```
resultant g = √(3.0² + 4.0²) = 5.0 N kg⁻¹
angle = tan⁻¹(4.0 / 3.0) = 53° north of east
F = mg = 2.0 × 5.0 = 10 N, in the same direction
```

*Annotation.* Adding the magnitudes (7.0 N kg⁻¹) is the usual error. Only fields along the same line can be added or subtracted as plain numbers.

Along the line joining two masses, the two fields point in opposite directions. Somewhere between them they are equal in size, and the resultant field is zero. That point lies **closer to the smaller mass**, because a smaller mass needs a shorter distance to produce the same field strength. The calculation uses g = GM/r², covered in the [Gravitational field of a point mass guide](/resources/cambridge-a-level-physics-9702-a-gravitational-field-of-a-point-mass/).

## Drawing field lines

Field lines are a picture of the field. Each rule below is tied to something physical, and examiners look for each:

1. **Arrows point towards the mass.** A field line shows the direction of the force on a small mass placed on it.
2. **Spacing shows strength.** Where lines are closer together, the field is stronger.
3. **Lines never cross.** At any point the resultant force has only one direction.
4. **Lines end on masses** and come in from far away. Gravity has no source that pushes outwards, so lines never start on a mass.
5. **Lines meet the surface of a uniform sphere at right angles**, pointing at its centre.

### The two standard patterns

**Radial field** (around an isolated sphere or point mass): straight lines pointing to the centre, evenly spaced around it, getting further apart with distance. The increasing spacing shows the field weakening with distance.

**Uniform field** (close to the surface, over a region small compared with the radius of the planet): parallel, equally spaced lines, perpendicular to the surface, pointing down. The field has the same strength and direction everywhere in the region. This is the field assumed in AS mechanics, where g is taken as constant and ΔE_P = mgΔh is used.

The uniform pattern is the radial pattern seen close up. Over a few hundred metres the radial lines are almost parallel because the planet's radius is so much larger than the region drawn.

### Two masses

For two masses such as a planet and its moon, lines curve towards both bodies. Near each body they are almost radial; far away the pattern looks like that of a single mass equal to the combined mass. There is a point between them, nearer the moon, through which no line passes: the point where the resultant field is zero.

## Common mistakes

- **Defining field strength as "the force on a mass"** without "per unit mass".
- **Drawing arrows pointing away** from a mass.
- **Drawing radial lines that do not meet the surface at right angles**, or that are unevenly spaced around a uniform sphere.
- **Drawing lines that cross** or that start on a mass.
- **Adding field strengths from two masses as plain numbers** when they are not along the same line.
- **Treating field strength and acceleration of free fall as the same idea.** They have the same unit and, near the Earth, the same value, but only an object with no other forces on it accelerates at g.

## Exam technique for the command words

- **Define** (gravitational field strength): force per unit mass, and add "on a small test mass placed at that point" if space allows.
- **State** (what is meant by a field of force): a region in which a mass experiences a force.
- **Sketch** or **draw** (field lines): at least five or six lines for a sphere, evenly spaced, straight, at right angles to the surface, all with arrows towards the centre. For a uniform field, parallel lines with equal spacing.
- **Explain** (why the field near the surface is uniform): the region is small compared with the radius, so the radial lines are almost parallel and equally spaced.
- **Calculate** (field or force): use g = F/m and W = mg, with vector addition where two fields act.
