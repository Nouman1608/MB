This page is a guide to Pearson Edexcel International GCSE Chemistry (4CH1) past papers for the June 2025 and November 2025 series. For each series you get a short account of what the examiners saw, written by us from Pearson's examiner reports for Papers 1C and 2C. We do not host any papers. Every paper, mark scheme and report is on Pearson's own site, linked below.

A note on grade thresholds: this page gives none. Pearson publishes grade boundaries through an interactive tool on its website, and we could not retrieve a boundary document for these series. We would rather leave the numbers out than guess them, so each series below says where to look instead.

## Where to get the official papers

- [Pearson Edexcel International GCSE Chemistry (2017) qualification page](https://qualifications.pearson.com/en/qualifications/edexcel-international-gcses/international-gcse-chemistry-2017.html)
- [Pearson Edexcel International GCSE Chemistry specification (PDF)](https://qualifications.pearson.com/content/dam/pdf/International%20GCSE/Chemistry/2017/specification-and-sample-assessments/international-gcse-chemistry-2017-specification.pdf)
- [Pearson past papers page](https://qualifications.pearson.com/en/support/support-topics/exams/past-papers.html)
- [Pearson grade boundaries page](https://qualifications.pearson.com/en/support/support-topics/results-certification/grade-boundaries.html)

Use the three documents together. Sit the paper first, under timed conditions. Mark it strictly with the mark scheme, which tells you what each mark needs. Then read the examiner report for that paper: it explains why answers that looked right still lost marks. Our notes below are a quick way into those reports.

Check which specification a paper was set on. 4CH1 is a linear qualification: first teaching was September 2017 and the first assessment was June 2019. The specification we checked against is Issue 3, September 2024. It has two untiered written papers taken in the same series: Paper 1C (4CH1/1C, 2 hours, 110 marks, 61.1 per cent) and Paper 2C (4CH1/2C, 1 hour 15 minutes, 70 marks, 38.9 per cent). Paper 1C assesses the core content, while Paper 2C assesses all content, including the material the specification marks in bold. Papers from before June 2019 belong to an older International GCSE and should not be used for 4CH1 practice.

Pearson also publishes regional variants of these papers (for example 1CR and 2CR in June 2025). This page covers 1C and 2C only.

## November 2025

Paper 1C was described as demanding, with several unfamiliar questions that rewarded secure specification knowledge over past-paper familiarity. Paper 2C was described as well answered overall, with practical chemistry and extended answers the weaker areas.

**Where marks were lost on 4CH1 1C**

- Changes of state and states of matter: boiling and evaporation were confused, and descriptions of solids and gases talked about movement when the answer needed arrangement of particles.
- Atoms and bonding: the gas made of single atoms and the gas with a triple bond were often guessed. Drawings of a chloride ion sometimes showed the atom's outer shell. In the long bonding comparison, many candidates assumed one compound was ionic and the other covalent, then used molecular language that earned nothing. Listing many terms in the hope that one scores was penalised.
- Practical chemistry: suggestions for improving an experiment often just said repeat and average. Candidates struggled to link a fault to the direction of its effect on the result, and to explain why an insoluble salt could be separated by filtering.
- Chemical tests: candidates either knew the ion tests well or mixed up unrelated ones. Some used a reagent that interferes with the test being done.
- Organic chemistry: the conditions for cracking were often replaced by those for other processes. Equations for polymerisation lost marks when the number of monomer units was left out. The idea of a functional group was muddled with a homologous series, and isomer drawings were often the same molecule rotated.
- Calculations: working was generally sound and error carried forward helped, but rounding, forgetting a sign on an enthalpy change, and applying a percentage yield caused slips. Explaining a yield above 100 per cent in terms of the method, such as a product not fully dried, was rare.

**Where marks were lost on 4CH1 2C**

- Halogens: the explanation of the reactivity trend focused on atomic structure when displacement evidence was wanted. Answers rarely included a reaction that does not occur or a colour change.
- Isotope probability: finding the chance of one combination of chlorine isotopes was done well, but few explained why the mixed combination counts twice.
- Metals and alloys: malleability was poorly explained. Candidates forgot to compare pure iron with steel and to refer to layers of atoms sliding.
- Electrolysis: ions were treated as atoms in half-equations, balancing oxygen was hard, and the colour of universal indicator at the positive electrode was explained without reference to hydrogen ions.
- Organic chemistry: units for pressure were missing, temperature explanations for fermentation were vague, and some candidates named the wrong reactants for ethanol production.
- Practical design: control variables for comparing catalysts were poorly specified, and many methods could not give a graph from which a rate could be found.
- Energetics: the bond energy calculation where one bond energy and the overall enthalpy change were both given was found hard, and many just did a routine bond energy sum.

## June 2025

The Paper 1C report praised the first half of the paper and found practical questions the most varied. The Paper 2C report called an early question an easy starter and found a number of later questions discriminating well.

**Where marks were lost on 4CH1 1C**

- Bonding vocabulary: intermolecular forces were named where covalent bonds were meant, and the reverse. This hurt answers on simple molecular substances and on giant structures such as diamond and graphite.
- Graphite: candidates often did not mention carbon or bonding, and used molecular wording for a giant structure.
- Chromatography: dyes and inks were mixed up, and an answer that repeated the question stem earned nothing.
- Evaluating experiments: repeating and averaging was offered as a way to improve accuracy, when that improves reliability. Explanations for a single anomalous reading often blamed human error.
- Equations: state symbols for salts were wrong, and formulae were mis-written. Handwriting that made ene and ane indistinguishable cost marks.
- Organic chemistry: the product of adding bromine to an alkene was named as a substituted alkane. Definitions of saturated were incomplete.
- Redox and calculations: applied redox was weak, reacting-mass ratios were sometimes inverted, and the wrong figure was carried into a percentage yield.
- Control variables for a fuel-heating experiment were weakly explained, and an enthalpy change often missed its sign.

**Where marks were lost on 4CH1 2C**

- Energetics and rates: curves needed to start at the origin and end level. Energy-level diagrams lost marks for arrows of the wrong length or with two heads. Temperature effects on rate needed more frequent and more successful collisions.
- Bonding and conduction: water must be described as covalent and magnesium chloride as ionic. Delocalised electrons were wrongly added to ionic compounds.
- Electrolysis half-equations: chlorine was written as a single atom.
- Organic chemistry: balancing a combustion equation and drawing the displayed formula of an ester were difficult. A few answers did not give an observation when asked for one.
- Titration and practical: burette readings needed two decimal places, the mean of concordant results was usually found, and the crystallisation method often omitted cooling or filtering.
- Equilibrium: the effect of pressure was explained poorly when the number of moles of gas is the same on both sides. The long gas-volume calculation in standard form gave few full marks.

## Frequently asked questions

**Where can I find 4CH1 past papers?**
Pearson publishes papers, mark schemes and examiner reports for International GCSE Chemistry on its past papers page. Your school's exams officer can also help with recent series.

**Which 4CH1 papers do I sit?**
Everyone sits both Paper 1C and Paper 2C in the same series. There are no tiers, so practise with the pair from one series.

**Which specification were these papers set on?**
June and November 2025 were set on the 2017 International GCSE Chemistry specification, first assessed in 2019. Check the front cover of any older paper against the current specification before relying on a question.

**What mistakes do 4CH1 examiners report most often?**
In the two series covered, the most common were vague practical answers, confusion between intermolecular forces and covalent bonds, missing or wrong state symbols, and calculation slips in rounding and signs.

**Can grade thresholds tell me what mark I need next time?**
No. Thresholds change every series and should only be used as a rough guide for the series they belong to, never to predict future ones.
