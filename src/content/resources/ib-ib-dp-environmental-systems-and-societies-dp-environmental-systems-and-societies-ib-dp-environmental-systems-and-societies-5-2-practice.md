## Section A

**1.** Outline what is meant by a food system. **[2]**

**2.** State two ways in which surplus nitrogen fertiliser causes environmental harm. **[2]**

**3.** Outline how a Bt crop protects itself from insect pests. **[2]**

**4.** Distinguish between food security and food sovereignty. **[2]**

**5.** State two features of integrated pest management. **[2]**

**6.** Outline one social cost of the Green Revolution. **[2]**

## Section B

**7.** (Data response) The table shows fictional nitrogen data for three farms growing the same crop.

| Farm | N applied / kg ha⁻¹ | N in harvested crop / kg ha⁻¹ | Yield / t ha⁻¹ |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | 220 | 110 | 8.0 |
| B | 160 | 104 | 7.8 |
| C | 90 | 72 | 5.6 |

**(a)** Calculate the nitrogen use efficiency of each farm. **[3]**
**(b)** Calculate the nitrogen surplus of Farm A and Farm B. **[1]**
**(c)** Using the data, evaluate which farm offers the best balance between food production and environmental impact. **[3]**

**8.** (Data response) A fictional region must produce 30 000 t of wheat a year. Under conventional farming the yield is 6.0 t ha⁻¹; under organic farming it is 4.0 t ha⁻¹.

**(a)** Calculate the area needed under each system. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate the percentage increase in area if the region switched entirely to organic farming. **[1]**
**(c)** Discuss whether the land-sparing argument means that conventional farming is better for biodiversity. **[4]**

**9.** Explain how the Haber-Bosch process changed global food production and the nitrogen cycle. **[4]**

**10.** Explain why the decline of pollinators threatens food production, and outline one response. **[3]**

**11.** Evaluate agroforestry as a sustainable farming system. **[5]**

**12.** "Technology, not changes in consumption, is the best way to feed a growing population sustainably." To what extent do you agree? **[9]**

## Answers

**1.** All the stages involved in feeding people, from inputs and production through processing, transport, retail and consumption [1] to the disposal of waste, with the connections and impacts between them [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Any two: nitrate leaches into water and causes eutrophication [1]; soil bacteria release nitrous oxide, a greenhouse gas [1]; ammonia released to the air forms fine particles that harm health; nitrate in groundwater makes drinking water unsafe. **[2]**

**3.** A gene from the bacterium *Bacillus thuringiensis* has been inserted into the crop [1], so the plant makes a protein that is toxic to certain insect pests that feed on it [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Food security is when all people have access to enough safe and nutritious food [1]; food sovereignty is the right of peoples to decide their own food and farming systems, including control over land and seed [1]. **[2]**

**5.** Any two: crop rotation to break pest cycles [1]; use of natural predators [1]; resistant varieties; monitoring pests and spraying only when numbers pass a threshold. **[2]**

**6.** Any one, developed: farmers who could afford seed, fertiliser and irrigation gained most, increasing inequality [1], while some small farmers borrowed to buy inputs and fell into debt or lost land [1]. **[2]**

**7. (a)** A: 110 ÷ 220 × 100 = **50%** [1]; B: 104 ÷ 160 × 100 = **65%** [1]; C: 72 ÷ 90 × 100 = **80%** [1].
**(b)** A: 220 − 110 = **110 kg ha⁻¹**; B: 160 − 104 = **56 kg ha⁻¹** (both needed) [1].
**(c)** Farm C has the highest efficiency but its yield is 30% lower than Farm A's, so more land would be needed for the same food [1]. Farm A's yield is only 0.2 t ha⁻¹ higher than B's, but its surplus is almost double, greatly increasing the risk of leaching and nitrous oxide [1]. Farm B offers the best balance: a yield close to A's with a much smaller surplus [1]. **[7]**

**8. (a)** Conventional: 30 000 ÷ 6.0 = **5 000 ha** [1]; organic: 30 000 ÷ 4.0 = **7 500 ha** [1].
**(b)** (7 500 − 5 000) ÷ 5 000 × 100 = **50%** [1].
**(c)** Conventional farming could spare 2 500 ha for habitat [1]. But spared land helps biodiversity only if it is actually protected, not used for other crops [1]. Conventional farming uses more pesticide and fertiliser, which can harm species on and beyond the farm, while organic farmland usually supports more wildlife within fields [1]. So conventional farming is better for species that need large undisturbed areas only where sparing is guaranteed; for farmland species, sharing may be better [1]. **[7]**

**9.** It allowed ammonia to be made industrially from atmospheric nitrogen [1], so farmers were no longer limited by natural fixation and manure, and yields rose greatly, supporting a much larger population [1]. It greatly increased the amount of reactive nitrogen entering the cycle each year [1], so excess nitrogen now leaks into water as nitrate and into the air as nitrous oxide and ammonia [1]. **[4]**

**10.** Many fruit, vegetable and seed crops need insect pollination to set fruit or seed [1], so fewer pollinators lowers yields or quality of these crops [1]. Response, any one: restricting neonicotinoids (as the EU did for outdoor use of three in 2018); planting wildflower margins; integrated pest management [1]. **[3]**

**11.** Indicative points, one mark each:
- Trees protect soil from wind and rain erosion and add organic matter through leaf fall [1].
- Deep roots bring up nutrients and some trees (legumes) fix nitrogen, reducing fertiliser needs [1].
- Trees store carbon and provide habitat, increasing biodiversity on the farm [1].
- Farmers gain extra products (fruit, timber, fodder) and spread risk [1].
- Limitation and judgement: trees compete with crops for light and water and take years to produce, so it suits places where long-term land tenure is secure; it is sustainable but not suited to all farms [1]. **[5]**

**12.** Indicative content. Extended answers in the real exam are marked holistically against the IB's criteria, so treat these points as a guide:
- The Green Revolution shows technology can raise output greatly and reduce the land needed per tonne [1].
- Precision agriculture and improved varieties can raise nitrogen use efficiency and cut pollution [1].
- GM crops such as Bt varieties can cut insecticide use and losses to pests [1].
- But technology has created new problems: pollution, groundwater depletion, resistance in pests [1].
- Technology often benefits wealthier farmers and depends on fossil-fuel inputs [1].
- Changing consumption, by eating lower in the food chain, frees large areas of land used for feed [1].
- Reducing food waste cuts the inputs needed upstream at every stage [1].
- Consumption change depends on culture, price and choice, so it may be slow [1].
- Judgement: neither is sufficient alone; technology raises efficiency, but without lower waste and more plant-rich diets in high-consuming societies, demand will outpace gains [1]. **[9]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Inverting the NUE formula.
- Comparing farms on one measure only (efficiency or yield) instead of both.
- Assuming spared land is automatically protected.
- Describing GM crops without naming a type and its mechanism.
- Giving one-sided answers to "to what extent" questions.
