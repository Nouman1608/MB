This guide covers section 1.4, Decisions on location, in Topic 1 of the Pearson Edexcel International GCSE in Business (4BS1) specification, Issue 2, February 2026. Its one point, 1.4.1, lists "the main factors influencing location decisions and relocation of a business":

- proximity to market, labour, materials and competitors
- nature of the business activity
- the impact of the internet on location decisions: e-commerce and/or fixed premises
- legal controls and trade blocs.

Paper 1 is set on a small business with a local or national market, so its location questions are usually about a site in a town. Paper 2 is set on a large business with a multinational or global market, so its questions can be about which country to produce or sell in. The first overview, with worked site comparisons, is in the [classification of businesses and location decisions guide](/resources/edexcel-igcse-business-classification-of-businesses-and-location-decisions/). Companion pages: the [decisions on location revision notes](/resources/edexcel-igcse-business-4bs1-decisions-on-location-revision-notes/) and the [decisions on location practice questions](/resources/edexcel-igcse-business-4bs1-decisions-on-location-practice/). Businesses and figures are invented; amounts are in dollars.

## 1. Which factor matters most?

Every business weighs the same factors, but the nature of its activity decides which one wins.

| Business | Factor that usually dominates | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Quarry | Materials | It must be where the stone is |
| Steelworks | Materials and transport | Inputs are heavy and bulky |
| Soft drinks bottler | Market | Adding water makes the product heavier than its inputs |
| Café or hairdresser | Market | Customers must come to the premises |
| Software developer | Labour | It needs skilled staff more than any physical input |
| Car showroom | Competitors (nearby) | Buyers like to compare models in one area |
| Online retailer | Labour and transport links | No passing trade is needed |

In an answer, name the dominant factor for the business in the case and explain why it outweighs the others.

## 2. Materials or market: a transport test

When transport is a large cost, compare what has to be moved.

### Worked example 1: a metal processor

A processor uses 4 tonnes of ore to make 1 tonne of metal. The mine and the main market are 300 km apart, and transport costs 0.20 per tonne per kilometre.

- **Locate near the mine:** carry 1 tonne of metal 300 km: 1 × 300 × 0.20 = **60** per tonne of output.
- **Locate near the market:** carry 4 tonnes of ore 300 km: 4 × 300 × 0.20 = **240** per tonne of output.

Locating near the materials saves 180 per tonne. The product loses weight in processing, so it is cheaper to process at the source.

A soft drinks bottler is the opposite: it adds water near customers rather than transporting heavy bottles a long way. When the product gains weight or becomes fragile or perishable, proximity to market usually wins.

## 3. Labour

Proximity to labour is about the number of workers, their skills and their cost.

- A small business needs a few people with the right skills within travelling distance.
- A large business may compare whole countries on wage levels and skills.
- Lower wages are not always cheaper overall: less-skilled workers may produce less, need more training or make more mistakes.

## 4. Competitors

Being near competitors can help: customers already travel to the area, suppliers and skilled workers are nearby, and the area becomes known for the product. It can also hurt: customers compare prices easily and rivals compete for the same staff. A business with a distinctive product may gain from a cluster; one selling the same thing as everyone else may do better where there is no direct rival.

## 5. The internet: e-commerce and/or fixed premises

The specification's wording, "e-commerce and/or fixed premises", points to three choices.

| Choice | Location effect | Suits |
|---|---|---|
| E-commerce only | Premises can be a cheap warehouse anywhere with good transport links | Goods that are easy to post and do not need to be seen first |
| Fixed premises only | Location must be where customers can reach it | Services done in person, such as haircuts |
| Both | A smaller shop or showroom plus a website and delivery | Goods customers like to see, try or collect, as well as order |

The internet widens the market for a small business without a move: a Paper 1 business can sell nationally from one town. For a Paper 2 business it can reduce the number of shops needed in each country.

## 6. Legal controls and trade blocs

**Legal controls.** Governments set rules on how land may be used, on pollution, noise and safety. They can rule out sites, add costs or push some activities away from housing. Describe them in general terms; you are not asked for particular laws.

**Trade blocs.** A trade bloc is a group of countries that agree to reduce barriers to trade between themselves (a standard definition; the specification names trade blocs without defining them). A business outside a bloc may pay tariffs to sell into it, so locating inside can make its goods cheaper for customers there.

### Worked example 2: a Paper 2 location choice

Calloway Bikes plc needs a new factory with 400 workers to supply a market inside a trade bloc. Annual output will be worth 50 million.

| | Country X (outside the bloc) | Country Y (inside the bloc) |
|---|---:|---:|
| Average wage per worker | 9,000 | 15,000 |
| Total wages | 3.6 million | 6.0 million |
| Tariff on goods entering the bloc | 12% of 50 million = 6.0 million | None |
| Wages plus tariff | 9.6 million | 6.0 million |

Country X saves 2.4 million in wages but pays 6.0 million in tariffs, so Country Y is 3.6 million a year cheaper on these costs. The judgement could change if the tariff were cut, if Country X workers were more productive, or if Calloway also planned to sell outside the bloc.

## 7. Relocation

The same factors explain why businesses move: the original reason for the site no longer holds, or a better one appears. Typical triggers are rising rent, customers or suppliers moving, a need for more space, new legal controls, or a change in trade policy. Relocation brings one-off costs, the risk of losing staff who will not move, and disruption to customers.

### Worked example 3: should a small business move?

Penfold Bakery pays rent of 2,400 a month in the town centre. A unit on the edge of town costs 1,500 a month, but the owner expects walk-in sales to fall by 6,000 a year (the lost profit on those sales). Moving costs 9,000 once.

- Rent saving: (2,400 − 1,500) × 12 = 10,800 a year.
- Net yearly gain: 10,800 − 6,000 = 4,800.
- Time to recover the move: 9,000 ÷ 4,800 = **1.9 years**.

The figures favour moving, but the sales estimate is uncertain. If the bakery can replace walk-in sales with online orders and delivery, the case gets stronger.

## 8. Annotated "evaluate" answer

**Evaluate whether Penfold Bakery should relocate.**

- *For:* the move saves 10,800 a year in rent and pays for itself in under two years.
- *Against:* the bakery relies on passing customers, so its sales could fall more than expected, and regulars may not follow.
- *Other factors:* online ordering could replace lost walk-in trade; the new unit may give space to supply cafés.
- *Judgement:* move only if the owner can build online or wholesale orders, because the saving depends on the sales estimate being right.

## Common mistakes

- Naming factors without saying which one matters most for the business in the case.
- Treating low wages as automatically cheaper without considering skills and productivity.
- Saying the internet removes the need for premises; it changes which premises.
- Ignoring tariffs when a large business chooses between countries inside and outside a trade bloc.
- Comparing yearly savings with a one-off cost without saying how long recovery takes.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why do some businesses locate near their raw materials?**
When the product loses weight in processing, it is cheaper to carry the finished goods than the heavier materials.

**How can a trade bloc affect where a business locates?**
Locating inside the bloc can avoid tariffs on goods sold to its members, making them cheaper for customers there.
