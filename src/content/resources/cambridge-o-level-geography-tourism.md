This guide covers **subtopic 3.4 Tourism** in Theme 3 Economic
Development of Cambridge O Level Geography (2217), 2026 series
(Version 3). It goes deeper than the
[Economic Development study guide](/resources/o-level-geography-economic-development/),
which introduces all seven Theme 3 subtopics in one place.

## What 3.4 asks you to do

The syllabus content for tourism has three parts and one case study:

| Part | What you must be able to do |
|---|---|
| Growth | Describe and explain the growth of tourism in relation to the main attractions of the physical and human landscape |
| Impact | Evaluate the benefits and disadvantages of tourism to receiving areas |
| Management | Show that careful management of tourism is needed for it to be sustainable |
| Case study | An area where tourism is important |

Notice the command words. "Describe and explain" growth means saying
*what* attracts visitors and *why* numbers have risen. "Evaluate" impacts
means weighing benefits against disadvantages and reaching a judgement.
"Demonstrate an understanding" of management means showing *how* a
named measure keeps tourism going without destroying what attracts
visitors in the first place.

## Why tourism has grown

Explanations fall into two groups.

**Attractions of the area (pull factors)**

- *Physical landscape*: beaches and warm, sunny climates; coral reefs;
  mountains and snow for skiing; volcanoes, waterfalls and national
  parks with wildlife.
- *Human landscape*: historic buildings and ruins, religious sites,
  museums, cities with shopping and nightlife, festivals, theme parks,
  and local culture and food.

**Changes that make travel easier (enabling factors)**

- Rising incomes and more paid holiday in many countries, especially
  among the growing middle class of newly industrialising economies.
- Cheaper and faster air travel, including budget airlines and
  long-haul jets, which bring distant destinations within reach.
- The internet: online booking, price comparison and social media
  that advertise places widely.
- Governments that promote tourism, build airports and resorts, and
  ease visa rules because tourism earns foreign currency.

A good "explain" answer links the two: a coastline has had beaches for
thousands of years, but it only became a major destination once an
airport, hotels and affordable flights allowed large numbers to reach
it.

## Benefits and disadvantages for receiving areas

A **receiving area** is the place visitors travel to. Sort impacts
into economic, social and environmental groups so your answer covers
the full range.

| | Benefits | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|
| Economic | Jobs in hotels, transport, guiding and crafts; foreign currency; taxes fund services; the **multiplier effect** (spending by tourists and workers supports farms, builders and shops) | Many jobs are seasonal, low-paid or held by outsiders; **leakage** of profits to foreign-owned hotel chains and tour operators; dependence on one industry that collapses if visitors stop coming |
| Social | New roads, airports, water supply and electricity that local people can also use; money to protect heritage; cultural exchange | Rising land and house prices; crowding; loss of traditional ways of life; behaviour that offends local customs |
| Environmental | Entry fees and taxes can pay for conservation of reefs, forests and wildlife | Damage to reefs and dunes; litter and sewage; heavy water use; footpath erosion; noise; air pollution from flights |

The key evaluation point is that the *balance* depends on who controls
tourism. Where hotels are locally owned and buy local food, more money
stays in the area and the multiplier effect is stronger. Where large
foreign companies own the resorts and import supplies, leakage is high
and local people gain less.

## Sustainable tourism management

**Sustainable tourism** meets the needs of today's visitors and host
communities while protecting the environment and culture so that
tourism can continue in the future. Measures you can use:

- **Limiting numbers**: permits, quotas or timed tickets at sensitive
  sites.
- **Zoning**: separating busy resort areas from protected zones.
- **Protected areas**: marine parks and national parks with rules on
  fishing, building and access.
- **Ecotourism**: small-scale, low-impact tourism that funds
  conservation and employs local people.
- **Local ownership and training**: so more income stays in the area.
- **Spreading demand**: promoting off-season visits and less-visited
  places.
- **Infrastructure**: proper sewage treatment, waste collection and
  water supply sized for peak numbers.

Always explain *why* the measure is sustainable: what it protects, and
how it keeps the area attractive to visitors in future.

## Case study: the Maldives

The Maldives is a small island country in the Indian Ocean, south-west
of India and Sri Lanka. It is made up of about 1,200 low-lying coral
islands grouped into atolls; most land is only a metre or two above sea
level. Tourism is its largest industry.

- **Attractions**: white coral-sand beaches, clear warm water, coral
  reefs for diving and snorkelling, and a tropical climate. These are
  physical attractions; the luxury resorts themselves are the main
  human attraction.
- **Growth**: the first resorts opened in the early 1970s. The
  international airport near the capital, Malé, and long-haul flights
  made the islands accessible to visitors from Europe and Asia.
- **Management approach**: resorts are mostly built on otherwise
  uninhabited islands, often described as "one island, one resort".
  This keeps large numbers of visitors apart from local communities.
- **Benefits**: tourism employs many people, earns foreign currency and
  funds airports, ports and services.
- **Disadvantages**: many resorts are foreign-owned, so profits leak
  abroad; most food and building materials are imported; resorts
  produce large amounts of rubbish, much of it taken to the island of
  Thilafushi near Malé, which became a landfill island; reefs are
  damaged by anchors, divers and construction, and suffer coral
  bleaching during marine heatwaves.
- **Longer-term risk**: because the islands are so low, sea-level rise
  threatens the beaches and land that the industry depends on.

When you use this or any case study, make every point place-specific:
name Malé, the atolls, the reefs and the resort-island policy, rather
than writing about "a tropical island" in general.

## Worked example: an evaluate question

**Question:** For a named area, evaluate the benefits and disadvantages
of tourism for local people. **[6]**

```
Named area:    the Maldives
Benefit:       tourism is the largest industry, providing jobs in
               resorts, boats and the airport, and income that pays
               for public services
Disadvantage:  many resorts are foreign-owned and most supplies are
               imported, so much of the money leaks out of the country;
               resort waste has created a landfill island near Malé
Judgement:     tourism brings real economic gains, but local people
               gain less than visitor spending suggests because of
               leakage, and the reefs and beaches that attract visitors
               are at risk -- so the benefits last only if waste and
               reef damage are managed
```

The answer earns its top marks from the final line: a judgement that
depends on evidence, not a list of points.

## Common mistakes

- Listing attractions without explaining why tourism *grew* (cheaper
  flights, higher incomes, internet booking).
- Writing only about benefits when the command word is "evaluate".
- Confusing "sustainable" with "popular": sustainable means it can
  continue without destroying the attraction.
- Naming a management measure without saying how it protects the area.
- Using a country name with no specific detail, such as a named place,
  feature or policy.
- Forgetting that impacts fall on different groups: hotel owners,
  workers, residents and the environment.

## Exam technique for Cambridge command words

- **Describe**: say what is there or what is happening, using evidence
  from a resource (photographs, graphs of visitor numbers).
- **Explain**: give reasons, linked with "because" or "so".
- **Suggest**: apply your knowledge to an unfamiliar place in a
  resource; more than one sensible answer is accepted.
- **Evaluate**: weigh both sides, then judge.

In the case-study question, plan four lines before you write: name the
area, two specific facts, one weakness or disadvantage, and a judgement.
