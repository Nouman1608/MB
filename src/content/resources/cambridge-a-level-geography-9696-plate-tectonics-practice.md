## Section A

**1.** Define the term "lithosphere". **[2]**

**2.** State **two** differences between oceanic crust and continental crust. **[2]**

**3.** Explain why oceanic crust is much younger than the oldest continental crust. **[3]**

**4.** At a convergent boundary between two oceanic plates, name the landform formed where the subducting plate bends down, and the landform formed on the overriding plate. **[2]**

**5.** Explain what is meant by "slab pull". **[2]**

**6.** Outline the processes that occur at a conservative plate boundary. **[3]**

## Section B

**7.** The table shows the age of basalt at different distances east of the axis of a fictional ocean ridge.

| Distance from ridge axis (km) | 0 | 30 | 75 | 150 | 240 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Age of basalt (million years) | 0 | 1.0 | 2.5 | 5.0 | 8.0 |

**(a)** Using the basalt at 150 km, calculate the rate at which the plate is moving away from the ridge, in cm per year. Show your working. **[2]**

**(b)** Assuming the plate on the other side moves at the same rate, state the rate at which the ocean is widening. **[1]**

**(c)** Explain how magnetic stripes on the sea floor support the theory of sea floor spreading. **[3]**

**8.** A fictional oceanic plate X, about 7 km thick, moves east towards a continental plate Y, about 40 km thick. Earthquake foci beneath plate Y become deeper towards the east, from about 20 km near the coast to about 300 km at 400 km inland. A chain of volcanoes and a high mountain range run parallel to the coast.

**(a)** Identify the type of plate boundary. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain the pattern of earthquake depths. **[3]**

**(c)** Explain the formation of the mountain range and volcanoes on plate Y. **[4]**

**9.** Explain the formation of an ocean ridge. **[5]**

**10.** Compare the processes and landforms of oceanic-continental convergence with those of continental-continental collision. **[6]**

**11.** Describe and explain the global pattern of ocean ridges, ocean trenches and fold mountains. **[6]**

**12.** "Subduction is the most important process in forming the landforms found at plate boundaries." Discuss this view. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** The rigid outer layer of the Earth [1], made up of the crust and the uppermost part of the mantle, which is broken into tectonic plates [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Two from: oceanic crust is denser than continental crust [1]; oceanic crust is thinner, mostly 5 to 10 km against 30 to 70 km [1]. Accept that oceanic crust is younger, or that it is mainly basalt while continental crust is mainly granite-type rock, in place of either. **[2]**

**3.** Oceanic crust is continually created at ocean ridges by sea floor spreading [1] and destroyed by subduction at trenches [1], so none survives much beyond about 200 million years, whereas continental crust is too buoyant to be subducted and can survive for billions of years [1]. **[3]**

**4.** An ocean trench [1] and a volcanic island arc [1]. **[2]**

**5.** The pulling force exerted on a plate by its cold, dense edge sinking into the mantle at a subduction zone [1], which drags the rest of the plate towards the trench [1]. **[2]**

**6.** Two plates slide past each other along a transform fault [1]. Crust is neither created nor destroyed [1]. Friction locks the plates so that stress builds up and is released suddenly as earthquakes [1]. **[3]**

**7. (a)** 150 km in 5 million years = 30 km per million years [1]; 3 000 000 cm / 1 000 000 years = 3 cm per year [1].

**(b)** 6 cm per year [1].

**(c)** As basalt cools at the ridge, magnetic minerals align with the Earth's magnetic field at that time [1]. The field reverses periodically, so the sea floor forms stripes of normal and reversed polarity [1]. The stripes are symmetrical on either side of the ridge and get older away from it, showing that new crust forms at the ridge and moves outwards [1]. **[6]**

**8. (a)** A convergent (destructive) oceanic-continental boundary [1].

**(b)** Plate X is denser and is subducted eastwards beneath plate Y [1]. Earthquakes occur where the descending slab grinds against and breaks within the surrounding rock [1], so foci follow the sloping slab, becoming deeper the further inland it has descended [1].

**(c)** As plate X sinks it is heated and releases water into the mantle above, lowering its melting point [1], so magma forms and rises to erupt as a chain of volcanoes parallel to the coast [1]. Compression where the plates converge crumples and thickens the crust of plate Y [1], folding and uplifting rocks and sediments into fold mountains [1]. **[8]**

**9.** Plates move apart at a divergent boundary [1]. Pressure falls in the hot mantle beneath, causing partial melting [1]. Basaltic magma rises and fills the gap, forming new oceanic crust by sea floor spreading [1]. Repeated eruption and intrusion build a long, raised submarine mountain range [1], with a central rift valley where the crust is pulled apart and transform faults crossing it [1]. **[5]**

**10.** In both, plates move towards each other and the crust is compressed, forming fold mountains [1]. At oceanic-continental boundaries, the denser oceanic plate is subducted [1], forming a trench offshore [1] and a chain of volcanoes from rising magma, as in the Andes [1]. In continental-continental collision, neither plate is dense enough to be subducted far, so crust crumples and thickens [1], producing very high fold mountains such as the Himalayas, with no trench and few volcanoes [1]. **[6]**

**11.** Ocean ridges run through the middle of the Atlantic and Indian Oceans and along the eastern Pacific [1], because they mark divergent boundaries where plates move apart [1]. Trenches are concentrated around the margins of the Pacific Ocean [1], where oceanic plates are subducted at convergent boundaries [1]. Fold mountains form a belt from the Alps to the Himalayas and along the western Americas [1], where plates converge and compress continental crust [1]. **[6]**

**12.** For the view: subduction forms ocean trenches [1]. Melting of the subducted slab produces magma for volcanic island arcs and continental volcanic chains [1]. Subduction drives compression that helps build fold mountains such as the Andes [1]. Slab pull at subduction zones is thought to be the main force moving plates, so it influences landforms elsewhere [1].

Against the view: ocean ridges and rift valleys form by sea floor spreading at divergent boundaries, with no subduction [1]. The highest fold mountains, the Himalayas, form by continental collision rather than ongoing subduction [1]. Conservative boundaries produce fault landforms by lateral movement alone [1].

Judgement: subduction explains the landforms of most convergent boundaries, but divergent and collision boundaries show that sea floor spreading and collision are equally important elsewhere, so the view is only partly valid [1]. **[8]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Forgetting to convert km per million years into cm per year.
- Confusing the half-rate of spreading with the full rate.
- Explaining melting at subduction zones simply as "friction".
- Describing earthquake patterns without linking them to the subducting slab.
- Discussing tectonic hazards when the question is about processes and landforms.
