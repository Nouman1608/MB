## What this sub-topic covers

| Spec point | What you must be able to do |
|---|---|
| 5.12 | Understand how restriction enzymes cut DNA at specific sites and ligase enzymes join pieces of DNA together |
| 5.13 | Understand how plasmids and viruses can act as vectors, which take up pieces of DNA and insert this recombinant DNA into other cells |
| 5.14 | Understand how large amounts of human insulin can be made from genetically modified bacteria grown in a fermenter |
| 5.15 | Understand how genetically modified plants can be used to improve food production |
| 5.16 | Understand that the term transgenic means the transfer of genetic material from one species to a different species |

**Genetic modification** (genetic engineering) means changing the genetic material of an organism by adding, removing or changing genes, usually by moving a gene from one organism into another.

The idea works because all living things use DNA as their genetic code, and cells read it in the same way. A human gene put into a bacterium can therefore be read by the bacterium and used to make the human protein.

## 5.12 The tools: restriction enzymes and ligase

### Restriction enzymes

A **restriction enzyme** is an enzyme that cuts DNA at a **specific sequence** of bases. Each restriction enzyme recognises one particular sequence and cuts there, and nowhere else. This is why the cuts are made at specific sites. Many restriction enzymes leave short single-stranded ends that can pair with matching ends from other DNA cut with the same enzyme. If two pieces of DNA are cut with the **same** restriction enzyme, their ends fit together.

### Ligase

**DNA ligase** is an enzyme that joins pieces of DNA together. It seals the gap between the inserted piece and the vector. DNA joined from two different sources is called **recombinant DNA**.

| Enzyme | Job | Memory aid |
|---|---|---|
| Restriction enzyme | Cuts DNA at specific sites | Restriction = cut |
| Ligase | Joins DNA pieces | Ligase = glue |

## 5.13 Vectors: plasmids and viruses

A **vector** is something used to carry a gene into another cell. The specification names two vectors.

- **Plasmids:** small circular rings of DNA found in bacteria, separate from the main chromosome. They can be cut, have a gene added, and then be taken up by bacteria.
- **Viruses:** viruses naturally inject their DNA into cells, so they can carry a gene into the cells of other organisms, including plant and animal cells.

The vector must be cut with the same restriction enzyme as the gene you want, so that the ends match.

## 5.14 Making human insulin with bacteria

Diabetes (type 1) is caused by too little insulin. In the past, insulin was extracted from the pancreas of pigs and cattle. Genetically modified bacteria now make **human insulin**.

### The steps

1. **Isolate the gene.** A restriction enzyme cuts the human insulin gene out of human DNA.
2. **Cut the vector.** The same restriction enzyme cuts open a bacterial plasmid.
3. **Join.** DNA ligase joins the insulin gene into the plasmid, forming a **recombinant plasmid**.
4. **Insert.** The recombinant plasmids are mixed with bacteria, and some bacteria take them up.
5. **Grow.** The modified bacteria are grown in a **fermenter** under controlled conditions (aseptic precautions, nutrients, optimum temperature and pH, oxygenation, agitation).
6. **Copy and express.** As the bacteria divide, they copy the plasmid. Each bacterium reads the insulin gene and makes **human insulin**.
7. **Extract.** The insulin is separated from the bacteria and purified, then used by patients.

### Why use bacteria?

- They reproduce very quickly, so there are large numbers.
- They make large amounts of insulin cheaply.
- The insulin is identical to human insulin, so it is less likely to cause an immune reaction.
- It avoids the need to kill animals, and the supply does not depend on animal numbers.

### Worked example 1: scaling up

*A fermenter holds 5000 dm³ of culture. Each dm³ yields 0.4 g of insulin. How much insulin does one batch give?*

```
5000 × 0.4 = 2000 g = 2 kg
```

One batch gives 2 kg of insulin (the value is illustrative). This shows why a fermenter, not a small flask, is used.

### Worked example 2: ordering the steps

*Put these statements in order: (A) bacteria are grown in a fermenter; (B) ligase joins the gene into the plasmid; (C) the plasmid is cut with a restriction enzyme; (D) the insulin gene is cut from human DNA; (E) bacteria take up the recombinant plasmid.*

The order is **D, C, B, E, A**. The gene and plasmid are cut with the same enzyme, they are joined, the plasmid enters bacteria and the bacteria are grown to make the product.

### Worked example 3: bacterial growth

*One modified bacterium is placed in a fermenter and divides every 30 minutes. How many bacteria are there after 6 hours?*

```
6 hours = 12 divisions of 30 minutes
2¹² = 4096
```

There are 4096 bacteria, each with a copy of the recombinant plasmid. The numbers rise quickly, so more insulin is made.

## 5.15 GM plants and food production

Genes can be transferred into crop plants to give them useful features. Examples:

| Feature | How it helps food production |
|---|---|
| Resistance to insect pests (a gene for a toxin that harms the pest) | Less damage and less use of pesticides, so higher yield |
| Resistance to herbicide | Farmers can spray weeds without killing the crop |
| Added nutrients (for example rice that makes a vitamin A precursor) | Helps to prevent malnutrition |
| Resistance to drought, salt or disease | Crops can grow in harsher places |
| Longer shelf life | Less food is wasted |

### Benefits and concerns

| Benefits | Concerns |
|---|---|
| Increased yield and food supply | Genes might spread to wild plants (cross-pollination) |
| Lower use of pesticides | Possible effects on other organisms, such as harmless insects |
| Improved nutrition | Unknown long-term effects on health |
| Crops can grow in difficult conditions | Seeds can be expensive, or controlled by a company |
| | Reduced biodiversity if one GM variety replaces others |

Make sure to give both sides in an evaluation question. A conclusion should be balanced, for example: "GM crops have clear benefits for food supply but need careful testing and monitoring."

## 5.16 Transgenic organisms

An organism is **transgenic** if it contains genetic material that has been transferred from a **different species**. The bacteria that make human insulin are transgenic, because they carry a human gene. A crop with a gene from a bacterium is also transgenic.

This is the difference from selective breeding, in which genes are moved only within a species by breeding.

### Worked example 4: decide if an organism is transgenic

*Rice is given a gene from a daffodil so that it makes more vitamin A precursor. Wheat is bred with a different wheat variety to improve yield. Which is transgenic?*

The rice is transgenic, because the gene comes from a different species. The wheat is not, because the cross is within one species.

## Common mistakes

- Saying that restriction enzymes "join" DNA. They cut. Ligase joins.
- Using different restriction enzymes for the gene and the plasmid. They must be the same so the ends match.
- Saying that bacteria "make the insulin gene". They read the inserted gene and make the protein.
- Writing that the plasmid is the "chromosome" of the bacterium. A plasmid is an extra small ring of DNA.
- Saying that genetic engineering is the same as selective breeding.
- Giving only advantages for GM plants when asked to evaluate.
- Saying GM bacteria make insulin in the human body. They make it in the fermenter, and it is then purified.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why must the same restriction enzyme cut both the gene and the plasmid?**
It leaves matching ends on both, so they can join together.

**Why can bacteria read a human gene?**
All living things use the same genetic code, so bacteria can read the gene and make the human protein.

**Is the insulin from GM bacteria different from human insulin?**
No. It has the same structure as human insulin.
