Social identity is how we see ourselves and how others see us because of the groups we belong to.
Key question 2.3 of Cambridge IGCSE Sociology (0495) asks *What influences our social identity?* The
syllabus answers through four areas: the key aspects of identity (age, gender, ethnicity and social
class), the digital self and online identities, the influence of globalisation, and postmodernist
claims that identity is now chosen rather than given. Exam answers should show how each influence
works and weigh the claim that people freely choose who they are.

This guide extends the identity section of the
[Paper 1 overview guide](/resources/igcse-sociology-research-methods-identity-and-inequality/).

## Syllabus coverage

Cambridge IGCSE Sociology 0495 (2025–2027), **2.3 What influences our social identity?**

- **2.3.1 Social identity** — key aspects: age, gender, ethnicity, social class; impact of each on
  individuals and social groups in different societies.
- **2.3.2 The digital self and online identities** — positive and negative impacts of social
  networks, social media and virtual communities on identity.
- **2.3.3 Globalisation** — (a) influence on individual and social/cultural identities, e.g.
  cultural defence, hybrid identities, homogenisation of identities; (b) positive and negative
  impacts on identity of cultural diversity, multiculturalism and global culture.
- **2.3.4 Postmodernist views of identity as chosen rather than given** — digital identities;
  identities linked to consumption patterns and lifestyle in a "pick and mix" society.

## How does age shape identity?

Age categories such as childhood, youth, adulthood and old age are **socially constructed** — their
meaning varies between societies and over time. In some societies young people take on adult work
and marriage early; in others, long education extends youth into the twenties. Jane Pilcher (*Age
and Generation in Modern Britain*, 1995) shows age as a source of both identity and inequality.
Older people may be respected as wise elders in some cultures and stereotyped as dependent in
others.

## How does gender shape identity?

Sociologists distinguish **sex** (biological) from **gender** (socially learned masculinity and
femininity). Ann Oakley (1972) described how families shape gender identity through manipulation
and canalisation. Gender identities differ widely between societies, and in many places they are
changing as women enter education and paid work — though expectations about domestic roles often
change more slowly.

## How does ethnicity shape identity?

Ethnicity refers to shared culture, such as language, religion, customs, history and sense of
origin. Ethnic identity can be strengthened by family, religious institutions and community
festivals, and by experiences of discrimination that draw a group together. Tariq Modood and
colleagues' Fourth National Survey of Ethnic Minorities in Britain (*Ethnic Minorities in Britain*,
1997) found that religion was an important part of identity for many minority ethnic respondents.

## How does social class shape identity?

Social class is based on economic position — occupation, income and wealth. Class can influence
accent, tastes, leisure, values and aspirations. Pierre Bourdieu argued that **cultural capital**
(knowledge, tastes and skills valued by dominant groups) is passed on in families and helps
reproduce class. Some sociologists argue class identity is weakening as traditional industries
decline; others point out that class still shapes life chances.

## What is the digital self?

The **digital self** is the identity people present and build online through profiles, posts,
images and interactions.

| Positive impacts | Negative impacts |
|---|---|
| Freedom to explore and express identity, especially for marginalised young people | Pressure to present a perfect image; comparison and low self-esteem |
| Virtual communities connect people with shared interests worldwide | Cyberbullying and online harassment |
| Maintaining ties with family across distance, including migrant families | "Filter bubbles" reinforce narrow views |
| Platforms for activism and minority voices | Privacy risks and permanent digital records |

danah boyd's *It's Complicated* (2014), based on interviews with American teenagers, argued that
young people use social media to extend their social lives rather than replace them, while Sherry
Turkle's *Alone Together* (2011) warned that constant connection may weaken face-to-face
relationships.

## How does globalisation influence identity?

**Globalisation** is the growing interconnection of societies through trade, travel, migration,
media and technology. The syllabus names three possible influences:

- **Homogenisation of identities** — global brands, media and platforms make young people in many
  countries share similar fashions, music and values, sometimes described as Westernisation.
- **Hybrid identities** — people combine elements of different cultures, for example the children
  of migrants mixing the language, food and music of two societies. Stuart Hall wrote about
  identities formed in diasporas.
- **Cultural defence** — when a culture feels threatened, people may strengthen their traditional
  or religious identity in response.

**Impacts of diversity (2.3.3 b):**

| | Positive | Negative |
|---|---|---|
| **Cultural diversity** | Exposure to new foods, languages, ideas | Misunderstanding and prejudice |
| **Multiculturalism** | Different groups keep their identities while sharing citizenship | Critics claim it can lead to separate communities |
| **Global culture** | Shared global connections, global youth culture | Loss of local languages and traditions |

The global success of South Korean pop music and television is a useful example: it shows global
culture is not only Western, and that local cultures can become global.

## Is identity now chosen rather than given?

**Postmodernist view (2.3.4):** in a "pick and mix" society people build identities from
consumption and lifestyle — brands, music, fashion, fitness, online communities — rather than
inheriting them from class, religion or place. Zygmunt Bauman described modern life as "liquid",
with identities constantly being remade. **Digital identities** allow people to present different
selves on different platforms.

**Criticisms:** choice depends on money and access; class, gender and ethnicity still limit options;
many people keep strong inherited identities based on family and religion; postmodernism may
describe affluent young people more than others.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the difference between hybrid identity and homogenisation?** Homogenisation means
identities becoming the same; hybridity means new mixed identities that combine different cultures.

**Does social media make identity more free?** It can give more room to experiment, but online
identities are also shaped by peer pressure, algorithms and the platform's rules.

**How should I answer "Evaluate the view that identity is chosen"?** Set postmodernist arguments
about consumption and digital identities against evidence that age, gender, ethnicity and class
still shape identity, then give a judgement.
