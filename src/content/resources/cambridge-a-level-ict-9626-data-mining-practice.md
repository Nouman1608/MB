## Section A

**1.** Define the term data mining. **[2]**

**2.** State the **six** stages of the data mining process in order. **[3]**

**3.** Describe what happens in the data preparation stage. **[2]**

**4.** Give **two** reasons why data mining is used in healthcare. **[2]**

**5.** State **one** advantage of keeping all information in one place for data mining. **[1]**

## Section B

**6.** Fairhaven Stores analyses 500 transactions. 150 contain pasta, and 90 of these also contain pasta sauce.

**(a)** Calculate the proportion of all transactions that contain both pasta and pasta sauce, as a percentage. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the percentage of pasta buyers who also bought pasta sauce. **[2]**

**(c)** Suggest **one** action that Fairhaven Stores could take using this result. **[1]**

**7.** A hospital has 2,400 past patient records. It uses 75% to build a model and keeps the rest to test it. The model predicts correctly for 522 of the test records.

**(a)** Calculate the number of records in the training set and in the test set. **[2]**

**(b)** Calculate the accuracy of the model as a percentage. **[2]**

**(c)** Explain why the model is tested on records that were not used to build it. **[2]**

**8.** A company has 12,000 customer records. A check shows that 3% are duplicates.

**(a)** Calculate how many records are duplicates. **[1]**

**(b)** Explain why the duplicates should be removed during the data preparation stage. **[2]**

**9.** Explain what is meant by the evaluation stage and what may happen after it. **[3]**

**10.** A bank wants to use data mining to reduce credit card fraud. Describe how the bank would carry out the **business understanding** and **deployment** stages. **[4]**

**11.** A government agency uses data mining on telephone and travel records to look for threats to national security. Discuss the ethical and privacy concerns of this use. **[6]**

**12.** A supermarket chain offers a loyalty card. Evaluate the advantages and disadvantages of the chain's use of data mining on loyalty card data, for the chain and for customers. **[6]**

**13.** Explain, with an example, how data mining is used to study social and economic trends. **[3]**

---

## Answers

**1.** The process of searching large sets of data [1] to find patterns, links or trends that were not previously known and can support decisions [1]. **[2]**

**2.** Business understanding [1], data understanding [1], data preparation, data modelling, evaluation, deployment [1 for the last four in the correct order]. **[3]**

**3.** The data is made ready for mining [1]: errors, duplicates and missing values are cleaned, relevant fields are selected and data from different sources is converted to a common format and combined [1]. **[2]**

**4.** Any two from: finding links between symptoms, treatments and outcomes to improve diagnosis [1]; identifying disease outbreaks early [1]; planning hospital resources such as beds and staff [1]; finding which treatments work best for which groups of patients [1]. **[2]**

**5.** Any one from: a complete view of the data can be analysed without searching many systems [1]; patterns across different sets of data are easier to find [1]; less duplication of data [1]. **[1]**

**6. (a)** 90 ÷ 500 [1] = 18% [1].

**(b)** 90 ÷ 150 [1] = 60% [1].

**(c)** For example: place pasta sauce next to the pasta, or offer a discount on both together [1]. **[5]**

**7. (a)** Training set: 0.75 × 2,400 = 1,800 [1]; test set: 2,400 − 1,800 = 600 [1].

**(b)** 522 ÷ 600 [1] = 87% [1].

**(c)** A model can fit the records used to build it too closely [1]. Testing on unseen records gives a more honest measure of how well it will work on new patients [1]. **[6]**

**8. (a)** 0.03 × 12,000 = 360 [1].

**(b)** Duplicates count some customers more than once [1], which gives results that are biased and wrong, such as inflated totals [1]. They also waste storage and processing time. **[3]**

**9.** The model is tested for accuracy on data that was not used to build it [1] and checked against the aims set in business understanding [1]. If the results are not good enough, the team returns to an earlier stage, such as data preparation or modelling, and repeats the work [1]. **[3]**

**10.** Business understanding: decide the objective, such as reducing fraud losses by a stated amount [1]; agree the budget, the time scale and how success will be measured [1]. Deployment: the model is put into the bank's systems, for example to score each card payment as it happens [1] and alert staff or block suspicious payments [1]. The model should then be monitored, because patterns of fraud change. **[4]**

**11.** Privacy: records about innocent people are collected and examined, without their knowledge or consent [1]. Data collected for one purpose is used for another [1]. Profiling could wrongly label a person as a threat because they fit a pattern, leading to unfair treatment [1]. Large stores of sensitive data could be stolen or misused [1]. Constant surveillance may deter people from lawful activity such as free speech [1]. Balance: it may help prevent serious attacks [1], and safeguards such as laws, oversight and deleting data when it is not needed can reduce the concerns [1]. Maximum 6 marks. **[6]**

**12.** Chain: finds buying patterns to plan stock and promotions [1]; targeted offers raise sales and loyalty [1]; can detect fraudulent use of cards [1]. Costs of systems and storage; personal data held in one place is at risk of theft [1]. Customers: more relevant offers and discounts [1]; but their data may be shared or reused without their full understanding [1], and profiling could lead to unfair prices or offers [1]. Conclusion, for example that benefits are real if the chain is open about how data is used and protects it [1]. Maximum 6 marks. **[6]**

**13.** Governments collect large sets of data such as census returns, spending figures and travel patterns [1]. Data mining finds patterns, such as areas where population is rising faster than school places [1]. This is used to plan services such as housing, transport and education [1]. **[3]**

## Where marks are usually lost

- Describing a generic search of a database rather than finding unknown patterns.
- Missing the order of stages, or leaving out evaluation and deployment.
- Not showing the division in percentage questions.
- Writing "privacy" with no explanation of what is done with the data and what harm follows.
- Discussing only organisations or only individuals when both are named.
