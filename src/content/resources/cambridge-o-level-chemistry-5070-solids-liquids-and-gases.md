Section 1.1 of the Cambridge O Level Chemistry 5070 syllabus (2026–2028) has five learning outcomes. You must be able to state the distinguishing properties of solids, liquids and gases; describe their structures "in terms of particle separation, arrangement and motion"; describe and explain the five changes of state using kinetic particle theory; "interpret and explain heating and cooling curves"; and explain the effects of temperature and pressure on the volume of a gas. 5070 has no Core/Extended split, so every outcome is examined for every candidate.

The broader [States of Matter and Kinetic Particle Theory guide](/resources/states-of-matter-and-kinetic-particle-theory/) introduces the three states. This guide goes further into the reasoning examiners reward: deciding a state from melting and boiling point data, reading every section of a heating or cooling curve, and writing gas-volume explanations that score full marks.

## The particle description: three variables

Every answer about structure should cover the three variables the syllabus names. Use them as a checklist.

| | Solid | Liquid | Gas |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Separation** | Particles touching, very close | Particles close, mostly touching | Particles far apart compared with their size |
| **Arrangement** | Regular, repeating pattern (lattice) | Irregular, random | Random |
| **Motion** | Vibrate about fixed positions | Move around, sliding past each other | Move quickly and randomly in all directions |
| **Forces between particles** | Strong enough to hold positions fixed | Still significant, but particles can move | Very weak at this level |

A liquid's particles are almost as close as a solid's. Students often draw liquid particles widely spaced, which loses the mark for separation. Most substances expand only slightly on melting; the big jump in spacing happens on boiling.

### Linking structure to properties

The distinguishing properties follow directly from the table.

- **Shape.** A solid keeps its shape because its particles cannot leave their positions. Liquids and gases flow, so they take the shape of the container.
- **Volume.** Solids and liquids have a fixed volume because their particles are already touching. A gas spreads to fill any container.
- **Compressibility.** Only gases are easily compressed, because only gases have large empty spaces between particles.
- **Density.** Gases have much lower densities than solids or liquids of the same substance, again because of the spacing.

An "explain" question wants the property **and** the particle reason, linked with "because".

## Deciding the state from data

A common structured question gives a table of melting and boiling points and asks for the state at a stated temperature. Apply one rule:

- below the melting point: **solid**
- between the melting point and the boiling point: **liquid**
- above the boiling point: **gas**

**Worked example 1.** Use the data to state the physical state of each substance at room temperature, 25 °C.

| Substance | Melting point / °C | Boiling point / °C |
|---|---|---|
| Ethanol | −114 | 78 |
| Bromine | −7 | 59 |
| Oxygen | −218 | −183 |
| Sodium chloride | 801 | 1413 |

*Answer.* Ethanol: 25 °C lies between −114 °C and 78 °C, so liquid. Bromine: between −7 °C and 59 °C, so liquid. Oxygen: 25 °C is above its boiling point of −183 °C, so gas. Sodium chloride: below 801 °C, so solid.

Take care with negative numbers. −183 °C is a lower temperature than −7 °C, even though 183 is the larger number. A follow-up question might ask which substance is a liquid over the widest range: subtract the melting point from the boiling point. For ethanol that is 78 − (−114) = 192 °C; for bromine 59 − (−7) = 66 °C.

## Changes of state explained

The five changes named in the syllabus pair up as opposites.

| Change | From → to | Energy | What happens to the particles |
|---|---|---|---|
| Melting | solid → liquid | taken in | vibrations become strong enough to overcome some forces; particles leave fixed positions |
| Freezing | liquid → solid | given out | particles slow, forces pull them into fixed positions in a regular pattern |
| Boiling | liquid → gas | taken in | particles throughout the liquid gain enough energy to overcome the forces and escape; bubbles of gas form |
| Evaporating | liquid → gas | taken in | only the most energetic particles at the surface escape |
| Condensing | gas → liquid | given out | gas particles lose energy, slow down and are pulled together by the forces |

### Boiling compared with evaporating

These both turn a liquid into a gas, and examiners test the difference.

- **Boiling** happens at one temperature, the boiling point, and throughout the whole liquid.
- **Evaporating** happens at any temperature below the boiling point, and only at the surface.

Evaporation has a cooling effect: the particles that escape are the fastest ones, so the average energy of the particles left behind falls. That is why a liquid left in an open dish slowly cools its surroundings as it evaporates.

## Heating and cooling curves

A heating curve is a graph of temperature against time for a substance heated at a steady rate. Read it section by section.

1. **Sloping section, solid.** Energy increases the vibration of the particles, so temperature rises.
2. **First flat section, melting.** Solid and liquid are both present. Energy is used to overcome forces between particles, not to increase their kinetic energy, so temperature stays constant at the melting point.
3. **Sloping section, liquid.** Particles move faster; temperature rises.
4. **Second flat section, boiling.** Liquid and gas are present. Energy overcomes the remaining forces; temperature stays at the boiling point.
5. **Sloping section, gas.** Temperature rises again.

The boiling plateau is usually **longer** than the melting plateau. Much more energy is needed to separate particles completely than to let them slide past each other, so at a steady heating rate boiling takes longer.

**Worked example 2.** A sample of molten substance Y is left to cool. Its temperature is recorded every minute.

| Time / min | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 | 8 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Temperature / °C | 80 | 70 | 61 | 54 | 54 | 54 | 54 | 47 | 40 |

(a) State the freezing point of Y. (b) Explain why the temperature stays constant between 3 and 6 minutes. (c) State the physical state of Y at 7 minutes.

*Annotated answer.*
(a) 54 °C: the value of the plateau.
(b) Y is freezing. As particles move into fixed positions, forces of attraction form between them and energy is given out. This energy balances the energy lost to the surroundings, so the temperature stays constant until all of Y has frozen.
(c) Solid: the plateau has ended and the temperature is falling again below the freezing point.

Notice that the freezing point and melting point of a pure substance are the same temperature. For (b), "the particles are changing state" scores nothing; you must say that energy is released as forces form, or for a heating curve, that energy is used to overcome forces.

## Temperature, pressure and gas volume

The syllabus wants the effects **described** and **explained** using particles.

**Increasing temperature at constant pressure.** The particles gain kinetic energy and move faster. They hit the container walls harder and more often. If the container can expand, for example a gas syringe or a balloon, the gas spreads into a larger volume until the pressure inside again matches the pressure outside. So **volume increases**.

**Increasing pressure at constant temperature.** Pushing harder on the gas forces the particles closer together, into a smaller space. Because gas particles have large gaps between them, this is possible. So **volume decreases**.

A full-mark explanation names the change in particle speed or spacing, and links it to collisions with the walls. Writing "the gas expands because it gets hotter" only describes.

**Worked example 3.** A gas syringe holds 60 cm³ of air at room temperature. The syringe is placed in a beaker of hot water and the plunger moves out. Explain this observation.

*Answer.* The air particles gain energy and move faster [1], so they collide with the plunger harder and more frequently [1]. This pushes the plunger out, increasing the volume until the pressure inside equals atmospheric pressure [1].

## Common mistakes

- Saying particles get bigger, or melt, when heated. The particles stay the same size; their energy and spacing change.
- Drawing liquid particles far apart. They are close together but irregular.
- Writing that temperature rises during a change of state.
- Treating boiling and evaporating as the same process.
- Saying energy is given out when a substance melts. Melting, boiling and evaporating all take energy in; freezing and condensing give energy out.
- Misreading negative temperatures in data tables.

## Exam technique: the command words

- **State**: a short answer, no reason needed (for example "liquid").
- **Describe**: say what the particles are like or what happens, using the three variables.
- **Explain**: give the particle reason, mentioning energy and forces between particles.
- **Interpret** (curves): identify the section, name the state or change, and give the relevant temperature from the graph.
