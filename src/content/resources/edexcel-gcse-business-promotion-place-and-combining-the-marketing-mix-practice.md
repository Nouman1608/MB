## Questions

**1.** Define the term "branding". **[2]**

**2.** A sports-drink business pays £3,000 so that its name appears on the shirts of a local under-16 football team. State which promotion strategy this is. **[1]**

**3.** Juno Juice is launching a new fruit drink and hands out free samples in a shopping centre. Outline one reason this strategy is appropriate for a new drink. **[2]**

**4.** Cinder Coffee gives away 800 free sample sachets at a market, at a total cost of £360. Afterwards, 15% of those who took a sachet buy a 250g bag at £6.40. Calculate the number of buyers, the revenue from them, the cost per buyer and the revenue above the cost of the campaign. **[4]**

**5.** Explain one advantage to a business of targeted advertising online. **[3]**

**6.** Elmcrest Pet Treats normally sells a pack at £5.00 and sells 400 packs a week. During a special offer the price is cut by 15% and sales rise to 520 packs a week. Calculate the percentage increase in weekly revenue during the offer. **[4]**

**7.** Tarn and Thistle Teas sells loose-leaf tea. It wants to reach two segments. Segment A is students aged 18 to 22 with small budgets who spend a lot of time on social media. Segment B is customers aged 65 and over, who like to try a product before buying and read the local newspaper. Analyse how Tarn and Thistle Teas could use different promotion strategies for the two segments. **[6]**

**8.** Explain one disadvantage to a producer of selling its goods through retailers instead of directly to the public online. **[3]**

**9.** Corbel Pottery makes ceramic mugs that can break in the post. A retailer buys each mug for £10 and sells it for £16. Corbel's own website sells the same mug for £15, and its delivery, packaging and payment costs are £3.50 per mug.

**(a)** Calculate the retailer's share of the selling price as a percentage. **[2]**
**(b)** Calculate how much Corbel keeps per mug sold online, and how much more this is than the amount received per mug from the retailer. **[2]**
**(c)** Analyse why Corbel might still choose to sell some mugs through retailers. **[4]**

**10.** Explain one way in which a decision to raise the price of a product could influence the business's promotion. **[3]**

**11.** Aldous Activewear is choosing between two ways to promote a £40 training-kit bundle to women aged 18 to 30 living within ten miles of its shop.

Option A: sponsor a local netball team for £2,400. About 1,500 spectators will see the name during the season, and the owner expects 5% of them to buy a bundle.

Option B: pay £1,500 for online adverts shown to 12,000 people in that segment, and expect 0.8% of them to buy a bundle.

Justify which option Aldous Activewear should choose. **[6]**

**12.** Kirkstead Pet Foods sells organic dog food at £14 a bag. The product uses organic ingredients and is sold through independent pet shops and the company's own website. Promotion is sponsorship of local dog shows. A supermarket has launched a similar product at £8, advertised on television and sold in every branch. Evaluate whether Kirkstead's integrated marketing mix can keep its competitive advantage. **[8]**

## Answers

**1.** Creating a name, logo, design or image for a product or business [1] that customers recognise and that sets it apart from rivals [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* A definition tends to gain credit when it says what branding is and what it does. "The logo on a product" describes one part only.

**2.** **Sponsorship** [1]. **[1]**
*Examiner insight:* Give one answer. Writing two strategies risks including a wrong one next to the right one.

**3.** The drink is new, so shoppers do not know whether they will like it [1]. Tasting a free sample lets them judge it without risk, which makes them more likely to buy a full bottle [1]. **[2]**
*Examiner insight:* An outline answer tends to need two linked points set in the context. The second should follow from the first, not repeat it.

**4.** Buyers: 15% × 800 = **120** [1]. Revenue: 120 × £6.40 = **£768** [1]. Cost per buyer: £360 ÷ 120 = **£3.00** [1]. Revenue above cost: £768 − £360 = **£408** [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Show the working for each figure. A slip in the number of buyers affects the later figures, so clear working lets the later steps be seen.

**5.** The business can choose who sees the advert, for example by age, location or interests [1]. Money is therefore not spent reaching people who are unlikely to buy [1]. This improves the chance that each pound spent on advertising produces a sale, lowering the cost of winning each customer [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Explain answers tend to gain credit for a fact followed by two developments. "It reaches more people" without saying why they are the right people stays at one point.

**6.** Offer price = £5.00 × 85% = £4.25 [1]. Revenue before = 400 × £5.00 = £2,000 [1]. Revenue during = 520 × £4.25 = £2,210 [1]. Percentage increase = (2,210 − 2,000) ÷ 2,000 × 100 = **10.5%** [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* Percentage change means the change divided by the original figure. Using the new figure as the divisor is a common slip.

**7.** Segment A, students: viral advertising via social media, such as a funny video that students share [1]. It costs little and spreads through the segment's own networks, which suits people who spend a lot of time on social media [1]. A special offer, such as a discount code on a first order, also suits small budgets [1]. Segment B, aged 65 and over: product trials, such as sample sachets at local events [1], suit people who like to try before buying and reduce the risk of buying an unfamiliar tea [1]. An advert in the local newspaper reaches readers of that paper [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* Points tend to gain credit when they use the segment details in the stem, such as small budgets or newspaper readers. A list of strategies with no link to either segment stays general.

**8.** A retailer buys the product at a lower price than it sells it for and keeps the difference [1]. The producer therefore receives less per item than it would from selling directly [1], which reduces its revenue and profit unless the retailer buys enough extra units to make up for it [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* A strong answer links the retailer's share to the producer's revenue. "Retailers are expensive" alone stays at one point.

**9. (a)** Retailer's share = £16 − £10 = £6 [1]. £6 ÷ £16 × 100 = **37.5%** [1]. **[2]**
**(b)** Kept online = £15 − £3.50 = **£11.50** [1]. Difference from the retailer route = £11.50 − £10 = **£1.50** more per mug [1]. **[2]**
**(c)** A retailer already has customers who visit the shop, so Corbel reaches buyers it has not had to find itself [1]. Customers can see and handle the mug before buying [1], which may help sales of an item they would otherwise worry about receiving broken [1]. The retailer may order large quantities, giving Corbel steady volume, whereas online sales depend on Corbel promoting its website and paying for delivery and breakages [1]. **[4]**
*Examiner insight:* In part (b), subtract the costs from the online price before comparing. Part (c) is stronger where points use the stem, here breakage in the post.

**10.** A higher price tends to signal quality or exclusivity [1], so promotion should stress the quality and reliability that justify the price, not low prices or discounts [1]. It should also be aimed at customers willing to pay more, for example through branding that suggests a premium product [1]. **[3]**
*Examiner insight:* Answers tend to gain credit when they say what changes in the second element, here promotion. Stating that price and promotion are "connected" is not enough.

**11.** Option A: 5% × 1,500 = 75 buyers [1]. Revenue = 75 × £40 = £3,000, which is £600 above the £2,400 cost [1]. Option B: 0.8% × 12,000 = 96 buyers, revenue = 96 × £40 = £3,840, which is £2,340 above the £1,500 cost [1]. The cost per buyer is £32 for Option A and about £15.63 for Option B [1]. In favour of Option A, sponsorship can build awareness and goodwill that last beyond the season, and the figures are forecasts [1]. I recommend Option B, because it brings more buyers for less money, and it is aimed at the exact segment, though Aldous should check the 0.8% against actual results [1]. **[6]**
*Examiner insight:* A justify answer tends to gain credit when it uses the figures in the stem and ends with a clear recommendation. These revenue figures ignore the cost of making the bundles, so treat them as a first comparison.

**12.** In favour: the product, price, place and promotion fit one message. Organic quality and a premium price of £14 [1], independent pet shops and a website [1] and sponsorship of local dog shows reaching committed owners [1] all say the same thing, which makes the offer clear and hard for a rival to copy as a whole [1]. Against: the supermarket price of £8 is well below £14 and the product is available in every branch with television adverts, so it reaches far more people [1]. Kirkstead probably has a much smaller promotion budget than a supermarket chain, so it is unlikely to match this reach [1]. The advantage only lasts if the target market values organic quality enough to pay the extra £6 a bag [1]. Overall, I judge that Kirkstead can keep its advantage with owners who put quality first, because the mix is consistent, but it will probably lose price-sensitive buyers, so it should watch sales for signs of switching [1]. **[8]**
*Examiner insight:* An evaluate answer tends to gain credit when it weighs the mix against the rival's threat and reaches a supported conclusion. Describing the mix only stays descriptive.

## Where marks are usually lost

- Defining branding as only a logo or a name.
- Naming a promotion strategy without linking it to the segment in the stem.
- Giving "social media" for viral advertising with no mention of sharing.
- Not linking the retailer's share to the producer's reduced revenue.
- Dividing a percentage change by the new figure.
- Forgetting to subtract delivery and packaging costs when comparing online and retailer routes.
- Describing the four elements one by one when asked how one influences another.
- Claiming a competitive advantage without saying what rivals offer.
- Finishing a justify or evaluate answer without a supported judgement.
